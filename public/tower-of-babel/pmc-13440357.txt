Surviving the Nucleus Pulposus Desert: Next‐Generation Strategies for Intervertebral Disc Cell Therapy
Authors: Tynhinane Hamidouche, Namdev More, Tiffany Lee, Mia Orr, Anne Camus, Devina Purmessur, Sarah E. Gulbrand, Nadeen O. Chahine, Lara Silverman, Dmitriy Sheyn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLow back pain remains the leading cause of disability worldwide, with intervertebral disc degeneration representing a major biological contributor. Although cell‐based therapies have shown promise in preclinical models, clinical translation has yielded modest and inconsistent outcomes. Accumulating evidence suggests that therapeutic failure reflects not only limitations in cell source or differentiation potential, but also the hostile biochemical and biomechanical microenvironment of the degenerative disc. Hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, acidity, lactate accumulation, fibrosis, senescence, inflammation, and abnormal mechanical loading collectively impair cell survival, integration, and long‐term function.MethodWe performed a comprehensive review of the literature using PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, with emphasis on studies published between 2020 and 2026. Evidence was critically evaluated to examine advances in cell‐based therapies for IVDD, including cell sources, mechanisms of repair, biomaterial‐assisted delivery systems, microenvironment‐targeted strategies, translational studies, and emerging technologies that enhance regenerative efficacy.DiscussionCurrent evidence indicates that successful disc regeneration depends not only on selecting an appropriate therapeutic cell source but also on overcoming the biological constraints imposed by the degenerative niche. We critically compare the regenerative potential of mesenchymal stromal cells, nucleus pulposus cells, and induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived therapies, highlighting their respective advantages and limitations. We further discuss how biomaterial carriers, extracellular vesicles, developmental biology‐guided differentiation, genetic engineering, preconditioning approaches, and smart delivery platforms are being integrated to improve cell survival, phenotype stability, extracellular matrix restoration, and functional repair.ConclusionFuture success in intervertebral disc regeneration will require integrated therapeutic strategies that combine optimized cell sources with biomaterial‐assisted delivery, microenvironment modulation, and precision bioengineering. Advancing these complementary approaches will be essential for achieving durable biological repair, restoring disc structure and function, and translating regenerative therapies into effective clinical treatments for patients with degenerative disc disease.

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is a global epidemic hiding in plain sight—common, costly, and biologically more complex than spinal imaging suggests. It is the leading cause of disability worldwide, affecting ~619 million people and projected to reach 843 million by 2050 due to population aging [1]. Approximately 40% of chronic LBP cases are associated with intervertebral disc (IVD) degeneration (IVDD), yet research has historically focused on IVDD rather than LBP itself [2]. Despite extensive efforts, no approved therapies are yet capable of halting or reversing disc degeneration by restoring native disc structure and biological function [3]. Current treatments, including physical therapy, pharmacological management, discectomy, spinal fusion, and total disc replacement, primarily address symptoms or mechanical instability rather than biological restoration, depending on disease severity [3, 4, 5, 6]. Over the past two decades, biological repair strategies encompassing both cell‐based and cell‐free therapies, including growth factor delivery, gene therapy, extracellular vesicles, and biomaterial‐based systems, have been explored to restore the extracellular matrix composition and disc structure [5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]; (Figure 1).

Schematic illustration of major treatment approaches investigated for IVD degeneration, including pharmacological therapies, gene therapy, biomaterials, growth factor delivery, and cell‐based therapies. Each modality is associated with distinct challenges that have limited durable clinical success, including safety concerns, poor bioactivity, limited retention, inadequate delivery, short biological half‐life, and poor survival of transplanted cells.

The nucleus pulposus (NP) is a central target for regenerative repair because NP cell dysfunction and loss are key features of disc degeneration. Although the IVD is an integrated structure composed of the NP, annulus fibrosus (AF), and cartilaginous endplate (CEP), this review focuses on NP biology and NP‐targeted regenerative strategies while recognizing that AF integrity and CEP transport strongly influence the NP microenvironment.

Many regenerative approaches have demonstrated encouraging anabolic effects in vitro and in preclinical models, but durable clinical regeneration remains limited [4]. In practice, many interventions described as “regenerative” achieve transient improvements rather than long‐term restoration of disc function [8, 10, 12]. This translational gap reflects fundamental differences between experimental models and the degenerative human disc environment, as well as strong placebo responses that may mask limited biological effects in clinical trials [13]. Advances in stem cell biology have enabled the generation of notochordal‐like cells, precursors of NP cells, and NP‐like cells from human induced or embryonic pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs or ESCs), offering improved control over cell identity and scalability [14, 15, 16, 17]. In regenerative cell‐mediated strategies, therapeutic cells are intended to promote IVD repair by differentiating toward an IVD‐like phenotype and/or by releasing trophic and anti‐inflammatory mediators that support matrix restoration and modulation of the local microenvironment [8]. However, regenerative success depends not only on cell identity or anabolic capacity, but also on the ability of transplanted cells to survive, integrate, and function within the NP microenvironment [18].

The adult NP resides within an avascular and isolated niche characterized by chronic hypoxia, low glucose availability, accumulation of lactic acid, acidic extracellular pH, high osmolarity, and sustained mechanical loading [19, 20, 21]. As degeneration progresses, nutrient transport further declines, inflammatory mediators increase, and vascular and immune cell infiltration may occur, [22, 23] defining the NP as a “physiological desert”. These challenging conditions likely contribute to variable clinical outcomes and highlight a critical gap in current regenerative approaches, which are often evaluated without sufficient consideration of the microenvironment that ultimately determines cellular fate and function [4, 24, 25]. Clinical and translational studies reported limited durability for growth factor delivery, gene‐based interventions, and biomaterial strategies, largely due to rapid degradation, cell leakage, or impaired biological activity under IVD‐like conditions [4, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. True regeneration remains rare, particularly in advanced degeneration, underscoring the need to rethink regenerative success beyond phenotype specification or matrix supplementation alone [8, 12, 29]. Emerging strategies, therefore, increasingly emphasize environmental compatibility, stress tolerance, cell preconditioning, protective biomaterial niches, and controlled delivery systems [30, 31, 32]. Together, these findings indicate that durable IVD regeneration will require integrated strategies that address the interacting metabolic, inflammatory, mechanical, structural, and delivery‐related constraints of the degenerative disc niche.

In this review, we aim to (1) synthesize current clinical and translational progress, (2) examine the challenges that continue to limit durable IVD regeneration, (3) define the key biological and microenvironmental barriers imposed by the NP microenvironment, and (4) examine how next‐generation engineered solutions may enable durable IVD cell therapy.

Current Clinical Landscape of IVD Cell Therapies

Cell‐based therapies have emerged as a promising approach for the treatment of IVDD, aiming to restore the disc structure by promoting ECM production, rehydration of the NP, and inflammatory regulation. Various cell types have been investigated to evaluate their therapeutic potential, each offering unique regenerative properties (Table 1). Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), commonly isolated from bone marrow, umbilical cord, and adipose tissue, are among the most extensively studied and clinically advanced cell types due to their relative safety, accessibility, and low immunogenicity [44, 45]. MSCs possess multipotent differentiation capacity within mesenchymal lineages including chondrogenic lineage and can be expanded in culture with moderate scalability; however, their maturity is dependent on the source and their proliferative potential and differentiation capacity is optimal at lower passages [46]. Further, while utilizing native disc cells offers advantages through their innate adaptation to the disc environment, NP and Annulus Fibrosus (AF) cells offer lower scalability because of their limited yield during isolation [47, 48]. Due to their scalability and pluripotency, iPSCs and ESCs have emerged as attractive alternatives to generate Notochordal‐like and NP‐like cells; however, they require rigorous differentiation protocols to mitigate the risks of teratoma formation and allogeneic rejection [20, 49]. Despite encouraging results in preclinical in vitro and in vivo models, survival in the harsh disc environment and the development of effective delivery vehicles poses consistent challenges to long‐term clinical success [50, 51, 52].

Current clinical trials for cell therapies primarily investigate the effects of injections of multiple cell types, including cells derived from MSC (autologous and allogeneic) and NP allografts in patients with chronic discogenic LBP who have failed conservative treatments [33, 35, 37, 53] (Table 1). These current trials are generally early‐ to mid‐phases, most commonly using randomized, double‐blind, saline‐ or sham‐controlled designs [33, 35, 53]. Throughout the studies, improvements in pain have been evaluated using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) or the Numeric Pain Rating Scale (NPRS), and disability has been evaluated by the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) over 6–12 months and sometimes up to 24 months [33, 35, 40, 53]. MRI‐based outcomes are generally used to assess disc height, signal intensity, and morphology for structural changes that correlate with pain improvement, but structural changes have been modest and inconsistent across studies. This variability likely reflects differences in baseline degenerative disc severity, patient selection, cell source, cell dose, delivery vehicle, and imaging methodology, which together make cross‐study comparison difficult and limit translational interpretation, (Table 1), but still have been viewed as promising therapies for the future. Although current clinical trials have shown encouraging symptom improvements in some cohorts, these trials have also revealed limitations in design (Table 1) interpretation, and translational maturity by several design features. Most current clinical trials have enrolled small patient populations at a limited number of centers, representing a small portion of the real‐world population with existing comorbidities [35, 37, 40, 42]. Due to this limitation, the trial's statistical significance is restricted, thereby decreasing its external validity, especially in a condition with substantial clinical heterogeneity. In addition, follow‐up durations rarely extend past the 12–24‐month mark, which is relatively short compared to the long natural timeline of IVDD, and limits assessment of durability [33, 35, 37, 40, 53]. The literature also lacks standardization in degenerative disc grading, reporting of cell dose, and use of carriers or vehicles, further complicating comparison between trials. Trial populations have often been small, not fully stratified by baseline degeneration grade. Cell doses have varied across studies rather than following a standardized range; for example, published MSC protocols have used doses around low‐million to tens‐of‐million cells. Importantly, MRI findings have not consistently demonstrated durable structural regeneration, [35, 37, 39, 43] and no trial has directly evaluated long‐term cell survival, integration, or matrix replacement in humans.

While cell survival in the harsh disc niche remains a primary concern, recent clinical evidence suggests that the timing of intervention and the placebo effect of the delivery procedure are significant hurdles [17, 36]. Recent analyses of randomized controlled trials indicate that saline control groups have achieved significant improvements in VAS and ODI scores up to 12 months, showing no statistical difference from biologic agents in some cohorts, indicating that injection alone may contribute to the observed benefit [17]. This may also help explain why biological agents have sometimes failed to show large between‐group differences. A hypothesis is that injection‐related changes in disc biochemistry and pressure may temporarily mask the regenerative effects of the cells. Uncertainty regarding cell migration, off‐target differentiation, and immune rejection further limits translational maturity. As a result, only a couple of trials [35, 53] have progressed to controlled Phase 2 or early pivotal programs, with allogeneic NP allografts appearing closest to commercialization, having a 60%–75% responder rate [33, 53]. However, future larger and longer‐term studies will be required to assess the dosage, delivery, and longevity of cell‐based therapies for clinical use in the treatment of IVDD.

Why Clinical Success Remains Elusive: Barriers in the Degenerative Niche

Microenvironmental Hostility

The degenerative IVD is characterized by a complex interplay of physicochemical stressors that create a hostile micro‐ and macroenvironment for resident and transplanted cells [19, 54, 55, 56]. The healthy NP is avascular and chronically hypoxic (1%–5% O2), relying on diffusion through the cartilage endplate (CEP), which provides approximately 75% of the necessary nutrients [57]. Under these conditions, NP cells depend primarily on glycolysis for energy production, resulting in sustained lactate production that is normally regulated through metabolic exchange with the CEP [44, 45, 46]. Lactate is therefore not merely a waste product but a part of a tightly controlled metabolic coupling between the NP and the CEP, as deletion of the lactate transporter MCT1 has led to severe IVDD [47]. With aging and degeneration, CEP calcification and altered mechanical loading alter tissue hydration and solute movement within the matrix, reducing oxygen and glucose availability [48, 49]. As diffusion declines, oxygen tension can fall below 1% and glucose concentrations may decrease to less than 0.5 mM, conditions associated with suppression of matrix synthesis and significant cell death [48, 49, 50]. This structural impairment shifts cellular metabolism toward greater reliance on anaerobic glycolysis, promoting lactate accumulation and extracellular acidification, with pH levels reported as low as 5.7 in highly degenerated discs [20, 21, 45, 46, 49, 51].

The acidic microenvironment further exacerbates cellular dysfunction by activating stress pathways, including acid‐sensing ion channels and inflammatory signaling cascades, which can trigger Ca2+ influx and inflammasome signaling pathways that promote different types of cell death [33, 35, 52, 53]. These processes promote the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines like IL‐1β and TNF‐α, reinforcing metabolic imbalance. In this context, MCT1‐mediated lactate uptake forces cells to inadvertently “re‐poison” themselves with their own metabolic waste, [37, 45, 46], while emerging evidence suggests that lactate‐driven epigenetic modifications, such as histone lactylation, further modulate cellular phenotype [44]. Extracellular acidity has been shown to independently suppress cell viability and proteoglycan synthesis and induce matrix‐degrading enzymes, in some cases exceeding the effects of pro‐inflammatory cytokines [40, 42]. However, whether acidification represents an initiating driver of degeneration or primarily amplifies pre‐existing structural and mechanical damage remains unknown. Furthermore, this acidic shift has been linked to discogenic pain through the upregulation of neurotrophic markers, which facilitate pathological nerve ingrowth [36, 39, 43]. Impaired nutrient transport, metabolic dysregulation, and extracellular acidification collectively reshape the NP microenvironment, leading to low nutrient availability, lactate accumulation, and altered pH. This “desert”‐like niche represents a major barrier to regeneration, as transplanted cells must adapt to these constraints to survive and function.

Biological Barriers

In a healthy disc, NP is a soft and jelly‐like structure highly hydrated, proteoglycan‐rich, characterized by abundant laminin‐integrin interactions, aggrecan, type II collagen, and other matrix molecules that support cell clustering and proteoglycan production. With aging and degeneration, NP homeostasis is progressively disrupted by an active fibrotic remodeling process in which the tissue transforms into a disorganized, collagen‐dense matrix. NP cells undergo fibroblastic and myofibroblast transition, driven in part by TGF‐β signaling and the recruitment of circulating myeloid‐derived fibrocytes and αSMA+ myofibroblasts [34, 38]. This shift results in irreversible fibrocartilaginous tissue formation, increased matrix stiffness, and loss of NP‐specific matrix organization, fundamentally altering the mechanical environment and driving cellular dysfunction. Exposure to a stiffer matrix activates mechanosensitive pathways, including MRTF and YAP/TAZ signaling, promoting fibroblastic phenotypes and loss of NP identity [41, 58, 59]. This demonstrates that NP fibrosis is an active, cell‐driven process that reshapes both the biomechanical and biochemical environment.

Cells residing in or delivered into a fibrotic NP face a stiff niche that reinforces degenerative phenotypes rather than supporting regeneration [60]. This stiff microenvironment is a central biological barrier limiting cell integration and survival while promoting scar‐like tissue formation instead of restoration of a healthy NP phenotype. Together with metabolic decline, these changes establish a self‐reinforcing cycle of multi‐modal degeneration, barrier, that is further amplified by chronic inflammation and immune cell recruitment (Figure 2).

Cellular senescence is a stable state of cell‐cycle arrest that further contributes to this barrier. In the context of IVDD, senescent NP cells accumulate with degeneration grade and acquire a senescence‐associated secretory phenotype (SASP) that promotes inflammation, catabolism, and metabolic dysfunction [61, 62]. Recent studies suggest that this barrier may be partially reversible through reprogramming approaches, such as exosome‐mediated delivery of the OKS factors, which can ameliorate senescent cell phenotypes by resetting the epigenetic marks, reducing SASP and catabolic enzymes, restoring proteoglycans expression, and attenuating IVDD and pain in preclinical models [63, 64, 65]. These findings underscore senescence as both a barrier and a potential therapeutic target that could affect engraftment. However, the long‐term stability and safety of these strategies remain unclear.

Structural and Procedural Barriers to Intradiscal Therapy

Intradiscal injection remains the principal route for delivering regenerative therapies to the intervertebral disc. Although needle puncture may theoretically contribute to degeneration, large clinical studies report this outcome to be rare [66, 67]. However, the mechanically dynamic disc environment presents major barriers to successful cell transplantation [53]. The AF not only contributes to disc biomechanics, but also plays a critical role in maintaining therapeutic containment following intradiscal injection. AF disruption through fissures or tears may promote acute leakage and hydrostatic expulsion of transplanted cells, with studies estimating that up to 70% of injected cells may be lost shortly after delivery [12, 13]. In addition to reduced efficacy, cell leakage may also contribute to off‐target tissue formation and osteophyte‐like changes, further emphasizing the importance of structural containment during regenerative therapy delivery [68]. Existing AF repair approaches are largely designed to restore mechanical integrity and reduce extrusion [69], whereas regenerative strategies frequently prioritize biological restoration without fully addressing long‐term retention and stabilization. In parallel, the CEP, the primary nutrient gateway of the disc, is essential for maintaining nutrient diffusion and metabolic homeostasis within the NP [14, 15]. CEP calcification and structural disruption can impair solute transport, further restricting nutrient availability and potentially limiting long‐term survival and function of transplanted cells. Consequently, the degeneration stage and residual structural integrity of the disc may critically influence therapeutic efficacy. Earlier stages of degeneration, where AF containment and nutrient transport are relatively preserved, may be more amenable to biologic approaches such as growth factor or cell‐based therapies, whereas advanced degeneration with severe structural disruption, altered biomechanics, or impaired transport may require combined tissue engineering and biomechanical restoration strategies. Quantitative modeling studies further suggest that recovery of disc biochemical and mechanical properties after cell transplantation is likely to occur slowly, with meaningful clinical improvement potentially taking years to develop [70, 71]. This further underscores that successful disc regeneration depends not only on the regenerative potential of the implanted cells, but also on their ability to remain retained and function within a structurally damaged disc. This has driven growing interest in approaches such as biomaterial‐supported delivery systems, AF repair strategies, and restoration of CEP transport to improve cell retention and mechanical support after transplantation.

Role of Inflammation in Degeneration

Many factors serve as initiating events that lead to the abnormal production of cytokines and catabolic molecules by IVD cells, including genetic predisposition, smoking, infection, abnormal biomechanical loading, decreased nutrient transport, and aging. Regardless of the trigger, degeneration is mediated by abnormal production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as TNFα, IL‐1β, IL‐6, and IFN‐γ by both resident disc cells and recruited immune cells [72, 73]. These mediators promote upregulation of catabolic enzymes and chemotactic factors, which suppress ECM production, leading to cell apoptosis and GAG loss [72, 74, 75, 76, 77]. Structural disruption of the disc, such as tears and clefts, enables interaction with systemic circulation, facilitating immune cell infiltration and amplifying inflammation. The interaction between the IVD milieu and systemic circulation may contribute to both disc degradation and pain through the recruitment of immune cells, while inflammatory signaling further drives nociceptive sensitization via the induction of neurotrophic and pronociceptive mediators. IVD cells are heterogeneous and undergo phenotypic transformation, in part by interactions with exogenous cells that mediate degeneration. Immune cells, including macrophages, monocytes, and T cells, contribute to phenotypic shifts in NP cells toward degenerative and fibrotic states [78, 79]. Single‐cell analysis of disc patients further shows that matrix‐producing cell populations dominate non‐degenerated discs, whereas cells associated with stress response and inflammation are enriched in degenerated samples, supporting a phenotypic shift of NP cells toward fibrotic states [36, 78, 80]. Interestingly, subsets of NP cells associated with nociceptive and neurogenic pathways found in painful degenerated human IVDs also increased pain‐related behaviors in vivo [36]. The full spectrum of cellular identities and specific functions of the immune cell populations in IVDD and pain remains under investigation. Although small animal models confirm that inflammation contributes to degeneration and pain, the extent to which these models fully recapitulate human disease remains an open question. Some studies have optimized injury to simulate broader inflammatory activation (e.g., multiple stabs, optimized needle sizes) and to produce a state of persistent inflammation in the disc [81, 82, 83, 84, 85]. Puncture injury has identified clusters of IVD cells, endothelial cells, myeloid cells, and lymphoid cells post injury, with distinct phases of inflammation and proliferation post injury [86, 87]. A notable difference in degenerated IVDs compared to controls is the increase in myeloid and lymphoid cells in degenerated IVDs [87]. Interestingly, novel MSC populations as well as sex‐based differences in injury immune responses have been identified post‐puncture, [87, 88] highlighting that sex‐specific immune responses may influence both disease progression and therapeutic outcomes, underscoring that repair strategies may need to be sex‐optimized [87, 88]. Additional animal models using inflammatory stimuli such as LPS or TNFα confirm that inflammation induces macrophage infiltration, cytokine production, and pain‐related behaviors, correlating with degeneration severity, [89, 90] highlighting the importance of inflammation and degeneration to the pain behavior [66, 91, 92] and the difficulty in reversing discogenic pain. However, findings from transgenic models suggest that inflammatory activation alone is not always sufficient to drive structural degeneration, indicating that additional factors contribute to disease progression [67, 68]. On the other hand, progeria mice (Ercc1−/Δ) exhibit increased NF‐κB activity and ECM degradation, which is attenuated by p65 inhibition [67, 68, 69]. More recently, NF‐κB overactivation in the disc drives progressive degeneration [70]. These inflammatory processes involve chemotactic signaling, catabolic enzyme expression, and macrophage recruitment, which can be therapeutically mitigated with an inflammatory‐resolving macrophage secretome [70, 71]. Genetic deletion of chemokine expression reduces immune cell migration and protects against biomechanical, although structural restoration remains limited [93]. This suggests that the timing of such inflammatory interventions may be an important variable to further optimize efficacy.

Role of Inflammation in Cell Therapy

Stem cells, particularly mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), are widely used for IVD repair due to ease of collection and their ability to promote regeneration. IVD is an immune‐privileged tissue due to its avascular nature, which makes it suitable for allogeneic cell transplantation. Interestingly, undifferentiated MSCs are thought to promote regeneration via cross‐talk with host NP cells, suggesting that host IVD cells can regulate MSC differentiation after transplantation [94, 95]. However, the inflammatory microenvironment strongly influences therapeutic outcomes. Elevated catabolic and inflammatory activity can alter MSC survival, differentiation, and immunomodulatory function [96, 97, 98]. MSCs are primed by inflammatory mediators released from degenerate or inflamed tissues toward either an anti‐ or pro‐inflammatory phenotype [99]. Inflammatory priming strategies, including IL‐1 or IFN‐γ exposure, have been shown to enhance anti‐inflammatory and chondroprotective properties [100, 101]. IFN‐γ promotes expression of anti‐pathogenic proteins, enhances MSCs' survival, while limiting inflammation and fibrosis [102]. The effects are further strengthened under conditions and include improved suppression of T‐cell proliferation, even following exposure to pro‐inflammatory macrophages [103]. Despite promising strategies, the efficacy of primed MSCs in the chronically inflamed disc environment has not been established. Indeed, as degeneration progresses, inflammatory mediators and immune cell infiltration strongly influence therapeutic outcomes. The response of host disc cells and donor MSCs depends on the degenerative state, loading conditions, and the carrier used for delivery [104]. Conditioned media from umbilical cord MSCs have also been shown to be protective of insults leading to IVD cell senescence and ECM degradation [105]. The timing of MSC delivery is a critical determinant of therapeutic outcome. One study found that stem cell treatment of injured rabbit IVDs 7 months post injury was more beneficial than 1 month post injury [106], with the beneficial effects of MSCs thought to be masked by inflammation during the early stages of degeneration, although findings remain conflicting [107]. Enrichment of MSCs and the associated increase of glycosaminoglycan suggest that cell therapies administered at an early stage of injury/disease progression may have greater chances of mitigating matrix loss, possibly through promotion of MSC activity by the inflammatory microenvironment associated with injury. Indeed, rather than being a barrier, early‐stage inflammation may provide the essential cues required for stem cells to exhibit anti‐inflammatory and immune‐modulating activity [108, 109]. In co‐cultures with disc cells, MSCs suppress matrix degeneration, inflammatory cytokine production [110, 111, 112], and immune cell proliferation [113, 114, 115, 116].

Developmental Blueprint: Lessons From Notochordal Biology

Recently, developmental biology, genetics, and emerging single‐cell sequencing approaches have converged to define key principles of notochordal biology that now inform translational research [117, 118, 119, 120]. These developmental insights provide a framework to understand why regenerative strategies often fail in the adult disc, where cells are exposed to a markedly different microenvironment than during development. Lineage tracing studies in mice have demonstrated that NP cells originate from the embryonic notochord, which arises during gastrulation from axial mesoderm progenitors specified by NODAL, WNT, BMP, and FGF signaling gradients [121, 122]. Notochordal cells (NC) also produce SHH, a key morphogen that guides somite differentiation into sclerotomes, ultimately forming vertebral bodies, CEP, and the AF [61, 123, 124, 125, 126]. During organogenesis, NC adopt a vacuolated morphology, where fluid‐filled organelles occupy nearly 80% of the cell volume and function as structural and osmotic regulators [127, 128]. The notochord regresses at vertebral body levels while persisting in the disc anlagen through a tightly regulated balance between proliferation, apoptosis, and ECM remodeling driven by molecular and physical cues that are not yet completely characterized [129, 130, 131, 132, 133, 134]. Developmental studies across species [135], and ex vivo approaches [136, 137, 138] have shown that persisting NC play a crucial role in maintaining NP tissue integrity by supporting NPCs survival and proteoglycan‐rich matrix deposition. In humans, notochordal phenotype is prominent during childhood but is progressively lost before adolescence and replaced by smaller, non‐vacuolated NPCs. This transition, marked by changes in gene expression, signaling activity, and cellular composition, contributes to the disc's increasing vulnerability to degeneration with age [139, 140, 141, 142]. Single‐cell transcriptomics studies have further revealed striking cellular heterogeneity in the developing and mature NP and characterized transcriptomic signatures that enable monitoring of cell identity across development, aging, and degeneration [14, 143, 144, 145, 146, 147]. This challenges the idea of a single “ideal” cell source for regeneration. Instead, it emphasizes the need to understand the dynamic cellular ecosystem established during development and maintained postnatally. This knowledge is essential for improving disc health, designing disease‐modifying treatments, and innovating cell therapies [148, 149].

Fundamental questions of clear therapeutic relevance persist, including how NC self‐renew, achieve full maturation, and respond to mechanical cues to maintain lineage stability. Knowledge on human notochord and NP development remains scarce due to ethical constraints [150, 151]. Thus, more critically, the developmental blueprint derived from notochordal biology has guided the design of in vitro pluripotent stem cell‐based models, identifying key regulators of lineage specification and matrisome composition [14, 15, 16, 152, 153, 154, 155, 156, 157, 158]. Although the fidelity of downstream notochordal differentiation has been achieved, enhancing efficiency and reducing off‐target differentiation requires further improvements, even though recent identification of specific surface markers opens up prospects for cell purification and the implementation of more detailed functional analyses [14].

Traditional monolayer cultures are limited in stabilizing cellular identity, as notochord‐like cells display considerable heterogeneity and do not reach full maturation, often lacking hallmark features, including vacuoles and ECM secretion. Advanced 3D culture platforms, such as hydrogels, native NP‐ or laminin‐rich matrix, and organoid systems, more faithfully recapitulate developmental architecture, microenvironment, and intercellular dialogue [11, 159]. A recently developed 3D human trunk organoid model replicates key embryonic features, including a central notochord‐like structure flanked by somites and a neural tube [17]. Consistent with in vivo development, this model exhibits a relatively low number of notochordal‐like cells, yet displays an ECM‐rich notochordal sheath and temporal neural patterning driven by SHH. Such 3D systems are well suited to investigate unresolved biological questions concerning how a balanced biochemical, mechanical, and cellular microenvironment sustains NC self‐renewal, paracrine signaling, and ECM homeostasis. Importantly, these developmental principles have direct implications for regenerative strategies, suggesting that successful therapies will require not only appropriate cell identity but also recreation of key developmental cues, including matrix composition, mechanical environment, and paracrine signaling.

Further insight is needed into key regulatory factors, matrisome composition, and cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions that govern NP maturation, including the role of notochordal sheath and vacuolation in this dynamic process. In summary, the greatest contribution of developmental biology to NP regeneration is not a single protocol or cell type, but a paradigm shift. Overall, notochordal biology still offers a promising framework that links embryogenesis to adult tissue maintenance and degeneration. What developmental biology teaches us about how the notochord is specified, maintained, and transformed into the NP informs essential principles to guide future innovations aimed at restoring disc structure, function, and long‐term homeostasis.

Cell‐Free Approaches: Extracellular Vesicles (EVs) & Beyond

Cell‐free approaches for the repair and regeneration of the degenerate IVD are gaining attention in response to the current limitations of many potential biological strategies under investigation, as described above. Cell‐free approaches include but are not limited to delivering secretome‐based bioactive ligands, such as growth factors and gene‐based therapies, which can be packaged and delivered via viral vectors or nanoparticles such as EVs. Soluble factors have been widely utilized to regenerate the IVD. Specifically, soluble factors from healthy mature NPC or immature notochordal cells have been shown to induce differentiation of human and canine MSCs and promote an anabolic phenotype in degenerated human and canine NP cells in vitro [160, 161, 162, 163]. An alternative cell‐free approach is EVs, lipid cell‐membrane‐derived nanoscale particles capable of carrying proteins and genetic cargo such as DNA and various RNAs [164]. They are non‐immunogenic, host‐derived, stable, and biocompatible, overcoming many of the limitations associated with viral delivery methods [165]. Naïve EVs from primary cells and cell lines have demonstrated the potential to modulate cell phenotype and function. In terms of genetic cargo, EVs can also be engineered to carry specific plasmid DNA with transcripts coding for relevant proteins, such as transcription factors, that do not integrate into the host genome and therefore diminish risks related to mutagenesis [166]. Furthermore, EVs can also be functionalized with specific ligands that enable delivery to specific cells or tissues [167]. Naïve EVs isolated from MSCs, NCs, and iPSCs and used to treat IVD cells or MSCs, respectively, have demonstrated promising results regarding IVD regeneration and repair, [168, 169, 170, 171] promoting increases in healthy NP and AF phenotypic and anabolic markers and decreases in catabolic markers in vitro and in vivo [168]. Naïve EVs isolated from iPSC‐derived MSCs have been shown to rejuvenate senescent NPCs and limit degeneration when injected into the IVDs of a rat tail model of IVDD [172]. Recent work by Ambrosio et al., compared the effects of naïve EVs derived from NPCs (Tie2 enhanced versus those cultured in normal IVD media) with EVs derived from MSCs in vitro and in a rat caudal model of IVDD and demonstrated that NPCs were superior in mitigating degeneration and pain markers in both models [173]. More recently, engineered eEVs are being utilized to deliver genetic cargo to the IVD and IVD cells [174]. Engineered EVs from both donor human NPCs or primary embryonic mouse fibroblasts (PMEFs) to overexpress the developmental pioneer transcription factor FOXF1, which were then successfully taken up by recipient human NPCs in vitro or mouse IVD cells in vivo, as demonstrated by increases in FOXF1 expression in these cells. Furthermore, when PMEF‐derived eEVs loaded with FOXF1 were delivered to a mouse lumbar puncture model of IVDD, significant increases in structural restoration such as increases in IVD hydration, GAG, mechanical properties and disc height, were observed together with decreases in pain behaviors [166]. Engineering EVs to carry factors like FOXF1 may represent a strategy to reprogram resident cells toward a Notochordal‐like phenotype to restore the blueprint in situ without the risks of off‐target cell differentiation. Self‐powered triboelectric‐responsive microneedles with controllable release of optogenetically engineered EVs have been developed to deliver specific cargo, in this case the protein TRAM to mitigate the effects of DNA damage in senescent NPCs of rats in vivo [175]. To increase the efficiency of EV uptake and mitigate impaired caveolae‐mediated endocytosis due to inflammation in NPCs, EVs from human MSC donor cells have been engineered to express caveolae‐associated protein 2 (Cavin‐2, Cavin‐2‐EVs), where TNFα‐treated NPCs demonstrated enhanced Cavin‐2‐EV uptake with reductions in cell death [176]. EVs derived from a human MSC line have also been engineered to over‐express anti‐inflammatory protein TSG‐6 via CRISPR‐CAS9 gene editing with modulation of proinflammatory gene expression in IVD cells treated with IL‐1β [177].

Major biological and technical challenges limit the use of EVs as therapeutic agents. These include substantial heterogeneity within the biological environment, as EVs derived from the plasma membrane, viable cells, apoptotic cells, and healthy cells can exhibit distinct biological functions. To date, no definitive surface markers are available to reliably separate these EV subtypes, and this heterogeneity can significantly influence their biological effects. Other key technical challenges are inconsistency with production and batch‐to‐batch variability, variability in protein, DNA and RNA cargo, size and number generated, dynamic biodistribution clearance (short‐half‐life) in vivo [178] and the efficiency of packaging both proteins and transcripts [179]. As highlighted in a recent review article by Corraini et al., there are challenges regarding the interpretation of findings from many studies using cell‐derived naïve EVs therapeutically as many do not use protocols that adhere to the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV) [180].

Engineering Next‐Generation Cell Therapies

Functional Engineering of iNCs and iNPCs

While different cell‐based approaches for IVD regeneration have been investigated in human studies [10], their clinical benefits remain limited and largely based on short follow‐up periods [8, 16, 18, 181]. This has shifted attention toward pluripotent stem cell‐derived approaches, enabling the generation of induced notochordal cells (iNCs) and induced nucleus pulposus cells (iNPCs) with improved fidelity to native disc phenotypes, while also offering scalability and potentially engineering potential [18, 19, 20]. Supporting this concept, a recent study demonstrated that NP and annulus fibrosus progenitor cells outperform mesenchymal stromal cells in human‐scale engineered discs by producing more appropriate extracellular matrix and avoiding the metabolic drift toward hypertrophic features [182].

Advances in genome engineering, particularly CRISPR‐based approaches, have further demonstrated that modulation of anabolic, catabolic, and inhibition of inflammatory pathways can transiently improve disc structure and cellular phenotype in vitro and in animal models, supporting the feasibility of functionally enhancing disc‐relevant cell types [183, 184, 185, 186]. For example, targeting ZNF865 in adipose‐derived stem cells significantly enhanced collagen II and GAG production, improving mechanical properties without inducing hypertrophy [186]. In parallel, partial epigenetic reprogramming has also been explored to alleviate senescence‐associated features and improve proliferative and metabolic characteristics of NPC [90].

However, these approaches remain primarily validated in controlled experimental settings, with limited data in clinically relevant large‐animal models. Importantly, most engineering strategies remain focused on addressing the downstream hallmarks of degeneration, such as matrix loss and inflammation, rather than addressing long‐term function and survival within an extremely hostile disc environment [187, 188, 189, 190]. This likely contributes to the limited durability observed in clinical translation, even when cell identity and delivery are optimized. In addition, it remains unclear whether transplanted cells durably engraft and functionally integrate within the degenerative disc or instead act predominantly through transient trophic and immunomodulatory effects. Long‐term persistence, maintenance of disc‐specific identity under degenerative conditions, and the risk of phenotypic drift toward undesired mesenchymal or hypertrophic states remain insufficiently characterized. Reported differentiation efficiencies and maturation states also vary substantially across protocols, further complicating direct comparison and translational standardization. Collectively, these limitations likely contribute to the limited durability observed in clinical translation, even when cell identity and delivery are optimized.

As a potential future direction, synthetic gene circuits may offer a framework for engineering adaptive cell responses to the disc microenvironment. Although not yet applied in IVD systems, evidence from other avascular and mechanically loaded tissues suggests that closed‐loop regulatory systems capable of sensing and responding to metabolic or inflammatory cues may enhance cellular resilience [191, 192, 193]. In this context, engineered cells could function as biosensors, dynamically activating survival, buffering, or matrix‐preserving pathways in response to stressors such as pH changes, lactate accumulation, or mechanical overload. However, significant challenges remain, including circuit stability, signal specificity, scalability, and long‐term safety in mechanically active tissues. Thus, it is necessary to reframe the engineering of iNCs and iNPCs around intrinsic functional fitness, incorporating matrix modulation and mechanobiological resilience rather than phenotype optimization alone (Figure 3).

Schematic illustration of next‐generation regenerative approaches designed to address the hostile NP microenvironment. Key strategies include the generation of developmentally relevant iPSC‐derived NP‐like cells, preconditioning to enhance adaptation to metabolic, inflammatory, and mechanical stress, and smart biomaterial platforms that improve cell retention, survival, and controlled therapeutic delivery.

Preconditioning Strategies

Given the avascularity, low cellularity, and metabolically restrictive nature of the IVD microenvironment, preconditioning strategies have been explored to enhance the survival and function of the transplanted cells. Hypoxic preconditioning enhances MSCs' robustness through HIF‐1α dependent pro‐survival, paracrine signaling, and matrix synthesis in vivo, attenuating disc degeneration in rat models, [30, 194, 195] although low oxygen tension alone is insufficient to generate a stable NP–like phenotype [196]. Mechanical preconditioning, along with inflammatory or disc‐conditioned media exposure, reshapes MSC secretome toward an immunomodulatory and matrix‐supportive profile, while enhancing NP‐like phenotypes and retention without inducing angiogenesis or neurogenesis [197, 198, 199]. Non‐stress‐based strategies using pharmacological agents, growth factors, or biological mediators can similarly enhance pro‐survival, metabolic, and reparative signaling pathways in transplanted cells [200]. Quiescence preconditioning of NP stem cells enhances survival and regenerative efficacy through adaptive downregulation of cellular metabolism, addressing the nutrient‐deficient disc environment [201]. These studies suggest that while preconditioning is necessary for effective disc repair, current approaches often examine individual stressors in isolation, despite transplanted cells being exposed to their simultaneous and sustained interaction in vivo, highlighting the need for strategies that pre‐adapt cells to the combined metabolic, osmotic, and mechanical stresses of the degenerated disc rather than optimizing responses to single cues alone.

Immune/Safety Strategies

Although the IVD has long been considered relatively immune‐privileged, recent single‐cell transcriptomic profiling has revealed the presence of discrete immune cell populations within both healthy and degenerating discs, suggesting a more complex “immune‐monitored” environment than previously understood [108, 202]. While iNCs show promise in porcine and mouse models, the risk of tumorigenicity from residual undifferentiated cells or genomic instability acquired during prolonged in vitro expansion remains a primary regulatory hurdle for spinal applications [20, 203]. Although differentiated cells are generally more stable and subject to rigorous quality control before implantation, the risk of aberrant differentiation within the harsh, acidic IVD microenvironment necessitates long‐term in vivo safety monitoring [204]. Xenotransplantation studies further indicate that human iPSC‐derived cells and primary NPCs do not trigger systemic immune responses, further supporting their potential for allogeneic therapeutic use [205]. However, disc degeneration disrupts this protection through annular tears and neovascularization, which act as broken fences that allow local inflammatory signaling to facilitate immune cell infiltration and increase the risk of immune recognition and clearance of transplanted cells [206]. Recent evidence supports the concept that controlling local immune signaling rather than systemic immunosuppression can create a permissive microenvironment for disc regeneration [207]. Similarly, synthetic gene circuits and genome engineering approaches introduce additional concerns regarding off‐target effects, unintended pathway activation, and long‐term regulatory stability within mechanically active tissues [184, 188, 208]. In the IVD, the acidic and hypoxic microenvironment may further impact the fidelity of these gene circuits and the survival of engineered cells. Additional safety concerns include inflammatory foreign body responses and local toxicity associated with implanted biomaterials or their degradation byproducts, which may exacerbate inflammation and pain within the disc environment [209]. Consequently, future therapies will likely integrate stem cell engineering strategies, including immune cloaking, immune checkpoint modulation, and selective HLA editing with biomaterial encapsulation and local immunomodulation to achieve durable engraftment [210, 211, 212].

Advanced Biomaterial Strategies in NP Regeneration

Biomaterial applications in IVD regeneration are not merely passive; they can actively enhance biological function by restoring biomechanical properties, mimicking the native extracellular matrix, modulating the tissue microenvironment, and enabling the delivery of cells and bioactive molecules.

Biomaterials & Disc Biomechanics: Ensuring Functional Integration

Cell therapies for IVD regeneration are faced with an extremely challenging biomechanical loading environment. The discs are the primary structures in the spine responsible for permitting multi‐directional loading while bearing and distributing repetitive and often high magnitude loads which occur during our daily activities. These loads can exceed five times body weight with complex activities [213]. The mechanical function of the disc is closely linked to its composition. In a healthy disc with a high concentration of sulfated glycosaminoglycans, the fixed charge density of the NP generates substantial swelling pressure that is constrained by the annulus fibrosus and cartilage endplates, supporting axial loading of the spine [214]. The healthy NP is a viscoelastic material that exhibits both fluid‐like and solid‐like behaviors depending on the applied loading conditions [215]. During IVDD, however, the NP loses proteoglycan content and becomes increasingly fibrotic. The swelling pressure of the degenerative NP is therefore substantially reduced, and permeability is increased [216]. NP aggregate modulus under uniaxial confined compression is reduced with degeneration, while the dynamic shear modulus is increased [216, 217]. Overall, these changes compromise the whole disc mechanical function, leading to reduced motion segment stiffness in early degeneration [218]. Due to the compositional and mechanical changes occurring in early‐stage disc degeneration, hydrogel augmentation of the NP has long been an area of considerable research interest. Early NP replacement devices utilizing stiff, bulk hydrogel materials were plagued by issues with migration, extrusion, and clinical complications [219]. Therefore, research efforts are now primarily focused on the development and translation of soft injectable hydrogel biomaterials, typically formulated from hyaluronic acid, collagen II, and other naturally‐derived biomaterials or extracellular matrices [220, 221, 222, 223, 224, 225, 226]. These hydrogels have been utilized for cell‐free NP augmentation, as well as serving as carriers for therapeutic cell sources. Injectable hydrogels can be delivered to the disc minimally invasively through small‐gauge needles, minimizing damage to the disc, extrusion risk, and unwanted off‐target effects of cell therapy such as osteophyte formation [227, 228]. Hydrogel carriers have also been demonstrated to have tunable mechanical properties similar to those of the native NP, [229, 230] and delivery of hydrogels to degenerative discs has been shown to restore whole motion segment mechanical function [220, 226, 231]. Numerous studies have also demonstrated that soft (0.3 to 2 kPa) hydrogels support progenitor cell chondrogenesis, as well as the maintenance of healthy NP cell phenotype [224, 232, 233, 234, 235, 236, 237]. A glycerol‐crosslinked PVA hydrogel (GPG) exhibits a compressive modulus of 4.4 ± 0.2 kPa, which closely matches that of healthy NP tissue and mimics the native NP compressive mechanics [238]. This “smart” hydrogel not only delivers NP cells but also protects them from mechanical stress within the IVD, thereby better replicating ECM‐like function.

Biomaterials Effectively Deliver Cells and Restore IVD Functions

Cell therapies aim to address the root cause of IVDD, replenish NPCs, and promote matrix production. However, direct injection of these cells remains challenged by poor cell survival, limited proliferation, and inadequate functional differentiation within the compromised disc microenvironment [11, 239]. Recent research has focused on developing effective delivery (cargo) systems for cell transplantation (Figure 4). Hydrogels are among the most attractive carriers because they mimic the native ECM‐like structure and promote cell adhesion and proliferation. Collagen, alginate, hyaluronic acid, gelatin, polyethylene glycol (PEG), and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) are notable polymers that have been widely used for IVDD due to their excellent biocompatibility, injectability, and biomechanical properties [9]. Despite these advances, conventional hydrogels can still present challenges in injectability and biodegradability [240]. These limitations can be addressed using norbornene chemistry [241]. For instance, gelatin modified with tetrazine–norbornene bioorthogonal ligation enables the precursor to remain a free‐flowing liquid for 5–10 min and then gel across a wide temperature range, supporting easy injectability and efficient encapsulation of bioactive agents such as TGF‐β [242]. However, these approaches mainly improve material properties and often lack direct pharmacological activity, such as modulation of the tissue microenvironment.

Advanced injectable biomaterials and their mechanisms in IVD repair and regeneration. Overview of injectable therapeutic platforms currently explored for IVD repair, including hydrogels, extracellular vesicles (EVs), nanoformulations, and cell‐, drug‐, or gene‐loaded microgels. These biomaterial systems provide mechanical support and therapeutic delivery while promoting cell survival, redox balance, immunomodulation, inflammation control, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and tissue regeneration within the degenerative disc microenvironment.

IVD Microenvironment Modulating Biomaterials

A key component of microenvironment modulation is control of inflammation, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and low pH. Sodium alginate hydrogel combined with poly(N‐isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAAm), silicate ceramics (SCs), and NP cells have been proposed as a cell‐carrying hydrogel with immunomodulatory activity. Here, silicate ceramics act as a reservoir for Mg2+, which supports polarization of macrophages from the M1 to the M2 phenotype, thereby reducing inflammation, slowing IVDD progression, and supporting IVD regeneration [243]. Beyond immunomodulation, inflammation can be reduced by scavenging free radicals. Gelatin modified with phenylboronic acid and the natural anti‐inflammatory molecule epigallocatechin‐3‐gallate functions as a cytoprotective hydrogel that scavenges ROS and has demonstrated IVD regeneration in a rat IVDD model [244]. In addition, IL‐1β is a major cytokine contributing to IVD inflammation; sodium alginate hydrogels loaded with melatonin and bioglass have been developed, where melatonin (a tryptophan‐based molecule) helps neutralize IL‐1β and provides anti‐inflammatory effects, while bioglass helps retain mechanical properties similar to the native NP [245].

The native IVD is avascular; however, degeneration is further complicated by aberrant vascularization, where inflammatory cytokines drive pathological nerve and vessel ingrowth, further accelerating disease progression [246]. Bevacizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody that targets and neutralizes vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF‐A), thereby inhibiting angiogenesis [247] was loaded on PLGA‐PEG‐PLGA hydrogels to provide sustained release of bevacizumab, thereby limiting IVDD progression [245, 248]. Increased vascularization also raises local oxygen turnover, which can accelerate ECM degradation. To restore a hypoxic‐like environment, laccase has been incorporated into smart alginate–gelatin hydrogels; laccase‐mediated reactions consume oxygen and induce hypoxia, which upregulates the HIF‐α pathway and increases expression of ACAN and collagen II [249]. However, the in vivo stability of these formulations under acidic pH remains unclear, raising concerns about effectiveness. To exploit local acidity, carboxymethyl chitosan combined with oxidized dextran, phenylboronic acid, and luteolin can form hydrogels in the presence of free protons via Schiff‐base reactions. This system not only responds to pH but also exhibits anti‐apoptotic activity and may prevent IVDD progression through activation of the PI3K/AKT/NF‐κB signaling pathway [250].

Although significant progress has been made in both conventional and smart hydrogels, these systems are typically bulk materials. While they are injectable and can encapsulate cells and bioactive agents, cell survival within bulk hydrogels can be limited: cells near the surface often show higher viability than cells in deeper regions, which may experience greater stress [251]. To further improve cell‐based approaches, major advances have been made in cargo systems such as microgels, nanofibers, and nanoparticles [252, 253].

Microgels are among the most extensively explored delivery systems for IVD therapy due to their injectability, improved cell viability, favorable mechanical properties, and ease of functionalization. They have been used for cell delivery, gene delivery, drug delivery, and microenvironment modulation as previously reviewed [254, 255, 256]. Here, we focus on smart microgels designed specifically for microenvironment modulation. Microgels fabricated from a composite matrix of GelMA and chondroitin sulfate methacryloyl, loaded with MSCs and Prussian blue, have dual functions: they deliver cells and act as broad‐spectrum ROS scavengers [257]. Microgels can also carry bioactive molecules to promote differentiation of progenitor cells into NPCs; for example, circFADS1‐loaded PLGA microgels, combined with NP tissue‐derived stem cells, can release circFADS1 and stimulate differentiation toward NP‐like phenotypes [258]. Similar to smart hydrogels, smart microgels can modulate the IVD microenvironment. GelMA microgels containing the Wnt5a‐mimetic peptide Foxy5 and BMSCs can promote NP differentiation and alleviate inflammation by reducing ROS through inhibition of the IL‐17B/NF‐κB signaling pathway, leading to IVD regeneration in vivo [259]. Smart microgels have also been applied to gene delivery. For example, miR‐155‐5p can inhibit apoptosis via the PI3K/AKT pathway; GelMA microgels loaded with miR‐155 nanoparticles enable release under acidic pH, providing anti‐inflammatory effects and promoting ECM production [260, 261]. Likewise, siGrem1 delivered via nanoparticles and encapsulated in microgels shows anti‐apoptotic activity, and combinations such as siGrem1 with Cyc3 have demonstrated controlled release in vivo [262].

Beyond delivery of cells and genes, microgels can also recruit endogenous cells to support repair and regeneration. GelMA combined with small molecules such as BPAA‐GFF (a custom fibrillar peptide) and kartogenin can provide dual activity: BPAA‐GFF attracts progenitor cells through chemotaxis, while kartogenin promotes progenitor differentiation into NPCs [263]. Despite these advances, retention strategies for microgels within the IVD remain uncertain, opening new directions for research. In addition to cells and genes, microgels are promising carriers for EVs and drugs for IVDD treatment. For example, chitosan microgels loaded with the anti‐inflammatory drug celecoxib have shown IVD repair by locally blocking inflammatory cytokines [264]. Current individual delivery systems fail to simultaneously achieve the biological, biochemical, and biomechanical restoration required for treating IVD degeneration. Hybrid carrier systems offer a promising strategy to overcome these limitations, paving the way for the development of comprehensive and ideal therapeutic platforms.

Regulatory & Manufacturing Landscape

While many promising therapeutic approaches for LBP have been proposed, clinical translation remains challenging for several reasons. New treatment options—particularly cell and gene therapies—require a lengthy and costly development pathway to establish good manufacturing practice (GMP)–compliant protocols, conduct extensive nonclinical testing, and often complete multiple clinical trials prior to regulatory approval. Transitioning research processes into cleanroom manufacturing environments requires adaptation to tightly controlled conditions, replacement of research‐grade materials with clinical‐grade quality ancillary reagents, and the addition of numerous in‐process and final release assays that necessitate method development and qualification [168]. In many cases, the manufacturing process itself must be optimized to achieve clinically and commercially relevant scale through the implementation of automated bioreactors and closed‐system platforms, as early research is frequently performed at a small scale. These transitions are ultimately required to support the large potential patient population eligible for LBP therapies. In parallel, appropriate nonclinical models must be developed to evaluate safety and demonstrate potential biological activity. For IVDD and LBP, available animal models provide only a limited approximation of the human mechanical and biochemical condition and are generally insufficient to predict clinical efficacy [277]. The limitation of current IVDD models is not the absence of systems, but the fact that each captures only part of the human condition. Models that allow pain behavioral readouts are typically small animals, such as rodents, where disc size is markedly reduced and notochordal cells (NC) persist into adulthood, creating a regenerative environment that does not exist in adult human discs [278, 279]. In contrast, some larger systems better approximate human disc size and loading, but they differ in cellular fidelity: sheep and bovine discs are more human‐like because they are NC‐transient, whereas porcine discs retain NC into maturity, which may bias repair responses [148, 280]. Ex vivo bovine organ culture provides useful control over disc biology but cannot capture systemic loading or pain [281]. Efficacy is often demonstrated in systems that either favor biological repair or fail to test clinically relevant outcomes, helping explain why multiple clinical trials, despite encouraging nonclinical data, have demonstrated only modest clinical benefit.

Conducting clinical trials in LBP presents additional challenges. Primary and secondary endpoints are often based on patient‐reported outcomes, which are inherently subjective and associated with substantial interpatient variability. As a result, achieving adequate statistical power may ultimately require substantially larger patient cohorts than those used in many current IVDD studies, particularly when endpoints rely on subjective pain‐ and function‐based outcomes with high interpatient variability. Although reported clinical trials have averaged approximately 65 patients per study, larger confirmatory trials may be necessary to reliably detect clinically meaningful effects [282]. Large, multisite trials are logistically complex and costly, further complicated by regional differences in standard‐of‐care treatment algorithms and clinical practice patterns, which hinder protocol harmonization. Although regulatory agencies have implemented expedited programs and regulatory incentives for therapies targeting rare diseases, indications such as LBP remain subject to full regulatory scrutiny, with few available regulatory shortcuts. Consequently, development timelines are long and financial risk high, reducing the attractiveness of these programs to investors. In addition, many clinical sites lack the infrastructure, specialized equipment, and training required to handle live cell‐based products or manage cryogenic storage, creating further limitation to commercialization. Nevertheless, successful regulatory approval and commercial launch of an effective LBP therapy would represent a substantial opportunity [283], given the significant disease prevalence and unmet medical need in the US and globally.

The Road Ahead

Over the past few decades, IVD regeneration has largely been framed as a problem of cell insufficiency: replace what is lost and restore what has degenerated. Yet the accumulating evidence presented in this review suggests a different conclusion. The degenerative disc is not simply depleted; it is biologically reprogrammed. It represents a metabolically constrained, mechanically stiffened, inflammatory, and senescence‐enriched ecosystem that actively instructs cellular behavior. Delivering developmentally competent cells into such an environment without addressing its instructive cues is unlikely to yield durable restoration.

Integrated strategies are therefore essential. Degeneration is maintained by converging metabolic, inflammatory, fibrotic, and mechanobiological pathways. Therapeutic success will require coordinated modulation of these axes rather than sequential or isolated intervention. Regeneration must be approached as a systems‐level recalibration of disc homeostasis. The convergence of smart carriers and engineered cells offers a rational path forward. Biomaterials should function not only as delivery vehicles but as microenvironmental modulators, buffering acidity, mitigating oxidative stress, and restoring mechanical compliance. In parallel, engineered iPSC‐derived disc cells equipped with adaptive regulatory programs could sense environmental stressors such as pH shifts, lactate accumulation, or inflammatory activation and respond through context‐dependent survival and matrix‐preserving pathways. Such dynamic responsiveness may prove more important than static phenotype specification.

Finally, progress will depend on biomarkers and trial design that align biological endpoints with clinical outcomes. Given that pain relief, rather than structural restoration, defines clinical success, regenerative strategies must be evaluated in the context of their clinical impact and in preclinical systems that more closely recapitulate the biochemical, mechanical, and cellular features of the human degenerative disc. While disc regeneration may alleviate pain, structural repair alone may be insufficient once sensitization is established. Importantly, many regenerative studies continue to prioritize structural and molecular endpoints without adequately assessing pain outcomes or functional recovery. Future strategies must therefore target both tissue regeneration and the inflammatory and neurobiological mechanisms that sustain discogenic pain in parallel. Quantitative imaging, molecular signatures of senescence and inflammation, and degeneration‐stage stratification are needed to distinguish true structural regeneration from procedural effects. Genetic stratification, particularly regarding risk loci in ACAN, MMP genes, and collagen variants, may further refine this approach, as patients with distinct genetic backgrounds may exhibit differential responses to regenerative therapies, influencing cell survival, integration, and matrix restoration. In addition, age and sex are critical biological variables that influence disc matrix integrity, immune responses, and progenitor cell fitness, and should be incorporated into patient stratification and trial design to ensure consistent regenerative outcomes. The future of IVD therapy will depend not only on better cells but on a deeper integration of developmental biology, mechanobiology, biomaterials science, and translational methodology.
