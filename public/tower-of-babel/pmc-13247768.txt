Benchmarking performance in robotic liver resection: are we measuring what matters?
Authors: Francesca Tozzi, Filip Gryspeerdt, Hasan Eker, Niki Rashidian
Published: 2026

Robotic liver resection (R-LR) is steadily gaining adoption worldwide. Despite potentially higher costs, the robotic platform offers increased dexterity, superior ergonomics, and enhanced visualization. It facilitates a less steep learning curve that helps overcome the limitations of laparoscopic liver resection (L-LR), whose diffusion has been gradual and largely confined to pioneering centers with extensive expertise (1). These characteristics have driven a rapid yet unstandardized evolution of R-LR. The swift pace of minimally invasive innovation has often outstripped the generation of high-level evidence and standardized benchmarks to guide case selection, training, and outcome reporting (2). Benchmark outcomes are crucial because crude comparisons of outcomes between centers may be misleading, reflecting heterogeneity in referral patterns and local practice rather than true differences in quality. Among the various indicators used to assess performance, the “textbook outcome” has emerged as a comprehensive composite measure encompassing intraoperative safety, postoperative morbidity, and recovery quality. By anchoring best achievable results in well-defined cohorts, benchmarking provides a shared framework for quality assurance, audit, structured training, and global comparability as robotic platforms continue to diffuse (3).

The recent study by Goh et al., “Defining Global Benchmarks for Robotic Liver Resections: An International Multicenter Study”, published in Annals of Surgery, provides benchmark indicators of perioperative outcomes in R-LR. Benchmarks were established for four standardized R-LR procedures: left lateral sectionectomy (LLS, H23), left hepatectomy (LH, H234 ±1), right hepatectomy (RH, H5678 ±1), and right posterior sectionectomy (RPS, H67). Screening of 5,213 minimally invasive R-LR performed across 51 centers between 2016 and 2022 was conducted. Benchmarking for 16 intra- and postoperative outcomes was restricted to 518 low-risk robotic cases from 25 expert centers. Cutoffs were defined at the 75th percentile of individual center medians, after which the benchmark cohort was compared with high-risk expert cases and low-risk non-expert cases. In addition to individual indicators, the authors employed the textbook outcome as a composite benchmark, an increasingly used methodology to summarize perioperative quality into a single measurable endpoint. Benchmark values describe best-achievable performance for individual perioperative indicators, whereas textbook outcome is an all-or-nothing composite measure summarizing a complication-free perioperative course.

The study by Goh et al. is unique in establishing globally derived benchmark indicators for R-LR. It provides a rigorous methodological framework and aggregates data from 51 centers worldwide, applying standardized benchmarking across predefined procedures. In doing so, it generates a comprehensive global reference for R-LR. Limitations include the retrospective design with potential selection bias and center-level heterogeneity, restriction to short-term perioperative outcomes without oncologic follow-up, small procedure-level numbers at some sites and incomplete consideration of the learning curve and resource variability. The benchmark cohort represented only 518 low-risk cases out of 5,213 screened resections, reflecting an idealized subset drawn from expert centers and underscoring that these values describe optimal-case performance rather than outcomes generalizable to unselected practice. Moreover, not all participating centers performed both robotic and laparoscopic hepatectomy, limiting internal comparability of platform-specific outcomes across institutions. The study period [2016–2022] also spanned meaningful evolution in robotic platforms and surgeon experience, and the absence of longitudinal case-load or temporal analyses limits interpretation of how learning progression may have influenced benchmark attainment (4).

Additionally, a few considerations are warranted when interpreting these results. L-LR has been practiced for more than two decades, yet its global benchmark values were only defined recently, reflecting a long maturation phase before standard setting (5,6). By contrast, the robotic benchmarks emerge early in the diffusion curve and are derived from low-risk cases in expert centers that were already pioneers in L-LR. The early definition of clear reference standards can enhance safety, support standardized reporting, and guide training and program growth across regions. However, these values should be interpreted as best achievable performance in comparable low-risk contexts and carefully mapped to local case mix, resources, and experience (3). This is a high-quality study that fills an early methodological gap and supports the safe standardization of R-LR. Nevertheless, as the benchmarks are derived from a highly selected cohort, they should be interpreted cautiously to avoid underestimating outcomes in unselected real-world practice. This distinction is critical when translating benchmark data into practice.

When compared with prior laparoscopic benchmark indicators, robotic benchmarks consistently demonstrate lower rates of open conversion and blood loss across all major procedures. These findings highlight the technical stability and refined precision of the robotic platform but also reflect the highly selected, low-risk composition of the benchmark cohort. Perioperative outcomes were more favorable for left-sided resections, while results for RH remained comparable between robotic and laparoscopic benchmarks, indicating that procedure complexity continues to influence outcomes regardless of approach. Taken together, these comparisons confirm that R-LR can achieve excellent intraoperative and short-term results under optimal conditions, yet they also emphasize that these values represent best-case performance rather than targets universally attainable in every setting. The study also applied the textbook outcome framework to capture composite perioperative success, underscoring that even under optimal conditions these benchmarks represent best-case performance rather than expectations for all practice environments (4-7). These observations are consistent with the ROC’N’ROLL randomized controlled trial, which found no significant differences in perioperative outcomes or 90-day quality-of-life measures between robotic and laparoscopic hepatectomy, helping contextualize benchmark performance as reflective of optimized low-risk expert-center practice (8).

Procedure-specific complications such as bile leak and post-hepatectomy liver failure (PHLF) were not included among the benchmark outcome indicators. Reliance on overall major morbidity by Clavien-Dindo may have diluted the signal from these events, which are central to the safety profile of liver resection. Their omission, including the absence of International Study Group of Liver Surgery (ISGLS) defined bile leak and PHLF, represents a meaningful gap that future benchmarking efforts should address. A complementary study by Li and colleagues addressed this gap by explicitly benchmarking bile leak and PHLF for R-LR using ISGLS definitions and by stratifying cutoffs according to resection extent and Iwate difficulty, reinforcing the need for procedure-specific safety metrics within robotic benchmarking (9).

These two landmark studies can be regarded together as complementary references. Goh et al. defined global benchmark values for four mainstay R-LR procedures, while Li et al. broadened the scope by including both major and minor resections, stratifying outcomes by Iwate difficulty, and, importantly, incorporating procedure-specific complications such as bile leak and PHLF. Complementary findings from Li et al. further show that anatomically minor yet technically complex resections, including posterosuperior segment and posterior sectionectomies, are associated with increasing morbidity as Iwate difficulty rises, underscoring that procedural complexity rather than resection extent is the key driver of risk (4,9).

Furthermore, a few general considerations are warranted. Benchmark outcome indicators emphasize that patient selection is more important than center volume for achieving high-quality results and implementing a successful R-LR program. In Goh et al., deviations from benchmark values were driven primarily by case-related risk, as high-risk patients fell outside benchmark thresholds even in expert centers, whereas low-risk cases achieved comparable outcomes across centers regardless of their designation. This confirms that case selection, rather than institutional designation alone, is the principal determinant of achieving benchmark performance. Goh et al. set a ceiling for what is achievable under optimal conditions and provide valuable targets for audit and training. However, these data are derived from low-risk cases in expert centers with long-standing experience in minimally invasive L-LR (4). They should therefore be viewed as aspirational guideposts rather than universal standards applicable to every case mix and resource setting. Centers that do not meet these values in comparable low-risk cases should regard this as a signal to review patient selection, perioperative processes, and team proficiency, while transparently accounting for local resources.

Two key elements determine whether a program can achieve benchmark-level performance. The first is patient selection. On multivariable analysis, Li et al. demonstrated that fulfilling benchmark inclusion criteria independently reduced the odds of failing to achieve a textbook outcome, whereas increasing Iwate difficulty was associated with higher risk (9). Low-volume centers can match benchmark outcomes when patient selection is rigorous. Although lower volume centers may achieve benchmark outcomes when early cases are carefully selected, complex resections such as anterior and posterior sectionectomies require institutional maturity, coordinated robotic team proficiency, and structured experience in minimally invasive liver surgery.

The second is structured implementation. The Brescia European guidelines on minimally invasive liver surgery recommend a stepwise pathway, discouraging major R-LR until at least 25 minor R-LR have been completed, and note that prior laparoscopic experience is helpful but not mandatory (10). The robotic platform can also mitigate the early learning curve typical of laparoscopy, underscoring the importance of deliberate selection and staged adoption over center volume during program development (4). A practical learning curve for low- and intermediate-difficulty cases is estimated at 60–75 procedures, whether robotic or laparoscopic, when training is structured and concomitant (1,11,12). This supports simultaneous exposure to robotic and laparoscopic platforms early in training, provided case complexity is limited and supervision is formalized through both proctoring and mentoring (13). Evidence from high-volume programs indicates that early planned conversions, cautious case selection, and progressive procedural complexity contribute to excellent long-term performance (14). For centers transitioning to robotics, this implies adopting a staged pathway anchored in lower difficulty and lower risk cases, auditing outcomes against published benchmarks, and expanding indications only after demonstrating safety at each stage. The Brescia guidelines emphasize that the surgical approach should reflect local expertise and resources, and that open surgery remains appropriate when selection criteria are not met (10).

Program scale alone is not the determinant of success. Outcomes within benchmark limits are attainable even in lower-volume centers when patient selection is rigorous, while complex resections should be introduced only after proficiency in minor procedures has been achieved. This aligns with guideline recommendations for stepwise expansion and underscores the importance of structured training and credentialing (4,10). Recent diffusion data from the E-AHPBA indicate that, although access to robotic platforms is widespread, procedural volumes remain modest in many centers, with only a very small fraction performing more than 80 R-LRs per year. Economic and logistical barriers persist. These constraints make benchmark outcomes invaluable as directional goals to guide patient selection, training, and service organization, rather than rigid thresholds applicable to all practice settings (15).

In conclusion, we commend the study by Goh et al., whose benchmarks represent valuable reference standards for best achievable performance in defined low-risk settings. Their application in routine practice should be context-sensitive, accounting for local resources, experience, and case mix. Integration into clinical practice requires a structured strategy emphasizing stepwise adoption, transparent auditing, and incorporation into multicenter registries. Long-term follow-up will be essential to confirm their durability and relevance. This includes oncologic outcomes such as margin status, recurrence, and survival, as well as patient-centered outcomes such as quality of life, postoperative function, and recovery trajectories. Looking forward, the continued evolution of R-LR will benefit from iterative refinement of these benchmarks and their integration with data science and artificial intelligence to enable more precise risk adjustment and real-time performance monitoring.
