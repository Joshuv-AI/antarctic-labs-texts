Predictive pre-resection robotic ligament balancing in total knee arthroplasty is accurate across surgeons and patient factors
Authors: Alexander D Orsi, Vasili Karas, Jeffrey DeClaire, Andrew Lehman, Christopher Plaskos, Simon Coffey, Jeffrey Lawrence
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNew robotic total knee arthroplasty (TKA) technology enables force-controlled pre-resection joint gap assessment. Its accuracy across surgeons and patient factors remains unknown. This study evaluated how accurately this technology achieved planned joint gaps and whether accuracy was influenced by surgeon, preoperative deformity, sex, BMI, and age. Six hundred seventy-two consecutive robotic TKAs from five surgeons were retrospectively reviewed. A robotic ligament tensioner applied 70–100 N medially and laterally before bony resections. Mean (ME) and mean absolute (MAE) predicted gap errors (final minus planned) were calculated at 10°, 45°, and 90° flexion. Linear regression evaluated associations between error and preoperative alignment and sex. A sub-analysis included BMI and age. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests compared errors between surgeons and deformity groups. Overall, MAE was 1.1 mm medially and 1.3 mm laterally. The largest ME occurred laterally in flexion in varus knees (-0.7 mm; tighter than plan), and laterally in extension in valgus knees (0.6 mm; looser than plan). Extension errors were similar in flexion-contracture and sagittal-neutral knees (ME within ± 0.1 mm), while hyper-extended knees were 0.5 mm looser than planned. Increasing BMI was associated with lateral flexion gap tightening (-0.59 mm per 10 kg/m2 increase, p = 0.018). ME was within 1.4 mm across all surgeons and flexion angles. Robotic TKA with force-controlled pre-resection gap assessment accurately achieved targeted joint gaps across a large patient cohort. While surgeon and patient factors influenced joint gap error, these effects were small. Awareness of these variations may help optimize implant planning.Keywords: Total Knee Arthroplasty, Robotic-assisted surgery, digital tensioning, pre-resection balance, accuracy

Introduction

Until now, manual assessment has been the only method for assessing bicompartmental ligament laxity before bone resections in total knee arthroplasty (TKA). While practical, manual assessment is known to be highly variable and subjective, depending on surgeon experience and technique [3, 4]. Recent studies have demonstrated variability in soft tissue laxity within preoperative alignment groups, suggesting that coronal deformity alone is insufficient to predict intraoperative gap behavior [9]. Additionally, preoperative functional laxity assessment may better reflect patient-specific soft tissue characteristics than intraoperative surgeon-defined laxity [14]. Existing technologies can measure individual compartment gaps with manual tensioners prior to resection, and one system can assess the soft tissue robotically, but requires an initial tibial resection [25, 32]. To overcome these limitations, a novel system was recently developed for robotic soft tissue assessment of both compartments simultaneously prior to any bone resection [17]. The ability of this technology to accurately achieve a joint gap plan created before bony resections represents a new approach to intraoperative knee balancing and patient-specific surgical planning. However, the accuracy and reliability of this approach across a large, diverse patient population and among multiple surgeons have not previously been established.

The primary hypothesis of this study was that a force-controlled pre-resection balancing system would accurately achieve planned joint gaps with errors within clinically acceptable limits. Secondary hypotheses were that accuracy would be consistent across surgeons and patient factors including preoperative deformity, sex, BMI, and age. This study therefore investigates the accuracy of a pre-resection robotic balancing workflow in achieving planned joint gaps in a large multicenter cohort and evaluates the influence of surgeon and patient factors on joint gap accuracy.

Methods

Patients & study design

All primary TKAs performed between February 2024 and June 2026 with the ApolloKnee™ system (Corin Group, Cirencester, UK) at 4 hospitals were retrospectively reviewed. ApolloKnee™ is an imageless surgical navigation system which can work in conjunction with two surgical robots, a robotic bone mounted cutting guide, as well as a robotic ligament tensioner (BalanceBot). The inclusion criteria comprised up to the first 200 cases performed by surgeons who had completed more than 30 ApolloKnee cases, and cases in which femoral and tibial components were planned simultaneously using only pre-resection BalanceBot data (e.g. without reacquiring gap data after the tibial resection), and cases that included a final balance acquisition during femoral trialing. Two cases which were statistical outliers in terms of their preoperative sagittal alignment were excluded, one with > 20° of hyperextension and one with > 30° flexion contracture. This resulted in 672 cases from five surgeons (S1: 173, S2: 90, S3: 109, S4: 200, and S5: 100).

Sex and deformity data was available for all patients. The cohort was 54% female, with a mean ± standard deviation (SD) preoperative coronal alignment of 4 ± 5° varus [range: 13° valgus to 17° varus], and a preoperative sagittal alignment of 5 ± 6° flexion contracture [range: 16° hyperextension to 26° flexion contracture].

BMI and age were only available for 67% and 42% of patients, respectively, due to local privacy regulations pertaining to storing patient health information offshore. A sub-analysis of patients with available data demonstrated a mean ± SD age of 67 ± 8 years [range: 43 to 88 years], and BMI of 32 ± 6 kg/m2 [range: 19 to 51 kg/m2].

All data was automatically uploaded to CorinConnect from the internet-connected Apollo stations at each site and subsequently de-identified and transferred to the CorinRegistry for retrospective review (Ethics Approval/IRB: USA WCGIRB: 120190312, AUS Bellberry: 2020-08-764-A-1). This study received no funding.

Surgical technique

All knees were performed using a pre-resection ligament balancing workflow. All were performed with a medial parapatellar approach and using one of two implant systems (Apex: n = 460, or Unity: n = 212, both from Corin Group, Cirencester UK). Two surgeons preserved the PCL and used CR or Medial Congruent (MC) inserts. The other 3 sacrificed the PCL and used Ultra-congruent (UC) or MC inserts. Following mediolateral osteophyte removal and image-free registration of the femur and tibia, preoperative coronal and sagittal alignment (hip-knee-ankle [HKA] angle) was recorded with the knee held in maximum extension while suspended at the foot. A robotic ligament tensioner (BalanceBot, Corin Group, Cirencester, UK) was then pinned to the proximal tibia to acquire medial and lateral joint gap measurements, Fig. 1a. Measurements began with the knee positioned at 90° of flexion, the tibia supported at the ankle or foot, and maintained in neutral rotation. Once the display indicated the target load was achieved, the knee was slowly and carefully extended while the system continuously acquired the medial and lateral gaps as the knee moved from 90° flexion to 10° flexion (extension). Care was taken to avoid applying additional varus or valgus loads during acquisition. An applied distraction force of 70–100 N was used in both flexion and extension, according to surgeon preference, with a median [IQR] force across all surgeons of 70 [70–80] N in flexion and 90 [80–90] N in extension. The system highlighted when sufficient gap data was collected medially and laterally at each flexion angle. The pre-resection gap measurements were used to inform the pre-resection implant plan, with planned joint gaps updated according to the selected femoral and tibial component orientations, Fig. 1b. Femoral resections were then performed with either a bone mounted robotic cutting guide (n = 558) or a navigated cut block (n = 114) depending on surgeon preference. Tibial resections were either performed with the navigated cut block (n = 587) or the bone mounted robotic cutting guide (n = 85). After bony resections and any residual posterior osteophyte removal, final gap measurements were obtained with the femoral trial in place and the ligament tensioner in place of the tibial insert with the same loads applied. Planned and final joint gaps were measured as the distance from the tibial cut to the femoral component on the medial and lateral sides.

Data analysis

Patients were grouped based on preoperative coronal alignment as Valgus (≤ 2° valgus), Neutral (−2° valgus to 3° varus), or Varus (≥ 3° varus), similar to previous publications [6, 27]. Patients were also grouped based on preoperative sagittal alignment as hyperextension (Hyper; ≤−2°), Neutral (−2° to 5° flexion contracture), or flexion contracture (FC; ≥5° flexion contracture) similar to previous publications [16, 21]. A breakdown of the preoperative alignment is shown in Fig. 2. Also, the femoral and tibial recut rate was reported.

Distribution of cases by preoperative coronal and sagittal alignment. Dashed lines indicate group boundaries. Counts and percentages shown for each subgroup

Mean signed error (ME) and mean absolute error (MAE) of the predicted gaps (final minus planned) were calculated at 10°, 45°, and 90° flexion, and overall MAE and ME were calculated by pooling the data at all three flexion angles.

Associations between ME and preoperative coronal alignment, preoperative sagittal alignment, and sex were evaluated using multiple linear regression models stratified by flexion angle and ligament compartment. ME was modeled as the dependent variable, with preoperative coronal alignment, preoperative sagittal alignment, and sex included as predictors. A sub analysis was performed including BMI and age data where available. Regression coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals, and p-values were reported.

Joint gap MAE and ME were compared between preoperative alignment groups using Kruskal-Wallis tests and followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction. Statistical significance was set to p < 0.05. All analyses were performed in the R environment (Version 4.5.2) [24].

Results

Overall joint gap error

Overall, throughout flexion, MAE was 1.1 mm medially (and 1.1 mm at 10°, 45°, and 90°) and 1.3 mm laterally (1.1, 1.3, and 1.4 mm at 10°, 45°, and 90°), Table 1. Overall ME was 0.1 mm medially (0.1, 0.1, and 0.2 mm at 10°, 45°, and 90°) and − 0.5 mm laterally (−0.2, −0.5, and − 0.6 mm at 10°, 45°, and 90°), indicating relatively tighter than planned gaps laterally in midflexion and flexion, Table 1.

Regression analysis

Coronal alignment was associated with medial ME throughout flexion (β ranging from 0.037 to 0.072, p < 0.001), and negative lateral ME at 10° (β = −0.051, p < 0.001), see supplementary Table 3. Sagittal alignment was associated with medial ME at 10° and 45° (β ranging from − 0.025 to −0.023, p < 0.016), and lateral ME at all flexion angles (β ranging from − 0.023 to −0.032, p < 0.033). Sex was also associated with ME, with males demonstrating lower ME than females medially at 10°, 45°, and 90° and laterally at 45° and 90° (β ranging from − 0.332 to − 0.624, p < 0.001).

Sub-analysis including BMI and age

Including BMI and age did not substantially alter the relationships between preoperative alignment, sex, and ME. Coronal alignment remained significantly associated with medial ME at 10° and 45° flexion, however sagittal alignment and ME were no longer significantly associated, Table 4. Male sex remained associated with lower ME, particularly at 90° flexion.

BMI and age demonstrated limited associations with ME. Higher BMI was associated with slightly lower lateral ME at 90° (β = −0.059, p = 0.018), while increasing age was associated with slightly greater lateral ME at 10° (β = 0.027, p = 0.015), Table 4. Surgeon comparison

Surgeon-specific differences in gap measurements were generally small. At 10° of flexion (extension), some surgeons achieved slightly tighter or looser gaps, although all deviations were < 0.6 mm from the planned target and < 0.8 mm between surgeons, Table 2. At 90° (flexion), S1 consistently achieved tighter-than-planned gaps whereas S2 tended to achieve looser-than-planned gaps relative to the other surgeons. Despite these trends, all measurements remained within 1.4 mm of the plan. The largest inter-surgeon differences were observed between S1 and S2, reaching 1.7 mm medially and 2.0 mm laterally, Table 2. Pairwise comparisons identified several significant differences, particularly at 90° of flexion, indicating subtle but measurable inter-surgeon variability.

Discussion and conclusions

The most important finding of this study is that a novel technology capable of bicompartmental robotic assessment of joint gaps prior to bone resection accurately achieved its planned joint gaps with an overall MAE of 1.1 mm medially and 1.3 mm laterally, and ME within 0.5 mm. Among the evaluated factors, preoperative coronal alignment was the most consistent predictor of ME, particularly on the medial side, while male sex was associated with lower ME, especially in flexion. Although coronal alignment was significantly associated with ME, the magnitude of these effects was generally small. For example, each 1° change in coronal alignment corresponded to only a 0.04–0.07 mm change in ME, suggesting limited clinical relevance. Associations with sagittal alignment were statistically significant but comparatively smaller in magnitude than coronal alignment. FC and valgus knees had greater errors overall compared to other preoperative deformity groups, and errors tended to be higher laterally than medially. Furthermore, BMI was significantly negatively correlated with ME laterally in flexion. Finally, although significant differences were observed between surgeons, most were mostly small differences and not likely clinical meaningful. The results support the accuracy, reliability, and reproducibility of this workflow across varying patient anatomies and surgeon users.

Overall, the system was accurate to an MAE of 1.1 mm medially and 1.3 mm laterally. Minimal bias was observed, with ME within 0.1 mm medially and − 0.5 mm laterally. These results compare favorably with the previous version of the system which required a tibia-first workflow, where Shalhoub et al. reported a root mean squared error (RMSE) of 1.5 mm [25]. Because RMSE is typically greater than MAE for a given dataset, an RMSE of 1.5 mm is consistent with the MAE observed in this study, indicating broadly similar overall accuracy between the tibial-cut first and pre-resection workflows.

In contrast, manual gap assessment in TKA has demonstrated variable reproducibility, with conflicting results reported across studies [4, 5, 28, 31]. Although some studies have reported gap prediction accuracy within 1–2 mm using manual stress-based balancing techniques, these approaches are inherently limited by reliance on subjective surgeon-applied forces for assessment of final balance [5, 22]. Additional investigations have highlighted substantial variability in manual balancing. Elmasry et al. reported inter-surgeon differences of up to 16.5 Nm in applied torque and 5 mm in measured gaps [4]. Additional studies demonstrated surgeon-dependent distraction forces, systematic underestimating of flexion gaps compared with standardized applied loads [28], and poor prediction of mediolateral imbalance at higher flexion angles [19]. Furthermore, manual assessments have shown limited correlation with planned implant positioning, with reported deviations ranging from − 3.0° to + 3.0° and low agreement with ligament tensioner measurements (R² = 0.157) [31]. Collectively, these findings suggest that robotic and sensor-assisted techniques may provide more accurate and reproducible approaches for evaluating knee balance compared to manual methods.

Valgus knees were looser than planned laterally, particularly in extension. This is consistent with prior literature describing the inherent unpredictability with valgus knees [1]. With valgus knees it may be more difficult to predict how much the lateral joint space will open after bone resection and implant insertion, which directly impacts the accuracy of the plan. Furthermore, surgeons typically apply more restrictive resection and alignment boundaries for their valgus patients to avoid leaving the limb in valgus after TKA, which constrains their ability to correct imbalance through bone cuts alone [18]. As a result, surgeons typically plan valgus knees with a relatively tight lateral compartment, and subsequently rely on soft-tissue release, which may explain why the final lateral extension gap exceeds preoperative prediction [30].

We found that knees with FC > 10° did not markedly impact gap prediction accuracy in extension, with mean errors within 0.2 mm medially and − 0.5 mm laterally across the neutral and varus coronal deformity groups. This implies that the influence of posterior osteophytes may not pose as large of a concern to gap balance accuracy in extension as previously thought [13, 29]. This is an important finding because in a pre-resection balancing workflow, it is difficult to access the posterior regions of the knee before resections to remove osteophytes. The high accuracy observed in extension in FC knees may be attributed to the technique of targeting gaps at 10° flexion rather than 0° extension. This relaxes the posterior capsule and lessens the effect of capsule ‘tenting’ on the posterior osteophytes, while also avoiding the knee screw-home mechanism that occurs when the knee is brought into full extension [10, 20]. We did find however that a subset of 49 FC+Varus knees lacked pre-resection balance data at 10°, indicating that they could not be corrected to 10° pre-resection target and were balanced between 10–20° flexion. These more severe FC knees did report an error of 1.1 mm looser than planned gaps medially at 20°. It may be that osteophyte removal or posterior capsule release may play an important role when treating these more severe FC knees, and thus surgeons should anticipate this during gap planning.

In the neutral, varus and FC deformity groups, which represent 78% of our study cohort and the majority of the TKA population, accuracy was highest medially throughout flexion and laterally in extension, with ME within − 0.5 mm. Slightly larger errors were seen laterally in flexion in these groups however, with ME from − 0.8 to −0.9 mm, indicating tighter gaps post-resection (Fig. 1b). This lateral flexion gap tightening was a surprising finding as gaps typically loosen post-resection [10, 20]. This may be related to the difference in the paddle design between the pre-resection and post-resection configuration of the robotic tensioner. The pre-resection paddles utilize thin, flat surfaces of approximately one mm to allow insertion into the joint, while the post resection paddles have concave surfaces replicating the final tibial insert surface. It may be that some femoral rollback occurs laterally in flexion during the dynamic balance acquisitions, and the amount of rollback may be different for the two distinct surface topologies, ultimately impacting the gap measurement [15]. Moreover, a significant negative correlation was found between BMI and the lateral flexion gap, where the gap tightened by −0.59 mm per 10 kg/m2 increase in BMI. Increasing limb weight may therefore cause increasing differences in the gap kinematics between pre- and post-resection acquisitions. Finally, two out of the five surgeons had lateral flexion gap ME ≤−1.0 mm, suggesting surgeon related factors may also contribute to this error.

In the present study, males demonstrated lower ME compared with females, which may reflect differences in soft tissue behavior between sexes. Previous biomechanical studies have demonstrated that female knees exhibit greater laxity, reduced stiffness and higher energy loss compared with male knees [2, 23, 26]. Increased soft-tissue stiffness in males may limit compartment opening, resulting in smaller measured gaps under equivalent loading conditions, and consequently lower ME values. Conversely, greater energy loss in female knee soft-tissues may contribute to their knees opening-up during the procedure, resulting in greater ME compared to males. Therefore, sex-related differences in soft-tissue stiffness may partially explain the observed differences in ME between males and females.

The retrospective study design is a limitation, as it does not include a control group and limits the ability to standardize surgical technique across sites. Multiple knee implant designs were used in this study, as well as multiple osteotomy tools; however, errors were not assessed between implants or osteotomy tools. Given the low magnitude of errors observed across surgeons, differences between implants and osteotomy tools are not expected to substantially influence joint gap prediction accuracy. Ligament laxity phenotyping was not included but could be an area for future research. Recent studies highlight variability in individual knee laxity phenotypes, suggesting that preoperative laxity phenotyping may provide additional insight into joint gap accuracy and patient-specific balancing [7, 8, 11, 12]. BMI and age data were only available for a subset of patients due to regional privacy regulations, and this non-random availability may introduce selection bias when interpreting association with ME. Additionally, soft tissue releases performed between the initial and final gap assessments were not captured, which may have affected gap prediction accuracy. All participating surgeons had extensive experience with the previous generation of the system, and therefore the findings may not be generalized to new users. Furthermore, a learning curve analysis was not performed, however the inclusion of each surgeon’s initial cases provides a real-world, potentially conservative, estimate of the system’s accuracy. Lastly, due to the low number of recut cases, analyses evaluating the association between recut status and ME were not performed.

This study has shown that a new technology capable of bicompartmental robotic assessment prior to bony resections resulted in accurate final gaps across a wide range of patient factors and surgeons. This study supports the integration of predictive planning using a novel pre-bone resection robotic soft tissue assessment tool and provides a platform for cut order agnostic surgical protocols as a safe and effective means of optimizing soft tissue laxity.
