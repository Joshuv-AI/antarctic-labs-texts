Evaluation of modified essay questions (MEQs) as an assessment tool in third-year medical students’ modular summative assessment
Authors: Ayman Elsamanoudy, Mohamed Shehata, Amer Almarabheh, Zienab Alrefaie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AimWhether case-based modified essay questions (MEQs) are crucial to summative assessment in medical curriculum is still debatable. The current study aimed to evaluate third-year medical students’ performance in case-based MEQs and multiple-choice questions (MCQs) in summative assessment in the endocrine module.MethodsStudents’ scores in mid and final module MEQs and MCQs were analyzed over four successive years from 2018/2019 to 2021/2022, where comparisons were made between students’ scores in MEQs and MCQs, and between scores of students of different categories. Moreover, the correlation between MEQs and MCQ scores and total grades was evaluated.ResultsThe study revealed better students’ performance in MCQs compared to MEQs, and this pattern persisted over the study period reflecting that case-based MEQs were challenging to students. High-performing students got higher scores in MEQs than the low performers, denoting the ability of MEQs to discriminate between high and low grades. It was also observed that MEQs correlated significantly with students’ final grades similar to the MCQs indicating its importance as an assessment tool. Concerning the COVID year, students got higher grades in MCQs and lower scores in MEQs compared to other years of the study period which clarifies that MCQs offer a reliable assessment tool during exceptional circumstances.ConclusionImplementing case-based MEQs and MCQs in the summative assessment of medical curriculum favored discrimination between high and low scorers, minimized chances for students’ guessing and fostered deep learning and analytical comprehension of knowledge, and ensured medical students’ exposure to various item types. The current findings highlight the potential of using well constructed case-based MEQs in high stake medical exams together with MCQs for comprehensive assessments of problem solving skills.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06469-w.

Introduction

Effective learning requires active student engagement with all learning materials. It goes beyond simple knowledge acquisition to influencing learners’ behaviors and abilities [1]. Superficial learning, on the other hand, hinders the transfer of knowledge into practical application [2]. In contrast, deep learning promotes the purposeful application of knowledge to relevant situations [3].

Assessment is a central pillar in education; it measures knowledge and competencies’ obtainment, fosters learning, and offers feedback to learners and tutors. The testing effect suggests that testing within a specific context can enhance recall and retention of learned material. It helps consolidate memories and creates retrieval cues that link information to specific contexts [4].

The programmatic assessment reflects the purposeful use of various assessment tools in different educational contexts, including formative, diagnostic, and summative assessments during the learning cycle [5].

Despite the variety of assessment strategies used in medical education, written assessments remain widely used, including selected-response questions like multiple-choice questions (MCQs) and constructed-response questions like essays and modified essay questions (MEQs). MEQs require students to generate answers in response to specific triggers [6].

High-stakes assessments must achieve the overall utility of students’ assessment, including reliability, validity, and acceptability. Additionally, they should have a positive impact on education and learning. MCQs are generally believed to have accepted reliability. At the same time, constructed-response questions have better validity and are superior in testing higher cognitive levels. However, debate still exists about whether MCQs can assess high cognitive skills cost-effectively [7]. Another possible advantage of using selected-response questions (MCQs) in high-stakes exams over constructed-response ones is that it is evaluated by a single assessor [8].

MEQs are widely used in medical assessment. They consist of a clinical scenario followed by a set of successive questions requiring short answers. One method used to enhance the reliability of constructed-response questions is providing model answers with rubrics.

Deep learning was stimulated by case-based MEQs in biochemistry summative assessment in the study of Bansal et al. [9], and short essay questions showed higher discriminating power than MCQs that helped to differentiate high-achieving from low-achieving students [10]. A recent study indicated that MEQs can outperform MCQs in assessing level III problem-solving skills, as defined by Bloom’s taxonomy [11].

We observed consistent lower student performance in case-based MEQs compared to MCQs in the summative endocrine module assessments over the years. This observation led us to question whether carefully designed MEQs could effectively assess deeper levels of learning, such as interpretation and reasoning. To investigate this, we conducted a study comparing the performance of third-year medical students in MCQs and case-based MEQs in the summative assessment of the endocrine module over four consecutive years. This study aimed to address the comparative effectiveness of MEQs in discriminating performance levels and to understand their utility during the unique challenge presented by the COVID-19 shift to online exams.

Methods

This study is a longitudinal cross-sectional study that aimed to evaluate the MEQs as an assessment tool for students in the pre-clerkship phase. The study population included all third-year medical students who completed the endocrine module over four successive academic years (n = 471 for the year 2018/2019, n = 399 for the year 2019/2020, n = 416 for the year 2020/2021, and n = 351 for the academic year 2021–2022) in the Faculty of Medicine, King Abdulaziz University in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia.

Students’ scores in 4 exams (Mid-module MEQ, Mid-module MCQ, final MEQ, and final MCQ) over the study period were analyzed to achieve the study objectives.

To compare the students’ overall performance, they were classified into five categories based on their final module scores. The categories are as follows: the first category comprised students who scored less than 60%, the second from 60 to 69%, the third from 70 to 79%, the fourth from 80 to 89%, and the fifth category for students who scored 90% and above.

Assessment plan in the third year 5-credit endocrine module

The summative assessment included various assessment methods: mid-module MCQs, final MCQs, mid-module case-based MEQs, final MEQs, objective structured practical exam [OSPE], problem-based learning [PBL], and research assignment tasks. Marks allocated for each assessment are (15%, 37%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 3% and 10%) respectively. It should be noted that mark distribution, exam setting, and allocated time for each assessment method were unified throughout this study.

MCQs were prepared by the teaching faculty of each subject, ensuring that in each exam, at least 60% of the questions are new ones and that most questions include clinical vignettes/or are case-based.

Regarding the MEQs, the module examination committee prepares annually 5–6 case-based scenarios with 4–5 MEQs each. The committee sets the model answers and allocates marks for each question. The questions were formulated in a way that requires brief answers to eliminate subjectivity during exam correction (refer to the supplementary material).

MCQs and MEQs exam setting

The setting of the written MEQs is determined to be before the MCQs, so students don’t get clues from the MCQs testing.

Both mid and final MCQs were uploaded to the QuestionMark platform by the authorized member of the module committee enough time ahead of the assessments. Students took them through the same platform. While the case-based MEQs were handed to students half an hour before the MCQs test, students were given the allocated time, and papers were collected before access to the MCQs was allowed.

MCQ marks were obtained via the QuestionMark and copied to the grades’ matrix sheet, while the MEQs were corrected according to the model answer, and marks were recorded in the matrix. All remaining assessment marks were also added to the grades matrix to calculate students’ overall final grades.

Regarding the COVID year (2020), all assessments were distantly conducted via the BlackBoard platform. The mid-exam, including both MCQs and MEQs, ran without asking students to keep their cameras open, which was mandated in the final exam setting. In the post-COVID year (2021), exams were conducted on campus using the QuestionMark platform for MCQs testing with paper-based MEQ exams as previously used to be.

For this module, the passing mark is set at 60% of the total score. Medical students must achieve at least this percentage to pass the module, in line with the faculty’s grading policies. This criterion has been consistently applied across all assessments and academic years covered in the study.

The validity and reliability indices together with item analysis for each assessment tool are regularly taken care of by the assessment unit and reported to the module committee for analysis and interpretation. To close the assessment cycle, the module committee provides feedback to the assessment unit regarding any corrective actions taken.

Statistical analysis

The quantitative variables were presented as the mean and standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the mean performance of medical students in both the Multiple-Choice Questions MCQs and the Modified Essay Questions MEQs for different academic years. The post-hoc test using the Scheffe method was used to assess the significance of multiple comparisons across different academic years for both the MCQs and the MEQs. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to measure the relationship between the continuous variables. The Fisher’s z-transformed correlation was used to verify the significant difference between the two correlations. The Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS), version 29 (Chicago, IL, USA), and Graph Pad Prism, version 7, were used to analyze the collected data. Statistical significance was set at p-value < 0.05.

Results

The results revealed differences in the performance of 3rd -year medical students in the endocrine module final MCQs exams for the four academic years (70.64, 65.70, 77.60, and 72.34), respectively compared to the performance in the MEQs final exams (56.24, 54.31, 48.5, and 60.88) and the same pattern applied when comparing mid MCQs testing to mid MEQs one (Table 1). To verify if these differences are statistically significant, an analysis of variance test (ANOVA) indicated that there were statistically significant differences (P < 0.001) between the mean performance of medical students in both the MCQs and the MEQs (Fig. 1A & B). To assess the significance of multiple comparisons for both the MCQs and the case-based MEQs across different academic years, a post-hoc test using the Scheffe comparison method was conducted. The results indicated statistically significant differences in the mean performance of medical students in the final MCQ exam. Specifically, the performance of medical students in the COVID year 2020–2021 was significantly better than in the other academic years (2018/2019, 2019/2020, and 2021/2022). On the other hand, for the MEQ final exam, the opposite was observed where the performance of third-year medical students in the academic year 2020–2021 was significantly lower than in the other academic years of the study period (Table 1).

Discussion

The value of using constructed-response short essay questions in high-stakes medical exams is questioned by many educationalists for its subjectivity, feasibility, and high-cost effectiveness, although many others recommend it for its high validity and testing effect. If well-constructed, the case-based scenario with sequential modified short answer questions MEQs can resolve such debate by reducing subjectivity yet fostering deep learning and assessment of high cognitive skills.

The current study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of MEQs as an assessment tool in the pre-clerkship endocrine module. Our findings indicate that students consistently performed better in MCQs compared to MEQs, suggesting that MEQs may be perceived as more challenging. This disparity was particularly evident among average and below-average students, with a more pronounced performance gap between MCQ and MEQ scores. Interestingly, the COVID-19 pandemic did not seem to inflate MEQ scores, indicating that the observed differences were not solely due to external factors. While both MCQs and MEQs correlated significantly with the final grade, their strengths and weaknesses suggest that they should be used as complementary assessment methods to evaluate students’ cognitive abilities comprehensively.

The results of our study reveal a clear disparity in student performance between MCQs and case-based MEQs. Students consistently outperformed in MCQs compared to MEQs. In 2018, for example, the average score for mid-MCQs was 74.73%, significantly higher than the 48.11% average for mid-MEQs. This trend persisted across all four years of the study. Researchers have attributed the higher MCQ scores to factors such as familiarity, the possibility of guessing, and the prevalence of recall questions. Conversely, the potential for bias and subjectivity in MEQ marking may contribute to lower performance in this format [12,, 13].

Higher MCQ scores than MEQ scores can also be attributed to the nature of MCQs, which often rely on recognition rather than recall, as the answers and distractors are already presented to students. This fact makes MCQs easier for students to select the answers for the choices. MCQs also allow for a high degree of guessing, which can inflate scores, especially in questions assessing lower-order skills like memorization. This combination of recognition, guessing, and typically lower cognitive demands likely contributes to higher MCQ scores [14].

The well-constructed Modified Essay Question is considered more effective for assessing higher-order thinking because it requires students to engage deeply with the material. Moreover, MEQs enhance students to recall information without guessing, construct ideas, and articulate responses in their own words. By doing so, MEQs encourage critical thinking, application of knowledge, and problem-solving skills [15]. MEQs types of questions also encourage students to analyze situations, integrate multiple concepts, and apply theoretical knowledge to practical situations [16]. This could explain the low scores, especially from low-achiever students.

To ensure objectivity in our assessment process, the case-based scenarios and corresponding MEQs were developed by a team of expert question writers. Rigorous measures were implemented to minimize subjectivity, including limiting answer length, preparing a detailed answer key, and assigning specific marks to each component of the response as demonstrated in the supplementary material.

While the MCQs, many of which were also case-based, were designed to assess deeper learning and analytical skills, which was probably helped by the clinical nature of the endocrine module. Students consistently performed better on these assessments. This disparity may be attributed to several factors, including the potential for guessing chance that reaches 20–25%, and the challenges associated with constructing comprehensive and accurate responses to MEQs where students need to students generate the appropriate answer on their own based on analysis and evaluation of the given information. Additionally, the subjective nature of MEQ scoring, despite efforts to mitigate bias, could contribute to lower performance.

Furthermore, our observations highlight a recurring issue of spelling errors, particularly with medical terminology, in student responses to MEQs. While minor errors like (hyperthyroidism instead of hyperthyroidism) were not penalized, the importance of frequent writing assessments in medical education cannot be overstated.

Palmer et al., 2010 [8], reported that 50% of MCQs assessed higher cognition as opposed to only 25% of MEQs in the set of exit exams. The authors suggested that flaws in the writing of MEQs and its marking scheme are responsible for the failure of MEQs to achieve its purpose. Given the positive outcomes of our study and the emphasis on rigorous question development and frequent student practice through formative MEQ tests, we advocate for the increased use of case-based MEQs in summative medical assessments.

To assess the discriminatory power of MEQs, we examined the performance of high-performing and low-performing students. Our analysis revealed that higher-performing students (above 90%) achieved a significantly higher mean score of 74.81% in MEQs compared to lower-performing students (60–69%) who scored 49.56%. This suggests that MEQs can effectively differentiate between students of varying abilities, supporting our hypothesis.

The discriminative power of the essay questions, in general, is higher than that of the MCQs [10, 17]. They are better at assessing higher-level cognitive skills, as they require students to construct responses and apply knowledge to scenarios. MEQs are discriminative in performance, potentially providing more insights into student understanding and reasoning. These types of questions encourage deeper learning, pushing students to understand and integrate knowledge [18, 19]. These could justify why high-scoring students achieve better in MEQs than low-scoring students. Furthermore, students’ unfamiliarity and anxiety might have impacted their performance in MEQs, especially since very few courses and modules use case-based MEQs in their assessment plans in our institution.

This differentiation in MEQ scores is significant because it suggests that MEQs effectively distinguish student understanding ability [20]. MEQs require more comprehensive responses and experience [21], which might explain the broader spread of scores. High performers likely demonstrate a deeper understanding and better application of knowledge, reflected in their higher scores. Moreover, high-achieving students generally show high capabilities in these areas because their study habits focus on a deep understanding of the material through active recall, concept mapping, and applying knowledge to different contexts. These study strategies reinforce learning and prepare students to construct comprehensive, well-organized answers in MEQs [22]. All these skills are obvious in high achievers. Conversely, lower performers might struggle with the constructed-response nature and complexity of MEQs, leading to lower scores.

However, it’s worth noting that even the highest-performing students did not achieve perfect scores on MEQs, with an average score of 74.81% compared to 94.46% in MCQs. This suggests that MEQs pose a greater challenge and may be a more rigorous tool for assessing higher-order thinking skills. This increased difficulty can encourage students to develop a deeper understanding of the subject matter and enhance their analytical abilities [7].

The observed patterns and discrepancies in performance across the categories for MEQs suggest that they are an effective tool for differentiating between various levels of students’ understanding and ability [17]. They seem to challenge students more than MCQs, potentially leading to a more remarkable development of critical thinking and application skills [14]. However, this also means that they might be more stressful or challenging, and educators must ensure alignment of teaching methods with MEQs-style assessments and adequate preparation of students for this assessment type.

Conversely, the consistently higher scores in MCQs suggest that this format may be more reliable for assessing foundational knowledge and may be less intimidating for students. This could make MCQs a valuable tool for ensuring a minimum level of understanding across a wide range of topics [23].

Over the four academic years, the Pearson correlation values indicated a positive relationship between students’ final grades and their mid-MEQs (r = 0.644) and final MEQ scores (r = 0.655). This result suggests that MEQs could reasonably predict the student’s overall performance in the course. In contrast, the correlation between MCQ scores and final grades was generally higher. This correlation might reflect MCQs consistent and objective nature in evaluating students’ knowledge across a broad range of content [24]. The strong correlation indicates that MCQs are a reliable predictor of students’ final performance [25], possibly due to their ability to cover more content and assess a broad spectrum of the curriculum. On the other hand, in our assessment system, MCQs type of items is given more weight when compared to MEQs weight. This gives an additional reason for better correlation to the overall performance. Having both methods significantly correlated to the final score emphasizes the importance of considering the different methods of assessments for their complementary role. While MCQs generally offer a reliable measure across various contexts, MEQs can provide a deeper insight into students’ understanding and ability.

Important findings of the current study include the high correlation of MCQs’ scores with final grades during the pandemic year (2020–2021), suggesting that MCQs might be more resilient as an assessment tool in times of educational disruption. This finding could be explained by the high global achievement of the students during the COVID-19 lockdown and the educational disruption that might be due to the online exam implementation during COVID times [26]. This period presented an unprecedented scenario for educators and students, leading to unpredicted results on the efficacy of online versus traditional in-person exams. Several factors could contribute to these findings: The shift to online exams may have reduced the usual exam stress and allowed students to perform in a more comfortable environment [27], students might have had access to more resources than they typically would during in-person exams depending on the nature of the online assessments [28], and finally, students might have adapted their learning strategies to better suit the online format over time, leading to improved performance [29].

MCQs promoted fairness in varied home environments by requiring brief answers rather than lengthy responses to essays or MEQs, which can be challenging to complete amid distractions. So, MCQs type of questions minimized the influence of external factors on student performance, helping to reduce score variability. As mentioned above, MCQs enabled automated grading, providing quick feedback to students and reducing instructors’ grading burden during the transition to online education [30]. This could explain the higher correlation of MCQs scores with final grades during the COVID-19 year.

In the current study, some limitations are encountered. Firstly, the lack of longitudinal follow-up data prevents us from assessing the long-term impact of the assessment methods. Secondly, the findings are specific to the endocrine module, curriculum, and student population studied, limiting their generalized ability to other modules and academic contexts. Finally, while efforts were made to minimize subjectivity in scoring, it remains a potential limitation in MEQ assessments.

The COVID-19 pandemic introduced unique challenges to the assessment process, including the transition to remote testing. The lack of in-person supervision during online exams may have led to potential biases, such as increased opportunities for cheating or distractions. Additionally, disparities in access to technology and stable internet connections could have impacted student performance, particularly in MEQs that require focused attention and detailed responses. These factors may have influenced the results of the study and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Finally, a balanced approach to assessment is recommended, combining the strengths of both MEQs and MCQs. While MCQs can efficiently assess a broad range of knowledge, MEQs are better suited for evaluating higher-order thinking and application skills. To enhance the reliability and validity of MEQs, it is crucial to implement strategies such as standardizing marking schemes, training assessors, and using detailed rubrics to minimize subjectivity. Additionally, the challenges posed by online assessments during the COVID-19 pandemic highlight the need for innovative approaches to ensure the integrity and fairness of both MEQ and MCQ formats.

In conclusion, the study highlighted evident styles in student performance between Multiple Choice Questions (MCQs) and Modified Essay Questions (MEQs), with students generally scoring higher in MCQs. This observation could be attributed to MCQs’ structure and familiarity, enabling a broader curriculum assessment and less subjective marking. In contrast, although MEQs scored lower, were found to be discriminative in assessing higher cognitive skills and deeper understanding, particularly among higher-achieving students. The study noted a strong correlation between MCQ scores and final grades during the COVID-19 pandemic, suggesting MCQs’ resilience as an assessment tool in disrupted educational settings.

However, the consistently lower MEQ scores prompted concerns about student preparation and the need to encourage the use of MEQs-style assessments in other courses to familiarize students with it. The study also observed a clear stratification of MEQ scores across different performance categories, emphasizing its effectiveness in distinguishing student understanding and ability. It also indicated that while MCQs are generally more reliable in assessing a baseline of knowledge, MEQs provide deeper insights into students’ learning and skills.
