Linear Convergence of Diffusion Models Under the Manifold Hypothesis
Peter Potaptchik, Iskander Azangulov, George Deligiannidis

Abstract
Score-matching generative models have proven successful at sampling from complex high-dimensional data distributions. In many applications, this distribution is believed to concentrate on a much lower d d -dimensional manifold embedded into D D -dimensional space; this is known as the manifold hypothesis. The current best-known convergence guarantees are either linear in D D or polynomial (superlinear) in d d . The latter exploits a novel integration scheme for the backward SDE. We take the best of both worlds and show that the number of steps diffusion models require in order to converge in Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence is linear (up to logarithmic terms) in the intrinsic dimension d d . Moreover, we show that this linear dependency is sharp.



1
Introduction

Score-matching generative models
Ho et al. (2020)
;
Song et al. (2021)
such as diffusion models have become a leading paradigm for generative modeling. They achieve state-of-the-art results in many domains including audio/image/video synthesis
Evans et al. (2024)
;
Dhariwal and Nichol (2021)
;
Ho et al. (2022)
, molecular modeling
Watson et al. (2023)
, and recently text generation
Lou et al. (2024)
. Informally, diffusion models take samples from a distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, gradually corrupt them with Gaussian noise, and then learn to reverse this process. Once trained, a diffusion model can turn noise into new samples from the data distribution by iteratively applying the denoising procedure.

Due to the empirical success of diffusion models, there has been a push
Oko et al. (2023)
;
Wibisono et al. (2024)
;
Wu et al. (2024)
to better understand their theoretical properties, in particular, their convergence guarantees.

An important question is to determine the
iteration complexity
of diffusion models. In this paper, this refers to the number of steps diffusion models require in order to converge in Kullback-Leibler divergence to the original distribution regularized by a small amount of Gaussian noise.
Assuming only the existence of a second moment,
Benton et al. (2024)
prove that the iteration complexity is at most linear (up to logarithmic factors) in
D
.

While this result is tight in the general case, many real-world distributions actually have a low-dimensional structure. The assumption that a distribution lives on a
d
-dimensional manifold is called the
manifold hypothesis
, see e.g.
Bengio et al. (2013)
. This hypothesis has been supported by empirical evidence in many settings, e.g. image data, in which diffusion models are particularly successful. Therefore, the study of diffusion models under this assumption has garnered increased interest
Kadkhodaie et al. (2024)
;
Tang and Yang (2024)
;
Pidstrigach (2022)
.

Recently
Li and Yan (2024)
have shown that there is a special discretization design guaranteeing an iteration complexity of
d^{4}
in the intrinsic dimension
d
. The current
Azangulov et al. (2024)
best-known bound scales as
d^{3}
.

Our Contribution

In this work, we improve upon these results and show that the number of steps diffusion models require to converge in
KL divergence is
linear
(up to logarithmic terms) in the
intrinsic
dimension
d
. This is formalized in
10
. Additionally, we prove that the linear dependency is sharp.

The proof follows the structure of
Chen et al. (2023b)
and
Benton et al. (2024)
combined with a result from
Azangulov et al. (2024)
providing bounds on the score function depending only on the intrinsic dimension
d
. A key insight of our proof exploits the inherent martingale structure in diffusion processes. As we show, with the right SDE discretization, the corresponding error can be represented as a sum of easy-to-control martingale increments leading to a very concise argument.

We posit that this scaling is one of the major reasons why diffusion models are able to perform so well on tasks such as synthetic image generation. While the extrinsic dimension of image datasets is very large, e.g.
\approx 1.5\cdot 10^{5}
for ImageNet,
Pope et al. (2021)
estimate that the true intrinsic dimension is much lower, e.g. around
50
for ImageNet. Our result implies that the number of steps diffusion models need to sample scales as the latter rather than the former. This helps explain why diffusion models are able to generate crisp image samples with less than
1000
iterations
Ho et al. (2020)
. We note that this is not the discretization generally used in practice; however, a single-line modification to existing implementations would result in this discretization scheme.

In Section
2
, we give an overview of diffusion models, our assumptions and the discretization scheme that we use. We introduce our main result in Section
4
and give its proof in Section
5
. We conclude in Section
6
.

Concurrent work:

Two weeks after the first version of our work was made publicly available, an independent work appeared by
Huang et al. (2024)
who derive similar bounds. In addition, they relax some of our assumption on the support and boundedness of the measure.

Preliminaries

2.1
Diffusion Models

Suppose we want to generate samples from a distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Diffusion models solve this by first specifying a forward noising process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
up to some time
T
. This process is defined as the evolution of data
X_{0}\sim\mu
according to an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) SDE

\begin{cases}dX_{t}=-X_{t}dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t},&t\in(0,T]\\
X_{0}\sim\mu,\end{cases}

where
\{B_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is a Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Letting
c_{t}:=\exp(-t)
and
\sigma^{2}_{t}:=1-\exp(-2t)
, we note that
X_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle dist.}}{{=}}c_{t}X_{0}+\sigma_{t}Z_{D}
where
Z_{D}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Id_{D})
. We use
p_{t}
to denote the marginal density of
X_{t}
.

The reverse process
\{Y_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}:=\{X_{T-t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
, under mild assumptions
Anderson (1982)
, satisfies

\begin{cases}dY_{t}=(Y_{t}+2\nabla\log p_{T-t}(Y_{t}))dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t}^{\prime},&t\in(0,T]\\
Y_{0}\sim p_{T},\end{cases}

(1)

where
\{B_{t}^{\prime}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is another Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. By generating samples
Y_{0}\sim p_{T}
and then simulating (
1
) up to time
T
, we can obtain samples
Y_{T}\sim\mu
from the data distribution. The main idea behind diffusion models is to simulate these dynamics approximately since neither
p_{T}
nor the score function
s_{t}:=\nabla\log p_{t}
are known.

In practice, we solve these problems by building an approximate process
\hat{Y}_{t}
. Due to the exponential convergence of the OU process to a standard normal distribution, for a sufficiently large
T
, we have that
p_{T}\approx\mathcal{N}(0,\Id_{D})
. So, we initialize as
\hat{Y}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Id_{D})
. Second, we learn a score approximation
\hat{s}_{t}\approx s_{t}
which is used instead of the true score function. The approximation
\hat{s}_{t}
is usually parameterized by neural networks and trained via a denoising score-matching objective.

Finally, we note that the SDE (
1
) cannot be simulated exactly and instead a discretization scheme must be introduced. More precisely, first, in order to ensure numerical stability, an early stopping time
\delta>0
is chosen. Next, the interval
[0,T-\delta]
is divided into
K
time steps
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{K}=T-\delta
. The final discretization is given by

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\hat{Y}_{t_{k+1}}=\alpha_{k}\hat{Y}_{t_{k}}+\beta_{k}\hat{s}_{T-t_{k}}(\hat{Y}_{t_{k}})+\eta_{k}Z_{k},&0\leq k<K\\
\hat{Y}_{t_{0}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\Id_{D}\right),\end{cases}

(2)

where
Z_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\Id_{D})
and
\alpha_{k},\beta_{k},\eta_{k}
are real numbers. The discretization schedule
t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{K}
and coefficients
\alpha_{k},\beta_{k},\eta_{k}
are hyper-parameters specifying the design of the sampling procedure. We discuss the choice of the discretization coefficients and their importance in Section
3
, and we specify the discretization schedule
\{t_{k}\}
that gives a linear in
d
convergence rate in Section
4
.

2.2
Assumptions and Notation

Throughout the paper, we assume that the distribution
\mu
satisfies the manifold hypothesis which we state more formally in the following.

1
.

\mu
is supported on a smooth, compact,
d
-dimensional,
\beta\geq 2
-smooth manifold
M
embedded into
\mathbb{R}^{D}
.

We also make the following assumption for ease of presentation. The general case is handled by rescaling and shifting.

2
.

We assume
\diam M\leq 1
and
0\in M
.

Without any restrictions on the manifold
M
, we cannot hope to obtain bounds independent of the ambient dimension
D
. As an intuitive counter-example, consider
D
-dimensional Hilbert curves
M_{n}
, see Figure
1(a)
. In the limit, these curves cover the entire
D
dimensional cube
M_{\infty}:=[0,1]^{D}
. Moreover, any measure on
M_{\infty}
can be seen as a weak limit of measures on
M_{n}
. This makes sampling from
M_{n}
(for large enough
n
) as hard as sampling from
M_{\infty}
, which according to
(
Benton et al., 2024
, Appendix H)
scales as at least
D
.

Therefore, we should place additional assumptions on the complexity of
\mu
and
M
in order to avoid such pathological cases.
Informally (see details in Appendix
A
), the complexity of
M
depends both on its global (volume) and local (smoothness) properties. We control its smoothness by introducing a scale
r>0
at which
M
is locally flat.
To control the measure
\mu
, we assume that it has a density
p_{0}(dy)
(w.r.t. the standard volume form
dy
) bounded from above and below. We assume logarithmic control over the discussed quantities.

3
.

There is a constant
C>0
such that
\Vol M\leq e^{dC}
,
r>e^{-C}
, and
e^{-dC}\leq p_{0}\leq e^{dC}
.

Note that we impose different assumptions on
r
and
\Vol M
, this follows from a relation between the radius and volume of the
d
-dimensional sphere, i.e.
{\Vol B_{d}(0,r)\propto r^{d}}
.

4
.

Informally, on scales larger than
r
, the manifold
M
is not flat anymore. So, if
M
is corrupted by noise of magnitude greater than
r
, over-smoothing may destroy the geometric structure, see Figure
1(b)
.
Diffusion models stopped at time
\delta
add Gaussian noise proportional to
\sqrt{\delta}
.
To capture the shape of
M
the stopping time should be chosen to satisfy
\sqrt{\delta}\lesssim r
. Thus we should expect
\log\delta^{-1}\geq C
.

Recall that
\delta
denotes the early stopping time, and
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{K}=T-\delta
are the
K
discretization time steps. Let
\gamma_{k}:=t_{k+1}-t_{k}
be the
k
-th step size. We control the score estimation error of
\hat{s}_{t}
as follows.

5
.

The score network
\hat{s}_{t}(x)
satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\gamma_{k}\@Enonstarred\|s_{T-t_{k}}(X_{T-t_{k}})-\hat{s}_{T-t_{k}}(X_{T-t_{k}})\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{score}^{2}.

3
Discretization Scheme

In this section, we describe a discretization scheme that allows for polynomial convergence in the intrinsic dimension
d
.
The most popular discretization scheme (
2
) is the so called
exponential integrator
. This involves a naive approximation of (
1
) by

\begin{cases}d\hat{Y}_{t}=(\hat{Y}_{t}+2\nabla\hat{s}_{T-{t_{k}}}(\hat{Y}_{t_{k}}))dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t}^{\prime},&t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})\\
\hat{Y}_{0}\sim p_{T},\end{cases}

(3)

where the score part of the drift is made piecewise-constant. The discretization coefficients
\alpha_{k},\beta_{k},\eta_{k}
are then chosen so that (
2
) corresponds to solving (
3
) exactly.

However, as pointed out in
Li and Yan (2024)
, the iteration complexity of the exponential integrator grows with
D
, and, actually, there is a unique setting of the discretization coefficients in (
2
) that yields an iteration complexity independent of
D
. It is given by

\begin{cases}\hat{Y}_{t_{k+1}}=c^{-1}_{\gamma_{k}}\hat{Y}_{t_{k}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\gamma_{k}}}{c_{\gamma_{k}}}\hat{s}_{T-t_{k}}(\hat{Y}_{t_{k}})+\sigma_{\gamma_{k}}\frac{\sigma_{T-t_{k+1}}}{\sigma_{T-t_{k}}}Z_{k},&Z_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\Id_{D})\\
\hat{Y}_{t_{0}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\Id_{D}\right).\end{cases}

(4)

6
.

This discretization scheme corresponds to the DDPM update rule
Ho et al. (2020)
.

In Appendix
B
, we provide a simple argument that derives (
4
) as a correction of (
2
). We summarize it below. We recall Tweedie’s formula
Robbins (1956)
stating that

s_{t}(x)=\frac{c_{t}\mathbb{E}[X_{0}|X_{t}=x]-x}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}=-\frac{\mathbb{E}[Z_{D}|X_{t}=x]}{\sigma_{t}},

(5)

for
X_{t}=c_{t}X_{0}+\sigma_{t}Z_{D}
, where
X_{0}\sim\mu
and
Z\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\Id_{D}\right)
are independent. We recall
(
Azangulov et al., 2024
, Theorem 15)
, which gives the following.

7
.

Let
\mu
be a measure satisfying Assumptions
1
–
3
. Let
t>0
and let
X_{t}=c_{t}X_{0}+\sigma_{t}Z_{D}
, where
X_{0}\sim\mu
and
Z_{D}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\Id_{D}\right)
are independent. Then with probability at least
1-\delta

\|\sigma_{t}s(t,X_{t})+Z_{D}\|\lesssim\sqrt{d\left(C+\max\{\log(c_{t}/\sigma_{t}),0\}\right)+\log\delta^{-1}}.

In particular, substituting (
5
) into Theorem
7
and using that
\diam M\leq 1

\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbb{E}[X_{0}|X_{t}]-X_{0}\|^{2}]\lesssim d\left(C+\max\{\log(c_{t}/\sigma_{t}),0\}\right).

Thus, we propose to approximate the score
s_{t}(x_{t})
for
t<t^{\prime}
by the following

s_{t}(x_{t}|t^{\prime},x_{t^{\prime}}):=\frac{c_{t}\mathbb{E}[X_{0}|X_{t^{\prime}}=x_{t^{\prime}}]-x_{t}}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}=c^{-1}_{t^{\prime}-t}\frac{\sigma^{2}_{t^{\prime}}}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}s_{t^{\prime}}(x_{t^{\prime}})-\frac{x_{t}-c^{-1}_{t^{\prime}-t}x_{t^{\prime}}}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}.

(6)

By Theorem
7
, this approximation induces an error that only scales with the intrinsic dimension
d
. We analogously define
\hat{s}_{t}(x_{t}|t^{\prime},x_{t^{\prime}})
to be a correction of the score estimate
\hat{s}_{t}
obtained by substituting
\hat{s}_{t^{\prime}}(x_{t^{\prime}})
instead of
s_{t^{\prime}}(x_{t^{\prime}})
into (
6
).

8
.

The scheme (
4
) can be obtained by solving the following continuous-time dynamics

\begin{cases}d\hat{Y}_{t}=\left[\hat{Y}_{t}+2\hat{s}_{T-t}(\hat{Y}_{t}|T-t_{k},\hat{Y}_{t_{k}})\right]dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t}^{\prime},&t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})\\
\hat{Y}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\Id_{D}\right).\end{cases}

(7)

This can be proven by integrating the linear SDE (
7
). See (
11
) and then a discussion in Remark
17
for additional properties of this discretization scheme.

Main Results

Before stating our main result let us introduce the discretization schedule, first introduced in
Benton et al. (2024)
which we use in what follows.

9
.

Fixing integers
L<K
, and choose
T:=\kappa L+1
and
\delta:=(1+\kappa)^{L-K}
, one can take the uniform partition
t_{k}=\kappa k,k<L
of
[0,T-1]
and the exponential partition

t_{L+m}=T-(1+\kappa)^{-m}
of
[T-1,T-\delta]
for
0\leq m\leq K-L
.

The main result of our paper is the following.

10
.

Let
\mu
be a measure satisfying assumptions
1
–
3
and let
\hat{s}_{t}
be a score approximation satisfying Assumption
5
with
\{t_{k}\}_{k\leq K}
satisfying definition
9
. Then the process
\hat{Y}_{t}
following (
4
) satisfies

\displaystyle\KL(\hat{Y}_{T-\delta}\|X_{\delta})\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}_{score}+De^{-2T}+\kappa+d\kappa^{2}(K-L)\left)(\log\delta^{-1}+C\right),

(8)

where we use
\KL(X\|Y)
to denote the
\KL
-divergence between the laws of
X
and
Y
.

This bound consists of four terms: (i)
\varepsilon^{2}_{score}
corresponding to the error in score approximation; (ii)
De^{-2T}
corresponding to the initialization error; (iii)
\kappa
corresponding to the discretization error for
t\in[0,T-1]
; (iv)
d\kappa^{2}(K-L)(\log\delta^{-1}+C)
corresponding to the discretization error for
t\in[T-1,T-\delta]
. By choosing
K
to be sufficiently large, we obtain a linear (up to logarithmic factors) in
d
bound on the iteration complexity in the following.

11
.

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
10
, for a given
\kappa<1/4
and tolerance
\varepsilon>0
, choosing
L\simeq\kappa^{-1}\left(\log D+\log\varepsilon^{-1}\right)
and
K-L\simeq\kappa^{-1}\log\delta^{-1}
, diffusion models with
K
denoising steps achieve an error bounded as

\KL(\hat{Y}_{T-\delta}\|X_{\delta})\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}_{score}+\frac{d(\log\delta^{-1}+\log\varepsilon^{-1}+\log D)(\log\delta^{-1}+C)\log\delta^{-1}}{K}.

Tightness of linear bound.

In the best case, a diffusion model learns the score function exactly and is initialized at the true initial distribution.
Let
\pi
be a compactly supported distribution on
\mathbb{R}
such that a diffusion model with a given discretization scheme and perfect score/initialization achieves an
\bar{\varepsilon}
error in
\KL
.

Consider the product measure
\pi^{\otimes d}\otimes\left(\delta_{0}\right)^{\otimes(D-d)}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{D-d}
. Note that its score function is

(s_{t}(x_{1}),\ldots,s_{t}(x_{d}),-\sigma^{-2}_{t}x_{d+1},\ldots,-\sigma^{-2}_{t}x_{D})
. So, by the tensorization of
\KL
, assuming perfect score estimation and initialization for
\pi^{\otimes d}\otimes\left(\delta_{0}\right)^{\otimes(D-d)}
, the same discretization scheme as above will result in a
d\bar{\varepsilon}
error. Therefore a linear dependence in
d
is optimal.

Proof of Theorem
10

We defer the proofs of lemmas to Appendix
C
. The main idea of the proof of Theorem
10
is to exploit the martingale structure of
\@Enonstarred[X_{0}|X_{t}]
, which we now formalize. We introduce the functions
m_{t}(x):=\@Enonstarred[X_{0}|X_{t}=x]
and define a stochastic process
\{m_{t}(X_{t})\}_{t\in[0,T]}
. Note that
m_{t}(X_{t})=\@Enonstarred[X_{0}|X_{t}]
. The next result states that this process is a martingale.

12
.

Define the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma(X_{s}:s\geq t)
. The process
\{m_{t}(X_{t})\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is a martingale w.r.t.
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
. In particular, for
t<t^{\prime}

\displaystyle\@Enonstarred[m_{t}(X_{t})|X_{t^{\prime}}]=m_{t^{\prime}}(X_{t^{\prime}}).

(9)

By Assumption
1
,
X_{0}
is a.s. bounded. Therefore the process
\{m_{t}(X_{t})\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is square integrable. So, the following lemma on the orthogonality of martingale increments can be applied.

13
.

Let
\{M_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be a square integrable martingale in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
w.r.t. a filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
. Then for any
t_{1}<t_{2}<t_{3}
we have

\displaystyle\@Enonstarred\|M_{t_{3}}-M_{t_{1}}\|^{2}=\@Enonstarred\|M_{t_{3}}-M_{t_{2}}\|^{2}+\@Enonstarred\|M_{t_{2}}-M_{t_{1}}\|^{2}.

We are now ready to present the proof of Theorem
10
. Our first steps coincide with
Chen et al. (2023a)
;
Benton et al. (2024)
. We begin by decoupling the errors coming from score estimation, approximate initialization and SDE discretization. This is formalized by the following lemma.

14
.

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
10

\displaystyle\KL(\hat{Y}_{T-\delta}\|X_{\delta})\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}_{score}+De^{-2T}+\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\int^{t_{k+1}}_{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}\|s_{T-t}(X_{T-t}\mid T-t_{k},X_{T-t_{k}})-s_{T-t}(X_{T-t})\|^{2}dt.

(10)

Note that we get we get the first and second terms of the desired bound in (
8
) plus the discretization error of the ideal score approximation. So, it is sufficient to bound this latter sum by
\kappa+d\kappa^{2}(K-L)(\log\delta^{-1}+C)

The next observation is that (
5

\displaystyle s_{t}(x|t^{\prime},x^{\prime})-s(t,x)=\frac{c_{t}}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}\left(\mathbb{E}[X_{0}|X_{t}=x]-\mathbb{E}[X_{0}|X_{t^{\prime}}=x^{\prime}]\right)=\frac{c_{t}}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}\left(m_{t}(x)-m_{t^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})\right).

Applying Lemma

For

We return to explaining the nature of first-order correction in (

Benton et al. (2024)
also leverage martingale properties of the score function. They note that

is a martingale. However, since they consider a standard exponential integrator scheme, the discretization error which is given by the difference in drifts

and the score term

Conclusion & Future Work

In this work, we studied the iteration complexity of diffusion models under the manifold hypothesis. Assuming that the data is supported on a
d
-dimensional manifold, we proved the first linear in
d
iteration complexity bound w.r.t.
\KL

This result is equivalent to an

convergence rate in

where

Appendix A
Elements of Manifold Learning

We follow
(
Azangulov et al., 2024
, Section 2.2)
in defining the class of regular smooth manifolds of interest. We give only the bare minimum details required to describe it. For a more comprehensive discussion, see
Divol (2022)
.

We recall that a
d
-dimensional manifold
Lee (2013)
is a topological space
M
that is locally isomorphic to an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In other words, for each
y\in M
, there is an open set
y\in U_{y}\subseteq M
and a continuous function
\Phi_{y}:U_{y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\Phi_{y}
is a homeomorphism onto its image. The smoothness of the manifold is defined as the smoothness of the functions
\Phi_{y}
.

When a manifold
M
is embedded into
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, a key quantity
Federer (1959)
used to control the regularity of the embedding is called the
reach

\tau=\tau(M)
and is defined as

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle:=\sup\{\varepsilon:\forall x\in M^{\varepsilon},\,\,\,\,\exists!\,y\in M\,\text{ s.t. }\dist(x,M)=\|x-y\|\},

where
M^{\varepsilon}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}:\dist(x,M)<\varepsilon\}
is the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
M
.
Equivalently,
\tau
is the supremum over the radii of neighborhoods of
M
for which the projection is unique. The reach controls
Divol (2022)
both the global and local properties of the manifold.

In particular, the reach controls the scale at which
M
admits a natural smooth parameterization
\Phi_{y}
which will be used to control the smoothness of the manifold. More precisely, for a point
y\in M
, let
\pi_{y}:=\pi_{T_{y}M}
be the orthogonal projection onto the tangent space
T_{y}M\simeq\mathbb{R}^{d}
at
y
. Then
Aamari and Levrard (2018)
the restriction of
\pi_{y}
to
M\cap B_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}(y,\tau/4)
is one-to-one and
B_{T_{y}M}(0,\tau/8)\subseteq\pi_{y}(M\cap B_{D}(y,\tau/4))
. Defining
\Phi_{y}
as the inverse of
\pi_{y}\big|_{M\cap B_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}(y,\tau/4)}
, we have constructed a local parameterization
\Phi_{y}:B_{T_{y}M}(0,\tau/8)\rightarrow M
of the manifold
M
at the point
y
.

We assume that the
\Phi_{y}
are in
C^{2}(B_{T_{y}M}(0,\tau/8))
, and define
L:=L(M):=\sup_{y}\|\Phi_{y}\|_{C^{2}(B_{T_{y}M}(0,\tau/8))}
. From a geometric perspective, this allows us to compare tangent vectors at different points by applying parallel transport which is defined in terms of a second-order differential operator. Finally, we define
r
in Assumption
3
as
r:=\min\left(\tau,L\right)/8
.

\|X_{0}-m_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\leq\int_{M}\|X_{0}-y\|^{2}\mu_{0|t}(dy|X_{t}).

As shown in
(
Azangulov et al., 2024
, Theorem 15)
,
if

r(t,\delta,\eta)=2(\sigma_{t}/c_{t})\sqrt{20d\left(\log_{+}(\sigma_{t}/c_{t})^{-1}+4C\right)+8\log\delta^{-1}+\log\eta^{-1}},

then with probability at least
1-\delta

\int_{y\in M:\|X_{0}-y\|\leq r(t,\delta,\eta)}\mu_{0|t}(dy|X_{t})\geq 1-\eta.

Integrating
\|X_{0}-y\|^{2}
w.r.t.
\mu_{0|t}(dy|X_{t})
we get that with probability at least
{1-\delta}

\|X_{0}-m_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\leq\int_{M}\|X_{0}-y\|^{2}\mu_{0|t}(dy|X_{t})\leq r^{2}(t,\delta,\eta)+\eta.

Taking an expectation w.r.t. both
X_{0}
and
X_{t}
we get

\mathbb{E}{\|X_{0}-m_{t}(X_{t})\|}^{2}\leq r^{2}(t,\delta,\eta)+\eta+\delta.

Choosing
\delta=\eta=\min\left(1,(\sigma_{t}/c_{t})^{2}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\|X_{0}-m_{t}(X_{t})\|}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4(\sigma_{t}/c_{t})^{2}\left(20d\left(\log_{+}(\sigma_{t}/c_{t})^{-1}+4C\right)+18\log_{+}(\sigma_{t}/c_{t})^{-1}\right).

Therefore

\mathbb{E}{\|X_{0}-m_{t}(X_{t})\|}^{2}\lesssim(\sigma_{t}/c_{t})^{2}d\left(\log_{+}(\sigma_{t}/c_{t})^{-1}+C\right).

As we mentioned, we can limit ourselves to the case
t\leq 1
. For
t\in[\delta,1]
, we have
(\sigma_{t}/c_{t})^{2}\simeq t
and
\log_{+}(\sigma_{t}/c_{t})^{-1}\lesssim\log\delta^{-1}
. This shows that

\mathbb{E}{\|X_{0}-m_{t}(X_{t})\|}^{2}\lesssim td\left(\log\delta^{-1}+C\right).

∎
