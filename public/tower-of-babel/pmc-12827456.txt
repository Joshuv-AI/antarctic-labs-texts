Biosorption potential and plant growth promoting activities of zinc tolerant novel Chitinophaga niastensis HMR31 from zinc-infested zone
Authors: Ali Asger Bhojiya, Harshada Joshi, Sudhir K Upadhyay, Devendra Jain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeavy metal contamination in soil poses serious threats to agriculture, human health, and ecosystems. Bacterial biosorption has emerged as a sustainable, cost-effective, and eco-friendly approach for mitigating metal pollution. In this study, bacterial strain HMR31 was characterized through a polyphasic approach and evaluated for its ability to biosorb Zn(II), Fe(II), Pb(II), Cd(II), and Cr(III). Bacterial Zn accumulation was confirmed by AAS, SEM-EDX, and TEM analyses. The optimal pH, temperature, contact time, initial metal concentration, and biomass dose for heavy metal biosorption were determined. Phenotypic, chemotaxonomic (major fatty acids: iso-C15:0 and C16:1 5c; major hydroxy fatty acid: iso-C17:0 3-OH), and phylogenetic analyses of the 16 S rRNA gene identified the isolate as Chitinophaga niastensis HMR31. The biosorption efficiency followed the order Zn(II) > Fe(II) > Pb(II) > Cd(II) > Cr(III). Optimal biosorption was achieved at pH 7.0, 35 °C, 60 min contact time, 50 mg/L initial metal concentration, and 1.5 mg/mL biomass dose. The strain also exhibited multiple plant growth-promoting traits, including IAA, GA3, NH3, HCN, siderophore, ACC deaminase, and phosphate solubilization activity. In a pot experiment with Vigna radiata under zinc stress, inoculation with HMR31 alleviated toxicity and enhanced growth parameters. Thus, Chitinophaga niastensis HMR31 can be treated as a potential candidate for a concerted method in bioremediation to convert metal-contaminated sites to productive land.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32802-4.Keywords: Chitinophaga, Phenotypic, Chemotaxonomic, Phylogenetic, Heavy metal, Biosorption, Plant growth promotersSubject terms: Biotechnology, Environmental sciences, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Heavy metals are elements that occur naturally in the Earth’s crust. Human activities, including mining, industrial processes, agriculture, and inappropriate waste management, have drastically increased their global ecological impact. Lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), mercury (Hg), arsenic (As), chromium (Cr), zinc (Zn), and other metals are among them1. Soil pollution by heavy metals constitutes a significant environmental concern that occurs when the concentration of certain metallic elements in the soil exceeds safe levels, leading to adverse effects on ecosystems, human health, and plant growth2. Several methods are employed to address and prevent heavy metal pollution of soil, including chemical remediation, soil amendments, waste management, and microbial bioremediation3. Among all the remediation and prevention methods, using heavy metal-tolerant bacteria for bioremediation has several advantages. Compared to traditional methods, it is usually more cost-effective and environmentally friendly.

Certain microorganisms, known as heavy metal-tolerant bacteria, possess specialized defenses and survival mechanisms allowing them to thrive in environments with high levels of heavy metals, which are lethal to most organisms4. These bacteria have developed a remarkable ability to adapt to extreme conditions and have become of significant interest in environmental and biotechnological applications. They have developed a range of mechanisms to cope with metal toxicity. These mechanisms include metal efflux pumps to remove metals from their cells, metal-binding proteins, and enzymes that can detoxify or immobilize heavy metals5. Because of their potential in bioremediation, the practice of employing living organisms to clean up polluted environments, heavy metal-tolerant bacteria have drawn interest. Heavy metals can be extracted from contaminated soil, water, and sediments using these bacteria. In bioremediation, these bacteria facilitate the immobilization and transformation of toxic metals into less harmful forms6. Through cooperative metabolic processes and synergistic interactions, bacterial consortia, particularly native strains, are highly successful in enhancing heavy metal tolerance and reduction7–9. Considering their distinctive capabilities, metal-tolerant bacteria require more accurate techniques for isolation and characterization in order to be used in bioremediation and biotechnological applications, which are aimed at addressing the environmental and health issues associated with heavy metal contamination.

Polyphasic characterization of bacteria is an approach used in microbiology to identify and classify bacterial species by considering multiple characteristics and techniques. This method takes into account various aspects of bacterial biology, including their genetic, phenotypic, and ecological attributes, in order to obtain a more comprehensive and accurate understanding of a bacterial species10. Genetic techniques, such as DNA sequencing and DNA-DNA hybridization, play a crucial role in polyphasic characterization11. Bacteria can be characterized based on their observable characteristics, including their shape, size, color, growth requirements, and metabolic capabilities. Bacterial species exhibit different metabolic pathways and biochemical reactions. Identification of species can be aided by knowledge of the metabolic potential of bacteria, such as their capacity to ferment particular sugars or use particular substrates12. Establishing the evolutionary relationships between bacterial species is a fundamental part of polyphasic characterization. Fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) analysis is a phenotypic method for characterizing bacteria that are unresponsive in standard biochemical identification assays13. FAME can be employed to compare fatty acid composition in membranes among bacterial strains and species.

The biosorption potential of heavy metal-tolerant bacteria is a fascinating and environmentally significant aspect of bioremediation and metal pollution mitigation14. This approach harnesses the unique abilities of certain bacteria to remove and sequester heavy metals from contaminated environments. Metal-tolerant bacteria employ various mechanisms for biosorption, including adsorption onto the cell surface, intracellular accumulation, precipitation, and chelation, and EPS production15,16. The biosorption potential of heavy metal-tolerant bacteria has found applications in diverse fields, including wastewater treatment, bioremediation of contaminated sites, and the recovery of valuable metals from industrial waste streams17–19. The biosorption potential of heavy metal-tolerant bacteria represents an ecologically friendly and sustainable way to deal with the critical issue of heavy metal pollution.

By mitigating the detrimental impacts of heavy metals in the soil, bacteria that are tolerant of heavy metals serve as a crucial means for promoting plant growth in areas affected by these metals. Alongside the mechanism of resistance to heavy metals, several bacteria produce chemical substances such as indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), siderophores, and phosphate-solubilizing enzymes, which enhance nutrient uptake and improve plant resilience under stress conditions20. By creating a more favorable rhizosphere environment, heavy metal-tolerant bacteria not only help plants survive in contaminated soils but also contribute to phytoremediation efforts by facilitating the establishment and growth of vegetation in degraded ecosystems. The aim of this study is to evaluate the biosorption potential of heavy metals and plant growth-promoting activities of a zinc-tolerant novel isolate, Chitinophaga niastensis HMR31, obtained from a zinc-infested zone.

Materials and methods

Sampling site, soil sample collection, and its physicochemical analysis

The study area for the present investigation is Zawar, Udaipur, Rajasthan (India). Zawar (24° 21′ N; 73° 41′E), an ancient zinc smelting site, is situated about 40 km southeast of Udaipur, which was contaminated by heavy metals (Supplementary Fig. 1). Samples of soil were collected from different areas of the tailings of Zawar. Before being processed in the lab, the soil samples were preserved in a sterilised plastic bag and kept in the refrigerator to ensure low biological activity. The soil samples were air-dried, sieved, and kept for analysis. The electrical conductivity, pH, and organic carbon were analyzed, and quantitative analysis of heavy metals in soil samples was carried out using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer.

Isolation of heavy metal-tolerant bacteria

Using the standard pour plate method, bacteria tolerant to heavy metals were isolated on nutrient agar enriched with zinc sulphate heptahydrate (1 mM). The petriplates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Isolated bacteria were further screened for high tolerance on nutrient agar incorporated with 5 mM ZnSO4.7H2O. The isolate showing the highest tolerance level was selected for further analysis. Subsequently, this selected isolate was subjected to characterization, evaluation of biosorption efficiency, and plant growth-promoting (PGP) traits. This two-step selection approach was adopted to focus on isolates with superior metal tolerance before assessing their PGP potential.

Phenotypic characterization of the bacteria

Morphological and biochemical characterization

The size, colour, form (circular, filamentous, and irregular), elevation (flat, convex, and umbonate), margin (entire, undulate, erose, and filamentous), and optical properties (opaque, translucent, and transparent) of isolated colonies from a purified strain cultured on solidified agar plates were carefully noted. Morphological characterization of the isolate was based on light microscopy. Gram staining was used to observe the cells using a 100X oil immersion microscope (Model CH20i, Olympus, Japan). Along with the Gram reaction, the cell morphology (cocci, bacilli, and cocco-bacilli) and cell arrangement (scattered, bunches, and chains) were recorded. The Hanging Drop method was used to measure the bacterial motility. Biochemical characterization of isolate was done by studying biochemical attributes such as the synthesis of acid from glucose, oxidation fermentation reaction (OF) properties, catalase and oxidase activity, citrate utilization, lysine utilization, ornithine utilization, malonate utilization, phenylamine deamination, H2S production, nitrate reduction, arginine hydrolysis and Exoenzyme activity tests like starch hydrolysis on starch plates, urease activity and proteolysis as hydrolysis of skim milk and gelatin using standard chemical media. Isolate was checked for its ability to grow on specific media such as cetrimide agar and MacConkey agar, and checked for its ability to produce pigments such as pyocyanin, fluorescein, and flexirubin. The data obtained from morphological and biochemical characterizations were used to identify the isolate according to Bergey’s Manual of Systematics Bacteriology21 and PIBWin Software22.

Fatty acid Methyl esterase analysis

Tryptic Soy Broth Agar was used to cultivate all strains for 48 h at 28 °C. The MIDI Identification System (MIDI, Microbial ID, Newark, DE, USA) methodology was used to acquire and quantify the fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs). A software program (MIDI) was used to identify and quantify FAMEs and to analyse the fatty acid profiles numerically.

Molecular characterization of the bacteria

The following procedures were used for 16 S rRNA sequencing and phylogenetic analysis: Universal primers 27F and 1492R were used to amplify the 16 S rRNA genes of each isolate. Amplified PCR fragments were sequenced by Bangalore Genei Pvt. Ltd., Bangalore, India. The basic local alignment search technique (BLASTn) was used to compare the 16 S rRNA gene’s partial sequences with sequences that were available in the gene bank databases in order to identify the closest taxa. CLUSTAL W version 1.8 was used to perform multiple sequence alignments. The phylogenetic tree was constructed using evolutionary distances and the neighbor-joining method. Tree topologies were evaluated by bootstrap analysis of 1000 data sets using MEGA 11.023 (Centre for Evolutionary Functional Genomics, The Biodesign Institute, Arizona State University, Phoenix, AZ, USA).

Biosorption experiment

The biosorption efficiencies of the isolated bacterial strain against heavy metals, such as Zn, Fe, Pb, Cd, and Cr, were assessed using the methodology outlined by Kour et al.24. Stock solutions of heavy metals were prepared using analytical-grade salts dissolved in distilled water. Zinc solution was prepared from ZnSO₄·7 H₂O, iron from FeSO₄·7 H₂O, lead from Pb(NO₃)₂, cadmium from CdCl₂·H₂O, and chromium from K₂Cr₂O₇. Each stock solution was prepared at a known concentration (1,000 mg/L) and stored in acid-washed polyethylene bottles at 4 °C. Working solutions of different concentrations were freshly prepared by appropriate dilution of the stock solutions with sterile distilled water prior to use in experiments. The isolated HMR isolate was inoculated into nutrient broth supplemented with heavy metal concentrations of 25 mg/L, 50 mg/L, 75 mg/L, and 100 mg/L and incubated for 72 h in a shaking condition at 150 rpm. After 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h, samples were drawn, and the residue and supernatant were separated using centrifugation for 5 min at 4000 rpm. The concentration of heavy metals in the supernatant was measured using an Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer (AAS) (Model AAS 4141, ECIL, India). The method outlined by Akhtar et al.26 was used to compute the bioaccumulation factor. After 24 h, the bioaccumulation factor was computed by dividing the amount of heavy metal in the microorganisms by the amount of heavy metal in the medium (broth).

Optimum conditions for the biosorption of heavy metals

In order to ascertain whether Chitinophaga niastensis was capable of absorbing heavy metal ions, batch biosorption studies were conducted. The effect of five parameters was studied: pH range (pH 3, 5, 7, and 9), contact time (0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120 min), metal ion concentration (25, 50, 75 and 100 mg/ L), bacterial biomass (0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, and 3.0 mg/ml), and temperature (15℃, 25℃, 35℃ and 45℃). The specified quantity of bacterial biomass and metal ion solution (100 mL) was incubated in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks with continuous agitation via a mechanical shaker at a rate of 150 r.p.m. under the stated experimental conditions. After optimizing the condition, the samples were filtered out at a late log phase, and atomic absorption spectrophotometry was used to determine the concentration of any remaining metal ions in the filtrate. Three replicates of the aforementioned process were conducted under identical experimental conditions.

SEM, SEM-EDS, and TEM analysis

The pelleted HMR31 cells, post-biosorption, were fixed using 3% glutaraldehyde, subsequently dried via freeze-drying (Model HC3055, Labtech, India), and positioned on the stud surface. They were then sputter-coated with gold particles and photographed using SEM (Carl Zeiss, EVO 181, Germany) at an operating voltage of 30.0 kV. Further, energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Inca Penta FETx3, Oxford Instruments, UK) was also performed to analyze the elemental composition of the surface of the HMR strain after Zn biosorption. Using a 120 kV accelerating voltage, electron micrographs were generated on a transmission electron microscope (TEM) (Tecnai 12, FEI, US). Biomass samples were fixed in a 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution for two hours, followed by a 24-hour fixation in a 1% osmium tetroxide solution at room temperature after equilibrating with a 50 mg/L metal solution for 24 h. After that, they were embedded in Epon812 resin and dehydrated using a series of ethanol. An Ultramicrotome (ULTRACUT E, Reichert-Jung, Germany) was used to cut sections that were about 0.35 mm thick. Following that, the portions were placed on copper grids with a mesh size of 20027,28.

Characterization of multiple plant growth‑promoting activities

Standard protocols previously described by Kour et al.24 and Jain et al.29 were used to test bacterial inoculates for a range of activities that promote plant growth, including the production of IAA, ACC deaminase, ammonia, HCN, GA3, siderophores, and phosphate solubilization.

In vitro studies on the effect of zinc-tolerant bacteria on the growth and yield of Vigna radiata seedlings

Pot experiments applying selected bacterial strains exhibiting diverse plant growth-promoting (PGP) activities and significant heavy metal tolerance were conducted in triplicate employing a completely randomised design (CRD) to examine the impact of bacterial isolate on Vigna radiata as per our previous research30. Soil used for plant experiments was sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min before sowing to eliminate indigenous microbial communities, ensuring that the observed effects were solely attributable to the inoculated strain HMR31. The seeds inoculated with the bacterial treatment were sown, while the uninoculated control was concurrently maintained. After 30 days of germination, several plant growth characteristics were measured, including shoot length, root length, root number, leaf number, and chlorophyll content.

Analysis of metals in plants

The root and shoot samples were washed, dried at 70 °C for 24 h, and then ground into a fine powder. A known weight (200 mg) of the powdered sample was digested with a mixture of concentrated nitric acid (HNO₃) and perchloric acid (HClO₄) in a ratio of 3:1 until a clear solution was obtained. The digest was cooled, filtered, and diluted with distilled water to a known volume for subsequent heavy metal estimation using AAS29. To evaluate how much metal is accumulated and absorbed by soil-dwelling organisms, the bioconcentration factor (BCF) was computed. According to its concentration in the substrate, it indicates the organisms’ capacity to accumulate a specific metal31.

Statistical analysis

Standard deviation and standard error for the experiments were analyzed on Microsoft Excel. The significant difference was analyzed using the JMP software version 11 using the Tukey–Kramer HSD test at p = 0.05.

Results and discussion

Sampling and isolation of bacteria

Hazardous metal-tolerant bacteria may be found under sampling conditions with high levels of heavy metals32. Locating metal-contaminated areas was the best option for sampling sites, which were chosen with the goal of isolating bacteria that tolerate heavy metals.

The study area in the present study was heavily polluted soil and mine spoil in Zawar, Udaipur, Rajasthan (India) (Table 1). In contaminated soils and waters, numerous microorganisms have been identified for employing in biodegradation and bioremediation33–35. In the present investigation, efforts were made to isolate bacterial strains that tolerate heavy metals. The standard pour plate method was used to recover 15 native bacterial strains from three soil samples collected from multiple points of a heavy metal-contaminated site from the tailings of Zawar near Udaipur, Rajasthan, India. The samples were inoculated on nutrient agar supplemented with a 1 mM concentration of zinc sulfate heptahydrate. Additionally, a zinc concentration of 5 mM was used to grow each of these isolates. One yellow-colored bacterial strain, HMR31, was chosen from among them based on its ability to grow at this concentration. Its cultural, morphological, and biochemical traits were noted. In the present study, isolation was carried out on a nutrient agar medium amended with a heavy metal compound. Several workers have chosen nutrient agar supplemented with heavy metals for the isolation of heavy metal-tolerant bacteria36–39. The incorporation of heavy metals into nutrition agar creates selection pressure, resulting in the proliferation of a limited number of strains resistant to these metals, whereas nutrient agar supports the growth of diverse bacteria. The current study demonstrated that microorganisms found in heavy metal-contaminated soil exhibit environmental adaptability and acclimatization. It’s possible that these isolates developed metal resistance mechanisms to shield sensitive cellular constituents.

Phenotypic characterization of the bacteria

Cultural and morphological characterizations are basic microbial techniques used to group bacteria. The isolate HMR31 was studied in detail for colony characteristics, i.e., color, shape, surface, margin, and elevation. The findings of the study showed that strain HMR31’s colonial morphology was small, yellow, circular, entire, convex, opaque, and mucoid colonies. During the Gram staining of strain HMR31, cellular morphology, including organization, shape, and Gram reaction, was observed. The strain was identified as gram-negative, and its cellular structure was determined to be a rod, while its cellular arrangement appeared to be scattered. The HMR31 strain was immobile at 37 °C and room temperature.

Based on the variations in the biochemical activities of different bacteria, biochemical tests were employed to identify the species of bacteria. The biochemical tests conducted in this study were compared with the descriptions recorded in Bergey’s Manual of Systematic Bacteriology and PIBwin software. When grown on Hugh & Leifson oxidation-fermentation medium, the HMR31 strain exhibited a positive response for glucose oxidation but a negative response for glucose fermentation. The strain HMR31 gave a positive reaction for oxidase activity, and gave a negative reaction for citrate utilization, lysine utilization, ornithine utilization, malonate utilization, phenylamine deamination, H2S production, nitrate reduction, and arginine hydrolysis. On studying functional characteristics like the production of enzymes, strain HMR31 gave a positive reaction for catalase activity and urease activity, and gave a negative reaction for amylase activity, casein, and gelatin hydrolysis. On differential media such as Cetrimide agar and MacConkey agar, the isolate failed to grow. On King’s A and B media, the isolate was unable to produce pigment. On flooding the bacterial colonies with 20% KOH solution, the color of the colonies of isolate HMR31 changed to brown in a few seconds, indicating the production of flexirubin pigment. The morphological and biochemical data (Table 2) were further compared with the PIBwin software and Bergey’s Manual of Systematic Bacteriology. Based on biochemical analysis aided by PIB software, an identification score was calculated, which should be more than 0.95 for a successful identification. The results of biochemical tests for isolate HMR31 were fed into PIB software, and an identification score (ID) of 0.72408 was observed, which was less than the threshold value of 0.95. Thus, the most likely taxon related to isolate HMR31 suggested was Pseudomonas paucimobilis.

Fatty acid methyl esterase analysis

Gas chromatography-based fatty acid methyl ester analysis (GC-FAME) is used to detect microorganisms in clinical and environmental circumstances. It depends on the growth environment, such as the temperature and media. FAME analysis is frequently specific enough to enable comparisons at the strain or subspecies level in addition to identification. MIDI Sherlock microbiological identification allows microorganisms to be identified at the species level. It was first proposed by Abel and coworkers that gas chromatographic analysis may be used to classify microorganisms40. Measurements of retention time, the amount of time it takes for a particular fatty acid to pass through the GC column, are used with this method to identify fatty acids (Sherlock Analysis Software, MIDI Systems, Inc.). A library of retention times that correspond to the retention time of a fatty acid from an unidentified sample is included in the GC’s analytical software program. Microorganisms possess species-specific fatty acid profiles41. The membrane fatty acid content of the isolate in the current study aligns with that of its corresponding species, as previously described42.

The isolate was found to contain 45–50 distinct fatty acids. According to Table 3, isolate HMR31 contained all six of the Chitinophaga genus’s signature fatty acids: iso-C15:0, iso-C15:0 3-OH, C16:0, C18:0, C16:1 ω5c, iso-C17:0 3-OH, and Summed Feature 3 (SF3). The isolate contained one fatty acid (C12:0) in addition to the signature fatty acids. The main fatty acids of strain HMR31 were unsaturated C16:1 ω5c and saturated iso-C15:0, constituting 46.34% of the total fatty acids. Species of the genus Chitinophaga also exhibited significant (≥ 4%) levels of hydroxylated fatty acid iso-C17:0 3-OH, C12:0, and summed characteristic 3 (which consists of C16:1 ω7c and/or C16:1 ω6c) (Supplementary Fig. 2). The isolate contained 12.79%, 27.06%, 18.59%, 40.16%, and 0.52% of saturated, unsaturated, hydroxyl, branched, and cyclopropane fatty acids, respectively. According to the isolates’ whole-cell fatty acid composition, Gram-positive isolates were more likely to include branched-chain (iso and anteiso) fatty acids, while Gram-negative bacteria were more likely to contain straight-chain and hydroxyl fatty acids43. A similar finding was noted for the gram-negative bacterial strain HMR31 in the current investigation. The strain HMR31 was determined to be Chitinophaga niastensis according to the presence of these biomarkers.

Molecular characterization of the bacteria

The identification of environmental isolates and species without adequate phenotypic characterisation necessitates molecular methodologies44. Both defining taxa and identifying species found in clinical, veterinary, and environmental samples are common applications for 16 S rRNA data45–48. The biochemical and physiological traits permitted only initial identification because of intrinsic limitations49. Nucleotide sequences of the partial 16 S rRNA gene, corresponding to positions 27–1492 of the Escherichia coli 16 S rRNA gene, were utilised to evaluate the isolates in order to more precisely identify them. Primers are designed to amplify variable areas while binding to conserved regions. In the present study, isolate HMR31 gave a specific band of 1500 bp with universal bacterial primers, namely 27 F and 1492R, which was further submitted to Bangalore Genei Pvt. Ltd., Bangalore, India, for sequencing. The 16 S rRNA sequence data were analysed using BLAST and submitted to the NCBI GeneBank database with the accession number KT735167. Isolate HMR31 showed 99% sequence similarity with Chitinophaga niastensis sp. nov. (type strain JS16–4T) and therefore, this isolate was finally identified as Chitinophaga niastensis HMR31. This novel species of bacteria was first reported by Weon et al.42 who isolated and identified the bacterial strain as Chitinophaga niastensis JS16–4T from the soil of Jeju Island, Republic of Korea, based on 16 S rRNA gene sequence analysis. Using the multi-sequence alignment tool, the phylogeny of the heavy metal-tolerant bacterial strain HMR31 and closely related species was examined; the results are shown in Fig. 1. The isolate showed the highest sequence similarity to Chitinophaga niastensis, clustering closely with reference strains C. niastensis JS16-4 and C. niastensis NBRC 106,413 in the phylogenetic tree. This analysis confirmed the taxonomic identity of the strain and highlighted its evolutionary relationship within the genus Chitinophaga. The phylogenetic tree demonstrates that the isolate forms a distinct clade, supporting its classification as a Zn-tolerant strain with potential bioremediation applications.

The constraints of phenotypic identification of isolates from natural sources encompass the influence of environmental pressures on gene expression and the impacts of cultural circumstances on morphological and biochemical characteristics46,50. Another problem is that databases of commercially available identification techniques have a high degree of accuracy for common species, making it difficult to find rare strains and species that are infrequently isolated51,52. The isolate HMR31 in this study was assigned to a distinct genus compared to those identified through biochemical tests, based on the findings from FAME and 16 S rRNA gene sequence analysis.

Based on biochemical tests, the isolate HMR31 was tentatively identified as Pseudomonas paucimobilis and was finally identified as Chitinophaga niastensis based on FAME analysis and 16 S rRNA sequencing. In a few instances, 16 S rRNA sequencing and conventional identification by studying biochemical characteristics gave different results. Aksornchu et al.53 reported the difference between identification based on biochemical tests and 16 S rRNA analysis. Isolate B-10 was discovered to belong to the genus Ochrobactrum by 16 S rRNA analysis, while biochemical assays showed that it belonged to the species Pseudomonas. Many factors may have contributed to the identification’s limitations, including the identification of the only phenotypic features (which only make up a portion of the polyphasic taxonomic approach that is advised to identify microorganisms), the gram-negative oriented analyses (PIB, API 20NE, and BIOLOG GN2 systems) that made it difficult to identify some isolates, the quantity of tests in the strips, and the database’s content limitations54.

Biosorption experiment

To investigate the capability of the HMR isolate to remove heavy metals, the nutrient broth enriched with Zn, Fe, Pb, Cd, and Cr concentrations of 25 mg/L, 50 mg/L, 75 mg/L, and 100 mg/L was inoculated with an overnight-grown culture of isolated HMR isolate. The concentration of heavy metals in the cell-free supernatants was measured in 72 h inoculated flasks in a shaking condition using AAS. The results showed that the selected HMR strain effectively removed heavy metals from the nutrient broth medium, with zinc biosorption being the highest and followed by iron, lead, cadmium, and chromium (Fig. 2).

Mean biosorption efficiency percentages of Zn, Fe, Pb, Cd, and Cr achieved by HMR31 bacterial isolate. Bars represent standard deviation (SD).

The highest amounts of zinc, iron, and cadmium were removed by HMR31 from the nutrient broth medium supplemented with 50 mg/L of these elements (89.346%, 88.146%, and 79.96%, respectively). In contrast, HMR31 was able to remove 80.476% and 64.132% of lead and chromium from the medium in the nutrient broth medium supplemented with 25 mg/L of these elements. According to Ezzouhri et al.55 and Shazia et al.56, the presence of one or more tolerance strategies or resistant mechanisms exhibited by microorganisms may be the reason for the variation in metal tolerance, with the isolate determining the level of resistance. Additionally, Pradhan and Rai57 showed that when metal concentrations increased, this was accompanied by the biosorption of heavy metals. This investigation also showed that the bacterial strain biosorbs zinc relatively more than it does Fe, Pb, and Cd in the single metal solution. Its increased biosorption by the microorganism may be due to the fact that Zn has a smaller ionic radius (74 pm) than Fe (77 pm), Pb (119 pm), and Cd (94 pm). Tobin et al.58 demonstrated that molecules with a smaller ionic radius may more readily sorbed onto a Wxed patch of sorbent. Higher Zn biosorption among Fe, Cd, and Pb based on ionic radius was in good agreement with the results of Igwe and Abia59 and Ali et al.60.

The BAF values correspond to the extent to which microorganisms accumulate heavy metals. It is calculated by the ratio of heavy metals in bacterial cells to heavy metals in the supernatant. The BAF value of more than 1 signifies that the microorganisms are hyperaccumulators of heavy metals. In the present study, the high bioaccumulation factor of Zinc (2.84), Iron (1.78), and Cadmium (1.12) by C. niastaensis HMR31 cultured in broth with respective heavy metals suggests that bacteria are hyperaccumulators of Zn, Fe, and Cd (Fig. 3). Since there was no high BAF value to go along with the high percentage loss of Cr and Pb resulted on by the growth of C. niastaensis HMR31, it is possible that this loss was caused by alternative heavy metal resistance mechanisms rather than accumulation26.

Optimum conditions for the biosorption of heavy metals

Chitinophaga niastensis, an isolated bacterium, was used in this experiment to adsorb the heavy metal ions. Five parameters were examined in relation to the biosorption of heavy metal ions: temperature, pH value, contact time, initial concentration of heavy metal ions, and biosorbent dose. The selection of these parameters for optimization experiments was crucial, as these factors directly affect both microbial physiology and metal availability. pH plays a key role in determining metal ion solubility and the ionization state of functional groups on the bacterial cell surface, thereby influencing biosorption. Temperature impacts enzymatic activity and membrane fluidity, which govern the metabolic performance of the strain. Similarly, the initial metal concentration affects not only the driving force for biosorption but also the stress response of the bacteria. Biomass concentration increases the number of active sites available on the bacterial surface for binding metal ions. Optimized contact time determines equilibrium attainment and reveals kinetic behavior. Together, these parameters define the conditions under which the strain exhibits maximum tolerance and removal efficiency.

The biosorption efficiency of Chitinophaga niastensis HMR31 for Zn(II) was significantly influenced by several physicochemical parameters such as pH, biomass concentration, temperature, contact time, and initial metal ion concentration. The adsorption capacity of the biosorbent was found to be highly pH-dependent61. As shown in Fig. 4a, biosorption increased from 59.49% at pH 3 to 85.70% at pH 7, followed by a gradual decline at higher pH levels. The optimum pH for zinc ion biosorption was thus determined to be 7. Reduced sorption at low pH could be attributed to competition between H⁺ ions and metal ions for active sites, while the decrease at high pH may result from metal hydroxide formation and decreased surface availability62–66. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that surface charge and cell wall chemistry vary with pH, affecting metal uptake efficiency67,68.

Effect of (a) pH, (b) Biomass concentration, (c) Temperature, (d) contact time, and (e) Initial metal concentration on the biosorption of Zn metal ions by C. niastensis HMR31. Bars represent standard deviation (SD).

Biomass concentration also played a crucial role in zinc removal efficiency. Increasing the concentration from 0.5 to 1.5 mg/mL enhanced biosorption, beyond which no significant improvement was observed (Fig. 4b). A biomass concentration of 1.5 mg/mL was found optimal, as higher concentrations likely caused cell aggregation and site overlapping, thereby reducing available surface area69. Temperature exhibited a similar influence, with maximum biosorption (85.69%) recorded at 35 °C (Fig. 4c). A moderate temperature favored greater metal ion mobility and binding, whereas further increases led to reduced biosorption due to possible biosorbent damage, decreased surface area, and metal ion desorption65,66,70,71. The reason for this phenomenon is that cells contract at varying temperatures, hence reducing the contact surface area62. Higher temperatures make heavy metal ions more likely to desorb from biosorbent surfaces72–74, and the boundary layer diminishes, slowing the removal of heavy metal ions75.

Contact time and initial metal ion concentration were also critical parameters affecting biosorption performance. As shown in Fig. 4d, Zn(II) uptake increased rapidly during the first 60 min due to abundant vacant sites, reaching equilibrium thereafter. This biphasic behavior corresponds to a rapid surface adsorption phase followed by slower intracellular diffusion63,76,77. A contact time of 60 min was therefore chosen as optimal. The effect of initial metal ion concentration (25–100 mg/L) revealed that biosorption increased from 76.98% to 86.20% as concentration rose from 25 to 50 mg/L, owing to enhanced electrostatic interactions between metal ions and the biosorbent surface (Fig. 4e)64,78. However, a decline in removal efficiency at higher concentrations likely resulted from saturation of available binding sites. Collectively, these findings indicate that optimal biosorption of Zn(II) by C. niastensis HMR31 occurred at pH 7, 1.5 mg/mL biomass concentration, 35 °C, 60-minute contact time, and an initial Zn(II) concentration of 50 mg/L.

SEM, SEM-EDX and TEM analysis

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) are very useful tools for examining bacterial interactions with heavy metals. SEM provides comprehensive information regarding the shape of the bacteria, whereas EDX demonstrates the presence of elements, such as heavy metals, on and within the bacterial cells. This combination enables scientists to observe the accumulation of metals, observe changes in shape, and gain insight into the mechanisms by which bacteria resist and absorb metals. The cell surface of zinc-treated HMR31 exhibited rod forms with rough boundaries and was coated with sediments (Fig. 5a). Alterations in cell morphology may be interpreted as a defensive strategy in response to a stressful environment, as previously reported29. EDX serves as an effective technique for the chemical and elemental characterisation of biosorbents and has been widely utilised79. EDX spectra captured signals of carbon and oxygen, presumably present in the polysaccharides and proteins of the biosorbents (Fig. 5b). The EDX spectra additionally revealed Zn (II) signals, indicating the presence of Zn (II) in the cell post-adsorption (Fig. 6).

As demonstrated in Fig. 7, isolate C. niastaensis HMR31 can remove Zn with high concentrations using both adsorption and absorption mechanisms. The presence of zinc deposits on the bacterial surface and within the cells was confirmed by C. niastaensis HMR31 Transmission Electron micrographs obtained both before and after the zinc treatment experiments. Different mechanisms of tolerance to heavy metal stress are displayed by bacteria. The type of mechanism used to counteract heavy metal stress also affects how heavy metals are distributed within bacterial cells. The distribution of zinc in the cells and on the cell surface of C. niastaensis may suggest that the heavy metal is controlled by ion efflux pumps found on the cell surfaces and that zinc forms an intracellular metal complex.

Characterization of multiple plant growth‑promoting activities

Heavy metal-tolerant bacteria are the ideal option for bioremediation research because of their ability to stimulate plant growth in a heavy metal-contaminated area. Therefore, the production of IAA, ammonia, siderophore, GA3, and HCN; ACC deaminase activity; and phosphate solubilization were among the several PGP attributes that were tested under both normal and zinc stress conditions (Table 4). Results indicate that, under normal conditions (without Zn), C. niastaensis HMR31was found positive for IAA production, ACC Deaminase activity, GA3 production, ammonia production, HCN production, siderophore production, and phosphate solubilization. Under heavy metal stress conditions, it also showed this ability. C. niastaensis HMR31 was positive for ACC Deaminase activity and HCN production with and without Zn. Under normal conditions, production of IAA, ammonia, GA3, siderophore index, and phosphate solubilization index by C. niastaensis HMR31 was found to be 11.54 µg/ml, 1.52 µg/ml, 45.33 µg/ml, 2.25, 4.95 respectively, whereas 9.21 µg/ml, 1.02 µg/ml, 31.22 µg/ml, 1.15, 3.15 were found under Zinc stressed conditions, respectively. According to a number of studies, bacterial strains’ production of compounds that promote plant development was generally decreased by heavy metals in culture media; nevertheless, some PGP properties were able to persist or even improve in spite of heavy metal stress80–83. Since heavy metals hinder a variety of biochemical processes, including the synthesis of enzymes and hormones, the decrease in PGP activities is probably caused by the inhibitory effects of heavy metals on bacterial growth and activity. The synthesis of these growth-promoting compounds declined as the concentration of heavy metals increased. Consequently, even though zinc stress typically hinders plant growth and can affect the activities of beneficial bacteria, some tolerant strains, such as HMR31, can nevertheless enhance growth characteristics by generating a variety of substances that promote plant growth, which eventually helps alleviate some of the stress experienced by the plants.

In vitro studies on the effect of zinc-tolerant bacteria on the growth and yield of Vigna radiata seedlings

The effect of the heavy metal-tolerant isolate Chitinophaga niastensis HMR31 was analyzed on the growth of mung bean (Vigna radiata L.) in soil containing zinc sulfate heptahydrate (ZnSO4⋅7H2O). The experimental design comprised three treatments: the first treatment (control) involved sowing seeds in soil devoid of ZnSO4⋅7H2O and bacteria; the second treatment (uninoculated control) involved sowing seeds in soil containing ZnSO4⋅7H2O; the third treatment involved inoculating seeds with Chitinophaga niastensis HMR31 and sowing them in soil with ZnSO4⋅7H2O. The outcomes of these tests are displayed in Table 5 as the shoot tolerance index (STI) and root tolerance index (RTI).

Vigna radiata L. showed significant differences in shoot and root length (p < 0.05), Root and Leaf Number, and Total Chlorophyll content between control plants and uninoculated plants. It is revealed from the table that shoot length was significantly decreased to 20.5 cm in the uninoculated control as compared to that of the control (31 cm). Similarly significant reduction was observed in root length, which was decreased to 7.0 cm in the uninoculated control in comparison to that of the control (13 cm). Shoot length and root length were significantly decreased by 33.9% and 46.2%, respectively as compared to the control. STI and RTI were calculated as 0.66 and 0.53, respectively. Similarly, average root number, leaf number, and total chlorophyll content decreased to 3.8%, 20%, and 8.97% respectively, as compared to the control. These data supported the findings of other studies that proposed that in an environment stressed by heavy metals, plant growth in terms of shoot and root length declines84–87. They proposed that a decrease in cell division and the harmful effects of heavy metals on photosynthesis, respiration, and protein synthesis could be the cause of the inhibitory effect of high zinc on root and shoot length. Because the mechanisms required to repair the damage to membranes and proteins require energy, the growth reduction caused by zinc stress may be partially caused by an energy deficit. It was discovered in this investigation that root growth was more responsive than shoot growth. According to some theories, the endodermis acts as a barrier to the radial transit of metal in the root, preventing it from moving to the shoots, which is why metal builds up in the roots88.

Vigna radiata L. showed significant differences in shoot length, root length, root number, leaf number, and total chlorophyll content (p < 0.05) between uninoculated control and plants inoculated with heavy metal-tolerant bacteria (Table 5). Inoculation of the heavy metal-tolerant bacteria increased tolerance to ZnSO4.7H2O of Vigna radiata L. in soil containing 1000 mg/kg of ZnSO4.7H2O, and improvement was recorded in shoot length, root length, root number, leaf number, and total chlorophyll content of the plant. On inoculating seeds with Chitinophaga niastensis HMR31, shoot length, root length, root number, leaf number, and total chlorophyll content significantly increased to 25.2 cm, 10.1 cm, 13, 5.4, and 35.4, respectively, as compared to those of uninoculated control (20.5 cm, 7 cm, 10.1, 4, and 28.4, respectively). Shoot length, root length, root number, leaf number, and total chlorophyll content were significantly increased by 22.9%, 44.3%, 28.71%, 35%, and 24.64% respectively, as compared to the uninoculated control. STI and RTI were calculated as 1.23 and 1.44, respectively. According to Abbasi et al.85, when Pseudomonas fluorescence was present, the mung bean’s root length in chromium-containing soil increased to 14.8 cm from the control’s 14.6 cm. Comparing the seed bioinoculation with Pseudomonas fluorescens to the non-coated seeds, Petriccione et al.89 discovered that the former encouraged an increase in shoot and root length in the metal-contaminated soil. In pot trials, Ali et al.90 found that adding bacteria that promote plant development effectively increases spinach growth in soil contaminated with cadmium. The current investigation’s findings are consistent with the previously reported research.

In the present study, RTI and STI were calculated as greater than one in treatment with Chitinophaga niastensis HMR31. According to Burd et al.91, a TI of 1.0 means that the treatment was ineffective, a TI > 1.0 means that the treated plants grew more than the control, and a TI < 1.0 means that the treated plants grew less than the control. In this regard, isolate Chitinophaga niastensis HMR31 positively affected the growth in treated plants in the present study. Bacteria resistant to heavy metals can enhance plant growth in metal-contaminated soil in several ways, such as immobilising metals on the cell surface or within intracellular polymers, precipitating them, or biomethylating them. They can enhance heavy metal solubility by generating organic ligands, complexing cationic metals, or desorbing anionic species via ligand exchange with siderophores and secreting organic acids92–95. In the current study, the improvement of plant development by bacteria under heavy metal-stressed conditions may be associated with the mechanisms described above.

Analysis of metals in plants

While phytoextraction has the capacity to eliminate heavy metals from the environment, high quantities of these metals can render them poisonous, even to plants that are capable of accumulating or tolerating them. Using metal-resistant bacteria is one method to reduce the harmful effects of heavy metals absorbed from the environment on some plants. The accumulation of metal in the edible plants was analysed to assess toxicity levels. The bioconcentration factor (BCF) was calculated to evaluate metal deposition and uptake by organisms in the soil. This indicates the organism’s capacity to accumulate a particular metal, determined by its concentration in the substrate31.

The ratio of heavy metals dissolved in the soil medium to those accumulated in each plant tissue is known as the bioconcentration factor, or BCF. Results (Table 5) clearly show that the presence of zinc in soil negatively affects the growth of the plant Vigna radiata. BCF of uninoculated control, i.e., seeds sown in soil with ZnSO4⋅7H2O, and in inoculated treatment, i.e., seeds inoculated with Chitinophaga niastensis HMR31 and sown in soil with ZnSO4⋅7H2O were 1.32 and 0.97, respectively (Table 6). BCF value of more than 1 signifies that the plant is accumulating zinc, which is affecting the growth of the plant as well. Whereas BCF < 1 in the inoculated treatment indicates that bacteria are guarding plants by accumulating heavy metals inside their cells and assisting in plant growth. The presence of bioremediating microorganisms in the rhizosphere is responsible for the relatively low degree of heavy metal accumulation in the plant species under study. Huang et al.96 assert that mycorrhizae safeguard host plants against the phytotoxic effects of excessive copper, zinc, and lead by altering the speciation from a bioavailable to a non-bioavailable form.

Conclusions

Heavy metals are significant environmental pollutants that reduce soil fertility and adversely affect plant life. This study identified and characterized C. niastensis HMR31, isolated from highly contaminated mine soil in Zawar, Udaipur (India), as a bacterium with promising in vitro potential for heavy metal remediation. A polyphasic identification approach, incorporating 16 S rRNA sequencing, FAME profiling, and phenotypic-biochemical characterisation, facilitated precise species-level identification. FAME analysis has demonstrated reliability and cost-effectiveness as an adjunct to molecular techniques. Under controlled laboratory conditions, the strain showed notable biosorption of multiple heavy metals, with the highest uptake observed for zinc, followed by iron, lead, cadmium, and chromium. Optimal biosorption occurred at pH 7, 35 °C, a 60-minute contact time, 50 mg/L metal concentration, and 1.5 mg/mL biosorbent dosage. In addition, C. niastensis HMR31 demonstrated plant growth-promoting characteristics, such as the synthesis of gibberellins and indole acetic acid, and increased shoot and root lengths by 22.9% and 44.3%, respectively, compared to controls. However, these findings are limited to in vitro experiments, and the strain’s performance under complex environmental conditions, interactions with native microbiota, molecular basis of metal tolerance and long-term stability remain to be validated. Moreover, Zn-enriched biomass produced during microbial remediation may present environmental hazards upon degradation; thus, future studies should focus on effective biomass management via stabilization, immobilization, or metal recovery strategies, which will be crucial for ensuring long-term environmental safety and sustainable bioremediation.
