Research on dynamic prediction of vegetation coverage by precipitation-evapotranspiration in arid regions based on CNN-LSTM hybrid model
Authors: Kai Peng, Yanfei Zhang, Tiejun Liu, Zijing Li, Wentao Liang, Hualin Liu, Yawen Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAiming to address the challenges of phenological lag and the dominant role of water factors (precipitation and evapotranspiration) in dynamically predicting vegetation coverage in arid regions, this study proposes a hybrid CNN-LSTM-based prediction method. By employing the Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI) to classify the study area into different arid region types, it provides diverse samples of vegetation coverage changes under various drought conditions for training the CNN-LSTM model. The model integrates precipitation-evapotranspiration data from multiple arid region types along with target prediction data. The CNN extracts spatial features of precipitation and evapotranspiration, while the LSTM captures long-term temporal dependencies, enabling dynamic vegetation coverage prediction. Furthermore, the study quantifies both the single-year and comprehensive dynamic change indices of vegetation coverage to characterize its temporal variations. Experimental results demonstrate that the model effectively identifies differences in rainfall characteristics across areas with different drought severity levels. In arid regions, the contribution rate of heavy rainfall to the total rainfall anomaly (25.15%) is substantially higher than that in semi-arid regions (1.55%), with statistical tests confirming a significant difference (P < 0.05). On the test set, the Pearson correlation coefficient between the model’s predictions and the measured data reaches 0.95, indicating strong agreement with reality. The dynamic change indices accurately capture phased variations in vegetation coverage from 2000 to 2022.Keywords: CNN-LSTM hybrid model, Standardized precipitation evapotranspiration index, Precipitation-evapotranspiration in arid regions, Vegetation coverage, Dynamic prediction, Dynamic change indexSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Arid regions have extremely fragile ecosystems, characterized by scarce precipitation and high evaporation rates. Vegetation, as a crucial component of ecosystems, serves not only as an important natural resource but also as a sensitive environmental indicator1. Fractional Vegetation Cover (FVC), which quantifies the proportion of a statistical area occupied by the vertical projection of vegetation, is a key parameter for surface cover and an essential indicator of ecological and environmental changes2. It describes the coverage of vegetation on the ground surface and serves as one of the key metrics for characterizing vegetation status and the regional ecological environment.

Under the global warming trend, climatic factors in arid regions, such as rainfall patterns and evaporation rates, have exhibited significant spatiotemporal variability3. The spatiotemporal distribution of rainfall has shifted, with increased frequency and intensity of extreme rainfall events, while rising temperatures have led to higher evaporation rates, exacerbating water loss. These changes affect vegetation growth, distribution, and coverage, altering vegetation phenology and growth cycles and leading to the degradation or death of some vegetation. In arid regions, water resources are scarce, and precipitation is the primary water source, while evaporation is an important pathway for water loss. Precipitation and evaporation directly influence the water supply required for vegetation growth, while vegetation coverage affects surface evaporation and water retention. Studying the dynamic relationship between precipitation, evapotranspiration, and vegetation coverage is of great significance for understanding water balance in arid regions and facilitating rational water resource utilization, providing a scientific basis for water resource management and ecological restoration. Therefore, in-depth exploration of this dynamic relationship and achieving accurate dynamic predictions of vegetation coverage have become urgent priorities in ecological research.

Previously, numerous scholars have conducted a series of studies on vegetation cover, achieving certain results in aspects such as the analysis of its influencing factors and its spatiotemporal characteristics. Hideki et al. design a new vegetation loss model that considers transmission frequency and seasonal characteristics to measure and analyze the seasonal changes in deciduous tree vegetation in Japan. When electromagnetic waves of different frequencies (such as microwaves and millimeter waves) penetrate or bypass vegetation, their signal intensity is attenuated due to the blocking, scattering, and absorption effects of vegetation. By transmitting signals of specific frequencies in different vegetation areas and under different vegetation density conditions, and then receiving and measuring the signal attenuation, vegetation cover can be estimated. In addition to vegetation, atmospheric water vapor, aerosols, and other objects on the ground may also cause scattering, absorption, or other interference to electromagnetic wave signals, affecting the accuracy of signal attenuation measurements and thereby reducing the estimation accuracy of vegetation cover4.

Batnyambuu et al. study a vegetation cover estimation method combining ground vegetation data with remote sensing, utilizing the complementary integration of field measurements and remote sensing imagery to establish a quantitative relationship between vegetation cover and spectral characteristics, thereby enabling large-scale, high-precision vegetation cover estimation. Radiative transfer models (such as PROSAIL) require input parameters such as Leaf Area Index (LAI), Leaf Angle Distribution (LAD), and soil reflectance, which are difficult to obtain accurately in practical applications, often relying on empirical values or simplified assumptions, leading to discrepancies in model simulation results5.

Mishra et al. study the correlation between rainfall data and vegetation loss using Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR). They use the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), enhanced vegetation index (EVI), and rainfall distribution from global precipitation measurement data to generate an influence map of cyclones on vegetation. However, the geographically weighted regression model assumes that spatial non-stationarity changes smoothly with spatial continuity. In reality, vegetation loss may be influenced by discrete geographic features (such as rivers, roads, and land use boundaries), resulting in abrupt spatial variations that the model struggles to capture6.

Thomas et al. investigate a method for generating vegetation feature maps based on satellite hyperspectral images, leveraging the high spectral resolution of hyperspectral data to capture leaf-canopy-level spectral absorption and reflection characteristics. By applying physical models or data-driven algorithms, they infer vegetation physiological and biochemical parameters, structural traits, and coverage status, ultimately producing spatially continuous vegetation feature maps for coverage analysis. However, hyperspectral data primarily reflect instantaneous spectral properties, while seasonal vegetation changes exhibit phenological lag, making single-timepoint data inadequate for tracking dynamic processes. Multi-temporal data fusion can mitigate this issue, though spectral stability across time (e.g., sensor calibration drift) may introduce errors7.

Given this context, the study focuses on arid regions, with the aim of comprehensively utilizing multi-source data and advanced analysis techniques to deeply explore the dynamic influence mechanisms of precipitation and evapotranspiration on vegetation coverage and to construct a more accurate and effective method for predicting vegetation coverage dynamics. The findings are expected to provide theoretical and decision-making support for the scientific management and sustainable development of arid region ecosystems, thereby contributing to their conservation and restoration.

Dynamic prediction of vegetation coverage in arid regions

Multi-type early warning level analysis based on standardized precipitation evapotranspiration index (SPEI)

The Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI) integrates precipitation and potential evapotranspiration data, providing a comprehensive assessment of regional water balance that is particularly suitable for analyzing drought characteristics under climate change conditions8. In this study, SPEI serves as the basis for drought classification prior to predicting dynamic vegetation cover changes in arid regions. Since vegetation cover status varies significantly across different drought severity zones, this classification step is essential for achieving accurate vegetation cover predictions across multiple drought zone types9.

Experimental analysis

Experimental setup

Data sources and data sets

The Gobi region constitutes a vital component of Inner Mongolia’s ecosystem, spanning most of Alxa League—including portions of Ejina Banner, Alxa Left Banner, and Alxa Right Banner—with its northern boundary extending to the China-Mongolia border. To the east, it neighbors desert areas of Bayannur City, while its southern periphery adjoins Gansu Province’s Hexi Corridor. Characterized by an arid climate with scarce precipitation and intense evaporation, this region presents severe hydrological constraints on vegetation growth, making it an ideal study area for examining precipitation-evapotranspiration impacts on vegetation cover. Accurate prediction of vegetation cover dynamics here holds significant implications for developing targeted ecological conservation strategies, rational livestock management planning, and climate change adaptation. The study area encompasses the Inner Mongolian Gobi region (Fig. 2), incorporating Ejina Banner, Wulate Rear Banner, Wulate Middle Banner, and adjacent zones. This hyper-arid climate zone supports primarily drought-resistant shrubs and herbaceous plants within a fragile ecosystem highly sensitive to precipitation-evapotranspiration variations.

Gobi area in Inner Mongolia.

The meteorological data source includes annual precipitation data from the China Meteorological Science Data Sharing Service Network (http://cdc.cma.gov.cn/). Annual evaporation (i.e., potential evapotranspiration) data are obtained from the Resource and Environment Data Cloud Platform of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (http://www.resdc.cn/). Daily data from 118 meteorological stations in the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region and its surrounding areas from 2000 to 2022 are adopted. Strict quality control is carried out on the original data, including eliminating abnormal values and filling short-term missing data at individual stations using spatial interpolation (such as the inverse distance weighting method). Finally, the daily data are aggregated into monthly values, and annual values are calculated for subsequent analysis. Vegetation coverage data are obtained from the Resource and Environment Data Cloud Platform (http://www.resdc.cn/) of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, and represent a product derived from inversion based on remote sensing images such as MODIS. These data are used as the target variable (true value) for model prediction. Land use data also come from the Resource and Environment Data Cloud Platform of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, and are used to help understand the underlying surface conditions of the study area.

Rainfall evaporation data is a comprehensive concept, which mainly refers to two key water balance factors: precipitation and potential evapotranspiration. These factors serve as the fundamental variables for calculating the Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI) and are directly input into the forecasting model. After rigorous preprocessing and spatialization, the precipitation and evapotranspiration data, as the core driving factors, are transformed into accurate predictions of vegetation coverage through the CNN-LSTM hybrid model.

To train and evaluate the CNN-LSTM hybrid model, all the data from 2000 to 2022 are randomly divided into three parts according to the ratio of approximately 7:1:2: training set, validation set, and test set. The training set is used for directly training the model and learning parameters; the validation set is used to adjust the model hyperparameters and monitor the training process to prevent overfitting; after the model training and tuning, the test set is used to evaluate the generalization performance of the model finally and independently.

Role and processing of precipitation and evapotranspiration data

In this study, rainfall evaporation data serve as the core input features driving the CNN-LSTM forecasting model. Since the CNN requires regular grid‑like spatial data as input, whereas the original meteorological data are collected from discrete stations, the data are spatialized. The term "rainfall evaporation data" here primarily refers to two key water‑balance factors: precipitation and potential evapotranspiration. To convert point‑based observations from 118 meteorological stations into spatial grid data suitable for the CNN, the Kriging interpolation method is adopted. This method fully accounts for spatial autocorrelation and geographical structure in meteorological variables, providing unbiased and optimal spatial estimates. In practice, precipitation and potential evapotranspiration data for each time step are interpolated separately to generate continuous spatial grid maps covering the entire study area. The interpolation is performed in an ArcGIS/Python environment, and the resolution adopted matches that of the vegetation cover remote sensing products used, ensuring that all data layers are perfectly aligned and consistent in space. After interpolation, the precipitation and potential evapotranspiration at each time step form spatial distribution maps. These two raster layers are treated as independent channels, which together constitute a multi‑channel spatial data cube that serves as input to the CNN at the corresponding time step. For each geographical grid unit (pixel), time series of precipitation and potential evapotranspiration from 2000 to 2022 are extracted, and a sliding‑window sample format acceptable to the model is constructed.

Role in the model: (1) In the CNN: The multi-channel spatial data cube constructed previously is fed into the CNN. The convolutional kernels of the CNN automatically learn and extract the spatial distribution patterns, spatial synergy characteristics, and local spatial structures of precipitation and potential evapotranspiration by sliding over the grid data. (2) In the LSTM: The spatial feature sequence for each time step, extracted by the CNN, is then input into the LSTM to capture the time-lag effects and long-term dependencies of water conditions on vegetation.

In summary, the site data are successfully converted into grid data suitable for the CNN through Kriging spatial interpolation, which establishes the prerequisite for the CNN to extract spatial features. After rigorous preprocessing and spatialization, the precipitation and evapotranspiration data, serving as the core driving factors, are transformed into accurate predictions of vegetation coverage via the CNN-LSTM hybrid model.

Conclusion

Arid regions constitute a substantial portion of Earth’s terrestrial surface, characterized by fragile ecosystems where vegetation coverage serves as a crucial indicator of ecological quality. Vegetation significantly influences regional climate patterns, soil conservation, and biodiversity maintenance while playing a pivotal role in water cycle processes. The accurate prediction of vegetation coverage dynamics is essential for comprehending ecosystem structures and functions in arid regions, as well as for developing scientifically sound ecological conservation and restoration strategies. This study addresses the inherent complexities in predicting vegetation coverage dynamics across arid regions by proposing an innovative CNN-LSTM hybrid modeling approach, with the following key research outcomes:

A hybrid prediction framework integrating SPEI-based arid region classification with CNN-LSTM is developed, utilizing CNN to extract spatial patterns of precipitation and evapotranspiration while employing LSTM to capture temporal dependencies, thereby overcoming traditional methods’ limitations in modeling phenological lags and multi-factor interactions. The established dynamic change index system enables precise quantification of vegetation coverage variations, with both single-year and composite indices demonstrating a 0.95 correlation coefficient with observed vegetation dynamics, accurately reproducing the temporal evolution of vegetation coverage from 2000 to 2022.

Although the CNN-LSTM hybrid model proposed in this study demonstrates strong performance in dynamically predicting vegetation coverage in the Gobi region of Inner Mongolia, several limitations remain that should be addressed in future work:

The model construction and validation are based on the specific environmental conditions of the Gobi region in Inner Mongolia. Although the methodology is generalizable, its direct applicability and predictive accuracy in other types of arid regions (such as the Sahel region in Africa or desert areas in Central Asia) require verification with more extensive geographical datasets.

The model construction and validation are based on the specific environmental conditions of the Gobi region in Inner Mongolia. Although the methodology is generalizable, its direct applicability and predictive accuracy in other types of arid regions (such as the Sahel region in Africa or desert areas in Central Asia) require verification with more extensive geographical datasets.

The current model primarily considers two core climatic driving factors—precipitation and evapotranspiration. However, vegetation dynamics are also significantly influenced by soil texture, groundwater, human activities (such as grazing and afforestation), and more detailed topographic factors. Insufficient consideration of these elements may affect prediction accuracy in certain areas.

Despite the aforementioned limitations, the methods and findings of this study retain important universal value and scientific significance: this study successfully validates the effectiveness of the CNN-LSTM hybrid model framework in addressing the "climate-vegetation" coupling prediction problem. This technical framework itself is transferable and can be adapted to other regions or similar ecological prediction tasks, such as predicting soil moisture and net primary productivity. The study reveals that in extremely arid regions, high-intensity, low-frequency rainfall events represent key hydrological processes driving vegetation changes. This finding underscores that, against the background of increasing extreme precipitation events due to future climate change, understanding the ecological effects of such pulse precipitation events is crucial for predicting the evolution of dryland ecosystems. As simple yet effective quantitative tools, the proposed single-year and comprehensive vegetation coverage dynamic change indices can be widely applied in long-term dynamic monitoring and evaluation of vegetation in other regions, assisting ecological managers in promptly identifying key change stages and long-term trends.
