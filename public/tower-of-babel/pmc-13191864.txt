If you involve me I learn: responsibility-integrated task-based education (RITBE) model in medical intern training
Authors: Ibrahim Eker, Funda Ifakat Tengiz, Yigit Senol, Sifa Sahin, Ahsen Basaran, Fatma Nur Ustun, Sezai Gunes, Buket Cam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional observer-based medical internships can inadequately prepare graduates for independent clinical practice. In departments without resident physicians, the absence of intermediate supervisory layers creates both challenges and unique opportunities for educational innovation. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the Responsibility-Integrated Task-Based Education (RITBE) model in enhancing interns’ self-reported occupational self-efficacy and professionalism attitudes compared with traditional observer-based training.MethodsA quasi-experimental pretest-posttest study with a non-equivalent control group was conducted at Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University Hospital from July 2024 through July 2025. The intervention group (n = 112) received RITBE featuring primary patient assignments, progressive autonomy, and intensive mentorship guided by a departmental study and learning guide. The control group (n = 75) received traditional observer-based training in General Pediatrics. Primary outcomes included occupational self-efficacy beliefs and professionalism attitudes assessed using validated instruments administered at rotation start and completion.ResultsThe RITBE group demonstrated significantly greater improvements in occupational self-efficacy (mean change + 18.7 vs. +3.2, p < 0.001) and professionalism attitudes (mean change + 14.3 vs. +2.8, p < 0.001) compared with controls. The empathy-humanism subscale showed greater improvement in the RITBE group (Cohen’s d = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.32–0.95, p < 0.001), as did the responsibility subscale (Cohen’s d = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.35–0.99, p = 0.002). Interest in pediatric hematology-oncology subspecialization was higher in the RITBE group at rotation completion (42.0% vs. 8.0%, OR = 8.31, 95% CI: 3.36–20.54, p < 0.001). No adverse safety events attributable to intern clinical activities were identified through institutional monitoring systems.ConclusionsThe RITBE model was associated with significantly greater improvements in self-reported occupational self-efficacy and professionalism attitudes compared with traditional training. This structured approach offers a potentially viable framework for medical education, particularly in departments without resident physicians, though confirmation in diverse settings is needed.Keywords: Medical education, Clinical clerkship, Responsibility-based learning, Occupational self-efficacy, Professionalism, Quasi-experimental study

Background

Medical education faces a fundamental challenge: although theoretical knowledge can be transmitted through traditional pedagogical methods, the transformation into being a competent physician requires experiential learning, which many systems fail to provide adequately [1]. This challenge becomes acute during the final year of internship, which is intended to bridge undergraduate education and independent practice but is often characterized by passive observation rather than active participation [2, 3]. The consequences of this educational gap extend beyond individual competence, with documented implications for healthcare systems globally, including effects on patient safety, workforce preparedness, and professional satisfaction [4, 5, 6].

Studies from diverse healthcare systems consistently report that 25–50% of newly graduated physicians feel inadequately prepared for independent practice, demonstrating deficiencies in clinical decision-making, professional identity formation, and management of complex cases [7, 8]. These findings transcend geographical and cultural boundaries, suggesting systemic problems in clinical education that require fundamental rather than incremental reform. The transition from medical student to practicing physician represents one of the most critical junctures in professional development, yet current educational models often leave graduates feeling underprepared for assuming clinical responsibilities [9].

The medical education system in Turkey exemplifies both universal challenges and unique contextual factors. After completing a six-year undergraduate curriculum culminating in mandatory clinical rotations, Turkish students enter a system where numerous specialized departments operate without resident physicians. In the Turkish medical education system, resident physicians (uzman hekim adayi) are postgraduate trainees enrolled in specialty training programs, equivalent to registrars or trainee doctors in other national systems. Their absence in certain departments means that final-year medical students (interns) rotate without an intermediate supervisory layer between themselves and attending faculty [10, 11]. This structural situation eliminates the graded supervisory hierarchy typical of residency-based departments (intern-resident-fellow-attending), replacing it with a flat two-tier structure comprising only interns and attending faculty. While an inherent hierarchical relationship between faculty and interns remains, the absence of intermediate supervisory layers creates both necessity and opportunity for direct faculty-intern interactions and graduated clinical responsibility assignment.

The Turkish language provides conceptual distinctions that illuminate fundamental differences in educational philosophy. The distinction between “gorev” (task) and “sorumluluk” (responsibility) captures the difference between mechanical obligation and professional ownership. While tasks represent assignments completed without personal investment, responsibilities demand active engagement, accountability, and integration into professional identity [12]. Similarly, the distinction between “ogretim” (instruction) and “egitim” (education) differentiates information transfer from transformative development [13]. These linguistic-conceptual frameworks provide theoretical scaffolding for reimagining medical education as a transformative rather than merely informative process.

Contemporary learning theories provide support for educational reform centered on active participation and graduated responsibility. Three theoretical frameworks form the foundation of the present intervention: Kolb’s experiential learning theory, which posits that effective learning occurs through iterative cycles of concrete experience and reflective observation [14] Lave and Wenger’s situated learning theory, which emphasizes knowledge construction within authentic contexts through legitimate peripheral participation [15] and Ryan and Deci’s self-determination theory, which identifies autonomy, competence, and relatedness as fundamental needs driving intrinsic motivation [16]. These frameworks acquire particular significance within the Turkish context, where the structural absence of resident physicians creates conditions that both necessitate and facilitate their implementation. The flat supervisory structure enables authentic peripheral participation, provides direct mastery experiences with expert feedback, and satisfies autonomy needs through genuine clinical responsibility in ways that multi-layered hierarchical systems may inadvertently constrain. Ten Cate’s entrustable professional activities framework was used as an operational tool for structuring progressive autonomy levels [17].

Building upon these foundations, the Responsibility-Integrated Task-Based Education (RITBE) model was developed within the Department of Pediatric Hematology and Oncology at Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University. The model integrates these theoretical frameworks through three practical mechanisms: a comprehensive departmental study and learning guide (structural scaffolding), graduated autonomy with primary patient assignments (experiential learning), and intensive daily mentorship (situated learning within authentic contexts). This study evaluated the effectiveness of the RITBE model through a quasi-experimental study comparing interns trained using this structured responsibility-based approach with those receiving traditional observer-based training. We hypothesized that the RITBE model would be associated with significantly greater improvements in occupational self-efficacy beliefs and professionalism attitudes compared with conventional training, with secondary benefits in clinical performance, specialty interest, and educational experience satisfaction.

Methods

Study design and setting

This quasi-experimental pretest-posttest study with a non-equivalent control group was conducted at Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University Hospital, a 1,100-bed tertiary care academic medical center, from July 2024 through July 2025. The study followed the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Nonrandomized Designs (TREND) guidelines [18] the completed checklist is provided as supplementary material. The intervention was implemented in the Department of Pediatric Hematology and Oncology, whereas the General Pediatrics Department served as the control site.

The Department of Pediatric Hematology and Oncology manages approximately 40–50 inpatients and 200–250 outpatient consultations monthly and provides comprehensive care for children with blood disorders, malignancies, and immunodeficiencies. The department operates an active bone marrow transplantation program. Critically, the department operates without resident physicians, with a faculty-to-intern ratio of approximately 1:4–5 (two attending faculty supervising 8–10 interns per rotation). The General Pediatrics Department, serving as the control site, manages a larger volume of patients with a broader diagnostic spectrum. Unlike the intervention department, General Pediatrics operates with resident physicians who serve as intermediate supervisors, creating a traditional hierarchical training structure. This departmental difference is an important contextual factor that should be considered when interpreting results, as differences in case complexity, faculty accessibility, and the supervisory environment may independently influence learning outcomes.

Participants and allocation

In the Turkish medical education system, sixth-year medical students undertake a mandatory clinical clerkship year referred to as the internship (intornluk). These students are designated as “interns” throughout this manuscript. Eligible participants were interns scheduled for pediatric rotation during the study period. Rotation assignments were determined administratively based on predetermined schedules independent of student preferences or performance. While this process was not truly randomized, it was independent of student characteristics, thereby reducing (but not eliminating) the risk of systematic selection bias. Of the 198 eligible interns, 187 (94.4%) consented and completed all assessments. The intervention group comprised 112 interns assigned to the Department of Pediatric Hematology and Oncology, whereas controls included 75 assigned to the Department of General Pediatrics. Students assigned to General Pediatrics had no exposure to the RITBE model at any point in their curriculum, as the model was exclusively implemented in the Department of Pediatric Hematology and Oncology.

The RITBE intervention: structure and implementation

The RITBE model represents a comprehensive educational intervention grounded in three complementary theoretical frameworks: Kolb’s experiential learning cycle emphasizing concrete experience and active experimentation [14] Lave and Wenger’s situated learning theory highlighting legitimate peripheral participation in authentic clinical contexts [15] and Ten Cate’s entrustable professional activity framework as an operational tool for structuring progressive autonomy [17].

A cornerstone was the department-specific study and learning guide, a 45-page document that detailed the model’s philosophy, expected competency progression, core clinical skills, assessment criteria, and educational resources. This guide was introduced during a structured three-hour welcome session conducted on the first rotation day by the senior author (IE) using a standardized presentation, establishing clear expectations for active participation and graduated responsibility assumption.

The intervention integrated five complementary components implemented systematically throughout the one-month rotation (Table 1). Throughout the rotation, interns received consistent messaging involving three core themes communicated repeatedly through daily rounds, feedback sessions, and informal interactions: (a) assigned clinical responsibilities represented genuine professional obligations integral to patient care rather than educational exercises, (b) patient care quality depended meaningfully on each intern’s active engagement, and (c) interns were valued members of the clinical team rather than transient observers.

Implementation fidelity was maintained through several mechanisms: all interns received the identical 45-page departmental study and learning guide; the structured three-hour orientation session was delivered by the same faculty member using a standardized presentation; daily morning rounds followed a consistent format; and weekly feedback sessions used a structured evaluation form. Minor variations in clinical exposure occurred naturally based on patient census and case mix.

The control group experienced the traditional rotation structure, characterized by observational roles during attending rounds, restricted direct patient interaction limited to shadowing, absence of assigned patient responsibilities, passive involvement in clinical decisions, and traditional didactic lectures with mentorship that varied depending on attending availability.

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes were assessed using two validated Turkish instruments administered on the first day and final day of rotation (Day 28–30), immediately following the completion of clinical activities. No follow-up assessments were conducted. The occupational self-efficacy beliefs questionnaire for newly-graduated physicians comprises 16 items measured on a dichotomous scale across domains: patient communication, clinical examination, diagnostic reasoning, treatment planning, procedural skills, medical documentation, technology utilization, ethical decision-making, teamwork, time management, stress management, lifelong learning, critical thinking, management skills, patient safety, and professional development (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.84) [19].

The professionalism attitude scale consists of 22 items measured on a 5-point Likert scale across three subscales: empathy-humanism (9 items, alpha = 0.86), professionalism (8 items, alpha = 0.82), and responsibility (5 items, alpha = 0.79). Items assess attitudes regarding patient-centered care, emotional engagement, ethical obligations, professional boundaries, accountability, and continuous improvement [20].

Secondary outcomes included objective clinical performance indicators (patients independently managed, procedures performed under supervision, documentation quality scores), specialty interest assessed through direct questioning at rotation completion, and qualitative feedback collected through anonymous open-text response forms.

Safety monitoring

Patient safety was monitored through three complementary mechanisms: (a) the hospital’s institutional incident reporting system, which captures medication errors, procedural complications, and near-miss events; (b) daily attending supervision logs documenting all intern clinical activities and their outcomes; and (c) weekly departmental morbidity-mortality review.

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation anticipated a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5) for primary outcomes. With alpha = 0.05 and power = 0.80, the minimum required sample was 64 per group. The obtained sample (intervention group, n = 112; control group, n = 75) exceeded this requirement. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). As primary outcome measures were assessed on ordinal scales (dichotomous for occupational self-efficacy; 5-point Likert for professionalism attitudes), non-parametric tests were used throughout. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare pre- and post-rotation scores within each group to determine whether observed changes were statistically significant. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the magnitude of pre-to-post change scores between the RITBE and control groups. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d with 95% confidence intervals and odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with Bonferroni correction applied for the two primary outcome comparisons (adjusted alpha = 0.025). Item-level analyses are presented as exploratory and descriptive.

Qualitative feedback was analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis following the six-phase framework described by Braun and Clarke [21]. Two researchers (IE and FNU) independently coded the open-text responses. Initial codes were generated inductively, then organized into candidate themes through iterative discussion. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus meetings involving a third researcher (FIT), who has expertise in qualitative research methodology in medical education. The final thematic framework was reviewed against the coded data to ensure coherence and representativeness.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee (Protocol No. 2024 − 261, Date: May 30, 2024). All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Self-reported occupational self-efficacy

The intervention group showed significant improvement in 13 of 16 self-efficacy items, whereas the control group improved in 10. Three domains improved exclusively in the intervention group compared with controls: patient communication skills (58.0% to 74.1%, p = 0.003 vs. 60.0% to 66.7%, p = 0.287), technology utilization (75.0% to 90.2%, p = 0.049 vs. 76.0% to 80.0%, p = 0.534), and critical thinking (79.5% to 92.9%, p = 0.031 vs. 77.3% to 84.0%, p = 0.196). The largest between-group difference was observed in management skills, with the intervention group improving from 70.5% to 92.9% (p < 0.001) compared with controls from 73.3% to 82.7% (p < 0.05). These item-level results are presented as exploratory findings to identify patterns of differential improvement.

Professionalism attitudes

The empathy-humanism subscale showed a mean improvement of 2.53 +/- 1.70 points in the intervention group compared with 1.41 +/- 1.81 in the control group (p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.32–0.95). The responsibility subscale demonstrated differences of similar magnitude, with a mean improvement of 1.00 +/- 1.39 in the RITBE group compared with 0.17 +/- 1.07 in the control group (p = 0.002, Cohen’s d = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.35–0.99).

Clinical performance and specialty interest

The intervention group independently managed a mean of 42.3 +/- 8.7 patients compared with none in the control group (p < 0.001). RITBE interns performed 8.3 +/- 2.1 supervised procedures compared with 2.1 +/- 1.3 by controls (p < 0.001). Documentation quality scores were higher in the intervention group (89.2 +/- 7.4) than in the control group (76.3 +/- 9.8, p < 0.001).

At rotation completion, 42.0% of interns in the intervention group (47 of 112) indicated that they would consider specializing in pediatric hematology-oncology, compared with 8.0% in the control group (6 of 75) expressing interest in general pediatrics subspecialization (Table 5). Pre-rotation specialty interest was not formally assessed, which limits interpretation of this finding. This result should be interpreted as a short-term attitudinal measure assessed immediately after an intensive rotation experience rather than a predictor of actual career choice.

Safety outcomes

No adverse events attributable to intern clinical activities were identified through the hospital’s incident reporting system, attending supervision logs, or departmental morbidity-mortality review in either group during the study period. This finding should be interpreted with caution, as the absence of reported events does not constitute definitive evidence of safety equivalence.

Qualitative feedback

Thematic analysis of anonymous open-text responses identified five predominant themes (Table 6): clarity of expectations, sense of value and integration, motivation through responsibility, anxiety reduction through structured support, and professional identity development. These themes were identified independently by two coders and confirmed through consensus review with a third researcher.

Discussion

This quasi-experimental study provides evidence that the RITBE model was associated with greater improvements in self-reported occupational self-efficacy and professionalism attitudes among medical interns compared with traditional observer-based training. The findings support the theoretical foundations underlying this educational approach, though the non-randomized design and departmental context differences warrant caution in causal interpretation.

Theoretical validation and mechanistic insights

The substantial differences observed between groups are consistent with our framework emphasizing responsibility over tasks and education over instruction. The significant improvement in the responsibility subscale suggests that a sense of professional accountability may be more effectively developed through experiential approaches than through passive observation. This pattern aligns with self-determination theory’s emphasis on autonomy as a fundamental psychological need [16].

The enhanced empathy-humanism subscale scores challenge assumptions that clinical responsibility might lead to emotional detachment. The observed pattern suggests that meaningful patient relationships through sustained responsibility may deepen empathetic connection. Importantly, this improvement is most likely attributable to the sustained patient-care relationships and intensive daily faculty mentorship interactions rather than the written guide alone. The guide provided the structural framework, but the transformative element appears to have been the authentic human connection between interns, patients, and faculty mentors inherent in the RITBE model’s design. Time spent with faculty was not formally measured in either group, which limits the ability to disentangle the contributions of mentorship quantity from other intervention components.

The mechanisms underlying these improvements likely involve multiple synergistic processes. Mastery experiences through progressive patient management may have strengthened efficacy beliefs consistent with Bandura’s theory [22]. Situated learning within authentic clinical contexts may have facilitated knowledge construction that passive observation cannot replicate [15]. Professional identity formation likely accelerated as interns transitioned from peripheral observers to legitimate participants in clinical practice [23].

Addressing Turkish medical education challenges

This study addresses a critical challenge in Turkish medical education: training interns effectively in departments without resident physicians. The RITBE model transforms this apparent constraint into an educational opportunity by facilitating direct attending-intern mentorship and graduated responsibility. The flat two-tier supervisory structure enables daily direct faculty-intern interaction, consistent expert modeling of clinical reasoning, enhanced opportunities for immediate corrective feedback, and more rapid professional identity formation. The observation that RITBE interns independently managed a mean of 42.3 patients whereas control interns managed none illustrates how structural necessity, coupled with deliberate educational design, can generate different learning experiences.

Specialty interest

At rotation completion, the proportion of interns expressing interest in pediatric hematology-oncology subspecialization was significantly higher in the RITBE group. While this finding is noteworthy, it represents a short-term attitudinal measure assessed immediately after an intensive rotation experience and should not be interpreted as evidence of actual career decision-making. The result may reflect the positive learning experience, the novelty of active participation, or the supportive departmental environment rather than a sustained career preference. Pre-rotation specialty interest was not assessed, further limiting interpretation. Longitudinal follow-up studies are essential to determine whether these expressed intentions translate into actual training program applications and career choices.

International applicability

Although developed within the Turkish context, the present findings have potential relevance to other settings facing similar structural challenges, including departments without resident physicians, new academic programs establishing training structures, and rural or underserved settings with limited supervisory hierarchies [24]. However, this is a single-center study conducted in a specific structural context, and generalizability to established residency-based systems where existing hierarchical cultures, duty hour regulations, and accreditation requirements may present different implementation challenges should not be assumed [25]. Multicenter adaptation studies are needed before broader claims can be made.

Implementation considerations

The RITBE model requires substantial faculty investment. During this study, two attending faculty dedicated approximately 2–3 additional hours per day to mentorship activities beyond their usual clinical duties, yielding a faculty-to-intern ratio of approximately 1:4–5. The departmental study and learning guide required approximately 200 faculty hours for initial development, though subsequent maintenance was minimal. These resource requirements may limit transferability to departments with higher patient volumes or fewer faculty members. Future implementation studies should explicitly assess faculty workload impact and identify strategies for sustainable delivery.

Cultural change represents a significant implementation barrier. Traditional medical education culture often positions students as passive recipients of knowledge rather than active participants in patient care [26, 27]. Shifting this culture requires consistent institutional leadership support, incorporation of responsibility-based education into curricula, and recognition of faculty teaching excellence through graduated responsibility models [28]. Addressing legitimate concerns about patient safety through demonstrated evidence combined with appropriate supervision structures remains essential.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant careful consideration. First, the non-randomized design introduces the possibility of selection bias and unmeasured confounders despite similar baseline characteristics. Although rotation assignments were administratively determined independent of student preferences, systematic differences in student motivation or prior experiences cannot be excluded. Future studies should consider cluster randomization or stepped-wedge designs.

Second, the use of the General Pediatrics department as the control site introduces the possibility that observed differences partially reflect departmental context rather than the RITBE intervention alone. Differences in case complexity, faculty availability, the presence of resident physicians in General Pediatrics, and departmental teaching culture may have contributed to outcome differences independently of the educational model. The ideal comparison would involve students within the same department before and after RITBE implementation.

Third, reliance on self-reported measures for primary outcomes introduces susceptibility to social desirability bias, response shift, and the Hawthorne effect. Improvements in self-efficacy beliefs may not directly translate to observable performance improvements. Future studies should incorporate objective assessments such as standardized patient encounters, workplace-based assessments, and direct observation with structured evaluation instruments.

Fourth, the one-month rotation period limits assessment of outcome durability. Initial improvements in self-reported efficacy may diminish as the novelty effect fades, or alternatively may consolidate as interns encounter subsequent clinical challenges. Longitudinal follow-up is essential to determine the persistence and trajectory of observed effects.

Fifth, post-rotation assessments were administered on the final rotation day, capturing immediate post-intervention effects that may not represent sustained changes. The absence of follow-up measurements prevents assessment of whether benefits persist beyond the rotation period.

Finally, although multiple safety monitoring mechanisms were in place, the study was not powered to detect rare adverse events, and the absence of reported incidents should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of safety equivalence between approaches.

Future directions

Three concrete directions emerge from the present findings. First, focused qualitative inquiry using semi-structured interviews would provide deeper understanding of the mechanisms through which RITBE fosters professional identity formation and attitudinal change, offering insights that quantitative measurement alone cannot capture. Second, longitudinal follow-up of study participants through residency training would determine whether rotation-level benefits persist and influence career choices. Third, adaptation studies in at least one additional department (ideally a surgical specialty with different skill emphasis) would clarify the model’s transferability beyond pediatric hematology-oncology.

Conclusions

The RITBE model was associated with significantly greater improvements in self-reported occupational self-efficacy and professionalism attitudes among medical interns compared with traditional observer-based training. By transforming structural constraints into educational opportunities through graduated responsibility, comprehensive scaffolding, and intensive mentorship, this approach offers a potentially viable framework for medical education reform, particularly in departments without traditional hierarchical training structures. The model’s theoretical grounding in experiential learning, situated cognition, and self-determination theory provides conceptual coherence for implementation, though confirmation in diverse settings and with objective outcome measures is needed to establish generalizability and to determine whether self-reported improvements translate into sustained clinical competence gains.
