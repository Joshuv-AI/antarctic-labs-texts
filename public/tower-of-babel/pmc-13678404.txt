Financial harms of regular gaming: Cross-national evidence from the international gaming survey
Authors: Benjamin Johnson, Gary Chung Kai Chan, John B Saunders, Daniel Stjepanović, Wim van den Brink, Caitlin McClure-Thomas, Jiang Long, Kristiana Siste, Min Zhao, Na Zhong, Pim Widdershoven, Anne Marije Kaag, Janni Leung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsGaming disorder is recognised in ICD-11 as an addictive disorder associated with persistent gaming leading to functional impairment. While many video games include monetised features such as microtransactions and other in-game purchases, financial harms are rarely examined as a potential negative consequence. The present study examined associations between higher versus lower risk of gaming disorder and two financial outcomes: game-related overspending and financial harm.MethodsData were from the International Gaming Study 2024, a cross-sectional survey of 1,522 adult regular gamers from Australia (n = 303), China (n = 314), Indonesia (n = 300), Netherlands (n = 303) and the United States (n = 302). Gaming disorder was screened using the Gaming Disorder Identification Test (GADIT), with participants classified into higher-risk (≥5) versus lower-risk (<5) groups.ResultsIn adjusted logistic models, membership in the higher-risk group was strongly associated with overspending (OR = 5.36, 95% CI: 3.31–8.69) and financial harm (OR = 7.76, 95% CI: 4.88–12.35). More specifically, among participants in the higher-risk group 80.2% reported overspending and 77.8% reported financial harm compared to 42.6 and 30.6% in the lower-risk group. Odds of financial harms were higher in China and Indonesia than in Australia, while odds in the United States and Netherlands did not differ significantly from Australia.Discussion and ConclusionsFinancial harms from gaming are common and most pronounced among individuals at higher risk of gaming disorder but are not limited to this group. These findings highlight the need for greater awareness of the risk for gaming-related financial harm, alongside strengthened consumer protection measures.Keywords: gaming disorder, financial impact, IGD

Introduction

Gaming disorder (GD) was included in the 11th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) as a clinically recognisable disorder due to addictive behaviour (WHO, 2024). It is defined as a persistent or recurrent pattern of gaming behaviour, characterised by impaired control over gaming, increasing priority given to gaming over other activities, and continuation or escalation of gaming despite negative consequences, resulting in marked distress or significant impairment in personal, family, social, educational or occupational functioning over at least 12 months (WHO, 2024). Research indicates that gamers meeting criteria for GD show substantial functional impairment and complications, including poorer mental health, sleep problems and disruptions to work, study and relationships compared with gamers not meeting criteria for GD (Billieux et al., 2017). Meta-analyses suggest that gaming disorder is present in roughly 2–3% of the population worldwide, although estimates vary considerably by region, age group and assessment method, with higher rates typically observed in adolescents and males and in studies with GD diagnoses based on self-report questionnaires (Stevens, Dorstyn, Delfabbro, & King, 2021).

Over the past decade, the economics of video games have shifted from upfront fixed-price purchases to the emergence of monetisation models that encourage recurring in-game spending after initial access. These include microtransactions, loot boxes and gacha mechanics, which can encourage repeated purchases through incremental or randomised rewards and have been described as predatory monetisation schemes with a serious potential for financial harm (King & Delfabbro, 2018; Xiao, Newall, & James, 2024). Despite these concerns, existing screening tools for gaming disorder largely omit financial harms. In a systematic review of 32 gaming disorder instruments, only five included any items on negative financial consequences, such as gaming-related debt or overspending, with most scales instead focusing on emotional distress, social dysfunction or physical impairments (King et al., 2020).

Although prior studies have examined loot boxes, impulsive spending, and game-related overspending, less is known about whether gaming-related expenditure translates into broader financial burden. Much of the existing literature and policy discussion has focused on loot boxes or other gambling-like mechanics. For example, the Belgian Gaming Commission concluded in 2018 that some paid loot boxes met the definition of games of chance under Belgian gambling law, illustrating how regulatory attention has often centred on loot box mechanics (Belgian Gaming Commission, 2018). However, monetised gaming environments extend beyond loot boxes and include repeated in-game purchases and micro-transactions. Financial harms from gaming may therefore arise through a broader set of spending mechanisms than loot boxes alone. Evidence on these broader financial consequences is needed to inform policy and consumer protection responses that address the wider monetisation environment in contemporary gaming.

Analysis of data from the 2022 International Gaming Study (IGS22) reported that people meeting, or at higher risk of meeting, ICD-11 criteria for GD had substantially higher odds of reporting that they overspent, defined as perceiving that they spent more than they should on games and in-game purchases (Leung et al., 2025). However, this measure captures perceived deviation from intended spending rather than whether such spending was experienced as financially burdensome or disruptive to everyday life. As a result, the extent to which elevated game-related spending translated into perceived financial hardship remained unclear. In addition, as the study was conducted exclusively in high-income Western countries, it remains unclear whether these associations generalise to countries with different cultural and economic contexts.

The present study aimed to address research gaps by estimating the association between risk of gaming disorder and two distinct financial outcomes: perceived game-related overspending and self-reported financial harms from gaming. By assessing multiple forms of game-related overspending and financial burden separately, the study sought to provide an evidence base on gaming-related financial harms beyond loot boxes and gambling-like mechanics.

Methods

Data source

IGS24 is a multi-country investigation of adults who play video games, designed to answer research questions on how to assess Gaming Disorder, which individuals are most affected, and what the negative consequences of GD are. The survey was originally developed in English, then translated into Chinese, Dutch, and Indonesian, with translation quality assessed through consensus-and back-translation to English and comparison with the original version.

Participants

Participants were 1,522 adults aged 18–59 who played video games for at least 3 h per week, recruited through the Nielsen Global Insight online panel (Nielsen, 2026) in Australia, China, Indonesia, the Netherlands and the United States. A quota of 300 participants per country was applied, set to recruit 150 participants who reported being sex-assigned male and 150 who reported female. A quota for each age group was also applied to over-sample younger individuals (50% of sample <31 years of age; 25% of sample 31–45 years of age; 25% > 45 years of age). Total country sample sizes slightly exceeded 300 as some participants reported another sex or did not specify. The initial sample screening was completed by Nielsen prior to data collection and was confirmed by eligibility screening within the survey, where playing less than 3 h per week or falling outside the eligible age range were exclusion criteria. Eligible participants completed the online self-report survey in the dominant language of their country (Chinese, Dutch, English or Indonesian). As recruitment used an online panel with quotas for age and sex, the sample is not intended to be nationally representative for education or other sociodemographic characteristics.

Measures

Gaming disorder

Risk of GD was assessed using the Gaming Disorder Identification Test (GADIT), a validated brief instrument aligned with the ICD-11 diagnostic framework (Chan, Saunders, et al., 2024). The translated GADIT is provided in Supplementary Materials 1. The scale comprises 8 items with 2 items assessing each of the 4 core gaming disorder features, comprising impaired control, increasing priority, continued gaming despite harms, and functional impairment. Participants were instructed to indicate how frequently in the past 12 months they experienced an outcome on a 5-point Likert-scale: “Every day/Most days/Some days/Rarely/Never”. Responses of every day or most days were coded as a score of 1, and some days, rarely, or never were coded as 0. A total score was derived by adding these 8 dichotomous scores (range 0–8), and participants with a score ≥5 were categorised as at higher risk of GD. Those who scored <5 were classified as lower-risk of GD (Chan, Saunders, et al., 2024).

Game related overspending and financial harms

The financial harm items were developed by the authorship team for the International Gaming Study, which included researchers with expertise in gaming disorder, behavioural addictions, gambling-related harms, addiction epidemiology, and lived experience of gaming addiction. The items were designed to capture face-valid financial consequences that may arise from gaming or gaming-related purchases in the past 12 months. Participants were asked whether gaming or gaming-related purchases had: (1) led them to prioritise gaming over important financial commitments; (2) created financial pressure, such as accruing debt, missing bill payments, borrowing money, or taking extra work to finance gaming; or (3) resulted in serious financial consequences, such as significant debt, sale of assets, inability to afford essential expenses, or reliance on financial support from others. Each item was assessed using a binary yes/no response format. Internal consistency was high for these three items (Cronbach's alpha = 0.80). Participants were classified as experiencing financial harms if they endorsed any of these indicators. Game-related overspending was assessed separately by asking whether participants had spent more money than they should have on gaming-related expenditures; overspending was coded as present if participants endorsed overspending on in-game purchases, loot boxes, microtransactions, or in-game gambling. The Cronbach's alpha for these four items was 0.75.

Sample characteristics

Sample characteristics included sex, marital status, parental status, highest level of education, age and employment or study status (working and/or enrolled in study vs neither).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted via Stata/SE 18 (Stata, 2026). Cross-tabulations were used to examine the distribution of game-related overspending and perceived financial harm by risk of gaming disorder, country, and sample characteristics. Group differences were assessed using chi-square tests, with missing data excluded pairwise for descriptive statistics. Levels of missingness were low, ranging from 0.20 to 0.33% across variables, and were therefore unlikely to introduce meaningful bias. For the regression analyses, missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations, with 20 imputed datasets generated. Multiple binary logistic regression models were fitted to examine factors associated with overspending (Model 1) and financial harm (Model 2), respectively. Explanatory variables included risk of gaming disorder, country, and sample characteristics. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 99.38% confidence intervals (CIs). This stringent confidence level was applied to account for multiple comparisons. We also conducted supplementary analysis using the gaming disorder measure (GADIT) as a continuous variable and the results were presented in the Supplementary Table 3.

Ethics

Ethical approval was obtained from The University of Queensland, Australia (2024/HE000079), and from institutional review boards in each of the participating countries.

Overall, overspending and financial harm differed by country (Table 2; both p < .001) with the highest proportions reported in China (overspending 63.3%; financial harm 41.4%) and Indonesia (overspending 59.2%; financial harm 49.5%).

Overspending also differed by sex, parental status, and work or study status (Table 2). Males reported overspending more often than females (53.3% vs 43.9%), participants with children more than those without (51.4% vs 45.6%), and those working or studying more than those not working or studying (50.7% vs 39.0%). In contrast, financial harm did not differ by these characteristics (all p > .05). Overspending and financial harm were more common in younger age groups: overspending was 51.7% in those aged 18–25 and 56.3% in those aged 26–40 versus 36.5% in those aged 40–59, while financial harm was 44.6 and 43.8% versus 25.8%, respectively (Table 2; both p < .001).

We have conducted item-level analyses and presented the findings in the Appendix Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. Participants at higher risk of gaming disorder were more likely to endorse each individual financial burden and game-related overspending item.

Adjusted logistic regression results showed that higher risk of gaming disorder was strongly associated with both financial harm outcomes (Table 3). Individuals at higher-risk of GD had significantly higher odds of reporting overspending (OR = 5.36) and financial harm (OR = 7.76) compared with those at lower-risk. With Australia as the reference category, odds were higher in China (overspending OR = 3.00; harm OR = 1.78) and Indonesia (overspending OR = 2.48; harm OR = 2.53) but not for the Netherlands and the United States (Table 3). Females had lower odds of overspending than males (OR = 0.65), but sex was not associated with financial harms. Participants aged 41–59 years had significantly lower odds of both overspending (OR = 0.49) and financial harm (OR = 0.42) compared with those aged 18–25 years. Marital status, employment or study status, education level, and parental status were not significantly associated with either outcome after adjustment.

Discussion

This cross-national study examined the association between risk of gaming disorder and two financial outcomes: self-reported game-related overspending and perceived financial harm from gaming. We also aimed to examine whether these associations and the prevalence of financial harms varied across countries and key sociodemographic subgroups, including age group and sex.

In models adjusted for country, sex, age group, marital status, employment/study status, education level, and parental status, participants at higher risk of gaming disorder had more than five times higher odds of reporting overspending and almost eight times higher odds of reporting financial harm compared with those at lower risk of gaming disorder. These adjusted associations indicate that financial harms from gaming are strongly concentrated among individuals at higher risk of gaming disorder. Nonetheless, the prevalence of financial harms in the lower-risk group is still substantial.

Our results closely align with earlier analyses of the IGS22, which found that individuals at higher-risk of GD had approximately six to nine times higher odds of reporting different forms of game-related overspending (Leung et al., 2025), and extend this work in two important ways. First, we replicated the robust link between gaming disorder risk and overspending in a broader multi-national sample, while also observing cross-national variations in financial harms. Second, by including a separate indicator of perceived financial harm, we showed that overspending is not merely a matter of regretted discretionary spending but is frequently experienced as genuine financial strain, with individuals at higher-risk of gaming disorder almost eight times more likely to report that gaming created financial harm than those at lower-risk. This pattern suggests that for a substantial subgroup, problematic gaming involves material financial harms in addition to psychosocial and health impairments, supporting the future inclusion of financial harm items in gaming disorder measures in adult samples (Chan, Saunders, et al., 2024).

A key contribution of this study is that it broadens the focus beyond loot boxes and perceived overspending. Previous research has largely focused on loot box engagement, gambling-like mechanics, or whether players spend more than they intended. The present study assessed multiple forms of game-related overspending and, importantly, separately examined self-reported financial burden. The financial burden items captured more consequential experiences, including prioritising gaming over important financial commitments, financial pressure, and serious financial consequences. This provides an initial empirical basis for considering gaming-related financial harm as a broader issue within monetised gaming environments, rather than only a loot box-specific problem.

The higher odds of overspending and self-reported financial harm observed in China and Indonesia suggest that gaming-related financial burden may vary across countries, and this difference exists after adjusting for gaming disorder risk. One possible explanation is that financial consequences are amplified by economic context: China and Indonesia are classified as upper-middle-income economies, whereas Australia, the United States and the Netherlands are high-income economies (World Bank, 2023). Comparable levels of game spending may therefore represent a larger share of disposable income and be more readily experienced as overspending or financial strain. Differences in local game markets and monetisation models may also contribute. Asian markets are dominated by free-to-play mobile games with intensive in-app purchase and gacha-style monetisation (Google & Niko Partners, 2020), which create frequent opportunities for small, repeated purchases that can accumulate into problematic spending. However, an alternative explanation is that the observed country differences partly reflect cultural variation in how participants interpreted subjective concepts such as overspending, financial pressure, and serious financial consequences. The financial harm items were not formally tested for measurement invariance across countries, so these cross-national findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating. Future research should identify the specific game types, monetisation environments, and cultural interpretations of financial burden that contribute to gaming-related financial harm.

Females had significantly lower odds of reporting game-related overspending than males. This pattern suggests that risky game spending is more common among males, consistent with meta-analytic evidence that gaming-related harms are more prevalent in males (Chew & Wong, 2022) and that males spend more time and money on games overall and are more likely to use monetised features such as loot boxes (Liao et al., 2025; Wilson, Butler, Quigg, & Sumnall, 2024). Males may be more vulnerable to reward-driven in-game purchasing because they report higher impulsivity and risk-taking, whereas females report greater perceived downside and lower expected enjoyment, which may reduce spending (Cross, Copping, & Campbell, 2011; Harris & Jenkins, 2006). Another plausible reason is sex differences in gaming genre and platform, with females tending more towards casual (74.8%) and social games (24%) which are commonly free-to-play and can be played on their mobile phones, compared to competitive and action genres with high-priced consoles and PC games targeted to males (Lange, Wühr, & Schwarz, 2021; Rennick & Roberts, 2025).

At the same time, there was no sex difference in financial harm. This may reflect broader sex-related economic inequalities. Females on average earn less, are more likely to work part time, perform more unpaid care work, accumulate less wealth over the life course, and face substantially higher risks of poverty in midlife and older age (Women's Economic Equality Taskforce, 2023). Females also report higher financial anxiety and may experience more consumer guilt which may amplify their perceived spending harm albeit spending less nominally compared to males (Kayal, Simintiras, & Rana, 2017). Therefore, even relatively limited game-related overspending may be harder for females to absorb financially.

Gaming-related financial harms were similar for 18–25 and 26–40-year-old participants, but people aged 41–59 were less likely to both overspend on games and experience their spending as a financial harm. This suggests that gaming-related financial harms are concentrated in early and mid-adulthood and only start to decline once people reach their forties and fifties, possibly reflecting more stable finances or more cautious spending in this group. One explanation is that older adults generally make fewer in-game microtransactions: purchase likelihood declines with age, and minors and young adults tend to buy loot boxes more frequently and spend more than adults (Dai, Huang, Nagasawa, Sawada, & Oshio, 2024; Kim et al., 2023). Younger adults also engage more with the payment technologies used for in-app purchases: in the Reserve Bank of Australia's 2022 Consumer Payments Survey, almost two-thirds of Australians aged 18–29 used mobile payments in the diary week, compared with substantially lower rates among older groups (Nguyen & Watson, 2023).

Limitations

Although this study provides important information on financial harm, our sample did not include adolescents under 18 years or adults aged 60 years and over, so we could not examine these two groups and determine if they have distinct patterns of play and therefore differed in vulnerability to financial harm. Additionally, we did not collect information on the genres of games participants primarily played. This is important because game genre is likely to shape players' exposure to monetisation mechanisms and different games might be popular in different countries (ref?).

The financial burden items were author-generated and have not yet been formally validated as a gaming-specific financial harm scale. Although the items had strong face validity and showed high internal consistency in the present sample, future research should develop and validate more comprehensive measures of gaming-related financial harm. Such work could draw on the gambling harms literature, where validated instruments have been developed to assess financial harms across multiple levels of severity.

Furthermore, we assessed perceived overspending and harm rather than absolute expenditure or spending as a proportion of income, which limits interpretation of the severity and real-world impact of gaming-related financial problems. Subjective financial strain is conceptually distinct from objective financial conditions because it captures perceived manageability and stress. However, research comparing self-reported financial wellbeing with objective or administrative financial indicators suggests these measures are typically correlated, supporting the use of perceived harm as a proxy for hardship, although they are not interchangeable. Future studies should incorporate objective expenditure and income-linked metrics to quantify severity and test concordance with perceived harm (Comerton-Forde et al., 2022). Additionally, future research should expand the sample to older individuals, collect detailed data on the games, platforms and monetisation features that people engage with, and incorporate granular measures of spending such as monthly dollar amounts and percentage of income spent on gaming. Income should also be measured in future waves of the IGS to test whether age and country differences in financial harm are partly explained by underlying income differences.

In addition, the prevalence estimates should be interpreted cautiously. The overspending measure captured perceived spending beyond what participants felt they should have spent and may therefore include post-purchase regret or perceived excessive discretionary spending. Given that our sample comprised regular gamers who played at least 3 h of games per week, the percentage cannot be interpreted as population statistics and can only be interpreted as sample characteristics in this study. Lastly, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Well-designed longitudinal studies are required to test temporal ordering and causal mechanisms (Chan et al., 2022; Chan, Sun, et al., 2024).

Conclusion

This cross-national study showed that financial harms from gaming are common and observed across all groups and were more pronounced among people at higher risk of gaming disorder, who had substantially higher odds of both overspending and experiencing gaming as a financial harm. Financial impacts were more likely in younger adults, males, and players in China and Indonesia, suggesting that age, sex and economic context shape who is most exposed to risky monetisation. A substantial minority of participants who were in the lower-risk profile groups still reported overspending and perceived financial harm from gaming. Exposure to heavily monetised gaming systems may be common and it may lead to financial strain across a broad spectrum of players. These findings highlight the need for greater awareness of gaming-related financial burden and suggest that consumer protection discussions should consider the broader monetisation environment, rather than focusing only on loot boxes or gambling-like mechanics.
