Presentations of idiopathic hemorrhagic diarrhea syndrome in dogs in relation to meteorological factors
Authors: Michelle Karnstedt, Amna Asif, Terence Z Sibanda, Romy M Heilmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCauses for idiopathic acute hemorrhagic diarrhea syndrome (AHDS) in dogs remain uncertain. Recent discoveries suggest a role for enterotoxin-producing Clostridium perfringens, but the triggers for toxin production and AHDS onset are unknown. Meteorological factors have not been examined as a possible AHDS trigger.Hypothesis/ObjectivesRetrospective case–control study aiming to examine the effects of weather conditions and weather changes on the prevalence of idiopathic AHDS.AnimalsDogs (n = 412) presented with acute gastrointestinal (GI) clinical signs between January 2016 and January 2021 at a tertiary veterinary care center.MethodsDogs were stratified into 3 groups: idiopathic AHDS, acute gastroenteritis, and other causes for acute GI signs. This categorization was based on the combination of patient history, physical examination, and further diagnostic evaluation (eg, laboratory and imaging diagnostics). Meteorological data were extracted for each dog based on the geocodes of their primary location and were analyzed for associations with AHDS presentations by building a random forest model and calculating odds ratios.ResultsIncreased wind speeds and shortwave radiation exposure, lower temperatures and sunlight intensity, and dry weather were linked to increased idiopathic AHDS presentations in dogs in the investigated geographical region.Conclusions and clinical importanceOur explorative study provides an outlook for prospective investigations to further evaluate potential effects of meteorological factors on the gut-brain-microbiome axis and their role in the development of idiopathic AHDS. In addition to shedding more light on pathogenetic mechanisms of an idiopathic condition, understanding these environmental and weather-related effects could be important in predicting and managing this condition.

Introduction

Idiopathic acute hemorrhagic diarrhea syndrome (AHDS) is an acute, potentially life-threatening gastrointestinal (GI) disorder with an onset of sudden hemorrhagic diarrhea and a substantial fluid loss into the intestinal lumen.1 Clinical findings depend on the severity of dehydration and potential complications at the time of presentation. Some features can help differentiate idiopathic AHDS from other conditions, such as vomiting as the first clinical sign in most cases, rapid clinical improvement with adequate supportive care, and the disease not being transmissible.1 Blood loss in patients with AHDS is usually minor in comparison to the loss of fluid into the intestinal lumen,2 causing affected dogs to develop marked hemoconcentration as a hallmark sign of idiopathic AHDS.1 In uncomplicated idiopathic AHDS, leukogram changes from systemic bacterial infection or inflammation are lacking, and the presence of an inflammatory leukogram should raise suspicion of an enteropathogen infection or pancreatitis as the cause of hemorrhagic acute diarrhea (AD).1 Establishing a diagnosis of idiopathic AHDS requires clinical observations and the exclusion of other causes of AD.1

The exact etiology of idiopathic AHDS remains unknown. However, clostridial overgrowth and the release of specific toxins causing GI tissue damage (eg, Clostridium perfringens enterotoxin) are presumed to contribute to the pathology.1 Necrotizing enteritis toxin F (NetF) of C. perfringens, a β-pore-forming toxin that destroys the plasma membrane of susceptible cells, is suspected to be a major virulence factor in idiopathic AHDS.3 The exact trigger for the production and release of this toxin remains unknown.1 Dietary factors, GI motility changes, stress, and other yet-to-be-identified factors may affect or initiate episodes of idiopathic AHDS as multifactorial conditions.1,4

The gut–brain axis is a focus of recent investigations centered on neurologic effects,5–7 but reverse brain-gut signals are likely also important. Studies investigating diarrhea patterns in correlation to weather changes in humans found certain weather conditions linked to incidences of non-infectious AD (high temperature, drop in humidity, precipitation) and viral gastroenteritis (low temperature, high humidity).8,9 One veterinary study investigating frequencies of gastroenteritis diagnosis in correlation with meteorological data showed seasonal variations in the numbers of cases with acute gastroenteritis and a strong association of canine parvovirus (CPV) case admissions with monthly wind speed and inverse humidity.10 Possible similar effects have not been investigated in dogs with idiopathic AHDS. Thus, we aimed to determine whether meteorological factors could influence the occurrence of idiopathic AHDS presentations. Our central hypothesis was that the pattern of specific weather conditions and weather changes is associated with the rate of idiopathic AHDS presentations.

Materials and methods

Study population

Electronic medical records (EMR) of all dogs presented to the Department for Small Animals, Leipzig University, Germany, between January 2016 and January 2021 were searched to identify all cases presented with AD, non-hemorrhagic diarrhea, or vomiting (Figure 1). The complete EMRs of 412 dogs presented with acute clinical signs (≤6 days before presentation) compatible with a GI condition were reviewed for our case–control study. The dogs were stratified into 3 groups: idiopathic AHDS (n = 227), acute gastroenteritis (n = 122), and other causes for acute GI signs (n = 63; Figure 1; Supplementary file 1). Stratification was based on the documented patient history, physical examination, and diagnostic evaluation (see Appendix A). Dog owners were not contacted for further follow-up information.

Flow chart summarizing the patient population (n = 412 dogs; all independent subjects, no duplicates) of the study, stratified by the disease group and diagnostics performed to diagnose idiopathic AHDS cases (n = 227; red box) and differentiate these from acute gastroenteritis cases (green box) and other diagnoses (orange boxes).

Meteorological data set creation

Daily weather data were obtained from the global Integrated Forecasting System, operated by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts, for the study period (January 2016 through January 2021; see Appendix B and Figure S1).11,12 Variables included temperature and apparent temperature, photo period and solar exposure, wind atmospheric dynamics, and hydrometeorological variables. Weather variables were obtained for each dog’s primary residence and were geocoded and linked to each dog in the study based on the day of hospital admission, with data extracted from the day the patient was presented to the hospital to 14 days before. Accounting for the lagged effect of weather variables, their mean, rolling mean for the 7- and 14-day periods before presentation, and peak values (to detect sudden changes) for 1 and 2 weeks before hospital admission, were considered.

Data analysis

Four analytical approaches were applied. First, descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study sample using a Kruskal–Wallis test followed by a Dunn’s test and, if indicated, Holm’s sequential Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.13 Second, principal component analysis (PCA) was used to explore the underlying dimensionality and correlation structure of the dataset (see Appendix C and Figure S2). Third, a random forest classifier was developed to classify between idiopathic AHDS and acute gastroenteritis using weather variables.14 Model hyperparameters were optimized using a grid search over the following parameter space: variations in the number of trees, tree depth, node splitting criteria, leaf size, and bootstrap sampling strategy. Finally, associations between short-term weather variability and occurrences of AHDS and acute gastroenteritis were assessed using odds ratios (OR). R software and Python were used for statistical analyses. The STROBE guidelines were applied (see Appendix D).15

Results

Patient population

Patient characteristics for the different groups of dogs showed no difference in sex distribution and body weight among groups (Table 1). Dogs with idiopathic AHDS were significantly older than dogs with acute gastroenteritis (P = .05) but not compared to dogs with other diseases causing acute GI signs (P = .35). No differences were found in the proportion of purebred vs. mixed breed dogs among these groups. The most represented breeds in the idiopathic AHDS group were Yorkshire terriers (n = 18), Chihuahuas (n = 14), Labrador retrievers (n = 14), and Jack Russell terriers (n = 9), whereas in the acute gastroenteritis group, the most represented breeds were Yorkshire terriers (n = 7), French bulldogs (n = 7), and Chihuahuas (n = 6). Drugs affecting the GI tract had been received in 52% of the dogs in the group with other diseases causing acute GI signs and comprised mostly non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (n = 26).

Principal component feature analysis

Principal component analysis identified a strong low-dimensional structure in both datasets. For acute gastroenteritis, the first 3 principal components (PCs) had Eigenvalues of 22.77, 9.11, and 5.49, explaining 45.54%, 18.21%, and 10.98% of the total variance, respectively (cumulative variance = 74.74%). Similarly, for AHDS, PC1–PC3 had Eigenvalues of 21.97, 9.94, and 5.54, accounting for 43.94%, 19.89%, and 11.09% of the variance (cumulative variance = 74.92%). Although 18 components had Eigenvalues > 1 (Kaiser criterion), inspection of the scree plot and cumulative variance supported retention of the first 3 components for interpretation (see Figures S3 and S4).

Principal component 1 (PC1; capturing the largest amount of variation) was dominated by temperature and radiation metrics, with the most significant variables including temperature (mean, min, max) and apparent temperature (mean, min, max), shortwave radiation, sunshine and daylight duration, and snowfall-related variables such as snowfall sum and sum roll mean. Thus, variations in daily temperature, sunshine duration, and radiation were found to play a significant role in explaining the variation in PC1. The negative loading values for temperature, shortwave radiation, sunshine and daylight duration, and snowfall (Figure 2) suggest that observations with a positive PC1 score tend to have lower values for these variables.

Variables determining the principal components. The loadings plot shows the correlation between the individual variables (mean, rolling mean for the 7- and 14-day periods before presentation, and peak values for 1 and 2 weeks before hospital admission) and the strength and direction of their contribution to the principal components. PC1 was dominated by temperature and radiation metrics, PC2 was mostly driven by precipitation and wind speed, and PC3 reflected a combination of health-related variables.

Similarly, PC2 (capturing the second-largest amount of variation) was determined to be driven by precipitation and wind speed. The most significant variables of PC2 included precipitation and rain-related metrics, such as precipitation (sum and hours), rain sum, wind speed, wind gusts, and the dominant wind direction. These findings suggest that the variance in PC2 is primarily influenced by atmospheric dynamics, particularly wind patterns and precipitation events. The loadings for precipitation, rain, and wind-related factors also were positive, indicating that observations with positive PC2 scores tend to have higher values for these variables, but these loadings were not as high as for PC1 (Figure 2).

Although the PC3 (capturing the third-largest amount of variation) loadings were not significantly higher, these reflected a combination of health-related variables (serum protein and albumin concentrations, neutrophil counts) with positive loading values that led to higher values of PC3. Additional weather variables (eg, precipitation, wind), with higher precipitation and lower sunshine or snowfall, potentially contributed to higher PC3 values.

Classifier performance for predicting idiopathic AHDS vs. acute gastroenteritis

Data classification and stepwise feature reduction from 139 variables during the feature selection using correlation (see Appendix C and E, Figures S2 and S5) rendered 11 variables in the random decision forest model (RDFM) that were determined to be important (Figure 3). These variables included: maximum wind speed, dominant wind direction, and maximum wind gust at 10 m above the ground; sunshine duration; shortwave radiation; snowfall sum; daylight duration; minimum and maximum temperature at 2 m above ground; precipitation sum and precipitation hours. Using AHDS as the positive class, the optimized RDFM classifier achieved a receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC-AUC) of 0.94, indicating excellent discrimination between AHDS and acute gastroenteritis. Class-wise performance remained best for “idiopathic AHDS,” with a precision of 70% (Figure 4; Appendix F and Figure S5). A recall of 91% showed that the model identified 91% of all actual “idiopathic AHDS” cases, and the F1 score of 79% demonstrated a reasonable balance between precision and recall, with “idiopathic AHDS” representing the best-performing class. In contrast, “acute gastroenteritis” was poorly predicted, with a low recall of 75% and a low F1 score of 81%, suggesting that the model struggled with the “acute gastroenteritis” classification.

Analysis of the variables indicated that those variables related to wind atmospheric dynamics (wind direction, wind speed, and wind gusts) were most influential in the model. Features related to the thermal environment (sunshine duration and temperature) also played significant roles. Risk association between idiopathic AHDS and acute gastroenteritis with environmental conditions

The 11 variables determined to be important in the RDFM were included in a logistic regression model, and ORs (with 95% CI) were calculated. Wind speed, minimum temperatures, and radiation were strong predictors of the likelihood of an “idiopathic AHDS” classification (Figure 5, Appendix G, and Figure S6). Daylight also had a positive but smaller effect on this diagnosis. More sunshine, higher maximum temperatures, and prolonged rainfall were found to decrease the odds of an “idiopathic AHDS” diagnosis classification.

Discussion

We investigated the effect of short-term weather conditions on idiopathic AHDS onset in dogs when compared to cases presented to a tertiary-level care clinic with acute gastroenteritis or other causes for acute GI signs. Our results suggest that wind dynamics (wind speed and wind direction), temperature changes (lower temperatures), higher shortwave radiation exposure (equating to a higher intensity of sunlight), and higher precipitation levels are linked to a higher chance of idiopathic AHDS presentation. More sunshine, higher maximum temperatures, and prolonged rainfall were associated with decreased chances of idiopathic AHDS presentation. The strong multicollinearity among these meteorological variables suggests that these environmental effects operate as correlated weather patterns rather than independent risk factors. This interpretation might help in further understanding the environmental and weather-related variables that could impact the likelihood of various health conditions and might be useful to predict and manage their outcomes. These results align with anecdotal reports of weather changes being associated with idiopathic AHDS (or acute gastroenteritis) onset in some dogs.

A slightly increased likelihood of presentations of an idiopathic AHDS episode also was seen with longer daylight hours and higher total precipitation. In contrast, higher maximum temperatures, increased sunshine, more precipitation hours, higher wind gusts, and more snowfall were linked to a decreased risk of idiopathic AHDS presentation. A minor protective effect for idiopathic AHDS onset also was detected for a change in the dominant wind direction. Some of these findings, if interpreted independently, might appear contradictory, but weather factors are strongly interrelated and do not occur or change in isolation. For example, the precipitation data would indicate differences between severe precipitation over a short period (“heavy weather”) and light precipitation over a longer period. Furthermore, triggering weather factors may vary among individual dogs. For example, dogs with darker haircoat or skin could be affected by prolonged sunshine much differently than dogs with lighter haircoat or skin. Similarly, dogs with a thick haircoat might be affected differently by precipitation, harsh winds, or lower temperatures than dogs with a light haircoat or no hair. These are important limitations of our study, because we did not account for such variations among dogs within a group. In addition to shedding more light on potential pathogenic mechanisms of idiopathic AHDS, understanding potential environmental and weather-related effects could be important for predicting and managing this recurrent condition. However, our research is primarily exploratory and not suitable for drawing firm conclusions as to which weather factors, if any, may trigger the onset of AHDS. Doing so requires additional studies to evaluate a possible link between weather factors or changes and AHDS development and to determine how these factors could impact the management of this idiopathic syndrome. Moreover, the dogs included in our study were a convenience population presented to a tertiary care clinic and were mostly from the greater Leipzig area. Similar studies conducted across different climate zones or comparing dogs from different climatic conditions might yield more variable or more conclusive results or both.

Consistent with the population of dogs in our study, idiopathic AHDS has been reported to occur predominantly in small breed dogs, such as Yorkshire terriers, Miniature schnauzers, Cavalier King Charles spaniels, Dachshunds, Chihuahuas, Maltese, or Miniature pinschers.1 However, we had no control group of dogs from the general hospital population. Thus, the result might be biased towards more popular breeds or breeds more often presented to the hospital and not accurately represent a breed predisposition. Consistent with our findings, a sex predilection for this condition has not been documented, and a higher incidence of idiopathic AHDS typically is observed in middle-aged dogs, consistent with the median age of 5 years in our study.2

Wind speed being positively correlated with the risk of idiopathic AHDS presentations aligns with the only previous veterinary study published 27 years ago, and reporting incidence of a gastroenteritis diagnosis in correlation with meteorological data.10 That study showed seasonal variations in the total number of dogs presenting to a tertiary veterinary care center, with peaks during the southern hemisphere summer months (September–January) and decreased presentations during the winter months (May–July).10 Average numbers of dogs diagnosed with acute gastroenteritis and those admitted to the CPV isolation ward also indicated a seasonal pattern, with acute peaks in early summer and decreasing numbers in late summer.10 The reported correlation of the CPV caseload with monthly wind speed and inverse humidity values10 also agrees with the findings in our study.

The PCA analysis showed that PC1, PC2, and PC3 together explain different aspects of climate variability, with PC1 primarily capturing temperature (heat)-related trends and PC2 representing wind (storm)-related dynamics, whereas PC3 indicated health-related variables with weather conditions. Weather and heat variables were found to play a significant role in idiopathic AHDS episodes (see Figures S3 and S4) and also were found to be associated with the onset of other disease states. However, wind-related variables were more commonly associated with idiopathic AHDS and, to some extent, with acute gastroenteritis. Interestingly, intense weather conditions and wind patterns were identified as risk factors for the onset of both idiopathic AHDS and acute gastroenteritis. All 3 categories of data points were densely spread to the PC2 and PC3 scales, with some exceptions of the idiopathic AHDS points on positive PC2 and PC3 scales, which were related to high precipitation and wind values.

An investigation of the patterns of AD presentations in correlation to weather conditions in humans also found a peak incidence of AD in the summer and decreased case numbers in the winter months.8 Similar to our results, average daily temperatures were also highest during the time of increased AD incidence, whereas relative humidity and rainfall levels were low at that time.8 In contrast, cases of infectious gastroenteritis caused by rotavirus were found to be less frequent with higher daily average and minimum temperatures and lower humidity but presented at an increased rate when temperatures were lower and humidity was higher.9 This study also identified seasonal changes in incidences, with higher rates of rotavirus cases in the northern hemisphere winter (low temperature, high humidity) and a decrease in case numbers during the summer months.9

Results of the previous veterinary study led to the hypothesis that an association between increased wind speed and acute gastroenteritis could be caused by the negative effects harsh winds may have on mucous membranes, predisposing to enhanced susceptibility to pathogen invasion across those membranes.10 The current hypothesis of the development of idiopathic AHDS in dogs suggests that an overgrowth of C. perfringens strains, with the production of specific toxins (eg, NetF), plays a role in the onset of this syndrome.1 NetF-encoding C. perfringens strains are not invariably present in dogs with idiopathic AHDS, but their prevalence in this condition is significantly higher than in dogs with other GI diseases or healthy dogs.1 As a normal habitant in the intestine of humans and animals, invasion of NetF toxin-producing C. perfringens is likely not necessary to cause an episode of AHDS. Thus, an increase in wind speed could be a possible trigger for clostridial overgrowth by causing stress for the dog and disturbing mucous membrane homeostasis. Such stressors could also be either direct or indirect consequences of behavioral changes or alterations in normal routines (eg, shorter or fewer vs. longer or more walks or other outside activities).16 Similar inferences might also hold for higher precipitation levels, sunlight duration, daylight duration, and higher temperatures, which can determine the level of owner-linked activities and directly affect the dog’s well-being.16

Variations in physiological variables (eg, blood pressure changes in response to temperature variations) could be a possible explanation, because increasing temperatures can cause vasodilatation with increased perfusion of the skin for a cooling effect on the body. Vasodilatation can lead to a decrease in systemic blood pressure, whereas lower temperatures in the environment can have the opposite effect, and such changes in systemic blood pressure can affect the GI tract. Wind speed is also dependent on barometric pressure, and a large-scale study in humans suffering from knee osteoarthritis showed that the severity of pain correlated with barometric pressure and temperature changes.17 Similar relationships could play a role in the pathogenesis of AHDS in dogs. However, a direct effect of barometric pressure on the prevalence of idiopathic AHDS presentation was not investigated in our study. Such possible interdependencies with other weather factors remain to be further explored. Changes in weather conditions, such as the level of precipitation, often are accompanied by fluctuations in barometric pressure.

The effect of snowfall on the prevalence of idiopathic AHDS may have been over- or underestimated in our study, because very few days with snowfall were represented in the data set (ie, data dilution).

Preclinical studies have established the concept of a gut–brain microbiome axis involving bidirectional communication between the gut and its microbiome, and the nervous system.18,19 Alterations in these communication channels have been identified in rodent models and correlate with digestive, psychiatric, and neurological disorders in humans.19 For example, altered gut–brain interactions have been demonstrated in irritable bowel syndrome in humans, but a causative relationship remains to be determined.18,19 Communication channels among the brain, gut, and microbiome are well characterized and include neural, endocrine, and inflammatory pathways. Alterations in the blood–brain barrier and intestinal permeability likely modulate these interactions.18 The gut microbiome is established in the first 3 years of life but can be modulated later by diet, medication, and stress.18 Given the connectedness and complexity of the gut–brain–microbiome axis, its role in the development of idiopathic AHDS and association with weather changes seems plausible. However, the GI tract is only one organ system affected by changing weather conditions in humans, and many people with migraine or tension-type headaches report specific weather conditions or changes as triggers for episodes.20 However, studies have not shown a statistically significant link between weather and migraine onset.20 This situation may be a consequence of unidentified triggers, a heterogeneous population with variable triggers, variable responses to the same triggers, or variable lag times to migraine onset.20 Given these similarities, studies that investigate the possible influence of weather conditions on idiopathic AHDS episodes face similar limitations. Thus, similar to migraine research, moving away from group associations and observing individual patients over time as their own controls might yield more meaningful results.20 This approach could guide future research on meteorological effects on idiopathic AHDS occurrence.

Meteorological factors are interrelated and do not occur or change in isolation, which presents a limitation when determining possible effects of single weather factors on the occurrence of a condition such as idiopathic AHDS. To minimize such interaction effects, strongly correlated features were filtered out using a correlation matrix. Still, limitations remain with the interpretation of such data, because all weather factors occur interdependently, and determining a single triggering factor for idiopathic AHDS occurrence seems impossible. Also, most of the study population was from the greater Leipzig area. Thus, our results reflect the effects of weather factors in this specific climate zone. Compared with other geographic areas, this low-altitude region has a mild climate with seasonal weather changes, and the results of our study might have been different in other climate zones (eg, regions without seasonal changes, with more extreme temperatures, or of higher altitude). Consistent with this reasoning, the aforementioned studies all investigated the effects of weather conditions or changes on GI diseases in regions closer to the equator, representing climate zones different from those of our study.8–10

We investigated the association of meteorological factors with presentation of dogs to a tertiary level clinic. Hence, selection bias is a risk because dogs successfully treated, euthanized, or that died at primary care clinics were not considered. This limitation might cause a bias towards cases where clinical signs were severe and required tertiary-level care, or when presentation to our clinic was the best option. Thus, the wider population level of this disorder cannot be fully captured in our study, which could impact the generalizability of our results. However, idiopathic AHDS is a diagnosis of exclusion and other diseases causing acute GI signs must be excluded. Although doing so cannot be accomplished for all patients, such a diagnostic evaluation is less likely to be completed at primary care centers. Furthermore, idiopathic AHDS is a disorder characterized by a sudden onset of hemorrhagic diarrhea with severe fluid loss,1 making affected dogs likely to be in need of tertiary level care. Another limitation was that weather variables were obtained for each dog’s primary residence, as stated by the owner. Because of the retrospective design of our study, confirmation that the dog spent time at its home location exclusively during the 14 days leading up to the hospital presentation was not possible. Also, travel history was only recorded for some dogs in the EMR and was not included in the analysis. Lastly, not all dogs included in the study had a complete fecal examination performed. Thus, an endoparasite infection could not be entirely excluded in all dogs.

In conclusion, our study suggests that certain weather conditions or changes, including increased wind speeds, lower temperatures, increased shortwave radiation exposure, and lower sunlight intensity, are linked to an increase in idiopathic AHDS presentations. Further studying these environmental and weather-related effects could be important in understanding the pathogenetic mechanism of this idiopathic condition. Furthermore, identification of certain weather conditions or changes that are linked to an increased chance of AHDS presentations could help optimize long-term management of affected dogs, where veterinarians and owners might be prepared for the increased risk of an AHDS episode with certain weather changes. This exploratory research provides a framework for future studies to evaluate the effects of meteorological and other factors on the gut-brain-microbiome axis and their potential role in the development of idiopathic AHDS.
