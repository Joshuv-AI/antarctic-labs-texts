Understanding regional disparities in obesity: a multiscale geographically weighted analysis in the United States
Authors: Jonah K Amponsah, Jeffrey A Thompson, Shellie D Ellis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObesity varies markedly across U.S. counties, and global models often miss place-specific determinants. While prior studies document higher prevalence in nonmetropolitan areas, the geographic variation in its determinants—known as spatial heterogeneity—remains underexplored. We linked age-adjusted adult obesity prevalence with socioeconomic indicators, and behavioral risks for 3106 contiguous counties. A global OLS model served as a baseline, followed by estimation of Multiscale Geographically Weighted Regression (MGWR). MGWR outperformed global OLS (adjusted : 0.801 vs. 0.566; AICc: 13,580.92 vs. 15,764.21), confirming non-stationarity and revealing covariate-specific scales. Metropolitan status was generally protective, but its effect attenuated or reversed in parts of the West. Income and educational attainment are broadly inverse with minimal dispersion across counties, suggesting near-global behavior in this specification. Short sleep shows a strong positive association with little spread, while binge drinking is positive and slightly more variable. Employment is narrowly positive with almost no spatial dispersion. Bandwidth diagnostics separate near-global from local processes: metro and employment operate at large bandwidths, education and binge drinking at meso scales, and income, short sleep, and marriage at finer scales. As a benchmark, metro-only models showed a uniformly protective but locally varying metro effect that attenuated once socioeconomic and behavioral covariates were included. Findings confirm non-stationarity and argue for a two-tier translation: system-level policies for near-global factors and community-tailored interventions for localized risks, with attention to Western metropolitan vulnerabilities and Southeastern rural constraints.Keywords: MGWR, Obesity, Spatial analysis, Regional disparities, Socioecological model, County-level analysis

Introduction

Obesity remains a major U.S. public health challenge with wide-ranging consequences—from increased risks of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and several cancers to reduced quality of life and substantial economic burden [1, 27, 30, 50, 62, 66]. Adult obesity prevalence has more than doubled over the past four decades; the most recent CDC estimates place prevalence at roughly 40% among adults (2021–2023) and 20% among children and adolescents aged 2–19 [10, 20]. Secular trends have been linked to declines in physical activity, increased sedentary time, and greater consumption of energy-dense, ultra-processed foods [5, 60]. These national averages, however, conceal marked geographic disparities.

County-level variation reflects socioeconomic conditions, health behaviors, and built environments, with rural and nonmetropolitan areas often showing higher prevalence due to resource constraints, limited healthcare, and poverty [4, 32, 43, 52, 73]. Urban areas are not uniformly protective, as socioeconomic inequality and environmental barriers can elevate rates [41, 49]. Determinants—diet, physical activity, alcohol use, sleep, income, education, and access to food/recreation—likely operate at varying spatial scales [30, 65]. Drawing on the socioecological model and neighborhood-effects theory, we view this variation as multilevel, with individual behaviors (e.g., sleep) at local levels, community resources (e.g., metro access) at meso scales, and structural inequalities (e.g., income, education) at broader national levels [14, 44, 65]. Anomalously high rates in Western metros may stem from food deserts and sedentary jobs [41], while Southeastern rural patterns may reflect limited recreation and healthy food access, suggesting place-tailored policies like urban planning or community programs, revisited in the Discussion.

Conventional global regression, including ordinary least squares (OLS) and multilevel models, assumes spatial stationarity, applying one coefficient universally and obscuring local differences that lead to blunt policy guidance [2, 13, 22, 23, 26, 28]. Evidence shows obesity varies more by neighborhood context than individual attributes, necessitating local models [6, 17, 29, 31, 45, 47, 55, 59, 61]. Geographically weighted regression (GWR) relaxes this by estimating location-specific coefficients; multiscale GWR (MGWR) advances the approach by estimating covariate-specific bandwidths, revealing the spatial scale at which each determinant operates [23–25, 57, 58, 72]. Treating bandwidths as first-class results distinguishes near-global processes (large bandwidths) from locally contingent ones (small bandwidths), aligning with socioecological and neighborhood-effects theory and enabling scale-appropriate policy interpretation.

Using 3106 contiguous U.S. counties, this study quantifies spatial heterogeneity in obesity prevalence linked to socioeconomic (income, education, employment, marital status) and behavioral (binge drinking, sleep) covariates, identifies operative scales via adaptive bandwidths, and maps significant local deviations. Anchored by metro-nonmetro comparisons and framed by socioecological theory, OLS provides a global baseline with robust inference, while MGWR elucidates mechanisms and policy levers. To anchor interpretation, we present descriptive metropolitan–nonmetropolitan comparisons and treat urbanicity (metro) as an explicit covariate. Recent studies [40, 56] highlight regional variation in built-environment and behavioral effects, motivating our approach to estimate place-varying impacts explicitly.

Research links obesity to socioeconomic gradients—income, education, employment—alongside behavioral factors such as diet, alcohol use, and sleep, and neighborhood contexts including food outlet mix, access to parks and recreation, and healthcare availability [12, 29, 59, 64, 70]. The socioecological model and neighborhood-effects theories provide a conceptual scaffold: macro-level forces shaped by labor markets and policy often operate at coarser spatial scales, whereas built and social environments and related behaviors plausibly exert effects at neighborhood-to-county scales [14, 44]. Treating spatial scale as an empirical object rather than an assumption follows directly from this framing.

Methods

Study design and data sources

We performed a cross-sectional ecological analysis of U.S. counties using age-adjusted adult obesity prevalence (2020) as the dependent variable from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), maintained by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [11]. To gain a deeper understanding of prevalence of obesity at the county level, we incorporated additional data from CDC and the United States Census. Socioeconomic covariates (median household income, educational attainment, employment status, marital status) were drawn from the American Community Survey (ACS) 5-year estimates, 2016–2020 [68] aligned to the study year. This five-year estimate allows for a more stable and reliable estimation of these socioeconomic indicators for most counties in the U.S., as it aggregates data over multiple years to ensure accuracy and privacy protection. Behavioral covariates (binge drinking and short sleep) were obtained from CDC PLACES county-level estimates [9]. To capture place-of-residence effects, counties were classified using the USDA 2013 Rural–Urban Continuum Codes (RUCC) [69]. For analytic consistency and spatial contiguity, the sample was restricted to the contiguous United States (n = 3106 of 3143 counties).

Measures

Obesity prevalence was the age-adjusted percentage of adults (aged ≥ 18 years) with body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2. Income was operationalized as median household income in U.S. dollars, scaled in $10,000 units to enhance interpretability. Income is a well-established correlation of obesity, particularly through dietary cost and quality pathways [16, 18]. Education was measured as the proportion of adults with at least high school attainment. Educational attainment is inversely associated with obesity across many U.S. populations and is recognized as a key social determinant [12, 64]. Labor-market context was represented by the labor-force participation rate; the unemployment rate was dropped to avoid potential redundancy, with all remaining global VIFs < 5 [33, 36, 38]. Employment conditions shape income, time constraints, and stress-related behaviors that may influence obesity risk [70]. Marital status was operationalized as the percentage currently married. The complementary category—“not married” (including never married, widowed, and divorced individuals)—was excluded due to perfect inverse collinearity. This variable was retained given robust evidence from observational and longitudinal studies linking marriage and marital transitions to weight gain [15, 34, 35, 51]. Binge drinking was the percentage of adults reporting ≥ 4 (women) or ≥ 5 (men) drinks on a single occasion in the past 30 days. Short sleep was the percentage reporting fewer than seven hours per night; both have established links to adiposity [8, 39, 46, 67]. Urbanicity was modeled using a binary metropolitan/nonmetropolitan indicator (metro = 1 if RUCC 1–3, 0 otherwise) resulting in 1160 metropolitan counties. While categorical variables can sometimes introduce local multicollinearity in MGWR, binary indicators used as explanatory variables generally do not pose such issues—particularly when they exhibit sufficient spatial variation and are not strongly correlated with other predictors. Metro status was retained after confirming global VIF < 5 [33, 36, 38], and given its role as a contextual moderator of the dependent variable rather than a source of collinearity among explanatory factors [21]. All percentage variables are expressed in percentage-point units. Where necessary, continuous predictors were centered and scaled prior to modeling for numerical stability.

To assess model adequacy and motivate the use of local modeling, we examined several diagnostics. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF), with variables flagged and removed at VIF > 5, while recognizing the limitations of rigid thresholds as noted by O’brien [53]. Heteroskedasticity and potential non-stationarity were assessed using the Breusch–Pagan/Koenker test [7, 37], with the Koenker statistic reported for the full OLS model. Significant results supported the use of multiscale geographically weighted regression (MGWR), and heteroskedasticity-robust inference was applied using HC3 and HC5 estimators.

To detect spatial autocorrelation in residuals, we computed Moran’s I using a row-standardized k-nearest-neighbors weights matrix [48]. Model fit was summarized using adjusted R2 and corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc). To contextualize urban–rural disparities, we conducted a two-sample mean difference test and calculated standardized mean differences in obesity prevalence between metropolitan and nonmetropolitan RUCC groupings.

Results

Descriptive geographic patterns in obesity prevalence

Analysis of 2020 obesity prevalence across 3106 contiguous U.S. counties revealed substantial geographic variation, consistent with socioecological and neighborhood-effects frameworks. Counties in the Southern and Midwestern regions, notably the Deep South, Appalachia, and Mississippi Delta, exhibited the highest obesity rates (35–50%), while Western and Northeastern counties generally showed lower prevalence (20–30%), as illustrated in Fig. 1. A two-sample t-test confirmed significant urban–rural disparities: metropolitan counties (RUCC 1–3) had a mean obesity prevalence of 34.9% compared to 37.1% in nonmetropolitan counties (RUCC 4–9), with a mean difference of . The standardized mean difference (Hedges’ g = 0.48, 95% CI 0.41, 0.55) indicated a moderate effect size, underscoring urbanicity’s role in structuring obesity patterns.

Global OLS models

Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression was used to establish baseline global associations. The initial model, which included only the binary metropolitan indicator, revealed that metropolitan counties had significantly lower obesity prevalence (− 2.17%, 95% CI − 2.50, − 1.84; p value < 0.001). However, the model explained limited variability in county-level obesity rates, with an adjusted of 0.051 and an AICc of 18,189.64 (Table 1). Diagnostic tests indicated that global assumptions were violated: the Koenker BP test showed significant non-stationarity (BP = 5.8751, p value = 0.01536), and Moran’s I revealed strong spatial autocorrelation in residuals (I = 0.630, p value < 0.001). These findings justified the use of a local modeling approach.

Several covariates demonstrated significant associations with obesity prevalence. Higher median household income was strongly protective, with each $10,000 increase associated with a 1.39% reduction in obesity. Educational attainment also showed an inverse relationship, as counties with higher proportions of adults holding at least a high school diploma exhibited lower obesity rates (− 0.12%). In contrast, a greater percentage of currently married adults was positively associated with obesity (+ 0.03%). Higher employment rates were positively associated with obesity prevalence (+ 0.12%). Behavioral risk factors were also significant; binge drinking (+ 0.10%) and insufficient sleep (+ 0.66%) were both positively associated with obesity.

MGWR models

The multiscale geographically weighted regression (MGWR) model, allowing spatially varying coefficients, substantially improved fit over OLS. For the metro-only model, adjusted R2 increased to 0.651 (AICc = 15,318.96), with metro coefficients ranging from − 3.09% to − 0.45% (median = − 1.52%), indicating widespread but heterogeneous protective effects (Table 3). The Monte Carlo test for spatial variability revealed the local parameter estimates exhibit a significant spatial variability. The residual spatial autocorrelation was not significant (Moran’s I = 0.0134, p = 0.1144), suggesting that MGWR effectively accounted for spatial structure in the data.

Discussion

This study employs Multiscale Geographically Weighted Regression (MGWR) to reveal spatial heterogeneity in obesity determinants across U.S. counties. A global OLS model offered only moderate fit and violated key assumptions—heteroskedasticity [7, 37] and strong positive residual autocorrelation [48]—indicating that constant-effect models inadequately represent spatially patterned health outcomes. Allowing coefficients to vary across space and scale with MGWR markedly improved fit (Adj. R2 = 0.801; ) and reduced residual structure (Moran’s I = − 0.0297), confirming that much of the unexplained variations in OLS reflects spatially varying relationships [25, 58].

Metro-only benchmarks clarify what urbanicity explains on its own. In unadjusted OLS, metropolitan counties had 2.17 percentage points lower obesity prevalence than non-metropolitan counties (Table 1), but residuals showed strong spatial clustering. In the metro-only MGWR, the metro effect remained.

consistently negative across space but varied locally (median − 1.52; range − 3.09 to − 0.45; Table 3), operating at a meso spatial scale (bandwidth = 806 neighbors; DoD = 0.745; MC p = 0.020; Table 4) and largely eliminating residual spatial autocorrelation. In the extended MGWR, the metro effect attenuated (median ≈ − 0.11; Table 5) once socioeconomic and behavioral covariates were included, while overall model fit improved (Adj. R2 = 0.801), indicating that much of the urban–rural contrast is accounted for by socioeconomic and behavioral covariates. Substantively, this supports reading urbanicity as a proxy for broader structural and behavioral contexts rather than a stand-alone driver.

Local-coefficients distributions (Table 5) shows that metropolitan status was generally protective—consistent with urban advantages in infrastructure and access [4, 43]—but attenuated or slightly reversed in several Western metros, plausibly reflecting food-retail gaps, sedentary employment, and long commutes [41, 49]. Income and educational attainment were inversely related to obesity with comparatively limited spatial variation, suggesting structural inequalities tied to economic and educational opportunity act broadly [12, 16, 18, 70]. Behavioral risks were more place-contingent: binge drinking and short sleep showed pronounced local heterogeneity—particularly in parts of the East and Southeast—consistent with community norms and environmental context [19, 63]. Employment was positively associated with obesity at a global level, with higher prevalence observed among individuals in precarious jobs and those working more than 40 h per week [42, 54]. Marital status shifted from a positive global association to mixed local effects in MGWR, patterns that may reflect regional differences in household support and stress exposures [3, 26]. Non-stationarity diagnostics, including significant Monte Carlo tests where computed, reinforce the appropriateness of MGWR over single-bandwidth GWR [25]. The local values (≈0.78–0.82) was highest in parts of the South/Midwest and lower in a few interior/coastal pockets, consistent with meso/local covariates driving fit there and pointing to unmeasured neighborhood factors elsewhere.

Covariate-specific bandwidths (Table 6) separated near-global from local processes: metropolitan status and employment operated at almost national scales with no evidence of non-stationarity (MC p = 0.349 and 0.761), education and binge drinking at meso scales and are non-stationary (MC p = 0.006 and < 0.001), and short sleep, income, and marriage at more local scales and non-stationarity is borderline for income (MC p = 0.095) but significant for short sleep and marriage (MC p = 0.002, 0.042).

Substantively, these patterns accord with socioecological and neighborhood‐effects theory [14, 44]. Structural conditions—urbanicity and labor markets—appear broadly consistent nationwide, while education and binge drinking vary at regional scales, and income, short sleep, and marriage show finer, context-dependent modulation. In the extended MGWR, the associations for % employed and % binge drinking remain positive and consistent with OLS; MGWR primarily refines their magnitudes and highlights modest spatial dispersion.

Policy implications follow from scale. Near‐global determinants (urbanicity, employment) point to system‐level levers (e.g., workforce and infrastructure policy). Meso and local processes argue for place‐tailored strategies: in Western metros, planning and retail policies to mitigate food deserts and support active transport; in parts of the rural Southeast, community programs emphasizing nutrition education and sleep hygiene. MGWR identifies where associations strengthen, attenuate, or change sign; translating maps into action should be guided by theory‐consistent mechanisms rather than spatial patterns alone [40, 56].

Limitations include the ecological, cross-sectional design; model-based PLACES/BRFSS estimates; and potential omission of confounders such as detailed diet, transportation modes, health-care access, housing costs, and retail mix. Results may be sensitive to kernel choice, adaptive bandwidths, neighborhood definitions, and the modifiable areal unit problem. Although local collinearity diagnostics were acceptable [53, 71] and residual spatial structure was substantially reduced, a small negative Moran’s I for MGWR residuals suggests mild over-dispersion after flexible local fitting.

Future research should move beyond cross-sectional mapping to test real-world mechanisms and interventions. This includes using longitudinal or panel MGWR to track how spatial relationships shift over time, applying quasi-experimental designs to evaluate changes in food and recreation environments, and integrating more detailed data on the built environment and climate. Embedding spatially varying coefficients within multilevel or Bayesian models can help account for uncertainty. To improve generalizability, studies should include out-of-sample validation and test sensitivity to bandwidth and neighborhood definitions. Equity-focused analyses are essential for identifying where targeted strategies can most effectively reduce disparities [40, 56].

Conclusion

This MGWR analysis highlights the multiscale nature of county-level obesity across the U.S., revealing spatial patterns that a global OLS model obscures. A global OLS model captured only part of the signal and left substantial spatial structure, whereas the extended MGWR improved fit ) and markedly reduced residual autocorrelation. Bandwidths and local coefficients showed near-global behavior for urbanicity and employment, meso-scale variation for education and binge drinking, and finer-scale modulation for income, short sleep, and marriage. The metro-only benchmark confirmed a uniformly protective but locally varying urbanicity effect that attenuates when socioeconomic and behavioral covariates are included, indicating that urbanicity partially proxies broader structural and behavioral contexts.
