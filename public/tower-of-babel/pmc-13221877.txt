Paroxysmal Pause-Dependent Atrioventricular Block
Authors: Timothy Nathan Kwan, Hariharan Raju, Jamie Cham, Harry Mond, Raymond W. Sy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPause-dependent atrioventricular (AV) block is a cause of syncope and cardiac arrest characterized by abrupt but transient conduction failure with ventricular standstill despite previous AV conduction. It is frequently under-recognized.Case SummaryWe describe 4 patients presenting with syncope. Baseline electrocardiograms were nondiagnostic, but continuous monitoring captured episodes of sudden high-grade AV block initiated by pauses or premature beats, without progressive PR prolongation. All patients underwent permanent pacemaker implantation, with resolution of symptoms during follow-up.DiscussionPause-dependent AV block is thought to arise from the inability of diseased His-Purkinje tissue to sustain its resting potential during phase 4 of the cardiac action potential. It may mimic neurological or vasovagal events. Diagnosis depends on timely rhythm documentation. Early recognition is essential because pacing is highly effective in preventing recurrence.Take-Home MessagesSyncope with suspicion for a cardiac cause should prompt prolonged monitoring. Pacing of pause-dependent paroxysmal AV block can prevent further events.

Paroxysmal pause-dependent atrioventricular (AV) block (phase 4 block) is an under-recognized cause of syncope and cardiac arrest, typically occurring in elderly patients with underlying conduction system disease. It is characterized by abrupt, unpredictable episodes of ventricular standstill. We present a multicenter series of illustrative cases, followed by a review of the underlying electrophysiological mechanisms, differential diagnosis, and management of this rare but treatable condition.Take-Home Messages•Although pause-dependent paroxysmal AV block is often not evident on the resting ECG, prolonged rhythm monitoring can secure the diagnosis.•Once identified, permanent pacing is highly effective in preventing recurrence and potentially life-threatening events.

Case Series

Case 1

An 89-year-old woman presented with recurrent syncope on a background of type 2 diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and cognitive impairment. Her 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) showed first-degree AV block (PR 260 ms) with narrow QRS (85 ms) and frequent ventricular bigeminy (Supplemental Figure 1). Inpatient telemetry revealed recurrent episodes of paroxysmal AV block (Figure 1). The PR interval remained unchanged immediately before AV block and in the first conducted beat after recovery of AV conduction. Case 3

A 78-year-old man presented with recurrent syncope. He had no history of heart disease. Baseline ECG (Supplemental Figure 3) showed sinus rhythm of rate 70 beats/min with right bundle branch block and left anterior fascicular block. Recovery of 1:1 AV conduction occurred 11 seconds later after a junctional escape beat and a ventricular ectopic beat (Figure 3). Discussion

Mechanism

These 4 cases illustrate ventricular standstill arising from a shared underlying pathophysiology but triggered by different events. It is critically important to distinguish them from other types of ventricular standstill. The common precipitant was an ectopic beat, which is the hallmark of paroxysmal pause-dependent AV block.

Paroxysmal pause-dependent AV block is underpinned by disease in the infranodal His-Purkinje system. Diseased His-Purkinje fibers fail to maintain a stable hyperpolarized resting potential during diastole. This results from abnormal leak of ions during phase 4 of the myocardial action potential, resulting in the alternative name “phase 4 block.”1 Progressive depolarization during diastole may inactivate fast sodium channels, rendering the His-Purkinje system unresponsive to subsequent impulses, resulting in ventricular standstill (Figure 5). Hence, the term “bradycardia-dependent” or “pause-dependent” AV block has also been used, since prolongation of the His-His interval is required to initiate block.Figure 5Action Potential of Normal and Diseased His-Purkinje FibersMembrane potential plateaus during phase 4 in healthy cells, whereas in patients with paroxysmal atrioventricular block, ion leak occurs during phase 4.

The prolonged diastolic interval is most frequently related to a compensatory pause after an ectopic beat that may be atrial, ventricular, or junctional in origin. Reversion pause after tachyarrhythmia, vagal stimulation, and spontaneous sinus slowing are rarer clinical scenarios that may also induce bradycardia-related paroxysmal block through a similar mechanism. AV block usually persists until a critically timed ectopic beat repolarizes the His-Purkinje tissue to its resting membrane potential, restoring excitability (Figure 5). A well-timed ventricular ectopic beat is more likely than a supraventricular ectopic beat to reset the His-Purkinje system because a larger source current may be required to reset partially depolarized His-Purkinje tissue, and upstream AV nodal block may prevent a supraventricular ectopic impulse from reaching the His Purkinje system.

Clinical features

The condition should be considered in older patients presenting with syncope, particularly when prodromal symptoms are absent. While there is often evidence of distal conduction system disease in the form of bundle branch block on the baseline ECG, 20% to 30% of patients have a normal QRS, as illustrated in cases 1 and 4.1 Underlying structural heart disease may also be present. On ECG monitoring, sudden loss of 1:1 AV conduction typically follows a pause that is initiated by a premature ectopic beat. There is no change in PR interval prior to AV block, and a critically timed ectopic beat may be seen immediately prior to resumption of AV conduction.

Differential diagnosis

Paroxysmal pause-dependent AV block is a cause of syncope and cardiac arrest that is important to distinguish from other causes of ventricular standstill. Careful measurement of the P-P and PR intervals before AV block and immediately after AV block can help to establish the likely mechanism of AV block.

The most common differential diagnosis is vagally mediated AV block, which may appear similar but is usually accompanied by a clinical prodrome in the context of triggers for increased vagal tone, such as pain or phlebotomy (Table 1).2 On monitoring, gradual P-P prolongation, PR lengthening, and Wenckebach AV sequences are typical (Table 1). Adenosine-sensitive AV block is another rare cause of paroxysmal AV block without change in P-P and PR intervals, but it is not precipitated by bradycardia or pause, and it does not require an escape beat to restore AV conduction (Table 1).3 It usually occurs without prodrome. A low plasma adenosine level and excessive response to intravenous adenosine have been reported to be markers of adenosine-sensitive AV block.4 Although it does not progress to persistent AV block, permanent pacing prevents recurrent syncope, and theophylline has also been proposed as an alternative to pacing.5

Finally, it should be noted that degenerative distal conduction disease may also manifest as “acceleration- or tachycardia-dependent” AV block, whereby impulses encounter refractory His-Purkinje tissue.6 Indeed, acceleration-dependent AV block is thought to be a more common cause of paroxysmal AV block than pause-dependent AV block.7 At higher atrial rates, the His-His interval shortens such that subsequent impulses arrive during late repolarization (or even postrepolarization), when the diseased His-Purkinje tissue is still refractory. Consequently, higher degrees of AV block manifest at higher sinus rates, such as progression of Mobitz 2 AV block to complete AV block.

Management

Evidence for the management of paroxysmal pause-dependent AV block is limited to case reports and case series.1,8 The disease is life threatening because ventricular escape rhythms are often absent or unreliable.9 Randomized controlled trials have not been performed to assess the role of pacing in any high-grade AV block, including paroxysmal pause-dependent AV block.10 Nevertheless, based on observational and mechanistic data, international guidelines recommend pacing for all symptomatic AV block in the absence of a reversible cause (eg, acute myocardial ischemia).10,11 Specifically, after paroxysmal pause-dependent AV block is identified in the absence of a reversible cause, it should be regarded as a medical emergency,6 and urgent permanent pacemaker implantation is recommended, or temporary transvenous pacing if implantation is delayed and the episodes are recurrent.3,8

Conclusions

Recognition of paroxysmal pause-dependent AV block is critical, as patients frequently lack a reliable escape rhythm and remain at high risk of sudden cardiac death without prompt pacing.
