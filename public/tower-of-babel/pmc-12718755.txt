Unusual Development of Central Diabetes Insipidus after Endovascular Treatment for a Medial Tentorial Dural Arteriovenous Fistula
Authors: Yuta Oka, Yoshinobu Horio, Munenari Matsuishi, Koichiro Suzuki, Ryuhei Takeyama, Shintaro Yoshinaga, Kei Maki, Mio Nakazato, Koichiro Takemoto, Hiroshi Abe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMedial tentorial dural arteriovenous fistulas (DAVFs) are rare lesions that may cause intracranial hemorrhage or non-hemorrhagic symptoms due to venous congestion from retrograde deep venous drainage. We report a case of a medial tentorial DAVF presenting with cognitive decline and impaired consciousness, successfully treated with transvenous embolization (TVE) and straight sinus angioplasty, and complicated postoperatively by central diabetes insipidus (CDI).Case PresentationA 55-year-old man with diabetes mellitus presented with cognitive decline, disorientation, aphasia, and motor impairment without limb paralysis. CT revealed bilateral thalamic hyperdensities, and MRI showed thalamic and medial occipital hypointensities. CT venography demonstrated straight sinus occlusion. Angiography identified a tentorial DAVF fed by the posterior meningeal and tentorial arteries, draining into the superior vermian vein (Borden III, Cognard III). On day 3, TVE and straight sinus angioplasty achieved complete shunt obliteration. Postoperatively, serum sodium increased from 157 mEq/L on day 1 to 164 mEq/L on day 2, accompanied by thirst, polydipsia, and polyuria. Correction of hyperglycemia did not improve the symptoms. The absence of urine output reduction during water restriction and marked improvement after I-deamino-8-D-arginine vasopressin (DDAVP) confirmed CDI. DDAVP was tapered from day 58 and stopped on day 68 without recurrence. The patient was discharged on day 72.ConclusionTo our knowledge, this is the first report of CDI after treatment of a medial tentorial DAVF. In patients with deep venous congestion, similar complications may occur. Careful postoperative monitoring of urine output and electrolytes is essential for early recognition and management.

Introduction

Dural arteriovenous fistulas (DAVFs) are characterized by abnormal arteriovenous shunts formed between dural arteries and sinuses or intracranial veins.1) Of these DAVFs, medial tentorial DAVFs are relatively rare, and although they frequently present with intracranial hemorrhage, they can also manifest with various non-hemorrhagic symptoms due to venous congestion resulting from retrograde flow into deep cerebral veins.2) Central diabetes insipidus (CDI) is a disorder caused by impaired synthesis or secretion of antidiuretic hormone (ADH), which may result from tumors, trauma, or inflammatory diseases. The clinical presentation typically includes polydipsia, polyuria, and dehydration, with a daily urine output over 3000 mL and urine osmolality below 300 mOsm/kg.3)

We report a case of a medial tentorial DAVF presenting with cognitive decline and impaired consciousness, which was treated with transvenous embolization (TVE) and sinus angioplasty of the straight sinus (SS). Postoperatively, the patient developed CDI. We discuss this case with a review of the relevant literature.

Case Presentation

A 55-year-old man with a history of diabetes mellitus (DM) had been unable to work for 6 days prior to admission. Two days before hospitalization, he visited an internal medicine clinic because of abnormal behavior; however, no diagnostic evaluation for intracranial pathology was performed. He was emergently transported to our hospital due to cognitive decline and disturbance of consciousness. Upon arrival at the hospital, he exhibited signs of disorientation and aphasia. No instances of paralysis were observed. His blood pressure was 114/72 mmHg, and laboratory findings showed no significant abnormalities. Head CT revealed high-density areas in the bilateral thalami (Fig. 1A). T2*-weighted MRI showed low-intensity areas in the bilateral thalami (Fig. 1B), and FLAIR imaging showed sparse hyperintensity in the bilateral thalami (Fig. 1C). However, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) revealed no hyperintense lesions (Fig. 1D). The superior vermian vein, vein of Galen, basal vein of Rosenthal, and internal cerebral veins were identified on MRA (Fig. 1E and 1F). The following day, CT venography revealed occlusion of the SS (Fig. 1G). Cerebral angiography confirmed the presence of a medial tentorial DAVF; the shunt was located in the midline region of the cerebellar tentorium, with feeders arising from the posterior meningeal artery and tentorial artery. The draining vein was the superior vermian vein. The SS was occluded, and venous congestion of the deep cerebral veins was observed. It was classified as Borden type III and Cognard type III. On the 3rd hospital day, TVE and sinus angioplasty of the SS were performed.

Endovascular surgery

Preoperative DSA revealed that the occipital artery (OA) branched from the right vertebral artery (VA), and the mastoid branch of the OA connected to the posterior meningeal artery, feeding the shunt (Fig. 2A and 2C–2E). We considered this route unsuitable for transarterial embolization (TAE) due to its long and tortuous course to the shunt point. Angiography of the right VA in the delayed arterial phase demonstrated venous drainage into the cavernous sinus via the uncal vein from the basal vein of Rosenthal (Fig. 2B). Angiography of the right internal carotid artery (ICA) demonstrated that the tentorial artery, serving as a feeder, originated from the inferolateral trunk (ILT). However, navigating a catheter was considered difficult because of the steep angulation at the origin of the ILT (Fig. 2F and 2G). On the other hand, we noted that the occipital sinus was well-developed and provided favorable access to the occluded SS. Considering the feasibility of sinus angioplasty following shunt occlusion, TVE was selected.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia. An 8-Fr sheath was inserted through the right femoral vein, and a 4-Fr sheath was inserted into the left femoral artery. Thereafter, 4000 units of heparin were administered. A 4-Fr OK-1 catheter (Gadelius Medical, Tokyo, Japan) for angiography was inserted into the right VA. An 8-Fr Roadmaster 90 cm (Goodman, Aichi, Japan) was inserted into the right internal jugular vein. Through the 8-Fr Roadmaster, a 6-Fr Navien 115 cm (Medtronic, Irvine, CA, USA) was navigated to the proximal SS via the occipital sinus. The occluded SS was carefully crossed using an Excelsior SL-10 (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) microcatheter and a CHIKAI 014 200 cm (Asahi Intecc, Aichi, Japan), which were then advanced to the shunt point. Coil embolization was subsequently performed to achieve complete shunt occlusion. The embolization was accomplished using 4 coils, i-ED Coil 12 Complex Infini Silky Soft 2–3 mm × 4 cm (Kaneka Medix, Osaka, Japan), and 2 coils, i-ED Coil 12 Complex Infini Silky Soft 1 mm × 3 cm. The Excelsior SL-10 was then exchanged for a UNRYU XP 2.5 mm × 10-mm balloon catheter (Kaneka Medix) using a CHIKAI Extension (Asahi Intecc). Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) was performed in the SS by adjusting the balloon position (Fig. 2H). Angiography from the right VA and ICA showed that the shunt had disappeared and that the reconstructed SS was patent (Fig. 2I, 2K and 2L). Postoperative angiography from the right VA in the delayed arterial phase revealed the disappearance of the drainage pathway. A change in venous drainage involving the cavernous sinus was observed between the preoperative and postoperative angiography (Fig. 2L).

Anesthesia was induced with propofol and fentanyl and maintained with remifentanil. The blood pressure at the initiation of surgery was 110/67 mmHg. Following PTA, systolic blood pressure increased to 140–160 mmHg and was managed with a combination of propofol and nicardipine. Intraoperative urine output was 500 mL.

Postoperative course

Postoperatively, systolic blood pressure was maintained below 140 mmHg with continuous nicardipine infusion at 12 mg/h. Oral calcium channel blocker therapy was initiated on postoperative day 1, and the nicardipine dose was reduced to 2 mg/h on the following day and discontinued on postoperative day 3. MRI performed on postoperative day 1 revealed no hyperintense lesions on DWI, indicating the absence of irreversible changes (Fig. 3A). FLAIR imaging demonstrated increased hyperintensity in the bilateral thalami compared with the preoperative findings (Fig. 3B). On the same day, laboratory tests revealed hypernatremia with a serum sodium level of 157 mEq/L. Because the patient had diabetes, the elevation was initially thought to be due to osmotic diuresis caused by hyperglycemia. Glycemic control was initiated using insulin, and intravenous fluids were administered to correct the electrolyte imbalance. The following day, polyuria persisted (urine output: 3550 mL/day), accompanied by a further rise in serum sodium to 164 mEq/L. Despite adequate intravenous fluid administration, urine output remained elevated at over 3000 mL/day. On postoperative day 8, we conducted a water deprivation test followed by administration of I-deamino-8-D-arginine vasopressin (DDAVP). The absence of a decrease in urine output during water restrictions, combined with a marked reduction in urine volume and an increase in urine specific gravity after DDAVP administration, led to the diagnosis of CDI. DDAVP therapy was initiated on postoperative day 9, resulting in improved urine output (Fig. 4).

On postoperative day 1, an elevation in serum sodium levels was observed. Despite glycemic control and intravenous fluid administration, hypernatremia persisted, and urine output progressively increased over time. On postoperative day 8, a water deprivation test followed by DDAVP administration revealed a persistently low urine specific gravity, leading to a diagnosis of CDI. As his serum sodium levels normalized, the patient’s mental status improved, and his speech became clearer. However, cognitive dysfunction persisted. He was transferred to a rehabilitation ward on day 21. From postoperative day 58, DDAVP was gradually tapered and discontinued on day 68, without recurrence of polyuria or electrolyte disturbance. He was discharged home on postoperative day 72. At 6 months postoperatively, brain MRI T2*-weighted imaging showed reduction of the bilateral thalamic hypointense signals (Fig. 3C). On FLAIR imaging at the same time, the hyperintense lesions in the bilateral thalami had disappeared and became hypointense (Fig. 3D). Susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) also demonstrated corresponding hypointense signals, and these findings were considered to represent the chronological evolution of hemorrhagic components (Fig. 3E). DSA demonstrated no evidence of residual shunt, with SS patency maintained (Fig. 3F–3I). Mild attentional impairment remained 6 months after onset, with a modified Rankin Scale score of 1. No recurrence of CDI was observed.

Discussion

In medial tentorial DAVFs with marked deep venous congestion, patients often present with impaired consciousness,2) which may delay the recognition of concomitant electrolyte abnormalities. As demonstrated in the present case, postoperative management should include careful monitoring for CDI.

Endovascular treatment options for medial tentorial DAVFs are TAE and/or TVE. TAE is considered a primary treatment option, especially in non-sinus-type DAVFs.4) However, TAE may be associated with significant procedural risks in cases where feeders have a high risk of refluxing embolic material into the vasa nervorum or have dangerous anastomoses with the ICA system.5–7) On the other hand, TVE can serve as a curative treatment if complete occlusion of the shunt is achieved. Nevertheless, in cases with multiple venous drainage pathways, abrupt hemodynamic changes at the time of shunt occlusion may lead to rupture of fragile venous structures, resulting in hemorrhagic complications.5,8) In the tentorial region, dilated and elongated cortical veins are frequently observed. These veins are considered to increase the risk of perforation or other intraprocedural complications during embolization.9,10) As far as we know, there are currently no reports on sinus angioplasty of the SS. However, previous reports have described sinus angioplasty for DAVFs involving the transverse–sigmoid sinus complex to improve venous outflow.11) Due to the absence of an accessible arterial route, TAE was not feasible in this case. Despite the technical difficulties and inherent risks, we opted for TVE combined with sinus angioplasty of the SS. As a result, long-term patency of the SS was achieved, allowing for more physiological venous drainage.

ADH is primarily synthesized in the magnocellular neurons of the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus. It is transported via axonal pathways to the posterior pituitary, from which it is released into the systemic circulation. The secretion of ADH is predominantly regulated by osmoreceptors located in the anterior wall of the 3rd ventricle, as well as by atrial volume receptors and arterial baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch. Plasma ADH levels increase sharply when plasma osmolality exceeds approximately 280 mOsm/kg. Through stimulation of V2 receptors in the renal collecting ducts, ADH promotes the reabsorption of free water, thereby maintaining serum sodium concentration within a narrow physiological range. Conversely, when the effective circulating volume decreases by more than 10%–20% and is accompanied by hypotension, ADH secretion is also stimulated. In such cases, ADH acts on V1a receptors in vascular smooth muscle to induce vasoconstriction, contributing to the restoration of blood pressure.12)

We found no previous reports of the development of CDI following treatment for DAVF. In the present case, CDI occurred postoperatively and persisted for an extended period. However, as plasma levels of ADH were not measured preoperatively or intraoperatively, the underlying mechanism remains speculative.

It is plausible that the deep venous congestion caused by the arteriovenous shunt had already compromised hypothalamic function to some extent. The hypothalamus normally drains into the cavernous sinus. However, as demonstrated by preoperative and postoperative SWI, preoperative FLAIR abnormalities likely reflected retrograde flow into the deep venous system, resulting in venous congestion and vascular ectasia. The subsequent postoperative evolution into bilateral thalamic hyperintensity is considered to represent the resolution of venous ectasia after treatment, accompanied by the unmasking and persistence of edematous changes. These imaging findings, together with the prolonged postoperative polyuria, suggest that retrograde venous drainage from the deep venous system into the cavernous sinus, which also serves as a drainage pathway for the hypothalamus, resulted in impaired hypothalamic venous outflow. This impaired outflow may have contributed to transient hypothalamic dysfunction and a delayed recovery of its function. In this case, intraoperative hypertension was observed, and blood pressure management proved challenging. Following shunt occlusion and successful sinus angioplasty of the SS, the potential mechanism underlying the observed rise in blood pressure involved the release of ADH from the congested veins, which subsequently entered the circulatory system. This transient increase in circulating ADH levels may have prevented the onset of CDI during the procedure. In fact, no intraoperative polyuria was noted.

During the procedure, anesthetic management included an increased administration of propofol and remifentanil to control intraoperative hypertension. Both agents are known to suppress ADH synthesis in the hypothalamus.13) There have been prior reports of CDI triggered by propofol administration.14,15) In this case, it is conceivable that these anesthetic agents exerted a suppressive effect on an already compromised hypothalamus, further reducing ADH production.

It was presumed that the polyuria and electrolyte abnormalities observed in the intensive care unit were due to depletion of stored ADH, which was not followed by sufficient new synthesis and secretion. This may explain the absence of intraoperative polyuria despite the subsequent development of CDI postoperatively.

While previously reported cases of anesthesia-induced CDI have shown resolution within the perioperative period, the present case demonstrated persistent polyuria and hypernatremia beyond this timeframe.14,15) The patient had comorbid DM and developed marked hyperglycemia postoperatively, likely due to CDI-induced polyuria. There are case reports of CDI developing after hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS), a condition characterized by profound hyperglycemia.16) In those reports, CDI persisted for several weeks even after glycemic control was achieved, which is consistent with the clinical course observed in our case. It is possible that the prolonged CDI in this case resulted from a combination of pathophysiological changes related to the DAVF, anesthesia-induced CDI, and HHS-related CDI.

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, measurement of plasma ADH levels before and during treatment is warranted, and accumulation of further cases is needed to better understand this phenomenon.

Conclusion

We report a rare case of CDI following treatment of a medial tentorial DAVF. To our knowledge, this is the first documented case of CDI occurring after treatment of a medial tentorial DAVF. Given the profound venous congestion often observed in deep cerebral veins associated with such lesions, similar pathophysiological responses may occur postoperatively. In cases with significant deep venous congestion, patients frequently present with impaired consciousness, making it difficult to promptly detect electrolyte disturbances due to CDI. Therefore, meticulous postoperative monitoring of urine output and serum electrolyte levels is essential to ensure early recognition and appropriate management of this potentially serious complication.
