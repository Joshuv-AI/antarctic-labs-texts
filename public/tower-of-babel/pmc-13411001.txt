Conical intersection induced by the Jahn–Teller effect in molecular dynamic simulations
Authors: Ya Peng, Hui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study elucidates the role of a conical intersection induced by the Jahn–Teller effect (CIJT) in modulating the reactivity of the oxygen–methane reaction, a prototypical system involving excited states. Using ab initio calculations, we constructed a diabatic potential energy matrix to characterize the molecular structure at the CIJT and its associated twin saddle points. These structural features orchestrate population transfer between electronic states, with the CIJT serving as a critical junction for interstate transitions. It involves an interplay between two excited states and introduces a geometric phase effect, resulting in destructive interference that manifests as distinct nodal patterns when a wave packet is launched through the system. These characteristics significantly influence the reaction pathway, altering the accessibility of competing electronic states and thus modulating reactivity. Our findings thus establish a direct connection between JT induced topology and nonadiabatic reactivity, highlighting a mechanism by which conical intersections actively control excited-state dynamics in reactive intermediates.

Introduction

The oxidation of methane has attracted considerable attention across energy science, environmental studies, and safety engineering, playing a pivotal role in combustion chemistry, atmospheric chemistry, and even interstellar chemistry. As the simplest hydrocarbon and the principal component of natural gas, methane's reactivity with oxygen initiates chain processes that control product distributions and energy release. Experimental determinations of the rate constant for reaction CH4 + O → CH3 + OH span many orders of magnitude near room temperature (roughly 10−19 to 10−16 cm3 per molecule per s) and converge toward larger values at high temperature (10−11–10−10 cm3 per molecule per s for 1500–2500 K),1 while the NASA data evaluation recommendation for atmospheric model calculations currently assumes that atmospheric branching ratios for CH3 + OH as 75 ± 15% after accounting for oxygen atom collisional quenching and potential singlet–triplet kinetics.2 Despite considerable theoretical and experimental effort, detailed mechanistic understanding of the nonadiabatic steps that control branching and reactivity, particularly under excited-state conditions, remains incomplete.

The Jahn–Teller effect (JTE) and the associated geometric phase (GP) provide a compelling framework for understanding nonadiabatic structure and dynamics in systems with (near-)degenerate electronic states. The JTE arises from coupling between electronic and nuclear motion,3 breaking the Born–Oppenheimer approximation and shaping molecular dynamics across chemistry and physics.4–7 In its canonical E⊗e form, a doubly degenerate electronic state couples to a pair of degenerate vibrational modes, producing a conical intersection induced by Jahn–Teller effect (CIJT) at high-symmetry geometries such as C3v or D3h (see SI). Conical intersection acts as ultrafast funnels for non-adiabatic transitions and governs photochemical reactivity.8–12 One of defining features is the GP, which generates interference as atoms moving encircle around, profoundly affecting wave packet motion.13–15 These effects have been observed in small molecules and extended systems, and recent experiments demonstrate that the GP can be exploited to control non-adiabatic dynamics.6,7,16–20 Quantum wave-packet methods are widely regarded as the gold standard for treating JTE and GP effects, as they properly resolve the breakdown of classical trajectories in the vicinity of conical intersections.21,22 Their high computational cost, however, limits applications to relatively simple systems. In the context of reactive scattering dynamics, Xie and co-workers23 have successfully extended GP studies from simple direct reactions to complex-forming reactions, while Zhang and co-workers24 combined quantum wave-packet calculations with experiment to demonstrate backward-scattering oscillations in the low-energy regime, providing clear signatures of GP effects.

In the prototypical reaction of methane with triplet oxygen (CH4 + O(3P) → CH3 + OH), JTE and GP effect govern the interplay between twin saddle points (SPs) and the CIJT.25–27 Two nearly degenerate SPs are situated in close proximity to a C3v CIJT, distinguished by subtle variations in the CHO bending angle.28–36 Unlike the stable minima commonly associated with the JTE, the non-stationary CIJT strongly modulates nuclear motion in its vicinity, leading to interference and nodal structures in wave packets that dictate non-adiabatic reactivity. Through the integration of electronic structure calculations and wave packet dynamics, this study elucidates the mechanistic interplay of the twin SPs and CIJT, revealing their pivotal role in non-adiabatic dynamics.30,31,37,38

Most previous theoretical studies treated the two symmetry-related SPs as independent reactive bottlenecks on one single adiabatic potential energy surface (APES).30,31,37,38 Whether the CIJT dynamically couples these pathways and how the associated GP affects the reaction dynamics remain largely unexplored. The objective of the present work is therefore not merely to investigate these issues in this system, but to establish the role of the CI as a dynamical hub connecting the twin SPs pathways. The paper is structured as follows: Section 2 describes the electronic-structure calculations, the construction of the diabatic potential-energy matrix and the reduced-dimensional quantum dynamics. Section 3 presents and discusses the resulting potential-energy features and dynamical signatures, with an emphasis on the geometric-phase effects. Finally, Section 4 summarizes the main findings and outlines implications for broader reactive systems.

Methods

Electronic structure calculations

The methane–oxygen radical reaction CH4 + O(3P) → CH3 + OH presents near-degenerate electronic states and CIs. Although single-reference methods such as CCSD(T) have been widely applied to this system,30,34 single-reference approaches give an unbalanced description of regions with strong static correlation and near-degeneracy, and therefore may not yield quantitatively reliable relative energetics around crossings. To obtain a balanced treatment of the ground and low-lying excited states and of the crossing region, we adopted a state-averaged complete active space self-consistent field (SA-CASSCF)39,40 reference and included dynamic correlation via internally-contracted multireference configuration interaction (icMRCI).41–43 The SA-CASSCF reference ensures a consistent multireference description of the electronic configurations relevant to the CI region, while icMRCI recovers a substantial portion of dynamic correlation on top of that reference, improving energetic accuracy for single-point energies. Crucially, for our diabatization strategy, icMRCI yields state-consistent CI vectors that permit CI-coefficient-based quasi-diabatization and the construction of a smooth diabatic potential energy matrix (DPEM) for the two lowest triplet states studied in this work.

Although the subsequent potential energy surface (PES) construction and dynamics calculations focus on the lowest two triplet states, SA-CASSCF calculations averaging over four electronic states (13A, 23A, 33A and 11A) are employed in this work. This approach provides a balanced and reliable description of the minimum-energy crossings (MEXs), taking into account the characteristics of the reaction and the nature of the electronic states involved. This strategy ensures convergence of the ab initio iterative procedure while consistently accounting for higher-lying electronic states. Crossings among the PESs introduce significant complexity into this radical reaction. In particular, the MEXs between the first excited triplet state (23A) and the second excited triplet state (33A) occur at substantially higher energies than those between the ground (13A) and first excited triplet state (23A). Consequently, to capture the influence of crossings involving higher-energy surfaces above 1.06 eV, the second triplet excited state (33A) is included in the electronic-structure calculations. However, this state is not included in the subsequent surface-fitting procedure. Regarding the singlet–triplet (11A–13A) crossing,28,29,33 the spin–orbit coupling remains small across all geometries (Fig. S3), suggesting that intersystem crossing is unlikely to play a significant role in the dynamics. Consequently, this mechanism is beyond the scope of the present study. In the four-state calculations, only a single higher singlet state (11A) is included, while contributions from other singlet states are totally neglected.

The Dunning's correlation-consistent basis set cc-pVDZ is chosen to ensure numerical stability and accurate electronic structure calculations. Within a fixed basis set, gradually expanding the active space and extrapolating the resulting energies and optimized geometries is a well-established strategy for approaching the full configuration interaction limit. The active space is denoted as CAS(e, o), where e is the number of active electrons and o is the number of active orbitals. As either e or o increases, the number of configurations in the configuration interaction expansion grows combinatorially, leading to a rapid increase in computational cost. In the present work, the full-valence active space corresponds to CAS(14e, 12o). Calculations are carried out sequentially for CAS(14e, 10o), CAS(14e, 11o), and CAS(14e, 12o), and the corresponding numbers of configuration state functions (NCSF) are collected in Table 1. Details of the ab initio procedure are provided in the SI.

In this study, the MOLPRO 2008.1 package was utilized for performing ab initio calculations of all electronic structures. At each nuclear geometry, the eigenstates of the electronic Hamiltonian are well-defined and can be labeled as 11A and 13A or, in the local symmetry-adapted basis, 3A″ and 3A′. These points were computed in C1 symmetry, without imposing any symmetry constraints. For the analysis and presentation of electronic structure near the conical-intersection seam, we systematically exploit Cs symmetry at geometries that preserve a reflection plane (σ-plane). This symmetry approach is both physically justified and widely adopted in previous studies of the CH4 + O(3P) system when examining intersection regions.50–54 In the adiabatic representation, uadi1 and uadi2 are employed to describe the lowest two triplets state surfaces. In contrast, the diabatic representation is constructed to minimize derivative couplings, and the basis functions are not exact eigenstates at each geometry. Consequently, one employs the diabatic matrix elements Wdia11 and Wdia22, together with the coupling Wdia12, rather than assigning irreducible representations directly.

Dynamics simulations

Reduced-dimensional dynamics has been widely and successfully employed in the study of the ABC3 + D → AC3 + BD reaction.55,56 Numerous studies have shown that four- or even lower-dimensional rigid-body models are sufficient to capture the dominant reaction pathway and the essential nonadiabatic couplings.57–59 A 4D model60 has been used in the quantum scattering calculations for CH4 + Cl → CH3 + HCl reaction. The calculated thermal rate constants are 12–45% smaller than the experimental results over the temperature range 200–800 K. Some simpler models, with only 3D freedom,61 have been employed to study the relevance of the umbrella mode in this CH4 + O(3P) → CH3 + OH reaction, in which all the C–H bond lengths of CH3 are fixed. They found in the calculations that the final vibrational distribution of the umbrella mode in product (methyl group –CH3) is not inverted, which agrees with experimental results. These studies demonstrate that a reduced-dimensional description can reproduce the essential physics near the SP and the CI region. As for the 2D model,16,22,62 it is widely used in quantum wave packet dynamics to elucidate the role of the GP. The phase acquired by an arbitrary closed loop encircling a CI in full-dimensional nuclear coordinate space is identical to that accumulated by its projection onto the 2D branching space used in this work. This equivalence arises from two key facts: (i) the gauge invariance of the geometric phase,63 and (ii) the CI geometry being fully optimized in the full-dimensional space. In the present work, we adopt a reduced-dimensional approach, selecting four key coordinates for both PES construction and quantum wave-packet simulations; the coordinate definitions and numerical parameters are summarized in Table 2. Under these conditions, the reduced-dimensional treatment is expected to reliably reproduce the qualitative features of geometric-phase-induced interference patterns and the resulting nodal structures. The transferability of such models, however, has clear limits. A full-dimensional description becomes necessary when quantitatively accurate observable, such as state-resolved reaction probabilities, branching ratios, or rovibrational product distributions are targeted. In these cases, multidimensional energy redistribution and couplings involving nominal spectator modes may become important, requiring a full-dimensional treatment for quantitative predictive power. Wave-packet simulations were carried out on the newly constructed DPEM (see Time-dependent quantum wave-packet calculations in the SI for details).

The 4D DPEM is specifically designed to capture the dominant couplings in the CH4 + O(3P) reaction; near the CI seam, nonadiabatic interactions are governed primarily by the O–C approach coordinate and the umbrella-inversion motion of methane. We note explicitly that the dynamical manifestations of the GP depend on the initial nuclear preparation and on the translational energy of the incoming wave packet. In the simulations, the initial wave packet is prepared in the electronic ground state with methane in its ground ro-vibrational level. The initial wave packet is localized near the MEX (at a distance of 0.7 Bohr from the MEX) and is prepared as a Gaussian-shaped wave packet representing the electronic ground state. This low-energy initial condition is chosen to clearly reveal GP induced interference effects within the g–h branching space, thereby highlighting the characteristic nodal structures arising from destructive GP interference in the near-threshold regime.

Results and discussion

JT-induced orbital degeneracy splitting

The geometric parameters with the total energies, optimized geometries of stationary points along with the MEX are provided in Tables 3 and S1. The ab initio calculated C–H bond distance in methane for the CH4 + O(3P) reactant is 2.0709 Bohr, in close agreement with the experimental values,64,65 suggesting that the chosen ab initio calculation method provides a reasonable description of the CH4 + O(3P) reaction system. A triplet oxygen atom forms a transient van der Waals complex with methane via weak O–H interactions, its approach along the reaction coordinate dictating the symmetry of complex and reaction pathway. The evolution of molecular electrostatic potential (ESP) surfaces during MEX formation is shown in Fig. 1, with top-view contour cuts. At a large O–C distance of 6.50 Bohr, the ESP of methane retains near-tetrahedral (Td) symmetry with minimal distortion, while the oxygen atom exhibits approximate D∞h (triplet, Fig. 1a) or C2v (singlet, Fig. 1c) symmetry due to weak intermolecular interactions. In O(3P), two 2p electrons occupy separate half-filled p orbitals perpendicular to the C3 axis, yielding near-D∞h symmetry. In contrast, O(1D) has two 2p electrons doubly occupying one p orbital, resulting in C2v symmetry. Top-view contours indicate that O(3P) favors a linear C–H–O configuration in the van der Waals complex or MEX, while O(1D) leads to a barrierless abstraction reaction without any stationary point, consistent with previous report.27 As the O–H distance decreases to 2.2006 Bohr, the oxygen binds to hydrogen, forming a trigonal pyramidal geometry at the MEX. At the MEX, with an O–C distance of 4.82 Bohr in Fig. 1b, the new C–H bond is longer than a typical C–H bond but shorter than a hydrogen bond. The Jahn–Teller effect lifts the orbital degeneracy, stabilizing the asymmetric trigonal pyramidal structure.30,31,66 The outer region along the bond extension exhibits lower electron density and a positive electrostatic potential, forming a σ-hole as the oxygen atom's polarizability decreases relative to the inner O–H moiety. This σ-hole appears near the oxygen along the O–H direction, with a surface area of 8.53 Bohr2 and an average ESP of 0.03438 a.u., consistent with characteristic σ-hole behavior.

Positioned near the highest energy along the reaction path, the MEX triggers a JTE distortion, shown by changes in the oxygen 2p orbitals (Fig. 2 and S2). This distortion stems from the degeneracy of the 2px and 2py orbitals under C3v symmetry. Coupling to non-totally symmetric vibrational modes reduces the symmetry to Cs, lifting the E-state degeneracy and driving the system toward twin SPs. At the linear C–H–O configuration with C3v symmetry, the 2px and 2py orbitals are degenerate, while the 2pz orbital, oriented along the C3 axis, overlaps weakly with the hydrogen 1s orbital, partially stabilizing the high-symmetry geometry. The MEX is non-stationary, with the largest gradient components of the 3A″ and 3A′ states in the σ plane, and is represented in Fig. 3. Slight deviations from C3v lift the orbital degeneracy: for ∠CHO >180°, the 2px orbital energy exceeds that of 2py; for ∠CHO <180°, the reverse occurs. Gradients vanish near minima, and pseudorotation about the C3 axis reveals no additional crossings (Fig. 4). The widening energy gap between the lowest two states underscores JT-induced orbital splitting as the key mechanism guiding the system from the MEX to the twin SPs, shaping the non-adiabatic reaction pathway. Surfaces in gh-plane

The crossing line through the MEX point represents the points at which the two electronic states, in this case, the 3A″ and 3A′ states, have the same energy. Beyond this point, the degeneracy of these states is progressively lifted in a linear manner along two orthogonal directions via the von Neumann–Wigner theorem.67 These directions define a branching space, and every symmetry transformation in this space can be represented by a unitary operator, up to a phase factor. In the present study, the presence of a prominent JTE necessitates a description of molecular dynamics near CIs in terms of the ideas used to characterize the spectra in JT systems. In these systems, one typically distinguishes between JT active modes, which include the tuning and coupling modes that contribute to the description of the double cone for the two states in the branching (g–h) plane. The remaining N-2 inactive modes, on the other hand, span an intersection space. The coupling mode is the coupling gradient (non-adiabatic coupling) vector h⃑ in the adiabatic representation with 3A″ symmetry required to coupled 3A″ and 3A′ states. The tuning mode is the energy different gradient vector g⃑ with 3A′ symmetry. The ab initio APES and DPEM are constructed in our work by reduced-dimensional approaches. The APES and DPEM surfaces are depicted in 3D surfaces in Fig. 6 to describe the vicinity of the MEX in g–h plane. The electronic state degeneracy is broken, resulting in the formation of a double-cone PES in these coordinates. In Fig. 6a, the conical is represented at the point of highest symmetry, where the two APESs are degenerate. The lower one reveals a lower cone located at the coordinate origin, bordered by three-lobed parts, resembling a shamrock. The Fig. 6b and c present the diagonal and off-diagonal elements of the DPEM around the CI region, and illustrate the corresponding first two lowest diabatic states. More contours of the adiabatic and diabatic potential surfaces are represented in Fig. S6a–g, respectively. Although the scale is necessarily large for Wdia11, Wdia12, and Wdia22, their smoothness is evident. This finding provides valuable insights into the electronic structure of the system and highlights the critical features of the APESs governing its dynamics.

Wavepacket interference

The GP plays a central role in nonadiabatic dynamics, introducing phase differences and interference effects that critically shape the wavefunction evolution.68–70 To illustrate this effect in the vicinity of the CIJT near the transition state region, time-dependent wave packet simulations71–74 are carried out on two different models constructed by neural network fitting.75,76 In the diabatic representation, the potential energy matrix includes Wdia11, Wdia22, and the antisymmetric coupling Wdia12, while in the adiabatic representation the two APESs (uadi1 and uadi2) are treated separately.

Representative snapshots taken every 130 fs shortly after the wave packet traverses the MEX are shown in Fig. 7. The modulus of the wave function exposes both population redistribution and interference patterns that originate from the GP. In the diabatic coupled model (Fig. 7a and c), a pronounced nodal line appears on the g > 0 side of the CI region, a direct hallmark of GP-induced destructive interference. Red and black crosses indicate geometries with ∠CHO = 179.8° and 179.7°, corresponding to the two SPs, which lie close to the CI in the g–h plane. The wave-packet patterns at these SPs differ markedly: the packet launched from the left SP is split by the CI and appears effectively bisected at the right SP, which is a direct manifestation of the path-dependent phase accumulated around the CI. By contrast, the adiabatic uncoupled model (Fig. 7b and d), which omits the GP, shows no nodal structure and instead exhibits a smoother density evolution: the packet is initially disrupted by the CI but rapidly recombines at the right SP. Further comparisons among diabatic and adiabatic uncoupled simulations are provided in Fig. S9 and S10, and consistently show qualitatively different wave-packet distributions before and after passage of the CI, underscoring the strong topological influence of the CI and GP on nuclear dynamics. The magnitude and spatial pattern of GP-induced interference are very sensitive to (at least) two aspects of the initial preparation of wave packet: the initial width and translational energy. Depending on the initial width, this can increase the fraction of amplitude that encircles the CI (enhancing GP contrast) or broaden the sampling so that destructive interference between encircling pathways partially wash out (reducing nodal contrast). Thus the qualitative appearance of nodal lines and the timescale of population transfer both depend on the initial width. As for the initial translational energy, a larger incident translational momentum increases the wave packet's kinetic momentum in the approach coordinate and generally shorten the interaction time with the CI region. Shorter interaction time reduce the time available to develop coherent phase differences between encircling pathways and may reduce the visibility of nodal structures; conversely, at near-threshold (low) translational energies the packet spends more time in the CI catchment region and GP effects are more pronounced. Moreover, higher translational energy may populated different approach geometries, changing the relative weight of encircling versus direct non-encircling passages.

A more quantitative characterization of the dynamical behavior is provided in Fig. 8. In the diabatic representation Fig. 8a, the population of the initially occupied upper electronic state begins to decrease at approximately ∼60 fs. This time scale corresponds to the moment when the propagating wave packet first reaches the closed region of the CI, where strong nonadiabatic coupling enables population transfer between the two electronic states at approximately ∼100 fs. The population subsequently drops rapidly to about 0.25 by ∼120 fs and then approaches constant thereafter, indicating that the main nonadiabatic transition event occurs during a relatively short time window as the packet traverses the CI region. In contrast, the adiabatic uncoupled model that neglects the GP produces qualitatively different electronic state population dynamics in Fig. 8b. In this approximation one adiabatic state remains almost completely populated throughout the simulation window, while the other state remains nearly zero. This behavior reflects the absence of the GP induced interference that normally arises when nuclear motion explores pathways that partially encircle the conical intersection. Without this phase information, the model fails to capture the correct nonadiabatic population transfer mechanism. Similarly, the autocorrelation function S(t), in Fig. S11, decays to zero by ∼80 fs in the diabatic model (indicating departure from the CI region), whereas it remains appreciable beyond 160 fs in the GP-neglected adiabatic uncoupled model, implying an unphysical long-lived localization near the CI.

Taken together, these results highlight that the conical intersection does not merely act as a region of population transfer but also serves as a topological source of phase accumulation. When the nuclear wave packet evolves along pathways that partially encircle the CI, it acquires a Berry phase of π, leading to destructive interference and the formation of nodal structures in the wavefunction. This phase-sensitive interference fundamentally alters the spatial distribution and population dynamics of the wave packet, demonstrating that GP effects are essential for a physically correct description of the nonadiabatic dynamics in the CIJT region.

Wave-packet simulations performed directly on a diabatic potential-energy matrix remain uncommon for this reaction; to our knowledge, the present study provides one of the first explicit demonstrations of GP-induced interference in such a diabatic framework. Unlike previous studies which primarily treated the twin SPs as quasi-independent reactive bottlenecks, our results demonstrate that the CIJT acts as a topological hub that dynamically couples the twin SPs via GP-driven interference. This coupling leads to destructive interference and nodal structures in the nuclear wave packet, which are entirely absent in GP-neglected descriptions with the uncoupled adiabatic PESs model. This behavior goes beyond the established picture of non-adiabatic transitions as simple population transfer near a CI, and instead reveals phase-sensitive control of reaction pathways in a prototypical radical reaction.

Conclusion

This work employs high-level ab initio electronic-structure calculations and time-dependent quantum wave packet simulations to reveal the structural features of CIJT in the excited-state oxygen with methane reaction system. The newly constructed reduced-dimensional DPEM identifies the CIJT connected to twin SPs at CHO angles of 179.3° and 180.6°, with these nearly degenerate but configurationally distinct points arising from Jahn–Teller-induced symmetry breaking from C3v to Cs. Wave packet propagation on the diabatic surfaces visualizes GP-induced destructive interference, which manifests as a nodal line after 130 fs. This nodal line is absent in adiabatic uncoupled simulations that omit the GP. These molecular structural insights are clearly manifested in the calculated potential energy landscape: the lifted orbital degeneracy, the distorted trigonal pyramidal geometry at the MEX, and the double-cone topology in the g–h plane. They correlate directly with altered reactivity, influencing non-adiabatic transitions, population redistribution, and reaction pathways in this reactive intermediate. The GP dictates phase-sensitive dynamics, sustaining wave packet coherence beyond 160 fs in the diabatic description while accelerating decay without it. The present results reveal that the CI serves as a dynamical hub linking the twin SPs pathways through GP-induced interference. Consequently, the two pathways cannot always be regarded as independent reaction channels, providing a new mechanistic picture for the CH4 + O(3P) reaction beyond the conventional single-surface description. By providing smooth diabatic potentials, this study establishes quantitative structure reactivity relationships for excited-state processes, with broader implications for understanding non-adiabatic phenomena in photochemical and reactive systems involving unstable species and chemical intermediates.
