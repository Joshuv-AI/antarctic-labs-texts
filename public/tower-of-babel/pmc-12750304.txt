Comparing simultaneous integrated boost whole‐brain radiotherapy and local irradiation for brain metastases in lung cancer: A retrospective analysis
Authors: Qian Bi, Jing Shen, Xin Lian, Wentai Zhang, Fuquan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the differences between simultaneous integrated boost whole‐brain radiotherapy (SIB‐WBRT) and brain metastases local irradiation efficacy, and determine predictors of outcomes for lung cancer brain metastases patients in therapy.MethodsThis retrospective analysis evaluated patients with brain metastases treated with SIB‐WBRT (whole brain at 40 Gy/20 fractions, tumor boosted to 56–60 Gy/20 fractions) and localized irradiation (prescribed doses ranging from 20–60 Gy over 2–25 fractions, typically administered as 30 Gy/5 fractions, with a biologically effective dose [BED] of 28–100.8 Gy) at PUMCH between September 2015 and December 2021. The primary outcome was intracranial progression‐free survival (iPFS), with secondary outcomes encompassing overall survival (OS), development of new intracranial lesions, and tumor control. Propensity score matching was employed to compare the two retrospective groups. Survival curves for iPFS, OS, new intracranial lesions, and tumor control were generated and estimated using the Kaplan‐Meier approach. Cox proportional hazards modeling was applied to examine the relationships between pertinent variables and clinical outcomes.ResultsAfter Propensity score matching, 59 patients were enrolled in each group (SIB‐WBRT and local irradiation), with a total of 118 patients included in the analysis. No significant difference was observed between the 2 groups in iPFS (HR = 0.8, 95%CI: 0.4‐1.3, P = 0.288), OS (HR = 1.1, 95%CI: 0.7‐1.8, P = 0.653), or incidence of intracranial new lesions (HR = 0.6, 95%CI: 0.4‐1.2, P = 0.144). However, a statistically significant difference was noted in local control time between the groups, with local irradiation brain metastases being superior (HR = 0.4, 95% CI: 0.2‐0.8, P = 0.005). Tumor volume and other factors were related to local control.ConclusionCompared to local irradiation, SIB‐WBRT did not significantly improve iPFS in patients with brain metastases. Compared to SIB‐WBRT, local irradiation improved local control. Tumor volume and other factors may be associated with local control.

INTRODUCTION

Brain metastasis represent a significant and challenging complication in malignant tumors patients, 1 contributing to substantial neurological morbidity and poor survival outcomes. The brain metastases development not only precipitates a decline in quality of life and neurological function but also compromises the overall efficacy of oncologic therapy strategies. Consequently, the effective management of brain metastases remains a critical objective in improving patient prognosis and quality of life.

Radiotherapy remains a cornerstone in the brain metastases management, with simultaneous integrated boost whole‐brain radiotherapy (SIB‐WBRT) and local irradiation emerging as two principal approaches. SIB‐WBRT, which delivers a comprehensive dose to the entire intracranial volume while simultaneously boosting the tumor lesions, has demonstrated therapeutic efficacy. However, its extensive irradiation of healthy brain tissue raises concerns regarding neurotoxicity, including cognitive decline, and mood disturbances, and personality changes. Additionally, SIB‐WBRT does not provide complete protection against the emergence of new metastatic lesions, further survival and complicating long‐term disease control.

Advances in radiotherapy technology has underscored the potential of localized irradiation as an alternative therapy strategy to whole‐brain radiotherapy. The precision of local irradiation allows for targeted therapy of brain metastases, thereby sparing normal brain tissue and reducing neurocognitive toxicity. Nevertheless, the comparative efficacy and safety profiles of SIB‐WBRT and localized irradiation remain a topic of ongoing debate within the oncology community. Direct comparative studies are needed to elucidate the relative benefits and limitations of these 2 therapy modalities.

To address this gap, we conducted a retrospective analysis of survival outcomes, local control, and the development of new lesions in patients treated with either SIB‐WBRT or local irradiation at Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH). Our findings, combined with a review of relevant international studies, aim to provide evidence‐based insights into the optimal radiotherapeutic strategy for brain metastases.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient enrollment

This retrospective study included patients with brain metastases secondary to lung cancer who underwent either SIB‐WBRT or local irradiation at the Department of Radiation Oncology, PUMCH, between September 2015 to December 2021. 2 Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histologically confirmed lung cancer with radiologically confirmed brain metastases; (2) completion of SIB‐WBRT or local irradiation; (3) completion of the entire course of radiotherapy; and (4) availability of at least 1 post‐therapy imaging study to assess therapy response. 2 Patients were excluded if they lacked follow‐up data after therapy. This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of PUMCH (Approval Number: S‐K1982). 3

Data collection

Clinical and demographic data could be retrieved from the electronic medical records of PUMCH. Collected data included:

Clinical characteristics: primary tumor type, pathological subtype, molecular pathology findings, number and volume of metastatic lesions, extent of extracranial disease progression, dose fractionation and radiotherapy technique, and synchronous therapy. Patient characteristics: demographic data such as age and sex. Follow‐up data: intracranial lesion control, survival status, emergence of new lesions, and follow‐up duration.

Radiotherapy protocol

All patients underwent radiotherapy using a 6‐MV X‐ray linear accelerator. Then radiation delivery techniques included volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), fixed‐field intensity‐modulated radiotherapy (FF‐IMRT), and TomoTherapy. Patients were immobilized with a thermoplastic mask, and simulation was performed using 2–3 mm CT slices. Planning images were fused with 3D‐T1 enhanced MRI to delineate target volumes and organs at risk (OARs).

SIB‐WBRT

The clinical target volume (CTV) was defined as the entire brain parenchyma, while the gross tumor volume (GTV) included visible tumors on MRI. The planning target volume (PTV) was generated by expanding the CTV and GTV by 2–3 mm. The prescribed dose for the GTV was 56–60 Gy (median, 60 Gy) and 40 Gy for the CTV, both administered in 20 fractions (5 fractions per week). Dose constraints were set at ≤54 Gy to 0.03 cc of the brainstem and ≤8 Gy to 1% of the lens.

Brain metastasis local irradiation

The GTV was defined as the visible tumor volume on MRI. PTV was created by expanding the GTV by 2–3 mm. The prescribed dose ranged from 20–60 Gy (in 2–25 fractions), with the most common regimen being 30 Gy/5. The biological effective dose (BED) for the cohort ranged from 28–100.8 Gy.

For local irradiation of brain metastases, the GTV was delineated based on the tumor region visible on MRI scans. The GTV was extended by 2–3 mm to create the PTV. Prescribed doses for the GTV ranged from 20 to 60 Gy, delivered over 2 to 25 fractions, with the most frequent regimen being 30 Gy in 5 fractions. The BED across all patients varied from 28 to 100.8 Gy.

Patient follow‐up

Patient follow‐up were followed 1 month after therapy and subsequently every three months. Therapy response was assessed by enhanced MRI of the brain, and responses could be assessed based on the Response Assessment in Neuro‐Oncology criteria by experienced radiation oncologists. Adverse events were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint was intracranial progression‐free survival (iPFS), defined as the interval from the radiotherapy end to intracranial death or progression. 4 Progression was defined as radiologic evidence of recurrent or new intracranial lesions. 5

Secondary endpoints included: Overall survival (OS): Time from the radiotherapy end to last follow‐up or death. 6 Local control: Time from radiotherapy completion to last follow‐up or tumor recurrence. Time to new intracranial lesions: Time from the radiotherapy end to the new metastases detection.

Statistical analysis

A total of 188 patients met the inclusion criteria (before propensity score matching). Propensity score matching (1:1) was used to balance baseline characteristics between treatment groups, including sex, age, pathology type, number of tumors, primary tumor control status, targeted therapy administration, and tumor volume. Matching was performed using a multivariable logistic regression model with a caliper width of 0.02. Kaplan‐Meier analysis was used to estimate iPFS, OS, local control, and new lesion incidence. Cox proportional hazards models were used to compare outcomes between SIB‐WBRT and local irradiation, adjusting for potential confounders. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), GraphPad Prism (version 8.0), and SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). A two‐sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. 7

Radiation necrosis and adverse events

No radiation necrosis was observed during follow‐up in either treatment group.

Univariable and multivariable analyses

Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to determine predictors of iPFS, local control, and the incidence of new lesions.

In the univariable analysis, no significant predictors of iPFS or the incidence of new lesions were identified. However, several factors were associated with local control, including treatment modality, age, metastasis volume, and targeted therapy status. Compared to SIB‐WBRT, localized irradiation was independently related to improved local tumor control in the multivariable analysis. Larger metastasis volume was associated with poorer local control. Receipt of targeted therapy was related to improved local control. Regarding iPFS, multivariable analysis did not identify any significant predictive factors (P > 0.05). Similarly, no significant predictors of new lesion development were identified in the multivariable model (P > 0.05). See Tables 3, 4, and 5 for detailed results.

DISCUSSION

With a larger number of patients included in this study, and more segmentation patterns regarding local irradiation of brain metastases, the gap in this field can be effectively filled by the relevant data results. In addition, this research applied the propensity score matching method to minimize the influence of other factors further, and the results are more reliable. We summarized the local irradiation of brain metastases and SIB‐WBRT efficacy in controlling intracranial metastases in this single‐center retrospective research. The median values of iPFS were 17.4 (95% CI:10.2‐24.7) and 9.8 (95% CI:6.8‐12.8) months, respectively, and no difference was found to exist (HR = 0.8, 95% CI:0.4‐1.3, P = 0.288). Regarding toxicity, the radionecrosis was not observed, indicating safe and reliable efficacy of both techniques. And after further analysis, it was found that patient age, treatment modality, and others at the time of receiving head radiotherapy were independent prognostic factors possibly for local control.

Local irradiation of brain metastases presents significant superiority in local control of tumors. Several studies have been conducted to compare the efficacy of WBRT/WBRT+SRS and local irradiation of brain metastases, and these studies evaluated the local control of the two therapeutic strategies in patients with brain metastases. 8 , 9 , 10 In 1999, a retrospective study compared the efficacy of WBRT+SRS with that of SRS, and 1‐year local control in the two groups of WBRT+SRS and SRS were 79% and 71%, respectively (P>0.05), and no significant difference was observed. 11 The findings were consistent with another study. 8 In our study, the one‐year local control rate in the whole brain group was slightly lower due to the SIB‐WBRT technique. However, after further analysis, it was found that the local control in the local irradiation of brain metastases group was better than that in the SIB‐WBRT group, suggesting that local irradiation of brain metastases may be a promising therapeutic strategy. Compared with SIB‐WBRT, it showed more excellent results in achieving local control of tumors, probably because local irradiation of brain metastases can inhibit tumor growth and progression more effectively by targeting the brain metastases with precise localization and high dose radiation administration. However, higher‐level evidence is still needed to further validate the results.

No difference was observed between the two treatment modalities in terms of new lesion control. Most studies have various results in new lesion control between WBRT and local irradiation of brain metastases. 12 , 13 Japanese multicenter randomized controlled trial mainly compared the efficacy of WBRT plus SRS and SRS, the results of which were published in 2006, and the 1‐year intracranial new lesion control rate for both treatment modalities was 58% vs. 36% (P = 0.003). 14 Consistent with its results, ASTRO annual meeting in 2000 reported that WBRT alone and WBRT plus gamma knife were superior to gamma knife alone (81% and 77% vs. 57%, P‐value not stated). 15 However, our results did not find any difference in new lesion control between the two groups of SIB‐WBRT and local irradiation of brain metastases. It is possible that the inconsistency of the results was due to factors such as study design, sample selection, and treatment regimen. Ultimately, further studies and large‐scale clinical trials are still needed to evaluate and determine the role of localized irradiation of brain metastases and SIB‐WBRT in the control of new lesions.

Localized irradiation of brain metastases provides better protection of cognitive function. This viewpoint is supported by current research. Several studies have shown that localized irradiation of brain metastases is more effective in reducing the irradiated volume of brain tissue compared with whole‐brain radiotherapy, thereby reducing damage to normal brain tissue and hopefully reducing the risk of treatment‐related cognitive impairment. A prospective study compared the effects of local irradiation of brain metastases and whole‐brain radiotherapy on cognitive function. 16 The results of the study showed that patients who received localized irradiation of brain metastases presented better protection of cognitive function relative to those who received whole‐brain radiotherapy during the follow‐up period. Specifically, patients treated with localized irradiation of brain metastases demonstrated better memory, attention, and executive functioning on cognitive assessments. This finding provides meaningful guidance for treatment selection for patients with brain metastases, especially for those who need to protect cognitive function, such as younger patients with better self‐care skills. The mechanism of cognitive protection by localized irradiation of brain metastases, in addition to reducing the irradiated volume of brain tissue, may be that localized irradiation of brain metastases can target and treat brain metastatic lesions more accurately, and control the growth and progression of the brain metastases more effectively, thus better protecting the cognitive function of patients. 17

Localized irradiation of brain metastases may lead to the development of radionecrosis. As a serious complication, radionecrosis may have a significant impact on patients' quality of life and treatment outcome. In studies related to localized irradiation of brain metastases, some studies have reported that patients treated with localized irradiation of brain metastases developed radionecrosis at the time of subsequent review. 18 However, no radionecrosis occurrenced in our study maybe due to the limited sample size. It is possible that the occurrence of radionecrosis could not be observed because of the small sample size, or that some patients who had already developed radionecrosis were excluded from our research at subsequent follow‐up for various reasons. In addition, the assessment and judgment of radionecrosis is not an easy task. Non‐surgical methods to determine the difference between radionecrosis and tumor progression are challenging and often require multiple reviews or adjunctive functional imaging to assist in the determination.

LIMITATIONS

The findings of this study should be considered with the following constraints. Firstly, the study had a limited sample size and lacked a uniform chemotherapy control group. Secondly, incomplete data in patients’ medical records and inconsistent follow‐up for those with unfavorable brain metastases prognoses may have influenced the assessment of treatment outcomes. Thirdly, data regarding cognitive changes post‐treatment were not available. 19

CONCLUSION

Compared to local irradiation, no improvement in iPFS was found in patients with SIB‐WBRT. Local irradiation may be more beneficial for tumors local control. Age, treatment modality, metastasis volume, and targeted therapy status might be related to local control.
