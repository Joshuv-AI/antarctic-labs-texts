GABAergic projections from the suprachiasmatic nucleus to the preoptic area regulate the timing of torpor in mice
Authors: Sheikh Mizanur Rahaman, Shota Miyazaki, Chang-Ting Tsai, Akihiro Yamanaka, Chi Jung Hung, Michihiro Mieda, Takahiro J. Nakamura, Hiroshi Yamaguchi, Daisuke Ono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Torpor is a state of hypometabolism accompanied by a reduction in body temperature, which serves as a survival strategy enabling animals to endure harsh environments. While the circadian clock is thought to regulate the timing of torpor, the underlying neural mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we show that inhibitory neurotransmission via gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) in the central circadian clock, suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), is crucial for encoding the timing of torpor induction in female mice. Optogenetic activation of GABAergic neurons in the SCN projecting to the preoptic area (POA) suppresses torpor. These SCN neurons form monosynaptic connections with two distinct types of POA neurons: excitatory neurons that promote torpor induction, and inhibitory neurons that suppress the activity of these excitatory neurons. Furthermore, the disconnection of neural output in the SCN abolishes the timing of torpor. Retrograde tracing revealed that arginine vasopressin (Avp) neurons in the SCN innervate the POA, and selective deletion of vesicular GABA transporter in Avp neurons of the SCN leads to impaired torpor induction. This hypothalamic neuronal circuit is crucial in ensuring the survival of animals during harsh environmental conditions.

Introduction

Living organisms have evolved to respond to environmental changes driven by the rotation of the planet. Many animals adjust their reproductive and metabolic functions in response to seasonal shifts1. To detect daily changes in photoperiod and synchronize with their environment, they rely on their circadian clock. This internal timekeeping system aligns their circadian clock with the day-night cycle and precisely regulates the timing of essential physiological functions.

Almost all cells in the body contain the circadian clock2. In mammals, the central circadian clock is located in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus, which is critically important in regulating the timing of physiology and behaviors3,4. Furthermore, almost all SCN neurons are GABAergic and have neuronal and humoral outputs to other brain regions to regulate the timing of physiological functions, such as sleep/wakefulness, corticosterone release, and drinking behaviors5–8.

Among the various adaptive animal behaviors, torpor (daily torpor) and hibernation are unique strategies for surviving harsh winter conditions. By detecting changes in photoperiod and ambient temperature, certain animals reduce their metabolic rates, entering hibernation or torpor to conserve energy9,10. Laboratory mice also exhibit torpor when fasting in a cold environment11. These hypometabolic states differ primarily in duration, with hibernation being more prolonged and torpor relatively brief, typically occurring within a day. While the circadian clock has long been recognized as crucial for the regulation of torpor and hibernation12–14, the underlying neural mechanisms remain poorly understood. In this study, we used optogenetics, electrophysiology, neuronal tracing, and whole-brain neuronal activity mapping to identify previously uncharacterized hypothalamic circuits, cell types, and neurotransmitters that regulate the timing of torpor in mice.

Results

The circadian clock regulates the timing of torpor

To investigate the underlying mechanisms of the circadian clock-controlled torpor in mice, we first induced torpor in mice and measured core body temperature (TB) using a temperature logger implanted into the abdominal cavity. Female mice were chosen for this study because they seem to exhibit deeper torpor compared to male mice15. Additionally, Cry1/Cry2-deficient mice, which display arrhythmic behavior under constant darkness16, were used as a model for circadian output dysfunction. The body weight was not different between the two groups. To induce torpor, mice were subjected to a low ambient temperature (16 °C) and then to 24 h food deprivation under light-dark (LD) conditions, starting either at light onset (zeitgeber time 0: ZT 0) or dark onset (ZT 12). Wild-type (WT) mice displayed a decrease in TB from the middle of the dark phase to the beginning of the light phase. Whereas, Cry1/Cry2-deficient mice primarily exhibited a decrease in TB during the dark phase (Fig. 1a, c, e). We also confirmed that the estrous cycle did not affect TB during torpor (Supplementary Fig. 1). These results imply that the timing of torpor is regulated by the circadian clock. However, considering the experiment was conducted in LD cycles, torpor induction could be influenced by light masking. To eliminate this effect, we induced torpor in these mice under constant darkness (DD). Mice were exposed to a low ambient temperature and DD for 24–36 h, followed by 24 h food deprivation starting from extended ZT0 or ZT12. WT mice showed a decrease in TB around the middle of the subjective night to the beginning of the subjective day, which was similar to the daily distribution of occurrence of torpor under LD cycles. However, Cry1/Cry2-deficient mice showed a decrease in TB throughout the entire 24 h (Fig. 1b, d, f). Neuronal activities in the SCN suppress torpor

We next examined how the central circadian clock, the SCN, regulates the timing of torpor. To achieve this, we measured multi-unit activity (MUA) in the SCN in vivo (Fig. 2a, b). Torpor began to be induced at the phase of lowest neural activity in the SCN, and it was rarely observed during the middle of the day, a phase characterized by elevated SCN neuronal activity (Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Fig. 2). This led us to hypothesize that heightened SCN activity may suppress torpor induction during the day. To examine the relationship between SCN neuronal activity and torpor, we utilized Vgat-IRES-Cre mice crossed with LSL-ChR2-EYFP mice, enabling Cre-dependent expression of a blue light-gated cation channel fused to an enhanced yellow fluorescent protein (Supplementary Fig. 3). Since the majority of SCN neurons are GABAergic, we used Vgat-IRES-Cre mice for this experiment. Subsequently, we implanted an optical fiber above the SCN in these mice (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 4). Torpor was induced under LD cycles, and we optogenetically activated SCN neurons between ZT 14 and 24. Interestingly, mice with optogenetically activated SCN neurons did not exhibit a significant decrease in TB, unlike control mice without SCN activation during the night (Fig. 2e, f). Activation of SCN neurons under cold conditions with ad libitum food did not elevate TB (Supplementary Fig. 5). This result indicates that SCN neuronal activity does not promote a generalized increase in thermogenesis. We also confirmed that light illumination into the SCN of mice lacking ChR2 expression during food deprivation resulted in a decrease in TB (Supplementary Fig. 6). Identification of candidate output brain regions regulated by the SCN

To identify the downstream neural circuits originating from the SCN that regulate the timing of torpor, we sampled the brains from torpor-induced (Torpid mice: without optogenetic stimulation of the SCN) and torpor-suppressed (Non-torpid mice: with stimulation) mice from Vgat-IRES-Cre/LSL-ChR2-EYFP mice (Fig. 3a, b). We then immunohistochemically stained with c-fos, which is a marker of neuronal activity in the whole brain, and quantified the number of c-fos positive cells in various brain regions. Torpor-induced mice exhibited higher c-fos positive cells in the preoptic area (POA), lateral septum ventral (LSv), bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST), and paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus (PVN). On the other hand, torpor-suppressed mice displayed a significant reduction in the number of c-fos positive cells in these brain regions (Fig. 3c, d and Supplementary Fig. 7). We also performed anterograde tracing of the SCN to identify its neuronal output pathways. By introducing AAV-derived Cre-dependent synaptophysin-GFP fusion protein into the SCN of Vgat-IRES-Cre mice, we observed that pre-synapses of the SCN were located in the POA, LSv, and PVN areas (Fig. 3e). Optogenetic activation of SCN neurons projecting to the POA suppresses torpor

To evaluate which neuronal pathway is critical for the temporal regulation of torpor, we used AAV to express ChR2-EYFP selectively in Vgat-positive cells of the SCN (Fig. 4a). We then implanted an optical fiber(s) in the POA, LSv, or PVN (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 8). Subsequently, we induced torpor in these mice and applied blue light illumination to the axonal fibers during the night phase. We found that mice with optogenetic excitation of SCN axonal fibers in the LSv or PVN still showed a decrease in TB during the dark phase, comparable to control mice. However, mice with optogenetic excitation of SCN axonal fibers in the POA did not exhibit in a decrease in TB, when compared to control mice (Fig. 4c, d). Optogenetic suppression of SCN neurons during the day produced a modest decrease in body temperature under conditions of fasting and a cold environment

Given that optogenetic activation of Vgat neurons in the SCN suppresses torpor, we hypothesized that optogenetic inhibition of SCN neuronal activity during the subjective day could induce torpor under cold conditions with food deprivation. To investigate this, we utilized AAV to express light-activated Gi/o-coupled rhodopsin fused with a red fluorescent protein (eOPN3-mScarlet) in Vgat-positive cells of the SCN and implanted an optical fiber above the SCN (Fig. 5a). We confirmed that green light (535 nm) illumination to eOPN3-expressing neurons in SCN brain slices resulted in a reduced firing rate and hyperpolarization of resting membrane potentials (Supplementary Fig. 9). After starting food deprivation in a cold environment, we illuminated green light into the SCN between ZT 4 and 9 to suppress neuronal activity (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 10). Within the POA, excitatory neurons implicated in torpor induction are present17. If their excitability fluctuates across the circadian cycle—low during the day and high at night—this could explain why deep torpor rarely occurs during the day, even when SCN-mediated GABAergic inhibition is reduced by eOPN3. To test this hypothesis, we performed electrophysiological recordings from Vglut2-positive neurons in the POA (Fig. 5d). We found that both resting membrane potential and firing frequency of these neurons were significantly higher at ZT 18 compared to ZT 6 (Fig. 5e–g). These results indicate that the timing of torpor is regulated not only by inhibitory input from the SCN but also by circadian modulation of excitability in POA glutamatergic neurons.

Disconnection of neural output in the SCN abolishes the timing of torpor

To further understand the relationship between the SCN and torpor, we performed an electrical lesion of the SCN (SCNx) to completely ablate SCN neurons (Supplementary Fig. 11). We first measured locomotor activity in WT mice using an infrared sensor under LD cycles. Then, we electrically lesioned the SCN. These SCNx mice did not exhibit circadian behavioral rhythms, even under LD cycles (Fig. 6a). Subsequently, we induced torpor in these mice. Paradoxically, we observed that the TB of SCNx mice did not decrease significantly after food deprivation compared to sham-operated control mice (Fig. 6b, c). While the SCN has both neural and humoral output systems, it remains unclear which system governs the timing of torpor. To address this question, we performed the isolation of SCN (iSCN) by physically severing neuronal connections from the SCN using a Halász knife, as previously described18 (Supplementary Fig. 11). This procedure truncates axons extending from the SCN to external regions. Mice with iSCN surgery exhibited weak circadian behavioral rhythms (Fig. 6a). Notably, their TB also failed to decrease significantly after food deprivation, mirroring the results observed in SCNx mice (Fig. 6b, c). These results suggest that neuronal outputs, rather than humoral outputs from the SCN, are critical for the timing of torpor entry.

Hypothetical neuronal circuits that regulate torpor timing

Based on these results, we propose an alternative hypothesis for circadian clock-controlled torpor in mice as follows. GABAergic neurons in the SCN project to both glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons in the POA. Within the POA, GABAergic neurons innervate glutamatergic neurons. During the day, higher neuronal activity in the SCN suppresses glutamatergic neurons in the POA, which inhibits torpor induction. Indeed, previous research has demonstrated that glutamatergic neurons in the POA are involved in torpor induction17. However, during the night, reduced neuronal activity in the SCN enhances the excitability of glutamatergic neurons in the POA, potentially leading to torpor induction. When the neuronal output from the SCN is disrupted by iSCN, GABAergic inhibition from the SCN to the GABAergic neurons in the POA is absent. This absence results in increased inhibitory input from GABAergic neurons to glutamatergic neurons in the POA, leading to the inhibition of torpor.

SCN neurons have GABAergic outputs to both glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons in the POA

Next, we tested the hypotheses discussed above by investigating neuronal connectivity between the SCN and POA using slice patch-clamp recordings (Fig. 7a, j, s). To selectively manipulate GABAergic neurons in the SCN, we used AAV to express ChR2-EYFP in Vgat-positive cells in the SCN of Vgat-IRES-Cre mice. Additionally, we expressed mCherry under the CaMKII promoter to label excitatory neurons in the POA. Alternatively, we used Cre-dependent mCherry expression to label inhibitory neurons in the POA. Brain slices were prepared, and patch-clamp recordings were performed on either excitatory or inhibitory neurons in the POA. During these recordings, we stimulated the nerve terminals of GABAergic SCN neurons located in the POA. Current clamp recordings showed that the membrane potential was hyperpolarized in both excitatory and inhibitory neurons, and spontaneous firing was suppressed following blue light stimulation (Fig. 7b, c, k, l and Supplementary Fig. 12). To further investigate the neuronal connectivity, we recorded these neurons in whole-cell voltage clamp mode at a −60 mV holding potential. Activation of SCN GABAergic nerve terminals in the POA induced post-synaptic currents (PSCs) in both excitatory and inhibitory neurons in response to a blue light pulse (Fig. 7d, m). These light-induced PSCs were blocked by a GABAA receptor antagonist, gabazine. (Fig. 7d, e, f, m–o). To determine whether GABAergic neurons in the SCN have direct inputs to both excitatory and inhibitory neurons in the POA, we applied tetrodotoxin (TTX, 1 μM), a voltage-gated sodium channel blocker, which blocked the light-induced PSCs. The combination of TTX and 4AP (4-aminopyridine, 1 mM), a voltage-gated potassium channel blocker, rescued the light-induced PSCs (Fig. 7g, h, i, p, q, r). To more precisely characterize inhibitory inputs onto excitatory neurons in the POA, we used Gad67-tTA;Vglut2-Cre mice and performed electrophysiological recordings. We used AAV to selectively express ChR2–EYFP in Gad67-positive SCN neurons and tdTomato in Vglut2-positive POA neurons. Optogenetic activation of GABAergic nerve terminals originating from the SCN significantly suppressed the neuronal activity of Vglut2-positive excitatory neurons in the POA, demonstrating the presence of a direct inhibitory circuit from the SCN to POA excitatory neurons (Supplementary Fig. 13). Next, to identify the neuronal connectivity from inhibitory to excitatory neurons within the POA, we also performed slice patch-clamp recordings. AAV was used to express ChR2-EYFP in Vgat-positive cells in the POA of Vgat-IRES-Cre mice and mCherry under the CaMKII promoter to label excitatory neurons in the POA. These experiments revealed that GABAergic neurons in the POA form direct synaptic connections with excitatory neurons in the POA, inhibiting their activity via GABAergic transmission (Fig. 7a–t and Supplementary Fig. 13). Using Gad67-tTA;Vglut2-Cre mice with AAV-mediated expression, we further confirmed that optogenetic activation of local GABAergic neurons within the POA robustly suppressed the neuronal activity of Vglut2-positive excitatory neurons (Supplementary Fig. 13).

The SCN contains several distinct neuronal populations, each contributing to different physiological functions. To determine which SCN neurons are involved in regulating the timing of torpor, we performed retrograde tracing experiments. Red RetroBeads were injected into the POA, and subsequent immunohistochemical staining for arginine vasopressin (AVP) and vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP) in the SCN was performed (Fig. 8a). These two populations, Avp and Vip neurons, are the largest and second-largest in the SCN, respectively. SCN neurons provide inhibitory outputs to GABAergic and glutamatergic neurons in the POA. Additionally, both Avp and Vip neurons of the SCN project to the POA (Figs. 7 and 8a–c). To determine whether GABAergic outputs from Avp or Vip neurons in the SCN are essential for regulating the timing of torpor, we generated mice with specific deletions of the Vgat in these neurons by crossing Avp-Cre or Vip-IRES-Cre mice with Vgat-flox mice, resulting in Avp-Vgat KO or Vip-Vgat KO mice. Torpor was then induced in these mice under DD conditions. We found that Vip-Vgat KO mice displayed a decrease in TB during the subjective night under food deprivation, similar to control mice, indicating that GABAergic signaling in Vip neurons is not essential for the timing of torpor. However, the minimum TB of Avp-Vgat KO mice under food deprivation was significantly higher than that of control mice (Fig. 8d, e). Furthermore, when Vgat was rescued in the SCN of Avp-Vgat KO mice, they exhibited torpor similar to the control (Fig. 8d, e and Supplementary Fig. 14). These results indicate that GABAergic signaling from Avp neurons in the SCN plays a crucial role in the circadian regulation of torpor in mice.

Discussion

By employing a combination of optogenetics, whole-brain c-fos mapping, electrophysiology, neurotracing, and Cre-loxP-based gene manipulation, we have identified functional hypothalamic neuronal circuits that regulate the timing of torpor in mice (Fig. 9). Our optogenetic and c-fos mapping experiments revealed that the SCN-POA pathway is involved in the regulation of the timing of torpor. Although neuronal and diffusible outputs from the SCN have been proposed19, our SCN lesion and isolation experiments show that only the neuronal output pathway is critical for torpor regulation (Fig. 6). Specifically, GABAergic neurotransmission from the SCN plays a pivotal role in controlling torpor. During the day, neuronal activity in the SCN is high, which suppresses torpor induction, whereas during the night, SCN activity is low, allowing torpor entry. While SCN GABAergic output suppresses torpor during the middle of the day, the excitability of POA glutamatergic neurons—key drivers of torpor induction—also follows a circadian rhythm, being lower during the day and higher at night. This SCN-POA dual regulation may explain why deep torpor is rarely expressed during the day. The presence of intrinsic circadian modulation in POA glutamatergic neurons points to the possibility that local oscillatory mechanisms, alongside SCN input, cooperate to gate the induction of torpor at specific circadian phases.

SCN-lesioned and isolated mice did not exhibit deep torpor, showing only a modest reduction in body temperature, which was unexpected. One possible explanation is that lesioning or isolating the SCN disrupts the inhibitory–excitatory balance within the POA. We found that SCN GABAergic neurons project both to POA excitatory neurons that promote torpor and to POA GABAergic neurons that inhibit these excitatory cells. When the SCN is lesioned, disinhibition of POA GABAergic neurons may occur, leading them to exert stronger inhibition onto POA excitatory neurons. This excessive inhibition could suppress the activity of torpor-promoting neurons, preventing the expression of deep torpor. Another possibility is that SCN ablation disrupts the circadian rhythms of endocrine and metabolic signals20, which may contribute to torpor induction. If these systemic rhythms are required to reach the physiological threshold for torpor entry, their loss could explain why SCNx and iSCN mice fail to exhibit deep torpor. These possibilities remain speculative, but together they highlight the likelihood that SCN control of torpor involves both local hypothalamic circuits and SCN-dependent systemic rhythms.

Torpor often exhibits ultradian rhythms, typically consisting of three- to four-hour bouts. This pattern suggests that torpor is not regulated solely by the SCN but may also involve an ultradian oscillator. External signals within the brain, occurring every several hours (ultradian rhythms), could influence the SCN and drive changes in its neural activity. Alternatively, a direct signal to the POA or other thermoregulatory regions may change body temperature during torpor. The involvement of an ultradian rhythm mechanism in the regulation of torpor warrants further investigation as a key factor.

In addition to this, torpor is rarely observed at the beginning of the subjective night. During this phase, mice are hyperactive, which might be related to food-seeking behaviors. These temporal regulations of torpor induction are advantageous for energy conservation under harsh conditions, such as cold temperatures and food scarcity. Since the coldest ambient temperatures typically occur in the latter half of the night to early morning in nature, small animals like mice can conserve energy by entering torpor during this time window.

Our study identified that SCN neurons project to excitatory neurons in the POA, an area known to contain diverse neuronal populations involved in body temperature regulation21,22. Among them, several types of neurons, such as Adcyap-, Ptger3-, Esr1-, Tripm2-, Opn5, Galanin, and Qrfp-positive neurons, have been implicated in mediating reductions in TB15,17,23–27. Excitatory neurons in these groups may receive neuronal inputs from the SCN and regulate torpor induction. Additionally, we found that SCN neurons also innervate GABAergic neurons in the POA, which in turn suppress the activity of the glutamatergic neurons in the POA. GABAergic neurons in the POA are suggested to play a suppressed role in torpor28, but the specific types of GABAergic neurons in the POA involved in torpor regulation remain unclear. Identifying these neuronal subtypes will be key to understanding the precise neural mechanisms governing torpor.

Torpor in mice is regulated not only by the circadian clock but also by the ambient temperature and food availability29. Information about ambient temperature is ultimately sent to the POA22. On the other hand, signals related to hunger are transmitted to the arcuate nucleus (ARC), dorsomedial hypothalamic nucleus (DMH), and paraventricular nucleus (PVN)30,31. Since neuronal interactions among these brain regions exist, circadian information could be relayed to these areas with POA neurons, which play a critical role in regulating torpor.

Within the SCN neuronal circuitry, neuropeptides like VIP and AVP are essential for synchronizing cellular circadian rhythms32,33. However, GABA in the SCN may not be involved in synchronization within the SCN but rather in regulating circadian outputs34,35. Additionally, it has been reported that Avp neuronal fibers in the SCN innervate rostral parts of the hypothalamus, including the POA36,37. Thus, Avp neurons in the SCN are likely crucial for circadian output regulation. However, some Avp-Vgat KO mice in our study did not enter torpor, while others did. The cause of this variability remains unclear, but previous studies have reported that in certain Avp-Vgat KO mice, the neural activity rhythm in the SCN becomes biphasic34. In the present study, it is possible that Avp-Vgat KO mice that failed to enter torpor exhibited such a biphasic SCN firing rhythm, which may have suppressed torpor throughout the day.

Torpor (daily torpor) and hibernation share several physiological characteristics, such as a decrease in TB and hypometabolism in response to harsh environmental conditions9,10. Notably, in hamsters, the onset of both torpor and hibernation (first bout of deep torpor) occurs during the middle of the night, a pattern consistent with our findings in mice12,13 (Fig. 1). These results suggest that similar neuronal circuits may regulate the timing of torpor and hibernation. However, there are key differences between the two states. Torpor typically involves a reduction of TB that does not persist for more than 24 h, whereas hibernation involves prolonged periods of a reduction of TB lasting more than several days9,10. Additionally, the thermoregulatory setpoint during torpor is generally higher than that during hibernation11,38. These distinctions suggest that while the circadian clock governs the timing of both torpor and hibernation, other mechanisms likely regulate the maintenance of a reduction of TB during hibernation. Factors such as ambient temperature and food contents may influence these processes39,40, although further research is needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying torpor and hibernation.

Methods

Animals

We used wild-type, Cry1/2 deficient41, Vgat-IRES-Cre42, Vglut2-IRES-Cre42, Avp-Cre43, Vip-IRES-Cre44, Vgat-flox45, Gad67-tTA46, and LSL-ChR2-EYFP47 mice with C57BL/6 J background. Mice were bred and reared in the animal facility at Nagoya University, where environmental conditions were controlled (lights-on 8:00–20:00, ambient temperature 23 ± 2 °C, humidity 60 ± 10 %). Mice had free access to food pellets and water. Female mice (2–7 months for Fig. 1, 2–4 months for other figures) were used in the experiments. Torpor induction experiments were performed in a cold room at 16 °C (TBR-6W6X-P, ESPEC), except for the in vivo multi-unit activity recording experiment at room temperature. All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of the Research Institute of Environmental Medicine, Nagoya University, and Meiji University, Japan (approval #: R240003, R240004, and MUIACUC20-126).

Recording of body temperature

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (2–5%; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp.), and a temperature logger (DST nano-T, StarOddi or iButton, Analog Devices) was implanted in the abdominal cavity. The outer metal jacket of the iButton was peeled off and coated with silicone, weighing approximately 1.7 g. The core body temperature of mice was recorded every 5 min. After implanting the loggers, at least one week of recovery was allowed before the experiments were performed.

In vivo multiunit activity recording of the SCN

Experiments were performed as previously described48. Bipolar electrodes for the SCN were constructed from pairs of Teflon-coated stainless-steel wires (bare diameter, 75 µm; Unique Medical, Tokyo, Japan) and an uncoated platinum-iridium wire (diameter, 75 µm; A-M Systems, Sequim, WA, USA). Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and placed on a stereotaxic instrument (David Kopf Instruments, Tujunga, CA, USA). The electrodes were inserted into the brain, aimed at the SCN (0.2 mm posterior and 0.2 mm lateral to the bregma, 5.7 mm depth from the skull surface), and attached directly to the skull with an orthodontic bond (3 M, Saint Paul, MN, USA). A body temperature logger (DST nano-T; Star-Oddi, Gardabaer, Iceland) was implanted intraperitoneally in each mouse. After surgery, each mouse was transferred to an open-top cage and individually housed for at least one week to recover. Output signals were processed by differential-input integration amplifiers (Burr-Brown, Tucson, AZ, USA) and then fed into AC amplifiers (bandpass, 500 Hz to 5 kHz; gain, × 10,000). Spikes were discriminated by amplitude and counted in 1 min bins using a computer-based window discrimination system and then stored on the computer. Following the study’s completion, each animal was deeply anesthetized with isoflurane, and a positive current (2 µA; 60 s) was passed through the recording electrodes. The brains were removed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer containing 5% potassium ferrocyanide (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Serial coronal sections (40 µm) were stained with neutral red, and blue spots of deposited iron were identified at the recording site.

Viruses

Adeno-associated virus (AAVs) were produced using a triple-transfection, helper-free method and purified as described previously43. Titers of recombinant AAV vectors were determined by quantitative PCR: AAV-CAG-flex-Synaptophysin-GFP, 4 × 1012 genome copies/ml; AAV-CAG-flex-ChR2-EFYP, 3 × 1012 genome copies/ml; AAV-CaMKIIa-mCherry, 2 × 1012 genome copies/ml; AAV-hSyn-flex-eOPN3-mScarlet, 1 × 1013 genome copies/ml; AAV-hSyn-flex-tdTomato, 2 × 1012 genome copies/ml; AAV-tetO-ChR2-EYFP, 3 × 1012 genome copies/ml; AAV-EF1a-DIO-Vgat, 1.7 × 1013 genome copies/ml; AAV-EF1a-DIO-mCherry, 2.7 × 1012 genome copies/ml.

SCNx surgery

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and placed on a stereotaxic instrument. Small holes were made in the skull using a dental drill burr (0.2 mm anterior to the bregma). A stainless-steel electrode (0.3 mm diameter; Unique Medical, Tokyo, Japan), coated entirely with epoxy resin except for the tip (0.3 mm in length), was inserted into the SCN [anteroposterior (AP), 0.0 mm; mediolateral (ML) ± 0.0 mm; dorsoventral (DV), −5.8 mm from bregma/skull surface]. The SCNx was generated by passing a direct electrical current of 1.0–3.0 mA for 15–20 s with an isolator49. For sham-operated mice, the electrode was inserted, but an electrical current was not applied. Spontaneous movements were measured under light/dark cycles to confirm the loss of circadian rhythm.

iSCN surgery

We designed a microknife (TY215-011, Unique Medical, Tokyo, Japan) for mice, referencing the one originally developed by Dr. Halász50. A 0.2 mm inner-diameter stainless steel was placed in a stainless tube (inner-diameter, 0.2 mm; outer-diameter, 0.4 mm). One side of the stainless steel was bent to create an arc with a cutting radius of approximately 0.8 mm and a height of approximately 0.8 mm, and the other side was bent to a right angle as a handle. The upper side of the stainless-steel tube was covered with a shrink tube. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and placed on a stereotaxic instrument. The shaft of the knife was positioned at the bregma, and the tip of the knife was set to the skull surface. For the iSCN surgery, the knife was lowered 6.0 mm ventral to the dura. The knife was rotated 90° on the posterior (from 4.5 to 7.5 of the clock), raised 0.1 mm followed by a 270° turn on the lateral and posterior (from 1.5 to 10.5 of the clock), and then raised 0.1 mm followed by a 360° turn. Spontaneous movements were measured under light/dark cycles.

Surgery for in vivo optogenetics

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and placed on a stereotaxic instrument. Small holes were made in the skull using a dental drill burr. A fiber-optic cannula(s) [6.0 mm in length, 400-µm-diameter core, and 0.39 numerical aperture (NA); KYOCERA Corporation] was stereotaxically implanted just above the SCN (AP, 0.0 mm; ML ± 0.2 mm; DV, −5.8 mm), the LSv (AP, +1.2 mm; ML, ±0.7 mm; DV, −3.5 mm), the POA (AP, +0.8 ~ + 1.0 mm; ML, ±0.5 mm; DV, −4.8 ~ −5.3 mm), and the PVN (AP, −0.1 mm; ML, 0.0 mm; DV, −4.9 mm). They were fixed with dental cement. Carprofen (20 mg/kg; Zoetis Inc., Japan) was administered the day of surgery for its anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties. After surgery, mice were housed for at least 7 days for recovery, and then an optic cable was connected to the mice in the cage for at least 7 days before starting the experiment.

In vivo optical manipulation

A flexible fiber-optic cable (BFYL4LS01; 400-μm-diameter core; 0.39 NA; Thorlabs) was connected to an optical fiber implanted in the mice, and the cable was attached to an LED light source (470 nm; Thorlabs) as previously reported7. To manipulate membrane potentials in SCN neurons, we applied blue light (470 nm, frequency: 16 Hz, duration: 10 ms for 10 s every 20 s, 8 Hz in average, ZT 16 - 24) or green light (575 nm, frequency: 0.05 Hz, duration: 1 s every 20 s, ZT 4-9).

Brain slice preparation for electrophysiological recording

Electrophysiological recordings were performed as previously reported51. Three to four weeks after AAV injection, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) and decapitated. Brains were rapidly removed and placed in ice-cold, oxygenated (95% O2 and 5% CO2) cutting solution (in mM: 110 K-gluconate, 15 KCl, 0.05 EGTA, 5 HEPES, 26.2 NaHCO3, 25 glucose, 3.3 MgCl2, 0.0015 CPP). Coronal brain slices (300-µm thickness) were prepared using a vibratome (VT-1200S, Leica). Slices were incubated in oxygenated bath solution (in mM: 124 NaCl, 3 KCl, 2 MgCl2, 2 CaCl2, 1.23 NaH2PO4.2H2O, 26 NaHCO3, 25 glucose) at 35 °C for 30 min, followed by another 30 minutes at room temperature (RT). Slices were transferred to a recording chamber (RC-26G, Warner Instruments) and perfused with oxygenated bath solution at 1.5 ml/min using a peristaltic pump (Miniplus3, Gilson, USA).

Electrophysiology setup

An infrared camera (C3077-78, Hamamatsu Photonics) and an electron-multiplying charge-coupled device camera (Evolve 512 delta, Photometrics) were mounted on an upright fluorescence microscope (BX51WI, Olympus) to visualize slices. Borosilicate glass micropipettes (GC150-10, Harvard Apparatus) were pulled using a horizontal puller (P1000, Sutter Instrument) and had resistances of 4–6 MΩ. For current-clamp recordings, a K-gluconate–based internal solution (in mM: 138 K-gluconate, 10 HEPES, 8 NaCl, 0.2 EGTA, 2 Mg-ATP, 0.5 Na2-GTP; pH 7.3, osmolality 285–290 mOsm) was used. For voltage-clamp recordings, a KCl-based internal solution (in mM: 145 KCl, 1 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, 1.1 EGTA, 2 Mg-ATP, 0.5 Na2-GTP; pH 7.3, osmolality 280–290 mOsm) was used, with 1 mM QX-314 added at −60 mV holding potential.

Whole-cell patch clamp procedure

Fluorescent-labeled cells were identified, and the pipette was advanced toward the cell with positive pressure applied. After giga seal formation, the membrane was ruptured by suction to establish a whole-cell configuration. Recordings were performed with an Axopatch 200B amplifier (Molecular Devices) and digitized at 10 kHz (low-pass filtered at 5 kHz) using a Digidata 1550 A (Molecular Devices). Patch-clamp data were collected with pClamp 10.7 software. Blue (475 ± 17.5 nm) and green (549 ± 7.5 nm) light were delivered through a fluorescence microscope objective lens via a Spectra light engine (Lumencor, USA).

Retrograde tracing

WT mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and stereotaxically injected with 50 nl of red fluorescent RetroBeads (Lumafluor) into the POA. Four days after the RetroBeads injection, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and injected with colchicine (10 µg/µl, 1.8 µl) into the lateral ventricle. Two days after that, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and perfused with saline, followed by a 10 % formalin solution.

Measurement of locomotor activity

We measured the spontaneous movement of mice with a passive infrared sensor that detects changes in animal thermal radiation due to movement, as described previously52. Mice were housed individually in polycarbonate cages placed in a light-tight and air-conditioned box. The amount of movement was recorded every minute using a computer software (Clocklab, Actimetrics)

Histological study

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and perfused with saline, followed by a 10% formalin solution (Wako). Brains were extracted and fixed in the same formalin solution for 24 h at 4 °C, then transferred to a 30% sucrose solution at 4 °C for at least 2 days. Coronal sections (40 µm thick) were prepared using a cryostat (Leica CM3050S; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and stored in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 4 °C. Immunostaining was performed as described previously7. Briefly, brain sections were immersed in a blocking buffer (1% bovine serum albumin and 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS), followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C. After washing with blocking buffer, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. For checking of SCN lesion, the brain slices were immersed twice in PBS for 5 min, followed by incubation in a 500x dilution of Neurotrace (N21480, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at RT for 20 min. After discarding the solution, the slides were washed for 3 min in PBS-BX, then 3 min in PBS, and stained with DAPI. Finally, the sections were mounted and imaged using a fluorescence microscope (BZ-710, Keyence) or a confocal microscope (LSM710; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Primary and secondary antibodies were diluted in blocking buffer as follows: rabbit anti-c-fos (1:1000 dilution, Abcam, ab222699), rabbit anti-AVP (1:1000 dilution, Gunma U.), rabbit anti-VIP (1:1000 dilution, Immunostar, 20077), CF 594–conjugated donkey anti-rabbit antibody (1:1000 dilution; Biotium), and CF 488–conjugated donkey anti-rabbit antibody (1:1000 dilution; Biotium).

Data analysis

The mean body temperature minus 5 standard deviations (32.5 °C) was calculated for mice housed at 23 °C. The animals were considered to be in a hypothermic state if their body temperature dropped below this threshold during the torpor induction experiment. The number of mice exhibiting torpor at each recording time point (measured at 5 min intervals) was visualized using a Rayleigh plot (Fig. 1).

After acquiring c-fos fluorescence images, the mean background intensity plus three standard deviations was used as the threshold for defining a signal. A cluster of 3–20 pixels (pixel size: 3.77 µm) meeting this threshold was classified as a single c-fos-positive cell. The spatial distribution of signals was aligned to the Allen Brain Atlas using SHARCQ, matching the shape of the brain section to the atlas53. The number of c-fos-positive cells in each brain region was then quantified in Fig. 3.

Electrophysiological data were analyzed by Clampfit10 (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA).

Statistics

We performed the following statistical analyses in Prism 8.0 (GraphPad), OriginPro 2020 (OriginLab), or Oriana4 (Kovach Computing Service) software: Student’s t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), two-way repeated measures ANOVA, mixed-effects model test, Sidak’s multiple comparisons test, Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test, Tukey–Kramer test, Hotelling’s test, and Hotelling’s two sample test.
