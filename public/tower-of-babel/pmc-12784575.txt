Physicochemical features of intrinsically disordered regions predict DNA-demethylation-promoting activity of transcription factors
Authors: Xian-Yang Qin, Yuta Sakamoto, Feifei Wei, Hajime Nishimura, Yuri Nakanishi, Shiori Maeda, Harukazu Suzuki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTranscription factor (TF)-dependent DNA demethylation is associated with generation of specific DNA methylation profiles in normal cellular development and disease, although only a small fraction of TFs are known to promote DNA demethylation.ResultsHere, we systematically predicted which TFs have DNA-demethylation-promoting activity. Experiments with deletion mutants of the TFs RUNX1 and SPI1 revealed that this activity is associated with a relatively long intrinsically disordered region (IDR). Examination of the IDRs from eight TFs previously confirmed to have such activity revealed that at least one IDR was active in each TF. We constructed a Random Forest classifier based on 25 numeric physicochemical features extracted from length-controlled 26 positive (active) and 32 negative (inactive) IDRs. Four key features— aromaticity, aliphatic index, fractional charge ratio, and side chain hydrophobic density—were identified as the most informative contributors to prediction of positive IDRs. A model based on these features achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.84, with an optimized decision threshold of 0.303. Applying this model to all TFs, we predicted 959 of 2364 IDRs to be positive, corresponding to 825 of 1308 TFs. The model correctly identified all of 14 previously validated positive TFs. The predicted positive TFs showed significant enrichment of Gene Ontology terms related to morphogenesis and development and may be clinically relevant to certain cancer types.ConclusionThe developed model with high predictive performance and the predicted TFs with DNA-demethylation-promoting activity will be useful for further analysis of TFs involved in generation of DNA methylation profiles in normal cell development and disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13072-025-00655-w.

Background

DNA methylation is a well-studied stable epigenetic modification. In mammals, DNA methylation of CpG dinucleotides in enhancer and promoter regions tends to be negatively correlated with gene expression and is important in many biological processes such as X-chromosome inactivation, genomic imprinting, and transposon silencing [1, 2]. Aberrant DNA methylation is associated with the development of diseases such as cancers [3].

DNA methylation status is regulated by methylation and demethylation. De novo DNA methylation is regulated by DNA methyltransferases 3a and 3b [4, 5]. Methylated CpG cytosines are passed on to daughter cells during cell division by DNA methyltransferase 1 [6, 7]. Active DNA demethylation involves several steps, including a series of oxidizations catalyzed by ten-eleven translocation enzymes (TETs) followed by base excision repair or cell division [8–10].

In mammalian cells, the DNA methylation profile changes as the cells differentiate along their lineage [11]. Most CpG dinucleotides are de novo methylated after embryo implantation in the uterine wall, although the CpG islands in the regulatory regions of house-keeping genes remain unmethylated. As cells differentiate, they develop a characteristic methylation profile, mostly through demethylation of distinct genomic regions that is characteristic of the particular cell type. Hence, DNA demethylation plays an important role in the formation of methylation profiles that are unique to each cell type.

A subgroup of transcription factors (TFs), called pioneer TFs, bind to permissive heterochromatin regions and promote histone modification and DNA demethylation at their binding sites so that the chromatin becomes accessible to other TFs and transcriptional regulators [12–16]. Because TFs themselves have no enzymatic activity for DNA demethylation, TF-mediated DNA demethylation is thought to be carried out by recruiting the DNA demethylation machinery (i.e., TETs) [17]. We have demonstrated that the TF RUNT-related transcription factor 1 (RUNX1) promotes DNA demethylation by recruiting TET2 to the binding sites of RUNX1 [18]. We have also shown that the TF GATA6 physically interacts with TET2 and promotes DNA demethylation at its binding sites during hepatocyte differentiation [19]. We have developed an in vitro method for examining the DNA-demethylation-promoting activity of TFs [20]; by using this method, we have identified dozens of TFs with DNA-demethylation-promoting activity [20, 21].

Although the method and that from another group [22] are useful, the experiments are time-consuming, expensive, and do not identify all TFs with DNA-demethylation-promoting activity. RUNX1- and GATA6-binding sites are enriched at DNA demethylation loci during differentiation of hematopoietic and hepatocyte cells, respectively [18, 19]. To systematically predict TFs with DNA-demethylation-promoting activity, we explored the TF-binding motif (TFBM) enrichment at loci differentially methylated between embryonic stem cells and terminally differentiated cells by using publicly available DNA methylation data [21], and identified a number of TFBMs enriched at DNA demethylation loci. However, the TFBMs of the same TF family are so similar that it was not possible to determine which TF in the family was responsible for DNA-demethylation-promoting activity, even with the addition of TF gene expression information. We found that some TFs belonging to the same family have the DNA-demethylation-promoting activity, while others do not [21].

Here, we attempted to systematically predict which TFs have the DNA-demethylation-promoting activity from the association of TF regions with the activity. We found that the DNA-demethylation-promoting activity of the TFs RUNX1 and SPI1 is conferred not by specific short sequences but by a relatively long intrinsic disordered region (IDR). We demonstrated that specific IDRs are associated with DNA-demethylation-promoting activity in eight TFs that have previously been experimentally validated to have the activity. On the basis of our original dataset of TFs with DNA-demethylation-promoting activity, we developed a Random Forest machine learning model with high predictive performance, capable of predicting this activity of TFs from the physicochemical properties of their IDRs. The positively predicted TFs showed significant enrichment of the gene ontology (GO) terms related to morphogenesis and development, and we suggested such TFs to be clinically relevant to certain cancer types.

Methods

Construction of TF mutants

The RUNX1 deletion mutants were constructed using pENTR-RUNX1, in which open reading frame of RUNX1 (accession number; NM_001754.4) is cloned at BamHI-XhoI site of pENTRTM3C plasmid (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The RUNX1 sequence, except the region to be deleted, was amplified together with the vector sequence by inverse PCR. The amplified fragments were self-ligated with LigationHigh ver.2 (Toyobo, Japan), followed by transformation into E. coli. The plasmids were purified using the PureYield Plasmid Miniprep System (Promega, USA).

The SPI1 transactivation (TA) domain deletion mutants were constructed as follows. The RUNX1 sequence, except that corresponding to the RUNX1300 − 480 region, was amplified from pENTR-RUNX1 together with the vector sequence by inverse PCR to obtain the pENTR-RUNX11 − 299 fragment. The TA domain sequence of SPI1 (accession number; NM_001080547.1) was PCR-amplified from pENTR-SPI1 by using primers overlapping by 20 bp with pENTR-RUNX11 − 299. The two fragments were connected with NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (NEB, USA) to construct pENTR-RUNX11 − 299–SPI11 − 102, which was used to construct SPI1 TA domain deletion mutants using the same method as for the RUNX1 deletion mutants.

Constructs for examining the DNA-demethylation-promoting activity of the IDRs of other TFs were created using the same approach as for pENTR-RUNX11 − 299–SPI11 − 102. The IDR sequences of the TFs of interest were obtained from Tesei et al. [23]. Sequences corresponding to the IDRs were PCR-amplified and connected with the pENTR-RUNX11 − 299 fragment, resulting in constructs encoding chimeric TF mutants.

All constructs in pENTR were confirmed by Sanger sequencing and transferred into the CSII-EF-RfA-IRES2-puro vector [18] by the Gateway LR recombination technique (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Lentivirus vectors for TF mutant expression were produced as previously described [24].

In vitro assay for DNA-demethylation-promoting activity

The method we reported previously [20] was used with slight modifications. Briefly, 293T cells (RIKEN Bio Resource Center, Japan) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL, 100 mg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cells were infected overnight with the lentivirus vectors at a multiplicity of infection of 7–15 in the presence of 8 mg/mL polybrene, followed by puromycin selection at 3 µg/mL for 10 days. Genomic DNA was extracted from the 293T cells by using a NucleoSpin Tissue Kit (Macherey-Nagel GmbH & Co., Germany) and treated with bisulfite by using an EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit (Zymo Research Corp., USA).

DNA methylation status of specific loci in the bisulfite-treated genomic DNA was measured by quantitative methylation-specific PCR [18]. Loci whose demethylation is known to be promoted by RUNX1 and negative-control loci were subjected to real-time PCR amplification by using methylation- and non-methylation-specific primers in Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus (Thermo Fisher Scientific). DNA methylation status of each locus was calculated from the Ct value for the methylation- and non-methylation-specific primers.

Reduced representation bisulfite sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from 293T mock, 293T wild-type RUNX1, and 293T RUNX11 − 299–SPI11 − 102 cells and was subjected to the reduced representation bisulfite sequencing by using a Zymo-seq RRBS Library Kit (Zymo Research Corp.). The DNA methylation data was registered in the GEO database (GSE311367).

Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) and Western blot

HaloTag-fused TET2 expression vector pFN21A-TET2 (Kazusa, Japan, #pFN21AB9898) was transfected into the lentivirus vector-infected 293T cells using a Fugene HD (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were collected at 48 h after transfection and HaloTag-based co-IP assays were performed using the HaloTag Mammalian Pull-Down System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Western blot was performed as described previously [25] using Anti-RUNX1 primary antibody (Abcam, England, ab230980, 1:5,000) and Anti-Rabbit HRP-Linked Whole Ab Donkey secondary antibody (Cytiva, USA, NA934V, 1:10,000). Chemiluminescence signals were detected using ECL Prime Western blotting Detection Reagent (Cytiva).

Prediction of DNA-demethylation-promoting activity of TF IDRs

The TF IDR sequences and the physicochemical features were taken from Tesei et al. [23]. In addition, other physicochemical properties of IDRs, including molecular weight, hydrophobicity, charge at pH 7, isoelectric point (pI), aromaticity, instability index, aliphatic index, and Boman index, were calculated using the Peptides R package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/Peptides/index.html). Aromaticity was computed using a custom function that quantifies the proportion of aromatic residues (F, W, Y) in each IDR. In our previous study, the DNA-demethylation-promoting activity of full-length TFs was examined [20, 21]. To generate IDR-level labels, the following criteria were applied. For TFs without DNA-demethylation-promoting activity, all of their IDRs were labeled as negative. For TFs with such activity and only one IDR, that IDR was labeled as positive. For TFs with two or more IDRs (RUNX1 and eight additional TFs), each IDR was individually labeled as positive or negative based on its experimentally evaluated activity (ref [18]. and this study). This procedure resulted in a dataset consisting of 26 positive and 53 negative IDRs, which was used for model training. A length-controlled filtering was applied to exclude negative IDRs whose lengths were outside the distribution range of positive IDRs (mean ± 2 SD). A Random Forest classifier was then constructed to predict DNA-demethylation-promoting activity on the basis of the physicochemical features of the length-controlled IDRs (26 positive and 32 negative IDRs). The model was trained using the Random Forest R package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/randomForest/index.html) with 1000 trees, and feature importance was assessed using the mean absolute SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) values. To optimize model performance and feature selection, we applied sequential forward selection based on the feature importance ranking. At each step, the top N features (ranging from 1 to the total number of features) were used to train a Random Forest model, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was calculated using 5-fold cross-validation with the caret package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/caret/index.html). The number of features that yielded the highest AUC was selected as optimal and used to train the final model. To improve classification accuracy, we further optimized the decision threshold using Youden’s index, which balances sensitivity and specificity. Calibration of predicted probabilities was assessed using Brier score and expected calibration error (ECE), with calibration curves comparing observed positive rates to mean predicted probabilities per bin. This optimized threshold was used to predict the DNA demethylation-promoting activity of individual IDRs from 1308 TFs obtained from Tesei et al. [23]. For each TF, the IDR with the highest predicted probability was considered representative. TFs with the highest IDR probability exceeding the Youden index–derived cutoff were predicted to possess DNA demethylation-promoting activity. The full training/evaluation scripts were shown in Supplementary File S1.

Bioinformatics analysis of TFs associated with DNA-demethylation-promoting activity

TFs predicted to have or lack the DNA-demethylation-promoting activity were subjected to GO analysis (biological process). Gene expression and phenotype metadata from 9,185 primary tumors covering 32 The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cancer types were obtained from the UCSC Xena Toil RNA-seq Recompute Hub (https://toil.xenahubs.net). Of these, 9,163 patients with corresponding phenotype and clinical information were used for survival and prognosis analyses, and 7,184 patients with corresponding DNA methylation data were used for the global DNA methylation correlation analysis. From the ranked list of all TFs, we derived three types of demethylation scores for each TCGA tumor sample: we defined the Yes score as the weighted sum of expression values of the top 50, 100, 150, or 200 TFs with the highest predicted probabilities of promoting DNA demethylation; the No score as the weighted sum of expression values of the bottom 50, 100, 150, or 200 TFs with the lowest predicted probabilities; and the Yes/No ratio score as the ratio of the top to bottom predicted TFs. Patients in each cancer type were stratified into high- and low-score groups on the basis of the median value of each score. Overall survival analysis was then conducted using Kaplan–Meier estimators and univariate Cox proportional hazards models. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.

Results

A relatively long amino acid sequence is associated with the DNA-demethylation-promoting activity of RUNX1 and SPI1

We have previously reported that the DNA-demethylation-promoting activity of RUNX1 is associated with its amino acid sequence 300–380 (RUNX1300 − 380) [18], which overlaps with the TA domain. We failed to identify any obvious sequence or motif similarity between RUNX1300 − 380 and other TFs that we previously identified as having DNA-demethylation-promoting activity [20, 21].

To identify the shortest region required for DNA-demethylation-promoting activity, we generated deletion mutants of RUNX1300 − 380 in 20-amino-acid increments. We expressed the deletion mutants in 293T cells and examined their DNA-demethylation-promoting activity (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1). Deleting the 20 N-terminal amino acids of RUNX1300 − 380 (RUNX1Δ210 − 320) did not influence the activity, but deletion of the 40 and 60 N-terminal amino acid sequences (RUNX1Δ210 − 340 and RUNX1Δ210 − 360) gradually decreased it. A deletion mutant lacking the entire RUNX1300 − 380 region (RUNX1Δ300 − 480) showed little activity, as did the mock control.

A gradual restoration of the activity was observed as the N-terminal side of RUNX1300 − 380 was extended to 20, 40, and 60 amino acids (RUNX1Δ321 − 480, RUNX1Δ341 − 480 and RUNX1Δ361 − 480, respectively). Importantly, full activity comparable to that of WT RUNX1 was associated with a relatively long region, RUNX1321 − 380, but not with any of the 20-amino-acid sequences.

A similar experiment was conducted with the TA domain of the ETS-type TF SPI1 with validated DNA-demethylation-promoting activity [12, 21]. Reduced representation bisulfite sequencing analysis showed that genome-wide DNA demethylation was observed near the RUNX1-binding sites in 293T cells expressing a chimeric protein consisting of RUNX11 − 299 and SPI11 − 102, similar to wild-type RUNX1-expressed 293T cells (Supplementary Fig. S2). To identify the region associated with this activity within SPI11 − 102, chimera proteins, consisting of RUNX11 − 299 and a series of deletion mutants of SPI11 − 102, were expressed in 293T cells and the DNA-demethylation-promoting activity at the RUNX1 target sites was measured (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S3). As in RUNX1, the full activity comparable to that of SPI11 − 102 seemed to be associated with a relatively long region, SPI123 − 82, not with any of the 20-amino-acid sequences. The results suggest that the DNA-demethylation-promoting activity of RUNX1 and SPI1 is associated with relatively long regions.

IDRs are associated with DNA-demethylation-promoting activity of eight TFs

The TA domain, an IDR, serves as a surface for interaction with various proteins, including the transcription initiation complex [26, 27]. To check whether DNA-demethylation-promoting activity of TFs is associated with IDRs, we selected eight TFs from various families (FOXA1, NEUROD1, FEV, NR1H4, MYOD1, ETS1, FOSB, and GATA2), which all have DNA-demethylation-promoting activity [20, 21]. Since all eight TFs have two IDRs, we examined whether each IDR is associated with such activity. Chimeric proteins consisting of RUNX11 − 299 and an IDR of one of the target TFs were overexpressed in 293T cells and DNA methylation status of the RUNX1 target sites was measured (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. S4). In all eight TFs, at least one IDR was associated with this activity. In FOSB, FOXA1, and MYOD, both IDRs were active, although the activity of the C-terminal IDR of MYOD1 (MYOD1174 − 320) was weaker than that of the N-terminal IDR of MYOD1 (MYOD11 − 96).

The IDR-mediated DNA demethylation is thought to be carried out by recruiting the DNA demethylation machinery. We examined physical interactions of TET2 DNA demethylation protein with FEV and FOSB IDRs by co-IP assay (Supplementary Fig. S5). As we expected from the DNA-demethylation-promoting activity (Fig. 1C), RUNX11 − 299-FEV136 − 238, RUNX11 − 299-FOSB1 − 150, and RUNX11 − 299-FOSB255 − 472 were co-precipitated by HaloTag-TET2, whereas RUNX11 − 299-FEV1 − 45 was not. Because RUNX11 − 299 was not co-precipitated by HaloTag-TET2, the results suggest physical interactions between TET2 and FEV/FOSB IDRs.

Thus, the results indicated that DNA-demethylation-promoting activity is associated with IDRs in the TFs examined, although not all IDRs are active and there may be other regions associated with such activity.

Random forest analysis for such specific properties of IDRs

Recently, Tesei et al. [23] reported that the properties of each predicted IDR, expressed in terms of parameters, are linked to the subcellular localization and function of the protein. Building on this concept, we hypothesized that specific biophysical features of IDRs may underlie the ability of certain TFs to promote DNA demethylation. We used the Random Forest method to distinguish IDRs with and without DNA-demethylation-promoting activity. A Random Forest classifier was constructed to predict DNA-demethylation-promoting activity on the basis of 25 numeric physicochemical features extracted from length-controlled 26 positive and 32 negative IDRs (Fig. 2A). Feature importance was evaluated using the mean absolute SHAP values (Fig. 2B). Sequential forward feature selection based on this ranking identified four key features— aromaticity, aliphatic index, fractional charge ratio (fcr), and side chain hydrophobic density (shd)—as the most informative contributors to predictive performance (Fig. 2C). Aromaticity evaluates structural stability on the basis of the ratio of phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan (aromatic amino acids). Aliphatic index evaluates thermal stability on the basis of the ratio of alanine, valine, leucine, and isoleucine. Fcr indicates large fractions of charged residues. Shd quantifies the patterning of hydrophobic residues. With these four features, the final model achieved an AUC of 0.84 (Fig. 2D), with an optimized decision threshold determined by Youden’s index (cutoff = 0.304) (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, the calibration curve, which compares binned predicted probabilities with the observed positive rate, yielded a Brier score of 0.165 and an ECE of 0.159, indicating moderate predictive accuracy and reasonable calibration of predicted probabilities (Fig. 2E).

Prediction of IDRs and TFs associated with DNA-demethylation-promoting activity

We applied this final model to IDRs of all TFs and found that 40.6% of them (959 out of 2,364 IDRs) were predicted to have DNA-demethylation-promoting activity (Supplementary Table S1). In positive IDRs, the aliphatic index and fcr were significantly lower, while aromaticity and shd were significantly higher (Fig. 3A). These features may underline the differences in DNA demethylation potential because they were distinct from those of entire proteins. In the final prediction results, 1 out of 32 negative IDRs were misclassified as positive (Fig. 3B), highlighting the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity.

We predicted that 63.1% of the TFs (825 out of 1,308) possess DNA-demethylation-promoting activity (Supplementary Table S2); the prediction results for all TF families are presented in Supplementary Table S3. Among TF families with at least 10 members (Table 1), the percentage of TFs potentially active in this respect tended to be high in families related to development (such as the T-box and homeobox families) and low in TF families related to signal transduction (such as the MYB and RXR-like families). Finally, we examined how accurately we could classify 14 TFs with known DNA-demethylation-promoting activity [20, 21] but unknown contributions of individual IDRs. We predicted all of 14 TFs as active, indicating the model robustness and discriminative stability (Fig. 3C).

Properties of TFs predicted to have DNA-demethylation-promoting activity

We applied GO analysis to the 825 positively and 483 negatively predicted TFs using all 1,308 TFs as the universe. In the biological process analysis, 23 GO terms were significantly enriched in the positively predicted TFs (adjusted p-value < 0.05, Table 2). These terms were related to morphogenesis and development, such as embryonic morphogenesis (GO:0048598), embryo development (GO:0009790), and animal organ development (GO:0048513); this was consistent with the higher percentage of TFs with predicted DNA demethylation potential in development and morphogenesis-related TF families (Table 1). Notably, no significantly enriched GO terms were identified for the negatively predicted TFs.

Because abnormal DNA methylation is common across cancers, we examined the clinical relevance of TFs predicted to possess DNA-demethylation-promoting activity using TCGA transcriptomic and clinical data. In the multivariate Cox regression, including clinical covariates (age_at_initial_pathologic_diagnosis, gender, race, histological_grade, tumor_status, margin_status, and residual_tumor), none of the demethylation TF-based scores showed significant associations with survival. However, in the univariate Cox regression, high Yes or Yes/No ratio scores were commonly associated with worse prognosis in kidney papillary cell carcinoma and stomach adenocarcinoma across all TF cutoffs (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S6A). Notably, kidney papillary cell carcinoma exhibited a significantly worse prognosis (FDR < 0.05) with TF cutoffs of 100, 150, and 200. In contrast, the No score stratification had no significant prognostic value on these cancers (Supplementary Fig. S6B), demonstrating that the prognostic power is exclusive to the Yes-based scores. We further investigated the association between the Yes TF score at a cutoff of 100 and global DNA methylation status across cancer types (Fig. 4B). Analysis of mean β-values revealed that high Yes-scores were significantly correlated with lower DNA methylation states in brain lower grade glioma, thymoma, stomach adenocarcinoma, and head & neck squamous cell carcinoma. These results suggest that TFs with DNA-demethylation-promoting activity may drive tumor-specific global hypomethylation, potentially contributing to differential cancer prognosis.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically predicted which TFs possess DNA-demethylation-promoting activity. We showed that the DNA-demethylation-promoting activity of the TFs RUNX1 and SPI1 is associated with their IDRs, and that specific IDRs are associated with the activity in eight other TFs. We also successfully predicted IDRs and TFs associated with DNA demethylation using a Random Forest machine learning model with high predictive performance. Finally, we found that the predicted positive TFs were associated with morphogenesis and development, and we suggested such TFs to be clinically relevant to certain cancer types.

We have previously identified more than 30 TFs with DNA-demethylation-promoting activity [20, 21], suggesting that many TFs may have this activity where TFs may associate with the DNA demethylation machinery (e.g., TET DNA demethylation enzyme) through a general mechanism. Because it is widely known that IDRs form interaction surfaces with various proteins [26, 27], it is reasonable to hypothesize that specific TF IDRs mediate association with the DNA demethylation machinery. Methods represented by KMAP [28] demonstrate that DNA-binding motifs among family TFs are similar and ambiguous, which may characterize family TFs by diversifying features such as the DNA-demethylation-promoting activity of IDR. Nonetheless, this does not preclude other mechanisms. In fact, Chen et al. [29] reported that c-MYC recruits TET2 via the SMAD nuclear interacting protein 1 (SNIP1). Interestingly, SNIP1 interacts with TET2 through its IDR, although the SNIP1 IDR is predicted as negative in our model (the confidence probability of 0.218).

In the final model, all true-positive IDRs in the training set were judged to be positive, while 1 out of 32 true-negative IDRs was judged to be positive (false-positive). This result suggests that the negative IDRs may be judged as false-positives in the prediction list (Supplementary Table S1). However, the confidence probability of these false-positive IDRs was 0.317, which is much lower than that of the true-positive (0.707–0.988, Fig. 3B). Therefore, the accuracy of predictions would be improved by taking into account the confidence probabilities.

To evaluate the final model, we applied it to 14 TFs previously determined to have DNA-demethylation-promoting activity (Fig. 3C). Although all of 14 TFs was predicted to have this activity, MSC, MYF6, MYOG, ASCL2, and TCF21 had probability values of 0.31 to 0.43, a range similar to that for the false-positive IDRs (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, all of these five TFs belong to the bHLH family and have two relatively short IDRs (40–99 amino acid residues) at the N- and C-termini. Our method predicts DNA-demethylation-promoting activity of each IDR independently, but we cannot rule out that multiple IDRs cooperate to exert DNA-demethylation-promoting activity. In the latter case, we need to incorporate cooperativity into the current prediction model to clearly distinguish with and without the activity and reduce false-negatives.

GO terms associated with morphogenesis/development were enriched in TFs with DNA-demethylation-promoting activity. Such TFs may be expressed during cell differentiation to promote DNA demethylation around the TF binding sites, creating cell-type-specific DNA methylation profiles and thus supporting cell-specific gene expression.

Abnormal DNA methylation is a hallmark of many cancers and is typically characterized by coexistence of global hypomethylation and focal hypermethylation [30]. Our analysis suggests that TFs predicted to promote DNA demethylation may contribute to shaping cancer-specific methylation landscapes in a subset of tumor types. We observed associations between high expression of these TFs, global hypomethylation, and poorer prognosis in stomach adenocarcinoma, whereas no such association was evident in other cancers, indicating potential context dependency. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting oncogenic consequences of hypomethylation, including activation of cancer-germline genes [31] and enhancer deregulation leading to transcriptional reprogramming [32]. Whereas focal hypermethylation at CpG islands is thought to be mediated by repressive complexes, such as those of the Polycomb group [33], the TF-driven hypomethylation may act in parallel to promote oncogene activation and chromatin remodeling. These findings highlight a previously underappreciated role of TFs in modulating both local and global methylation states, and offer a new perspective on the interplay between methylation dynamics and cancer progression. However, these observations should be interpreted with caution, as the availability of complete clinical covariates varies substantially across cancer types and may limit the strength of TCGA-based survival inferences.

There is growing recognition that enhancers and promoters are potential therapeutic targets in cancer [34], highlighting the translational relevance of understanding TF regulatory mechanisms. For instance, a single nucleotide substitution in an enhancer element that alters HOXA2 binding has been linked to prostate cancer prognosis, illustrating how TF activity at cis-regulatory elements directly influences disease [35]. Furthermore, the DNA methylation status of TF binding sites is a critical determinant of binding affinity, with a growing number of TFs, particularly those involved in development, exhibiting methylation-sensitive or preferential binding [36]. In this context, our results provide a valuable resource by predicting which TFs, and through which specific disordered regions, are likely to actively shape the DNA methylation landscape. While this activity could function at various genomic loci, it is particularly consequential at enhancers, where dynamic DNA demethylation is an established hallmark of activation [37]. This bridges a critical gap between TF binding, enhancer biology, and epigenetic remodeling, offering new hypotheses for how IDR properties could inform the future targeting of TF-driven gene regulatory programs in disease.

Conclusion

In this study, we showed that the IDRs of TFs are associated with TFs’ DNA-demethylation-promoting activity, and provided a reliable list of TFs with this activity. We hope that this list will be useful for researchers interested in development and morphogenesis, as well as cancer and other diseases involving deregulation of cellular methylation status.
