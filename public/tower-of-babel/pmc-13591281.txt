Differential analysis of microbial interaction networks
Authors: Marianna Milano, Pietro Hiram Guzzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrobiome studies increasingly indicate that disease-associated shifts cannot be understood from compositional changes alone. The functional architecture of microbial communities—encoded in patterns of association among microbial gene families—may reveal how these systems reorganize across biological conditions. Here, we present a network-based framework for characterizing microbiome rewiring across conditions. The approach combines condition-specific network inference, differential network analysis, and pathway-level network analysis to identify associations that are gained, lost, or altered between groups, with a specific focus on sex-dependent differences. We apply the framework to inflammatory bowel disease, type 2 diabetes, and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ACVD), comparing male and female-specific microbial gene family networks within each disease context. Across these settings, differential networks flag large numbers of candidate rewired associations; however, permutation testing (sex labels shuffled, group sizes preserved, 500 permutations for gene-family networks, and 1000 for pathway networks) shows that the global amount of apparent rewiring is not greater than expected under the null at the global or edge level in any cohort, and that most edges exclusive to one group are induced by group-specific feature filtering rather than by a genuine change in association (∼ 80%–83% in ACVD). We therefore present the method as a rigorously validated framework and a cautionary case study: the differential-network machinery is sound, but the headline biological signal in a naive analysis is largely a property of correlation thresholding and, for the longitudinal inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) cohort, of pseudoreplication. The only non-null result across all validations is a SOHPIE-DNA per-taxon test in the IBD disease arm (15 taxa at FDR < 0.05), which we report as a single nominal finding requiring independent replication. Code, data, and supplementary information are available at https://github.com/mmilano87/NetMicrobiome.

Introduction

The human microbiome is a complex and dynamic ecosystem composed of different microorganisms that coexist with the host and contribute to its physiological functioning. These microbial communities are involved in multiple biological processes, including metabolism, immune system regulation, and neurological activity, and are essential for nutrient processing, immune development, and cellular signaling [1]. Therefore, the microbiota is widely recognized as a key component in maintaining host homeostasis [2].

Alterations in microbiome composition and organization have been associated with a broad range of pathological conditions, including metabolic, cardiovascular, and neurodegenerative diseases. In particular, changes in gut microbial composition have been linked to type 2 diabetes (T2D), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and Alzheimer’s disease, indicating that the microbiome may play an active role in disease onset and progression rather than acting as a marker [3]. These effects are mediated by complex interaction patterns between host and microbial communities, where both commensal and pathogenic microorganisms modulate host signaling pathways, immune responses, and cellular processes [4–6].

Despite these advances, most microbiome studies rely on differential abundance or diversity measures, which primarily describe compositional variations across conditions and may only partially reflect the underlying interaction structure of microbial communities. Indeed, the microbiome can be naturally modeled as a complex system of interacting entities, where microbial taxa (i.e. microbial units such as species, genera, or operational taxonomic units) are linked through cooperative, competitive, and antagonistic relationships. In this context, network-based approaches provide a suitable framework to characterize microbial organization by representing taxa as nodes and their associations as edges, thus enabling a systems analysis of community structure [7].

Within this framework, network analysis allows the identification of key structural properties, such as hubs and modules, which are often associated with functional relevance and system stability [3]. Importantly, this representation also enables the comparison of network structures across conditions, providing a natural basis to investigate how microbial associations change in different biological states.

A central concept emerging from this perspective is network rewiring, defined as the reorganization of association patterns across conditions. In particular, rewiring captures variations in the presence, strength, or sign of associations between microbial entities, reflecting condition-specific modifications in network connectivity. By explicitly modeling these changes, rewiring analysis provides insights into the mechanisms underlying phenotypic variation that cannot be captured by composition-based approaches alone [8].

To systematically capture these changes, differential network analysis (DiNA) has been introduced as a powerful framework for comparing networks across different conditions. Within the context of network rewiring, DiNA provides a formal approach to quantify how association patterns are reorganized between distinct biological states. In particular, given two networks representing different conditions, such as healthy versus diseased or male versus female subjects, DiNA aims to identify variations in connectivity patterns by explicitly comparing their edge structures [9].

By focusing on differences at the edge level, rather than solely on node changes, differential networks enable the identification of associations that are gained, lost, or significantly altered between conditions. These changes directly reflect rewiring events, capturing modifications in the presence, strength, or sign of associations between entities. As a result, DiNA provides a more comprehensive characterization of system alterations, revealing condition-specific association patterns. Overall, differential network analysis represents a key tool to investigate how biological systems reorganize under different conditions, allowing the identification of association changes that underlie phenotypic variability.

In this study, we propose a network-based framework to investigate microbiome alterations across different biological conditions, with a particular focus on sex-specific differences. Our approach integrates network construction, rewiring analysis, and differential network modeling to characterize changes in microbial association patterns.

Specifically, we construct condition-specific microbiome networks separately for male and female subjects across three disease i.e. inflammatory (IBD), metabolic (T2D), and cardiovascular [atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ACVD)] conditions. Differential networks are then derived to identify significant variations in associations between microbial gene families, capturing association changes between the two groups.

To further interpret these alterations from a functional perspective, we perform pathway-level differential network analysis on the gene families involved in rewiring, allowing us to link network changes to biological processes.

Our results show that differential network analysis captures rewiring patterns that complement standard abundance-based approaches. In particular, we characterize sex-associated association changes and evaluate, with permutation nulls and a differential-abundance baseline, whether they exceed chance. The differential network flags candidate pathways not seen in the individual networks, but we show that their number does not survive a label-shuffling null; we report this transparently rather than as discovery. Overall, these findings support the adoption of a systems perspective to better understand the mechanisms underlying microbiome-related diseases.

Motivation

Network-based approaches have become a powerful framework for investigating the structure and functionality of microbial communities, enabling the characterization of associations among microbial entities beyond simple abundance profiles [10, 11]. However, several methodological challenges still limit their effectiveness in capturing condition-specific alterations in microbiome organization.

A major limitation of existing studies lies in the adoption of global feature sets shared across conditions, which may obscure association patterns that are specific to a given biological state. In this setting, networks inferred from different groups are often directly compared without accounting for the fact that relevant features may differ across conditions.

In addition, most microbiome studies rely on differential abundance analysis to identify condition-associated changes [12, 13]. While effective at detecting variations in microbial composition, these approaches do not capture how relationships among microbial entities are reorganized. As a result, alterations occurring at the association level may remain undetected.

The intrinsic properties of microbiome data further complicate network inference. Microbial abundance data are characterized by sparsity, compositional constraints, and nonlinear dependencies, which can introduce biases and instability in correlation-based methods [14, 15]. These issues may affect the reliability of inferred networks and limit the identification of robust association patterns.

Furthermore, biological stratification factors, such as sex-specific differences, are often not explicitly incorporated into network-based analyses, despite increasing evidence of their relevance in microbiome-associated diseases [16–18]. Accounting for such factors is essential for a more accurate characterization of microbiome organization.

Motivated by these limitations, we propose a framework aimed at modeling microbiome alterations at the level of association dynamics. In particular, our approach is designed to (i) infer condition-specific networks based on group-dependent feature selection, (ii) identify rewiring patterns through differential network analysis, and (iii) provide a functional interpretation of these changes via pathway-level differential network analysis.

By focusing on association variations and integrating biological stratification, the proposed framework enables a more comprehensive representation of microbiome organization across different conditions.

Related work

Network-based approaches have become a widely adopted framework for modeling the human microbiome, as they enable the characterization of associations among microbial entities beyond simple abundance profiles [19]. By representing microbial taxa or gene families as nodes and their associations as connections, these methods provide a system view of community organization and have been extensively used to investigate microbiome structure and its relationship with disease.

To address the specific challenges of microbiome data, such as compositionality and sparsity, several computational methods have been developed for network inference. SparCC (Sparse Correlations for Compositional data) [20] estimates correlations between microbial taxa by inferring associations between latent absolute abundances through log-ratio transformations, under the assumption of a sparse underlying correlation structure.

CoNet [10] adopts an ensemble strategy by combining multiple association measures, including correlation, dissimilarity, and information-theoretic metrics, and identifies statistically significant associations through permutation and bootstrap procedures.

SPIEC-EASI (Sparse Inverse Covariance Estimation for Ecological Association Inference) [15] relies on graphical models and sparse inverse covariance estimation to infer direct associations, reducing the impact of indirect correlations and compositional effects.

NetCoMi [21] provides a comprehensive framework for microbiome network construction, comparison, and analysis, integrating different inference methods and supporting the computation of network properties for downstream analysis.

While these methods have significantly advanced microbiome network inference, they are primarily focused on the construction and analysis of individual networks. Most existing studies rely on topological descriptors or differential abundance at the node level, without explicitly addressing how association patterns change across conditions. As a result, the variability and reorganization of microbial associations remain only partially explored.

In parallel, differential network analysis has been extensively investigated in other domains, particularly in gene co-expression studies, where it is used to identify changes in connectivity patterns between biological states [8, 9]. These approaches have demonstrated that variations in network structure can reveal system alterations that are not detectable through feature-based analyses alone.

Building on this paradigm, recent studies have started to explore the application of differential network analysis in the microbiome domain. Early approaches rely on correlation-based network construction, where microbial association networks are inferred independently for each condition and compared through global or topological properties [22]. While these methods highlight the potential of network-based comparisons to uncover condition-specific association patterns, they are limited by their descriptive nature and do not explicitly model differences between networks.

More recent methods aim to incorporate statistical modeling into differential microbiome network analysis. For example, Ahn et al. [23] proposed SOHPIE-DNA, a regression-based framework that detects differential connectivity by modeling node centrality measures as a function of covariates. Similarly, tools such as NetCoMi [21] and MDiNE [24] enable the construction and comparison of microbial networks across conditions, focusing on differences in network topology or connectivity patterns. More advanced probabilistic approaches, such as the Bayesian covariance regression model TRECOR [25], allow the estimation of networks whose covariance structure varies with covariates, capturing context-dependent changes in microbial associations.

Despite these advances, existing approaches differ substantially in their objectives and modeling assumptions. In particular, most methods focus on node connectivity or global network properties, or model covariance structures without explicitly characterizing association changes. As a consequence, they do not provide a unified and formal framework to define and systematically quantify network rewiring across conditions.

For this reason, a direct comparison with existing tools is not straightforward, as they address related but fundamentally different problems. Network inference methods such as SparCC, CoNet, SPIEC-EASI, and NetCoMi are primarily designed for constructing microbial association networks, while differential approaches in both microbiome and other domains focus on connectivity or covariance changes rather than explicitly modeling variations in association patterns.

In this study, we propose a unified framework that integrates condition-specific network construction and differential network analysis to characterize changes in association patterns across multiple conditions and sexes. By introducing a formal definition of rewiring and enabling a systematic comparison of networks, our approach provides a coherent and interpretable framework to study microbiome organization.

To the best of our knowledge, this work represents one of the first attempts to systematically investigate microbiome rewiring through differential networks in a multi-condition and sex-aware setting, addressing a key gap in current methodologies.

Methodology

The analysis of the gut microbiome is inherently complex due to the high dimensionality and interdependence of microbial functional components. For this reason, in this study we adopt a network-based approach to model the microbiome as a system of co-abundant gene families, enabling the characterization of coordinated functional relationships. This representation enables the investigation of coordinated patterns of microbial activity and provides a natural framework to study how such associations vary across biological conditions.

We investigate microbiome organization by explicitly considering both disease status (healthy versus diseased) and sex (male versus female). Our objective is to characterize how functional relationships among microbial gene families vary across these groups, with the goal to capture differences in their association structure.

To this end, we adopt an association-network perspective, where microbiome alterations are captured through variations in the structure and strength of relationships between gene families inferred from group-specific data. This approach takes into account feature-level analysis and directly models differences in the organization of microbial functional networks.

Our methodological pipeline consists of three main steps. First, we construct group-specific functional networks independently for each condition and sex, using a dynamic feature selection strategy that allows each network to reflect the specific functional composition of the corresponding group. Second, we compare these networks to identify changes in microbial associations between groups, capturing variations in both the presence and strength of associations. Finally, we apply pathway-level differential network analysis on the set of gene families involved in the identified association changes, in order to highlight the biological processes most affected by the investigated factors.

To investigate sex-specific differences in the functional organization of the gut microbiome, we performed a differential network analysis aimed at identifying changes in the association structure between microbial gene families across conditions.

Within a network-based framework, biological differences are not only reflected by variations in the abundance of individual features, but also by changes in the relationships among them. These changes, referred to as network rewiring, describe how associations between components of the system are reorganized across conditions. Capturing such rewiring patterns enables the identification of functional alterations that cannot be detected through feature analysis alone.

Pathway-level differential network analysis

To investigate the biological relevance of the identified rewiring patterns, we performed a pathway-level analysis directly on the HUMAnN MetaCyc pathway-abundance layer available for the same samples, avoiding any dependence on external gene-family-to-pathway accession mappings. We restricted the analysis to named, unstratified MetaCyc pathways.

For each cohort we applied the same differential-network pipeline used for gene families, now at the pathway level: sex-specific pathway co-abundance networks were inferred independently for each group (centered log-ratio transform, Spearman correlation, association threshold τ ), and a differential pathway network was obtained by thresholding the change in association strength (δ ). Because pathway relative abundances are roughly two orders of magnitude smaller than gene-family values, the pathway networks were filtered by prevalence only. Each rewired pathway edge was labeled by the artifact-decomposition scheme used throughout this work, distinguishing genuine association changes (true_change, both endpoints passing the prevalence filter in both groups) from group-specific filtering artifacts (one endpoint absent from a network).

To assess whether the observed pathway rewiring exceeds chance, and to control for the fact that raw edge counts scale with network size and node degree, we used a label-shuffling permutation test: the sex labels were randomly permuted while preserving group sizes, the full pathway differential-network pipeline was re-run on each permutation, and empirical P-values were computed for three summary statistics—the total number of true_change pathway edges (size-sensitive), the number of distinct pathways carrying rewired edges (degree-robust), and the maximum per-pathway rewiring score (degree-robust). Here 500 permutations were used for gene-family networks and 1000 for pathway networks, per cohort. This replaces the original correlation-based module-versus-pathway ranking, whose reported false-discovery rates were not reproducible and did not control for network size.

Overall, this analysis provides a reproducible and network-size-aware interpretation of pathway rewiring, distinguishing candidate functional signals from thresholding and filtering artifacts.

Datasets

In this study, we analyzed large-scale human gut microbiome datasets to investigate sex-specific functional network rewiring across different disease contexts. We focused on three representative conditions characterized by distinct biological mechanisms: inflammatory, metabolic, and cardiovascular diseases. Specifically, we considered IBD, T2D, and ACVD.

These diseases were selected to capture complementary axes of microbiome–host interaction. IBD represents a prototypical inflammatory disorder driven by chronic immune activation [26], T2D is associated with metabolic dysregulation and insulin resistance [27], while ACVD reflects alterations in lipid metabolism and systemic vascular inflammation [28]. By analyzing these conditions, we aim to identify both shared and disease-specific patterns of microbiome functional organization.

All datasets were retrieved from the Curated Metagenomic Data repository [29], which provides uniformly processed shotgun metagenomic profiles.

The IBD dataset (HMP_2019_ibdmdb) is part of the Integrative Human Microbiome Project (iHMP) [30], and it includes patients affected by Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, along with healthy controls. It represents one of the most comprehensive resources for studying inflammation-driven microbiome alterations. The dataset comprises 1627 stool samples, but these are longitudinal repeated measures from only 130 unique subjects (median 12 visits per subject); the raw sample-level counts are 631 IBD-female, 570 IBD-male, 192 control-female, and 234 control-male sample-visits. Treating these repeated visits as independent samples constitutes pseudoreplication and severely inflates edge counts and rewiring significance (see Pseudoreplication of the longitudinal inflammatory bowel disease cohort). We therefore take the subject level as the primary unit of analysis for IBD, retaining one first-visit sample per subject (IBD: 51 male, 52 female; control: 15 male, 12 female), and report sample-level results only to demonstrate the pseudoreplication artifact.

To investigate metabolic and cardiovascular conditions, we used the MetaCardis cohort (MetaCardis_2020_a) [31], a large European multicenter study focusing on cardiometabolic diseases. This dataset includes individuals affected by Type 2 Diabetes, coronary artery disease, metabolic syndrome, and healthy controls. For the sex-stratified type 2 diabetes analysis we retained the subjects with a T2D or control label and known sex for which version-matched functional profiles were available, yielding 953 samples: 245 T2D-female, 258 T2D-male, 309 control-female, and 141 control-male (verified directly from the MetaCardis_2020_a metadata). We note that the counts stated in an earlier version of this manuscript (637 T2D-femaleand 526 T2D-male) were internally inconsistent with their stated total (1317) and are not reproducible from the current MetaCardis release; the values above are the exact counts used here. This cohort enables the study of microbiome alterations associated with insulin resistance, glucose metabolism, and systemic metabolic dysregulation. Unlike the IBD cohort, the MetaCardis samples are not longitudinal, so no subject-level collapsing is required.

To investigate cardiovascular alterations, we used the JieZ_2017 dataset available in curatedMetagenomicData [29]. This dataset includes subjects affected by ACVD and healthy controls. After filtering, the dataset consists of 53 ACVD females, 157 ACVD males, 101 control females, and 69 control males. This dataset enables the investigation of microbiome functional changes associated with lipid metabolism, inflammation, and cardiovascular risk.

All analyses were performed at the level of gene families, specifically UniRef90 clusters [32], rather than taxonomic profiles.

Each dataset is represented as a matrix of gene families by samples, where each entry corresponds to the relative abundance of a microbial functional unit. UniRef90 clusters group protein sequences sharing at least 90% sequence identity, providing a compact and functionally meaningful representation of microbial activity.

We chose to focus on the gene family level for several reasons. First, gene families represent functional units rather than taxonomic entities, enabling a more direct interpretation of microbiome activity. Second, they are more stable across individuals compared with species profiles. Third, they allow direct linkage to key biological processes, including glucose metabolism, insulin resistance, lipid metabolism, and inflammation. Finally, gene families are particularly suitable for network-based analyses, as they enable the study of functional associations and rewiring mechanisms.

Importantly, in this study the nodes of the networks do not represent human genes, but microbial gene families, allowing us to characterize the functional organization of the microbiome.

Gene family abundance matrices exhibit key properties typical of microbiome data. They are highly sparse, with most gene families present in a limited number of samples. They follow a power-law distribution, where a small number of functions are highly prevalent while many are rare. Additionally, they exhibit functional redundancy, as multiple gene families can contribute to the same biological pathway.

These characteristics motivate the use of network-based approaches to capture coordinated functional behavior and identify condition-specific rewiring patterns across different diseases and between sexes.

Results

To demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed network-based framework, we analyze microbiome data across three representative disease contexts: inflammatory (IBD), metabolic (T2D), and cardiovascular (ACVD), with a particular focus on sex-specific differences in microbial organization.

Differences in microbiome structure between males and females may provide insights into the biological mechanisms underlying variability in disease onset, progression, and response. In this context, we aim to characterize how associations between microbial gene families are reorganized across sexes, enabling the analysis of microbiome organization at the level of associations, complementing feature approaches.

For each condition, we first construct gene co-abundance networks separately for male and female subjects. We then derive differential networks by directly comparing these condition-specific networks, allowing us to explicitly capture rewiring patterns between the two groups. In this framework, differential networks represent the key component for identifying associations that are gained, lost, or significantly altered, thus providing a direct characterization of condition-specific changes in microbial connectivity.

To further interpret these association changes from a biological perspective, we perform pathway-level differential network analysis on gene families involved in the identified rewiring patterns. This step enables the identification of biological pathways associated with sex-specific differences, linking changes in network structure to functional mechanisms.

The results are organized by disease, enabling a systematic comparison of network topology and rewiring patterns across different biological contexts, while pathway-level differential network analysis is used to infer the associated functional changes.

In neither arm does the pathway-rewiring signal exceed the sex-shuffled null: in the control differential network the number of distinct rewired pathways is 153 observed versus 151.6 ± 2.6 (P =.400), and in the ACVD differential network 104 observed versus 109 ± 3.5 (P =.922); the total count of rewired pathway edges is likewise null-consistent in both arms (P =.266 and P =.934, respectively); the top per-pathway rewiring score is null-consistent in both arms (P =.419 and P =.940, respectively). The original KEGG false-discovery rates for ACVD were more moderate than for IBD/T2D but relied on the same nonreproducible UniRef90-to-KEGG mapping; the reproducible MetaCyc analysis finds no permutation-supported pathway-level differential network signal in either ACVD arm. The ranked candidate lists are available in the public repository as supplementary/enrichment_pathway_candidates_acvd.csv. This is consistent with the taxonomic-level ACVD result, where only the raw true_change edge count is borderline (observed 1710 versus null 1307.9 ± 238.4; P =.060) while global rewiring is null-consistent (control P =.469, ACVD P =.513).

In contrast, the ACVD differential network (Fig. 14) appears more fragmented and functionally simplified, with fewer pathways and a reduced number of multifunctional nodes. Overall, these results indicate that, unlike T2D where the differential network flags a larger raw count of candidate pathways, ACVD is characterized by a reduction in both structural and functional diversity, reflecting a constrained and disrupted microbiome organization.

Robustness and validation

Because the analyses above rely on correlation thresholding and on group-specific feature selection, we subjected every headline observation to a battery of controls: label-shuffling permutation nulls, an artifact decomposition of the differential edges, threshold-sensitivity and equal-sample-size analyses, covariate residualization, a differential-abundance baseline, an external benchmark, and a ground-truth simulation. We summarize the outcomes here. Selected validation summaries are provided in Supplementary Table S1 (worksheets S1a–S1e); complete machine-readable robustness outputs, including the full threshold-sensitivity grid, are available in the public repository. The consistent message is that the differential-network machinery behaves correctly, but the apparent biological rewiring in the naive analysis does not exceed chance once these controls are applied.

Permutation testing of global rewiring

For each cohort and arm we permuted the sex labels (preserving group sizes) and re-ran the full network-inference and differential pipeline (500 permutations for gene-family networks and 1000 for pathway networks), yielding empirical P-values for the number of rewired edges, the number of genuine association changes (true_change), and related statistics. The total amount of rewiring is not greater than expected under the null in essentially every arm: ACVD control P =.469 and ACVD P =.513; IBD (subject-level) control P =.106 and IBD P =.842; T2D control P =.058 and T2D P =.743. The true_change metric is likewise null-consistent, with a single borderline exception in the ACVD arm (observed 1710 versus null 1307.9 ± 238.4; P =.060), which we report as a weak, nonsignificant trend rather than a finding. We note two nominal count-metric excesses that do not survive scrutiny: the T2D-control raw rewired-edge count (P =.058) is driven by network size (the corresponding true_change metric gives P =.128), and, as discussed below, the sample-level IBD results in both arms are a pseudoreplication artifact.

Pseudoreplication of the longitudinal inflammatory bowel disease cohort

The IBD cohort is longitudinal: its 1627 samples are repeated visits from 130 subjects. Treating them as independent produces apparently overwhelming rewiring results under the permutation null in both arms (control: 142 944 observed rewired edges versus 56 109 ± 10 524 expected, P =.001; IBD: 86 533 versus 36 651 ± 2924, P =.001). This significance is an artifact of pseudoreplication rather than biology: shuffling sex across correlated repeated visits collapses the null mean ∼ 2–2.5-fold (control \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $134{\,}023 \rightarrow 56{\,}109$\end{document}, 2.4× ; IBD \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $81{\,}690 \rightarrow 36{\,}651$\end{document}, 2.2× ), while the observed value is essentially unchanged . Analyzed correctly at the subject level (one first-visit sample per subject), the raw rewiring is null-consistent in every arm (control P =.106, IBD P =.842).

The most diagnostic evidence that this is a statistical artifact rather than biology comes from the true_change metric, which is designed to be robust to the prevalence-filtering artifacts discussed below and is therefore the quantity a naive analyst would most trust. Its behavior is arm-dependent but equally revealing. In the control arm it is null at both levels (sample P =.28, subject P =.671). In the disease arm, however, it appears highly significant at the sample level (observed 21 590 versus null 11 281 ± 1023; P =.001) yet becomes null once repeated visits are collapsed to subjects (observed 19 649 versus null 17 367.7 ± 3338.7; P =.299). Crucially, this reversal is driven almost entirely by the null distribution: between the two levels the observed statistic changes by only 10%, whereas the null mean deflates 1.5× (17 368 → 11 281). In other words, pseudoreplication manufactures apparent significance even in the metric specifically constructed to be filtering-robust, and it does so by shrinking the reference distribution rather than by inflating the observed effect. All IBD results in this manuscript are therefore reported at the subject level; the sample-level numbers are retained only to document the artifact, and the full sample-versus-subject contrast for both arms and both metrics is provided in Supplementary Table S1, worksheet S1c.

Artifact decomposition of differential edges

Decomposing the rewired edges by type shows that the large “exclusive” edge counts are dominated by group-specific feature filtering rather than genuine association changes: in ACVD, 79.9% (control) and 83.2% (disease) of rewired edges are node_missing artifacts (one endpoint absent from a network because it failed the prevalence filter in that group), with genuine true_change edges accounting for only 17%–20%. This decomposition is what motivates using true_change and distinct-pathway counts, rather than raw edge counts, as the primary metrics throughout.

Differential-abundance baseline

To place the network results in context, we ran a standard compositional differential-abundance test (ALDEx2) on the ACVD species profiles. No species is significant at a Benjamini–Hochberg FDR below 0.05 in either arm (control: 15 of 544 raw P <.05, 0 surviving FDR, against 27 expected by chance; ACVD: 14 of 633 raw P <.05, 0 surviving FDR), and the maximum absolute effect size is small (<.3). The absence of a strong single-taxon abundance signal is consistent with, and provides context for, the null differential-network results.

External benchmark and ground-truth simulation

Benchmarking against NetCoMi—a widely used tool for constructing and comparing microbial association networks—yields zero statistically significant differential edges in all four evaluated arms (ACVD control and disease; IBD subject-level control and disease), consistent with our permutation-based conclusion that the between-sex differential signal is at the noise level. Finally, on simulated compositional data with a known set of planted differential edges, the pipeline localizes real signal well when ranking edges by the magnitude of the association change (high precision at the top of the ranking), but the aggregate true_change count is null-consistent even in the presence of real signal, because the centered log-ratio transform of sparse compositional data induces background edges. This is precisely the behavior we observe in the real cohorts, and it is why we frame the method as a validated tool that must be paired with permutation testing rather than as a discovery engine.

Cross-method reproducibility

To test whether our conclusions depend on the particular association estimator, we re-ran the entire differential pipeline under two additional and mechanistically distinct compositional network models—SPIEC-EASI [15], which infers conditional dependencies by sparse neighborhood selection with StARS stability selection, and SparCC [20], which estimates bootstrapped marginal correlations of log-ratio-transformed abundances—together with SOHPIE-DNA [23], a covariate-adjusted per-taxon pseudo-value regression that tests differential connectivity at the level of individual taxa rather than edges. These were applied to the two cohorts for which integer species counts can be reconstructed (ACVD and the subject-level IBD cohort), in both arms; the T2D cohort is excluded from these count-based checks because its source resource does not retain the per-sample read depths required to rebuild integer counts. Full numbers are given in the supplementary material (available at https://github.com/mmilano87/NetMicrobiome). Two points reconcile the methods. First, both estimators return nontrivial raw counts of differential edges (e.g. 580 and 655 for ACVD under SPIEC-EASI, 71 under SparCC), but neither carries a built-in significance test for the between-group difference, so these counts are descriptive rather than inferential; the IBD control arm makes this concrete, where the smallest group (12–15 subjects per sex) produces the largest apparent differences (284 and 450 “differential” edges) while the two sex-specific graphs are almost disjoint (Jaccard 0.02–0.04), a signature of small-sample instability rather than biology. Reassuringly, the two significance-calibrated tests—our CLR+Spearman permutation null and NetCoMi’s FDR-controlled comparison—remain null under both estimators and in every arm, so the paper’s central negative result is not an artifact of the correlation measure. Second, SOHPIE-DNA is likewise null in three of the four arms, but in the IBD disease arm it flags 15 differentially connected taxa at a Benjamini–Hochberg FDR below 0.05 (including Escherichia coli, Prevotella copri, Veillonella parvula, and Eggerthella lenta). We report this positive result plainly: it is not a contradiction of the edge-level null, because aggregating each taxon’s connectivity into a single covariate-adjusted test is far better powered than testing thousands of individual edges under multiplicity, and the signal is driven by the sex term (median \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $p_{\mathrm{sex}} = 7\times 10^{-4}$\end{document}) rather than by the age adjustment (age nonsignificant for 12 of the 15 taxa). It indicates that covariate-adjusted taxon-aggregate testing can surface sex-differential connectivity in the inflamed gut that edge-wise analyses are underpowered to detect—a concrete, testable direction for future work on adequately powered cohorts, and one that does not alter the conclusion that raw differential-edge counts must be interpreted against a permutation null.

Discussion

This study presents a network-based framework to investigate sex-specific differences in the functional organization of the gut microbiome across multiple disease contexts. By integrating co-abundance network construction, differential network analysis, and pathway-level network analysis, our approach provides a system perspective on microbiome alterations.

A key contribution of this work is the use of differential networks to explicitly model functional rewiring between male and female microbiomes. Unlike standard approaches focused on differential abundance, differential networks capture variations in the association structure between microbial gene families, enabling the identification of relationships that are gained, lost, or altered across conditions. This aspect is particularly relevant in complex microbial ecosystems, where interactions play a fundamental role in shaping community function [10]. In this framework, differential network analysis provides the methodological tool, while rewiring represents the biological signal of interest.

Across all analyzed conditions, the raw differential-network summaries are dominated by the presence or the absence of associations rather than by uniform shifts in correlation strength; we emphasize, however, that permutation testing shows this apparent rewiring does not exceed the sex-shuffled null in any cohort. In particular, rewiring is largely associated with the presence or absence of associations, suggesting that disease-related changes are dominated by the disruption or emergence of specific functional relationships. These findings are consistent with previous studies highlighting the importance of network organization in microbiome dynamics and disease [33, 34].

From a sex-specific view, control networks exhibit higher variability between males and females compared with disease conditions. Differential networks in healthy subjects are characterized by higher density, a larger number of rewired edges, and a greater occurrence of sign changes, indicating a flexible and dynamic association landscape. This suggests that, in physiological conditions, the microbiome supports multiple alternative configurations of functional relationships across sexes.

In contrast, disease conditions tend to constrain this variability, although with distinct patterns across diseases. In IBD, the reduction in network density and differential connectivity suggests a loss of global association structure, accompanied by a decrease in the diversity of rewiring patterns. However, this structural simplification is associated with an increase in the number of pathways with co-abundance edges, indicating that inflammatory conditions lead to a broad activation of microbial functions while limiting the emergence of novel association-driven signals.

A markedly different behavior is observed in T2D. While a reduction in connectivity is still present, the differential network identifies a substantially larger number of pathways with co-abundance edges compared to individual male and female networks, with a larger raw count of candidate pathways flagged by the differential network. This larger raw count is not supported by the pathway-level permutation test (see Section Robustness and validation), so we do not interpret it as evidence that metabolic-disorder functional alterations emerge specifically from coordinated association changes. In this context, we found no permutation-significant excess of rewiring in T2D; the larger raw pathway count is consistent with a finite-sample property of correlation thresholding rather than a distinct biological mechanism [8]. The co-abundance of pathways related to amino acid biosynthesis and carbohydrate metabolism further supports the role of the microbiome in host metabolic regulation [35].

In contrast, ACVD is characterized by a pronounced reduction in both structural and functional diversity. The number of pathways with co-abundance edges is drastically reduced, particularly in males, and the differential network identifies only a minimal number of novel pathways. This pattern is consistent with the observed fragmentation of network structure and reduced connectivity, suggesting a constrained and disrupted microbiome organization. Functionally, the dominance of pathways related to glycolysis and fermentation indicates a shift toward basic energy production processes, reflecting a simplified metabolic landscape. In this case, rewiring does not introduce new functional signals but rather reflects a loss of system complexity.

Taken together, the raw differential-network summaries differ across IBD, T2D, and ACVD; however, none of these cross-disease contrasts survives a label-shuffling permutation test, so we do not claim distinct mechanistic roles for rewiring across diseases. The cross-disease comparison is retained as a descriptive, hypothesis-generating observation.

The integration of pathway-level differential network analysis with differential networks further strengthens these findings. Despite the challenges associated with the functional annotation of microbial gene families, this step enables the translation of network changes into biological interpretation. Importantly, differential networks flag candidate pathways not seen when analyzing male and female networks separately; however, the number of such candidate pathways does not exceed the sex-shuffled permutation null in any cohort, so we interpret them as hypothesis-generating rather than as established discoveries.

The analysis of subnetworks centered on highly rewired nodes provides additional support for these observations. In control conditions, nodes tend to participate in multiple pathways, indicating a high degree of functional redundancy and flexibility. In contrast, disease networks exhibit a more constrained organization, with fewer multifunctional nodes and more localized functional associations. This suggests that rewiring affects not only association structure but also the integration of microbial functions across pathways.

From a methodological perspective, our results demonstrate that differential network analysis represents a framework to bridge network topology and functional interpretation. By capturing association changes, this approach provides insights into microbiome organization that complement feature-based analyses, though these insights must be validated with permutation testing to avoid over-interpreting correlation-thresholding artifacts.

In conclusion, this study presents a network-based framework for investigating microbiome rewiring across three disease contexts. The raw differential-network summaries differ across IBD, T2D, and ACVD; however, none of these cross-disease contrasts survives a label-shuffling permutation test, so we do not claim distinct mechanistic roles for rewiring across diseases. The cross-disease comparison is retained as a descriptive, hypothesis-generating observation. The central methodological message is that differential-network analysis is a valid and useful framework, but it must be paired with permutation-based significance testing and artifact decomposition to avoid over-interpreting correlation-thresholding artifacts. We present this work as both a rigorously validated tool and a cautionary case study: the machinery is sound, but the biological signal in a naive analysis is largely a property of thresholding and, for longitudinal cohorts, of pseudoreplication.

Conclusions

In this work, we introduced a network-based framework to investigate microbiome alterations by modeling differences in associations between microbial gene families across conditions. The proposed approach integrates condition-specific network construction, differential network analysis, and pathway-level network analysis to capture rewiring patterns between male and female subjects.

Microbiome data were first processed to construct co-abundance networks for each condition and sex. Subsequently, differential networks were derived by comparing male and female networks, allowing the identification of associations that are gained, lost, or altered between the two groups. This procedure enabled the characterization of rewiring patterns across three disease contexts, IBD, T2D, and ACVD.

The analysis revealed that microbiome alterations are largely driven by changes in the structure of associations between gene families. In particular, control conditions exhibited a higher number of rewired associations, suggesting a more flexible and heterogeneous organization, whereas disease conditions showed a reduction in association variability, indicating a more constrained network structure.

Differential networks were used to characterize condition-specific rewiring by identifying changes in associations between microbial gene families. The functional interpretation of these rewiring events was subsequently performed through pathway-level differential network analysis. This analysis highlighted metabolic and biosynthetic pathways, including amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate degradation, and energy-related processes, suggesting their involvement in disease-associated microbiome alterations. Importantly, no pathways were detected exclusively in the differential networks; the number of candidate pathways flagged only by rewiring does not exceed the sex-shuffled permutation null in any cohort, so we interpret them as hypothesis-generating signals rather than established discoveries.

Overall, the proposed framework provides a comprehensive approach to study microbiome organization across conditions, emphasizing the importance of considering association changes between microbial gene families. This perspective enables the identification of condition-specific mechanisms and supports a more detailed interpretation of microbiome-associated alterations.

From a translational perspective, this approach may contribute to improving the characterization of disease-associated microbiome profiles and supports future applications in biomarker discovery and personalized medicine. Further developments may include the integration of additional data layers, such as taxonomic profiles and host-related information, to better understand microbiome–host interactions and their role in complex diseases.
