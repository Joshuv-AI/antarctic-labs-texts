Oncology nurses’ knowledge, perceptions, and perceived barriers related to discussing physical activity with patients with cancer, including older adults
Authors: Schroder Sattar, Michelle B. Nadler, Linda Trinh, Kristen R. Haase, Sebastian Harenberg, Michelle Hannan, Rana Jin, Sarah Neil-Sztramko, Koen Milisen, Cindy Kenis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCancer and its treatments accelerate frailty in older adults with cancer (OACA). Exercise is safe and beneficial during and after treatment, yet many, including older adults, do not meet current exercise guidelines. The primary objective of this study was to understand oncology clinic nurses’ knowledge and current practices regarding exercise discussion and promotion in patients with cancer, including in older patients. The secondary objectives were to: (1) explore barriers to initiating exercise discussion and promotion; and (2) explore the relationship between oncology nurses’ exercise behavior, and their knowledge about existing guidelines and exercise promotion practices.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted via REDCap between February and May 2023 involving nurses who practice in outpatient oncology clinics. The survey was developed based on the expert consensus of the study team and was distributed through professional networks. Descriptive statistics and chi-square were used to analyze the collected data.ResultsNinety-seven nurses participated. The majority were aged 41–50 years (38.1%) and 31–40 years (27.8%). Mean years of practice was 13.5 (0.5–45). Many nurses lacked formal training on exercise discussion with patients (57.8%), yet expressed interest in learning (91%). Most nurses (90%) agreed exercise benefits patients during treatment. The majority (75%) reported discussing or recommending exercise to patients with cancer, especially advising patients to stay active during and after treatment. Barriers to promoting exercise included limited clinic time (77.7%) and concerns about safety especially for OACA (72.2%). 37.6% of nurses reported engaging in moderate-intensity aerobic activity. 26.6% endorsed both being quite/very knowledgeable in discussing exercise guidelines with patients with cancer in general and 74.7% reported typically discussing exercise with patients with cancer in general. A statistically significant association was found between nurses’ exercise promotion behaviour and perceived knowledge about exercise promotion (p = .01).ConclusionMany nurses agree that exercise promotion is important and consider it part of their scope of practice to discuss exercise with patients with cancer. Although perceived knowledge regarding specific recommendations is generally low, nurses express interest in enhancing their knowledge and participating in diverse educational opportunities. Institutions should prioritize nurse education to support efforts in exercise promotion.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04090-w.

Introduction

Cancer and the effects from its treatments can impact multiple body systems and may decrease physical and cognitive function and quality of life [1–9]. In older adults, this decline may accelerate the development of frailty and have lasting impacts after the completion of cancer treatment [10, 11] associated with reduced survival [12, 13]. It is well-established that exercise is safe and beneficial during and after cancer treatment [14, 15], including for older adults with cancer (OACA) [16–18] and for those with advanced disease [19–21]. Exercise protects against chronic inflammation [22], improves cardiorespiratory fitness [23, 24] and physical function [14], improves fitness and body composition [25–27], reduces anxiety and depressive symptoms [14] and cancer-related fatigue [24], improves social well-being [24], quality of life [28], and is associated with improved survival [29].

Exercise is defined as planned, structured, and repetitive, and an intermediate or final objective to improve or maintain physical fitness [30]. The exercise guidelines for cancer survivors recommend 90 min/week of aerobic exercise for certain symptoms, with the goal of reaching 150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous physical activities (MVPA), plus 2X/week of resistance training [31, 32]. However, 70% of cancer survivors, including OACA, do not meet current exercise guidelines [33–35]. OACA are especially vulnerable to functional decline from the effects of cancer and its treatments; however, OACA tend to be more sedentary [36, 37].

Evidence suggests that individuals living with and beyond cancer prefer receiving information about exercise from their oncology care professionals [38–43], and that such types of discussion help increase engagement in PA [38–40]. Patients who report hearing about exercise are more likely to undertake PA and are more likely to meet PA guidelines [44]. Despite the benefits, discussion of exercise during oncology clinic appointments is not common [45].Nurses spend more time with patients than other healthcare professionals [46, 47]; hence, oncology nurses are well-positioned to discuss PA with and make exercise recommendations for individuals with cancer [48], including OACA [49].

Despite the important role of nursing in discussing exercise, no studies have explored oncology nurses’ perceptions regarding counselling patients, including OACA, on exercise. Understanding oncology nurses’ knowledge, perception, and comfort level with respect to exercise promotion in outpatient oncology is an important first step to identify opportunities for implementation and/or to address any barriers to promoting exercise during clinic appointments. The primary objective of this study was to understand oncology clinic nurses’ knowledge and current practices regarding PA discussion and promotion in oncology patients, including in OACA. The secondary objectives were to: (1) explore any perceived barriers to initiating exercise discussion and promotion; and (2) explore the relationship between oncology nurses’ exercise behavior, and their knowledge about existing guidelines and exercise promotion practices.

Methods

Study design and sample

Using a cross-sectional design, we conducted an online survey (February 2023 – March 2023) via a secured survey system (REDCap). Inclusion criteria were nurses in any country working in outpatient oncology clinics, and ability to read and understand English. No exclusion criteria were applied. A convenience-based snowball sampling strategy was used. Due to the lack of a centralized registry and reliance on informal dissemination of the survey, the total number of individuals reached could not be estimated. Therefore, a formal sample size calculation was not feasible.

Recruitment

The survey was distributed through collaborating organizations, including the Nursing and Allied Health Group within the International Society of Geriatric Oncology (SIOG-NAH), the European Oncology Nursing Society (EONS), and the Canadian Association of Nurses in Oncology (CANO), who shared the invitation and survey link via internal email communications. The study team provided a standardized invitation template but did not have direct contact with members or access to distribution lists or membership numbers. The survey was also shared informally through professional networks and social media.

Human ethics and consent to participate

Research Ethics approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Board of the University of Saskatchewan prior to commencement of data collection. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. On the cover page of the survey, participants are advised that completing and submitting this survey signified their implied consent.

Data collection

The survey tool was developed based on the expert consensus from the study team, and in part drew from existing tools developed by co-authors (MN & SNS) [50, 51], as well as incorporated the Modified Godin Leisure-Time Exercise Questionnaire [52, 53]. Minor adjustments were made to incorporate questions specifically pertaining to OACA. The final version was not independently validated prior to use. The survey included 57 items in total, comprising a mix of item types, including multiple choice, Likert-scale, and select-all-that-apply formats. Specifically, the survey contained five sections: (1) demographic characteristics; (2) history of formal training in exercise discussion with patients who have cancer and preference for learning (three questions); (3) knowledge about PA in the cancer context (four questions each with sub-questions); (4) Current practices related to PA discussion and recommendation (four questions and some with sub-questions); (5) barriers and facilitators (one question with sub-questions); and (6) the nurse’s own PA behavior (two questions). At the end of the survey, respondents were invited to provide additional open-ended comments. Potential participants were informed the survey would take approximately 6–7 min to complete. See Additional file 1 for the survey.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the sample and address the primary objective (i.e. to understand oncology clinic nurses’ knowledge and current practices regarding PA promotion in patients, including OACA), and secondary objective (i.e. to explore any perceived and actual barriers to initiating PA discussion and promotion). Data on perceived knowledge and exercise promotion were dichotomized, with nurses who rated themselves as “Very knowledgeable” and “Quite knowledgeable” grouped as “yes”. Chi-square was used to explore the relationship between oncology nurses’ exercise behaviors and their perceived knowledge and exercise promotion behavior. Missing data was observed in some sections of the survey (detailed in each individual section below).All quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). The threshold for statistical significance was established at p < .05.

Results

A total of 97 nurses participated. Given the convenience, snowball sampling approach, an accurate response rate could not be calculated. The majority of respondents were located in Canada (39.6%, 38/97), followed by Belgium (33.3%, 32/97), and Ireland (19.8%, 19/97). Most were female (95.5%, 93/97) and worked an average 13.5 years (SD 10.7, range 0.5–45) in outpatient oncology practice. The most common patient care areas include breast (34%, 33/97), general (28.9%, 28/97), and lung (25.8%, 28/97) cancers (overlap exists). Notably, respondents occasionally chose to skip entire sections, resulting in a uniform pattern of missing data. To uphold transparency in presenting findings, the results reported are explicitly derived from the available data provided by respondents who completed the pertinent sections. No exclusion of data was made in our data analysis.

More than half (57.8%, 56/97) of respondents reported no formal training regarding exercise discussion or recommendations for patients with cancer; of whom, 91% (88/97) stated they would be interested in learning more. Preference for learning format included a webinar (74.2%, 72/97), online module (49.5%, 48/97), online workshop (40.2%, 39/97), in-service (42.3%, 41/97), in-person workshop (34%, 33/97), professional websites (30.9%, 30/97), and videos (28.9%, 28/97) (overlapping exists). The most preferred time willing to learning was one hour (37.1%, 36/97). See Table 1.

Knowledge and beliefs pertaining to patients in general

The majority of respondents agreed that exercise benefits both patients completed (90%; 87/97) and currently receiving treatment (90%, 87/97), and that exercise discussion should be a component of care they (as nurses) provide (88%, 85/97). The majority disagreed with statements related to misconceptions such as, “Patients should not exercise, including when they experience fatigue” (96%, 93/97), “Exercises will worsen pain” (95%, 92/97), and “Those with metastasis should not exercise” (91%, 88/97) (See Additional file 2). Of 79 respondents to the knowledge section, one-third (36%, 29/79) self-reported knowing how to discuss exercise guidelines with patients; 43% (34/79) stated they know how to encourage patients to exercise; 32.9% (26/79) know when to discuss exercise with patients, and 32.9% (26/79) know which patients to refer to exercise programs (See Fig. 1 - Self-reported knowledge).

Current practice pertaining to patients in general

Of 79 respondents who completed the current practice section, 75% (59/79) reported typically discussing or recommending exercise to patients with cancer. Topics likely to discuss include advice on keeping active during cancer treatment (93.7%, 74/79); keeping active after cancer treatment (92.4%, 73/79); and role of exercise in symptom management (72.2%, 57/79). Nurses felt that the best disciplines to discuss exercise with patients included physiotherapists (71%, 69/97), nurses (65%, 63/97), and physicians (56.7%, 55/97) (overlap exists due to multiple responses allowed) (Table 2).

Belief and practice pertaining to OACA

Of 77 nurses responding to questions about exercise related to OACA, the majority (94.8%, 73/77) disagreed with the statement “Exercise is unsafe for OACA”, and 96.1% (74/77) disagreed with the statement “OACA with metastasis should not exercise”. The majority agreed that regular exercise is beneficial to OACA undergoing cancer treatment (89.6%, 69/77) and who have completed treatment (93.5%, 72/77). Only 7.2% (7/77) reported they do not discuss exercise with OACA at all; and 5.2% (4/77) also stated they do not discuss exercise with any patients in general. (Table 2)

Barriers to discussing exercise with patients in general and in OACA

Barriers to exercise discussion with patients included limited time during clinic appointments (77.7%, 56/72); patients in general are usually told by healthcare professional/family to rest (59.7%, 43/72); don’t know how soon post-surgery/radiation is safe to exercise (47.2%, 34/72); and believe that there are situations where exercise is unsafe for some patients (76.3%, 55/72). There were some specific barriers related to discussion of exercise with OACA. These included uncertainty of how soon post-surgery/radiation is safe for OACA to exercise (48.6%, 35/72); belief that there are situations where exercise is unsafe for OACA (72.2%, 52/72); and not feeling prepared to discuss exercise with OACA (18%; 13/72). However, over half (61.1%, 44/72) disagreed with the statements “I don’t feel prepared to discuss exercise with OACA”, and “I perceive exercise lacks relevance to OACA” (76.4%, 55/72) (See Fig. 2– Barriers to exercise promotion to patients in general and to older adults with cancer, and Additional file 3). Education sessions (62.9%, 61/97) were the most endorsed method that would be helpful to facilitate discussion with OACA. Open-ended responses were scant (n = 9), but those recorded noted safety concerns for OACA including mobility issues/fall risks; fracture risks/bone density issues; and living alone with no support. Oncology nurses’ own PA level

In terms of nurses’ PA level, 67.6% (46/68) reported performing moderate-intensity aerobic activity at least 150 min/week. Additionally, 53% (36/68) reported both performing > = 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week and yes to typically discussing exercise with patients with cancer in general. No statistically significant association was detected (p = .62) between nurses’ own PA level and whether they typically discuss and/or recommend exercise to patients with cancer. Additionally, 18% (12/68) reported both performing > = 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week and being quite/very knowledgeable in discussing exercise guidelines with patients with cancer in general. No statistically significant association was found (p = .62) between nurses’ own PA level and whether they perceived themselves as being quite/very knowledgeable in discussing exercise guidelines with patients with cancer. Moreover, 26% (18/68) endorsed both being quite/very knowledgeable in discussing exercise guidelines with patients with cancer in general and 74.7% (51/68) reported typically discussing exercise with patients with cancer in general. There was a significant association (χ², p = .01) between whether nurses typically discuss exercise with patients in general and their perceived knowledge of how to discuss exercise guidelines with patients with cancer (Table 3).

Discussion

This study explored oncology nurses’ knowledge, perceptions, and perceived barriers related to discussing PA with patients with cancer, including OACA. Our findings suggest that nurses are open to discussing exercise with patients with cancer, including older adults, albeit low knowledge and barriers exist. While physiotherapists were identified as the leading professionals in this area, oncology nurses reported a high level of confidence in discussing PA. Nurses also express interest in enhancing their knowledge and participating in diverse educational opportunities. Oncology nurses believe exercise should be a component of care they provide as nurses, which is consistent with prior research [54, 55].

Additionally, nurses believe that exercise is beneficial to patients during and after cancer treatment, and the majority of respondents are making efforts to encourage patients to exercise, which is also consistent with another Australian study [54]. However, our findings also indicate that nurses may require support to discuss specific exercise guidelines or instructions with patients, including when to discuss, and know which patients to refer to exercise programs. An encouraging aspect is that the majority are interested in learning more and welcome educational opportunities.

We found approximately two-thirds reporting low knowledge as to when, how, and which patients to refer to exercise programs or professionals, and how to provide exercise counselling based on existing exercise guidelines. This self-reported knowledge appears to be lower compared to the findings in the nurse subgroup (n = 105) in a survey of oncology healthcare professionals [56] in terms of self-perceived knowledge, including self-reported poor knowledge on knowing how to counsel patients based on exercise guidelines, when to counsel patients on exercise, and how to encourage patients to participate in exercise. Of note, previous research also signalled limited knowledge regarding exercise discussion and prescription among oncology healthcare professionals [57, 58]. This suggests that when asked more closely about specifics related to exercise, gaps in knowledge exist. We did not assess whether they could correctly identify prevailing exercise guidelines. However, Hardcastle et al. [58] and Nadler et al. [50] found the majority of oncology healthcare professionals are unable to do so.

Less than half of our respondents reported having received some degree of formal training regarding exercise counselling, which is lower compared to a subgroup analysis of 55 oncology nurses [59], in which 71% reported receiving some form of postgraduate education on the use of exercise in the cancer population. The majority of our respondents do not subscribe to the rest model, as evidenced by their view regarding suitability for patients with cancer to exercise. This is improved from a survey performed in 2017 of oncology care professionals which found 59% of the nurses believed patients should rest when experiencing fatigue [50]. This suggests that over time there has been a shift from always advising “rest” to “keeping active” even if specific advice is not given. Future research could confirm if this is similar in different oncology settings and populations.

The proportion of nurses who self-reported promoting/discussing exercise with patients appear to be higher than the average reported (58.1%) in a systematic review based on studies that mostly included a mix of oncology care professionals (i.e. physicians, nurses, and other disciplines). The barriers perceived by nurses, such as lack of time [55], and risk to patient find support in previous research on oncology nurses about patients with cancer [54]. Although most nurses in this study believe exercise is beneficial for OACA, many have reservations about recommending exercise to this population. Our findings show the proportion of those who have concerns about safety regarding patients in general compared to older patients is similar; also, the number of nurses stating they don’t discuss or recommend exercise is similar regardless of whether it is about patients in general or OACA, thus suggesting that patient age (i.e. older age) may not necessarily influence nurses’ beliefs regarding the relevance of exercise promotion practice. While it is reassuring that age may not be the factor limiting discussion of exercise, given our small sample, further research is needed to investigate this, specifically, with larger sample size, and a more focused investigation of exercise promotion practice for OACA. Qualitative work could also further explore barriers of nurses in exercise promotion in this population.

In our study, nurses reported concerns about fall risk amongst OACA. While evidence suggests that PA, including exercise programs for OACA are safe and acceptable among OACA [16, 60, 61], this population is indeed at higher risk for falls and bone fractures [62–64] compared to their younger counterparts. The risk is higher for older adults who are frail [65]. A recent prospective multicenter study of 5759 older patients found over one-third of older patients experienced a fall over the past 12 months, with an injury rate of nearly 67% [65]. Given the implications for falls in this population [66], routine fall risk screening and exercise as part of the multifactorial fall intervention have been recommended in the oncology setting for older adults [67]. Education to nurses on how to counsel OACA regarding exercise in ways that mitigate the risk of falls may be warranted [68].

Over half of the respondents met the guideline for at least 150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous intensity aerobic activity, similarly to other studies [56] on oncology healthcare professionals, and may in part explain the finding that respondents are generally active in promoting PA with patients. There is likely an element of responder bias as those who are physically active may be more likely to complete a survey about exercise. While evidence [56, 69] suggests that oncology healthcare professionals who are physically active are more likely to discuss exercise with patients with cancer; no such relationship was found in our study. It is unclear if this was due to the small sample size, or whether it was due to not knowing the how and when regarding exercise promotion, or other barriers such as lack of time and feeling unfamiliar with referral resources. The statistically significant finding in our study on the association between self-perceived knowledge and exercise promotion practice appears to support the latter point. More in-depth research is warranted to further examine these relationships.

Findings from our survey show that nurses broadly recommend patients to “stay active” but do not provide specific guidelines or recommendations. This is also observed among other oncology healthcare professionals [44, 56, 70]. The literature suggests that although many oncology nurses inquire about patients’ physical activity, they may be less able to provide specific recommendations [55]. Up-skilling and improving nurses’ understanding of PA and exercise as medicine would be highly beneficial [71], since it is not their primary area of training and generally receive little continuing education opportunities regarding exercise counselling. As a result, nurses may face challenges even though they may wish to promote PA to patients [54]. As the survey did not include a question testing nurses’ actual knowledge on current guidelines, their knowledge on current guidelines is unknown. Additionally, our findings suggest that barriers go beyond a lack of knowledge, but also include organizational and other barriers such as lack of time or referral resources that hamper their ability to promote exercise. To our knowledge, although there are ample resources and tips on exercise available for patients and survivors and cancer [34], few are specifically for the older population with cancer. Moreover, simply making recommendations is not sufficient. To capitalize on the opportunity during clinical interaction [72], it is important to incorporate an outline or resources for exercise (or a referral to other healthcare professionals who can) when making exercise recommendations in addition to an outlet or resource to promote participation in exercise and patient outcomes [73, 74]. Collaboration and communication among healthcare professionals can provide a pathway to enhance access to services and resources for patients [56, 72].

Limitations and strengths

The small sample size, missing data, and limited representation across practice areas (e.g., overrepresentation of medical oncology) and regions limit the generalizability of findings and our ability to explore factors associated with exercise-related knowledge and referral practices in greater depth. A formal sample size calculation was not feasible due to the lack of a centralized sampling frame and the indirect nature of recruitment. We could not estimate how many individuals were reached, as we had no access to distribution lists or membership counts. The use of a convenience-based, snowball sampling approach also limits representativeness and introduces the likelihood of responder bias - those more engaged or interested in exercise may have been more inclined to participate. We did not collect information on public versus private practice or hospital type, nor did we ask about awareness of national or regional physical activity guidelines, which may have added useful context. The survey instrument was not independently validated, and knowledge was assessed via self-report rather than objective testing (e.g., we did not evaluate understanding of recommendations from ACSM or CSEP). It is likely that, on average, oncology nurses are less knowledgeable than those who participated. The small sample size also limited our ability to stratify by clinical area and conduct regression analyses with adequate power. Finally, the low number of free-text responses prevented meaningful qualitative analysis. However, this study has it strengths. A unique aspect of this study is specific assessment of exercise promotion in OACA, which is known to be a growing yet often an under-researched population. Although previous research has found risk to patients and safety to be among barriers/concerns, it was not explicitly explored or reported under the survey method used by previous studies. Additionally, we used a validated questionnaire that is previously used in oncology settings. Respondents geographically spanned multiple countries. We also asked respondents to elaborate in free-text comments regarding concerns about discussing exercise with OACA in order to gain additional insights.

Practice implication

Institutions should provide more resources to support exercise counselling and professional development opportunities, especially for nurses via webinars, learning modules, in-services, and leverage existing resources to supplement exercise counselling [75]. Educational opportunities may help oncology nurses become familiarized with current exercise guidelines as well as how, when, and which patients to refer to an exercise specialist or exercise programs. Having clear referral pathways to exercise specialists and physiotherapists may also facilitate referral of patients [76]. In addition to these strategies, systems-level supports such as guideline adherence monitoring and clinical alert systems may help embed physical activity promotion into routine care. These tools can prompt timely provider action, identify missed opportunities, and support more consistent and scalable implementation across oncology settings.

Conclusion

Nurses show interest in discussing exercise in patients with cancer, including OACA; although they commonly counsel to “stay active” they report low knowledge on more specific exercise counseling. Encouragingly, nurses express interest in enhancing their knowledge and are willing to participate in diverse educational opportunities to effectively promote exercise. This suggests that additional training and education, specifically geared toward counseling OACA with falls assessment and specific recommendations to decrease risk of falls, could improve nurses’ exercise counseling skills for all patients with cancer.
