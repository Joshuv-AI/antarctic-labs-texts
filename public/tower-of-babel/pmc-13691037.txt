Breastfeeding as a protective factor of maternal depression: a multi-country analysis from Africa and South Asia
Authors: Amna Khalid, Rizwan Qaisar, Azhar Hussain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association between breastfeeding (BF) and maternal mental health remains underexplored in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). This study examined the relationship between BF and depressive symptoms among mothers in selected LMICs across Africa and South Asia.MethodsWe conducted a secondary analysis of nationally representative Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data from Lesotho (n = 793; 2023–2024), Mozambique (n = 5092; 2022–2023), Bangladesh (n = 4971; 2022), and Nepal (n = 1401; 2022). The pooled sample comprised 12,257 mothers aged 15–49 years with complete data on BF status and depressive symptoms. Depression was assessed using DHS items adapted from the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ), applying a dichotomous cut-off score of ≥ 10. BF was categorized as ever versus never. Country-specific and pooled logistic regression models were estimated, adjusting for maternal age, education, and household wealth.ResultsBreastfeeding prevalence was high across countries (Lesotho: 92.3%; Mozambique: 96.3%; Bangladesh: 95.7%; Nepal: 98.9%), while the prevalence of depressive symptoms ranged from 4.0 to 7.6%. BF was associated with lower depressive symptoms in unadjusted analyses but reached significance only in Bangladesh (β = -0.861, p < .001) and the pooled sample (β = −0.449, p < .01) in the adjusted model. Overall, mothers who never breastfed showed a consistently higher risk of depression than those who had breastfed.ConclusionThis multi-country analysis provides evidence of an inverse association between BF and maternal depressive symptoms in LMICs, with significant effects observed primarily in Bangladesh. Integrating maternal mental health support into BF promotion programs may be beneficial. Longitudinal studies with more detailed BF measures are needed to clarify causal pathways.Keywords: Breastfeeding, Maternal depression, Postpartum mental health, Demographic and health surveys, Low- and middle-income countries

Introduction

Breastfeeding (BF) is one of the most effective ways to promote the well-being of both mother and child [5]. However, contrary to the World Health Organization’s recommendations, fewer than half of infants under 6 months old are exclusively breastfed [25]. Despite the well-established benefits of BF for both maternal and child health and nutrition, inadequate BF is responsible for 16% of child deaths each year [24]. Although global rates of BF have increased modestly, with South Asia the primary contributor to this improvement [23], the overall prevalence of exclusive BF in Africa remains low [3, 15]. In these regions, food insecurity and malnutrition are high. Mothers may struggle to meet their own nutritional needs, which can hinder effective BF and limit the nutritional benefits for their infants [1].

BF not only supplies essential nutrients to the baby but also nurtures a profound emotional and psychological connection between the mother and her infant [17]. While the physical merits of BF in mothers [5] are firmly established, there has been a growing focus in recent years on its psychological dimensions. In terms of maternal mental health, BF is linked to: release of oxytocin which enhances mother–child emotional bond and aids in regulating stress and mood [16, 20],improved maternal self-esteem and confidence [6],enhanced maternal sense of accomplishment and power [5], and a lower risk of postpartum depression [8, 10, 18, 21]. A recent systematic review reported significant relationships between BF and maternal mental health outcomes, namely symptoms of postpartum depression and anxiety [27].

Maternal well-being is crucial for both the mother and the baby’s healthy development. Globally, approximately 10% of pregnant women and 13% of postpartum women experience a mental disorder, most commonly depression. The prevalence is even higher in developing countries, affecting 15.6% of women during pregnancy and 19.8% after childbirth. In severe cases, maternal suffering can be profound, sometimes leading to suicide. Beyond the direct impact on mothers, these disorders can impair maternal functioning and adversely affect the growth and development of their children [26].

Multiple factors can impact the rates of depression among BF mothers. A clearer understanding of this variability is essential for informing policies and interventions aimed at achieving the World Health Assembly’s global EBF target for 2025 [2, 24]. Furthermore, a significant evidence gap remains on the mental health outcomes of BF. Despite the high disease burden, few studies have analyzed these associations using nationally representative, population-based data. Given that interventions in these high-risk populations could have the most significant impact, further investigation is warranted. Moreover, cross-country comparisons enable a better understanding of differences in health status and contextual factors across nations, thereby supporting the development of locally appropriate, context-specific feeding programs and policies.

Several recent studies have examined associations between infant and young child feeding practices and maternal health outcomes using Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data [11, 14]. While these studies provide important insights, many are limited to single-country settings, which restricts generalizability and cross-country comparisons.

The present study builds on this evidence by using the most recent DHS datasets from four low- and middle-income countries: Lesotho (2023–24), Mozambique (2022–23), Bangladesh (2022), and Nepal (2022). We aim to estimate the degree and direction of association between breastfeeding and maternal depressive symptoms across diverse settings, while also examining key sociodemographic correlates of these outcomes.

Understanding these associations across multiple countries is important for informing maternal and child health policies, particularly in settings where both mental health burden and breastfeeding practices remain suboptimal. The findings may help guide context-specific interventions that simultaneously support breastfeeding practices and maternal psychological well-being.

Material and methods

This study analyzed cross-sectional secondary data from the most recent Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) for two African countries, Lesotho (2023–24) and Mozambique (2022–23), and two Asian countries, Bangladesh (2022) and Nepal (2022). The DHS Program routinely collects nationally representative data on maternal and child health, nutrition, fertility, family planning, HIV/AIDS, and immunization. All DHS surveys employ standardized instruments and procedures, ensuring comparability across countries and survey years. Each survey used a stratified two-stage random sampling design. In the first stage, each country was divided into subnational regions based on geographical or administrative boundaries, which were further stratified into urban and rural areas. Within these strata, enumeration areas (EAs) were selected using probability proportional to population size, based on the most recent national census. In the second stage, a household listing was conducted in each selected EA, and approximately 25 households were selected by systematic random sampling for interviews. Detailed descriptions of survey tools, sampling procedures, and household listing methods were provided in the respective country-level DHS reports [12].

The depression outcome was derived from the nine-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) embedded within the DHS mental health module (variable mth22). Each item assessed the frequency of depressive symptoms over the past two weeks on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), yielding a total score between 0 and 27. The PHQ-9 is a widely validated screening tool for depressive symptoms in population-based studies. The binary outcome was constructed using the standard PHQ-9 scoring cut-off, where scores of 0–9 indicated no or minimal depressive symptoms and scores of 10–27 indicated the presence of clinically relevant depressive symptoms, consistent with established PHQ-9 classification guidelines [13].

The exposure variable related to BF was created from the DHS variable m4_1 labelled “duration of BF” with the outcomes: “ever BF, not currently BF”; “never BF”; “still BF”. The dichotomous BF variable was coded as 0 when the respondent had never BF (“never BF”) and it was coded as 1 when the respondent presently or previously had BF (“still BF” or “ever BF, not currently BF”).

DHS variables v012 for age, v133 for education, and v190 for wealth were used as control variables. The initial sample size comprised 14,385 respondents, but 2128 had missing values for the anxiety variable, and thus 12,257 individuals were included in the analysis. The statistical model is:

Results

Table 1 presents the distribution of depression and BF status across four countries—Lesotho, Mozambique, Bangladesh, and Nepal—along with the combined totals for the entire sample of 12,257 respondents. Overall, 94.3% of participants reported not being depressed, while 5.7% indicated depression. BF prevalence was notably high, with 96.1% of the total sample BF and only 3.9% not BF. Country-level patterns reveal some variation. In Lesotho, among 793 respondents, 93.7% were not depressed, and 6.3% were depressed. BF was reported by 92.3% of respondents in Lesotho, leaving 7.7% who were not BF—the highest proportion of non-BF individuals among the four countries. Mozambique, with a large subsample of 5092 respondents, shows 92.4% without depression and 7.6% with depression. BF is highly prevalent here, at 96.3%, with only 3.7% not BF. Bangladesh, which accounts for 4971 respondents, has the lowest depression rate among the countries: 96.0% reported no depression, and just 4.0% reported depression. BF is similarly widespread, with 95.7% BF and 4.3% not BF. Nepal, with 1401 respondents, shows 94.9% not depressed and 5.1% depressed. Interestingly, Nepal has the highest BF rate at 98.9%, and only 1.1% of respondents were not BF.

Discussion

This multi-country analysis of nationally representative DHS data from Lesotho, Mozambique, Bangladesh, and Nepal offers new insights into the association between BF and maternal depression in diverse African and South Asian settings. Across all countries, BF was associated with a lower likelihood of depressive symptoms, although the magnitude and statistical significance varied. The association was most robust in Bangladesh and in the pooled sample, even after adjusting for maternal age, education, and wealth. These findings align with extensive evidence suggesting that BF may confer psychological benefits for mothers and reduce postpartum depressive symptoms [8, 10, 27].

The inverse association between BF and depression observed in this study is consistent with physiological and psychological mechanisms described in earlier research. BF promotes the release of oxytocin and prolactin, hormones that facilitate relaxation, reduce stress reactivity, and strengthen maternal–infant bonding [20]. Psychologically, BF may enhance maternal self-efficacy and emotional connectedness, both of which are protective against depressive symptoms [5, 6]. However, these patterns must be interpreted cautiously. Although the direction of association was consistent, significant findings emerged only in Bangladesh and the pooled sample. The lack of significance in Lesotho, Mozambique, and Nepal does not necessarily indicate a lack of association, instead, it may reflect methodological constraints, such as the small number of non-BF mothers, particularly in Nepal, which limits statistical power and leads to wide confidence intervals. This challenge has been documented in LMIC studies, reporting very high BF prevalence [1, 7]. Additionally, several key contributors to maternal mental health, such as prior depression, social support, intimate partner violence, BF challenges, obstetric complications, and infant illness, were not available in the DHS datasets. These unmeasured variables may influence both BF behavior and maternal mood, potentially leading to residual confounding. While our analysis adjusted for age, education, and wealth, the potential influence of these omitted factors should be acknowledged when interpreting the results.

A strength of this study is its cross-country comparison, which highlights variation between African and South Asian contexts. The strongest association between BF and lower depressive symptoms was observed in Bangladesh, consistent with regional findings showing that optimal BF practices and BF support programs contribute to maternal well-being [18]. South Asian health systems have invested in structured BF interventions—including Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiatives and community health worker counseling—which may reinforce BF initiation and continuation while simultaneously offering psychosocial support [9].

By contrast, the weaker associations in Lesotho and Mozambique may reflect broader structural challenges common in many African contexts. High maternal workloads, food insecurity, infectious disease burden, and limited access to postpartum mental health care have been identified as major stressors that contribute to maternal depressive symptoms [15, 19, 22]. These systemic pressures may overshadow the subtler psychological benefits of BF.

Differences in BF exclusivity and initiation rates may also play a role. Exclusive and early BF—shown to produce the most substantial physiological and psychological benefits [8]—is more common in South Asia compared to many African countries [3]. However, because this data was not available, this nuance could not be examined in the present study.

Implications for practice and/or policy

The findings have important implications for maternal and child health policy. BF promotion platforms—which are widely implemented in LMICs—may serve as effective channels for integrating maternal mental health support. High-quality BF counseling has been shown not only to improve feeding practices but also to reduce maternal distress [18]. Leveraging these existing entry points could therefore yield dual benefits.

Policy efforts should also reflect contextual variation. In settings where BF is nearly universal, additional focus may be needed to support women experiencing BF challenges or depressive symptoms, who may be less visible within routine services. In African contexts characterized by socioeconomic strain, interventions may need to incorporate broader structural supports—such as reducing maternal workload, improving nutrition, and expanding access to postpartum mental health services to maximize the benefits of BF.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. BF may reduce depression, depression may reduce BF, or both may influence each other simultaneously [16, 27]. Longitudinal research is needed to disentangle these pathways.

Second, BF was measured using a simple “ever vs. never” classification, which does not capture exclusivity, duration, early initiation, or BF challenges—factors known to influence maternal mental health meaningfully. Significant variability in BF behavior may therefore be obscured.

Third, DHS interviews women at varying postpartum intervals, which may influence depression symptom expression. Without postpartum timing information, differences related to time since childbirth cannot be examined.

Future studies should employ longitudinal designs to clarify temporal pathways between BF and maternal depression. Inclusion of detailed BF indicators, such as exclusivity, duration, initiation timing, nighttime feeding, and BF challenges, would offer greater analytic precision. Other confounding variables (e.g., parity, employment status, partner characteristics) may influence the observed associations and may be studied in future studies. Mixed-methods studies could further illuminate how systemic, family, and health system factors shape BF and maternal mental health. Intervention trials that integrate BF counselling with low-intensity psychological support delivered by community health workers may provide scalable strategies in LMIC settings.

Conclusion

This multi-country study provides evidence on the relationship between breastfeeding and maternal depressive symptoms across four low- and middle-income countries using nationally representative DHS data. Overall, the findings indicate a generally inverse association between breastfeeding and depression, although the strength and statistical significance of this relationship varied across countries, with more consistent effects observed in Bangladesh and in the pooled analysis.

These results highlight the importance of considering breastfeeding within a broader maternal mental health framework, particularly in settings where women face multiple social and structural stressors. The findings underscore the need for integrated public health approaches that jointly promote optimal infant feeding practices and maternal psychological well-being, while also accounting for contextual differences across countries. Further research is warranted to explore underlying mechanisms and to inform tailored, context-specific interventions in diverse healthcare settings.
