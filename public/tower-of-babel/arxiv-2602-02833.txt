Endogenous Product Design: A Linear Demand Approach
Afonso Rodrigues

Abstract




Abstract

This paper develops a linear-demand framework to investigate endogenous product design. The key assumption is that the same product characteristics which drive goods utility also (at least partially) shape competitive interactions across products. I model this relationship allowing for differences in each characteristic’s relevance to competition, their absolute intensity per good, and correlations to other characteristics. The framework is novel in its broad applicability to settings with any finite number of goods, firms, and attributes, allowing for both vertical and horizontal differentiation, all in an empirically testable model. Under Bertrand price competition I show that across different market structures, a pattern emerges: product differentiation along product attributes that firms control is primarily vertical, with horizontal differentiation only in latent attributes. Counter to standard intuition, simulations show that allowing for endogenous design can imply higher consumer surplus under monopoly than under competition, as monopoly’s stronger incentives for attribute investment translate into higher effective quality.

1
Introduction

Consider the following setup. A firm wishes to maximise profits by strategically setting the price(s) and attributes of its good(s). Consumers derive utility from said good(s) based on those attributes; the attribute choices of the firm’s competitors also shape demand elasticities and the intensity of price competition. We want to know how said firm may optimise their good(s)’ attributes and what product differentiation and market structure are likely to arise as a result. How can we determine this?

This paper develops a novel approach to modelling endogenous product design. I provide a novel micro-foundation for the structure of the price effects within a market, based on the product attribute choices of individual firms and the preferences of consumers. Such micro-foundation attempts to determine what is the best approximation to a latent price effects matrix if we assume it is partially a function of observable product characteristics. The main assumption is that the same attributes which drive goods utility also (at least partially) drive competition in the goods market.

Quasilinear quadratic utility is a local second-order approximation to any well-behaved utility function, with well-behavedness depending on strict concavity, i.e. a negative definite Hessian. My proposed approach leverages this approximation and the properties of definite matrices to endogenise product design without affecting the non-singularity of the implied demand Jacobian. In guaranteeing positive definiteness in the face of changing attributes, the model always produces well-behaved individual demand functions, which can be used in profit-maximisation exercises yielding a unique price equilibrium and closed-form solutions.

The model’s novelty lies also in its application to any fixed number of firms and attributes. Market structure is purely endogenous; I assume no transportation costs, and no line (Hotelling, 1929), circle (Salop, 1979), or spoke (Chen and Riordan, 2007); no matching (Perloff and Salop, 1985); no consumer information heterogeneity or captivity (Varian, 1980; Butters, 1977); and no capacity constraints (Levitan and Shubik, 1972).

In a multi-product monopoly setting for a single attribute, I find that monopoly profits depend only on the length/norm of the attribute vector, its intensity, not how it’s distributed across goods. With the same norm, a monopolist is indifferent between one all-encompassing product, a vertically differentiated ladder of goods, or many homogeneous products. If instead the monopoly determines more than one attribute, the paper shows an orientation incentive: monopoly profits rise when the different attribute vectors become more aligned. In the optimum, the attribute vectors are parallel. There is no incentive towards horizontal differentiation except in latent attributes not controlled by the monopolist. The intensities of each attribute are shown to nonetheless depend on a ratio, of the marginal utility each attribute yields to consumers over the contribution of said attribute to product competition. Greater attribute marginal costs and price sensitivity lower the intensity itself.

In a multiple single-product firms setting, I consider two possible equilibria: attribute exclusivity, where each attribute belongs to exactly one firm and only one firm gets to load on said attribute; and attribute non-exclusivity where firms all load on the same attributes identically (i.e. a symmetric equilibrium). In all cases, I show that agents load on their attributes exactly as derived by the ratio of marginal utility to marginal contribution to competition. However, while under attribute exclusivity the intensities are identical to those imprinted by the multi-product monopoly setting for multiple attributes, under non-exclusivity the optimal overall intensity must be solved numerically - though we prove said solution is unique under the same conditions which make positive-intensity equilibria possible in the other settings.

Attribute intensity is shown to be always higher under monopoly than under single-product competition. However, most surprisingly, I show through a simple simulation of model primitives that, with endogenous product design, the monopoly regime generates higher consumer surplus than the competitive regime. This is because monopoly’s greater investment in attributes is effectively translated into a greater investment into quality.

2
Literature review

There is a relatively long tradition in microeconomic theory of approximating product competition via some measure of closeness in a product space. Early models endogenise horizontal differentiation via location choice, be it over a line, a circle, or most recently, a spoke (Hotelling, 1929; D’Aspremont, Gabszewicz, and Thisse, 1979; Salop, 1979; Chen and Riordan, 2007). In subsequent models of vertical competition (e.g. Spence, 1975; Shaked & Sutton, 1982), quality choice is the measure instead; a utility function uni-dimensional parameter that raises consumers’ willingness to pay and (usually) marginal or fixed costs, and along which a firm locates optimally.

Fewer papers have explored both horizontal and vertical differentiation simultaneously. Economides (1993) considers a model with one horizontal characteristic and one vertical characteristic with firms distributed over a Salop (1979) circle. Gabszewicz and Wauthy (2012) introduce vertical differentiation onto a Hotelling (1929) line by shifting consumer density across the line. The model in the present paper attempts to generalise these efforts, allowing for multi-dimensional attribute choice on both the extensive and intensive side, for any number of firms and goods.

Defining product utility as a function of its characteristics is not a recent phenomenon either. Lancaster (1966) pioneered this approach, whereby a product is defined as a vector whose dimensions reflect different abstract characteristics, and consumers have preferences defined over said characteristics, purchasing products given their respective attribute "basket". More recently, characteristics-based models have begun to incorporate measures of competitive pressure defined as a function of the similarity between product pairs in a product characteristics space. In industrial organisation research, the most common such measure is cosine similarity.

The first known usage of cosine similarity in Economics appears to be in Allen (1957). However, its introduction to Industrial Organisation is far more recent. Hoberg and Phillips (2016) take advantage of text-based measures of product similarity to identify competitors. They target a subset of firms/goods, define them over a set of descriptives identified via textual analysis of 10-K fillings by US public firms, and compute the cosine similarity between these sets. They assume this cosine similarity approximates the cosine similarity between the goods’ real attributes; this approach is thereby said to situate these goods in a multi-dimensional product space. In this space, the cosine similarity defines the angle between two goods, with said angle assumed to negatively correlate with degree of competitive pressure. More recently, Pellegrino (2024) applies this measure to markup estimation in a quasi-linear quadratic utility model, and Voelkening (2025) exploits the same model for studying attribute choice in a single-product duopoly setting.

Cosine similarity however faces issues which limit its applicability in a more generalised model. As a measure of competitive pressure, it lacks a micro-foundation, ascribes equal weights to all characteristics regardless of their importance to consumers, only accounts for observed characteristics (exogenously chosen by the researcher), and does not account for the overlapping information these may contain for defining a given product. Mathematically, it also yields a poorly behaved demand Jacobian in an equilibrium where product attributes are endogenously chosen (Voelkening, 2025). To address these issues, the present paper introduces a general measure of product competition based on product characteristics, which nests cosine similarity, and provides the necessary mathematical tractability to allow for multi-dimensional attribute choice across any number of firms and goods.

Model preliminaries

Quasi-linear quadratic utility is a local second-order approximation to any well-behaved utility function (more on this in Rodrigues, 2025). In quasi-linear quadratic utility models (QQUM), a representative consumer’s utility maximisation problem is given by:

\max_{\boldsymbol{q}}\ U(\boldsymbol{q})=\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\delta}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}\Sigma\boldsymbol{q}+q_{0}\ \ \ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ \ \ \boldsymbol{q}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{p}+q_{0}\leq Income

N\times N

The properties of the QQUM are well-understood.

One of the most appealing features of QQUM is its ability to generate linear demand systems that remain tractable even when a market consists of a very large number of differentiated goods. Quasi-linearity allows the derived demands to depend solely on the vector of prices (without assumptions about market size or income effects) and the interactions among goods, as captured by an interaction matrix. In effect, the QQUM yields linear demand functions of the following form:

\boldsymbol{q}=\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\delta}+\phi\boldsymbol{p})

\frac{\partial log(q_{a})}{\partial log(p_{b})}=\phi\{\Sigma^{-1}\}_{ab},\ \forall a,b\in\mathcal{K}

(1)

The model imposes no artificial category boundaries between goods. This is in contrast to alternative specifications where the aggregation over many goods may require additional restrictions which may lack microeconomic justification. The

Throughout the rest of the paper, we rely additionally on the optimal price, equilibrium quantity, and equilibrium level of profits in two instances: where firms set prices individually, and where a single multi-product monopolist does so. Attribute-based measure of product competition

In what follows, I propose a general attribute-based measure of product competition.

First, we need to distinguish

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{N\times 1}=X_{N\times K}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{K\times 1}+\boldsymbol{v}_{K\times 1}

If two product characteristics are highly correlated, say, e.g., size and weight, it may not be adequate to consider them the relevant units of optimisation for a profit-maximising firm or utility-maximising consumer. Instead, consumers effectively compare the set of goods on those two characteristics simultaneously, and so do the firms. In other words, the true variable of interest will be an underlying

In this sense, the matrix

Optimal product design

Let us briefly introduce a mathematical property which will drive many of the results of this model. In matrix algebra, the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury formula formalises the following equality:

\Sigma^{-1}=(I_{N}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\boldsymbol{s}_{k}\boldsymbol{s}_{k}^{\prime})^{-1}=(I+S\Gamma S^{\prime})^{-1}=I-S(\Gamma^{-1}+S^{\prime}S)^{-1}S^{\prime}

5.2.3
On the existence of multiple equilibria

Beyond the symmetry scenario above, we cannot express alternative equilibria easily, as no general closed-form solution for these exists. To overcome this limitation, firm best-response functions are simulated and optimised numerically. I adjust for corner solutions (as
\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\geq 0
) by repeatedly computing implied quantities from the linear demand system and then updating each firm’s price using its single-firm best response, while enforcing a shutdown rule whenever the implied intercept for its demand becomes non-positive. Firms that end up with zero sales are removed from the market and the pricing equilibrium is recomputed on the remaining active firms; this is repeated until the set of active firms no longer changes. Equilibrium is approximated: holding rivals’ designs fixed, each firm locally improves its own design by taking a small number of finite-difference gradient-ascent steps on its objective. The algorithm iterates over firms and repeats until designs stop changing.

Below, a graph illustrates these results in a setting with 6 firms determining their optimal attribute orientations across a selection of 4 different simulated initial conditions. Heatmap colours refer to attribute vector intensity; alignment is measured via cosine between attribute vector orientation and
\Gamma^{-3/2}\boldsymbol{b}
. I find that, despite the existence of multiple local equilibria, the equilibria found via this approach all coalesce around a symmetric attribute orientation, with the attribute profile matching the orientation rule found above. This strongly suggests the equilibrium defined above is unique in orientation (if not in intensity or which firms end up exiting the market).

Given the attributes of the goods under monopoly vs single-product firms are virtually identical, one may wish to learn which setting yields greater

Raising attribute intensity raises product quality but increases competitive pressure on other goods along that attribute. A monopoly is able to internalise that business stealing effect while benefiting more from market expansion. Greater competition in the single-product firms setting limits the ability of said firms to fully capture the extra value created (as defined by lower markups).

Extension: estimating latent
U

Throughout the paper, we have worked with
S=ZU
. We have claimed that both are observable despite the fact
U
is in general not. In this section, I break down how we may minimally parameterise
U
such that it can be estimated via econometric methods.

6

One such econometric method for continuous demand systems is treated in depth in Rodrigues (2025).
Note that for any orthogonal
U\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
,

SS^{\prime}=(ZU)(ZU)^{\prime}

When we wish to express that some directions in
\mathrm{span}(X)
are more important for competition, we may rotate
Z
within the span by a matrix
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and apply direction weights.

In this section, I propose the following specification for
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
:

U(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\prod_{0\leq i<j\leq K}G(i,j,\theta_{ij})

for
G(i,j,\theta_{ij})
a Givens rotation (Golub and van Loan, 1983, p.240):

G(i,j,\theta_{ij})=\begin{bmatrix}1&\cdots&0&\cdots&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\cos(\theta_{ij})&\cdots&\sin(\theta_{ij})&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&-\sin(\theta_{ij})&\cdots&\cos(\theta_{ij})&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&\cdots&0&\cdots&1\\
\end{bmatrix}

with non-
\{0,1\}
elements where the rows
i
and
j
(self-)intersect. A Givens rotation turns two coordinates and leaves all the others unchanged. For example, for a 2-by-2
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, if
\boldsymbol{y}=U(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{x}
, the
\boldsymbol{y}
is obtained by rotating
\boldsymbol{x}
counter clockwise through an angle
\theta_{ij}
.

We can confirm
G(i,j,\theta_{ij})
is orthogonal:
G^{\prime}G=I
;
\det(G)=1
; and
G(i,j,\theta_{ij})^{-1}=G(i,j,-\theta_{ij})
. Furthermore, Givens rotations’ flexibility allows us to, by chaining a small number of such two–coordinate rotations, get a rotation along multiple coordinates at once. Each factor uses a chosen coordinate pair
(i,j)
and one angle
\theta_{ij}
.

If
Z=[\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{z}_{K}]
has orthonormal columns (a basis for the span of
X
), we can turn those directions by
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
:

S=ZU(\boldsymbol{\theta})=[\boldsymbol{s}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{s}_{K}]

and then proceed as we have so far. Because
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is orthogonal, the
\boldsymbol{s}_{k}
remain orthonormal.

There are a few caveats. Firstly, note that the order of the multiplication in
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
matters:
G(i,k,\theta_{ik})G(i,j,\theta_{ij})\neq G(i,j,\theta_{ij})G(i,k,\theta_{ik}),\ \forall i,j,k
, so they do not commute. For empirical estimation of our model, however, this does not matter. The
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-parameters are meant only to provide the necessary fit for how our product characteristics
X
enter the inverse of the demand system’s interaction effects matrix through
f(\cdot)
. Secondly, the total number of parameters to estimate can be relatively large. For a given matrix
X
of product characteristics, by QR decomposition we get
X=ZR
. If we restrict
Z
to only the first few components, as in PCA, then for
K
the number of components there are up to
\frac{K(K-1)}{2}

\boldsymbol{\theta}
-parameters to estimate. Nonetheless, this still compares favourably to e.g. the random coefficients (mixed) logit, even without accounting for the added simulation requirements of that model. It depends on the number of attributes, not of goods.

With
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})
defined via Givens rotations, our regression equation is well-behaved in the
\boldsymbol{\theta}
parameters. Let:

M(\boldsymbol{\theta})=I+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\boldsymbol{s}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{s}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\prime}

Then:

M^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=I-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\boldsymbol{s}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{s}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\prime}

via orthonormality in the columns of S and the Sherman-Woodbury formula, with
\alpha_{k}=\frac{\gamma_{k}}{1+\gamma_{k}}\in[0,1)
and
\gamma_{p}\neq\gamma_{q},\ \forall p,q
. I will prove well-behavedness in
M^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
without loss of generality, allowing researchers to choose whichever alternative works best for their empirical specification.

Assume, via a Givens rotation in their 2D span, that only the pair
(\boldsymbol{s}_{p}(\theta),\boldsymbol{s}_{q}(\theta))
depends on
\theta
:

[\boldsymbol{s}_{p}(\theta)\ \boldsymbol{s}_{q}(\theta)]=[\boldsymbol{s}_{p}\ \boldsymbol{s}_{q}]R(\theta)\qquad R(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}.

All other
\boldsymbol{s}_{k}
are fixed.

Write the components of any vector
\boldsymbol{w}
in this orthonormal frame as
w=\sum_{k}w_{k}\boldsymbol{s}_{k}+\boldsymbol{w}_{\perp}
with
w_{k}=\boldsymbol{s}_{k}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{w}
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{\perp}\perp\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{s}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{s}_{K}\}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be given. Decompose

\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{v}_{\perp}+\sum_{k}v_{k}\boldsymbol{s}_{k}
,
\boldsymbol{h}=\boldsymbol{h}_{\perp}+\sum_{k}h_{k}\boldsymbol{s}_{k}
.
Define
\beta_{k}=1-\alpha_{k}=\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{k}}>0
,

\beta_{s}=\frac{\beta_{p}+\beta_{q}}{2}
,
\beta_{d}=\frac{\beta_{p}-\beta_{q}}{2}
,
and the 2D vectors
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{p},v_{q})^{\prime}
,
\boldsymbol{h}=(h_{p},h_{q})^{\prime}
.

Note that, necessarily:

\boldsymbol{h}_{\perp}=\boldsymbol{v}_{\perp}\qquad h_{j}=\beta_{j}v_{j}\text{ for all }j\notin\{p,q\}

These are required because by definition
M(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{-1}
acts as the identity on
\boldsymbol{w}_{\perp}
and as scaling by
\beta_{j}=1-\alpha_{j}
along
y_{j}
for
j\notin\{p,q\}
.

With
Y(\theta)=[\boldsymbol{s}_{p}(\theta)\ \boldsymbol{s}_{q}(\theta)]=[\boldsymbol{s}_{p}\ \boldsymbol{s}_{q}]R(\theta)
and

\mathrm{diag}(\beta_{p},\beta_{q})=I-\mathrm{diag}(\alpha_{p},\alpha_{q})
:

M(\theta)^{-1}\big|_{pq}=I_{2}-R(\theta)\mathrm{diag}(\alpha_{p},\alpha_{q})R(\theta)^{\prime}=R(\theta)\mathrm{diag}(\beta_{p},\beta_{q})R(\theta)^{\prime}.

Expanding
R(\theta)\mathrm{diag}(\beta_{p},\beta_{q})R(\theta)^{\prime}

\cos 2\theta=\cos^{2}\theta-\sin^{2}\theta
and
\sin 2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta
, we have:

\displaystyle R(\theta)\mathrm{diag}(\beta_{p},\beta_{q})R(\theta)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{p}\cos^{2}\theta+\beta_{q}\sin^{2}\theta&(\beta_{p}-\beta_{q})\cos\theta\sin\theta\\
(\beta_{p}-\beta_{q})\cos\theta\sin\theta&\beta_{p}\sin^{2}\theta+\beta_{q}\cos^{2}\theta\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\beta_{s}I+\beta_{d}\begin{bmatrix}\cos 2\theta&\sin 2\theta\\
\sin 2\theta&-\cos 2\theta\end{bmatrix}

such that
\boldsymbol{h}_{2}=M(\theta)^{-1}\big|_{pq}v_{2}

\boldsymbol{h}=\beta_{s}\boldsymbol{v}+\beta_{d}\begin{bmatrix}\cos(2\theta)&\sin(2\theta)\\
\sin(2\theta)&-\cos(2\theta)\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{v}

Let:

\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{h}-\beta_{s}\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\qquad J(\boldsymbol{v})=\begin{bmatrix}v_{p}&v_{q}\\
-v_{q}&v_{p}\end{bmatrix}\qquad T(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos 2\theta&\sin 2\theta\\
\sin 2\theta&-\cos 2\theta\end{bmatrix}

Note
J(\boldsymbol{v})[\cos 2\theta;\ \sin 2\theta]^{\prime}=[\cos 2\theta v_{p}+\sin 2\theta v_{q};\ -\cos 2\theta v_{q}+\sin 2\theta v_{p}]^{\prime}=T(\theta)\boldsymbol{v}

\cos(2\theta)
and

r=\beta_{d}J(\boldsymbol{v})\begin{bmatrix}\cos(2\theta)\\

subject to
||[\cos 2\theta,\sin 2\theta]||_{2}=1
as the solution is by definition in the unit circle.

Then a closed-form LS angle is obtained from the direction of

\hat{\theta}=\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{atan2}\big(u_{2},\ u_{1}\big)\text{ if }\beta_{d}>0\\

Extension: multiple representative consumers

Perhaps the most inflexible assumption made above is that there exists a single representative consumer. Let us now consider how the results change once we allow for multiple consumers. We first consider what point-identification requires, and then suggest lower and upper bounds for what multiple representative consumers implies for expected profits and consumer welfare.

We consider first the simplest case, that of multi-good monopoly with identical
\boldsymbol{\delta}
and
S
across representative consumers. Consumers differ only in
\Gamma
; should both
S
and
\Gamma
remain the same, we would be back to the single representative consumer case regardless of
\boldsymbol{\delta}
. The assumption on
S
is the least restrictive which also allows a meaningful simplification of the problem. For as long as consumers differ not in
how
product characteristics matter but rather
how much
, this assumption is appropriate. For example, consumers likely agree on a given phone’s characteristics - battery type, camera specs, screen size - as well as what they mean for the relevant dimensions of competition between phones - performance, camera quality, portability, speed; consumers disagree only on how much they care for, say, a fast phone instead of a durable one.

Without loss of generality, assume 2 representative consumers. The combined Hessian of the consumers is therefore:

\Sigma=I_{N}+\mu(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{1k}\boldsymbol{s}_{k}\boldsymbol{s}_{k}^{\prime})+(1-\mu)(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{2k}\boldsymbol{s}_{k}\boldsymbol{s}_{k}^{\prime})

with inverse:

\Sigma^{-1}=I_{N}-S[\mu(\Gamma_{1}^{-1}+SS^{\prime})^{-1}+(1-\mu)(\Gamma_{2}^{-1}+SS^{\prime})^{-1}]S^{\prime}

The same trick we used earlier in the same setting, keeping orthonormality everywhere but in a pair of attributes and optimising over their angle, can also be used here. For
h=1,2
:

(\Gamma^{-1}_{h}+S^{\prime}S)^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}A^{-1}_{h}&0\\
0&C^{-1}_{h}\end{bmatrix}\qquad\ \text{for}\ \qquad A^{-1}_{h}=\frac{1}{\Delta_{h}}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\gamma_{hj}}+||\boldsymbol{s}_{j}||^{2}&-\boldsymbol{s}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{s}_{j}\\
-\boldsymbol{s}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{s}_{j}&\frac{1}{\gamma_{hi}}+||\boldsymbol{s}_{i}||^{2}\end{bmatrix}

for
\Delta=(1/\gamma_{hi}+d)(1/\gamma_{hj}+f)-(\boldsymbol{s}_{i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{s}_{j})^{2}>0
and
C^{-1}_{h}
extrapolated from above to all
h
. From here, we can write:

\Sigma^{-1}=I_{N}-[\boldsymbol{s}_{i}\ \boldsymbol{s}_{j}](\mu A_{1}^{-1}+(1-\mu)A_{2}^{-1})[\boldsymbol{s}_{i}\ \boldsymbol{s}_{j}]^{\prime}+S_{-(ij)}(\mu C_{1}^{-1}+(1-\mu)C_{2}^{-1})S_{-(ij)}^{\prime}

The term
S_{-(ij)}
refers to
S
with the
i
th and
j
th columns removed. Note that the third term is constant in
\boldsymbol{s}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{s}_{j}
. Without loss of generality, we can therefore focus on a two-attributes setting and study their angle, dropping that last term.

It can then be showed that, from (
2
), we can write the expected profit function of the monopoly as:

E\Pi_{\{ij\}}=\mu E\Pi_{\{ij\}}^{1}+(1-\mu)E\Pi_{\{ij\}}^{2}

If earlier we showed that profits were ever increasing in
\boldsymbol{s}_{1}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{s}_{2}
, the same applies for each consumer here. In other words, the presence of differing curvatures is insufficient for a monopoly to yield horizontal differentiation in observed characteristics.

When then can we expect that it does? We need both
\boldsymbol{\delta}
and
\Gamma
to differ across consumers simultaneously. If so, and proceeding as we have so far, we get the following expected profit function:

E\Pi=-\frac{1}{4\phi}(\mu\delta_{1}+(1-\mu)\delta_{2})^{\prime}(\mu\Sigma_{1}+(1-\mu)\Sigma_{2})^{-1}(\mu\delta_{1}+(1-\mu)\delta_{2})

Taking the derivative in
\boldsymbol{s}_{1}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{s}_{2}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E\Pi_{\{{ij}\}}}{\partial(\boldsymbol{s}_{1}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{s}_{2})}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\phi}\bigg[2\bigg[\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{s}_{1}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{s}_{2}}(\mu\Sigma^{-1}_{1}\delta_{1}+(1-\mu)\Sigma^{-1}_{2}\delta_{2})^{\prime}\bigg]

\displaystyle-(\mu\Sigma^{-1}_{1}\delta_{1}+(1-\mu)\Sigma^{-1}_{2}\delta_{2})^{\prime}(\mu\Sigma_{1}+(1-\mu)\Sigma_{2})^{-1}\bigg[\frac{\partial}{\partial(\boldsymbol{s}_{1}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{s}_{2})}(\mu\Sigma_{1}+(1-\mu)\Sigma_{2})\bigg]\bigg]

\displaystyle(\mu\Sigma_{1}+(1-\mu)\Sigma_{2})^{-1}(\mu\Sigma^{-1}_{1}\delta_{1}+(1-\mu)\Sigma^{-1}_{2}\delta_{2})

Unlike in our previous case, where the net effect could be shown to be positive, meaning that the product attributes should end up parallel, no such effect appears here. In other words, depending on whether a given setting is best suited for a multi-representative consumer interpretation or not, we may or may not have vertical differentiation in observed characteristics.

Computationally, we can see that there is no closed-form solution for determining the orientation. In fact, the same will be the case for selecting the magnitude. Simulation methods need to be employed to determine the optimal angle between two attributes across goods. The same can be shown for the single-product firms setting: adding up
\boldsymbol{q}_{1}^{*}(\Gamma_{1},\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1})+\boldsymbol{q}_{2}^{*}(\Gamma_{2},\boldsymbol{\delta}_{2})
does not yield a unified demand function in an aggregate of the heterogeneous parameters when
\Gamma_{1}\neq\Gamma_{2}
and
\delta_{1}\neq\delta_{2}
.

Consider instead the simplest possible setting: one multi-product monopolist, two representative consumers (
\mu=1/2
), and determine whether the monopolist wants to (i) design two products, each tailored to a representative consumer; (ii) design two identical ones; or (iii) something in between. Assume there are two attributes the monopolist can choose over (i.e.
S
is a
2\times 2
matrix); representative consumer
1
has
\boldsymbol{b_{1}}=(b_{H},b_{L})^{\prime}
and consumer
2
is swapped,
\boldsymbol{b_{2}}=(b_{L},b_{H})^{\prime}
; and:

\Gamma_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{H}&0\\
0&\gamma_{L}\end{bmatrix}\qquad\Gamma_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{L}&0\\
0&\gamma_{H}\end{bmatrix}

(3)

Lastly, fix intensities
\boldsymbol{s}_{1}=\boldsymbol{s}_{2}=1
. Using the formulas above, we can express the equilibrium
\rho^{*}=\boldsymbol{s}_{1}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{s}_{s}\in[0,1]
for varying ratios of
b_{H}/b_{L}
and
\gamma_{H}/\gamma_{L}
. If
\rho^{*}=1
, the conditions of the problem suggest that the monopolist decides to produce goods with equivalent attributes i.e.
\boldsymbol{s}_{1}||\boldsymbol{s}_{2}
; for any
\rho^{*}<1
, some degree of horizontal differentiation in observed attributes is optimal.

The clearest implication we can observe in this graph is that, for as long as each element in the diagonal of
\Gamma
is positively correlated with the corresponding element in
\boldsymbol{b}
across consumers, there is no incentive towards horizontal differentiation in observed attributes. This matches well with what we observed earlier, where optimal attribute targeting by both the multi-product monopolist and the single-product firms depended on a ratio between
\Gamma
and
\boldsymbol{b}
, i.e.
\Gamma^{-3/2}\boldsymbol{b}
. A special case we mentioned above,
E(\boldsymbol{\delta}\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\prime})
, sets
\gamma_{k}=b_{k}^{2},\ \forall k
. This happens to be the one setting that allows us to aggregate multiple representative consumers into a single one, with the vertical differentiation implications that have been shown throughout the paper. In that setting, it is globally optimal to set
\rho^{*}=1
.

In fact, the same applies for the single-product firms setting under these conditions, represented in the graph below.

Despite there no longer being a single firm which internalises competitive effects, we see here once more how positive correlation between each element in the diagonal of
\Gamma
and the corresponding element in
\boldsymbol{b}
means that any attempt to deviate from greater competition in desirable attributes is rarely preferable to aligning with those attributes in greater demand, and positive correlation in both these benefits further coalesces optimal attribute choice along a single orientation.

It is also interesting to see that, for otherwise identical setups, horizontal differentiation is more likely in the multi-good monopoly. This appears to be the result of differentiation softening price competition while raising markups for both goods. This is internalised by the monopolist but less so by the single-product firms, which free-ride on the effort made by the other. Therefore, under duopoly, firms under-differentiate relative to what the monopolist does. By differentiating, the monopolist may also capture additional demand in features not already emphasized by its other good without losing the benefits of tailoring a good to the largest market. This reduced cannibalisation is not internalised by the single-product firms either.

In conclusion, multiple representative consumers may complicate the mathematics, but under sensible conditions do not change - they rather emphasize - the core findings of this paper.
