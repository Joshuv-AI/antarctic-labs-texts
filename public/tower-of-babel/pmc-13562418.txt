Sensor development based on stimuli-responsive materials for biomedical applications
Authors: Jesús E.L. Barriguete, Juan M. Gutiérrez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySoft materials have enabled us to reach, combine, and improve properties such as stability, flexibility, and compatibility. Factors essential for flexible electronics, wearable systems, and implantable devices. In recent years, the demand for technology and new devices has urged the fabrication of diverse materials. Among them, stimuli-responsive materials (“smart”) can modify their structure in response to external stimuli. Furthermore, part of their capabilities is to conserve/combine their properties, resulting in materials with great potential for various applications. The fusion of both materials can enhance their flexibility, resistance, adaptability, and biocompatibility, amidst other characteristics. Particularly, supporting their use in biomedical and organic systems applications. The conjunction of sensitive characteristics and other flexible materials is proper for design, development, and analysis of remote and on-site working. Recently, some devices have been incorporating these features as biological and environmental sensors, smart membranes in microfluidics and drug delivery, and flexible electronics for support and coating. Part of the current and future improvement in bioinspired materials could help develop implantable, biocompatible, hybrid, and wearable devices with better sensing response, improved efficiency, and the incorporation of organic materials. The future direction of soft materials in composites with stimuli-responsive materials is to expand their versatility and tailorability. Although working with them (organic and inorganic elements) is not easy, the combination has improved fundamental physicochemical, mechanical, electrical, and optical features. This combination is the future of sensor technology in biomedical and other scientific fields.This review explains elementary and key concepts, as well as explores the use of various “smart” materials and their characteristics in developing sensors and soft electronics for implants, wearables, drug delivery, and electrochemistry in biomedical applications.

Introduction

In recent years, there has been a considerable expansion and increase in the use of new techniques and experimental materials, aiming to develop “novel” materials with unique characteristics that achieve an exceptional condition meaningful for a specific application. This poses a significant challenge for materials science because traditional materials and fabrication methods need to be modified to satisfy the actual requirements and the new demand for diverse applications. One example is the fast development of new technologies and devices applied in computer science and electronics. Another important area is the use of alternative materials (based on organic materials) to increase, substitute, or modify the response and the specific requirements. The application of alternative materials has reduced production costs and the use of expensive materials, shortened development time, decreased component waste, and reduced the need for complex fabrication techniques.

The high demand for novel and alternative applications has constantly changed the materials used to design and develop sensors (mechanical, electrical, chemical, physical, etc.). A significant concern in the development of devices (e.g., biomedical, biotechnology, environmental, and so forth) is their incompatibility with biological systems or external agents.1,2 This reason has caused efforts to develop different devices based on bio-inspired materials, to fabricate advanced materials that allow adequate integration between systems with special properties such as high performance, biocompatibility, low environmental impact, and recovery of functionality.3 It is well known that the performance of a sensor can be modified, changed, or degraded by interactions with external systems (such as humidity, heat, cold, biological media, water, etc.). For example, polymer matrices allow an excellent interface to obtain great versatility, resistance, durability, fantastic physicochemical features, and variability.4,5

The stimuli-responsive materials (“smart”) have the capacity to modify their internal structure in a fast and reversible way due to an external stimulus. These changes can be attributed to modifications in their volume, structure, surface, solubility, and optical properties. Its characteristics can be adjusted by stimuli such as temperature, pH, humidity, magnetic and electrical fields, resistance, or potential. Stimuli-responsive materials

The versatility of these new stimuli-responsive materials has been extended to diverse applications, including flexible electronics and biomedical devices (electronics and medical), adhesives (chemical), dental and prosthetic implants (medical materials), fibres (textile), and aeroplanes and ships fabrication (aerospace and marine), among others.

Particularly soft electronics combine diverse materials (organic/inorganic, rigid/stretchable) to create an interface capable of detecting specific signals, reducing, or avoiding interactions that modify the surface or morphology of the element under study. Currently, its growing body of research enables the development/fabrication and implementation of new stretchable and flexible devices, which have become an alternative for improved acquisition, interfacing, and confidence in the recording and analysis of data for on-field and remote phenomena.6,7 This area is considered as next step in the novel electronics design and fabrication because based on the classical methodologies and the silicon semiconductors elaboration, one of the objectives on the combination of synthetic and natural materials is to improve and extend the manufacturing of electronics for new applications due to the addition of characteristics as adaptation, flexibility, stretching, mouldability, chemical stability, biocompatibility, degradability, and recyclability. All these features have and will continue to provide benefits in the actual electronics industry, as the implementation of recent synthesis and fabrication techniques has been driving reductions in production and costs. Besides, it will improve and consolidate alternative strategies and materials for updating electronics production and implementation.8,9 In this context, the development of flexible sensors is highly relevant, particularly for portable/wearable devices that monitor diverse signals, with health monitoring as one of the principal applications. This reduces the limitations of measurements in daily and normal activities. Furthermore, this wearable definition takes on meaning to acquire signals in alternative fields. Such examples include: movements of metal and mineral structures, electron mobility for electrical interactions, chemical reactions based on energy interactions, biological detection, and light detection for optical phenomena and radiation.10,11

This review focuses on recent advances in the implementation of smart materials as sensors and soft electronics arrangements in the biomedical field. The main intention is to present diverse essential and specific concepts and examples to illustrate the broad, extended use and combination of materials in biomedical applications. The use of stimuli-responsive materials, such as perfluoropolyether (PFPE), poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), poly(N-isopropyl acrylamide) (PNIPAAm), nickel-titanium alloy (NiTi - nitinol), poly(acrylamide) (PAAm), etc., has improved properties, including biocompatibility, responsiveness, stability, mechanical structure, and controllable degradation. The responsive feature of the composites has added to the performance as an implant structure, support, and extension, in applications such as ambient gas detectors, deformable/reversible structures, and long-term monitoring, and in functionality as a thermoreceptor, semiconductor, and compatible material in the manufacturing and practice of sensors and devices. The hybrid systems implemented (inorganic-organic materials) increase the incorporation of these elements to achieve more sensitive detection and greater adaptability, while also adding a dual or triple response characteristic. Its applications range from implants, chemical and gas sensors, to flexible wearables, thermomechanical support and multisensory devices. Finally, additional examples of its use in electronics, environmental monitoring, and construction, as well as its future potential applications and short-term uses, are discussed.

Sensors

The general definition of a sensor is a device with the capacity to acquire (input) and transform any kind of energy, such as physical, chemical, biological, electrical, optical, electromagnetic, and so forth, into an electrical energy signal (output) that corresponds to a variation of energy (observe Figure 2). The fundamental function in the transformation of energy is related to the effect that causes the application of an electric, magnetic, or other field on a fragment of material. It could be mechanical stress, electric voltage/current, or temperature gradient. These phenomena or effects are not mutually exclusive from each other, although they are commonly related. The importance of the characterization is that the cause and effect are kindred in the same energy domain, but the non-exclusivity generates cross effects. Hence, one energy (domain one) is transferred to another energy domain (two), being the cross effects the basis for the design of sensors and actuators too.Figure 2Sensor diagram

Sensor functionality will depend on the application. These can measure various changes in external phenomena; for example, in biomedical applications, changes in electrical and optical properties can describe phenomena within the human body, such as pressure, blood flow, temperature, and bone growth. Another example is chemical sensors; they are commonly used for detecting, quantifying, and monitoring specific concentrations of compounds, the presence of elements, and the determination of activity and interactions between elements in biological processes. The combination of measurements from diverse types of sensors can enable analysis, investigation, diagnosis, and even beyond, into therapies, restructuring, renewal, and design. One tendency in the development of sensors is to obtain a flexible structure support. This capacity has changed the design and fabrication of the devices, as the sensors must resist deformation and adapt to irregular structures, surroundings, and variable conditions. Different surfaces (ground, water, or air) and bodies (diverse animal species) are the objectives and challenges for the application of flexible structures. The testing and development of diverse materials to obtain the best flexible sensor fabrication have continued with the use of polymers due to their adaptability, biodegradability, biocompatibility, excellent mechanical properties, and low cost. All the properties and different combinations of materials and methods of elaboration have made them complete and satisfy the new demand of applications.

Flexible sensors

The flexible sensors are devices based primarily on organic components, such as polymers (i.e., poly(dimethylsiloxane) (PDMS), polyimide (PI), PVA, and so forth). Their characteristics for acquiring diverse signals and converting them into electrical signals must be the same as those of other common rigid sensors. In addition, be adaptable to special conditions (diverse structures or surfaces) that require the desired flexibility for field and remote applications. These sensors are designed, fabricated, and tested to fulfill the demands of different applications and working conditions, for example, as coating-layer isolators, in chemical interactions, under intense stress trials, and under changing climate conditions, among others. As well as these, they have other complementary functions thanks to their specific construction features that can enhance their duties as remote or mobile sensing, adaptable mechanical structures, novel or improved forms competing against rigid materials, supporting the performance and duration (working time), displacing, or replacing inorganic and high-cost elements, and as an extension for new and well-known field applications.

It has been reported that diverse materials are being used in the rapidly expanding field of the design, manufacturing, and development of flexible sensors. Among the applications, it is possible to find examples of determining temperature, proteins, elements, strain, or pressure. These can be detected through changes in conductivity, piezoresistive, piezoelectric, and capacitive effects. Some examples are mentioned in the next context as a reference. Flexible sensors made from Polyimide/Kapton exhibit thermal stability and high mechanical strength. The sensor developed by Seifi et al. 2022 with high accuracy, sensitivity, and fast response (resistive sensor type) was fabricated on a Kapton substrate, a composite of conductive material and nanoparticles in a ratio of poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrenesulfonate)/reduced graphene oxide (PEDOT: PSS/rGO) (1:0.5 wt%), and aluminium surface electrodes. This device showed a response time of 2 s, a temperature coefficient of resistivity (TCR) of 3.365%/°C, and a linear response over the range of 30–45°C, making it highly sensitive, low-cost, and flexible enough for human monitoring.14 Although Kapton has many advantages in terms of mechanical, electrical, and chemical characteristics, its complex composition (surface) blocks the deposition of matter (modification), causing delamination due to its hydrophobic and inert structure. Having this in mind, the work of Lin et al. 2021 exhibit modified nanoparticle-based inks of silver (Ag) and gold (Au) and post-treatment method (low manufacturing cost and solid performance) for the elaboration of stable sensors and long-lasting organic thin film transistor (OTFT), being the formulation of special 25% Au and 40% Ag printed ink-jet platform on Kapton HN films, a sensibly design. Additionally, it shows a resolution of 725 dpi, an adhesion rating of 5 B (ASTM-3359B), and stability tested by cyclic voltammetry (CV) for 50 cycles at each scan rate (less than 0.1 mA deviation) for the printed electrodes. Generating solid results for nitrate ion (NO3-) ground detection, single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) mercury (Hg2+) sensing, and portable protein perceiving.15 Other common material used by their flexibility, optical transparency, thermal stability, and chemical inert properties is PDMS, the formulation presented in the work of Li et al. 2020 is capable of detecting applied strain and contact force, the flexible sensor based on Ag/PDMS presents a good conductivity of 1.52 × 10-5 Ωm (150 wt% filler content), high strain sensitivity of GF = 939 (maximum), high resolution of 0.02 N (minimum resolution), and stretchability of 50% (maximum). These values have potential applications in real-time monitoring, motion capture, and soft haptic perception.16 A variant PDMS device fabrication method (Hwang et al. 2021), produces a capacitive pressure sensor (10 × 10 mm) with a hierarchically porous PDMS structure using particle and emulsion-template methods. The porous sensor structure has shown a pressure-measuring capacity of 400 kPa, which is 22.5 times higher than that of simple PDMS bulk and the double mono porous PDMS composite. The device demonstrates sufficient sensitivity for finger tapping and respiratory sensing. Besides, it shows potential for shape-prediction applications, useful as a wearable sensor.17 Another important well-used material with unique properties, such as thermostability, transparency, chemical resistance, lightweight, and mouldability, is PVA. In the proposal work by Chen et al. 2020 the film system fabricated on carbon nanotube/poly(vinyl alcohol)/zinc oxide (CNT/PVA/ZnO) materials shows piezoresistive responses of 3 to 1.27% and 1.86 to 2.50% under tension and compression, respectively. On the side of piezoelectric capabilities, it shows a response of 4.93 × 105 Pa for load detection. Besides, the high sensitivity of 0.259 mV/kPa is perfectly suited for health monitoring.18 The report by Qin et al. 2022, introduces a flexible piezoresistive pressure sensor system based on MXene and PVA; its microstructure design and morphology control determine its performance. Furthermore, it has adjustable conductivity (dispersion), resulting in a high sensitivity of 2320.9 kPa-1 (a low detection limit of 6 Pa), a fast response time of 70 ms, and good stability after 10000 cycles of testing. With these results, it is possible to detect a superficial pulse, making it strongly applicable to microforce sensing, miniaturization, and human-machine interfaces.19

Soft materials

A material could be defined as any element, substance, or constituent (matter) of something from a thing (individuality) that is made, composed, or can be used. Chemical and physical properties help us to describe, analyse, and understand what a material is in a simpler form.

There are different methods of modifying materials to enhance their purpose or specific function. The materials could be modified by diverse chemical, physical, biological, and radiation methods. Each technique can shift the structure: a simple example is surface modification, such as coating, etching, deposition, or ablation.27 Making blends or doping two or more materials could change or enhance their properties. For example, 1) the modification of mechanical characteristics could derive from improving the flexibility and adaptability, 2) as optical change, it results in a shift of refractivity, 3) for thermal conversion, an isolator surface as protection, 4) for electrical alteration, obtains a high conductivity as a consequence, and 5) for chemical adjustment, an effect of ion implantation as doping addition.

Furthermore, inspiration and bioinspiration for developing novel materials have been drawn from mimicking the shapes, structures, and functions of various natural objects and systems, as well as from other synthetic systems. Different industry requirements, along with data acquisition from remote or on-site devices, have demanded materials with specific characteristics to support continuously monitored systems. One important development in materials science is soft materials (Figure 4). These materials exhibit characteristics that define their internal structure, showing deformability in a very simple way.28,29,30 This behaviour comes from their physicochemical properties (elements) and the internal spatial organization (structural distribution). Moreover, important mechanical and physical aspects define the true behaviour and the conditions of soft materials, including large differences between the bulk and the shear modulus, finite-strain mechanics, and entropy (molecular structure and elastic restoring forces).31 This performance helps to understand the object’s deformation capacity (stretchability) without or with minimal damage to the shape. As well as its features being tailored for precise purposes.32 The applications in diverse areas and future demands require a design and elaboration that support multi-purpose (functionality), low cost, practical synthesis (easiness), and high efficiency. These sorts of polymers have taken advantage of other materials, for instance, ceramics, metals, and even natural materials, since these can be modified for specific applications,33 including adhesives, sealants and joints, waveguides, membranes, and collectors. Currently, they are applied in the manufacturing of tires (automotive), coatings (packaging), energy (storage and harvesting), or implants (medical).32,34 Figure 4 shows a general aspect of the classification of soft materials and the most common examples of each one.Figure 4Soft materials classification and examples

Polymers are chemical structures that can change their form and structure as temperature increases, making the polymer chains fluid and adaptable. These are commonly used in various areas, such as biomedical, electronics, environmental, construction, industrial food, and others. Their characteristics and applications of some of them are mentioned below. It is well known that poly(vinyl alcohol) is one of the most studied and common polymers. Among their most important characteristics are: water-soluble, non-ionic, non-hazardous, odorless, tasteless, and flexible. These properties increase its biocompatibility, safety when handled, environmental safety, and mechanical resistance.35 This material has many and different applications as membranes for drug delivery, wound healing,36 adhesives for biological implants, skin sensors support,37 meniscus and artificial cartilage,38 besides eye-drop lubricants and fibres for biomedical trade39; as a modifier, aggregate surface agent, and fibre reinforcement for construction areas (cement-composite)40; as an antimicrobial, barrier, and packaging for food industry.41 Another common polymer in the industry is polystyrene (PS). Its principal features are excellent biocompatibility, low permeability, non-toxicity, good adsorption, moisture absorption, and mechanical and chemical resistance. All these features make PS an exceptional candidate in biomedicine for use as a base for immobilising enzymes,42 membranes, and drug delivery systems43; as aggregates for concrete, insulation, and composites for structural integrity on building construction44,45; as pattern nanostructures (semiconductors and storage) for the lithography industry46; and as moulds and implants for foundry industry.47 Another important material is poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA); this material is widely applied due to its resistance to inorganic solvents, high optical transparency (92 %), high refractive index, flexibility, biocompatibility, and low cost. Among their applications in the field of biomedical is commonly used for intra-ocular lenses, dental prosthesis, and drug delivery48; in electronics, as thin films (coating and dielectric), electronic skin devices, and structure support (good flexibility and stability)49,50; and in automotive and space industry as heat preservation and radiation shielding in a nanocomposite with indium tin oxide (ITO) and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNT) respectively.51,52 Figure 5 shows the chemical structures of the most common polymers, which can be divided by their charge (charge number). Stimuli-responsive materials

The principal characteristic of stimuli-responsive materials is the modification of their internal structure due to an external stimulus (reversible). Depending on the physical or chemical conditions, some changes are reflected in the modifications of volume, structure, surface, solubility, and optical properties. The process of responding to external stimuli can be understood as the following stages: chemical or physical signal (reception), material properties or chemical change (shift), and macro- or microscopically mediated change (transduction).

pH and temperature responsiveness have attracted attention and are well known in biomedical and biotechnology fields because they are two measurements commonly studied in the human body that reflect specific characteristics of biological system behaviour. The pH response is based on the donation and reception of protons for the response in pH variation (changing charge modification). In a similar way, the temperature response is based on the phase transition driven by the system’s entropy (chemical vibration).

The importance of developing new materials (smart modification) with tailored characteristics has led to the use of stimuli-responsive materials across diverse applications in the electronic, medical, chemical, textile, aerospace, and marine industries.

Applications

Biomedical applications

For the biomedical field, the development and the continuous fabrication of responsive materials have been assiduous during the last decades because the use of external agents (materials) inside or in contact with biological tissue requires to be tested and approved (biocompatible, degradation, or chemical stability) for specific characteristics, all of these for the beneficial uses in improving healthcare procedures and other potential applications, such as therapies, implants, synthetic organ tissue, prostheses, etc. Besides the increase, the demand and the necessity of a personal health monitoring system due to the rise in diseases (new and long-term), environmental external agents, climate and global position, and the escalation in a stressful lifestyle require new and alternative methods of study to better understand and generate knowledge. Those measures have been implemented to upgrade conditions in the healthcare system, in research projects, and in the handling of personal information. A more personal or detailed sensing system requires additional rules to support its needs for registering, detecting, and analysing data in situ and in real time. Then, improvements in the incorporation or partial/complete substitution of elements in biological media have enabled more robust acquisition systems for laboratory and in-field measurements. As well as bioinspiration for the development of new devices (portable systems) based on the materials, structures, or mechanisms of other living organisms, increasing the use and fabrication of modified materials for specific needs, and the development of well-implemented operational devices. Some of the most common applications include wearable devices, flexible substrates, implants, chemical sensing, drug delivery, and biodegradable compositions, amid others.

Implants

Implant sensors and devices are an important topic in the development, monitoring, and improvement of healthcare services driven by the demand for and research into more specific biological data. These values (biological constants and variables) could and would address the design and fabrication of better and more reliable sensors, devices, or systems based on materials that can respond to and withstand diverse biological conditions (humidity, strain, pH, and temperature) and contain features of chemical inertness and biocompatibility.34,84 The innovation of neural prostheses and human-computer interfaces through the design of flexible/soft and tailored/compliant devices has enhanced the conditions for the design and fabrication of structural, mouldable, and thin components. Acquiring and recording data inside the human body is a meticulous task; it requires constant supervision, and proper implementation can improve the analysis and identification of real and on-site signals, to offer better and new solutions for healthcare, all of which are based on the latest and continuous types of diseases that require constant monitoring. A clear biomedical example of an on-site, implantable system is the hybrid hydrogel probe developed by Park et al. 2021. This device has several advantages due to the integration of a multidetector sensor. First, the optical waveguide system is fabricated from polycarbonate (PC), serving as a covering and the central wire for light transmission, with cyclic olefin copolymer (COC) as an isolator to improve light transmission through the PC core. Second, the fluidic channel is constructed from poly(etherimide) (PEI), a strong and flexible material that can contain the fluids under study. Third, the electrical channel is elaborated using 7 tin (Sn) microelectrodes, covered with PEI, which acts as an excellent insulator. This system is based on a hydrogel of PAAm and alginate (7 N m-1 stiffness) that allows the biointegration of the multisystem sensor, promotes swollen behaviour in contact with neural tissue and fluids, and helps improve device fixation, making it well-suited for real-time and free conditions. The majority of the probes do not have a cover of hydrogel materials; these are elaborated with the same materials mentioned before (PC, PMMA, PLGA, or PDMS) that are stiffer (>100 N m-1), being less flexible and compatible (elastic moduli of 1 MPa–10 GPa against 1–10 kPa from neutral tissue), or just focus on one function. In recent years, systems with dual or multiple detections have been increasingly applied; moreover, the combination of materials enriches functionalities and properties, such as electrical conductivity, stiffness, optical transmission, and biocompatibility, which are reduced or limited when using only one material.85 The stretchability in this sort of implementation is an important aspect of flexible devices. For example, the latest AMOLED devices have been continuously modified to achieve a simpler configuration, enabling use in flexible applications, compared to OLEDs or silicon polycrystalline, which offer lower stretchability. Another example of biomedical systems is the implantable soft light-emitting electrochemical cell array device, which is based on an electrically fully stretchable organic matrix, capable of detecting signals from peripheral nerves, as a wearable (skin) device and as flexible electronics. The elastic thin film array is a combination of a mesh of gold electrodes, a semi-flexible semiconductor, and an elastomer composed of dimethacrylate-modified perfluoropolyether (PFPE – DMA). This arrangement forms an elastic packaging layer across the micropatterning, resulting in a fully stretchable organic transistor. The PFPE – DMA shows a dielectric constant of 2.4 +/- at 20 Hz, without change after 100 cycles in a 100% strain test. Besides, the device was designed with an average active area of 3.5 × 5 mm, resulting in a ratio greater than 140, capable of providing up to 10 mA cm-2 excitation current density and a low voltage consumption of 50 mV.86 Devices with these characteristics were designed to mimic mechanical properties for full application in stretchable circuits and pressure sensors.

In the cardiovascular field, the use of implants has focused on stents and transcatheter valves.87 Shape memory alloys (SMA) and shape memory polymers (SMP) are materials composed of metals and polymers, respectively, that undergo a change in shape in response to thermal or mechanical stimuli; they have the capacity to recover their preestablished shape under large strains (memory). Additionally, these compounds can be preprogrammed to control and supervise their memory change behaviour. Among their more important attributes are good fatigue performance, superelasticity, resistance, and biocompatibility. The nickel-titanium alloy (NiTi) presents a particular characteristic: its shape memory - effect of transformation is based on thermoresponsiveness, which is clearly observed through the conversion from the martensite structure (monoclinic) at lower temperatures to the austenite structure (body-centred cubic) at higher temperatures. Based on these characteristics, other common examples include alloys of NiTi, nitinol copper (NiTiCu), CuAlBe, FeMnAlNi, and FeNiCoAlTaB, with variations in the ratios of elements that increase and modify their capacities due to particle size, mechanical variation, stress, thermal conductivity, and electrical conductivity.87,88 Depending on the conformation, it can fortify or reduce the physicochemical properties and the physical response.

One improvement in the performance of the equiatomic NiTi (1 mm) wires was achieved by modifying the manufacturing procedures. Firstly, an increase in the initial treatment temperature from 300 to 600°C; secondly, a fatigue strain test from 1 to 5% strain (rotating-bending) with a composition of austenite and R-phase. This modification shows an enhancement of the mechanical response above 2.6, with values up to 3250 fatigue cycles, compared with 1250 cycles for non-treated samples. Moving this to biomedical applications, a similar pretreatment modification from 400 to 450°C results in better fatigue-life performance on the wires applied at body temperatures, which is considered extremely important because these are usually tested at room temperature (25°C); thus, this variation is more convenient for biological applications, such as endodontic files and superelastic wires.89 These conditions during the development of implantable stents have generated long-term and stable devices with considerably optimised mechanical performance, given the high stresses imposed by blood flow and location, as well as the obstruction caused by accumulated fat. Thus, the thermal response and resistance are fundamental properties in the design and manufacturing of these devices. In another work with similar techniques of elaboration but in the field of flexible substrates applied for actuators, the analysis and the modification of SMA composite materials have led to significant improvements in the material’s performance and behaviour. Thin-film fabricated samples of nitinol, modified NiTiCu, and additions of aluminium/magnesium (NiTiCuAlMn) on flexible substrates of Kapton have shown stability under thermomechanical tests. The difference between the three systems is related to the composition of the smart films, where the incorporation of copper increases the velocity and extension-flexion change. Additionally, the magnesium enhances this modification because the particle size and the physicochemical properties, such as thermal and electrical conductivity, stimulate the thermal response in the systems. The test showed an arrangement configuration of a cantilever and a butterfly for thermal experimentation. The increase in temperature (>250°C) and the Joule heating setup made possible the extension of the smart thin films; hence, the decrease in both results in the flexion to its original form of the SMA/Kapton system. The tests reveal deflections of 1 and 4 mm during the 5 V power test (Joule heating), as well as a temperature increase of around 30–36.5°C during the recovery phase in the mentioned configuration. Besides, it is possible to appreciate a notable increase in the load bearing capacity. This thermomechanical behaviour manifests displacements of 1, 1.6, and 2.5 mm in the 0.5 Hz test for NiTi, NiTiCu, and NiTiCuAlMn alloys, respectively. The performance of the modified NiTi copper films makes them suitable for electrical test applications, where a variation of electricity is observable in diverse films in a Stewart platform arrangement, making them useful as micro grippers and applicable for smart circuits.90 Its incorporation in several scientific applications has been used in self-expanding vascular stents, wires (Orthodontics), orthopaedic parts, braces, frames, as well as for mechanical triggers and sensors.91,92,93

Chemical sensing

The chemical interaction between the sensing material (transducer) and a potential analyte alters its properties, resulting in changes in resistance, conductivity, structure, or volume. Where the selectivity and sensitivity are the main characteristics of chemical sensor design.94,95

There are diverse types of chemical sensors that are generally applied in the fields of food, environmental, safety, and medicine. The main detection systems are developed for gaseous, ionic, or molecular species, and for external stimuli (low percentage). Being the most common examples for environmental and safety applications, such as in the control of indoor air quality, traffic pollution, and detection of explosive gases; on the side of biomedical applications, the development has made it in the detection and analysis of air (breath) and blood by the measurement of oxygen (O2) concentration, additionally it is possible the detection of different compounds (i.e., H+ -pH, NOX, COX, SO2, O3, NH3 and volatile organic components) which have enormous applications. Ionic liquids (IL) and metal-organic frameworks (MOF) are among the most recently developed materials, owing to their physicochemical properties (increased sensitivity) and post-synthesis modifications (resulting in more stable, hybrid responses due to double-ion composition), respectively. Thus, those will exhibit better properties in terms of functionalization, activation groups and materials, and hybrid elements. Initially, metal-organic frameworks were used in chemistry for gas storage (hydrogen and methane) and as molecular separators (carbon). In the biomedical field, one important function is the detection of specific elements as gas sensor detectors or as electronic noses. The MOF’s principle is based on specific interactions through a peculiar molecular configuration; these are porous crystalline materials containing metal ions and organic ligands. This configuration will create a target reaction between elements, as well as used as a storage, filter/separator, or support other interactions. Several forms can be arranged for the design of these frameworks. As an example, a mixture of gas flows through the MOF thin film; at this point, functionalised groups target unique elements or molecules, ready to catch them as a singular sensitive detector. The elements or functional groups will make bonding, attaching the targeting elements/molecules inside the metal-organic networks. To measure this phenomenon, a transduction is realized by a piezoelectric mechanism. Here, a quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) arrangement in conjunction with an alternating current provoking oscillation. By the reaction of the absorbed gas molecules, the resonation frequency would be decreased. Different inert, polar, and non-polar gases can be detected by this setup.96 In a similar function’s principle but alternative configuration, 2D materials also focus their design on the targeting of molecules, ions, among others. There are two mechanisms in this configuration, physisorption (non-covalent bonding) and chemisorption (covalent bonding). A common sensing system is the pH meter, although size and external source limit its remote use. That is why the implementation of 2D materials for hydrogen ion sensing is increasing. A simple system of molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) and graphene oxide (GO) can detect the interaction on the surface of the gate dielectric and the electrolyte. The generation of a positive charge is due to the protonation of OH groups, and the negative charge is due to the deprotonation. For the metal ion sensing, it works based on the electrical interaction and adsorption. A configuration of 2D nanosheets, such as GO in a field-effect transistor (FET), results in a highly sensitive structure for detection or adsorption of heavy metal ions, helping to make short/fast identifications in real-time monitoring changes in resistance and conductance during the detection or adsorption of the target molecules. For the Nucleic acid sensing configuration, an equal form of MoS2 and stacked GO results in an ultrasensitive to DNA. The setup becomes biocompatible and perfect for immobilising DNA molecules, which creates a photoluminescence reaction due to the presence (increase) of the concentration of DNA, triggering a more positive charge on the surface (charge transfer from MoS2 to graphene), intensifying the photoluminescence. Finding this type of layout particularly useful because it is real-time and portable monitoring, especially for its use as an electronic tongue or taste sensor.97 Another great example of chemical sensors is a system with a dual function. Firstly, the flexible film composite was fabricated by a blend of thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) and polystyrene-b-poly(ethylene-co-butylene)-b-polystyrene (SEBS). This combination results in a flexible, stretchable material with highly resistant, stable, protective, and chemical resistance. Secondly, to increase its functionality, the flexible film was functionalised with acid carbon nanotubes (ACNT), accomplishing electrical conductivity and superhydrophobicity characteristics, as well as enhancing stretchability and thermal stability. A unique structure (porosity) and excellent elasticity brought as a consequence, a great conjunction resulting as a multifunctional sensor for chemical vapour and strain movements detection. In the function of chemical vapour detector, the device was tested using some volatile solvents as acetone (polar), heptane and toluene (non-polar) commonly used in the laboratory. During the process of swelling and de-swelling by the interaction of the vapours, the resistance responsivity was recorded to observe the behaviour of the organic elements. A sensing time of 60 s was used to check the sensitivity of the system to acetone, toluene, and heptane vapours. The sensitive film displayed a high sensitivity of 2.8, 1.4, and 1.1 cycle resistance, respectively. And a detection limit of 1 ppm for toluene vapour (lowest value in the literatures). Being the main point of the system, the variability on the conductive network formed between the organic molecules and the TPU/SEBS/ACNT matrix. For the strain functional detector, the tests showed that the stretching-realising cycle (finger, arm, and knee movements) at 20 cycles allows stable and periodic sensing signals for determining the strain sensing intensity in the composite. These values were 1.02 at a strain of 10%, 1.06 at 30%, and 1.16 at 50%. In addition, some of the material tends to recover its original form due to thermodynamic equilibrium. The unique properties of super hydrophobic, electrically conductive, superior flexibility, stretchability, and durability, made this device an incredible candidate as a wearable device in monitoring human motions, as wells as specific gas sensor or electronic nose in more stress conditions.98 One particular device, is a novel arrangement of metal-oxide hydrogel field-effect transistors (MOHFET), its design and construction are based on a mixture of acrylamide (AAm), N-[3(dimethylamine)propyl]acrylamide (DMAm), and 3-acrylamidophenylboronic acid (3-APB). At first, the obtained smart hydrogel was incorporated in a FET structure, being the hydrogel the medium of the channel material for the conduction in the device. The model was improved with the addition of gold nanoparticles (doped), enhancing the ion conductivity. The final system has the capacity to detects the ion concentration into the hydrogel by the addition of a specific analyte. In this case, the studied solution was phosphate buffered saline (PBS) at different concentrations, (similar human body ion concentration). The MOHFET device presents an ion conductivity variable between the two systems, in the first without AuNPs, the drain current (Id) at 0 concentration of PBS registered 6 μA, at 0.5 X it was 20 μA, and at 1.0 X indicated 39 μA. In the second with AuNPs, the current at 0 [PBS] registered 20 μA, at 0.5 X it was 54 μA, and at 1.0 X indicated 80 μA. Here is clear the increase of the ion conductivity in the doped device. Besides, values of capacitance for PBS = ∼4.8 × 10-6 F and deionised water (DI) = ∼3.5 × 10-6 F, and resistance of ∼2000 Ω for PBS and ∼4000 Ω for DI, showed a contrary behaviour to the sensor’s resistance. This device due to their FET sensing arrangement, has the potential to be applied as a non-invasive remote monitoring system or as an invasive surface contact monitoring system (oscillator telemetry) on the response to diverse chemicals.99 Another important function in the biological-chemical detection comes from the sensing of particular elements that play a different action on biological species. Contrary to the other mentioned devices, this has the ability to identify an external nerve agent simulant. The fluorescent thin-film smart hydrogel sensing material was elaborated on a matrix of AAm and N,N-methylene-bis-acrylamide (BAM), forming a solid flexible structure. The film was embedding by the 6,7-dihydroxycoumarin with the distinction of fluorescence under the presence of UV light (λ = 365 nm) and a fast fluorescence quenching in low concentrations of the Sarin stimulant diethylchlorophosphate (DCP) nerve agent. The device in a dual sensing activity, can detect the DCP in both aqueous and vapour phases. The hydrogel film exhibits fluorescence distinction (turn-off behaviour) after exposure it to the nerve agent for 60 seconds. After that, the phenomena can be easily observed under UV light, where the phosphate group from the DCP molecule, forms a Hydrogen-bonding with the hydroxyl group from the coumarin structure. For the aqueous phase, different types of water (tap, pool, DI, and bay) were tested, resulting in a constant intensity of fluorescence. Both detections manifest a real and versatile response to nerve simulant agents, perfectly applicable for biosecurity, in environmental, and as a portable sensor.100 This kind of system could improve the real-time on-site detection monitoring for the distinction of different organic compounds in biological and environmental security systems.101

Optical sensing

The optical optimization and mechanical properties have been favorable due to the design of three-dimensional network polymers based on hydrogel structures. These are biocompatible and very similar to soft tissues, and are widely used in biomedical applications. For photonics applications, the control of transparency is fundamental to obtain precise and straightforward measurements. Besides the conservation of their properties, such as reflection and intensity, is especially important for biological implementations. A controllable or necessary time of usability, is a basic requirement in the design and fabrication. There are several materials, for example, PMMA, PC, PLA, among others, that are used for the development of implantable probes. Their great capacity to withstand different conditions, durability, and ease of manufacturing make them suitable for application. Nevertheless, these present issues in integration, long-term use, and compatibility that reduce their confidence in biological execution. One of the strategies that has been used in recent years to establish better integration of the systems is the incorporation of other elements into the composite; this has supported, improved, and met conditions that enhance a more stable material during all the specific tasks and experiments in biological media. One team headed by Choi et al. 2013, mentioned the elaboration of biophotonics implantable hydrogel based on PEGDA, for this study the concentration of the PEG hydrogels tested, played an important parameter because was possible to determine that a specific molecular weight of 5 kDA of poly(ethylene glycol diacrylate) bring a desirable optical (more transparent) arrangement to waveguide the light in contrast with lower concentration that presented less transparency (lightly white coloration). The resulting hydrogels showed values of n = 1.35 as the refractive index and an optical loss of 0.23 dB cm-1 in the visual range (400–700 nm). These values allow testing the devices as an optical light-guiding device in a zigzag test by a laser beam (491 nm), as a hydrogel waveguide due to the fluorescence along the material to deliver light to external mediums, and as a sensing and therapeutic cell device, where encapsulation and cell implantation were viable for 8 days. All these conditions have led to the use of the hydrogel as an implantable device, supporting biocompatibility and adaptability, allowing reflection and scattering of light between the cells and films (refractive index 1.36–1.39 cytoplasm). Being perfect for optogenetics therapy and synthesis applications, stable and for in vivo testing.102 Biodegradable materials have been gaining ground in the last decade because of their properties for use in delivery, sensing, and transmission applications. The application of hybrid probes with degradable and biocompatible properties is excellent for this sort of optical bioapplications. Poly(lactic acid) (PLA), and poly(glycol acid) are among the most common materials used due to their degradable features. For this work, a variant of poly(L-lactic acid) (PLLA) has been used for the design and construction of waveguides for the development of sensing materials. Moreover, it is incorporated because it can form a solid/rigid structure with semi-flexible capacity, compatibility, as well as a degradable material. The elaborated and implanted fibre-optic sensor, with a diameter of 220 μm, under a simple thermal drawing process, detects and delivers intracranial light, deep brain fluorescence sensing, and optogenetic interrogation. The fibres of PLLA (similar results on PLGA) present semi-flexibility, transparency, and biodegradability; on the mechanical side, a bending stiffness of 1.5 × 104 N m-1, for the optical side, a refractive index of n = 1.47 (brain n = 1.36 and water n = 1.33) and transparency in visible range. Additionally, a measurement of the fibre loss coefficient (1.87 dB cm-1 to 4.90 dB cm-1) was performed to monitor the degradation (hydrolysis) of optical conditions over 7 days, as well as mechanical surface degradation in density and size. In the in vivo neural sensing and interrogation and optogenetics test, it was possible to detect deep brain recordings and infected cells via the green fluorescent protein, in addition to its use as a freely moving implant. During the tests, the slow but continuous degradation reducing the optical and mechanical properties in the implanted probe was observed, as mentioned before.103 In conclusion, controlling the material’s shape and structure expands its use and functions, with potential in photometry, regenerative medicine, optogenetic stimulation, and tissue engineering applications.

Rehabilitation monitoring

Flexible and portable electronic devices for biomedical, clinical medicine, and daily monitoring are essential for obtaining better and more confident data to improve man-machine interaction. One big challenge in the biomedical field has been integrating artificial intelligence with a compatible, adaptable interface, which has helped establish multiple effective ways to connect patients (users) and devices for a personal monitoring system. A flexible and stretchable dual-mode nanogenerator device for limb motion sensing and touch information interaction (triboelectric system) is based on a film/band fabricated from poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF), silver, and silicone materials to conform to a biomedical motion and rehabilitation device. PVDF material presents a high dielectric constant and chemical resistance, making it a fundamental material for adaptable applications under both static and dynamic conditions. Printable moulds were used to fabricate the silicone base substrate of the system. A thin PVDF wire, in conjunction with the Ag electrodes, was inlet into the silicone membrane in a semicircular structure. The wave structure and the dimensions of diameter and distance were essential for the stretchable conditions, making it ideal for flexible and adaptable devices. Its stretchability can reach 33.33%, which is useful for monitoring the degree of human joints (fingers, wrists, or elbows) around 30 to 90°. The performance and structure (piezoelectric mechanism) showed values of short-circuit current (Isc) from 62 to 175 nA for tensile length (stretching) in a frequency range of 1–2.5 Hz, and a piezoelectric constant of 19 pC N-1. Based on the electrostatic effect, the interaction of nitrile rubber with a positive charge over the band device causes the potential of the silver to change, hence making a flow of electrons (short-circuit), finally transferring the charge from the ground to the Ag electrodes, producing, at the end, an alternating current signal. All this at a frequency of 1 Hz, with a current and charge of 150 nA and 6 nC.104 The limb motion monitoring works as a piezoelectric generator, and the touch information interaction works as a single electrode for a triboelectric nanogenerator, being useful in rehabilitation training sensing and in man-machine interaction interface.105,106,107 The design, configuration, and structure of the stretchable device, in flexible motion-sensor and interaction charge transfer detector are appreciated in the Figure 9.Figure 9Diagram of a rehabilitation monitoring systemThe configuration of the flexible composite film is applied as a joint detection device; based on a silicone substrate, a piezoresistive reversible-stretching PVDF structure, and silver flexible wires for nanogeneration. Other applications

In construction and environmental applications, different external stimulation options yield similar results. One of the models fabricated for electrochromic response, CeO2/TiO2 (storage layer) combined with a hybrid film of WO3/poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)-poly(styrene sulfonate) (electrochromic layer), obtaining a switching speed of 12.7 s and a high coloration of 108.9 cm2 C-1, for thermochromic response the model elaborated with microgel colloid of PNIPAAm, using glycerol to decrease the temperature critical point to exhibit the chromic modification with a high transparency around 15°C and shifting to opaque at 50°C. And finally, for mechanochromic applications, a composite of coloured silica/PDMS with a mixture of rhodamine B and other PDMS silica nanoparticle layers, resulting in a translucent film at 20 % strain due to scattering from SiO2, as well as an increase in opacity (colour) near 80% strain.108

For electronic applications, the objective was to implement an alternative biodegradable and biocompatible material. One example is the application, such as optoelectronic switching, reported by Wu et al. 2018. The ultrathin PLA film was used as a substrate portioning mechanical support and biodegradability, as well as the combination of gold nanoparticles with alkali lignin, forming a matrix with the capacity to trap charge to control the conductivity of the film (tunneling). The entire system works as a resistance-switching device (write and read), with a sandwiched structure of aluminium/silver nanoparticles (NP) (Al/Au NPs: lignin/Al) and a PLA (skin-inspired substrate).109

The use of carbon quantum dots (CQDs) in environmental detection has been of great interest because of their characteristics; CQDs are biocompatible, non-toxic, water-soluble, antibacterial, high-temperature stable, chemically inert, small in size, easily functionalised, and tunable photoluminescence. The possible detection of diverse components in the environmental domain has been increasing because there are possibilities to adapt/select specific elements to enhance the material’s responsiveness for intelligent sensing systems. An innovative smart switch sensor capable of dual metal ion detection is based on nitrogen-doped carbon quantum dots (N-CQD) and a poly(acrylamide) hydrogel. This device shows a reversible fluorescence (on/off) response because of the electron transfer occurring on the surface with particles of Cu2+, Ag+, and L-cysteine, which is used as a recovering agent, with a limit of detection of 0.813 × 10–3, 0.468 × 10–3, and 0.437 × 10–3 mM, respectively.110 Diverse specific functional groups can detect different classes of particles; the detection parameters in the range of detection for the use of CQDs include metal ions, drugs and chemical warfare agents, anions, pollutants, and explosives.111 As a general objective, these systems can be applied to robust remediation of environmental pollution, real-time monitoring, and more recently, specifically for emerging pollutants.111,112

Future and challenges

One basic challenge is the selection of materials and the fabrication of flexible devices with responsive characteristics. These have been and will be an important goal in the fabrication and development of wearable sensors and devices. There is a long way to go because there are several materials on the market and in development. From the simple, such as polyethylene (PE), polyurethane (PU), poly(ethylene terephthalate) (PET), and so forth, to the complex, such as composites, blocks, networks, hybrids, biohybrids, among others, which exhibit incredible characteristics in elasticity, compatibility, flexibility, stability, chemical resistance, degradability, optical transparency, or even biocompatibility.

One crucial challenge and potential future for stimuli-responsive (“smart”) materials is to expand and improve their functionality and responsiveness. This advancement will make them a reference (leading) material and an alternative material in diverse applications. On the side of biomedical applications, it is well-known that there are some particular limitations, such as compatibility and integration. These are important details to be considered for its complete incursion. On the other hand, in applications in the fields of electronics, construction, or even the environment, use and experimentation are more accessible and open because the external medium (ambient conditions) will not generate a rejection (immune process) as a biological medium would. One important field of application (cutting edge), is in the creation, design, and manufacture of biohybrid materials. Some special features are necessary to fabricate a biohybrid material. For this, it requires the combination of a moiety of living/biological origin (organic) and other non-living/synthetic (inorganic) components.113,114 This sort of ensemble requires a balance in the composition to hold the structure and the functionality. Among its characteristics are responsiveness, self-healing or self-assembling, durability, and so forth.115 Two examples of biohybrid “smart” material applications are: 1) the work reported by Li and Schroeder 2018,116 in which they fabricated a hybrid material based on a combination of DNA-PNIPAAm. The copolymer was synthesized by a polymerase chain reaction, and it was grafted to observe the dynamic stretching and the relaxation behaviour due to its thermoresponsiveness at a low critical solution temperature (LCST) of 40°C. And 2) Chen et al. 2014117 reported that they elaborated building blocks based on Bacillus spores. Obtaining stimuli-responsive materials with high energy density. The application is using two types of spores, B. subtilis and B. thuringiensis, on a coated rubber sheet to improve their physical characteristics for a notorious and stable strain response and energy density.

Fabrication methods generate diverse types of challenges. These include the facilities, reagents, materials, equipment, and even locations. As well as the steps in the methodology (synthesis, deposition, coating, functionalization, etc.) to obtain the final device. Even if all those requirements are complete, the result would depend on the application. Nowadays, one line of development for novel smart devices that is working and has important challenges to resolve is the design and manufacturing of smart materials by 3D and 4D printing. One of the principal goals of this additive manufacturing technique (AM) is to reach micro- and nanoscale structures. Additionally, the personal functionalization and design content will generate more versatile models, or even more complex. Besides, it is necessary to balance some factors, such as biocompatibility, mechanical performance, biological integration, and, most importantly, stimulus responsiveness (4D). Moreover, alternative combinations such as textiles and papers could add and improve properties and response; therefore, they are totally useful and highly potential for applications, such as electronics, tissue engineering, and soft robotics. Offering advantages because they are renewable, inexpensive, green, and biocompatible.3,9,118,119,120,121,122

The development of multistimulus response devices is a complex and future challenge; for this, diverse aspects need to be observed and analysed. To support a demand of this kind, a well-designed and robust structure is necessary. Another important aspect to be considered is the use of several materials in the formation of the device, which is adaptable and body structure (mechanical conformation), covering (ambient/biological medium protection), and multiple responsive sensory spots (external stimuli). Hence, the construction of these sorts of devices requires detailed specification to combine the elements to conserve and obtain the desired responsive stage. One example is the characteristic that voltammetry sensors have; the arrangement is capable of measuring multiple analytes at the same time because of the redox potentials that each chemical species has. In this context, optoelectronic smart devices are leading the advancement because of different strategies of design and manufacture, supported by the current use of artificial intelligence (AI) and other technologies. Besides, the applications of diverse materials in this field are less restrictive than their use in biomedical applications.123,124

In brief, the use of stimuli-responsive materials for the design and manufacturing of sensors have been in a constantly development, several applications as biosignal monitoring (wearables), soft robotics actuators, motion sensing, biointegrated systems, controllable degradation, 3D/4D printing devices, harvesting nanogenerators, regenerative structures, bioinspired systems, and programmed systems; are an exciting and potentially beyond for the creation and development of new responsive advanced sensors. In the near future, more advanced and practical sensors and devices, in conjunction with responsive materials, will be in constant use, with some of these forming solid lines of research and application. Principally, in the development and manufacturing of biohybrid materials/devices, artificial biological circuits, regenerative and therapeutic materials, self-powering and self-charging sensors, soft robotics, and programmed advanced materials for implantable delivery systems and self-folding.113,125,126,127,128,129,130,131,132,133

Conclusions

The challenges that the material science field faces in developing new components have been and will continue to be problematic. On some occasions, it is not easy to understand all the requirements, time, and dedication that are necessary to resolve during the development period. Although this sounds “impossible”, thanks to the combination of diverse methods and techniques, multiple applications, and tenacity, it has been possible to obtain different and new results. Hybrid materials with specific functional groups, arrangements, and compositions have paved the way to increase and explore alternative fields of use, even though there have been enormous failures along the way.

Polymers are among the most widely used materials across various fields due to their important properties, such as chemical resistance, stability, light weight, and low cost. It is well known that soft materials exhibit enhanced flexibility, stability, and mobility. The use of rigid structures imposes considerable limitations on experimentation with certain devices. On the other hand, the adoption of flexible structures, despite other limitations, has enabled advanced functions such as establishing new interconnections among components, enhancing functionality, greater portability, remote monitoring, and adaptability to irregular structures in biological and external media.

The integration of smart/stimulus materials with other components in recent years has enabled advances and more sophisticated properties, enhancing adjustable stability, flexibility, compatibility, and responsiveness. These achievements have become essential, increasing their use in flexible electronics, wearables, and implantable devices. The specific and/or reversible responses of responsive materials have expanded the applications of hybrid systems. As is well known, working with biological systems has led to both advantages and disadvantages, but the challenges posed by blended properties indicate future fields of application.

Biomedical applications are complex; the requirements imposed by biological interactions do not permit rapid testing and manufacturing. In spite of this, it brings new areas of opportunity; the introduction of novel materials requires meticulous methods and techniques to ensure possible and positive interactions with the goal of acquiring signals in contact with a living medium for short-, medium-, and long-term periods.

Implant sensors or devices are among the most challenging applications because of their direct necessity in healthcare; the device’s interaction with the medium is always (for a specific period) with diverse biological components. Their goal is to measure and if possible, improve a biological process. Mechanical, chemical, and biological stress capacities are tested for continuous monitoring, actuation, and change of structure due to thermal or mechanical stimuli (memory). As well as for long-term applications, because they are normal in all physiological functions. Chemical sensors are among the most widely used sensors. In some applications, this type of sensor requires a low volume of biological samples; their results and/or measurements need to be precise and highly sensitive. In addition, some have dual sensing capabilities that open their use as a specific biomolecule detector or as a portable wearable detector. Moreover, they must be sensitive enough to filter external agents that may be harmful to humans. In optical sensing, the combination of structures has allowed better measurements in deeper tissues due to its transparency and compatibility. The stress conditions are similar to those of some implant devices because they are used for long-term experiments in non-accessible places, giving them multiple functions, as well as testing their capacities for short- and long-term use. The man-machine interaction in rehabilitation has been and will remain one of the most important applications due to the direct exchange of materials over human skin, for measuring the mechano-response and the interaction with flexible, stretchable supports. Besides, movement (remote sensing) can be used for monitoring dynamic mechanisms in humans.

Future directions and challenges in the composition of novel responsive materials will lie in the diverse configurations of elements, design, and manufacture, as well as in diverse fields of applications, due to their versatility and tailoring properties. Some examples that have a promising future of use and application are in the areas of construction, in the development of structural devices (flexion) and concrete shape (compression), food packaging on breathable parcels and element filter (permeable control), and environmental applications on humidity system control (weather conditions) and biodegradable system support (eco-friendly). Alternatively, other applications in the biomedical field, as mentioned before, include creating 4D printed structures with multiple responses for the design and fabrication of specific internal (micro- and nano-) and external “intelligent” structures (variable form) for tissue engineering and cell growth cultivation, programmable advanced materials, several implantable uses, self-powered, and biohybrid systems. On the electronics side, the integration of electronic hybrid systems has strong potential for the collection of elements (containers), memory devices (drives), and stretchable substrates (adaptable devices), among others.
