Associations between dietary nutrient intake and sleep disorders in cancer survivors base on NHANES 2005 to 2018
Authors: Qian Wu, XueFeng Yang, Zhiyu Wang, Kai Zhou, Hang Yang, Lixia Zhou, Xiaoxia Gou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Sleep disorders significantly impact the quality of life for many individuals, which remain largely unrecognized. It is widely believed that dietary nutrient intake plays a crucial role in promoting healthy sleep patterns. However, there is lack of research focusing on cancer survivors. This study utilized the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database to investigate the correlation between dietary nutrient intake and sleep disorders in cancer survivors. Analyzing data from 2882 cancer survivors in NHANES spanning from 2005 to 2018. Dietary nutrient intake was evaluated through two 24-h dietary recalls. Weighted multivariable logistic regression model was employed to further applied to explore the association between dietary fiber intake and sleep disorders while controlling for relevant confounding factors. Additionally, the nonlinear and dose–response relationships were explored using restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression along with smooth curve fitting and threshold effect analysis. Stratified analysis and interaction analysis were conducted to assess the consistency of the results. Univariable and multivariable analysis demonstrated that dietary fiber intake showed negative correlation with sleep disorders (P < 0.05). Treating dietary fiber intake as continuous variable. After accounting for all covariates, the study reaffirmed the protective role of dietary fiber (odds ratio [OR]: 0.99, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.98–0.99, P = 0.044). Analysis of dietary fiber intake in quartiles further supported this trend (P < 0.001). Interestingly, further investigations indicated that dietary fiber intake was not associated with sleep apnea, insomnia, restless legs syndrome, or other types of sleep disorders (P > 0.05). Notably, no statistically significant interactions were observed in all subgroup analyses except poverty income ratio (PIR) (P for interaction < 0.05), indicating that the protective effect of dietary fiber on sleep disorders was more pronounced in individuals with PIR ≥ 2.5. Our results suggest that adequate dietary fiber intake may be beneficial dietary strategy to reduce sleep disorders in cancer survivors.

Introduction

Sleep is essential for maintaining both physical and mental well-being, playing a crucial role in the body’s restoration and protection mechanisms1. However, sleep disorders are widespread in the general population and have been officially recognized as public health concern by the Centers for Disease Control (CDC)2. Sleep disorders are defined as the persistent inability to obtain sufficient sleep and can manifest in various forms such as insomnia, sleep apnea syndrome, narcolepsy, and restless legs syndrome3. The repercussions of sleep disorders extend to a range of adverse health effects, such as reduced quality of life, increased physical and psychological issues4. Furthermore, research has has shown the correlation between sleep disorders and chronic conditions like diabetes5, cardiovascular disease6, and depression7.

Approximately one in five men or women are estimated to develop cancer during their lifetime, with around one in nine men and one in ten women succumbing to the disease, according to the latest estimates from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC)8. With ongoing advancements in cancer diagnosis and treatment, the number of cancer survivors is expected to increase alongside the aging population, leading to significant increase in long-term survival rates9. Nevertheless, cancer survivors often face unique challenges in various aspects of their lives, including physical, psychological, economic, and social factors both during and after anti-tumor treatment. Sleep disorders are particularly common among this group of individuals.

External factors such as poor eating habits can disrupt the circadian rhythm of modern individuals, leading to sleep disorders like difficulty falling asleep and frequent waking up10. Sleep disorders are affected by many factors, including diet and environment, among which dietary factors are particularly important. Research has shown that diet rich in fiber and anti-inflammatory properties can enhance sleep quality11. One study revealing notable negative dose–response link between fiber-rich intake and overall mortality12. Conversely, recent studies have pointed out that higher consumption of added sugars, starches, high fats, and non-whole grains is linked to increased rates of insomnia13,14. Therefore, various research findings emphasize the important impact of dietary intake on sleep quality.

While research on the relationship between diet and sleep has expanded, there is a lack of information specifically on cancer survivors. Therefore, this study examined the association between dietary nutrient intake and sleep disorders using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). The objective is to provide evidence for dietary recommendations that may help prevent sleep disorders in cancer survivors and to raise awareness of the impact of dietary nutritional patterns on sleep disorders.

Methods

Study population

This study examined data from 70,190 participants ≥ 18 years of age in the NHANES spanning from 2005 to 2018. Initially, 3782 participants were identified based on whether they had been diagnosed with cancer or malignancy by healthcare professionals (variables MCQ220). Those who responded affirmatively were further asked to specify the type of cancer (variables MCQ230A). Subsequently, rigorous screening was conducted to ensure data accuracy, which involved: (1) excluding individuals without completed sleep questionnaires; (2) excluding participants without dietary nutrition data; (3) excluding data with missing covariates. Based on these criteria, 2882 participants were ultimately included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Approval for this study was obtained from the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Research Ethics Review Board (ERB), and written informed consent was obtained from the CDC for all surveyed individuals. The methods involved in this study were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations (Declaration of Helsinki).Fig. 1Study participant selection flowchart.

Study participant selection flowchart.

Sleep disorders

The NHANES survey used the SLQ050 variables to assess sleep disorders, and the researchers assessed sleep disorders using the Sleep Disorders Questionnaire, which inquired whether participants had ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that they have trouble sleeping (yes or no)15, those who responded affirmatively were then classified as having sleep disorders for further analyses. Similarly, among the SLQ070 variables, participants who self-reported sleep apnea (SLQ070A), insomnia (SLQ070B), restless legs syndrome (SLQ070C), and other sleep disorders (SLQ070D), and answered ‘yes’ were included in subsequent analyses and classified as having sleep disorders.

Dietary nutrient intake

To assess dietary nutrient intake, NHANES participants completed two 24-h dietary recall interviews. The first dietary recall was conducted through face-to-face interview at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC), where trained interviewers adhered to standardized protocol to collect detailed information about all foods consumed by the participant in the preceding 24-h. The second interview, conducted by telephone 3 to 10 days later, aimed to gather additional dietary data and enhance the accuracy of intake estimates16. In this study, the daily dietary nutrient intake was determined by averaging the two dietary recalls of each participant or by using single recall if only one was available. The dietary questionnaire used in this study included comprehensive list of 27 dietary nutrients from the NHANES database, such as energy (kcal), protein (g), carbohydrate (g), total sugar (g), dietary fiber (g), cholesterol (mg), vitamin A (μg), retinol (μg), α-carotene (μg), β-carotene (μg), vitamin B1 (mg), vitamin B2 (mg), vitamin B6 (mg), folic acid (μg), vitamin B12 (μg), vitamin C (μg), vitamin E (mg), vitamin K (μg), calcium (mg), phosphorus (mg), magnesium (mg), iron (mg), zinc (mg), copper (mg), sodium (mg), potassium (mg), and selenium (μg).

Covariates

The study examined various covariates, including demographic data such as age, gender, race/enthnicity, marital status, education levels, and poverty income ratio (PIR). chronic diseases considered in the analysis were hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes. Lifestyle behaviors like smoking status and alcohol drinking status were also taken into account, along with body mass index (BMI). Categorical variables encompassed gender (male, female), race (Mexican American, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, other race), and marital status (never married, divorced/widowed/separated, married/cohabiting). Education levels were categorized as less than high school, high school, and more than high school. Chronic diseases were determined through self-reported medical history, with hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes. Smoking was classified into no-smoking (less than 100 cigarettes in lifetime) and smoking (100 cigarettes in lifetime and ongoing). Alcohol drinking using was assessed based on whether participants had consumed alcoholic drinks. The PIR was calculated as household income divided by the poverty line, and participants were categorized into two groups based on the median value (2.5). While BMI was derived from weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2), BMI categories were defined according to World Health Organization (WHO) standards: thin (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5 ≤ BMI ≤ 24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25 ≤ BMI ≤ 29.9 kg/m2), and obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2).

Statistical analysis

Given the complex multi-stage sampling design employed in the NHANES, the selection of weight type adheres to the principle of the least common denominator, with weights calculated based on the specified time period. The variable “wtdrd2” refers to the dietary two-day sample weight, which is utilized because it incorporates dietary information from two consecutive 24-h dietary recalls for analysis. The sampling weights were divided into seven periods for further analysis, thereby ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the resulting estimates. Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviation (mean ± SD) and compared using t tests. Categorical variables were shown as proportions and analyzed using chi square tests. Dietary fiber intake was analyzed both as continuous variable and categorized into quartiles (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4). Based on multivariable logistic regression model, dietary fiber intake was treated as the independent variable, withe sleep disorders serving as the dependent variable. Model fitting was conducted evaluate the association between dietary fiber intake and sleep disorders or types of sleep disorders. Model I had no adjustments, while Model II adjusted for age, race, and gender. Model III expanded on Model II by including adjustments for BMI, education levels, marital status, alcohol drinking, smoke, PIR, diabets, hyperlipidemia, and hypertension. Smooth curve fitting and threshold effect analysis were conducted to examine the nonlinear and dose–response relationships between dietary fiber intake and sleep disorders. Subgroup analysis was conducted to identify factors that may interact with dietary fiber intake. The statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3.

Results

Baseline population

The study involved 2882 participants with an average age of 62.53 ± 0.35 years, of which 52.53% were female. Majority of the participants were non-Hispanic white with education more than high school, 60.34% were married/cohabiting, and most belonged to obese or overweight. While quarter had diabetes without hyperlipidemia, and half of had hypertension. Approximately 26.23% consumed alcohol, and around 55.07% were smokers. Among the participants, 35.70% reported experiencing sleep disorders. Compared with the non-sleep disorder group, participants with sleep disorders were more likely to be younger, married women, smokers, higher BMI, lower PIR, with hyperlipidemia and low dietary fiber, zinc, iron, vitamin A (P < 0.05). The relationship between dietary nutrient intake and sleep disorders

Logistic regression analysis was employed to investigate the relationship between dietary nutrient intake and sleep disorders in cancer survivors. The univariable analysis revealed that cancer survivors who reported sleep disorders had lower intakes of dietary fiber, β-carotene, vitamin A, and potassium compared to those without sleep disorders . Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that dietary fiber intake, total sugar, and zinc were negatively associated with sleep disorders (P < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 1–5). Three models were created using weighted logistic regression analysis after controlling for relevant confounding variables. The results showed that there was no statistical difference in the correlation between sugar and zinc intake and sleep disorders in either continuous or quartile analysis (P > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 6, Supplementary Table 7). However, when dietary fiber intake was treated as continuous variable, Model I indicated protective effect of dietary fiber intake on sleep disorders (odds ratio [OR]: 0.98, 95% confidence interval [CI] : 0.97–0.99, P = 0.005). In Model II, after adjusting for age, sex, and race, the odds of sleep disorders decreased by 2% for each unit increase in dietary fiber intake (OR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99, P = 0.018). Furthermore, in Model III, after controlling for all covariates, the protective effect of dietary fiber intake persisted, reducing the incidence of sleep disorders (OR: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98–0.99, P = 0.044). The restricted cubic spline (RCS) model demonstrated nonlinear relationship between dietary fiber intake and the incidence of sleep disorders (P overall = 0.003, P nonlinear = 0.004). As dietary fiber intake increased, the OR for sleep disorders gradually decreased, reaching minimum value of 26.5. The findings indicated that once dietary fiber intake reaches certain threshold, the prevalence of sleep disorders does not significantly decline with further increases in fiber intake. Conversely, there was slight increase in the prevalence of sleep disorders at higher levels of dietary fiber intake (Fig. 2). This observation may be attributed to various uncontrollable factors, including psychological influences. Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the correlation between dietary nutrient intake and sleep disorders in a cross-sectional analysis of cancer survivors. The study included 2882 cancer survivors, divided into two groups based on self-reported sleep disorders. A total of 27 dietary nutrients were examined, including macronutrients, dietary vitamins, and trace elements. Results from multivariable logistic analysis, adjusting for confounding factors, revealed that among cancer survivors, only dietary fiber intake was associated with sleep disorders. Furthermore, it was suggested that adequate dietary fiber intake may reduce the risk of sleep disorders.

Sleep disorders are prevalent among cancer survivors, with a review of 89 studies indicating that up to 95% of cancer patients experience sleep disorders, potentially as a result of cancer treatment side effects or cancer-related psychological issues17. A cohort study involving 3187 cancer participants from NHANES 2005–2016 revealed significant association between sleep disorders and the risk of cancer mortality (OR: 1.30, 95% CI: 1.02–1.66) after adjusting all variables9. Mogavero et al. emphasized the importance of accurately assessing and promptly addressing sleep disorders in cancer patients to enhance overall health and survival, response to anti tumor therapy, quality of life, and to reduce comorbidities and complications18. Out of the 2882 participants analyzed in this study, 35.67% reported experiencing sleep disorders. Subsequent research revealed that individuals with sleep disorders were more likely to be married women, have smoking habits, possess lower family income levels (lower PIR), suffer from hyperlipidemia, and have deficiencies in fiber, zinc, iron, and Vitamin A. These discoveries offer valuable insights for future investigations into the potential connection between sleep disorders in cancer survivors and these variables. Additionally, they furnish a basis for crafting precise interventions to enhance sleep quality in the future.

The study utilized multivariable logistic regression model to investigate the connection between dietary nutrients and sleep disorders in cancer survivors. The results revealed significant negative association between dietary fiber intake and sleep disorders (P < 0.05), even when considering it as continuous or category variable. This emphasizes the crucial role of dietary fiber in addressing sleep disorders among cancer survivors. Previous studies have indicated that increasing dietary fiber intake in daily dietary habits can be beneficial in addressing sleep disorders, which aligns with our findings. The previous study emphasized the mechanism by which dietary fiber can enhance sleep disorders through the gut-brain axis, while our study specifically examined a diverse cohort of cancer survivors10. It is worth noting that recent studies have not made distinctions among cancer survivors in similar research14,19–21.

Previous studies have explored the health advantages of dietary fiber consumption in at-risk groups, such as individuals who have survived cancer. Kim et al. discovered a modest link between high dietary fiber intake and decreased likelihood of metabolic syndrome in adult cancer survivors22, finding that aligns with our own conclusions. This association could be attributed to an increase in probiotics resulting from the metabolism of dietary fiber. The metabolites produced, along with the rise in short-chain fatty acids in the gut, may help inhibit inflammatory pathways and regulate sleep patterns. In fact, as far back as 2000, Kyuwoong Kim and colleagues recommended that cancer survivors incorporate adequate dietary fiber into their diets for overall health23. Our research delved into the impact of dietary fiber intake on sleep disorders among cancer survivors, revealing that a higher intake of dietary fiber was linked to a reduced risk of sleep disturbances even after adjusting for relevant variables. Hence, employing dietary management to enhance sleep quality represents a viable, convenient, and cost-effective approach24.

This study further analyzed the correlation between various types of sleep disorders (such as sleep apnea, insomnia, restless legs syndrome, and others) and dietary fiber. The results indicated that dietary fiber did not show statistically significant difference (all P > 0.05). However, several epidemiological studies have demonstrated that dietary pattern rich in nutrients like whole grains, vegetables, and fruits can act as a protective factor against insomnia and sleep apnea25–27. The complexity of sleep disorders being influenced by a combination of factors may explain this discrepancy, with dietary fiber being just one of the contributing factors. Therefore, while there may be potential link between dietary fiber intake and sleep disorders, it is important to consider multiple factors in order to understand this relationship. The results from our RCS analysis revealed that after reaching a specific level of dietary fiber intake, the decrease in the prevalence of sleep problems did not continue to show significant improvement. Surprisingly, once dietary fiber intake exceeds a certain threshold, there is a slight uptick in the prevalence of sleep disorders with further increases in dietary fiber.

The current study is subject to limitations. Firstly, the 24-h dietary nutrient intake assessment method used may introduce recall bias and other constraints. Secondly, the cross-sectional design of the study prohibits establishing relationship between dietary nutrition and sleep disorders. Lastly, as a correlational study, there is no intervention or follow-up included.

Conclusion

In summary, this cross-sectional study based on seven cycles of NHANE (2005–2018) data demonstrated nonlinear inverse association between dietary fiber intake and sleep disorders after adjusting for potential confounders. Despite the challenges in treating sleep disorders, this study suggests that appropriate dietary interventions can help reduce sleep disorders in cancer survivors.
