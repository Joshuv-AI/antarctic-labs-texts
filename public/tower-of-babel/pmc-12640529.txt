Predictors of permanent hypoparathyroidism following total thyroidectomy: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Shaza Samargandy, Abdulaziz S Alotaibi, Abdulaziz A Alzahrani, Suhail A Alzahrani, Ahmed H Alghamdi, Abdulrahman M Alotaibi, Hassan A Alsahafi, Ayman A Alsolami, Hani Z Marzouki, Saad Samargandy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND: Hypoparathyroidism (HPT) is a significant potential complication following thyroidectomy. Identifying predictors can aid in risk stratification, management, and potentially prevention.OBJECTIVE: To determine clinical and biochemical predictors of permanent HPT in post-thyroidectomy patients.DESIGN: Single-center, retrospective cohort studySETTINGS: Tertiary care center in Saudi ArabiaPATIENTS AND METHODS: We conducted a retrospective analysis of 1085 patients who underwent total thyroidectomy from 2015 to 2022. Patients who developed postoperative HPT were categorized into transient and permanent HPT groups. Demographic, surgical, and biochemical variables were analyzed. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of permanent HPT, using transient HPT as the reference.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURES: Identification of independent predictors associated with permanent HPT.SAMPLE SIZE: 1085 patients, 264 with postoperative HPTRESULTS: We had 264 patients (24.3%) who developed postoperative HPT: 207 (19.1%) had transient and 57 (5.2%) had permanent HPT. Independent predictors of permanent HPT included thyroid cancer (Odds ratio, OR 2.08, 95% Confidence Interval, CI 1.03–4.17), autoimmune thyroid disease (OR 2.01, 95% CI 1.00–4.08), increased thyroid weight (OR 1.52 per 1 SD increase, 95% CI 1.08–2.14), and longer hospital stay (≥7 days) (OR 3.53, 95% CI 1.87–6.68). Preoperative vitamin D deficiency was identified as the only modifiable risk factor (OR 2.25, 95% CI 1.17–4.32). A postoperative parathyroid hormone (PTH) level ≤1.52 pmol/L within 24 hours was strongly associated with the risk of permanent HPT.CONCLUSION: Permanent HPT is significantly associated with preoperative vitamin D deficiency, thyroid malignancy, autoimmune thyroid disease, and greater thyroid weight. A postoperative PTH level ≤1.52 pmol/L is a reliable early biochemical predictor. Risk-based stratification may support individualized patient management and follow-up planning.LIMITATIONS: This was a single-center, retrospective study, limiting generalizability, in addition to the heterogeneity in surgeon experience and the time of PTH measurements post-operatively.Keywords: Hypoparathyroidism, surgery, complication, thyroidectomy, predictors, vitamin D deficiency, PTH

INTRODUCTION

Hypoparathyroidism (HPT) is an endocrine disorder characterized by low serum calcium (Ca) levels with low or inappropriately low normal circulating parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels.1 One of its most common etiologies is postoperative HPT, which can complicate thyroid surgeries, regardless of surgical expertise. In a meta-analysis of 115 studies, the incidence of transient and permanent HPT following thyroid surgeries ranges from 19%-38% and 0%-3%, respectively. HPT lasting less than six months after total thyroidectomy is considered transient, whereas persistence beyond or more than six months is considered permanent.2

Permanent HPT can lead to chronic hypocalcemia requiring lifelong medications, which in turn affects the patients' quality of life and burdens the health care system. In a multinational study, 27.9% of inadequately controlled patients with permanent HPT had at least one HPT-related hospitalization, and even in adequately controlled HPT patients, 16.3% had one or more HPT-related hospital admissions. Moreover, 47.7% of inadequately controlled HPT patients had at least one HPT-related emergency department visit, and 38.5% of adequately controlled patients still required at least one emergency department visit.3 Previous literature has conflicting data regarding possible factors predicting postoperative HPT, such as preoperative vitamin D deficiency, unintentional removal of one or more parathyroid glands, the extent of thyroidectomy, and operative time.4

Understanding the risk factors of postoperative HPT is crucial for improving outcomes and guiding clinical practice. Therefore, this research aims to identify the factors associated with the development of permanent versus transient HPT in post-thyroidectomy patients.

METHODS

Study design

This retrospective study was conducted at King Abdulaziz University Hospital (KAUH) in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. It involved all adult patients (14 years of age and above per hospital policy) who underwent total thyroidectomy between 2015 and 2022 and developed postoperative HPT. The study was approved by the KAUH Ethics research committee (Reference Number 12–22), January 5, 2023.

Patients and variables

The study included adult patients (≥18 years) who underwent total thyroidectomy, with or without cervical lymph node dissection, and had sufficient follow-up to determine if permanent or transient HPT occurred. Those who underwent isthmectomy, subtotal thyroidectomy, hemithyroidectomy, lobectomy, or recurrent/persistent thyroid cancer surgeries were excluded.

Information extracted from the patients' medical records included demographics such as the patient's age at the time of surgery and gender. The collected clinical variables encompassed the type of HPT postoperatively based on the above definitions. Also, it included pre-operative and day 1 postoperative serum calcium (Ca), PTH, and phosphate levels and whether there was a pre-operative vitamin D deficiency (Vitamin D <50 nmol/L) or not. There was no unified time for postoperative PTH level sample extraction as protocols were variable among surgeons. However, all PTH samples were collected within the first 24 hours post-thyroidectomy. Other details recorded were the length of hospitalization (LOH) in days, the duration of operation in hours, the volume of the thyroid gland post-thyroidectomy (mL), and previous neck surgeries. Patients who underwent lobectomy then completion surgery were considered to have a history of previous neck surgery. Data regarding the presence of thyroid cancer, autoimmune thyroid disease, lymph node dissection, and the number of parathyroid glands resected during surgery, if any, were also recorded. Parathyroid glands were preserved when encountered. Formal parathyroid gland identification and/or re-implantation protocol was not standardized among surgeons. We have also noted those maintained on prophylactic Ca and/or vitamin D analogues in the early postoperative period.

Patients with HPT were identified as having:

Low or inappropriately normal post-operative PTH in the presence of low Ca levels. Samples were collected within the first 24 hours after surgery post-operative day 1 (POD1) for both PTH and calcium.

Low or inappropriately normal post-operative PTH in the presence of low Ca levels. Samples were collected within the first 24 hours after surgery post-operative day 1 (POD1) for both PTH and calcium.

Continued need for >1200 mg Ca and/or active vitamin D analogues supplementation to maintain normocalcemia despite normal PTH.

Continued need for >1200 mg Ca and/or active vitamin D analogues supplementation to maintain normocalcemia despite normal PTH.

They were labelled as transient HPT if recovered in less than six months after the thyroid surgery date, and permanent HPT if it persisted beyond six months.5 The assay used is a chemiluminescent immunoassay (Abbott Architect, intra-assay CV <7%). Normal PTH level is (1.6–6.9 Pmol/L) and normal Ca level is (2.12–2.52 nmol/L).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed with SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary NC) with a significance level set at 0.05. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants' characteristics. Missing covariate data were handled using multiple imputation by fully conditional specification (FCS) under the assumption of missing at random. The imputation model included all analysis variables and auxiliary predictors of missingness. Convergence was assessed using trace plots, and the distributions of imputed and observed values were compared for plausibility. Estimates were pooled across 10 imputed datasets using Rubin's rules to account for within- and between-imputation variability.

Logistic regression models examined univariable and multivariable associations between study predictors and the presence of permanent HPT, with transient HPT being the reference. Variables from the univariable analysis with a P value <.2 were included in the multivariable models. For the multivariable analysis determining the predictors of permanent HPT, we separately examined patient-related and postoperative biochemical predictors. The postoperative biochemical predictors (Ca, phosphate, and PTH) were considered mediator variables in the association between pre-operative patient predictors and permanent HPT and, therefore, were not combined with patient-related factors in the multivariable analysis. Another reason for not incorporating the two groups of predictors in a single model was to mimic the actual sequence of patient assessment upon presentation and preparation for surgery. To identify permanent PTH, optimal cut-off values of postoperative Ca and PTH were obtained using the highest value of the Youden's J statistic and calculated the corresponding cut-off values using the fitted univariable logistic regression model.

RESULTS

We screened 1085 post-total thyroidectomy patients' files. Of those patients, 264 (24.3%) developed postoperative HPT: 207 (19.1%) had transient and 57 (5.2%) had permanent HPT. The majority of patients with postoperative HPT were females (n=229, 87%), and with a mean age of 46.2 years. The majority of the 264 HPT patients had transient HPT (n=207, 78%), while the rest had permanent HPT (n=57, 22%). Most of the patients (n=184, 69.7%) with HPT did not receive prophylactic vitamin D analogues, and 187 (70.8%) patients were not maintained on prophylactic calcium pre-operatively.

Pre-operative vitamin D deficiency was recorded in 75 patients (33%), and autoimmune thyroid disease was found in 52 patients (20%) of the patients. Only 43 patients (17%) of the cohort had incidental parathyroidectomy intra-operatively, and only 72 patients (27%)(27%) had a LOH ≥7 days. For patients with transient HPT, 42 patients (20.1%) had LOH ≥7 days, while in patients with permanent HPT 28 patients (48.6%) had a LOH ≥7 days. The median (Q1, Q3) POD1 PTH in patients with transient HPT was 2.1 Pmol/L (0.9, 4.5), while in patients with confirmed permanent HPT, the median POD1 PTH was 0.9 Pmol/L (0.4, 2.1). In the univariate analysis as seen in Figure 1, we identified the following significant factors for permanent HPT: Pre-operative vitamin D deficiency (Odds ratio, OR=1.99, 95% Confidence Interval, CI 1.1–3.61), presence of autoimmune thyroid disease (OR=1.94, 95% CI 1.03–3.67), thyroid cancer diagnosis (OR=1.87, 95%CI 1.06–3.29), LOH ≥7 days (3.75, 95% CI 2.10–6.73), POD1 Ca level (OR=2.03, 95% CI 1.49–2.78), POD1 phosphate level (OR=1.92, 95% CI 1.25–2.95), POD1 PTH level (OR=3.01, 95% CI 1.84–4.90). We did not find age, gender, or pre-operative calcium level to be significantly related to the risk of permanent HPT, with OR 0.83 per 10 years increase in age (95%CI 0.63–1.09), OR 0.54 (95% CI 0.26–1.15), OR 0.86 per 1mmol/L decrease in Ca level (95%CI 0.58–1.27), respectively. Neither lymph node dissection (OR 0.86, 95%CI 0.44–1.65), nor the previous history of neck surgery (OR 2.21, 95%CI 0.85–5.73), nor longer duration of surgery (OR 0.7, 95%CI 0.37–1.33), or incidental parathyroid gland (PTG) removal (OR 1.24, 95%CI 0.59–2.62) were associated significantly with permanent HPT in univariable analysis.

Table 2 summarizes the multivariable analysis results and details variables falling into patient-related and post-operative biochemical factors. We found pre-operative vitamin D deficiency significantly associated with permanent HPT with an OR of 2.25, 95% CI (1.17, 4.32). Additionally, the presence of thyroid cancer was associated with permanent HPT with an OR of 2.08, 95% CI (1.03, 4.17). Autoimmune thyroid disease had a borderline significance with an OR of 2.01, 95% CI (1.00–4.08). Patients with larger thyroid gland volume were also more likely to develop permanent rather than transient HPT, OR 1.52, 95% CI (1.08, 2.14) per 1 standard deviation (SD) increase in thyroid volume. Regarding the post-operative factors, we found that permanent HPT was 3.53-fold, 95% CI (1.87, 6.68) more likely to occur in patients with a LOH ≥7 days. For post-operative biochemical factors, the odds of permanent HPT increased by 2.48-fold for each 1 SD decrease in post-operative PTH level, with a 95% CI (1.41, 4.36). Similarly, with each 1 SD decrease in calcium level, the odds of permanent HPT increased by 1.73-fold, 95% CI (1.23, 2.42). In the full model, POD1 PTH (OR 2.59, 95% CI 1.31–5.09 per 1 SD decrease) and prolonged hospital stay (OR 2.39, 95% CI 1.11–5.14) remained significant.

In order to identify the optimal cut-off values of post-operative Ca and PTH levels for the identification of permanent HPT in patients who underwent thyroidectomy, we utilized Youden's J statistic, as shown in Figures 2A and 2B. We found that Day 1 post-operative PTH level of 1.52 Pmol/L and below to be the best predictor of permanent HPT. Similarly, Ca levels at Day 1 post-operative period of 1.97 mmol/L and below were associated with identification of permanent HPT. Biochemical factors below the beforementioned suggested cut-off values would increase specificity while sacrificing sensitivity and, hence, the ability to truly predict permanent HPT.

DISCUSSION

In this study, where only cases of post-operative HPT were included, we found that most patients had transient HPT (78%). We identified several pre-operative, intraoperative, and post-operative factors that can predict the risk of permanent HPT. The only modifiable predictor was the pre-operative vitamin D deficiency; otherwise, all the others were not. We also identified the post-operative cut-off PTH value of 1.52 Pmol/L and below to predict the risk of permanent HPT.

In our analysis, we did not find incidental PTG removal to affect the risk of permanent HPT. Similar findings were reported in other studies, but some limited this finding to no more than one PTG accidental excision.6,7 However, other studies with larger sample sizes reported an increased risk of permanent HPT with incidental parathyroidectomy, and the risk increases with the increased number of resected PTGs.8,9 With the introduction of microsurgical techniques and the use of loupes in thyroid surgery, the incidence of inadvertent injury to the PTH glands and their vascular pedicles has decreased.10,11

Pre-operatively, the prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in our cohort (33%) aligns with existing literature, indicating how common it is among Saudis, though the prevalence improved in Saudi Arabia compared to the period before 2008.9 While many studies confirmed the association between pre-operative vitamin D deficiency and immediate post-operative hypocalcemia12,13 few studies have looked into this factor with the risk of permanent HPT. We found in this study that pre-operative vitamin D deficiency is related to long-term HPT. A systematic review and meta-analysis of post-thyroidectomy permanent HPT had similar results only when the pre-operative vitamin D deficiency is severe, that is pre-operative 25(OH)D ≤10 ng/mL.14 Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the link between low vitamin D and permanent HPT. One of those theories is PTG hyperplasia resulting from prolonged periods of low vitamin D, which in turn increases the risk of PTG injury in patients undergoing thyroid surgeries.15–17 When an empirical pre-thyroidectomy high dose of vitamin D was administered in a cohort who were mostly vitamin D repleted, no significant difference was found in the risk of permanent HPT.18

Some of the surgical factors that were proposed in some studies to affect the risk of post-operative HPT are the operative duration and whether lymph node dissection was performed or not, especially central lymph node dissection. There is conflicting evidence for the impact of lymph node dissection on the risk of permanent HPT. Contrary to some studies,19,20 no association was found in our study between lymph node dissection and permanent HPT. Nevertheless, other studies concluded similar results and found lymph node dissection is only associated with an increased risk of transient HPT21. Furthermore, we did not find longer operative time to be related to the risk of permanent HPT. It is speculated in some studies that longer operative duration will reflect on the risk of PTG devascularization and therefore HPT. In our study, the majority of the patients were operated in 1–3 hours. In most of the literature review we conducted, the operative duration was close to our results, with an average of 100–120 minutes.22

Similarly, other studies have reported that longer operative duration increases the risk of early postoperative transient HPT but not permanent HPT. Only when comparing patients with permanent HPT to those with normal postoperative PTH levels did a prolonged operative time appear to increase the risk of long-term hypoparathyroidism and hypocalcemia.23–25

Patient's related factors also play a major role in determining the risk of post thyroidectomy HPT. Patients' gender, age, thyroid volume, and underlying thyroid disease were examined to determine their influence on the risk of post-operative HPT. We found that malignant and autoimmune thyroid disease are linked to permanent HPT. In this subset of patients, particularly Grave's disease (GD) and thyroid cancer, the thyroid tissue is rich in inflammation, fibrosis, and highly vascular, with a resultant increased possibility of bleeding intraoperatively. This, in turn, might obscure the surgical field and complicate the course of the surgery. Furthermore, the prolonged inferior thyroid arterial ligation till ensuring complete excision especially in cancer cases further adds to the risk of transient and permanent HPT.26–28 Hashimoto thyroiditis (HT) despite sharing the inflammatory and fibrosis component in its pathophysiology like GD, yet the risk of transient and permanent HPT is less pronounced. In fact, a meta-analysis of 22 940 post-thyroidectomy patients found no relation between HT and post-operative hypocalcemia.29 Heavier thyroid weight or larger volume correlates significantly with permanent hypocalcemia in our analysis and also in other comparable studies.26,30 Patient's age and gender were not found in the current study, nor were they found in many different studies to be related to post-operative permanent HPT.6,29–32

With post-operative biochemical markers, levels correlating with the risk of permanent HPT are post-operative Ca and PTH levels of 1.97 mmol/L and below and 1.52 pmol/L and below, respectively, collected at the first 24 hours post-surgery. In a systematic review and meta-analysis of 115 post-thyroidectomy studies, Ca<1.88 mmol/l, 24 hours after surgery was associated with persistent HPT six months to one-year post-surgery.33 Similar to our findings, Almaquist et al reported a post-operative PTH cut-off level predicting permanent HPT to be 1.6 Pmol/l, with the highest risk when PTH level one day post-surgery is below 0.7 Pmol/l.34 Other investigators found that an intact PTH concentration of 0.58 Pmol/L measured 1 day post-operatively identified permanent HPT with a sensitivity of 100% and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 100%. Yet, post-operative serum Ca level ≤1.93 mmol/L was only 63.6% sensitive and 78.1% specific as a predictive marker for permanent HPT, which is less helpful than post-operative serum PTH.35 Comparably, a prospective study of seventy post-thyroid surgery patients reached a similar conclusion, where intact PTH concentration 24-hours post-operatively of 0.62 pmol/L or less, was found to be associated with permanent HPT with a NPV of 100%.36 Other researchers utilized a different method to estimate the risk of permanent HPT at which the percentage of post-operative PTH decline was used. A 70% drop in PTH level at the first post-operative day was found to predict permanent HPT with a sensitivity reaching 100% (95%CI: 85.8–100%), but with a lesser specificity of 73% (95% CI: 62.5–83.7%).37

Implications for practice

Preoperative vitamin D deficiency is a modifiable risk factor for permanent HPT. POD1 PTH ≤1.52 pmol/L should be considered high-risk for permanent HPT. Such patients warrant intensified supplementation, prolonged monitoring, and endocrinology follow-up.

LIMITATIONS

While our large-cohort study provides valuable insights, it has some limitations. The retrospective nature of the study, and the single-center design limit generalizability. Not all the surgeries were performed by the same surgeon, and we acknowledge the heterogeneity in surgeon experience. For similar reasons, the timing of PTH measurements post-operatively and the preference regarding administering prophylactic Ca and/or vitamin D analogues varied among surgeons. The absence of systematic parathyroid preservation/autotransplantation records limits interpretability.

CONCLUSION

The findings highlight the importance of comprehensive pre-operative assessment and vigilant post-operative monitoring for those who are most likely to develop transient and persistent HPT, and take clinical measures to mitigate complications of long-term HPT. Implementing these predictors in clinical practice can improve patient outcomes and reduce the incidence of these complications. Prospective studies with standardized protocols and sampling times are warranted.
