Hypertensive patient’s journey in Poland
Authors: Anna Szyndler, Milena Stryczyńska-Lewińska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPoor adherence to medical treatment is a significant public health issue, with nearly 50% of patients failing to comply with prescribed medications. In hypertension (HTN), self-reported non-adherence may also affect over half of patients, leading to severe consequences, including increased morbidity and mortality. Given the multifaceted nature of adherence barriers, a structured approach is needed. This study aims to analyse the Patient Journey in hypertension management within Poland, highlighting experiences, key challenges and faced emotions for both patients and physicians, as well as opportunities for improving adherence.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted using in-depth individual interviews with 12 hypertensive patients from various regions of Poland and 14 medical doctors (primary care physicians and cardiologists) from public and private healthcare facilities. The study examined patient experiences, physician perspectives, and systemic factors that influence adherence.ResultsThe patient journey in HTN management consists of six stages: pre-diagnosis, diagnosis, therapy implementation, first-year therapy, cardiovascular event, and long-term therapy. These stages differ in the barriers and emotions experienced by the patient and the clinician, allowing the focus to be directed on selected aspects that most effectively support the diagnostic and therapeutic process. Key identified patients’ barriers include lack of symptom awareness, insufficient informational support, emotional reactions to diagnosis, polypharmacy, financial constraints, and limited healthcare provider engagement. Physicians face challenges related to time constraints, limited systemic support, and patient reluctance to cooperate.ConclusionsUnderstanding the patient journey provides a structured approach to identifying modifiable adherence barriers. Addressing these factors through patient education, tailored support strategies, and improved physician-patient communication can enhance long-term adherence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13749-8.Keywords: Hypertension, Treatment, Patient education, Communication, Patient experience, Disease management, Diagnostic process, Therapeutic process, Cardiology, Cardiovascular diseases, Patient journey

Introduction

The World Health Organisation (WHO) emphasises that poor adherence to medical treatment poses a significant challenge to public health [1], affecting roughly 50% of patients [1, 2]. In Poland, among patients prescribed hypertensive medicines for the first time, over 24% did not even purchase the prescribed medication [3], and persistence rates vary, depending on age and sex, ranging from 82% in men at 3 months after therapy initiation to 58% among women at 12 months [4]. Lack of adherence remains the main barrier preventing patients from fully benefiting from prescribed medications, leading to medical complications, psychological and social burdens, reduced quality of life, increased risk of treatment resistance, preterm deaths, and inefficiencies in healthcare resource utilisation [1, 2]. The issue of inadequate adherence affects all patient groups, but is particularly significant in chronic diseases, including hypertension [2, 5]. Adherence rates depend strongly on the assessment method: self-reported non-adherence reaches 55.5%, while pill count indicates a lower rate of 46.6% [1]. Poor management of HTN has severe consequences - nearly 10 million deaths in 2019 were directly linked to the condition around the world [1]. Hypertension has the highest mortality among diseases, and cardiovascular disease (CVD) is projected to see the greatest increase in deaths [1]. By 2030, CVD-related deaths are expected to exceed 22 million annually, representing an increase of nearly 5 million compared to 2012 [1]. A ten-year forecast analysing five European countries (Italy, Germany, France, Spain, and England) suggests that improving adherence to antihypertensive therapy to 70% could result in substantial cost savings [1].

According to the WHO [5], factors influencing adherence to medical recommendations can be categorised into five categories: the patient, the disease, the therapy, the healthcare system, and the socio-economic environment. Konstatainou et al. conducted a systematic review [6] encompassing 243 studies on this topic, 12 of which focused on hypertension. In their publication, the factors identified for hypertensive patients included, among others, acceptance of the diagnosis, comorbid illnesses, absence of symptoms, and the complexity of the medication regimen, as well as fear or concerns about the need for treatment, its side effects, or the consequences of the disease.

The number of factors influencing patient adherence identified in the literature may discourage healthcare providers (HCPs) from making efforts to improve adherence. One possible strategy to overcome this complexity is to focus solely on modifiable factors. However, it is essential to acknowledge that some non-modifiable factors, such as patient age, are not necessarily independent risk factors but rather predictors of other correlated, modifiable influences. For instance, the reduced adherence observed among younger patients may arise from personal beliefs or perceptions that discourage medication use, difficulty in accepting diagnosis and the necessity of treatment, internalized stigma associated with the condition, or a lower motivation to prioritize consistent treatment over old lifestyle choices [7]. A lower level of education can limit patients’ understanding of their condition, its treatment and healthcare instructions, including prescription guidelines and medical terminology [8, 9]. Thus, although a physician cannot change a patient’s age or education level, they can improve adherence by addressing factors linked to these non-modifiable characteristics. An alternative approach to managing influencing factors is to leverage the chronic nature of hypertension and identify the most critical factors relevant to the specific stage of the diagnostic and therapeutic process in which the patient currently finds themselves.

Given these challenges, a structured approach is necessary to understand patient behaviour. One such method is the Patient Journey (PJ) approach, which involves collecting and systematically organising information about the actions taken by patients, their environment, and healthcare professionals; the available systemic and personal support options; as well as the challenges, barriers (so-called “pain points”), needs, and emotions of patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers involved in treatment and care. This journey covers the entire process - from early symptoms (pre-diagnosis) through diagnosis and treatment to the end of care. Such an analysis aims to improve understanding of the diagnostic and therapeutic journey from the perspective of all stakeholders, ultimately pinpointing key areas for enhancing adherence to therapeutic advice.

The aim of this article is to describe the Patient Journey in hypertension treatment within the realities of clinical practice in Poland. Among European countries, Poland stands out unfavourably due to its low number of practising physicians per 100,000 inhabitants, the percentage of GDP allocated to healthcare, the high prevalence of hypertension (approximately 11 million or 25% of the population), and the high number of needed medical consultations [10, 11]. These factors indirectly indicate a below-average level of care for patients with chronic diseases, including hypertension.

Methods

For our survey (qualitative research commissioned by Servier Polska sp. z o.o. and carried out by Cognosco Sp. z o.o., July 2024, personal communication), we applied the methodology of Patient Journey (PJ), the concept that maps the interactive touchpoints that the patient experiences, and describes the user experience across the continuum of care. Given the absence of a single, universally accepted definition of the PJ methodology, we adopted the classification outlined in the article by Joseph et al. [12]. The authors present a taxonomy that integrates patient-centred perspectives with different approaches derived from consumer experience mapping. Journey maps can help identify process pain points and highlight opportunities for improvement across settings and contexts. Based on the methodology and results we obtained, we have confirmed and selected the stages of PJ to be: pre-diagnosis, diagnosis, therapy implementation, first year of therapy, cardiovascular event, and long-term therapy. On each of those stages, we defined touch-points (the points where the patients interact with information, health care providers, system) and sources of knowledge, that is problems and barriers influencing adherence.

To explore patients’ perspectives, we conducted a qualitative study using online in-depth, individual interviews and focus groups, among hypertensive patients and physicians.

We used a structured scenario for a qualitative interview, allowing flexibility in style and the responders’ style of speech. Analysis focused on participants’ opinions, beliefs, behaviours, and emotional aspects. All scripts used in the survey were specifically developed for this study and are presented in the supplementary materials (Supplement 1 and 2). All interviews were conducted while maintaining respondents’ anonymity at every stage of implementation and analysis. The interviews were conducted by a moderator-researcher with extensive experience in qualitative research with patients and healthcare professionals.

The qualitative analysis

To ensure the validity and trustworthiness of qualitative data, patients and physicians were recruited using a standardised screening questionnaire. A structured qualitative interview scenario covered issues from both the physician’s and the patient’s perspectives. Themes were identified and triangulated across responders’ groups (patients and physicians) to ensure depth and consistency of interpretation.

To explore participants’ lived experiences and the meanings they ascribe to them, we used Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA). Data analysis followed an iterative process typical of IPA, involving repeated reading of transcripts, making notes on key observations and meanings, identifying emerging themes, and exploring relationships within and between cases. Interviews were transcribed verbatim from audio -video recordings, and included only with participants’ prior consent. Transcriptions were prepared by an experienced transcriber.

The report was prepared by a researcher with a background in qualitative methods and psychology, ensuring a deeper interpretation of the data. The researcher who conducted the interviews subsequently performed an in-depth analysis of the spoken and transcribed content (from the interview transcripts), thereby developing the report and its conclusions.

Participants

Twelve adult patients with diagnosed and treated hypertension participated in the study. All patients participated in an interview aimed at understanding their hypertension path and current management challenges (supp. 1). Patients were recruited in person at the outpatient clinics. The respondents came from different regions of Poland (Gdansk, Warsaw, Bydgoszcz, Poznan, Myslowice, Krakow, Bialystok, Lodz). Fifty per cent of participants were female; 50% were aged over 65, and 50% were aged under 65; 50% were diagnosed 1–3 years ago, and 50% were diagnosed more than 8 years ago.

Additionally, 14 medical doctors, recruited by phone, from various cities (Bialystok, Bydgoszcz, Gdansk, Katowice, Lodz, Poznan, Warsaw, Wroclaw), working in both public and commercial/private healthcare facilities, participated in the study. Online in-depth individual interviews (suppl. 2) were conducted with three primary care practitioners (PCPs) managing patients with hypertension, and four participated in online focus groups. Analogous individual and focus group interviews were conducted with seven cardiologists. All physicians devoted at least 50% of their professional time to the outpatient activities. All interviews were conducted online because digital communication (online communicators) is widely available and used by both physicians and patients (also those over 60 years of age). Moreover, most respondents preferred this method of contact due to its time efficiency and lower health risks associated with avoiding travel. The reason for choosing this method was also cost and comfort, while achieving the same outcome as if the interviews had been conducted in person

Ethical considerations

The Declaration of Helsinki was followed in conducting the research. The research did not qualify as a medical experiment under Polish laws on the Professions of Physicians and Dentists [13] and the Regulation of the Polish Minister of Health on the bioethics committee and the bioethics Appeal Committee [14]; therefore, no ethical approval was required from the local ethics committee.

The interviews were conducted as part of marketing research, following the ICC/ESOMAR International Code on Market, Opinion and Social Research and Data Analytics general principles [15] and the internal ethics code of the marketing company [16]. All participants gave their consent to participate in the interviews and signed the designated contracts. All interviews were anonymous, and no personal data was collected. The results of the interviews did not alter the patient pathway; it did not involve any direct patient intervention or deviations from medical practice. The study did not involve vulnerable populations and did not gather sensitive personal data. The study was conducted with attention to respondents’ psychological well-being.

Results

Patient journey

Pre-diagnosis

The patient journey for hypertension consists of six stages (Fig. 1) [12]. In the pre-diagnosis stage, the patient is unaware of their condition; does not experience symptoms, misinterprets them or minimises their significance, “I thought that I was healthy and nothing could get me, but it appears something completely opposite” - male, 69 yrs. Some patients experience sudden spikes in blood pressure with associated symptoms, prompting them to visit the emergency department, a primary care physician (PCP), or a cardiologist: “I was fine, everything was ok till the moment I felt dizzy” – female, 56 yrs. Blood pressure is measured incidentally, for example, during a social visit to someone already diagnosed with hypertension. At this stage, the patient’s unmet needs include routine preventive screenings at the PCP level, a lack of awareness regarding symptoms that could indicate hypertension and its complications, no habit of measuring blood pressure, a lack of belief that hypertension could affect them, and associating hypertension with old age or a lack of vitality. Typical emotions at this stage include the minimization of symptoms and the repression of associated feelings, eventually giving way to fear that drives the patient to seek medical help.

Pain points of the diagnostic and therapeutic process in patients with hypertension in Poland

At this stage, the physician’s actions include measuring blood pressure in the office, recommending home measurements, providing instructions or educational materials on proper measurement techniques, and offering initial information about the diagnostic process and possible diagnoses, “During the first visit, I introduce elements of non-pharmacological treatment for hypertension. […] I recommend home blood pressure monitoring and order tests to assess risk factors.” – cardiologist. The physician’s emotions include irritation or surprise at the patient’s behaviour, which they perceive as inappropriate.

Diagnosis

During the diagnostic stage, patients measure their blood pressure at home based on the physician’s recommendations, most often from their primary care physician (PCP). Patients undergo diagnostic tests and attempt to implement changes in accordance with the physician’s recommendations and/or their own beliefs. Patients with high initial blood pressure also begin taking medications prescribed by the doctor. Patients experience shock, “I was totally surprised, and I asked if I had to take those pills for the rest of my life?”, male 69 yrs., mobilization, breakdown, sadness, “I started thinking that maybe I could go to the hospital”, female, 50 yrs. - resignation, quiet acceptance, or relief. Some patients begin actively seeking information (primarily online) and/or emotional support from close family members or friends. During their independent search for information online, patients often encounter information overload, finding contradictory, “If I look at the internet some information is wrong, false but there is also some really good, accurate ones”, male, 69 yrs., controversial, and unreliable content, which discourage them from seeking further information, “On each page there was something different, and there was that it causes those horrible things […] I stopped looking”, female, 50 yrs. A key barrier, apart from the emotional challenges, is insufficient informational support from the PCP, “no, no. my doctor didn’t give me any information to read” – female, 50 yrs.

The physician’s role at this stage is to perform recommended procedures within their practice, including diagnostic tests to identify potential causes and complications of the disease. They also provide the patient with information about the diagnosis after initially signalling the possible issue and preparing them, “I don’t want to scare them. I want to give them some time to tame it, give them a chance. If they got scared, they would reject, cancel it, and wouldn’t measure blood pressure. But I don’t want to distance them, I wish to kind of prepare them for the future management”, PCP. The information about the diagnosis should be supplemented with details on the disease, treatment, and non-pharmacological recommendations, along with educational materials, “You have to repeat, repeat, repeat. […] When someone is diagnosed with hypertension for the first time, they definitely won’t remember even half of what they’re told.”, PCP.

The challenge faced by the physician at this stage is the unpredictable patient reaction to the diagnosis, “Young people, often initially are in some kind of denial; immediately associate it with the idea that they’ll have to take medication for the rest of their lives, that it’s a lifelong diagnosis. Therefore, there are a few patients who initially don’t fully believe the diagnosis” - cardiologist. It is challenging to manage the patient’s reluctance to pharmacological treatment, “Some, [patients] they do not want to take drugs. At the beginning, they say they will change something in their life. Some accept medicines straight away, and some say they will buy the medicines, but they have to think about whether to take them or not”, cardiologist, especially when time is limited during the visit, and a tight schedule makes it difficult to schedule follow-up visits optimally. As a result, another barrier emerges: the lack of focus on providing real support for lifestyle changes beyond merely giving information, addressing patients’ insufficient motivation, and sometimes relying on patients’ fear for motivation. Despite these challenges, the situation is not perceived as problematic. The physician at this stage typically has confidence in the diagnosis, with no doubts arising from their trust in their knowledge and experience in treating hypertension, as well as the belief that they can convey the diagnosis in a way suited to the patient and handle their reaction effectively, “Everything can be presented in a way that motivates patients to systematically take their medicines and care for their health. However, there is a fraction that will do nothing more than take pills”, cardiologist.

Implementation of therapy

At this stage, the patient attempts to follow the recommendations with varying degrees of success. They maintain a blood pressure diary and attend follow-up appointments. The patient seeks ways to live with the disease – they may work on making appropriate, authentic lifestyle changes or, conversely, on cognitively and emotionally marginalising the issue,”..the diet was difficult at the beginning, because I really like meat and spicy food”, male, 56 yrs. Adherence to recommendations may be limited due to a lack of knowledge, skills, motivation, acceptance of the disease (remaining in denial, repression, or rebellion), “I had my doubts. After all it’s all new, it is an interference in one’s body and it may cause stuff”, male, 69 yrs., insufficient support from the physician or others, in establishing appropriate habits, “… at the beginning, it happened [forgetting drugs] … I hoped that maybe I didn’t have to take the medicines”, male, 56 yrs., and a lack of accompaniment in the process of implementing changes. The presence of other conditions perceived as more critical, burdensome, and limiting (e.g., mobility issues), “I just had hip surgery, an endoprosthesis […] so I was looking for more information on my hip”, male, 72 yrs., or a lack of financial resources can also hinder adherence. Depending on the patient’s psychological profile and the support they receive from the physician and their close environment, “I basically got support, especially from my wife […], it is the closest support that is the most important while receiving any important information”, male, 62 yrs., they may experience various emotional states, ranging from positive mobilization (viewing the situation as a challenge or signal that the patient has been living unhealthily), “It is not in my character. If I have something, I need to find out everything about it – what it is and how to live with it”, female, 74 yrs., to quiet acceptance, resignation, emotional repression, or denial, “When I saw others, and that they have bigger issues than me, it seems my hypertension is not such a big deal”, male, 69 yrs.

At this stage, the PCP works to establish the optimal treatment plan; schedules follow-up visits at least once a month; recommends continued home blood pressure measurements, and reviews the results during subsequent visits. Additional diagnostics are ordered to explore the causes of hypertension and any possible complications. As necessary, the PCP refers the patient to specialists. The physician may also provide patient education and/or offer emotional support, “My doctor tries to motivate me to take care of myself even better”, male, 56 yrs.

However, cardiologists rarely see patients who have had their diagnosis and treatment started recently. Their role is to focus on expanding diagnostics to identify the causes of cardiovascular diseases and on establishing therapies for complications. They support the PCP as part of a coordinated care approach. Both types of physicians (PCP and specialist) educate the patient about possible reactions to treatment, raise awareness of concerning symptoms, “You always have to explain to the patient what their condition involves. […] That hypertension isn’t just a number on the monitor, but a disease affecting the whole body.”, cardiologist, and present steps to take in problematic situations, “At the initial stage, when the diagnosis is made, it’s definitely worth educating the patient so they know how to act and what to do when their blood pressure is high.”, PCP.

Barriers for the PCP at this stage include a lack of time for the patient, physician workload, and the absence of support from other healthcare workers in educating the patient, “There’s a bit of a shortage of educators. […] Even the nurse we have here already has plenty of other responsibilities.”, PCP. Cardiologists face challenges such as limited organisational capacity for frequent patient follow-ups, “At the outpatient clinic, we have a very large number of patients. Appointment dates are quite far apart.”, cardiologist, systemic restrictions on visit frequency, and referrals of patients without proper indications. At this stage, physicians focus primarily on pharmacological treatment. Non-pharmacological interventions are considered desirable but also challenging to implement, leading to a belief that a policy of small changes is the most appropriate for patients to succeed, “Setting too many goals—or goals that are too ambitious—for a patient during the first or second visit is almost certainly a recipe for failure.”, PCP. With standard therapeutic procedures, physicians feel competent in pharmacotherapy and patient management; they understand the patient’s concerns and exhibit patience, especially towards older patients. Emotional challenges arise with problematic patients, where establishing the optimal therapy is more difficult. In cases of non-adherence, physicians may feel surprised, impatient, or occasionally resentful due to the patient’s questioning of the diagnosis or recommendations, “In my practice, I try to avoid situations where the doctor cares more about the patient’s health than the patient does about their own.”, PCP; “If someone considers themselves an expert in everything—including medicine—and comes just to hear another opinion, but deep down believes they know best and will treat themselves anyway, then that’s probably the core issue. […] If I feel that someone doesn’t trust me, then … there’s no need to continue the cooperation.[…] I suggest that the patient change their doctor”, cardiologist.

First year of therapy

The patient achieves stabilization in both their condition and daily life, “Now, I have a peace of mind […] everything works fine. […] My blood pressure is almost normal, and I have it under control with drugs”, female, 74 yrs. They develop new habits through trial and error or develop unhealthy rationalizations. Occasionally, they may forget to take their medication, but over time, they establish routines and use aids to help them follow recommendations. Some patients, after an initial period of concern about their illness, abandon treatment or their decision to make lifestyle changes, “I have always thought that those pills have some side effects”, male, 56 yrs. Patients who find it difficult to adhere to medical recommendations (e.g., related to obesity or strong nicotine addiction) may lose motivation, especially without sufficient support, “I have one addiction - smoking. And it is not good, I tried to drop it a little. Everyone is addicted to something apparently”, male, 55 yrs. Once blood pressure is stabilized, the patient may believe they have been cured, making pharmacotherapy seem unnecessary. A barrier at this stage is the lack of personal contact with the doctor: remote visits involving the continuation of prescriptions or issuing prescriptions for a year, as they do not provide the required support for treatment maintenance. The emotions experienced by the patient vary, depending on their approach to the illness and the degree of adherence to recommendations. This may include satisfaction with a new lifestyle and acceptance of the illness or denial and marginalization of the hypertension issue, as well as feelings of failure and dissatisfaction with themselves, “When I saw that everything goes well – I relaxed”, male, 69 yrs.

During this period, the PCP schedules frequent follow-up visits until the blood pressure is stabilized, continually educating the patient on both pharmacotherapy and non-pharmacological methods. If there are problems with treatment implementation, suspicion of co-occurring cardiovascular diseases, or if the patient requests it, a referral to a cardiologist is issued. For patients with stabilized blood pressure, the emphasis on home blood pressure measurements may be reduced. For patients without risk factors who cooperate, the educational content may be scaled down. The cardiologist is involved in managing patients with established cardiovascular problems. They usually manage patients with occasional visits, prescribing pharmacotherapy for extended periods and directing continued treatment to the PCP, “If there’s no need and the blood pressure is well controlled, I absolutely recommend continuing treatment with the primary care physician.”, cardiologist; “In my case, it’s more of a consultation, and then I refer the patient back to their regular doctor for further care.”, cardiologist. The cardiologist focuses on providing information and is less involved in motivational efforts. A barrier at this stage is the lack of real support for lifestyle changes, patients’ lack of persistence and cooperation in therapy, and the lack of focus from doctors on working with non-problematic patients (limiting educational, verifying, and motivating action), “About 10% of patients want to change their lifestyle, but most claim that when it comes to physical activity or exercise, they have arthritis changes, or they’re older and not in good shape.”, PCP. The emotions experienced by the physician include satisfaction, calmness, and routine, particularly in cases of cooperative patients with stabilised blood pressure; possibly a rationalised, mild uncertainty when changing treatment. Negative emotions occasionally arise in particularly drastic cases of non-adherence, especially with regard to pharmacotherapy. Patients who undermine the doctor’s authority through their behaviour may cause discouragement, “There are days when I feel like I should be tearing my hair out before noon.”, cardiologist, a decrease in engagement and impatience, “I’m simply running out of strength. […] You just can’t work with people like that at all.”, cardiologist.

Cardiovascular event

As a result of a cardiovascular incident, the patient is hospitalized. After hospitalization, they are more likely to implement lifestyle recommendations. The experience of the incident shifts the patient’s priorities, and they find motivation to follow recommendations and seek ways to support non-pharmacological treatment, “Actually, I am very glad for what has happened, with this shift in my life, because I simply became a different person”, female, 71 yrs. Loved ones become more involved due to anxiety related to hospitalisation, “My wife cheered for me, to go to the doctor […] I can count on my wife. She reminded me about the pills, not to overuse salt”, male, 56 yrs. Patients need emotional support and a sense of being cared for; however, this need is not always met. Hypertension may be seen as a less significant cardiovascular issue, and its pharmacotherapy may be regarded as supplementary to the “main” cardiovascular disease. As a result, blood pressure medications may be treated by the patient as dietary supplements. Patients may feel scared, “They feel overwhelmed with the need to take drugs for the rest of their life”, cardiologist; threatened, or helpless; they may also be motivated to lead a healthier lifestyle,”I sleep well, I like taking walks, longer walks especially, for I really enjoy walking”, male, 54 yrs., or feel more secure with specialist care (cardiologist, hospital outpatient clinic).

The PCP intensifies care for the patient by taking more aggressive therapeutic actions, increasing monitoring, education, and motivation, and referring patients to a cardiologist. The cardiologist may modify the pharmacotherapy. Some doctors may have concerning tendencies to rely on the patient’s fear, frightening them with complications and consequences of the cardiovascular incident, for motivational support, “The patient has to be aware about the dangers of neglecting treatment”, cardiologist; “I explain the consequences of the disease, and what might happen if they wouldn’t take their drugs”, cardiologist. The PCP may feel anxious about the patient’s health, become more vigilant, and become more involved in their care. Cardiologists tend to show more calmness and emotional distance.

Long-term therapy

After many years of therapy, the patient reaches a stage where they consider the disease to be manageable, stable, and well-controlled, “Now I have such a good [blood pressure] control”, female, 71 yrs., which can lead to a recurrence of incorrect behaviours and a weakening of adherence to other recommendations followed at the beginning of therapy, despite regular medication use. The patient may stop regular blood pressure measurements or perform them incorrectly, visit the doctor less frequently, and continue obtaining prescriptions through teleconsultations, “At the beginning, I was writing down my BP, but since there is no big difference between them, and they seem stable, there is no need to measure and write them down any more”, male, 67 yrs. They may rationalize conscious deviations from recommendations, “If I have a drink, let’s say at 5 p.m., it does not affect me”, female, 71 yrs. They are now less interested in expanding their knowledge about the disease, even though progressing comorbidities may require some recommendations to be re-evaluated, “I generally think that now, at this stage, I know a lot […], so I don’t look for anything new, not to find me something again”, female, 71 yrs. On the other hand, emerging comorbidities may also serve as motivation to make lifestyle changes. At this stage, patients do not experience significant emotions related to the disease and do not have major emotional needs. The exception is those with additional cardiological burdens, who may experience anxiety related to potential serious complications such as heart attacks or strokes.

On the physician’s side, there is a decrease in the intensity of patient education, assuming that all necessary information has already been provided, “Honestly? He (physician) asks me if everything is fine, if I have high blood pressure episodes. If I say I’m fine - he just prescribes me drugs and that’s it”, male, 54 yrs. The patient’s general condition is monitored periodically. Any changes in therapy or intensification of treatment occur as needed; sometimes this results in more frequent visits, but usually does not cause significant problems for either the doctor or the patient. For patients attempting to reduce risk factors for cardiovascular incidents, it is essential to motivate them and appreciate their efforts, “I start praising him a lot. I say things like, ‘You look great, this is wonderful! Please tell me how you managed it, because honestly, I’m truly impressed.”, PCP. Barriers at this stage include acceptance of the lack of lifestyle changes in some patients, possible difficulties in motivating patients to take medication, and a lack of cooperation between doctors from different specialities and/or other healthcare professionals on promoting patients’ adherence to hypertension treatment. Physicians primarily feel satisfaction with patients who have been successfully motivated to make changes, as well as calmness and a routine approach with non-problematic patients. Regarding patients whose condition is deteriorating, anxiety may arise, especially in cases of complications such as cardiovascular incidents, “It’s a very unpleasant moment when complications arise, because I was treating the patient, right? And now something has come up, something has started happening.”, cardiologist.

Discussion

In Poland, the healthcare system is based on primary care, with general practitioners (GPs) playing a crucial role in screening, diagnosing, and initiating treatment. They are also responsible for the prevention at the individual patient level, and continuation of an efficient treatment. After the diagnosis, patients with difficult-to-control or secondary hypertension are referred to specialised hypertension units, often located within larger hospitals or academic centres, which offer advanced diagnostics and personalised management. Emergency rooms (ERs) may also play a role in hypertension detection, as elevated blood pressure is commonly one of the reasons for, or an additional finding during, visits for other acute conditions, which can sometimes lead to a new diagnosis. According to the meta-analysis, 0.5% of all acute admissions to the ER were due to hypertensive emergencies, whereas the Greek data report a prevalence of 0.91% of all ER visits during a year (2017/18) attributed to hypertensive emergencies or urgencies. [17, 18].

In a Polish study based on a single-centre experience, 19,4% of all patients presenting to the ER with elevated blood pressure had not been diagnosed with hypertension before the incident, of whom 55,5% were confirmed hypertensive with ABPM after discharge [19].

The coordination between GPs, hypertension specialists, and hospital-based care should ensure continuity of treatment, supported by national registries and guidelines that promote standardised management across different levels of care.

Achievements and challenges in adherence

In a WHO 2003 report on long-term treatment adherence, the process has been described as “the extent to which a person’s behaviour – taking medication, following a diet, and/or executing lifestyle changes, corresponds with agreed recommendations from a health care provider” [5]. The authors of the report emphasised the active role and responsibility of the patient in managing chronic diseases. It also indicated five different dimensions that influence adherence (health system, economic, patient, disease and treatment related). However, using this definition, the precise description of the process of following a recommendation and engaging in one’s own disease management may be challenging.

Therefore, a new taxonomy was proposed to describe patients’ adherence at different stages of treatment (initiation, implementation, and discontinuation), as well as persistence vs. non-persistence. It also points to the process of managing adherence by health system providers, patients, and their support networks [20].

In the last 20 years, significant progress has been made in improving adherence to recommendations. The surveyed experts (5 from the EU and 5 from the USA) highlighted several key achievements in this field: addressing economic barriers in health policies; simplifying treatment plans and creating systems to track adherence data; focusing more on patient-related reasons for non-adherence and using patient-reported outcomes; recognising disease-related challenges and promoting whole-person, team-based care; making patients central to clinical decisions; and using digital tools and mobile apps to support medication adherence. [21]. Despite this, there are still areas requiring further development, as a 2014 Cochrane review found that existing strategies to enhance medication adherence for chronic conditions are generally complex and have limited effectiveness [22].

The role of emotions and patient education

Our study confirmed some of the factors previously described as common among patients worldwide [23–29] (Fig. 2). Furthermore, it highlighted the significant role of emotional factors, which is not surprising considering that emotions, alongside knowledge, are the source of human attitudes. The importance of human emotions in influencing their health behaviour and choices was highlighted in a recent paper by Burnier et al. [30]. The conclusion of the patients’ answers to the question concerning their drug-taking behaviours was that the risk of non-adherence is the highest in patients with a high emotional and QoL risk score. Those patients were also less eager to discuss their behaviour with their health providers. Patients at higher risk of non-adherence expressed a greater need for more information on hypertension and its complications, side effects, lifestyle measures, and practical guidance on how to take their medications. Before the patient can take constructive actions, they must first address their emotions related to the diagnosis and find the motivation to start making changes. In Bournier’s paper, the patients prone to non-adherent behaviours were slightly younger, and their burden of treatment was higher; they also presented with higher levels of stress. In our study, we also identified potential actions to address certain issues (Fig. 2).

Patients’ emotions and actions at each step of the hypertension patient journey

The importance of knowledge is unquestionable: patients who possess a thorough understanding of their illness and its potential complications are 7 times more likely to consistently follow their prescribed medication regimen [31]. Ongoing education for both patients and doctors is necessary, as it results from the continuous development of medical science and evolving needs over time. It is also important to note that the patient may believe, after years of living with the disease, that they have acquired sufficient knowledge and skills to act appropriately, which may be a false belief. Therefore, this education should not only include updates on strictly clinical aspects but also focus on identifying patients’ misconceptions about treatment [32]. For example, a person diagnosed with hypertension who also has a family history of cardiovascular disease may develop the perception that a heart attack is inevitable, leading them to neglect preventive measures, such as initiating prescribed medication [2]. Another popular myth is the belief that the patient can notice the symptoms of high blood pressure or that high blood pressure is caused by stress [32]. In our findings, as well as in publications, patients express a strong need for further education, especially those with a high risk of nonadherence and a high emotional risk score [30]. It is also noteworthy that among patients seen in ED for hypertensive urgencies or emergencies, anxiety and stress play a major role as a probable cause of sudden blood pressure rise, with the rates reaching 51.7% among women and 37.9% among men in a Greek registry [18].

In many healthcare systems, including Poland’s, educational interventions may seem challenging due to their time-consuming nature. On the other hand, the involvement of other members of the healthcare system in patients’ education and care may positively influence patients’ experience [33]. The wider health education at all levels, in schools as well as social or public media, may positively influence attitudes towards prevention, early diagnosis and treatment persistence, which may help increase the rates of adherence on every level, since almost one fourth of Polish patients do not fulfil their first in life hypotensive drug prescription [3]. Also, implementing prevention programs at the primary care level, such as Moje Zdrowie in Poland, may increase the rates of hypertension diagnosis and improve the efficacy of treatment.

Therefore, the adoption of new technologies could help overcome the diagnosis and persistence difficulty.

New technologies for improving adherence

The use of new technologies is a very complex area and includes issues related to drug design (innovative drug design, such as single pill combinations or long-acting drugs), device design (trackable pills and smart drug packaging that assess adherence and share data or alert a patient and/or caregiver; wearable sensors), as well as the implementation of software for healthcare providers and patients. Mobile applications play a significant role in this area, as the percentage of Polish patients who actively use the Internet increased from 10% in 2007 to 50% in 2017; a similar increase was observed in most European countries [34]. Mobile apps and web-based tools can serve as: a knowledge repository, schedule organizers, reminders, alerting the patient/caregiver/healthcare professional in case of non-adherence, automated drug renewal options, and a gamification tool [35]. Preliminary results are available on the effectiveness of technology-based behavioural interventions. Key elements, as evidenced by improvements in medication adherence rates across various conditions, encompassed patient education on both the illness and prescribed treatment, emphasizing the importance of adherence, and incorporating reinforcement strategies or motivational messaging [6]. These components were predominantly conveyed through digital means, particularly via SMS or text messages, which served as reminders, educational tools, and sources of encouragement. The primary focus of these interventions was to address common obstacles such as forgetfulness and limited health literacy regarding the condition and its treatment.

The use of mobile apps for hypertension was examined in a qualitative substudy evaluating such an app in Sweden [36]. Through the use of the system, patients developed a clearer understanding of how their lifestyle influenced their blood pressure, which in turn inspired them to adopt healthier habits. As they became more knowledgeable about their own BP management, they approached follow-up consultations well-informed and ready to engage actively in their care. This shift enabled healthcare professionals to adopt a more supportive role, guiding rather than directing the conversation. No longer the sole gatekeepers of medical data and expertise, clinicians instead acted as advisors, tailoring their specialised knowledge to meet individual patient needs. As a result, both parties experienced a more balanced, collaborative consultation dynamic, laying the foundation for a stronger, more effective partnership. These findings were confirmed in other studies, also showing that active patient involvement in the therapeutic process improves adherence and treatment outcomes [37]. A systematic review of five studies evaluating different telemedicine techniques showed a decrease in blood pressure of 10.4/4.8 mm/Hg (SBP/DBP, respectively) [38]. Another similar review showed a reduction of systolic blood pressure of 6.49 mmHg (−8.52 to −4.46 mmHg) and diastolic blood pressure of 3.30 mmHg (95% CI: −4.58 to −2.01) compared to standard care [39]. These results are encouraging in the short-term perspective, but healthcare providers should consider the limitations of digital interventions, including the lack of direct human interaction [40]. Another challenge for system designers is to develop user-friendly technological solutions that reduce barriers for older and less digitally literate patients, thereby overcoming these barriers and facilitating better collaboration with patients.

In some countries, pharmaceutical companies play a role in educating patients about the importance of adherence. For example, a pharma-sponsored patient management program has been launched to help individuals monitor their blood pressure, follow prescribed medication regimens, and track progress [2]. This program also includes educational materials, dietary plans, and exercise routines to support overall health. Another initiative is an online health education platform that provides over 100 customizable resources [2]. Its goal is to equip individuals with chronic illnesses with self-management tools and facilitate closer collaboration with healthcare providers. However, there is limited published data on the effectiveness or cost-effectiveness of such initiatives. Perhaps a beneficial way to optimise costs with satisfactory efficiency is to use social media, particularly YouTube, and the first such attempts have already been made [41].

The role of doctor-patient communication

A separate field requiring close attention is physicians’ education. Our study demonstrated how variable and intense emotions arise during the therapeutic process, affecting both the patient and the physician. Unmanaged emotions can create a feedback loop, turning misunderstandings into conflicts and conflicts into discouragement and neglect of the therapeutic process. In the study by Burnier et al. [30], negative feedback about the therapy was the reason for non-adherence in 3.9% of patients in the overall study population and in 6.1% of patients in the high-risk subgroup for non-adherence. In turn, the lack of appropriate communication regarding the patient’s financial capabilities resulted in non-adherence in 2.4% and 3.1% of patients, respectively [29]. The effectiveness of doctor-patient communication depends not only on the patient’s level of education and health literacy but also on the physician’s demeanour, tone of voice, nonverbal communication, and psychological and cultural factors [42]. General communication skills play a fundamental role, including active listening, message formulation, nonverbal communication, empathy, assertiveness, and the ability to overcome difficulties [42]. Physicians may struggle to maintain a balance between empathy and compassion while navigating systemic challenges, as heavy workloads and limited time can hinder meaningful discussions that influence treatment outcomes [43]. A systematic review focused on barriers to doctor-patient communication revealed that the most common influence techniques used by physicians include presenting various scenarios of possible outcomes depending on treatment adherence; invoking fear by highlighting the worst-case scenario; using comparisons and analogies to visualize the problem; reducing tension through reassurance and goal-oriented motivation; introducing one change at a time; using simple language, positive nonverbal cues, and providing quick feedback [43]. A meta-analysis of this review’s data found that a lack of communication training for physicians is associated with a 12% reduction in patient adherence to medical recommendations. In comparison, poor communication increases this risk by 19% [42]. In another similar meta-analysis, clinician education resulted in a reduction of SBP by 6.09 mmHg (95% CI: 2.32–9.85), while patient education and patient reminder systems decreased DBP by 4.86 mmHg (95% CI: 0.88–8.83) [44] Poor communication, can lead patients for example to conceal the true reasons for non-adherence to medical recommendations, making it impossible for healthcare providers to take adequate corrective measures [2].

In particular, intentional concealment of the truth from the physician or difficulty coping with certain factors may manifest as justifying non-adherence to medical recommendations by claiming to have “forgotten” [2], as also reported by patients in our study. This is further supported by the fact that interventions based solely on reminders have not proven effective [45]. This highlights the crucial role of clinicians in developing communication skills. The specific scope of these competencies has been discussed by other authors in their review [42, 46].

Based on our work, we can also suggest actions that may improve the experience for both patients and physicians, thereby increasing satisfaction and the effectiveness of interactions. On the healthcare providers’ side, investing in patient-centred communication [5], offering health workers opportunities to improve their medical communication skills, and implementing a more structured follow-up in hypertension care may facilitate cooperation.

Additionally, prioritising communication skills in university curricula may enhance cooperation and adherence in the future [47].

Limitations of the study, future research and suggested directions

Our study has several limitations. The most notable one seems to be the sample size, which limited the natural variability of the participants, stemming from individual psychological differences. We fully acknowledge that the relatively small sample size, drawn largely from urban centres, limits the generalizability of the findings—particularly with regard to rural and underrepresented populations. However, it was comparable to other studies of this kind [48]. Despite our best efforts to achieve as much diversity in the study sample as possible across controlled variables, we cannot exclude the possibility that results from a different, equally sized respondent group might differ due to participants’ varying personal experiences, and different systems of care. Additionally, we did not control for all potential variables that could affect the responses. For example, by recruiting only one doctor from the city, we did not account for possible differences between districts, which could stem from local socioeconomic and demographic variations within different areas of the same town. However, in our study, we collected qualitative information, and any future subsequent research may use other quantitative methods, i.e. translate the aspects collected now into the scale of the phenomenon.

The study involved only doctors, and data from other healthcare professionals, such as physiotherapists, nurses, or dietitians, were reported indirectly. The study was conducted according to the principles of anonymity, but in qualitative research involving direct contact, we cannot exclude bias arising from difficulties in disclosing data that participants perceive as controversial or sensitive.

Our study drew on insights from patients and physicians to understand the lived experience of managing chronic disease. While the scope of this work was necessarily limited, these findings highlight important aspects of patient and physician perspectives, as well as the experiences and difficulties faced by both parties at different stages of the diagnostic and therapeutic process, that deserve further investigation. We agree that future studies should build on this by including larger and more diverse populations—patients, physicians, and other health care providers—and complementary approaches, such as surveys, to extend the reach and generalizability of these insights.

In conclusion, the emotions and difficulties faced by patients and doctors vary at different stages of treatment, which allows, in the context of limited resources, to focus attention on the aspects that are critical for adherence to recommendations at a given moment. Further studies could focus on more precise patient profiling to continue personalising treatment and on assessing the impact of patient-tailored interventions on therapeutic adherence.
