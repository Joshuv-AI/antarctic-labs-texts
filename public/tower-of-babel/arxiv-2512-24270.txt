Strategic Network Abandonment
Sandro Claudio Lera, Andreas Haupt

Abstract
Socio-economic networks, from cities and firms to collaborative projects, often appear resilient for long periods before experiencing rapid, cascading decline as participation erodes. We explain such dynamics through a framework of strategic network abandonment, in which interconnected agents choose activity levels in a network game and remain active only if participation yields higher utility than an improving outside option. As outside opportunities rise, agents exit endogenously, triggering equilibrium readjustments that may either dissipate locally or propagate through the network. The resulting decay dynamics are governed by the strength of strategic complementarities, measuring how strongly an agent’s incentives depend on the actions of others. When complementarities are weak, decay follows a heterogeneous threshold process analogous to bootstrap percolation: failures are driven by local neighborhoods, vulnerable clusters can be identified ex ante, and large cascades emerge only through bottom-up accumulation of fragility. When complementarities are strong, departures propagate globally, producing rupture-like dynamics characterized by metastable plateaus, abrupt system-wide collapse, and limited predictive power of standard spectral or structural indicators. The comparative effective of intervention depends on the strength of complementarity as well: Supporting central agents is most effective under strong complementarities, whereas targeting marginal agents is essential when complementarities are weak. Together, our results reveal how outside options, network structure, and strategic interdependence jointly determine both the fragility of socio-economic networks and the policies required to sustain them.



The abandonment of socio-economic systems often begins slowly and then accelerates rapidly, mirroring the well-known observation that change happens gradually, then suddenly
[
12
]
.
Cities, firms, or collaborative projects can experience long periods of slow decline, with individual departures appearing inconsequential, followed by a sudden cascade that rapidly dismantles the socio-economic network.
Understanding when gradual decay escalates into sudden collapse is a central challenge for predicting the stability of social and economic systems
[
15
]
.

Complex networks are often analyzed through the lens of accumulation and infection, emphasizing how interactions emerge and reinforce one another over time.
For network decline, for example, the departure of individual agents is modelled to directly produce cascades depending on connectivity and heterogeneity
[
17
,
24
,
21
]
.
The accumulation perspective on network decline has been especially influential in the study of infrastructure systems, such as power grids or communication networks, where random breakdowns or targeted attacks can precipitate large-scale failures
[
2
,
10
,
11
]
.
In many socio-economic systems, however, decline unfolds without explicit accumulation: agents disengage voluntarily as participation becomes less attractive relative to outside opportunities.

Work in economics and sociology has emphasized that participation in networks is often a matter of strategic choice
[
19
,
6
,
9
,
3
]
.
Network games have demonstrated that equilibrium activity levels depend strongly on the position of agents in the network,
with outcomes closely linked to measures such as Bonacich centrality
[
5
,
8
,
7
,
14
]
.
However, these approaches typically analyze static equilibria rather than the dynamic unraveling of networks through iterative exit.
Meanwhile, empirical studies of urban decline and organizational collapse document how the departure of some participants can reduce the attractiveness of remaining,
ultimately leading to a dynamics of rapid depopulation or project abandonment
[
15
,
4
]
.

To account for such dynamic decay, we study the
strategic abandonment
of networks governed by economic agents.
We model a network of agents who remain active only if their internal (equilibrium) utility exceeds that of an outside option that is increasing in value.
The network is initialized with a fixed structure, and agents choose activity levels through a linear-quadratic game, leading to a unique equilibrium whose utilities are closely related to Bonacich centrality
[
5
]
.
As external opportunities gradually improve, the least-benefited agents exit first.
Their departure reduces the utility of remaining agents, potentially triggering further exits.
This process repeats iteratively, producing either slow, incremental decline or sudden cascades that rapidly dismantle the network.

Our framework provides a theoretical basis for understanding how voluntary departures propagate through interconnected systems.
It highlights the conditions under which decay remains localized and when it becomes systemic, offering new insight into the endogenous collapse of networks in economic, social, and technological domains.

A Model for Strategic Network Abandonment

Single-Step Equilibrium

We consider a network consisting of
N
agents connected by weighted, undirected links.
The structure of the network is described by its adjacency matrix
A
, where
A_{ij}>0
if there is a link between agent
i
and
j
, and
A_{ij}=0
otherwise.

Each agent
i
chooses a scalar activity level
x_{i}\geqslant 0
, representing how much effort to invest in the system.
Agent
i
’s utility is given by a standard linear-quadratic form
[
19
,
6
,
9
,
3
]
:

U_{i}=\alpha x_{i}-\frac{1}{2}x_{i}^{2}+\beta x_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}x_{j},

(1)

This linear-quadratic structure is widely used because it arises as a first-order approximation to a broad class of network interaction games,
making it a natural and highly general framework for modeling strategic behavior in socio-economic systems.
The first term captures the direct utility from agent
i
’s own effort, while the second term represents the cost of exerting effort.
The final term captures the benefit an agent receives from their own effort from the effort of its neighbors, scaled by the complementarity parameter
\beta\geqslant 0
.
Larger values of
\beta
imply stronger strategic complementarities, meaning an agent gains more from being connected to active neighbors.

Given a fixed network
A
, the vector of equilibrium activity levels
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
is determined by simultaneous best responses.
Solving the first-order conditions yields a unique Nash equilibrium provided that
\beta
is sufficiently small so that the matrix
I-\beta A
is invertible, which occurs whenever
\beta\rho(A)<1
, where
\rho(A)
is the spectral radius of
A
.
The equilibrium actions are given by

x^{*}(A)=\alpha(I-\beta A)^{-1}\mathbf{1},

(2)

where
\mathbf{1}
is a vector of ones.
The term
(I-\beta A)^{-1}\mathbf{1}
is known as the
Bonacich centrality
of the network.
It generalizes degree centrality by accounting for both direct and indirect connections, with longer paths discounted by powers of
\beta
.
Agents with high Bonacich centrality are strongly connected to others who are themselves well connected.

At equilibrium, the utility of agent
i
is

U_{i}^{\ast}(x_{i})=\frac{1}{2}\left(x_{i}^{*}\right)^{2},

(3)

so ranking agents by equilibrium utility is equivalent to ranking them by their activity level
x_{i}
.
Thus, in what follows, we often use the terms
“utility”
and
“centrality”
interchangeably.

We now study how the network evolves when some agents abandon the system over time.
We begin at time
t=0
with an initial network
A^{(0)}
.
The corresponding equilibrium activity levels are

x^{(0)}\equiv x^{*}\big(A^{(0)}\big),

and the initial utilities are

U^{(0)}_{i}\equiv U^{*}\big(x^{(0)}_{i}\big),

for each agent
i
.

To model the possibility that agents leave when their utility is too low, we introduce an
outside utility

u_{\mathrm{out}}^{(t)}
.
This represents the minimum utility an agent must achieve to justify staying in the system, with lower-utility agents exiting to pursue outside opportunities.
Examples include a town resident migrating into a larger city, a firm going out of business, or an individual abandoning a collaborative project.

At the initial stage, we gradually raise the outside utility until it equals the minimum utility among the currently active agents:

u_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}=\min_{i}U^{(0)}_{i}.

By construction, at least one agent, and possibly several, now has utility exactly equal to
u_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}
.
These agents exit the system, and we remove them from the network.

Removing these agents changes the equilibrium of the remaining network, because their connections and payoffs are altered.
We therefore recompute the equilibrium activities and utilities for the reduced network, holding the outside utility fixed at the current value
u_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}
.
If, after this recalculation, additional agents now have utilities below
u_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}
, they too will exit.
We repeat this process until no further agents leave at this fixed threshold.
At that point, the set of remaining agents is labeled as the next-stage network
A^{(1)}
, and the procedure continues with
t=1
.
This procedure is repeated until no agent remains in the system.
At each stage, we wait for the network to fully re-equilibrate before increasing the outside utility further.
This
adiabatic approximation
ensures that the network is always in a steady state before new external pressures—that is, the outside option—are increased.

This iterative removal process generates a sequence of progressively smaller networks

A^{(0)}\supset A^{(1)}\supset A^{(2)}\supset\cdots,

together with an increasing sequence of outside utility levels

u_{\mathrm{out}}^{(0)}<u_{\mathrm{out}}^{(1)}<u_{\mathrm{out}}^{(2)}<\cdots,

until all agents have exited the system.

Local and Global Decay Dynamics

We now turn to the analytical characterization of the iterative decay process described above.
Our goal is to understand under what conditions a small initial set of departures remains localized or, alternatively, triggers a global cascade that ultimately results in the full abandonment of the network.

General Problem Formulation

Recall that equilibrium activities are given by Eq. (
2
) so each agent’s utility is influenced not only by its direct neighbors but also by all indirect connections, weighted by powers of
\beta
.
When some agents are removed, the entire inverse matrix
(I-\beta A)^{-1}
changes, implying that every agent’s utility may shift, even if it is far from the initially removed agent.
This makes the decay process non-local.

Suppose that a set of agents
S
leaves the network, leaving the remaining active agents
R
.
We can then decompose the adjacency matrix into four blocks—
A_{RR},A_{RS},A_{SR},A_{SS}
—where
A_{SR}
captures cross-connections between two groups
S,R\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}
, and similar for the other matrix blocks.
The change in the equilibrium activity levels of the surviving agents due to the departure of
S
then becomes

\Delta x_{R}^{(-S)}=\alpha\big[(I-\beta A_{RR})^{-1}\beta A_{RS}\mathbf{1}_{S}\big],

(4)

where
\mathbf{1}_{S}
is a vector of ones over the departing agents.
This expression shows that the impact of a departure propagates along paths of all lengths through the surviving network, with longer paths discounted by higher powers of
\beta
.
This becomes explicit when we expand the inverse via the Neumann series

(I-\beta A)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\beta^{k}A^{k},

(5)

which reveals that each term
A^{k}
counts all walks of length
k
among the surviving agents,
weighted by the factor
\beta^{k}
that down-weights longer paths.

Approximate Local Dynamics

The problem simplifies considerably when
\beta
is small relative to the network’s connectivity.
Formally, when
\beta\rho(A)\ll 1
, we can approximate the inverse as

(I-\beta A)^{-1}=I+\beta A+O(\beta^{2}).

Substituting into the equilibrium, we obtain

x_{i}\approx\alpha\big(1+\beta d_{i}\big),

(6)

where
d_{i}=\sum_{j}A_{ij}
is the (weighted) degree of agent
i
.
In this limit, utilities depend only on direct neighbors.
The utility changes caused by removing a set of agents
S
are then approximately proportional to the number of direct neighbors that have exited.
That is, an agent fails when it has lost enough immediate neighbors.
Concretely, and as detailed in
B
, each agent
j
can be assigned an integer threshold

\theta_{j}=\left\lceil\frac{U_{j}-u_{\text{out}}^{(t)}}{\alpha^{2}\beta}\right\rceil.

(7)

The rule for failure becomes simple:
an agent fails whenever the number of its failed neighbors equals or exceeds its threshold
\theta_{j}
.

This small-
\beta
approximation is mathematically equivalent to a
generalized
k
-core peeling process
, also known from heterogeneous percolation
[
24
,
16
,
18
]
.
A key insight is that large-scale cascades require a sufficient density of
vulnerable
agents whose thresholds are low enough to be triggered by a small initial shock.
When such agents are scattered throughout the network, a single failure typically remains localized.
However, if they form a connected cluster that spans a macroscopic fraction of the network, an initial departure can ignite a self-sustaining chain reaction.
This leads to a sharp transition: as the fraction of vulnerable agents or the network connectivity crosses a critical value, the cascade size jumps abruptly from negligible to system-wide.
In random graph ensembles such as Erdős–Rényi or configuration models, these critical conditions can be computed exactly and are known to depend on both the degree distribution and the distribution of individual thresholds.
Thus, in the small-
\beta
regime, the strategic network abandonment process behaves like a well-understood universality class of threshold cascades, where the long-run outcome is determined primarily by the local structure of vulnerable agents rather than by global indirect effects.

Real-World Dynamics

Empirical evidence suggests that the low-
\beta
approximation is rarely adequate in economic and social network games.
In most applications, individuals’ actions are strongly interdependent, and payoffs exhibit pronounced strategic complementarities rather than weak, local interactions.
Field and laboratory studies of linear–quadratic network games consistently estimate
\beta
in the range of
0.2
to
0.5
, implying substantial mutual reinforcement of actions across connected agents
[
6
,
9
,
3
]
.
These magnitudes indicate that real networks typically operate in a high-
\beta
regime, where behavior depends on direct but also on indirect connections and local shocks propagate widely through feedback effects, yet remain well-defined in the spectral sense, with
\beta\rho(A)\lesssim 1
ensuring a unique and stable equilibrium.
Accordingly, the small-
\beta
limit, where utilities depend only on immediate neighbors and cascades follow simple threshold rules, captures only a narrow slice of economically relevant behavior.
This is also consistent with empirical observations that real socio-economic networks, whether firms, cities, or online communities, rarely exhibit abrupt collapses.
Instead, they tend to decay gradually, with feedbacks cushioning shocks and slowing the disintegration of activity (Figure
2
, left).

The same qualitative difference arises in our model when comparing networks with weak and strong complementarities.
To illustrate these dynamics, we generate a network of
N=300
agents from the power-law cluster (Holme–Kim) ensemble which combines preferential attachment with triadic closure.
Each new agent forms
m=2
links to existing agents, and with probability
p=0.1
each new edge closes a triangle, producing a network with both a heavy-tailed degree distribution and realistic clustering typical of social and collaboration networks.
Finally, the edge weights are uniformly rescaled such that
\rho(A)=3
, ensuring that
\beta\rho(A)=0.9
for
\beta=0.3
.
We then simulate the network decay with relatively large (
\beta=0.3
) and relatively small
(\beta=0.05)
complementary value, respectively.
As shown in Figure
2
(middle), for small
\beta
, where utilities depend primarily on local neighbors, the decay proceeds in abrupt, punctuated steps corresponding to discrete
k
-core removals as predicted by Eq.(
7
).
When
\beta
is larger, in contrast, indirect feedbacks diffuse the impact of each agent’s departure more widely across the network,
leading to a more gradual reduction in overall activity.
This difference can be traced to the distribution of effective influence within the network.
At low
\beta
, heterogeneity is captured largely by degree; at high
\beta
, it is captured by Bonacich centrality, which integrates paths of all lengths and therefore exhibits substantially greater dispersion.
As illustrated in Figure
2
(right), the Gini coefficient of the Bonacich centralities is consistently higher than that of degrees, reflecting this greater diversity in strategic influence.
The resulting heterogeneity cushions the system against coordinated collapse, because nodes differ widely in their effective centrality and thus respond asynchronously to rising outside options.

Global Dynamics and the Limits of Predictability

Unlike the low-
\beta
regime that can be reduced to local threshold rules or the structure of vulnerable clusters,
the high-
\beta
regime exhibits a qualitatively different mode of decay.
Departures propagate not only through local neighborhoods but globally along all walks represented in the Neumann expansion (
5
).
This dependence on long-range reinforcement mirrors the behavior of globally coupled failure models,
such as
fiber-bundle models
with global load sharing
[
23
,
20
,
1
,
22
]
.
In these systems, individual failures redistribute load uniformly across surviving components,
producing a metastable state that persists until a global stability condition is violated.
Collapse then occurs abruptly and system-wide, without the early-warning signatures typical of second-order transitions,
and with substantial variability in the critical point across realizations due to small structural differences.

Similarly, in our strategic network abandonment, exiting agents trigger equilibrium responses across the entire network.
This produces a metastable region in which the network shrinks gradually while its global susceptibility remains well below any critical threshold,
followed by a sudden, discontinuous drop in activity that leads to a global collapse.
The abruptness of this transition, together with its sensitivity to fine structural details of the network,
explains why predicting the timing of large collapses is inherently difficult in the high-
\beta
regime.

Figure
3
illustrates this behavior across
20
realizations of the same network ensemble.
The number of remaining agents decays gradually before collapsing at different outside options in different realizations,
despite identical model parameters.
The resolvent norm
\|(I-\beta A)^{-1}\|_{2}
, which plays the role of a global susceptibility,
decreases monotonically as the network loses connectivity and provides no advance signal of the imminent rupture.
Likewise, the inverse participation ratio (IPR) of the leading eigenvector remains nearly flat,
exhibiting only a sharp upward spike at the moment of collapse.
This shows that although the decay dynamics are fully deterministic conditional on the network,
small structural differences across realizations produce substantial variability in the collapse point.
We refer to

for an exact, sufficient condition that characterizes an imminent, global collapse of the system.

How to Sustain a Strategic Network

In many socio-economic systems, it may be desirable to prevent or delay network decay by providing targeted support to selected agents.
Such support may take the form of subsidies, credit access, or institutional investment that increases an agent’s stand-alone incentive to remain active in the system.
Up to this point, the individual incentive parameter
\alpha
has been assumed homogeneous across agents.
We now relax this assumption and allow for heterogeneous incentives
\alpha_{i}
, reflecting that some agents may receive greater external support than others.
In this case, the equilibrium actions in Eq. (
2
) become

x=(I-\beta A)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha},

(8)

with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N})
.

A straightforward approach to designing support policies is to allocate a fixed budget so as to maximize overall welfare in the network.
This leads to the incentive-targeting problem, where the welfare-maximizing allocation aligns support with the dominant eigenvector of the adjacency matrix
A

[
13
]
.
Such a policy concentrates resources on the most central and strategically influential agents and can be interpreted as a
top-down
or
trickle-down
strategy in which benefits are expected to diffuse through the network via complementarities.

However, this welfare-maximizing approach does not account for the presence of an outside option that may cause low-utility agents to exit.
By directing support primarily toward already central agents, a trickle-down strategy may leave peripheral or disadvantaged agents below the outside-utility threshold.
These agents then exit the network despite increases in aggregate welfare, which in turn may trigger further departures and ultimately reduce both participation and overall welfare.
This motivates a complementary perspective that focuses explicitly on stabilizing vulnerable agents rather than maximizing welfare at a single point in time.

Rather than maximizing total welfare, our immediate objective is thus to ensure that no agent’s equilibrium utility falls below the outside option
u_{\mathrm{out}}
.
This formulation captures a stabilization policy aimed at preventing further network decay by supporting the agents that are closest to leaving.
We seek the minimal additional support
\boldsymbol{y}\geqslant 0
such that the updated incentives
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{y}
yield equilibrium utilities above the outside option for all agents:

U_{i}^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\big[(I-\beta A)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime}\big]_{i}\Big)^{2}\;\geqslant\;u_{\mathrm{out}}

(9)

for all
i
.
The corresponding optimization problem can be written as

\displaystyle\min_{y\geqslant 0}

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}

(10)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle(I-\beta A)^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{y})\;\geqslant\;\sqrt{2\,u_{\mathrm{out}}}\,\mathbf{1}.

Equation (
10
) defines a linear optimization problem with a convex feasible set, since
(I-\beta A)^{-1}
is a positive semidefinite linear operator whenever
\beta\rho(A)<1
.
Since both the objective and the constraints are linear, the problem can be solved efficiently with convex programming methods.

Figure
4
displays the solution by illustrating how total welfare evolves under our minimal-support scheme (bottom-up approach, BU) as the outside option increases.
As expected, higher outside options require larger total support
\|\mathbf{y}^{*}\|
, since more incentives are needed to keep all agents above the threshold.
Nevertheless, by construction, the number of active agents in the system remains constant, and overall welfare continues to rise with additional support.
This pattern stands in sharp contrast to conventional welfare-maximizing interventions (top-down approach, TD)
[
13
]
.
When the same total budget
\|\mathbf{y}^{*}\|
is allocated to the high-centrality agents according to the TD approach,
the resulting trickle-down effects fail to sustain the least advantaged agents.
Some agents still fall below the participation threshold, causing exits that ultimately reduce total welfare.

These two intervention strategies thus represent fundamentally different responses to network fragility.
Which approach is more effective depends critically on the spectral structure of the underlying network (Figure
4
, bottom).
When the spectral radius
\beta\rho(A)
is large, the equilibrium map
(I-\beta A)^{-1}
amplifies shocks strongly and distributes them broadly across the network.
In this regime, strategic complementarities are powerful: increases in the incentives of central agents propagate widely, indirectly raising the utilities of many others.
As a result, allocating support according to the welfare-maximizing solution is highly efficient.
In contrast, when the spectral radius is small, the network’s amplification capacity is limited and the indirect effects of central agents are weak.
Spillovers dissipate quickly, and increases in the incentives of well-connected agents fail to lift low-utility agents above the outside option.
In this regime, a welfare-maximizing strategy can be ineffective or even counterproductive:
it directs resources toward agents who already benefit from strong positions in the network, while those at the margin remain vulnerable to exit.
Stabilizing the network instead requires targeted support to these vulnerable agents.

Taken together, these insights suggest a simple principle for sustaining strategic networks.
When
\beta\rho(A)
is large, supporting central agents is an efficient and self-reinforcing mechanism for maintaining activity.
When
\beta\rho(A)
is small, ensuring the viability of the least advantaged agents becomes essential.
Thus, the spectral radius provides a natural diagnostic for choosing between a top-down, welfare-maximizing intervention and a bottom-up, vulnerability-based stabilization strategy.

Discussion and Conclusion

We have presented a framework for understanding how networks decay when strategic, economically motivated agents face improving outside options.
A central insight of our analysis is that the mode of decay depends strongly on the strength of strategic complementarities
\beta
, or more generally on the amplification factor
\beta\rho(A)
.
In the low-
\beta
regime, exit dynamics resemble classical threshold cascades: failures depend primarily on local neighborhoods, vulnerable clusters can be identified ex ante, and large-scale cascades emerge only when local fragility accumulates.
In such locally driven systems, interventions are most effective when they follow a bottom-up logic that stabilizes the most vulnerable agents directly, because influence diffuses only weakly and support does not propagate far.

In the high-
\beta
regime, by contrast, departures propagate through the entire network, coupling each agent’s incentives to all others through a global multiplier.
Here, decay has a rupture-like character: standard spectral or structural metrics provide little advance warning, and small structural differences produce large variability in the collapse point.
In this globally amplified setting, support targeted at the most central agents, the welfare-maximizing, top-down approach, is most effective, because even small interventions diffuse broadly throughout the network.

These results underscore that the effectiveness of intervention depends critically on the interplay between network structure, complementarities, and evolving outside opportunities.
Monitoring the network’s amplification factor
\beta\rho(A)
provides a concrete diagnostic for determining whether local or global support strategies are more appropriate.

Several limitations offer avenues for future work.
We have assumed homogeneous and symmetric interaction weights, whereas heterogeneous or asymmetric influences, which would move the model closer to disordered or spin-glass-like systems, may generate richer patterns of decay.
We have also adopted an adiabatic approximation in which the network re-equilibrates after each local cascade; relaxing this assumption would enable the study of non-equilibrium decay when agents adjust more rapidly than the system can settle.

In conclusion, our results highlight that network fragility is not universal but regime-dependent, and that sustaining a strategic network requires interventions calibrated to the degree of interdependence that governs its collective dynamics.

Appendix A
Decay Process is Well-Defined

In this appendix,
we show that the union of stable subsets is stable, which guarantees a unique maximal stable survivor set at each stage of the decay process.
In other words, the cascade outcome at a fixed outside utility is order-independent, and the process is well-defined.

Theorem 1
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
be a (nonnegative) adjacency matrix and let
\beta\geqslant 0
.
For any node subset
S\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}
, write
A[S]
for the principal submatrix of
A
induced by
S
, and
\mathbf{1}_{S}
for the
|S|
-dimensional all-ones vector.
Fix
c\geqslant 0
.
If for two subsets
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}
we have

\displaystyle(I-\beta\,A[S_{1}])^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{S_{1}}\;\geqslant\;c\,\mathbf{1}_{S_{1}}

(11)

\displaystyle(I-\beta\,A[S_{2}])^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{S_{2}}\;\geqslant\;c\,\mathbf{1}_{S_{2}},

(12)

then their union also satisfies

(I-\beta\,A[S_{1}\cup S_{2}])^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{S_{1}\cup S_{2}}\;\geqslant\;c\,\mathbf{1}_{S_{1}\cup S_{2}}.

(13)

Proof.

Using the Neumann series (valid for
\beta
such that
I-\beta A[S]
is invertible), we have

(I-\beta\,A[S])^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{S}\;=\;\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\beta^{k}\,A[S]^{k}\Big)\mathbf{1}_{S}

for any
S
.
For a fixed
i\in S
, the
i
-th entry of
\big(\sum_{k\geq 0}\beta^{k}A[S]^{k}\big)\mathbf{1}_{S}
equals the weighted count of all walks that start at
i
and remain entirely within the induced subgraph on
S
, with walks of length
k
weighted by
\beta^{k}
.

Now take
S=S_{1}\cup S_{2}
.
For any
v\in S_{1}
, the set of walks that remain within
S_{1}\cup S_{2}
is a superset of the walks that remain within
S_{1}
; hence the corresponding weighted counts (entrywise) in
(I-\beta A[S_{1}\cup S_{2}])^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{S_{1}\cup S_{2}}
dominate those in
(I-\beta A[S_{1}])^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{S_{1}}
.
Therefore, by (
11
), for all
i\in S_{1}
,

\big[(I-\beta A[S_{1}\cup S_{2}])^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{S_{1}\cup S_{2}}\big]_{i}\;\geqslant\;c.

An identical argument using (
12
) gives the same inequality for all
i\in S_{2}
.
Combining these two cases yields (
13
) on
S_{1}\cup S_{2}
.
∎

Appendix B
Derivation of Local Threshold Rule

In the small-
\beta
regime, indirect effects are negligible and equilibrium actions depend primarily on direct neighbors.
From Eq. (
6
), the equilibrium action of agent
i
is

x_{i}\approx\alpha\big(1+\beta d_{i}\big),

(14)

where
d_{i}
is the weighted degree of agent
i
,
d_{i}=\sum_{j}A_{ij}
.
Suppose a set of neighbors
S
of agent
i
leaves the network.
Let the total weight of the removed edges be

m_{i}=\sum_{j\in S}A_{ij}.

The new action of agent
i
becomes

x_{i}^{\prime}\approx\alpha\big(1+\beta(d_{i}-m_{i})\big)=x_{i}-\alpha\beta m_{i}.

(15)

Equilibrium utility is given by (
3
).
For small changes, a first-order approximation yields

\Delta U_{i}=U_{i}^{\prime}-U_{i}\approx x_{i}\Delta x_{i}.

(16)

Substituting
\Delta x_{i}=-\alpha\beta m_{i}
and using
x_{i}\approx\alpha
to leading order, we obtain

\Delta U_{i}\approx-\,\alpha^{2}\beta m_{i}.

(17)

At time
t
, let the outside utility be
u_{\mathrm{out}}^{(t)}
and define the reserve utility of agent
i
as

r_{i}=U_{i}-u_{\mathrm{out}}^{(t)}.

(18)

Agent
i
fails when
U_{i}^{\prime}\leqslant u_{\mathrm{out}}^{(t)}
, i.e.

U_{i}+\Delta U_{i}\leqslant u_{\mathrm{out}}^{(t)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\alpha^{2}\beta m_{i}\geqslant r_{i}.

(19)

In a weighted network, failure occurs when the total lost weight
m_{i}
exceeds
r_{i}/(\alpha^{2}\beta)
.
In an unweighted network,
m_{i}
equals the number of failed neighbors.
We therefore define the integer threshold

\theta_{i}=\left\lceil\frac{r_{i}}{\alpha^{2}\beta}\right\rceil,

(20)

so that age
i
fails whenever the number of failed neighbors is greater than or equal to
\theta_{i}
.

Appendix C
Condition for Global Collapse

Below, we present Theorem
2
which provides an exact condition for a global collapse in the high-
\beta
regime. First, we require some definitions and a proposition.

Definition 1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with adjacency matrix
A
. Denote by
b(G)\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
its vector of Bonachich centralities and by
\underline{b}:=\min_{i\in V}b_{i}(G)
the lowest centrality in the network. Define the set of
rich
nodes by

R:=\{i\in V:b_{i}(G)>\underline{b}\}

and the set of poor nodes as
P:=V\setminus R
. For each rich node
i\in R
, denote by

d_{\mathrm{out}}^{R}(i):=\bigl\lvert\{j\in P:(i,j)\in E\}\bigr\rvert

the number of
poor
neighbors and let
d_{\mathrm{out}}^{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
be the corresponding vector.
Finally, define

q\coloneqq\beta\penalty\ \underline{b}\penalty\ (I-\beta A_{RR})^{-1}d_{\mathrm{out}}^{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}

and denote by
q_{i}
its
i
-th entry.

With the argument given in the proof of Theorem
1
, we observe that Bonacich centrality is monotone under edge (and node) deletions. This allows us to state and prove a sufficient condition for a level of the outside option with a cascade, i.e., where all nodes leave the network.

Theorem 2
.

If

q_{i}\;\geq\;b_{i}(G)-\underline{b}\quad\text{for all }i\in R,

then for every nonempty
S\subseteq V
,

\min_{i\in S}b_{i}(G[S])\leq\underline{b},

where
b(G[S])
denotes Bonacich centrality on the induced subgraph
G[S]

with the same parameters
(\alpha,\beta)
.

Proof.

The proof consists of four steps:

Step 1: Poor nodes always drop out

Take any
i\in P
, so
b_{i}(G)=\underline{b}
. For any induced subgraph
G[S]
with

i\in S
, by monotonicity we have

b_{i}(G[S])\leq b_{i}(G)=\underline{b}.

Therefore, if
S\cap P\neq\emptyset
, then

\min_{j\in S}b_{j}(G[S])\leq\underline{b}.

Hence it remains only to consider sets
S
with
S\subseteq R
.

Step 2: Drop vectors

For any nonempty
T\subseteq V
, define the drop vector

\Delta^{T}:=b_{T}(G)-b(G[T])\in\mathbb{R}^{T}_{\geq 0}.

Monotonicity implies that each
\Delta^{T}
is entrywise nonnegative.
To compute
\Delta^{R}
explicitly,
partition the adjacency matrix according to
V=R\cup P
:

A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{RR}&A_{RP}\\
A_{PR}&A_{PP}\end{pmatrix},\qquad b(G)=\begin{pmatrix}b_{R}(G)\\
b_{P}(G)\end{pmatrix}.

The global Bonacich equation
(I-\beta A)b(G)=\alpha\mathbf{1}
becomes

\begin{pmatrix}I-\beta A_{RR}&-\beta A_{RP}\\
-\beta A_{PR}&I-\beta A_{PP}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}b_{R}(G)\\
b_{P}(G)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\mathbf{1}_{R}\\
\alpha\mathbf{1}_{P}\end{pmatrix}.

Looking at the
R
-block,

(I-\beta A_{RR})b_{R}(G)-\beta A_{RP}b_{P}(G)=\alpha\mathbf{1}_{R},

so

(I-\beta A_{RR})b_{R}(G)=\alpha\mathbf{1}_{R}+\beta A_{RP}b_{P}(G).

(21)

For the induced subgraph
G[R]
with adjacency matrix
A_{RR}
, the Bonacich
vector
b(G[R])\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
satisfies

b(G[R])=\alpha\mathbf{1}_{R}+\beta A_{RR}b(G[R]),

hence

(I-\beta A_{RR})b(G[R])=\alpha\mathbf{1}_{R}.

(22)

Subtracting (
22
) from (
21
) gives

(I-\beta A_{RR})\bigl(b_{R}(G)-b(G[R])\bigr)=\beta A_{RP}b_{P}(G).

By definition
\Delta^{R}=b_{R}(G)-b(G[R])
, so

(I-\beta A_{RR})\Delta^{R}=\beta A_{RP}b_{P}(G).

Since
I-\beta A_{RR}
is invertible,

\Delta^{R}=\beta(I-\beta A_{RR})^{-1}A_{RP}b_{P}(G).

(23)

Step 4: Lower bound on
\Delta^{R}
and nonnegativity of the inverse

By definition of
P
, every entry of
b_{P}(G)
equals
\underline{b}
, so

b_{P}(G)=\underline{b}\mathbf{1}_{P}.

Thus

A_{RP}b_{P}(G)=\underline{b}A_{RP}\mathbf{1}_{P}.

Using
(
23
), we obtain

\Delta^{R}=\beta(I-\beta A_{RR})^{-1}A_{RP}b_{P}(G)=\beta\underline{b}(I-\beta A_{RR})^{-1}A_{RP}\mathbf{1}_{P}.

The vector
A_{RP}\mathbf{1}_{P}
has entries

(A_{RP}\mathbf{1}_{P})_{i}=\sum_{j\in P}A_{ij}=\bigl|\{j\in P:(i,j)\in E\}\bigr|=d_{\mathrm{out}}^{R}(i).

Hence

\Delta^{R}=\beta\underline{b}(I-\beta A_{RR})^{-1}d_{\mathrm{out}}^{R}=q.

(24)

Step 5: Drops on subsets
S\subseteq R

To finish the proof, we will show that there cannot be a subset
S\subseteq R
of nodes that all have utility larger than
\underline{b}
.
Hence all agents will leave the network.
If
R=\emptyset
, then a cascade is automatic. Therefore, assume
S\subseteq R
is nonempty, and fix any
i\in S
.
The subgraph
G[S]
is an induced subgraph of
G[R]
, so by monotonicity inside
R
,

b_{i}(G[S])\leq b_{i}(G[R]).

Thus

\Delta^{S}_{i}=b_{i}(G)-b_{i}(G[S])\geq b_{i}(G)-b_{i}(G[R])=\Delta^{R}_{i}.

Combining with (
24
),

\Delta^{S}_{i}\geq\Delta^{R}_{i}=q_{i}.

By assumption,
q_{i}\geq b_{i}(G)-\underline{b}
for all
i\in R
.
Hence for fixed
i\in S\subseteq R
,

\Delta^{S}_{i}\geq b_{i}(G)-\underline{b},

so

b_{i}(G[S])=b_{i}(G)-\Delta^{S}_{i}\leq b_{i}(G)-(b_{i}(G)-\underline{b})=\underline{b}.

Therefore, for every nonempty
S\subseteq R
there exists (in fact, the sufficient condition is strong enough for this to hold for all)
i\in S
with

b_{i}(G[S])\leq\underline{b}
. Combined with the case
S\cap P\neq\emptyset
from Step 1,
this shows that for every nonempty
S\subseteq V
,

\min_{i\in S}b_{i}(G[S])\leq\underline{b}.

∎
