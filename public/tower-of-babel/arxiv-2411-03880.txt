Compact p p -adic analytic groups in which centralizers are abelian
Luis Mendonça, Thomas S. Weigel, Theo Zapata

Abstract
. Using methods of associative algebras, Lie theory, group cohomology, and modular representation theory, we construct profinite p p -adic analytic groups such that the centralizer of each of their non-trivial elements is abelian. The paper answers questions of P. Shumyatsky, P. Zalesskii, and T. Zapata in the Israel J. Math., v. 230, 2019.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 20E18; Secondary 20J05

1.
Introduction

Describing centralizers is often one of the fundamental strides in understanding a group.
The condition that the centralizer of every non-trivial element in a group is abelian defines a CA-group and, in recent years, the equivalent condition of the transitivity of commutation among non-trivial elements, has attracted much attention in Group Theory and other areas of Algebra (see
[
Wu98
]
,
[
FR08
]
and
[
SZZ19
]
).

The classification of finite (and locally finite) CA-groups has been the subject of considerable study, spanning from 1925 through to 1998. Weisner initially showed that finite CA-groups are either simple or solvable (cf.
[
Wei25
]
and
[
Wu98
]
). Then in the Brauer–Suzuki–Wall theorem (see
[
BSW58
]
) finite CA-groups of even order fall into a trichotomy: they are either abelian, Frobenius groups, or two-dimensional projective special linear groups over a finite field of even order, namely
\textup{PSL}(2,2^{m})
for
m\geqslant 2
. Of particular note is the 1957 landmark contribution of M. Suzuki
[
Suz57
]
, wherein he proved that simple CA-groups must necessarily be of even order. Suzuki’s work was a crucial precursor to the outstanding Feit–Thompson theorem on the solubility of groups of odd order.

Moreover, the significance of infinite CA-groups in contemporary mathematics is underscored by their connection with the theory of residually free groups (cf.
[
LS01
, p. 10]
,
[
Wu98
]
,
[
FR08
]
and
[
AFW15
]
). Specifically, finitely generated residually free CA-groups are limit groups, which played a pivotal role in the solution of the Tarski problems. In these groups, each centralizer is also malnormal (i.e., any conjugate by an element outside of the subgroup intersects trivially the subgroup itself); such groups are termed
CSA-groups
. Examples of infinite CA-groups are plentiful and include free groups, torsion-free hyperbolic groups, certain subgroups of
\textup{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
, free Burnside groups of large odd exponent, free soluble groups, Tarski monsters, one-relator groups with torsion, certain small cancellation groups, and the fundamental groups of certain compact 3-manifolds.

Profinite CSA-groups, however, are still quite mysterious.
In response to
[
SZZ19
, Question 3]
, we offer the following class of profinite CSA-groups, thereby answering the posed question affirmatively.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
D
be a central division algebra of prime degree
\ell
over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.
If

is odd and

then the profinite

p

To our knowledge the first paper which studied infinite profinite CA-groups is
[

]

p
or abelian.

In the present paper

-adic analytic groups, a class of profinite groups extensively studied

2.
Lie algebras

Throughout this section let

Lemma 2.1

)
.

If

If, moreover,

Lemma 2.2

(
[

)
.

Let

be an abelian ideal of

If

is CA and acts fixed-point-freely by inner derivations on

then

is CA.

2.1.
Solvable Lie algebras

Proposition 2.3
.

If
L
is solvable and CA, then
L
is either abelian, or metabelian of the form

, where

are abelian with

acts fixed-point-freely on

Proof.

Apart from the inequality on dimensions all can be found in
[
KM10
, Th. 2.3]
. Now, since

acts fixed-point-freely on

, if

are linearly independent, then for any

in

the elements

[x,y_{1}],\ldots,[x,y_{n}]\in N
are also linearly independent. Indeed, from
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}[x,y_{i}]=0
with
a_{i}\in k
, we get that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}y_{i}
fixes

,
which by fixed-point-freeness gives us

for all

. This proves the inequality.
∎

Notice that any possible dimensions in the proposition above do occur: given
0<r\leqslant n
, find a field extension
K
of

of degree

, and consider the Lie
K
-algebra with basis
\{x,y\}
and bracket

. It is CA, and as a Lie

. Clearly it contains subalgebras of the form

, and these must be CA too.

2.2.
Simple Lie algebras

Recall first that the

Recall also that a Lie algebra is called

By passing to the centroid

Lemma 2.4

Let

Proof.

By Lemma

∎

If

Now, according to

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}

Evidently, if a scalar extension of a Lie algebra is CA, then the algebra itself is CA.
Note that

∎

From Veisfeiler’s classification of semisimple

Suppose

Next we use the fact that each finite dimensional simple division algebra over a

Finally, suppose that

Proposition 2.7

(a)

Let

Then

is a CA
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-Lie algebra.

(b)

-Lie algebra and

\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-sublattice of

-sublattice of

Theorem 2.10

Let

be primes. Let

be a finite extension of

Since

3.7.
\mathrm{SL}_{2}
-like finite groups with the
\Omega^{\circ}
-fixed point property

Under the notation of Proposition
3.8
(d) we say that a finite group

G
with central involution
\sigma
has the
\Omega_{2}(\bar{G},\mathbb{F}_{2})^{\circ}
-fixed point property
, where
\bar{G}=G/\langle\sigma\rangle
, if
(\Omega_{2}(\bar{G},\mathbb{F}_{2})^{\circ})^{H}=\{0\}
for all
H\in\mathrm{Odd}(G)
.

Fact 3.9
.

Let
(G,\sigma)
be a finite group with central involution
\sigma
in
G
with the

\Omega_{2}(\bar{G},\mathbb{F}_{2})^{\circ}
-fixed point property. Then for every non-trivial odd order
subgroup
H
of
G
, one has
(\Omega^{-})^{H}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

By (
3.24
) one has an isomorphism

(3.29)

\Omega^{-}/2\Omega^{-}\simeq\Omega_{2}(\bar{G},\mathbb{F}_{2})^{\circ}.

Suppose that there exists a non-trivial subgroup
H\in\mathrm{Odd}(G)
satisfying
(\Omega^{-})^{H}\not=0
.
Then, as
(\Omega^{-})^{H}
is a pure subgroup of
\Omega^{-}
, one has

(3.30)

\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(((\Omega^{-})^{H}+2\Omega^{-})/2\Omega^{-})=\mathrm{rk}_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}((\Omega^{-})^{H}).

and thus, by (
3.29
),
(\Omega_{2}(\bar{G},\mathbb{F}_{2})^{\circ})^{H}\not=0
, a contradiction. Hence, for all

H\in\mathrm{Odd}(G)
,
H\not=\{1\}
one has
(\Omega^{-})^{H}=0
.
∎

3.9.
The generalized quarternion groups

For
n\geqslant 3
let

(3.31)

Q_{2^{n}}=\langle\,x,y\mid x^{2^{n-1}}=y^{4}=1,\,yxy^{-1}=x^{-1},\,x^{2^{n-2}}=y^{2}\,\rangle

denote the generalized quarternion group of order
2^{n}
.
The following property is well-known (cf.
[
Gor80
, p. 191]
).

Fact 3.10
.

The element
\sigma=y^{2}=x^{2^{n-2}}
is the unique element in
Q_{2^{n}}
of order
2
.
In particular,
\mathrm{Z}(Q_{2^{n}})=\langle\sigma\rangle
.

From Theorem
1.7
one deduces the following result.

Corollary 3.11
.

For
n\geqslant 3
and
Q_{2^{n}}
the generalized quaternion group, the pro-
2
group
\Upsilon({Q}_{2^{n}},\sigma,2)
is CA and virtually abelian.

Proof.

As
\mathrm{Odd}(Q_{2^{n}})=\{\{1\}\}
and since
\sigma
is the only involution in
Q_{2^{n}}
,
(Q_{2^{n}},\sigma)
is
\mathrm{SL}_{2}
-like.
Therefore, Theorem
1.7
yields the claim.
∎

As
\Omega_{2}(Q_{2^{n}},\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{-}
is the maximal (abelian open normal) subgroup of
\Upsilon(Q_{2^{n}},\sigma,2)
, Corollary
3.11
answers
[
SZZ19
, Question 4]
affirmatively.

3.10.
2-local
\mathrm{SL}_{2}
-like groups

Another consequence of Theorem
1.7
is the following.

Corollary 3.12
.

Let
G
be a
2
-local
\mathrm{SL}_{2}
-like group
with the
\Omega_{2}(\bar{G},\mathbb{F}_{2})^{\circ}
-fixed point property.
Then

\Upsilon(G,\sigma,2)
is CA.

Here we called a finite group
G
to be
2
-local, if

|\mathrm{Syl}_{2}(G)|=1
, i.e.,
P_{2}\in\mathrm{Syl}_{2}(G)
is normal in
G
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\Upsilon=\Upsilon(G,2,\sigma)
and
C=C_{\Upsilon}(x)
.

Case 1:

\mathrm{ord}(\tau_{G}(x))
is even.
In this case one concludes from
Theorem
1.7
that

C=P\ltimes O_{2^{\prime}}(C)
,
where
P\in\mathrm{Syl}_{2}(C)
is cyclic.
As
G
is
2
-local,
P_{2}\in\mathrm{Syl}_{2}(G)
is normal in
G
and

P=P_{2}\cap C
is normal in
C
.
As
P\cap O_{2^{\prime}}(C)=\{1\}
, one concludes that

C\simeq P\times O_{2^{\prime}}(C)
.
In particular, as
G
is
\mathrm{SL}_{2}
-like,
C
is abelian.

Case 2:

\mathrm{ord}(\tau_{G}(x))
is odd different to
1
:
As
\langle\tau_{G}(x)\rangle
is of odd order and non-trivial,
Fact
3.9
implies

(3.32)

C\cap\Omega^{-}=(\Omega^{-})^{\langle\tau_{G}(x)\rangle}=\{1\}.

In particular,
C
is a finite subgroup of
\Upsilon
.
If its order is odd, it is abelian by condition (i) of the definition of being
\mathrm{SL}_{2}
-like, and there is nothing
to be proved. If
C
is of even order, then
H=\tau_{G}(C)
contains
\sigma
, and by the same argument which was used in the
proof of Lemma
3.7
one concludes that the sequence

(3.33)

\textstyle{\{1\}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\Omega_{2}(H,\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{-}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{j}
\textstyle{\Upsilon(H,\sigma,2)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\tau_{H}}
\textstyle{H\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\{1\}}

must split, implying that
\Omega_{2}(H,\mathbb{Z}_{2})^{-}=0
.
Thus Proposition
3.6
and condition (i) of the definition of being
\mathrm{SL}_{2}
-like
imply that
C
is abelian.

Case 3:

\tau_{G}(x)=1
:
In this case
x\in\Omega^{-}\smallsetminus\{1\}
.
As
\Omega^{-}
is abelian, one has
\Omega^{-}\subseteq C
.
Suppose there exists
z
in
C\smallsetminus\Omega^{-}
, and let
\bar{z}=\tau_{G}(z)
.
Then
\bar{z}\not=1_{G}
.
If
\bar{z}
would be of even order, there would exist
d\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
\sigma=\bar{z}^{d}
,
and thus
x=x^{-1}
, a contradiction.
If
\bar{z}
would be of odd order, then

x\in C\cap\Omega^{-}=(\Omega^{-})^{\langle\bar{z}\rangle}

contradicting Fact
3.9
. This shows that
C=\Omega^{-}
.
∎

3.11.
The
2
-modular representation theory of
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(3)

Note that
G=\mathrm{SL}_{2}(3)
has a unique
2
-Sylow subgroup isomorphic to the quaternion group of order
8
, thus containing a unique involution
\sigma
, and that the only non-trivial subgroups of odd order of
G
have order
3
.
In particular,
G
is
\mathrm{SL}_{2}
-like
and
\bar{G}=G/\langle\sigma\rangle\simeq C_{3}\ltimes V_{4}
, where

V_{4}
denotes the Klein four-group.

As
\bar{G}
is
2
-local, the irreducible

\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}[\bar{G}]
-modules
\{\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2},S_{1},S_{2}\}
correspond to the irreducible
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}[C_{3}]
-modules and thus are all linear.
Let
P_{0}
denote the projective indecomposable
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}[\bar{G}]
-module
such that
P_{0}/\operatorname{Rad}(P_{0})\simeq\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
. Then
P_{0}\simeq\mathrm{Ind}_{C_{3}}^{\bar{G}}(\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2})
, where
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
denotes the trivial left
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}[\bar{G}]
-module. In particular,
\dim_{\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}}(P_{0})=4
. Considering
V_{4}
as left
\mathbb{F}_{2}[C_{3}]
-module, where

C_{3}
is acting by conjugation, yields that
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}V_{4}\simeq S_{1}\oplus S_{2}
. Thus, by the Willems-Stammbach theorem (cf.
[
Sta87
]
,
[
Wil85
]
), one concludes that

JP_{0}/J^{2}P_{0}\simeq S_{1}\oplus S_{2}
, where
J=\operatorname{Rad}(\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}[\bar{G}])
. Hence the Loewy structure of
P_{0}
can be described by

(3.34)

P_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}\\
S_{1}\oplus S_{2}\\
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}\end{bmatrix}

Defining
P_{1}
as the minimal projective cover of
S_{1}
and
P_{2}
as
the minimal projective cover of
S_{2}
, one obtains

P_{1}\simeq S_{1}\otimes P_{0}
and
P_{2}\simeq S_{2}\otimes P_{0}
with Loewy structure

(3.35)

P_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}S_{1}\\
S_{2}\oplus\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}\\
S_{1}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\qquad P_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}S_{2}\\
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}\oplus S_{1}\\
S_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

From these facts one concludes that the trivial left

\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}[\bar{G}]
-module
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
has a minimal projective resolution

(3.36)

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\Omega_{2}(\bar{G},\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{P_{1}\oplus P_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{P_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{0}

and therefore,

(3.37)

\Omega_{2}(\bar{G},\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2})=\begin{bmatrix}S_{1}\oplus S_{2}\oplus\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}\\
S_{1}\oplus S_{2}\end{bmatrix}

In particular, the image of

\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}\otimes\Omega_{2}(\bar{G},\mathbb{F}_{2})^{\circ}
in
the Grothendieck group
G_{0}(\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{2}[\bar{G}])
coincides with
2[S_{1}]+2[S_{2}]
,
and thus
G
has the
\Omega_{2}(\bar{G},\mathbb{F}_{2})^{\circ}
-fixed point property.
