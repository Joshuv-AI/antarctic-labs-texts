Superadditivity properties and new valid inequalities for the vehicle routing problem with stochastic demands
Robin Legault, Panca Jodiawan, Jean-François Côté, Leandro C. Coelho

Abstract
Over the past thirty years, the vehicle routing problem with stochastic demands has emerged as a canonical application of the integer L-shaped method, leading to an extensive body of literature and several methodological refinements. Recently, the disaggregated integer L-shaped (DL-shaped) method, which decomposes the recourse function by customer rather than treating it as an aggregate cost, has been proposed and successfully applied under the detour-to-depot recourse policy. However, the validity of this new approach and its generalizability to other policies have not been thoroughly investigated. In this work, we provide a necessary and sufficient condition for the validity of the DL-shaped method, namely, the superadditivity of the recourse function under concatenation. We demonstrate that the optimal restocking policy satisfies this superadditivity property. Moreover, we rectify an incorrect argument from the original paper on the DL-shaped method to rigorously establish its validity under the detour-to-depot policy. We then develop a DL-shaped algorithm tailored to the optimal restocking policy. Our algorithm exploits new dynamic programming-based lower bounds on the optimal restocking recourse function. We also introduce new valid inequalities that generalize the original DL-shaped cuts and speed up computations by an order of magnitude. Computational experiments show that our DL-shaped algorithm significantly outperforms the state-of-the-art integer L-shaped algorithm from the literature. We solve several open instances to optimality, including 14 single-vehicle instances, which constitute the most challenging variant of the problem.



Keywords:
Vehicle routing problem, Stochastic demands, Optimal restocking, Integer L-shaped method, Superadditivity

1
Introduction

Since the seminal work of
Tillman 1969
, the vehicle routing problem with stochastic demands (VRPSD) has been extensively studied
(
Oyola et al. 2017
,
Oyola et al. 2018
)
. The VRPSD can be described as the problem of designing routes for vehicles of limited capacity to serve, at minimal expected cost, a set of random demands whose realization is only observed upon arrival at the customer location. The present work considers the standard VRPSD, which models a homogeneous fleet, a single depot, and no time windows.

We study the problem under the
a priori
optimization framework
(
Bertsimas et al. 1990
)
, i.e., the routes are designed before the observation of the demands, and each vehicle can perform restocking trips to the depot along its route. The actions that can be taken to anticipate or react to a stockout are defined by a recourse policy. In the
classical
, or
detour-to-depot
(DTD) policy
(
Dror et al. 1989
)
, the only available action is to perform a round trip to the depot when a
failure
occurs, i.e., the demand of the current customer exceeds the vehicle’s residual capacity. Alternatively, in the
optimal restocking
(OR) policy
(
Yee and Golden 1980
,
Yang et al. 2000
)
, after serving each customer, vehicles also have the option of performing a
preventive
trip to the depot before traveling to the next customer.

Exact algorithms for the VRPSD

Over the last three decades, a rich body of exact algorithms has been developed to solve the VRPSD under the DTD and OR policies. These algorithms can be divided into integer L-shaped methods
(
Gendreau et al. 1995
,
Hjorring and Holt 1999
,
Laporte et al. 2002
,
Jabali et al. 2014
,
Louveaux and Salazar-González 2018
,
Salavati-Khoshghalb et al. 2019
,
Hoogendoorn and Spliet 2023
,
Parada et al. 2024
,
Hoogendoorn and Spliet 2025
)
and branch-price-and cut (BP&C) methods
(
Christiansen and Lysgaard 2007
,
Gauvin et al. 2014
,
Florio et al. 2020a
,
Florio et al. 2023
)
. Integer L-shaped approaches model the VRPSD as a two-stage stochastic program and rely on edge-flow or arc-flow formulations. Within a branch-and-cut (B&C) framework, they iteratively generate valid inequalities that tighten the approximation of the recourse function and characterize feasible routes. On the other hand, BP&C methods embed column generation inside a branch-and-bound (B&B) tree. The master problem is a set-partitioning model whose columns represent complete routes. At each iteration, a resource-constrained shortest-path problem
(
Irnich and Desaulniers 2005
)
is solved to identify a new route with negative reduced costs.

Although both integer L-shaped and BP&C algorithms operate within a B&B framework, their computational bottlenecks differ fundamentally, resulting in complementary performance profiles. In integer L-shaped algorithms, most of the effort is spent repeatedly solving the linear programming (LP) relaxation of the relaxed master problem, which becomes increasingly complex as more cuts are generated. Computational efficiency therefore relies on devising valid inequalities that rapidly tighten the master problem, thereby limiting the number of explored nodes. In comparison, the main computational burden in BP&C methods lies in solving an NP-hard pricing problem
(
Ota and Fukasawa 2025
)
at each iteration. In state-of-the-art BP&C algorithms, this pricing subproblem is solved via backward labeling, where each label represents a partial path from a customer back to the depot. The worst-case number of labels generated grows exponentially with the maximum number of customers that can be visited on a route, and the computational cost of extending each label scales linearly with both the vehicle capacity and the cardinality of the random demand supports. Thus, recent developments in BP&C methods for the VRPSD largely focus on techniques designed to limit label proliferation, notably dominance rules and reduced-cost bounds. The BP&C methods of
Florio et al. 2020a
,
Florio et al. 2023
, which leverage effective labeling strategies and benefit from the tight LP relaxation provided by the set-partitioning formulation of the VRPSD, achieve state-of-the-art results on instances characterized by many short routes and moderate vehicle capacities. However, when optimal solutions consist of only a few routes, the decomposition benefit of column generation weakens significantly, as each column encapsulates a substantial portion of the decisions that form a feasible solution. Notably, in the single-vehicle scenario, the set-partitioning formulation collapses to solving a single resource-constrained shortest-path problem. Reflecting a structural similarity with the traveling salesman problem, where the best-performing exact methods are based on edge-flow formulations strengthened by cutting planes
(
Applegate 2006
)
, integer L-shaped algorithms remain the state of the art for instances involving a small number of long routes.

Integer L-shaped algorithms

Traditional integer L-shaped methods for the VRPSD model the expected recourse cost using a single auxiliary variable, which is progressively tightened using valid inequalities. In the VRPSD literature, these inequalities have typically been categorized as either
optimality cuts
or
lower bounding functionals
(LBFs), although both types can be described as optimality cuts from a broader Benders decomposition perspective
(
Hooker 2023
)
. Optimality cuts denote valid inequalities that are tight at the current solution or explicitly exclude the current solution from the feasible domain, whereas LBFs are more general lower bounds that can be
active

over a larger subset of the feasible domain. Optimality cuts alone are sufficient to ensure convergence of the algorithm. However, they are typically active for a single integer feasible solution and thus do not substantially contribute to improving the global lower bound.

The significant progress of integer L-shaped methods over the last thirty years is principally attributable to the development of LBFs that either provide tighter bounds or apply to broader classes of solutions. Most contributions along these lines are based on the notion of a
partial route
, which corresponds to a sequence of ordered and unordered sets of nodes that starts and ends with singletons comprising only the depot. Any route that visits the same customers and respects their partial ordering is then said to
adhere
to the partial route. The
partial route inequalities
were introduced by
Hjorring and Holt 1999
for the single-vehicle VRPSD under the DTD policy, extended to multiple vehicles by
Laporte et al. 2002
, generalized by
Jabali et al. 2014
, and adapted to the OR policy by
Louveaux and Salazar-González 2018
and
Salavati-Khoshghalb et al. 2019
. Recently,
Hoogendoorn and Spliet 2023
,
Hoogendoorn and Spliet 2025
departed from the common practice of representing the overall recourse cost by a single auxiliary variable. They developed the
partial route-split
and
multi-route-split
inequalities, which decompose the recourse cost into individual routes. Like standard partial route inequalities, these LBFs impose a lower bound that is enforced whenever the solution contains routes that adhere to each partial route in a collection.

Subsequently,
Parada et al. 2024
proposed further decomposing the recourse cost by customer. They introduced new optimality cuts and LBFs called
path-cuts
(P-cuts) and
set-cuts
(S-cuts), which remain active across all solutions featuring specific paths or sets of consecutively visited customers. This contrasts with all the optimality cuts and partial route inequalities previously described in the literature, which are only active when a solution contains a set of routes that form a predefined partition of a given set of customers. The resulting B&C algorithm, called the
disaggregated integer L-shaped
(DL-shaped) method, empirically dominates the previous integer L-shaped methods under the DTD policy. However, in addition to relying on policy-specific lower bounds, the DL-shaped method requires specific conditions on the recourse function to be a valid exact algorithm. These conditions have only been developed for the DTD policy, leaving the generalizability of the DL-shaped method as an open question. More fundamentally, as we will show, the conditions have not been adequately characterized. We summarize our contributions as follows.

Problem formulation

We introduce our notation and basic definitions in Section
2.1
. Section
2.2
then presents the mathematical formulation of the VRPSD, and Section
2.3
discusses the recourse function.

2.1
Notation and definitions

We consider an edge-flow formulation of the VRPSD. The problem is defined on a complete undirected graph
G:=(N_{0},E)
with nodes
N_{0}:=\{0\}\cup N
, where
0
represents the depot, and
N:=\{1,...,n\}
is the set of customers. The edge set is denoted as
E:=\{\{i,j\}:i,j\in N_{0},i\neq j\}
, where the edges
e\in E
are assigned non-negative traveling costs
c_{e}
satisfying the triangle inequality. For any subset of customers
S\subseteq N
, we denote by
E(S):=\{\{i,j\}\in E:i,j\in S\}
the set of edges that have both endpoints in
S
. Similarly, for any node
h\in N_{0}
, we denote by
\delta(h):=\{e\in E:h\in e\}
the set of edges incident to
h
.

We define a
path
as a sequence
p=(i_{1},\dots,i_{t})
of distinct customers, and denote by
N(p):=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{t}\}\subseteq N
the nodes the path visits, where
i_{k}\neq i_{k^{\prime}}
for
k\neq k^{\prime}
. A sequence
p^{\prime}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{t^{\prime}})
is called a
subsequence
of
p
if there exists a strictly increasing sequence of indices
1\leq a_{1}<\dots<a_{t^{\prime}}\leq t
such that
j_{k}=i_{a_{k}}
for all
k\in\{1,\dots,t^{\prime}\}
. Also, for any
1\leq a\leq b\leq t
, the sequence
p^{\prime}=(i_{a},\dots,i_{b})
is called a
subpath
of
p
. A subsequence can thus be obtained by removing arbitrary elements from a path, whereas a subpath can be obtained by removing elements only from its beginning and its end. For conciseness, we say that a path
p^{\prime}
is a subsequence (resp. subpath) of a set of paths
\bar{\mathcal{P}}
if there exists a path
p\in\bar{\mathcal{P}}
of which
p^{\prime}
is a subsequence (resp. subpath).

2.2
Vehicle routing problem with stochastic demands

We consider a fleet of identical vehicles, each with capacity
Q
. The customer demands are modeled as independent, non-negative random variables
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i\in N}
with supports
\{\Xi_{i}\}_{i\in N}
and means
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\in N}
. Each customer must be visited by exactly one vehicle, and each vehicle’s route is required to form a simple cycle on
G
that starts and ends at the depot. Every vehicle is allowed to serve a set of customers if their total expected demand does not exceed
fQ
, where we refer to the parameter
f>0
as the load factor. We denote by
M\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
the set of permitted fleet sizes, with
M~=~\left\{\left\lceil\frac{1}{fQ}\sum_{i\in N}\mu_{i}\right\rceil,\dots,n\right\}
being sufficient to model an unlimited fleet.

For each edge
e\in E
, an integer variable
x_{e}
represents the number of times edge
e
is traversed. For edges
e\in E(N)
linking customer nodes,
x_{e}
is binary. For edges
e\in\delta(0)
incident to the depot, the variable may take values in
\{0,1,2\}
, with
x_{e}=2
meaning that a vehicle serves a single customer. A binary variable
z_{m}
indicates whether the selected fleet comprises
m
vehicles. The objective is to minimize the sum of the first-stage cost (distance traveled along the planned routes) and the second-stage cost (expected recourse cost). The VRPSD under the a priori optimization paradigm is formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\delta(0)}x_{e}=2\sum_{m\in M}mz_{m},

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\delta(i)}x_{e}=2,

\displaystyle i\in N,

(3)

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E(S)}x_{e}\leq|S|-\left\lceil\frac{1}{fQ}\sum_{i\in S}\mu_{i}\right\rceil,

\displaystyle S\subseteq N,

(4)

\displaystyle\sum_{m\in M}z_{m}=1,

(5)

\displaystyle x_{e}\in\{0,1,2\},

\displaystyle e\in\delta(0),

(6)

\displaystyle x_{e}\in\{0,1\},

\displaystyle e\in E(N),

(7)

\displaystyle z_{m}\in\{0,1\},

\displaystyle m\in M.

(8)

Constraint (
2
) ensures that the number of routes corresponds to the selected fleet size. Constraints (
3
) guarantee that each customer is visited exactly once and that the flow is conserved at every customer node. The rounded capacity inequalities (

) eliminate subtours and ensure that the total expected demand on any route does not exceed the allowed load factor. Constraint (

2.3
Recourse function

In any route-based policy, i.e., when vehicles serve disjoint sets of customers
(
Dror and Trudeau 1986
)
, the total expected recourse cost
\mathcal{Q}(x)
can be decomposed by route. Indeed, a feasible solution to the VRPSD consists of a set of independent undirected cycles on
G
. Each cycle specifies the consecutive visit of a sequence
p=(i_{1},\dots,i_{t})
of customers, which can be visited in either orientation. We denote by
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{p}
the expected recourse cost of route
(0,p,0)
in the specific orientation specified by path
p
, and we let
\mathcal{Q}_{p}:=\min\{\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{t})},\bar{\mathcal{Q}}_{(i_{t},\dots,i_{1})}\}
be its expected recourse cost in its best orientation. We characterize a first-stage solution
x
comprising
m
routes as
\mathcal{P}(x)=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\}
, where
\{N(p_{1}),\dots,N(p_{m})\}
forms a partition of
N
, and the paths
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}
are taken in an arbitrary orientation. The recourse cost of
x
can then be expressed as
\mathcal{Q}(x)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}(x)}\mathcal{Q}_{p}
.

2.3.1
Optimal restocking policy

Under the OR policy, the random demands
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i\in N}
are assumed to be discrete, with probability masses
\{\rho_{i}^{s}:=\mathbb{P}[\xi_{i}=s]\ \forall s\in\Xi_{i}\}_{i\in N}
. For simplicity, we assume that
\Xi_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
for each
i\in N
. For a route
(0,p,0)=(i_{0},i_{1},\dots,i_{t},i_{t+1})
, the expected recourse cost-to-go
F^{p}_{i_{j}}(q)
when the vehicle leaves node
i_{j}
, for
j\in\{0,\dots,t{-}1\}
, with residual capacity
q\in\{0,\dots,Q\}
, is given by the following Bellman equation:

F^{p}_{i_{j}}(q):=\min\begin{cases}H^{p}_{i_{j+1}}(q):=\sum\limits_{s\in\Xi_{i_{j+1}}}\left[c_{i_{j+1}}^{F}\Psi(s,q)+F^{p}_{i_{j+1}}\left(\Psi(s,q)Q+q-s\right)\right]\rho_{i_{j+1}}^{s},\\
H^{*p}_{i_{j},i_{j+1}}:=c_{i_{j},i_{j+1}}^{P}+H^{p}_{i_{j+1}}(Q),\end{cases}

(9)

with the boundary condition
F^{p}_{i_{t}}(q):=0\ \forall q\in[0,Q]
. This boundary reflects that, after serving the last customer
i_{t}
, the vehicle travels back to the depot as planned in the a priori solution, which does not incur a recourse cost. The terms
H^{p}_{i_{j+1}}(q)
and
H^{*p}_{i_{j},i_{j+1}}
denote the cost-to-go of proceeding directly to customer
i_{j+1}
and of making a restocking trip beforehand. Performing a preventive return to the depot between customers
i_{j}
and
i_{j+1}
allows the vehicle to reach customer
i_{j+1}
with full capacity
Q
, but incurs an additional cost of
c_{i_{j},i_{j+1}}^{P}:=b^{P}+c_{0,i_{j}}+c_{0,i_{j+1}}-c_{i_{j},i_{j+1}}
, which comprises the additional travel distance caused by the restocking trip and a fixed penalty term
b^{P}\geq 0
. Since the distance matrix respects the triangle inequality, proceeding to the next customer is always preferable when the vehicle has full capacity, i.e.,
F^{p}_{i_{j}}(Q)=H^{p}_{i_{j+1}}(Q)
. A failure occurs when the residual capacity of the vehicle does not suffice to serve the demand of a customer. Specifically, we denote by
\Psi(s,q):=\lceil(s-q)/Q\rceil^{+}
the number of restocking trips required to fulfill a demand of
s
, given that the residual capacity of the vehicle is
q
. The cost of a failure at customer
i_{j}
is given by
c_{i_{j+1}}^{F}:=b^{F}+2c_{0,i_{j+1}}
. It comprises the round-trip distance between the customer and the depot and a fixed penalty term
b^{F}\geq 0
. We assume that preventive restocking trips are not more penalized than failures, i.e.,
b^{P}\leq b^{F}
. We denote by
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\text{OR}}_{p}:=F^{p}_{i_{0}}(Q)
the total expected recourse cost of the route
(0,p,0)
under the OR policy.

2.3.2
Detour-to-depot policy

Under the DTD policy, the expected recourse cost
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\text{DTD}}_{p}
of route
(0,p,0)=(i_{0},i_{1},\dots,i_{t},i_{t+1})
can be computed from (
9
) by setting
H^{*p}_{i_{j},i_{j+1}}
to
+\infty
in the case of discrete demands, and is more generally defined as:

\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\text{DTD}}_{p}:=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\xi_{i_{k}}\leq lQ<\sum_{k=1}^{j}\xi_{i_{k}}\right]c^{F}_{i_{j}},

(10)

where the factor multiplying each coefficient
c^{F}_{i_{j}}
corresponds to the expected number of failures at customer
i_{j}
.

3
The disaggregated integer L-shaped method

We start by reviewing the standard integer L-shaped method in Section
3.1
. We then provide a formal description of the DL-shaped method in Section
3.2
, and characterize the conditions under which this approach yields a valid exact algorithm for the VRPSD.

3.1
Integer L-shaped method

A textbook integer L-shaped method for the VRPSD (as described in
Laporte and Louveaux 1998
) can be implemented from the edge-flow formulation (
1
)–(
8
) by replacing the recourse function
\mathcal{Q}(x)
by a single auxiliary variable
\Theta\geq 0
. The problem can then be solved through B&C by enforcing the rounded capacity inequalities (
4
) as lazy constraints, and, at each integer node with solution
(x^{\nu},\Theta^{\nu})
such that
\Theta^{\nu}<\mathcal{Q}(x^{\nu})
, separating the classical optimality cut:

\Theta\geq L+(\mathcal{Q}(x^{\nu})-L)\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E(N)\\
x^{\nu}_{e}=1\end{subarray}}x_{e}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E(N)\end{subarray}}x^{\nu}_{e}+1\right),

(11)

where
L\geq 0
is a global lower bound on the expected recourse. Inequality (
11
) reduces to
\Theta\geq\mathcal{Q}(x^{\nu})
if
x=x^{\nu}
, and imposes a lower bound of at most
L
on
\Theta
for any integer solution
x\neq x^{\nu}
. It is thus a valid lower bound on the recourse function
\mathcal{Q}
for any feasible first-stage solution, and is tight at
x=x^{\nu}
. Since the feasible domain of the VRPSD has finite cardinality, these properties suffice to guarantee the finite convergence of the integer L-shaped method to an optimal solution. In practice, this cut has several drawbacks. First, it is only valid for a fixed number of vehicles (
|M|=1
), as discussed in
Hoogendoorn and Spliet 2025
. Furthermore, it is active for a single integer solution, which makes it inefficient at improving the LP relaxation of the master problem. To accelerate convergence, practical implementations such as those of
Laporte et al. 2002
,
Jabali et al. 2014
,
Louveaux and Salazar-González 2018
, and
Salavati-Khoshghalb et al. 2019
also generate LBFs. We refer the reader to the recent works of
Hoogendoorn and Spliet 2023
,
Hoogendoorn and Spliet 2025
for a complete review and corrected versions of the LBFs implemented in the integer L-shaped algorithms from the literature.

3.2
Disaggregated integer L-shaped method

The DL-shaped method introduced by
Parada et al. 2024
decomposes the auxiliary variable
\Theta
into a sum of customer-specific auxiliary variables
\sum_{i\in N}\theta_{i}
. Its master problem can be expressed compactly by introducing some notation. Let
\mathcal{P}=\{\text{$p$ : $\sum_{i\in N(p)}\mu_{i}\leq fQ$}\}
denote the set of feasible paths. From there, for each path
p=(i_{1},\dots,i_{t})\in\mathcal{P}
, define
p(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}x_{i_{j},i_{j+1}}
as the total flow along its edges. For each subset of customers
S\subseteq N
, define
\Pi(S,m):=\left\{\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{m}:\{N(p_{1}),\dots,N(p_{m})\}\text{ partitions $S$}\right\}
as the set of all partitions of
S
into exactly
m
feasible paths,
m(S):=\min\{m\in\mathbb{N}:\Pi(S,m)~\neq~\emptyset\}
as the minimum number of vehicles required to cover
S
, and
\mathcal{L}(S,m):=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(S,m)}\sum_{p\in\pi}\mathcal{Q}_{p}
as the smallest recourse cost achievable by partitioning
S
into exactly
m
feasible paths. Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{S\subseteq N\}
be any collection of subsets of customers, and for each
S\in\mathcal{S}
, let
1\leq m_{S}\leq m(S)
and
0\leq\mathcal{L}_{S}\leq\mathcal{L}(S,m_{S})
be fixed values. The master problem of the DL-shaped method follows.

\displaystyle(\text{MP})\quad\min

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E}c_{e}x_{e}+\sum_{i\in N}\theta_{i}

(12)

s.t.

\displaystyle\text{\eqref{utilizedVeh}--\eqref{z_val}},

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N(p)}\theta_{i}\geq\mathcal{Q}_{p}\left(p(x)-|N(p)|+2\right),

\displaystyle p\in\mathcal{P},

(13)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in S}\theta_{i}\geq\mathcal{L}_{S}\left(\sum_{e\in E(S)}x_{e}-|S|+m_{S}+1\right),

\displaystyle\ S\in\mathcal{S},

(14)

\displaystyle\theta_{i}\geq 0,

\displaystyle i\in N.

(15)

The P-cut (
13
) associated with a path
p\in\mathcal{P}
imposes that the total recourse cost attributed to the customers of
N(p)
be at least the recourse cost
\mathcal{Q}_{p}
of route
(0,p,0)
if
p
is a subpath of
\mathcal{P}(x)
. By contrast, the S-cut (
14
) associated with a set
S\in\mathcal{S}
imposes that the total recourse cost attributed to the customers of
S
be at least
\mathcal{L}_{S}
when there exists
m_{S}
subpaths of
\mathcal{P}(x)
that partition the customers of
S
. The P-cuts and the S-cuts respectively act as optimality cuts and LBFs. In Theorem
1
, we characterize the problems to which the DL-shaped method can be applied.

Definition 1

(Superadditivity property)
.

An instance of the VRPSD satisfies the superadditivity property if
\mathcal{Q}_{(p_{1},p_{2})}\;\geq\;\mathcal{Q}_{p_{1}}+\mathcal{Q}_{p_{2}}\ \forall\,p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
such that
(p_{1},p_{2})~\in~\mathcal{P}
.

Theorem 1
.

The DL-shaped master problem MP is a valid reformulation of problem (
1
)–(
8
) if and only if the superadditivity property holds.

Proof.

We first show that the superadditivity property is necessary by exposing invalid P-cuts if the recourse function is not superadditive. We then show that this property is sufficient for the equivalence of models MP and (
1
)–(
8
).

Necessity

Assume that the recourse function is not superadditive, i.e., there exist two paths
p^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
whose concatenation
p_{1}:=(p^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime})\in\mathcal{P}
satisfies
\mathcal{Q}_{p_{1}}<\mathcal{Q}_{p^{\prime}}+\mathcal{Q}_{p^{\prime\prime}}
. From there, consider any integer first-stage solution
(x^{\nu},z^{\nu})
whose a priori routes include
(0,p_{1},0)
. Such a feasible solution is guaranteed to exist if
\max M\geq m(N)+1
. Denote by
\mathcal{P}(x^{\nu})=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\}\in\Pi(N,m)
the set of paths formed by this solution. Since

and
p^{\prime\prime}(x^{\nu})=|N(p^{\prime\prime})|-1
by construction, the P-cuts (
13
) of
p^{\prime}
and
p^{\prime\prime}
are active at

, and their sum imposes the following lower bound on the auxiliary variables:

\sum_{i\in N(p_{1})}\theta_{i}=\sum_{i\in N(p^{\prime})}\theta_{i}+\sum_{i\in N(p^{\prime\prime})}\theta_{i}\;\geq\;\mathcal{Q}_{p^{\prime}}+\mathcal{Q}_{p^{\prime\prime}}>\mathcal{Q}_{p_{1}}.

(16)

Similarly, the P-cut (
13
) of each path
p_{j}
,
j\in\{2,\dots,m\}
is active at
x=x^{\nu}
. Since
\{N(p_{j})\}_{j=1}^{m}
partitions

, summing these P-cuts yields the following lower bound:

\sum_{i\in N\setminus N(p_{1})}\theta_{i}=\sum_{j=2}^{m}\sum_{i\in N(p_{j})}\theta_{i}\geq\sum_{j=2}^{m}\mathcal{Q}_{p_{j}}

(17)

By adding inequalities (
16
) and (
17
), we obtain that the a priori solution
(x^{\nu},z^{\nu})
yields a strictly higher objective value for MP than for the original formulation:

\sum_{e\in E}c_{e}x^{\nu}_{e}+\sum_{i\in N}\theta_{i}>\sum_{e\in E}c_{e}x^{\nu}_{e}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{Q}_{p_{j}}=\sum_{e\in E}c_{e}x^{\nu}_{e}+\mathcal{Q}(x^{\nu}).

We conclude that the DL-shaped method is invalid if the recourse function is not superadditive.

Sufficiency

Assume the recourse function is superadditive.
Let
(x^{\nu},z^{\nu})
be an integer-feasible solution of the first-stage
constraints (
2
)–(
8
), and
\mathcal{P}(x^{\nu})=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\}\in\Pi(N,m)
be the set of paths it forms. The original formulation’s recourse evaluates to:

\mathcal{Q}(x^{\nu})\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{Q}_{p_{j}}.

(18)

For every path
p_{j}\in\mathcal{P}(x^{\nu})
the P-cut (
13
) reads

\sum_{i\in N(p_{j})}\theta_{i}\geq\mathcal{Q}_{p_{j}}
. Summing over over

gives the lower bound:

\sum_{i\in N}\theta_{i}\;\geq\;\mathcal{Q}(x^{\nu}).

(19)

It follows from (
18
) and (
19
) that the solution
(x^{\nu},z^{\nu})
yields an objective value that is not smaller for MP than for the original formulation. To conclude the proof, it remains to exhibit an assignment
\{\theta^{\nu}_{i}\}_{i\in N}
of the auxiliary variables that satisfies constraints (
13
)–(
15
) and sums to
\mathcal{Q}(x^{\nu})
.

For each path
p_{j}=(i^{j}_{1},\dots,i^{j}_{t_{j}})\in\mathcal{P}(x^{\nu})
, we set each
\theta^{\nu}_{i^{j}_{k}}
,
k\in\{1,\dots,t_{j}\}
, to the incremental contribution of customer
i^{j}_{k}
to the expected recourse of the partial path
(i^{j}_{1},\dots,i^{j}_{k-1})

\theta^{\nu}_{i^{j}_{k}}=\Delta_{i^{j}_{k}}:=\mathcal{Q}_{(i^{j}_{1},\dots,i^{j}_{k})}-\mathcal{Q}_{(i^{j}_{1},\dots,i^{j}_{k-1})},

where
Q_{()}=0
. First, superadditivity implies that each incremental contribution satisfies

, and the non-negativity constraints (
15
) are thus respected. Telescoping yields:

\sum_{i\in N(p_{j})}\theta^{\nu}_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{t_{j}}\Delta_{i^{j}_{k}}=\mathcal{Q}_{p_{j}},

and we indeed verify that
\sum_{i\in N}\theta^{\nu}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{Q}_{p_{j}}=\mathcal{Q}(x^{\nu})
. From there, we show that no P-cut (
13
) is violated at
(x,\theta)=(x^{\nu},\theta^{\nu})
. Consider an arbitrary path
p\in\mathcal{P}
. First, if there is no path
p_{j}\in\mathcal{P}(x^{\nu})
of which
p
is a subpath, then
p(x^{\nu})\leq|N(p)|-2
. The right-hand side of the P-cut is then non-positive at

, and the P-cut is trivially satisfied. Otherwise,
p=(i^{j}_{a},\dots,i^{j}_{b})
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and

, and the right-hand side is
\mathcal{Q}_{p}
. The left-hand side evaluates to:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N(p)}\theta^{\nu}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=a}^{b}\Delta_{i^{j}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=a}^{b}\left(\mathcal{Q}_{(i^{j}_{1},\dots,i^{j}_{k})}-\mathcal{Q}_{(i^{j}_{1},\dots,i^{j}_{k-1})}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}_{(i^{j}_{1},\dots,i^{j}_{b})}-\mathcal{Q}_{(i^{j}_{1},\dots,i^{j}_{a-1})}

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{Q}_{p},

where the inequality applies superadditivity to the split

, which gives:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{(i^{j}_{1},\dots,i^{j}_{b})}\geq\mathcal{Q}_{(i^{j}_{1},\dots,i^{j}_{a-1})}+\mathcal{Q}_{(i^{j}_{a},\dots,i^{j}_{b})}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{(i^{j}_{1},\dots,i^{j}_{b})}-\mathcal{Q}_{(i^{j}_{1},\dots,i^{j}_{a-1})}\geq\mathcal{Q}_{(i^{j}_{a},\dots,i^{j}_{b})}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{(i^{j}_{1},\dots,i^{j}_{b})}-\mathcal{Q}_{(i^{j}_{1},\dots,i^{j}_{a-1})}\geq\mathcal{Q}_{p}

(x,\theta)=(x^{\nu},\theta^{\nu})

the subpaths that form a partition of

in the solution, where for each

\mathcal{P}(x^{\nu})
of which

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in S}\theta_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{S}}\sum_{i\in N(\bar{p}_{j_{k}})}\theta_{i}\geq\sum_{k=1}^{m_{S}}\mathcal{Q}_{\bar{p}_{j_{k}}}\geq\mathcal{L}_{S},

For each subset of customers

, and provided a lower bound
m_{S}
on the number of vehicles needed to serve them, the S-cut (
14
) requires a lower bound
\mathcal{L}_{S}
on
\mathcal{L}(S,m_{S})
, the smallest recourse cost of any set of routes partitioning
S
into
m_{S}
paths. In Section
5.1.1
, we present a general method to construct this lower bound when
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}^{\text{OR}}
. In Section
5.1.2
, this method is specialized to the case of Poisson-distributed demands.

5.1.1
General lower bound

Let
S=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{|S|}\}\subseteq N
be a set of customers, and index the vehicles serving them as
k\in\{1,\dots,m_{S}\}
. Since the fleet is homogeneous, we can assume without loss of generality that for each
j\in\{1,\dots,m_{S}\}
, customer
i_{j}
will be visited by a vehicle
k\leq j
. For each
k\in\{1,\dots,m_{S}\}
, let
c^{F}_{(k)}:=\min_{j\geq k}c^{F}_{i_{j}}
and
c^{P}_{(k)}:=\min_{j,l\geq k}c^{P}_{i_{j},i_{l}}
respectively denote the smallest failure cost and the smallest preventive return cost accessible to vehicle
k
, and let
c^{R}_{(k)}:=\min\{c^{F}_{(k)},c^{P}_{(k)}\}
be the cost of the cheapest recourse action this vehicle can perform. Also, for
d\in\{0,\dots,\lfloor fQ\rfloor\}
, let
\rho(d)\leq\mathds{P}\left(\sum_{i\in S^{\prime}}\xi_{i}>Q\right)
be a lower bound on the probability that a vehicle serving a set of customers
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
with total expected demands
\sum_{i\in S^{\prime}}\mu_{i}=d
needs at least one recourse action (either a failure or a preventive restocking trip) to complete its route.

By construction,
g_{k}(d):=\rho(d)c^{R}_{(k)}
is a lower bound on the expected recourse cost of vehicle
k
given that the total expected demands of the customers it visits is
d
. A lower bound on
\mathcal{L}(S,m_{S})
can thus be constructed by partitioning the expected demands
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\in S}
into vehicles 1 to
m_{S}
to minimize the sum of the single-vehicle functions
\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m_{S}}
. In particular, solving a relaxation of this minimization problem also gives a lower bound on
\mathcal{L}(S,m_{S})
. We construct such a relaxation by replacing the set of expectations
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\in S}
by
\bar{D}:=D/\bar{\mu}
undistinguishable groups of
\bar{\mu}:=\text{GCD}(\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\in S})
units of expected demand. The resulting problem is formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{1}_{S}:=\min

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m_{S}}g_{k}(d_{k})

(29)

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m_{S}}d_{k}=D,

(30)

\displaystyle d_{k}=n_{k}\bar{\mu},

\displaystyle k\in\{1,\dots,m_{S}\},

(31)

\displaystyle n_{k}\in\{0,1,\dots,\bar{n}\},

\displaystyle k\in\{1,\dots,m_{S}\}.

(32)

where
\bar{n}:=\lfloor fQ/\bar{\mu}\rfloor
is the largest number of groups of
\bar{\mu}
units of expected demand that can be assigned to a vehicle under the allowed load factor.

Problem (
29
)–(
32
) can be solved by dynamic programming, where the minimum cost of distributing
\bar{d}\in\{0,\dots,\bar{D}\}
groups of expected demand into vehicles
1
to
k
is expressed for each
k\in\{1,\dots,m_{S}\}
as:

G_{k}(\bar{d}):=\begin{cases}\min\limits_{y\in\{0,1,\dots,\min\{\bar{d},\bar{n}\}\}}(g_{k}(y\bar{\mu})+G_{k-1}(\bar{d}-y)),&\text{ if }k\in\{2,\dots,m_{S}\},\\
g_{1}(\bar{d}\bar{\mu}),&\text{ if }k=1\text{ and }\bar{d}\leq\bar{n},\\
+\infty,&\text{ if }k=1\text{ and }\bar{d}>\bar{n},\end{cases}

(33)

Theorem
7
directly follows from this construction.

Theorem 7
.

For
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}^{\text{OR}}
,
\mathcal{L}^{1}_{S}:=G_{m_{S}}(\bar{D})
is a valid lower bound for the S-cut (
14
), i.e.,
\mathcal{L}^{1}_{S}\leq\mathcal{L}(S,m_{S})
.

We select the indices
i_{1},\dots,i_{m_{S}}
greedily by assigning at each iteration
k=1,\dots,m_{S}-1
to index
i_{k}
the unassigned customer that leads to the largest minimum recourse cost
c^{R}_{(k+1)}
for the next vehicle. In case of equality, the candidate customer with the smallest failure cost is selected. We apply the bound
\mathcal{L}^{1}_{S}
only to instances in which the demands are identically distributed with expectation
\mu
. We thus precompute the probability of mandatory recourse action as
\rho(t\mu)=\mathds{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t}\xi_{i}>Q\right)
for each number of customers
t\in\{0,\dots,\lfloor fQ/\mu\rfloor\}
.

5.1.2
Distribution-specific lower bound

We build on the construction of the previous section and keep the same notation. Consider an arbitrary path
p_{k}
visited by vehicle
k\in\{1,\dots,m_{S}\}
, with
N(p_{k})\subseteq\{i_{k},\dots,i_{|S|}\}
the set of customers it covers, and
d_{k}=\sum_{i\in N(p_{k})}\mu_{i}
their total expected demand. Decompose each customer
i\in N(p_{k})
into
\mu_{i}/\bar{\mu}
subcustomers whose demands are modeled as independent and identically distributed Poisson random variables with expectation
\bar{\mu}
, and consider a path derived from
p_{k}
, where all the subcustomers of the same group are visited consecutively. Denote by
p^{\prime}_{k}
this modified path. The OR policy on
p_{k}
corresponds to a restricted version of the OR policy on
p^{\prime}_{k}
, in which preventive returns are prohibited between subcustomers of the same group. It follows that
\mathcal{Q}^{\text{OR}}_{p^{\prime}_{k}}\leq\mathcal{Q}^{\text{OR}}_{p_{k}}
. From there, by replacing each failure cost by its lower bound
c^{F}_{(k)}
and each preventive return cost by its lower bound
c^{P}_{(k)}
in the cost-to-go recursion (
9
) of path
p^{\prime}_{k}
, we can compute a lower bound on
\mathcal{Q}^{\text{OR}}_{p^{\prime}_{k}}
that does not depend on the sequence of visited customers in
p_{k}
, but only on
N(p_{k})
. As such, for a residual capacity
q\in\{0,\dots,Q\}
and
\bar{d}\in\{1,\dots,\bar{n}\}
subcustomers remaining to serve, the onward recourse cost-to-go of vehicle
k
admits the lower bound:

\widetilde{F}^{k}_{\bar{d}}(q):=\min\begin{cases}\widetilde{H}^{k}_{\bar{d}}(q):=&\sum\limits_{s=0}^{+\infty}\left[c_{(k)}^{F}\Psi(s,q)+\widetilde{F}^{k}_{\bar{d}-1}\left(\Psi(s,q)Q+q-s\right)\right]\rho^{s},\\
\widetilde{H}^{*k}_{\bar{d}}:=&c_{(k)}^{P}+\widetilde{H}^{k}_{\bar{d}}(Q),\end{cases}

where
\rho^{s}
denotes the probability mass function of the Poisson distribution with expectation
\bar{\mu}
evaluated at
s
, and the boundary condition is
\widetilde{F}^{k}_{0}(q^{\prime}):=0,\forall q^{\prime}\in\{0,\dots,Q\}
.

For each vehicle
k\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and each value
\bar{d}\in\{0,\dots,\bar{n}\}
, we denote by
\widetilde{g}_{k}(\bar{d}\bar{\mu}):=\widetilde{F}^{k}_{\bar{d}}(Q)
the resulting lower bound on the expected recourse cost of the route of vehicle
k
if it is assigned
\bar{d}\bar{\mu}
units of expected demand. By replacing
g_{k}
by
\widetilde{g}_{k}
in the dynamic programming equation (
33
), we obtain a new lower bound on the cost of distributing
\bar{d}\in\{0,\dots,\bar{D}\}
groups of
\bar{\mu}
units of expected demand into vehicles
1
to
k
, which we denote by
\widetilde{G}_{k}(\bar{d})
. Theorem
8
directly follows from this construction.

Theorem 8
.

For
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}^{\text{OR}}
, if
\xi_{i}\sim\text{Poisson}(\mu_{i})

\forall i\in S
,
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{S}:=\widetilde{G}_{m_{S}}(\bar{D})
is a valid lower bound for the S-cut (
14
), i.e.,
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{S}\leq\mathcal{L}(S,m_{S})
.

5.2
New valid inequalities

As for standard integer L-shaped methods, the performance of the DL-shaped method critically relies on the strength of its LBFs. However, irrespective of the quality of the lower bound
\mathcal{L}_{S}
on
\mathcal{L}(S,m_{S})
, the S-cuts (
14
) cannot efficiently tighten the approximation of the recourse function for sets
S\in\mathcal{S}
that can be partitioned into
m_{S}
paths with vanishing recourse costs. This is a common issue under the OR policy, where recourse costs can often be moved to the first stage by constructing long routes that make available cheap preventive recourse actions. We illustrate this behavior with a small example.

, with capacity

, and penalty parameters
b^{F}=b^{P}=0
. Under the OR policy, each path
p\in\{(1,2,3,4),(2,3,4,1),(3,4,1,2),(4,1,2,3)\}
yields an optimal route
(0,p,0)
with first-stage cost
5
and second-stage cost
\mathcal{Q}^{\text{OR}}_{p}=\frac{1}{8}
. Now, observe that any feasible route that comprises edge
\{1,3\}
or
\{2,4\}
has first-stage cost 7, and is thus suboptimal, but has a second-stage cost of 0. Indeed, such routes allow preventive restocking trips with cost
c^{P}_{i,j}=0
, and any preventive restocking trip ensures that no failure can occur in this example. Consequently,
\mathcal{L}(N,1)=0
, and the S-cut of set
S=N
can only impose a trivial lower bound on the recourse function. Furthermore, any strict subset
S\subset N
of the customers satisfies
\sum_{i\in S}\xi_{i}\leq Q
with probability 1, which implies that
\mathcal{L}(S,1)=0
. It follows that S-cuts cannot provide strictly positive lower bounds for this instance. In this context, the DL-shaped method thus exclusively relies on P-cuts to approximate the recourse function. Here, at least four P-cuts, one for each optimal path
p\in\{(1,2,3,4),(2,3,4,1),(3,4,1,2),(4,1,2,3)\}
, must be generated to achieve convergence. These P-cuts read:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N}\theta_{i}\geq\frac{1}{8}\left(x_{(1,2)}+x_{(2,3)}+x_{(3,4)}-2\right)

(34)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N}\theta_{i}\geq\frac{1}{8}\left(x_{(2,3)}+x_{(3,4)}+x_{(1,4)}-2\right)

(35)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N}\theta_{i}\geq\frac{1}{8}\left(x_{(3,4)}+x_{(1,4)}+x_{(1,2)}-2\right)

(36)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N}\theta_{i}\geq\frac{1}{8}\left(x_{(1,4)}+x_{(1,2)}+x_{(2,3)}-2\right)

(37)

By contrast, under the DTD policy, any feasible path
p
visiting the four customers provides the same recourse cost
\mathcal{Q}^{\text{DTD}}_{p}=\frac{1}{8}
. For
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{Q}^{\text{DTD}}
, we thus have
\mathcal{L}(N,1)=\frac{1}{8}
, and the recourse function can be represented exactly by a single S-cut associated with the set of customers
S=N
:

\sum_{i\in S}\theta_{i}\geq\mathcal{L}_{S}\left(\sum_{e\in E(S)}x_{e}-|S|+m_{S}+1\right)=\frac{1}{8}\left(\sum_{e\in E(S)}x_{e}-2\right)

As illustrated in this example, edges
\{i,j\}\in E(S)
with small preventive return cost
c^{P}_{i,j}
can make the S-cuts very weak for the OR recourse function. To obtain stronger LBFs, we propose a generalization of the S-cuts that bound the recourse for a restricted set of the feasible partitions of
S
into paths. Specifically, this subset is defined based on the edges that can compose these paths. We call these E-cuts, and formally introduce them here.

For a set of customers
S\subseteq N
and an edge set
E_{S}\subseteq E(S)
, let
\bar{\mathcal{P}}(E_{S})\subseteq\mathcal{P}
denote the set of feasible paths on the subgraph
(S,E_{S})
of
G
. Define
\bar{\Pi}(E_{S},m):=\left\{\pi\in\Pi(S,m):\pi\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{P}}(E_{S})\right\}
as the set of all partitions of
S
into exactly
m
feasible paths formed only of edges of
E_{S}
,
\bar{m}(E_{S}):=\min\{m\in\mathbb{N}:\bar{\Pi}(E_{S},m)\neq\emptyset\}
as the minimum number of vehicles needed to cover the customers of
S
on graph
(S,E_{S})
, and
\bar{\mathcal{L}}(E_{S},m):=\min_{\pi\in\bar{\Pi}(E_{S},m)}\sum_{p\in\pi}\mathcal{Q}_{p}
as the smallest recourse cost achievable by partitioning
S
into exactly
m
of these feasible paths. The E-cut of the edge set
E_{S}
is given by:

\sum_{i\in S}\theta_{i}\geq\mathcal{L}_{E_{S}}\left(\sum_{e\in E_{S}}x_{e}-|S|+m_{E_{S}}+1\right),

(38)

where
1\leq m_{E_{S}}\leq\bar{m}(E_{S})
and
0\leq\mathcal{L}_{E_{S}}\leq\bar{\mathcal{L}}(E_{S},m)
are fixed values.

Theorem 9
.

The E-cut (
38
) is a valid inequality if the superadditivity property holds.

Proof.

The construction described in the proof of Theorem
1
to show that the S-cuts are implied by the P-cuts directly extends to the E-cuts.
∎

Theorem 10
.

The E-cuts generalize the P-cuts and the S-cuts.

Proof.

The definition of the S-cut associated with a set
S\subseteq N
is recovered by taking
E_{S}=E(S)
. The P-cut of a path
p=(i_{1},\dots,i_{t})\in\mathcal{P}
corresponds to the strongest valid E-cut for customers
S=N(p)
and the edge set
E_{S}=\{\{i_{j},i_{j+1}\}\}_{j=1}^{t-1}
.
∎

In practice, building good E-cuts involves a trade-off, as restricting the set
E_{S}
increases the bound
\bar{\mathcal{L}}(E_{S},m)
, but reduces the set of solutions for which the E-cut is active. We propose a simple edge-set selection strategy that proves to be effective. Let
x^{\nu}
be a first-stage solution, either integer or fractional, and let
S\subseteq N
be an arbitrary set of customers. We select the edge set
E_{S}=E_{S}(x^{\nu}):=\{e\in E(S)|c^{P}_{e}\geq c^{P}_{e^{\nu}}\}
by excluding the edges whose preventive return cost is strictly less than
c^{P}_{e^{\nu}}
, where
e^{\nu}\in\argmin_{e\in E(S):x^{\nu}_{e}>0}c^{P}_{e}
is an active edge with minimal preventive return cost. In particular, this definition ensures that the generated E-cut is active for the current solution. Finally, to compute the lower bound
\mathcal{L}_{E_{S}}
on
\bar{\mathcal{L}}(E_{S},m_{E_{S}})
, the S-cut bounds
\mathcal{L}^{1}_{S}
and
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{S}
can be directly applied by replacing
c^{P}_{e}
by
+\infty
in the definition of
c^{P}_{(k)}
for each edge
e\in E(S)\setminus\bar{E}
. This yields the analogous lower bounds
\mathcal{L}^{1}_{E_{S}}
and
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{E_{S}}
, which respectively apply for general demand distributions and Poisson-distributed demands.

Going back to our example, we observe that, for
S=N
, applying the above selection strategy to any solution in which the expensive edges
\{1,3\}
and
\{2,4\}
are inactive yields the edge-set
E_{S}=E(S)\setminus\{\{1,3\},\{2,4\}\}
, with lower bound
\bar{L}(E_{S},1)=\frac{1}{8}
. This makes the following inequality a valid E-cut:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in N}\theta_{i}\geq\frac{1}{8}\left(x_{(1,2)}+x_{(2,3)}+x_{(3,4)}+x_{(1,4)}-2\right)

(39)

Since the E-cut (
39
) implies the four P-cuts (
34
)–(
37
), it provides an exact representation of the OR recourse function via a single DL-shaped cut.

5.3
Implementation

We now describe our B&C implementation of the DL-shaped method. The linear relaxation of MP is solved at each node of the B&B tree. After obtaining a solution
(x^{\nu},\{\theta_{i}^{\nu}\}_{i\in N})
, we generate the rounded capacity inequalities (
4
), the P-cuts (
13
), the S-cuts (
14
), and the E-cuts (
38
) as follows:

1.

We heuristically separate violated rounded capacity inequalities (
4
) using the CVRPSEP package
(
Lysgaard 2003
)
and add the identified inequalities to the model. For each set of nodes
S
associated with a violated rounded capacity inequality, we check whether the corresponding S-cut and E-cut are violated as well; if so, we also add them to the model.

2.

If the solution is fractional, we identify its connected components. For each connected component
S\subseteq N
, if the associated S-cut and E-cut are violated, we add them to the model. Furthermore, if the solution includes a path that covers
S
, i.e.,
\left|\{e\in E(S):x^{\nu}_{e}>0\}\right|=|S|-1
, we also add the associated P-cut to the model if it is violated.

3.

If the solution is integer and there are no violated rounded capacity inequalities, we extract all the subpaths
p^{\prime}
from
\mathcal{P}(x^{\nu})
. For each subpath, we consider the associated customers
S=N(p^{\prime})
and evaluate the P-cut of path
p^{\prime}
, the S-cut of set
S
, and the E-cut of edge-set
E_{S}(x^{\nu})
. We then add the six most violated cuts of each type (P-cuts, S-cuts, and E-cuts) to the model.

The procedure ends when all the nodes of the B&B have been explored. The incumbent solution is then optimal. In our implementation, we also generate an initial pool of cuts comprising all the nontrivial S-cuts for sets of customers of cardinality
|S|\in\{2,3,4\}
for instances with
n\leq 32
customers, and of cardinality
|S|\in\{2,3\}
for larger instances. For these S-cuts, we compute
\mathcal{L}(S,1)
by enumeration, and take
\mathcal{L}_{S}=\mathcal{L}(S,1)
. For the S-cuts and E-cuts generated in callbacks for a set
S\subseteq N
, we take
m_{S}=m_{E_{S}}=\Big\lceil\frac{1}{\lfloor fQ/\bar{\mu}\rfloor\bar{\mu}}\sum_{i\in N}\mu_{i}\Big\rceil
, where
\bar{\mu}=\text{GCD}(\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\in S})
, and use the lower bounds of Section
5.1
.

6
Computational results

Our computational study is structured as follows. In Section
6.2
, we evaluate the impact of our new valid inequalities on the overall performance of the DL-shaped algorithm. In Section
6.3
, we compare our algorithm to state-of-the-art methods from the literature on standard benchmark instances of the VRPSD under the OR policy. In Section
6.4
, we report results on a new set of instances and discuss instance characteristics that affect the performance of our algorithm. The instances and our computational setup are presented in Section
6.1
.

6.1
Instances and experimental setup

To compare our algorithm with methods from the literature, we use two existing sets of benchmark instances. The first one, proposed by
Louveaux and Salazar-González 2018
, is composed of 32 instances in which the demands are identically distributed according to a discrete symmetric triangular distribution. The second set was generated based on 90 CVRP instances (sets A, B, E, F, and P) from the CVRPLIB repository
(
Uchoa et al. 2017
)
. The customer demands follow the Poisson distribution with expected values matching the deterministic demands from the original CVRP instances.

In addition, we adapt two benchmark sets from
Jabali et al. 2014
and
Parada et al. 2024
. These instances were originally designed for the VRPSD under the DTD policy, and use normally distributed demands. The set of
Jabali et al. 2014
contains 270 instances, where the number of customers ranges from 40 to 80, and the number of vehicles ranges from two to four. That of
Parada et al. 2024
comprises 1,980 instances with the number of customers and the number of vehicles ranging from 20 to 120 and two to seven. We modify these instances by assuming that the customer demands are Poisson-distributed, with the same expectations as in the original instances. As the distance matrix provided in existing instances may violate the triangle inequality, we use the Floyd-Warshall algorithm to enforce this assumption in our experiments. To facilitate the comparison with previous works, the penalty parameters are set to
b^{F}=b^{P}=0
for all the instances, and the load factor parameter is set to
f=1
, unless otherwise specified.

The DL-shaped method was implemented in C
++
with CPLEX version 22.1 using callbacks, and compiled using g
++
. It was run single-threaded on a computing cluster node with an AMD EPYC™ Rome 7532 2.4 GHz processor and 48 GB of RAM. We used an adaptive large neighborhood search algorithm
(
Pisinger and Ropke 2007
)
to warm-start the model. A time limit of 1 hour per instance was applied for all the experiments.

6.3
Comparison with state-of-the-art algorithms

In this section, we compare our DL-shaped algorithm with two integer L-shaped algorithms from the literature (LSG18, presented in
Louveaux and Salazar-González 2018
and HS25, presented in
Hoogendoorn and Spliet 2025
), and a BP&C algorithm (FHM20, presented in
Florio et al. 2020a
). The results we report for each existing method are taken from the corresponding paper. Note that an improved BP&C algorithm was proposed in
Florio et al. 2023
, but we report results from
Florio et al. 2020a
since the experiments therein are more extensive. The conclusions we draw from our comparisons with FHM20 regarding the performance profile of BP&C methods also apply to the improved algorithm.

We consider the four variants of the VRPSD studied in
Hoogendoorn and Spliet 2025
. These variants are distinguished by the inclusion or relaxation of the
fixed route constraint
(FRC) and
expected capacity constraints
(ECCs). The FRC fixes the number of vehicles (
|M|=1
), and the ECCs prohibit routes whose total expected demand exceeds the vehicle capacity (
f=1
). These constraints can be relaxed by taking
M~=~\left\{\left\lceil\frac{1}{fQ}\sum_{i\in N}\mu_{i}\right\rceil,\dots,n\right\}
and
f~=~\sum_{i\in N}\mu_{i}/Q
. The variants VRPSD, ECC-VRPSD, FRC-VRPSD, and Basic-VRPSD include both the FRC and ECCs, only the ECCs, only the FRC, and none of these constraints, respectively. As observed by

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we provided a comprehensive analysis of the DL-shaped method for the VRPSD. In particular, we characterized a superadditivity property of the recourse function that is both necessary and sufficient for the validity of the method. We established that this superadditivity property holds under suitable conditions for the DTD policy, and without further assumptions for the OR policy. We developed new lower bounds on the OR recourse function based on dynamic programming and introduced new valid inequalities generalizing both the optimality cuts and the LBFs of the original DL-shaped method. We proposed a practical implementation of the DL-shaped method under the OR policy. Our algorithm outperforms the state-of-the-art B&C methods from the literature, particularly in instances comprising a small number of long routes. Future works on the DL-shaped method for the VRPSD could focus on developing stronger policy-specific bounds, other edge-selection strategies for the E-cuts, and exploring hybrid decomposition strategies to leverage the complementarity of the B&C and BP&C methods. Our results for the single-vehicle variant of the VRPSD could also motivate the development of DL-shaped methods for stochastic variants of the traveling salesman problem.
