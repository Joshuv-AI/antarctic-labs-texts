The Boundary Harnack Principle and the 3 ​ G 3G Principle in Fractal-Type Spaces
Anthony Graves-McCleary, Laurent Saloff-Coste

Abstract
We prove a generalized version of the 3 ​ G 3G Principle for Green’s functions on bounded inner uniform domains in a wide class of Dirichlet spaces. In particular, our results apply to higher-dimensional fractals such as Sierpinski carpets in 𝐑 n \mathbf{R}^{n} , n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , as well as generalized fractal-type spaces that do not have a well-defined Hausdorff dimension or walk dimension. This yields new instances of the 3 ​ G 3G Principle for these spaces. We also discuss applications to Schrödinger operators.

Introduction

We are interested in the behavior of a Green’s function
G(x,y)
in a domain at points near the boundary of the domain, as well as ratios of the Green’s function across the entire domain. As a warm up and to introduce many of the concepts involved, we start with a section in Euclidean space. Then we make the leap to the abstract setting, working in domains in metric measure spaces with no
a priori

Principle, a concept linked to the main theorem and defined in

Classical Preliminaries and Motivation

For this section, work in the open unit ball
B
in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
with
n\geq 3
. Let
\alpha(n)
denote the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
and let
c_{n}=\frac{1}{n(n-2)\alpha(n)}
. For
x,y\in B
,
x\neq y
and
y\neq 0

G_{B}(x,y)=c_{n}\left(\left\|x-y\right\|^{2-n}-\left(\left\|y\right\|\left\|x-\frac{y}{\left\|y\right\|^{2}}\right\|\right)^{2-n}\right).

Then if
x\neq 0
let

G_{B}(x,0)=c_{n}\left(\left\|x\right\|^{2-n}-1\right).

This is the classical Green’s function for
B
. Note that for any
\xi\in\partial B
and any
x\in B
we have
\lim_{y\rightarrow\xi}G_{B}(x,y)=0
. In fact, for
x\in B
fixed and
y\in B
sufficiently close to
\partial B
,

resembles the distance to the boundary
d(y,\partial B)
, up to constants.

In other domains such as cones, the Green’s function
G(x,y)
may have different boundary decay, but in classical settings it can controlled by the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle. Let
D
be a domain in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. With an eye towards generalizing, write
d(x,y)=\left\|x-y\right\|
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
D
be a domain let
G
be a function on
D\times D
. We say that
D
satisfies the
Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle
for
G
if there exist constants
r_{0},M,C>0
such that for all
\xi\in\partial D
,
0<r\leq r_{0}
, and for all
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in D
with
Md(x_{j},\xi)<r\leq d(y_{j},\xi)
,

\frac{G(x_{1},y_{1})}{G(x_{2},y_{1})}\leq C\frac{G(x_{1},y_{2})}{G(x_{2},y_{2})}.

(2.1)

Note that in the inequality
2.1
,
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
can be switched. The definition of the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle for more abstract domains is exactly the same. This is all stated in terms of
G
, but when
G
is truly the Green’s function of the domain
D
, this implies a similar bound for any two positive harmonic functions
u,v>0
defined around
\xi
that vanish on
\partial D
. See Lierl
[
15
]
for further details.

The Euclidean open unit ball
B
satisfies the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle for its Green’s function
G_{B}
. The Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle has a rich history and has been generalized with regard to both the domain and the kind of Green’s function it concerns. It originated in papers by Ancona
[
2
]
, Dahlberg
[
11
]
and Wu
[
20
]
in the late 1970s. See Armitage and Gardiner
[
3
]
section 8.7 for a textbook presentation in a Lipschitz domain in Euclidean space. See Barlow and Murugan
[
7
]
for a more comprehensive review as well as a state-of-the-art proof on inner uniform domains in Dirichlet spaces satisfying only an elliptic Harnack inequality.

Our other central concept, the
3G
Principle, arises from the desire to control ratios of the Green’s function on the entire domain. This is particularly important in the study of conditional Brownian motion and Schrödinger operators, which we will discuss in section 6. In the ball, there is a constant such that for any
x,y,z\in B
,

\frac{G_{B}(x,z)G_{B}(y,z)}{G_{B}(x,y)}\leq C\left(\left\|x-z\right\|^{2-n}+\left\|y-z\right\|^{2-n}\right).

(2.2)

Notably
\left\|x-z\right\|^{2-n}
is, up to a constant, the Green’s function of all of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. Therefore this “
3G
” ratio of Green’s functions in the ball can be controlled by Green’s functions from a larger domain. In generalizing this, we can avoid reference to any such larger domains by use of an additional benchmark Green’s function. We are now ready to state our version of the
3G
Principle.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
D
be a domain and let
G
be a function on
D\times D
. Fix
o\in D
and let
g(x)=G(x,o)\wedge 1
be the benchmark Green’s function. We say that
D
satisfies the
Generalized
3G
Principle
with respect to
G
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
x,y,z\in D
we have

\frac{G(x,z)G(y,z)}{G(x,y)}\leq C\left(\frac{g(z)}{g(x)}G(x,z)+\frac{g(z)}{g(y)}G(z,y)\right).

(2.3)

In practice this will be independent of choice of
o\in D
, up to constants. The presence of
g
is necessary in many cases due to boundary vanishing of the Green’s function. In applications, the expression
\frac{g(z)}{g(x)}G(x,z)
will be bounded above by the Green’s function of the entire space, therefore relating this back to
2.2
. See Lemma
4.2
below for more details. The
2.2
form of the
3G
Principle appears for bounded Lipschitz domains in Euclidean space in Cranson, Fabes, and Zhao
[
10
]
with a view towards Schrödinger operators, which we will discuss in a later section. The Generalized
3G
Principle appears in Riahi
[
19
]
. The proofs of the
3G
Principle almost always make use of the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle, which raises the question of whether they are equivalent. Hansen
[
13
]
demonstrated the equivalence between the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle and a form of the
3G
Principle (see Definition
2.4
below) in an abstract setting that, in particular, applies to bounded uniform domains in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 3
.

We now state an alternate form of the Generalized
3G
Principle which is routine to verify as equivalent to the original.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
D
be a domain and let

be a function on

. Fix

be the benchmark Green’s function. Also let

3G

\frac{1}{\widetilde{G}(x,y)}\leq C\left(\frac{1}{\widetilde{G}(x,z)}+\frac{1}{\widetilde{G}(z,y)}\right).

(2.4)

Proof:
Trivial.∎

This alteration is the same as stating that
\widetilde{G}(x,z)\wedge\widetilde{G}(z,y)\leq C\widetilde{G}(x,y)
. This suggests the following strengthening:

Definition 2.4
.

Let
D
be a domain and let
G
be a function on
D\times D
. Fix

(2.5)

It is this version of the
3G
Principle that we will focus on in our main theorem

To close this section, let us remark about the kinds of domains we will study. In Euclidean space, the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle and the Generalized
3G
Principle do not hold on all domains even in Euclidean space with classical Green’s function. We will impose some structure on our domains to get results, although it is a worthwhile question whether our assumptions can be weakened. Up this point, the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle has mainly been studied on inner uniform domains, to be defined in section 5. Some Euclidean domains, such as the slit disk
D=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbf{R}^{2}\colon x^{2}+y^{2}<1\right\}\setminus\left\{(x,0)\colon 0\leq x<1\right\}
are inner uniform with a boundary different from their usual Euclidean boundary.

Inner uniformity requires the notion of length of curves. We avoid this for now by substituting the interior corkscrew condition plus the (classical) Harnack inequality, defined later in
3.5
.

3
Abstract Setting

In this section we establish the abstract setting for our main theorem
3.6
. We are working with the least amount of information needed to express a Green’s function as well as control its behavior. Notably we will not make direct reference to harmonicity, so we eschew the use of Dirichlet spaces in this section. They will appear in the examples. Our ambient space will be a metric measure space:

Definition 3.1
.

A

(X,d,\mu)
where
(X,d)
is a metric space and
\mu

.

Given a metric measure space

. Given a subset

we let
\partial D
denote its boundary and
\delta(\cdot)=d(\cdot,\partial D)
. Given

we let
B(y)=B(y,\delta(y)/2)
and
D(y)=D\setminus B(y)

To work with our abstract Green’s function, we need some amount of control over the volume of balls.

Definition 3.2

A metric measure space

such that for all

C^{-1}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{\beta}\leq\frac{\Psi(R)}{\Psi(r)}\leq C\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{\beta^{\prime}}.

(3.1)

The scale and volume functions inspire the following general definition.

Definition 3.4
.

A function

for all

For example, if

is doubling for each

In this section let
(X,d,\mu)
be a metric measure space satisfying (VD). Let
\Psi
be a scale function for

. Let

be open and precompact. We will consider a function

Assumption 3.5
.

There exist constants
M\geq 3
,
r_{0}>0
,

,

such that the following four properties hold:

(i)
Interior Corkscrew Property: For every
\xi\in\partial D
and all

\delta(x)>r/M

(ii)
Quasi-symmetry: For all

G(x,y)\leq C_{0}G(y,x).

(3.2)

(iii)
Quasi-polynomial decay: For all

G(x,y)\leq C_{0}\frac{\Psi(d(x,y))}{V(x,d(x,y))}.

(3.4)

(iv)
Harnack’s inequality: For all

(3.5)

.

(v)
For all
x,y,z\in D

, we have

\frac{\Psi(d(y,z))}{\Psi(d(x,y))}\leq C\frac{V(z,d(y,z))}{V(x,d(x,y))}.

(3.6)

Note that if

holds for
(X,d,\mu)

also holds for

. This is a consequence of the volume doubling property and properties of the scale function. Therefore when working with

we may divide

to assume that

has metric diameter

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
,

Note that implicitly in the statement of the Strong Generalized

Principle we have picked a distinguished point

The function

To motivate this definition,

Lemma 3.7

Let

Similarly for
\eta

Let

Proof of the Main Theorem

We begin with the proof that the
\mathcal{B}
-approximation property implies the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle.

Proof of Theorem
3.10
,
\Leftarrow
direction:
Assume
3.5
and assume that
D
satisfies
\mathcal{B}
-approximation.

Fix
\xi\in\partial D
,
0<r\leq r_{0}
, and
x_{1},x_{2},y\in D
with
Md(x_{j},\xi)<r\leq d(y,\xi)
. By the
\mathcal{B}
-approximation property, for every choice of
A_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(x_{j},y)

\frac{G(x_{1},y)}{G(x_{2},y)}\asymp\frac{g(x_{1})g(y)\Psi(d(x_{1},y))}{g(A_{1})^{2}V(x_{1},d(x_{1},y))}\colon\frac{g(x_{2})g(y)\Psi(d(x_{2},y))}{g(A_{2})^{2}V(x_{2},d(x_{2},y))}.

(4.1)

Now
r(1-\frac{1}{M})\leq d(x_{j},y)\leq 1
so that
d(x_{1},y)\asymp d(x_{2},y)
. Further,
d(x_{1},x_{2})\leq 2r/M\preceq d(x_{j},y).
Therefore by volume doubling and doubling for
\Psi
we have
V(x_{1},d(x_{1},y))\asymp V(x_{2},d(x_{2},y))
. Thus we have that

\frac{g(x_{1})g(y)\Psi(d(x_{1},y))}{g(A_{1})^{2}V(x_{1},d(x_{1},y))}\colon\frac{g(x_{2})g(y)\Psi(d(x_{2},y))}{g(A_{2})^{2}V(x_{2},d(x_{2},y))}\asymp\frac{g(x_{1})}{g(x_{2})}\colon\frac{g(A_{1})^{2}}{g(A_{2})^{2}}.

(4.2)

Thus to prove the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle, it suffices to produce a uniform bound on
g(A_{1})/g(A_{2})
.

If
r(x_{1},y)\geq\epsilon/2
and
r(x_{2},y)\geq\epsilon/2
, then
\delta(A_{j})>\epsilon/(2M)
for
j=1,2
and therefore
g(A_{1})\asymp 1\asymp g(A_{2})
by Lemma
3.12
.

So let us suppose that

\rho:=2r(x_{1},y)\wedge r(x_{2},y)<\epsilon,

(4.3)

and choose
A\in\mathcal{A}_{\rho}(\xi)
. We intend to show that
A\in\mathcal{B}(x_{1},y)\cap\mathcal{B}(x_{2},y)
(and this will finish the proof). To that end we may assume without loss of generality that

r(x_{1},y)\leq r(x_{2},y).

(4.4)

Let us recall that, by our assumption at the beginning of this proof,
Md(x_{j},\xi)<r<d(y,\xi)
. This implies that, for
j=1,2
,

r(x_{j},y)\geq d(x_{j},y)\geq d(y,\xi)-d(x_{j},\xi)>r-\frac{r}{M}\geq\frac{2r}{M}

(4.5)

whence

d(x_{j},A)\leq d(x_{j},\xi)+d(\xi,A)<\frac{r}{M}+\rho<3r(x_{j},y),

(4.6)

d(y,A)\leq d(y,x_{j})+d(x_{j},A)<4r(x_{j},y).

Indeed, this is because
d(x_{j},\xi)=\delta(x_{j})\leq r(x_{j},y)
,
\rho=2r(x_{1},y)\wedge r(x_{2},y)\leq 2r(x_{j},y)
. Also,
d(y,x_{j})\leq r(x_{j},y)
and the rest follows.

Furthermore we have

\delta(A)>\frac{\rho}{M}=\frac{2r(x_{1},y)}{M}.

(4.7)

So certainly
A\in\mathcal{B}(x_{1},y)
. To prove that
A\in\mathcal{B}(x_{2},y)
it remains to show that
2r(x_{1},y)\geq r(x_{2},y)
. Obviously,

\delta(x_{2})\vee d(x_{1},x_{2})\leq d(x_{1},\xi)+d(x_{2},\xi)<\frac{2r}{M}<r(x_{1},y),

(4.8)

where the last inequality follows from earlier estimates on
r(x_{j},y)
. Consequently,
d(x_{2},y)\leq d(x_{2},x_{1})+d(x_{1},y)<2r(x_{1},y)
. Since
\delta(y)\leq r(x_{1},y)
, we finally conclude that

r(x_{2},y)=d(x_{2},y)\vee\delta(x_{2})\vee\delta(y)<2r(x_{1},y).

(4.9)

Therefore
A\in\mathcal{B}(x_{2},y)
. We can therefore use
\mathcal{B}
-approximation to conclude that the uniform boundary Harnack inequality holds. This concludes the proof of the
\Leftarrow
direction of Theorem
3.10
.∎

Proof of Theorem
3.10
,
\Rightarrow
direction:
Let us assume that
D
satisfies the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle with respect to
G
. Our goal for the
\Rightarrow
direction is to prove that
\mathcal{B}
-approximation holds. This is the harder direction and will be accomplished in parts, according to the positioning of points
x,y\in D
with respect to the boundary and to each other.

Let us first observe the following: If
x\in\left\{\delta<6\epsilon\right\}
, then
d(x,o)\geq\delta(o)-6\epsilon\geq\delta(o)/2
. Therefore

\left\{\delta<6\epsilon\right\}\subseteq D(o),

(4.10)

g=G(\cdot,o)\textrm{ on }\left\{\delta<6\epsilon\right\}.

(4.11)

This will be used without being mentioned explicitly.

Fix
x,y\in D
and
A\in\mathcal{B}(x,y)
.

Claim:

g(A)\asymp 1\textrm{ when }r(x,y)\geq\frac{\epsilon}{8M^{2}}.

(4.12)

Proof of Claim:
Indeed, if
r(x,y)\geq\epsilon
, then
A=o
,
g(A)=1
. Suppose that
r(x,y)<\epsilon
. Then
\delta(A)>r(x,y)/M\geq\epsilon/(8M^{3})
so this follows from Lemma
3.12
.∎

Without loss of generality, assume

\delta(x)\leq\delta(y).

(4.13)

Let us choose points
\xi,\eta\in\partial D
such that
d(x,\xi)=\delta(x)
and
d(y,\eta)=\delta(y)
, respectively. Several cases will be considered:

(a)
(Both
x
and
y
far from
\partial D
) Suppose for this case that
\delta(x)\geq\epsilon/(8M^{2})
. Then
g(x)\asymp g(y)\asymp 1\asymp g(A)
be Lemma
3.12
. Moreover,
G(x,y)\asymp\frac{\Psi(d(x,y))}{V(x,d(x,y))}
by Lemma
3.13
. Thus
\mathcal{B}
-approximation holds in this case.

(b)
(
x
close to
\partial D
and
y
far) Suppose for this case that
\delta(x)<\epsilon/(8M^{2})
and
\delta(y)\geq\epsilon/(2M)
. Let
r=\epsilon/(8M)
and choose
x_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{r/M}(\xi)
. By Lemma
3.12
and by the claim
4.12
,

g(x_{1})\asymp g(y)\asymp 1\asymp g(A).

(4.14)

Obviously
d(x_{1},\xi)<r/M
,
d(x,\xi)=\delta(x)<r/M
, and
r<\delta(y)\leq d(y,\xi)
. Thus, by the Uniform Boundary Harnack principle,

\frac{G(x,y)}{g(x)}\asymp\frac{G(x_{1},y)}{g(x_{1})},

(4.15)

where
G(x_{1},y)\asymp\frac{\Psi(d(x_{1},y))}{V(x_{1},d(x_{1},y))}
by Lemma
3.13
. Moreover,

\frac{d(x,y)}{2}-d(x,x_{1})\geq\frac{\delta(y)-\delta(x)}{2}-\left(\delta(x)+\frac{r}{M}\right)\geq\frac{\epsilon}{M}\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{8M}-\frac{1}{8M}\right)>0.

(4.16)

Therefore
d(x_{1},y)\asymp d(x,y)
: indeed,
d(x_{1},y)\leq d(x_{1},y)+d(x,y)\leq 2d(x,y)+d(x,x_{1})\leq\frac{5}{2}d(x,y)
, and conversely
d(x,y)\leq d(x_{1},y)+d(x_{1},x)\leq d(x_{1},y)+\frac{d(x,y)}{2}
so that
d(x,y)\leq d(x_{1},y)
. The
\mathcal{B}
-approximation property follows for case (b).

(c)
(
x
and
y
both potentially close to
\partial D
, but close to each other) Assume for this case that
\delta(y)<\epsilon
and
d(x,y)<\delta(y)/2
, i.e.
x\in B(y)
. Then

G(x,y)\asymp\frac{\Psi(d(x,y))}{V(x,d(x,y))}.

(4.17)

Moreover,
\delta(x)\geq\delta(y)/2
and
r(x,y)=\delta(y)<\epsilon
whence
\delta(A)>r(x,y)/M
and

d(y,A)\leq 5r(x,y)\leq 5M\delta(y)\wedge\delta(A).

(4.18)

In addition,

\delta(A)\leq\delta(y)+d(y,A)<\delta(y)+5r(x,y)<6\epsilon.

(4.19)

Therefore by the Harnack inequality we find that

g(x)\asymp g(y)\asymp g(A).

(4.20)

This proves the
\mathcal{B}
-approximation in case (c).

(d)
Having established (a)-(c), we may assume in the following that

\delta(x)<\frac{\epsilon}{8M^{2}},

\delta(x)\leq\delta(y)<\frac{\epsilon}{2M},

d(x,y)>\frac{\delta(y)}{2}.

(e)
(
x
and
y
far apart, but not overly far) Suppose for this case that
d(x,y)\leq 6M^{2}\delta(x)
. Then

d(x,y)\asymp\delta(y)\asymp\delta(x)\asymp r(x,y)

(4.21)

and
\delta(y)\leq d(x,y)+\delta(x)\leq(6M^{2}+1)\delta(x)<\epsilon
whence
r(x,y)<\epsilon
. Therefore
\delta(A)\asymp r(x,y)
and
d(x,A)<5r(x,y)
. So we have

g(y)\asymp g(x)\asymp g(A).

(4.22)

Fix
z\in\partial B(y,\delta(y)/2)
. Then
\delta(z)\geq\delta(y)/2\geq\delta(x)/2
,
d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+\delta(y)/2\leq 2d(x,y)
, and therefore
d(x,z)\leq 24M^{2}\delta(x)\wedge\delta(z)
. By
3.5
(iii) and (iv) as well as (VD) and (
\Psi
D),

G(x,y)\asymp G(z,y)\asymp\frac{\Psi(d(z,y))}{V(z,d(z,y))}\asymp\frac{\Psi(\delta(y))}{V(z,\delta(y)/2)}\asymp\frac{\Psi(d(x,y))}{V(x,d(x,y))}.

(4.23)

Thus
\mathcal{B}
-approximation holds in the case (e).

(f)
For this case let
6M^{2}\delta(x)<d(x,y)\leq 2M\delta(y)
. Then
r(x,y)\leq 2M\delta(y)<\epsilon
,

d(x,y)\asymp\delta(y)\asymp r(x,y)\asymp\delta(A).

(4.24)

Taking
r=\delta(y)/3
we have

d(x,\xi)=\delta(x)<\frac{\delta(y)}{3M}=\frac{r}{M}

(4.25)

and

d(y,\xi)-r\geq d(x,y)-r-\delta(x)>\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{3M}\right)\delta(y)\geq 0.

(4.26)

Fix
x_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{r/M}(\xi)
. Then
\delta(x_{1})>r/M^{2}
and
d(x_{1},\xi)<r/M
. By the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle,

\frac{G(x,y)}{g(x)}\asymp\frac{G(x_{1},y)}{g(x_{1})}.

(4.27)

Moreover, using
\delta(y)<2d(x,y)
,

\left|d(x,y)-d(x_{1},y)\right|\leq d(x,x_{1})<\delta(x)+\frac{r}{M}<\left(\frac{1}{6M^{2}}+\frac{2}{3M}\right)d(x,y)<\frac{d(x,y)}{3}

(4.28)

whence
\frac{2}{3}d(x,y)<d(x_{1},y)<\frac{4}{3}d(x,y).
Furthermore,
d(x,y)\leq 2M\delta(y)\leq 6M^{3}\delta(x_{1}).
So, by Lemma
3.13
as well as (VD) and (
\Psi
D),

G(x_{1},y)\asymp\frac{\Psi(d(x_{1},y))}{V(x_{1},d(x_{1},y))}\asymp\frac{\Psi(d(x,y))}{V(x,d(x,y))}.

(4.29)

Moreover, Harnack’s inequality
3.5
(iv) yields that

g(y)\asymp g(A)\asymp g(x_{1}).

(4.30)

Thus

\frac{G(x,y)}{g(x)g(y)}\asymp\frac{G(x_{1},y)}{g(x_{1})g(y)}\asymp\frac{\Psi(d(x,y))}{g(A)^{2}V(x,d(x,y))}.

(4.31)

This establishes the
\mathcal{B}
-approximation property in case (f).

(g)
For the final case, suppose that
2M\delta(y)<d(x,y)
. In particular,
r(x,y)=d(x,y)
. Take

r:=\frac{d(x,y)\wedge\epsilon}{2},\textrm{ }x_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{r/M}(\xi),\textrm{ }y_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{r/M}(\eta).

(4.32)

Then
x,x_{1}\in B(\xi,r/M)
and
y,y_{1}\in B(\eta,r/M)
. Moreover,
d(y,y_{1})\leq\delta(y)+r/M
and
d(y,\xi)\geq d(x,y)-\delta(x)
whence

d(y_{1},\xi)-r\geq d(y,\xi)-r-d(y,y_{1})

\geq d(x,y)-r-\delta(x)-\delta(y)-\frac{r}{M}>\left(1-\frac{1}{2}-2\cdot\frac{1}{2M}-\frac{1}{2M}\right)d(x,y)\geq 0.

Similarly,

d(x_{1},\eta)-r\geq d(x,\eta)-r-d(x,x_{1})>0.

(4.33)

By the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle,

\frac{G(x,y)}{g(x)}\asymp\frac{G(x_{1},y)}{g(x_{1})}

(4.34)

and

\frac{G(y,x_{1})}{g(y)}\asymp\frac{G(y_{1},x_{1})}{g(y_{1})}

(4.35)

and therefore, by quasi-symmetry,

\frac{G(x,y)}{g(x)g(y)}\asymp\frac{G(x_{1},y_{1})}{g(x_{1})g(y_{1})}.

(4.36)

Moreover,

\left|d(x_{1},y_{1})-d(x,y)\right|\leq d(x_{1},x)+d(y_{1},y)<\delta(x)+\delta(y)+\frac{2r}{M}<\frac{2}{M}d(x,y)

(4.37)

whence
\frac{1}{3}d(x,y)<d(x_{1},y_{1})<2d(x,y)
. Obviously,
\epsilon d(x,y)\leq 2r
, since
d\leq 1
and
\epsilon\leq 1
. Therefore
d(x_{1},y_{1})\leq 4r/\epsilon\leq(4M^{2}/\epsilon)\delta(x_{1})\wedge\delta(y_{1})
and, by Lemma
3.13
as well as (VD) and (
\Psi
D),

G(x_{1},y_{1})\asymp\frac{\Psi(d(x_{1},y_{1}))}{V(x_{1},d(x_{1},y_{1}))}\asymp\frac{\Psi(d(x,y))}{V(x,d(x,y))}.

(4.38)

If
r=\epsilon/2
, i.e. if
d(x,y)\geq\epsilon
, then
g(x_{1})\asymp g(y_{1})\asymp g(A)\asymp 1
by Lemma
3.12
. If
r<\epsilon/2
, then
d(x,y)<\epsilon
and therefore
g(x_{1})\asymp g(y_{1})\asymp g(A)
by the Harnack inequality
3.5
(iv). Thus

\frac{G(x_{1},y_{1})}{g(x_{1})g(y_{1})}\asymp\frac{\Psi(d(x,y))}{V(x,d(x,y))g(A)^{2}}

(4.39)

and the proof of Theorem
3.10
is finished.∎

Now it is time to prove Theorem
3.11
. We begin with a lemma that is a generalized version of Carleson’s estimate.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that
D
satisfies the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle with respect to
G
. Let
g=1\wedge G(\cdot,o)
be the benchmark Green’s function. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that:

(i) For all
\xi\in\partial D
, every
0<r<\epsilon
, and every
A\in\mathcal{A}_{r/M}(\xi)

(ii) If
\xi\in\partial D
,
0<s\leq r<\epsilon
and
A\in\mathcal{A}_{r}(\xi)

(4.41)

(iii) If
x,y,z\in D
satisfy
d(z,x)\leq d(z,y)

g(A)\leq Cg(B)

(4.42)

for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(x,y)
and
B\in\mathcal{B}(z,y)
.

Proof:
(i) Fix
\xi\in\partial D
,
0<r<\epsilon
,
A\in\mathcal{A}_{r/M}(\xi)
, and choose
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{2Mr}(\xi)
. Then
r<d(A,A^{\prime})<3Mr<3M^{2}\delta(A)\wedge\delta(A^{\prime})
and therefore, by Lemma
3.13
,

G(A,A^{\prime})\asymp\frac{\Psi(d(A,A^{\prime}))}{V(A,d(A,A^{\prime}))}.

(4.43)

For every
x\in D\cap B(\xi,r/M)
,
d(x,A^{\prime})>r
whence
G(x,A^{\prime})\leq C_{0}\frac{\Psi(d(x,A^{\prime}))}{V(x,d(x,A^{\prime}))}\asymp G(A,A^{\prime})
, the latter because
d(x,A)\asymp r\asymp d(A,A^{\prime})
and the above application of Lemma
3.13
.

The uniform boundary Harnack principle implies that

\frac{g(x)}{g(A)}\asymp\frac{G(x,o)}{G(A,o)}\leq C\frac{G(x,A^{\prime})}{G(A,A^{\prime})}\preceq C.

(4.44)

(ii) Fix
\xi\in\partial D
,
0<s\leq r<\epsilon
and
A\in\mathcal{A}_{r}(\xi)
. Let
x\in D\cap B(\xi,Ms)
such that
\delta(x)>s/M
. Choose
\widetilde{x}\in\mathcal{A}_{s/M}(\xi)
and
\widetilde{A}\in\mathcal{A}_{r/M}(\xi)
. Then
g(\widetilde{x})\leq Cg(\widetilde{A})
by (1). Moreover,
\delta(\widetilde{x})>s/M^{2}
whence

d(x,\widetilde{x})\leq d(x,\xi)+d(\widetilde{x},\xi)\leq(M+1)s\leq 2M^{3}\delta(x)\wedge\delta(\widetilde{x}).

(4.45)

Thus
g(x)\asymp g(\widetilde{x})
by Lemma
3.13
. In particular, taking
r
instead of
s
,
g(A)\asymp g(\widetilde{A})
.

(iii) Fix
x,y,z\in D
such that
d(z,x)\leq d(z,y)
. Then
d(x,y)\leq d(x,z)+d(z,y)\leq 2d(z,y)
and
\delta(x)\leq\delta(z)+d(z,x)\leq\delta(z)+d(z,y)
and therefore

r(x,y)=\delta(x)\vee\delta(y)\vee d(x,y)\leq 2(\delta(z)\vee\delta(y)\vee d(z,y))=2r(z,y).

(4.46)

If
r(z,y)\geq\epsilon/2
, then
g\asymp 1
on
\mathcal{B}(z,y)
by Lemma
3.12
, and the conclusion holds since
g(A)\leq c_{0}
.

So suppose that
r(z,y)<\epsilon/2
and therefore
r(x,y)<\epsilon
. Choose
\eta\in\partial D
with
d(y,\eta)=\delta(y)
and take
A\in\mathcal{A}_{r(x,y)}(\eta)
and
B\in\mathcal{A}_{r(z,y)}(\eta)
. Using Lemma
3.7
we have

\mathcal{A}_{r(x,y)}(\xi)\cup\mathcal{A}_{r(x,y)}(\eta)\subseteq\mathcal{B}(x,y)

(4.47)

and analogously for
(z,y)
. Therefore we have
A\in\mathcal{B}(x,y)
and
B\in\mathcal{B}(z,y)
. Moreover, taking
s=r(x,y)/2
we have
s\leq r(x,z)
,
\delta(A)>r(z,y)/M>s/M
and
d(A,\eta)<r(x,y)\leq Ms
. Thus
g(A)\leq Cg(B)
by (2). As discussed above, the values of
g
are comparable on any set of the form
\mathcal{B}(x,y)
. The conclusion follows.∎

Proof of Main Theorem
3.6
,
\Leftarrow
direction:
Assume that
D
satisfies the Strong Generalized
3G
Principle with respect to
G
. We wish to prove that
D
satisfies the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle with respect to
G
. Recall the alternate formulation of the Generalized
3G
Principle given in Proposition

. Namely, that if
\widetilde{G}(x,y)=G(x,y)/(g(x)g(y))
, then we have that

\frac{\widetilde{G}(x,z)\widetilde{G}(y,z)}{\widetilde{G}(x,y)}\leq C\left(\widetilde{G}(x,z)+\widetilde{G}(z,y)\right)

(4.48)

for all
x,y,z\in D
.

Fix
\xi\in\partial D
,
0<r\leq r_{0}
, and
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\in D
with
Md(x_{j},\xi)<r
and

, as in the setup for the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle. Since

, we have

(4.49)

So the Strong Generalized
3G
Principle implies that

\widetilde{G}(x_{1},y_{1})\leq C\widetilde{G}(x_{2},y_{1})

(4.50)

and

\widetilde{G}(x_{2},y_{2})\leq C\widetilde{G}(x_{1},y_{2}).

(4.51)

Therefore

\frac{G(x_{1},y_{1})}{G(x_{2},y_{1})}\colon\frac{G(x_{1},y_{2})}{G(x_{2},y_{2})}=\frac{\widetilde{G}(x_{1},y_{1})\widetilde{G}(x_{2},y_{2})}{\widetilde{G}(x_{2},y_{1})\widetilde{G}(x_{1},y_{2})}\leq C^{2}.

(4.52)

This finishes the
\Leftarrow
direction of the Main Theorem
3.6
.∎

Proof of Main Theorem

,

\frac{g(z)}{g(x)}G(x,z)\preceq\frac{\Psi(d(x,z))}{V(x,d(x,z))}.

(4.56)

If

,

. The result then follows from Assumption

(iii).

Therefore assume that

. By the

-approximation property, for any

we have

\frac{G(x,z)}{g(x)g(z)}\preceq\frac{\Psi(d(x,z))}{V(x,d(x,z))g(A)^{2}}

or

\frac{g(z)}{g(x)}G(x,z)\preceq\frac{\Psi(d(x,z))g(z)^{2}}{V(x,d(x,z))g(A)^{2}}.

To finish we must show that
g(z)\preceq g(A)
. Let
\xi\in\partial D
be such that
d(z,\xi)=\delta(z)
. Note
\delta(z)\leq r(x,z)<\epsilon
. Set
r=\delta(z)
and find
0<s\leq r
such that
Ms>r
. By the Interior Corkscrew Property
3.5
(i), there exists
A\in\mathcal{A}_{r}(\xi)
, which notably also satisfies
A\in\mathcal{B}(x,z)
by Lemma
3.7
. Since
\delta(z)>r/M>s/M
, we can apply Lemma
4.1
(ii) to conclude that
g(z)\preceq g(A)
as desired.∎

5
Inner Uniform Domains and the Interior Corkscrew Property

A central class of examples for the main theorem will occur on inner uniform domains. To define them, let us first start with length spaces.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. The length
L(\gamma)\in[0,\infty]
of a continuous curve
\gamma\colon[0,1]\rightarrow X
is given by

L(\gamma)=\sup\sum_{i}d(\gamma(t_{i-1}),\gamma(t_{i})),

(5.1)

where the supremum is taken over all partitions
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k}=1
of
[0,1]
.
(X,d)
is a
length space
if
d(x,y)
is equal to the infimum of the lengths of continuous curves joining
x
to
y
.

Definition 5.2
.

A metric space
(X,d)
is a
geodesic space
if for any
x,y\in X
there is a continuous curve
\gamma\colon[0,1]\rightarrow X
such that
\gamma(0)=x
,
\gamma(1)=y
, and
d(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))=\left|s-t\right|d(x,y)
for all
s,t\in[0,1]
.

Next we introduce the intrinsic distance on a domain
D\subseteq X
.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
D\subseteq X
be a domain. We define the
intrinsic distance

d_{i}
by

d_{i}(x,y)=\inf\left\{L(\gamma)\colon\gamma\colon[0,1]\rightarrow D\textrm{ continuous, }\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(1)=y\right\}.

(5.2)

It is well known that
(D,d_{i})
is a length space. We let
(\widetilde{D},d_{i})
denote its completion. For
x\in\widetilde{D}
we let
B_{\widetilde{D}}(x,r)=\left\{y\in\widetilde{D}\colon d_{i}(x,y)<r\right\}
and
B_{D}(x,r)=D\cap B_{\widetilde{D}}(x,r)
.

We let
\partial_{\widetilde{D}}D=\widetilde{D}\setminus D
, with accompanying distance to the boundary
\delta_{\widetilde{D}}(x)=d_{i}(x,\partial_{\widetilde{D}}D)
. If
V\subseteq D
is open, let
\overline{V}^{d_{i}}
denote the completion of
V
with respect to
d_{i}
. We then write

\partial_{\widetilde{D}}V=\overline{V}^{d_{i}}\setminus V.

Definition 5.4
.

Let
D
be a domain in a complete, separable, locally compact length space. Let
\gamma\colon[0,1]\rightarrow D
be a rectifiable, continuous curve in
D
. Let
c_{D},C_{D}>0
. We say that
\gamma
is a
(c_{D},C_{D})
-inner uniform curve
if

L(\gamma)\leq C_{D}d_{i}(\gamma(0),\gamma(1)),

(5.3)

and

\delta_{D}(\gamma(t))\geq c_{D}d_{i}(\gamma(0),\gamma(t))\wedge d_{i}(\gamma(t),\gamma(1))

(5.4)

for all
0\leq t\leq 1
. The domain
D
is called an
inner uniform domain
if any two points in
D
can be joined by a
(c_{D},C_{D})
-inner uniform curve.

The following lemma yields the existence of inner uniform curves between any two points in
\widetilde{D}
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete, locally compact, separable length space. Let
D
be a
(c_{D},C_{D})
-inner uniform domain. Then for any
x,y\in\widetilde{D}
there exists a
(c_{D},C_{D})
-inner uniform curve joining
x
to
y
in the
d_{i}
metric.

Proof:
See
[
7
]
, Lemma 2.6.∎

Example 5.6
.

Let
D\subseteq\mathbf{R}^{n}
be bounded and Lipschitz. Then
D
is inner uniform.

Example 5.7
.

Let
D=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbf{R}^{2}\colon x^{2}+y^{2}<1\right\}\setminus\left\{(x,0)\colon 0\leq x<1\right\}
. Then
D
is inner uniform.

The above example
5.7
is an important example of an inner uniform domain because it is not uniform, i.e. the inner metric cannot be replaced by the Euclidean metric in the definition of inner uniform curves.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
D
be an inner uniform domain in a complete, separable, locally compact length space. Then
D
satisfies the Interior Corkscrew Condition as a subset of
\widetilde{D}
: namely, there exists
M\geq 3
and
r_{0}>0
such that for every
\xi\in\partial_{\widetilde{D}}D
and all
0<r\leq r_{0}
, there exists
x\in D
such that
d_{i}(x,\xi)<r
and
\delta_{D}(x)>r/M
.

Proof:
Fix
o\in D
and set
r_{0}=\delta_{D}(o)/2
. Let
\xi\in\partial_{\widetilde{D}}D
. Let
\gamma
be a
(c_{D},C_{D})
-inner uniform curve connecting
\xi
to
o
. For
0<r\leq r_{0}
, by the intermediate value theorem there exists
t
with
x=\gamma(t)
such that
d_{i}(x,\xi)=r
. By inner uniformity of
\gamma
, we have
\delta_{D}(x)\geq c_{D}d_{i}(\xi,x)\wedge d_{i}(x,o)
. By the triangle inequality,
d_{i}(x,o)>r
. Thus
d_{i}(\xi,x)\wedge d_{i}(x,o)=r
. Setting
M=3\vee(c_{D})^{-1}
finishes the proof.∎

Thus Assumption
3.5
(i) is established for inner uniform domains. To discuss the classical Harnack principle, Assumption
3.5
(iv), seems to by definition require a Green’s function. We can circumvent this by phrasing the Harnack principle in terms of chains of balls; when the Green’s function finally does come into play, the classical elliptic Harnack inequality in a ball will do the rest.

6
Dirichlet Spaces and Applications

In this section, we introduce the concept of Dirichlet spaces. See
[
12
]
for a more complete discussion of Dirichlet spaces.

Definition 6.1
.

A
metric measure Dirichlet (MMD) space
is a tuple
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
, where
(X,d)
is a locally compact, separable metric space,
\mu
is a Radon measure on
X
with full support, and
\mathcal{E}
is a regular strictly local Dirichlet form on
L^{2}(X,d\mu)
with domain
\mathcal{F}
. If
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
is local or strictly local we refer to the Dirichlet space as the same.

Example 6.2
.

The classical Dirichlet space is
\mathbf{R}^{n}
with the Euclidean metric and Lebesgue measure
dx
with form

\mathcal{E}(f,f)=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\left|\nabla f\right|^{2}dx

(6.1)

with domain
\mathcal{F}=W^{1,2}(\mathbf{R}^{n},dx)
. This is a metric measure Dirichlet space.

If the heat semigroup
(P_{t})_{t>0}
of
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
has an integral kernel
p(t,x,y)
with respect to
\mu
we call this the
heat kernel
. By definition this satisfies

P_{t}f(x)=\int_{X}f(y)p(t,x,y)d\mu(y)

(6.2)

for all
f\in L^{2}(X,d\mu)
. In the classical case
6.2
we have

p(t,x,y)=\frac{1}{(4\pi t)^{n/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}}{4t}\right).

(6.3)

We mention the heat kernel because it is closely related to the Green’s function for a Dirichlet space

G(x,y):=\int_{0}^{\infty}p(t,x,y)dt

(6.4)

provided this integral is finite. Estimates for the heat kernel also yield estimates on the Green’s function which will be essential in applying our Main Theorem
3.6
.

To get the necessary bounds from Assumption
3.5
(iii), we will impose bounds on the heat kernel of
X
. We will need to study scale functions and volume growth once again. Indeed, let
\Psi\colon[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be a scale function and define for
s>0

\Phi(s)=\sup_{r>0}\left(\frac{s}{r}-\frac{1}{\Psi(r)}\right).

(6.5)

For example, if
\Psi(r)=r^{\beta}
, then
\Phi(s)=s^{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}}\left(\frac{1}{\beta^{\frac{1}{\beta-1}}}-\frac{1}{\beta^{\frac{\beta}{\beta-1}}}\right).

Definition 6.3
.

We say that an MMD space
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
satisfies (full)
sub-Gaussian heat kernel estimates (HKE(
\Psi
))
with respect to
\Psi
if
\Psi
is a scale function and there are constants
c_{i}>0
such that for all
t>0
and all
x,y\in X
the heat kernel
p(t,x,y)
of
X
satisfies

\frac{c_{1}}{V(x,\Psi^{-1}(t))}\exp\left(-c_{2}t\Phi\left(c_{3}\frac{d(x,y)}{t}\right)\right)\leq p(t,x,y),

p(t,x,y)\leq\frac{c_{4}}{V(x,\Psi^{-1}(t))}\exp\left(-c_{5}t\Phi\left(c_{6}\frac{d(x,y)}{t}\right)\right).

The heat kernel estimates HKE(
\Psi
) have profound implications. We collect the ones that will be useful to us here.

Definition 6.4
.

(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
satisfies the
(scale-invariant) elliptic Harnack inequality (EHI)
if there exist
C>0
and
0<\theta<1
such that for any open ball
B
in
X
and any
u\in\mathcal{F}
such that
u\geq 0
and
u
is harmonic in
B
we have

\esssup_{\theta B}u\leq\essinf_{\theta B}u.

Note that our harmonic functions will have continuous versions, so in practice we refer to maxima and minima instead of essential suprema and infima.

Definition 6.5
.

(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
satisfies the
Poincaré inequality PI(
\Psi
)
on
X
if
\Psi
is a scale function and there exist constants
C_{P}>0
and
a_{P}\geq 1
such that for any ball
B
in
X
and any
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

\int_{B}\left|f-f_{B}\right|^{2}d\mu\leq C_{P}\Psi(R)\int_{a_{P}B}d\Gamma(f,f).

Here
f_{B}=\mu(B)^{-1}\int_{B}fd\mu
and
\Gamma(f,f)
is the energy measure of
f
.

Definition 6.6
.

(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
satisfies the
resistance condition RES(
\Psi
)
if there exists a constant
C_{res}>1
such that for any ball
B
with radius
R>0
such that
2B\neq X
,

C_{res}^{-1}\frac{\mu(B)}{\Psi(R)}\leq\textrm{Cap}_{2B}(B)\leq C_{res}\frac{\mu(B)}{\Psi(R)}.

Here
Cap
is capacity.

Proposition 6.7
.

Let
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
be an MMD space that satisfies HKE(
\Psi
). Then
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
satisfies volume doubling, EHI, PI(
\Psi
), and RES(
\Psi
).

Proof:
See Lierl
[
15
]
, Theorem 2.12.∎

For our purposes, HKE(
\Psi
) alone is not enough to capture the desired behavior of Green’s functions, and so we must impose further requirements on
\Psi
and
V
.

Assumption 6.8
.

Assume that there exists
\gamma>0
,
C>0
, and
A>0
such that for our scale function
\Psi
and volume profile
V
and all
0<r\leq R\leq\textrm{diam}(X)/A
we have

C^{-1}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{\Psi(R)}{\Psi(r)}\leq C\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{\gamma}\frac{V(R)}{V(r)}.

(6.6)

In
\mathbf{R}^{n}
for
n\geq 3
, the scale function
\Psi(r)=r^{2}
and volume profile
V(r)=r^{n}
satisfies Assumption
6.8
with
\gamma=n-2
. In
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{R}^{2}
,
6.8
fails. This assumption may therefore be viewed as a form of transience or non-parabolicity.

The significance of this assumption for us is twofold. First, in a Dirichlet space satisfying HKE(
\Psi
), we can integrate the heat kernel to obtain an estimate on the Green’s function of the space, if that integral is finite. Second, there exist examples of spaces satisfying HKE(
\Psi
) for a wide range of scale functions and volume profiles, thus giving new examples of our Main Theorem.

6.1
Proof that Assumption
3.5
holds in inner uniform domains under HKE(
\Psi
) and Assumption
6.8

In this section we demonstrate that our main assumption
3.5
holds for a large array of inner uniform domains in MMD spaces. We begin with a connectness result essential to proving the classical Harnack inequality
3.5
(iv). This is similar to Proposition 2.8 in
[
17
]
which concerns manifolds; we have adapted this result to a Dirichlet space setting.

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
be an MMD space satisfying HKE(
\Psi
) and Assumption
6.8
. Assume also that
(X,d)
is a length space. Then there exists
\kappa\geq 2
and
A>0
such that for any
o\in X
and
0<R<\textrm{diam}(X)/A
, the annulus
B(o,R)\setminus\overline{B}(o,2^{-1}R)
is contained in a connected component of
B(o,R)\setminus\overline{B}(o,\kappa^{-1}R)
.

Proof:
We will use that HKE(
\Psi
) implies volume doubling as well as PI(
\Psi
) (
6.5
): there exist
C_{P}>0
and
a_{P}\geq 1
such that for all
x\in X
,
r>0
, and all
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

\int_{B(x,r)}\left|f-f_{B(x,r)}\right|^{2}d\mu\leq C_{P}\Psi(r)\int_{B(x,a_{P}r)}d\Gamma(f,f).

(6.7)

Here
f_{B}=\mu(B)^{-1}\int_{B}fd\mu
and
\Gamma(f,f)
is the energy measure of
f
.

Let
A
be such that for any
0<R<\textrm{diam}(X)/A
and any
o\in X
, the annulus
B(o,R)\setminus\overline{B}(o,R/2)
is nonempty. Let
0<R<\textrm{diam}(X)/A
be fixed. For
i\geq 0
set
A_{i}=B(o,2^{-i}R)\setminus\overline{B}(o,2^{-(i+1)}R)
. Set
B_{i}=B(o,2^{-i}R)
. If
A_{0}
is connected, we are done with
\kappa=2
. Otherwise let
X
and
Y
be connected components of
A_{0}
. Let
I
be the least integer, if it exists, such that
X
and
Y
are contained in the same connected component of
B(o,R)\setminus\overline{B}(o,2^{-I-1}R)
. If
2^{-I+3}\geq a_{P}^{-1}
we are once again done with
\kappa=2^{I+2}
, so henceforth we can assume that
2^{-I+3}<a_{P}^{-1}
. If no such
I
exists, we let
I
be arbitrary.

By definition,
X
and
Y
are in different connected components of
B(o,R)\setminus\overline{B}(o,2^{-I}R)
. let
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
denote these components and let
Z=X^{\prime}\cap B(o,2^{-I+1}R)
.

By RES(
\Psi
) (
6.6
), which is a consequence of HKE(
\Psi
), there exists
\varphi\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\varphi\in\mathcal{F}
,
\varphi=1
on
B(o,2^{-I+1}R)
,
\varphi=0
outside of
B(o,2^{-I+2}R)
, and

\int_{B(o,2^{-I+2}R)\setminus B(o,2^{-I+1}R)}d\Gamma(\varphi,\varphi)=\mathcal{E}(\varphi,\varphi)\leq C_{res}\frac{V(2^{I}R)}{\Psi(2^{I}R)}.

(6.8)

The constant
C_{res}
is independent of
o
,
I
and
R
.

We now define
f
as follows:

f=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{ on }X^{\prime}\setminus Z\\
1-\varphi&\textrm{ on }Z\\
0&\textrm{ else}\\
\end{cases}.

Multiplying
f
by a cutoff function that is
1
in
B(o,2^{-1}R)
and
0
outside
B(o,R)
if necessary, we may assume that
f\in\mathcal{F}
. Applying the Poincaré inequality to
f
, we see that

\int_{B(o,2^{-1}a_{P}^{-1}R)}\left|f-f_{B(o,2^{-1}a_{P}^{-1}R)}\right|^{2}d\mu\leq C_{P}\Psi(2^{-1}a_{P}^{-1}R)\int_{B(o,2^{-1}R)}d\Gamma(f,f).

(6.9)

Note that
2^{-I+4}<2^{-1}a_{P}^{-1}
. Let
J\in\mathbf{N}
be least such that
2^{-J}<2^{-1}a_{P}^{-1}
. Let
X_{J}=X^{\prime}\cap A_{J}
and
Y_{J}=Y^{\prime}\cap A_{J}
. By connectedness, there exist points
x_{J}\in X_{J}
and
y_{J}\in Y_{J}
such that
d(o,x_{J})=d(o,y_{J})=3\cdot 2^{-J-2}R
. By considering balls
B(x_{J},2^{-J-3}R)
and
B(y_{J},2^{-J-3}R)
and applying volume doubling, we find that there is a constant
C_{VD}>0
such that
V(o,2^{-1}a_{P}^{-1}R)\leq C_{VD}(\mu(X_{J})\wedge\mu(Y_{J}))
.

Furthermore, since
J\leq I-4
, we have that
f=1
on
X_{J}
and
f=0
on
Y_{J}
. Therefore setting
B=B(o,2^{-1}a_{P}^{-1}R)
we have

\int_{B}\left|f-f_{B}\right|^{2}d\mu\geq\frac{\int_{B}\int_{B}\left|f(x)-f(y)\right|^{2}d\mu(x)d\mu(y)}{2\mu(B)}\geq\frac{\mu(X_{J})\mu(Y_{J})}{2\mu(B)}\geq\left(\frac{1}{2C_{VD}^{2}}\right)\mu(B).

Note that
f
is locally constant on
B(o,2^{-1}R)\setminus Z
so using the strict locality of the Dirichlet form
\mathcal{E}
,

\int_{B(o,2^{-1}R)}d\Gamma(f,f)=\int_{Z}d\Gamma(f,f)\leq\mathcal{E}(\varphi,\varphi)\leq C_{\textrm{res}}\frac{V(2^{-I}R)}{\Psi(2^{-I}R)}.

The Poincaré inequality gives us that

\left(\frac{1}{2C_{VD}^{2}}\right)\mu(B)\leq C_{P}\Psi(2^{-1}a_{P}^{-1}R)C_{\textrm{res}}\frac{V(2^{-I}R)}{\Psi(2^{-I}R)}.

Moving it all to one side gives

1\leq 2C_{VD}^{2}C_{P}C_{res}\left(\frac{\Psi(2^{-1}a_{P}^{-1}R)}{\Psi(2^{-I}R)}\right)\left(\frac{V(2^{-I}R)}{V(2^{-1}a_{P}^{-1}R)}\right).

Applying
6.8
gives

1\leq 2C_{VD}^{2}C_{P}C_{res}\left(\frac{2^{-I}}{2^{-1}a_{P}^{-1}}\right)^{\gamma}.

Since
\gamma>0
and all constants involved are independent of
o
,
R
, and
I
, we must have that
I
is bounded above. This also proves that such an
I
must exist, as if it does not, we can use this argument for arbitrarily large
I
to get a contradiction.∎

The conclusion of the above lemma is sometimes known as the relatively connected annuli (RCA) condition. We now build the necessary concepts to apply the elliptic Harnack inequality.

Definition 6.10
.

(X,d)
be a metric space and let
U\subseteq X
be a connected open set and
M\geq 1
. For
x,y\in U
, an
M
-Harnack chain
from
x
to
y
in
U
is a sequence of balls
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
each contained in
U
such that
x\in M^{-1}B_{1},y\in M^{-1}B_{n}
, and
M^{-1}B_{j}\cap M^{-1}B_{j+1}\neq\emptyset
for each
j=1,\dots,n-1
. The number
n
of balls in a Harnack chain is called its length. Write
N_{U}(x,y;M)
for the length of the shortest
M
-Harnack chain in
U
from
x
to
y
.

Lemma 6.11
.

Let
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
be an MMD space satisfying HKE(
\Psi
). Let
D\subseteq X
be a
(c_{D},C_{D})
-inner uniform domain such that
\partial D\neq\emptyset
. Then for each
M>1
there exists
C>0
depending only on
c_{D},C_{D}
and
M
such that for all
x,y\in U

C^{-1}\log\left(\frac{d_{i}(x,y)}{\delta_{D}(x)\wedge\delta_{D}(y)}+1\right)\leq N_{D}(x,y;M)\leq C\log\left(\frac{d_{i}(x,y)}{\delta_{D}(x)\wedge\delta_{D}(y)}+1\right)+C.

(6.10)

Proof:
See Barlow and Murugan
[
7
]
, Lemma 4.7.∎

Lemma 6.12
.

Let
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
be an MMD space satisfying HKE(
\Psi
) and Assumption
6.8
and such that
(X,d)
is a length space. There exists a constant
C>0
such that if
o\in X
and
R>0
and
u>0
is a positive harmonic function in
X\setminus\left\{o\right\}
, then
u(x)\leq Cu(y)
for any
x,y\in B(o,R)\setminus\overline{B}(o,R/2)
.

Proof:
Let
\kappa>0
be as in Lemma
6.9
. We now demonstrate the existence of
N\in\mathbf{N}
such that for any
o\in X
and
R>0
, the annulus
A=B(o,R)\setminus\overline{B}(o,\kappa^{-1}R)
can be covered by
N
balls of radius at most
4^{-1}\kappa^{-1}R
.

Indeed, let
B_{1},\dots,B_{M}
be a maximal pairwise disjoint set of balls of radius
16^{-1}\kappa^{-1}\theta R
that meet
A
. Here
\theta
is as in the statement of the elliptic Harnack inequality. By maximality, the balls
5B_{1},\dots,5B_{M}
cover
A
. Therefore
\mu(A)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mu(5B_{j})\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mu(B_{j})
. However, by the triangle inequality, the balls
B_{j}
are all contained in the ball
B(o,(1+8^{-1}\kappa^{-1})R)
which has volume
\asymp B(o,R)
by volume doubling. Thus we have that
M\mu(B(o,16^{-1}\kappa^{-1}R)\preceq\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mu(B_{j})\leq\mu(A)\preceq B(o,R)
. We then conclude by volume doubling that
M\preceq 1
with the constant involved depending only on that given from volume doubling. This proves the existence of such an
N
claimed above.

So let
B_{1},\dots,B_{N}
be balls of radius
16^{-1}\kappa^{-1}\theta R
as in the claim. Let
x,y\in B(o,r)\setminus\overline{B}(o,r/2)
. Using Lemma
6.9
, we can build a
\theta
-Harnack chain from
x
to
y
in
X\setminus\left\{o\right\}
of balls from
\left\{B_{1},\dots,B_{N}\right\}
that naturally has length
\leq N
. Note that
\theta^{-1}B_{j}\subseteq X\setminus\left\{o\right\}
for each
j
, so the elliptic Harnack inequality (EHI) applies to each ball
B_{j}
with a uniform constant. The conclusion of the lemma follows.∎

We now state the result that allows us to apply the main theorem to inner uniform domains.

Theorem 6.13
.

Let
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
be an MMD space such that
(X,d)
is a geodesic space and let
\Psi
,
V
be a scale function and volume profile for
X
satisfying Assumption
6.8
. Assume also that HKE(
\Psi
) holds. Let
D
be a bounded inner uniform domain in
X
such that
\textrm{diam}(D)<\textrm{diam}(X)/4
and let
G
be its Green’s function. Then Assumption
3.5
holds with respect to
D
and
G
.

Remark:
The condition that
\textrm{diam}(D)<\textrm{diam}(X)/4
can be weakened considerably; we choose it here for simplicity of proof and of use. For example, one could replace this with the condition that the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
D
within
X
is strictly positive and use results from Lierl
[
15
]
.

Proof:
(i) The Interior Corkscrew Property holds by Proposition
5.8
.

(ii) Quasi-symmetry is trivial: the Green’s function is symmetric.

(iii) We start with the lower bound. First let
x,y\in D
be distinct such that
d_{i}(x,y)=d(x,y)\leq\delta_{d}(y)/2
. Assume first that
d(x,y)>(3/7)\delta_{D}(y)
. Let
\Gamma
be a path from
x
to
y
of length
d(x,y)
and let
z
lie on the image of
\Gamma
with
d(z,y)=\delta_{D}(y)/7
. Let
R=(3/7)\delta_{D}(y)
and note that
B(y,2R)\neq X
. By Lemma 3.13 in Lierl
[
15
]
, the Green’s function
G_{B(y,R)}
of
B(y,R)
satisfies
G_{B(y,R)}(y,z)\geq C\frac{\Psi(d(y,z))}{V(z,d(y,z))}
, where the constant
C
is independent of
z
and
y
. If
d(x,y)<R
then by the same as well as domain monotonicity of Green’s functions we have
G(x,y)\geq G_{B(y,R)}(x,y)\geq C\frac{\Psi(d(x,y))}{V(x,d(x,y))}
as desired. Using a Harnack chain of balls centered on
\Gamma
, we can then use EHI to conclude that
G(x,y)\preceq G_{D}(z,y)\preceq C\frac{\Psi(d(x,y))}{V(x,d(x,y))}
as desired. Finally if
d(x,y)\leq(3/7)\delta_{D}(y)
then no such chain is necessary and we repeat the argument with
z=x
.

Now for the upper bound. Let
x,y\in D
be distinct. Since
\textrm{diam}(D)<\textrm{diam}(X)/4
, if
R=\textrm{diam}(D)
then
B(x,2R)\neq X
and
y\in B(x,R)
. The upper bound in Lemma 3.13 in Lierl
[
15
]
then yields exactly the result we desire.

(iv) Let
y\in D
, let
k\in\mathbf{N}
and let
x_{1},x_{2}\in D(y)
such that
d(x_{1},x_{2})\leq k\delta(x_{1})\wedge\delta(x_{2})
. By Lemma
6.11
, there is a
\delta^{-1}
-Harnack chain
B_{1},\dots,B_{N}
joining
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
of length at most
C\log(k+1)+C
.

Case 1:
y\notin B_{j}
for all
j
.

Here we can apply the scale-invariant elliptic Harnack inequality EHI in each ball
\delta^{-1}B_{j}
to conclude that
G(x_{1},y)\leq C^{C\log(k+1)+C}G(x_{2},y)
as desired.

Case 2:
y\in B_{j}
for some
j
.

Let
R=\delta_{D}(y)/2\kappa
,
\kappa>0
from Lemma
6.9
, and let
A=B(y,R)\setminus B(y,\kappa^{-1}R)
. Let
S=\left\{x\colon d(x,y)=R\right\}
.
j_{1}=\min\left\{j\colon S\cap B_{j}\neq\emptyset\right\}
and
j_{2}=\max\left\{j\colon S\cap B_{j}\neq\emptyset\right\}
. Note that
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
exist by connectness. Write
B_{j_{1}}=B(x_{j_{1}},r_{j_{1}})
and
B_{j_{2}}=B(x_{j_{2}},r_{j_{2}})
.

If
y\notin B_{j_{1}}\cup B_{j_{2}}
we can apply Lemma
6.12
to get that
\sup_{B_{j_{1}}}G(\cdot,y)\leq\inf_{B_{j_{2}}}G(\cdot,y)
, and finish the proof with applying the elliptic Harnack inequality across the Harnack chains from
B_{1}
to
B_{j_{1}}
and from
B_{j_{2}}
to
B_{N}
.

If
y\in B_{j_{1}}
, we note by the triangle inequality that
r_{1}\leq 2\delta_{D}(y)
. Furthermore, since
y\in B_{j_{1}}
and
S\cap B_{j_{1}}\neq\emptyset
, we conclude that
r_{1}\geq\frac{1}{4}\delta_{D}(y)
. By a covering argument similar to the proof of Lemma
6.12
, there exists a universal constant
Q
such that we can cover
\delta^{-1}B_{j_{1}}
in
Q
balls
B
of radius
\frac{1}{5\delta}\delta_{D}(y)
. Note that for each such ball
B
, we have
\delta B\subseteq D
and
y\notin\delta B
.

We can therefore , via a simple recursion argument, find a
\delta^{-1}
-Harnack chain of at most
Q
balls connecting
B_{j_{1}-1}
to
S
while avoiding
y
. Therefore we have an
\delta^{-1}
-Harnack chain of length
j_{1}-1+Q
from
x_{1}
to
S
which avoids
y
. A similar argument yields an
\delta^{-1}
-Harnack chain of length
N-j_{2}+Q
from
S
to
x_{2}
which avoids
y
. Applying the elliptic Harnack inequality to these chains as well as Lemma
6.12
to
S
yields the Harnack inequality
G(x_{1},y)\leq C(k)G(x_{2},y)
as desired.∎

(v) This follows from Assumption
6.8
.∎

Thus, if
D
is a domain in
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
under the hypotheses of Theorem
6.13
, by our Main Theorem
3.6
, the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle is equivalent to the Strong Generalized
3G
Principle on
D
. We can further state that both of these are simply true, due to the following theorem of Barlow and Murugan
[
7
]
.

Theorem 6.14
.

(Barlow, Murugan 2019) Let
D
be an inner uniform domain in
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
under all the same hypotheses on
D
and
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
as Theorem
6.13
. Let
G(x,y)
be the Green’s function of
D
. Then
D
satisfies the Uniform Boundary Harnack Principle with respect to

.

We therefore have the following immediate corollary.

Corollary 6.15
.

Let
D
be an inner uniform domain in
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
under all the same hypotheses on
D
and
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
as Theorem

. Let

be the Green’s function of

. Then

satisfies the Strong Generalized

.

Proof:
This follows from combining our Main Theorem

with Theorem

as well as the above Theorem

.∎

6.2
Examples: Fractals and Generalized Fractals

Let us consider the Sierpinski carpet. Our presentation is taken from
[
8
]
. Let
L=3
,
I=3^{n}-1
and consider
[0,1]^{n}
as a union of the
3^{n}
closed cubes of the form
\prod_{j=1}^{n}[a_{j}/3,(a_{j}+1)/3]
for
a_{j}=0,1,2
. Enumerate these cubes
C_{0},C_{1},\dots,C_{3^{n}-1}
with
C_{0}=[1/3,2/3]^{n}
. Let
\psi_{i},

i=1,\dots,I
be the orientation-preserving affine maps that take
F_{0}=[0,1]^{n}
onto
C_{i}
. Set
\psi_{1}(x)=L^{-1}x
. Define
\mathcal{P}(A)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{I}\psi_{i}(A)
for
A\subseteq\mathbf{R}^{n}
. Taking an
m
-fold composition we define
F_{m}=\mathcal{P}^{(m)}(F_{0})
and then
F=\bigcap_{m=0}^{\infty}F_{m}
. This yields the compact Sierpinski carpet, as pictured in Figure
2
for dimension
n=2
. Notably we will focus on higher dimensions as
n=2
fails Assumption
6.8
.
F
has Hausdorff dimension
d_{f}=\log(3^{n}-1)/\log 3
; let
\mu_{F}
denote
d_{f}
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
F
.
F
is a length space with length metric
d
comparable to the Euclidean metric.
(F,d,\mu_{F})
is a metric measure space.

Figure 2:
Compact Sierpinski carpet in
\mathbf{R}^{2}
. Public domain image from Wikimedia Commons.

For a more comprehensive study of Dirichlet forms on the Sierpinski carpet, see
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
, and
[
8
]
. There are still many open questions, but notably by
[
6
]
, there exists, up to a constant, a unique regular strictly local Dirichlet form on
F
that is invariant under the symmetries of
F
. Let
\mathcal{E}
denote this Dirichlet form and let
\mathcal{D}
denote its domain. Notably, there exists
\beta>2
such that
(F,d,\mu_{F},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D})
satisfies HKE(
\Psi
) with
\Psi(r)=r^{\beta}
. This quantity
\beta
is known as the
walk dimension
of
F
, due to it being almost surely the Hausdorff dimension of the trace of an interval of Brownian motion in
F
provided
\beta\leq d_{f}
. Furthermore, if
n\geq 3
, then indeed
\beta<d_{f}
and therefore Assumption
6.8
holds.

There are many examples of inner uniform domains for which our results apply. For one of particular note, let
E=\left\{x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}\colon x_{j}=1\textrm{ for some }j=1,\dots,n\right\}
. Then
E\subseteq\partial[0,1]
and
E\subseteq F
. Consider the domain
D=F\setminus E
in
F
with boundary
E
. This notably fails our condition on diameter, since
D
has the same diameter as
F
. However,
(1/9)D=\left\{(1/9)x\colon x\in D\right\}
is a domain in
F
with sufficiently small diameter, and hence by our results, the Strong Generalized
3G
Theorem holds. But by the fractal symmetry of
F
, the Dirichlet form on
(1/9)D
is a scalar multiple to that on
D
itself, so
D
satisfies the Strong Generalized
3G
Theorem.

It is notable to compare our result with the paper of Hansen
[
13
]
. The main result of that paper can be used in place of our main Theorem; however, our Theorem
6.13
is still required to show that the assumptions of Hansen’s result hold on the Sierpinski carpet.

For our next example, we turn to the paper of Murugan
[
18
]
, which constructs MMD spaces with prescribed heat kernel estimates.

Theorem 6.16
.

(Murugan, 2024) Let
V,\Psi\colon[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be doubling functions and let
C>1
be such that for all
0<r\leq R
,

C^{-1}\frac{R^{2}}{r^{2}}\leq\frac{\Psi(R)}{\Psi(r)}\leq C\frac{RV(R)}{rV(r)}.

(6.11)

Then there exists an unbounded MMD space
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
that satisfies HKE(
\Psi
) and such that
(X,d)
is a geodesic space and
V
is a volume profile for
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
.

In particular, if instead
\frac{\Psi(R)}{\Psi(r)}\leq C\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{\gamma}\frac{V(R)}{V(r)}
for some
\gamma>0
and all
0<r\leq R
, then the resulting unbounded MMD space will satisfy Assumption
6.8
, and thus any bounded inner uniform domain in that space will be subject to the Main Theorem
3.6
. We refer to Murugan’s paper for the details of the construction, which is somewhat complicated. The spaces themselves are quotients of trees; trees by themselves have too little volume to satisfy Assumption
6.8
, but by gluing them together carefully one can control the volume profile in an adequate manner.

Proposition 6.17
.

Let
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
be an MMD space constructed via the proof of Theorem
6.16
in
[
18
]
. Then there exist points
x\in X
and
c,C>0
such that for all
k\in\mathbf{Z}
, the ball
B(x,2^{k})
is
(c,C)
-inner uniform.

Proof:
See
[
18
]
, Proposition 3.26.∎

What is noteworthy about the spaces in Theorem
6.16
is that neither
\Psi(r)
nor
V(r)
need be strictly single powers of
r
. Rather they can be irregular, growing at different rates depending on the scale of
r
. Thus these spaces lack the symmetry of spaces such as the Sierpinski carpet, which has a well-defined Hausdorff dimension and well-defined walk dimension. See
[
13
]
for a result similar to our Main Theorem, but which does not cover such irregular spaces as these.

6.3
Application: Conditional Gaugability of Schrödinger Operators

Let
D
be a bounded inner uniform domain in an unbounded geodesic MMD space
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
satisfying HKE(
\Psi
) and Assumption
6.8
. Let
G(x,y)
be the Green’s function of
X
and let
G_{D}(x,y)
be the Green’s function of
D
. Since
X
is unbounded,
G
necessarily exists by Assumption
6.8
. In fact, by similar estimates to those appearing in the proof of Lemma 3.13 in
[
16
]
, we have that
G(x,y)\asymp\frac{\Psi(d(x,y))}{V(d(x,y))}
globally. Recall Lemma
4.2
, a form of the
3G
Principle relating
G_{D}
to
G
.

Let
(B_{t})_{t>0}
be the Markov process on
X
associated with
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
. In the classical setting, this is Brownian motion, so we can think of
(B_{t})_{t>0}
as generalized Brownian motion. This process has transition kernel
p(t,x,y)
, where
p
is the heat kernel. In
D
, we can consider the same process with killing at the boundary of
D
. This process has transition kernel
p_{Dir}(t,x,y)
, where
p_{Dir}
is the Dirichlet heat kernel in
D
. We can further consider the conditional process in
D
with transition kernel
G_{D}(x,y)^{-1}p_{Dir}(t,x,z)G_{D}(z,y)
. The Markov process associated with this transition kernel is
(B_{t})_{t>0}
in
D
conditioned to end at
y
. We denote expectation with respect to this process started at
x
and ending at
y
by
E^{x}_{y}
. Let
\tau_{D}
denote the lifetime of the process.

Let
W\colon D\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
be such that
\sup_{x\in X}\int_{D}G(x,y)\left|W(y)\right|d\mu(y)<+\infty
. Set
\left\|W\right\|=\sup_{x\in X}\int_{D}G(x,y)\left|W(y)\right|d\mu(y)
. We can think of
W
as the potential for a Schrödinger operator
-\mathcal{L}+W
, where
-\mathcal{L}
is the generator of the heat semigroup on
X
.

Utilizing Lemma
4.2
, we have that

E^{x}_{y}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{D}}\left|W(B_{s})\right|ds\right]=\int_{D}\frac{G_{D}(x,z)G_{D}(z,y)}{G_{D}(x,y)}\left|W(z)\right|d\mu(z)

\preceq\int_{D}(G(x,z)+G(z,y))\left|W(z)\right|d\mu(z)\preceq\left\|W\right\|.

Thus we have: if
\left\|W\right\|
is sufficiently small, then

\sup_{x,y\in D}E^{x}_{y}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{D}}\left|W(B_{s})\right|ds\right]<1.

From here we can apply Khasminskii’s Lemma to conclude that
W
is conditionally gaugeable in
D
, i.e.

\sup_{x,y\in D}E^{x}_{y}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\tau_{D}}\left|W(B_{s})\right|ds\right)\right]<+\infty.

This implies, for example, the existence of a Green’s function for the Dirichlet space
(X,d,\mu,\mathcal{E}+W\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>,\mathcal{F})
. We refer the reader to Chen
[
9
]
for more on the gaugeability of Schrödinger operators, as well as Hansen
[
14
]
for extensive applications of the
3G
Principle to Schrödinger operators.
