Global trends and future directions in COVID-19 and leukemia research from 2020 to 2024
Authors: Zelin Yang, Hui Zhao, Jie Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 has disproportionately affected leukemia patients, with outcomes significantly worse than those in the general population due to their immunocompromised status. Despite substantial growth in COVID-19 and leukemia research, there remains a lack of bibliometric analyses examining the global research landscape in this critical domain.MethodsThis study employed bibliometric methodology to analyze 346 publications examining the intersection of COVID-19 and leukemia from 2020 to 2024 using the Web of Science Core Collection database. Data visualization and analysis were conducted using VOSviewer (version 1.6.19), CiteSpace (version 6.2.R3), and the biblioshiny R package.ResultsThe analysis encompassed 66 countries, with publications from multiple institutions and researchers across 173 journals. The United States leads with 96 publications and 2,778 citations, with Tel Aviv University as the most influential institution. The Cureus Journal Of Medical Science has published the most papers (counts = 21), while the Blood has the most co-citations (1,177). Tamar Tadmor is the most prolific author (counts = 11, citations = 916), and Y. Herishanu has the highest co-citations (n = 104). Current research hotspots primarily encompass COVID-19 clinical outcomes, vaccine immunogenicity patterns, disease-specific management strategies, and therapeutic treatment adaptations. Future research directions should focus on long-term outcome monitoring, precision vaccination strategies, digital healthcare integration, global collaborative frameworks, and crisis preparedness mechanisms.ConclusionThis bibliometric analysis indicates that COVID-19 has transformed leukemia research, moving from broad immunocompromised patient strategies to precision medicine integrating infection risk. The established knowledge base and international collaboration provide a solid foundation for future infectious disease challenges in vulnerable cancer populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04072-z.

Introduction

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has transformed global healthcare systems and medical research priorities since its emergence in late 2019 [1]. As of mid-2025, the pandemic has resulted in over 778 million confirmed cases and approximately 7.09 million deaths worldwide [2]. While the World Health Organization declared the end of the COVID-19 public health emergency in May 2023, research implications and clinical considerations for vulnerable populations, particularly leukemia patients, have extended well beyond the acute pandemic phase [3].

The pandemic’s disruption has been particularly profound for patients with hematological malignancies. Leukemia, a heterogeneous group of blood cancers characterized by abnormal proliferation of white blood cells, affects approximately 474,000 individuals globally each year [4]. These patients face unique challenges due to their inherently compromised immune systems, which result from both the disease itself and the immunosuppressive effects of chemotherapy and other treatments [5–7].

The pathophysiological relationship between COVID-19 and leukemia is multifaceted and bidirectional. SARS-CoV-2 infection can directly affect hematopoiesis through viral invasion of bone marrow stromal cells and hematopoietic progenitor cells, potentially disrupting normal blood cell production and exacerbating cytopenias [8]. The virus utilizes angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptors expressed on hematopoietic stem cells and bone marrow stromal cells, facilitating viral entry and subsequent inflammatory responses [9–11]. Conversely, leukemia-induced immunosuppression creates a permissive environment for severe COVID-19 through: (i) quantitative and qualitative T-cell deficiencies that compromise viral clearance and adaptive immune responses [12, 13]; (ii) dysregulated cytokine production that may contribute to cytokine release syndrome and acute respiratory distress syndrome [14]; (iii) compromised B-cell function resulting in inadequate antibody production and poor vaccine responses [15]; and (iv) neutropenia and monocyte dysfunction that reduce innate immune defenses [16]. Additionally, certain leukemia treatments, particularly anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies and intensive chemotherapy, further suppress immune function and prolong viral shedding [17, 18]. This complex interplay underlies the substantially elevated morbidity and mortality observed in this patient population.

Early pandemic studies revealed alarming statistics for leukemia patients infected with COVID-19, with hospitalization rates reaching 73.1%, severe illness rates of 63.8%, and mortality rates as high as 31.2% [19]. These figures significantly exceeded those in the general population. Subsequent research has identified multiple risk factors associated with adverse outcomes, including advanced age, active malignancy status, comorbidities, and specific leukemia subtypes, with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) demonstrating particularly high mortality rates [20, 21]. However, enhanced infection prevention protocols, modified treatment regimens, and improved supportive care have contributed to gradual outcome improvements over the pandemic course.

The pandemic has catalyzed significant research focus on the COVID-19-leukemia intersection, influencing both research paradigms and clinical practice patterns. Traditional in-person clinical trials faced substantial disruptions, leading to rapid adoption of telemedicine, decentralized trial designs, and modified treatment protocols [22–26]. These adaptations have accelerated innovations in healthcare delivery with potential lasting benefits beyond the pandemic era. Furthermore, the pandemic has highlighted disparities in healthcare access and outcomes among different populations, prompting increased attention to health equity issues in leukemia care [27–30].

The pandemic has generated an unprecedented volume of scientific literature examining various aspects of COVID-19’s impact on cancer care. However, the rapid pace of publication, varying methodological approaches, and evolving clinical understanding have created challenges in synthesizing and interpreting this growing body of evidence. The field would benefit from systematic analyses that can identify key research trends, highlight knowledge gaps, and guide future investigative priorities.

Bibliometric analysis has emerged as a powerful tool for understanding research landscapes and identifying emerging trends in scientific fields [31]. This approach enables quantitative analysis of publication patterns, citation networks, research collaboration patterns, and thematic evolution within specific domains [32, 33]. In the context of COVID-19 and leukemia research, bibliometric analysis can provide valuable insights into how research priorities have evolved, which institutions and countries have led investigative efforts, and what key questions remain unanswered.

Despite substantial investigation into COVID-19’s impact on leukemia over the past four years, there remains a lack of bibliometric analysis examining the global research landscape specifically focusing on this intersection. Such analysis is crucial for understanding research trends, identifying influential publications and researchers, mapping international collaboration patterns, and recognizing emerging research hotspots that may guide future investigations.

Therefore, this study aims to conduct a bibliometric analysis of research specifically examining the COVID-19-leukemia intersection from 2020 to 2024, utilizing analytical techniques to examine publication trends, research hotspots, collaboration networks, and thematic evolution. By providing a systematic overview of the research landscape, this analysis will contribute to a better understanding of how the scientific community has responded to the challenges posed by the COVID-19-leukemia intersection and will identify key areas for future research investment and clinical focus. The findings will serve as a valuable resource for researchers, clinicians, funding agencies, and policymakers working to optimize outcomes for leukemia patients in the post-pandemic era. Materials and methods

Database selection and search methodology

A bibliometric investigation was conducted utilizing the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) database to systematically identify and analyze research publications examining the intersection of COVID-19 and leukemia, encompassing scholarly works from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2024. The research employed a search approach integrating specialized terminology associated with leukemia and the COVID-19 period (Supplementary Table 1), with searches conducted across multiple document fields including title, abstract, and author-assigned keywords.

To ensure a comprehensive and precise bibliometric examination, this study utilized the WoSCC platform. The WoSCC database offers several advantages, including extensive coverage of high-impact academic journals, standardized citation metrics, and seamless metadata exportation compatible with analytical software such as VOSviewer, CiteSpace, and bibliometrix. While alternative databases like Scopus, Google Scholar, and PubMed encompass substantial academic literature, WoSCC distinguishes itself through superior data consistency and structural integrity, thereby enhancing the analytical precision and investigative depth of the research [34, 35]. However, this single-database approach introduces inherent limitations, including a potential English-language bias and possible underrepresentation of research from non-Western regions, particularly Latin America, Africa, and non-English speaking countries, which may affect the global representativeness of our findings.

Selection parameters

Inclusion criteria: (i) scholarly publications including original research articles and review papers published within the 2020–2024 timeframe; (ii) research explicitly examining the intersection, interaction, or impact of COVID-19 on leukemia patients, treatment, or outcomes.

Exclusion criteria: (i) non-substantive document categories: conference abstracts, editorial commentaries, correspondence, supplementary book sections; (ii) publications demonstrating minimal or no direct examination of COVID-19’s relationship with leukemia.

Temporal scope validation

To address the temporal complexity of defining the “COVID-19 period”, we implemented additional screening criteria to ensure included publications genuinely addressed COVID-19-related research questions. Studies published in 2023–2024 were specifically evaluated to confirm they: (i) investigated COVID-19’s direct or indirect effects on leukemia patients; (ii) examined COVID-19-related changes in treatment protocols or clinical outcomes; (iii) analyzed vaccination responses or infection risks in leukemia populations; or (iv) assessed healthcare system adaptations during the COVID-19 period. This approach ensured that our analysis captured COVID-19-relevant research regardless of publication timing, accounting for the typical 12–24 month publication lag in academic literature.

Literature evaluation and refinement process

Two authors independently screened titles and abstracts, with discrepancies resolved by a third author. The initial bibliographic search generated 491 potential research records. Through a systematic screening methodology encompassing linguistic assessment, title and abstract examination, and relevance verification specific to leukemia research during the COVID-19 era, 145 publications were eliminated. The resultant curated dataset comprised 346 scholarly publications, stratified into 282 primary research investigations and 64 review manuscripts (Supplementary Table 1).

Bibliometric analytical frameworks

Three analytical platforms were deployed for multidimensional bibliometric investigation:

VOSviewer (version 1.6.19) was implemented to construct and graphically represent bibliometric networks [31, 36], including: (i) collaborative mapping of authorship interactions across countries, institutions, and individual researchers; (ii) co-citation analyses of academic journals and referenced literature; (iii) keyword co-occurrence network visualizations; (iv) network representation parameters incorporated node dimensionality (signifying publication volume) and chromatic variations (indicating cluster configurations or temporal dynamics), with interconnecting trajectories representing collaborative or citational relationships.

CiteSpace (version 6.2.R3) [37, 38]facilitated: (i) dual-map overlay analyses revealing citation pathways between academic disciplines; (ii) identification of citation bursts to detect emergent research trajectories; (iii) keyword clustering and chronological visualization techniques to illuminate thematic developmental progressions.

Biblioshiny (www.bibliometric.com) [39–41], the web-based interface for the R package bibliometrix (version 4.3.1), was applied to: (i) generate global research output distribution mappings; (ii) perform collaborative network assessments across geographical contexts.

Data processing and visualization

Microsoft Office Excel 2021 was used for quantitative data analysis, including calculation of publication trends. All visualizations were standardized for clarity and consistency across different analytical tools.

Data quality considerations

While bibliometric software provides automated processing of institutional and author data, this analysis did not implement systematic disambiguation procedures for author names or institutional affiliations. Consequently, reported counts of institutions and researchers represent database-recorded variations rather than definitively unique entities, and should be interpreted with appropriate caution regarding potential counting discrepancies due to name variations.

Results

The study encompassed 346 publications from 66 countries across 173 journals, with contributions from multiple institutions and researchers worldwide. This research landscape, while limited to WoSCC-indexed literature, reflects substantial international scientific collaboration in addressing the intersection of COVID-19 and leukemia.

The citation patterns show temporal variation in this research field, with citations increasing from 289 in 2020 to 1,117 in 2021, and reaching 1,980 citations in 2022, coinciding with the peak publication year. Subsequently, citations declined to 1,667 in 2023 and 1,049 in 2024, following the publication trend. The observed decline in both publication volume and citation frequency after 2022 may reflect several factors, including the natural evolution of the pandemic research cycle, shifting research priorities as acute pandemic concerns subsided, or the transition from urgent clinical questions to longer-term follow-up studies. This temporal pattern is consistent with typical research response patterns observed in other health crisis situations, where initial research surges are followed by more targeted, specialized investigations. Additionally, it is important to note that this overall trend analysis does not differentiate between the four major research hotspots identified in our study (clinical impact assessment, vaccine immunogenicity, disease-specific management, and therapeutic adaptations). The observed decline may reflect varying patterns across different research themes rather than uniform decreased interest, with some topics potentially showing continued growth while others may have reached saturation or shifted focus. Despite this recent decrease in both publication volume and citation frequency, research examining COVID-19’s impact on leukemia continues to investigate pandemic-related effects on leukemia patient outcomes.

Research output analysis revealed significant geographical patterns in leukemia-COVID-19 research productivity. The United States demonstrated the highest research output with 96 publications and 2,778 total citations, followed by Italy (55 publications, 1,696 citations) and Germany (26 publications, 637 citations) (Fig. 2B; Table 1). Spain achieved notable research impact with 1,337 total citations from 26 publications, while Israel contributed 1,212 citations from 21 publications. However, direct comparison of research impact across countries should be interpreted cautiously, as countries publishing earlier in the pandemic period (2020–2022) have had substantially more time to accumulate citations than those with later publication patterns. The data indicates that leukemia-COVID-19 research was concentrated primarily in developed nations with established research infrastructure, though citation accumulation patterns may reflect temporal publication differences rather than purely qualitative research impact differences across regions.

The bibliometric analysis of institutional contributions to leukemia research during the COVID-19 period reveals diverse collaborative networks with notable variations in productivity and impact (Fig. 2E). Based on the recorded affiliations in the database, Tel Aviv University (Israel) is the most prolific, with 11 publications and 875 citations. Additionally, the institutional collaboration network (Fig. 2E) demonstrates distinct collaborative clusters based on research partnerships and institutional relationships rather than strict geographic boundaries. The largest cluster (red) encompasses major cancer centers and universities including Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, Sapienza University, University of Cologne, Harvard Medical School, University of Milan, University of Barcelona, and University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center, representing a trans-Atlantic research consortium. Tel Aviv University serves as the most prominent bridging institution due to its central position and largest node size, while Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center acts as a key connector within the international research consortium, facilitating cross-continental knowledge exchange in leukemia-COVID research.

Top journals and co-cited journals

The analysis of the top 10 publishing journals revealed distinct patterns in publication volume and research impact within leukemia research during the COVID-19 period (Fig. 3A; Table 2). The Cureus Journal of Medical Science demonstrated the highest publication volume with 21 articles but relatively modest citation impact {40 citations, impact factor (IF) = 1.3, Q2}, while Frontiers in Oncology contributed 16 publications with substantially higher citations (186 citations, IF = 3.3, Q2). Notably, Blood, despite publishing only 8 articles, received 1,010 citations and the highest IF (IF = 23.1, Q1), indicating the exceptional quality and influence of leukemia research published in this premier hematological journal. Other specialized hematology and oncology journals, including Leukemia & Lymphoma (10 publications, 50 citations, IF = 2.2, Q3), Annals of Hematology (9 publications, 29 citations, IF = 2.4, Q2), and Hematological Oncology (9 publications, 40 citations, IF = 3.9, Q1), collectively contributed significant research output with varying degrees of citation impact. Additionally, the journal co-occurrence network analysis (Fig. 3A) reveals distinct clustering patterns that illuminate the interdisciplinary landscape of COVID-19 and leukemia research. The network demonstrates strong interconnections between specialized hematology journals (Blood, Leukemia & Lymphoma, Annals of Hematology) and broader clinical medicine publications (Cureus Journal of Medical Science, Frontiers in Oncology), indicating substantial cross-pollination between subspecialty hematological research and general medical practice during the pandemic. The clustering analysis identifies three primary journal groups: (1) core hematology-oncology journals forming a tightly connected cluster, reflecting established research collaborations within the field; (2) general medical and clinical journals that bridge hematology with broader clinical medicine; and (3) emerging interdisciplinary publications that integrate infectious disease, immunology, and cancer research perspectives. The network structure, characterized by strong edge connections between traditionally separate journal categories, demonstrates how the COVID-19 has necessitated unprecedented interdisciplinary collaboration, with hematology research increasingly drawing upon and contributing to general medicine, infectious disease, and public health literature. This integration is particularly evident in the central positioning of journals like Frontiers in Oncology and Clinical Infectious Diseases, which serve as bridges between specialized hematological research and broader clinical applications, highlighting the COVID-19’s role in breaking down traditional disciplinary boundaries in medical research.

Top co-cited references

In the co-cited reference analysis, Herishanu et al. (2021, Blood, v137, p3165) has the highest citation count (citations = 71) [42]. This is followed by Scarfò et al. (2020, Leukemia, v34, p2354) (citations = 68) [17] and Mato et al. (2020, Blood, v136, p1134) (Citations = 65) [18] (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Table 2). These three foundational studies established critical insights into COVID-19’s impact on chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) patients. Herishanu et al. demonstrated severely impaired vaccine responses in CLL patients, with only 39.5% achieving antibody responses to BNT162b2 mRNA vaccine compared to 100% in healthy controls, particularly poor in patients receiving active treatment (16.0% response rate) [42]. Scarfò et al. revealed that 79% of 190 CLL patients presented with severe COVID-19, with advanced age (≥ 65 years) as a significant risk factor and potential protective effects of bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors [17]. Mato et al. reported alarming outcomes in 198 CLL patients across 43 centers, documenting 90% hospitalization rates and 33% case fatality rates, with no significant differences between treatment-naive and treated patients [18]. Collectively, these studies established CLL patients as a uniquely vulnerable population with impaired immune responses, high severity rates, and substantial mortality risk during the COVID-19 period.

(2020, LEUKEMIA, v34, p1637) has the strongest citation burst (strength = 7.26) [43], followed by Treon et al. (2020, BLOOD, V135, P1912) (strength = 5.47) [44] and Jin et al. (2020, LANCET HAEMATOL, V7, PE351) (strength = 3.96) [45]. These papers provided pivotal early insights into COVID-19’s impact on hematological malignancies and potential therapeutic interventions. He et al. conducted the first comprehensive cohort study in Wuhan, demonstrating that while hematological cancer patients had similar COVID-19 infection rates as healthcare providers (10% vs. 7%), they experienced dramatically higher case fatality rates (62% vs. 0%, P = 0.002), establishing the severe vulnerability of this population [43]. Treon et al. presented compelling evidence for BTK inhibitor protection through a case series of six Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia patients on ibrutinib, where five patients on full-dose therapy (420 mg/d) experienced mild disease without hospitalization, while one patient on reduced-dose therapy required intensive care but rapidly improved upon dose escalation, suggesting dose-dependent protective effects against pulmonary complications [44]. Jin et al. highlighted critical diagnostic challenges through a case report of a 39-year-old CLL patient with COVID-19, demonstrating how underlying hematological conditions could mask typical infection markers and potentially extend incubation periods to 25 days, emphasizing the need for enhanced surveillance strategies in immunocompromised populations [45]. Collectively, these early reports established the framework for understanding increased mortality risk, potential therapeutic benefits of BTK inhibitors, and diagnostic complexities that would guide subsequent research in hematological malignancy management during the COVID-19 period.

Keyword analysis, thematic development, and future research directions

The keyword cluster analysis revealed four interconnected research domains that comprehensively characterized leukemia research during the COVID-19 period (Fig. 7A, Supplementary Table 3). The primary focus encompassed the direct clinical impact of COVID-19 on leukemia patients, with extensive investigation of disease outcomes, prognosis, mortality, and complications such as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and pneumonia, particularly in the context of emerging variants like Omicron (Clusters 1, 4, and 8). Concurrently, substantial research attention was directed toward COVID-19 vaccine efficacy and immune response patterns, specifically examining BNT162b2 mRNA COVID-19 vaccine immunogenicity, humoral and cellular immune responses, and antibody production in immunocompromised populations (Clusters 2, 3, 6, 7, and 9). The research landscape encompassed a comprehensive spectrum of hematological malignancies, including AML, CLL, chronic myelocytic leukemia (CML), acute leukemia, B-cell lymphoma, non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, and hairy cell leukemia, reflecting disease-specific COVID-19 period considerations (Clusters 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, and 9). Finally, therapeutic intervention strategies emerged as a critical research domain, focusing on treatment adaptations including targeted therapies such as BTK inhibitors (ibrutinib), venetoclax, monoclonal antibodies (rituximab, obinutuzumab), tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), chemotherapy protocols, and complex procedures like allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, emphasizing the balance between maintaining treatment efficacy and minimizing infection risks in immunosuppressed leukemia patients (Clusters 1, 3, 5, 6, 7, and 8).

Discussion

General information

This bibliometric analysis reveals the substantial global research response to the intersection of leukemia and COVID-19. The temporal distribution demonstrates a characteristic COVID-19 period research pattern, with output rapidly escalating from 33 publications in 2020 to a peak of 103 publications in 2022, followed by a gradual decline as the acute phase of the COVID-19 period subsided. This trajectory reflects the research community’s immediate mobilization to address urgent clinical questions regarding leukemia patient vulnerability, followed by more sustained investigation of long-term implications and therapeutic adaptations.

The geographic analysis reveals a marked concentration of research productivity in developed nations, with the United States leading in absolute output (96 publications, 2,778 citations) while smaller countries like Israel demonstrated superior research efficiency (57.71 citations per publication). This disparity underscores the critical role of established research infrastructure and international collaboration networks in facilitating rapid COVID-19 period response research. The predominance of institutions from North America, Europe, and select Middle Eastern centers reflects existing hematological research excellence rather than COVID-19-specific capacity building, suggesting that COVID-19 research built upon established scientific expertise rather than creating entirely new research paradigms.

The interdisciplinary nature of this research field is evidenced by the diverse journal landscape, spanning from specialized hematology publications to high-impact general medical journals, indicating successful integration of COVID-19 period considerations into established hematological research frameworks. The strong performance of specialized journals like Blood and Leukemia, alongside the prominence of general medical publications such as New England Journal of Medicine and Lancet in co-citation networks, demonstrates how COVID-19 research necessitated bridging subspecialty expertise with broader clinical and public health perspectives, ultimately advancing both fields through this forced convergence of research priorities.

Current research hotspots and emerging paradigms

Building upon this foundational research landscape, the keyword cluster analysis identifies four critical thematic domains that have emerged as the predominant research hotspots, fundamentally reshaping our understanding of leukemia management in the COVID-19 period and establishing new clinical paradigms that extend beyond the immediate COVID-19 crisis.

COVID-19 clinical impact and disease outcomes

The pronounced research focus on COVID-19’s direct clinical consequences in leukemia patients (Clusters 1, 4, and 8) represents a paradigmatic shift from theoretical risk assessment to evidence-based outcome prediction. The disproportionate vulnerability of leukemia patients to severe manifestations, including ARDS and pneumonia [46], has necessitated the development of novel prognostic frameworks that integrate traditional hematological risk stratification with COVID-19-specific factors. The emergence of variants, particularly Omicron, has added complexity to this domain, requiring continuous reassessment of risk profiles and outcome predictions. This research hotspot reflects the urgent clinical need to move beyond general immunocompromised patient guidelines toward leukemia-specific management protocols, highlighting how COVID-19 period research has accelerated the precision medicine approach in hematological oncology by forcing the field to rapidly develop disease-specific risk stratification tools [47, 48].

Vaccine immunogenicity and immune response patterns

The intensive investigation of vaccination strategies and immune responses (Clusters 2, 3, 6, 7, and 9) has evolved into one of the most clinically impactful research domains, fundamentally challenging traditional vaccination paradigms in immunocompromised populations. The documentation of impaired humoral and cellular responses to BNT162b2 mRNA vaccines has catalyzed the development of personalized vaccination strategies, including modified dosing schedules, alternative vaccine platforms, and immune response monitoring protocols [49, 50]. This research hotspot has broader implications beyond COVID-19, establishing new frameworks for evaluating vaccine efficacy in hematological malignancies that will likely influence future vaccination strategies for other infectious diseases [51–53]. The necessity of this research is underscored by the critical gap between general population vaccine guidelines and the unique immunological landscapes of leukemia patients, driving the development of evidence-based, disease-specific vaccination protocols [49, 50, 54].

Hematological malignancy spectrum and disease-specific considerations

The comprehensive coverage of diverse leukemia subtypes and related hematological malignancies (Clusters 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, and 9) represents a crucial evolution from generalized “cancer patient” approaches toward nuanced, disease-specific management strategies. The recognition that CLL, CML, and lymphoproliferative disorders each present distinct challenges during the COVID-19 period has driven the development of tailored clinical guidelines and research protocols [55, 56]. This research hotspot reflects the maturation of leukemia as a field, moving away from broad immunocompromised patient categories toward precise, molecular-level understanding of how different malignancies interact with infectious diseases. The clinical necessity of this approach is evidenced by the varying infection susceptibility, vaccine responses, and treatment modifications required across different hematological conditions, establishing a new standard for precision medicine in infectious disease management.

Therapeutic interventions and treatment adaptations

The focus on treatment modalities and COVID-19 period-related modifications (Clusters 1, 3, 5, 6, 7, and 8) has emerged as perhaps the most clinically transformative research domain, fundamentally altering therapeutic decision-making in leukemia. The discovery of potential protective effects of BTK inhibitors, the optimization of venetoclax protocols, and the careful management of anti-CD20 therapies during the COVID-19 period have established new treatment paradigms that balance oncological efficacy with infection risk [57–59]. This research hotspot has broader implications for future infectious disease preparedness, creating frameworks for rapid treatment adaptation that will likely influence clinical practice beyond the current COVID-19 period. The integration of infection risk into routine oncological decision-making represents a permanent shift in clinical practice, highlighting how COVID-19 period research has accelerated the development of more sophisticated risk-benefit analyses in cancer therapeutics.

These four research hotspots collectively illustrate how the COVID-19 period has stimulated significant research activity in leukemia care, generating new knowledge and methodological approaches that may influence future hematological oncology research and practice development beyond the immediate COVID-19 period. However, the translation of research findings into established clinical paradigms requires ongoing evaluation and validation through long-term follow-up studies and clinical outcome assessments.

Systemic impact and future implications

While the identified research hotspots reveal the immediate scientific response to the COVID-19 period, the broader systemic impact of COVID-19 on leukemia research extends far beyond these thematic domains, fundamentally reshaping the landscape of hematological oncology and establishing new paradigms for future clinical practice and research methodology.

Healthcare system restructuring and resilience Building

The COVID-19’s impact on leukemia research transcends clinical management, revealing and reconstructing the fundamental vulnerabilities and adaptive capacities of the entire hematological oncology healthcare system [27, 29]. The reallocation of medical resources, postponement of elective treatments, and implementation of infection control measures have forced the field to critically reassess the traditional “hospital-centered” treatment model [24, 60, 61]. This systemic disruption has catalyzed the development of more flexible and resilient healthcare delivery systems, including stratified care models, optimized home-based treatment protocols, and emergency medical resource allocation strategies [62–64]. More importantly, this process has exposed the particular vulnerability of leukemia patients during public health crises, prompting policymakers to include hematological malignancy patients in priority protection groups, thereby institutionally establishing the central position of hematology in crisis medicine [62].

Ethical complexities and clinical decision-making evolution

The COVID-19 has introduced unprecedented ethical dimensions to leukemia research, reflected in the significantly increased complexity of clinical decision-making. Physicians must now navigate intricate trade-offs between treatment benefits and infection risks that extend beyond traditional risk-benefit analysis frameworks [25]. Decisions regarding continuation of intensive chemotherapy, timing of stem cell transplantation, and individualized adjustment of immunosuppressive treatments require integration of patients’ oncological characteristics, immune status, social support systems, and local COVID-19 dynamics [62, 65]. This complexity has driven the development of novel clinical decision support tools and multidisciplinary collaboration models, while simultaneously advancing medical ethics in hematology by establishing more sophisticated ethical decision-making frameworks that provide crucial guidance for future crisis management.

Digital transformation and telemedicine development

The COVID-19 has accelerated digital transformation in hematology, with profound implications for redefining patient-physician interaction models and healthcare delivery mechanisms. The widespread adoption of remote monitoring technologies, digital symptom management platforms, and virtual multidisciplinary team consultations has not only addressed COVID-19 period-related healthcare accessibility issues but also established new patient-centered continuous care models [66–68]. This transformation has generated unprecedented real-time health data collection and analysis opportunities, providing an extraordinary data foundation for precision medicine development in hematological malignancies. Simultaneously, the proliferation of digital tools has exposed healthcare digital divide issues, prompting researchers to focus more intensively on medical equity and accessibility, driving the development of inclusive digital healthcare solutions [67, 69].

Long-term consequences and post-pandemic considerations

The enduring impact of the pandemic on leukemia research is reflected in renewed attention to patients’ long-term prognosis and quality of life. Issues such as unique manifestations of “long COVID” symptoms in leukemia patients, persistent effects of COVID-19 period-related psychological trauma, and potential long-term survival consequences of treatment delays have driven a paradigmatic shift from short-term treatment efficacy toward comprehensive population health management [27, 29, 70]. This transition has generated more comprehensive patient assessment tools and multidimensional prognostic indicator systems, while promoting deep interdisciplinary integration between hematology and public health, social medicine, and psychology. Critically, the COVID-19 period experience has highlighted the importance of establishing crisis response mechanisms, driving systematic development of emergency treatment guidelines, drug reserve strategies, and medical resource emergency allocation mechanisms for hematological malignancies.

Global collaboration and research methodology innovation

The globalized characteristics of leukemia research during the COVID-19 period reflect fundamental transformations in modern medical research. Rapid data sharing, swift initiation of international multicenter studies, and standardization of research protocols mark a fundamental shift from regionalized to globally networked hematological research [24, 70, 71]. This collaborative model has not only accelerated scientific discovery but also established global research response mechanisms for future crises. Additionally, the COVID-19 has driven research methodology innovations, including widespread adoption of pragmatic clinical trial designs, systematic application of real-world data, and development of adaptive clinical trial methods, establishing more flexible and efficient research paradigms for hematological research with impacts extending far beyond the COVID-19 period timeframe.

These research patterns suggest that COVID-19 has served as a significant driver of research activity and methodological innovation in hematology, with potential long-term influences on research directions and clinical practice development that warrant continued investigation and evaluation.

Limitation and future directions

Research limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this bibliometric analysis. The exclusive reliance on WoSCC introduces systematic biases including: (i) English-language bias potentially underrepresenting non-English research; (ii) geographical bias toward Western countries, underrepresenting research from Latin America, Africa, Asia, and other non-Western regions; (iii) journal selection bias favoring high-impact journals over regional or specialized publications; and (iv) potential underrepresentation of health sciences literature compared to Scopus and PubMed. These limitations may compromise the completeness of our global research landscape assessment. Additionally, our analysis specifically examines COVID-19-leukemia research rather than all leukemia research during 2020–2024, limiting generalizability to broader hematological research trends. Results should be interpreted as representing the COVID-19-leukemia research domain rather than general leukemia research patterns.

Methodological limitations include author and institutional name disambiguation challenges. Variations in name formatting (initials, middle names, transliteration from non-Latin scripts) and institutional recording may lead to inflated or deflated counts. Without systematic disambiguation procedures, reported institutional and author counts should be interpreted as estimates, particularly affecting researchers with common surnames or diverse linguistic backgrounds.

Temporal limitations include the evolving pandemic status (acute phase concluded May 2023) and relatively short observation period (2020–2024) that may not capture long-term research evolution given 12–24 month publication lags. Critical impact analysis limitations include inability to implement time-normalized citation metrics (FWCI), creating temporal bias where earlier publications (2020–2022) have longer citation accumulation periods than recent works (2023–2024). Citation counts should be interpreted as cumulative scholarly attention rather than normalized research quality indicators.

Additional analytical limitations include lack of benchmarking against general leukemia literature, absence of advanced centrality measures for collaboration networks, and missing year-by-year thematic breakdowns preventing assessment of differential trends across research hotspots. Inherent bibliometric limitations include potential keyword search gaps for emerging terminologies, inability to assess methodological quality or clinical relevance, overemphasis on citation visibility rather than practical clinical value, and incomplete capture of collaboration networks or qualitative knowledge transfer aspects.

Future improvement directions

Future investigations should address these limitations through strategic improvements. Multi-database integration (WoSCC, Scopus, PubMed) with standardized data harmonization protocols would reduce geographical and linguistic biases. Sophisticated author disambiguation strategies combining automated algorithms, manual verification, and ORCID identifier matching would improve accuracy.

Enhanced analytical approaches should include time-normalized impact metrics, benchmarking against general leukemia literature, and year-by-year thematic evolution tracking to distinguish field maturation from theme-specific trends. Advanced network analysis incorporating centrality measures (betweenness, closeness, eigenvector) and community detection algorithms would better reveal research clusters and collaboration dynamics.

Mixed-methods approaches combining quantitative bibliometric analysis with qualitative assessment of research quality, clinical implementation outcomes, and longitudinal network evolution would provide comprehensive insights. Development of COVID-19-specific bibliometric frameworks with dynamic weighting systems reflecting clinical significance variations across research domains would inform evidence-based resource allocation for future public health crises.

Expanding analysis beyond traditional publications to include research funding patterns, clinical guideline development, and patient outcome databases would provide comprehensive insights into real-world clinical practice transformation. However, this bibliometric analysis cannot directly demonstrate clinical practice changes or definitively establish paradigm shifts in patient care, requiring further investigation through outcome-based studies, clinical guideline analysis, and healthcare delivery assessments.

Conclusion

This bibliometric analysis of 346 publications examining the COVID-19–leukemia intersection shows that the COVID-19 period fundamentally influenced research on COVID-19’s impact on leukemia, with four critical domains emerging: clinical impact assessment, vaccine immunogenicity, disease-specific management, and therapeutic adaptations. The research evolution from 2020 to 2024 shows substantial global collaboration focusing on key clinical questions, including documentation of impaired vaccine responses in leukemia patients, investigation of protective effects of targeted therapies like BTK inhibitors, and exploration of treatment protocols balancing cancer control with infection prevention.

Our analysis reveals increased research attention toward precision medicine approaches that consider infectious disease risk alongside oncological factors, accelerated adoption of digital healthcare methodologies in research design, and enhanced international research collaboration networks. The sustained research focus on personalized vaccination strategies, targeted therapy optimization, and long-term pandemic effects suggests evolving research priorities in hematological oncology that may influence future clinical practice development and preparation for infectious disease challenges in vulnerable cancer populations.
