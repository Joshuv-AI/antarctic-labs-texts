Unique liver function in high myopia: associations with myopic macular degeneration
Authors: Yujun Pei, Jiaqi Meng, Wenwen He, Keke Zhang, Dongling Guo, Yi Lu, Xiangjia Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate liver function and lipid indexes in patients with high myopia and their association with myopic macular degeneration (MMD).MethodsThis cross-sectional comparative study included 995 emmetropic patients and 805 highly myopic patients. Serum levels of liver function and lipid indexes were measured using a Roche C702 biochemical analyzer. Ultra-widefield fundus photographs of eyes were classified according to the International META-PM Classification: Category 0: No myopic retinal degeneration, Category 1: tessellated fundus, Category 2: diffuse choroidal retinal atrophy, Category 3: patchy choroidal retinal atrophy, Category 4: macular atrophy. A machine learning model based on liver function indexes was employed to predict the presence of MMD in patients with high myopia.ResultsSerum levels of albumin (Alb), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT) and triglyceride (TG) were significantly lower, whereas high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL) and apolipoprotein-A (Apo-A) were higher in patients with high myopia than those in emmetropic patients (all P < 0.05). Significant differences in serum ALT and GGT were found among MMD categories (both P < 0.05). Multivariate logistic regression showed that MMD4 was associated with lower serum GGT than MMD1 (P < 0.05). The decision tree model to predict MMD achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.735 using serum GGT (sensitivity = 53.12%; specificity = 82.09%; P < 0.001). When using multiple liver function indexes, the AUC of the model reached 0.803 (sensitivity = 73.4%; specificity = 76.1%; P < 0.001).ConclusionWe identified close associations between liver function and MMD, suggesting serum GGT serve as a potential diagnostic indicator for MMD among highly myopic patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-025-04491-0.Keywords: Serum, High myopia, Myopic macular degeneration

Introduction

High myopia, characterized by an axial length (AL) of the eye ≥ 26 mm, has received widespread attention due to its rising prevalence [1]. High myopia, usually caused by a combination of genetic and environmental factors, is prone to many severe ocular complications, such as cataract, glaucoma, and macular degeneration [2, 3]. Myopic macular degeneration (MMD), a severe complication that threatens vision, involves structural degeneration of the retina and choroid caused by axial elongation, severely impairing patients’ quality of life [4, 5]. However, few effective methods for preventing and controlling complications in patients with high myopia have been identified.

Organ-to-organ communication plays a critical role in both physiological and pathological processes [6]. The liver, as a central metabolic organ, produces hormones, hepatokines, and metabolites that can affect distal tissues, including the retina. Liver diseases have also been found to be associated with ocular diseases, such as cataract and glaucoma [7, 8], highlighting a potential liver-eye metabolic axis. Moreover, lower alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels have been linked to eyelid edema in Graves orbitopathy, suggesting that liver function indexes may influence eye health [9]. As one of the most important anabolic organs in the human body, the liver is involved in regulating lipid, protein, and sugar metabolism, and also plays a crucial role in the body’s stress response [10].

Emerging evidence suggests that systemic metabolic disturbances, oxidative stress, and inflammation contribute to the development and progression of myopia. Several studies have reported alterations in serum C-reactive protein, complement components, and metabolites in myopic individuals [11, 12], indicating that systemic stressors may influence retinal physiology. Glutathione (GSH), a major intracellular antioxidant, is critical for maintaining redox homeostasis in retinal cells, including the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and photoreceptors [13]. Gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), a key hepatic enzyme in the γ-glutamyl cycle, regulates extracellular GSH degradation and recycling, thereby linking hepatic metabolism to systemic antioxidant capacity [14].

Furthermore, lipid metabolism may also contribute to ocular pathology. Liver-derived metabolites and cholesterol-related molecules, including vitamin D, have been implicated in the initiation and progression of myopia [15]. Altered serum lipid profiles, such as triglycerides, HDL, and apolipoproteins, may reflect systemic metabolic perturbations that influence retinal homeostasis [16, 17]. Therefore, we speculated that liver function and lipid metabolism might be associated with the development of high myopia and MMD. However, relevant evidence has been rare.

In this context, we performed a retrospective cross-sectional study to examine liver function and lipid indexes in patients with high myopia and also to assess their associations with AL and MMD based on a large sample population. This study seeks to provide preliminary evidence for a potential liver-eye metabolic axis and to explore how systemic metabolic and oxidative status may relate to myopic progression.

Materials and methods

Subjects

This study included consecutive patients diagnosed with high myopia (AL ≥ 26.00 mm of both eyes) and emmetropia (AL ranging from 21.00 mm to less than 24.50 mm in both eyes) examined at EENT Hospital of Fudan University between May 2021 and September 2023. Exclusion criteria were patients with: (1) prior history of liver disease (viral hepatitis, autoimmune hepatitis, alcoholic hepatitis, hyperbilirubinemia, cirrhosis, etc.); (2) patients receiving hepatotoxic drugs or chemotherapy; (3) patients diagnosed with multiple organ dysfunction syndrome; (4) inflammatory disease; (5) malignant tumor any; (6) besides myopic fundus changes, other severe forms of retinopathy including conditions like retinal vein occlusion; (7) metabolic disorders such as diabetes. Ultimately, 805 high myopic patients and 995 age- and gender-matched emmetropic subjects were included in the analysis.

Ophthalmic examination

Comprehensive ophthalmic examinations were conducted for all patients, including the slit-lamp examination, AL measurement, fundus photography, intraocular pressure assessment, B-scan ultrasonography, and fundus optical coherence tomography.

Measurement of serum liver function and lipid indexes

Measurement of serum liver function and lipid indexes was performed using samples collected as part of the Shanghai High Myopia Study, a prospective cohort in which highly myopic patients were systematically enrolled and routinely underwent comprehensive clinical assessments and blood sample collection. Our current study represents a retrospective analysis of this well-characterized cohort, and the serum liver function and lipid indexes were not measured specifically for this research. Serum of liver function and lipid tests were conducted for each patient. Serum levels of total protein (TP), albumin (Alb), globulin (Glo), direct bilirubin (DBIL) and total bilirubin (TBIL) were measured by Roche Cobas 8000 C702 (Mannheim, Germany). Serum enzyme levels, including AST, ALT, and GGT, were assessed using an enzymatic colorimetric assay. To ensure the accuracy of the detection system, daily internal quality control tests were conducted using the automatic biochemical analyzer of the above machine, maintaining a 2%-6% variation coefficient per month.

Classification of MMD

In a dark room, fundus photographs of the patient’s eyes were captured using the Optos 200Tx (Optos, England). The classification criteria established by the Meta-analysis of Pathological Myopia (META-PM) study group was used for the independent evaluation of fundus photographs of highly myopic eyes [18]. MMD was categorized by two experienced physicians (YP and JM) into five categories: Category 0: No myopic retinal degeneration, Category 1: tessellated fundus, Category 2: diffuse choroidal retinal atrophy, Category 3: patchy choroidal retinal atrophy, Category 4: macular atrophy. Figure 1 presents representative fundus photographs illustrating various categories of MMD. Any discrepancies between the two doctors were resolved by the senior ophthalmologist (XZ) who made the final decision. The presence of MMD was defined as MMD category ≥ 2.

Machine learning

This machine learning model was conducted among the patients with high myopia in the study. To assess the model’ s generalizability, the dataset was partitioned into a training set (80%) and a testing set (20%) using the ‘Caret’ R package, following commonly used conventions in similar cohort-based predictive analyses. A random seed of 1234 was set to ensure the reproducibility and consistency of the data split across different runs. The machine learning model incorporated only serum liver function indexes as predictors. Although baseline characteristics such as age, gender, AL, and MMD classification were reported for descriptive purposes, AL was not included in the model as a predictor. The MMD classification was recoded into a binary variable (1 representing MMD category = 0 or 1; 2 representing MMD category = 2, 3 or 4) and converted into a factor variable for further analysis.

A decision tree model was constructed using the ‘rpart’ R package, with multiple liver function indexes as independent variables, to predict the presence of MMD. The model’s performance was evaluated by calculating accuracy, Kappa coefficient, and other metrics using the confusion matrix, and by assessing its predictive capability through the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Additionally, a univariate analysis was conducted using GGT as the sole predictor to evaluate its ability to predict the presence of MMD, with performance further assessed using both the confusion matrix and the ROC curve.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are summarized as mean ± SD, while categorical data are represented as numbers and percentages for each category. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the data. Subsequently, depending on the distribution of the continuous variables (normal or non-normal), comparisons between the two groups for continuous variables were conducted using the Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U-test, respectively. Statistical analysis of categorical variables utilized the chi-square test. Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the associations between liver function indexes and AL, as well as between lipid indexes and AL. The concentration of liver function and lipid indexes across various MMD categories was assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test and Dunn post-hoc analysis. Statistically significant findings were determined by two-tailed P values less than 0.05. SPSS version 26.0 was utilized for statistical analysis (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA), while data visualization was carried out using GraphPad Prism 6 software (GraphPad Software Inc, San Diego, CA, USA).

There were no statistically significant differences in other serum liver function and lipid indexes between the two groups (all P > 0.05).

Associations between liver function or lipid indexes and MMD

The levels of ALT and GGT varied significantly among the different MMD categories (Kruskal-Wallis test, P = 0.023 and 0.02, respectively, Fig. 2A-B). Category 1 exhibited significantly higher levels of ALT and GGT compared to Category 2 and 4 (Kruskal-Wallis test and Dunn post-hoc analysis, P < 0.05, Fig. 2A and B). The concentration of GGT was also significantly lower in Category 4 than in Category 2 and 3 (all P < 0.05, Fig. 2B). Other indicators did not differ among MMD categories (all P > 0.05).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that, compared with patients with MMD Category 1, those in Category 2 and Category 3 had significantly longer AL (MMD2: OR = 2.114, P < 0.001; MMD3: OR = 3.62, P < 0.001), whereas serum ALT and GGT levels were not significantly associated (P > 0.05). Patients in MMD Category 4 were older (P < 0.001), had longer AL (OR = 3.94, P < 0.001), and showed lower GGT levels (OR = 0.94, P = 0.028) than those in Category 1 (McFadden’s R² = 0.256).

Machine learning for prediction of MMD

A decision tree model was further employed in the highly myopic patients to predict the presence of MMD based on liver function indexes (Fig. 3A and B). ROC analysis was performed to assess the predictive capability of the decision tree models: one based on GGT (Fig. 3C) and the other based on multiple liver function indexes (Fig. 3D). In the test set, the GGT-based decision tree model exhibited an ROC curve area (AUC) of 0.735, with a sensitivity of 53.12% and a specificity of 82.09% (P < 0.001). In contrast, the decision tree model utilizing multiple liver function indicators achieved an AUC of 0.803, with a sensitivity of 73.4% and a specificity of 76.1% (P < 0.001).

Discussion

High myopia has become a notable worldwide health issue, with forecasts suggestion that by 2050, the number of individuals affected will reach 938 million, comprising approximately 9.8% of the global population [19]. The prevalence of MMD, a common complication of high myopia, has also reached 2.1% of the global population [20], which greatly impairs patients’ vision and reduces their quality of life. However, effective tools for detection and prediction are lacking due to an unclear pathogenesis. Our study is the first to investigate liver function and lipid indexes in the highly myopic population. In this study, we identified the presence of altered liver function and lipid indexes in patients with high myopia, suggesting a potential link between liver and ocular diseases. Our findings also demonstrated a potential association of liver function, particularly GGT, with AL and MMD, implying that GGT might serve as an indicative marker for MMD.

For the first time, we identified unique characteristics of liver function and lipid indexes in high myopic patients compared to emmetropic individuals. We analyzed changes in serum liver function indexes in highly myopic patients, and observed lower levels of Alb, ALP, and GGT. Among these, reduced serum GGT is particularly noteworthy, as GGT is typically elevated under metabolic or inflammatory stress. Rather than indicating hepatic dysfunction, this paradoxical reduction may reflect an adaptive or dysregulated systemic metabolic state, with potential consequences for systemic redox balance and retinal antioxidant capacity. The liver, being crucial for protein synthesis and metabolism, plays a significant role in diseases related to inflammation, injury response, immunity, and metabolism. Our results support the hypothesis that the liver may have unique features in the highly myopic population and there may be an organ-organ conversation underlying the pathogenesis of high myopia.

Mechanistic studies increasingly support the concept of a liver-retina metabolic axis. A multi-omics analysis demonstrated coordinated metabolic and transcriptomic changes in both liver and retinal tissues under high-fat diet conditions, suggesting that hepatic metabolic perturbations may propagate to the retina via systemic circulation and hepatokines such as FGF21 and betaine [21], supporting the notion that systemic inflammatory and metabolic signals may modulate retinal homeostasis. Taken together, these evidences highlight the plausibility that hepatic and systemic signals, including low GGT, can influence retinal physiology under metabolic stress, providing a biological context for the associations observed in our study.

Notably, GGT may serve as a potential indicator reflecting metabolic alterations in the progression of high myopia and MMD. We found that GGT levels were lower in patients with high myopia and showed a weak negative correlation with AL (Supplementary Figure S1). Despite the statistical significance, the correlation was relatively weak, implying that GGT explains a negligible amount of variance in AL, which might reflect random variability rather than a true biological relationship. Furthermore, serum GGT level could distinguish between MMD Category 4 (macular atrophy) and other categories. Based on machine learning models, a serum GGT-based model achieved an AUC of 0.735 for predicting the presence of MMD, which increased to 0.803 when multiple liver function indexes were incorporated. This finding highlights a distinct systemic metabolic adaptation in high myopia.

Beyond its hepatic role, GGT is a key enzyme in the γ-glutamyl cycle that regulates glutathione metabolism and systemic redox homeostasis [22]. Dysregulation of this pathway may therefore influence retinal oxidative resilience in high myopia.

Mechanistically, GGT occupies a pivotal position in the γ-glutamyl cycle, governing the extracellular catabolism and intracellular resynthesis of GSH, the principal low-molecular-weight antioxidant in mammalian tissues [22–24]. Through regulating cysteine supply and GSH turnover, GGT maintains systemic redox homeostasis and protects against oxidative and metabolic stress [25]. However, dysregulation of this pathway can produce dual effects. On one hand, impaired GGT activity may restrict GSH recycling and weaken antioxidant defenses [22]; on the other hand, excessive GGT activity may generate reactive intermediates that paradoxically promote oxidative stress [26]. These bidirectional effects suggest that serum GGT serves as a sensitive but context-dependent biomarker of redox imbalance rather than a simple indicator of hepatic injury.

Accumulating evidence supports a central role of oxidative stress in the development of myopia and myopic maculopathy. Oxidative damage within the retina and sclera has been associated with extracellular matrix remodeling, choroidal thinning, and photoreceptor degeneration, which together represent key pathological features of progressive myopia [27]. Recent metabolomic analyses have identified altered GSH and thiol metabolism in both low and high myopia, reinforcing the concept that impaired GSH-dependent antioxidant capacity contributes to ocular elongation and degeneration [28]. Overall, these results suggest that systemic redox imbalance may contribute to retinal vulnerability in high myopia.

Previous studies have also reported associations between liver function and retinal or choroidal thickness in patients with high myopia, supporting the plausibility that hepatic metabolism may influence ocular structural parameters [29].

Extending this redox framework, experimental studies have shown that mitochondrial GSH (mGSH) is essential for maintaining redox balance in RPE and photoreceptors, with its depletion leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and cell death [30]. Thus, the reduced serum GGT observed in our cohort could reflect impaired systemic GSH recycling, limiting mGSH replenishment in RPE cells and exacerbating oxidative vulnerability under myopic stress. Moreover, a previous study has indicated a negative correlation between glutamine level and the severity of myopia [31]. Serum GGT levels have also been linked to the progression of advanced age-related macular degeneration [32], while in glaucoma models, GGT1 targets the catalytic subunit of glutamate-cysteine ligase (GCLC), inhibiting ferroptosis and autophagy in retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), thus protecting these cells from oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage [33]. Collectively, these findings suggest that in high myopia, reduced serum GGT may reflect altered systemic antioxidant metabolism, potentially limiting retinal antioxidant capacity and contributing to disease progression. Thus, our discovery of low GGT in high myopia is both statistically significant and biologically meaningful, revealing a potentially underappreciated dimension of systemic redox imbalance in myopic pathology.

Based on these observations, we propose several testable hypotheses: (1) decreased GGT expression or inducibility (genetic or metabolic origin) could reduce systemic GSH turnover; (2) excessive peripheral GSH consumption or redistribution under chronic oxidative stress may lower serum GGT; (3) highly myopic individuals may exhibit a distinct metabolic phenotype affecting liver-retina coupling, mitochondrial bioenergetics, and lipid transport.

With regards to lipid metabolism, our findings indicate that patients with high myopia exhibited lower TG levels compared to emmetropic patients, alongside higher levels of HDL and Apo-A. This suggests the involvement of lipid metabolism in the pathogenesis of high myopia. Previous studies have demonstrated there is a positive correlation between body mass index (BMI) and TG levels [34]. The incidence of myopia has been demonstrated to be associated with BMI [35, 36]. The relationship between lipid metabolism and BMI in highly myopic patients may be further explored in animal models. In terms of HDL and Apo-A, positive correlations between the two in normal populations have been reported. Previous research has linked serum Apo-A with advanced age-related macular degeneration [37], suggesting a relationship between lipid metabolism and the development of macular degeneration. However, such an association was not significant between Apo-A and MMD progression. While different types of macular degeneration may exhibit similar structural changes, their unique pathogeneses enable the differentiation of myopic macular degeneration from others through various indicator levels. Our findings may inspire the possibility of subsequent studies on the mechanisms of high myopia disease from a systemic metabolic perspective.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between altered liver function. Longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to clarify whether these hepatic alterations are causative factors, compensatory responses, or parallel metabolic manifestations. Secondly, while our analysis revealed associations between serum GGT and ocular parameters, the biological mechanisms linking hepatic metabolism and retinal pathology remain speculative. Currently, there are no well-established experimental models to directly investigate the liver-retina axis in myopia, limiting mechanistic validation. Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable preliminary evidence and generate testable hypotheses regarding the metabolic interplay between liver function and high myopia.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study for the first time characterized the unique profiles of liver function and lipid indexes in patients with high myopia, indicating a potential systemic link between hepatic metabolism and ocular pathology. Serum GGT levels were reduced in highly myopic individuals and were associated with the severity of MMD, suggesting that GGT may serve as a candidate biomarker for metabolic changes during myopic progression. These findings provide new insights into the liver-retina metabolic axis and highlight the need for longitudinal and mechanistic studies to clarify how hepatic redox and lipid metabolism influence high myopia and the progression of MMD.
