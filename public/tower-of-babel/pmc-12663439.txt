Methanol biotransformation for the production of biodegradable plastic monomer L-lactate in yeast
Authors: Wei Yu, Chenyue Zhang, Yunxia Li, Qing-An Chen, Qiang Fei, Jiaoqi Gao, Yongjin J Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMethanol is an ideal feedstock for biomanufacturing, and production of the biodegradable plastic monomer lactate from methanol is a promising approach for mitigating white pollution. However, it is challenging to engineer microbes for lactate production from methanol because of strong competition between product synthesis and cell growth. Here, Ogataea polymorpha is rewired to synthesize L-lactate from methanol, where the cofactor ratio of NADPH/NADP+ is higher than that of NADH/NAD+. By engineering gene expression, enhancing cell viability, modifying cofactor homeostasis, and performing mitochondrial compartmentalization, 2.5 g/L L-lactate is produced in a shake flask. Fed-batch fermentation in a bioreactor results in the highest titer of 25.0 g/L L-lactate from methanol, which is chemically synthesized from CO2. A techno-economic analysis and life cycle assessment are performed to evaluate the commercial potential, environmental impacts, and greenhouse gas mitigation performance of CO2-derived L-lactate. This study could lay the foundation for the carbon-neutral production of biodegradable plastic polylactic acid from CO2.Subject terms: Metabolic engineering, Metabolic engineering, Industrial microbiology

Introduction

A world without plastics seems unimaginable, as plastics are widely used in food packaging, agriculture, and transportation, among other industries1. However, fossil fuel-derived commodity plastics, such as polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), and polyvinyl chloride (PVC), are undegradable, which causes severe white pollution. When these plastics are fragmented into small pieces, known as microplastics, the risks to human health increase2. To address the problems of undegradable plastics and the heavy reliance on fossil fuels, biobased degradable plastics, among which polylactic acid (PLA) is the most widely used, have attracted considerable attention. PLA is industrially synthesized through ring-opening polymerization of lactide, which is obtained from the monomer lactate. Chemical synthesis of lactate uses petrochemical feedstocks and results in racemic mixtures of D/L-lactate3. In contrast, bioproduction generates enantiomerically pure lactic acid (D- or L-form), which facilitates the synthesis of poly(D-lactic acid), poly(L-lactic acid), and poly(DL-lactic acid), which have different properties4.

Biorefineries can be divided into three generations: the 1st generation (1G) uses glucose as feedstocks; the 2nd generation (2G) uses nonfood lignocellulosic biomass as feedstocks; and the 3rd generation (3G) uses one carbon (C1) source, such as CO2, methane, or methanol, as feedstocks5. Although major companies, including NatureWorks and BASF, have employed 1G feedstocks for lactate production with high yields (0.9 g/g)6, the large demand for PLA may suffer from competition with the food supply. 2G biorefinery has helped promote progress in lactate production from non-edible biomass feedstocks, but complex pretreatment and enzymatic saccharification still hinder profitability7. In the recent decade, we have witnessed a growing emphasis on 3 G biorefineries, including starch synthesis from CO28; the production of single-cell proteins from methane9 or methanol10; and the production of chemicals, such as fatty acids, fatty alcohols, and 3-hydroxypropionate, from methanol11–15. C1-fueled bioprocesses are independent of arable land and can mitigate greenhouse effects; thus, C1-fueled bioprocesses are promising bioprocesses for lactate production.

Among C1 feedstocks, methanol has many advantages, including convenient storage and transportation, efficient liquid-to-liquid mass transfer, and high reducing power16. In particular, methanol can be massively produced from CO2 via liquid sunshine processes (CO2 hydrogenation to methanol)17. Therefore, coupling liquid sunshine and methanol bioconversion enables the carbon-negative production of chemicals from CO2. However, the toxicity of methanol and its rigid metabolism limit the efficiency of chemical production12,18,19. Although many endeavors have been made to either engineer native methylotrophs such as Pichia pastoris and Ogataea polymorpha or construct synthetic methylotrophs such as Escherichia coli, the titers of lactate from methanol are far from adequate for commercialization. The main reason may be the trade-off between methanol metabolism and lactate synthesis, which compete not only for carbon flux but also for the energy supply, especially the cofactor NADH.

Here, we observe a pattern of cofactor distribution during methanol metabolism in the methylotrophic yeast O. polymorpha, which is an industrial strain with strong osmotic and thermal tolerance (reaching 50 °C)20,21. Although methanol metabolism is relatively rigid, the global metabolic redesign of O. polymorpha, which requires optimizing the gene expression of lactate dehydrogenase (Ldh), enhancing cell viability, engineering cofactor homeostasis, and performing mitochondrial compartmentalization, greatly increases L-lactate synthesis from sole methanol. Coupling liquid sunshine and methanol biotransformation achieved economic viability and enabled the carbon-negative production of L-lactate from CO2, which was evaluated via techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life cycle assessment (LCA). This study enables the production of PLA from CO2, a recycling economy that connects biodegradable plastics and carbon neutrality (Fig. 1).

Results

Constructing an L-lactate biosynthetic pathway from methanol alone

Lactate can be produced from an intermediate pyruvate in a central metabolism. This material is catalyzed by lactate dehydrogenase (Ldh) with NADH as a cofactor. In nature, lactic acid bacteria (LAB) can synthesize lactate from glucose with a high yield of 0.9 g/g due to the high efficiency of Ldh6. Here, the methylotrophic yeast O. polymorpha, which cannot produce native lactate, was adopted to produce L-lactate from methanol (Fig. 2a). Five different L-LDH genes from LAB (Supplementary Table 1) were evaluated for L-lactate production by using the methanol-inducible promoter PAOX1 in an episomal plasmid. PaLdh from Pediococcus acidilactici behaved the best among the tested specimens in O. polymorpha (strain HpPa), with 921 mg/L L-lactate being produced from 10 g/L methanol (Fig. 2b; LC‒MS determination in Supplementary Fig. 1). Optical isomer detection by HPLC revealed that the optical purity of L-lactate was >99% (Supplementary Fig. 2). Lactobacillus delbrueckii LdLdh and Lactobacillus helveticus LhLdh exhibited no L-lactate production (Fig. 2b), which, however, showed functional expression at the transcriptional level (Supplementary Fig. 3a). To ensure a stable and optimal expression level, PaLDH was integrated into the genome at different neutral sites with different promoters22,23. Five promoters were characterized by using enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP), which revealed that PAOX1 and PGAP were the strongest -inducible and constitutive promoters in methanol, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Despite variable expression levels of different neutral sites, as previously described22, the production of L-lactate from methanol was affected mainly by promoters rather than neutral sites (Fig. 2c). Strain 3A, which expressed PaLDH under the control of the PAOX1 promoter at the NS3 site, produced 655 mg/L L-lactate. To further promote cell fitness22,24, the LEU2 gene was recovered in situ in situ (Supplementary Fig. 4) from the strains HpPa and 3A, generating strains HPLA05 and HPLA06, respectively. Interestingly, strain HPLA06 produced 1.2 g/L L-lactate, with levels 25% greater than those of HPLA05 (Fig. 2d), demonstrating that one copy of PaLDH in the genome was sufficient to outperform multiple copies in the plasmid. Therefore, the engineered strain HPLA06 was used as the parent strain for further investigation.

Enhancing cell viability in methanol

L-lactate synthesis strongly competes with the tri-carboxylic acid (TCA) cycle for pyruvate25, resulting in a growth delay in methanol. We observed that the biomass of HPLA06 with 1.2 g/L L-lactate dramatically decreased relative to that of the wild-type strain (Supplementary Fig. 5). We evaluated the L-lactate tolerance of O. polymorpha, which showed that 5 g/L L-lactate exhibited no growth inhibition to O. polymorpha (Supplementary Fig. 6). This phenomenon prompted us to explore other strategies to promote cell robustness to increase L-lactate production.

Our previous study demonstrated that phospholipid homeostasis was essential for cell survival in methanol through the inactivation of LPL1 (encoding a putative lipase) and IZH3 (encoding a membrane protein)12. Therefore, single or double deletions of LPL1 and IZH3 were performed in HPLA06, and the results revealed that IZH3 deletion alone improved the L-lactate titer by 23%, with a similar growth profile, whereas LPL1 deletion negatively impacted L-lactate production (Fig. 3a). To determine the mechanism of improved L-lactate synthesis in the IZH3 deletion strain, propidium iodide (PI) staining26,27 was used to determine the cell death rate (PI-positive). As expected, L-lactate production caused more than 10% of the cells to die within 24 h, whereas IZH3 deletion marginally decreased the cell death rate to approximately 5% (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 7), thus increasing cell viability to promote L-lactate synthesis. In this case, we attempted to employ adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) to further improve the cell growth of the engineered strain HPLA06 in methanol. Unfortunately, the L-lactate titer decreased by 62% after 8 rounds of transfer, despite the 128% increase in biomass (Supplementary Fig. 8). Therefore, ALE decreased L-lactate production from methanol, and the mechanism remains to be explored in the future.

Engineering cofactor homeostasis to improve L-lactate synthesis

To promote the growth of L-lactate-producing cells, we believed that cofactor imbalance could be a limiting factor since L-lactate synthesis requires equal amounts of precursor pyruvate and the cofactor NADH (Fig. 4a). Glucose metabolism is conducted mainly through glycolysis, which generates NADH, whereas methanol assimilation is conducted via the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) and xylulose-5-phosphate (Xu5P) cycle, which generate NADPH (Fig. 4a). We found that the cofactor NADPH was more abundant in methanol than in glucose (Fig. 4b). Hence, the cofactor preference of PaLdh was engineered from NADH to NADPH by a single-point mutation at the cofactor binding site (V40R)28,29 (Supplementary Fig. 9). However, one copy integration of PaLDHV40R dramatically decreased L-lactate production (Fig. 4c), probably owing to its relatively low activity. Another copy of PaLDHV40R (strain HPLA13) resulted in 31% higher L-lactate production than did the control strain (strain HPLA07) with native PaLDH (Fig. 4c). However, the third PaLDHV40R copy (strain HPLA17) decreased both cell growth and L-lactate production (Supplementary Fig. 10). In addition, cofactor determination revealed that strain HPLA13, with two copies of NADPH-dependent PaLDHV40R, could achieve cofactor balance, differing from observation in the L-lactate-producing strain with NADH-dependent PaLDH (Fig. 4d). In particular, NADPH-dependent PaLdh did not promote L-lactate synthesis during glucose cultivation (Fig. 4e), considering the similar ratios of NADPH/NADP+ and NADH/NAD+ (Fig. 4b), which demonstrated the trait of a relatively high NADPH/NADP+ ratio during methanol metabolism for product synthesis. It is worth mentioning that PGAP-driven LDH constructs were used for the engineered strains to strengthen LDH gene expression in glucose cultivation. Finally, the strategy of promoting cell viability by deleting the IZH3 gene was used to obtain strain HPLA14, which produced 2.1 g/L L-lactate from 10 g/L methanol, a level 57% greater than that of strain HPLA13 (Fig. 4f). Similarly, adding a third copy of LDHV40R in HPLA14 did not further improve L-lactate production (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Mitochondrial compartmentalization of Ldh

In yeast, pyruvate can enter mitochondria to produce acetyl-CoA, which is further catabolized to yield NADH. In this case, mitochondria are likely an ideal compartment for L-lactate synthesis because of the abundant supply of pyruvate and NADH. Here, the dual-regulation of cytoplasmic and mitochondrial engineering for L-lactate production was carried out by simultaneously expressing NADPH-dependent Ldh in the cytoplasm and NADH-dependent Ldh in mitochondria (Fig. 5a). For mitochondrial localization, an N-terminal mitochondrial targeting signal (MTS) from subunit IV of yeast cytochrome oxidase (CoxIV)30 was selected for fusion with eGFP. Fluorescence microscopy revealed the correct mitochondrial localization of the MTS-fused eGFP by using the mitochondrial marker Mito-tracker Red as a reference (Fig. 5b). MTS was subsequently used to target NADH-dependent PaLdh into mitochondria under the control of the growth phase-dependent promoter Pro-Y1331. Integration of the PaLDH gene into the NS5 and NS6 loci resulted in 7% and 22% increases in L-lactate production, respectively (Fig. 5c). The engineered strain HPLA22 produced 2.5 g/L L-lactate, with a yield of 0.25 g/g methanol. Further increasing the copy number of PaLDH did not promote product synthesis (Supplementary Fig. 12).

Fed-batch fermentation in bioreactors for L-lactate production

To enable stable L-lactate production, the URA3 gene was recovered in situ from strain HPLA22, which marginally increased the L-lactate titer (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 12). To evaluate the potential of carbon-negative L-lactate production, liquid sunshine technology (chemical catalysis of CO2 to methanol)32 and methanol biotransformation (bioconversion of methanol to L-lactate) were combined. The CO2-derived methanol was a solution of methanol and water with a ratio of 2.25:1 (v/v). The high methanol concentration of 593 g/L without impurities was suitable for methanol feeding. The prototrophic strain HPLA22u was subsequently used for fed-batch fermentation with CO2-derived methanol as the sole carbon and energy source. After 252 h of fermentation, 114 g/L methanol was consumed, resulting in the production of 25.0 g/L L-lactate, with a yield of 0.22 g/g (Fig. 5d).

TEA and LCA of L-lactate biomanufacturing derived from CO2

We developed a process employing CO2-derived methanol for the manufacture of L-lactate to conduct TEA and LCA for biorefinery utilizing recombinant O. polymorpha HPLA22u by Aspen plus v14 (Fig. 6a). On the basis of the production metrics and assumed parameters obtained from experimental results or the literature33–35 (Supplementary Tables 2-3), the proposed production scenario was indicative of the capacity of a plant to produce 18,500 tons of L-lactate annually through three major conversion steps, as illustrated in Supplementary Figs. 13-14, which depicts the primary carbon flow within the various subsystems and the detailed process design in Aspen plus, respectively. It is worth noting that projected production capacity corresponds with standard industrial lactic acid plant scales (15,000-30,000 tons per year)36 and is in accordance with capacities frequently utilized in published TEA studies on third-generation biomanufacturing37,38. The capital expenses (CAPEX) for this plant are described in Fig. 6b and Supplementary Table 4, including fixed capital investment (FCI), total indirect costs (TICs), and total direct costs (TDCs). Among the five modules of the process (methanol production, L-lactate production, L-lactate purification, wastewater treatment, and utilities), the L-lactate production subsystem was determined to necessitate the greatest installation cost for equipment. The CAPEX for equipment and installation of five modules was 2.3%, 59.5%, 12.3%, 7.3%, and 18.6%, respectively (Supplementary Table 4). Comprehensive investment details for equipment and installation corresponding to each individual component are available in Supplementary Fig. 15. Notably, the bioreactors accounted for the predominant share of expenditures associated with equipment and installation in CAPEX. The investment in the purification unit is less extensive compared to the traditional separation process, the membrane separation approach operates under milder conditions and involves fewer steps, hence decreasing both energy requirements and complexity39,40. In terms of operational expense (OPEX), raw materials constituted the primary factor in manufacturing costs (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Table 5). Notably, this analysis considered carbon trading as a potential credit for the entire process. The ongoing implementation of carbon tax policies and the rise in carbon trading prices markedly enhanced economic efficiency41. Furthermore, the residual biomass can be marketed as nitrogen fertilizer through anaerobic digestion. When we assumed operation of the L-lactate production process for 30 years with an internal rate of return (IRR) of 10%, the net present value (NPV) became zero at an L-lactate minimum selling price (MSP) of $2.29 kg-1, which was comparable to the prevailing market price of $1.85–3.15 kg-1 34,42. The cost distribution for the MSP of five modules was 31.2%, 43.9%, 9.5%, 3.0%, and 12.4%, respectively (Supplementary Table 6). The MSP of the baseline scenario indicated the economic viability of CO2-derived L-lactate production and its prospective ability to achieve economic competitiveness.

On the basis of the findings of CAPEX and OPEX, the costs associated with equipment, installation, feedstock, and carbon tax revenue exhibited the highest sensitivity to MSP. Consequently, we adjusted the values of eight pertinent parameters to generate the corresponding variation range for a single-point sensitivity analysis. We intended to ascertain the impacts of different parameters on the scale-up process of L-lactate derived from CO2 and to find prospective opportunities for enhanced commercial viability (Fig. 6d). Sensitivity analysis indicated that the yield of L-lactate from CO2-derived methanol at the break-even point was highly sensitive to overall costs; an increase in L-lactate yield from 0.22 to 0.26 g/g led to a 15.4% reduction in the MSP, which was chiefly attributed to the strong correlation between the increase in annual plant capacity and the MSP. Moreover, lower productivity and titer could lead to a 11.2–12.0% increase in MSP, as CAPEX significantly correlated with this parameter of annual production. To sustain the annual 18,500-ton capacity with lower productivity, increased equipment investment was needed for the acquisition of additional bioreactors. Since yield, productivity, and titer were closely linked to the carbon flux directed toward L-lactate, which impacted the overall capacity of the plant, these parameters were notable as major cost drivers. In addition, the fluctuations in the carbon source price and power costs exhibited significant effects. When the solar power price ranges from 0.02–0.04 $/kWh, the MSP varies by ±(5.0–5.9)% ($2.15–2.40 kg−1). Considering the regional variation in solar power costs, the MSP fluctuates significantly with the geographical location of the plant43 (Supplementary Fig. 16). For example, countries with abundant solar resources such as Pakistan show solar power prices close to the global minimum (<0.025 $/kWh), resulting in comparatively lower MSP, whereas regions with higher solar power prices such as Canada (>0.10 $/kWh) may increase the MSP by over 13%. These results emphasize the critical role of site selection in improving the economic competitiveness of biomanufacturing L-lactate from CO2. The findings indicated that the parameters related to L-lactate production in this study substantially influenced the economic viability of process commercialization and underscored the opportunity for cost reduction through improved carbon source conversion efficiency and reduced atmospheric CO2 capture cost. Although the MSP of the base scenario demonstrated remarkable economic performance, enhancements in fermentation-related parameters could further augment its economic feasibility.

Environmental impact assessment is a vital element influencing the viability of sustainably scaling up and commercializing the L-lactate production process. A preliminary LCA was performed to illustrate the global warming potential (GWP) linked to the production of 1 kg of L-lactate, with the life cycle inventory (LCI) derived from the Aspen Plus simulation (Supplementary Table 7). As shown in Fig. 6e, the cradle-to-gate life cycle GWP of 1 kg of L-lactate produced from methanol was 1.74 kg CO2-eq. The contribution analysis of GWP hot spots revealed that power consumption (52.19%), CO2 emission from combined heat and power (32.62%), and the nitrogen source for cell growth (14.62%) were the primary contributors across all impact categories (Supplementary Table 8). It is worth mentioning that from the standpoint of economic value allocation, the by-product of fertilizer could result in a GWP decrease of 0.277 kg CO2-eq, so further diminishing the carbon footprint. The results indicated that the suggested approach for L-lactate production could significantly decrease greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions through the incorporation of alternative renewable energy sources. Notably, the ammonia production process, known as Haber–Bosch, could be characterized by high energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions due to fossil fuel combustion, contributing to the global warming potential. Nonetheless, to guarantee the appropriate development of microorganisms, this environmental influence is necessary. As a reference, the environmental impacts of the current L-lactate production method were compared with those of fossil-based refineries (produced via acetaldehyde44), and lignocellulose-based biorefineries (including lignocellulosic biomass45, biomass saccharides44, and vine shoots46) demonstrated a potential reduction in GWP reaching 2.56 tons of GHG emissions per ton of L-lactate produced (Fig. 6f). The competitive advantage of GWP principally stemmed from CO2 serving as the carbon source for process design, which is often considered a principal GHG in the atmosphere, with the synthesis of 1 ton of L-lactate resulting in a carbon deposition of 7.29 tons (Supplementary Table 8). Consequently, the biomanufacturing of L-lactate from CO2-derived methanol using O. polymorpha provides a viable approach for harnessing atmospheric GHGs to achieve low-carbon production while possessing the potential for commercial competitive business activities.

Discussion

The green and sustainable production of biodegradable plastics is a feasible route for solving the problem of white pollution caused by petroleum-derived plastics1. PLA, the most frequently used biodegradable plastic, is synthesized through the ring-opening polymerization of lactide derived from the lactate monomer. In this study, we establish a carbon-negative technology by systematically engineering the methylotrophic yeast O. polymorpha, achieving the highest L-lactate titer from C1 feedstocks (Table 1).

Comparison of lactate bioproduction from one-carbon feedstocks

Although CO2 can be used directly to produce lactate, the titer and productivity are much lower than those from methanol (Table 1). The main reason is the low gas-to-liquid transfer rate in gas fermentation. In comparison, the high liquid-to-liquid transfer rate in methanol fermentation greatly facilitates microbial cell growth and chemical production. Compared with glucose, methanol is an ideal feedstock with a 50% higher degree of reduction47, which facilitates the production of reduced products, such as fatty acids, fatty alcohols, isoprenoids, and organic acids11–15,48. In addition, methanol derived from liquid sunshine technology enables the carbon-negative production of chemicals17,32. For example, some microbes have evolved to utilize methanol naturally as the sole carbon and energy source, such as methylotrophic bacteria (Methylobacterium extorquens and Bacillus methanolicus) and methylotrophic yeasts (P. pastoris and O. polymorpha). The developed genetic manipulation tools promote cell factory construction22,49–54. In particular, researchers have endeavored to engineer model microorganisms, such as Escherichia coli, Corynebacterium glutamicum, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae, to utilize methanol, which has become an alternative route for methanol biorefinery55–58.

Methylotrophic yeasts, including P. pastoris and O. polymorpha, have been engineered to produce the PLA monomer lactate from methanol59,60. To our knowledge, efficient bioproduction of lactate from methanol relies on a sufficient supply of precursor pyruvate, the enzyme Ldh, and the cofactor NAD(P)H, which requires extensive redesign of the cellular metabolism. While O. polymorpha, like other yeasts such as S. cerevisiae and P. pastoris, possesses an LDH gene in its genome, these yeasts naturally produce no lactate. Lactate production only occurs when a bacterial LDH gene is expressed. We speculate that the native yeast LDH may encode an enzyme catalyzing the reverse reaction (lactate to pyruvate), which is consistent with their ability to degrade lactate. We found that the Ldh from P. acidilactici demonstrated the highest performance for L-lactate production among the five screened LDH genes.

In this work, multidimensional metabolic engineering strategies are employed to construct an L-lactate overproducer in O. polymorpha. However, severe growth delay can be observed due to the strong competition for pyruvate between L-lactate synthesis and native metabolism. Consistent with a previous study in O. polymorpha60, ALE fails to synergistically promote cell growth and L-lactate production for an unknown reason. In our previous study, we found that mutation of IZH3 and LPL1 was responsible for the growth recovery of the fatty acid-producing strain, which cannot grow in methanol12. LPL1 possesses the activity of phospholipase/lysophospholipase for degrading various phospholipid substrates, and IZH3 is a membrane protein related to zinc metabolism. izh3Δ mutation was found to alleviate the DNA damage caused by antimicrobial peptide61, and it is speculated that the izh3Δ mutation may also attenuate the damaging effects of formaldehyde during L-LA production. In contrast, the disruption of LPL1 blocked phospholipid degradation, which may direct more carbon flux from pyruvate to acetyl-CoA to synthesize lipid; therefore, the flux from pyruvate to L-lactate was reduced.

Interestingly, we detected an abundant pool of cofactors in O. polymorpha during methanol metabolism, which was the key to alleviating the growth delay. Natural Ldh utilizes NADH as a cofactor to reduce pyruvate to lactate, which is completely opposite to the cellular levels of NADH/NAD+ and NADPH/NADP+ in O. polymorpha during methanol metabolism. In this case, the cofactor preference of PaLdh is modified for NADPH28,29, and L-lactate production from methanol is increased by 31%. The same strategy does not work in glucose owing to the similar cofactor ratios of NADH/NAD+ and NADPH/NADP+. Similarly, a cofactor engineering strategy successfully promotes lactate production from CO2 in cyanobacteria29, where a large pool of NADPH is formed during photosynthesis62. Therefore, cofactor balance and regeneration are crucial for improving bioproduction63,64.

Organelle engineering is an attractive strategy for increasing the productivity of yeast30,65,66. Mitochondria are the site where the TCA cycle occurs, containing abundant cofactors and metabolites, such as NADH, pyruvate, and acetyl-CoA. To fully exploit the precursor pyruvate and cofactor NADH, NADH-dependent PaLdh is further modified for mitochondria, increasing L-lactate production by 22%. The engineered O. polymorpha strain generated by using a combination of strategies produces 25.0 g/L L-lactate from CO2-derived methanol in fed-batch fermentation, with a yield of 0.22 g/g. Although the yield from methanol is lower than that from glucose67, the low cost of the feedstock and its independence from arable land may elucidate approaches for methanol/CO2 biotransformation for L-lactate production.

Although yeasts generally tolerate acidic conditions, we proved that O. polymorpha wild-type strain could tolerate up to 5 g/L L-lactate in minimal medium. In practical fed-batch fermentation where L-lactate is produced and secreted into the medium gradually, especially during the exponential and stationary phases, the tolerance may reach as high as 10-fold (50 g/L). Similarly, an E. coli strain tolerant to 15 g/L iso-butanol can produce about 20 g/L iso-butanol in a shake flask68.

The integration of TEA and LCA facilitated a comprehensive assessment of the CO2-to-L-lactate biomanufacturing process, revealing essential insights beyond basic experimental performance. Although the competitive potential is inadequate to that of established sugar-based and lignocellulosic processes (Supplementary Table 9), this study emphasizes the importance of third-generation biomanufacturing — particularly regarding reduced carbon source costs, optimized process utility, and carbon credit considerations — which collectively improve economic viability, resulting in an MSP lower than the market price of L-lactate. Furthermore, it is imperative to develop the TEA to identify key factors affecting return on investment, thereby guiding experiments, and mitigating investment risks35. This research demonstrated that production characteristics, including yield and productivity, are key cost determinants, providing explicit targets for metabolic engineering and process intensification to reduce MSP and enhance scalability. The LCA demonstrated that utilizing CO2-derived methanol as a carbon source facilitated a net GHG capture of 7.29 tones CO2 per ton of product, establishing this pathway as both carbon-negative and economically feasible. The TEA and LCA confirm the viability and sustainability of L-lactate production through CO2-derived methanol and provide strategic direction for the future advancement of carbon-based biomanufacturing.

In conclusion, we extensively engineered O. polymorpha at multiple levels of enzyme, cofactor, organelle, and cell variability for the efficient production of the biodegradable plastic monomer L-lactate from methanol. We envision that these engineering strategies and platform strains can be applied to promote the efficiency of methanol biotransformation for the production of other bulk chemicals, especially organic acids.

Methods

Plasmids, strains, and cultivation conditions

All codon-optimized genes and the primers used in this study are listed in Supplemental Data 1 and 2 in Source Data, respectively. All plasmids and strains used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 10 and Supplemental Data 3 in Source Data, respectively, and the strain construction process is described in Supplementary Fig. 17. The seed culture of the yeast strain was cultivated in YPD medium (10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L peptone, and 20 g/L D-glucose) at 37 °C. After transformation, the recombinant strains were placed in synthetic droplet medium (6.7 g/L yeast nitrogen base and 20 g/L glucose). The gRNA plasmid containing the URA3 selection marker was removed after two rounds of transfer to YPD medium. All the solid media were supplemented with 20 g/L agar. Escherichia coli DH5α was used for plasmid transformation and cultured in Luria–Bertani medium (10 g/L peptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, and 10 g/L NaCl) supplemented with 100 mg/L ampicillin at 37 °C.

Genetic manipulation

All engineered O. polymorpha strains were constructed via the CRISPR‒Cas9 system49. Gene deletion and integration were performed according to a standard procedure: gRNA design, plasmid and donor DNA construction, transformation, colony verification, and plasmid removal13,49. Gene integration was conducted at neutral sites in the genome, as previously described22. Donor DNA, consisting of upstream homologous arm (HA), promoter, target gene, terminator, and downstream HA, was generated by using overlap extension PCR (OE-PCR). Taking NS3-PAOX-LDH as an example, 1000 bp NS3 upstream HA, 1000 bp AOX promoter, 341 bp AMO terminator, 1000 bp NS3 downstream HA, and 972 bp PaLDH gene were PCR amplified from O. polymorpha genomic DNA and PaLDH synthetic plasmid, respectively. The donor DNA generated by OE-PCR was gel-purified and transformed with the NS3gRNA plasmid into O. polymorpha competent cells and selected on Synthetic Droplet (SD) plates. 10 clones were selected and verified by colony PCR. After PCR verification, the correct clones were cultured overnight in YPD medium and stored at −80 °C in 20% (v/v) glycerol, followed by the next round of genetic engineering.

Batch fermentation and fed-batch fermentation

Shake flask fermentation was performed at 37 °C and 220 r/min for 96 h in Delft medium (2.5 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 14.4 g/L KH2PO4, 0.5 g/L MgSO4·7H2O, 2 mL/L trace metal, and 1 mL/L vitamin solution69) containing 10 g/L methanol. The pH was adjusted to 5.6 by using 4 mol/L KOH, and 60 mg/L uracil was added to the medium if needed. But the pH was not further adjusted during cultivation in shake flasks. After fermentation, the broth was sampled to determine the OD600 and L-lactate titer by using spectrometry and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), respectively.

For fed-batch fermentation with methanol as the sole carbon source, single colonies of the engineered strain were cultivated in YPD medium for approximately 24 h and then transferred into minimal medium containing 20 g/L glucose with an initial OD600 of 0.5 for approximately 10 h. The seed cultures were inoculated into 1 L bioreactors (DasGip Parallel Bioreactor System, Eppendorf, Germany) with a working volume of 0.4 L. The pH, temperature, and dissolved oxygen (DO) were set as 5.6, 37 °C, and 30%, respectively. The pH was controlled at a constant 5.6 with the feeding 4 M KOH to neutralize the produced L-lactate. Initial agitation was set to 400 rpm, and increased to a maximum 800 rpm, depending on the DO level. Aeration was initially provided at 18 sL/h and increased to maximally 48 sL/h depending on the DO level. The initial batch fermentation was carried out in Delft medium containing 10 g/L methanol (Delft-M10). The control system was the same as previously described12, such as every 12 h, samples were collected to measure OD600, methanol, and L-lactate. Three biologically independent parallel experiments were conducted during the fermentation. The 10× Delft medium and CO2-derived methanol (593 g/L) were pumped into the vessels. The feeding rate was adjusted on the basis of the methanol consumption rate. During fermentation, the broth was sampled periodically to determine the OD600, residual methanol, and L-lactate produced by using spectrometry and HPLC.

Adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE)

ALE was carried out in Delft-M10 in a 100 mL shake flask. The whole process is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 8. Briefly, the parent strain HPLA06 was spotted on a YPD plate, and three independent colonies were cultivated in YPD medium at 37 °C and 220 r/min for 18 h. Strains were subsequently transferred to Delft-M10 medium at 37 °C and 220 r/min for 48 h, with an initial OD600 of 0.5. The evolved strains were transferred to the same medium with an initial OD600 of 0.5. After 8 transfers, the evolved strains with improved cell growth were subjected to L-lactate production from methanol.

Analytical methods

Cell growth was determined by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) via ultraviolet spectrophotometry. The fermentation sample was centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected and filtered at 0.22 μm for determination. The concentrations of methanol and L-lactate were analyzed via HPLC (Shimadzu LC-2030, Japan) with an Aminex HPX-87H column (300×7.8 mm; Bio-Rad, Hercules) equipped with an RID system. H2SO4 (5 mmol/L) was used as the mobile phase, with the column temperature and flow rate set to 50 °C and 0.5 mL/min, respectively. The results are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) of three biological replicates.

Mass spectrometry (MS) analysis of lactate was carried out via HPLC–MS (Agilent 6540 Q-TOF). The HPLC procedure was the same as that used for HPLC detection, and it was conducted for 30 min. The MS parameters were as follows: ESI + /ESI-drying gas flow rate: 8 L/min; gas temperature: 350 °C; nebulizer gas pressure: 35 psi; capillary voltage: 3500 V; fragmentor voltage: 75 V; skimmer voltage: 65 V; octopole voltage: 750 V; and mass range: 50–1000. The mass-to-charge (m/z) peak at 89.0258 was attributed to C3H6O3 (lactate) without H atoms. The total number of samples was 3, and the number of controls (wild-type strain without expression of LDH gene) was 1. The fermentation sample was centrifuged at 13,000 g for 3 min, and then filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. Then the sample was subjected to LC-MS detection. The software used in LC-MS was OpenLab ChemStation.

The identification of the optical isomer (D/L) of lactate was carried out via HPLC equipped with a UV detector by using a CHIRALPAK MA (+) column (DAICEL). CuSO4 (2 mM) was used as the mobile phase, and the column temperature and flow rate were set to 15 °C and 0.35 mL/min, respectively.

For cofactor ratio determination, the cells were collected at the mid-log phase (36 h for methanol cultivation and 18 h for glucose cultivation) and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) twice. The ratios of NADH/NAD+ and NADPH/NADP+ were measured according to our previous report12.

For promoter characterization, the eGFP fluorescence intensities of the engineered strains were detected as follows: 100 μL of cells cultured at different time points (2, 3, 4, and 5 d) were harvested and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 min. Then, the cell pellets were washed with 1 mL of H2O and diluted to OD600 = 0.2–0.8. The cell suspensions were subjected to a SPARK microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland) in a 96-well black-walled clear-bottom plate for fluorescence determination, with excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 nm and 528 nm, respectively. All the fluorescence intensities were normalized to the OD600 (absorbance at 600 nm).

Detection of the cell death ratio by using PI staining in flow cytometry was carried out according to our previous study12. Simply put, pre-cultured strains were transferred to YPM medium with an initial OD600 = 0.4. After 24 and 48 h cultivation at 37 °C, 220 r.p.m., cells were collected by centrifugation at 1000 g for 5 min, then washed twice with pre-cold PBS. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended in PI (5 μg/mL in PBS) for 10 min. Detection of PI fluorescence in each sample was analyzed on a flow cytometer (Sony) using FL-3 (488/620 nm). Then, 10,0000 cells were counted per sample to calculate the ratio of dead cells. Cells without PI staining were adopted to define the negative boundary. In the subsequent experiments, the same boundary and gating strategy was copied to calculate PI-positive cells.

Fluorescence microscopy analysis

To confirm mitochondrial targeting, MTS (MLSLRQSIRFFKPATRTLCSSRYLLQ) was fused to the N-terminus of the green fluorescent protein eGFP, and the samples were transformed into the yeast strain k–L. The engineered strain was incubated at 220 rpm at 30 °C in YPD medium for 24 h. A total of 1 mL of cell culture was sampled and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 min. Then, the cell pellets were suspended in Mito-Tracker Red Chloromethyl-X-rosamine (CMXRos) solution (Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos:PBS = 1:1000) preheated at 37 °C and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The incubated cells were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was resuspended in preheated PBS buffer at 37 °C. Afterward, 1 μL of cell culture was dropped onto microscope slides and covered with cover glass, and then, fluorescence targeting was observed with an EVOS M5000 microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

TEA and LCA analysis

Process simulation

A schematic depicting the generation of L-lactate from a CO2-derived methanol process is shown in Fig. 6a. This study entailed the design of an L-lactate production process using Aspen Plus v14 software, which was utilized to conduct calculations for mass and energy balance. The phase equilibrium and thermodynamic model has been constructed using the NRTL (non-random two-liquid), in accordance with a previous study70. The L-lactate manufacturing process consisted of five components: methanol production (A100), L-lactate production (A200), L-lactate purification (A300), wastewater treatment (A400), and utility (A500). The principal assumptions of Aspen Plus v14 are presented for M10 in a 100 mL shake flask. The assumptions of the whole process are illustrated in Supplementary Table 2. The detailed process simulation is described in the Supplementary Information.

Techno-economic analysis

TEA is a comprehensive methodology utilized to evaluate the feasibility of L-lactate production from CO2-derived methanol, considering both economic and technical perspectives. The estimation of CAPEX, OPEX, and MSP was conducted via an in-house model created by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL). Figure 6a shows the mass balance of the L-lactate production process simulated in Aspen Plus, with comprehensive mass and energy balance data available in Supplementary Data 4–7 in Source Data and Tables 11–13. The inside battery limit (ISBL) is typically utilized in TEA for fundamental considerations related to warehouse investments, site development, and supplementary piping. This study indicates that the ISBL encompasses the costs associated with equipment and installation for A100 to A300 (Fig. 6a). The fundamental assumptions and essential parameters for the process scale-up are presented in Supplementary Table 3 and are derived from reports and studies conducted by the NREL.

The biorefinery is projected to have an annual L-lactate production capacity of 18,500 tons, considering various influencing factors, including equipment size, raw material costs, and other related expenses. The costs associated with the equipment and raw materials utilized in the estimation of CAPEX and OPEX were sourced from published studies and official reports (Supplementary Table 13). The levelized cost of electricity for utility-scale photovoltaic solar power in the base scenario is $0.031 KWh−1, based on the latest data for high-efficiency regions in 202471. The costs associated with equipment varied according to the size of implementation and followed a power law relative to the original price72. The TEA functioned as an nth-plant model capable of estimating the investment required for the precommercial process72. The discounted cash flow method allowed for the determination of the MSP of L-lactate at the juncture where the NPV was zero and the IRR was 10%. All costs were adjusted to reflect 2024 US dollars to mitigate the impact of inflation on expenses34,72.

Life cycle assessment

The aim of the LCA was to quantify and analyze the environmental impacts of producing L-lactate from CO2. The system boundary was from cradle to gate (Fig. 6e). All stages, from raw material generation to L-lactate synthesis, were included inside the system boundaries. One kilogram of product mass was generally regarded as the functional unit in chemical LCA studies; hence, we utilized 1 kg of L-lactate as the functional unit to facilitate comparison with other research employing the same methods, thereby establishing a standard for both inputs and outputs. We investigated the L-lactate production route utilizing raw ingredients and energy inputs obtained from Aspen Plus. Supplementary Table 7 provides a comprehensive inventory of the life cycle associated with L-lactate production, derived from Aspen Plus. Moreover, we assessed the GWP (kg CO2-eq) of the L-lactate production process. The life cycle evaluation included background data from Ecoinvent v3.6 and references73, encompassing detailed categories and providers (Supplementary Data 8 in Source Data). The tool for reduction and assessment of chemicals and other environmental impacts (TRACI) version 2.1, created by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), was utilized to provide characterization elements for evaluating the possible consequences of inputs and emissions from production processes. The economic allocation method is chosen to assess the GWP of the by-product (biofertilizer)74. The cost of L-lactate is believed to correspond with the MSP of the basic scenario from this study, while Supplementary Table 13 encompasses the price of biofertilizer. All calculations and estimations are predicated on openLCA software version 2.2.
