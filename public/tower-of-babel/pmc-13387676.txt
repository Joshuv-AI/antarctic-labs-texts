Males’ perception and involvement in preconception care in Indonesian primary care in Bantul region, Yogyakarta
Authors: Rifyal S. Muhamad, Anis Widyasari, Diannisa I. E. Sangun, Fitriana M. Ekawati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Preconception care (PCC) aims to prevent morbidity and optimize the women’s health before pregnancy occurs. The success of the PCC would need a mutual involvement of both the women and their spouse. In Indonesia, males’ involvement in PCC is rare, and research on this subject is limited. This study aimed to explore men’s involvement and factors associated with men’s involvement in PCC in Bantul region, Yogyakarta.Methods:This study applied a cross-sectional survey from July to September 2024 at three primary health care centers in Bantul, involving 100 men aged >18 years of those bride-to-be or those planning for conception. The data were analyzed descriptively and sought for any correlation between their perception and involvement in PCC with the aid of SPSS.Results:The results revealed varying levels of males’ involvement in PCC, with 46% categorized as high, moderate (28%), and low (26%). Knowledge levels were a critical determinant, with men exhibiting higher knowledge showing greater involvement (P < 0.05). Positive attitudes and effective spousal communication were also correlated with higher levels of engagement in PCC. Men’s challenges toward the invovelvement includes, time constraints, financial limitations, and cultural barriers.Conclusion:Males’ involvement in PCC programs in Bantul is relatively high; their knowledge, attitudes, and spousal communication are pivotal factors influencing engagement. Further research is needed to address the involvement challenges including cultural norms, financial constraints, and limited awareness through targeted educational campaigns and accessible healthcare services.

Introduction

Preconception care (PCC) is a preventive healthcare strategy designed to optimize the health status of reproductive-age individuals prior to conception. This approach is critical for reducing morbidity and mortality among women and infants and enhancing pregnancy outcomes globally. Annually, over 200 million pregnancies are reported worldwide with more than half of which are unplanned, thereby intensifying the need for robust PCC interventions to mitigate associated health risks.[1] Internationally, PCC has been acknowledged since 1979 for its role in significantly diminishing adverse pregnancy outcomes. It encompasses strategic health interventions including risk assessment, health education, and the management of pre-existing health conditions. These interventions aim to prepare individuals physically and psychologically for pregnancy, improving long-term health outcomes for both mothers and their offspring.[2]

In Indonesia, particularly in Bantul, Yogyakarta, the implementation of PCC faces unique challenges. Despite the established benefits of PCC, there is a significant gap in the engagement of men in these programs. Data reveal that only a little precentage of Indonesian men participate actively in family planning decisions, which is suboptimal given their potential impact on health outcomes.[3] The role of men in PCC is crucial yet underrecognized. Active male involvement has been shown to enhance the effectiveness of PCC through shared responsibility in family planning and health optimization before conception. Research indicates that increased male engagement in PCC not only supports women to establish health decisions but also contributes to more balanced and informed choices regarding reproductive health.[4]

The crucial role of male involvement in PCC spans various dimensions including biological, behavioral, and socioeconomic impacts, significantly influencing health outcomes for both partners and their offspring. Biologically, men contribute half of the genetic material to the embryo. Issues such as poor sperm quality, genetic mutations, and lifestyle-related health problems can lead to adverse pregnancy outcomes such as miscarriages and congenital anomalies. By engaging in PCC, men can address these risk factors through genetic screenings, lifestyle modifications, and necessary medical interventions, optimizing the health of the offspring.[5] Behaviorally, men’s health behaviors have a profound impact on those of their partners; studies have shown that when men are involved in health-related educational programs, there is a concurrent improvement in the health behaviors of both partners. This synergy can lead to better management of preconception risks, such as reducing exposure to teratogens, which both partners may encounter in their environment.[6] On a broader scale, increased male participation in PCC fosters more balanced decision-making in family planning and health resource allocation, leading to equitable healthcare spending and more effective utilization of services.[7]

In Bantul, the urgency for enhanced PCC is underscored by alarming maternal and neonatal mortality rates. For instance, the maternal mortality rate in 2019 was reported at 145 per 100,000 live births, markedly higher than the national average. These statistics not only reflect the critical need for comprehensive PCC but also highlight the potential for significant improvements through structured interventions.[8] Given the high stakes of maternal and infant health, particularly in underserved regions like Bantul, it is essential to conduct extensive research to explore effective strategies for enhancing PCC, with a particular focus on increasing male involvement. This study aims to see men’s perception and involvement in preconceptive services in primary care in indonesia in Bantul District, Yogyakarta.

Materials and Methods

Research design

The research design for this study applied a cross-sectional observational method through a survey approach. The target population consists of males aged over 18 years who are either bride-to-be or planning for conception, identified in the waiting rooms of primary care clinics. Data collection took place in three Puskesmases in Bantul from May to August 2024. The data were analyzed descriptively to understand general trends and characteristics, and correlation analysis using SPSS-21 to explore potential relationships between the participants’ perceptions and their involvement in PCC.[9] This study has obtained ethical approval from the ethics committee with the ethical reference number KE/FK/0548/EC/2024.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis began with tests of validity and reliability to ensure that the research instrument accurately measures the intended constructs and was consistent over repeated trials. Content validity was confirmed through expert reviews, while construct validity was verified using Pearson correlation analysis in SPSS, with items deemed valid if their correlation coefficient surpasses the critical value and has a P value below 0.05. Reliability was assessed through Cronbach’s Alpha, with values above 0.7 considered acceptable, demonstrating strong internal consistency. For data analysis, descriptive analysis was used to summarize demographic characteristics, knowledge, attitudes, involvement, communication, and challenges related to male participation in preconception care, with results presented in tables and diagrams. Bivariate analysis using the Chi-square test was conducted to examine the relationships between categorical independent and dependent variables. A P value < 0.05 indicates a significant relationship. The strength of this relationship is further assessed using the odds ratio (OR). All analyses are performed using SPSS, a statistical software tool that supports robust data processing and analysis.[11]

Bivariate analysis

Bivariate analysis using the Chi-square method revealed significant relationships between various factors and male involvement in PCC. Table 4 shows that 27.0% of respondents with high male knowledge also had high involvement, with a P-value of 0.000, indicating a significant relationship between male knowledge and male involvement in PCC. In Table 5, 40.0% of respondents with positive attitudes toward PCC had high involvement, with a P-value of 0.000, confirming a significant relationship between male attitudes and male involvement. Table 6 shows that 40.0% of respondents with positive spousal communication had high male involvement, with a P value of 0.009, indicating a significant relationship between spousal communication and male involvement. Finally, Table 7 shows that 26.0% of respondents with positive perceptions of challenges had high involvement, with a P value of 0.019, suggesting a significant relationship between the challenges faced and male involvement in PCC. In conclusion, these results highlight that male knowledge, attitudes, spousal communication, and perceived challenges are significantly related to male involvement in PCC.

Discussion

This study revealed that males’ involvement in PCC in the Bantul region of Yogyakarta is relatively high. However, certain challenges persist, which may limit their participation. These findings bring attention to the influences of knowledge, attitudes, communication with partners, and challenges that men face in engaging with PCC. One of the primary findings of this study was that men’s knowledge significantly impacts their involvement in PCC. Men with higher awareness were more likely to attend health check-ups with their wives and engage in discussions about family planning. This aligns with the findings from studies conducted in Uganda and Nigeria, where higher levels of education and awareness were associated with greater involvement in reproductive health services. However, despite some awareness of PCC, many men in Bantul showed a limited understanding of specific aspects of PCC, such as HIV testing, which is critical for ensuring a healthy preconception period. This gap in knowledge is consistent with findings from other low- and middle-income countries, where men are often unaware of important preventive measures.[910] To address this gap, educational programs should not only introduce the concept of PCC but also provide detailed information about essential practices such as HIV testing, lifestyle changes, and nutrition. These programs must emphasize the importance of joint participation in PCC, highlighting that both partners need to take an active role in ensuring a healthy outcome for their future children. Moreover, programs should be designed to move beyond general awareness and focus on practical steps that men can take to support their partners’ health.

The study also found that men’s attitudes toward PCC played a significant role in their involvement in reproductive health activities. Men with positive attitudes were more likely to engage in health check-ups and discussions about family planning. This is consistent with findings from Uganda, Nigeria, and the United States, where supportive attitudes toward reproductive health led to greater male involvement.[91011] However, these studies also highlighted the persistence of traditional cultural norms in the study settings, where many men still perceive reproductive health as primarily a woman’s responsibility. This belief, rooted in patriarchal norms, limits male engagement in PCC and is supported by similar findings, who reported that men in traditional societies are often reluctant to participate in reproductive health.[12] To bridge this gap, educational interventions should focus on challenging traditional gender norms and promote the idea that reproductive health is a shared responsibility. Campaigns should stress the benefits of joint participation in PCC for the health of both partners and encourage men to view their involvement as crucial. Research has shown that open communication between couples significantly enhances male participation in reproductive health, suggesting that fostering discussions about family planning can help change negative attitudes.[13] These findings are consistent with a study in Bangladesh, who reported that men’s knowledge of PCC was generally low.[14] However, those with a better understanding were more likely to be actively involved in PCC. Moreover, a positive attitude toward PCC was significantly associated with higher levels of male engagement. This is supported by another study who found that men in China with a positive outlook on PCC were more likely to participate actively. Partner communication also emerged as a crucial factor as the majority of respondents in this study reported good communication with their partners regarding PCC.[15]

Our study also emphasized the importance of spousal communication in fostering male involvement in PCC. Men who had regular and open communication with their partners about health issues and family planning were significantly more likely to participate in PCC. This is consistent with previous studies, which demonstrated that effective communication between spouses can enhance male participation in reproductive health decisions. Conversely, limited communication was associated with lower male involvement, highlighting the impact of communication barriers on engagement.[13] To overcome communication barriers, interventions should promote open dialog between spouses. Educational programs that encourage couples to discuss family planning and reproductive health openly can help dismantle traditional gender norms and encourage men to take on more active roles. By emphasizing that reproductive health is a shared responsibility, such initiatives can increase male participation in PCC and improve health outcomes for both partners.[16] Adane (2024) further highlighted that effective communication between partners can substantially enhance male involvement in reproductive health.[17] Despite these positive associations, men still face several barriers to PCC engagement, such as economic constraints and cultural norms, as noted by Teshome (2020) in Ethiopia.[18] Overall, the results of this study align with previous research emphasizing the importance of improving knowledge, fostering positive attitudes, enhancing partner communication, and addressing challenges to strengthen male involvement in PCC.[19]

The study also identified several challenges that hindered male involvement in PCC, including time constraints, financial limitations, and cultural beliefs. Many men cited work commitments as a significant barrier to attending healthcare appointments with their wives. This finding is consistent with studies from Uganda, where men face similar challenges balancing work and healthcare responsibilities.[916] Additionally, financial constraints and limited access to healthcare services further limited male participation in PCC. These barriers are common in lower-income settings where men are often the primary breadwinners and face difficulties taking time off work for healthcare-related activities.[1620] The findings highlight the need for targeted interventions to reduce these barriers. One potential solution is to offer more flexible healthcare services, such as extended clinic hours or weekend appointments, to accommodate men’s work schedules. Providing financial support or subsidies for PCC-related healthcare services could also alleviate financial burdens and encourage men to participate more actively. Research has shown that when healthcare services are made more accessible and affordable, male involvement in PCC increases. Addressing these practical barriers is crucial to ensuring that men can fully engage in PCC.[4]

Strengths and limitations of this study

This study has several notable strengths. It is the first to explore male involvement in PCC in Indonesia, specifically in Bantul, Yogyakarta, addressing a significant gap in reproductive health research. By focusing on men’s roles, it provides valuable insights into their perceptions and engagement in PCC, emphasizing their importance in improving family health outcomes. The comprehensive survey approach captures critical variables such as knowledge, attitudes, spousal communication, and challenges, while the use of health centers as research locations ensures practical and actionable findings. However, the study has limitations. Its focus on Bantul District may limit the generalizability to other regions of Indonesia, where cultural and socioeconomic differences may influence male involvement in PCC. Potential selection bias is also a concern as participants were recruited in Puskesmas building, which may have been more health-conscious, leading to an overestimation of male engagement. Last, reliance on self-reported data may introduce social desirability bias, affecting the accuracy of responses.

Conclusion

The study revealed that male involvement in PCC in Bantul is influenced by a variety of factors, including knowledge, attitudes, communication with spouses, and challenges such as time and financial constraints. Further research is needed to improve men’s knowledge about PCC, challenging cultural norms, fostering open communication between spouses, and addressing practical challenges such as time and financial constraints. By addressing these barriers, we can promote greater male participation in PCC, ultimately leading to better health outcomes for both partners.
