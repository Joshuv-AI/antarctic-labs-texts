Unveiling the pathways of flavor formation in marinated lamb driven by ultrasound-assisted vacuum tumbling: An integrated multi-omics approach
Authors: Chenchen Xu, Shouwei Wang, Yan Zhao, Jiapeng Li, Shunliang Zhang, Jing Bai, Yuxuan Shi, Xianqi Yao, Anyu Gao, Bing Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Ultrasound-assisted vacuum tumbling, Marinated lamb, Flavor formation pathway, Metabolomics, Lipidomics

Introduction

China is the largest producer and consumer of lamb meat in the world, with a wide variety of lamb meat products available in the market [1]. Among these, marinated lamb products, such as marinated lamb slices and lamb kebabs, have gained popularity among modern consumers due to their distinctive flavor and convenience. However, traditional marination methods present several challenges, including low efficiency, inconsistent flavor, and unstable quality [2], which adversely affect consumer satisfaction and the economic profitability of lamb products. Therefore, enhancing the flavor and overall quality of marinated lamb through innovative and efficient processing technologies is essential for the meat industry.

Although the single vacuum tumbling (VT) technology has been widely used in the meat industry, it still has limitations in terms of marinating temperature, vacuum efficiency, and flavor enhancement, such as slow diffusion rate, insufficient marinade penetration, and unstable flavor formation [3]. To address these issues, several non-thermal processing technologies have rapidly emerged in the meat marinating process, including ultrasonic treatment [4], pulsed electric field [5], and high-pressure processing [6]. These approaches offer notable advantages in enhancing marinating efficiency and reducing salt consumption. Ultrasound-assisted marinating has received considerable attention due to its cavitation-induced acceleration of mass transfer, which enhances both the efficiency and effectiveness of the marinating process. Previous studies have shown that, compared to single tumbling treatment, ultrasound-assisted tumbling significantly improved the yield and product quality of beef [7], enhanced the salt content in meat [8], and promoted protein oxidation [3]. Consequently, the combined application of ultrasonic technology with other techniques has become a prominent research hotspot in current studies.

The flavor of meat is a critical factor in determining consumer acceptance and serves as a key indicator of the overall quality of meat products [9]. The formation of flavor compounds in processed meat is a complex process that involves lipid oxidation, the Maillard reaction, and Strecker degradation. Previous studies have shown that ultrasound-assisted vacuum tumbling (UVT) can enhance the overall flavor characteristics of beef, which is closely linked to lipid oxidation [10]. However, the generation of key flavor compounds in marinated lamb is influenced by the combined effects of the lamb's unique endogenous flavor precursors, exogenous marinade components, and processing technology, and its regulatory mechanisms remain unclear. Although the potential of UVT for meat flavor enhancement has been recognized, reliance on any single analytical technique, such as electronic nose (E-nose), gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), or gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS), often restricts the variety and quantity of detectable metabolites [11]. Therefore, to fully capture the subtle but noticeable changes in flavor compounds, it is essential to integrate advanced multi-omics techniques, providing a more comprehensive understanding of how new food processing technologies influence dynamic flavor changes [12].

Metabolomics, lipidomics, and flavoromics, as rapidly emerging technologies in recent years, offer a powerful and systematic approach for elucidating the complex biochemical pathways underlying flavor formation through their integrated application. Indeed, recent studies have successfully applied these multi-omics strategies to various meats. For instance, Zhou et al. [13] employed an E-nose, GC-MS, and lipidomics to identify the key lipid molecules responsible for the different aroma profiles of beef from two breeds. Similarly, Zhang et al. [14] used lipidomics and metabolomics techniques to demonstrate that the oxidation of glycerophospholipids is a key factor in developing the unique aroma of cured sea bass. However, there is a lack of research on the effects of UVT on the overall flavor formation and dynamic chemical changes of marinated lamb.

Accordingly, the primary objective of this study was to elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which UVT enhances the flavor profile of marinated lamb. To achieve this, an integrative multi-omics strategy combining flavoromics (E-nose and GC-IMS), lipidomics, and metabolomics was employed to systematically identify key flavor compounds and their precursors among the differential lipids and metabolites. Furthermore, correlation analysis was utilized to map the critical biochemical pathways driving this flavor evolution. The findings will offer innovative insights for optimizing meat processing technologies and serve as a valuable reference for developing high-quality, flavor-rich lamb products that align with consumer preferences.

Materials and methods

Materials

The M. longissimus lumborum muscles were obtained from six Small-tailed Han wethers left carcass (8 months old, with an average weight of 53 kg) at 48 h post-mortem from Beijing Musenweiye Halal Food Co., ltd (Beijing, China). All samples were transported to the laboratory within 30 min in a refrigerated container. Sodium chloride, sucrose, monosodium glutamate, sodium tripolyphosphate, and sodium D-isoascorbate were supplied by Beijing Jinmeitian Technology Development Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All other chemicals were of analytical grade from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Sample preparation and marinating treatment

The marinade was formulated to contain the following ingredients (w/w): 1.2 % sodium chloride, 0.8 % sucrose, 0.4 % monosodium glutamate, 0.5 % sodium tripolyphosphate, 0.15 % sodium D-isoascorbate, and 20 % water. The prepared marinade was pre-chilled at 4 °C before use. After the removal of visible connective tissue (fascia) and external fat, each loin (n = 6) was cut perpendicular to the fiber orientation into four blocks (each 6 cm × 6 cm × 3 cm; 105 ± 5 g). The four blocks from each animal were randomly assigned to four experimental groups (two tumbling treatments: UVT and VT; two refrigeration times: 1 d and 7 d), with six replicates per final experimental group.

Both tumbling treatments were performed using a custom-designed apparatus developed in our laboratory (Fig. 1), which incorporated electronic control, temperature control, ultrasonic, vacuum, drive, tumbling, and automated loading systems. The meat sample and pre-cooled marinade were placed together in the drum, with the internal temperature maintained at 4 °C. An intermittent tumbling protocol was applied for a total of 1 h, consisting of repeating cycles of 20 min of operation followed by 10 min of rest. The parameters for the UVT group were established based on previous studies [15] and preliminary experiments, and were set as follows: ultrasonic power, 350 W; ultrasonic frequency, 40 kHz; vacuum pressure, −0.09 MPa; drum inclination angle, 75°; and a rotational speed of 10 rpm. The VT group was treated using the same apparatus and parameters, but with the ultrasonic function deactivated. After the tumbling treatment, the meat samples, along with the marinade from the drum, were transferred to individual sealed containers. The samples were then allowed to marinate statically at 4 °C for 1 day (UVT1 and VT1) and 7 days (UVT7 and VT7) to allow for marinade equilibration.

E-nose analysis

The odor profiles of the marinated lamb samples were analyzed using a PEN3 portable E-nose (Airsense Analytics Inc., Schwerin, Germany) equipped with ten sensor probes. The method was adapted from Guan et al. [16] with slight modifications. Briefly, 1 g of minced sample was sealed in a 10 mL vial and incubated at 50 °C for 10 min. The E-nose parameters were set as follows: a carrier gas flow rate of 300 mL/min, a detection time of 90 s, and a self-cleaning time of 180 s. The stable sensor response at 70 s was selected for subsequent analysis, as preliminary experiments confirmed that the signals had reached a stable plateau by this time point. Each sample was measured in triplicate. The sensitivity characteristics of each sensor to different volatile substances are detailed in Supplementary material Table S1.

Headspace GC-IMS analysis of volatile compounds

The volatile organic compounds (VOCs) were analyzed using a FlavourSpec® GC-IMS instrument (GAS Dortmund, Germany), adapting the method of Hua et al. [17]. Minced lamb (2 g) was placed in a 20 mL vial and incubated at 60 °C for 20 min with stirring (500 rpm). Following incubation, 500 μL of the equilibrated headspace was automatically injected into the gas chromatograph (GC) in splitless mode. The injector temperature was maintained at 85 °C. All samples were prepared and analyzed in triplicate.

Chromatographic separation was performed on an MXT-5 capillary column (15 m × 0.53 mm, 1.0 μm film thickness) maintained at a constant temperature of 60 °C. High-purity nitrogen (99.999 %) served as the carrier gas. The programmed flow rate was as follows: 2 mL/min for 2 min, ramped to 10 mL/min over 8 min, then to 100 mL/min over 10 min, and held for 20 min (40 min total runtime). Following chromatographic separation, analytes were directed into the IMS ionization chamber, which featured a tritium (3H) source operating in positive ion mode. The drift tube was maintained at 45 °C, using high-purity nitrogen as the drift gas at a constant flow rate of 150 mL/min. VOCs were identified using VOCal software by comparing calculated retention indices (RI, using a C4–C9 n-ketone standard) and drift times to NIST 2020 and proprietary IMS databases.

Lipidomics analysis

For lipid extraction, approximately 50 mg of muscle was homogenized in a 2 mL tube with 280 μL of methanol–water (2:5, v/v). After adding 400 μL of methyl tert-butyl ether, the sample was ground (50 Hz, 6 min), sonicated in an ice bath (30 min), and left on ice (30 min). The mixture was centrifuged (13,000 g, 15 min, 4 °C), and the upper organic phase (350 μL) was collected and dried under nitrogen. The dried residue was then reconstituted in 100 μL of an isopropanol-acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) solution. After vortex mixing, low-temperature ultrasound extraction was performed for 5 min. Following a second centrifugation at 13,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was transferred to an injection vial equipped with an insert for analysis. A quality control (QC) sample was prepared by pooling 20 μL from each sample.

The lipidomic analysis of the samples was conducted using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system coupled with a Q-Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for high-resolution mass spectrometry. Chromatographic separation was achieved using an Accucore C30 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 2.6 μm; Thermo Scientific) at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of two solvents: phase A, a 50 % acetonitrile–water mixture (v/v), containing 0.1 % formic acid and 10 mmol/L ammonium acetate, and phase B, a mixture of acetonitrile, isopropanol, and water (10:88:2, v/v/v), supplemented with 0.02 % formic acid and 2 mmol/L ammonium acetate. The mobile phase gradient was delivered at a constant flow rate of 0.4 mL/min (5 μL injection) as follows: 0–4 min, a linear gradient from 35 % to 60 % B; 4–12 min, from 60 % to 85 % B; 12–15 min, from 85 % to 100 % B. The column was then washed isocratically with 100 % B for 2 min (15–17 min). Subsequently, the mobile phase composition was returned to the initial conditions of 35 % B over 1 min (17–18 min), and the column was re-equilibrated for 2 min (18–20 min) prior to the next injection.

The mass spectrometric (MS) analysis was performed using an electrospray ionization (ESI) source, with data acquired in both positive and negative ion modes. The instrument was operated over a mass range of m/z 200–2000. The ion source parameters were optimized as follows: the spray voltage was set to + 3 kV for positive mode and −3 kV for negative mode. The sheath gas and auxiliary gas flow rates were maintained at 60 psi and 20 psi, respectively, while the auxiliary gas heater temperature was set to 370 °C. For acquiring MS/MS spectra, a stepped normalized collision energy of 20 %, 40 %, and 60 % was applied.

Raw data were processed using Lipidsearch software (v. 4.0, Thermo Fisher, USA) for peak extraction, retention time alignment, and identification based on matching MS and MS/MS spectra with a metabolomics database. Lipid identification was performed with a mass error tolerance of less than 10 ppm, and lipid matches were validated based on MS/MS fragmentation patterns. Normalization of the data was conducted using the total peak area of each sample before multivariate analysis. Subsequently, data analysis was carried out using the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://cloud.Majorbio.com). Only lipidomic features present in at least 80 % of the samples were retained. Variables with a relative standard deviation (RSD) greater than 30 % in QC samples were excluded.

Non-targeted metabolomics analysis

Metabolomics analysis followed our previous method [18]. Briefly, meat samples (50 ± 5 mg) were homogenized with zirconium oxide beads in 400 μL of a precooled methanol–water (4:1, v/v) containing an L-2-chlorophenylalanine internal standard. The mixture was homogenized (50 Hz, 6 min, −10 °C), sonicated (40 kHz, 30 min, 5 °C), and stored at −20 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation (13,000 g, 15 min, 4 °C), the supernatant was collected. A QC sample was prepared by pooling 20 μL from each sample and was analyzed every 10 samples for stability.

The sample extracts were analyzed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system coupled with a quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (AB Sciex Triple TOF 6600, Framingham, MA, USA). The chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm, Waters, Milford, USA) maintained at 45 °C. The mobile phases consisted of solvent A (95 % water and 5 % acetonitrile, containing 0.1 % formic acid) and solvent B (47.5 % acetonitrile, 47.5 % isopropanol, and 5 % water, containing 0.1 % formic acid). The flow rate was set to 0.4 mL/min, with an injection volume of 10 μL. MS analysis was performed using an ESI in both positive and negative ion modes over a mass range of 50–1200 m/z. The ESI source parameters were optimized as follows: positive ion spray voltage, 5.5 kV; negative ion spray voltage, −4.5 kV; sheath gas, 50 psi; auxiliary gas, 50 psi; curtain Gas, 35 psi; source temperature, 500 °C; declustering potential, 80 V; normalized collision energy, 40 ± 20 eV. To ensure the stability of the entire detection process, a QC sample was analyzed after every 10 analytical samples. The method described by Xu et al. [18] was used to process and analyze the results of non-targeted metabolomics.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results are presented as mean ± standard error. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Multivariate statistical analyses were conducted to identify differential molecules between treatment groups. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using SIMCA-P software (version 14.1, Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden) to conduct an initial assessment of the overall distribution and clustering of the samples. Subsequently, the validity of the Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) model was evaluated through the R2 and Q2 parameters, with a Q2 value > 0.5 indicating good predictive ability. Significant features were defined as having a Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) score > 1.0 and a P-value less than 0.05, where the P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the False Discovery Rate. The relative abundance of differential lipid species and metabolites was visualized using heatmaps generated with the pheatmap package in R (version 4.3.3). Pathway analysis of the differential metabolites was performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca), and the results were mapped onto the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database.

To integrate sensory and omics data, an Orthogonal Projections to Latent Structures (O2PLS) model was built with E-nose/GC-IMS data as the X-matrix and differential lipids (DLs) and metabolites (DMs) as the Y-matrix. The top 20 variables from each dataset that contributed most significantly to the covariation between the matrices were selected based on the magnitude of their loading weights on the predictive component. Following this screening, a targeted Pearson correlation analysis was conducted on the key E-nose and GC-IMS variables identified. O2PLS modeling (o2plsda package) and Pearson correlation analysis were performed using R (version 4.3.3).

Results and discussion

E-nose analysis of marinated lamb

The radar plot displays the E-nose data of marinated lamb under different tumbling conditions during the refrigeration process, as shown in Fig. 2A. Among all sensors, the one with the most prominent response was W1W (sulfur compounds and terpene aromatics), followed by W2W (aromatic compounds and organosulfur substances). The results suggest that sulfur-containing compounds, terpenes, and aromatic substances played a dominant role in developing the characteristic flavor profile of the marinated lamb. Importantly, the responses of W1W and W2W in the UVT7 group were significantly higher than those in the VT7 group, suggesting that ultrasonic treatment promoted the release and accumulation of these compounds, thereby intensifying the flavor characteristics of the marinated lamb. This result is consistent with previous findings [19]. Furthermore, within each treatment group, the response values of key sensors such as W1W, W2W, and W1S, showed an increasing trend over time. Conversely, the responses from W5S (nitrogen/oxygen compounds), W2S (alcohols, aldehydes and ketones), and W3S (long-chain alkanes) showed a significant decrease. This observation was likely attributed to the oxidative degradation of proteins and lipids in the meat with the extension of storage time [20]. In addition, the responses of W1C, W3C, W6S, and W5C were all close to 1, reflecting their low sensitivity to the volatile substances present in the marinated lamb.

To visualize the global differences in the flavor profiles of the marinated lamb samples, PCA was performed on the electronic nose data, with the results presented in Fig. 2B. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) accounted for 72.65 % and 11.2 % of the total variance, respectively. Their cumulative contribution of 83.85 % confirms that the PCA model effectively captured the majority of the data's variance and could reliably represent the overall flavor characteristics of the samples. Samples from day 1 and day 7 were distinctly segregated along the PC1 axis, which represented the largest source of variation. Furthermore, PC2 effectively distinguished samples based on the different processing methods. The clear and distinct clustering of the four sample groups provided evidence that the E-nose could successfully resolve flavor differences attributable to both the tumbling technique (UVT and VT) and refrigeration time.

Characterization of VOCs fingerprints in marinated lamb by GC-IMS

The evolution of VOCs in marinated lamb was thoroughly characterized using GC-IMS. The resulting two-dimensional topographic plots provided a visual fingerprint of the VOCs for each sample (Fig. 3A). In these plots, each signal corresponded to a specific VOC, identified by its retention time (Rt) on the y-axis and ion drift time (Dt) on the x-axis. A red vertical line at an x-axis value of 1.0 indicated the normalized reaction ion peak (RIP). The points of varying colors on both sides of the RIP peak represented the concentration of individual VOCs, with darker colors corresponding to higher concentrations. As shown in the figure, VOCs were predominantly concentrated to the right of the RIP peak, with significant differences in signal intensities observed among the four treatment groups, especially between the UVT and VT groups. Furthermore, within each treatment group, both the diversity and concentration of VOCs increased as storage time was extended. To elucidate the dynamic changes in the volatile profile, a differential analysis was conducted using the initial UVT1 sample as a reference baseline (Fig. 3B). The resulting comparison plot revealed that the day 7 samples (VT7 and UVT7) exhibited prominent red regions, signifying a marked increase in the concentration of numerous compounds relative to the reference. Conversely, the blue regions denoted compounds that underwent a decrease in concentration. Therefore, these findings provided compelling evidence that the volatile composition of marinated lamb was substantially modulated by both the UVT processing and the duration of storage. As presented in Fig. 3C, the identified VOCs were categorized into 7 distinct chemical classes. Aldehydes, alcohols and ketones were the predominant classes, constituting 30 %, 27 % and 16 % of the total volatile profile, suggesting they were the principal contributors to the overall aromatic characteristics of the sample.

As shown in Table 1, a total of 36 volatile compounds were identified, including 11 aldehydes, 10 alcohols, 6 ketones, 5 esters, 2 acids, 1 furan, and 1 olefin. These compounds were classified into monomers (M), dimers (D), and neutral substances. The fingerprint shown in Fig. 3D clearly illustrates the dynamic variations of VOCs across the four treatment groups. The intensity of the red color in the fingerprint spectrum indicates the relative concentration of the compounds, with deeper red hues reflecting higher concentrations. In the region delineated by the red rectangle, the UVT1 group of marinated lamb was enriched with a high concentration of volatile compounds, including alcohols (2-Ethyl-1-hexanol, 1-Octen-3-ol, Octan-2-ol, (E)-2-hexen-1-ol), straight-chain aldehydes (Nonanal, Heptanal, and Hexanal), and ketones (2-Heptanone, 3-Octanone). Among these, compounds such as Nonanal, Hexanal, and 1-Octen-3-ol are established markers of polyunsaturated fatty acid degradation, imparting the characteristic grassy, fatty, and mushroom-like aromas to the lamb [21], respectively. The presence of these compounds indicates that the initial effect of the UVT treatment is an acceleration of lipid oxidation and decomposition during the preliminary marination phase. This hypothesis is further substantiated by the lipidomics analysis presented below. With extended storage time, the UVT7 group developed a more complex and mature flavor profile (highlighted in the green rectangle). This profile was characterized by a diverse array of alcohols (Octanol, Isopentyl alcohol, 1-Heptanol, 1-Hexanol), aldehydes (Benzeneacetaldehyde, Octanal), and sulfur-containing compounds like Methional. Notably, the UVT7 group was also rich in flavor compounds derived from the Maillard reaction and esterification, including Ethyl isovalerate, Methyl 3-methylbutanoate, 3-Methylbutanoic acid, Cyclohexanone, and 2-Acetylfuran. Methional, a product of the Strecker degradation of methionine, contributed desirable savory and meaty notes to the marinated lamb [22]. 2-Acetylfuran, a classic Maillard reaction product, added sweet, caramel-like, and nutty dimensions [23]. Furthermore, the formation of esters such as Ethyl isovalerate introduced fruity notes, while 3-Methylbutanoic acid provided a sharp, cheese-like complexity. The convergence of these fruity, savory, and sweet aromatic characteristics in the UVT7 group suggests that the extended 7-day aging period permitted the progression of slower Maillard and Strecker reactions, which depend on the interaction between amino acids and reducing sugars. The findings of this study also demonstrated that UVT processing was superior to the conventional VT method in terms of flavor compound generation. This observation is consistent with the hypothesized effects of acoustic cavitation, which is reported to create micro-channels within the meat. This process can potentially increase its permeability, allowing marinade ingredients to penetrate more deeply and rapidly [24]. More importantly, this process induces the lysis of muscle cells, releasing a greater abundance of endogenous flavor precursors such as free amino acids, nucleotides, and lipids [25]. It is hypothesized that UVT accelerates and enhances these key flavor pathways by creating an ideal environment that facilitates the critical interaction between these exogenous and endogenous precursors.

Lipidomics analysis of marinated lamb

Lipid composition and multivariate statistical analysis

A comprehensive profile of lipid molecules in marinated lamb samples from UVT and VT treatment groups was identified. To visualize the overall lipidomic differences between the treatment groups, we performed PLS-DA analysis. As depicted in the PLS-DA score plot (Fig. 4A), clear separation was observed between the different sample groups, indicating that both treatment group and refrigeration time significantly influenced the lipid composition of the marinated lamb. The first two components accounted for 39.6 % of the cumulative variance (24.6 % for Component 1 and 15 % for Component 2). The QC samples clustered tightly in the PLS-DA plot, further validating the robustness and reproducibility of the analysis results.

A total of 544 DLs were identified based on the screening criteria (VIP > 1, P < 0.05), with details provided in Supplementary Table S2. As shown in Fig. 4, Fig. 4, the identified lipids were classified into five major superclasses based on their structural characteristics. The identified lipids included glycerophospholipids (GP, 59.19 %), glycerolipids (GL, 27.76 %), sphingolipids (SP, 8.46 %), fatty acyls (FA, 3.86 %), and sterol lipids (ST, 0.74 %). GP constituted the most abundant lipid class, which is understood to be the primary building blocks of cellular membranes. GL represented the second major fraction, functioning principally as energy storage molecules within the muscle tissue. The remaining classes, SP, FA, and ST, were present in smaller proportions. This distribution is consistent with previous studies [26], indicating that GP and GL are the major lipid classes in lamb meat.

These lipids were further classified into numerous subclasses, of which triglycerides (TG), phosphatidylcholine (PC), and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) were the most abundant and are considered to be the most critical factors affecting the volatile flavor compounds in meat [27]. Interestingly, the abundance of TG species far exceeded that of PC and PE, which are the principal components of cellular membranes. Further analysis of the fatty acid chain composition revealed that polyunsaturated fatty acyls (PUFA) were the predominant class, accounting for 32.4 % of the identified species, followed by saturated fatty acyls (SFA; 21.4 %), odd-numbered fatty acyls (ODD; 20.6 %), and monounsaturated fatty acyls (MUFA; 17.9 %) (Fig. 4D). The predominance of PUFA is particularly noteworthy, as these molecules are not only critical precursors for the generation of volatile flavor compounds but also possess beneficial effects on human health.

Identification of DLs

As depicted in Fig. 4E, the statistical analysis of DLs revealed a significant and time-dependent impact of the UVT treatment compared to the conventional VT group. On day 1 of refrigeration (UVT1 vs. VT1), 180 DLs were identified, with 104 species upregulated and 76 downregulated. The upregulated profile was principally driven by an increase in PC, diglycerides (DG), cardiolipins (CL), and their lyso-forms, including monolysocardiolipins (MLCL) and dilysocardiolipins (DLCL). The downregulated fraction was predominantly composed of key membrane structural lipids such as PE, phosphatidylinositols (PI), lysophosphatidylcholines (LPC), and ceramides (Cer) (Fig. 4F). The results indicated a downregulation of structural lipids (PE, PI) and a concurrent upregulation of lipolysis markers (DG) and mitochondrial membrane components (CL derivatives), suggesting that external force disrupted the integrity of both cellular and subcellular membranes. This trend was markedly amplified by day 7 (UVT7 vs. VT7), with the total number of DLs increasing to 254, comprising 138 upregulated and 116 downregulated species. The upregulated profile was dominated by the accumulation of DG, while the downregulated fraction reflected a substantial and widespread loss of major structural phospholipids, including PC, PE, and CL (Fig. 4G), reflecting the enhanced lipolysis as the membrane structure degrades.

To further interpret the lipidomics data, we performed a pathway enrichment analysis on the differential lipids using the KEGG database (Fig. 4H). The results showed that glycerophospholipid metabolism and sphingolipid metabolism were the most significantly affected pathways. Glycerophospholipids are the primary components of the muscle cell membrane and are essential for its structural integrity [28]. The enrichment of this pathway suggests that the physical energy from UVT treatment (cavitation and mechanical stress) may not only damage the muscle cell membrane but also alter cell signaling. The other three pathways (glycerolipid metabolism, ether lipid metabolism, and linoleic acid metabolism) were closely linked to flavor formation. Lipid oxidation and hydrolysis are the main sources of volatile aroma compounds in marinated meats. UVT treatment appeared to accelerate these metabolic processes, increasing the release of free fatty acids (FFA) and thereby providing more chemical precursors for a richer, more complex flavor profile [29].

The results of the lipidomic analysis were visualized with a hierarchical clustering heatmap, which identified DLs (VIP > 1, P < 0.05) across the four treatment groups (Fig. 4I). A distinct separation was observed between the ultrasound-treated (UVT1 and UVT7) and VT-treated (VT1 and VT7) samples. The study found significant changes in the abundance of several lipid subclasses, most in TG, PC, LPC, PS, and Cer. In the UVT group, several long-chain TGs were significantly downregulated, specifically including TG (16:0/16:0/18:1), TG (16:1/18:1/18:1), TG (16:0/18:1/18:1), and the medium-chain TG (8:0/8:0/10:0). As the primary form of stored energy in muscle, the significant downregulation of TGs suggests that UVT accelerated their degradation, thereby generating abundant FFA that serve as rich flavor precursors for the meat. Both PC and LPC are key lipid flavor precursors that can be oxidized to form aromatic compounds like aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols during processing and storage. Throughout the refrigerated storage, PC levels decreased while LPC levels increased, which is consistent with the hydrolysis of PC to LPC by endogenous phospholipase [30]. In the UVT group, the abundances of PC (16:1/16:1), PC (18:4/16:1), PC (15:0/18:2), PC (16:1/18:1) and LPC (16:1), LPC (16:0), LPC (17:0), LPC (18:3), LPC (18:1), and LPC (20:5) were lower compared to the VT group. This implies that the cavitation effect of ultrasound promotes the degradation of membrane phospholipids (PC/LPC) and lipid oxidation, potentially generating more volatile compounds that enhance meat aroma early in processing. Furthermore, the level of Cer (d18:1/16:0) was lowest in the UVT1 group, whereas PS (18:0/18:1) abundance was lowest in the VT7 group. As a key signaling molecule in apoptosis and sphingolipid metabolism, the observed decrease in Cer may have resulted from the direct disruption of cell membrane sphingomyelin by ultrasonic energy. In contrast, while UVT-induced membrane rupture would be expected to decrease levels of the key membrane phospholipid PS, its concentration unexpectedly increased. A plausible explanation is that the substantial release of lipid substrates from other sources, such as TG degradation, enabled the rate of PS synthesis to exceed that of its degradation. This hypothesis finds support in a prior study, which suggested that elevated PS levels in pork may result from interconversion among phospholipids and between TGs and phospholipids [31].

Metabolomics analysis of marinated lamb

Metabolomic profile of marinated lamb

A non-targeted metabolomics approach utilizing UHPLC-MS/MS was employed to profile the key metabolites in marinated lamb from the four treatment groups. A total of 1,033 metabolites were annotated, with 423 and 610 metabolites detected in the positive and negative ionization modes, respectively. Multivariate statistical analysis using PLS-DA was applied to discriminate between the sample groups (Fig. 5A). The PLS-DA score plot revealed clear segregation among the treatments, with the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explaining 54.9 % and 14.6 % of the total variation, respectively. The distinct separation between the UVT7 and VT7 groups, in particular, points to significant treatment-induced metabolic shifts. The stability and reliability of the analytical platform were confirmed by the tight clustering of the QC samples. The annotated metabolites were categorized into 11 chemical superclasses (Fig. 5B). The most abundant class was lipids and lipid-like molecules, comprising 50 % of the annotated compounds, followed by organic oxygen compounds (13 %), organic acids and derivatives (9 %), and organoheterocyclic compounds (9 %). Less abundant classes were nucleosides, nucleotides, and analogs (6 %) and benzenoids (2 %), while organic nitrogen compounds, alkaloids, homogeneous non-metal compounds, and organosulfur compounds each constituted 1 % of the identified metabolites. These findings indicated that UVT treatment most significantly altered lipids and lipid-like molecules, followed by organic oxygen compounds and organic acids and their derivatives.

Changes in key significantly DMs

To elucidate the metabolic differences between marinated lamb treated with UVT and VT, a screening based on VIP > 1 and a P-value < 0.05 identified 443 DMs (Supplementary Table S3). Volcano plot analysis was employed to visualize metabolic disparities between the treatment groups after 1 and 7 days of refrigerated storage. On day 1, the comparison (UVT1 vs. VT1) revealed 165 DMs, comprising 71 upregulated and 94 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 5C). By day 7, 210 DMs were identified between the UVT7 and VT7 groups, with 85 upregulated and 125 downregulated in the former (Fig. 5D). These results demonstrated that the application of UVT induced significant alterations to the metabolic profile of marinated lamb during refrigerated storage. As illustrated in Fig. 5E, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted on the identified DMs. The results revealed that glycerophospholipid metabolism, purine metabolism, and nucleotide metabolism were the most statistically significant pathways of enrichment, while linoleic acid metabolism exhibited the highest pathway impact value. These disparities reflected that the effects of UVT treatment on marinated lamb were channeled primarily through lipid (particularly glycerophospholipid and fatty acid) and nucleotide metabolic pathways, which were closely linked to the formation of the VOCs.

The top 20 DMs ranked by VIP score were visualized using a hierarchical clustering heatmap (Fig. 5F). Significant changes in abundance were observed across these key metabolite classes, most prominently in phospholipids, nucleotides, and amino acids. Lysophosphatidylcholines (LysoPCs), the initial products of PC hydrolysis by phospholipase A, was found at their lowest levels in the UVT7 group. This included numerous LysoPCs containing a range of saturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids (C14-C22). This finding was consistent with our results for differential lipids, further suggesting that the UVT treatment might have enhanced lysophospholipase activity. Such an enhancement would be consistent with the rapid turnover and consumption of LysoPC intermediates, and the accompanying substantial increase in FFAs, which are crucial precursors for lipid oxidation and the generation of volatile flavor compounds [32]. This hypothesis was further supported by the increased levels of 2-lysophosphatidylcholine, a key lipid intermediate, confirming that UVT treatment accelerates the degradation of membrane phospholipids. Interestingly, although the lipidomic analysis revealed a general downward trend for PE in the UVT7 group, the abundances of Pe (20:1(11Z)/18:0) and Pe (18:0/18:0) were significantly increased. This discrepancy may be attributable to the fact that these specific PE species are characteristic constituents of mitochondrial membranes [33]. Their increased abundance likely indicates that UVT compromised mitochondrial integrity, leading to their release from the organelle. The levels of key umami-contributing nucleotides—Guanosine Monophosphate (GMP) and Inosine 5′-Monophosphate (IMP), along with their precursor Guanosine and the flavor-enhancing amino acid L-glutamine, were significantly decreased in the UVT group. This change reflected the enzymatic degradation of major flavor compounds that occurs during meat aging and cold storage. Furthermore, the increase in Adenosine Diphosphate (ADP) indicated a shift in purine metabolism, possibly resulting from enhanced degradation of purine nucleotides. Therefore, UVT treatment alters the composition of the precursors that contribute to the flavor formation of meat by accelerating the degradation of phospholipids and key flavor-related nucleotides.

Proposed formation pathways of key flavor compounds in marinated lamb

To establish the relationship between precursors and VOCs, an O2PLS model was constructed. The loading plots for the first two components (Fig. 6A) revealed the variables that contributed most significantly to the model. The left plot displayed the loadings for variables in the X-dataset (GC-IMS/E-nose data), and the right plot for the Y-block (DLs and DMs). The joint loading plot integrated the X and Y datasets, as shown in Fig. 6B. From this analysis, the top 20 loading elements with the highest correlations were identified. In the first quadrant, a significant positive correlation was observed between Cer, LysoPC, Ps, Cdp-Dg and the fruity-smelling compounds 2-Heptanone and Isoamyl acetate. A distinct cluster in the upper-left (second) quadrant indicated a strong positive correlation between TG, PC, IMP, GMP, and ketone flavor compounds (2-Butanone). It was also worthy of note that in the fourth quadrant, LPC, Pe, and ADP exhibited a significant positive correlation with the flavor compounds 2-Methyl-2-propanol, Ethyl Acetate, Methional, 2-Acetylfuran, 1-Heptanol, and Octanal.

Pearson correlations between the sensor responses and the GC-IMS-identified compounds among the top 20 flavor features were analyzed (Fig. 6C). The responses of sensors W2S and W3S showed an extremely significant positive correlation (P < 0.001) with 2-Butanone and 2-Heptanone, alongside a strong negative correlation (P < 0.05) with Isoamyl acetate, 2-Methyl-2-propanol, Ethyl Acetate, and Methional. Conversely, the W2W sensor response was significantly positively correlated with 2-Methyl-2-propanol, Ethyl Acetate, Isoamyl acetate, and Methional.

Based on the integrated multi-omics data, a hypothesized pathway for flavor formation in UVT-marinated lamb was proposed (Fig. 6D), which illustrated the inferred relationships among upstream metabolites (flavor precursors and intermediates), VOCs, and downstream perceptual features (E-nose). Initially, the UVT treatment disrupted cell membrane integrity, leading to the substantial release and accumulation of endogenous flavor precursors. These precursors and their intermediates were primarily involved in several key metabolic routes, including the nucleotide metabolism pathway (IMP, GMP, ADP), the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway (Pe, LysoPC, Ps, Cdp-Dg, PC, LPC), and the glycerolipid metabolism pathway (TG). Subsequently, these precursors underwent a series of crucial chemical reactions. The results from the first quadrant of the O2PLS model indicated that UVT accelerated the degradation of membrane phospholipids and their subsequent oxidation, which were critical driving factors for the formation of specific aromatic compounds. The correlations observed in the second quadrant revealed that the degradation of storage lipids (TG) synergistically influenced the meat's flavor profile in conjunction with the breakdown of umami nucleotides (IMP, GMP). Further analysis demonstrated a significant positive correlation between LPC, Pe, ADP, and a diverse range of flavor compounds. This suggested that the UVT treatment promoted a cascade of chemical reactions, including lipid oxidation, Strecker degradation, and the Maillard reaction [34], which collectively generated aldehydes, alcohols, ketones, esters, and furans. These results were further validated by E-nose analysis. Therefore, further investigation into the specific precursor degradation pathways modulated by UVT could facilitate the precise control over the generation of key aroma compounds, thereby providing a theoretical basis for the targeted flavor engineering of marinated meat products.

While this study presents a comprehensive multi-omics-based approach for deciphering the flavor formation mechanism in UVT-marinated lamb, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the flavor evaluation was limited to instrumental detection (E-nose) and lacked validation by a sensory panel, which is essential for linking chemical changes to actual human perception. Second, although the O2PLS model is powerful for identifying correlations, it cannot establish causal relationships between precursors and VOCs. Addressing these limitations in future research will help improve the understanding and control of flavor formation in marinated meat products.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study employed an integrated multi-omics approach to elucidate the putative mechanisms underlying the UVT-driven flavor enhancement of marinated lamb. Lipidomics and metabolomics analyses revealed that UVT disrupts the integrity of cellular membranes, leading to an accelerated degradation of lipids and the release of key flavor precursors, including the umami-contributing nucleotides GMP and IMP, and the amino acid L-glutamine. The study demonstrated a significant association between the rapid liberation of these endogenous precursors and the generation of aldehydes, alcohols, esters, and other VOCs, contributing to a richer and more complex flavor profile that develops during storage. These results provide novel molecular insights into meat flavor development and establish a theoretical basis for the strategic application of ultrasound technology in the meat processing industry. Future research should aim to systematically elucidate the quantitative relationship between UVT process parameters and the generation of characteristic aroma compounds, thereby establishing a scientific basis for the targeted modulation and optimization of flavor profiles.
