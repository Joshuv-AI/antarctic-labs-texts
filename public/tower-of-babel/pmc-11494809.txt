Association between exposure to greenspace and ECG abnormalities in China: a nationwide longitudinal study
Authors: Zhaoyang Pan, Man Cao, Xueyan Han, Jian Guo, Dengmin Huang, Wei Sun, Jiarun Mi, Yuanli Liu, Tao Xue, Tianjia Guan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGreenspace is an important modifiable environmental factor that is associated with health and well-being. Although previous research has shown that exposure to greenspace is beneficial for cardiovascular diseases, studies have rarely focused on early subclinical health outcomes such as electrocardiogram (ECG) abnormality.MethodsWe developed a longitudinal study using data from the China National Stroke Screening Survey (2013–2019). Monthly exposure to greenspace was assessed by the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) values. ECG abnormality was diagnosed by physicians based on the results of the 12-lead electrocardiogram. We used fixed-effects logistic regression with participant-specific intercepts to estimate the association between exposure to greenspace and ECG abnormalities.ResultsA total of 132,108 visits from 61,029 participants with ≥ 2 ECG measurements were included. At baseline, the lag-1 month average NDVI value was 0.29 (SD = 0.15). In the fully adjusted model, per 0.1-unit increment in lag-1 month average NDVI value was associated with a 3.0% (95% CI -1.6 ∼ 7.4%) decreased risk of ECG abnormality. In urban areas, the decreased risk associated with per 0.1-unit increment in NDVI was 14.81% (95% CI 9.38 ∼ 19.92%).ConclusionOur study found that higher exposure to greenspace was associated with a decreased risk of ECG abnormality, especially in urban areas. Our findings suggested that monthly exposure to greenspace might be beneficial to cardiovascular health, and greenspace provision and maintenance could be considered an important public health intervention in urban planning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20352-9.Keywords: Greenspace, ECG abnormality, Longitudinal study

Introduction

The electrocardiogram (ECG) is a noninvasive tool that records the electrophysiological activity of the heart through the body wall to assess cardiac function [1], which is important for the diagnosis of cardiovascular diseases such as atrial fibrillation [2] and myocardial infarction [3]. ECG tests are not recommended in healthy individuals because false-positive results could lead to unnecessary invasive procedures, overtreatment, and economic burden [4, 5]. However, several previous studies have reported the high prevalence (more than 50%) of abnormal ECG findings among participants without cardiovascular diseases [6]. Moreover, several cohort studies have demonstrated that people with ECG abnormalities were more susceptible to developing cardiovascular diseases and death [7, 8]. Therefore, more attention should be paid to ECG abnormalities.

Previous studies have demonstrated that exposure to environmental factors (fine particulate matter, noise, etc.) was associated with the risk of ECG abnormalities [9, 10]. However, the association between greenspace and ECG abnormalities has rarely been studied. Greenspace is characterized by the presence of trees, grasses, shrubs, and other types of plants, and it has increasingly been recognized as an influential and modifiable environmental factor that significantly contributes to health and well-being. Studying the health effects of exposure to greenspace is important for the designation of health-promoting public-health interventions. Previous research has revealed that exposure to greenspace is associated with lower risk of developing stroke [11], coronary heart diseases [12] and mortality [13]. However, studies have rarely focused on early subclinical health outcomes such as electrocardiogram (ECG) abnormalities.

The mechanisms regarding how greenspace is associated with health also warrant further study. On the one hand, greenspace exposure may have a positive impact on health by promoting physical activity and benefiting mental health, as well as reducing exposure to air pollution, heat and noise [14–16]. On the other hand, exposure to greenspace might increase the risk of accidental injuries and exposure to exogenous toxic or allergenic substances such as pollen [17]. It has been found that even hourly exposure to greenspace could enhance relaxation and restoration, which can provide benefit to cardiac autonomic nervous system [18]; however, only a few experimental studies with small sample sizes have focused on the short-term effects of exposure to greenspace on cardiovascular physiology (heart rate variability), and the results were inconsistent [19–21]. More epidemiological evidence on the association between relatively short-term exposure to greenspace and ECG abnormalities is warranted.

In this study, based on a large population and nationwide survey (the China National Stroke Screening Survey, CNSSS) in China, we conducted a longitudinal study to evaluate the association between long-term greenspace exposure and the risk of ECG abnormalities.

Methods

Study design and population

Our study population was obtained from the CNSSS program database between 2013 and 2019, which is an ongoing community-based program among residents aged ≥ 40 years in all 31 provinces in the Chinese mainland. More details about the CNSSS program can be found elsewhere [22, 23]. In short, CNSSS used a two-stage stratified cluster sampling among community residents over 40 years old. Face-to-face interviews were conducted using structured questionnaires for the participants by neurologists or physicians [24]. The subjects also underwent relevant physical examinations and laboratory tests, including ECG examination. Strict quality control standards were implemented for the program [22]. The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) participants were at least 40 years old; and (2) participants had two or more ECG measurements in the multiple visits. Finally, a total of 132,108 visits from 61,029 participants were included in the panel study with two or more measurements of ECG for each individual.

Greenspace exposure measurement

The normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) was used to assess the level of greenspace exposure where the participants resided. The NDVI is a normalized transformation of the near-infrared radiation (NIR) to red reflectance ratio, ρNIR/ρred, designed to standardize VI values to between − 1 and + 1. The NDVI values usually ranged from − 0.2 to 1. The greater the positive value is, the denser the vegetation distribution. We obtained the monthly averaged NDVI values from the moderate resolution imaging spectroradiometer (MODIS) vegetation indices product (MOD13A3, version 6) from NASA’s Terra satellite, which was a gridded Level 3 product in the sinusoidal projection with a spatial resolution of 1 km × 1 km [25]. Due to privacy protection, address information is only collected accurately at the community level. Therefore, to ensure that the greenspace exposure can cover the participants’ addresses, the original 1 km resolution datasets were upscaled to a coarser resolution of 10 km, the details were shown in supplementary materials. Gridded data of greenspace exposure were spatially matched with addresses of subjects geocoded as latitude and longitude.

Covariates

Based on the information collected from the questionnaire and physical examinations, the covariates considered in this longitudinal study include general demographic characteristics (i.e. age, sex, BMI, residential area), lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, milk intake), and self-reported history of chronic diseases related to ECG abnormalities (such as atrial fibrillation, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia). BMI is calculated by dividing the weight (kg) by the square of height (m) and then divided into four categories (underweight, normal, overweight and obesity) according to three cut-off values of 18.5, 24 and 28 kg/m2. The residential areas were recoded according to seven physical geographic regions in China (central, north, east, south, northwest, southwest, and northeast). Smoking status was a categorical variable with the definition of “Yes” indicating current or former smoker. Alcohol consumption was a categorical variable with the definition of “Yes” indicating alcohol consumption ≥ 3 times/week and ≥ 100 mL/time. Physical inactivity is considered a binary variable in this study, with the definition of “Yes” indicating exercise less than three times per week and less than 30 min at a time. Milk consumption is defined as whether milk is consumed more than 200 ml per day and more than 5 days per week. History of four chronic diseases were defined as binary variables, with “Yes” indicating the presence of a specific chronic disease (such as atrial fibrillation, hypertension, diabetes, or dyslipidemia), and “No” indicating the absence of that particular condition.

Outcome

The study outcome was ECG abnormality, which was diagnosed by physicians based on the results of the 12-lead electrocardiogram. Study participants with atrial fibrillation, ischemic changes, left ventricular hypertrophy, other types of arrhythmias, and other ECG abnormalities findings during the ECG examination will be defined as ECG abnormalities.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of participants at baseline and greenspace exposure for all visits. The missing values of covariates were imputed using multiple imputations from R-package mice.

We evaluated the associations between greenspace exposure and ECG abnormalities using fixed-effects logistic regression models with participant-specific intercepts. The fixed-effect model has been used in many longitudinal studies because it relies solely on within-individual changes and could eliminate time-invariant confonders [26–28]. Since the fixed-effects model could well control for the constant individual-level confounders (both measured and unmeasured) across the study period, it was widely used in the estimation of effect in panel study design [29–32]. The estimated effect of greenspace exposure on ECG abnormalities was reported as effect estimates corresponding to per 0.1 increment and per IQR increment in NDVI values. In multivariable models, the adjusted covariates were: the interaction term between follow-up time and baseline age, season, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, milk consumption, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and atrial fibrillation, which were available for each visit. Based on the Statistical Method of Defining Urban and Rural Areas (Tongjishang huafen chengxiang de guiding) established by the National Bureau of Statistics of China in 2008, the participants were recoded to urban and rural groups according to their addresses [33]. Stratified analysis and non-linear models were used to explore the health effects of greenspace exposure in urban and rural populations. The non-linear exposure–response relationships between greenspace and ECG abnormalities were explored using thin plate regression splines with three degrees of freedom. To explore whether certain characteristics may modify the relationship between greenspace and ECG abnormalities, we conducted subgroup analyses and performed the Wald tests to assess heterogeneity between subgroups.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of our primary results. The inclusion of individuals with repeated measurements may lead to selection bias, so the inverse-probability weighting method was adopted in the regression analysis to ensure a balance between the included and the excluded population by giving each individual a weight inversely proportional to their probability of selection [34]. We also specified three models (from Model 1 to Model 3), each with more adjusting variables, to adjust for known and suspected risk factors. The Model 3 was considered the fully adjusted model. Greenspace exposure with a lag of one to three months was calculated, with the lag period with the most significant effect chosen for the main analysis. The lag-1 month represents the NDVI values of the current month for the ECG measurement of each participant.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.1.2 (R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria). The associations were estimated by point estimates with 95% CIs, and a two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Study population

In total, 61,029 participants (132,108 visits) from the CNSSS program had two or more ECG measurements between 2013 and 2019. The demographic characteristics of the subjects were shown by the quartile of the one-month average NDVI values around the place of residence (Table 1). At baseline, all participants were 40 years or older, with an average age of 60.23 (Standard deviation (SD) = 10.26, median = 60.45, interquartile range (IQR) = 15.41). A total of 57.6% of the participants were women. The mean one-month NDVI value was 0.29 (SD = 0.15, median = 0.27, IQR = 0.21). During the study period, a total of 27,120 abnormal ECG measurements were recorded (20.53%).

Association between greenspace exposure and ECG abnormalities

Figure 1 illustrated the estimated association between exposure to greenspace and ECG abnormalities in three models on different lag months (from lag-1 to lag-3 months). The estimate of exposure to greenspace with lag-1 month was the strongest and was chosen as the lag exposure window for the exposure-response relationship and subgroups analyses. The result was robust after adjusting for lifestyle factors and self-reported history of chronic diseases. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), every 0.1-unit increment in the lag-1 month NDVI value was associated with a 3.0% (95% CI -1.6 ∼ 7.4%) decreased risk of ECG abnormalities. The associations between per IQR increment of NDVI and ECG abnormalities were shown in supplementary materials Figure S1 (for the entire population), Figure S2 (urban and rural population) and Figure S3 (subgroup analysis).

The results of stratification analysis according to urban and rural distribution were shown in Fig. 2. In urban areas, every 0.1-unit increment in lag-1 month NDVI was associated with a 14.81% (95% CI 9.38 ∼ 19.92%) decrease in the risk of ECG abnormalities, while the association was not significant for rural population.

Exposure − response relationship

The exposure–response curves for the relationships between the NDVI and ECG abnormality were shown in Fig. 3. In the whole population, point estimates of the odds ratio of exposure to greenspace on ECG abnormalities decreased as NDVI increased, albeit not reaching statistical significance. Stratified results showed a significant increase in protective effect against ECG abnormalities with increased exposure to greenspace for urban population and an inverted ‘U-shaped’ curve for rural population.

Effect modification

To identify the populations that may benefit more from NDVI exposure, we investigated the potential effect modifications of several participants’ characteristics (Fig. 4). The results showed significantly negative associations in the northeast region of China, with an estimated odds ratio of 0.58 (95% CI 0.51 ∼ 0.65). The difference across regions was statistically significant (P < 0.001). We found that NDVI exposure had a lesser but nonsignificant protective effect on ECG abnormalities in people aged 65 years or older than in those younger than 65 years (P = 0.649). Women were more likely to benefit from exposure to greenspace (P = 0.225). People with a history of atrial fibrillation or hypertension were more vulnerable (P < 0.001), while dyslipidemia was a protective factor for ECG abnormalities.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first longitudinal study that investigated the association between exposure to greenspace and ECG abnormalities in China. Based on a large nationwide population, we conducted a longitudinal study and found a negative but nonsignificant association between greenspace exposure (NDVI) and the risk of ECG abnormalities. In the stratified analyses, we found a significant estimate for the association between increased NDVI exposure and reduced risk of ECG abnormalities in urban dwellers, compared to rural residents.

In our study, increased exposure to greenspace was associated with reduced risk of ECG abnormalities, which was consistent with previous research, although the overall association was non-significant. Despite the importance of exploring short-term (monthly) exposure to greenspace and its health impact, the existing evidence was inconclusive. Most experimental studies using between-subjects designs have suggested that exposure to greenspace can lower blood pressure and heart rate and increase heart rate variability [19–21, 35, 36], since it has been found that short-term exposure to greenspace could enhance relaxation and restoration to benefit cardiac autonomic nervous system [18, 37]. In this study, we used ECG abnormality—which can serve as an early indicator of the cardiovascular system [38, 39]—as the health outcome, and captured the potential effect of short-term greenspace exposure, which provided further evidence regarding the health effect of short-term exposure of greenspace.

Although the overall negative association was not significant, we still observed a gradual decrease in the risk of ECG abnormalities as the exposure to greenspace increased in the exposure‒response curve. Other studies have also reported nonlinear associations between greenspace and health outcomes [26, 40]. It has been demonstrated that exposure to greenspace can promote cardiovascular relaxation by boosting the parasympathetic nervous system and inhibiting the sympathetic nervous system [41, 42]. Admittedly, the protective effects of exposure to greenspace may also come from increased physical activity as well as reduced air pollution and environmental noise [43–45]. Previous studies also reported nonlinear associations between green space exposure and physical activity [40, 46], which may partly explain the nonlinear exposure-response relationship between green space exposure and health outcomes.

In the stratified analyses, we found larger estimates for negative associations between greenspace and ECG abnormalities in the urban population, while its estimated association in the rural population was still not significant. Min et al. also found that the protective effect was only significant in urban areas when studying the health effects of greenspace on the nervous system [47]. There were several potential explanations. First, urban residents might be more sensitive to greenspace exposure in residential environments because there is less greenspace in cities [48]. In our study, the lag-1 month NDVI value in urban areas is 0.3, which is approximately 75% of that in rural areas. Second, the role of greenspace is different for urban and rural residents. Urban dwellers tend to actively seek out greenspace as a way to unwind or engage in physical activity, while those in rural areas may be exposed to greenspace as part of their daily work or activities such as farming [47].

In rural areas, the exposure‒response curve between greenspace and ECG abnormalities was inverted U-shaped, which was different from the monotonically decreasing curve in the urban population. This may be due to differences in types and usage of greenspace between urban and rural areas. The type of vegetation can potentially modulate its health benefits, for example, it has been found that tree canopy and forests were more consistently associated with better cardiovascular and respiratory health than grassland [49–52]. Although there is more greenspace in rural areas, it might be more difficult to access. In contrast, greenspace in urban areas is more likely to be utilized [53, 54].

In subgroup analyses, we observed that the negative association of exposure to greenspace was slightly stronger in people aged 65 years and older, which was also reported in other studies [17]. Previous research has shown that exposure to greenspace can promote social communication, which is important for health and well-being, especially for the elderly [55, 56]. The benefits of greenspace exposure were stronger in women, which might be explained by the fact that men and women have different physiological responses to greenspace exposure [17, 57]. Notably, a positive association was observed in people with a history of atrial fibrillation, which warrants further investigation. We also found negative associations between greenspace exposure and ECG abnormality was stronger in people with a history of dyslipidemia or alcohol consumption. Due to the exploratory nature of these analyses, the results should be interpreted with caution. Further studies regarding the association between greenspace exposure and ECG outcomes in susceptible populations were warranted.

Given that ECG is an early sensitive indicator related to cardiovascular health and that short-term exposure to greenspace could produces benefits on cardiovascular system, we considered a monthly exposure time window in our study, which was also used in previous studies [58, 59].

Our study has several strengths. First, to our knowledge, this is the largest study on exposure to greenspace and ECG abnormalities. In our study, we used data with repeated measurements to develop a longitudinal study design that could control unmeasured individual confounders, allowing better exploration of the association between exposure and outcomes. Moreover, physician-diagnosed ECG abnormality could be an early indicator of cardiovascular diseases. Therefore, our findings may suggest a potential pathway linking greenspace exposure and cardiovascular health.

There were several limitations in our study. First, the selection bias introduced by including participants with two or more visits should be acknowledged. However, we used the method of inverse-probability weighting to reduce this bias as much as possible. Second, although using the NDVI values to estimate the exposure to greenspace may not be accurate, this method has been widely applied in previous studies [26]. Third, there might be some residual confounding factors that have not been taken into account (e.g., some people with ECG abnormalities may appear normal at the time of investigation due to medication use). Moreover, the history of diseases was self-reported, which may lead to information bias.

Conclusion

Our study found that higher exposure to greenspace was associated with a decreased risk of ECG abnormalities, especially in urban areas. Our findings suggested that even short-term exposure to greenspace may provide benefit to cardiovascular health, and greenspace provision and maintenance could be considered an important public health intervention in urban planning.
