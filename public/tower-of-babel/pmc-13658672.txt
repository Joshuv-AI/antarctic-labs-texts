Prevalence and Severity of Potential Drug–Drug Interactions in People Living with HIV on Antiretroviral Therapy in the United States
Authors: Sean P. Fleming, Kyung Min Lee, Girish Prajapati, Wenying Quan, Kyle Roney, Shweta Kamat, Milena Murray, Samira Saad, Viktor Chirikov, Princy N. Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAs people living with HIV (PLWH) age, the risk for polypharmacy and potential drug–drug interactions (pDDIs) will likely increase, which may pose clinical challenges and contribute to increased healthcare burden.MethodsThis retrospective study used administrative claims data (1/1/19–1/31/25) from Optum’s de-identified Clinformatics® Data Mart Database and included adults with ≥ 1 antiretroviral therapy (ART) claim from 1/1/24–12/31/24 (index date: earliest ART claim). The prevalence and severity of pDDIs between ART and each non-ART comedication the PLWH received during the 30-day post-index period were quantified at the ART agent and ART regimen levels. DDI severity was categorized using the University of Liverpool HIV Drug Interactions Resource scale: contraindicated, major, minor, or no interaction. Prevalence of pDDIs was reported as the proportion of PLWH with any pDDI by severity category.ResultsOverall, 22,412 PLWH were included; 44.2% had ≥ 1 pDDI classified as contraindicated, major, or minor. At the ART regimen level, contraindicated pDDIs were most prevalent for darunavir/cobicistat/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (14.4%) and elvitegravir/cobicistat/emtricitabine/tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (14.0%). Major pDDIs were observed in up to 66.4% of PLWH receiving certain boosted ART, whereas contraindicated pDDIs were uncommon (< 7%) among PLWH on non-boosted integrase inhibitor- and non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor-based regimens. Across commonly prescribed ART regimens, 61.5% to 69.7% of PLWH had either no comedications or only comedications classified as having no interaction, with lower proportions observed for boosted regimens, and major pDDIs were predominantly associated with antidiabetic medications and statins.ConclusionsIn this large, real-world claims analysis, pDDIs between ART and non-ART medications were not uncommon in routine clinical practice. As the HIV population continues to age, systematic screening for drug interactions (particularly with comedications for chronic conditions such as diabetes and cardiovascular disease) and thoughtful, individualized ART selection may help mitigate interaction risk and support safe, effective long-term HIV management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40121-026-01435-z.

Introduction

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has substantially increased life expectancy among people living with HIV (PLWH) [1, 2]. As this population ages, PLWH experience an increasing burden of aging-related comorbidities that often require the use of multiple concomitant medications. Consequently, polypharmacy has become increasingly common among PLWH and is an important consideration in long-term HIV management [3]. The increasing use of concomitant medications also raises the likelihood of potential drug–drug interactions (pDDI), particularly between antiretroviral agents and medications prescribed for chronic comorbid conditions [3–5]. Because pDDIs may complicate medication management and influence treatment selection, identifying and minimizing clinically relevant pDDIs is an important component of optimizing HIV care [5].

DDI may alter drug exposure by increasing or decreasing plasma concentration levels, potentially leading to adverse effects or reduced therapeutic efficacy that can impact disease or symptom management [6]. There is also a correlation between DDIs and increased healthcare utilization, including higher rates of hospital visits, hospital admissions, and healthcare expenses [7–11]. Among PLWH, polypharmacy and DDIs may further increase the risk of ART nonadherence, which can adversely affect virologic outcomes [6, 12, 13].

ART agents differ substantially in their potential for DDIs due to class-specific pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties. Some ART drugs act as inhibitors or inducers of key drug-metabolizing enzymes, particularly cytochrome P450 isoenzymes, while others affect drug transporters such as P-glycoprotein [5, 13, 14]. Pharmacokinetic boosting agents, including ritonavir and cobicistat, are intentionally used to inhibit metabolic pathways and increase ART exposure but may also increase the risk of DDIs with concomitant non-ART medications. In addition, DDIs may arise through non-metabolic mechanisms, including additive or antagonistic pharmacodynamic effects, overlapping toxicities, or altered medication absorption. For example, integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTI) can interact with polyvalent metallic cations via chelation, resulting in reduced gastrointestinal absorption and lower plasma concentrations.

Given these diverse and regimen-specific mechanisms, understanding the risk of pDDIs between ART regimens and non-ART medications is critical to informing treatment decision-making for PLWH. The objective of this study was to describe and quantify the prevalence and severity of pDDIs between ART and non-ART medications among PLWH receiving ART, evaluated by (1) individual ART agents and (2) complete ART regimens.

Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

This retrospective cohort study of pDDIs between ART and non-ART medications in adult PLWH on ART was conducted using Optum’s de-identified Clinformatics® Data Mart Database (January 1, 2019–January 31, 2025; Fig. S1). Optum® CDM is a geographically diverse, nationally representative database that comprises medical and pharmacy claims data (including linked enrollment) from 1993 to present on more than 77 million lives in all 50 US states. The database is fully de-identified and compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996. Given that the data used in this study were de-identified, the study did not meet the National Institutes of Health guidelines for clinical research, so institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required. The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments.

Sample Selection

The study population included adults (aged ≥ 18 years) with ≥ 1 medical or prescription claim for an ART medication filled between January 1, 2024, and December 31, 2024 (Fig. S1). Eligible individuals were required to have a complete index ART regimen consisting of ≥ 1 anchor agent (protease inhibitor [PI], non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor [NNRTI], INSTI, fusion inhibitor, CCR5 antagonist, or capsid inhibitor) and ≥ 1 other anchor agent or nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI). Index ART regimens were identified using a claims-based algorithm that defined the index date as the date of the first pharmacy claim for any ART agent. All ART prescriptions within the subsequent 14 days, including the initial ART claim, were considered components of the index regimen.

Individuals were required to have ≥ 1 inpatient medical claim or ≥ 2 outpatient medical claims with International Classification of Diseases, 10th Edition, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) diagnosis codes for HIV or AIDS up to 5 years prior to and including the index date or during the 30 days after the index date (Table S1). Only the claims with an HIV/AIDS diagnosis code occurring on a different date than those for an HIV antibody screening test (Table S2) were considered to minimize misclassification of rule-out or referral codes as HIV diagnosis. PLWH were required to have continuous health plan enrollment for ≥ 12 months pre-index (baseline period) and ≥ 1 month after the index date (follow-up period).

PLWH were excluded if they had ≥ 1 prescription claims for emtricitabine plus tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (FTC/TDF), emtricitabine plus tenofovir alafenamide (FTC/TAF), or cabotegravir and no other ART agents during the baseline or follow-up period, as these were assumed to represent pre-exposure prophylaxis. PLWH with ≥ 1 medical claim with an ICD-10-CM diagnosis code for post-exposure prophylaxis on or after the index date (Table S1) were also excluded. Investigational ART agents were not captured as study treatments and therefore were not included in the analysis. In addition, use of lamivudine or tenofovir (TDF or TAF) for hepatitis B virus infection alone did not satisfy the requirements for an HIV diagnosis and a complete ART regimen.

Study Measures

Demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical characteristics were assessed during the 12-month baseline period. Baseline comorbidities (Table S3) were identified based on the presence of ≥ 1 inpatient or ≥ 2 outpatient ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes during this period.

The primary study outcomes were the prevalence and severity of pDDIs between index ART and comedications. A comedication was defined as any non-ART medication with a days’ supply that overlapped with the index ART for ≥1 day within the first 30 days after the index ART, regardless of prescription sequence (i.e., the comedication could be prescribed before the index ART or during the 30-day post-index period; Fig. S2). Comedications were identified using pharmacy claims for filled prescriptions based on National Drug Codes; the pharmacy claim end date was calculated by adding days’ supply to its fill date.

All non-ART medications included in the University of Liverpool HIV Drug Interactions Resource (hereafter referred to as the Liverpool database) [15] were evaluated for pDDIs with the index ART. For elvitegravir- and darunavir-based regimens, which are typically co-administered with a pharmacokinetic booster (cobicistat or ritonavir), pDDIs reflected the combined interaction profiles of the ART and cobicistat or ritonavir as reported in the Liverpool database. For practical reporting purposes, analyses were focused on the 15 most commonly prescribed index ART regimens in the dataset, which accounted for approximately 90% of all index regimens; all other regimens were categorized as “Other regimens”.

The severity of a pDDI was categorized using the four-item Liverpool scale [15]: contraindicated (do not co-administer), major (potential interaction which may require a dose adjustment or close monitoring), minor (potential interaction of weak severity) or no interaction (no clinically significant interaction). The pDDI severity categories are not mutually exclusive as PLWH who received multiple comedications could have had multiple pDDIs. To minimize potential bias, given that some comedications captured in claims data may not be listed in the Liverpool database, the proportion of PLWH who had either no comedications or only comedication(s) categorized as having no interaction was also calculated; absence of a listed interaction was not assumed to indicate lack of interaction [15].

Statistical Analysis

All study variables were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and proportions, and continuous variables were described using means and standard deviations (SD). All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software version 9.4.

The prevalence of pDDIs was estimated as the number and proportion of PLWH with ≥ 1 pDDI among all PLWH receiving ART. Prevalence by DDI category (contraindicated, major, minor, and no interaction) was calculated as the number of PLWH with ≥ 1 prescription for a comedication classified within a given pDDI severity level divided by the total number of PLWH on a specific ART agent or regimen. As a sensitivity analysis, prevalence estimates for each pDDI severity category were also calculated among PLWH with ≥ 1 comedication overlapping with index ART. The subset of PLWH used for the sensitivity analysis consisted of 69% to 92% of PLWH exposed to the included ART regimens.

For each index ART regimen, the five most frequently observed comedication classes and individual comedications associated with contraindicated and major pDDIs were identified and reported among PLWH with ≥ 1 comedication.

Antiretroviral Therapy

Across index regimens, INSTIs were most commonly prescribed (78.9%), followed by NNRTI (17.1%), PI (9.7%), and other ART classes (16.1%; Table 1); 91.1% of regimens included ≥ 1 NRTI. The most prevalent ART anchor agent was bictegravir (n = 9929; 44.3%), followed by dolutegravir (n = 7024; 31.3%) and rilpivirine (both oral and injectable) (n = 2809; 12.5%). The three most frequent index regimens were bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (BIC/FTC/TAF; n = 9672; 43.2%), dolutegravir/lamivudine (DTG/3TC; n = 2311; 10.3%), and dolutegravir/abacavir/lamivudine (DTG/ABC/3TC; n = 1753; 7.8%).

Potential Drug–Drug Interactions

Individual ART Agents

Complete ART Regimens

Overall, 44.2% (n = 9912) of PLWH receiving a complete ART regimen had ≥ 1 pDDI classified as contraindicated, major, or minor (Table S5). The highest prevalence of contraindicated pDDI was observed for cobicistat-boosted darunavir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide (DRV/c/FTC/TAF; 14.4%) and cobicistat-boosted elvitegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (EVG/c/FTC/TDF; 14.0%; Fig. 2). Results were qualitatively unchanged in sensitivity analyses excluding PLWH without comedications.

Common Comedications Associated with Contraindicated and Major Potential DDIs

Among PLWH receiving BIC/FTC/TAF (N = 111), the most common comedication classes associated with contraindicated pDDIs were gastrointestinal agents (n = 38; 34.2%; e.g., sucralfate) and anticonvulsants (n = 31; 27.9%; e.g., primidone, oxcarbazepine) (Tables S6 and S7). Major pDDIs with BIC/FTC/TAF (N = 1236) were most frequently associated with antidiabetic agents (n = 704; 57.0%; e.g., metformin) and antifungal agents (n = 172; 13.9%; e.g., ketoconazole) (Tables S8 and S9). For boosted regimens (EVG/c/FTC/TAF and DRV/c/FTC/TAF), contraindicated pDDIs were primarily driven by corticosteroids and anticoagulant/antiplatelet agents. In other common ART regimens, major pDDIs were predominantly associated with antidiabetic medications (DTG/3TC: n = 142, 69.6%; DTG/ABC/3TC: n = 121, 54.3%) and lipid-lowering agents (EVG/c/FTC/TAF: n = 311, 19.3%; DRV/c/FTC/TAF: n = 263, 26.9%).

Discussion

In this large retrospective analysis of commercially insured PLWH in the United States, nearly half of the study population had at least one pDDI between ART and a concomitant non-ART medication. More than 60% of PLWH had multimorbidity and used multiple concomitant medications, illustrating how aging and increasing treatment complexity create opportunities for clinically relevant interactions. Previous studies have similarly documented substantial DDI burden among PLWH and have linked DDIs with hospitalization, emergency department use, reduced quality of life, functional decline, and ART nonadherence [6, 7, 16, 17]. Our findings extend this literature by demonstrating that pDDIs remain common across a broad range of contemporary ART regimens in routine US clinical practice.

While the prevalence of pDDIs varied across individual ART agents and regimens, our results collectively highlight that DDI risk is not confined to complex or less commonly used therapies but remains relevant across modern ART regimens. Boosted ART regimens consistently demonstrated among the highest burden of contraindicated and major pDDIs, consistent with previous studies showing that boosted PI-and elvitegravir-based regimens have greater interaction potential than unboosted regimens [14, 18–25]. Comedication classes such as corticosteroids, anticoagulants, antiplatelets, lipid-lowering agents, and antidiabetic therapies were commonly associated with pDDIs, reflecting the substantial comorbidity and comedication burden among aging PLWH reported in a recent real-world study [3]. Major pDDIs were also observed among unboosted INSTI- and NNRTI-based regimens, indicating that clinically relevant interactions remain common despite the lower interaction potential of newer ART. This finding is consistent with recent literature suggesting that although newer ART regimens generally have lower DDI potential than older boosted regimens, the increasing prevalence of multimorbidity and polypharmacy among aging PLWH continues to sustain clinically relevant pDDIs [21, 26]. Consequently, advances in ART have reduced—but not eliminated—the need for routine DDI assessment in clinical practice.

The evolving ART landscape further underscores the relevance of these findings. Several newly approved and investigational oral ART regimens, including daily and long-acting oral two-drug combinations, are intended to reduce treatment complexity and improve long-term tolerability, but also introduce new pharmacologic considerations. Many of these regimens are anticipated to have relatively favorable DDI profiles; however, clinical and real-world experience remains limited for these combinations, and further evaluation is ongoing. As newer ART agents and regimens are introduced into broader PLWH populations, careful consideration of pDDIs and appropriate monitoring will remain important. Continued data generation will help to better characterize the DDI profiles of these ARTs across diverse clinical settings.

Taken together, these findings underscore the increasing complexity of ART selection and management among aging PLWH with multimorbidity and evolving comedication needs. Comprehensive medication review and DDI screening should therefore be incorporated throughout the treatment course, rather than limited to ART initiation, as chronic conditions and concomitant therapies change over time [21, 22]. When clinically appropriate, ART regimens with lower interaction potential may offer practical advantages for individuals with a high comorbidity burden or anticipated polypharmacy. Many of the most frequent pDDIs in our study involved medications used to manage common age-related conditions, consistent with previous studies [23, 25, 27], highlighting the importance of coordination among HIV specialists, primary care providers, pharmacists, and other subspecialists to identify and mitigate clinically meaningful interactions. Future analyses should build upon these descriptive findings by evaluating independent predictors of clinically important pDDIs using multivariable methods, which may help identify patient populations at greatest risk and inform targeted strategies for DDI prevention and medication management.

This study has several limitations. First, the descriptive design did not adjust for confounding by indication or clinical factors influencing ART and comedication selection. Second, pDDIs were classified using a single interaction database; classifications may differ from other resources in scope or classification. Third, the interaction classification may indicate potential rather than real-world clinical outcomes. Fourth, pharmacy claims data confirm dispensing but not medication administration or adherence and may not capture over-the-counter medications, supplements, or undocumented treatment changes that could influence true DDI exposure. Finally, results may not be generalizable to uninsured populations or PLWH covered by Medicaid or AIDS Drug Assistance Programs, where comorbidity profiles and medication access may differ. Nevertheless, the large claims-based population permitted evaluation of pDDIs among PLWH with diverse ART regimens and medical histories, and the use of the widely recognized Liverpool HIV Drug Interactions database [15] enhanced the clinical interpretability of our findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, findings from this large cohort study using administrative claims data indicate that pDDIs between ART and non-ART medications were commonly observed among PLWH in real-world clinical practice. As the PLWH population continues to age and ART options expand to include new daily and long-acting agents with diverse pharmacologic properties, careful consideration of DDI risk will remain essential to optimizing long-term treatment safety and effectiveness. Routine medication reconciliation, pharmacist-led medication review, use of validated HIV drug interaction resources or electronic prescribing decision-support tools, and periodic reassessment of concomitant medications may help identify and mitigate clinically relevant pDDIs in routine practice. The study underscores the need for ongoing vigilance, integrated medication management, and continued development of ART regimens with improved DDI profiles to support the evolving needs of PLWH.
