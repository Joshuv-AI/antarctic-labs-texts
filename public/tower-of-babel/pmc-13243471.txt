Age-dependent transcriptional remodeling of the liver and gallbladder in response to fasting in mice
Authors: Yuqiang Lu, Peipei Ying, Jiacheng Song, Wanqi Ding, Peipei Feng, Jia Du, Chen Ruan, Hao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAging and nutrient stress reshape the physiological landscape of metabolic organs, yet how these factors interact across development remains unclear. The liver and gallbladder, central to hepatobiliary function, may exhibit distinct transcriptional responses to aging and fasting, reflecting their divergent roles in metabolism and immune regulation. We performed bulk RNA sequencing of mouse liver and gallbladder tissues across three developmental stages (P27, 2M, 21M) under both fed and fasted conditions. We applied Mfuzz clustering and WGCNA to delineate temporal gene expression patterns and age-associated co-expression modules. The liver showed a gradual transcriptional shift from proliferative to immune-dominant states with age, while the gallbladder exhibited biphasic remodeling: early immune activation coupled with metabolic suppression, followed by reactivation of lipid metabolism in old age. Fasting induced conserved lipid catabolism in both organs, but age-specific regulatory modules revealed distinct adaptive mechanisms. Aged liver activated histone remodeling and translational programs while suppressing lipid metabolism. In contrast, the aging gallbladder displayed reduced insulin responsiveness and autophagic capacity, coupled with enhanced extracellular matrix reorganization. These findings reveal organ- and age-specific transcriptional strategies underlying hepatobiliary adaptation to aging and fasting, offering insight into the developmental logic and decline of metabolic resilience across the lifespan.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48575-3.Subject terms: Gastroenterology, Physiology

Introduction

Maintaining metabolic homeostasis during aging requires coordinated adaptation of multiple organs to both intrinsic developmental cues and extrinsic environmental stressors1,2. The liver and gallbladder, central to lipid processing and bile storage, respectively, are crucial components of the hepatobiliary system3,4, yet their developmental trajectories and age-related stress responses remain poorly defined. While the aging liver is known to exhibit steatosis, immune infiltration, and reduced regenerative capacity5–7, the gallbladder has historically received less attention, despite its emerging roles in immune surveillance and bile metabolism.

Fasting imposes a strong physiological challenge that reveals latent differences in tissue metabolic plasticity8,9. Although fasting-induced transcriptional programs have been well studied in the liver10,11, how these responses vary across developmental stages-and how they differ between metabolically active organs like the liver and more passive reservoirs like the gallbladder-remains unclear. Moreover, whether aging modulates the liver and gallbladder’s response to nutrient deprivation through convergent or divergent mechanisms has not been systematically explored.

Here, we performed bulk RNA sequencing of liver and gallbladder tissues from mice at three developmental stages (P27, 2M, and 21M) under fed and fasted conditions to delineate organ-specific and age-dependent transcriptional adaptations. Through integrative analysis including Mfuzz-based temporal clustering and WGCNA network modeling, we uncover distinct developmental and fasting-responsive gene modules, revealing profound divergence in how the liver and gallbladder remodel their metabolic and immune programs over time. Our findings provide a comprehensive molecular framework for understanding hepatobiliary aging and highlight novel insights into organ-specific vulnerabilities to nutrient stress.

Results

Developmental transcriptomic landscapes uncover organ-specific adaptation in liver and gallbladder

To explore how aging and fasting influence the transcriptional landscape of the hepatobiliary system, we performed bulk RNA sequencing of liver and gallbladder tissues collected from mice at three distinct ages-postnatal day 27 (P27), 2 month (2M), and 21 months (21M)-under both fed and fasted conditions. Upon dissection, we observed that the livers of juvenile (P27) and young adult (2M) mice exhibited a healthy reddish-brown appearance (Fig. 1a), indicative of normal perfusion and tissue integrity. In contrast, livers from aged mice (21M) appeared pale and discolored, likely consistent with hepatic steatosis or impaired vascularization, both of which have been associated with age-related metabolic decline and reduced hepatic function. This gross morphological change suggests a progressive deterioration of liver homeostasis with aging. Following acute fasting, livers from all three age groups appeared paler than their respective fed controls (Fig. 1a), a visual hallmark often associated with glycogen depletion and increased lipid mobilization. These changes are consistent with a conserved fasting-induced metabolic reprogramming across ages12; however, the preexisting pale phenotype in aged livers may reflect a diminished capacity to maintain metabolic flexibility under stress. In contrast, no overt morphological changes were observed in the gallbladders across different age groups or in response to fasting. Gallbladders from juvenile, young adult, and aged mice all exhibited comparable size, shape, and coloration, regardless of nutritional state (Data not shown). This apparent stability suggests that, unlike the liver, the gallbladder maintains its gross anatomical integrity during aging and acute metabolic stress. Such resilience may reflect the gallbladder’s relatively passive role in nutrient metabolism, functioning primarily as a reservoir for bile rather than a site of active metabolic regulation. Alternatively, it is possible that age- or fasting-induced changes in the gallbladder are more subtle, occurring at the molecular or cellular level rather than being discernible by gross examination. However, histological analysis revealed distinct age-related and starvation-induced changes in the liver (Extended Data Fig. 1a). In young (P27) and middle-aged (2M) normal mice, the predominant alteration was mild hepatocellular edema, while the overall lobular structure remained preserved. In contrast, aged mice (21M) exhibited focal hepatocellular necrosis accompanied by nuclear fragmentation and mild inflammatory infiltration, indicating progressive age-associated degeneration. Starvation induced pronounced hepatocellular steatosis across all age groups, characterized by the presence of multiple cytoplasmic lipid vacuoles. While the hepatic capsule and lobular organization remained intact, the degree of steatosis appeared comparable in young and middle-aged mice but was accompanied by more severe degenerative features in aged animals, suggesting that aging exacerbates hepatic vulnerability to nutritional stress. The gallbladder also demonstrated both age-dependent and starvation-associated alterations (Extended Data Fig. 1b). In young normal mice (P27), only mild epithelial edema and occasional focal shedding were observed. In middle-aged normal mice (2M), epithelial edema was more pronounced, with focal detachment and limited inflammatory infiltration. In aged normal mice (21M), crystalline eosinophilic deposits and epithelial edema were evident, reflecting progressive degenerative changes with age. Starvation elicited relatively modest changes in the gallbladder compared to the liver. In young starved mice, only mild epithelial and muscular edema was detected. In middle-aged starved mice, epithelial edema with minor inflammatory infiltration was present, resembling the alterations in normal controls. In aged starved mice, glandular dilatation and epithelial flattening were observed, indicating that starvation can accentuate age-related degenerative pathology. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that aging is primarily associated with progressive degenerative and inflammatory changes in both the liver and gallbladder, with hepatocellular necrosis and crystalline deposition most evident in aged mice. In contrast, starvation predominantly induces hepatocellular steatosis across all ages, while its effects on the gallbladder are less pronounced but appear to amplify age-associated alterations in older animals.

To gain molecular insights into the age- associated alterations observed in liver morphology, we performed bulk RNA sequencing of livers from mice at three developmental stages, both under fed and fasted conditions. We first compared the hepatic transcriptomes of young adult (2M) and juvenile (P27) mice. Several genes were significantly upregulated in young adult livers (Extended Data Fig. 2a and Table S1), including Nalf2, Ighg1, Sfrp5, Ar, Sult3a2, Xlr4a, Sult3a1, Tafa2, Chrm3, and Mup19. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed a significant overrepresentation of genes involved in sulfation processes (Fig. 1b). Sulfation plays a critical role in phase II detoxification and bile acid metabolism, and its upregulation may reflect the maturation of hepatic xenobiotic and hormonal processing capacity during early postnatal development13. This is consistent with the increased expression of sulfotransferases such as Sult3a1 and Sult3a2, which are known to be developmentally regulated and contribute to the metabolic handling of endogenous and exogenous compounds14,15. In contrast, genes downregulated in young adult livers relative to juvenile livers included Cd209g, Igf2bp3, Cd209f., Phf11a, Slfn1, Ccr3, Ppp1r42, and Ttc39aos1. These genes did not converge on any significantly enriched biological pathways, suggesting that their age-dependent reduction may reflect the gradual silencing of early developmental or immune-regulatory programs rather than a coordinated shift in specific liver functions. The lack of enriched pathways may also indicate that postnatal hepatic maturation is primarily driven by upregulation of function-specific gene modules, rather than broad downregulation of discrete biological processes.

We next compared the hepatic transcriptomes of aged (21M) and young adult (2M) mice to uncover molecular features associated with liver aging. A total of 39 genes were significantly upregulated in aged livers (Extended Data Fig. 2b and Table S1), including Or5m11b, Or5m5, Ighv3-6, Igkv4-55, Igkv1-110, Or2ag2b, and Igkv1-117. These genes were primarily enriched in pathways related to immunoglobulin production and B cell receptor signaling (Extended Data Fig. 2c), reflecting the well-documented infiltration or activation of immune cells-particularly B lymphocytes-in aged tissues. This immune-related transcriptional signature is consistent with the concept of "inflammaging," a chronic, low-grade inflammatory state that characterizes aging and contributes to tissue dysfunction, including in the liver16,17. In contrast, only six genes were significantly downregulated in aged livers, including Gm53778, Ccnb1, Cfap20dc, Chic1, Ckap2l, and Cib3. These genes were enriched in pathways related to the mitotic cell cycle (Extended Data Fig. 2d), suggesting a decline in hepatocyte proliferative capacity and regenerative potential with age. Reduced expression of key mitotic regulators such as Ccnb1 (Cyclin B1) aligns with previous findings that cellular senescence and decreased turnover are hallmarks of aging liver tissue18–20. Together, these results highlight a shift in the aging liver from a proliferative to an inflammatory transcriptional state. Further comparison of the liver transcriptomes of aged (21M) and juvenile (P27) mice showed genes upregulated in aged livers were significantly enriched in pathways related to complement activation and immunoglobulin production (Fig. 1c), reinforcing the notion that aging is accompanied by heightened immune activation and chronic low-grade inflammation within hepatic tissue. Activation of the complement cascade-particularly in the absence of infection-has been implicated in sterile inflammation and is increasingly recognized as a contributor to age-related liver dysfunction and fibrosis21,22. Conversely, genes downregulated in aged livers were enriched in pathways associated with response to interferon-β and IP-10 (CXCL10) production (Fig. 1d), suggesting a decline in antiviral and inflammatory signaling pathways. Interferon-β and IP-10 are critical components of the innate immune response, especially in defending against viral infections and regulating immune cell trafficking. Their reduced expression in the aged liver may indicate impaired responsiveness to inflammatory stimuli and diminished immune surveillance, potentially contributing to the increased susceptibility of elderly individuals to hepatic infections and tumorigenesis. Together, these findings reveal a paradoxical shift in the aging liver toward chronic immune activation (via complement and antibody-related pathways) alongside attenuated innate immune signaling, reflecting an imbalanced immune landscape that may underlie age-associated vulnerability to metabolic and infectious liver diseases.

We next analyzed transcriptomic differences in the gallbladder between young adult (2M) and juvenile (P27) mice. A total of 71 genes were significantly upregulated in the gallbladder of young adult mice (Extended Data Fig. 2e and Table S2), including multiple immunoglobulin-related genes such as Ighv1-53, Ighv1-76, Ighv1-77, Igkv4-80, Ighv14-2, and Igkv3-5. Enrichment analysis revealed significant activation of pathways associated with complement activation and phagocytosis (Fig. 1e), suggesting a developmental acquisition of immune surveillance functions within the gallbladder. These findings imply that the gallbladder undergoes postnatal immune maturation, possibly as an adaptation to increased exposure to gut microbiota-derived antigens or bile-mediated immunomodulation in early life. In contrast, 1,418 genes were significantly downregulated in the gallbladder of young adult mice compared to juveniles (Extended Data Fig. 2e), including Gpx6, Cyp2d12, Cyp4a31, Treh, Saa2, and Cyp2d9. These genes were enriched in pathways related to steroid metabolic processes and fatty acid metabolism (Extended Data Fig. 2g), suggesting a developmental attenuation of local metabolic activity. This downregulation may reflect a shift in gallbladder function from an early stage with active metabolic involvement-possibly linked to bile composition regulation-to a more specialized role in bile storage and immune defense in adulthood. Together, these results indicate that the gallbladder undergoes substantial transcriptional remodeling during the transition from juvenile to adult stages, characterized by reduced local metabolic activity and enhanced immune readiness. This shift may represent an organ-level adaptation to changing physiological demands and host-microbe interactions during early life.

We further compared the gallbladder transcriptomes of aged (21M) and young adult (2M) mice to examine age-associated transcriptional changes. In aged mice, 904 genes were significantly upregulated compared to young adults (Extended Data Fig. 2f and Table S2), including Igfbp1, Alpi, Sis, Cyp2c69, and Cyp2a22. These genes were enriched in pathways related to steroid metabolic processes and fatty acid metabolism (Extended Data Fig. 2h). Notably, this enrichment pattern is the inverse of what we observed in the comparison between young adult and juvenile gallbladders, where these metabolic pathways were downregulated. This reversal suggests that the aged gallbladder undergoes a reactivation of local metabolic programs, which may reflect compensatory mechanisms or dysregulated lipid and steroid handling during aging. Such metabolic reactivation has been linked to increased bile cholesterol saturation and may contribute to age-related susceptibility to gallstone formation or gallbladder dysfunction. Only 14 genes were significantly downregulated in the aged gallbladder relative to young adults (Extended Data Fig. 2f), including Myh6, Ffar3, Chat, Alox15, Sh2d7, and Trpm5. Although these 14 genes were not annotated to specific GO terms, previous studies have reported their functional relevance to these biological processes. These genes are involved in smooth muscle function, fatty acid sensing, cholinergic signaling, and immune regulation, suggesting that aging is associated with a subtle decline in gallbladder contractility and neuroimmune communication. The downregulation of Ffar3 and Chat, for instance, may point to impaired responsiveness to short-chain fatty acids and reduced parasympathetic input, both of which can impact bile ejection and epithelial tone. When comparing the gallbladders of aged and juvenile mice, we identified 131 upregulated genes in aged animals, including Zfp992, Zfp982, Vip, Spag6, Sp140l2, and Snhg11. These genes were significantly enriched in pathways related to phagocytosis and complement activation (Fig. 1f), reinforcing the notion of an age-associated enhancement in immune activity. This pattern suggests a progressive accumulation of innate immune reprogramming in the gallbladder over time, likely in response to chronic exposure to bile constituents and microbial antigens from the gut. Conversely, only 13 genes were downregulated in aged gallbladders compared to juveniles, including Hsd3b1, Akr1c18, Hamp2, Oas3, Cib3, and Oas2. These genes were enriched in pathways related to nuclease activity and response to interferon-β production (Fig. 1g), indicating a blunted antiviral and antimicrobial transcriptional response in aged tissue. The decreased expression of Oas2 and Oas3, in particular, may signal diminished interferon-stimulated gene activation, which could compromise mucosal immunity and antiviral defense in the aging gallbladder. Taken together, these results reveal that the gallbladder undergoes biphasic transcriptional remodeling during aging: an early postnatal suppression of metabolic activity accompanied by enhanced immune readiness, followed by a late-life reactivation of metabolic genes alongside chronic immune stimulation and impaired innate antiviral responses. This dynamic reprogramming likely reflects the gallbladder’s adaptation-and eventual maladaptation-to lifelong exposure to dietary, microbial, and bile-derived stimuli.

Overall, these results demonstrate that while the liver exhibits a progressive shift from metabolic activity to immune activation with age, the gallbladder undergoes a biphasic transcriptional transition-marked by early metabolic suppression and immune priming, followed by late-life metabolic reactivation and dysregulated innate immunity.

Temporal transcriptome remodeling highlights distinct maturation trajectories

We next performed time-series analysis of liver and gallbladder transcriptomes across developmental stages using the soft clustering algorithm MFuzz. In the liver, transcriptomic changes from juvenile (P27) to young adult (2M) to aged (21M) mice were classified into four dynamic expression clusters (Fig. 2a). Cluster 1 displayed a progressive upregulation trajectory. Genes in this cluster were significantly enriched in pathways related to RNA splicing and organic acid catabolic processes (Fig. 2b), suggesting a gradual enhancement of transcriptome processing capacity and energy substrate catabolism with age, potentially reflecting the liver’s increasing metabolic complexity during maturation and aging. Cluster 2 exhibited a modest early increase followed by a pronounced decline, with gene enrichment in extracellular matrix (ECM) organization (Fig. 2c). This trend indicates age-associated remodeling and potential degradation of liver structural integrity, consistent with known senescence-related ECM breakdown and fibrosis23,24. Cluster 3 showed a biphasic pattern of mild downregulation followed by sharp upregulation. The genes were enriched in oxidative phosphorylation and generation of precursor metabolites and energy (Fig. 2d), pointing to metabolic reactivation or compensation in aged livers, possibly in response to energy deficits or mitochondrial stress. Cluster 4 exhibited a steady downregulation across time. Enriched pathways included response to external stimulus and response to interferon-β, suggesting a progressive decline in the liver’s innate immune responsiveness and antiviral defense capacity (Fig. 2e), a hallmark of immunosenescence. These distinct transcriptional trajectories indicate that the liver undergoes coordinated metabolic and immune remodeling with age, marked by increased energy production and splicing activity, ECM restructuring, and declining immune vigilance.

In contrast, the gallbladder exhibited distinct time-dependent transcriptional dynamics, categorized into a separate set of four clusters (Fig. 2f). Cluster 1 showed a down-up pattern, with genes enriched in fatty acid metabolic processes and organic acid catabolism (Fig. 2g). This pattern suggests a transient reduction in metabolic activity during early adulthood followed by compensatory metabolic reactivation in old age, potentially reflecting age-related biliary adaptation or dysfunction. Cluster 2 followed a steadily increasing trajectory, with enrichment in lymphocyte activation and immune response activation (Fig. 2h), consistent with progressive immune sensitization or inflammation in the aging gallbladder, a response likely driven by cumulative exposure to bile acids, microbes, and inflammatory stimuli. Cluster 3 exhibited an up-down pattern, with gene enrichment in actin filament organization and synapse organization (Fig. 2i), indicating dynamic changes in epithelial or neuromuscular structure that may impact gallbladder contractility or epithelial barrier integrity over time. Cluster 4 displayed a slight increase in early adulthood followed by a pronounced decline, with genes enriched in ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis and extracellular structure organization (Fig. 2j). This suggests a late-life suppression of cellular biosynthetic machinery and structural maintenance, possibly linked to age-related tissue degeneration. Collectively, these time-series analyses reveal that while both the liver and gallbladder undergo age-associated transcriptional reprogramming, the liver exhibits gradual functional reorganization centered on metabolism and immune aging, whereas the gallbladder demonstrates oscillatory transcriptional responses indicative of adaptive plasticity followed by senescence-related decline.

We next applied weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to further delineate age-associated gene modules in the liver and gallbladder. WGCNA allows us to identify modules of highly co-expressed genes that correlate with continuous traits-specifically “Age” and “Fasting state”-across the entire dataset. This systems biology approach helps uncover gene networks and regulatory hubs that might be missed by simple pairwise comparisons, offering a more holistic view of the aging trajectory. In the liver (Fig. 3a), two top-ranked gene modules positively correlated with age (correlation > 0.8) were enriched in pathways related to the immune response, including antigen processing, leukocyte activation, and immunoglobulin production (Fig. 3b,c). This indicates that the aging liver undergoes a progressive shift toward an immune-dominant transcriptional state, likely reflecting chronic low-grade inflammation (“inflammaging”) and increased immune cell infiltration. In contrast, two top modules negatively correlated with age (correlation < -0.8) were enriched in pathways associated with extracellular matrix (ECM) organization (Fig. 3d) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) production (Fig. 3e). These findings suggest that ECM structural integrity and cytokine-mediated tissue remodeling capacity decline with age, which may contribute to impaired liver regeneration and increased fibrosis susceptibility in aged animals. In the gallbladder, only one high-confidence age-positive module (correlation > 0.8) was identified (Extended Data Fig. 3a), and it was enriched in genes related to the complement cascade (Extended Data Fig. 3b), indicating increased activation of innate immune pathways during aging. Conversely, the most negatively age-associated module (correlation < − 0.8) was enriched for lymphocyte activation (Extended Data Fig. 3c), suggesting a decline in adaptive immune surveillance in the aging gallbladder. Together, these co-expression network results reinforce a model in which aging drives a shift in both liver and gallbladder from tissue maintenance and immune balance toward heightened innate immune activity and reduced structural and adaptive immune capacity, consistent with hallmarks of immunosenescence and organ-specific aging trajectories.

Divergent fasting-induced gene expression programs across mouse developmental stages

To further investigate how aging influences metabolic adaptability, we next compared the hepatic transcriptional responses to acute starvation across different age groups. In juvenile mice, starvation induced the upregulation of 199 genes (Fig. 4a), including Dnah3, Capn11, Cidec, Aqp3, and Fgf21, with enrichment in pathways related to fatty acid metabolic process and lipid catabolic process (Fig. 4b), suggesting a robust mobilization of lipid reserves in response to energy deprivation. Meanwhile, 196 genes were downregulated (Fig. 4a), such as Chrna4, Serpina9, Cdh15, Hsd3b5, and Clec2h, primarily enriched in steroid metabolic process and cholesterol biosynthesis (Fig. 4c), indicating a suppression of anabolic lipid pathways during fasting. In young adult mice, starvation upregulated 235 genes (Fig. 4d), including Adra2a, Dlgap2, Cidec, Dnah3, Ren1, and Tg, with significant enrichment in fatty acid metabolic process and acylglycerol metabolic process (Fig. 4e), reflecting a continued reliance on lipolysis and fatty acid utilization. Conversely, 335 genes were downregulated (Fig. 4d), including Chrna4, Hsd3b5, Serpina9, Kntc1, and Nat8f7, enriched in steroid metabolic process and cholesterol biosynthesis (Fig. 4f), consistent with fasting-induced repression of lipid synthesis programs. In aged mice, 255 genes were upregulated upon starvation (Fig. 4g), such as Ren1, Mt2, Cux2, Apoa4, and Mt1, with pathways enriched for fatty acid metabolic process and olefinic compound metabolic process (Fig. 4h), suggesting that aged livers retain the ability to activate lipid catabolism, albeit potentially through distinct molecular mechanisms. A total of 218 genes were downregulated (Fig. 4g), including Hsd3b5, Chrna4, Krt4, Serpina9, and Selenbp2, with functional enrichment in sterol biosynthetic process and cholesterol biosynthesis (Fig. 4i), reflecting a conserved downregulation of sterol metabolism during energy stress. Together, these results demonstrate that although all age groups activate lipid catabolic programs in response to starvation, the specific gene expression signatures and pathway enrichments exhibit age-dependent differences, suggesting altered regulatory strategies for maintaining metabolic homeostasis across the lifespan.

We next examined the age-dependent transcriptional responses of the gallbladder to fasting. In juvenile mice, fasting induced the upregulation of 114 genes in the gallbladder (Extended Data Fig. 4a), including Lmod3, Capn11, Apoa4, Mfsd2a, and Scn9a, with enriched pathways related to fatty acid metabolic process and carboxylic acid catabolic process (Extended Data Fig. 4b), suggesting an early capacity for lipid mobilization during nutrient deprivation. In contrast, 59 genes were downregulated (Extended Data Fig. 4a), such as Chil4, Hsd3b5, Clnk, Sprr2a3, and Clec2h, with significant enrichment in steroid biosynthetic process and cholesterol metabolism (Extended Data Fig. 4c), indicating a metabolic shift away from anabolic sterol pathways under fasting conditions. In young adult mice, fasting triggered a more robust transcriptional response in the gallbladder, with 464 genes upregulated (Extended Data Fig. 4d)-including Trap1a, Apoa4, Igfbp1, Cyp4a14, and Cyp4a31-again enriched in fatty acid metabolic process and carboxylic acid catabolic process (Extended Data Fig. 4e), highlighting sustained lipid catabolism during fasting. Only 32 genes were downregulated (Extended Data Fig. 4d), including Ighv3-5, Lep, Plppr4, Otof, and Syt10, associated with ovulation from ovarian follicle and prostaglandin secretion (Extended Data Fig. 4f), suggesting fasting may transiently suppress reproductive and hormone-associated pathways even in non-reproductive tissues. Although fasting induced the expression of a subset of genes typically associated with hepatocyte metabolic function (e.g., Cyp4a14) in the gallbladder, this induction was not accompanied by the expression of canonical hepatocyte identity markers, suggesting a context-dependent transcriptional response rather than hepatocyte contamination. In aged mice, 176 genes were upregulated following fasting (Extended Data Fig. 4g), such as Ighv14-4, Acot3, Nppb, Tnfsf18, and Ppp1r3g, with pathway enrichment in fatty acid metabolic process and olefinic compound metabolic process (Extended Data Fig. 4h), indicating preserved, though possibly altered, lipid breakdown programs in the aging gallbladder. Meanwhile, 126 genes were downregulated (Extended Data Fig. 4g), including Igkv1-88, Ndp, Hsd3b5, Ntsr1, and Trim67, enriched in sterol biosynthetic and cholesterol biosynthetic processes (Extended Data Fig. 4i). Collectively, these findings suggest that the gallbladder maintains a conserved capacity to upregulate lipid catabolic programs during fasting across all ages, but the magnitude and specificity of transcriptional responses vary with age. Moreover, the consistent repression of sterol and cholesterol biosynthesis indicates a common adaptive mechanism to reduce energy-demanding anabolic processes during nutrient scarcity. Notably, the more extensive transcriptional remodeling in adult mice implies increased plasticity or metabolic demand at this life stage.

Maturation-dependent modulation of fasting-responsive genes

To further dissect the age-related transcriptional dynamics of liver and gallbladder responses to fasting, we performed time-series analysis using Mfuzz clustering. In the liver of fasted mice, transcriptional dynamics across young, middle-aged, and aged mice could be categorized into four clusters (Fig. 5a). Cluster 1 showed a progressive decline, with genes enriched in pathways related to ribosome biogenesis and cellular respiration (Fig. 5b), indicating a gradual reduction in protein synthesis and metabolic activity. Cluster 2 exhibited a transient upregulation followed by a decline, enriched for pathways involved in mRNA processing, histone modification, and autophagy (Fig. 5c), suggesting an early adaptive response to starvation that becomes attenuated with age. Cluster 3 displayed an initial downregulation followed by recovery, with enrichment in pathways associated with response to external stimuli and regulation of T cell activation (Fig. 5d), reflecting transient suppression and later partial restoration of immune-related transcriptional programs. Cluster 4 remained relatively stable at early stages but sharply increased at later stages, enriched for pathways linked to lymphocyte-mediated immunity and DNA replication (Fig. 5e), pointing to a late-stage activation of immune and proliferative responses. Collectively, these results suggest that starvation elicits distinct, age-dependent transcriptional trajectories in the liver, characterized by an early decline in biosynthetic and metabolic capacity, followed by modulation of chromatin and autophagy pathways, and ultimately involving compensatory activation of immune and proliferative programs. These findings are consistent with previous reports that aging diminishes metabolic plasticity and stress resilience, while promoting immune dysregulation and low-grade inflammation (inflammaging), thereby shaping the liver’s adaptive response to nutritional stress.

In the gallbladder of fasted mice, temporal transcriptomic profiling revealed four distinct gene expression clusters (Fig. 5f). Cluster 1 exhibited a progressively increasing expression pattern across age, with genes enriched in cytoplasmic translation and the B cell receptor signaling pathway (Fig. 5g), indicating a potential age-related enhancement of local immune surveillance and translational capacity during fasting. Cluster 2 followed a transient upregulation followed by downregulation, and was enriched in actin filament organization and regulation of supramolecular fiber organization (Fig. 5h), suggesting transient cytoskeletal remodeling as a response to fasting-induced mechanical or metabolic stress. Cluster 3 displayed an inverse trend-initial downregulation followed by upregulation-with gene enrichment in organic acid catabolic processes and fatty acid metabolic processes (Fig. 5i), reflecting a delayed but compensatory activation of energy-yielding lipid metabolism with age. Cluster 4 showed a steady decline in gene expression across the lifespan, with associated pathways including protein dephosphorylation and extracellular matrix organization (Fig. 5j), potentially indicating a gradual attenuation of tissue remodeling and signal transduction responses in the aging gallbladder under fasting conditions. These dynamic profiles suggest that the gallbladder adapts to fasting in an age-dependent manner, with differential modulation of immune, metabolic, and structural pathways that may underlie changes in its physiological plasticity across the lifespan.

To complement the time-series analysis, we conducted WGCNA to identify gene modules associated with age during fasting. In the fasted liver (Fig. 6a), two top gene modules positively correlated with age were significantly enriched in histone modification (Fig. 6b) and ribosome biogenesis (Fig. 6c), indicating a potential shift toward chromatin remodeling and translational readiness in aged liver during nutrient stress. Conversely, two top negatively correlated modules were associated with fatty acid metabolic processes (Fig. 6d) and extracellular structure organization (Fig. 6e), suggesting an age-related decline in the liver’s capacity for lipid catabolism and tissue maintenance under fasting conditions. In the fasted gallbladder (Extended Data Fig. 5a), two top positively age-correlated modules were enriched in pathways including extracellular matrix organization, neuron apoptotic process, protein localization to chromosome, and fatty acid oxidation (Extended Data Fig. 5b,c), reflecting increased structural remodeling and stress-associated signaling with age. In contrast, two top negatively correlated modules were enriched in response to insulin and autophagy (Extended Data Fig. 5d,e), highlighting a diminished capacity for metabolic regulation and cellular recycling in the aged gallbladder during fasting. Together, these results reveal that aging significantly alters the molecular programs engaged by both liver and gallbladder during fasting, with the liver exhibiting decreased metabolic flexibility and the gallbladder showing reduced insulin responsiveness and autophagic potential. These age-related transcriptional shifts may contribute to the differential organ-level adaptations to energy deprivation and the progressive decline in metabolic homeostasis with age.

Organ-specific temporal trajectories of transcriptional aging in liver and gallbladder

To determine whether age-associated transcriptional changes follow distinct temporal trajectories across organs, we integrated global dimensionality reduction with gene-level temporal and network-based analyses. Principal component analysis (PCA) of transcriptomic profiles revealed a progressive and continuous age-dependent shift in the liver across both fed and fasted conditions, consistent with a linear aging trajectory (Extended Data Fig. 6a). In contrast, gallbladder samples exhibited a distinctive non-linear distribution along the age axis, suggesting the presence of coordinated, stage-specific transcriptional transitions rather than a monotonic progression (Extended Data Fig. 6b).

Consistent with these global patterns, temporal clustering and co-expression network analyses (Mfuzz and WGCNA) further resolved the underlying gene expression dynamics. In the liver, age-associated gene modules predominantly exhibited monotonic expression changes, with genes progressively upregulated or downregulated over time. Functional annotation of these modules revealed sustained enrichment of pathways related to metabolic decline and inflammation, supporting a model of cumulative transcriptional aging. Strikingly, gallbladder aging was characterized by a “biphasic” transcriptional remodeling program, in which distinct gene modules displayed opposing temporal behaviors. One set of genes, associated with epithelial homeostatic maintenance and metabolic adaptation, was preferentially activated at early ages but repressed at later stages. Conversely, a second set, enriched for extracellular matrix reorganization and chronic immune activation, showed delayed activation during aging (Fig. 3 and Extended Data Fig. 3). Together, these coordinated shifts define a fundamental functional transition between early-stage maturation and late-stage degenerative remodeling in the gallbladder, a pattern distinct from the continuous aging process observed in the liver. Notably, although feeding status influenced the magnitude of these changes, it did not alter the fundamental temporal trajectories, indicating that while nutritional states can modulate the aging transcriptome, the organ-specific aging “program” remains intrinsically robust.

Discussion

Our study provides a comprehensive, age-resolved transcriptomic map of the liver and gallbladder under fed and fasted states, offering novel insight into how these hepatobiliary organs differentially adapt across the lifespan. Prior research has primarily focused on the liver as a central metabolic organ during aging and nutrient stress, with well-documented phenomena including hepatic steatosis, immune infiltration, and reduced regenerative capacity in aged liver19,25,26. In contrast, the gallbladder has remained largely understudied in the context of systemic metabolism and aging, despite its increasing recognition as an immune-modulatory and metabolically responsive tissue27,28. We confirm and extend previous findings by demonstrating that the aging liver undergoes a progressive shift from proliferation and metabolism toward immune activation. These transcriptional features, including upregulation of immunoglobulin and complement genes and downregulation of cell cycle and interferon signaling pathways, are consistent with the “inflammaging” paradigm16,17. In contrast, we uncover a previously unappreciated biphasic trajectory in the gallbladder: early-life metabolic downregulation coincides with immune priming, whereas old age is marked by reactivation of lipid metabolism alongside signs of innate immune dysregulation. This biphasic pattern diverges from the monotonic changes observed in the liver, highlighting organ-specific aging trajectories. The fasting response across developmental stages revealed both conserved and divergent features. Consistent with earlier studies, fasting activated lipid catabolism and suppressed sterol biosynthesis in the liver29–31; however, we show that these processes are modulated by age through distinct gene modules. In aged livers, for example, fasting upregulated genes involved in olefinic compound metabolism and ribosome biogenesis, indicating a possible shift in metabolic strategy and translational control. Notably, the gallbladder responded to fasting across all ages by activating fatty acid metabolism genes, but the magnitude and specificity of this response varied, with adult mice showing the most extensive transcriptional remodeling-suggesting greater metabolic plasticity or demand at that stage. Using WGCNA and time-series clustering, we revealed age-specific gene modules linked to histone remodeling, fatty acid oxidation, insulin signaling, and autophagy. These findings not only illuminate how regulatory programs change with age, but also point to specific mechanisms that may contribute to reduced metabolic resilience. For instance, the loss of autophagy-related gene expression in the aged gallbladder during fasting may underlie impaired cellular turnover and increased susceptibility to damage, an area that remains poorly explored in the literature.

An important consideration is the translational relevance of the organ-specific aging trajectories identified in this study. Although comparative analysis with human data would be of significant interest, high-quality human transcriptomic datasets that closely match our experimental design-namely, paired liver and gallbladder tissues sampled across juvenile, young adult, and aged stages under controlled fed and fasted conditions-are currently not available. Direct comparison with heterogeneous human datasets lacking precise matching for age, nutritional status, and tissue integrity could introduce substantial confounding effects, including variability in diet, post-mortem interval, and underlying pathology, potentially leading to misleading interpretations. Therefore, in the present work, we focused on establishing a well-controlled and mechanistically interpretable framework for organ-specific transcriptional aging in the mouse model. We anticipate that future studies incorporating systematically collected human samples or well-annotated human organoid and ex vivo models will be essential to evaluate the extent to which the biphasic gallbladder remodeling and linear liver aging programs observed here are conserved in humans.

While this study uncovers fundamental transcriptional dynamics in hepatobiliary aging and fasting, several open questions remain. First, our data are derived from bulk RNA sequencing, which captures averaged gene expression across diverse cell types. Future studies using single-cell or spatial transcriptomics will be essential to resolve cell-type-specific contributions, especially given the cellular heterogeneity of the liver and the under-characterized composition of the gallbladder. Second, although our transcriptional data point to metabolic and immune remodeling, functional validation of key regulatory pathways-such as complement activation, interferon suppression, and autophagy failure-will be necessary to determine their causal roles in organ dysfunction during aging. The use of in vivo genetic or pharmacologic perturbations could reveal the therapeutic relevance of these pathways. Third, our findings raise intriguing questions about organ-organ crosstalk during aging and stress. How gallbladder-derived signals, such as bile acids or immune mediators, affect liver metabolism-or vice versa-remains unexplored. Understanding such inter-organ communication could illuminate novel systemic aging mechanisms.

Besides, we included postnatal day 27 (P27), 2 months (2M), and 21 months (21M) to capture juvenile, adult-onset, and aged stages, respectively. While P27 represents a late juvenile stage and 21M reflects advanced aging, we acknowledge that 2M corresponds to a young adult period during which certain maturation processes may still be ongoing. This time point was chosen because it marks the onset of sexual maturity and the transition into adulthood, providing a biologically meaningful contrast to both juvenile and geriatric stages. Nonetheless, inclusion of a fully mature adult stage (e.g., 3–6 months) in future studies would allow for finer resolution of transcriptional changes occurring during the transition from development to aging. Such additional sampling could help further delineate whether the observed early-phase transcriptional features represent residual developmental programs or distinct adult adaptive states.

Finally, it is important to acknowledge that this study was performed exclusively in male mice. Given the well-documented sexual dimorphism in metabolic homeostasis, immune responses, and lifespan trajectories, our findings regarding age-dependent hepatobiliary remodeling should be interpreted as specific to male physiology. Sex differences are known to influence susceptibility to liver steatosis and gallstone formation, as well as the magnitude of inflammatory responses during aging. Therefore, future studies incorporating both sexes as biological variables are essential to determine whether the biphasic remodeling observed in the gallbladder and the immune-dominant shift in the liver are conserved in females or exhibit sex-specific divergence.

Conclusion

Our comprehensive transcriptomic profiling reveals that aging and fasting elicit distinct, age-stage-specific responses in the liver and gallbladder. The liver undergoes a progressive shift from metabolic to immune-dominant programs with aging, while the gallbladder demonstrates a biphasic remodeling pattern characterized by early metabolic suppression and late-life reactivation, particularly under nutrient stress. These findings underscore the divergent developmental logic and adaptive plasticity of hepatobiliary organs and suggest that interventions aimed at preserving metabolic homeostasis during aging may require organ- and age-specific strategies.

Methods

The mice were housed under a 12 h:12 h light/dark cycle (lights on at 08:00, defined as ZT0). For the fasting experiments, food was removed at ZT0 (08:00 AM), and tissues were collected after 24 h of fasting at ZT0 (08:00 AM) the following day. All animals were sacrificed at the same Zeitgeber time (ZT0) to minimize the confounding effects of the circadian clock on gene expression. For transcriptomic profiling (RNA-seq), n = 3 biological replicates were utilized for each group (Liver/Gallbladder × P27/2M/21M × Fed/Fasted). For histological analysis (H&E staining), n = 3 biological replicates were examined per group. All procedures were randomized before analysis, and phenotypic evaluations were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation.

Mice liver and gallbladder collection

C57BL/6J mice (22–25 g; Zhejiang Institute of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Hangzhou, China), were utilized for the study. All animal experiments involving liver and gallbladder tissue collection were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Tongde Hospital, Zhejiang Province, China (approval [2025]016). The authors complied with the ARRIVE guidelines. Mice were housed in a pathogen-free facility with controlled temperature and humidity, and all efforts were made to minimize suffering. Initially, the mice were anesthetized using inhalation of the anesthetic agent Isoflurane. Subsequently, while fully anesthetized, they were euthanized via cervical dislocation, followed by the collection of tissue samples.

HE staining

Liver and gallbladder samples were harvested from mice immediately after sacrifice and fixed in 4% neutral-buffered formalin at room temperature for 24–48 h. Fixed tissues were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were cut at a thickness of 5 μm using a rotary microtome and mounted on glass slides. Finally, stained slides were examined under a light microscope (Nikon ECLIPSE E100), and images were captured by using scanner (KIBIO, #KF-PRO-120).

Bulk RNA-seq sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA from mice liver and gallbladder at normally fed and fasted condition were extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration, purity, and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Samples meeting the following criteria were considered suitable for downstream processing: concentration > 50 ng/μL, RNA integrity number (RIN) > 7.0, and OD260/280 > 1.8. For each sample, approximately 1 μg of total RNA was used for rRNA and globin mRNA depletion using the Ribo-off Globin & rRNA Depletion Kit (Vazyme). The depleted RNA was fragmented at 94 °C for 5–7 min using magnesium ion-based hydrolysis. Strand-specific libraries were generated using the Hieff NGS® Ultima Dual-mode RNA Library Prep Kit (Yeasen). In brief, first-strand cDNA was synthesized from the fragmented RNA using random hexamer primers and reverse transcriptase. Second-strand synthesis incorporated dUTP to maintain strand specificity. The resulting double-stranded cDNA was end-repaired and A-tailed to facilitate adapter ligation. Adapter-ligated fragments were size-selected with magnetic beads and subjected to 11–15 cycles of PCR amplification to obtain libraries with an average insert size of ~ 300 ± 50 bp. Final libraries were purified and quantified using the Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Kit on a Bioanalyzer 2100. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq™ X Plus platform using a 150 bp paired-end (PE150) strategy, following the manufacturer’s standard protocols (Hangzhou Repugene Technology).

Quality control of FASTQ files was conducted using FastQC (v0.11.9) and Fastp32. Trimmed FASTQ files were mapped to the GRCm39 mice reference genome with STAR (v2.7.8a)33. The quantification of RNA expression based on RefSeq gene annotation was performed with featureCounts in subread (v2.0.2) to generate raw count metrics. Function “ComBat()” in package sva (v3.46.0) was used to correct batch effect of RNA-seq data before downstream analysis. Differential expression analysis was calculated by “DESeq2”34 package (v1.38.3) in R (4.2.1) and data visualization was accomplished using the “ggplot2” package (v3.5.0) in R. Pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was performed with clusterProfiler package (Log2 FC ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05) (v4.6.2)35.

Time-series cluster (mfuzz) analysis

To identify transcripts with dynamic expression changes in the liver and gallbladder of normally fed and fasted mice across aging, we first applied log₂ transformation to normalize variance in relative expression values across samples. Transcripts showing high variability (standard deviation > 0.5) were selected for downstream analysis. Fuzzy c-means clustering was then performed on the average gene expression matrix derived from each age group using the Mfuzz package (v2.58.0)36, with the number of clusters set to k = 4 and fuzzification parameter m = 2. This approach enabled the identification of genes with distinct temporal expression trajectories throughout aging. The resulting expression patterns were visualized using the mfuzz.plot() function, facilitating quantitative delineation of stage-specific transcriptional programs in the liver and gallbladder under both nutritional conditions.

WGCNA analysis

For detecting and critical genes in different groups, WGCNA package (v1.73)37 was applied to calculate trait-related gene modules and network. Raw counts were normalized using DESeq2’s variance stabilizing transformation and potential batch effects was corrected by “ComBat()” as above mentioned. Weighted gene co-expression networks were built separately for each organ using WGCNA. The optimal soft-thresholding power that ensured scale-free topology (R2 > 0.85) was first calculated using the pickSoftThreshold() function. An unsigned adjacency matrix was then constructed using the selected power value and transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM) to measure network clustered and dynamic tree cutting was applied to identify modules with parameters minModuleSize = 30, deepSplit = 2. To associate traits and calculated gene modules, module eigengenes (MEs) were calculated as the first principal component of each module’s expression matrix. Correlations between MEs and two critical traits in this research, aging and hungry, were calculated and corresponding significance was determined using Student asymptotic p-values with false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Modules with FDR < 0.05 were considered significantly associated. Visualization of module-trait correlation was drawn with function “labeledHeatmap()”. Genes within significant modules underwent GO enrichment analysis using clusterProfiler and hub genes were identified as those with high module membership (kME > 0.8).

Gene ontology analysis

To characterize transcriptional alterations in liver and gallbladder tissues across conditions, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified from bulk RNA-seq data using the DESeq2 package (v1.38.3) in R. Genes with an absolute log₂ fold change ≥ 2 and a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly upregulated or downregulated. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the enrichGO() function from the clusterProfiler package (v4.6.2), with the “Biological Process” category selected for ontology annotation and org.Mm.eg.db used as the reference database for Mus musculus. GO terms with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were retained. The top 10 enriched biological processes were visualized and ranked by −log10 (adjusted p).
