Medullary cavity expansion is mediated by distinct cell populations during fetal bone development
Authors: Eriko Sumiya, Kohei Saeki, Kenta Nakano, Chie Kikutake, Noriko Kurisaki, Natsuko Nakaima, Mami Kurumata-Shigeto, Yumiko Kitada, Yuka Morioka, Yasuhiro Go, Mikita Suyama, Yuki Yoshimura, Motohito Goto, Mamoru Ito, Manabu Nakayama, Haruhiko Akiyama, Lucie Peduto, Tadashi Okamura, Yuki Matsushita, Shinichiro Sawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone marrow (BM) is the primary site of postnatal hematopoiesis, yet how it is established during embryogenesis remains poorly understood. BM formation is initiated by the invasion of vascular and mesenchymal cells into a preformed cartilage template, generating the primary ossification center (POC). Here, we identify CD55+CD90+ mesenchymal cells as early regulators of osteoclastogenesis within the POC. These cells share a matrix-degrading transcriptional program with FABP5+ septoclasts that localize to the chondro-osseous interface of developing bone. Fate-mapping analyses reveal that CD55+CD90+ cells transiently give rise to septoclasts during perinatal development, whereas osteoclast-supporting activity is subsequently assumed by LepR+ bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs). During fracture repair, FABP5+ septoclasts and LepR+ BMSCs are redeployed within the callus, indicating reactivation of a developmental osteogenic program. Together, our findings uncover a conserved mechanism that links skeletal remodeling to the establishment of the BM microenvironment required for hematopoiesis.Subject terms: Bone development, Development

Introduction

Bone marrow (BM), sheltered within bone, serves as the principal site of hematopoiesis in terrestrial vertebrates. The developmental transition of hematopoiesis from the fetal liver to the BM is essential for postnatal survival, supporting efficient oxygen transport and immune defense. Elucidating the microenvironmental cues that govern fetal BM development is therefore of fundamental importance. Although recent studies have provided insights into the hematopoietic niche within the BM1,2, the mechanism by which the marrow and the surrounding bone structure develop in a coordinated manner during embryogenesis remains incompletely understood.

Most skeletal elements form through endochondral ossification, a process in which a cartilage template is progressively replaced by bone and BM3. This process is characterized by three tightly coupled events: (i) elimination of hypertrophic chondrocytes through apoptosis or transdifferentiation, (ii) invasion of blood vessels and perichondrium-derived cells into the cartilage, and (iii) deposition of cortical and trabecular bone matrix by osteoblasts. These events collectively occur within the developing primary ossification center (POC), whose expansion drives longitudinal bone growth. In mice, as the POC expands, chondro-osseous replacement proceeds, promoting longitudinal bone growth. In mice, BM formation is initiated by vascular invasion around embryonic day 15.5 (E15.5), followed by the entry of hematopoietic cells via the circulation at approximately E16.54. Concurrently, osteoblasts and BM stromal cells (BMSCs) arise from chondrocytes or perichondrial progenitors5–7. In animals in which vascular or mesenchymal cell invasion is impaired, formation and expansion of the medullary cavity are disrupted, resulting in defective entry of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and impaired establishment of hematopoiesis8.

Osteoclasts, bone-resident macrophages specialized for bone resorption, are essential for bone remodeling and homeostasis. Osteoclast differentiation strictly depends on two signaling pathways mediated by colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF1)9 and receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL)10. Mice lacking RANKL fail to generate osteoclasts, resulting in impaired BM cavity formation and compensatory extramedullary hematopoiesis11. These observations highlight the central role of RANKL-dependent osteoclastogenesis in establishing a functional marrow space.

RANKL has been shown to be expressed by cells of mesenchymal origin, a population that remains poorly characterized due to high phenotypic and functional heterogeneity in vivo. In this study, to define the cellular sources of RANKL during fetal bone development, we combined single-cell transcriptomics with inducible lineage tracing and identified CD55+CD90+ mesenchymal cells originating from the perichondrium as early sources of RANKL, whereas at later stages this role shifts to BMSCs distributed throughout the nascent marrow cavity. These osteoclastogenic populations partially share transcriptional signatures with septoclasts that re-emerge during fracture repair. Together, our findings uncover a conserved osteogenic program that orchestrates bone and BM formation across development and regeneration.

Results

POC is the site of osteoclast differentiation

In mouse femurs, BM development begins around E15.5, when CD45+ hematopoietic cells appear in the nascent POC among invading blood vessels, preceding HSC entry at E16.54 (Fig. 1a). Tnfsf11 (RANKL) and Csf1 were expressed at higher levels in the E15.5 diaphysis than in the epiphysis and metaphysis, indicating osteoclastogenic potential in the early POC (Fig. 1b). Fetal osteoclasts originate from yolk-sac-derived erythro-myeloid progenitors (EMPs)12,13. In Csf1rMer-icre-Mer; Rosa26LSL-tdTomato mice pulsed with tamoxifen at E8.5, CD45+tdTomato+ mononuclear cells appeared in the perichondrium at E13.5, and multinucleated CD45+tdTomato+ cells localized near CD31+ endothelial cells by E15.5 (Fig. 1c). In RANK-EGFP reporter mice (Tnfrsf11a-Cre-Ires-Egfp BAC Tg), these multinucleated cells expressed RANK and showed Tartrate-Resistant Acid Phosphatase (TRAP) activity (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Together, these data indicate that osteoclast induction begins in the early POC during vascular invasion.

Osteoclasts are essential for bone marrow cavity development

To examine the role of fetal osteoclasts, we analyzed bone from Tnfsf11Cre/Cre mice, in which RANKL expression is disrupted by Cre insertion14. TRAP staining confirmed the absence of osteoclasts, consistent with previous studies in RANKL- and RANK-deficient mice showing impaired vascular invasion, cartilage resorption, and hematopoietic cavity formation5,11 (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Immunohistochemistry showed a significant reduction of CD45+ and CD31+ areas in neonatal bone of Tnfsf11Cre/Cre (hereafter RANKL KO) mice (Fig. 1d, e). Flow cytometry confirmed significantly reduced CD45+ hematopoietic cells, CD31+ endothelial cells, and LepR+ BMSCs in BM from RANKL KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). At E15.5, von Kossa staining revealed similar calcification patterns in mutants and controls, restricted to cartilage matrix surrounding hypertrophic chondrocytes. From E16.5 onward, however, abnormally calcified deposits accumulated in the BM cavity of RANKL KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 1f, g). This phenotype could be attributed to defects in mesenchymal cell-derived RANKL, as a similar phenotype is observed in Prrx1-Cre; Tnfsf11flox/Δ mice (Fig. 1f, g). By late gestation, mutants exhibited reduced BM area and persistent cartilaginous tissue within the marrow cavity, as shown by von Kossa- and safranin O-staining (Supplementary Fig. 1h, i), indicating a failure to clear calcified and cartilaginous matrices required for hematopoietic space formation.

Together, these data demonstrate that mesenchymal-derived RANKL and the osteoclasts it induces are essential to expand medullary cavity volume and support hematopoietic cell colonization.

POC serves as a local source of RANKL

To identify RANKL-expressing cells in early developing bone, we conducted single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of E15.5 diaphyseal cells (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). We found that most Tnfsf11+ cells localized to the Col10a1+ hypertrophic chondrocyte cluster, with a smaller fraction in a Prrx1high mesenchymal cluster (Supplementary Fig. 2d). However, while Tnfsf11 expression in femoral hypertrophic chondrocytes of Col10a1Cre; Tnfsf11flox/Δ mice was markedly reduced (Supplementary Fig. 2e, f), Col10a1Cre; Tnfsf11flox/Δ mice exhibited no statistically significant differences in osteoclast development and BM cavity formation at E17.5 (Supplementary Fig. 2g, h). These findings suggest that fetal osteoclastogenesis depends on RANKL sources other than hypertrophic chondrocytes.

We then focused more closely on the early POC. In situ hybridization revealed Tnfsf11 expression in cells adjacent to Tnfrsf11a+ osteoclasts (Fig. 1h). To visualize and trace these cells, we used Tnfsf11tTA/+; LC1; Rosa26LSL-tdTomato mice, in which RANKL+ cells are permanently labeled (Fig. 1i). At E14.5, tdTomato+ cells were enriched in the posterior perichondrium (Fig. 1j), and by E15.5, they were closely associated with invading CD31+ vasculature and traversed the Col1a1-EGFP+ osteoblast layer (Fig. 1j, k). These findings suggest that perichondrial RANKL+ cells migrate into the POC along with the invading vasculature.

Osteoclastogenic cells line the osteochondral interface

In addition to RANKL, CSF1 is essential for osteoclastogenesis9. In Csf1tTA/+; LC1; Rosa26LSL-tdTomato mice15, tdTomato+ cells showed a broader distribution than in Tnfsf11tTA/+; LC1; Rosa26LSL-tdTomato mice, (Supplementary Fig. 3a, Fig. 1j), suggesting overlap but not identity between CSF1+ and RANKL+ populations. To resolve this, we generated double fate-mapping mice (Csf1VCre/+; Tnfsf11tTA/+; LC1; Rosa26LSL-tdTomato/VSV-EGFP) using orthogonal Cre/loxP and VCre/VloxP systems16,17 (Supplementary Fig. 3b). After confirming no cross-labeling between these systems (Supplementary Fig. 3c), we found that at E15.5, CSF1+ (EGFP+) and RANKL+ (tdTomato+) cells co-localized in wedge-shaped clusters migrating into the cartilage (Fig. 2a). Double-positive cells were enriched at the POC front, adjacent to CD45+ multinucleated osteoclasts. RNA-seq analysis confirmed that these cells co-express Csf1 and Tnfsf11, but not chondrocyte (Col2a1 and Col10a1) or osteoblast (Col1a1 and Bglap) markers (Supplementary Fig. 3d, Fig. 2b).

CD55+CD90+ cells contribute to fetal osteoclast development

To define the transcriptomic features of osteoclastogenic cells in the fetal POC, we performed scRNA-seq on RANKL fate-mapped cells at E15.5, hereafter E15-RANKL cells. After quality control, unsupervised clustering identified five clusters (Fig. 2c, d, Supplementary Fig. 4a): perichondrial cells (cluster 0; Aspn and Postn), osteoblasts (cluster 3; Sp7, Col1a1, and Bglap), pre-osteoblasts (cluster 1; lower Sp7 and Alpl), immature BMSCs (cluster 2; Ebf1, Cxcl12, and Kitl), and a distinct population (cluster 4) expressing Csf1, Tnfsf11, and matrix metalloproteinases (Mmp9, Mmp13, and Mmp14), which have been reported to contribute to cartilage matrix degradation18–23. Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis revealed Cd55 and Thy1 as specific markers for cluster 4 (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 4a). Flow cytometry subdivided E15-RANKL cells based on CD55 and CD90 expression (Fig. 2e). RNA-seq analysis confirmed that CD55+CD90+ cells expressed the highest levels of Csf1, Tnfsf11, and Mmp transcripts, suggesting strong osteoclastogenic and matrix-degrading potential (Fig. 2f). Immunohistochemistry localized CD55+tdTomato+ and MMP9+tdTomato+ cells to the chondro-osseous border (Fig. 2g, h). Analysis of Cd55-Cre knock-in mice further revealed that Cd55-Cre+ cells were predominantly positioned adjacent to multinucleated osteoclasts (Supplementary Fig. 4b).

To assess function, we generated Cd55Cre/+; Tnfsf11flox/Δ mice. In these Cd55-Cre-mediated RANKL-deficient embryos, TRAP+ osteoclasts were markedly reduced, accompanied by delayed POC expansion (Fig. 2i, j). Together, these results identify CD55+CD90+ mesenchymal cells as key drivers of early osteoclast development, vascular invasion, and medullary cavity formation.

RANKL is expressed by BMSCs from late gestation through adolescence

By E17.5, despite increased calcified deposits, TRAP+ areas in Cd55-Cre RANKL-deficient femurs remained comparable to those in controls (Fig. 3a, b), indicating that compensatory RANKL sources preserved BM space in these mice.

To trace the temporal contribution of RANKL-expressing cells, we performed period-restricted labeling in Tnfsf11tTA/+; LC1;Rosa26LSL-tdTomato mice (Fig. 1i). The persistence of RANKL-expressing cells beyond E15.5 was evident from the appearance of tdTomato+ cells following Dox withdrawal (Fig. 3c), with further expansion upon continuous labeling through P1 (Fig. 3d). When labeling was terminated at E15.5, tdTomato+ E15-RANKL cells were broadly distributed throughout the BM at P1 (Fig. 3e). At E17.5, these tdTomato+ cells included Osterix (Sp7)+ osteoblasts, LepR+ BMSCs, and NG2+ periarterial cells (Fig. 3f), highlighting the heterogeneity of E15-RANKL cell descendants. In contrast, termination of labeling at E14.5 restricted tdTomato+ cells predominantly to the perichondrium/periosteum, with few detected in BM at E15.5 or P1 (Fig. 3g), suggesting that outer and inner perichondrial cells at E14.5 follow distinct developmental fates.

scRNA-seq analysis of E15-RANKL cells at E15.5 (Fig. 2c), E17.5 and P3 revealed six transcriptionally distinct clusters: osteoblasts, Cxcl12low BMSCs, Cxcl12high BMSCs, immature cells, periosteal cells, and pericytes (Fig. 3h–j, Supplementary Fig. 5a). Periosteal cells (cluster 4) remained transcriptionally distinct across all time points. Fate-mapped E15-RANKL cells were initially enriched in the immature cluster (cluster 3), and progressively transitioned toward more differentiated states from E15.5 through E17.5 to P3 (Fig. 3i).

Consistent with scRNA-seq data showing that Tnfsf11 and Csf1 expression was largely restricted to Cxcl12low and Cxcl12high BMSCs (Fig. 3j), bulk RNA-seq analysis further revealed predominant expression of these osteoclastogenic factors in LepR+ BMSCs isolated from P2 fate-mapped mice (Supplementary Fig. 5b). In situ hybridization of E18.5 femurs confirmed Tnfsf11 expression in non-osteoblast Col1a1-EGFP- regions adjacent to Tnfrsf11a+ osteoclasts (Fig. 3k). TRAP+ osteoclasts were in direct contact with LepR+ BMSCs but not with Col1a1-EGFP+ osteoblasts (Fig. 3l). These findings are consistent with a model in which LepR+ BMSCs provide osteoclastogenic cues during late fetal and early postnatal periods.

In postnatal bone, RANKL is expressed by several mesenchymal sources, including osteoblasts, osteocytes, hypertrophic chondrocytes, and BMSCs24. To assess the contribution of osteoblasts to BM cavity formation, we used the Col1a1-Cre line, which employs an osteoblast-specific Col1a1(2.3 kb) promoter to drive Cre expression25. In 3-week-old Col1a1-Cre; Tnfsf11flox/flox mice, osteoblasts expressed significantly lower levels of Tnfsf11 than the corresponding population in control mice, confirming efficient Cre-mediated reduction of RANKL in this model (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). In contrast to Prrx1-Cre; Tnfsf11flox/Δ mice (Fig. 1f, g), Col1a1-Cre; Tnfsf11flox/flox mice displayed no overt defects in osteoclastogenesis and BM cavity development (Supplementary Fig. 5e, f). Thus, osteoblast-derived RANKL makes only a minimal contribution to osteoclast maintenance during fetal bone development, compared with LepR+ BMSCs and other mesenchymal subsets.

BMSCs continuously provide RANKL in adult bone marrow

We next examined the distribution of RANKL-expressing cells in the postnatal BM. At P21, E15-RANKL cells were rare and predominantly localized to the diaphysis, with few present in the metaphysis (Fig. 4a, b). In contrast, P1-derived RANKL fate-mapped cells (hereafter P1-RANKL cells) were broadly distributed throughout the bone, including the metaphyseal region (Fig. 4a, b). Consistent with a postnatal lineage shift, E15-RANKL cells at P21 were confined to diaphyseal stromal cells, including LepR+ and NG2+ populations, with no detectable contribution to the Sp7+ osteoblast lineage, whereas Sp7+ cells were observed in continuously labeled mice (Fig. 4c). Notably, at P21, around 80% of LepR+ BMSCs originated from RANKL+ progenitors that emerged after E15.5, indicating that RANKL+ precursors arising post-E15.5, particularly postnatal period, give rise to adult LepR+ BMSCs (Fig. 4d, e, Supplementary Fig. 6a, b).

By 8 weeks of age, E15-RANKL cells retained a distribution and heterogeneity similar to those observed at P21 (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). In contrast, the overall frequency of RANKL-expressing cells increased from P21 to adulthood in continuously labeled mice (Fig. 4f, g). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that LepR+ BMSCs are continuously replenished from postnatal RANKL+ progenitors and serve as a sustained source of RANKL in the adult bone marrow.

E15-CD55+ cells give rise to septoclasts in early developing bone

Based on their robust expression of osteoclastogenic and matrix-degrading genes, together with their localization at the chondro-osseous border, we hypothesized that CD55⁺CD90⁺ cells within POC contribute to endochondral bone development. We compared these cells with septoclasts—cartilage-degrading mesenchymal cells at the growth plate border26–28—and identified Fabp5, Mmp9, and Ctsb as shared markers (Fig. 2d). Immunostaining confirmed FABP5 expression in CD55⁺ E15-RANKL cells (Fig. 5a), which by E17.5 had relocated to the chondro-osseous interface while maintaining FABP5 expression (Fig. 5b). These observations raise the possibility that FABP5+ cells in developing bone originate from perichondrium.

To test this possibility, we next examined the developmental origins of FABP5+ cells. Lineage-tracing experiments demonstrated that FABP5+ cells are derived predominantly from E14.5 Dlx5+ perichondrial cells, with minimal contribution from E13.5 Fgfr3+ chondrocytes7 (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b), indicating a perichondrial origin.

Postnatal septoclasts give rise to multiple stromal lineages

To follow the fate of Fabp5-expressing cells in a temporally controlled manner, we generated Fabp5CreERT2/+; Rosa26 LSL-tdTomato mice (Fig. 5c). Tamoxifen administration at E15.5 labeled Fabp5+ cells that localized to the metaphyseal chondro-osseous junction at E18.5 and retained FABP5 expression (Fig. 5c). Notably, numerous tdTomato+ descendants were observed in the metaphyseal region (Fig. 5c). Although FABP5+ cells labeled at E15.5 were no longer detectable at the growth plate border by P21 (Fig. 5d), mirroring the transient fate of E15-RANKL cells (Supplementary Fig. 7c), FABP5+ cells labeled at P14 persisted through P21 and gave rise to LepR+ BMSCs and/or Sp7+ osteoblasts (Fig. 5e–h).

Together, these data indicate that E15 CD55⁺CD90⁺ cells arise from the early perichondrium and transiently populate the developing chondro-osseous junction. Their postnatal counterparts, FABP5+ septoclasts, localize to this interface and can give rise to stromal and osteogenic progeny in postnatal bone.

Osteoclastogenic cells re-emerge during fracture healing

Endochondral ossification driven by cartilage resorption is essential for fracture healing29. During this process, FABP5+ septoclasts re-emerge and promote cartilage resorption through MMPs26. Given the transcriptional similarity between fetal CD55+CD90+ cells and FABP5+ septoclasts, we hypothesized that FABP5+ septoclasts re-emerging during fracture repair retain fetal-like osteoclastogenic properties.

To test this hypothesis, we reanalyzed a published scRNA-seq dataset of murine mesenchymal cells following bone fracture (GSE154247)26 (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Fig. 8a). This analysis revealed the emergence of Fabp5+ populations co-expressing Tnfsf11, Csf1, and Mmp9 (Fig. 6b, c, Supplementary Fig. 8b), suggesting an osteoclastogenic and matrix-degrading transcriptional program.

To examine the spatial relationship between osteoclasts and FABP5+ cells, we employed a tibial fracture model using Tnfsf11tTA/+; LC1; Rosa26LSL-tdTomato mice, in which tdTomato labeling was induced by Dox withdrawal one week before fracture (Figs. 1i, 6d). Histological analysis revealed that, two weeks after fracture, ELF97+ osteoclasts were localized adjacent to FABP5+ septoclasts lining the chondro-osseous boundary within the tibial callus containing cartilaginous tissue (Fig. 6e, Supplementary Fig. 8c). In unfractured tibiae, FABP5+ septoclasts and tdTomato+ cells were enriched in the metaphyseal region (Supplementary Fig. 8d). In contrast, following fracture, tdTomato+FABP5+ cells and tdTomato+LepR+ BMSCs accumulated within the diaphyseal callus of fractured bone (Fig. 6f, g, Supplementary Fig. 8e). Approximately 35% of tdTomato+ cells were LepR+ (Supplementary Fig. 8f), suggesting that both FABP5+ septoclasts and LepR+ BMSCs contribute to the pool of RANKL-expressing cells that support osteoclast-mediated bone remodeling during fracture repair.

To further define the identity of RANKL-expressing cells during fracture repair, we performed scRNA-seq analysis of tdTomato+ cells isolated from fractured bone. This analysis identified two major osteoclastogenic clusters—Cxcl12high and Cxcl12low BMSCs—both of which co-expressed Tnfsf11 and Csf1 (Fig. 6i–k, Supplementary Fig. 8g). Higher resolution reanalysis of these populations resolved four transcriptionally distinct subsets: Adipoqlow (cluster 0), Alpl+ Fabp5- (cluster 1), Adipoqhigh (cluster 2), and Fabp5+Mmp9+ (cluster 3) (Fig. 6l, m). Based on their characteristic gene expression profiles, cluster 1 corresponds to Osteo-CARs, cluster 0 and 2 to Adipo-CARs, and cluster 3 to septoclasts26,30,31. Notably, all four populations exhibit an osteoclastogenic signature in the fracture setting, supporting the notion that multiple stromal cell types cooperatively provide RANKL and CSF1 to promote osteoclastogenesis during bone regeneration.

Together, these findings suggest that fracture healing involves reactivation of a fetal-like ossification program in which FABP5+ septoclasts and BMSCs cooperatively provide osteoclastogenic cues through RANKL and CSF1, thereby coordinating cartilage resorption with bone regeneration.

Discussion

The transition from the prenatal to postnatal environment imposes profound physiological challenges on mammals. In this study, we examined perinatal BM cavity formation as a critical developmental process establishing the physical architecture and cellular niche required for postnatal hematopoietic expansion.

First, we identify key cellular components that orchestrate perinatal BM cavity development during endochondral ossification. Our data indicate that osteoclastogenic support is not static but instead undergoes a developmental functional handoff. During early fetal stages, osteoclastogenesis is supported by CD55+CD90+ mesenchymal cells localized to the perichondrium, whereas at later stages this role shifts to BMSCs broadly distributed throughout the nascent marrow cavity. This temporal reorganization of osteoclastogenic niches appears to be essential for progressive cavity expansion and for establishing a permissive environment for postnatal hematopoiesis. Consistently, only limited impairment of BM cavity formation in CD55-lineage-specific RANKL-deficient mice indicates the existence of a multi-layered osteoclastogenic program during bone development.

Notably, elements of this developmental osteoclastogenic program are redeployed in the adult bone fracture repair. We observed the emergence of RANKL-expressing stromal populations and septoclasts, suggesting that fetal mechanisms governing matrix remodeling and cavity formation are reactivated during skeletal regeneration. Together, these findings support a model in which septoclasts and BMSCs cooperatively establish osteoclastogenic niches in contexts requiring rapid bone remodeling, including both fetal ossification and postnatal fracture healing.

Recent studies have identified two major subsets of Cxcl12-abundant-reticular (CAR) BMSCs in adult BM: Adipoq+ CARs (Adipo-CARs, also termed as marrow adipogenic lineage precursors, MALPs) and Alpl+ CARs (Osteo-CARs). Importantly, Adipo-CARs, which produce high levels of RANKL30,31 are indispensable for trabecular bone remodeling during homeostasis and postmenopausal bone loss32,33. In this context, our fate mapping analyses unexpectedly revealed a developmental connection between postnatal Fabp5+ cells and LepR+ BMSCs, a compartment that largely encompasses Adipo-CARs. Although this relationship does not establish a direct lineage hierarchy, the shared osteoclastogenic features and partial lineage overlap raise the possibility that Fabp5+ septoclasts and Adipo-CARs are governed by common transcriptional programs regulating RANKL and CSF1 expression. Moreover, accumulation of multiple RANKL-expressing populations—including Alpl+Fabp5− BMSCs (Osteo-CARs), Adipoqhigh BMSCs (Adipo-CARs), and Fabp5+Mmp9+ septoclasts—within the fracture callus suggests that overlapping regulatory mechanisms control RANKL expression across distinct contexts, namely bone development and regeneration. Elucidating the molecular basis of these osteoclastogenic programs, including postnatal epigenomic regulation of RANKL expression, will be an important direction of future studies.

Finally, although we identify CD55+CD90+ cells as the earliest osteoclastogenic population in the fetal bone, the mechanisms that activate this program remain incompletely defined. Notably, the transcription factor ETS1—previously implicated in septoclast-associated matrix remodeling34 and RANKL induction in inflammatory fibroblasts35—was selectively enriched in CD55+CD90+ cells. This raises the possibility that ETS1 acts as a shared upstream regulator coordinating matrix-degrading and osteoclastogenic signaling during fetal bone development. Defining the in vivo functional contribution of ETS1 will be an important goal for future work.

In summary, our study delineates the cellular hierarchy of the early POC and reveals a conserved osteoclastogenic program that links skeletal remodeling with hematopoietic niche formation. These findings provide a conceptual framework for understanding how developmental ossification processes are repurposed during adult bone repair.

Fracture model

For fracture healing experiments, the right tibia was surgically fractured, while the contralateral tibia served as an untreated control. Three-week-old mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of a three-agent anesthetic mixture consisting of medetomidine (0.75 mg/kg), midazolam (4 mg/kg), and butorphanol (5 mg/kg). After skin incision, the surrounding muscle tissue was carefully detached from the tibia using a periosteal elevator to expose the bone. The exposed tibia was transected using surgical scissors. A fixation pin (PRE-30025, TSK laboratory) was inserted from the proximal tibial head, and the wound was closed with sutures (F17-40B2, Akiyama). Anesthesia was reversed by intraperitoneal injection of atipamezole (0.75 mg/kg).

Histological analysis

For embryonic and early postnatal samples (≤ postnatal day 1), hind limbs were dissected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight at 4 °C, rinsed, and stored in PBS for one day. Samples were then immersed in 30% sucrose in PBS overnight, embedded in O.C.T. compound (Sakura FineTek, 4583-B), and frozen. Cryosections were prepared using a TissueTek Polar cryostat (Sakura FineTek) at a thickness of 30 μm for confocal microscopy or 14 μm for TRAP, von Kossa, and Safranin O staining. For juvenile and adult samples (≥3 weeks), femora or tibiae were dissected and fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C, rinsed in PBS, and decalcified in 0.5 M EDTA (pH 8.0) at 4 °C for 2–5 days with gentle agitation, with EDTA replacement every three days. Samples were subsequently cryoprotected, embedded and sectioned as described above. Stabilization pins were removed from fractured tibiae prior to fixation. Sections were mounted on a MAS-coated glass slide (Matsunami Glass Ind., MAS-01).

For immunohistochemistry, frozen sections were rehydrated in PBS-TS (0.1% TritonX-100/1% FBS in PBS) and blocked in 10% FBS/PBS-TS for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were incubated with primary antibody (1:100) at 4 °C overnight, followed by secondary antibodies (1:250) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Dojindo Molecular Technologies Inc., D523) or TO-PRO-3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, T3605), and sections were mounted with Fluoromount-G (SouthernBiotech, 0100-01). Antibodies used for immunofluorescence staining is as follows: CD45.2 (TONBO, clone 104, 0-0454-U100), CD31 (BD Pharmingen, clone MEC13.3, 553370), CD55 (R&D Systems, BAF5376), CD90.2 (TONBO, clone 30-H12, 75-0903), MMP9 (R&D Systems, BAF909), LepR (R&D Systems, BAF497), Ter119 (BioLegend, clone TER-119, 116211), Cre (Cell Signaling Technology, clone D7L7L, 13-5921), SP7 (Abcam, ab22552), NG2 (Merck Millipore, AB5320), GFP (ThermoFisher scientific, A10262), FABP5 (Cell Signaling Technology, 39926 T), Donkey anti-Rat IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor™ 488 (Thermo Fisher scientific, A21208), Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor™ 488 (Thermo Fisher scientific, A21206), Goat anti-Chicken IgY (H + L) Alexa Fluor™ 488 (Thermo Fisher scientific, A11039), Goat anti-Rat IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor™ 555 (Thermo Fisher scientific, A21434), Goat anti-Rat IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor™ 647 (Thermo Fisher scientific, A21247), Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor™ 647 (Thermo Fisher scientific, A31573), Streptavidin Brilliant Violet 421 (BioLegend, 405225), Streptavidin PE (TONBO, 50-4317-U500).

Reactions were monitored microscopically and terminated by rinsing with water. Sections were counterstained with TO-PRO-3 and mounted with Fluoromount-G for imaging.

Von Kossa staining was performed on 14-μm frozen sections using a commercial silver staining kit (Merck Millipore, 100362) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, followed by counterstaining with 0.1% Nuclear Fast Red solution (Merck Millipore, 100121) and mounted using G-mount (Genostaff, GM-01) and Softmount (FUJIFILM Wako, 192-16301).

The sections were counterstained with 0.08% fast green (FUJIFILM Wako, 061-00031) for 5 min then rinsed in 1% acetic acid and water. The sections were further dried and submerged in xylene and mounted using Softmount (FUJIFILM Wako).

The sections were further dried and submerged in xylene and mounted using Softmount (FUJIFILM Wako).

Bulk RNA-seq analysis

Cells enzymatically dissociated from femora, tibiae and humeri of mice at the indicated age were directly sorted into the reaction mix of the SMART-Seq HT PLUS kit (Takara Bio, R400749). mRNA from 20–70 sorted cells per sample was reverse-transcribed and amplified for 16 cycles to generate sufficient cDNA libraries for next-generation sequencing.

Processing of scRNA-seq data

Raw sequencing data (E15_2021_Jul_bone) were demultiplexed and aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10), and UMI counting was performed using Cell Ranger v6.0.1 (10x Genomics). Downstream analyses were conducted using Seurat v4.050.

Cells with fewer than 500 genes or with mitochondrial gene content exceeding 5% were excluded. Data were normalized and scaled using standard Seurat workflows. Two thousand highly variable genes were identified using FindVariableFeatures, and data scaling was performed using ScaleData. PCA was performed on the scaled data. Unless otherwise stated, the top 50 principal components were used for graph-based clustering using the Louvain algorithm (FindNeighbors and FindClusters, resolution 0.2–0.4). UMAP was used for dimensionality reduction. Differentially expressed genes were identified using FindAllMarkers. Clusters exhibiting low gene complexity and mixed lineage marker expression were classified as low quality and excluded, and the filtered data were reprocessed through the same analytical pipeline.

A cluster containing substantial contributions from E15-RANKL-tdTomato datasets were defined as “E15-RANKL cluster” and isolated for downstream analyses. Subsequent reclustering identified four clusters, one of which was excluded due to erythroid lineage contamination.

Merged analysis of the perinatal RANKL-Tomato datasets

Processing and analysis of fracture scRNA-seq datasets

Public scRNA-seq data (GSE154247) and fracture scRNA-seq data generated in this study (GSE315785) were processed using Seurat v5 in R v4.5.1. For reanalysis of GSE154247, sparse gene-by-cell count matrices were constructed and converted into Seurat objects, retaining genes detected in at least three cells and cells expressing more than 200 genes. For GSE315785, scRNA-seq data were generated using the CellRanger pipeline, and Seurat objects were created using the same criteria. For all samples, quality control metrics included detected gene number, total UMI counts, and mitochondrial gene content. For GSE154247, cells <500 or >6000 genes, or >25% mitochondrial content were excluded. For GSE315785, cells with <200 genes, >30,000 UMIs, or >15% mitochondrial gene content were excluded. Cell cycle scores were calculated using CellCycleScoring, and datasets were normalized using SCTransform with regression of S and G2/M scores. After normalization, 3000 highly variable features were selected using SelectIntegrationFeatures. Datasets were prepared for integration using PrepSCTIntegration, integration anchors were identified using FindIntegrationAnchors, and integrated expression matrices were generated using IntegrateData. PCA was performed on the integrated data, and the first 30 principal components were used for downstream analyses. UMAP was used for visualization. Cell–cell neighborhood graphs were constructed using FindNeighbors, and unsupervised clustering was performed using the Louvain algorithm with a resolution of 0.1. Differentially expressed genes were identified using FindAllMarkers on the SCT assay, considering genes with a minimum detection rate of 25% and a log₂ fold-change ≥0.25.

Image processing and quantification

Bright-field images were acquired using an LSM780 microscope with an Axiocam305 color camera, controlled by AxioVision software (Carl Zeiss). Fluorescence images were obtained using LSM780 or LSM980 confocal microscopes (Carl Zeiss). Images for ELF97 staining quantification were acquired using an Axio Vert.A1 microscope (Carl Zeiss). Images were processed using ZEN Black and ZEN Blue (Carl Zeiss), Adobe Photoshop (Adobe), and ImageJ. All confocal images are shown as maximum-intensity projections. Within each experiment, identical laser excitation and detection settings were applied across samples. Image quantification was performed using ImageJ. For CD31 and CD45 immunostaining, binary images were generated, and the total stained area within the POC was quantified. For von Kossa staining, binary images were generated, and particles smaller than 500 μm2 within the POC were counted. For TRAP staining, binary images were generated, and the total stained area was quantified. For ELF97 staining, binary images were generated to quantify both the total stained area and the number of particles larger than 500 μm2. The POC area in each section was manually defined.

Graphical representation

Schematic illustrations were created using BioRender (https://www.biorender.com).

Statistics and reproducibility

Data are presented as mean ± SEM, unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v9.5. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using unpaired two-tailed t-test; when variance was significantly unequal (as assessed by F test), the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. Normality was not formally tested. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Sample sizes are indicated in figure legends, and no statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes. No covariates were tested or included in the statistical analysis. Samples were excluded only in cases of failed genotype identification or technical issues resulting in sample damage. Histological analyses were performed on femora from at least three mice derived from two or more independent litters. Bulk RNA-seq experiments were conducted using two or more biologically independent samples. scRNA-seq experiments were repeated multiple times, as described in the Method section, due to low cell yields. For each scRNA-seq library, cells from three or more animals were pooled.
