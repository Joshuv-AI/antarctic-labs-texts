Design of model Boger fluids with systematically controlled viscoelastic properties Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Jonghyun Hwang and Howard A. Stone

Abstract
The subject of viscoelastic flow phenomena is crucial to many areas of engineering and the physical sciences. Although much of our understanding of viscoelastic flow features stem from carefully designed experiments, preparation of model viscoelastic fluids remains an outstanding challenge; for example, fabricating a series of fluids with varied fluid shear moduli G 0 G_{0} , but with an identical relaxation time τ \tau , is nontrivial. In this work, we harness the non-ideality of nearly constant-viscosity elastic fluids (commonly known as ‘Boger fluids’) made with polyisobutylene to develop an experimental methodology that produces a set of fluids with desired viscoelastic properties, specifically, G 0 G_{0} , τ \tau , and the first normal stress difference coefficient ψ 1 \psi_{1} . Through a simple linear algebraic manipulation between the rheological properties of interest ( G 0 G_{0} , τ \tau , ψ 1 \psi_{1} ) and the fluid compositions in terms of polymer concentration c c , molecular weight M w M_{w} , and solvent viscosity η s \eta_{s} , we developed a ‘design equation’ that takes G 0 G_{0} , τ \tau , ψ 1 \psi_{1} as inputs and calculates values for c c , M w M_{w} , η s \eta_{s} as outputs. Using this method, fabrication of sample viscoelastic fluids whose rheological properties are a priori known can be achieved.



I
INTRODUCTION

One fundamental limitation in working with polymeric materials is the difficulty in predicting or estimating their mechanical and rheological properties before the samples are fabricated. For example, varying one design parameter, such as the molecular weight of the polymer, affects both the modulus
G_{0}
as well as the relaxation time
\tau
of the fabricated material in distinct ways. Meanwhile, in many viscoelastic flow experiments, polymer concentration is often varied, as changing the concentration offers convenience in sample preparation and allows probing the effect of fluid elasticity. However, a change in polymer concentration not only varies
G_{0}
, but also affects the overall solution viscosity, the time scale of the fluid, and therefore also the normal stress effects of the fluid. Such common practices make it challenging to distinguish the mechanical and chemical origins of observed differences in fluid flow behavior.

Many interesting viscoelastic fluid phenomena are affected or governed by the combination of
G_{0}
and
\tau
. For example,
G_{0}\tau
provides a measure of the polymeric contribution to the solution viscosity and
G_{0}\tau^{2}
provides a measure of the additional stress (though not the dimensions of stress) caused by the deformation of the polymer. These features further obfuscate attempts to understand the mechanical nature of polymeric fluids. Hence, we identified a need for investigation of the effect of varying the fluid design parameters for a viscoelastic solution, such as polymer concentration
c
, polymer molecular weight
M
, and solvent composition and viscosity
\eta_{\text{s}}
. More importantly, we developed a simple method to precisely design viscoelastic fluids, in this case polymer solutions, with desired viscoelastic properties.

Characterizing mechanical and rheological properties of polymer solutions presents various challenges. For example, polymer interactions at high concentrations of polymers make the dynamics of the fluid hard to interpret. Therefore, to highlight single polymer kinetics, studies often are in the dilute limit, where the concentration of the polymer is near-vanishing. Nonetheless, in the low polymer concentration limit, experimental measurements become challenging as a shear rheometer is often incapable of collecting reliable torque readings
Ewoldt et al. 2015
.

Despite these limitations, single polymer kinetics can provide important insights about the characteristics of the material, such as polymer-solvent interactions, hydrodynamic interactions, and polymer conformations
Larson and Magda 1989
;
Schroeder et al. 2003
. Such knowledge can become a basis for understanding the behavior of polymers for concentrations above the dilute limit.

Specifically, when a fluid has only a small concentration of polymer, the composition of the solution can be tuned to fabricate an idealized polymer solution whose viscosity stays nearly constant with respect to the fluid shear rate. Such materials, called ‘Boger fluids’
Boger 1977
, are made by mixing a dilute concentration of polymers in a compatible, high viscosity solvent. Some examples of Boger fluids that are widely used in flow experiments include (1) polyacrylamide dissolved in glycerol
Jackson et al. 1984
;
Boger 1977
;
Chen and Datta 2025
, (2) polystyrene dissolved in styrene oligomers
Rothstein and McKinley 1999
;
Magda et al. 1993
;
Clasen et al. 2006
;
Larson 2005
, and (3) polyisobutylene dissolved in polybutene (high fraction of isobutylene oligomers)
Verhoef et al. 1999
;
Liu et al. 2011
;
Su et al. 2022
. These fluids offer experimental and theoretical benefits by tending to decouple the effect of shear thinning from effects driven by fluid elasticity (e.g., the first normal stress difference
N_{1}
). Hence, Boger fluids have been used in various viscoelastic flow experiments, such as polymer flows in porous media
Chen and Datta 2025
, and in providing a simulated viscoelastic environment for active materials
Liu et al. 2011
;
Su et al. 2022
.
However, despite its usefulness in experiments, viscoelastic properties of Boger fluids are not
a priori
known prior to the sample fabrication. For example, it remains a challenge to fabricate a series of fluids with different shear moduli
G_{0}
but with an identical relaxation time
\tau
.

In this article, we use the tools available from the classical literature of fluid viscoelasticity to design Boger fluids and control their rheological properties systematically. First, in §
II
, we discuss the fabrication methods for polyisobutylene-based Boger fluids and the applicability of the Zimm model for describing the rheology. Second, in §
III
, we identify and experimentally verify the scaling relationships
Rubinstein and Colby 2003
between the rheological properties, i.e., shear modulus
G_{0}
, relaxation time
\tau
, polymer-solvent interaction parameter
\chi
, as well as the first normal stress difference coefficient
\psi_{1}
, and the fluid design parameters, i.e., polymer concentration
c
, polymer molecular weight
M
, and the solvent viscosity
\eta_{\text{s}}
. We also point out that the Zimm approximation
More et al. 2023
for
\psi_{1}=2\chi G_{0}\tau^{2}
overestimates the actual
\psi_{1}
by a constant factor
\delta=O(\chi)
, such that
\psi_{1,~\text{measured}}=\delta\psi_{1,~\text{predicted}}
. Lastly in §
IV
, we introduce a framework to achieve target values for
G_{0}
,
\tau
, and
\psi_{1}
by changing the fluid composition.

The ideas presented in this work can be useful in ways other than designing Boger fluids with target viscoelastic properties. For example, our approach can provide an alternative way of inferring/estimating viscoelastic properties of fluids, whose measurements are below the torque resolution of a shear rheometer, from the measured values of the viscoelastic properties of other fluids that provide higher torque readings. Furthermore, our method can be used to test theories for viscoelastic flows that are based on Oldroyd-B or FENE-P type models, which assume weak viscoelasticity and dilute polymer concentrations. This knowledge combined with other rheometrical methods may also allow inference of other material parameters; for example, our recent work
Hu et al. 2025
suggests that the characterization introduced here, combined with the capillary breakup rheometry (CaBER) method, can provide a measure of extensibility of polymer molecules.

MATERIAL CHARACTERIZATION

II.1
Material preparation and rheological measurements

We begin by introducing the ingredients of our work and our approach for characterizing mechanical properties. The model Boger fluids are made with polyisobutylene (PIB) as the polymer, and a mixture of polybutene (PB) and light mineral oil as the solvent. II.3
Zimm rheology

Many of the widely used formulations of Boger fluids exhibit viscoelastic characteristics that follow predictions of the Zimm model
Zimm 1956
, which assumes a solution with a dilute polymer concentration, where the dispersed polymer chains hydrodynamically interact with the surrounding solvent
Rubinstein and Colby 2003
. In particular, the Zimm model considers the polymer and the volume of solvent surrounding the polymer to translocate similar to how ‘solid’-like objects move inside a viscous solvent. Hence, the presence of polymers modifies the overall solution viscosity similar to how solid spherical particles dispersed in a liquid increase the effective solution viscosity. Therefore, the change in the solution viscosity due to the presence of this ‘polymer ball’ is sensitive to the radius of gyration of the polymers, and this size effect is captured by the inverse of the polymer fractal dimension,
\nu
, which is also related to the polymer-solvent interaction parameter
More et al. 2023

\chi=1-1/(3\nu)
. We note that the Rouse model, which is often discussed in conjunction with the Zimm model, is not applicable in our study, as the Rouse model is more appropriate to discussions of polymer melts.

There are many polymer solutions that are known to exhibit Zimm-like dynamics. For example, experiments show that dynamic moduli,
G^{*}(\omega)
, of polyisobutylene-polybutene-based Boger fluids closely follow the three-parameter model that Zimm theory describes, comprised of a shear modulus
G_{0}
, Zimm relaxation time
\tau
, and polymer-solvent interaction parameter
\chi(\nu)
introduced in the previous paragraph
More et al. 2023
. However, fitting a three-parameter model to a set of two curves, e.g., storage
G^{\prime}(\omega)
and loss
G^{\prime\prime}(\omega)
moduli, where
\omega
is the angular shear frequency, leaves room for ambiguity; multiple sets of distinct values for
G_{0},\tau
, and
\chi
can yield acceptable fitting results. One way to avoid this multiplicity is to select an appropriate combination of the polymer and solvent with an
a priori
known value of
\chi
. For example, polystyrene dissolved in dioctyl phthalate is at the
\theta
-condition
Johnson et al. 1970
at 22
∘
C, which constrains
\chi=1/3
, or
\nu=1/2
. However, this method relies on the availability of such known chemical compositions, and often
\theta
-solvents can be toxic, volatile, or hard to work with.

Specifically, the Zimm model expresses the dynamic moduli,
G^{\prime}(\omega)
and
G^{\prime\prime}(\omega)
of a fluid, as
Rubinstein and Colby 2003

G^{\prime}(\omega)=G_{0}\frac{\omega\tau~\text{sin}[\chi~\text{arctan}(\omega\tau)]}{[1+(\omega\tau)^{2}]^{\chi/2}},

(2a)

G^{\prime\prime}(\omega)-\eta_{\text{s}}\omega=G_{0}\frac{\omega\tau~\text{cos}[\chi~\text{arctan}(\omega\tau)]}{[1+(\omega\tau)^{2}]^{\chi/2}},

(2b)

where the complex modulus is defined as
G^{*}(\omega)=G^{\prime}(\omega)+iG^{\prime\prime}(\omega)
. Eq. (
2a
) predicts that at high oscillation frequencies (
\omega\tau\gg 1
) the moduli vary as

\lim_{\omega\tau\gg 1}G^{\prime}(\omega)\approx G_{0}~\text{sin}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\chi\right)(\omega\tau)^{1-\chi},

(3a)

\lim_{\omega\tau\gg 1}G^{\prime\prime}(\omega)-\eta_{\text{s}}\omega\approx G_{0}~\text{cos}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\chi\right)(\omega\tau)^{1-\chi}.

(3b)

Likewise, at low frequencies (
\omega\tau\ll 1
), the asymptotic behaviors of
G^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime\prime}
are

\lim_{\omega\tau\ll 1}G^{\prime}(\omega)\approx G_{0}\chi(\omega\tau)^{2},

(4a)

\lim_{\omega\tau\ll 1}G^{\prime\prime}(\omega)-\eta_{\text{s}}\omega\approx G_{0}(\omega\tau).

(4b)

These asymptotic limits suggest that with an appropriate method for fitting the experimentally measured curves of
G^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime\prime}
as a function of
\omega
, the three control parameters (
G_{0},\tau,\chi
) can be identified independently. To demonstrate this approach to material property measurement, we prepared a solution of

The process we used to identify the shear modulus
G_{0}
, the longest relaxation time
\tau
(Zimm relaxation time), and the polymer-solvent interaction parameter
\chi
is as follows. We first begin by identifying
\chi
. For both of the
G^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime\prime}
curves, Eq. (
3
) predicts
G^{\prime},G^{\prime\prime}-\eta_{s}\omega\propto\omega^{1-\chi}
for
\omega\tau\gg 1
. However, the fitting of
G^{\prime\prime}-\eta_{s}\omega
is sensitive to the quality of the measurement of the solvent viscosity, and the
G^{\prime}
data tend to curve upward at high values of
\omega
instead of increasing at a constant rate. This increase of
G^{\prime}
has also been observed in other Boger fluids fabricated with oligomeric solvents; one study
Clasen et al. 2006
suggests that such a variation indicates the existence of shorter relaxation modes coming from the weak elasticity of the oligomeric solvent. These features make power-law fitting challenging in solutions made with low polymer concentrations or low polymer molecular weights, because the region of the data points with a consistent power-law increase becomes narrower.

Instead, we examine the ratio of
G^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime\prime}
at high frequencies to find

\chi=\lim_{\omega\tau\gg 1}\frac{2}{\pi}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{G^{\prime}}{G^{\prime\prime}-\eta_{\text{s}}\omega}\right),

(5)

and identify a region where
\chi(\omega)
remains at a constant value. An example of applying this method is illustrated in Fig.
2
(a,
ii
). Since
\tan(\chi)
is expected to plateau at a constant value when both
G^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime\prime}
increase at the same rate, we seek the point where
d\chi/d\omega=0
, or the smallest (slope,
\geq 0
). Since the measurements were done with values of
\omega
that are equally spaced in log space, we applied finite differencing to the log-valued
\chi
and
\omega
to find the value of
\chi
where the slope is a minimum, i.e.,

\min\left[\frac{\Delta\log(\chi(\omega))}{\Delta\log(\omega)}=\frac{\log(\chi(\omega_{i+1})/\chi(\omega_{i-1}))}{\log(\omega_{i+1}/\omega_{i-1})}\right],

(6)

for

the number of data points measured. In Fig.

\eta_{s}

with a standard deviation of
\pm 0.023

\chi

What does this value of
\chi
tell us about our fluid? For an ideal polymer chain dissolved in a theta solvent,
\chi\approx 1/3
(or equivalently,
\nu=\nu_{\theta}=1/2
). For our solution, we measured experimentally that
\chi=1-1/(3\nu)\approx 0.3
, which corresponds to
\nu=0.47
. Therefore, PB-light mineral oil can be considered a poor solvent for PIB, yet is not so far away from being a
\theta

\nu
is nearly equivalent to
\nu_{\theta}
, due to mixing of polyisobutylene (PIB) with isobutylene, a major component in PB.

Next, the values of
G_{0}
and
\tau
can be fitted from the data in the intermediate to low frequency regime. With
\chi
determined, the free parameters are reduced to two (
G_{0},\tau
), and so next we fit Eqs. (
2a
) to the curves of
G^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime\prime}
. Because the
G^{\prime}
measurements in the low-
\omega

G_{0}
and
\tau
that best fit the data points in the intermediate-
\omega

G^{\prime}\propto\omega^{2}
to
G^{\prime}\propto\omega^{1-\chi}
(region highlighted in orange in Fig.

Lastly, although not frequently discussed to our knowledge in most of the literature on Zimm-like fluids, the first normal stress difference
N_{1}
can be related to the oscillatory measurements by an approximation in the low shear frequency limit
Bird et al. 1987
;
More et al. 2023
,

\psi_{1}\equiv\lim_{\dot{\gamma}\rightarrow 0}\frac{N_{1}}{\dot{\gamma}^{2}}\approx\lim_{\omega\rightarrow 0}\frac{2G^{\prime}}{\omega^{2}}\approx 2\chi G_{0}\tau^{2}.

(7)

This approximation, however, does not best capture the measured values of the first normal stress difference coefficient
\psi_{1}
. Hence, we include another fitting constant
\delta
in Eq. (
7
), such that
N_{1}\approx 2\delta\chi G_{0}\tau^{2}\dot{\gamma}^{2}=\delta\chi\psi_{\text{OB}}\dot{\gamma}^{2}
, where
\psi_{\text{OB}}=2G_{0}\tau^{2}
is the first normal stress difference coefficient predicted with the Oldroyd-B model. Hence, the factor
\delta\chi
signifies the deviation of the real value from an ideal limit. Often, measurements of
\psi_{1}
plotted on a log-log scale are compared directly with Eq. (
7
, which provides a seemingly acceptable fit
More et al. 2023
. However, we found that
\delta
was typically on the order of
\chi
and in the range
0.2-0.4
, and hence when
\psi_{1}
is plotted on a linear scale, it is lower than what Eq. (

III
Characterizing the theory-experiment discrepancies

In §
II
, we described a method for producing a dilute viscoelastic solution using three readily available chemical components, PIB, PB, and light mineral oil. Also, we discussed how the three fitting parameters in the Zimm model,
G_{0},\tau
, and
\chi
, can be identified consistently based on experimental measurements of the viscoelastic moduli. In this section, we test how the viscoelastic parameters (
G_{0},\tau,\psi_{1}
, and
\delta\chi
) change as design parameters
c,M
, and
\eta_{s}
are varied. Furthermore, we will discuss the scaling concepts and theoretical predictions for the dynamics of dilute polymer solutions and how those predictions deviate from the behaviors observed in the model material. We find that while the scaling theory generally captures the trend, appreciable differences remain between the model predictions and the behavior of the model material. This non-ideality of the model system becomes the basis of our fluid design scheme, and we show how we harness this non-ideality to control independently the rheological parameters of interest.

To identify the polymer concentration dependence of

, and
\delta\chi

\eta_{\text{s}}
and the polymer molecular weight
M
, and varied only

. The fitted values of

as functions of
c
are shown in Fig.
4
(a-d). Here,
\psi_{\text{OB}}=2G_{0}\tau^{2}
is not independently measured but is comprised of the fitted values
G_{0}
and
\tau
The scaling theory predicts that both
G_{0}
and
\psi_{1}
scale linearly with
c
, while
\tau
is independent of
c
. Also, the Zimm theory considers a single coil in an infinite solvent, and hence
\chi
is assumed constant.

Specifically,
G_{0}
in the Zimm theory is expressed as a function of the Kuhn length
b
, degree of polymerization
N
, polymer volume fraction
\phi
, and thermal energy scale
k_{B}T
, i.e.,
G_{0}=k_{B}T\phi/(Nb^{3})
. Since
\phi
is proportional to
c
in a constant-density fluid, we expect that
G_{0}\propto c
, and this prediction is confirmed by the oscillatory shear measurements presented in Fig.
4
(a).

The Zimm relaxation time
\tau
is (theoretically) expected to remain constant for dilute polymer concentrations where polymers are assumed to not interact with each other
Del Giudice et al. 2017
;
Rubinstein and Colby 2003
. However, numerous papers report
Del Giudice et al. 2017
that even when
\eta_{\text{sp}}\propto c
(dilute regime),
\tau
increases weakly with
c
. Our measurements likewise indicate a weak dependence,
\tau\propto c^{0.10\pm 0.01}
, as shown in Fig.
4
(b). The exponent of 0.1 will be system dependent and can stem from the differences in the polymer molecular weight, stiffness, and solvent types; e.g., the exponent is approximately
0.76
, for a dilute polystyrene-dioctyl phthalate system
Del Giudice et al. 2017
. Similarly, experiments show
\chi
varies with
c
. Although we do not comment on why both
\chi
and
\tau
increases with
c
, this may nuance that polymer to polymer interaction should be considered even in the ‘dilute’ regime.

The rotational shear measurements show that
\psi_{1}\propto c^{1.41\pm 0.06}
. Considering that
\tau\propto c^{0.10\pm 0.01}
and that
\psi_{1}\propto G_{0}\tau^{2}
, the semi-empirical prediction gives
\psi_{1}\propto c^{1.2}
. This difference suggests that the factor of
\delta\chi=O(0.1)
is
c
-dependent, as shown in Fig.
4
(d). In the plot, we present
\delta\chi
, not just
\delta
, to assess their combined effect on
\psi_{1}
. Therefore, we show that Eq. (
7
) would over-predict
N_{1}
by a factor of
\delta^{-1}
. Interestingly,
\psi_{\text{OB}}=\psi_{1}/\delta\chi\propto c^{1.22}
, which is close to the semi-empirical prediction of
\psi_{1}
on
c
.

The Zimm model predicts that the relaxation time is proportional to the cube of the radius of gyration

of the polymer
Gupta et al. 2000

\tau\approx\eta_{\text{s}}R_{g}^{3}/(k_{B}T)

. Therefore,
\tau\propto M^{3\nu}
. For our material,

and hence we expect
\tau\propto M^{1.41}
. Our measurements closely confirm this trend, giving

(Fig.

\psi_{1}

, while the measurements give
\psi_{1}\propto M^{1.86\pm 0.05}
(Fig.
5
(c)). We also find experimentally that
\delta\chi
is
M
-dependent, such that
\delta\chi\propto M^{-0.31\pm 0.07}
(Fig.
5
(d)).

It is important to note that the nominal values of
M
, provided by the manufacturer, do not reflect the measured values of the molecular weights (see Table
1
). The discrepancies can depend on the proprietary production methods and the degradation under environmental stressors. To avoid these uncontrolled variances, we refer to the nominal
M
of the products we obtained; while the exact power-law exponents may vary between different batches of the product, they can be readily identified from a few measurements, as we demonstrate in Fig.
5
. In the Supplementary Material, we include how the result changes when

, not

IV.1
Design matrix

Note that the power-law relationships presented in Table
3
can be re-written as (with
\nu=0.47
)

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&-1&0\\
0&1.41&1\\
1&1.82&2\\
\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log_{10}c\\
\log_{10}M\\
\log_{10}\eta_{\text{s}}\\
\end{array}\right)\propto\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log_{10}G_{0}\\
\log_{10}\tau\\
\log_{10}\psi_{1}\\
\end{array}\right).

(9)

The determinant of the coefficient matrix on the left-hand side of Eq. (
9
) is zero, so the columns are linearly dependent. This outcome is expected, as
\psi_{1}
is determined by
G_{0}
and
\tau
. This implies that one property can remain constant while the other two are varied simultaneously.

Instead, the experimental measurements shown in Figs.
4
,
5
, and
6
and Table
4
indicate otherwise. Converting the numbers in Table
4
into matrix form yields the following equation (rounded to two decimal places, and ignoring the ranges),

\textbf{C}\approx\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1.00&-0.93&0.01\\
0.10&1.54&0.84\\
1.41&1.86&1.61\\
\end{array}\right),~\textbf{C}\cdot\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log_{10}c\\
\log_{10}M\\
\log_{10}\eta_{\text{s}}\\
\end{array}\right)\propto\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log_{10}G_{0}\\
\log_{10}\tau\\
\log_{10}\psi_{1}\\
\end{array}\right).

(10)

Each row of this equation contains quantities of mixed dimensions, as indicated by the use of the proportionality symbol ‘
\propto
’. To simplify notation and ensure dimensional consistency, we use overbar (‘
\overline{\cdot}
’) symbol to denote ratios between properties in a new gel (to be designed) relative to those of a reference gel, e.g.,
\overline{M}=M_{\text{new}}/M_{\text{ref}}
. This new notation makes it easier to understand how properties in a new gel differ from those of a gel of reference. Then, we compute

\displaystyle(\log_{10}\overline{G},

\displaystyle\log_{10}\overline{\tau},\log_{10}\overline{\psi})^{\text{T}}

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\log_{10}G_{\text{0,new}},\log_{10}\tau_{\text{new}},\log_{10}\psi_{\text{1,new}})^{\text{T}}

\displaystyle-(\log_{10}G_{\text{0,ref}},\log_{10}\tau_{\text{ref}},\log_{10}\psi_{\text{1,ref}})^{\text{T}}

and invert the expression to write

\displaystyle\textbf{C}^{-1}

\displaystyle\approx\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}-19.277&-31.590&16.624\\
-21.490&-33.467&17.618\\
41.508&66.009&-34.123\\
\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle~\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log_{10}\overline{c}\\
\log_{10}\overline{M}\\
\log_{10}\overline{\eta}_{s}\\
\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\textbf{C}^{-1}\cdot\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\log_{10}\overline{G}\\
\log_{10}\overline{\tau}\\
\log_{10}\overline{\psi}\\
\end{array}\right).

Since the design matrix
C
has full rank, and is therefore invertible, there is a unique mapping between
\overline{G},\overline{\tau},
and
\overline{\psi}
to
\overline{c},\overline{M},
and
\overline{\eta}_{s}
, and vice versa. As an example, if one wants to design fluids, with constant
G_{0}
and
\tau
(i.e.,
\overline{G}=\overline{\tau}=1
) but variable
\psi_{1}
(e.g.,
\overline{\psi}=0.1
), one can compute
\textbf{C}^{-1}\cdot(0,0,-1)^{\text{T}}
to determine the corresponding values of
\overline{c},\overline{M},
and
\overline{\eta}_{s}
, thereby yielding the target gel recipe. Given Eq. (
11
), the matrix equation Eq. (
IV.1
) is dimensionless, and we stress that the these dimensionless numbers inside
\textbf{C}^{-1}
are only valid for the PIB-PB Boger fluid system we studied. For different model Boger fluid systems, these numbers will be different, though can be measured with the process shown in earlier sections.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we integrate known concepts from polymer physics and rheology to provide a means for creating well-controlled materials with prescribed rheological properties. The framework we developed for a PIB-PB based dilute viscoelastic fluid is based on experimental measurements of rheological properties using a conventional rheometer. With a few initial measurements (e.g., identifying the linearity limit
c=c^{*}
) and identifying the power-law relationship between the input parameters and the output properties, our framework can further be extended to control the viscoelastic properties of various Boger fluids, whether they are made with polyisobutylene, polyacrylamide, polystyrene, or some other types of flexible Gaussian polymer chains.

The methodology demonstrated here for designing viscoelastic fluids provides intuition for the observations in various viscoelastic flow experiments. For example, one can design different fluids with either constant
\eta_{s}
and varied
\psi_{1}
or constant
\psi_{1}
and varied
\eta_{s}
to examine if the source of the observed viscoelastic behavior originates from the effects associated with the fluid viscosity or the fluid elasticity. Furthermore, this method can be used to estimate the change in molecular weight of the polymers after degradation or scission by using
\overline{G},\overline{\tau},
and
\overline{\psi}
as inputs and
\overline{c},\overline{M},
and
\overline{\eta}_{s}
as outputs using Eq. (
10
). This capability is made possible by the non-ideality of the actual fluids, which allows a linear mapping between
\overline{G}
,
\overline{\tau}
,
\overline{\psi}
and
\overline{c}
,
\overline{M}
,
\overline{\eta}_{s}
.

Lastly, we suggest a couple of avenues for future work to improve the methodology introduced here. First, a design scheme that depends less on the quality of fitting will make our idea more robust. Our method relies on the fitting to identify the power-law exponents in the matrix equation. However, torque limitations in a conventional shear rheometer, combined with the polydispersity of commercially sourced polymers, can reduce the reliability of the fitting. We found that applying the time-temperature superposition principle to avoid the low-torque limit is generally not helpful here, because changing temperature does not translate the curve in the vertical direction (i.e., to have higher
G^{\prime}
values) and also affects
\chi
. Second,
\psi_{1}
is not constant over the full range of
\dot{\gamma}
but rather decreases at high
\dot{\gamma}
. Such decrease in
\psi_{1}
is hard to predict and requires numerous fitting parameters
Das et al. 2024
. However, given recent advances in the machine learning techniques and their applicability in understanding viscoelastic flows
Lennon et al. 2023
, we believe gaining control on
\psi_{1}
over the wider range of
\dot{\gamma}
is possible if machine learning is combined with our design scheme.

In conclusion, we presented a practical framework for designing dilute viscoelastic fluids with controlled
G_{0}
,
\tau
, and
\psi_{1}
using readily available materials. This approach enables tailored Boger fluids for flow experiments while providing insight into how concentration, molecular weight, and solvent viscosity shape viscoelastic behavior.
