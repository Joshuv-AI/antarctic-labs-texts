Comparative Analysis of Color Stability among 3D-Printed Resin-Based, CAD/CAM, and Conventional Interim Fixed Prosthodontic Materials
Authors: Shahad Abdulrahman Alshamrani, Bushra Fahad Alobaid, Hamad Mohammad Alharkan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:This study aims to assess and compare the color stability of 3D-printed, computer-aided design–computer-aided manufacturing (CAD-CAM)-milled, and conventional materials used in interim dental restorations.Materials and Methods:Sixty-four disc-shaped specimens (10 mm diameter, 1.5 mm thickness) were fabricated from four types of interim materials: autopolymerizing polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), bis-acryl composite resin, CAD-CAM PMMA resin, and 3D-printed provisional resin. Each group of polished specimens was divided into four subgroups (n = 4) and immersed in different staining solutions (distilled water, cola, coffee, and tea) at 37°C and 100% humidity without light exposure for 1 day, 7 days, and 30 days. Color changes were measured using a calibrated clinical spectrophotometer.Results:The type of material, staining solution, immersion period, and their interactions significantly influenced color stability (P < 0.001). Bis-acryl and 3D-printed materials exhibited the most discoloration, with coffee and tea causing the highest color changes. CAD-CAM-milled and PMMA materials demonstrated superior color stability, particularly in water and cola.Conclusion:Bis-acryl and 3D-printed materials showed significant and clinically unacceptable color changes, while CAD-CAM-milled and PMMA materials maintained color stability within clinically acceptable limits (ΔE ≈ 3.3). CAD-CAM and PMMA are preferred for interim restorations where color stability is critical.

INTRODUCTION

Interim fixed dental prostheses are an essential component of contemporary dental treatment, providing critical functions such as thermal insulation to protect the prepared tooth structure and stabilization of occlusal relationships. These prostheses are pivotal in evaluating the esthetic, functional, and therapeutic success of a treatment plan prior to the placement of the final prosthesis.[1] Traditionally, interim prostheses have been fabricated using chemical or dual-polymerized resin materials, applied either directly or indirectly. While these materials are readily available and cost-effective, they come with certain limitations.[2] With the advancement of digital dentistry, CAD-CAM (computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing) subtractive technology has been introduced for the fabrication of interim restorations. This technology has significantly enhanced the mechanical properties of prostheses due to the superior polymerization conditions it allows.[3] However, the widespread adoption of CAD-CAM technology faces challenges, including high costs, significant material waste, and the requirement for costly milling machines in dental practices. In response to these challenges, additive manufacturing methods, particularly 3D printing, have emerged as a promising alternative. 3D printing offers the potential to create highly accurate, complex dental restorations with minimal material waste, reduced fabrication time, and lower costs, especially with the growing accessibility of affordable 3D printers suitable for in clinic use.[45] Despite these advantages, certain properties of 3D-printed materials, such as stain resistance and surface roughness, remain underexplored.[6]

The unique resin chemistry of 3D printing, combined with factors such as the surface oxygen inhibition layer and the necessity for postcuring, may influence the color stability and surface roughness of interim prostheses.[7] Understanding these factors is crucial for maximizing the benefits of 3D printing in dental applications, particularly for interim restorations that are used in esthetically sensitive areas. Color stability is a significant consideration in the selection of interim materials, especially given that discoloration during long-term treatment can lead to patient dissatisfaction and increased costs due to the need for replacement.[89] Several factors can contribute to discoloration, including incomplete polymerization, water sorption, chemical reactivity, surface roughness, diet, and oral hygiene practices.[10] Additionally, surface roughness is a critical factor in the success of dental treatments. Rough surfaces on interim restorations can cause soft tissue irritation, leading to inflammation or discomfort. Moreover, rough surfaces can promote bacterial plaque accumulation, increasing the risk of periodontal disease and compromising oral health. Such surfaces can also impede effective oral hygiene, exacerbating discoloration and negatively impacting patient satisfaction and treatment outcomes.[11]

This background underscores the need for further research into the properties of 3D-printed interim materials, particularly their color stability and surface roughness. A deeper understanding of these factors is essential to optimize the use of 3D printing technology in the fabrication of interim fixed dental prostheses, ensuring both functional and esthetic success in dental treatments.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Specimen preparation

A total of 64 disc-shaped specimens were prepared, each with a diameter of 10 mm and a thickness of 1.5 mm, using four different types of interim fixed prosthodontic materials: autopolymerizing polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) (Bosworth Trim Plus; Keystone Industries, NJ, USA), bis-acryl composite resin (Protemp 4; 3M ESPE, Seefeld Bavaria, Germany), CAD-CAM PMMA resin, and 3D-printed provisional resin (NextDent C&B MFH; NextDent B.V., Soesterberg, Netherlands). Sixteen specimens were fabricated from each material. The sample size was determined through a power analysis using G*Power software (V3.1.9.7 for Windows; Heinrich Heine Universität Dusseldorf) to achieve a power of 95% with a significance level of 0.05 and an expected effect size of f: 0.547 based on the study by Taşın et al.[5]

The PMMA and bis-acryl specimens were fabricated according to the manufacturers’ recommendations using a stone mold. After polymerization, the specimens were removed from the mold, and excess material was carefully trimmed. A virtual disc was designed using 3D modeling software (Meshmixer; Autodesk, Inc.; San Rafael, CA) to generate both the CAD-CAM-milled and 3D-printed specimens. The CAD-CAM specimens were milled from a 98.5 mm PMMA disc using a 5-axis computer numerical control (CNC) dental milling machine (inLab MC X5; Dentsply Sirona Inc.; Charlotte, NC). The 3D-printed specimens were fabricated using a digital light processing (DLP) 3D printer (NextDent 5100 3D Printer; NextDent B.V.; Soesterberg, Netherlands).

All specimens underwent polishing by a technician using fine (24 μm) and superfine (8 μm) aluminum oxide polishing discs for 1 minute per specimen, without water irrigation, to standardize the surface treatment. After polishing, the specimens were cleaned in an ultrasonic bath with distilled water for 5 minutes and then stored for 24 hours in 37°C distilled water.

Storage and stainability evaluation

The 64 polished specimens were divided into four groups based on the material type. Each group was further divided into four subgroups, each containing four specimens. These subgroups were stored in different solutions to evaluate their stainability over three time intervals: 1 day, 7 days, and 30 days. The storage solutions were selected to represent common dietary staining agents: distilled water, cola, coffee, and tea. Specifically, 300 mL of distilled water, 300 mL of Coca-Cola, 3 g of Nescafé dissolved in 300 mL of boiling distilled water, and one sachet of Lipton tea steeped in 300 mL of boiling distilled water for 5 minutes were prepared. Each specimen was stored individually in a small bottle with 50 mL of the respective solution at 37°C in a dark environment, and the solutions were refreshed every 2 days.

Color measurements were taken using a calibrated clinical spectrophotometer (Vita Easyshade Advance; VITA Zahnfabrik; Bad Säckingen, Germany) at baseline and after each storage period (1 day, 7 days, and 30 days). For each measurement, three readings were taken at the center of each specimen using a silicone alignment jig, and the average value was recorded. The color differences (ΔE76) were calculated for each specimen at each storage time point using the International Commission on Illumination (CIE1976) color difference formula:

The spectrophotometer had a probe diameter of 5 mm, with a reported accuracy of 93.75% and high repeatability. A custom-made silicone mold was used to position the spectrophotometer head at the center of the specimens, and the spectrophotometer was recalibrated after each group measurement.

Statistical analysis

To determine the impact of immersion in different discoloring solutions over time on the color stability of interim materials, a three-factor ANOVA parametric test was conducted. The normality of the data distribution was confirmed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (P > 0.05), and homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test (P > 0.05). A significance level of α = 0.05 was applied to all inferential analyses. For comparisons between means, particularly concerning the interaction between factors and time points, a multiple comparison of means using the Bonferroni test was performed. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 20.0 software (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

In terms of the main effect of interim material, the Bis-acryl (13.38 ± 10.87) and 3D-printed (11.50 ± 9.39) materials exhibited the most substantial discoloration. In contrast, CAD-CAM-milled (2.99 ± 1.81) and laboratory-fabricated PMMA (4.38 ± 2.24) materials showed the least discoloration and the highest color stability.

Regarding the effect of discoloring solutions, coffee (14.35 ± 10.48) and tea (11.72 ± 8.68) caused the highest color changes, indicating significantly greater discoloration compared to cola (3.96 ± 1.87) and water (2.21 ± 1.18) [Figure 1].

Boxplot chart of ΔE values for the tested interim materials immersed in discoloring solutions for three time periods

The effect of the immersion period on color change varied depending on the type of immersion solution, as indicated by a significant interaction (P < 0.001). All groups, except for those immersed in water, showed a significant increase in ΔE values over time (P < 0.001). The water group did not exhibit significant differences between the 1-day, 1-week, and 1-month immersion periods (P = 0.095 and P = 0.057, respectively). Specimens immersed in different discoloring solutions (coffee and tea) showed the highest ΔE values after 1 month, with these increases being statistically significant compared to those observed after 1 day and 1 week (P < 0.001).

Pairwise comparisons revealed that all interim materials experienced significant color changes (ΔE) when immersed in coffee and tea solutions. The CAD/CAM-milled materials exhibited the lowest discoloration, followed by laboratory-fabricated PMMA. The Bis-acryl and 3D-printed materials showed the highest ΔE values, with significant differences between these materials and CAD/CAM-milled PMMA observed at all immersion periods. The difference between Bis-acryl and 3D-printed materials was significant at the 1-week and 1-month immersion periods but not at the 1-day immersion period. For cola and water immersion, there were no significant differences in color changes (ΔE) between the interim materials at the three immersion periods, except for the Bis-acryl fabricated interim materials. Bis-acryl showed significantly higher discoloration (ΔE) and poorer color stability compared to all other interim materials across the three immersion periods, even when immersed in water.

DISCUSSION

This in vitro study aimed to assess and compare the color stability of 3D-printed, CAD-CAM-milled, conventional PMMA, and Bis-acryl materials used in interim restorations after immersion in four different staining solutions over three time intervals. The null hypothesis, which suggested no difference in discoloration characteristics based on the interim material, type of colorant, or storage duration, was rejected. The findings indicated that both the material type and the conditions of exposure to colorants significantly influenced discoloration.

The study results demonstrated that coffee was the most staining solution, as evidenced by the highest mean ΔE76 values, consistent with findings from previous research by Tasın et al.,[5] which also reported significant staining in 3D-printed interim prosthodontic materials when immersed in coffee. This outcome highlights the significant impact of coffee as a staining agent, especially for materials used in interim dental restorations.

In the present study, CAD-CAM-fabricated specimens exhibited superior color stability compared to other materials, which aligns with the findings of Rayyan et al.,[12] who also reported enhanced color stability in CAD-CAM materials. The optimal polymerization conditions in the CAD-CAM process likely contribute to this improved stability, reducing the susceptibility of these materials to staining.

The results also showed that the PMMA group demonstrated greater color stability in coffee solution compared to the Bis-acryl group, similar to the findings of Turgut et al.[13] Their study suggested that Bis-acryl composite resin samples were more prone to discoloration due to the accumulation of coffee contents in the material’s microstructures. This phenomenon might explain the significant discoloration observed in Bis-acryl materials in the current study. A significant difference was observed between the PMMA and Bis-acryl composite resin groups in response to coffee, tea, cola, and water. This contrasts with the study by Değer et al.,[14] which reported no significant difference between these materials across various solutions. The discrepancy could be attributed to differences in methodology, material composition, or sample preparation techniques between the studies. Interestingly, in our study, Bis-acryl interim materials exhibited the most significant discoloration, while laboratory-fabricated PMMA showed the least discoloration and the highest color stability. This finding contrasts with research by Sham et al.,[9] where Bis-acryl-methacrylate-based resins demonstrated acceptable color stability and were identified as the most color-stable provisional prosthodontic materials compared to methyl/ethyl methacrylate-based resins. The differences in outcomes might stem from variations in the specific formulations of Bis-acryl and PMMA used or differences in the immersion conditions. Moreover, the current study’s results indicated that color stability was affected over time, with samples becoming increasingly stained after each successive time interval. This observation aligns with the study by Aburaisi et al.,[15] which reported that tooth-colored restorations tend to become more discolored as time progresses. This reinforces the importance of considering long-term color stability when selecting materials for interim restorations, particularly in esthetic areas.

CONCLUSION

This study underscores the significant influence of both material type and exposure conditions on the color stability of interim dental restorations. CAD-CAM and PMMA materials exhibited superior resistance to discoloration, while Bis-acryl materials were more susceptible to staining, particularly in coffee and tea solutions. These findings suggest that material selection should be carefully considered in clinical practice, especially for cases requiring prolonged use of interim restorations in esthetically sensitive areas.
