Peripheral administration of 11.7% hypertonic saline and phlebitis risk in acute ischemic stroke requiring hyperosmolar therapy in South Korea
Authors: Beom-Seok Seo, Hyesuk Hong, Jong Hwa Hong, Jisu Jung, Hyunsoo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertonic saline (HTS) is used for hyperosmolar therapy in acute ischemic stroke (AIS) but is traditionally administered via central venous catheter to minimize vascular complications. Procedural burden and urgency often necessitate peripheral administration in practice. We investigated phlebitis risk associated with peripheral 11.7% HTS in AIS patients requiring hyperosmolar therapy.MethodsThis retrospective study included 200 AIS patients receiving hyperosmolar therapy with mannitol (January 2022–December 2024), classified into an HTS group (n=67) receiving additional peripheral 11.7% NaCl and a no-HTS group (n=133). Phlebitis was assessed using the Infusion Nurses Society Phlebitis Scale. The primary outcome was phlebitis within 72 hours of therapy initiation. Multivariable logistic regression assessed the independent association between HTS use and phlebitis, with a secondary analysis examining frequency-dependent risk.ResultsPhlebitis occurred in 31.3% (21/67) of the HTS group versus 15.8% (21/133) of the no-HTS group (P=0.016). Among those with phlebitis, most cases were mild (grade 1, 90.5%; grade 2, 9.5%), with no grade 3 or higher events. HTS was independently associated with phlebitis (adjusted OR, 2.19; 95% CI, 1.02–4.70; P=0.044) but not with mortality or hospital length of stay. Single administration was not associated with increased risk, whereas two administrations (adjusted OR, 5.43; P=0.005) and three or more (adjusted OR, 3.59; P=0.016) were associated with markedly elevated risk (P for trend=0.002).ConclusionsPeripheral 11.7% HTS was associated with increased phlebitis risk, though cases were predominantly mild. Risk increased with repeated rather than single-dose administration, suggesting caution is warranted when multiple peripheral doses are required.

INTRODUCTION

Cerebral edema is a critical complication of acute ischemic stroke (AIS), contributing to elevated intracranial pressure (ICP), cerebral herniation, and early neurological deterioration [1]. Timely ICP control is therefore a fundamental therapeutic priority in the acute phase of stroke management [2,3]. Hyperosmolar therapy remains the cornerstone of medical ICP management, with mannitol and hypertonic saline (HTS) serving as the principal pharmacologic agents [4,5].

HTS exerts its therapeutic effect by generating a robust osmotic gradient that facilitates translocation of water from the cerebral parenchyma into the intravascular compartment. Owing to a reflection coefficient approaching 1.0 across the intact blood–brain barrier, HTS provides a sustained osmotic effect with reduced risk of intracerebral accumulation compared to mannitol [5-7]. Beyond osmotic pressure reduction, HTS expands intravascular volume, augments mean arterial pressure, and improves cerebral perfusion pressure, offering potential hemodynamic benefits in patients with compromised cerebral perfusion [8-10]. Several prior studies and guideline statements suggest that HTS may be considered particularly in cases where the response to mannitol is inadequate, or as a complementary agent in refractory ICP elevation [4,11,12].

Historically, HTS has been recommended for administration via central venous catheter (CVC) [4,13], based on concerns that its high osmolarity may induce endothelial injury when infused peripherally, increasing the risk of thrombophlebitis, infiltration, extravasation, and tissue necrosis [13-15]. However, in acute neurological emergencies requiring rapid ICP reduction, the time required for CVC placement and its associated procedural risks may introduce clinically significant delays in treatment. This practical constraint has prompted increasing clinical interest in whether peripheral HTS administration can be performed safely under monitored conditions.

Prior investigations into peripheral HTS safety have yielded conflicting results, with reported complication rates varying widely across studies depending on HTS concentration, infusion method, and observation window [16-21]. Studies have predominantly focused on 3% HTS administered as a continuous infusion, with limited evidence available for higher-concentration bolus formulations [20]. In clinical practice, high-concentration bolus formulations are increasingly utilized for acute ICP crises, offering more rapid pressure reduction with lower infusion volumes compared to continuous 3% HTS infusion [22]. While 23.4% NaCl is the standard high-concentration formulation in Western practice, it is not commercially available in South Korea; 11.7% NaCl, which delivers an equivalent sodium dose in twice the volume, represents the only available high-concentration formulation in Korean stroke centers. Critically, evidence regarding peripheral administration of high-concentration HTS specifically in AIS patients—and whether phlebitis risk scales with the number of administrations—has not been systematically evaluated, leaving a clinically important gap in the guidance for real-world practice. Current major guidelines continue to recommend CVC administration as the standard of care, though the supporting evidence remains low-quality or based on expert consensus [4]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether peripheral administration of 11.7% NaCl HTS independently increases phlebitis risk in AIS patients receiving hyperosmolar therapy, and to characterize the frequency-dependent nature of this risk.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The current study was approved by Institutional Review Board at Chonnam National University Hospital (No. CNUH-2026-186). A waiver for informed consent was provided because of study subject anonymity and minimal risk to the participants. The data used in this study are available upon reasonable request following the submission of a legitimate academic research proposal to be assessed by the corresponding author.

Study Subjects

This retrospective study was conducted at a single comprehensive stroke center. Consecutive patients admitted with AIS within 48 hours of symptom onset between January 2022 and December 2024 were included (n=2,512). After excluding patients who did not receive hyperosmolar therapy (n=2,275) and those younger than 19 years (n=5), 232 patients who received hyperosmolar therapy for cerebral edema management during hospitalization were identified. Patients who received HTS via CVC (n=12) or those with incomplete infusion data (n=20) were excluded, resulting in a final analytic cohort of 200 patients (Figure 1). All patients received peripheral intravenous mannitol as the primary hyperosmolar agent and were classified into two groups: those who additionally received peripheral HTS (HTS group, n=67) and those who received mannitol alone (no-HTS group, n=133).

Data Collection

Baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were systematically extracted from electronic medical records. Initial National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores were assessed by emergency department neurologists or certified stroke unit nurses at the time of admission. Pre-stroke modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores were obtained through structured interviews with patients or their caregivers, with functional independence defined as mRS 0–1. Vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, atrial fibrillation, coronary heart disease, prior stroke, peripheral artery disease, and smoking status) and medication history (antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants, antihypertensive drugs, antidiabetic agents, and statins) were documented at admission through medical record review and patient interviews. Stroke subtypes were independently determined by two neurologists according to the Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) classification, incorporating predefined imaging criteria [23,24]. Laboratory parameters were obtained from the first blood tests performed at admission. All patients underwent brain imaging (computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging) within 24 hours of admission.

Hyperosmolar Treatment

All study participants received mannitol as the first-line hyperosmolar agent for cerebral edema control. Mannitol was administered at doses of 0.5–1.5 g/kg, repeated three to six times daily at the discretion of the attending physician. The decision to administer additional HTS was made by the attending neurologist based on clinical judgment, primarily guided by insufficient neurological improvement or signs of progressive cerebral edema despite mannitol therapy, including worsening level of consciousness or radiologic evidence of midline shift.

The HTS formulation used in this study was 11.7% NaCl 20 ml ampoules (11.7% Sodium Chloride inj., JW Pharmaceutical), which is commercially available in South Korea. Each dose consisted of three ampoules (total 60 ml, approximately 120 mEq Na), which is equivalent in sodium content to 23.4% NaCl 30 ml recommended in North American guidelines [4]. Since the 23.4% NaCl formulation is not commercially available in South Korea, the 11.7% NaCl formulation was used to achieve the same sodium dose. HTS was administered as a bolus via peripheral intravenous access within 15 minutes and was repeated at 4–6-hour intervals as needed. All patients underwent regular monitoring of serum sodium and electrolyte levels during HTS administration, with a target serum sodium concentration of 145–155 mEq/L. HTS was administered through peripheral venous catheters of 20 gauge or larger. Peripheral infusion sites were assessed by nursing staff at every shift change (minimum 8-hour intervals) for signs of phlebitis including erythema, edema, pain, and induration. Upon detection of suspicious findings, infusion was immediately discontinued and switched to the contralateral vein.

Definition of Phlebitis

Phlebitis was defined and graded according to the Infusion Nurses Society (INS) Phlebitis Scale, a validated 5-point classification system based on clinical signs at the infusion site [13,25]. The scale ranges from 0 (no symptoms) to 4 (severe thrombophlebitis with purulent drainage). Grade 0 indicates the absence of any clinical signs. Grade 1 is defined as erythema at the access site with or without pain. Grade 2 is characterized by pain at the access site with erythema and/or edema. Grade 3 comprises pain accompanied by erythema, streak formation, and a palpable venous cord. Grade 4, the most severe, is defined as pain with erythema, streak formation, a palpable venous cord exceeding one inch in length, and purulent drainage.

Phlebitis assessment was conducted retrospectively through comprehensive medical record review, including nursing documentation, physician notes, and photographic images of infusion sites when available. Two independent assessors—a neurologist (HK) and a neurocritical care nurse (HH)—reviewed all available documentation and assigned INS grades. All discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion, and final grades were determined by agreement between both reviewers. For patients with documented venous ultrasonography or other imaging studies of the affected extremity, radiologic findings were incorporated into the assessment. For the primary analysis, phlebitis was defined as any event with INS grade ≥1.

Outcome

The primary outcome was the occurrence of phlebitis within 72 hours of hyperosmolar therapy initiation, defined as the development of clinical signs consistent with INS grade ≥1 following peripheral intravenous infusion. The observation window was anchored to the initiation of hyperosmolar therapy rather than to the time of the last HTS administration, in order to apply a uniform and prospectively defined observation period across all patients, given that the number and timing of administrations varied between individuals. Secondary outcomes included in-hospital mortality, favorable functional outcome at 3 months (defined as mRS score 0–3, reflecting the expected severe neurological burden in patients requiring hyperosmolar therapy), and hospital length of stay (LOS), calculated as the number of days from admission to discharge.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between the HTS and no-HTS groups. Continuous variables were expressed as mean±standard deviation and compared using Student t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables (initial NIHSS score and pre-stroke mRS) were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using Fisher's exact test with Monte Carlo simulation (B=10,000).

Event rates for phlebitis and mortality were presented as the number of events, percentage, and 95% CI calculated using the exact binomial method. Between-group differences in phlebitis were assessed using the chi-square test. The distribution of phlebitis severity by INS grade was presented as patient counts and percentages for each grade. LOS was presented as median (interquartile range) and compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U-test. In-hospital mortality was additionally analyzed using the log-rank test, with a maximum observed follow-up duration of 91 days.

Multivariable logistic regression was used for the binary outcome of phlebitis, with results expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CI. Cox proportional hazards regression was used for in-hospital mortality, with results expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CI; the proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. For LOS, which exhibited a right-skewed distribution, log-transformation was applied prior to analysis, and multivariable linear regression was performed; results are expressed as the regression coefficient (β) with 95% CI. Covariates in all adjusted models were selected a priori based on clinical relevance. The final set of covariates comprised age, initial NIHSS score, pre-stroke mRS score, prior antiplatelet use, reperfusion therapy, and TOAST classification.

To examine the dose-response relationship between HTS administration frequency and phlebitis risk, patients were stratified into four groups: no HTS (reference), HTS with one administration, two administrations, and three or more administrations. Phlebitis event rates are presented with exact binomial 95% CIs for each group. Crude and adjusted ORs relative to the no HTS reference group were estimated using logistic regression. The Cochran-Armitage trend test was used to assess the statistical significance of the monotonic trend in crude phlebitis rates across the four ordered groups. For the adjusted trend test, HTS administration frequency was entered as an ordinal variable (coded 0, 1, 2, ≥3) in the multivariable logistic regression model, and the corresponding p-value was derived from the Wald test of this ordinal term. In addition, among HTS patients with available administration count data (n=67), the continuous dose-response relationship between HTS administration frequency and each clinical outcome (phlebitis and LOS) was modeled using restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression with three knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the administration frequency distribution. To identify independent predictors of phlebitis among all study participants, a multivariable logistic regression model was constructed with HTS use as the primary exposure variable and the aforementioned covariates simultaneously entered. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A two-sided P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

Among the 200 patients, 67 (33.5%) received additional peripheral HTS (HTS group), while 133 (66.5%) received mannitol alone (no-HTS group). The mean age was 75±11 years, and 88 (44.0%) were male. Pre-stroke functional independence (mRS 0–1) was observed in 194 patients (97.0%). Most baseline characteristics were similar between the two groups; however, the HTS group had a higher initial NIHSS score compared to the no-HTS group (median, 14 vs. 13; P=0.022) and higher prior antiplatelet use (25.4% vs. 12.8%, P=0.030). Vascular risk factors including diabetes and peripheral arterial disease, reperfusion therapy, TOAST classification, and laboratory parameters showed no significant differences between the groups (Table 1).

Phlebitis and Clinical Outcomes

Phlebitis occurred in 42 patients (21.0%), with an incidence of 31.3% (21/67) in the HTS group and 15.8% (21/133) in the no-HTS group, representing a significantly higher rate in the HTS group (P=0.016). Regarding INS grade distribution, grade 1 accounted for the majority with 38 patients (90.5%), while grade 2 occurred in 4 patients (9.5%), all exclusively in the HTS group (6.0% vs. 0.0%, P=0.012). Grade 3 or higher severe phlebitis was not observed in either group.

For secondary outcomes, in-hospital mortality was 23.9% (16/67) in the HTS group and 15.0% (20/133) in the no-HTS group, with no statistically significant difference (P=0.171). Favorable functional outcome at 3 months (mRS 0–3) also showed no difference between the groups (20.9% vs. 25.6%, P=0.490). The median LOS was 10.9 days (interquartile range [IQR], 4.6–18.6) in the HTS group and 9.1 days (IQR, 5.5–13.7) in the no-HTS group, with no significant difference (P=0.341) (Table 2).

In multivariable analysis, HTS use was independently associated with phlebitis occurrence (adjusted OR, 2.19; 95% CI, 1.02–4.70; P=0.044). In contrast, no independent association was observed with in-hospital mortality (adjusted HR, 1.48; 95% CI, 0.73–2.97; P=0.274) or LOS (adjusted β, 0.024; 95% CI, −0.262 to 0.311; P=0.867) (Table 3).

Frequency-Response Relationship

Stratified analysis by HTS administration frequency revealed phlebitis incidence rates of 15.8% in the no-HTS group, 10.0% in the single-administration group, 53.3% in the two-administration group, and 45.5% in the three-or-more-administration group. Using the no-HTS group as reference, the adjusted OR was 0.51 (95% CI, 0.11–1.75; P=0.325) for the single-administration group, showing no significant risk increase, whereas it was significantly elevated at 5.43 (95% CI, 1.65–18.22; P=0.005) for the two-administration group and 3.59 (95% CI, 1.25–10.27; P=0.016) for the three-or-more-administration group (P for trend=0.002) (Table 4).

RCS analysis demonstrated a significant association between HTS administration frequency and phlebitis risk, with evidence of non-linearity (P for overall effect=0.002, P for non-linear effect=0.028) (Figure 2A). The predicted probability of phlebitis increased steeply from the first to third administration, reaching an estimated plateau of approximately 45%–50% beyond three administrations, consistent with the threshold pattern observed in the categorical analysis. For LOS, a significant overall association with HTS administration frequency was observed (P for overall effect=0.031); however, the non-linear term did not reach statistical significance (P for non-linear=0.186) (Figure 2B), suggesting a broadly increasing trend in LOS with more frequent administrations rather than a distinctly non-linear pattern.

Independent Predictors of Phlebitis

In multivariable logistic regression analysis of all study participants, HTS use was the only independent predictor of phlebitis occurrence (adjusted OR, 2.21; 95% CI, 1.03–4.75; P=0.04). Age, sex, initial NIHSS score, pre-stroke mRS, reperfusion therapy, TOAST classification, prior antiplatelet use, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and atrial fibrillation all showed no statistically significant association (Table 5).

DISCUSSION

In this study of AIS patients requiring hyperosmolar therapy, those who received 11.7% NaCl HTS via peripheral intravenous access had a phlebitis incidence of 31.3%, significantly higher than the 15.8% observed in the no-HTS group, and HTS use was confirmed as an independent predictor of phlebitis in multivariable analysis (adjusted OR, 2.19; P=0.044). Notably, however, a distinct dose-response relationship was observed according to administration frequency. The incidence sharply increased from the second peripheral HTS administration (adjusted OR, 5.43; P=0.005), and this trend was statistically significant (P for trend=0.002). Furthermore, the severity of phlebitis was mostly mild, with INS grade 1 accounting for the majority (90.5%), grade 2 in only 4 patients (9.5%), and no grade 3 or higher severe phlebitis or tissue necrosis observed. Additionally, HTS use showed no independent association with in-hospital mortality or LOS, indicating that the clinical harm from HTS was confined to predominantly non-severe phlebitis.

The severity distribution of phlebitis in this cohort warrants brief consideration. The majority of events were INS grade 1, which is generally reversible and managed conservatively through catheter removal and relocation, typically resolving within several days without invasive intervention. Although four grade 2 events occurred exclusively in the HTS group (6.0% vs. 0.0%, P=0.012), suggesting that HTS may occasionally contribute to phlebitis extending beyond simple erythema, no event reached grade 3 or higher and no tissue necrosis was observed. Taken together, the vascular injury associated with peripheral 11.7% HTS was predominantly mild and clinically manageable, although even low-grade events may accumulate clinical relevance when repeated peripheral administration is required.

Our observed phlebitis incidence of 31.3% is substantially higher than the pooled 6.2% phlebitis rate reported in a meta-analysis of peripheral 3% HTS studies [17], and the 3.7% complication rate reported in a bolus 3% HTS study [16]. This difference is expected given the markedly higher osmolarity of 11.7% NaCl (4,004 mOsm/L) relative to 3% NaCl (1,027 mOsm/L). Notably, even 3% HTS has been associated with phlebitis rates as high as 47% in some cohorts [21], suggesting that factors beyond osmolarity alone, such as infusion duration and administration method, may also contribute to vascular complications, though detailed comparison with our bolus protocol is not straightforward. For higher-concentration formulations, prior studies have predominantly assessed extravasation rather than phlebitis as their primary endpoint, and were conducted almost exclusively in single-dose emergency department settings. The largest such study reported an extravasation incidence of 6.4% with peripheral 23.4% NaCl across 863 administrations, which is a figure not directly comparable to our phlebitis incidence, given the differences in outcome definition, observation window, and clinical context. This lack of comparability arises because that study focused predominantly on single emergency department administrations assessed over 24 hours, whereas our study involved repeated inpatient dosing observed over 72 hours. Our findings may therefore extend rather than contradict prior evidence, by characterizing vascular complications across a broader outcome spectrum and a clinically distinct administration context [20].

The recommendation for CVC administration of HTS is based on the concern that high osmolarity solutions, which far exceed the 900 mOsm/L threshold considered safe for peripheral venous administration, may induce endothelial injury, increasing the risk of thrombophlebitis and extravasation [15]. While this concern is physiologically grounded, the requirement for CVC access introduces a practical dilemma in acute ICP emergencies: central line placement is time-consuming and carries its own procedural risks including infection and thrombosis [26]. In this context, the time-to-treatment argument is clinically important. Prior studies have demonstrated that CVC placement can introduce meaningful delays in osmotherapy initiation, and that earlier treatment is associated with better neurological outcomes in patients with acute ICP elevation [20,27]. When central access is not immediately available, peripheral administration of a single HTS dose may represent the only means of delivering timely osmotherapy. Our finding that single-dose peripheral administration was not associated with a statistically significant increase in phlebitis risk, whereas two or more administrations were associated with such an increase, may provide some quantitative context for this clinical consideration, though prospective data are needed before firm recommendations can be made.

Another key contribution of this study is the first systematic quantification of frequency-dependent phlebitis risk with peripheral high-concentration HTS. While prior studies addressed the binary question of "is peripheral administration safe," our study examined the more clinically actionable question of "how many times can it be administered peripherally." The absence of statistically significant increased risk with single administration (adjusted OR, 0.51; P=0.325) may reflect a genuine threshold below which a single hyperosmolar bolus does not cause sufficient cumulative endothelial injury to manifest as clinically detectable phlebitis. The numerically lower incidence in the single-administration group compared to the no-HTS reference (10.0% vs. 15.8%) may additionally reflect selection of patients with relatively mild cerebral edema who required only one dose, rather than a true protective effect. In contrast, the sharp risk increase from the second administration (OR, 5.43) is both statistically and clinically clear, consistent with a model of cumulative endothelial damage in which repeated hyperosmolar exposure before sufficient vascular recovery compounds rather than resets prior injury [14,28]. This non-linear dose-response relationship was further corroborated by RCS analysis, demonstrating a marked increase in phlebitis probability beginning at the second administration, consistent with the elevated rates observed in the categorical analysis for two or more administrations. The apparent decline in the adjusted OR from the two-administration group (5.43) to the three-or-more-administration group (3.59) should not be interpreted as a true reversal of the dose-response relationship. Both subgroups were small (n=15 and n=22, respectively), yielding wide and largely overlapping confidence intervals (1.65–18.22 and 1.25–10.27), such that the two point estimates are statistically indistinguishable. This instability, together with the previously described possibility of survivor bias, likely accounts for the non-monotonic pattern. The RCS analysis offers a complementary and more stable perspective, depicting a continuous rise in phlebitis probability that plateaus rather than reverses beyond the third administration. Taken together, the categorical and spline analyses are best interpreted as concordant evidence of a threshold-type increase in risk emerging at the second administration, rather than as a precisely graded dose-response across each additional dose.

We used the 11.7% NaCl formulation, which is the only commercially available high-concentration HTS product in South Korea and is equivalent to 23.4% NaCl 30 ml in sodium content (120 mEq), but differs in volume (60 ml vs. 30 ml) and osmolarity (approximately 4,004 mOsm/L vs. 8,008 mOsm/L). Theoretically, the larger volume (60 ml) may increase the dilution effect with blood in the peripheral vein, lowering the contact osmolarity on vascular endothelial cells, which could reduce phlebitis incidence compared to the 23.4% formulation [14,29]. On the other hand, the increased infusion time associated with larger volumes may prolong vascular endothelial cell exposure to the hyperosmolar environment. Since previous studies have reported that longer infusion times increase phlebitis risk, if our infusion duration was longer than that for 23.4%, this could have increased phlebitis risk [28]. Given these opposing theoretical effects, the net phlebitis risk of 11.7% NaCl relative to 23.4% NaCl remains uncertain, and our observed incidence should not be extrapolated to other formulations without further comparative data. Importantly, to our knowledge, no prior study has specifically examined the peripheral administration safety of 11.7% NaCl. As the only commercially available high-concentration HTS formulation in South Korea, this agent is used in routine clinical practice without an established evidence base for peripheral safety, further highlighting the clinical relevance of our findings.

Our study has several important limitations. First, the single-center retrospective design precludes the complete exclusion of residual confounding, and indication bias is inherent given that the decision to administer HTS rested entirely on the judgment of the attending physician. Patients in the HTS group had higher initial NIHSS scores, raising the possibility that greater neurological severity, rather than HTS itself, contributed to the observed risk of phlebitis through more intensive treatment or more frequent venous access. Although both groups received peripheral mannitol as the first-line agent, the number of mannitol administrations and the cumulative dose were not recorded and could not be included as covariates; a higher overall infusion burden in the HTS group therefore cannot be excluded. However, the primary outcome of this study was the occurrence of phlebitis rather than mortality or functional prognosis, which are the outcomes influenced by neurological severity, and in the multivariable model neurological severity was not significantly associated with the occurrence of phlebitis. Nevertheless, because of the observational design, residual or unmeasured confounding cannot be entirely excluded, and the findings of this study should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Second, the small subgroup sizes in the two-administration (n=15) and three-or-more-administration (n=22) groups yield wide confidence intervals and unstable point estimates, limiting the precision of the frequency-stratified analysis. Third, detailed peripheral intravenous catheter-related variables— including catheter gauge, insertion site location, indwelling duration, and replacement frequency—were not systematically recorded, precluding site- and catheter-specific risk analyses. These factors are recognized contributors to phlebitis development, and their omission may have introduced additional unmeasured confounding access. Consequently, the three-or-more-administration group may have been enriched with patients with better vascular tolerance, which could potentially explain the paradoxically lower adjusted OR in that group compared to the two-administration group (3.59 vs. 5.43). Fourth, phlebitis was assessed retrospectively through medical record review rather than prospective bedside evaluation. This approach may have resulted in under-ascertainment of mild grade 1 events not formally documented in nursing records. Furthermore, inter-rater reliability was not formally quantified; however, all discrepancies were resolved through consensus between two independent reviewers with photographic and ultrasonographic evidence incorporated where available, partially mitigating this limitation. Fifth, the 72-hour observation window, while longer than the 24-hour window used in prior single-dose studies, was selected to accommodate the delayed phlebitis manifestation expected with repeated inpatient dosing. However, phlebitis events occurring beyond 72 hours, which may occur with later administrations, would not have been captured, potentially leading to underestimation of cumulative risk. Finally, misclassification of phlebitis caused by concomitant irritant medications as HTS-related cannot be entirely excluded.

Despite these limitations, several strengths of this study merit acknowledgment. First, to our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the peripheral administration safety of 11.7% NaCl HTS in AIS patients. Second, by evaluating phlebitis risk according to the number of administrations, this study systematically quantified frequency-dependent risk across different categories. This approach addresses a clinically specific and actionable question—how many times HTS can be safely administered peripherally—which prior binary safety studies of other HTS formulations were unable to answer.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that peripheral administration of 11.7% high-concentration NaCl in AIS patients increases phlebitis risk; however, with single-dose administration, the risk increase is not evident, and most phlebitis that occurred was mild and clinically manageable. These findings suggest the possibility of considering single-dose high-concentration HTS bolus administration via peripheral vein as rescue therapy in emergency situations when central venous access is not immediately available. However, repeated administration of two or more doses sharply increases phlebitis risk, and in such cases, central venous access should be actively pursued. This study is hypothesis-generating in nature, and prospective multicenter studies and pragmatic randomized trials based on standardized protocols are needed to establish such clinical guidelines.

KEY MESSAGES

▪ Peripheral administration of 11.7% hypertonic saline was independently associated with phlebitis in acute ischemic stroke patients requiring hyperosmolar therapy, though observed cases were predominantly mild with no severe tissue injury.

▪ Phlebitis risk showed a frequency-dependent pattern: single-dose administration was not associated with a statistically significant risk increase, whereas two or more administrations were associated with markedly elevated risk.

▪ These findings may inform clinical decision-making regarding peripheral hypertonic saline use when central venous access is not immediately available, and highlight the need for prospective studies to establish definitive guidance.
