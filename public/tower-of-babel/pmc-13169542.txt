Targeting MET in non-small cell lung cancer brain metastases: from molecular mechanism to precision therapy
Authors: Xinyu Wang, Yuxin Chi, Fengchun Mu, Hongliang Mao, Chen Yang, Ming Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBrain metastases (BM) remain a major cause of morbidity and treatment failure in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Among actionable pathways, mesenchymal–epithelial transition factor (MET) is clinically consequential not only as an oncogenic driver in a molecular subset of tumors but also as a convergent bypass route that mediates acquired resistance in the much larger EGFR-mutant population, making MET an important focus in the context of intracranial progression under targeted-therapy pressure.Main bodyAvailable genomic datasets suggest a distinct distribution of MET alterations in BM, with MET amplification relatively enriched in intracranial lesions while MET exon 14 skipping is less frequent than in primary tumors. Mechanistically, MET signaling supports key steps of brain colonization, including trans-endothelial invasion across the blood–brain barrier, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and context-dependent adaptation to the brain microenvironments; evidence from other tumor types further reinforces these pro-metastatic programs. Precision management requires robust molecular profiling: RNA-based next-generation sequencing improves detection of splice events such as MET exon 14 skipping, while tissue and liquid biopsy (including cerebrospinal fluid–derived ctDNA when feasible) can capture intracranial clonal evolution. Clinically, selective MET tyrosine kinase inhibitors (e.g., capmatinib, tepotinib) have demonstrated intracranial activity in small BM subgroups, and combination strategies, most notably dual EGFR/MET blockade using MET inhibitors or EGFR/MET-directed antibodies, are emerging as rational approaches for MET-driven resistance, although heterogeneous CNS endpoints and prior brain-directed therapies limit cross-study comparability.Short conclusionMET is an actionable vulnerability in NSCLC-BM and should be considered beyond treatment-naïve MET-driven disease, extending to patients with acquired MET amplification during EGFR-TKI exposure. BM-dedicated trials with standardized intracranial endpoints and integrated pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic assessment are needed to optimize patient selection, sequencing, and resistance-informed combination strategies.Keywords: MET alterations, MET exon 14 skipping, MET amplification, Brain metastasis, Non-small cell lung cancer

Background

The treatment outcomes of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) are constrained by the high incidence of brain metastases (BM) and its poor prognosis [1–3]. Epidemiological studies indicate that approximately 10% of patients present with BM at initial diagnosis, while an additional 26%–53% develop BM during the progress of disease [2, 4]. Although advances in systemic therapies have prolonged overall survival in NSCLC, this has also increased the cumulative risk of BM, making it one of the leading causes of treatment failure [1, 5, 6]. The median survival after treatment for NSCLC-BM remains poor, typically only 7–12 months [7].

The unique anatomical and physiological characteristics of the brain present persistent challenges for the treatment of BM. The blood-brain barrier (BBB) markedly limits the penetration of most chemotherapeutic agents and certain targeted therapies into the central nervous system (CNS) [8–10]. Conventional approaches, including whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT), stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), and surgical resection, provide only limited long-term control and are frequently associated with neurocognitive decline and reduced quality of life [11, 12]. In recent years, targeted therapies such as EGFR-TKIs and ALK-TKIs have significantly improved intracranial disease control in patients harboring driver gene mutations [13–17]. Nonetheless, acquired resistance remains a major obstacle, and strategies for BM treatment targeting emerging molecular drivers, such as mesenchymal–epithelial transition factor (MET), remain in the early exploratory stage.

Among these, MET holds particular significance due to its dual pathogenic role. As a classical proto-oncogene, MET alterations not only play a pivotal role in the development of BM in NSCLC but also serve as a major mechanism of acquired resistance in EGFR-mutant NSCLC [18]. These alterations, which promote tumor cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and angiogenesis [19–21], include: (i) MET exon 14 skipping mutations (METex14 skipping), (ii) gene amplification, (iii) point mutations, (iv) gene fusions, and (v) protein overexpression [17]. METex14 skipping occurs in 3%–4% of NSCLC cases, whereas MET amplification is observed in 1%–5% at baseline and has been reported in 20%–24% of EGFR-TKI resistant cases [22–24]. MET gene fusions, predominantly KIF5B-MET, occur in 0.26%–0.5% [25] of NSCLC cases. MET protein overexpression occurs in 15%–70% of NSCLC cases, reaching up to 65% in lung adenocarcinoma subtype [26]. Importantly, more than 30% of patients with MET alterations develop BM, with a threefold higher mortality risk compared to MET–wild type [27, 28]. Nevertheless, comprehensive epidemiological evidence regarding the frequency of specific MET alterations in non-small cell lung cancer brain metastases (NSCLC-BM) remains limited.

Preliminary evidence suggests that MET activation is preferentially enriched in BM compared with paired primary lung tumors, indicating a potential biological role in CNS dissemination [29]. However, existing studies investigating MET-related mechanisms in NSCLC-BM remain fragmented and lack systematic synthesis. Furthermore, although selective MET inhibitors such as capmatinib and tepotinib have been approved for METex14-positive NSCLC, evidence guiding MET-targeted strategies for NSCLC-BM, particularly regarding their intracranial objective response rate (iORR) and intracranial disease control rate (iDCR), remains limited [30].

This review therefore aims to provide a systematic overview of the incidence, mechanisms, diagnostic approaches, therapeutic strategies and future perspectives of MET alterations in NSCLC-BM, thereby providing a theoretical foundation and guiding future precision therapies.

Incidence of MET gene alterations in NSCLC-BM

Although no large-scale study has systematically reported the overall prevalence of MET alterations in NSCLC-BM, existing evidence suggests a differential pattern: MET amplification appears to be relatively enriched in BM, whereas METex14 skipping occurs at lower frequencies. For example, Richard et al. (2022) analyzed 3,035 NSCLC-BM samples and reported that, compared with primary NSCLC, the incidence of MET amplification increased significantly from 2.3% to 4.4% (P < 0.05), whereas METex14 skipping decreased from 2.4% to 1.0% (P < 0.05) [31].

To further validate these trends, we analyzed genomic data from 233 patients included in the MSKCC 2023 NSCLC-BM cohort (cBioPortal ID: bm_nsclc_mskcc_2023) [32]. The distribution of MET alterations was as follows:

Clinically, MET alteration subtype has distinct therapeutic implications. MET exon 14 skipping more often represents a primary, oncogene-addicted driver state and is generally associated with higher and more durable responses to selective MET-TKIs (including intracranial activity with CNS-penetrant agents). In contrast, copy-number–driven MET amplification is commonly observed in the setting of EGFR-TKI selective pressure and may reflect a more heterogeneous resistance biology [33, 34], for which MET-TKI monotherapy can be less reliable and EGFR/MET combination strategies may be considered to optimize CNS control.

Accordingly, MET-targeted strategies for NSCLC-BM may be considered beyond treatment-naïve METex14-mutant disease to encompass the larger, high-need population with acquired MET amplification, potentially emerging even prior to overt EGFR-TKI failure.

MET signaling–mediated molecular mechanisms in NSCLC-BM

Structure and signaling pathway of c-MET

The MET proto-oncogene encodes the transmembrane tyrosine kinase receptor c-MET, whose only known ligand is hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) [19]. c-Met is primarily composed of two structural parts: extracellular and intracellular domains. The extracellular domain mainly contains one semaphorin (SEMA) domain, one plexin-semaphorin-integrin (PSI) domain, and four consecutive immunoglobulin-plexin-transcription factors (IPT1-4) domains; the intracellular domain is mainly composed of three parts: a juxtamembrane (JM) domain, a tyrosine kinase (TK) domain, and a C-terminal multifunctional docking site (MDS) [17, 19, 35]. When HGF binds to c-MET, it triggers receptor dimerization and autophosphorylation at key tyrosine residues (Tyr1234/1235 and Tyr1349/1356), thereby initiating multiple downstream signaling cascades [19, 36, 37]. In contrast, phosphorylation at Tyr1003 within the JM domain facilitates the recruitment of the E3 ubiquitin ligase c-Cbl, which mediates c-MET ubiquitination [38]. This modification promotes receptor internalization, followed by either recycling back to the plasma membrane or lysosomal degradation, thereby regulating its expression and signaling [39].

MET signaling is tightly regulated in both spatial and temporal dimensions, with phosphorylated tyrosine residues at the C-termini serve as docking sites that directly recruit effectors such as the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K), the non-receptor tyrosine kinase Src, the growth factor receptor-bound protein 2 (GRB2) and SH2 domain-containing transforming protein (SHC) adaptors, the tyrosine phosphatase SHP2, phospholipase Cγ1 (PLCγ1), and the transcription factor STAT3 [40–44]. Through both direct and indirect interaction, MET engages the scaffolding adaptor proteins such as α6β4 integrin and GRB2-associated-binding protein 1 (GAB1), which further amplifies downstream signaling outputs [45, 46].

Upon MET activation, the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway is engaged through multiple routes: extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERKs) are activated via the canonical GRB2-SOS→RAS→RAF→MEK→ERK module [40, 47], whereas c-Jun N-terminal kinases (JNKs) and p38 MAPKs are stimulated through the RAS–PI3K→RAC→MEKK→MEK→JNK/p38 axis [48–52], collectively regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, transformation and apoptosis. The PI3K–Akt pathway can be activated directly by MET and/or indirectly via Ras, and promotes cell survival and proliferation by inhibiting pro-apoptotic BCL-2-associated death promoter (BAD), activating E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase MDM2 to degrade p53, suppressing glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β) to antagonize the expression of positive cell cycle regulators, and stimulating mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) activity [40, 53, 54]. In addition, STAT3, directly phosphorylated by MET, dissociates from the receptor, dimerizes, translocates to the nucleus, and acts as a transcription factor to regulate genes involved in cell proliferation and differentiation [43, 44]. In parallel, PI3K/AKT- and Src-dependent activation of IκB kinase (IKK) induces degradation of nuclear factor-κB inhibitor-α (IκBα), thereby releasing nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) to enter the nucleus and regulate mitogenic and anti-apoptotic processes [55–57].

Signal transduction is further modulated by the colocalization of signal transducers in organelles and vesicles. Through clathrin-mediated endocytosis, MET sustains ERKs activation within early endosomes, later relocating them to focal complexes to drive cell migration—a process regulated by protein kinase Cɛ (PKCɛ) [39, 58].

Collectively, these interconnected pathways coordinate critical oncogenic processes—including proliferation, survival, migration, invasion, and angiogenesis—thereby establishing the core molecular framework that drives MET-dependent tumorigenesis and metastatic progression [20, 21] (Fig. 2).

Overview of MET downstream signalling pathways. Binding of HGF to MET induces receptor dimerization and autophosphorylation at Tyr1234/1235 (kinase domain) and Tyr1349/1356 (C-terminal multifunctional docking site), recruiting key adaptors and effectors such as GRB2, GAB1, PI3K, SHP2, and STAT3. This activates multiple canonical pathways: (1) MAPK cascades (GRB2–SOS→Ras→Raf→MEK→ERK; Ras–PI3K→Rac→MEKK→JNK/p38), regulating proliferation, differentiation, and stress responses; (2) PI3K–Akt axis, promoting survival, inhibiting apoptosis, and activating mTOR; (3) STAT3 pathway, driving transcription of genes linked to cell cycle progression and invasion; (4) IκBα–NF-κB pathway, in which PI3K/Akt and Src activate IKK, relieve NF-κB inhibition, and induce pro-survival/pro-inflammatory gene expression. MET signalling is further amplified via co-receptors such as α6β4 integrin and CD44v6, linking to cytoskeletal rearrangement, and sustained in early endosomes to support migration. Collectively, this network coordinates proliferation, survival, migration, EMT, and brain metastasis formation in NSCLC

Role of MET signaling in NSCLC cell crossing of the blood–brain barrier

A rate-limiting step in metastatic progression is the invasion and colonization of distant organs by circulating tumor cells [59]. For BM, penetration across the BBB represents the critical bottleneck in this process [60, 61]. The integrity of the BBB is primarily maintained by tight junctions (TJs) and adherens junctions (AJs) in brain microvascular endothelial cells. These junctions consist of a complex network of proteins including occludin, junctional adhesion molecules (JAMs), claudins, zonula occludens (ZO), and vascular endothelial (VE)-cadherin, collectively safeguard barrier stability [62]. Disruption of this structural network results in the loss of intercellular contacts and the formation of paracellular gaps, thereby facilitating tumor cell trans-endothelial migration [63]. Studies have demonstrated that NSCLC cells can activate MET through the mesothelin–JNK–MET signaling axis, thereby enhancing the invasiveness of tumor cells disseminated in the bloodstream by promoting the release of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [64]. These MMPs degrade endothelial TJs and facilitate the transmigration of tumor cells across the BBB, ultimately enabling colonization within the CNS (Fig. 3). In addition, MET signaling has been implicated in promoting epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in NSCLC, which can enhance migratory and invasive capacity and may facilitate BM dissemination [65].

Functional studies have provided direct evidence for the critical role of MET signaling in driving NSCLC-BM. In EGFR-mutant NSCLC models, specific knockdown of the MET gene did not affect overall tumor burden or the occurrence of bone metastases, but it markedly delayed the brain metastatic potential of tumor cells in vitro. More importantly, in animal models, MET knockdown reduced the incidence of BM from 100% in control groups to only 30% [66]. These findings underscore the unique role of aberrant MET activation in NSCLC cells in facilitating their ability to traverse the BBB and successfully colonize the brain.

Potential role of MET-driven metabolic reprogramming in adaptation to brain metastasis

To adapt to the unique microenvironment of the brain, metastatic tumor cells undergo extensive metabolic reprogramming to meet energy demands and cope with immune and nutritional constraints [67–73]. In NSCLC-BM, metabolic remodeling has been suggested, including a shift toward increased glycolytic reliance relative to oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), potentially enabling efficient glucose utilization to sustain elevated energy and biosynthetic requirements [74]. Previous studies have shown that MET signaling can promote a glycolytic phenotype and increase lactate secretion in certain tumor types, a metabolic adaptation that enhances tumor cell survival and invasive capacity [75]. However, direct evidence linking MET to metabolic adaptation specifically in NSCLC-BM remains limited; therefore, we present this as a hypothesis-generating concept that warrants further experimental and clinical validation. Elucidating the mechanisms by which MET regulates tumor metabolism may provide deeper insights into the molecular basis of NSCLC-BM and open new avenues for targeted therapeutic strategies.

MET activation modes: oncogene expedience vs. addiction

Notably, two distinct modes of MET activation have been described in tumors: “oncogene expedience” and “oncogene addiction” [76]. Oncogene expedience generally arises during late-stage tumor progression under the influence of the tumor microenvironment. It drives metastasis through the coordinated activity of multiple non-redundant pathways—RAS promoting proliferation, PI3K facilitating invasion, and JNK/STAT providing supportive signals [43, 48, 77–79]. In this context, targeting any key pathway can substantially suppress the associated malignant phenotype. By contrast, oncogene addiction stems from driver gene mutations that induce constitutive MET hyperactivation. Tumor cells rely primarily on core pathways, such as RAS and PI3K, to sustain malignant behaviors. Effective inhibition in this context requires direct targeting of the MET receptor itself, although such tumors are prone to signaling escape mechanisms (Fig. 4).

Distinct modes of MET activation. MET signaling can be activated via (i) oncogene expedience, driven by the tumor microenvironment and coordinated activity of multiple pathways (e.g., RAS, PI3K, JNK/STAT), or (ii) oncogene addiction, resulting from driver mutations that induce constitutive MET hyperactivation, rendering tumor cells reliant on core pathways and sensitive to direct MET inhibition

As BM represents an advanced progression of NSCLC, both activation modes may coexist, providing a conceptual framework for therapeutic strategy. Context-dependent “oncogene expedience”, potentially driven by brain microenvironmental adaptation or EGFR-TKI pressure, may be best addressed by multi-pathway inhibition to disrupt cooperative signaling networks. In contrast, mutation-driven “oncogene addiction” requires highly selective MET inhibitors with efficient CNS penetration, with vigilance for bypass pathway activation.

Cross-cancer mechanisms underlying MET-driven brain metastasis

Beyond its established role in NSCLC-BM, studies from other malignancies have identified MET-driven mechanisms that provide cross-cancer insights into the metastatic process. In breast cancer BM, activated MET signaling promotes the adhesion of tumor cells to brain endothelial cells by inducing the secretion of IL8 and CXCL1. Moreover, MET activation stimulates IL1β release, which in turn activates tumor-associated astrocytes to secrete HGF, establishing a MET-driven positive feedback loop that maintains a microenvironment conducive to metastatic cell survival [80]. In melanoma BM, MET receptor activation may be elicited by inflammatory processes mediated by stromal cell-derived HGF, thereby promoting tumor self-maintenance and expansion and potentially counteracting the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [81]. Collectively, these findings underscore the pivotal role of MET signaling in driving BM across tumor types and provide a biological rationale for the broader development of MET-targeted interventions against BM.

Molecular diagnostics of MET alterations in NSCLC-BM

Available detection methods for MET alterations

Accurate molecular detection is critical for patient stratification and targeted therapy selection in NSCLC-BM. Among the actionable driver alterations, METex14 skipping and amplification are particularly dependent on next-generation sequencing (NGS) for reliable identification due to their splice-site and copy-number complexity.

In clinical practice, DNA-based NGS remains more commonly used because RNA is inherently more prone to degradation and requires stringent sample preservation conditions. However, DNA-based NGS may fail to detect certain splice-site variants—such as METex14 skipping—owing to limited intronic probe coverage, whereas RNA-based NGS can overcome this limitation by directly sequencing expressed transcripts [82].

For instance, among NSCLC patients who tested negative by conventional laboratory testing, DNA-based NGS identified METex14 skipping in 5.8% and MET amplification in 0.9% of cases. Furthermore, in the subgroup that was negative by DNA-based NGS (42.4% of patients), RNA-based NGS detected additional METex14 skipping events in 2.1% of cases [83]. Another study demonstrated that the concurrent use of DNA- and RNA-based NGS increased the detection rate of METex14 skipping by 18.6% compared with DNA-based NGS alone, underscoring the complementary value of RNA sequencing for comprehensive MET alteration profiling [84].

Therefore, in laboratories with appropriate testing capabilities, a combined DNA- and RNA-based NGS approach is recommended to maximize the detection of MET alterations and provide a more reliable molecular basis for subsequent personalized targeted therapies.

Detection of MET alterations in different sample types

Beyond the choice of sequencing platform, the accuracy of MET alterations detection in NSCLC-BM also depends critically on the type and accessibility of biological samples used for molecular testing. Although tissue biopsy remains the gold standard for molecular diagnosis, its use in NSCLC-BM is often restricted by lesion accessibility and procedural risk [31]. Liquid biopsy technologies thus provide valuable, minimally invasive alternatives. Plasma circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) testing has demonstrated high concordance with tissue-based assays across metastatic cancers; however, its sensitivity in NSCLC-BM is limited by the restrictive effects of the BBB, resulting in reduced detection rates [85]. In contrast, cerebrospinal-fluid (CSF)-derived ctDNA more accurately reflects the genomic landscape of intracranial tumors and yields higher detection rates for driver mutations [86, 87]. CSF ctDNA analysis can also predict intracranial tumor response and capture clonal evolution during treatment [88]. Furthermore, as metastatic precursors of distant disease, circulating tumor cells (CTCs) can be detected in the blood as single cells or clusters with enhanced survival and metastatic potential [89]. Although still in the exploratory stage, the integration of CTC analysis with other molecular diagnostic modalities holds promise for improving the detection of MET alterations and other actionable mutations in NSCLC-BM, thereby informing personalized therapeutic strategies.

Therapeutic strategies targeting MET in NSCLC-BM

Highly selective MET inhibitors

Candidate populations for MET-TKI therapy

Early clinical attempts to target MET alterations in NSCLC primarily explored the combination of broad-spectrum MET inhibitors with EGFR-TKIs (e.g., tivantinib or onartuzumab plus erlotinib). However, pivotal phase III trials such as METLung and ATTENTION failed to demonstrate meaningful survival benefit [90–92].

The identification of the METex14 skipping represented a major breakthrough in MET-targeted therapy. This alteration results in the deletion of the JM domain of the c-MET receptor, thereby preventing c-Cbl–mediated degradation and driving persistent activation of downstream signaling. To date, the METex14 skipping remains the principal indication for MET-TKI approval and guideline recommendation.

In addition, patients with high-level MET amplification (> = 5 gene copies) may derive clinical benefit from MET-TKI, though the efficacy is generally less pronounced compared with METex14 skipping disease. For example, the GEOMETRY mono-1 trial demonstrated that capmatinib achieved meaningful intracranial responses and prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with high-copy MET amplification, whereas the benefit in low-copy amplification was limited [93].

Regarding MET protein overexpression (assessed by IHC), although it is relatively common in NSCLC, its utility as an independent predictive biomarker remains controversial, and it is currently employed primarily as an adjunctive screening method [94–96]. Notably, the INSIGHT study demonstrated that, in patients with acquired EGFR-TKI resistance, those harboring MET amplification or high MET expression (IHC 3+) achieved superior PFS and overall survival (OS) when treated with tepotinib plus gefitinib compared with chemotherapy [97]. This finding underscores the importance of comprehensive biomarker-based screening strategies to optimize patient selection.

In summary, while patients harboring METex14 skipping represent the definitive indication for MET-TKI therapy, individuals with high-level MET amplification may also derive benefit in specific clinical contexts, albeit with considerable variability in efficacy. Future efforts should focus on standardizing detection criteria, optimizing patient selection, and validating the therapeutic value of MET-TKIs in patients with MET amplification or protein overexpression through prospective clinical trials.

Classification of MET-TKIs

Several classes of MET inhibitors have been developed and can be categorized according to their binding mode to the kinase ATP pocket. Type I inhibitors bind to the active conformation of the ATP pocket and are subdivided into Type Ia and Type Ib [98]. Type Ia inhibitors (e.g., crizotinib) interact with Y1230, the hinge region, and the glycine residue G1163 at the solvent front, but display limited selectivity [98]. In contrast, Type Ib inhibitors (including capmatinib, tepotinib, savolitinib, glumetinib, and vebreltinib) engage more strongly with Y1230 and the hinge region while sparing G1163, thereby achieving higher specificity for MET and markedly reducing off-target activity [28]. These agents represent the predominant class of MET-TKIs in current clinical practice. Type II inhibitors (e.g., merestinib, glesatinib, cabozantinib) target the inactive conformation of the ATP pocket and exhibit broader kinase inhibition profiles [98, 99]. Type III inhibitors are non-ATP-competitive allosteric inhibitors that act outside the ATP pocket by inducing conformational changes; however, clinical candidates remain in early development and none have yet been approved [100].

Clinical advances of MET-TKIs and intracranial efficacy

To date, a total of 76 clinical trials investigating MET-targeted therapies in NSCLC have been registered (Table 1, data retrieved from ClinicalTrials.gov, accessed October 2025). The majority of these studies focus on NSCLC harboring METex14 skipping or MET amplification, with a smaller subset exploring other alterations such as MET overexpression. Notably, most trials targeting MET amplification are conducted in the context of EGFR-mutant NSCLC, reflecting the emerging strategy of combining MET inhibition to overcome EGFR-TKI resistance. The primary agents under investigation are Type Ib MET inhibitors, with capmatinib being the most extensively studied, followed by tepotinib and savolitinib. Vebreltinib, glumetinib, and Type II inhibitors remain under evaluation in early-phase studies. Notably, only two registered clinical trials (e.g., NCT02132598 and NCT05120960) specifically listed brain metastases as an inclusion condition; however, neither has yet reported CNS efficacy data.

Ongoing and completed clinical trials of MET tyrosine kinase inhibitors in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)

Despite the high prevalence of BM in NSCLC, systematic evaluation of CNS activity of MET-TKIs remains limited. Through a comprehensive review of the 76 MET-targeted trials on ClinicalTrials.gov and corresponding publications indexed in PubMed, we identified six prospective clinical trials and one retrospective real-world study (RECAP) that included BM subgroups and provided intracranial response outcomes (Table 2). Overall, available data suggest that selective MET inhibitors exhibit clinically meaningful intracranial activity in selected patients. However, cross-study comparisons are inherently limited by heterogeneous eligibility criteria, prior brain-directed therapies, imaging schedules, and variable intracranial endpoint definitions (iORR/iDCR/iPFS), which constrain head-to-head interpretation across studies. Standardized CNS response criteria (e.g., RANO-BM) with prespecified MRI time points (e.g., every 6–8 weeks during early treatment) would improve comparability. With these caveats, capmatinib provides the most extensive evidence, with reported iORRs ranging from 45% to 81% and iDCRs exceeding 90% across multiple studies. Median intracranial progression-free survival (mPFS) varied between 3 and 14.5 months, reflecting heterogeneity in prior treatments and assessment criteria. Savolitinib and tepotinib have also demonstrated encouraging CNS responses, with iORRs of 50% and 66.7%, respectively, in small cohorts. Nevertheless, most intracranial analyses were exploratory and based on limited patient numbers, often including individuals who had received prior treatment, which may confound response interpretation.

In addition, the multicenter, single-arm, phase II KUNPENG study (NCT04258033) evaluated vebreltinib in patients with MET exon 14–mutant NSCLC. In the subgroup of patients with BM (n = 5), both the systemic objective response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR) reached 100%, with a median progression-free survival (mPFS) of 6.4 months. However, due to the very limited sample size, intracranial efficacy could not be reliably assessed.

Overall, these findings highlight both the therapeutic potential of MET-TKIs in NSCLC-BM and the persisting evidence gap regarding their CNS activity. There remains an urgent need for prospective, dedicated trials specifically designed for NSCLC patients with BM to rigorously evaluate the intracranial efficacy of MET-TKIs. Such studies should incorporate standardized CNS endpoints and carefully control for the confounding effects of prior brain-directed therapies, thereby providing a more accurate assessment of the true CNS activity of MET-targeted strategies.

Combination strategies targeting MET in NSCLC-BM

EGFR/MET dual inhibition

In patients with NSCLC harboring concurrent EGFR mutations and MET alterations—particularly MET amplification as a mechanism of acquired EGFR-TKI resistance—dual inhibition has emerged as a promising approach [34]. The mechanism is MET amplification results in bypass of EGFR downstream signaling through STAT, MAPK and PI3K pathways [34]. Clinical trials demonstrate that combining selective MET-TKIs (e.g., capmatinib or tepotinib) with EGFR-TKIs (e.g., osimertinib or gefitinib) can overcome bypass signaling, restore sensitivity, and achieve meaningful intracranial responses. The INSIGHT-2 trial exemplifies this strategy, showing improved progression-free survival and intracranial disease control in patients with MET-mediated EGFR-TKI resistance [101]. Optimizing timing, dosing, and patient selection based on molecular profiling remains critical for maximizing benefit.

Notably, amivantamab, the first fully human bispecific antibody targeting both EGFR and MET, has demonstrated significant efficacy in patients with brain metastases. In the MARIPOSA study, among patients with baseline brain metastases, the combination of amivantamab plus lazertinib achieved more than double the 3-year iPFS rate compared with osimertinib monotherapy (38% vs. 18%), and markedly prolonged the 3-year intracranial duration of response (icDoR) (51% vs. 0%) [102]. The combination also extended the median intracranial DoR to 35.7 months versus 29.6 months with osimertinib, while reducing the risk of death by 33% (HR = 0.67) [103].

In the MARIPOSA-2 trial, amivantamab combined with chemotherapy further improved intracranial outcomes, extending the median intracranial PFS to 12.5 months compared with 8.3 months in the chemotherapy-alone arm (HR = 0.55) [104]. Importantly, this benefit remained significant in patients without prior brain radiotherapy, confirming robust intracranial efficacy independent of local treatment [104].

MET inhibitors combined with chemoradiotherapy or anti-angiogenic therapy

Accumulating evidence indicates that the MET/HGF axis not only drives tumorigenesis but also contributes to resistance against chemoradiotherapies and anti-angiogenic treatments. In NSCLC, MET activation can upregulate membrane efflux transporters, thereby reducing the intracellular concentration of chemotherapeutic drugs and diminishing treatment efficacy [105]. Conversely, MET knockdown has been shown to restore cisplatin sensitivity by increasing intracellular drug accumulation within tumor cells [106].

During radiotherapy, MET upregulation promotes tumor cell invasion, inhibits apoptosis, and activates ATM-mediated DNA damage repair pathways, thereby facilitating adaptive responses to radiation-induced injury [107]. In addition, VEGF blockade may induce hypoxia-driven transcriptional activation of MET, fostering angiogenesis and conferring resistance to anti-angiogenic therapy [108, 109].

Collectively, these findings suggest that combining MET inhibition with these therapies can overcome or reverse resistance mechanisms, enhance chemo- and radiosensitivity, and improve overall antitumor responses. Future clinical trials should focus on evaluating safety, tolerability, and efficacy in metastatic NSCLC, with particular interest in combinations active in the CNS to address BM.

Antibody–drug conjugates

Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) targeting MET, such as telisotuzumab vedotin (Teliso-V), offer a promising therapeutic strategy for advanced or metastatic NSCLC with MET overexpression, by enabling the selective delivery of cytotoxic payloads to tumor cells.

In the phase II LUMINOSITY trial, Teliso-V achieved durable tumor responses in patients with c-Met protein–overexpressing, nonsquamous, EGFR-wild-type NSCLC, with particularly pronounced efficacy among those exhibiting high c-Met expression [110]. Adverse events were generally manageable, and the overall safety profile was favorable, supporting its potential application beyond METex14 skipping mutations.

Furthermore, results from a phase Ib study demonstrated that Teliso-V in combination with osimertinib elicited promising antitumor activity and good tolerability in patients with c-Met-overexpressing, EGFR-mutated NSCLC who had progressed after prior osimertinib treatment, highlighting its potential as a strategy to overcome TKI resistance [111].

However, dedicated data in brain metastasis cohorts remain scarce, and further investigation is warranted to elucidate the intracranial efficacy of MET-targeted ADCs such as Teliso-V.

Overcoming resistance mechanisms

Resistance to MET inhibitors arises through diverse and overlapping mechanisms. On-target resistance mechanisms involve mutations within the MET tyrosine kinase domain (TKD), with activation loop mutations at codons D1228 and Y1230 conferring resistance to type I MET-TKIs by disrupting inhibitor binding, and non–activation loop mutations at L1195 and F1200 associated with resistance to type II inhibitors [112–114]. In addition, alterations in signaling components, including mutations or amplifications in KRAS, BRAF, PIK3CA, MYC and PTEN, sustain downstream activation despite MET blockade [115–118]. Bypass signaling through EGFR, HER2, or HER3 amplification further maintains PI3K–AKT–mTOR and MAPK pathway activity, promoting tumor survival and proliferation [115, 119].

To address these mechanisms, sequential switching between type I and type II MET-TKIs, or combining MET-TKIs with anti-HGF/MET antibodies, may enhance pathway suppression and overcome resistance [120]. Integrative approaches combining MET inhibition with PI3K/MAPK pathway inhibitors have also shown potential to restore drug sensitivity [118]. Moreover, prolonged TKI exposure can induce glycolytic reprogramming and lactate accumulation, stimulating NF-κB–mediated HGF secretion from fibroblasts and reactivating MET signaling [121]. Targeting this metabolic–HGF–MET axis, alongside dynamic molecular profiling for real-time resistance monitoring, may enable adaptive and precision management of MET-driven NSCLC, particularly in patients with BM.

Conclusions

MET represents a clinically consequential node in NSCLC-BM because of its dual relevance: it serves as a primary oncogenic driver in a molecular subset of tumors and as a convergent bypass pathway mediating acquired resistance in the much larger EGFR-mutant population. Available genomic datasets support a distinct pattern of MET alterations in BM, in which MET amplification appears relatively enriched, whereas METex14 skipping is less frequent in intracranial lesions. Mechanistically, MET signaling promotes tumor cell invasion across the BBB, EMT, and potentially metabolic adaptation to the brain microenvironment. Two mechanistic paradigms, “oncogene addiction” and “oncogene expedience”, together capture the heterogeneity of MET activation in NSCLC-BM and define distinct therapeutic vulnerabilities.

From a translational perspective, selective MET inhibitors have demonstrated clinically meaningful intracranial activity in small BM subgroups; however, the overall CNS evidence base remains limited by heterogeneous eligibility criteria, prior brain-directed therapies, and inconsistent CNS endpoints. In parallel, diagnostic accuracy for MET alterations, particularly amplification and METex14 skipping, depends on platform choice (DNA vs. RNA) and specimen type (tissue vs. plasma vs. CSF), underscoring the need for standardized testing workflows in NSCLC-BM. Collectively, current data support MET as an actionable vulnerability in NSCLC-BM and justify expanding the therapeutic focus beyond treatment-naïve METex14-mutant disease to include patients with acquired MET amplification in the setting of EGFR-TKI exposure and CNS progression.

Future directions

Building on the above evidence, several hypothesis-generating directions merit prospective validation. First, as MET alterations do not confer uniform sensitivity to MET-TKIs, we hypothesize that BBB constraints in BM may differentially attenuate benefit for copy-number-driven MET amplification relative to the “signal-persistence” biology of METex14 skipping; stratified trials with prespecified intracranial endpoints would be essential to test this. In addition, MET has been reported to sustain signaling from early endosomes, which may limit the efficacy of ATP-binding site TKIs; accordingly, rational strategies—such as co-targeting endocytic/recycling pathways or distal ERK signaling—merit evaluation. Because adequately powered, BM-dedicated studies remain scarce, there is a need for trials that include measurable intracranial disease, use standardized neuro-oncology endpoints (e.g., RANO-BM), and integrate CSF PK/PD assessments to connect BBB penetration with treatment efficacy across regimens. Moreover, patients with microenvironment-driven “oncogene expedience” may further benefit from combining MET-TKIs with anti-angiogenic therapy and chemotherapy to enhance CNS penetration and potency. Finally, routine RNA-based NGS to capture splice events, alongside DNA-based NGS, may support adaptive treatment decisions.
