Letter to the Editor – ‘Early identification of delayed wound healing in complex diabetic foot ulcers treated with a dermal regeneration template: a novel clinical target and its risk factors’
Authors: Hewen Guan, Yuankuan Jiang, Jingrong Lin
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

Recently, a clinical study titled ‘Early identification of delayed wound healing in complex diabetic foot ulcers treated with a dermal regeneration template: a novel clinical target and its risk factors’ sparked great interest in a new non-surgical alternative treatment proposed for diabetic foot ulcers1. This retrospective study classified patients into graft success and graft failure groups based on the effectiveness of dermal regeneration template (DRT) transplantation and conducted intergroup statistical analysis. The authors also proposed an assessment tool to guide clinical practice, highlighting a correlation between low graft take rates on day 21 and delayed wound healing. By comparing differences between the two groups, the authors identified four independent risk factors influencing this treatment, enabling clinicians to better address and intervene in these risk factors to reduce the occurrence of graft failure. The study comprehensively explores the significant potential of DRT in promoting diabetic foot ulcer healing, suggesting that DRT will be chosen by more patients in the future. However, to ensure diabetic foot ulcer patients can more accurately select appropriate treatment methods, we believe some issues in this study need attention.

Firstly, this article conducted statistical analysis on baseline data before the treatment of patients, including patient characteristics, laboratory data and examinations, wound characteristics, and classification. Compared to the assessment method proposed by the authors, which evaluates graft take rates on day 21, a clinical prediction model incorporating multiple predictive factors should be developed based on patient clinical data to infer the long-term therapeutic effects of DRT. Currently, machine learning algorithms have been widely applied in various fields of clinical research. Tong et al.’s2 study identified seven key predictive factors through multivariate analysis and established a prediction model for post-thoracoscopic atrial fibrillation occurrence. The advantage of such model development is that it allows clinicians to collect quantifiable predictive variables and assess risks. Presently, artificial intelligence is gradually being proposed in the classification, diagnosis, and prognosis models of diabetic foot ulcers, including predicting clinical events (severity, infection) and risk stratification. This study can develop a new classification model based on patient clinical images and data using techniques such as deep learning and machine models to predict graft success rates. However, the development of machine learning models requires a large amount of data for training and evaluation of model performance through multiple validation cohorts. Therefore, increasing the sample size and external validation of this study are essential.

Secondly, diabetic foot ulcers, as chronic and difficult-to-heal wounds, are associated with multiple factors. A recent study has shown that the detection of wound pH value and surface temperature can serve as predictive indicators of wound healing3. This clinical study in question should incorporate more clinically relevant indicators to predict wound healing from multiple aspects. Additionally, the microbial community present in diabetic foot ulcers is closely related to wound healing. Percival et al.’s4 study suggests that diabetic foot ulcers provide an ideal habitat for microorganisms and discusses the possibility of complex microbial communities in wound samples based on both traditional bacterial culture and molecular genomics of diabetic foot ulcer microbiota. The study being discussed should further explore the association between DRT and different microbial communities by incorporating relevant analysis of patients’ skin microbiota.

Finally, regarding the extensive repair of large-area foot skin wounds in diabetic foot ulcers, traditional skin flap transplantation remains one of the main treatment methods. Ou et al.’s5 study found that the combination transfer of multiple skin perforator flaps can more substantially repair large-area wounds in the foot and ankle. The study in question will enhance its consideration of long-term treatment effects and recurrence rates by extending the follow-up period for DRT therapy and providing a better comparison with the currently used skin flap transplantation method. Additionally, the sample size of the current study is limited, so the conclusions drawn need further validation through multi-centre and more prospective studies to ensure accuracy and a broader scope. By increasing the validation through multi-centre cohorts, the generalisability of the study results can be more comprehensively evaluated, ensuring the effectiveness, reliability, and clinical applicability of the study conclusions across different races and populations.

In conclusion, the proposed DRT is a new alternative treatment for diabetic foot ulcers that is highly clinically applicable. Addressing the issues we raised above can further improve the realisation of personalised medicine for diabetic foot ulcers. The optimisation suggestions outlined above complement and enhance the study protocol, aiming to support the study results from multiple perspectives and further enhance the rigour of the study.
