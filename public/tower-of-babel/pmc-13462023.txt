A preliminary cross-sectional study of bipolarity and primary pharmacotherapy in long-term stable patients with major depressive disorder
Authors: Taku Maruki, Takashi Tsuboi, Hitoshi Maeshima, Yasuyuki Matsumoto, Teruo Tada, Koichiro Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with major depressive disorder (MDD) who exhibit high bipolarity are at increased risk of future diagnostic conversion to bipolar disorder. However, treatment recommendations for MDD with bipolarity are constrained by limited direct evidence. We examined the association between bipolarity and primary pharmacotherapy in long-term stable outpatients with MDD and explored a clinically useful Bipolarity Index (BI) threshold.MethodsIn this two-center cross-sectional study, participants were classified into an antidepressant (AD) group or a mood stabilizer/second-generation antipsychotic (MS/SGA) group. Bipolarity was assessed with the BI. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to identify a BI cutoff discriminating MS/SGA from AD.ResultsOf 103 participants, 54 were assigned to the AD group and 49 to the MS/SGA group. The BI score was significantly higher in the MS/SGA group than in the AD group (24.1 ± 12.9 vs 13.3± 7.0; p < 0.001). ROC analysis indicated a BI cutoff of 16 (Area Under the Curve = 0.765), yielding 69.4% sensitivity and 77.8% specificity for identifying the MS/SGA group.ConclusionAmong long-term stable outpatients with MDD, higher BI scores were associated with the use of MS and/or SGA rather than antidepressant monotherapy as primary pharmacotherapy. In this Japanese stable outpatient sample, a BI score of 16 or above identified patients who were more likely to be receiving MS and/or SGA. These findings reflect prescription patterns observed in this cross-sectional sample and should not be interpreted as evidence that MS and/or SGA are preferable treatments for patients with greater bipolarity. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify the clinical significance of BI-defined bipolarity and its relationship to treatment outcomes in patients with MDD.Keywords: Major depressive disorder, Bipolar disorder, Bipolarity, Bipolarity index, Mood stabilizer, Second-generation antipsychotic, Pharmacotherapy

Introduction

Approximately one-third of patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) develop treatment-resistant depression (TRD) despite standard therapies such as antidepressants. (Rush et al. 2006) One explanation highlights limitations of current DSM/ICD-based operational criteria: patients presenting with depressive episodes but without (hypo)mania are initially diagnosed with MDD, and a proportion subsequently meet criteria for bipolar disorder (BD). (Sharma et al. 2005; Inoue et al. 2006; Li et al. 2012; Murao et al. 2024) About two-thirds of individuals with BD present with a depressive episode at illness onset, and when the illness begins with a depressive episode, the cumulative time spent in depressive states exceeds that spent in manic states across the lifespan. (Daban et al. 2006; Bartoli et al. 2024) Thus, an initial MDD label often persists until (hypo)mania emerges, with recognition of BD sometimes delayed by a decade or more. (Hirschfeld et al. 2003; Kessing et al. 2017) During this interval, antidepressants—agents not generally recommended as monotherapy for bipolar depression— are commonly prescribed, which may contribute to poor response, treatment resistance, and increased risks of manic/hypomanic switching and suicide. (Stahl et al. 2017; Yatham et al. 2018; Rybakowski 2012).

To address this challenge, several studies have evaluated bipolarity in patients diagnosed with MDD as a means to predict the risk of later diagnostic transition to BD. (Inoue et al. 2015; Takeshima and Oka 2013; Benazzi 2004; Kiejna et al. 2006; Perlis et al. 2006) In this context, bipolarity denotes clinical characteristics in MDD associated with such transition risk, commonly including early onset of depression, family history of BD, mixed features, recurrent depressive episodes, cyclothymic temperament, and a history of suicide attempts.

However, even if the likelihood of diagnostic transition can be predicted, evidence remains limited regarding optimal pharmacotherapy for MDD patients with high bipolarity. For example, some treatment guidelines recommend pharmacotherapy aligned with bipolar depression such as mood stabilizers (MS) and second-generation antipsychotics (SGA) for MDD with mixed features, yet the supporting data largely derive from studies of bipolar depression with mixed features rather than from cohorts strictly diagnosed with MDD. (Stahl et al. 2017; Yatham et al. 2021; Natale et al. 2022) As a result, treatment recommendations for MDD with prominent bipolarity are constrained by a paucity of direct evidence, underscoring the need for studies that specifically examine pharmacotherapy for patients with MDD who exhibit high bipolarity but have not (yet) met criteria for BD.

The aim of this study was to investigate the association between primary pharmacotherapy and bipolarity in patients with long-term stable MDD. We hypothesized that patients with lower bipolarity would be more likely to receive antidepressants alone, whereas those with higher bipolarity would be more likely to receive MS or SGA, with or without antidepressants. Because this was a cross-sectional study, the objective was to describe prescribing patterns in relation to bipolarity rather than to evaluate treatment effectiveness or validate pharmacological strategies. In particular, no treatment outcome data, such as symptom improvement, relapse prevention, or long-term prognosis, were included in the present study.

Methods

Design and setting

This two-center cross-sectional study was conducted from June 2022 to April 2024 at the Department of Neuropsychiatry, Kyorin University Hospital (Tokyo, Japan), and the Department of Psychiatry, Juntendo Koshigaya Hospital (Saitama, Japan). The study protocol was approved by the School of Medicine Research Ethics Committee, Kyorin University (R04-003-05) and the Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine, Juntendo University (E22-0225). The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments and to Japan’s Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment after a full explanation of the study procedures.

Participants

Eligible participants were consecutive outpatients aged 18–65 years who met DSM-5 criteria for MDD. To be included, participants were required to have maintained the same primary pharmacotherapy—defined as the same primary antidepressant, MS, or SGA—for at least 6 months before enrollment; dose reduction was permitted, but switching or initiation of a new primary agent was not. For the purposes of this study, patients meeting this criterion were considered to have long-term stable MDD.

Exclusion criteria were dementia (major neurocognitive disorder), current psychotic symptoms, alcohol- or substance-use disorder, and severe physical illness.

Clinical assessments

Participants completed the Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology–Self Report (QIDS-SR) and the Temperament Evaluation of Memphis, Pisa, Paris, and San Diego–Auto Questionnaire (TEMPS-A). The Bipolarity Index (BI) was completed by each participant’s treating psychiatrist on the basis of clinical information and medical records, and the resulting data were collected and analyzed by the authors.

The QIDS-SR is a widely recognized self-report questionnaire of depressive symptom severity. (Rush et al. 2003) It consists 16 items assessing nine symptom domains (sleep, sad mood, appetite/weight, concentration/decision making, self-view, suicidal ideation, general interest, energy/fatigue, and psychomotor changes); items are rated on a 4-point scale (0 = none, 3 = severe) with a total score of 0-27. Conventional severity bands are: no depression (0–5), mild (6–10), moderate (11–15), severe (16–20), and very severe (≥21).

The TEMPS-A is a 110-item yes/no self-report questionnaire that evaluates five affective temperaments—depressive, cyclothymic, hyperthymic, irritable, and anxious. (Akiskal et al. 2005) Several studies have supported its utility in differentiating MDD from BD. (Morishita et al. 2020; Mendlowicz et al. 2005; Mazzarini et al. 2009).

The BI is a clinician-rated tool designed to quantify manic/hypomanic factors and support the diagnosis of BD. (Aiken et al. 2015; Sachs 2004; Robins and Guze 1970) It has demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy. (Sayyah et al. 2022) The BI comprises five domains: (I) episode characteristics, (II) age of onset, (III) course of illness and associated features, (IV) response to treatment, and (V) family history. Each domain is scored 0-20, yielding a total score of 0-100; higher scores indicate greater bipolar features.

Current psychotropic medications were abstracted from medical records and confirmed with patients. Associations between pharmacotherapy and BI scores were analyzed as described below. The primary outcome was the BI score; secondary outcomes were the BI cutoff value and TEMPS-A scores in both groups.

Classification by primary pharmacotherapy

Current psychotropic medications were abstracted from medical records and confirmed with participants at the time of assessment. Participants were classified into two groups according to their primary pharmacotherapy: the AD group, consisting of patients treated with antidepressants alone, and the MS/SGA group, consisting of patients treated with MS or SGA, with or without concomitant antidepressants. Patients who were not receiving pharmacotherapy at the time of assessment were excluded rather than assigned to the AD group, because the absence of pharmacotherapy is not equivalent to antidepressant treatment. The primary objective of the study was to examine the association between pharmacotherapy pattern and BI score, rather than to evaluate treatment effectiveness.

Data analysis

Continuous variables were compared using two-sided t tests, and dichotomous variables were compared using χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was set at p value of <0.05 (two-sided) was considered statistically significant. For the five BI subdomain comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied, and the adjusted significance threshold was set at p < 0.01. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to determine the optimal cutoff of the BI total scores for discriminating the MS/SGA group from the AD group. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess discrimination, with AUC values of 0.7–0.8, 0.8–0.9, and 0.9–1.0 interpreted as acceptable, high, and excellent accuracy, respectively. (Swets 1988) The Youden index (J = sensitivity + specificity − 1) was applied to identify the optimal cutoff value, representing the point with the highest combined sensitivity and specificity. In addition, two exploratory post hoc analyses were performed. First, as a sensitivity analysis, we recalculated a modified BI (BI – BI IV) total score excluding domain IV (response to treatment) and compared this score between the two treatment groups. Second, we performed a multivariable logistic regression analysis to examine whether BI remained associated with treatment class after adjustment for selected clinical covariates. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 103 participants (50 male and 53 female) were enrolled, and none withdrew consent. Of these, 54 were classified into the AD group and 49 into the MS/SGA group. The mean age was similar between groups (48.3 ± 10.8 years in the AD group vs 48.0 ± 12.6 years in the MS/SGA group). There were significant differences in age at onset (40.6 ± 9.4 years in the AD group vs 34.2 ± 10.8 years in the MS/SGA group, p = 0.002) and in QIDS-SR scores (6.8 ± 4.6 in the AD group vs 9.1 ± 5.7 in the MS/SGA group, p = 0.027). No other significant between-group differences were observed. Regarding pharmacotherapy, lithium, aripiprazole, and quetiapine were prescribed more frequently in the MS/SGA group. BI score

The mean BI total score was significantly higher in the MS/SGA group than in the AD group (24.1 ± 12.9 vs 13.3 ± 7.0; p < 0.001). By domain, the MS/SGA group showed higher scores on episode characteristics, age of onset, course of illness and associated features, and response to treatment (all p < 0.001) with no significant difference in family history (Table 2).

To examine whether BI was independently associated with treatment class, we performed a multivariable logistic regression analysis with treatment class as the dependent variable and age at onset, sex, anxiolytic use, hypnotic use, QIDS-SR score, and BI as independent variables. BI was significantly associated with treatment class (odds ratio = 1.104, 95% confidence interval = 1.040–1.173, p = 0.001), whereas age at onset, sex, anxiolytic use, hypnotic use, and QIDS-SR score were not significant.

BI cutoff for identifying MS/SGA group

The AUC for discriminating the MS/SGA group from the AD group using BI total score was 0.765 (95% CI, 0.67–0.86), indicating acceptable discrimination (Fig. 1). The Youden index identified 16 as the optimal cutoff, yielding 69.4% sensitivity and 77.8% specificity (Table 3). Accordingly, a BI total score ≥16 best identified patients in the MS/SGA group.

Discussion

In this cohort of long-term stable outpatients with MDD, higher bipolarity as indexed by the BI was associated with the use of MS and/or SGA as primary pharmacotherapy. However, this finding reflects treatments that were actually prescribed in this sample, rather than treatments that should be prescribed. It may indicate that, in routine clinical practice, clinicians were more likely to prescribe these medications to patients who already exhibited more prominent bipolar features, raising the possibility of confounding by indication.

Because the groups differed not only in BI but also in other clinically relevant characteristics, we additionally performed a multivariable logistic regression analysis including age at onset, sex, anxiolytic use, hypnotic use, QIDS-SR score, and BI. In this model, BI remained significantly associated with treatment class (OR = 1.104, 95% CI = 1.040–1.173, p = 0.001), whereas the other covariates were not significant. These results suggest that the association between BI and MS/SGA-based pharmacotherapy was not fully explained by the measured covariates. Nevertheless, the cross-sectional observational design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding remains an important concern.

Another methodological concern is that one domain of the BI reflects response to treatment, whereas the grouping variable in the present study was current pharmacotherapy. This creates a risk that the predictor and outcome were not fully independent constructs. In Table 2, the response-to-treatment domain showed one of the largest between-group differences (AD: 0.5 ± 1.5 vs. MS/SGA: 3.9 ± 5.0, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = -0.96), raising the possibility that part of the observed association was built into the measurement strategy itself. Nevertheless, sensitivity analysis showed that the modified BI score excluding the response-to-treatment domain (BI-BI Ⅳ) also differed significantly between the two groups, suggesting that the overall association was not explained solely by the treatment-response component.

The family history domain did not differ significantly between groups, although the mean score was numerically higher in the MS/SGA group. One possible explanation is that a family history of BD is known to be one of the strongest predictors of diagnostic conversion from MDD to BD. (Rhee et al. 2023) Accordingly, depressed patients with a stronger familial loading for BD may have been more likely to develop clearer bipolar features over time and, as a result, may have been relatively underrepresented in a long-term stable MDD outpatient sample such as ours. This interpretation remains speculative.

ROC analysis suggested that a BI total score ≥ 16 differentiated the MS/SGA group from the AD group with acceptable discrimination (AUC = 0.765), indicating a Bipolarity Index of 16 or above marked patients who were more likely, in this Japanese stable outpatient sample, to be receiving mood stabilizers and or second-generation antipsychotics.

These findings should be interpreted cautiously in light of prior studies and current treatment guidelines. Although the present study does not allow conclusions about treatment effectiveness, the findings of Moderie et al. may be compatible with the possibility that antidepressants combined with AP and/or MS could be clinically relevant in a subset of MDD patients with bipolarity-related features, particularly TRD. (Moderie et al. 2022) In contrast, Nuñez et al. compared TRD with bipolar depression and reported that class C personality disorders and anxiety disorders were more frequent in the TRD group, suggesting that TRD should not simply be regarded as a prodromal or attenuated form of BD. (Nuñez et al. 2018) From this perspective, TRD alone may be insufficient to infer BD, and other bipolarity-related features, including mixed features, may need to be assessed more broadly in clinical practice. In this context, the present findings may support the value of evaluating bipolarity comprehensively, including with instruments such as the BI, when interpreting prescribing patterns in patients with MDD. Our findings may also be interpreted in light of the recent review by Hernandorena et al. (Hernandorena et al. 2025) They showed that clinical prescribing patterns differ substantially across BD-I, BD-II, and MDD, with lithium and SGA prescribed far more frequently in bipolar disorders than in MDD. Specifically, lithium was prescribed in 54.4% of BD-I, 38.0% of BD-II, and 6.78% of MDD cases, whereas SGA were prescribed in 41.6%, 22.3%, and 15.9%, respectively. Although that study compared diagnostic categories, whereas the present study examined variation in bipolarity within an MDD-only cohort, both studies are consistent with the possibility that clinicians tend to select MS and/or SGA more often as bipolar features become more prominent. These considerations are also broadly consistent with current treatment guidelines, in which clinicians may avoid AD monotherapy and may favor MS or SGA when bipolar features are suspected. (Stahl et al. 2017; Natale et al. 2022).

Prior reports have shown that cyclothymic and hyperthymic temperaments on the TEMPS-A can distinguish between MDD and bipolar disorder. (Morishita et al. 2020; Mendlowicz et al. 2005; Mazzarini et al. 2009) By comparison, most previous studies evaluated differences across diagnostic categories such as MDD and BD, whereas the present study examined variation in bipolarity within an MDD-only cohort. To our knowledge, we were unable to identify prior studies that directly investigated temperament differences across levels of bipolarity within MDD. This difference in study design may partly explain why no significant between-group differences were observed in TEMPS-A subscale scores in our sample.

The preferential selection of aripiprazole, quetiapine, and lithium in the MS/SGA group may be broadly compatible with their reported antidepressant-related pharmacological properties. However, any mechanistic interpretation should be made cautiously, as the present study was cross-sectional and not designed to test pharmacological mechanisms. (Tuplin and Holahan 2017; Jensen et al. 2008; Scheuch et al. 2010).

Strengths include a prespecified definition of long-term stability, two-center recruitment, and use of standardized instruments (QIDS-SR, TEMPS-A, BI). The finding that most BI subdomains—except family history—differed between groups, albeit with modest mean differences, supports the notion that cumulative rather than single features may guide real-world prescribing. This favors a comprehensive, measurement-based evaluation of bipolarity in routine care (Fortney et al. 2017).

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design in a long-term stable outpatient sample precludes causal claims about medication effectiveness and may not generalize to acute or recurrent phases. Second, confounding by indication is likely: clinicians may preferentially prescribe MS/SGA to patients with higher bipolarity, producing the observed association. Third, group assignment rules (classifying medication-free patients into the AD group) and modest sample size may influence estimates. Fourth, family history effects might be underestimated if such cases were underrepresented among stable outpatients. (Post et al. 2015) Fifth, treatment resistance may also have influenced treatment selection. In MDD, augmentation with SGA and lithium is a recognized pharmacological strategy for treatment-resistant depression, and treatment resistance itself has been staged using methods such as the Maudsley Staging Method because of its relevance to longer-term outcome. (Dold and Kasper 2017; Fekadu et al. 2009) In BD, treatment resistance is likewise a central clinical problem, and combination pharmacotherapy involving MS and/or SGA is commonly used in treatment-resistant cases. (Gitlin 2006) However, because neither the presence of treatment resistance, the number of antidepressants used before stabilization, nor the number of hospitalizations was systematically assessed in the present study, these factors could not be included in the analyses. Sixth, all participants were recruited in Japan; generalizability may vary with health-system context and drug availability. Finally, BI is a clinician-rated composite reflecting validators rather than a diagnosis. Because BI ratings were completed by the treating psychiatrists rather than by independent raters, the influence of clinician judgment and rater-related bias cannot be completely excluded.

In conclusion, among long-term stable MDD outpatients, higher BI scores were associated with MS and/or SGA rather than antidepressant as primary pharmacotherapy. Before any clinical implementation, prospective studies are needed to validate whether BI-informed pharmacotherapy is associated with improved outcomes and to clarify the role of MS and/or SGA in MDD patients at elevated risk of future diagnostic conversion to BD.
