Electrophysiological characteristics of atypical “node-like” accessory pathway in the aortomitral continuity: A case series

Introduction

Atypical accessory pathways with “atrioventricular (AV) node–like” conduction properties are well described in the literature, often referred to as Mahaim accessory pathway (MAP). The electrophysiological properties associated with MAPs include absent or minimal pre-excitation on resting electrocardiograms (ECGs),1 decremental AV conduction,2,3 adenosine sensitivity,4,5 distinct “Mahaim pathway potential” at the site of the pathway, and, in most cases, exclusively anterograde accessory pathway conduction.1 MAPs may present as AV or atriofascicular pathways. For the purpose of our discussion, “Mahaim pathway” refers to pathways exhibiting these conduction properties, irrespective of the anatomic location.

Although the proximal insertions of most MAPs localize to the lateral tricuspid annulus, long and short MAPs arising from the lateral mitral annulus and left atrial appendage are rarely described in the literature.6,7 We report 2 cases of an accessory pathway with MAP-like properties located at the aortomitral continuity (AMC). We aimed to describe the electrophysiological, mapping, and ablation characteristics of these pathways; describe them in the context of preexisting literature; and hypothesize their embryologic origins.

Cases

Case 1

A 36-year-old woman with a structurally normal heart presented to our clinic with recurrent paroxysms of a wide complex tachycardia, having undergone 4 previous attempted ablation procedures. A baseline ECG showed no manifest pre-excitation. ECG during tachycardia showed an atypical right bundle branch block pattern in V1 with positive precordial concordance, inferior QRS axis, negative QRS in aVR and aVL, qS morphology in lead I, and 1:1 ventriculoatrial (VA) relationship (Figure 1A). Particularly notable was the subtle variation in QRS morphology during sustained tachycardia (most remarkably seen as variation of QRS height in the inferior leads) (Figure 1A). During electrophysiology study performed under moderate sedation, ventricular pacing and para-Hisian pacing maneuvers confirmed retrograde VA conduction occurred through the AV node only. At baseline autonomic state, manifest pre-excitation was not noted with atrial decremental pacing or extrastimulus testing. On isoproterenol, a sustained wide complex tachycardia with a cycle length of 460 ms, 1:1 VA relationship, VA interval of 112 ms, and a concentric pattern of atrial activation was induced with ventricular pacing. Notable differential diagnoses at this point included an antidromic reciprocating tachycardia (ART), a pre-excited supraventricular tachycardia, or a ventricular tachycardia. Atrial premature complex delivered during septal refractoriness advanced the next ventricular activation and reset the tachycardia (Figure 1B). However, His-synchronous ventricular premature beat failed to perturb the tachycardia. These features confirmed the diagnosis of ART mediated by an accessory pathway. Conduction through this accessory pathway occurred only during isoproterenol infusion and demonstrated decremental conduction. 2 distinct QRS morphologies were noted during tachycardia associated with distinct AV intervals, suggestive of 2 distinct ventricular insertions. Decrement in AV conduction was noted during atrial pacing (Figure 1C).

Mapping of the earliest ventricular activation during tachycardia demonstrated earliest activation in the left atrial appendage with a “far-field” local ventricular electrogram 20 ms earlier than the surface QRS (Figure 1D). Ventricular activation along the mitral annulus occurred later, consistent with ventricular insertion of the accessory pathway away from the annulus. Further mapping of the mitral annulus revealed a discrete pathway potential at the AMC during sinus rhythm (Figure 2A). Radiofrequency ablation at this site using a 3.5-mm irrigated-tip catheter inserted through the transseptal approach using 25–30 W targeting 5–10 Ω reduction in impedance resulted in pathway automaticity with acceleration and eventual abatement (Figure 2B). The fluoroscopic views showing catheter position at the site of successful ablation are presented in Figure 3. There was no evidence of antegrade pathway conduction or inducible tachycardia despite aggressive stimulation on postablation testing. Case 2

The second patient is a 23-year-old man with a history of pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect, surgical repair with right ventricle to pulmonary artery conduit at 8 days of age, and subsequent multiple conduit revision surgeries including implantation of a bioprosthetic pulmonary valve. In addition, he had significant systemic venous anomalies including bilateral superior venae cavae with the left superior vena cava draining to the coronary sinus and acquired occlusion of both femoral veins and the right internal jugular vein. He presented to the emergency room with palpitations and a regular wide complex tachycardia, terminated with administration of intravenous beta-blocker.

His baseline ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm with right bundle branch block, but no evidence of manifest pre-excitation. Electrophysiology study was performed under moderate sedation with propofol. Catheters included a duodecapolar catheter in the coronary sinus via the left internal jugular vein and persistent left superior vena cava, a deflectable catheter to the right ventricle via the right subclavian vein, and a mapping catheter positioned via retrograde aortic access to record a left-sided His. At baseline autonomic tone, intermittent pre-excitation was seen with anterograde conduction across an accessory pathway with an effective refractory period of 740 ms. During infusion of isoproterenol, anterograde accessory pathway conduction improved with an effective refractory period of 350 ms. Atrial extrastimulus testing showed decremental conduction across the accessory pathway (Figure 4A and 4B). A regular tachycardia with a cycle length of 335 ms, 1:1 VA relationship, and QRS morphology identical to the fully pre-excited beats was induced with isoproterenol (Figure 5A). QRS morphology was similar to that seen in case #1, with an atypical right bundle branch morphology in V1, positive precordial concordance, inferior axis, QS complex in aVR and aVL, and 1:1 VA relationship. The QRS morphology in lead I with an rS pattern was subtly different from that seen in case #1. An intermittently alternating cycle length with variation in QRS morphology and AV interval was observed spontaneously (Figure 5B). The mitral annulus was mapped during atrial pacing with pre-excitation through the retrograde aortic approach. As in the previous case, no favorable candidate sites with early ventricular activation were found along the mitral annulus. On mapping the AMC, a discrete pathway potential (Figure 5C) was recorded. Of note, the degree of AV separation and the relative lateness of the local ventricular electrogram at this site, as in the previous case, suggest that the distal ventricular insertion in both cases was distant from the ablation site. Radiofrequency ablation at the AMC using a 3.5-mm irrigated-tip catheter at 30 W targeting impedance drop of 5–10 Ω led to an immediate loss of pre-excitation. In both cases, catheter position, contact, and stability were monitored using intracardiac echocardiography. 4 years after this procedure, the patient continues to do well with no arrhythmia recurrence.

Discussion

We report 2 patients with a pathway located at the AMC with unusual electrophysiological features including exclusive anterograde conduction, absence of pre-excitation at baseline autonomic tone, isoproterenol sensitivity, decremental conduction, and spontaneous variation in QRS morphology suggestive of multiple ventricular insertions. Discrete pathway potential was recorded at the AMC with the local ventricular electrogram occurring relatively late, consistent with a short AV MAP. Electroanatomic mapping focused on identifying the pathway potential rather than the earliest ventricular activation, which represents the distal ventricular insertion. Finally, pathway automaticity during ablation, akin to “Mahaim” or junctional automaticity, heralded successful pathway elimination.

We read with interest literature establishing the electrophysiological characteristics of accessory pathways ablated at the AMC. There remains a paucity of reported cases, with significant variations in clinical and electrophysiological characteristics reported including robust retrograde conduction, sustained orthodromic reentry, and a distinct pattern of pre-excitation similar to that encountered in our patients.8, 9, 10 Li et al8 described a series of 8 patients, of whom 7 demonstrated retrograde conduction without decremental properties and 1 demonstrated bidirectional conduction. Jastrzebski9 described a patient with manifest pre-excitation at baseline and bidirectional pathway conduction. More recently, Saraf et al10 described a bidirectionally decremental pathway at the AMC mediating orthodromic reciprocating tachycardia. However, in contrast to some of these reports, our literature search yielded only 1 other description of a “Mahaim” like accessory pathway arising from this area with latent pre-excitation and exclusively anterograde decremental conduction.11 The patient described by Johner et al11 presented with a monomorphic wide complex tachycardia, arising from the superior mitral annulus. In addition, isoproterenol sensitivity observed in both our patients was not previously described.

In contrast to MAPs found along the tricuspid annulus, Mahaim-like accessory pathways with insertion along the mitral annulus are relatively unusual.9,12, 13, 14 Furthermore, although right-sided pathways with distal insertions into the right bundle are more commonly encountered than AV connections, most left-sided MAPs seem to be represented by short AV connections, similar to cases we report here.6,15 Finally, when present, these pathways may be represented by indistinct and difficult-to-elicit latent pre-excitation, leading to potential for their underdetection. The propensity of the right AV annulus to manifest these conduction system–like pathways can be understood from its embryologic origins. Differentiation of primitive myocardial cells in the AV canal region, possibly driven by effects of neural crest cell migration to the regions of the AV septum and great arteries, seems to manifest with the presence of AV node–like tissue dominantly along the right AV annulus.15,16 Interestingly, subsequent studies have reported nests of AV node–like tissue along both annuli in adult mammal heart specimens, although these seem to be embryologic remnants with no clear functionality in the grown animal.17,18

It is harder to intuitively appreciate the genesis of conduction or arrhythmia from an apparently fibrous structure typified by the AMC. It has been hypothesized that a primitive interventricular ring gives rise to the left and right bundles and a third septal bundle in the developing embryo. Although the left and right bundles persist to form their adult counterparts, the third nonbranching bundle seems to encircle the developing left ventricular outflow tract and eventually regresses with maturity.18,19 This has been validated in histopathologic studies where cellular nests, histologically and electrophysiologically resembling AV junctional tissue, have been described in the region of the AMC.17,20 These cells have previously been implicated in the genesis of automatic and triggered ventricular arrhythmias from this area.18,20 It is plausible that this tissue can manifest itself as an atypical AV bypass tract with nodal conduction properties as in our case. These cases highlight the importance of embryologic and anatomic correlation to contextualize unusual arrhythmia presentations in the electrophysiology laboratory.

Conclusion

Mahaim-like short AV accessory pathways may occur in the region of the AMC and be representative of embryologic remnants of the conduction axis. In our patients, these pathways presented with pre-excited tachyarrhythmias and a characteristic pattern of latent pre-excitation and anterograde-only decremental conduction and isoproterenol sensitivity. These cases highlight the importance of a high degree of suspicion to guide the diagnosis and mapping of these unusual pathways.
