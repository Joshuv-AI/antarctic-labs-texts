Data linkage studies on veteran suicidality: a scoping review of the field, knowledge gaps and evidence appraisal
Authors: Nicolas Rivero Weidemüller, Shannon Dias, Emilia Janca, Luke T. Bayliss, Ed Heffernan, Carla Meurk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsSuicidality among veterans is a serious issue that impacts veterans, their families, friends, and communities. Using data linkage to examine suicidality among veterans remains an emerging area. This review examines the application of data linkage to questions on suicidality among veterans from within the Five Eyes alliance to generate learnings that can be applied to the Australian context.MethodsThis scoping review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Peer reviewed databases and grey literature were searched. Studies were assessed against the Reporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-Collected Data (RECORD) Statement.Results58 studies were included in the review. 60% of these were identified as being of good or excellent quality. Most (72.4%) were conducted in the US, followed by Canada (13.8%) and Australia (8.6%). The majority of studies examined suicide deaths (81%), while 31% assessed ideation, self-harm, or attempts.DiscussionStudies were overwhelmingly health focussed, with examination of psychosocial determinants and social care pathways lacking. The literature was inconsistent in describing methods and there was no documented evidence of lived experience involvement.ConclusionThe review highlights benefits to undertaking data linkage research within the Australian context and has noted several gaps to address that could enhance the evidence-base globally. Enhancements to the RECORD statement are recommended. Enhancements in ethics, governance and information sharing protocols between institutions should be investigated to support more comprehensive linkages that facilitate the examination of longitudinal risk factors, health and psychosocial trajectories, service transitions, and multi-systemic influences on veteran suicidality.Clinical trial numberNot applicableSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08164-0.

Introduction

Suicidality among veterans

Suicidality among veterans, including suicidal ideation, intentions, plans, attempts, suicide death, and non-suicidal self-harm, has profound consequences for the individual their families, friends, and communities [1], [2–5]. Previous research has reported higher rates of suicidality among veterans than the general population [6, 7]. Veterans may also experience increased psychosocial risk factors for suicidality than the general population, including mental health and substance use problems, anger and aggression issues, and social, financial, and legal pressures [7–10]. There remain numerous gaps in understanding veteran suicidality, including the role of institutional processes (e.g., insurance claims processes for healthcare), multimorbidities in suicidality, and longitudinal journeys before, during and after service [11]. There are also entrenched biases in existing literature associated with cohorts examined, with literature on North American veterans predominating and studies tending to focus on ex-serving members [11]. As such, currently serving members are often excluded from veteran-focused analyses, despite the importance of understanding suicidality across the full service trajectory, including during active service.

What is data linkage?

Data linkage refers to the joining of two or more datasets to build a dataset that is more informative than if each dataset was considered individually. Data linkage is commonly undertaken to connect information about people [12]. However it can be used in other ways, for example to connect information about geographic regions (e.g. to examine the relationship between extreme weather events and health conditions) or even organisations. In the context of research with people, or individual-level data linkage, this method can be used to connect multiple types of data, including data that has been collected by researchers (e.g. surveys) and administrative data (e.g., emergency department data collections and hospital admissions data collections).

Benefits of data linkage

Data linkage is an adaptable method that can generate evidence to inform our understanding of health, healthcare and health and social policy [13]. Understanding the data linkage process and its utility is helped by considering how an individual’s interactions with health, social, and administrative services offers insight about their health status and social circumstances. Most individuals have multiple contacts with a myriad of services throughout their lives, from birth to death (Fig. 1) [14]. For example, a hypothetical Australian veteran may be engaged with the hospital system at birth (which is then recorded in the Register of Births, Deaths and Marriages [RBDM]), and have General Practitioner visits throughout childhood. They may then join the Australian Defence Force in early adulthood through which they receive health care, engage with the Department of Veterans’ Affairs at transition, experience episodes of suicidality recorded through contact with community mental health services and the emergency department, and then die in hospital many years later, which would then be recorded in the RBDM. Exploring these trajectories across veterans more broadly can help identify patterns, predictors, rates of suicidality-related service contacts, and other important touchpoints. This can help build complex pictures of the systems in which we live, including examination of social determinants of health, co-occurring risk and protective factors, the use of particular social and health pathways, and the effectiveness of existing services. Sometimes, data linkage can help shed light on the seemingly simple, but in practice, it may be complicated to answer questions. In the Australian context, for example, measuring suicide deaths among veterans requires linkage between death registries (which document information about deaths but not occupational status of those who die) and Defence personnel records (which identify the occupational status of a person but not information about their death) [7, 8]. In the case of suicide related calls to police, data linkage is needed to establish the demographic characteristics of individuals, because this information is not collected at the point of call as structured data within police datasets [15].Fig. 1Example of contact with health, social and administrative services throughout a lifetime of a hypothetical individual*. *in reality individuals are likely to have multiple contacts with many of these services over their lifetime

Other unique benefits of data linkage, particularly linkage of routinely collected administrative data, includes the ability to comprehensively profile whole populations, the efficiency of using routinely collected information for secondary purposes (reducing reliance on resource-intensive primary data collection), and the minimisation of loss to follow-up and other types of bias compared to sample-based designs [16].

Taken together, linked data can be leveraged to generate evidence to better understand and improve responses to suicidality among veterans.

Data linkage processes and techniques

Common to all data linkage is the need to use ‘identifiers’ to ensure that records across datasets are matched appropriately. For individual-level linked data, this means making sure that data are rightly attributed to the right individual across datasets [12]. Identifiers used in individual-level data linkage can include identification numbers, participant personal information (e.g., name, age, sex, date of birth), and/or Statistical Linkage Keys (i.e., a unique string of letters and numbers generated from participant personal information) [17]. Beyond small datasets, where matching can occur manually, a range of statistical methods facilitate this process. These are categorised as: 1) deterministic data linkage, which involves the matching of records across datasets to one unique individual based on exact, predefined criteria (e.g., matching records based on a unique identification number used across datasets), and 2) probabilistic data linkage, which employs statistical models to determine the likelihood that records from different datasets refer to the same individual, even with discrepancies (e.g., matching records based on the participant’s personal information, and accounting for slight differences in spelling of names, variations in address formats, or missing data in one of the records) [12]. Deterministic linkage offers high transparency and specificity because matches rely on exact identifiers, but it may miss true matches when data are incomplete or inconsistent [18]. Probabilistic linkage improves sensitivity by accommodating discrepancies and missing information, although it is more methodologically complex and carries a small risk of false matches [18]. Taken together, these methods each have distinct strengths and limitations that warrant careful consideration when designing and interpreting data linkage studies.

Data linkage ethics and governance

In countries such as Australia, data linkage is used routinely to consolidate data for cohorts of millions of people for a range of purposes, including to inform planning and service delivery, to estimate the burden of health conditions, to describe patterns of health service use, and to identify key trends to guide priorities [19, 20]. In Australia, data linkage is routinely used in research and national reporting on suicide and suicide prevention, including by government agencies [21], and is similarly applied in veteran populations to examine suicidality-related risk, outcomes, and service use across systems [7]. Given the scale and sensitivity of these data, robust ethical oversight and governance frameworks are essential to ensure appropriate use, privacy, and public trust.

Data linkages, especially those involving data relating to large numbers of people and retrospective (including historical) data, are subject to unique ethical issues and oversight that vary by jurisdiction. Within Australia, where personal information is being used for linkage for the purposes of research, there are strict and well-established ethical standards and governance processes that must be followed, as outlined by the National Health and Medical Research Council’s (NHMRC) ethical guidelines and supporting documentation [22, 23]. Further, data linkage of administrative datasets collected by Federal, State and Territory Governments in Australia is conducted by accredited state and national level data linkage units [24]. Further administration may be necessary, depending on the governance and legislative requirements of the institutional owners of datasets, often referred to as “data custodians”.

Depending on the characteristics of the datasets being linked, the linkages that occur may be “consented” (i.e, where consent is explicitly sought from individuals whose data are to be linked) or “unconsented” (i.e., where consent is not explicitly sought). Where linkage is to occur without gathering participant informed consent, researchers are required to obtain a “waiver of consent” from a human ethics committee credentialed by the NMHRC. The NHMRC guidelines outline nine criteria that must be met for a waiver to be granted ([22], pp. 21–22). Among these are requirements to uphold individual privacy and confidentiality. Through this process, NHMRC auspiced Human Research Ethics Committees are empowered to approve exemptions that exist within Australia’s privacy legislation for public health research [23].

Preserving privacy and confidentiality requires a multi-faceted approach, spanning data transfer, storage, access and use. Frameworks such as the “five safes” – safe people (users of data are appropriately trained and legally bound), safe projects (data are used appropriately), safe settings (data are appropriately stored and used), safe data (appropriate protections are used to minimise identification and disclosure risks) and safe outputs (published outputs minimise disclosure risks) - provide a structured approach to ensuring confidentiality is protected [25]. Taken together, such safeguards are essential for maintaining a social licence to operate in suicide-related research, especially for veteran populations, where the use of sensitive, cross-sector data requires a high level of public trust and careful stewardship.

Principle of separation

One technique that contributes to ensuring ‘safe data’ handling in the context of linkage studies bears special mention: the principle of separation. The principle of separation provides a ‘gold-standard’ approach to preserving privacy and confidentiality in data linkage studies where individual consent is not obtained [26]. This is a process whereby the transfer of identifying participant information, such as names and dates of birth, and content data (e.g., information about health service use) are never accessed together by anyone working with the data [26]. In addition, the researchers who receive the linked dataset will never receive it with participant personal information [26].

Even in a situation where the principle of separation is adhered to, and researchers are not provided with directly identifying information, there are still situations where it may be possible to identify individuals who display rare combinations of characteristics. Adherence to methods of statistical disclosure control, provide mechanisms to minimise these risks [25]. Fitting within the five safes framework of ‘safe outputs’, this step involves applying rules, such as the ‘frequency rule’ or ‘cell dominance rule’ to ensure that published information does not inadvertently disclose identities of individuals whose data are used [25].

Lived experience involvement

Involvement of individuals with lived experience in research is best practice, both in terms of ensuring matters of importance and relevance to those who the findings are purported to benefit are examined, but also that findings are communicated with due regard to the people behind the data [27–30]. Nevertheless, frameworks for lived experience participation in data heavy studies, such as data linkages, has lagged behind involvement in other areas. Lived experience involvement can take many forms, including lived experience expert groups and lived experience co-investigators and co-authors. Ongoing and meaningful lived experience involvement in data linkage studies can ensure the study’s social licence to operate, even in the absence of obtaining individual consent. Lived experience engagement is particularly important in research involving populations who may hold diverse perspectives on and experiences with institutional systems, including veteran communities, where it may strengthen the relevance, credibility, and acceptability of suicide prevention research.

Limitations of data linkage

Like all research methods, data linkage has limitations. From an epidemiological perspective, there can be issues in estimating the overall prevalence of a phenomenon or condition in a group of interest, given that detection can only occur in the context of service use. Addressing missing data is complex, especially when distinguishing between recording gaps and true disease or illness absence [31]. In other words, where a condition is not recorded, a determination must be made as to the likelihood of a person not experiencing the condition, versus the condition being present but not identified or documented. Linkage of multiple datasets can be useful insofar as it can increase confidence that lack of a record of a condition means the absence of illness, because there are more opportunities for detection across multiple datasets. Assessment of the sociodemographic profile of an individual can be more complete in some cases, because missing data issues associated with a single dataset lessen when multiple sources are combined; limitations may nevertheless occur where the format of the questions asked are not comprehensive (e.g., regarding sex and gender).

The current review

Data linkage has become an important approach for advancing understanding of suicidality among veterans, enabling examination of risk pathways, service use, and outcomes across the multiple systems that shape veterans’ lives [32]. The methodological features of these studies, including how suicidality is defined, how datasets are combined, and how ethical and governance considerations are addressed, influence how findings are interpreted and applied to policy and prevention efforts. However, these methodological and reporting characteristics have not been systematically synthesised in the context of veteran suicidality.

The need to better use available data to improve outcomes for veterans was identified by the recent Australian Government Royal Commission into Defence and Veteran Suicide [33]. In this context, an assessment of the benefits and limitations of data linkage studies is timely. This scoping review aimed to identify existing data linkage studies from countries comprising the Five Eyes Alliance (i.e., The United States of America (USA), the United Kingdom (UK), Canada, and New Zealand (NZ), in addition to Australia) examining suicidality among veterans, to determine knowledge gaps and critically appraise the quality of evidence in this area. Countries within the Five Eyes Alliance share broadly comparable veteran service systems and administrative data infrastructures, and comparison across jurisdictions provides insight into differences in data linkage governance, processes, and reporting. Through this process, learnings may be derived that can be applied to the Australian context to maximise the value of data linkage to benefit veterans.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis: extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [34]. The review protocol was registered with the Open Science Framework (OSF; DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/SQCMB).

In this review, veterans include current-serving members of a defence force, defined as “any person currently serving […] and who has served at least one day” (both active/permanent and reserve), and ex-serving members, which include anyone who has “served at least one day and has since discharged” [33].

Eligibility criteria and information sources

Peer reviewed research as well as grey literature in English (following the focus on the Five eyes group) were included and reference lists of included articles were searched.

Inclusion criteria for articles were (1) articles that report findings on veterans who have experienced suicidality; (2) articles that report findings of record or data linkage of individuals across at least two datasets (at least one of which was administrative in nature i.e., data is routinely collected by healthcare providers for administrative and billing purposes and not primarily for research); (3) articles that examine suicidality defined to include suicidal thoughts, intentions, behaviours, suicide attempts, intentional self-harm (ISH), and suicide deaths as a primary outcome. Diagnostic entities such as non-suicidal self-injury or self-harm (NSSI) were also included; as was “suicide by cop”, a forensic phenomenon when a suicidal individual provokes a law enforcement officer in to killing him/her.

Accordingly, exclusion criteria were that the articles (1) focused on assisted suicide, terrorist suicide, child soldiers, or resistance fighters, (2) were non-English language studies; (3) studies of veterans based on countries outside of the Five Eyes alliance, (4) animal model studies; and (5) editorials, opinion pieces, or conference abstracts without full text.

Search

Search terms were structured according to the Population-Concept-Context framework [35]: veterans with suicidality (Population), examined through a data linkage study (Concept), in the Five Eyes group (Australia, Canada, NZ, UK and USA) (Context).

Five key electronic databases (PubMed, EMBASE, PsycINFO, Scopus, and the USA Veterans Affairs PTSD database PTSDPubs) based on the logic < veterans’ terms > AND <suicidality terms > AND <data linkage terms > AND <countries from the Five Eyes group>, with terms reviewed by a research librarian, for literature published from inception (1 January 1970) to 29 January 2024. Relevant grey literature was reviewed through desktop search of sources published on Defence, Veterans Affairs, and Government Health websites for the Five Eyes Group, inclusion of references known to authors, and followed up with snowballing. In some cases, where entities undertake annual reporting but the substance of the report was otherwise the same, we only included the most recent report.

Selection of sources of evidence

Title and abstract screening and full text review were independently undertaken by LB and SD. NR undertook the desktop search of grey literature. Similarly, data extraction was conducted by NR, LB, and SD (all Senior Research Assistants). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion between authors with regards to the eligibility criteria.

Data extraction

Data items extracted for each article were: reference details; aims; country (of data); study type (cross-sectional; cohort; case-control; surveillance); cohort details; whether the article examined suicide deaths, ISH and non-fatal suicidal behaviours, or both; measurement of suicidality constructs; individual administrative datasets that comprise the linkage; linkage methods and techniques. Sources were reviewed to ascertain whether consenting procedures were reported, defined as any explicit description of consent within methods or ethics sections. Information about lived experience involvement was also examined. In this context, lived experience means being a veteran, or the loved one of a veteran, who has “experienced suicidal thoughts, survived a suicide attempt, supported a loved one through suicidal crisis, or been bereaved by suicide.” [36] Lived experience involvement was then extracted as any explicit description of the involvement of individuals with lived experience in consultation, co-design, or advisory roles in any part of the manuscript. For both consent processes and lived experience, where information was not reported, this was recorded as not described.

Critical appraisal

The overall quality of the included studies was appraised based on their comprehensiveness in reporting against the Reporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-Collected Data (RECORD) Statement [37]. RECORD is an adaptation of the STROBE statement [38], and was designed to improve transparency and completeness of reporting for studies using routinely collected health data. RECORD includes items specific to data linkage studies, such as detailed reporting of data sources, methods of linkage, linkage quality evaluation, and data cleaning procedures (33). The quality appraisal tool consists of 30 criteria in the form of binary categorical items, each assigned a value of 1 (yes; item fulfilled) or 0 (no; item not fulfilled). The quality of all included articles was graded into 4 categories (Excellent, 85%+; Good, 70–84%; Fair 50–69%; Poor, <50%). Critical appraisal was undertaken independently by SD and NR for all studies, and any uncertainties were resolved through discussion with co-authors.

Data analysis and synthesis

The narrative synthesis was guided by the patterns, advances, gaps, evidence for practice, and research recommendations (PAGER) framework [39]. NR and SD conducted a thematic analysis to address the aims of the included studies, consistent with the PAGER approach of identifying key themes/groupings, examining gaps, and considering patterns that exist within and across groupings and themes.

The remaining studies were graded fair (n = 21, 36.2%) to poor (n = 2, 3.4%) quality. Notably scarce was reporting of participant characteristics and linkage methods. Items relating to setting (item 5), data sources/measurement (item 8), outcome data (item 15) and main/key results (items 16 and18) were reported in all included studies. Details on consenting arrangements were mentioned in n = 17 sources (25.9%). Of those who reported on consenting arrangements, twelve studies (70.6%) obtained a waiver of consent, three studies (17.6%) obtained direct informed consent, and two studies (11.8%) stated that obtaining participant consent was not necessary for their study. Lived experience involvement, as per our definition, was not mentioned in any of the included studies.

Veteran cohort characteristics

Sociodemographic and sample characteristics are shown in Table 3. Most studies (n = 43, 74.1%) were based on data on ex-serving members, nine studies (15.5%) focussed only on current-serving members, and 6 studies (10.3%) focussed on both. The majority of study cohorts were predominantly male (75–100%) and white Caucasian (63–98%). Cohort size ranged from 100 to 18.8 million people.

Linkage and identification methods

Linkage techniques and identifiers are summarised in Table 2. Thirty-seven (63.8%) of the studies reported on the data-linkage process. Of these, 14 (37.8%) specifically reported on using deterministic or ‘exact’ linkage (with or without manual medical/clerical record review), 10 (27.0%) reported using probabilistic linkage (with or without manual medical/clerical record review) techniques exclusively, and 13 (35.1%) reported using a combination of probabilistic, deterministic and/or manual clerical review.

Where details were provided, most studies used a combination of information to identify individuals across datasets. The most commonly used identifying information were names (n = 26, 70.3%), unique personal identifiers, often social security numbers (n = 24, 64.9%), or demographic information (e.g., age or sex) (n = 17, 45.9%). Less commonly used information included geo identifiers, such as addresses (n = 6, 16.2%) or mortality related identifiers. Five studies (13.5%) used a unique linkage key based on encoded identifiers in place of personal identifiers.

Aims, study outcomes and outcome measures

This report examines the characteristics of ex-serving members using a newly established data asset of public hospital service use (including admitted patient care and emergency departments). It specifically focuses on public hospital care for conditions associated with suicidal behaviour, including mental health-related care, alcohol and other drug use, and intentional self-harm, to identify the particular characteristics and care needs of ADF members.

More specifically, AIHW analyses the proportion of ex-serving members who received public hospital care in 2019–20 and who received that care primarily for conditions associated with suicidal behaviour. Comparisons to other hospitalised Australians are used to provide context and better understand the specific care needs of ADF service members.

Records of hospitalisation for treatment following intentional self-harm were included if they met the following criteria:

a principal diagnosis in the ICD-10-AM range S00–T75, T79 (Injury, poisoning and certain other consequences of external causes)

the first reported external cause code in the record in the ICD-10-AM range X60–X84, Y87.0 (external causes of morbidity).

The term ‘intentional self-harm’ in the National Hospital Morbidity Database (NHMD) provides information on patients admitted to hospital for self-poisoning or self-injury, with or without suicidal intent – and therefore includes both suicide attempts and non-suicidal self-harming behaviours.

Causes of mortality included in the NDI are recorded as ICD-10 codes, as derived by Australian Bureau of Statistics from death certificates. Causes of mortality have been grouped according to ICD-10 chapter. Subgroups have been created (where there is sufficient data) for ‘intentional self-harm,’ ‘deaths of despair’ and ‘deaths of undetermined intent.’ Deaths from intentional self-harm are a subgroup of deaths of despair. Aims and purpose of data linkage studies: topics examined

The studies reviewed were overwhelmingly health focussed, with 12 studies (20.7%) focussed specifically on assessing suicide rates, including time trends and comparison with civilian populations. Other frequent foci were: health service use (including pharmaceutical use) (n = 12, 20.7%), risk and protective factors (n = 11, 19%), comorbidity of suicidality with other health conditions (n = 9, 15.5%), effects of deployment and combat exposure (n = 7, 12.1%), methodological research, for example, examinations of completeness of death registry information (n = 6, 10.4%), mental health conditions related to suicidality (n = 5, 8.6), and geospatial and rurality studies (n = 2, 3.5%). Only one group of authors focussed on social determinants of health (n = 1, 1.7%).

Discussion

The studies included in this review reveal a notable preponderance of US based research focused on ex-serving members. Limited studies tracked veterans through service and transition into civilian life. There was a lack of focus outside health sectors. This is a clear gap given the importance of psychosocial factors, and complex journeys across health and social care systems. For example, there is some evidence of overrepresentation of veterans in the criminal justice system [97], and incarceration is associated with increased suicide risk [98] Further, only one study examined social determinants of health among veterans, suggesting that the unique potential of data linkage in this area has not yet been fully realised. These gaps indicate unexplored opportunities for using data linkage to examine both social determinants and longitudinal journeys spanning health, service transitions, and multi-systemic factors. Enhancements in ethics, governance and information sharing protocols are needed to facilitate this.

A focus of the research reviewed has been on suicide deaths. This is understandable given that this requires linking of high-quality data sources (death index and coronial records) with other data sources to determine veteran identity and other characteristics of interest. Examination of non-fatal suicidality, in contrast, was more heterogeneous in source and identification. This highlights the complexity of accurately measuring the prevalence of non-fatal suicidal phenomena. Future research should consider differences between non-fatal self-harm that is intentional compared to non-fatal self-harm that does have suicidal intent.

No included studies explicitly reported lived experience involvement according to our predefined criteria. However, lived experience input may not always be clearly defined and reported on across studies. As such, our finding might reflect absence of explicit reporting, rather than confirmed absence of lived experience engagement. Regardless, clearer and more consistent reporting of lived experience involvement is needed to enable accurate appraisal of how lived experience is integrated into research design and governance.

Quality of reporting and data linkage transparency

With the increase in studies using routinely collected data, the RECORD statement was developed to inform good reporting [37]. However, our review found that there was patchy adherence to reporting. Moreover, the RECORD checklist, while valuable, does not fully capture the nuances of data linkage methods. In particular, key information regarding consenting arrangements and other data handling procedures that support good practice in preserving privacy and confidentiality of data, such as the principle of separation and statistical disclosure control practices are not indicated and not reported. Increased community awareness and community standards around right to privacy and confidentiality challenge researchers and report writers to enhance transparency in how data linkage is conducted and interpreted. There is an opportunity for modification of the RECORD checklist to include reporting on these items, as well as lived experience involvement, as part of ensuring that researchers adhere to good data governance practices. Presently, adherence to protocols is largely promissory, insofar as researchers account for necessary factors through approvals processes, including ethics approvals, without strong mechanisms to ensure that these processes are adhered to in a study’s implementation or reporting [99].

Conclusion

This review highlights the value of data linkage. Expanding the focus of data linkage studies beyond the health sector and improvements in reporting of methods and lived experience involvement, have global applicability. The concentration of studies to the US context, and limitations in generalisability of findings pertaining to services and suicidality across countries, highlight the importance of locally specific studies. Linkage studies that facilitate understandings of multi-systemic and longitudinal journeys through service and transition are needed and Australia has the potential to be a global leader. Realising this potential requires enhancements in governance and information sharing protocols between institutions.
