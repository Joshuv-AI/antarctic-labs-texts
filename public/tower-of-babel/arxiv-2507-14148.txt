Abstract
The integration of Optical Intelligent Reflective Surfaces (OIRSs) into Visible Light Communication (VLC) systems is gaining momentum as a valid alternative to RF technologies, harnessing the existing lighting infrastructures and the vast unlicensed optical spectrum to enable higher spectral efficiency, improved resilience to Line-of-Sight (LoS) blockages, and enhanced positioning capabilities. This paper investigates the problem of localizing a low-cost Photo Detector (PD) in a VLC-based indoor environment consisting of only a single Light Emitting Diode (LED) as an active anchor, and multiple spatially distributed single-element OIRSs. We formulate the problem within an indirect, computationally efficient localization framework: first, the optimal Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimators of the LoS and Non-Line-of-Sight (NLoS) distances are derived, using a suitable OIRS activation strategy to prevent interferences. To overcome the grid-based optimization required by the ML NLoS estimator, we devise a novel algorithm based on an unstructured noise variance transformation, which admits a closed-form solution. The set of estimated LoS/NLoS distances are then used within a low-complexity localization algorithm combining an Iterative Weighted Least Squares (IWLS) procedure, whose weights are set according to the inverse of the Cramér-Rao Lower Bound (CRLB), with an adaptive beam steering strategy that allows the OIRSs network to dynamically align with the PD, without any prior knowledge of its position. Accordingly, we derive the CRLB for both LoS/NLoS distance estimation and PD position estimation. Simulation results demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach in terms of localization accuracy, robustness against OIRSs misalignment conditions, and low number of iterations required to attain the theoretical bounds.

I
Introduction

The
growing scarcity of the
Radio-Frequency (RF)
spectrum has driven the development of
Visible Light Communication (VLC)
as a promising alternative for wireless data transmission
[
1
]
. Recently standardized under IEEE 802.11bb
[
2
]
, the
VLC
technology offers the potential to provide high-speed broadband internet services by harnessing
Light Emitting Diodes (LEDs)
. Leveraging the existing widespread lighting infrastructures — commonly found in residential, industrial, and urban environments —
VLC
reduces deployment costs while simultaneously enhancing energy efficiency, the latter being a critical requirement for next-generation communication systems
[
3
]
. This can be achieved through the inherent advantages of
LEDs
, including their extended operational lifespan and lower power consumption.
Moreover, the unlicensed VLC spectrum, spanning approximately 400–800 THz, is thousands of times wider than the RF spectrum and enables data rates up to 10 Gbps, offering a promising opportunity for high-capacity communication
[
4
]
.

Indoor Positioning Systems (IPSs)
utilizing
VLC
have attracted growing interest from academia and industry due to their immunity to multipath interference and inter-room leakage, stemming from the high propagation loss and inability of visible light to penetrate walls
[
5
]
. These features offer enhanced spatial confinement compared to RF-based systems (e.g., WiFi, Bluetooth), motivating the development of dedicated VLC positioning algorithms
[
6
]
.

Several signal processing techniques have been explored for
VLC
-based positioning. Among them,
Received Signal Strength (RSS)
-based methods are widely adopted due to their simplicity and effectiveness, taking advantage from the strong correlation between received optical power and distance under low noise conditions
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
. Time-based approaches, such as
Time-Of-Arrival (TOA)
and
Time-Difference-Of-Arrival (TDOA)
, use propagation delays from multiple anchors but face practical challenges such as strict synchronization requirements, need for high-speed
Photo Detectors (PDs)
, and vulnerability to multipath and
Non-Line-of-Sight (NLoS)

[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. Angular methods, such as
Angle-of-Arrival (AOA)
, exploit direction of incoming rays but require specialized hardware like directional
LEDs
or arrays, and are impaired in presence of significant obstructions
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
.

Recent studies have explored the integration of
VLC
with
Optical Intelligent Reflective Surfaces (OIRSs)
— also referred to as
Intelligent Reflective Surfaces (IRSs)
or
Reconfigurable Intelligent Surfaces (RISs)
— with the term
OIRS
adopted here to highlight their optical domain distinction
[
19
]
.
Integration of this technology into
VLC
systems offers numerous advantages, including enhanced channel capacity
[
20
,
21
]
, improved robustness to
Line-of-Sight (LoS)
blockages via alternative paths
[
22
,
23
]
, and optimized spectrum and energy utilization
[
5
,
24
]
.
In
VLC
applications, two
OIRS
hardware architectures are generally available:
mirror array-based
, which rely on geometric optics with tiltable mirrors, and
metasurface-based
, which use subwavelength dielectric structures to induce programmable phase shifts
[
25
,
26
]
. In this work, we focus on mirror array-based
OIRSs
, for which practical implementations already exists
[
27
]
.

I-A

Related Work

To fully exploit the potential of
OIRSs
in enhancing communication performance, it is essential to properly tune the tilts (for mirror array-based
OIRSs
) or phase shifts (for metasurface-based
OIRSs
) to effectively steer the reflected light toward the user. To enable such targeted steering,
OIRS
-assisted systems are commonly integrated with
VLC
-based
IPSs
, which typically rely on multiple
LEDs
serving as anchor nodes.
This is the case of
[
28
]
, where the authors propose an integrated
VLC
and
Visible Light Positioning (VLP)
framework. In the initial phase, the receiver position is estimated by sequentially activating individual
LEDs
and processing the resulting
LoS

RSS
measurements. In the subsequent phase, an
OIRS
leverages the estimated position to enhance the communication link. The study also investigates the use of the
NLoS
path via the
OIRS
as a means to mitigate
LoS
blockages. A fundamental theoretical analysis of the positioning performance is conducted by deriving and evaluating the
Cramér-Rao Lower Bound (CRLB)
, assuming some prior knowledge of the user position subject to a certain estimation error.
In
[
29
]
, a direct positioning algorithm based on
Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)
is proposed, employing multiple
LEDs
and
OIRSs
. Unlike prior works that rely on the additive Lambertian model, the reflected link is modeled using a mixed diffuse/specular reflection model, considering the existence of a reflection point near the center of each reflecting element, enabled by an orientation adjustment mechanism that leverages signals from multiple
LEDs
to obtain an initial estimate of the user position. Unfortunately, the resulting
Maximum Likelihood (ML)
estimator lacks a closed-form solution and requires numerical search methods to be implemented.
The work in
[
30
]
extends
[
29
]
by introducing known orientation mismatches between the desired and actual
OIRS
directions, with their impact quantified via a
Misspecified Cramér–Rao Bound (MCRB)
analysis.
In
[
31
]
, a direct positioning algorithm is developed for joint estimation of user position and orientation using multiple
LEDs
and signal separation techniques to extract channel gains from both direct and
OIRS
-reflected paths. A particle swarm optimization method is adopted to solve the positioning problem. Due to the
Uniform Linear Array (ULA)
-based
RIS
design, beam steering is confined to a single axis, and the phase shift matrix is pre-aligned to maximize channel gain, implicitly requiring some prior knowledge of the receiver location.

I-B

Contributions

The aforementioned works tackle the
OIRS
-assisted
VLC
positioning problem from diverse and insightful perspectives, but generally assume multiple
LEDs
and adopt a direct localization framework, often requiring some prior knowledge or an initial estimate of the user position. Moreover, despite their superior performance
[
32
]
, direct methods may lack closed-form solutions and rely on intensive numerical optimization, limiting their applicability in low-cost
VLC
devices.

In this work, we take a different path and investigate the problem of localizing a
PD
in an
IPS
setup that leverages only a single
LED
combined with mirror array-based
OIRSs
, spatially distributed in the environment. Our method relies on an indirect, computationally efficient localization framework based on suitable closed-form estimators that do not require any prior knowledge on the user location.
Moreover, we focus on the case of single-element
OIRSs
, which serve as the fundamental building blocks of larger mirror arrays. As shown in
[
25
]
, a multi-element
OIRS
can be indeed approximated as the superposition of its individual elements, making the single-element case both challenging and worth investigating due to its limited degrees of freedom.
Specifically, the main contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

We derive the
ML
estimators of the
LoS
distance (between the
LED
and the
PD
) and
NLoS
distances (between each
OIRS
and the
PD
), leveraging a structured activation protocol to avoid interference among
OIRSs
, followed by a successive subtraction that isolate the sole
NLoS
components. To overcome the need of a grid-based optimization required by the
ML

NLoS
distance estimator, we propose a suitable relaxed
ML
estimator based on an unstructured transformation of the noise variance, which admits a closed-form solution.

•

An iterative localization algorithm is developed following an indirect estimation paradigm, using the closed-form estimates of the
LoS
and
NLoS
distances as input for a low-complexity
Iterative Weighted Least Squares (IWLS)
algorithm. The weights of the
IWLS
are set according to the inverse of the
CRLB
, evaluated at the estimated distances to account for path-dependent uncertainty. Remarkably, the algorithm operates without any prior knowledge of the
PD
position, leveraging an adaptive beam steering mechanism to dynamically orient the distributed
OIRSs
toward the
PD
, and converges to the theoretical bounds within a few iterations.

•

We derive the
CRLB
for
LoS
/
NLoS
distance estimation and subsequent position estimation, in a setup involving a single
LED
and distributed single-element
OIRSs
, and conduct a theoretical analysis of the achievable performance. As a byproduct, the
CRLB
is used to set the weighting matrix of the
IWLS
algorithm.

•

A simulation campaign is carried out to assess the performance of the proposed distance and position estimation algorithms in comparison with the corresponding
Distance Error Bound (DEB)
and
Position Error Bound (PEB)
, as a function of different system parameters and operating conditions.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Sec.
II
introduces the system geometry, outlines the
LoS
and
NLoS
channel models, and defines the main problem. Sec.
III
and
IV
present the proposed
LoS
and
NLoS
distance estimators, including both
ML
and relaxed ML approaches. In Sec.
VI
, we conduct a Fisher information analysis and derive the theoretical bounds for distance and position estimation. The complete localization algorithm is detailed in Sec.
V
, and its performance, along with that of the distance estimators, is assessed in Sec.
VII
. Conclusions are drawn in Sec.
VIII
.

-assisted

N

[

]

II-A

System Geometry

The
LED
emitting incoherent light is located at a known position
\bm{q}=\left[q_{x}\ q_{y}\ q_{z}\right]^{\mathsf{T}}
. The
n
-th
OIRS
, described by its width
W

H

), has its center located at
\bm{w}_{n}=\left[w_{n,x}\ w_{n,y}\ w_{n,z}\right]^{\mathsf{T}}
. The reconfigurability of the

-th

Leveraging this reconfigurability, we define two distinct operating modes, controlled by a binary variable

. In the first mode, referred to as

(

placed on the rightmost wall in Fig.

). In the second mode, called

(

), the

The

is located at an unknown position
\bm{u}=\left[u_{x}\ u_{y}\ 0\right]^{\mathsf{T}}
. Accordingly,

denotes the

distance between the

and the

, with corresponding irradiance and incidence angles
\theta
and

.
The actual reflection point on the
n
-th

is denoted by
\bm{r}_{n}=\left[r_{n,x}\ r_{n,y}\ r_{n,z}\right]^{\mathsf{T}}
. All vectors are expressed in the global coordinate reference system

.
For the sake of

channel characterization,
\bm{r}_{n}
can be computed via a transformation between the global and the local reference system of the
n
-th
OIRS
(see Supplementary Material S-I).
The distance between
\bm{r}_{n}
and the
PD
is given by
d_{n}=\|\bm{r}_{n}-\bm{u}\|
,
while the distance between the
LED
and
\bm{r}_{n}
is denoted as
s_{n}=\|\bm{q}-\bm{r}_{n}\|
. Additionally,
\theta_{n}
represents the irradiance angle between the
LED
and
\bm{r}_{n}
, whereas
\varphi_{n}
denotes the incidence angle between
\bm{r}_{n}
and the
PD
.

II-B

Visible Light Channel Gain Model

The channel gain over the
LoS
path (
LED
-
PD
) in a

system is characterized as
[
19
]

h=\frac{ATG(m+1)}{2\pi d^{2}}\cos^{m}(\theta)\cos(\varphi),

(1)

with

the light detector area,
T
the optical filter gain, and

is the Lambertian index with

denoting the half-intensity radiation angle.
The term

G=\begin{cases}\frac{f^{2}}{\sin^{2}{\psi}},&\text{$0\leq\varphi\leq\psi$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(2)

with
\psi
the
PD

Field of View (FoV)
and

the refractive index.

In indoor

systems, specular reflection dominates

propagation, as penetration, diffraction, and diffuse reflection are negligible due to severe signal attenuation and the extremely short wavelengths involved
[
5
,
33
]
.
Such a contribution can be modeled using (
1

LED
-
OIRS
and

-
PD
paths as
[
5
]

h_{n}=\rho\frac{ATG(m+1)}{2\pi(s_{n}+d_{n})^{2}}\cos^{m}(\theta_{n})\cos(\varphi_{n}),

(3)

with

As it can be noted, the path loss in (

25

34

II-C

Noise Model

The electric current induced by the noise at the PD follows a Gaussian distribution
[
6
,
35
]

\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),

(4)

with
\sigma^{2}
being the noise power, accounting for the contribution of two distinct noise sources: shot noise and thermal noise.

The shot noise term represents a random variation in signal intensity due to the discrete nature of particle interactions, such as photons striking the sensor, causing fluctuations, especially at low signal levels.
This phenomenon can be expressed by means of its variance as
[
6
]

\sigma_{\text{\tiny{S}}}^{2}=2qRPB+2qI_{1}B,

(5)

where
q
is the electron charge,
R
is the
PD
responsivity,
B
is the bandwidth, and
I_{1}
is the current induced by the background light. The total received power
P=p\left(h+\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}h_{n}\right)
includes contributions from the
LoS
component
h
and the
NLoS
paths
h_{n}
reflected by the active
OIRSs
(i.e., with
a_{n}=1
), with
p
being the radiometric transmitted power.

The second term represents the thermal noise, resulting from the thermal agitation of electrons within the
PD
circuit
[
6
]
. Specifically, its variance can be expressed as
[
36
]

\sigma_{\text{\tiny{T}}}^{2}=\frac{8\pi\kappa\tau}{G_{0}}\nu AI_{2}B^{2}+\frac{16\pi^{2}\kappa\tau\varrho}{g}\nu^{2}A^{2}I_{3}B^{3},

(6)

where
\kappa
is the Boltzmann’s constant,
\tau
is the absolute temperature,
G_{0}
is the open-loop voltage gain, and
\nu
is the fixed capacitance of
PD
. Furthermore,
\varrho
is the channel noise factor,
g
the transconductance,
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
are the noise bandwidth factors
[
35
]
. It is worth noting that
\sigma^{2}=\sigma_{\text{\tiny{S}}}^{2}+\sigma_{\text{\tiny{T}}}^{2}
has an explicit dependency on both the
LoS
distance
d
and the
NLoS
distances
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
(related to the
OIRS
-
PD
paths), through the received signal power
P
appearing in
\sigma_{\text{\tiny{S}}}^{2}
. This signal-dependent nature of the noise variance will be explicitly used in the derivation of our estimation algorithms.

II-E

Problem Definition

Our aim is to tackle the problem of estimating the unknown
PD
position
\bm{u}
, using information conveyed by both
LoS
and
NLoS
signal contributions. To this aim, an indirect (two-step) adaptive localization paradigm is pursued: in a first step, we estimate the
LoS
distance
d
between the
LED
and the
PD
, along with the set of
NLoS

\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, each corresponding to a signal path via the
n
-th
OIRS
. Then, in the second step, these estimated distances are used to initialize a
low-complexity
iterative localization algorithm. The resulting
PD

\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
of each
OIRS
. This process aims to resolve the initial misalignment and progressively reorient all
OIRSs
toward the
PD
, so enhancing the accuracy of the localization process.

III
LoS (LED-PD) Distance Estimation

To estimate the
LoS
distance
d
, we initially consider a configuration where all the
N

OIRSs
are set in
deactivated
mode (i.e.,
a_{n}=0\,\,\forall n=1,\ldots,N
). The
PD
collects
K\in\mathbb{N}
independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples of noncoherent light emitted by the
LED
, denoted as
\mu_{0,k}
, with
k=1,\dots,K
, and expressed following (
9
) as

\mu_{0,k}=Rph+\eta_{0,k},

(11)

with
\eta_{0,k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{0})
and

\sigma^{2}_{0}=\sigma_{\text{\tiny{T}}}^{2}+2qRphB+2qI_{1}B
. Accordingly, we have that
\mu_{0,k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\mu}_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0})
, with
\bar{\mu}_{0}=Rph
.

III-A

Maximum Likelihood LoS Distance Estimation

In this section, we derive the
ML
estimator of the
LoS
distance
d
, leveraging the dependency appearing in both the channel gain (
7
) and the shot noise variance term (
5
).
To this aim, using (
5
)-(
7
) we rewrite

\sigma^{2}_{0}(d)=a+b\bar{\mu}_{0}(d),

(12)

with
a=\sigma_{\text{\tiny{T}}}^{2}+bI_{1}
and
b=2qB
. Furthermore, using (
7
) we make explicit the dependency of
\bar{\mu}_{0}(d)
on the unknown
d
as

\bar{\mu}_{0}(d)=\frac{\xi}{d^{m+3}},

(13)

with
\xi={\frac{RpATG(m+1)(q_{z})^{m+1}}{2\pi}}
a known coefficient.

The likelihood function for the
LoS
distance estimation problem is given by

L^{\text{\tiny LoS}}(\bm{\mu}_{0};d)=\prod^{K}_{k=1}f(\mu_{0,k};d)=\displaystyle\frac{e\!\!\!^{-\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\mu_{0,k}-\frac{\xi}{d^{m+3}}\right)^{2}}{2\left(a+b\frac{\xi}{d^{m+3}}\right)}}}{{\left[2\pi(a+b\frac{\xi}{d^{m+3}})\right]^{\frac{K}{2}}}},

(14)

where
\bm{\mu}_{0}=[\mu_{0,1}\ \cdots\ \mu_{0,K}]^{\mathsf{T}}
is a vector stacking the
K
observations and
f(\mu_{0,k};d)
denotes the probability density function (pdf) of
\mu_{0,k}
given
d
.
The
MLE
problem can be then formulated as

\hat{d}^{\text{\tiny ML}}=\argmin_{d}\mathcal{L}^{\text{\tiny LoS}}(\bm{\mu}_{0};d),

(15)

where, for convenience, we transformed the original maximization problem through the negative log-likelihood function

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{\tiny LoS}}(\bm{\mu}_{0};d)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{\tiny def}}}{{=}}-\ln L^{\text{\tiny LoS}}(\bm{\mu}_{0};d)

\displaystyle=\frac{K}{2}\ln{\left[2\pi\left(a+b\frac{\xi}{d^{m+3}}\right)\right]}+\frac{\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\left(\mu_{0,k}-\frac{\xi}{d^{m+3}}\right)^{2}}{2(a+b\frac{\xi}{d^{m+3}})}.

(16)

To solve the optimization problem in (
15
), we seek the value of
d
that satisfies
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}^{\text{\tiny LoS}}(\bm{\mu}_{0};d)}{\partial d}=0
.
Given that
a
,
b
,
m
,
d
,
\xi
, and
K
are strictly positive real values, after some algebraic manipulations, we obtain the following closed-form solution

\!\!\!\hat{d}^{\text{\tiny ML}}\!=\!\left[\frac{\xi\left(\!\!\sqrt{\!(a\!+\!bS_{1})^{2}\!+\!b^{2}(S_{2}\!-\!S_{1}^{2})\!+\!\frac{b^{4}}{4}}\!+\!a\!+\!\frac{b^{2}}{2}\!\!\right)}{2aS_{1}+bS_{2}-ab}\right]^{\frac{1}{m+3}}\!\!\!\!,

(17)

where we introduced the summation terms

S_{1}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mu_{0,k},\quad S_{2}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mu_{0,k}^{2}.

(18)

By closely examining (
17
) and leveraging the definitions in (
18
), we observe that the sample mean of the observables,
S_{1}
, and the sample mean of their squared values,
S_{2}
, serve as
sufficient statistics
for the
ML
estimator of
d
. More specifically, (
17
) depends on both
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, not only as separate terms, but also through the term
(S_{2}-S_{1}^{2})
capturing the sample variance of the observations.

III-B

Relaxed ML LoS Distance Estimation

For comparison, we derive an alternative
LoS
distance estimation algorithm that takes advantage of an unstructured transformation of the noise variance
\sigma^{2}_{0}(d)
: it neglects the dependence of the latter from the unknown
d
and, more in general, its signal-dependent structure in (
12
).
Under this relaxed model, the dependency on the sought
d
remains only in
\bar{\mu}_{0}(d)
(mean of the observables), while the variance
\sigma^{2}_{0}
is treated as an additional unknown nuisance parameter to be estimated. The corresponding relaxed
ML
(RML) estimation problem, again formulated as an equivalent minimization problem based on the negative log-likelihood function, can be expressed as

\hat{d}^{\text{\tiny RML}}=\argmin_{d}\left[\argmin_{\sigma^{2}_{0}}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{\tiny LoS}}(\bm{\mu}_{0};d,\sigma^{2}_{0})\right],

(19)

with

\!\!\!\!\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{\tiny LoS}}(\bm{\mu}_{0};d,\sigma^{2}_{0})\!=\!\frac{K}{2}\ln{(2\pi\sigma_{0}^{2})}+\frac{1}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\!\left(\!\mu_{0,k}\!-\!\frac{\xi}{d^{m+3}}\!\right)^{\!2}\!\!.

(20)

It is simple to observe that the inner minimization in (
19
) with respect to
\sigma^{2}_{0}
is solved by

\hat{\sigma}_{0}^{2}=\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\left(\mu_{0,k}-\frac{\xi}{d^{m+3}}\right)^{2}.

(21)

Substituting this value back in (
20
), neglecting unnecessary constant terms, and considering a monotonic transformation of the
log-likelihood function leads to a compressed
cost function which, with a slight abuse of notation, is denoted by

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{\tiny LoS}}(\bm{\mu}_{0};d)=\frac{1}{K}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\left(\!\mu_{0,k}-\frac{\xi}{d^{m+3}}\!\right)^{2},

(22)

and the RML estimation problem reduces to

\hat{d}^{\text{\tiny RML}}=\argmin_{d}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{\tiny LoS}}(\bm{\mu}_{0};d).

(23)

The solution of the RML estimation problem can be then obtained in closed form as

\hat{d}^{\text{\tiny RML}}=\sqrt[m+3]{\frac{\xi}{S_{1}}}.

(24)

By directly comparing (
24
) and (
17
), it is noteworthy that, in the case of the RML estimator, only the sample mean
S_{1}
serves as a sufficient statistic for its computation.

IV
NLoS (OIRS-PD) Distance Estimation

This section deals with the estimation of the distances
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, each corresponding to a
NLoS
path received by the
PD
via the
n
-th
OIRS
.
To prevent interference among
OIRSs
, we employ a structured activation protocol in which only one
OIRS
operates in
active reflection
mode at a time (which is tantamount to having
\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{n}=1
). The activation follows a predefined sequence known to the
PD
, that collects
K_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
samples of the incoming signals from the
n
-th
OIRS
, denoted by
\mu_{n,k}
with
k=1,\dots,K_{n}
, including
LoS
and
NLoS
as

\mu_{n,k}=Rp(h+h_{n})+\eta_{n,k},

(25)

where
\eta_{n,k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{n})
, with
\sigma^{2}_{n}=a+bRp(h+h_{n})
, denotes the additive noise.
We can now take advantage of the
LoS
estimation step (in previous Sec.
III
) and perform a subtraction of the estimated
LoS
contribution from (
25
) as

\chi_{n,k}=\mu_{n,k}-\bar{\mu}_{0}(\hat{d})\approx Rph_{n}\!+\eta_{n,k},\!\!

(26)

with
\hat{d}
being either
\hat{d}^{\text{\tiny ML}}
or
\hat{d}^{\text{\tiny RML}}
. After compensating for the
LoS
component in the mean of
\mu_{n,k}
, the new observations approximately follow
\chi_{n,k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\chi}_{n},\sigma^{2}_{n})
, with
\bar{\chi}_{n}=Rph_{n}
.
We observe that
h_{n}
in (
8
) explicitly depends on the reflection point
\bm{r}_{n}
, which, in turn, is a function of the unknown
PD
position
\bm{u}
(as detailed in Supplementary Material S-I). To streamline the derivation and avoid overloading the notation, the following development of the
NLoS
distance estimators is conducted with respect to
\bm{r}_{n}
. Later in Section
V
, we will then present the complete iterative two-step localization algorithm that starts with an initial misalignment — using
\bm{w}_{n}
in place of
\bm{r}_{n}
in the
NLoS
distance estimators — and progressively refines the orientation of the
OIRS
network toward the
PD
through an adaptive beam steering strategy.

IV-A

Maximum Likelihood NLoS Distance Estimation

The
ML
estimator of the
NLoS
distance
d_{n}
is derived following the same lead of Sec.
III-A
. More specifically, we first make explicit the dependency of
\sigma^{2}_{n}
on the desired
d_{n}
as

\sigma^{2}_{n}(d_{n})=a+b[\bar{\chi}_{n}(d_{n})+\bar{\mu}_{0}(\hat{d})],

(27)

where we keep using the estimate
\hat{d}
(either
\hat{d}^{\text{\tiny ML}}
or
\hat{d}^{\text{\tiny RML}}
) in place of
d
.
Also in this case, we recast
\bar{\chi}_{n}(d_{n})
to expose its dependence on
d_{n}
as

\bar{\chi}_{n}(d_{n})=\frac{\omega}{(s_{n}+d_{n})^{2}d_{n}},

(28)

with
\omega=\frac{Rp\rho ATG(m+1)\left(q_{z}-r_{n,z}\right)^{m}r_{n,z}}{2\pi{s_{n}}^{m}}
. The likelihood function for the
NLoS
distance estimation problem is given by

\displaystyle\!L^{\text{\tiny NLoS}}(\bm{\chi}_{n}

\displaystyle;d_{n})\!=\prod^{K_{n}}_{k=1}f(\chi_{n,k};d_{n})=

\displaystyle=\!\frac{e^{-\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}\left(\chi_{n,k}-\frac{\omega}{(s_{n}+d_{n})^{2}d_{n}}\right)^{2}}{2\left[a+b\left(\frac{\xi}{\hat{d}^{m+3}}+\frac{\omega}{(s_{n}+d_{n})^{2}d_{n}}\right)\right]}}}{\left\{2\pi\left[a+b\left(\frac{\xi}{\hat{d}^{m+3}}+\frac{\omega}{(s_{n}+d_{n})^{2}d_{n}}\right)\right]\right\}^{\frac{K_{n}}{2}}},

(29)

where
\bm{\chi}_{n}=[\chi_{n,1}\ \cdots\ \chi_{n,K_{n}}]^{\mathsf{T}}
and
f(\chi_{n,k};d_{n})
denotes the pdf of
\chi_{n,k}
given
d_{n}
. By resorting to the negative log-likelihood function, the
MLE
problem is formulated as

\hat{d}^{\text{\tiny ML}}_{n}=\argmin_{d_{n}}\mathcal{L}^{\text{\tiny NLoS}}(\bm{\chi}_{n};d_{n}),

(30)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{\tiny NLoS}}(\bm{\chi}_{n};d_{n})=-\ln L^{\text{\tiny NLoS}}(\bm{\chi}_{n};d_{n})=

\displaystyle=\frac{K_{n}}{2}\ln 2\pi+\frac{K_{n}}{2}\ln\!{\left[\!a\!+\!b\!\left(\frac{\omega}{(s_{n}+d_{n})^{2}{d_{n}}}+\frac{\xi}{\hat{d}^{m+3}}\!\right)\!\right]}

\displaystyle+\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}(\chi_{n,k}-\frac{\omega}{(s_{n}+d_{n})^{2}d_{n}})^{2}}{2\left[a+b\left(\frac{\omega}{(s_{n}+d_{n})^{2}{d_{n}}}+\frac{\xi}{\hat{d}^{m+3}}\right)\right]}.

(31)

Unlike the
LoS
scenario, a closed-form solution for
d_{n}
that minimizes (
30
) is not available in the
NLoS
case. Since the objective function is highly non-linear, but depends only on the unknown
d_{n}
, the
ML
estimate
\hat{d}^{\text{\tiny ML}}_{n}
can be obtained by performing a one-dimensional grid search over the space of
d_{n}
. To avoid the need for numerical optimization, in the following we propose a relaxed formulation of the
ML
estimator tailored to the
NLoS
scenario.

IV-B

Relaxed ML NLoS Distance Estimation

Similarly to the
LoS
case, we adopt an unstructured transformation of the noise variance
\sigma^{2}_{n}(d_{n})
, relaxing its dependency on the unknown
d_{n}
.
As a result,
\sigma^{2}_{n}
becomes an additional nuisance parameter to be estimated jointly with the desired
d_{n}
. The corresponding RML estimation problem is expressed, in terms of the negative log-likelihood function, as

\hat{d}_{n}^{\text{\tiny RML}}=\argmin_{d_{n}}\left[\operatorname*{min}_{\sigma^{2}_{n}}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{\tiny NLoS}}(\bm{\chi}_{n};d_{n},\sigma^{2}_{n})\right],

(32)

with

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{\tiny NLoS}}(\bm{\chi}_{n};d_{n},\sigma^{2}_{n})=-\ln L^{\text{\tiny NLoS}}(\bm{\chi}_{n};d_{n},\sigma^{2}_{n})=

\displaystyle=\frac{K_{n}}{2}\ln{(2\pi\sigma_{n}^{2})}+\frac{1}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K_{n}}\left(\chi_{n,k}-\frac{\omega}{(s_{n}+d_{n})^{2}d_{n}}\right)^{2}.

(33)

The inner minimization in (
32
) yields a closed-form expression for the estimate of
\sigma_{n}^{2}
as

\!\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}\!=\!\frac{1}{K_{n}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K_{n}}\left(\chi_{n,k}-\frac{\omega}{(s_{n}+d_{n})^{2}d_{n}}\right)^{2}.

(34)

Substituting the above estimate into (
33
), omitting constant terms that do not affect the minimization, and applying a monotonic transformation, we obtain the following compressed cost function

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{\tiny NLoS}}(\bm{\chi}_{n};d_{n})=\frac{1}{K_{n}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K_{n}}\left(\chi_{n,k}-\frac{\omega}{(s_{n}+d_{n})^{2}d_{n}}\right)^{2},

(35)

and the RML estimation problem in (
32
) reduces to

\hat{d}_{n}^{\text{\tiny RML}}=\argmin_{d_{n}}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{\tiny NLoS}}(\bm{\chi}_{n};d_{n}).

(36)

Minimization in (
36
) is conducted by differentiating
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{\tiny NLoS}}(\bm{\chi}_{n};d_{n})
with respect to
d_{n}
, manipulating the resulting expression to obtain a more tractable depressed cubic equation in
d_{n}
(details are reported in Supplementary Material S-II), leading to the following closed-form solution

\hat{d}_{n}^{\text{\tiny RML}}=\frac{\gamma T_{1}s^{2}_{n}}{3\upsilon}+\frac{\upsilon}{3\gamma T_{1}}-\frac{2{s_{n}}}{3},

(37)

with
T_{1}=\frac{1}{K_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}\chi_{n,k}
,
\gamma=\sqrt[3]{2}
, and

\upsilon\!=\!\left(\!3\sqrt{3\omega T_{1}^{4}\!\left(27\omega\!+\!4T_{1}s^{3}_{n}\right)}\!+\!2T_{1}^{3}s^{3}_{n}\!+\!27\omega T_{1}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\!\!\!.

(38)

From (
37
)–(
38
), it is evident that, similarly to the RML distance estimator in the
LoS
case, the sample mean
T_{1}
of the new observations
\chi_{n,k}
represents a sufficient statistic also for the RML estimator in the
NLoS
scenario.

V
Iterative Indirect Localization Algorithm with Adaptive Beam Steering

In this section, we present a low-complexity iterative localization algorithm based on an indirect estimation paradigm, where the position of the
PD
is inferred through a two-step process: an initial
distance estimation
phase, followed by a subsequent
position estimation
step that leverages the distance estimates obtained in the first stage. The algorithm operates without requiring any prior information on
\bm{u}
, and employs a successive adaptive beam-steering strategy to iteratively reorient the
OIRS
network toward the
PD
position. We detail in the following each involved processing step.

LoS
&
NLoS
Distance Estimation:

Following the indirect (two-step) localization paradigm, the first stage involves the estimation of the
LoS
distance
d
, as well as of the
NLoS
distances
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
. The former is carried out by setting all
N

OIRSs
in deactivated mode (
a_{n}=0,\forall n
), and using either the ML estimator (
\hat{d}^{\text{\tiny ML}}
in (
17
)) or the RML estimator (
\hat{d}^{\text{\tiny RML}}
in (
24
)). Once an estimate of the
LoS
distance is obtained, the algorithm proceeds with the
NLoS
distance estimation, using either the
ML
estimator derived in Sec.
IV-A
, or the RML estimator in Sec.
IV-B
. At this point, we observe that both the ML cost function in (
IV-A
) — whose numerical minimization with respect to
d_{n}
for each
n
-th
OIRS
-
PD
link yields the
ML
estimate — and the closed-form RML estimator in (
37
)-(
38
) depend on the reflection point
\bm{r}_{n}
, which in turn is a function of the unknown
PD
position
\bm{u}
. Consequently, a direct evaluation of the
NLoS
estimators would require some form of a priori information about
\bm{u}
. This limitation is particularly critical at the beginning of the localization procedure, when no estimate of
\bm{u}
is yet available.

To overcome this need, we propose to initially evaluate the
NLoS
distance estimators using
\bm{w}_{n}
in place of
\bm{r}_{n}
— assuming that all
OIRSs
are already perfectly steered towards the
PD
position — which leads to having
\tilde{d}_{n}=\|\bm{w}_{n}-\bm{u}\|
and
\tilde{s}_{n}=\|\bm{q}-\bm{w}_{n}\|
in place of
d_{n}
and
s_{n}
in (
IV-A
) and (
37
)-(
38
), respectively.
This is tantamount to paying the price of an initial misalignment as a trade-off to enable the estimation of the
NLoS
distances as either
\{\hat{d}^{\text{\tiny ML}}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
or
\{\hat{d}^{\text{\tiny RML}}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, without any prior information on
\bm{u}
. This initial approximation is progressively refined over the iterations of the localization algorithm through a suitable adaptive beam steering strategy, which will be presented in the following.

Position Estimation:

Once the
PD
has obtained the estimates of the
LoS
and
NLoS
distances — denoted, for notational convenience, by
\hat{d}
and
\{\hat{d}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, respectively (with their exact values depending on the chosen estimation method, either
ML
or RML) — it proceeds to estimate its own position. To this aim, we stack the estimated distances into a vector
\hat{\bm{d}}=[\hat{d}\ \hat{d}_{1}\ \cdots\ \hat{d}_{N}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
and set up a system of
N+1
(with
N\geq 2
to make the problem well-posed) nonlinear equations linking
\hat{\bm{d}}
with the sought
PD
position
\bm{u}
as

\hat{\bm{d}}^{2}=\bm{f}(\bm{u})+\bm{\zeta},

(39)

with
\bm{\zeta}=[\zeta\ \zeta_{1}\ \cdots\ \zeta_{N}]^{T}

denoting the vector of the generic errors associated to the
LoS
and
NLoS
distance estimation process, respectively,
\hat{\bm{d}}^{2}
the element-wise square of
\hat{\bm{d}}
, and the nonlinear vector function
\bm{f}(\bm{u})\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
is given by

\bm{f}(\bm{u})=\left[\|\bm{u}-\bm{q}\|^{2},\;\|\bm{u}-\bm{w}_{1}\|^{2},\;\cdots,\;\|\bm{u}-\bm{w}_{N}\|^{2}\right]^{T}.

(40)

The
PD
position is then estimated by solving a Weighted Least Squares (WLS) optimization problem formulated as

\hat{\bm{u}}=\min_{\bm{u}}\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{u})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{W}\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{u}),

(41)

where
\bm{\epsilon}(\bm{u})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\hat{\bm{d}}^{2}-\bm{f}(\bm{u})
, and the diagonal weight matrix
\bm{W}=\mathrm{diag}(\text{w}_{0},\text{w}_{1},\ldots,\text{w}_{N})
. We anticipate that the weights in
\bm{W}
will be set according to the inverse of the
CRLB
associated with the distance estimation problems, whose derivation is proposed in Sec.
VI
.
Considering the limited computational capabilities available on a low-cost
PD
, we solve (
41
) using a low-complexity
IWLS
approach. More specifically, denoting with
\hat{\bm{u}}^{(i)}
the
PD
position estimate at current iteration
i
, we employ the following iterative update rule
[
37
, Ch. 8]

\hat{\bm{u}}^{[i+1]}=\hat{\bm{u}}^{[i]}+\left((\bm{H}^{[i]})^{T}\bm{W}\bm{H}^{[i]}\right)^{-1}(\bm{H}^{[i]})^{T}\bm{W}\bm{\epsilon}^{[i]},

(42)

with
\bm{H}^{[i]}=\left.\frac{\partial\bm{f}(\bm{u})}{\partial\bm{u}}\right|_{\bm{u}=\hat{\bm{u}}^{[i]}}
the Jacobian of
\bm{f}(\bm{u})
,
\bm{\epsilon}^{[i]}=\hat{\bm{d}}^{2}-\bm{f}(\hat{\bm{u}}^{[i]})
, and
\bm{f}(\bm{u})\approx\bm{f}(\hat{\bm{u}}^{[i]})+\bm{H}^{[i]}(\bm{u}-\hat{\bm{u}}^{(i)})
. The IWLS algorithm
stops when the difference between the current and the updated estimate
\left\lVert\hat{\bm{u}}^{[i+1]}-\hat{\bm{u}}^{[i]}\right\rVert<\varepsilon
for a predefined threshold
\varepsilon
or if a maximum number of
iterations is reached.

VI
Cramér-Rao Lower Bound

In this section, we derive the
Fisher Information Matrix (FIM)
and the corresponding
CRLB
for both i)
LoS
and
NLoS
distance estimation, and ii)
PD
position estimation. These results lead to the definition of the
DEB
and, subsequently, the
PEB
. As a byproduct, the
DEB
is employed to set the weights
\{\text{w}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
in the weighting matrix
\bm{W}
used for the IWLS update rule in (
42
). Furthermore, both the
DEB
and
PEB
will be used in Sec.
VII
as theoretical performance benchmarks for the proposed distance estimation and localization algorithms.

VI-A

Distance Error Bounds

We start by deriving the DEB on the estimation of the
LoS
distance
d
, knowing that any unbiased estimator
\hat{d}
of
d
has a
Mean Squared Error (MSE)
lower bounded by
[
37
]

\mathbb{E}[(\hat{d}-d)^{2}]\geq J_{d}^{-1},

(45)

where
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation operator. The Fisher information
J_{d}
for the
LoS
model is

\displaystyle J_{d}

\displaystyle=\!-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\!\frac{\partial^{2}\ln L^{\text{\tiny LoS}}(\bm{\mu}_{0};d)}{\partial d^{2}}\!\right]\!

\displaystyle=\!\frac{K(m\!+\!3)^{2}\mathrm{SNR}_{0}(d)(\sigma^{2}_{0}(d)\!+\!b^{2}/2)}{d^{2}\sigma^{2}_{0}(d)},

(46)

with
\mathrm{SNR}_{0}(d)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\bar{\mu}^{2}_{0}(d)/\sigma^{2}_{0}(d)
the
SNR
in the
LoS
case (whose expression easily follows from (
10
), after setting
a_{n}=0,\forall n
). Accordingly, the DEB on
d
is given by

\mathrm{DEB}(d)=\sqrt{J_{d}^{-1}}.

(47)

From (
VI-A
) and (
47
), we observe that the DEB exhibits intuitive scaling behavior: it decreases as the number of observations
K
increases or under higher SNR conditions, while it grows with increasing
d
(considering a fixed transmit power
p
).

Similarly, any unbiased estimator
\hat{d}_{n}
of the
NLoS
distance
d_{n}
can achieve
MSE
performance lower bounded by
\mathbb{E}[(\hat{d}_{n}-d_{n})^{2}]\geq J_{d_{n}}^{-1}
, with the Fisher information for the
NLoS
model

\displaystyle J_{d_{n}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\ln L^{\text{\tiny NLoS}}(\bm{\chi}_{n};d_{n})}{\partial{d_{n}}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{K_{n}\mathrm{SNR}_{n}(d_{n})(s_{n}+3d_{n})^{2}(\sigma^{2}_{n}(d_{n})+b^{2}/2)}{d^{2}_{n}(s_{n}+d_{n})^{2}\sigma^{2}_{n}(d_{n})},

(48)

where
\mathrm{SNR}_{n}(d_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\bar{\chi}^{2}_{n}(d_{n})/\sigma^{2}_{n}(d_{n})
captures the SNR of the
NLoS
reflection generated by the
n
-th
OIRS
(obtained from (
10
) by setting only the
n
-th coefficient
a_{n}=1
, with all others set to zero, and after
LoS
compensation, as per (
26
)). Accordingly, the DEB on
d_{n}
is given by

\mathrm{DEB}(d_{n})=\sqrt{J_{d_{n}}^{-1}}.

(49)

Despite its slightly more involved structure, (
VI-A
) shares a similar scaling behavior with (
VI-A
), with the accuracy in the estimation of
d_{n}
that tends to improve as
\mathrm{SNR}_{n}(d_{n})
increases or as more observations
K_{n}
are available, whereas it degrades at a faster rate than (
VI-A
) as
d_{n}
increases, owing to the increased path loss over the reflected path.

As anticipated, the DEB for both
LoS
and
NLoS
distance estimation are used to set the entries of the weighting matrix
\bm{W}
in the
IWLS
update rule (
42
), with
\text{w}_{0}=1/\mathrm{DEB}(\hat{d})
and
\text{w}_{n}=1/\mathrm{DEB}(\hat{d}_{n})
, where
\hat{d}
and
\{\hat{d}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
are the distances estimated via either the ML or RML approach.

VI-B

Position Error Bound

Let
\bm{d}=[d\ d_{1}\ \cdots\ d_{N}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1\times 1}
denote the vector stacking the unknown
LoS
and
NLoS
distances. The joint likelihood function, accounting for all the information associated to the
LoS
and
NLoS
paths, can be expressed as

L(\bm{\mu};\bm{d})=L^{\text{\tiny LoS}}(\bm{\mu}_{0};d)\times\prod_{n=1}^{N}L^{\text{\tiny NLoS}}(\bm{\mu}_{n};d,d_{n}),

(50)

where
\bm{\mu}_{n}=[\mu_{n,1}\ \cdots\mu_{n,K_{n}}]^{\mathsf{T}}
is the vector stacking the
K_{n}
samples of the signals received by the
PD
via the
n
-th
OIRS
, in the form of (
25
).
The complete
FIM

\bm{J}_{\bm{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times(N+1)}
in the distance domain is defined as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\bm{J}_{\bm{d}}

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\ln L(\bm{\mu};\bm{d})}{\partial\bm{d}\partial\bm{d}^{T}}\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}{\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial^{2}\ln L(\bm{\mu};\bm{d})}{\partial d\partial d}&\frac{\partial^{2}\ln L(\bm{\mu};\bm{d})}{\partial d\partial d_{1}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial^{2}\ln L(\bm{\mu};\bm{d})}{\partial d\partial d_{N}}\\
\frac{\partial^{2}\ln L(\bm{\mu};\bm{d})}{\partial d_{1}\partial d}&\frac{\partial^{2}\ln L(\bm{\mu};\bm{d})}{\partial d_{1}\partial d_{1}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial^{2}\ln L(\bm{\mu};\bm{d})}{\partial d_{1}\partial d_{N}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial^{2}\ln L(\bm{\mu};\bm{d})}{\partial d_{N}\partial d}&\frac{\partial^{2}\ln L(\bm{\mu};\bm{d})}{\partial d_{N}\partial d_{1}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial^{2}\ln L(\bm{\mu};\bm{d})}{\partial d_{N}\partial d_{N}}\end{bmatrix}}.

(51)

The expression for each entry of
\bm{J}_{\bm{d}}
is given in Supplementary Material S-III.

We now derive the FIM in the position domain
by means of a transformation from the vector of distances
\bm{d}
to the vector of location parameters
\bm{\upsilon}=[u_{x}\ u_{y}]^{T}
. The
FIM
of
\bm{\upsilon}
follows by applying a
2\times(N+1)
transformation matrix
\bm{T}
as

\bm{J}_{\bm{\upsilon}}=\bm{T}\bm{J}_{\bm{d}}\bm{T}^{\mathsf{T}},

(52)

where

\!\!\bm{T}\!=\!\frac{\partial\bm{d}^{T}}{\partial\bm{\upsilon}}\!=\!{\begin{bmatrix}{\partial d}/{\partial u_{x}}&{\partial d_{1}}/{\partial u_{x}}&\cdots&{\partial d_{N}}/{\partial u_{x}}\\
{\partial d}/{\partial u_{y}}&{\partial d_{1}}/{\partial u_{y}}&\cdots&{\partial d_{N}}/{\partial u_{y}}\end{bmatrix}},

(53)

with

\displaystyle\frac{\partial d}{\partial u_{x}}=\frac{u_{x}-q_{x}}{\|\bm{u}-\bm{q}\|},\qquad\frac{\partial d}{\partial u_{y}}=\frac{u_{y}-q_{y}}{\|\bm{u}-\bm{q}\|},

(54)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial d_{n}}{\partial u_{x}}=\frac{u_{x}-r_{n,x}}{\|\bm{u}-\bm{r}_{n}\|},\qquad\!\!\!\frac{\partial d_{n}}{\partial u_{y}}=\frac{u_{y}-r_{n,y}}{\|\bm{u}-\bm{r}_{n}\|}.

(55)

Finally, the
PEB
is given by

\mathrm{PEB}=\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\left(\bm{J}^{-1}_{\bm{\upsilon}}\right)},

(56)

with
\mathrm{tr}(\cdot)
the trace operator.

VIII
Conclusion

In this work, we have presented a novel approach for visible light-based indoor localization that leverages only a single
LED
transmitter and multiple distributed single-element
OIRSs
. Departing from existing direct localization frameworks, we proposed an indirect estimation paradigm built upon computationally efficient, closed-form estimators: the optimal ML estimator for the
LoS
distance, and a tailored RML estimator for the
NLoS
distances, the latter circumventing the numerical optimization required by its optimal ML counterpart. Building upon these estimators, we designed a low-complexity
IWLS
localization algorithm, whose weights are derived from the
CRLB
to account for path-dependent uncertainty on distance estimation. Notably, the algorithm integrates an adaptive beam-steering mechanism to iteratively refine the orientation of the OIRSs and face initial pointing mismatches, so enabling the localization process without any prior knowledge on the
PD
position. Fundamental Fisher information analyses were conducted to derive the CRLBs for distance and position estimation under the proposed setup. Extensive numerical evaluations demonstrated the accuracy, robustness, and efficiency of the proposed distance and position estimation algorithms across various SNR regimes and OIRS misalignment conditions. Specifically, the ML
LoS
estimator unveiled excellent estimation performance even when working with a very small sample size, attaining the corresponding theoretical bound already for low values of the SNR. On the other hand, the RML
NLoS
estimator provided performance close to the optimal ML, but at a significantly reduced cost. Moreover, both ML and RML NLoS estimators exhibited a good robustness to
OIRS
orientation errors of up to
20\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
, guaranteeing estimation accuracy in the order of a few centimeters. Overall, the proposed localization algorithm, combining IWLS and the adaptive beam steering strategy, required only a couple of iterations to correctly re-orient the
OIRS
network toward the
PD
, achieving RMSE values close to the corresponding PEB, and offering a spatial coverage of about 80% of the area when an average of three
OIRSs
are deployed on each wall.

Possible directions of future work could include the joint optimization of
LED
and
OIRSs
placement, as well as the extension of the proposed approach to multi-
PD
localization scenarios. Another promising direction involves investigating the use of metasurface-based
OIRSs
, capable of of absorbing or phase-shifting selectively different wavelengths, to further enhance localization performance.
