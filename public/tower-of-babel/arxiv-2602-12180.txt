How Sampling Shapes LLM Alignment: From One-Shot Optima to Iterative Dynamics
Yurong Chen, Yu He, Michael I. Jordan, Fan Yao

Abstract
Standard methods for aligning large language models with human preferences learn from pairwise comparisons among sampled candidate responses and regularize toward a reference policy. Despite their effectiveness, the effects of sampling and reference choices are poorly understood theoretically. We investigate these effects through Identity Preference Optimization, a widely used preference alignment framework and show that proper instance-dependent sampling can yield stronger ranking guarantees, while skewed on-policy sampling can induce excessive concentration under structured preferences. We then analyze iterative alignment dynamics in which the learned policy feeds back into future sampling and reference policies, reflecting a common practice of model-generated preference data. We prove that these dynamics can exhibit persistent oscillations or entropy collapse for certain parameter choices, and characterize regimes that guarantee stability. Our theoretical insights extend to Direct Preference Optimization, indicating the phenomena we captured are common to a broader class of preference-alignment methods. Experiments on real-world preference data validate our findings.

In this section, we consider the one-shot, offline setting and study the properties of the

Our results extend immediately to general reference policies by replacing

3.1
Axiom of (im)possibility under sampling

We begin by examining whether
IPO
satisfies certain natural desiderata,
treating it as
a preference aggregation method that maps
preference matrix to a
distribution over responses.
For a sampling distribution
\bm{\mu}\in\Delta_{K}
,
(
3

We say that

Since

Definition 2
.

•

A nonempty set

.
The

•

When

Definition 3

Let

3.2
Skewed sampling amplifies skewness

We now study how the sampling distribution shapes the
IPO
solution’s

We focus on ST preference matrices, for which the induced ranking is invariant to

Our main finding is that, under a natural structural condition on

To state the result formally, we first recall the definition of majorization.

Definition 9
.

A vector
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{K})\in\mathbb{R}^{K}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}\geq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k},\quad\forall m\in[K-1].

Further, we denote by

A vector

is at least as large as the global average.

.

Let

\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}^{\prime}

\bm{\theta}(\bm{\mu})=\beta^{-1}(\log\bm{\pi}^{\star}(P;\bm{\mu})-\log\bm{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}})

(4)

more concentrated on higher-ranked responses relative to a baseline

(tails are uniformly dominated by heads) for any

Corollary

4
Long-Term Effects of Iterative IPO

In this section, we study the long-run dynamics in iterative preference alignment pipelines, which are increasingly common in practice:
candidate responses are generated by the deployed policy and labeled by humans; the resulting data are used to fine-tune the next policy, which is then redeployed to collect the next round of data. Meanwhile, the reference model is often refreshed
over time to track improvements in the current foundation model.
Therefore, both the sampling distribution and the reference policy evolve endogenously over rounds and shape subsequent updates.

We model such a deployment pipeline as a discrete-time dynamical system over policies. At round
t
, the incumbent policy is
\bm{\pi}_{t}\in\Delta_{K}
and the next-round alignment procedure derives the reference policy and sampling distribution from
\bm{\pi}_{t}
.
We capture these dependencies using two mixing parameters
\alpha,\lambda\in[0,1]
.
Specifically, for the reference policy that stabilizes the update, we consider a geometric mixture

\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}^{(t)}\ \propto\ \bm{\pi}_{t}^{\alpha}\,\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}^{\,1-\alpha},

(5)

interpolating between the current policy
\bm{\pi}_{t}
and a fixed base reference policy
\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}
, capturing the practice of dynamic reference models in
9
. Similarly, for the sampling distribution that determines which pairs of responses are likely to be compared by annotators, we consider an affine mixture

\bm{\mu}_{t}\ :=\ \lambda\,\bm{\pi}_{t}+(1-\lambda)\,\bm{\pi}_{0},

(6)

that interpolates between on-policy sampling from the deployed model and an off-policy distribution
\bm{\pi}_{0}
(e.g., a frozen earlier model or a fixed data source), capturing a practice introduced in IPO-MD
(
4
)
. This abstraction reflects a realistic deployment regime in which training and deployment are interleaved, and where practitioners explicitly mix on-policy and off-policy data to trade off adaptivity and stability.

By
Proposition

1
, the IPO update induced by these choices admits a closed-form policy mapping.
Substituting the mixed reference and mixed sampling distributions yields the following dynamical system.

Definition 11
.

The
mixed reference/sampling IPO (MRS-IPO) dynamics

\displaystyle\bm{\pi}_{t+1}=\mathrm{MRS}(\bm{\pi}_{t};P,\alpha,\beta,\lambda,\bm{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}},\bm{\pi}_{0}),

initialized by
\bm{\pi}_{1}=\bm{\pi}_{1}
is defined by

\bm{\pi}_{t+1}=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\log\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}^{(t)}+\beta\,P\bm{\mu}_{t}\right),t\geq 1,

(7)

where
\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}^{(t)}
and
\bm{\mu}_{t}
are defined in (
5
), (
6
),
\alpha\in[0,1]
controls how strongly the
reference
model tracks the current deployment,

\lambda\in[0,1]
controls how strongly the
sampling
is on-policy,

\beta>0
is the IPO inverse temperature,
and
P
is the underlying pairwise preference matrix.

The MRS-IPO dynamics (
7
) includes several common iterative alignment practices.
\alpha=0
indicates a fixed reference policy overtime while
\alpha=1
corresponds to a fully self-referential deployment, i.e., encouraging newly aligned model to remain close to the previous version. Similarly,
\lambda=1
corresponds to fully on-policy preference collection from the deployed model, whereas
\lambda<1
captures the widespread practice of incorporating off-policy data to improve stability and coverage.

Despite the naturalness of the MRS-IPO dynamics, we show they can exhibit fundamental long-term failure modes: non-convergence/oscillations (

4.1
Condorcet cycles can induce policy oscillation

We begin with preference structures that violate transitivity.
A canonical failure mode is a
Condorcet cycle
(e.g., rock–paper–scissors), where local pairwise comparisons
cannot be globally reconciled into a single ranking.
Such a cyclic structure is common in practice whenever preferences depend on nuanced trade-offs among latent-attributes or arise from heterogeneous annotators.

We show that in the presence of a Condorcet cycle, the
MRS-IPO dynamics can be
unstable
and fail to converge.
We
give an explicit construction for

Let

\frac{1}{2}+a&\frac{1}{2}-a&\frac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix},

The underlying reason that causes instability is that the MRS-IPO update behaves like mirror descent type algorithm and the shift-invariant component of

Theorem 13

For any
\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref},\bm{\pi}_{0}\in\Delta_{K}^{\circ}

4.2
Strong transitivity can lead to entropy collapse

We next present a complementary negative phenomenon, which is even more concerning from a long-term deployment perspective.
Section

3.2
showed that for strongly transitive preferences,
in a single round
, preference-aligned sampling can already strengthen concentration in the learned IPO solution.
In
iterative
alignment, since such a preference-aligned sampling can arise endogenously when deploying the current policy, we can show that MRS-IPO amplifies this effect over time: the deployed policy may become increasingly concentrated and eventually collapse to an extreme distribution, even when both the sampling distribution and the reference policy have full support. We formalize this observation and identify a key factor that drives this phenomenon.
Throughout this section we assume
P
is
strictly strongly transitive
(SST), meaning that
P
is ST and for any
i\in[K-1]
,
P_{ik}>P_{i+1,k}
holds for at least one
k\in[K]
. This assumption strengthens ST by requiring strict dominance at least one columnwise
3
3

3

SST assumption is made purely for clarity; the corresponding results under the weaker ST assumption convey the same qualitative message but require heavier notation (see
Section

C.3
).
. We also define a distribution
\bm{\pi}\in\Delta_{K}
(
\pi_{1}\geq\pi_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\pi_{K}
) is
\varepsilon
-collapsed
if
\pi_{1}\geq 1-\varepsilon
, meaning nearly all probability mass concentrates on the top-ranked response. The next
Theorem

8
shows that the long-term behavior of MRS-IPO depends crucially on the factor
\beta\lambda/(1-\alpha)
:

Theorem 14
.

Assume
P
is SST.
Let
\bm{\pi}_{0}\in\Delta_{K}^{\circ}
and
\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}\in\Delta_{K}^{\circ}
.
Consider the MRS-IPO dynamics
\bm{\pi}_{t+1}=\mathrm{MRS}(\bm{\pi}_{t};\alpha,\beta,\lambda)
. These dynamics exhibit the following two forms of collapse.

•

(
\varepsilon
-collapse when
\beta\lambda/(1-\alpha)
is large).

If
\alpha,\lambda\in[0,1)
, there exists a finite

such that for all
t\geq T

\bm{\pi}_{t,1}\ \geq\ 1-\left(\exp\!\left(\frac{\beta\lambda\delta}{2(1-\alpha)}\right)-1\right)^{-1},

where
\delta\coloneqq\min_{i\in[K-1]}\{(1-\lambda)\sum_{j=1}^{K}\pi_{0,j}\,(P_{ij}-P_{i+1,j})\}

•

If
\alpha=1

such that for all

H(\bm{\pi}_{t+1})<H(\bm{\pi}_{t}),

where
H(\bm{\pi})\coloneqq-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\pi_{i}\log\pi_{i}

\bm{\pi}_{t}\to\bm{e}_{1}

Theorem

14
identifies two limit regimes for MRS-IPO. When the reference policy is fully self-referential (
\alpha=1
), preference advantages accumulate additively across iterations, leading to monotone entropy decay and winner-take-all collapse. When
\alpha<1

not
remove the tendency toward concentration: as
\beta\lambda/(1-\alpha)
increases, the limiting policy becomes arbitrarily close to the extreme distribution that concentrates on the top response.

From a practical standpoint, this result highlights a common failure mode in iterative alignment pipelines.
Heavier reliance on the current model as a reference (larger
\alpha
closer to
1

\beta
), or more on-policy sampling (larger
\lambda

is that the collapse mechanism depends primarily on the update hyperparameters
(\alpha,\beta,\lambda)
and the preference structure, and is largely insensitive to the specific choice of reference policy
\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}
or baseline sampling distribution
\bm{\pi}_{0}
.
Consequently, entropy collapse should be viewed as an
inherent property of the self-reinforcing dynamics
in strongly transitive environments: choosing a more diverse reference model or mixing with a diverse baseline distribution cannot fundamentally prevent long-run concentration, because the influence of any static external source is geometrically diluted over successive iterations.

6
Conclusions

We have presented a theoretical investigation of how sampling and reference choices govern offline and iterative preference alignment, using IPO and DPO as case studies. Our results show that careless choices of these factors can yield compromised ranking guarantees, policy oscillations, or entropy collapse. We then identify parameter regimes that recover stability and avoid collapse in iterative preference learning. Our findings suggest that the sampling and dynamical effects are likely to extend across various preference-alignment methods beyond IPO/DPO.

Our findings point to a key open problem: how to design alignment pipelines with principled, adaptive control over sampling and reference updates—otherwise, instability is a predictable outcome of feedback-driven alignment. Addressing this challenge requires treating preference alignment as a stochastic online learning system rather than a static offline optimization problem. We view this shift in perspective as essential for building reliable alignment methods at scale.

B.5
Social choice axioms that IPO satisfies under arbitrary sampling

Given two preference matrices
P^{(1)},P^{(2)}
, define the pooled matrix with mixture weight
\lambda\in(0,1)

\displaystyle P^{(\lambda)}\coloneqq\lambda P^{(1)}+(1-\lambda)P^{(2)}.

Similarly, for two sampling distributions
\bm{\mu}^{(1)},\bm{\mu}^{(2)}\in\Delta_{K}
, define the pooled sampling distribution with mixture weight
\lambda\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}^{(\lambda)}\coloneqq\lambda\bm{\mu}^{(1)}+(1-\lambda)\bm{\mu}^{(2)}.

Definition 15
.

An aggregation rule
F:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\Delta_{K}
is said to satisfy the following axioms if

1.

Pooling consistency.

(a)

(
Preference pooling
)
For two
matrices
P^{(1)}
,
P^{(2)}
,

F(P^{(1)})=F(P^{(2)})
implies
F(P^{(\lambda)})=F(P^{(1)})
for all
\lambda\in(0,1)
.

(b)

(
Data pooling
, IPO-level)
For any
P
and samplings
\bm{\mu}^{(1)},\bm{\mu}^{(2)}
,

F_{\bm{\mu}^{(1)}}(P)=F_{\bm{\mu}^{(2)}}(P)
implies
F_{\bm{\mu}^{(\lambda)}}(P)=F_{\bm{\mu}^{(1)}}(P)
for all
\lambda\in(0,1)
.

2.

Pairwise monotonicity.

If
P
and
P^{\prime}
differ only at
(i,j)
with
P_{ij}>P^{\prime}_{ij}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{F(P)_{i}}{F(P)_{j}}\ \geq\ \frac{F(P^{\prime})_{i}}{F(P^{\prime})_{j}}.

Intuitively, pooling consistency captures the idea that aggregating indistinguishable sources of data should not change the outcome: if two preference matrices (or sampling distributions) lead to the same
IPO
solution, then any mixture of them should do so as well. Pairwise monotonicity formalizes a basic responsiveness requirement: strengthening the preference of
i
over
j
, while leaving all else unchanged, should not decrease the relative weight assigned to
i
over
j
.

Theorem 16
.

For any sampling distribution
\bm{\mu}
, the induced rule
F_{\bm{\mu}}
satisfies preference-pooling consistency and
pairwise monotonicity; and IPO satisfies data-pooling consistency.

Proof.

We first prove the preference pooling consistency.
F_{\bm{\mu}}(P^{(1)})=F_{\bm{\mu}}(P^{(2)})
implies that for any
i,j
,

\displaystyle\frac{\sum^{K}_{k=1}P^{(1)}_{ik}\mu_{k}}{\sum^{K}_{k=1}P^{(1)}_{jk}\mu_{k}}=\frac{\sum^{K}_{k=1}P^{(2)}_{ik}\mu_{k}}{\sum^{K}_{k=1}P^{(2)}_{jk}\mu_{k}}\penalty\ ,

and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\sum^{K}_{k=1}(\lambda P^{(1)}_{ik}+(1-\lambda)P^{(2)}_{ik})\mu_{k}}{\sum^{K}_{k=1}(\lambda P^{(1)}_{jk}+(1-\lambda)P^{(2)}_{jk})\mu_{k}}=\frac{\lambda\sum^{K}_{k=1}P^{(1)}_{ik}\mu_{k}+(1-\lambda)\sum^{K}_{k=1}P^{(2)}_{ik}\mu_{k}}{\lambda\sum^{K}_{k=1}P^{(1)}_{jk}\mu_{k}+(1-\lambda)\sum^{K}_{k=1}P^{(2)}_{jk}\mu_{k}}=\frac{\sum^{K}_{k=1}P^{(1)}_{ik}\mu_{k}}{\sum^{K}_{k=1}P^{(1)}_{jk}\mu_{k}}\penalty\ .

The data pooling consistency can be proved similarly. Specifically,
F_{\bm{\mu}^{(1)}}(P)=F_{\bm{\mu}^{(2)}}(P)
implies that for any
i,j
,

\displaystyle\frac{\sum^{K}_{k=1}P_{ik}\mu^{(1)}_{k}}{\sum^{K}_{k=1}P_{jk}\mu^{(1)}_{k}}=\frac{\sum^{K}_{k=1}P_{ik}\mu^{(2)}_{k}}{\sum^{K}_{k=1}P^{(2)}_{jk}\mu^{(2)}_{k}}\penalty\ ,

and therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\sum^{K}_{k=1}P_{ik}(\lambda\mu^{(1)}_{k}+(1-\lambda)\mu^{(2)}_{k})}{\sum^{K}_{k=1}P_{jk}(\lambda\mu^{(1)}_{k}+(1-\lambda)\mu^{(2)}_{k})}=\frac{\lambda\sum^{K}_{k=1}P_{ik}\mu^{(1)}_{k}+(1-\lambda)\sum^{K}_{k=1}P_{ik}\mu^{(2)}_{k}}{\lambda\sum^{K}_{k=1}P_{jk}\mu^{(1)}_{k}+(1-\lambda)\sum^{K}_{k=1}P_{jk}\mu^{(2)}_{k}}=\frac{\sum^{K}_{k=1}P_{ik}\mu^{(1)}_{k}}{\sum^{K}_{k=1}P_{jk}\mu^{(1)}_{k}}\penalty\ .

As for Pairwise monotonicity, as
P
and
P^{\prime}
differ only at
(i,j)
with
P_{ij}>P^{\prime}_{ij}
, then for any sampling distribution
\bm{\mu}
,

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{F_{\bm{\mu}}(P)_{i}}{F_{\bm{\mu}}(P)_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum^{K}_{k=1}P_{ik}\mu_{k}-\sum^{K}_{k=1}P_{jk}\mu_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k:k\neq j}P_{ik}\mu_{k}-\sum_{k:k\neq i}P_{jk}\mu_{k}+P_{ij}\mu_{j}-P_{ji}\mu_{i}

\displaystyle>\sum_{k:k\neq j}P^{\prime}_{ik}\mu_{k}-\sum_{k:k\neq i}P^{\prime}_{jk}\mu_{k}+P^{\prime}_{ij}\mu_{j}-P^{\prime}_{ji}\mu_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum^{K}_{k=1}P^{\prime}_{ik}\mu_{k}-\sum^{K}_{k=1}P^{\prime}_{jk}\mu_{k}

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{F_{\bm{\mu}}(P^{\prime})_{i}}{F_{\bm{\mu}}(P^{\prime})_{j}}\right),

and therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{F(P)_{i}}{F(P)_{j}}\ \geq\ \frac{F(P^{\prime})_{i}}{F(P^{\prime})_{j}}.

∎

since
\pi^{\star}_{i}=1/3

-a&0&a\\

Thus for any initialization

s_{t,i}-s_{t,i+1}=(P\bm{\pi}_{t})_{i}-(P\bm{\pi}_{t})_{i+1}\geq\delta_{i}\geq 0,

and
\delta_{1}>0
. Therefore the weights
w_{t,i}:=\exp(\beta s_{t,i})
satisfy
w_{t,1}>w_{t,1}\geq\cdots\geq w_{t,K}
.
When
\alpha=1
, the update is a monotone exponential tilt
\bm{\pi}_{t+1}\propto\bm{\pi}_{t}\odot\bm{w}_{t}
.
Standard majorization arguments for monotone tilting imply
\bm{\pi}_{t+1}
majorizes
\bm{\pi}_{t}
for all large
t
,
and since Shannon entropy is strictly Schur-concave,
H(\bm{\pi}_{t+1})<H(\bm{\pi}_{t})
unless
\bm{\pi}_{t}
is a vertex.
Finally,
\bm{\pi}_{t}\to\bm{e}_{1}
implies
H(\bm{\pi}_{t})\to 0
.

2.

\alpha<1
prevents exact collapse.
From (
34
) and the bound
|(P\bm{\pi}_{t})_{i}-(P\bm{\pi}_{t})_{i+1}|\leq 1
(since
P\in\mathcal{P}\in[0,1]^{K\times K}
),
unrolling this recursion we have

\displaystyle|r_{t+1,i}-r_{{\rm ref},i}|

\displaystyle=\left|\alpha(r_{t,i}-r_{{\rm ref},i})+\beta\lambda\big((P\bm{\pi}_{t})_{i}-(P\bm{\pi}_{t})_{i+1}\big)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha|r_{t,i}-r_{{\rm ref},i}|+\beta\lambda

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(\alpha|r_{t-1,i}-r_{{\rm ref},i}|+\beta\lambda)+\beta\lambda

\displaystyle\leq\cdots

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{t}|r_{1,i}-r_{{\rm ref},i}|+\beta\lambda(1+\alpha+\cdots+\alpha^{t-1}),

which implies

|r_{t,i}-r_{{\rm ref},i}|\leq\alpha^{t}|r_{0,i}-r_{{\rm ref},i}|+\beta\lambda/(1-\alpha)
.
Hence all adjacent log-ratios are uniformly bounded, which implies that all coordinates of
\bm{\pi}_{t}
remain bounded away from
0
,
so
\bm{\pi}_{t}
cannot converge to a vertex and
H(\bm{\pi}_{t})
is bounded below by a positive constant.

3.

Finally we prove
\varepsilon
-collapse for large
\beta\lambda/(1-\alpha)
. By Lemma
25
,
(P\bm{\pi}_{t})_{i}-(P\bm{\pi}_{t})_{i+1}\geq\delta_{i}
.
Substitute this into (
34
) to obtain the lower bound recursion

\displaystyle r_{t+1,i}\

\displaystyle\geq\ \alpha r_{t,i}+(1-\alpha)r_{{\rm ref},i}+\beta\lambda\delta_{i}

\displaystyle>\alpha r_{t,i}+(1-\alpha)r_{{\rm ref},i}+\beta\lambda\delta_{i}/2+\beta\lambda\delta_{i}/2

(41)

\displaystyle>\alpha^{2}r_{t-1,i}+(1+\alpha)((1-\alpha)r_{{\rm ref},i}+\beta\lambda\delta_{i}/2)+\beta\lambda\delta_{i}/2

\displaystyle>\cdots

\displaystyle>\alpha^{t}r_{1,i}+(1+\alpha+\cdots+\alpha^{t-1})((1-\alpha)r_{{\rm ref},i}+\beta\lambda\delta_{i}/2)+\beta\lambda\delta_{i}/2

(42)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\alpha^{t})\left(r_{{\rm ref},i}+\frac{\beta\lambda\delta_{i}}{2(1-\alpha)}\right)+\beta\lambda\delta_{i}/2.

(43)

Now choose a sufficiently large
T=T(r_{{\rm ref},i},\alpha,\beta,\lambda,\delta_{i})
such that
\beta\lambda\delta_{i}/2\geq\alpha^{t}\left(r_{{\rm ref},i}+\frac{\beta\lambda\delta_{i}}{2(1-\alpha)}\right)
, from (
43
) we have

r_{t+1,i}>r_{{\rm ref},i}+\frac{\beta\lambda\delta_{i}}{2(1-\alpha)}.

When
P
is SST,
\frac{\beta\lambda\delta_{i}}{2(1-\alpha)}
is strictly positive for all
i\in[k]
and therefore Lemma
24
implies
\pi_{t,1}\geq 1-\varepsilon
for all
t\geq T
, given
\varepsilon=\left(\exp\left(\frac{\beta\lambda\delta}{2(1-\alpha)}\right)-1\right)^{-1}
. When
P
is ST
+
,
\frac{\beta\lambda\delta_{i}}{2(1-\alpha)}
is strictly positive for
i=1
and nonnegative for
i\geq 2
, Lemma
24
gives
\varepsilon=(K-1)\exp\left(-\frac{\beta\lambda\delta}{2(1-\alpha)}\right)
.

Appendix D
Additional Discussion of DPO

In this section, we now present a parallel set of theoretical results for DPO. In contrast to IPO, DPO generally does not admit a closed-form expression for the optimal policy
\bm{\pi}^{\star}
; instead,
\bm{\pi}^{\star}
is characterized implicitly through a first-order optimality condition (FOC) given by the following
Proposition

27
. Such an implicit characterization makes extending the IPO analysis to DPO substantially more challenging. Nevertheless, in this section, we develop DPO analogues of the main paper IPO results wherever possible, noting that some statements must be presented in a weaker form due to additional technical obstacles.

D.1
DPO optima under general sampling distributions

Similar to Proposition
1
, which characterizes the optimizer of the IPO population loss, we have the existence and uniqueness result for the DPO solution as follows:

Proposition 27

(Existence and Uniqueness of the DPO Optimum)
.

Given preference matrix
P\in\mathcal{P}
, the DPO problem with sampling strategy
\bm{\mu}\in\Delta_{K}^{\circ}
and reference policy
\bm{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}}\in\Delta_{K}^{\circ}

is equivalent to solving the convex optimization problem

\min_{\bm{\pi}\in\Delta_{K}}\left[-\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq K}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\,P_{ij}\,\log\sigma\!\left(\beta^{-1}\log\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref},i}}-\beta^{-1}\log\frac{\pi_{j}}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref},j}}\right)\right],

(44)

which admits a unique solution
\bm{\pi}^{\star}\in\Delta_{K}
. Moreover,
let
\bm{\theta}^{\star}=\beta^{-1}(\log\bm{\pi}^{\star}-\log\bm{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}})
and let
Q
be the Bradley–Terry matrix induced by
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
, i.e.
Q_{ij}=\sigma(\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star})
, then the first-order optimality condition for its solution
\bm{\pi}^{\star}
is

\operatorname{Diag}(\bm{\mu})\,(P-Q)^{\top}\bm{\mu}=\bm{0}.

(45)

Proof.

Define the reparametrization

\theta_{i}\;=\;\beta^{-1}\Bigl(\log\pi_{i}-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref},i}\Bigr),\qquad i\in[K].

Then
\pi_{i}=\pi_{\mathrm{ref},i}e^{\beta\theta_{i}}
and the DPO objective becomes

L(\bm{\theta})\;=\;-\sum_{i,j=1}^{K}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\,P_{ij}\,\log\sigma(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}),

the simplex constraint

\sum_{i}\pi_{i}=1
is equivalent to the smooth equality constraint

\sum_{i=1}^{K}\pi_{\mathrm{ref},i}e^{\beta\theta_{i}}=1.

(46)

First of all, we show the convexity of
L(\bm{\theta})
. Let
\ell(x):=-\log\sigma(x)=\log(1+e^{-x})
which satisfies
\ell^{\prime\prime}(x)=\sigma(x)(1-\sigma(x))>0,\forall x\in\mathbb{R}

and is therefore strictly convex. The objective in (
44
) can be written as
L(\bm{\theta})=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{K}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}P_{ij}\,\ell(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})
, with the understanding that
\bm{\theta}
is defined up to an additive constant
(since
\bm{\pi}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{\pi}(\bm{\theta}+c\mathbf{1})
for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
). Thus optimizing over
\bm{\pi}\in\Delta_{K}
is equivalent to optimizing over a gauge-fixed affine subspace, e.g.
\{\bm{\theta}:\sum_{i}\theta_{i}=0\}
. Each term
\ell(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})
is the composition of a strictly convex function
with a linear map, hence is convex in
\bm{\theta}
.
Since
\mu_{i}\mu_{j}P_{ij}\geq 0
,
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
is a nonnegative weighted sum of convex functions
and is therefore convex.

In addition, we show strict convexity of
L
modulo the constant-shift invariance by computing its Hessian. Using
\nabla(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})=\bm{e}_{i}-\bm{e}_{j}
, we have

\nabla^{2}\ell(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})=\ell^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})\,(\bm{e}_{i}-\bm{e}_{j})(\bm{e}_{i}-\bm{e}_{j})^{\top}.

Hence

\nabla^{2}L(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{K}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}P_{ij}\,\ell^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})\,(\bm{e}_{i}-\bm{e}_{j})(\bm{e}_{i}-\bm{e}_{j})^{\top}.

(47)

Using the dueling identity
P_{ij}+P_{ji}=1
and the symmetry
\ell^{\prime\prime}(-x)=\ell^{\prime\prime}(x)
,
we may group terms
(i,j)
and
(j,i)
to obtain

\nabla^{2}L(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq K}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\,\ell^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})\,(\bm{e}_{i}-\bm{e}_{j})(\bm{e}_{i}-\bm{e}_{j})^{\top}.

(48)

For any
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
,

\bm{v}^{\top}\nabla^{2}L(\bm{\theta})\,\bm{v}=\sum_{i<j}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\,\ell^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})\,(v_{i}-v_{j})^{2}.

If
\bm{\mu}
has full support, then each coefficient

\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\,\ell^{\prime\prime}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})
is strictly positive. Therefore,

\bm{v}^{\top}\nabla^{2}L(\bm{\theta})\,\bm{v}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v_{i}=v_{j}\ \ \forall i,j,

i.e.
\bm{v}\in\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}
.
Thus the Hessian is positive semidefinite with nullspace exactly equal to the constant-shift direction, and
L(\bm{\theta})
is strictly convex on any gauge-fixed subspace
(e.g.
\sum_{i}\theta_{i}=0
or
\theta_{K}=0
). Hence the original OP (
44
) is strictly convex over the reparameterized space
\{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{K},\sum_{i\in[K]}\theta_{i}=0\}
,
with a unique optimizer
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
.

Finally, since the mapping

\bm{\theta}\;\longleftrightarrow\;\pi_{i}=\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref},i}e^{\beta\theta_{i}}}{\sum_{k}\pi_{\mathrm{ref},k}e^{\beta\theta_{k}}}

is a bijection between the gauge-fixed
\bm{\theta}
-space
\{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{K},\sum_{i\in[K]}\theta_{i}=0\}
and
\Delta_{K}^{\circ}
, we conclude that OP
44
admits a unique optimum
\bm{\pi}^{\star}\in\Delta_{K}
.

Now we derive the first-order condition for
\bm{\pi}^{\star}
. Note that solving OP (
44
) is equivalent to minimizing
L(\bm{\theta})
subject to
(
46
), and we let
\bm{\theta}^{\star}=\beta^{-1}(\log\bm{\pi}^{\star}-\log\bm{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}})

and define the Bradley–Terry matrix
Q
by

Q_{ij}\;=\;\sigma(\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star}),\qquad i,j\in[K].

For the first step, we derive the KKT condition, which reduces to
\nabla F(\bm{\theta}^{\star})=\bm{0}
. Consider the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta},\lambda)\;=\;L(\bm{\theta})+\lambda\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}\pi_{\mathrm{ref},i}e^{\beta\theta_{i}}-1\right).

At an optimal solution
(\bm{\theta}^{\star},\lambda^{\star})
, the KKT stationarity
conditions read

\nabla L(\bm{\theta}^{\star})+\lambda^{\star}\nabla\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}\pi_{\mathrm{ref},i}e^{\beta\theta_{i}}\right)\Big|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}^{\star}}=\bm{0}.

(49)

Since
L(\bm{\theta})
depends only on differences
\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}
, it is invariant
to common shifts:
L(\bm{\theta}+c\bm{1})=L(\bm{\theta})
for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Differentiating
with respect to
c
at
c=0
gives

\bm{1}^{\top}\nabla L(\bm{\theta})=0\qquad\text{for all }\bm{\theta}.

(50)

On the other hand,

\nabla\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K}\pi_{\mathrm{ref},i}e^{\beta\theta_{i}}\right)\Big|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}^{\star}}=\beta\,\bm{\pi}^{\star},

and
\bm{1}^{\top}(\beta\bm{\pi}^{\star})=\beta>0
.
Taking the inner product of (
49
) with
\bm{1}
and using
(
50
) yields
\lambda^{\star}\beta=0
, hence
\lambda^{\star}=0
and therefore

\nabla L(\bm{\theta}^{\star})=\bm{0}.

(51)

Next we compute
\nabla L(\bm{\theta})
and identify the matrix form. Recall that
\frac{d}{dx}\log\sigma(x)=\sigma(-x)
. For each
k\in[K]
,
only the terms with
i=k
or
j=k
depend on
\theta_{k}
, so

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_{k}}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mu_{k}\mu_{j}\,P_{kj}\,\sigma\!\bigl(-(\theta_{k}-\theta_{j})\bigr)+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mu_{i}\mu_{k}\,P_{ik}\,\sigma\!\bigl(-(\theta_{i}-\theta_{k})\bigr)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mu_{k}\mu_{j}\,P_{kj}\,\sigma(\theta_{j}-\theta_{k})+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mu_{i}\mu_{k}\,P_{ik}\,\sigma(\theta_{k}-\theta_{i}).

Evaluating at
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
and using
Q_{jk}=\sigma(\theta_{j}^{\star}-\theta_{k}^{\star})
and

Q_{ki}=\sigma(\theta_{k}^{\star}-\theta_{i}^{\star})
gives

\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_{k}}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})=-\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mu_{k}\mu_{j}\,P_{kj}\,Q_{jk}+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mu_{i}\mu_{k}\,P_{ik}\,Q_{ki}.

Now use the defining property of preference matrices
P\in\mathcal{P}
, namely

P_{ik}=1-P_{ki}
, together with the Bradley–Terry symmetry
Q_{ki}=1-Q_{ik}
.
Then, for each
i
,

P_{ik}Q_{ki}-P_{ki}Q_{ik}=(1-P_{ki})(1-Q_{ik})-P_{ki}Q_{ik}=1-P_{ki}-Q_{ik}=P_{ik}-Q_{ik}.

Hence

\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_{k}}(\bm{\theta}^{\star})=\mu_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mu_{i}\,(P_{ik}-Q_{ik}).

Stacking these
K
equalities gives the vector identity

\nabla L(\bm{\theta}^{\star})=\operatorname{Diag}(\bm{\mu})\,(P-Q)^{\top}\bm{\mu}.

Combining the above expression with (
51
) yields

\operatorname{Diag}(\bm{\mu})\,(P-Q)^{\top}\bm{\mu}=\bm{0},

which is exactly (
45
).
∎

D.2
The dependency of DPO solutions on sampling

To study how DPO solution depends on sampling strategy, we assume the reference policy in the DPO population loss
\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}=\bm{\mu}_{0}
be the uniform distribution on
\Delta_{K}
without loss of generality and let
\bm{\mu}
have full support. Then, the first-order condition (FOC) (
45
) given in
Proposition

27
can be further simplified as

F(\bm{\theta}^{\star},\bm{\mu}):=(P-Q(\bm{\theta}^{\star}))^{\top}\bm{\mu}=\bm{0}.

(52)

The new FOC (
52
) gives an implicit mapping from any sampling strategy
\bm{\mu}\in\Delta_{K}^{\circ}
to the unique DPO solution
\bm{\pi}^{\star}(P;\bm{\mu})
by specifying its logits
\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P;\bm{\mu})=\beta^{-1}\log\bm{\pi}^{\star}(P;\bm{\mu})
up to a constant shift. The following result shows that the unique solution is invariant to sampling when and only when the underlying preference structure
P
is derived from a BT model.

Proposition 28

(Sampling Invariance Occurs
Only
Under True Bradley–Terry Preferences)
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{P}
be an arbitrary preference matrix. The solution
\bm{\pi}^{\star}(P;\bm{\mu})
of OP (
44
) is independent of the sampling strategy
\bm{\mu}
(i.e.,
\bm{\pi}^{\star}(P;\bm{\mu})
is a constant over all
\bm{\mu}\in\Delta_{K}
with full support)
if and only if

P
is exactly representable as a Bradley–Terry model, i.e.,

P_{ij}=\sigma(r_{i}-r_{j})\quad\text{for some }r\in\mathbb{R}^{K}.

Proposition
28
highlights a crucial but underappreciated aspect of RLHF-style preference learning:

•

If the true pairwise preferences
do not
come from a BT model (which is almost always the case), then
DPO does not recover an intrinsic “ground truth” alignment policy
.
Instead, it recovers a policy that also depends on the sampling strategy. Such a message is self-evident for IPO due to the weighted square-sum format of its loss function, but is not explicitly pointed out for DPO to the best of our knowledge.

•

The only sampling-invariant situation is the perfectly idealized world where human preferences admit a single global utility function
r_{i}
, and where
P_{ij}
depends solely on
r_{i}-r_{j}
.
This assumption is extremely restrictive: real-world preference judgments violate transitivity, context independence, and independence of irrelevant alternatives—all incompatible with the BT model.

Thus, the sampling policy is not merely a data-collection choice: it acts as an implicit regularizer that selects
which
among many possible BT approximations the DPO objective converges to. This has an important implication:

Even in the infinite-data limit, DPO learns a policy determined jointly by the preference structure
P
and the sampling distribution
\bm{\mu}
.

In other words, “more data” does not wash out the effect of sampling; rather, the sampling strategy systematically biases the learned policy.

Proof.

We prove the “if” and “only if” directions of the claim separately.

(If).
Suppose
P
is BT-consistent, i.e.,
\exists\bar{\bm{\theta}}
such that
P_{ij}=\sigma(\bar{\theta}_{i}-\bar{\theta}_{j}),\forall i\neq j
. Then for any
\bm{\mu}
, the FOC (
52
) gives

F(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})=\sum_{i<j}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}(\bm{e}_{i}-\bm{e}_{j})\left(P_{ij}-\sigma(\bar{\theta}_{i}-\bar{\theta}_{j})\right)=\sum_{i<j}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}(\bm{e}_{i}-\bm{e}_{j})\left(\sigma(\bar{\theta}_{i}-\bar{\theta}_{j})-\sigma(\bar{\theta}_{i}-\bar{\theta}_{j})\right)=0.

(53)

And it is obvious that
\bm{\theta}=\bar{\bm{\theta}}
is a stationary point of
F(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})=0
. By strict convexity of the OP (
44
) on
\Delta_{K}
, the stationary point of
F(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})=0
is unique and corresponds to the global minimizer. Therefore, the if direction holds.

(Only If).
Conversely, assume there exists a
\bm{\pi}^{\star}
such that
\bm{\pi}^{\star}(P;\bm{\mu})=\bm{\pi}^{\star}
for every
\bm{\mu}
. Let
\bar{\bm{\theta}}
be any logit of
\bm{\pi}^{\star}
, then
F(\bar{\bm{\theta}},\bm{\mu})=0
holds for all
\bm{\mu}
. Pick any pair
i\neq j
and construct a distribution
\bm{\mu}=\bm{\mu}_{\varepsilon}^{(i,j)}
with probability mass concentrating on
i,j
, i.e.,
\mu_{i}=\mu_{j}=\frac{1}{2}(1-\varepsilon),\mu_{k}=\frac{\varepsilon}{K-2},\forall k\neq i,j
, and let
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. The continuity of
F
gives

\bm{0}=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}F(\bar{\bm{\theta}},\bm{\mu}_{\varepsilon}^{(i,j)})=\frac{1}{4}(P_{ij}-\sigma(\bar{\theta}_{i}-\bar{\theta}_{j}))(\bm{e}_{i}-\bm{e}_{j}).

(54)

Therefore,
P_{ij}=\sigma(\bar{\theta}_{i}-\bar{\theta}_{j})
must hold for pair
(i,j)
. Since
(i,j)
was arbitrarily chosen,
P
must be BT-consistent with
\bm{\pi}^{\star}
.

∎

D.3
How offline sampling influences the concentration of DPO solutions

In this section, we present a result in parallel to
Theorem

10
that shows the same message: a more skewed sampling distribution
\bm{\mu}^{\prime}
that are perturbed in the direction aligning with the original order of
\bm{\pi}
results in more concentrated learned policy.

Proposition 29

(Effect of Sampling on Pairwise Gaps of DPO Solution)
.

For any fixed
P\in\mathcal{P}
, let
\bm{\pi}(\bm{\mu})
be the solution of OP (
44
) under sampling distribution
\bm{\mu}\in\Delta_{k}^{\circ}
, and, without loss of generality, assume that the corresponding logits satisfy
\frac{\bm{\pi}(\bm{\mu})}{\bm{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}}}\;\text{ is in descending order, i.e., }\;\theta_{1}>\theta_{2}>\cdots>\theta_{K}
, where
\bm{\theta}(\bm{\mu})=\beta^{-1}(\log\bm{\pi}(\bm{\mu})-\log\bm{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}})
.
Consider a perturbed sampling strategy of the following form

\bm{\mu}^{\prime}=\bm{\mu}+\delta\bm{\mu}_{0}

for some small
\delta>0
. Let
\bm{\pi}^{\prime}=\bm{\pi}(\bm{\mu}^{\prime})
be the solution of OP (
44
) under
\bm{\mu}^{\prime}
and
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
be the corresponding logit. Then we have

1.

If the perturbation direction is
\bm{\mu}_{0}=1/\bm{\mu}
(elementwise), it holds that

\sum_{i<j}\frac{P_{ji}}{\mu_{i}\mu_{j}}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})>\sum_{i<j}\frac{P_{ji}}{\mu_{i}\mu_{j}}(\theta^{\prime}_{i}-\theta^{\prime}_{j}).

(55)

2.

If the perturbation direction is
\bm{\mu}_{0}=\bm{e}_{K}
, it holds that

\sum_{i\neq K}\frac{P_{Ki}}{\mu_{i}}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{K})>\sum_{i\neq K}\frac{P_{Ki}}{\mu_{i}}(\theta^{\prime}_{i}-\theta^{\prime}_{K}).

(56)

Remarks.

The quantities in (
55
) and (
56
) can be interpreted as
weighted averages of pairwise logit gaps
under the learned policy. (
55
) aggregates pairwise gaps across all response pairs, while (
56
) focuses on gaps between the lowest-scored response and the rest. Larger values of these metrics correspond to more
concentrated
policies where probability mass is more skewed toward a subset of high-scoring responses.

Proposition
29
shows that when the sampling distribution is perturbed

•

toward a more uniform distribution (via
\bm{\mu}_{0}=1/\bm{\mu}
), or

•

toward increased exploration of the currently worst-scored response (via
\bm{\mu}_{0}=\bm{e}_{K}
),

the corresponding weighted pairwise logit gaps must decrease.
In other words, these perturbations make the DPO solution less extreme, redistributing probability mass more evenly across responses and thereby increasing policy diversity.

Compared to the sharp, pairwise-specific characterizations obtained for IPO in Theorem
10
and Corollary
3.2
, our counterpart result on DPO only characterizes directional changes in aggregated gap metrics under small local sampling perturbations, rather than providing a global or itemwise guarantee. We note that this gap reflects intrinsic technical difficulties in the DPO analysis rather than a fundamental difference in behavior. Indeed, despite the weaker theoretical form, Proposition
29
conveys the same qualitative message:
skewed sampling amplifies policy concentration, while more uniform or exploratory sampling mitigates it
. We further demonstrate in our experiments that enlarged pairwise logit gaps under skewed sampling arise for DPO across a much broader range of regimes than those covered by the theory.

For the rest of this section, we present a full version of
Proposition

29
in the following Theorem, explicitly characterizing how the weighted pairwise logit gaps depend on the environment parameters and provide its proof.

Theorem 30

(Effect of Sampling on Pairwise Gaps of DPO Solution)
.

For any fixed
P\in\mathcal{P}
, let
\bm{\pi}(\bm{\mu})
be the solution of OP (
44
) under sampling distribution
\bm{\mu}\in\Delta_{k}^{\circ}
, and, without loss of generality, assume that the corresponding logits satisfy
\frac{\bm{\pi}(\bm{\mu})}{\bm{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}}}\;\text{ is in descending order, i.e., }\;\theta_{1}>\theta_{2}>\cdots>\theta_{K}
, where
\bm{\theta}(\bm{\mu})=\beta^{-1}(\log\bm{\pi}(\bm{\mu})-\log\bm{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}})
.
Consider a perturbed sampling strategy of the following form

\bm{\mu}^{\prime}=\bm{\mu}+\delta\bm{\mu}_{0}

for some small
\delta>0
. Let
\bm{\pi}^{\prime}=\bm{\pi}(\bm{\mu}^{\prime})
be the solution of OP (
44
) under
\bm{\mu}^{\prime}
and
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
be the corresponding logit. Then we have

1.

If the perturbation direction is
\bm{\mu}_{0}=1/\bm{\mu}
(elementwise), then the functional

G^{(1)}(\bm{\pi};\bm{\mu})\;=\;\sum_{i<j}\frac{P_{ji}}{\mu_{i}\mu_{j}}\log\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{j}}

(57)

strictly decreases when we move from
\bm{\pi}
to
\bm{\pi}^{\prime}
. Specifically, we have

G^{(1)}(\bm{\pi};\bm{\mu})-G^{(1)}(\bm{\pi}^{\prime};\bm{\mu})>\beta\Delta_{1}(K-1)^{1.5}\delta-O\left(\beta\sum_{i<j}e^{{}^{-(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})}}\right),

where
\Delta_{1}=\min_{1\leq i\leq K-1}\left(\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{P_{ij}-\sigma(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})}{\mu_{j}}\right)^{2}
is a strictly positive constant.

2.

If the perturbation direction is
\bm{\mu}_{0}=\bm{e}_{K}
(only the
K
-th coordinate increases), then the functional

G^{(2)}(\bm{\pi};\bm{\mu})\;=\;\sum_{i\neq K}\frac{P_{Ki}}{\mu_{i}}\log\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{K}}

(58)

strictly decreases, i.e.,

G^{(2)}(\bm{\pi};\bm{\mu})-G^{(2)}(\bm{\pi}^{\prime};\bm{\mu})>\beta\Delta_{2}(K-1)^{1.5}\delta-O\left(\beta\sum_{i<j}e^{{}^{-(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})}}\right),

where

(59)

The implicit mapping determined by (

\frac{\partial\tilde{\bm{\theta}}}{\partial\bm{\mu}}=-\left(\frac{\partial\tilde{F}}{\partial\tilde{\bm{\theta}}}\right)^{-1}\cdot\frac{\partial\tilde{F}}{\partial\bm{\mu}}=\tilde{M}^{-1}(P-Q(\bm{\theta}))_{1:(K-1),1:K}

Lemma 31

(Spectral bound for the Fisher–Laplacian)

For any full–support

Proof of Lemma

By construction,

\tilde{\Sigma}_{ii}=\sum_{j\neq i}\mu_{j}\,\sigma_{ij}(1-\sigma_{ij})\;\leq\;\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j\neq i}\mu_{j}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{4},

(65)

For the off–Diagonal entries,

\lambda_{\max}(\tilde{\Sigma})\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq K-1}\Bigl(\tilde{\Sigma}_{ii}+\sum_{j\neq i}|\tilde{\Sigma}_{ij}|\Bigr)\leq\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j\neq i}\sqrt{\mu_{i}\mu_{j}}.

\sum_{j\neq i}\sqrt{\mu_{i}\mu_{j}}\leq\sqrt{K-1}\sqrt{\mu_{i}(\sum_{j\neq i}\mu_{j})}=\sqrt{K-1}\sqrt{\mu_{i}(1-\mu_{i})}\;\leq\;\frac{\sqrt{K-1}}{2}.

(68)

(69)

Definition 32

The

(80)

Q(\bm{\theta})
is the BT matrix induced by
\bm{\theta}
,
\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}^{(t)}
and
\bm{\mu}_{t}
are defined in (
5
), (
6

controls how strongly the

\lambda\in[0,1]

Our next

Proposition 33

(Cycling regime for MRS–DPO under a Condorcet cycle)
.

Let
K=3
and let
P=P^{\mathrm{RPS}}_{a}\in\cal P
be the rock–paper–scissors
(Condorcet cycle) preference matrix

P^{\mathrm{RPS}}_{a}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}+a&\frac{1}{2}-a\\[2.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}-a&\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}+a\\[2.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}+a&\frac{1}{2}-a&\frac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad a\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2}).

Assume
\bm{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}}=\bm{\pi}_{0}=\bm{\mu}_{0}\triangleq(\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{3})\in\Delta_{3}^{\circ}
and

\log\bm{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(t)}=(1-\alpha)\log\bm{\mu}_{0}+\alpha\log\bm{\pi}_{t},\qquad\bm{\mu}_{t}=(1-\lambda)\bm{\mu}_{0}+\lambda\bm{\pi}_{t}.

Consider the MRS–DPO map defined in Definition
32
:

\bm{\pi}_{t+1}=\mathrm{softmax}\!\Big(\log\bm{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(t)}+\beta\,\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu}_{t})\Big),\qquad\beta>0.

Then
\bm{\mu}_{0}
is a fixed point. Moreover, if

\alpha^{2}+\frac{16a^{2}}{3}\,\beta^{2}\lambda^{2}\;\geq\;1,

(81)

then
\bm{\mu}_{0}
is not locally attracting and the iterates exhibit persistent oscillations
(rotations) around
\bm{\mu}_{0}
; consequently the MRS–DPO last iterate does not converge to
\bm{\mu}_{0}

for generic interior initializations.

Proof.

We first shown
\bm{\mu}_{0}
is a fixed point. Let
\bm{\mu}=\bm{\mu}_{0}
and
\bm{\theta}=\bm{0}
. Then
Q(\bm{0})_{ij}=\sigma(0)=\frac{1}{2}
for all
i,j
.
Since
P^{\mathrm{RPS}}_{a}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}
is skew-symmetric with zero column sums,
we have
(P^{\mathrm{RPS}}_{a}-Q(\bm{0}))^{\top}\bm{\mu}_{0}=\bm{0}
. Hence
\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu}_{0})
may be chosen
as
\bm{0}
. With
\bm{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(t)}=\bm{\mu}_{0}
at
\bm{\pi}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{0}
, we obtain

\bm{\pi}_{t+1}=\mathrm{softmax}(\log\bm{\mu}_{0}+\beta\bm{0})=\bm{\mu}_{0},

so
\bm{\mu}_{0}
is a fixed point.

Next we derive the linearization of
\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu})
at
\bm{\mu}=\bm{\mu}_{0}
.
Define
F(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})\triangleq(P-Q(\bm{\theta}))^{\top}\bm{\mu}
. At
(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})=(\bm{0},\bm{\mu}_{0})
we have

F(\bm{0},\bm{\mu}_{0})=\bm{0}
as above. We compute the partial derivatives at
(\bm{0},\bm{\mu}_{0})
.

First,
F
is linear in
\bm{\mu}
, so

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\bm{\mu}}(\bm{0},\bm{\mu}_{0})=(P-Q(\bm{0}))^{\top}=(P-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top})^{\top}.

Second, for
Q_{ij}(\bm{\theta})=\sigma(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})
we have

\partial Q_{ij}/\partial\theta_{k}=\sigma^{\prime}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})(\delta_{ik}-\delta_{jk})
and

\sigma^{\prime}(0)=\tfrac{1}{4}
. Writing
F_{m}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})=\sum_{i}(P_{im}-Q_{im}(\bm{\theta}))\mu_{i}
,

\frac{\partial F_{m}}{\partial\theta_{k}}(\bm{0},\bm{\mu}_{0})=-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mu_{i}\frac{\partial Q_{im}}{\partial\theta_{k}}(\bm{0})=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mu_{i}(\delta_{ik}-\delta_{mk})=-\frac{1}{4}(\mu_{k}-\delta_{mk}).

With
\mu_{0,k}=\tfrac{1}{3}
, this yields the
3\times 3
matrix

\frac{\partial F}{\partial\bm{\theta}}(\bm{0},\bm{\mu}_{0})=\frac{1}{6}I-\frac{1}{12}(\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}-I),

whose restriction to the tangent space
T
is simply
\frac{1}{4}I_{T}
(because
\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}
vanishes on
T
).
Therefore, by the implicit function theorem (modulo the standard shift-invariance gauge, which is immaterial because
softmax is shift-invariant),

D_{\bm{\mu}}\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu}_{0})\big|_{T}=-\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\bm{\theta}}(\bm{0},\bm{\mu}_{0})\big|_{T}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\bm{\mu}}(\bm{0},\bm{\mu}_{0})\big|_{T}\right)=-4\,(P-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top})^{\top}\big|_{T}.

For
P\in\cal P
, the matrix
S\triangleq P-\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}
is skew-symmetric, hence
S^{\top}=-S
, and thus

D_{\bm{\mu}}\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu}_{0})\big|_{T}=4\,S\big|_{T}.

(82)

Finally we linearize the full MRS–DPO map at
\bm{\mu}_{0}
. Let
\bm{\pi}=\bm{\mu}_{0}+\bm{\delta}
with
\delta\in T
small. Then

\log\bm{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(t)}=(1-\alpha)\log\bm{\mu}_{0}+\alpha\log(\bm{\mu}_{0}+\bm{\delta}),\qquad\bm{\mu}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{0}+\lambda\delta

Using elementwise Taylor expansion,
\log(\bm{\mu}_{0}+\bm{\delta})=\log\bm{\mu}_{0}+3\bm{\delta}+o(\|\bm{\delta}\|)

(since each coordinate of
\bm{\mu}_{0}
equals
1/3
).
Using (
82
),
\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu}_{t})=4\lambda S\bm{\delta}+o(\|\bm{\delta}\|)
.
Hence the softmax argument is

\log\bm{\pi}_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(t)}+\beta\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu}_{t})=\log\bm{\mu}_{0}+\Bigl(3\alpha I+4\beta\lambda S\Bigr)\bm{\delta}+o(\|\bm{\delta}\|).

The Jacobian of
\mathrm{softmax}
at
\log\bm{\mu}_{0}
is
\mathrm{Diag}(\bm{\mu}_{0})-\bm{\mu}_{0}\bm{\mu}_{0}^{\top}
; restricted to
T
it equals
\frac{1}{3}I_{T}
.
Therefore,

\bm{\delta}^{+}\;\triangleq\;\bm{\pi}_{t+1}-\bm{\mu}_{0}=\frac{1}{3}\Bigl(3\alpha I+4\beta\lambda S\Bigr)\bm{\delta}+o(\|\bm{\delta}\|)=\Bigl(\alpha I+\frac{4\beta\lambda}{3}S\Bigr)\bm{\delta}+o(\|\bm{\delta}\|).

For the RPS matrix,
S=P-\frac{1}{2}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}
equals
aA
where

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&-1\\
-1&0&1\\
1&-1&0\end{pmatrix}

has eigenvalues
0,\pm i\sqrt{3}
. Thus on
T
the eigenvalues of the linearized map are

\lambda_{\pm}=\alpha\pm i\,\frac{4\sqrt{3}}{3}\,\beta\lambda\,a.

Their modulus is

|\lambda_{\pm}|=\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\frac{16}{3}\beta^{2}\lambda^{2}a^{2}}.

If (
81
) holds, then
|\lambda_{\pm}|\geq 1
and the fixed point
\bm{\mu}_{0}

is not locally attracting; since
\Im(\lambda_{\pm})\neq 0
whenever
a\beta\lambda>0
,
the iterates rotate (oscillate) rather than approach
\bm{\mu}_{0}
, yielding persistent cycling behavior.
Finally, if
\alpha=1
and
\lambda>0
, then
|\lambda_{\pm}|>1
for every
\beta>0
, so the regime holds.
∎

Theorem 34

(A sufficient stability regime for MRS–DPO)
.

Assume
P\in\mathcal{P}
and fix
\alpha\in[0,1)
and
\lambda\in[0,1)

\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref},\bm{\pi}_{0}\in\Delta_{K}^{\circ}

\bm{\mu}(\bm{\pi})\;:=\;(1-\lambda)\bm{\pi}_{0}+\lambda\bm{\pi},\qquad\underline{\mu}\;:=\;(1-\lambda)\min_{i}\pi_{0,i}\;>\;0.

For each
\bm{\mu}\in\Delta_{K}^{\circ}
, let
\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
denote the (gauge-fixed) DPO optimizer in the

\theta
-parameterization, i.e.
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{\theta}^{\star}=0
.

Assume the DPO optimizer is uniformly bounded on the sampling set induced by
\lambda
:

B\;:=\;\sup_{\bm{\pi}\in\Delta_{K}^{\circ}}\ \Big(\max_{i}\theta_{i}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu}(\bm{\pi}))-\min_{i}\theta_{i}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu}(\bm{\pi}))\Big)\;<\;\infty,

(83)

and let
s_{B}:=\sigma^{\prime}(B)>0
where
\sigma(t)=1/(1+e^{-t})

Consider the MRS–DPO update defined by

\bm{\pi}_{t+1}=\mathrm{softmax}\!\Big((1-\alpha)\log\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}+\alpha\log\bm{\pi}_{t}+\beta\,\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu}(\bm{\pi}_{t}))\Big),\qquad t\geq 1,

(84)

with any initialization
\bm{\pi}_{1}\in\Delta_{K}^{\circ}
and
\beta>0
. Let
\Pi:=I-\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}
and define the centered logit coordinate

\bm{x}(\bm{\pi}):=\Pi\log\bm{\pi}\in\mathbf{1}^{\perp}.

If

L\;:=\;\alpha\;+\;\frac{\beta\lambda}{\underline{\mu}^{2}\,s_{B}}\;<\;1,

(85)

then the MRS–DPO dynamics is globally stable in centered-logit distance:
there exists a unique fixed point
\bm{\pi}^{\infty}\in\Delta_{K}^{\circ}
of (
84
), and for all
t\geq 1
,

\bigl\|\bm{x}(\bm{\pi}_{t})-\bm{x}(\bm{\pi}^{\infty})\bigr\|_{2}\;\leq\;L^{\,t-1}\,\bigl\|\bm{x}(\bm{\pi}_{1})-\bm{x}(\bm{\pi}^{\infty})\bigr\|_{2}.

Consequently,
\bm{\pi}_{t}\to\bm{\pi}^{\infty}
as
t\to\infty
(and in particular the dynamics cannot cycle).

Proof.

First we rewrite the update as a map on centered logits. Let
\bm{z}_{t}:=(1-\alpha)\log\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}+\alpha\log\bm{\pi}_{t}+\beta\,\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu}(\bm{\pi}_{t}))
so that

\bm{\pi}_{t+1}=\mathrm{softmax}(\bm{z}_{t})
.
Using
\log\mathrm{softmax}(\bm{z})=\bm{z}-\log(\sum_{i}e^{z_{i}})\mathbf{1}
and
\Pi\mathbf{1}=0
, we obtain the exact identity

\bm{x}(\bm{\pi}_{t+1})=\Pi\log\bm{\pi}_{t+1}=\Pi\bm{z}_{t}.

Moreover, since
\bm{x}(\bm{\pi}_{t})=\Pi\log\bm{\pi}_{t}
, we have
\Pi(\alpha\log\bm{\pi}_{t})=\alpha\bm{x}(\bm{\pi}_{t})
, and therefore

\bm{x}(\bm{\pi}_{t+1})=\alpha\,\bm{x}(\bm{\pi}_{t})+(1-\alpha)\Pi\log\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}+\beta\,\Pi\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu}(\bm{\pi}_{t})).

(86)

Because we fix the gauge
\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{\theta}^{\star}=0
, we have
\Pi\bm{\theta}^{\star}=\bm{\theta}^{\star}
and we may drop
\Pi
on that term.

Define the induced map on
\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
:

F(\bm{x}):=\alpha\bm{x}+(1-\alpha)\Pi\log\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}+\beta\,\bm{\theta}^{\star}\!\big(P,\bm{\mu}(\mathrm{softmax}(x))\big),

so that
\bm{x}(\bm{\pi}_{t+1})=F(\bm{x}(\bm{\pi}_{t}))
.

For
\bm{\pi}=\mathrm{softmax}(\bm{x})
, the Jacobian is
J(\bm{\pi})=\mathrm{Diag}(\bm{\pi})-\bm{\pi}\bm{\pi}^{\top}
.
For any
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
,

\bm{v}^{\top}J(\bm{\pi})\bm{v}=\sum_{i}\pi_{i}v_{i}^{2}-\Big(\sum_{i}\pi_{i}v_{i}\Big)^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(v_{I})\leq\frac{(\max_{i}v_{i}-\min_{i}v_{i})^{2}}{4}\leq\frac{2\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}}{4}=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2},

where
I\sim\bm{\pi}
and we used
\max_{i}v_{i}-\min_{i}v_{i}\leq\sqrt{2}\|\bm{v}\|_{2}
.
Thus
\|J(\bm{\pi})\|_{2\to 2}\leq 1/2
uniformly, and by the mean value theorem,

\|\mathrm{softmax}(\bm{x})-\mathrm{softmax}(\bm{y})\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}.

(87)

Now we derive the Lipschitz continuity of the DPO optimizer map
\bm{\mu}\mapsto\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu})
. Fix
\bm{\mu}\in\Delta_{K}^{\circ}
and let
Q(\bm{\theta})
be the Bradley–Terry matrix induced by
\bm{\theta}
,

Q_{ij}(\bm{\theta})=\sigma(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})
.
Under the dueling identity, the DPO population objective in
\bm{\theta}
has gradient

g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu}):=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta};\bm{\mu})=\mathrm{Diag}(\bm{\mu})\,(Q(\bm{\theta})-P)\,\bm{\mu},

and Hessian on
\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
given by the Laplacian form

H(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq K}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\,\sigma^{\prime}(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})\,(\bm{e}_{i}-\bm{e}_{j})(\bm{e}_{i}-\bm{e}_{j})^{\top}.

By the bounded-oscillation assumption (
83
), every difference
\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star}
lies in
[-B,B]
,
so
\sigma^{\prime}(\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star})\geq s_{B}
.
Also, for
\bm{\mu}=\bm{\mu}(\bm{\pi})
we have
\mu_{i}\geq\underline{\mu}
.
Hence for any
\bm{v}\in\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
,

\bm{v}^{\top}H(\bm{\theta}^{\star},\bm{\mu})\bm{v}\geq\underline{\mu}^{2}s_{B}\sum_{i<j}(v_{i}-v_{j})^{2}=\underline{\mu}^{2}s_{B}\,K\,\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2},

where we used
\sum_{i<j}(v_{i}-v_{j})^{2}=K\|\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}
on
\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
.
Therefore
g(\cdot,\bm{\mu})
is strongly monotone on
\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
with modulus

m:=\underline{\mu}^{2}s_{B}K.

(88)

Next, fix
\bm{\theta}
and write
M:=Q(\bm{\theta})-P
.
Since
M_{ij}\in[-1,1]
, we have
\|M\|_{2}\leq\|M\|_{F}\leq K
.
For any
\bm{\mu},\bm{\nu}\in\Delta_{K}
,

g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})-g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\nu})=\mathrm{Diag}(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\nu})M\bm{\mu}+\mathrm{Diag}(\bm{\nu})M(\bm{\mu}-\bm{\nu}),

and thus

\|g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\mu})-g(\bm{\theta},\bm{\nu})\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\nu}\|_{2}\,\|M\bm{\mu}\|_{2}+\|M\|_{2}\,\|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\nu}\|_{2}\leq K\|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\nu}\|_{2}+K\|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\nu}\|_{2}=2K\|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\nu}\|_{2}.

Now let
\bm{\theta}_{\mu}^{\star}:=\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu})
and
\bm{\theta}_{\nu}^{\star}:=\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\nu})
(both in
\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
),
so
g(\bm{\theta}_{\mu}^{\star},\bm{\mu})=0
and
g(\bm{\theta}_{\nu}^{\star},\bm{\nu})=0
.
By strong monotonicity (
88
),

m\|\bm{\theta}_{\mu}^{\star}-\bm{\theta}_{\nu}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq\|g(\bm{\theta}_{\mu}^{\star},\bm{\mu})-g(\bm{\theta}_{\nu}^{\star},\bm{\mu})\|_{2}=\|g(\bm{\theta}_{\nu}^{\star},\bm{\nu})-g(\bm{\theta}_{\nu}^{\star},\bm{\mu})\|_{2}\leq 2K\|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\nu}\|_{2},

and hence

\|\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu})-\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\nu})\|_{2}\leq\frac{2K}{m}\|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\nu}\|_{2}=\frac{2}{\underline{\mu}^{2}s_{B}}\,\|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\nu}\|_{2}.

(89)

Finally, we derive contraction of the centered-logit map. Let
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
and set
\bm{\pi}=\mathrm{softmax}(\bm{x})
,
\bm{\pi}^{\prime}=\mathrm{softmax}(\bm{y})
.
Since
\bm{\mu}(\bm{\pi})=(1-\lambda)\bm{\pi}_{0}+\lambda\bm{\pi}
, we have

\|\bm{\mu}(\bm{\pi})-\bm{\mu}(\bm{\pi}^{\prime})\|_{2}=\lambda\|\bm{\pi}-\bm{\pi}^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}

by (
87
).
Combining with (
89
) gives

\|\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu}(\bm{\pi}))-\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu}(\bm{\pi}^{\prime}))\|_{2}\leq\frac{2}{\underline{\mu}^{2}s_{B}}\cdot\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}=\frac{\lambda}{\underline{\mu}^{2}s_{B}}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}.

Therefore,

\|F(\bm{x})-F(\bm{y})\|_{2}\leq\alpha\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}+\beta\cdot\frac{\lambda}{\underline{\mu}^{2}s_{B}}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}=\Big(\alpha+\frac{\beta\lambda}{\underline{\mu}^{2}s_{B}}\Big)\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}=L\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}.

Under the condition (
85
),
L<1
, so
F
is a contraction on the complete space

(\mathbf{1}^{\perp},\|\cdot\|_{2})
.
By Banach’s fixed point theorem,
F
admits a unique fixed point
\bm{x}^{\infty}
and
\bm{x}(\bm{\pi}_{t})\to\bm{x}^{\infty}
geometrically.
Let
\bm{\pi}^{\infty}:=\mathrm{softmax}(\bm{x}^{\infty})
; then
\bm{\pi}^{\infty}
is the unique fixed point of (
84
) and

\bm{\pi}_{t}\to\bm{\pi}^{\infty}
.
∎

Finally, we present the long-term characterization of MRS-DPO dynamics under SST preference structures in the following Theorem, which corresponds to
Theorem

14
and reveals a similar concern of entropy collapse.

Theorem 35

(Entropy/peak-collapse of MRS–DPO under SST)
.

Assume that
P\in\cal P
is SST. Let
\bm{\pi}_{0}\in\Delta_{K}^{\circ}
and
\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}\in\Delta_{K}^{\circ}
. Consider the MRS–DPO dynamics
of Definition
32
,

\bm{\pi}_{t+1}=\mathrm{softmax}\!\Big(\log\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}^{(t)}+\beta\,\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu}_{t})\Big),\qquad t\geq 1,

where
\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu})
is defined implicitly by the DPO FOC

F(\bm{\theta}^{\star},\bm{\mu})=(P-Q(\bm{\theta}^{\star}))^{\top}\bm{\mu}=\bm{0}
, and
Q(\bm{\theta})
is the BT matrix induced by
\bm{\theta}
.

Define the (SST) gap constants

\underline{\delta}\;\coloneqq\;\min_{i\in[K-1]}\ \min_{j\in[K]}\bigl(P_{ij}-P_{i+1,j}\bigr)\;>\;0,\qquad\delta\;\coloneqq\;\min_{i\in[K-1]}\Big\{(1-\lambda)\sum_{j=1}^{K}\pi_{0,j}\,(P_{ij}-P_{i+1,j})\Big\}\;>\;0,

(90)

(where
\delta>0
follows from
\bm{\pi}_{0}\in\Delta_{K}^{\circ}
and SST).

Then the MRS–DPO dynamics exhibits the following two forms of collapse.

•

(
\varepsilon
-collapse for
\alpha,\lambda\in[0,1)
when
\beta/(1-\alpha)
is large).

Let

\kappa\;\coloneqq\;\min_{i\in[K-1]}\log\frac{\pi_{{\rm ref},i}}{\pi_{{\rm ref},i+1}}\quad(\text{finite since }\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}\in\Delta_{K}^{\circ}),\quad c\;\coloneqq\;\frac{1}{2}\Big(\kappa+\frac{4\beta\delta}{1-\alpha}\Big),\quad\varepsilon\;\coloneqq\;\bigl(e^{c}-1\bigr)^{-1}.

If
\alpha,\lambda\in[0,1)
and
c>0
(in particular, if
\beta\delta/(1-\alpha)
is sufficiently large),
then there exists a finite
T
such that for all
t\geq T
,

\bm{\pi}_{t,1}\ \geq\ 1-\varepsilon.

(91)

In particular, if
\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}
is
aligned
with the SST order (i.e.
\pi_{{\rm ref},1}\geq\cdots\geq\pi_{{\rm ref},K}
, so that
\kappa\geq 0
),
then one may take
c=\frac{2\beta\delta}{1-\alpha}
and hence

\varepsilon=(\exp(\frac{2\beta\delta}{1-\alpha})-1)^{-1}
.

•

(True entropy collapse when
\alpha=1
).

If
\alpha=1
, then
\bm{\pi}_{t}\to\bm{e}_{1}
and
H(\bm{\pi}_{t})\to 0
as
t\to+\infty
.
Moreover, there exists a finite
T
such that for all
t\geq T
,

H(\bm{\pi}_{t+1})<H(\bm{\pi}_{t}),

(92)

where
H(\bm{\pi})\coloneqq-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\pi_{i}\log\pi_{i}
is the Shannon entropy.

Proof.

Throughout the proof, fix
t
and abbreviate

\bm{\mu}\coloneqq\bm{\mu}_{t},\qquad\bm{\theta}^{\star}\coloneqq\bm{\theta}^{\star}(P,\bm{\mu}),\qquad Q\coloneqq Q(\bm{\theta}^{\star}),\qquad\bm{\pi}^{+}\coloneqq\bm{\pi}_{t+1},\qquad\bm{\pi}\coloneqq\bm{\pi}_{t},\qquad\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}\coloneqq\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}^{(t)}.

First we utilize the row-form of the DPO FOC and derive a gap lower bound for
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
. Since
P,Q\in\cal P
we have
(P-Q)^{\top}=-(P-Q)
(skew-symmetry), and the FOC

(P-Q)^{\top}\bm{\mu}=\bm{0}
is equivalent to

(P-Q)\bm{\mu}=\bm{0}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mu_{j}\bigl(P_{ij}-Q_{ij}\bigr)=0,\ \ \forall i\in[K].

(93)

Thus for each
i
,

\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mu_{j}P_{ij}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mu_{j}Q_{ij}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mu_{j}\,\sigma(\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star}).

(94)

Fix any
i\in[K-1]
and subtract (
94
) for
i
and
i+1
:

\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mu_{j}\bigl(P_{ij}-P_{i+1,j}\bigr)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mu_{j}\Big(\sigma(\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star})-\sigma(\theta_{i+1}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star})\Big).

(95)

Because
\sigma
is globally
1/4
-Lipschitz (since
\sup_{x}\sigma^{\prime}(x)=1/4
), for each
j
,

\sigma(\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star})-\sigma(\theta_{i+1}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star})\leq\frac{1}{4}(\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{i+1}^{\star}).

Plugging this into (
95
) and using
\sum_{j}\mu_{j}=1
yields the key lower bound

\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{i+1}^{\star}\ \geq\ 4\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mu_{j}\bigl(P_{ij}-P_{i+1,j}\bigr).

(96)

Under SST , the right-hand side is strictly positive; in particular,

\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{i+1}^{\star}\ \geq\ 4\underline{\delta}\qquad\text{for all }i\in[K-1]\text{ and all }\bm{\mu}\in\Delta_{K}.

Moreover, since
\bm{\mu}_{t}=(1-\lambda)\bm{\pi}_{0}+\lambda\bm{\pi}_{t}
and
(P_{ij}-P_{i+1,j})\geq 0
for all
j
,

\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mu_{t,j}(P_{ij}-P_{i+1,j})\ \geq\ (1-\lambda)\sum_{j=1}^{K}\pi_{0,j}(P_{ij}-P_{i+1,j})\ \geq\ \delta,

so for all
t
and all
i\in[K-1]
,

\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{i+1}^{\star}\ \geq\ 4\delta.

(97)

Next, we derive the log-ratio recursion formula for the policy iterates. By the softmax and multiplicative form of the update

\pi^{+}_{i}=\frac{\pi_{{\rm ref},i}\,e^{\beta\theta_{i}^{\star}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{{\rm ref},k}\,e^{\beta\theta_{k}^{\star}}},

hence for each
i\in[K-1]
,

\log\frac{\pi^{+}_{i}}{\pi^{+}_{i+1}}=\log\frac{\pi_{{\rm ref},i}}{\pi_{{\rm ref},i+1}}+\beta(\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{i+1}^{\star}).

(98)

Assume
\alpha,\lambda\in[0,1)
, Using
\log\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}^{(t)}=(1-\alpha)\log\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}+\alpha\log\bm{\pi}_{t}
Subtracting for coordinate
i+1
from
i
gives

\log\frac{\pi_{{\rm ref},i}}{\pi_{{\rm ref},i+1}}\ =\ \alpha\log\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{i+1}}+(1-\alpha)\log\frac{\pi_{{\rm ref},i}}{\pi_{{\rm ref},i+1}}\ \geq\ \alpha\log\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{i+1}}+(1-\alpha)\kappa.

(99)

Combining (
98
), (
99
), and (
97
) yields the uniform recursion

\log\frac{\pi^{+}_{i}}{\pi^{+}_{i+1}}\ \geq\ \alpha\log\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{i+1}}+(1-\alpha)\kappa+4\beta\delta,\qquad\forall i\in[K-1].

(100)

Let the log ratio
r_{t,i}\coloneqq\log(\pi_{t,i}/\pi_{t,i+1})
. Then (
100
) implies

r_{t+1,i}\geq\alpha r_{t,i}+(1-\alpha)\kappa+4\beta\delta.

Solving this affine recursion gives

r_{t,i}\ \geq\ \alpha^{t-1}r_{1,i}+(1-\alpha^{t-1})\Big(\kappa+\frac{4\beta\delta}{1-\alpha}\Big).

(101)

If
c=\frac{1}{2}(\kappa+\frac{4\beta\delta}{1-\alpha})>0
, then since
\alpha^{t-1}\to 0
there exists
a finite
T
such that for all
t\geq T
and all
i\in[K-1]
,

\log\frac{\pi_{t,i}}{\pi_{t,i+1}}\ \geq\ c.

(102)

From (
102
), we have
\pi_{t,i+1}\leq\pi_{t,i}e^{-c}
and by induction

\pi_{t,k}\leq\pi_{t,1}e^{-(k-1)c}
for all
k\geq 2
. Hence

1-\pi_{t,1}=\sum_{k=2}^{K}\pi_{t,k}\leq\pi_{t,1}\sum_{m=1}^{K-1}e^{-mc}\leq\frac{\pi_{t,1}}{e^{c}-1},

which implies
1\leq\pi_{t,1}(1+\frac{1}{e^{c}-1})=\pi_{t,1}\frac{e^{c}}{e^{c}-1}
and thus

\pi_{t,1}\ \geq\ \frac{e^{c}-1}{e^{c}}\ =\ 1-e^{-c}\ \geq\ 1-(e^{c}-1)^{-1}\ =\ 1-\varepsilon.

This proves (
91
).

In addition, if
\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}
is aligned with the order
1\succ\cdots\succ K
(nonincreasing), then
\kappa\geq 0
and the stated simplification follows.

Finally we show the true collapse when
\alpha=1
and the induced entropy monotonicity. Since
\alpha=1
,
\bm{\pi}_{\rm ref}^{(t)}=\bm{\pi}_{t}
and (
98
) becomes

\log\frac{\pi_{t+1,i}}{\pi_{t+1,i+1}}=\log\frac{\pi_{t,i}}{\pi_{t,i+1}}+\beta(\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{i+1}^{\star}).

(103)

By (
96
) and SST , we have

\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{i+1}^{\star}\geq 4\underline{\delta}
for all
i
, hence from (
103
)

\log\frac{\pi_{t+1,i}}{\pi_{t+1,i+1}}\ \geq\ \log\frac{\pi_{t,i}}{\pi_{t,i+1}}+4\beta\underline{\delta},

so each adjacent log-ratio diverges to
+\infty
at least linearly in
t
. The same geometric-series argument as above
then yields
\pi_{t,1}\to 1
, i.e.
\bm{\pi}_{t}\to\bm{e}_{1}
.

Since
\bm{\pi}_{t}\to\bm{e}_{1}
, we also have
H(\bm{\pi}_{t})\to 0
.
Finally,
H
is continuous on
\Delta_{K}^{\circ}
and strictly Schur-concave, and the update with
\alpha=1
is a strict
reweighting toward action
1
with uniformly separated weights
(
\theta_{1}^{\star}>\theta_{2}^{\star}>\cdots>\theta_{K}^{\star}
and gaps
\geq 4\underline{\delta}
). Therefore, there exists a neighborhood
of
\bm{e}_{1}
in which the MRS–DPO step strictly decreases entropy. Since
\bm{\pi}_{t}\to\bm{e}_{1}
, the trajectory eventually enters this
neighborhood and remains there, which proves the existence of a finite
T
such that (
92
) holds for all
t\geq T
.
∎

Appendix E
Experimental Details and Additional Results

E.1
Dataset description

All experiments were conducted using the
NVIDIA HelpSteer
dataset, which contains real human feedback collected for LLM alignment.
The raw dataset consists of
35{,}331
response-level records, each associated with a textual prompt and a model-generated response.

Available annotations.

Each response is independently evaluated along multiple qualitative attributes.
Specifically, the available fields include:

{helpfulness, correctness, coherence, complexity, verbosity}
.

These scalar scores are used as the basis for constructing pairwise preference matrices.

An example entry from the dataset is shown below:

{‘‘prompt’’: ‘‘Explain why the sky is blue.’’,
‘‘response’’: ‘‘The sky appears blue due to Rayleigh scattering...’’,
‘‘helpfulness’’: 3,
‘‘correctness’’: 4,
‘‘coherence’’: 4,
‘‘complexity’’: 2,
‘‘verbosity’’: 2
}

Data filtering and preprocessing.

Our analysis focuses on preference dynamics over fixed-size sets of competing
responses.
To enable a uniform experimental protocol across prompts with varying numbers
of responses, we restrict attention to prompts with at least
K=4
associated
responses and construct preference instances by selecting
K=4
responses per
prompt uniformly at random.
This choice balances expressiveness and data coverage: most prompts in the dataset correspond to at most
4
responses, and preference sets of size
4
are sufficiently rich to admit both ST and cyclic structures.

Resulting structure.

After preprocessing, each retained prompt induces a local preference learning instance consisting of a fixed-size set of
K=4
responses, together with their corresponding multi-dimensional evaluation scores.
This collection of
7{,}497
local instances forms the basis from which all preference matrices used in the experiments are derived.

E.2
Construction of preference matrices

For learning instance after the preprocessing, each prompt associates with
K=4
responses, we construct a local probabilistic preference matrix

P\in[0,1]^{K\times K},

where
P_{ij}\in[0,1]
represents the probability that response
i
is preferred over response
j
, and
P_{ij}+P_{ji}=1
for any
i,j\in[K]
.

Randomized attribute selection.

Let
\mathcal{D}
denote the set of available evaluation attributes.
For each unordered pair of responses
(i,j)
, we independently sample an attribute

d\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{D}),

and denote by
s_{i}^{(d)}
and
s_{j}^{(d)}
the corresponding scalar scores.

Matrix Construction.

For each
(i,j)
, given the selected attribute
d
from the last step, we generate the probability related to
(i,j)
via sigmoid function:

P_{ij}=\mathrm{sigmoid}(s^{(d)}_{i}-s^{(d)}_{j})=\frac{\exp\!\big(s_{i}^{(d)}\big)}{\exp\!\big(s_{i}^{(d)}\big)+\exp\!\big(s_{j}^{(d)}\big)},\qquad P_{ji}=1-P_{ij},

with
P_{ii}=\tfrac{1}{2}
by convention.
By construction, the resulting matrix satisfies

P_{ij}+P_{ji}=1
for all
i\neq j
and thus is a valid preference matrix.

While scores on the same attribute yield a ranking over responses, independent randomization of attributes across response pairs can introduce conflicting pairwise comparisons across different dimensions.
As a result, the aggregated preference matrix need not correspond to any single latent scoring function, nor even be transitive.
We classify them into two categories: ST matrices defined in
Definition

7
, and cyclic matrices that are not transitive.

Preference Structure Verification.

To verify ST matrices in the experiments, for each matrix
P
, we first extract a candidate total order
by sorting responses according to their aggregate preference scores
\sum_{j}P_{ij}
.
We then verify whether
P
satisfies the strongly transitive condition in

Definition

7
with respect to this order.
Matrices that fail this verification are not classified as strongly transitive.

We test a matrix and verify it is cyclic if its induced dominance graph contains a
directed cycle, i.e., there exist indices
i_{1},\dots,i_{m}
with
m\geq 3
such that

P_{i_{\ell}i_{\ell+1}}>\tfrac{1}{2}
for all
\ell
(indices modulo
m
).

Matrices that are neither ST nor cyclic are excluded from the analysis.

Statistics.

After filtering, the dataset contains
4,924
ST preference matrices and
118
cyclic preference matrices.

E.3
Experimental setup in
Section

5

All experiments in
Section

5
are conducted using preference
matrices constructed from the dataset, without additional sampling noise.
These matrices represent probabilistic pairwise comparisons aggregated from
human evaluation scores, as described in

Section

E.2
.

Unless otherwise stated, for each preference instance and parameter configuration

(\alpha,\beta\lambda)
, we fix
\lambda=0.5
and set
\beta=v/0.5
for each value
v
of
\beta\lambda
. We initialize the dynamics with

\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}=\pi_{0}
equal to the uniform distribution over
K
responses,
set the initial policy
\pi_{1}=\pi_{0}
, and run the MRS-IPO dynamics for

T=3000
iterations.
This horizon is sufficient to observe convergence, collapse, or persistent
non-convergent behavior across all parameter regimes considered.

E.3.2
Statistical metrics for aggregated experimental results in
Section

5.3
and
Section

E.5

In
Section

5.3
and
Section

E.5
, we consider aggregated phenomena across all instances constructed from the HelpSteer dataset.

Specifically, for ST preferences, policy collapse is quantified using
the Shannon entropy of the terminal policy:

H(\bm{\pi}_{t})\;=\;-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\pi_{t,i}\log\pi_{t,i}.

Low entropy indicates more concentration on a small subset of responses, implying more serious policy collapse.

For cyclic preferences,
we measure the oscillatory behavior of the dynamics using the time-averaged variance:

\mathrm{CS}(\bm{\pi})\;=\;\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\operatorname{Var}_{t\geq T_{0}}\left[\pi_{t,i}\right],

computed using policies after a burn-in period
T_{0}
.
This metric converges to zero if and only if the dynamics converge to a fixed point, and
increases with the amplitude of persistent oscillations.

E.4
Finite sample preference noise under single parameter variations

We consider preference matrices constructed from the dataset as true underlying preference matrices.
To account for the finite sample variability inherent in real-world preference
data, we introduce controlled stochastic perturbations to these matrices before
running the dynamics and conduct additional experiments examing the behavior of MRS-IPO dynamics under finite sample preference estimation.
The goal of these experiments is not to study noise as a perturbation per se,
but to assess whether the qualitative dynamical behaviors identified in
Section

4

persists when preferences are instantiated from finite data.

Again, we consider the ST matrix and cyclic matrix in
Section

E.3.1
. For each preference matrix

P\in[0,1]^{K\times K}
, we generate an empirical realization

\widehat{P}
by independently sampling a fixed number
n
of pairwise comparisons
for each unordered response pair.
For each pair
(i,j)
, the empirical preference value
\widehat{P}_{ij}
is obtained
by averaging the outcomes of
n
independent Bernoulli trials with success
probability
P_{ij}
, yielding

\widehat{P}_{ij}\in\Big\{0,\tfrac{1}{n},\tfrac{2}{n},\dots,1\Big\},\qquad\widehat{P}_{ji}=1-\widehat{P}_{ij}.

Importantly, this finite sample preference realization
\widehat{P}
is sampled

once per experimental run
and then held fixed throughout the entire
execution of the MRS-IPO dynamics.
That is, preference noise is introduced at the level of the preference instance,
rather than being resampled over time or depending on the evolving policy

\pi_{t}
.
This design isolates the effect of static estimation noise in the preference
matrix and avoids confounding preference dynamics with on-policy resampling
effects.

For each parameter of
\alpha,\beta,\lambda
, we change its value with two other parameters fixed to observe how the dynamics change as one parameter varies.
Unless otherwise stated, all noisy experiments use the same algorithmic
initialization as in the noiseless setting.
Specifically, we set
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}=\pi_{0}
to be the uniform distribution
over the
K
responses, initialize the policy with
\pi_{1}=\pi_{0}
, and run the
dynamics for
T=3000
iterations.
Throughout these experiments, we set the number of finite sample realizations per
preference instance to
50
, which provides a stable estimate of the mean
trajectory and variability without obscuring instance level effects and fix
n=5
, which introduces moderate
variability without overwhelming the underlying preference signal.

For parameter regimes in which finite sample realizations remain approximately
phase aligned (e.g., convergent dynamics under strongly transitive preferences or
weakly oscillatory regimes under cyclic preferences), we summarize behavior by
reporting the mean trajectory across realizations, with shaded regions indicating

\pm
one standard error (Figures
9
–
11
and Figures
5
–
7
).

In contrast, in regimes where the dynamics depend strongly on the realized
samples, such as collapse under strongly transitive preferences or strong
oscillations under cyclic preferences, finite sample realizations can differ
substantially in onset time, phase, or amplitude.
In these cases, averaging trajectories can cancel the phenomena and obscure the
underlying dynamical structure.
Accordingly, we present
representative individual
finite sample realizations to illustrate the diversity of behaviors that arise in
practice (Figure
12
,
8
).

Overall, these experiments indicate that the qualitative dynamical regimes
predicted by the theory, convergence, collapse, and persistent cycling, remain
observable even when the idealized structural assumptions (e.g., strict strong
transitivity or perfectly cyclic preferences) are mildly violated due to finite
sampling noise.
