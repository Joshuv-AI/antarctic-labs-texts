Simultaneously optimizing symmetry shifts and tensor factorizations for cost-efficient Fault-Tolerant Quantum Simulations of electronic Hamiltonians
Konrad Deka and Emil Zak

Abstract
In fault-tolerant quantum computing, the cost of calculating Hamiltonian eigenvalues using the quantum phase estimation algorithm is proportional to the constant scaling the Hamiltonian matrix block-encoded in a unitary circuit. We present a method to reduce this scaling constant for the electronic Hamiltonians represented as a linear combination of unitaries. Our approach combines the double tensor-factorization method of Burg et al. von Burg et al. 2021 with the the block-invariant symmetry shift method of Loaiza and Izmaylov Loaiza and Izmaylov 2023 . By extending the electronic Hamiltonian with appropriately parametrized symmetry operators and optimizing the tensor-factorization parameters, our method achieves a 25% reduction in the block-encoding scaling constant compared to previous techniques. The resulting savings in the number of non-Clifford T-gates, which are an essential resource for fault-tolerant quantum computation, are expected to accelerate the feasiblity of practical Hamiltonian simulations. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our technique on Hamiltonians of industrial and biological relevance, including the nitrogenase cofactor (FeMoCo) and cytochrome P450.



I
Introduction

One of the most promising applications of quantum computing is the simulation of quantum chemistry Hamiltonians, a task that exceeds the capabilities of classical computing for certain classes of industrially relevant molecules. A central focus in this field is the computation of Hamiltonian eigenvalues, which have been extensively studied theoretically
von Burg et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2021
;
Low and Chuang 2019
;
Berry et al. 2019
. The eigenvalues of electronic Hamiltonians correspond to the energy levels of electronic states. Differences in these energy levels for varying molecular structures and compositions inform about the relative stability of compounds and pathways for chemical transformations. Calculating reaction pathway diagrams is pivotal for designing innovative materials, including solvents, donor/acceptor molecules for energy storage
Kim et al. 2022
, catalysts for nitrogen fixation
Beinert 1997
;
Reiher et al. 2017
and for artificial photosynthesis
Bartschi et al. 2024
.

In this work, we focus on two molecules for which quantum chemistry calculations at the
chemical accuracy
of 1 mHartree or better are currently beyond the reach of classical computers
Goings et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2019
. The first is the FeMo cofactor (Fe
7
MoS
9
C) of the nitrogenase enzyme, a catalyst participating in the biological nitrogen fixation process
Reiher et al. 2017
. The second system is the Cpd I structure in the cytochrome P450 enzyme
Shaik et al. 2009
, a protein that governs the metabolism of drugs in humans
Liu et al. 2007
;
Boobis et al. 2009
. Structurally similar to the heme molecule in the hemoglobin protein
Cho et al. 2008
, Cpd I represents a class of biologically significant molecules.

Density Matrix Renormalization Group (DMRG) studies of FeMoCo
Li et al. 2019
and Cpd I
Goings et al. 2022
suggest that quantum computation can be advantageous for these systems. The largest DMRG calculations to date for FeMoCo
Li et al. 2019
, in the spin
S=3/2
ground state with 76 orbitals and 113 electrons (forming a Hilbert space of approximately
10^{35}
elements), highlight the need for carefully choosing the basis set to adequately capture electron correlation effects, particularly for open-shell molecules. For this reason, achieving chemical accuracy for these compounds with reliable error estimates remains challenging for classical computing methods
Marti and Reiher 2010
;
Li et al. 2019
;
Elfving et al. 2020
.

Quantum computational methods, in principle, enable removing limitations of classical algorithms for systems with high complexity in three key aspects. One limitation is memory, which quantum computation addresses by using qubits. Another is the ability to calculate eigenvalues of a given input Hamiltonian with arbitrary precision and accuracy, directly controlled by the simulation parameters. Like classical computation, quantum computation utilizes a basis set representation of the electronic Hamiltonian, which can be systematically extended and improved, to increase the calculation accuracy, as ensured by the variational principle
Helgaker et al. 2000
. Finally, the total computation time can be shorter for a quantum computer implementation
Elfving et al. 2020
.

Quantifying the advantage carried by a quantum computation over classical methods for electronic structure calculations requires a method for quantum resource estimation. On the classical computing end, it is necessary to determine the accuracy of the energy levels and the associated computational complexity for a given Hamiltonian. The verdict of quantum advantage depends on these metrics and is a subtle, actively debated topic
Lee et al. 2023
;
Daley et al. 2022
;
Krol et al. 2024
. Quantum resource estimation is fundamental to assessing quantum advantage as it involves counting the number of qubits and non-Clifford T-gates, with the latter setting the runtime for the quantum computation
Gidney et al. 2024
. Reducing this runtime is essential for advancing the practical utility of a quantum computation. In this work, we focus on minimizing this algorithmic runtime to accelerate quantum electronic structure computation and to reduce their overall cost.

For our quantum computation framework we choose quantum phase estimation (QPE)
Abrams and Lloyd 1999
, being the most efficient algorithm for calculating Hamiltonian eigenvalues on a fault-tolerant quantum computer
Lee et al. 2021
. In QPE, the phases generated by time-evolution under Hamiltonian
H
are accumulated by appropriate input eigenstates of the Hamiltonian at a controllable precision
\epsilon
. The time evolution operator,
e^{iHt}
, a core component of QPE, is most efficiently implemented for electronic Hamiltonians using qubitization
Low and Chuang 2019
;
Berry et al. 2019
;
von Burg et al. 2021
. To perform qubitization, one needs to construct a
block encoding
of the Hamiltonian
H
, that is, a quantum circuit
C
whose unitary matrix contains
H/\lambda
(where
\lambda>0
) as a submatrix. The implementation of
e^{iHt}
then requires
\mathcal{O}(\lambda t)
repetitions of circuit
C
.
Since the runtime of the QPE algorithm scales linearly with the block-encoding scaling constant
\lambda
, lowering its value has been subject to several recent studies
von Burg et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2021
;
Loaiza and Izmaylov 2023
;
Cohn et al. 2021
;
Oumarou et al. 2024
;
Cortes et al. 2024
.
In this work we consider the electronic Hamiltonian studied also in Burg et al.
von Burg et al. 2021
, which can be written as:

H=\sum_{i,j,\sigma}h_{ij}a_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}a_{j\sigma}+\sum_{i,j,\sigma,k,l,\tau}g_{ijkl}a_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}a_{j\sigma}a_{k\tau}^{\dagger}a_{l\tau},

(1)

where
0\leq i,j,k,l<N
are the orbital indices,
\sigma
and
\tau
denote spin indices.
a_{i\sigma}
annihilates electron in spin-orbital
(i,\sigma)
.
h_{ij}
represents the one-body terms, and
g_{ijkl}
describes two-body electron-electron interactions. Matrix elements of the electronic Hamiltonians for FeMoCo and Cpd I were reported in previous works
Reiher et al. 2017
;
von Burg et al. 2021
;
Rocca et al. 2024
;
Goings et al. 2022
, which also discuss the associate error estimates. For demonstrating our technique, here we adapt the electronic Hamiltonians from refs
Reiher et al. 2017
;
Goings et al. 2022
for FeMoCo and Cpd I (cytochrome P450), respectively.

The block-encoding scaling constant
\lambda
can be lowered by representing the electron-electron interaction tensor
g_{ijkl}
with lower-rank tensors. Burg et al.
von Burg et al. 2021
, introduced the
double factorization
algorithm (DF), for the electronic ground state energy calculations using a Hamiltonian represented with lower-rank tensors.
DF involves performing eigenvalue decomposition of the
g_{ijkl}
tensor twice, giving a block-encoding scaling constant we denote as
\lambda_{DF}
. Since then, further improvements in the value of
\lambda
have been made by choosing alternative decomposition schemes, including
tensor hypercontraction
method of Lee et al.
Lee et al. 2021
,
compressed double factorization
(CDF) by Cohn et al.
Cohn et al. 2021
and
regularized compressed double factorization
(RCDF) by Oumarou et al.
Oumarou et al. 2024
.

A qualitative progress in lowering
\lambda
came through with a
block-invariant symmetry shifts
(BLISS) method by Izmaylov and Loaiza
Loaiza and Izmaylov 2023
. BLISS assumes the Hamiltonian
H
is applied to a state
|\psi\rangle
, meaning that it is sufficient to consider a different Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}
,
as long as
(H-\tilde{H})|\psi\rangle=0
. In the case of electronic Hamiltonians, the state
|\psi\rangle
that enters the QPE algorithm describes a fixed number of electrons, i.e
(N_{e}-n_{e})|\psi\rangle=0
where
n_{e}
is a positive integer and
N_{e}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{i\sigma}a_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}a_{i\sigma}
is the total electron number operator.
In ref.
Loaiza and Izmaylov 2023
the symmetry-shifted Hamiltonian is chosen as
\tilde{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=H+B(N_{e}-n_{e})
, where

B
stands for a Hermitian operator. Izmaylov and Loaiza apply this observation to the double-factorization technique as well as other factorizations
Loaiza and Izmaylov 2023
;
Bellonzi et al. 2024
, demonstrating a numerical reduction in the value of
\lambda
for all factorizations considered.
Recently, Rocca et al.
Rocca et al. 2024
introduced the
symmetry-compressed double factorization
(SCDF), improving on the CDF
Cohn et al. 2021
and RCDF
Oumarou et al. 2024
methods in two ways. First, the circuit implementation of CDF and RCDF is more expensive than the original DF, which is addressed in
Rocca et al. 2024
. Second, SCDF incorporates a simplified version of the method from ref.
Loaiza and Izmaylov 2023
by replacing the original electronic Hamiltonian
H
with
\tilde{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=H+(b_{1}N_{e}+b_{2})(N_{e}-n_{e})
, for optimized values of constants
b_{1},b_{2}
.

In this work, we propose a technique for further reducing
\lambda
within the double factorization framework.
We simplify the SCDF approach of Rocca et al.
Rocca et al. 2024
and incorporate the full BLISS method
Loaiza and Izmaylov 2023
.
Our implementation achieves a minimum 25% reduction in
\lambda_{DF}
for complex systems such as FeMoCo
Rocca et al. 2024
;
Reiher et al. 2017
and cytochrome P450 molecules
Goings et al. 2022
, compared with other techniques
Rocca et al. 2024
;
Loaiza and Izmaylov 2023
;
von Burg et al. 2021
.

II
Methodology

In this section, we lay out details of the double-factorization technique, formulated to naturally include the BLISS method, discussed in sec.
IV
.

II.1
Definitions and conventions

First, we introduce definitions and conventions used throughout the paper.
Let
N\geq 1
denote the number of orbitals encoded in the quantum states of
2N
qubits, with each qubit representing a spin-orbital. The
fermionic spin-orbital annihilation/creation operators
,
a_{j\sigma},a^{{\dagger}}_{j\sigma}
respectively, destroy and create electrons in the spin-orbital
j,\sigma
, where
0\leq j<N
and
\sigma\in\{0,1\}
. These fermionic ladder operators satisfy the following canonical anti-commutation relations:

\displaystyle a_{j\sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{ for all }j,\sigma,

(2)

\displaystyle\left\{a_{j\sigma},a_{j\sigma}^{\dagger}\right\}

\displaystyle=I\quad\textrm{ for all }j,\sigma,

(3)

\displaystyle\left\{a_{j\sigma},a_{k\tau}\right\}

\displaystyle=0\quad\textrm{ if }j\neq k\textrm{ or }\sigma\neq\tau.

(4)

The qubit encoding of these operators is achieved via the Jordan-Wigner transformation, which maps fermionic operators to Pauli operators as follows:

\displaystyle a_{j\sigma}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{2}Z_{0}Z_{1}\dots Z_{j-1+N\sigma}\left(X_{j+N\sigma}+iY_{j+N\sigma}\right).

(5)

\displaystyle a_{j\sigma}^{\dagger}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{2}Z_{0}Z_{1}\dots Z_{j-1+N\sigma}\left(X_{j+N\sigma}-iY_{j+N\sigma}\right).

(6)

Here
Z_{0}
is the Pauli-
Z
gate acting on the first qubit (indexed from zero), and
iY_{j+N\sigma}
represents the Pauli-
Y
gate acting on the
(j+N\sigma)
-th qubit. We define the spin-independent, one-electron
orbital excitation
operators by summing over the spin variable
\sigma
:

E_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{\sigma\in\{0,1\}}a_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}a_{j\sigma}.

(7)

Using these definitions, the electronic Hamiltonian introduced in eq.
1
can be expressed with the orbital excitation operators as:

H=\sum_{i,j=0}^{N-1}h_{ij}E_{ij}+\sum_{i,j,k,l=0}^{N-1}g_{ijkl}E_{ij}E_{kl},

(8)

where
h_{ij},g_{ijkl}
are real coefficients. The terms
h_{ij}
represent the one-body matrix elements of the Hamiltonian (symmetric,
h_{ij}=h_{ji}
), while
g
describe the two-body electron-electron interaction terms. The coefficients
g_{ijkl}
exhibit the standard
8
-fold symmetry constraints:
g_{ijkl}=g_{jikl}=g_{ijlk}=g_{klij}
, as detailed in
Helgaker et al. 2000
.

II.2
Block encoding of double-factorized electronic Hamiltonian

In this section, we discuss the double-factorization method for the electronic Hamiltonian and its block-encoding. For further details on the double-factorization approach, we refer the reader to
von Burg et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2021
;
Rocca et al. 2024
;
Cohn et al. 2021
;
Oumarou et al. 2024
.

Block-encoding

Let
A
be a
2^{m}\times 2^{m}
matrix. A unitary operator (circuit)
U
acting on
n
qubits (i.e.
U
is a
2^{n}\times 2^{n}
matrix)
block encodes
A
if

\left(|\underbrace{0\dots\dots 0}_{n-m\textrm{ zeros}}\rangle\otimes I\right)U\left(\langle\underbrace{0\dots\dots 0}_{n-m\textrm{ zeros}}|\otimes I\right)=A.

(9)

The following Proposition summarizes elementary operations that can be performed on block-encoded matrices:

Proposition 1
.

The following operations on block encodings are possible:

(i)

If matrices
A_{1},\dots A_{k}
are block-encoded and
\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{k}
are arbitrary coefficients, then the linear combination
\frac{1}{|\alpha|}\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}A_{j}
, where
|\alpha|=\sum_{j}|\alpha_{j}|
, can also be block-encoded.
Specifically, if
U_{1}\dots U_{k}
are quantum circuits acting that block encode
A_{1}\dots A_{k}
respectively, then it is possible to block-encode
\frac{1}{|\alpha|}\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}A_{j}
with a single controlled application of each
U_{j}
and
\mathcal{O}(k)
additional gates.

(ii)

If a matrix
A
is block-encoded, it is posssible to block-encode
2A^{2}-I
.
Specifically, if
U
is a quantum circuit acting on
n
qubits that block encodes a
2^{m}\times 2^{m}
matrix
A
,
the block-encoding of
2A^{2}-I
requires two applications of
U
and
\mathcal{O}(n-m)
additional gates.

(iii)

A quantum circuit
U
is its own block encoding.

For a detailed proof, we refer to section VII.A of the supplementary information in
von Burg et al. 2021
.

Double-factorization of the electronic Hamiltonian

The double-factorization method begins by decomposing the two-body electronic energy tensor
g_{ijkl}
as follows:

g_{ijkl}=\tilde{g}_{ijkl}(R)+\epsilon_{ijkl},

(10)

where

\tilde{g}_{ijkl}(R)=\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}A_{rij}A_{rkl}.

(11)

Here
\tilde{g}_{ijkl}(R)
is a decomposition of the rank-4
g
tensor into a sum of
R\leq N
symmetric matrices
A_{r}
(i.e.
A_{rij}=A_{rji}
).
The truncation parameter
R
is chosen such that
\tilde{g}_{ijkl}(R)
approximates
g_{ijkl}
within the error
||\epsilon||_{F}
, where
||.||_{F}
is the Frobenius norm
Oumarou et al. 2024
, defined as:

||\epsilon||_{F}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sqrt{\sum_{ijkl}|\epsilon_{ijkl}|^{2}}=\sqrt{\sum_{ijkl}|g_{ijkl}-\tilde{g}_{ijkl}(R)|^{2}}.

(12)

To evaluate the quality of the approximation, the spectral norm can be used to compare the electronic Hamiltonians constructed from
g_{ijkl}
and
\tilde{g}_{ijkl}(R)
:
||\sum_{ijkl}g_{ijkl}E_{ij}E_{kl}-\sum_{ijkl}\tilde{g}_{ijkl}E_{ij}E_{kl}||
. However, computing the spectral norm is as expensive as solving the electronic Schroedinger equation itself. Instead, as discussed in refs.
von Burg et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2021
;
Rocca et al. 2024
, a reasonable mitigation involves using the Frobenius norm for the electronic Hamiltonian matrix elements. Its quality has been benchmarked in refs.
Rocca et al. 2024
;
Lee et al. 2021
, showing consistency with the spectral-norm measures calculated at the CCSD(T) level of theory. For the benchmarked Hamiltonians the truncation parameter
R
required to achieve chemical accuracy (
1
mHartree) typically range in
R=4N-6N

Rocca et al. 2024
.

The decomposition given in eq.
11
can be used to represent the electronic Hamiltonian in the following form:

\tilde{H}(R)=\sum_{ij}h_{ij}E_{ij}+\sum_{i,j,k,l=0}^{N-1}\tilde{g}_{ijkl}(R)E_{ij}E_{kl}=\sum_{ij}h_{ij}E_{ij}+\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}\left(\sum_{ij}A_{rij}E_{ij}\right)^{2}=One(A_{-1})+\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}One(A_{r})^{2}.

(13)

where we adopted the notation from ref.
von Burg et al. 2021
, denoting
One(A)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}E_{ij}
, and the one-body electronic operator is written as
One(A_{-1})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{ij}h_{ij}E_{ij}
.
One-body operators
One(A)
are further factorized to rank-1 operators via decomposition of the symmetric
N
-by-
N
matrices
A
:

A_{ij}=\sum_{t}\lambda_{t}u_{ti}u_{tj}^{T},\quad\textrm{ where }u_{t}=(u_{ti})_{i=0\dots N-1}\textrm{ is a unit length vector.}

(14)

The form of eq.
14
implies the quantity
\sum_{t}\lambda_{t}=\tr A
equals the sum of eigenvalues of
A
. In particular, the decomposition shown in eq.
14
can be chosen as the eigenvalue decomposition of
A
. We note however that the double-factorization procedure does not require the vectors
u_{t}
to be mutually orthogonal.

The decomposition given in eq.
14
produces linear combinations of the fermionic ladder operators denoted as

B_{u\sigma}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{j}u_{j}a_{j\sigma}.

(15)

where
u=(u_{0},\dots u_{N-1})
is a real unit vector and
a_{j\sigma}
is the annihilation operator for spin-orbital
j
, defined in eq.
6
.
The
B_{u\sigma}
operator represents a rotation of the spin-orbital basis and satisfies fermionic commutation rules given in eq.
4
:

B_{u\sigma}^{\dagger}B_{u\sigma}+B_{u\sigma}B_{u\sigma}^{\dagger}=\sum_{i}u_{i}^{2}\left(a_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}a_{i\sigma}+a_{i\sigma}a_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}\right)+\sum_{i<j}u_{i}u_{j}\left(a_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}a_{j\sigma}+a_{i\sigma}a_{j\sigma}^{\dagger}+a_{j\sigma}^{\dagger}a_{i\sigma}+a_{j\sigma}a_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}\right)=I.

A similar calculation shows that
B_{u\sigma}^{2}=0
. As a result we observe that:

\left(B_{u\sigma}^{\dagger}B_{u\sigma}\right)^{2}=B_{u\sigma}^{\dagger}B_{u\sigma}B_{u\sigma}^{\dagger}B_{u\sigma}=B_{u\sigma}^{\dagger}\left(I-B_{u\sigma}^{\dagger}B_{u\sigma}\right)B_{u\sigma}=B_{u\sigma}^{\dagger}B_{u\sigma}.

B_{u\sigma}^{\dagger}B_{u\sigma}
is thus Hermitian and idempotent, hence represents an orthogonal projection. As a consequence,
V_{u\sigma}=2B_{u\sigma}^{\dagger}B_{u\sigma}-I
is unitary. This unitary can be efficiently implemented as a quantum circuit. Burg et al.
von Burg et al. 2021
proposed a sequence of
Givens rotations
generated by the Majorana representation to decompose the unitary:
V_{u\sigma}=i\gamma_{u\sigma 0}\gamma_{u\sigma 1}
, where
\gamma_{u\sigma x}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{j}u_{j}\gamma_{j\sigma x}
and
\gamma_{j\sigma 0}=a_{j\sigma}+a^{{\dagger}}_{j\sigma}
,
\gamma_{j\sigma 1}=-i(a_{j\sigma}-a^{{\dagger}}_{j\sigma})
. Similarly, Lee et al.
Lee et al. 2021
utilizes Givens rotations to efficiently represent
V_{u\sigma}
.

The one-electron operators
One(A)
can be expressed with
B_{u\sigma}
and
B_{u\sigma}^{{\dagger}}
as follows:

One(A)=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}E_{ij}=\sum_{tij}\lambda_{t}u_{ti}u_{tj}E_{ij}=\sum_{t\sigma}\lambda_{t}\left(\sum_{i}u_{ti}a_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}\right)\left(\sum_{j}u_{tj}a_{j\sigma}\right)=\sum_{t\sigma}\lambda_{t}B_{u_{t}\sigma}^{\dagger}B_{u_{t}\sigma}.

(16)

Because
2B_{u_{t}\sigma}^{\dagger}B_{u_{t}\sigma}-I
is unitary for each
t=0\dots N-1
and
\sigma=0,1
, it represents its own block encoding. Thus, following Proposition
1
(i), the block-encoding of the one-electron operators can be written as:

T_{1}(A)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{2\sum_{t}|\lambda_{t}|}\sum_{t\sigma}\lambda_{t}(2B_{u_{t}\sigma}^{\dagger}B_{u_{t}\sigma}-I)=\frac{1}{\sum_{t}|\lambda_{t}|}\left(One(A)-\tr A\right)=\frac{1}{\Lambda}\left(One(A)-\tr A\right),

(17)

where
\Lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{t}|\lambda_{t}|
.

Applying Proposition
1
(ii) to eq.
17
gives the block-encoding for the two-body operators as:

T_{2}(A)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=2\left(\frac{1}{\Lambda}\left(One(A)-\tr A\right)\right)^{2}-I=\frac{2}{\Lambda^{2}}\left(One(A)^{2}-2One(A)\tr A+(\tr A)^{2}\right)-I.

(18)

According to eq.
13
, there are
R+1
matrices
A_{r}
that form the decomposed electronic Hamiltonian. For each
r
, we assume a decomposition:

A_{rij}=\sum_{t}\lambda_{rt}u_{rti}u_{rtj}

(19)

where
\Lambda_{r}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{t}|\lambda_{rt}|
. Eigenvalue decomposition is optimal with respect to minimizing the overall block-encoding cost, as discussed in detail in Appendix
IX
. For this reason, all rank-2-to-rank-1 tensor factorizations are assumed to be eigenvalue decompositions.

Applying Proposition
1
(i) to the operators
T_{2}(A_{0}),\dots T_{2}(A_{R-1})
with weights
\Lambda_{0}^{2}/2,\dots\Lambda_{R-1}^{2}/2
, gives a block-encoding for the sum of two-electron operators:

T_{3}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r}\Lambda_{r}^{2}}\sum_{r}\left(One(A_{r})^{2}-2One(A_{r})\tr A_{r}\right)+\textrm{constant term}.

(20)

The constant term in eq.
20
is irrelevant to the block encoding and can be omitted.
In eq.
20
the sum
\sum_{r}One(A_{r})^{2}
represents the block-encoding of the two-body electron operator given in eq.
13
. The other component in the sum in eq.
20
, that is
-2\sum_{r}One(A_{r})\tr A_{r}
, is incorporated into the one-body term.
To simplify, we express the sum
\sum_{r}One(A_{r})\tr A_{r}
in terms of the orbital excitation operators defined in eq.
7
:

\sum_{r}One(A_{r})\tr A_{r}=\sum_{rij}A_{rij}E_{ij}\tr A_{r}=\sum_{ij}E_{ij}\cdot\left(\sum_{k}\sum_{r}A_{rij}A_{rkk}\right)=\sum_{ij}E_{ij}\left(\sum_{k}g_{ijkk}\right).

Accordingly, we define new matrix elements for the one-body operators:
h^{\prime}_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=h_{ij}+2\sum_{k}g_{ijkk}
and perform eigendecomposition on
h^{\prime}_{ij}
in place of
h_{ij}
given in eq.
13
:

h^{\prime}_{ij}=\sum_{t}\lambda_{t}u_{-1,i}u_{-1,j},\quad\textrm{ where }u_{-1}=(u_{-1,i})_{i=0\dots N-1}\textrm{ is a unit length vector.}

(21)

Using the new decomposition of the one-body terms in the electronic Hamiltonian, we repeat the block-encoding procedure shown in eq.
17
for
h^{\prime}_{ij}
giving a block-encoding of the form
T_{1}(h^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\Lambda_{-1}}\left(One(h^{\prime})-\tr h^{\prime}\right)
,
where
\Lambda_{-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{t}|\lambda_{t}|
.
Finally, we apply Proposition
1
(i) to the two operators

T_{3}
and
T_{1}(h^{\prime})
with coefficients
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r}\Lambda_{r}^{2}
and
\Lambda_{-1}
.
This results in a block-encoding of the electronic Hamiltonian written as:

\mathbf{U}[H]=\frac{1}{\lambda_{DF}}\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r}\Lambda_{r}^{2}\right)T_{3}+\Lambda_{-1}T_{1}(h^{\prime})\right)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{DF}}\left(\sum_{r}One(A_{r})^{2}+\sum_{ij}h_{ij}E_{ij}+\textrm{const}\right)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{DF}}\left(H+\textrm{const}\right),

(22)

where

\lambda_{DF}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r}\Lambda_{r}^{2}+\Lambda_{-1}.

(23)

For the purposes of block-encoding, the constant term in eq.
22
can be ignored.
In conclusion, the double-factorization method block encodes
H/\lambda_{DF}
, where
\lambda_{DF}
is given in eq.
23
.
The value of the
\lambda_{DF}
constant given in eq.
23
depends on the decompositions shown in eq.
11
,eq.
14
and eq.
21
.

III
Optimizing the double factorization method by norm minimization

Our objective is to minimize the value
\lambda_{DF}
, an optimization problem which can be formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\textrm{Find: }

\displaystyle\textrm{symmetric $N\times N$ matrices $A_{0},\dots A_{R-1}$},

(24)

\displaystyle\textrm{Constraint: }

\displaystyle||\epsilon||_{F}^{2}=\sum_{ijkl}|g_{ijkl}-\sum_{r}A_{rij}A_{rkl}|^{2}\textrm{ sufficiently small},

\displaystyle\textrm{Minimize: }

\displaystyle\lambda_{DF}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r}||{A_{r}}||_{\textrm{nuc}}^{2}+||{h^{\prime}}||_{\textrm{nuc}}.

Note that
h^{\prime}
does not depend on the choice of
A_{0}\dots A_{R-1}
and therefore can be ignored in the optimization objective.
As discussed in Appendix
IX
we use the value decomposition for each
A_{r}
given in eq.
19
and
h^{\prime}
given in eq.
21
. Since
A_{r}
and
h^{\prime}
are symmetric matrices, the appropriate block-encoding scaling constants are the respective nuclear norms:
\Lambda_{r}=||{A_{r}}||_{\textrm{nuc}}
and
\Lambda_{-1}=||{h^{\prime}}||_{\textrm{nuc}}
. We define the nuclear norm as:
||{A}||_{\textrm{nuc}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{t}\sigma_{t}
, where
\sigma_{t}
are the singular values of
A
. In section
IV
we discuss a technique for lowering the value of
\lambda_{DF}
utilizing the symmetry shift technique of Izmaylov and Loaiza
Loaiza and Izmaylov 2023
.

IV
Hamiltonians parametrized by symmetry-shift operators

In Hamiltonian simulation, particularly for eigenvalue calculations with the quantum phase estimation algorithm
Lee et al. 2021
;
von Burg et al. 2021
, the electronic Hamiltonian
H
acts on a state
|\psi\rangle
with a fixed
number of electrons

n_{e}
. The associated Hermitian operator
N_{e}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}E_{ii}
, known as
number of electrons operator
, satisfies the equation:
(N_{e}-n_{e})|\psi\rangle=0
.

Loaiza and Izmaylov
Loaiza and Izmaylov 2023
exploit this property to replace the electronic Hamiltonian
H
with a
shifted
Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=H+B(N_{e}-n_{e})
, where
B
stands for any operator. For
\tilde{H}
to remain Hermitian,
B
must also be Hermitian and commute with the number of electrons operator
N_{e}
. Non-hermiticity of
\tilde{H}
would spoil the properties required for the double-factorization and is therefore undesirable. Following ref.
Loaiza and Izmaylov 2023
, we define:

\tilde{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=H+\left(\sum_{ij\sigma}\xi_{ij}a_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}a_{j\sigma}+\kappa\right)\left(N_{e}-n_{e}\right)

(25)

where constants
\kappa
and
\xi_{ij}=\xi_{ji}
are subject to optimization. The symmetry-shifted electronic Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}=H+B(N_{e}-n_{e})
is then converted to the form shown in eq.
8
. To preserve the
8
-fold orbital permutation symmetry of the two-electron tensor in the electronic Hamiltonian, we use the following identity:

(E_{ij}+E_{ji})(N_{e}-n_{e})=-n_{e}E_{ij}-n_{e}E_{ji}+\sum_{r=0}^{R-1}\frac{1}{2}(E_{ij}E_{rr}+E_{ji}E_{rr}+E_{rr}E_{ij}+E_{rr}E_{ji}).

Rewriting the symmetry-shifted electronic Hamiltonian in a permutationally invariant form carries a benefit that
\tilde{H}
takes the form of the original electronic Hamiltonian written as:

\tilde{H}=\tilde{c}+\sum_{i,j=0}^{N-1}\tilde{h}_{ij}E_{ij}+\sum_{i,j,k,l=0}^{N-1}\tilde{g}_{ijkl}E_{ij}E_{kl},

(26)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{ij}

\displaystyle=h_{ij}-n_{e}\xi_{ij}+\kappa\delta_{ij},

(27)

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{ijkl}

\displaystyle=g_{ijkl}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\xi_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{ij}\xi_{kl}\right).

(28)

Such a formulation enables to adopt the double-factorization technique of Burg et al.
von Burg et al. 2021
straightforwardly. For this reason, gate counting procedures, circuits and discussion of ref.
von Burg et al. 2021
remains valid in the present case.

Using the symmetry-shifted Hamiltonian written in eq.
26
, we formulate the optimization problem outlined in section
III
as follows:

\displaystyle\textrm{Find: }

\displaystyle\kappa,\textrm{ symmetric }N\times N\textrm{ matrix }\xi,\textrm{ and symmetric }N\times N\textrm{ matrices }A_{0},\dots A_{R-1},

(29)

\displaystyle\textrm{Constraint: }

\displaystyle\sum_{ijkl}|\tilde{g}_{ijkl}-\sum_{r}A_{rij}A_{rkl}|^{2}\textrm{ sufficiently small},\textrm{ where }\tilde{g}_{ijkl}=g_{ijkl}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\xi_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{ij}\xi_{kl}\right),

\displaystyle\textrm{Minimize: }

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r}||{A_{r}}||_{\textrm{nuc}}^{2}+||{\tilde{h}^{\prime}}||_{\textrm{nuc}},\textrm{ where }\tilde{h}_{ij}=h_{ij}-n_{e}\xi_{ij}+\kappa\delta_{ij}\textrm{ and }\tilde{h}^{\prime}_{ij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\tilde{h}_{ij}+2\sum_{k}\tilde{g}_{ijkk}.

The implementation of a solution to the problem of finding optimal
\kappa
and
\xi_{ij}
is discussed in
section
V
.

V
Implementation details for the norm-reduction method

In this section we discuss implementation details for obtaining a solution to the optimization problem shown in eq.
29
. For this purpose, we define parametrized residue and block-encoding scaling constant as follows:

\displaystyle\textrm{Err}(\kappa,\xi,A)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{ijkl}\left(\tilde{g}_{ijkl}-\sum_{r}A_{rij}A_{rkl}\right)^{2},

(30)

\displaystyle\lambda(\kappa,\xi,A)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r}||{A_{r}}||_{\textrm{nuc}}^{2}+||{\tilde{h}^{\prime}}||_{\textrm{nuc}}.

The problem stated in eq.
29
translates to the problem of minimizing
\lambda(\kappa,\xi,A)
under the constraint
\textrm{Err}(\kappa,\xi,A)=0
. We define a cost function, similarly to the method discussed in ref.
Rocca et al. 2024
:

\textrm{Total}(\kappa,\xi,A)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=C_{\textrm{approx}}\textrm{Err}(\kappa,\xi,A)+\lambda(\kappa,\xi,A),

(31)

where
C_{\textrm{approx}}
is a constant. As initial conditions for the optimization, we chosen
\kappa=0,\xi_{ij}=0
, and
A

to be the standard double-factorization (that is, we treat the
g_{ijkl}
tensor as a
N^{2}\times N^{2}
matrix,
which is guaranteed to be symmetric and semipositive-definite,
take eigendecomposition
g_{ijkl}=\sum_{r}d_{r}V_{rij}V_{rkl}
, then we set
A_{rij}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sqrt{d_{r}}V_{rij}
).
The gradient descent method, as implemented in the Adam optimizer
Kingma and Ba 2017
, was used to minimize
\textrm{Total}(\kappa,\xi,A)
. Our Python implementation uses the JAX package for efficient linear algebra operations
Bradbury et al. 2018
.
Since JAX is capable of carrying out automatic differentiation, there was no need to compute the gradients symbolically. Despite the function
\textrm{Total}(\kappa,\xi,A)
being not differentiable at specific points, due to the nuclear norm involving absolute values of eigenvalues, the numerical gradient descent method performs without problems. The weight
C_{\textrm{approx}}
is chosen arbitrarily and required a short trial and error procedure.

VII
Conclusions

We developed a technique for block-encoding the electronic Hamiltonian, which utilizes the Hamiltonian’s symmetries and tailored tensor-network decompositions. Our approach represents the Hamiltonian in a symmetry-shifted form introduced in ref.
Loaiza and Izmaylov 2023
, preserving orbital-permutation symmetries.
The resulting symmetry-shifted Hamiltonian is doubly-factorized with parameters optimized using a gradient descent method.
In calculations of the electronic Hamiltonian eigenvalues with quantum phase estimation, the algorithmic complexity counted in the number of T-gates is directly proportional to the block-encoding scaling constant. Our results demonstrate a 25% reduction in this constant, compared to the best known implementations for molecules of both industrial and biological importance, including the Nitrogenase enzyme cofactor (FeMoCo) and cytochrome P450 (Cpd I structure). With the electronic orbital basis set sizes considered in this work, classical computing calculations of the electronic Hamiltonian eigenvalues for FeMoCo and P450 are presently beyond reach. Our protocol is applicable to the general class of electronic Hamiltonians and can be used to lower the fault-tolerant quantum computing cost of the Hamiltonian eigenvalue calculations.
