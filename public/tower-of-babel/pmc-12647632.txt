High-throughput transcriptomic screening reveals entrectinib as a repositioning opportunity in 19q12 autism spectrum disorder
Authors: Drishti Guin, Ursula Haditsch, Joseph J. Bellucci, Sabine Topka, Katherine E. Dyer, Gabriel A. Rivera Del Toro, Michael R. Blanco, Natalie F. Downs, Nicole Perfito, Arun Mahadevan, Christopher M. Moxham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Discovering new and viable therapies for genetic diseases is a time-consuming and cost-intensive process, especially for rare disorders. In this study, we highlight how a high-throughput drug discovery platform was utilized to uncover drugs at scale that normalized the signature for a rare neurological neurodevelopmental disease, 19q12 autism spectrum disorder (ASD) associated with deficiencies in ZNF536 and TSHZ3. We first identified the transcriptomic fingerprint of the disease in an in vitro disease model in the form of dysregulated pathways. Subsequently, we measured the biological impact of small molecule drugs in a relevant wild-type cell line and uncovered an approved drug Entrectinib that induced the opposite effect to that in the disease fingerprint, demonstrating the capability to normalize the disease fingerprint. Entrectinib was further prescribed off-label to the identified patient with 19q12 and drug effect was characterized both from blood collection and neuropsychological assessments. Biomarkers from blood recapitulated Entrectinib’s pharmacodynamic effect and normalized the disease signature. We show how generation of transferrable transcriptomics-derived disease signatures allows for measuring drug effects on a signature in related wild-type cell lines, making the screen universally applicable and reducing the need for expensive screens in disease models.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26015-y.

Introduction

A rare disease or disorder is defined as such if it affects < 200,000 individuals in the US at any given time1. There are over 7000 rare diseases and together they affect over 30 million people in the US1. While molecular genetics has helped uncover the root cause in many cases, resource limitations have made research and development of viable therapies for these diseases exceedingly difficult. Therefore, pipelines focused on discovering new opportunities at speed and scale for available FDA approved small molecules coupled with robust, statistically viable validation studies in the patient have the potential to be highly successful at lower cost for patients.

One such rare genetic disorder is a form of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) caused by structural variations in the q12 band of chromosome 192–6. Affected genes include ZNF536 and TSHZ3 which encode zinc-finger proteins that act as transcriptional regulators along with a myriad of other diverse functions7 and have been previously associated with ASD2,3. Both genes have been implicated in a network of complex biological processes that drive neurogenesis and neuronal differentiation of progenitors to neurons during development2,8. Our results similarly show that the down-regulation of these genes lead to the de-enrichment of pathways implicated in neurogenesis and neuron differentiation in patient-derived neurons. This further leads to disruption of synaptic connectivity and imbalance in neurotransmitter transport of these patient-derived neurons9,10. Electrophysiological assessments of patient neurons further showed that these pathway dysregulations lead to overexcitability when compared to controls.

We present a high-throughput platform for profiling FDA-approved small molecules that combines low-depth DRUG-seq with a computationally efficient analysis pipeline11,12. This approach yields detailed comprehensive transcriptomic snapshots of pharmacodynamic responses, creating opportunities for drug repurposing13,14. We further show that pairing these drug profiles with transferable, transcriptomics-derived disease signatures can generate novel therapeutic hypotheses. Focusing on key functional dysregulations in the disease allows us to generate transferrable signatures. Transferability ensures that drug effect on the respective signature can be measured in related wild-type lines making the screen more universally applicable and obviating the need for expensive screens in disease models.

This approach enabled the discovery of Entrectinib, traditionally used as a cancer therapy that was repurposed to be therapeutic for 19q12 ASD at doses below those typically used for the approved oncology indication. Drug efficacy was further validated using RNA-seq of whole blood collected from the patient as well as clinical evaluations over a period of 6 months. The RNA-seq results showed improvement of disease causing pathways, and the physicians treating the patient also reported improvements in various clinical endpoints.

Results

ZNF536 and TSHZ3 lead to aberrant neurogenesis in patient cells

A 3 year old female diagnosed with ASD and global developmental delay was enrolled in an approved research protocol (WCG IRB protocol #2001-01) with a goal to understand whether any currently approved drugs might be repurposed as a potential therapeutic for the patient. Available treatment options were limited both because of lack of genetic characterization and because effective treatment options are limited for ASD. Genetic characterization data (Supplementary Table S1) identified two candidate disease-relevant genes: ZNF536 and TSHZ3. Both TSHZ3 deletion as well as ZNF536 duplication have been previously implicated in a form of ASD2,3.

To further validate the transcriptional effect of the observed 19q12 alterations, induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) were generated from both healthy parent and affected child. These were then differentiated to produce neural progenitor cells (NPCs) (Methods). Both lines displayed a morphology characteristic of NPCs when assessed by immunocytochemical (ICC) staining (Supplementary Fig. S1) for the expression of Ki-67, a proliferation marker. The expression of Ki-67 decreased as a function of neuronal maturation in both lines (Fig. 1), underpinning the evolution of progenitors to a mature neuronal population.

The NPCs were matured into neurons (Methods) for 20 days and gene expression was profiled at multiple time points during maturation using RNA sequencing followed by differential expression analysis (DEA) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) for biological pathways (Methods). Expression of ZNF536 and TSHZ3 (measured by transcripts per million (TPM)) increased as a function of maturation time (Fig. 2A) and recapitulated previously published results: ZNF536 increased rapidly at early stages (iPSC-day 14) of neuronal differentiation while later stages displayed constant levels of ZNF536 (day 14+)8.

Raw TPM levels in the patient (Fig. 2A, dashed lines) and DEA comparing patient to control line showed significant down-regulation of both ZNF536 (q-value < 0.0001) and TSHZ3 (q-value < 0.1) in affected child-derived cells starting at the NPC stage and persisting at all neuronal timepoints sequenced (Fig. 2B). The magnitude of ZNF536 down-regulation is anti-correlated (Pearson correlation coefficient = − 0.81, p-value = 0.073) with maturation time point.

Down-regulation of TSHZ3 and ZNF536 was validated by qPCR in NPCs and was significant in patient compared to parent (ZNF536 p-value = 0.015, TSHZ3 p-value = 8.066E-05) (Fig. 2C).

Previously published results have shown that both genes are key regulators of neurogenesis. ZNF536 encodes a zinc-finger protein that is highly expressed in the brain and plays an important role in neuronal differentiation during early brain development. Repression of ZNF536 has been shown to cause aberrant enhancement of retinoic acid induced neuronal differentiation in p19 cells8. TSHZ3 similarly encodes another zinc-finger protein2, and represents a hub gene in a module of co-expressed genes that control early cortical development15.

Our findings aligned with these results; analysis of gene ontology (GO) terms using enrichment analysis revealed that the top pathways associated with these genes2,8 shared a common theme of being associated with neurogenesis and neuron differentiation (Fig. 2D). Additionally, a collection of other significant pathways searched by keywords associated with neurogenesis such as neuron differentiation, neurodevelopment, axonogenesis, etc. were also majorly de-enriched starting at the NPC stage showing premature down-regulation of neurogenesis (Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary Table S2).

The leading edge genes from these neurogenesis pathways revealed a number of significantly (q-value < 0.1) down-regulated genes in patient-derived neurons compared to the control (Fig. 2E,F, Supplementary Fig. S3). In the top significantly down-regulated genes, a large number were regulatory elements involved in neurogenesis and neuronal differentiation; SLIT116,17, NKX6-118, NKX6-219, ZNF5368, FOXG120, FOXA121, HOXA222, DLX1 and DLX223 etc. (Fig. 3A).

Two important signaling pathways associated with cell proliferation, the sonic hedgehog signaling pathway via the gene SHH and regulation of the Notch signaling pathway (Fig. 3B, top three rows), showed lower NES in the patient line compared to control24–26. The down-regulation of widespread regulatory genes involved in neurogenesis as well as signaling pathways that control cell proliferation, implies that the evolution of iPSC to progenitors to matured neurons in the cell line from the affected child is disrupted.

Identification of a disease signature associated with 19q12 ASD

The pathways identified above showed overwhelmingly that neurogenesis was disrupted in the patient-derived neurons. However, since most neurogenesis happens prenatally, these pathways may not be representative of current neurodevelopmental status in the patient27–29. We therefore needed to understand how changes in neurogenesis reflected across functional pathways in mature neurons.

To reveal neuronal pathways co-regulated with neurogenesis pathways, we built a co-expression network using pairwise pathway correlation across maturation timepoints (Methods). Community detection using a greedy modularity algorithm partitioned the network into eight modules (Fig. 4A). A module is a group of co-regulated pathways that are more connected to each other than to pathways outside the group. This means that pathways within a module tend to be activated or deactivated together, suggesting they work together to perform specific biological functions. Module 1 (Fig. 4A), containing 389 pathways (Supplementary module pathways file), was enriched for neurogenesis and neurodevelopmental pathways alongside other similar but unrelated cell differentiation and growth pathways. This included, for example, non-brain tissue-specific differentiation pathways that are driven by a core set of common genes that also drive differentiation of brain-specific cell types. In addition, two other neuron-relevant pathway groups were identified, related to excitatory neurotransmission and synapses.

The first group of pathways represented processes involved in the transport of glutamate, the most abundant excitatory neurotransmitter, and therefore a core function of these neurons30. A central figure in this function is SLC17A6 (Solute Carrier Family 17 Member 6), a gene that encodes the Vesicular Glutamate Transporter 2 (vGLUT2) protein. vGLUT2 plays an important role in excitatory synaptic transmission and plasticity by filling vesicles with the neurotransmitter glutamate31–35. Overexpression of vGLUT2 has been previously linked to ASD caused by MEF2C haploinsufficiency36, and its imbalance has been implicated in some sensory processing impairments in SLC17A6 KO mice35. SLC17A6 was significantly (q-value < 5E−11) over-expressed in patient-derived neurons during maturation relative to control, indicating dysregulated neurotransmission (Fig. 4B).

GSEA revealed a number of pathways that featured SLC17A6 in the leading edge geneset. To assess overall pathway activity in neurons, the mean NES was calculated for days 7–20 (excluding precursor datapoints). Significance was determined by combining individual p-values using Fisher’s method, followed by false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Six significant pathways were identified, with the gene SLC17A6 featuring in the leading edge geneset. A majority of these pathways also appeared in module 1 indicating co-regulation between neurogenesis and changes in the SLC17A6-pathways. The movement of glutamate is intricately linked with that of calcium (Ca2+) ions during excitatory synaptic transmission37,38. We therefore also observed that of the 12 pathways in the network associated with the keyword “calcium”, 8 belonged to module 1 and shared ~ 70% genes with the leading edge geneset of SLC17A6-related pathways. Collectively, these pathways were grouped into the functional category of glutamate-associated neurotransmitter activity, representing a signature of dysregulated neurotransmission. All significant pathways in this group were enriched in patient neurons compared to control during neuronal differentiation (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. S4, Supplementary Table S3).

The second group of pathways focused on synaptic transmission. Neurons in the brain function via the formation and transmission of synapses between each other to perform a wide variety of complex tasks39. An early indicator of dysregulated synaptic transmission was down-regulation of the gene NTN1 (q-value < 1.5E−38) (Fig. 5B) among the leading edge genes of neurogenesis pathways40,41. Inactivation of NTN1 (Netrin-1) also leads to disruption of excitatory synaptic connectivity42. Moreover, vGLUT2 (SLC17A6) up-regulation has also been associated with overexcitability and synaptic dysfunction in mice with MEF2C haploinsufficiency36. Indeed, we observed that synaptic plasticity pathways were over-represented in module 1 compared to all other modules, indicating co-disruption of synaptic pathways downstream of neurogenesis. Similar to the results observed with the pathways in Fig. 5A, a majority of the synaptic pathways also showed higher activity in the patient neurons (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Fig. S4, Supplementary Table S4). Together, the enrichment of these pathway groups indicated a phenotype of hyperexcitability in the patient neurons.

To further validate the functional consequences of the 19q12 transcriptional disease signature, we performed multi-electrode array (MEA) recordings on differentiating neurons derived from patient and parent NPCs between day 13 and day 22 in vitro (the same time frame used for the RNA-seq data). A burst was defined as ≥ 5 spikes within 100 ms on a single electrode; no network-level bursts or synchrony were detected in either group during this period. While the total number of spikes did not differ between patient and parent cultures (Fig. 6A), patient-derived neurons exhibited clear evidence of hyperexcitability at the level of single-electrode activity. Specifically, patient-derived neurons showed a significantly greater number of bursting electrodes (Fig. 6B), more bursts per electrode (Fig. 6C), and prolonged burst duration (Fig. 6D) compared with controls. These changes occurred without an increase in overall spike counts, indicating that spiking activity was redistributed into more robust bursting events rather than globally elevated firing rates. Taken together, these data indicate that patient neurons display a hyperexcitable bursting phenotype during early in vitro development, characterized by a higher propensity for bursts and extended intra-burst activity in the absence of network-wide synchronization.

These two identified pathway classes, related to synapse formation and neurotransmission, impaired in 19q12, highlight disrupted neuronal function leading to hyperexcitability and can be utilized as the disease signature for 19q12 ASD in the patient (Table 1). Contrary to neurogenesis, the evolution of neuroplasticity occurs well into adulthood and has been implicated in ASD, and is therefore relevant to the disease biology43–45. The overall signature was stored as a vector of identified pathways along with the respective magnitude of change in patient neurons, calculated by multiplying the mean NES across days 7–20 with the respective significance level (-log10[q-valueFisher test]).

The use of signatures offers various advantages over single gene/protein based target approaches. The reliance of pathways on multiple genes ensures that they capture representative activity even when subsets of tissue-specific genes are absent. Importantly, using disease signatures as a target provides a system-wide and transferable view of disease-associated molecular changes46. This approach removes the need to conduct compound or perturbation screens directly in disease-specific cellular models, which are often costly, technically challenging, or unavailable. Instead, by quantifying the enrichment or depletion of a disease signature, one can effectively “transfer” the disease fingerprint into related experimental contexts (including wild-type lines) for screening. In doing so, the same screening framework can be universally applied across multiple diseases without generating new disease-specific datasets. This not only accelerates discovery at lower cost but also offers a practical route to robust biomarkers of disease activity that can be tracked in patients during treatment.

Low dose entrectinib normalizes disease signature in a high throughput drug screen

Using a modified DRUG-seq protocol as described in the Methods section (Fig. 7A), a collection of 515 bioactive, FDA approved molecules were profiled in wild-type NGN2-derived glutamatergic neurons. These compounds spanned diverse ChEMBL target classes (Fig. 7B). NGN2-derived glutamatergic neurons were matured for 15 days in a 384-well plate, followed by treatment with each respective drug in 10-point concentration responses for 72 h and then sequenced to profile the transcriptomic response to each respective compound concentration. In parallel, a high content imaging assay was used to generate phenotypic profiles of the neurons under drug treatment (Supplementary Fig. S5). Each plate contained 16 DMSO control wells in addition to treated wells.

In the original DRUG-seq computational scheme, an important step involved assessing DMSO well quality prior to calculation of fold changes11. The authors generated random groupings of 3 wells that were compared to all other wells. Only the most similar wells were used for downstream analysis. However, this process is time consuming since 560 simulations are required to fully sample 16 DMSO wells. We opted for a much faster approach. To assess the similarity of a DMSO well to all the other wells Bray Curtis similarity scores (based on gene TPMs) were generated for all possible well pairs. Prior to similarity calculation, any genes that were expressed in less than 50% of wells were removed, and gene-level TPMs were weighted by the fraction of wells the gene was expressed in. An average of all pairwise similarities was calculated for all wells, and the distribution of similarity scores was used to fit a Gaussian curve. Wells with average similarity scores greater than two standard deviations from the mean of the Gaussian distribution were removed (Supplementary Fig. S6). Additionally, genes were filtered based on an adaptive filtering approach, instead of using a constant cutoff, to account for sequencing depth variations across plates (Supplementary Fig. S7, DRUGseq methods).

For each treatment condition (compound + concentration) on a plate, DEA and GSEA were performed. The influence of each treatment on the signature pathways (Table 1) was measured by calculating the cosine correlation between the patient-derived signature and the corresponding NES vector from treated wild-type neurons. Cosine correlation captures the similarity between two vectors, where negative values indicate opposing directions (reversal of disease markers) and positive values indicate alignment (enrichment of disease markers) (Fig. 7C). To find compounds with a biologically relevant impact, we first filtered for those exhibiting a dose-dependent effect on the signature, based on the principle that a true biological effect should vary as a function of concentration. A linear model was fitted to the per-compound correlation as a function of log10(concentration). The p-values provided a quality estimate, where significance indicated a concentration-dependence and vice versa (Fig. 7D). Compounds were filtered to include only significant fits (p-values < 0.05).

Of the 515 small molecules profiled, 12 exhibited a significant response to treatment (Fig. 7E) and also showed cosine similarity < − 0.5 (indicating signature reversal) for at least 3 concentrations. Among these, only one was approved for pediatric use. Entrectinib, an FDA-approved inhibitor of tropomyosin receptor kinases (TRKs) and ROS1, is approved for use in treating neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase gene (NTRK)-fusion positive solid tumors47.

Cytotoxicity of Entrectinib during the screen was assessed using the lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay as well as from imaging metrics (Supplementary Fig. S8). Below 14 nM, no cytotoxicity was detected, and dose dependence of cosine correlation was supported by Pearson’s r = -0.96 (p-value: 0.039). This dose-dependent negative correlation between disease signature and Entrectinib effect on signature pathways (Fig. 7F, Supplementary Fig. S9) shows that the drug normalizes the disease signature in wild-type neurons. Average NES of both signature pathway groups were regulated in the opposite direction (NES < 0, Supplementary Fig. S10, S11) during Entrectinib treatment than that observed in the patient neuronal model (NES > 0) (Fig. 7G). At concentrations > 14 nM, with the onset of cytotoxicity, the disease signature reversal was lost (Supplementary Fig. S9). Concentrations that exhibited signature reversal were much lower than the steady state plasma concentration of 2 µM observed in patients being prescribed the FDA approved dosage for the treatment of cancer48.

On closer examination of these pathways, we observed a significant down-regulation of the genes HTR3A, STX4, STX8, STXBP3, and VAMP2 among others (Supplementary Fig. S12). HTR3A, a serotonin gated ion channel, binds serotonin allowing movement of cations (Na+, K+, Ca2+) that lead to rapid excitation and neurotransmitter release49. Down-regulation of HTR3A in the context of excitatory neurons is limited but could be a mechanism to balance hyperexcitability in patient neurons. Additionally, STX4, STX8, STXBP3, and VAMP2 are integral members of the SNARE complex that also facilitates neurotransmitter release by driving synaptic vesicle fusion to the presynaptic membrane regulated by calcium50. SNARE complex pathways were de-enriched at lower drug concentrations (Supplementary Fig. S12). Both HTR3A and the SNARE complex are intricately tied to Ca2+ ions, a key class of pathways de-enriched post-treatment. The down-regulation of these genes and pathways offers an insight into how Entrectinib dampens neurotransmitter release to balance hyperexcitability.

These results suggested that Entrectinib at low concentrations could normalize the disease gene expression profile in the patient.

Dosage was gradually increased over the first 6 months from 350 mg to 1120 mg/week dose. Approximately 2 months after the dosage was increased to 1120 mg/week, it was dropped back to the intermediate dose of 560 mg/week, after the parents reported declining improvement at higher doses.

In a study of Entrectinib-resistant cancer lines a similar activity, opposite of its intended outcome, was observed stemming from increased PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK signaling54,55. This phenomenon, when a drug causes an effect opposite of its intended outcome, is known as the paradoxical drug effect56. Various other studies have demonstrated similar paradoxical effects at low concentrations compared to higher FDA-approved concentrations57–60. We therefore hypothesized that the effect observed in the patient is a compensatory mechanism specific to the low dosage conferring resistance-like activity unlike its anti-cancer activity observed at higher doses58,60. This activity is inversely related to dosage, such that pathway activity decreases at higher doses (at month 6) (Fig. 8A, orange bars). These results aligned with the parents’ reports of a decline in the child’s improvement at higher doses.

Entrectinib reverses the disease molecular signature in patient

The disease signature pathways are linked to functions specific to neurons and therefore may not be relevant in other tissues (like blood). This was corroborated by differential activity levels between both tissues: 789 genes featured in the leading edge set of these pathways in neurons, compared to only 269 in blood, with a common set of only 105 genes. The lower number of active, pathway-specific genes in blood consequently resulted in non-significant pathway enrichment scores. Given that, we focused our analysis on the individual genes within these pathways that are active in blood to understand the effect of Entrectinib in the patient.

All leading edge genes from analysis of pathway enrichment in blood were identified. To assess if Entrectinib normalized gene expression, we calculated the cosine correlation between the fold changes of patient post-treatment vs. pre-treatment samples and the fold changes of sibling vs. patient pre-treatment samples (Fig. 8B). Cosine correlation values increased with treatment time, indicating that Entrectinib normalized the patient’s gene expression profile towards the sibling control level. Unlike the pharmacodynamic effect shown in Fig. 8A, a significant dose-specific decrease in correlation was not observed.

In the patient pre-treatment blood samples, a large number of the identified leading edge genes were down-regulated compared to the sibling control (Fig. 9A). Both 3 and 6 month post-treated samples showed that Entrectinib reversed the expression pattern of these genes (Fig. 9B,C). Additionally, some genes that were up-regulated at the lower-dosage (3 months post-treatment) were down-regulated at the higher dosage, further emphasizing the anti-correlation of therapeutic effect to dosage.

A key regulator of synaptic plasticity that emerged from this study is the signaling protein retinoic acid receptor alpha encoded by the RARA gene. RARA has varied roles in the formation of neurons during embryonic development and later in neuronal function61–63.

During brain development, ZNF536 negatively regulates RARA activity that drives neuronal differentiation via transcription of neuron-specific genes8. Additionally, previous data also suggests that TSHZ3 may respond to RARA via a RARA binding domain during neurogenesis64. Post-development, RARA helps with maintaining synaptic homeostasis: (1) by driving BDNF-TrkB signaling for presynaptic and (2) promoting translation of AMPA receptors for postsynaptic homeostasis63,65. Because RARA was closely associated with both affected genes and signature pathways, we further explored whether Entrectinib affected RARA expression.

RARA was found to be significantly down-regulated in the patient compared to sibling control and Entrectinib normalized RARA levels in patient blood accompanied with normalization of retinoic acid receptor signaling activity and AMPA and BDNF signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. S13, S14). The up-regulation of RARA could therefore be a central biomarker of Entrectinib’s effect on the disease signature in blood.

Clinical outcomes: observed patient improvements following treatment

These results from patient blood transcriptomic profiling indicate that Entrectinib at low dose in the patient was able to reverse the disease molecular phenotype. However, it remained to be seen whether expression level changes translated to observable therapeutic outcomes and reversal of symptomatology.

To measure therapeutic changes post-treatment cognitive, behavioral, and developmental changes were monitored using virtual neuropsychology evaluations with a trained provider. Nineteen months after the pre-treatment evaluation (Methods), the clinician reported that the patient presented to be calmer, had dramatically improved communication with caregivers, and did not exhibit any frustration-related disruptive behaviors that had been observed during the baseline evaluation. This was in alignment with the observed reduction in challenging behaviors post-treatment by the parents.

Quantitatively, the patient’s DAYC-2 overall cognitive score was consistent with a 23 month old child, which was increased from the 17 month old cognitive level that was noted at the baseline evaluation (Supplementary Table S5). The patient’s cognitive developmental level had plateaued around that of a 2 year old child, however a total of 6 months of progress within 2 years is noteworthy compared to 17 months of progress made within her first 5 years, suggesting at least some acceleration in cognitive development post-treatment.

Significant qualitative improvements were also demonstrated by caregiver reported Vineland measures (Fig. 10). Raw scores for communication all showed growth, with remarkable improvement in written scores that nearly doubled post-treatment, as well as in daily living skills. For example, at the follow-up evaluation the patient was reportedly toilet trained, which had been a major challenge for caregivers. Additionally, the patient made an extraordinary 6 months worth of progress in her personal domain. Caregivers also reported changes in the child’s ability to navigate and function within the community and although assistance and supervision were still required, there was notable progress reported in her ability to communicate her needs and tolerate frustration. Socially, the patient made meaningful qualitative progress, i.e. engaging with other children outside of her parental circle.

Discussion

Development of high-throughput RNA-sequencing technologies like DRUG-seq can be leveraged to scale up drug discovery, focused on discovering novel mechanisms of action and targets for known and novel small molecules. This is uniquely suited but not limited to the challenge of drug discovery for rare diseases. We demonstrate how RNA-seq can be employed end-to-end; to generate target hypotheses, novel therapeutic opportunities for those targets and finally be used to track appropriate therapeutic endpoints post-treatment in a patient.

A patient, aged 5 years, diagnosed with a complex neurodevelopmental genetic disorder resulting in autism spectrum disorder and global developmental delay, was enrolled in an IRB approved research protocol to find new opportunities for available FDA approved drugs. Through a combination of genomics and transcriptomics of patient-derived cells we discovered that TSHZ3 and ZNF536, two transcription factors related to neurogenesis, were down-regulated in the patient. Consequently, neurons derived from the patient iPSCs showed a complex down-stream phenotype with over-activity of synaptic transmission and glutamatergic neurotransmitter transport pathways. Consistent with this, MEA recordings revealed that patient-derived neurons exhibited abnormal bursting dynamics, with more frequent and prolonged bursts despite unchanged overall firing rates—indicating a shift toward pathological excitability patterns rather than a simple increase in neuronal activity. These findings are in line with previous reports demonstrating that neurons carrying ASD-associated mutations also show aberrant excitability and bursting behavior67, supporting the notion that dysregulated excitability may represent a convergent mechanism across diverse neurodevelopmental disorders.

Together, these transcriptomic and electrophysiological data defined a patient-specific disease signature that provided a framework for therapeutic interrogation. Using this signature we identified a drug, Entrectinib, in a high-throughput drug screen in wild-type glutamatergic neurons that generated an opposing signature at low concentrations, potentially via deactivation of the SNARE complex that dampened neurotransmitter release to balance hyperexcitability. This outcome was antithetical to Entrectinib’s anti-cancer activity and resembled resistance-associated activity. This phenomenon is known as the “Paradoxical Drug Effect”, where a drug causes an effect opposite to its intended outcome, often observed at different concentrations57–60. In this study, Entrectinib, an anti-cancer drug, showed a paradoxical activation of signaling pathways at lower doses in the patient, unlike its anti-cancer activity at higher doses. These findings highlight the complex, underexplored polypharmacology that emerges when profiling global drug responses across a broad concentration range, including typically overlooked sub-nanomolar levels. Although low-concentration effects are weaker, they can still be significant when analyzed using inference-sharing across concentrations.

The patient was consequently prescribed low-dose Entrectinib under the supervision of a physician. The progress in the patient was tracked using whole blood RNA-sequencing pre- and post-treatment along with a control sample from an unaffected sibling. Pre-treatment, the genes involved in the disease signature pathways were down-regulated compared to the control sample but normalized post-treatment within 6 months to levels observed in the control sample.

This study reveals a potential path to approaching discovery of novel therapeutic opportunities by leveraging high-throughput RNA-seq to profile drugs at scale in a model system and identifying these opportunities based on normalization of a disease signature as target. Using a disease profile generated as a set of dysregulated pathways or genes allows us to transfer this disease fingerprint into other related wild-type systems for screening. This obviates the need for costly and technically challenging compound screens directly in disease-specific cellular models and allows for screening data to be more widely applicable.

While the current study is limited to characterizing Entrectinib for 19q12 ASD, it is possible that this molecule may be therapeutic for other types of ASD associated with hyperexcitability. Further studies are needed to understand whether Entrectinib could be more universally therapeutic in other forms of ASD.

Methods

Patient cell model generation

iPSC generation

All patient cells described in this section were collected with consent from the participant or the legal guardian of the participant in accordance with protocols reviewed and approved by the WGC Institutional Review Board (IRB) (WCG IRB protocol #2001-01). Patient fibroblasts were collected via skin punch biopsy performed by a physician. The samples were then sent to Sampled (Piscataway, NJ) for isolation and cryopreservation of fibroblasts.

Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) were generated from a patient-derived fibroblast line and a related parental control line (JP0000013 and JP0000009, respectively) reprogrammed using the StemRNA 3rd Generation Reprogramming kit (REPROCELL Inc). 6 clones from each cell line were picked and expanded; 3 clones for each cell line (JP9.4, JP9.5, JP9.6, JP13.1, JP13.2 and JP13.3) were characterized via G-banding for karyotyping and immunofluorescent staining of two pluripotency markers.

NPC generation and expansion

NPCs were generated at the Stem Cell Core at Columbia University (New York, NY). Neural progenitor cells (NPCs) were generated from (iPSCs from both healthy parent and affected child using a modified 2D cortical glutamatergic protocol based on the STEMdiff neural system (STEMdiff™ SMADi Neural Induction Kit, Stem Cell Technologies). iPSCs were plated in a 96-well spheroid plate and treated with neural induction media containing SMAD inhibitors (SMADi) and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF, 5ng/ml). On day 5, embryoid bodies (EBs) were replated onto Matrigel-coated plates. Over the next several days, media was gradually switched to N2/B27 media without Vitamin A ((DMEM/F12 (Gibco 11330032), Neurobasal medium (Gibco 21103-049), N2-B supplement (Stem Cell Technologies), B27 supplement without Vitamin A (Gibco 12587-010), Glutamine (Gibco), Non-Essential Amino Acids (Gibco 11140-050), and beta-Mercaptoethanol (Gibco)), supplemented with SMADi (LDN193189 (250nM), SB431542 (10 µM)), and ascorbic acid (200 µM). Neural rosettes formed and were selectively replated on day 12 onto Matrigel-coated plates following trypsinization. Cells were cultured for another 4 days in N2/B27 media without Vitamin A and cryopreserved on day 16. Neural progenitor cells (NPCs) were thawed and expanded in T75 flasks coated with poly-l-ornithine and laminin, using STEMdiff™ Neural Progenitor Medium (Stem Cell Technologies). Cells were maintained with full media changes every 2–3 days until they reached 70–80% confluence for further passaging or experimentation.

Neuron maturation

NPCs were differentiated into neurons using BrainPhys™ Neuronal Medium (StemCell Technologies). NPCs were plated on poly-l-ornithine and laminin-coated plates at a density of 1.25 × 105 cells/cm2. The medium was switched to BrainPhys™ medium supplemented with N2, Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF, 20 ng/mL), Glial-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (GDNF, 20 ng/mL), dibutyryl-cAMP (500 µM), and ascorbic acid (200 µM). Media changes were performed every 2–3 days, and cells were maintained for 14–20 days to promote neuronal maturation.

Astrocyte generation

Neural progenitor cells (parent and patient NPCs) were plated on Geltrex™-coated flasks (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A1413302) and differentiated into astrocyte precursors using StemDiff™ Astrocyte Differentiation Medium (StemCell Technologies, 100-0013) supplemented with the manufacturer’s supplement kit for 10 days. During this period, cells were passaged twice and either cryopreserved as astrocyte precursors or transferred into StemDiff™ Astrocyte Maturation Medium (StemCell Technologies, 100 − 0015) supplemented with components A and B for an additional four passages to promote astrocytic maturation. Cultures were maintained on Geltrex-coated flasks with medium changes every 2–3 days. Mature astrocytes were cryopreserved in Neural Progenitor Freezing Medium (StemCell Technologies, cat. no. 05891) for subsequent use in co-culture experiments.

Molecular and cellular assays

Multi-electrode array (MEA) measurements

Forty-eight–well MEA plates (Axion Biosystem, CytoView MEA 48, M768-tMEA-48B) were coated with poly-L-ornithine (2 h at 37 °C), washed four times with sterile water, and air dried under sterile conditions. Plates were subsequently coated with laminin (10 µg/mL) the day before plating. Neural progenitor cells (parent and patient NPCs) were mixed with astrocytes (parent and patient, repsectively) at a ratio of 6:1 (60,000 NPCs : 10,000 astrocytes per well). Cells were centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, resuspended in medium containing laminin (0.1 µg/mL), and spot-seeded onto MEA plates. Cultures were maintained for 21 days in BrainPhys™ Neuronal Medium (StemCell Technologies), with media changes performed every 3–4 days. Prior to each recording session, plates were equilibrated in the MEA instrument for 1 h to stabilize culture conditions.

Imaging

Immunocytochemistry was performed on 3 96-well PerkinElmer Phenoplates seeded with neurons. Neuronal induction was performed by seeding NPCs in the 3 plates with BrainPhys Complete Neuronal media (StemCell, cat# 05790). All plates were split between parent and patient cell lines, and further split into two seeding densities, 30k and 40k cells/well. Plates were fixed with 4% Paraformaldehyde (Thermo Scientific, cat# 043368-9 M) for 15 min followed by 3× washes with DPBS (Gibco cat# 14190144) on days 1, 7 and 14. Cells were permeabilized with 0.25% TritonX-100 solution (Thermo Scientific, cat# 85111) diluted in DPBS for 15 min prior to blocking and staining. All plates were stained with DAPI and the proliferation marker Ki67 (Thermo Scientific, cat# MA5-14520). High Protein Blocking Buffer (eBioscience, Thermo Scientific cat# 00-4952-54) was added prior to primary antibody staining for 45 min, followed by primary antibody solution diluted (1:500) in blocking buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following morning, the primary antibody solution was removed and 2 washes were performed with DPBS. Secondary antibody (Thermo Scientific) solution was diluted in Low Protein Blocking Buffer (eBioscience, Thermo Scientific cat# 00-4953-54, 1:2000 dilution) and incubated at room temperature for 1 h protected from light. Two washes with DPBS were performed, followed by a brief incubation with DAPI (Thermo Scientific cat# D1306, 1:10,000 dilution) for 5 min at room temperature. A final DPBS wash was performed followed by a wash with sterile distilled water.

Plates were imaged using the PerkinElmer Opera Phenix confocal microscope at 20x magnification. Analysis was performed in PerkinElmer Harmony Image Analysis software (v5.1) via integration of manually trained cell masks to determine live/dead cell status through fluorescence intensity, nuclear shape, size and roundness. The program was trained on 5 distinct, randomly selected well sections. Live cell fraction (LCF) was then calculated by determining the proportion of live to dead nuclei. Ki67 + nuclei were identified, quantified and compared to overall nuclei number for each timepoint.

Bulk RNA-sequencing

Sequencing of patient neurons

RNA extraction was performed using Qiagen RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat: 74134). RNA quality and purity was checked by testing 2 uL of RNA on a High Sensitivity RNA screentape (Agilent Tapestation). RNA concentration was quantified using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Invitrogen). Bulk RNA libraries were prepared from 200 ng of RNA input using Illumina Stranded Total RNA Prep with Ribo-Zero Plus kit. RNA library quality was checked by running 1uL of each library on a High Sensitivity D1000 screentape (Agilent Tapestation), and concentration was quantified by qPCR using a KAPA Universal Illumina library quantification kit (Roche). Samples were sequenced in batches of 8 or less, on a NextSeq 1000 using the standard loading protocol for a P2 200 cycle sequencing cartridge.

Whole blood RNAseq

Patient data collection was performed in accordance with protocols reviewed and approved by the WGC Institutional Review Board (IRB) (WCG IRB protocol #2001-01). All data was collected with consent from the legal guardians of the patient.

Whole blood samples were collected approximately 3 and 6 months after treatment started with Entrectinib along with a pre-treatment sample (Table 3). Blood samples were collected < = 24 h after dosing. Blood was collected in PAXgene tubes and shipped at room temperature to the sequencing facility (Genewiz, NJ) for processing within 24 h for RNA sequencing. The RNA was extracted using the PAXgene RNA extraction kit. Barcoded libraries for RNA-seq were prepared with 100 ng of RNA using NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, Ipswich, MA) for Illumina with QIAGEN QIAseq FastSelect -rRNA HMR Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) per manufacturer’s protocol. Paired-end sequencing (150 bp x 2) was performed on NovaSeq X Plus sequencers (Illumina, San Diego, CA).

DRUG-seq

NGN2-driven neuronal model

10 cm dishes for NGN2-induced neural differentiation were coated with Matrigel according to the manufacturer’s manual and incubated overnight at 37 °C. On the day of induction, iPSCs were removed using Accutase (StemCell, Cat# 07920), centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min and resuspended in NGN2 Neural Induction Media containing 10 µM Y-27,632 (StemCell, Cat# 72304). NGN2 Neural Induction Media was made using a base of Knockout DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Cat# 12660012) with the addition of 1x N2 Supplement-B (StemCell, Cat# 07156), 1x MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids (Gibco, Cat# 11140050), 1 µg/mL Natural Mouse Laminin (1 mg/mL stock Life Technologies, Cat# 23017-015), 10 ng/mL NT-3 in sterile distilled water (StemCell, Cat# 78074), 10 ng/mL BDNF in sterile distilled water (StemCell, Cat# 78133) and 2 µg/mL doxycycline (stock concentration 20 mg/mL, Fisher Scientific, Cat# AAJ67043AD). Cells were counted using Trypan Blue (Invitrogen, Cat# T10282) on the Invitrogen Countess and plated at 5 × 106 cells/10 cm dish. Daily media changes were performed on days 2–3 with NGN2 Neural Induction Medium.

One day prior to replating cells for neuronal maturation, two 384-well PerkinElmer Pheno Plates were coated with 0.1 mg/mL Poly-D-Lysine (Gibco, cat# A3890401) at 37 °C overnight. The next morning, the plates were washed 3x with sterile water and dried under a BSC with the fan turned on. At least 2 h prior to replating, 10 µg/mL Natural Mouse Laminin (Gibco, Cat# 23017015) diluted in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Cat#11320033) was added to the plates and returned to a 37 °C incubator.

On day 4, cells were replated at 20,000 cells/well in 384-well plates (PerkinElmer Phenoplates) for neuronal maturation in NGN2 Neuronal Medium containing 2 µg/mL doxycycline (Fisher Scientific, Cat# AAJ67043AD) and 5 µM of U-FdU. NGN2 Neuronal Medium was made using 1:1 DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Cat#11320033) and Neurobasal-A Medium (Gibco, Cat# 10888022) as the base with the addition of 1× MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids (Gibco, Cat# 11140050), 0.5× GlutaMAX Supplement (Gibco, Cat# 35050061), 1× N2 Supplement-B (StemCell, Cat# 07156), 1× B-27 Plus Supplement (Gibco, Cat# A3582801), 10 ng/mL NT-3 (StemCell, Cat# 78074), 10 ng/mL BDNF (StemCell, Cat# 78133) and 1 µg/mL Natural Mouse Laminin (Gibco, Cat# 23017015). Half media changes were performed every 3–4 days from Day 7-Day 15 using NGN2 Neuronal Medium.

Neurons were imaged using an Incucyte for quality control purposes prior to drug treatment using a basic confluence analysis program, followed by a Neurotrack program to identify cell body clusters and neurites.

Drug treatment

Neurons were treated with a selection of FDA-approved small molecule compounds added at one-third decreasing dose for 10 doses: 10 µM, 3.33 µM, 1.11 µM, 0.37 µM, 0.12 µM, 40 nM, 14 nM, 5 nM, 2 nM, and 0.5 nM in quadruplicate. Additionally, a total of 16 wells were treated with DMSO as control on each 384-well plate. Compounds and DMSO controls were dispensed directly into the 384-well cell culture plates using an Echo 650 Liquid Handler (Beckman Coulter). Cells were held at 37 °C for 72 h following drug treatment. Following drug treatment, one of the two plates was used for RNA sequencing and the second was used for live cell imaging (Supplementary Methods).

Sequencing

Libraries were prepared using a customized version of the DRUG-seq protocol11,12. Briefly, cell culture media was aspirated using a STARlet liquid handler (Hamilton) and lysis buffer was dispensed directly into each well with a Multidrop Combi Reagent Dispenser (ThermoFisher Scientific) and placed on a shaker for 15 min at 900 rpm. Cell lysate was transferred into 384-well PCR plates and reverse transcription (RT) mix was dispensed into each well. Using an Echo 650 Liquid Handler (Beckman Coulter), barcoded RT primers were dispensed into each well. Plates were incubated at 42 °C for 2 h. Following RT, each well was pooled into a single tube and purified using DNA Clean & Concentrator-100 (Zymo Research) followed by concentration with Agencourt RNAClean XP beads (Beckman Coulter). Single-stranded DNA was removed by incubating cDNA with Exonuclease I (New England Biolabs) followed by pre-amplification of the cDNA with DRUG-seq PCR primers. cDNA was fragmented and tagged with Illumina sequencing adapters using Tagmentation DNA Enzyme (TDE1). A final PCR was performed to amplify the libraries and add P7, P5, and index sequences. Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina).

Data analysis

Statistical analysis for molecular and cellular assays

Data are presented as mean ± SD, or mean ± SEM. All statistical analyses (excluding sequencing, see below for Data Analysis) in the present study were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Significance was calculated with one-way or two-way ANOVA with post hoc tests. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and the number of asterisks represented the degree of significance.

Bulk RNA-seq

Bulk RNA-seq paired fastq files were aligned using Kallisto to the human GRCh38 assembly68. Differential expression analysis (DEA) was performed using the R Sleuth package to obtain fold change values by comparing a test group to a control group69. Pathway analysis was performed based on fold change values from Sleuth for a control and test comparison using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using the R ssGSEA package70.

Significance testing of aggregated measures

In certain cases pathway enrichment scores were aggregated across timepoints or groups. A combined p-value for aggregated fold changes and pathway enrichment scores was calculated using the Fisher test for the individual p-values across multiple timepoints and concentrations. These p-values were corrected for false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

Generation of co-expression networks

Pathway enrichment scores across 5 maturation timepoints (iPSCs, NPCs, days 7–20 neurons) were used to build co-expression networks using the igraph python package. Pairwise correlation between pathway normalized enrichment scores across the profiled timepoints was computed by weighted combination of pairwise Pearson and cosine correlation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:weighted\:correlation\:{pathways}_{i,j}\:=\:{w}_{pearson}\times\:{Pearson}_{i,j}+{w}_{cosine}\times\:{cosine}_{i,j},$$\end{document} where wpearson and wcosine are the weights applied to each correlation measure. Both weights were set to 0.5. A combination of both correlation measures ensures a hybrid measure that accounts for both co-regulation of magnitude (via Pearson correlation) and the co-regulation of direction (via cosine correlation).

These weighted pairwise correlations were used to build a network where the edge weights correspond to the correlation and pathways are nodes connected by these edges. An edge cutoff of 0.9 was used to prune edges with magnitude of correlation below 0.9 yielding only highly correlated pathways. Module detection of nodes in the network was performed using the greedy modularity algorithm71.

DRUG-seq

The fastq file for the compound treated 384-well plate was aligned and demultiplexed into wells using Kallisto and Bustools for fast quantification of transcripts68,72. Transcript-level counts were collapsed into gene-level counts for further analysis. Reads were mapped to the human GRCh38 assembly and transcript to gene mapping was performed using gtf version 107.

Gene-level fold changes were calculated using pyDESeq2 for differential expression analysis (DEA) comparing wells treated with Entrectinib pairwise at each concentration to DMSO-vehicle control wells73,74. Prior to the DEA, each quadruplicate set of sample wells at each concentration and the 16 DMSO control wells were checked for quality. Wells with poor sequencing quality due to low sequencing depth (for example, due to cytotoxicity) were removed. Additionally, any DMSO wells that were dissimilar to the ensemble distribution were removed. This sought to exclude any “bad” DMSO wells that deviated from the population and could artificially inflate the number of DE genes. Finally, a number of genes were also removed from the DEA analysis that were detected sparsely throughout the passing DMSO and compound-treated wells (Supplementary Information methods).

Pathway analysis was performed using the fold change values from pyDESeq2 using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)70. To accommodate multiple concentrations a modified GSEA approach was utilized. Briefly, gene-level fold changes were ranked for a full concentration series for Entrectinib. An enrichment score was then calculated for each gene-concentration combination using published procedures for GSEA.

Patient recruitment and ethics

The patient, a 5 year old female with ASD, was enrolled following a self-referral. The patient’s legal guardians, actively seeking novel therapeutic strategies, initiated contact with our research team in 2022. The research team consisted of researchers from Transcripta Bio, independent scientific council, patient legal guardians and physicians. Following comprehensive discussions and a review of the experimental protocol, the guardians provided written informed consent for all study procedures. The study protocol received full approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) (WCG IRB Protocol #2001-01).

Clinical evaluation

The patient had two neuro-psychology evaluations, one on 6-18-2023 and another on 1-24-2025. The patient was ~ 5 years 11 months at her first evaluation and 7 years 7 months during the second. Both evaluations were additionally performed at the same time of day to minimize any differences due to time of testing.

At the evaluations, two tests were administered, the DAYC-2 (Developmental Assessment of Young Children, Second Edition) for cognitive functioning and caregiver reported Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales (VABS) for adaptive functioning. All assessments were conducted by a single, licensed pediatric neuropsychologist who was trained and certified in its administration. The patient’s primary clinical challenges were in adaptive functioning, specifically, communication and daily living skills. The VABS-II is the gold standard for measuring these exact behaviors. Our therapeutic goal was to improve real-world functional abilities, which VABS is designed to capture. Unlike diagnostic instruments, which measure stable, core autistic traits and are less sensitive to short-term behavioral changes, adaptive behavior scales like the Vineland are well-suited for measuring functional gains over the course of an intervention.

The DAYC-2 is a measure of developmental functioning in young children. Information is obtained through direct observation/assessment as well as caregiver report. It helps identify developmental delays, determine strengths, and track progress over time. To understand functional skills VABS-3 was administered. This is a measure based on parental report and is a standardized measure of adaptive behavior - the things that people do to function in their everyday lives.

In addition to the aforementioned physician-performed evaluations, daily evaluations were also conducted by trained teaching staff at the patient’s school.
