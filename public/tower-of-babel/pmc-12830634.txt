Finite element analysis of flexural behavior in ultra-high-strength concrete beams with BFRP and hybrid BFRP-steel reinforcement
Authors: Nageh M Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThree-dimensional finite-element (FE) simulations were developed to assess the flexural response of ultra-high-strength concrete (UHSC) beams reinforced with basalt-FRP (BFRP) bars and with BFRP–steel hybrids. The models were calibrated against published tests and reproduced midspan force–deflection curves, reinforcement strains, and crack development with good fidelity. A parametric study examined (i) the BFRP reinforcement ratio relative to a balanced level and (ii) the BFRP-to-steel area ratio in hybrid sections. Results show that increasing the BFRP ratio raises ultimate flexural capacity but reduces deformability and energy dissipation. Hybrid reinforcement recovers inelastic reserve while preserving the durability advantages of FRP, yielding a more favorable balance between strength and serviceability. A yield-anchored modified ductility index tailored for hybrid sections is formulated to quantify post-yield rotational capacity. The findings provide design guidance for proportioning UHSC beams in conventional and aggressive environments: BFRP reinforcement ratios of ρf/ρfb ≥ 1.4 are suitable where strength and durability dominate, whereas hybrid layouts are recommended when controlled deflection and ductility are also required.Keywords: Ultra-high-strength concrete (UHSC), Hybrid steel-FRP reinforcement, Finite element modeling (FEM), Flexural behavior, Ductility index.Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Fiber-reinforced polymer (FRP) bars are increasingly used as a substitute for steel in reinforced-concrete members because they are immune to corrosion, possess high specific tensile strength, and perform well in harsh environments1–5. FRP reinforcement is being actively explored for application in deep beams, compression members (CFFTs), and beam–column joints, as well as in recycled and seawater–sea-sand concretes6–10. Among available FRP types, basalt FRP (BFRP) offers an attractive compromise: it provides better chemical stability than E-glass FRP at a comparable cost, and it is substantially less expensive than carbon FRP while maintaining satisfactory mechanical capacity11,12. A recognized drawback, however, is the essentially linear-elastic response up to rupture and the comparatively low modulus, which can result in larger service deflections and crack widths and a sudden, non-ductile failure mode.

Material-level strategies to mitigate this brittleness include hybrid rebars that combine different FRP types or incorporate an FRP jacket over a steel core13–15. These composite bars can deliver higher stiffness, improved post-yield behavior, and steel corrosion protection, while FRP–FRP hybrids can be engineered for pseudo-ductility to delay catastrophic failure.

Ultra-high-strength concrete (UHSC) has also gained prominence for its exceptional compressive strength, enhanced durability, and dense microstructure16,17, making it attractive for long-span bridges, high-rise buildings, and infrastructure in severe environments. However, UHSC’s inherent brittleness often requires large volumes of steel reinforcement, leading to congestion, construction difficulties, and honeycombing risk. Integrating FRP reinforcement into UHSC can enhance corrosion resistance and extend service life, yet UHSC’s high stiffness and strength significantly influence cracking, serviceability, and reinforcement–concrete interaction.

Despite extensive research on FRP-reinforced beams and on UHSC performance separately, there is limited understanding of how BFRP reinforcement ratio and hybridization with steel affect the flexural response, serviceability, and ductility of UHSC beams. Addressing this gap is essential for developing design approaches that exploit UHSC’s material advantages while overcoming its brittleness through advanced reinforcement strategies.

A common means of improving the behavior of members reinforced with FRP bars is to increase the BFRP reinforcement ratio—consistent with ACI 440.1R guidance—to restrain deflection and cracking, although serviceability often remains the governing design limit18. At the section level, hybrid reinforcement that combines FRP and steel exploits the complementary benefits of both materials: steel contributes stiffness and ductility, whereas FRP provides corrosion resistance and high tensile strength19,20. Experimental and analytical studies report that such hybrid RC beams exhibit higher post-cracking stiffness, improved deformability, and better crack control than beams reinforced only with FRP bars21–23.

Ductility is a key performance metric for structural safety and resilience. In conventional steel-reinforced concrete beams, it is commonly quantified as the deformation at ultimate capacity normalized by the deformation at first yield of the steel reinforcement24, yielding a non-dimensional measure that may be expressed in terms of curvature, rotation, or displacement. FRP RC beams, lacking a yield point, require alternative indices such as energy-based measures, deformation ratios at specific loads, or pseudo-yield points from bilinear approximations3. In hybrid FRP–steel beams, the steel yields first, enabling the use of conventional ductility indices while capturing FRP’s role in sustaining loads beyond yield. Such indices are critical for assessing serviceability, energy dissipation, and failure warning capacity in both FRP-only and hybrid designs.

Finite-element (FE) models were developed to evaluate the bending response of ultra-high-strength concrete beams reinforced with BFRP bars and with BFRP–steel hybrids. The parametric program assessed the influence of BFRP reinforcement ratio, the extent of steel substitution, and reinforcement configuration on flexural capacity and ductility, providing guidance for the design of durable high-performance members for both conventional and corrosive environments.

Finite element modeling

Three-dimensional finite-element (FE) models (Fig. 1) were developed for UHSC beams reinforced with BFRP bars, with and without partial steel substitution, to quantify the effects of reinforcement ratio and BFRP–steel hybridization. The numerical framework was verified against published test data. Simulations were executed in ATENA 3D Engineering v5.925, using element formulations, constitutive laws, and mesh densities selected to reproduce the governing response mechanisms. To reduce computational cost, only half of each beam was modeled by exploiting geometric symmetry; the symmetry plane was restrained against out-of-plane motion (ux = 0). A roller support was modeled by applying a vertical displacement constraint (uz=0) along the midline of the support steel plate to allow for beam-end rotation, rather than constraining the entire surface. The load was applied vertically (z-axis) with the displacement control mode at the nodes located in the middle of the loading steel plates, corresponding to the experimental loading points. This approach was selected because it has been shown in the literature26,27 to better reproduce the complete load-deflection behavior, including the post-peak softening response, compared to force-controlled methods. This is particularly important for capturing the failure mechanisms in both ductile (steel-reinforced) and brittle (FRP-reinforced) beams. The specific magnitude of the prescribed displacement was not a pre-defined single value but was applied incrementally until structural failure occurred. This is inherent to the nonlinear analysis procedure employed a Newton–Raphson solution scheme with convergence checked at each displacement increment. Elements in both the concrete and steel meshes were set to a nominal size of 20–25 mm.

Concrete modeling

In this study, concrete was modeled using the 20-node isoparametric brick element, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This element is a higher-order, three-dimensional finite element designed to model complex stress states in nonlinear concrete structures. It employs Gaussian integration to compute stiffness and internal force matrices with high numerical accuracy. Typically, a 3 × 3 × 3 integration scheme (27 Gauss points) is used, which is particularly effective for nonlinear analyses involving advanced material models. This integration approach ensures accurate capture of stress and strain distributions within the element, especially in regions with steep gradients due to cracking, crushing, or other localized nonlinearities28. The nonlinear behavior of concrete was simulated using the internal material model named “CC3DNonLinCementitious2”, which is part of the ATENA Engineering software suite developed by Cervenka Consulting. This constitutive model combines both fracture mechanics and plasticity theory to capture the complex nonlinear behavior of concrete underloading. The fracture part of the model is based on a smeared crack approach utilizing the Rankine failure criterion to determine crack initiation. For modeling concrete crushing under compressive stresses, plastic behavior is represented using the Menétrey-Willam failure surface, which is a three-parameter failure criterion particularly suited for concrete and similar quasi-brittle materials29. This fracture-plastic approach allows simultaneous simulation of tensile cracking and compressive crushing in a unified framework. The shear behavior of cracked concrete is modeled using the Modified Compression Field Theory (MCFT), originally proposed by Vecchio and Collins30. This approach accounts for the interaction between the normal and shear stresses in cracked regions and allows for realistic modeling of post-cracking shear resistance. In addition, the model considers compressive strength reduction in directions parallel to crack planes, reflecting the degradation of material properties after cracking. Another significant feature of the CC3DNonLinCementitious2 model is the inclusion of tension stiffening, which represents the contribution of the concrete between cracks to the overall stiffness of reinforced concrete members. This effect is critical for capturing the post-cracking load-deformation behavior, particularly in tension-dominated regions.

Reinforcement modeling

In this study, reinforcement bars, including longitudinal reinforcement and stirrups, were modeled using discrete truss (1D bar) elements embedded within the three-dimensional concrete elements. This discrete approach is advantageous for accurately capturing the mechanical response of individual reinforcing elements, especially in regions with concentrated stresses such as beam steps, load introduction zones, and supports. FRP and steel reinforcements were assumed to be perfectly bonded to the concrete, with no slip at the interface28. The truss element used in ATENA is a one-dimensional finite element designed to carry axial forces only, without bending or shear deformation. Each element connects two nodes and simulates axial tension or compression based on the defined cross-sectional area and constitutive law. This makes the truss element particularly suitable for modeling both steel and FRP reinforcement, which primarily act in tension or compression in reinforced concrete members.

For steel reinforcement, a bilinear elasto-plastic material model with strain hardening was used. The stress-strain relationship was defined by the modulus of elasticity (Es), yield strength (fy), and hardening modulus, reflecting the steel’s post-yield behavior. Default hardening parameters from ATENA’s material database were used unless experimental data were available. This model permits stress to increase after yielding, effectively simulating the ductile behavior of conventional steel reinforcement31. For FRP rebars, a linear elastic–brittle material model was adopted, as FRP materials exhibit no yielding and fail suddenly once their ultimate tensile strength is reached. The constitutive behavior was defined by the modulus of elasticity (Ef) and the ultimate (ffu) tensile strength, based on manufacturer data or established literature. In ATENA, this behavior can be modeled using a linear stress-strain law with failure at a prescribed strain limit. Since FRP is anisotropic and generally brittle, no strain hardening was included.

Modeling of loading and support plates

In the finite element model, both the support and loading plates were represented using steel plates with a width of 50 mm, extending across the full width of the beam to ensure uniform stress distribution. To allow beam-end rotation and better reflect actual structural behavior, vertical displacement constraints were applied only along the midline of the support plate, rather than across its entire surface. The loading plate was modeled similarly, with prescribed vertical displacement applied at its center to simulate the experimental loading conditions. Both plates were assigned an elastic modulus of 200 GPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.3, corresponding to typical structural steel. The interfaces between the plates and the concrete beam were not assumed to be perfectly bonded. Instead, contact elements were introduced to simulate potential sliding. A frictional contact model was employed with a normal stiffness of 5 N/mm3 and a coefficient of friction of 0.4, representing common steel–concrete interface conditions32,33. This modeling strategy allowed for relative slip under load while maintaining numerical stability and physical realism.

Parametric study

The primary objective of this study is to optimize the flexural performance of UHSC beams through longitudinal-reinforcement hybridization. Using a validated finite-element (FE) model, the author conducted a comprehensive parametric study to identify effective configurations of longitudinal tensile reinforcement for UHSC members. The analysis focuses on tensile-reinforcement variables-namely the FRP reinforcement ratio (ρf) and the basalt-FRP-to-steel hybridization ratio (Af/As). Transverse reinforcement is kept constant (to satisfy shear capacity) and is not treated as a study parameter. In the shear spans, 8 mm steel stirrups at 80 mm spacing were used in all beams. Two 12 mm steel bars were kept constant as the top (compression) reinforcement, while the bottom (tension) reinforcement varied by specimen. All specimens had a total length of 2500 mm, a clear span of 2100 mm, and a rectangular cross-section of 150 mm (width) × 250 mm (depth). Beams were tested in four-point bending with a shear span of a = 700 mm. The concrete compressive strength was set to f’c = 113 MPa, corresponding to the reference beam B2-NF from the validated experimental program of Hasgul et al.34. The BFRP bars were modeled with a modulus of 48 GPa and an ultimate tensile strength of 1100 MPa, consistent with the material properties reported by Hussein et al.11. Steel reinforcement, where present, was assigned a yield strength of 462 MPa and an elastic modulus of 200 GPa, as characterized in Hasgul et al.34.

As shown in Fig. 5a, FRP-reinforced UHSC beams exhibited a bilinear load-deflection response comparable to that of beams with normal-strength concrete. The response includes a linear elastic segment before cracking, followed by a segment of reduced stiffness after cracking. The pre-cracking behavior was nearly identical across all beams. Once cracking occurred, a sudden deflection jump appeared—less pronounced with higher reinforcement ratios—followed by a continued increase in deflection at reduced stiffness.

Figure 5b illustrates that increasing ρf/ρfb leads to higher ultimate load capacity, although the rate of increase diminishes beyond the balanced ratio. For over-reinforced beams, increasing ρf/ρfb from 1.4 to 2.0 (a 43% increase) resulted in a 17% gain in ultimate load. However, a further increase of 50% (from 2.0 to 3.0) led to only a 20.8% gain. In contrast, for under-reinforced beams, increasing ρf/ρfb from 0.6 to 0.8—a relatively modest 33.3% increase—produced a substantial 28% improvement in ultimate load. These findings highlight the more efficient reinforcement utilization in under-reinforced beams, where the structural response is more sensitive to changes in reinforcement ratio. This diminishing return in strength suggests that an upper limit for the reinforcement ratio should be established to optimize material usage and cost efficiency. FRP stress further reinforces this conclusion. For ρf/ρfb = 1.4, the FRP bars reached 92% of their tensile capacity, while for ρf/ρfb = 3.0, only 60% was utilized, indicating underuse of the material at higher ratios.

In terms of deflection behavior, increasing the reinforcement ratio above the balanced value reduced the maximum deflection, as shown in Fig. 5b. This trend aligns with traditional behavior observed in steel-reinforced concrete sections.

The slope of the post-cracking portion of the load–deflection curve—referred to as post-cracking stiffness—is clearly influenced by the reinforcement ratio (Fig. 5a and c). This stiffness increases almost linearly with ρf/ρfb. Compared to the reference beam (ρf/ρfb = 1.4), the post-cracking stiffness increased by 78% for the ρf/ρfb = 3.0 beam, while it decreased by 48% for the ρf/ρfb = 0.6 beam.

In this study, the service load was defined as the load corresponding to a mid-span deflection reaching the ACI-prescribed limit of span/360. Based on this criterion, the service load demonstrated a strong dependency on the reinforcement ratio (Fig. 5c and d), increasing nearly linearly with ρf/ρfb. Compared to the reference beam, the service loads of the ρf/ρfb = 0.6 and 0.8 beams were 25% and 16% lower, respectively, while those of the ρf/ρfb = 2.0 and 3.0 beams were 19% and 37% higher, respectively. It is also worth noting that, for the over-reinforced beams investigated in this study, the service load did not exceed 18% of their ultimate load, while for the under-reinforced beams, it remained below 27%. These values are noticeably lower than the typical ranges reported in the literature, which estimate service loads to fall between 25% and 35% of the nominal strength4,39. These results emphasize that BFRP-reinforced beam design is largely governed by serviceability rather than ultimate strength. Excessive deflections are a common issue in practice, particularly in steel-reinforced members. If conditions allow, introducing an initial camber during construction can help mitigate excessive deflections under service loads38.

BFRP-steel hybridization ratio

A promising approach to mitigate the brittle failure of FRP–UHSC flexural members is the use of hybrid tensile reinforcement, which combines steel and FRP bars. Hybrid reinforcement leverages the complementary properties of both materials, addressing the limited ductility of FRP bars while enhancing beam ductility and stiffness through the superior plastic behavior of steel. Figure 6 shows that hybrid-reinforced sections exhibit a tri-linear load–deflection response, characterized by three distinct stages: cracking, steel yielding, and either concrete crushing or FRP rupture, depending on the reinforcement configuration. This behavior differs from the typical bi-linear response observed in purely FRP-reinforced beams. At low hybridization ratios (Af/As =0.25 to 0.5 Fig. 6a), the response is relatively ductile, with significant post-yield deformation before failure. However, as the FRP content increases beyond this range, the load capacity after the peak drops more sharply, indicating a more brittle failure mode. For higher hybridization ratios (Af/As = 1 to 4 Fig. 6b), the beams demonstrate increased ultimate load capacity but decreased post-peak ductility. The curves become stiffer, and the failure occurs more abruptly, consistent with the linear-elastic behavior of FRP up to rupture. Although ρsf, f exceeded ρf, b for all the tested beams, some specimens failed by steel yielding followed by FRP rupture. This occurred specifically in beams where ρsf, f was less than 1.4 times ρf, b as confirmed by the maximum FRP stress values reported in Table 5. In all beams, steel yielding was observed, as the condition ρsf, s ≤ ρs, b was satisfied. Notably, Lei Pang et al.39 recommended two reinforcement conditions to ensure a ductile failure mode: ρsf, s ≤ 0.75ρs, b and ρsf, f ≥ 1.4ρf, b.

At the service deflection limit (span/360), increasing the hybrid reinforcement ratio Af/As leads to a noticeable improvement in beam stiffness, thereby enhancing the service load capacity, as illustrated in Fig. 7. For example, at the allowable deflection limit, the service load increases from 40.5 kN for the fully FRP-reinforced beam (B-F) to 74.3 kN for the fully steel-reinforced beam (B-S), confirming the beneficial effect of higher steel content on flexural stiffness under service conditions.

Influence of BFRP-to-Steel Hybrid reinforcement ratio on beam behavior under service load conditions.

Figure 8 illustrates the variation of ultimate load, yield load, and service load with respect to the BFRP-to-steel hybrid reinforcement ratio (Af/As). The figure reveals that the rate of change in all three load parameters is more pronounced for Af/As less than 1.0, while the curves tend to flatten beyond Af/As of approximately 2.0. This indicates that the reinforcement ratio has a greater influence on structural behavior in the under-reinforced range (i.e., steel-dominated configurations), where even modest changes in Af/As result in significant improvements in yield and service loads.

Variation of Service, Yield, and Ultimate Loads with BFRP-to-Steel Hybrid Reinforcement Ratio.

Decreasing the hybrid reinforcement ratio (Af/As) below 1.0 leads to a notable increase in both the service load and yield load, primarily due to the higher proportion of ductile steel reinforcement. However, this adjustment also results in a slight reduction in ultimate load capacity, as FRP—despite its brittle nature—contributes significantly to the load-bearing capacity at higher stress levels. These findings suggest that maximizing ultimate load alone may not be an optimal design strategy, especially if it compromises serviceability and ductility. Instead, a more desirable approach involves achieving higher service and yield loads with a moderate ultimate capacity and a ductile failure mode. Ahmed Hussain et al. reported that the design of hybrid reinforced concrete (HRC) beams is predominantly governed by serviceability limits due to the low axial stiffness of BFRP bars. Based on both experimental and numerical results, they concluded that the hybrid reinforcement ratio (Af/As) should be maintained below 1.9 to satisfy service deflection requirements.

As shown in Fig. 8, increasing the hybrid reinforcement ratio (Af/As) enhances the ultimate load capacity, which gradually approaches the capacity of the fully FRP-reinforced beam (B-F) as the ratio exceeds 2.0. However, this marginal gain in strength beyond Af/As = 2.0 indicates a diminishing return, making such configurations structurally excessive and economically inefficient. This trend highlights the contribution of FRP bars to improving flexural strength at the ultimate limit state.

Conversely, both the service load and yield load decrease with increasing Af/As, due to the reduction in steel content, which plays a critical role in controlling stiffness and yielding behavior under service conditions.

In summary, Fig. 8 underscores the importance of selecting a balanced hybrid reinforcement ratio that effectively leverages the strengths of both materials to achieve an optimal combination of strength, ductility, and serviceability. The results suggest that maintaining Af/As below 1.0 is more desirable for ensuring high performance at the service stage while still achieving adequate ultimate strength.

Ductility of FRP and hybrid FRP–steel reinforced beams

Because FRP bars exhibit linear-elastic behavior up to rupture without a distinct yield point, the conventional ductility index used for steel-reinforced concrete beams (ultimate deflection divided by yielding deflection) is not directly applicable. To address this limitation, several alternative ductility and deformability models have been proposed40–43. Three of the most widely cited are the models by Naaman and Jeong (1995)40, Mufti et al. (1996)41, and Abdelrahman et al. (1995)42. The three models will be implemented in this study to evaluate the ductility of BFRP and BFRP-steel reinforced beams. Furthermore, a new ductility index tailored specifically for hybrid-reinforced beams is proposed herein.

Naaman and Jeong40 proposed an energy-based ductility index calculated as the ratio of the total energy absorbed up to failure (Etot) to the elastic energy (Eel) stored in the member. This method relies on integrating the area under the load–deflection (or moment–curvature) curve up to failure and separating the elastic portion from the total energy. Abdelrahman et al.42 proposed an equivalent deformability factor µ defined as the ratio between the actual deformation at the ultimate state of the member Δu and the equivalent deformation of the uncracked section corresponding to the same ultimate moment level ΔI.

These trends are specific to the parameters investigated in this study, and variations in factors such as concrete strength, FRP type, and beam dimensions may influence the results.

The proposed ductility index, µmod, provides an effective means of quantifying the post-yield ductility of hybrid BFRP-steel UHSC beams within the specific parametric framework of this study. It is important to note that the index’s formulation and the resulting values are specific to the material properties (UHSC, BFRP) and geometries analyzed herein. Therefore, a comprehensive investigation into the index’s sensitivity to a wider range of parameters—such as concrete strength, FRP type and modulus, and beam dimensions—is a prerequisite for its potential future adoption in design guidelines.

Design recommendations

Based on the finite element analysis and parametric study presented in this research, the following design recommendations are proposed for the flexural design of ultra-high-strength concrete (UHSC) beams reinforced with BFRP bars and hybrid BFRP-steel reinforcement. These guidelines aim to help designers balance the competing demands of strength, serviceability, and ductility.

Beams reinforced with BFRP bars only

For applications where corrosion resistance is paramount and some loss of deformability is acceptable, such as in aggressive environmental conditions:

Reinforcement ratio: To prevent sudden brittle failure due to FRP rupture, sections should be designed as over-reinforced. A BFRP reinforcement ratio of ρf/ρfb ≥ 1.4 is recommended, as per ACI 440.1R. This ensures a failure mode governed by concrete crushing.

Reinforcement ratio: To prevent sudden brittle failure due to FRP rupture, sections should be designed as over-reinforced. A BFRP reinforcement ratio of ρf/ρfb ≥ 1.4 is recommended, as per ACI 440.1R. This ensures a failure mode governed by concrete crushing.

Optimal upper limit: While increasing ρf/ρfb enhances ultimate capacity and service load stiffness, the rate of return diminishes significantly beyond ρf/ρfb = 2.0. Furthermore, FRP material utilization drops considerably (e.g., to ~ 60% at ρf/ρfb = 3.0). Therefore, exceeding this ratio is structurally inefficient and not economically justified. A practical upper limit of ρf/ρfb ≤ 2.0 is suggested.

This comfortably exceeds the referenced11 recommendation of J ≥ 4.0, ensuring a sufficient deformation capacity and a non-brittle failure mode.

Beams with hybrid BFRP-steel reinforcement

For applications where a combination of durability, strength, and controlled ductility is required:

Optimal hybridization ratio: To achieve a favorable balance of high service load, good yield capacity, and adequate ultimate strength with ductile behavior, it is recommended to maintain Af/As ≤ 1.0. Configurations within this range leverage the stiffness of steel for serviceability and the strength of BFRP for ultimate capacity.

Conclusions

This study examined the flexural performance of UHSC beams reinforced with BFRP bars and with hybrid BFRP–steel configurations using validated 3D finite-element models. The main findings are:

The developed FE model reproduced the experimental beams with small errors—ultimate capacities within ≈ 5–8% and deflections generally within ≤ 9%. In addition, ACI-440 strength predictions and the FE results agreed with an average difference of ≈ 6.8%, confirming model reliability.
