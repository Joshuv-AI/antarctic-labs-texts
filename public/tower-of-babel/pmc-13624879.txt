Geospatial Optimization of Trauma Care Resource Allocation and Timely Access for Severely Injured Patients
Authors: Ryan B. Choi, Deeksha Bidare, Byung Hee Kang, Junsik Kwon, Kyungwon Jung, Jeff Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionCan geospatial optimization identify trauma system configurations that improve timely access to care and quantify trade-offs between efficiency and equity under fixed resource constraints?FindingsIn this cross-sectional study using decision analytical modeling of a regional trauma system that treats approximately 5825 patients with severe injury annually in Gyeonggi province, South Korea, the baseline median travel time to trauma care–capable hospitals was 60 minutes. Efficiency-focused optimization of 3 hospital upgrades reduced population-weighted trauma access burden by 10%, while equity-focused optimization of the same 3 upgrades increased the proportion of severely injured patients with 60-minute access from 49% to 67%.MeaningGeospatial optimization can identify targeted reallocation strategies that may meaningfully improve timely trauma care access and reveal decisions that may exacerbate geographic inequities, offering policymakers a reproducible framework for resource allocation before resources are committed.

Introduction

Trauma systems are built through high-cost decisions about where to locate and sustain specialized care,1 yet these decisions are rarely guided by formal evaluation of how limited resources should be allocated to maximize timely access for injured populations. Traumatic injury remains a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, and delays in access to definitive care are associated with increased mortality and long-term disability.2 Timely access to trauma care–capable hospitals3—facilities with specialized resources, expertise, and readiness to manage traumatic injuries—has consistently been associated with lower mortality and morbidity.4,5 However, establishing and maintaining trauma care–capable hospitals requires substantial investment, including specialists, operating room capacity, intensive care infrastructure, and 24-hour readiness. Because these resources are finite, universal access to timely trauma care remains elusive. Geographic access inequities persist across both low to middle–income and high-income countries,6,7 underscoring a shared global challenge: how to allocate limited trauma care capability to maximize population-level benefit.

Most prior assessments of trauma care access have been descriptive, mapping geographic coverage without explicitly linking population distribution, injury burden, and resource constraints to actionable resource allocation decisions. As a result, trauma system expansion and consolidation decisions are often made without quantifying their population-level consequences, raising the possibility that existing systems may be systematically misallocating limited resources. Addressing this critical gap requires a decision support model that translates geography, injury incidence, transport time, and available resources into resource-constrained decisions about where trauma care capability should be built, expanded, or reduced.

In this study, we evaluated how trauma care resources can be reallocated across hospitals to improve timely access under explicit resource constraints using a geospatial optimization approach applied to a regional trauma system. The model identifies geospatial configurations of hospitals in which trauma care capability should be built, sustained, or reduced to maximize timely access. Using population-level data from a developing trauma system, we illustrate how this model can inform strategic investment and divestment decisions. This approach offers health systems and policymakers a reproducible framework to evaluate access implications of alternative trauma system configurations before committing limited resources.

Methods

Study Design and Reporting Standards

We conducted a cross-sectional, nonidentifiable population-level geospatial modeling study using data from the 2022-2023 Gyeonggi-do Severe Trauma Transport Status report. The study followed the relevant elements of the reporting guidelines for health care simulation research extensions to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.8 All data were aggregated and nonidentifiable. In accordance with Stanford University and Ajou Medical Center Institutional Review Boards’ guideline, this study did not meet criteria for ethics review because the study used publicly available and aggregated data recorded in a manner that precludes the identification of participants.

Patients and Public Involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research. This study used aggregated, nonidentifiable population-level data and simulated geographic modeling to assess trauma system configurations, which precluded direct patient partnership or burden assessment.

Conceptual Overview

Our geospatial optimization model evaluated alternative allocations of trauma care capability across hospitals under explicit resource constraints and quantified their implications for population-level access. Population distribution, injury incidence, ground transport times, and hospital locations were treated as inputs. Under a specified resource constraint, the model systematically evaluated candidate configurations of trauma care–capable hospitals and identified the configuration that optimizes access. By comparing the most optimal configurations across investment and divestment scenarios, the model estimated the marginal access gains or losses associated with each resource allocation decision.

Setting

The model was applied to Gyeonggi province, South Korea, as an illustrative example. South Korea’s national trauma system originated in 2012 through a government initiative establishing regional trauma centers to provide 24-hour definitive trauma care.9 As of 2025, 17 regional trauma centers operated nationwide. In addition, other hospitals—regional emergency medical centers, local emergency medical centers, and local emergency medical institutions—provide progressively lower levels of trauma care, such as treating less severely injured patients or stabilizing patients before interfacility transfer.3 For clarity, we refer to these hospitals, including regional trauma centers, as tiers 1 through 4 hospitals, corresponding to descending trauma care capability.

Gyeonggi province encompasses dense urban centers, sparsely populated rural areas, and hospitals spanning the full spectrum of trauma care capability, making it a suitable setting for evaluating geographic access under resource constraints. The province’s relatively young trauma system and finite funding further motivate the relevance of a resource-constrained optimization model to inform trauma system planning.

Data Source

Data were collected from January 2022 to December 2023. Hospital names, addresses, and trauma care tiers were obtained from provincial emergency medical authority records. Using the Gyeonggi-do Severe Trauma Transport Status report (2022-2023), we compiled city- and town-level incidence of severe traumatic injury across the province.10 Severe trauma was defined using operational criteria applied in prehospital emergency medical service patient records, aligned with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidelines for field triage of injured patients.11 As such, severe traumatic injury incidence data reflect the demand for high-acuity trauma care identified during the prehospital phase. Demographic characteristics, urbanicity (urban or rural), and geographic coordinates for the smallest administrative units within each city or town (neighborhoods) were obtained from the Korean Statistical Information Service database.

Travel Time Estimation

Ground transport time was estimated from the geographic centroid of each neighborhood to all hospitals (tiers 1-4) using OpenStreetMap road network data (OpenStreetMap Foundation). Hospital and neighborhood locations were geocoded by latitude and longitude, and the fastest ground transport routes were calculated using Valhalla routing engine with the auto costing profile, which accounts for speed limits and historic travel times. To standardize comparisons across scenarios, routing queries were performed for 08:00 am local time on August 16, 2025, representing typical weekday morning traffic conditions. The total travel time was calculated as the sum of estimated ground transport time and an additional 34 minutes to account for the median emergency medical services dispatch and on-scene times in Gyeonggi province, based on 2023 provincial emergency medical service performance data. Air transport of patients with severe trauma within Gyeonggi province is rare and performed almost exclusively at a single regional trauma center; this analysis thus excluded air transport.

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome was trauma access burden (TAB), defined as the sum across all neighborhoods of annual incidence of severe injury cases times travel time in minutes to the nearest tier 1 or tier 2 hospital, a population-weighted objective function that penalizes both delays in access and high injury burden simultaneously. Lower values represent better population-level access to higher-capability trauma care within the province.

Secondary Analysis

In a secondary analysis emphasizing equitable access, the model was reformulated to maximize the proportion of severely injured patients with access to a tier 1 or 2 hospital within 60 minutes. Whereas the primary analysis focused on system-wide efficiency by minimizing TAB, this secondary analysis prioritized equitable access by maximizing access within a commonly used benchmark time for trauma care delivery.

Sensitivity Analyses

We conducted 4 prespecified sensitivity analyses. First, we examined the distribution of total trauma access time stratified by neighborhood urbanicity. Second, to characterize the efficiency-equity trade-off, we added a supplemental analysis visualizing this trade-off as a Pareto frontier (eMethods in Supplement 1). Third, to assess the influence of optimization target on hospital selection, we reanalyzed the MILP substituting an unweighted travel time objective for the trauma-weighted formulation at each value of k from 0 to 30, comparing selected hospitals and achieved TAB under each approach. Fourth, to assess robustness to temporal variation in travel conditions, we applied independent log-normal noise to each cell of the travel time matrix across 500 iterations and reanalyzed the MILP at k = 3 for each perturbed matrix, reporting hospital selection frequency across iterations.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed from May to November 2025. No inferential statistical tests were performed. All analyses were deterministic based on MILP optimization. Summary measures of population-level travel time are reported as medians (IQR). Optimization outcomes are reported as absolute values and percentage changes in TAB (minutes × cases per year) and as proportions of severely injured patients with access within 60 minutes. All analyses were conducted in R, version 4.5.0 (R Project for Statistical Computing), and Python, version 3.13 (Python Software Foundation) using publicly available packages.

Equity Assessment

Geographic disparities in trauma access were observed across neighborhood urbanicity. Urban neighborhoods had a median total access time of 53.5 (IQR, 47.1-63.0) minutes compared with 86.5 (IQR, 74.6-96.4) minutes in rural neighborhoods, representing a 33-minute gap attributable to both greater road network density and proximity to existing tier 1 and 2 hospitals (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). To further characterize the efficiency-equity trade-off under the k = 3 upgrade constraint, we constructed a Pareto frontier across 11 values of the biobjective weighting parameter α. Three distinct optimal configurations emerged: the efficiency-optimal solution (α = 1.0) minimized TAB at 332 301 minutes × cases/year but yielded the highest geographic inequality (Gini coefficient = 0.145), while the equity-optimal solution (α = 0) reduced the Gini coefficient to 0.132 at a TAB cost of 1.7% (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). The balanced solution (α = 0.1 to α = 0.6) achieved intermediate performance, reducing inequality with minimal efficiency loss.

Robustness of Hospital Selection for TAB Optimization

When the optimization target was changed from trauma-weighted to unweighted travel time, 1 of the 3 upgrade selections was shared across both formulations. While the remaining 2 selections diverged, evaluating the travel time–only selection on the TAB metric yielded a burden 1.7% higher than the trauma-weighted optimum (eFigure 3 in Supplement 1). Both formulations exhibited diminishing marginal returns beyond upgrades of k = 5 to k = 7. In the Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis, 224 unique 3-hospital configurations were identified across 500 perturbed iterations. All 3 hospitals from the primary solution ranked among the 6 most frequently selected candidates overall (eFigure 4 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we demonstrated that under fixed resource constraints, trauma system performance varies substantially depending on how resources are allocated, and that targeted reallocation is associated with meaningful improvements in timely access to care. Applied to a large and heterogeneous province within a developing trauma system, the model generated optimized configurations that minimized TAB and quantified trade-offs between system-wide efficiency and equitable access. By explicitly evaluating alternative configurations under constrained resources, the model provides decision-makers with measurable estimates of the marginal gains or losses associated with investment and divestment decisions.

Optimizing access to trauma care remains a pressing and universal challenge. Low- and middle-income countries bear a disproportionate share of the global injury burden, yet often lack mature trauma systems, characterized by fragmented prehospital care and substantial geographic inequities in access to definitive trauma care.9,14 Similar access gaps persist even in high-income settings, where trauma systems have required repeated redesign to address changing population distribution, infrastructure, and resource availability.6,7

A key barrier to improving trauma system design has been the absence of reproducible methods to evaluate how resource allocation decisions translate into population-level access. Our study addresses this gap by introducing an optimization-based approach that moves beyond descriptive access mapping or expert-driven planning exercises. The model evaluates counterfactual system configurations and identifies those that optimally improve access under defined constraints. Although demonstrated using data from Gyeonggi province, the model is designed to be transferable, requiring only hospital locations, population distribution, and estimated injury incidence—inputs that are increasingly available or can be reasonably approximated in many settings, including low- and middle-income countries. By quantifying the marginal returns associated with resource investment or divestment and explicitly comparing efficiency- and equity-oriented objectives, the model provides a decision framework to evaluate how alternative allocation strategies would affect access before resources are committed.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the availability and quality of data vary across settings. While some countries lack reliable injury registries or census data, the model can accommodate proxy metrics or expert-informed estimates and be updated as data systems improve. Second, the model does not incorporate hospital capacity such as staffed beds, operating room availability, or specialist coverage. Consequently, hospitals identified as geographically optimal could face operational saturation at specific times. Although estimating day-to-day access has practical limitations, capacity and additional constraints can be incorporated in future extensions as data become available. Third, hospital locations and travel times were treated as static, but infrastructure and traffic patterns evolve. The model is intended to support iterative planning and can be rerun with updated inputs to reflect changing conditions. Similarly, cross-border patient flows were not captured in the provincial transport data, and the model treats Gyeonggi province as a closed system. Patients near provincial borders may have shorter access to trauma centers in adjacent regions than the model reflects. Fourth, trauma care capability was treated as a binary attribute in our illustrative example, whereas the effective capacity of trauma-capable hospitals varies across settings. Our model optimizes for geographic access without incorporating facility-level patient volumes, which are an important determinant of trauma care quality. While expanding the number of trauma-capable hospitals improves access, it may simultaneously dilute patient volumes across facilities, a trade-off that remains difficult to quantify given that the optimal volume per trauma center remains uncharacterized.15 Future extensions of this model should incorporate heterogeneous facility capability and volume constraints as data become available. Fifth, the optimization framework assumes that trauma care capability can be allocated or withdrawn at the system level. This assumption is most valid in settings with centralized trauma designation authority, such as South Korea, where the national government directly funds and designates trauma centers. However, even in decentralized systems such as the US, our framework can be used to identify geographic access gaps and inform designation policy or targeted investment decisions at the regional or state level. Finally, the model evaluated spatial access rather than downstream clinical outcomes or cost-effectiveness. Timely access to trauma care is a well-established determinant of survival. Given established associations between delays to trauma care and mortality, improving access is a necessary pillar of system-wide efforts to meaningfully reduce preventable deaths.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study of a regional trauma system in South Korea, we demonstrated that under fixed resource constraints, targeted reallocation of trauma care capability was associated with meaningful improvements in timely access to care and reductions in geographic inequities. These findings provide a decision framework to evaluate how alternative allocation strategies would affect access before resources are committed. Integrating optimization-based approaches into trauma system planning could enable more efficient and transparent allocation of limited health care resources.
