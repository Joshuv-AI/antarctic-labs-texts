Multimodal ultrasound for precise quantitative evaluation of diaphragmatic lesions in idiopathic inflammatory myopathy: a promising assessment tool
Authors: Mei Tian, Min Shu, Ruiqian Guo, Sujiao Cao, Li Qiu, Yuanjiao Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM) is a systemic autoimmune disorder in which respiratory failure, driven by diaphragmatic dysfunction and interstitial lung disease (ILD), is a leading cause of mortality. This study aimed to evaluate IIM-related diaphragmatic involvement via multimodal ultrasound, integrating high-frequency ultrasound (HFUS), M-mode imaging, and ultrasound viscoelasticity imaging (UVI).MethodsIn this study, 93 IIM patients and 51 matched healthy controls (HCs) underwent diaphragmatic multimodal ultrasound assessments, including measurements of thickness, excursion, and viscoelastic properties [shear wave speed (Cs), viscosity index (Vi), and dispersion slope (Disp)]. Multivariate logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were conducted to identify independent predictors of IIM and to assess the diagnostic efficacy of distinguishing IIM patients from HCs. Subgroup ROC analysis was performed within the IIM cohort to evaluate parameters for identifying respiratory involvement. Additionally, correlations between ultrasound parameters and clinical indicators (serological markers, antibodies, and ILD status) were analyzed. In total, 17 IIM patients completed post-treatment follow-up ultrasound examinations.ResultsCompared with the HCs, the patients with IIM exhibited a significantly reduced diaphragmatic thickening fraction (DTF) (HC group: 111.8% vs. IIM group: 74.2%, P<0.001), thickness at end-forced inspiration (3.5 vs. 2.6 mm, P<0.001), quiet breathing excursion (22.8 vs. 14.3 mm, P<0.001), and forced breathing excursion (70.5 vs. 44.0 mm, P<0.001), as well as an elevated Cs_mean (3.1 vs. 3.6 m/s, P<0.05) and Disp_mean (3.9 vs. 4.6 m/s/kHz, P<0.05). Thickness at end‑forced inspiration [odds ratio (OR) =0.33; P<0.001] and forced breathing excursion (OR =0.94; P<0.001) were identified as independent diagnostic markers. For discriminating between patients with IIM and HCs, forced breathing excursion demonstrated the best diagnostic performance among the individual parameters [area under the curve (AUC) =0.84, P<0.001], while a combined model integrating thickness, excursion, and viscoelastic parameters achieved superior diagnostic efficacy (AUC =0.89, P<0.001). For identifying respiratory involvement within the IIM cohort, conventional morpho-functional parameters failed (all AUC <0.65), while the Vi_mean showed significant discriminative ability (AUC =0.72, P<0.010), with the combined model achieving excellent performance (AUC =0.88, P<0.001). Correlation analyses revealed that forced breathing excursion was negatively correlated with ILD (r=−0.29). Post-treatment follow-up revealed significant improvement and normalization of diaphragmatic excursion (DE) and DTF (P<0.05), whereas the thick ness parameters remained unchanged (P>0.05). Myoglobin (MB) decreased significantly (P<0.05), while conventional serological markers, including the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), and creatine kinase (CK), showed no significant change (P>0.05).ConclusionsMultimodal ultrasound enables the early detection and quantitative monitoring of diaphragmatic impairment in IIM. Viscoelastic parameters, particularly the Vi, may aid in the identification of patients with respiratory involvement. Diaphragmatic functional parameters (excursion and the DTF) are responsive to treatment and may serve as objective biomarkers for monitoring the therapeutic response, complementing conventional serological assessments.Keywords: Idiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM), diaphragm, high-frequency ultrasound (HFUS), ultrasound viscoelasticity imaging (UVI), multimodal ultrasound

Introduction

Idiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM) is a heterogeneous group of autoimmune diseases characterized by chronic muscle inflammation and progressive weakness. Key histopathological features include lymphocytic infiltration of skeletal muscle, along with myofiber degeneration and necrosis (1). The prognosis of IIM varies widely, with 10-year survival rates reported to range from 20% to 90% across cohorts in Europe, North America, and Japan (2). Respiratory complications, particularly interstitial lung disease (ILD) and aspiration pneumonia, are the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in patients with IIM (2).

Respiratory involvement in IIM is defined by the presence of unexplained dyspnea (at rest or on exertion), and/or ILD confirmed on high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) (3,4). ILD is the most common pulmonary manifestation, affecting approximately 40–50% of patients across most myositis subtypes, with the exception of inclusion body myositis (IBM) (5,6). This condition is associated with a poor prognosis; approximately half of affected patients die from respiratory failure (3). Its prevalence exhibits marked geographical variation: a meta-analysis of 10,130 patients reported a global ILD prevalence of 41% among patients with polymyositis/dermatomyositis (PM/DM), with significantly higher rates in Asian cohorts (50%) compared to European (26%) and American (23%) populations (7). This heavy disease burden underscores the critical need for early detection and monitoring of respiratory involvement in IIM.

The involvement of respiratory muscles, particularly the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, is a critical manifestation of IIM that may adversely affect multiple systems as the disease progresses. Pulmonary manifestations often precede extrapulmonary symptoms by several months and are estimated to occur in 16–65% of affected individuals (8). Given that the diaphragm is the primary inspiratory muscle, its dysfunction directly contributes to restrictive ventilatory impairment and is a key factor increasing patient morbidity and mortality (9). Therefore, the early identification and assessment of IIM-related diaphragmatic dysfunction are crucial for guiding timely intervention and improving patient prognosis and quality of life.

Currently, respiratory involvement in IIM patients is assessed using a combination of modalities, including pulmonary function tests (PFTs), chest auscultation, and HRCT for the detection of ILD (3). However, these methods primarily evaluate global respiratory function or parenchymal lung abnormalities rather than directly assessing diaphragmatic function itself. Serological antibodies and inflammatory markers serve as key indicators for assessing immune activity in IIM. However, their correlation with diaphragmatic dysfunction remains unclear, and they do not accurately reflect the pathophysiological status of local muscle tissue. Further, assessing diaphragmatic function in routine clinical practice remains challenging. Muscle biopsy, the diagnostic gold standard for IIM, is invasive and requires specific tissue sampling. Similarly, the utility of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in the early detection of muscle inflammation is limited by high costs, extended acquisition times, and potential contraindications (10). Moreover, the thin configuration of the diaphragm poses significant technical challenges for MRI, particularly when the muscle is atrophic.

Ultrasonography, a non-invasive, real-time, portable, and radiation-free modality, demonstrates substantial potential for evaluating muscle pathology (11-13). Sonographic evaluation of the diaphragm was first described by Haber in 1975 (14). Since then, ultrasonography has emerged as a valuable tool for diagnosing diaphragmatic myositis, a potential contributor to respiratory impairment in IIM patients (15). High-frequency ultrasound (HFUS) delineates muscle morphology and enables the precise measurement of diaphragmatic thickness, while M-mode ultrasonography permits the accurate measurement of diaphragmatic excursion (DE), providing a dynamic method for functional assessment (9). However, conventional morphological and kinematic assessments lack sensitivity to alterations in the microstructure and mechanical properties of muscle tissue, which represent the core pathological processes in IIM.

Shear wave elastography (SWE) can quantitatively assess tissue stiffness by measuring the speed of shear waves propagated through tissue (16,17). However, SWE primarily reflects the elastic modulus (stiffness) of tissue, modelling tissues as homogeneous, purely elastic bodies. In reality, biological tissues are heterogeneous and complex biomechanical structures that exhibit both elastic solid properties and viscous fluid properties (18). Owing to the viscous nature of biological tissues, the propagation velocity of shear waves within them varies with frequency, a phenomenon known as dispersion (19).

Ultrasound viscoelasticity imaging (UVI) enables the simultaneous evaluation of tissue elasticity and viscosity, thereby providing more comprehensive biomechanical information and demonstrating promising diagnostic value in liver, kidney, and breast diseases (20-22). Liu et al. (22) applied UVI in pediatric Henoch-Schönlein purpura nephritis and found that the viscosity coefficient (Vmean) achieved excellent diagnostic performance [area under the curve (AUC) =0.95] and showed significant correlations with inflammatory markers, suggesting that viscosity parameters are sensitive indicators of inflammatory activity. In patients with acute gout, Zhang et al. (23) reported that UVI parameters (mean shear wave velocity and maximum dispersion coefficient) effectively differentiated acute gout from non-gouty arthropathy, with a combined model achieving an AUC of 0.885. These studies provide evidence that UVI can detect inflammatory changes in human tissues, supporting its potential application in IIM.

To date, research has primarily applied SWE to the evaluation of appendicular skeletal muscles in IIM patients (24-26). Thus, we sought to investigate whether the diaphragm in IIM patients exhibits distinct muscle properties compared to healthy controls (HCs), and whether UVI could serve as a non-invasive tool for detecting such abnormalities. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first application of UVI for the quantitative characterization of the diaphragm in IIM patients. By integrating HFUS, M-mode imaging, and UVI, we systematically evaluated diaphragmatic thickness, excursion, and viscoelastic properties in IIM patients compared to HCs to identify potential diagnostic imaging biomarkers. Further, we investigated the correlations between these sonographic parameters and key clinical indicators, including myositis-related antibodies, systemic inflammatory markers, and the presence of comorbid ILD. Additionally, we performed a longitudinal analysis to evaluate changes in these ultrasound parameters pre- and post-treatment, assessing their utility in monitoring therapeutic efficacy. Ultimately, we sought to establish a non-invasive, quantitative, and reproducible multimodal ultrasound framework for assessing diaphragmatic involvement, providing comprehensive biomechanical insights to facilitate the early prediction of respiratory failure and enable dynamic prognostic monitoring in IIM patients. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0133/rc).

Methods

This study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Sichuan University {No. 2024[2122]}. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants

Between June 2024 and November 2025, consecutive patients with IIM were prospectively recruited from West China Hospital of Sichuan University. Eligible participants were required to be aged ≥18 years and meet the 1975 Bohan and Peter (27,28) or the 2017 European League Against Rheumatism/American College of Rheumatology criteria (29) for IIM. Diagnosis and subtyping were performed by experienced rheumatologists who were blinded to the participants’ ultrasound findings. The exclusion criteria included: the presence of scars, inflammation, or tumors at the examination site; conditions precluding supine positioning or cooperation (e.g., severe heart failure); pregnancy; and coexisting neuromuscular or metabolic myopathies. Age- and sex-matched HCs were recruited via advertisement during the same period, applying identical exclusion criteria. Demographic data, including age, sex, height, and weight, were recorded for all participants.

Clinical and laboratory data collection

Respiratory involvement was defined based on clinical symptoms and imaging findings: (I) unexplained dyspnea (at rest or on exertion) attributable to respiratory muscle weakness, as documented in medical records by attending rheumatologists or pulmonologists; and/or (II) ILD confirmed by HRCT, as reported by experienced radiologists.

Clinical and laboratory data were collected from medical records when available, including serological antibodies (myositis-specific and myositis-associated autoantibodies, such as anti-Jo-1, anti-Ro52, anti-dsDNA, and anti-snRNP), myoglobin (MB), creatine kinase (CK), inflammatory markers [erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP)], chest HRCT findings, and PFT results. Manual muscle strength testing of the proximal upper limbs according to the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale [0–5] was also recorded when available (30). All available data closest to the time of ultrasound examination were recorded.

Multimodal ultrasound assessment

Examinations were performed using a Resona A20 system (Mindray, Shenzhen, China). Diaphragmatic thickness and viscoelasticity were assessed with a linear array probe (LM18-5WU MHz), while excursion was measured with a convex array probe (SC7-1U MHz). With the participant in the supine position, the right diaphragm was imaged. A high-frequency linear array probe was positioned in the 8th or 9th intercostal space between the right anterior axillary and midaxillary lines, with the beam oriented perpendicularly to the diaphragm. The diaphragm was visualized as a three-layered structure, comprising a central hypoechoic muscular band bounded by hyperechoic pleural and peritoneal layers. Diaphragmatic thickness was measured at quiet end-inspiration, quiet end-expiration, and forced end-inspiration (Figure 1A). The diaphragmatic thickening fraction (DTF) was calculated as follows: DTF = [(thickness at forced end-inspiration − thickness at quiet end-expiration)/thickness at quiet end-expiration] × 100% (31).

Viscoelastic properties of the diaphragm were assessed using the same ultrasound system equipped with viscoelastic imaging capability. After obtaining optimal B-mode images of the right hemidiaphragm in the zone of apposition, participants were instructed to hold their breath at end-inspiration to minimize respiratory motion artifacts. The transducer was held perpendicular to the diaphragm with minimal pressure to avoid tissue compression, and kept immobile for 3–5 seconds to ensure stable image acquisition. A circular region of interest (ROI) of 1 mm in diameter was manually placed within the diaphragmatic muscle substance, carefully avoiding the pleural line, peritoneal lining, and adjacent vessels (Figure 1B). Care was taken to position the ROI in an area with homogeneous muscle texture and to exclude any artifacts from adjacent structures. The system automatically generated three viscoelastic parameters based on shear wave propagation: (I) shear wave speed (Cs; m/s), reflecting tissue elasticity; (II) viscosity index (Vi; Pa·s), representing energy dissipation due to friction between tissue components; and (III) dispersion slope (Disp; m/s/kHz), quantifying the frequency-dependent behavior of Cs as influenced by tissue viscosity (32). Each measurement was repeated three times, and the mean values were used for analysis.

DE was measured using M-mode ultrasound. The probe was placed between the anterior axillary and midclavicular lines to obtain a view encompassing the liver, inferior vena cava, and the hyperechoic diaphragmatic line. The amplitude of DE, defined as the vertical distance from the baseline to the peak of the inspiratory deflection (Figure 1C), was recorded during both quiet and forced inspiration. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and the mean values were used for analysis. The patients with IIM underwent follow-up multimodal diaphragmatic ultrasound 6 to 12 months post-treatment.

To minimize bias, ultrasound examinations were performed by two experienced sonographers with more than 5 years of experience in musculoskeletal ultrasound and more than 3 years of experience in SWE. Both operators were blinded to the clinical details of the participants. The participants were positioned in the examination room by an assistant before the sonographer entered, and communication was limited to procedural instructions. Prior to the main study, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to assess inter-operator reliability, yielding values ranging from 0.83 to 0.99, which demonstrated excellent reproducibility (Table S1). After confirming adequate reliability, the remaining participants were scanned by either one of the two sonographers following the standardized protocol.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and all graphical representations were constructed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or as median [interquartile range (IQR)] for non-normally distributed data. Inter-group comparisons were conducted using the independent-samples Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages [n (%)] and were compared using the Pearson Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test.

Correlation analyses between variables were evaluated according to their data scales: Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was applied for pairs of continuous variables, point-biserial correlation was used for relationships between continuous and dichotomous variables, and the Pearson Chi-squared test was employed for pairs of categorical variables. To identify independent predictors of IIM, variables with a P<0.10 in the univariable analysis were subsequently included in a multivariable forward stepwise binary logistic regression model. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The diagnostic efficacy of multimodal ultrasound parameters for IIM-related diaphragmatic involvement was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and AUC values. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

The study included 93 IIM patients (DM: 46%; PM: 54%) and 51 HCs. The median disease duration of the IIM patients was 11 months. All participants underwent measurements of diaphragmatic thickness and excursion, while 48 IIM patients and 41 HCs also underwent diaphragmatic viscoelasticity assessment. No significant differences were observed in age, sex, or body mass index (BMI) between the two groups (Table 1). All IIM patients underwent chest HRCT, and a subset also underwent concurrent serological testing. Respiratory involvement was present in 53 IIM patients (57%). Specifically, dyspnea was reported in 26 patients (28%), and ILD was confirmed by HRCT in 45 patients (48%). Anti-Ro-52 antibodies were detected in 17 patients (33%). PFT data were available for 23 IIM patients (25%), among whom ventilatory impairment was classified as normal in 2 (9%), mild in 9 (39%), and moderate to severe in 12 (52%). Manual muscle strength testing of the proximal upper limbs was documented in all IIM patients, with a median MRC score of 4 (IQR, 4–5). Muscle strength impairment (defined as MRC ≤3) was present in 21 patients (23%), providing a quantitative measure of disease activity in this cohort. Multimodal ultrasound of the diaphragm

Compared with the HCs (Figure 2A), the IIM patients exhibited a significantly lower DTF, thickness at end‑forced inspiration, and excursion during both quiet and forced breathing (all P<0.001). Conversely, the Cs_mean and Disp_mean were significantly elevated in the IIM patients (P<0.05), while the Vi_mean did not differ significantly between the IIM patients and HCs. Spearman correlation analysis (Figure 2B) revealed that in the IIM patients, the Cs_mean was negatively correlated with thickness at end-forced inspiration (r=−0.35), and the Disp_mean was negatively correlated with excursion during forced breathing (r=−0.32).

Multivariate logistic regression identified thickness at end‑forced inspiration and excursion during forced breathing as independent predictors of IIM (both P<0.001, Figure 2C). Each 1-mm increase in thickness was associated with a 67% reduction in the odds of IIM (OR =0.33; 95% CI: 0.18–0.59).

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of multimodal ultrasound parameters for detecting diaphragmatic abnormalities in IIM, ROC curve analysis was first performed comparing the IIM patients with the HCs (Figure 2D). This analysis revealed that forced breathing excursion had the highest discriminative ability among the individual parameters, with an AUC of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.75–0.92), a cut-off value of 62.5 mm, a sensitivity of 75%, and a specificity of 78%. The thickness and excursion parameters showed moderate performance (AUC: 0.73–0.77); while the viscoelastic parameters exhibited high specificity (93% for Cs and 95% for Disp). A combined model integrating thickness, excursion, and viscoelasticity showed superior diagnostic efficacy (AUC =0.89, 95% CI: 0.82–0.95; Table 2).

To explore whether the diagnostic performance of the ultrasound parameters varied according to clinical characteristics, an exploratory subgroup analysis was performed within the IIM cohort based on the presence of respiratory involvement (Table 3 and Figure 2E). The results showed that the conventional morpho-functional parameters (including diaphragmatic thickness, excursion, and DTF) failed to effectively differentiate between IIM patients with and without respiratory involvement (all AUC <0.65, P>0.05, Table 3). Conversely, the diaphragmatic viscoelastic parameter Vi demonstrated significant discriminative ability. The Vi_mean yielded an AUC of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.57–0.86), with a sensitivity of 65%, a specificity of 72%, and an optimal cut-off value of 1.9 Pa·s. Notably, the combined model integrating multiple parameters achieved excellent diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.77–0.98).

Correlation between ultrasound parameters and clinical indicators

A correlation matrix heatmap was constructed to evaluate the associations between the diaphragmatic ultrasound parameters and 21 clinical variables (Figure 3A). Anti-Ro-52 positivity was significantly correlated with elevated CRP (r=0.34) and ESR (r=0.43) levels (Figure 3B,3C). Additionally, a positive correlation was observed between anti-Ro-52 positivity and the presence of ILD (r=0.33), with the prevalence of ILD higher in anti-Ro-52 positive patients (76% vs. 41%, P<0.05; Figure 3D). Regarding diaphragmatic function, DE during deep breathing showed a significant negative correlation with the presence of ILD (r=−0.29, Figure 3E).

Preliminary evaluation of treatment response

Among the 17 IIM patients (DM: 47%; PM: 53%) who completed post-treatment follow-up, all received corticosteroid therapy, and some received additional immunosuppressive agents, including cyclosporine, tacrolimus, cyclophosphamide, or mycophenolate mofetil. Following treatment, significant improvements were observed in multiple diaphragmatic functional parameters. Specifically, both the quiet and forced breathing excursions of the IIM group increased significantly (both P<0.05) and were comparable to those of the HC group post-treatment (Figure 4A,4B). Additionally, the DTF showed a significant increase after treatment (Figure 4C, P<0.05). Conversely, the static diaphragmatic thickness parameters remained unchanged (P>0.05). In the subset of IIM patients with longitudinal serological data, markers of inflammation and muscle damage showed divergent responses: MB decreased significantly (P<0.05), while the ESR, CRP, and CK did not change significantly (Figure 4D).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the structural, functional, and biomechanical characteristics of the diaphragm in patients with IIM using HFUS, M-mode, and UVI. Our findings not only provide further evidence that the diaphragm is a primary target organ in IIM but also reveal novel alterations in its viscoelastic properties. This establishes a direct pathophysiological link between microscopic tissue biomechanics and the observed macroscopic functional impairment.

The IIM patients exhibited significant reductions in the DTF, thickness at end-forced inspiration, and DE, while thickness during quiet breathing remained unchanged. This pattern suggests that impaired contractile efficiency may manifest prior to detectable structural atrophy (33). The DTF, which reflects the efficiency of muscle fiber contraction, is more sensitive than static thickness measurements for assessing diaphragmatic dysfunction (34), a finding consistent with observations in IBM (35) and Duchenne muscular dystrophy (36). The concurrent decline in these parameters represents a multidimensional ultrasound phenotype indicative of impaired diaphragmatic contractile reserve in early-stage IIM.

Typical pathological features of IIM include muscle fiber degeneration, interstitial fibrosis, and inflammatory infiltration (37), all of which can alter tissue biomechanics. By applying UVI to the diaphragm for the first time, we observed elevated Cs_mean and Disp_mean values in the IIM patients. An increased Cs_mean indicates greater tissue stiffness, which aligns with previous studies linking SWE-assessed stiffness to fibrosis in IIM patients (38,39). Our results extend this association to respiratory muscles, suggesting that the diaphragm undergoes similar fibrotic remodeling. Our study revealed that the IIM patients exhibited significantly higher Disp_mean values compared to the HCs. Research has shown that dispersion arises from the frequency-dependent propagation of shear waves within viscous components of biological tissues (40). Consequently, analyzing the dispersion characteristics of shear waves provides an indirect means of quantifying tissue viscosity. An elevated Disp_mean reflects increased tissue viscosity, which may serve as a surrogate marker for active inflammation, thereby complementing the fibrotic implications of the Cs_mean and providing novel biomechanical perspectives into the pathophysiology of IIM (40,41).

The Vi_mean showed a similar upward trend to the Disp_mean in IIM patients, but this difference did not reach statistical significance. This finding may be attributable to several factors. First, the modest sample size of the UVI subgroup (n=48) may have limited statistical power to detect significant differences in viscosity, which may exhibit greater inter-individual variability than dispersion parameters. Second, from a pathophysiological perspective, viscosity reflects complex tissue interactions, including energy dissipation during shear wave propagation (42), which may be influenced by competing pathological processes such as edema, inflammation, and fibrosis. These processes may have opposing effects on viscous properties, potentially attenuating group differences. Correlations between these viscoelastic parameters and diaphragmatic thickness further enriches this pathophysiological narrative.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified diaphragmatic thickness at end-forced inspiration and excursion during forced breathing as independent diagnostic predictors of IIM. Reduced diaphragmatic thickness at end-forced inspiration reflects impaired contractile function, providing a sensitive marker for diaphragmatic involvement (sensitivity =88%) and representing a promising quantitative imaging biomarker. This interpretation is further supported by the concomitant reduction in the DTF observed in our IIM cohort. The DTF, which quantifies the dynamic change in muscle thickness from rest to inspiration, is a well-established sonographic indicator of diaphragmatic contractile performance. Reductions in contractile function are indicative of impaired neural drive or myofiber dysfunction and correlate with diminished muscle strength. Notably, research in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis has similarly established an association between diaphragmatic thickness at peak inspiration and pulmonary function parameters (43).

DE during forced breathing also demonstrated robust diagnostic performance (AUC =0.84), validating its role as a pivotal functional indicator dependent on neural drive and contractile efficiency (44,45). Further, the established correlation between excursion, lung function, and clinical symptoms underscores its clinical utility (46). While the high specificity of viscoelastic parameters (>92%) strongly suggests IIM-related pathology, their moderate AUC values indicate a potential risk of missing early or mild cases when used in isolation. The primary contribution of this study is the validation of a multimodal parameter model, which showed superior diagnostic efficacy (combined AUC =0.89). By integrating thickness, excursion, and viscoelasticity, this model provides a comprehensive characterization of diaphragmatic involvement, advancing IIM assessment from single-parameter evaluation toward a quantitative and holistic ultrasound-based approach.

Beyond the diagnostic discrimination between the IIM patients and HCs, our subgroup analysis within the IIM cohort addressed the more clinically relevant question of identifying patients at risk for respiratory involvement. Notably, conventional morpho-functional parameters, including diaphragmatic thickness, excursion, and the DTF, failed to differentiate between patients with respiratory involvement and those without, suggesting that these parameters reflect global myopathic involvement rather than being specifically linked to respiratory complications. Conversely, the viscoelastic parameters, particularly the Vi_mean, demonstrated significant discriminative ability (AUC =0.72), with the combined multimodal model achieving excellent diagnostic performance (AUC =0.87). This distinction carries important pathophysiological implications. While conventional parameters capture contractile dysfunction common to most IIM patients, the discriminative ability of the Vi suggests that viscoelastic properties may be more sensitive to inflammatory and edematous changes that characterize active disease in patients with respiratory involvement. This interpretation aligns with previous studies demonstrating that viscosity parameters correlate with inflammatory markers in Henoch-Schönlein purpura nephritis (22) and can be used to effectively differentiate acute inflammatory arthritis from non-inflammatory arthropathies (23), supporting the notion that UVI parameters reflect tissue inflammatory activity. Thus, conventional parameters confirm diaphragmatic involvement in IIM, while viscoelastic parameters add value by identifying patients requiring closer monitoring for respiratory deterioration, advancing diaphragmatic ultrasound from simple abnormality detection toward nuanced risk stratification.

This study identified significant correlations that define a clinical phenotype integrating serological profiles, ILD, and diaphragmatic dysfunction in IIM. It is important to distinguish between two distinct mechanisms of respiratory impairment in IIM: parenchymal lung disease (ILD) and respiratory muscle weakness (diaphragmatic dysfunction). These processes are pathophysiologically distinct but may coexist and interact clinically. In patients with ILD, diaphragmatic dysfunction may arise secondarily due to increased elastic load from stiff lungs, chronic hypoxemia, or systemic inflammation affecting respiratory muscles. Conversely, primary diaphragmatic dysfunction can exacerbate dyspnea in patients with underlying ILD. In this study, we included ILD as a clinical variable to characterize our cohort and to explore whether diaphragmatic ultrasound parameters differ between IIM patients with and without ILD. A negative correlation was observed between forced breathing excursion and the presence of ILD (r=−0.29), indicating that DE during deep inspiration is reduced in IIM patients with comorbid ILD. The relatively weak correlation likely reflects the multifactorial nature of respiratory impairment in IIM, where diaphragmatic function is influenced not only by mechanical loading from ILD but also by the degree of primary myopathic involvement. Previous research has consistently reported decreased DE in ILD patients, correlating with impaired pulmonary function and exacerbated clinical symptoms, such as dyspnea and reduced exercise tolerance (47). Our finding suggests that concomitant diaphragmatic dysfunction may contribute to respiratory symptoms in these patients, highlighting the value of comprehensive respiratory assessment that includes both pulmonary imaging and diaphragmatic functional evaluation.

Further, anti-Ro-52 antibodies are established risk factors for ILD in IIM patients (48), with some reports indicating up to an eight-fold increased risk (49). While some literature suggests that anti-Ro-52 positivity may not universally increase the incidence of ILD, it is significantly associated with an increased predisposition to symptomatic disease in patients with ILD (50), corroborating our observations. In our cohort, the prevalence of ILD was markedly higher in the anti-Ro-52 positive patients (76% vs. 41%), highlighting the utility of this antibody in stratifying patients at high risk for pulmonary involvement. The positive correlation between anti-Ro-52 and elevated inflammatory markers (ESR/CRP) further reflects a more severe systemic inflammatory phenotype. Consequently, monitoring DE not only facilitates the diagnosis of IIM but also offers a functional surrogate for predicting progressive respiratory failure, particularly within high-risk serological subgroups. This underscores the importance of the early identification and management of respiratory muscle dysfunction to improve clinical outcomes (51).

The 17 IIM patients who underwent longitudinal assessment showed significant improvements in diaphragmatic function post-treatment. Both DE and DTF increased significantly. Conversely, the static thickness parameters remained unchanged, suggesting that functional recovery precedes structural remodeling. This rapid improvement in DE and DTF may reflect the recovery of neuromuscular function and the resolution of interstitial inflammatory edema (52), while structural muscle regeneration may necessitate a more prolonged recovery period. The significant reduction in MB provided biochemical corroboration of treatment response, while the conventional inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP, and CK) did not change significantly, which is consistent with findings by Lodin et al. (53) that IIM patients often do not exhibit significant elevations in systemic inflammatory markers. Thus, our results suggest that imaging parameters may be more sensitive than traditional serological indicators for monitoring short-term therapeutic response. However, we acknowledge that the longitudinal follow-up cohort was relatively small, and these findings should be considered preliminary. Larger prospective studies with extended follow-up are needed to confirm the utility of diaphragmatic ultrasound for monitoring treatment response in IIM.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, diaphragmatic viscoelasticity was not uniformly assessed in all IIM patients, and the relatively modest sample size for longitudinal analysis limits the generalizability of the study findings; these preliminary findings require validation in larger, prospective cohorts. Second, the lack of direct histopathological correlation and comprehensive physiological assessments constrains mechanistic interpretation. Additionally, PFT data were available only in a subset of patients due to the retrospective nature of this study, as PFTs were not routinely performed during clinical care. This constrains our ability to link diaphragmatic findings to functional respiratory capacity. Further, we did not qualitatively or quantitatively assess ILD severity; future studies should incorporate validated grading systems to determine whether diaphragmatic dysfunction is independently associated with ILD extent or primarily reflects concurrent myopathic involvement. Despite these constraints, this study provides pioneering insights into multimodal ultrasound for IIM. Future research integrating larger populations, multidimensional assessments, and concurrent PFTs is essential to establish the clinical utility of these biomechanical markers.

Conclusions

This study established a robust multimodal ultrasound framework, incorporating HFUS, M-mode, and the pioneering application of UVI, for the comprehensive and quantitative assessment of diaphragmatic involvement in patients with IIM. Our findings demonstrate that diaphragmatic thickness, excursion, and viscoelastic properties serve as effective diagnostic biomarkers for IIM, with a combined model achieving excellent discriminative performance (AUC =0.89). Notably, while conventional morpho-functional parameters reflect global diaphragmatic involvement, viscoelastic parameters, particularly the Vi, aid in the identification of IIM patients with respiratory involvement. Further, the significant post-treatment improvement in DE and the DTF underscores the potential of functional ultrasound parameters as sensitive, non-invasive tools for monitoring therapeutic response. By providing unique biomechanical insights into diaphragmatic pathophysiology, this multimodal approach facilitates the early identification of respiratory dysfunction, ultimately contributing to more precise prognostic stratification and optimized management of patients with IIM.
