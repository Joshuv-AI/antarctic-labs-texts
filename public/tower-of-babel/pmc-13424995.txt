Risk-benefit considerations for antirheumatic drug use in pregnancy, breastfeeding, and male preconception: expert perspectives
Authors: Andrea Pluma, Linda Rüegg, Sabrina Hamroun, Irene Cecchi, Axel Finckh, Yvette Meissner, Frauke Förger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesManaging antirheumatic drug therapy during family planning, pregnancy, and breastfeeding remains challenging in rheumatology. Since inactive rheumatic disease is an important prerequisite for favourable fertility and pregnancy outcomes, clinicians must weigh the risk of drug exposure against the risk of uncontrolled disease. This study explored how considerations of disease activity influence prescribing decisions.MethodsWe surveyed 20 experts from the EULAR (European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology) task force on their use of antirheumatic drugs in reproduction, pregnancy, and lactation.ResultsIn pregnant women, antimalarials, sulfasalazine, azathioprine, calcineurin inhibitors, colchicine, and the tumour necrosis factor inhibitor certolizumab would mostly be prescribed as if the patient were not pregnant. Decisions to use other biologics during gestation were based on knowledge of relative drug safety and the risk of active disease without treatment. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and glucocorticoids during pregnancy were largely restricted to the control of active disease. In breastfeeding women, experts would use most antirheumatic drugs as if the patient were not breastfeeding, including many biologics. In male patients planning a family, experts would use many drugs as if the patient did not plan to father a child, including colchicine, methotrexate, leflunomide, sildenafil, and biologics, while Janus kinase inhibitors and other drugs with limited data would be avoided or used only to treat severe disease.ConclusionsThese findings highlight a progressive shift towards individualised, disease activity-guided prescribing, alongside growing confidence in the relative safety of several antirheumatic drugs. Expert surveys help define consensus, identify uncertainties, and guide future practice.

INTRODUCTION

The treatment of patients with rheumatic diseases during the phase of reproduction, pregnancy, and breastfeeding remains a challenging task. From the patient’s perspective, there is often great concern that the medication could harm their child. From the physician’s perspective, the challenge is to minimise the risk of harm from medication for the child while preventing the negative impact of active disease states on reproduction, pregnancy and general maternal health [1]. Given the current evidence and EULAR (European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology) recommendations on antirheumatic drugs in the context of reproduction, pregnancy, and lactation [2], we intended to analyse to what extent experts apply considerations of disease activity when using antirheumatic drugs in their day-to-day practice.

METHODS

In December 2023, 20 prescribers of the EULAR task force for recommendations on the use of antirheumatic drugs in reproduction, pregnancy, and lactation were asked to fill in a web-based questionnaire similar to the one used for the previous EULAR task force about a decade ago [3]. The current questionnaire addressed the drug use in the day-to-day management of patients before or during pregnancy, during breastfeeding, and in male patients planning to father a child. For each drug, respondents had to choose among four options: (1) recommend the drug in the same way as if the patient was not pregnant/breastfeeding/planning to father a child, (2) recommend the drug only if one feared at least moderate disease in its absence, (3) recommend the drug only if one feared severe disease in its absence, or (4) never recommend the drug in pregnancy/while breastfeeding/in a male patient planning to father a child.

RESULTS

Drug use in pregnancy

The results show that the potential risk of disease flares in the absence of the medication plays an important role when considering prescribing antirheumatic drugs in a pregnant woman (Fig 1). Nevertheless, some medications would be prescribed by 55% to 90% of the experts in the same way as for nonpregnant patients; these include traditional pregnancy-compatible drugs such as antimalarials, sulfasalazine, thiopurines, cyclosporin, tacrolimus, colchicine, and intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIGs). Among tumour necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi), 84% of experts would prescribe the Fc-free compound certolizumab irrespective of the patient being pregnant or not; the percentage was lower for other TNFi (etanercept: 33%, infliximab 21%, adalimumab 21%, and golimumab 16%). TNFi were more liberally used in pregnancy compared to non-TNFi biologics (eg, rituximab, tocilizumab, and ustekinumab), and the majority of experts would base their decision on the estimation of the risk of flares during pregnancy without the biologic. Regarding nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and oral glucocorticoids, experts would recommend these drugs almost exclusively for the control of active disease states. Among vasodilating drugs, sildenafil would be used by 22% of the experts in the same way as if the patient were not pregnant, yet the majority would mainly see it as a treatment option for more severe disease states. For drugs with sparse or insufficient data, eg, iloprost, bosentan, vocloporin, apremilast, leflunomide, or Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi), the majority of respondents would never prescribe these drugs in pregnant patients, and a minority would only use them to treat severe disease. Drug use during breastfeeding

Compared to drug use in pregnancy, the majority of experts would prescribe compatible drugs to breastfeeding patient in the same way as if the patient did not breastfeed; this accounts for glucocorticoids, NSAIDs, colchicine, antimalarials, sulfasalazine, thiopurines, cyclosporine and tacrolimus, and most biologics (Fig 2). Regarding NSAIDs, experts were more restrictive with selective COX-2 inhibitors, like celecoxib and etoricoxib, compared to ibuprofen or diclofenac. Regarding biologics during the first 2 weeks postpartum, 47% of experts would give them to breastfeeding patients just as they would if the patient did not breastfeed, and 54% only if they feared moderate or severe disease activity without the biologic. Drug use in male patients planning a family

For male patients who wish to father a child, 60% to 80% of experts would use most antirheumatic drugs in the same way as for men not planning to conceive; this applies to NSAIDs, glucocorticoids, colchicine, antimalarials, thiopurines, cyclosporine, tacrolimus, methotrexate, leflunomide, mycophenolate, sildenafil, IVIG, and most biologics (Fig 3). For biologics, concerns about potential disease flare in the absence of therapy were raised more often with non-TNFi agents (eg, guselkumab, anifrolumab, mepolizumab, eculizumab), whereas TNFi were commonly used in men regardless of family planning status. Regarding JAKi, 38% to 43 % of experts would never recommend them to male patients who are planning to conceive, and 38% to 48 % would consider them for the control of active disease. DISCUSSION

In recent years, the use of antirheumatic drugs during pregnancy has continued to evolve towards a more individualised approach based on new evidence on drug safety and the need for effective disease control, as this is considered a major determinant for favourable pregnancy outcomes. Compared to previous expert surveys, one in 2016 [3] and another in 2022 [4], we observed a shift with more permissive use of compatible conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs and biologics, especially TNFi, during pregnancy, along with increased caution regarding the use of glucocorticoids and NSAIDs. The current expert decisions reflect the growing body of reassuring evidence on biologics in pregnancy, including the lack of serious infections of infants exposed in utero; on the other hand, glucocorticoids and NSAIDs are associated with risks that depend on the dose and duration of exposure [2,5]. In line with our survey results, others also noted an increase in the use of TNFi during pregnancy, though its use was often avoided in late pregnancy [[6], [7], [8]]. Moreover, in the context of conflicting information between recommendations and the summary of product characteristics, it can be difficult for treating physicians to find a shared decision on the use of an individual drug with the patient [9].

After delivery, disease flares are common, and treatment must be adjusted to a compatible medication in breastfeeding mothers. International guidelines recommend exclusive breastfeeding for about 6 months [10]. The complex components of human milk, including antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and immunoregulatory factors, also reduce life-threatening risks in premature infants like necrotising enterocolitis or sepsis [10]. Given the accumulated evidence, more biologics are now compatible with breastfeeding [3,5]. This explains the increase in the proportion of experts who would use biologics (TNFi and non-TNFi) without restrictions in breastfeeding patients compared to the 2016 survey [2,3,5]. Regarding the transfer of biologics in colostrum (the low milk volume during the 2-5 days postpartum), data show slightly higher levels compared to those in mature milk (ie, tocilizumab below 0.5 µg/ml) [5]. Nevertheless, most experts would use biologics during the first 2 weeks postpartum only if they feared moderate or severe disease activity without this treatment, taking into account aspects such as maternal wound healing and the risk of infection.

In male patients who wish to have children, expert opinions on the use of antirheumatic drugs differ significantly from those for pregnant women, in that a considerable number of drugs were recommended in the same way as if the patient did not wish to conceive a child with their partner. This view reflects the current knowledge about many antirheumatic drugs, which did not demonstrate increased rates of adverse drug-related effects on pregnancy outcomes after paternal exposure, with most reassuring evidence for TNFi biologics [5]. Even drugs like methotrexate and mycophenolate that proved to be teratogenic when used by women during the first trimester have not shown increased rates of harm to the embryo when used by male patients during conception [5]. This reflects the latest EULAR recommendations, which consider both drugs to be compatible for men planning to father a child [2]. Regarding fertility, methotrexate has no negative impact on sperm quality, while cyclophosphamide can disrupt spermatogenesis and lead to permanent infertility at cumulative doses of above 4000 mg/m2 [5,11,12], explaining the very restrictive use of cyclophosphamide in very severe disease and after sperm cryopreservation. The experts’ view on JAKi in men planning a family was rather cautious, which mirrors the discrepancy between reassuring data on semen parameters for filgotinib, yet very limited data for all JAKi on pregnancy outcomes following paternal exposure [5,13]. Accordingly, EULAR recommends that alternative treatment options to JAKi should be considered for male patients who wish to father a child [2].

Collectively, these results underscore the essential components of personalised decision-making, namely, considerations of disease activity in the absence of an effective medication and current evidence on the relative safety of many antirheumatic drugs with regard to reproduction, pregnancy, and breastfeeding. Conducting surveys among experts represents a valuable tool to systematically gather up-to-date knowledge and identify emerging trends regarding drug use in this context. Such surveys help to highlight consensus, reveal areas of uncertainty, and guide future research and clinical practice in this complex field.
