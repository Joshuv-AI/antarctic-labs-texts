Abstract
We embed a taste for nonconformity into a canonical Bikhchandani–Hirshleifer–Welch social-learning model. Agents value both correctness and choosing the minority action (fixed or proportion-based bonus). We study exogenous signals and endogenous acquisition with a fixed entry cost and convex cost of precision in a Gaussian–quadratic specification. Contrarian motives shift equilibrium cutoffs away from one-half and expand the belief region where information is purchased, sustaining informative actions; conditional on investing, chosen precision is lower near central beliefs. Welfare is shaped by a trade-off: mild contrarianism counteracts premature herding, whereas strong contrarianism steers actions against informative social signals and induces low-value experimentation. A tractable characterization delivers closed-form cutoffs, comparative statics, and transparent welfare comparisons. Applications include scientific priority races and academic diffusion, where distinctiveness yields rents yet excessive contrarianism erodes information aggregation.



Keywords:
social learning; information cascades; nonconformity; endogenous information acquisition; herding.

1
Introduction

Why do individuals sometimes follow the crowd, and at other times seek to stand apart? Sequential models of social learning have shown how rational agents observing others’ actions may disregard private signals, generating herding and fragile cascades
(
Bikhchandani et al., 1992
;
Smith and Sørensen, 2000
)
. Such dynamics matter in diverse settings, from financial markets to cultural adoption. Yet these canonical frameworks typically assume that agents care only about the accuracy of their actions, not about their relative position to the crowd.

In practice, the desire to differentiate from others—whether for reputational, identity-based, or cultural reasons—plays a central role. Scientists may pursue contrarian theories for the reward of priority; consumers may seek distinctiveness in fashion; political actors may defy consensus to appeal to niche constituencies. These motives for nonconformity are well documented outside economics, but little is known about their implications for the informational dynamics of cascades.

This paper introduces a simple way to study contrarian preferences within the BHW framework. We embed a utility bonus from choosing the minority action into a sequential social-learning model. The analysis yields three sets of results. First, contrarian motives shift equilibrium cutoffs, expanding the region of beliefs in which agents experiment on their private signals. Second, when information acquisition is endogenous, contrarian preferences widen the set of beliefs where signals are purchased but lower the precision chosen conditional on investing. Third, welfare displays an inverted-U shape: mild contrarianism improves information aggregation by sustaining experimentation, while strong contrarianism undermines learning by inducing costly, low-value deviations from the crowd.

Our leading application is the diffusion of ideas in science. As
Planck, 1949
and
Kuhn, 1962
emphasized, progress often depends on scholars willing to resist orthodoxy. Yet excessive contrarianism risks fragmentation and the erosion of shared standards of evidence. By formalizing this trade-off, our framework links the sociology of science with the economics of cascades. The same logic also applies in cultural diffusion and online platforms, where nonconformist incentives shape collective outcomes.

2
Related Literature

Our work sits at the intersection of the information-cascades tradition and preference-based (non-)conformity. The canonical sequential social-learning models—beginning with
Bikhchandani et al., 1992
and surveyed recently by
Bikhchandani et al., 2024
—show how rational agents, observing predecessors’ actions, may rationally ignore their own signals and herd, leading to fragile aggregate outcomes and even complete breakdown of learning. Building on this foundation,
Smith and Sørensen, 2000
characterized when learning can stall in “pathological” ways and clarified conditions under which herds (or limit cascades) arise; see also their overview in
Smith and Sørensen, 2011
. A parallel literature studies observation constrained by networks (rather than full histories):
Acemoglu et al., 2011
characterize Bayesian learning over general networks and the conditions ensuring asymptotic learning. More recent contributions, such as
Dasaratha and He, 2022
, extend this perspective to richer network structures and identify when persistent mislearning can occur.

A large recent wave examines
why
learning fails in richer informational environments. Misspecification and coarse inference can generate persistent mistakes even without payoff externalities:
Bohren, 2016
shows model-misspecified learners may herd on wrong actions;
Frick et al., 2020
show that misinterpreting others’ motives/actions can make social learning extremely fragile. Empirically,
Eyster et al., 2014
document systematic mislearning in laboratory cascades, while
Bohren et al., 2019
illustrate how inference errors and discrimination dynamics can sustain mistaken beliefs in field data. Relatedly,
Kartik and Van Weelden, 2020
analyze informational herding under model uncertainty. These contributions underscore that both belief formation and feedback channels matter for the diffusion of actions, norms, and ideas. In product-market settings, social learning can interact with dynamic competition:
Lukyanov and Azova, 2025
show that duopoly pricing with observational learning yields price dispersion and cascade risk with sharp comparative statics for precision and search frictions, offering a complementary market-structure lens.

We differ in that we introduce
a taste for nonconformity
directly into a BHW-type environment. Classic preference-based conformity goes back to
Bernheim, 1994
, where actions signal dispositions and social status concerns generate conformity. See also
Akerlof and Kranton, 2000
and
Bénabou and Tirole, 2011
for the broader identity-economics perspective. Our paper inverts the sign: agents obtain utility from
being in the minority
. While preferences for distinctiveness are well documented in adjacent literatures (social psychology and identity; e.g., “need for uniqueness”), there is comparatively little in economics embedding
anti-conformist
tastes inside a sequential social-learning game with Bayesian inference. Related work that mixes identity/uniqueness motives with social dynamics (e.g.,
Golman et al., 2021
) or delayed anti-conformist responses (
Touboul, 2014
) points to rich dynamics, but these are not cast in the BHW Bayesian-learning environment that disciplines inference from actions. Our contribution is to keep the BHW backbone and show how a tractable nonconformist utility shifter (either fixed or increasing in the size of the opposing crowd) systematically reshapes thresholds, experimentation, and welfare.

In settings with payoff externalities, actions can affect others’ payoffs directly (beyond informational externalities). Papers blending social learning with payoff complementarities or strategic interactions—e.g.,
Dasgupta et al., 2000
,
Ali and Kartik, 2012
—study how coordination motives and forward-looking incentives affect herding. Our setting is different: the externality we add is
taste-based
(nonconformity) rather than technological, and we keep informational externalities as in BHW. This allows clean separation: how do agents’ optimal cutoffs and experimentation regions shift solely because they prefer to deviate from the crowd?

A second strand we connect to is
endogenous information acquisition
. In BHW and follow-ups, signal structures are typically exogenous. We instead allow agents to pay a fixed cost to acquire a signal and then choose its precision at convex cost, in the spirit of the information-choice literature (see
Veldkamp, 2011
;
Vives, 2008
). Classic dynamic experimentation and strategic delay (e.g.,
Chamley and Gale, 1994
) highlight that informational externalities distort investment timing even without taste heterogeneity. Recent empirical work, such as
Lu et al., 2021
, documents how information acquisition responds to beliefs in markets, providing motivation for our endogenous-precision extension. We show that embedding nonconformist preferences into a sequential acquisition problem yields sharp comparative statics for the extensive margin (acquire vs. skip) and the intensive margin (chosen precision). Parametric Gaussian-quadratic examples then deliver closed-form thresholds and enable welfare comparisons that incorporate the utility from distinctiveness. A related informational design angle appears in
Lukyanov, 2025
, where an observer aggregates reports from reputation-motivated experts of uncertain precision; light-touch scoring or small deviation costs restore strict informativeness and asymptotic efficiency in both binary and Gaussian variants.

Finally, our targeted applications—scientific priority and cultural/academic diffusion—tie into work on reputation and advice. Experts may shade recommendations due to reputational forces, affecting downstream learning and diffusion; see, e.g.,
Lukyanov and Vlasova, 2025
;
Lukyanov et al., 2025b
. These papers are complementary: reputational conservatism or correction costs distort learning in one direction, while our mechanism isolates intrinsic contrarian motives as a primitive preference shifter. Closer to our normative angle,
Lukyanov and Cheredina, 2025
study conditions under which cascades persist and the costs of correcting them, offering a welfare lens that our analysis extends by allowing society to value minority positions per se. Outside advice markets,
Lukyanov et al., 2025a
analyze career-concerns when workers endogenously choose between public, signal-generating self-employment and opaque firm employment, yielding an absorbing opacity region; the selection and inference forces they identify dovetail with our emphasis on belief dynamics under nonconformity.

Relative to the JEL survey
(
Bikhchandani et al., 2024
)
, we add a simple, microfounded anti-conformist utility to the baseline BHW game and (i) characterize how it shifts equilibrium cutoffs and crowd-following/defying regions; (ii) endogenize information acquisition (fixed cost and convex precision cost) to map public belief into optimal precision for conformists vs. nonconformists; and (iii) deliver transparent welfare comparisons that
include
the utility from distinctiveness. Empirically minded readers can interpret nonconformity as identity/priority rents in scientific communities; theoretically, our results isolate when minority-seeking stabilizes experimentation and when it devolves into uninformative contrarianism.

Remark 3.1

4
Equilibrium Analysis

This section characterizes action cutoffs and their comparative statics under contrarian preferences.
We first show that optimal actions are posterior-threshold rules with a cutoff that shifts monotonically in the nonconformist intensity
k
.

In the Gaussian specification we obtain an equivalent log-likelihood ratio cutoff and, later, endogenize information acquisition to map public beliefs into investment regions and chosen precisions.

Proposition 4.1
.

Fix
k\geq 0
and an information structure with log-likelihood ratio
\ell(s)
.
At any public belief
\mu
, the optimal action is a cutoff rule.
Equivalently, there exists a function
\tau(k,\mu)
increasing in
k
such that the agent chooses
a=1
iff

\log\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}+\ell(s)\;\geq\;\tau(k,\mu).

When
k=0
,
\tau(0,\mu)=0
(the Bayesian benchmark); for
k>0
, the cutoff shifts to favor the minority action.

Proof sketch.

The nonconformist bonus is decreasing in predicted popularity, which is monotone in
\mu
.
Thus the bonus enters additively in the
a=1
vs.
a=0
comparison in LLR units, shifting the indifference threshold by
\tau(k,\mu)
; monotonicity in
k
follows.
An equivalent posterior form appears in Lemma
4.2
.
∎

The proof follows by noting that the contrarian bonus is strictly decreasing in the predicted popularity
p_{a}(h)
, which is itself monotone in
\mu
. Hence, the bonus enters additively into the comparison of expected utilities, shifting the indifference threshold. The Gaussian example in Section
4.3
makes this explicit.

In the Gaussian specification we normalize
s\mid\theta\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2})
with precision
\rho=1/\sigma^{2}
, so the log-likelihood ratio is
\ell(s)=\rho\,(s-\tfrac{1}{2})
(means
0
and
1
, equal variances).

With proportion-based nonconformity
b(p)=k(1-p)
and the standard popularity approximation
p_{1}(h)\approx\mu

c(h)\;=\;\frac{1}{2}+k\!\left(p_{1}(h)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\;\approx\;\frac{1}{2}+k\!\left(\mu-\tfrac{1}{2}\right),

which corresponds in LLR units to

\tau(k,\mu)\;=\;\logit\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}+k(\mu-\tfrac{1}{2})\Big)\;\approx\;2k\!\left(\mu-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\quad\text{for beliefs near }\tfrac{1}{2}.

Hence, contrarian intensity shifts the decision boundary linearly in
\mu
, and more strongly so around the center.

We unify the nonconformist term via a bonus function

Fixed bonus for deviating from the modal action.

.

Fix a history

\Delta U_{i}(s_{i},h)\;=\;\big(2\,\mu_{i}(s_{i},h)-1\big)\;+\;\Delta(h),\quad\text{where}\quad\Delta(h)\equiv b(p_{1})-b(p_{0}).

a_{i}=1\iff\mu_{i}(s_{i},h)\;\geq\;c(h),\qquad c(h)\;=\;\frac{1-\Delta(h)}{2}.

The baseline expected payoff of

Implications.

When

(no nonconformity), the cutoff is

Remark 4.3
.

The inequality
\mathbb{E}[1-p_{a}\mid\rho^{\prime}]\!\geq\!\mathbb{E}[1-p_{a}\mid\rho]
uses symmetry and that “minority” is defined relative to
\mu
(via

). This covers beliefs in a neighborhood of

4.3

Corollary 4.4
.

With
b(p)=k\,\mathbf{1}\{p<1/2\}
and
k\geq 0
,

\Delta(h)\;=\;\begin{cases}-k,&p_{1}>1/2,\\[2.0pt]
\;\;k,&p_{1}<1/2,\\[2.0pt]
\;\;0,&p_{1}=1/2,\end{cases}\qquad c(h)=\begin{cases}(1+k)/2,&p_{1}>1/2,\\[2.0pt]
(1-k)/2,&p_{1}<1/2,\\[2.0pt]
1/2,&p_{1}=1/2.\end{cases}

Thus a more (less) popular action faces a higher (lower) posterior cutoff.

Corollary 4.5
.

With
b(p)=k\,(1-p)
and
k\geq 0
,

\Delta(h)\;=\;k\,(p_{0}-p_{1})\;=\;k\,(1-2p_{1}),\qquad c(h)\;=\;\frac{1-\Delta(h)}{2}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}\;+\;k\Big(p_{1}-\tfrac{1}{2}\Big).

Hence

\frac{\partial c}{\partial p_{1}}=k

4.2
Informative actions and cascades

Actions are informative when they correlate with the underlying state after conditioning on the public belief. In the canonical BHW environment, thresholds that sit at
\tfrac{1}{2}
generate early herding and, once the social signal overwhelms typical private realizations, cascades in which actions cease to react to information. A taste for nonconformity perturbs the action threshold away from
\tfrac{1}{2}
in the direction of the minority (Lemma
4.2
), so the mapping from beliefs to action probabilities shifts even when the signal technology is unchanged.

The question we address here is whether these shifts sustain responsiveness to information or merely replace conformity with uninformative contrarianism. The answer depends on where the public belief lies: near the center, contrarian thresholds can keep actions sensitive to private signals; far from the center, they can push behavior against an already informative social signal. The Gaussian specialization in Section
4.3
makes these forces explicit and links them to the appearance or disappearance of cascades.

Given history
h
, let
\underline{\mu}(h):=\inf_{s}\mu_{i}(s,h)
and
\overline{\mu}(h):=\sup_{s}\mu_{i}(s,h)
denote the posterior range induced by the most unfavorable and most favorable private signals (well-defined under MLRP).

Definition 4.6
.

At history
h
, agent
i
’s action is
informative
iff

\underline{\mu}(h)<c(h)<\overline{\mu}(h)
.
Equivalently, there exist signal realizations that lead to each action.

Definition 4.7
.

A
(1-)cascade
occurs at
h
if
\underline{\mu}(h)\geq c(h)
so that
a_{i}=1
regardless of
s_{i}
.
A
(0-)cascade
occurs at
h
if
\overline{\mu}(h)\leq c(h)
so that
a_{i}=0
regardless of
s_{i}
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Fix a history
h
. Relative to

, any non-degenerate

shifts

away from

in the direction that disfavors the currently popular action. Consequently:

If

If

In particular, for small

Parts (1)–(2) follow from Lemma

Remark 4.9

If the likelihood ratio

Lemma 4.10

Fix an interior public belief
\mu\in(0,1)

\rho^{*}(\mu,k)\in\arg\max_{\rho\geq 0}\Big\{\,G(\mu,\rho,k)-\tfrac{c}{2}\rho^{2}-F\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\rho>0\}\,\Big\},

Write the objective as

For any

Because

The lemma isolates the extensive margin: stronger nonconformity makes experimentation worthwhile at more beliefs. To understand the net effect on information acquisition, we next examine the intensive margin in the Gaussian specification, where the marginal value of precision can be expressed directly in terms of the signal threshold.

Gaussian signals

6
Applications and Motivation

Scientific progress often turns on the tension between conformity and contrarian insight. A scholar’s private signal—whether from research, experiment, or novel intuition—may clash with prevailing orthodoxy, and the personal payoff to standing apart can be substantial. As Max Planck put it in his
Scientific Autobiography
:
7
7

7

Later popularized as “Planck’s Principle” in the sociology of science.

“A new scientific truth does not triumph by convincing its opponents and making them see the light, but rather because its opponents eventually die, and a new generation grows up that is familiar with it”
(
Planck, 1949
, pp. 33–34)
.

Kuhn echoed this theme in his
Structure of Scientific Revolutions
, where paradigm shifts are portrayed less as proofs than as generational change and persuasion:

“Paradigm change cannot be justified by proof. Instead it must be decided by persuasion and conversion, and often by generational replacement”
(
Kuhn, 1962
, p. 151)
.

These remarks highlight the persistent role of contrarian motives in academic life: they keep inquiry alive against the pull of consensus. Our framework formalizes this intuition in a sequential-learning environment: by embedding a direct payoff to nonconformity, we can study when such motives expand experimentation and when they risk fragmenting standards of evidence.

Similar forces appear in cultural diffusion, fashion, and online networks, where status often derives from distinctiveness. While our primary application is scientific discovery, these parallels illustrate the broad relevance of contrarian motives in shaping collective outcomes.

These examples motivate our theoretical exercise: when does a preference for nonconformity strengthen the informational role of individual actions, and when does it distort them? The analysis that follows formalizes this trade-off, showing how contrarian motives affect cutoffs, precision choices, and ultimately welfare.

7
Discussion and Concluding Remarks

Our analysis shows how embedding contrarian motives into a sequential learning environment reshapes both information acquisition and welfare. On the positive side, nonconformist preferences expand the set of public beliefs under which agents find it worthwhile to experiment, thereby sustaining informative actions and counteracting premature herding. At the same time, these preferences lower chosen precision conditional on investing, reflecting the substitution of “being different” for “being accurate.” The welfare consequences follow an inverted-U pattern: mild contrarianism is socially beneficial, but excessive contrarianism drives agents to act against already-informative public beliefs, generating correctness losses and inefficient experimentation.

Beyond the formal results, the framework sheds light on broader phenomena. In scientific communities, priority races and reputational incentives make standing apart from orthodoxy valuable, but contrarianism taken too far undermines shared standards of evidence. The same logic applies in cultural diffusion, where nonconformist motives keep diversity alive yet risk fragmentation. Our model thus offers a disciplined way to analyze when minority-seeking stabilizes experimentation and when it turns into destructive contrarianism.

The simplicity of our specification suggests several extensions. Allowing heterogeneity in contrarian intensity
k
—possibly private information—would capture richer identity-based motives and may generate assortative dynamics or segmentation. Introducing networks of observation, or continuous-time arrivals, could further align the model with empirical settings such as scientific collaboration or social media. Finally, linking contrarianism with forward-looking incentives (as in reputation models) may illuminate how agents balance distinctiveness with credibility.

Taken together, the results highlight a central message: contrarian motives, when moderate, improve learning by counteracting conformity, but when extreme, undermine the informativeness of actions. This trade-off connects the sociology of science with the economics of cascades, offering both a positive framework and a normative lens for thinking about conformity, diversity, and the dynamics of ideas.

\mu=\tfrac{1}{2}
it equals
1/2
). On the extensive margin, Proposition
4.14
implies beliefs with
\rho^{*}(\mu,0)=0
may switch to
\rho^{*}(\mu,k)>0
for
k>0
, raising welfare whenever the net value clears
F
. Hence the right-derivative is
\geq 0
everywhere and
>0
on any set of
\mu
of positive measure where the investment margin is (or becomes) active.

Formally, write
G(\mu,\rho,k)=\mathrm{Corr}(\mu,\rho,k)+k\,M(\mu,\rho)
, where

M(\mu,\rho)
is the expected minority-selection probability under the optimal cutoff.
Under MLRP,
M(\mu,\rho)
is (weakly) increasing in
\rho
, so
G
has increasing
differences in
(\rho,k)
. By Lemma
4.13
, the maximized value

\max_{\rho\geq 0}\{G(\mu,\rho,k)-\tfrac{c}{2}\rho^{2}\}
is (weakly) increasing in
k
,
which delivers the nonnegative extensive-margin effect.
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.2
.

Fix
(c,F)
and consider beliefs in a neighborhood of
\mu=\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Under the proportion-based bonus and the Gaussian cutoff,
\tau(k,\mu)
moves linearly away from
0
as
k
rises, so actions become increasingly contrarian with respect to the public belief. Near
\mu=\tfrac{1}{2}
, the correctness component of
G
is strictly decreasing in
|\tau|
while, by symmetry, the contrarian bonus is (to first order) insensitive to precision at
\mu=\tfrac{1}{2}
. Since precision choices incur convex costs, for sufficiently large
k
the decline in expected correctness and the information cost dominate the (linear) bonus term. Continuity then yields
\partial_{k}W^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mu,k)<0
for all
k\geq\bar{k}
on an open neighborhood of
\mu=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Proof of Proposition
5.4
.

Fix
\lambda\in[0,1)
. Write
W_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mu,k)=\Pr\{\text{correct}\mid\mu,\rho^{*}\}+\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[b(p_{a})\mid\mu,\rho^{*}]-\tfrac{c}{2}\rho^{*2}-F\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\rho^{*}>0\}
with
\rho^{*}=\rho^{*}(\mu,k)
.

Local increase at
k=0
.
For each
\mu
,

\left.\partial_{k}W_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mu,k)\right|_{k=0}\;=\;\lambda\cdot\left.\partial_{k}\mathbb{E}[b(p_{a})]\right|_{k=0}\;+\;\left.\partial_{\rho}W_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mu,0)\right|_{\rho=\rho^{*}(\mu,0)}\cdot\left.\partial_{k}\rho^{*}(\mu,k)\right|_{k=0}.

The first term is weakly positive (linear bonus, downweighted by
\lambda\in[0,1)
). The second term is nonnegative wherever the investment margin is active and strictly positive on a set of beliefs of positive
\Pi
-measure by Lemma
C.1
and Lemma
4.10
. Integrating over
\Pi
yields a strictly positive aggregate derivative at
k=0
.

Eventual decline.
Take a neighborhood
U
of
\mu=\tfrac{1}{2}
with
\Pi(U)>0
. As
k\to\infty
,
c(\mu,k)=\tfrac{1}{2}+k(\mu-\tfrac{1}{2})
implies
\tau(k,\mu)=\log\frac{c}{1-c}\to\pm\infty
and the signal threshold
s^{*}(\mu,k,\rho)
moves into a tail for
\mu\in U
. Then
\partial_{\rho}G(\mu,\rho,k)\to 0
uniformly on
U
and, with
F\geq 0
and convex costs,
\rho^{*}(\mu,k)\to 0
on
U
. Thus

W_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mu,k)\;\to\;\Pr\{\text{contrarian action is correct}\}\;+\;\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[b(p_{a})]\quad\text{on }U,

with the first term
<1/2
and the second term
linear
in
k
but downweighted by
\lambda<1
. Because behavior becomes fully contrarian in
U
while the evaluator discounts the bonus, the correctness loss dominates for sufficiently large
k
, yielding
\partial_{k}W_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mu,k)<0
on
U
. Integrating over
\Pi
gives
\partial_{k}\mathcal{W}_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{eq}}(k)<0
for all
k\geq k_{u}(\lambda)
, for some finite
k_{u}(\lambda)
. By continuity, there exists
k_{\ell}(\lambda)\leq k_{u}(\lambda)
with strict increase on
[0,k_{\ell}(\lambda)]
and strict decrease on
[k_{u}(\lambda),\infty)
.
∎
