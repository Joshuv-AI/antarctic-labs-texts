Identification of microbiome-associated loci in super hybrid rice reveals the regulation of OsBRI1 on nitrogen-dependent root microbiome assembly
Authors: Mengting Zhang, Yinyue Wang, Qiongqiong Zhang, Huacai Wang, Yongsheng Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHost genetics is known to determine the composition of the plant microbiome, which plays an essential role in plant growth and nutrient use efficiency. Although an increasing number of candidate genetic loci controlling microbial selection have been identified through the population-level association studies in plants, only a few have been shown to shape microbiome assembly, and the genetic factors driving microbiome assembly remain largely elusive.ResultsIn this study, we use a segregating F2 population of LYP9, the widely cultivated super hybrid rice, to map root microbiome-associated loci by simultaneous whole genome resequencing and 16S rDNA amplicon sequencing. We identify 60 quantitative trait loci (QTLs) that correlate with the abundance of 43 ASVs of the root microbiome and reveal several microbiome-associated hotspot genomic regions. One such region is associated with different microbial taxa and contains 7 cytochrome P450 genes potentially involved in the brassinosteroid (BR) biosynthesis pathway. We further show that the miR444-overexpressing rice plants with increased BR biosynthesis alter the composition of the root microbiome and that the BR receptor OsBRI1 confers the distinction between nitrate- and ammonium-grown rice root microbiomes.ConclusionsUsing QTL analysis, our study identifies a set of genetic loci that link the root microbiome composition of the hybrid rice LYP9 and reveals that OsBRI1 regulates the assembly of the nitrogen-dependent rice root microbiome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07653-w.Keywords: Root microbiome, QTL, Nitrogen, OsBRI1, Super hybrid rice LYP9

Background

Plants host diverse but taxonomically structured microbial communities collectively termed the plant microbiome. The plant microbiome has co-evolved with its hosts over millions of years and plays an essential role in plant growth, resistance to invading pathogens, and acquisition of limiting nutrients [1–4]. For instance, it has been shown that the root microbiome is reshaped to alleviate phosphate starvation and iron limitation in Arabidopsis and rice [3–6]. The bacteria of the taxa Oxalobacteraceae in the rhizosphere were enriched by nitrogen (N) deprivation to promote maize growth and N acquisition [7]. A highly conserved core bacterial microbiome with N-fixation capacity inhabits the xylem sap and significantly contributes to N accumulation in maize [8].

Besides the external environments, host genetics is a major driver to shape the assembly of plant microbiomes. This is evident from the fact that plant domestication and crop diversification have a substantial impact on the heritability of microbiome composition and function [9–11]. As a promising strategy to manipulate the microbiome through genetic improvement for crop breeding, some studies are searching for host genetic variation and genes involved in plant microbiome assembly [12, 13]. Quantitative trait loci (QTL) mapping and genome-wide association studies (GWAS), two major methods deciphering the genetic loci controlling crop growth- and yield-related traits, have recently been exploited to investigate plant genetic loci associated with microbiome traits using natural populations and hybrid populations of plants. Such studies have resulted in the identification of many causal plant genetic loci correlated with the composition of the rhizosphere, phyllosphere, and rhizoplane communities in Arabidopsis, maize, tomato, foxtail millet, rice, barley, and sorghum [14–20]. Analysis of the genes located at these microbiome-associated loci through gene-enriched KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes), GO (Gene Ontology), and RNA-seq approaches reveals that some signaling pathways and genes, such as the specialized metabolite biosynthesis, phytohormone biosynthesis, and immune system, could participate in shaping the plant microbiome [14–20]. For example, QTL mapping using the barley segregating population between the elite cultivar (Hordeum vulgare ssp. spontaneum) ‘Barke’ and the wild ancestor accession (Hordeum vulgare ssp. spontaneum), Escudero-Martinez et al. identified the genetic locus QRMC-3HS, which includes a nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat (NLR) gene, as a major determinant of barley microbiome composition [18]. GWAS and subsequent functional studies demonstrate that the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway synthesizes the compound 4-hydroxycinnamic acid (4-HCA) via OsPAL02, thereby enriching the bacteria belonging to Pseudomonadales in the rice phyllosphere [20]. Nevertheless, there is still a lack of direct evidence linking the roles of most identified genetic loci to plant microbiome assembly.

Ammonium (NH4+) and nitrate (NO3−) are two major inorganic N forms which can be directly absorbed by plant roots. The application of NH4+ and NO3− usually results in acidification and alkalinization of the extracellular environment, respectively [21, 22], and differentially affects the carbon and N metabolism [22–24]. NH4+ and NO3− also act as signals to differentially regulate genome-wide gene expression of roots [25]. These observations suggest that the root microbiomes of plants grown with NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻ may differ, though further investigation is required to confirm this. Brassonisteroinds (BRs) are a kind of plant-specific steroidal hormone that play critical roles in plant growth and response to biotic and abiotic stresses [26]. In addition, BRs have been shown to play an important role in NO3−- and NH4+-triggered root growth [27, 28]. It is interesting to know whether BRs are involved in the root microbiome assembly of plants grown with NH4+ and NO3−.

In this study, we perform QTL analysis using a segregating LYP9 F2 population of 171 lines to identify rice genetic loci linking root bacterial abundance through whole genome resequencing and 16S rDNA amplicon sequencing. LYP9 is a widely cultivated super-hybrid rice from its parental cultivars 93-11 and PA64S and shows root microbiome heterosis [29]. We identify 60 QTLs that correlate with the abundance of 43 ASVs of the root microbiome and reveal several microbiome-associated hotspot genomic regions. One such region is associated with different microbial taxa and contains 7 cytochrome P450 genes potentially involved in the BR biosynthesis pathway based on the KEGG. We further show that BR biosynthesis and signaling affect the assembly of the root microbiome and that OsBRI1 confers the distinction between NO3−- and NH4+-grown root microbiome. Our study through QTL analysis identifies a set of genetic loci linking LYP9 root bacterial community composition and reveals that OsBRI1 regulates N-dependent root microbiome assembly in rice.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The F2 segregation population of LYP9 was used for QTL mapping. All plants were genotyped by resequencing. To collect samples from the F2 population for resequencing and amplicon sequencing, rice seeds were dehulled and surface sterilized using 75% (v/v) ethanol and 3% (w/v) sodium hypochlorite to remove the seed surface-associated microbes, then germinated and grown on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (1/2 MS) agar medium for 14 days. The rice seedlings were then transplanted individually into the paddy field of Shunyi Farm, Beijing, China (40.130° N, 116.655° E) with a spacing of 25 cm between individuals. After about two months of growth, leaf samples were collected for genotyping, and root samples were collected for amplicon sequencing.

To collect root samples from the miR444-OE and osbri1 rice plants for amplicon sequencing, seeds from miR444-OE and osbri1 rice plants and corresponding wild type (WT) rice plants Oryza sativa ssp. japonica cv. Nipponbare and Taichung65 (conserved in our laboratory), respectively, were germinated and grown for 14 days in 1/2 MS agar medium as described above, then 5 seedlings each were individually transplanted into a plastic box (30 cm wide and 60 cm high) filled with the paddy field collected from Shunyi Farm, Beijing, China (40.130° N, 116.655° E) and grown in the greenhouse. Blank soil with no plants was established at the same time. For N nutrition treatments, rice plants were watered once every 7 days with the Kimura B solution [30] containing 10 mM NH4+ or 10 mM NO3− as the sole N source. After approximately two months of growth, root samples were collected from well-grown rice plants for amplicon sequencing.

QTL mapping

For plant genotyping, DNA was extracted from the leaf tissue of the parental plants and F2 individuals using the CTAB method. After checking by ND-2000 (NanoDrop Technologies), high-quality DNA samples (OD260/280 = 1.8 ~ 2.0, OD260/230 ≥ 2.0) were used for sequencing library construction according to the instructions of the TruSeq TM Nano DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Briefly, 1 µg of DNA was fragmented using the fragmentation buffer, followed by end repair, phosphorylation, and ‘A’ base addition. After quantification by TBS380, the paired-end DNA-seq library was sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq NovaSeq 6000 (PE150) from Major Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Raw reads were assigned to individual samples according to their nucleotide barcodes using the Axe package (v0.3.3) [31]. The low-quality reads were trimmed or discarded using Fastp software (v0.23.2) [32]. Then, the high-quality reads were mapped to the reference genome of Oryza sativa ssp. japonica cv. Nipponbare [33] using the BWA MEM software (v1.0.6) under default mapping parameters [34].

For linkage map construction and subsequent QTL mapping, the molecular markers were called using the GATK pipeline (v4.3.0.0) [35]. Variants were filtered using standard hard filtering parameters according to the GATK Best Practices pipeline (v4.3.0.0) [35]. Markers with a call rate >70% and sequence depth ≥ 2-fold were then used for linkage map construction using a two-step method with MSTmap software (v1.0–4) [36]. QTLs were detected for each of the ASV traits using the CIM method implemented in R/qtl software (v1.5.0) [37]. The LOD significance thresholds (P < 0.05) were determined by performing 1,000 permutation tests.

Amplicon sequencing

Amplicon sequencing was performed as previously described by Zhang et al. [29]. Briefly, root samples were thoroughly washed with sterile water until no visible soil particles remained. Three representative roots, approximately 10 cm in length, were then selected from each plant and pooled in a sterile 2 mL tube as a single root sample. For each F2 individual, nine roots were collected to form three replicate samples. Bulk soil samples were collected from unplanted sites as controls. All samples were stored at −80 °C until DNA extraction. Total DNA was extracted from the root samples using an E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After checking the integrity and quality of the extracted DNA on a 1% (w/v) agarose gel, the concentration and purity of the DNA samples were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 UV-vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, USA). The DNA samples were then used as templates for PCR to amplify the V3-V4 regions (515F-806R) or V5-V7 regions (799F-1193R) of bacterial 16S rDNA. Each sample was amplified in triplicate in a 20 µL reaction volume containing 4 µL of 5 × TransStart FastPfu buffer, 2 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µL of 5 µM forward primer, 0.8 µL of 5 µM reverse primer, 0.4 µL of TransStart FastPfu DNA Polymerase, and 10 ng template DNA. PCR amplification was performed according to the following parameters: 95 °C for 3 min; 27 cycles at 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 45 s, and 72 °C for 10 min; and a final hold at 4 °C. Sterile water instead of DNA template was included as a negative control. The reactions were then run on a 2% (w/v) agarose gel, and the desired PCR products were excised from the gel and purified using an AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After the quantification of DNA concentration using a Quantus™ Fluorometer (Promega, USA), the purified amplicons from each replicate were mixed and subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) according to the standard protocol by Major Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Amplicon sequencing data processing and statistical analysis

Amplicon sequencing data were processed as previously described by Zhang et al. [29]. Briefly, the raw 16S rDNA sequencing reads were demultiplexed, quality-filtered using Fastp (v0.19.6) [32] and merged using FLASH (v1.2.11) [38]. The high-quality sequences were then de-noised using the DADA2 pipeline plugin in QIIME2 [39, 40] with the recommended parameters to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). The taxonomy of each ASV representative sequence was classified with the Naive Bayes consensus taxonomy classifier implemented in QIIME2 using the 16S rDNA database (Silva v138) for bacteria [41]. The alpha (α) diversity index (Shannon index and Chao index) was calculated using the Mothur sofeware (v1.30.2) [42]. The significance of differences between groups was tested by Kruskal-Wallis. To estimate beta (β) diversity, Bray-Curtis distances between samples were used for principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) using the R (v3.3.1) vegan package (v2.5-3) [43]. The significance of differences between groups was tested by PERMANOVA (permutational multivariate analysis of variance). Relative abundance of bacterial taxa at the phylum level was classified using the python package (v2.7) in R (v3.3.1) [44]. The differentially enriched ASVs between groups were calculated using the DESeq2 package (v1.42.0) [45] or Wilcoxon rank sum test and plotted using a heatmap by the Complex Heatmap package (v2.18.0) in R (v3.3.1) [46]. When the P value was less than 0.05, the FDR-adjusted P value was below 0.2, and the log2 fold change was greater than 1.5, the ASVs were considered differentially enriched between groups. Functional annotation of bacterial taxa was carried out using the FAPROTAX database (v0.3.1) [47]. The functional significance of differences between groups was tested by Wilcoxon rank sum.

Results

Rice root microbiome-based QTL mapping

In order to map root microbiome-related QTLs of rice, we used a LYP9 F2 segregation population derived from the cross between the paternal variety 93-11 and the maternal cultivar PA64S. 93-11, PA64S and 171 F2 individuals were grown in paddy field (Beijing, Shunyi farm) and their genomes were resequenced individually using Illumina platform each with ~ 10 × coverage, resulting in a total of 1010.15 GB sequence data. The trimmed reads of 93-11, PA64S, and each F2 individuals were aligned to the rice reference genome (Oryza sativa ssp. japonica cv. Nipponbare) [33] for genotyping, and the mapped ratio per individual was over 98% of the total number of reads. A total of 4,381,023 high-quality SNPs were obtained on 12 chromosomes. Then, high-density bin genetic map was subsequently generated according to these SNPs using the software SEG-Map with a total of 1653 recombinant bins, and the total genetic distance of the linkage map was 1480.2 cM (Figs. 1A and 2; Supplemental Table 1).

The root-associated microbiome (microbes intimately colonizing on the root surface (rhizoplane) and dwelling inside roots (endosphere)) of the above-described population, including 4 93-11 plants, 4 PA64S plants, and 171 F2 individuals each with 3 replicates, were characterized by amplicon sequencing of the bacterial 16S rDNA gene V3-V4 region. After removing chimeric and the sequences derived from the organelle genomes, we obtained 327,808 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) belonging to 1829 genera, of which 655 genera were specifically in the F2 individuals (Fig. 1B). A PCoA analysis of ASVs demonstrated that the root microbiome of the F2 segregation population distributed around the parental varieties 93-11 and PA64S (Fig. 1C). Separation between the three replicates of the F2 populations was not observed (Fig. 1C). These observations suggest a significant impact of genotype on the root microbiome composition of the F2 lines.

For QTL mapping, we prioritized the 60 enriched ASVs, whose present in the F2 population was 70% or more (Supplemental Table 2). Their relative abundances in the F2 segregating population were used as quantitative phenotypes to search for significant associations with the established genetic markers. The relative abundances of the 60 ASVs in the F2 individuals were characterized by applying the rank-based inverse normal transformation (INT) method (Supplemental Table 2). Then, we performed genetic dissections using a composite interval-mapping method, resulting in 60 QTL regions (with LOD cut-off > 3; P < 0.05) for 43 ASVs mainly on chromosomes 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, and 11 (Fig. 2; Supplemental Table 3). The effect sizes of the 60 QTLs on ASV relative abundance ranged from 1.95% to 15.3%, with an average effect size of approximately 7.4% (Supplemental Table 3). The 43 ASVs represented 18.12% of the total ASVs and mainly belonged to Actinobacteria, AlphaProteobacteriota, Anaerolineae, Bacilli, Bacteroidia, and Coriobacteriia at the class level (Fig. 2; Supplemental Table 4). Interestingly, the intervals of a number of these QTLs overlapped, suggesting that these genomic regions are QTL hotspots linking with different microbial taxa (Fig. 2; Supplemental Table 5).

Host genetics and growth associate with microbiome assembly and structure

To further elucidate how host genetics affect the root microbiome of the F2 individuals, genes located within the ASV-associated QTL regions were annotated and enriched in rice biological pathways. Genes in each of the 37 ASV-associated QTL regions were found to be enriched in pathways by KEGG analysis (Fig. 3; Supplemental Table 6), which mainly included phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940), purine metabolism (ko00230), terpenoid backbone biosynthesis (ko00900), plant-pathogen interaction (ko04626), MAPK signaling pathway-plant (ko04016), pentose phosphate pathway (ko00030), fructose and mannose metabolism (ko00051), glycine, serine and threonine metabolism (ko00260), cyanoamino acid metabolism (ko00460), alpha-linolenic acid metabolism (ko00592), and brassinosteroid biosynthesis (ko00905) (Fig. 3; Supplemental Table 6). Interestingly, though the genes in each ASV-associated QTL region were enriched in specific pathways, some pathways recurred in the gene-enriched KEGG for a few ASV-associated QTL regions (Fig. 3). The pathways enriched in at least three ASV-associated QTL regions are shown in Supplemental Fig. 1. For example, the pentose phosphate pathway (ko00030) was significantly enriched in the 10 ASV-associated QTL regions (ASV6, ASV20, ASV35, ASV66, ASV102, ASV166, ASV194, ASV254, ASV323, and ASV532, which belong to the classes Bacilli, Anaerolineae, Bacteroidia, CPR2, Alphaproteobacteria, and Clostridia, Supplemental Table 4). The pathway of the brassinosteroid biosynthesis (ko00905) was significantly enriched in the 4 ASV-associated QTL regions (ASV6, ASV40, ASV223, and ASV254, which belong to the classes Bacilli, Anaerolineae, Actinobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria, Supplemental Table 4).

To further investigate the potential association between microbial communities and rice growth, data on rice growth traits, including the plant height, fresh weight, tiller number, leaf length, and leaf width, were collected from the aforementioned sequenced population samples (Fig. 1A). Then the PCoA values and the quantitative growth trait data were used to perform correlation analysis. Both db-RDA and linear regression analysis revealed a trend towards significant correlations between plant height, fresh weight, or leaf width, and microbiome. The strongest correlation was observed between leaf width and microbiome (R2 = 0.1342, P = 0.001 for db-RDA analysis; R2 = 0.028, P = 0.0002 for linear regression analysis, Supplemental Fig. 2). These results suggest that rice growth is closely related to microbiome structure. Further correlation analysis using growth traits and core microbial community (the 50 ASVs with the highest relative abundance in all samples; Supplemental Table 7) identified 46 ASVs that showed positive or negative correlations with at least one of the growth traits (P < 0.05; Spearman’s rank correlation test). Of these, 13 ASVs showed a strong positive correlation (P < 0.01) with all three traits: leaf width, fresh weight and plant height (Supplemental Fig. 3 and Supplemental Table 7). These ASVs are mainly distributed among the phyla Proteobacteria, Chloroflex, Bacteroidota and Actinobacteria (Supplemental Table 7). Furthermore, 21 of the 46 trait-associated ASVs were also included among the 43 QTL-associated ASVs (Supplemental Table 7). Together, these observations indicate that host genetics are associated with microbiome assembly and structure, which may correspondingly facilitate host growth.

BR biosynthesis-promoted rice plants have altered root microbiome

KEGG analysis showed that the genes enriched in the BR biosynthesis pathway in the 4 ASV-associated QTL regions (ASV6, ASV40, ASV223, and ASV254) encode 7 cytochrome P450 proteins (LOC_Os09g26950, LOC_Os09g26970, LOC_Os09g08990, LOC_Os09g08920, LOC_Os09g26980, LOC_Os09g26940, LOC_Os09g26960) (Supplemental Table 6). We noticed that the ASV6-, ASV40-, and ASV223-associated QTL regions overlap and represent a potential QTL hotspot related to the rice root microbiome assembly on chromosome 9 (Supplemental Table 5). The functions of these 7 genes are based on KEGG enrichment analysis, so further studies are needed to determine whether they are actually involved in the biosynthesis of BRs. Given the potentially redundant roles, knockout of one or more of the 7 genes may not affect BR biosynthesis. To investigate whether the alteration of BR biosynthesis really affects the assembly of the rice root microbiome, we took advantage of the OsmiR444-overexpressing (miR444-OE) rice plants, which accumulate higher BR metabolites [27]. Then, whether miR444-OE rice plants have a different root microbiome from WT was investigated. To do this, the dehulled and sterilized WT and miR444-OE rice seeds were germinated and grown on 1/2 MS agar medium for 14 days, then transferred to grow in a plastic box containing paddy soils (collected from Shunyi Farm, Beijing). Rice plants were watered once every 7 days with the Kimura B solution. Unplanted soils were also treated and used as the bulk soils. After two months, the well-grown rice root samples (10 WT; 7 miR444-OE) and 3 bulk soil samples were collected to analyze the root-associated bacterial microbiome by subjecting them to DNA amplicon sequencing of the bacterial 16S rDNA gene V5-V7 region. A total of 6,555 ASVs were obtained. Compared with WT roots, miR444-OE roots had increased relative levels of Proteobacteriota (68.54% vs. 57.2%) and had decreased relative levels of Bacteroidota (2.16% vs. 7.45%) and Desulfobacterota (0.26% vs. 2.95%) (Fig. 4A). In addition, although the microbiome diversity between miR444-OE and WT roots showed no obvious difference as revealed by alpha diversity analyses (within-sample diversity) (data not shown), unconstrained principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distance (beta diversity, between-sample diversity) revealed that the samples of miR444-OE and WT roots were clustered separately (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.225, P = 0.003) (Fig. 4B). In addition, 23 ASVs tended to have altered relative levels in miR444-OE compared to those in WT (DESeq2 analysis, false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P < 0.2, log2 fold change >1.5; Fig. 4C; Supplemental Table 8). Among them, 6 ASVs were up-regulated in miR444-OE mainly belonging to Proteobacteriota (including Alphaproteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria and Actinobacteria), and 17 ASVs were down-regulated in miR444-OE. The prediction of the bacterial function using the FAPROTAX database indicated that the ASVs altered by miR444-OE were related to the nitrogen (N) nutrient cycle, such as nitrate reduction, nitrate respiration, and nitrogen respiration (Fig. 4D). Taken together, the above observations indicate that miR444-OE, a BR biosynthesis-promoted rice material, has distinct root microbiome from WT. Furthermore, the ASVs altered by miR444-OE are involving in N nutrient cycles, consistent with that the promoted BR biosynthesis in miR444-OE alters the rice responsiveness to N-dependent root growth [27].

OsBRI1 is required for NH4+- and NO3−-dependent root microbiome assembly

We wondered whether BR signaling affects the assembly of the rice root microbiome. Since rice is an NH4+-preferring plant and BR signaling plays a key role in NH4+-triggered root growth and NH4+ accumulation in rice [27, 48, 49], we then tried to investigate whether BR signaling is required for NH4+-dependent root microbiome assembly using the BR receptor mutant osbri1. To do this, the dehulled and sterilized WT rice seeds (rice variety TC65) and osbri1 seeds were germinated and grown on 1/2 MS agar medium. Then, 14-day-old seedlings were transferred to a plastic box filled with paddy soil (collected from Shunyi Farm, Beijing) for continuous growth and watered once a week with Kimura B hydroponic nutrient solution containing 10 mM NH4+ as the sole N source. For comparison, plants were also watered with Kimura B hydroponic nutrient solution containing 10 mM NO3− as the sole N source. Unplanted soils were also treated with the hydroponic nutrient solutions as bulk soils. After two months of growth, root samples were taken from the well-grown rice plants, and bulk soil samples were collected. Finally, 10 NH4+-treated root samples (WT-A), 7 NO3−-treated root samples (WT-N), 10 NH4+-treated osbri1 root samples (osbri1-A), 9 NO3−-treated osbri1 root samples (osbri1-N), 5 NH4+-treated bulk soil samples (Soil-A), and 5 NO3−-treated bulk soil samples (Soil-N) were collected. These samples were then subjected to DNA amplicon sequencing of the bacterial 16S rDNA gene V3-V4 region to profile the root-associated microbiome. Totally, 5,510,031 high-quality bacterial sequences were obtained from the 46 samples. After filtering out sequences from chloroplasts and mitochondria, these sequences were collapsed into 17,577 ASVs.

We firstly analyzed whether the microbiome of NH4+- and NO3−-treated rice roots differed. Analysis of the relative microbial abundance at the phylum level showed little difference in bacterial communities between NH4+- and NO3−-treated soils or roots. However, in comparison to soils, NH4+- and NO3−-grown roots were mainly enriched in members of Proteobacteriota and Bacteroidota and depleted in members of Acidobacteria, Chloroflexi, and Actinobacteria (Fig. 5A). Alpha diversity analyses (within-sample diversity) revealed that the bacterial diversity did not show the obvious differences between NH4+- and NO3−-treated soils but consistently showed higher diversity than the NH4+- and NO3−-grown root samples based on different metrics (Shannon index and Chao index) (Fig. 5B, C). In addition, a significant difference was observed between NH4+- and NO3−-grown roots, in which NH4+-grown roots had higher bacterial diversity than NO3−-grown roots (Fig. 5B, C) (Kruskal-Wallis H test, P < 0.001), indicating that NH4+-grown rice roots recruited more bacterial species than NO3−-grown rice roots. Unconstrained principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) of the Bray-Curtis distance (beta diversity analysis, between-sample diversity) showed that the bacterial communities of the bulk soil samples were grouped together independently of NH4+ and NO3− and formed distinct clusters from the root samples, as shown by separation along the first coordinate axis (Fig. 5D). In contrast, the bacterial communities of the NH4+- and NO3−-grown roots formed distinct clusters and separated along the second coordination axis (Fig. 5D), indicating that the bacterial community composition differs between NH4+- and NO3−-grown rice roots.

Then, we analyzed whether OsBRI1 affects the NH4+- and NO3−-dependent composition of the rice root microbiome. Analysis of the relative microbial abundance at the phylum level revealed minor differences for bacterial communities between NH4+-grown WT and osbri1 roots and between NO3−-grown WT and osbri1 roots (Fig. 5E). Alpha diversity indices (Shannon index and Chao index) indicated a decrease in bacterial diversity in the NH4+-grown osbri1 roots (osbri1-A) compared with the NH4+-grown WT roots (WT-A) (Kruskal-Wallis H test, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5F, G). Beta diversity analysis revealed that the bacterial communities were distinct between NH4+-grown osbri1 and WT roots, which were separated along the first coordination axis (Fig. 5H). Similarly, the microbiome of NO3−-grown WT (WT-N) and osbri1 roots (osbri1-N) also showed significant differences in alpha diversity (Shannon index) and beta diversity (Fig. 5E-H; Kruskal-Wallis H test, P < 0.001). Consistent with the differences in the bacterial community composition between NH4+- and NO3−-grown rice roots (Fig. 5A-D) and between WT and osbri1 rice roots (Fig. 5E-H), PERMANOVA analysis showed that both the plant genotype and nutrition type had significant effects on root bacterial communities with a more prominent role of plant genotype (PERMANOVA analysis R2 = 0.093, P = 0.001 for plant genotype; R2 = 0.065, P = 0.004 for nutrient) (Supplemental Table 9). Together, these observations indicate that osbri1 and WT roots have distinct bacterial microbiomes under the growth conditions using NH4+ or NO3− as the sole N source, suggesting that BR signaling is required for NH4+- and NO3−-dependent root microbiome assembly.

OsBRI1 contributes to the microbiome differences between NH4+- and NO3−-grown rice roots

We noticed that the bacterial communities of WT-N and osbri1-A roots were clustered together in the PCoA analysis (Fig. 5H). In addition, the clustering heat map of microbial community samples based on Bray-Curtis distance analysis also showed that compared with WT-A, osbri1-A was closer to WT-N in microbial community structure (Fig. 6A), further indicating that osbri1 roots grown with NH4+ recruit a similar bacterial community to WT roots grown with NO3−. These observations also suggested that OsBRI1 is involved in the differences in the root microbiome between NH4+- and NO3−-grown rice roots. To better understand the role of OsBRI1 in the root microbiome differences between NH4+- and NO3−-grown rice, we screened for ASVs with potential alteration in relative abundance between WT-A and WT-N, and between WT-A and osbri1-A (Wilcoxon rank sum test, FDR-adjusted P < 0.2). Among these, ASVs with suggestive differences in relative abundance between Soil-A and Soil-N were excluded. Finally, 913 ASVs specifically regulated by NH4+- or NO3− in WT or osbri1 roots were obtained (Supplemental Table 10) and were used to calculate the community distance. The resulting microbial clustering heatmap clearly showed that osbri1-A had a similar microbial community structure to WT-N, but not to WT-A (Fig. 6B), suggesting that OsBRI1 mediates the discrepancy between NH4+- and NO3−-shaped root microbiome in rice. We also attempted to find OsBRI1-dependent NH4+-responsive ASVs. Compared to that in WT-N, 50 ASVs exhibited increased or decreased trends in relative abundance in WT-A (DESeq2 analysis, FDR-adjusted P < 0.2, log2 fold change > 1.5; Fig. 6C; Supplemental Table 11). Compared to that in osbri1-A, 45 ASVs showed increased or decreased trends in relative abundance in WT-A (DESeq2 analysis, FDR-adjusted P < 0.2, log2 fold change > 1.5; Fig. 6C; Supplemental Table 11). Among the 50 NH4+-responsive ASVs and the 45 OsBRI1-dependent ASVs, 14 ASVs were decreased and 1 ASV was increased in both WT-N and osbri1-A compared to WT-A (DESeq2 analysis, FDR-adjusted P < 0.2, fold change > 1.5; Fig. 6C, D; Supplemental Table 11), suggesting that NH4+ enriched the 14 OsBRI1-dependent ASVs. Among the 45 OsBRI1-dependent ASVs, the relative abundance of 30 ASVs (Fig. 6C; Supplemental Table 11) tended to be altered in osbri1-A, but was not changed in WT-N, compared to that in WT-A, suggesting that the OsBRI1-dependent regulation of the 30 ASVs was independent of NH4+ or NO3−. The 15 OsBRI1-dependent NH4+-responsive ASVs (14 enriched ASVs and 1 repressed ASV) belonged to the classes Actinobacteria (1 ASV), Bacteroidia (5 ASVs), Clostridia (2 ASVs), Desulfovibrionia (1 ASV), Desulfuromonadia (3 ASVs), Gammaproteobacteria (2 ASVs), Negativicutes (1 ASV) (Fig. 6D, Supplemental Table 11). Bacterial function prediction using the FAPROTAX database indicated that the 15 ASVs were involved in nutrient cycles such as fermentation, nitrate reduction, sulfate respiration, and respiration of sulfur compounds (Supplemental Fig. 4), suggesting that the 15 OsBRI1-dependent NH4+-responsive ASVs play a role in plant nutrient acquisition processes.

Discussion

Agricultural production must increase to feed a growing human population and ensure food security. Conventional agriculture faces serious challenges in maintaining yields, such as drought, high temperatures, nutrient stress, and pathogen and pest threats. Agricultural microbiome engineering has received increasing attention due to the multiple benefits of microbiomes in plant growth and adaptation to biotic and abiotic stresses. In addition to the construction of synthetic communities, genetic manipulation of the host plant is considered as a promising way to cultivate microbiome-engineered crops [50–54]. GWAS and QTL studies, which exploit natural variation within plant populations, have been used to identify microbiome-associated genetic loci as a prerequisite for harnessing soil microbes for sustainable crop production. An increasing number of candidate genetic loci controlling microbial selection of the leaf, rhizoplane, and rhizosphere have been identified in model Arabidopsis and several crops. However, direct evidence linking most of these identified genetic loci to plant microbiome assembly remains to be established [14–20]. The genes within these genetic loci are significantly enriched in specific metabolic pathways, immune pathways, and nutrient-related pathways [17, 19, 20], consistent with the fact that these pathway-related genes have been shown to influence plant microbiome assembly and function [55–57]. In the present study, we performed QTL analysis of a rice segregating F2 population using the ASV abundance as a quantitative trait, which allows the detection of QTLs for rare microbial taxa [17]. We identified 60 QTLs that correlate with the abundance of 43 ASVs in the root microbiome of the super hybrid rice LYP9 (Fig. 2). We also found 46 ASVs that show significant correlations rice growth traits, such as leaf length and widen, plant fresh weight and tiller number. Interestingly, approximately 49% of these QTL-associated ASVs overlapped with these trait-associated ASVs (Supplement Fig. 3 and Supplemental Table 7). Further investigation is needed to establish whether these QTLs influence growth traits by shaping the assembly and structure of the root microbiome, or whether they cause differences in the assembly and structure of the root microbiome and whether these trait-associated microbes affect rice growth.

These QTL genes are enriched in different biological pathways, such as the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway (ko00940), biosynthesis of secondary metabolites-unclassified (ko00999), terpenoid backbone biosynthesis (ko00900), plant-pathogen interaction (ko04626), MAPK signaling pathway-plant (ko04016), and sulfur metabolism (ko00920), that are primarily related to plant metabolism, immunity, and nutrient (Fig. 3). Some enriched pathways have been reported to be involved in the homeostasis and function of the plant microbiome. For example, it has been shown that the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway (ko00940) synthesizes 4-hydroxycinnamic acid to enrich Pseudomonadales bacteria and to play an important role in rice health [20]. Purines (related to ko00230) enrich root-associated Pseudomonas to improve wild soybean growth under salt stress [58]. The triterpene biosynthesis (related to ko00900) shapes specific root microbial community in Arabidopsis [59]. In addition, the plant-pathogen interaction (ko04626) and MAPK signaling pathway-plant (ko04016) are also the enriched pathways of the rice genes in the phyllosphere microbiome-associated loci identified by GWAS analysis [20], suggesting that the two plant immunity-related pathways may shape the rice microbiome [55, 60]. Further, the pentose phosphate pathway (ko00030), fructose and mannose metabolism (ko00051), glycine, serine and threonine metabolism (ko00260), cyanoamino acid metabolism (ko00460), and alpha-linolenic acid metabolism (ko00592), as energy and amino acid metabolic pathways, could shape the rice root microbiome because fatty acids, sugars and amino acids have been shown to alter key phenotypes related to plant root growth, microbial colonization, and symbiotic interactions [61–64]. Interestingly, some QTL-enriched pathways, such as the pentose phosphate pathway (ko00030) and fructose and mannose metabolism (ko00051), which recur with up to ten QTLs (Supplemental Fig. 1). It will be interesting to further investigate whether and how these QTLs influence the composition of the rice root microbiome. One such pathway, the BR biosynthesis pathway (ko00905), is enriched in 4 ASV-associated QTLs containing a hotspot genomic region. Further study indicated that BR biosynthesis and signaling are involved in the N-dependent assembly of the rice root microbiome (Figs. 4, 5 and 6).

N is one of the essential macronutrients for plant growth and development. N concentrations such as N starvation or high N concentration alter the composition of the root microbiome [65–68]. NH4+ and NO3− are the two main forms of inorganic N taken up directly by roots. The microbiome of roots grown with NH4+ and NO3− remains to be investigated. Rice is an NH4+-preferring plant, although high NH4+ concentrations have some negative effects on rice growth, and also uses NO3− as a N source. We found that rice roots develop a microbiome that is different in composition and structure when plants are grown with NO3− or NH4+ as the sole N source. In particular, in contrast to NO3−, NH4+ increases the bacterial diversity and the abundance of Bacteroidota and Firmicutes in the rice root microbiome (Fig. 5), which may play a role in alleviating NH4+ stress on plants [2, 69]. We showed that the BR receptor BRI1 is required for NO3−- and NH4+-dependent assembly of the root microbiome, in which the NH4+- and NO3−-dependent compositions and structures of the rice root microbiome are altered in osbri1 with decreased bacterial diversity and altered community structure (Fig. 5). We also showed that OsBRI1 contributes to the differences between the NO3−- and NH4+-dependent root microbiome with the observation that osbri1-A and WT-N roots have a similar microbial community structure (Fig. 6). It should be kept in mind that this conclusion was drawn based on an analysis of differentially enriched ASVs, which were obtained according to the following criteria: P value less than 0.05, FDR-adjusted P value below 0.2, and log2 fold change greater than 1.5. Further confirmation of this conclusion requires more biological samples and stricter statistical criteria. Even so, it is reasonable that the underlying mechanisms of OsBRI1-mediated NO3−- and NH4+-dependent root microbiome assembly and differences may be involved in BR-mediated root growth regulation, as we have previously shown that BR biosynthesis and signaling contribute to the root growth differences between NO3−- and NH4+-triggered rice plants [27]. Since the use of NO3−- or NH4+ fertilizer affects the outcome of plant-pathogen interactions and BRs regulate plant immunity [70, 71], whether BRs confer the differential effects of NO3−- and NH4+-mediated defense responses leading to the formation of the NO3−- and NH4+-specific microbiome in rice roots needs further investigation. In addition, it is well known that the environmental pH significantly affects the composition and function of the root microbiome [72, 73]. NH4+ and NO3− usually lead to acidification and alkalinization of the extracellular environment, respectively [21, 22]. It is interesting to know whether BRs regulate the acid-base balance of the root environments, thereby influencing the N-dependent root microbiome in rice.

Conclusions

The plant microbiome plays a vital role in plant growth and nutrient acquisition, and host genetics is an important factor in determining the composition of the plant microbiome. In this study, we identify a set of genetic loci (QTLs) associated with the root bacterial community composition in the super hybrid rice LYP9. These QTLs are primarily enriched in pathways associated with plant metabolism, immunity and nutrient. The bacterial communities associated with these QTLs predominantly belong to Actinobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria, Anaerolineae, Bacilli, Bacteroidia and Coriobacteria. Some QTL-linked bacterial communities exhibit a strong positive correlation with rice growth traits. Notably, one of the QTLs harbors genes that are potentially involved in BR biosynthesis. Increasing BR biosynthesis by overexpressing miR444 alters the components of the rice root microbiome. In addition, the BR receptor OsBRI1 confers the NO3−- and NH4+-dependent rice root microbiome assembly and differences. Our study provides valuable insights into the genetic regulation of root microbiome assembly in super hybrid rice and reveals novel connections between BRs and N-dependent microbiome structure and function.
