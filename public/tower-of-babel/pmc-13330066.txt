Challenging the more-is-better dogma: A precision-optimized AAV gene therapy for SMA
Authors: Chunjuan Song, Jingbo Liu, Qingzeng Wang, Peixin Zhu, Jiayi Xu, Ying Zhou, Zhucheng Lin, Xin Xin, Wei Hu, Zhongwan Li, Shuyuan Chen, Ye Zhang, Shaoyong Li, Junhui Li, Yigang Wang, Jingjing Luo, Xiaoyuan Jia, Ni Yuan, Lijun Wang, Guo-jie Ye, Yi Wang, Zhenhua Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is a severe neurogenetic disorder and the leading inherited cause of infant mortality. Although the available gene therapy has shown substantial efficacy in treating SMA, safety concerns, including hepatotoxicity, underscore the need for optimization. In this study, our findings challenge the prevailing “more-is-better” paradigm, demonstrating that both insufficient and excessive transgene expression are suboptimal. To maximize therapeutic benefit while minimizing risk, we extensively optimized the expression cassette in adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based constructs and selected EXG001-307, an intra-cerebrospinal fluid (intra-CSF)-delivered AAV9 gene therapy engineered for tissue-selective and quantitatively controlled SMN expression. Comparative studies demonstrated that intra-CSF-administered EXG001-307 may offer improved efficacy and safety relative to the other candidates. In subsequent investigational new drug-enabling studies, EXG001-307 demonstrated dose-dependent improvements in survival, body weight gain, and motor function in SMA model mice. Safety evaluations in rats and juvenile nonhuman primates further confirmed a favorable safety profile, with no evidence of systemic toxicity or sustained dorsal root ganglion pathology. Analyses in vector genome biodistribution and transgene expression revealed robust central nervous system transduction consistent with therapeutic benefit. These findings support clinical translation of EXG001-307 and highlight the importance of vector genome engineering and targeted delivery for safe and effective gene therapies.

Introduction

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors have become the foundation of many clinical gene therapy programs including spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), owing to their favorable safety profile, stable transduction, and long-term expression. SMA is a rare genetic disorder and the leading inherited cause of mortality in children under 2 years of age. Although the currently approved gene therapy onasemnogene abeparvovec (Zolgensma) has demonstrated substantial clinical benefits, safety concerns, including hepatotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, and neurotoxicity, have been reported in both clinical and preclinical studies. Previous studies have demonstrated that certain toxicities, particularly acute liver injury and sinusoidal endothelial cell damage, are capsid mediated and associated with high-dose AAV vector administration.1,2 In addition, emerging evidence suggests that some adverse effects may also result from uncontrolled SMN protein overexpression.3,4,5 Therefore, it underscores the need for next-generation approaches that enable precise regulation of the transgene expression.

Innovation in the AAV-based gene therapy field has been driven primarily by capsid engineering to enhance tissue tropism. However, it is now increasingly recognized that vector genome design, particularly the regulatory architecture controlling transgene expression, is also critical for therapeutic success. Early-generation AAV vectors often employed strong and ubiquitous promoters under the assumption that “more is better,” whereas this paradigm has since been challenged by accumulating evidence that supraphysiological expression of certain transgenes can be deleterious. For example, Rett syndrome results from loss of MECP2, whereas genomic duplication leading to MECP2 overexpression causes MECP2 duplication syndrome, another severe neurodevelopmental disorder.6,7 These contrasting conditions highlight the critical need for tightly regulated MeCP2 expression to ensure proper neural development and function. Preclinical studies also echo these findings, showing that overexpression of MeCP2 induces neurotoxicity, manifested by hindlimb abnormalities, motor dysfunction, and premature death.7,8,9 Like MeCP2 overexpression, excessive frataxin is also deleterious in preclinical models, resulting in cardiac and hepatic toxicity.10,11 Overexpression of exogenous proteins has also been implicated in dorsal root ganglion (DRG) degeneration.12,13 In the context of SMA, clinical dose of onasemnogene abeparvovec is 1.1 × 1014 vg/kg; however as reported, modestly higher dose levels in the preclinical evaluations were associated with dose-dependent toxicities: in mice, administration at doses equal to or higher than 2 × 1014 vg/kg induced severe toxicity and premature mortality,3,5,14 while in nonhuman primates (NHPs) and piglets, profound liver failure and DRG degeneration necessitated early euthanasia.15

The observed toxicity in SMA studies likely stems from either an excessive viral particle load, supraphysiological SMN overexpression, or a combination of both. While it is challenging to delineate the primary contributor, SMN overexpression is clearly a critical driver of the adverse effects. Emerging evidence indicates that SMN at supraphysiological levels can induce hepatotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, and neurotoxicity through gain-of-toxic-function mechanisms, leading to organ dysfunction and impaired motor performance3,4,5,16; however, using a milder promoter to drive less SMN expression at the same AAV vector dose markedly attenuates these adverse effects.3,5 Consistently, administration of scAAV9-CB-SMN in NHPs induced hepatotoxicity, characterized by transaminase elevations and histopathological changes, whereas no liver injury occurred with empty capsids or a promoterless vector at the same dose, indicating that hepatic toxicity results from viral transduction and transgene expression rather than vector load alone.17 These findings suggest that it is crucial to maintain transgene expression within a disease-specific safety window. For SMA, this therapeutic window is possibly narrow, underscoring the need for precise transgene regulation and vector design to balance efficacy and safety.

In addition to precise control of overall expression levels, we propose that a critical determinant of therapeutic efficacy in AAV-based gene therapies is the capacity to differentially regulate transgene expression across different organs according to therapeutic need. To achieve this, several strategies can be employed, including the use of tissue-specific promoters, incorporation of cis-regulatory elements, and insertion of miRNA target sites to suppress expression in non-target tissues or cell types.

Tissue-specific promoters provide the first layer of spatial control, directing expression to therapeutically relevant cell types. However, promoter specificity alone often proves insufficient to fully eliminate ectopic expression, particularly in organs susceptible to AAV transduction such as the liver and DRGs. This limitation has spurred the development of other regulatory tools, including miRNA binding sites that actively suppress off-target expression through post-transcriptional silencing. For instance, incorporation of target sequences for liver-specific miR-122, hematopoietic miR-142, or DRG-enriched miR-183 has been shown in preclinical models to effectively suppress transgene expression in non-target tissues,13,18,19 thereby mitigating undesirable transduction in specific cell types and reducing associated toxicities. Collectively, these multilayered strategies highlight the importance of combining transcriptional and post-transcriptional control mechanisms to achieve precise and safe transgene expression in AAV-based gene therapies. Such comprehensive construct optimization enables what may be termed the “Goldilocks principle” of gene therapy, achieving expression levels that are sufficient to confer therapeutic benefit but not so high as to induce toxicity. Nowhere is this balance more crucial than in neurological disorders, where the therapeutic window between efficacy and toxicity can be exceptionally narrow.4,8,10,15

Beyond construct optimization, the route of administration (ROA) is also a critical determinant of both efficacy and safety. Systemic intravenous (i.v.) administration of AAV9, leveraging its natural capacity to cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB), has been clinically validated and enables broad transduction of both central and peripheral tissues. However, following systemic delivery, vectors must navigate multiple anatomical hurdles: they circulate through peripheral vasculature, cross the endothelial barrier of the central nervous system (CNS), and then diffuse within brain parenchyma to reach neurons. As a result, extremely high vector doses are required to achieve therapeutic CNS expression, which is associated with dose-limiting toxicities such as hepatotoxicity and thrombotic microangiopathy. In contrast, intra-cerebrospinal fluid (intra-CSF) administration, including routes such as intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.), intrathecal/lumbar (IT/lumbar) or intracisternal MagNA injection, delivers the vector directly into the CSF, thereby bypassing the BBB.20,21,22,23 This approach enables more efficient engagement of perivascular and parenchymal pathways, facilitating transduction of spinal cord and brainstem motor neurons at substantially lower doses. Together, it highlights how ROA profoundly shapes vector biodistribution, efficacy, and safety, underscoring the importance of optimizing delivery strategies for CNS-directed AAV gene therapies.

Given all the considerations mentioned above, to develop an optimal gene therapy product for SMA, we applied a systematic optimization platform incorporating (i) promoter strength and specificity, (ii) transgene sequence optimization through codon optimization using multiple algorithms, combined with cleavage-resistant design to modify protein stability, (iii) modular regulatory elements for tunable control, and (iv) tissue-selective miRNA regulation to minimize undesirable expression. Subsequently, we compared i.v. and IT delivery routes to determine the ROA that maximizes CNS biodistribution and efficacy while reducing peripheral exposure and immune activation. These efforts culminated in the development of EXG001-307, an optimized AAV9-based candidate administered intrathecally, which demonstrated controlled SMN expression, efficient CNS targeting, robust efficacy, and a favorable safety profile in preclinical models.

EXG001-307 was previously reported as a preclinical candidate through i.v. administration, demonstrating efficacy and preliminary safety in animal models.3 In the present study, we describe the process of selecting EXG001-307 as the lead candidate and evaluating its performance across different ROAs. Comparative assessment identified IT delivery via lumbar puncture as the optimal ROA. Subsequent dose range-finding study in SMNΔ7 mice confirmed robust therapeutic activity of EXG001-307 with a clear dose-response relationship. Furthermore, non-GLP safety studies in rats and GLP-compliant toxicology studies in juvenile NHPs established a favorable safety profile, supporting its advancement toward clinical development.

In summary, the findings of this study affirm the “Goldilocks principle” for gene therapy, demonstrating that a careful design to achieve “just-right” magnitude of transgene expression is a critical determinant for both safety and efficacy. EXG001-307 via IT/lumbar administration is now under an investigator-initiated trial in SMA patients (NCT06888661). In parallel, an investigational new drug (IND) application has been initiated with the China NMPA and is in preparation for submission to the US FDA.

Results

Comprehensive optimization and comparative analysis of AAV constructs identified lead candidates

The FVB mouse strain with homozygous targeted mutations in the SMN locus, commonly known as the SMNΔ7 mouse model (genotype: Smn−/−; SMN2+/+; SMNΔ7+/+; Jackson Laboratory stock #005025) serves as the standard model for SMA preclinical testing.24,25 The SMNΔ7 mice exhibit hallmark disease characteristics, including impaired mobility shortly after birth, retarded growth, reduced body weight, and significantly shortened lifespans when compared with wild-type (WT) controls. In this study (study design summarized in Table 1, Study #1), we used this model to evaluate novel designs and compare engineered constructs incorporating diverse components administering the vectors to animals on postnatal day (PND) 1 (24–48 h after birth). To address the safety concerns associated with peripheral organs (liver and heart), we used a highly neuron-specific promoter, the human synapsin 1 promoter (hSyn1).26,27 As shown in Figure 1A and Table S1, the hSyn1 promoter alone (EXG001-301) achieved better survival at 4 × 1014 vg/kg (median survival 33 days) compared with scAAV9-CB-hSMN (Zolgensma design), which used the strong ubiquitous CMV enhancer/CBA promoter (800-bp version) and yielded a median survival of 19.5 days. Recognizing that SMN protein is required not only in motor neurons but also in peripheral tissues, including skeletal muscle, heart, liver, and components of the neuromuscular junction, exclusive neuronal expression of SMN may limit therapeutic efficacy.28,29,30 Moreover, insufficient transgene expression may also be suboptimal. We then incorporated additional enhancers or regulatory elements in constructs EXG001-304, EXG001-305, EXG001-306, EXG001-307, EXG001-324, and EXG001-325 (see Table S1 for details) to achieve high SMN expression in the CNS while reducing peripheral expression. This strategy contrasts with the ubiquitous CBA promoter used in Zolgensma, which leads to indiscriminate expression across various organs, resulting in supraphysiological levels of SMN in both the heart and liver. As shown in Figures 1A, S1A, and S1B and Table S1, coupled with other promoters or regulatory elements, such as ProC3 enhancer in EXG001-304, ProB15 enhancer in EXG001-305, CMV enhancer in EXG001-307, SV40 promoter in EXG001-324, and human CYP26A1 gene promoter in EXG001-325, all showed better survival than EXG001-301 with the hSyn1 neuron-specific promoter alone. Especially, EXG001-307, hSyn1 promoter combined with the CMV enhancer, demonstrated the best performance in enhancing the survival of the SMNΔ7 model mice at both 2 × 1014 vg/kg (median survival 35 days) and 4 × 1014 vg/kg (median survival 191 days). This optimized expression profile achieved the critical balance between CNS efficacy and peripheral safety.

Beyond evaluating promoters and enhancers, we examined other cis-regulatory elements in the AAV genome to modulate hSMN transgene expression. These elements include post-transcriptional regulatory sequences (WPRE, miniWPRE) and the neuron-restrictive silencer element (NRSE), which suppresses gene expression in non-neuronal tissues via binding to RE1-silencing transcription factor (REST) (Table S1, rows with “Other cis-regulatory elements”). As shown in Figure S1C, incorporation of WPRE (EXG001-335) or miniWPRE (EXG001-339), intended to enhance transgene stability, modestly improved therapeutic efficacy from median survival 35 days to 41 or 42 days in SMNΔ7 mice at 2 × 1014 vg/kg compared with the parental control EXG001-307, which lacks these elements. However, as shown in Figure S1C, adding miniWPRE in EXG001-341 (median survival 119 days; solid green line) did not enhance efficacy compared with the parental construct EXG001-340 (median survival 205 days; dashed green line), which had already achieved increased expression through mRNA stabilization following codon optimization (Figures 1B and 1C; discussed below). We next tested whether the NRSE element, which is supposed to restrict hSMN expression to neuronal tissues, will be beneficial. However, neither its insertion within (EXG001-336) nor upstream (EXG001-337) of the extended CBA promoter (890 bp) improved efficacy compared with the parental control EXG001-002 (Figure S1D). These results were unexpected. Owing to the early mortality of these animals, no tissues were collected to evaluate the impact of the NRSE element on SMN expression across different tissues. This limitation warrants further investigation to better characterize the tissue-specific regulatory effects of the NRSE element.

Concurrently, we evaluated a series of codon-optimized transgene variants to determine whether enhanced expression achieved through optimized codon usage could further improve therapeutic efficacy when combined with neuron-preferential promoters and regulatory elements. The goal was to maximize expression in neuronal tissues while minimizing expression in non-neuronal tissues, thereby reducing potential toxicity. As shown in Figure 1B and Table S1, EXG001-340, a codon-optimized derivative of EXG001-307, markedly extended median survival to 205 days at 2 × 1014 vg/kg (black solid line), compared with 35 days for EXG001-307 (blue dashed line). As mentioned above, EXG001-340 indeed markedly increased hSMN transgene expression across all examined tissues (Figure 1C). Other codon-optimized variants, including EXG001-401 and EXG001-403, also improved survival (37 and 55 days, respectively) at the dose of 2 × 1014 vg/kg, relative to EXG001-307, further supporting the benefit of codon optimization. However, at the higher dose of 4 × 1014 vg/kg, EXG001-340 unexpectedly reduced median survival to 29.5 days (green solid line in Figure 1B), in contrast to its parental construct EXG001-307, which exhibited a dose-response relationship with survival increasing from 35 days at 2 × 1014 vg/kg to 191 days at 4 × 1014 vg/kg (Figures 1A and 1B; Table S1). Animals that died early following administration of EXG001-340 at 4 × 1014 vg/kg did not undergo comprehensive necropsy or histopathological evaluation, as performed in our previous study.3 However, gross examination was conducted and revealed enlarged heart and systemic swelling (Figure S1I). These divergent dose responses indicate a threshold effect, whereby increased SMN expression is beneficial only up to a certain level, beyond which excessive expression becomes detrimental. Further evaluation of SMN protein expression in major organs and tissues following administration of EXG001-340 at 4 × 1014 vg/kg would be valuable to define this therapeutic window and substantiate this conclusion.

To further examine the role of post-translational stability, we engineered two constructs derived from EXG001-340 that disrupted the calpain cleavage site within SMN, mapped to residues 192–194.31,32 Specifically, EXG001-425 deletes residues 192–193, and EXG001-426 deletes residues 193–194. These variants were designed to prevent calpain-mediated cleavage and test whether increased SMN stabilization could further enhance the therapeutic efficacy of EXG001-340. In plasmid-transfected HEK293 cells, transfection with EXG001-425 or EXG001-426 markedly reduced cleaved SMN bands in cell pellets, supernatants, and exosomes, while correspondingly increasing full-length SMN compared with the parental EXG001-340 (Figure 1D). These findings indicate that deletion of amino acid residues 192/193 or 193/194 confers resistance to calpain-mediated cleavage. However, this increased resistance did not translate into enhanced therapeutic benefit in vivo (Figure 1E). As shown in Figure 1E and Table S1, EXG001-425-treated animals had a shorter median survival (107 days) compared with EXG001-340-treated animals (205 days). These results suggest that eliminating the calpain cleavage site may impair SMN protein function, or, alternatively, that increased protein stabilization leads to supraphysiological SMN accumulation, which may surpass a tolerable threshold and exert toxic effects. To distinguish between these possibilities, comparative evaluation of EXG001-425 or EXG001-426 with EXG001-340 at lower dose levels would be informative and warrants further investigation in future studies.

As noted above, SMN protein is also required in peripheral tissues. However, to mitigate cardiac and hepatic toxicity arising from supraphysiological SMN expression, we engineered AAV constructs incorporating tandem arrays of four target sites for liver and heart-enriched microRNAs. Specifically, target sites for miR-122, miR-133, and miR-30 were introduced.19,33,34 This design enables selective suppression of transgene expression in hepatocytes and cardiomyocytes while preserving therapeutic expression in motor neurons. For comparison, we included three reference vectors: EXG001-002, driven by the extended ubiquitous CBA promoter (890 bp); EXG001-301, containing the neuron-specific hSyn1 promoter; and EXG001-307, which combines the hSyn1 promoter with a CMV enhancer to achieve high neuronal expression while minimizing peripheral activity.

As expected, incorporation of miRNA target sites, either individually or in combination, into EXG001-002 (resulting in EXG001-202, EXG001-204, and EXG001-206) markedly extended the survival of SMNΔ7 mice compared with the parental construct lacking these elements (Figure 1F; Table S1). By contrast, introduction of the same miRNA-targeting strategies into EXG001-301 or EXG001-307 did not yield additional survival benefit, indicating that their promoter-driven expression profiles may already minimize peripheral toxicity. While these results imply a relationship between specific miRNA incorporation and expression reduction in targeted organs or tissues, confirmatory experiments were not performed in order to prioritize program progression and accelerate development. Further mechanistic studies will be needed to fully elucidate the impact of these miRNA-targeting elements.

Through systematic design and experimental evaluation, we identified EXG001-307 and its codon-optimized derivative EXG001-340 as the lead candidates for further development, as both demonstrated the strongest efficacy at specific dose levels.

To investigate the mechanistic basis of these differences, we compared tissue-specific SMN expression following i.c.v. and i.v. administration. As outlined in Table 1 (Study #3), SMNΔ7 mice were treated with EXG001-307 on PND 1 via i.c.v. at three doses (2 × 1011vg/mouse [1.3 × 1014 vg/kg]; 6 × 1010 vg/mouse [4 × 1013 vg/kg]; and 3 × 1010 vg/mouse [2 × 1013 vg/kg]) or via i.v. at 2 × 1014 vg/kg (∼3 × 1011vg/mouse). As comparators, an additional group received scAAV9-CB-hSMN on PND 1 via i.c.v. at 2 × 1011 vg/mouse (1.3 × 1014 vg/kg), and a group of untreated WT mice served as controls. 2 weeks post dose, SMN protein levels were analyzed in key tissues, including spinal cord, brain, heart, and liver (Figure 2E). Due to the technical challenges of collecting DRGs from mice at this age, DRGs were not harvested or analyzed. EXG001-307 via i.c.v. administration consistently yielded markedly higher expression in CNS tissues, even at the lowest i.c.v. dose (3 × 1010 vg/mouse [2 × 1013 vg/kg]), compared with i.v. delivery at a 10-fold higher dose (2 × 1014 vg/kg; ∼3 × 1011 vg/mouse). In contrast, scAAV9-CB-hSMN, driven by a ubiquitous promoter, induced substantially higher peripheral expression than EXG001-307, particularly in the heart, even after i.c.v. administration, consistent with previous i.v. findings.3 WT animals were used as a reference for endogenous SMN protein levels in this study. However, heterozygous carrier animals may represent another physiologically relevant benchmark for the minimal SMN level required for normal function. As shown in Figure S2J, SMN protein levels in heterozygous carriers were approximately 50% of those observed in WT animals across the major tissues examined. To exclude the possibility that these differences were attributable to vector titer variability, the same lots of EXG001-307 and the benchmark scAAV9-CB-hSMN demonstrated comparable vector biodistribution in different tissues, as reported in our previous publication.3 To further confirm route-dependent differences, vector genome biodistribution was assessed at the DNA level (Figure 2F). The results mirrored protein expression, with i.v. administration yielding higher vector copies in peripheral organs such as the liver and heart, but lower levels in CNS tissues, compared with i.c.v. delivery.

Taken together, these results (Study #2 and 3), in conjunction with findings from Study #1 (Figures 1 and S1), establish EXG001-307 as the promising candidate, demonstrating optimal CNS expression, balanced peripheral distribution, and robust therapeutic benefit following construct design and intra-CSF administration. Based on these data, EXG001-307 was selected as the preclinical candidate for advancement into the following IND-enabling studies, including dose range finding (Study #4) and toxicology assessments (Study #5 and 6).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that i.c.v. delivery of EXG001-307 confers potent and durable therapeutic benefit in SMA mice, thereby supporting its advancement into further nonclinical development.

Eight homozygous SMNΔ7 females treated with EXG001-307 via i.c.v. were paired with heterozygous males, of which five achieved pregnancies and produced litters (Figure 3F; Table S3; Videos S1 and S2). Genotyping of the offspring confirmed Mendelian segregation of alleles, and heterozygous progeny were phenotypically normal. These heterozygous offspring were further bred to generate second- and third-generation litters, demonstrating preserved fertility and transmission across generations (Table S3).

Intrathecal/lumbar-administered EXG001-307 was well tolerated and achieved dose-dependent CNS transduction in rats

Nonclinical studies of AAV vectors delivered systemically (i.v.) or intrathecally (IT) have consistently shown efficient transduction of DRG sensory neurons, often accompanied by toxicity across species including NHPs.12,13,15,35 Similar effects have been observed in rats, where AAV9 administration caused nerve fiber and neuronal degeneration in DRGs, peripheral nerves, and spinal cord. In Wistar Han rats, minimal to mild DRG degeneration or necrosis was reported at 5 × 1013 vg/kg without clinical signs.36 Other studies showed progression from minimal-to-moderate DRG degeneration at 2–4 weeks to widespread DRG and sciatic nerve fiber loss by week 8, accompanied by elevated serum neurofilament light chain (NfL), a biomarker of neuronal damage.37

Given these prior observations, we conducted a pilot safety study in Wistar Han rats to evaluate the preliminary toxicity of EXG001-307, with particular focus on potential DRG and spinal cord findings, before proceeding to pivotal GLP toxicology studies in NHPs. In this 16-week study (outlined in Table 1, Study #5), male WT Wistar Han rats received a single IT/lumbar injection of EXG001-307 at either 8.5 × 1011 or 1.7 × 1012 vg/animal (equivalent to 4.3×1012 and 8.5×1013 vg/kg respectively). Treatment was well tolerated, with no mortality, clinical signs, or behavioral/neurological deficits compared with saline controls. Safety and biodistribution of EXG001-307 following IT/lumbar administration in juvenile cynomolgus macaques

General tolerability and clinical observations

A pivotal GLP-compliant toxicology study was performed to assess the safety of EXG001-307 following a single IT/lumbar injection in juvenile cynomolgus macaques (1.5–2 years of age). Animals received either vehicle or EXG001-307 at low (1.0 × 1013 vg/animal; 4×1012 vg/kg) or high (3.0 × 1013 vg/animal; 1.2×1013 vg/kg) doses and were monitored for 4 or 26 weeks.

No treatment-related adverse effects were observed in either group across all evaluated parameters, including mortality, morbidity, clinical observations, functional observational battery (FOB), body weight, food consumption, electrocardiogram (lead II), blood pressure, respiration rate, body temperature, hematology, clinical chemistry, coagulation (representative data shown in Figure S2A), CSF pathology, ophthalmology, or bone marrow cell morphology.

Histopathology

At the week 4 necropsy (Figure 5C), minimal to mild mononuclear cell infiltration and inflammation were observed in the DRG neurons across all groups, predominantly affecting the lumbar and sacral segments. With respect to axonopathy, minimal to mild changes were noted in a subset of animals in the sacral DRG: 2/4 in the vehicle group, 1/4 in the low-dose group, and 3/6 in the high-dose group. The high-dose group showed a comparable incidence to the vehicle control but with slightly greater severity, exhibiting mild axonopathy in 2 of 6 animals. No lesions were detected in cervical, thoracic, or lumbar segments. Comparable but less pronounced changes were also observed in vehicle-treated animals, suggesting a possible contribution from procedural effects (Figure 5C). Outside of the DRG, minimal focal mononuclear cell infiltration was occasionally noted in the brain cortex (1/4 vehicle, 1/4 low dose, 2/6 high dose), all of which were minimal severity and considered background findings (Figure S2B). By the week 26 necropsy, all lesions had fully resolved, with no treatment-related changes detected in the brain, spinal cord, DRG, or any other organs (Figure S2C). This temporal pattern aligns with prior preclinical findings for Zolgensma, in which DRG neuronal degeneration and spinal nerve axonopathy peaked between 2 and 6 weeks post treatment and showed partial resolution by 1 year.38 Furthermore, comprehensive histopathological assessment of other major organs, including heart, spleen, lungs, kidneys, adrenal glands, thymus, gastrointestinal tract, reproductive organs, and injection sites, revealed no test article-related lesions at either time point.

Immunogenicity

EXG001-307 administration induced anti-AAV9 capsid antibodies and neutralizing antibodies (NAbs) in all vector-treated animals (Figures S2D and S2E). Despite these humoral responses, no treatment-related abnormalities were detected in cytokine profiles, lymphocyte subset distributions, complement activity, circulating immune complexes, bone marrow, or immune organ histology (thymus, spleen). In addition, no T cell responses against AAV9 or hSMN were detected in EXG001-307-treated peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) following stimulation with AAV9 or SMN protein peptide libraries through ELISpot assays.

Vector biodistribution, transgene expression, pharmacokinetics, and shedding

Vector biodistribution analysis revealed widespread distribution of vector genomes across multiple tissues, with the highest levels detected in the liver, heart, spinal cord, brain, DRG, spleen, and lymph nodes (Figures 5D, S2F, and S2G). Robust transgene expression was confirmed by hSMN mRNA (quantitative reverse transcription PCR [qRT-PCR], Figure 5E) and protein (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay [ELISA], Figure 5F) in disease-relevant tissues, particularly the spinal cord and brainstem. Notably, transgene mRNA and protein levels were consistently high across all spinal cord segments and showed no significant decline over time. To directly assess the transduction in motor neurons, RNAscope/Basescope in situ hybridization (ISH) was performed on spinal cord lumbar sections (Figures 5G and 5H). Using consecutive sections stained with anti-ChAT antibody to identify motor neurons, transgene-specific probes revealed robust expression of hSMN mRNA within ChAT+ motor neurons, with quantification showing ∼30% transduction at the low dose and ∼50% at the high dose. In this study, we did not perform a head-to-head comparison between EXG001-307 and scAAV9-CB-hSMN, which could provide additional insight into potential clinical outcomes. However, given that EXG001-307 also utilizes the AAV9 capsid, we anticipate that its transduction efficiency is likely comparable to that of scAAV9-CB-hSMN.

Pharmacokinetic analysis demonstrated vector DNA peaked in blood and CSF within one to two timepoints post dosing, followed by a gradual decline to near-background levels by week 26 (Figures 5I and 5J). Similarly, transient vector DNA was detected in urine and feces but declined over time, falling below the limit of detection by day 93 (Figures 5K and 5L).

Overall safety conclusion

Based on the absence of treatment-related adverse effects at the highest tested dose (3.0 × 1013 vg/animal or 1.2×1013 vg/kg), the high dose was designated the no-observed-adverse-effect level. The entire dataset supports a favorable safety profile for EXG001-307 in juvenile NHPs, with efficient CNS delivery, high target tissue expression, transient and reversible microscopic findings, and manageable immunogenicity.

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to develop a next-generation AAV-based therapy for SMA with improved efficacy and reduced toxicity compared with currently approved treatments. To this end, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of a large panel of AAV constructs in the SMA mouse model (SMNΔ7) to identify vectors with optimal therapeutic potential. Survival benefit served as the key selection criterion, ultimately leading to the identification of EXG001-307 as the lead candidate. Importantly, i.c.v. administration in neonatal mice produced markedly superior efficacy relative to systemic i.v. delivery, underscoring the critical role of direct CSF delivery in achieving robust CNS transduction. At the same time, this approach maintained balanced peripheral organ transduction as well, ensuring adequate SMN expression to meet the physiological requirements of peripheral tissues. With the lead candidate and optimal ROA established, subsequent dose-ranging studies in neonatal SMNΔ7 mice and toxicology assessments in adult rats and juvenile NHPs demonstrated dose-dependent survival of up to 501 days at 2 × 1011 vg/animal (1.3 × 1014 vg/kg) in mice, along with a favorable safety profile in both rats and macaques.

While advances in novel capsid engineering continue to drive innovation in the AAV field, optimizing the vector genome, including promoters and various regulatory elements, is also pivotal for achieving an optimal transgene expression level to balance efficacy and safety. Excessive transgene expression in gene therapy models of genetic deficiency has been associated with overexpression-induced toxicity,4,5,10,11,12,16,30 underscoring the critical need for precise transcriptional control. This exemplifies the “Goldilocks principle,” in which transgene expression must be carefully balanced to be neither insufficient nor excessive. The commonly used CBA ubiquitous promoter provides strong and broad expression suitable for first-generation vectors; however, as therapeutic programs advance, incorporating second-generation promoters and regulatory modules that enable cell type-specific, tunable, restricted, or more controlled expression becomes essential.

As mentioned above, that supraphysiological transgene expression may be detrimental and toxic. Our findings provide direct evidence supporting this concept and further demonstrate that transgene expression must be finely tuned to an optimal range, as both insufficient and excessive expression fail to achieve the desired outcome. To address this, we developed the FUTURE platform (Finely Universal Tune for Regulatable Expression), which integrates optimized promoters, regulatable DNA elements, microRNA target sites, codon optimization, and administration route considerations to finely tailor transgene expression for each therapeutic indication, including SMA. In our study, EXG001-340 differed from EXG001-307 only in carrying a codon-optimized hSMN transgene, which enhanced transcript stability and protein expression compared with EXG001-307 in both i.v. or i.c.v. administrations (Figure S1E, in vitro; Figures 1C and S1H, in vivo), while all other vector elements remained identical. At a lower dose (2 × 1014 vg/kg), EXG001-340, which enhances SMN protein expression, markedly outperformed EXG001-307, extending median survival from 36 to 224 days (Figure S1F) when dosed through i.v. These results indicate that increased SMN expression is beneficial when maintained below a certain threshold. However, at the higher dose of 4 × 1014 vg/kg, this trend reversed: EXG001-307 extended median survival of SMNΔ7 mice to 191 days, whereas survival of animals treated with EXG001-340 declined sharply to 29.5 days following i.v. administration (Figure S1G). This paradoxical survival reduction in EXG001-340 high-dose group, despite greater transgene expression, strongly suggests a dose-limiting toxicity mediated by supraphysiological SMN levels. Comprehensive safety and histopathological evaluations were not conducted in the EXG001-340 high-dose cohort and warrant further investigation in future studies. Further investigation revealed that intra-CSF administration of EXG001-307 yielded the best efficacy, outperforming all other tested candidates. We propose that intra-CSF delivery facilitates efficient neuronal transduction, such that once a sufficient proportion of neurons receive adequate vector copies, robust therapeutic benefit can be achieved without reliance on strong expression cassettes driving excessive SMN production. By contrast, supratherapeutic SMN expression driven by potent regulatory elements within individual cells, including neurons, hepatocytes, and cardiomyocytes, appears to exert detrimental effects. Importantly, intra-CSF-administered EXG001-307 can also enter the systemic circulation via CSF outflow, thereby enabling transduction of peripheral tissues to meet their physiological requirement for SMN. At the same time, this route limits excessive vector accumulation in the liver and heart, reducing the risk of AAV vector overload and supraphysiological SMN expression-associated adverse effects.

Subsequent pharmacology, dose-ranging, and safety assessments further validated the therapeutic promise of EXG001-307 when administered intrathecally. Long-term longitudinal evaluation of SMNΔ7 mice following EXG001-307 administration demonstrated no neuronal abnormalities or evidence of DRG toxicity. In contrast, other studies of mice overexpressing SMN under GUSB promoter reported late-onset (4 months of age) hindlimb clasping and sensorimotor deficits.4 Pilot safety studies in rats and pivotal GLP-compliant toxicology evaluations in juvenile cynomolgus macaques, encompassing pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, shedding, and immunogenicity, revealed no significant adverse findings. Importantly, EXG001-307 exhibited minimal DRG pathology at week 4 necropsy, which fully resolved at week 26 necropsy, supporting a favorable neuronal safety profile. In addition, no evidence of liver injury was observed in juvenile NHPs, as serum AST and ALT levels remained comparable to pre-dosing baselines (Figure S2A), and no histopathological abnormalities were detected in the liver. The only exception was a single animal in the high-dose group, which exhibited a transient AST increase of 7.96-fold and an ALT increase of 2.6-fold on day 15. Both values returned to baseline by day 27 without clinical signs. In all other animals, clinical chemistry parameters showed only minor fluctuations (≤2-fold), consistent with normal physiological variation. This contrasts with prior preclinical studies of Zolgensma, in which AST and ALT elevations ranged from 2- to 77-fold in NHPs.17

A major clinical challenge for AAV gene therapy is the presence of pre-existing NAbs, which can block vector uptake and reduce transduction following systemic administration.39 This often necessitates patient exclusion and complicates enrollment. The challenge is particularly acute in rare diseases, where the limited patient population and high seropositivity rates (30%–70%) make screening and enrollment especially difficult.40 In this study, we evaluated the impact of baseline anti-AAV9 antibodies on IT delivery in NHPs with diverse TAb/NAb titers. Although higher baseline NAbs and TAbs (red and green circles in Figures S2H and S2I) reduced vector distribution in peripheral tissues, CNS biodistribution and transduction remained unaffected, indicating that IT administration can overcome systemic humoral immunity for CNS targeting (Figure S2J). This finding aligns with previous and recent reports demonstrating that CSF delivery enables effective CNS transduction despite circulating antibodies.41,42,43 This effect is likely attributable to limited antibody penetration across the blood-CSF barrier, the close proximity of vector to target cells, and the more rapid clearance of IgG from CSF relative to its diffusion from blood.42,44 In our study, the highest NAb titer evaluated was 1:3,962 and the highest total antibody (TAb) titer was 1:1,003, and animals with extremely high titers were not specifically enrolled. However, the data reported by Vono et al. support the conclusion that even markedly elevated anti-AAV9 TAb levels (up to 1:327,680) do not compromise CNS biodistribution (e.g., spinal cord) after IT delivery. From a translational standpoint, our findings together with these published data suggest that IT administration of EXG001-307 may reduce the need to exclude patients based solely on anti-AAV9 serostatus, potentially broadening trial eligibility and facilitating enrollment.42,43 However, we recognize that subtle quantitative effects on transgene expression cannot be excluded, particularly given the substantial heterogeneity in anti-AAV9 antibody titers observed in real-world clinical populations. Reported total anti-AAV9 antibody (TAb) titers in humans span a wide range, from <1:10 to approximately 1:600,000, and inter-assay variability further contributes to differences in reported values.45,46 Accordingly, assessment of TAb/NAb status remains advisable in clinical studies, not necessarily as an exclusion criterion, but to enable stratified analyses of efficacy and safety between seropositive and seronegative participants, and to better interpret potential variability in clinical response. This approach could alleviate a critical bottleneck in rare disease gene therapy development. Moreover, these data emphasize the necessity of integrating delivery route and immune considerations early in preclinical evaluation and regulatory planning to optimize therapeutic outcomes.

A unique finding in our SMA mouse model was the restoration of reproductive capacity following treatment. EXG001-307-treated SMNΔ7 mice not only survived to sexual maturity, but a subset (five out of eight) also produced healthy offspring with normal fertility, an outcome rarely observed given the severe neuromuscular impairment and early lethality of untreated animals. This recovery suggests that the therapy restores neuromuscular, autonomic, respiratory, and reproductive function to an extent sufficient to support complex physiological processes such as mating and gestation. These results carry translational implications for long-term quality of life in SMA patients, extending beyond motor improvements to fundamental life functions. In the present study, we did not perform a head-to-head comparison with scAAV9-CB-hSMN1 with respect to breeding fitness, and such comparative evaluation may warrant further investigation. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating that SMNΔ7 model mice are capable of normal breeding and producing offspring following gene therapy.

In conclusion, our findings underscore the importance of careful vector design and rigorous evaluation during the early stages of gene therapy development. Regulatory elements must be tailored to specific disease contexts, as there is no universal, one-size-fits-all approach, and higher expression is not invariably beneficial. Instead, each component of the vector must be optimized to achieve a therapeutic balance between efficacy and safety. Within this framework, the present study provides compelling nonclinical evidence supporting the advancement of EXG001-307 toward clinical development, highlighting its potential to address limitations of existing SMA therapies and contribute meaningfully to the evolving landscape of gene therapy for neurological disorders. The demonstrated safety profile, robust CNS targeting, and functional recovery highlight its potential to overcome limitations of current SMA therapies and broaden the treatment landscape. Early clinical experience from an investigator-initiated trial, in which three patients have already been dosed, has demonstrated a favorable safety profile and preliminary benefit. Ongoing regulatory interactions and planned IND submissions collectively underscore the promise of EXG001-307 as a novel and impactful therapeutic candidate for patients with SMA.

Materials and methods

Objectives and study design

We aimed to design and optimize a novel AAV construct for SMA therapy, benchmarked against the control vector scAAV9-CB-hSMN (structurally identical to Zolgensma/onasemnogene abeparvovec-xioi). A multi-stage screening study (Study #1) was conducted in SMNΔ7 mice using an iterative approach to compare constructs varying in promoters, enhancers, transgenes, miRNA binding sites, and other regulatory elements (summarized in Table 1).

Based on Study #1, lead candidates were further evaluated in Study #2 following different ROAs (i.v. or intra-CSF) administration on PND 1, with assessments of survival, body weight, and motor performance. In addition, Study #3 was designed to evaluate vector biodistribution and transgene expression, with multiple groups of animals administered either i.v. or i.c.v. and necropsied 2 weeks post dosing. Liver, heart, spinal cord, and brain tissues were preserved in RNAlater, flash-frozen, or fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for RT-qPCR, ELISA, and ISH (RNAscope/Basescope), respectively.

From these results, EXG001-307, featuring a hSyn1 neuron-specific promoter with a CMV enhancer, administered via intra-CSF injection, was selected as the clinical candidate. Its pharmacology, safety, and biodistribution were assessed in three IND-enabling studies: (i) a dose range-finding study in SMNΔ7 mice (Study #4), (ii) a non-GLP pilot safety study in adult rats (Study #5), and (iii) a GLP-compliant pivotal toxicology study in juvenile NHPs (Study #6). In Study #4, PND 1 SMNΔ7 mice received EXG001-307 via i.c.v. at doses 2.0 × 1010–2.0 × 1011 vg/animal, which is equivalent to 1.3 × 1013– 1.3 × 1014 vg/kg, to assess efficacy, dose-response, and benchmark comparisons. Studies #5 and #6 employed single IT/lumbar administration to characterize EXG001-307 safety in rats and cynomolgus macaques, with the GLP NHP study (Study #6) providing comprehensive safety, immunogenicity, biodistribution, and shedding data to support clinical translation.

AAV vector production and characterization

A panel of AAV constructs, with different promoters, cis-regulatory elements, gene of interest (GOI), and miRNA-targeting sites, was designed, cloned, and packaged into AAV9 capsids using self-complementary (sc) genomes for vector production. These vectors were then manufactured by a triple plasmid-transfection system in adherent HEK293 cells and purified with an iodixanol gradient (vectors used in Studies #1–#5 in Table 1). The clinical candidate, EXG001-307 (scAAV9.CMVenh.hSyn1.hSMN), was manufactured in a GMP grade by a triple plasmid-transfection system in suspension HEK293 cells and purified by an affinity chromatography via an AAVX resin, followed by anion exchange chromatography (Study #6 in Table 1).

The purified vectors were buffer-exchanged into a sterile formulation solution composed of water for injection with 20 mM Tris, 1 mM MgCl2, 200 mM NaCl, and 0.005% (w/v) Poloxamer 188 (Pluronic F-68), adjusted to pH 8.0. Endotoxin levels were quantified using the Limulus amebocyte lysate assay, while sterility was confirmed by direct inoculation testing. Vector genome titers were determined by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR). Capsid protein purity was evaluated by SDS-PAGE, and the percentage of full capsids of this lot was 106.1%, determined by comparing the transgene titer (ddPCR) to the total capsid titer (ELISA).

Animals and in vivo procedures

All animal procedures were conducted in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Academies Press, 8th edition) and adhered to AAALAC International standards, IACUC regulations, and institutional SOPs.

Mice

Mice were maintained under controlled environmental conditions (40%–60% humidity, 18°C–23°C) with a 12-h light/dark cycle and provided ad libitum access to food and water. To ensure sex-balanced representation, studies incorporated approximately equal numbers of male and female mice. We employed the established SMNΔ7 mouse model (JAX #005025) for Studies #1–#4. These Smn−/−;SMN2+/+;SMNΔ7+/+ mutants on an FVB/N background lack endogenous SMN (replaced by LacZ) while expressing two human transgenes: full-length SMN2 and Δ7SMN. As documented (The Jackson Laboratory, 2013), homozygotes show progressive SMA-like pathology including neonatal growth defects, PND 5-onset weakness, and median 17.7-day survival. Genotyping confirmed SMN knockout status via tail DNA PCR.

In Study #1, SMNΔ7 mice received i.v. injections of different vector constructs on PND 1 (24–48 h after birth), and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was conducted as the primary efficacy endpoint for reliable screening. Following the selection of the lead constructs EXG001-307 and EXG001-340 based on their robust survival efficacy, Study #2 and Study #3 compared i.v. versus intra-CSF (specifically i.c.v.) administration by evaluating behavior and transduction efficiency across central and peripheral tissues. The animals were also dosed on PND 1. scAAV9-CB-hSMN benchmark reference (2 × 1011 vg/animal or 1.3 × 1014 vg/kg), and WT animals without treatment served as controls. Animals in the EXG001-340 group were necropsied prior to reaching natural death, when intergroup differences had already become apparent. In Study #4, SMNΔ7 mice received a single i.c.v. injection of either EXG001-307 (at four dose levels: 2 × 1011 vg/animal [1.3 × 1014 vg/kg], 6 × 1010 vg/animal [4 × 1013 vg/kg], 4 × 1010 vg/animal [2.7 × 1013 vg/kg], or 2 × 1010 vg/animal [1.3 × 1013 vg/kg]) or vehicle control on PND 1, administered as either bilateral (10 μL total; 5 μL/ventricle) or unilateral (3–5 μL) injections; daily viability monitoring was conducted until endpoint criteria were met with Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, while body weights were recorded every 2–3 days through study day 98 (with PND 0 designated as birth day), and functional assessments included righting reflex tests (PND 8, 10, 12, 14) and open field tests (PND 14 and 17).

Rats

Only adult male rats were used in this study, based on prior evidence indicating DRG toxicity in males following AAV administration.47 Rats were housed in individually ventilated cages (IVC; ≤2 animals/cage) under controlled conditions (20°C–26°C, 40%–70% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) compliant with China’s GB 14925-2010 standards. Sterilized corncob bedding (8 mesh; Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bio-engineering Co.) and irradiation-sterilized maintenance feed (same supplier) were provided ad libitum; both were routinely screened for contaminants (e.g., heavy metals, aflatoxin B1, pathogens) via certified analysis. Reverse osmosis drinking water, tested semi-annually for physicochemical and microbiological parameters, was available freely. Animals were uniquely identified by tail markings and color-coded cage cards (recording study/dose/sex details).

17 WT male adult Wistar Han rats (6–8 weeks of age) were divided into a total of three groups (4–7 animals per group) and observed for 16 weeks after a single administration. Each rat was administered by IT/lumbar injection, which was consistent with the proposed ROA for clinical use. The three test groups were EXG001-307 low-dose group (8.5 × 1011 vg/animal or 4.3 × 1012 vg/kg), EXG001-307 high-dose group (1.7 × 1012vg/animal or 8.5 × 1012 vg/kg), and saline control group.

NHPs

Juvenile cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis) were used in this study. A total of 34 animals (50% male, 50% female) were acclimated prior to dosing, with 32 (equal sex distribution) ultimately included. At dosing, animals were 1.5–2 years of age, with mean body weights of 2.56 kg (males) and 2.38 kg (females). All monkeys were obtained from AKAIB (Zhanjiang) Biotechnology Co. and were confirmed free of tuberculosis, parasites, Salmonella, and Shigella before enrollment.

Animals were housed in same-sex, same-dose pairs in stainless-steel cages under controlled environmental conditions (16°C–28°C; 30%–70% relative humidity; 12-h light/dark cycle) in compliance with Chinese National Standard GB 14925–2010. Monkeys received ad libitum access to standardized primate diet (Ke’ao Xieli Co., batch 24058111), which met Chinese National Standards GB 14924.3–2010/GB/T 14924.2–2001 for nutritional composition and contaminant limits. Fresh fruit was provided twice weekly, and water was available ad libitum via bottles or automated systems.

32 juvenile cynomolgus macaques were randomly assigned to three groups: vehicle control (n = 10), low dose (n = 10), and high dose (n = 12). EXG001-307 was administered via a single IT/lumbar injection at 1.0 × 1013 vg/animal (low dose; 4 × 1012 vg/kg) or 3.0 × 1013 vg/animal (high dose; 1.2 × 1013 vg/kg). Vehicle control animals received an equivalent volume of formulation buffer. Within each group, half of the animals were necropsied at week 4 and the remaining half at week 26 post administration.

Vector administration

i.v. injections in neonatal mice

i.v. injections (30 μL) via the temporal vein were performed on PND 1, within 24–48 h after birth, in SMNΔ7 mice. Neonatal mice were anesthetized by placing them on ice for 1–3 min. The temporal vein, located anterior to the ear bud and beneath the surrounding capillaries, was identified as a faintly visible vessel running dorsally to ventrally, merging into the jugular vein. A syringe was used to draw 30 μL of the test articles or vehicle, and the syringe needle was inserted at an angle into the temporal vein. Successful entry was confirmed by observing whether blood filled the needle tip upon passing through the skin. Gentle pressure was applied to the syringe, and a successful injection was confirmed when whitening of the facial veins was observed. The needle was maintained in the vessel for 5–15 s post injection to ensure proper delivery.

i.c.v. injections in neonatal mice

Neonatal FVB SMNΔ7 mice (PND 1; 24–48 h after birth) received either unilateral (3–5 μL) or bilateral (10 μL; 5 μL per ventricle) i.c.v. injections (see Table 1). Dams were temporarily removed, and pups were handled with bedding to minimize rejection. Anesthesia was induced by brief hypothermia on ice. Injections were performed with a 10-μL microsyringe via a periorbital approach, 4 mm lateral to the midline and 2 mm deep, with a 30-s dwell time before needle withdrawal. Bilateral injections were administered sequentially. Pups were returned to their cage for recovery before reintroduction of the dams.

IT/lumbar injections in rats

Adult male Wistar Han rats were anesthetized with intraperitoneal xylazine (4 mg/kg) and Zoletil (10 mg/kg), with anesthesia depth confirmed by loss of the hind paw withdrawal reflex. The dorsal surface from tail to caudal thoracic vertebrae was shaved, and the animal was positioned prone with the lumbar spine flexed over a support. The intervertebral space between L5 and L6 was identified by palpating the iliac crests and locating the adjacent spinous processes. A <2-cm midline skin incision was made to expose the injection site. A 29G insulin syringe containing the prepared vector solution (50 μL or 85 μL) was inserted perpendicularly into the vertebral canal. Correct placement was confirmed by tail flick, after which the solution was injected slowly. The needle was retained for 1 min before withdrawal. The wound was sterilized and sutured, and animals were monitored until full recovery.

IT/lumbar injection in NHPs

Cynomolgus macaques were fasted prior to anesthesia and anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (∼25 mg/kg) via i.v., with dosage adjusted as needed. Animals were positioned in lateral recumbency with maximal head and spine flexion to widen the lumbar intervertebral space. Following shaving and povidone-iodine disinfection, the puncture site between L4-L5 or L5-L6 was identified by palpation. A 0.6 × 25 mm sterile needle was inserted perpendicularly into the subarachnoid space; correct placement was confirmed by CSF efflux. CSF was collected into sterile tubes for analysis as required. The test article was injected at ∼1 mL/min, after which the needle was withdrawn and gentle pressure applied to the site. Animals were maintained in the Trendelenburg supine position for at least 15 min post injection.

Efficacy of AAV vectors in SMA mouse model

Survival monitoring

SMNΔ7 mice received different AAV vectors or vehicle and were observed daily for survival, clinical signs, and general activity. The study continued for over 500 days, concluding when the final animal reached the predefined endpoint.

Animal body weight

The day of birth was designated as PND 0, and dosing was performed on PND 1, within 24–48 h after birth. Body weight was measured every 2–3 days through PND 98. To provide a clearer depiction of growth patterns and minimize visual complexity, certain graphs present body weight data at 12-day intervals.

Righting reflex test

On PND 8, 10, 12, and 14, righting reflex assessments were conducted in surviving mice. Animals were placed in the left lateral decubitus position on a temperature-controlled platform (36°C), and the latency to return to a normal supine position was recorded on video. A maximum cutoff time of 30 s was applied; animals exceeding this threshold were assigned a value of 30 s, while actual latencies were recorded for those that righted within the limit.

Open field test

On PND 14 and 17, locomotor activity was evaluated using an open field assay. Animals were placed in the testing chamber for a 3-min habituation period prior to data acquisition. During the subsequent 5-min test, movements were video recorded, and average velocity and total distance traveled were quantified.

Safety assessment

In-life observations

Rats

WT rats were observed at least twice daily from 1 day prior to dosing through the entire period post treatment. Health assessments included survival, general appearance (eyes, nose, mouth, urogenital and perianal regions), body condition, and injection site integrity (e.g., redness, swelling, scabbing). Behavioral and neurological parameters, such as activity, locomotor function, startle response, tremors, paralysis, and gait, were monitored alongside respiratory and excretory status for signs of distress or abnormalities. Animals exhibiting clinical signs of toxicity received increased monitoring. Veterinarians verified observations weekly or more frequently if warranted. Moribund or deceased animals were recorded, and necropsy was performed without delay. All procedures followed Exegenesis Bio’s standard operating protocols.

NHPs

Cynomolgus macaques were monitored twice daily for morbidity, mortality, and clinical observations documented injection site condition, behavioral status, feeding and drinking patterns, coat appearance, excretions, and other indicators of toxicity. Detailed physical examinations (one to two times/week) assessed general appearance, neurological status, and systemic abnormalities. FOB assessments were performed during acclimation (pre-dose) and on study days 14, 27, 55, 90, 140, and 180 following SOP, which included cage-side observations, open field evaluations, motor coordination tests, and sensory/motor reflex examinations. Body weight was recorded prior to dosing and at scheduled intervals (week 1, 2, 4, 8, 13, 17, 21 and 26) until necropsy; food consumption was measured as 24-h intake, and rectal temperature was monitored at the same timepoints. Cardiovascular and respiratory parameters were assessed at defined intervals via telemetry (lead II ECG, blood pressure) and respiratory monitoring. Stable 30-s waveform segments were analyzed using ecgAUTO software.

Clinical pathology

Detection of NfL release in serum or CSF

Serum samples from rats and both serum and CSF samples from cynomolgus macaques were collected, aliquoted, snap-frozen on dry ice, and stored at −80°C until analysis. NfL concentrations in rat serum were measured using a Rat NfL ELISA Kit (NOVUS, NBP2-81185), whereas NfL concentrations in macaque serum and CSF were determined using an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) on the MESO QuickPlex SQ 120 platform (Meso Scale Discovery, USA; cat. #K156AKGS, lots #K00S0198 and #K00S0211), following the manufacturers’ protocols. All samples were assayed in technical duplicate, with repeat measurements performed when the coefficient of variation between replicates exceeded 20%. For rat samples, absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader, and concentrations were derived from a standard curve generated from known calibrators. For macaque samples, ECLIA signal intensities were processed using Watson LIMS software (v7.4, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and concentrations were interpolated using a four-parameter logistic curve fit.

Histopathology

At week 16 post injection, rats were euthanized and transcardially perfused with cold saline via the left ventricle to clear blood, with the right atrium opened to allow outflow. Following perfusion, liver, heart, brain, spinal cord (cervical, thoracic, lumbar segments), and DRGs (cervical, thoracic, lumbar segments) were collected. Tissue samples from the left side were snap-frozen on dry ice and stored at −80°C for molecular analyses, while contralateral tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) for histological and immunofluorescence studies. Eyeballs and testes were initially fixed in modified Davidson’s solution for ≥24 h before transfer to 10% NBF. Fixed tissues were paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histopathological evaluation was conducted by a qualified pathologist under light microscopy. H&E staining of DRGs and lumbar spinal cord was performed by Hangzhou Alliance Bio following their standard operating procedures.

Immunology

Complement components and circulating immune complexes were quantified in serum samples collected at predefined study intervals. T lymphocyte subsets (CD3+, CD4+, CD8+) were characterized by flow cytometry using heparinized whole blood. Serum cytokine levels (IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-2, IL-6, and IL-10) were measured using Meso Scale Discovery electrochemiluminescence assays. Anti-AAV9 and anti-hSMN antibodies were detected via ECLIA and ELISA, respectively, while neutralizing antibodies were assessed using validated cell-based assays. For ELISpot analysis, PBMCs were isolated by density gradient centrifugation (Dakeyi reagents), cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen, and processed according to validated protocol. All immunoassays incorporated sample integrity controls and were performed in compliance with manufacturer and institutional SOPs.

Viral genomic DNA biodistribution

Following organ collection, residual blood was gently removed by pressing the tissue on sterile gauze. Approximately 10–60 mg of each tissue was transferred to a 2.0-mL centrifuge tube, flash-frozen on dry ice, and stored at ≤−65°C until analysis. Total DNA was extracted from frozen mouse and rat tissues using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, or from NHP tissues using the MagNA Pure 96 System with the DNA and Viral NA Large Volume Kit (program: DNA Tissue LV 4.0; 200 μL input and elution volumes). TaqMan quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed with primers and probes specific to the EXG001-307 vector (Tables S4 and S5). A standard curve was generated by plotting quantification cycle (Cq) values against serial dilutions of a plasmid containing the target sequence. Sample Cq values were interpolated from the standard curve to determine viral genome copy numbers, which were normalized to diploid genome equivalents and expressed as vector genomes per diploid cell or vector genomes per μg DNA.

Transgene (hSMN) mRNA expression

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR

Following tissue collection, residual blood was gently removed by pressing the tissue on sterile gauze. Approximately 10–60 mg of each sample was placed in a 2.0-mL centrifuge tube containing RNAlater solution, incubated at 4°C overnight, and stored at ≤ −65°C until analysis. For mouse and rat tissues, total RNA was extracted using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit, and first-strand cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit. For NHP tissues, samples (preferentially from central regions) were dissected and homogenized in 2.0-mL grinding tubes containing 3–5 ceramic beads (3 mm diameter) and 400 μL lysis buffer per 15 mg tissue (400 μL for samples <15 mg). Mechanical disruption was performed using a SUPER homogenizer (6,500 rpm, 20 s pulses × 3 with 20 s intervals; repeated 1–3 cycles as needed). After brief centrifugation, 350 μL of homogenate was transferred to a 96-well plate and processed on the MagNA Pure 96 system using the Cellular RNA LV 2.0 Kit (program: RNA Tissue FF Standard LV 4.0; 350 μL input/50 μL elution). RNA concentration was determined with a μDrop Plate (Varioskan LUX, dsRNA quantification program), and extracts were either used immediately or stored at ≤−60°C.

Quantification of hSMN transgene expression was performed by TaqMan qPCR using primers and probes specific for hSMN (Table S6). Mouse, rat, and NHP GAPDH mRNA served as the internal control. Primer and probe sequences for quantification of housekeeping GAPDH are provided in Tables S7, S8, and S9. Relative hSMN mRNA expression was calculated as 2ˆ(−ΔCq), where ΔCq = Cq_hSMN − Cq_GAPDH.

In situ hybridization and immunohistochemical analysis

Consecutive lumbar spinal cord sections from low-dose and high-dose group macaques in Study #6 at each necropsy time point (weeks 4 and 26) were processed for parallel BaseScope ISH and immunohistochemical analysis (IHC). Animals in vehicle control were used as negative control. For hSMN mRNA detection, 10% neutral formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections underwent RNA target retrieval followed by hybridization with species-specific oligonucleotide probes (BaseScope Reagent Kit, ACD Bio). Signal amplification generated punctate fluorescent dots (each representing a single mRNA molecule), with positive (PPIB) and negative (dapB) control probes included in each run to validate assay specificity. Adjacent sections were immunostained with anti-ChAT antibody (1:200, Millipore Sigma AB144P) to identify motor neurons. Quantification was performed to calculate the percentage of ChAT+ neurons exhibiting hSMN mRNA puncta. All procedures followed manufacturer protocols with RNase-free conditions.

Transgene (hSMN) protein expression

ELISA

Tissue samples (10–20 mg) were homogenized in Pierce RIPA Buffer (1:5 w/v; minimum 100 μL for samples <20 mg) with 3–5 ceramic beads (3 mm diameter) using a SUPER homogenizer (4,500 rpm, 20 s pulses × 6 with 20 s intervals; repeated 1–3 cycles if necessary). Homogenates were incubated on ice for 30 ± 5 min, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, and supernatants were aliquoted (100 μL primary aliquot, remainder stored at ≤ −60°C). Total protein was quantified using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. SMN protein levels were measured by ELISA using an anti-SMN antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# MABE230), with absorbance read at 450 nm on a microplate reader. Concentrations were determined from a standard curve generated with known SMN protein standards.

Western blot

Cell pallets or tissue samples (10–20 mg) were homogenized in Pierce RIPA Buffer using a SUPER homogenizer. Homogenates were incubated on ice for 30 ± 5 min, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, and supernatants were aliquoted and stored at <−60°C. Total protein was quantified using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. SMN protein levels were analyzed by western blot using an anti-SMN antibody (BD Biosciences, cat. #610647). Equal amounts of total protein were denatured, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibody, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody incubation for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence and quantified with ImageJ. SMN expression was normalized to β-tubulin as a loading control.

Viral vector shedding

Viral vector shedding was assessed in a GLP-compliant toxicology study in cynomolgus macaques. Urine (3 mL, thymol-preserved) and fecal samples were collected during acclimation and on study days 5, 16, 26, 58, 93, 143, and 180 without fasting, and stored at ≤−60°C within 2 h. For urine, DNA was extracted from 200-μL aliquots using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen) with 400 ng oligo (dT) carrier, Proteinase K digestion (20 μL, 56°C, 10 min), ethanol precipitation, sequential Buffer AW1/AW2 washes, and elution in 50 μL Buffer AE. Fecal DNA was extracted from 100 mg homogenized in PBS (1:5 w/v), treated with Proteinase K (50 μL, 2 mg/mL, 65°C, 10 min), and processed on the MagNA Pure 96 System with the DNA and Viral NA Large Volume Kit (Pathogen Universal 500 program; 500 μL input/50 μL elution). DNA concentration was determined using μDrop spectrophotometry (Varioskan LUX), and extracts were stored at ≤−60°C. Viral genome copy numbers were determined as described above, under Viral genomic DNA biodistribution.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted on GraphPad Prism software version 8.0.2. One-way ANOVA and two-way ANOVA were adopted for analysis. Tukey’s or Dunnett’s tests or other suitable methods were used as the analytical methods to compare significant differences between groups; during intergroup comparisons, t test was used for two-tailed testing between groups. p < 0.05 indicates a significant difference between groups.
