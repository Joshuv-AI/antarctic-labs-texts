Temporal Profile of Stroke-Associated Pneumonia and Deep Vein Thrombosis after Stroke
Authors: Runhua Zhang, Huiqing Hou, Xingquan Zhao, Gaifen Liu, Yongjun Wang, Ruijun Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPoststroke complications pose significant challenges in the management of stroke patients and are associated with adverse outcomes. The potential interplay and temporal sequence of stroke-associated pneumonia (SAP) and deep vein thrombosis (DVT) following stroke remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate the association between SAP and DVT and to elucidate temporal sequence of these complications after acute ischemic stroke (AIS), intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), and subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH).MethodsWe conducted a study using the in-hospital Medical Complications after Acute Stroke (iMCAS) registry at Beijing Tiantan Hospital from 2014 to 2016. We documented the incidence of SAP and DVT and the time from stroke onset to diagnosis of each complication. A time-dependent Cox regression model was used to evaluate the association between SAP and DVT, and the temporal sequence was compared across AIS, ICH, and SAH.ResultsA total of 1,771 patients were included, comprising 1,129 with AIS, 314 with ICH, and 328 with SAH. The incidence of SAP was higher than that of DVT in AIS (7.6% vs. 1.9%), ICH (18.8% vs. 5.7%), and SAH (16.8% vs. 7.9%). SAP occurrence was significantly associated with subsequent DVT development (HR = 3.65, 95% CI: 2.08–6.40, p < 0.001). This association was also confirmed in AIS and ICH. The median time from stroke onset to SAP was 4 days (IQR: 2–6), and to DVT was 8 days (IQR: 5–12). SAP occurred significantly earlier than DVT in all stroke subtypes.ConclusionSAP was significantly associated with an increased risk of subsequent DVT, with a consistent temporal sequence in which SAP preceded DVT.

Introduction

Stroke remains the leading cause of death in China, despite a declining trend in the age-standardized incidence [1]. Poststroke medical complications, such as stroke-associated pneumonia (SAP), are pivotal in determining the prognosis of stroke patients [2–5]. SAP occurs in approximately 10% of stroke cases and is one of the most common medical complications [6, 7]. Numerous efforts have been made to assess the impact of SAP on stroke outcomes, including the development of risk models for SAP [8–11]. However, the interplay between SAP and non-pneumonia medical complications has not been thoroughly elucidated.

In our previous study, we identified a significant association between SAP and several non-pneumonia medical complications, particularly deep vein thrombosis (DVT) after acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) [12]. We hypothesize that pneumonia may be associated with these non-pneumonia medical complications [12]; however, establishing a temporal sequence has been challenging due to the lack of information on the timing of these events.

Given that SAP is the most prevalent complication after stroke, it is essential to investigate whether SAP is temporally associated with the development of other stroke-associated complications, with a specific focus on DVT, another frequent complication. Clarifying this relationship may provide insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying both SAP and DVT. In this study, we aimed to (1) examine the potential association between SAP and DVT across different stroke subtypes; and (2) delineate the temporal profile of SAP and DVT in relation to stroke subtypes.

Methods

Study Population

All patients included in this study were derived from the in-hospital Medical Complications after Acute Stroke (iMCAS) registry. iMCAS is a prospective cohort study with the primary aim to compare the risk of medical complications by stroke subtypes, investigate the potential interrelationship between medical complications, and explore biomarkers or neuroimaging markers for medical complications. Patients admitted to the Neurology Department of Beijing Tiantan Hospital between May 2014 and May 2016 were consecutively enrolled. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients were aged 18 years or older; (2) hospitalization was due to a primary diagnosis of AIS, ICH, or subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), according to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria; (3) the diagnosis of stroke was confirmed by head computed tomography and/or magnetic resonance imaging of the brain; (4) the interval from stroke onset to hospital admission was less than 7 days. Exclusion criteria were (1) refusal to participate or lack of informed consent; (2) death or discharge within 24 h of admission; (3) missing key variables for the primary analysis, including DVT status and SAP status.

Data Collection

Trained research coordinators extracted relevant data from medical records, which were then verified for completeness, correct coding, and appropriate application of the diagnostic algorithm by a research specialist. The following data were collected for all patients: demographic information including age and sex; stroke risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, atrial fibrillation, coronary heart disease, and current smoking status; comorbidities including congestive heart failure, valvular heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, peripheral artery disease, hepatic cirrhosis, peptic ulcer, renal failure, arthritis, and cancer; pre-stroke dependence indicated by a modified Rankin Scale score of 3 or higher; admission stroke severity assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score; blood pressure measurements including systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP); BMI; antiplatelets administered within 48 h of admission; DVT prophylaxis (anticoagulation [unfractionated heparin, low-molecular-weight heparin, warfarin, or nonvitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants], intermittent pneumatic compression, or both); time from stroke onset to the diagnosis of medical complications; and the duration of hospital stay.

Definitions of SAP and DVT

In the iMCAS, medical complications were diagnosed by treating physicians. SAP was diagnosed according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention criteria for hospital-acquired pneumonia, based on clinical and laboratory indices of respiratory tract infection, including fever, cough, auscultatory respiratory crackles, new purulent sputum, or positive sputum culture, and was verified by typical chest X-ray findings [12]. DVT was diagnosed based on clinical manifestations, such as swelling, pitting edema, redness, tenderness, and presence of superficial collateral veins, and verified by sequential compression Doppler ultrasound [12]. Only cases of SAP and DVT that developed after stroke were included in the count. The date of onset for SAP and DVT was defined as the date of clinical diagnosis.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and proportions. The Student’s t test or Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. To evaluate the association between SAP and subsequent DVT while accounting for the temporal sequence, we performed a Cox proportional hazards regression model with SAP as a time-dependent covariate. Follow-up time was calculated from stroke onset to DVT occurrence, death, or discharge. No patients were lost to follow-up during hospitalization; censoring occurred only due to death or discharge. SAP was treated as a time-dependent covariate, with patients considered unexposed from stroke onset until the day before SAP diagnosis and exposed from the day of SAP diagnosis onward. Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and previous literature, including age, sex, baseline NIHSS score, history of atrial fibrillation, smoking status, DVT prophylaxis, and antiplatelet agents administered within 48 h of admission. The proportional hazards assumption was tested for all fixed covariates using Schoenfeld residuals. No violation was detected (all p > 0.05). Subgroup analyses were performed according to stroke subtype (AIS, ICH, SAH). Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings: adjusting for baseline inflammatory markers (neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio [NLR] and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein [hs-CRP]) to explore potential effect modification. There were no missing data for key variables, including NIHSS score and time to DVT diagnosis. For NLR, 27 cases (1.5%) had missing values, and given the small proportion, we used complete-case analysis. For hs-CRP, 266 patients (15.0%) had missing values. Because the missing data mechanism could not be definitively determined, we applied two complementary approaches. First, complete-case analysis was performed using patients with observed hs-CRP. Second, multiple imputation using predictive mean matching was conducted under the missing-at-random assumption, generating ten imputed datasets. The imputation model included all baseline covariates and outcome variables. Cox proportional hazards regression with a time-dependent SAP variable was performed on each imputed dataset, and the results were pooled using Rubin’s rules. The results from both approaches are reported to provide a comprehensive assessment of the association between SAP and DVT after adjusting for hs-CRP. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the time from stroke onset to diagnosis between SAP and DVT. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The clinical characteristics are shown in Table 1. From May 2014 to May 2016, a total of 1,771 patients were enrolled in the iMCAS study (1,129 with AIS, 314 with ICH, and 328 with SAH). The mean age was 57.1 ± 12.9 years, and 72.5% were male. The median length of hospitalization was 14 days (IQR: 11–16) for AIS, 14 days (IQR: 12–18) for ICH, and 14 days (IQR: 11–18) for SAH. There were 200 (11.3%) patients who developed SAP, and 66 (3.7%) who developed DVT during hospitalization. Older patients tended to develop SAP during hospitalization. Furthermore, patients with hypertension, atrial fibrillation, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease were more likely to develop SAP. Patients with SAP had significantly longer hospital stays.

Incidence of SAP and DVT by Subtypes

Figure 1 illustrates the incidences of SAP and DVT after AIS, ICH, and SAH. In the AIS cohort, the cumulative incidence of SAP was significantly higher than that of DVT (7.6% vs. 1.9%; p < 0.001), as was the case in the ICH cohort (18.8% vs. 5.7%; p < 0.001), and the SAH cohort (16.8% vs. 7.9%; p < 0.001).

Association between SAP and DVT

The association between SAP and DVT in AIS, ICH, and SAH is shown in Table 2. After adjusting for all potential confounders, SAP was significantly associated with the development of DVT in all strokes (HR = 3.65, 95% CI: 2.08–6.40, p < 0.001), as well as in AIS (HR = 4.14, 95% CI: 1.28–13.39, p = 0.018) and ICH (HR = 3.45, 95% CI: 1.09–10.89, p = 0.035). Nevertheless, the association between SAP and DVT was not significant in SAH (HR = 1.36, 95% CI: 0.54–3.44, p = 0.510).

Sensitivity Analysis

To assess whether baseline inflammatory status explained the observed association between SAP and DVT, we added baseline log-transformed NLR and hs-CRP separately to the time-dependent Cox model. The HR for SAP decreased from 3.65 (95% CI: 2.08–6.40, p < 0.001) in the full model to 3.06 (95% CI: 1.69–5.55, p < 0.001) after adjustment for log(NLR). In complete-case analysis (excluding 15% missing hs-CRP), the HR for SAP decreased from 3.65 (95% CI: 2.08–6.40, p < 0.001) to 2.84 (95% CI: 1.42–5.69, p < 0.001), suggesting that hs-CRP may partially account for the observed association. Likelihood ratio tests showed that the addition of log(NLR) marginally improved model fit (LR = 3.62, p = 0.056), while the addition of log(hs-CRP) significantly improved model fit (LR = 15.83, p < 0.001). We performed multiple imputation using predictive mean matching to handle missing hs-CRP; this yielded an adjusted HR of 4.90 (95% CI: 2.63–9.15, p < 0.001). The discrepancy between the two methods likely reflects the influence of missing data. Notably, both approaches confirmed that SAP remained significantly associated with DVT after adjustment for hs-CRP.

Temporal Sequence of SAP and DVT

Temporal profiles of stroke onset to diagnosis of SAP and DVT are shown in Figure 2. The median time to SAP after stroke was 4 days (IQR: 2–6). Within the first week following a stroke, 151 cases of SAP (75.5%) were recorded, with the highest incidence on the fifth day. The median time from onset to diagnosis of SAP after AIS, ICH, and SAH was 4 days (IQR: 2–7), 3.5 days (IQR: 2–5), and 5 days (IQR: 3–7), respectively.

Temporal profiles of SAP (a) and DVT (b).

The median time to DVT after stroke was 8 days (IQR: 5–12). A total of 26 DVTs occurred in the first 7 days (39.4%), peaking on the ninth day. The median time from onset to the diagnosis of DVT after AIS, ICH, and SAH was 10.5 (IQR: 4–14), 7.5 (IQR: 4–9), and 7 (IQR: 5–12) days, respectively.

Patients with severe stroke (NIHSS >12) developed SAP significantly earlier (median 3 days, IQR: 1.5–4.0) than those with less severe stroke (NIHSS ≤12) (median 5 days, IQR: 3–7; p < 0.001). However, no significant difference was observed in the time of DVT onset between patients with severe and less severe strokes (5 days [IQR: 3–9] vs. 8 days [IQR: 5–14]; p = 0.174). Additionally, stratification by age and sex revealed no significant differences in the timing of SAP or DVT onset.

Figure 3 illustrates the distribution of onset days for SAP and DVT after stroke. The median time to onset of SAP was significantly shorter compared to DVT for AIS (4 days vs. 10.5 days; p < 0.001), ICH (3.5 days vs. 7.5 days; p < 0.001), and SAH (5 days vs. 7 days; p = 0.007).

Discussion

In the current study, we explored the potential relationship between SAP and DVT after acute stroke. We found that the incidence of SAP was higher than that of DVT in patients with AIS, ICH, and SAH. Furthermore, SAP was significantly associated with the development of DVT after AIS and ICH. The time from stroke onset to SAP occurrence was significantly shorter than the time to DVT onset in AIS, ICH, and SAH patients.

Many studies have shown that medical complications are among the leading causes of prolonged hospitalization and increased mortality after acute stroke [13–15]. DVT is a common complication after stroke. Previous studies have found that older age, female sex, obesity, and severity of ischemic stroke are risk factors for DVT after stroke [16, 17]. Although many studies have focused on specific complications, the interrelationship among these complications is not well understood. SAP is another common medical complication in patients after AIS [18, 19] and SAH [20, 21]. In our previous study, we investigated whether complications after stroke develop independently or not by assessing the interrelationship among them. We found that pneumonia was significantly associated with the development of several non-pneumonia medical complications, including DVT [12]. We proposed the hypothesis that the association between non-pneumonia medical complications after stroke could be moderated by SAP [12]. In the present study, the association between SAP and DVT was verified in patients with AIS and ICH. However, in the SAH subgroup, the association between SAP and DVT was not statistically significant. Several factors may account for this finding. First, the pathophysiology of SAH differs substantially from that of AIS and ICH. Second, the sample size of the SAH subgroup was relatively small, and the number of DVT events may have been insufficient to detect a modest association. Taken together, the absence of a significant association in SAH does not preclude a clinically meaningful relationship, and larger, SAH-specific studies are warranted to further explore this question. In a recent study, the authors developed a prediction model for venous thromboembolism, in which mean corpuscular volume, partial thromboplastin time, D-dimer, as well as lung infection were included [22]. Notably, the odds ratio of lung infection was substantially higher than the odds ratios of the other factors [22]. However, these studies did not delineate the temporal course of SAP and DVT.

Temporal sequence is a necessary condition for establishing a potential causal relationship between SAP and DVT. One study assessed the temporal profile of SAP and urinary tract infection (UTI) in the Virtual International Stroke Trials Archive from randomized acute stroke trials [23]. The study found that the median time to occurrence of SAP was 4.0 days (IQR: 2–12) and for UTI was 6.0 days (IQR: 3–14), with SAP occurring significantly earlier than UTI (p < 0.001) [23]. In our study, we found that the median time from stroke onset to the occurrence of SAP was 4 days (IQR: 2–6), consistent with the previous study [23], and the median time to occurrence of DVT was 8 days (IQR: 5–12). Furthermore, the time to development of SAP was significantly earlier than that of DVT. To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare the temporal profile of SAP and DVT across all stroke subtypes.

These results are consistent with our hypothesis that SAP might be associated with the development of DVT. Several pathophysiological mechanisms may explain this association. The immune response to acute cerebral ischemia is a major factor in stroke pathobiology [24, 25], and immune imbalance might affect the development of ICH [26]. The severity of SAH, combined with the clinical course, is strongly associated with the duration of immunodepression (a persistent suppression of cellular immune response and impaired pro-inflammatory cytokine release by monocytes and lymphocytes) and occurrence of SAP [27, 28]. Using two distinct models of acute middle cerebral artery occlusion, acute brain ischemia has been shown to induce a local neuroinflammatory reaction and altered peripheral immune homeostasis simultaneously [29]. Central nervous system injury leads to secondary immunodeficiency, central nervous system injury-induced immunodepression, and infection [30]. However, the link between SAP and DVT likely extends beyond simple immune dysfunction to a complex “infection-inflammation-coagulation” axis. Systemic inflammation triggered by SAP may act as a driver of a prothrombotic state. Pro-inflammatory cytokines released during pneumonia, such as CRP and TNF-α, are known to induce the expression of tissue factor on monocytes and vascular endothelial cells, thereby initiating the extrinsic coagulation cascade [31, 32]. Furthermore, intense systemic inflammation can lead to endothelial activation and the release of neutrophil extracellular traps, which provide a scaffold for platelet aggregation and fibrin deposition, potentially increasing the risk of DVT in immobilized stroke patients [33]. This additional inflammatory burden from SAP, superimposed on the poststroke hypercoagulable state, provides a biological rationale for our observation that SAP often precedes or coincides with the development of DVT. In the present study, the complete-case analysis showed that both baseline inflammatory markers attenuated the SAP-DVT association, with hs-CRP showing a more pronounced effect compared to NLR. This suggests that systemic inflammation, as reflected by hs-CRP, may play a more substantial role in explaining the observed association than immune cell imbalance alone. Notably, multiple imputation for hs-CRP (15% missing) yielded an adjusted HR of 4.90, which differed from the complete-case estimate (HR = 2.84). This inconsistency highlights the uncertainty introduced by missing data. Nonetheless, both methods confirmed that SAP remained significantly associated with DVT after hs-CRP adjustment, indicating that SAP exerts an independent effect on DVT risk beyond baseline inflammatory status. It is important to note that NLR and hs-CRP were measured at admission, before the onset of SAP. Therefore, the sensitivity analyses adjusting for these markers should be interpreted as an assessment of baseline inflammatory status rather than as evidence of a mediating pathway from SAP to DVT. Given the uncertainty due to missing hs-CRP data, these findings warrant cautious interpretation, and future studies with complete biomarker data are needed to confirm the role of hs-CRP and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Our study has several implications. Typically, various medical complications following a stroke appear to be independent of each other and are managed separately. Our findings, however, reveal a significant temporal and associative link between SAP and DVT, suggesting that these complications may not occur in isolation. Although effective strategies to prevent SAP remain limited [34–36], our results highlight that patients who develop SAP represent a high-risk population for subsequent DVT. These observations are hypothesis-generating and suggest that future studies should evaluate whether targeted thromboprophylaxis in this subgroup could reduce the burden of DVT.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the potential for surveillance bias cannot be excluded. In this study, DVT diagnosis was based on clinical suspicion followed by Doppler confirmation rather than systematic screening. Patients who develop SAP are often more severely ill and may undergo more intensive monitoring, which could increase the likelihood of DVT detection. While we adjusted for baseline NIHSS score as a proxy for stroke severity and clinical complexity, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., intensity of nursing care, frequency of clinical assessments) may remain. Thus, the observed association between SAP and DVT may partly reflect differential diagnostic intensity.

Second, the onset times for SAP and DVT were defined based on clinical diagnosis dates rather than the actual time of symptom onset. This distinction is particularly relevant for DVT, which may remain clinically silent for several days before detection, potentially introducing misclassification of the true temporal sequence. We acknowledge this limitation and interpret the observed temporal relationship with appropriate caution. Additionally, we did not track the incidence of complications after discharge; however, existing literature indicates that the highest risk window for SAP and DVT is within the first 2 weeks poststroke [23, 37], and our study captured the median onset of SAP at 4 days during the primary hospitalization, suggesting that we likely captured the majority of clinical events.

Third, residual confounding from unmeasured factors may persist. The iMCAS registry did not systematically record information on immobilization status, ICU admission, dysphagia, or early mobilization protocols – all of which may influence the risk of both SAP and DVT. Nonetheless, baseline NIHSS score, a strong proxy for stroke severity, was adjusted for in all models and is known to correlate closely with immobility, swallowing dysfunction, and the need for intensive care. Thus, adjustment for NIHSS likely mitigates a substantial portion of the confounding attributable to these unmeasured factors.

Fourth, the generalizability of our findings is constrained by the study design and population selection. This was a single-center study, and data were collected between 2014 and 2016. Stroke management strategies (e.g., early mobilization, thromboprophylaxis protocols) have since evolved, which may limit applicability to current practice. Moreover, the inclusion criterion of onset-to-admission time <7 days may have introduced selection bias by overrepresenting patients with more severe strokes, potentially leading to higher reported incidence rates of SAP and DVT than in the general stroke population. However, the core findings – the association between SAP and DVT and the temporal sequence (SAP preceding DVT) – remained consistent across stroke severity strata (NIHSS ≤12 vs. >12), suggesting robustness to this selection bias. Multicenter studies using more recent, unselected populations are warranted to validate our results.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that SAP is significantly associated with subsequent DVT in stroke patients, with a consistent temporal sequence in which SAP precedes DVT. Although effective strategies to prevent SAP remain limited, our findings identify patients who develop SAP as a high-risk group for DVT. These observations are hypothesis-generating and suggest that further research is warranted to evaluate whether targeted thromboprophylaxis in this population may reduce DVT risk.
