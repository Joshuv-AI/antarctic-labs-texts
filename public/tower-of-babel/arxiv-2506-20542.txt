The Phases of Chaos


Abstract
We develop a novel physical picture to understand certain universal properties of the GUE matrix model which are typically ascribed to quantum chaos, i.e. the ramp and the plateau. We argue that these features should instead be associated with a pattern of spontaneous (or weak explicit) symmetry breaking. In this language, the GUE matrix model corresponds to an effective theory that describes the symmetry-broken phase, and where the Hermitian matrix of the GUE should be understood as a massive σ \sigma field. The physics of this symmetry-broken phase governs certain particular features of the ramp such as its length and shape. However, the simple existence of a ramp is more universal and phase independent; it is related to sum rules obeyed by a large class of matrix models that constrain the interpolation to the plateau regime. Finally, the plateau is controlled by the symmetry-restored phase, which we call confined chaos .

A clear separation between these phases only happens in the large volume limit, where a phase transition becomes possible. In practice, all we really need is a large parameter available in our theory that quantifies the size of configuration space. So, even at finite volume, a large
N

L

It is important to remember that when our observables are made to scale with this large parameter, starting from the symmetry broken phase, it is possible to probe the physics of symmetry restoration. In this sense one would expect to see a transition from a perimeter- to an area-law when the size of

What we are advocating for is that the transition between power-law behavior and exponential decay in
t\lambda
is evidence for a transition from a symmetry-broken to a symmetry-restored phase. In this and further examples we see that
L
acts as the volume scale. In this interpretation, once our dimensionless parameter
t\lambda
begins to probe the volume scale, the theory has explored the entire phase space and there are no longer any effective degrees of freedom at play. In this case, emergent degeneracies are lifted and whatever symmetry was broken must be restored.

But what symmetry could the operator
Z(\beta)
be charged under? From the perspective of the GUE, this is not obvious. In what follows, we will argue that the GUE (and its perturbative deformations) should be understood as an effective theory for a symmetry broken phase. Here there is an important distinction between matrix models and
d\neq 0
quantum field theories. In the latter case we would expect the low energy physics to be controlled by a Goldstone massless boson and a massive field
\sigma
related to radial excitations.
3
3

3

This is the common nomenclature when studying symmetry breaking in linear
\sigma
-models.
Concretely, a simple
U(1)
example can be described by the following action:

S_{U(1)}=\int\text{d}^{d}x\,\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left|\partial\phi\right|^{2}+V\left(|\phi|^{2}\right)\right\}=\int\text{d}^{d}x\,\left\{\frac{e^{2\sigma}}{2}\left[(\partial\varphi)^{2}+(\partial\sigma)^{2}\right]+V\left(e^{2\sigma}\right)\right\}\penalty\ ,

(1.8)

where we have parameterized
\phi\equiv e^{i\varphi+\sigma}
. Near the minimum of
V
, the
\sigma
field describes a massive excitation whereas
\varphi
corresponds to a massless Goldstone boson.
Evidence of the original
U(1)
symmetry has been washed away once we flow to the effective theory where the symmetry acts non-linearly. In a matrix model (corresponding to
d=0
above), there is no spatial modulation of the fields and thus there is little functional difference between a spontaneously-broken global symmetry and a gauge symmetry. What we mean by this is the following: for any compact symmetry we can explicitly integrate over the degenerate vacua of the theory, leaving no light degrees of freedom behind. This means that the massive
\sigma
-field describes the full effective theory in a matrix model.

Hence we advocate for interpreting the GUE matrix model as the effective
\sigma
-field (
H
in the GUE) theory. As there is no Goldstone, it is not clear how to identify the underlying symmetry from within the GUE theory. But we do seem to get an important hint: the exponential decay of
\langle Z(it)\rangle_{\rm GUE}
signals that there
exists
a symmetry preserving phase, without revealing what symmetry is being restored. What happens concretely is that, at large
t\lambda
the potential term
\text{Tr}\,H^{2}
in the GUE action mainly plays the role of an IR regulator which suppresses large
H
excursions in the matrix integral. But most of the physics is not sensitive to this IR regulator. A useful analogy is that of a quantum mechanical system in the WKB approximation. At large energies, all wave-functions become plane waves with small, potential-dependent, modulations; the potential acts as an IR regulator that determines the spread of this wave-function. From this perspective we can say that at
infinite

t\lambda
, there exists a
U(1)
symmetry which acts by translation:
H\rightarrow H+a
, with
a
a constant, that is restored. However, at large but finite
t\lambda
, there are exponential tails in the eigenvalue distributions that weakly break the symmetry through the IR regulating scale
\lambda
.

In order to proceed, we need a UV theory, the analog of
S_{U(1)}
—the linear complex boson theory above in (
1.8
)—which preferably has a continuous symmetry that can be spontaneously broken, as well as a remaining massive
\sigma
field. We will demand that the symmetry at stake be compact. This will ensure IR finiteness of the system without invoking the scale
\lambda
, meaning the symmetry can be realized exactly. Whether this happens in practice as opposed to situations where the symmetry is weakly broken will not be crucial for the arguments below. The main difference between these cases is the explicit form of the exponential approach to the symmetry restored phase.

Once we have our UV theory, we would like to show that it reduces to the GUE in the symmetry broken phase. If the GUE describes the universal physics of a phase that what we call (deconfined)
quantum chaos
, then the symmetry preserved phase represents an
altogether different
effective theory which we henceforth call
confined chaos
. We will study this physics in the remainder of this work and show that universal features like the ramp or the plateau usually discussed in the context of universal quantum systems can be associated to the different possible phases of chaos that these systems can manifest.

2
General rules for single-trace Hermitian matrix models

Before getting into details about the specific UV theory we will consider, we first need to set a few expectations. We refer the reader to appendix
A
for a self-contained (for our purposes) review of single-trace Hermitian matrix models and their exact solutions via the method of orthogonal polynomials. In this section we use these known results to present certain behaviors that must be obeyed by the expectation values of interest to us, in any theory. We will call these behaviors
sum rules
. These rules are theory-independent and rely on the finiteness of these models and the fact that we can compute integrals over a finite number of degrees of freedom.

2.1
Sum rule for the one-point function

In a general Hermitian matrix model, the one-point function of
Z(it)\equiv\text{Tr}\,e^{-iHt}
must satisfy certain constraints. The most obvious of which is that

\langle Z(0)\rangle_{\rm MM}=\langle\text{Tr}\,\mathds{1}\rangle_{\rm MM}=L\penalty\ ,

(2.1)

since this quantity computes the dimension of the Hilbert space of an
L\times L
Hamiltonian. As reviewed in appendix
A
, for a generic matrix ensemble defined by a particular potential
V(H)

\langle\cdot\rangle_{\rm MM}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\int[\text{d}H]\,(\cdot)e^{-\text{Tr}\,V(H)}\penalty\ ,

(2.2)

the expectation value of
Z(it)
must take the following general form

\langle Z(it)\rangle_{\rm MM}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}E\,e^{-iEt}\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\psi_{j}(E)^{2}\penalty\ .

(2.3)

The quantities
\psi_{j}(E)
are called the ‘wavefunctions’ of the ensemble. These wavefunctions are related to the orthogonal polynomials
P_{j}
of the matrix ensemble as follows (see (
A.9
), (
A.12
), and (
A.25
) for definitions):

\psi_{j}(E)\equiv\frac{P_{j}(E)}{\sqrt{h_{j}}}e^{-\frac{V(E)}{2}}\penalty\ ,

(2.4)

and are orthonormal:

\int\text{d}E\,\psi_{i}(E)\psi_{j}(E)=\delta_{ij}\penalty\ .

(2.5)

We will shortly be interested in taking a large-
L
limit, so let us define
V(E)\equiv L\,v(E)
and let us moreover assume that our ensemble is such that the polynomial coefficients in the rescaled potential
v(E)
are all
\mathcal{O}(1)
. Then at late times,
t\sim L
, let us define
t\equiv L\tilde{t}
, and write

\langle Z(it)\rangle_{\rm MM}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}E\,e^{-L(iE\tilde{t}+v(E))}\times(\text{polynomial in $E$})\penalty\ .

(2.6)

We can approximate this integral using the saddle point method. In this regime, the integral is dominated by a complex energy
E^{*}
which satisfies:

v^{\prime}(E^{*})=-i\tilde{t}\penalty\ .

(2.7)

Thus at late times we find:

\langle Z\left(iL\tilde{t}\right)\rangle_{\rm MM}\propto e^{-L(iE^{*}\tilde{t}+v(E^{*}))}=e^{-L(v(E^{*})-E^{*}v^{\prime}(E^{*}))}\penalty\ .

(2.8)

If the quantity
(v(E^{*})-E^{*}v^{\prime}(E^{*}))
has a positive real part, we note that this expectation value becomes exponentially small at late times. For example, taking
v(E)
to be dominated by its Gaussian piece, that is
v(E)=\frac{E^{2}}{2\lambda^{2}}+\text{subleading}\dots
, we find:

\langle Z\left(it\right)\rangle_{\rm MM}\underset{t\sim L}{\propto}e^{-\frac{(t\lambda)^{2}}{2L}+\text{subleading}\dots}\penalty\

(2.9)

where we have reinstated
t\sim\mathcal{O}(L)
in favor of
\tilde{t}
. Hence we have derived a particular general expection for the behavior of
\langle Z(it)\rangle_{\rm MM}
: it must start at
L
at
t=0
and eventually decay to zero, for a general class of Hermitian matrix models. While this rule does not tell us anything about the functional form of
\langle Z(it)\rangle_{\rm MM}
save for its early- and late-time asymptotic values, we can distinguish
qualitatively
between different matrix models by how quickly or slowly this transition from
L
to zero happens.

In general, we can even estimate how long this interpolating region is. If we consider imaginary time
t=i\beta
we can see that the result above comes from a saddle dominated by energies
E^{*}=-\frac{\beta\lambda}{L}\lambda
. On the other hand the calculation can only be valid when it is this exponentially suppressed region of the eigenvalue distribution that dominates. For energies in the range
E\in[-2\lambda,2\lambda]
it is well known that the large
L
behavior is controlled by the Wigner semi-circle law, see appendix (
B.2.1
). Putting these results together we expect the exponentially suppressed regime to kick in at
\beta\lambda\sim 2L
, in agreement with the discussion in the introduction.

How to interpret this result? One could write the exponential decay above in a suggestive fashion:

\langle Z\left(it\right)\rangle_{\rm MM}\underset{t\sim L}{\propto}e^{-t\Lambda_{IR}(t)}

(2.10)

where
\Lambda_{IR}
is a
\mathcal{O}(L^{0})
(time dependent) IR scale. Looking at this we are tempted to interpret the exponential decay as a sort of symmetry restored phase emerging where we can shift
H\rightarrow H+a
, where
a
is a constant. The specific time dependence of
\Lambda_{IR}
captures the fact that the potential actually weakly breaks the symmetry by exponentially small effects.

2.2
Sum rule for the spectral form factor

Here we derive a similar sum rule for for the connected two-point function and particularly the spectral form factor. The spectral form factor is generally defined as follows (see e.g.
[
16
]
for a standard reference):

\text{S}(t)\equiv\langle Z(it)Z(-it)\rangle_{\rm MM}

(2.11)

although we will sometimes use it to mean simply the
connected part
of the spectral form factor,
\text{S}^{c}(t)\equiv\langle Z(it)Z(-it)\rangle_{\rm MM}^{c}
.

The method of orthogonal polynomials allows us to write the following general formula for the connected two-point function in a general class of matrix models (see equations (
A.38
) and (
A.36
)):

\langle Z(\beta_{1})Z(\beta_{2})\rangle^{c}_{\rm MM}=-\sum_{j,k=0}^{L-1}\int\text{d}E_{1}\text{d}E_{2}\,e^{-\beta_{1}E_{1}-\beta_{2}E_{2}}\psi_{j}(E_{1})\psi_{j}(E_{2})\psi_{k}(E_{1})\psi_{k}(E_{2})\penalty\ ,

(2.12)

The orthonormality relation (
2.5
) immediately implies that, as in (
2.1
):

\text{S}^{c}(0)=\langle Z(0)Z(0)\rangle^{c}_{\rm MM}=-L\penalty\ .

(2.13)

Moreover, by following the same steps that led to (
2.9
), then we can similarly conclude that, at late times the connected SFF goes as:

\text{S}^{c}(t)\underset{t\sim L}{\propto}-e^{-2(V(E^{*})-E^{*}V^{\prime}(E^{*}))}\penalty\ ,

(2.14)

and thus is exponentially small if
V(E^{*})-E^{*}V^{\prime}(E^{*})
has a positive real part, as before.
For ensembles governed by GUE universality, that is, if the matrix ensemble is such that
V(E)=L\frac{E^{2}}{2\lambda^{2}}+\text{subleading}\dots
, this means
\text{S}^{c}(t)\propto-e^{-\frac{(t\lambda)^{2}}{L}+\text{subleading}\dots}
at late times. This late behavior is called the
plateau
.

Once again is is tempting to write this as:

\text{S}^{c}(t)\underset{t\sim L}{\propto}-e^{-2t\Lambda_{IR}(t)}\penalty\ ,

(2.15)

which is the familiar behavior we expect for correlators of charged operators in a theory that linearly realizes the symmetry these operators are charged under, and where
\Lambda_{IR}(t)
takes the place of the mass of the relevant degree of freedom.

Hence, it is a generic expectation in a large class of matrix models that the (connected) SFF interpolate between
-L
and zero in a time proportional to
L
. This interpolating behavior is often called the
ramp
. In essence, we are concluding that ramps are generic in a large class of matrix models,
but their shapes are not
. The initial shape of the ramp and the time required to reach the plateau depend crucially on the realized symmetries of the model.

In the next section we will introduce a particular UV model that will exhibit the physics that we’ve so far been alluding to. The symmetry of interest will be realized exactly but can be spontaneously broken. In this UV model the ‘partition function’ operator
Z(it)
is naturally charged under a continuous
U(1)
symmetry. What we will show is that, while the sum rules we derived above are generic, the shapes of the various correlation functions, will depend in important ways on whether this
U(1)
symmetry is preserved or spontaneously broken. For the two-point function in particular, we will show that, while the existence of a ramp is generic across a large class of matrix models, the
shape of the ramp
—how the sum rule and the eventual approach to the plateau is fulfilled—depends heavily on the ensemble of interest and whether the underlying
U(1)
symmetry is preserved or broken.

3
Unitary matrix models

We will now discuss
unitary
matrix models. Unitary matrix models were first introduced by Dyson
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
and are sometimes referred to as the “circular ensembles”
[
21
]
. Like in the GUE example (
1.5
), we will define expectation values in a general unitary matrix model as follows:

\langle\cdot\rangle_{\rm UMM}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\int[\text{d}U]\,(\cdot)\exp\left\{-V(U)\right\}\penalty\

(3.1)

where now
U
is an
L\times L
unitary matrix, meaning its eigenvalues can be expressed as
\lambda_{i}=e^{i\theta_{i}}
with
-\pi\leq\theta_{i}\leq\pi
,
[\text{d}U]
is the standard Haar measure, and
\mathcal{N}
is a normalization that ensures
\langle 1\rangle_{\rm UMM}=1
.

A unitary matrix
U
satisfies

UU^{\dagger}=\mathds{1}\penalty\ ,

(3.2)

and this condition is invariant under

U\rightarrow e^{i\varphi}U\penalty\ ,\qquad\qquad\varphi\in(0,2\pi)\penalty\ .

(3.3)

We would like to build our effective theory around this
U(1)
symmetry, and to explore the consequences of its spontaneous breaking. To start, let us discuss how to build matrix potentials
V(U)
that are invariant under this
U(1)
symmetry (see e.g. section 3 of
[
22
]
).

Naively, one might imagine starting with a single-trace term, for example
\text{Tr}U+\text{Tr}U^{\dagger}
or perhaps
\text{Tr}U^{2}+\text{Tr}U^{-2}
as in the GUE,
4
4

4

We add the complex conjugates so that our potentials are real.
but neither choice is invariant under the
U(1)
symmetry of interest, so do not suit our purposes. We might also be compelled to consider a term such as
\text{Tr}\left(UU^{\dagger}\right)
, but this is obviously trivial since
\text{Tr}\left(UU^{\dagger}\right)=\text{Tr}\,\mathds{1}=L
. Instead, the basic building blocks for such an invariant
V(U)
turn out to be powers of multi-trace objects:

\left(\text{Tr}U^{n_{1}}\text{Tr}U^{n_{2}}\dots\text{Tr}U^{n_{k}}\right)^{t_{k}}\penalty\ ,\quad\quad\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{k}=0\penalty\ ,\quad\quad t_{k}>0\penalty\ .

(3.4)

Thus the lowest-order non-trivial
U(1)
-symmetric potential is of the following form:

V(U)=-a\text{Tr}U\text{Tr}\,U^{\dagger}\penalty\ ,\qquad\qquad a>0\penalty\ .

(3.5)

Note our choice of sign for this potential, as it will be important in what follows. Unlike the Hermitian matrix models described earlier, this is a double trace potential, and is negative semi-definite. As we will show, this model has all the features of symmetry breaking that we seek. This will be our UV theory. We call this particular unitary matrix model, with the potential given by (
3.5
), the
Yang-Mills Matrix Model
. We will explain the origin of this name further below. We denote expectation values in this model as follows:

\langle\cdot\rangle_{\rm YM}=\frac{1}{Z(a)}\int[\text{d}U]\,(\cdot)\exp\left\{a\text{Tr}U\text{Tr}\,U^{\dagger}\right\}\penalty\ .

(3.6)

To get some intuition on the potential (
3.5
), let us study the case
L=2
, for which:

V_{L=2}=-4a\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}}{2}\right)\penalty\ .

(3.7)

In the
U(1)
preserving phase, by definition, we must have:

\left\langle\text{Tr}\,U^{k}\right\rangle_{\rm YM}=0\penalty\ ,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}\penalty\ .

(3.8)

Let us consider what this means for
k=1
. If
\left\langle\text{Tr}U\right\rangle_{\rm YM}=0
then

\left\langle\text{Tr}\,U\right\rangle_{\rm YM}=\left\langle e^{i\theta_{1}}+e^{i\theta_{2}}\right\rangle=\left\langle e^{i\theta_{2}}\left(1+e^{i(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})}\right)\right\rangle=0\quad\implies\quad\left\langle\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\right\rangle\approx\pi\penalty\ .

(3.9)

Plugging this into the the potential above, we notice that this corresponds to a local
maximum
(see figure
2
). Hence, we have chosen the sign of our potential in (
3.5
) such that it is energetically disfavored to preserve the
U(1)
symmetry. In other words, the chosen sign of the potential favors symmetry breaking, with the coupling constant
a

3.2

3.3.1
Summary

Before explaining how we derive these results, let us briefly summarize the phase structure of the model defined in (

The result of a simple large-

3.3.2
Derivation of the Large-
L
free energy

Now let us explain how we derive this result. We start with the GWW partition function, which we have labeled as

in (

\mathcal{N}_{\rm GWW}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{L}\frac{\text{d}\theta_{i}}{2\pi}\right]\,\left|\Delta\left(e^{i\theta}\right)\right|^{2}\exp\left\{Lg\sum_{i=1}^{L}\cos\theta_{i}\right\}\penalty\ ,

where

\Delta\left(e^{i\theta}\right)\equiv\prod_{k<j}^{L}\left(e^{i\theta_{j}}-e^{i\theta_{k}}\right)=\text{det}\left[e^{i(j-1)\theta_{k}}\right]\penalty\

(3.25)

is the standard Vandermonde determinant. We may bring the Vandermonde determinant into to the exponent as follows:

\mathcal{N}_{\rm GWW}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{L}\frac{\text{d}\theta_{i}}{2\pi}\right]\,\exp\left\{Lg\sum_{i=1}^{L}\cos\theta_{i}+\sum_{i<j}^{L}\log\left[4{}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}}{2}\right)\right]\right\}\penalty\ ,

(3.26)

which will allow us to perform a Coulomb-gas analysis (see

First let us introduce the “equilibrium” eigenvalue density:

\rho_{\rm eq}(\theta)\equiv\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\delta(\theta-\theta_{i})\penalty\ .

(3.27)

To establish some nomenclature: We will use the term

to denote quantities whose limits exist

The equilibrium density allows us to replace the sums with integrals in the following way:

Lg\sum_{i=1}^{L}\cos\theta_{i}\rightarrow L^{2}g\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\theta}{2\pi}\rho_{\rm eq}(\theta)\cos\theta

(3.28)

and

\sum_{i<j}^{L}\log\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}}{2}\right)\right]\rightarrow\frac{L^{2}}{2}\fint_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\theta}{2\pi}\fint_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\gamma}{2\pi}\,\rho_{\rm eq}(\theta)\rho_{\rm eq}(\gamma)\log\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\theta-\gamma}{2}\right)\right]\penalty\ ,

\mathcal{N}_{\rm GWW}\approx L!\int[D\rho_{\rm eq}(\theta)]\text{d}\xi\,e^{L^{2}F_{\rm eq}[\rho_{\rm eq}]}\penalty\ ,

(3.30)

where the

\displaystyle F_{\rm eq}[\rho_{\rm eq}]\equiv

\displaystyle\penalty\ g\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\theta}{2\pi}\rho_{\rm eq}(\theta)\cos\theta+\frac{1}{2}\fint_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\theta}{2\pi}\fint_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\gamma}{2\pi}\,\rho_{\rm eq}(\theta)\rho_{\rm eq}(\gamma)\log\left[4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\theta-\gamma}{2}\right)\right]

We now proceed to evaluate (

\left\langle\rho_{\rm eq}(\theta)\right\rangle_{\rm GWW}=\begin{cases}1+g\cos\theta\penalty\ ,&g<1\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ |\theta|\leq\pi\\

Since we are taking

3.4
Exact observables in the Haar random model

We have established that the phases of the model are controlled by the GWW model on either side of the transition. For
a<1

The simplicity boils down to the fact that, in this ensemble, we have exact expressions for the one and two-point densities

\langle\rho(\theta)\rangle_{\rm Haar}=L\penalty\ ,\qquad\langle\rho(\theta)\rho(\phi)\rangle_{\rm Haar}^{c}=-\left[\frac{\sin\left(L\frac{\theta-\phi}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\phi}{2}\right)}\right]^{2}\penalty\ .

(3.45)

A simple computation gives

\left\langle\text{Tr}U^{k}\right\rangle_{\rm Haar}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\theta}{2\pi}e^{ik\theta}\langle\rho(\theta)\rangle_{\rm Haar}=L\,\delta_{k0}\penalty\ ,

(3.46)

revealing a very sharp example of the sum rule described generally in section

For the two-point function we find:

\displaystyle\text{S}_{\rm Haar}^{c}(k)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\text{Tr}U^{k}\text{Tr}U^{-k}\right\rangle^{c}_{\rm Haar}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\phi}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\theta}{2\pi}e^{ik(\theta-\phi)}\langle\rho(\theta)\rho(\phi)\rangle^{c}_{\rm Haar}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-L+k&k\leq L\\
0&k>L\end{cases}\penalty\ ,

(3.47)

revealing an exact linear ramp. This again obeys the sum rule described in section
2.2
, where this two-point function goes to zero in exactly

steps. One important observation of this paper is that, the
shape
of this ramp will change in the symmetry broken phase. Precisely, the ramp in the GUE phase is
not

. This changes drastically both in the GUE and the symmetry broken phase of this model, which agree with each other, as we will show.

Also notice that there is no exponential behavior for

—the ramp simply ends. This is a consequence of the preserved symmetry in this phase. The effective potential is exactly flat and once different eigenvalues decorrelate, the spectral form factor is exactly zero. In other words there is no
\Lambda_{IR}

We will adapt a calculation in

Let us begin by considering the following integral

A^{f,g}\equiv\int[\text{d}U]\,\text{det}[f(U)]\,\text{det}[g(U)]\exp\left\{\frac{Lg}{2}\text{Tr}\left(U+U^{\dagger}\right)\right\}\penalty\ ,

(3.48)

where, for now,

A^{f,g}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left|\Delta\left(e^{i\theta}\right)\right|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{L}\frac{\text{d}\theta_{j}}{2\pi}\,e^{Lg\cos\theta_{j}}f\left(e^{i\theta_{j}}\right)g\left(e^{i\theta_{j}}\right)\penalty\ .

(3.49)

Now, recall that the Vandermonde can be expressed as follows:

\Delta\left(e^{i\theta}\right)=\text{det}\left[e^{i(j-1)\theta_{k}}\right]=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{L}}(-1)^{\sigma}\prod_{k=1}^{L}e^{i(\sigma_{k}-1)\theta_{k}}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{L}}(-1)^{\sigma}\prod_{k=1}^{L}e^{i(k-1)\theta_{\sigma_{k}}}\penalty\ ,

(3.50)

where

is the permutation group of

Since the Vandermonde determinant is squared, plugging in the above expression leads to two separate sums over
S_{L}
. However, we can exploit the fact that the integrand is permutation symmetric to collapse the double sum into a single sum, at the expense of a factor of
L!
. Thus we are tasked with evaluating:

A^{f,g}=L!\sum_{\sigma\in S_{L}}(-1)^{\sigma}\prod_{j=1}^{L}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\theta_{j}}{2\pi}\,e^{i\left(\sigma_{j}-j\right)\theta_{j}}e^{Lg\cos\theta_{j}}f\left(e^{i\theta_{j}}\right)g\left(e^{i\theta_{j}}\right)\penalty\ .

(3.51)

If we denote by

A_{k}^{f,g}\equiv\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\theta}{2\pi}\,e^{ik\theta}e^{Lg\cos\theta}f\left(e^{i\theta}\right)g\left(e^{i\theta}\right)\penalty\ ,

(3.52)

then we see that the expression (

Using (

Let us, for a moment, step back and make a particular observation about the GUE. The GUE is an ensemble of random Hermitian Hamiltonians where the distribution favors states with energies near

Discussion:

In figure
5

11

It is certainly possible to alter the

(see equations (

[

and discussed for unitary matrix models in
[

In figure
6
we showed that, once we reach volume scale
L
, the exponential approach to symmetry restoration distinguishes between the various possible ensembles, meaning the approach depends sensitively on the exact form of the potential. Starting in the preserved phase

, the expectation values are completely different, we find
\left\langle\text{Tr}U^{k}\right\rangle_{\rm YM}=0
for all

.

Let us tie this observation to holography. We typically understand one-point functions, such as
\langle Z(it)\rangle_{\rm GUE}
or
\left\langle\text{Tr}\,U^{k}\right\rangle_{\rm YM}
as arising holographically from a low-dimensional gravity path integral with certain boundary conditions. What we would like to advocate for here is that
the geometric description only makes sense in such a symmetry broken phase
. Of course, since expectation values vanish in the preserving phase, there can be no geometric picture. More to the point: the exit from the preserving phase, diagnosed by the exponential parts of the plots in figures
5
and
6
, are sensitive to the UV completion of the model. Here we see how the GUE and YM results differ in important ways, while agreeing in the ‘geometric’ part of the curve.

Now that we have this out of the way, we will continue and analyze the spectral form factor using the same logic. Here we will also uncover interesting results.

4
Conclusions

In this work we have presented a novel perspective on the physics of random matrix models and their quantum-chaotic properties. It is often said that the GUE captures several universal features of quantum chaos, but in our view, the origin and implications of this universality are not often highlighted, particularly for the observables we care about. We have advocated that the GUE is the universal effective action describing the symmetry-broken phase of a system enjoying a global
U(1)
symmetry. It matters little for our results if this symmetry is actually present in the UV or if it is emergent. Even if we think of the GUE matrix model as descending from a UV theory with explicit symmetry breaking, it remains true that for observables that probe very large times, i.e.
t\lambda\gg 1
, the
U(1)
symmetry is emergent since the matrix model potential acts simply as an IR regulator, and hence contributes only to exponentially small effects in this regime. As a consequence, the plateau indeed corresponds to symmetry restoration.

We conclude from the above analysis that there exist (at least) two phases of quantum chaos that describe
different
universal behaviors. The first is the usual (deconfined) quantum chaos described by the GUE as an effective model. This phase is responsible for an extended ramp and a weak non-linear dependence on time during the ramp. The other phase is the
confined chaos phase
, which controls the physics of the plateau.

Let us quickly summarize some of the results presented above and interpret them in the context of quantum mechanics:

1.

Sum rules: once a system is described by a finite (but large) matrix model of size
L
some general features are universal and related to the finiteness of configuration space. Observables involving
Z(it)
must be of order
L
as
t\rightarrow 0
as they encode the size of this space. Furthermore, if the potential has a typical scale
\lambda
, at
\lambda t\sim\alpha L
(for some
\alpha
of
\mathcal{O}(1)
) the integral over matrices is controlled by a saddle point approximation and observables become exponentially suppressed in
L
. This gives a universal ramp, and observables must go from being order
L
to exponentially suppressed within times of order
L
. The precise length and shape of the ramp depends on the particular phase that the model describes.

2.

Once we consider times
t\lambda\gg L
we enter a symmetry restored phase where the form of the potential contributes only exponentially small effects and there is a (possibly emergent)
U(1)
symmetry.

3.

When the symmetry is unbroken the system becomes Haar random. One-point functions vanish exactly and two-point functions show linear “short” ramps that reach
0
at finite discrete time
L
. This phase corresponds to
confined chaos
.

4.

If the symmetry is spontaneously broken the system has an effective description in terms of a Hermitian matrix model. When this theory is weakly coupled it can be described by the GUE ensemble. In that case the ramp becomes parametrically longer than in the symmetry preserved phase (when
g_{*}\gg 1
). Therefore, long ramps are controlled by a
deconfined
quantum chaos phase. The end of the ramp is nothing other than the restoration of the symmetry at late times. While the form of the exponential decay is not universal (as it depends on the effective
\Lambda_{IR}
scale), the existence of the plateau is controlled by the
confined
phase.

From the point of view of reproducing the properties of quantum mechanical chaotic systems there is a very precise way in which we can characterize the physics of the phases described above. An abstract finite Hilbert space of size
L
has a natural
U(L)
gauge symmetry acting on it which corresponds to the conjugation by unitary matrices of all operators in the theory. There is also a
U(1)
symmetry that acts as multiplication by a phase. This symmetry can be thought as global
12
12

12

In a matrix model, the difference between a compact global and gauge symmetry blurs for neutral observables.
as phase differences between quantum states are in principle observable (e.g. through particle statistics and interference experiments). The confined phase discussed before corresponds to an ensemble of quantum gates given by unitary operators acting on this space. We can think of these quantum gates as a form of discrete time evolution. The potential corresponding to the YM model described in this work corresponds to a probability distribution that favours unitary operators that on (Hilbert space) average act just by multiplying states by a phase. Even in this case (for
a<1
) the system can preserve the
U(1)
symmetry because of configuration space effects encoded in the Haar measure which favor unitary operators that are random. When the potential becomes too strong the symmetry breaks and, instead, we end up choosing a unitary operator close the identity with a gaussian measure (for
a\gg 1
) for the effective Hamiltonian associated to the gate. Effectively we have morphed the problem from the choice of a random gate to the choice of a random Hamiltonian. This makes time evolution continuous in the effective description. Here we are in the deconfined phase associated to the GUE universality of quantum chaos. While this picture is satisfying from the quantum system perspective, it remains unexplored in the context of holography in quantum gravity. Interesting connections to the realization of bulk geometries through tensor networks
[
42
]
are possible here.

It remains for us to state that we have yet to develop a fully-systematic characterization, in a Wilsonian sense, of the space of effective theories for matrix models. It would be interesting to formalize this in the future. Sadly, little of our intuition from higher dimensions translates to the realm of matrix models. There is no base space notion of energy scales or an associated Hilbert space. The distinction between (compact) global and gauge symmetries is blurred. The difference between spontaneous and explicit symmetry breaking is more subtle. But the results presented here point to a possible reorganization of the degrees of freedom of matrix models that might prove useful in understanding the various universal behaviors present in different phases of these models. Maybe holography is the appropriate language here. A more complete and systematic understanding could have multiple applications, ranging from the study of quantum chaotic system to models of quantum gravity. As we hinted at in the introduction, it would seem that the very notion of gravity is deeply tied to symmetry breaking as the partition function can only acquire an expectation value in this phase. What, then, is the meaning of the symmetry preserving phase? Is there a non-geometric dual to
confined chaos
? We leave these questions for future research.

Appendix A
Review: Hermitian matrix ensembles with single-trace potentials

In this review section, we review for the reader how to compute exact expectation values in a random hermitian matrix model with an arbitrary single-trace potential. We adapt the techniques found in several reviews on the subject
[
10
,
43
,
44
,
45
,
16
]
.
In a random matrix model (RMT), we consider an
L\times L
random Hermitian matrix
H
and compute expectation values with respect to the the norm:

\langle\cdot\rangle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\int[\text{d}H]\,(\cdot)\exp\left\{-\text{Tr}\,V(H)\right\}\penalty\

(A.1)

Here the normalization constant
\mathcal{N}
is chosen such that
\langle 1\rangle=1
, and
V(H)
is some polynomial in the matrix
H
.
The measure is explicitly:

[\text{d}H]\equiv\prod_{i=1}^{L}\text{d}H_{ii}\prod_{i<j}^{L}\text{d}\text{Re}H_{ij}\,\text{d}\text{Im}H_{ij}

(A.2)

We will focus mainly on the
Gaussian Unitary Ensemble
(GUE), for which the the explicit formula is:

\langle\cdot\rangle_{\rm GUE}=2^{-L/2}\left(\frac{L}{\pi\lambda^{2}}\right)^{L^{2}/2}\int[\text{d}H]\,(\cdot)\exp\left\{-\frac{L}{2\lambda^{2}}H_{ij}H_{ji}\right\}\penalty\ .

(A.3)

Our main interest will be in computing correlation functions of
loop operators
:

\langle Z(\beta_{1})Z(\beta_{2})\dots Z(\beta_{k})\rangle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\int[\text{d}H]\,\left[\text{Tr}\,e^{-\beta_{1}H}\text{Tr}\,e^{-\beta_{2}H}\dots\text{Tr}\,e^{-\beta_{k}H}\right]e^{-\text{Tr}\,V(H)}\penalty\ .

(A.4)

The loop operator is itself related to the
resolvent operator
via a Laplace transform:

R(E)=\text{Tr}\frac{1}{E-H}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{d}\beta\,Z(\beta)\,e^{\beta E}\penalty\ ,

(A.5)

Which incidentally implies:

Z(\beta)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}\text{d}E\,R(E)\,e^{-\beta E}.

(A.6)

From this definition we naturally see how the density of states giving rise to
\langle Z(\beta)\rangle
can be thought of as arising from the residues of the resolvent, or, in case
\langle R(E)\rangle
develops a branch cut, from the discontinuity across the cut. In any case, this motivates why we should study loop operators, as they contain the exact same information as the resolvent.

A.1
General theory of orthogonal polynomials

Since we will only be interested in objects composed of traces of powers
H
, we can reduce the problem to the eigenvalues. This well known reduction leads to:

\langle\cdot\rangle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\int\left[\prod_{i=1}^{L}\text{d}\lambda_{i}\right]\,\Delta(\lambda)^{2}(\cdot)\exp\left\{-\sum_{i=1}^{L}\,V(\lambda_{i})\right\}\penalty\ ,

(A.7)

where again
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
is chosen such that
\langle 1\rangle=1
and we have introduced the Vandermonde determinant

\Delta(\lambda)\equiv\prod_{i<j}^{L}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i})=\text{det}\,\lambda_{i}^{j-1}\penalty\ .

(A.8)

The exact solution of our problem lies in assuming that
V(\lambda)
is a function that admits a set of orthogonal polynomials as follows
[
10
,
43
,
44
,
45
]
:

\int\text{d}\lambda\,e^{-V(\lambda)}P_{n}(\lambda)P_{m}(\lambda)=h_{n}\,\delta_{nm}\penalty\ .

(A.9)

where we have chosen to
P_{n}
to be normalized such that its highest power of
\lambda
comes with coefficient 1:

P_{n}(\lambda)=\lambda^{n}+c_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1}+\dots+c_{0}\penalty\ .

(A.10)

This definition is convenient, since it allows us to write:

\Delta(\lambda)=\text{det}\,\lambda_{i}^{j-1}=\det P_{j-1}(\lambda_{i})\penalty\ ,

(A.11)

which we can see by starting with the matrix
\lambda_{i}^{j-1}
and constructing a new matrix by adding linear combinations of preceding columns, which leaves the determinant invariant, until we ultimately obtain the matrix
P_{j-1}(\lambda_{i})
. From the definition (
A.9
) we can equivalently define a set of ‘wavefunctions’
\psi_{n}

\psi_{n}(\lambda)\equiv\frac{P_{n}(\lambda)}{\sqrt{h_{n}}}e^{-\frac{V(\lambda)}{2}}

(A.12)

which satisfy:

\int\text{d}\lambda\,\psi_{n}(\lambda)\psi_{m}(\lambda)=\delta_{nm}\penalty\ .

(A.13)

A.1.1
Normalization

Now we can compute the normalization
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
. This quantity can be thought of as the partition function of the random matrix theory. Writing out the determinant:

\det P_{j-1}(\lambda_{i})=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{L}}(-1)^{\sigma}\prod_{i=1}^{L}P_{\sigma_{i}-1}(\lambda_{i})\penalty\ ,

(A.14)

where
S_{L}
is the set of permutations of
L
elements, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int\left[\prod_{i}^{L}\text{d}\lambda_{i}\right]\,\left(\sum_{\sigma\in S_{L}}(-1)^{\sigma}\prod_{i=1}^{L}P_{\sigma_{i}-1}(\lambda_{i})\right)\left(\sum_{\gamma\in S_{L}}(-1)^{\gamma}\prod_{j=1}^{L}P_{\gamma_{j}-1}(\lambda_{j})\right)\exp\left\{-\sum_{k=1}^{L}\,V(\lambda_{k})\right\}\penalty\

(A.15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{L}}\int\left[\prod_{i}^{L}\text{d}\lambda_{i}\right]\,\prod_{i=1}^{L}P_{\sigma_{i}-1}(\lambda_{i})^{2}\exp\left\{-\sum_{k=1}^{L}\,V(\lambda_{k})\right\}\penalty\

(A.16)

\displaystyle=L!\prod_{n=0}^{L-1}h_{n}\penalty\ .

(A.17)

In going from the first line to the second line we used orthogonality, and in going from the second line to the third line we used that all
L!
permutations give the same result.

A.1.2
One-point function

The one point function is defined as follows

\displaystyle\langle Z(\beta)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\int[\text{d}H]\,\text{Tr}\,e^{-\beta H}e^{-\text{Tr}\,V(H)}

(A.18)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\beta)^{n}}{n!}\int[\text{d}H]\,(\text{Tr}H^{n})\,e^{-\text{Tr}\,V(H)}

(A.19)

therefore it suffices to compute
\langle\text{Tr}\,H^{n}\rangle
for arbitrary
n
for us to obtain the full one-point function. To do so, we pass to the eigenvalue language:

\langle\text{Tr}\,H^{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{L!\prod_{s=0}^{L-1}h_{s}}\int\prod_{i}^{L}\text{d}\lambda_{i}\,\Delta(\lambda)^{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{L}\lambda_{j}^{n}\right)\prod_{k=1}^{L}e^{-V(\lambda_{k})}\penalty\ .

(A.20)

By relabeling, we can write this as:

\displaystyle\langle\text{Tr}\,H^{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{L!\prod_{s=0}^{L-1}h_{s}}\int\prod_{i}^{L}\text{d}\lambda_{i}\,\Delta(\lambda)^{2}\lambda_{1}^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{L}e^{-V(\lambda_{k})}

(A.21)

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{L!\prod_{s=0}^{L-1}h_{s}}\int\prod_{i}^{L}\text{d}\lambda_{i}\,\left(\sum_{\sigma\in S_{L}}(-1)^{\sigma}\prod_{i=1}^{L}P_{\sigma_{i}-1}(\lambda_{i})\right)\left(\sum_{\gamma\in S_{L}}(-1)^{\gamma}\prod_{j=1}^{L}P_{\gamma_{j}-1}(\lambda_{j})\right)\lambda_{1}^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{L}e^{-V(\lambda_{k})}\penalty\ .

(A.22)

Now for every
\lambda_{i}
for
i>1
, this proceeds similarly as when we calculated the normalization factor
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
, but now we are only permuting
L-1
elements. This leads to

\displaystyle\langle\text{Tr}\,H^{n}\rangle=\frac{L}{L!\prod_{s=0}^{L-1}h_{s}}(L-1)!\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\frac{\prod_{s=0}^{L-1}h_{s}}{h_{j}}\int\text{d}\lambda_{1}\,P_{j}(\lambda_{1})^{2}\lambda_{1}^{n}e^{-V(\lambda_{1})}

(A.23)

which we may conveniently rewrite as

\langle\text{Tr}\,H^{n}\rangle=\int\text{d}\lambda\,\lambda^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\psi_{j}(\lambda)^{2}\penalty\ .

(A.24)

From this we conclude that

\langle Z(\beta)\rangle=\int\text{d}\lambda\,e^{-\beta\lambda}\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\psi_{j}(\lambda)^{2}

(A.25)

It is tempting to read this equation as defining an expectation value for the density of states of some theory:

\langle Z(\beta)\rangle=\int\text{d}E\,e^{-\beta E}\langle\rho(E)\rangle

(A.26)

where

\langle\rho(E)\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\psi_{j}(E)^{2}\penalty\ .

(A.27)

We will study this expression further in a specific example.

A.1.3
Two-point function

Let us now repeat the analysis of the previous section now for the two point function:

\displaystyle\langle Z(\beta_{1})Z(\beta_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\int[\text{d}H]\,\text{Tr}\,e^{-\beta_{1}H}\text{Tr}\,e^{-\beta_{2}H}e^{-\text{Tr}\,V(H)}

(A.28)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{n,m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\beta_{1})^{n}}{n!}\frac{(-\beta_{2})^{m}}{m!}\int[\text{d}H]\,(\text{Tr}H^{n})(\text{Tr}H^{m})\,e^{-\text{Tr}\,V(H)}\penalty\ ,

(A.29)

meaning it suffices to compute
\langle\text{Tr}\,H^{n}\text{Tr}\,H^{m}\rangle
for arbitrary
n,m
for us to obtain the full two-point function. To do so, we pass to the eigenvalue language:

\langle\text{Tr}\,H^{n}\,\text{Tr}\,H^{m}\rangle=\frac{1}{L!\prod_{s=0}^{L-1}h_{s}}\int\prod_{i}^{L}\text{d}\lambda_{i}\,\Delta(\lambda)^{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{L}\lambda_{j}^{n}\right)\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}\lambda_{l}^{m}\right)\prod_{k=1}^{L}e^{-V(\lambda_{k})}\penalty\ .

(A.30)

Now we have to be careful and treat the
j=l
and
j\neq l
terms separately in the above sum. Thus we have, after a relabeling:

\displaystyle\langle\text{Tr}\,H^{n}\,\text{Tr}\,H^{m}\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{L}{L!\prod_{s=0}^{L-1}h_{s}}\int\prod_{i}^{L}\text{d}\lambda_{i}\,\Delta(\lambda)^{2}\lambda_{1}^{n+m}\prod_{k=1}^{L}e^{-V(\lambda_{k})}

\displaystyle+\frac{L(L-1)}{L!\prod_{s=0}^{L-1}h_{s}}\int\prod_{i}^{L}\text{d}\lambda_{i}\,\Delta(\lambda)^{2}\lambda_{1}^{n}\lambda_{2}^{m}\prod_{k=1}^{L}e^{-V(\lambda_{k})}\penalty\ .

(A.31)

The first line is the same as in the one-point function section, meaning it will give a contribution of
\langle Z(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})\rangle

\langle Z(\beta_{1})Z(\beta_{2})\rangle=\langle Z(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})\rangle+\int\text{d}E_{1}\text{d}E_{2}\,e^{-\beta_{1}E_{1}-\beta_{2}E_{2}}\langle\rho(E_{1})\rho(E_{2})\rangle

where

\langle\rho(E_{1})\rho(E_{2})\rangle=\det\begin{pmatrix}K_{L}(E_{1},E_{1})&K_{L}(E_{1},E_{2})\\
K_{L}(E_{2},E_{1})&K_{L}(E_{2},E_{2})\end{pmatrix}

(A.33)

and

K_{L}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\psi_{j}(x)\psi_{j}(y)\penalty\ .

(A.34)

Let us now expand out (

\langle\rho(E_{1})\rho(E_{2})\rangle=\langle\rho(E_{1})\rangle\langle\rho(E_{2})\rangle+\langle\rho(E_{1})\rho(E_{2})\rangle^{c}

where
\langle\rho(E)\rangle

\langle\rho(E_{1})\rho(E_{2})\rangle^{c}\equiv-\sum_{j,k=0}^{L-1}\psi_{j}(E_{1})\psi_{j}(E_{2})\psi_{k}(E_{1})\psi_{k}(E_{2})

(A.36)

is the connected contribution to the exact density of states. Given this structure, we now identify

\langle Z(\beta_{1})Z(\beta_{2})\rangle=\langle Z(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})\rangle+\langle Z(\beta_{1})\rangle\langle Z(\beta_{1})\rangle+\langle Z(\beta_{1})Z(\beta_{2})\rangle^{c}\penalty\ ,

(A.37)

where

\langle Z(\beta_{1})Z(\beta_{2})\rangle^{c}\equiv\int\text{d}E_{1}\text{d}E_{2}\,e^{-\beta_{1}E_{1}-\beta_{2}E_{2}}\langle\rho(E_{1})\rho(E_{2})\rangle^{c}\penalty\ .

(A.38)

We also collect here a nice fact about orthogonal polynomials, known as the Christoffel-Darboux formula, which states:

K_{L}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\psi_{j}(x)\psi_{j}(y)=\sqrt{\frac{h_{L}}{h_{L-1}}}\left[\frac{\psi_{L}(x)\psi_{L-1}(y)-\psi_{L}(y)\psi_{L-1}(x)}{x-y}\right]

Applying this to the one-point function, we see that

\langle\rho(E)\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\psi_{j}(E)^{2}=\sqrt{\frac{h_{L}}{h_{L-1}}}\left[{\psi_{L}^{\prime}(E)\psi_{L-1}(E)-\psi_{L}(E)\psi_{L-1}^{\prime}(E)}\right]\penalty\ .

(A.40)

Even simpler, the two-point function has the following density:

\langle\rho(E_{1})\rho(E_{2})\rangle^{c}=-{\frac{h_{L}}{h_{L-1}}}\left[\frac{\psi_{L}(E_{1})\psi_{L-1}(E_{2})-\psi_{L}(E_{2})\psi_{L-1}(E_{1})}{E_{1}-E_{2}}\right]^{2}\penalty\ .

(A.41)

A.2
The Loop Equations

Besides orthogonal polynomials, there is another way to attack the problem, which will indicate how the symmetry preservation/breaking occurs. This technique is known as the loop equations. Let us derive them. We begin with the definition of our matrix ensemble (
A.1
) and measure (
A.2
), which we repeat here

\langle\cdot\rangle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\int[\text{d}H]\,\left(\cdot\right)\exp\left\{-\text{Tr}\,V(H)\right\}\penalty\ ,

(A.42)

where

[\text{d}H]\equiv\prod_{i=1}^{L}\text{d}H_{ii}\prod_{i<j}^{L}\text{d}\text{Re}H_{ij}\,\text{d}\text{Im}H_{ij}\penalty\ .

(A.43)

If we have done things correctly, the expectation values defined in this way should be are invariant under arbitrary reparametrizations of the matrix
H\rightarrow f(H)
. Let us now consider
f(H)
infinitesimally close to the identity element:

f(H)=H+\epsilon\,H^{k}\penalty\ .

(A.44)

Under this transformation we find

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\text{Tr}\,V(H)\right\}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\exp\left\{-\text{Tr}\,V(H)\right\}\penalty\ \left[1-\epsilon\text{Tr}\left(V^{\prime}(H)\cdot H^{k}\right)+O\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)\right]\penalty\ ,

(A.45)

\displaystyle[\text{d}H]

\displaystyle\longrightarrow[\text{d}H]\left[1+\epsilon\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\text{Tr}\left(H^{l}\right)\text{Tr}\left(H^{k-1-l}\right)+O\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)\right]\penalty\ .

(A.46)

The final answer should not depend on
\epsilon
, implying the following Ward identity:

\left\langle\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\text{Tr}\left(H^{l}\right)\text{Tr}\left(H^{k-1-l}\right)\right\rangle=\left\langle\text{Tr}\left(V^{\prime}(H)\cdot H^{k}\right)\right\rangle

(A.47)

for any
k
.
Multiplying (
A.47
) by
E^{-k-1}
and summing over
k
, we find:

\left\langle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\frac{\text{Tr}\left(H^{l}\right)}{E^{l+1}}\frac{\text{Tr}\left(H^{k-1-l}\right)}{E^{k-l}}\right\rangle=\left\langle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\text{Tr}\left(V^{\prime}(H)\cdot H^{k}\right)}{E^{k+1}}\right\rangle\penalty\ .

(A.48)

But this is just the power series expansion for the following formal expression:

\left\langle R(E)^{2}\right\rangle=\left\langle\text{Tr}\left[V^{\prime}(H)\cdot(E-H)^{-1}\right]\right\rangle\penalty\ .

(A.49)

Denoting for the moment
V^{\prime}(H)=V^{\prime}(E)+\left(V^{\prime}(H)-V^{\prime}(E)\right)
and defining:

\langle P(E)\rangle\equiv\left\langle\text{Tr}\left\{\left[V^{\prime}(E)-V^{\prime}(H)\right]\cdot(E-H)^{-1}\right\}\right\rangle,

(A.50)

The loop equations simplify to:

\boxed{\left\langle R(E)^{2}\right\rangle=V^{\prime}(E)\left\langle R(E)\right\rangle-\left\langle P(E)\right\rangle}\penalty\ .

(A.51)

Finally, we can inverse Laplace transform the expression (
A.49
) to obtain a constraint on the partition function, rather than on the resolvents. This leads to

\boxed{\int_{0}^{\beta}\text{d}\beta^{\prime}\left\langle Z(\beta^{\prime})Z(\beta-\beta^{\prime})\right\rangle=-V^{\prime}(-\partial_{\beta})\left\langle Z(\beta)\right\rangle}\penalty\ .

(A.52)

Note that these loop equations imply a kind of sewing relation between
n
-point functions and are derived from the infinite reparametrization symmetry of the integral.

Appendix C
Orthogonal polynomials of the GWW model

Recall that we can reduce the GWW model to an integral over the eigenvalues of
U
:
e^{i\theta_{j}}
, for
j=1,\dots,L
. We wish to compute expectation values as follows:

\langle\cdot\rangle_{\rm GWW}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{\rm GWW}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{L}\frac{\text{d}\theta_{i}}{2\pi}\right]\,\left|\Delta\left(e^{i\theta}\right)\right|^{2}(\cdot)\exp\left\{Lg\sum_{i=1}^{L}\cos\theta_{i}\right\}\penalty\ ,

(C.1)

where

\Delta\left(e^{i\theta}\right)\equiv\prod_{k<j}^{L}\left(e^{i\theta_{j}}-e^{i\theta_{k}}\right)=\text{det}\left[e^{i(j-1)\theta_{k}}\right]\penalty\ .

(C.2)

To do this, it will be useful to introduce the orthogonal polynomials for this ensemble. These were discovered in
[
33
]
. Typically orthogonal polynomials are designed such that their leading polynomial has unit coefficient:

P_{j}(z)=z^{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}a_{i}z^{i}\penalty\ .

(C.3)

As such, they are not orthonormal, and rather satisfy

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\theta}{2\pi}\,e^{Lg\,\cos\theta}P_{j}\left(e^{i\theta}\right)P_{k}^{*}\left(e^{i\theta}\right)=h_{j}\delta_{jk}\penalty\ ,

(C.4)

with the
h_{j}
to be determined.
To define these orthogonal polynomials, first note the identity:

I_{n}(Lg)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\theta}{2\pi}e^{Lg\cos\theta}e^{in\theta}\penalty\ ,

(C.5)

where
I_{n}(x)
is the modified Bessel function. The orthogonal polynomials for this ensemble are then

P_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{c_{n}}\text{det}\begin{pmatrix}1&z&z^{2}&\dots&z^{n}\\
I_{0}(Lg)&I_{1}(Lg)&I_{2}(Lg)&\dots&I_{n}(Lg)\\
I_{1}(Lg)&I_{0}(Lg)&I_{1}(Lg)&\dots&I_{n-1}(Lg)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
I_{n-1}(Lg)&\dots&\dots&I_{0}(Lg)&I_{1}(Lg)\end{pmatrix}\penalty\ ,

(C.6)

and defined such that
P_{0}(z)
=1 and, similarly, we have defined the coefficients
c_{n}
such that

c_{0}=1\penalty\ ,\qquad\qquad c_{n\geq 1}=\text{det}[I_{k-l}(Lg)]_{k,l=1,\dots,n}\penalty\ .

(C.7)

The coefficients
h_{n}
are then found to be:

h_{n}=c_{n+1}/c_{n}\penalty\ .

(C.8)

These orthogonal polynomials satisfy the following recursion relation

P_{n+1}(z)=zP_{n}(z)+(-1)^{n+1}z^{n}P_{n}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)\sqrt{1-\frac{h_{n+1}}{h_{n}}}

(C.9)

The choice of leading coefficient in
P_{j}(z)
allows us to write
[
21
]
:

\Delta\left(e^{i\theta}\right)=\text{det}\left[e^{i(j-1)\theta_{k}}\right]=\text{det}\left[P_{j-1}\left(e^{i\theta_{k}}\right)\right]\penalty\ ,

(C.10)

Normalization:

The above exposition allows us to readily compute the normalization

section

A.1.1

\mathcal{N}_{\rm GWW}=L!\prod_{n=0}^{L-1}h_{n}=L!\times c_{L}\penalty\ ,

Two-point function:

Similarly, for the two-point function we have:

\left\langle\text{Tr}\,U^{n}\text{Tr}\,U^{m}\right\rangle_{\rm GWW}=\left\langle\text{Tr}\,U^{n+m}\right\rangle_{\rm GWW}+\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\theta_{1}}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\theta_{2}}{2\pi}e^{in\theta_{1}+im\theta_{2}}\langle\rho(\theta_{1})\rho(\theta_{2})\rangle_{\rm GWW}\penalty\ ,

(C.18)

where

\langle\rho(\theta_{1})\rho(\theta_{2})\rangle_{\rm GWW}=\det\begin{pmatrix}K_{L}(\theta_{1},\theta_{1})&K_{L}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\\
K_{L}(\theta_{2},\theta_{1})&K_{L}(\theta_{2},\theta_{2})\end{pmatrix}

(C.19)

and

K_{L}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}\psi_{j}(\theta_{1})\psi_{j}^{*}(\theta_{2})\penalty\ .

(C.20)

Expanding out (
C.19
), we may write

\langle\rho(\theta_{1})\rho(\theta_{2})\rangle_{\rm GWW}=\langle\rho(\theta_{1})\rangle_{\rm GWW}\langle\rho(\theta_{2})\rangle_{\rm GWW}+\langle\rho(\theta_{1})\rho(\theta_{2})\rangle^{c}_{\rm GWW}\penalty\ ,

(C.21)

with

\langle\rho(\theta_{1})\rho(\theta_{2})\rangle_{\rm GWW}^{c}\equiv-K_{L}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})K_{L}(\theta_{2},\theta_{1})\penalty\ .

(C.22)

This implies that we can write

\left\langle\text{Tr}\,U^{n}\text{Tr}\,U^{m}\right\rangle_{\rm GWW}=\left\langle\text{Tr}\,U^{n+m}\right\rangle_{\rm GWW}+\left\langle\text{Tr}\,U^{n}\right\rangle_{\rm GWW}\left\langle\text{Tr}\,U^{m}\right\rangle_{\rm GWW}+\left\langle\text{Tr}\,U^{n}\text{Tr}\,U^{m}\right\rangle_{\rm GWW}^{c}\penalty\ ,

(C.23)

just as we saw in (
3.63
) and where we have defined

\left\langle\text{Tr}\,U^{n}\text{Tr}\,U^{m}\right\rangle_{\rm GWW}^{c}\equiv\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\theta_{1}}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}\theta_{2}}{2\pi}e^{in\theta_{1}+im\theta_{2}}\langle\rho(\theta_{1})\rho(\theta_{2})\rangle^{c}_{\rm GWW}\penalty\ .

(C.24)

For the circular ensembles, the orthogonal polynomials
P_{j}(z)
admit a Christoffel-Darboux formula
[
43
]
:

\sum_{j=0}^{L-1}P_{j}(z)P_{j}^{*}(\zeta)=\frac{z^{L}\zeta^{L}\left(P_{L}(z)\right)^{*}P_{L}\left(\frac{1}{\zeta}\right)-P_{L}(z)\left(P_{L}\left(\frac{1}{\zeta}\right)\right)^{*}}{1-z\zeta}\penalty\ ,

(C.25)

where we are considering variables on the unit-circle such that
z^{*}=1/z
and
\zeta^{*}=1/\zeta
.
This implies

K_{L}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=e^{i(L-1)\left(\frac{\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}}{2}\right)}\frac{e^{-iL\left(\frac{\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}}{2}\right)}\psi_{L}(\theta_{1})\psi_{L}^{*}(\theta_{2})-e^{iL\left(\frac{\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}}{2}\right)}\psi_{L}^{*}(\theta_{1})\psi_{L}(\theta_{2})}{2i\sin\left(\frac{\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}}{2}\right)}\penalty\ .

(C.26)

From this we can derive that

\langle\rho(\theta)\rangle_{\rm GWW}=i\left[\psi_{L}(\theta)\partial_{\theta}\psi_{L}^{*}(\theta)-\psi_{L}^{*}(\theta)\partial_{\theta}\psi_{L}(\theta)\right]-L\,\psi_{L}(\theta)\psi_{L}^{*}(\theta)\penalty\ .

(C.27)
