Seagrass meadows are among the world’s most ecologically and economically important marine ecosystems. Although they cover only 0.1% of the seafloor, they sequester carbon at rates rivalling tropical rainforests, protect coastlines, and support marine biodiversity (do Amaral Camara Lima et al., 2023). Yet they are declining globally due to anthropogenic pressures including coastal development, pollution, and climate change (Waycott et al., 2009; Dunic et al., 2021). While ocean acidification and ocean warming are pervasive stressors under accelerating global change, Pazzaglia et al. (2025) have demonstrated that elevated dissolved CO2 may enhance seagrass resilience by increasing thermal tolerance in Posidonia oceanica seedlings through the CO2 fertilization effect.

Understanding plant responses to rising CO2 levels across marine and terrestrial systems is vital for predicting future ecosystem resilience and climate feedbacks. This article compares shared physiological mechanisms (e.g. Rubisco efficiency and biomass allocation) with terrestrial plants with ecosystem-specific divergent adaptations (e.g. bicarbonate assimilation and sediment carbon storage) to CO2 enrichment. By synthesizing evidence across biomes, it unravels whether CO2 fertilization follows universal patterns or is context dependent, with implications for conservation under future elevated CO2 environments.

Seagrasses, including Posidonia oceanica, are foundational coastal ecosystems that support ecosystem stability. These underwater meadows provide habitat for threatened species such as turtles and dugongs, and nursery grounds for both artisanal fisheries and >20% of the world’s largest commercial fisheries (Hughes et al., 2009; Unsworth et al., 2019). Alongside contributing to global food security, seagrass meadows provide other ecosystem services including coastal protection, improved water quality, and nutrient cycling (UNEP, 2020; Duarte et al., 2025). Seagrass ecosystems are also well recognized as nature-based solutions to mitigate climate change, storing carbon at rates up to 35 times faster than tropical rainforests, a process termed ‘blue carbon’ (Duarte et al., 2013; Macreadie et al., 2021).

Seagrasses are the only flowering plants (angiosperms) in marine environments. Unlike algae, they possess true roots, leaves, and vascular tissues, reflecting their evolutionary return from land to sea. Molecular evidence suggests that seagrasses evolved from monocotyledonous ancestors that recolonized marine environments ∼100 million years ago, subsequently adapting to salinity, submergence, hydrodynamic stress, and low-light conditions (Les et al., 1997; Olsen et al., 2016). Today, ∼72 seagrass species occur across six families (e.g. Posidoniaceae, Zosteraceae, and Cymodoceaceae), forming extensive meadows from tropical to temperate coastlines (Short et al., 2011; Fig. 1).

Shared mechanisms between terrestrial and marine plants

Comparative analyses of seagrasses and terrestrial plants reveal both convergent and divergent strategies in response to elevated CO2, highlighting the complexity of their ecological niches. The CO2 fertilization effect is a phenomenon well documented in terrestrial C3 plants; elevated CO2 reduces Rubisco limitation and photorespiration, enhancing carbon assimilation (Ainsworth and Long, 2021; Chen et al., 2022). Temperate forests, crops, and grasslands show on average an ∼30% increase in photosynthetic productivity under elevated CO2 (Jablonski et al., 2002; Ainsworth and Long, 2005). Even after partial photosynthetic acclimation to elevated CO2, carbon uptake and biomass allocation can remain enhanced (Leakey et al., 2009). Most seagrasses also use C3 photosynthesis (Koch et al., 2013); therefore, increased dissolved CO2 can similarly enhance net photosynthesis and reduce the energetic cost of photorespiration (Koch et al., 2013; Raven et al., 2014).

Seagrasses are often carbon limited; however, under elevated CO2, many species show increased leaf growth, biomass, pigment content, and vegetative development (Viana et al., 2023; Pansini et al., 2025). Lower pH levels under ocean acidification can also stimulate chlorophyll and carotenoid production, improving the light-harvesting system and photosynthetic efficiency (Pansini et al., 2025). Consequently, ocean acidification may, under some conditions, enhance seagrass productivity and carbon sink capacity, particularly when interacting with warming in ways that stimulate carbon metabolism and dissolved organic carbon fluxes (Yamuza-Magdaleno et al., 2025).

Challenges of the marine environment: divergent seagrass responses

Despite shared C3 physiology, seagrass responses to CO2 differ from those of terrestrial plants because marine environments impose unique constraints. Terrestrial plants respond to elevated CO2 partly by reducing stomatal conductance (gs) and improving water-use efficiency (Ainsworth and Long, 2005). Seagrasses, by contrast, are fully submerged and lack functional stomatal gas exchange. The extraordinary evolutionary adaptation of seagrasses to thrive in marine conditions involved gene loss (e.g. stomatal genes), reduction (e.g. genes for terpenoid synthesis), and acquisition (e.g. sulphation-related genes) associated with submergence, salinity tolerance, ion regulation, and sulphur metabolism (Olsen et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2024). Specialized traits such as salt-excreting structures, root-based ion exclusion, and internal aeration systems enable survival in saline, anoxic sediments (Touchette, 2007; Fig. 2).

Marine carbon acquisition is also fundamentally different. Dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) diffuses ∼10 000 times more slowly in water than atmospheric CO2(g), and its availability is strongly modulated by pH and temperature (Koch et al., 2013). As bicarbonate (HCO3−) is the dominant inorganic carbon form in seawater, many seagrasses, including P. oceanica, use HCO3− uptake where HCO3− is actively transported via extracellular enzymatic conversion to CO2 via carbonic anhydrase and related mechanisms (Beer and Koch, 1996; Larkum et al., 2017; Rubio et al., 2017; Fig. 2). As ocean acidification increases dissolved CO2 relative to HCO3−, seagrasses may benefit from reduced energetic costs of carbon acquisition.

Elevated CO2 may also influence reproduction. In terrestrial C3 plants, greater carbohydrate availability can increase reproductive output (i.e. more flowers, fruits, and seeds); however, this does not necessarily translate into greater reproductive viability, with seed [N] shown to decline by ∼15% under elevated CO2 (Jablonski et al., 2002). In seagrasses, elevated CO2 has been linked to earlier flowering, higher flower density in Zostera marina (Palacios and Zimmerman, 2007), as well as increased seed size (Hernán et al., 2016) and improved germination through parental carryover effects (i.e. seeds harvested from flowering shoots after CO2 exposure) in other species (Lowell et al., 2021; Fig. 2). These responses suggest that CO2 enrichment may benefit some early life-history stages, although outcomes remain species and context dependent.

Recent work also indicates that elevated CO2 can improve stress tolerance, with seagrass populations showing adaptive gene expression responses under concurrent climate stressors (Olivé et al., 2017). Pazzaglia et al. (2025) found that seedlings exposed to elevated CO2 exhibited enhanced thermal tolerance during heat stress, supported by stronger antioxidant defences, as evidenced by increased oxygen radical absorbance capacity. Transcriptomic analyses revealed the up-regulation of oxidative stress response genes (e.g. GSTF8, FDX3, and OCP3), showing a robust antioxidant defence capacity twice as high as seedlings in ambient CO2 conditions, enhancing their ability to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), reduce ROS damage, and protect cellular integrity under warming. Intricate transcriptomic adjustments were also identified, including the down-regulation of PSBS and PGR5 genes, involved in non-photochemical quenching, suggesting a shift in photoprotective mechanisms to support photosynthetic performance under warming (Pazzaglia et al., 2025; Fig. 2).

While the CO2 fertilization effect offers a potential buffer for seagrass productivity, field observations from natural analogues of ocean acidification reveal that CO2 enrichment does not universally benefit seagrasses. Volcanic CO2 vent studies, where pH plummets to levels beyond near-future projections (e.g. ocean pH is predicted to drop to ∼7.7 by 2100, a 100–150% decrease; Jiang et al., 2023), demonstrate that extreme or prolonged acidification can impair development and productivity. For example, P. oceanica seedlings exposed to very low pH (e.g. pH ∼5.65 versus ambient ∼8.1) showed constrained morphological development and metabolic remodelling of structural components that appeared to be a costly acclimation response rather than a pathway to enhanced productivity (Pansini et al., 2023; Fig. 2). More strikingly, long-term exposure of Cymodocea nodosa to high-CO2 vent conditions reduced net productivity, contradicting the expectation of a straightforward fertilization effect (Olivé et al., 2017). These findings suggest that the benefits of increased carbon availability in short-term experiments can be weakened or reversed under sustained, multi-stressor field conditions, where factors such as altered sediment chemistry, microbial activity, nutrient loading, light limitation, and community shifts co-occur.

Seagrass productivity is also tightly coupled to sediment biogeochemistry (Ruiz-Halpern et al., 2008). Low pH levels associated with ocean acidification can stimulate microbial decomposition, increasing sulphide (H2S) production and the risk of sulphide intrusion into seagrass tissues (Holmer and Hasler-Sheetal, 2014). Increased photosynthesis may partly offset this by enhancing internal oxygen transport to roots via aerenchyma-equipped rhizomes, facilitating radial oxygen loss and supporting microaerophilic sulphide-oxidizing bacteria in the rhizosphere (Martin et al., 2019). Under combined ocean warming, acidification, and nutrient loading, seagrasses may also up-regulate sulphide oxidation pathways (e.g. SOX-related genes), suggesting a potential microbial defence mechanism against sulphide toxicity (Zhang et al., 2023; Fig. 2).

Community interactions may further complicate CO2 responses. Although CO2 enrichment can increase seagrass photosynthetic efficiency (Apostolaki et al., 2014; Pajusalu et al., 2016), it may also favour algal or epiphyte growth, increasing light limitation and offsetting productivity gains. However, CO2 effects on epiphytes may alter community composition (e.g. reducing coralline algae while increasing filamentous forms) without necessarily increasing total epiphyte biomass (Campbell and Fourqurean, 2014; Fig. 2). Notably, these CO2-mediated effects appear decoupled from nutrient-driven productivity patterns common in terrestrial systems (Campbell and Fourqurean, 2014).

Conclusion

CO2 fertilization operates through shared mechanisms in terrestrial and marine plants, including enhanced Rubisco efficiency, reduced photorespiration, and increased biomass production. However, seagrass responses are mediated by a more complex interplay of physical and chemical factors, including slow carbon diffusion in water, bicarbonate use, salinity stress, sediment anoxia, sulphide toxicity, epiphyte dynamics, and microbial interactions. As a result, the benefits of elevated CO2 are non-linear, species-specific, and strongly context-dependent (Borum et al., 2016; Guerrero-Meseguer et al., 2020; Pansini et al., 2023).

Rising atmospheric CO2 (pCO2atm) will increase dissolved CO2(aq) and lower oceanic pH. Given that many seagrasses are currently carbon limited, moderate CO2(aq) enrichment may enhance photosynthesis, growth, reproduction, and even heat tolerance, as shown for P. oceanica seedlings (Pazzaglia et al., 2025). Nevertheless, these potential benefits must be considered alongside broader climate change pressures, including marine heatwaves, eutrophication, sediment deterioration, coastal development, and biodiversity loss. Elevated CO2 may therefore buffer some physiological stressors, but it is unlikely to offset the full suite of anthropogenic threats driving global seagrass decline (Waycott et al., 2009; Dunic et al., 2021).
