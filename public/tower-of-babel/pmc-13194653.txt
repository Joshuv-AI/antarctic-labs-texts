Impact of Triton solution irrigation protocol on root fracture resistance: a comparative in vitro study of different irrigation protocols
Authors: Pierre Alsayegh, Helen Rushdi Ayoubi, Yasser Alsayed Tolibah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the effect of an irrigation protocol using Triton solution on the fracture resistance of treated root compared with other common irrigation protocols.Materials and methodsSixty-four human mandibular premolars with single roots and single canals were decoronated to a length of 15 mm. The samples were prepared using the ProTaper system Universal Rotary System. The roots were randomly divided into four groups (n = 16 per group) according to the irrigation protocol used. In G1: (First experimental group), the canals were irrigated with Triton all-in-one irrigation during preparation and as a final irrigation. In G2: (Second experimental group), the canals were irrigated with 5.25% Sodium Hypochlorite (NaOCl) during preparation and 5.25% NaOCl + 17% Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) + 2% Chlorhexidine (CHX) as a final irrigation. In G3: (Positive control group), the canals were irrigated with 5.25% NaOCl + distilled water. In G4: (Negative control group), the canals were irrigated with only distilled water. Each sample was tested for fracture on a universal testing machine at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min. The fracture resistance of each sample was recorded, and the data were statistically analysed using a one-way ANOVA and Chi-square tests. The failure modes were observed and assessed. All data were analysed at a 95% confidence interval (α = 0.05).ResultsThe mean fracture resistance values ranged from 297.81 ± 61.39 N in G1 to 364.50 ± 84.38 N in G4, with no observed significant differences among the groups (p = 0.112). Regarding failure patterns, oblique fractures were the most frequently observed type, followed by vertical and comminuted fractures, with no significant differences in their distribution among the groups (p = 0.993).ConclusionRoots treated with the Triton solution protocol demonstrated fracture resistance similar to those treated with common irrigation protocols.Subject terms: Endodontics, Dental materials

Introduction

Dentin toughness is considered the primary indicator of its ability to resist fracture [1].

Endodontic irrigants affect morphological, physical and chemical dentin characteristics [2]. For example, sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl)–based irrigation protocols have been shown to affect the mechanical properties of dentin, with the extent of these effects depending on solution concentration and duration of use [1]. These effects include changes in dentin microhardness, surface roughness, and elastic modulus [3]. NaOCl has been reported to cause a greater reduction in elastic modulus and fracture resistance than Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), particularly at higher concentrations and with prolonged exposure time [4].

EDTA possesses strong chelating properties, leading to widening of dentinal tubule diameters, increased dentin permeability, and degradation of dentin collagen fibres [4]. The effects of sodium hypochlorite and EDTA on the properties of root dentin can be attributed to significant calcium loss from dentin when these solutions are used for durations ranging between one and five minutes [5].

Chlorhexidine (CHX) is among the irrigating solutions used in root canal irrigation due to its favourable properties [6]. It exhibits a broad-spectrum antimicrobial effect against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, aerobic and anaerobic microorganisms, as well as viruses and fungi [6]. However, one of its main disadvantages is its inability to dissolve organic tissues [6]. The effectiveness of chlorhexidine depends on the environment’s pH and is significantly reduced in the presence of organic matter [7].

Continuous chelation was proposed as an approach that combines the disinfecting and organic tissue-dissolving effects of NaOCl with the capacity of chelating solutions to remove inorganic debris [8].

Triton (Brasseler, Savannah, USA) is a newly developed all-in-one endodontic irrigant that integrates the antimicrobial and tissue-dissolving properties of NaOCl with the chelating effects of EDTA and the antimicrobial effects of CHX. According to the manufacturer, Triton contains a reduced concentration of NaOCl (4%) combined with specific mild chelating agents and a proprietary surfactant system, which distinguishes its mechanism of action from that of conventional chelating solutions. Owing to this formulation, Triton is intended to be used as a single, comprehensive irrigating solution throughout the entire root canal procedure, rather than being restricted to use as a final irrigant [9].

The non-NaOCl components of Triton contribute to the active dissolution of dentinal debris and smear layer, thereby reducing the organic load that would otherwise consume sodium hypochlorite through buffering reactions. As a result, a lower concentration of NaOCl can remain effective, maintaining its antimicrobial and tissue-dissolving activity without being rapidly neutralised by organic remnants [9].

Triton has been evaluated in several in vitro studies and shown to offer several advantages as an irrigant. It effectively removes debris, demonstrating superior performance compared to other protocols, particularly in the middle and apical thirds of the root canal [10]. Triton also outperforms Dual Rinse HEDP in smear layer removal and is comparable to traditional NaOCl/EDTA protocols [9]. It has significant antifungal properties, showing lower residual Candida albicans levels [10]. In addition, Triton enhances tissue dissolution, particularly when prewarmed or continuously warmed, compared with other solutions such as Twin Kleen™ and 3% NaOCl [11]. Triton also improves dentin wettability and penetration, potentially enhancing cleaning efficiency, while maintaining effects on dentin roughness similar to those of established protocols [6]. Furthermore, it exhibits favourable physicochemical properties, including optimal surface tension and dentin penetration, and demonstrates cytotoxicity similar to NaOCl but lower than that of 17% EDTA, with no genotoxic effects [12, 13]. These results support Triton as a promising irrigant with effective cleaning and antimicrobial properties.

Although several recent studies have investigated the physicochemical properties, antimicrobial activity, smear layer removal, and cytocompatibility of the all-in-one irrigant Triton, there is a lack of experimental evidence regarding its effect on the fracture resistance of endodontically treated teeth. Most published research has focused on antimicrobial efficacy, tissue dissolution, and dentin surface modification, while the potential influence of Triton on the mechanical integrity of root dentin has not yet been evaluated. This gap underscores the need for in vitro investigations to determine whether the combined action of Triton on dentin’s organic and inorganic components can affect root fracture resistance, providing crucial information for its clinical application.

This in vitro study aimed to evaluate the effects of the Triton all-in-one endodontic irrigant and two other irrigation protocols commonly used in endodontic treatment on root fracture resistance. The null hypothesis is that there are no significant differences in fracture resistance force or observed failure mode between Triton and other irrigation protocols.

Materials and Methods

Ethical approval and settings

This experimental study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study received ethical approval from the Local Ethics Committee of Damascus University (N2326). It followed the CRIS Guidelines (Checklist for Reporting In-Vitro Studies). This study was performed at the Faculty of Dental Medicine, Damascus University, in the Department of Endodontics and Operative Dentistry.

Solutions

The substances evaluated in the present study were distilled water, 5.25% NaOCl (Ibn Al-Haytham, Damascus, Syria), 17% EDTA (Meta Biomed, Chungcheongbuk-do, Korea), 2% CHX (Maquira, Maringa, Brazil) and Triton all-in-one irrigation (Brasseler, Savannah, USA).

Sample size calculation

G*Power 3.1 software (Universität Kiel, Germany) was used to calculate the sample size. The power was estimated at 95%, the probability of Type 1 error (α) was 0.05, and the effect size (f) was 0.5 (estimated from a similar previous study [14]), resulting in a total sample size of 64 teeth. The sample consisted of 64 freshly extracted human mandibular premolars with single roots and single canals.

Selection and preparation of specimens

The premolars were cleaned, immersed in 0.9% physiological saline solution, and stored at 4 °C until the beginning of the study and subsequent use [15]. Teeth from patients aged 30–45 years were included in the study. The teeth included in this study were recently extracted for clinical reasons related to orthodontic or periodontal disease, and no extractions were performed specifically for research purposes. Informed consent was obtained from all patients for the use of the extracted teeth in the study.

Periapical radiographs using a digital sensor (EzSensor HD; Vatech, Gyeonggi-do, Korea) were taken for each tooth from the buccal and lingual aspects to confirm the internal canal anatomy and to ensure the absence of additional canals, calcifications, pathological findings, or resorptive defects [16].

The premolars were examined under a magnifying lens (×10 magnification) (Carson handheld, Ronkonkoma, New York, USA) to ensure the absence of cracks or fractures. After standardising the working length, the specimens were decoronated by transversely sectioning the roots at 15 mm using a double-faced diamond disc (Hager & Meisinger GmbH, Neuss, Germany) with a low-speed straight handpiece and an air/water spray coolant (Being, Foshan, China).

After sectioning, the buccolingual and mesiodistal dimensions of the specimens were measured using a digital caliper (Insize, China). Dentin thickness at the buccal, lingual, mesial, and distal surfaces was measured as the distance between the canal wall and the external root surface at the middle third of the root. Each measurement was performed three times, and the mean value was recorded. This procedure was used to select roots with approximately comparable dentin thickness across all groups.

The distribution of dentin thickness values for each group was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The results did not indicate significant deviation from normality (all p > 0.05), suggesting an approximately normal distribution (Table 1).

The root canals were initially manually prepared using #8, #10, and #15 K-files (Mani, Inc., Japan), after which canal patency was confirmed. Subsequently, the canals were mechanically prepared using the ProTaper Universal rotary system (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland), starting with an orifice opener file and progressing to a rotary file with the appropriate taper, selected according to the canal diameter to ensure adequate shaping without excessive removal of dentin. The range of final preparation sizes was limited to F2–F3 to minimize variability.

Irrigation was carried out using a 5-mL disposable syringe fitted with a 30-gauge side-vented irrigation needle, which was inserted passively into the canal up to approximately 1 mm short of the working length without binding. Unless otherwise specified, the irrigants were delivered slowly and allowed to remain in the canal during instrumentation.

Irrigation was performed during instrumentation according to the protocol assigned to each group, using a syringe and a side-vented needle. The same irrigation protocol and volume were applied to all specimens within each group.

Group 1 (First experimental group)

During instrumentation, each canal received Triton solution distributed between files, with 1 mL delivered after each K-file and rotary file until a total volume of 5 mL was reached. Final irrigation was performed with 1 mL of Triton solution left in the canal for 3 minutes, followed by flushing with 5 mL of distilled water according to the manufacturer’s instructions [9].

Group 2 (Second experimental group)

During preparation, each canal was irrigated with 1 mL of 5.25% NaOCl after each K-file and rotary file [17]. Final irrigation consisted of 5.25% NaOCl for 1 minute, followed by 17% EDTA for 1 minute [2], and 2.5 mL of 2% CHX for 1 minute [18]. Between each irrigant, the canals were flushed with 1 mL of distilled water to neutralize chemical interactions.

Group 3 (Positive control group)

During preparation, each canal was irrigated with 1 mL of 5.25% NaOCl after each K-file and rotary file [17]. Final irrigation consisted of 5.25% NaOCl left in the canal for 3 minutes, followed by flushing with 5 mL of distilled water.

Group 4 (Negative control group)

During preparation, each canal was irrigated with 1 mL of distilled water after each K-file and rotary file until a total volume of 5 mL was reached. Final irrigation consisted of distilled water left in the canal for 3 minutes, followed by flushing with 5 mL of distilled water.

The prepared roots will be dried by paper points and then stored in an incubator at 37 °C with 100% humidity for 7 days, before fracture resistance testing [14].

All preparation and irrigation procedures were made by a single specialist (4 years of experience) in endodontics (P.A.).

Preparation for the mechanical test

In all groups, the root surfaces of the specimens were coated with a layer of liquid peel-off latex (AL’VER, France) to simulate the physiological behaviour of the periodontal ligament. The coated roots were then embedded in acrylic cylindrical blocks with a 20 mm diameter, leaving 2 mm of the uncoated root portion exposed above the acrylic surface to simulate clinical conditions (Fig. 1).

Fracture test

Fracture resistance testing was performed using a universal testing machine (Instron Universal Test Machine, Norwood, USA) (Fig. 2). A cylindrical stainless-steel rod with a 3 mm diameter was mounted on the upper crosshead and positioned centrally over the root canal orifice, while the acrylic blocks were secured on the lower platform. A compressive load was applied parallel to the long axis of the tooth at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min until root fracture occurred [14]. Fracture was recognized both by a sudden drop in the recorded load on the testing machine and visually, using a magnifying lens (×10 magnification), to confirm the presence of cracks or separations in the root structure. The maximum force at fracture was recorded in Newtons (N) by an independent examiner (Y.A.T.) who was blinded to the irrigation protocols used in the study.

Fracture resistance testing procedure.

Application of load during the fracture resistance test.

Failure mode

After completion of the fracture resistance test, the fractured specimens were carefully examined to determine the mode of failure. Fractures were identified by visual inspection using a magnifying lens (×10 magnification) to assess the direction and pattern of the crack lines in the root structure. Based on these observations, the fracture patterns were classified into three categories: vertical fractures, oblique fractures, and comminuted fractures.

Vertical Fracture: This failure mode occurs when the crack extends vertically along the root, often leading to significant damage and compromise of the tooth structure. It is typically associated with severe stress or poor canal sealing.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted utilising IBM SPSS software version 24 (IBM SPSS Statistics® version 24, IBM Corp., New York, USA). Shapiro-Wilk tests indicated that fracture resistance values were normally distributed across the three groups (p > 0.05). Moreover, homogeneity of variance was assessed using the Levene test, which indicated that variances were homogeneous among groups (p > 0.05); hence, intragroup comparisons of fracture resistance values were performed using the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni test. The chi-square test was used to analyse failure mode frequencies across groups. Data were analysed at a 95% confidence interval, and the significance level was set at 0.05.

Discussion

Common endodontic irrigation protocols often involve multiple sequential solutions, making clinical application time-consuming and technique-sensitive. In response to these limitations, Triton was introduced in 2022 as an all-in-one irrigating solution that combines smear layer removal and disinfection capabilities in a single step [19, 20]. Recent in vitro research has demonstrated that Triton exhibits favourable physicochemical properties, including lower surface tension and enhanced dentin penetration compared with conventional irrigants, supporting its potential as a simplified, effective alternative to traditional multi-step protocols [6, 12].

Although the final preparation size varied between F2 and F3 according to individual canal diameter, the dentin thickness at the middle third was comparable among groups. This minimized the confounding effect of file size differences on fracture resistance while reflecting clinical variability, as root canals naturally vary in diameter. The slight variation in final file size thus maintains both methodological rigor and clinical relevance.

The irrigation protocols and volumes were selected to replicate commonly used clinical practices reported in the literature [2, 9, 17]. Total irrigation volumes and contact times were standardized across groups as much as possible to reduce variability and isolate the effects of the irrigant type on dentin properties. Notably, research on irrigation volume and contact time confirms that prolonged exposure and higher volumes of NaOCl reduce fracture resistance, underscoring the importance of clinically relevant irrigation exposure times [20].

A limitation of the methodology is that minor differences in canal anatomy and taper could not be completely eliminated. However, random allocation and careful selection of specimens based on dentin thickness ensured that these variations did not bias the results significantly.

Overall, the methodological approach—including measurement of dentin thickness, controlled irrigation protocols, and standardized fracture testing—enhances the internal validity of the study while maintaining its applicability to clinical practice.

The findings of the present in vitro study were interpreted in light of previously reported effects of endodontic irrigants on dentin properties, as no directly comparable in vitro studies evaluating root fracture resistance using similar irrigation protocols, particularly involving Triton, are currently available. Therefore, the discussion was based on established evidence regarding irrigant–dentin interactions to provide a plausible explanation for the observed results.

In the present in vitro study, root canal obturation was intentionally omitted to eliminate the potential confounding effect of root-filling materials on fracture resistance. This approach was adopted particularly because certain sealers, notably calcium silicate–based bioceramic sealers, have been reported to reinforce the root structure and increase fracture resistance compared with some conventional sealers [21]. Therefore, excluding root filling in the current experimental design enabled a more direct assessment of the effects of irrigation protocols on dentin and fracture resistance, without the additional variable introduced by sealer–dentin interactions.

In this in vitro study, the aim was to evaluate the effect of a newly introduced irrigating solution (Triton) and two commonly used irrigation protocols on root fracture resistance.

Based on the results, the highest arithmetic mean of the values of the amount of force applied to the roots was seen in G4 (distilled water) followed by G3 (5.25% NaOCl) and G2 (5.25% NaOCl + 17% EDTA + 2% CHX) and the lowest arithmetic mean of the values of the amount of force applied to the roots was seen in G1 (Triton). However, the values were not significantly different (p > 0.05).

These results can be interpreted as follows: In group 2, previous studies have suggested that the interaction of NaOCl with mineralized dentin promotes deeper EDTA penetration, thereby facilitating apatite dissolution [14], and by dissolving the “sparse ghost mineral layer” in collagen, EDTA exposes the underlying dentin, making it susceptible to irreversible degradation by NaOCl, which in turn promotes the gradual dissolution of remaining apatite crystals [22], and in the same group, CHX has been reported to inhibit matrix metalloproteinase–mediated collagen degradation, thereby preserving the collagen matrix in demineralized dentin [14], which may explain the absence of statistically significant differences in the values of the amount of force applied to the roots observed in G2.

The use of NaOCl alone, without the adjunctive application of a chelating agent for smear layer removal, may have limited the extent of dentin demineralisation. Consequently, reduced mineral loss may have restricted the penetration of NaOCl into deeper regions of the dentinal tubules, thereby minimising collagen fibre degradation [2, 14], which promotes dentin viscoelasticity, toughness, and fatigue resistance [19]. This mechanism may explain the higher arithmetic mean of the force applied to the roots observed in G3 compared with the other experimental groups.

Although Triton appears to remove the smear layer less efficiently than 17% EDTA, it still promotes smear layer removal and increased dentin permeability due to the presence of citric acid as a chelating agent, surfactants, and NaOCl [9]. This partial removal may have facilitated deeper penetration of NaOCl into the dentinal tubules in G1 compared with distilled water in G4, leading to greater collagen degradation and mineral loss. In contrast, distilled water preserved the smear layer and dentin structure, which may explain the lower fracture resistance observed in G1.

Consistent with the present findings, Barakat et al. reported that NaOCl irrigation at different concentrations and temperatures did not significantly alter the compressive strength of dentin under controlled conditions, suggesting that under clinical exposure levels irrigant-induced changes may not always translate into significant mechanical weakening [23].

On the other hand, previous studies have examined the impact of various final irrigation solutions, such as QMix, EDTA with a surfactant (REDTA), and BioPure MTAD, on the fracture resistance of endodontically treated teeth. These studies reported that irrigants containing surfactants, like QMix or REDTA, can enhance fracture resistance compared with conventional solutions, while some irrigants, such as chlorhexidine or prolonged BioPure MTAD exposure, may reduce it [24]. The difference between the current study and the previous study may be attributed to variations in obturation, irrigant exposure time, or specific chemical composition of the solutions used.

The limitation of this study lies in its focus solely on the impact of irrigation protocols on the tooth root structure. However, a clinical simulation model, which should be included in future studies, would evaluate the effect of Triton on fracture resistance after root canal fillings with different sealing materials, as well as its impact on the quality of the obturation materials.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, the irrigation protocols evaluated did not affect the fracture resistance of the treated roots. Although samples irrigated with distilled water (negative control group) exhibited the highest mean fracture resistance values, followed by samples irrigated with common irrigation protocols and samples irrigated with Triton, the differences among the groups were not statistically significant. Furthermore, the distribution of failure modes was comparable among the groups, with oblique fractures being the most frequently observed pattern. These findings suggest that the Triton does not compromise the mechanical integrity of root dentin when compared with common irrigation approaches.
