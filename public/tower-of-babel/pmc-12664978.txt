What works for men? Participant perspectives from a mixed-gender weight-management trial with incentives
Authors: Katya Garza, Hisham Ahmad, Bhabna Pati, Corrine I Voils
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMen are underrepresented in behavioral weight-loss trials, limiting the generalizability of findings. While gender-tailored programs have shown success, most research and community-based weight management programs remain mixed-gender. Little is known about men's experiences in these settings or how to best adapt mixed-gender programs to improve their relevance to men.PurposeTo explore men's experiences regarding weight management and to qualitatively evaluate their experiences in the Log2Lose trial.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with men who participated in the Log2Lose trial, a randomized study evaluating the effects of financial incentives on weight loss. Interviews explored motivations for enrolling, satisfaction with program components, and suggestions for improvement. Data were analyzed using conventional content analysis.ResultsNineteen men completed interviews. Most self-identified as White, were middle-aged, married, and college educated. Six themes emerged: 1) Prior weight loss efforts were self-directed and hard to sustain; 2) Rising health concerns prompted men to seek support; 3) Higher-engagers framed weight loss as a personal responsibility, whereas lower-engagers preferred more external accountability; 4) Traditional social roles influenced dietary changes; 5) Peer relatability defined group experiences more than gender composition; 6) Men valued practical content but wanted more tailored support.ConclusionsMen valued their participation in the Log2Lose trial and identified ways to improve relevance and engagement. This study provides participant-informed guidance to support the development of mixed-gender behavioral interventions that appeal to men and promote more equitable representation in weight loss research.Keywords: Qualitative analysis, Men's health, Obesity intervention, Behavioral research, Clinical trials

Introduction

Worldwide obesity prevalence has more than doubled since 1990 [1]. In 2022, an estimated 18.5 % of women and 14.0 % of men were living with obesity [1]. While obesity is more prevalent among women, men face a higher risk of mortality from obesity-related conditions [[2], [3], [4]]. This disparity has been attributed to a combination of biological and behavioral factors, such as differences in fat distribution and lower utilization of preventive health services [5,6]. Although behavioral weight-management interventions have shown efficacy for weight loss [7,8], male participation is disproportionately low [9,10]. A 2012 review of randomized trials reported that only 27 % of participants worldwide were men [10], and this proportion has remained largely unchanged in subsequent behavioral weight-loss studies [11,12].

One potential explanation for the underrepresentation of men is that behavioral weight-loss trials do not align with their preferences or needs [11,13]. Prior qualitative research has found that men favor practical, goal-driven content, self-directed learning, and settings that reflect their interests and identities (e.g., workplaces, sports clubs, peer groups) [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Competition, humor, physical activity, and improving performance are attractive program features, whereas calorie counting and public weigh-ins are viewed as overly prescriptive, feminine, and less appealing [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Financial incentives have also been shown to promote health behavior change by introducing elements of internal competition that may be particularly motivating for men [[20], [21], [22]]. Consistent with this, one trial found that incentives improved weight loss only among male participants [23]. However, these preferences are not universal and may vary with age, cultural background, and geographic context [19,[24], [25], [26]]. Recognition of these factors has led to the development of gender-sensitive weight-management programs designed for men [27] that have shown success in engaging male participants and supporting sustained weight loss [28,29].Because the majority of weight management programs in research and non-research settings are not gender-specific [9,30], finding strategies that improve the inclusion of men in them is critical. Accepting low male enrollment as an inherent limitation of behavioral weight loss research undermines the generalizability of findings to men, reinforces the perception that weight loss is a concern primarily for women, and influences the design and delivery of programs to disproportionately reflect the preferences and experiences of women.

There is limited evidence on how best to engage men in mixed-gender weight loss interventions. It remains unclear whether certain program components can be adapted to better align with men's preferences or if the gender-specific setting itself is necessary to foster appeal. Understanding how men perceive and navigate mixed-gender programs is essential to inform the development of more inclusive weight management programs.

This study aims to explore men's experiences regarding weight management and to qualitatively evaluate their experiences in the Log2Lose trial.

Methods

The Log2Lose trial

This ancillary qualitative study was part of the Log2Lose trial, a randomized, single-masked study that enrolled 706 adults with obesity from Dane County, WI, and Durham County, NC. Full details of the trial design and procedures have been published [31]. All participants received a behavioral weight management program that included 16 mixed-gender group sessions via videoconference and eight individual counseling phone calls. The study comprised three 26-week phases: incentivized weight loss (Phase I), incentivized weight maintenance (Phase II), and non-incentivized weight maintenance (Phase III). Participants were randomized in a 2x2 factorial design to receive adjunctive weekly incentives for calorie logging, weight loss, or both or no incentives in Phases I and II. The trial was conducted in four parallel cohorts. All interventionists and research staff were female for Cohort 1 and most of Cohort 2, after which a male interventionist and research assistant were hired.

At enrollment, participants were given a study workbook, exercise band, fitness tracker, cellular scale, and instructed to download fitness and dietary tracking mobile applications. Participants were asked to log all food and beverage intake and to self-weigh on the cellular scale regularly. Incentivized arms were eligible for weekly incentives based on calorie logging and/or weight loss, according to randomization arm and phase-specific criteria communicated to participants. The study platform collected data from participants’ scales, dietary applications, and activity trackers to determine incentive eligibility weekly. Those in incentive arms received weekly text messages indicating whether an incentive had been earned and encouraging continued self-monitoring and weight loss. All arms received two motivational messages per week without incentive-related content.

The primary objective of the trial was to compare the proportion of participants achieving ≥5 % weight loss at 26 weeks across study arms to assess the efficacy of different financial incentives interventions. Body weight was collected at 26, 52, and 78 weeks, and participants were compensated for providing outcomes. To ensure completion of this ancillary qualitative study before the trial ended, only participants from the first two cohorts who had completed the 78-week assessment and had consented to future contact were recruited.

Recruitment

A total of 74 trial participants who self-identified as male were eligible for recruitment. To capture a range of perspectives, we sought input from men with higher and lower engagement in the weight-management counseling components (group sessions, calls, or both). Higher engagement was defined as attending at least half of virtual group sessions and individual calls, while lower engagement was defined as attending fewer than half. Recruitment communication framed participation as an opportunity for men to share their experiences in the Log2Lose trial and opinions on how to improve male representation in weight-management research. All men with lower engagement (n = 29) were initially contacted, along with 21 men with higher engagement. Due to the high response rate and the fact that we achieved informational redundancy among men with higher engagement, we did not contact the remaining 24 in this group. Non-responders in the lower engagement group received a second contact attempt.

Data collection

Individual semi-structured interviews were conducted via online video conferencing in November 2023. Verbal consent was obtained at the beginning of each interview. Each interview lasted between 22 and 66 min, with an average duration of 42 min, and was audio-recorded. Participants received $40 in compensation for their time.

A semi-structured interview guide (Table 1) was used to facilitate discussion, covering topics such as prior weight loss experiences, motivations for enrolling in the trial, satisfaction with the Log2Lose intervention components, barriers to participating in the program, and recommendations for improvement. Minor revisions to the guide were made during data collection based on insights gained from early interviews. Interviews continued until no new information emerged. Study procedures were performed in compliance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines and were approved August 1, 2023 by the University of Wisconsin Institutional Review board (#2020-1693).

Data analysis

Transcripts were analyzed using conventional content analysis in NVivo 14. Three transcripts were open-coded during a team meeting to generate emergent codes and develop a codebook with definitions. Subsequently, each transcript was independently coded by two analysts. Coded transcripts were reviewed in team meetings, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Following coding, queries were generated for each code. One analyst reviewed each query and drafted thematic statements summarizing the content. These statements were refined during team meetings, and overarching themes were developed from the finalized statements.

Reflexivity

The interview guide was developed by a doctoral-level behavioral science investigator who identifies as a non-Hispanic White woman (CV) and a master's-level research assistant who identifies as a Latina woman (KG). Interviews were conducted by a bachelor's-level research assistant who identifies as a South Asian man (HA). Data analysis was conducted by the two research assistants (KG, HA), the behavioral science investigator (CV), a medically trained, and a master's-level qualitative researcher who identifies as a South Asian woman (BP). To enhance the dependability of our study findings, the transcripts were generated by an institutional transcription core. A member of our team compared the transcripts to the recorded audio. To enhance trustworthiness of our findings, we emailed a draft summary of the results to all interviewees and asked them whether our write-up accurately represented their experiences, whether anything was missing or needed to be removed, and whether they had anything else to share. Four men responded, of whom two recommended minor revisions. The results were edited accordingly and returned to them for final review.

Results

Twenty-three men expressed interest in participating in interviews. Three men with lower engagement were lost to follow-up, and one with higher engagement was not scheduled due to data saturation. Nineteen interviews were completed. Table 2 presents the characteristics of interviewed and non-interviewed men. Most interviewees were white, married, had a history of previous weight loss attempts, and were highly engaged in the weight counseling components of the Log2Lose trial. Of the 19 interviewed, only five had lower engagement in group sessions, individual calls, or both. Non-interviewees tended to have overall lower engagement. Men often described past weight loss efforts as self-directed and difficult to sustain

Many participants, with higher and lower engagement, reported a long history of weight management challenges characterized by repeated cycles of short-term success followed by weight regain. A couple of participants described working with personal trainers or nutritionists during previous weight loss attempts but cited cost and competing time demands as primary barriers to continuing. Others reported using commercial programs such as Weight Watchers™ or Noom™. However, most men had attempted to lose weight independently, relying on intermittent fasting, restrictive diets, or self-guided exercise routines. As one participant explained, “It was just me and the Internet, you know, looking up things to do” (Incentives for weight loss, higher engagement, 29 yrs). These independent efforts were often described as “quick fixes” that were difficult to sustain, particularly when stress, injury, or changes in routine disrupted their progress.

Only higher-engagement participants attributed their prior self-directed weight loss efforts to cultural expectations that men should be self-reliant and independent. One participant shared that many overweight men he knew would never consider joining a weight loss program, explaining, “I think it has more to do with the cultural, suck it up, be a man, and you can do this by yourself. You don't need help, you don't need someone monitoring what you eat and telling you what to do” (Incentives for both, higher engagement, 60 yrs).

Men turned to external weight management support due to rising health concerns

Health concerns were the most reported catalyst that prompted both higher- and lower-engagement participants to seek external support after years of self-directed efforts. Many enrolled following recent diagnoses, worsening symptoms, or concern about future obesity-related complications. Others were encouraged by their healthcare providers to lose weight, which influenced their decision:

I am a kidney donor, and … meeting with my nephrologist in follow-up visits since donating, he’s just really stressed the importance of staying healthy as much as possible because obviously they don’t want me to develop diabetes, or any other kind of kidney disease … I have high-blood pressure, so this is no longer good for my kidney … I do have diabetes that runs in my family, so I guess just trying to get in front of it before it happens (Incentives for weight loss, lower engagement, 38 yrs).

In addition to health concerns, both higher- and lower-engagement participants were drawn to join the study because it offered structure and credibility that had been missing from previous weight loss programs. They appreciated that it was evidence-based, led by a university, free, focused on sustainable behavior change, and offered professional support. For some, particularly those with analytical backgrounds, the program's emphasis on calorie and weight tracking was a primary reason for enrolling. One participant shared, “The thing that attracted me to the study was the data keeping side of it. I'm a retired engineer. So the idea of keeping data and logging data … that intrigued me. That got me” (No incentives, higher engagement, 68 yrs).

Although men did not report financial compensation for outcome assessments or technological gadgets (i.e., tracker and scale) as primary motivators, several described that these features enhanced the appeal once they were already considering participation. One participant, who had been encouraged by his wife to participate, shared that “the little [non-financial] incentives were for sure the tipping point … I was like, oh you get a FitBit, that's cool … So, probably the spousal push combined with a little bit of swag” (Incentives for weight loss, lower engagement, 44 yrs).

Higher-engagement participants framed weight loss as a personal responsibility, whereas lower-engagers preferred more external accountability

While many men joined the study for its supportive structure, a few higher-engagement participants framed their weight-loss journey as driven by personal responsibility and self-discipline. They viewed the program as a helpful tool rather than the primary driver of their success or setbacks. As one man explained, “Not that I'm looking for a scapegoat … it all came down to me. I don't know that it was anything else that the study could have or should have done, would have made me more successful” (Incentives for weight loss, higher engagement, 49 yrs). Others echoed the belief that applying the program's lessons was a personal obligation, not something interventionists could enforce. In contrast, several men with lower engagement expressed a preference for greater accountability and directness. While they appreciated the supportive environment, they felt it was sometimes too gentle. They believed they would have benefited from more candor, challenge, or “tough love” to help sustain motivation and progress. One participant remarked, “This felt so nice and so gentle and so … Much more like a warm hug, and not like a loving push … I want to be pushed in my spaces … because I think that's how I respond better, generally” (Incentives for weight loss, lower engagement, 44 yrs).

Traditional social roles may influence men's ability to make dietary changes

For some higher- and lower-engagement participants, weight loss was not only a personal effort, but also one shaped by social roles and family dynamics. One participant noted that being responsible for preparing meals gave him more control over his diet but led to tension with his spouse:

I think I aggravated the shit out of my wife … with the calorie counting. She definitely has probably been on 40 diets, and all sorts of things. To have me never be on a diet and then all of a sudden, you know, holy shit what happened? It works for him, and it doesn’t work for me? (Incentives for calorie logging, higher engagement, 65 yrs).

Some participants also shared experiences of being teased by family members for adopting new behaviors such as weighing food or refusing to eat foods that were not in support of their weight loss goals.

Those who were not the primary household cook often found it difficult to implement dietary changes and expressed uncertainty about how to involve their partners in weight loss efforts. Men expressed a need for strategies to navigate these dynamics within their homes. One participant suggested incorporating guidance on how to communicate study goals and dietary changes with family members:

You can only lecture to a husband and a family so much, but if he's not cooking, not preparing the meals, he doesn't have the impact over his diet that his wife [does] … I can see where were some of the guys would have to maybe get a little bit of lesson and how to explain to the wife what they were doing and why and things like portion control and things like that (No incentives, higher engagement, 68 yrs).

Men's experiences in the group setting were shaped less by gender composition and more by the ability to relate to others

Some men reflected on how social dynamics within the program influenced their experience and ability to engage. When asked to react to the gender composition of the majority-female study team, most higher- and lower-engagement participants were indifferent. However, one participant noted that representation still mattered, stating, “I think all of us want to see a little bit of ourselves in those who are leading us in all kinds of different spaces” (Incentives for weight loss, lower engagement, 44 yrs). Most men also did not feel that the majority-female composition of their study groups negatively affected their experience but appreciated having other men present. Several shared that they were accustomed to female-dominated weight loss settings and were unsurprised by the group composition. Still, a few higher- and lower-engagement men expressed difficulty connecting to other group members (men and women alike) due to differences in gender, age, or life stage. They found that group discussions often centered on experiences they did not share, such as being a working mother or managing unsupportive spouses, which made it difficult to relate. Others observed that women tended to speak more, shaping the direction of conversation:

I feel like men in general are less vocal, too, even when they do participate. So, I think even if we were more like even, I do remember like in general … the women speaking definitely … they were speaking more than the men, just, not because like they talk a lot, but because the men don’t talk a lot. Like the men are very quiet (Incentives for calorie logging, higher engagement, 29 yrs).

To address the challenge relating to people at different life stages, men suggested organizing groups based on shared characteristics. One participant proposed:

I would think maybe pairing … men of a similar age. Men who have kind of similar circumstances together … so they connect more. So if you're you know, in between a certain age, but have kids, like you would all be together. Or if you were a certain age and single, not that like married or without kids or something like that. Like, you may be able to connect a little better on certain lifestyles (Incentives for both, lower engagement, 39 yrs).

Men appreciated the practical content but wanted more tailored support to match their needs, routines, and prior experience

Both higher- and lower-engagement men generally appreciated the program's focus on practical, sustainable weight-loss strategies. They found the lessons on portion control, self-monitoring, and habit awareness to be straightforward and useful. However, a few participants with prior experience in weight management felt the lessons were too basic or repetitive. They hoped for options that would allow them to bypass content they already knew and instead focus on areas where they were still struggling. Others wanted the material to be more responsive to their individual needs and progress.

The desire for more personalization also emerged in participants’ reflections on the individual calls with the dietitians. A few men indicated they had expected the calls to provide more tailored guidance or to function as a space for clarifying material from the group sessions. While many higher- and lower-engagement men found these calls appealing and helpful for staying accountable and revisiting goals, several participants shared that the conversations often felt too general and overly structured. The format was frequently described as too focused on predetermined prompts, which some felt limited the opportunity to discuss specific concerns or revisit session content in greater depth. However, one participant shared that the structured format worked well for him:

I felt like we were clearly working our way down to a checklist of things that we needed to talk about, but that didn't make it less useful or interesting for me. I mean, I kind of appreciated that. I may have been less happy with the phone calls if it felt like therapy every time we talked (Incentives for weight loss, higher engagement, 46 yrs).

Both higher- and lower-engagement men also indicated a need for more individualized feedback based on the data they provided throughout the program. One participant suggested that the intervention could have been improved by providing participants with access to a centralized location to view the history of their logged metrics, such as calories, steps, and weight. He noted that having a clear record of progress, along with structured opportunities to reflect on these data with the dietitian, might enhance motivation, particularly for men who respond well to data-driven approaches. While some participants wanted more support in visualizing and interpreting their data, others found value in independently tracking their progress. For example, one man shared that using the tools provided (activity tracker, scale, and dietary application) helped him see the impact of his behaviors, creating a sense of internal competition:

“I liked the fact that I was able to keep track of what I was eating during the day, seeing how many calories it was … looking at Fitbit at the end of the day, or after weighing myself the next day and seeing how much weight I have lost, you know, almost becomes a bit of an internal competition. The feedback and the knowing where you are and seeing the impact of your actions” (No incentives, higher engagement, 68 yrs).

Participants also recommended expanding the program's financial incentive element. While some found the small rewards tied to logging food and losing weight motivating, they suggested extending incentives to other components of the program. As one man reflected,

One of the things I found most enjoyable and compelling about the whole experience was the gamification of it … That I found really fun, as like ooh, it’s Monday and I hit my goal, and am I going to make two bucks, or eight bucks, or anything? Small amounts, but it still felt like, ooh, this is fun. If there had been like a way to tie some of those … gamifying sort of things into the dietician calls, I think that would have been genius … I feel like just the incentivization through random rewards is so powerful, and I found that in my own experience. Like in an embarrassing level of motivation. I’m like, I’m going to hit those four dollars hard (Incentives for weight loss, lower engagement, 44 yrs).

A participant who received incentives for weight loss emphasized that rewarding engagement in recommended program behaviors could help sustain motivation. He described an incentive design where rewards could reflect the degree of participation:

“If people are doing what the program asked them to … you give them 10 dollars for doing all 5 things. You give them 8 dollars because they did 4 out of 5 things … whatever it might be, then it is like, hey, good job, because you did these things. You came to class, you have been logging your stuff, and you have been getting your steps in, you just did not weigh yourself … it can still be a motivator. Like hey, we see you, we acknowledge you are trying to some degree, then if they are not doing anything, do not give them anything! They are not participating, why should they get any money?” (Incentives for weight loss, lower engagement, 38 yrs).

Discussion

Qualitative methods are valuable for identifying intervention features that appeal to men, which could improve male enrollment [10]. Qualitative research on men's experiences in male-only programs has found that men feel more comfortable discussing challenges and adopting help-seeking behaviors when framed around performance or strength rather than calorie counting and weight loss [16,[32], [33], [34], [35]]. Other qualitative studies with men who have not participated in a mixed-gender program reveal that men tend to prioritize health and functional outcomes over aesthetics and often expect that participating as a minority in mixed-gender groups could elicit feelings of shame or exclusion [13,[35], [36], [37]]. Far less is known about how men experience mixed-gender behavioral weight-loss programs, including their perceptions of group dynamics, individual counseling, and incentive structures. This study addresses this gap by qualitatively examining men's experiences with weight management in a mixed-gender program with financial incentives. The present findings identify program components that may require tailoring to enhance men's participation and satisfaction, given that the majority of weight-loss programs are not gender-specific [9,10,30].

Consistent with prior literature, participants often framed weight loss as a personal responsibility and emphasized independence [36,38]. These perspectives may help explain the success of self-directed designs frequently used in male-focused interventions [20,21,29,39,40]. Nonetheless, men also articulated a clear interest in group and individual counseling that allows for participant-led discussion and flexibility to address specific concerns. A flipped classroom model, where didactic content is delivered asynchronously and live sessions are dedicated to interactive discussion, may offer an effective means of balancing self-directed learning with the structured support men seek from weight loss interventions [41]. This approach may also facilitate the organization of groups based on shared characteristics, such as gender or age, which participants recommended to improve relatability and foster a stronger sense of peer support. A flipped classroom would also allow participants to engage with content most relevant to their circumstances and share specific materials with household members when appropriate. Men expressed a need for guidance on shared food preparation and strategies to communicate dietary goals with the individual managing meals. Offering optional content that can be viewed with the household cook may help address this need without placing additional burden on participants who already manage cooking responsibilities. Involving partners in the intervention could improve effectiveness as well, although prior research in this area has generated mixed findings on the outcome of weight change of the index participant [[42], [43], [44], [45]]. Partner-assisted intervention may enhance other outcomes of value to participants, such as relationship quality [43], and can yield behavior change and weight loss in the untreated spouse [[45], [46], [47], [48]].

Independent of incentive group assignment, men responded positively to using self-monitoring tools and expressed interest in receiving more personalized feedback based on their tracked data, along with an expansion of gamification features. Although strategies such as badges, leaderboards, and challenges have been widely incorporated into men-only interventions [[49], [50], [51]], the present findings suggest that these elements may also enhance engagement in mixed-gender programs without requiring fully gamified or gender-specific designs. These results highlight the potential for integrating scalable and low-burden gamification strategies, such as performance-based feedback or recognition of behavioral “streaks,” into standard behavioral weight loss interventions to better reflect male participants’ preferences and support sustained motivation.

There are several limitations to consider when interpreting these findings. First, although we sought to capture a range of perspectives by recruiting men with both high and low engagement in the intervention, most interview participants were highly engaged in the weight management counseling components of the Log2Lose trial. These individuals may have had more favorable views of the program or been more comfortable discussing their experiences than those who declined to participate. It will be important for future research to fully capture the perspectives of men with lower engagement. Second, all interviews were conducted by a male research staff member, which may have influenced participants to provide socially desirable responses. Relatedly, all analysts except the male research assistant were women, which may have influenced interpretation. To counteract this bias, we conducted member checking. A related limitation is that only four men responded to our request for feedback.

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. It is among the few qualitative studies to examine men's experiences in a mixed-gender behavioral weight loss intervention, a setting that reflects most clinical and community programs. By drawing on perspectives from men across two geographically distinct sites, the study generated participant-informed, actionable recommendations to improve the relevance and appeal of mixed-gender interventions. Recruitment materials should highlight components that are likely to appeal to men, as evidence suggests that men are interested in weight loss but are not adequately engaged during the recruitment process. Future mixed-gender programs may benefit from flexible content delivery, more individualized feedback based on self-monitoring data, integration of gamification elements, and participant-led group or individual counseling. Facilitating subgroup-based interactions may also enhance relatability and peer support.

Conclusion

This qualitative study explored how men perceived and engaged with a mixed-gender behavioral weight-loss program. By capturing men's perspectives, researchers can strengthen the inclusiveness of mixed-gender interventions without shifting away from this widely used approach. These findings build on limited qualitative work in this area and highlight a path toward interventions that reflect the needs of all participants. Future behavioral weight-loss research can use this guidance to design programs that are more representative and equitable.
