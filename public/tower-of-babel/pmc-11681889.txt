Comparison of crisis response planning and treatment as usual for active duty service members at risk for suicide: Study protocol for a stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial in a military treatment facility
Authors: Kristen H. Walter, Pia R. Khandekar, Alexander C. Kline, Erin L. Miggantz, Nicholas P. Otis, Lisa H. Glassman, Cynthia J. Thomsen, Guy Brock, Craig J. Bryan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuicide is one of the leading causes of death among U.S. service members, and rates of suicide among military personnel have increased over the past two decades. To address this serious issue, effective preventive treatments are needed in settings where at-risk service members are frequently seen, such as emergency departments and inpatient psychiatric units. This study will compare the longitudinal effects of crisis response planning (CRP) and treatment as usual (TAU) on suicidal thoughts and behaviors among active duty service members seeking emergent care for suicidality at a military treatment facility.MethodsThe current study is conducted through a consortium, Augmenting Suicide Prevention Interventions for Service Members. This article details an ongoing stepped-wedge cluster randomized clinical trial that compares rates of suicidal thoughts and behaviors among service members at risk for suicide following care from CRP-trained providers versus untrained providers (i.e., TAU). Participants complete assessments at pretreatment and every 3 months up to 1 year. Primary outcomes include suicide attempts and behaviors, and suicidal ideation is a secondary outcome. Moderators of treatment effects will also be examined. The methodological development of this trial is discussed, along with clinical and ethical considerations for suicide prevention research in emergency, inpatient, and military treatment settings.ConclusionProviding evidence-based treatment for suicidality that addresses service members’ unique needs is crucial to reduce suicide rates and facilitate mental health recovery in this population. This study aims to inform future implementation and dissemination of CRP in healthcare systems to ultimately decrease suicide among service members.Clinical trials identifierNCT05795764.

Introduction

The latest available data indicate that the suicide rate for active duty service members is 25.1 per 100,000 [1] and remains elevated relative to the age- and gender-adjusted general population [2]. Military suicide has far-reaching impacts, including the loss of individual lives; devastating effects on the morale, readiness, and psychological health of fellow unit members [3,4]; and reductions in the well-being of military families [5,6]. Military researchers and the Suicide Prevention and Response Independent Review Committee [7]—established to conduct a comprehensive review of Department of Defense (DoD) efforts to prevent and address suicide—have identified strategies and recommendations to reduce suicidal behaviors. However, little is known about when or for whom empirically supported strategies are most likely to reduce suicidality.

Because emergency department (ED) medical professionals, psychiatric consult service providers, and providers in inpatient psychiatric units frequently assess and respond to acutely suicidal service members, implementing evidence-based suicide risk assessment and treatment in these settings is critical. Additionally, although training mental health clinicians in effective, evidence-based approaches to address suicidality is an important avenue for care delivery; training providers in other disciplines (e.g., physicians, nurses) or training paraprofessionals can extend the reach of these treatments. Implementing scalable and effective preventive interventions in acute psychiatric settings (e.g., EDs, inpatient psychiatric units) is vital given the high risk for suicide following discharge from these settings [8,9]. Among active duty service members, the risk of dying by suicide in the first 30 days following psychiatric hospitalization was shown to be 8.2 times higher than the risk one year after hospitalization [9]. Effective, brief treatments that reduce suicide risk are especially needed in the critical period following acute psychiatric care. The current study aims to address these needs by implementing and evaluating an evidence-based approach to addressing suicidality among service members with providers from diverse disciplines in military ED and psychiatric inpatient settings. Specifically, providers will be trained in crisis response planning (CRP), a brief psychological intervention that has been shown to significantly reduce suicide attempts and suicidal ideation among active duty military personnel and veterans [10,11].

To date, only two randomized controlled trials of CRP have been published, which compared CRP and treatment as usual (TAU) among soldiers presenting for an emergency behavioral health appointment [10] and CRP and safety planning among military personnel and veterans receiving outpatient trauma-focused therapy for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [11]. Results of both studies showed 50–76 % fewer suicide attempts during follow-up among those receiving CRP versus TAU. However, these randomized trials of CRP were conducted in specialty or embedded behavioral health outpatient clinics. In a nonrandomized clinical trial comparing the effectiveness of an intervention similar to CRP—the safety planning intervention—and TAU in ED settings also showed significantly greater reductions in follow-up suicidal behaviors [12]. Additional research is needed to replicate and extend these findings to other clinical settings, a recommendation consistent with the most recent Department of Veterans Affairs (VA)/DoD clinical practice guideline (CPG) for the Assessment and Management of Patients at Risk for Suicide [13]. Furthermore, the effectiveness of CRP when administered by providers under routine conditions versus by study providers in efficacy research designs has yet to be evaluated. This study aims to address these knowledge gaps.

Additionally, little is known about moderators of CRP effectiveness, though preliminary evidence suggests that protocol fidelity may be correlated with safety planning outcomes [14,15]. In these studies, conducted through the VA, the quality and completeness of safety plans were highly variable, and higher quality plans were associated with greater reductions in suicidal behaviors and psychiatric inpatient hospitalizations. These findings suggest that intensive, structured training focused on protocol fidelity, a feature of the current study, could enhance the effectiveness of treatments and interventions to prevent suicidality. Other moderators of CRP effectiveness—the identification of which may help increase treatment effectiveness—are less known.

Several studies have identified patient factors that moderate other clinical outcomes across psychotherapy treatments for depression and suicidality. These include demographic characteristics like age, gender, military/civilian status [[16], [17], [18], [19]], cognitive ability/functioning [17,20], marital and employment status [17,20], personality characteristics [21], symptom severity and duration [17,20,22], and psychological comorbidities [21]. Lastly, aspects of the therapeutic context may impact the effectiveness of CRP and other therapeutic interventions. For example, patient–provider interaction quality has been shown to moderate CRP effects on suicidality among service members [23]. Better understanding of the specific moderators of CRP effectiveness can help providers deliver personalized, targeted care to at-risk patients when using this intervention.

The primary aim of this stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial (SW-CRT) is to assess suicidal thoughts and behaviors over a 1-year period for service members presenting to the ED at a large military treatment facility (MTF) with elevated suicide risk who received CRP versus TAU. It is hypothesized that follow-up suicide attempt rates and severity of suicidal ideation will be significantly reduced among service members receiving CRP compared with those receiving TAU. A secondary aim is to examine potential moderators of CRP effects, such as clinician-specific (e.g., professional specialty, years of experience, military/civilian), patient-specific (e.g., gender, age, rank, psychiatric diagnoses), and contextual factors (e.g., treatments delivered within the ED vs. psychiatric inpatient unit). Although it is expected that suicidal behaviors and ideation will vary by characteristics of the service member, the provider, and the context, specific moderator hypotheses are not proposed given the limited literature and unique population. In addition, an exploratory aim will statistically examine associations between indicators of suicide risk extracted from medical record data and those based on self-reported study data. Collectively, results will provide novel, actionable information about the parameters that influence how, when, and for whom CRP is most likely to reduce suicidality within acute clinical care settings.

Material and methods

This study uses a SW-CRT design [24,25] to compare treatment effects on follow-up suicide attempts and suicidal ideation among service members who initially present to the ED with elevated risk for suicide. Providers will be trained in groups over time (Fig. 1), and outcomes for service members will be compared between those who received care from a CRP-trained provider versus care from a provider not yet trained in CRP (i.e., TAU). Approximately half of the participants will receive TAU, and the other half will receive CRP. Both CRP and TAU will be delivered in the ED (for those discharged) or the psychiatric inpatient unit (for those admitted). Service members who choose not to participate will continue to receive standard care without disruption. Participant recruitment began in June 2023 and is expected to be completed by October 2026. Consenting participants complete self-report and clinical measures at pretreatment and every 3 months for the duration of 1 year. Additionally, an implementation substudy will involve a subsample of providers who received CRP training through the study. These providers will be recruited to complete a qualitative questionnaire regarding their experiences delivering CRP to service members in ED and psychiatric inpatient settings. The qualitative questionnaire will be administered at the end of the study period or when the provider rotates out of the ED or psychiatric inpatient unit.

Participants

Participants will include up to 700 U.S. active duty service members who initially present to the ED at Naval Medical Center San Diego (NMCSD) with a primary concern related to suicidal ideation, plan, intention, or attempt. All participants must also be at least 18 years of age and able to provide consent. As such, the only exclusion criterion is inability to provide informed consent due to impaired mental status (e.g., acute intoxication, psychosis, mania, altered consciousness) or lack of English language comprehension.

Trial design

To compare outcomes between CRP and TAU, the study uses an SW-CRT design [24,25]. In this design, rather than randomizing at the individual patient level, randomization occurs in groups or “clusters” of providers at different intervals across the study period. Each cluster of providers starts the trial in the control (i.e., TAU) condition, before crossing over to the intervention condition at regularly timed intervals [26]. All clusters ultimately receive the intervention by the end of the trial, with exposure to the intervention occurring at different time points across the study. The SW-CRT thus offers a pragmatic research design that is particularly useful in evaluating the effect of interventions at the service-delivery level.

Groups (i.e., “clusters”) of providers are randomly assigned to receive CRP training in one of five groups, with these trainings occurring at staggered intervals or “steps” (Fig. 1). Clusters were identified based on those who transitioned to the ED or inpatient psychiatric unit (e.g., trainees or active duty providers starting at the command) and randomization of permanent employees. During the first 4 months of the study, no provider groups received CRP training; they solely provided TAU. Beginning in the fifth month, the first cluster of providers received CRP training and accordingly moved from the TAU control condition to CRP. This procedure is repeated every 4 months until all five randomized provider clusters receive the CRP training. Over the course of this 24-month study, the first and last trainings will occur at the 5th and 21st months, respectively. Each provider within each cluster provides the treatment to two cohorts of service members in the study (i.e., one group of service members receive treatment before the clinician is trained in CRP [TAU condition], and the other group receives treatment after the clinician has completed CRP training [CRP condition]).

The SW-CRT design aligns optimally with the study aims and offers several advantages. First, all providers eventually receive formal training in an evidence-based intervention (CRP). Providers also function as their own control, as each group of providers delivers TAU with service members before receiving CRP training, after which they implement CRP. Another key advantage is that the CRP training schedule approximates the way in which interventions are typically implemented in healthcare systems, enhancing generalizability to actual clinical practice. The naturalistic features of this pragmatic trial design prevent disruption to patient care while also providing the means for robust evaluation of the effectiveness of CRP [24]. The SW-CRT provides an ideal design for this study given that there is evidence supporting the effectiveness of CRP [10,11], but this treatment has yet to be evaluated at the service-delivery level [24].

Treatments

Crisis Response Planning (CRP). CRP is a brief, single-session psychotherapeutic intervention that can be provided to patients at risk of suicidal behavior. In CRP, a provider collaborates with the patient to (1) conduct a narrative assessment of the events preceding suicidal thoughts or behavior, and (2) develop a personalized plan for identifying and managing distress that could escalate to a suicide attempt. The personalized CRP document, which is typically handwritten by the patient on an index card, includes personal warning signs for an acute crisis, self-management strategies to regulate emotional distress, reasons for living, sources of social support, and professional (psychological/medical) crisis services.

Providers working in the ED and the psychiatric inpatient unit participate in a 4-h, standardized CRP training featuring a didactic section that reviews relevant theory, empirical evidence, and core competencies for the care of patients at risk for suicide, and a description of CRP procedures with demonstration videos and facilitated discussion. The training can be flexibly administered either in person or remotely as circumstances dictate and is conducted by qualified CRP trainers. Following the training workshop, providers have access to CRP “champions” who offer to consult, review cases, problem-solve barriers to implementation, and reinforce treatment fidelity. Providers also receive a small booklet that provides ready reference and review of the CRP process. The booklet includes summaries of key topics from the training, “tips” based on actual provider experiences, lessons learned, frequently asked questions, sample entries, and a self-assessment (i.e., fidelity) checklist. Index cards for patients to write their crisis response plan on are supplied to providers along with booklets.

Treatment as usual (TAU). Existing clinical practices in the NMCSD ED include the following elements recommended in the VA/DoD suicide risk CPG [13]: (1) all patients are screened for suicidal ideation at every visit; (2) for those with positive screens, a suicide risk assessment interview is conducted by a mental health professional; (3) a safety planning form with means restriction (such as the Stanley-Brown [27]) is completed; and (4) patients are referred for follow-up mental health treatment as needed. Other elements of TAU could include behavioral and psychotropic interventions, referrals to specialty mental health care, and admission for psychiatric inpatient care.

Treatment fidelity

Members of the Augmenting Suicide Prevention Interventions for Service Members (ASPIS) Training and Fidelity Core affiliated with The Ohio State University will train clinicians to administer the study interventions with fidelity, support follow-up consultation and supervision to maximize fidelity, and assess fidelity for clinical staff. Members of the Training and Fidelity Core have been trained and certified by the intervention developers and have extensive experience conducting training workshops and providing supervision to clinicians learning to use these interventions.

Treatment fidelity for this study will be calculated for approximately 10–20 % of participants receiving CRP, consistent with standards in the field. Fidelity will be monitored via chart review using published scoring systems that mirror the fidelity monitoring checklists used in randomized clinical trials (RCTs) and contained in the CRP pocket booklet [10].

Assessments

Consenting service members complete pretreatment self-report assessments using the secure, electronic survey platform REDCap. This initial assessment is conducted on a computer or study phone in either the ED or psychiatric inpatient unit at NMCSD. Study staff are available if there are any concerns or questions during the initial assessment. Participants do not receive compensation for the initial assessment because it is completed during duty hours.

Self-reported follow-up assessments are completed using the REDCap platform at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months posttreatment. Participants receive a notification via email and text message with a link to complete each follow-up assessment, which can be completed on a device and location of the participant's choosing. This approach permits follow-up assessments to be completed while service member participants are off duty, which is a DoD requirement for receipt of study compensation. Participants are eligible for a $25 Amazon gift card code for each follow-up assessment completed during off-duty hours. Follow-up data are collected remotely to reduce attrition associated with the mobility of military participants (e.g., permanent change of station, deployment) and/or changes in military status that could restrict participants' access to military installations (e.g., separation, discharge). Follow-up assessments prioritize the collection of primary and secondary outcomes over other variables and outcomes less central to project aims, mitigating missing data for the most important outcome variables.

The various projects conducted through the ASPIS Consortium will collect Common Data Elements (CDEs) from enrolled participants using a standardized assessment schedule (i.e., pretreatment, and 3, 6, 9, and 12 months posttreatment). This approach enables data harmonization across studies, enhances data quality, and strengthens statistical power. Three types of CDEs will be collected (see Table 1): (1) suicide-related outcomes, (2) theory-specific variables, and (3) covariates. Consistent with National Research Action Plan recommendations [28], CDEs were selected to align with studies previously funded by DoD, which will facilitate future sharing of data, collaboration, and advancement of suicide prevention research. In addition to the CDEs, each project can opt to include additional measures. Suicide-related outcomes

The primary outcome of this study is suicide attempt and other suicidal behaviors (e.g., preparatory behaviors, interrupted and aborted suicide attempts) as assessed with the Self-Injurious Thoughts and Behaviors Interview–Revised (SITBI-R [29]). For each thought or behavior endorsed on the SITBI-R, additional questions are asked to garner contextual information. The SITBI-R demonstrates strong psychometrics for both validity and reliability as a measure of self-injurious outcomes, including when administered in an online format as in the current trial [29]. The secondary outcome is severity of suicidal ideation, assessed with the 19-item Scale for Suicide Ideation (SSI [30]). The SSI measures the severity of suicidal thoughts, plans, urges, and behaviors in the past week on a 3-point ordinal scale. The first 5 items are completed, and the remaining items are asked if a participant endorses active or passive suicidal ideation. Items are summed to yield a total score, with higher scores reflecting more severe suicidal ideation. The SSI is a well-validated, reliable measure that also displays strong psychometrics when delivered via self-report [31,32].

Theory-specific variables

Process and mechanism variables were selected to reflect variables hypothesized by three prominent models of suicide: interpersonal-psychological, cognitive, and neuroscientific. Perceived burdensomeness and thwarted belonging are assessed using an abbreviated version of the Interpersonal Needs Questionnaire (INQ; [33]), which has been developed and validated by the Military Suicide Research Consortium (MSRC [34]). Each item on the INQ is rated on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (not at all true for me) to 7 (very true for me). Hopelessness is evaluated using an abbreviated version of the Beck Hopelessness Scale (BHS; [35]), also validated by the MSRC [34]. Each item on the BHS is answered using a true/false format, with higher scores indicating greater hopelessness. Suicidal beliefs are examined using the Brief Suicide Cognitions Scale (BSCS; [36]). The BSCS includes six items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). Total scores range from 6 to 30, with higher scores reflecting more severe suicidal beliefs. Emotion regulation is measured using the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale-Short Form (DERS-SF; [37]), a self-report instrument with excellent psychometric properties. The DERS-SF contains 18 items rated on a 5-point scale, which comprise six subscales (lack of emotional awareness; lack of emotional clarity; limited access to emotion regulation strategies; difficulties engaging in goal-directed behavior; nonacceptance of emotional responses; impulse control difficulties). Impulsivity is determined using the Monetary Choice Questionnaire (MCQ; [38]), a validated, 27-item measure. In completing the MCQ, participants choose between two options: a larger, delayed sum of money versus a smaller, immediate sum of money. Reinforcement sensitivity is assessed using the widely used behavioral inhibition system and behavioral activation system (BIS/BAS) scales [39], which comprise a self-report measure of avoidance and approach tendencies.

Covariates

A standardized demographics form is used to collect data on sex assigned at birth, gender, age, race, and military-specific variables (e.g., rank, branch of service). Data on mental health treatment utilization is collected using the Posttreatment Health Interview, a researcher-developed instrument previously used in DoD-funded studies. The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System computer adaptive tests (PROMISCATs; [40]) are used to evaluate quality of life across seven domains (physical function; anxiety; depression; fatigue; sleep disturbance; social function; pain interference). Computer adaptive tests have shown precise measurement, with scores reported as T scores (M = 50, SD = 10) for each of the seven domains.

Psychological symptoms

In addition to the CDEs, self-reported symptom measures commonly used in the Military Health System (MHS) are included at each study assessment point. PTSD symptom severity is assessed with the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; [41]), which corresponds to PTSD criteria in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5; [42]) Each of the 20 items is rated on a scale from 0 to 4. Depression symptom severity is evaluated with the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; [43]), and each of the nine items are rated on a 0 to 3 scale. Anxiety symptom severity is examined using the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder screener (GAD-7; [44]), with each item rated from 0 to 3. The AUDIT-C [45] is a validated, 3-item screener for heavy drinking and probable alcohol use disorder, with items endorsed on a 0 to 4 scale. For the PCL-5, PHQ-9, GAD-7, and AUDIT-C, higher scores reflect greater symptom severity. In addition to symptom measures, the Moral Injury and Distress Scale (MIDS; [46]) is included at each assessment and evaluates distress regarding potentially morally injurious events. The MIDS first asks participants to identify potentially morally injurious events, then report the degree to which morally injurious sequelae has affected them in the past month. Participants rate 18 statements on a scale from 0 to 4. Items are then summed, with higher scores indicating greater severity of moral distress. The MIDS has demonstrated strong psychometric properties [46].

Archival data

In addition to assessment measures, medical record data will be extracted from the MHS Data Repository (MDR). For each participant, these data will include medical visit information (visit dates, diagnostic codes, and procedure codes) for the year preceding their study enrollment. MDR data will be linked to study data using participants’ DoD ID and then coded once data are merged. Archival data sources will be used to examine potential patient-level moderators of treatment effectiveness.

Qualitative questionnaire

To complement quantitative participant data, an implementation substudy will be conducted with providers delivering CRP. This substudy will feature a qualitative questionnaire to assess providers’ experiences using CRP, perceived benefits of or difficulties with providing the CRP intervention, and perceived facilitators of and barriers to implementing CRP training and to using this intervention within military ED and psychiatric inpatient settings. Qualitative questionnaire content will be based on items used in similar previous efforts [47,48].

Data analysis

A power analysis was conducted to determine the sample size needed for proposed primary study analyses. In this high-risk patient population, the expected base rate for suicide attempts at 12 months after the index visit is 25 % [12,49]. Using an analytic formula for stepped-wedge trials [50], the design effect for our study is 2.27, based on the intraclass correlation (0.1), number of trainings (5), number of providers per training (8), number of baseline months for control implementation (4), length of each step (4 months), and average number of subjects enrolled per month per provider (0.75). With up to 700 total participants (350 per treatment condition), this gives an effective sample size of 154 participants per arm. This sample size provides 80 % power to test a TAU sample percentage of 25 % versus an alternative of 12.5 % (α = 0.05, two-sided alternative) assuming an estimated 15 % dropout. For the secondary outcome of suicidal ideation, this sample size provides 80 % power (α = 0.05) to detect a standardized mean difference at the final measurement of 0.35.

This trial will follow Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for stepped-wedge cluster randomized trials [51], with analysis of primary (suicide attempts and other behaviors) and secondary (suicidal ideation) outcomes using mixed models, consistent with prior recommendations for these designs [25]. Mixed models naturally account for outcome data that are missing at random, and multiple imputation methods can account for pretreatment covariates with missing values [52]. The index of local sensitivity to non-ignorability will assess potential for non-random dropout (e.g., whether illness severity portends earlier dropout [53]).

The primary aim, evaluating whether CRP reduces suicidal behaviors relative to TAU, will be tested using a Wald or likelihood ratio test. Summary statistics for outcome variables in each group over time will demonstrate any time or seasonal trends in the data. The secondary aim, identifying any service member, provider, and contextual factors that moderate treatment effects on suicidal behaviors and severity of suicidal ideation, will be evaluated using interaction terms between each characteristic and the intervention. Moderation analysis will further disaggregate primary outcome data by patient, provider, and contextual characteristics to illuminate potential interactions between characteristics and the intervention. Data analysis for the implementation substudy will involve calculating descriptive statistics for quantitative variables and using Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA; [54]) to analyze qualitative data. RTA involves several phases: data familiarization; code generation; generation, reviewing, defining and naming themes; and reporting. These phases are not conducted in a linear fashion but may be repeated as needed to thoroughly understand and characterize participant response. At least two trained study coders will independently use RTA to analyze the qualitative data. They will then collaboratively develop and refine a final set of themes that incorporates coders’ original themes.

Study considerations

Risk mitigation

Given the high-risk nature of this population, a primary implementation concern was systematically assessing and maintaining the safety of participants. Across studies through the ASPIS Consortium, an item from the SSI [30] that asks, “In the last week, how certain have you felt that you would make a suicide attempt” is used to monitor or “flag” at-risk responses through the follow-up periods. More specifically, if a participant indicates, “I am sure that I shall make a suicide attempt,” Core staff and study teams receive an email notification that a flag has been triggered and the participant should be contacted for further evaluation. While a flag indicates elevated risk, it does not specifically identify a participant as meeting criteria for an involuntary hold (i.e., presence of serious and imminent self-harm with intent and plan to take one's life). Given the acute setting for this study, the team also flags responses at the pretreatment assessment. Since responses are flagged immediately, this allows for onsite research staff to further assess the participant and inquire about the intended treatment plan from appropriate staff members (e.g., nurses, attending physician).

When a participant response is flagged at follow-up assessment, a CRP-trained clinician attempts to contact the participant by phone as soon as feasible within a 24-h time period following the notification. The intention of the contact is to conduct a suicide risk assessment and facilitate any necessary emergency care services. Additionally, the contact is designed to offer the participant refinement of their existing safety plan without implementing CRP (if the participant is not already assigned to the experimental condition) or provide additional refinement of the existing CRP (if in the experimental condition). In these infrequent instances, particular care is taken by study staff to prevent treatment contamination while also maintaining participant safety. Flag notifications and action taken by the team members are documented in study files.

Pragmatic trial feasibility and implementation

Given that this pragmatic trial involves care settings within an actual military hospital, careful consideration was given to balancing the feasibility of conducting this study and implementing CRP with minimizing the burden and disruption to patients, staff, and workflow in the acute care settings. Several strategies were employed to preempt issues in conducting this trial within the acute care settings, whereas others were developed in response to challenges that emerged. A primary strategy to enhance study feasibility and CRP implementation has been to embed research staff within the ED and psychiatric inpatient units. This approach offers several advantages, such as facilitating communication, establishing working relationships with clinic staff, cultivating study buy-in, accessing potential participants and necessary resources (e.g., computer access, rooms), and understanding the clinical workflow in the respective units, all of which help to balance clinical research and care provision.

Similarly, CRP-trained study staff serve as “champions” for clinic providers by offering case consultation, reinforcing treatment fidelity, and addressing barriers to implementation, all on an ad hoc basis to conveniently provide clinical support when needed. For example, although CRP is a brief intervention (approximately one 30–60 min session), military providers expressed concern about the time needed to deliver the intervention. Frequently cited barriers among providers for delivering CRP included reduced time for patient encounters due to a large census (i.e., number of patients) and required clinical documentation and procedures. CRP could require additional provider time engaging with the service member than TAU and might present a barrier to implementation; however, current evidence supports CRP as resulting in significantly fewer suicidal behaviors in the months following the intervention [[10], [11], [12]]. The use of CRP could therefore lead to greater downstream efficiency, time savings, and cost effectiveness. Consultation with providers about the potential long-term benefit (e.g., reductions in future attempts, hospitalizations, and self-harm) has helped address this barrier to CRP implementation that emerged during the study.

Another valuable strategy for conducting this pragmatic trial involves the sustained and bidirectional exchanges of information as part of this study among key stakeholder groups: (a) research investigators and staff conducting the study, (b) clinicians at the MTF providing care to service members, and (c) military leadership assisting in study implementation and overseeing clinical care at the hospital. This communication has facilitated the timely knowledge transfer among these groups that has optimized study implementation and efficiency while mitigating disruption to patient care. This includes, for example, ongoing assessment of any practical barriers faced by clinical staff in the acute care settings, and once identified, collaborative problem-solving to address them. Sustained and bidirectional communication about the study to leadership is especially important for conducting research in military settings given that service members in leadership positions are typically in their assigned role for approximately 2–4 years. This has obvious implications for longitudinal research, which can exceed this time period. Leadership may turn over multiple times during the course of a study, so ongoing communication between the study staff and clinical leadership is necessary to keep everyone apprised of the status and to maintain support for study collaboration, problem-solving, and implementation. Importantly, this line of communication is facilitated by embedded study staff, as their presence in the acute care settings affords them greater awareness of study progress, practical barriers, and staffing and leadership changes.

Discussion

Suicide remains a serious issue among service members, and efforts that bridge research and clinical practice may be the most efficient approach to mitigating this problem. Although CRP is effective in decreasing suicidal thoughts and behaviors compared with TAU, few studies examining the effectiveness of CRP have been conducted and they have been mostly limited to outpatient military treatment settings. Additional research is needed to replicate and extend this evidence base to emergency medical and psychiatric inpatient settings. The current pragmatic, randomized trial seeks to accomplish this by using a large sample size and real-world providers representing a diversity of professional disciplines, with care delivered in acute care settings within an MTF. A substudy is also included to elicit feedback from CRP-trained providers regarding facilitators of and barriers to implementation, which can inform the delivery of CRP in acute care settings. This multifaceted study involves implementing CRP and comparing it with TAU while simultaneously evaluating its effects at the service-delivery level. This design affords the opportunity to simultaneously train providers and enhance clinical knowledge to improve care delivered to service members at risk for suicide.

This study has many strengths, one of which is that it is conducted through a consortium, allowing for research collaboration and the merging of data from CDEs across studies, and consequently, the potential for considerable advances in suicide prevention. Another strength of this pragmatic trial is the SW-CRT design, which offers methodological rigor while accounting for the way care is delivered in clinical settings. As a pragmatic trial, there are minimal exclusion criteria and no waitlist or delayed treatment condition, so service members minimally receive TAU, or the standard of care delivered in acute care settings. Treatments are provided by real-world providers across disciplines, such as psychiatrists, psychologists, social workers, residents, interns, and corpsmen. These aspects enhance the external validity and generalizability of study findings. Study data are collected longitudinally over the course of 1 year, allowing for the evaluation of longer-term treatment effects. Furthermore, study data from the large sample of service member participants will be linked with medical record data to investigate moderators of CRP treatment response. Study data include quantitative data from participants and providers as well as qualitative data from CRP-trained providers regarding facilitators of and barriers to the implementation and delivery of CRP. Taken together, the pragmatic trial design balances scientific rigor with practical care delivery to expand understanding of treatment approaches to address suicidality among service members in acute care settings.

The study also has several limitations. Although this is a pragmatic trial of service members in a military treatment setting, generalizability warrants consideration because results may not extend beyond this population or the MHS. While the 1-year follow-up period is a strength for evaluating longer-term effectiveness of CRP, it may also be a limitation in that attrition could be high because of the transient nature of service members due to changing duty stations, deployments, or leaving military service. To mitigate this, study leadership has accordingly implemented several ways of contacting participants and through multiple means (i.e., email, text message), but attrition could be a concern for service member participants. Although aspects of CRP, such the narrative assessment approach, may be expected to confer greater therapeutic benefit to service members at risk for suicide, it is possible that outcomes between CRP and TAU may be attenuated due to similar safety planning components. Lastly, although there are strengths of the pragmatic nature of the trial and SW-CRT design, there are also limitations associated with effectiveness research. Inclusion and exclusion criteria, for example, are intentionally minimal to recruit a generalizable sample, however this may affect internal validity of the study. Similarly, clinic policies and procedures, and their changes over time, may impact study variables in ways that may not be fully measured.

Conclusions

This study achieves multiple aims, including implementation of an evidence-based treatment for suicidality in a naturalistic clinical setting, training providers in an additional effective clinical tool, assessing outcomes and moderators of CRP response, and evaluating facilitators of and barriers to delivering CRP. The pragmatic trial requires balancing demands of clinical research with those of care provision. Several issues were thoughtfully evaluated prior to study initiation, such as selecting the SW-CRT design, addressing patient safety and risk mitigation, and embedding study staff into the ED and inpatient psychiatry unit. Other issues emerged during the study and accordingly led to collaborative troubleshooting between study staff and clinical providers, such as competing demands for delivering CRP and changes in clinical leadership. These considerations can inform the methodological framework of future research, dissemination, and implementation efforts in acute settings with high-risk populations. Ultimately, this study connects research and clinical practice to provide clinical knowledge for the implementation of brief interventions in military and real-world treatment settings with populations at risk for suicide.
