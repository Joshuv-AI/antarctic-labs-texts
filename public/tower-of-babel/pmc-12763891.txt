Integrated multi-omics profiling for risk stratification in Asians with COPD
Authors: Oliver W. Meldrum, Pei Yee Tiew, Huiying Xu, Dorrain Yanwen Low, Fransiskus Xaverius Ivan, Jayanth Kumar Narayana, Tavleen Kaur Jaggi, Jianhong Ching, Sanjay H. Chotirmall
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComorbidity-based risk stratification in Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) incompletely captures inherent biological heterogeneity, particularly in Asian populations that demonstrate high-risk clinical phenotypes including prior pulmonary tuberculosis. We investigated whether integrated sputum multi-omics could improve risk stratification in an Asian COPD cohort.MethodsWe conducted a prospective, multicenter assessment of N = 56 Asians with established COPD, classified as high- (N = 25; cardiovascular or ex-tuberculosis) or low-risk (N = 31; diabetic or low-comorbidity) based on established co-morbidity phenotyping. Sputum was subjected to mucus analysis (MUC5AC, MUC5B, mucus solids, rheology), metabo-lipidomics (LC-MS/MS) and microbiome assessment (shotgun metagenomics). Multivariate statistics was employed to integrate datasets.ResultsHigh-risk Asian COPD demonstrates abnormal mucus biochemistry characterized by elevated MUC5AC; extensive metabo-lipidomic alterations characterized by dysregulated tryptophan-kynurenine metabolism and lipid remodeling with enrichment of lysophosphatidylcholines and triacylglycerols. Microbial networks are disrupted in high-risk patients, typified by antagonistic interactions driven by K. pneumoniae, H. influenzae and Neisseria spp. Integrative assessment combining all datasets partitioned the cohort into two clusters: SNF 1 (N = 34) and SNF 2 (N = 22), the former representing an unfavorable group characterized by exacerbations, hospitalizations, mucus dysfunction, microbial pathogens and dysregulated metabo-lipidomic pathways. Remarkably, 42% (N = 13 of 31) of the originally classified low risk COPD exhibited the unfavorable SNF 1 endotype, distinguished by more severe exacerbations (hospitalizations), K. pneumoniae and elevated hypoxanthine, creatine, spermine and phosphatidylcholines.ConclusionIntegrative multi-omics profiling of Asian COPD significantly refines clinical risk stratification identifying a novel ‘high-risk’ biological endotype. Substantial proportions of clinically low-risk COPD exhibit this endotype, suggesting that clinical and co-morbidity profiling in Asians with COPD should be supplemented by molecular assessment to ensure accurate risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03440-1.

Background

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality and is characterized by persistent respiratory symptoms, progressive airflow limitation and recurrent exacerbations [1, 2]. In Asia, unique risk factors including climate and infections such as prior pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) add further complexity and are shown to contribute to inflammation and mortality risk [3–5]. These global differences in COPD phenotypes go beyond epidemiology alone, and suggest inimitable risks, mechanisms and likely treatment response. Therefore, studies assessing Asian COPD endophenotypes including comorbidities should be prioritized to optimize its management [6].

While current COPD risk stratification guides treatment, current approaches fail to capture the complex biological heterogeneity underlying inter-individual variability in exposures, outcomes, exacerbation frequency and treatment response [7, 8]. Emerging evidence implicates mucus abnormalities, particularly MUC5AC and mucus hyperconcentration as key drivers of airway obstruction and accelerated lung function decline [9–11]. Altered biophysical properties of mucus further accelerate disease progression, particularly in relation to specific pathogens prevalent in Asian populations such as Klebsiella pneumoniae [9, 12, 13].

Going beyond mucus dysfunction, metabo-lipidomic profiling in COPD reveals perturbations in pathways regulating oxidative stress, inflammation and energy metabolism [14]. For example, COPD following pulmonary TB exhibits distinct tryptophan catabolic signatures, different to that observed in smoking-related COPD [15]. Likewise, cigarette smoke–driven activation of sphingomyelinase elevates ceramide levels in airway epithelial and immune cells, promoting alveolar apoptosis, barrier disruption, and defective phagocytosis, leading to increased bacterial persistence and exacerbations [16]. Parallel investigations of the airway microbiome and metabolome in mild COPD demonstrate shifts in bacterial communities that correlate with disease severity and exacerbations [17]. Importantly, no study to date concurrently integrates microbiome profiles with mucus biochemistry, metabolomic and lipidomic data, especially in the Asian setting, where clinically established high-risk co-morbidity groups have been identified [3].

To improve COPD outcomes in Asia, we need to move beyond conventional clinical markers and capture the complex geographic heterogeneity of this disease [18, 19]. Here, we apply a novel integrated multi-omic framework combining mucus, metabolomic, lipidomic and microbiomes to supplement established risk stratification in an Asian COPD cohort.

Methods

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating hospitals and institutions (Reference: CIRB 2021/2042 and NTU-IRB IRB-2021-02−040). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Subjects & specimen collection

We prospectively recruited patients with COPD at two major tertiary referral hospitals between January 2021 and June 2023 in Singapore: Singapore General Hospital and Tan Tock Seng Hospital. Inclusion criteria included spirometry-confirmed COPD and optimized treatment as per GOLD guidelines and clinical stability—defined as the absence of exacerbations, an acute worsening of respiratory symptoms requiring antibiotics and/or oral corticosteroids in the 4 weeks preceding study inclusion and sampling [2]. Exclusion criteria included a prior history of documented asthma or bronchiectasis or chronic oral corticosteroid use (>5 mg/day for >6 weeks), active malignancy, other immunosuppressive therapy and active mycobacterial disease. Detailed demographic, clinical, and comorbidity data were defined and collected and patients categorized into clinical groups as previously published with diabetic and low-comorbidity groups combined to form a single low-risk category [3]. Medication histories were reviewed, and no participant was receiving long-term macrolides. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) scans were reviewed to identify co-existing bronchiectasis. All patients provided a sputum obtained spontaneously by deep‑cough in the outpatient setting, with the aid of a physiotherapist as required. Samples were immediately placed on ice, transferred to the lab, aliquoted within 1 h and stored at − 80 °C until further experiments.

Mucus analysis

Sputum mucus solids were determined gravimetrically following lyophilization. Individual mucin 5AC (MUC5AC) and mucin 5B (MUC5B) glycoproteins were quantified using stable isotope dilution mass spectrometry using a Triple Quadruple mass spectrometer (QTRAP 6500, Sciex). Rheological properties of sputum were determined using a stress-controlled Haake MARS III rheometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Karlsruhe, Germany) fitted with a 35 mm titanium parallel plate. Rheological properties, including complex viscosity (η*), elastic modulus (G’), viscous modulus (G’’), and tanδ were measured using a narrow-gap (40 μm) parallel plate rheometer via frequency sweeps (1–100 rad/s), with values reported at 10 rad/s. Metabolomics and lipidomics: Sputum samples underwent sequential extraction with ethanol and isopropanol following viscosity reduction with dithiothreitol (DTT). Metabolites and lipids were analyzed using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC coupled with a Waters Xevo TQ-S triple quadrupole mass spectrometer operated in multiple-reaction-monitoring (MRM) mode to simultaneously quantify 26 compound classes, covering 14 small molecules and 12 lipid classes. Shotgun metagenomics: DNA extracted from sputum was sequenced using Illumina HiSeq 2500 with 250-bp paired-end reads. Following quality control and human genome removal, taxonomic profiling was performed using MetaPhlAn4, functional profiling with HUMAnN2, and antibiotic resistance gene identification using ShortBRED. Statistical integration: Similarity Network Fusion (SNF) was employed to integrate normalized data matrices from all omics platforms. Network analysis was performed using Cytoscape with centrality metrics calculated to identify key taxa. Comprehensive methodological details are provided in Additional File 1.

Results

Study population

Fifty-six Asians with COPD (96% male; mean age, 70.3 ± 8.5 years) were prospectively enrolled and stratified into four previously published [3] Asian COPD risk groups that differ based on inflammation and mortality risk, namely prior tuberculosis (Ex TB, N = 14), cardiovascular (CVS; N = 11), diabetic (N = 8), and low-comorbidity (N = 23) (Table 1). For further assessment, we grouped these into “high-risk” (i.e., Ex-TB and CVS; N = 25) and “low-risk” (diabetic and low-comorbidity; N = 31) categories (Table 2). Comparisons between groups revealed that “high-risk” COPD had significantly higher exacerbation frequency. For all downstream analyses we combined diabetic and low-comorbidity groups into a single low-risk category (N = 31). No participant met radiological criteria for bronchiectasis.

Biochemical but not biophysical mucus properties differ between high- and low-risk Asians with COPD

Measurements of mucus hydration and mucin content indicated that high risk COPD (i.e., Ex-TB and CVS) demonstrated significantly higher mucus solids (P < 0.05; Fig. 1A) and MUC5AC concentrations (P < 0.05; Fig. 1B) versus the low-risk group, while MUC5B concentrations appeared comparable across all groups (Fig. 1C). Consequently, MUC5AC-to-MUC5B ratio was highest in the high-risk groups (P < 0.05; Fig. 1D). In contrast, biophysical (rheological) measures including complex viscosity (η*; Fig. 1E), its elastic (Gʹ) and viscous (Gʺ) components (Fig. 1F and G), and their ratio, tanδ (Fig. 1H), revealed no significant alteration between groups.

Sputum metabolomic profiles differ between high- and low-risk Asians with COPD

Metabolomic profiling of sputum demonstrates distinct profiles in the Ex-TB, CVS and low-risk groups, respectively (Fig. 2A; Table S2). Clear separation between the Ex-TB and low-risk (Fig. 2B; PERMANOVA, P < 0.001), CVS and low-risk (Fig. 2C; P < 0.001) and Ex-TB and CVS (Figure S2A; P < 0.004) groups were identified by supervised Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA). To interrogate specific metabolites driving such differences, we employed Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) analysis (Fig. 2D; Figure E2B; Table S3) which revealed that the high-risk Ex-TB group had significantly lower sputum methionine, kyneureine and tryptophan compared to the low-risk group. In contrast, metabolic changes in the high-risk CVS group were characterized by significantly lower sputum choline, spermine, betaine, and indole. Head-to-head comparison between the high-risk groups (Ex-TB and CVS) showed further reductions in methionine, ADMA and kynurenine in the Ex-TB group (Figure S2B). Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted major disruption to tryptophan metabolism in Ex-TB and linoleic acid metabolism in both high-risk groups (Fig. 2E and Figure S2C), further confirmed by comparing individual metabolite fold‑change. The Ex‑TB group exhibited the largest depletions in methionine, kynurenine, tryptophan and ADMA while the CVS group revealed modest decreases in trigonelline, indole, betaine, spermine and choline (Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) adjusted P < 0.05) (Fig. 2F).

Sputum lipidomic profiles differ between high- and low-risk Asians with COPD

Aggregated lipid class data, including acylcarnitines and phosphatidylcholines reveal that the CVS group had higher overall lipid levels compared to either Ex-TB or low-risk groups (Fig. 3A; Figure S3A; Table S4). Subsequent PLS-DA confirmed significant group separation between Ex-TB and low-risk (Figs. 3B; PERMANOVA, P < 0.001), CVS and low-risk (Figs. 3 C; PERMANOVA, P < 0.001), and between both high-risk groups (Figure S3B; PERMANOVA, P < 0.03). Phosphatidylcholines emerged as major discriminators with lysophosphatidylcholine (LPS) species, including LPC 20:4, LPC 18:2, and LPC 18:1 ranking highest in CVS versus the low-risk group (Fig. 3D; Table S5). Comparisons between Ex-TB and low-risk groups reveal lower VIP scores, with certain phosphatidylcholines and sphingomyelin species, including PC O-44:4, PC 36:6 and SM 33:1 decreased in the Ex-TB group (Fig. 3D). Direct comparisons of lipid profiles between the high-risk groups (i.e., Ex-TB versus CVS) reveal additional lipid species of importance, including triacylglycerol (TG) 18:0/36:4, phosphatidylcholines (PC) O-28:0 and PC O-34:3 all elevated in CVS (Figure S3C). To assess if medication confounded observed multi-omic differences, we compared ICS and mucolytic use between clinical groups. No significant differences were observed in medication distribution (all Fisher’s exact P > 0.24; Table 2), indicating comparable medication exposure across groups. PERMANOVA analyses adjusting for ICS and mucolytic use as covariates confirmed that group differences in metabolomic and lipidomic profiles between Ex-TB and low-risk groups remained statistically significant (metabolomics: adjusted P = 0.014; lipidomics: adjusted P = 0.002), demonstrating that observed multi-omic differences are independent of these medications.

Microbial networks differ in high‑risk Asians with COPD

Having identified mucus and metabo-lipidomic differences in high-risk Asians with COPD, we next investigated for alterations to airway microbiome profiles given the links between microbial communities, mucus and metabo-lipidomic dysfunction in airway disease [9, 14, 17, 20]. Taxonomic composition showed broadly similar community structure (Fig. 4A) with no significant differences in Shannon (α)‑diversity, except for the modestly higher diversity in the Ex-TB group versus low-risk individuals (P < 0.05; Figure S4A), with no significant difference in β-diversity between groups (Figure S4B-S4D). Having detected no substantial taxonomic differences between groups, we next assessed microbial association networks (Fig. 4B and D) [21]. Interestingly, microbial interactomes reveal greater occurrence of antagonistic (negative) interactions between microbes in both high-risk groups (i.e., Ex-TB: 34% and CVS: 37%) versus the low-risk group (22%). Within each network, microbes were classified as busy (high degree centrality - number of direct interactions), critical (high stress centrality - importance for network integrity) and influential (high betweenness centrality - control over information flow, including indirect effects). These metrics (Table S6) demonstrate that Klebsiella (degree: CVS 14, Ex-TB 10 vs. low-risk 7; stress: CVS 70, Ex-TB 28 vs. low-risk 18), Haemophilus (degree: Ex-TB 18 vs. CVS 11, low-risk 9; stress: Ex-TB 102 vs. CVS 46, low-risk 40), and Neisseria spp. (degree: CVS 15, Ex-TB 14 vs. low-risk 10; stress: CVS 94, Ex-TB 86 vs. low-risk 44) exhibit higher busyness and criticality in high-risk Asian COPD (Fig. 4B and D). Analysis of negative extraction controls (n = 3) and sequencing blanks (n = 3) demonstrated minimal background contamination (Figure S1). Extraction blanks yielded 100-fold lower read counts than patient samples, while sequencing blanks yielded 10,000-fold lower reads. Taxonomic profiling revealed that negative controls consisted primarily of environmental contaminants (e.g., Ralstonia, Bradyrhizobium). Critically, no pathogenic taxa of clinical interest (Klebsiella, Haemophilus, Moraxella) were detected in negative controls, validating our patient-derived microbiome profiles.

Integrated assessment of mucus-microbiome-metabo-lipidomic profiles reveal two risk groups

Having identified individual differences in mucus, microbiome and metabo-lipidomic profiles between groups, we next assessed if holistic data integration provides additional insight to improve patient risk stratification. Employing Similarity Network Fusion (SNF), our cohort was partitioned into two robust clusters (silhouette score: 0.78): SNF Cluster 1 (purple; N = 34) and SNF Cluster 2 (orange; N = 22) (Fig. 5A). Patients in SNF Cluster 1 exhibit higher exacerbation frequency (Fig. 5B; P < 0.05) despite similar lung function (as FEV1% predicted, Fig. 5C) and symptoms (as CAT score, Fig. 5D) compared to SNF cluster 2. Mucus parameters, including rheology, did not reveal significant differences between clusters, however, Cluster 1 demonstrates significantly higher MUC5AC concentrations (Figure E5). Taxonomic profiling reveal enrichment of pathogens, including Klebsiella pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and Neisseria spp. in SNF Cluster 1 (BH adjusted P < 0.01), whilst SNF Cluster 2 was characterized by commensals, including Streptococcus salivarius, Veillonella atypica, and Gemella Sanguinis (Fig. 5E; BH adjusted P < 0.01). Functional pathway analysis reveals that SNF Cluster 1 is enriched for amine, polyamine and isoprenoid biosynthesis, validated by our prior metabolomics experiments (Fig. 5F; BH adjusted P < 0.01). SNF Cluster 1 harbors significantly higher relative abundance of multidrug and peptide-antibiotic resistance gene classes (Fig. 5G; BH adjusted P < 0.01) while metabolic profiling separated clusters through spermine and xanthine metabolism (Fig. 5H and I; Table S7). Lipidomic data indicates phosphatidylcholines as a major differentiator between clusters (Fig. 5J and K; Table S8).

Comparing the original Asian COPD clinical risk groups (Table 2) against the SNF clusters reveals that 84% of high-risk (i.e., N = 21 out of 25) and 42% of low-risk (N = 13 out of 31) were assigned to Cluster 1 (Fig. 6A-B). To understand why a subset of low-risk patients co-segregated with this “high-risk” cluster, we re-examined the low-risk cohort by SNF assignment. This revealed no differences in exacerbation frequency, lung function (as FEV₁ % predicted) or symptoms (as CAT score) between low-risk patients assigned between SNF clusters (Figure S6A). Importantly, however, low risk individuals within SNF Cluster 1 experienced higher hospitalization rates (Figure S6A). Similarly, sputum mucus chemistry—percent solids, MUC5AC, MUC5B, and the MUC5AC/MUC5B ratio—did not differ by cluster (Figure S6B). In contrast, MaAsLin2 analysis revealed that these individuals in SNF Cluster 1 had significantly more Klebsiella pneumoniae, an established pathogen in the airways of patients with COPD and bronchiectasis (Figure S6C) [22, 23], while metabo-lipidomics differences were marked by elevated hypoxanthine, creatine, spermine and phosphatidylcholines (Figure S6D; Tables S9–S10). To assess discriminative ability and identify overfitting, we performed permutation testing (n = 1000 permutations) for all PLS-DA models (Table S11). Ex-TB versus low-risk comparisons achieved significance in both metabolomics (P = 0.03) and lipidomics (P = 0.019), as did CVS versus low-risk comparisons (P = 0.027 and P = 0.049, respectively), though elevated error rates (27–33%) suggest modest discriminative power. Ex-TB versus CVS comparisons did not reach significance (P = 0.457–0.612), consistent with 84% of both groups co-segregating into the same SNF endotype. SNF-derived clusters demonstrated superior discrimination (all P < 0.001; error rates 2–29%), indicating multi-omic integration provides more robust risk stratification than individual molecular platforms.

Discussion

Here, we apply a novel, integrative, multi-omic framework to refine COPD risk stratification in an Asian cohort, revealing a distinct ‘high-risk’ biological endotype, imperceptible through conventional clinical approaches. In prior work, we identify cardiovascular and ex-TB clinical phenotypes as ‘high-risk’ in Asians with COPD [3]. These groups demonstrate higher mortality, significant inflammation, and complex cytokine networks. Here, we extend these findings to illustrate that they also exhibit significant mucus hyper concentration, increased MUC5AC, disrupted metabo-lipidomic pathways and dysbiotic microbial networks. Through an integrative assessment combining mucus, metabolomics, lipidomics and microbiomics, we further reveal a distinct biological endotype (i.e., SNF Cluster 1), characterized by frequent exacerbations, hospitalizations, mucus dysfunction, microbial pathogens and dysregulated spermine, hypoxanthine and phosphatidylcholine pathways. This endotype interestingly included a significant number of individuals previously classified as ‘low-risk’ by clinical criterion alone. Supplementary analyses reveal that these presumed clinically ‘low-risk’ individuals demonstrate more severe rather than frequent exacerbations (i.e., higher hospitalization rates) and are characterized by elevated Klebsiella pneumoniae. These findings attest that clinical stratification alone remains inadequate, underestimating true COPD severity in some Asians, corroborating the value of molecular profiling and multi-omics through an integrative approach. The Ex-TB phenotype may be particularly relevant in regions with endemic tuberculosis, while cardiovascular-associated COPD is a globally prevalent comorbidity.

Our finding that elevated mucus solids and higher MUC5AC concentrations occur in high-risk COPD aligns with prior studies which show mucus hyperconcentration as a central driver of disease progression and exacerbations in COPD [9, 11]. Interestingly, despite these significant biochemical differences, we observed no major alterations in biophysical (rheological) parameters. This apparent discordance warrants careful biophysical interpretation, as mucus rheology is determined not solely by mucin concentration but by the interplay of mucin polymer size, cross-linking density, hydration state, ionic environment, and non-mucin components [24, 25]. Several mechanisms may explain this discordance: first, adequate hydration can prevent gel formation despite elevated mucin levels, as mucus rheology becomes concentration-dependent only above critical gelation thresholds [26, 27]. Second, the MUC5AC: MUC5B ratio, rather than absolute MUC5AC concentration, may be more determinant of viscoelastic properties, as these mucins differ fundamentally in polymerization and cross-linking capacity [28]. Third, inflammatory mediators, proteases, or bacterial products prevalent in high-risk COPD may modify mucin polymer structure through proteolytic cleavage or altered disulfide bonding, reducing cross-linking efficiency despite elevated concentrations [29]. Our oscillatory rheology measurements at 10 rad/s (1.6 Hz) capture bulk viscoelastic behavior at a physiologically relevant frequency; however, micro-scale rheological heterogeneity or alterations at different frequency ranges warrant future investigation using complementary techniques such as particle-tracking microrheology [30]. Although several studies have previously emphasized MUC5AC as a marker of muco-obstructive lung disease [10], our findings extend these observations to an Asian population enriched for ex-tuberculosis phenotypes [3, 12, 31].

Sputum metabolomics reveal notable disruptions in the tryptophan-kynurenine- nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) biosynthesis axis, particularly in the Ex-TB group. Such metabolic dysbiosis is associated with increased exacerbation susceptibility, elevated oxidative stress and inflammation [32, 33]. However, our cross-sectional design precludes determination of causality—oxidative stress and inflammation may drive metabolic dysbiosis, microbial metabolic products may promote oxidative stress, or these may represent parallel processes with shared upstream drivers. Recent mechanistic studies further underscore NAD+ metabolism as a key regulator of epithelial inflammation and susceptibility to bacterial infection, through NAD+ salvage enzyme depletion (e.g., NMNAT1), reducing cellular NAD+ availability and impairing antimicrobial defense [34]. Consequently, direct NAD⁺ supplementation has demonstrated suppression of IL-8 in COPD, reinforcing the therapeutic potential of metabolic repletion strategies to attenuate infection‑induced exacerbations [35]. Our data further align with the distinct cytokine signatures reported in smoking-related (i.e., elevated IL-1RA, IL-1β, IL-17, GRO and sCD40L) and post-TB COPD (i.e., elevated IL-1α, IL-6, TNF-α and IL-8) suggestive that phenotype-specific metabolic-inflammatory crosstalk contributes to COPD severity [36].

Our observations of tryptophan-kynurenine pathway disruption in Asian COPD align with recent multi-omic investigations by Yan et al. in Chinese patients with COPD [37]. Their integrated sputum metagenomics, metabolomics, transcriptomics and proteomics identified depleted tryptophan catabolism and reduced indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production by airway lactobacilli, mechanistically linked to neutrophilic inflammation and epithelial apoptosis via IL-22 signaling. The convergent identification of tryptophan metabolic perturbations across independent Singaporean and Chinese cohorts strengthens the biological plausibility that this pathway represents a conserved and potentially targetable feature of Asian COPD pathophysiology.

In cardiovascular-related COPD, lipidomics demonstrates elevated airway lysophosphatidylcholine (i.e., LPC2:40 and LPC18:2) and triacylglycerols (TG14:0/36:3), known to contribute to the development of allergic lung diseases by promoting increased expression of IL-17 and the recruitment of neutrophils under chronic inflammatory stress [38]. With increasing interest in the use of biologics in COPD, these findings may aid patient stratification [39–41]. Moreover, direct comparisons of lipid profiles between CVS and Ex-TB COPD groups highlight specific triacylglycerols (TG 18:0/36:4) and phosphatidylcholine species (PC O-28:0, PC O-34:3) significantly elevated in the former suggestive that certain lipid components may be influenced by cardiovascular comorbidities. Our findings also align with plasma studies in COPD, where Kim et al. show that the plasma sphingomyelins: SM (d18:1/24:0) and SM (d18:1/24:1), are elevated in COPD, while the ceramides: Cer (d18:1/18:0) and Cer (d18:1/24:1), inversely correlate with FEV₁ [33]. Likewise, Bowler et al. demonstrate that five plasma sphingomyelins inversely associate with emphysema severity and that trihexosylceramides may predict frequent exacerbations [42]. In contrast, low‑risk patients maintain more balanced lipid profiles suggesting preserved energy homeostasis and attenuated inflammation. Considered together with our findings, these works reveal a conserved COPD lipidome, characterized by depletion of structural sphingomyelins and enrichment of pro‑inflammatory glycerophospholipids and glycosphingolipids which converge to promote mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress and exacerbation susceptibility.

Shotgun metagenomic profiling identified altered (antagonistic) microbial networks in high-risk COPD. These relationships were dominated by organisms, including K. pneumoniae, H. influenzae and Neisseria spp., all previously implicated in severe or exacerbation-prone respiratory phenotypes in Asian populations [21, 43, 44]. Detailed network metrics reveal that high-risk pathogens such as K. pneumoniae or H. influenzae were busy, critical, and influential in high-risk COPD while commensals such as Gemella and Prevotella exhibit comparable characteristics in the low-risk group. We hypothesize that microbial antagonism, coupled to mucus hyperconcentration and metabo-lipidomic disturbance creates a pro-inflammatory milieu conducive to pathogen persistence [9]. Such phenomena reflect observations in COPD, where disordered metabolism and microbial dysbiosis synergistically promote disease progression [17]. Integration of mucus, metabolic, lipidomic and microbial data uncovered two robust endotypes, transcending the comorbidity-based grouping. Remarkably, 42% (N = 13 of 31) of clinically classified low-risk patients were segregated into the unfavorable endotype which exhibits higher hospitalizations, elevated K. pneumoniae and distinct polyamine (e.g., spermine) signatures. While individual molecular platforms showed variable discriminative ability for pairwise clinical group comparisons with some models demonstrating modest classification performance due to small sample sizes (Supplementary Table S11), SNF integration leveraged complementary information across multiple data streams to achieve superior discrimination (all permutation p < 0.001). Critically, standard isolated clinical or mucus-based metrics alone were unable to detect such individuals, illustrating the value of molecular profiling and multi-omics through an integrative approach. While SNF Cluster 1 overlaps with the clinically defined frequent exacerbator phenotype (70.6%, 24/34 patients), multi-omic integration extends beyond exacerbation frequency. Notably, 29.4% (10/34) of Cluster 1 patients were less frequent exacerbators yet exhibited the same high-risk molecular signature (elevated MUC5AC, K. pneumoniae enrichment, dysregulated metabolism), representing biological risk preceding clinical manifestation. Conversely, 13 frequent exacerbators belonged to Cluster 2, lacking these persistent molecular features. This imperfect concordance (Fisher’s exact P = 0.40) demonstrates that multi-omic profiling identifies underlying mechanisms and pre-clinical high-risk states beyond clinical exacerbation frequency, validating the frequent exacerbator phenotype as a distinct biological endotype. Furthermore, although Ex-TB and CVS groups exhibited phenotype-specific metabolic signatures, permutation testing revealed modest discriminative power between these two high-risk groups (P = 0.457–0.612), consistent with our finding that 84% of both groups co-segregated into the same unfavorable SNF endotype, reinforcing their shared core pathobiology despite distinct comorbidity profiles.

Although Ex-TB and CVS patients share adverse clinical outcomes including elevated exacerbation rates (Table 2), they represent biologically distinct COPD phenotypes warranting separate consideration. Ex-TB demonstrates unique metabolic features, particularly disruption of the tryptophan-kynurenine-NAD + biosynthesis axis, reflecting persistent immunometabolic alterations following mycobacterial infection [32–34]. This phenotype is of particular importance in Asian COPD populations where tuberculosis prevalence remains high. In contrast, CVS-associated COPD exhibits distinct lipidomic signatures characterized by elevated lysophosphatidylcholines and triacylglycerols, suggesting systemic metabolic-inflammatory crosstalk. Direct metabolic and lipidomic comparisons between Ex-TB and CVS groups confirm these phenotype-specific differences.

Microbial pathway analysis confirms the unfavorable endotype as characterized by frequent exacerbations and excess amine and polyamine biosynthesis. Reduction in corresponding spermine is known to orchestrate epithelial damage, recruit immune cells and promote extracellular matrix remodeling, all fundamental processes in COPD pathogenesis and disease progression [45]. Concurrently, raised xanthine and hypoxanthine suggest dysregulated purine catabolism in this unfavorable group, while the opposing cluster displays more effective purine salvage pathways. Such robust convergence of metabolomic, lipidomic, and microbial data lends additional support to the notion of such biological endotypes in Asians, identifying fresh molecular targets for future therapeutic development. Furthermore, antimicrobial resistance gene profiling indicate that the unfavorable endotype harbors significantly higher abundance of multidrug and peptide–antibiotic resistance genes, highlighting an important further layer of pathobiological complexity warranting future attention, akin to the emerging resistotypes in bronchiectasis [46].

Clinically, our study provides an initial basis to consider several therapeutic targets. First, interventions aimed at restoring NAD+ homeostasis or modulating kynurenine pathway enzymes may be associated with lower oxidative stress and its associated inflammatory cascade, important to preventing COPD exacerbations [34]. Second, therapies targeting lipid metabolism, especially those focused on dysregulated fatty acid oxidation or optimization of phospholipid composition may assist in maintaining alveolar and airway barrier integrity. Third, controlling mucus hyperconcentration with mucolytics or emerging mucin-targeting therapies holds significant potential to avoid bacterial entrapment and subsequent exacerbation [47]. Fourth, our mucus–microbiome analyses highlight metabolically driven shifts in community structure and interventions that rebalance mucus chemistry (e.g., modulating mucin O-glycan availability or local pH). These changes may recalibrate microbial ecology and blunt pathogen overgrowth [31]. Finally, microbial network behavior should be considered in designing antibiotic regimes, as high-risk pathogens such as K. pneumoniae flourish in synergy with metabolic disruption [48].

While these multi-omic signatures identify potential therapeutic targets for risk stratification, their direct translation into clinical practice requires rigorous validation. Future studies should employ targeted measurements, including targeted metabolomics, quantitative PCR assays, and specific protein quantification in larger, independent cohorts to validate these discovery-phase findings. These will need follow up clinical trials thereafter.

Despite several novel insights, our study has important limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; longitudinal studies and interventional trials will be essential to establish causality, clinical utility, and temporal stability of these molecular signatures. Our dedicated focus on Asians, while inherent and purposeful in design, limits generalizability to other populations, warranting replication in multi-ethnic and geographically diverse cohorts. Our study cohort was predominantly male (96%), limiting generalisability to Asian females with COPD. While this sex distribution may reflect smoking patterns in older Asian populations, sex-related differences in COPD are well-documented. In Asian populations, women demonstrate distinct clinical presentations with more dyspnea and exacerbations [49, 50], and have different risk factor profiles, with biomass fuel smoke exposure being a major contributor to COPD in Asian women [51]. Detailed statin-exposure data for the CVS subgroup were unavailable, and we cannot fully exclude medication-related influences on observed airway lipid profiles. Finally, exploring additional omics platforms such as proteomics or immune profiling may further refine these early-stage endotype classifications.

Conclusions

In summary, through application of an integrative, multi-omic framework, we refine COPD risk stratification in Asians through identification of a ‘high-risk’ biological endotype, undetectable by clinical assessment. By combining mucus, metabolomics, lipidomics and microbiomics, we reveal that this endophenotype is characterized by exacerbations, hospitalizations, mucus dysfunction, microbial pathogens and dysregulated metabo-lipidomic pathways. Substantial proportions of clinically low-risk individuals were found to exhibit this high risk endophenotype, confirming that clinical stratification in Asian COPD remains inadequate. Through advancing our molecular understanding of COPD heterogeneity in this Asian cohort, these data warrant follow up longitudinal and interventional work that may pave the way toward more precise, personalized management strategies for COPD in populations with similar clinical and environmental risk profiles.
