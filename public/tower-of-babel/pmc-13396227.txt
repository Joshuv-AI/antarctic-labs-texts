Development of 3D printable temperature-sensitive resin
Authors: Daiki Kurihara, Nicholas Slusher, Hirotaka Sakaue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Internal temperature measurements are vital for the development in the industrial, electronic, and aerospace fields for a variety of applications, from heat exchangers to material selection. Specifically, advancements in additive manufacturing and 3D printing have enabled these fields to rapidly prototype and produce complex geometries at much lower costs. However, these designs will require testing and validation via internal temperature measurements before widespread use, which is further complicated by the increased complexity. These internal temperature measurements will ideally have spatial and temporal resolutions to aid calculations such as heat transfer. This study proposes a temperature-sensitive resin (TSR) by combining a commercially available 3D-curable resin with a thermographic luminophore and demonstrates that the TSR layer is temperature sensitive. It is demonstrated that the TSR can be used with a commercially available 3D printer and that testing equipment can measure the internal temperature along a 2D slice of a 3D-printed model by monitoring luminescent output. This temperature-sensitive resin and measurement technique, if further developed, may provide internal temperature measurements with high spatial and temporal resolution for potential use in the development of items such as heat exchangers, microfluidic devices, and aerospace bodies.

Advances in additive manufacturing, especially in the field of 3D printing, have revolutionized many industries and research fields in recent years.1–8 The ability to quickly produce an accurate 3D design with internal geometry without the need for complicated machining is ideal for many applications. These advances are especially relevant to heat exchangers and micro/milli-channel flows, where recent advances have allowed for more complicated internal geometries that increase heat transfer rates and cooling potential.9–14 However, many of these advances rely on comparisons or designs based on numerical simulation, which may require experimental validation and testing.11,15–20 The advances in 3D printing have further increased the need for spatially and temporally resolved temperature measurements. These measurements can be used to study the removal of excess thermal energy via fluid flow, which is vital in many industrial and electronic applications, such as heat exchangers and micro/milli-channel flows.10,21,22,29,30 The widespread use of heat exchangers to transfer thermal energy to keep devices within temperature specifications has been well studied and shows constant advances.20,31 In the field of electronics, improvements in manufacturing processes have allowed for smaller electronic devices, allowing for denser placements. This increase in density has also led to an increase in the heat transfer required to keep components within ideal operating temperatures.32 This heat transfer can be achieved via microchannel flow, allowing coolant circulation within an electronic device.33 For both traditional heat exchangers and micro/milli-channel flows, knowing the temperature distribution around cooling channels is important for knowing the efficiency of local heat transfer and overall design.19,24,29–31,33,34 Therefore, a spatially and temporally resolved measurement is ideal for properly designing and studying these channel flows. However, determining the temperature distribution inside a heat exchanger or microchannel is difficult due to its enclosed nature and often complex channel structure 15,20,34,35.

Installing devices such as thermocouples may be difficult due to manufacturing, and may limit measurements to only several points within the system, and may be too large for microchannel flows20. Additionally, infrared (IR) thermography requires that the temperature distribution be close to the object’s surface or for the IR signal to freely pass through the channel material, which is often unrealistic for studying a target location inside the microchannel device.20 Temperature-sensitive paint (TSP) is a luminescent sensor used to determine spatially and time-resolved surface temperature distribution.36–41 TSP relies on the fundamental photochemistry of each luminescent molecule. When excited by a specific wavelength of light, these molecules absorb the excitation light and subsequently emit light at a different wavelength. For the luminescent molecules used in TSP, the intensity of emitted light is a function of temperature, given by models based on the Arrhenius relation over small temperature ranges or a polynomial fit over wider temperature ranges36. Through calibration, the intensity of emitted light can be related to the temperature at that position. This calibrated TSP system would provide the spatially and temporally resolved temperature measurements required for studying microchannel flow. If the TSP layer can be printed at a target location of the micro/milli-channel device, it only gives the spatiotemporal temperature of the TSP layer. While TSP is ideal for studying micro/milli-channel flow, a conventional approach requires spraying or coating TSP, which is not possible at a target location inside the micro/milli-channel. However, before addressing the micro/milli-channel measurement questions, the means of isolating the TSP around a given structure must be addressed.

This study proposes the development of a temperature-sensitive resin (TSR) that can be printed with a 3D printer. The TSR would follow the same photochemical process as a TSP. By printing the resin into a model that matches the geometry of a heat exchanger or microchannel, it may be possible to achieve spatially and temporally resolved temperature measurements of a system in future work.

Results and discussion

Rhodamine B was chosen as the temperature-sensitive luminescent dye for the study due to its wide-scale use in the field of temperature-sensitive paints.36,38,42,43 Rhodamine B is a modified version of the base Rhodamine structure, with the modification appearing on the carboxylic acid group, which introduces an activated component to the luminescent internal conversion process, making it temperature sensitive.44 The absorption spectrum of rhodamine B is between 450 and 600 nm, with a peak at 550 nm. The emission spectra range is from 550 nm to 650 nm, with a peak emission at 590 nm. Sensors were prepared with and without Rhodamine B mixed with UV-curable resin. As seen in Fig. 3, there are two distinct emission peaks. The peak located at 440 nm is due to the emission from the resin, and the other peak at 600 nm is from the rhodamine B. It is clear that the addition of rhodamine B reduces the 440 nm peak of the resin. This reduction is due to the energy transfer of the resin emission to the rhodamine B absorption and is seen in other luminescent sensors with two components.36 Since the region of interest is in the emission output from rhodamine B, the integration range was taken to be 600 ± 30 nm, which is denoted by the grey shading in Fig. 3. This integration range was used for all results in calibration.

Each sensor was then subjected to a temperature calibration by heating the sensor to a known temperature. Integrating the emission output from 600 ± 30 nm yields the result shown in Fig. 4. Uncertainty of each calibration data was estimated from the standard deviation of the three different sensors of the same concentration, with a mean uncertainty of 4% from the mean value.

All sensors showed a non-linear temperature response below 20 °C. It is considered that the non-linearity was due to the non-linearity of the emission of the resin itself. The TSR sensor without rhodamine B, which corresponds to the temperature calibration at 0.00mM, showed a nonlinear increase in intensity below 20 °C. Even though Fig. 4 shows the emission at wavelengths 600 ± 30 nm, this increase in intensity also occurred across all wavelengths. Since the emission output of the resin overlaps with the absorption of the rhodamine B, the rhodamine B receives additional excitation by the resin emission. The non-linear change in emission from the resin contributes to the non-linear response of the TSR sensor as a whole. From 20 to 140 °C, all TSR sensors showed a linear response. A linear fit from 20 to 140 °C yields a temperature sensitivity as shown in Table 1. The high-concentration sensors exhibited an increased sensitivity of about 17%. The resin also proved to be temperature-sensitive in this range, but its temperature sensitivity was significantly lower than that of the rhodamine B sensors, and therefore, it is excluded from this discussion.

The overall intensity of each sensor was determined by integrating its emission at the reference condition and normalizing to the 1.00 mm sensor, yielding the following total emission intensity (Fig. 5). The emission intensity appears to increase with increasing concentration. It is reported that at high concentrations (such as above 2 mm in ethanol), rhodamine B will begin to self-quench.45 The self-quenching is observed at high molarity due to the tendency of rhodamine B to aggregate in a prepared solution. The aggregation then allows for a long-range dipole-dipole energy transfer, reducing emission output.45 The created sensors did not show self-quenching, indicating that the self-quenching threshold is higher than 1.00 mm.

Conclusion and outlook

Based on luminescent imaging, a novel approach in measuring internal temperature via a 3D printer was developed and introduced. Rhodamine B, as a temperature-sensitive probe, was embedded into a curable resin for 3D printers to produce a 3D-printable temperature-sensitive resin (TSR). The TSR was produced at various luminophore concentrations and demonstrated a linear response to the temperature over a 120 °C range, which was from 20 to 140 °C. The TSR was also demonstrated to be easily printable with a UV 3D printer. This TSR demonstrated that it may be a candidate for future work on studying the internal temperature distribution in heat exchangers and micro/milli-channel flow.

Methods

The temperature-sensitive resin (TSR) sensors were developed with Rhodamine B (Sigma Aldrich) as the temperature-sensitive luminophore dissolved in Siraya Tech Blu-Tough Resin at the following concentrations: 0.00 mm, 0.01 mm, 0.05 mm, 0.10 mm, 0.50 mm, 1.00 mm. As shown in previous studies, the intensity and sensitivity of rhodamine dyes in various solvents are dependent on the concentration; a range of concentrations was chosen to study the effect on the sensor.36,42,43 Three sensors at each concentration were prepared for a total of 18 sensors. The maximum concentration of 1.00 mm was chosen due to the aggregation of rhodamine B within the resin. Above 1.00 mm, this aggregation resulted in defects in the curing process due to the change in absorption and curing of the resin to UV light. Once the resin with the added rhodamine B was prepared, it was placed in a photocuring 3D printer (Elegoo Saturn 4 Ultra UV). Sensors were then printed in the shape of a square measuring 10 mm x 10 mm and 2 mm thick. The Saturn 4 prints models one layer at a time via UV curing of the local layer near the stage. The stage is then raised, and the next layer is cured via UV light. This process means that the shape of the model made of the TSR is flexible and can be made into any necessary test geometry. A diagram of the printing process is shown previously in Fig. 2c.

All calibrations were performed in a calibration chamber controlled by a temperature controller (Linkam THMS600-PS and Linkam T95-HS). This system was responsible for setting and controlling the sensor temperature, and all test sensors remained on the stage for the duration of testing. The temperature calibration range was set from − 60 °C to 140 °C in 10 °C increments. The limitation of the testing setup gives the lower temperature limit at -60 °C, and the 140 °C maximum temperature was set by the resin manufactorers reccomended working temperatures. The fibers from a spectrometer (FluoroMax-4, Horiba Scientific) were placed with optical access to the sensor resting on the temperature-controlled stage to measure emission and absorption.

During absorption measurements, calibration chamber was set to a constant temperature of 20 °C. The absorption spectra were recorded from 300 to 625 nm in 1 nm increments. These were recorded for two sensors: one with and one without rhodamine B. The sensor with rhodamine B had a 0.1 mm concentration. The first sensor without rhodamine B measured emission at 500 nm with the excitation wavelength varied from 300 to 450 nm in 1 nm increments. The second sensor with rhodamine B measured the emission at 650 nm with excitation ranging from 300 to 620 nm in 1 nm increments. All measurements were normalized by the maximum intensity.

The spectrometer was set to an excitation of 365 nm, corresponding to a UV wavelength, to capture the emission spectra. A 385-nm long-pass filter (An Asahi Spectra Co.) was placed in front of the detector fiber of the spectrometer to remove excitation light and limit the measurement to light emission from the sensor. Emissions from the sensors were recorded in 1 nm intervals ranging from 400 nm to 750 nm. The reference condition used for normalization and temperature calibration was 20 °C.
