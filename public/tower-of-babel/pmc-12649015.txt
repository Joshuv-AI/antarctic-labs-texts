Prevalence of ischemic heart disease among cardiac patients in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Abraham Tsedalu Amare, Tekalign Amera Birlie, Tigabu Munye Aytenew, Yeshiambaw Eshetie, Berihun Bantie, Molla Azmeraw, Gashaw Kerebeh, Gebrie Kassaw Yirga, Mengistu Ewunetu, Astewule Andargie Baye, Walelign Asmie Afewrork, Amanu Addis Melesew, Bekalu Mekonen Belay, Yirgalem Abere
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIschemic heart disease (IHD) remains a major global public health concern, affecting approximately 126 million individuals (1,655 per 100,000), which accounts for about 1.72% of the world’s population. In Africa, the burden of IHD is also significant, with an estimated prevalence of 880 cases per 100,000 population, ranking it as the eighth leading cause of mortality on the continent. In Ethiopia, several primary studies have investigated the prevalence of IHD among cardiac patients; however, their findings have been variable and fragmented. Importantly, there is no national-level pooled estimate of IHD prevalence among this patient population, which presents a critical gap for health policy formulation, clinical decision-making, and resource allocation. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the pooled prevalence of IHD among cardiac patients in Ethiopia through a systematic review and meta-analysis.MethodsElectronic databases, HINARI, PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar were systematically searched, besides free electronic search engines such as Google. We evaluated the quality of included studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for cross-sectional studies.ResultsThe search initially identified 201 articles, and among these, 10 studies (6,405 participants) met the inclusion criteria for analysis. The pooled prevalence of IHD in Ethiopian hospitals among cardiac patients was (13.41, 95% CI: 9.31, 17.51); I2 = 97.62%; p-value < 0.001).Conclusion and recommendationsThis meta-analysis found a pooled prevalence of 13.41% for IHD among cardiac patients in Ethiopia, with substantial heterogeneity across studies. These findings underscore the need for improved ischemic heart disease prevention, early diagnosis, and management strategies in Ethiopian hospitals. Future prospective studies are recommended to provide more accurate prevalence estimates.Keywords: Ischemic heart disease, Cardiac disease, Meta-analysis, Ethiopia

Introduction

Ischemic heart disease is a dynamic process involving atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries or functional alterations in coronary circulation, which can be modified through lifestyle changes, pharmacological therapies, and revascularization [1].

Globally, Ischemic heart disease (IHD) affects around 126 million individuals (1,655 per 100,000), which is approximately 1.72% of the world’s population. It is predicted to increase to more than 1,845 per 1000,000 by 2030 [2]. It is the world’s leading cause of death, responsible for 16% of the world’s total deaths [3]. There were 9.0 million deaths attributed to ischemic heart disease, and it was also the leading cause of death globally in 2021 [4]. In Africa, IHD affects 880 persons per 100,000 population and is ranked eighth among Africa’s leading causes of mortality [2, 5]. In North Africa and the Middle East, ischemic heart disease (IHD) resulted in the loss of 0.8 million lives and 18.0 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). In 2019, the proportion of premature deaths attributed to IHD in the North Africa and the Middle East region was notably higher than the global averages: 26.8% versus 16.9% for women and 18.4% versus 14.8% for men [6]. Within Ethiopia itself, the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2017 data shows that cardiovascular diseases are the country’s leading cause of mortality, with IHD ranking first among CVDs in prevalence, followed by stroke and rheumatic heart disease [7]. In sub-Saharan Africa, age-standardized mortality rates for ischemic heart disease (IHD) are projected to increase by 70% in men and 74% in women by 2030. However, efforts to understand the true burden of IHD in the region are hindered by several critical challenges, including limited access to reliable health data, inadequate information on life expectancy, disease incidence, and cause-specific mortality rates [8]. These challenges are compounded by inadequate diagnostic capacity throughout much of the region, largely due to shortages of trained physicians, particularly cardiologists—and poor access to essential diagnostic tools such as resting 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs), exercise ECGs, cardiac biomarkers (e.g., troponins, CKMB), and advanced imaging techniques including echocardiography, coronary angiography, computed tomography (CT) angiography, intravascular ultrasound (IVUS), and radionuclide myocardial perfusion imaging [9]. As a result of these limitations, the reported prevalence of IHD among adult cardiac populations from previous primary studies in Ethiopia ranges widely from 3.1% to 57% [10–19]. Importantly, there is no national-level pooled estimate of IHD prevalence among this patient population, which presents a critical gap for health policy formulation, clinical decision-making, and resource allocation. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the pooled prevalence of IHD among cardiac patients in Ethiopia through a systematic review and meta-analysis.

Methods

Reporting

It is reported in the PRISMA checklist for systematic reviews and meta-analyses [20]. The protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), registration number CRD420250653376.”

Data extraction and search strategy

An electronic search was conducted using multiple databases, including PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, the free Google database, and the reference lists of eligible articles. The search strategy for PubMed included the terms: “incidence” OR “prevalence” OR “magnitude” OR “proportion” AND “ischemic heart disease” OR “myocardial ischemia” AND “Ethiopia.”

Inclusion criteria

The review was restricted to studies involving human participants, published in English between 2010 and 2024. We have searched all cross-sectional, cohort, and case-control studies conducted on patterns of cardiovascular disease, ischemic heart disease, and patterns of ischemic heart disease among patients who underwent echocardiographic, chest x-rays, and electrocardiographic examinations in Ethiopia and reported the prevalence of ischemic heart disease (IHD) and were published in English. However, all included Articles were cross-sectional studies with the confirmatory diagnosis of Echocardiography and Chest x-ray. Articles having no abstracts and/or full texts and studies conducted as systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and qualitative studies were excluded.

Study selection

All retrieved studies were imported into EndNote version 8 to remove duplicates. Two independent investigators (ATA and TAB) screened the titles and abstracts, followed by full-text reviews to assess study eligibility. Discrepancies between investigators were solved through discussion.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers (ATA and TAB) extracted data using a structured Microsoft Excel sheet. In cases of inconsistency, the data extraction process was repeated, and if discrepancies persisted, a third reviewer (YEA) was involved. Extracted data included the first author’s name, year of publication, study location, study design, sample size, response rate, and effect size of the included studies.

Outcome measure

The primary outcome of interest was the prevalence of ischemic heart disease among cardiac patients visiting hospitals in Ethiopia.

Operational definition

The proportion of cardiac patients diagnosed with ischemic heart disease (IHD) confirmed by echocardiography was considered to have IHD, as reported in the included studies.

Data analysis

The extracted data were analyzed using STATA version 17. The pooled prevalence of IHD was calculated using a random-effects DerSimonian-Laird model. Publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot, where asymmetry indicated potential bias, and Egger’s test (p < 0.05) confirmed its significance. Heterogeneity across studies was evaluated using the I² statistic, which ranges from 0 to 100%. I² values of 0%, 25%, 50%, and 75% were interpreted as indicating no, low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. A p-value < 0.05 in the χ² test of Cochrane’s Q statistic indicated significant heterogeneity.

To explore the sources of heterogeneity, a Galbraith plot (Fig. 1) was used to identify potential outliers. In addition, subgroup analysis, leave-one-out meta-analysis, and sensitivity analysis were conducted to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall pooled estimate. A forest plot was used to present the effects of independent factors on ischemic heart disease (IHD) (Fig. 1).

Results

Search results

A total of 201 articles were identified through the combined electronic database search. Of these, 15 were removed due to duplication. Seventy-seven (77) studies were excluded because the full texts were not accessible to assess the outcomes of interest, while 54 were excluded due to language and study design differences. An additional 25 studies were excluded as they were conducted outside Ethiopia. Following a full-text review, 20 more studies were excluded for not reporting the outcomes of interest along with complete sociodemographic variables. Ultimately, 10 studies were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis.

Characteristics of the included primary studies

Ten studies [10, 16–19, 21–24] Cross-sectional study designs were included in this meta-analysis to estimate the pooled prevalence of ischemic heart disease among cardiac patients visiting hospitals in Ethiopia.

Quality assessment

Two reviewers (ATA & TAB) evaluated the quality of the studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) quality assessment checklists for cross-sectional studies. Their inconveniences were settled by other experts from the group (TMA). The quality assessment tool has the following criteria: a sample frame appropriate to address the target population, an appropriate way of recruiting study participants, the study subjects and setting described in detail, data analysis conducted with sufficient coverage of the identified sample, sample size adequacy, and appropriate statistical analysis. Studies were considered low risk/high quality when they scored 50% or higher on the quality assessment tools. Whereas, studies that scored less than 49% on the quality assessment checklist were categorized as high-risk/low quality [25]. Hence, in this review, all articles scored greater than 50% (S3 Checklist).

Risk of bias assessment

A standardized adopted assessment tool [26] was used to evaluate the risk of bias. Based on this tool, of the total of 10 included studies, 8 scored eight of the 10 questions, and 2 studies scored seven of the 10 questions. Studies were classified as ‘low risk’ if eight or more of the questions received ‘Yes’, as ‘moderate risk’ if six to seven of the 10 questions received ‘Yes’, and as high risk’ if five or fewer. Accordingly, all the included studies [10–19, 24] had a low risk of bias (Table 1).

Metanalysis

The pooled prevalence of ischemic heart disease

A total of 10 eligible studies [10–19, 24] with a total sample size of 6405, ranging from 216 to 1,500 participants, were included in the final meta-analysis. In this study, the pooled prevalence of IHD among cardiac patients visiting Ethiopian hospitals was 13.41% (95% CI: 9.31, 17.51); I2 = 97.62%; p-value < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Publication bias

The funnel plot indicated asymmetrical distribution (Figs. 3 and 4), and the p-value of Egger’s test was < 0.001, indicating significant publication bias among studies.

Investigation of heterogeneity is assessed by examining the percentage of I2 statistics in the forest plot, which indicates the presence of significant heterogeneity among the included studies (I2 = 97.62%; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Therefore, sensitivity and subgroup analyses were performed to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity (Table 2).

Subgroup Meta-Analysis

To explore sources of heterogeneity in the prevalence of ischemic heart disease (IHD) among cardiac patients in Ethiopia, subgroup analyses were conducted based on region, mean age, and sample size of the included studies.

By region

The pooled prevalence of IHD varied substantially by geographic region:

The highest prevalence was observed in studies conducted in the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples (SNNP) Region, with a pooled prevalence of 37.12% (I² = 98.89%, p < 0.0001). Studies from the Amhara Region showed a prevalence of 8.92% (I² = 92.16%, p < 0.0001), and those from Addis Ababa reported 7.49% (I² = 96.09%, p < 0.0001). In the “Other Regions” category (not specified), the pooled prevalence was 8.44% (I² = 96.27%, p < 0.0001) (Table 2).

By mean age

Studies with participants having a mean age below 37 years reported the highest IHD prevalence at 20.24% (I² = 99.21%, p < 0.0001). In contrast, studies with a mean age of 37 years or more had a lower prevalence of 11.39% (I² = 87.62%, p < 0.0001) (Table 2).

By sample size

Subgroup analysis based on sample size revealed that studies with sample sizes < 500 had considerably higher heterogeneity (I² = 98.07%, τ² = 209.671) compared to studies with sample sizes ≥ 500 (I² = 93.53%, τ² = 7.801). This suggests that smaller studies contribute significantly to the overall heterogeneity, possibly due to methodological differences, lower precision, or context-specific variations. The overall heterogeneity across all included studies remained very high (I² = 97.62%, τ² = 40.971), highlighting substantial inconsistency in the reported prevalence of ischemic heart disease among cardiac patients in Ethiopia (Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis

We have conducted a leave-one-out meta-analysis to investigate the influence of a single study on the prevalence of ischemic heart disease among cardiac patients. The analysis result revealed that the effect sizes range from 9.19 to 14.83, indicating that removing any single study does not significantly alter the overall estimate.

The smallest pooled prevalence was seen when the study by Tesfaye A. et al. (2020) was left out, resulting in an estimate of 9.19% (95% CI: 6.45, 11.93). This significant drop shows that the study greatly affected the overall effect size. On the other hand, the highest pooled prevalence occurred when the study by Adem A. et al. (2023) was excluded, raising the estimate to 14.83% (95% CI: 9.94, 19.72). Despite these changes, all versions of the sensitivity analysis were still statistically significant (p < 0.001), reinforcing the reliability and consistency of the meta-analysis results (Figure 5).

Discussion

This meta-analysis included ten primary studies conducted between 2010 and 2024, assessing the prevalence of ischemic heart disease (IHD) among cardiac patients in Ethiopia. The pooled prevalence was found to be 13.41% (95% CI: 9.31–17.51; I² = 97.62%; p < 0.001). This estimate aligns with findings from South Africa (19.5%) [27] and the 2019 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, which highlighted a disproportionately high burden of IHD-related deaths and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs in low- and lower-middle-income countries [28]. In particular, modifiable risk factors accounted for 94.2% of IHD deaths in lower-middle-income countries, including Ethiopia.

This indicates the need for the capacity-building of healthcare professionals, especially at primary and secondary care levels. The knowledge and skill gap in ECG interpretation and cardiac care—cited as a possible reason for inter-study variability—signals the need to integrate comprehensive cardiology training modules into the medical and nursing curricula and to offer continuous professional development (CPD) for frontline clinicians. The observed regional and methodological variations across studies call for the establishment of a national cardiovascular disease registry to track trends, monitor interventions, and support evidence-informed policymaking. Without coordinated national action, the burden of IHD in Ethiopia is likely to escalate further, contributing to premature mortality and straining the health system.

In 2019, the population-attributable fraction (%) of IHD deaths to all modifiable risk factors combined was highest in lower‐middle‐income countries (94.2; 95% uncertainty interval, 91.9–96.2), followed by low‐income countries (92.5; 90.0–94.7) [29]. Recent evidence suggests that Africa is having an epidemiological transition with higher rates of non-communicable diseases (NCDs). IHD contributed to three hundred sixty-one thousand(361000) deaths in the African region in 2005, and this number is expected to be more than double by 2030 [30]. The finding in this study is higher than the study findings in Nigeria, 3.4% [31] and Ghana, 7.3% [32]. The rising prevalence of IHD in Ethiopia has crucial implications for public health policy. The high pooled prevalence observed in this study underscores the urgent need for nationwide cardiovascular screening programs, particularly targeting adults with known risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and sedentary lifestyles.

Inversely, it is lower than the study findings in Somalia, 24.4% [33]. These differences may stem from varying levels of healthcare access, diagnostic capacity, and underlying risk factor distribution across countries. Somalia’s higher prevalence could reflect both a growing burden of cardiovascular risk factors and limited preventive healthcare infrastructure, where late diagnosis and untreated hypertension or diabetes are common. In contrast, Nigeria and Ghana report lower IHD prevalence, which may be attributed to underdiagnosis due to weaker diagnostic infrastructure, including limited availability of electrocardiograms (ECGs), echocardiography, and trained personnel in peripheral and rural settings This variation may be attributed to variations in sample sizes, eligibility criteria, knowledge and skill gap of professionals doing ECG, Echocardiography, and variability of the ECG and echocardiography machine itself [34, 35].

Whereas, the increase in the prevalence of IHD could be explained by the change in the pace and direction of economic development, rates of urbanization, the changes in life expectancy, associated with different pathophysiological factors, and causes an increased rate of atherosclerotic disease in sub-Saharan (SSA) countries [36]. The prevalence of ischemic heart disease in SSA has shown a significant rise due to the rising prevalence of risk factors, especially hypertension, diabetes, overweight and obesity, physical inactivity, tobacco use, and dyslipidemia, mainly due to an increase in urbanization [35, 37–39]. Traditional modifiable atherothrombotic cardiovascular risk factors of smoking, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, obesity, physical inactivity, and poor dietary habits identified from HIC populations are now palpably emergent in SSA [40–42].

This study detected significant publication bias, as evidenced by Egger’s test (p < 0.01). This suggests that smaller studies with non-significant or lower prevalence estimates may have been less likely to be published or indexed in major databases, leading to an overestimation of the pooled prevalence. This bias is common in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where studies with negative or null findings often remain unpublished due to limited journal access, lack of funding, or editorial preferences for statistically significant results.

However, the presence of publication bias indicates the nationwide prevalence of ischemic heart disease (IHD) among cardiac patients in Ethiopia may differ from the pooled estimate reported in this meta-analysis. While publication bias typically causes overestimation, since studies with higher prevalence or statistically significant findings are more likely to be published. It is also possible that the opposite can occur, i.e., studies with lower prevalence are selectively published, which yields underestimation. The direction of the bias should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Our findings also highlight the importance of employing inclusive search strategies to minimize the risk of publication bias. Although we conducted an extensive search on several databases, the omission of grey literature and unpublished work may have excluded studies with null results, as these are less likely to be published in indexed journals.

This would have been likely to bias the pooled prevalence estimate by overestimating those with higher reported prevalence. Adherence to study registration protocols for open-access publication of all results, regardless of outcome, would eliminate such bias in future research. Future meta-analysis has to incorporate grey literature and unpublished data to make a more balanced and comprehensive estimate of IHD pooled prevalence. According to our findings, policymakers and other stakeholders ought to give paramount importance to prevention measures and early detection to minimize the effects of ischemic heart disease in Ethiopia.

Our subgroup analysis revealed a higher prevalence of ischemic heart disease (IHD) among those with a mean age below 37 years (20.24%) compared to those ≥ 37 (11.39%). This suggests that younger Ethiopian adults are bearing a substantial burden of IHD risk. This finding is congruent with local study findings. In Addis Ababa, a community-based study among adults aged 18–64 (mean age 31 years) found a hypertension prevalence of 22.1% using JNC7 criteria, with many cases previously undiagnosed [43]. Similarly, another study finding in urban and rural Wolaita, among adults aged 25–64, reported high levels of modifiable cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors in younger age groups, including physical inactivity (44%), elevated blood pressure (22%), low HDL (31%), and hypertriglyceridemia (15.5%) [44]. In addition, a diabetes study in Hawassa found relatively elevated prevalence even in the age group (30–39 years), with a prevalence of 18.4% [45]. Together, these findings suggest that before the age of 37 years, many Ethiopians already have hypertension, dyslipidemia, obesity, and other risk factors known to drive IHD. Thus, the finding of 20.24 IHD prevalence among the age group below 37 years in this study is plausible and highlights the urgency of early preventive interventions, screening, and lifestyle modifications beginning in early adulthood in Ethiopia.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This systematic review and meta-analysis study is the first in Ethiopia to show the current pooled prevalence of ischemic heart disease. Nevertheless, the study had limitations. Firstly, the number of eligible primary studies was relatively small, which might threaten the representativeness of the pooled estimate. Retrospective designs may have missing or less accurate data, which may lead to the exclusion of meaningful predictors. Qualitative studies and non-English language papers were excluded. While the primary outcome of interest was quantitative (pooled prevalence), qualitative studies might have provided contextual insight into access to healthcare, diagnostic challenges, and cultural determinants of disease detection, which may have an indirect effect on the pooled prevalence estimates.

Conclusion

The pooled prevalence of ischemic heart disease (IHD) in Ethiopia was 13.41%, which is relatively high. Given this, we recommend the following:

For healthcare providers

Strengthen early screening programs for high-risk populations, using advanced ECG and other reliable diagnostic tools, to enable timely detection and management.

For policymakers and stakeholders

Prioritize the integration of cardiovascular disease prevention strategies into national health programs.

For researchers

Conduct prospective, multicenter observational studies to investigate the specific sociodemographic, lifestyle, and diagnostic tools contributing to the high burden of IHD in Ethiopia.
