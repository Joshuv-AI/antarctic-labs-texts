Timing-Error Optimized Architecture for Current-Steering DACs Thanks:
Ramin Babaee, Student Member, IEEE, Shahab Oveis Gharan, Martin Bouchard, Senior Member, IEEE

Abstract
We propose a novel digital-to-analog converter (DAC)weighting architecture that statistically minimizes the distortion caused by random timing mismatches among current sources. To decode the DAC input codewords into corresponding DAC switches, we present three algorithms with varying computational complexities. We perform high-level Matlab simulations to illustrate the dynamic performance improvement over the segmented structure.



I
Introduction

With the rapid growth of high-speed communication systems, high resolution, and high data rate DACs are a necessity. Current-steering DAC is a prominent architecture that enables the use of DACs in high-speed applications
[
1
,
2
]
.

The DAClinear performance is influenced by both static and dynamic distortion sources. The current cell mismatches introduce amplitude errors that affect the static performance. However, the timings skew among current switches impacts the frequency-dependent dynamic performance. As the data rate increases, the signal degradation arising from timing mismatches becomes the leading impairment
[
3
]
.

The redundancy offered by the segmented structure provides an excellent opportunity for utilizing two classes of mitigation techniques. Dynamic element matching (DEM)randomizes the selection of unary cells. This algorithm improves the spurious free dynamic range (SFDR)performance by converting the frequency-dependent distortion into white noise. However, the total signal-to-noise-and-distortion ratio (SNDR)remains unchanged
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. Calibration schemes address the mitigation of mismatch errors by impairment estimation followed by a correction. The studies in
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
focus on amplitude mismatch mapping, while
[
13
,
14
]
investigates a mapping technique for reducing timing errors.

In our paper
[
15
]
, we introduced the concept of weighting optimization for minimizing statistical amplitude errors. This study focuses on statistically random timing errors where the induced glitch errors are proportional to the weight of the current source. We propose a general weighting scheme that decides on the current levels of different DAC switches in order to minimize DAC dynamic distortion sources.

II
Timing Glitch Analysis

Fig. 1:
Timing error in settling behavior of DACswitches.

Let
N
and
L
denote the number of input bits and current switches of a DAC, respectively. The weights of current sources are represented by an
L
-dimensional basis vector
\mathbf{B}
, and the binary representation of a given integer input
x
in the range
[0\quad 2^{N}-1]
is expressed by an
L
-dimensional binary vector
\mathbf{W}(x)
. Note that
\mathbf{B}_{i}=2^{i}
and
\mathbf{B}_{i}=1
for a binary DAC and a unary DAC, respectively. Consequently, the current output of the DAC is formulated as

x=\mathbf{W}^{\text{T}}(x)\mathbf{B}.

(1)

A transition from input
x
to
y
may cause a change in the state of each switch of the DAC, which can be formulated as

\mathbf{c}(x,y)=\mathbf{W}(y)-\mathbf{W}(x),

(2)

where
\mathbf{c}(x,y)
is a vector with
L
elements and
\mathbf{c}_{i}(x,y)\in\{+1,0,-1\},\;0\leq i\leq L-1
. A timing skew vector
\mathbf{\tau}
of size
L
is defined to represent the transition time of the switches. Ideally, all the values should be
0
. But, due to component mismatch, the values are distributed between
-\frac{1}{2}
to
\frac{1}{2}
, where they are normalized by the sampling period
T
. Statistically,
\mathbf{\tau}_{i}
values are assumed to be random, independent, and identically distributed, and have a Gaussian distribution with a mean of zero and a variance of
\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{\tau}}
. Fig.
1
illustrates timing errors among DACswitches using an exponential transient response. Although an exponential function is more realistic, for the purpose of the analysis in this section, we assume the desired transient response of DACswitches is an ideal step function
u(t)
with transition time at
0
, i.e.,

u(t)=\begin{cases}0,\hskip 43.05542pt&t\leq 0,\\
1,\hskip 43.05542pt&t>0.\\
\end{cases}

(3)

It is worth mentioning that a step function transition model may not be an accurate model of the hardware, but it allows for an efficient formulation of the glitch error and optimizing the DAC architecture. The simulation results show that this simplification can still provide a very significant improvement.

Assuming switching on and off transients have the same response, the DACoutput can be written as

z(t)=x+\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\mathbf{c}_{i}(x,y)\mathbf{B}_{i}u(t-\tau_{i}),\hskip 17.22217pt-\frac{T}{2}<t\leq\frac{T}{2}.

(4)

Given that the ideal transition time is
0
, one can derive the transient error at the output of the DACas

e_{g}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\mathbf{c}_{i}(x,y)\mathbf{B}_{i}\Pi(\frac{t-\tau_{i}/2}{\left\lvert\mathbf{\tau}_{i}\right\rvert})\text{sgn}(\tau_{i}),

(5)

where
\Pi(t)
is the rectangular function defined as

\Pi(t)=\begin{cases}0,\hskip 43.05542pt&|t|>\frac{1}{2},\\
\frac{1}{2},\hskip 43.05542pt&|t|=\frac{1}{2},\\
1,\hskip 43.05542pt&|t|<\frac{1}{2},\\
\end{cases}

(6)

and
\text{sgn}(t)
is the sign function. Note that for notation simplicity, we drop the argument
(x,y)
from
\mathbf{c}_{i}
terms. Without loss of generality, we assume
\tau_{i}
values are sorted such that
\mathbf{\tau}_{0}\leq\mathbf{\tau}_{1}\leq\dots\leq\tau_{L-1}
. Eq. (
5
) can be then expanded as

e_{g}(t)=\begin{cases}-\mathbf{c}_{0}\mathbf{B}_{0},\hskip 215.2771pt&\mathbf{\tau}_{0}\leq t<\mathbf{\tau}_{1},\\
-\mathbf{c}_{0}\mathbf{B}_{0}-\mathbf{c}_{1}\mathbf{B}_{1},\quad&\mathbf{\tau}_{1}\leq t<\tau_{2},\\
\ldots&\ldots\\
-\sum_{i=0}^{m}\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i},\quad&\tau_{m}\leq t<0,\\
\sum_{i=m}^{L-2}\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i},\quad&0\leq t<\tau_{m+1},\\
\ldots&\ldots\\
\mathbf{c}_{L-2}\mathbf{B}_{L-2},\quad&\tau_{L-2}\leq t<\tau_{L-1},\\
\end{cases}

(7)

where
\tau_{m}
is the largest negative number. The mean square error (MSE)associated with the transition from
x
to
y
is then defined as

\displaystyle C(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\left[{\int_{-\frac{T}{2}}^{\frac{T}{2}}\left\lvert e_{g}(t)\right\rvert^{2}dt}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i})^{2}(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n})

\displaystyle\hskip 12.91663pt-(\sum_{i=0}^{m}\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i})^{2}\tau_{m}+(\sum_{i=m}^{L-2}\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i})^{2}\tau_{m+1}

\displaystyle\hskip 12.91663pt+\sum_{n=m+1}^{L-2}(\sum_{i=n}^{L-2}\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i})^{2}(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n})\Bigg].

(8)

Since the timing errors are independent of the current weights and representation vectors, the metric
C(x,y)
can be simplified to

\displaystyle C(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{T}\sum_{n=0}^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor-1}\mathbb{E}\left[{(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i})^{2}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[{\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 4.30554pt-\frac{2}{T}\mathbb{E}\left[{(\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i})^{2}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[{\tau_{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}}\right]

\displaystyle=2\sum_{n=0}^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}\mathbb{E}\left[{(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i})^{2}}\right]\mu_{n},

(9)

where

\mu_{n}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\left[{\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n}}\right],\quad&\text{if }n<\lfloor L/2\rfloor,\\
\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\left[{\tau_{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}}\right],\quad&\text{if }n=\lfloor L/2\rfloor.\\
\end{cases}

(10)

Note that
\mu_{n}
only depends on 2 parameters
L
and
\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{\tau}}
and it scales linearly with
\sigma_{\mathbf{\tau}}
. The expression inside the summation can be calculated as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i})^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[{(\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i})^{2}}\right]+\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{c}_{j}\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{j}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{n+1}{L}\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}(\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 4.30554pt+\frac{n(n+1)}{L(L-1)}\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{L-1}\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{c}_{j}\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{j}.

(11)

By re-writing Eq. (
9
), upon the above result, we obtain

\displaystyle C(x,y)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{n=0}^{\lfloor L/2\rfloor}(n+1)(D+nS)\mu_{n},

(12)

where
D
and
S
are defined as

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}(\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i})^{2},

(13)

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L(L-1)}\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{L-1}\mathbf{c}_{i}\mathbf{c}_{j}\mathbf{B}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{j}.

(14)

Having obtained the MSE associated with a transition from
x
to
y
, we are now able to derive the total cost function. If there is only one representation for every input
x
such that
\mathbf{W}^{\text{T}}(x)\mathbf{B}=x
, then the basis is complete, e.g. a binary DAC. In this case, the total cost can be calculated as

C_{\text{total}}=\sum_{x=0}^{2^{N}-1}\sum_{y=0}^{2^{N}-1}\text{Pr}\left({x,y}\right)C(x,y),

(15)

where
\text{Pr}\left({x,y}\right)
is the transition probability from input
x
to input
y
.

III
Architecture Design

For an over-complete basis, there may be multiple representations for an input value. Let
\mathcal{R}(y)
denote the set of all possible representations for input
y
, i.e.,

\mathcal{R}(y)=\left\{\mathbf{W}|\mathbf{W}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{B}=y\right\}.

(16)

In the case of an over-complete basis, we have multiple choices for the representation of
y
among the set
\mathcal{R}(y)
. Here, we propose selecting the representation
\mathbf{W}(y)\in\mathcal{R}(y)
which minimizes the timing glitch expected distortion power
C(x,y)
. Assuming we make such an optimum selection, the metric
C_{\text{total}}
can be calculated as

\displaystyle C_{\text{total}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{2^{N}-1}\sum_{y=0}^{2^{N}-1}\text{Pr}\left({x,y}\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{W}(x)\in\mathcal{R}(x)}\text{Pr}\left({\mathbf{W}(x)|x}\right)\min_{\mathbf{W}(y)\in\mathcal{R}(y)}C(\mathbf{W}(x),\mathbf{W}(y)),

(17)

where
\text{Pr}\left({\mathbf{W}(x)|x}\right)
is the probability distribution of representations of
x
. Note that we have changed the notation
C(x,y)
to
C(\mathbf{W}(x),\mathbf{W}(y))
to emphasize that the cost associated with a transition from
x
to
y
depends on their corresponding representation vectors. Also note that Eq. (
17
) is a generalized form of Eq. (
15
) where for a complete basis, the representation sets
\mathcal{R}(x)
and
\mathcal{R}(y)
have a cardinality of
1
and thus,
\text{Pr}\left({\mathbf{W}(x)|x}\right)=1
.

The DAC architecture design can be expressed as finding the DAC current switch levels
\mathbf{B}_{0},\mathbf{B}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{B}_{L-1}
such that the distortion power due to timing glitch is minimized. Mathematically saying, given the DAC input bit resolution
N
and number of DAC switches
L
, we aim to find the over-complete basis
\mathbf{B}
which minimizes the distortion function, specifically,

\mathbf{B}_{\text{opt}}=\argmin_{\mathbf{B}}C_{\text{total}}.

(18)

Note that the optimization expression (
18
) is a non-linear, non-convex, discrete problem. The dimension of search space is
L
and each element of vector
\mathbf{B}
may have an integer value in the range
[1\hskip 4.30554pt2^{N}]
. Therefore, the maximum size of the search space is
2^{NL}
, which grows exponentially with both
N
and
L
. Therefore, an exhaustive search is not feasible except for small values of
L
and
N
.

IV
Representation Selection

In the previous section, we derived the optimum choice of DAC architecture current sources, i.e.,
\mathbf{B}_{\text{opt}}
. However, given an over-complete basis
\mathbf{B}
, there are multiple projections from the set
\mathcal{R}(x)
which can represent DAC input value
x
. In this section, we consider three different schemes for choosing the best representation for DAC input, assuming the basis
\mathbf{B}
is already derived. The methods differ in terms of their computational complexity versus their achieved performance.

1) Viterbi (optimal)
: Given an input sequence
x[0]
,
x[1]
, …,
x[M-1]
, the optimal representations can be chosen such that the statistical error power in Eq. (
17
) averaged over transitions of the sequence is minimized.
The optimization problem is formulated as

\argmin_{\mathbf{W}(x[0]),...,\mathbf{W}(x[M-1])}\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}C(\mathbf{W}(x[m-1]),\mathbf{W}(x[m])).

(19)

Each node
(y,m)
is connected to a node
(y^{\prime},m+1)\in\mathcal{R}(x[m+1])
with a directed edge
e
and an associated cost
c(e)
to such edge which is defined as
c(e)=C(\mathbf{W}(y),\mathbf{W}(y^{\prime}))
. The cost of a path
(e_{1},e_{2},...,,e_{M})
is defined as
c(e_{1})+c(e_{2})+...+c(e_{M})
. It can be verified that the optimum value for the above optimization problem can be derived by finding the minimum cost path from a node at
(y,0)
to a node at
(y^{\prime},M-1)
. Such an optimization problem is a well-known Dynamic programming problem that can be solved using the Viterbi algorithm
[
17
]
. The Viterbi algorithm runs sequentially through the Trellis graph: For iteration
m
, it finds the minimum cost path from nodes at position
0
to any of the nodes
(y,m)
, assuming the minimum cost paths for nodes
(y^{\prime},m-1)
is already derived in the previous iteration. Computational complexity of Viterbi algorithm is equal to
\mathcal{O}(M\mathbb{E}\left[{|\mathcal{R}(x)|^{2}}\right])
, which is equivalent to
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{E}\left[{|\mathcal{R}(x)|^{2}}\right])
per each DAC sample.

2) Sequential greedy
: Given the significant computational complexity of the Viterbi approach, one can use a greedy approach to sequentially find the best representation for
x[m]
which minimizes the transition cost from
x[m-1]
to
x[m]
, assuming the representation for
x[m-1]
is already known. More precisely, we have

\argmin_{\mathbf{W}(x[m])}C(\mathbf{W}(x[m-1]),\mathbf{W}(x[m])).

(20)

Note that this approach produces a sub-optimum result. Furthermore, computational complexity of this algorithm is
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{E}\left[{|\mathcal{R}(x)|}\right])
per sample. Hence, it has a lower computational complexity than the Viterbi algorithm. However, similar to the Viterbi method, this approach requires samples to be decoded sequentially which is not ideal for a highly parallel digital signal processing (DSP)architecture.

3) Memoryless representation
: This approach chooses a unique representation
\mathbf{W}(x)
for each DAC input codeword irrespective of its previous and next sample values, namely,

\argmin_{\mathbf{W}(x[m])}\mathbb{E}_{z}\big[C(\mathbf{W}(x[m]),\mathbf{W}(z))\big]+\mathbb{E}_{z}\big[C(\mathbf{W}(z),\mathbf{W}(x[m]))\big]

(21)

Therefore, there is no dependency between samples, and each DAC input can be processed independently. This approach is ideal for parallel implementation in the hardware. In this case, all codeword mappings can be computed offline and the mapping of the input signal to the current weights is performed by a lookup table (LUT)in hardware.

VI
CONCLUSION

We studied the glitches caused by random timing errors of DACswitches. We formulated the MSEand derived an optimization problem that was used to find architectures that attain significant improvement over segmented DACs. We then studied the input signal decoding for the proposed DAC architecture and proposed three decoding algorithms with different levels of computational complexity versus attained performance. The proposed memoryless mapping technique offers a simple implementation through LUT, which is suitable for high-speed applications. Although the paper focused on SNDR improvement, one can use the same framework for deriving superior architectures using other metrics such as SFDR. In the next step of this research, we will conduct transistor-level simulations to verify the potential benefit of the proposed DAC architecture.
