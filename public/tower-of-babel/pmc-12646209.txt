METTL3-dependent m6A RNA methylation suppresses aberrant mammary epithelial differentiation and neoplastic transformation
Authors: Yihao Li, Xintao Qiu, Zachary Sandusky, Kaitlin Tagliaferri, Rong Li, Xin Yang, Tao Zhang, Shimeng Liu, Pengze Yan, Feng Lu, Marcus Jones, Tengfei Xiao, Wei Li, Seth Goldman, Jie Cui, Kornelia Polyak, X. Shirley Liu, Henry W. Long, Richard I. Gregory, Karen Adelman, Jennifer M. Rosenbluth, Myles Brown
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceEffective prevention of breast cancer remains elusive, largely due to limited understanding of the mechanisms driving early neoplastic transformation in the mammary epithelium. We show that METTL3-dependent m6A methylation is essential for maintaining epithelial hierarchy by repressing aberrant luminal gene expression. Our findings reveal cell type–specific patterns of m6A regulation and underscore its critical role in mammary tissue homeostasis.

Breast cancer is the most diagnosed cancer in women worldwide (1). In contrast to the advances in breast cancer treatment, our understanding of early events driving oncogenic transformation and increased cancer risk lags behind. Although several high-penetrance germline alterations have been shown to confer a very high risk of breast cancer, these genes explain only a small fraction of breast cancer cases (2). While common genetic variants and endocrine-associated transcription factors contribute substantially to the attributable risk of developing breast cancer, the mechanisms driving this increased risk are largely unknown.

Recent single-cell analysis of normal and breast cancer tissues has identified the cell type–specific gene signatures that are potentially correlated with breast cancer development. It remains a challenge to define whether those signature genes are functionally involved in aberrant epithelial growth and oncogenic transformation (3). CRISPR-based genetic screens have provided powerful tools for identifying cancer driver genes (4). These screens have been conducted largely in cancer cell lines and may not be sufficient to identify determinants of early oncogenic processes. Importantly, screens in cancer cell lines are insensitive for the identification of potential tumor suppressor genes whose inactivation leads to increased premalignant cell proliferation (5). To address this limitation, it is necessary to characterize genetic fitness in precancerous models. In this study, we implemented CRISPR knockout (KO) screens in both primary normal and immortalized human mammary epithelial cells to investigate the effect of genetic perturbations on unrestrained cellular growth and survival, a hallmark of the early stage of tumorigenesis. We identified several genes play a growth suppressive role in the mammary epithelium including an essential role of the N6–methyladenosine (m6A) RNA methyltransferase METTL3 in regulating mammary epithelial cell fate and growth.

Results

CRISPR KO Screens Identify the Key Regulators of Mammary Epithelial Cell Fitness.

To systematically identify the key mechanisms regulating sustained mammary epithelial cell growth, we sought to perform CRISPR KO screens in a robust in vitro model. Normal primary mammary epithelial cells can be cultured under conditions that maintain the mammary cell hierarchy and fitness (6), providing a system for unbiased functional genomic screens. To ensure adequate sgRNA representation in the mammary epithelial cell screens, we designed a CRISPR-Cas9 KO library that targets more than 6,100 cancer-associated genes with 10 guide RNAs per gene which includes 1) oncogenes (OG) and tumor suppressor genes (TSG), 2) transcription factors (TFs), RNA binding proteins (RBPs), and cell surface proteins (CSPs), 3) frequently mutated genes in cancers, 4) differentially expressed genes in TCGA tumors versus normal tissues. We conducted CRISPR KO screens in both normal primary human mammary epithelial cells (PHMEC) and the immortalized mammary epithelial cell line MCF10A to identify key genes whose depletion inhibits or potentiates epithelial growth and validated the top screen hits in 3D breast organoid models capable of recapitulating the aberrant epithelial proliferation and differentiation seen early in neoplastic transformation (Fig. 1A).

Utilizing the MAGeCK-VISPR algorithm (7), we analyzed the gene essentiality by quantifying the sgRNA abundance changes in the mammary epithelial cells that were transduced with pooled sgRNAs at day 0 and the same cells cultured for 21 d. As expected, the gene dependencies of PHMEC and MCF10A cells are significantly correlated (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). We compared the gene essentiality in mammary epithelial cells with 33 breast cancer cell lines from the DepMap CRISPR screen dataset (8). Most negatively selected genes in PHMEC and MCF10A cells were also essential in the breast cancer cell lines (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). These common essential genes are enriched for fundamental cellular functions, such as cell cycle and DNA/RNA processing (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). We next analyzed the positively selected genes whose inactivation induces increased fitness in PHMEC and MCF10A cells (Fig. 1B). Many known tumor suppressive genes are highly positively selected in the two mammary epithelial cell lines, whereas only a small number of TSGs are positively selected across the breast cancer cell lines, as many of these harbor inactivating mutations in known TSG such as TP53 and PTEN (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). In total, we identified 217 genes that when lost significantly promote cell fitness in PHMEC or MCF10A cells. These genes are functionally associated with organismal development, RNA metabolic process, signal regulation, cell growth, and epithelial development (Fig. 1 C and D).

We focused on the 21 genes that when lost were significantly positively selected in both PHMEC and MCF10A cells for further analysis. Loss of 17 of these genes was also positively selected in breast cancer cell lines, including several well-known tumor suppressor genes such as CDKN1A and PPTN12. Interestingly, key regulators of cellular differentiation including NF2, LATS2, TGFBR2, and TP63, were among the top positive selection hits in both normal MEC and breast cancer cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E) (9–11). Strikingly, loss of several epigenetic regulators including SMARCA4, METTL3, RBM15, and KMT2D are specifically positively selected in normal MEC and are negatively selected in breast cancer cells. Of these genes, the m6A methyltransferase METTL3 and the RNA binding protein RBM15 are components of the same complex and largely essential in the breast cancer cell lines. Moreover, all sgRNAs targeting METTL3 were highly enriched at the end time point of the screen (SI Appendix, Fig. S1F). These findings encouraged us to further investigate the role of the RNA methyltransferase METTL3 in mammary epithelial growth and differentiation.

Loss of METTL3 Promotes Cell Growth and Reprograms Gene Expression in the Mammary Epithelium.

To validate the results of the CRISPR screens, we depleted endogenous METTL3 expression in PHMEC and MCF10A cells using 2 distinct sgRNAs targeting METTL3 (sgMETTL3-2 and sgMETTL3-4) and tested the effect of METTL3 depletion on cell growth (Fig. 2A). Consistent with the CRISPR screen results, loss of METTL3 expression enhanced mammary epithelial cell growth in PHMEC and MCF10A cells (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Importantly, METTL3 KO also resulted in increased proliferation of MCF10A cells grown as mammospheres (Fig. 2C).

METTL3 and the RNA methyltransferase complex are critical for RNA transcription, splicing, and stability (12, 13). To identify the regulatory mechanisms that contribute to increased cell fitness in mammary epithelial cells upon METTL3 depletion, we assessed the RNA expression profile in control and METTL3 KO cells. Loss of METTL3 in MCF10A cells leads to a reprogramming of gene expression, including a substantial number of genes that were significantly up-regulated (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Gene ontology analysis of these upregulated genes highlighted the important role of inflammatory pathways and estrogen signaling in mammary epithelial cells upon METTL3 depletion (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed that the differentially expressed genes in MCF10A KO cells showed significantly positive enrichment in breast luminal and breast cancer invasive gene signatures (Fig. 2F), indicating loss of METTL3 drives gene expression programs involved in mammary epithelial cell luminal differentiation and breast cancer. Consistent with the changes in RNA expression, METTL3 depletion increased protein levels of breast luminal keratins (KRT19, KRT8, and KRT18) and decreased protein levels of the basal keratin (KRT14) in PHMEC and MCF10A cells (Fig. 2G). Using short hairpin RNA interference, we confirmed that silencing of METTL3 caused upregulated expression of breast cancer gene and luminal signatures (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C–E). These observations suggest that METTL3 depletion significantly promoted mammary epithelial cell lineage-specific gene expression programs.

To further understand how METTL3 inactivation affects the gene expression that is associated with aberrant epithelial differentiation and growth, we assessed whether METTL3 enzymatic activity is required to regulate RNA expression in MCF10A cells by ectopically expressing wildtype or catalytically inactive mutant of METTL3 in METTL3-depleted MCF10A cells (Fig. 2H). Restoration of wildtype METTL3 expression could revert the gene expression changes upon METTL3 loss, whereas expression of the catalytic mutant METTL3 had no rescue effects (Fig. 2I). Together, our findings suggest that METTL3 is indispensable for maintaining normal mammary epithelial gene expression pattern in a m6A methyltransferase-dependent manner and that loss of METTL3 enables upregulated expression of luminal genes, contributing to sustained mammary cell proliferation.

METTL3 Ablation Drives Epithelial Differentiation in Normal Breast Organoids.

The significant increase of luminal genes upon METTL3 depletion in MCF10A cells led us to speculate that METTL3 KO might affect mammary epithelial cell state, thereby regulating the growth in the primary breast tissues. Therefore, we evaluated the impact of METTL3 loss on mammary epithelial cellular complexity and outgrowth in primary human breast organoid models (14). We used lentiviral transduction to introduce CAS9 and CRISPR sgRNAs targeting METTL3 in 3 normal breast organoid cultures derived from breast tissue of donors with no known cancer predisposition syndrome or history of breast cancer who underwent reduction mammoplasty (Fig. 3 A and B). Consistent with our findings in mammary epithelial cell lines, METTL3 depletion resulted in significant increase of organoid number and growth compared to control organoids (Fig. 3 C and D). We then assessed the organoid structures and lineage changes using fluorescent staining for mammary cell lineage markers. Typical luminal AV/luminal progenitor, luminal HS/mature luminal, and basal structures were observed in all organoid cultures (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A), suggesting the organoid models preserved the multiple cell types present in the mammary epithelium. Importantly, METTL3 depletion enhanced the expression of the luminal keratin KRT8 and inhibited the expression of basal keratin KRT14 in breast organoids (Fig. 3 E and F). These data demonstrate that METTL3 depletion promotes aberrant luminal differentiation in the mammary epithelium.

To systematically understand the cellular and molecular changes that associated with aberrant mammary epithelial differentiation upon METTL3 loss, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing in the normal breast organoids with or without METTL3 sgRNA-mediated depletion (sgMETTL3-4). Our analysis revealed a marked shift in mammary epithelial cell subpopulations in METTL3-depleted cells compared with control (Fig. 3G). Using published mammary epithelial cell type signatures (15), we were able to detect lineage-specific gene expression of the major mammary epithelial cell populations in the normal breast organoids including basal, luminal HS, and luminal AV signatures (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B).

To identify how METTL3 regulates the mammary epithelial cell hierarchy, we performed k-means clustering analysis of cell populations from control and METTL3 KO organoids and determined six distinct clusters (Fig. 3H). Of note, we found a distinct cell cluster that is exclusive to control organoid cells (control distinct), while METTL3 depletion resulted in a shift of mammary cells to a different specific subpopulation (sgMETTL3 distinct) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). In addition, several mixed clusters containing both control and METTL3 KO cells were observed, corresponding to different subsets of basal and luminal cells (Fig. 3H and SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Differential gene expression analysis distinguished a subset of genes that were significantly changed among each cluster (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). Importantly, significantly upregulated genes in the sgMETTL3 distinct cell cluster are consistently highly enriched for interferon and luminal-associated (androgen response) pathways (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E), further suggesting that METTL3 loss induces mammary epithelial luminal differentiation.

We next used mammary cell lineage signatures to characterize which cell type is altered by METTL3 loss (15). METTL3 depletion had no significant effect on basal cell populations, evidenced by the highly expressed basal gene signature in mixed cluster 3 (Fig. 3I). Remarkably, the sgMETTL3 distinct cluster expressed both HS and AV signatures to a greater extent than any other cluster, which underlines the essential role of METTL3 in regulating mammary luminal cell differentiation (Fig. 3I). Specifically, we observed substantial increases in the expression of classic luminal marker KRT19 and EpCAM, as well as luminal HS markers MUC1 and XBP1 in the cell populations with METTL3 KO (Fig. 3J). In contrast, METTL3 depletion has no effect on the KRT5-positive basal cell population (Fig. 3J). Together, these data suggest that METTL3 governs cellular plasticity, and its loss promotes aberrant luminal cell differentiation within the mammary epithelium, thereby leading to increased cellular proliferation and neoplastic transformation.

Genome-Wide Characterization of METTL3-Dependent m6A Modifications in Mammary Epithelial Cells.

Given that the enzymatic activity of METTL3 is required for the repression of luminal gene expression in the mammary epithelium (Fig. 2I), we set out to determine the regulatory mechanisms downstream of METTL3-dependent RNA m6A modifications in the mammary epithelium. Therefore, we performed m6A methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq) to identify the changes of mRNA m6A abundance in control and METTL3 KO cells. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S4A, the total mRNA m6A meRIP-seq signal intensity was decreased by METTL3 KO compared with control MCF10A cells. However, these METTL3-dependent m6A mRNA modifications did not correspond to differentially expressed genes upon METTL3 KO (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B), implying METTL3 enzymatic activity might regulate other RNA regulatory processes in mammary epithelial cells.

Recent studies have found that m6A methylation plays a pivotal role in regulating the expression of chromatin-associated regulatory RNAs, which includes RNAs transcribed from the promoter, enhancer, and repeats (16, 17). Consistent with the increased gene expression in METTL3 KO cells, we found that loss of METTL3 significantly enhanced global nascent RNA expression in MCF10A cells by EU staining (Fig. 4 A and B). Thus, we hypothesized that dysregulation of nascent transcripts might play the key role in regulating mammary epithelial cell plasticity and growth. To test this hypothesis, we isolated chromatin-associated RNAs followed by meRIP-seq (ChAR meRIP-seq) to profile m6A abundance in these RNAs in both control and METTL3-depleted MCF10A cells. In contrast to the small number (1,665) of METTL3-dependent m6A sites identified in the mRNA meRIP-seq data (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4A), we observed 10,562 chromatin-associated RNA sites were lost upon METTL3 depletion (Fig. 4 D and E), indicating METTL3 largely targets chromatin-associated transcripts in mammary epithelial cells.

Next, we compared the distribution of m6A sites captured by mRNA meRIP-seq and ChAR meRIP-seq. As expected, the decreased m6A signals on total mRNAs upon METTL3 depletion are highly enriched in exon transcripts. In contrast, the METTL3-dependent ChAR m6A sites were enriched in transcripts of intronic and intergenic regions, suggesting the METTL3-dependent m6A sites are abundant in noncoding transcripts (Fig. 4 F and G). To further characterize which class of chromatin-associated RNA are the primary targets of METTL3, we analyzed the m6A deposition on RNAs transcribed from major noncoding genomic regions, including promoter, enhancer, and repeat regions. Importantly, loss of METTL3 specifically reduced m6A modifications of RNAs transcribed most strikingly from repeat regions (Fig. 4H).

Previous studies have shown that m6A modifications are prevalent in transcripts of repeat regions, particularly in the transcripts of transposable elements (TEs) (18–20); however, it remains unclear which TEs are modified by m6A in mammary epithelial cells. We classified METTL3-dependent m6A TE peaks in MCF10A cells and found that the LTR and LINE families represent 40 and 32% of m6A-dependent TE transcripts, respectively (Fig. 4I). Specifically, METTL3-mediated m6A sites are present abundantly in the L1, ERVK, and ERVL subfamilies (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). These results suggest that transcripts of transposable elements are the primary target of METTL3 in mammary epithelial cells and may potentially be involved in regulating mammary epithelial cell fitness and differentiation.

METTL3 Depletion Induces Expression of TE Transcripts and STAT Signaling.

To elucidate whether the METTL3-dependent m6A methylation on TE transcripts impacts their RNA expression levels in mammary epithelial cells, we directly measured nascent RNA synthesis in control and METTL3-depleted MCF10A cells by precision nuclear run-on sequencing (PRO-seq). In agreement with our total RNA-seq and EU staining results, loss of METTL3 led to significant global alteration in nascent RNA synthesis (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). A majority of the 4724 TE fragments containing METTL3-dependent m6A sites in MCF10A cells were upregulated by METTL3 ablation, including 168 significantly increased TEs (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S5C), suggesting METTL3 regulates expression of TE transcripts in mammary epithelial cells.

We next examined which TE family expression levels were significantly increased upon METTL3 depletion. Increased RNA expression levels of LTR, LINE, SINE, and DNA families were observed among significantly upregulated METTL3-dependent TEs. Specifically, the transcripts of the LTR family showed the highest average fold changes among those significantly upregulated TEs (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, we analyzed the top TE subfamilies that were significantly upregulated due to METTL3 loss (LFC > 1.5, P < 0.05) and found that L1, L2, ERV1, MaLR, MIR, and Alu showed substantial enrichment in METTL3 KO cells (Fig. 5C). The ChAR meRIP-seq and PRO-seq signals on the gene track of LTR1A2, L1PA7, and Alusp confirmed that METTL3 depletion inhibits the m6A modification and consequently leads to increased expression levels of those TE transcripts (Fig. 5D). Our findings revealed that loss of METTL3 specifically eliminates m6A modifications on TE transcripts, thereby increasing their expression levels in mammary epithelial cells.

We next asked which transcription programs or pathways are associated with upregulated expression of TEs upon METTL3 depletion. We observed that interferon signaling is the highest enriched pathway of the upregulated genes in METTL3-depleted MCF10A cells versus control cells (Figs. 2E and 5E). In line with this, the phosphorylation levels of STAT1, the major transcription factor downstream of interferon-JAK-STAT signaling, were significantly increased by METTL3 depletion in MCF10A cells (Fig. 5F).

We then investigated whether blocking STAT signaling could reverse the gene expression program mediated by METTL3 inhibition. Consistent with the transcriptomic change observed in METTL3 KO cells, pharmacological inhibition of METTL3 by treatment with its inhibitor STM2457 similarly altered gene expression in MCF10A cells (Fig. 5G). Interestingly, the genes induced by STM2457 treatment were blocked by the JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib, suggesting that the JAK-STAT pathway is critical for regulating the gene expression program upon METTL3 inhibition (Fig. 5G).

STAT Signaling Interacts With GATA3 to Drive Mammary Epithelial Differentiation.

We next investigated the underlying mechanisms of STAT signaling in driving global gene expression changes upon METTL3 loss. Using the computational method LISA (21), we integrated the nascent transcript data from PRO-seq with publicly available ATAC-seq and ChIP-seq data to determine which transcription factors act as potential regulators of differentially expressed genes in METTL3-depleted cells. Upregulated nascent transcripts by METTL3 KO are significantly enriched for luminal transcription factors such as FOXA1, GATA3, HOXB13, ESR1, and AR, as well as inflammatory transcription factors including STAT3, STAT1, and RELA (Fig. 6A), indicating the putative functional connection between inflammatory and luminal transcription factors in regulating mammary epithelial differentiation and growth. Therefore, we performed ATAC-seq in control or METTL3-depleted MCF10A cells to evaluate whether changes in chromatin state are associated with the transcription of mammary epithelial luminal genes. The increased accessible chromatin regions coincided with the distribution of increased PRO-seq signals upon METTL3 depletion (Fig. 6B), further supporting our findings that luminal genes are transcriptionally upregulated by METTL3 loss.

Among the transcription factors that potentially drive the gene expression change in METTL3 KO cells, GATA3 is a critical transcription factor in the mammary epithelium, which coordinately regulates ESR1 and FOXA1 mediated transcription, and drives luminal epithelial differentiation and breast cancer progression (22). We therefore performed GATA3 ChIP-seq to examine GATA3-bound DNA regions in MCF10A cells upon METTL3 depletion. As shown in Fig. 6C, METTL3 KO led to a substantial increase of GATA3 DNA binding activity in mammary epithelial cells. Moreover, those increased GATA3 DNA binding sites also highly overlapped with the STAT3-bound DNA regions as well as the DNA binding sites of luminal-associated transcription factors such as AR and ESR1 (Fig. 6D), indicating the coordinate role of STAT transcription factors in regulating the recruitment of luminal transcription factors to their DNA binding sites.

To validate whether the STAT3 transcription factor interacts with GATA3-bound DNA regions induced by METTL3 depletion, we identified STAT3-dependent DNA binding sites in MCF10A cells by analysis of published STAT3 ChIP-seq data (23). We found 2087 GATA3-STAT3 overlapping DNA binding peaks were increased by METTL3 loss and the chromatin accessibility at these GATA3/STAT3-bound DNA regions was markedly increased (Fig. 6E). The genes nearby these accessible GATA3-STAT3 overlapping peaks are enriched for inflammatory signaling and estrogen response (Fig. 6F). Together, these results demonstrate that loss of METTL3 in mammary epithelial cells increases TE expression and activates STAT transcription activity, coordinating with GATA3-mediated luminal gene transcription from shared genomic regulatory regions.

Role of m6A Regulation During Neoplastic Transformation and Luminal Differentiation in the Mammary Gland.

Since loss of function of growth suppressive genes is frequent in early stages of tumorigenesis, we asked whether METTL3 depletion alters the development of mammary neoplasia in vivo. For these studies, we used the MCF10AT model of early breast tumorigenesis which was originally derived from HRAS-transformed MCF10A premalignant lesions in mice (24). We depleted METTL3 in MCF10AT cells and injected these cells or control MCF10AT cells into the mammary fat pads of NOD/SCID gamma (NSG) mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B). In line with the previous report that MCF10AT cells are weakly tumorigenic, we observed few (2/15) MCF10AT xenografts developed neoplastic lesions and those that developed were small (Fig. 7 A and B). Remarkably, CRISPR-mediated depletion of METTL3 in MCF10AT cells led to a significant increase in tumorigenic activity, as well as in the size of the neoplastic lesions (Fig. 7 A and B), suggesting a role for METTL3 in suppressing neoplastic transformation.

Distinct expression patterns of m6A regulators and TEs are associated with mammary luminal differentiation. To determine whether m6A regulators exhibit cell type–specific expression patterns that are linked to mammary epithelial differentiation, we analyzed the single-cell expression levels of m6A methyltransferase subunits METTL3 and METTL14, as well as the m6A demethylase FTO in publicly available single-cell RNA-seq data from normal human mammary glands (15). As shown in Fig. 7C and SI Appendix, Fig. S6C, the expression levels of METTL3 and METTL14 are similar across the various cell types. Strikingly, the m6A demethylase FTO was highly and consistently expressed in luminal HS cells (Fig. 7C and SI Appendix, Fig. S6C), suggesting that the physiological regulation of m6A RNA modification may be through the demethylase activity to promote luminal cell differentiation. We further investigated the relationships between TE expression levels and luminal cell populations in the human mammary gland. METTL3-dependent TEs in MCF10A cells (Fig. 4H) were assembled into transcripts and analyzed in the same normal human mammary gland scRNA-seq dataset. Luminal HS cells displayed the highest expression levels of METTL3-dependent TEs compared with basal and luminal AV cells (Fig. 7D), supporting a positive correlation between increased TE levels and luminal cell differentiation. Consistent with our finding in mammary epithelial cell lines (Fig. 5C), major TE subfamilies including L1, L2, Alu, ERVK, and ERV1 were significantly enriched in luminal HS cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). These results highlight the potential role of TEs in driving luminal lineage specification during mammary gland development (Fig. 7E).

Discussion

Previous studies have been focused on the oncogenic role of METTL3 and the development of small molecule inhibitors targeting METTL3 (25); however, the role of METTL3 in precancerous tissues is much less well understood. Our study revealed a critical function of METTL3 as a suppressor of mammary epithelial cell growth. Our findings are linked to the previous reports that m6A modification is critical in regulating the RNAs transcribed from transposable elements, particularly enriched for long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs) and long terminal repeat elements (LTRs) (17, 18). A recent study showed that METTL3 inhibitors can increase the accumulation of double-stranded RNA and induce interferon response in cancer cells (26). Additionally, inhibition of DNA methylation by DNMT inhibitors has been reported to induce ERV demethylation and expression, leading to increased dsRNA levels, activation of sensors such as MDA5 and TLR3, and downstream signaling through MAVS and IRF7 (27, 28). METTL3 can modulate chromatin accessibility and transcription through effects on heterochromatin and histone methylation (29, 30). A recent study shown that the METTL3–METTL14 complex recruits DNA methyltransferase DNMT1 to facilitate DNA methylation and regulate gene expression during embryonic stem cell differentiation (31), suggesting that METTL3 may also contribute to global transcriptional regulation during cell differentiation and tissue homeostasis. In our study, we have not observed a significant chromatin bound activity by METTL3 in mammary epithelial cells. Inflammatory signaling-induced lineage plasticity has been observed during the development of prostate adenocarcinomas, in which the JAK/STAT pathway initiated a mixed luminal-basal cell population in a cell autonomous manner (32). Previous reports have shown that STAT5 is prominent in controlling mammary epithelial cell differentiation and mammary gland development, and loss of STAT5 leads to disruption of luminal progenitor cells that differentiate into alveolar cells (33). We demonstrate the important cross talk between STAT signaling and luminal transcription factors in regulating mammary epithelial cell fate.
