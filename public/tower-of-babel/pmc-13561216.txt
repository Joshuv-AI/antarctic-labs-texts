A Suggestion to Use “Pedicle Reach” Instead of “Pedicle Length” for Communication in Free Flap Surgery
Authors: Steven Liben Zhang, Fu-Chan Wei
Published: 2026

Microsurgical free flap transfer has become a standard practice in reconstructive surgery today. Nevertheless, the terminology used to describe these procedures often lacks clear definition or consensus, leading to significant confusion in literature, clinical training, and professional communication. Notable examples include the terms “pedicle length” and “pedicle reach.” Although these are distinct concepts, they are frequently used interchangeably by reconstructive surgeons, leading to significant perioperative communication errors.

The lack of a standardized definition for pedicle length in existing literature has resulted in widespread ambiguity.1–5 When such misunderstandings occur intraoperatively, they may necessitate the selection of alternative recipient vessels or lead to unanticipated vein grafting. These adjustments not only increase surgical duration and complexity but, in extreme cases, may force the abandonment of the harvested flap or result in total flap failure.

Although defining a universal standard for pedicle length is crucial to mitigating these risks, achieving a global consensus remains a formidable challenge. A parallel can be seen in the 2-decade-long debate over the definition of a “perforator flap,” which remains inconsistently applied among surgeons today.6

Compared to the narrow and often confusing nature of pedicle length, pedicle reach offers a more comprehensive and practical solution. This concept integrates 3 critical determinants of successful reconstruction: the flap pedicle, the recipient vessels, and the orientation of the flap inset.

Flap pedicle factors: Pedicle reach accounts for the location of the source vessel division,1 luminal diameter, the number of branches,2,3 the position of the branch closest to the source vessel,4 and the specific entry point(s) into the flap.5

Consequently, we suggest that reconstructive microsurgeons shift their conceptual and communicative focus from pedicle length to pedicle reach to enhance surgical precision and outcomes.
