Exploring the Influence Factors of Pelvic Tilt Restoration After Surgical Treatment of Lumbar Degenerative Diseases: Value of Preoperative Standing-Sitting Radiograph
Authors: Zhiqian Luo, Yi Zhao, Tongyu Liu, Hanyu Guo, Yudong Zhao, Shengxi Jin, Haotian Yang, Weipeng Qiu, Zhuoran Sun, Weishi Li, Siyu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy DesignSingle-center retrospective study.ObjectivesIn lumbar degenerative diseases (LDD), pelvic retroversion compensates for sagittal malalignment. Surgical restoration of lumbar lordosis (LL) may reduce increased pelvic tilt (PT), while the influence factors of PT restoration (rPT) remain unclear. This study aimed to identify potential factors affecting rPT in LDD surgery and its correlation to postoperative clinical outcome.Methods161 LDD patients underwent surgical treatment were included. Sagittal parameters were measured in whole spine radiographs in standing and sitting. Changes from standing to sitting were denoted as Δ. The LL restoration (rLL) and rPT was calculated as postoperative - preoperative values. Clinical outcomes were evaluated using the visual analogue scale (VAS) and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). The influence factors of rPT were analyzed, and the sagittal parameters affecting post-operative ODI, leg pain VAS and low back pain VAS was examined.ResultsrLL (r=-0.403), PT (r=-0.375), SS (r=0.400), L4-S1 lordosis (r=0.324), LL (r=0.319) and TPA (r=-0.291) in standing, ΔPT (r=0.304) and ΔSS (r=-0.254) was significantly related to rPT. A predictive formula for rPT was developed as rPT= -0.265×PT- 0.114×rLL+ 0.112×ΔPT+ 2.841° (R2=0.350, P<0.001). Postoperatively, low back pain VAS was related to PT (r=0.358, p<0.01) and PI-LL (r=0.246, p<0.05), and ODI was related to SVA (r=0.253, p<0.05).ConclusionLL restoration, preoperative PT, and ΔPT were identified as predictors of rPT. This study revealed the potential clinical value of standing-sitting radiograph in surgical planning of LDD, and further highlights the importance of restoring sagittal balance in lumbar surgery.Keywords: lumbar degenerative disease, spinopelvic alignment, standing-sitting radiograph, lumbar interbody fusion

Introduction

In recent decades, spinopelvic alignment has become an important focus in the study of lumbar degenerative disease (LDD). In the development of lumbar degenerative diseases (LDD), a progressive decrease of lumbar lordosis (LL) induces an anterior shift of the center of gravity . To maintain global sagittal balance, patients recruit compensatory mechanisms, most notably pelvic retroversion, which is characterized by an increased pelvic tilt (PT).1,2 Correcting such sagittal malalignment in spine and pelvis is important for better outcome after surgical treatment of LDD.3-6

Unlike lumbar lordosis (LL) which can be directly processed through lumbar decompression and fixation, the restoration of pelvic tilt (PT) is influenced by a combination of factors, including the restoration of LL and the relief of clinical symptoms, so it is difficult to predict PT restoration during surgery.7,8 Besides, different extent of compensatory pelvic retroversion may obscure the patient’s physiological PT, which makes it more complicated for spine surgeons to consider PT during surgical planning.1,2 Therefore, identifying the influence factors of PT restoration is necessary to provide references for lumbar surgery, and evaluating the state of both spinal curvature and pelvic rotation would be beneficial. It may help surgeons anticipate the degree of PT correction achievable after LL reconstruction.

To evaluate the state of pelvic rotation, dynamic radiological examinations in different positions are important. Comparing to the classic flexion-extension X-ray radiographs, larger changes in LL and PT were reported between standing and sitting postures, which could be better in recognizing the range of spinopelvic motion preoperatively. 9 Changes of spine and pelvis between standing and sitting was reported to be related to lumbar degeneration.10,11 However, whether these standing-to-sitting dynamic characteristics can predict postoperative PT restoration has not been investigated.

The present study aims to explore the influence factors of postoperative PT restoration in the surgical treatment of LDD. Our hypothesis is that both spinopelvic alignment and pelvic dynamic characteristics—quantified as the change in PT from standing to sitting— play an important role in the restoration of PT after rebuilding lumbar curvature, as shown in Figure 1. The present study is expected to help surgeons anticipate PT correction after LL reconstruction and support individualized surgical planning.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study was a single-center retrospective study in patients with lumbar degenerative diseases. The study was performed within the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the hospital with the requirement for informed consent being waived.

Participants

A total of 308 patients who underwent posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) for lumbar degenerative diseases from 2018 to 2023 were included in this study. The inclusion criteria were: (1) underwent PLIF in our center for clinical diagnosed lumbar degenerative diseases; (2) patients aged over 30 years were enrolled; (3) LL was restored after surgery (postoperative LL> preoperative LL), indicating successful surgical restoration of lordosis; (4) with completed perioperative clinical data required; (5) with complete 1-year clinical follow-up after surgery. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of former spinal or lower extremity surgery; (2) with non-degenerative spinal diseases such as vertebral fracture, spinal tuberculosis, spinal tumor, congenital spinal deformity etc.; (3) with neuromuscular diseases; (4) with severe hip or knee joint diseases. Of the 308 patients, 147 were excluded for the following reasons: 56 were excluded because of a lack of available follow-up X-ray, 91 were excluded because postoperative LL did not exceed preoperative LL. Ultimately, 161 patients were included in the analysis.

Surgical Procedures

Surgical indications for fusion included segmental instability, such as spondylolisthesis, or predictable instability due to excessive removal of the facet joints. All patients underwent laminotomy, posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) with insertion of a PEEK cage, and fixation with a transpedicular screw fixation device. Intraoperatively, the paraspinal muscles were first stripped from their attachments on the spinous processes along the bilateral laminae and retracted laterally to expose the articular processes; pedicle screws were then implanted in a routine fashion. Next, the interspinous ligament and spinous process were removed, and a laminectomy was completed. Thereafter, the intervertebral disc tissue was excised, and an appropriately sized PEEK cage packed with autogenous bone was placed as a replacement; a rigid connecting rod was then applied to connect the pedicle screws. All surgeries were performed by five senior chief physicians in our department with comparable seniority and experience, using a standardized technique.

Assessment

Preoperatively, the clinical symptoms of the patients were recorded. The visual analogue scale (VAS; 0-10) was used to evaluate the low back and leg pain, and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI; 0-100) score was used to evaluate the patients’ quality of life. Outcome data were collected at two primary time points: (1) preoperatively, within one week before the surgery, and (2) at the 12-month postoperative follow-up visit. All assessments were administered by an independent research coordinator using standardized written and verbal instructions to all patients.

Radiology Evaluation

All patients underwent anteroposterior and lateral whole spine X-ray examination in standing preoperatively and postoperatively. Postoperative imaging was routinely performed at 12 months after surgery. All patients completed the final follow-up, during which identical standing radiological work-ups were conducted. Additionally, 84 of them further underwent radiography while sitting.

GE Optima XR646 digital radiography system (General Electric) was used to take the radiographs. For standing radiographs, patients maintained a natural upright posture with fingers resting on the ipsilateral clavicles to prevent arm overlap. For sitting radiographs, patients sat upright on a height-adjustable stool, maintaining the same clavicular hand position, with hips and knees flexed at 90° and feet flat on the floor.

The sagittal parameters were measured by two spine surgeons with more than 3 years’ clinical experience using the PACS system (Picture Archiving and Communication System, GE Healthcare) of the hospital. The following parameters were measured: (1) pelvic parameters: pelvic incidence (PI), PT, sacral slope (SS); (2) spinal parameters: LL, thoracic kyphosis (TK), thoracolumbar kyphosis (TLK): the angle between the upper endplate of T11 and the lower endplate of L1, L4-S1 lordosis: the angle between the upper endplate of L4 and the sacral endplate; (3) global parameters: sagittal vertical axis (SVA), T1 pelvic angle (TPA). The measurement protocol was shown in Figure 2. The extent of postoperative restoration was mathematically defined as the postoperative value minus the preoperative standing value (i.e., rPT = postoperative PT - preoperative PT; rLL = postoperative LL - preoperative LL). Further, Δ represents the change from standing to sitting, such as ΔPT means the PT in sitting minus PT in standing. Intra- and inter-observer agreement of spinopelvic parameters were evaluated using ICC, based on measurements by two independent observers in 20 patients, with one observer remeasuring after 4 weeks for intra-observer reliability.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM). Continuous variables were shown in form of mean± standard deviation. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies. Independent sample t-test was used to compare the rPT between the groups with and without S1 screw fixation across the overall cohort. Paired-samples t-test was applied to the following comparisons within the same patients: (1) preoperative versus postoperative standing sagittal parameters for all patients; and (2) for the subset of patients with available sitting radiographs, preoperative versus postoperative standing parameters were re-analyzed, and standing versus sitting parameters were compared. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to explore the relation among the sagittal parameters, and the relationship between postoperative sagittal parameters and clinical outcome. S1 screw fixation and number of fused segments were entered into the regression model as candidate predictors. Variables showing a statistically significant association with rPT in univariate correlation analysis were entered into a stepwise multiple linear regression model to identify independent predictors of rPT. Additionally, based on clinical experience and reviewer feedback, surgical factors with potential influence on sagittal correction, including the number of fused segments and S1 screw fixation, were also entered as candidate predictors. The entry and removal criteria for the stepwise procedure were set at P<0.05 and P>0.10, respectively. Multicollinearity was assessed by variance inflation factor (VIF), with values<5 considered acceptable. Model assumptions were checked using normal probability plots of residuals, scatterplots of residuals versus predicted values, and Durbin–Watson statistic. Statistical significance was set at the level of P<0.05.

Exploring the Predictive Factors of rPT

To explore the potential predictive factors of rPT, the Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated. In sitting position, no sagittal parameters were related to rPT, as shown in Figure 4. Concerning the dynamic characteristics from standing to sitting, ΔPT (r=0.304, P=0.005) and ΔSS (r=-0.254, P=0.020) was significantly related to rPT, as shown in Figure 4.

All sagittal parameters statistically related to rPT together with S1 screw fixation and number of fused segments were further included in stepwise linear regression analysis. S1 screw fixation and the number of fused segments were entered as candidate predictors in the stepwise linear regression model but were ultimately excluded from the final model because neither variable reached statistical significance. As shown in Table 5, in the linear regress model, PT (P<0.001) and rLL(P=0.030) were negatively related to rPT, while ΔPT(P=0.015) was positively related to rPT. Therefore, a predictive formula of rPT was established based on the previous parameters: rPT= -0.265×PT- 0.114×rLL+ 0.112×ΔPT+ 2.841° (R2=0.350, P<0.001).

Discussion

In recent decades, the role of spinopelvic alignment in spinal diseases has been gradually noticed and investigated by more and more spine surgeons. In the progression of LDD, change of spinopelvic alignment develops simultaneously, presenting as a decrease in LL with anterior shift tendency of spinal gravity, and pelvic retroversion was considered to be one of the most important compensation mechanisms to avoid further imbalance of the trunk. As a result, a decreased average LL together with an increased average PT could usually be observed in patients with LDD.1,2 Restoring such sagittal malalignment plays an important role in reducing the prevalence of low back pain and mechanical complications postoperatively.3-6 Therefore, evaluating the state of lumbar curvature together with pelvic rotation in detail is essential for the surgical planning of LDD.

The rationale for comparing patients with and without S1 screw fixation was to determine whether inclusion of the sacrum in the fusion construct influenced PT restoration. The independent-samples t-test showed no significant difference in rPT (-3.1±5.1° vs -2.8±4.4°, P=0.712) between the two groups, and S1 fixation was subsequently entered into the stepwise linear regression model as a candidate predictor, where it was also not retained. These results indicate that S1 fixation did not affect PT restoration, allowing us to exclude it as a confounding variable and pool these patients in the main analysis.

Although the importance of sagittal balance gradually becomes widely noticed in lumbar surgery, it could still be confusing to control postoperative PT when making surgical plans in clinical practice. The most important reason is that as a positional parameter, the restoration of PT partially relies on the rebuilding of LL and is difficult to be directly controlled or predicted in surgery. 7 Generally, a larger LL restoration is associated with a relatively anteverted postoperative pelvis, 12 which is also shown in our results. From a biomechanical perspective, rLL inherently acts a negative predictor for rPT: a greater surgical increase in LL directly removes the mechanical requirement for compensatory pelvic retroversion, forcing the pelvis to antevert and resulting in a more negative rPT (i.e., a greater magnitude of PT reduction). However, from an individualized point of view, it was observed that varying magnitudes of rPT could still occur even under similar rLL restoration. The low coefficient of determination (R2=0.162, p<0.001) of our regression analysis indicated that rLL alone could not fully predict the extent of PT restoration. The result suggests that achieving substantial PT restoration requires a more profound understanding of the factors influencing postoperative PT improvement beyond LL reconstruction alone. The possible reasons could be comprehensive, including individual variations of spinopelvic alignment, extent of pelvic compensation activated, the impact of clinical symptoms on posture and neuromuscular factors.13-15 Exploring the relationship between these factors and pelvic retroversion would be beneficial for the surgical planning of LDD.

When evaluating pelvic rotation preoperatively, the key is to identify the state of pelvic compensation. The absolute value of preoperative PT is a useful reference. In the present study, larger preoperative PT is an independent predictive factor of larger absolute value of rPT (β=-0.417, P<0.001). Because rPT is defined as the postoperative minus preoperative PT (yielding a negative value upon restoration), this negative correlation mechanically implies that patients with larger preoperative PT have a highly retroverted pelvis with more pelvic compensation activated, providing a greater “ceiling” or biomechanical runway for reduction after surgery. However, unlike the well-known PT<20° proposed by Schwab et al 16 in patients with adult spinal deformity(ASD), there is still lack of a cut-off value of PT in patients with LDD. The current situation could be due to the relatively conservative pelvic compensation in patients with LDD comparing to ASD, 17 which leads to a small-scale average rPT (-3.0±4.8° in this study) after surgical treatment of LDD.5,18 Considering the small-scale rPT, a simple threshold of PT could be difficult to distinguish the compensation and physiological variation in spinopelvic alignment. Therefore, for patients with LDD, an individualized point of view would be especially important.

PI, the anatomical parameter of pelvis, is a vital reference for the individualized evaluation of spinopelvic alignment due to its relatively stability in adulthood and close relation to other sagittal parameters.19-21 With the geometric relationship PI=PT+SS, PI was considered to be positively related to the capacity of pelvis retroversion. 1 However, in a recent study, no statistical relevance was found between pelvis extension reserve and PI, 22 and also in our study, PI was not related to the postoperative rPT (r=0.081, P=0.306), indicating that PI-based predictive models could be limited in evaluating pelvic compensation. Therefore, dynamic analysis of spinopelvic alignment in standing-sitting posture was additionally applied in this study. As a common functional posture in daily life, sitting requires a relatively flat LL with retroverted pelvis, 23 and the process from standing to sitting presented larger change in LL and PT comparing to the common extension-flexion radiograph. 9 Further, Zhou et al 15 recently revealed PT in sitting is similar among asymptomatic people with different pelvic position in standing, indicating ΔPT from standing to sitting could be a valuable parameter to reflect the potential of pelvic retroversion. Similar findings have been reported in patients with adult spinal deformity ASD. Previous studies demonstrated that ASD patients exhibited reduced standing-to-sitting changes in PT and other sagittal parameters compared with asymptomatic individuals, suggesting that compensatory mechanisms could be substantially activated in standing posture and that the remaining compensatory reserve was limited. 24 In the present study, ΔPT was demonstrated to be an independent predictive factor of rPT (β=0.226, P=0.015) in LDD patients with restored LL after surgery. Because a more negative rPT indicates greater PT restoration, this positive coefficient mathematically signifies that a smaller preoperative ΔPT predicts a greater magnitude of PT reduction. Combining the previous studies, a possible explanation is that preoperatively, smaller ΔPT indicates relatively less pelvic retroversion reserve and more pelvic compensation, and more restoration of pelvic retroversion could be expected in those patients after rebuilding LL. If a patient presents with a small ΔPT, surgeons can prioritize standard focal LL restoration, anticipating a robust and predictable PT restoration. These results showed that the standing-sitting radiograph could be with potential clinical value in the individualized evaluation of pelvic compensation in patients with LDD, which is worth further investigating in the future.

The correlation between sagittal balance and postoperative clinical outcome has been widely demonstrated. In the present study, the postoperative PT and PI-LL presented low to moderate relation to the VAS of low back pain, and larger SVA was found to be associated with poorer quality of life for patients. The present results are in accordance to the previous studies,25-27 which again stressed the necessity of rebuilding sagittal balance and pelvic rotation in the surgical treatment of LDD. A proper postoperative spinopelvic alignment could be important to avoid stress concentration in adjacent segments and to decrease the consumption of paraspinal muscles, which is potential causations for residual low back pain after surgery.28,29

Limitations

As an explorative study, the present study built a predictive model for rPT, and revealed importance of restoring sagittal balance in surgical treatment of LDD. However, several limitations should still be noticed. Firstly, in this study, the influence of bony alignment was the main concern, while the influence of muscle, ligament or other soft tissue was not included, which needs further exploration. Secondly, most of the patients in this study underwent surgery with less than 3 levels, and if the predictive model in this study is applicable in LDD patients with multi-level surgery needs to be further examined. Thirdly, only patients with restored LL were included. This may introduce selection bias, but was necessary because our aim was to investigate why PT restoration varies despite successful LL correction. Fourthly, although a standard surgical technique was applied in this study, there is still potential bias as a single-center study. However, the present study is expected to provide clues for the future larger scale, multicentric and prospective study to further explore the clinical value of standing-sitting radiograph in the surgical planning of LDD.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that LL restoration, preoperative PT and preoperative change of PT in standing and sitting were independent predictive factors of PT restoration in the surgical treatment of LDD, and a predictive formula was further built. Moreover, postoperative PT and PI-LL were associated with the residual low back pain after surgery, and a smaller SVA was related to better quality of life. The present study revealed the potential clinical value of standing-sitting radiograph in surgical planning of LDD, and again stressed the importance of restoring sagittal balance in lumbar surgery.
