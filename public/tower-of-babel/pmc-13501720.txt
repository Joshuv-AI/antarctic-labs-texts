Mitral valve vegetations in an elderly patient with acute myeloid leukemia: navigating the overlap between infection and malignancy: a case report
Authors: Mais Musleh, Mahmoud Alhamadeh Alswij, Khaled Omar ALdosh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a hematologic malignancy that can rarely manifest with clinical features resembling infective endocarditis (IE), particularly in elderly individuals. Non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis (NBTE), a paraneoplastic complication, may present with sterile cardiac vegetations and systemic embolic events, posing significant diagnostic challenges.Case presentationWe report the case of an 82-year-old Syrian Arab woman who presented with fever, generalized fatigue, and confusion. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed vegetations on the mitral valve, raising suspicion for IE. However, repeated blood cultures remained negative, and inflammatory markers were only mildly elevated. Laboratory investigations showed marked leukocytosis, anemia, and thrombocytopenia. The presence of sterile vegetations, progressive leukocytosis, and absence of microbiologic evidence supported a diagnosis of NBTE as a paraneoplastic manifestation of AML.ConclusionThis case illustrates the importance of considering hematologic malignancies in the differential diagnosis of culture-negative endocarditis. NBTE should be suspected in patients with cardiac vegetations, systemic embolic signs, and unexplained leukocytosis, particularly when blood cultures are repeatedly sterile. Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a clonal hematopoietic malignancy characterized by the proliferation and accumulation of immature myeloid precursors in the bone marrow and peripheral blood, resulting in ineffective hematopoiesis and bone marrow failure. It is the most common acute leukemia in adults, with an incidence that increases with age, and a median age at diagnosis of approximately 68 years [1]. Despite its relatively low overall prevalence, AML accounts for a disproportionate percentage of leukemia-related mortality, particularly in older adults, due to its aggressive course and limited tolerance for intensive chemotherapy in this population [2].

Cardiac manifestations of AML are uncommon and may occur through leukemic infiltration, thromboembolic phenomena, or paraneoplastic hypercoagulable states. One such paraneoplastic manifestation is non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis (NBTE), a rare but clinically significant entity characterized by sterile fibrin–platelet vegetations on cardiac valves and a marked tendency toward systemic embolization [3–5]. NBTE may closely mimic infective endocarditis (IE), often leading to diagnostic delay.

Here, we report an elderly patient who presented with mitral valve vegetations initially suggestive of IE but was ultimately diagnosed with AML complicated by presumed NBTE. This case highlights the diagnostic complexity of culture-negative endocarditis in immunocompromised patients and emphasizes the importance of considering NBTE in the differential diagnosis of valvular lesions in patients with unexplained leukocytosis or systemic embolic events.

Case presentation

A 82-year-old Syrian Arab woman presented to the emergency department with a fever of 38.5 °C, accompanied by generalized fatigue, pallor, and new-onset confusion. Three months prior, she had been admitted for an ischemic cerebrovascular event, during which she experienced altered mental status, a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 13/15, right-sided facial droop, and a normal echocardiogram with a preserved ejection fraction of 60%. Her white blood cell count at that time was within normal limits. She was discharged on atorvastatin 40 mg and bisoprolol 5 mg.

Upon current presentation, physical examination revealed that distal extremities were cool to touch, no lymphadenopathy or hepatosplenomegaly. Vital signs showed a respiratory rate of 34 breaths per minute and a blood pressure of 90/60 mmHg. Chest radiography CXR remained unremarkable and abdominal ultrasonography was unremarkable.

Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated marked leukocytosis with a white blood cell count of 19,000/μL, lymphocyte predominance (lymphocytes 75%, neutrophils 15%), severe anemia (hemoglobin 6.4 g/dL), and profound thrombocytopenia (platelet count 12,000/μL). Inflammatory markers were elevated, including C-reactive protein (CRP) at 8 mg/dL and procalcitonin (PCT) at 0.42 ng/mL. Additional findings included elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 560 U/L, serum creatinine of 1.4 mg/dL, serum calcium of 8.1 mg/dL, phosphate of 2.3 mg/dL, and uric acid 9 mg/dL. Coagulation studies revealed PT 19.8 seconds (control 12.0 seconds; activity 30%), INR 2.8, and aPTT of 76 seconds. Liver function tests showed total bilirubin 1.4 mg/dL, direct bilirubin 0.4 mg/dL, ALT 30 U/L, AST 45 U/L, and albumin 3.7 g/dL.

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed mobile echodense vegetations attached to both mitral valve leaflets, with larger involvement of the posterior leaflet, accompanied by severe mitral regurgitation and reduced left ventricular systolic function (ejection fraction 35%) (Fig. 1) and (Supplementary material video 1, 2). These findings were consistent with infective endocarditis (IE). On the second day of hospitalization, the patient developed an abrupt deterioration in consciousness (GCS 5/15), necessitating transfer to the intensive care unit. Brain MRI showed no acute intracranial hemorrhage or new ischemic stroke.

On 5th day of hospitalization, the patient’s white blood cell count rose markedly, peaking at 80,000/μL, with a pronounced left shift and a predominance of circulating immature cells, raising strong suspicion for an underlying hematologic malignancy.

Peripheral blood smear demonstrated monomorphic red blood cells, severe thrombocytopenia, and approximately 90% circulating blasts. The blasts were medium to large in size, with a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, fine chromatin, and prominent nucleoli. Immunophenotyping by flow cytometry revealed strong expression of CD34 (80%), CD117 (92%), CD33 (85%), myeloperoxidase (MPO) (91%), and HLA-DR (55%), confirming the diagnosis of acute myeloid leukemia (AML). Cytogenetic analysis showed a normal female karyotype (46,XX).

According to the 2022 European LeukemiaNet (ELN) classification, the patient was provisionally categorized as intermediate risk (cytogenetically normal AML), and considering the patient’s advanced age and poor performance status, induction therapy with the hypomethylating agent azacitidine at a dose of 100 mg once daily for 5 consecutive days was initiated.

During treatment, the patient developed acute arterial thrombosis of the right lower extremity, confirmed by Doppler ultrasonography. Therapeutic anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin was initiated. After 5 days of treatment, laboratory studies revealed WBC 5,000/μL, hemoglobin 9.5 g/dL, platelet count 22,000/μL, and CRP 6 mg/L.

Despite supportive measures and an initial hematologic response—evidenced by a reduction in leukocyte count from 80,000/μL to 5,000/μL and partial improvement in inflammatory markers—the patient’s clinical condition rapidly deteriorated. She died due to developed refractory shock, multi-organ dysfunction, and progressive coagulopathy as shown in Table 1. Discussion

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a clonal hematologic malignancy marked by the accumulation of immature myeloid precursors with impaired differentiation into functional blood elements [1]. Although it is the most common type of acute leukemia in adults, AML remains relatively rare, accounting for approximately 1% of adult malignancies in the United States. Nevertheless, its aggressive clinical course and high relapse rate contribute to nearly 2% of all cancer-related mortality [2].

We report an 82-year-old woman who presented with fever, confusion, and echocardiographic evidence of mitral valve vegetations—features highly suggestive of infective endocarditis (IE). Progressive leukocytosis and subsequent peripheral blood smear and flow cytometric analysis ultimately established the diagnosis of AML. This overlap illustrates the diagnostic complexity of elderly patients presenting with nonspecific systemic manifestations in whom infection and hematologic malignancy may coexist.

In cases of suspected culture-negative endocarditis, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is more sensitive than transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) for detecting valvular vegetations and should be performed whenever feasible [6]. In addition, extended microbiologic evaluation, such as serologic testing for fastidious organisms (for example, Coxiella burnetii, Bartonella spp., Tropheryma whipplei) and fungal biomarkers, are recommended in persistent culture-negative cases. Although these tests were not performed in our patient due to rapid clinical deterioration, the repeatedly sterile cultures, the mild inflammatory response, and hematologic findings favored a non-infectious etiology.

Non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis (NBTE) emerged as the most plausible diagnosis. NBTE is characterized by sterile fibrin–platelet vegetations with minimal inflammatory infiltration and a marked tendency toward embolization due to their friable nature [4, 5, 7, 8]. It is classically associated with advanced malignancies, particularly mucinous adenocarcinomas, but may also occur in hematologic cancers. Valvular vegetations have been reported in up to 20% of patients with advanced malignancy [9]. In our patient, the combination of sterile vegetations, systemic embolization, and newly diagnosed AML strongly supports NBTE as a paraneoplastic manifestation.

The pathogenesis of NBTE is multifactorial and is driven by malignancy-associated hypercoagulability mediated by cytokine release, endothelial injury, and increased expression of procoagulant factors [10, 11]. Another theoretical diagnostic consideration is cardiac infiltration by myeloid sarcoma (MS), an extramedullary manifestation of AML reported in 2.5–9.1% of cases [12–14]. Although no discrete mass was detected, leukemic infiltration may contribute to valvular pathology in selected patients.

Management of NBTE relies on two parallel strategies: treatment of the underlying malignancy and prevention of systemic embolization. Systemic anticoagulation is the cornerstone of therapy; however, in AML patients, its use is complicated by frequent thrombocytopenia and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).

Systemic anticoagulation is the cornerstone of NBTE management. Heparin-based regimens, particularly low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), are preferred over vitamin K antagonists due to superior efficacy in malignancy-associated hypercoagulability. However, anticoagulation in AML is frequently limited by profound thrombocytopenia and active DIC. Expert recommendations suggest that anticoagulation may be cautiously initiated when platelet counts exceed 30–50 × 10⁹/L, ideally with platelet transfusion support, particularly in patients with documented embolic events.

In our patient, prolonged coagulation parameters were consistent with DIC, necessitating cautious therapeutic decision-making. Anticoagulation with low–molecular-weight heparin was initiated following the development of arterial thrombosis, reflecting the delicate balance between thrombotic and hemorrhagic risks in this population. Given the patient’s advanced age and comorbidities, azacitidine was selected as disease-directed therapy.

From an infectious disease standpoint, IE remains a clinically significant cause of morbidity and mortality in elderly individuals. The diagnosis is generally guided by the modified Duke criteria, which emphasize positive blood cultures, evidence of endocardial involvement, and systemic embolization [3]. However, culture-negative endocarditis presents a particular diagnostic dilemma, accounting for 2.5–31% of all IE cases [15]. Common etiologies include prior antibiotic exposure and infections caused by fastidious organisms such as Coxiella burnetii, Bartonella spp., and Tropheryma whipplei [16].

Fungal endocarditis (FE) is another important differential diagnosis in immunocompromised hosts, including those with hematologic malignancies. Candida and Aspergillus species are the predominant pathogens, often presenting with large vegetations, systemic emboli, and high mortality exceeding 50% [17]. Diagnosis can be challenging due to frequent culture negativity—especially in Aspergillus infections—necessitating adjunctive testing such as serum galactomannan or β-D-glucan assays [17, 18]. In our patient, however, there was no clinical, microbiological, or imaging evidence supporting FE. Inflammatory markers were only mildly elevated, blood cultures remained negative, and the rapid hematologic deterioration suggested a malignant, rather than infectious, process.

Nonetheless, in patients with AML and valvular lesions, empirical antimicrobial therapy is justified until infective etiologies are definitively excluded [19]. This case emphasizes the need for early and repeated blood cultures, advanced imaging, and comprehensive hematologic evaluation when managing culture-negative endocarditis in the elderly or immunocompromised.

Conclusions

This case illustrates the diagnostic complexity of valvular vegetations in an elderly patient, ultimately attributed to NBTE as a paraneoplastic manifestation of AML. It underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach when facing overlapping infectious and malignant presentations. Furthermore, it highlights the necessity for future research to elucidate the pathophysiological relationship between NBTE and AML and to optimize risk-adapted management strategies for this unique and high-risk patient population.
