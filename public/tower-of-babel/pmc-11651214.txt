Mentalizing About Dynamic Social Action Sequences Is Supported by the Cerebellum, Basal Ganglia, and Neocortex: A Meta‐Analysis of Activation and Connectivity
Authors: Frank Van Overwalle, Elien Heleven, Naem Haihambo, Meijia Li, Qianying Ma, Min Pu, Chris Baeken, Natacha Deroost, Kris Baetens
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe posterior cerebellum and anterior basal ganglia are critical subcortical structures for learning and identifying dynamic action sequences, in concert with the neocortex. The present analysis investigates the role of action sequences during social mentalizing, termed here dynamic or sequential social mentalizing. Although the role of the cerebellum in dynamic social mentalizing was extensively investigated during the last decade, the basal ganglia were long ignored. We conducted an activation likelihood estimation coordinate‐based meta‐analysis of sequential social mentalizing tasks (with 485 participants in 17 studies). These tasks required participants to make social mentalizing inferences ranging from low‐level goals to high‐level beliefs and traits, while either memorizing, generating or predicting temporal sequences of the social actions involved (i.e., social sequencing condition), or not (i.e., social non‐sequencing control condition), or did so for nonsocial objects (i.e., nonsocial sequencing control condition). The tasks also occasionally included inconsistencies in social behavior. Results revealed that the cerebellum exhibited a preference for social, sequencing, and inconsistent information, while the basal ganglia showed a preference for sequencing and inconsistency, without a general preference for social input. Meta‐analytic connectivity analysis further showed evidence of coactivation between mentalizing areas of the cerebellum, basal ganglia and cerebral neocortex. The present work underscores the role of subcortical structures in social mentalizing about dynamic action sequences.

Introduction

Dynamic action sequences are an essential part of our everyday life. We encounter them not only in motor behavior, such as when we learn to walk, ride a bike or drive a car, but also in mental tasks, such as arithmetic and language, as well as in our social interactions and our thoughts about them. Social interactions show quasi‐ritual maneuvers of turn‐taking, information exchange, mutual understanding and alternate step‐taking in collaboration. To understand our social interactions, we need to “mentalize” about other people, that is, to infer their mental state or “theory of mind” (i.e., their intentions, beliefs, preferences, emotions, traits, and so on). To do so adequately, we use information on their actions during social event sequences.

The main question addressed in this article is which neocortical and subcortical brain structures support dynamic social cognition, in particular social mentalizing, and how these structures interact with each other. We define dynamic or sequential social mentalizing as the understanding of the mental state of a person based on social actions while they unfold sequentially in time. A dynamic view on social mentalizing is important, because in line with a predictive coding framework (Friston 2012), the brain is constantly trying to predict the next stage of the social world, in order to engage fluently in social behavior, just like it does for nonsocial physical events. Social interaction, which typically involves a give and take from both parties, requires a good sense of the mental state of the interacting persons which goes beyond the mere observation of their actions, because this allows better anticipation about what is coming next, and quick corrective responses if expectations are not met. For instance, when a person gets closer to you, it is more critical to know why than just observing the behavior: it might reflect the person's romantic or harmful goal, emotions of lust or anger, or traits of friendliness or aggression. This prepares for adequate action such as a gentle kiss or a distant look. Moreover, the repetition of habitual social sequences may lead to automatization of temporal sequences in complex hierarchies of coordinated social behaviors, which frees up neural and behavioral resources for the intake of novel information, allowing more fluent, adaptive and sophisticated human action and thought. It has been argued that the neocortex alone might not be sufficient to account for the different levels at which observed actions can be understood (Caligiore et al. 2013). We propose that during social mentalizing, the concerted processing of neocortical and subcortical structures of the cerebellum and basal ganglia is needed.

The cerebellum and basal ganglia have traditionally been thought of as subcortical structures responsible for movement coordination, control, learning and automatization (Doyon et al. 2009), but recent research has uncovered their contribution to a variety of non‐motor mental functions. During mammalian phylogenetic evolution, the number of neurons in the posterior cerebellum increased in close concert with the neocortex. In humans, the cerebellum contains about 80% of all neurons in the brain, while the basal ganglia less than 1% (Lent et al. 2012), and the cerebellar cortex is about 80% of the surface of that of the cerebral neocortex (Sereno et al. 2020).

The non‐motor mental functions of the cerebellum and basal ganglia include divers processes such as action observation, emotion understanding, reward‐ and error‐based learning, language, decision‐making, working memory, and spatial navigation (Bostan and Strick 2018; Eisinger et al. 2018; Haber and Knutson 2010; Lanciego, Luquin, and Obeso 2012; Pierce and Péron 2020; Schmahmann et al. 2019; Simonyan 2019). The cerebellum and basal ganglia are organized in multiple neural networks with connections to the cerebral neocortex, encompassing a diversity of functions and neural networks as revealed by various atlases of these three brain structures (Buckner et al. 2011; Choi, Thomas Yeo, and Buckner 2012; King et al. 2019; Van Overwalle et al. 2023; Yeo et al. 2011).

Recent evidence demonstrated that the cerebellum is involved in learning the dynamic sequences of social actions during mentalizing (Van Overwalle, Coninck, et al. 2019; Van Overwalle, Pu, et al. 2021; Van Overwalle and Heleven 2023). The cerebellum also exchanges information on sequential social mentalizing with the cerebrum through reciprocal functional connections (Haihambo and Van Overwalle 2023; Li, Pu, Baetens, et al. 2024; Ma et al. 2023; Pu et al. 2023; Van Overwalle, Van de Steen, and Mariën 2019; Van Overwalle, Van de Steen, van Dun, et al. 2020). However, no systematic overview yet exists of the relative contribution of distinct social or sequential aspects of cerebellar functioning. There is also growing evidence that the basal ganglia, involved in reward‐driven sequence learning (Janacsek et al. 2020), support social functions with respect to the understanding of observed biological motion (Caligiore et al. 2013), although we are not aware of research investigating its role in dynamic social mentalizing. Moreover, what is the exact interconnection between the cerebellum, basal ganglia and cerebral neocortex in dynamic social mentalizing? The aim of the present meta‐analysis is to put some light on these questions.

The Cerebellum

The cerebellum is generally known for its critical role in error‐based learning (Caligiore et al. 2019). This subcortical brain structure is tightly connected to the cerebral neocortex via reciprocal connections, which enable a highly synchronized exchange of neural signals. These connections consist of downward connections that initiate at specific functional areas in the neocortex and transmit information via the midbrain pons to specific areas in the cerebellum, which is then projected back upward to provide feedback to the neocortex, mainly via the thalamus (Granziera et al. 2009; Kelly and Strick 2003). Contrary to earlier proposals that these reciprocal connections consist of massive parallel multisynaptic one‐to‐one axonal connections (Kelly and Strick 2003), recent animal research demonstrated markedly divergent and unidirectional connectivity, where, for example, axons from the pons consist of multiple branches that connect to several cerebellar lobules, often in both hemispheres (Biswas et al. 2019; Na, Sugihara, and Shinoda 2019). However, over learning, initially divergent connections converge onto a shared set of neural activity, supporting reciprocal functional connectivity (Wagner et al. 2019; Zhu et al. 2023). It is assumed that the topographical location of these reciprocal connections in the cerebellum determine its various functionalities, shaping a functional network structure of the cerebellum (Buckner et al. 2011; Ji et al. 2019; Nettekoven et al. 2023; Van Overwalle et al. 2023), in close parallel with similar networks in the cerebral neocortex (Yeo et al. 2011). Both brain structures include the mentalizing network, which supports social mentalizing and takes up the larger part of the default mode network (Andrews‐Hanna et al. 2010; Andrews‐Hanna, Smallwood, and Spreng 2014), which is also referred to as the default mode network B (Braga et al. 2019; Braga and Buckner 2017).

Based on the uniform architecture of the cerebellar cortex and the theory of a universal cerebellar transform function derived from it (Schmahmann et al. 2019), it has been suggested that the cerebellum plays a critical role in the identification and learning of temporal and spatial sequences in motor and non‐motor mental processes, as postulated by the sequencing hypothesis (Leggio et al. 2011; Leggio and Molinari 2015). The sequences identified from neocortical input are encoded in cerebellar internal models, and after repeated exposure, are fine‐tuned and automatized, and so allow individuals to quickly and fluently anticipate and prepare dynamic reactions to salient environmental stimuli (Ito 2008). Crucially, by integrating and predicting sequences in real‐time, internal models enable observers to detect prediction errors (i.e., the difference between expected and actual outcome of internal and external stimuli) even while the action is ongoing, which allow them to quickly anticipate potential problems and prepare corrective actions, thereby also improving future predictions (Ito 2008; Wolpert, Miall, and Kawato 1998).

The Basal Ganglia

The basal ganglia are widely acknowledged as playing a central role in reward‐driven learning (Caligiore et al. 2019). This structure is also involved in sequence learning (Janacsek et al. 2020) and together with the cerebellum might form an interconnected functional system that acts on the timing, selection, and sequencing of ongoing movements and actions in order to reach a rewarding outcome (Baetens et al. 2020; Bostan and Strick 2018; Milardi et al. 2019). The basal ganglia consist of nuclei including the striatum (composed of the caudate, putamen, and nucleus accumbens), which is by far the largest subcortical structure in the mammalian brain, and intrinsic nuclei (e.g., globus pallidus). The basal ganglia are connected to the cerebral neocortex via a number of distinct parallel loops. The striatum is the main nucleus of the basal ganglia that gets input via these parallel loops from many cerebral neocortical areas, that is relayed to the intrinsic basal ganglia nuclei and then projected back to the neocortex, mainly via the thalamus (Lanciego, Luquin, and Obeso 2012; Simonyan 2019). The input from the cerebral neocortex is topographically organized, with motor and non‐motor mental functional subdivisions mapped onto different parts of the striatum (Arsalidou, Duerden, and Taylor 2013; Choi, Thomas Yeo, and Buckner 2012). The various reward functions in motor and mental processes and their interconnections with other brain structures, are reflected in a network structure of the basal ganglia (Choi, Thomas Yeo, and Buckner 2012) which reveals the same canonical networks as in the cerebellum (Buckner et al. 2011) and the cerebral neocortex (Yeo et al. 2011), including the mentalizing/default mode network.

The basal ganglia play a crucial role in the initiation and execution of goal‐directed actions in response to rewarding stimuli. This function regulates motor behavior, as well as a broad range of non‐motor behaviors, including emotions, language, decision‐making, procedural learning, and working memory (Arsalidou, Duerden, and Taylor 2013; Simonyan 2019). The striatum processes reward information received from dopamine neurons in the midbrain that signal motivation and reward levels (Bostan and Strick 2018; Ikemoto, Yang, and Tan 2015; Jin, Tecuapetla, and Costa 2014; Schultz, Dayan, and Montague 1997). These reward signals allow the basal ganglia to learn from positive or negative outcomes of actions and modify their output accordingly, modulating behavior and mental states that influence how the individual will react to similar situations in the future (Caligiore et al. 2013, 2019). These learned rewards enable observers to detect reward prediction errors (i.e., the difference between the expected and the actual reward obtained), which is crucial in changing priorities for action in a given social situation (Daniel and Pollmann 2014; Schultz, Dayan, and Montague 1997). Identifying the prime reward underlying an action and the temporal trajectory directed toward it, is pivotal in understanding many social actions and the motives and emotions that drive the person (Caligiore et al. 2013).

Social Mentalizing in the Neocortex, Cerebellum, and the Basal Ganglia

As noted earlier, social mentalizing refers to the understanding of other people's mental state, which is supported by the mentalizing/default mode network (Andrews‐Hanna et al. 2010; Andrews‐Hanna, Smallwood, and Spreng 2014; Braga et al. 2019; Braga and Buckner 2017). A schematic anatomical representation of this mentalizing network is shown in Figure 1. The contribution of the neocortical mentalizing network to social mentalizing is well established in earlier meta‐analyses (Schurz et al. 2014; Van Overwalle 2009). Key areas in the neocortex include the temporoparietal junction (TPJ), medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), and precuneus (PCun).

Not shown is the bilateral temporoparietal junction in the cerebrum.

Research has confirmed that the cerebellum is also involved in social mentalizing, and in particular the posterior cerebellar Crus 1 and 2 (Van Overwalle et al. 2014, 2015; Van Overwalle, Van de Steen, et al. 2020; Figure 1). Recent neuroimaging, neurostimulation and patient studies consistently showed that the posterior cerebellar Crus area is recruited for mentalizing about social action sequences. These mentalizing inferences range from low‐level social goal‐directed navigation (Li et al. 2021, 2023; Li, Haihambo, et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024; Li, Pu, Ma, et al. 2024; Tedesco et al. 2017), to higher‐level beliefs by others (Catoira et al. 2023; Heleven, van Dun, and Van Overwalle 2019; Ma et al. 2021, 2022; Van Overwalle, Coninck, et al. 2019), to high‐level personality traits (Pu et al. 2020, 2021; Pu, Heleven, et al. 2022; Pu, Ma, et al. 2022). When predicting future social action sequences based on mentalizing inferences (e.g., the trait of a person), the Crus area is also activated (Haihambo et al. 2022, 2024; Haihambo, Haihambo, Ma, Baetens, Bylemans, et al. 2023; Haihambo, Ma, Baetens, Pu, et al. 2023), and additionally, lobule IX, which is also a part of the mentalizing network located in the inferior posterior cerebellum (Figure 1). A brief overview of most of the mentalizing tasks in recent research that investigated the role of social action sequences is shown in Figure 2. The cerebellum is also strongly activated in these tasks when social inconsistencies occur, for example, when social trajectories are unexpected or violate social norms (Li et al. 2023; Li, Pu, Ma, et al. 2024), and when behaviors are inconsistent with inferred personality traits (Pu et al. 2021) and stereotypes (Pu, Ma, et al. 2022). Such inconsistencies are known to trigger the mentalizing network in order to identify the source of the social violation (Van Overwalle and Baetens 2009).

In this prediction task, sentences had to be selected which were consistent with advanced information about a protagonist's trait, and had to be put in a correct chronological order (Haihambo et al. 2022).

In addition, there is growing evidence for reciprocal functional connections between the mentalizing network in the cerebellum and the cerebral neocortex. The neocortex might provide input for sequence detection in the cerebellum, which may return safe or error feedback signals as output to the neocortex (Leggio and Molinari 2015). Using dynamic causal modeling, several neuroimaging studies uncovered reciprocal functional connections between the posterior cerebellar Crus 1 and 2, and key areas in the neocortical mentalizing network (Ma et al. 2023; Pu et al. 2023; Van Overwalle, Van de Steen, and Mariën 2019; Van Overwalle, Van de Steen, van Dun, et al. 2020). These mentalizing areas, and the connections between them, demonstrate that the cerebellum has an important modulatory influence on social thinking in the neocortex.

Although this recent work on the cerebellum has widened our insights on social mentalizing in subcortical brain areas, research remained largely silent on a potential role for the basal ganglia. Nonetheless, the basal ganglia are also implicated in the mentalizing/default model network, especially the anterior part, termed the striatum, which encompasses the caudate and putamen (Choi, Thomas Yeo, and Buckner 2012; Figure 1). With the exception of some authors (Caligiore et al. 2013; Daniel and Pollmann 2014; Schmahmann et al. 2019; Van Overwalle et al. 2014, 2015; Van Overwalle, Van de Steen, et al. 2020), however, many reviews and meta‐analyses on the functionality of the cerebellum and/or basal ganglia are remarkedly silent on a distinct “social” function in these subcortical structures (Arsalidou, Duerden, and Taylor 2013; Bostan and Strick 2018; Eisinger et al. 2018; Haber and Knutson 2010; Ikemoto, Yang, and Tan 2015; Lanciego, Luquin, and Obeso 2012; Simonyan 2019). Therefore, an outstanding question is: Do the basal ganglia support mentalizing based on dynamic sequences of human social actions in interaction with the cerebellum and neocortex?

Present Meta‐Analysis

To answer this question, the present meta‐analysis investigates the role of the subcortical cerebellum and basal ganglia in supporting dynamic social mentalizing. Detailed knowledge of the involvement of the cerebellum and basal ganglia in these processes may improve our understanding of their potential social functions to contribute to the well‐known role of the cerebral neocortex in social mentalizing, and may also point to their potential roles in social dysfunctions and mental disorders (Van Overwalle, Baeken, et al. 2021).

In summary, there is evidence to support the idea that both the cerebellum and the basal ganglia are involved in initiating and motivating action responses and sequences (Fermin et al. 2016). Furthermore, both subcortical structures also communicate directly with each other and with the cerebral neocortex via reciprocal pathways (Bostan, Dum, and Strick 2010; Bostan and Strick 2018; Chen et al. 2014; Hoshi et al. 2005; Kostadinov and Häusser 2022; Xiao et al. 2018). These pathways allow the basal ganglia to incorporate temporal sequential information from the cerebellum to guide the most appropriate and rewarding action plan, as well as the cerebellum to encode specific action sequences that lead to the expected reward from the basal ganglia (Caligiore et al. 2013; Wagner et al. 2017). Both the basal ganglia and cerebellum receive neocortical inputs that enable them to select and modify sequences of rewarding social behaviors, and to send feedback signals to the neocortex when behavioral adjustments are required (Caligiore et al. 2013).

To analyze the contribution of the cerebellum and basal ganglia in mentalizing based on dynamic social actions, we conducted a meta‐analysis of neuroimaging studies that investigated the critical role of sequencing across a variety of social mentalizing inferences (see Figure 2). Essentially, these tasks required participants (a) to make classic mentalizing inferences ranging from low‐level goals to high‐level beliefs and traits in combinations with (b) detecting, memorizing, or predicting the temporal sequence of the underlying social actions. We excluded lower‐level social processes involving the observation of human movements (so‐called “mirroring”) as these are supported by distinct neural circuits (Molenberghs, Cunnington, and Mattingley 2012; Van Overwalle and Baetens 2009) and which do not involve mentalizing inferences. Therefore, we focus on mentalizing tasks which manipulate temporal information of static, schematic or verbal stimuli, rather than fluent biological movements or interactions such as in videos.

To investigate the preferential functionality of the neocortex, posterior cerebellum and anterior basal ganglia in these studies, we isolated the preference of each of these structures for distinct functional aspects such as (1) social input, (2) sequencing in the social domain, (3) social inconsistencies and, as a way of global baseline, for (4) nonsocial sequencing. These distinct functional preferences were identified by contrasting the main experimental condition involving sequential social mentalizing against the relevant opposing condition (see Section 2). New is that we systematically compare for the first time the preference of these various inputs.

Based on earlier research on dynamic social mentalizing reviewed above, for the cerebellum, we hypothesize a strong preference for (1) social, (2) sequencing, and (3) inconsistency information in the social domain in Crus 1, 2, and/or lobule IX, and only minimal activation in the (4) nonsocial domain. For the basal ganglia, we explored these social mentalizing preferences for the first time. Based on the meta‐analysis by Janacsek et al. (2020), we hypothesize a preference for (2) sequencing in the striatum. Besides that, we did not have any clear hypotheses.

In addition to a meta‐analysis of activation in the cerebellum and basal ganglia, we also conducted a meta‐analytic connectivity modeling (MACM) analysis. That is, for the social domain, we explore functional coactivations between the cerebellum, basal ganglia and key mentalizing areas in the cerebral neocortex. For the cerebellum, we hypothesized strong connectivity during distinct aspects of dynamic social mentalizing (cf. preferences 1–3) with neocortical key mentalizing areas. Note that this analysis is novel, because earlier research on functional connectivity during dynamic mentalizing was based on a single preference/contrast (Haihambo and Van Overwalle 2023; Li, Pu, Baetens, et al. 2024; Ma et al. 2023; Pu et al. 2023; Van Overwalle, Van de Steen, and Mariën 2019; Van Overwalle, Van de Steen, van Dun, et al. 2020), while the present analysis compares the strength of connectivity across distinct preferences. For the basal ganglia, we explore for the first time whether similar connectivity patterns exist with the cerebellar and neocortical key mentalizing areas.

Methods

Selection of Studies and Contrasts

We selected published fMRI studies that reported brain activity in the cerebellum or basal ganglia with a neurotypical population during sequential social mentalizing tasks, that is, which involved social mentalizing based on a sequence of events, including judgments on goals, beliefs, traits or prediction of actions. The studies were searched using PubMed and published up to May 2024, applying the following logical combination of terms in the title or abstract: “fMRI” AND (“mentaliz*” OR “social*”) AND (“sequenc*” OR “predict*”) AND (“cerebell*” OR “basal” OR “caudate” OR “putamen”). In addition, 17 articles on the topic of sequential social mentalizing by the lab of Frank Van Overwalle and colleagues, were also included.

Inclusion Criteria

Studies were included in the meta‐analysis if they investigated brain activation in the main experimental condition while participants were required to make or use mentalizing judgments (e.g., on goals, beliefs, or traits) while implicitly learning, or explicitly memorizing, generating, or predicting sequences of past or future actions. The meta‐analysis investigated preferences for specific information by exploring four contrasts listed below (for brevity, in nominating the conditions of interest, we drop the term “mentalizing” because this is implied in all social conditions): Social specificity by contrasting mentalizing based on social action sequences (i.e., mentalizing about the actions of human or human‐like agents, of which the sequence had to be generated or memorized) against nonsocial sequences (i.e., inferring the characteristics of objects that underwent various events, of which the sequence had to be generated or memorized). Sequencing specificity by contrasting mentalizing based on social sequences (i.e., generation or memorization of sequences) against a social non‐sequential control condition involving social mentalizing without applying or generating any action sequences. Social inconsistency specificity which is a higher‐order function of social error processing in mentalizing by contrasting inconsistencies in ongoing social mentalizing (i.e., based on actions that are inconsistent with the trait or stereotype implied by foregoing action sequences, or violate social expectations and norms) against social mentalizing that does not imply such inconsistencies during action sequences. Nonsocial sequencing specificity by contrasting nonsocial sequences (which do not invite or implicate social mentalizing as illustrated above, i.e., inferring the characteristics of objects during events that they underwent, of which the sequence had to be generated or memorized) against a nonsocial non‐sequential control condition; this comparison provides a baseline to evaluate the preference for sequencing in the social domain (contrast 2).

Studies were included when they analyzed at minimum one of the specificities/contrasts 1–4 listed above. All selected tasks involved human or human‐like actions, either depicted visually (e.g., showing a cartoon‐like action of a person) or described verbally in short sentences. To exclude the participation of the action observation/mirror network, only schematic cartoon‐like, still figurative or verbal representations of events was allowed (see Figure 2). All studies reported increased activation in mentalizing areas of the posterior cerebellar Crus 1 and 2 (Buckner et al. 2011; Ji et al. 2019; Nettekoven et al. 2023; Van Overwalle et al. 2023) during a critical contrast 1–4. Note that noninvasive cerebellar and sham placebo stimulations were each included as separate “studies” (cf. Haihambo et al. 2024).

Selection of Studies

We identified 95 articles through the PubMed search after screening the title and/or abstract with the search terms listed above. After full‐text reading of the articles, and based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, we included only the 17 studies by the lab of Van Overwalle. To make sure that we did not miss any other relevant study, we additionally analyzed all 207 articles of the meta‐analysis by Van Overwalle, Van de Steen, et al. (2020). This meta‐analysis identified all studies that activated the posterior cerebellum in five regions of interest (ROI) reflecting social mentalizing processes. We searched for studies that were (a) coded as belonging to the “social” domain in this analysis, (b) with the terms “sequenc*” or “predict*” in the title, and (c) with the contrasts of each study reflecting these or similar terms or concepts. After a full text reading of six articles that survived these criteria, ultimately, none of the studies involved an implicit or explicit sequencing manipulation, and therefore none were included.

Participants

After selection, all studies came all from the lab of Frank Van Overwalle and colleagues, and involved human participants. We were not able to identify studies from other labs and universities that fulfilled the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Across all included studies, the participants (total n = 485, Table S1) were healthy and right‐handed (using the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory; Oldfield 1971). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant. The studies were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the University Hospital of Ghent, where all studies were conducted. Scanning for the studies took place on the same 3 T Siemens Magnetom Prisma fit scanner system (Siemens Medical Systems, Erlangen, Germany) using a 32‐ or 64‐channel radiofrequency head coil at the university of Gent. All studies used identical experimental procedures (using Eprime; Psychological Software Tools; https://pstnet.com) and a single fMRI software program (SPM12; Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology, London, UK), including the same in‐house scripts for running the fMRI analyses.

Design, Stimulus Material, and Procedures

For each type of task, we only briefly summarize the essential aspects of the design (primary contrasts between the main experimental vs. control conditions), materials, and procedure. For more details, we refer to the individual studies listed in Table S1. The sequencing tasks are described here in order of increasing complexity of the social mentalizing inferences made: goal/intentions (Task 1), beliefs (Tasks 2–3), traits (Task 4), and predictions (Task 5).

Task 1: Social Navigation

The social navigation task developed by Li et al. (2021) depicted a protagonist—a smurf—moving step‐after‐step through a spatial grid in order to reach a desired goal (e.g., a cake, money, etc.; Figure 2, Task 1). Participants were required to observe the trajectory taken by the smurf toward one of many desirable goals, while avoiding obstacles such as trees, plants, and rocks. Based on the end position of the smurf, after each trial, they had to indicate which goal the smurf had in mind. In the sequencing condition, they additionally had to memorize and subsequently reproduce the observed trajectory, while in the non‐sequencing condition they did not. In the nonsocial control condition, a ball followed exactly the same trajectory, but this was introduced as rolling around on an uneven terrain and stopping at a hole. After each trial, participants had to indicate what the end position was of the ball.

Inconsistency Manipulation

In some navigation studies, the trajectory implied a behavior and implied goal that was inconsistent with social expectations (e.g., providing an unwanted gift) or social norms (i.e., trespassing and damaging a forbidden area).

Task 2: Belief Sequencing

In this task, events were portrayed using cartoon‐like pictures (Picture Sequencing task; Langdon and Coltheart 1999; Figure 2, Task 2) or in brief sentences (Verbal Sequencing task developed by Heleven, van Dun, and Van Overwalle 2019); based on the Faux Pas test by Baron‐Cohen et al. (1999). In both tasks, the events were presented in a randomized order, and participants had to put them in a correct chronological order. The social events involved false beliefs (e.g., a chocolate is secretly eaten by someone else so that the chocolate box is surprisingly empty from the perspective of the protagonist), true beliefs (e.g., a chocolate is eaten jointly by the protagonist with someone else) and social scripts/routines (e.g., someone going shopping), whereas the nonsocial events involved mechanical scenarios (e.g., a truck accidently damaging a tree).

Task 3: Belief Serial Response Time

In the belief serial response time (SRT) task developed by Ma et al. (2021), participants were requested to identify true and false beliefs of two protagonists—Papa Smurf and Smurfette (Figure 2, Task 3). Participants saw one of two smurfs, receiving green flowers, flankered by other green plants, at four locations on top of the screen, and were requested to report how many flowers the smurf thought to have received. When the smurf was facing the screen, he or she could see the flowers and therefore held a true belief about the number of flowers. Conversely, when the smurf was facing away from the screen, he or she could not see any changes and therefore held a false belief. In this case, the correct answer was the number of flowers the smurf thought to have received the last time he or she held a true belief. Participants were told (explicit version) or not told (implicit version) that there was a standard sequence built in these true‐false beliefs which was repeated over the course of the experiment (without revealing the sequence itself).

Inconsistency Manipulation

In this SRT task, learning was demonstrated by faster responses after many repetitions of the same standard sequence (training phase), but slower responses when the standard sequences were interspersed by unexpected sequences in which the total sequence or only the true‐false belief sequence was randomized (test phase).

Task 4: Trait‐Implying Action Sequencing

In this task developed by Pu et al. (2020), participants were requested to observe a given sequence of actions performed by a human protagonist described in short sentences all implying the same trait (e.g., giving a compliment, buying a present, and picking up a book, and so forth, imply the trait: kindness; Figure 2, Task 4). In the sequencing condition, they additionally had to memorize and recognize the correct sequences of these actions, while in the non‐sequencing condition they had not. In the nonsocial control condition, objects were described that underwent mechanical movements or changes, and that implied the same characteristic (e.g., moving at high speed, arriving in record time, etc. imply the characteristic: fast).

Inconsistency Manipulation

In some studies, one or two actions in the second half of the sentences implied a trait that was inconsistent with the trait implied in the first half of the sentences (e.g., giving a slap, gossiping about a colleague, and laughing at a speech, etc. imply malice).

Task 5: Social Action Prediction

In this social prediction task developed by Haihambo et al. (2022), the logical order of the previous task was reversed. In particular, participants were first given the trait, intention or preference of a protagonist (e.g., Fumak is dishonest), and then they had to select four out of six possible actions that could be predicted as very likely to occur on the basis of that advanced information. In the sequencing condition, they additionally had to put these sentences in the correct chronological order, while in the non‐sequencing condition they did not. In the nonsocial control condition, characteristics of objects were described first (e.g., fast) and then participants had to select mechanical movements or changes that implied the same characteristic.

Activation likelihood Estimation Meta‐Analyses

We used the activation likelihood estimation (ALE) procedure to analyze our data, as implemented by GingerALE 3.0.2 (Eickhoff et al. 2009; Laird et al. 2005). The ALE procedure attempts to reveal clusters of consistent activation across studies. In particular, based on the collection of peak coordinates from each contrast in the selected studies, ALE estimates the probability that at least one of the peaks lies within a voxel. This computation is repeated for each voxel in the brain and results in an ALE map. A statistical threshold for the ALE map identifies real activation if the null hypothesis that the activation foci/peaks are spread uniformly throughout the brain (i.e., determined by random clustering through determined by 1000 simulations) is rejected.

Selection of Coordinates

For the present analyses of activation and connectivity, we identified significant areas in the cerebellum, the basal ganglia, and the cerebrum. Since our main interest was on these specific areas, we used coordinates from ROI analyses, which was supplemented by whole brain coordinates. This was also often done in the original studies, because ROIs are typically required for the identification and analysis of smaller subcortical areas, while this is evidently less needed for the larger cerebral neocortex. Moreover, ROIs are typically used for connectivity analyses, such as for instance dynamic causal modeling (Haihambo and Van Overwalle 2023; Li, Pu, Baetens, et al. 2024; Ma et al. 2023; Pu et al. 2023; Van Overwalle, Van de Steen, and Mariën 2019; Van Overwalle, Van de Steen, van Dun, et al. 2020). ROI analyses focused on the predicted location of these smaller structures with a correction for a smaller ROI volume, which is more tolerant than a whole‐brain correction. An analogous ROI analysis was performed on key mentalizing areas of the cerebral neocortex to ensure that they would also be identified, however, with somewhat larger ROI volumes (i.e., radius) given the larger surface of cerebral areas.

Contrasts and Thresholds

We conducted ALE analyses for each of the four contrasts. Given that we combined whole‐brain and ROIs in our meta‐analysis, an analysis of peak coordinates, as in ALE, rather than t‐maps, is an optimal option. We conducted ALE analyses with standard cluster‐formation threshold of p < 0.001, uncorrected, and cluster‐inference threshold of p < 0.05, family‐wise error (FWE) corrected. However, note that most of the peaks in the database came from ROIs which implicate a more tolerant correction of multiple comparisons (i.e., small volume instead of whole brain). To compensate for this, we also calculated a more stringent thresholds for cluster‐formation of p < 0.0001 and cluster‐inference of p < 0.01 (FWE corrected). This resulted in very similar outcomes (see Tables 3 and 4), so that we focus the report here on the standard p < 0.001 (uncorrected) cluster‐formation with p < 0.05 (FWE corrected) cluster‐inference threshold.

Meta‐Analytic Connectivity Modeling

In addition to a meta‐analysis of activation in the cerebellum and basal ganglia, we also conducted a MACM analysis for each of the four contrasts mentioned earlier. This analysis is based on coactivation between two brain structures. This is done by selecting significant activation in a single subcortical structure (e.g., cerebellum or basal ganglia) and observing activation in the other (sub)cortical structures. This is also known as the selected subcortical structure being a “seed” for activation in the other structures. This procedure prunes away coordinates that are not “seeded,” and hence leads to reduced strength and volume of the clusters from the ALE analysis. Note that this coactivation analysis does not imply any direction of connectivity. It consisted of the following steps: For cerebellar connectivity, for each contrast that identified activation in Crus 1 or 2 or lobule IX in the database, we selected the coordinates in the other (basal ganglia and cerebral) areas, and computed an ALE analyses on this subset. This can be considered a coactivation analysis with the (mentalizing area of the) cerebellum as a seed area. Results

In this study, we investigated social, sequencing, and inconsistency specificity for the posterior cerebellum and basal ganglia. That is, we tested the activation in the social domain of contrasts reflecting: (1) social specificity: social sequencing > nonsocial sequencing, (2) sequencing specificity: social sequencing > social non‐sequencing, and (3) social inconsistency > social consistency contrasts. Additionally, we compared this with activation in the nonsocial domain for the (4) nonsocial sequencing > nonsocial non‐sequencing contrast. For the cerebellum, we hypothesized strong social, sequencing, and inconsistency specificity for the social domain (contrasts 1–3) in the mentalizing areas Crus 1, 2, and/or lobule IX, and minimal activation in the nonsocial domain (contrast 4). For the basal ganglia, we also hypothesized a strong contrast pointing to sequential specificity (contrast 2), and had no hypotheses for the other contrasts.

ALE Analyses

To investigate the activation of the cerebellum and basal ganglia in more detail, we conducted ALE analyses for each of the four contrasts (Table 1). In the ALE analysis, we mainly relied on ROIs, because this leads to relatively higher significance (due to the smaller volume to control for the number of comparisons), and thus higher representation of the areas that our meta‐analysis focuses on. Whole brain coordinates were also included to potentially identify other areas that would show convergence of activation across studies outside the mentalizing areas that we specified and hypothesized. As we will see, however, no areas were significant outside those of the ROIs.

Results are evaluated with respect to activation strength (z‐value, which is independent from cluster threshold or ROI size) and activation extent (cluster volume).

Activation in the Cerebellum

Consistent with our hypotheses, the mentalizing ROIs of the posterior cerebellum showed large clusters of activation for each of the three social contrasts (Figure 3 and Table 4). Social specificity generated the largest extent of activation (contrast 1—blue; z = 8.17; volume = 11,656 mm3), and was also the only contrast that activated lobule IX. The next largest cluster was observed for inconsistency specificity (contrast 3—green; z = 5.48; volume = 5336 mm3) followed by sequencing specificity (contrast 2—red; z = 6.09; volume = 2424 mm3). Sequencing in the nonsocial domain activated much smaller cluster (contrast 4—gray: z = 5.65; volume = 872 mm3). Figure 3 shows that all contrasts activated the mentalizing network in Crus 1 and 2 as specified in the atlas by Buckner et al. (2011) (the far right in Figure 3).

Activation in the Basal Ganglia

The basal ganglia revealed activation mainly in the anterior part of the striatum (i.e., caudate and putamen), which was more limited. In particular, there was no significant social specificity (contrast 1) and no significant nonsocial specificity either (contrast 4). However, there was sizeable activation for sequencing (contrast 2—red: z = 6.82; volume = 5696 mm3) and for social inconsistency (3—green: z = 6.55; volume = 6184 mm3; Figure 3). This implies that although the basal ganglia do not show any general preference for social material, they do show specificity for some social aspects such as sequencing (contrast 2) and social inconsistency (contrast 3), while they do not show preference for nonsocial sequencing (contrast 4). Figure 3 shows that the contrasts mainly activated the anterior striatum. The activation is concentrated in the mentalizing and executive control networks as specified by Choi, Thomas Yeo, and Buckner (2012) (on the far right in Figure 3); but note that this functional designation should be taken with some caution, as the mentalizing network covers the same area almost entirely at somewhat inferior locations of the Choi atlas (e.g., around z = 5 to −2 in Choi, Thomas Yeo, and Buckner 2012).

Activation in the Cerebral Neocortex

Although not our main interest, the results confirm a major role of mentalizing in the neocortical mentalizing ROIs for all four contrasts (top of Figure 3). Of interest, there is little differentiation in specificity, except for social inconsistency (Table 4). All neocortical areas revealed the largest activation for social selectivity (contrast 1—blue; z = 8.50; volume = 22,184 mm3), followed by social sequencing (contrast 2—red; z = 7.30; volume = 13,528 mm3), and the TPJ and PCun also showed some activation for nonsocial sequencing (contrast 4—gray: z = 5.60; volume = 5360 mm3). In contrast, social inconsistency activated mainly the mPFC (3—green: z = 5.02; volume = 1880 mm3).

Discussion

Research on social sequencing and the role of the posterior cerebellum has gained momentum in the last decade, but the role of the basal ganglia has been hereto largely neglected. To take stock of the findings so far, the present meta‐analysis evaluated, for the first time, the relative contribution of these two subcortical structures in concert with the cerebral neocortex during mentalizing based on social action sequences, and contrasted this evidence against nonsocial sequence learning. This analysis was extended to include a meta‐analytic connectivity analysis, which aimed to explore the patterns of coactivation between all three structures. In this analysis, we included a variety of social sequencing tasks (Table S1) which are of great interest because they involved (a) classic mentalizing inferences ranging from low‐level goals to high‐level beliefs and traits while participants (b) learned, memorized, generated or predicted the temporal sequence of the underlying social actions. In particular, to evaluate the functionality of the cerebellum and basal ganglia in these tasks, we tested the preference for (1) social input, (2) sequencing in the social domain, (3) social inconsistencies and, as a way of global baseline, for (4) nonsocial sequencing.

Overall, the probability (z‐values) of the ALE‐scores and total volume of ALE clusters converged on a number of clear‐cut meta‐analytic results. As hypothesized, for the posterior cerebellum, we found that the mentalizing areas in Crus 1 and 2, and in lobule IX, were preferentially recruited for social sequencing, including a preference for both social and sequencing information, as well as for social inconsistency information. For the basal ganglia, we observed in its anterior part a preference for sequencing and inconsistency information (in the social domain), but no preference at all for social information or nonsocial sequencing in general as these contrasts did not survive conventional significance levels. The mentalizing areas in the cerebrum showed the same pattern of preferences for social (including sequencing and inconsistency) information as the cerebellum, except that the least preference was revealed for social inconsistencies.

We also found clear evidence for distinct MACM coactivation between the subcortical and cortical structures of our study, and also between the subcortical cerebellum and basal ganglia. Overall, this connectivity suggests active neural information exchange during social sequence processing as well as during social inconsistencies. However, this pattern was generally reduced when looking at connectivity with the basal ganglia, resulting in some connections that did not reach a conventional significance level.

As might be expected, the activity in the cerebellar Crus 1/2 and lobule IX was located in the mentalizing network specified by Buckner et al. (2011) (Figure 3) as well as the activity in the basal ganglia specified by Choi, Thomas Yeo, and Buckner (2012) (at around z = 5 to −2; Figure 3) but it is possible that the basal ganglia might also recruit some part of the executive network. Activity in the cerebral neocortex was entirely located in the mentalizing network, and specifically in the default mode network B (see Braga et al. 2019; figure 13 bottom). The present results suggest that the default mode network B might extend to the present sites of activity in the posterior cerebellar Crus and inferior Lobule X, and the anterior basal ganglia.

Although we found right lateralization of activity in the cerebellum and reversed left lateralization in the basal ganglia, in particular for social sequencing specificity, it seems premature to draw firm conclusions from it. To illustrate, in past research, it was believed that belief understanding recruited the right TPJ (Saxe and Wexler 2005), but later meta‐analyses (e.g., Van Overwalle 2009; Schurz et al. 2014) found no clear evidence for this, and reported the recruitment of both the left and right TPJ. More research is needed to confirm the present hemispheric preference.

The Posterior Cerebellum Supports Social Action Sequences and Inconsistencies

Based on recent studies on the role of the social cerebellum, we hypothesized that the main function of the mentalizing network in the posterior cerebellum (Crus 1 and 2, lobule IX) is to identify dynamic social mentalizing based on temporal action sequences that helps to predict, prepare, and plan adequate social responses. The results of the meta‐analyses showed evidence for a social preference of the cerebellar mentalizing network, suggesting that a main function of the posterior cerebellum is to identify social actions. This was followed by somewhat weaker (and right lateralized) preference for sequencing, which seems to suggest that the pure sequencing function of the cerebellum may be less outspoken than its social function, at least in the studies that were included in the present meta‐analysis. In comparison, the nonsocial sequencing function in the cerebellar mentalizing network was very limited. This is in line with other studies reporting that, for instance, animations of nonhuman shapes moving in a biological manner (e.g., one shape “chasing” another) typically recruit mentalizing activity, also in the cerebellum (Van Overwalle et al. 2014). Note that the role of the inferior posterior cerebellum lobule IX was mainly confined to social (sequencing) detection during prediction (i.e., prediction tasks; see Table S1) and did not play a significant role in identifying sequences of past behaviors. Perhaps its position in the inferior cerebellum close to the anterior cerebellum, responsible for engaging in motor behavior, explains this preferential function, as prediction might be considered as simulating future actions in one's mind.

It is important to note that the somewhat weaker preference for sequencing in the social domain in the cerebellum in comparison with its social preference may be due to a methodological limitation in the selected studies rather than reflecting a genuine effect. The reason is that the non‐sequencing conditions in many tasks often included some indications of timing and order when observing actions to enforce strict parallels between conditions. For example, in all conditions of the Navigation and Trait‐implying tasks, the same sequential trajectories/trait‐implying actions were observed, thus exposing participants to the same order of events; the only difference being that these trajectories/actions had to be memorized (sequencing) or not (non‐sequencing). In other tasks, order in the non‐sequencing conditions was included to ensure that there was only one correct answer for generating a correct sequence (e.g., in the scenarios in Belief Sequencing tasks) or to make correct predictions (e.g., in Prediction tasks). Although order was difficult to avoid in the stimulus material, this implies that the presence of sequencing may have invited some minimal cerebellar activation, even in the so‐called “non‐sequencing” control conditions, leading to a weaker contrast with the main sequencing condition. In sum, the weaker evidence for sequencing specificity as opposed to social specificity, might be a methodological artifact of the experimental manipulations. If this reasoning is correct, this implies that the sequencing function of the basal ganglia might also be stronger than it appears from the sequencing contrast analysis.

More generally, the unintentional or unavoidable presence of sequencing information or cues in social material in other neuroimaging studies on social mentalizing may perhaps also explain why these studies sometimes reveal cerebellar activation without an explicit manipulation of sequences. Many social behaviors often inherently involve dynamic sequences of actions that the cerebellum might pick up. Earlier meta‐analyses (Van Overwalle et al. 2014; Van Overwalle, Van de Steen, et al. 2020) indeed found substantial activation of the posterior cerebellum for a large variety of social tasks without explicit sequencing manipulation (about 30% in Van Overwalle et al. 2014). However, activation rose to almost 100% when thinking more explicitly about the past or future, which might more strongly invite sequencing. The internal narrative structure of to the past or future may at minimum lead us to think of these events in their inherent chronological order in relation to current events.

The ALE analysis revealed that the inconsistency contrast was very strong. This indicates that the higher‐order function of signaling social errors, which may require novel predictions for adaptive social action, is processed in the mentalizing network of the posterior cerebellum, without the systematic contribution of another (e.g., executive control) network.

One might argue that the present results withstanding, they do not present strong evidence for a role of the posterior cerebellum in dynamic social mentalizing. First, it could be that the social stimuli in the meta‐analysis encompass various parallel sequences such as facial expressions, actions, sounds, and so on, which naturally elicit stronger activation in the posterior cerebellum. Although action observation (or “mirroring”) might also activate the posterior cerebellar Crus area (Sokolov et al. 2012, 2018), this is not very likely a major source for the present findings given the impoverished, schematic and verbal nature of all tasks in this meta‐analysis, with the explicit goal to avoid as much as possible any visual or auditive perception of biological movement. Second, one might argue that participants could infer or learn sequences based on nonsocial information related to actions (e.g., the chocolate box in Task 2, Figure 2). This explanation is not very likely either, as the results showed no systematic activation outside the mentalizing network of the cerebellum. Taken together, it is difficult to provide explanations for the present results other than social mentalizing based on action sequences.

The Basal Ganglia Support Sequences Without General Social Preference

Based on prior research, we hypothesized that the mentalizing network in the anterior basal ganglia would reveal a strong sequencing preference, although we had no clear idea of its social functionality. In general, our hypotheses were largely confirmed by our meta‐analyses. We found no evidence for a general preference for social or nonsocial material. Instead, we found strong sequencing specificity for social material in the basal ganglia, and perhaps surprisingly, also for inconsistencies in following social norms and regularities. In contrast, sequencing specificity was not found for nonsocial material. Peak activations were predominantly found in the caudate, and to a lesser extent in the putamen of the anterior striatum. Thus, while the basal ganglia do not show a general preference for social or nonsocial content, this meta‐analysis demonstrates that this structure does show a preference for specific social input, such as social sequencing and social inconsistency. This pattern of results would have made much more sense if there was also a preference for nonsocial sequencing besides social sequencing, but it did not. Perhaps this might be due to a lack of power, although we have no convincing arguments to support this claim. For example, the lack of nonsocial sequencing specificity is not due to the number of contrasts (8 and 7 for the sequencing > non‐sequencing contrast in the social and nonsocial domain respectively, see contrasts 2 and 4 in Table S1). We have not been able to resolve this puzzle.

Perhaps, the most important conclusion from the present analysis is the limited preference in general for social material by the anterior basal ganglia (besides a preference for social sequencing and inconsistency), which is the main difference with the posterior cerebellum. Taken from one perspective, it appears that the cerebellum is very responsive to social actions, especially when the underlying sequence is explicit and obvious, but also when implicit or implied, as noted earlier (Van Overwalle et al. 2014; Van Overwalle, Ma, and Heleven 2020). In contrast, the anterior striatum only requires sequencing information to engage it.

One way to understand the divergent specificity of the cerebellum and basal ganglia is that both subcortical structures developed somewhat separately during evolution, and specialized their functional preference. Perhaps the dopamine‐related system of the basal ganglia encourages the reinforcement of increasingly stronger and complex chains of actions (Caligiore et al. 2013, 2019), predominantly promoting its sequencing function. For instance, we are excited and motivated when buying a gift long before an anniversary (i.e., revealing a long chain of extended gratification), and not only when observing the happiness on the beneficiary's face (i.e., short chain of immediate gratification). Reinforcement learning models (Sutton and Barto 1998) provide a theoretical learning account of how reward may determine behavior over increasingly longer time intervals. In contrast, the function of the cerebellum might be more closely related to finding or predicting an optimal series of actions immediately preceding a goal within a plethora of possibilities encoded in its internal models, promoting its specificity within a given domain—here the social domain (Caligiore et al. 2019).

The Mentalizing Neocortex Supports Social Action Sequences

Although not the main focus of this meta‐analysis, we found that the pattern of specificity in the mentalizing network of the cerebrum ran in close parallel that observed in the social cerebellum. However, there was also a noteworthy difference. Contrary to the cerebellum, the mentalizing network in the cerebral neocortex did not show a strong preference for social inconsistencies, which recruited only the dorsal mPFC. No other networks related to executive control or conflict monitoring (Yeung, Botvinick, and Cohen 2004) were involved. This is surprising. Although violations of social expectations might require a re‐evaluation of many earlier social judgments, the activation of only the dorsal mPFC suggests that only the person is reevaluated (Heleven and Van Overwalle 2015), and no other temporary aspect (i.e., goals, beliefs, preferences). It makes sense that reevaluating high‐level stable characteristics of a person is most relevant, especially in light of future interactions with an inconsistent and hence untrustworthy person, as opposed to temporary lower‐level mental thoughts which are less reliable to predict future interactions.

Functional Connectivity Between the Cerebellum, Basal Ganglia, and Cerebrum

We found evidence for robust coactivation between mentalizing areas in the posterior cerebellum, basal ganglia and the cerebral neocortex. Overall, the hierarchical order in terms of peak strength and volume of connectivity most often showed close parallels with the ALE activation results. This is to be expected given that our connectivity analysis is based on coactivation of brain structures (i.e., activation is a minimal condition to show functional connectivity).

In particular, the connectivity between the cerebellum and basal ganglia showed an asymmetric pattern. With the cerebellum as seed, there was only co‐activation with social sequencing and social inconsistencies in the basal ganglia (in line with the basal ganglia ALE results); with the basal ganglia as seed, there was coactivation in the cerebellum under all contrasts. With respect to the connectivity with the cerebrum, both the cerebellum and the basal ganglia as seed revealed co‐activation in cerebrum for all contrasts.

The present cerebello‐cerebral connectivity findings are schematically illustrated in Figure 5. Since coactivation is without directionality, we took as leading principle for Figure 5 that activation in an area is a minimal condition to exchange neural signals to another area via reciprocal connections. Consequently, only reciprocal connectivity is shown when both areas are significantly activated for a given type of information. This limits the basal ganglia in exchanging signals to other areas only on social sequencing and social inconsistency. Overall, Figure 5 suggests robust, although sometimes asymmetric functional connectivity between the basal ganglia, the cerebellum and the cerebrum, which was not explored earlier for social mentalizing. The present analysis might provide some impetus and hypotheses for future in‐depth dynamic causal modeling studies investigating the detailed interaction between the nuclei of the basal ganglia, cerebellum and cerebrum to evaluate whether this circuit shows reciprocal connectivity for all types of information, or a distinct pattern or hierarchy.

The present coactivation results broadly align with evidence from earlier dynamic causal modeling studies which suggested strong reciprocal functional connectivity between cerebral and cerebellar mentalizing areas for processing social sequencing information and managing social inconsistency (Haihambo and Van Overwalle 2023; Li, Pu, Baetens, et al. 2024; Ma et al. 2023; Pu et al. 2023; Van Overwalle, Van de Steen, and Mariën 2019; Van Overwalle, Van de Steen, van Dun, et al. 2020). Using a dynamic causal modeling approach, these studies revealed the facilitatory or inhibitory polarity of connectivity as well as its directionality from and to each brain structure. A coactivation analysis cannot provide this evidence. However, a limitation of these earlier dynamic causal modeling studies is that they were limited to an analysis of (a) connectivity between the cerebellum and cerebrum, (b) of one social sequencing task type, and (c) for one contrast instead of evaluating, for example, the differential impact of social and sequencing specificity (contrast 1 & 2) or of the nonsocial domain (contrast 4). These limitations were overcome in the present study, which thus allowed for the first time to demonstrate functional coactivation under very specific preferences across distinct task manipulations.

Social Behavior Is Dynamic

An important implication of the present analysis is that it points to the important role of the cerebellum and basal ganglia, two very large subcortical structures, in social cognition, especially in the analysis of dynamic social mentalizing. For too long, social neuroscience was focused on relative static social behavior, with little regard for the rich dynamic nature of social action and mentalizing. As noted earlier, a dynamic analysis is important, because the brain is constantly trying to predict the next stage of the social environment, in order to engage fluently in social behavior. Dynamic analysis is also important because social behavior often involves social interaction, with give and take from both parties, which is impossible without a good sense of mentalizing on the when of actions, anticipation about what is coming next and a quick corrective response if expectations are not met. Without the subcortical cerebellum, social behavior would be less coordinated, with less awareness of what comes next, and hence replete with many social errors.

An example of such behavior can perhaps be best observed from people with autism, because there is converging evidence that a sizeable part of this pathology originates from deficiencies in the cerebellum (Hadaya et al. 2022; Limperopoulos et al. 2007; Wang, Kloth, and Badura 2014). This population requires more time to analyze social beliefs in a temporal context than neurotypical controls (Heleven et al. 2022). Likewise, individuals with cerebellar disorders show clear evidence of disrupted social understanding of biological action (Cattaneo et al. 2011) and understanding of social beliefs (Van Overwalle, Coninck, et al. 2019). Perhaps many other social difficulties (e.g., schizophrenia, Moberget and Ivry 2019) might profit from further scrutiny of the functionality of the social cerebellum and basal ganglia, to get a better understanding of their underlying pathology. Moreover, noninvasive cerebellar stimulation might potentially aid in alleviating some of their difficulties (Ciricugno et al. 2024; Van Overwalle et al. 2024).

Limitations

We are aware of several limitations of our work. First, we did not include work from other labs, although we searched for it. The main reason is that as far as we know, no other researchers have embarked on an investigation of the cerebellum involving temporal sequences during social mentalizing and with at least one adequate non‐sequence control condition. Observation of human biological movement was eliminated in all studies included, to avoid confounds with action observation (or mirror) processes (Molenberghs, Cunnington, and Mattingley 2012; Van Overwalle and Baetens 2009). Nonetheless, other researchers could have come up with more creative ideas to avoid some of the pitfalls we noted above, such as the fact that it is difficult to avoid implicit sequencing processes when observing dynamic social actions. However, an advantage of including the studies of a single lab is that other, mainly methodological, factors have been held constant (scanner and imaging parameters and other analytic choices such as spatial normalization, specifying regions of interest, and so on), and hence reduce potential sources of uninteresting variation in the results. Moreover, the cerebellum is often incompletely covered in many studies, leaving out the inferior cerebellum or having it preprocessed and analyzed inappropriately (Baetens et al. 2020). This problem was avoided in the studies included in the analysis by scanning the cerebellum in its entirety.

Second, although our analysis applied the same rigorous methodology, the coordinates that were entered in the analysis came from a combination of ROIs and whole brain analyses. The use of hypothesis‐driven ROIs may have favored the identification of social mentalizing areas at the expense of other areas that are yet unknown to participate in the process. Note, however, that the use of ROIs is typical for analysis and identification of smaller subcortical areas and also for other connectivity analyses such as dynamic causal modeling (Haihambo and Van Overwalle 2023; Li, Pu, Baetens, et al. 2024; Ma et al. 2023; Pu et al. 2023; Van Overwalle, Van de Steen, and Mariën 2019; Van Overwalle, Van de Steen, van Dun, et al. 2020). As noted earlier, we compensated for this by running our analysis with tolerant and stringent thresholds, which confirmed the main results. Also note that variability in thresholding is a common problem in meta‐analyses, where the customary tactic is to accept any reasonable threshold from the original study. As we had access to the t‐maps of all studies, we also engaged in a comparative analysis of this richer dataset using the SDM software (Radua et al. 2012), but several technical issues prevented us from running this analysis.

Third, it is important to note that a coactivation analysis does not imply any direction of the functional connectivity. To identify directionality, more advanced techniques, such as dynamic causal modeling, are needed based on the original fMRI timeseries data. This was done in past research for most of the original studies reported here (Haihambo and Van Overwalle 2023; Li, Pu, Baetens, et al. 2024; Ma et al. 2023; Pu et al. 2023), and showed strong evidence for reciprocal connectivity in both directions.

Conclusion

The posterior cerebellum and anterior basal ganglia are critical structures for social mentalizing based on the identification of dynamic action patterns. Although the role of the cerebellum was extensively investigated in the social domain during the last decade, the role of the basal ganglia was long ignored. To reveal the differential contribution of these two subcortical structures, we conducted a meta‐analysis of recently published social sequencing tasks which encompassed a variety of social mentalizing inferences ranging from low‐level goals to high‐level beliefs and traits, while participants learned, memorized, generated, or predicted the temporal sequence of the social actions involved. The results showed that the mentalizing network of the posterior cerebellum was preferentially recruited for social input, and especially for social sequencing, while the anterior basal ganglia were preferentially recruited for social sequencing, without a general preference for social input. Moreover, connectivity analyses showed that the three (subcortical and neocortical) structures were functionally connected (see Figure 5). The present work underscores the role of subcortical structures in social cognition of dynamic actions.
