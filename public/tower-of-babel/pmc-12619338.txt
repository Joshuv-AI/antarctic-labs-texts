Association between 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk score and incident cardiovascular events across stages 0–3 of cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic syndrome: evidence from the China health and retirement longitudinal study (CHARLS)
Authors: Zhitong Li, Wenxia Ren, Xuhui Li, Ruixue Duan, Linlin Gao, Jiaojiao Li, Yajing Bai, Yuanyuan Yan, Angxian Lü, Anqi Zhao, Shiwei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome framework integrates cardiometabolic risks. However, longitudinal evidence on the association between atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease 10-year risk (ASCVD10 year) scores and cardiovascular events across CKM syndrome stages remains limited in large aging populations.MethodsWe analyzed data from 4,565 adults aged ≥ 45 years in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). ASCVD10 year scores were calculated using the China-PAR model at baseline (2012) and follow-up (2015), with cumulative risk scores derived accordingly. Participants were stratified into four trajectory clusters via k-means clustering based on ASCVD score dynamics. Logistic regression, restricted cubic spline models, subgroup analysis, and mediation analyses were conducted to assess associations and underlying pathways.ResultsHigher baseline and cumulative ASCVD10 year scores were significantly associated with increased risks of cardiovascular disease (CVD), heart disease, and stroke across CKM stages 0–3 (all P < 0.0001). Compared to the low-risk cluster, the highest-risk cluster exhibited a > 4-fold increased CVD risk (OR = 4.12, 95% CI: 2.81–6.02). Nonlinear associations were observed for CVD and heart disease, with inflection points indicating stronger effects at lower ASCVD ranges. Subgroup analyses revealed stronger associations in individuals ≤ 65 years, females, and those with hypertension or diabetes. Mediation analyses identified HbA1c, fasting glucose, and the TyG index as partial mediators, accounting for 3–6% of the total effect.ConclusionsASCVD10 year scores, particularly cumulative exposure, are robust predictors of cardiovascular events across early CKM stages in middle-aged and older adults. These findings support the utility of longitudinal ASCVD10 year scores in preventive cardiometabolic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05295-2.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with a particularly pronounced burden in low- and middle-income countries such as China, where rapid urbanization and lifestyle changes have driven an increase in cardiometabolic risk factors [1, 2]. The interplay between cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic (CKM) systems has garnered increasing attention as a critical framework for understanding the progression of cardiometabolic diseases [3]. The American Heart Association’s (AHA) Presidential Advisory Statement introduced the concept of CKM syndrome, which integrates cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic dysfunction into a spectrum of stages (0–4) to better characterize disease risk and progression [4]. This framework highlights the need for early risk assessment and intervention to mitigate the escalating burden of CVD, particularly in populations with a high prevalence of metabolic disorders such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia.

Accurate risk stratification is essential for identifying individuals at high risk of CVD and implementing timely preventive measures [5]. The 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk (ASCVD10 year) score, particularly the China-PAR model tailored for the Chinese population, has emerged as a robust tool for predicting CVD risk by incorporating demographic, clinical, and lifestyle factors such as age, gender, blood pressure, waist circumference, cholesterol levels, smoking status, diabetes, and family history of CVD [6]. While the ASCVD risk score provides a snapshot of risk at a single time point, its dynamic changes over time and its association with incident cardiovascular events across varying stages of CKM syndrome remain underexplored. Longitudinal studies tracking these changes are critical for understanding how risk evolves and how it correlates with clinical outcomes in diverse populations [7].

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative cohort of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older, offers a unique opportunity to investigate the association between ASCVD risk scores and cardiovascular outcomes in the context of CKM syndrome [8]. Previous studies using CHARLS data have highlighted the increasing prevalence of cardiometabolic risk factors in China, including obesity, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus, which are closely linked to chronic kidney disease (CKD) and CVD [9]. However, few studies have systematically examined how baseline and cumulative ASCVD risk scores relate to incident cardiovascular events across the spectrum of CKM syndrome stages, particularly in a Chinese population. This gap is significant given the distinct demographic, genetic, and lifestyle factors that influence cardiometabolic health in China compared to Western populations [10].

The present study aims to address this gap by leveraging CHARLS data to evaluate the association between baseline and cumulative ASCVD10 year scores and incident cardiovascular events (CVD, heart disease, and stroke) across CKM syndrome stages 0–3. By employing advanced statistical techniques, including k-means clustering, machine learning, restricted cubic spline regression, and mediation analyses, we seek to elucidate the trajectories of ASCVD risk, identify nonlinear associations, and explore mediating pathways involving metabolic markers such as HbA1c and the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index. Our findings are expected to provide novel insights into the progression of cardiometabolic risk and inform targeted interventions for reducing CVD burden in China and similar populations globally.

Materials and methods

Study population and exclusion criteria

We conducted a nationwide cohort study using data from CHARLS, which includes Chinese adults aged 45 years and older. CHARLS is a comprehensive survey designed to gather a broad range of socio-demographic, health-related, and lifestyle information through structured interviews and clinical assessments. Detailed information on the design and methodology of CHARLS has been published in prior studies [8]. The study adhered to ethical guidelines as outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Board of Peking University (IRB 00001052–11015). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

The baseline survey for CHARLS was conducted between June 2011 and March 2012, with follow-up data collected every two years thereafter. The data collection involved face-to-face interviews conducted by trained personnel using computer-assisted personal interview techniques to ensure consistency and reliability [11]. These interviews captured socio-demographic characteristics, medical history, lifestyle factors, and included clinical assessments such as blood pressure measurements, blood sampling, and anthropometric evaluations. This rigorous approach allowed for the comprehensive collection of various health indicators.

For the present study, we used data from the baseline cohort collected in 2011–2012 (Wave 1) and subsequent follow-up data from 2013 (Wave 2), 2015 (Wave 3), 2018 (Wave 4), and 2020 (Wave 5). To ensure the validity and relevance of our analysis, we applied strict inclusion and exclusion criteria. From the initial cohort of 17,705 participants in the CHARLS baseline survey, we excluded 13,140 individuals based on the following criteria: [1] age under 45 years at baseline; [2] missing key variables required to calculate the ASCVD10 year scores for 2012 or 2015, including gender, age, blood pressure, waist circumference (WC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), total cholesterol (TC), smoking status, diabetes mellitus, family history of CVD, and residential area; [3] pre-existing CVD, stroke, or heart disease at baseline; [4] absence of stages 0–3 of CKM syndrome at baseline; [5] lack of follow-up data on cardiovascular outcomes; and [6] incomplete anthropometric, health-related, or socio-demographic data. After applying these exclusions, 4,565 participants remained in the final cohort for analysis. A detailed overview of the inclusion and exclusion criteria is presented in Fig. 1.

Assessment of ASCVD10 year scores and cumulative ASCVD10 year scores

The ASCVD10 year scores were assessed using the China-PAR risk model (https://www.cvdrisk.com.cn/ASCVD/Eval), which has been established as the most suitable model for the Chinese population [7]. This model incorporates several key factors, including gender, age, blood pressure, WC, HDL-c, TC, smoking status, diabetes, family history of CVD, and residential area. Based on the China-PAR risk score, participants were classified into three categories: low risk (< 5%), intermediate risk (5%–9.9%), and high risk (≥ 10%). Cumulative ASCVD10 year scores were calculated using hematological data from Waves 1 and 3. The formula used for calculation was as follows: (ASCVD10 year 2012 + ASCVD10 year 2015)/2 × time interval (2015 − 2012). Detailed definitions and further information are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Definition of CKM syndrome stages 0 to 4

The stages of CKM syndrome were defined using a comprehensive approach that integrates established clinical risk factors with biomarkers reflecting the interrelated pathophysiological processes underlying the syndrome, as adapted from AHA Presidential Advisory Statement [4]. The classification consists of five stages, ranging from Stage 0 to Stage 4. Detailed operational definitions and diagnostic thresholds for each stage are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Stage 0 is characterized by the absence of clinically significant CKM risk factors in otherwise healthy individuals. Stage 1 is defined by the presence of excess body fat or dysfunctional adiposity, which includes general obesity, abdominal (central) obesity, or impaired glucose regulation (prediabetes). Stage 2 includes individuals with diagnosed metabolic disorders, such as diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, metabolic syndrome, or moderate-to-high-risk CKD, as defined by the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) classification. Renal function was assessed using the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), calculated via the Chinese Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (C-MDRD) equation, in accordance with KDIGO guidelines [12, 13]. Stage 3 is characterized by subclinical CVD, as indicated by an elevated predicted 10-year CVD risk using the Framingham risk score, or the presence of very high-risk CKD (stage G4 or G5) according to KDIGO guidelines [14]. Stage 4 represents the most advanced stage of CKM syndrome, marked by clinically evident CVD, including coronary heart disease, heart failure, stroke, peripheral artery disease, and atrial fibrillation (AF).

Ascertainment of outcomes

CVD was designated as the primary outcome of interest in this study, while heart disease and stroke were considered secondary outcomes. The occurrence of heart disease was determined based on participants’ affirmative responses to the question: “Have you been diagnosed with a heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems by a doctor?” Similarly, stroke was identified through self-reported physician diagnosis in response to the question: “Have you been diagnosed with stroke by a doctor?” The diagnosis of CVD was classified based on participants’ self-reported medical history, which indicated either heart disease or stroke. This methodology aligns with established practices in previous epidemiological studies [15, 16]. Incident CVD events were defined as newly diagnosed cases that occurred during the study’s follow-up period, which spanned from the baseline data collection in 2011 to the most recent follow-up in 2020, or until participant dropout, whichever occurred first.

Data collection

Data for this study were obtained from a nationwide prospective cohort survey conducted using standardized protocols. The baseline survey collected a comprehensive range of variables, including demographic characteristics (age, sex, education level—college or above, middle school, primary school, or no formal education), marital status (married or other), and residential location (urban or rural). Lifestyle behaviors were also recorded, including smoking status (current, former, or never) and alcohol consumption (yes or no). Anthropometric measurements included body mass index (BMI), WC, and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR). Biochemical indicators were obtained from fasting blood samples and included white blood cell count (WBC), platelet count (PLT), C-reactive protein (CRP), fasting blood glucose (FBG), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), TC, triglycerides (TG), HDL-c, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine, uric acid, and eGFR [17]. The TyG index, a surrogate marker for insulin resistance, was calculated using the following formula: TyG index = ln [TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL)/2] [18]. Cardiometabolic conditions were defined based on either self-reported physician diagnoses or clinical criteria. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 80 mmHg [19]. Diabetes was diagnosed by self-report, or if FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or random plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L [20]. Dyslipidemia was identified if any of the following were present: TC ≥ 5.2 mmol/L, LDL-c ≥ 3.4 mmol/L, TG ≥ 1.7 mmol/L, HDL-c < 50 mg/dL for women or < 40 mg/dL for men, or a self-reported diagnosis of lipid disorder [21]. Medication use for hypertension, diabetes, or dyslipidemia was also documented, including antihypertensive, antidiabetic, and lipid-lowering agents.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed data, or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies with percentages. Group comparisons for categorical variables were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate, while continuous variables were compared using the independent t-test or one-way ANOVA for normally distributed data, and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data.

To categorize participants based on changes in ASCVD10 year scores from 2012 to 2015, we applied k-means clustering within a logistic regression framework. K-means, an unsupervised machine learning algorithm, partitions data by minimizing within-cluster variance to generate K distinct clusters [22]. The optimal number of clusters was determined by identifying the inflection point where the reduction in within-cluster variance plateaued, which occurred at K = 4 in our analysis (Supplementary Figure S1). Consequently, participants were categorized into four groups based on ASCVD10 year score trajectories: [1] Cluster 1 (sustained low risk, n = 2,199), with scores increasing from 2.32 ± 1.30 in 2012 to 2.99 ± 1.54 in 2015; [2] Cluster 2 (sustained moderate risk, n = 1,401), increasing from 6.72 ± 2.27 to 8.21 ± 2.24; [3] Cluster 3 (high and rapidly increasing risk, n = 731), rising from 12.21 ± 3.58 to 15.24 ± 3.74; and [4] Cluster 4 (consistently high, stable increasing risk, n = 234), increasing from 22.03 ± 6.33 to 24.33 ± 6.36 (Fig. 2).

Before analyzing the association between ASCVD 10 year scores and cardiovascular events, we employed machine learning algorithms for feature selection to enhance prognostic model precision. Specifically, we utilized the eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm combined with SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) values to identify and prioritize important predictors [23]. The Python package (version 3.10.4) was used for feature selection, and SHAP summary plots were generated to visualize the importance and contribution of each variable (Fig. 3). Missing data were handled using multiple imputation to maximize statistical power and minimize bias. Logistic regression models were subsequently used to examine the association between baseline and cumulative ASCVD 10 year scores and incident cardiovascular events, with results expressed as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Three models with incremental adjustments were developed: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age, gender, education, marital status, residence, smoking status, and drinking status), and Model 3 (additionally adjusted for CRP, BMI, WC, LDL-c, eGFR, BUN, HDL-c, TC, PLT, and uric acid). The selection of covariates was guided by clinical relevance and variable importance rankings from the XGBoost analysis. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), detailed in Supplementary Table S3.

To investigate potential nonlinear associations between ASCVD 10 year scores and incident cardiovascular events across CKM syndrome stages 0–3, we applied RCS regression models with four knots. Subgroup and interaction analyses were performed, stratified by age, gender, marital status, residence, smoking status, drinking status, CKM syndrome stage, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Additionally, mediation analyses were conducted using the “mediation” package to explore pathways through which ASCVD 10 year scores may influence cardiovascular event risk. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3), and a two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 4,565 participants were categorized into four groups based on changes in ASCVD10 year scores: sustained low-risk (Cluster 1, n = 2,199), sustained moderate-risk (Cluster 2, n = 1,401), high and rapidly increasing risk (Cluster 3, n = 731), and consistently high-risk (Cluster 4, n = 234). The mean age was 58.01 years, with Cluster 1 being the youngest (53.60 years) and Cluster 4 the oldest (68.60 years). Females made up 54.94% of the sample, with the highest proportion in Cluster 1 (76.13%). Education levels varied, and Cluster 4 had the highest proportion with no formal education (46.58%). Smoking and alcohol consumption increased with ASCVD risk, and clinical measures like BMI, WC, CRP, and glucose levels were highest in Cluster 4. The prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia also increased across clusters, with the highest rates in Cluster 4. Medication use for hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia was more common in higher-risk clusters. Specific results can be seen in Table 1, while Supplementary Table S3 presents a comparison of baseline characteristics between participants included in the analysis and those excluded.

Subgroup and interaction analysis

Subgroup and interaction analyses of baseline and cumulative ASCVD10 year scores on cardiovascular outcomes revealed significant differences in risk associations across various demographic and clinical factors (Supplementary Table S7 ~ Table S12). For both baseline and cumulative ASCVD10 year scores, age emerged as a key modifier, with individuals aged ≤ 65 years showing a stronger association with CVD compared to those aged > 65. Gender also influenced risk, with females demonstrating a higher association for both baseline (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.05–1.12) and cumulative (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.03–1.05) ASCVD scores. Other significant moderators included hypertension, where individuals with hypertension had a stronger association with CVD, and diabetes, which also interacted with both baseline and cumulative ASCVD scores to modify risk. Marital status, education level, and residence also showed interactions, particularly with cumulative ASCVD10 year scores, suggesting that individuals with lower educational attainment and those living in urban areas had a higher risk of cardiovascular events. Additionally, for stroke as an outcome, younger individuals, females, and urban residents exhibited stronger associations with ASCVD scores.

Mediation analysis

Mediation analysis (Fig. 5) revealed significant partial mediation of the associations between ASCVD risk scores and cardiovascular outcomes by metabolic markers. For the relationship between baseline ASCVD10 year score and CVD risk, a dual mediation effect was observed: HbA1c mediated 5.17% of the total effect and FBG mediated 6.46% of the total effect. Similarly, for baseline ASCVD10 year score and heart disease risk, HbA1c mediated 6.32% of the total effect. Regarding cumulative ASCVD10 year score, its association with composite CVD risk was partially mediated by HbA1c (3.12% proportion mediated), while its association with heart disease risk was also mediated by HbA1c (4.03% proportion mediated). Finally, the association between cumulative ASCVD10 year score and stroke risk was significantly mediated by the TyG index, accounting for 6.24% of the total effect. Crucially, direct effects of both baseline and cumulative ASCVD10 year scores on all cardiovascular outcomes remained highly significant (P < 0.001) after accounting for these mediators. To assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding in the mediation analysis, we performed a sensitivity analysis using bounding methods, such as E-values, to evaluate the robustness of our results (Table S13). The findings suggest that the observed indirect effects are unlikely to be substantially biased by unmeasured confounders.

Discussion

Comparison with existing research results

Our study adds to the robust body of evidence linking ASCVD risk scores to cardiovascular outcomes, while uniquely extending this understanding to the novel CKM syndrome framework. The China-PAR model, tailored to the Chinese population, has been validated in prior studies as an effective tool for predicting cardiovascular events, often outperforming global models like Framingham or SCORE in this demographic [24–26]. For instance, Guo et al. (2022) demonstrated its superior calibration and discrimination in Chinese cohorts [26], a finding corroborated by our results showing significant associations between baseline ASCVD10 year scores and CVD (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.04–1.07), heart disease (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.03–1.07), and stroke (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.05). These effect sizes align with studies in other populations using different risk scores, such as the Framingham Risk Score, which reported HRs ranging from 1.66 to 3.64 per unit increase for CVD events in Chinese hypertensive patients [27].

A distinctive contribution of our study is the use of cumulative ASCVD10 year scores, calculated as the average of 2012 and 2015 scores adjusted for time, to capture longitudinal risk trajectories. This approach revealed stronger associations with cardiovascular outcomes compared to baseline scores alone, a finding that echoes emerging evidence on the prognostic value of cumulative risk exposure [28, 29]. For example, studies in diabetic populations have shown that cumulative glycemic burden, measured via repeated HbA1c assessments, predicts CVD events more accurately than single-point measures [30]. Our tertile analysis further supports this, with risks escalating from Cluster 1 to Cluster 3, suggesting that sustained or increasing ASCVD risk over time amplifies cardiovascular hazard across CKM stages.

The observation of non-linear associations between cumulative ASCVD10 year scores and CVD/heart disease, identified through RCS models, is particularly noteworthy. While most prior studies assume linear relationships between risk scores and outcomes [31, 32], our threshold effect analyses revealed inflection points where risk accelerates more rapidly below certain levels. This non-linearity may reflect a saturation effect in later CKM stages or heightened vulnerability in earlier stages, a pattern less commonly reported in the literature [33]. However, similar J-shaped or U-shaped relationships have been observed in other contexts, such as blood pressure and CVD risk [34], suggesting that non-linear dynamics may be more prevalent than previously recognized. To our knowledge, this is the first study to document such patterns across CKM syndrome stages, highlighting a need for further exploration.

Subgroup analyses revealed that the association between ASCVD10 year scores and cardiovascular outcomes was modified by several factors, including age, gender, and the presence of hypertension or diabetes. Notably, younger individuals (≤ 65 years) and females exhibited stronger associations between ASCVD risk and CVD, which contrasts with some previous studies suggesting that risk prediction models may underestimate risk in these groups [35, 36]. For example, the Framingham Risk Score has been criticized for underpredicting risk in women and younger adults [37, 38]. Our findings, however, suggest that the China-PAR model may be more sensitive in these subgroups, possibly due to its incorporation of WC and other factors relevant to the Chinese population. Additionally, the stronger associations observed in individuals with hypertension and diabetes are consistent with the established synergistic effects of these conditions on cardiovascular risk [39].

Our mediation analysis, showing that HbA1c and the TyG index partially mediate the ASCVD-outcome relationship, builds on prior work linking metabolic dysregulation to cardiovascular risk [40]. The TyG index, a marker of insulin resistance, has been independently associated with CVD in multiple populations, with studies reporting hazard ratios of 1.1–1.5 for high versus low TyG levels [41]. However, the modest proportion mediated in our study (3–6%) suggests that other factors—such as inflammation or oxidative stress—play substantial roles, a hypothesis supported by research on multi-pathway mechanisms in metabolic syndrome [42]. Compared to studies in Western populations, our findings may reflect unique metabolic profiles in Chinese adults, warranting cross-population comparisons.

In addition to the main findings, it is important to consider the potential limitations of applying the ASCVD10 year risk score to patients with AF. AF may alter cardiovascular risk profiles due to its association with increased thromboembolic events and potential changes in heart function. This highlights the need for further research to validate the ASCVD10 year score specifically in this subgroup of patients to ensure its accuracy in predicting outcomes. Moreover, anticoagulant therapy, commonly prescribed to AF patients, may also influence cardiovascular outcomes, particularly stroke risk, in those with CKM syndrome. As anticoagulation therapy can affect thrombus formation and ischemic events, its impact on long-term cardiovascular health should be explored further. For additional context on antithrombotic therapy in AF patients, the recent study provides valuable insights into its effects on patient outcomes [43].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths that bolster its reliability and relevance. The use of CHARLS, a large, nationally representative cohort of 4,565 participants followed from 2011 to 2020, provides substantial statistical power and generalizability to middle-aged and older Chinese adults. The longitudinal design, with biennial follow-ups, enabled us to capture incident events and assess risk trajectories over time, a significant improvement over cross-sectional studies. The China-PAR model’s specificity to the Chinese population enhances the accuracy of our risk estimates, addressing a common limitation of applying Western-derived models to Asian cohorts. Additionally, our use of advanced statistical methods—k-means clustering for risk trajectories, XGBoost for feature selection, and spline regression for non-linear associations—offers methodological rigor and novel insights into risk dynamics.

Despite these strengths, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, our reliance on self-reported outcomes for CVD, heart disease, and stroke introduces potential misclassification bias. Although validated in prior epidemiological studies, self-reports may overestimate or underestimate true event rates compared to clinically confirmed diagnoses, potentially attenuating or inflating our effect estimates. Moreover, reporting accuracy may differ by education and urbanicity, potentially introducing bias. Our models accounted for these key sociodemographic factors to reduce such measurement error. Second, the exclusion of 13,140 participants from the original 17,705 due to missing data or pre-existing conditions may limit generalizability. While multiple imputation mitigated some bias, residual selection effects could skew our findings toward healthier or more compliant individuals. Third, the study period (2011–2020) spans a decade of evolving medical practices and health trends in China, such as increased use of statins or changes in hypertension management, which may confound temporal associations. Fourth, the China-PAR model, while advantageous for our population, restricts direct comparability with studies using other risk scores, complicating meta-analyses or global syntheses. Fifth, the mediation analysis, though insightful, explained only a small fraction of the total effect, indicating unmeasured confounders or mediators (e.g., genetic factors) that we could not assess due to data limitations.Sixth, the use of logistic regression may overestimate relative risks for common outcomes and cannot account for censoring or competing risks like death. Without exact event dates in CHARLS, time-to-event analysis was not feasible. Future studies with detailed timing and mortality data should consider Cox or Fine–Gray models to better capture temporal dynamics and competing risks. Seventh, using the Framingham risk score to define CKM stage 3 and the China-PAR score as the exposure may introduce conceptual dependence between these measures. Although both assess cardiovascular risk, they emphasize different aspects, which could affect the observed associations. Future work should incorporate more independent measures—such as biomarkers or imaging—to better clarify the relationship between CKM stages and cardiovascular outcomes. Eighth, we estimated cumulative ASCVD risk by averaging the 2012 and 2015 ASCVD scores and multiplying by the 3-year interval. While this method is practical, it assumes a linear progression of risk, which may not fully capture potential non-linear trajectories. Finally, our focus on CKM stages 0–3, excluding Stage 4 (clinical CVD), limits applicability to end-stage disease, though this was intentional to study incident rather than prevalent outcomes. These limitations, while notable, do not negate the study’s contributions but highlight areas for refinement in future work.

Implications for practice and research

Our findings have significant implications for clinical practice and public health. The consistent association between ASCVD10 year scores and cardiovascular events across CKM stages 0–3 suggests that risk assessment should not be reserved for advanced disease but integrated into routine care for all stages, including apparently healthy individuals (Stage 0). Clinicians could use the China-PAR model to identify at-risk patients early, enabling preventive measures like lifestyle interventions or pharmacotherapy to halt progression. The stronger effects in younger individuals and females further imply that risk stratification should be tailored to these groups, challenging traditional assumptions that prioritize older men.

The mediation by metabolic markers underscores the importance of targeting glycemic control and insulin resistance. Interventions reducing HbA1c or TyG levels—through diet, exercise, or medications like metformin—could mitigate cardiovascular risk, particularly in early CKM stages where non-linear effects are pronounced. For policymakers, our results advocate for incorporating ASCVD risk screening into national health programs, especially given China’s rising burden of metabolic and CVD. A cumulative risk approach could enhance risk prediction tools, offering a more dynamic assessment for long-term prevention strategies.

For research, our study raises several questions. The non-linear associations and threshold effects require mechanistic studies—perhaps using imaging or biomarkers—to understand why risk escalates rapidly at certain points. The partial mediation by metabolic factors suggests a need to explore additional pathways, such as inflammation or renal dysfunction, which were not fully captured in CHARLS. Linking the data to medical records or claims data could provide more precise diagnostic information, improving the accuracy of risk predictions and reducing misclassification. Interventional trials testing whether lowering ASCVD scores in early CKM stages reduces event rates would provide causal evidence, bridging the gap between observation and action. Finally, replicating our findings in non-Chinese populations or with other risk models could clarify whether these patterns are universal or context-specific, informing global CKM syndrome guidelines.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates a strong and graded association between ASCVD 10 year scores and incident cardiovascular events across stages 0–3 of CKM syndrome in a nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults. Our findings confirm the utility of the China-PAR ASCVD risk model in stratifying cardiovascular risk within the context of multimorbidity and highlight the added predictive value of incorporating longitudinal risk trajectories and metabolic mediators. The results underscore the importance of early identification and intervention for individuals at higher CKM stages and with elevated or increasing ASCVD risk, to prevent future cardiovascular complications. These insights support a shift toward more dynamic, integrated, and stage-specific approaches in cardiovascular risk assessment and management, particularly in aging populations with complex comorbidity profiles.
