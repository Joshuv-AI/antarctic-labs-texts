Weighted averages of SL 2 ⁡ ( ℝ ) \operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}) automorphic kernel Part I: non-oscillatory functions
Lasse Grimmelt, Jori Merikoski

Abstract
. We prove a theorem that evaluates weighted averages of sums parametrised by congruence subgroups of SL 2 ⁡ ( ℤ ) \operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}) . In the proof, spectral methods are applied directly to the automorphic kernel instead of going over sums of Kloosterman sums. In number theoretical applications this better preserves the specific symmetries throughout the application of spectral methods. In a separate paper we apply the main theorem to quadratic polynomials and obtain new results about their greatest prime factor and the equidistribution of their roots to prime moduli.

11F72, 11N75 primary

1.
Introduction

The determinant equation
ad-bc=h
is ubiquitous in number theory and often we wish to count its integer solutions in dyadic ranges and subject to congruence restrictions. The go-to approach has been to apply Poisson summation and show cancellation along the resulting
sums of Kloosterman sums
. The
Kuznetsov trace formula
is the crucial ingredient in this approach. The seminal work of Deshouillers and Iwaniec
[
4
]
has since produced countless applications (cf. for instance,
[
2
,
14
,
17
,
19
]
), colloquially referred to as the
Kloostermania
.

In
[
8
]
, the authors developed an alternative approach that directly applies spectral theory to a Poincaré type series. In this paper, we advance this perspective further and interpret the counting problem as a weighted average of a two-variable
\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
automorphic kernel function. In contrast to
[
8
]
that still relied on one application of the spectral large sieve, this allows us to sidestep the spectral large sieve and with it Kuznetsov’s formula and Kloosterman sums completely. This paper is the part I of a two-part series, in the part II we will generalise to oscillatory weight functions.

Our main theorem inherits all benefits of the results in
[
8
]
, for example, it is easier to apply compared to the classical Kloosterman sum approach in cases which would require delicate considerations about cusps and scaling matrices. Furthermore, it has a new advantage when averaging over the level variable is available, as it fully preserves existing divisor switching symmetry. See Section
1.1
for announcement of applications to quadratic polynomials. The proofs of these applications are based on Theorem
8.1
and appear in the companion paper
[
9
]
. In Section
1.2
we give further simple motivational examples.

We now introduce some notation for the statement of main theorem. Let
\Gamma\subseteq\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
be a congruence subgroup of level
q\geq 1
and fix a group character
\chi(\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix a & b \\ c& d\endlx@physics@smallmatrix))=\chi(d)
for
\chi
a Dirichlet character modulo
q.
We let
\Gamma
act on the Lie group
\mathrm{G}:=\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
by multiplication from the left
\mathrm{g}\mapsto\gamma\mathrm{g}
. Given a compactly supported function
F:\mathrm{G}\to{\mathbb{C}}
, we construct the
automorphic kernel function

\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma,\chi}F:\mathrm{G}\times\mathrm{G}\to{\mathbb{C}}
by

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma,\chi}F)(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\overline{\chi}(\gamma)F(\tau_{1}^{-1}\gamma\tau_{2}).

We shall write
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma,\chi}
when
\Gamma
and
\chi
are clear from the context.

If
F
is a smooth bump function and its support is large and not too skewed compared to the level
q
, we expect that the sum over
\Gamma
is well approximated by the corresponding integral over
\mathrm{G}
against the Haar measure
\mathrm{d}\mathrm{g}
, normalised by the volume of the fundamental domain
\Gamma\backslash\mathrm{G}
. This leads us to define the discrepancy
\Delta=\Delta_{\Gamma,\chi}

\displaystyle\Delta F(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{K}F(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})-\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\chi\,\mathrm{principal}}}{|\Gamma\backslash\mathrm{G}|}\int_{\mathrm{G}}F(\mathrm{g})\mathrm{d}\mathrm{g}.

The main goal is to show that this discrepancy is small, at least in some average sense.

Definition 1

(Weighted average discrepancy)
.

For a compactly supported
F:\mathrm{G}\to{\mathbb{C}}
and finitely supported weights
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}:\mathrm{G}\to{\mathbb{C}}
we define the
weighted average discrepancy

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{1}|\Delta F|\alpha_{2}\rangle=\sum_{\tau_{1},\tau_{2}}\overline{\alpha_{1}(\tau_{1})}\alpha_{2}(\tau_{2})\Delta F(\tau_{1},\tau_{2}).

We also require a definition that encapsulates the notion of a smooth dyadic bump function.

Definition 2

(Smooth dyadically supported function)
.

For
n,J\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,
\delta>0,
and
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
define the space of
J
times differentiable dyadically supported functions.

\displaystyle C_{\delta}^{J}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}):=\big\{f\in

\displaystyle C^{J}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}):\,f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\,\,\text{supported on}\,

\displaystyle(|x_{1}|,\dots,|x_{n}|)\in[X_{1},2X_{1}]\times\cdots\times[X_{n},2X_{n}],

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x_{1}}^{J_{1}}\cdots\partial_{x_{n}}^{J_{n}}f\|_{\infty}\leq\prod_{i\leq n}(\delta X_{i})^{-J_{i}}\quad\text{for all}\quad 0\leq J_{1}+\cdots+J_{n}\leq J\big\}.

We define for any
R>0
and
\mathrm{g}=\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix a &b \\ c& d\endlx@physics@smallmatrix)\in\mathrm{G}
the
R
-skewed hyperbolic size (cf. (
2.5
) also)

\displaystyle u_{R}(\mathrm{g}):=\tfrac{1}{4}(a^{2}+(b/R)^{2}+(cR)^{2}+d^{2}-2).

For any statement
S
we let
\mathbf{1}\{S\}
denote the indicator function that
S
holds. We denote
\theta=\theta(\Gamma,\chi):=\max\{0,\operatorname{Re}(\sqrt{1/4-\lambda_{1}(\Gamma,\chi)})\}
with
\lambda_{1}(\Gamma,\chi)
denoting the smallest positive eigenvalue of the hyperbolic Laplacian for
\Gamma
with character
\chi
. The
Selberg eigenvalue conjecture
states that
we have
\theta=0
and the best unconditional bound for congruence subgroups is
\theta\leq 7/64
by Kim and Sarnak
[
11
]
.

The most technical version of our main result is given in Theorem
7.1
, for now we state the following special case.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
be a congruence subgroup with a group character
\chi
. Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{c}(\mathrm{G})
. Let
A,C,D,\delta>0
with
AD>\delta
and let
f\in C^{10}_{\delta}(A,C,D)
. Denote
F:\mathrm{G}\to{\mathbb{C}},\,F(\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix a & b \\ c& d\endlx@physics@smallmatrix)):=f(a,c,d)
and denote
R_{1}=A/C,R_{2}=D/C
. Then for any choice
X_{0}X_{1}X_{2}\geq AD
with
X_{0},X_{1},X_{2}\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{1}|\Delta F|\alpha_{2}\rangle\ll\delta^{-O(1)}(AD)^{1/2+o(1)}X_{0}^{\theta}\sqrt{\,\langle\alpha_{1}|\Delta k_{X_{1}^{2},R_{1}}|\alpha_{1}\rangle\,\langle\alpha_{2}|\Delta k_{X_{2}^{2},R_{2}}|\alpha_{2}\rangle},

where
\langle\alpha_{i}|\Delta k_{X_{i}^{2},R_{i}}|\alpha_{i}\rangle\geq 0
for certain smooth functions
k_{Y,R}:\mathrm{G}\to[0,1]
which satisfy

\displaystyle k_{Y,R}(\mathrm{g})\leq\frac{\mathbf{1}\{u_{R}(\mathrm{g})\leq Y\}}{\sqrt{1+u_{R}(\mathrm{g})}}.

Theorem
1.1
starts with an automorphic kernel and, after passing through a spectral expansion and Cauchy-Schwarz, requires the estimation of two new kernel functions on the right-hand side. On the one hand, corresponding to the
diagonal contribution
of the Cauchy-Schwarz, the kernel
k_{X_{i}^{2},R_{i}}
have concentrated mass
\asymp 1
in the skewed ball
|a|,|d|\leq 1,|b|\leq R_{i},|c|\leq 1/R_{i}
. Outside this ball, the kernel decay due to
1/\sqrt{1+u_{R_{i}}(\mathrm{g})}
. On the other hand, corresponding to the
off-diagonal
contribution, the limiting case in the support occurs when
|a|,|d|\asymp X_{i}
,
|b|\asymp R_{i}X_{i},

|c|\asymp R_{i}^{-1}X_{i}
. There the new kernel
k_{X_{i}^{2},R_{i}}
are supported around
(A_{i},C_{i},D_{i})
which satisfy
A_{i}D_{i}=X_{i}^{2},\frac{A_{i}}{C_{i}}=\frac{D_{i}}{C_{i}}=R_{i}
, analogously to the original function
F
. Therefore, if we consider
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
,
R_{1}=R_{2},

X_{0}=1
, and
X_{1}=X_{2}=\sqrt{AD}
, then the statement of Theorem 1.1 is trivial. In applications, we win over the trivial bound by taking
X_{0}>(AD)^{\varepsilon}
, which exploits the existence of a spectral gap
\theta<1/2
.

The statement has other symmetries, for instance, as a corollary we see that the it stays true if we replace
\Gamma
by the conjugate
\mathrm{a}^{-1}\Gamma\mathrm{a}
for any diagonal matrix
\mathrm{a}\in\mathrm{G}
.

1.1.
Applications to quadratic polynomials

In the companion paper
[
9
]
, we consider quadratic polynomials of the shape
an^{2}+h
and obtain new results about their greatest prime factor, the equidistribution of their roots to prime moduli, and a certain divisor problem which is used in the recent work of the second author
[
13
]
.

With respect to the greatest prime factor, we obtain the following theorem that improves upon
[
12
,
16
]
in two aspects. First, we unconditionally obtain the exponent 1.312 that previously was only available under Selberg’s eigenvalue conjecture. Second, we get uniformity in the shift
h
under an assumption involving the Legendre symbol
(\tfrac{x}{p})
. To state the assumption, we define

\displaystyle\varrho_{a,h}(k):=\#\{\nu\in{\mathbb{Z}}/k{\mathbb{Z}}:a\nu^{2}+h\equiv 0\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{k})\}.

This is a multiplicative function with
\varrho_{a,h}(p)=1+(\frac{-ah}{p})
for
\gcd(a,p)=1
. In contrast to all previous works, the proof is independent of progress towards the Selberg eigenvalue conjecture – any fixed spectral gap would give the same quality of result.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists some small
\varepsilon>0
such that the following holds for all
X>\varepsilon^{-1}
. Let
1\leq h\leq X^{1+\varepsilon}
be square-free and let
1\leq a\leq X^{\varepsilon}
with
\gcd(a,h)=1
. Suppose that for any
X^{\varepsilon}<Y<Z\leq X^{2}
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{Y\leq p<Z}\frac{\log p}{p}\varrho_{a,h}(p)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\log Z/Y+1/\varepsilon.

Then there exists
n\in[X,2X]
such that the greatest prime factor of
an^{2}+h
is greater than
X^{1.312}
.

For
ah\leq X^{\varepsilon^{2}}
the hypothesis can be shown unconditionally by the zero-free region, since a possible Siegel zeros would help to make the sum small. It seems likely that this hypothesis is a fundamental obstruction, as its contraposition means that the
an^{2}+h
have more small prime factors than expected.

In similar spirit, we can also obtain uniformity in
h
for the equidistribution of roots of quadratic congruences to prime moduli, generalising the work of Duke, Friedlander, and Iwaniec
[
7
]
. We again need to include the distribution of
\varrho_{a,h}(p)
in the statement. However, here the issue is of opposite nature and the theorem becomes trivial if
\varrho_{a,h}(p)
is
0
too often, i.e. if there is a Siegel zero for the real character
(\frac{-ah}{\cdot})
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
1\leq h\leq X^{1+o(1)}
be square-free and let
1\leq a\leq X^{o(1)}
with
\gcd(a,h)=1
. Then for any
0\leq\alpha<\beta\leq 1
we have

\displaystyle\#\Bigl\{(p,\nu):p\leq X,\,a\nu^{2}+h\equiv 0\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{p}),\,\frac{\nu}{p}\in(\alpha,\beta]\Bigr\}=(\beta-\alpha)\sum_{p\leq X}\varrho_{a,h}(p)+o\bigl(\pi(X)\bigr).

Finally, in
[
9
]
we show a technical variant of the divisor problem for the binary polynomial
ax^{2}+by^{3}
. This was used by the second author
[
13
]
to show the following result about primes of the shape
ax^{2}+by^{3}
, conditional to the hypothesis that certain Hecke eigenvalues exhibit square-root cancellation along the values of binary cubic forms.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
a,b>0
be coprime integers. Assume that
[
13
, Conjecture
\mathrm{C}_{a}(\varepsilon)
]
holds for all
\varepsilon>0
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{x\leq X^{1/2}}\sum_{y\leq X^{1/3}}\Lambda(ax^{2}+by^{3})=(1+o(1))X^{5/6}.

1.2.
Examples

It is instructive to consider what Theorem
1.1
says if
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}(q)
,
\chi=1
,
\delta\gg 1,
and
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\delta_{I}
, that is, when

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{1}|\Delta F|\alpha_{2}\rangle=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}F(\gamma)-\frac{1}{|\Gamma\backslash\mathrm{G}|}\int_{\mathrm{G}}F(\mathrm{g})\mathrm{d}\mathrm{g}.

An application of Theorem
1.1
shows that this discrepancy is bounded by

\displaystyle\ll(AD)^{1/2}X_{0}^{\theta}K_{1}^{1/2}K_{2}^{1/2},

where, by using

\displaystyle K_{i}\leq\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}(q)}k_{X_{i},R_{i}}(\gamma)\leq\sum_{\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix a & b \\ c & d\endlx@physics@smallmatrix)\in\Gamma_{0}(q)}\frac{{\mathbf{1}}\{|a|^{2}+|b/R_{i}|^{2}+|R_{i}c|^{2}+|d|^{2}\leq X_{i}^{2}\}}{\sqrt{|a|^{2}+|b/R_{i}|^{2}+|R_{i}c|^{2}+|d|^{2}}}.

Terms with

\ll 1+(R_{i}q)^{-1}
and terms with

\ll 1+R_{i}

If the ranges are skewed, that is, if one of the ratios

is larger than

[
4

. In general, this illustrates how our kernel-based approach leads to results of at least the same strength as using the spectral large sieve.

1.3.
A generalisation to linear functionals

Theorem
1.1
applies more generally to linear functionals in place of the finitely supported weights. This language also allows for a conceptual proof sketch, see the next subsection. Let
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{G})
denote the set of linear functionals
C(\mathrm{G})\to{\mathbb{C}}
. For
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{G})
denote the image of
f\in C(\mathrm{G})
by
\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}:=\alpha(f)\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Definition 3

(Compactly supported linear functional)
.

We define the set of compactly supported linear functionals via

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}(\mathrm{G}):=\bigcup_{K\subseteq\mathrm{G}\,\text{compact}}\bigcup_{c>0}\Bigl\{\alpha\in\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{G}):|\langle f\rangle_{\alpha}|\leq c\sup_{\mathrm{g}\in K}|f(\mathrm{g})|\text{ for all }f\in C(\mathrm{G})\Bigr\}.

We make this definition to ensure convergence of the spectral expansion in the proof. It can be relaxed to include more general functionals, as long as the relevant sums are absolutely convergent. In practice, we are interested in functionals given by weighted sums
f\mapsto\sum_{\tau\in T}\alpha(\tau)f(\tau)
for some finite set
T\subseteq\mathrm{G}
and weight
\alpha:T\to{\mathbb{C}}
. For any
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{G})
we define the complex conjugate by

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle_{\overline{\alpha}}=\overline{\langle\overline{f}\rangle_{\alpha}}.

Definition 4

(Sesquilinear form induced by a binary function)
.

For any
F\in C(\mathrm{G}\times\mathrm{G})
we define the sesquilinear form
\mathcal{L}_{c}(\mathrm{G})\times\mathcal{L}_{c}(\mathrm{G})\to{\mathbb{C}}

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{1}|F|\alpha_{2}\rangle:=\langle\langle F\rangle_{\overline{\alpha_{1}}}\rangle_{\alpha_{2}},

where
\overline{\alpha_{1}}
acts on the left variable of
F(\mathrm{g}_{1},\mathrm{g}_{2})
and
\alpha_{2}
acts on the right variable.

Restriction to
\mathcal{L}_{c}(\mathrm{G})
means that this is well-defined, that is,
\langle F\rangle_{\overline{\alpha_{1}}}
defines a continuous function in the second variable, and we have
\langle F\rangle_{\overline{\alpha_{1}}}\rangle_{\alpha_{2}}=\langle\langle F\rangle_{\alpha_{2}}\rangle_{\overline{\alpha_{1}}},
where in both expressions
\overline{\alpha_{1}}
acts on the left variable and
\alpha_{2}
acts on the right variable. This may be viewed as a tensor product of
\overline{\alpha_{1}}
and
\alpha_{2}
, that is, for
F(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\overline{f_{1}(\tau_{1})}f_{2}(\tau_{2})
we have

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{1}|F|\alpha_{2}\rangle=\overline{\langle f_{1}\rangle_{\alpha_{1}}}\langle f_{2}\rangle_{\alpha_{2}}

Letting the binary function be
\Delta F(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
, this generalizes the weighted average discrepancy to linear functionals and Theorem
1.1
applies to the finitely supported weights replaced by
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{c}(\mathrm{G})
.

1.4.
Sketch of the proof of Theorem
1.1

As suggested by the notation, Theorem
1.1
may be seen as a quantitative variant of the popular
L^{\infty}L^{2}L^{2}
inequality

(1.1)

\displaystyle|\langle\mathbf{a}_{1}|\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\ast}\mathbf{X}_{0}\mathbf{X}_{2}|\mathbf{a}_{2}\rangle|\leq\|\mathbf{X}_{0}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\sqrt{\langle\mathbf{a}_{1}|\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\ast}\mathbf{X}_{1}|\mathbf{a}_{1}\rangle\,\langle\mathbf{a}_{2}|\mathbf{X}_{2}^{\ast}\mathbf{X}_{2}|\mathbf{a}_{2}\rangle},

which is true for any
\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2}\in\mathbf{H}
and bounded operators
\mathbf{X}_{0},\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathbf{X}_{2}
on a Hilbert space
\mathbf{H}
with the inner product
\langle\cdot|\cdot\rangle
. We now explain this correspondence.

The first step in the proof of Theorem
1.1
is to reduce to the balanced case
R_{1}=R_{2}=1
via what we call
unskewing
. Denote
\mathrm{a}[y]:=\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix y^{1/2} & \\ & y^{-1/2}\endlx@physics@smallmatrix)
and

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}(\mathrm{g}):=F(\mathrm{a}[R_{1}]\mathrm{g}\mathrm{a}[R_{2}]^{-1})

and define linear functionals
\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}
via

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle_{\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}}=\langle r_{\mathrm{a}[R_{j}]}f\rangle_{\alpha_{j}}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{1}|\Delta F|\alpha_{2}\rangle=\langle\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}|\Delta\widetilde{F}|\widetilde{\alpha}_{2}\rangle\text{ and }\langle\alpha_{j}|\Delta k_{X_{j}^{2},R_{j}}|\alpha_{j}\rangle=\langle\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}|\Delta k_{X_{j}^{2},1}|\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}\rangle.

Noting that
\widetilde{F}\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix a & b \\ c& d\endlx@physics@smallmatrix))=\widetilde{f}(a,c,d)
with
\widetilde{f}\in C^{10}_{\delta}(\sqrt{AD},\sqrt{AD},\sqrt{AD})
, it then suffices to prove the theorem for
R_{1}=R_{2}=1
.

Let us denote the convolution on
\mathrm{G}
by
(F_{1}\ast F_{2})(\mathrm{g})=\int_{\mathrm{G}}F_{1}(\mathrm{h})F_{2}(\mathrm{h}^{-1}\mathrm{g})\mathrm{d}\mathrm{h}
. We sketch the remaining proof for the special case that
\chi=1
and
F=\overline{F_{1}}\ast F_{0}\ast F_{2}
, where
F_{j}:\mathrm{G}\to{\mathbb{C}}
are bi-
\mathrm{K}
-invariant smooth bump functions supported on
u(\mathrm{g})=u_{1}(\mathrm{g})\asymp X_{j}
with
AD=X_{0}X_{1}X_{2}
. While the general case requires a more concrete approach compared to this abstract sketch, the input is morally the same. The main difference is that the involved functions do not factorise neatly and are not bi-
\mathrm{K}
-invariant. The factorisation is still approximately true on the spectral side and, as a result of the unskewing, the latter can be accounted for by incorporating wiggle room in the left and right
\mathrm{K}
-types up to height at most
(AD)^{o(1)}
.

For bi-
\mathrm{K}
-invariant
F
the kernel function
\mathcal{K}F(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
is right-
\mathrm{K}
-invariant in both coordinates. The compactly supported linear functionals on
C(\mathrm{G}/\mathrm{K})
form a vector space
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{c}(\mathrm{G}/\mathrm{K})
.
For any small
\delta>0
, let
\psi_{\delta}(u(\mathrm{g}))=\psi(\delta^{-1}u(\mathrm{g}))
for
\psi:[0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
a smooth function supported on
[0,1]
and
\psi=1
on
[0,1/2]
. We define the normalised convolution

\displaystyle h_{\delta}=\frac{1}{\|\psi_{\delta}\ast\psi_{\delta}\|_{1}}\psi_{\delta}\ast\psi_{\delta},

so that
h_{\delta}
approximates the Dirac mass on
u(\mathrm{g})=0
with respect to the Haar measure
\mathrm{d}\mathrm{g}
. A key observation is that this defines an inner product on
\mathcal{L}
via

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\rangle_{\delta}:=\langle\alpha_{1}|\Delta h_{\delta}|\alpha_{2}\rangle.

The positive-definiteness
\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{\delta}>0
for
\alpha\neq 0
follows by the spectral expansion of an automorphic kernel (the pretrace formula, Proposition
3.1
) and the spectral positivity of convolutions of real valued even functions (cf. Lemma
6.3
). Conjugate symmetry follows by using
h_{\delta}(\mathrm{g})=h_{\delta}(\mathrm{g}^{-1})
to get
\Delta h_{\delta}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\Delta h_{\delta}(\tau_{2},\tau_{1})
.

A compactly supported
f\in C(\mathrm{K}\backslash\mathrm{G}/\mathrm{K})
defines an operator
\mathbf{X}_{f}:\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{L}
via convolution

\displaystyle\langle F\rangle_{\mathbf{X}_{f}\alpha}=\langle F\ast f\rangle_{\alpha}.

Then, since Dirac delta is the unit for convolutions, for
F=\overline{F_{1}}\ast F_{0}\ast F_{2}
we have
as
\delta\to 0

(1.2)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{X}_{F_{1}}\alpha_{1},\mathbf{X}_{F_{0}}\mathbf{X}_{F_{2}}\alpha_{2}\rangle_{\delta}\to\langle\alpha_{1}|\Delta F|\alpha_{2}\rangle.

Furthermore, uniformly in sufficiently small
\delta>0
, we have the
L^{\infty}
-estimate

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{X}_{F_{0}}\|_{\mathrm{op},\delta}:=\sup_{0\neq\alpha\in\mathcal{L}}\sqrt{\frac{\langle\mathbf{X}_{F_{0}}\alpha,\mathbf{X}_{F_{0}}\alpha\rangle_{\delta}}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{\delta}}}\ll X_{0}^{1/2+\theta},

which follows from Lemmas
4.2
,
4.3
, and Proposition
5.1
. Then by (
1.1
), which is of course true more generally for bounded operators on an inner product space, we get

(1.3)

\displaystyle|\langle\mathbf{X}_{F_{1}}\alpha_{1},\mathbf{X}_{F_{0}}\mathbf{X}_{F_{2}}\alpha_{2}\rangle_{\delta}|\ll X_{0}^{1/2+\theta}\sqrt{\langle\mathbf{X}_{F_{1}}\alpha_{1},\mathbf{X}_{F_{1}}\alpha_{1}\rangle_{\delta}\langle\mathbf{X}_{F_{2}}\alpha_{2},\mathbf{X}_{F_{2}}\alpha_{2}\rangle_{\delta}}.

As
\delta\to 0
we have

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{X}_{F_{j}}\alpha_{j},\mathbf{X}_{F_{j}}\alpha_{j}\rangle_{\delta}\to\langle\alpha_{j}|\Delta(F_{j}\ast F_{j})|\alpha_{j}\rangle,

and in Lemma
6.4
we show that

\displaystyle|(F_{j}\ast F_{j})(\mathrm{g})|\ll X_{j}k_{X_{j}^{2},1}(\mathrm{g}).

Plugging this into (
1.3
) and using (
1.2
), we obtain the bound claimed in Theorem
1.1
.

In the more technical Theorem
7.1
, we add Hecke operators that naturally arise when considering a determinant equation
ad-bc=h
with
|h|>1
. There we use an
L^{\infty}
-bound on the spectral side for the Hecke eigenvalues. Note that treating them as a part of the linear functionals is already contained in Theorem
1.1
. Recalling the multiplicativity relations for the Hecke operators, this shows that the determinant
h
behaves analogously to the scales
X=X_{0}X_{1}X_{2}
, the only difference is that for
h
we cannot choose an arbitrary factorisation but require a factorisation over integers. We also note that for large
h
we need to normalise the matrices
\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix a& b\\ c& d\endlx@physics@smallmatrix)
by
1/\sqrt{h}
to get a weight on
\mathrm{G}
, which replaces
X
by
X/h
.

1.5.
Further discussion

We were partially inspired by the recent work of Pascadi
[
16
]
, who showed how the spectral large sieve for exceptional eigenvalues may be bounded in terms of a counting problem in physical space. Indeed, we suspect that Theorem
1.1
could also be proved via Poisson summation and the Kuznetsov trace formula, but this seems circuitous. If required, one can even apply the Poisson summation formula to
a,d
to write
\Delta k_{X_{i}^{2},R_{i}}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
in terms of sums of Kloosterman sums, which shows that we have not lost any information compared to the Kloostermania approach. In Theorem
7.1
we use an
L^{\infty}
bound for the Hecke eigenvalues, which allows us to capture the advantages of the recent work of Assing, Blomer, and Li
[
1
]
.

The main benefit compared to previous approaches is that we can carry all information about the functionals
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
through the Cauchy-Schwarz application, which is especially useful if one has averaging over the level variable. We then get essentially optimal results by bounding the non-negative
\langle\alpha_{i}|\Delta k_{X_{i}^{2},R_{i}}|\alpha_{i}\rangle
crudely by
\langle\alpha_{i}|\mathcal{K}k_{X_{i}^{2},R_{i}}|\alpha_{i}\rangle
and estimating these rather trivially (cf.
[
9
]
for more details).

As mentioned, we win over trivial upper bounds solely thanks to the spectral gap
\theta<1/2
. Remarkably, the applications (Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
,
1.4
) do not depend at all on the quality of this gap and we get the same results as long as
\theta\leq 1/2-\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
independent of
\Gamma
. In particular, the results are independent of progress towards Selberg’s eigenvalue conjecture. We expect that this holds true for other applications where one has level averaging, such as primes in arithmetic progressions to large moduli, where also currently the best ranges
[
16
]
rely on the Kim-Sarnak bound
[
11
]
.

A remaining advantage of Kloostermania compared to the results here is in the case when an oscillating weight
F
causes the spectral expansion to blow-up along the regular spectrum. This happens, for instance, if one considers divisor correlations
d(n)d(n+h)
with the rough cut-off
n\leq X
or with a very large shift
h>X^{1+\varepsilon}
, or if one studies the fourth moment of the zeta function in a short interval. In part II, we will bridge this gap by showing how to handle oscillatory weight functions
F
with at least as good uniformity as one gets from Kloostermania, while keeping all the advantages of this paper. This will be achieved by a more general unskewing procedure that involves both
\mathrm{a}[y]
and
\mathrm{n}[x]
matrices in contrast to using only
\mathrm{a}[y]
matrices.

2.1.
Coordinates

We start by introducing two coordinate systems for the Lie group

\displaystyle\mathrm{G}:=\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})=\bigg\{\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix a&b \\ c& d\endlx@physics@matrix):a,b,c,d\in{\mathbb{R}},ad-bc=1\bigg\}

following mostly the notation in

\displaystyle\mathrm{N}:=

\displaystyle\bigg\{\mathrm{n}[x]=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 1&x \\ & 1\endlx@physics@matrix):x\in{\mathbb{R}}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}:=

\displaystyle\bigg\{\mathrm{a}[y]=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\sqrt{y }& \\ & 1/\sqrt{y}\endlx@physics@matrix):y\in(0,\infty)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}:=

\displaystyle\bigg\{\mathrm{k}[\theta]=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\cos\theta& \sin\theta\\ -\sin\theta& \cos\theta\endlx@physics@matrix):\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}\bigg\}

We will refer to the groups
\mathrm{N},\mathrm{A},\mathrm{K}
and their elements
\mathrm{n},\mathrm{a},\mathrm{k}
without further notice and these symbols are reserved for this purpose.

We then have the
Iwasawa decomposition

\displaystyle\mathrm{G}=\mathrm{N}\mathrm{A}\mathrm{K},

where the representation
\mathrm{g}=\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix a& b \\ c & d\endlx@physics@smallmatrix)=\mathrm{n}[x]\mathrm{a}[y]\mathrm{k}[\theta]
is uniquely defined for
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
via the sign
\pm
of
a,b,c,d
and

(2.1)

\displaystyle x=\frac{ac+bd}{c^{2}+d^{2}},\quad y=\frac{1}{c^{2}+d^{2}},\quad\theta=\arctan\bigg(-\frac{c}{d}\bigg)

and

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{n}[x]\mathrm{a}[y]\mathrm{k}[\theta]=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix \sqrt{y} \cos\theta- \frac{x}{\sqrt{y}} \sin\theta& \sqrt{y} \sin\theta+ \frac{x}{\sqrt{y}} \cos\theta\\ - \frac{1}{\sqrt{y}} \sin\theta&\frac{1}{\sqrt{y}} \cos\theta\endlx@physics@matrix).

The first two Iwasawa coordinates
(x,y)
correspond to the upper half-plane
\mathcal{H}:=\{x+iy:y>0\}
, which is isomorphic to the set of cosets
\mathrm{G}/\mathrm{K}
, in the sense that the group action on
\mathcal{H}
via
z\mapsto\frac{az+b}{cz+d}
is the matrix multiplication from the left on
\mathrm{G}/\mathrm{K}
.

We also have the
Cartan decomposition

\displaystyle\mathrm{G}=\mathrm{K}\mathrm{A}\mathrm{K},

where the representation
g=\mathrm{k}[\varphi]\mathrm{a}[e^{-\varrho}]\mathrm{k}[\vartheta]
,
\cosh(\varrho)=2u+1
, is unique provided that
\varrho>0
and
\varphi\in[0,\pi)
. We have

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{k}[\varphi]\mathrm{a}[e^{\varrho}]\mathrm{k}[\vartheta]=\mathrm{k}[\varphi+\pi/2]\mathrm{a}[e^{-\varrho}]\mathrm{k}[\vartheta-\pi/2]=\mathrm{k}[\varphi+\pi]\mathrm{a}[e^{\varrho}]\mathrm{k}[\vartheta-\pi].

The Cartan coordinates are best described by the isomorphism of groups via conjugation by the Cayley matrix
\mathcal{C}=\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix 1 & -i \\ 1 & i\endlx@physics@smallmatrix)
, which diagonalizes
\mathrm{K}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})

\displaystyle\cong\operatorname{SU}_{1,1}({\mathbb{R}}):=\bigg\{\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix \alpha& \beta\\ \overline{\beta} & \overline{\alpha}\endlx@physics@matrix):\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{C}},\,|\alpha|^{2}-|\beta|^{2}=1\bigg\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{g}

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathcal{C}\mathrm{g}\mathcal{C}^{-1}.

From this we can compute for
\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix a& b \\ c& d\endlx@physics@smallmatrix)=\mathrm{n}[x]\mathrm{a}[e^{t}]\mathrm{k}[\theta]=\mathrm{k}[\varphi]\mathrm{a}[e^{-\varrho}]\mathrm{k}[\vartheta]

(2.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\alpha=\frac{1}{2}(a+d+i(b-c))=e^{i\theta}(\cosh(t/2)+ie^{-t/2}x/2)=e^{i(\varphi+\vartheta)}\cosh(\varrho/2),\\
\beta=\frac{1}{2}(a-d-i(b+c))=e^{-i\theta}(\sinh(t/2)-ie^{-t/2}x/2)=e^{i(\varphi-\vartheta)}\sinh(-\varrho/2).\end{cases}

The first two Cartan coordinates
(\varphi,e^{-\varrho})
correspond to the polar coordinates of the Poincaré disk model of the upper half-plane

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\cong\{|z|<1\}:z\mapsto\frac{z-i}{z+i}.

Denoting

\displaystyle u(z,w):=\frac{|z-w|^{2}}{4\operatorname{Im}(z)\operatorname{Im}(w)},\quad z,w\in\mathcal{H},

we have
\cosh(\varrho)=2u(\mathrm{g}i,i)+1
. For
\mathrm{g}=\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix a & b \\ c& d\endlx@physics@smallmatrix)\in\mathrm{G}
we define

(2.5)

\displaystyle u(\mathrm{g}):=u(\mathrm{g}i,i)=\tfrac{1}{4}(a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+d^{2}-2).

We will denote
\mathrm{a}_{u}=\mathrm{a}[e^{-\varrho}]
for
\varrho>0
such that
\cosh(\varrho)=2u+1
.

We need the following lemma to pass from Cartan coordinates to Iwasawa coordinates.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
\mathrm{k}[\varphi]\mathrm{a}[e^{-\varrho}]=\mathrm{k}[\varphi]\mathrm{a}_{u}=\mathrm{n}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{k}[\theta]
, then

\displaystyle e^{i\theta}=e^{i\varphi}\bigg(\frac{\cosh\tfrac{\varrho}{2}+e^{-i2\varphi}\sinh{\tfrac{\varrho}{2}}}{\cosh\tfrac{\varrho}{2}+e^{i2\varphi}\sinh{\tfrac{\varrho}{2}}}\bigg)^{1/2}=e^{i\varphi}\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{1+1/u}+e^{-i2\varphi}}{\sqrt{1+1/u}+e^{i2\varphi}}\bigg)^{1/2},

with the branch of square-root defined by
1^{1/2}=1
and cut along
(-\infty,0]
.

Proof.

Denote Cayley matrix conjugates by

\displaystyle\mathrm{k}^{1}[\varphi]

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{C}\mathrm{k}[\varphi]\mathcal{C}^{-1}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix e^{i\varphi} \\ & e^{-i\varphi}\endlx@physics@matrix)

\displaystyle\mathrm{a}^{1}[e^{-\varrho}]

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{C}\mathrm{a}[e^{-\varrho}]\mathcal{C}^{-1}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\cosh(\varrho/2) & -\sinh(\varrho/2) \\ -\sinh(\varrho/2) & \cosh(\varrho/2)\endlx@physics@matrix)

\displaystyle\mathrm{n}^{1}[x]

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{C}\mathrm{n}[x]\mathcal{C}^{-1}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix 1+\frac{ix}{2}& -\frac{ix}{2}\\ \frac{ix}{2} & 1-\frac{ix}{2}\endlx@physics@matrix).

If
\mathrm{k}[\varphi]\mathrm{a}[e^{-\varrho}]=\mathrm{n}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{k}[\theta]
, then
\mathrm{k}^{1}[\varphi]\mathrm{a}^{1}[e^{-\varrho}]\mathrm{k}^{1}[-\theta]=\mathrm{n}^{1}\mathrm{a}^{1}.
In particular, denoting by
\diamond
the action of
\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix a & b \\ c&d\endlx@physics@smallmatrix)
on
{\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
by Möbius transformations

, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{k}^{1}[\varphi]\mathrm{a}^{1}[e^{-\varrho}]\mathrm{k}^{1}[-\theta]\diamond 1=\mathrm{n}^{1}\mathrm{a}^{1}\diamond 1=1.

Therefore, we have
\mathrm{k}^{1}[-\theta]\diamond 1=\mathrm{a}^{1}[e^{\varrho}]\mathrm{k}^{1}[-\varphi]\diamond 1
, so that

\displaystyle e^{-i2\theta}=\mathrm{k}^{1}[-\theta]\diamond 1=\mathrm{a}^{1}[e^{\varrho}]\mathrm{k}^{1}[-\varphi]\diamond 1=\frac{e^{-i\varphi}\cosh\tfrac{\varrho}{2}+e^{i\varphi}\sinh{\tfrac{\varrho}{2}}}{e^{i\varphi}\cosh\tfrac{\varrho}{2}+e^{-i\varphi}\sinh{\tfrac{\varrho}{2}}}=e^{-2i\varphi}\frac{\cosh\tfrac{\varrho}{2}+e^{i2\varphi}\sinh{\tfrac{\varrho}{2}}}{\cosh\tfrac{\varrho}{2}+e^{-i2\varphi}\sinh{\tfrac{\varrho}{2}}}.

The lemma follows by multiplying by

and taking the the square-root, where the sign is determined by continuity from the sign at

. This is justified since by
\operatorname{Re}(\sqrt{1+1/u}+e^{-i2\varphi})>0
we have

\displaystyle\frac{\cosh\tfrac{\varrho}{2}+e^{i2\varphi}\sinh{\tfrac{\varrho}{2}}}{\cosh\tfrac{\varrho}{2}+e^{-i2\varphi}\sinh{\tfrac{\varrho}{2}}}=\frac{\sqrt{1+1/u}+e^{-i2\varphi}}{\sqrt{1+1/u}+e^{i2\varphi}}\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(-\infty,0].

∎

Finally, we define the

which paramterise the big cell

\displaystyle\mathrm{g}=\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix a& b \\ c& d\endlx@physics@matrix)=\mathrm{n}[r_{1}]\mathrm{w}\mathrm{a}[c^{2}]\mathrm{n}[r_{2}],\quad r_{1}=\frac{a}{c},\quad r_{2}=\frac{d}{c},\quad\mathrm{w}=\mathrm{k}[\pi/2].

2.2.
Differential operators

Following the notation in

We construct the

In the Iwasawa coordinates

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{+}

\displaystyle=e^{2i\theta}(2iy\partial_{x}+2y\partial_{y}-i\partial_{\theta}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-}

\displaystyle=e^{-2i\theta}(-2iy\partial_{x}+2y\partial_{y}+i\partial_{\theta}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{x}_{3}

The operators

Integration

The invariant Haar measure

2.4.
Projections to fixed types

For
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
we say that a function
f:\mathrm{G}\to{\mathbb{C}}
has left type
\ell_{1}
(resp. right type
\ell_{2}
) if for all
\mathrm{k}[\theta]
we have

\displaystyle f(\mathrm{k}[\theta]\mathrm{g})=f(\mathrm{g})e^{i\ell_{1}\theta}.\quad(\text{resp. }f(\mathrm{g}\mathrm{k}[\theta])=f(\mathrm{g})e^{i\ell_{2}\theta}).

For any integrable
f:\mathrm{G}\to{\mathbb{C}}
and any
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
we define the projections to a fixed type by

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}f)(\mathrm{g}):=\int_{\mathrm{K}\times\mathrm{K}}f(\mathrm{k}[\theta_{1}]\mathrm{g}\mathrm{k}[\theta_{2}])e^{-i\ell_{1}\theta_{1}-i\ell_{2}\theta_{2}}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{k}[\theta_{1}]\mathrm{d}\mathrm{k}[\theta_{2}].

By (
2.3
) we have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}f)(\mathrm{g})=(\mathcal{F}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}f)(\mathrm{k}[-\pi]\mathrm{g}\mathrm{k}[\pi])=(-1)^{\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}}(\mathcal{F}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}f)(\mathrm{g}),

which implies that
\mathcal{F}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}f
vanishes unless
\ell_{1}\equiv\ell_{2}\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{2})
. We then have for any smooth compactly supported
f:\mathrm{G}\to{\mathbb{C}}
the Fourier series expansion

(2.18)

\displaystyle f(\mathrm{g})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\\
\ell_{1}\equiv\ell_{2}\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{2})\end{subarray}}(\mathcal{F}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}f)(\mathrm{g}).

Let
\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}
. The basic functions (defined using the Iwasawa coordinates)

(2.19)

\displaystyle\phi_{\ell}(\mathrm{g},\nu):=y^{1/2+\nu}e^{i\ell\theta}

3.
Spectral expansion of an automorphic kernel

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is based on applying the spectral expansion of an automorphic kernel, the Cauchy Schwarz inequality, and reversal of the spectral expansion. In particular, we need to be able to do this for general kernel functions involving different left and right types.

For a smooth compactly supported
F:\mathrm{G}\to{\mathbb{C}}
, congruence subgroup
\Gamma
, and a group character
\chi
of parity
\kappa\in\{0,1\}
, recall the construction of an automorphic kernel

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}F)(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=(\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma,\chi}F)(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\overline{\chi}(\gamma)F(\tau_{1}^{-1}\gamma\tau_{2}).

Using the Fourier expansion (
2.18
) we can write

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}F)(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\\
\ell_{1}\equiv\ell_{2}\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{2})\end{subarray}}(\mathcal{K}\mathcal{F}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}F)(\tau_{1},\tau_{2}).

For any
\gamma\in\Gamma
we have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}F)(\tau_{1},\gamma\tau_{2})=\chi(\gamma)(\mathcal{K}F)(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=(\mathcal{K}F)(\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1},\tau_{2}).

Thus, for
\ell_{1}\equiv\ell_{2}\not\equiv\kappa\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{2})
we see by the action of
\mathrm{k}[\pi]=\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix-1 & \\ & -1\endlx@physics@smallmatrix)\in\Gamma
with
\chi(\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix-1 & \\ & -1\endlx@physics@smallmatrix))=(-1)^{\kappa}
that

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}\mathcal{F}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}F)(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bigg((\mathcal{K}\mathcal{F}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}F)(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})+(-1)^{\kappa}(\mathcal{K}\mathcal{F}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}F)(\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix-1 & \\ & -1\endlx@physics@smallmatrix)\tau_{1},\tau_{2}))\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bigg((\mathcal{K}\mathcal{F}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}F)(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})-(-1)^{2\kappa}(\mathcal{K}\mathcal{F}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}F)(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\bigg)=0.

Hence, the Fourier expansion can be restricted to
\ell_{1}\equiv\ell_{2}\equiv\kappa\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{2})

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}F)(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\\
\ell_{1}\equiv\ell_{2}\equiv\kappa\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{2})\end{subarray}}(\mathcal{K}\mathcal{F}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}F)(\tau_{1},\tau_{2}).

\displaystyle=2\int\mathcal{F}_{\ell,\ell}f_{Z}(\mathrm{a}_{u})\mathcal{P}_{\nu}^{(\ell,\ell)}(\mathrm{a}_{u})\mathrm{d}u=2\hat{F}(\ell)\int\psi_{Z}(\mathrm{a}_{u})\mathcal{P}_{\nu}^{(\ell,\ell)}(\mathrm{a}_{u})\mathrm{d}u.

For
C
sufficiently large we have by Taylor approximation
\hat{F}(\ell)\gg L^{-1}
for
|\ell|\leq L
. By Lemma
4.3
for
Z^{\nu}\geq c_{0}L^{6}
and
\nu Z^{\nu/10}\geq\eta c_{0}^{1/10}\gg 1
we have

\displaystyle\int\psi_{Z}(\mathrm{a}_{u})\mathcal{P}_{\nu}^{(\ell,\ell)}(\mathrm{a}_{u})\mathrm{d}u\gg\frac{1}{Z^{1/4}(1+|\ell|)}\int_{Z^{1/2}}^{2Z^{1/2}}u^{-1/2+\nu}\mathrm{d}u\ll\frac{Z^{\nu/2}}{L}.

Thus, for
\nu\in(\eta,1/2-\eta)
with
Z^{\nu}\geq c_{0}L^{6}
we get by Lemma
6.3

\displaystyle L^{4}\langle k^{\mathrm{exc}}_{Z},\mathcal{P}_{\nu}^{(\ell,\ell)}\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}=L^{4}|\langle f_{Z},\mathcal{P}_{\nu}^{(\ell,\ell)}\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}|^{2}\gg Z^{\nu}.

∎

7.
Main theorem

Recalling the definition of a Hecke operator (
2.22
), for any function
f:\mathrm{G}\times\mathrm{G}\to{\mathbb{C}}
and
h_{1},h_{2}\geq 1
we define the two variable Hecke operator

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}_{h_{1},h_{2}}f)(\mathrm{g}_{1},\mathrm{g}_{2})=(\mathcal{T}_{h_{1}})_{\mathrm{g}_{1}}(\mathcal{T}_{h_{2}})_{\mathrm{g}_{2}}f(\mathrm{g}_{1},\mathrm{g}_{2}),

where
(\mathcal{T}_{h})_{\mathrm{g}_{i}}
is the Hecke operator
\mathcal{T}_{h}
acting on the
\mathrm{g}_{i}
coordinate. Theorem
1.1
is contained as special case of the following, after restricting to
h_{1}=h_{2}=1
.

Analogous to the choice
X_{0}X_{1}X_{2}
, we have a choice with the Hecke operators
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, either treat them as a part of the linear functionals or use an
L^{\infty}
-bound on the spectral side. The first option is already contained in Theorem
1.1
, the second option is covered by the following. Naturally, in the case that, say,
h_{1}
factorises as
h_{1}=h_{0}h_{1}^{\prime}
with
\gcd(h_{0},h_{1}^{\prime})=1
, the following also contains the hybrid version by using the multiplicativity relation
\mathcal{T}_{h_{1}}=\mathcal{T}_{h_{0}}\mathcal{T}_{h_{1}^{\prime}}
and absorbing one Hecke operator into the linear functional. We see that
h_{i}
and
X_{i}
behave analogously, the only difference is that for the
X_{i}
we can choose an arbitrary factorisation whereas for
h_{i}
we require a factorisation over integers.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a congruence subgroup of level
q
and let
\chi
be a group character for
\Gamma
. Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{c}(\mathrm{G})
. Let
A,C,D>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
with
AD>\delta
and let
f\in C^{J}_{\delta}(A,C,D)
with
J\geq 10
even. Denote
F:\mathrm{G}\to{\mathbb{C}},\,F(\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix a & b \\ c& d\endlx@physics@smallmatrix)):=f(a,c,d)
and
R_{1}=A/C,R_{2}=D/C
.

Let
\beta_{1}(h),\beta_{2}(h)
be complex coefficients with finite support. Denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\max_{\lambda\in\{\lambda_{V},\lambda_{\mathfrak{c},\nu}\}}|\mathcal{H}(\lambda)|,\quad\mathcal{H}(\lambda)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{1},h_{2}\\
\gcd(h_{1}h_{2},q)=1\end{subarray}}\beta_{1}(h_{1})\beta_{2}(h_{2})\overline{\lambda(h_{1})}\lambda(h_{2}).

Then for any choice
X_{0}X_{1}X_{2}\geq AD+1
with
X_{0},X_{1},X_{2}\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(h_{1}h_{2},q)=1\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\beta_{1}(h_{1})\beta_{2}(h_{2})\langle\alpha_{1}|\mathcal{T}_{h_{1},h_{2}}\Delta F|\alpha_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\ll_{J}\delta^{-O(1)}(AD)^{1/2+o(1)}\mathcal{H}X_{0}^{\theta}\sqrt{\,\langle\alpha_{1}|\Delta k_{X_{1}^{2},R_{1}}|\alpha_{1}\rangle\,\langle\alpha_{2}|\Delta k_{X_{2}^{2},R_{2}}|\alpha_{2}\rangle}

where
\langle\alpha_{1}|\Delta k_{X_{i}^{2},R_{i}}|\alpha_{1}\rangle\geq 0
with

\displaystyle k_{Y,R}(\mathrm{g})=k_{Y}(\mathrm{a}[R]^{-1}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{a}[R])

for some smooth functions
k_{Y}:\mathrm{G}\to[0,1]
which satisfy for any
0\leq J_{0}+J_{1}+J_{2}\leq J

(7.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{c}^{J_{0}}\partial_{a}^{J_{1}}\partial_{d}^{J_{2}}k_{Y}(\mathrm{g})\leq\delta^{-O(J_{0}+J_{1}+J_{2})}|a|^{-J_{0}}|c|^{-J_{1}}|d|^{-J_{2}}\frac{\mathbf{1}\{u(\mathrm{g})\leq Y\}}{\sqrt{1+u(\mathrm{g})}}.

Proof.

7.1.
Unskewing

We first reduce the proof to the case
R_{1}=R_{2}=1
. Denoting

\displaystyle F_{1}(\mathrm{g}):=F(\mathrm{a}[R_{1}]\mathrm{g}\mathrm{a}[R_{2}]^{-1})

and defining
\alpha_{j}^{\prime}
via (recall (
2.7

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle_{\alpha_{j}^{\prime}}=\langle r_{\mathrm{a}[R_{j}]}f\rangle_{\alpha_{j}},

we have

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{1}|\mathcal{T}_{h_{1},h_{2}}\Delta F|\alpha_{2}\rangle=\langle\alpha_{1}^{\prime}|\mathcal{T}_{h_{1},h_{2}}\Delta F_{1}|\alpha_{2}^{\prime}\rangle,\quad\langle\alpha_{i}|\Delta k_{X_{i}^{2},R_{i}}|\alpha_{i}\rangle=\langle\alpha_{i}^{\prime}|\Delta k_{X_{i}^{2},1}|\alpha_{i}^{\prime}\rangle.

Furthermore,
F_{1}\smallmatrixquantity(\lx@physics@smallmatrix a & b \\ c& d\endlx@physics@smallmatrix))=f_{1}(a,c,d)
for some
f_{1}\in C^{J}_{\delta}(\sqrt{AD},\sqrt{AD},\sqrt{AD})
. Thus, it suffices to prove the theorem for
R_{1}=R_{2}=1
, that is
A=D=C
.

7.2.
Spectral expansion

By the assumption
A=D=C
, we see that
F(\mathrm{g})
satisfies (
5.1
) with
\delta_{1}=\delta^{2}
. Indeed, the derivatives
\partial_{\varphi},\partial_{\vartheta}
can be bounded using (
2.13
) since
r_{1},r_{2}\asymp 1
, and the differentiation by
\Omega
can be bounded using the Iwasawa coordinates (
2.11
) by similar fashion as in
[
8
, Section 7.3]
to make the change of variables.

By Proposition
3.1
and applying the Hecke operators
\mathcal{T}_{h_{1},h_{2}}
via (
2.23
) and (
2.24
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(h_{1}h_{2},q)=1\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\beta_{1}(h_{1})\beta_{2}(h_{2})\langle\alpha_{1}|\mathcal{T}_{h_{1},h_{2}}\Delta F|\alpha_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\\
\ell_{1}\equiv\ell_{2}\equiv\kappa\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{2})\end{subarray}}\sum_{V\in\mathcal{B}(\Gamma,\chi)}\langle F,\mathcal{P}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}_{V}\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{H}(\lambda_{V})\overline{\langle\varphi_{V}^{(\ell_{1})}\rangle_{\alpha_{1}}}\langle\varphi_{V}^{(\ell_{2})}\rangle_{\alpha_{2}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\\
\ell_{1}\equiv\ell_{2}\equiv\kappa\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{2})\end{subarray}}\sum_{\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}(\Gamma,\chi)}\frac{1}{4\pi i}\int_{(0)}\langle F,\mathcal{P}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}_{\nu}\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{H}(\lambda_{\nu,\mathfrak{c}})\overline{\langle E^{(\ell_{1})}_{\mathfrak{c},\nu}\rangle_{\alpha_{1}}}\langle E^{(\ell_{2})}_{\mathfrak{c},\nu}\rangle_{\alpha_{2}}\mathrm{d}\nu.

By Proposition
5.1

\displaystyle\langle F,\mathcal{P}^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}_{\nu}\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}\ll(1+AD)^{1/2+\widetilde{\nu}}\frac{\log(1+AD)(1+\ell_{1})(1+\ell_{2})}{(1+(\delta_{1}|\nu|)^{2}+(\delta_{1}\ell_{2})^{2}+(\delta_{1}\ell_{1})^{2})^{J^{\prime}}},

where
\widetilde{\nu}=\nu
if

and
\widetilde{\nu}=0
otherwise. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{1},h_{2}\\
(h_{1}h_{2},q)=1\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\beta_{1}(h_{1})\beta_{2}(h_{2})\langle\alpha_{1}|\mathcal{T}_{h_{1},h_{2}}\Delta F|\alpha_{2}\rangle\ll(1+AD)^{1/2+o(1)}\mathcal{H}X_{0}^{\theta}(\mathcal{C}(X_{1}X_{2})+\mathcal{E})

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(X_{1}X_{2})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\\
\ell_{1}\equiv\ell_{2}\equiv\kappa\,\,(\mathrm{mod}\,{2})\end{subarray}}\sum_{V\in\mathcal{B}(\Gamma,\chi)}\frac{(1+|\ell_{1}|)(1+|\ell_{2}|)}{(1+(\delta_{1}|\nu_{V}|)^{2}+(\delta_{1}\ell_{2})^{2}+(\delta_{1}\ell_{1})^{2})^{J^{\prime}}}\bigl|X_{1}^{\widetilde{\nu}_{V}}\langle\varphi_{V}^{(\ell_{1})}\rangle_{\alpha_{1}}\bigr|\,\bigl|X_{2}^{\widetilde{\nu}_{V}}\langle\varphi_{V}^{(\ell_{2})}\rangle_{\alpha_{2}}\bigr|

7.3.
Applying Cauchy-Schwarz

By applying Cauchy-Schwarz we get

8.
