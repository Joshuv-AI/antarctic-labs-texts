Trigeminocerebellar artery as a subsequent offending vessel for recurrent trigeminal neuralgia after initial microvascular decompression: illustrative case
Authors: Shunsuke Hatakenaka, Keisuke Onoda, Ryosuke Doi, Junpei Kato, Takashi Iimori, Tomihiro Wakamiya, Masahiro Indo, Tatsuya Tanaka, Takashi Agari, Kimihiro Nakahara, Takashi Sugawara, Kazuaki Shimoji, Eiichi Suehiro, Hiroshi Itokawa, Akira Matsuno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDThe most common cause of trigeminal neuralgia (TN) is vascular compression at the root entry zone of the trigeminal nerve, most frequently by the superior cerebellar artery (SCA). Trigeminocerebellar artery (TCA) involvement in TN is rare, and its management with microvascular decompression (MVD) has not been elucidated.OBSERVATIONSThe authors report the case of a 64-year-old woman with TN caused by compression from the SCAs who underwent successful MVD with complete symptom resolution. The TCA had been identified intraoperatively; however, it was not considered the offending vessel and was not fully transposed owing to the presence of small perforating branches. Three years later, TN recurred, predominantly in the left V3 distribution. MRI showed that the SCAs were not compressing the trigeminal nerve. Rather, the TCA had adhered to and was compressing the nerve. Revision MVD revealed strong adhesion of the TCA between the sensory and motor roots of the trigeminal nerve. After splitting the roots and transposing the TCA, including sacrificing a small perforating branch, complete symptom relief was achieved without neurological deficits.LESSONSTransposition should be considered when the TCA is identified during MVD for TN, even if it is not the initial apparent offending vessel. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26172

Trigeminal neuralgia (TN) is primarily caused by compression of the root entry zone of the trigeminal nerve. Microvascular decompression (MVD) was developed by Jannetta et al. in the 1960s and is the gold-standard surgical technique for TN with high efficacy.1,2

The superior cerebellar artery (SCA) is the most common offending (causative) vessel in TN, accounting for approximately 74.3% of reported cases. Other offending vessels include the anterior inferior cerebellar artery and vein.3

TN involving the trigeminocerebellar artery (TCA) as the offending vessel is rare. The TCA is a unique branch of the basilar artery supplying both the trigeminal nerve root and the cerebellar hemisphere. It is anatomically divided into the pontine, trigeminal, cerebellopontine, and cerebellar segments and is thought to represent a vascular variant related to the primitive trigeminal artery.4

Marinković and Gibo4 identified the TCA in 1 of 22 cadaver dissections (4.5%), while Tuccar et al.5 identified the TCA in 12 of 90 sides (13.3%), indicating that its anatomical incidence is not uncommon. Regarding TN-related cases, Ichimasu et al.6 reported TCA involvement in 13 of 146 TN cases (8.9%) in which MVD was conducted, and Goto and Inoue.7 identified the TCA in 15 of 460 cases (3.3%). These findings suggest that the frequency of the TCA being identified is approximately 4%–13%; its involvement as an offending vessel in TN is estimated at 3%–9%.

Herein, we report a rare case wherein, in the initial surgery for TN, the SCA was identified as the offending vessel. However, the TCA was clearly identified as the offending vessel in the subsequent recurrence of TN.

Illustrative Case

A 64-year-old woman visited our department for the management of TN in January 2023, with symptoms involving the left V2/V3 (maxillary/mandibular nerve) distribution. The MVD procedure was performed with the patient in the right lateral decubitus position using the standard retrosigmoid approach. The intraoperative auditory brainstem response (ABR) was continuously monitored. Intraoperative findings revealed two SCAs compressing the trigeminal nerve from the dorsal cranial side; the SCAs were identified as the offending vessels and were transposed toward the tentorium (Fig. 1A and B). Intraoperatively, the TCA was observed to run between the sensory and motor roots of the trigeminal nerve. Although attempted, complete transposition of the TCA was not performed because the artery exhibited the distribution of small perforating branches to the trigeminal nerve. Therefore, only minor adjustments of the contact site were made (Fig. 1C). Postoperatively, the patient had complete resolution of the TN.

The patient experienced TN recurrence in September 2025, predominantly in the left V3 distribution. MRI indicated that the SCAs, the previously identified offending vessels, were no longer in contact with the trigeminal nerve, whereas the TCA was running adjacent to the nerve (Fig. 2). Therefore, revision MVD was planned. Intraoperatively, the previously transposed SCAs were confirmed to no longer be in contact with the trigeminal nerve; however, the TCA running from Meckel’s cave to the root entry zone was strongly adherent to and compressing the trigeminal nerve (Fig. 3A). The trigeminal nerve showed no obvious adhesions to the surrounding structures, and no deformities were observed. The adhered TCA was detached from the trigeminal nerve. The sensory and motor roots of the trigeminal nerve were separated (Fig. 3B), and the TCA was transposed toward the motor root (Fig. 3C and E). The small arterial branch to the trigeminal nerve identified during the initial surgery was coagulated and cut (Fig. 3D).8 The postoperative course of the patient was uneventful. No neurological deficits were noted, and the TN resolved completely. Postoperative MRI confirmed successful separation of the TCA from the trigeminal nerve (Fig. 4). Discussion

This rare case is notable in that during the initial MVD surgery, the SCAs were identified as the offending vessels for TN, whereas the TCA, although in contact with the trigeminal nerve, was not causing clear nerve compression. Complete symptom relief was achieved after the SCA transposition. However, 3 years later, the TCA had become firmly adherent to the trigeminal nerve, causing recurrent TN. During the revision surgery, the TCA was observed to run between the sensory and motor roots and showed strong adhesions. Symptom resolution was achieved after trigeminal nerve root separation and TCA transposition. TN can result from adhesions or deformities; however, in the present case, the adhesions around the nerve were not significant and no obvious deformities were observed. We suspect that this condition arose as a result of the blood pulsation of the TCA vessel that was transmitted directly to the nerves as a result of its significant adhesions to the nerves.

To our knowledge, no previous report has described a case in which a TCA that was not the offending vessel at the initial MVD became the causative vessel of TN recurrence, making this rare case important and clinically informative.

The developmental origin of the TCA is thought to be related to embryological vascular development. Willinsky et al.9 suggested that the TCA may represent a caudal remnant of the primitive trigeminal artery resulting from incomplete regression, which may explain its close anatomical relationship with the trigeminal nerve. The anatomical relationship between the TCA and the trigeminal nerve root is variable. The trigeminal segment may form loops, encircle or contact the nerve root, and course very close to the root entry zone; in some cases, particularly in the intraneural type, it may even travel between the sensory and motor roots. Such anatomical variations may contribute to TN and increase the technical complexity of MVD. In cases in which the TCA passes between the motor and sensory roots, Jito and Nozaki described longitudinal dissection of the trigeminal nerve to create space between the vessel and the nerve roots, followed by arterial transposition, as an effective technique for adequate decompression.10 For looped or encircling configurations, Goto and Inoue reported loosening and mobilization of the arterial loop as the key decompressive maneuver.7 The TCA frequently gives rise to perforating branches to the trigeminal nerve root, the lateral pons, and the middle cerebellar peduncle; in some cases, sacrifice of these perforators is necessary to achieve adequate decompression. Marinković and Gibo reported that occlusion or injury of these arteries may theoretically cause a lateral midpontine syndrome-like presentation, including facial sensory disturbance, masticatory weakness, and cerebellar ataxia.4 Marinković and Gibo4 and Tuccar et al.5 reported that the TCA gives rise to several branches contributing to the blood supply of the trigeminal nerve root, suggesting the presence of collateral circulation. This may explain why sacrifice of one or two tiny perforators does not necessarily result in neurological dysfunction, although such branches should be preserved whenever possible. Therefore, MVD involving the TCA may be more technically challenging than standard MVD.

In the present case, the TCA penetrated between the sensory and motor roots of the trigeminal nerve. The TCA was not considered as the offending vessel at the initial surgery; however, postoperative adhesions can occur after MVD.11 Considering its anatomical proximity to the trigeminal nerve, the TCA may become an offending vessel postoperatively, as observed in the present case.

When a TCA is identified during MVD for TN, transposition should be considered, even if it is not the apparent offending vessel.

Observations

This case demonstrates that TN can recur after MVD when the TCA has been identified intraoperatively. To our knowledge, no previous cases have been reported in which the TCA, identified during the initial MVD but excluded as the causative vessel, was the offending vessel in TN recurrence after MVD.

Lessons

If the TCA has been identified during MVD for TN, albeit not as the causative vessel, surgeons should consider transposition at that time to prevent TN recurrence. This article is limited in that only a single case was reported. More reports of such cases are required to fully elucidate this rare occurrence.
