A Quantum Programming Language for Coherent Control
Kathleen Barsse, Romain Péchoux, Simon Perdrix

Abstract
. We introduce a programming language that allows for the coherent control of arbitrary quantum operations. The problem of defining coherent control beyond the unitary case, using, for example, a quantum conditional in the presence of recursion or iteration has long been known to be a major difficulty. We resolve this problem by defining an operational semantics based on appropriate Kraus decompositions and a denotational semantics based on vacuum-extensions. We show that the language is universal for vacuum-extensions and that the two semantics are adequate. Moreover, we define a notion of observational equivalence: two programs are equivalent if their probability of termination is the same in any context. The denotational semantics is shown to be fully abstract for observational equivalence.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Context and Motivations

In standard models of quantum computation, quantum states are given by superpositions of classical states, and quantum processes describe the evolution of such states. This is the “quantum data, classical control” paradigm, which is known to enable a speedup in comparison to classical algorithms, e.g.,
Shor 1997
;
Grover 1996
. One step further, one can also consider the superposition of quantum processes themselves. This notion is refered to as
coherent control
or
quantum control
. A fundamental example of coherent control is the
quantum case
process, sometimes called
half quantum switch
or
quantum conditional
. Given unitary gates
U
and
V
, the quantum case acts on a pair of control and target qubits as follows: if the control qubit is in state
\lvert 0\rangle
,
U
is applied to the target qubit, if the control qubit is in state
\lvert 1\rangle
,
V
is applied to the target qubit, otherwise
U
and
V
are applied in a coherent superposition. This process can be thought of as a quantum version of the classical “
if
” statement where the two branches can run in a superposition.

In comparison to quantum processes with fixed or classically ordered gates, models with coherent control are known to provide some advantages in computational complexity
Colnaghi et al. 2012
;
Facchini & Perdrix 2015
;
Araújo et al. 2014
;
Kristjánsson et al. 2024
and communication tasks
Ebler et al. 2018
;
Abbott et al. 2020
;
Kristjánsson et al. 2020
. For this reason, the possibility of including coherent control in quantum programming languages is attracting a great deal of interest (see, for instance, the review article
Valiron 2022
).

The integration of quantum control into a programming language is inherently challenging.

First, the definition of the quantum case itself poses some difficulties, in particular when we go beyond the unitary case.
Typically, the most general evolutions of quantum states are modeled as
quantum channels
, i.e. completely positive trace preserving linear maps, which include standard unitary evolutions as well as probabilistic measurements.
While the quantum case process is well defined on unitary maps—namely, as
(U,V)\mapsto\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert\otimes U+\lvert 1\rangle\langle 1\rvert\otimes V
, its generalization to quantum channels is ill-defined, as shown in
Bădescu & Panangaden 2015
. Furthermore, the use of coherently controlled operations such as the quantum switch
Chiribella et al. 2013
;
Abbott et al. 2020
was shown to enable an exponential speedup in comparison to models with classically ordered gates; and importantly, unitary gates alone are insufficient to demonstrate this advantage
Kristjánsson et al. 2024
. Therefore, we would ideally like a programming language to be able to express the quantum control of arbitrary quantum evolutions.

The second main challenge concerns the combination of quantum control and recursion. The quantum case operation was shown to be incompatible with the Löwner ordering
Bădescu & Panangaden 2015
. The development of a programming language that can combine a quantum case with classical recursion (or iteration) has been an open problem for more than 10 years, and it is essential to solve it to enable the development of high-level quantum programming languages in all their generality.

1.2.
Contributions

In this paper, we solve the above longstanding open issues by providing a semantically well-behaved programming language which allows for both classical and quantum control of arbitrary processes.
The quantum control is formulated as a primitive called “
qcase
”, which we present as a quantum version of the classical conditional statement. For the sake of simplicity, the language exclusively uses quantum data, with no need for additional classical registers. The novelty of our programming language is the possibility for both coherent control of arbitrary quantum processes, which are not restricted to unitary maps, and iteration via a classically controlled while loop.

In order to assign a formal meaning to programs, we construct an operational semantics, consisting in a probabilistic big-step transition system, and a denotational semantics. Despite the apparent issues surrounding the definition of quantum case
Bădescu & Panangaden 2015
, this operation is physically meaningful. In order to unambiguously define the action of the quantum case on general channels, it is necessary to specify additional information on the implementation of each input channel
Kristjánsson et al. 2020
;
Abbott et al. 2020
.

In the operational semantics, this additional information takes the form of an extra bit (Definition
3.2
and Figure
4
) associated with each possible evolution. This choice is based on Kraus decompositions as discussed in
Bădescu & Panangaden 2015
;
Abbott et al. 2020
, and allows us to define a default transition for each probabilistic statement. In the denotational semantics, the additional information is expressed using the framework of
vacuum-extended operations
(Definition
4.5
and Figure in
5
), as proposed in
Kristjánsson et al. 2020
;
Chiribella & Kristjánsson 2019
. This enables us to define the semantics of the quantum case semantics in a physically meaningful way, while ensuring the usual expected properties such as compositionality.

We prove the following main properties of the language:

•

Universality (Theorem
5.3
): the language is universal for vacuum-extended operations. That is, every vacuum-extended operation can be realized as the interpretation of some program. Moreover, universality still holds in the absence of the measurement primitive (Example
3.9
), and approximate universality is shown when the set of built-in unitary gates is restricted to the Hadamard gate
H
and the
T
-gate (Proposition
5.5
).

•

Adequacy (Theorem
5.6
): the independently-defined operational and denotational semantics are adequate, meaning that they describe the same behavior of programs. Consequently, this links the Kraus decomposition based approach of the operational semantics to the vacuum-extension approach of the denotational semantics.

•

Full abstraction (Theorem
5.13
): we introduce a notion of observational equivalence (Definition
5.12
), which states that two programs are observationally equivalent if their probability of termination is the same whatever the context. This formalizes the idea that the observable behavior of two programs is indistinguishable. We show that the denotational semantics is fully abstract for observational equivalence. Hence, the denotational semantics exactly captures the observable behavior of programs.

All these concepts are illustrated by numerous examples scattered throughout the paper, giving an idea of how expressive the language can be.

1.3.
Overview

We now give a brief overview of our language (Figure

S_{0}
and

\text{{{{qcase}}}}\ \mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow S_{0},1\rightarrow S_{1})

is the statement that executes

and

in a coherent superposition, depending on the state of

\text{{{{meas}}}}\ \mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow S_{0},1\rightarrow S_{1})

\mathtt{q}

is executed, and if the outcome is

S_{1}
is executed.
For both

and

or

is required to be reversible. One of the difficulties of languages with quantum control is defining iteration. We include a

\text{{{{while}}}}\ \mathtt{q}\ \text{{{{do}}}}\ S

Since quantum control is known not to preserve the Löwner ordering

, a coherently controlled while loop would be ill-defined. Thus,

is a measurement-based loop: with each iteration, the control qubit q is measured in the computational basis, and the loop is exited once output 0 is obtained. Integrating loops into a quantum programming language poses problems that are not encountered on lower-level models such as quantum circuits. For example, measurement cannot be postponed to the end of the computation as standardly done.

The action on superpositions is obtained by linearity. Therefore, it is necessary to define the action of programs both on regular inputs and on the empty input. Given that the transition system is probabilistic, it suffices to assign to each program a default transition: when the input is empty, this is the transition that occurs. Accordingly, transitions will have the form

Then, the denotational semantics (Figure

;
Kristjánsson et al. 2020

(\mathcal{C},F)
, where
\mathcal{C}
is a quantum operation (i.e., a completely positive trace non-increasing linear map), and
F
is a linear map called a
transformation matrix
. Although it is not possible to define the quantum case operation on pairs of quantum operations (at least not in a way that is consistent with the definition on unitary maps)
Bădescu & Panangaden 2015
, the quantum case is well-defined on quantum operation and transformation matrix pairs. This can be explained as follows: the quantum operations alone do not contain enough information to adequately define the quantum case, but the transformation matrix specifies all the necessary information about the implementation of the channel
Abbott et al. 2020
;
Kristjánsson et al. 2020
. Therefore, each program is interpreted as a pair
(\mathcal{C},F)
.

The adequacy result (Theorem
5.6
) states that the two semantics describe the same behavior of programs. Therefore, we establish a link between the addition of default transitions in the operational semantics, and the addition of transformation matrices in the denotational semantics. The full abstraction result (Theorem
5.13
) takes this correspondence further by showing that the denotational semantics matches the notion of observational equivalence.
As an example of observational equivalence, we will show that for all statements
S_{0},S_{0}^{\prime},S_{1},S_{1}^{\prime}
, the programs
\text{{{{qcase }}}}\mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow S_{0},\ 1\rightarrow S_{1});\ \text{{{{qcase }}}}\mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow S_{0}^{\prime},\ 1\rightarrow S_{1}^{\prime})
and
\text{{{{qcase }}}}\mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow S_{0};S_{0}^{\prime},\ 1\rightarrow S_{1};S_{1}^{\prime})
are observationally equivalent, which is essentially an interchange law for the quantum case. As a counterexample, we will show that
\text{{{{qcase}}}}\ \mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow S,1\rightarrow S)
is generally not equivalent to
S
. While it may seem surprising that a program in superposition with itself acts differently than the original program, this phenomenon stems from the probabilistic behavior of quantum operations. Indeed, if the statement
S
has, say, two possible outcomes, then
\text{{{{qcase}}}}\ \mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow S,1\rightarrow S)
allows for superpositions of both outcomes, one arising from each branch, which would not be possible with a single copy of
S
.

1.4.
Related Work

There are several existing programming languages that include constructs for coherent control, which we summarize here. The paper
Altenkirch & Grattage 2005
develops the first such language, where coherent control is expressed via an “
\mathtt{if}^{\circ}
” statement that does not measure the control qubit. In
Sabry et al. 2018
;
Chardonnet et al. 2021
, quantum control is expressed in form of a pattern-matching construct. All three of these languages feature orthogonality tests to ensure that programs are meaningful. In
Voichick et al. 2023
, quantum features are presented as a generalization of classical programming concepts, including quantum control. In
Dave et al. 2025
, the authors show how to combine classical and quantum control within a same framework. The paper
Hainry et al. 2023
presents a language mixing iteration and quantum case with the aim of characterizing quantum polynomial time. The paper
Arrighi et al. 2023
integrates quantum control by expressing the wiring between quantum gates as superpositions. In
Ying et al. 2024
, the authors include both the quantum control of unitary gates and iteration. In another line of research, approaches based on the
\lambda
-calculus
Arrighi & Dowek 2017
;
Díaz-Caro et al. 2019
;
Díaz-Caro & Malherbe 2022
implement quantum control using superpositions of
\lambda
-terms. In these examples, quantum control exclusively concerns reversible operations. A language with control of irreversible operations was first introduced
Bădescu & Panangaden 2015
, where the semantics of terms is based on Kraus decompositions. However, the language does not include primitives for iteration or recursion.

From another perspective, the coherent control of arbitrary operations has been studied in the physics literature
Vanrietvelde & Chiribella 2021
;
Dong et al. 2019
;
Abbott et al. 2020
;
Kristjánsson et al. 2020
. It is useful to distinguish two types of quantum control, as was done in
Kristjánsson et al. 2020
. The first kind is the control of choice of quantum channels—where several alternative channels run in a superposition. This is the kind of control that is studied in this paper. The second is the control of causal orders, where instead of controlling which operation to apply, we choose the order in which a set of operations are applied. A well-known example of control of orders is the
quantum switch

Chiribella et al. 2013
, where two operations are performed in a superposition of orders. The former kind of control was shown to demonstrate an advantage in a communication task involving superpositions of depolarizing channels
Abbott et al. 2020
.

There exist several frameworks addressing the ambiguity in the definition of the quantum case. In order to meaningfully define the action of the quantum case, it is necessary to specify additional information on the implementation of each input channel. One way to achieve this is to describe quantum channels via their Kraus
decomposition, optionally specifying input environment states
Bădescu & Panangaden 2015
;
Abbott et al. 2020
. Moreover, this allows one to make finer between operations than would be possible in the standard quantum channel framework
Abbott et al. 2020
;
Branciard et al. 2021
. A second option is the notion of vacuum-extended channels
Kristjánsson et al. 2020
;
Chiribella & Kristjánsson 2019
. Here, the domain of quantum channels is extended to act on an extra “vacuum” state, which symbolises the empty input.

This paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we present the syntax and well-formedness rules of our language. In Sections
3
and
4
, we describe the operational and denotational semantics of the language, respectively. Then we present our main results in Section
5
: universality of the language for vacuum-extended operations (Section
5.1
), adequacy between the operational and denotational semantics (Section
5.2
), and full abstraction (Section
5.3
). The detailed proofs of these results are given in the appendices. Lastly, we give our summary and conclusion in Section
6
.

We now present a big-step operational semantics for the language. Given a program

the main originality of our operational semantics, namely the introduction of an additional bit in the transitions to capture the behavior of the

3.1.
Quantum States

We present some notions from quantum computing and introduce the necessary notations.
We will use standard notations from linear algebra: for all pair of Hilbert spaces

A quantum system is represented by some Hilbert space

and
\{k+1,\ldots,n\}
.
For
\lvert\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{\Gamma}
and
\lvert\phi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{\Delta}
, define:

\lvert\psi\rangle_{\Gamma}\otimes\lvert\phi\rangle_{\Delta}\triangleq\text{PERM}_{\pi_{\Gamma,\Delta}}(\lvert\psi\rangle\otimes\lvert\phi\rangle)

Intuitively, this notation indicates that the variables of
\Gamma
and
\Delta
are put into the correct order according to
\preceq
. As a particular case, the expression
\lvert\psi\rangle_{\mathtt{q}}\otimes\lvert\phi\rangle_{\Gamma}
inserts the qubit
\mathtt{q}
into the correct position. Using these notations, the choice of ordering
\preceq
will remain implicit in the definition of the operational and denotational semantics.

3.2.
Towards a Semantics for the Quantum Case

The operation semantics describes the evolution of a quantum state
\lvert\psi\rangle
induced by a program. The action of standard statements such as unitary gate applications and measurements will be defined in the usual way. The main challenge is the definition a transition rule for the
qcase
statement. In this section, we present some features of the operational semantics that guide our definition of the
qcase
rule.

3.2.1.
Big-Step Semantics

We choose to define a big-step operational semantics, which simplifies the definition of the
qcase
rule. As an example, consider the following statement:

\text{{{{qcase}}}}\ \mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow\text{{{{new qbit }}}}\mathtt{r};\mathtt{r}*=H;\text{{{{discard}}}}\ \mathtt{r},1\rightarrow\text{{{{skip}}}})

The 0 branch temporarily contains an extra qubit variable
\mathtt{r}
, while the 1 branch does nothing. Then, in a small-step scenario, the intermediate states of the two branches are not necessarily defined over the same set of variables. This would result either in technical complications due to the superposition of systems with differing dimensions, or in unnecessary restrictions on well-formed statements, aimed at avoiding distinct variable sets across the branches of a
qcase
.
For this reason, big-step semantics appear to be better-suited to describing the action of
qcase
.

3.2.2.
A Default Transition for Unexecuted Programs

The main originality of the operational semantics is the introduction of an extra bit of information in the computed values.
Roughly speaking, this bit is used to select a single execution trace from among all possible branches of the transition system, which acts as the default transition when no input is provided to the program. This default transition is useful for defining the semantics of a
qcase
.
Indeed, consider the execution of a statement

\text{{{{qcase}}}}\ \mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow S_{0},1\rightarrow S_{1})

on input state
(\alpha\lvert 0\rangle+\beta\lvert 1\rangle)_{\mathtt{q}}\otimes\lvert\psi\rangle
. Intuitively the input
\lvert\psi\rangle
is passed to
S_{0}
with amplitude
\alpha
and to
S_{1}
with amplitude
\beta
. Notice that when
\beta=0
no input is actually passed to
S_{1}
; and similarly no input is passed to
S_{0}
when
\alpha=0
. Hence, the semantics of the
qcase
somehow relies on the behavior of the statements
S_{i}
when no inputs are passed to them.
We thus develop an operational semantics that defines the behavior of a program not only on actual inputs, but also when no inputs are provided. In the latter case, it yields a
default
transition of the program, characterised by the extra bit
\nu
:
\nu
is
1
on the default transition and
0
on the other ones.

In Rule (U), for

The three nondeterministic statements — namely

\nu=1
. The transition system can also be viewed as a probabilistic one in a straightforward way. We use a fairly standard encoding of the probabilistic behavior of quantum computing, where the probability of each transition is encoded in the norms of its quantum states: if
[S,\lvert\psi\rangle]_{\Gamma}\rightarrow(\lvert\psi^{\prime}\rangle,\nu)
, then the statement
S
transforms the state
\frac{\lvert\psi\rangle}{\|\lvert\psi\rangle\|}
into the state
\frac{\lvert\psi^{\prime}\rangle}{\|\lvert\psi\rangle^{\prime}\|}
with probability
\frac{\|\lvert\psi^{\prime}\rangle\|^{2}}{\|\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}}
. This probability is independent of
\nu
.

We now give a brief intuition for each rule of Figure

\lvert 0\rangle

\mathtt{q}

Finally, Rule (Q) for

The output state of the

\nu_{1}\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathtt{q}}\otimes\lvert\psi_{0}\rangle_{\Gamma^{\prime}}+\nu_{0}\lvert 1\rangle_{\mathtt{q}}\otimes\lvert\psi_{1}\rangle_{\Gamma^{\prime}}

We define the probability of termination of programs, which follows from the encoding of transition probabilities. We consider all values attainable from a fixed configuration
[S,\lvert\psi\rangle]_{\Gamma}
. These values will formally be described as a multiset, which is a similar concept to sets where elements may appear multiple times, which account for multiple derivations leading to the same value.

Definition 3.3 (Output ensemble).

The
output ensemble

\mathscr{E}(c)
of a configuration
c\in\text{{{{Conf}}}}
, is defined as the multiset whose support is

\left\{v\in\text{{{{Values}}}}\setminus\{(0,0)\}\ \middle|\ c\rightarrow v\right\}

and where the multiplicity of each value
v
is defined as the number of distinct derivations of target
v
.

The value
(0,0)
is removed as it does not necessarily have finite multiplicity, as required by the definition of a multiset, and does not provide any information on the behavior of the program.

Definition 3.4 (Probability of termination).

Given a program
(\Gamma;S;\Delta)
and a quantum state
\lvert\psi\rangle\neq 0

such that
\mathscr{E}([S,\lvert\psi\rangle]_{\Gamma})=\{(\lvert\psi^{\prime}_{i}\rangle,\nu_{i})\}_{i}
, the
probability of termination
of
S
on input
\lvert\psi\rangle
is defined as

p(S,\lvert\psi\rangle)_{\Gamma}\triangleq\sum_{i}\frac{\|\lvert\psi^{\prime}_{i}\rangle\|^{2}}{\|\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}}.

A justification of the well-definedness of transition probabilities, as well as a more precise definition of multiset, are given in Appendix
A.2
. From now on, we will omit the subscript
\Gamma
in transitions
[S,\lvert\psi\rangle]_{\Gamma}\rightarrow(\lvert\psi^{\prime}\rangle,\nu)
and probabilities
p(S,\lvert\psi\rangle)_{\Gamma}
when it is clear from the context.

3.4.
Choice of the Default Transition

The introduction of a default transition is inspired by a series of works in quantum foundations
Oi 2003
;
Åberg 2004
;
Dong et al. 2019
;
Vanrietvelde & Chiribella 2021
;
Abbott et al. 2020
on quantum control of general quantum transformations, represented as sets of possible evolutions—commonly referred to as Kraus operators. While the sole information of the set of Kraus operators is not sufficient to define its controlled version, various extensions have been introduced to this end. In particular,
pinned Kraus operators
, introduced in
Vanrietvelde & Chiribella 2021
, consist in pinpointing a specific element of the set of Kraus operators to enable quantum control. Similarly we identify, in the operational semantics, a default transition that allows the definition of the semantics of the
qcase
.

In
Dong et al. 2019
, the authors show that quantum control can be done when an additional complex number
\nu_{i}
is associated to each possible evolution (i.e., each Kraus operator),
such that
\sum_{i}|\nu_{i}|^{2}=1
. In our setting, we consider a particular case where these amplitudes are restricted to
\{0,1\}
, which amounts to selecting a default evolution—the only one for which the amplitude is
1
.

The use of bits for the amplitudes is a deliberate design choice for our language. It ensures deterministic transitions when no inputs are provided to the programs. Note that one could have opted for alternative default transitions—for example, by swapping the roles of (D
0
) and (D
1
). More generally, other admissible choices exist that may not yield determinism but still preserve essential properties of the language, such as universality and adequacy (see Sections
5.1
and
5.2
). However, we show in Lemma
A.3
that in the presence of iteration, some choices should be avoided; in particular, selecting (W
1
) as the default transition would result in non-termination.
Our specific choice of coefficients is further discussed in Appendix
B
.

In quantum foundations, other approaches to the quantum control of general quantum evolutions have been considered, in particular by extending the domain and co-domain with an extra state
\lvert\text{vac}\rangle
representing the empty input
Chiribella & Kristjánsson 2019
;
Kristjánsson et al. 2020
, i.e., when no inputs are given to the program. We follow this alternative interpretation for the denotational semantics of the language (See Section
4
) and show the adequacy of the two semantics, underlying the equivalence of these various approaches to account for quantum control on general quantum evolutions, even in the presence of recursion.

Remark 3.5.

Notice that one could also consider a generalization of the language where the choice of
\nu_{i}
is left to the programmer, e.g.,
\text{{{{meas }}}}\mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow^{\nu_{0}}S_{0},1\rightarrow^{\nu_{1}}S_{1})
for arbitrary
\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
as long as
|\nu_{0}|^{2}+|\nu_{1}|^{2}=1
. This would not increase the expressive power of the language (see Section
5.1
). Consequently, we opt for the simpler syntax that leaves these parameters implicit.

3.5.
Examples

Example 3.6 (CNOT and SWAP).

The CNOT and SWAP are the 2-qubit unitary gates defined as follows on computational basis states

\text{CNOT}:\lvert x,y\rangle\mapsto\lvert x,y\oplus x\rangle\qquad\qquad\text{SWAP}:\lvert x,y\rangle\mapsto\lvert y,x\rangle,

with
\oplus
being the addition modulo 2. The CNOT acts by flipping the second qubit, called the target qubit, only when the first qubit, called the control qubit, is in state
\lvert 1\rangle
. The SWAP exchanges the states of the two qubits. Although the language only has single-qubit unitary gates built-in, the CNOT and SWAP gates can be implemented using the
qcase
primitive. For the CNOT, consider the following statement:

\displaystyle S_{\text{CNOT}}^{\mathtt{c},\mathtt{t}}\ \triangleq\ \text{{{{qcase}}}}\ \mathtt{c}\ (0\rightarrow\text{{{{skip}}}},1\rightarrow\mathtt{t}\ *=X)

where

is the Pauli matrix defined on computational basis states as
X:\lvert x\rangle\mapsto\lvert 1-x\rangle
,
\mathtt{c}
represents the control qubit and
\mathtt{t}
represents the target qubit. Hence, one can derive the judgment

\mathtt{c},\mathtt{t}\vdash S_{\text{CNOT}}^{\mathtt{c},\mathtt{t}}\triangleright\mathtt{c},\mathtt{t}
.

For all
\alpha\lvert 00\rangle+\beta\lvert 01\rangle+\gamma\lvert 10\rangle+\delta\lvert 11\rangle\in St(\mathcal{H}_{\mathtt{c},\mathtt{t}})
, assuming
\mathtt{c}\preceq\mathtt{t}
(i.e.,
\mathtt{c}
is the first qubit), we have:

(Q)

[\text{{{{skip}}}},\alpha\lvert 0\rangle+\beta\lvert 1\rangle]\rightarrow(\alpha\lvert 0\rangle+\beta\lvert 1\rangle,1)
[\mathtt{t}\ *=X,\gamma\lvert 0\rangle+\delta\lvert 1\rangle]\rightarrow(\gamma\lvert 1\rangle+\delta\lvert 0\rangle,1)
[S_{\text{CNOT}}^{\mathtt{c},\mathtt{t}},\alpha\lvert 00\rangle+\beta\lvert 01\rangle+\gamma\lvert 10\rangle+\delta\lvert 11\rangle]\rightarrow(\alpha\lvert 00\rangle+\beta\lvert 01\rangle+\gamma\lvert 11\rangle+\delta\lvert 10\rangle,1)

which matches the action of the CNOT gate. Then, since that SWAP gate can be decomposed into a sequence of three CNOT gates, we obtain the corresponding statement:

S_{\text{SWAP}}\ \triangleq\ S_{\text{CNOT}}^{\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q}}\ ;\ S_{\text{CNOT}}^{\mathtt{q},\mathtt{p}}\ ;\ S_{\text{CNOT}}^{\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q}}.

4.
Denotational semantics

We now give the denotational semantics for our language. The aim is to assign to each program some mathematical operation that fully describes its behavior. Standard denotational semantics for quantum programming languages are based on
quantum operations
(i.e., completely positive trace non-increasing maps), which are known to be insufficient to describe the coherent control of arbitrary operations
Bădescu & Panangaden 2015
. Therefore, we take a distinct approach based on vacuum-extended channels
Chiribella & Kristjánsson 2019
;
Kristjánsson et al. 2020
.

We begin with an overview of the standard notions of quantum channels and quantum operations. Then, using this framework we define the notion of vacuum-extensions, in turn allowing us to define the
\mathtt{QCASE}
operation. From here, we will give the denotational semantics of programs.

4.1.
Quantum Operations and Quantum Channels

With the aim to define the denotational semantics, we present the notions of quantum channels and quantum operations, which are used to describe the evolution of probability distributions of quantum states (see, for instance,
Nielsen & Chuang 2010
).

A probability distributions of quantum states over a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is represented by a density matrix over
\mathcal{H}
, i.e., a positive semi-definite element of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
with trace at most one. We write the set of density matrices over
\mathcal{H}
as
D(\mathcal{H})
. The most general kind of evolutions of density matrices are given by
quantum operations
. A quantum operation from
\mathcal{H}_{A}
to
\mathcal{H}_{B}
is defined as a higher order linear map from
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
to
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
that is completely positive and trace non-increasing. We explain these notions as follows:
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A}),\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{B}))
is said to be
completely positive
if it preserves positive semi-definiteness when tensored with the identity. That is, for all finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{E}
and for all positive semi-definite
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E})
, the operator
(\mathcal{C}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{E})(\rho)
is also positive semi-definite, where
\mathcal{I}_{E}
is the identity map over
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{E})
.
\mathcal{C}
is
trace non-increasing
on positive semi-definite inputs if for all positive semidefinite
\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
, we have
Tr[\mathcal{C}(\rho)]\leq Tr[\rho]
.
A
quantum channel
from
\mathcal{H}_{A}
to
\mathcal{H}_{B}
is defined as a quantum operation from
\mathcal{H}_{A}
to
\mathcal{H}_{B}
that is trace-preserving. Intuitively, quantum channels map probability distributions of quantum states to probability distributions, whereas quantum operations map probability distributions of quantum states to probability subdistributions.

Throughout this paper, we will use the standard font
F
,
G
,
\ldots
to denote linear maps, except when referring to higher-order maps such as quantum channels and operations, where we will use calligraphic letters
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{D}
,
\mathcal{C}_{0}
,
\mathcal{C}_{1}
,
\dots
In particular, we write the identity map over
\mathcal{H}_{A}
as
I_{A}
, and the identity channel over
\mathcal{H}_{A}
(i.e., the identity map over
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
) as
\mathcal{I}_{A}
.

Quantum operations and quantum channels form symmetric monoidal categories, which we write as
QO
and
QC
, respectively. In both
QC
and
QO
, objects are finite dimensional Hilbert spaces. In
QO
, the morphisms
\text{{{{QO}}}}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B})
are given by quantum operations from
\mathcal{H}_{A}
to
\mathcal{H}_{B}
and, in
QC
, the morphisms
\text{{{{QC}}}}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B})
are given by quantum channels from
\mathcal{H}_{A}
to
\mathcal{H}_{B}
. In both categories, the composition rule corresponds to the usual notion of composition for linear maps, and the categorical operation
\otimes
coincides with the linear-algebraic tensor product.

The Löwner ordering
\leq
is defined on the set of Hermitian matrices Herm(
\mathcal{H}_{A}
) as follows:

if
M-N
is positive semi-definite. The set of density matrices

, equipped with the Löwner ordering, is a pointed directed-complete partial order (pointed dcpo):

Lemma 4.1.

(D(\mathcal{H}_{A}),\leq)
is a pointed dcpo.

Remark 4.2.

If

is the supremum of a directed subset

of

, then it is the topological limit of a sequence of elements in that directed set, with respect to the standard Euclidian topology on
\mathbb{C}^{d_{A}\times d_{A}}
, where

is the dimension of
\mathcal{H}_{A}
.

The Löwner ordering is also defined on quantum operations as follows: for all
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}\in\text{{{{QO}}}}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B})
,
\mathcal{C}\leq\mathcal{D}
if for all Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{E}
and for all

, we have

.

Lemma 4.3.

(\text{{{{QO}}}}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B}),\leq)
is a pointed dcpo. Moreover, the supremeum of a directed subset is given by the pointwise supremum, taken with respect to the Löwner ordering on Hermitian matrices.

Lemma 4.4.

If

of

Lastly, the Kraus representation is a convenient way to describe quantum channels and operations. Kraus’s theorem states that any quantum operation
\mathcal{C}\in\text{{{{QO}}}}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B})
can be written as
\mathcal{C}:\rho\mapsto\sum_{i}K_{i}\rho K_{i}^{{\dagger}}
, where the

are linear maps in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B})
satisfying

. Moreover

if and only if

is a quantum channel.

4.2.
Vacuum-Extensions

The goal of the section is to provide an adequate definition of the
\mathtt{qcase}
on quantum operations. As a starting point, the quantum case protocol is defined on unitary matrices as follows. Given two (single-qubit) unitary matrices
U,V
acting on some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
as input, define:

\mathtt{qcase}(U,V)\triangleq\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert\otimes U+\lvert 1\rangle\langle 1\rvert\otimes V

This can be interpreted as follows: we call the first qubit the control qubit, and the second the target qubit. If the control qubit is in state
0
,
U
is applied to the target qubit. If the control qubit is in state
1
,
V
is applied to the target qubit. Otherwise,
U
and
V
are applied in a coherent superposition, depending on the control qubit. Although the definition on unitary maps is straightforward, extending this function to quantum channels and operations poses some difficulties. Suppose that we would like to define an operations
\mathtt{QCASE}
such that given the unitary channels
\mathcal{U}=U(\cdot)U^{{\dagger}}
and
\mathcal{V}=V(\cdot)V^{{\dagger}}
as inputs, we have
\mathtt{QCASE}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})=W(\cdot)W^{{\dagger}}
with
W\triangleq\mathtt{qcase}(U,V)
. Such a process is ill-defined
Abbott et al. 2020
. To see this, set
U\triangleq I
and
V\triangleq e^{i\theta}I
for some phases
\theta\in[0,2\pi[
. Then the corresponding channels are
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{I}
, regardless of
\theta
. However, the channel
\mathtt{QCASE}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})=W(\cdot)W^{{\dagger}}
corresponding to
W
do depend on
\theta
. This is because the global phase of
V
becomes a local phase of
W
, and thus becomes observable. Therefore it is not possible to define such an operation on channels.

In order to define an adequate quantum case operation, it is necessary to specify more information about the physical implementation of each input channel
Abbott et al. 2020
;
Kristjánsson et al. 2020
. This can be done using the framework of vacuum-extensions
Kristjánsson et al. 2020
;
Chiribella & Kristjánsson 2019
, which we summarize here.

Let
\mathcal{C}\in\text{{{{QO}}}}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B})
be a quantum operation. The idea is to extend the definition of
\mathcal{C}
to describe its action when it receives a regular input, when its input is empty, and importantly, when it receives any superposition of regular and empty inputs. To formalize the concept of “empty input”, we define a one-dimensional Hilbert space Vac.
We write
\lvert\text{vac}\rangle
to denote a unit vector of
Vac
called the
vacuum state
, which is unique up to global phases. The purpose of
\lvert\text{vac}\rangle
is to represent an empty input. Then, a vacuum extension of
\mathcal{C}
is a quantum operation
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}\in\text{{{{QO}}}}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\oplus\text{Vac},\mathcal{H}_{B}\oplus\text{Vac})
, whose action on regular inputs matches that of
\mathcal{C}
, and that returns the vacuum state when its input was the vacuum state, up to some amplitude. Formally, we have the following definition:

Definition 4.5.

Let
\mathcal{C}\in\text{{{{QO}}}}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B})
be a quantum operation. A

of

is a quantum operation
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}\in\text{{{{QO}}}}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\oplus\text{Vac},\mathcal{H}_{B}\oplus\text{Vac})
satisfying:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{C}}(\lvert\text{vac}\rangle\langle\text{vac}\rvert)

\displaystyle=\lambda\lvert\text{vac}\rangle\langle\text{vac}\rvert

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{C}}(P_{A}\rho P_{A})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}(P_{A}\rho P_{A}),\ \forall\rho\in D(\mathcal{H}_{A})

where
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
, and
P_{A}
is the orthogonal projector onto
\mathcal{H}_{A}
.

If

is a Kraus decomposition of a vacuum extension

From here, it is possible to define the quantum case. Consider a pair of vacuum-extended operations

5.
Properties of Quantum Programs

We now show the main results on our programming language, starting with universality.

5.1.
Universality

5.1.1.
General Case

Informally, we will show that for all pair
(\mathcal{C},F)
representing a vacuum extension, there exists a program implementing
(\mathcal{C},F)
. We begin by defining sub-unitary operations.

Definition 5.1 (Sub-unitary matrices and operations).

A matrix
U
is said to be
sub-unitary
if there exist matrices
U_{1},U_{2},U_{3}
such that

\begin{bmatrix}U&U_{1}\\
U_{2}&U_{3}\end{bmatrix}

is unitary. A quantum operation
\mathcal{C}
is said to be sub-unitary if there exists a sub-unitary matrix
U
such that
\mathcal{C}=U(\cdot)U^{{\dagger}}
.

We show that every vacuum-extended operation can be obtained by composing a vacuum extension of a sub-unitary operation with a vacuum extension of a traceout. This result is analogous to
Selinger 2004
, Theorem 6.12
.

Lemma 5.2.

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}\in\text{{{{QO}}}}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\oplus\text{\emph{Vac}},\mathcal{H}_{B}\oplus\text{\emph{Vac}})
be a vacuum extension of a quantum operation
\mathcal{C}\in\text{{{{QO}}}}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B})
. Then
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
can be factorized as
\widetilde{Tr_{C}}\circ\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
for some system
C
, where:

•

\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
is a vacuum extension of a sub-unitary operation
\mathcal{G}\in\text{{{{QO}}}}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{C}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
, and

•

\widetilde{Tr_{C}}
is a vacuum extension of the traceout channel
Tr_{C}\in\text{{{{QC}}}}(\mathcal{H}_{C}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B},\mathcal{H}_{B})
.

Lemma
5.2
can be adapted to the case of vacuum extensions as provided in Equation
2
, where the parameter
\lambda
of Equation
1
is set to
1
. Such a vacuum extension can be described as a pair
(\mathcal{C},F)\in\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma;\Delta}
. Written with operation and transformation matrix pairs, there exist a system
\Delta^{\prime}
, a sub-unitary matrix
U\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\Gamma},\mathcal{H}_{\Delta^{\prime},\Delta})
, and a normalized state
\lvert\psi\rangle\in St(\mathcal{H}_{\Delta^{\prime}})
such that:

(4)

(\mathcal{C},F)=(Tr_{\Delta^{\prime}},\langle\psi\rvert\otimes I_{\Delta})\circ(U(\cdot)U^{{\dagger}},U)

where

of Lemma

can always be chosen with its dimension a power of 2. The universality property result states that every vacuum-extended operation of this form can be realized as the interpretation of a program:

Theorem 5.3 (Universality).

Let
\Gamma
and
\Delta
be environments and
(\mathcal{C},F)\in\mathcal{K}_{\Gamma;\Delta}
a vacuum-extended operation. Then there exists a program
(\Gamma;S;\Delta)
such that
\llbracket S\rrbracket_{\Gamma}=(\mathcal{C},F)
.

The proof consists in the following steps (see Appendix
D.1
for the detailed proof). First, we show that any unitary operation (specifically, a vacuum-extended channel of the form
(U(\cdot)U^{{\dagger}},U)
, for some unitary
U

Barenco et al. 1995
, and implementing all CNOT gates as was done in Examples
3.6
and
4.10
. Using this result, we show that any sub-unitary operation can also be realized as the interpretation of a program. Unlike unitary operations, sub-unitary operations may be trace decreasing. This feature is captured by combining measurement and the non-terminating LOOP program from Examples
3.8
and
4.12
. Finally, we can implement any vacuum-extended operation by decomposing it into a sub-unitary operation followed by a traceout (Equation
4
), and finding a suitable program for each of these operations. In conclusion, the result shows that the interpretation of programs covers all the vacuum extensions in
\mathcal{K}
.

Remark 5.4.

The universality result concerns vacuum extensions represented as a pair
(\mathcal{C},F)\in\mathcal{K}
, which does not cover all vacuum extensions (because we fixed
\lambda=1
in Equation
1
). Therefore, it is more accurate to say that the language is universal for vacuum extended operations that are trace-preserving on vacuum inputs, than all vacuum extended operations.

5.1.2.
Restriction to a Discrete Set of Qubit Gates

So far, we have assumed that we have an infinite set of single-qubit unitaries at our disposal. To represent a more realistic setting, we now suppose that we only have access to some finite set of gates. We will show that any vacuum-extended channel can be approximated up to arbitrary accuracy.
In this section, we will suppose that the only single-qubit gates built into the language are

H\triangleq\ \lvert x\rangle\mapsto\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{2}}}(\lvert 0\rangle+(-1)^{x}\lvert 1\rangle)\qquad T\triangleq\ \lvert x\rangle\mapsto e^{ix\frac{\pi}{4}}\lvert x\rangle

E\bigl((\mathcal{C},F),(\mathcal{D},G)\bigr)\triangleq\bigl\|\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{F}-\tilde{\mathcal{D}}^{G}\bigr\|_{\diamond}.

5.2.
Adequacy

We have independently defined an operational semantics and a denotational semantics for our language. The operational semantics, on the one hand, defines the evolution of an input state induced by a program. The denotational semantics, on the other hand, assigns to each program a vacuum-extended channel that describes its behavior. In this section, we show adequacy between the two semantics, which will confirm that the two semantics describe the same behavior of programs. The result is stated as follows:

Theorem 5.6 (Adequacy).

Let
(\Gamma;S;\Delta)
be a program and
\lvert\psi\rangle\in St(\mathcal{H}_{\Gamma})
an input state. Suppose that
\llbracket S\rrbracket_{\Gamma}=(\mathcal{C},F)
and
\mathscr{E}([S,\lvert\psi\rangle]_{\Gamma})=\{(\lvert\psi^{\prime}_{i}\rangle,\nu_{i})\}_{i}
. Then:

\mathcal{C}(\lvert\psi\rangle\langle\psi\rvert)=\sum_{i}\lvert\psi^{\prime}_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}^{\prime}\rvert\qquad\text{ and }\qquad F\lvert\psi\rangle=\sum_{i}\nu_{i}\lvert\psi^{\prime}_{i}\rangle

This correspondence between the two semantics also links the construction of default transitions to the notion of vacuum states from the denotational semantics. This result is shown by structural induction on
S
(see Appendix
E
). For the

statement, we use the fact that the denotational semantics is given by the supremum of iterated applications of
\mathscr{F}_{\mathtt{q}}^{S}
to
(0,0)
, and proceed by induction on the number of iterations of the
while
loop.

5.3.
Observational Equivalence and Full Abstraction

The final result on the language concerns the notion of observational equivalence. Two programs are said to be observationally equivalent if their observable behavior, in any context, is identical.
5
5

5

We call this property “observational equivalence” rather than “contextual equivalence” to avoid confusion with the notion of contextuality in quantum mechanics.
We show that programs are observationally equivalent if and only if they have the same denotational semantics.

A context
C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta}
is a statement with exactly one hole
[\cdot]^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta}
indexed by environments
\Gamma
and
\Delta
.

Definition 5.10 (Contexts).

For all
\Gamma
and
\Delta
, contexts are defined by the following grammar:

\displaystyle C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta}\ ::=\ \

\displaystyle[\cdot]^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta}\ |\ S;C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta}\ |\ C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta};S\ |\ \text{{{{meas}}}}\ \mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow S,1\rightarrow C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta})\ |\ \text{{{{meas}}}}\ \mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta},1\rightarrow S)

\displaystyle|\ \

\displaystyle\text{{{{qcase}}}}\ \mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow S,1\rightarrow C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta})\ |\ \text{{{{qcase}}}}\ \mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta},1\rightarrow S)\ |\ \text{{{{while}}}}\ \mathtt{q}\ \text{{{{do}}}}\ C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta}

where
S
is a statement.

We introduce the inference rule

(C)

\Gamma,\Sigma\vdash[\cdot]^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta}\triangleright\Delta,\Sigma

in order to extend well-formedness to contexts. Setting
\text{Var}([\cdot]^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta})\triangleq\emptyset
, we recursively define the set of variables
\text{Var}(C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta})
appearing in
C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta}
. For any statement
S
and context
C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta}
, let
C[S]
be the statement obtained by substituting
S
for
[\cdot]^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta}
in
C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta}
.

Definition 5.11 (Compatibility).

A context
C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta}
is
compatible
with a program
(\Gamma;S;\Delta)
if

(1)

\emptyset\vdash C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta}\triangleright\emptyset
is derivable, and

(2)

BV
(S)\cap\text{Var}(C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta})=\emptyset
.

If
C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta}
is compatible with
(\Gamma;S;\Delta)
, then
C[S]
is well formed. Specifically,
\emptyset\vdash C[S]\triangleright\emptyset
is derivable. This can be shown by using Lemma
A.2
and the weakening property (Lemma
2.1
). The definition of compatibility ensures that i) the context
C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta}
introduces all the variables of
\Gamma
and deletes all the variables of
\Delta
(even when they are not actually used in
S
), and ii) the context can introduce some extra variables that are not in
S
. As an example, the context:

\displaystyle C^{\mathtt{q},\mathtt{r}\rightarrow\mathtt{q},\mathtt{r}}\ \triangleq\

\displaystyle\text{{{{new qbit }}}}\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q},\mathtt{r};\ \text{{{{qcase}}}}\ \mathtt{p}\ (0\rightarrow[\cdot]^{\mathtt{q},\mathtt{r}\rightarrow\mathtt{q},\mathtt{r}},1\rightarrow\text{{{{skip}}}});\ \text{{{{discard }}}}\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q},\mathtt{r}

is compatible with the program
((\mathtt{q},\mathtt{r});\mathtt{q}*=H;(\mathtt{q},\mathtt{r}))
.

Definition 5.12 (Observational equivalence).

Two programs
(\Gamma;S;\Delta)
and
(\Gamma;S^{\prime};\Delta)
are said to be
observationally equivalent
, written as
(\Gamma;S;\Delta)\approx(\Gamma;S^{\prime};\Delta)
, if for all context
C^{\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta}
compatible with
(\Gamma;S;\Delta)
and
(\Gamma;S^{\prime};\Delta)
, we have
p(C[S],\lvert\rangle)=p(C[S^{\prime}],\lvert\rangle)
.

Here
\lvert\rangle
is a one-dimensional input state, distinct from the vacuum state
\lvert\text{vac}\rangle
. In short, observational equivalence formalizes the idea that there is no observable difference between the behavior of two programs. We now show that our denotational semantics is fully abstract wrt. observational equivalence:

Theorem 5.13 (Full abstraction).

(\Gamma;S;\Delta)\approx(\Gamma;S^{\prime};\Delta)
if and only if
\ \llbracket S\rrbracket_{\Gamma}=\llbracket S^{\prime}\rrbracket_{\Gamma}
.

We summarize the proof as follows (see Appendix
F
for the full proof). To show that two programs with the same denotation are observationally equivalent, we use the compositionality of the denotational semantics. To show the reverse implication, we show its contrapositive: for all pair of programs whose denotation is different, we construct a context for which the probability of termination is different. Each program is interpreted as a quantum operation and transformation matrix pair. If their quantum operations are different, we construct a context that prepares an input state for which the outputs are different, and performs a measurement that distinguishes them. If their quantum operations are the same, but their transformation matrices are different, we first perform a
qcase
operation, resulting in new programs with different quantum operations, and proceed as in the first case.

Therefore, the denotational semantics exactly captures the observable behavior of programs. This further justifies the use of vacuum extensions for semantics of quantum programming languages.

5.4.
Examples

Example 5.14.

Consider the statements

\displaystyle S_{1}\

\displaystyle\triangleq\ \text{{{{skip}}}}

\displaystyle S_{2}\

\displaystyle\triangleq\ \text{{{{new qbit }}}}\mathtt{q};\ \text{{{{discard}}}}\ \mathtt{q}

\displaystyle S_{3}\

\displaystyle\triangleq\ \mathtt{q}*=I

First, the judgments
\emptyset\vdash S_{1}\triangleright\emptyset
and
\emptyset\vdash S_{2}\triangleright\emptyset
are derivable, and we have:

\displaystyle\llbracket S_{1}\rrbracket_{\emptyset}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{I}_{\emptyset},I_{\emptyset})

\displaystyle\llbracket S_{2}\rrbracket_{\emptyset}

\displaystyle=\llbracket\text{{{{discard}}}}\ \mathtt{q}\rrbracket_{\mathtt{q}}\circ\llbracket\text{{{{new qbit}}}}\mathtt{q}\rrbracket_{\emptyset}=(Tr_{\mathtt{q}},\langle 0\rvert_{\mathtt{q}})\circ(\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert_{\mathtt{q}},\lvert 0\rangle_{\mathtt{q}})=(\mathcal{I}_{\emptyset},I_{\emptyset})

Therefore by Theorem
5.13
,
(\emptyset;S_{1};\emptyset)\approx(\emptyset;S_{2};\emptyset)
: initializing and deleting a qubit is equivalent to doing nothing. Second, the judgments
\mathtt{q}\vdash S_{1}\triangleright\mathtt{q}
and
\mathtt{q}\vdash S_{3}\triangleright\mathtt{q}
are derivable, and we have:

\displaystyle\llbracket S_{1}\rrbracket_{\mathtt{q}}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{I}_{\mathtt{q}},I_{\mathtt{q}})=\llbracket S_{3}\rrbracket_{\mathtt{q}}

Therefore,
(\mathtt{q};S_{1};\mathtt{q})\approx(\mathtt{q};S_{3};\mathtt{q})
. This example illustrates why it is necessary to specify the input and output environments when stating the observational equivalence of two programs. In particular,
\forall\Gamma,\ \forall\Delta,\ (\Gamma;S_{2};\Delta)\not\approx(\Gamma;S_{3};\Delta)
.

Example 5.15.

We now show that discard statements with respect to different bases are not equivalent. Consider the following statement indexed by a unitary
U
, which discards the qubit
\mathtt{q}
:

\text{D}_{U}\ \triangleq\ \mathtt{q}*=U;\text{{{{discard}}}}\ \mathtt{q}

The judgment
\mathtt{q}\vdash D_{U}\triangleright\emptyset
is derivable. We have:

\displaystyle\llbracket\text{D}_{U}\rrbracket_{\mathtt{q}}

\displaystyle=(Tr_{\mathtt{q}},\langle 0\rvert_{\mathtt{q}})\circ(U_{\mathtt{q}}(\cdot)U^{{\dagger}}_{\mathtt{q}},U_{\mathtt{q}})=(Tr_{\mathtt{q}},\langle 0\rvert_{\mathtt{q}}U_{\mathtt{q}})

Therefore
(\mathtt{q};\text{D}_{U};\emptyset)\approx(\mathtt{q};\text{D}_{V};\emptyset)
if and only if
U^{{\dagger}}\lvert 0\rangle=V^{{\dagger}}\lvert 0\rangle
, meaning that discards with respect to different bases are generally not equivalent. This is different than the usual framework of quantum operations, where operations performed just before a discard can be ignored. Indeed, when
\llbracket\text{D}_{U}\rrbracket_{\mathtt{q}}\neq\llbracket\text{D}_{V}\rrbracket_{\mathtt{q}}
only the transformation matrices differ, the quantum operations both being the usual traceout operation.

Example 5.16.

We consider the previous coin flip example (see Examples
3.7
and
4.11
):

\displaystyle\text{COIN}\ \triangleq\

\displaystyle\text{{{{while}}}}\ \mathtt{q}\text{{{{ do }}}}\mathtt{q}*=H

We have shown in Example

that
\llbracket\text{COIN}\rrbracket_{\mathtt{q}}=\left(Tr(\cdot)\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert,\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert\right)
. We now show that COIN is observationally equivalent to a discard/prepare program DP defined by:

\text{DP}\ \triangleq\ \text{{{{discard}}}}\ \mathtt{q};\text{{{{new qbit }}}}\mathtt{q}

\text{Indeed, }\llbracket\text{DP}\rrbracket_{\mathtt{q}}=(\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert,\lvert 0\rangle)\circ(Tr_{\mathtt{q}},\langle 0\rvert_{\mathtt{q}})=\left(Tr(\cdot)\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert,\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert\right)=\llbracket\text{COIN}\rrbracket_{\mathtt{q}}.

Therefore
(\mathtt{q};\text{COIN};\mathtt{q})\approx(\mathtt{q};DP;\mathtt{q})
. This is because repeatedly measuring a qubit until we obtain the state
\lvert 0\rangle
has the same effect as discarding the qubit and preparing it again in the state
\lvert 0\rangle
.

Example 5.17.

Consider the following statements:

\displaystyle S\ \triangleq\

\displaystyle\text{{{{qcase }}}}\mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow S_{0},\ 1\rightarrow S_{1});\ \text{{{{qcase }}}}\mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow S_{0}^{\prime},\ 1\rightarrow S_{1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle S^{\prime}\ \triangleq\

Example 5.18.

We aim to study the action of the
qcase
primitive when its branches contain probabilistic statements. Consider the following statement implementing a single coin flip:

\displaystyle\text{COIN}_{1}\ \triangleq\ \text{{{{new qbit }}}}\mathtt{q}\ ;\ \mathtt{q}*=H\ ;\ \text{{{{meas }}}}\mathtt{q}\ (0\rightarrow\text{{{{skip}}}},1\rightarrow\text{{{{skip}}}})

The qubit
\mathtt{q}
will finish in state
\lvert 0\rangle
or
\lvert 1\rangle
, each with probability
\frac{1}{2}
each. Formally, we can derive
\emptyset\vdash\text{COIN}_{1}\triangleright\mathtt{q}
, and we can show that:

\llbracket\text{COIN}_{1}\rrbracket_{\emptyset}=\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert+\lvert 1\rangle\langle 1\rvert\right),\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\lvert 0\rangle\right)

Then, consider the statement consisting of a
qcase
where each branch contains one coin flip:

\text{QCOIN}_{1}\ \triangleq\ \text{{{{qcase}}}}\ \mathtt{p}\ (0\rightarrow\text{COIN}_{1},1\rightarrow\text{COIN}_{1})

We can derive
\mathtt{p}\vdash\text{QCOIN}_{1}\triangleright\mathtt{p},\mathtt{q}
, and

\llbracket\text{QCOIN}_{1}\rrbracket_{\mathtt{p}}=\overline{\text{{{{qcase}}}}}_{\mathtt{p}}\left[\llbracket\text{COIN}_{1}\rrbracket_{\mathtt{p}},\llbracket\text{COIN}_{1}\rrbracket_{\mathtt{p}}\right]=\left(\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}\nicefrac{{a}}{{2}}&0&\nicefrac{{b}}{{2}}&0\\
0&\nicefrac{{a}}{{2}}&0&0\\
\nicefrac{{c}}{{2}}&0&\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}&0\\
0&0&0&\nicefrac{{d}}{{2}}\end{bmatrix}\ ,\ \begin{bmatrix}\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{2}}}&0\\

This is different from
\llbracket\text{COIN}_{1}\rrbracket_{\mathtt{p}}
. This might seem surprising, because QCOIN

is nothing but two instances of COIN
1
in superposition. However, this is a consequence of the probabilistic behavior of COIN

on the other hand are generally not observationally equivalent.

6.
Conclusion

We have developed a high-level programming language combining classical and quantum control and succeeded in developing well-defined semantic approaches, thus resolving a problem that has been open for a decade.
The language has strong properties: universality for vacuum-extensions, adequacy, and full abstraction, which demonstrate the relevance of the approaches used (appropriate Kraus decompositions and vacuum-extensions) to define its semantics.
Future work concerns the extension of this study to a higher-order language and the compilation of programs to lower-level quantum models.

We prove that the set of bound variables of a well-formed statement (Definition

The sets of input and variables of well-formed statements are defined inductively as follows:

\begin{array}[]{lclclcl}\text{in}(\text{{{{skip}}}})&\triangleq&\emptyset&&\text{out}(\text{{{{skip}}}})&\triangleq&\emptyset\\

A.2.
Probabilistic Structure of the Operational Semantics

The probability of a transition
[S,\lvert\psi\rangle]_{\Gamma}\rightarrow(\lvert\psi^{\prime}\rangle,\nu)
is defined as
\frac{\|\lvert\psi^{\prime}\rangle\|^{2}}{\|\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}}
if
\lvert\psi\rangle\neq 0
, and
0
otherwise. We must check that the transition probabilities are well-defined. That is, the coefficients
\frac{\|\lvert\psi^{\prime}\rangle\|^{2}}{\|\lvert\psi\rangle\|^{2}}
(when
\lvert\psi\rangle\neq 0
) for transitions originating from the same configuration sum to at most one, and thus form a probability subdistribution.

In order to consider all possible values attainable from a fixed configuration, we recall the definition of multiset. Multisets are a similar concept to sets, where elements may appear multiple times. This will be used to reflect the fact that multiple derivations from the same configuration may lead to the same value, but their transition probabilities must still be counted separately as they correspond to distinct program executions. Formally, a multiset over a set
A
is a pair
(A,M)
where
M:A\mapsto\mathbb{N}
is the function that maps each element to its multiplicity. We will write multisets in brackets
\{\}
similarly to sets, repeating each element according to its multiplicity. For example, the multiset
(\{a,b,c\},(a\mapsto 1,b\mapsto 2,c\mapsto 0))
will be written as
\{a,b,b\}
. The support of
M
is defined as the subset of elements of
A
that appear in
M
at least once:

\text{Supp}(M)\triangleq\{a\in A\ |\ M(A)>0\}

Standard operations on sets are adapted to multisets as follows. Given multisets
M
and
N
over
A
, we say that
a\in M
if
A\in\text{Supp}(M)
, and we say that
M\subseteq N
if for all
a\in A
,
M(a)\leq N(a)
. The intersection of
M
and
N
is defined by
(M\cap N)(a)=\min(M(a),N(a))
for all
a\in A
. The union of
M
and
N
is defined by
(M\cup N)(a)=\max(M(a),N(a))
for all
a\in A
. The disjoint union of
M
and
N
is defined by
(M\uplus N)(a)=M(a)+N(a)
for all
a\in A

\displaystyle=\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert_{\mathtt{q}}(\lvert\psi\rangle\langle\psi\rvert)\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert_{\mathtt{q}}+\sum_{i,j}\lvert\psi_{ij}^{\prime\prime}\rangle\langle\psi_{ij}^{\prime\prime}\rvert

\displaystyle\begin{subarray}{c}\text{ by induction hypothesis}\\
\text{(inner induction)}\end{subarray}

Combining this with Equation

, we obtain the first result. Then, we have

We conclude the proof by taking the limit in
