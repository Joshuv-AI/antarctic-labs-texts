The PhoPQ two-component regulatory system as an important regulator of bacterial stress response
Authors: Aleksandra Maria Omelaniuk, Dawid Gmiter, Wiesław Kaca
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo-component regulatory systems (TCSs) are fundamental regulatory mechanisms that enable bacteria to detect and respond to a wide range of environmental signals. Each TCS typically consists of two proteins: a membrane-associated histidine kinase that acts as a sensor, and a cytoplasmic response regulator that mediates transcriptional changes. This design allows TCSs to enable bacteria to precisely regulate their physiology and adapt to changing environmental conditions. Among these systems, PhoPQ is one of the better characterised examples, particularly in Gram-negative pathogens. The PhoPQ system responds to environmental signals such as cationic antimicrobial peptides, low pH, and changes in metal ion concentrations (e.g., Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Fe³⁺). Activation of PhoPQ initiates the regulation of many downstream genes, including those responsible for lipid A modification, which in turn contributes to bacterial resistance to polymyxin B. A growing body of evidence points to the key role of PhoPQ in the virulence and survival of many Gram-negative bacteria. This makes PhoPQ not only an important element of bacterial pathogenicity, but also a promising therapeutic target for the development of new pharmacological strategies.Keywords: Two-component regulatory system, PhoPQ, Gram-negative bacteria, Polymyxin B, Antimicrobial polypeptides

Introduction

Survival of bacteria in the environment depends on their ability to respond to external stimuli. Therefore, one of the most important characteristics of bacteria is their capacity to regulate gene expression in response to environmental conditions. This ability enables them to adapt to both favourable and unfavourable environments. Initially, it was believed that gene regulation in bacteria occurred exclusively through transcriptional control by proteins. However, it is now known that the mechanisms underlying gene expression regulation are more diverse. One such mechanism used by bacteria is two-component regulatory systems (TCSs) [1–3].

TCSs are multifaceted regulatory mechanisms that are commonly found in prokaryotes. A typical TCS consists of two proteins. The first protein is a sensor with histidine kinase (HK) activity that is anchored in the inner membrane (IM). It contains a receptor domain that detects environmental signals and a transmitter domain with kinase activity. The function of this domain is to receive external stimuli and subsequently transmit the signal by phosphorylating the appropriate response regulator (RR). The second protein is the RR, which is located in the cytoplasm. It is a two-domain structure consisting of a receiver domain with a conserved aspartate residue and an effector domain that contains a DNA-binding motif. An exception to this are TCSs with hybrid sensor kinases. In these systems, key phosphotransfer domains, namely the kinase domain, the receiver (REC) domain, and in some cases the HPt domain (as exemplified by the VirA protein from Agrobacterium tumefaciens) are incorporated into a single polypeptide chain. Its function is to mediate the cellular response to external stimuli [3–7]. TCSs have a characteristic structure, consisting of a homodimeric transmembrane HK and a cytoplasmic RR. Upon detection of environmental stimuli via its sensory domain, the HK undergoes autophosphorylation by transferring the γ-phosphate group from ATP to a highly conserved histidine residue within its transmitter domain. The RR, through its N-terminal receiver domain, catalyses the transfer of the phosphoryl group from the conserved histidine residue of the kinase to an invariant aspartate residue within its own structure. This phosphorylation activates the output domain of the regulator, which, in most cases, possesses DNA-binding properties and functions as a transcriptional regulator. In addition to autokinase activity and the ability to phosphorylate the RR, many HKs also exhibit phosphatase activity. The balance between autokinase and phosphatase activities finely tunes the level of phosphorylated RR, thereby regulating the strength and duration of the cellular response [3, 8].

The phosphorylated RR is transcriptionally active and initiates the cellular reaction. As part of this response, the RR often activates the transcription of genes encoding both components of the system, thereby forming a positive feedback loop. The activated RR binds to the promoters of target genes or further regulates the expression of downstream genes. Ultimately, the action of a TCS leads to coordinated changes in global gene expression profiles [2, 3, 9, 10]. The function of TCSs varies depending on the type of TCS and the bacterium in which the system is present (Table 1). TCSs often involve activation of antibiotic defence mechanisms and physiological changes in the cell that enhance antibiotic resistance [6].

The PhoP/PhoQ system, a member of the OmpR family, has been thoroughly studied in Salmonella enterica, Escherichia coli, Shigella flexneri and other bacterial strains [42]. The PhoPQ TCS is conserved among many pathogenic and non-pathogenic bacteria. It plays a key role in LPS remodelling in Gram-negative bacteria through regulation of > 200 genes [9, 43–46].

In this review article, we present the current state of knowledge on the structure, mechanism of action, and signaling of the PhoPQ system, with particular emphasis on interspecies differences and its role in responding to key environmental stimuli. We also highlight emerging therapeutic strategies targeting PhoPQ, as potential tools for combating antibiotic-resistant Gram-negative bacteria. Through this comprehensive overview, we aim to demonstrate that PhoPQ is not only a fundamental regulator of bacterial pathogenesis but also a promising target for modern antimicrobial therapies.

PhoPQ TCS

The HK PhoQ is a master regulator of virulence in bacteria for the PhoPQ TCS. HK is activated by responses to environmental cues such as cationic antimicrobial peptides, mildly acidic pH, increased osmolarity, biliary unsaturated long-chain fatty acids, low Mg2+, and low Ca2+ conditions [9, 43–47]. PhoQ is a homodimer of 487 amino acids [7]. Like other HKs, PhoQ undergoes autophosphorylation at the conserved histidine residue, and this phosphate group is then transferred to the conserved aspartate residue on the PhoP regulator. This results in activation of the PhoP regulator by phosphorylation in response to several environmental stimuli, which then regulates expression of downstream genes [9, 43, 45, 46, 48].

PhoQ sensor kinase was initially described as a protein consisting of a periplasmic (sensory) region, transmembrane region, and cytoplasmic region [49, 50]. The periplasmic region is a sensor that can detect a change in the surrounding medium. The transmembrane region typically consists of two antiparallel helices extending across the membrane located at the N and C termini of the periplasmic region. The cytoplasmic region contains a small signalling domain at its N terminus, known as the HAMP domain. The HAMP domain (so named because of its conserved presence in HKs, denylyl cyclases, methyl acceptor proteins, and phosphatases) is widely distributed in several proteins and plays a role in converting the signal from the periplasm into catalytic activity. The remainder of the cytoplasmic region is a well-conserved HK domain that interacts with RR proteins [49, 50].

Recent articles have described PhoQ protein as consisting of five domains: sensory domain, transmembrane domain (TM), cytosolic domain (HAMP), dimerisation domain (DHp) and catalytic domain (CA) [45, 48, 51].

PhoQ detects changes in the environment via its sensory domain, which is surrounded by two trans-membrane helices. These helices are responsible for attaching the PhoQ molecule to the OM. This domain is responsible for many of the molecular interactions that underpin the function of this molecule. These include the central hydrogen bond network in the Per-ARNT-Sim fold, the inter-monomer salt bridge, the membrane-directed acid cluster, and the juxta-membrane region at the periplasm/internal membrane interface. Mutations that alter these molecular interactions change the conformation of the sensory domain and the sensitivity of PhoQ to signals from the environment [47, 48, 51]. The second domain in PhoQ is the TM domain, which connects the sensory domain with the cytosolic domains. The next domain is the HAMP domain which is composed of 50 amino acids long and composed of two α-helices, which are connected by an unstructured linker region. The function of HAMP in PhoQ is to link extracellular and trans-membrane domains to cytoplasmic catalytic domains. The HAMP domain is essential for proper signal transduction, as a change of even one amino acid in this domain can cause a change in the protein. The HAMP domain connects the transmembrane domain (TM) of PhoQ to two other cytoplasmic domains, namely the dimerisation domain containing the phospho-histidine residue (DHp) and the catalytic domain, which is responsible for ATP binding (CA). The cytoplasmic domains of PhoQ interact with the related RR PhoQ. The correct timing of PhoQ activation is essential for virulence [48, 51].

The RR in the TCS PhoPQ, or PhoP protein, belongs to the OmpR/PhoB family, which is the largest family of RRs and contains thousands of proteins. These proteins have two main distinct domains: N-terminal receiver domain, and C-terminal effector domain (DNA-binding domain) (DBD) [52, 53]. The receiving domain of the protein consists of a central five-stranded β-sheet that is sandwiched between α-helices on either side. The domain contains a conserved aspartate residue that receives a phosphate group from a related histidine protein. Phosphorylation of the receiver domain is likely to be responsible for stabilising the dimer and causing structural changes to the protein, thereby activating the effector domain in some way. The effector domain acts as a transcriptional regulator and is also a DNA-binding domain. In the case of PhoP, the DNA recognition sites are tandem repeats, suggesting that these RRs bind to DNA as tandem dimers. The influence of PhoPQ on virulence factors

PhoPQ system of human pathogens is involved in low magnesium, acid tolerance and low pH adaptation, polymyxin resistance, and virulence. Although PhoQ in different microorganisms can detect the same or similar environmental signals, the regulons triggered by phosphorylated PhoP differ among bacterial species (Table 2) [2]. Given the wide range of the PhoP regulons, loss of PhoPQ function typically has pleiotropic effects on bacteria [2]. In consequence, multiple studies in the research process have shown that the PhoP/PhoQ cascade plays a crucial role in stress survival and regulation of virulence in various pathogenic bacteria [42].

Mg 2+ and other cations

Magnesium is one of the most abundant metals in the cells of a wide variety of organisms. It occurs as a divalent ion, and in that form, it participates in many basic cellular processes. In bacteria, magnesium is involved in the regulation and stabilisation of membranes and is a cofactor for several important enzymes [55]. The TCS PhoPQ has several inducible signals responsible for gene regulation in Gram-negative bacteria; one of which is the amount of Mg2+ ions in the bacterial environment [55, 56]. At high concentrations of Mg2+, the periplasmic detection domain that constructs the PhoQ protein binds to Mg2+ ions. This results in a PhoQ conformation with low autokinase activity but high phosphatase activity towards phosphorylated PhoP. This keeps the expression of PhoP-dependent genes low. A different situation is observed for restriction and dissociation of Mg2+ ions. There is a conformational change in the PhoQ protein, which increases its autokinase activity and suppresses its phosphatase activity. This leads to accumulation of PhoP and an increase in the expression of its regulon [10]. Restriction of Mg2+ ions, for example, affects P. aeruginosa biofilm development [55, 56] and upregulates genes during adhesion of bacteria to epithelial cells [44]. In the case of E. coli, Mg2+ ion restriction increases resistance to polymyxin B [57]. Mg2+ restriction induces retS transcriptional repression and promotes the transition from a planktonic state to a biofilm lifestyle. The retS gene encodes the RetS protein that is required for the expression of the type III virulence-associated secretion system and for repression of exopolysaccharide biosynthesis genes of the biofilm matrix RetS. Repression of retS directly interacts and forms heterodimers with the GacS sensor protein, preventing activation of the GacAS pathway and inhibiting biofilm formation that occurs through direct repression by PhoPQ. Reduced Mg2+ concentration is an important environmental factor stimulating the development of P. aeruginosa biofilm, which is resistant to antibiotics. P. aeruginosa in the planktonic growth state is also capable of type III secretion [56].

The Ca2+ concentration gradients in the plasma membrane and intracellular organelles enable highly dynamic flows through structured channel openings, generating tightly controlled spatial and temporal patterns. Dynamics in space and time are encoded and decoded into variable and sometimes opposing cellular signals [58]. Several metabolic and functional changes induced by bacterial endotoxins (LPS) during endotoxic shock are associated with a disruption of intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis in some tissues. LPS binds to the plasma membrane of target cells, disrupting trans-membrane signalling mechanisms and metabolic processes [59]. The saturated hydrocarbon chains of lipid A are confined to the cell membrane by van der Waals forces, while the bivalent Ca2+ ions binding to the lipid A phosphoesters act as bridges connecting and stabilising neighbouring LPS molecules. The barrier function of the OM is enhanced by the high negative charge carried by the lipid A phosphoester groups, in addition to the phosphate and carboxyl groups in the core and O-antigen sugars. TCS PhoPQ contains a sensory kinase that can detect environmental signals, such as a reduction in Ca2+ in the cell envelope [60–62]. PhoPQ expression is activated in response to limiting extracellular concentrations of divalent ions, in addition to autoregulation of the oprH-phoPQ operon by PhoP itself. The discovery that BrlR (a member of the MerR family of activators of multidrug efflux pumps) acts as a repressor of phoPQ gene expression provides a new level of control of PhoPQ TCS regulation. Resistance of P. aeruginosa to the cationic antimicrobial peptide (CAP) colistin is mutually regulated by PhoPQ and the transcriptional regulator BrlR. BrlR acts as an activator of multidrug efflux pump genes and a repressor of PhoPQ activation [63]. Among the genes identified as Ca2+ induced is an operon (PA2656-2659) encoding the CarSR TCS. The CarSR proteins are closely related to those of other TCSs, such as PhoPQ and PmrAB. The PhoQ kinase of the PhoPQ system binds Ca2+ and regulates transcription of genes, including those that encode virulence factors. The transcriptional response of TCS induced by addition of Ca2+ is dependent on the growth mode: expression of carSR is induced only in planktonic cultures, while expression of pmrAB and phoPQ is reduced in biofilms and planktonic cultures. In Vibrio cholerae, carSR expression negatively regulates polysaccharide production and biofilm formation in response to elevated Ca2+ concentrations. In E. coli, AtoSC is induced by Ca2+ and regulates the biosynthesis and intracellular distribution of cPHB [complexed poly-(R)3-hydroxybutyrate], building non-protein complexes that function as voltage-gated Ca2+ channels. The TCS CarSR is also responsible for the detection of elevated levels of external Ca2+ by P. aeruginosa and responds by inducing its regulatory targets, carO and carP. CarO and CarP affect intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis, surface motility, tobramycin resistance, and production of the virulence factor pyocyanin [64].

Iron is one of the essential elements for bacterial growth and participates in various physiological processes such as the tricarboxylic acid cycle and electron transport. Exogenous iron induces bacterial resistance to tigecycline (e.g. in P. aeruginosa). A recent study showed that iron promoted kanamycin resistance in Streptomyces coelicolor and Mycobacterium smegmatis, indicating some link between the Fe3+ ion and bacterial antibiotic resistance [65]. Metal ions, including Fe3+, affect the binding of some antibiotics to target sites and control their absorption via porin and siderophore [66]. In Gram-negative bacteria, low Mg2+ and Ca2+ concentrations stimulate PhoQ, and high Fe3+ concentrations can activate a subset of PhoP-regulated genes and the PhoPQ regulatory protein, by stimulating the PmrA-PmrB TCS. PhoPQ in Y. pestis is induced during gastrointestinal infection in fleas and is essential for the formation of an adherent biofilm in the foregut, which significantly enhances bacterial transmission during flea bites. Therefore, PhoPQ may be required for neutrophilic immunity in Y. pestis [67, 68].

Low pH

Low pH is one of the signals that enables PhoPQ to recognise and respond to the intracellular environment, where pH regulates gene expression, biochemical activity, and cellular behaviour. Slightly acidic pH activates the major virulence regulatory system PhoP/PhoQ in S. Typhimurium. The PhoQ sensor contains an extra-cytoplasmic domain involved in signal detection and a cytoplasmic domain controlling the activation of the PhoP regulator. The Salmonella PhoQ sensor kinase can be directly activated by acidic pH. Since Salmonella encounters a pH < 5.5 in the phagosomes of the host cells (macrophage phagosomes are a harsh environment characterised by low Mg 2+ concentration, low pH, and the presence of deleterious substances, including antimicrobial peptides, protease, lysozyme and reactive oxygen species), this is probably a physiologically relevant signal for PhoQ activation. A gradual decrease in pH can lead to an increase in PhoP dependent gene activation, as well as PhoP phosphorylation. Moreover, the sensory domain changes its conformation upon acidification [69–71].

Polymyxin B as one of the cationic antimicrobial polypeptides

Cationic antimicrobial peptides (CAPs) play an important role in host defence against microbial infection and are key effectors of the host innate immune response. Polypeptides, through their antimicrobial and immunomodulatory activities, have the potential to become antimicrobial drugs. In Gram-negative bacteria, CAPs, which have a net positive charge and amphipathic structure, bind to the negatively charged LPS on the OM and alter the integrity of the bacterial membrane by dissolving or forming pores [37, 72]. Pathogenic microorganisms have evolved distinct mechanisms to resist killing by CAPs, including excretion by pumps and cleavage by proteases. In many bacterial species, genes conferring resistance to CAPs are regulated by TCSs [36, 37, 72]. One of the most popular groups of CAPs are polymyxins, including colistin and, above all, polymyxin B. The use of polymyxin B, to treat intractable infections caused by pathogenic Gram-negative bacteria has increased dramatically over the past decade. At the same time, the number of reports of resistance to polymyxins has also increased, which simultaneously threatens the clinical utility of these antibiotics [45]. Polymyxin B is a CAP that contains a fatty acid side chain attached to a heptacyclic ring, which consists mainly of diaminobutyric acid [72]. PhoPQ TCS plays a key role in regulating the expression of lipid A modification genes in Gram-negative bacteria, and consequently resistance to polymyxin B [45]. The resistance system has been best characterised in the Gram-negative E. coli. PhoPQ responds to limited concentrations of divalent cations or to antimicrobial peptides alone [72, 73]. In addition to LPS modification with 4-aminoarabinose (Ara4N), PhoPQ can also modify lipid A by regulating PagP palmitoyltransferase. The PhoPQ system is also responsible for activating the transcription of genes such as pagL (the gene responsible for encoding the PagL protein, an enzyme discovered in Salmonella enterica serotype Typhimurium). PagL is responsible for modification in LPS by hydrolysing the ester bonds at position 3 of lipid A, thus releasing the primary 3-hydroxymyristoyl group [72–74] and the pmrD gene, and PmrD post-transcriptionally activates PmrA, leading to expression of PmrA-regulated genes. Thus, the end result of activation of the PhoPQ signalling system is the development of resistance to cationic host defence peptides, due to reduced peptide binding and altered membrane hydrophobicity [72, 73]. In S. Typhimurium, which has the PmrA-PmrB TCS, this system increases the expression of genes involved in covalent modifications of LPS. LPS modifications reduce the negative charge of LPS and consequently reduce the attraction and binding of CAPs to the OM. The PhoPQ TCS is also a major regulator of virulence in S. Typhimurium and is involved in the regulation of CAP resistance, by increasing expression of PmrD protein. This leads to activation of PmrA protein (a response regulator that is responsible for growth and virulence within macrophages) [75], and ultimately modification of LPS [36, 37]. S. Typhimurium also possesses a seven-gene polycistronic unit (pmrHFIJKLM, referred to as the pmrF operon for short). The pmrF operon is co-transcribed under the control of PmrA and participates in LPS modification and resistance to CAPs and polymyxin B. The gene products of the pmrF operon, with the exception of pmrM, are essential for the biosynthesis and addition of (Ara4N)‚ to the 4’ phosphate of lipid A; a modification that contributes to the net negative charge of LPS and consequently reduces the attraction and binding of CAPs to the OM [36, 37, 76, 77].

Targeted therapies directed at the PhoPQ TCS

There is currently a dynamic increase in antibiotic resistance among many pathogenic bacteria, which causes complications and difficulties in clinical treatment. Bacteria possess several virulence-regulating factors, such as TCSs. These have been identified as targets that can be used to reduce bacterial infections. In contrast to classical antibiotics, the new compounds do not show cytotoxicity or haemolysis at effective concentrations. This indicates their potential as safe therapeutic agents and provides an ideal focal point for the discovery of new compounds with potential pharmacological applications. This contributes to understanding the regulatory and mechanistic aspects of binary regulatory systems such as PhoPQ [78, 79]. In 2011, Cai et al. presented four potential inhibitors of the HK of the PhoPQ TCS, the PhoQ protein in Shigella flexneri 2a 301. Based on a 3D model of the PhoQ Sf 301 HK domain, 64 molecules were selected as potential inhibitors based on their molecular diversity, shape complementarity, and ability to form hydrogen bonds in the PhoQ-binding pocket. An expression system containing the intracellular HK domain of PhoQ was developed to confirm the interaction with PhoQ. Enzyme activity was assessed using a pyrophosphate assay (reflecting the real-time kinase reaction, albeit with lower sensitivity) and the more sensitive luminescent kinase assay. The study was extended to S. enterica SL1344, where the homologous PhoPQ system also regulates virulence. The three inhibitors showed a similar inhibitory effect on invasion, suggesting the possibility of their broad application [78]. Hydrazone derivatives have been extensively studied to create a chemical diversity with diverse biological properties, such as antimicrobial, anticonvulsant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer activities. Over the past two decades, N-acylhydrazones have proven to be a versatile and promising motif in drug design and medicinal chemistry. In 2024, Lobertti et al. proposed N ′-(thiophene-2-ylmethylene)benzohydrazide hydrazone (A16B1) as a potent blocker of PhoPQ activity. This compound inhibits the PhoPQ TCS by acting on the PhoQ domain that spans the second transmembrane region of the protein, negatively regulating its kinase activity. A16B1 exhibits antiviral properties by reducing intramacrophage replication of Salmonella [79]. In 2012, Tang et al. published a study describing eight compounds with potential as drugs targeting PhoP, specifically the functionally important α4-β5-α5 interface in S. enterica. These compounds were designed to prevent the critical protein-protein interaction (PPI), selectively disrupt the activity of the transcription factor, and inhibit the expression of virulence genes. The authors demonstrated that the identified compounds interfere with the formation of the PhoP-DNA complex. Results from a series of in vitro assays and biophysical analyses indicate that these compounds destabilize the PhoP-DNA complex by directly inhibiting the PhoP homodimer. One likely mechanism of action involves binding to the C-terminal DNA-binding domain, thereby directly preventing the formation of the PhoP-DNA complex. An alternative mechanism may involve interaction with the N-terminal regulatory domain or the linker region, resulting in allosteric effects that impede the conformational changes required for DNA binding [80].

Conclusion

Two-component regulatory systems (TCSs) are multidimensional regulatory mechanisms that are commonly found in prokaryotes. They enable bacteria to adapt to life under a range of favourable as well as unfavourable conditions. One of these is the TCS PhoPQ found in many Gram-negative bacteria. It is responsible for virulence in them in response to environmental cues such as cationic polypeptides, low pH or metal ions. PhoPQ consists of two proteins, namely the PhoQ and PhoP. The PhoQ protein is a histidine kinase located in the outer membrane. The PhoP protein is a response regulator, located in the cytoplasm. Despite many reports, the PhoPQ system is still insufficiently understood. Nevertheless, current knowledge shows that it is a conserved system important in the pathogenicity of many bacteria. This indicates its potential as a therapeutic target and provides a point for further research into new compounds with potential pharmacological applications. Therefore, understanding the regulatory and mechanistic aspects of binary regulatory systems such as PhoPQ is important for further science and medicine.
