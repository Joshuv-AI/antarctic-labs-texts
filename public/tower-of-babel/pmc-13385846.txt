Hydrophobic porous polysulfone membrane contactors with gravity-driven Ca(OH)2 absorption for low-energy CO2 removal from biogas
Authors: Barbaros Durmuş, Nilüfer Nacar Koçer, Bahtiyar Öztürk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The upgrading of biogas to biomethane requires efficient and energy‐conservative removal of carbon dioxide (CO₂) to enhance calorific value and downstream usability. In this study, a gravity‐driven hydrophobic polysulfone (PSf) hollow‐fiber membrane contactor was systematically evaluated for CO₂ removal from a synthetic CH₄/CO₂ (60/40 v/v) gas mixture under ambient conditions (23 ± 1 °C and 1 atm). Unlike conventional membrane contactor systems that rely on mechanically driven liquid circulation, the present configuration operates without liquid pumping, thereby eliminating liquid‐phase energy demand and simplifying system architecture. Comparative experiments were conducted using tap water as a physical absorbent and a low‐concentration Ca(OH)₂ solution (0.1 wt%) as a reactive absorbent to assess hydrodynamic, mass transfer, and energy performance under realistic decentralized operating conditions. CO₂ removal efficiencies exceeding 90% were achieved for both absorbents at optimized operating conditions (gas flow rate: 122.5 mL min−1; liquid flow rate: 800 mL min−1), with maximum values of 92–93% for tap water and approximately 94% for Ca(OH)₂. The gravity‐driven falling‐film liquid flow regime promoted effective gas–liquid interfacial contact while minimizing liquid‐side mass transfer resistance. Specific energy consumption was calculated by accounting exclusively for gas handling and analytical power requirements, yielding low values of 0.47 kWh kg−1 CO₂ for tap water and 0.45 kWh kg−1 CO₂ for Ca(OH)₂. Long‐term stability tests conducted over 30 days (8 h day−1) demonstrated sustained CO₂ removal performance with only gradual efficiency decline, attributed primarily to progressive CaCO₃ precipitation rather than membrane wetting or structural degradation. The novelty of this work lies not in the introduction of new membrane materials or absorbents, but in the system‐level demonstration of a pump‐free, gravity‐driven membrane contactor that integrates low energy consumption, operational simplicity, and stable performance. The results provide practical insights into the design of modular, low‐maintenance membrane contactor systems for decentralized and energy‐efficient biogas upgrading applications.

Introduction

Biogas, produced by the anaerobic digestion of organic substrates, typically contains 50–70% methane (CH₄) and 30–45% carbon dioxide (CO₂), along with trace amounts of H₂S, N₂, and water vapor1,2. Elevated CO₂ concentrations reduce the calorific value and combustion efficiency of biogas, thereby limiting its direct utilization in energy systems. Consequently, upgrading raw biogas to biomethane with CH₄ contents exceeding 95% has become a key requirement for climate-neutral energy strategies3,4.

Conventional CO₂ separation technologies, including amine absorption, cryogenic distillation, and pressure swing adsorption, are industrially mature but are associated with high thermal energy demand, solvent degradation, chemical losses, and operational complexity5,6. For instance, amine scrubbing requires energy-intensive solvent regeneration, while PSA and cryogenic routes rely on pressurization and/or deep cooling, increasing overall energy demand and operational complexity1,5.

These limitations have driven growing interest in membrane-based separation processes, which offer advantages such as lower specific energy consumption, modular scalability, and simplified operation7–9.

Hydrophobic porous membrane contactors enable gas–liquid mass transfer across a large interfacial area while maintaining complete phase separation, resulting in compact system designs and stable operation10–13 Among available membrane materials, polysulfone (PSf) hollow-fiber membranes are widely preferred due to their mechanical robustness, thermal and chemical stability, and tunable pore structure14,15. Alternative polymers such as polyethersulfone (PES), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), polypropylene (PP), and polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) have also been investigated; however, PSf membranes generally provide a favorable balance between permeability, durability, and fabrication cost.

The application of hollow-fiber membrane contactors for gas–liquid absorption is not new. Early pioneering work by Zhang and Cussler16 demonstrated the feasibility of CO₂ absorption into aqueous media using hollow-fiber membrane contactors, establishing the fundamental principles for subsequent research in this field. Since then, numerous studies have explored membrane contactor performance using both physical and chemical absorbents.

Recent pilot-scale investigations have shown that PSf membrane contactors combined with alkaline absorbents can achieve CO₂ removal efficiencies exceeding 90%, with reported energy savings compared to conventional amine-based systems17,18. In addition, recent studies emphasize that energy accounting (e.g., auxiliary electricity for gas handling and monitoring) should be reported alongside removal efficiency when evaluating membrane contactor-based upgrading systems1,19. The influence of gas and liquid flow rates on CO₂ capture in hollow-fiber membrane contactors has been extensively analyzed, as these parameters govern gas residence time and liquid-film boundary-layer resistance13.

Despite this extensive body of literature, most reported membrane contactor systems rely on mechanically driven liquid circulation and primarily emphasize absorption efficiency, while system-level energy performance and operational simplicity receive comparatively less attention. In particular, gravity-driven operation, which eliminates liquid pumping energy, has been scarcely investigated, even though it offers significant potential to reduce specific energy consumption and system complexity in decentralized biogas upgrading applications11,19,20.

Accordingly, the novelty of the present study does not lie in the introduction of a new membrane material or absorbent chemistry, but rather in the systematic evaluation of a gravity-driven polysulfone hollow-fiber membrane contactor operated under realistic gas–liquid flow conditions. The study explicitly quantifies CO₂ removal efficiency, mass transfer coefficients, specific energy consumption (kWh kg−1 CO₂), and long-term operational stability. This system-oriented approach addresses key gaps identified in recent membrane contactor research and provides practical insights for low-energy, decentralized biogas upgrading6,20.

Material and methods

In this study, a membrane-based separation approach was employed to selectively remove carbon dioxide (CO₂) from a synthetic methane–carbon dioxide (CH₄–CO₂) gas mixture, simulating typical biogas composition. The experimental system was designed to investigate the performance of a hydrophobic porous polysulfone membrane under varying operational conditions. Specifically, the effects of gas and liquid flow rates, as well as the type of absorbent (tap water and calcium hydroxide solution), on the CO₂ removal efficiency were systematically examined. All tests were conducted at ambient temperature and atmospheric pressure in a laboratory-scale membrane contactor setup, allowing for precise control and measurement of gas–liquid interactions. This section provides detailed information on the materials, membrane characteristics, experimental apparatus, operating parameters, and analytical methods used throughout the study.

Materials and chemicals

All chemicals and gases used in this study were of analytical or high purity and were used without further purification. The synthetic gas mixture was prepared using methane (CH₄, 99.99% purity) and carbon dioxide (CO₂, 99.99% purity), supplied by Messer Group (Germany), and introduced into the system at controlled flow rates using mass flow controllers.

Calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂, analytical grade, ≥ 95% purity) was obtained from a commercial supplier and dissolved in deionized water to prepare a 0.1 wt% alkaline absorbent solution. Laboratory tap water, with a total hardness below 150 mg CaCO₃ L−1, was used as a reference physical absorbent to represent realistic decentralized operating conditions.

The polysulfone (PSf) hollow-fiber membrane module was commercially supplied and used as received. All solutions were prepared freshly prior to the experiments to ensure consistent chemical composition.

Membrane properties

The study utilized a hydrophobic, porous polysulfone (PSf) hollow‐fiber membrane, chosen for its excellent chemical resistance in alkaline environments and robust thermal stability, both critical for long‐term gas–liquid contactor applications Water contact‐angle measurements confirmed a surface hydrophobicity of 122° ± 2°, effectively preventing pore wetting under gravity‐driven liquid flow.

Polysulfone (PSf) membranes are widely recognized for their chemical stability under alkaline conditions, which makes them suitable for gas–liquid contactor applications involving high-pH absorbents. Previous studies have reported that PSf membranes maintain structural integrity and hydrophobic characteristics over a broad pH range (typically pH 2–13), with negligible polymer backbone degradation under moderate alkaline exposure.

In the present study, the Ca(OH)₂ absorbent operated at pH ≈ 12, which falls within the documented chemical stability window of PSf. No chemical surface modification or coating was applied to the membrane. The absence of alkaline-induced degradation under the selected operating conditions is supported by stable removal performance and post-operation contact angle measurements.

The fiber wall thickness (40 µm; Table 1) and the porous structure provide a high surface-to-volume ratio that supports rapid CO₂ transport across the gas–liquid interface. The membrane module has a manufacturer-specified active area of 0.012 m2, and this value was used as the reference area for all area-normalized performance calculations throughout the manuscript (e.g., flux and mass-transfer coefficients) to maintain direct comparability with standard membrane contactor reporting. If mentioned, Happel’s free-surface cell concept is considered only as a qualitative framework to interpret fiber-bundle hydrodynamics and does not redefine the active area used for performance normalization. Experimental setup and procedure

The bench-scale experimental system, depicted schematically in Fig. 1, comprised four primary modules: (i) gas supply and control, (ii) liquid absorbent circulation, (iii) membrane contactor unit, and (iv) gas analysis and measurement21. A synthetic CH₄–CO₂ gas mixture (60:40 v/v,99.99% purity; Messer Group, Germany) was delivered to the lumen side of the horizontally oriented polysulfone (PSf) hollow-fiber membrane module via independent mass-flow controllers (MF1 for CH₄, MF2 for CO₂; Brooks 5850E, ± 1% accuracy). Gas flow rates were varied from 122.5 to 389 mL min−1, with controller uncertainty ± 1%.Fig. 1Schematic of the countercurrent polysulfone membrane contactor system for CO₂ removal from a CH₄–CO₂ gas mixture.

Schematic of the countercurrent polysulfone membrane contactor system for CO₂ removal from a CH₄–CO₂ gas mixture.

Liquid absorbent (either laboratory tap water with total hardness < 150 mg CaCO₃ L−1 or 0.1 wt % Ca(OH)₂ solution prepared from analytical-grade reagent and deionized water, pH 12 ± 0.1, stability < ± 0.05 pH over 30 days) was introduced to the shell side under gravity‐driven flow from an elevated reservoir. The rotational flow was monitored downstream of the module using a rotameter (Cole-Parmer, ± 1% accuracy) and adjusted between 250 and 1 000 mL min−1. After a stabilization period of ~ 15 min at each condition, CO₂ removal experiments were conducted for 60 min, during which inlet and outlet samples were collected via dedicated sampling ports.

Key geometric and porosimetric properties of the membrane module are summarized in Table 1. The module’s active surface area was specified as 0.012 m2, with average pore diameter 0.22 µm and porosity 60% ± 2%—parameters chosen to maximize CO₂ mass-transfer while preventing pore wetting22.

All experiments were carried out at 23 ± 1 °C and 1 atm Gas effluent volumetric flow was measured using a soap-bubble flowmeter. The CO₂ and CH₄ concentrations in the inlet and outlet gas streams were quantified by gas chromatography (GC) using samples collected from dedicated sampling ports after steady-state was reached. The CO₂ removal efficiency was subsequently calculated from the GC-determined inlet and outlet molar fractions.

The Ca(OH)₂ absorbent solution was prepared fresh prior to each experimental run and supplied to the membrane module under gravity-driven flow without continuous recirculation. The system was operated in a once-through configuration, and no long-term storage of the alkaline solution was applied between experimental runs.

To minimize uncontrolled CaCO₃ accumulation, the absorbent reservoir was visually inspected before each experiment, and no external filtration or regeneration step was employed during operation. Under the selected operating conditions and Ca(OH)₂ concentration (0.1 wt%), precipitation remained limited and did not cause immediate clogging, membrane wetting, or unstable operation within the experimental timescale.

During the long-term stability experiments, the Ca(OH)₂ absorbent solution (0.1 wt%) was prepared fresh at the beginning of each experimental day. The membrane contactor was operated for 8 h per day under gravity-driven, once-through conditions, during which the absorbent was continuously supplied to the shell side and discharged without recirculation. At the end of each daily operation period, the system was shut down and the remaining absorbent solution was discarded; no solution was stored or reused on subsequent days.

No chemical cleaning, flushing, or mechanical maintenance procedures were applied between daily runs. Only visual inspection was performed to ensure stable liquid flow and the absence of clogging or abrupt membrane wetting. This operating protocol was intentionally selected to reflect low-maintenance, decentralized application scenarios and to allow assessment of gradual performance changes associated with CaCO₃ accumulation under realistic operating conditions.

Data analysis

The experimental results obtained from the membrane contactor system were analyzed using standard mass balance and transport equations to ensure consistency and reproducibility. All experiments were conducted under steady-state conditions, and each operating point was repeated three times (n = 3). The reported values represent mean results with associated standard deviations.

Carbon dioxide removal efficiency (η) was determined based on the inlet and outlet gas-phase CO₂ concentrations measured by gas chromatography (GC). Steady-state conditions were assumed after approximately 15 min of operation for each gas–liquid flow combination.

Overall mass-transfer coefficients were calculated from experimentally determined CO₂ fluxes using a resistance-in-series approach, where all area-normalized terms were referenced to the active membrane area (A = 0.012 m2, Table 1).

Specific energy consumption (SEC) was evaluated by accounting for the electrical power demand of the gas handling/compression unit and the gas composition measurement system; liquid pumping energy was not included because the liquid phase was supplied under gravity-driven operation.

Measurement uncertainties associated with the mass-flow controllers (± 1%) and rotameters (± 1%) were incorporated into the uncertainty analysis. Uncertainty related to GC-based gas composition measurements was addressed through standard uncertainty propagation procedures. Error bars correspond to ± 1 standard deviation.

The observed mass transfer behavior can be interpreted within the framework of classical film theory, where the overall resistance to CO₂ transfer is governed by the combined gas-side and liquid-side boundary layers. Under the investigated operating conditions, the gas-phase resistance was found to be relatively small due to the low gas flow rates, whereas the liquid-side film constituted the dominant mass transfer resistance, particularly at lower liquid velocities.

In the presence of Ca(OH)₂, CO₂ absorption is additionally influenced by reaction-enhanced mass transfer. Dissolved CO₂ reacts rapidly with hydroxide ions to form carbonate species, effectively reducing the interfacial CO₂ concentration and sustaining a high driving force across the membrane–liquid interface. This reaction-enhanced absorption mechanism explains the consistently higher mass transfer coefficients observed for Ca(OH)₂ compared to tap water under identical hydrodynamic conditions.

However, at higher liquid flow rates and prolonged operation, the formation of CaCO₃ precipitates may locally increase liquid-side resistance, partially offsetting the benefits of chemical enhancement. Therefore, the overall mass transfer performance reflects a balance between hydrodynamic film thinning, reaction-driven enhancement, and precipitation-induced resistance. These findings highlight the importance of simultaneously considering transport phenomena and reaction effects when evaluating alkaline absorbents in gravity-driven membrane contactor systems.

Results and discussion

Effect of gas flow rate on CO2 removal

The influence of gas flow rate on CO₂ removal efficiency was evaluated at three rates—122.5, 252, and 389 mL min−1—while maintaining a constant liquid flow of 1 000 mL min−1. As shown in Fig. 4, the lowest gas flow (122.5 mL min−1) yielded the highest removal, reaching 92.4% ± 0.8% with tap water and 93.4% ± 0.7% with Ca(OH)₂. This superior performance reflects the extended residence time, which enhances gas–liquid interfacial contact and absorption capacity. This observation is consistent with prior membrane-contactor literature, which reports that increasing gas throughput shortens gas residence time and can thereby reduce CO₂ removal efficiency under otherwise comparable operating conditions24.Fig. 4CO₂ removal efficiency (%) vs. gas flow rate (mL min−1) at fixed liquid flow of 1 000 mL min−1 for tap water (blue circles) and Ca(OH)₂ solution (orange squares). Data are mean ± 1 SD (n = 3).

CO₂ removal efficiency (%) vs. gas flow rate (mL min−1) at fixed liquid flow of 1 000 mL min−1 for tap water (blue circles) and Ca(OH)₂ solution (orange squares). Data are mean ± 1 SD (n = 3).

Increasing the gas flow to 252 mL min−1 led to a moderate decline in efficiency (tap water: 78.9% ± 1.0%; Ca(OH)₂: 80.2% ± 0.9%), due to reduced contact time and a thinner boundary layer. At the highest flow (389 mL min−1), removal dropped further to 64.8% ± 1.2% for tap water and 66.3% ± 1.1% for Ca(OH)₂, underscoring the impact of hydrodynamic limitations on mass‐transfer resistance in high‐velocity regimes.

Error bars represent ± 1 SD from three independent replicates (n = 3). These results confirm the theoretical expectation that lower gas velocities enhance interfacial mass transfer by increasing residence time and reducing boundary layer thickness.

Effect of liquid flow rate on CO2 removal

The influence of liquid flow rate on CO₂ removal was evaluated at a constant gas flow of 122.5 mL min−1—this flow corresponds to the peak gas-side efficiency observed previously. Liquid flow rates were varied from 250 to 1 000 mL min−1, and removal efficiencies are plotted in Fig. 5.Fig. 5CO₂ removal efficiency (%) versus liquid flow rate at a fixed gas flow of 122.5 mL min−1. Tap water (blue circles) and Ca(OH)₂ solution (orange squares) data are mean ± 1 SD (n = 3).

CO₂ removal efficiency (%) versus liquid flow rate at a fixed gas flow of 122.5 mL min−1. Tap water (blue circles) and Ca(OH)₂ solution (orange squares) data are mean ± 1 SD (n = 3).

At 250 mL min−1, tap water achieved 78.3% ± 1.1% removal, while Ca(OH)₂ reached 80.5% ± 0.9%. Increasing the liquid flow to 500 mL min−1 improved efficiencies to 85.4% ± 0.9% and 88.6% ± 0.8% for tap water and Ca(OH)₂, respectively. Further raising the flow to 1 000 mL min−1 yielded maximum removals of 92.1% ± 0.7% (tap water) and 94.1% ± 0.6% (Ca(OH)₂). This improvement is consistent with membrane-contactor literature, where higher liquid flow rates reduce the liquid-side mass-transfer resistance (via a thinner boundary layer and enhanced interfacial renewal), thereby increasing CO₂ absorption performanc25. However, the benefit of increasing liquid flow rate may plateau once the liquid-side boundary-layer resistance is sufficiently reduced and the overall mass-transfer resistance becomes controlled by the membrane and/or gas-side contributions, as also reported in membrane contactor studies showing weak sensitivity to liquid flow for higher-resistance membranes13,26.

The concentration of Ca(OH)₂ used in this study (0.1 wt%) was intentionally selected to balance chemical absorption efficiency and long-term operational stability. While higher Ca(OH)₂ concentrations could enhance CO₂ removal through increased availability of hydroxide ions, preliminary observations during extended operation indicated that elevated concentrations promote accelerated CaCO₃ precipitation. This precipitation progressively increases liquid-side mass transfer resistance and may partially block membrane pores, leading to performance deterioration over time.

Therefore, a relatively low Ca(OH)₂ concentration was adopted to minimize precipitation-induced fouling while maintaining high removal efficiency. Under the investigated operating conditions, the selected concentration enabled stable CO₂ removal exceeding 90% without severe membrane wetting or blockage during long-term testing. These findings highlight that Ca(OH)₂ concentration optimization involves a trade-off between instantaneous chemical absorption enhancement and sustainable membrane operation, which is particularly critical for gravity-driven, low-maintenance systems.

In the present study, tap water was deliberately selected as an absorbent to reflect realistic operating conditions for decentralized biogas upgrading systems, where the use of deionized or chemically purified water is often impractical. Unlike idealized laboratory solvents, tap water contains naturally occurring dissolved inorganic species, such as calcium and magnesium ions, which may influence gas–liquid mass transfer behavior through modifications in liquid-film properties rather than through direct chemical interaction with CO₂.

Previous studies have indicated that such impurities can alter hydrodynamic characteristics and interfacial resistance, although their impact is generally secondary compared to dominant parameters such as gas and liquid flow rates and contact time. Within the scope of this work, the observed trends in CO₂ removal efficiency were primarily governed by hydrodynamic and operational conditions, suggesting that the presence of typical tap water impurities did not dominate the overall absorption performance.

A detailed assessment of impurity-specific effects and water chemistry optimization was considered beyond the scope of the present study and is identified as a relevant direction for future investigation, particularly for long-term field-scale applications.

Notably, the gravity‐driven configuration employed here achieves comparable efficiencies without mechanical pumping, reducing energy use and system complexity—an important consideration for decentralized biogas upgrading. Additionally, the alkaline Ca(OH)₂ solution provides a strong chemical driving force: hydroxide ions react with dissolved CO₂ to form CaCO₃ precipitates, continuously restoring a steep concentration gradient and further enhancing absorption rates.

This analysis confirms that increasing liquid flow is an effective strategy to maximize CO₂ uptake, especially when combined with gravity‐driven operation and alkaline absorbents to minimize energy consumption.

Under the gravity-driven configuration employed in this study, the liquid phase was supplied to the shell side of the membrane module without pressurization, resulting in a falling-film type flow regime along the outer surface of the hollow fibers rather than a fully flooded (full-pipe) condition. In this regime, the liquid forms a thin film that flows downward under gravity, continuously renewing the gas–liquid interface.

Such a falling-film hydrodynamic behavior is known to reduce liquid-side mass transfer resistance by limiting boundary layer thickness and enhancing interfacial contact efficiency. This flow regime is particularly advantageous for gravity-driven membrane contactors, as it promotes effective CO₂ absorption while avoiding excessive liquid hold-up and minimizing the risk of membrane wetting. The observed increase in CO₂ removal efficiency with increasing liquid flow rate is therefore attributed not only to enhanced absorbent availability but also to improved interfacial mass transfer under falling-film conditions.

The implications of precipitation management strategies, including filtration or periodic solution replacement, are therefore identified as important considerations for future pilot- and field-scale applications.

Resource implications of gravity-driven operation

The gravity-driven operation fundamentally alters the energy and resource balance of membrane contactor systems by eliminating liquid-phase pumping requirements and simplifying system hydraulics. Within this framework, the specific energy consumption and associated resource demands were evaluated to assess the practical implications of the proposed configuration for decentralized biogas upgrading applications. Membrane contactors are widely discussed as a process-intensification route that can reduce footprint and potentially lower energy penalties relative to conventional absorption columns, while key operational challenges (e.g., wetting and long-term stability) remain central to practical deployment24,30.

As illustrated in Fig. 8, the specific energy consumption (Es) of the gravity-driven membrane contactor remained consistently below 0.6 kWh kg−1 CO₂ across all investigated gas and liquid flow conditions for both tap water and Ca(OH)₂ absorbents. The minimum Es value, approximately 0.45 kWh kg−1 CO₂, was obtained when using Ca(OH)₂ at a gas flow rate of 200 mL min−1 and a liquid flow rate of 800 mL min−1. Beyond energy performance, the implications of operating the system in a once-through, gravity-driven mode were also examined in terms of liquid and absorbent consumption. Under the representative operating condition employed in this study (Q_L = 48 L h−1, corresponding to a liquid flow rate of 800 mL min−1), the normalized liquid demand per unit volume of treated biogas was calculated as a function of gas flow rate. At the lowest investigated gas flow rate (122.5 mL min−1, approximately 0.00735 m3 h−1), the liquid consumption corresponded to approximately 6.5 m3 of water per m3 of treated biogas. Increasing the gas flow rate to 252 and 389 mL min−1 reduced the normalized liquid demand to approximately 3.2 and 2.0 m3 m−3, respectively, indicating a strong dependence of water consumption on gas throughput.

For experiments conducted with a Ca(OH)₂ concentration of 0.1 wt%, the corresponding chemical consumption scaled directly with liquid throughput. Depending on the selected gas flow rate, the estimated Ca(OH)₂ consumption ranged from approximately 2.06 to 6.53 kg per m3 of treated biogas. These values provide an order-of-magnitude assessment of alkali demand under once-through operation and offer practical insight into material requirements for decentralized implementation.

From an operational perspective, the once-through gravity-driven configuration offers clear advantages, including reduced system complexity, minimal maintenance requirements, and the avoidance of fouling risks associated with liquid recirculation. However, continuous consumption of water and alkaline absorbent may become a limiting factor for long-term or large-scale operation, particularly in rural or off-grid settings where water availability, chemical logistics, and solid management infrastructure are constrained.

Although beyond the scope of the present study, future system designs may integrate closed-loop liquid operation combined with controlled CaCO₃ precipitation management, filtration, or recovery as a value-added by-product. Such strategies have the potential to substantially reduce net liquid and chemical consumption while preserving the inherent low-energy advantages of gravity-driven membrane contactor systems.

In the present study, the gravity-driven operating mode is considered a reference configuration representing a lower-bound scenario for liquid-phase energy consumption, rather than a fully developed industrial process design.

Future studies that integrate gravity-assisted flow with controlled liquid recirculation and precipitation management strategies may further improve the industrial applicability of the proposed approach.

Conclusions and future work

This study systematically evaluated CO₂ removal from a synthetic CH₄/CO₂ (60/40 v/v) gas mixture using a gravity-driven polysulfone (PSf) hollow-fiber membrane contactor. Comparative experiments with tap water and Ca(OH)₂ absorbents revealed maximum CO₂ removal efficiencies of 92–93% and 94%, respectively. Under the optimum operating conditions (122.5 mL min−1 gas flow and 800 mL min−1 liquid flow), the specific energy consumption was 0.47 kWh kg−1 CO₂ for tap water and 0.45 kWh kg−1 CO₂ for Ca(OH)₂.

The specific energy consumption values reported in this study reflect the operational energy demand of the gravity-driven membrane contactor under laboratory-scale conditions and do not account for additional process steps such as absorbent regeneration or solid CaCO₃ handling and disposal, which would be required in a full-scale implementation.

Long-term testing conducted over 30 days (8 h day−1, approximately 240 h total) demonstrated that the gravity-driven membrane contactor sustains high CO₂ removal efficiency over extended operation, despite a gradual performance decline attributed to precipitation-related increases in liquid-side mass transfer resistance. Importantly, no abrupt efficiency loss or membrane wetting was observed during the testing period, indicating operational robustness rather than fully stabilized steady-state behavior.

The pump-free, gravity-driven design eliminates liquid-pumping energy, thereby simplifying system architecture and reducing operational complexity. The alkaline nature of Ca(OH)₂ enhances CO₂ absorption through rapid CO₂–OH− reactions and continuous CaCO₃ formation, which sustains a strong concentration gradient and promotes mass transfer. These characteristics highlight the potential of gravity-driven membrane contactors as modular and energy-efficient solutions for decentralized biogas upgrading.

Future work should focus on pilot-scale demonstrations to assess scale-up effects and real field conditions, as well as on precipitation management strategies such as periodic solution replacement or surface cleaning protocols. In addition, comprehensive techno-economic assessments—including capital expenditure, operating costs, and payback periods—will be essential to evaluate commercial viability. Comparative investigations of alternative membrane materials (e.g., PVDF and PTFE) and system integration strategies will further support the development of next-generation low-energy biomethane upgrading technologies.
