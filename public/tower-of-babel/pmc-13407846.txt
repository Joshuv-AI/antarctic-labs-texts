Effects of online collaborative learning and simulation-based learning on pedagogical knowledge and teaching efficacy in digital education
Authors: Chia-Chen Chang, Wen-Chi Wu, Li-Chu Chen, Ping-Hsiu Tsai, Chen-Yin Tung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs digital learning becomes increasingly central to teacher professional development, understanding how different online pedagogical models influence learning outcomes has become essential. This quasi-experimental study compared the effects of online collaborative learning (OCL) and simulation-based learning (SBL) on pedagogical knowledge and teaching efficacy in 674 primary and secondary school teachers in Taiwan (OCL group: n = 399; SBL group: n = 275). The OCL and SBL groups participated in a 3-h digital health education enhancement programme. The OCL group engaged in structured online collaboration and reflective dialogue, whereas the SBL group participated in scenario-based simulations that replicated authentic classroom situations. Paired-sample analyses indicated significantly higher pedagogical knowledge and teaching efficacy in both groups following the interventions. The OCL group exhibited greater gains in pedagogical knowledge, whereas the SBL group exhibited greater enhancement of teaching efficacy. Linear mixed-effects models controlling for demographic covariates confirmed distinct patterns of change in the groups. After adjustment, higher education level and specialisation in health education exhibited positive and negative associations, respectively, with teaching efficacy. Moreover, cognitive load was positively associated with knowledge gain but negatively associated with teaching efficacy. These findings highlight that OCL effectively supports teachers’ conceptual understanding and knowledge integration, whereas SBL improves their applied competence and instructional confidence through experiential learning. The results underscore the importance of aligning digital pedagogical approaches with professional development objectives and provide empirical evidence to guide differentiated, technology-integrated teacher training programmes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48620-1.Keywords: Digital education, Instructional efficacy, Online collaborative learning, Online teaching methods, Pedagogical knowledge, Simulation-based learningSubject terms: Education, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Advances in scientific knowledge and the COVID-19 pandemic have accelerated the digital transformation of health education, necessitating the development of instructional strategies that transcend traditional temporal and spatial constraints1,2. Although online healthcare initiatives have expanded, they predominantly target healthcare professionals or students, leaving a notable gap in terms of the professional development of health educators3,4. To bridge this gap, appropriate online platforms should be developed to empower in-service teachers and enhance their pedagogical capacity and learning efficiency5,6. Consequently, exploring digital instructional modes for advancing the training of health educators is critical.

Theoretical foundation

Collaborative learning (CL) is theoretically grounded in social constructivism7, which posits that knowledge is coconstructed through social interaction within a zone of proximal development. Traditional CL emphasises face-to-face peer engagement, whereas online CL (OCL) extends the framework of social constructivism by leveraging digital affordances to facilitate the construction of knowledge through asynchronous or synchronous discourse8,9. To achieve effective online instruction, a complete spectrum of professional knowledge, customised learning strategies, and infrastructure for interactive discourse must be integrated10,11.

The contribution of OCL to teacher education lies in its shift from passive information acquisition to active knowledge construction. Specifically, the collaborative nature of OCL fosters sustained peer interaction, shared reflection, and critical thinking, and OCL is thus different to self-paced or didactic online instruction8,12. By rendering pedagogical knowledge highly accessible and facilitating professional development through interactive modules, OCL enables educators to continually improve their professional expertise13,14. OCL can enable health educators to collaboratively manage complex interdisciplinary content, thereby enhancing their pedagogical capacity.

Simulation-based learning in teacher education

Simulation-based learning (SBL), a critical bridge between theoretical knowledge and real-life teaching scenarios, provides immersive environments for active experimentation15. Grounded in experiential learning theory16, SBL enables educators to practice decision-making and apply abstract health concepts in a safe, simulated context17,18. In contrast to conventional online learning, which often lacks interpersonal interaction, SBL enhances engagement and facilitates the application of knowledge in real classroom challenges19–21.

The unique contribution of this study lies in its comparison of OCL and SBL. OCL encourages social construction, reflection, and shared problem-solving12, whereas SBL offers the experiential practice required to develop instructional confidence22. These paradigms can collectively combine collaborative and experiential learning to meet the complex professional development needs of health educators23. Drawing upon established pedagogical design sequences that prioritise information delivery, iterative discourse, and knowledge building8,24, the present study operationalised an integrated OCL–SBL framework. The primary objective was to enhance health educators’ pedagogical competence and instructional efficacy, thereby addressing the complexities inherent in digital health curricula21,25.

Sociodemographic factors and cognitive load

Despite their advantages, digital approaches introduce complexities by increasing the cognitive demands made of learners, which can negatively affect learning motivation26,27. The nature of cognitive load differs considerably across instructional modes. OCL requires teachers to integrate multiple collaborative information streams, whereas SBL involves real-time, immersive decision-making28. Furthermore, according to cognitive load theory29 and the expertise reversal effect30, an individual’s prior knowledge and teaching experience determine the mental effort required by them to process different digital learning modalities. Understanding how sociodemographic characteristics interact with cognitive load is essential for optimising the design of digital tools to ensure that these tools support rather than overwhelm health educators. In this study, gender, educational background, and teaching experience were selected as key sociodemographic variables on the basis of the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology, which identifies these factors as critical moderators of technology-enhanced learning efficacy31.

Research questions

To address the aforementioned gaps in digital professional development for health educators, this study developed and evaluated a systematised framework integrating OCL and SBL. By examining the interplay between the instructional modes, cognitive load, and individual characteristics, this research obtained empirical evidence indicating how teacher training can be optimised in the digital age. This study was guided by the following research questions:

What are the effects of OCL and SBL on the assimilation of medical-resource-related pedagogical knowledge in health educators?

Methods

This study employed a quasi-experimental design with preintervention and postintervention assessments to evaluate the instructional effectiveness of two digital learning approaches: OCL and SBL. To ensure that all participating teachers received professional development regarding health policy, an active control group was established rather than a nonintervention control group. This approach allowed for assessment of the comparative effectiveness of the two pedagogical strategies while adhering to ethical standards relating to equitable educational access. In addition, the primary aim of this study was to compare the relative effectiveness of OCL and SBL rather than to contrast intervention and nonintervention conditions within the context of a real-world professional development programme.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of National Taiwan Normal University, Taipei (Approval No. 202106HS006; approved on September 10, 2021). Data were collected through an anonymous online survey. Potential participants received an introductory information sheet, and submission of a completed questionnaire implied consent for participation; thus, written informed consent was not required. No personally identifiable information was collected, and all data were recorded anonymously. All procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

The participants were primary and junior high school teachers in Taiwan who had joined a digital teaching improvement programme. They were assigned to an OCL group or SBL group (Fig. 1). Rather than being randomly assigned, the participants voluntarily enrolled in the learning cohort they preferred given their schedule and learning preference. This self-selection approach was used to respect teachers’ autonomy and ensure full participation throughout the programme.

Participant counts in this study.

In total, 432 and 340 teachers participated in the OCL and SBL interventions, respectively. Teachers who did not complete both the preintervention and postintervention questionnaires and those who failed to attend all instructional sessions were excluded, leading to a final sample of 674 teachers (OCL: n = 399; SBL: n = 275). The required minimum sample size was estimated at 251 by using G*Power 3.1 (omnibus one-way analysis of variance with fixed effects) under the assumption of a small effect size (f = 0.10), an α value of 0.01, statistical power of 0.98, and two predictors. This conservative specification was used to ensure adequate power for detecting small effects in a large and heterogeneous teacher population. To verify the adequacy of the sampling, sensitivity analysis was conducted under the aforementioned assumptions (α = 0.05, statistical power = 0.80, and two groups). The smallest detectable effect size was f = 0.11 (η2 ≈ 0.01), indicating sufficient sensitivity for detecting weak between-group effects32. The observed effect sizes (η2 ≈ 0.02–0.03) exceeded this threshold, confirming that the sample size was adequate for making meaningful inferences. The aforementioned approach aligns with methodological guidance that prioritises sensitivity reporting over post hoc power estimation33.

Intervention procedures

Virtual instruction was conducted through Google Meet, with the total teaching duration per cohort being 3 h. The curriculum was designed in accordance with established principles of virtual pedagogy34 and incorporated structured learning stages such as provision of information, occasional communication, online discussion, and knowledge-building tasks. The participants in the two groups used identical instructional materials related to ‘A Smart Medical Visit’s Six Essentials’ and the MediQuest board game to strengthen their decision-making and health policy awareness.

Although they accessed the same content, the two groups differed in terms of the instructional strategies that were used (Table 1). The focus in the OCL group was collaborative content navigation and peer discussion, whereas that in the SBL group was scenario-based contextualisation and digital role-play (Fig. 2). The OCL and SBL interventions are described in the following:

A.OCL intervention: The teacher participants engaged in CL through an online discussion platform. For knowledge acquisition, instructors used demonstration videos, and the participants could collaboratively navigate the content and discuss open-ended questions (e.g., causes of health insurance collapse). In the teaching efficacy phase, the group explored multimedia resources and shared lesson plan ideas through a collaborative Q&A board, constructing knowledge through peer interaction.

OCL intervention: The teacher participants engaged in CL through an online discussion platform. For knowledge acquisition, instructors used demonstration videos, and the participants could collaboratively navigate the content and discuss open-ended questions (e.g., causes of health insurance collapse). In the teaching efficacy phase, the group explored multimedia resources and shared lesson plan ideas through a collaborative Q&A board, constructing knowledge through peer interaction.

B.SBL intervention: In the SBL group, an experiential simulation format was adopted for instruction. Instructors delivered content through scenario-based presentations with clinical simulation examples, providing contextualised learning experiences. Instead of open discussion, the participants engaged in digital role-play scenarios that simulated medical-resource-related decision-making. Real-time feedback was provided by the instructors after each simulation phase to guide the participants’ reflection.

SBL intervention: In the SBL group, an experiential simulation format was adopted for instruction. Instructors delivered content through scenario-based presentations with clinical simulation examples, providing contextualised learning experiences. Instead of open discussion, the participants engaged in digital role-play scenarios that simulated medical-resource-related decision-making. Real-time feedback was provided by the instructors after each simulation phase to guide the participants’ reflection.

Assessment tools

The assessment tools developed and used in this study were a multiple-choice, medical-resource-related pedagogical knowledge test and two scales measuring medical resource teaching efficacy and cognitive load, respectively; these tools were administered both before and after the interventions. Demographic variable data were also collected.

Test for assessing medical-resource-related pedagogical knowledge

The participants’ understanding of the National Health Insurance programme of Taiwan was measured using a five-item multiple-choice test. The test covered five essential domains: universal coverage, the principle of mutual aid, ability-based payments, attention to the disadvantaged, and limitations on resource distribution. Each item had a single correct answer, and 1 point was awarded per correct response, with no penalty for incorrect answers. An example item is ‘Which of the following groups cannot join the National Health Insurance programme?’. The test items were discovered to have acceptable discriminative power of 26.18 (p < 0.001). Moreover, the content validity index (CVI) of the test was 0.88, exceeding the recommended threshold and indicating satisfactory content validity35.

Medical resource teaching efficacy scale

Teaching efficacy was assessed using a 27-item scale designed to measure the teacher participants’ self-perceived competence in medical resource education. The participants responded to the scale items on the basis of a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The 27-item scale comprised four subdimensions: lesson preparation, material application, diverse strategies, and atmosphere cultivation. Example items are ‘I can design health insurance education activities suited to my students’ needs’ and ‘I can effectively apply teaching materials related to the National Health Insurance programme’. Higher scores reflected higher perceived teaching efficacy. The CVI for the aforementioned scale was 0.92, and its internal consistency reliability was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.95). Although the high Cronbach’s α value of 0.95 suggests potential item overlap, it likely reflects conceptual relatedness among the subdimensions rather than redundancy; the CVI does support the content relevance of the items36.

Cognitive load scale

The participants’ cognitive load during online learning was assessed using an eight-item self-report scale developed by the research team. The same digital environment was used in the OCL and SBL interventions. Each item in the cognitive load scale was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), and the sum of all item scores ranged from 8 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater perceived cognitive load. A representative item is ‘I had to exert considerable effort to understand the content presented in the intervention’. The cognitive load scale’s CVI was 0.86, and its Cronbach’s α value was 0.97, indicating excellent internal consistency reliability. Although this high Cronbach’s α value suggests potential item overlap, it likely reflects the breadth and complexity of cognitive load in digital learning contexts. The CVI of 0.86 supported the content relevance of the items35. Overall, all the instruments used in this study were discovered to have strong psychometric properties and were appropriate for evaluating teachers’ learning outcomes in an online instructional context37–40.

Demographic variables

Data were collected on four demographic variables in this study: gender, age, educational level, and teaching experience.

Data analysis

All analyses were conducted using SAS. Descriptive statistics were used to analyse demographic variables. Moreover, paired-samples t tests were performed to examine within-group differences between preintervention and postintervention scores.

To evaluate the effects of the interventions, multivariable linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were fitted for each outcome variable, with a random intercept incorporated for each participant to account for within-subject correlations. Each model included fixed effects for study group (SBL vs. OCL), time (postintervention period vs. preintervention period), their two-way interaction (group × time), and four prespecified covariates (gender, educational level, teaching field, school location, school level, and cognitive load). We controlled for these covariates to isolate the interventions’ effects because the literature links educational background to digital efficacy38,41, gender to digital interaction styles37,40, and experience to technology self-efficacy37,39. The categorical covariates were dummy-coded (gender: female = 1, male = 0; educational level: undergraduate = 0, postgraduate = 1). The group × time interaction term represented the between-group difference in the change from the preintervention period to the postintervention period.

To ensure alignment between the research questions, assessment tools, and analytical strategies, pedagogical knowledge and teaching efficacy were treated as outcome variables corresponding to Research Questions 1 and 2, respectively, whereas Research Question 3 was addressed by incorporating the cognitive load and sociodemographic variables as covariates in the LMMs.

Results

Comparative analysis of background variables

Male teachers constituted 20.8% and 11.64% of the OCL and SBL groups, respectively (p< 0.01). Individuals aged over 41 years accounted for 62.91% and 67.27% of these groups, respectively (p = 0.244). Teachers with a postgraduate degree comprised 57.39% and 66.18% of the OCL and SBL groups, respectively (p< 0.05). Moreover, teachers with more than 10 years of experience accounted for 62.41% and 66.18% of these groups, respectively (p = 0.316). The number of health education teachers in the OCL group was significantly lower than that in the SBL group (20.3% vs. 68.36%, p< 0.001). Teachers from urban schools represented 65.66% of the OCL group and 42.18% of the SBL group (p <0.001). Primary and senior high school teachers constituted 90.23% and 9.77%, respectively, of the OCL group and 39.27% and 60.73%, respectively, of the SBL group (p < 0.001). In addition, teachers in schools with more than 20 classes represented 64.91% of the OCL group and 60% of the SBL group (p = 0.195). Finally, the average scores of the OCL and SBL groups were 15.47% and 26.67%, respectively (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Pedagogical knowledge and teaching efficacy

Paired-samples t tests revealed significant improvements in pedagogical knowledge following the interventions. The average scores in the OCL and SBL groups increased from 0.67 [standard deviation (SD) = 0.20)] to 0.85 (SD = 0.17; p < 0.001) and from 0.68 (SD = 0.17) to 0.73 (SD = 0.16; p < 0.001), respectively. Furthermore, the mean teaching efficacy score increased from 73.73 (SD = 9.95) to 77.32 (SD = 9.68) in the OCL group (p < 0.001) and from 67.71 (SD = 12.73) to 75.69 (SD = 10.12) in the SBL group (p < 0.001), indicating that both interventions significantly enhanced the teachers’ confidence and perceived capacity to engage and instruct their students effectively (Table 3).

Effects of background variables on pedagogical knowledge and teaching efficacy

The LMMs, which controlled for the background variables, revealed that time, group, and the group × time interaction had significant main effects on both pedagogical knowledge and teaching efficacy.

The background variables had different effects on pedagogical knowledge and teaching efficacy. Female teachers significantly outscored their male counterparts on pedagogical knowledge [β = − 0.04, 95% confidence interval (CI) = − 0.07 to − 0.01, p = 0.008), indicating that gender had a significant influence on this outcome variable. Moreover, pedagogical knowledge had a significant positive association with cognitive load (β = 0.004, 95% CI = 0.003–0.01, p < 0.001). By contrast, teaching efficacy was positively associated with a postgraduate qualification (β = 2.79, 95% CI = 1.30–4.28, p < 0.001) but negatively associated with health education specialisation (β = − 2.47, 95% CI = − 4.20 to − 0.75, p = 0.005) and cognitive load (β = − 0.11, 95% CI = − 0.20 to − 0.01, p = 0.030). School-related factors had no significant effects on either outcome variable.

The results indicated that group (β = 0.05, 95% CI = 0.02–0.09, p = 0.004) and time (β = 0.17, 95% CI = 0.15–0.19, p < 0.001) had significant positive main effects on pedagogical knowledge. The interaction analysis revealed that the OCL group exhibited a greater improvement in pedagogical knowledge than did the SBL group (group × time: β = − 0.12, 95% CI = − 0.15 to − 0.09, p < 0.001). Group (β = − 5.12, 95% CI = − 7.30 to − 2.94, p < 0.001) and time (β = 3.59, 95% CI = 2.75–4.44, p < 0.001) also had significant main effects on teaching efficacy. The interaction effect indicated that the SBL group exhibited a greater improvement in teaching efficacy than did the OCL group (group × time: β = 4.39, 95% CI = 3.06–5.71, p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrated that both OCL and SBL significantly enhanced the teacher participants’ pedagogical knowledge and teaching efficacy, albeit through distinct learning mechanisms. The OCL group engaged with a structured online collaborative platform that emphasised peer interaction and reflective dialogue42 and thereby supported their conceptual understanding of medical resource allocation and policy design. By contrast, the SBL group navigated instructor-guided simulated environments that replicated real classroom challenges43, enabling them to apply decision-making strategies in realistic medical resource scenarios.

OCL was discovered to result in greater gains in pedagogical knowledge than was SBL, likely because the OCL intervention emphasised systematic knowledge organisation and structured peer-based learning17. Such collaborative structuring fosters cognitive elaboration and deepens conceptual understanding12,25,43. Other research indicated that online collaborative environments promote the internalisation of professional knowledge by integrating the cognitive and social dimensions of learning18. The interactive nature of online collaborative platforms reinforces active participation and sustained engagement, contributing to the retention of pedagogical knowledge25,44.

Interaction analyses clarified the distinct trajectories of the two learning outcomes examined in this study. Significant group × time interactions were discovered for both pedagogical knowledge and teaching efficacy, suggesting that OCL and SBL affected the learning process in different but complementary ways. Compared with the SBL group, the OCL group exhibited greater improvement in pedagogical knowledge, reflecting the enhancement of conceptual clarity through collaboration42. Conversely, the SBL group exhibited larger postintervention gains in teaching efficacy, highlighting the value of experiential simulation in translating knowledge into practice17,45. This pattern supports the argument that the two modalities address different aspects of teacher training: OCL cultivates cognitive mastery, whereas SBL reinforces performative competence and confidence through embodied practice.

The pronounced improvement in teaching efficacy in the SBL group aligns with the findings of studies demonstrating that simulation and experiential learning enhance self-efficacy, problem-solving, and reflective behaviours17,45. By enabling teachers to navigate realistic decision-making scenarios, SBL strengthens the affective and behavioural dimensions of professional growth, which are often insufficiently addressed in purely cognitive learning environments46,47. Moreover, the collaborative discourse inherent in OCL enables teachers to refine their instructional reasoning, synthesise knowledge, and critically appraise strategies. The aforementioned findings collectively suggest that the combination of OCL and SBL is the optimal approach for professional development and can effectively integrate conceptual depth with contextual applicability.

An analysis of background variables provided further insights into the conditions influencing digital teaching efficacy. Teachers with a postgraduate qualification had significantly higher teaching efficacy scores than did those with a lower-level qualification. This finding aligns with a study indicating that higher educational levels are associated with superior digital literacy and greater mastery of online pedagogical techniques41. By contrast, health education specialists exhibited smaller gains in teaching efficacy following the intervention than did teachers with other specialisations, potentially reflecting domain-specific constraints or pre-existing expectations that limited perceived improvement. Regarding gender, female teachers had significantly higher pedagogical knowledge scores than did their male counterparts. This finding aligns with research suggesting that female educators often express higher confidence in their ability to facilitate online courses and promote cognitive engagement in students40, and high confidence is critical for knowledge construction in OCL contexts.

Cognitive load was positively associated with pedagogical knowledge but negatively associated with teaching efficacy in the present study. This suggests that although a certain level of germane load is required for deep cognitive processing, the perception of excessive extraneous load may undermine teachers’ confidence in their performance27. This finding underscores the importance of designing online environments that balance complexity with usability.

Overall, the research findings highlight how different digital learning approaches address complementary aspects of professional growth. OCL fosters collaborative knowledge construction through peer interaction, aiding conceptual clarification, whereas SBL enhances applied competence by situating teacher training within adaptive, scenario-based contexts. Building on these insights, professional development programmes should integrate OCL and SBL within a hybrid learning framework. For instance, an OCL module can be initially used to cultivate theoretical and reflective engagement through peer dialogue42, following which SBL sessions can be conducted to allow participants to apply their knowledge in simulated contexts. Such an approach would enhance knowledge retention and transfer and bridge the gap between digital learning and classroom implementation, creating a sustainable model for continual professional growth.

Although this study makes several contributions, it has certain limitations that warrant consideration. First, the unequal distributions of gender and specialisation in the OCL group versus SBL group may have introduced residual confounding even though these variables were statistically controlled for in the LMM analysis. Second, the self-reported measures may have been influenced by social desirability bias48. Third, the absence of a nonintervention control group represents a methodological limitation. Although the active control design ensured equitable access to training, threats to internal validity, such as history or maturation effects, could not be entirely ruled out. However, to mitigate these risks, the instructional content and duration were strictly standardised, ensuring that the observed differences were attributable to the specific pedagogical modalities rather than extraneous factors. Fourth, this study examined only short-term effects; longitudinal designs are required to evaluate the sustained effects of OCL and SBL on teachers’ identity formation, their classroom practice, and students’ learning outcomes. Future research may also explore adaptive hybrid models that allow adjustment of the balance between collaboration and simulation in accordance with participants’ experience levels and learning preferences.

Conclusion

This study examined the effects of OCL and SBL on teachers’ pedagogical knowledge and teaching efficacy. Its results indicated that both OCL and SBL significantly improved teachers’ pedagogical knowledge and teaching efficacy, but through distinct mechanisms. Compared with SBL, OCL fostered greater gains in pedagogical knowledge by promoting structured collaboration, reflective dialogue, and cognitive integration. By contrast, SBL facilitated greater improvement in teaching efficacy by providing immersive, scenario-based practice that translates conceptual understanding into applied competence. These findings collectively suggest that OCL and SBL can serve complementary functions in teacher professional development, enhancing cognitive mastery and professional self-efficacy, respectively.

An analysis of background variables enabled refinement of the aforementioned insights. A postgraduate qualification was positively associated with teaching efficacy, whereas specialisation in health education was negatively associated with this outcome variable. Gender differences were also discovered, with female teachers having greater pedagogical knowledge than male teachers did. Furthermore, cognitive load was positively associated with pedagogical knowledge but negatively associated with teaching efficacy, underscoring the delicate balance required between stimulating mental engagement and avoiding cognitive overload. From a theoretical perspective, these findings align with the principles of constructivist and situated learning, emphasising that effective digital pedagogy should integrate both collaborative reflection and experiential simulation.

The findings of this study suggest that educational institutions should adopt a hybrid approach that strategically combines OCL with SBL to strengthen both the conceptual and practical dimensions of health educator training. Structured collaboration can be employed to establish theoretical foundations, following which simulation modules can be adopted to enable teachers to apply knowledge in realistic contexts. Such an integrated design can improve knowledge retention, learning engagement, and the application of knowledge to classroom practice. Future research should employ longitudinal and mixed-method designs to determine whether these effects can be sustained and identify the optimal sequencing of collaborative and experiential learning in digital teacher training programmes.
