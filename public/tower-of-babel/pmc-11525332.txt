Management of pulmonary hypertension in special conditions
Authors: Ioana R. Preston, Luke S. Howard, David Langleben, Mona Lichtblau, Tomas Pulido, Rogerio Souza, Karen M. Olsson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Care of pulmonary hypertension (PH) patients in special situations requires insightful knowledge of the pathophysiology of the cardiopulmonary system and close interaction with different specialists, depending on the situation. The role of this task force was to gather knowledge about five conditions that PH patients may be faced with. These conditions are 1) perioperative care; 2) management of pregnancy; 3) medication adherence; 4) palliative care; and 5) the influence of climate on PH. Many of these aspects have not been covered by previous World Symposia on Pulmonary Hypertension. All of the above conditions are highly affected by psychological, geographical and socioeconomic factors, and share the need for adequate healthcare provision. The task force identified significant gaps in information and research in these areas. The current recommendations are based on detailed literature search and expert opinion. The task force calls for further studies and research to better understand and address the special circumstances that PH patients may encounter.

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a complex clinical condition that requires a multidisciplinary approach for its appropriate diagnosis and management. Furthermore, there are special situations where insightful knowledge of the pathophysiology of the cardiopulmonary system and close interaction with different specialists become even more significant. The role of this task force was to gather knowledge about five particular conditions that PH patients may be faced with. These conditions are 1) perioperative care; 2) management of pregnancy; 3) medication adherence; 4) palliative care; and 5) the influence of climate on PH.

Perioperative care of patients with PH

The presence of PH confers an increased risk for patients who undergo surgery. Associated risks include right ventricular (RV) decompensation and cardiogenic shock, arrhythmias, cardiac arrest, need for prolonged mechanical support, need for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), and death. A survey of 17 million patients from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project National Inpatient Sample data from 2004 to 2014 undergoing major noncardiac surgery identified PH as an independent risk factor for in-hospital death, myocardial infarction and ischaemic stroke [1]. The perioperative risk did not decline over time. PAH group 1 patients had additional increased risk of pulmonary embolism, cardiogenic shock and cardiac arrest. A prospective, 3-year questionnaire-based survey collected data on 114 patients who underwent major surgery from 2007 to 2010 [2]. 43% were in functional class III/IV. 82% of the interventions were performed under general anaesthesia and 18% under spinal anaesthesia. Major complications occurred in 6.1% of the patients and overall perioperative mortality was 3.5%. However, the mortality rate was 15% in emergency procedures, compared with 2% in nonemergency procedures. Risk factors for major complications were an elevated right atrial pressure, a prior 6-min walk distance <399 m, the need for vasopressors and the need for emergency surgery. A retrospective cohort from a large single centre conducted between 2010–2017 identified 196 procedures requiring general anaesthesia in 131 patients with pre-capillary PH (groups 1, 3, 4) [3]. 27% of patients had complications, including four deaths. There were more complications in those undergoing moderate- to high-risk versus low-risk procedures. The multivariate analysis established a higher complication risk for patients with World Health Organization (WHO) functional class III/IV and elevated serum N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels.

Assessing risk for perioperative complications in PH patients

Validated risk scores to identify surgical PH patients at the greatest risk of complications have not been developed to date; furthermore, risk scores validated for other conditions may not be applicable to PH. Therefore, we recommend incorporating an assessment of PH severity in the setting of risks conferred by the particular surgery. Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) group 1 assessment can use the validated risk scores, such as the French predictive equation [4], COMPERA 2.0 [5], REVEAL 2.0 and REVEAL Lite 2 [6], acknowledging that the risk scores have not been studied in PAH patients undergoing surgery. A comprehensive echocardiographic assessment with focus on right chamber evaluation is a strong expert recommendation for all types of PH. Noninvasive cardiac parameters associated with increased risk of mortality reported in a retrospective study of PAH patients were right-axis deviation, RV hypertrophy, RV index of myocardial performance ≥0.75 and RV systolic pressure/systemic systolic blood pressure ≥0.66 [7]. Presence of comorbidities, especially cardiopulmonary conditions, should be taken into consideration. Surgical risk factors in PAH patients include the need for emergent surgery, for intraoperative vasopressors, and operating time >3 h [7]. Lastly, a surgical checklist has been associated with improved outcomes in all patients [8] and could be implemented in PH patients. A risk score to predict the perioperative mortality risk has been developed but requires further validation [9]. This score includes three low-risk noninvasive variables (WHO functional class, 6-min walking distance and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) or NT-proBNP) and the procedural risk variables based on American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association perioperative guidelines. These included inherent risk of major cardiovascular events (“elevated” or ≥1% risk versus “low” or <1% risk), emergency procedure, length of procedure (>3 h), and location of the patient immediately prior to the procedure (inpatient versus outpatient). The score was calculated by adding a patient-level component and a procedural component. Perioperative care

Several recent reviews describe in detail management of PH patients undergoing surgery [10, 11]. Prior to surgery, PH patients should avoid interruption of targeted therapies. It is essential to avoid hypervolaemia (more commonly seen) or hypovolaemia prior to surgery, to maximise cardiac performance and tissue perfusion during and after surgery. For elective surgeries, PH treatment should be optimised in an outpatient setting. In selected PAH patients with more severe haemodynamic impairment, admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) and placement of a pulmonary artery (PA) catheter may help in assessing and optimising volume status and potential need for short-term prostacyclin infusion. The use of a PA catheter intraoperatively should remain at the discretion and expertise of the anaesthesiologist in severe PAH patients. In general, we recommend using a central venous catheter and maintaining adequate systemic pressures using vasopressor support, if necessary, and avoiding fluid administration. Central venous access can also be used to monitor mixed venous saturation (SvO2), which is influenced by cardiac output and haemoglobin; decreases in SvO2 should prompt further evaluation of cardiac function and/or haemoglobin levels. For patients on parenteral prostacyclins, the dose does usually not need manipulation intraoperatively. Moreover, infusion interruptions should absolutely be avoided. However, post-operatively, if RV failure occurs, prostacyclin dose may be increased. Intraoperative administration of inhaled nitric oxide or epoprostenol can aid in controlling PA pressures [12].

Among the vasopressors available, we recommend norepinephrine and/or vasopressin, both of which have minimal effects on pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) in low doses. Inotropic agents can be used to enhance RV contractility, usually in conjunction with vasopressors, to minimise systemic vasodilation.

Careful consideration for the type of anaesthesia is an important step. Both general anaesthesia and intubation, and spinal anaesthesia may result in haemodynamic instability. Multimodal anaesthesia, including regional and neuroaxial anaesthesia and pain control, may avoid intubation and reduce the risk of haemodynamic instability. If general anaesthesia is required, we recommend rapid sequence intubation and avoidance of high positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP). Post-operatively, PH patients may require intensive monitoring of RV function; transient RV dysfunction may occur, even in previously compensated PH patients. Temporary mechanical circulatory support may be required post-operatively. For patients at high risk of RV failure, the team may consider placing the cannulas pre-operatively. However, even in patients with compensated RV, the stress of surgery can induce RV-plegia post-operatively and the need for pressor support for a few days is not uncommon. In this setting, transient hypoxaemia may occur and last for days, requiring oxygen supplementation. Persistent hypoxaemia, particularly during positive pressure ventilation may raise the question of a persistent foramen ovale. Careful monitoring in the days following surgery is crucial to avoid/treat RV decompensation.

Pregnancy in PH patients

Historically, the prognosis for pregnant women with PH was fraught with considerable risks, including high maternal mortality and adverse fetal outcomes. However, with a more comprehensive understanding of the pathophysiology and advancements in medical management, the outcomes have shown a promising shift. Improved risk stratification, meticulous monitoring and tailored interventions have contributed to mitigating some of the risks associated with PH during pregnancy. Nevertheless, despite these advancements, pregnancy in PH remains challenging. The delicate balance between maternal wellbeing and fetal risks necessitates individualised approaches, often requiring a multidisciplinary team comprising of obstetricians, anaesthetists, cardiologists, pulmonologists, neonatologists and other specialised care providers.

The 2022 European PH guidelines have issued the following recommendations for women of childbearing potential and PAH: 1) women of childbearing potential should be counselled at the time of diagnosis about the risks and uncertainties associated with becoming pregnant; 2) contraceptive advice should be offered; and 3) women with PAH who consider pregnancy or who become pregnant should receive prompt counselling in an experienced PH centre to facilitate genetic counselling and shared decision-making [13, 14].

Previous PH guidelines have issued recommendations that women with PAH should avoid becoming pregnant and those who become pregnant should consider termination [15–18]. The third recommendation was a major difference. The main reasons for this change were improved pregnancy outcomes in women with PAH and other forms of PH, but also the notion that shared decision-making has become increasingly important in clinical medicine.

During the 7th World Symposium for Pulmonary Hypertension, task force members reviewed publications on pregnancy outcomes in women with PH published since the last World Symposium, which took place in early 2018. The results of this literature search were used to update recommendations for PH and pregnancy.

Maternal and fetal pregnancy outcomes in PH

Between 2017 and 2023, several case series have been published describing maternal and fetal pregnancy outcomes of pregnancies in women with various types of PH (table 1). Most publications came from developing countries, predominantly China, and comprised >3500 cases. The diagnosis of PH was usually based on echocardiography, and PH severity was determined by the estimated systolic pulmonary artery pressure. Congenital heart disease and left heart disease (predominantly rheumatic heart disease) were the leading causes of PH. Maternal mortality ranged from 2% to 40%, with mortality risk being particularly high in women with Eisenmenger syndrome, echocardiographic signs of severe PH and a presumed diagnosis of idiopathic PAH (IPAH). Neonatal mortality ranged from 0 to 15%.

Only seven publications on pregnancy outcomes in women with PH were retrieved from developed countries (USA, Western Europe, Australia, Korea), comprising 156 cases. IPAH and congenital heart disease PAH were the leading diagnoses. Maternal mortality was 0 is most series and 16% in one study [19], while there were no neonatal deaths.

A meta-analysis of studies mainly from China and India published between January 1990 and April 2023 found a significant decrease in maternal mortality in patients with mild PH before and after 2010, but no change in mortality over time in patients with moderate or severe PH [20]. Another meta-analysis [21] including studies from developing and developed countries published between 2008 and 2018 found a maternal mortality of 12%. Of note, only 48% of the patients in this analysis received any kind of PH therapy.

Several findings can be derived from these data: 1) Despite continued improvement, pregnancy in women with PH continues to be associated with a considerable mortality risk.2) Both the maternal and the fetal/neonatal risks associated with pregnancy in women with PH vary substantially and are associated with PH type, disease severity and available medical resources.3) In pregnant women with mild PH and in those whose disease is well controlled with medical therapy, the maternal and fetal/neonatal mortality risks are low.4) The likelihood of maternal and fetal/neonatal complications is particularly high in pregnant women with Eisenmenger syndrome and in women with severe, poorly controlled PH, especially in settings where medical resources are limited.

Management of pregnancy in women with PH

The following section addresses the management of pregnant women with PH with a particular emphasis on women with PAH. Several statements are based on the authors’ clinical experience rather than on robust evidence.

Supportive measures

General supportive measures of pregnant women with PH include the use of diuretics as needed and the use of oxygen in patients who are hypoxaemic, with special attention to avoiding fetal hypoxaemia. Iron supplementation should be given as needed. Anticoagulation is not generally recommended, but should be considered in women with a heightened risk of thromboembolic complications.

Monitoring during pregnancy

Pregnant women with PH should be regularly monitored at the PH centre. Monitoring intervals depend on disease severity and pregnancy stage and may range from a few days to 2–3 months. Basic monitoring includes clinical assessment, echocardiography and BNP or NT-proBNP measurements. Increasing dyspnoea on exertion and fluid retention during pregnancy are uncommon in the first two trimesters and should be considered a warning sign at this stage. However, in the third trimester, these symptoms are more ambiguous as they also occur in healthy women.

Close echocardiographic monitoring of right heart function is essential during pregnancy. However, increases in systolic PA pressures and right chamber dimensions may reflect physiological adaptive changes to the increased volume load during pregnancy, and must not necessarily be interpreted as signs of disease progression, especially if RV function remains stable. Some of parameters to assess RV are tricuspid annulus plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), TAPSE/systolic PA pressure ratio, fractional area of change and visual RV-contraction [22].

BNP/NT-proBNP measurements are highly valuable. These biomarkers remain stable during an uncomplicated pregnancy and any increase beyond the normal variability should be interpreted as a warning sign.

Right heart catheterisation is usually not necessary during pregnancy unless the PH diagnosis is made during pregnancy, or when the results are expected to change management.

PAH therapy during pregnancy

In women with PAH who become pregnant, medical therapy often needs to be adapted. Endothelin receptor antagonists (ERAs), riociguat and selexipag have a teratogenic potential and are thus considered contraindicated during pregnancy, although human data are limited. Clinical experience suggests that ERA and selexipag can be safely paused during pregnancy in most patients and reinstituted after delivery (provided that the patient is not breastfeeding). Phospodiesterase-5 inhibitors and prostacyclin analogues are considered safe and therefore form the mainstays of PAH treatment during pregnancy. The use of these therapies has to be individualised. In case of maternal deterioration after the first trimester, ERAs were reinstituted after careful risk assessment in isolated cases [23]. In women with well-controlled PAH, the clinical course is usually stable during pregnancy. Clinicians have expressed the notion that there may be factors, perhaps circulating hormones, protecting women with PH from clinical deterioration during pregnancy. In fact, clinical deterioration in a pregnant woman with PH may be an indicator of fetal problems such as imminent abortion. However, there is a recognised risk of PAH deterioration within the first 2 years after a pregnancy.

An emerging new treatment for PAH is sotatercept, an activin signalling inhibitor [24–27]. Pregnancy was an exclusion criterion in clinical trials with sotatercept; hence, human data are unavailable. With the haemodynamic and clinical benefits observed with sotatercept, it is expected that pregnancy will become a topic in some women receiving this treatment. However, sotatercept may have adverse effects on fertility, as it decreases follicle-stimulating hormone levels. In addition, activin signalling is crucial for the development of oocytes, as well as for the maintenance of the uterine endometrium during implantation and pregnancy [28, 29]. In female rats, sotatercept reduced fertility and fecundity. In addition, rat fetal body weight was reduced and ossification delayed. In offsprings exposed to sotatercept via milk, delays in weight gain and prolonged time to sexual maturation were observed at high dose levels (≥5 mg·kg−1).

Based on the available animal data, it is possible that women treated with sotatercept may have difficulties conceiving, and fetal adverse events of sotatercept cannot be excluded. The long half-life of sotatercept, which is detectable in the organism for ≥2 months after the last administration, makes fetal exposure inevitable in unplanned pregnancies. Hence, effective contraception is crucial in women of childbearing potential treated with sotatercept. Women treated with sotatercept who become pregnant or consider becoming pregnant should be counselled about the uncertainties surrounding the use of sotatercept and pregnancy, including the fact that the safety of withholding sotatercept for longer time periods is unknown.

Delivery and post-partum care

In pregnant women with PH, the delivery period and, even more so, the immediate post-partum hours are often challenging. The risk of haemodynamic collapse and right heart failure are highest in the first 12 h after delivery, necessitating close monitoring. Nothing in the peripartum management of women with PH is evidence-based, hence the following statements are, once again, based on clinical experience.

Mode and timing of delivery

The optimal delivery mode in pregnant women with PH is unknown. However, published data suggest that caesarean section (C-section) is the preferred delivery mode in these patients.

While spontaneous, induced and supported vaginal deliveries are feasible in women with PH, most centres prefer preterm delivery during gestational week 36–38. The main reason for this is the preference of a planned and structured daytime delivery when all necessary resources are readily available. As induction of labour at this stage is unreliable and unpredictable, and because of concerns about potential detrimental effects of prolonged labour, many centres prefer C-section.

Bleeding complications after C-section are relevant to women with PH and must be avoided. Obstetricians often use oxytocin to increase uterine tone after delivery, which has been shown in animal models to increase PVR. Solid human data are not available, but clinical experience suggests that low-dose oxytocin can be safely administered after delivery in women with PH.

Anaesthesia and peripartum monitoring

There are no controlled studies comparing different anaesthetic approaches for C-section in women with PH. Both general anaesthesia and regional (spinal or peridural) anaesthesia are feasible, although published data suggest that most centres prefer regional anaesthesia in these patients.

Peripartum monitoring should focus on the early detection of right heart failure. Cardiac catheterisation is usually not required, but most centres place an arterial line for continuous monitoring of systemic blood pressure and a central venous line to monitor central venous pressure and central venous oxygen saturation. A drop in central venous oxygen saturation is often the first sign of right ventricular failure. Many anaesthetists use pre-emptive low-dose vasopressors and/or low-dose inotropes during C-section, especially at the time of delivery, during which a drop in systemic blood pressure is common. In advanced PAH cases, pre-delivery cannulation for potential need for ECMO can be considered.

Post-partum care

Patients with more than mild PH are usually monitored in an intermediate or intensive care unit at least 24–72 h after delivery. Continuous monitoring as described in the previous section is complemented by echocardiography and laboratory assessments (including haemoglobin, troponin, NT-proBNP and lactate).

The management of post-partum right heart failure depends on various factors including the type of PH, triggering factors (such as blood loss or arrhythmia), therapy hitherto and local resources. Inotropes and vasopressors are often effective to stabilise transient deteriorations. In women who develop progressive right heart failure refractory to optimised medical treatment, extracorporeal life support (ECLS) might be considered (where available). When discussing this option, one must bear in mind that the use of ECLS in such settings has a high likelihood of failure unless urgent lung transplantation is a realistic option.

Breastfeeding is another issue for which no solid data are available for women with PH. Breastfeeding is certainly possible for women with mild PH who don't receive PAH medications. In women with PAH, therapy is commonly intensified immediately after delivery, especially by reinstitution of ERAs. In such scenarios, breastfeeding is contraindicated. These aspects should be discussed with the patient upfront, i.e. when pregnancy is considered or during early stages of pregnancy, so that ablactation can be initiated right after delivery.

Medication adherence in PH

The treatment algorithm for patients with PAH has become more complex and includes a growing choice of medications [13]. The use of multiple therapies in combination is now the standard of care. Patients may also be taking other medications to control cardiac function and fluid retention, as well as oxygen. The diseases associated with PAH, such as connective tissue disease, HIV infection and congenital heart disease, require additional specific therapies. As the patients age, common diseases of life such as diabetes, systemic hypertension, heart disease, cancer and others will require treatment. Thus, polypharmacy (the use of five or more medications [30]) is frequent and expected in PAH, and medication adherence will increasingly be an issue.

The WHO defines adherence as “the extent to which a person's behaviour – taking medication, following a diet, and/or executing lifestyle changes, corresponds with agreed recommendations from a health care provider” [31]. Adherence has been increasingly recognised as an important cause of treatment failure, especially in the setting of polypharmacy. It represents a major burden on world health, particularly in common diseases such as diabetes, systemic hypertension, HIV infection and tuberculosis. Five dimensions of adherence have been identified, including condition-related factors, patient-related factors, health system related factors, social/economic factors and therapy-related factors (figure 3) [31]. In many countries, women face a greater burden to achieve adherence [32]. While many of these factors may vary between diseases and countries, education on medication use, availability and access to affordable care and medications, and continuous monitoring and reassessment of therapies are central issues [31, 33, 34]. Another common issue for therapy-related factors is simplification of treatment regimens [35–39]. Fixed-dose combination therapy has been introduced for hypertension, diabetes, HIV infection and other diseases; it has improved adherence, and may reduce cost.

Five interacting dimensions which affect adherence. Information from [31].

>80% of PAH patients take five or more medications, with a median of nine [40, 41]. Adherence rates in PAH varying between 48% and 76% have been reported [42–46], with recent studies finding a 60% rate [47, 48]. Need for multiple daily doses, length of time on therapy, and number of other therapies impact adherence. A country's healthcare system structure, and patients’ financial status and need for copayments, are also factors [46, 49]. Age and comorbidities may contribute [45] and the nature and frequency of PAH medication-related side-effects can affect adherence [46, 50, 51]. Improved adherence may be associated with reduced hospitalisations [45]. To date, only one fixed-dose combination therapy of macitentan and tadalafil has reached clinical use. It has shown pharmacological bioequivalence to the separate individual components [52–54].

Medication adherence will become a growing issue as PAH treatment regimens combined with therapies to treat concurrent diseases increase in number and complexity. Barriers to adherence, including a need for clinical tools to assess adherence, the need for behavioural and educational tools and resources, systemic gaps in care and socioeconomic issues and insufficient technology for communication will need to be addressed to ensure optimal care.

Recommendation for adherence at this point is to increase the awareness of this growing aspect of PH patient care.

Palliative care in PH patients

Palliative care is no longer about just dying peacefully, but also living well in the context of a life-shortening illness, in particular where predicting death may be very difficult and potentially many years away. Over the past 20 years, treatment of PAH has advanced with indisputable benefits on exercise capacity, haemodynamics and morbidity. Despite these successes, many, if not most, patients are still left with a significant psychological burden to varying degrees, impaired quality of life, social isolation and cardiopulmonary symptoms.

Palliative care has historically, and to a significant extent still is, considered as end-of-life care for cancer patients and access to it is triggered if the answer to the questions of “would I be surprised if my patient was still alive in 6–12 months?” is “yes”. This concept underlies much of the lack of engagement with palliative care in PH. Physicians worry that referral to palliative care may be seen as “giving up hope” and that palliative care cannot run in parallel with disease-targeted drugs [55]. Patients have similar fears and those with more severe disease and greater anxiety may paradoxically be less receptive to palliative care [56].

Consequently, many patients underutilise aspects of palliative care, such as advance healthcare directives and too many patients receive inappropriate attempts at cardiopulmonary resuscitation and mechanical ventilation at the end of life. Better use of palliative care leads to patients dying in more appropriate settings, such as at home or in hospices, rather than in hospitals and ICU surroundings [57, 58].

Furthermore, while there is limited evidence in PH, in other disease areas, early use of palliative care can improve QoL, symptoms, engagement, reduced caregiver burden and future planning [59–61]. Contrary to belief, it is not associated with worse survival [62, 63], and examples of symptom and QoL monitoring and management have been shown to improve survival [64].

There has therefore been a consistent drive to redefine palliative care. Many definitions exist, with significant overlap, but mostly agreeing on palliative care being a holistic and individualised approach to meet the needs of patients suffering from serious, life-shortening illness which are not addressed by disease-targeted treatments. This would also include the needs of their caregivers.

Understanding that this is a needs-based, and therefore needs-triggered, approach can help define its scope in PH and when aspects of it should be deployed. Needs will vary over time from diagnosis to death, and have been suggested to make up eight domains [65]: 1) having access to appropriate structure and processes of care;2) treatment of physical symptoms;3) addressing psychological and psychiatric symptoms;4) assessment and management of social aspects such as patient and family support needs;5) caring for spiritual, religious and existential needs;6) helping to integrate cultural aspects of care;7) end-of-life care;8) safe management of ethical and legal aspects of care, such as advance care planning, surrogate decision-making, regulatory and legal considerations and related end of life issues with attention given to patient autonomy.Since palliative care should be about meeting needs of patients, this requires a mechanism by which those needs can be identified and tracked over time. The first step in achieving this is ensuring a compassionate, time-rich multidisciplinary environment where patients and their caregivers needs can be explored. Useful tools to deploy in this setting are multidomain PH-specific QoL questionnaires. The EmPHasis-10 has been shown to be an independent predictor of survival [66], but also provides a rapid assessment of aspects of QoL, such as the physical, social and emotional. This, and other disease specific measures, such as the Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension – Symptoms and Impact Questionnaire (PAH-SYMPACT), can not only serve as icebreakers, but trigger referrals and interventions, or more detailed assessments of depression and anxiety with specific patient-reported outcome tools. In the United Kingdom, the use of at least one QoL assessment per year is a national audit standard by which PH centres are assessed by the Department of Health and Social Care (https://digital.nhs.uk/data-and-information/clinical-audits-and-registries/national-pulmonary-hypertension-audit).

The influence of climate on PH

The consequences of climate change, such as extreme temperatures and weather events, the destruction of ecosystems and severe biodiversity loss, to consequent food scarcity, social instability and mass human displacement affect the health of humans and the natural systems humans rely on [67].

Advising patients with PH about the consequences they might face due to climate change and the possible actions they can take will become important in all parts of the world [68].

Patients with cardiovascular diseases are at four to seven times greater risk of mortality during extreme heat events [69, 70]. During heatwaves, patients should be advised to stay inside and as cool as possible and to measure their blood pressure and heart rate closely and adjust diuretic therapies if necessary. Patients on intravenous prostacyclins and implanted pumps should be aware of faster flow rate during extreme heat [71].

Reduction of air pollution (less active travel, less waste, less energy consumption, reducing household air pollution) is of importance for planetary and individual cardiorespiratory health [72]. On a personal level, a change in lifestyle (e.g. active travel (like walking or cycling), plant-based diet, smoking cessation, etc.) might help reduced carbon dioxide emission [73, 74] and prevent decompensation of the most common diseases associated with PH (group 2 and 3).

The impact of the health sector on carbon dioxide emissions via industrial production, waste and energy consumption is immense [75, 76]. The diagnostic algorithm for PH requires a multitude of tests [14]. Further research is needed to find less wasteful, more efficient ways to diagnose the disease. Identifying low-risk patients will reduce healthcare system overuse, while early treatment and close monitoring in high-risk patients might reduce hospitalisation. The option of telemonitoring [77] and eHealth [78] in stable, low-risk PH patients would be of benefit and should be further explored. Efficacy and safety of therapies remain the most important priorities, but the carbon footprint throughout the lifecycle of a product should also be considered. Data regarding the latter are lacking. Mostly, inhaled therapies for airway diseases [79] and cardiovascular healthcare [80] have been studied so far. Low environmental-impact solutions should be considered whenever possible. In the field of PH, rehabilitation is of low environmental impact and can be offered to stable, medically treated patients [81].
