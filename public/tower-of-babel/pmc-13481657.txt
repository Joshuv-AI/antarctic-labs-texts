TFPI2 expression predicts resistance to immunotherapy plus tyrosine kinase inhibitor and informs first-line therapy selection in metastatic renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Jiajun Wang, Lingzhi Du, Ying Wang, Hang Wang, Yanjun Zhu, Xianglai Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCombinations of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy (IO) and anti-VEGF tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) are recommended as first-line therapy for metastatic renal cell carcinoma (RCC). We aimed to evaluate the predictive value of tissue factor pathway inhibitor 2 (TFPI2) for IO + TKI response.MethodsTFPI2 expression and its association with IO + TKI response and progression-free survival (PFS) were analyzed in the ZS-MRCC and JAVELIN Renal 101 cohorts. Tumor microenvironment analyses were conducted in ZS-HRRCC and TCGA-KIRC cohorts using RNA-sequencing, flow cytometry, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence.ResultsTFPI2 expression was elevated in IO + TKI non-responders (p = 0.044) and predicted shorter PFS in ZS-MRCC (p = 0.026) and JAVELIN Renal 101 (p = 0.007) cohorts. Multivariate analysis confirmed TFPI2 as an independent prognostic factor (HR = 2.42; p = 0.030). High-TFPI2 tumors exhibited reduced Granzyme B+CD8+ (p = 0.05) and Granzyme B+CD4+ (p = 0.02) T cell infiltration, with elevated exhaustion markers including TIGIT (p = 0.039), CTLA4 (p = 0.021), EOMES (p = 0.047), and TCF1 (p = 0.034), a marker of progenitor exhausted T cells (Tpex). A random forest model integrating TFPI2 and immune-related genes stratified IO + TKI benefit: the RF score-low group derived significant benefit from IO + TKI (HR = 0.477; 95% CI: 0.354–0.644), whereas the RF score-high group did not (HR = 0.936; 95% CI: 0.692–1.265, p for interaction = 0.001).ConclusionTFPI2 overexpression is associated with immune evasion and poor PFS in RCC patients treated with IO + TKI. The RF model may facilitate precision treatment selection between IO + TKI and TKI monotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13402-026-01224-3.Keywords: Tissue factor pathway inhibitor 2, Renal cell carcinoma, Tyrosine kinase inhibitor, Immunotherapy, Immune evasion

Introduction

Kidney cancer ranks 14th among all cancer types worldwide, with more than 430,000 new cases and over 150,000 deaths annually [1], of which approximately 90% are renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [2]. Approximately 80,980 new cases of RCC will be diagnosed in the USA, and about 14,510 patients will die from the disease in 2025 [3], while advanced or metastatic RCC can lead to poorer survival in most patients [4]. Although combined anti-PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapies (IO) and anti-VEGF tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have demonstrated clinical efficacy [5–8] and are recommended by current RCC clinical guidelines [4], fewer than half of patients derive substantial benefit, and over 70% experience treatment-related adverse events [5–8]. This limited efficacy is partly attributable to heterogeneous responses, with a significant proportion of patients failing to achieve durable clinical benefit. Consequently, there remains an urgent and unmet need for predictive biomarkers to guide therapeutic decision-making.

Tissue factor pathway inhibitor-2 (TFPI2, also known as placental protein 5) is a Kunitz-type serine protease inhibitor initially identified in placenta [9]. TFPI2 regulates components of the coagulation cascade and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, including thrombin and matrix metalloproteinases, thereby inhibiting tumor invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis [10]. Aberrant promoter hypermethylation-mediated silencing of TFPI2 has been reported in multiple cancers, including gastric cancer [11], oral squamous cell carcinoma [12], and colorectal carcinoma [13], and is associated with enhanced tumor invasion, metastasis, and worse prognosis [14]. Interestingly, Kobayashi et al. [15] observed that elevated serum TFPI2 levels correlated with poor prognosis in ovarian and endometrial cancers, suggesting that TFPI2 may act as a tumor-promoting factor in certain contexts. In RCC, TFPI2 has recently emerged as a potential biomarker: serum TFPI2 levels are higher in RCC patients compared with healthy controls, particularly in clear cell RCC (ccRCC), and elevated TFPI2 expression is associated with higher tumor grade, metastatic disease, and poorer overall survival [16].

Despite these findings, the utility of TFPI2 as a predictive biomarker for combined IO + TKI therapy in RCC remains unexplored, and its potential role in mediating tumor immune evasion is largely unknown. Given its regulatory effects on extracellular matrix remodeling and angiogenesis—key processes that shape the tumor microenvironment and influence immune cell infiltration, TFPI2 may impact therapeutic efficacy by modulating the immune landscape of RCC.

In this study, we evaluated TFPI2 expression in RCC specimens and examined its association with response to IO + TKI therapy and progression-free survival (PFS). We further characterized the immune microenvironment of TFPI2-high tumors across additional cohorts. Finally, we developed a random forest model integrating TFPI2 and immune-related gene expression to stratify patients by predicted benefit from IO + TKI versus TKI monotherapy, providing a framework to guide precision immunotherapy in RCC.

Materials and methods

Cohorts and datasets

Patients with metastatic RCC (mRCC) treated with IO + TKI were analyzed from two independent cohorts: the Zhongshan Hospital metastatic RCC (ZS-MRCC) cohort and the JAVELIN Renal 101 clinical trial cohort.

The ZS-MRCC cohort initially enrolled 51 patients with pathologically confirmed mRCC who received IO + TKI therapy and had no history of other malignancies. The exclusion criteria were: refusal to participate, unavailable sample, not passing sample quality control, or loss of follow-up. Six patients were excluded owing to sample unavailability or loss to follow-up, leaving 45 patients for the final analysis. Tumor response was evaluated according to RECIST 1.1 criteria [17] by computed tomography every 6–8 weeks. Progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the interval from therapy initiation to radiographic progression or death, served as the primary endpoint.

The JAVELIN Renal 101 cohort comprised 726 patients with metastatic RCC (mRCC) enrolled in this randomized phase III study [7]. Among them, 354 patients received avelumab plus axitinib (IO + TKI arm), whereas 372 received sunitinib monotherapy (TKI arm). Clinical eligibility criteria, baseline characteristics, progression-free survival (PFS), and available transcriptomic and genomic data were obtained from previously published reports [7, 18].

For extended immunologic and functional analyses, two additional datasets were included. The ZS-HRRCC cohort comprised tumor specimens from 40 patients with high-risk localized RCC treated at Zhongshan Hospital. The TCGA-KIRC dataset included 530 cases of clear cell RCC, with transcriptomic and clinical data retrieved from the UCSC Xena browser (https://xena.ucsc.edu/) [19]. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria for these cohorts are summarized in Table 1.

All studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (B2021-119). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

RNA-sequencing procedures

RNA sequencing was conducted for samples from the ZS-MRCC and ZS-HRRCC cohorts. Total RNA was extracted using the MagBeads Total RNA Extraction Kit (Cat. #T02-096), followed by purification with the RNAClean XP Kit (Cat. #A63987, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) and DNase treatment with the RNase-Free DNase Set (Cat. #79254, QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). RNA libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Cat. #NR604-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequencing data were normalized to fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) for downstream analyses.

Hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

In the ZS-HRRCC dataset, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) were assessed on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections following the recommendations of the International TILs Working Group [20]. IHC staining was performed as previously described [21]. Slides were scanned with the PANNORAMIC® 250 Flash III DX digital slide scanner (3DHISTECH Ltd.) and analyzed using the CaseViewer software (3DHISTECH Ltd.). For each specimen, IHC staining was evaluated in six randomly selected high-power fields by three independent investigators blinded to clinical data, and mean values were used for subsequent analyses.

Flow cytometry analysis

Fresh peripheral blood and RCC tumor samples were collected perioperatively. Detailed procedures for flow cytometry have been described previously [22]. For peripheral blood, red blood cells were lysed using RBC Lysis Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to isolate leukocytes. For tumor specimens, freshly resected tissues were minced, enzymatically digested with collagenase IV (Sigma) and DNase I (Sigma), and passed through a 70-µm strainer. Single-cell suspensions were subsequently treated with RBC Lysis Buffer. After Fc receptor blockade, cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against surface markers, followed by intracellular staining using the Intracellular Fixation & Permeabilization Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Labeled cells were resuspended in staining buffer and analyzed on a BD LSRFortessa™ X-20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data were processed with FlowJo software v10.0 (Tree Star).

Machine learning models for risk stratification

To develop risk prediction models, the expression levels of TFPI2, CD274, PDCD1, CD8A, GZMK, GZMB, and CD4 were selected as candidate input features. Multiple machine learning algorithms were applied, including supervised principal components (SuperPC), survival support vector machine (survivalSVM), stepwise Cox regression (stepCox), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), and random forest (RF), with concordance index (C-index) used as the primary criterion for algorithm selection. For the final RF model development, the IO + TKI arm of the JAVELIN Renal 101 cohort (n = 354) was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%, n = 247) and a held-out test set (30%, n = 107) prior to any model fitting. Variable selection was performed exclusively on the training set using a combined Variable Importance (VIMP) and minimal depth approach, two complementary RF-native metrics that identify variables contributing most reliably to survival prediction while penalizing those with spurious associations. The final RF model was configured with the following hyperparameters: number of trees = 200, terminal node size = 15, average number of terminal nodes = 11.2, number of variables tried at each split = 6, resampling method = sampling without replacement (swor), resample size = 156, and log-rank splitting rule with 10 random split points. Out-of-bag (OOB) prediction error was computed on the training set as an unbiased estimate of model generalizability. The RF score for each patient was derived from the ensemble prediction of the fitted model and used as a continuous risk index for subsequent analyses. Model construction and evaluation were performed by R software (version 4.3.3, https://www.r-project.org/) using the following packages: mixOmics, survcomp, superpc, survivalsvm, CoxBoost, plsRcox, glmnet, randomForestSRC, and ggRandomForests.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, or Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test or Kruskal-Wallis H test. Correlation analyses were performed with Spearman’s rank correlation. Survival outcomes were assessed using Kaplan-Meier analysis with the log-rank test and Cox proportional hazards regression. For the multivariable Cox regression model in the ZS-MRCC cohort, model complexity was deliberately restricted to three covariates (TFPI2 expression, tumor histology, and IMDC risk group) to maintain an acceptable events-per-variable (EPV) ratio given the available sample size. A post hoc statistical power calculation for the Cox regression model was performed using the Schoenfeld method based on the observed hazard ratio, number of events, and group allocation. Treatment-predictive interactions were evaluated using Cox regression with an interaction term between biomarker group and treatment arm. Statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.3, https://www.r-project.org/) with the survival and survminer packages for survival analysis, and forestplot, ComplexHeatmap, and ggplot2 for data visualization. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

High TFPI2 expression is associated with progression and poor survival in RCC

Analysis of the TCGA-KIRC dataset revealed that TFPI2 expression increased with advancing disease stage (stage IV vs. stage I, p = 0.005; stage IV vs. stage II, p = 0.049; stage IV vs. stage III, p = 0.013; Fig. 1A) and tumor grade (grade 4 vs. grade 1, p = 0.021; grade 4 vs. grade 2, p = 0.014; grade 4 vs. grade 3, p = 0.033; Fig. 1B). Furthermore, RCC patients with high TFPI2 expression exhibited significantly poorer overall survival compared to those with low expression (p < 0.001, Fig. 1C).

High TFPI2 expression is associated with resistance to IO + TKI therapy

In the ZS-MRCC cohort of metastatic RCC patients receiving IO + TKI treatment, TFPI2 expression was significantly higher in non-responders compared with responders (p = 0.044, Fig. 1D). When patients were stratified into high- and low-TFPI2 groups based on the upper tertile of expression, the low-TFPI2 group exhibited a significantly higher proportion of complete response (CR) and partial response (PR) (p = 0.014, Fig. 1E). Representative IHC images of TFPI2 expression in high- and low-TFPI2 tumor specimens from the ZS-MRCC cohort are shown in Fig. S1A.

TFPI2 as an independent prognostic marker for PFS under IO + TKI

The prognostic significance of TFPI2 expression was first evaluated in the ZS-MRCC cohort. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated that patients with high TFPI2 expression had significantly shorter PFS compared with those with low expression (log-rank p = 0.026, Fig. 1F). In univariate Cox regression analysis, elevated TFPI2 expression was significantly associated with an increased risk of progression (HR 2.322; 95% CI: 1.078–5.001; p = 0.031; Fig. 1G). After adjustment for tumor histology and IMDC risk group in a multivariable Cox model, TFPI2 remained an independent predictor of poor PFS (HR 2.420; 95% CI: 1.089–5.379; p = 0.030; Fig. 1G). These findings were independently validated in the IO + TKI arm of the JAVELIN Renal 101 study, in which high TFPI2 expression was likewise associated with shorter PFS (log-rank p = 0.007, Fig. 1H).

Correlation between TFPI2 expression and T cell abundance in RCC

To investigate the relationship between TFPI2 expression and immune cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment, H&E and IHC staining were performed on RCC specimens in ZS-HRRCC cohort (Fig. 2A). No significant correlation was observed between TFPI2 expression and the density of CD8+ T cells (p = 0.50, Fig. 2B) or CD4+ T cells (p = 0.22, Fig. 2C). Further evaluation using flow cytometry (Fig. 2D) on freshly resected RCC tissues showed a non-significant trend toward lower proportions of CD8+ T cells (Spearman’s ρ = -0.23, p = 0.16, Fig. 2E) and higher CD4+ T cell proportions (Spearman’s ρ = 0.27, p = 0.10, Fig. 2F) in tumors with high TFPI2 expression, although these associations did not reach statistical significance.

Correlation between TFPI2 expression and T cell function in RCC

We next analyzed the association between TFPI2 expression and GZMB+ T cells in the ZS-HRRCC cohort using immunohistochemistry. TFPI2 expression was significantly negatively correlated with overall GZMB+ cell abundance (Spearman’s ρ = -0.36, p = 0.02, Fig. 3A). Subset analysis revealed a borderline significant inverse correlation with GZMB+CD8+ T cells (Spearman’s ρ = -0.31, p = 0.05, Fig. 3B) and a significant inverse correlation with GZMB+CD4+ T cells (Spearman’s ρ = -0.36, p = 0.02, Fig. 3C). In contrast, TFPI2 expression showed positive, though non-significant, correlations with PD1+CD8+ T cells (Spearman’s ρ = 0.25, p = 0.12, Fig. 3D) and PD1+CD4+ T cells (Spearman’s ρ = 0.10, p = 0.55, Fig. 3E).

We further evaluated the expression of immune checkpoint molecules and transcription factors in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) by IHC (Fig. 3F). TFPI2 expression was positively correlated with several immune checkpoint markers, including TIGIT (p = 0.039) and CTLA4 (p = 0.021), while associations with TIM3 (p = 0.10) and LAG3 (p = 0.06) did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 3G). Similarly, TFPI2 expression was positively associated with transcription factors involved in heterogeneous exhausted T cell states, including TCF1 (p = 0.034), TOX (p = 0.012), and EOMES (p = 0.047), whereas the correlation with T-bet did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.078). Notably, TCF1 serves as a marker of progenitor exhausted CD8+ T cells (Tpex), a stem-like subset that retains proliferative capacity and can differentiate into more terminally exhausted T cells. Representative IHC images of TIGIT, CTLA4, TOX, and EOMES expression in high- and low-TFPI2 tumor specimens are shown in Fig. S2A. To further characterize terminally exhausted T cell phenotypes, we assessed the proportions of dual co-expressing CD8+ T cell subsets by flow cytometry. High-TFPI2 tumors exhibited a non-significant increase in PD1+TIGIT+CD8+ T cells (p = 0.21, Fig. S2B) and a significant increase in PD1+TIM3+CD8+ T cells (p = 0.004, Fig. S2C).

Immunofluorescence staining was also performed to characterize exhaustion-related phenotypes. TFPI2 expression showed positive correlations with TIGIT+CD3+ cells (Spearman’s ρ = 0.36, p = 0.02, Fig. 4A), LAG3+CD3+ cells (Spearman’s ρ = 0.12, p = 0.46, Fig. 4B), TCF1+CD3+ cells (Spearman’s ρ = 0.36, p = 0.02, Fig. 4C), and EOMES+CD3+ cells (Spearman’s ρ = 0.43, p < 0.001, Fig. 4D).

TFPI2 links to an immunoevasive tumor microenvironment in RCC

To investigate whether TFPI2 expression is associated with an immunosuppressive tumor phenotype, we analyzed markers of angiogenesis, stromal activation, macrophage infiltration, proliferation, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) in the ZS-HRRCC cohort. Immunohistochemistry showed negative correlations between TFPI2 expression and CD34 (p = 0.14, Fig. 5A) as well as α-SMA (p = 0.007, Fig. 5B), while positive correlations were observed with CD68 (p = 0.32, Fig. 5C), Ki67 (p = 0.20, Fig. 5D), and FOXP3 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.17, p = 0.31, Fig. 5E). Flow cytometry further confirmed a positive association between TFPI2 expression and Treg abundance (Spearman’s ρ = 0.28, p = 0.08, Fig. 5F).

Consistently, transcriptomic analysis in the JAVELIN Renal 101 cohort demonstrated positive correlations between TFPI2 expression and multiple immunosuppressive cytokines, including CCL2 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.16, p < 0.001), CCL5 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.11, p < 0.001), IL6 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.25, p < 0.001), and IL10 (Spearman’s ρ = 0.15, p < 0.001, Fig. 5G).

Correlation between TFPI2 and somatic mutations

Analysis of the JAVELIN Renal 101 cohort (Fig. 5H) revealed that tumors with high TFPI2 expression were characterized by an increased prevalence of SETD2 (p < 0.001) and PTEN (p < 0.001) mutations, while exhibiting significantly lower frequencies of BAP1 (p < 0.001) and RICTOR (p < 0.001) mutations, suggesting a distinct mutational landscape associated with elevated TFPI2 expression.

Machine learning-based RF risk score predicts IO + TKI benefit in metastatic RCC

To develop a predictive model for IO + TKI therapy, multiple machine learning algorithms were applied, including SuperPC, survivalSVM, stepwise Cox regression (StepCox), gradient boosting machine (GBM), and random forest (RF), using gene expression data for TFPI2, CD274, PDCD1, CD8A, GZMK, GZMB, and CD4 as candidate input features. Algorithm comparison was performed in the full IO + TKI arm of the JAVELIN Renal 101 cohort using C-index as the primary selection criterion, and the RF algorithm demonstrated superior performance (Fig. 6A).

For model development, the IO + TKI arm was randomly partitioned into a training set (70%, n = 247) and a held-out test set (30%, n = 107). Variable selection was performed on the training set using a combined VIMP and minimal depth approach (Fig. 6B). From the initial seven candidate genes, CD274 was excluded based on low VIMP and high minimal depth, and the remaining six variables (TFPI2, CD8A, PDCD1, GZMK, GZMB, and CD4) were retained for the final model, with their relative feature importance shown in Fig. 6C. The out-of-bag (OOB) prediction error on the training set was 0.448.

Time-dependent ROC analysis in the full IO + TKI arm demonstrated AUCs of 0.60, 0.65, and 0.71 at 0.5, 1, and 1.5 years, respectively (Fig. 6D). Using the median RF score as the cutoff, Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed that patients with high RF scores experienced significantly worse PFS compared to those with low RF scores within the IO + TKI arm (HR = 1.932, p < 0.001; Fig. 6E). External validation in the independent ZS-MRCC cohort (n = 45) demonstrated that high RF scores were associated with significantly worse PFS (HR = 2.624, 95% CI: 1.120–6.147, p = 0.023; Fig. 6F). In the TKI monotherapy arm of JAVELIN Renal 101 cohort, the RF score did not significantly stratify PFS (p = 0.743; Fig. 6G).

To further evaluate treatment-predictive utility, the entire JAVELIN Renal 101 cohort was stratified into four groups according to RF score (high vs. low) and treatment arm (IO + TKI vs. TKI monotherapy; Fig. 6H). Using the IO + TKI/RF score-low group as the reference, the HRs for the remaining three groups were 2.14 (TKI/RF score-low), 1.92 (IO + TKI/RF score-high), and 2.04 (TKI/RF score-high), respectively (all p < 0.001).

Multivariable Cox regression analyses of TFPI2 expression and RF score are shown in Fig. 6I. When stratified by TFPI2 expression alone, both TFPI2-high (HR = 0.695, 95% CI: 0.499–0.969, p = 0.032) and TFPI2-low (HR = 0.627, 95% CI: 0.478–0.823, p = 0.001) subgroups derived significant benefit from IO + TKI, with no significant treatment interaction (p for interaction = 0.639). In contrast, stratification by RF score revealed a significant treatment-predictive interaction: the RF score-low subgroup showed significant benefit from IO + TKI (HR = 0.477, 95% CI: 0.354–0.644, p < 0.001), whereas the RF score-high subgroup did not (HR = 0.936, 95% CI: 0.692–1.265, p = 0.665), with a significant interaction term (p for interaction = 0.001).

Discussion

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) remains a highly heterogeneous malignancy, with metastatic disease associated with poor prognosis. The BIONIKK study highlighted that a biomarker-guided strategy can improve therapeutic outcomes for RCC patients receiving immunotherapy [23]. Additional research has identified candidate prognostic markers for immunotherapy in clear cell RCC, including MYBL1 [24], TPD52L2 [25], and PLAC8 [26]. Despite these advances, robust biomarkers specifically predictive of response to IO + TKI combination therapy in RCC remain lacking, underscoring the need for further investigation.

Although originally recognized for its role in regulating coagulation and extracellular matrix remodeling [9], TFPI2 has also been implicated in immunotherapy resistance and modulation of the tumor microenvironment [10]. In this study, we identified TFPI2 as a key biomarker associated with both adverse survival outcomes and immune evasion in metastatic RCC. High TFPI2 expression correlated with shorter progression-free survival (PFS) in both the ZS-MRCC cohort and the JAVELIN Renal 101 trial, and remained an independent predictor after multivariate adjustment, suggesting that TFPI2 may serve not only as a prognostic marker but also as a predictive marker for IO + TKI therapy response. Mechanistically, elevated TFPI2 expression was associated with reduced proportions of functionally cytotoxic Granzyme B+ T cells and increased expression of exhaustion‑associated markers, including TIGIT, CTLA4, TOX, EOMES, and TCF1. However, TCF1 should be interpreted in the context of heterogeneous exhausted T cell differentiation rather than as a driver of terminal dysfunction. TCF1 marks a progenitor exhausted T cell subset (Tpex) that retains stem‑like self‑renewal capacity and can give rise to more terminally exhausted cells under chronic antigen stimulation, and these progenitor cells are preferentially responsive to immune checkpoint blockade [27–29]. The concurrent association of TFPI2 with both progenitor (TCF1) and exhaustion (TOX, EOMES) programs suggests that TFPI2 may influence the balance and differentiation trajectory of exhausted T cell subsets in the tumor microenvironment, a hypothesis that warrants future functional validation in RCC. Since these checkpoints and transcription factors are closely linked to T cell dysfunction [30], our findings indicate that elevated TFPI2 may promote T cell exhaustion within the tumor microenvironment, offering mechanistic insight into the reduced responsiveness of high-TFPI2 tumors to IO + TKI therapy.

Our analysis revealed a distinct immunosuppressive milieu in high-TFPI2 tumors, characterized by enhanced regulatory T cell abundance and increased expression of immunosuppressive cytokines, including CCL2, CCL5, IL6, and IL10. This observation aligns with previous reports implicating TFPI2 in extracellular matrix remodeling and angiogenesis [31], both of which can influence immune cell trafficking and activation. Moreover, high TFPI2 tumors exhibited specific mutational patterns, including increased SETD2 and PTEN mutations and decreased BAP1 and RICTOR mutations, suggesting that TFPI2 may reflect broader molecular programs linked to immune escape.

Although functional experiments on TFPI2 were not performed in the present study, emerging evidence from recent mechanistic research provides insight into how TFPI2 may modulate the tumor microenvironment. In glioblastoma, TFPI2 has been shown to act as a secreted effector that engages its receptor CD51 on microglia, activating downstream STAT6 signaling and driving immunosuppressive microglial polarization, which in turn inhibits antitumor T cell activity and contributes to resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy in preclinical models [32]. Although this mechanism was characterized in a different tumor type, it provides a plausible basis for the immunosuppressive phenotype observed in high-TFPI2 RCC tumors in our study. Additionally, TFPI2 has been implicated in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) regulation, affecting tumor invasion, angiogenesis, and stromal architecture, all of which are key modulators of immune cell trafficking and function within the TME [31]. These effects on the ECM provide a potential structural basis for the reduced cytotoxic T cell activity and elevated exhaustion markers observed in high-TFPI2 tumors. Beyond its role in extracellular matrix remodeling, TFPI2 may regulate immunity by promoting M2-like macrophage polarization via PPARγ signaling [33]. Given that M2-polarized macrophages are major producers of immunosuppressive cytokines including IL-10 and CCL2, this mechanism offers a potential explanation for the elevated immunosuppressive cytokine expression observed in high-TFPI2 tumors in the JAVELIN Renal 101 transcriptomic analysis, and is consistent with the positive correlation between TFPI2 expression and regulatory T cell abundance identified in our cohort. Functional studies in RCC are needed to validate these mechanisms.

The RF score-based machine learning classifier, integrating TFPI2 and five immune-related genes (CD8A, PDCD1, GZMK, GZMB, and CD4), demonstrated meaningful performance in predicting survival outcomes and stratifying patients likely to benefit from IO + TKI versus TKI monotherapy. Notably, the RF score showed significant prognostic value in the IO + TKI arm but not in the TKI monotherapy arm of JAVELIN Renal 101, suggesting that the model captures immune-specific rather than generic prognostic signals. The treatment-predictive interaction identified by Cox regression, in which only the RF score-low subgroup derived significant benefit from IO + TKI, highlights the potential clinical utility of the RF score in guiding personalized treatment selection. Furthermore, external validation in the independent ZS-MRCC cohort confirmed the prognostic stratification capacity of the RF score, supporting its generalizability despite the sample size limitation of the institutional cohort.

While our study provides evidence for the predictive and functional significance of TFPI2, several limitations merit consideration. First, the ZS-MRCC cohort was relatively small (n = 45). The multivariable Cox regression model was deliberately restricted to three covariates to maintain an acceptable events-per-variable ratio, and a post hoc power calculation estimated statistical power at approximately 72%, modestly below the conventional 80% threshold. Nevertheless, the prognostic significance of TFPI2 was independently validated in the large JAVELIN Renal 101 cohort (n = 354), supporting the robustness of our findings. Second, the RF score was developed and internally validated within the JAVELIN Renal 101 dataset, and while external validation in the ZS-MRCC cohort yielded consistent results, multi-center validation in a larger, dedicated IO + TKI-treated cohort remains an important priority for future work. Third, mechanistic studies directly interrogating TFPI2-mediated immune suppression in RCC were not performed, leaving open questions regarding causality. Future studies should explore whether TFPI2 inhibition can restore T cell function and sensitize tumors to IO + TKI therapy.

In conclusion, TFPI2 overexpression defines a subset of RCC tumors with immunoevasive features and poor response to IO + TKI therapy. Integrating TFPI2 expression with immune gene signatures into an RF-based predictive model may inform personalized treatment strategies and improve outcomes in metastatic RCC.
