Umbelliferone preserves skin epidermal barrier integrity in diabetes-induced keratinocytes by regulating actin dynamics and suppressing ROS-mediated apoptosis
Authors: Dong Yeon Kim, Young-Hee Kang, Min-Kyung Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe skin serves as a fundamental protective barrier against environmental insults, with the epidermis, particularly the stratum corneum and tight junctions (TJs) connecting keratinocytes, playing a crucial role. Chronic hyperglycemia can impair these TJs, leading to compromised epidermal barrier function and diabetic skin complications. This study aimed to investigate the effects of umbelliferone on epidermal barrier function under type 2 diabetic conditions.MethodsHaCaT keratinocytes were cultured under hyperglycemic conditions induced by 33 mM glucose with or without 1–20 μM umbelliferone to evaluate cellular protection and barrier-related protein regulation. Type 2 diabetic db/db mice were administered umbelliferone orally at 10 mg/kg per day for 10 weeks. The expression of epidermal barrier-related proteins in HaCaT cells and skin tissues was quantified by Western blot analysis.ResultsUmbelliferone enhanced multiple components essential for maintaining the skin barrier. It upregulated filaggrin, increased the expression of the TJ proteins ZO-1 and Occludin, and elevated AQP3 and HAS2 levels to support epidermal hydration, while reducing HYAL1 expression. Under impaired wound healing conditions induced by hyperglycemia, umbelliferone promoted cell migration via modulation of F-actin organization, Rho GTPase signaling, and integrin β1 expression. Additionally, it reduced ROS accumulation and alleviated high glucose–induced apoptosis.ConclusionsUmbelliferone preserves epidermal barrier integrity by strengthening cell–cell junctions, enhancing hydration, promoting cell migration, and providing protection against oxidative stress and apoptosis. These findings suggest the therapeutic potential of umbelliferone in managing and preventing diabetic skin complications.

Introduction

The skin serves as a vital physical barrier against environmental threats, largely due to the epidermis, where both the stratum corneum and tight junctions (TJs) between keratinocytes play critical roles [1, 2]. The stratum corneum, the outermost layer of the epidermis, is composed of corneocytes—terminally differentiated, enucleated keratinocytes—that provide structural protection. Keratinocytes, the predominant cell type in the epidermis, form a multi-layered epithelium essential for this barrier function. During wound healing, keratinocytes proliferate, differentiate, migrate, and support angiogenesis to restore the epidermal barrier, driving re-epithelialization [3]. TJs, including claudins, occludin, junctional adhesion molecules (JAMs), and zonula occludin (ZO), connect adjacent keratinocytes to create a selective permeability barrier. These proteins form both charge-selective small pore pathways and size-selective pathways, with claudins playing a central role in controlling paracellular diffusion and maintaining tissue homeostasis [4, 5]. Additionally, TJs help maintain cellular polarity; ZO proteins interact with TJ-associated membrane proteins to regulate the permeability barrier for larger molecules [6]. Dysregulation of TJ proteins is associated with conditions marked by impaired skin barrier function, such as diabetes, psoriasis, and atopic dermatitis [7].

In diabetes, characterized by chronic hyperglycemia, wound healing (encompassing hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling) is often delayed. When the skin barrier is compromised, re-epithelialization must be rapidly activated to restore epidermal structure and function. This process is essential for wound healing and serves as a key indicator of healing progress. Promoting keratinocyte migration and proliferation at the wound site is critical for restoring barrier function. However, studies have shown that keratinocytes at the edges of chronic diabetic wounds, despite their capacity for high proliferation and migration, often struggle to differentiate and form stable, layered structures [8]. Moreover, hyperglycemia induces reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which impairs tight junction formation and compromises epithelial barrier integrity [9]. Excessive ROS can result in cellular damage, inflammation, disruption of antioxidant systems, and apoptosis [10]. Thus, inhibiting ROS generated by both external and internal stimuli to prevent keratinocyte apoptosis is crucial for minimizing skin damage.

Umbelliferone (UMB), also known as 7-hydroxycoumarin, is a naturally occurring coumarin compound widely found in various plant species, including herbs such as sanicle, celery, cumin, fennel, parsley, hogweed, and carrots [11]. Numerous studies have highlighted the pharmacological properties of umbelliferone, emphasizing its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant roles [12, 13]. Additionally, UMB has shown therapeutic effects in diabetic nephropathy [14], and our recent study demonstrated its ability to enhance collagen formation and improve delayed wound healing in dermal fibroblasts from diabetic skin [15]. Related studies have also explored umbelliferone’s role in modulating atopic dermatitis in epidermal keratinocytes [16]. However, despite these extensive findings, its effects on maintaining the epidermal barrier in diabetic keratinocytes remain unclear.

In this study, we investigated the effects of umbelliferone on maintaining the epidermal barrier in diabetic keratinocytes. Our results indicate that umbelliferone enhances TJ integrity, improves epidermal moisture retention, and promotes cell migration by regulating actin dynamics and downstream signaling pathways such as Rho GTPases. Furthermore, umbelliferone mitigates ROS accumulation induced by hyperglycemia, thereby reducing oxidative stress and preventing apoptosis. These findings provide new insights into the mechanisms underlying epidermal barrier dysfunction in diabetic skin and highlight umbelliferone’s potential therapeutic applications for diabetic skin conditions, such as chronic wounds, where TJs play a critical role.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

Umbelliferone was acquired from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical (St. Louis, MO, USA). The following antibodies were obtained from different sources: Rabbit polyclonal antibody of ZO-1 (Cat# 61-7300) was supplied by Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Rabbit polyclonal antibody of Filaggrin (Cat# ENZ-ABS181) was supplied by Enzo Life Sciences (Farmingdale, NY, USA). Mouse monoclonal antibodies of Bcl-2 (Cat# ab692) and F-actin (Cat# ab205) were supplied by Abcam Biochemicals (Cambridge, UK). Rabbit polyclonal antibody of Bax (Cat# sc-20067) was obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA, USA). Mouse monoclonal antibodies of Occludin (Cat# sc-5562), Integrin β1 (Cat# sc-8978), RhoA (Cat# sc-418), ROCK (Cat# sc-17794), Rac1 (Cat# sc-514583), Cdc42 (Cat# sc-8401), AQP3 (Cat# sc-518001), HAS2 (Cat# sc-365263), and HYAL1 (Cat# sc-101340) were also obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA, USA). Mouse monoclonal β-actin antibody (Cat# A1978) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical (St. Louis, MO, USA). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG and goat anti-mouse IgG were purchased from Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories (West Grove, PA, USA).

Cell Culture

The immortalized human keratinocyte cell line (HaCaT) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 μg/ml streptomycin, 2 mM glutamine, 0.75 μg/ml human epidermal growth factor, and 75 μg/ml hydrocortisone at 37 °C humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 in air. HaCaT were sub-cultured at 85% confluence. To mimic a hyperglycemia environment, immortalized keratinocytes were incubated in media containing 33 mM glucose, compared with normal media containing 5.5 mM glucose. Additionally, HaCaT were incubated for 3days in media containing 1–20 μM unbelliferone.

The viability of HaCaT was assessed using the MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-dephenyltertrazolium bromide) assay. The cells were incubated with 1 mg/ml MTT solution at 37 °C for 3 hours, resulting in the formation of an insoluble purple formazan product. This product was then dissolved in 250 μl of isopropanol. The optical density of the solution was measured using a microplate reader at a wavelength of 570 nm.

In vivo animal experiments

Adult male db/db mice (C57BLKS/+Leprdb Iar; Jackson Laboratory, Sacramento, CA, USA) and age-matched non-diabetic male db/m littermates (C57BLKS/J; Jackson Laboratory, Sacramento, CA, USA) were used in this study. Since db/db mice typically develop hyperglycemia by 7–8 weeks of age, 7-week-old mice were selected for the experiment. Mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions on a 12-hour light/dark cycle at 23 ± 1 °C and 50 ± 10% relative humidity. They were provided with a standard laboratory chow diet (CJ Feed, Seoul, Republic of Korea) and had ad libitum access to water at the Hallym University animal facility. Before beginning the experimental interventions, all animals were allowed a one-week acclimatization period. Mice were then divided into three subgroups (n = 9–10 per group): a non-diabetic db/m control group and two db/db groups. One db/db group received a daily oral gavage of umbelliferone (10 mg/kg body weight), while the other group received daily water gavage for 10 weeks. Throughout the study, no mortality or signs of exhaustion were observed in any group

All experiments were approved by the Hallym University Committee on Animal Experimentation and conducted in accordance with the University’s Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (hallym(2023-18)).

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis was performed using whole cell lysates obtained from HaCaT (3.5 × 105 cells/dish) and skin tissue extracts from mouse skin. The lysates were prepared using a lysis buffer containing 1 M β-glycerophosphate, 1% β-mercaptoethanol, 0.5 M NaF, 0.1 M Na3VO4, and protease inhibitor cocktails. Equal amounts of protein from HaCaT and skin tissue lysates were loaded and separated by electrophoresis on 8–15% SDS-PAGE gels, and then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. To block nonspecific binding, the membranes were incubated for 3 hours with either 3% fatty acid-free BSA or 5% nonfat dry skim milk. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific to the target proteins. After washing the membranes with Tris-buffered saline-Tween 20 for 10 minutes, they were incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated to HRP (goat anti-rabbit IgG, goat anti-mouse IgG, or donkey anti-goat IgG) for 1 hour. The levels of each target protein were detected using an Immobilon Western chemiluminescent horseradish peroxidase substrate (Millipore Corporation, Burlington, MA, USA) and detected through chemiluminescence using a ChemiDoc (Cytiva). As comparative controls, incubation with a mouse monoclonal β-actin antibody was also carried out. Quantitative analysis of Western blot results was conducted using densitometry. Band intensities were measured with ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) and normalized to the corresponding β-actin bands. Relative expression levels were calculated and statistically analyzed.

Rhodamine phalloidine staining

HaCaTs (0.7 × 10^5 cells/slide) were cultured and exposed to 33 mM glucose in the presence or absence of 1-20 μM umbelliferone. The cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 10 minutes and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Following that, cells were treated with 10 units of the fluorescent dye rhodamine phalloidin and incubated for 20 minutes. Additionally, nuclear staining was performed using 1 μg/mL of 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Each slide was then mounted using VectaMount mounting medium from Vector Laboratories (Burlingame, CA, USA. Fluorescent images were captured using an Axiomager Optical fluorescence microscope (Nicon GmbH, Düsseldorf, Germany). The protein level of F-actin was quantified using an image analysis program provided by the microscope system.

Scratch wound assay

To assess the impact of glucose on the motility of immortalized keratinocytes and the potential effects of umbelliferone, an in vitro scratch wound assay was conducted. HaCaT cells were seeded in 12-well plates and incubated for 24 hours in media containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Upon reaching confluency, a horizontal scratch was made in each well using a pipette tip. Following the scratch, the cells were incubated for an additional 24 hours in culture media, either with or without 33 mM high glucose, and with or without 1–20 μM umbelliferone. Images of the scratch wounds were captured in 2–3 microscopic fields per well using the microscope system. The extent of keratinocyte migration was indicated by the scratched area. Quantification of the wound area was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA)

Immunostaining

HaCaT cells (0.7 × 10^5 cells/slide) were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 10 minutes, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 on ice. To prevent nonspecific protein binding, cells were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 hour. The primary antibody for ZO-1 was diluted 1:100 in 5% BSA and incubated overnight at 4 °C. For cytochemical staining, FITC-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Sigma-Aldrich Chemical, St. Louis, MO, USA) was diluted 1:500 in PBS and applied for 30 minutes. The primary antibody for AQP3 was also diluted 1:200 in 5% BSA and incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by staining with Cy3-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Sigma-Aldrich Chemical, St. Louis, MO, USA) diluted 1:500 in PBS for 30 minutes. Nuclear staining was performed with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) diluted 1:1000 in PBS, and slides were mounted with glycerol (Sigma-Aldrich Chemical, St. Louis, MO, USA). Images were acquired using an inverted microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) for visualization.

ROS formation

Dihydroethidium (DHE, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) staining was performed to assess ROS production in HaCaT cells. HaCaT cells (0.7 × 10^5 cells/slide) were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 10 minutes, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton-X100 on ice for 10 minutes. Cells were then incubated with 20 μM DHE for 1 hour to stain for ROS. Nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 minutes, and stained cells were mounted with a mounting medium. Skin tissue specimens were also prepared by fixation and sectioned to 8 μm thickness, followed by DHE staining. Images of the stained slides and tissue sections were captured using an inverted optical microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Oxidative generation in HaCaT cells was assessed using 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCF-DA) staining. Cells challenged with 33 mM glucose were incubated with 10 μM DCF-DA in pre-warmed DMEM for 30 minutes at 37 °C. After dye loading, cells were washed thoroughly with phosphate-buffered saline, and nuclear staining with DAPI was performed. Images of the cultured cells were also captured using an optical microscope.

Hoechst 33258 staining

After incubating HaCaT cells for 15 minutes on glass-covered 24-well plates, cells were treated with 10 µg/mL Hoechst 33258 (Promega Co., Madison, WI, USA) for nuclear staining. Cells exhibiting fragmented or condensed nuclei were categorized as apoptotic. Each slide was imaged using an optical microscope system to detect nuclear morphology.

Immunohistochemical staining

For immunohistochemical staining, skin samples were collected at the end of the experiments and fixed in 10% buffered formalin. The paraffin-embedded skin tissues were sectioned at 8 μm thickness, deparaffinized, and rehydrated through xylene and graded ethanol solutions. Sections were incubated overnight with a primary antibody against ZO-1, followed by FITC-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG. Nuclear staining was performed with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA). Each slide was mounted with VectaMount mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). Images were captured using an optical microscope.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining of skin tissue and measurement of epidermis/dermis thickness

Paraffin-embedded skin specimens were cut into 8 μM thick sections, dewaxed and rehydrated, and stained with eosin reagent to assess and hematoxylin for counterstaining. The stained tissue slides were examined using a microscope equipped. The thickness of skin epidermis and dermis was analyzed by ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), with a significance level set at p < 0.05. Significant differences between treatment groups, indicated by different letters in the figures, were determined using one-way ANOVA. Tukey’s post hoc test was subsequently applied to identify specific group differences. Results are expressed as mean ± SD.

The protective role of umbelliferone against high glucose-induced suppression of epidermal barrier protein expression

Western blot analysis revealed that treatment with umbelliferone in a dose-dependent manner resulted in a gradual increase in the expression of filaggrin, which plays a key role in the differentiation of epidermal keratinocytes (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, the expression of ZO-1 and Occludin, proteins involved in tight junctions between cells, was assessed via Western blot. The results indicated that the decreased expression of these proteins under high-glucose conditions was restored following umbelliferone treatment (Fig. 2A). Additionally, to visualize ZO-1 at cell junctions, immunofluorescence staining was performed using FITC (fluorescein isothiocyanate). In high-glucose conditions, ZO-1 between the cells showed a reduction, resulting in weak green fluorescence (indicated by white arrows). In contrast, HaCaT cells treated with umbelliferone exhibited strong green fluorescence of ZO-1, indicating the restoration of tight junctions (Fig. 2B). In addition, Western blot analysis using skin tissue from the diabetic animal model, db/db mice, showed that the expression of epidermal barrier-associated proteins such as filaggrin, ZO-1, and occludin was decreased in diabetic skin, mirroring results from in vitro studies. Directed cell migration is a physical process requiring significant changes in cell shape and adhesion to the extracellular matrix (ECM). In this context, we examined the protein expression of the dynamic filamentous actin (F-actin), which generates physical forces during migration, and integrin β1, which mediates adhesion to the ECM. Prevention of impaired skin epidermal moisturizing ability by Umbelliferone

The metabolism of HA depends upon its synthesis and degradation by HAS and HYAL enzymes, respectively. HAS2 is an enzyme involved in the synthesis of hyaluronic acid, which contributes to the formation of the skin’s moisture barrier and supports elasticity. Conversely, HYAL is an enzyme responsible for the degradation of hyaluronic acid, opposing the function of HAS. AQP3 is a membrane transport protein embedded in the cell membrane, responsible for moving extracellular water into the cell and playing an essential role in water transport in keratinocytes. To examine changes in moisture-retention-related proteins in epidermal keratinocytes exposed to a high glucose environment, we assessed the expression of AQP3, HYAL1, and HAS2 through Western blot analysis (Fig. 5A). In high glucose conditions, HYAL activity increased, while HAS2 protein expression decreased, along with a reduction in AQP3 levels. We also investigated whether a high glucose environment induces apoptosis in skin keratinocytes. From the previous MTT assay results, we observed approximately a 20% reduction in cell viability in HaCaT cells cultured in high glucose compared to those in standard culture media (Fig. 1C). For further confirmation, nuclear staining using Hoechst was performed on HaCaT cells. As shown in Fig. 5C, fragmented and shrunken nuclei were observed in the HG (33 mM) environment. In contrast, umbelliferone treatment led to a dose-dependent reduction in nuclear fragmentation. To further support these findings, Western blot analysis was conducted to measure the expression of the pro-apoptotic marker Bax and the anti-apoptotic marker Bcl-2 (Fig. 6D). As expected, in high glucose conditions, the expression of the apoptosis marker Bax increased, while the expression of the anti-apoptotic marker Bcl-2 decreased. However, with umbelliferone treatment, the expression levels of these proteins were reversed. Additionally, analysis of Bax and Bcl-2 protein expression in diabetic mouse skin tissue supported the in vitro findings (Fig. 6E).

Discussion

Skin complications are well-documented in individuals with diabetes, where chronic hyperglycemia plays a critical role in the pathology of various secondary complications across multiple organ systems, including the skin. Previous studies have shown that umbelliferone can enhance wound healing and improve dermal barrier function in diabetes-induced skin fibroblasts, counteracting the delayed healing effects commonly observed in diabetic skin [15]. In this study, we investigated the effects of umbelliferone on epidermal barrier disruption, ROS accumulation, and apoptosis in keratinocytes under hyperglycemic conditions, further exploring its impact on the diabetic epidermis—a focus not previously covered in depth. Our findings indicate that umbelliferone strengthens cell-to-cell junctions within the epidermis and promotes cell migration by regulating filamentous actin dynamics through modulation of GTP-binding proteins, thereby enhancing skin moisture retention capacity. Furthermore, umbelliferone prevents hyperglycemia-induced ROS accumulation and apoptosis, contributing to overall epidermal barrier integrity.

Keratinocytes, cells in the epidermis, are essential for forming the epidermal permeability barrier (EPB), which protects the skin from external environments. However, both human diabetic patients and murine models of type 2 diabetes exhibit altered epidermal permeability barriers [17]. Tight junctions (TJs) in the skin contribute to various functions, including barrier integrity, cell differentiation, and proliferation. Studies have shown that claudin-based TJs serve as a paracellular barrier in mouse epidermis [18]. The abnormal expression of TJ proteins has been linked to common human inflammatory skin disorders, such as atopic dermatitis (AD) and psoriasis (PS), as well as skin tumors [19, 20]. Recent research also suggests that TJs in skin cells are compromised not only due to inflammatory responses but also from the effects of diabetes [15, 17, 21]. In this study, we investigated the alterations in TJs in keratinocytes under hyperglycemic conditions and in diabetic skin tissues. We observed decreased expression of the epidermal differentiation marker filaggrin, along with TJ proteins ZO-1 and occludin, in keratinocytes exposed to chronic hyperglycemia. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining of skin tissue in diabetic mice revealed a significant reduction in ZO-1 expression in the epidermis. However, with umbelliferone treatment, cell junctions were maintained at levels comparable to the control group.

Skin epidermal moisture retention is crucial for maintaining the integrity and function of the skin barrier, as moisture balance directly affects the barrier’s structure, strength, and resilience against environmental stressors. Key proteins involved in this process—such as AQP3, HAS2, and HYAL1—play interconnected roles that collectively support barrier health. Hyaluronic acid (HA), a major component of the extracellular matrix, is essential for skin hydration and structure [22]. HA not only retains moisture but also contributes to skin elasticity, provides a structural matrix for collagen and elastin, and supports cell migration during wound healing [23]. AQP3 acts as a water and glycerol channel in keratinocytes, aiding in moisture retention. HAS2 synthesizes HA, supporting the extracellular matrix, while HYAL1, a lysosomal enzyme, breaks down hyaluronan, thereby regulating HA levels. When the skin barrier is damaged, its protective function is compromised, leading to water loss, dryness, and increased susceptibility to external irritants. Our study found that hyperglycemia disrupts HA homeostasis by accelerating HA degradation and reducing its synthesis, which weakens the skin barrier. HAS2 and HYAL1, enzymes responsible for HA synthesis and degradation, respectively, are crucial for maintaining HA balance in the skin. Furthermore, hyperglycemia was shown to decrease AQP3 expression, disrupting moisture balance and impairing barrier function. H&E staining in diabetic mice revealed a reduction in both epidermal and dermal thickness, confirming these disruptions. However, treatment with umbelliferone restored HA balance and AQP3 expression, strengthening the epidermal barrier under hyperglycemic conditions.

Impaired keratinocyte migration leads to delayed re-epithelialization, a critical process in wound healing that functions to close the wound and restore skin integrity. Cell migration is a highly regulated, multistep process involving leading-edge protrusion, ECM adhesion, traction generation, and rear adhesion release [24]. The movement is primarily driven by the dynamic filamentous actin (F-actin) cytoskeleton, which generates the necessary forces for migration [25]. During migration, F-actin polymerizes to form structures like lamellipodia and filopodia at the cell’s leading edge, allowing the cell to push forward [26]. Additionally, F-actin contractions help generate traction forces that pull the cell body forward [27]. This cytoskeletal structure connects to the ECM through integrins, which are transmembrane proteins that act as anchors and signal transmitters between the ECM and the cytoskeleton, including F-actin [28]. Integrin-mediated linkages provide essential traction forces and activate signaling pathways that regulate actin dynamics, enabling cells to respond to their external environment [27]. Key regulators, particularly Rho family GTPases, coordinate cell protrusion, adhesion, and contraction by linking the actin cytoskeleton with integrin-mediated adhesion through a complex network of mechanical and biochemical signals [28]. Various studies have demonstrated increased activation of RhoA, Cdc42, and Rac1 in cell motility [29, 30]. Specifically, RhoA activation facilitates focal adhesion complex formation and actin stress fiber development, while Rac1 and Cdc42 control actin polymerization, driving lamellipodia formation and membrane protrusions at the leading edge in migrating cells [31]. Our findings, confirmed through a cell scratch assay, demonstrate that hyperglycemia significantly inhibits keratinocyte migration, as shown by reduced expression of migration-related proteins such as F-actin and integrin β1. Rhodamine-phalloidin staining further revealed cytoskeletal disruptions with notable loss of filaments. We also observed decreased activation of key migration signaling molecules, including RhoA, ROCK, Rac1, and Cdc42, indicating reduced expression of migration-related factors in diabetic conditions. In contrast, umbelliferone treatment restored these proteins and improved migratory capacity in both keratinocytes and diabetic skin tissue, suggesting umbelliferone’s potential therapeutic benefits in diabetic wound healing.

It is well established that ROS production increases in diabetes while intracellular antioxidant defenses are diminished, leading to elevated oxidative stress. Chronic hyperglycemic conditions amplify oxidative stress through multiple pathways, rather than a single route. Excessive intracellular glucose metabolism activates several mechanisms, including aldose reductase activity, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, NAD(P)H oxidase activation, and shifts in the NADPH/NADP ratio [32–34]. Numerous studies have reported that the oxidative stress resulting from excessive ROS production under hyperglycemia is a major contributor to diabetes-related complications [35–37]. While a minimal level of ROS is necessary for cell survival, excessive ROS accumulation within cellular compartments can result in slowed cell growth, cell cycle arrest, and apoptosis [38]. Chronic hyperglycemia is known to cause the buildup of ROS in various cell types throughout the body, ultimately leading to cell death [39, 40]. Apoptosis, a crucial process for programmed cell death, generally functions to remove damaged or harmful cells. However, in the diabetic environment, imbalances between pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic factors have been reported to increase cell death [41, 42]. Our findings demonstrate that hyperglycemic conditions in keratinocytes elevate oxidative stress, as shown by increased ROS accumulation with DHE staining revealing elevated superoxide anions (O₂•⁻) and DCF-DA indicating a broader ROS increase, including hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and hydroxyl radicals (•OH).

We also observed excessive DNA fragmentation in keratinocytes exposed to high glucose and found that hyperglycemia stimulated pro-apoptotic Bax signaling while suppressing anti-apoptotic Bcl-2. Notably, treatment with umbelliferone reduced hyperglycemia-induced ROS accumulation and restored the balance of apoptosis-regulating factors, effectively inhibiting cell death.

The findings of this study suggest that umbelliferone holds promise in addressing epidermal barrier dysfunction by preventing oxidative stress, ROS accumulation, and apoptosis in diabetic keratinocytes. Specifically, it appears to preserve cellular junctions and maintain epidermal hydration, thereby supporting skin barrier integrity under hyperglycemic conditions

While our findings provide compelling evidence for the protective effects of umbelliferone on the epidermal barrier under hyperglycemic conditions, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the in vitro component of this study utilized HaCaT cells, an immortalized human keratinocyte line, which—despite their widespread use—may not fully recapitulate the behavior of primary human keratinocytes in vivo. Second, our in vivo experiments were conducted exclusively in male db/db mice, a widely accepted model for type 2 diabetes, but interspecies differences and sex-specific factors may limit the direct extrapolation of our results to human patients.

Future studies should aim to validate these findings in primary human keratinocytes and in additional in vivo models, including both sexes and potentially larger animals, to better approximate human physiology. Moreover, the long-term safety, pharmacokinetics, and optimal dosing of umbelliferone for dermatological use in diabetic individuals warrant further investigation. Mechanistic studies exploring its impact on broader signaling networks, inflammatory mediators, and clinical wound healing endpoints would further strengthen the translational relevance of our work.

Conclusions

In summary, our study examined the effects of umbelliferone on the skin epidermal barrier in high glucose-induced keratinocytes and diabetic skin tissues. We found that umbelliferone strengthened the epidermal barrier by preserving cell-to-cell junctions and enhancing skin hydration through the regulation of key enzymes. It also promoted keratinocyte migration and supported actin dynamics by modulating Rho GTPase signaling. Furthermore, umbelliferone inhibited the elevated accumulation of ROS and cell apoptosis under diabetic conditions. Overall, these findings highlight the potential of umbelliferone as a therapeutic agent for diabetic skin complications.
