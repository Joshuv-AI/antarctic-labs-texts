SNAP23 regulates CPEB4 in the autophagy of hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Jianzuo Yao, Jingyi Wu, Yaoye Tao, Xie Zhang, Shenzhe Fang, Dansong Yu, Haibiao Wang, Xiaomin Yao, Hong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SNAP23 and CPEB4 exhibit promising potential in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). This study aimed to investigate their roles in HCC progression and the underlying molecular mechanisms. Using bioinformatics analysis and in vitro experiments in Huh7 cells, we found that SNAP23 was upregulated in HCC and positively correlated with CPEB4. Silencing either gene significantly suppressed cell proliferation and migration while inducing apoptosis. Mechanistically, SNAP23 acted upstream of CPEB4 to regulate autophagy and oxidative stress. Knockdown of SNAP23 or CPEB4 impaired autophagic flux and reduced reactive oxygen species levels. These findings identify SNAP23 and CPEB4 as critical regulators of HCC growth and motility through autophagy‑dependent pathways, highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49363-9.

Introduction

Currently, primary liver cancer (PLC) ranks sixth in incidence and third in mortality among all malignancies, posing a serious threat to the health of the global population1. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the predominant form of PLC at 90%, primarily relies on surgery for long-term patient survival. However, hepatectomy is viable only for early, resectable tumors. Given that a large proportion of patients are diagnosed with advanced-stage cancer, nonsurgical approaches became necessary for HCC treatment2. Therefore, it is critical to unveil the molecular pathways of HCC and provide targeted therapies.

SNAP23 (Synaptosomal-associated protein of 23 kDa) belongs to the SNARE (Soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor) complex, a group of proteins known in membrane fusion events3. It interacts with VAMP (Vesicle-associated membrane protein) and STX (Syntaxin) to assemble the SNARE complex and mediate the specific fusion4,5. SNAP23 contributes to the progression of cancer cells by various pathways. For example, SNAP23 promoted the malignant progression of ovarian cancer through increasing cell apoptosis; SNAP23 inhibited the cell cycle of cervical cancer; SNAP23-STX4-VAMP7 complex promoted invasion of breast cancer cells by degradation of extracellular matrix6–8. Moreover, SNAP23 phosphorylation induces the formation and secretion of tumor exosomes, which promotes tumor progression9,10. However, roles of SNAP23 in HCC are yet to be defined. It is necessary to further study SNAP23 to understand the pathophysiology and progression of HCC, which would improve the prognosis of HCC patients.

CPEBs (Cytoplasmic polyadenylation element binding proteins) are RNA-binding proteins that specifically target CPE sequences within the 3’ untranslated regions of mRNA, thereby controlling the translation process11. This protein family includes four members including CPEB1-4. CPEB4 is expressed in the brain, heart, lungs, and kidneys, and it also been shown to be abnormally expressed in a variety of tumors, such as up-regulated expression in colorectal cancer, malignant glioma, and pancreatic cancer12–14. In addition, CPEB4 allowed cells to adapt to sustained endoplasmic reticulum stress and then maintained cellular fitness, which kept the cytotoxic activity of CD8 T lymphocytes to play an antitumor role15. For HCC, CPEB4 was usually down-regulated in cancer tissue, but it was also found to be up-regulated in the early stage of HCC16,17. This bidirectional expression indicates the complex function of CPEB4 in HCC.

Autophagy, a universally conserved mechanism across all eukaryotes, is essential for recycling senescent organelles and waste proteins in cells. Within the framework of HCC, autophagy assumes a dual function, acting as an inhibitor of cell growth factor in the early stage while also promoting tumor development in established HCC tissue18. Meanwhile, modulating autophagy, either by inducing or inhibiting it, could regulate the proliferation of HCC cells, which provides a potential strategy for HCC treatment19. Previous research has demonstrated that SNAP23 is involved in autophagy, both canonical and non-canonical20,21. Moreover, CPEB4 affects autophagy by modulating the post-transcriptional translation of genes associated with this process22,23. However, the interplay between SNAP23 and CPEB4 and their roles in HCC autophagy remain to be explored.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the specific roles of SNAP23 and CPEB4 in the pathogenesis and progression of HCC, with a particular focus on their functions in autophagy regulation within HCC cells. Additionally, we seek to elucidate the potential regulatory relationship between SNAP23 and CPEB4 in HCC. The findings are expected to provide a novel theoretical foundation for a deeper understanding of HCC mechanisms and to identify potential therapeutic targets for HCC treatment.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

Bioinformatics analysis was performed in the TIMER3 platform (https://compbio.cn/timer3/). To discern the differential expression patterns of the SNAP23 gene across various types of cancer, the DE module within the “Exploration” section was employed. The correlations between the gene expression of SNAP23 and CPEB4, SNAP23 and ATG12, SNAP23 and P62, CPEB4 and ATG12, CPEB4 and P62 were also conducted using the Gene_Correlation feature.

Cell culture and gene silence

The Huh7 cell line was obtained by Qingqi Biotechnology Development Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The THLE-2 cell line was purchased from BaiDi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). The cell cultures were nurtured in a solution of DMEM, enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin and streptomycin. The incubation process occurred at 37 °C within a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The culture media used for these cells was supplied by Biosharp (Beijing, China).

To knockdown SNAP23 or CPEB4 in vitro, three small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) against SNAP23 (si-SNAP23) and three CPEB4 (si-CPEB4) mRNAs were respectively designed by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The interference sequences were listed in Table 1. Huh7 cell line was divided into following groups: the GP-transfect-Mate group (a transfection reagent, from GenePharma), si-SNAP23-1, si-SNAP23-2, si-SNAP23-3, si-CPEB4-1, si-CPEB4-2 and si-CPEB4-3 group. After 48 h of transfection, we detected mRNA expression, followed by protein expression at 72 h.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

To evaluate the expression of SNAP23 in hepatocellular carcinoma cells, immunofluorescence staining was performed on Huh7 and THLE-2 cells. Cells seeded on glass coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA to prevent non-specific binding. The samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a rabbit primary antibody against SNAP23 (ProteinTech, Chicago, USA), followed by incubation with Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Abbkine, Wuhan, China) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 5 µg/mL DAPI, and coverslips were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium. Fluorescence images were captured using a microscope, and the mean fluorescence intensity of SNAP23 was quantified using ImageJ software.

Real time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

The processes of total RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and gene expression quantification were all conducted using kits provided by Vazyme, a biotechnology company based in Nanjing, China. The 20 µl qPCR mix consisted of 1 µl cDNA, 10 µl ChamQ SYBR Master Mix, and 0.4 µl of each 10 µM primer solution. The protocol included a 95 °C denaturation for 30 s, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s, a melting curve from 95 °C to 60 °C, and a final 95 °C step for 15 s. The sequences of the primers used are detailed in Table 2.

Western blot

The target cells were lysed with PMSF and RIPA buffer (Biosharp, Beijing, China), a formulation that contains protease inhibitors to prevent degradation. Cell lysates were spun at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, after which the supernatants were extracted for protein quantification using a BCA kit (Biosharp, Beijing, China). SDS-PAGE (Biosharp, Beijing, China) was conducted with 20 µg of each sample, followed by protein transfer to a PVDF membrane (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The membranes were probed with primary antibodies against SNAP23, CPEB4, ATG12, P62, LC3 and β-actin overnight at 4 °C, then with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. All antibodies used were provided by ProteinTech Group, Inc. (Chicago, USA). Details regarding these antibodies are provided in Table 3. Band visualization was achieved with an ECL imaging system (NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China), and band intensity quantification was performed by IQTL 7.0 software.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)

The procedure involved seeding cells into 96-well plates at a density of 1000 cells per 100 µl of medium in each well, then maintained in an incubator for 1.5 h set to 37 °C with a 5% CO2 atmosphere. At specific time (0, 24, 48, 72 and 96 h), each well was added with 10 µl CCK-8 reagent (GLPBIO, Montclair, USA). The metabolic activity of the cells was assessed by optical density value at 450 nm.

The terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT)-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL)

The TUNEL assay was employed utilizing a kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) to detect apoptosis in Huh7 cells. Treated Huh7 cells were fixed with formaldehyde. Then followed by permeabilization to allow the TUNEL reagents access to the DNA within the cells. The TUNEL reaction introduces labeled nucleotides at DNA breaks, indicating apoptotic cells. Finally, after application of DAPI staining, TUNEL-positive cells were marked by green fluorescence. The quantitative analysis of apoptosis was enumerated by ImageJ software. The apoptotic index was calculated as the percentage of cells exhibiting TUNEL positivity among all DAPI-stained nuclei.

Wound healing

Cells were grown to 80% confluence and then scored with five perpendicular lines. Wound areas were photographed at 0, 24, and 48 h of culturing by a microscope. The wound healing rate was calculated as: [(Initial wound area - Area at time of photography) / Initial wound area] × 100%, with measurements taken using ImageJ software.

Autophagic flux detection

To detect the effect of SNAP23 and CPEB4 in autophagic flux, Huh7 cells were transfected with a mCherry-GFP-LC3b plasmid, synthesized by GenePharma, Shanghai, China, targeting three distinct groups: GP-transfect-Mate, si-SNAP23-1, and si-CPEB4-1. Following transfection, cells were maintained at 37 °C with a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 12 h. Subsequently, the culture medium was refreshed with a fresh complete medium, and cells were further incubated for 48 h. After washing and fixing, cells were mounted on glass slides for fluorescence analysis. Confocal microscopy was conducted promptly to visualize the autophagic flux.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

ROS were determined utilizing a kit from UElandy Inc. (Suzhou, China), which incorporates the dihydroethidium (DHE) probe. By the manufacturer, the oxidized byproducts of DHE were integrated into the chromosomal DNA, resulting in the emission of red fluorescence. Following the cellular intervention, a 20 µM solution of DHE was introduced and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 1 h. The resultant red fluorescence within the cells, indicative of ROS levels, was subsequently measured using laser confocal microscopy to assess both the intensity and alterations in fluorescence.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and were subjected to statistical analysis and graphical representation using GraphPad Prism version 8.0, where a T-test was employed to compare the means. The criterion for statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05.

Results

Expression of SNAP23 in HCC cells

TIMER3 tool was employed to analyze the TCGA pan-cancer dataset, which discerned that SNAP23 exhibited higher expression in malignant tissues as opposed to the surrounding benign tissues in several cancer types, including CHOL (Cholangiocarcinoma), ESCA (Esophageal carcinoma), HNSC (Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma), LIHC (Liver hepatocellular carcinoma) and STAD (Stomach adenocarcinoma). Conversely, a reduction in SNAP23 expression was observed in cancerous tissues relative to their non-cancerous counterparts in KICH (Kidney chromophobe), LUAD (Lung adenocarcinoma), LUSC (Lung squamous cell carcinoma), PCPG (Pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma), PEAD (Rectum adenocarcinoma) and UCEC ( Uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma). Furthermore, in SKCM (Skin cutaneous melanoma), elevated SNAP23 levels were detected in metastatic lesions in contrast to primary tumor tissues (Fig. 1A).

As shown in Fig. 1B, the mRNA level of SNAP23 was significantly higher in Huh7 cells than in THLE-2 cells. This upregulation was also confirmed at the protein level, with SNAP23 expression being markedly elevated in Huh7 cells compared to THLE-2 cells (Fig. 1C and D). In addition, immunofluorescence staining revealed that SNAP23 was localized to the plasma membrane (Fig. 1C). These results collectively demonstrate that SNAP23 is upregulated in Huh7 cells, thereby this cell line was selected for subsequent RNA interference experiments.

Interference of SNAP23 and CPEB4 in Huh7 cells

RNA interference technology was applied to silence SNAP23 and CPEB4 in Huh7 cells, and their interference efficiency needs to be detected. A total of 5 groups were designed for SNAP23 or CPEB4, including the control group, GP-transfect-Mate group, and 3 experimental groups (si-RNA-1, si-RNA-2 and si-RNA-3). Western blot of si-SNAP23 results were shown in Fig. 2A and B, and si-CPEB4 results in Fig. 2C and D. The RT-qPCR results of si-SNAP23 were shown in Fig. 2E, and si-CPEB4 results in Fig. 2F. These results showed that the si-SNAP23-1 and si-CPEB4-1 group exhibited the highest interference efficiency compared to the GP-transfect-Mate group in Huh7 cells. Therefore, this study selected si-SNAP23-1 and si-CPEB4-1 for the next experiment.

Based on the fact that SNAP23 and CPEB4 have the same action trend in HCC expression, proliferation, apoptosis and migration, we further investigate the relationship between SNAP23 and CEPB4 in HCC by bioinformatics analysis, RT-qPCR and Western blot. Discussion

The enduring therapeutic outcomes for HCC remain suboptimal, largely attributable to a high rate of tumor recurrence post-treatment. Even with surgery, the 5-year recurrence rate for HCC can reach 70%, markedly degrading patient outcome24. Therefore, identifying novel therapeutic targets and devising potent pharmaceuticals for HCC is imperative. A previous study indicated an increase in SNAP23 mRNA levels within HCC, revealing that SNAP23 could serve as a promising therapeutic target for this disease25.

To further detect their function in HCC, RNA interference technology in Huh7 cells was used in this study. Firstly, SNAP23 and CPEB4 gene expression were notably upregulated in human liver cell line Huh7. Additionally, both SNAP23 and CPEB4 knockdown have been demonstrated to reduce the proliferation and migration capabilities of HCC cells, whereas promoted cell apoptosis. Tumor-derived exosomes contributed to tumorigenesis and metastasis. SNAP23 was identified to mediate the exosome secretion process in breast, cervical and melanoma cancer cells, which promoted cancer metastasis26,27. In this study, SNAP23 knockdown inhibited Huh7 cells’ migration by regulating autophagy and oxidative stress, which enriched the role of SNAP23 in cancer.

Interestingly, CPEB4 knockdown also showed this phenomenon, and SNAP23 is the upstream gene of CPEB4 in HCC cells. As a membrane protein, SNAP23 contributes to membrane fusion by forming SNARE complexes and is implicated in cellular signaling transduction28. The Mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway activates downstream molecules through sequential phosphorylation, with the final phosphorylation of MAPK proteins entering the nucleus to initiate transcription of downstream genes. Extracellular regulated protein kinases (ERK), a MAPK protein, mediates the RAS/RAF/ERK pathway closely related to various cellular processes29. Many SNARE proteins interact with RAS, and SNAP23 is involved. Furthermore, ERK can regulate the activity of CPEB4, achieving post-transcriptional regulation of CPEB4 target genes30,31. It is hypothesized that the mechanism of SNAP23 regulation of CPEB4 may involve the activation of ERK phosphorylation through the RAS/RAF/ERK pathway. As a critical conduit for mitogenic signals, this pathway regulates fundamental neoplastic processes including cell cycle progression, with its aberrant activation serving as a key molecular hallmark in numerous malignancies. The clinical translation of this knowledge has led to the approval of multiple targeted agents, such as BRAF and MEK inhibitors, which have significantly improved outcomes for patients with MAPK-driven tumors32. Based on our preliminary literature review, we identified that CPEB4 may abnormally activate the MAPK signaling pathway, thereby promoting the progression of gallbladder cancer33. Hence, it leads us to hypothesize that a similar signaling pathway might also be irregularly activated in HCC. This work provides the first evidence that SNAP23 modulates CPEB4 expression in HCC cells. Further research is warranted to explore precise regulatory mechanisms by which SNAP23 influences CPEB4, as well as to understand the etiology of HCC better and to pave the way for the creation of potentially efficacious treatment options.

The mRNA of the autophagy key factor ATG12 contains a CPE sequence, which is bound by CPEB4 to regulate the post-transcriptional translation of ATG12, affecting autophagosome maturation22,23. This regulatory direction was also observed in our research. Autophagosome maturation involves two ubiquitination-like processes: the linkage of ATG5 with ATG12 and the conjugation of LC3. The LC3 protein is processed by ATG4 at its C-terminus, leading to the generation of the cytosolic form known as LC3-I. Upon the initiation of autophagy, LC3-I engages in a ubiquitin-like conjugation process facilitated by ATG7 and ATG3, leading to the formation of LC3-II, which is then integrated into autophagosomes. The ratio of LC3-II/LC3-I is commonly used to assess autophagy levels. In our study, the reduced LC3-II/LC3-I ratio could indicate either suppressed autophagosome maturation or enhanced clearance. However, the downregulation of ATG12, together with the impairment of autophagosome-lysosome fusion (as evidenced by relatively increased autophagosomes but decreased autolysosomes) upon SNAP23 or CPEB4 silencing, supports suppressed autophagosome maturation rather than enhanced clearance. SNAP23 and CPEB4 are not only involved in autophagosome maturation but also affect the formation of autophagolysosomes in hepatocellular carcinoma cells. This study reveals for the first time that SNAP23 modulates autophagy in liver cancer cells by regulating CPEB4, offering a novel mechanistic insight into autophagic processes in HCC.

P62 serves as a substrate for autophagy, yet it is not exclusively reliant on this pathway for its degradation. The ubiquitin-proteasome system is an alternative mechanism capable of degrading P6234. In our research, the inhibition of autophagy was coupled with a decrease in P62 levels, potentially due to the enhanced activity of the ubiquitin-proteasome system as a compensatory mechanism when autophagy is dampened. Future investigations should concentrate on the interplay between the SNAP23-CPEB4 axis and the balance between the ubiquitin-proteasome system and autophagy. Moreover, P62 exerts its antioxidant function by competitively binding to Keap1, thereby stabilizing and activating Nrf2, which leads to the transcriptional upregulation of cytoprotective and antioxidant genes35. It is plausible that the diminished ROS levels resulting from SNAP23 or CPEB4 knockdown attenuate the cellular reliance on P62-Nrf2-mediated antioxidant defense, consequently leading to decreased P62 expression.

SNAP23 contributes to ROS generation in colon cancer cells via oxidative phosphorylation, and knockdown of SNAP23 by siRNA significantly attenuates ROS production36. However, in non-small cell lung cancer cells, silencing CPEB4 results in elevated ROS production37. In our study, the silencing of SNAP23 or CPEB4 in Huh7 cells led to a decreased level of ROS. These data indicate that SNAP23 drives ROS production through a pathway involving CPEB4, yet the molecular details of how CPEB4 facilitates ROS generation in HCC require further investigation. It is recognized that the buildup of ROS triggers the onset of autophagy. This correlation suggests that SNAP23 and CPEB4 may regulate autophagy in liver cancer cells by influencing ROS levels, indicating a potential role for these proteins in modulating the cellular response to autophagy through oxidative stress. Given that autophagy functions as a suppressor of apoptosis, the increased apoptosis observed in Huh7 cells after si-SNAP23 or CPEB4 may be attributed to the decrease in autophagic activity38. SNAP23 and CPEB4 may provide treatment targets for HCC patients. Further research into the mechanistic details and clinical validation of these findings is warranted to maximize the therapeutic potential of SNAP23 and CPEB4 as targets for HCC treatment.

Conclusion

In this study, the expression levels of SNAP23 were mostly different in cancers and higher in HCC cells than normal liver cells. After silencing the genes of SNAP23 or CPEB4 in Huh7 cells, we observed a significant reduction in cell proliferation and migration capabilities, while apoptosis levels were increased. Concurrently, the expression of autophagy markers ATG12, P62, and LC3II/LC3I was decreased and autophagic flux was blocked, along with a decrease in ROS levels. Knockdown of SNAP23 reduced CPEB4 expression, but CPEB4 silencing had no reciprocal effect on SNAP23 levels. These findings suggest that SNAP23 acts as an upstream regulator of CPEB4, influencing the proliferation and migration of HCC, potentially through mechanisms related to cellular autophagy and oxidative stress.
