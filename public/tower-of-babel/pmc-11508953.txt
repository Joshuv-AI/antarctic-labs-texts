Effect of aspirin on platelet-rich plasma of diabetes mellitus with lower extremity atherosclerosis
Authors: Jiajia Zhang, Shenglan Shang, Wanbing Liu, Yangyang Cheng, Fan Hu, Zhengwang Cao, Ling Yue, GuangDa Xiang, Tao Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aim: Platelet-rich plasma (PRP), enriched with multiple growth factors, is a promising adjunctive therapy for diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs). As a classic anti-platelet drug for diabetic patients, the effects of aspirin on the content of growth factors in PRP remains unclear.Methods: Our study enrolled diabetic patients who were currently taking or not taking aspirin as the research subjects, with healthy volunteers as the control. PRP from these individuals was activated with glucose calcium and thrombin. Growth factors levels in PRP activated supernatant (PRP-AS) and wound healing ability of platelet gel (PG) in the full-thickness skin defect diabetic mouse model were compared.Results: We found the level of growth factors in PRP-AS derived from two groups of diabetic patients were not statistically different, whereas both lower than that from healthy volunteers. Similarly, we found better wound healing ability of PG from healthy volunteers than those from diabetic patients, but no difference between the two groups of diabetic patients in the mouse model.Discussion: Aspirin does not interfere with autologous PRP therapy when using calcium gluconate and thrombin as agonists. However considering the content of growth factors, PRP from healthy volunteers is a preferable option for promoting DFU repair.

Introduction

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is a concentrated solution of platelets (PLT) obtained from centrifuged whole blood, which contains abundant bioactive factors derived from PLT. Due to the ability to promote wound healing and tissue regeneration, PRP has been widely used in various fields such as orthopedics, oral and maxillofacial surgery and plastic surgery [1]. Recently, PRP has also been increasingly used in the treatment of refractory skin wounds [2,3]. Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are one of the common types of refractory wounds in clinical and PRP treatment is considered as a promising adjunctive therapy [4,5]. Various growth factors released by PLT in PRP has been shown to have a significant impact on promoting wound healing in DFUs [6].

PRP can be obtained either from a patient's own blood (autologous PRP) or from donated blood from volunteers (allogeneic PRP). However, in both cases, the functional core of PRP remains the PLT [7]. The quality of PRP is closely related to the ability of PLT to synthesize and release growth factors, which in turn can be influenced by factors such as age, underlying diseases and medication usage [8]. Aspirin is a classic anti-platelet drug that is commonly prescribed to diabetic patients, which is due to the high morbidity of lower-extremity arterial disease (LEAD) before the occurrence of ulcers [9,10]. While it is well-established that aspirin has the ability to inhibit PLT aggregation and slow down the progression of atherosclerosis, its effects on PLT synthesis and the release of growth factors are still not fully understood. Based on this, we have two main concerns. Firstly, we need to determine if DFU patients currently taking aspirin are still suitable candidates for autologous PRP treatment. Secondly, while autologous PRP from DFU patients has been shown to accelerate wound healing, we are interested in comparing the effectiveness of autologous PRP obtained from DFU patients versus allogeneic PRP sourced from healthy individuals, in terms of promoting wound repair.

Our study enrolled diabetic patients with LEAD who were currently taking or not taking aspirin as the research subjects, with healthy volunteers as the control. Considering the primary role of growth factors in promoting wound repair in PRP, we firstly compared the levels of growth factors that closely related to wound healing in PRP from these three group. We also evaluated the healing-promoting effects of the PRP from each group on skin wounds in diabetic mice. Our study aims to assess the impact of the diabetic mellitus with LEAD and aspirin medication on the quality of PRP, aiming to provide a theoretical basis for personalized PRP treatment plans for DFU patients.

Methods

Ethics

Our study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Central Theater Command General Hospital ([2023]013-01). All participants signed informed consent prior to being recruited for this study, and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All animal care and experimental procedures were approved by Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Central Theater Command General Hospital (20221027).

Subjects & study design

A total of 45 individuals were recruited for this study. Group A included 15 patients with Type 2 diabetes and LEAD, who had not taken anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) in the past 3 months due to poor compliance or adverse drug reactions. Group B consisted of 15 Type 2 diabetes patients with LEAD who had been consistently taking enteric-coated aspirin tablets (100 mg/day) for the past 3 months without the use of other anticoagulants or NSAIDs. The presence of atherosclerotic plaques in the lower limbs was confirmed through color Doppler ultrasound in both groups of diabetic patients, and none of the patients had foot ulcers at the time of enrollment. Group C comprised 15 healthy volunteers who donated blood at the blood transfusion department of our hospital. These volunteers had no history of metabolic diseases, blood system diseases, thrombosis, or the use of drugs that affect PLT and coagulation function in the past 3 months. The exclusion criteria were as follows: diabetic ketoacidosis, hyperglycemic hyperosmotic coma, pregnancy, severe respiratory insufficiency of the heart, liver and kidney, immune system disease, co-infection and inflammation of the body, thrombocytopenia or functional deficiency, abnormal coagulation function, moderate or severe anemia.

The baseline clinical data of subjects were collected and compared. Peripheral venous blood was then drawn to prepare PRP, which was activated to form platelet gel (PG). In vitro, the activated supernatant (PRP-AS) from the PG was subsequently extracted, and the levels of eight platelet-released growth factors associated with wound healing in the PRP-AS were examined. In vivo, PG from the aforementioned three groups of subjects was used to intervene in skin wounds of diabetic mice, and the wound healing outcomes were compared. Preparation of PRP

A volume of 40 ml of peripheral venous blood was collected using a sterile syringe containing sodium citrate as an anticoagulant. A small amount of the sample was set aside for PLT and WBC counting. The blood was then filtered using a Sterile Acrodisc™ WBC syringe filter (Pall, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions to remove leukocytes. PRP was prepared through a two-stage centrifugation process. Firstly, the samples were centrifuged at 600 rpm for 10 min to separate the plasma layer, platelet layer, and red blood cell layer. The red blood cell layer at the bottom were discarded. The top plasma layer and middle platelet layer up to 1 mm above the red blood cell layer were carefully aspirated and transferred to another centrifuge tube. Secondly, the remaining sample was centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 20 min to precipitate the PLT. The supernatant obtained is the platelet-poor plasma (PPP). Top 2/3 of the PPP was removed and the platelets were resuspended in the remaining PPP to obtain the desired PRP. The PLT and leukocyte concentrations of the PRP samples were determined. To ensure comparability between PRP samples, PPP was used to adjust the PLT concentration to 1000 × 109/l in cases where the initial PLT concentration exceeded 1000 × 109/l.

Activation of PRP

According to our previous study [11], calcium gluconate solution containing thrombin was used as an agonist to activate the PRP. The activation process involved adding 0.1 ml of a 10% calcium gluconate solution with 10U of thrombin to every 1 ml of PRP in a sterile polypropylene tube. This activation mixture, namely PG, was then incubated for 60 min at 37°C in a water bath. After incubation, the PG was centrifuged at 3000×g for 10 min to remove the gel and cellular debris. The resulting activated supernatants were named as PRP-AS, which were collected and kept at -80°C for further analysis.

Quantification of growth factors in PRP-AS

The concentrations of various growth factors in the PRP-AS samples from the three groups were measured using Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (R&D Systems, MN, USA). The growth factors measured included PLT-derived growth factor BB (PDGF-BB), VEGF, TGF-β1, bFGF, IGF-1, EGF, HGF and differentiation factor 11 (GDF11). The measurements were carried out according to the manufacturer's protocol, with at least three independent repetitions performed.

Diabetic mouse model

Wild-type C57BL/6J male mice were adaptively fed with regular diet for one week and then switched to a high-fat and high-sugar diet for four weeks. A 2% streptozotocin (STZ) solution was prepared using a 0.1 mol/l sodium citrate buffer (pH = 4.5). The above-mentioned mice were intraperitoneally injected with STZ at a dose of 30 mg/kg in a fasted state daily for 5 consecutive days, with a 12-h fasting period before each injection. The mice were continued to be fed with the high-fat and high-sugar diet. On the third, seventh and fourteenth days after the last injection, random blood glucose levels were measured from the tail vein of the mice. Mice with random blood glucose levels >16.7 mmol/l on the fourteenth day were considered to have successfully developed diabetes and were used for subsequent experiments.

Full-thickness skin defect mouse model & experimental groups

The above-mentioned diabetic model mice were anesthetized using inhalation anesthesia, followed by skin preparation and disinfection. Circular full-thickness skin defects with a diameter of 8 mm were created on the back of the mice. Gauze was used to apply pressure to the wound for hemostasis.

The mice were randomly divided into four groups using a random number table method as follows: control group (n = 3), sterile gauze to cover the defects; PG-A; PG-B; PG-C. In PG-A, PG-B, PG-C group, the defects of mice were treated by the PG obtained from individuals in corresponding group with three mice per PRP sample.

Measurement of wound closure

After surgery, the wounds were photographed at 0, 3, 7 and 14 days. The edges of each wound were traced, and the wound area was measured using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, MD, USA). The degree of wound closure was calculated using the formula:Wound closure rate (%)=[(A0-At)A0]×100

Where A0 represents the initial wound area (t = 0) and At represents the wound area at each time interval.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., NY, USA). Continuous variables were shown as mean ± SD, categorical variables were shown as number (%). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed for evaluating the significance of differences with a p value of <0.05 deemed statistically significant.

Additionally, the blood routine examination exhibited no notable variations in the level of PLT and leukocytes in blood among group A, group B and group C. Comparison of growth factors in PRP-AS

The level of growth factors in PRP-AS were investigated. ELISA results showed no statistically significant differences in the levels of various growth factors in PRP-AS obtained from patients of Group A and Group B (PRP-A and PRP-B), indicating that the intake of 100 mg/day aspirin did not alter the ability of platelets to release growth factors in diabetic patients with LEAD (Table 3). Compared with healthy blood donors (Group C), the levels of VEGF, TGF-β and GDF-11 in PRP-A were lower than those in PRP-C; the levels of PDGF-BB, VEGF, TGF-β and GDF-11 in PRP-B were lower than those in PRP-C (Table 3). These findings suggest that the disease status of diabetes and LEAD negatively affects the ability of platelets to release growth factors.

Discussion

DFUs are a common complication of diabetes, seriously affecting patients' quality of life and pose a threat to their safety, leading to high medical expenses and heavy social burden [12,13]. As a growth factor therapy, PRP therapy has emerged as a promising treatment for DFU healing. PRP contains a high concentration of platelets, approximately 5–ten-times greater than that found in peripheral blood. In a resting state, platelets have the ability to synthesize various growth factors, including PDGF, TGF-β, FGF, VEGF, IGF-1, EGF, HGF and GDF11, storing them in alpha granules or vesicle systems [14]. Before applying to the wound, PRP undergoes activation process through chemical activation involving substances such as thrombin, calcium ions and adenosine, or physical activation including differential centrifugation, repeated freezing and thawing, etc. During this activation, platelets are strongly stimulated and promptly release particles and vesicles (microvesicles and exosomes), facilitating the delivery of the growth factors to the damaged tissue to promote cell proliferation, matrix synthesis, angiogenesis and re-epithelialization, ultimately accelerating wound healing [15,16]. The diabetic mouse with full-thickness skin defect model was used to investigate the effects and mechanisms of biomaterial on promoting wound healing [17,18]. Generally, the mice treated with the vehicle without STZ were used as a control. However, the vehicle group was omitted in this study, as we focused solely on the effects of three different sources of PRP on wound healing in diabetic mice, rather than the effects of diabetes on the healing of skin lesions.

The synthesis and release of growth factors by platelets involve complex signal pathways, and any factors that affect the relevant pathways can potentially impact the quantity and quality of growth factors in PRP. Antiplatelet drugs are one of the common influencing factors faced by DFU patients [19,20]. Impaired blood circulation caused by LEAD is one of the main mechanisms underlying DFUs and a significant impediment to wound healing. The use of antiplatelet drugs to inhibit atherosclerosis is one of the treatment strategies for DFUs [21]. The Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Diabetic Foot recommend antiplatelet therapy with 75–100 mg/day of aspirin for LEAD patients [12]. Aspirin delays the progression of atherosclerosis by inhibiting platelet aggregation, but its effect on platelet synthesis and release of growth factors remains controversial. Ramsook et al. suggested that effective PRP therapy required the temporary cessation of antiplatelet drugs for patients receiving antiplatelet treatment [22]. Jayaram et al. analyzed the levels of growth factors in autologous leukocyte-rich PRP (LR-PRP) before and after continuous aspirin intake for 14 days in male healthy volunteers and found that aspirin reduced the levels of VEGF, PDGF-AB and TGF-β1 in arachidonic acid (AA)-activated LR-PRP [19]. Kariyazono et al. found that aspirin decreased the levels of TGF-β1 in PRP samples activated by collagen in healthy volunteers [23]. However, Velier et al. believed that the use of PG for wound repair was not affected by the use of antiplatelet drugs [24]. Anitua et al. found that aspirin did not affect the levels of PDGF-AB, VEGF, IGF-1, HGF and TGF-β1 in PRP activated by CaCl2 in patients receiving antiplatelet treatment [25,26]. Smith et al. reported that aspirin had no effect on the levels of PDGF-BB and TGF-β1 in PRP activated by thrombin in post-cardiac surgery patients [27]. However, differences in the health status of the study subjects, variations in leukocyte content in PRP and discrepancies in PRP activation methods make it difficult to directly compare and draw unified conclusions for these findings about the effect of antiplatelet drugs on growth factor level of PRP.

Diabetic patients with LEAD enrolled in our study represent a high-risk group for DFUs and potential candidates for autologous PRP therapy, which are frequently encountered in the hospital ward to facilitate their rapid recruitment and aligning well with the research objectives. The patients were divided into two groups based on whether they had been on aspirin therapy. We found no significant differences in demographic characteristics and baseline information between the two groups, suggesting that the severity of lower extremity circulatory disorders caused by LEAD was similar, ensuring the comparability. Furthermore, there were no differences in the level of platelet in peripheral blood between the two groups and the levels of growth factors in PRP-AS derived from two groups. Based on these findings, we concluded that aspirin therapy at a daily dose of 100 mg does not affect platelet count and the ability of platelets to release growth factors in diabetes patients with LEAD. We speculate that this may be related to the activation mechanisms of platelets. Platelets can be activated by arachidonic acid (AA), thrombin and adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and others. AA is released from platelet membrane upon stimulation and is subsequently converted into precursor prostaglandins by cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1). These are further processed to form thromboxane A2, which strongly activates platelets and induce platelet aggregation through paracrine signaling [28]. Thrombin and ADP activate platelets by interacting with PAR-2/-4 receptors and P2Y receptors on the platelet membrane, respectively. These interaction triggers a series of signal transductions pathways that are independent of COX-1 [14,29]. Nevertheless, aspirin exerts its pharmacological effects by inhibiting COX-1/COX-2 related pathways. In this study, thrombin was used as an activator. Thus, aspirin does not interfere with the activation of platelets by glucose calcium and thrombin, and the subsequent release of growth factors. This suggests that for DFU patients on aspirin therapy, it may not be necessary to discontinue medication and wait for platelet renewal before conducting autologous PRP therapy using this activation method, thus avoiding prolonging the patient's hospital stay. To validate the hypothesis, PRP activated with glucose calcium and thrombin was applied to skin wounds in diabetic mice. Our findings showed no difference in wound healing promotion between the PGs derived from two patient groups, which is consistent with the results of the growth factors, providing further support for our viewpoint.

The choice between autologous PRP and allogeneic PRP is also a contentious issue. Autologous PRP is preferred by patients as it avoids the risk of blood-borne infections and immune reactions. However, in cases where patients have severe anemia, malnutrition, coagulation disorders, decreased platelet count, or platelet dysfunction, allogeneic PRP may be considered [30,31]. Generally, the treating physician guides the patient's choice based on the aforementioned practical factors. However, if the patient is able to provide autologous PRP and is also willing and able to obtain allogeneic PRP, the choice becomes a worthy question to consider. Our perspective is that choosing based on maximizing the acquisition of growth factors is a wise approach. Therefore, our study compared autologous PRP from diabetic patients with allogeneic PRP from healthy volunteers. We found that the levels of PDGF, VEGF, TGF-beta and GDF11 in PRP-AS from the diabetes patient were significantly lower than those from healthy blood donors. Some studies suggested that the growth factors released by platelets play a positive role in promoting wound healing with PRP. Specifically, PDGF and TGF-β are important for promoting tissue cell proliferation and collagen synthesis, while VEGF is a key factor in stimulating angiogenesis at the wound site [32]. Our previous study found that GDF11 enhance neovascularization in the ischemic areas of the lower limbs in diabetic mice by improving the function of endothelial progenitor cells, and this pro-angiogenic effect may also benefit the healing of DFUs [33]. Another study had reported that GDF11 promotes wound healing in diabetic mice via stimulating endothelial progenitor cell mobilization and neovascularization [34]. Therefore, we consider the above growth factors are conducive to DFU healing, and use them as observation and analysis indicators. Studies have shown that diabetes affects mean platelet volume and width [35]. Hyperglycemia lead to platelet adhesion, aggregation and release dysfunction, resulting in abnormal activation [36]. Abnormal expression of surface glycoproteins on platelets in diabetic patients, decreased membrane lipid fluidity, increased sensitivity to stimulants, promote atherosclerosis and thrombus formation [37]. Although existing studies indicated that platelet activation was increased in diabetic patients, our in vitro findings suggested that platelets from diabetic patients release less growth factors than those from healthy volunteers. Similarly, we found that PG from healthy volunteers had better wound healing ability in diabetes mice in vivo. We speculate that the platelets in diabetic patients, after being abnormally activated for a long time, may enter a secondary “exhaustion” state, resulting in a decrease in their ability to synthesize certain growth factors. Therefore, platelets from diabetic patients are at a disadvantage compared with those from healthy individuals. Certainly, these ideas still need further research to confirm.

Due to the aim of evaluating the effect of aspirin on platelet-released growth factors, leukocytes were filtrated to reduce interference from growth factors released by leukocytes. Leukocyte depletion was also conducted on blood samples from healthy volunteers, as using leukocyte-poor allogeneic blood products is beneficial in reducing the occurrence of immune adverse reactions. Therefore, using leukocyte-poor PRP in our study would provide more practical results. Despite the promising results, there is room to optimize our research overall. First, more samples were required in the future study with only 15 subjects in each group. Second, the clinical characteristics of diabetic patients with concomitant LEAD included in this single-center study may not accurately represent the overall characteristics of this patient population. Third, the age range covered by the subjects in our study was limited. Since age is also a factor influencing platelet function, further stratified research is needed with subjects spanning a wider age range. Fourth, the heterologous immune reaction of human-derived PRP in mice is important to consider. Since we used leukocyte-poor PRP, most mice did not experience significant adverse reactions; however, a few exhibited rubbing behaviors, highlighting the need for vigilance and further investigation into immune reactions caused by allogeneic PRP. Last, further controlled clinical intervention studies are needed to provide stronger evidence as our in vivo study only conducting in a mouse model.

Conclusion

Our study found that aspirin did not alter the ability of platelets to release growth factors in diabetic patients with LEAD when using calcium gluconate and thrombin as agonists. This suggests that aspirin does not interfere with the use of autologous PRP therapy as long as appropriate agonists are used. Furthermore, although autologous PRP is commonly used, we observed allogeneic PRP from healthy donors contains higher levels of certain platelet-derived growth factors and promotes faster wound healing. Importantly, compared with intravenous infusion of allogeneic blood products, the topical application of allogeneic PRP on DFU wounds significantly reduces immune-related adverse reactions, especially when using leukocyte-poor PRP [38]. Based on this, we recommend considering PRP from healthy volunteers as a preferable option for promoting DFU repair.
