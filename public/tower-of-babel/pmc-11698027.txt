Single-Molecule Multivalent Interactions Revealed by Plasmon-Enhanced Fluorescence
Authors: Kasper
R Okholm, Sjoerd W Nooteboom, Johan Nygaard Vinther, Vincenzo Lamberti, Swayandipta Dey, Ebbe Sloth Andersen, Peter Zijlstra, Duncan S Sutherland
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultivalency as an interaction principle is widely utilized in nature. It enables specific and strong binding by multiple weak interactions through enhanced avidity and is a core process in immune recognition and cellular signaling, which is also a current concept in drug design. Here, we use the high signals from plasmon-enhanced fluorescence of nanoparticles to extract binding kinetics and dynamics of multivalent interactions on the single-molecule level and in real time. We study mono-, bi-, and trivalent binding interactions using a DNA Holliday Junction as a model construct with programmable valency and introduce a step-binding model for binding kinetics relevant for structured macromolecules by including an experimentally extractable binding restriction term ω to quantify the effects from conformation, steric effects, and rigidity. We used this approach to explore how length and flexibility of the DNA ligands affect binding restriction and binding strength, where the overall binding strength decreased with spacer length. For trivalent systems, increasing spacer length additionally activated binding in the trivalent state, giving insight into the design of multivalent drug or targeting moieties. By systematically changing the receptor density, we explored the binding super selectivity of the multivalent HJ at the single-molecule level. We find a polynomial behavior of the trivalent binding strength that clearly shows receptor-density-dependent selective binding. Interestingly, we could exploit the rapidly decaying near fields of the plasmon that induce a strong dependence of the signal on the position of the dye to observe binding dynamics during single multivalent binding events.Keywords: multivalency, single-molecule fluorescence, plasmonic nanoparticles, fluorescence enhancement, DNA nanotechnology

Introduction

In nature, the principle of multivalency enables specific and strong, but still reversible, binding between receptors and ligands.1−3 Multivalency involves binding of multiple reversible chemical interactions between two units, where each interaction is relatively weak. However, their collective binding dramatically enhances binding strengths due to enhanced avidity, also known as functional affinity.4 The interaction of viruses with host cells is an important example that is mediated through multiple weak bonds, leading to uptake by the host cells via strong multivalent binding.1 These multivalent interactions can occur between a single receptor–ligand pair where multiple binding motifs are involved,5 between a multivalent object and a surface (e.g., virus–host cell interaction6 or immune macromolecular recognition of a pathogen surface7) or between two cell surfaces leading to adhesion or signaling.8 Another important property of multivalent interactions is the selectivity of certain receptor densities that can lead to super selective binding, where strong binding only takes place above a threshold receptor density.9,10 Multivalency has been the subject of strong research interest for more than 25 years since the seminal paper by Whitesides et al.1

The perspectives of studying multivalency are manifold. One is to fundamentally understand multivalent interactions in order to give insight into different natural molecular and cellular processes. Another perspective is to use the underlying principles of multivalent binding in drug design11−13 and in supramolecular chemistry,2,14 as well as for molecular imaging agents15 and functional materials.16,17 Various models on the nature of multivalent interactions have been proposed and used to explain different multivalent systems in terms of their thermodynamic and kinetic properties.1,18−21 Though these models can successfully explain fundamental concepts, they do not sufficiently address multivalent binding in terms of molecular structure, flexibility, and steric effects. A better understanding of these factors is necessary to unlock the potential of multivalency in drug design and to understand the mechanism of drug action during blocking of multivalent drug targets.22

Multivalent binding is often measured and evaluated based on ensemble-averaged techniques and assays, with surface plasmon resonance, ELISA, ICT50, and bilayer interferometry as common examples.2 These techniques allow for easy quantification of binding constants and avidity enhancement. However, multivalent interactions are inherently heterogeneous and require a single-molecule approach to understand the underlying dynamic behavior during binding that drives the avidity and can lead to surface mobility and walking.23 A range of single-molecule approaches have been used on multivalent systems, e.g., single-molecule fluorescence,24 force spectroscopy using AFM,25,26 and nanopores.27 While these techniques have been used to study the binding kinetics of multivalent systems, none of them have been able to capture the dynamics of a multivalent interaction during a binding event or gain insights into multivalent selectivity on the single-molecule level.

Here, we present a method that enables the extraction of binding kinetics while also providing direct observations of binding dynamics during multivalent interactions at the single-molecule level. We demonstrate the approach to study mono-, bi-, and trivalent binding and the principles for macromolecular design. Our approach, termed plasmon-enhanced fluorescence of multivalent systems at the single-molecule level (PEFOMS), makes use of plasmon-enhanced fluorescence from gold nanoparticles28 that provides superior signals of binding events.29 Importantly, the rapidly decaying optical fields near the particles allow us to directly observe the binding dynamics during individual multivalent ligand–receptor binding events. We use a DNA-based interaction framework of DNA-coated nanoparticles and 4-way junctions as a multivalent ligand–receptor system with programmable valency, binding strength and receptor density. Here, we apply the single-molecule approach to study avidity and observe complex dynamics during single binding events for multivalent systems and compare this to an extended step-binding model taking into account macromolecular conformation. Furthermore, we systematically vary the receptor densities to probe the difference in binding selectivity of bivalent and trivalent interactions. The approach has potential application to study a range of biomolecules including proteins, by changing the surface functionalization of the nanorods from DNA to, for example, proteins30 or carbohydrates,31 and provide insight into biologically relevant multivalent interactions and inform the design of synthetic multivalent macromolecules.

Results and Discussion

Plasmon-Enhanced Fluorescence from Single Molecules Binding to Gold Nanorods

We used PEFOMS to measure the bound-state lifetimes of fluorescently labeled molecules. The principle is illustrated in Figure 1 and adopts the framework used in DNA-based point accumulation for imaging in nanoscale topography (DNA-PAINT)32,33 using plasmon enhancement to get brighter signals and distance-dependent readout. In our approach, we use DNA-coated gold nanorods immobilized on a coverslip and imaged in a total internal reflection (TIRF)-based microscope34,35 as shown in Figure 1a. Under illumination, gold nanorods support localized surface plasmon resonances (LSPR) from the collective oscillations of electrons in the particle.36 At the plasmon resonance, the electric field is strongly enhanced in the vicinity of the nanorod, leading to distance-dependent excitation enhancement. In addition, the coupling between the dye and the particle results in modification of the radiative and nonradiative decay rates, leading to a distance-dependent emission enhancement.29,37−39 The plasmon-enhanced fluorescence on DNA-coated gold nanorods can be exploited to track binding events of dye-labeled DNA probes and quantify binding kinetics.35 The nanorods are functionalized with 30 nt long-thiolated DNA strands here termed receptor strands. This ensures that the dye is spaced from the particles enough to avoid the quenching of the fluorescence signal. To represent a ligand–receptor interaction, this strand was further designed to contain a specific sequence in its end-segment which serves as a transient sequence- and length-dependent binding site for a complementary DNA sequence, termed ligand DNA. The receptor DNA was attached using a freeze–thawing approach to maximize the density of DNA on the gold particle surface.40 The average receptor density was 1.6 corresponding to a distance of 3.5 nm between receptor DNA sites assuming closed packing on the surface (see S4).

The gold particles appear as bright diffraction-limited spots in the microscope due to one-photon luminescence (PL) as shown in Figure 1b,c. The PL signal varies slightly from particles to particles due to differences in plasmon wavelength with respect to the laser excitation wavelength, among other effects, which results from inevitable polydispersity in wet-chemical synthesis protocols.41 The PL signal creates a stable baseline and ensures that each particle can be easily identified from the dark background. Upon binding of a ligand DNA, carrying a dye that spectrally overlaps with the plasmon, to the gold nanorod, its fluorescence output gets enhanced, and the diffraction spot brightness is thus greatly increased (Figure 1c). We can thereby follow the signal from each particle over time and generate a time trace from each particle, as shown in Figure 1d. The large intensity bursts come from single ligands binding to the particle. Through tuning of the molecule concentration, we ensure that the intensity bursts represent single-ligand binding events (see S7). The intensity of the bursts at an individual particle varies which is expected as the molecules can bind to the particle at different locations which are subject to different field enhancement and hence different fluorescence enhancement. Figure 1e shows a BEM simulation of a 40 nm by 82 nm nanorod and the fluorescent enhancement factor around the nanorod that clearly highlights how the plasmon-enhanced signal strongly depends on the position of the dye relative to the particle.37,38 The highest enhancement is reached around the ends of the nanorod, where it can exceed fluorescence enhancement factors of 70. The cross-sectional plot of the enhancement factors along the black line, in the inset of Figure 1e, shows that the enhancement factor changes greatly depending on the position along the line and the maximum value is reached around a 3.5 nm distance from the particle. In our case, the dye is likely to be no closer than 5 nm due to the receptor DNA layer avoiding quenching. The enhancement at locations at the sides of the nanoparticles can be below 1 (so deenhancement) due to the lensing effect of the nanorod focusing the optical field at the ends of the rods.

The blue time trace in Figure 1d represents a region of interest (ROI) in the background, i.e., not on a particle, where a fluorescent molecule (DNA ligand with dye) binds nonspecifically to the surface, and no plasmon enhancement of the fluorescence occurs which clearly highlights the advantage of using plasmon-enhanced fluorescence to generate signals with superior brightness, thereby enabling a high signal-to-noise ratio even for short binding events.42 To experimentally show the distance-dependent signal, we designed 4 different DNA ligands: two single-stranded ligands with the dye placed on either 3′ or 5′ end of the ligand and two 4-way Holliday junction (HJ) with the dye placed close to or far from the ligand shown in Figure 1f. We measure the enhancement factor by comparing the 5 most intense events from each particle, assumed to be from ligands binding at the tip of the nanorods, with the average intensity for a dye when it does not experience any plasmon enhancement. Figure 1f shows the enhancement factor per particle for each ligand in red and the associated mean value. As expected, we observe the highest enhancement factor for the single ligand, with the dye closest to the particle. The two Holliday junctions give rise to less enhancement compared with the single-stranded ligands. We hypothesize that this is due to repulsion between the receptor strands and the Holliday junction, causing the bound receptor strand to be stretched out further from the nanorod, which leads to a longer distance to the nanorod compared to the single imagers. Placing the dye opposite the ligand further decreases the enhancement signal. For all four ligands, there is a large spread of enhancement values observed at different nanoparticles. This is due to polydispersity of the nanorod size and aspect ratio, resulting in differences in the spectral overlap between the plasmon resonance and the laser wavelength leading to large differences in enhancement factors,41 so this type of variation of fluorescence signal will not be seen in single-event traces.

Figure 1g shows a close-up of the time trace. Following the methodology in qPAINT,33 we can define the characteristic bound-state lifetime and off-times in the measurements. Binding events are identified when the intensity signal jumps above and below a threshold value, corresponding to events occurring at the ends of the nanorods. The off-time (toff), also called the dark-time, is the time between binding events. The bound-state lifetime (ton), also called on-time, is the time duration of a binding event. We focus on the bound-state lifetimes to measure the strength of the binding interactions.

Multivalent Kinetics from Holliday Junctions

To study multivalency, we engineered the number of ligand motifs in the interaction. We use DNA–DNA interactions as a model system for multivalent interactions, here addressing the case of a macromolecule binding to a surface that displays many receptors, e.g., MBL or C1q recognition of pathogen surfaces in the innate immune response.7 While previous work was focused on the kinetics of multivalent host–guest interactions of flexible macromolecules,25 we chose a structured scaffold to resemble the rigid structure of most biomacromolecules. Our scaffold is a 4-way junction known as a Holliday Junction43 (HJ), specifically HJ744,45 (Figure 2a). Nucleic acid scaffolds have gained increased interest for multivalent ligand display46 because of their programmability, low cost, and enhanced stability when introducing modified nucleic acids.47 The HJ, composed of four 22 nt long strands, which are referred to as R, B, H, and X arms45 (schematically shown in Figure 2a), enables tuning and redesigning of the ligand sequences for appropriate monovalent bound-state lifetimes (or equivalently the off-rate, koff). Each of these strands can be modified to carry either a ligand sequence or a fluorescent dye, which we exploited to construct mono-, bi-, and trivalent HJs (HJ1, HJ2, and HJ3, respectively). Figure 2b shows the mean structure of the HJ with 3 ligands from an oxDNA simulation.48 We used a ligand sequence of 8 nt, based on a commonly used sequence for DNA-PAINT,32,35 to modify the 3′ end of strand(s) H, X, and H or X, H, and B arms for HJ1, HJ2, and HJ3, respectively. For the fluorescent label, we used ATTO655 as fluorescent dye on the 5′ end of HJ strand R. Using these 3 constructs, we aimed to measure and compare the bound-state lifetimes of the individual constructs. Figure 2c shows exemplary time traces from mono-, bi-, and trivalent HJ binding to the receptor strand on the nanorods, clearly demonstrating that ton increases with the number of ligands. For better comparison of bound-state lifetimes for different HJs, we collected all events from the single particles in each field of view (FOV) and plotted the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of all lifetimes. Figure 2d, showing the CDFs of a mono-, bi-, and trivalent HJ with an 8 nt ligand, confirms the trend observed in the single-time traces (Figure 2c). The avidity effect from the multivalent binding is clear when considering the CDFs of the different constructs and will be subject to discussion later. To quantitatively compare the binding, we fitted the CDF for each construct with a stretched exponential function to extract the characteristic mean bound-state lifetime < τon > using49where . The off-rate koff is directly linked to τon:32. We can then compare the different constructs quantitatively via the extracted τon values from the exponential fits.

DNA can form base-stacking interactions that greatly stabilize DNA–DNA binding.50,51 Here, we wanted to study the effect of multivalency on the ligand–receptor interactions without any interference from other binding interactions. Therefore, we designed single-ligand HJs with inserted T-nucleotide spacers (T-spacers) to separate the ligand sequence from the double-stranded junction arm shown in Figure 2e. In Figure 2f, we have compared the measured bound-state lifetimes of each of the HJs and the single-stranded single ligand. We see that without any T-spacers, the binding interaction is much stronger than the single-stranded ligand because of base-stacking stabilization. Previously, it has been found that a two-nucleotide separation is enough to avoid base stacking, but the effect of a single-nucleotide separation is unclear.50,51 Here, we find that a single T-spacer decreases the effect of base stacking but is insufficient to avoid base stacking completely. We see that T2 is enough to avoid base-stacking effects as the bound-state lifetime is similar to the single-stranded ligand, and we find that adding a T4-spacer does not affect binding compared to the T2-spacer ligand. We therefore chose to design the ligand sequences with T2-spacers, which is also the case for Figure 2c,d. In the following sections, we will look closer at the bivalent and trivalent HJs to get insights into the binding behavior.

Studying Bivalent Interactions

To quantitatively explore the multivalent interactions, we turned to mechanistic models. First, we considered the interaction of bivalent HJ (HJ2). We start from the work of Huskens et al.25 which developed a framework for quantitatively describing avidity effects of unstructured polymers, and we include a binding restriction term ω with values from 0 to 1 to cover the effects of conformation, steric effects, and rigidity in limiting the additional binding arising from multivalency. The concept of restriction was first introduced by Netz and Liese21,52 to describe the effect of ligand orientation when placed on the ends of polymeric chains. In our work, the concept of the restriction factor is more general. Ultimately, it accounts for all effects that restrict the binding capabilities for any binding interaction following an initial binding event in a multivalent setting. Thus, we describe the bivalent interaction of a single HJ as a stepwise binding mechanism as depicted in Figure 3a. The first step is an intermolecular binding between one arm of the HJ and a receptor strand on the nanorod. Here, the on-rate is determined by the on-rate of the single monovalent ligand (kon,M). After the first binding step, the HJ can follow two routes. First, it can dissociate from the particle with an off-rate determined by the off-rate of the single ligand (koff,M). The second route is an intramolecular binding step with the second ligand where the on-rate depends on the effective concentration (Ceff),20,53 the monovalent on-rate, and the binding restriction (ω). The effective concentration is an important property in multivalent binding and can be thought of as the probability for the second ligand to find receptor DNA. It is dependent on the length, L, of the bivalent object, i.e., the distance between the two binding entities of a bivalent object and the surface density of receptor DNA. At macromolecular length scales, the effective concentration is in the mM range.

The probing volume, V(L), is the available volume in which the ligand can move around. In previous work,25 the probing volume was estimated by considering the ability of the second ligand to move freely in a volume confined by the length of the polymer connecting the two ligands (Figure 3b). It was presented as a tethered polymer but was considered as a freely diffusing object, equally probable to reside at any place in the volume at any time. This model correlated well with data for a flexible bivalent macromolecule,25 which is relevant for intrinsically disordered proteins or unstructured polymer constructs, but likely cannot represent the structure present within most macromolecular proteins. Here, we elaborated on this model to include structural considerations of the multivalent macromolecule, which is relevant for the more rigid structures of most macromolecular proteins, represented by the HJ. The HJ arms can stack on each other, thereby giving rise to the two distinct isoforms I and II depicted in Figure S1. HJ7 used here has been reported to coexist with almost equal isoform ratios at similar MgCl2 concentrations as used here.45 In our bivalent experiments, the two ligand sequences are on the 3′ ends of H and X. From oxDNA simulations, we can measure the distance between the ligands on H and X shown in Figure S6.

In a first attempt, we set ω = 1 to recover the previous work of Huskens et al.; we used a probing volume within which the second ligand can move around freely. We termed this the unstructured model. The span of the volume is determined by the length of the HJ and the ligand sequence. For bivalent HJs, the bound-state lifetimes predicted by this model were significantly higher than those extracted experimentally (Figure 3d). The increase in the experimental bound-state lifetime from the monovalent to the bivalent HJ is around an ∼20 times increase, while the unstructured model predicts an ∼800 times increase. This inconsistency between the model and experimental values shows as expected that the molecular conformation limits the motion of the ligands reducing the avidity effect. We attempted to represent changes in Ceff resulting from limited mobility of the molecule in what we term the structured model (Figure 3c); however, this does not substantially improve the model’s ability to explain the experimental data.

Restricted Binding Decreases Avidity

We believe that the structure of the molecule and interactions with the surface mean that after binding of a first-ligand arm, the second ligand is not equally distributed in the probing volume but rather located in some parts of the probing space more often than in others, and this can be represented by ω having values less than 1, which for the bivalent Holliday junction gave a value of 0.026. Figure 4a shows a conceptual sketch of the positional probability of the second ligand for the bivalent HJ and how the restriction factor affects binding. In this scenario, the second ligand is less likely to be positioned close to the surface, which would be needed for binding, thus restricting its binding probability. We hypothesize that this positioning could be caused by repulsion between the receptor DNA surface and the HJ, while the steric hindrance within the HJ and its rigid structure further restrict the availability of the ligand.

The restriction factor makes the space close to the surface less likely, thus restricting the binding of the second ligand. We now apply this formalization to describe the binding for the trivalent binding HJ in a stepwise manner to now include an additional step with the third ligand binding and corresponding restriction factors shown in Figure 4b. The first two steps are essentially the same as in the bivalent case, except that the statistical prefactors now take into account three available ligands initially. We here assume that when the second ligand binds, the two ligands on X and H are spaced by the maximum distance between these arms. Ceff and the restriction factor for the second step established in the bivalent-step model can thus be used for those parameters in the trivalent-step model as Ceff,2. A clear indication that the distant binding (X-H) is preferred over the close binding (X-B) in the second binding step is found by comparing two bivalent HJ constructs with ligands on the XH and XB arms. The XH construct that we used for the bivalent measurements will have ligands distant in both isoforms, whereas for the XB construct, the ligands are close in isoform I and far apart in isoform II. The two constructs show almost identical CDFs of the extracted bound-state lifetimes (see Figure S12) which suggests that the XB preferably binds when in isoform II, i.e., when the ligands are distant. When bound with two arms, the HJ is brought in close contact with the surface. The third arm now experiences Ceff and binding restriction different from those in the second step. To distinguish between the restriction factors and Ceff in the two steps, the second step (bivalent binding) carries subscript 2 (mono- to bivalent) and third step 3 (bi- to trivalent). The derivation of the bound-state time for the trivalent case is described in S6.

The probing volume for the third step is estimated using the maximum distance between X and B ligands and the H and B ligands obtained from oxDNA simulations. The experimentally measured bound-state lifetimes for the trivalent HJs are shown in the table in Figure 4c. The restriction factors for the two multivalent steps are also shown as well. The increase in lifetimes from the bivalent to the trivalent constructs is a factor of ∼6 which is much less than what was observed from monovalent to bivalent binding (∼20). This relatively small bound-state lifetime increase is concurrent with a much more restricted binding for the third binding ligand (a smaller restriction factor value). When already bound through two interactions, the HJ is brought close to the surface, and we hypothesize that it is therefore subject to steric effects on the third ligand arm that restrict its binding capabilities. Furthermore, we expect there to be an increased surface repulsion between the HJ and DNA surface.

Increasing Ligand Length and Flexibility in the HJ

We wanted to apply our model to investigate how the length and flexibility of the binding arms affect the binding kinetics through restriction. Similar to what we did in Figure 2e, we introduced T-spacers into the sequence in all ligands to vary the distance between ligands which is graphically illustrated in Figure 5. For each addition of T-spacers, we can use the maximum distance between the ligands from oxDNA simulations to estimate the effective concentration, see S5 and S6. Figure 5b shows histograms of the distances from the double-stranded HJ arm to the center of the ligand, i.e., the ligand length with the addition of T-spacers. It clearly shows how the ligand length is extended with addition of spacers, which will decrease Ceff. The bound-state lifetimes for each construct and valency are shown in Figure 5c (left y-axis).

Spacer sequences consist of multiple T nucleotides ranging from 2 to 12. (b) Histogram of the ligand extensions with added T-spacers obtained from oxDNA simulations. (c) Plot of the bound-state lifetimes of the bivalent (circle) and trivalent (square) HJ on the left axis with added T-spacers, and the ratio of bound-state lifetimes between trivalent and bivalent HJs in orange on the right axis. (d) Plot of the restriction effect, 1/ω, for the bivalent (red) and trivalent (black) HJs with added T-spacers.

Addition of T-spacers reveals a steady decrease in the bound-state lifetimes of the bivalent HJ as the spacers become longer. This is explained by the expected decrease in effective concentration when increasing the spacer length.20 In Figure S10 are plotted the Ceff changes with the addition of T-spacer according to the structured and unstructured models. The graphs shows that addition of T-spacers reduces Ceff due to an increased probing volume as expected.20 Interestingly, the bound-state lifetimes of the trivalent HJs only decrease slightly. We observe that the ratio of the bound-state lifetimes for the trivalent/bivalent HJs (see Figure 5c in orange, right axis) increases with spacer length and is close to doubled after the addition of 12 T-spacers. We interpret this to mean that the third binding arm on the trivalent HJs is initially very restricted, and the additional T-spacers can activate binding. This is reflected in a reduction in the binding restriction, as shown in Figure 5d. Here, we show the binding restriction for the monovalent to bivalent (orange) and bivalent to trivalent (black) binding steps. We have plotted the restriction as 1/ω to be more intuitive such that a higher value refers to more binding restriction. Without any T-spacers, trivalent binding is very restricted compared to bivalent. The restriction on the third ligand drops with the addition of T-spacers, whereas it stays constant when adding T-spacers on the ligands of the bivalent HJ. With 12 T-spacers, the restriction of the bivalent and trivalent HJ approaches the same value. This indicates that the restriction from the HJ structure itself is apparently almost completely relieved for the third ligand when adding 12 spacers, likely because the HJ is thus extended far away from the binding interface. By adding the T-spacers, we add flexibility that can release the steric effects present in trivalent binding. Here, we show that although addition of spacers in general leads to a decrease of bound-state lifetimes, the addition of spacers for trivalent HJs is more important as compared to bivalent as it can allow activation of the third ligand. Addition of spacers for the bivalent HJs merely leads to a decrease of the effective concentration and lower bound-state lifetimes without a change of the restriction effect, suggesting that the intrinsic shape/conformation of the molecule-limiting accessibility and/or surface repulsion is the main cause of this base of restriction, while trivalent binding has an additional strong steric effect which can be relieved by added flexibility in the ligand. We find this result interesting for understanding and designing multivalent ligands as previous work on the effects of linker lengths has, to our knowledge, mainly focused on the decrease in effective concentration and the role of rigidity and the resulting binding strengths.56,57

Single Molecule Insights into Selectivity

One particularly interesting property of multivalent binding is the selectivity toward a receptor surface, which leads to super selectivity where effective binding is only achieved above a threshold receptor density.9,10,58 Super selectivity is defined from an ensemble surface coverage measurement, i.e., a from SPR or BLI type measurement. Here, we wanted to study multivalent selectivity on the single-molecule level using the PEFOMS system.

Using DNA as a model framework allowed us to systematically change the density of receptor strands by simply mixing the receptor strands with 30 nt long T (termed dilution strand) sequences at different ratios when constructing the receptor– nanorod interface graphically shown in Figure 6a. Hereby, we can gain insights into the selectivity of multivalent interactions at the single-molecule level. Figure 6b shows the quantified bound-state lifetimes for trivalent (yellow) and bivalent (orange) HJs at different receptor strand concentrations. At 10% receptor strand concentration, the bivalent and trivalent HJs have similar binding strength. However, it is clear that the binding strength of the trivalent HJ rapidly increases with increasing receptor strand concentration compared to the bivalent HJ. According to eqs 2, 3, the binding strength should scale with the receptor density as Γs and for the bivalent and trivalent HJs, respectively. The dotted lines in Figure 6b are linear and second-order polynomial overlay plots, as a guide to the eye, which nicely show how the trivalent HJ increases as a polynomial and the bivalent HJ as a linear function. The polynomial behavior of the receptor concentration-dependent binding strength shows how, on the single-molecule level, multivalent ligands increase their selectivity and ultimately reach super selective binding.

Multivalent Binding from a DNA Tetrahedron

In addition to the HJ structure, we have assembled tetrahedron DNA structures (TETs) (Figure S13) that can carry up to three ligands59,60 but with a more rigid structure than the HJs. The sides of the tetrahedron are spanned by 17 bp, and each corner, from where the ligand extends, is separated by ∼6 nm, slightly shorter than the HJ. Figure S13 shows exemplary time traces from mono-, bi-, and trivalent TET structures that show the increased binding strength when adding ligands in a similar way as for the HJ system albeit with higher nonspecific binding than for the HJs.

Direct Observations of Binding Dynamics during Multivalent Interactions

So far, we have used the plasmon-enhanced fluorescence signal only to detect binding events and quantify individual bound-state lifetimes. A powerful benefit, specific to a plasmon-enhanced approach, is the strong and rapidly decaying enhancement near the particle, which induces a strong dependence of the fluorescence intensity on the exact location of the dye with respect to the particle. We exploit the strong distance-dependent signal of the plasmon-enhanced fluorescence to give information about binding dynamics during the bound state that may be encoded in the greatly fluctuating signal of the individual traces. This opens the potential to study the transitions between monovalent, bivalent, and trivalent states to gain insights into the dynamic behavior of multivalent interactions.

During binding of the bivalent and trivalent HJs, we observe signal fluctuations that are different from those of a monovalent binding signal (where no transitions during the bound state are expected). In Figure 7a are shown exemplary time traces from a 12 nt monovalent ligand and in Figure 7b the trivalent HJ. The signal from the monovalent ligand is most often a box-like signal which, when binding occurs, reaches one level that it fluctuates around. The difference in the fluorescence signal between individual binding events is expected, as the monovalent ligand can bind at different locations on the metal particle where different field strengths are expected, giving differences in fluorescence enhancement. In contrast, the signal during a single binding event of the trivalent HJ most often does not remain at a single fluorescence intensity level but shows many jumps between fluorescence levels during the binding event before finally unbinding. The fluctuations from the 12 nt ligand (Figure 7a) are most likely due to dye blinking, resulting in a signal that digitally fluctuates between zero and nonzero values, which manifests as a fractional or full reduction in fluorescence output for an individual frame dependent on the length of the blinking event. These fluctuations are expected to be present at a similar level in the trivalent HJ signal (Figure 7b). However, the monovalent and trivalent traces are very different, which can be seen from the histograms of the intensity levels in Figure 7a,b. The trivalent HJ events are spread out over a larger range of fluorescence intensity levels compared to the 12 nt monovalent ligand, which is evident by the histogram and the corresponding relative standard deviations (RSDs).

On the right plots, a histogram of the signal intensities for each frame along with the relative standard deviation (RSD) of the signal values. (b) Time traces of a trivalent HJ with 8 nt ligands that show different signal fluctuations compared to the 12 nt free ligand. We propose that the fluctuations observed in the trivalent binding signal are due to the changing position of the dye during dynamic stepwise binding. When the trivalent HJ is bound, each arm of the HJ can bind and unbind multiple times during the overall binding event, which should change the position of the dye in each step. The time scale of the transitions between mono-, bi-, and trivalent binding during a single binding event can be estimated from the step-binding model. The estimated values for the ligands on the trivalent HJ are listed in S8. The transition rates for the unbinding steps are 2 or 3 times faster than the monovalent rate due to the additional statistical factors. Because of the much faster transition from monovalent to bivalent binding, we expect the HJ to be bound in the trivalent state most of the time within a binding event and transition between the trivalent and bivalent states. Using a 10 ms framerate, which is the fastest possible in the setup that we have used for all time traces shown thus far, we expect on average a binding step event in each frame. Due to the distance-dependent signal enhancement, the signal will change for every transition, and so we would expect drastically different signal fluctuations for the trivalent HJ compared to the 12 nt imager. It has been proposed that multivalent objects can walk and even hop around on receptor surfaces61 which would also give rise to signal fluctuations, likely on a slower time scale as the HJ gradually explores areas of varying enhancement. Here, we have shown a proof of principle of the measurement of dynamics during binding events of multivalent ligands. The time resolution of fluorescence measurements can in principle be improved in particular by exploiting the relatively high plasmon-enhanced signals to reach time scales relevant for studying individual transitions in bound states during multivalent binding events. As proof of principle, we show 1000 fps time traces of a trivalent HJ shown in Figure S16, but the microsecond time scale is possible42

Conclusion

In this work, we have used plasmon-enhanced fluorescence to study multivalent interactions at the single-molecule level. Using a model system formed from a DNA scaffold (Holliday Junction), we have applied the plasmon-enhanced approach to study monovalent, bivalent, and trivalent interactions and quantitatively extract bound-state lifetimes. We have developed a model framework and introduced the general concept of binding restriction to take into account how the structure and molecular conformation of multivalent ligands affect the effective concentration and the binding. For our system, we observe that the bivalent to trivalent transition is subject to a great amount of binding restriction. By comparing the effect of increased linker length and flexibility on bivalent and trivalent constructs, we confirmed the general effect that the addition of spacers leads to reduced bound-state lifetimes through a resultant decrease in the effective concentration of the ligand. Interestingly, we found that trivalent binding was activated and much less restricted by adding length and flexibility to the binding arms. We systematically changed the receptor density on the nanorods to gain insights into the binding selectivity of the multivalent HJs. We found a polynomial behavior of the binding strength for the trivalent HJ highlighting the selectivity toward the receptor density, eventually reaching superselectivity. Furthermore, we demonstrated the measurement of binding dynamics within single binding events of multivalent systems by utilizing the distance-dependent plasmon-enhanced fluorescence to probe changes in the dye position that arise from the binding dynamics. This approach has the potential for broad application to provide quantitative insight into multivalent interactions and to guide the design of multivalent synthetic macromolecules for use in therapeutic or bioimaging applications.

Materials and Methods

Materials

DNA strands labeled with ATTO655 and ATTO643 were purchased from Eurofins Genomics. All other DNA strands were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). TAE was purchased from Gibco, and both MgCl2 and NaCl were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. BSA-biotin and Neutravidin were purchased from Thermo Scientific. Gold nanorods (A12-40-650 citrate) were purchased from Nanopartz. Accugel 40% was purchased from National Diagnostics, and we used the ultralow range ladder from Invitrogen. The gold etchant was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Optical Setup

We used an Oxford nanoimager (ONI) microscope operating in TIRF mode equipped with a 100×/1.4 NA oil immersion objective. The microscope was gradually heated when imaging was started at room temperature. Therefore, we performed all imaging at 27 °C, at which the temperature was stable throughout the imaging sessions. For all experiments, except the 1000 fps acquisition, we used a 640 nm laser as an excitation source at 20 mW power . Each image was recorded on a 427 × 520-pixel region with 117 nm pixel size.

For the 1000 fps data, we used an objective-type total internal reflection microscope on a Nikon Ti2 microscope with an oil-immersion objective (Nikon Apo TIRF 60× Oil DIC N2, 1.49 NA). A 637 nm excitation laser (OBIS FP 637LX, Coherent) was used to excite the sample in picosecond polarization via a dichroic mirror (ZT640rdc, Chroma). The maximum power density on the sample was approximately to avoid the influence of photothermal heating on the dynamics. Fluorescence was collected through the same objective, and the leftover laser reflection was removed by a notch filter (ZET635NF, Chroma) and a long-pass filter (FELH0650, Thorlabs). The signal was detected on a CMOS camera (Teledyne Photometrics Prime BSI Express), in 11-bit mode, and cropped to 128 × 128 pixels to allow for 1000 frames per second acquisition. Note that for the 1000 fps acquisition, we used ATTO643-labeled HJs.

Data Analysis

To generate time traces, we used in-house software as described previously.35,41 In brief, the fluorescence time traces were generated by summing the intensity in a 7 × 7 pixel region from identified particles in each frame. Time-trace analysis was carried out using homemade MATLAB scripts. We employed a threshold value, and frames above the threshold were identified as events and the duration as the number of consecutive frames within the event multiplied by the camera acquisition time. As each event is counted in the integer number of frames, we correct the duration of the last frame to only last half the acquisition time to avoid overestimating the duration of fast events. The CDF of bound-state lifetimes was fitted, where is the mean bound-state lifetime. For the fast events (7 nt and 8 nt imagers and monovalent HJs) where lifetimes were close to the camera frame duration, we used A = 1 and excluded the first data point in the CDF (which is poorly defined and makes up many of the total events) from the fit. For each construct, we measured in three different FOV and therefore got three CDFs and extracted lifetime values. The reported bound-state lifetime values are the average of the three lifetime values, and the error is the standard deviation of the three lifetime values for each construct.

Holliday Junction Assembly

For the Holliday junction assembly, we mixed the four strands (R, B, H, and X) at equimolar concentrations in 1 × PBS and then heated to 70 °C followed by a linear temperature cooling to 4 °C over 90 min using a thermocycler. All assembled constructs were analyzed by 12% Native PAGE using 200 V for 2 h with 1 × TBE as running buffer and approximately 0.5 pmol amount of HJ construct. The gel was stained and imaged with an Amersham Typhoon laser scanner.

Gold Particle DNA Coating Preparation

We have used a freeze–thawing approach to coat the nanorods with DNA.40 First, we mixed TCEP and Thiol-DNA for 5 min with 200× excess TCEP. DNA and TCEP were added to an Eppendorf tube containing the citrate-capped AuNR suspension. DNA was approximately 2.5 × 105 × excess to AuNRs. The suspension was briefly mixed on a vortex, and then, we added SDS to a final concentration of 0.01%SDS. We vortexed and centrifuged briefly and put the suspension on dry ice for a few minutes until frozen. After freezing, the suspension was left at room-temperature to thaw. Then, we repeated the freeze–thawing cycles once more. Here, we kept the particle suspension on freeze until approximately an hour before use. After thawing, we centrifuged the particles at 10.000× G for 2 min such that the particles formed an oily pellet. We carefully removed the supernatant and resuspended in 1 × TAE 0.01%SDS. We repeated the centrifugation and resuspension 3 times.

Sample Preparation

We have used 24 × 60 mm #1.5 microscope cover glasses as a sample substrate. First, we flushed the slides with acetone and then ethanol for 10 s each. After that, we blow-dried the slides and further cleaned in a UV/Ozone oven for approximately 1 h. Then, we attached ibidi sticky slides (sticky slide VI 0.4, ibidi) on the clean glass surface to make our flow cells. We functionalized the surface with BSA-biotin, Neutravidin, and receptor DNA strands by sequentially adding the different solutions to the channel and washing in between. First, we added 50 μL of 1 mg/mL BSA-biotin in 1 × TAE with 100 mM NaCl to the channel and incubated for 5 min. The channel was then washed with 100 μL of 1 × TAE with 100 mM NaCl twice. Then, we added 50 μL of 0.5 mg/mL in 1 × TAE with 100 mM NaCl Neutravidin to the channel, incubated for 3 min, and again followed by a double washing step. After that, we added 50 μL of 25 nM receptor DNA in 1 × TAE with 100 mM NaCl and washed four times after incubating for 3 min. Then, we added a gold particle suspension to the channel. The particle coverage is tuned by the concentration of the particle suspension and incubation time. Usually, we dilute the particle suspension to half of the concentration obtained right after the purification step after freeze–thawing. We would add 30 uL of the suspension to the channel and wash twice with 1 × TAE with 10 mM MgCl2 after 10 s incubation.

Imaging Conditions

Depending on the bound-state lifetimes of the HJ or single ligand, we used 10 pM–2 nM in 1 × TAE with 10 mM MgCl2, and to be sure, we were observing single events. For constructs with short bound-state lifetimes, we used high sample concentration and 10 ms camera integration time (100 s–1 frame rate), whereas for constructs with long bound-state lifetimes, we used low concentration and 50 ms camera integration time. We recorded 10000–20000 frames for each FOV depending on the event frequency (which depends on the concentration). All measurements that are used in comparison were conducted the same day to have as identical conditions as possible for the measurements in comparison. We used ATTO655-labeled HJs for all experiments except the measurements using 1000 fps where we have used ATTO643 instead. During measurements, we monitored the temperature close to the microscope slide inside the microscope with an HHR506R thermocouple thermometer (Omega Engineering, Inc.), to ensure the same temperature for all measurements in comparison.

oxDNA Simulations

We performed a comprehensive oxDNA molecular dynamics simulation of the trivalent binding Holliday junction with different lengths of the T-spacer to estimate the flexibility and restriction of each system. Specifically, a Holliday junction based on 6GDH RCSB PDB62 as a starting point was modified using Oxviewer48 to fit the sequence and specifications of the 3-ligand junction with ligands of length 8. A junction was designed for each of the T-spacer lengths, 0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12, and the designs were exported to the oxDNA.top and.dat formats for simulation using Oxviewer. We then performed a short relaxation of the structure by running 104 simulation steps of harmonic force relaxation followed by 104 steps of the oxDNA Monte Carlo simulation. Simulations were then performed using default settings (precise information available in the configuration files) for 109 simulation steps corresponding to approximately 3.03 μs. The molecular configuration was saved every 104 steps resulting in a trajectory consisting of 105 complete configurations for analysis.

Simulations

We carried out numerical simulation of the optical properties of NP and fluorescence enhancement using the boundary element method (BEM), with the MNPBEM17 toolbox,63 as previously described.41 Here, we used a 40 nm × 82 nm nanorod that was modeled as a cylinder capped by hemispheres, and the ATTO655 dye with 30% quantum yield was modeled as a point-like dipole where three perpendicular orientations of the dipole were considered and averaged in resulting quantities, assuming that the dye rotates faster than its fluorescence lifetime. Light interaction of the NR and dye was calculated with 637 nm illumination polarized along the long axis of the rods. A total of 82 × 40 relative positions between the NP and dipole were simulated. We applied a 2D interpolation function to obtain high-resolution images of the total fluorescence enhancement. Decay rate enhancements were calculated over the entire emission spectrum of the dye. The total fluorescence enhancement was calculated by multiplying the near-field intensity enhancement and quantum yield modification, neglecting fluorescence saturation.
