Primary mediastinal large B cell lymphoma in pregnancy: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Pu Wei, Shenglan Tang, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary mediastinal B-cell lymphoma (PMBCL) is a subtype of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) that typically presents as a large mass originating in the mediastinum and infiltrating the surrounding organs, resulting in pleural and pericardial effusions. The occurrence of PMBCL during pregnancy presents a significant challenge to clinical management, necessitating a comprehensive consideration of the unique risks posed to both the pregnant woman and the foetus when selecting the optimal treatment. This report presents cases of PMBCL diagnosed during late pregnancy and reviews the pertinent literature, emphasising the necessity of prompt diagnosis and the implementation of tailored management strategies for different stages of pregnancy to enhance pregnancy outcomes.Case presentationA 29-year-old woman with four documented pregnancies, yet no documented deliveries. At 15 weeks' gestation, the patient presented at the local hospital with a cough, which was diagnosed as an upper respiratory tract infection. She was treated with oral antibiotics; however, there was no significant improvement in her condition. During the 28th week of gestation, the patient's respiratory distress and dysphagia worsened, and she was unable to assume a recumbent position. The patient was admitted to our hospital with a computed tomography scan indicating the presence of a sizable mediastinal mass. Given the potential risks, benefits, and recent status, a decision was made to perform an emergency caesarean section. The procedure resulted in the successful delivery of the foetus, with Apgar scores of 6, 7, and 7 at 1, 5, and 10 minutes, respectively. A comprehensive examination of the neonate revealed no additional abnormalities and demonstrated normal developmental progress after a period of over four months. Following delivery, the diagnosis of PMBCL was confirmed by puncture biopsy of the mediastinal mass and PET-CT. The pregnant woman then received five doses of DA-EPOCH-R chemotherapy, resulting in a significant reduction of the mediastinal mass and remission of her symptoms.ConclusionThis case study illustrates the significant challenges associated with diagnosing and treating Primary mediastinal B-cell lymphoma during pregnancy. In this rare and complex case, the timing of termination of pregnancy and the choice of treatment regimen are of critical importance in order to improve maternal and infant outcomes.Keywords: Primary mediastinal large B cell lymphoma, Pregnancy, Treatment

Introduction

The incidence of malignant tumours during pregnancy is approximately 1 in 1,000, with the most common being breast cancer, malignant melanoma, cervical cancer and lymphoma in that order. Among the lymphomas occurring during pregnancy, Hodgkin’s lymphoma (HL) is the most common, followed by non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL). Primary mediastinal B-cell lymphoma (PMBCL) is a particularly prevalent form of NHL, characterised by a mature aggressive large B-cell lymphoma originating in the mediastinum. This is most commonly observed as a large mediastinal mass on imaging, with lymph node and bone marrow involvement being rare. On account of its distinctive clinical and pathological characteristics, it is regarded as a discrete disease entity within the World Health Organisation classification. Although lymphoma occurring during pregnancy is a rare occurrence, it represents a significant threat to both the pregnant woman and the foetus. The diagnosis of lymphoma in pregnancy presents a number of challenges, as does the treatment of the mother while minimising potential harm to the foetus. Given the rarity of PMBCL, current research on prenatal treatment is largely based on case reports and small case series. This report presents cases of PMBCL diagnosed during late pregnancy and reviews the relevant literature, emphasising the importance of early diagnosis and management strategies tailored to the different stages of pregnancy to improve pregnancy outcomes.

Case presentation

A 29-year-old gravida 4 para 0 woman sought medical attention at a local hospital for a cough at her 15 weeks’ gestation. The attending physician at the local hospital diagnosed an upper respiratory tract infection and initiated treatment with oral azithromycin and alloxacillin. The patient did not experience a notable improvement in her condition following the administration of the prescribed medication. At 28 weeks of gestation, the patient’s cough worsened, becoming predominantly dry with a slight production of sputum. This was accompanied by shortness of breath, dysphagia and an inability to assume a recumbent position. Despite the administration of oral antibiotics, the patient’s symptoms remained unresolved, prompting her to seek further medical attention at our hospital.

The patient had a favourable lifestyle and did not smoke or consume alcohol. The patient was in good health prior to this pregnancy, with no history of lung or heart disease, and no family history of tumours or genetic disorders.

A physical examination was conducted, during which the patient was observed with a nasal cannula delivering 2 L/min of oxygen. The patient’s temperature was also recorded. The patient’s temperature was 36.7 °C, pulse 105 beats/min, respiration 21 breaths/min, blood pressure 126/79 mmHg, with clear and soft sounds, and shortness of breath. There was no cyanosis of lips and mouth, and no jugular vein distension. The examination revealed low respiratory sounds in the left lung, with no discernible wet rales. The heart rhythm was unchanged, and no pathological murmurs were heard. Additionally, the abdomen was distended, consistent with a 29-week pregnancy.

A computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest (including the mediastinum) revealed anterior mediastinal occupancy, with a size of approximately 160 mm by 96 mm (Fig. 1). This finding was considered to be indicative of a malignant neoplastic lesion. The presence of multiple solid nodules in both lungs precludes the complete exclusion of a metastatic tumour. CT revealed the presence of left pleural effusion, accompanied by left lung inflation insufficiency and scattered pulmonary inflammation. A reduction in the amount of effusion present within the right pleural cavity was observed. Additionally, there was evidence of increased pericardial effusion. There was evidence of enlargement of multiple mediastinal lymph nodes.

In view of the uncertainty regarding the nature of the mediastinal mass and the potential for continuing the pregnancy to result in increased airway compression and respiratory and circulatory failure, the medical team opted to terminate the pregnancy in order to clarify the diagnosis, and to devise an appropriate treatment plan. Following the administration of a dexamethasone injection to facilitate fetal lung maturation, a lower uterine segment caesarean section was performed, resulting in the delivery of an immature male infant weighing 1430 g. The infant’s Apgar score at 1 min was 6 (1 point each for respiration, response, muscle tone, and skin colour), and at 5 and 10 min, it was 7 (1 point each for respiration, response, and muscle tone). Following a joint discussion among the relevant specialists, including obstetricians, radiologists, respiratory physicians, cardiothoracic surgeons, medical oncologists, and ICU doctors, it was concluded that, based on the current symptoms and signs and examination results, malignant lymphoma with metastasis was the most probable diagnosis. However, the patient’s tumour index of NSE was high, and combined with the CT imaging manifestations of the lungs, small-cell lung cancer also needed to be considered. Furthermore, the patient’s presentation with the disease during pregnancy, along with the elevation of AFP and CA 125, necessitates the exclusion of malignant teratomas as a potential diagnosis. It is recommended that a biopsy be performed as soon as possible in order to clarify the diagnosis.

A puncture biopsy of the mediastinal mass was conducted following the caesarean section. The pathological findings indicated a diffuse distribution of lymphocytes, with readily visible nuclear schizis, some cytoplasmic translucency, and visible fibrous septations. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed the following: CK (-), CD20 (+), PAX-5 (+), CD23 (+), p53 (+), CD30 (+), CD15 (-), BoB-1 (+), 0CT2 (+), CD10 (-), BCL-6 (+), BCL-2 part (+), MUM-1 (+), c-MYC (+) 20%, CyclinD1 (-), CD21 (-), ALK(1A4)(-), TdT(-), CD3 partially weak(+), CD5 partially weak(+), CD2 partially weak(+), CD4 partially weak(+), CD8 partially weak(+), Ki-67(+) 70–80%, EBV in situ hybridisation: EBER(-) (Fig. 2). The patient was diagnosed with PMBCL, a clear diagnosis that was assessed as stage IV according to PET-CT (Fig. 3). Additionally, the patient exhibited an LDH of 1296 U/L, which was greater than twice the upper limit of normal, and was clinically staged as progressive, with invasion of the adrenal glands and liver and a poor prognosis, according to the International Prognostic Index (IPI). The decision was taken to utilise the DA-EPOCH-R chemotherapy regimen. The patient has undergone five cycles of chemotherapy thus far, with a notable reduction in the size of the mediastinal mass.

Discussion and conclusions

The incidence of malignant tumours during pregnancy is approximately 1 in 1,000 [1–3]. The most prevalent malignant tumours, in order of prevalence, are breast cancer, malignant melanoma, cervical cancer and lymphoma [4]. Among the lymphomas occurring in pregnancy, HL is the most prevalent, followed by NHL. Among NHL, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common [5, 6]. PMBCL, a subtype of DLBCL, accounts for less than 10% of DLBCL cases and occurs predominantly in young females. PMBCL typically presents as a large mass originating from the mediastinum and infiltrating the surrounding organs, resulting in pleural effusion and pericardial effusion. Lymph node and bone marrow involvement are rare [7]. On account of its distinctive clinical and pathological characteristics, it is regarded as a discrete disease entity within the World Health Organisation classification.

The most common clinical presentation of PMBCL is a large anterior mediastinal mass with localised tumour compression, which can lead to rapidly progressive symptoms such as dyspnoea, cough, dysphagia, damage to the airway and large blood vessels, and obstruction of the superior vena cava (SVC). The SVC syndrome can cause facial swelling or head swelling, cough, and shortness of breath as well as facial congestion, prominent neck and chest veins, arm oedema, pleural heart effusion, and rare pericardial tamponade.

The diagnosis of PMBCL is based on the presence of specific clinical symptoms and pathological manifestations, which often necessitates differentiation from other forms of non-specific diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL, NOS) involving the thymus, classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) and mediastinal grey zone lymphoma (MGZL). The tumour indicators NSE, AFP and CA125 were elevated in this patient, necessitating the exclusion of lung cancer, malignant teratoma and tumours of germ cell origin.

An understanding of the stage of the disease may facilitate the treatment of PMBCL. The staging of lymphoma can be performed via a number of modalities, including CT imaging, 18 F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET), and whole-body magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). In light of the carcinogenic and teratogenic risks associated with ionising radiation, it is advisable to minimise foetal exposure. The radiotracer employed in PET imaging crosses the placenta, resulting in a higher dose of radiation reaching the foetus during the early and mid-gestation periods [8]. The additional benefit of FDG-PET imaging in comparison to CT at the time of diagnosis is limited, with its primary clinical application occurring at the end of treatment and during mid-term evaluation. Whole-body MRI is frequently employed as an alternative to PET-CT scanning, as it does not expose the patient or the foetus to the potential risks associated with ionising radiation.

Once the diagnosis and staging are complete, further considerations arise with regard to the selection of an appropriate regimen, dosage adjustments in accordance with the pregnancy, and dosing schedules in relation to the planned birth. Rituximab combined with CHOP (R‑CHOP) has long been the standard chemotherapy regimen for patients diagnosed with large B‑cell lymphoma. Studies have demonstrated that in patients with primary mediastinal large B‑cell lymphoma (PMBCL), R‑CHOP achieves 2‑year progression‑free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) rates exceeding 80% and 90%, respectively [9, 10]. In a cohort study, Maggen et al. [11] reported outcomes of 80 pregnant patients with non‑Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), 89% of whom received R‑CHOP. Among those with large B‑cell lymphoma (LBCL) treated with R‑CHOP, the 3‑year PFS and OS rates were 83% and 96% for limited‑stage disease, and 61% and 73% for advanced‑stage disease, respectively. These findings suggest that R‑CHOP is a feasible treatment option for pregnant patients with lymphoma. Another study reported similar results, with a 5‑year OS rate of 82% for diffuse large B‑cell lymphoma (DLBCL) and PMBCL, along with favourable neonatal outcomes [12]. It is noteworthy that approximately 70% of patients subsequently receive consolidative local radiotherapy. To mitigate the short‑ and long‑term adverse effects associated with radiotherapy, a single‑arm phase II study published in 2013 investigated 6‑8 cycles of dose‑adjusted etoposide, prednisone, vincristine, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and rituximab (DA‑EPOCH‑R) in patients with newly diagnosed PMBCL. After a median follow‑up of 63 months, the 5‑year event‑free survival (EFS) and OS rates were 93% and 97%, respectively [13]. Real‑world data have further confirmed that R‑CHOP followed by radiotherapy or DA‑EPOCH‑R yields comparable efficacy in PMBCL, with 5‑year PFS rates of 91.3% and 92.6%, respectively [14].

PMBCL is a rare occurrence, which has led to a lack of consensus regarding the optimal chemotherapy regimen for this condition. The prevailing view is that patients with PMBCL are treated with the R-CHOP regimen during pregnancy and then transitioned to the DA-REPOCH-R regimen after delivery. Some studies have demonstrated the capacity of etoposide to induce fetal cranial abnormalities and skeletal malformations [15]. Nevertheless, evidence indicates that mid- and late-gestational etoposide exposure does not result in congenital malformations [16–19]. Annunzio et al. [20] reported favourable outcomes in four patients with PMBCL who received the DA-REPOCH chemotherapy regimen during pregnancy. The timing of the commencement of chemotherapy is also of great importance. The period preceding the tenth week of gestation represents a critical stage of organogenesis, during which the use of chemotherapeutic agents and radiotherapy carries a substantial risk of teratogenicity. Following the 10-week mark of gestation and prior to the birth of the infant, the continued development and maturation of the foetal organs renders the period vulnerable to the effects of drug exposure. However, the likelihood of serious structural abnormalities is minimal, and thus, chemotherapy is regarded as a relatively safe intervention during the mid and late stages of pregnancy. Furthermore, chemotherapy can cause neutropenia and result in immunosuppression in the foetus. Consequently, it is not recommended for pregnant women to deliver within three weeks of chemotherapy, which means that chemotherapy is not usually administered after 34 weeks of gestation. The patient reported in this case presented with severe SVC syndrome, which required immediate treatment to relieve the patient’s symptoms and early diagnosis in order to choose an appropriate treatment plan. Consequently, a caesarean section was performed to terminate the pregnancy following the administration of dexamethasone, which has been demonstrated to facilitate fetal lung maturation. A puncture biopsy was conducted without delay following the surgical procedure, with the objective of establishing a definitive diagnosis of PMBCL. Furthermore, a PET-CT scan was undertaken to ascertain the stage of the disease, which was determined to be stage IV. Ultimately, a DA-EPOCH-R chemotherapy regimen was selected (Table 1).

The occurrence of PMBCL during pregnancy is an infrequent occurrence, which results in diagnostic challenges and postponements of treatment. The management of PMBCL during pregnancy necessitates the involvement of a multidisciplinary team, comprising obstetricians, anaesthetists, haematologists, intensivists and other specialists. Despite the paucity of retrospective data, the majority of patients with combined PMBCL during pregnancy have favourable maternal and foetal outcomes. The most challenging aspects of this condition are establishing a diagnosis and managing PMBCL in the early stages of pregnancy, as well as determining the optimal time for delivery in the late stages. As knowledge of chemotherapeutic agents continues to expand and new agents emerge, it is increasingly important to have empirical and safety data on their use in pregnancy, which can inform the management of patients with combined PMBCL in pregnancy.
