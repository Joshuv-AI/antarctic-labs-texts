The response characteristics and stability evaluation of vegetated slope under strong wind
Authors: Yanlin Liu, Fei Wang, Feng Ji, Lianhua Zhang, Jianjun Zhao, Chen Zheng, Jibin Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
As one of the major geological disasters in southeastern China, typhoon-induced vegetation slope instability causes significant loss of life and property each year. Despite the criticality of this issue, the response mechanism of vegetated slopes to wind loading in terms of soil deformation and stability still remains unclear. This research conducted field investigations on 330 historical landslides in Yongjia County, Zhejiang Province, analyzing their spatiotemporal distribution and developmental characteristics to establish a conceptual model. The influence of wind and vegetation conditions on slope dynamic parameters and soil strength parameters were subsequently determined through numerical simulations using FLAC3D software, model tests, and direct shear tests. The results show that: (1) The influence of wind on the dynamic response of vegetated slopes is significantly affected by wind speed. At wind forces ≤ 12 (hurricane: 32.7–36.9 m/s), vegetation plays a positive role in enhancing slope stability. At wind forces ≥ 13 (typhoon: 37.0–41.4 m/s), vegetation exerts a negative influence under the combined action of strong wind loads. (2) Based on the experimentally determined evolution of soil strength parameters, a formula c (v, w) was fitted to express the variation of cohesion with wind speed (v) and moisture content (w). (3) The optimized evaluation model demonstrates increased sensitivity compared to the traditional model, resulting in a 17.88% increase in sliding force and a 10.62–11.64% increase in anti-sliding force. This research accounts for both the direct and indirect effects of wind loading on vegetated slopes. The findings are expected to enhance the stability assessment of vegetated slopes under strong winds and facilitate the development of more accurate machine learning and statistical models in the future.

Introduction

Landslides triggered by typhoons pose a significant threat to human life and property worldwide. The southeast coastal region of China, experiencing an average of 7 typhoon landfalls annually, is particularly hard-hit by these events1–3. For example, the 2019 Super Typhoon Lekima harmed 14.025 million people nationwide, with a direct economic loss reaching 7.178 billion dollars4. According to the field investigation, as of 2021, historical landslides in Yongjia County, Zhejiang Province, account for 63.7% of all regional geological disasters, and 80.9% of them were formed during typhoons. That is, among all types of typhoon-induced geological disasters, landslides possess the highest number and damage intensity, and landslides mainly occur on high vegetation-covered slopes5. However, the failure mechanism of typhoon-induced landslides is complex and the evaluation method is not definite, which strongly reduce the accuracy of landslide risk assessment and prediction results. As a result, these disasters have become a major engineering geology problem that restricts the construction of Zhejiang Common Prosperity Demonstration Zone6.

Through field investigations, numerical modeling, and physical experiments, researchers have delved into the failure mechanisms, early warning models, and stability evaluations of typhoon-induced landslides7–9. However, these investigations have predominantly focused on extreme rainfall events during typhoons, ignoring the intricate interplay between wind and vegetation. Many researchers have gradually focused on the complex inherent mechanism in the typhoon-induced landslides, which should consider not only rainfall but also wind10. For example, taking advantage of wind tunnel tests, Sun et al.11 measured the wind pressure on vegetation and calculated the slope stability factor. The results revealed that when wind speed is greater than or equal to 17 m/s, the wind load increases the sliding force by about 10%. In a similar vein, employing theoretical formulas and numerical techniques, Zhuang et al.12 elucidated that at wind speeds reaching 45 m/s, the factor of safety (Fs) of slopes decreases by 20%. There exists an imperative to comprehensively quantify the variations in vegetated slope stability under wind disturbances, thereby addressing critical research gaps concerning the failure mechanisms of vegetated slopes triggered by typhoons.

Meanwhile, the synergistic effect of wind and vegetation has great influences on slope stability by changing the mechanical response and soil strength of slope. Among them, using physical experiments, numerical simulations, and theoretical derivation, several investigations have explored the quantitative relationship between slope failure processes and factors such as wind load models13, slope structure14,15, vegetation cover density 16, and plant species17. However, the aforementioned studies primarily focus on the impact of a single factor on slope failure, neglecting the complex nature of wind and the diverse morphology of vegetation. On the other hand, vegetation changes soil strength by controlling mechanical and hydrological characteristics of slope18. Therefore, researchers have conducted extensive investigations on the hydrologic promoting19 and mechanical strengthening effect of roots20. And some of them focus on clarifying the positive effects of roots on soil strength changes, like Torabbeigi et al.21 and Liu et al.19 respectively employed numerical and physical experiments to reveal the capability of vegetation in defending coastlines against waves. Many findings taking into account vegetation species22, planting layout patterns17, root structure23 and root distribution density24, but the negative effects of wind on vegetation were not considered. Then, with the frequent occurrence of vegetated slope instability events during typhoons, the adverse effects of vegetation on slope under strong wind have drawn more attention25,26. Specifically, based on numerical simulation, Mcguire27 proposed that slope consolidation with vegetation has limitations, and Jiang28 found through statistical analysis that the landslide susceptibility model considering both the positive and negative effects of vegetation would be more accurate. In other words, it is necessary and urgent to explicitly revealed the quantitative effects of wind and vegetation factors on slope dynamic response and soil strength degradation.

Therefore, this study selected the Longchuan landslide in Yongjia County during Typhoon Hagupit as the research object and acquired the wind speed threshold of the slope dynamic response using the numerical software FLAC3D. In addition, through wind loading tests and direct shear tests, we fitted the degradation formula of soil strength that changes with the variation of wind speed. Finally, a slope stability evaluation model that simultaneously considers the effects of wind and vegetation was constructed. The results will quantify the response of slope to wind load and vegetation influence, thereby optimizing existing evaluation model and enhance the accuracy of slope stability assessment in the study area and pave the way for the future development of machine learning models, ultimately strengthening landslide hazard risk assessment and management capabilities.

Typhoon-induced landslides

Geomorphological and geological characteristics

The study area encompasses the Yongjia County (28° 01′ 08′′ E, 120° 19′34″N to 28° 36′54″E, 120°59′19″) in Zhejiang Province, covering an area of 2677.5 km2 (Fig. 1a,b). The landform is dominated by structural erosion hills, with an elevation range of 200–500m. The annual average temperature is 18.5 °C, the annual average precipitation is 1743.9 mm, and the maximum daily precipitation is as high as 278.0 mm. The rainfall is mainly affected by typhoon and rainy season. The lithology of the outcrop in the area around the landslide is the Lower Cretaceous Series (K1c). The lithology is mainly composed of sandstone and mudstone interbedded with coarse sandstone, glutenite and tuff, and the weathering of the rock mass is serious.Fig. 1Spatial distribution map of historical landslides in Yongjia County (software: ArcGIS; version number: 10.7, http://www.esri.com; Digital elevation data from https://www.gscloud.cn.).

Spatial distribution map of historical landslides in Yongjia County (software: ArcGIS; version number: 10.7, http://www.esri.com; Digital elevation data from https://www.gscloud.cn.).

As of August 2023, totally 331 landslides have occurred in the study area. According to the time records of each landslide emergency report (Data provided by Zhejiang Geology and Mineral Technology Co., LTD), 230 landslides were formed during typhoon events, accounting for 70% of the total number of landslides, and the occurrence times were all within 24 h after typhoon landing. It is notable that a large proportion of the 230 typhoon-induced landslides are distributed on densely vegetated slopes. 80% of the landslides are located in the closed evergreen broadleaved forest, and the NDVI (Normalized Differnce Vegetation Index) is mainly concentrated in the range of 0.66 ~ 0.74. According to the field investigation, slope vegetation includes Moso bamboo, tea tree, tree, shrub and cultivated land, and Moso bamboo accounts for 65.4% of the total area. Additionally, 86.4% of the events are distributed below the elevation of 500 m, and 57.8% of the slopes have a slope angle of 15°–30° and a slope aspect of 157.5°–202.5°. These landslides were generated by 12 typhoons: Wendy, Rananim, Haitang, Matsa, Morakot, Haikui, Kong-rey, Fitow, Meranti, Lekima, Hagupit and In-Fa, with 58% of the landslides occurring above wind force 5 (8.0 ~ 10.7 m/s) (Fig. 1c). Human engineering activities are frequent in the study area, and there are obvious slope cutting phenomena in the front and rear slopes of the house, and the slope cutting degree is within 55° ~ 75°.

Correlation of landslide with typhoon and vegetation

The data show that all the 330 landslides are small soil landslides, and the geomorphic unit belongs to the low mountainous area of erosion and denudation hills in southeastern Zhejiang. The sliding body is loose gravel silty clay and completely weathered soil, and the underlying bedrock is tuff. That is, the geological environment conditions of the landslide are consistent, so the correlation analysis of environmental dynamic factors is further carried out.

According to the historical data of landslides, this research screened the five years with the highest number of landslides from 1999 to 2022, which corresponed to six typhoons, such as Wendy, Rananim, Haitang, Matsa, Haikui and Lekima. Meanwhile, the average daily precipitation, wind speed and the number of landslides were analyzed through meteorological data released by the National Tibetan Plateau Data Center29, the National Science & Technology Infrastructure of China30, and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) (Fig. 2). The results showed that: (1) The peak precipitation occurred in June to July and July to September in the study area. In particular, the landslides were concentrated in August to September, accounting for 86.1% of the total number of landslides. From June to July, the precipitation is also very large, but only a few landslides occur. (2) From mid-late July to late September is the active period of tropical cyclone, and the fluctuating wind speed reaches its peak. The number of landslides also increases sharply, coinciding with the time of wind speed peak. At the same time, according to the statistics of Zhejiang Meteorological Observatory, from 1991 to 2020, the plum rain in Zhejiang began on June 8, lasted for 30 days, and the typhoon landed from mid-late July to September. These results reveal that the landslides during typhoon are not only related to rainfall, but also closely related to strong winds, and the direct and indirect effects caused by strong winds should not be ignored.Fig. 2Daily data of precipitation, wind speed and number of landslides in 5 typical years. Conceptual model and parameters

The Longchuan landslide occurred after the landing of Typhoon No. 4 in 2020. The landslide is located in Longchuan Village, Sanjiang Street, Yongjia County, Wenzhou City, Zhejiang Province (120° 44′ 40″ E, 28° 05′ 13″ N). The landslide mass is tongue-shaped and has a main sliding direction of 300°, with a longitudinal length of about 15 m, a transverse width of about 20 m and a thickness of about 2 ~ 3 m. The field survey shows that the slope predominantly covered by Moso bamboo, with trees and tea trees scattered. The culms of Moso bamboo are straight, round and hollow, with a general height of about 10–20 m and a diameter of about 8–16 cm at breast height. According to the height-diameter formula, the Moso bamboo is 13 m tall, with a diameter at breast height of 10 cm, a crown width of 2 m and a height under the branches of 5.5 m. The roots of bamboo are buried about 40 cm underground, with a horizontal distribution range of 1 m (Fig. 5b). The length of the geometric model of the Longchuan landslide is 35 m long (Y axis), 20 m high (Z axis) and 20 m wide (X axis). The height under the branches is 5.5 m, and the root system is buried 40 cm underground, and the diameters of trunk and crown are 10 cm and 2 m, respectively (Fig. 5c). According to the root–soil composite models established by the researchers (Köstler et al., 1968), the plate-like root system of Moso bamboo was simplified into a cylinder.

Optimization of slope stability evaluation

Existing slope stability evaluation models analyze the quantitative relationship between sliding and anti-sliding forces. In this study, considering the strong wind load in the southeast coast area and the dense vegetation coverage of the regional slopes, two aspects influencing the slope stability were taken into account: (1) the direct influence of wind load on the sliding force of landslides, (2) the influence of wind-induced vegetation shaking and vibration on soil strength.

Specifically, three models, namely the traditional calculation model, the model considering wind load and the optimization model proposed in this study, were compared to verify the sensitivity of the calculation method.

(a) Traditional model: The model does not consider the influence of rainfall infiltration and dynamic load; it only calculates the sliding and anti-sliding forces generated under the self-weight of sliding mass. Effect of vegetation density on slope dynamic response

It can be seen that the slope displacement variation differs remarkably under different wind load conditions (Fig. 11). From working condition 2 to working condition 5 (2.45 ~ 34.8 m/s), the displacement of the vegetation-covered slope gradually decreases with the increase of vegetation density, suggesting a negative correlation. From condition 6 to condition 9 (39.2 ~ 53.5 m/s), however, the slope displacement increases with the increase of vegetation density, which is positively correlated. Such a distinct difference can be attributed to the fact that when the wind speed is less than 34.8 m/s (condition 5), the vegetation mainly facilitates soil consolidation. By contrast, when the wind speed is ≥ 39.2 m/s (condition), the influence of wind-induced vegetation shaking and vibration on soil strength is larger than the consolidation effect exerted by the root system. Consequently, the load on tree canopy gradually increases with the increase of vegetation density, thus enhancing the dynamic load transferred into the slope interior.Fig. 11Displacement versus vegetation density curve.

Discussion

The majority of research on typhoon-induced landslides has focused on elucidating the role of rainfall conditions in slope instability, often classifying these landslides as rainfall-induced and analyzing factors such as rainfall patterns36, infiltration intensity9, and preferential flow pathways37. While some scholars have investigated the influence of wind, exploring factors such as typhoon track38, wind speed11, and wind direction6, our study focuses on the impact of wind on vegetated slopes. We aim to quantify the contribution of wind loading to typhoon-induced landslides, beyond the influence of rainfall. Using numerical simulations, we investigated the slope response characteristics under 9 wind speed scenarios and 3 vegetation density models. Notably, a significant shift in displacement occurred when wind speeds exceeded 36.9 m/s, with displacement increasing rapidly with wind speed. Under these conditions, the reinforcing effect of roots on the slope was limited, while the vibrational effect of vegetation facilitated deformation. This finding aligns with the accelerated deformation stage of typhoon-induced landslide evolution observed in Shen et al.7 rainfall-based physical model. Further support comes from Zhuang et al.12, who, through numerical simulations, demonstrated a 20% decrease in slope stability coefficient for winds exceeding Force 14 (45 m/s), suggesting that winds beyond this threshold can significantly alter slope integrity and stability. Another significant finding is the increased sensitivity of our optimized slope stability assessment model, resulting in a lower factor of safety (Fs) compared to traditional models by 0.065 (the impact of rainfall infiltration was not considered in this study). Consistent with the results of Sun et al.11 physical experiments, we observed a decrease in Fs of 0.031 when wind direction same with the slope orientation, indicating the direct influence of wind loading. Additionally, the three models employed in this study utilize actual data from the Longchuan landslide, with calculated Fs values consistently less than 1, consistent with the actual instability of the case study, demonstrating model credibility. Furthermore, our model incorporates the deterioration relationship of soil strength parameters and the sudden change in wind speed, which is not accounted for in existing models. This segmented Fs calculation approach offers a more accurate representation.

Our research utilized the following datasets: (1) Landslide data: 331 landslides recorded in Yongjia County, Zhejiang Province from 1999 to 2021, including occurrence time, location, and corresponding emergency investigation reports; (2) Meteorological data: rainfall and wind speed data from 11 rainfall stations and wind speed monitoring stations within the study area, obtained through an application submitted by the 11th Geological Brigade of Zhejiang Province to relevant authorities. Consequently, the slope stability assessment model developed based on the landslide development characteristics and meteorological conditions of Yongjia County is currently applicable only to the study area. Its accuracy can be further enhanced by incorporating additional landslide case studies. Meanwhile, building upon this foundation, we intend to expand the research scope to neighboring counties and cities, summarizing landslide development characteristics across a wider range of areas. Ultimately, our goal is to develop a comprehensive slope stability assessment methodology applicable to the entire Southeast Coastal region of China.

Feedback from real-world landslide risk assessments highlights the limitations of existing evaluation models, with 80% of landslides occurring outside designated risk control zones. Developing more accurate landslide susceptibility models that integrate machine learning and statistical methods is crucial for mitigating future landslide risk39,40. The optimized model presented in this paper is grounded in the physical mechanisms underlying slope instability. When combined with landslide susceptibility assessment models, it can enhance the accuracy of slope stability evaluations in the Southeast Coastal region of China. This will enhance risk management in these vulnerable areas, ultimately reducing the loss of human life, property, and ecological damage.

Conclusions

This research addresses the gap in current research by comprehensively investigating the response of vegetated slopes under wind loading. Considering both direct and indirect effects of wind loads, we introduce a wind speed threshold value (v0) and a soil strength parameter degradation formula (c(v,w)) to optimize the slope stability assessment model, resulting in improved accuracy compared to existing models.
