Natural Privacy Filters Are Not Always Free: A Characterization of Free Natural Filters Thanks: Authors BH, EL, PM, and PT in alphabetical order.
Matthew Regehr, Bingshan Hu, Ethan Leeman, Pasin Manurangsi, Pierre Tholoniat, Mathias Lécuyer

Abstract
We study natural privacy filters, which enable the exact composition of differentially private (DP) mechanisms with adaptively chosen privacy characteristics. Earlier privacy filters consider only simple privacy parameters such as Rényi-DP or Gaussian DP parameters. Natural filters account for the entire privacy profile of every query, promising greater utility for a given privacy budget. We show that, contrary to other forms of DP, natural privacy filters are not free in general. Indeed, we show that only families of privacy mechanisms that are well-ordered when composed admit free natural privacy filters.

As such, there is a sustained push to integrate and deploy DP in a large number of applications

. This practical DP push also motivates important theoretical progress.

A prominent direction of theoretical improvement comes from alternative definitions of DP, that enable tighter privacy loss accounting under composition and improve the privacy-accuracy tradeoff. The most successful definitions include
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP
[
16
]
(known as approximate DP), the Rényi divergence family of DP definitions such as zCDP
[
21
,
7
]
and RDP
[
40
]
, and privacy definitions that generalize approximate DP by considering all
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP values compatible with a given mechanism, such as the privacy loss distribution (PLD)
[
47
]
and
f
-DP
[
15
]
.
The latter definitions, PLD and
f
-DP, are theoretically appealing as they tightly capture resilience to membership inference attacks framed as hypothesis tests
[
51
,
15
]
. However, composition under these definitions is more complex and can be computationally difficult, with analyses of specific algorithms that rely on numerical approaches to handle composition in practice
[

,
29
]
.

Two other theoretical improvements enable new forms of composition that are necessary requirements for practical, real world DP usage.
First, concurrent composition
[
49
]
supports composition of DP mechanisms running over multiple rounds of interaction with the user—such as the sparse vector technique
[
19
]
—that are running in parallel.
This is required in practical systems which typically have to handle queries from multiple parties simultaneously, and where it is not possible to enforce a sequential ordering of the queries. Even individual users may want to run DP computations concurrently with long-running, multi-step mechanism (e.g.,
[
35
]
).

Second, adaptive composition, the main topic of this paper, enables the composition of DP mechanisms with adaptively chosen privacy parameters (as opposed to regular composition, in which these parameters have to be known in advance).
Adaptive composition is formalized through the notion of
privacy filters

[
43
]
, which accept DP mechanisms as long as their composition remains within a bound fixed in advance (more details in §
2
).
Such adaptive composition is necessary to support long running applications, in which analysts can adaptively query a system with mechanisms and DP parameters of their choice
[
37
,
35
,
36
]
.
Furthermore, filters enable crucial privacy accounting savings through advanced versions of parallel composition
[
37
]
, as well as individual (personalized) DP
[

]
, which enables data dependent privacy accounting in ML model training
[
25
,
54
]
and measurement systems
[

,
28
]
.

A key question in DP composition is whether these various improvements combine with each other.
Ideally these more flexible forms of composition come for free: that is, that can we apply composition results under concurrent mechanisms or adaptive budgets using privacy filters, without a penalty in the final privacy loss after composition.
Flexible composition for free is important not only because it is efficient privacy-wise, but also because it enables reusing all existing composition results, including tight analyses for specific mechanisms and empirical computational results.

As it turns out, concurrent composition is indeed free for all DP definitions
[
50
,
39
]
.
Moreover, concurrent and adaptive filter composition reduces to adaptive filters
[
30
]
, and thus has the same composition cost.
Therefore, the full expressivity of both concurrency and adaptivity hinges on free privacy filter composition for different notions of DP.
Filters, however, are not as well understood.
We know that adaptive composition under privacy filters is free for
\varepsilon
-DP
[
43
]
and Rényi-based DP definitions
[
26
,
38
]
(zCDP, RDP). Filters are also free for Gaussian mechanisms under
f
-DP and PLD accounting
[
46
,
34
]
. However, for
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP, the best known result for adaptive composition with filters is only asymptotically free, with worse constants than non-adaptive composition
[
52
]
. Even less is known about filters under
f
-DP and PLD. In particular, we consider
natural filters
, which leverage lossless composition of the privacy characteristics of individual queries. Natural filters can be realized via PLDs, privacy profiles, or
f
-DP accounting.
In this paper, we make three important contributions towards fully understanding natural privacy filters:

•

First, in §
3
we present a general theory of adaptive mechanisms that issue adversarial queries with a constrained per-query privacy cost.

•

In §
4
we apply this theory to the natural filter and, surprisingly, we show that adaptive composition is NOT free under the PLD nor
f
-DP notions of privacy (see
Corollary

1
).

•

Finally, we show in §
6
that filters are only free for very restricted families of mechanisms: those that are closed under composition, and admit totally ordered tradeoff functions.

Our work also includes some minor contributions including that PLDs can be assigned a natural ordering satisfying a completeness property enabling one to take a supremum of PLDs (see
Proposition

11
). As far as we are aware this property has not been documented in the privacy accounting literature. We alo document in §
5
some families of queries and budgets that do and do not admit free filters.

2
Preliminaries

Differential privacy is the study of randomized algorithms (called mechanisms)
M:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{Y}
where
\mathcal{D}
denotes some space of datasets containing sensitive personal information. Differential privacy requires that an adversary should not gain much statistical advantage for inferring whether the mechanism was run on dataset
D_{1}\in\mathcal{D}
or a second dataset
D_{2}\in\mathcal{D}
when the datasets are close to each other in some sense. That is, the distributions of
M(D_{1})
and
M(D_{2})
should be difficult to distinguish from one another in the hypothesis testing sense and this should hold uniformly for any close datasets. The measure of closeness for datasets is generally defined by a neighbouring relation but this turns out not to be relevant to our work. In fact, our conclusions hold for an arbitrary pair of datasets. For this reason, we fix throughout a single arbitrary pair of datasets
(D_{1},D_{2})\in\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}
and view the privacy characteristics of a mechanism
M
in terms of the hypothesis testing problem
M(D_{1})
vs.
M(D_{2})
.

In our work we reason about the composition of adaptive mechanisms, which are permitted to inspect outcomes of any previously run mechanism. It is enough to define adaptivity for just two rounds of interaction as we can define more complex adaptively composed mechanisms inductively.

Definition 1
.

Let
M_{1}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{Y}_{1}
be a mechanism and let
M_{2}:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}_{1}\to\mathcal{Y}_{2}
be an adaptive mechanism. The adaptive composition of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
is the mechanism

\displaystyle(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})(D):=(Y_{1},Y_{2}),Y_{1}\sim M_{1}(D),Y_{2}\sim M_{2}(D;Y_{1}).

It is natural in general to view the privacy characteristics of any private mechanism as a hypothesis testing problem
[
51
,
14
]
. As such it is desirable to furnish hypothesis testing problem
(P,Q)
with some kind of ordering so that we can compare the privacy guarantees offered by various mechanisms. We also require an ordering so that we can make sense of privacy filters. Fortunately testing pairs can be equipped with a very natural order, namely the Blackwell order.

Definition 2
.

For a pair of distributions
(P,Q)
defined on a common probability space
\Omega
and a second pair
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
defined on
\Omega^{\prime}
, we say that
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
dominates
(P,Q)
in the Blackwell order, written
(P,Q)\preceq(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
, if we can find a Markov kernel
\phi
from
\Omega^{\prime}
to
\Omega
such that
P=\phi P^{\prime}
and
Q=\phi Q^{\prime}
. We write
(P,Q)\prec(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
if
(P,Q)\preceq(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
but not vice-versa.

Informally, the Blackwell order says that more information is available for distinguishing
P^{\prime}
and
Q^{\prime}
compared to
P
vs
Q
. For mechanisms, we can interpret this as
(M(D_{1}),M(D_{2}))\preceq(M^{\prime}(D_{1}),M^{\prime}(D_{2}))
when
M
can be reconstructed from
M^{\prime}
by introducing additional randomness through the kernel
\phi
. Note that the Blackwell order is a partial order.

Instead of a carrying around a pair of distributions to represent the privacy characteristics of a mechanism, we can consolidate them into the privacy loss distribution (PLD)
[
20
]
. As we will see the PLD possesses very desirable composition properties and is a practical basis for privacy loss computations. To formalize the PLD, we require likelihood ratios. Recall that, for a distribution
P
absolutely continuous with respect to a distribution
Q
(
P\ll Q
) on a probability domain
\Omega
, their likelihood ratio is the Radon-Nikodym derivative
\ell(\omega):=\frac{dP}{dQ}(\omega)
. However, in some cases, we would like to consider private mechanisms that violate absolute continuity and still construct the PLD. This can be achieved by taking any measure
\nu
on
\Omega
such that
P,Q\ll\nu
(e.g.
\nu:=P+Q
) and setting
\ell(\omega):=\frac{dP}{d\nu}(\omega)/\frac{dQ}{d\nu}(\omega)
(
\cdot/0:=\infty
). This construction of the likelihood ratio is unique only outside of a null-measure set
1
1

1

More carefully, assume
P,Q\ll\nu,\nu^{\prime}
and set
\mu:=\nu+\nu^{\prime}
so that
P,Q\ll\nu,\nu^{\prime}\ll\mu
. By Radon–Nikodym and the chain rule for measures, we have, for
\omega\sim P
, that
\frac{dP}{d\nu}(\omega)/\frac{dQ}{d\nu}(\omega)=\frac{dP}{d\mu}(\omega)/\frac{dQ}{d\mu}(\omega)=\frac{dP}{d\nu^{\prime}}(\omega)/\frac{dQ}{d\nu^{\prime}}(\omega)
with probability
1
.
, which is enough to construct a unique PLD. For this reason, we denote by
\frac{dP}{dQ}
the likelihood ratio of
P
with respect to
Q
even when
P\not\ll Q
.

Definition 3
.

Let
P
and
Q
be distributions on
\Omega
. The PLD of
(P,Q)
, denoted
\operatorname{PLD}(P\parallel Q)
, is the distribution over
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
of
\log(\frac{dP}{dQ}(\omega)),\omega\sim P
.

For shorthand, we denote by
\operatorname{PLD}(M):=\operatorname{PLD}(M(D_{1})\parallel M(D_{2}))
the PLD of a mechanism
M
. Note that a distribution can be easily recognized as a PLD as follows.

Proposition 1
.

A distribution
L
on
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is the PLD of some
(P,Q
) if and only if it satisfies
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim L}[e^{-Z}]\leq 1
. In this case, one such pair is
(L,L^{\prime})
where
L^{\prime}
is the Esscher tilt of
L
, namely
dL^{\prime}(z):=e^{-z}dL(z)
for
z\in\mathbb{R}
and
L^{\prime}(\{-\infty\}):=1-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim L}[e^{-Z}]
.

PLDs also inherit the Blackwell order: For PLDs
L
and
L^{\prime}
, we say that
L\preceq L^{\prime}
if there exist pairs
(P,Q)
and
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
such that
L:=\operatorname{PLD}(P\parallel Q)
,
L^{\prime}=\operatorname{PLD}(P^{\prime}\parallel Q^{\prime})
, and
(P,Q)\preceq(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
. It is straightforward to show that this order is well-defined regardless of the underlying representations
(P,Q)
and
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
.

PLDs also form a monoid under convolution that is monotone in the Blackwell order.

Proposition 2
.

For any PLDs
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3},L^{\prime}_{1},L^{\prime}_{2}
,

1.

The convolution
L_{1}\oplus L_{2}
is also a PLD;

2.

The identically zero distribution
Id
is a PLD such that
L_{1}\oplus\text{Id}=\text{Id}\oplus L_{1}=L_{1}
;

3.

(L_{1}\oplus L_{2})\oplus L_{3}=L_{1}\oplus(L_{2}\oplus L_{3})
; and

4.

If
L_{1}\preceq L^{\prime}_{1}
and
L_{2}\preceq L^{\prime}_{2}
, then
L_{1}\oplus L_{2}\preceq L^{\prime}_{1}\oplus L^{\prime}_{2}
.

A highly prized property of PLDs is that the privacy characteristics of an adaptive mechanism corresponds to convolution of PLDs, provided that each component fixes a privacy budget in advance. This is important because the convolution of a long sequence of distributions can be computed very efficiently by applying the fast Fourier transform
[
33
]
. Note that we will require more than this to understand fully adaptive composition where the privacy bound of each component is itself adaptive.

Proposition 3
.

Let
M_{1}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{Y}_{1}
and
M_{2}:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}_{1}\to\mathcal{Y}_{2}
be adaptive mechanisms such that
\operatorname{PLD}(M_{1})\preceq L_{1}
and
\operatorname{PLD}(M_{2}(\cdot;y_{1}))\preceq L_{2}
for every
y_{1}\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}
. Then
\operatorname{PLD}(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})\preceq L_{1}\oplus L_{2}
. Moreover, if
\operatorname{PLD}(M_{1})\preceq\operatorname{PLD}(M^{\prime}_{1})
and
\operatorname{PLD}(M_{2}(\cdot;y_{1}))\preceq\operatorname{PLD}(M^{\prime}_{2}(\cdot;y_{1}))
for every
y_{1}\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}
, then
\operatorname{PLD}(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})\preceq\operatorname{PLD}(M^{\prime}_{1}\otimes M^{\prime}_{2})
.

Closely related to the PLD is the hockey-stick curve: a convex reparameterization of the privacy profile of a mechanism generalizing the classical notion of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP.

Definition 4
.

For a pair of distributions
(P,Q)
over
\Omega
, their hockey-stick divergence at order
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}:=(0,\infty)
is

\displaystyle H_{x}(P\parallel Q):=\sup_{E\subseteq\Omega}P(E)-xQ(E).

Again, for convenience, we write
H_{x}(M):=H_{x}(M(D_{1})\parallel M(D_{2}))
and sometimes
h_{M}(x):=H_{x}(M)
for the hockey-stick curve of a mechanism
M
. We will rely on hockey-stick curves in order to reason about adversarial behaviour of adaptive mechanisms. The hockey-stick curve is very closely related to the classical
(\varepsilon,\delta)
form of differential privacy. In particular, by construction of the hockey-stick divergence a mechanism
M
satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP exactly when
H_{e^{\varepsilon}}(M)\leq\delta
. In general, hockey-stick curves are characterized by a few simple conditions.

Proposition 4

(
[
55
]
Lemma 9)
.

A curve
h:\mathbb{R}^{\times}\to[0,1]
is a hockey-stick curve of some pair
(P,Q)
if and only if
h
is convex and decreasing such that
\lim_{x\to 0}h(x)=1
and
h(x)\geq 1-x
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
.

Hockey-stick curves are also closely related to PLDs and can be fully recovered from the PLD via the following formula.

Proposition 5
.

For any mechanism
M
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\times}
,

\displaystyle h_{M}(x)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim\operatorname{PLD}(M)}[(1-xe^{-Z})_{+}]

where
(t)_{+}:=\max\{0,t\}
.

Proof.

For any pair
(P,Q)
defined on
\Omega
with likelihood ratio
\ell
, we have that

\displaystyle H_{x}(P\parallel Q)

\displaystyle=\sup_{E\subseteq\Omega}P(E)-xQ(E)

\displaystyle=\sup_{E\subseteq\Omega}\int_{E}\frac{d(P-xQ)}{dP}(\omega)\,dP(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sup_{E\subseteq\Omega}\int_{E}1-x/\frac{dP}{dQ}(\omega)\,dP(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(1-x/\frac{dP}{dQ}(\omega)\right)_{+}\,dP(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(1-xe^{-\log(\ell(\omega))})_{+}\,dP(\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim\operatorname{PLD}(P\parallel Q)}[(1-xe^{-Z})_{+}].

∎

For convenience, we will sometimes write
h_{L}
to denote the unique hockey-stick curve associated to a PLD
L
, namely
h_{L}(x):=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim L}[(1-xe^{-Z})_{+}]
. Moreover, like PLDs, hockey-stick curves also capture adaptive composition in a natural way.

Proposition 6
.

Let
M_{1}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{Y}_{1}
and
M_{2}:\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Y}_{1}\to\mathcal{Y}_{2}
be adaptive mechanisms and let
\ell^{1}:=\frac{dM_{1}(D_{1})}{dM_{1}(D_{2})}
denote the likelihood ratio for
M_{1}
. Then

\displaystyle h_{M_{1}\otimes M_{2}}(x)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Y_{1}\sim M_{1}(D_{1})}[h_{M_{2}(\cdot;Y_{1})}(x/\ell^{1}(Y_{1}))].

Proof.

Let
\ell
denote the likelihood ratio for
M_{1}\otimes M_{2}
and let
\ell^{2}_{y_{1}}
denote the likelihood ratio for
M_{2}(\cdot;y_{1})
. By Bayes’ rule, we have
\ell(y_{1},y_{2})=\ell^{1}(y_{1})\cdot\ell^{2}_{y_{1}}(y_{2})
, so we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{PLD}(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})\equiv\log(\ell^{1}(Y_{1}))+\log(\ell^{2}_{Y_{1}}(Y_{2})),Y_{1}\sim M_{1}(D_{1}),Y_{2}\sim M_{2}(D_{1};Y_{1}).

By
Proposition

5
and the law of total expectation, it follows that

\displaystyle h_{M_{1}\otimes M_{2}}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Z\sim\operatorname{PLD}(M_{1}\otimes M_{2})}[(1-xe^{-Z})_{+}]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Y_{1}\sim M_{1}(D_{1})}[\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Y_{2}\sim M_{2}(D_{1};Y_{1})}[(1-xe^{-(\log(\ell^{1}(Y_{1}))+\log(\ell^{2}_{Y_{1}}(Y_{2})))})_{+}]]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Y_{1}\sim M_{1}(D_{1})}[\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Y_{2}\sim M_{2}(D_{1};Y_{1})}[(1-x/\ell^{1}(Y_{1})\cdot e^{-\log(\ell^{2}_{Y_{1}}(Y_{2}))})_{+}]]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Y_{1}\sim M_{1}(D_{1})}[\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Z_{2}\sim\operatorname{PLD}(M_{2}(\cdot;Y_{1})}[(1-x/\ell^{1}(Y_{1})\cdot e^{-Z_{2}})_{+}]]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Y_{1}\sim M_{1}(D_{1})}[h_{M_{2}(\cdot;Y_{1})}(x/\ell^{1}(Y_{1}))].

∎

Our last characterization of privacy is given the Type I/Type II error tradeoff curve, also known as the
f
-DP framework of privacy
[
13
]
.

Definition 5
.

Let
(P,Q)
be any distributions on
\Omega
. For any hypothesis test
\phi:\Omega\to\{P,Q\}
for distinguishing
P
and
Q
, we call
\alpha_{\phi}:=\mathbb{P}_{\omega\sim P}[\phi(\omega)=Q]
its Type I error and
\beta_{\phi}:=\mathbb{P}_{\omega\sim Q}[\phi(\omega)=P]
its Type II error. The tradeoff curve for
(P,Q)
is defined as

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}(P\parallel Q):=\inf_{\phi}\{\beta_{\phi}:\alpha_{\phi}\leq\alpha\}.

For convenience, we sometimes write the tradeoff curve of a mechanism
M
with respect to the fixed dataset pair
(D_{1},D_{2})
as
T_{\alpha}(M):=T_{\alpha}(M(D_{1})\parallel M(D_{2}))
or sometimes as
\tau_{M}(\alpha):=T_{\alpha}(M)
where appropriate. In general, we can characterize valid tradeoff curves as follows.

Proposition 7

(
[
13
]
Proposition 2.2)
.

A curve
\tau:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is a tradeoff curve for some testing problem
(P,Q)
exactly when
\tau
is continuous, convex, decreasing, and
\tau(\alpha)\leq 1-\alpha
for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
.

Like hockey-stick curves, it is enough to understand the PLD of a testing problem in order to obtain its tradeoff curve. The following formula can be derived by the well-known Neyman–Pearson lemma.

Proposition 8
.

Let
(P,Q)
be a pair of distributions with tradeoff curve
\tau(\alpha):=T_{\alpha}(P\parallel Q)
and let
F
denote the CDF of
\operatorname{PLD}(P\parallel Q)
. Then

\displaystyle\tau(\alpha)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\operatorname{PLD}(P\parallel Q)}[1(F(Z)>\alpha)\cdot e^{-Z}]

It is common to represent a pair of distributions by either their hockey-stick curve or their tradeoff function. We show that there is a natural link between these representations via inversion and convex conjugacy. For a tradeoff function
\tau
of a testing problem
(P,Q)
, we write
\tau^{-1}(\beta):=\inf\{\alpha\in[0,1]:\tau(\alpha)\leq\beta\}
for its inverse, namely the tradeoff curve for the testing problem
(Q,P)
. For a convex function
g
, we denote its convex conjugate by
g^{*}(y):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}xy-g(x)
. Note that we can extend tradeoff functions to the real-line by setting them to
\infty
outside their support
[0,1]
. Note that a special case of the following formula appears in
[
13
]
(Prop 2.12) for symmetric tradeoff functions but this generalization is new to the best of our knowledge.

Proposition 9
.

Let
(P,Q)
be a pair of distributions with tradeoff function
\tau(\alpha):=T_{\alpha}(P\parallel Q)
and hockey-stick curve
h(x):=H_{x}(P\parallel Q)
. Then

\displaystyle h(x)=1+(\tau^{-1})^{*}(-x)

Proof.

First, let
L
denote the PLD of
(P,Q)
and let
F
be its CDF. By
Proposition

8
,

\displaystyle\tau(\alpha)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim L}[1(Z>F^{-1}(\alpha))\cdot e^{-Z}].

Therefore, by
Proposition

5

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim L}[(1-xe^{-Z})_{+}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim L}[1(Z>\log(x))\cdot\underbrace{(1-xe^{-Z})}_{>0\iff Z>\log(x)}]

\displaystyle=\sup_{\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim L}[1(Z>\varepsilon)\cdot(1-xe^{-Z})]

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim L}[1(Z>F^{-1}(\alpha))\cdot(1-xe^{-Z})]

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha\in[0,1]}1-\alpha-x\tau(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha^{\prime}\in[0,1]}1-\tau^{-1}(\alpha^{\prime})-x\alpha^{\prime}

(
\alpha^{\prime}=\tau^{-1}(\alpha)
)

\displaystyle=1+(\tau^{-1})^{*}(-x).

∎

It follows as a natural consequence that the Blackwell order on pairs, the ordering induced by the tradeoff function, as well as the hockey-stick induced ordering are all equivalent.

Proposition 10
.

Let
(P,Q)
and
(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
be pairs of distributions with tradeoff functions
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
respectively as well as hockey-stick curves
h
and
h^{\prime}
respectively. The following are equivalent

(i)

(P,Q)\preceq(P^{\prime},Q^{\prime})

(ii)

\tau\succeq\tau^{\prime}

(iii)

h\preceq h^{\prime}

The equivalence of (i) and (ii) is given by a celebrated theorem of Blackwell
[
6
, Theorem 10]
. As for the equivalence of (ii) and (iii), this now follows by applying Fenchel–Moreau duality to
Proposition

9
and recalling that convex conjugation is an order-reversing operation.

As a consequence, it turns out that PLDs endowed with the Blackwell order possess the same remarkable completeness property that enables analysis in the real numbers.

Proposition 11
.

For any non-empty family of PLDs
\mathcal{L}
dominated by at least one PLD, there exists a unique PLD
\sup\mathcal{L}
that dominates
\mathcal{L}
but is dominated by every upper bound for
\mathcal{L}
. In this case,

(i)

h_{\sup\mathcal{L}}(x)=\sup\{h_{L}(x):L\in\mathcal{L}\}

(ii)

\tau_{\sup\mathcal{L}}=\operatorname{conv}\{\tau_{L}:L\in\mathcal{L}\}

where
(\operatorname{conv}{\mathcal{F}})(\alpha):=\sup\{f(\alpha):f\text{ is convex},f\preceq\mathcal{F}\}
denotes the lower convex envelope.

This result follows immediately from
Proposition

10
and from noticing that the properties characterizing hockey-stick curves (see
Proposition

4
) are all closed under pointwise suprema. Part (ii) follows from noticing that all of the properties of tradeoff curves (see
Proposition

7
) are also closed under the lower convex envelope operation.

We rely on the supremum extensively in order to reason about natural privacy filters. In particular, the former characterization says that the supremum of PLDs is given by a pointwise supremum in the space of hockey-stick curves, which will be particularly useful for showing our main result. As far as we are aware, the supremum property of PLDs has not actually been documented in the literature.

Query-Restricted Adversaries

In this section we develop a general theory of the privacy characteristics of adversarial adaptive mechanism when the queries the adversary may issue are restricted. This will lead directly to a theory of natural filters as we will see in §

Definition 6
.

Let
k
be a positive integer. We define a privacy rule of length

\displaystyle L_{i}:=\operatorname{PLD}(M_{i}(\cdot;y_{1},\dots,y_{i-1}))\in\Gamma(L_{1},\dots,L_{i-1})

We will write

to denote the set of

-adversaries and we will denote by

\displaystyle\operatorname{PLD}(\Gamma):=\sup\{\operatorname{PLD}(M):M\in\operatorname{Adv}(\Gamma)\}

It turns out that the maximum privacy loss for an adversary respecting a rule

occurs in some sense when the adversary first plays a move and then adversarially chooses a optimal strategy tuned to ensure that the overall strategy is optimal at a particular given point. Note that by

Lemma 1

Let

and, for

\displaystyle\operatorname{PLD}(\Gamma)=\sup\{L\oplus\operatorname{PLD}(\Gamma_{L}):L\in\Gamma(\lambda)\}

where

denotes the rule of length of

that fixes the first move played against

.

Proof.

The case where

is immediate, so we assume

\displaystyle\operatorname{PLD}(\Gamma)\preceq\sup\{L\oplus\operatorname{PLD}(\Gamma_{L}):L\in\Gamma(\lambda)\}.

To that end, consider any
\Gamma
-adversary

as the adaptive composition of

with some

and let

and the desired inequality follows by taking suprema.

More surprising is the reverse inequality, which we prove constructively by passing to hockey-stick curves. Let

that has PLD

-adversary

\displaystyle H_{\kappa}(M^{\prime}_{\kappa})\geq\sup_{M^{\prime\prime}\in\operatorname{Adv}(\Gamma_{L})}H_{\kappa}(M^{\prime\prime})-\varepsilon.

Now consider the
\Gamma

\displaystyle M(x):=(Y_{1},Y^{\prime}),Y_{1}\sim M_{1}(x),Y^{\prime}\sim M^{\prime}_{\kappa/\ell(Y_{1})}(x).

\displaystyle h_{M}(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Y_{1}\sim M_{1}(x_{1})}[h_{M^{\prime}_{\kappa/\ell(Y_{1})}}(\kappa/\ell(Y_{1}))]

\displaystyle\geq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Y_{1}\sim M_{1}(x_{1})}[\sup_{M^{\prime\prime}\in\operatorname{Adv}(\Gamma_{L})}h_{M^{\prime\prime}}(\kappa/\ell(Y_{1}))-\varepsilon]

\displaystyle=(h_{M_{1}}\otimes\sup_{M^{\prime\prime}\in\operatorname{Adv}(\Gamma_{L})}h_{M^{\prime\prime}})(\kappa)-\varepsilon.

In this section, we give a proof of

As we have seen,
Theorem

3
can be proved by showing that, for any family
\mathcal{F}
that is not well-ordered, we can find

for which

\displaystyle g_{1}\otimes\operatorname{conv}\{g_{1},g_{2}\}\prec\operatorname{conv}\{g_{1}\otimes g_{1},g_{1}\otimes g_{2}\}

Equivalently, in the language of PLDs, we would like to show that if

and

are not well-ordered, then
L_{1}\oplus\sup\{L_{1},L_{2}\}\succ\sup\{L_{1}\oplus L_{1},L_{1}\oplus L_{2}\}

Our first step is to show that gaps between PLDs can be amplified in some sense by composition. For a distribution
A
in the reals, we denote by
\supp(A):=\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\forall\text{open }U\ni t,A(U)>0\}
the support of

Proposition 14

(PLD Gap Amplification)
.

Let
A,B,B_{1},B_{2}
be PLDs so that
B_{i}\prec B

with
h_{B_{i}}(e^{\varepsilon_{i}+u})<h_{B}(e^{\varepsilon_{i}+u})
for some
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\in\supp(A)
and some
u\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

\displaystyle A\oplus B\succ\sup\{A\oplus B_{1},A\oplus B_{2}\}.

Proof.

First, it is clear that
A\oplus B\succeq\sup\{A\oplus B_{1},A\oplus B_{2}\}
, so we just need to find a single gap.

To that end, by continuity, we can find a constant
\rho>0
s.t
\inf_{\varepsilon\in(\varepsilon_{i}+u\pm\rho)}h_{B}(e^{\varepsilon})-h_{B_{i}}(e^{\varepsilon})>0
. Now, set
\varepsilon^{\star}:=\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+u
and notice that

\displaystyle h_{A\oplus B}(e^{\varepsilon^{\star}})-h_{A\oplus B_{i}}(e^{\varepsilon^{\star}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim A\oplus B}[(1-e^{\varepsilon^{\star}-Z})_{+}]-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim A\oplus B_{i}}[(1-e^{\varepsilon^{\star}-Z})_{+}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z_{1}\sim A}[\mathbb{E}_{Z_{2}\sim B}[(1-e^{(\varepsilon^{\star}-Z_{1})-Z_{2}})_{+}]-\mathbb{E}_{Z_{2}\sim B_{i}}[(1-e^{(\varepsilon^{\star}-Z_{1})-Z_{2}})_{+}]]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim A}[h_{B}(e^{\varepsilon^{\star}-Z})-h_{B_{i}}(e^{\varepsilon^{\star}-Z})]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{Z\sim A}(Z\in(\varepsilon_{2-i}\pm\rho))\cdot\inf_{\varepsilon\in(\varepsilon_{2-i}\pm\rho)}h_{B}(e^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+u-\varepsilon})-h_{B_{i}}(e^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+u-\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{Z\sim A}(Z\in(\varepsilon_{2-i}\pm\rho))\cdot\inf_{\varepsilon\in(\varepsilon_{i}+u\pm\rho)}h_{B}(e^{\varepsilon})-h_{B_{i}}(e^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle>0.

In particular,

h_{\sup\{A\oplus B_{1},A\oplus B_{2}\}}(e^{\varepsilon^{\star}})=\max\{h_{A\oplus B_{1}}(e^{\varepsilon^{\star}}),h_{A\oplus B_{2}}(e^{\varepsilon^{\star}})\}<h_{A\oplus B}(e^{\varepsilon^{\star}})
.
∎

Proposition 15

Critically, non-trivial closed and divisible subsets of

Now, let

\displaystyle T\supseteq\overline{\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}}T}=\overline{\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigcup_{t\in T}\{a_{n}^{k}t:n\in\mathbb{N}\}}\supseteq\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigcup_{t\in T}\overline{\{a_{n}^{k}t:n\in\mathbb{N}\}}\supseteq\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigcup_{t\in T}\{a^{k}t\}=a^{\mathbb{Z}}T.

We also require a slight variation on the usual characterization of convexity: a concave curve that passes above and then below a convex curve must remain below it.

Lemma 3
.

Let
f,g:(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}
so that
f
is convex and
g
is concave. Then, if
f(x_{1})\geq g(x_{1})
and

Suppose we could find

. Then, by convexity of

\displaystyle f(x_{1})\leq\frac{x_{2}-x_{1}}{x_{2}-x}\underbrace{f(x)}_{<g(x)}+\frac{x_{1}-x}{x_{2}-x}\underbrace{f(x_{2})}_{\leq g(x_{2})}<\frac{x_{2}-x_{1}}{x-x_{2}}g(x)+\frac{x_{1}-x}{x-x_{2}}g(x_{2})\leq g(x_{1}),

which is a contradiction.

,
\supp(h_{1})
,
\supp(h_{2})
and second, we derive a recurrence for the values of

and

at the points where they meet. By analyzing the characteristic polynomial of this recurrence, we can show that

and

Now, our first major goal is to argue that

can be expressed of the form

for some

and

and

for some

and
s_{2}\in(b_{2},ab_{1})

To this end, we first argue that

lives in both
\overline{X(h_{1},h_{2})}
and
\overline{X(h_{2},h_{1})}

. In this case,

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\in\partial E(h_{1},h_{2})

\displaystyle=\partial(X(h_{1},h_{2})\cup X(h_{2},h_{1}))^{C}

\displaystyle=\partial(X(h_{1},h_{2})\cup X(h_{2},h_{1}))

\displaystyle\subseteq\partial X(h_{1},h_{2})\cup\partial X(h_{2},h_{1})

\displaystyle\subseteq C(h_{1},h_{2}),

so, by construction of
C(h_{1},h_{2})
, there is some punctured neighbourhood
(xr^{-1},xr)\setminus\{x\}
of

lying outside of

, which would contradict

\textrm{int}(E(h_{1},h_{2}))
has empty boundary, so it must be both closed and open in
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
. But
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
cross, so we cannot have
\textrm{int}(E(h_{1},h_{2}))=\mathbb{R}^{\times}
. By connectivity of
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
, the only possibility is that
\textrm{int}(E(h_{1},h_{2}))=\varnothing
. In particular, by construction of the simple crossing points, we have

\displaystyle E(h_{1},h_{2})=\partial E(h_{1},h_{2})\subseteq C(h_{1},h_{2})\subseteq\partial X(h_{1},h_{2})\cap\partial X(h_{2},h_{1})\subseteq\overline{X(h_{1},h_{2})}\cap\overline{X(h_{2},h_{1})}.

. First, notice that
\supp(h_{i})
divides
E(h_{1},h_{2})

\supp(h_{i})
must link

with

\supp(h_{i})
links

with
X(h_{2},h_{1})

\supp(h_{i})
divides

and

and such that the elements of
\supp(h_{1})
and of
\supp(h_{2})
are multiplicatively separated by at least a factor of

contains no more than two points. Indeed, suppose we could find

,

or of

. By Proposition

(b_{1},ab_{1})
contains at least two points from

or from

, which is impossible because these points were multiplicatively separated by

|B|\leq 2
. On the other hand, if

, then we have

, so in all cases we can write

for some

.

Finally, we determine the structure of
\supp(h_{1})
and
\supp(h_{2})
. For

and

,

or

as well.

h_{1}
and
h_{2}
over the crossing points. To this end, consider

\displaystyle y^{\prime}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=h_{2}(a^{k+1}b_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{k+1}b_{1}-a^{k+1}b_{2}}{a^{k+1}b_{1}-a^{k}s_{2}}h_{1}(a^{k}s_{2})+\frac{a^{k+1}b_{2}-a^{k}s_{2}}{a^{k+1}b_{1}-a^{k}s_{2}}h_{2}(a^{k+1}b_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{a(b_{1}-b_{2})}{ab_{1}-s_{2}}\left(\frac{b_{2}-s_{2}}{b_{2}-b_{1}}y_{k}+\frac{s_{2}-b_{1}}{b_{2}-b_{1}}y^{\prime}_{k}\right)+\frac{ab_{2}-s_{2}}{ab_{1}-s_{2}}y_{k+1}

\displaystyle y_{k+2}=\underbrace{\frac{a(s_{1}-b_{1})}{b_{2}-s_{1}}}_{=:U}y^{\prime}_{k}+\underbrace{-\frac{as_{1}-b_{2}}{b_{2}-s_{1}}}_{=:V}y_{k+1}+\underbrace{\frac{ab_{1}-s_{1}}{b_{2}-s_{1}}}_{=:W}y^{\prime}_{k+1}

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} y^{\prime}_{k+1}&=U^{\prime}y_{k}+V^{\prime}y^{\prime}_{k}+W^{\prime}y_{k+1}\\
y_{k+2}&=Uy^{\prime}_{k}+Vy_{k+1}+Wy^{\prime}_{k+1}\\
y^{\prime}_{k+2}&=U^{\prime}y_{k+1}+V^{\prime}y^{\prime}_{k+1}+W^{\prime}y_{k+2}\\
y_{k+3}&=Uy^{\prime}_{k+1}+Vy_{k+2}+Wy^{\prime}_{k+2}\end{aligned}\right.

for

. By isolating

in terms of
y_{k},y_{k+1},y_{k+2}
from the first two equations, substituting into the equation for
y^{\prime}_{k+2}
, and finally substituting this into the equation for

, we obtain the symmetrized recurrence

\displaystyle y_{k+3}=UU^{\prime}y_{k}+(UW^{\prime}+U^{\prime}W-VV^{\prime})y_{k+1}+(V+V^{\prime}+WW^{\prime})y_{k+2}

for

satisfies a linear homogeneous recurrence with characteristic polynomial

\displaystyle f(t)=t^{3}-(V+V^{\prime}+WW^{\prime})t^{2}-(UW^{\prime}+U^{\prime}W-VV^{\prime})t-UU^{\prime},

which after applying some computational effort factors as

\displaystyle f(t)=(t-1)(t-a)\left(t-a\left(\frac{s_{1}-b_{1}}{b_{2}-s_{1}}\right)\left(\frac{s_{2}-b_{2}}{ab_{1}-s_{2}}\right)\right).

lies in
\mathbb{R}^{\times}
. On the other hand, a homogeneous linear recurrence over the integers admits a bounded non-constant solution if and only its characteristic polynomial has a non-unit root lying on the complex unit circle (see e.g. Section 2.3 of
[
24
]
). Therefore either
y_{k}=h_{1}(a^{k}b_{1})=h_{2}(a^{k}b_{1})
is unbounded, which is impossible because hockey-stick curves are bounded, or it must be constant, which is also impossible because hockey-stick curves are monotone, so this would force
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
to be constant and thus well-ordered.
∎

\alpha_{i}^{\star}\ \textrm{s.t.}\ \alpha_{i}^{\star}=g_{i}(\alpha_{i}^{\star})
(otherwise they would never cross).
This means that their linear segments cross: one function starts strictly lower, and then ends-up strictly higher by
\min\{\alpha_{1}^{\star},\alpha_{2}^{\star}\}
(again, otherwise they would never cross). Without loss of generality, we reorder functions so that
g_{2}
starts strictly lower.

We now know that
\delta_{2}>\delta_{1}\geq 0
(
g_{2}
starts lower);
\varepsilon_{1}>\varepsilon_{2}\geq 0
(
g_{1}
has a steeper slope to catch-up to
g_{2}
so that they can cross); and
\alpha_{2}^{\star}>\alpha_{1}^{\star}
. The last inequality holds because
g_{1}
is strictly lower by the time is crosses
y=x
at
\alpha_{1}^{\star}
, so at that point
g_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{\star})>\alpha_{1}^{\star}
, and by continuity of
g_{2}
we have that
\alpha_{2}^{\star}>\alpha_{1}^{\star}
.
Since we are dealing with approximate DP curves, we also know that
\alpha_{i}^{\star}=\frac{1-\delta_{i}}{1+e^{\varepsilon_{i}}}
.

Now, in
Theorem

2
, pick
\mathcal{H}=\{g_{1},g_{2}\}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
and
g=g_{1}\in\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
.
We make a few useful observations about the first segment of
g_{1}\otimes g_{1}
,
g_{1}\otimes g_{2}
, and
g_{1}\otimes\operatorname{conv}(g_{1},g_{2})
, by applying

:

The first segment of
g_{1}\otimes g_{1}
starts at
(1-\delta_{1})^{2}
, with slope
-e^{2\varepsilon_{1}}
for
\alpha\in[0,\alpha_{1}^{\star 2}]
. The second slope is
-e^{\varepsilon_{1}}e^{-\varepsilon_{1}}=-1
.

The first segment of
g_{1}\otimes g_{2}
starts at
(1-\delta_{1})(1-\delta_{2})
, with slope
-e^{\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}}
for

-e^{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}}<-1

The first segment of
\operatorname{conv}(g_{1},g_{2})
starts at
(1-\delta_{2})
(the lowest point at
\alpha=0

g_{1}
and
g_{2}

The first segment of

(1-\delta_{1})(1-\delta_{2})
, with first slope
\frac{\delta_{1}-\delta_{2}}{\alpha_{1}^{\star}}e^{\varepsilon_{1}}-e^{2\varepsilon_{1}}

Let us study the first segment of
\operatorname{conv}(g_{1}\otimes g_{1},g_{1}\otimes g_{2})
and compare it to that of
g_{1}\otimes\operatorname{conv}(g_{1},g_{2})

starts lower. Since

As a result, either that first slope is steep enough such that

Case 1:

2

f
