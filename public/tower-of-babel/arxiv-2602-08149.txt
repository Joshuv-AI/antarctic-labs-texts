Dial-SummEr : A Structured Evaluation Framework of Hierarchical Errors in Dialogue Summaries
Sahana Ramnath, Nima Chitsazan, Mingyang Zhou, Chia-Hsuan Lee, Shi-Xiong Zhang, Stephen Rawls, Sambit Sahu, Sangwoo Cho, Xiang Ren, Genta Indra Winata, Akshaj Kumar Veldanda

Abstract
Dialogues are a predominant mode of communication for humans, and it is immensely helpful to have automatically generated summaries of them (e.g., to revise key points discussed in a meeting, to review conversations between customer agents and product users). Prior works on dialogue summary evaluation largely ignore the complexities specific to this task: (i) shift in structure , from multiple speakers discussing information in a scattered fashion across several turns, to a summary’s sentences, and (ii) shift in narration viewpoint , from speakers’ first/second-person narration, standardized third-person narration in the summary. In this work, we introduce our framework Dial-SummEr to address the above. We propose Dial-SummEr ’s taxonomy of errors to comprehensively evaluate dialogue summaries at two hierarchical levels: Dialogue-Level that focuses on the broader speakers/turns, and Within-Turn-Level that focuses on the information talked about inside a turn. We then present Dial-SummEr ’s dataset composed of dialogue summaries manually annotated with our taxonomy’s fine-grained errors. We conduct empirical analyses of these annotated errors, and observe interesting trends (e.g., turns occurring in middle of the dialogue are the most frequently missed in the summary, extrinsic hallucinations largely occur at the end of the summary). We also conduct experiments on LLM-Judges’ capability at detecting these errors, through which we demonstrate the challenging nature of our dataset, the robustness of our taxonomy, and the need for future work in this field to enhance LLMs’ performance in the same. There exists a large body of prior work on summarization evaluation:

For example, previous works do not take into account the fact that dialogues have a unique hierarchical structure (at the speaker-turn level, and at the within-a-turn level); they also do not acknowledge the errors that could occur when the narration perspective switches from the first/second to third person. Further, some taxonomies also suffer from errors that have overlapping or unclear definitions: for example, in

Related Work

Summarization Evaluation.
SummEval
7
identified core relevant properties that a summary has to satisfy (coherence, consistency, fluency, relevance) and released corresponding human annotations on a subset of CNN/DailyMail summaries
30
; these human annotations could be used to calculate the correlation with evaluation scores by popular summarization metrics such as ROUGE
22
, BERTScore
42
, etc. in order to identify the best evaluation scoring strategies. Eventually, works such as
18
;
34
;
27
proposed further fine-grained categories to measure the quality of model-generated summaries;
41
performs summarization evaluation with LLMs taking on multiple static and dynamic evaluation roles.
35
;
31
take this one step further to use LLM feedback with respect to these fine-grained categories to improve summarization models through reinforcement learning.

Dialogue summarization evaluation.
Following the same steps as in SummEval, DialSummEval
8
releases human annotations for dialogue summaries from SAMSum
9
on the categories of coherence, consistency, fluency and relevance, to test the correlation between human annotations and automatic metrics. Later, TofuEval
38
released human annotations on dialogue summaries from MediaSum
45
and MeetingBank
12
with fine-grained error categories (such as reasoning, entity misreferencing, tense/modality, etc. errors) that could be used to analyze LLM-Judges; recently,
14
released synthetic hallucinated general/topic-specific summaries of customer/travel agent call transcripts and annotations on fine-grained error categories (factual consistency, adjective usage, knowledge congruence, style alignment) for the same which can be used to test LLM-Judges.
In our work
, we develop fine-grained error categories to prioritize explainability and informativity of evaluation. We propose a hierarchical error taxonomy to mirror the structure of dialogues, and we include speaker/turn-based errors, to fill the gap left by existing works on the same. We discuss in Appendix
A
how prior taxonomies face issues of unclear definitions of errors; we address these issues by adding macro-level errors in our taxonomy and ensuring that all of them are clearly defined and mutually exclusive.

In our framework
Dial-SummEr
, we propose a novel
taxonomy
designed to categorize errors in dialogue summaries across two hierarchical levels:
Dialogue-Level
captures macro-level inconsistencies related to the structural integrity of the conversation and the turns.
Within-Turn-Level
addresses the micro-level semantic discrepancies focusing on the content within a specific utterance.
The errors are defined below (visualized in Table
4
, Appendix
B
).

3.1
Descriptions of Errors

Wrong Turn Sequence
:
The dialogue’s turns are presented in the wrong sequence in the summary.

Missed Turn
:
The summary has missed to mention one or more conversational turns.

Speaker Misattribution
:
The speakers are swapped/misreferenced in the summary.

Speaker Identity Bias
:
The summary makes assumptions about a speaker’s identity (gender/race/etc.,)

Viewpoint Distortion
:
The summary presents what was
said
in the conversation as a true fact.
Further explanation:
The summary is ideally expected to have each sentence narrated (in third person) from the perspective of the
speakers
; this is to ensure that if the speakers said something inaccurate or false (for example, wrong world-knowledge, or false information about themselves or their actions), the summary does not propagate the error. A summary is hence said to have a
Viewpoint Distortion
error if any of its sentences are written fully or partially from the point of view of a third-person narrator who is
not
a speaker, who assumes that everything said by the speakers is correct (for example, world-knowledge) or did actually happen (for example, the action the speaker said they did or are doing or will do).

Wrong Linking
:
The summary presents information present in the conversation but links the wrong items together.

Changed Meaning
:
The summary has changed a word or a phrase from the conversation in a way that
changes the meaning
of what the speakers said. This error includes:
exaggeration
of words/events by the summary,
change in sentence type
from the dialogue to the summary (e.g., question in the dialogue written as a statement in the summary),
semantic misinterpretation
of ambiguous or idiomatic phrases or homonyms, as well as
any other changed conversation
from the dialogue to the summary.

Extrinsic Conversation
:
The summary mentions that a speaker said something which was not said in the source conversation.

Extrinsic Context
:
The summary contains external explanations for something discussed in the conversation (for example the definition for an entity, the reason why a speaker said something, correcting what a speaker said using world knowledge, etc.).

Missed Conversation
:
The summary has missed
any
information mentioned in the dialogue; this error is
extremely harsh
.

We present an example annotation of a dialogue summary using
Dial-SummEr
in Table
1
. All
Dialogue-Level
errors are novel categories proposed in this work;
Within-Turn-Level
categories are adapted and extended from prior works such as
38
;
35
. Further, in accordance with prior literature, we classify these errors (except
Viewpoint Distortion
) into two broad categories:
hallucination
and
incompleteness
.
Missed Turn
and
Missed Conversation
are incompleteness errors; every other error (apart from
Viewpoint Distortion
) is a hallucination error. We exclude
Viewpoint Distortion
from this classification since
Viewpoint Distortion
is a subjective error; it is acceptable in low-stakes cases, but becomes an issue in high-stakes cases (such as, when the dialogue is between a client and a bank’s customer agent).

Note on incompleteness based errors:
We show two incompleteness errors, one on each level of information. While hallucination errors are definitive in terms of right and wrong, incompleteness errors are present in a gray area. A harsh evaluation is to expect every single detail in the conversation to be present in the summary. A less harsh but
vague
strategy to check whether the summary includes all “necessary” details; however, the question of what is necessary to include in a summary is entirely user-dependent (or in the case of data annotation, annotator-dependent). In our work, we present two levels of incompleteness:
Missed Turn
and
Missed Conversation
. The former error checks for incompleteness on a broad level, checking whether every single turn in the conversation (excluding greeting-only turns which
we
deem as trivial) is mentioned in the summary; note that we do not ask for every detail in every turn to be included in the summary, only that the summary has to acknowledge the presence of the turn. The
Missed Conversation
error on the other hand is extremely harsh, and we check for
any detail
in the conversation (again, excluding greetings) that is missing in the summary; this is the harshest possible evaluation of incompleteness. We leave other moderate definitions of incompleteness to future/domain-specific work.

3.2
Addressing the complexities in dialogue summary evaluation

Our taxonomy address the shift in structure from the dialogue to the summary by having hierarchical
Dialogue-Level
and
Within-Turn-Level
errors that mirror the same. Further, it addresses the issue of the shift in narration by having speaker and narration specific errors (
Speaker Misattribution
,
Speaker Identity Bias
,
Viewpoint Distortion
), all of which only occur when the narration switches from the speakers’ first/second perspectives to the summary’s third-person perspective.

Dataset

In this section, we describe the creation of our framework’s dialogue summary evaluation dataset.
We start with the aim of selecting a high-quality,
multi-turn
dialogue dataset that has realistic, detailed (but not overly verbose or technical/niche) conversations; we select
Anthropic-Test

2
, a widely used dataset that contains human-AI conversations on various topics (e.g., casual chat, information-seeking, decision-making). We pick 200 conversations from
Anthropic-Test
that have four or more turns.

1

1

We take the “chosen” assistant response in the dataset.
We generate summaries for them using
Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct
(prompt in Appendix
C
) with greedy decoding: we instruct it to generate summaries that are faithful to the dialogue, concise but still including all necessary details, and presented as a paragraph.
For each conversation + summary pair, for each
Dial-SummEr
error, human annotators are instructed to mark “YES” if the summary exhibits that error, and “NO” if not; When they capture a certain error, they are also instructed to write down the reason for their decision on the detected error type, and mark the specific sentences in the summary (or in the dialogue, for incompleteness) that showed this error.

Annotation:
We recruit a team of in-house, full-time annotators who are experts in generating dialogue summaries, as well as in analyzing errors in the same. We use two annotators per instance for each error (annotation instructions in Appendix
D
); the authors did one round of post-processing to break any ties and to ensure correctness. We remove 8 instances which had low quality dialogues and/or summaries, to get a final of
192
conversation and summary pairs that are completely annotated; we release our final dataset
Dial-SummEr
publicly for future research.

6
Detecting errors using LLM-as-a-Judge

Given a dialogue and its summary, we conduct experiments to observe if open-weight and proprietary LLMs can serve as high-quality judges to detect coarse-grained and fine-grained errors in the summary. We test LLM-Judges on the following two aspects: Can they correctly identify if the summary exhibits a specified error or not? And if so, can they isolate the exact sentences in the summary that exhibit the error?

,
Speaker Misattribution
and
Extrinsic Conversation
. All the judges struggle with the
Changed Meaning
error. We hypothesize that these LLMs have more tolerance for changes in meaning than the annotators (we release a moderately harsh annotation for this error).

Overall,
GPT-5
performs reasonably well at detecting errors, with high performance at the
Missed Turn
and
Viewpoint Distortion
errors, and moderately high performance at the
Extrinsic Context
and
Speaker Misattribution
errors.
Claude-Sonnet-4.5
performs well at detecting
Extrinsic Context
,
Speaker Misattribution
and
Wrong Turn Sequence
errors, with moderate to bad performance in the other errors.
Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct
performs moderately well at detecting
Wrong Turn Sequence
and
Speaker Misattribution
errors, with low to bad performance in the other errors.
Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct
is the worst performing judge out of the four, with almost random performance (
\sim
50-60% balanced accuracy) in most errors. This general trend shows that dialogue summary error detection through prompting is an emergent property (with respect to model size) for the most part. While fine-tuning is a potential solution for the same, it would require a larger amount of high-quality, human-annotated data which we leave to the future work.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we propose
Dial-SummEr
, our framework for dialogue summary evaluation. We propose
a novel error
taxonomy
that mirrors the hierarchical nature of information in dialogues, and contains errors related to hallucination, incompleteness, and narration.
Then, we annotate and release a new
inference dataset
that contains detailed human annotations of fine-grained errors in dialogue summaries, the exact sentences they occurred in, as well as annotator comments on the same. We present empirical analyses on the same, reporting error frequencies as well as interesting trends of where in the summary errors typically occur. We also test open-weight and proprietary LLMs on their capability at detecting these errors, and we find that LLMs largely struggle at this task; adding our taxonomy to the few-shot prompt helps boost performance, but future work such as fine-tuning or reward-based learning are essential to improve LLMs’ error detection capabilities, including further error correction and improved summarization capabilities.

Limitations

In this section, we discuss the limitations of our work.

Taxonomy.
We ignore fluency/grammar related errors in our taxonomy, owing to the high capability of language models today in generating fluent English. However, the same cannot be said for all languages, and we acknowledge this as a language limitation of our work. We also stress that any downstream users of our work must (i) decide the level of incompleteness they want to monitor for, and (ii) decide if
Viewpoint Distortion
is an error for their task or not (for example,
8
does not consider this to be an error, as seen in their summaries).

Dataset.
A primary limitation is the size of our dataset
Dial-SummEr
: we annotate and release only 192 conversation-summary pairs (200 before filtering), owing to the high effort and cost of annotating the summaries with our extensive taxonomy; a secondary limitation is the lack of any summary exhibiting the
Speaker Identity Bias
error (we do not synthetically generate any summaries with this error). Though the total number of conversations are on the lower side, we release a total of 192 dialogue summaries * 10 errors
=
1,920 summary-level error labels, as well as sentence level labels on 835 summary sentences; we hope that our dataset motivates future work in this area.

LLM-Judge experiments.
While we measure balanced accuracy of LLMs at detecting the errors, we do not check the actual reasoning generated by them; evaluation of the reasoning generated by LLMs is highly important in order to trust the LLM-Judges, and we leave this for future work. Further, we do not excessively prompt engineer for our experiments, and we acknowledge that with more prompt engineering, the LLMs could have achieved better performance at detecting errors.

Practical limitations.
We conduct experiments with
GPT-5
and
Claude-Sonnet-4.5
which are API-based, and with
Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct
and
Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct
on our GPU servers. Owing to GPU constraints, the maximum local model size we could experiment with was 70B; we hope to benchmark more open-source models larger than 70B in the future.

Biases in LLMs.
We use models
GPT-5
,
Claude-Sonnet-4.5
,
Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct
,
Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct
and
Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct
in either hallucination detection or summary generation. These are models that have been exposed to large, unknown training datasets; any biases learned by these models could potentially propagate to the summaries or their evaluation of summaries (for example, the summarizer could add in a phrase commenting about the demographic of a speaker, or about a social situation discussed in the conversation, based on perceived clues).
36
discusses social biases (gender, racial) in content selection and hallucination in summarization;
1
investigates age-related bias in summarization of reviews in the clinical domain;
17
discusses name-nationality bias in summarization.
We address social biases in our work by:
(i) we include the
Speaker Identity Bias
error in our taxonomy; though we do not encounter this error in our dataset, we include it for general usage in future works, (ii) while we do not have an exclusive error for social bias for topics discussed within a turn, any social bias generated by the summarizer will be captured by either our
Changed Meaning
or
Extrinsic Conversation
or
Extrinsic Context
errors.

A second kind of bias that can occur is position bias, wherein the summarizer focuses only on certain parts of the source and ignores the rest; this is discussed in works such as
39
;
6
.
We address position bias in our work by
introducing the
Missed Turn
error, which finds out the parts of the source conversation ignored in the summary.

Lastly, egocentric bias (self preference)
20
;
15
is a bias wherein LLM-judges prefer responses that are generated by the same model as the judge itself.
We address egocentric bias in our work by
ensuring that the error evaluation LLMs are different than the summarizer LLM
Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct
; however, one of our LLM-Judges
Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct
is of the same family as the summarizer (could have shared training data as well), results given by this LLM could demonstrate egocentric bias.

Trust and Risk.

As mentioned in
23
, a user’s trust in a language model is highly subjective. Some users use task accuracy as a proxy for trust, some require a transparent mechanistic/post-hoc reasoning process, some analyze patterns of bias/hallucination, etc. In this work, we provide experimental results of LLMs’ accuracy on predicting errors, and also prompt the LLMs to generate their reasoning for their prediction; we do not evaluate this reasoning since it is out of scope for this work (our primary goal is releasing our taxonomy and dataset), but we encourage future research on the same. We also include discussions on biases, and how we tackle them using our taxonomy. We urge any researcher/downstream user who utilize our taxonomy, dataset and prompts to perform manual, qualitative analysis of LLM-generated summaries as well as LLM-Judge predictions to ensure quality and trust.

Appendix A
Related Work Extended

Summarization.
There exists a sea of prior works
30
;
33
;
7
;
19
;
18
which focus on summarization through the lens of news articles; the most popular dataset being CNN/Dailymail (CNN and DailyMail news articles collected over many years
10
, and summaries created by
30
). Dialogue summarization, however has lesser focus, possibly due to its higher difficulty, as well as lesser number of research datasets available. One of the first dialogue summary datasets to be released was SAMSum
9
; they employed linguists and language experts to write realistic dialogues and also write down summaries for the same. These dialogues and summaries could be used to train and test language models on the task of dialogue summarization;
9
originally provided results with ROUGE
22
. SAMSum was also pivotal in establishing the challenging nature of dialogue summarization and the need for better evaluation metrics for the same (they found that metrics like ROUGE are more suitable to analyze news article summarizations, and not that suitable for dialogue summarization). SAMSum, as well as later dialogue summarization works such as
38
discuss the unique and challenging nature of dialogue summarization such as informal and colloquial nature of dialogues (including the use of slang language), the presence of more noise such as misspellings/wrong grammar, variety of linguistic structures in the dialogue (such as questions, greetings, discussions), the scattered presence of information across multiple turns, and the presence of varying points of view due to the different speakers.

Hallucination evaluation.
There are a large number of works in contemporary NLP literature that focus on developing LLM (or other) evaluators to detect hallucination. This includes
21
;
25
;
5
;
24
;
11
;
28
;
40
which focus on hallucination of LLMs when performing a variety of NLP tasks such as QA, summarization, claim verification etc.,
4
which focuses on hallucinations and hallucination snowballing within a dialogue itself, and
38
;
8
;
7
;
18
;
31
;
27
that focus specifically on dialogue summarization. These works introduce hallucination taxonomies that are of varying degrees of granularity: (a) binary: is there hallucination or not?, (b) coarse-grained: faithfulness, completeness, conciseness, and (c) fine-grained: specific fine-grained categories under faithfulness such as entity error, relation error, hyperbole, out of article error, etc.

Prior evaluation methods versus ours (extended).
Overall, prior works on dialogue summarization evaluation range from reference-based metrics (such as ROUGE),
to binary hallucination evaluation,
to slightly fine-grained evaluation (coherence, consistency, etc. as in
8
), to extremely fine-grained evaluation as in
38
;
34
; we opt to go with the last category of evaluation since it is the most informative and explainable. Prior fine-grained error taxonomies however are largely unstandardized, leading to difficulty in comparing summarization models and datasets across different papers; we compile errors proposed in several papers and ensure that all of them are included in our taxonomy’s error categories. Further, prior taxonomies also face the problem of incompleteness of taxonomy (no prior work includes speaker/turn-based errors), and unclear definitions of some errors: For example, in
38
“contradiction” error is largely unclear; a summary sentence which contains any other error in the taxonomy (such as an entity error or a tense error) can also be considered to have a contradiction error (as mentioned in Section
). Similarly in
31
, both the predicate error and the circumstance error fall under the broader entity error which is also a category in the taxonomy. We address these issues by adding macro-level errors in our taxonomy, and further ensuring that all errors are clearly defined and mutually exclusive.

(2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P_{s}}=\{\,s_{i}\mid p_{i}=\text{YES}\,\}

(3)

5

sklearn.metrics.balanced
\_
accuracy

score
