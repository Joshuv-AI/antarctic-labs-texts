On the Reachability Problem for One-Dimensional Thin Grammar Vector Addition Systems
Chengfeng Xue, Yuxi Fu

Abstract
. Vector addition systems with states (VASS) are a classic model in concurrency theory. Grammar vector addition systems (GVAS), equivalently, pushdown VASS, extend VASS by using a context-free grammar to control addition. 1.
Introduction

Vector addition systems with states (VASS) are a fundamental model in concurrency theory. The most widely studied problem is the reachability problem, addressing the issue of whether an initial configuration can reach a target configuration via a nonnegative run.

The reachability problem for VASS was recently shown to be Ackermann-complete
(
9
;
6
;
7
;
4
;
16
)
. For VASS parameterized by dimension
d
(
d
-VASS), the reachability problem is in
\mathbf{F}_{d}

(
9
)
, where
\mathbf{F}_{d}
denotes the fast-growing hierarchy defined in
(
22
)
. The reachability problem is solved using the
KLM algorithm
, which was first introduced in the 1980s
(
12
;
13
;
19
)
and continuously refined over decades
(
9
;
16
;
15
)
. Regarding the lower bound, Czerwiński et al. proved the
\mathbf{F}_{d}
hardness for
(2d+3)
-dimensional VASS through iterative refinements
(
7
;
4
)
. Although
PSPACE
-completeness
(
23
)
has been established for 2-VASS, and 2-
EXPSPACE
upper bound
(
5
)
has been obtained for 3-VASS, the gap between the upper and bounds remains huge for higher-dimensional VASS reachability problems.

Many variants of VASS have been investigated.
Grammar vector addition systems (GVAS) are vector addition systems controlled by context-free grammars (CFGs). A
d
-dimensional GVAS (
d
-GVAS) is a CFG whose terminals are vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Pushdown VASS (PVASS) extends VASS with a pushdown stack. By the classical equivalence between pushdown automata and CFGs, GVAS and PVASS are equivalent. The coverability, boundedness and reachability problems of GVAS have been studied. Leroux, Sutre and Totzke established an
EXPTIME
upper bound for the boundedness problem of 1-GVAS
(
17
)
, and an
EXPSPACE
upper bound for the coverability of 1-GVAS
(
18
)
. Bizière and Czerwiński showed the decidability of the reachability of 1-GVAS
(
2
)
, and later, Guttenberg, Keskin and Meyer proved the decidability of
d
-GVAS reachability for arbitrary dimensions
(
11
)
.

There is a clear dividing line between the hardness of different GVASes, known as
thinness
. A GVAS is
thin
, if no nonterminal can produce more than one copy of itself, i.e., there exists no derivation of the form
X\Rightarrow\alpha X\beta X\gamma
. Thinness is closely related to the
index
, introduced by Atig and Ganty
(
1
)
. A GVAS is
k
-indexed, if in every derivation, there exists a derivation in which at most
k
nonterminals appear simultaneously. A GVAS is thin if and only if it is finitely indexed. The significance of thinness lies in the fact that many studies reduce general GVAS to thin GVAS
(
2
;
18
)
.
Atig and Ganty also proved the decidability of the reachability of thin GVAS
(
1
)
.

The best-known upper bound for thin GVAS is derived from
(
10
)
. Guttenberg, Czerwiński and Lasota generalized the edge relations of VASS from vector additions to reachability relations, and introduced a universal algorithm to approximate the reachability sets of such extended VASS. As a consequence, the reachability problem for thin
d
-GVAS indexed by
k
is in
\mathbf{F}_{4kd+2k-4d}
via reduction to their extended model. For thin 1-GVAS, the upper bound is
\mathbf{F}_{6k-4}
. Furthermore, the reduction from 1-GVAS to thin 1-GVAS proposed in
(
2
)
implies that the reachability problem for general 1-GVAS is Ackermannian. Whether there exists a better upper bound for thin 1-GVAS (and consequently for 1-GVAS), or even an elementary one, remains open.

Compared to VASS, the reachability problem for GVAS presents several significant challenges. Firstly, it is a folklore that the reachability problem for
d
-GVAS can be reduced to the coverability problem for
(d+1)
-GVAS. When investigating GVAS reachability, simple properties of coverability, such as the existence of small coverability certificates for
d\geq 2
, are not available. Secondly, the finite reachability set of
d
-GVAS is hyper-Ackermannian
(
14
)
. An Ackermannian reachability set can already be constructed for thin 1-GVAS. Hence, any method relying on brute-force enumeration of reachability certificates (e.g., the KLM algorithm) cannot overcome the Ackermannian barrier, even for thin 1-GVAS.

Our contribution.
In the present paper, we study the reachability problem for thin 1-GVAS. Our main contribution is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1.

The reachability problem for thin 1-GVAS indexed by
k
is in
\mathbf{F}_{2k}
.

Since
k\geq 2
for all nontrivial GVAS, the
\mathbf{F}_{2k}
upper bound improves upon the previous
\mathbf{F}_{6k-4}
result. To achieve this, we generalize the KLM decomposition for VASS to thin GVAS. The core idea of the KLM decomposition is as follows. A nonnegative run (
\mathbb{N}
-run) of VASS can be divided into many segments, each of which forms a strongly-connected component. For each such component, an integer programming system–the characteristic system–is constructed to describe the
\mathbb{Z}
-run, where non-negativity is not ensured.
However, as long as the characteristic system satisfies a certain condition, called perfectness, one can construct an
\mathbb{N}
-run from a
\mathbb{Z}
-run. To ensure perfectness, decompositions are performed on components iteratively. The termination of decomposition is guaranteed using a suitable
ranking function
.

We apply the above approach to thin GVAS by dividing the derivation tree into exponentially many “strongly-connected” segments. To capture the derivation behavior of these segments, we introduce the
KLM tree
, which can be applied to thin GVAS of arbitrary dimension. Furthermore, we define a ranking function that accounts for both the dimension and the index. In the light of the difficulty of the coverability problem and the pumping techniques for GVAS, we further restrict our attention to the one-dimensional case in decomposition step. We employ graph-theoretic techniques together with coverability results for thin 1-GVAS, and finally bound the length of the decomposition sequence by
\mathbf{F}_{2k}
, which yields our conclusion.

3.
Grammar Vector Addition Systems

A
d
-dimensional grammar vector addition system
(
d
-GVAS) is a context-free grammar where all terminals are integer vectors. It is defined as the tuple:

\mathcal{G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}(\mathcal{X},\Sigma,\mathcal{P},S_{0}),

where
\mathcal{X}
is a finite set of nonterminals,
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is a finite set of terminal vectors,
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times(\Sigma\cup\mathcal{X}^{2})
is a finite set of production rules in Chomsky normal form, and
S_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
is the starting symbol.
The start symbol
S_{0}
may may appear on the right side of a production rule.

We use uppercase letters
A,B,X,Y,\dots
to denote nonterminals in
\mathcal{X}
. The distinction between nonterminals and sets is clear from the context. Terminal vectors in
\Sigma
are denoted as
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\dots
. production rules in
\mathcal{P}
are written as
X\rightarrow AB
or
X\rightarrow\mathbf{u}
. A derivation
X\Rightarrow\alpha
is obtained through application of production rules where
\alpha\in\{\mathcal{X}\cup\Sigma\}^{*}
is a string of terminals and nonterminals. A derivation is
complete
if
\alpha\in\Sigma^{*}
.

A GVAS is
proper
if every symbol can be derived from
S_{0}
, and every nonterminal has a complete derivation. For the rest of the paper, we restrict our attention to the proper GVAS. We will investigate subsets of
\mathcal{G}
. For
X\in\mathcal{X}
, let
\mathcal{G}_{X}=(\mathcal{X}^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime},\mathcal{P}^{\prime},X)
be the proper sub-GVAS induced by
X
.
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
collect all the production rules that can be derived from
X
, and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime}
are obtained accordingly.

The size of GVAS.
Given
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{X},\Sigma,\mathcal{P},S_{0})
, let
|\mathcal{G}|
be the total number of symbols and rules. Let
\left\|{\mathcal{G}}\right\|=\mathrm{max}_{\mathbf{u}\in\Sigma}\left\|{\mathbf{u}}\right\|_{1}
be the largest
l_{1}
-norm of terminals. We define:

\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{G})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}|\mathcal{G}|{\cdot}\left\|{\mathcal{G}}\right\|.

3.1.
Thinness and Index

In
(
2
)
, Bizière et al. distinguish GVAS by whether it is thin or branching. A GVAS is
thin
if no nonterminal derives more than one copy of itself (i.e., there exists no derivation like
X\Rightarrow\alpha X\beta X\gamma
). The
index
of CFG (similarly, GVAS) is introduced in
(
1
)
. For a GVAS and a complete derivation
S_{0}\Rightarrow\alpha
, reordering the application of production rules gives different derivation sequences, like
S_{0}\rightarrow\alpha_{1}\rightarrow\alpha_{2}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow\alpha
. A derivation sequence is
optimal
, if it minimizes
\max\{\#_{\mathrm{NT}}(\alpha_{i})\}
, denoted by
k_{S_{0}\Rightarrow\alpha}
, where
\#_{\mathrm{NT}}(\alpha_{i})
stands for number of nonterminals occurring in
\alpha_{i}
. The index of
\mathcal{G}
is denoted by
\iota(\mathcal{G})
, given by:

\iota(\mathcal{G})=\sup\left\{k_{S_{0}\Rightarrow\alpha}\mid S_{0}\Rightarrow\alpha\text{ is optimal}\right\}.

Bizière et al. point out that every thin GVAS has a finite index
(
2
)
. In fact, a GVAS is thin iff it is finitely indexed. We are going to define thinness and the index in a graph-theoretic perspective. The equivalence of different definitions is proved in Appendix
A
.

The production graph.
The production graph
\widetilde{G}(V,E)
of
\mathcal{G}
is a directed graph with
V=\mathcal{X}\cup\Sigma
. Every production rule of
X\rightarrow AB
is mapped to a pair of edges
(X,A),(X,B)\in E
. Every rule of
X\rightarrow\mathbf{u}
is mapped to the edge
(X,\mathbf{u})\in E
. Figure
1
illustrates the production graph. Consider the strongly-connected components (SCCs) in
\widetilde{G}
. For the production rule
X\rightarrow AB
, one of the following holds:

(1)

X,A,B
are in the same SCC. The rule is called
nondegenerate
.

(2)

Exactly one of
A,B
is in the SCC of
X
. The rule is
partially degenerate
. If
B
shares the same SCC with
X
, the rule is
left-degenerate
; it is
right-degenerate
otherwise.

(3)

Neither
A
nor
B
is in the SCC of
X
. The rule is
fully degenerate
.

If there exists any nondegenerate rule, every nonterminal in that SCC is capable of deriving two copies of itself, which violates the thinness. The production graph in Figure
1
contains no nondegenerate rules.

Definition 3.1 (Thinness).

A GVAS
\mathcal{G}
is thin if
\mathcal{P}
contains no nondegenerate production rules.

We abbreviate thin GVAS as TGVAS, and focus on TGVAS in the rest of the paper. For TGVAS
\mathcal{G}
, we have two observations about the index based on
\widetilde{G}
:

(1)

For nonterminals
X,Y
in the same SCC, the indices of their induced sub-GVASes are equal, since
X
derives
Y
and
Y
derives
X
. That is,
\iota(\mathcal{G}_{X})=\iota(\mathcal{G}_{Y})
. For instance, there are three nontrivial SCCs
\{X,Y,Z\},\{C,D\},\{E,F\}
in Figure
1
. Nonterminals in the same SCC share the same index.

(2)

The index
\iota(\mathcal{G})
, as well as the index
\iota(\mathcal{G}_{X})
for any
X\in\mathcal{X}
, is finite and computable through dynamic programming. We compute indices from lower SCCs up to the topmost SCC. The crux is that for
X\rightarrow AB
, we always expand first the nonterminal in a lower SCC, or with a smaller index. For example, in Figure
1
, given the right-degenerate rule
Z\rightarrow XF
with
\iota(F)=2
, it is immediate that
\iota(Z)\geq 3
since one more nonterminal is required for symbol
X
.

Denote the set of nonterminals in the topmost SCC of
\widetilde{G}
as
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{top}}(\widetilde{G})
. We define the index recursively (see Figure
1
):

Definition 3.2 (Index).

Given a TGVAS
\mathcal{G}
. If
\mathcal{G}
is trivial,
\iota(\mathcal{G})=1
. Otherwise,
\iota(\mathcal{G})
is the smallest
k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
rendering true the following statements for all
X\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{top}}(\widetilde{G})
.

•

If there exists a left- (or right-) degenerate rule
X\rightarrow AY
(or
X\rightarrow YA
), then
k\geq\iota(\mathcal{G}_{A})+1
.

•

If there exists a fully-degenerate rule
X\rightarrow AB
and
\iota(\mathcal{G}_{A})\neq\iota(\mathcal{G}_{B})
, then
k\geq\mathrm{max}\{\iota(\mathcal{G}_{A}),\iota(\mathcal{G}_{B})\}
. If
\iota(\mathcal{G}_{A})=\iota(\mathcal{G}_{B})
, then
k\geq\iota(\mathcal{G}_{A})+1
.

The rule set
\mathcal{P}
is:

X\rightarrow AY

A\rightarrow\mathbf{u}_{A}

Y\rightarrow BZ

B\rightarrow\mathbf{u}_{B}

Z\rightarrow XF

B\rightarrow\mathbf{u}_{B}^{\prime}

Z\rightarrow CD

P\rightarrow\mathbf{u}_{P}

C\rightarrow PD

Q\rightarrow\mathbf{u}_{Q}

C\rightarrow PQ

S\rightarrow\mathbf{u}_{S}

D\rightarrow CQ

E\rightarrow\mathbf{u}_{E}

F\rightarrow SE

T\rightarrow\mathbf{u}_{T}

E\rightarrow FT

X\rightarrow\mathbf{u}_{X}

3.2.
Derivation Trees

A derivation tree
\mathcal{T}
is a binary tree representing a derivation. Nodes in
\mathcal{T}
are denoted by lowercase letters
p,q,x,y,n,\dots
. Each node
p\in\mathcal{T}
is labeled with a symbol
\sigma(p)\in\mathcal{X}\cup\Sigma
. For every internal (non-leaf) node
p
, we have
\sigma(p)\in\mathcal{X}
, and one of the following cases applies:

(1)

p
has two children
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathcal{T}
and
\sigma(p)\rightarrow\sigma(c_{1})\sigma(c_{2})\in\mathcal{P}
. For simplicity, we refer to such situation as “
p
has the production rule
\sigma(p)\rightarrow\sigma(c_{1})\sigma(c_{2})
”.

(2)

Or,
p
has the rule
\sigma(p)\rightarrow\sigma(c_{1})
where
\sigma(c_{1})\in\Sigma
and
c_{1}
is a leaf of
\mathcal{T}
.

The symbols of leaves in
\mathcal{T}
are not necessarily terminals. If they are all terminals, then
\mathcal{T}
is
complete
. Otherwise,
\mathcal{T}
is
incomplete
. We write
\mathcal{T}_{X}
to emphasize the root symbol being
X
. Given a derivation tree
\mathcal{T}
, let
\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{T}},\Sigma_{\mathcal{T}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{T}}
denote the set of nonterminals, terminals and rules occurring in
\mathcal{T}
. The subscript notation is extended to any reasonable sets or structures. The sum of all terminals in
\mathcal{T}
is the
displacement
of
\mathcal{T}
. Given two nodes
p,q\in\mathcal{T}
. If
p
is an ancestor of
q
, we denote
p\prec q
. The subtree rooted by node
p
is
\mathrm{sub}{(p)}
.

Segments and cycles.
A
segment
is an incomplete derivation tree with exactly one nonterminal leaf. Let the root be
p
and the nonterminal leaf be
q
. The segment is denoted by
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
. Given a complete derivation tree
\mathcal{T}
and
p,q\in\mathcal{T}
with
p\prec q
, the structure
(\mathrm{sub}{(p)}\setminus\mathrm{sub}{(q)})\cup\{q\}
is
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
. If
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
satisfies
\sigma(p)=\sigma(q)
, it is a
cycle
, denoted by
\mathrm{cyc}{(p,q)}
. A cycle is
simple
, if it contains no strict subcycles. Intuitively, a cycle is the composition of simple cycles.

Configurations.
We define
configurations
on complete derivation trees and segments. For every node
p
, it has a configuration tuple
(\mathbf{l}_{p},\mathbf{r}_{p})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
. For a complete derivation tree
\mathcal{T}
rooted by
r
,
(\mathbf{l}_{r},\mathbf{r}_{r})
is often fixed. Then, all configurations are determined as follows:

(1)

Consider any internal node
p
. If
p
has the rule
\sigma(p)\rightarrow\sigma(c_{1})\sigma(c_{2})
, then
\mathbf{l}_{p}=\mathbf{l}_{c_{1}}
,
\mathbf{r}_{p}=\mathbf{r}_{c_{2}}
and
\mathbf{r}_{c_{1}}=\mathbf{l}_{c_{2}}
.

(2)

If
p
has the rule
\sigma(p)\rightarrow\sigma(c_{1})
and
\sigma(c_{1})\in\Sigma
, then
\mathbf{l}_{p}=\mathbf{l}_{c_{1}}
,
\mathbf{r}_{p}=\mathbf{r}_{c_{1}}
and
\mathbf{l}_{c_{1}}+\sigma(c_{1})=\mathbf{r}_{c_{1}}
.

The computation rules above also apply for segments, following a slightly different computational order illustrated in Figure
2
. Notice that for
\mathrm{seg}{(x,y)}
, fixing either
(\mathbf{l}_{x},\mathbf{r}_{x})
or
(\mathbf{l}_{y},\mathbf{r}_{y})
decides all other configurations.

A complete derivation tree
\mathcal{T}
, or a segment
\mathrm{seg}{(x,y)}
is
nonnegative
, if all configurations are nonnegative, i.e.,
(\mathbf{l}_{p},\mathbf{r}_{p})\geq(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})
for every
p
. In the sequel, we adopt the
most-simplified assumption
for derivation trees. That is, there do not exist nodes
p,q
such that
p\prec q
,
\sigma(p)=\sigma(q)
and

4.
KLM Trees

The KLM decomposition algorithm for VASS
(
9
;
16
)
is based on KLM sequences, whose components encode the SCCs of VASS. Similarly, the components of a KLM tree capture the strong connectivity in derivation trees of TGVAS. We define the KLM tree for dimension
d
, in order to provide a generalization of the
\mathbb{Z}
-reachability of
d
-TGVAS.

Strongly-connected segments.
Given
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
of a complete derivation tree, let
\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
be the path from
p
to
q
. The production graph
\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}}
is constructed according to rules used in
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
. Let the topmost SCC of
\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}}
be
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{top}}(\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}})
. We define the strong connectivity on segments by:

Definition 4.1.

A segment
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
is
strongly connected
, if for
n\in\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
, it holds
\sigma(n)\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{top}}(\widetilde{G}_{\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}})
. That is, all nonterminals in
\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
lie in the same SCC.

4.1.
Divisions

Given a complete derivation tree
\mathcal{T}
rooted by
r
. The following procedure produces a
division
of
\mathcal{T}
.

(1)

If
\mathcal{T}
only contains a terminal, the procedure terminates.

(2)

Find some
p\in\mathcal{T}
where
\mathrm{seg}{(r,p)}
is strongly connected.

(3)

Store
\mathrm{seg}{(r,p)}
and do division on the children of
p
.

Let all the segments so obtained be the set
\mathrm{Div}{(\mathcal{T})}
. There always exists a division since all segments can be trivial (containing only one node). Lemma
4.2
implies the existence of a small division whose size is bounded by
\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{G})})
. Before that, we introduce
the topmost SCC division
. Given a complete, nontrivial
\mathcal{T}
rooted by
r
, there exists node
p\in\mathcal{T}
satisfying:

(1)

Segment
\mathrm{seg}{(r,p)}
is strongly connected.

(2)

Nonterminals in
\mathrm{path}{(r,p)}
are
unique
. For
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathcal{T}
, if
n_{1}\in\mathrm{path}{(r,p)}
and
n_{2}\notin\mathrm{path}{(r,p)}
, then
\sigma(n_{1})\neq\sigma(n_{2})
.

To achieve this, consider the production graph
\widetilde{G}_{\mathcal{T}}
constructed by rules used in
\mathcal{T}
. Let the topmost SCC be
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{top}}(\widetilde{G}_{\mathcal{T}})
. We initialize the node pointer
p
as
r
, and check the rules on
\mathcal{T}
iteratively:

(1)

If
p
has a fully-degenerate rule on
\mathcal{T}
, or
p
has a terminal child, then terminate.

(2)

If
p
has a partially-degenerate rule
\sigma({p})\rightarrow\sigma(c_{1})\sigma(c_{2})
, update
{p}
by
c_{1}
if
\sigma(c_{1})\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{top}}(\widetilde{G}_{\mathcal{T}})
and update
{p}
by
c_{2}
if
\sigma(c_{2})\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{top}}(\widetilde{G}_{\mathcal{T}})
. Go back to step (1).

The node
p
obtained at termination is the desired one. Strong connectivity follows directly from the definition of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{top}}(\widetilde{G}_{\mathcal{T}})
and uniqueness is guaranteed by the definition of fully-degenerate rules.

Lemma 4.2.

Given a TGVAS
\mathcal{G}
and a complete derivation tree
\mathcal{T}
. There exists a division
\mathrm{Div}{(\mathcal{T})}
with
|\mathrm{Div}{(\mathcal{T})}|\leq{\mathrm{exp}}({\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{G})}})
.

Proof.

Applying the topmost SCC division on
\mathcal{T}
yields the first segment
\mathrm{seg}{(r,p)}
. If
p
has two children
c_{1},c_{2}
, divide
\mathrm{sub}{(c_{1})}
and
\mathrm{sub}{(c_{2})}
recursively. By uniqueness,
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{sub}{(c_{1})}},\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{sub}{(c_{2})}}\subsetneqq\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{T}}
and the recursion depth has a limit of
|\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{T}}|
. We have
|\mathrm{Div}{(\mathcal{T})}|\leq 2^{|\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{T}}|}\leq{\mathrm{exp}}({\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{G})}})
.
∎

4.2.
KLM Components and KLM Trees

Thanks to the existence of a small division, it suffices to depict the derivation behavior of strongly-connected segments. We next introduce the KLM component and the KLM tree, and explain how they faithfully capture derivation trees. The KLM components and KLM trees are syntactic objects given by the TGVAS grammar, whereas the segments and derivation trees are semantic objects.

Definition 4.3.

A KLM component is the tuple:

\mathcal{C}=\left(P,Q,\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}},\rho_{\mathrm{D}}\right).

Nonterminals
P,Q\in\mathcal{X}
are the source and target symbols, respectively.
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
are three sets of production rules.
\rho_{\mathrm{D}}\in\mathcal{P}
is a single production rule.

We are only interested in the KLM component that captures a strongly-connected segment. Figure
3
shows how a strongly-connected segment (left, denoted by
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
, and nonterminals
C,D
not included) is captured by a KLM component (right). The nonterminals along
\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
form the sequence:
\{X,Y,Z,X,Y,Z\}
. The exit rule is
Z\rightarrow CD
. The idea of “capturing” is straightforward: let the rule sets
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}
collect production rules occurring in
\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
, on its left, and on its right, respectively. The definition is as below.

Definition 4.4.

Given the KLM component
\mathcal{C}
and a strongly-connected segment
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
.
\mathcal{C}
captures
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
with the exit rule
\sigma(q)\rightarrow\sigma(c_{1})\sigma(c_{2})
if the following statements are valid.

(1)

P=\sigma(p),Q=\sigma(q)
and
\rho_{\mathrm{D}}=\sigma(q)\rightarrow\sigma(c_{1})\sigma(c_{2})
.

(2)

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}
is the set of production rules used along
\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
.

(3)

Let
N_{\mathrm{L}},N_{\mathrm{R}}
be two sets of tree nodes. For any parent node
n\in\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}\setminus\{q\}
, its left child
c_{1}
and right child
c_{2}
, if
c_{2}\in\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
, let
c_{1}\in N_{\mathrm{L}}
; otherwise, let
c_{2}\in N_{\mathrm{R}}
. The rule sets
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}
satisfy the following conditions:

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}=\bigcup_{n\in N_{\mathrm{L}}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sub}{(n)}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}=\bigcup_{n\in N_{\mathrm{R}}}\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sub}{(n)}}.

We write
\mathcal{C}\rhd\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
for the capturing relation, omitting the exit rule. Usually
\rho_{\mathrm{D}}
is clear from context or is immaterial.

A KLM component
\mathcal{C}
is trivial, if
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}=\varnothing
. Hence,
\rho_{\mathrm{D}}
is the only rule in the trivial
\mathcal{C}
. Observe that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}\cap\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}\cap\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}=\varnothing
by thinness. The rule sets
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}
may share identical production rules. In this case, they are treated as different. Nonterminals in
\mathrm{path}{(p,q)},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}
are denoted by
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{SCC}},\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{L}},\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{R}}
respectively. Terminals in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}
or
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}
are denoted by
\Sigma_{\mathrm{L}}
or
\Sigma_{\mathrm{R}}
. For illustration, in Figure
3
,
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{SCC}}=\{X,Y,Z\}
and
\Sigma_{\mathrm{L}}=\{\mathbf{u}_{A},\mathbf{u}_{B}\}
. In order to distinguish sets like
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{SCC}}
or
\Sigma_{\mathrm{L}}
belonging to different KLM components, we write
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{SCC}}(\mathcal{C})
or
\Sigma_{\mathrm{L}}(\mathcal{C})
.

Figure 3.
A strongly-connected segment captured by a KLM component.

Definition 4.5.

A KLM tree
\mathcal{KT}
is a binary tree whose nodes are KLM components or terminals. For
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{KT}
, if
\rho_{\mathrm{D}}=Q\rightarrow AB
,
\mathcal{C}
has two children
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}
whose sources
P(\mathcal{C}_{1})=A
and
P(\mathcal{C}_{2})=B
. If
\rho_{\mathrm{D}}=Q\rightarrow\mathbf{u}_{Q}
,
\mathcal{C}
has one child
\mathbf{u}_{Q}
. Terminal leaves are considered distinct, even if they have the identical values.

Definition 4.6.

A KLM tree
\mathcal{KT}
captures a complete derivation tree
\mathcal{T}
, if there exists a division
\mathrm{Div}{(\mathcal{T})}
and a bijection (maintaining the tree order) from KLM components
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{KT}
to segments
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}\in\mathrm{Div}{(\mathcal{T})}
such that
\mathcal{C}\rhd\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
.
The capturing relation is denoted by
\mathcal{KT}\rhd\mathcal{T}
.

4.3.
The Characteristic System

The syntax of a KLM component defines a class of possible, strongly-connected segments. To provide a more detailed characterization, we introduce an integer linear programming (ILP) system for each KLM component, known as the
characteristic system
. The characteristic system of
\mathcal{C}
contains following variables:

(1)

Configuration variables. A KLM component
{\mathcal{C}}
has four configuration variables
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, standing for the configurations at node
p
or
q
with respect to the captured segment
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
.

(2)

Variables of Parikh images of production rules. For each rule
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}
,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}
or
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}
, the variable
\#(\rho)\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
denotes the Parikh image (how many times it occurs). Notice that identical rules in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}
are treated distinctly.

Intuitively, a nonterminal must be produced and consumed the same number of times in order to form a complete derivation tree. Therefore, we introduce the implicit
degree variables
. Given
X\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{SCC}}
, let
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{in}}(X)\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}
be the set of rules producing
X
, and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{out}}(X)\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}
be the set of rules consuming
X
. We define the degree variables as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{deg_{in}}{(X)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{in}}(X)}\#(\rho),

\displaystyle\mathrm{deg_{out}}{(X)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{out}}(X)}\#(\rho).

Given
A\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{L}}
, we define
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{in}}(A)\subseteq(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}\cup\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}})
. It covers rules in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}
that produce
A
, and rules in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}
that produce
A
on the left. For example,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{in}}(A)
contains rules
B\rightarrow AA\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}
and
X\rightarrow AY\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}
, but not the rule
X\rightarrow YA\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}
. The variables
\mathrm{deg_{in}}{(A)},\mathrm{deg_{out}}{(A)}
are defined analogously, except that
\mathrm{deg_{in}}{(A)}
counts the Parikh image of rules like
B\rightarrow AA
twice.
For terminals, the in-degrees are equivalent to the Parikh images. Therefore, given a terminal
\mathbf{u}\in\Sigma_{\mathrm{L}}
and the rule set
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{in}}(\mathbf{u})\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}
producing
\mathbf{u}
, we define the Parikh image variable as:

\#(\mathbf{u})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{in}}(\mathbf{u})}\#(\rho).

The characteristic system for a KLM component
\mathcal{C}
is denoted by
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{C}}
, containing the following equations:

\displaystyle\#(\rho)

\displaystyle\geq 1,

\displaystyle\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}+\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\Sigma_{\mathrm{L}}}\#(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}}+\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\Sigma_{\mathrm{R}}}\#(\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{deg_{in}}{(X)}+\mathds{I}_{X=P}-\left(\mathrm{deg_{out}}{(X)}+\mathds{I}_{X=Q}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

where the logic indicator
\mathds{I}_{\Psi}=1
if
\Psi
is true and
\mathds{I}_{\Psi}=0
if not.

The characteristic system
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{u}}
for a terminal
\mathbf{u}
on the KLM tree contains variables
\mathbf{l}^{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{r}^{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{l}^{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{r}^{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\mathbf{l}^{\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}^{\mathbf{u}}.

Basically, the characteristic system of a KLM tree is composed of ILP systems like
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{C}}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{u}}
. Moreover, the configuration variables should be subject to a global characteristic system in order to guarantee the consistency. The global characteristic system
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Global}}
is defined as follows. For any
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{KT}
, if it has two children
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}
, we add the equation:

\left(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}_{1}}\right)=\left(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}_{1}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}_{2}},\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}_{2}}\right).

Otherwise, it has a terminal child
\mathbf{u}
. We write:

\left(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}}\right)=\left(\mathbf{l}^{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{r}^{\mathbf{u}}\right).

In some cases, the configuration variables of KLM components (or terminals) are fixed. The characteristic system
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Config}}
is introduced for such configuration constraints. Considering the reachability problem
\mathrm{\mathbf{Reach}}(\mathcal{G},\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})
, we have the equation:

\left(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}\right)=\left(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t}\right).

The characteristic system for a KLM tree is the union of ILP systems introduced above.

Definition 4.7.

The characteristic system for KLM tree
\mathcal{KT}
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{KT}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\left(\bigwedge_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{KT}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{C}}\right)\land\left(\bigwedge_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{KT}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{u}}\right)\land\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Global}}\land\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Config}}.

The size of KLM trees.
The size of a KLM tree
\mathcal{KT}
also serves as an upper bound for the unary size of
\mathcal{KT}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{KT}}
. Let
|\mathcal{C}|
be the total number of symbols, rules, variables and equations in all characteristic systems involving
\mathcal{C}
. Let
\left\|{\mathcal{C}}\right\|
be the largest
l_{1}
-norm in the systems above. We define
\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{C})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}|\mathcal{C}|\cdot\left\|{\mathcal{C}}\right\|
.
Moreover, we define the size of
\mathcal{KT}
as:

\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\sum_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{KT}}\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{C})}+\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{KT}}\left\|{\mathbf{u}}\right\|_{1}.

The initial capturing tree lemma
(Lemma
4.8
) follows immediately from Lemma
4.2
, together with the construction of the characteristic system.

Lemma 4.8.

Given the reachability problem
\mathrm{\mathbf{Reach}}(\mathcal{G},\mathbf{s},\mathbf{t})
and a instance of a complete, nonnegative derivation tree
\mathcal{T}
, there exists a small KLM tree
\mathcal{KT}
such that:

(1)

\mathcal{KT}\rhd\mathcal{T},

(2)

\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})}\leq{\mathrm{exp}}(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{G})}+\left\|{\mathbf{s}}\right\|_{1}+\left\|{\mathbf{t}}\right\|_{1}).

4.4.
Ranks

First, we introduce the
geometric dimension

(
9
;
23
;
10
)
of KLM components. We may investigate cycles under the rules of a KLM component
\mathcal{C}
. A
top cycle
under
\mathcal{C}
, denoted by
\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)}
, is a cycle constructed within the syntax of
\mathcal{C}
that additionally satisfies
\sigma(x)=\sigma(y)\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{SCC}}
.

Let the symbols of the leaves of
\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)}
under DFS traversal (preorder) be the sequence:
\{\mathbf{u}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{m_{1}},\sigma(y),\mathbf{u}_{m_{1}+1},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{m_{2}}\}
. The effect of the cycle is defined as a vector in
\mathbb{N}^{d}\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
:

\Delta(\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\mathbf{u}_{i},\sum_{i=m_{1}+1}^{m_{2}}\mathbf{u}_{i}\right).

Let
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{C}}
be the set of all possible top cycles under
\mathcal{C}
. We define the set of effects in
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{C}}
as:

\Delta(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\left\{\Delta(\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)})\mid\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C}}\right\}.

The geometric dimension of a nontrivial KLM component
\mathcal{C}
is denoted by
\mathrm{dim}{(\mathcal{C})}
, defined as the dimension of the vector space spanned by the effects of possible top cycles under
\mathcal{C}
. In formal terms, we define:

\mathrm{dim}{(\mathcal{C})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\mathrm{dim}{(\Delta({\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{C}}))}.

An observation is that
\mathrm{dim}{(\mathcal{C})}\neq 0
as long as
\mathcal{C}
is nontrivial. Otherwise, there must exist
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
satisfying
n_{1}\prec n_{2},\sigma(n_{1})=\sigma(n_{2})
and
\Delta(\mathrm{cyc}{(n_{1},n_{2})})=(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})
, which violates the most-simplified assumption. We conclude that
\mathrm{dim}{(\mathcal{C})}\in[1,2d]
for any nontrivial
\mathcal{C}
.

In a further scenario where a KLM component is decomposed, smaller KLM components may be extracted from the rule sets
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}
. We now introduce
the index of KLM components
, which quantifies how many times such extractions may occur. The index of a nontrivial
\mathcal{C}
is defined as the largest index among induced sub-GVASes under
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}
or
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}
(instead of
\mathcal{G}
):

\iota(\mathcal{C})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\max_{X\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{L}}\lor X\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{R}}}\{\iota(\mathcal{G}_{X})\}.

Here we slightly abuse notation by writing
\mathcal{G}_{X}
for the sub-GVAS under
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}
if
X\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{L}}
, or under
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}
if
X\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{R}}
. If
\mathcal{G}
is
k
-indexed, then
\iota(\mathcal{C})\in[1,k-1]
for any nontrivial
\mathcal{C}
.

Definition 4.9 (Rank of KLM components).

The rank of a nontrivial
\mathcal{C}
is the pair:

\mathrm{rank}{(\mathcal{C})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\left(\iota(\mathcal{C}),\mathrm{dim}{(\mathcal{C})}\right).

There are
(2kd-2d)
different ranks. We compare
\mathrm{rank}{(\mathcal{C})}
under lexicographic order. The rank of
\mathcal{KT}
is a vector in
\mathbb{N}^{2kd-2d}
, where the
i
-th coordinate counts the number of KLM components with the
i
-th largest rank. For example, the largest rank is
(k-1,2d)
, the second largest is
(k-1,2d-1)
, the
(2d+1)
-st largest is
(k-2,2d)
, and so on.

Definition 4.10 (Rank of KLM trees).

The rank of
\mathcal{KT}
is defined as:

\mathrm{rank}{(\mathcal{KT})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\left(\left(\sum_{\mathrm{rank}{(\mathcal{C})}=(k-1,2d)}1\right),\dots,\left(\sum_{\mathrm{rank}{(\mathcal{C})}=(1,1)}1\right)\right).

We also compare
\mathrm{rank}{(\mathcal{KT})}
under lexicographic order. The rank of a KLM component is computable by enumerating simple cycles under its rule sets (to compute the geometric dimension), and by dynamic programming (to compute the index).

4.5.
Solutions and Boundedness

Given
\mathcal{KT}\rhd\mathcal{T}
, the characteristic system
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{KT}}
is sound and admits solutions representing complete derivation trees. However, they are not necessarily nonnegative. Moreover,
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{KT}}
admits at least one solution
\mathrm{sol}_{\mathcal{T}}
standing for
\mathcal{T}
. A solution is called
minimal
if there is no other solution that is pointwise less than or equal to it. Let
\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}
be a minimal solution under
\mathrm{sol}_{\mathcal{T}}
. By Lemma
2.2
, there exists a size bound for
\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}
:

\left\|{\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})}).

The homogeneous version of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{KT}}
, denoted by
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{KT}}^{0}
, is obtained by replacing all nonzero constants with
0
. More precisely, we replace the corresponding equations with the following ones:

\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{C}}
::

\#(\rho)\geq 0,\mathrm{deg}_{\mathrm{in}}(X)-\mathrm{deg}_{\mathrm{out}}(X)=0.

\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{u}}
::

\mathbf{l}_{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{u}}.

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Config}}
::

(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}})=(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0}).

Let
H
denote the set of all nonnegative, minimal solutions to
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{KT}}^{0}
. The set
H
is known as the
Hilbert basis
. Solutions in
H
are compositions of cycles, since they fulfill the Euler condition. It is clear that
\mathrm{sol}_{\mathcal{T}}\in\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}+\mathbb{N}{(H)}
. We define the sum of solutions in
H
as:

\mathrm{Sol}_{H}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\sum_{\mathrm{sol}\in H}\mathrm{sol}.

By Lemma
2.2
, we have
\left\|{\mathrm{Sol}_{H}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})})
.

For a certain variable
v
, let
\mathrm{sol}(v)
denote the value of
v
in the solution
\mathrm{sol}
. The condition
\mathrm{Sol}_{H}(v)=0
means that for any
\mathrm{sol}\in\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}+\mathbb{N}{(H)}
, we have
\mathrm{sol}(v)=\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}(v)
. Such variable
v
is called
bounded
.
A variable is
unbounded
otherwise. Bounded variables have an upper bound of
\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})})
.

5.
Decomposing KLM Trees

The refinement sequence. Configuration constraining and orthogonalization are always performed collectively. The ``decomposition-cleaning'' loop brings about a strict decrease in rank.

In this section, we focus on the one-dimensional case. We aim to convert a KLM tree into a
perfect
KLM tree via a sequence of refinements,
where a perfect KLM tree is a certificate of the reachability. We introduce four refinements: configuration constraining, orthogonalization, algebraic decomposition and combinatorial decomposition, each of which guarantees a perfectness property.

Following Leroux et al. in
(
16
)
, we distinguish between two types of refinements: the
cleaning operations
, including configuration constraining and orthogonalization, and the
decompositions
, including the others. Applying any refinement on
\mathcal{KT}\rhd\mathcal{T}
gives a new KLM tree
\mathcal{KT}^{\prime}\rhd\mathcal{T}
with
\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT}^{\prime})}\leq{\mathrm{exp}}(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})})
. A cleaning operation does not change the rank of
\mathcal{KT}
. A decomposition strictly decreases the rank, since it decomposes at least one KLM nontrivial component into KLM components with smaller ranks. The refinement sequence is shown in Figure
4
. The length of the refinement sequence is limited by Lemma
2.1
.
We refer to Appendix
B
for the detailed proofs of the lemmas sketched in the main text.

5.1.
Perfectness

Given
\mathcal{KT}\rhd\mathcal{T}
, there exists a solution
\mathrm{sol}_{\mathcal{T}}
describing
\mathcal{T}
. Nevertheless, the size of
\mathrm{sol}_{\mathcal{T}}
is unbounded, hence the reachability cannot be deduced solely from
\mathcal{KT}
. However, this limitation does not hold for a
perfect
KLM tree. In section
6
, we show that one can directly construct a nonnegative, complete derivation tree from a perfect KLM tree, without knowing the derivation tree it captures. Here, before the definition of the perfectness properties
(
10
)
, we introduce several concepts.

Orthogonality.
Generally, for a nontrivial
\mathcal{C}\rhd\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
, if all top cycles have effect
0
on the left or right side, then configurations along
\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
on that side are entirely determined by their symbols. Such property is called orthogonality
(
9
)
or rigidity
(
16
)
.

Definition 5.1.

Given a nontrivial
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{KT}
. If for every
\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{C}}
, it holds that
\Delta(\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)})[1]=0
, then
\mathcal{C}
is left-orthogonal; if
\Delta(\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)})[2]=0
, it is right-orthogonal.

Pumpability.
For a nontrivial KLM component
\mathcal{C}
, the pumpability is discussed on configuration variables
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}}
. Let
\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})
be the set of configuration variables to be pumped. If
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
is bounded and
\mathcal{C}
is not left-orthogonal, then
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})
. If
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
is bounded and
\mathcal{C}
is not right-orthogonal, then
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})
, and analogously for
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}}
.

Consider a nontrivial
\mathcal{C}
with at least one of
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
in
\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})
.
\mathcal{C}
is
forward-pumpable
, if there exists a
forward-pumping cycle

\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)}
such that:

(1)

\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C}}
and
\sigma(x)=\sigma(y)=P(\mathcal{C})
.

(2)

If both
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})
, fix the root configuration of
\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)}
by
(\mathbf{l}_{x},\mathbf{r}_{x})=(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}})
. We require
\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)}
be nonnegative and
(\mathbf{l}_{y},\mathbf{r}_{y})\geq(\mathbf{l}_{x}+1,\mathbf{l}_{y}+1)
.

(3)

If
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})
and
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}\notin\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})
, fix the root configuration by
(\mathbf{l}_{x},\mathbf{r}_{x})=(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}},+\omega)
. We require
\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)}
be nonnegative and
\mathbf{l}_{y}\geq\mathbf{l}_{x}+1
. The symmetric case is handled similarly.

The
backward-pumpable
condition is symmetric. For instance, if
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})
, we fix the leaf configuration by
(\mathbf{l}_{y},\mathbf{r}_{y})=(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}})
and require
(\mathbf{l}_{x},\mathbf{r}_{x})\geq(\mathbf{l}_{y}+1,\mathbf{r}_{y}+1)
.

A component
\mathcal{C}
is
pumpable
, if it is both forward-pumpable (when applicable, i.e., when
\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})
contains
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
or
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
) and backward-pumpable.
\mathcal{C}
is called
f
-pumpable
for function
f(\cdot)
, if the pumping cycles
\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)}
satisfy
|\mathrm{path}{(x,y)}|\leq f(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{C})})
.

We are ready to introduce the perfectness properties and the corresponding refinements.

Definition 5.2 (Perfectness).

A KLM tree
\mathcal{KT}\rhd\mathcal{T}
is called
perfect
if it satisfies the following four properties:

•

Fully constrained.

Every bounded configuration variable is fixed in the characteristic system

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Config}}
.

•

Fully orthogonalized.

For every left-orthogonal component
\mathcal{C}
with bounded
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
and
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}}
,
the left configurations are hard-encoded in
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{SCC}}
.
The right-orthogonal case is handled similarly.

•

Production unboundedness.

For every nontrivial
\mathcal{C}
and every production rule
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}(\mathcal{C})\cup\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}(\mathcal{C})\cup\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}(\mathcal{C})
,
the variable
\#(\rho)
is unbounded.

•

\mathrm{Exp}
-pumpability.

Every nontrivial
\mathcal{C}
is
\exp
-pumpable.

5.2.
Configuration Constraining

In order to meet the fully-constrained property, Lemma
5.3
is introduced. Applying Lemma
5.3
to
\mathcal{KT}
is denoted by the refinement operator
\mathrm{\mathbf{Constr}}
. That is,
\mathrm{\mathbf{Constr}}(\mathcal{KT})=\mathcal{KT}^{\prime}
, where
\mathcal{KT}^{\prime}
is the new KLM tree obtained.

Lemma 5.3 (Constraining).

Given
\mathcal{KT}\rhd\mathcal{T}
. There exists a fully-constrained KLM tree
\mathcal{KT}^{\prime}
such that:

(1)

\mathcal{KT}^{\prime}\rhd\mathcal{T}
,

(2)

\mathrm{rank}{(\mathcal{KT}^{\prime})}\leq\mathrm{rank}{(\mathcal{KT})}
,

(3)

\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT}^{\prime})}\leq{\mathrm{exp}}(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})})
.

Proof.

Assume that in some component
\mathcal{C}
, the variable
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
is bounded. Add the following constraint equation to
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Config}}
:

\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}=\mathrm{sol}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}).

By boundedness, we have
\mathrm{sol}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbf{l}_{P}^{\mathcal{C}})=\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathbf{l}_{P}^{\mathcal{C}})
, and by Lemma
2.2
, the value is in
{\mathrm{exp}}(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})})
. The size of the new KLM tree is also in
\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})})
. Since we make no other modifications, the rank remains unchanged.
∎

5.3.
Orthogonalization

Suppose the KLM tree is fully constrained. The orthogonalization is performed on orthogonal KLM components with bounded configuration variables. The refinement operator is
\mathrm{\mathbf{Ortho}}
.

Lemma 5.4 (Orthogonalization).

Given
\mathcal{KT}\rhd\mathcal{T}
being fully constrained. There exists a fully-constrained, fully-orthogonalized KLM tree
\mathcal{KT}^{\prime}
such that:

(1)

\mathcal{KT}^{\prime}\rhd\mathcal{T},

(2)

\mathrm{rank}{(\mathcal{KT}^{\prime})}\leq\mathrm{rank}{(\mathcal{KT})},

(3)

\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT}^{\prime})}\leq{\mathrm{exp}}(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})}).

Lemma 5.5.

The left-orthogonality of a KLM component
\mathcal{C}
(and the right-orthogonality as well) is decidable in
EXPSPACE
.

First, observe that for any left-orthogonal
\mathcal{C}
and any cycle under the rule set
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}(\mathcal{C})
, the cycle has a displacement of
0
(recall that the displacement of a cycle is the sum of terminals). Moreover, whenever a nonterminal (such as
A
) is produced directly by a left-degenerate rule in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}(\mathcal{C})
(such as
X\rightarrow AY
), the displacements of all these subtrees of
A
are identical. The displacement is called the
uniform displacement
, denoted by
\mathbf{u}_{A}
. Clearly,
\mathbf{u}_{A}
is bounded by
\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{C})})
since it is the displacement of some acyclic, complete derivation tree under
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}(\mathcal{C})
.

Sketch of Proof.

First, we check whether such uniform displacements exist. Then, we construct a weighted, directed graph
G(\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{SCC}},E)
by mapping left-degenerate rules like
X\rightarrow AY\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}(\mathcal{C})
to the edge
(X,Y,\mathbf{u}_{A})
, and right-degenerate rules like
X\rightarrow YB\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}(\mathcal{C})
to the edge
(X,Y,0)
. The weight sum of any cycle in
G
must be
0
. All checks can be done in
EXPSPACE
.
∎

If
\mathcal{C}\rhd\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
is left-orthogonal,
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
and
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}}
share the same boundedness. If both
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
and
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}}
are bounded, the left configuration of any
n\in\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
is also bounded by
\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{C})})
and can be determined solely from
\sigma(n)
. To see this, consider the graph
G(\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{SCC}},E)
constructed above. The left configuration is obtained through an acyclic path in
G
, which is adding at most
|\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{SCC}}|
uniform displacements to
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
. This value, denoted by
\mathbf{l}_{\sigma(n)}
, is called the
uniform configuration
.

For
X\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{SCC}}
, the uniform left configuration
\mathbf{l}_{X}
is effectively computable. We intend to replace symbol
X
with the new symbol
(_{\mathbf{l}_{X}}X)
where
\mathbf{l}_{X}
is hard-encoded, such as
(_{3}X),(_{5}Y)
. For the right-orthogonal case, new symbols are
(X_{3}),(Y_{5})
and so on. Since the uniform configurations are hard-encoded, for the left-degenerate rule
(_{3}X)\rightarrow A(_{5}Y)
, the derivation tree
\mathcal{T}_{A}
must be a nonnegative, complete derivation tree with the root configuration
(3,5)
. The rule set
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}(\mathcal{C})
is no longer relevant. Our attention now shifts to whether such certificate exists. Therefore, rules in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}(\mathcal{C})
are replaced with rules like
A\rightarrow 5-3
, namely the
certificate rules
. Notice that
5-3=2
is exactly the uniform displacement
\mathbf{u}_{A}
.

Proof for Lemma
5.4
.

Consider a nontrivial
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{KT}
which is left-orthogonal. Suppose that
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
and
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}}
are bounded. The uniform displacements and the uniform left configurations are computable and bounded by
\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{C})})
. We reconstruct
\mathcal{C}
as follows:

(1)

For any rule
X\rightarrow AB\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}(\mathcal{C})
, replace it with
(_{\mathbf{l}_{X}}X)\rightarrow A(_{\mathbf{l}_{B}}B)
.

(2)

Replace
P(\mathcal{C})
with
(_{\mathbf{l}_{P}}P)
and replace
Q(\mathcal{C})
with
(_{\mathbf{l}_{Q}}Q)
.

(3)

The rule set
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}(\mathcal{C})
is cleared. For every left-degenerate rule
(_{\mathbf{l}_{X}}X)\rightarrow A(_{\mathbf{l}_{Y}}Y)
, add the certificate rule
A\rightarrow\mathbf{l}_{Y}-\mathbf{l}_{X}
.

The size of the new KLM component (including values like
\mathbf{l}_{X}
) is bounded by
\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{C})})
. The index does not increase, since rules in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}(\mathcal{C})
are now simplified. The geometric dimension does not change. We conclude that
\mathcal{KT}^{\prime}
satisfies the requirement above.
∎

In the sequel, configuration issues on the orthogonalized side are no longer considered. The certificate rule
A\rightarrow\mathbf{l}_{Y}-\mathbf{l}_{X}
serves as a reachability subproblem
\mathrm{\mathbf{Reach}}(\mathcal{G}_{A},\mathbf{l}_{X},\mathbf{l}_{Y})
with
\iota(\mathcal{G}_{A})\leq\iota(\mathcal{G})-1
. In our final algorithm, such subproblems are checked recursively. Moreover, the definition of
\rhd
is extended. For
\mathcal{C}\rhd\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
where
\mathcal{C}
is left-orthogonalized, on top of all previous conditions, the condition involving
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}
is replaced by:

For
n\in\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
such that
n
has a left-degenerate rule
\sigma(n)\rightarrow\sigma(c_{1})\sigma(c_{2})
on the segment, there exists a certificate rule in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}(\mathcal{C})
representing
\mathrm{sub}{(c_{1})}
.

5.4.
Algebraic Decomposition

The algebraic decomposition is performed on any nontrivial KLM component
\mathcal{C}
with any bounded
\#(\rho)
. The refinement operator is
\mathrm{\mathbf{Decomp_{A}}}
.

Lemma 5.6 (Algebraic decomposition).

Given
\mathcal{KT}\rhd\mathcal{T}
. If there exists some nontrivial
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{KT}
with at least one bounded
\#(\rho)
, then there exists another KLM tree
\mathcal{KT}^{\prime}
such that:

(1)

\mathcal{KT}^{\prime}\rhd\mathcal{T},

(2)

\mathrm{rank}{(\mathcal{KT}^{\prime})}<\mathrm{rank}{(\mathcal{KT})},

(3)

\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT}^{\prime})}\leq{\mathrm{exp}}(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})}).

Let
\mathcal{C}\rhd\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
. We aim to decompose
\mathcal{C}
into smaller KLM components whose ranks strictly decrease. In order to maintain the capturing relation, we have to subdivide
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
into smaller segments. We construct a division set
\mathrm{Div}{(\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)})}
containing these segments which renders true the following statements:

(1)

Bounded rules are excluded from segments in
\mathrm{Div}{(\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)})}
.

(2)

Every segment in
\mathrm{Div}{(\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)})}
is strongly connected.

(3)

Let the original division set of
\mathcal{T}
be
\mathrm{Div}{(\mathcal{T})}
. Then:

\mathrm{Div}^{\prime}{(\mathcal{T})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}(\mathrm{Div}{(\mathcal{T})}\setminus\{\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}\})\cup\mathrm{Div}{(\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)})}

is a new division of
\mathcal{T}
.

(4)

The size
|\mathrm{Div}{(\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)})}|
is bounded by
\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})})
.

Our main idea is illustrated in Figure
5
.
Diagram (A) shows
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
with two bounded rules, while diagrams (B)–(E) illustrate the four steps of the division procedure.

Step 1: Virtual tree construction.

In this step, we intend to locate the bounded rules in
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
. Let the set of bounded rules be
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{B}}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}\cup\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}\cup\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}
. Let the node set
N_{\mathrm{B}}
collect all nodes in
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
that have a bounded rule. We additionally include the node
q
in
N_{\mathrm{B}}
since
\rho_{\mathrm{D}}
is used exactly once. Denote the lowest common ancestor (LCA) of nodes
n_{1},n_{2}
as
\mathrm{lca}(n_{1},n_{2})
. Consider the LCA-closure of
N_{\mathrm{B}}
:

\mathrm{LCA}(N_{\mathrm{B}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}N_{\mathrm{B}}\cup\left\{\mathrm{lca}(n_{1},n_{2})\mid n_{1},n_{2}\in N_{\mathrm{B}}\right\}.

Nodes in
\mathrm{LCA}(N_{\mathrm{B}})
form the
virtual tree
of
N_{\mathrm{B}}
. In Figure
5
, diagram (B), the virtual tree contains four nodes: node
q
, two nodes with bounded rules, and their LCA.

Step 2: Segment fracturing.

Every edge of the virtual tree is going to be a new segment. For every
q^{\ast}\in\mathrm{LCA}(N_{\mathrm{B}})
, we construct
\mathrm{seg}{(p^{\ast},q^{\ast})}
and decide
p^{\ast}
by:

(1)

Let
r\in\mathrm{LCA}(N_{\mathrm{B}})
be the root of the virtual tree. For
q^{\ast}\in\mathrm{LCA}(N_{\mathrm{B}})
and
q^{\ast}\neq r
, it has a parent in the virtual tree, denoted by
\mathrm{par}(q^{\ast})
.

(2)

Node
\mathrm{par}(q^{\ast})
has two children
c_{1},c_{2}
on
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
. Let
p^{\ast}
be
c_{1}
if
q^{\ast}\in\mathrm{sub}{(c_{1})}
, and
c_{2}
otherwise.

(3)

For
q^{\ast}=r
, let
p^{\ast}=p
.

In this way, bounded rules serve as bridges between segments and are therefore effectively excluded. See Figure
5
, diagram (C).

Step 3: SCC division.

In this step, we further subdivide segments
\mathrm{seg}{(p^{\ast},q^{\ast})}
into strongly-connected ones. We construct a directed graph
G
by merging nodes on
\mathrm{path}{(p^{\ast},q^{\ast})}
which share the same symbol. The SCC condensation graph of
G
is a path. Each SCC is mapped to a subpath, denoted by
\mathrm{path}{(p^{\dagger},q^{\dagger})}
, and gives a strongly-connected segment
\mathrm{seg}{(p^{\dagger},q^{\dagger})}
. In Figure
5
, diagram (D), the original path
\{X,Y,X,Y,Z,W,Z,W\}
is divided into
\{X,Y,X,Y\}
and
\{Z,W,Z,W\}
.

Step 4: Subtree completion.

In any feasible division of a complete derivation tree, each segment has either exactly two children or none. However, for a segment
\mathrm{seg}{(p^{\dagger},q^{\dagger})}
obtained in step 3, the subtree rooted by a child of
q^{\dagger}
is not necessarily covered in any segment. Subdivisions of these subtrees should be included in the final division set, as shown in Figure
5
, diagram (E).

(1)

Let
\mathrm{Div}{(\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)})}
collects all strongly-connected
\mathrm{seg}{(p^{\dagger},q^{\dagger})}
first. We check all
\mathrm{seg}{(p^{\dagger},q^{\dagger})}
as follows.

(2)

The children of
q^{\dagger}
must be covered. For an uncovered child
c_{1}
, we can infer that
c_{1}\notin\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
. Therefore,
\mathrm{sub}{(c_{1})}
is a complete derivation tree.

(3)

By Lemma
4.2
, there exists a small division of
\mathrm{sub}{(c_{1})}
. The size bound is
|\mathrm{Div}{(\mathrm{sub}{(c_{1})})}|\leq\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{C})})
. We add all segments in
\mathrm{Div}{(\mathrm{sub}{(c_{1})})}
to
\mathrm{Div}{(\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)})}
.

The division of a bounded segment is done. The new division of
\mathcal{T}
given by:

\mathrm{Div}^{\prime}{(\mathcal{T})}=(\mathrm{Div}{(\mathcal{T})}\setminus\{\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}\})\cup\mathrm{Div}{(\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)})}

is a feasible one. The size of
\mathrm{Div}{(\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)})}
is bounded by Lemma
5.7
, proved in Appendix
B
.

Lemma 5.7.

The division set
\mathrm{Div}{(\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)})}
for the algebraic decomposition has a size bound of
\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})})
.

Sketch of Proof for Lemma
5.6
.

Given
\mathcal{KT}\rhd\mathcal{T}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{C}
contains at least one bounded
\#(\rho)
. Subdividing
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
gives
\mathrm{Div}{(\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)})}
. For each segment
\mathrm{seg}{(p^{\ddagger},q^{\ddagger})}\in\mathrm{Div}{(\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)})}
, we systematically construct a new KLM component
\mathcal{C}^{\ddagger}\rhd\mathrm{seg}{(p^{\ddagger},q^{\ddagger})}
according to the definition of the capturing relation. We distinguish these new KLM components by two cases:

(1)

The overlapping case.
If
\mathrm{path}{(p^{\ddagger},q^{\ddagger})}\subseteq\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
, the rule sets
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}(\mathcal{C}^{\ddagger})
,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}(\mathcal{C}^{\ddagger})
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}(\mathcal{C}^{\ddagger})
are the subsets of the counterparts in
\mathcal{C}
. If
p^{\ddagger}=p
or
q^{\ddagger}=q
,
\mathcal{C}^{\ddagger}
inherits the configuration constraining equations in
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{Config}}
. We have
\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{C}^{\ddagger})}=O(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{C})})
.

(2)

The disjoint case.
Otherwise,
\mathrm{path}{(p^{\ddagger},q^{\ddagger})}\cap\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}=\varnothing
. The new KLM component
\mathcal{C}^{\ddagger}
contains only rules in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}(\mathcal{C})
or
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}(\mathcal{C})
. We also have
\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{C}^{\ddagger})}=O(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{C})})
.

By Lemma
5.7
, it is immediate that
\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT}^{\prime})}\leq\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})})
.
We analyze the decrease in the rank according to the two cases above. For the disjoint case, it immediately follows that
\iota(\mathcal{C}^{\ddagger})\leq\iota(\mathcal{C}^{\ddagger})-1
. For the overlapping case,
\iota(\mathcal{C}^{\ddagger})\leq\iota(\mathcal{C}^{\ddagger})
, and we provide a sketch of
\mathrm{dim}{(\mathcal{C}^{\ddagger})}<\mathrm{dim}{(\mathcal{C})}
by contradiction. Assume that
\mathrm{dim}{(\mathcal{C}^{\ddagger})}=\mathrm{dim}{(\mathcal{C})}
. We can construct a top cycle in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C}}
with at least one bounded rule, whose effect is in
\mathbb{Q}(\Delta(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C}^{\ddagger}}))
. Therefore, the Parikh image of the bounded rule is represented as a linear combination of unbounded ones, yielding a contradiction. See Appendix
B
for a detailed proof.
∎

Combinatorial Decomposition

The combinatorial decomposition is performed on any nontrivial, fully-constrained and fully-orthogonalized KLM component
\mathcal{C}
with
\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})\neq\varnothing
. The refinement operator is
\mathrm{\mathbf{Decomp_{C}}}
.

Lemma 5.8 (Combinatorial decomposition).

Given a fully-constrained and fully-orthogonalized KLM tree
\mathcal{KT}\rhd\mathcal{T}
. If
\mathcal{KT}
is not
\exp
-pumpable, then there exists another KLM tree
\mathcal{KT}^{\prime}

(1)

\mathcal{KT}^{\prime}\rhd\mathcal{T},

(2)

\mathrm{rank}{(\mathcal{KT}^{\prime})}<\mathrm{rank}{(\mathcal{KT})},

(3)

\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT}^{\prime})}\leq{\mathrm{exp}}(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})})

Lemma
5.9
and Lemma

establish the following fact: if there exists a sufficiently large configuration, then
\exp
-pumpability holds. Actually, it is the contrapositives of Lemmas

and

that are useful for the proof of Lemma

.

Lemma 5.9 (One-sided forward-pumping).

Let
\mathcal{KT}
be fully constrained. For a nontrivial
\mathcal{C}\rhd\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}

\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C}),\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}\notin\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})

, then we can construct a forward-pumping cycle
\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)}
with length bound
|\mathrm{path}{(x,y)}|\leq\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{C})})
.

Lemma 5.10 (Two-sided forward-pumping).

Let
\mathcal{KT}
be fully constrained. For a nontrivial
\mathcal{C}\rhd\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
, if
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})

\mathbf{l}_{n_{i}}\geq\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{C})})

\mathbf{r}_{n_{j}}\geq\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{C})})

\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)}
with length bound
|\mathrm{path}{(x,y)}|\leq\exp(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{C})})
.

The main pumping technique for VASS is the
Rackoff lemma

(
21
)
, based on induction. By appending a short pumping cycle for
(n-1)
counters after a sufficiently large configuration, we obtain a pumping cycle for
n
counters. However, this does not hold directly for GVAS. For
\mathrm{cyc}{(x,y)}

|\mathrm{path}{(x,y)}|
is small) does not imply that the effect is limited. Another choice is to discuss the pumpability on the relation-extended VASS model
(
10
)
, which fits well with the
backwards coverability algorithm
, a straightforward yet computationally expensive brute-force algorithm.

For 1-TGVAS, we combine the techniques described above and exploit the
EXPSPACE

forward-coverability upper bound function
:

Definition 5.11.

Given

Let the identical function be
\mathrm{Id}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N},\mathrm{Id}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}x

(1)

For a left-degenerate rule

(2)

(1)

The cycle is from

to

.

(2)

(3)

By such conversion, it suffices to study the cycles in

6.
Perfect KLM Trees

In this section, given the reachability proble
\mathrm{\mathbf{Reach}}(\mathcal{G},s,t)
, we denote the size of input by
M\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}(\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{G})}+|s|+|t|)
. Binary encoding or unary encoding is irrelevant. We define the following operators with the composition of four basic refinement operators.

\begin{gathered}\mathrm{\mathbf{Clean}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\mathrm{\mathbf{Ortho}}\circ\mathrm{\mathbf{Constr}},\\
\mathrm{\mathbf{Ref_{A}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\mathrm{\mathbf{Clean}}\circ\mathrm{\mathbf{Decomp_{A}}},\\
\mathrm{\mathbf{Ref_{C}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\mathrm{\mathbf{Clean}}\circ\mathrm{\mathbf{Decomp_{C}}}.\end{gathered}

For any
\mathcal{KT}
,
\mathrm{\mathbf{Clean}}(\mathcal{KT})
is another KLM tree that is fully-constrained and fully-orthogonalized. If
\mathcal{KT}
is not unbounded on production rules, we apply
\mathrm{\mathbf{Ref_{A}}}(\mathcal{KT})
; if
\mathcal{KT}
is not
\exp
-pumpable, we apply
\mathrm{\mathbf{Ref_{C}}}(\mathcal{KT})
. In either case, the resulting KLM tree has a smaller rank. Applying the refinement sequence in Figure
4
gives a perfect KLM tree.

Theorem 6.1.

Given an 1-TGVAS
\mathcal{G}
with index
k
and the reachability problem
\mathrm{\mathbf{Reach}}(\mathcal{G},s,t)
. If there exists a complete, nonnegative derivation tree
\mathcal{T}
, then there exists a perfect KLM tree
\mathcal{KT}
such that:

(1)

\mathcal{KT}\rhd\mathcal{T}
,

(2)

\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})}\leq g(M)
for some
g\in\mathscr{F}_{2k-1}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.8
, there exists an initial capturing KLM tree
\mathcal{KT}
such that
\mathcal{KT}\rhd\mathcal{T}
and
\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT})}\leq\exp(M)
. Applying
\{\mathrm{\mathbf{Ref_{A}}},\mathrm{\mathbf{Ref_{C}}}\}^{*}
on
\mathrm{\mathbf{Clean}}(\mathcal{KT})
gives a sequence of KLM trees:
\{\mathcal{KT}_{0},\mathcal{KT}_{1},\dots\}
. We prove the length bound of this sequence by constructing a
(f,m)
-controlled bad sequence:

(1)

Let
f\in\mathscr{F}_{2}
be a proper function that bounds the size increase in
\mathrm{\mathbf{Ref_{A}}}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{Ref_{C}}}
.

(2)

Let
m=f(\exp(M)).

(3)

We define
x_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}\mathrm{rank}{(\mathcal{KT}_{i})}
for
i\geq 1
. We have
x_{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{2k-2}
. It holds that
\left\|{x_{i}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq f^{i}(m)
since we have
\left\|{\mathrm{rank}{(\mathcal{KT}_{i})}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\mathrm{size}{(\mathcal{KT}_{i})}
. By Lemma
5.6
and Lemma
5.8
, the sequence
\{x_{i}\}
is bad and contains no increasing pair.

It follows from Lemma
2.1
that the length function of this sequence (as a function of
m
) is in
\mathscr{F}_{2k-2+2-1}=\mathscr{F}_{2k-1}
. The size of the last element (the perfect KLM tree) can be represented as
g(M)
for some
g\in\mathscr{F}_{2k-1}
.
∎

The following fact is important: a perfect KLM tree
\mathcal{KT}
is a certificate to the reachability problem itself.

Lemma 6.2.

Let
\mathcal{KT}\rhd\mathcal{T}
be a perfect KLM tree. If
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{KT}}
has a solution, then one can construct, without knowing
\mathcal{T}
and merely from
\mathcal{KT}
, another complete, nonnegative derivation tree
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{KT}\rhd\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.

Sketch of Proof.

The same technique as the KLM algorithm for VASS.
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{KT}}
has a minimal solution
\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}
. Denote the sum of the Hilbert basis by
\mathrm{Sol}_{H}
. Our goal is to determine a coefficient
b\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}+b\cdot\mathrm{Sol}_{H}
depicts a complete, nonegative derivation tree.

We derive the segment non-negativity for each nontrivial
\mathcal{C}
independently. Let its forward- and backward-pumping cycles be
\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})
and
\theta_{2}(\mathcal{C})
, respectively. The solution
b\cdot\mathrm{Sol}_{H}
serves mainly to repeat
\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})
and
\theta_{2}(\mathcal{C})
. Suppose
\mathcal{C}
is not orthogonal. For any configuration variable, say
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
, either
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})
or
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
is unbounded. In both cases, choosing
b
large enough allows
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
to be lifted to arbitrarily large values. This guarantees the non-negativity of the entire segment. We refer to Appendix
C
for the detailed choice of
b
and the arrangement of the segment.

∎

The perfectness properties are effectively decidable, according to Lemma
5.5
and Lemma
5.12

7.
Conclusion

We have shown that the reachability problem for
k
-indexed 1-TGVAS is in
\mathbf{F}_{2k}
, improving the previous known
\mathbf{F}_{6k-4}
. We generalized the KLM decomposition method to KLM trees, providing an effective characterization of the
\mathbb{Z}
-reachability of TGVAS.

Future research.
The exact complexity of the reachability problem for 1-GVAS remains open. A
PSPACE
-hardness is obtained in
(
3
)
. An Ackermannian upper bound follows from the reduction from general 1-GVAS to 1-TGVAS
(
2
)
. Achieving a non-Ackermannian upper bound would require non-KLM-based approaches, such as analyzing the smallest derivation tree. For
d
-TGVAS, there is still room for improvement in the existing
\mathbf{F}_{4kd+2k-4d}
upper bound. The approximation method in
(
10
)
is widely applicable. By combining this approximation technique with the KLM tree, we conjecture an upper bound of
\mathbf{F}_{2kd}
. Finally, the validity and necessity of the index as a measure remain open questions; for instance, whether there exists a lower bound parameterized by the index is to be investigated.

i\in[1,m]
. Its configuration
(\mathbf{l}_{n_{i}},\mathbf{r}_{n_{i}})
is the linear i
nterpolation of
(\mathbf{l}_{n_{1}},\mathbf{r}_{n_{1}})
and
(\mathbf{l}_{n_{m}},\mathbf{r}_{n_{m}})
. More precisely, we have:

(\mathbf{l}_{n_{i}},\mathbf{r}_{n_{i}})=\frac{m-i}{m-1}(\mathbf{l}_{n_{1}},\mathbf{r}_{n_{1}})+\frac{i-1}{m-1}(\mathbf{l}_{n_{m}},\mathbf{r}_{n_{m}}).

It is immediate that the configuration of
n_{i}
is nonnegative.

Non-negativity of segments.
Given a segment
\mathrm{seg}{(x,y)}
. Denote the number of nodes as
|\mathrm{seg}{(x,y)}|
. Denote the largest absolute value as
\left\|{\mathrm{seg}{(x,y)}}\right\|
. Intuitively, as long as the configuration of node
x
(or
y

\mathbf{l}_{x},\mathbf{r}_{x}\geq|\mathrm{seg}{(x,y)}|\cdot\left\|{\mathrm{seg}{(x,y)}}\right\|,

then
\mathrm{seg}{(x,y)}
is a nonnegative segment.

We make a review on the pumpability. For a nontrivial
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{KT}

\mathcal{C}

\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})

(3)

If

(4)

If

Denote the forward-pumping cycle by

Proof.

We construct such
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
from a solution of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{KT}}
. Let one of the minimal solution of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{KT}}
be
\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}
. Let the Hilbert basis (i.e., the set of all nonnegative, minimal, homogeneous solutions) be
H
. Recall the definition of the sum of solutions in
H
:

\mathrm{Sol}_{H}=\sum_{\mathrm{sol}\in H}\mathrm{sol}.

Let
\#(\mathrm{sol})
be the vector of all Parikh image variables in solution
\mathrm{sol}
. That is, we omit all configuration variables in
\mathrm{sol}
. Moreover, when considering the Parikh image vector of
\mathcal{C}
, we write
\#(\mathrm{sol})(\mathcal{C})
. We denote the Parikh image vector of
\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C}),\theta_{2}(\mathcal{C})
as
\#(\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})),\#(\theta_{2}(\mathcal{C}))
. We have
\#(\mathrm{Sol}_{H})\geq\mathbf{1}
, since all Parikh image variables are unbounded.

Since
\mathcal{KT}
is fully constrained, for any solution
\mathrm{sol}
and any bounded configuration variable
v
, we have
\mathrm{sol}(v)=\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}(v)
. We construct
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
according to the solution:

\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}+b\cdot\mathrm{Sol}_{H}.

And the rest of the proof is to determine the coefficient
b
.

Step 1: covering the pumping cycles.
For every nontrivial
\mathcal{C}
, denote the number of nodes in
\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})
by
|\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})|
and the largest absolute value of terminals in
\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})
by
\left\|{\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})}\right\|
. We obtain the following bound
b_{1}
by:

b_{1}=\max_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{KT}}\{|\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})|\cdot\left\|{\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})}\right\|+|\theta_{2}(\mathcal{C})|\cdot\left\|{\theta_{2}(\mathcal{C})}\right\|\}+1.

Clearly,
b_{1}\cdot\#(\mathrm{Sol}_{H})
covers the Parikh images of
\#(\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})),\#(\theta_{2}(\mathcal{C}))
for all
\mathcal{C}
.

Step 2: organizing the residual rules.
Consider the Parikh image
\#(\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}})(\mathcal{C})
. Since it fulfills the Euler equations in
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{C}}
, it is the Parikh image of some segment. Denote the segment by
\mathrm{seg}{(x_{3},y_{3})}
or
\gamma(\mathcal{C})
. We have
\sigma(x_{3})=P(\mathcal{C})
and
\sigma(y_{3})=Q(\mathcal{C})
. Let the bound
b_{2}
be:

b_{2}=\max_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{KT}}\{|\gamma(\mathcal{C})|\cdot\left\|{\gamma(\mathcal{C})}\right\|\}+1,

where
|\gamma(\mathcal{C})|,\left\|{\gamma(\mathcal{C})}\right\|
are defined analogously. Moreover, we denote:

b_{3}=b_{1}\cdot(b_{2}+1).

The residual Parikh image is defined as:

\#_{\mathrm{res}}(\mathcal{C})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{def}}}}{{=}}b_{3}\cdot\#(\mathrm{Sol}_{H})(\mathcal{C})-\#(\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C}))-\#(\theta_{2}(\mathcal{C})),

which is the Parikh image of a top cycle in
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{C}}
. To see this, notice that every production rule in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{R}}
appears at least once in
\#_{\mathrm{res}}(\mathcal{C})
since
b_{3}>b_{1}
. Moreover, for any nonterminal in
\#_{\mathrm{res}}(\mathcal{C})
, its in-degree and out-degree coincide. Denote the top cycle with the Parikh image
\#_{\mathrm{res}}(\mathcal{C})
by
\mathrm{cyc}{(x_{4},y_{4})}
or
\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C})
. Since
\sigma(x_{4})
and
\sigma(y_{4})
can be any nonterminal in
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{SCC}}
, we assume that
\sigma(x_{4})=\sigma(y_{4})=Q(\mathcal{C})
. The upper bound of the sizes of
\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C})
for all
\mathcal{C}
is given by:

b_{4}=\max_{\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{KT}}\{|\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C})|\cdot\left\|{\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C})}\right\|\}.

Step 3: the pumping step.
We construct the strongly-connected segment
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
with
\mathcal{C}\rhd\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
by appending segments as:
We are going to decide how many times
\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C}),\theta_{2}(\mathcal{C}),\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C})
are duplicated in order to guarantee the non-negativity of
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
. Let the bound
b_{5}
be:

b_{5}=(b_{2}+b_{4}).

We claim that:

\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}=\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})^{b_{5}}\gamma(\mathcal{C})\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C})^{b_{5}}\theta_{2}(\mathcal{C})^{b_{5}}

is a nonnegative segment. Consequently, we have:

b=b_{3}\cdot b_{5}

according to the Parikh image.

We check the configuration non-negativity with case analysis as follow:

On the orthogonal side.
Consider the left-orthgonal case. All left configurations in
\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
are hard-encoded. For a left-degenerate rule
(_{\mathbf{l}_{X}}X)\rightarrow A(_{\mathbf{l}_{Y}}Y)\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{SCC}}(\mathcal{C})
, we have the certificate rule
A\rightarrow\mathbf{l}_{Y}-\mathbf{l}_{X}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{L}}(\mathcal{C})
. We replace certificate rules in
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
with a complete, nonnegative derivation tree. All configurations to the left of
\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
are nonnegative.

On the non-orthogonal side.
We will illustrate the non-negativity for
\mathcal{C}
which is neither left- nor right-orthogonal. We discuss the boundedness of
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
and
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
. If
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
is unbounded, we have
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}\notin\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})
and:

\displaystyle\mathbf{l}_{p}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}})+b\cdot\mathrm{Sol}_{H}(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}})

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}})+b.

Otherwise, we have
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})
and
\mathbf{l}_{p}=\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}})
. The case is symmetric for
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
.

First, we prove the configuration non-negativity of
\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})^{b_{5}}
. W.l.o.g., suppose that
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
is unbounded and
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}
is bounded. That is,
\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})
and
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}}\notin\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})
. The configuration satisfies:

(\mathbf{l}_{p},\mathbf{r}_{p})\geq(\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}})+b,\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}})).

At the end of
\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})^{b_{5}}
, the configuration is at least:

(\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}})+b-b_{1}\cdot b_{5},\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}})+b_{5}).

The lower bound above is no less than
(b_{1}\cdot b_{2}\cdot b_{5},b_{5})
.

Since
\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})
is a pumping cycle, it is always repeatable if the starting configuration is
(+\omega,\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}})
. In fact, any duplication of
\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})
guarantees the non-negativity of configurations to the right of
\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
. Moreover, by the following inequality:

|\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})|\cdot\left\|{\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})}\right\|\cdot b_{5}\leq b_{1}\cdot b_{5}\leq b_{1}\cdot b_{2}\cdot b_{5},

the
b_{5}
-duplication of
\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})
is also nonnegative for any configuration to the left of
\mathrm{path}{(p,q)}
. We conclude that
\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})^{b_{5}}
is nonnegative.

Because of the same reason, the duplication
\theta_{2}(\mathcal{C})^{b_{5}}
is also nonnegative. In general, regardless of whether the variables
\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{src}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}},\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^{\mathcal{C}}
belong to
\mathrm{Pump}(\mathcal{C})
, the configuration at the end of
\theta_{1}(\mathcal{C})
, or symmetrically, at the beginning of
\theta_{2}(\mathcal{C})
, is at least
(b_{5},b_{5})
.

Next, we show the non-negativity in
\gamma(\mathcal{C})
.The configuration at the beginning of
\gamma(\mathcal{C})
is at least
(b_{5},b_{5})
. Since:

|\gamma(\mathcal{C})|\cdot\left\|{\gamma(\mathcal{C})}\right\|\leq b_{2}\leq b_{5},

the segment
\gamma(\mathcal{C})
is also nonnegative. The configuration at the end of
\gamma(\mathcal{C})
is at least:

(b_{5}-b_{2},b_{5}-b_{2})=(b_{4},b_{4}).

At last, we prove the non-negativity of the duplication
\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C})^{b_{5}}
. Denote these identical cycles by the sequence:

\{(\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C}))_{1},\dots,(\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C}))_{b_{5}}\}.

It suffices to show the non-negativity of
(\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C}))_{1}
and
(\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C}))_{b_{5}}
. At the beginning of
(\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C}))_{1}
, the configuration is at least
(b_{4},b_{4})
. At the end of
(\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C}))_{b_{5}}
, the configuration is at least
(b_{5},b_{5})
. Immediately, it follows from:

|\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C})|\cdot\left\|{\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C})}\right\|\leq b_{4}\leq b_{5}

that both
(\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C}))_{1}
and
(\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C}))_{b_{5}}
are nonnegative.

We have constructed
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
for every nontrivial
\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathcal{C}\rhd\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
.
\mathrm{seg}{(p,q)}
is nonnegative, and its strong connectivity directly follows from the construction: the top cycle
\theta_{3}(\mathcal{C})
covers all nonterminals in
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{SCC}}
.

The rest of the KLM tree is easy to obtain. We construct a trivial segment for each trivial KLM component. We connect KLM components and terminals by the exit rules (
\rho_{\mathrm{D}}
). Let
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
be the complete derivation tree under the solution:

\mathrm{sol}_{\mathrm{min}}+b\cdot\mathrm{Sol}_{H}.

We conclude that
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is nonnegative and
\mathcal{KT}\rhd\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.
