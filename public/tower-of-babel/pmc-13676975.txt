Predicting the Risk of Unplanned Hospital Encounters in Hemodialysis Patients: A Comparison Between Clinicians and a Machine Learning Prediction Algorithm
Authors: Samiksha Singh, Lesley Donovan, Meggie Debnath, Maitreyee Sidhaye, David Dai, Yang Zhu, Mitchell Hughes, Sunghoon Minn, Sanja Neskovic, Jamie Beverley, Tony Antoniou, Ivana Prce, Sebnem Kuzulugil, Michael Fralick, Derek Beaton, Muhammad Mamdani, Ron Wald

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationale & ObjectiveDialysis patients have a higher risk of hospitalization than the age-matched general population. Unplanned hospital encounters (UHEs), including emergency department visits and hospitalizations, may be preventable but are difficult to anticipate. The PPV for the 30-day UHE was comparable between clinicians and the model (0.25 for clinicians vs 0.21 for the ML model; P = 0.54), but sensitivity was nearly three-fold higher for our ML model relative to clinicians (0.24 for clinicians vs 0.73 for the ML model; P < 0.001)LimitationsOur ML-based prediction model was only developed within a cohort comprising HD recipients at a single center, limiting external validation.ConclusionsUsing ML, we were able to anticipate UHE with greater sensitivity than clinician predictions with comparable PPV. Over 500,000 Americans receive dialysis for the treatment of kidney failure.1 The majority of these individuals receive in-center hemodialysis (HD), typically 3 times a week.2 Owing to complications related to dialysis, as well as significant comorbidity burden, these patients have high rates of hospitalization.3 Recurrent hospitalizations are common, with up to 31.1% of patients rehospitalized shortly after hospital discharge.4 Visits to the emergency department, whether leading to subsequent inpatient hospitalization or not, are frequent as well.5 Hospitalizations account for 35% of the total costs associated with the care of HD recipients.6,7

HD recipients frequently interact with the health care system, thus providing a compelling opportunity to anticipate the risk of unplanned hospital encounters (UHEs) by developing prediction tools that may enable preventive interventions. Accordingly, we developed and validated a machine learning (ML) model to quantify the evolving risk of imminent hospitalization or presentation to the emergency department, collectively termed a UHE, in a cohort of HD recipients. The performance of this model was compared against the predictions of clinicians directly involved in the care of these individuals.

Methods

We conducted a cohort study comprising patients treated in the Hemodialysis Program of St Michael’s Hospital, an urban academic health sciences center in Toronto, Canada. The program had the capacity to deliver HD to approximately 300 patients across 2 sites. Our first objective was to create a risk prediction tool for occurrence of a UHE in the subsequent 7 and 30 days, respectively, using routinely collected data. Given the recurrent nature of HD treatments, the dynamic status of patients, and the constant updating of data, the model was designed to generate a revised weekly prediction of a UHE. Second, we compared predictions generated by the model with those of the clinicians directly caring for the patients. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of Unity Health Toronto (REB# 16-371 and REB# 21-006).

Study Population: Model Development

We considered all individuals who received maintenance HD at St Michael’s Hospital from October 2015 to April 2022 (Table S1). Comparison of the model with the predictions of clinicians was conducted from September 2021 to April 2022.

Outcome Measures

The outcomes of interest were UHEs in the subsequent 7 days and 30 days. UHE was defined as an emergency department visit or a nonelective hospitalization at St Michael’s Hospital.

For the model versus clinician comparison study, the metrics for comparison were positive predictive value (PPV) and sensitivity.

Data Sources and Variables

We queried the following 2 data sources for model creation: (1) the St Michael’s Hospital Electronic Data Warehouse, which contains patient-level data for laboratory tests, emergency encounters, and inpatient encounters, and (2) the dedicated dialysis electronic medical record (Renal Insight, Constellation Kidney Group, Markham, Ontario, Canada). Data used for this study fell into the following categories: (1) patient level, including demographics and comorbid conditions; (2) session level, including measurements obtained during each patient’s dialysis sessions, such as pre- and postdialysis weight, pulse, and blood pressure; and (3) dialysis machine--generated data, including summaries of measurements from the dialysis machine during a session, such as hematocrit, venous pressure, and recirculation (Table S2).

Data Preprocessing

Using the dialysis machine data, we calculated summary statistics, such as minimum, maximum, and median values, to represent the data at a session level. We also created a UHE timeline that identified all relevant events for the cohort and created binary variables of UHEs in the next 7 days and 30 days from the dialysis session date. Next, we used International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, codes to create a comorbidity timeline that included a Charlson Comorbidity Index score,8 a weighted score, and binary indicators for various comorbid conditions. With the patient-level and hospitalization data, we created variables that represented information such as recent admission type (eg, emergency visit), recent admission service, number of historical admissions, and time since the last hospital discharge. Finally, we created additional baseline variables that calculated the mean values across various dialysis session measurements, using data from each patient’s first 5 available dialysis sessions. The baseline variables included systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, pulse, ultrafiltration rate, volume of blood processed, fluid removed, and pre and postdialysis weight (Table S2).

We combined patient-level data with data available from the dialysis sessions to form a data set for model development in which each row of data represented information captured at the time of 1 HD session. We excluded sessions that took place in the inpatient setting, following receipt of a kidney transplant, or which did not involve HD (eg, isolated ultrafiltration). After these preprocessing steps, we were left with 154 input features from 170 variables (Table S2).

Machine Learning Model Development

Model training was done using the R technique from R Core Team, 2021,9 using the tidymodels family of packages.10 We used the Gradient Boosting classification modeling approach11---specifically XGBoost12,13---to develop the final prediction model. Both 7-day and 30-day UHEs were deemed important and useful clinical targets for both the modeling and intervention. Ultimately, we decided to deploy an ensemble model that synthesized the risks of UHEs at 7 and 30 days. The ensemble model took the maximum predicted probability for each patient from both models. Our rationales for the ensemble model were as follows: (1) because the model runs weekly to deliver a report to clinicians, the 7-day target information is useful for the weekly clinical interventions; thus the weekly prediction provided for a patient aligns with risk appetites and preferred clinical workflows (ie, planning interventions at the beginning of work week), and (2) both 7-day and 30-day UHEs are clinically relevant prediction windows for future planning in the short term (ie, the subsequent week) and in the longer term (the next month).

The training set consisted of 70% of randomly selected patients. The remaining 30% of patients were reserved for a testing set, which was used to evaluate the model’s performance using unseen data. We used 5-fold cross-validation to tune the XGBoost model hyperparameters using the training data. In the training data, UHEs occurred in 2.9% of, and 11.2% of, patients in the subsequent 7 and 30 days, respectively. In the testing data, UHEs occurred in 3.5% of, and 13.1%, of patients in the subsequent 7 and 30 days. We applied down sampling (technique used in ML model training to help boost or weigh smaller or rare outcomes) to the data when developing both the 7-day and 30-day models, with a ratio of majority to minority class frequency as 5 to account for the class imbalance of the outcome.14

We assessed multiple performance metrics, which are as follows: area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) curve, PPV, and sensitivity. Model selection and deployment was driven by PPV and sensitivity.

Model-Based Predictions Versus Clinician Predictions

We compared clinician-collected and model-generated predictions of whether or not a patient would have a UHE. Six clinicians (1 physician and 5 nurse practitioners), who were primary providers in the HD program, were asked to predict whether their own cohort of patients would experience a UHE. On a weekly basis, clinicians were asked if they thought their patients would be seen in the emergency department or admitted within the next 7 and 30 days on a binary yes and no question. Predictions from both the clinical team and the ML model were concurrently collected between September 2021 and April 2022.

Both sensitivity and PPV were the key metrics in comparing performance of clinicians with algorithm predictions. We provided summaries of performance as well as direct tests of performance. We compared PPV via relative predictive values15 and we tested sensitivity using the McNemar test.16 For both tests, we used the DTComPair package.17

Model Results

With the testing data set, the 7-day and 30-day models had AUROCs of 0.723 and 0.712, respectively (Table 2). The sensitivity for the 7-day and 30-day models was 0.739 and 0.657, respectively (Figs S1 and S2). The PPV for the 7-day model was 0.06 and that for the 30-day model was 0.22. Whereas these 2 models had comparable AUROCs, both the 7-day model and the 30-day model had different strengths. The 7-day model had a much higher sensitivity than the 30-day model (0.739 vs 0.657), but the 30-day model had a much higher PPV than the 7-day model (0.22 vs 0.06). Ultimately, we deployed an ensemble of these 2 models (ie, the maximum predicted value from each of the 7-day and 30-day model), with an emphasis on key clinical performance metrics (PPV and sensitivity). The ensemble model had an AUROC of 0.719, a sensitivity of 0.727, and a PPV of 0.212. The ensemble model was of high practical, clinical, and statistical value, because it had a comparable AUROC with both original models, a comparable sensitivity with the 7-day model, and a comparable PPV with the 30-day model (summary of model results; Table 2).

Clinician Prediction Versus Model Prediction of UHEs

We compared clinician predictions with the ensemble model predictions. We had clinicians make predictions for UHEs at 7 days and 30 days. We then used the ensemble model to make predictions on the same patients with a 7-day outcome and a 30-day outcome. The goal was to have head-to-head predictions of a clinician versus the ensemble model used for deployment.

For 7-day predictions of UHEs, clinicians and the model had sensitivities of 0.250 and 0.625, respectively (P < 0.001), whereas clinicians and the ensemble model had PPVs of 0.140 and 0.066, respectively (P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

Using serially updated data from our hospital database and HD-specific electronic medical records, we developed models that predicted UHEs with reasonable sensitivity but modest PPV. However, the model had considerably greater sensitivity than clinicians in anticipating UHEs in both the subsequent 7 and 30 days, respectively. In brief, the ML model identified nearly 3 of 4 at-risk patients for 30-day UHEs compared with clinicians who identified approximately 1 in 4.

Although the model outperformed clinicians in sensitivity for the 30-day model and had a comparable PPV as well, the 7-day window is clinically relevant to tailor interventions. For example, surveillance and targeted interventions for the 5 highest risk patients on each HD shift may be feasible to implement. There was a designated nurse practitioner and nephrologist assigned to each shift, and our dialysis program had 13 shifts between 2 dialysis centers, so a total of 65 high-risk patients would approximately be flagged each week. The risk scores derived from the XGBoost ML model can be delivered in an email format (termed HemoAlert) to clinicians before the start of their weekly work week. Thus, the 7-day window is an actionable window for intensified interventions as opposed to the 30-day framework.

Potential interventions for high-risk patients include intensive care plan review, increased time spent on clinical rounds for those 5 patients identified as being at highest risk, as well as interdisciplinary referrals. A follow-up phone call is a feasible add-on intervention to the weekly clinical workflow as well for those who received intensive interventions. Based on the anticipated interventions, the clinical team also has planned to ascertain whether the number of UHEs decreases at the 6-month and 1-year mark post deployment of this XGBoost-based ML model.

ML can enhance prognostication in health care, particularly in which traditional statistical models fail to capture complex interacting factors on a particular outcome.18 We aimed to address one of the most significant challenges in the care of maintenance HD recipients, which was as follows: timely recognition of impending hospital encounters. Whereas our work focused on predicting UHEs in a relatively short timeframe, others have looked at a longer time horizon. An initiative at 54 clinics operated by a large dialysis organization used an ML model to identify patients at high risk of frequent hospitalization in the subsequent 12 months.19 A multidisciplinary intervention targeted to such patients led to a hospitalization rate that was lower than that observed in 54 control clinics. Other applications of ML in the HD population include the prediction of mortality,20,21 anticipation of intradialytic hypotension,21,22 and guidance regarding the optimal dry weight.23

A retrospective study by Gallagher et al24 examined the performance of an unplanned readmission risk model for kidney failure patients receiving dialysis who were admitted under general medicine. Their risk model had a greater sensitivity in their higher risk grouping (71% vs 32%) and PPV (33% vs 27%). They have highlighted that tailoring interventions for these high-risk patients receiving dialysis could ultimately affect readmission rates.24

It stands to reason that if even a minority of UHEs can be prevented by anticipating their occurrence, this may have a significant impact on readmissions and thereby morbidity, mortality, and health care costs. Unlike other high-risk populations, maintenance HD recipients have frequent and consistent contact with health care providers. Each HD session generates significant amounts of structured and unstructured data, which can yield important prognostic information. Synthesizing this information in an ML model can provide insights on the risk of clinical deterioration that expert clinicians may not be able to anticipate using the same data despite the benefit of clinical judgement.

From a modeling perspective, we pursued models that could be easily deployed and maintained in practice (by way of MLOps systems and software). Although our modeling strategy was not the most parsimonious, our development choices were the most parsimonious when considering how to operationalize an ML model. Our model and intervention were tailored to the needs of our hospital. Although our model may not be valid outside our organization, our primary goal was to design a system that was tailored for our internal needs. We hope that similar work can be performed in other dialysis programs and across dialysis networks.

Finally, we only captured UHEs that took place at St Michael’s Hospital, which may have led to underascertainment of our primary outcome. Most health encounters of dialysis recipients occur at the institution in which they receive dialysis care; we estimate that ∼10% of our dialysis patients have UHEs in other institutions. In Canada’s universal health care system, there are no restrictions on where patients can seek emergency or inpatient care. Thus, the sensitivity and PPV of our models may be underestimated if patients had a UHE in an institution other than ours.

Our study has several strengths. We studied a large and diverse population of HD recipients. Our models were informed by an extensive array of demographic and clinical data including granular information from dialysis sessions. The frequent rerunning of the model captured the inherently evolving risks affecting HD patients. In addition, we placed the significance of the models in the appropriate context by comparing their performance with predictions made by clinicians who know their patients best. Finally, our outcomes were clinically relevant by allowing 7-day and 30-day horizons for risk prediction. These intervals could conceivably enable adequate lead time to implement an intervention aimed at preventing UHEs.

There are several limitations to consider. The model was developed and validated at a single dialysis program, thereby limiting generalizability to broader settings. The model had reasonable sensitivity but a poor PPV. As such, many patients declared as “high risk” would not be expected to experience an imminent UHE. Although the model performed better than clinician prediction with respect to some performance characteristics, there may be opportunities to leverage other data sources that may enhance model performance. These include unstructured data (eg, health care provider notes) and health records from outside our hospital (eg, primary care).

In conclusion, we developed and validated an ML model for identifying HD recipients at high risk of imminent UHEs. Accurate and timely identification of HD recipients at risk of hospital encounters is imperative as some UHEs are preventable but often difficult to anticipate. Further research is needed to test whether integration of the information generated by these models into clinical care translates into better patient outcomes and ultimately reduces UHEs.
