k k NN-Graph: An adaptive graph model for k k -nearest neighbors
Jiaye Li, Gang Chen, Hang Xu, Shichao Zhang

Abstract
The k k -nearest neighbors ( k k NN) algorithm is a cornerstone of non-parametric classification in artificial intelligence, yet its deployment in large-scale applications is persistently constrained by the computational trade-off between inference speed and accuracy. Existing approximate nearest neighbor solutions accelerate retrieval but often degrade classification precision and lack adaptability in selecting the optimal neighborhood size ( k k ). Here, we present an adaptive graph model that decouples inference latency from computational complexity. By integrating a Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graph with a pre-computed voting mechanism, our framework completely transfers the computational burden of neighbor selection and weighting to the training phase. Within this topological structure, higher graph layers enable rapid navigation, while lower layers encode precise, node-specific decision boundaries with adaptive neighbor counts. Benchmarking against eight state-of-the-art baselines across six diverse datasets, we demonstrate that this architecture significantly accelerates inference speeds, achieving real-time performance, without compromising classification accuracy. These findings offer a scalable, robust solution to the long-standing inference bottleneck of k k NN, establishing a new structural paradigm for graph-based nonparametric learning.

Related Work

The performance of the traditional
k
NN algorithm is largely constrained by two key, yet often decoupled, challenges
9314060
;

;

: determining the optimal value of
k
and ensuring efficient neighbor search. This section provides a review of advances designed to address these limitations, highlighting the fundamental gaps that our proposed adaptive graph model aims to bridge.

2.1
k
Selection

In the
k
NN algorithm, the choice of
k
critically influences both model complexity and generalization. A small
k
(
e.g.,
k=1
) results in a complex decision boundary prone to overfitting, while a large
k
smooths the boundary but risks underfitting by obscuring local patterns. Thus,
k
represents a fundamental bias-variance trade-off. Strategies for determining
k
can be categorized into fixed and adaptive approaches.

Fixed
k
approaches
rely on empirical heuristics, such as setting
k
to the square root of the training size (
\sqrt{n}
), or more systematically, using grid search combined with cross-validation
rahim2022cross
. While robust and computationally straightforward, the primary limitation of these methods lies in their “one-size-fits-all” nature. Assigning a single, globally optimal
k
to all query points overlooks local density variations and non-uniformities in the data distribution
song2025demand
, leading to suboptimal compromise between global complexity and local adaptability.

Adaptive
k
approaches
aim to overcome this limitation by dynamically determining the most appropriate
k
for each query sample based on its local neighborhood characteristics
zhang2017learning
. Existing adaptive strategies include:

1.

Local density and structure based methods
, which adjust
k
by computing local neighborhood statistics such as mean distance or distance variance, preferring a larger
k
in dense regions for robustness and a smaller
k
near class boundaries to maintain local precision
amer2025effective
.

2.

Error estimation methods
, which formalize
k
-selection as a local optimization problem by selecting the
k
that minimizes estimated local classification error or empirical risk
9269360
.

3.

Sparse learning methods
, which represent a modern mainstream approach, determining an adaptive
k
by learning sparse reconstruction relationships among data points
zhang2018novel
;
zhang2014efficient
;
zhang2017learning
;
zhang2016self
.

Although adaptive methods are theoretically superior, they suffer from a critical practical drawback: the local analysis or optimization process must be repeated for every test sample, which introduces significant computational overhead. Crucially, these methods enhance accuracy but
do not address the underlying
O(n)
complexity of the nearest neighbor search itself
.

2.2
Efficient Nearest Neighbor Search and Indexing

The computational cost of the
k
NN algorithm is dominated by the nearest neighbor search, which is exacerbated by adaptive
k
methods that necessitate neighborhood analysis at different scales. To accelerate this procedure, research has traditionally focused on two independent search paradigms:

Exact Nearest Neighbor Search Indices:
Tree-based structures, such as KD-Tree, Ball-Tree, and VP-Tree, partition the data space to ensure accurate identification of the true nearest neighbors. However, their search efficiency deteriorates markedly when the required number of neighbors (
k
) is large. Furthermore, as general-purpose search frameworks, they are not intrinsically coupled with adaptive
k
selection, often leading to suboptimal inference performance due to non-adaptive neighborhood information.

ANN Search:
Techniques like LSH, Product Quantization, and graph-based indices (
e.g.,
HNSW) trade guaranteed accuracy for substantially higher speed
10192056
;
akhil2025zonal
. While effective for large-scale datasets, the inherent approximate nature of these methods means the returned neighbors are not guaranteed to be the true closest points. This limitation
inevitably compromises the final classification accuracy and robustness
of the subsequent
k
NN inference.

2.3
Integrated Search and
k
Selection Paradigms

To address the fundamental trade-off between efficiency and accuracy, recent studies have explored methods that integrate neighbor search and adaptive
k
selection. The
k
*tree method
7898482
represents a key step, organizing training data into a tree where each leaf node precomputes and stores an optimal
k
value for its local subset. Routing a query point to a leaf node simultaneously provides a reduced search space and an adaptively determined
k
.

However,
k
*tree and its variants exhibit a significant architectural limitation: the leaf nodes are often excessively large, containing all samples that share the same “optimal
k
” without finer-grained partitioning. This design implies that the method effectively operates as a reduced-sample
k
NN, still requiring
additional local neighbor searches and distance computations within the leaf nodes
during inference. The structure is not fully exploited to eliminate the search step entirely, leaving substantial room for improvement in achieving near-zero latency.

In contrast to these existing paradigms,
our work proposes a radical shift by decoupling inference latency from the complexity of neighbor aggregation. We do not merely reduce the search space; instead, we integrate the adaptive
k
selection and the weighted voting outcome directly into a hierarchical graph index structure. This architecture enables
logarithmic-time inference
with
instantaneous classification retrieval
upon node traversal, effectively overcoming the fundamental computational limitations of prior
k
NN search and adaptive selection methods.

3
Methods

We present the
Adaptive Graph Model for
k
NN (
k
NN-Graph)
, a framework designed to resolve the fundamental trade-off between inference efficiency and classification accuracy. Unlike traditional approaches that perform expensive neighbor searches during inference, our method introduces a paradigm shift by decoupling the computational complexity of neighborhood determination from the query phase. This is achieved through two tightly integrated components: a
Kernelized Self-Representation Module
that adaptively learns the optimal local topology during training, and a
Hierarchical Graph Indexing Module
that encodes these decisions into a navigable structure for
logarithmic-time retrieval
.

3.2
Adaptive Neighborhood Learning via Kernelized Self-Representation

To capture the complex, nonlinear manifold structure inherent in high-dimensional real-world data, we move beyond linear subspace assumptions. We propose a kernelized self-representation model that reconstructs each data point as a sparse linear combination of other points in a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS).

3.2.1
The Kernelized Optimization Objective

For each training sample
\mathbf{x}_{j}
, we seek a sparse coefficient vector
\mathbf{w}_{j}
by minimizing the reconstruction error combined with an adaptively weighted
\ell_{1}
-norm sparsity constraint. The optimization problem is formulated as:

\min_{\mathbf{w}_{j}}\left\|\mathbf{K}(\cdot,j)-\mathbf{K}\mathbf{w}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda^{(j)}\|\mathbf{w}_{j}\|_{1},\quad\text{s.t. }w_{jj}=0,

(1)

where
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a composite kernel matrix designed to integrate both feature-based proximity and label consistency. It is defined as:

\mathbf{K}_{ij}=\alpha\exp\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)+(1-\alpha)K_{\text{class}}(i,j).

(2)

Here,
K_{\text{class}}(i,j)
takes the value 1 if
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\mathbf{x}_{j}
share the same class label, and a discount factor
\gamma
(set to 0.1) otherwise. This composite kernel ensures that the learned neighbors are not only geometrically close but also semantically consistent.

3.2.2
Density-Aware Adaptive Regularization

A critical innovation of our framework is the dynamic determination of the regularization parameter
\lambda^{(j)}
. A fixed
\lambda
would impose a uniform sparsity level across the dataset, ignoring local density variations. To address this, we introduce a density-aware mechanism.

We first compute a multi-scale local density estimate
\rho(j)
for each sample
\mathbf{x}_{j}
by averaging the inverse distances over various neighborhood scales. Based on this estimate, the adaptive regularization parameter is derived as:

\lambda^{(j)}=\lambda_{\min}+(\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min})\cdot(1-\rho(j)).

(3)

This formulation enforces a simpler model (stronger sparsity, higher
\lambda
) in low-density regions to prevent overfitting, while allowing a more complex model (weaker sparsity, lower
\lambda
) in high-density regions to capture fine-grained local structures. The optimization of Eq. (
1
) is solved efficiently via coordinate descent, as detailed in Algorithm
1
.

3.3
Hierarchical Graph Indexing with Precomputed Decisions

The solution to Eq. (
1
) yields a sparse matrix
\mathbf{W}
, where the non-zero entries in column
j
define the optimal neighbor set
N^{*}(j)=\{i:w_{ij}\neq 0\}
and the optimal neighbor count
K_{j}=|N^{*}(j)|
. Unlike conventional methods that stop at neighborhood identification, our framework proceeds to construct a specialized index.

3.3.1
Embedding Precomputed Intelligence into HNSW

We construct a HNSW graph, but with a fundamental architectural modification. Instead of storing only raw data, each node in our graph is an “intelligent” container storing the precomputed classification result.

For every training node
j
, we compute a
weighted consensus label

\hat{y}_{j}
during the training phase:

\hat{y}_{j}=\operatorname{weighted\_mode}\left(\{(y_{j},w_{\text{self}})\}\cup\{(y_{i},w_{ij})\mid i\in N^{*}(j)\}\right),

(4)

where
w_{\text{self}}
is a self-reinforcement weight derived from the maximum neighbor weight.

The HNSW structure is then built using these enriched nodes. The hierarchy consists of multiple layers where lower layers encode the precise, learned connectivity
N^{*}(j)
, and upper layers provide long-range links for logarithmic-time navigation.

3.3.2
Logarithmic-Time Inference Mechanism

During the inference phase, the computational complexity is strictly decoupled from the training set size
n
for the purpose of classification logic. For a query
q
:
1. The system traverses the HNSW graph starting from the top layer to locate the nearest node
\mathbf{x}_{nearest}
(Algorithm
2
).
2. Upon reaching this node, the system
immediately
retrieves the stored precomputed label
\hat{y}_{nearest}
.
3. This label is returned as the final prediction.

This design eliminates the need for calculating distances to
k
neighbors or performing a voting process at runtime, effectively reducing the classification operation to a graph lookup.

3.5
Theoretical Guarantees

We provide theoretical insights into the structural stability of the learned graph. Ensuring that the sparse learning process does not result in a fragmented graph is critical, as
the navigability of the HNSW index relies on the connectivity of the underlying topology
. Disconnected components or isolated nodes would trap the greedy search algorithm in local optima, preventing the query from reaching its true nearest neighbor.

Proposition 1

(Connectivity Stability)
.

Let
\mathbf{K}
be the kernel matrix and
\mathbf{k}_{j}
be its
j
-th column. If the minimum regularization parameter satisfies
\lambda_{\min}<\min_{j}\|\mathbf{K}(\cdot,j)\|_{2}^{2}
, then every sample
\mathbf{x}_{j}
is guaranteed to have at least one neighbor (
w_{ij}\neq 0
for some
i
), preventing the emergence of isolated nodes.

Proof.

(Sketch) Consider the objective function for sample
\mathbf{x}_{j}
. The trivial zero solution
\mathbf{w}_{j}=\mathbf{0}
yields a reconstruction cost equal to the squared norm of the feature vector in RKHS,
i.e.,
\|\mathbf{k}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}
.
Consider activating a connection to the most similar sample
i
(where the kernel similarity
K_{ij}
is maximal). This reduces the reconstruction error significantly.
Provided that the regularization penalty
\lambda^{(j)}
is strictly smaller than this reduction in reconstruction error (which is enforced by the bound
\lambda_{\min}<\min_{j}\|\mathbf{k}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}
), the optimization objective will always achieve a lower value with a non-zero weight than with the zero vector.
Consequently, the optimal solution must contain at least one non-zero element, guaranteeing that
\mathbf{x}_{j}
is connected to the graph.
∎

3.6
Complexity Analysis

To theoretically quantify the efficiency gains of the proposed
k
NN-Graph framework, we analyze the time complexity of the inference phase and compare it with traditional
k
NN and standard Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) methods.

3.6.1
Computational Cost of Inference

Let
n
be the number of training samples,
d
be the feature dimensionality, and
k
be the number of neighbors.

Traditional
k
NN:
The brute-force
k
NN algorithm computes the distance between the query and every training sample, followed by a sorting or selection operation. The complexity is bounded by:

T_{\text{$k$NN}}=O(n\cdot d+n\log k).

(5)

This linear dependence on
n
creates the fundamental scalability bottleneck for large datasets.

Standard Graph-based ANN (
e.g.,
HNSW):
These methods utilize a graph structure to navigate to the candidate region in logarithmic time. However, to ensure retrieval precision, they typically perform a beam search maintaining a candidate pool of size
k^{\prime}
(where
k^{\prime}\geq k
), compute exact distances for these candidates, and rank them. The complexity is approximately:

T_{\text{ANN}}=O(\bar{m}\log n\cdot d+k^{\prime}\cdot d),

(6)

where
\bar{m}
is the average degree of the graph nodes and
k^{\prime}
represents the size of the dynamic candidate list (
e.g., efSearch
parameter). The term
k^{\prime}\cdot d
reflects the overhead of maintaining the priority queue and verifying candidates.

Proposed
k
NN-Graph:
Our method leverages the hierarchical graph for navigation but fundamentally eliminates the post-retrieval computation. Upon reaching the nearest node via greedy search, the precomputed consensus label is retrieved in
O(1)
time. The inference complexity is strictly limited to the graph traversal:

T_{\text{Ours}}=O(\bar{m}\log n\cdot d).

(7)

Key Advantage:
Crucially, our method removes the dependence on the neighbor count
k
(or candidate pool
k^{\prime}
) during inference. In scenarios with large
k
(
e.g.,
in dense regions) or high dimensionality, the elimination of the local distance computation and voting term (
k\cdot d
) results in significant speedups compared to standard ANN methods, effectively rendering the inference cost independent of the decision complexity.

4.3
Experimental Implementation and Evaluation Protocols

4.3.1
Validation Protocol

To ensure the statistical reliability of our results, we employed a rigorous
10-fold cross-validation
protocol across all datasets. For each experimental run, the dataset was randomly partitioned into ten stratified subsets; nine were utilized for training, while the remaining fold served as the test set. This process was iterated ten times to ensure that every fold functioned as the validation target exactly once. The final reported performance metrics represent the
average outcomes
aggregated over these ten independent runs, minimizing the bias introduced by random data splitting.

4.3.2
Evaluation Metrics

To provide a holistic assessment of model performance, particularly given the potential class imbalance in real-world datasets, we utilized a suite of
macro-averaged metrics
alongside standard classification accuracy. Unlike micro-averaging, macro-averaging treats all classes equally regardless of their sample size, providing a robust estimate of the model’s generalization capability across minority classes. The specific metrics are defined as follows:

•

Average Classification Accuracy:
Measures the global proportion of correctly classified samples. While intuitive, it serves primarily as a baseline indicator of overall consistency.

•

Macro-Precision:
Evaluates the model’s discriminative precision by averaging the precision scores calculated independently for each class. This metric reflects the model’s ability to minimize false-positive errors across diverse categories.

•

Macro-Recall:
Assesses the coverage completeness by averaging the recall scores of individual classes. A high macro-recall indicates that the model effectively identifies positive samples across all categories, minimizing the miss rate.

•

Macro-F1 Score:
computed as the harmonic mean of Macro-Precision and Macro-Recall. This is the critical metric for imbalanced datasets, as it penalizes models that achieve high accuracy by merely overfitting to majority classes, ensuring a balanced trade-off between precision and sensitivity.

4.3.3
Experimental Design

Our empirical evaluation is structured into four distinct phases to comprehensively validate the proposed framework:

1.

Comparative Classification Analysis:
Benchmarking the proposed method against state-of-the-art baselines using the aforementioned multi-dimensional metrics to verify classification effectiveness.

2.

Inference Efficiency Analysis:
Quantifying the computational advantage of our method by comparing the average inference latency on test data against all baseline algorithms.

3.

Ablation Study:
Deconstructing the framework to isolate the specific contribution of the
Adaptive Neighborhood Learning
module, contrasting the proposed data-driven topology against a static graph construction baseline (HNSW).

4.

Convergence Analysis:
Empirically investigating the optimization stability and convergence behavior of the proposed kernelized self-representation learning module.

Conclusion

In this work, we have addressed the persistent efficiency-accuracy dilemma in non-parametric classification by introducing the
k
NN-Graph
framework. This model represents a structural paradigm shift: by decoupling inference latency from computational complexity, we have transformed the
k
NN algorithm from a theoretically attractive but computationally prohibitive method into a scalable solution for real-time applications.

The core innovation lies in the synergistic integration of a
kernelized self-representation model
with a
hierarchical graph index
. This architecture allows the system to autonomously learn optimal local topologies and precompute complex decision boundaries during the training phase. Consequently, the inference process is redefined from an exhaustive search into a rapid, logarithmic-time graph navigation task.

Extensive benchmarking across diverse datasets demonstrates that this “precompute-and-navigate” strategy achieves order-of-magnitude accelerations in inference speed while simultaneously maintaining or even surpassing the classification precision of existing methods. By effectively dismantling the computational bottleneck, this work bridges the gap between theoretical innovation and engineering implementation. Ultimately, it establishes a robust and scalable foundation for the next generation of similarity-based learning systems, enabling the efficient deployment of non-parametric methods in high-dimensional and time-critical environments.
