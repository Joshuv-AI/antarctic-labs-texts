Thermophysical Response Patterns of Pore Structures in Coals of Different Ranks
Authors: Chen Guo, Xinhang Yu, Jinxiao Yang, Junzhe Gao, Jiang Gou, Xiankuo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the differential evolution characteristics of pore structures in coals of various ranks under heat treatment conditions is fundamental for developing coal thermal mining technologies. This study systematically collected coal samples from the Zhangjiamao Mine in the Ordos Basin, the Dahebian Mine in western Guizhou, and the Sihe Mine in the Qinshui Basin, representing a series of samples with different coal ranks. Through drying, saturation, and heating processes, combined with low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) testing and pore fractal geometry theory, the effects of heat treatment at 200 °C, 300 °C, and 400 °C on NMR T 2 spectra, pore size distribution, two-dimensional relaxation distribution, and pore fractal dimensions of coal samples were investigated. The results indicate that after heat treatment, the porosity of coal samples across all coal ranks increased, with low-rank coal exhibiting the greatest increase, suggesting that low-rank coal pore structures possess the strongest temperature sensitivity. When the temperature reached 300 °C, the porosity of high-rank coal began to increase significantly, suggesting that its pore structure requires a higher activation temperature for thermal evolution. The DHB coal sample exhibited caking properties, and significant thermal expansion of the coal matrix after heating led to a smaller porosity at 300 °C compared to ZJM and SH samples. Heat treatment promoted the transformation of micropores into mesopores and macropores. Low-rank coal was characterized by an increase in mesopore volume, the pore connectivity of medium-rank coal was optimized, and the macropore volume of high-rank coal increased markedly with rising temperature. After heat treatment, the fractal dimensions of mesopores and macropores generally decreased, indicating reduced pore surface irregularity and enhanced connectivity, which facilitates fluid seepage. This study reveals the thermophysical differential response patterns of pores in coals of different ranks. Heat treatment affects coal pores though expansion, increase in number, and improved connectivity, creating favorable conditions for the fluid product during thermal extraction. The research findings provide theoretical and parametric support for geological site selection and process control of thermal mining, thereby promoting the development of thermal extraction technologies for coal resources.

Introduction

Thermal recovery of coal resources has broad application prospects for achieving clean and efficient utilization of coal. The pore structure of coal determines the seepage conditions and production efficiency of pyrolysis fluids. The evolution of pores during pyrolysissuch as expansion, interconnection, merging, or the formation of new poresrestructures the pore system, thereby influencing the production process and efficiency. Pore evolution behaviors vary significantly at different temperatures: at low temperatures, slight expansion of micropores and small pores predominates, while at medium to high temperatures, the massive release of volatiles leads to the formation of more interconnected pores and even macroscopic fractures ultimately affecting thermal extraction efficiency. Furthermore, due to differences in pore structure, mechanical properties, and material composition among coals of different ranks, the evolution of pore structure with temperature during heat treatment follows distinct patterns. Recent investigations of thermally stressed porous media have shown that elevated temperatures significantly modify pore-scale transport properties and connectivity patterns under sustained thermal gradients. This finding further underscores the importance of conducting in-depth investigations into the evolution patterns of coal pore structures under complex thermodynamic conditions. Therefore, uncovering the differential responses of pore structures in coals of varying ranks under thermal treatment conditions has become a core scientific issue in advancing clean and efficient thermal extraction technologies.

Traditional methods for characterizing coal pore structure, such as scanning electron microscopy, mercury intrusion porosimetry, and gas adsorption, can achieve static characterization but exhibit significant limitations. Scanning electron microscopy struggles to capture the dynamic evolution of pores, mercury intrusion may cause micropore damage due to high-pressure intrusion, and gas adsorption fails to simultaneously reflect fluid mobility and pore connectivity. , Consequently, these methods are inadequate for accurately characterizing the dynamic evolution of pores under varying thermal conditions. In recent years, low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) technology has been widely applied in unconventional oil and gas accumulation and development research due to its advantages of being rapid, nondestructive, and capable of real-time monitoring, providing a technical means to reveal the dynamic evolution of coal pores during thermal treatment. Yao et al. identified a strong correlation between the transverse relaxation time T 2 of coal and its pore structure, establishing a pore distribution calculation model based on T 2 spectra; Mandelbrot introduced topological fractal theory, offering an effective tool for describing rock physical properties via NMR methods; , Zhou et al. further revealed the relationship between the fractal dimension of pore structure and seepage space, providing a basis for evaluating permeability; Zhao et al. derived a fractal geometric expression for NMR T 2 values; Lin et al. combined NMR technology with fractal analysis to investigate the impact of thermal treatment on coal pore properties. Recent data-driven approaches have further emphasized the importance of integrating microstructural characterization with predictive modeling frameworks in clay-dominated porous systems. This suggests that for coal, as a porous medium, a single characterization method or static analysis is no longer sufficient to meet the research needs of complex pore evolution. There is an urgent need to integrate multiscale experimental data with intelligent prediction models to deeply reveal the pore structure response mechanisms of different coal ranks under heat treatment conditions.

Although existing studies have preliminarily explored the impact of thermal treatment on coal pores, they have predominantly focused on a single coal rank or a single temperature, failing to systematically reveal the differential responses of pore structures in coals of different ranks under varying temperatures. Therefore, systematically investigating the pore evolution patterns of different coal ranks at various pyrolysis temperatures, and uncovering the transformation of pore types, changes in porosity, and evolution of pore fractal dimensions under thermal treatment, are urgent needs for refining the theory of thermal evolution of coal pores and promoting the fluidized extraction of coal resources.

Based on this, this study takes low-, medium-, and high-rank coals as research objects, employs thermal treatment as the primary method, and utilizes NMR technology to examine the T 2 spectrum characteristics, pore size distribution, two-dimensional relaxation distribution, and fractal dimension variations of coal samples at 200 °C, 300 °C, and 400 °C. The research aims to reveal the thermophysical response patterns and mechanisms of pore structures in different coal ranks, providing precise parameter support and a theoretical basis for the effective production of pyrolysis fluids, thereby advancing the development of thermal mining technologies for coal resources.

Samples and Methods

Coal Sample Collection and Preparation

The experimental coal samples were collected from the Zhangjiamao Coal Mine in the northern Ordos Basin, the Dahebian Coal Mine in western Guizhou, and the Sihe Coal Mine in the southern Qinshui Basin. The primary reasons for selecting these areas are as follows: (1) The coal ranks in these three regions differ significantly, and their coal characteristics are highly representative. (2) These areas are all coal-rich regions in China with substantial resource reserves and typical occurrence conditions, making them of significant engineering application value for studying the thermal mining of coal resources.

The samples from these three regions were designated as ZJM, DHB, and SH, respectively. From each region, three cylindrical plug samples with a diameter of 2.5 cm and a length of 2.5–5 cm were prepared. Taking the ZJM coal sample as an example, the three plug samples were labeled ZJM-1, ZJM-2, and ZJM-3 for heat treatment and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments at 200 °C, 300 °C, and 400 °C, respectively. The experimental temperatures were selected based on the following considerations: 400 °C corresponds to the pyrolysis temperature range for medium- to low-rank coals, at which coal tar is abundantly generated, marking the threshold for in situ pyrolysis of tar-rich coal. Higher temperatures could induce significant melting in caking bituminous coals of medium rank, potentially interfering with measurements such as NMR. Additionally, due to the limited scope of this study, it is impractical to simulate all temperature points. Therefore, this study focuses on heat treatment simulations at temperatures between 200 and 400 °C.

The pulverized coal generated during sample preparation was sieved to specified particle sizes for proximate analysis, maceral composition identification, and maximum vitrinite reflectance measurement. The test results indicate that the ZJM, DHB, and SH coal samples correspond to low-rank, medium-rank, and high-rank coal, respectively. The ZJM sample exhibits high inertinite and moisture content, along with low mineral content and ash yield. The SH sample shows high vitrinite and fixed carbon content. The DHB coal sample has the highest ash yield and volatile matter yield, which is associated with its high mineral and liptinite content, respectively (Table ). NMR Experiments

By analyzing the relaxation characteristics of nuclear magnetic signals, multidimensional information on the pore structure can be obtained. When a core sample is placed in a uniform static magnetic field (B0), the hydrogen-rich nuclei in the fluid within the core interact with the magnetic field, generating a magnetization vector (MZ). At this point, applying a radiofrequency pulse (B 1) at the Larmor frequency perpendicular to the static magnetic field direction induces a nuclear magnetic resonance signal, as illustrated in Figure . This signal contains information such as frequency, phase, and amplitude. The collected nuclear magnetic signals can be inverted to obtain the T 2 spectrum of the sample. Since there is a corresponding relationship between the T 2 spectrum and the pore structure, parameters such as porosity, permeability, movable fluid saturation, irreducible fluid saturation, and pore size distribution can be derived from the T 2 spectrum.

T 2 relaxation time is influenced by several factors, and its expression is given by 1 1T2=1T2S+1T2B+1T2D where T 2S is the surface relaxation time; T 2B is the bulk relaxation time; T 2D is the diffusion relaxation time. The transverse relaxation time is typically dominated by the surface relaxation time; therefore, the T 2 relaxation time can be approximately expressed as 2 1T2≈1T2S=ρ2SV=ρ2FSr where ρ2 is the transverse surface relaxivity; S is the pore surface area; V is the pore volume; r is the pore radius; and F S is the geometric shape factor (F S = 3 for spherical pores; F S = 2 for cylindrical pores). As shown in eq , T 2 is proportional to the pore radius r, meaning that a larger pore radius results in a longer relaxation time.

The surface relaxivity ρ2 is a critical parameter for converting nuclear magnetic resonance T2 spectra into pore size distributions, and its value directly affects the accuracy of quantitative pore size results. In this study, a combination of mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) and NMR technology was employed to determine ρ2. The pore-specific surface area of coal samples was obtained through MIP experiments, while the T 2 distribution was measured via NMR. Based on eq , the surface relaxivity of coal samples at various ranks was inversely calculated to ensure the reliability of pore size conversion. The experimental results indicate that the surface relaxation rates of the coal samples are as follows: 3.960 nm/ms for the ZJM coal sample, 1.233 nm/ms for the DHB coal sample, and 2.563 nm/ms for the SH coal sample.

The signal amplitude in the T2 spectrum obtained from NMR testing is positively correlated with the quantity of pores at the corresponding scale, with the peak intensity reflecting the total volume of pores of that size. The area under the T2 spectral peak represents the pore volume. Furthermore, the continuous distribution of the T2 spectrum indicates the presence of multiscale pores within the coal sample, along with favorable connectivity between pores and microfractures.

Results and Discussion

T 2 Spectrum Characteristics and Pore Size Distribution

T 2 Spectrum Characteristics

The test results, as shown in Figures , , and , indicate that the coal samples exhibit 2–3 main peaks, with the P1 and P2 peak having a relatively large signal amplitude, corresponding to adsorption pores and seepage pores, respectively, while the P3 peaks are comparatively smaller, corresponding to fractures.

NMR curves of the ZJM coal sample before and after heat treatment at different temperatures.

NMR curves of the DHB coal sample before and after heat treatment at different temperatures.

NMR curves of the SH coal sample before and after heat treatment at different temperatures.

As shown in Figure , the NMR curve of the ZJM coal sample exhibits a distinct bimodal characteristic. The peak P3 is relatively small, indicating that the fractures are less developed. A comparison of the NMR curves before and after heat treatment reveals a decrease in the peak intensity of P1, suggesting a reduction in the number of adsorption pores. In contrast, the peak intensity of P2 increases, indicating an increase in the number of seepage pores. Additionally, the position of the P1 peak shifts to the left, while that of the P2 peak shifts to the right, demonstrating that after heat treatment, the pore size and total pore volume of adsorption pores in the low-rank coal decrease, while those of seepage pores and fractures increase.

As shown in Figure , the NMR curve of the DHB coal sample exhibits a continuous trimodal pattern with well-defined peaks, indicating favorable development of all pore types and good connectivity among adsorption pores, seepage pores and fractures. A comparison of the NMR curves before and after heat treatment reveals that at 200 °C, the P1 peak shows no significant change, while the P2 peak broadens, suggesting that the number of adsorption pores remains relatively stable, whereas the number of seepage pores increases. The intensity of the P3 peak also increases, indicating a rise in the number of fractures. At 300 °C, the development of the P1 peak weakens, the P2 peak strengthens significantly, and the P3 peak shows a slight enhancement. These results demonstrate that after heat treatment, the total volume of adsorption pores in medium-rank coal decreases, while the total volume of seepage pores and fractures increases.

As shown in Figure , the NMR curve of the SH coal sample exhibits a distinct bimodal characteristic. The signal amplitude of the P3 peak is very low, indicating that high-rank coal is predominantly characterized by the development of adsorption pores and seepage pores, while fractures are essentially undeveloped. Comparing the NMR curves before and after heat treatment, the P1 peak value decreases after heat treatment, while the peak value of the P2 peak increases significantly, suggesting a reduction in adsorption pores and an increase in seepage pores. As the treatment temperature rises, the peak value of the P3 peak gradually increases. The peak proportions of the P3 peak after treatment at 200 °C, 300 °C, and 400 °C are 0.369%, 0.371%, and 5.52%, respectively, indicating that the fractures volume of the high-rank coal sample increases with higher treatment temperatures. Additionally, the signal amplitude of the trough between the P1 and P2 peaks shows a decreasing trend after heat treatment. When the temperature reaches 300 °C or higher, the separation between the P1 and P2 peaks becomes more pronounced, suggesting that the connectivity between adsorption pores and seepage pores deteriorates after heat treatment.

Two-Dimensional Relaxation Distribution Density

Different hydrogen-containing fluids or different phases of the same fluid do not exhibit independent T 1 and T 2 values but rather possess characteristic T 1/T 2 ratios and two-dimensional distribution patterns. The two-dimensional data set acquired by measuring two independent time variables (T 1 and T 2) integrates the evolution information on the spin system under T 1 and T 2 relaxation mechanisms. Two-dimensional nuclear magnetic resonance relaxation distribution (T 1–T 2) enables rapid identification of multiphase fluids within matrix pores and reflects the distribution of pores at different scales. , Under the influence of diffusion coupling effects exhibited by hydrogen-containing substances in coal under saturated conditions, the T 1 and T 2 spectra of hydrogen atoms in free water within inorganic pores and bound water within organic pores overlap, making it impossible for the T 2 spectrum to distinguish the distribution locations of various hydrogen-containing substances in coal. In contrast, the two-dimensional T 1–T 2 spectrum obtained through inversion can more accurately differentiate between different hydrogen-containing fluids and different phases of the same hydrogen-containing fluid (e.g., bound water, capillary water, and free water). The Effect of Heat Treatment on the Fractal Dimension of Pore Structure in Coal Samples

Fractal dimension is a method for quantitatively characterizing the irregularity of pore surfaces, which can reflect the heterogeneity and complexity of the internal pore structure of coal. Fractal dimension can quantitatively describe pore surface roughness, the complexity of pore distribution, and spatial irregularity, serving as a key parameter reflecting the heterogeneity of pore structures. Traditional pore characterization methods mostly rely on macroscopic statistics or averaged parameters, which struggle to capture the variations in pore distribution and complexity across different scales. In contrast, fractal dimension enables a unified quantification of pore structures across multiple scales, offering a more refined representation of pore evolution characteristics and addressing the limitations of conventional methods in describing pore heterogeneity.

The fractal dimension is an effective parameter for quantitatively characterizing the irregularity and complexity of pore structures. The fractal model based on the NMR T 2 spectrum assumes a linear relationship between T 2 and pore size, with the pore fluid being in a free diffusion state. As shown in Table , the fractal dimension D 1 of the micropore section is entirely negative, which clearly contradicts the fundamental definition in fractal geometry theory that the fractal dimension should lie between 2 and 3.

The occurrence of negative values is speculated to be due to the following reasons: in the micropore stage, the adsorption force on the surface of coal rock pores and fractures becomes the dominant force, restricting the free flow of fluids and the self-diffusion of fluid molecules. As a result, both T 2B and T 2D have significant and non-negligible effects on nuclear magnetic resonance relaxation time. This leads to a deviation from the linear relationship between T 2 and pore size. Consequently, the logarithmic relationship between cumulative pore volume fraction and relaxation time no longer follows the simple linear model described by eq in the micropore range, resulting in a distorted fitted slope (3-D) and the generation of a physically meaningless negative value. In other words, D 1 here is merely a mathematical fitting parameter and does not reflect the true structural characteristics of micropores. In contrast, the fractal dimensions of the mesopore (D 2) and macropore (D 3) segments range between 2.75 and 2.99. Notably, the fractal dimension of macropores is very close to 3, with both falling within a reasonable range. This discrepancy indicates that as the aperture size increases, adsorption pores gradually transform into seepage pores. Consequently, the surface adsorption force weakens, and the pore space becomes sufficient for fluids to exhibit bulk-phase properties, and its impact on the free flow of fluids and the self-diffusion of fluid molecules decreases. Consequently, the influence of T 2B and T 2D on relaxation time becomes negligible. Although the R 2 values in some mesopore segments are slightly lower, potentially due to mixed pore types (combining primary and secondary pores) or lower signal-to-noise ratios, the overall trend remains meaningful.

Therefore, this paper primarily discusses the influence of heat treatment on the structural complexity of effective percolation pores (mesopores and macropores) based on D 2 and D 3. As shown in Table and Figures –, After heat treatment, the fractal dimensions of mesopores and macropores in coal samples generally decrease to varying degrees, suggesting a reduction in pore size heterogeneity and a simplification of the pore structure. Overall, thermal effects optimize the pore structure by expanding pores and enhancing connectivity, thereby reducing the tortuosity of fluid migration pathways and improving both the connectivity and permeability of the pore system, thermal effects lead to a decrease in the complexity of the configuration between pores and throats, enhance connectivity among different pores, and facilitate the production of pyrolysis fluids.

Origins of Varying Thermal Responses Among Coal Samples

Low-rank coal contains a significant amount of oxygen-containing functional groups, such as carboxyl (−COOH), hydroxyl (−OH), and methoxy (−OCH3). These functional groups exhibit poor thermal stability. At temperatures around 200 °C, although the macromolecular (aromatic nuclei) of the coal remains relatively stable, the oxygen-containing functional groups and side chains begin to undergo cleavage and decomposition. This decomposition leads to the rearrangement of the organic macromolecular structure, resulting in significant changes in its pore structure. Additionally, the low-rank coal sample is rich in inertinite, which typically retains the cellular cavities of plant structures and inherently contains a large number of macropores and mesopores. These existing pore systems provide favorable diffusion pathways for heat transfer and the escape of gases generated during pyrolysis. When heated, heat can more easily penetrate the interior of coal particles through the pore network, thereby accelerating the thermal decomposition reactions.

In high-rank coal, the oxygen-containing functional groups have largely fallen off, and the aromatic structures are highly condensed, resulting in very stable chemical properties (Figure ). A higher temperature (around 400 °C) is required to disrupt its internal structure. Its pore structure, which is dominated by micropores, is also less prone to collapse or expansion due to thermal stress.

The high mineral content in the medium-rank coal sample also significantly influences their thermal evolution behavior. The primary mineral types are clay minerals and quartz. At around 300 °C, clay minerals undergo a dehydroxylation reaction, losing crystalline water, a process that leads to the reorganization of the mineral lattice. Quartz, acting as rigid particles, can induce microfissures through interfacial stress upon heating, resulting in increased porosity. The combined action of these two mineral types, coupled with the pronounced melting behavior and thermal expansion of medium-rank coal, determines its unique pore response characteristics after heat treatment.

Conclusions

(1)After heat treatment, the porosity of all coal samples increased, and the magnitude of porosity change gradually increased with rising heat treatment temperatures. At the same heat treatment temperature, the ZJM coal sample exhibited the greatest increase in porosity, indicating that the pore structure of low-rank coal is more sensitive to temperature. When the heat treatment temperature reached 300 °C, the porosity of the SH coal sample began to increase significantly, suggesting that the thermal evolution of high-rank coal pore structure requires a higher activation temperature. Under the 300 °C heat treatment condition, the porosity increase of the DHB coal sample was lower than that of the ZJM and SH coal samples, which can be attributed to the caking property and thermal expansibility of medium-rank coal. Medium-rank coal exhibits the poorest thermal stability, low-rank coal shows moderate thermal stability, and high-rank coal has the best thermal stability. High-volatile bituminous coal undergoes thermal melting and swelling when heated, which, under in situ conditions, can cause severe damage to the coal structure and block pores, hindering in situ pyrolysis development. In contrast, low-rank coals and high-rank coals (anthracite), which lack expansibility and caking properties, have better potential for stable thermal mining.(2)After heat treatment, the coal samples exhibited changes such as increased porosity, a reduction in the volume of micropores, and an increase in the volume of mesopores and macropores, indicating that the heat treatment has pore-expanding and pore-enhancing effects. After heat treatment, the fractal dimensions of mesopores and macropores in coals of different ranks showed a decreasing trend, indicating a reduction in the irregularity of pore surfaces, a simplification of pore structures, and enhanced pore connectivity.(3)The modification effect of heat treatment on the pore structure of coal is constrained by coal rank. Significant pore enlargement and expansion effects occur in low-rank coal at 200 °C, medium-rank coal at 300 °C, and high-rank coal at 400 °C, indicating that higher-rank coals require a higher temperature threshold to achieve effective pore modification. This pattern can provide a basis for setting targeted heat treatment temperatures to improve the seepage capacity of coal reservoirs across different ranks, thereby offering insights for optimizing thermal recovery processes in coal resource extraction. However, this study did not include permeability measurements, which limits the understanding of fluid transport behavior in heat-treated coal samples. Therefore, further seepage experiments are necessary to reveal the permeability evolution of coal after thermal treatment and to quantitatively establish the relationship between pore structure modification and permeability changes. In addition, thermal simulation experiments at higher temperatures also need to be conducted.
