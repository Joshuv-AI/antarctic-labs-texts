Diagnostic and Prognostic Value of Epigenetic Markers in Neurological Diseases. A Narrative Review
Authors: Olga Grodzka, Justyna Wołos, Małgorzata Kupc, Sara Ahmadova, Ceren Eyileten, Izabela Domitrz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neurological disorders are a leading cause of disability and death, yet sensitive and specific biomarkers remain limited. Epigenetic modifications link genetic background with environmental influences and may improve diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment stratification. The study aimed to summarise the current knowledge on epigenetics in neurological diseases, primarily focusing on their diagnostic value. We performed a narrative review with elements of systematic methodology. PubMed was searched up to November 21, 2025, using broad neurology and epigenetics terms followed by disease-specific queries. Sixty-five original studies on epigenetic markers with diagnostic or prognostic utility in neurological diseases were included. Across neurological disorders, multiple disease-associated epigenetic signatures have been reported. In neurodegenerative diseases, deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) methylation patterns, histone modifications, and non-coding ribonucleic acid (RNA) profiles discriminate patients from controls and correlate with disease stage and clinical outcomes. In multiple sclerosis, methylation changes in genes involved in disease pathogenesis and characteristic microRNAs may distinguish relapsing from progressive forms and relate to disability. In ischemic stroke and transient ischemic attack, epigenetic alterations in vascular, metabolic, circadian, and inflammatory pathways, together with non-coding RNAs and extracellular vesicle signatures, are associated with stroke risk, severity, and outcome. Evidence in epilepsy, migraine, myasthenia gravis, dystrophies, and leukodystrophies suggests additional disease-specific epigenetic candidates. Epigenetic biomarkers across DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNAs hold substantial potential to enhance precision neurology, from early diagnosis to prognostic stratification and therapy guidance. However, current evidence is heterogeneous and often based on small cohorts and variable methodologies. Large, longitudinal studies with standardised protocols are required before routine clinical implementation.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Diagnosis of Neurological Disorders

Neurological disorders are within a broad spectrum of different conditions, various clinical presentations, and distinct diagnostic methods (Townsend 2024). The common part of all of them is that they affect the nervous system, either central (CNS) or peripheral (PNS), being the first cause of disability and second the most common cause of death globally (Feigin et al. 2020). Rapid diagnosis and timely initiation of treatment without delay, especially in acute neurological conditions, is crucial to achieve the best possible outcomes (Wahlster and Johnson 2024). Before moving to other diagnostic tools, an essential part of the diagnostic process should be raised, a neurological examination (Schmidt et al. 2021; Saper 2018). This specific kind of patient’s physical examination contains different tests to recognize abnormalities in different locations of the CNS or PNS, if any (Bhaskar et al. 2020). The most common elements regard cranial nerves, muscle tone and strength in upper and lower limbs, deep tendon reflexes, sensation of all types, cerebellar testing, and gait, all guided by the patient’s history and overall presentation (Saper 2018; Seraji-Bzorgzad et al. 2019). However, if a specific condition is suspected, other, more dedicated tests can be applied, such as abnormal muscle fatigability examination in myasthenia gravis (MG) (Benatar 2010).

Nevertheless, the constantly growing science and technology provide novel possibilities for additional testing, including a number of tests, such as blood tests, lumbar puncture (LP) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), electroencephalography (EEG), electromyography (EMG), or electroneurography (ENG), and finally, genetic testing (Silveira-Moriyama and Paciorkowski 2018; McLane et al. 2015). Each of them can be used to diagnose more than one disease; however, for each disease, the most sensitive and specific diagnostic methods are recommended (McLane et al. 2015). The gold standard for ischaemic stroke recognition is currently MRI in a specific stroke protocol, including diffusion weighted imaging (DWI). Similarly, MRI is warranted for multiple sclerosis (MS) diagnosis to demonstrate dissemination in space, and sometimes in time, when some, but not all, lesions are contrast-enhancing lesions (Hemond and Bakshi 2018). When an infection of the nervous system is suspected, LP with CSF examination should be conducted, not only for making a diagnosis, but also for differentiation between possible aetiologies (Buch et al. 2018). On the other hand, conditions affecting the PNS, neuropathies the most commonly, are usually diagnosed by EMG or ENG (Ross 2012).

Although specific biomarkers (such as molecules derived from blood, CSF, or saliva or unique results of imaging examination) are promising tools to significantly facilitate the diagnostic process of various diseases (Ahmad et al. 2020; Sharma et al. 2023), there are multiple diseases, also in neurology, where specific biomarkers, sufficiently sensitive and specific, are lacking (Grodzka et al. 2025; van Dijkman et al. 2017; Sharma et al. 2023). Therefore, seeking such support is currently a crucial issue in ongoing studies. Among biological markers, epigenetic ones constitute an innovative approach for precision medicine (Fig. 1) (Dumitrescu 2018).

Epigenetic Markers

Epigenetic mechanisms provide a dynamic interface between the genome and the environment, playing a critical role in the pathogenesis of neurological diseases. The stability and detectability of these epigenetic marks in accessible biofluids make them promising candidates for biomarkers to aid in diagnosis, prognosis, and monitoring therapeutic response (Qureshi and Mehler 2014). Epigenetic biomarkers, stable yet reversible DNA‑based, histone‑based, and RNA‑based modifications, provide a multilayered read‑out of gene‑regulatory disturbances that underlie many neurological disorders. The primary types of these biomarkers include deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) methylation, histone modifications, non-coding ribonucleic acids (ncRNAs), such as microRNAs (miRNAs, miRs), each measured with specific technologies. Regarding this issue, novel advances are being made in various neurological disorders. Genome‑wide methylation profiling of brain and peripheral blood has revealed global hypomethylation coupled with locus‑specific hyper‑ or hypomethylation that correlates with disease pathology in conditions such as Alzheimer’s Disease (AD), Parkinson’s Disease (PD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and others (Mordaunt et al. 2019). Histone acetylation and methylation changes alter chromatin accessibility and drive neuroinflammatory gene programs, providing both mechanistic insight and potential therapeutic targets. Dysregulated ncRNAs modulate synaptic and immune pathways; for example, miR‑29 and miR‑124 are consistently altered in AD, whereas miR‑133b changes are linked to Parkinsonian neurodegeneration (Qasim et al. 2025). These epigenetic signatures can be measured using bisulphite sequencing or array‑based methylation assays, chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP‑seq) for histone marks, and RNA‑seq or quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for ncRNA expression, enabling both tissue‑based and minimally invasive blood‑based testing. In clinical neurology, epigenetic biomarkers serve three core purposes: (1) diagnostic discrimination, distinguishing patients from controls with high accuracy; (2) prognostic stratification, predicting disease progression and therapeutic response; and (3) therapeutic guidance, informing the use of epigenetic drugs such as histone‑deacetylase inhibitors or RNA‑targeted therapies (Benčik et al. 2025).

Aim of the Study

Considering all the above, the current narrative review focuses on epigenetic biomarkers of various types, which are deemed promising in different neurological diseases. The comprehensive analysis of all the novel possibilities aims to show a potential direction for improving the diagnostic process and to reveal the possibilities that lie ahead. Given the broad role of epigenetics in neurology, beyond diagnostic biomarkers, the review discusses at some points additional findings from existing literature.

Methodology

The review was performed narratively; however, it incorporates a few systematic methods, such as following inclusion and exclusion criteria, applying specific keywords, and defining the search time frame, which are described below. The primary search through the PubMed Database for the studies to include in the narrative review was performed up to 06.10.2025 using the EndNote 21 programme (licenced by O.G.). Additional searches were conducted between October 12, 2025, and November 21, 2025. The following keywords were used to primarily identify appropriate studies: ‘neurological disorder’, ‘neurological disease’, ‘neurology’, ‘epigenetic marker’, ‘epigenetic biomarker’, ‘diagnosis’, ‘diagnostic’, ‘prognosis’, ‘prognostic’, ‘prediction’, ‘predictive’, and led to screening of 674 studies. Further searches included keywords with names of specific diseases: Alzheimer’s disease’, ‘amyotrophic lateral sclerosis’, ‘epilepsy’, ‘Huntington’s disease’, ‘migraine’, ‘Parkinson’s disease’, ‘stroke’, ‘Wilson’s disease’. The searches were conducted by specialists from various fields, including neurology and epigenetics. Finally, 65 studies were included in the narrative review.

To be included, an article had to describe the usage as diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers of at least one epigenetic biomarker in a neurology disease. Only original articles were allowed. Studies covering other types of biomarkers or studies about diseases from different fields of medicine were excluded.

Findings

Neurodegenerative Disorders

Alzheimer’s Disease

The disease, on which epigenetics the most studies have already been conducted, is AD, the leading cause of dementia globally. Despite years of exploring, the pathogenesis, especially the epigenetic part, is not completely understood. Among the different epigenetic mechanisms, DNA methylation, histone deacetylation, and miRNAs acting should be listed.

DNA Methylation

A number of studies analysed DNA methylation as a potential diagnostic marker in AD, suggesting a proper direction for the research development on epigenetic biomarkers. Acha et al. (2023) measured methylation levels of 21 genes selected based on a literature review, together with the level of phospho-Tau 181 (pTau181) protein, a well-known biomarker for AD. Subsequently, researchers compared the diagnostic efficacy of various panels, including DNA methylation of several genes and pTau181 with pTau181 alone. It was demonstrated that DNA methylation levels of three genes, NXN, ABCA7, and HOXA3, added to pTau181 increased the diagnostic performance in comparison to a single pTau181 level. A slightly similar study was performed by Macías et al. (2025), who compared the methylation positions and regions between AD patients and healthy controls. The number of 102 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) correlated with AD, and 30 of them were involved in the neurological processes. Furthermore, the DMRs were located within HKR1, ZNF154, HOXA5, TRIM40, ATG16L2, and ADAMTS2 genes and showed a significant correlation with the ApoE genotype, more precisely the presence of ApoE ε4. Plasma p-tau levels were associated with DNA methylation changes. In another study, Mitsumori et al. (2025) compared AD patients with brain amyloidosis to those with intact cognitive function and without brain amyloidosis. In the following genes, ANKH, MARS, ANKFY1, LINC00908, and KLF2, the regions with significantly different methylation patterns were found. Additionally, by adding methylation levels of MARS and ANKH to the ApoE genotype, the area under the curve (AUC) of 0.90 in the discovery dataset and 0.81 in the validation dataset was achieved. A comparison between AD, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and no cognitive dysfunction was performed in the study by Qian et al. (2024). Researchers found 17 DMRs, from which 10 were located on certain genes: NPY5R, RNF212, CAMK1, GIPC2, PDE9A, TENM4, FBXO39, RAD52, PPP2R5E, and KATNAL2. Among these 17 sites, seven differentiated between controls and MCI, seven between controls and AD, and, finally, three between AD and MCI. Furthermore, by combining methylation patterns with CSF characteristics, an AUC of 0.8534 and 0.7675 was achieved for distinguishing AD from healthy controls and MCI, respectively. A partially similar approach was implemented in the epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) performed by Smith et al. (2024). Authors demonstrated DMRs that were associated with the levels of either chitinase 3-like 1 (YKL-40) protein or neurofilament light chain (NfL) in CSF, more precisely, five and seven loci, respectively.

Bahado-Singh et al. (2023) is another research group that studied cytosine-based DNA methylation in AD, more precisely, by the analysis of cfDNA in AD patients. As the two most differently methylated genes, researchers identified CYP51A1 and CYP2S1. Furthermore, the cytosine methylation with the CYP genes was explored to find combinations of the highest predictive accuracy. It was demonstrated that the combination of cg17852385/cg23101118 + cg14355428/cg22536554 had 100% sensitivity and 92.8% specificity for AD detection (AUC = 0.942). Similarly, using cfDNA, Chen et al. (2022) revealed seven genes whose methylation patterns correlated with Mini-Mental-State Examination (MMSE) or Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) tests and thus, showed a potential to support the diagnosis of AD, especially the late-onset type. Sung et al. (2016) also analysed the relationship between MMSE and methylation status. Authors targeted HMOX-1 as an analysed gene, since it is related to pathological features of AD. The results revealed lower methylation of HMOX-1 in AD patients in comparison to those with MCI or healthy individuals. Moreover, it correlated with MMSE scores; thus, it could be valuable in predicting AD progression. EWAS was also performed by Vasanthakumar et al. (2020), who demonstrated DMRs between AD, MCI, and healthy people. They were abundant near genes such as BDNF, BIN1, and APOC1, which are related to brain and neurodegeneration. Additionally, those loci that presented with the most significant differences in methylation also correlated with MMSE results.

Konki et al. (2019) compared DNA methylation within the 23 pairs of twins, supporting the thesis that environmental factors can influence disease risk by epigenetic processes. Eleven genomic regions reached at least 15% median methylation difference. Additionally, researchers measured DNA methylation not only in blood but also in the brain cortex; however, not in twin pairs. Neurons of the anterior hippocampus cortex demonstrated altered DNA methylation of the ADARB2 gene. Additionally, the factors, such as age, sex, ApoE genotype, and smoking, had an impact on the ADARB2 methylation level. The changes of epigenetic markers in the brain cortex have also been analysed in the study by Mastroeni et al. (2010). Authors demonstrated a decreased immunoreactivity of two of the DNA methylation markers: 5-methylcytosine and 5-methylcitidine in the region involved in the AD pathophysiology, the entorhinal cortex layer II. Also, eight factors stabilizing DNA methylation were lower in AD neurons compared to control neurons. Zhong et al. (2025) performed a Mendelian Randomization study and screened for cytosine-guanine dinucleotide (CpG) sites associated with AD in the following areas of the brain: cerebellum, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, frontal cortex, pons, and temporal cortex. They confirmed a number of 289 different loci, related to AD risk, from which the vast majority (285) were located in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. Finally, for 19 loci, their methylation level was significantly linked to AD risk.

A slightly different study was conducted by Hüls et al. (2025), who focused on the correlation between a well-known biomarker for AD, total tau (t-tau) in CSF, and DNA methylation assessed in blood. It appeared that methylation of cg22976567 in the LMNA gene was associated with increased levels of t-tau in CSF.

Not only does DNA methylation per se seem to have an essential role in AD development, but also the polymorphism of the gene encoding methyltransferases, enzymes that transfer a methyl group from a donor to a substrate, and thus, are responsible for methylation. Ling et al. (2016) in their remarkable study regarding DNA-methyltransferase 3 A (DNMT3A), showed that the carriers of different genotypes show significantly different risk for late-onset AD, which can be used to predict susceptibility to AD.

Furthermore, based on the DNA methylation, specific epigenetic clocks can be developed from DNA methylation patterns. In the study by Bonham et al. (2025), two of them, DNAmPhenoAge and DNAmGrimAge, were able to predict a progression to either MCI or AD, showing a putative function in assuming the possible cognitive outcome. Hao et al. (2024) analysed epigenetic clocks together with a telomere length of leukocytes (LTL) in their Mendelian Randomisation study. HannumAge epigenetic clock and LTL shortening both contributed to late-onset AD, but not early-onset AD, showing the role of biological ageing in the AD pathogenesis.

Also, preclinical studies performed on animal models focused on DNA methylation in AD and cognitive decline. Cosín-Tomás et al. (2018) analysed a mouse model of a cognitive decline related to ageing, Senescence-Accelerated Mouse Prone 8 (SAMP8), in comparison to Senescence-Accelerated Mouse Resistant 1 (SAMR1), representing a healthy control. Both groups had representatives of different ages: 2 and 9 months. It was presented that 92 genes were differently expressed. Moreover, a decrease in global methylation was observed in SAMP8 mice compared to the SAMR1 group, while an opposite observation, a decrease, was present in SAMP8 mice regarding hydroxymethylation levels.

Finally, oppositely to all previously described studies, Sperança et al. (2008) performed a study comparing methylation patterns of three regions within ribosomal DNA between AD patients and healthy controls, and no statistical significance was reached.

Histone Deacetylation

Histone deacetylases (HDAC), crucial enzymes in epigenetic alterations, were analysed in the brain of AD patients by Geng et al. (2023), who focused on the class I: HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC3, and HDAC8. Researchers have found that HDAC1 and HDAC8 genes were expressed more in the cortex of patients with AD compared to the control group. On the contrary, the expression of HDAC2 and HDAC3 was decreased. Mahady et al. (2019) confirmed the negative correlation between AD and HDAC2 expression. The authors presented that a decrease of HDAC1 levels was observed in the cholinergic basal forebrain neurons in the nucleus basalis of Meynert, the characteristic location of degeneration for the AD patient’s brain, compared to healthy controls and MCI patients. However, another study (Martínez-Iglesias et al. 2022) did not support the findings of Geng et al., showing a higher HDAC3 activity in the hippocampus of mice used as the experimental model of AD. Additionally, expression of PSEN2, encoding presenilin 2, and ApoE genes decreased, and the interleukin (IL) 6 level increased in comparison to the control.

Narayan et al. (2015) compared epigenetic alterations in the inferior and middle temporal lobes between brains derived postmortem from AD patients and patients without neurological abnormalities. The levels of acetyl histone H3 and H4, as well as histone proteins H3 and H4, were higher in AD neurons. Additionally, the increase in those histone biomarkers correlated significantly with the load of pTau and β-amyloid plaques, which play a vital role in AD pathogenesis.

Different Expression of MicroRNA

MicroRNAs, with their function to regulate gene expression, are an important element of epigenetic modifications. Chatterjee and Roy (2016) analysed various epigenetic biomarkers for AD by performing the screening through the experimentally validated human protein-protein interaction database. As a result, 11 novel miRNAs have been predicted by computer modelling to play a role in AD. Van der Auwera et al. (2023) analysed the data from the general population cohort from the Study of Health in Pomerania (SHIP-TREND). They presented two miRNAs, miR-485-3p and miR-19a-3p, related to decreased and increased memory scores, respectively. Furthermore, other miRNAs (miR-103a-3p, let-7d-5p, miR-93-3p, let-7f-5p, miR-142-3p, miR-93-3p) were associated with verbal memory changes. Since all these miRNAs have been described already in the context of AD, they can possibly act as biomarkers, predicting neurodegeneration and AD.

Other Epigenetic Markers

Apart from the mechanisms mentioned above, there are other ones, less common in research. Leone et al. (2019) conducted a study measuring the expression of a novel epigenetic determinant, acting indirectly on gene expression, a nucleoporin 153 (Nup153), in adult neural stem cells (NSCs). The research group demonstrated a reduction in Nup153 expression in AD NSCs, while the overexpression of Nup153 resulted in higher proliferation and differentiation to cells positive for neuronal markers.

Parkinson’s Disease

Parkinson’s Disease is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized predominantly by motor manifestations, including bradykinesia, tremor, and rigidity (Henderson et al. 2021). However, the course of PD is currently hard to predict, and novel tools are needed for this purpose. Chuang et al. (2019) performed a study to find epigenetic markers associated with a quick progression of motor and cognitive symptoms in PD. They followed a PD patient cohort for an average of 5.1 years and assessed their motor and cognitive status using the MMSE and the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS), respectively. Based on the EWAS, significantly altered methylation levels were indicated in a number of CpGs. The methylation level in 7 CpGs was correlated with a 4-point decline in MMSE, while the correlation between motor progression and methylation levels was reported for 8 CpGs. These hyper- and hypomethylated sites were located in different genes, with some of them already known as associated with PD pathogenesis, including PTPRN2 and SATB1. Methylation patterns were also assessed by Henderson et al. (2021), who sought differences between methylomes and transcriptomes of PD patients and healthy controls in order to find potential PD-specific blood-based biomarkers. The researchers identified 31 DMRs and reported under-expression of 9 genes and over-expression of 21 genes in PD.

In a different study, Dashtipour et al. (2017) used brain tissue samples from PD patients to perform genome-wide DNA methylation profiling. They found signature patterns of altered CpG methylation levels, with synphilin-1, SNCAIP gene methylation being linked to a higher risk of PD development. Brain tissue was also used to validate the results of an imputation-based analysis performed by Rawlik et al. (2016). In their study, hypermethylation at the cg10917602 site was significantly correlated with higher PD susceptibility. Although PD is largely a sporadic disease, a substantial number of patients carry a specific gene contributing to the PD pathogenesis (Dashtipour et al. 2017). Ryan et al. (2025) carried out an interesting study, where they analysed large genomic datasets containing messenger RNA (mRNA), miRNA, DNA methylation, and single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) profiles in PD patients. The researchers reported that patients with a causal PD mutation and idiopathic PD patients share a similar DNA methylation pattern, supporting the crucial role of other factors determining PD development. Moreover, the authors presented a model integrating DNA methylation and SNPs data, which could support an early PD diagnosis in genetically predisposed persons, as the presence of a gene mutation alone does not determine the development of PD. Finally, specific miRNAs are being proposed as potential novel PD biomarkers. Chen et al. found miR-4639-5p to be significantly upregulated in people with PD compared to healthy controls and patients with essential tremor, a common PD mimic (Chen et al. 2017). Therefore, it could potentially serve as a tool to differentiate between these two conditions.

Huntington’s Disease

A next disease belonging to the group of neurodegenerative conditions is Huntington’s Disease (HD), presenting with involuntary movements, psychiatric disorders, and dementia. It is caused by a usually inherited mutation of the HTT gene encoding the HTT protein. Ratovitski et al. (2015) investigated the influence of either normal or mutant HTT on an enzyme, called protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5), which regulates symmetrical demethylation of arginine. In the study, the normal HTT stimulated PRMT5 more effectively than the mutant one. Thus, histones H2A and H4 presented with reduced methylation in the presence of mutant protein, both in mouse neurons and human brain tissue, showing epigenetics involved in HD and PRMT5 activation as a potential marker. Bai et al. (2015) indicated 136 loci with a high distribution of trimethyl-histone H3-lysine 4 (H3K4me3) in the neurons of the prefrontal cortex of HD patients compared to the control group. The genes with loci enriched in H3K4me3 were as follows: GPR3, TMEM106B, PDIA6, HES4, and JAGGED2. Additionally, the higher level of methylation of the HES4 promoter was related to the striatal degeneration and age of onset; therefore, not only diagnostic, but also prognostic epigenetic biomarkers may be indicated in future studies.

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis is a rare, terminal neurodegenerative disorder for which any developments are highly needed to give hope to the patients. Fernandez-Pombo et al. (2025), in their study, extracted DNA samples from leukocytes of patients with ALS at the time of diagnosis. It was presented that the methylation pattern of the D-loop of the GSTP gene and CpG site of the LINE-1 gene correlated with malnutrition, symptom progression, and death; thus, showing the potential role of epigenetic changes as prognostic markers of ALS. Lam et al. (2016) conducted a study on twin pairs, in which one person was affected by ALS, while the other was not, and acted as a control group. Participants classified to the study group presented with several differently methylated loci within genes, such as EGFR and TNFRSF11A, as well as pathways associated with tretinoin and H3K27me3 markers. Additionally, those affected by ALS had higher CD14 macrophage levels, lower T cell levels, and showed a spontaneous production of IL-6 and tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), which might be important for potential treatment.

Gomes et al. (2023), on the other hand, analysed miRNAs in patients with ALS, comparing them to patients with ALS-mimicking diseases and healthy controls. In the longitudinal analysis, six miRNAs (miR-26a-1-3p, miR-361-5p, miR-224-5p, miR-7-2-3p, miR-3159, and miR-630) significantly differed between ALS and healthy controls, while four miRNAs (miR-152-3p, miR-93-3p, miR-206, and miR-9-5p) differed between ALS and ALS-mimicking disorders. However, in the individual, more precise analysis, only miR-7-2-3p remained significantly altered when comparing patients with ALS and conditions mimicking ALS.

Finally, Merbaum et al. (2025) analysed methylome profiles of over five thousand patients with ALS, comparing them to over two thousand healthy controls. The researchers showed that, when involving blood cell proportions in analyses, the previous results showing increased biological age assessed by three epigenetic clocks have been questioned. It was presented that only those patients who had the C9orf72 repeat expansion presented with accelerated epigenetic ageing, which shows that epigenetics might be essential in a specific population of ALS patients.

Wilson’s Disease

The molecular pathogenesis of Wilson’s Disease (WD) is increasingly understood to extend beyond ATP7B mutations to encompass profound epigenetic dysregulation. A foundational study by Mordaunt et al. (Mordaunt et al. 2019) delineated a comprehensive epigenomic landscape, identifying 1,840 specific DMRs in WD liver tissue. These DMRs, enriched in pathways like folate/lipid metabolism and inflammation, were strategically located in key regulatory regions such as liver-specific enhancers and binding sites for transcription factors like HNF4A and FOXA1. Importantly, a blood-based DMR signature not only differentiated WD patients from controls but also distinguished between hepatic and neurologic manifestations, with a 44-DMR subset demonstrating high predictive value (AUC = 0.9), independent of changes in liver cell composition. Building on this, Sarode et al. (Sarode et al. 2021) uncovered an additional layer of epigenetic control in WD, focusing on histone modifications. Their research demonstrated that class IIa HDAC4/5 are dynamically regulated by disease progression and are sensitive to dietary and therapeutic interventions. In the tx-j mouse model, reduced HDAC4/5 levels in advanced disease were linked to increased H3K9 and H3K27 acetylation, which could be partially reversed by D-penicillamine and choline. This aberrant acetylation regulated key metabolic genes, including Pparα and Pparγ, providing a mechanistic link between copper overload and transcriptional dysregulation.

Complementing these stable epigenetic marks, Sánchez-Monteagudo et al. (2024) identified a dynamic circulating miRNA signature for WD monitoring. Their multi-stage study established that miR-122-5p, miR-192-5p, and miR-885-5p are consistently elevated in WD patients and correlate with standard liver injury markers. The translational relevance of this signature was confirmed in ATP7b‑/‑ mice, where orthologous miRNAs increased with disease progression.

Overall, epigenetic alterations, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and RNA-based mechanisms, play a significant role in the pathogenesis and progression of neurodegenerative diseases. These changes not only reflect underlying disease mechanisms but also show considerable potential as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers, particularly when combined with established clinical and molecular indicators. Multiple Sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis is a neuroinflammatory disease with various environmental and genetic factors contributing to its pathogenesis, while epigenetics is proposed as a link between them (Sokratous et al. 2018). Therefore, potential novel biomarkers of MS have been sought among epigenetic modifications of gene expression. Dunaeva et al. (2018) investigated whether there is a difference in terms of LINE-1 methylation in serum cfDNA between untreated relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS) patients and healthy controls. The researchers found differences between these groups, particularly in CpG sites in the L1PA2 subfamily, which were more frequently methylated in patients with MS. Therefore, the methylation level of particular CpG sites within the LINE-1 promoter could potentially serve as a diagnostic biomarker in MS management. Similarly, site-specific hypermethylation in a number of genes in MS patients was also reported by Sokratous et al. (2018), who included both treated and untreated patients in their study group. Furthermore, the level of methylation could also become a marker of disease progression, as proposed by Nociti et al. (2018). The researchers studied RRMS and secondary progressive MS (SPMS) patients, who were genotyped for the BDNF Val66Met polymorphism. Notably, this variation is reported to be associated with brain atrophy and cognitive performance in MS. The cytosine methylation level in the BDNF polymorphism-dependent CpG site was assessed using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) in all participants. The median level of methylation at 81% was able to divide patients into those at low, and those at high risk of quick disability development. Patients below this methylation threshold had a more aggressive disease course, defined as reaching an Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score of 6. Interestingly, even though patients with the Met-Met variant of this polymorphism have significantly lower methylation levels at this site, this group was not found to be at a higher risk of faster disease progression. However, some studies reported hypomethylation to be actually associated with MS treatment. Reyes-Mata et al. (2023) found that global DNA methylation in Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells (PBMCs) was lower in treated RRMS patients compared to those untreated, as well as healthy controls. Furthermore, the EDSS score tended to be positively associated with DNA methylation in female patients, while negatively associated in males. Since MS is predominantly a female disease, this observation supports further research in this field.

Moreover, epigenetic regulation could potentially provide biomarkers for differentiation between MS and other neuroinflammatory diseases, including neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders (NMOSD). Keller et al. (2015) reported significantly different miRNA expression profiles assessed by qRT-PCR in whole blood samples from NMOSD patients, MS patients, and healthy controls. Additionally, the usage of specific miRNAs as diagnostic markers for MS was also proposed in preclinical research by Khalifa et al. (2023), suggesting miR-218-5P as a potential biomarker based on their MS mouse model study.

Therefore, epigenetic alterations, particularly DNA methylation and miRNA expression, appear to play a significant role in multiple sclerosis by linking environmental and genetic factors. These changes show potential as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers, as well as tools for disease differentiation; however, their clinical applicability requires further validation due to variability related to treatment, sex, and disease stage.

Stroke

DNA methylation and other epigenetic processes are crucial in regulating gene expression in both neuronal and peripheral tissues, influencing susceptibility and recovery in ischemic stroke (IS). Recent studies have explored how ischemia, environmental exposures, obesity, and genetic background alter methylation patterns and non-coding RNA expression, revealing novel biomarkers and mechanistic links between metabolic, inflammatory, and neurodegenerative pathways in stroke pathophysiology.

DNA Methylation and Genetic Variants in Stroke

DNA methylation, an essential epigenetic modification, plays a crucial role in neurons, where growing evidence indicates it regulates both synaptic plasticity and neuronal network activity. Lee et al. (2013) investigated how a 5-minute period of transient global cerebral ischemia (followed by reperfusion) impacts the expression of DNA methyltransferase 1 (Dnmt1) in the cornu ammonis 1 (CA1) region of the hippocampus in gerbils, focusing in particular on timeframes when delayed neuronal death takes place. Their results showed that in sham-control animals, Dnmt1 was robustly expressed in all layers of the CA1. After ischemia, by 4 days post-reperfusion, there was a pronounced loss of surviving pyramidal neurons in the stratum pyramidale, and numerous degenerating neurons were observed. Dnmt1 immunoreactivity in the CA1 pyramidal layer was almost lost, with the lower level at day four, but the expression remained present in non‐pyramidal cells, interneurons mediated by gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), as well as in astrocytes in the CA1 region.

Mowing to clinical studies, another DNA methylation analysis was studied by Gómez-Úriz et al. (2015) regarding how obesity and IS may influence DNA methylation patterns in white blood cells, with particular emphasis on the KCNQ1 gene. In the discovery phase, the researchers revealed approximately 80 CpGs associated with stroke, 96 with obesity, and 59 showing stroke-obesity interactions. After validation, key findings included hypermethylation of the PM20D1 promoter in stroke patients, a significant stroke/obesity interaction at a CpG site in CALD1, and obesity-associated hypermethylation at two CpG sites in WT1 and KCNQ1 genes. In the replication phase, KCNQ1 hypermethylation was compared in obese individuals. Furthermore, the same gene rare variants were studied by a different research group (Postula et al. 2017), who conducted a comprehensive genetic investigation to elucidate whether rare, potentially damaging variants in platelet-related genes influence large-vessel IS (LVIS) susceptibility within a Polish population cohort. Following initial sequencing, researchers performed individual genotyping of rare variants (minor allele frequency < 1%) predicted to have deleterious functional consequences using computational algorithms. The cumulative minor allele frequency of deleterious rare variants within the KCNQ1 gene was significantly elevated in LVIS patients compared to controls, representing a three-fold increase in variant burden. Notably, locus-specific analysis identified substantial enrichment within the KCNQ1 gene, with six rare functional variants detected. Functional validation experiments demonstrated partial loss-of-function effects for three KCNQ1 variants, confirming their biological relevance to cellular physiology. Another study (Eyileten et al. 2022) focused on exploring the relationship between genetic variants in 84 platelet-related loci and LVIS susceptibility in Polish patients, given that platelets are crucial in causing cerebral ischemia through clot formation. DNA exons were compared between stroke patients (large-vessel atherosclerotic subtype, non-cardiac origin) and control participants who had existing coronary artery disease or elevated cardiovascular risk. The sequencing identified 789 frequent polymorphisms (703 SNPs and 86 indels) across the 84 targeted genes. When comparing minor allele frequencies between stroke and control groups, only two intronic polymorphisms showed statistically significant differences: rs12744536 in the RGS7 gene and rs398098426 in the ANKS1B gene. The RGS7 variant was associated with decreased IS risk, while the ANKS1B deletion-type variant was associated with increased stroke risk.

The study by Zhao et al. (2020) investigated the relationship between the AHCY gene’s DNA methylation and IS risk in a Chinese community. The findings revealed that the methylation degree of AHCY differed relevantly between the IS patients and control groups comprising patients with traumatic brain injury, with these associations remaining robust across both male and female subjects. For each age category examined, the stroke group persistently had a higher percentage of AHCY methylation than the controls. The percentage of methylated reference (PMR) values for AHCY was markedly elevated in patients with IS when compared to controls, also in males and females separately. Stroke patients also demonstrated distinctly higher AHCY PMR values than controls across all age groups. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis showed this marker might help discriminate between stroke cases and controls, but because the AUCs (0.70, 0.67, 0.75) were moderate rather than high, it is not yet a very good indicator for IS across all patient groups, including males and females specifically.

A noteworthy study was conducted by Cantone et al. (2020), who examined whether exposure to fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅) before acute IS influences DNA methylation of circadian “clock” genes, and whether those epigenetic changes relate to patients’ neurological outcome. The authors found that higher pre-stroke air pollution exposure was associated with altered methylation of multiple clock genes, and that methylation of CRY1 and PER1 correlated with poorer functional status. Interestingly, in settings of lower long-term PM₂.₅, methylation of CRY1/CRY2 showed a positive association with National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) for stroke symptoms; however, this relationship reversed under higher PM₂.₅ exposure.

One of the latest papers regarding HYAL2 DNA methylation was studied by Bi et al. (2024), who compared coronary heart disease (CHD) cases and stroke cases that developed disease within 2.5 years after recruitment to healthy controls. The researchers identified significant associations between altered methylation and increased disease risk. Notably, the study revealed disease-specific and sex-specific methylation patterns: three of four HYAL2 CpG sites showed significantly lower methylation in CHD cases compared to controls, while stroke cases demonstrated differences at two CpG sites, with particularly pronounced effects in females, younger individuals (< 70 years), and those without hypertension or cancer history. The discriminatory power of combined HYAL2 methylation sites achieved AUC values of 0.78 for CHD and 0.75 for stroke in non-hypertensive patients.

Stroke and RNA-Based Analysis

In the already mentioned study (Eyileten et al. 2022), the authors investigated whether profiles of circulating miRNAs associated with platelet function and inflammation, together with extracellular vesicles (EVs), could serve as biomarkers for acute IS, both for diagnosis and prognosis. Patients with acute IS were enrolled prior to cerebrovascular events and collected venous blood at 24 h and 7 days after stroke onset and compared to the control group. Key findings included that patients with high platelet reactivity had higher concentrations of platelet EVs (CD62⁺) and leukocyte EVs (CD45⁺) on day 1 compared with those with normal reactivity. In ROC analyses, individual biomarkers (let-7f, miR-19a-3p, platelet EVs, leukocyte EVs) showed moderate to good discriminatory power, but combining miR-19a-3p with platelet and leukocyte EV levels improved diagnostic performance to a higher AUC. Moreover, miR-19a-3p levels on day 1 were significantly higher in patients with more severe (moderate) strokes versus minor strokes. Mohammed et al. (2022) investigated the potential of specific long non-coding RNAs: lncRNA of NBAT1 and lncRNA of TUG1, along with miR-335 and miR-21, as serum biomarkers for acute IS diagnosis and their relationship with thyroid hormone levels. The results demonstrated that TUG1 and NBAT1 were distinctly upregulated in acute IS patients relative to controls, while miR-335 was downregulated; miR-21 was also elevated and showed positive correlations with TUG1 and NBAT1. Interestingly, NBAT1 exhibited exceptional diagnostic accuracy with 100% sensitivity and specificity at a cutoff value of 1.45, miR-21 demonstrated high diagnostic performance, and miR-335 showed moderate sensitivity and perfect specificity. Additionally, the study revealed significant positive correlations between TUG1 and carotid intima-media thickness, as well as with free tyrosine, whereas NBAT1 was significantly negatively correlated with high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels.

Another study (Zhang et al. 2025) investigated whether mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt) genes could serve as reliable biomarkers for IS. Gene expression data were analysed from human blood samples and mouse stroke models. A set of mitochondrial stress response genes was obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database, and candidate biomarkers were identified. Selected genes were validated in a Chinese cohort using RT-qPCR, comparing stroke patients diagnosed within 3 days of onset and healthy controls. The study identified three validated blood biomarkers: MCEMP1, CACNA1E, and (CLEC4D), with strong diagnostic value. All were highly associated with neutrophil-driven inflammation and predominantly expressed in neutrophils, with CLEC4D emerging as the most sensitive biomarker, potentially playing an active regulatory role in post-stroke neutrophil-mediated inflammation.

In ischemic stroke, both epigenetic and related alterations contribute to disease development, progression, and clinical outcome. Although several biomarkers demonstrate promising diagnostic and prognostic potential, their clinical applicability remains limited and requires further validation in larger, well-characterised cohorts. Epilepsy

Epigenetic and transcriptomic mechanisms are increasingly recognized as key contributors to epilepsy pathophysiology and treatment response variability. Recent studies have explored how alterations in DNA methylation and non-coding RNA expression affect drug resistance and chronic disease states.

The study led by Lv et al. (2019), investigated the role of DNA methylation in the EPHX1 gene promoter as a potential biomarker for carbamazepine (CBZ) resistance in epilepsy patients. The study involved equal groups of CBZ-resistant epilepsy individuals, CBZ-sensitive epilepsy individuals, and healthy controls. Results revealed markedly elevated methylation levels in the CBZ-resistant group in comparison to both the CBZ-sensitive group and controls, with the most substantial methylation observed at a specific CpG site NC_000001.11 (225806929–225807108). Additionally, a positive correlation was observed between EPHX1 methylation levels and seizure frequency, as well as disease duration, in the CBZ-resistant group. These outcomes imply that EPHX1 promoter methylation could act as a predictive marker for CBZ resistance in epilepsy treatment. Furthermore, targeting this methylation might provide possible treatment options.

The research done by Nomair et al. (2023) investigated the diagnostic potential of two miRNAs, miR-146a-5p and miR-132-3p, as serum biomarkers for epilepsy in Egyptian adults. Results reported that both miR-146a-5p and miR-132-3p exhibited significantly elevated serum levels in epilepsy patients compared to controls, with their combined levels achieving a moderate diagnostic accuracy reflected by an AUC of 0.714, while each of the miRNAs alone showed lower but still promising AUC values. Clinical correlations showed that in the focal epilepsy group, compared to generalised seizures, miR-146a5p levels varied between drug responders and non-responders, and higher expression of miR-132-3p was linked to longer duration of epilepsy, indicating that it may be useful in predicting the chronicity of the disease. The next study (Dwivedi et al. 2025) introduced a multimodal profiling approach for epileptic brain activity using explanted depth electrodes (MoPEDE), which enabled recovery of protein-coding transcripts, methylation of DNA, formerly published single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNAseq) data from surgically received epilepsy samples, and appropriate controls from brain regions involved in seizures. The researchers analysed samples from three patients with temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), focal cortical dysplasia (FCD), and Rasmussen’s encephalitis (RE). During stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG), thirty-three seizures were documented, and electrode samples were collected from seizure onset zones (SOZ), propagation zones (PZ), and non-involved zones (NIZ). In the FCD patient, PZ and NIZ samples showed poor transcriptomic correlation, with eight genes upregulated and thirty-six downregulated in PZ, enriched in translational and metabolic pathways. In the TLE patient, a comparison between SOZ and PZ revealed seventy-nine upregulated and eighty-one downregulated genes. The overexpressed genes were related to ion response and stem cell differentiation, while the downregulated ones were linked to proteolysis and catabolic processes, with epilepsy-related genes increased in SOZ. Finally, in the RE patient, two genes were upregulated and six downregulated in SOZ compared with PZ, which showed enrichment in pathways related to apoptosis, necrosis, and protein degradation. Analysis of the epilepsy-associated gene PTPRC showed reduced DNA methylation and increased transcription in PZ compared with NIZ. Correlated methylation patterns across SOZ and PZ suggested coordinated epigenetic remodeling, with enriched pathways involving GTPase signaling, G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) activity, phospholipid biosynthesis, and CNS development. In RE, shared genes were linked to chromatin localization and cytoskeletal organization, while PZ-specific genes were involved in lipid metabolism and axonal guidance. Overall, SEEG electrode-derived molecular data proved reliable, as expression profiles from SOZ and PZ in TLE and RE patients closely corresponded to those found in established epilepsy datasets. The findings demonstrated that nucleic acids recovered from depth electrodes could capture genomic and epigenomic information, complementing electrophysiological and imaging data.

Overall, epigenetic and transcriptomic alterations contribute to epilepsy pathophysiology, drug resistance, and disease progression. These changes show potential as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers but require further validation.

Migraine

The pathogenesis of migraine, a primary headache disorder, is not completely understood, and diagnosis is based on the official clinical criteria. Therefore, the analysis of the epigenetic role might be essential for improving management. Gerring et al. (2018) conducted a study in which patterns of DNA methylation in whole blood were compared between migraine patients and healthy controls. Researchers found 62 DMRs, which were located close to genes responsible for haemostasis and solute transportation. Moreover, these regions have been presented with an abundance of regulatory elements. On the other hand, Rosales-Gómez et al. (2025) focused on miRNAs in patients with migraine with additional comparison between migraine with aura and without aura, as well as episodic migraine and chronic migraine. It was demonstrated that miR-197-3p was increased in patients with aura compared to those without, while miR-143-3p was higher in participants affected by episodic migraine than in the chronic migraine group. Differences were also found in inflammatory cytokines between the specific subgroups.

In summary, epigenetic alterations, including DNA methylation and miRNA expression, may contribute to migraine pathophysiology and clinical heterogeneity. These changes show potential as biomarkers for disease subtypes but need further investigation.

Neuromuscular Disorders

Finally, other neurological diseases have been explored for epigenetic biomarkers. Preclinical studies are represented by Lin et al. (2023), who conducted a study to analyse methylation patterns in MG in comparison to healthy controls. In an animal model, two cell lines have been treated with demethylation drugs to verify the role of DMRs. It was revealed that CAMK1D hypomethylation differed significantly when comparing MG patients to healthy controls. Additionally, demethylation resulted in downregulation of PKD, YAP1, and STK3 genes in one cell line and CREB5 in two cell lines. Chen et al. (2020), on the other hand, performed another preclinical study analysing the role of miR-199a-1 in the mouse model of X-linked centronuclear myopathy. It was presented that miR-199a-1 and myotubularin 1 (the gene in which mutations are responsible for the disease) double knock-out resulted in prolonged lifespan and improved muscle characteristics clinically and histologically.

Among clinical studies, Grégoire et al. (2021) carried out an interesting study, in which methylation patterns in T cells were compared in patients with lower back pain in view of sex differences. Researchers demonstrated that differently methylated sites were abundant near the genes of different functions in females and males. More precisely, in females, the genes were mainly responsible for functions of the extracellular matrix, immune system, and epigenetic processes, while in males, the genes were involved in the major histocompatibility complex.

Different types of dystrophies have also been assessed. Banerji (2020) explored the epigenetic bases of fascioscapulofemoral muscular dystrophy (FSHD) by analysing the influence on transcription factor DUX4, which directly, or indirectly by inhibiting transcription factor PAX7, can cause the disease. Researchers presented that PAX7 repression is primary to DUX4 expression, and thus, PAX7 is a candidate for a diagnostic and prognostic marker of FSHD. In a different study, Nikolic et al. (2020) demonstrated that the methylation pattern of the region D4Z4 did not influence the clinical presentation of FSHD. Schlüter et al. (2018) compared different types within the spectrum of X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy (ALD), including the childhood manifestation, cerebral inflammatory demyelination (cALD), and adult-onset form, a mild axonopathy in spinal cords (AMN). It was presented that hypermethylation of the genes with the H3K27me3 at promoter regions, hypermethylation of the genes, such as MBP, CNP MOG and PLP1, participating in oligodendrocyte differentiation, and hypomethylation of the genes associated with immune system, including IFITM1 and CD59, suggesting them as putative biomarkers in cALD differentiation from AMN.

As presented above, epigenetic alterations, including DNA methylation and miRNA regulation, are also implicated in various neurological and neuromuscular disorders. Although these findings suggest potential diagnostic and mechanistic biomarkers, their clinical relevance remains limited.

Discussion

All the studies mentioned above demonstrate the wide usage of various epigenetic biomarkers in neurological disorders, revealing the potential possibilities to incorporate epigenetics in clinical management. Neurological conditions, such as those included in the work: neurodegenerative disorders, multiple sclerosis, ischemic stroke, epilepsy, and headaches, present with a multi-level regulatory architecture, involving stable DNA methylation, dynamic histone modifications, and ncRNAs.

Given the progressive course and limited therapies of neurodegenerative diseases, epigenetic biomarkers may serve as a significant improvement in the management of these disorders, not only supporting the diagnosis, but also predictive treatment outcomes. A growing body of evidence indicates that DNA methylation, histone modifications, and RNA-based mechanisms are consistently altered across major neurodegenerative disorders, including AD, PD, HD, and ALS. In particular, studies in AD have demonstrated widespread changes in DNA methylation patterns, as well as alterations in histone acetylation and miRNA expression. These findings suggest that epigenetic dysregulation is not only a secondary phenomenon but may have an active contribution to neurodegeneration by modulating gene expression, synaptic function, and neuroinflammatory pathways. Importantly, several studies highlight the added value of combining epigenetic markers with established biomarkers, such as tau proteins or ApoE genotype, significantly improving diagnostic accuracy. Similarly, in PD and HD, disease-specific methylation patterns and non-coding RNA profiles have been associated with disease risk, progression, and clinical heterogeneity, supporting their potential use as both diagnostic and prognostic tools. However, despite these promising findings, the practical utility of these epigenetic biomarkers remains limited mainly by heterogeneity between studies, small sample sizes, and the lack of standardised methodologies, which complicate performing direct comparisons and validation across cohorts. Integrated epigenetic and transcriptomic profiling, therefore, represents a particularly promising direction, offering a more comprehensive understanding of disease mechanisms and enabling the identification of complex biomarker profiles. Such approaches may facilitate the development of more precise and personalised diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in neurodegenerative diseases.

Moving to MS, DNA methylation patterns, particularly in genes like LINE-1 and BDNF, could potentially serve as biomarkers for diagnosing MS and monitoring its progression. The variation in methylation levels and their association with disease course may help in tailoring treatment plans. For instance, understanding methylation’s role in disease severity could allow for better prognosis predictions and more personalized therapeutic strategies. Finally, observed sex differences in the relationship between methylation and disease progression emphasize the need for further research to explore how male and female MS patients may respond differently to treatment or experience disease progression.

For IS, the studies demonstrate that delayed neuronal death may be linked to epigenetic modifications in specific brain regions shortly after cerebral ischemia, suggesting a crucial role of epigenetics in longitudinal prognosis after stroke and the potential patient’s outcome. Combining biomarkers reflecting different pathophysiological processes (platelet activation, inflammation, and miRNA dysregulation) may improve early diagnosis of IS. Additionally, various epigenetic patterns found in genes involved in stroke pathogenesis influence the IS’s overall risk. Factors, such as obesity, also have an impact on methylation changes that are further modulated by stroke status, proposing that some epigenetic signatures can act as potential biomarkers for assessing stroke risk in the context of other dysfunctions, including obesity-related metabolic dysfunction. Exploring epigenetics seems to play a crucial role in stratifying stroke risk and may facilitate patients demanding special care because of increased risk. Furthermore, understanding the genetic background of the disease, including epigenetics, has important value for novel therapies. For instance, the platelet role could have significant clinical implications for developing personalized platelet-directed pharmacotherapy, elucidating critical aspects of therapeutic advantages. Together, the mentioned studies highlight that IS is not only a vascular event but also an epigenetically regulated disorder, where changes in DNA methylation and non-coding RNA expression shape neuronal survival, inflammation, and recovery. Understanding these molecular signatures may pave the way for precision diagnostics and targeted epigenetic therapies to prevent and mitigate stroke-related brain injury.

Furthermore, studies underscore that epilepsy involves complex epigenetic and transcriptomic alterations influencing both neuronal excitability and therapeutic response. Understanding these molecular signatures for DNA methylation and ncRNAs as biomarkers for diagnostics and individualized antiepileptic treatment strategies. Additionally, novel, minimally invasive approaches, such as MoPEDE described above, can contribute to great advantages in exploring epilepsy mechanisms and hold potential for extending molecular profiling to other neurological disorders.

In other neurological disorders, which fall beyond the scope of this review, epigenetics could play a pivotal role in developing novel diagnostic methods, therapeutic strategies, and tools for predicting treatment efficacy. Its potential to uncover disease-specific molecular signatures may lead to more personalized approaches in both diagnosis and therapy.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. Firstly, the review was written narratively, with the lack of a systematic search strategy and selection process performed independently by at least two authors, which naturally led to including only part of the available articles on epigenetics in neurological disorders and raised the risk of potential selection bias. Also, the broad spectrum of literature in this wide field increases the risk of omitting some important studies. Exploring this issue in a systematic manner demands narrowing the scope of the review by focusing on specific epigenetic methods in particular disorders. Also, studies only in English were allowed; thus, valuable works written in other languages were not considered. Additionally, the heterogeneity across studies in terms of design, sample size, populations, and analytical methods limits the direct comparability of findings. The last limitation to mention here is the developing, hence, constantly changing area of epigenetics, posing a risk of the rapid obsolescence of certain studies and findings, along with discoveries not examined in our review. These factors should be considered when interpreting the conclusions, and one more time highlight the need for more standardised and large-scale investigations.

Conclusions and Future Perspectives

There is a constant need for more personalised management for various neurological disorders, since many of them remain challenging to treat. The role of epigenetics in precision medicine is rapidly growing, suggesting a new era of diagnostic and therapeutic tools based on epigenetic methods coming in the near future. Further research exploring its role in neurology and beyond is extremely warranted and, hopefully, may be a natural course of discovery of contemporary sciences. Clinicians should be aware of novel techniques that are currently of interest to researchers and focus on exploring and understanding them, since it is a chance to facilitate and improve patients’ lives.
