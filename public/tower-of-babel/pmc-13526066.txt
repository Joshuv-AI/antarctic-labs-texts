Measurement properties of a patient-reported outcome scale for peptic ulcer: a validation study using classical test theory and generalizability theory
Authors: Fang Xu, Na Luo, TianLe Fu, Yanxiang Gao, Qiaoyun Huang, Zaixue Mu, Xiran Xia, Jing Yang, Ying Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the measurement properties of the Patient-Reported Outcome Scale for Peptic Ulcer (PROISCD-PU) and to examine its reliability, validity, responsiveness, and domain-level measurement precision using an integrated framework of Classical Test Theory (CTT) and Generalizability Theory (GT).MethodsA total of 300 patients with peptic ulcer were recruited from tertiary hospitals and completed the PROISCD-PU. Classical Test Theory was used to evaluate internal consistency, construct validity, clinical discriminant validity, and responsiveness. Generalizability Theory was applied to identify sources of measurement variance and estimate relative generalizability (Eρ2) and absolute dependability (Φ) coefficients under the observed and alternative item configurations. Because the GT analyses were conducted separately for each domain, the findings were interpreted as evidence of domain-level measurement precision.ResultsThe PROISCD-PU demonstrated acceptable internal consistency for most domains and satisfactory construct validity, supported by item-domain correlation analyses and structural equation modeling. Clinical discriminant validity was partially supported, with significant differences after Holm–Bonferroni correction observed in the spirituality/belief health domain, general module subtotal, and disease-specific domain. Significant improvements were observed following treatment across all domains and the total scale, with standardized response mean values indicating moderate to large responsiveness. Generalizability analyses showed that person-by-item interaction was the major source of measurement variance. Absolute dependability (Φ) was acceptable for the mental health, social health, and disease-specific domains, whereas comparatively lower Φ coefficients were observed for the physical health and spirituality/belief health domains, indicating priorities for future refinement.ConclusionsThe PROISCD-PU demonstrated generally acceptable reliability, validity, responsiveness, and domain-level measurement precision for assessing patient-reported outcomes in peptic ulcer disease. The combined use of Classical Test Theory and Generalizability Theory provides complementary psychometric evidence supporting its application in clinical outcome evaluation and quality-of-life research. Future studies should evaluate longitudinal score stability in clinically stable patients and apply multivariate Generalizability Theory to optimize the reliability and scoring structure of the total composite score.Keywords: Peptic ulcer, Patient-reported outcomes, Quality of life, Validation, Reliability, Generalizability theory

Introduction

Peptic ulcer disease is a common chronic gastrointestinal condition characterized by recurrent symptoms, prolonged disease course, and substantial impact on daily functioning and well-being [1–3]. Although clinical assessment primarily relies on endoscopic findings and laboratory indicators, discrepancies are frequently observed between objective clinical measures and patients’ subjective experiences, particularly with respect to symptom severity, functional limitations, and perceived treatment benefit [4, 5]. These discrepancies highlight the importance of incorporating patient-reported outcomes (PROs) into outcome evaluation for peptic ulcer disease.

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) assessment has become an important component of outcome evaluation in chronic gastrointestinal disorders. However, traditional quality-of-life instruments often emphasize global functioning or overall life satisfaction and may lack sensitivity to disease-specific symptoms or short-term clinical change [6–8]. In contrast, PRO measures focus on patients’ direct perceptions of symptoms, treatment burden, and health status without interpretation by clinicians, offering improved responsiveness and clinical relevance, particularly in conditions with fluctuating symptom profiles such as peptic ulcer disease [9–12].

Despite increasing recognition of the value of PROs, disease-specific PRO instruments for peptic ulcer remain limited, especially those developed and validated using rigorous psychometric frameworks and adapted to Chinese populations. The Patient-Reported Outcome Scale for Chronic Diseases (PROISCD) was developed using a modular structure consisting of a general module applicable across chronic conditions and disease-specific modules tailored to individual diseases [5]. Based on this framework, the Patient-Reported Outcome Scale for Peptic Ulcer (PROISCD-PU) was developed; however, comprehensive evaluation of its measurement properties is still required.

Classical Test Theory (CTT) provides a foundation for assessing reliability and validity but offers limited insight into sources of measurement error. Generalizability Theory (GT), as an extension of CTT, allows decomposition of variance components and estimation of reliability under different measurement conditions, thereby enhancing precision and interpretability of outcome measures [13, 14]. The integration of CTT and GT is increasingly recommended in the development and validation of patient-reported outcome instruments.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to evaluate the measurement properties of the PROISCD-PU using a combined CTT and GT approach, with particular emphasis on reliability, validity, responsiveness, and measurement precision, in order to provide a patient-centered and psychometrically robust outcome measure for peptic ulcer disease.

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted in tertiary hospitals using convenience sampling. Consecutive outpatients and inpatients with a clinically confirmed diagnosis of peptic ulcer were recruited. The sample size met recommended criteria for scale validation, with at least 5–10 participants per item.

Eligible participants met the following inclusion criteria: (1) confirmed diagnosis of peptic ulcer; (2) ability to provide informed consent; and (3) sufficient educational level to complete self-administered questionnaires independently. Exclusion criteria included psychiatric disorders and comorbid severe diseases that could substantially affect health status or questionnaire responses.

The diagnosis of peptic ulcer was primarily established through gastroscopy and enteroscopy, in accordance with the diagnostic criteria outlined in the Diagnosis and Treatment Code for Peptic Ulcer formulated in Xi’an, China, in 2016 [15]. Endoscopic examinations played a crucial role not only in confirming the diagnosis but also in determining clinical staging and excluding other potential causes of gastrointestinal symptoms.

Data collection was conducted at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University and the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical College. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Kunming Medical University (Approval No. KMMU2021MEC031).

Instrument

PROISCD-PU (V1.0R) was developed using a modularized (“common module + specific module”) approach, with a total of 43 items. The common module PROISCD [16] (Patient Reported Outcome Measurement Scale System for Chronic Diseases) was developed by Professor Wan Chonghua and his research team on the basis of QLICD (Quality of Life in Chronic Diseases Measurement Scale System for Chronic Diseases) [17], with a total of 30 items, divided into four domains: physical health (PHD), mental health (MHD), social health (SHD), and mental/belief health (SBD), each of which is divided into different facets, The common modules are divided into 11 facets; The specific module PU consists of 13 items and is divided into 3 facets (Fig. 1).

Structure of PROISCD-PU (V1.0R)

The scale is based on a Likert 5-point scale, with scale items divided into forward and reverse items; no conversion is required for the forward entry scores, and the reverse entry scores = 6—the original entry scores, as well as a standardized conversion of the facets and domain scores calculated from the adjusted items.

Statistical analysis

Psychometric analysis

The reliability, validity, and responsiveness of the PROISCD-PU (V1.0R) were evaluated using Classical Test Theory (CTT). Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients, which were calculated for each domain and for the total scale based on patients’ first measurement data obtained at admission.

Construct validity was evaluated using correlation analyses [18] and structural equation modeling [19]. Item–domain correlation coefficients (r) were calculated to assess the relationships between items and their corresponding domains. In addition, multi-trait scaling analysis [20] was performed to examine convergent and discriminant validity according to two established criteria: (1) convergent validity was considered adequate when the item–domain correlation exceeded 0.40; and (2) discriminant validity was supported when the correlation between an item and its hypothesized domain was higher than correlations with other domains.

Clinical discriminant validity was assessed by comparing domain and module scores between patients at different clinical stages using independent-samples t-tests. Because six related score comparisons were performed, the Holm–Bonferroni procedure was applied to control the family-wise type I error rate. Both unadjusted and Holm-adjusted P values were calculated, with statistical significance determined according to the adjusted results. Group means and standard deviations, mean differences, t statistics, degrees of freedom, and corresponding P values were reported. Responsiveness was evaluated by comparing pre-treatment and post-treatment scores using paired-samples t-tests, supplemented by the standardized response mean (SRM) to quantify the magnitude of change over the treatment period. Because the study did not include an untreated control group or repeated assessments in clinically stable patients, the responsiveness analysis was interpreted as evidence of sensitivity to observed longitudinal change rather than as confirmation that the changes were attributable exclusively to treatment.

Generalizability theory (GT) analysis

In addition to CTT-based analyses, Generalizability Theory (GT) was applied to further evaluate the reliability and measurement precision of the PROISCD-PU. Separate one-facet crossed G-study designs were conducted for each PROISCD-PU domain. Because the present analysis was designed to evaluate domain-specific measurement precision, cross-domain covariance components and alternative weighting structures of the total score were not estimated.Within each domain, patients were defined as the objects of measurement and items were treated as the measurement facet. Using analysis-of-variance principles, the G-studies estimated variance components attributable to persons, items, and person-by-item interaction, thereby identifying the major sources of measurement variance within each domain.

The variance components obtained from the domain-specific univariate G-studies were subsequently used in D-studies to evaluate measurement precision under the observed and alternative numbers of items [21–23]. For each domain, the D-study estimated coefficients for both relative and absolute decisions. The relative generalizability coefficient (Eρ2) reflects the reliability of norm-referenced interpretations, such as differentiating or ranking patients relative to one another. The absolute dependability coefficient (Φ) reflects the dependability of criterion-referenced or individual-level interpretations, such as comparing a patient’s score with a specified reference level or evaluating changes in the same patient over time. Because the PROISCD-PU is intended primarily for individual clinical assessment and longitudinal outcome evaluation, the Φ coefficient was regarded as the principal index of measurement dependability, whereas Eρ2 was retained as a supplementary index for relative comparisons.

For the one-facet person-by-item design, relative error variance was derived from the person-by-item interaction variance divided by the number of items, whereas absolute error variance incorporated both item variance and person-by-item interaction variance after adjustment for the number of items. Accordingly, Eρ2 was calculated as the ratio of universe-score variance to the sum of universe-score variance and relative error variance, while Φ was calculated as the ratio of universe-score variance to the sum of universe-score variance and absolute error variance.

These analyses were intended to assess domain-level measurement precision and to examine the potential effects of changing the number of items within individual domains. Because cross-domain variance and covariance components were not estimated, the present univariate G-studies did not evaluate alternative domain-weighting schemes, the dependability of differently weighted total composite scores, or the optimal allocation of items across domains. Therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as an optimization analysis of the scoring structure of the overall composite score.

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 300 patients with peptic ulcer were included in the analysis. The majority of participants were Han Chinese (n = 281, 93.7%), with 207 males (69.0%) and 93 females (31.0%). Participants ranged in age from 11 to 87 years, with a mean age of 50.79 ± 16.58 years and a median age of 51.0 years.

Regarding educational attainment, 95 patients (31.7%) had completed primary school, 99 (33.0%) junior high school, 71 (23.7%) high school or vocational school, 13 (4.3%) junior college, and 22 (7.3%) held a bachelor’s degree. Clinically, duodenal ulcer was the most common diagnosis (n = 190, 63.3%), followed by gastric ulcer (n = 85, 28.3%), ileocolic ulcer (n = 14, 4.7%), and other ulcer types (n = 11, 3.7%).

Among the 246 patients with available clinical staging information, 111 were classified as being in the active phase and 135 in the inactive phase (including healing and scar stages), allowing for subsequent analyses of clinical discriminant validity.

Construct validity

Item–domain correlation analyses demonstrated that most items were strongly correlated with their hypothesized domains. With the exception of item PHD3 in the physical health domain (r = 0.19) and items PU7 and PU12 in the disease-specific domain (r = 0.39 and r = 0.21, respectively), all other items showed item–domain correlation coefficients exceeding 0.40. In contrast, correlations between items and non-target domains were generally weaker, supporting adequate convergent and discriminant validity (Table 1).

Responsiveness

A total of 278 patients completed the PROISCD-PU both before and after treatment and were included in the responsiveness analysis. Significant improvements were observed across all domains and for the total scale following treatment (all P < 0.01; Table 3). Standardized response mean (SRM) values ranged from 0.50 to 1.05, indicating moderate to large responsiveness. The largest responsiveness was observed for the total scale (SRM = 1.05), followed by the disease-specific domain (SRM = 0.95) and physical health domain (SRM = 0.95). These findings indicate that the PROISCD-PU is sensitive to changes in patient-reported health status observed during the treatment period.

Reliability

Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s α coefficients. The α values for the physical health, mental health, social health, spirituality-based health, and disease-specific domains were 0.700, 0.802, 0.753, 0.587, and 0.773, respectively. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the total scale was 0.874.

With the exception of the spirituality-based health domain, all domains demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (α ≥ 0.70). These findings provide evidence for the internal consistency of the PROISCD-PU; however, because the instrument is intended primarily for individual-level clinical assessment, reliability for clinical interpretation should be interpreted principally according to the absolute dependability coefficients (Φ) obtained from the Generalizability Theory analyses.

Under the current item configuration, the Φ coefficients were 0.622 for the physical health domain, 0.792 for the mental health domain, 0.711 for the social health domain, 0.524 for the spirituality/belief health domain, and 0.760 for the disease-specific domain. Accordingly, absolute dependability was acceptable for the mental health, social health, and disease-specific domains, comparatively lower for the physical health domain, and weakest for the spirituality/belief health domain. These results indicate that the precision of individual-level absolute score interpretation varied across domains.

The domain-specific D-study results further showed that increasing the number of items within a given domain produced incremental improvements in both Eρ2 and Φ. These findings may inform refinement of item numbers within individual domains. However, because the present analyses were conducted separately for each domain and did not estimate cross-domain variance and covariance components, they do not establish the reliability of alternative weighted total composite scores or determine the optimal distribution of items and weights across domains.

Discussions

This study provides comprehensive psychometric evidence supporting the measurement properties of the PROISCD-PU (V1.0R) in patients with peptic ulcer disease. Using an integrated framework combining Classical Test Theory (CTT) and Generalizability Theory (GT), the PROISCD-PU demonstrated satisfactory reliability, construct validity, clinical discriminant validity, responsiveness, and measurement precision, supporting its use as a patient-reported outcome measure in quality-of-life and clinical outcome evaluation.

Reliability and internal consistency

Consistent with the reliability results, most domains of the PROISCD-PU demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α coefficients meeting or exceeding recommended thresholds. The spirituality-based health domain showed relatively lower internal consistency, a finding that aligns with the inherently subjective and culturally influenced nature of spirituality-related constructs [24]. Similar patterns have been reported in previous outcome measurement studies, where spirituality domains often exhibit greater response variability. The total scale demonstrated high internal consistency, indicating that the items function coherently within the Classical Test Theory framework. However, because the PROISCD-PU is intended primarily for individual-level clinical assessment and longitudinal outcome evaluation, the Generalizability Theory results, particularly the Φ coefficients derived from the D-study, provide more clinically meaningful evidence regarding the reliability of score interpretation.

Construct validity and scale structure

Evidence for construct validity was supported by item–domain correlation analyses and structural equation modeling. Most items showed stronger correlations with their hypothesized domains than with non-target domains, satisfying criteria for convergent and discriminant validity. The structural equation model further demonstrated an acceptable overall fit, supporting the conceptual framework underlying the PROISCD-PU. Together, these findings indicate that the scale structure appropriately reflects key dimensions of patient-reported outcomes in peptic ulcer disease.

Clinical discriminant validity

The clinical-stage comparison provided partial evidence for the discriminant validity of the PROISCD-PU. Before adjustment, patients in the inactive phase generally reported higher scores than those in the active phase across most domains and the general module, indicating better perceived health status and lower symptom burden. However, after Holm–Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, statistically significant differences remained only for the spirituality/belief health domain, the general module subtotal, and the disease-specific domain. The stronger discrimination observed for the disease-specific domain is clinically plausible because its items directly assess symptoms and health effects associated with peptic ulcer disease, which are expected to vary with disease activity.

No significant difference was observed in the mental health domain, and the initially significant differences in the physical and social health domains were no longer statistically significant after multiple-testing correction. These findings suggest that psychological well-being and general health perceptions may be influenced by multiple factors beyond current disease activity, including individual coping strategies, social support, and other psychosocial characteristics. Consequently, stage-related differences may not be reflected equally across all domains of the instrument. Overall, the findings support the ability of the PROISCD-PU to distinguish between clinical stages in selected domains, particularly those most closely related to disease manifestations, while indicating that further refinement of some general health domains may enhance its discriminative performance.

Responsiveness to clinical change

Responsiveness analyses demonstrated that the PROISCD-PU is sensitive to changes over time following treatment. Significant improvements were observed across all domains and the total scale, with standardized response mean values indicating moderate to large responsiveness. Notably, the disease-specific domain and the total scale exhibited the largest responsiveness, highlighting the advantage of disease-specific patient-reported outcome measures in capturing short-term symptom change and treatment effects. This property is particularly important in peptic ulcer disease, where symptom fluctuation and treatment response may not be fully reflected by traditional clinical indicators alone.

Contribution, interpretation, and scope of the generalizability theory analysis

Generalizability Theory complemented the CTT-based analyses by providing additional information regarding domain-specific measurement precision beyond conventional reliability estimates, identifying sources of measurement variance, and distinguishing the dependability of relative and absolute score interpretations. Across all domains, person-by-item interaction accounted for the largest proportion of variance, suggesting that patients differed in how they responded to particular items. The variance components estimated in the domain-specific G-studies were subsequently used in D-studies to examine score precision under the observed and alternative item configurations.

The relative generalizability coefficient (Eρ2) reflects the reliability of relative or norm-referenced decisions, such as differentiating or ranking patients within a group. In contrast, the absolute dependability coefficient (Φ) incorporates both item-related variance and person-by-item interaction error and is more relevant to criterion-referenced and individual-level decisions, including comparison with a reference threshold and assessment of within-patient change over time. Given the intended use of the PROISCD-PU for clinical assessment and treatment evaluation, Φ was considered the primary index of practical importance, whereas Eρ2 provided supplementary evidence for relative comparisons.

Under the current item configuration, absolute dependability was acceptable for the mental health, social health, and disease-specific domains, but lower for the physical health and spirituality/belief health domains. The D-study results further demonstrated that increasing the number of items within domains resulted in incremental improvements in both Eρ2 and Φ coefficients; however, the magnitude of improvement varied across domains. The mental health, social health, and disease-specific domains achieved acceptable absolute dependability with the current item configuration, suggesting that adding more items would provide only limited improvement in measurement precision. In contrast, the physical health and particularly the spirituality/belief health domain showed comparatively lower Φ coefficients, indicating that these domains may benefit more from future refinement. Therefore, optimization of these domains should not rely solely on increasing the number of items but should also consider item content, construct homogeneity, scoring direction, and cultural relevance. Notably, the estimated Φ coefficient for the spirituality/belief health domain remained below 0.70 even when the number of items was increased to 12, further suggesting that improvements in item quality and construct representation may be more important than simply expanding item numbers.

These findings should be interpreted within the methodological boundaries of the present analysis. Because the GT analyses were conducted separately for each domain using univariate one-facet designs, they estimated only domain-specific measurement precision and variance components without evaluating the covariance structure among domains or the dependability of alternative total-score weighting strategies. Consequently, the present results describe the dependability of individual domain scores and the effects of changing item numbers within each domain, but they cannot independently confirm the multidimensional structure of the instrument, evaluate differently weighted total scores, or determine the optimal allocation of items and weights across domains. A unified estimate of total-score reliability under alternative domain-weighting schemes would require a multivariate Generalizability Theory framework incorporating cross-domain covariance components. Future studies applying multivariate Generalizability Theory are therefore warranted to evaluate the covariance structure among domains, compare alternative composite-score weighting and item-allocation strategies, and further optimize the measurement structure and reliability of the PROISCD-PU total score.

Patient-reported outcomes versus traditional quality-of-life instruments

Compared with traditional health-related quality-of-life [25] instruments, the PROISCD-PU places greater emphasis on patients’ direct perceptions of disease-specific symptoms and treatment-related change. While generic quality-of-life measures are valuable for broad comparisons, they may lack sensitivity to symptom-specific fluctuations in peptic ulcer disease [11, 26, 27]. The observed responsiveness and discriminant validity of the PROISCD-PU suggest that it offers enhanced sensitivity and clinical relevance for outcome evaluation, complementing existing quality-of-life instruments rather than replacing them.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the use of convenience sampling in tertiary hospitals, together with relatively restrictive eligibility criteria that excluded patients with significant comorbidities and other conditions that might affect questionnaire completion or interpretation, may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of patients with peptic ulcer disease, particularly those managed in primary or community care settings. Although these eligibility criteria were intended to reduce potential sources of measurement variability during the initial psychometric validation, future studies should include more heterogeneous patient populations across different healthcare settings to further evaluate the external validity and generalizability of the PROISCD-PU.

Second, although responsiveness was examined using pre-treatment and post-treatment assessments, the study did not include an untreated control group or longitudinal follow-up of clinically stable patients with repeated assessments. Therefore, we could not evaluate longitudinal score stability in the absence of treatment, confirm whether PROISCD-PU scores would remain unchanged without clinical change, or determine whether the observed improvements were attributable exclusively to treatment rather than natural symptom fluctuation, regression to the mean, or other time-related factors. Furthermore, the cross-sectional component of the study precluded a formal assessment of test–retest reliability. Future longitudinal studies should include untreated or clinically stable patients receiving no new intervention, use an appropriate retest interval, incorporate external indicators of clinical stability, and perform repeated assessments to provide stronger evidence regarding test–retest reliability, measurement error, longitudinal score stability, discriminant validity, and responsiveness.

In addition, although the PROISCD-PU contains multiple correlated domains, the present Generalizability Theory analyses were conducted separately for each domain using univariate one-facet designs. Cross-domain covariance components were therefore not estimated, and the analyses could not evaluate whether alternative domain weights or redistribution of items across dimensions would improve the dependability of the total composite score. Future studies should apply multivariate Generalizability Theory to evaluate the covariance structure of domain scores and compare the reliability of alternative weighting and item-allocation strategies.

Implications and conclusions

The PROISCD-PU demonstrated generally acceptable reliability, validity, responsiveness, and domain-level measurement precision. Clinical-stage comparisons provided partial support for discriminant validity, with the clearest differences observed in the spirituality/belief health, general module subtotal, and disease-specific scores. The instrument was also sensitive to changes over the treatment period; however, without untreated or clinically stable comparison groups, these changes cannot be attributed exclusively to treatment, and score stability remains to be confirmed.

By integrating Classical Test Theory with domain-specific univariate Generalizability Theory, this study provides complementary psychometric evidence for the PROISCD-PU. Future studies should assess score stability in clinically stable patients and apply multivariate G-theory to examine cross-domain covariance, domain weighting, item allocation, and the dependability of the total composite score.
