Generalized solutions to hyperbolic systems with random field coefficients
Jelena Karakašević, Michael Oberguggenberger, Martin Schwarz

Abstract




Abstract:

The paper addresses linear hyperbolic systems in one space dimension with random field coefficients. In many applications, a low degree of regularity of the paths of the coefficients is required, which is not covered by classical stochastic analysis. For this reason, we place our analysis in the framework of Colombeau algebras of generalized functions. We obtain new characterizations of Colombeau stochastic processes and establish existence and uniqueness of
solutions in this framework. A number of applications to stochastic wave and transport equations are given and the Colombeau solutions are related to classical weak solutions, when the latter exist.

Keywords:

Algebras of generalized functions, linear hyperbolic systems, random field coefficients, generalized stochastic processes.

AMS Subject Classification:
35D05, 35F40, 35R60, 46F30, 60H15

Introduction

This paper is devoted to the Cauchy problem for linear hyperbolic systems of the form of

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\left(\partial_{t}+\lambda(x,t)\partial_{x}\right)u&=&f(x,t)u+g(x,t),\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\\
u(x,0)&=&u_{0}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\end{array}

(1)

where
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
,
g=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{n})
;
\lambda
and
f
are
(n\times n)
-matrix valued functions. The coefficient matrix
\lambda
is assumed to be real-valued and diagonal,

\lambda={\rm diag}\,(\lambda_{1}.\ldots,\lambda_{n})
.
The focus of the paper will be on the case where the coefficients as well as the initial data are random fields on some probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,P)
.

Classical random solutions can be found, provided the paths of the random fields are sufficiently regular. For example, if the paths of
\lambda
are continuous in
(x,t)
and Lipschitz with respect to the first variable, while the entries
of
f
and
g
are continuous, the corresponding system of integral equations has an almost surely unique pathwise solution
u
. This assertion already fails in the deterministic case if the paths of
\lambda
are merely Hölder continuous

[
23
, Example 6.1]
.

From the viewpoint of applications it is desirable to admit coefficients of even lower regularity. The main applicative interest lies in wave propagation and transport in randomly perturbed media. In engineering applications random material parameters are often modelled by random fields with continuous, but nowhere differentiable paths
[
29
]
. A frequently used example is the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
[
3
, Section 8.3]
whose autocorrelation function is continuous, but not differentiable
[
29
, Formula (2.39)]
. Another source of examples arises from randomly layered media
[
24
]
. Certain scaling limits lead to diffusion processes as coefficients, such as Brownian motion

[
24
, Section 5.2]
. Scaling limits in the so-called Goupillaud medium may give rise to arbitrary Lévy processes
[
8
]
. It may even be necessary to insert distributional derivatives of such processes in the coefficient functions, see Example
6.4
below, when the geometric wave equation in a randomly perturbed geometry is studied.

In short, we wish to admit generalized stochastic processes as coefficients (or initial data) in (
1
). This entails that a meaning has to be given to the products of the coefficients with the generalized solution in (
1
).
To this end, we adopt the framework of Colombeau algebras of generalized functions. These are differential algebras of generalized functions which contain the space of Schwartz distributions as a subspace. They have found a wealth of applications in nonlinear partial differential equations and in nonsmooth geometry
[
32
]
. Colombeau stochastic processes have been considered since the 1990s
[
2
,
56
]
. We will rely on the notion of Colombeau stochastic processes as developed in
[
30
,
46
]
, with minor modifications.

Colombeau stochastic processes or Colombeau random fields are defined as equivalence classes of families
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
of random processes with smooth paths which satisfy certain asymptotic properties as
\varepsilon\to 0
. At fixed
\varepsilon
, (
1
) turns
into a system of random differential equations, which can be solved by classical techniques
[
11
,
33
]
. The key ingredient of the existence and uniqueness theory is to prove the asymptotic estimates for the representatives

(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
.

Section
2
briefly summarizes the classical deterministic theory for constructing continuous or differentiable solutions to (
1
) by integrating along characteristic curves. We also recall a basic a priori estimate which plays a central role in deriving the asymptotic bounds required in later sections. In Section
3
random classical solutions to (
1
) are constructed, and the required conditions on the coefficients are highlighted.
Though the results of these two sections are well-known, we found it useful to briefly recall them because the estimates become important on a more general level in the construction of the Colombeau solutions.

Section
4
starts with recalls on deterministic Colombeau theory and then presents spaces of Colombeau stochastic process (with or without moments). It is indicated how random Schwartz distributions can be embedded. A central result is a new asymptotic characterization (Lemma
4.1
) which forms a decisive step beyond
[
30
,
46
]
. The existence of expectation values, moments and the autocovariance function as members of Colombeau algebras of generalized functions is discussed.

The main new results are in Section
5
: existence and uniqueness of Colombeau solutions to the stochastic system (
1
) without moments (Theorem
5.1
) and with moments (Theorem
5.2
).
It is also shown how the generalized solutions relate to the classical solutions from Section
3
, when the latter exist. As a special case, generalized solutions to the scalar transport equation are considered. It is shown that
solutions exist under weaker assumptions, provided the drift coefficient depends on one of the variables
(x,t)
only.

Section
6
is devoted to applications. We start out with the one-dimensional wave equation with lower order terms (Example
6.1
) and its reduction to a first order system. This is applied to the stochastic wave equation with random propagation speed depending on
x
only (Example
6.2
). We turn to the constant coefficient wave equation with additive noise in the Colombeau sense in Example
6.3
. If the noise is embedded space-time Gaussian white noise, we show that the unique Colombeau solution is associated (in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
) to the well-known classical random field solution
[
70
]
. Example
6.4
addresses the geometric wave equation on the graph of a smooth function, viewed as a one-dimensional Riemannian manifold. The geometric wave equation fully decouples in a pair of transport equations. If the graph is perturbed by a stochastic process with continuous paths of locally infinite length, there appears to be no applicable classical solution concept, but Colombeau solutions can still be constructed. It is shown that in this case waves do not propagate at all, while for continuously differentiable perturbations, the propagation is as usual.
The final Example
6.5
takes up Ogawa’s analysis
[
59
]
of of the transport equation with white noise (in time) as coefficient. We construct a unique Colombeau solution and recover Ogawa’s results in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Note that the effect of adding time-dependent noises to the drift has become an issue of considerable interest in the past decade
[
9
,
22
,
23
]
.

The Appendix collects some facts about embedding distributions into Colombeau algebras. Working on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, an especially simple form of embedding can be used. The form of this embedding was introduced in
[
55
, Section 12]
without much detail. For the purpose of applying it to generalized stochastic processes, we provide the required estimates and also show that it coincides with the usual embedding as worked out in
[
32

. Following recent clarifications
[
57
]
, generalized stochastic processes – random Schwartz distributions or
L^{p}
-valued random linear functionals – can be embedded into spaces of Colombeau stochastic processes in a simple and unified way.
Examples of such processes as used in Section
6
are given, and it is shown how the requirements of the existence theory from Section
5
can be met. The Appendix ends with two counterexamples showing that certain relations between different variants of Colombeau spaces addressed in Section
4
do not hold.

2
Classical deterministic theory

In this section we briefly recall the classical deterministic theory. The characteristic curves of system (
1
) are the integral curves of the vector fields
\partial_{t}+\lambda_{i}(x,t)\partial_{x}
, where
\lambda_{i}
is the
i
-th entry in the diagonal matris
\lambda
.
We adjust them so that they pass through a given point
x_{0}
at time

t=t_{0}
. Hence the characteristic curves are the solutions to

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\frac{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pt}{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptt}\gamma_{i}(x_{0},t_{0},t)&=&\lambda_{i}\big(\gamma_{i}(x_{0},t_{0},t),t\big),\\
\gamma_{i}(x_{0},t_{0},t_{0})&=&x_{0}.\end{array}

(2)

There are various sufficient conditions under which the characteristic curves exist globally and depend continuously on the starting values
(x_{0},t_{0})
. Anticipating what is needed in the random case, we will use the following condition:

(LB)

(Local Lipschitz condition and global bound) For every compact subset
K\subset\mathbb{R}
and every compact time interval
I
, there are constants
\ell>0
,
c>0
such that, for all
i=1,\ldots,n
,

|\lambda_{i}(x,t)-\lambda_{i}(y,t)|\leq\ell|x-y|\quad\mbox{for\ all\ }x,y\in K,t\in I,

and

|\lambda_{i}(x,t)|\leq c\quad\mbox{for\ all\ }x\in\mathbb{R},t\in I.

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume that the
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{i}(x,t)
are continuous functions of
(x,t)
and that condition (LB) holds. Then equation (
2
) has a unique solution which exists globally in time and depends continuously on the initial data
(x_{0},t_{0})
.

If in addition the coefficients
\lambda_{i}
belongs to
{\mathcal{C}}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
k\geq 1
, the same is true of the characteristic flow, that is,
\gamma_{i}
belongs to
{\mathcal{C}}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

Proof.
Rephrase problem (
2
) as the integral equation

\gamma_{i}(x_{0},t_{0},t)=x_{0}-\int_{t}^{t_{0}}\lambda_{i}\big(\gamma_{i}(x_{0},t_{0},s),s\big)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pts

(3)

and consider
(x_{0},t_{0})
as fixed first, contained in the interior of some compact set
K\times I
. Let

{\mathcal{B}}_{\tau}=\{f\in{\mathcal{C}}[t_{0}-\tau,t_{0}+\tau]:\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\tau,t_{0}+\tau]}|f(t)-x_{0}|\leq 1\}.

The integral operator

({\mathcal{M}}h)(t)=x_{0}-\int_{t}^{t_{0}}\lambda_{i}(h(s),s)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pts

defines a contraction on
{\mathcal{B}}_{\tau}
for sufficiently small
\tau
, thus producing a local solution
h(t)=\gamma(x_{0},t_{0},t)
in
[t_{0}-\tau,t_{0}+\tau]
. The time of existence
\tau
depends only on the constants
\ell
,
c
in condition (LB) (and the choice of the compact sets
K
,
I
). Hence the solution can be continued globally in time.

Continuous dependence on the initial data
(x_{0},t_{0})
can be inferred by Gronwall’s inequality. The differentiability with respect to
(x_{0},t_{0},t)
in case the coefficients
\lambda_{i}
belong to
{\mathcal{C}}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
follows, e.g., from

[
34
, Corollary 4.1]
.
∎

Returning to system (
1
) and integrating along the characteristic curves results in the system of integral equations

\displaystyle u_{i}(x,t)

\displaystyle=u_{0i}\big(\gamma_{i}(x,t,0)\big)

(4)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}f_{ij}\big(\gamma_{i}(x,t,\tau),\tau\big)u_{j}\big(\gamma_{i}(x,t,\tau),\tau\big)+g_{i}\big(\gamma_{i}(x,t,\tau),\tau)\big)\Big)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pt\tau

for
i=1,\ldots,n
. We briefly sketch the existence theory for the system of integral equations (
4
).

Let
K_{0}=[-\kappa,\kappa]
be a compact interval. For
-T\leq t\leq T
, the region
K_{T}
is defined by

K_{T}=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:-T\leq t\leq T,|x|\leq\kappa-c|t|\}

(5)

where
c
is the constant in (LB) associated with the interval
I=[-T,T]
.
The region
K_{T}
is a domain of determinacy; for any point
(x_{0},t_{0})\in K_{T}
, all
n
characteristic curves joining this point with the

-axis remain in
K_{T}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let the coefficients in
\lambda
,
f
and
g
belong to
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Assume further that
\lambda
satisfies condition (LB). Then the following holds:

(a) If
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{C}}(K_{0})
, there is
T>0
such that the problem (
4
) has a solution
u\in{\mathcal{C}}(K_{T})
.

(b) For whatever
T_{0}>0
, there is at most one solution
u\in{\mathcal{C}}(K_{T_{0}})
.

(c) If
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R})
, then problem (
4
) has a unique global solution
u\in{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

(d) If
\lambda,f,g
belong to
{\mathcal{C}}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{k}(\mathbb{R})
for some
k\geq 1
, then
u\in{\mathcal{C}}^{k}(K_{T_{0}})
.

Sketch of proof.
Assertion (a) is obtained by showing that the integral operator on the right-hand side of (
4
) defines a contraction in the ball of radius one in
{\mathcal{C}}(K_{T})
around the free solution

\Big(u_{0i}\big(\gamma_{i}(x,t,0)\big)+\int_{0}^{t}g_{i}\big(\gamma_{i}(x,t,\tau),\tau)\big)\Big)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pt\tau\Big)_{i=1,\ldots,n}

(6)

for sufficiently small
T
. It is important to point out that the time of local existence depends only on the supremum norm of the
f_{ij}
,
g_{i}
on
K_{T}
and
u_{0}
on
K_{0}
.

Point (b) is obtained by applying Lemma
2.2
below to the difference of two solutions with the same data (which satisfies (
4
) with
g\equiv 0
,
u_{0}\equiv 0
).

(c) The global solvability follows from the a priori estimate implied by Lemma
2.2
and the fact that
K_{0}
can be chosen arbitrarily large.

(d) Differentiability is obtained by first applying the fixed point theorem in
{\mathcal{C}}^{k}(K_{T})
for small
T
and deriving a priori estimates on the derivatives using the lemma inductively. ∎

Finally, observe that for solutions in
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(K_{T})
, the system of integral equations (
4
) is equivalent with the system of differential equations (
1
). This equivalence holds more generally for locally integrable solutions in the weak sense. Further details on the classical theory can be found in
[
55
, Section 13]
.

In the sequel, we denote the maximum norm on vectors
g
and the corresponding matrix norm on matrices
f
simply by

|g|=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}|g_{j}|,\quad|f|=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|f_{ij}|.

(7)

Lemma 2.2
.

Under the hypothesis of Proposition
2.1
, assume that
u\in{\mathcal{C}}(K_{T})
is a solution to (
4
) for some
T>0
. Then

\displaystyle\sup_{(x,t)\in K_{T}}

\displaystyle|u(x,t)|

(8)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sup_{x\in K_{0}}|u_{0}(x)|+T\sup_{(x,t)\in K_{T}}|g(x,t)|\Big)\exp\big(T\sup_{(x,t)\in K_{T}}|f(x,t)|\big).

Proof.
Apply Gronwall’s inequality to the function

s\to\sup_{(x,t)\in K_{s}}|u(x,t)|
.
\Box

Remark 2.1
.

As preparation for the random case it is important to note that the solution to (
4
) can be obtained by Picard iteration, which converges in
{\mathcal{C}}(K_{T})
for arbitrarily chosen base interval
K_{0}
and time
T
. To see this, we write equation (
4
) in somewhat abusive, but suggestive notation as

u(x,t)=u_{0}\big|_{\gamma(x,t,0)}+\int_{0}^{t}\big(fu+g\big)\big|_{(\gamma(x,t,\tau),\tau)}\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pt\tau

The Picard iteration scheme is

\displaystyle u^{(0)}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{0}\big|_{\gamma(x,t,0)}

\displaystyle u^{(n+1)}(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{0}\big|_{\gamma(x,t,0)}+\int_{0}^{t}\big(fu^{(n)}+g\big)\big|_{(\gamma(x,t,\tau),\tau)}\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pt\tau

(9)

Introducing the bounds

C=\sup_{(x,t)\in K_{T}}|f(x,t)|,\quad M=\sup_{(x,t)\in K_{T}}|g(x,t)|+C\sup_{x\in K_{0}}|u_{0}(x)|

one gets the estimates

\sup_{(y,s)\in K_{t}}|u^{(1)}(y,s)-u^{(0)}(y,s)|\leq Mt\quad\mbox{for}\quad 0\leq t\leq T

and, by induction,

\sup_{(y,s)\in K_{t}}|u^{(n+1)}(y,s)-u^{(n)}(y,s)|\leq MC^{n}t^{n+1}/(n+1)!\quad\mbox{for}\quad 0\leq t\leq T.

Indeed, the induction step follows from the estimate

\sup_{(y,s)\in K_{t}}|u^{(n+1)}(y,s)-u^{(n)}(y,s)|\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{(y,s)\in K_{\tau}}|u^{(n)}(y,s)-u^{(n-1)}(y,s)|\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pt\tau,

which is based on the geometry of the characteristic curves. It follows that the sequence

u^{(n)}(x,t)=u^{(0)}(x,t)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(u^{(j)}(x,t)-u^{(j-1)}(x,t)\big)

converges absolutely on
K_{T}
. Passing to the limit in (
2.1
) one sees that the limit coincides with the unique solution to (
4
). More details on these type of arguments can be found in
[
63
, Section 10]
.

3
Random classical solutions

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,P)
be a probability space. A
random field

A
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a map

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R},(x,\omega)\to A(x,\omega)

(10)

such that the map
\omega\to A(x,\omega)
is measurable for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, more precisely, measurable with respect to
\Sigma
and the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{R})
.
The paths or trajectories of the random field are the maps
x\to A(x,\omega)
.

We may consider random fields with continuous or differentiable paths.
It is well known that a random field
A
with almost surely continuous paths is jointly measurable, that is, the map in (
10
) is measurable with respect to
{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\Sigma
.

Remark 3.1
.

Suppose
A
is a random field on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
B
a random field on
\mathbb{R}
, both with almost surely continuous paths. Then one may define the composed random field

(x,\omega)\to B\big(A(x,\omega),\omega\big).

As the composition of the Borel measurable maps
(x,\omega)\to\big(A(x,\omega),\omega\big)
and
(y,\eta)\to B(y,\eta)
it is also jointly measurable (and it has almost surely continuous paths).

We apply these observations to system (
1
) with random coefficients. We make the assumption that the entries of
\lambda,f,g
are random fields on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with almost surely continuous paths, and similarly for the initial data. In addition, the entries
\lambda_{i}(x,t,\omega)
are assumed to satisfy condition (LB), with additional care given to the dependence on
\omega
. We require the following conditions:

(AL)

(Almost sure local Lipschitz property)
For every compact subset
K\subset\mathbb{R}
, every compact time interval
I
, and almost all
\omega\in\Omega
, there is
\ell(\omega)>0
such that, for all
i=1,\ldots,n
,

|\lambda_{i}(x,t,\omega)-\lambda_{i}(y,t,\omega)|\leq\ell(\omega)|x-y|\quad\mbox{for\ all\ }x,y\in K,t\in I.

(UB)

(Uniform global bound)
For every compact time interval
I
there is a constant
c>0
such that, for all
i=1,\ldots,n
and for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
,

|\lambda_{i}(x,t,\omega)|\leq c\quad\mbox{for\ all\ }x\in\mathbb{R},t\in I.

The integral operator arising in solving the
i
th characteristic equation now reads

({\mathcal{M}}h)(t,\omega)=x_{0}-\int_{t}^{t_{0}}\lambda_{i}(h(s,\omega),s,\omega)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pts.

If
h(s,\omega)
is a jointly measurable process with continuous paths, the same holds for
\lambda_{i}(h(s,\omega),s,\omega)
, using Remark
3.1
. These properties are also preserved after integration, so that

({\mathcal{M}}h)(t,\omega)
will have the same property. Thus the integral operator preserves the measurability. Similarly to the procedure outlined in Remark
2.1
, the solution

h(t,\omega)=\gamma_{i}(x_{0},t_{0},t,\omega)
at fixed
(x_{0},t_{0})
can be obtained by successive iteration

h^{(0)}=x_{0},\quad h^{(n+1)}=x_{0}+\int_{t}^{t_{0}}\lambda_{i}(h^{(n)}(s,\omega),s,\omega)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pts

which converges in
{\mathcal{C}}(I)
for any compact time interval around
t_{0}
, which can be chosen independently of
\omega
. This proves that
\omega\to\gamma_{i}(x_{0},t_{0},t,\omega)
is measurable.
Continuity/differentiability with respect to
(x_{0},t_{0},t)
at fixed
\omega
follow as in Lemma
2.1
.

We turn to the random version of system (
1
) and (
4
). All participating functions are now random fields, that is,
u=u(x,t,\omega)
etc. Thanks to condition (UB), the domains of determinacy (
5
) do not depend on
\omega
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\lambda(x,t,\omega)

,

\mathbb{R}

Then there is an almost surely unique process

\mathbb{R}^{2}
with continuous paths which solves system (
4
) for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
.

In addition, if the paths of
\lambda
,
u_{0}
,
f
and

are
k

, and the process

).

Proof.
The proof follows the lines of Proposition

, making use of Remark
2.1
. One may fix a domain of determinacy

and perform the iteration scheme

), where
\gamma

,

,

4
Colombeau random fields

In this section we recall basic facts from the framework of Colombeau algebras of generalized functions
[
16
,
17
]
.
The basic reference is
[
32
]
; the spaces and algebras of Colombeau random fields (also termed Colombeau stochastic processes)
are modelled after
[
30
]
. We shall derive an important new characterization and also prove that the moments of certain Colombeau stochastic processes can be defined as Colombeau generalized functions.
While Colombeau algebras can be defined on any open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
– even on any smooth manifold with or without boundary – it will suffice for our purposes to consider Colombeau algebras defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
only.

We begin with the deterministic case. A family of real or complex numbers
(r_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
is called
moderate

if

\exists a\geq 0:|r_{\varepsilon}|={\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-a})\ \rm{as}\ \varepsilon\to 0.

It is called
negligible
if

\forall b\geq 0:|r_{\varepsilon}|={\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{b})\ \rm{as}\ \varepsilon\to 0.

The sets of moderate and negligible families of numbers are denoted by
{\mathcal{E}}_{M}
and
{\mathcal{N}}
, respectively.

The basic objects of the theory as we use it are families

(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
of smooth functions
u_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for

0<\varepsilon\leq 1
.
We single out the following subalgebras:

Moderate families of smooth functions
, denoted by
{{\mathcal{E}}}_{\mathrm{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, are defined by the property:

\forall K\Subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}:\;\big(\textstyle\sup_{x\in K}|\partial^{\alpha}u_{\varepsilon}(x)|\big)_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{M}

(12)

Negligible families of smooth functions
, denoted by
{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, are defined by the property:

\forall K\Subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}:\;\big(\textstyle\sup_{x\in K}|\partial^{\alpha}u_{\varepsilon}(x)|\big)_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\in{\mathcal{N}}

(13)

In other words, moderate families satisfy a locally uniform polynomial estimate in
(\varepsilon^{-1})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
together with all derivatives, while negligible families
vanish faster than any power of
\varepsilon
in the same situation. The negligible families
form a differential ideal in the collection of moderate families. The
Colombeau
algebra of generalized functions
is the factor algebra

{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})={{\mathcal{E}}}_{\mathrm{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})/{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

The algebra
{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
just defined coincides with the
special Colombeau
algebra
in
[
32
, Def. 1.2.2]
, where the notation
{\mathcal{G}}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

has been employed. It was called the
simplified Colombeau algebra
in
[
10
]
.

In the zero-dimensional case the factor algebra
{\mathcal{E}}_{M}/{\mathcal{N}}
is called the
ring of Colombeau generalized numbers
and usually denoted by
\widetilde{\mathbb{R}}

or
\widetilde{\mathbb{C}}
. It coincides with the ring of constants in the algebras
{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Restriction to hyperplanes.
The restriction of an element
u\in{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to a hyperplane
H
, e.g. given by
x_{d}=0

is defined on representatives by

u|_{H}=\ \mbox{class of}\ \big(u_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,0)\big)_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}.

Embedding of distributions.
The space of Schwartz distributions is embedded in
{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by convolution:

\iota:{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\;\iota(v)=\ \mbox{class of}\ \big(v\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})\big)_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]},

(14)

where
\rho\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a fixed test function with
\int\rho(x)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptx=1
,
\int x^{\alpha}\rho(x)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptx=0
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,
|\alpha|\geq 1
, and

\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-d}\rho\left(x/\varepsilon\right).

(15)

Further,
\chi\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a cut-off function which is identically equal to one in a neighborhood of zero.
The embedding respects derivatives and renders
\mathbb{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
a faithful subalgebra. More precisely, let

\sigma:{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\;\sigma(f)=\ \mbox{class of}\ [(\varepsilon,x)\to f(x)]

(16)

be the constant embedding. Then, for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,

\iota\circ\partial^{\alpha}=\partial^{\alpha}\circ\iota\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\iota|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\sigma.

Further, the embedding
\iota
does not depend on the choice of the cut-off function
\chi
. If
v\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the cut-off
\chi
can be omitted in formula (
14
).
More details on the embedding can be found in the Appendix.

The association relation.
We end this excursion into the deterministic case by recalling the
association relation
on the Colombeau
algebra
{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. It identifies elements of
{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if they
coincide in the weak limit. That is,
u_{1},u_{2}\in{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are called
associated
,

u_{1}\approx u_{2}
, if

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int\big(u_{1\varepsilon}(x)-u_{2\varepsilon}(x)\big)\psi(x)\,dx=0

for all test functions
\psi\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We shall also say that

u
is
associated with a distribution

v
if
u_{\varepsilon}\to v

in the sense of distributions as
\varepsilon\to 0
, that is,
u\approx\iota(v)
.

Turning to the stochastic case, let again
(\Omega,\Sigma,P)
be a probability space. The vector and matrix norms are defined as in (
7
).
For
1\leq p<\infty
, the

-norm of a random variable, vector or matrix is

\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=\Big(\mathrm{E}\big(|u|^{p}\big)\Big)^{1/p}=\Big(\int_{\Omega}|u(\omega)|^{p}\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptP(\omega)\Big)^{1/p}

and for

The space

comprises the measurable functions on

with finite

-norm. To unify notation, we will also write

for the space of measurable functions.

The basic objects in the stochastic theory of Colombeau algebras are again families of random fields

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with smooth paths.
Consider the following properties:

(S)

For almost all

.

(L

For all

These two properties will be shared by all spaces to follow.
The spaces

are obtained by requiring the additional property

(M

For almost all

and, respectively,

For almost all

For

The spaces are closed under differentiation;

Again, in the zero-dimensional case one obtains the spaces of

Remark 4.1
.

For
1\leq p<p^{\prime}\leq\infty
, the inclusions

{\mathcal{E}}_{M,L^{p^{\prime}}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset{\mathcal{E}}_{M,L^{p}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\qquad{\mathcal{N}}_{L^{p^{\prime}}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset{\mathcal{N}}_{L^{p}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})

are valid, but the corresponding maps on the factor spaces

are not injective.

Further,

{\mathcal{E}}_{M,L^{1}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is not contained in

so that there is no canonical map from
{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{1}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
This also implies that Colombeau random fields with values in

need not have paths which are Colombeau generalized functions; see

in the Appendix for details.

Embedding of generalized stochastic processes.
A

is a weakly measurable map

are measurable. Performing the convolution in (

in place of

, produces an embedding of these processes into

.
If in addition,

for all
\varphi\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and the map
\varphi\to\langle v,\varphi\rangle
is linear and continuous from

to

, then

defines an element of

. Again, details on these embeddings are worked out in the Appendix.

We proceed to an important observation which shows that in the defining properties (M

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
and suppose a family
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
satisfies conditions (S) and (L

For all

and all

\big(\big\|\sup_{x\in K}|\partial^{\alpha}u_{\varepsilon}(x)|\big\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\big)_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{M}

p

We show the argument only for

\sup_{x\in K}|u(x,\omega)|\leq|u(a,\omega)|+\int_{K}|\partial^{\beta}u(y,\omega)|\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pty

(17)

and

\displaystyle\big\|\sup_{x\in K}

\displaystyle|u(x)|\big\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=\left(\int_{\Omega}\big(\sup_{x\in K}|u(x,\omega)|\big)^{p}\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptP(\omega)\right)^{1/p}

An arbitrary compact subset

can be covered by finitely many cuboids as above, so we get the same inequality with a possibly larger compact set on the right-hand side.
The reverse inequality

\sup_{x\in K}\|u(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\big\|\sup_{x\in K}|u(x)|\big\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

is always true. Consequently, if

∎

We turn to proving that the

Let

Pick a representative

For whatever multi-index

(counted with multiplicity), where each

of order less or equal to

1
1

1

Corollary 4.1

Let

Theorem 5.1

Assume that the entries of

\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{2}u^{\circ}-(\lambda^{\circ})^{2}(x)\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\circ}=0,\\[4.0pt]
u^{\circ}(x,0)=u^{\circ}_{0}(x),\quad\partial_{t}u^{\circ}(x,0)=u^{\circ}_{1}(x).\end{array}

Assume that
\lambda^{\circ}
,

and

\mathbb{R}

\lambda^{\circ}

to the integral equation corresponding to the first order system (

To relate the classical and the Colombeau solutions, consider
\lambda=\iota(\lambda^{\circ})

u_{0}=\iota(u^{\circ}_{0})
,
u_{1}=\iota(u^{\circ}_{1})

\iota

50

u\in{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})

{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

Example 6.3
.

The stochastic wave equation with constant coefficient and additive noise in the Colombeau sense:

\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{2}u-\partial_{x}^{2}u=g(x,t),\\[4.0pt]
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad\partial_{t}u(x,0)=u_{1}(x).\end{array}

(33)

Assume that
u_{0},u_{1}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{p}}(\mathbb{R})
and
g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{p}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
where
p=0
or
1\leq p\leq\infty
. After reduction to system (
30
), Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
immediately
show that (
33
) has a unique solution u in
{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{p}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Assume for simplicity that
u_{0}=u_{1}=0
. Choosing representatives, it can be expressed, for
t\geq 0
, as

u_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{x-t+s}^{x+t-s}g_{\varepsilon}(y,s)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pty\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pts=\frac{1}{2}\langle g_{\varepsilon},\mathds{1}_{\Gamma(x,t)}\rangle

in the notation of distribution theory, where

\Gamma(x,t)=\{(y,s):0\leq s\leq t,x-t+s\leq y\leq x+t-s\}

is the backward light cone. In particular, when
g
is embedded space-time Gaussian white noise (Example
A.2
(b)),
g=\iota(\dot{W})
, a representative is given by

u_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\langle\dot{W}\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon}),\mathds{1}_{\Gamma(x,t)}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\langle\dot{W},\mathds{1}_{\Gamma(x,t)}\ast(\check{\chi}\check{\rho}_{\varepsilon})\rangle

where the check denotes inflection.
At fixed
(x,t)
, the function
(y,s)\to\mathds{1}_{\Gamma(x,t)}(y,s)
is bounded and has compact support. A slight modification of Lemma
5.1
shows that

\mathds{1}_{\Gamma(x,t)}\ast(\check{\chi}\check{\rho}_{\varepsilon})
converges to
\mathds{1}_{\Gamma(x,t)}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. The Itô isometry (
53
) gives that
u_{\varepsilon}(x,t)
is a Cauchy net in
L^{2}(\Omega)

and thus it converges in mean square to a limit denoted by
\widehat{W}(x,t)=\langle\dot{W},\mathds{1}_{\Gamma(x,t)}\rangle
. By a similar limiting argument, the covariance is given by

\mathrm{E}(\widehat{W}(x_{1},t_{1})\widehat{W}(x_{2},t_{2}))=\nu(\Gamma(x_{1},t_{1})\cap\Gamma(x_{2},t_{2}))

where
\nu
denotes Lebesgue measure.
This is well-known:
\widehat{W}
is a modified Brownian sheet
[
70
, pp. 281 – 283]
, and it is the unique random Schwartz distribution that is a pathwise weak solution of
\partial_{t}^{2}v-\partial_{x}^{2}v=\dot{W}
and vanishes for
t<0
.
In summary, the Colombeau solution has the pointwise regularity property that, for every
(x,t)
with
t>0
,
u_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\to\widehat{W}(x,t)
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Example 6.4
.

The geometric wave equation: Let
C
be the graph of a smooth function
y=c(x)
. Viewing
C
as a one-dimensional Riemannian manifold, the wave equation on
C
reads

\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{2}u-\lambda(x)\partial_{x}(\lambda\partial_{x}u)=0,\\[4.0pt]
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad\partial_{t}u(x,0)=u_{1}(x)\end{array}

(34)

where

\lambda(x)=(1+c^{\prime}(x)^{2})^{-1/2}.

(35)

Introducing
u^{\pm}=(\partial_{t}\mp\lambda(x)\partial_{x})u
, the corresponding first order system decouples:

\begin{array}[]{rcll}(\partial_{t}+\lambda(x)\partial_{x})u^{+}&=&0,&u^{+}(x,0)=u_{1}(x)-\lambda(x)u_{0}^{\prime}(x),\\[4.0pt]
(\partial_{t}-\lambda(x)\partial_{x})u^{-}&=&0,&u^{-}(x,0)=u_{1}(x)+\lambda(x)u_{0}^{\prime}(x),\\[4.0pt]
\partial_{t}u&=&\frac{1}{2}(u^{+}+u^{-}),&u(x,0)=u_{0}(x).\end{array}

(36)

One may add a stochastic perturbation to the curve
C
and thus is led to considering
c\in{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
. Then
\lambda
is automatically uniformly bounded.
It will follow from the analysis of the stochastic transport equation below that, due to the special form of
\lambda
, the logarithmic requirement of Theorem
5.1

is not needed to infer existence and uniqueness of a solution:
Given initial data belonging to
{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
, system (
36
) has a unique solution in
{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and so has equation (
34
).

Subcase, stochastic transport.
We start by analyzing the stochastic transport equation

(\partial_{t}+\lambda(x)\partial_{x})v=0,\quad v(x,0)=v_{0}(x)

(37)

with
\lambda
of the form (
35
). On the level of representatives, (
37
) reads

(\partial_{t}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,\omega)\partial_{x})v_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\omega)=0,\quad v_{\varepsilon}(x,0,\omega)=v_{0\varepsilon}(x,\omega)

(38)

where
\lambda_{\varepsilon}=(1+c_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(x,\omega)^{2})^{-1/2}
and
c_{\varepsilon}
is a representative of
c
. The case we have in mind is that
c=\iota(X)
where
X
is a random field belonging to
L^{0}(\Omega:{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}))
so that

c_{\varepsilon}=X\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})
. Note that classically much higher regularity of
c(x,\omega)
is required to give a meaning to a transport equation with a coefficient of the form (
35
)
[
22
, Scction 4.2]
or even to have a well-defined stochastic flow defining the characteristic curves
[
22
, Scction 4.4.1]
.

The following calculations are all meant to be pathwise at fixed
\omega\in\Omega
; we drop
\omega
in our notation for simplicity. The characteristic curves corresponding to (
38
) are obtained as solutions to

\frac{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pt}{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pt\tau}\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\tau)=(1+c_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\tau))^{2})^{-1/2},\quad\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,t)=x,

and the unique solution to (
38
) is given by
v_{0\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0))
. Introducing
L_{\varepsilon}(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\sqrt{1+c_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(y)^{2}}\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pty
, the relation

L_{\varepsilon}(x)-L_{\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0))=t

(39)

is readily obtained. At fixed
(x,t)
, the base point
\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0)
of the characteristic curve passing through
(x,t)
is characterized by the property that the arclength of the curve
y=c_{\varepsilon}(x)
between
\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0)
and
x
equals
t
.

At fixed
\varepsilon
, the function
L_{\varepsilon}
is smooth and strictly monotonically increasing; we can compute

\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0)=L_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(L_{\varepsilon}(x)-t).

We are going to show that
\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0)
defines an element of
{\mathcal{E}}_{M,L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
and is c-bounded at fixed
\omega
. Indeed,

\partial_{x}\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0)=\frac{L_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(x)}{L_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(L_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(L_{\varepsilon}(x)-t))}=\frac{\sqrt{1+c_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(x)^{2}}}{\sqrt{1+c_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0))^{2}}}

and

\partial_{t}\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0)=\frac{(-1)}{\sqrt{1+c_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0))^{2}}}.

(40)

The denominators are uniformly bounded, as are the denominators arising after higher order differentiation. Thus
\partial_{x}\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0)
and
\partial_{t}\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0)

are moderate. Further,

\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0)=x+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{t}\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,\tau,0)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pt\tau

which shows that
\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0)
is c-bounded and also concludes the proof that it belongs to
{\mathcal{E}}_{M,L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Therefore, if
v_{0\varepsilon}
belongs to

{\mathcal{E}}_{M,L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
then
v_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=v_{0\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0))
belongs to
{\mathcal{E}}_{M,L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
and represents a pathwise solution to
(
37
). Uniqueness of the Colombeau solution can be shown by the same argument as in the proof of Proposition
5.1
. In conclusion, given

v_{0}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
, the stochastic transport equation (
38
) with coefficient (
35
) has a unique solution

v\in{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
, as claimed.

Suppose that the process
X
defining
c=\iota(X)
has (almost surely) continuous paths which are of infinite length on every finite interval (as is the case with Brownian motion or the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process,

[
3
, Remark (5.2.8) and Section 8.3]
).
Then the relation (
39
) entails that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0)=x.

(41)

Indeed, if (
41
) does not hold, there is
x_{0}<x
and a sequence
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
such that
\gamma_{\varepsilon_{k}}(x,t,0)<x_{0}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
L_{\varepsilon}(x)
is a strictly increasing function of
x
, we have that
L_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\gamma_{\varepsilon_{k}}(x,t,0))<L_{\varepsilon_{k}}(x_{0})
, and so (
39
) implies that

t>L_{\varepsilon_{k}}(x)-L_{\varepsilon_{k}}(x_{0})=L_{\varepsilon_{k}}
, the arclength of the curve
C_{\varepsilon_{k}}
between
x_{0}
and
x
. We will obtain a contradiction by showing that this arclength tends to infinity as
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
. For
\omega
in a set of full measure, we have that
X_{\varepsilon_{k}}=X\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon_{k}})\to X
uniformly on
the interval
[x_{0},x]
(cf. Lemma
5.1
). Take a partition
x_{0}<x_{1}<\ldots<x_{n}=x
of this interval. Given
\eta>0
, we have that
|X_{\varepsilon_{k}}(x_{i})-X(x_{i})|\leq\eta
for all
k\geq k_{0}(\eta)
and all
i
.
Let
L_{\varepsilon_{k},n}
be the length of the polygonal chain through the points
(x_{i},X_{\varepsilon_{k}}(x_{i}))
. Then

L_{\varepsilon_{k},n}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{\varepsilon_{k}}(x_{i})-X_{\varepsilon_{k}}(x_{i-1})|\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X(x_{i})-X(x_{i-1})|-2n\eta=TV_{n}-2n\eta,

where
TV_{n}
converges to the total variation of
X
on
[x_{0},x]
, which is infinite. Given
M>0
, choose
n
such that
TV_{n}\geq M
and take
\eta=1/n
with corresponding
k_{0}(\eta)
. Then
L_{\varepsilon_{k}}\geq L_{\varepsilon_{k},n}\geq M-2
for

k\geq k_{0}(\eta)
. Suitably choosing
M
, this contradicts that
L_{\varepsilon_{k}}<t
. Thus (
41
) holds.

If in addition
v_{0\varepsilon}=v_{0}^{\circ}\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})
for some continuous function
v_{0}^{\circ}
, then

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}v_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=v_{0}^{\circ}(x)

almost surely for every
(x,t)
. Thus waves do not propagate on a curve of locally infinite length.

On the other hand, if
X
has continuously differentiable paths,
X\in L^{0}(\Omega:{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}))
, then
c_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(x)
converges locally uniformly to
X^{\prime}(x)
, and so
L_{\varepsilon}(z)
converges locally uniformly to
L(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\sqrt{1+X^{\prime}(y)^{2}}\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pty
, which obviously is a strictly increasing, locally bi-Lipschitz function on
\mathbb{R}
. Using the strict monotonicity of
L
, the Lipschitz property of
L^{-1}
and invoking
[
7
, Theorem 1]
it follows
that
L_{\varepsilon}^{-1}
converges locally uniformly as well, resulting in the limiting characteristic curves

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,t,0)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}L_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(L_{\varepsilon}(x)-t)=L^{-1}(L(x)-t))=:\gamma(x,t,0).

Again, if in addition
v_{0\varepsilon}=v_{0}^{\circ}\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})
for some continuous function
v_{0}^{\circ}
, then

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}v_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=v_{0}^{\circ}(\gamma(x,t,0)),

the usual propagation behavior.

Back to the wave equation.
Applying these results to the wave equation (
34
), observe that the corresponding system (
36
) has the characteristic curves

\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{+}(x,t,0)=L_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(L_{\varepsilon}(x)-t),\qquad\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{-}(x,t,0)=L_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(L_{\varepsilon}(x)+t).

(42)

From (
36
) we have that

u^{\pm}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=u_{1\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{\pm}(x,t,0))\mp\lambda_{\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{\pm}(x,t,0))u_{0\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{\pm}(x,t,0))

and
u_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=u_{0,\varepsilon}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(u_{\varepsilon}^{+}(x,s)+u_{\varepsilon}^{-}(x,s))\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pts
.
Using the calculation (
40
), it follows from (
42
) that

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{\pm}(x,t,0)=\mp\lambda_{\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{\pm}(x,t,0)).

Thus evaluating the integral and applying the substitution rule finally shows that
u_{\varepsilon}(x,t)
is given by

\frac{1}{2}\Big(u_{0\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{+}(x,t,0))+u_{0\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{-}(x,t,0))+\int_{0}^{t}\big(u_{1\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{+}(x,s,0))+u_{1\varepsilon}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{-}(x,s,0))\big)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pts\Big)

from where the limiting behavior similar to the single transport equation can be read off.

Example 6.5
.

This example resumes Ogawa’s analysis
[
59
]
of the transport equation with white noise in time as coefficient. Thus we study the equation

(\partial_{t}+\dot{W}(t)\partial_{x})u(x,t)=0,\qquad u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)

(43)

where
\dot{W}
is Gaussian white noise in time. (For simplicity, we drop the additional deterministic terms in
[
59
, Equation (1.1)]
which would not substantially change the discussion.)
We take
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
and interpret the coefficient as
\iota(\dot{W})\in{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
. Let
W
be a one-parameter Brownian motion (Example
A.2
(a)) and
W_{\varepsilon}=W\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})
be a representative of
\iota(W)
. A representative
of
\iota(\dot{W})
is given by
\dot{W}_{\varepsilon}(t,\omega)=\frac{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pt}{\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptt}W_{\varepsilon}(t,\omega)
. Then

\int_{\tau}^{t}\dot{W}_{\varepsilon}(s,\omega)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pts=W_{\varepsilon}(t,\omega)-W_{\varepsilon}(\tau,\omega);

this shows that
\iota(\dot{W})
is of almost certain local
L^{1}
-type. Thus Proposition
5.1
entails that (
43
) has a unique solution
u\in{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
, represented by

u_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\omega)=u_{0\varepsilon}\big(x-W_{\varepsilon}(t,\omega),\omega\big).

(44)

For a further analysis of its properties we focus on positive times.
First recall that
\mathrm{E}(W_{\varepsilon}(t))=0
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}(W_{\varepsilon}(t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}W_{\varepsilon}(t,\omega)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptP(\omega)=\int_{\Omega}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\chi(t-s)\rho_{\varepsilon}(t-s)W(s,\omega)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pts\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptP(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\chi(t-s)\rho_{\varepsilon}(t-s)\int_{\Omega}W(s,\omega)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptP(\omega)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pts

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\chi(t-s)\rho_{\varepsilon}(t-s)\mathrm{E}(W(s))\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pts=0.

The transition from the first to the second line is valid due to Fubini’s theorem, using that
W\in{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}:L^{1}(\Omega))
and the
s
-integration is over a compact interval.
A similar argument using the fact that
W\in{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}:L^{2}(\Omega))
shows that the variance of
W_{\varepsilon}(t)
can be computed as

\mathrm{E}(W_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\chi(t-s)\rho_{\varepsilon}(t-s)\chi(t-s^{\prime})\rho_{\varepsilon}(t-s^{\prime})\psi(s,s^{\prime})\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pts\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pts^{\prime},

(45)

where
\psi(s,s^{\prime})=\mathrm{E}(W(s)W(s^{\prime}))
, i.e,

\mathrm{E}(W_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2})=\big((\chi\rho_{\varepsilon}\otimes\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})\ast\psi\big)(t,t).

Since
(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon}\otimes\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})\ast\psi=((\chi\otimes\chi)(\rho_{\varepsilon}\otimes\rho_{\varepsilon}))\ast\psi
converges locally uniformly to the continuous function
\psi

(Lemma
5.1
), it follows that for
t
in a compact subinterval
I\subset(0,\infty)
,
\mathrm{E}(W_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2})
converges uniformly to
\psi(t,t)=t
and hence is strictly bounded away from zero
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Note that when
t
varies in the compact interval
I
, the domain of integration in (
45
) is bounded; we may differentiate under the integral sign, and any
t
-derivative
of
\mathrm{E}(W_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2})
is bounded by a negative power of
\varepsilon
on
I
. The boundedness from below on
I
shows that

\sigma_{\varepsilon}(t)=\sqrt{\mathrm{E}(W_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2})}

belongs to
{\mathcal{E}}_{M}(0,\infty)
and defines an invertible element of
{\mathcal{G}}(0,\infty)
. Further, for
t>0
,
W_{\varepsilon}(t)
has the non-degenerate Gaussian distribution
{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2})
, whose density

p_{\varepsilon}(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{\varepsilon}(t)}\exp\Big(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2}}\Big)

also defines an element of
{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty))
. In addition, for
0<t_{0}<t_{1}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\sup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{t}^{\beta}p_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{M}.

(46)

Our next task will be to study the expectation value of the solution
u\in{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
to (
43
). Theorem
5.2
is not applicable; nevertheless, the expectation value of
u
exists as an element oF
{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty))
in certain special cases.

Let
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R})
be a uniformly bounded (deterministic) Colombeau function, i.e., for some representative
(u_{0\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
,
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|u_{0\varepsilon}(x)|
is bounded independently of
\varepsilon
. Then

\mathrm{E}(u_{\varepsilon}(x,t))=\mathrm{E}\big(u_{0\varepsilon}(x-W_{\varepsilon}(t)\big)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u_{0\varepsilon}(x-y)p_{\varepsilon}(y,t)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pty=\big(u_{0\varepsilon}\ast p_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\big)(x).

We have

\sup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{t}^{\beta}(u_{0\varepsilon}\ast p_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t))\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|u_{0\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\sup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{t}^{\beta}p_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}

By (
46
), the net
\big(\mathrm{E}(u_{\varepsilon}(x,t))\big)_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
belongs to
{\mathcal{E}}_{M}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
and may serve to define the expectation value

\overline{u}(x,t)=\mathrm{E}(u(x,t))\in{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)).

We now show that Ogawa’s observation on the averaged quantity in
[
59
, Proposition 5]
has its counterpart in our setting.

Let us start with classical (deterministic) initial data
u^{\circ}_{0}\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
.

The setting of Ogawa.
If
u^{\circ}_{0}
is a continuously differentiable function with bounded derivative, then
[
59
, Theorem]
states that equation (
43
) has a unique solution
in the sense of
[
59
, Section 3]
with probability one, and it is given by
u^{\circ}(x,t,\omega)=u^{\circ}_{0}(x-W(t,\omega))
. Further, if the function
\overline{u^{\circ}}(x,t)=\mathrm{E}(u^{\circ}(x,t))

is sufficiently regular, then it satisfies the heat equation (
[
59
, Proposition 5]
)

\partial_{t}\overline{u^{\circ}}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\overline{u^{\circ}}=0,\qquad\overline{u^{\circ}}(x,0)=u^{\circ}_{0}(x).

The Colombeau setting.
Without further regularity assumptions on
u^{\circ}_{0}\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
, we can take initial data
u_{0}=\iota(u^{\circ}_{0})\in{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R})
, and equation (
43
) will have a unique

u\in{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
. As was shown above, it is represented by
u_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\omega)=u_{0\varepsilon}\big(x-W_{\varepsilon}(t,\omega)\big)
.
Further, if
u^{\circ}_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, then
u_{0}=\iota(u^{\circ}_{0})
is a uniformly bounded Colombeau function, the expectation value
\overline{u}(x,t)=\mathrm{E}(u(x,t))
exists as an element of
{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty))

and it satisfies the heat equation

\partial_{t}\overline{u}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\overline{u}=0\quad{\rm in}\quad{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)).

(This follows immediately from the fact that the kernels
p_{\varepsilon}(t,x)
satisfy the heat equation.)

Transition between the two solution concepts.
Assume that
u^{\circ}_{0}
is a uniformly continuous, bounded function on
\mathbb{R}
. A slight modification of Lemma
5.1
shows that

\|u_{0\varepsilon}-u^{\circ}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Since
W_{\varepsilon}(t,\omega)\to W(t,\omega)
locally uniformly, we have that, for almost all
\omega
,

u_{\varepsilon}(x,t,\omega)=u_{0\varepsilon}\big(x-W_{\varepsilon}(t,\omega)\big)\to u^{\circ}_{0}(x-W(t,\omega))=u^{\circ}(x,t,\omega)

locally uniformly on
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
, that is, the Colombeau solution
u
is associated with Ogawa’s solution
u^{\circ}
.

Concerning the expectation values we have, where
p(x,t)
denotes the probability density of
{\mathcal{N}}(0,t)
,

\displaystyle u_{0\varepsilon}\ast p_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)-u^{\circ}_{0}\ast p(\cdot,t)=(u_{0\varepsilon}-u^{\circ}_{0})\ast p_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)+u^{\circ}_{0}\ast(p_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)-p(\cdot,t)).

Both summands converges to zero in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
; the first one, because the
L^{1}
-norm of
p_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)
equals one, the second one, because

p_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t)-p(\cdot,t)
converges to zero in
L^{1}
. This shows that the expectation value of the Colombeau solution
\overline{u}
is associated with
the expectation value
\overline{u^{\circ}}
of Ogawa’s solution.

Properties of the embedding

Embedding of distributions.
The embedding of the space of Schwartz distributions in
{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
has been defined by equation (
14
)
with a fixed mollifier of the form (
15
). Here is the argument why it is well-defined. First, one has to show that the representative
\big(v\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})\big)_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
belongs to
{\mathcal{E}}_{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Let
v\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
K
a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\alpha
a multi-index.
For
x\in K
, the support of the test function
y\to\chi(x-y)\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)
is contained in the compact set
L=\{x\}-\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\chi
. By the continuity of the functional
v
on
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there are
C>0

|\langle\partial^{\alpha}v(y),\chi(x-y)\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\rangle|\leq C\sup_{y\in L}\sup_{|\beta|\leq m}|\partial^{\alpha+\beta}(\chi(x-y)\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y))|.

Thus

\sup_{x\in K}\big|\partial^{\alpha}v\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})(x)\big|\leq C\sup_{z\in K-L}\sup_{|\beta|\leq m}|\partial^{\alpha+\beta}(\chi(z)\rho_{\varepsilon}(z))|.

(47)

The latter term is obviously bounded by a negative power of
\varepsilon
, establishing moderateness of
\big(v\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})\big)_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
.

To prove the injectivity of
\iota
, take

and note first that
(\check{\chi}\check{\rho}_{\varepsilon})\ast\psi\to\psi
in
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where the check denotes inflection. Therefore,

\langle v\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon}),\psi\rangle=\langle v,(\check{\chi}\check{\rho}_{\varepsilon})\ast\psi\rangle\to\langle v,\psi\rangle.

(48)

On the other hand, if
K=\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\psi

(v\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon}))_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\in{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\sup_{x\in K}|v\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})(x)|\leq C_{q}\varepsilon^{q}
for whatever

\langle v\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon}),\psi\rangle\to 0
. It follows that
\langle v,\psi\rangle=0
.

We show next that the embedding coincides with the (usual) embedding as defined in
[
32
, Section 1.2.2]
.
For this, it suffices to show that the two embeddings coincide on
{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(using

\iota(v)=\ \mbox{class of}\ \big(v\ast\rho_{\varepsilon}\big)_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}

(49)

(v\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon}-\rho_{\varepsilon}))_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}

{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

and using that

is a continuous linear form on
{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there is

,

and
m\in\mathbb{N}

such that

|\langle v(y),(\chi(x-y)-1)\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\rangle|\leq C\sup_{y\in L}\sup_{|\alpha|\leq m}|\partial^{\alpha}((\chi(x-y)-1)\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y))|.

Thus, given

\sup_{x\in K}|\langle v(y),(\chi(x-y)-1)\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\rangle|\leq C\sup_{z\in K-L}\sup_{|\alpha\leq m}|\partial^{\alpha}|((\chi(z)-1)\rho_{\varepsilon}(z))|.

The latter term is easily seen to be negligible, using that
\rho
belongs to
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and that

for

for some

and all

It follows that the embedding (
14
) has all the properties elaborated in
[
32
, Section 1.2.2]
. In particular, it does not depend on the choice of the cut-off
\chi
; it commutes with partial derivatives; it coincides with the embedding

from (

. Finally, the cut-off is not needed when

Embedding of generalized stochastic processes.
Embeddings of generalized stochastic processes (
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

) have been described in
[
30

46
]
.
Using results of
[
57
]
, a unified and somewhat simpler treatment is possible, which we adopt here.

As noted in Section
4
, a random Schwartz distribution is a weakly measurable map
X:\Omega\to{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, i.e., for all
\psi\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the maps

\omega\to\langle X(\omega),\psi\rangle
are measurable
[
35
, Section 1.3(ii)]
,
[
68
]
. The space of random Schwartz distributions may be denoted by
L^{0}(\Omega:{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
where
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra generated by the weak topology. A
linear random functional
or
generalized random function
on
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a sequentially continuous linear map
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{0}(\Omega)
, the latter space equipped with the metric topology of convergence in probability
[
28
, Section III.1.2]
,
[
38
, Section 2.3]
; we use the notation
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}:L^{0}(\Omega))
. It is known that every linear random functional has a version which is a random Schwarz distribution
[
38
, Theorem 2.3.3]
. However, more is true. The linear map
\kappa:L^{0}(\Omega:{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\to{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}:L^{0}(\Omega))
, defined by
\kappa(X)(\varphi)(\omega)=\langle X(\omega),\varphi\rangle
for
\varphi\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and almost all
\omega
, is a bijection, as has been shown in
[
57
]
. Denoting by
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}:L^{p}(\Omega))
the space of linear continuous maps
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{p}(\Omega)

p\geq 1
, we have the inclusions

{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}:L^{p}(\Omega))\subset{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}:L^{0}(\Omega))=L^{0}(\Omega:{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),

and we can embed these spaces simultaneously into
{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
and into
{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{p}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
, respectively.

We begin by embedding
L^{0}(\Omega:{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. If
X
is a random Schwartz distribution and
\omega\in\Omega
, we can form the net

\big(X(\omega)\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})\big)_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
. At fixed
\varepsilon
it is a random field on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by

(x,\omega)\to\big(X(\omega)\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})\big)(x)=\langle X(y,\omega),\chi(x-y)\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\rangle

and it obviously satisfies the conditions (S) and (L
0
) of Section
4
.
The estimate (
47
) with
X(\omega)
in place of
v
shows that the net
\big(X(\omega)\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})\big)_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
is almost surely moderate, i.e., satisfies condition (M
0
).
Going over to the factor spaces, we thus obtain an embedding

\iota:L^{0}(\Omega:{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\to{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{0}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d});

(50)

the injectivity follows from the same argument as in the deterministic case. This embedding shares all the properties of the embedding of
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
into
{\mathcal{G}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, pathwise at fixed
\omega\in\Omega
. In particular, it commutes with derivatives and coincides with the embedding

\sigma:F\to{\rm class\ of\ }[(\varepsilon,x,\omega)\to F(x,\omega)]
for random fields
F
with smooth paths.

Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
and assume in addition that
\langle X,\varphi\rangle\in L^{p}(\Omega)
for all
\varphi\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and that the map
\varphi\to\langle X,\varphi\rangle
is linear and continuous from
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to

, that is,
X\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}:L^{p}(\Omega))
.
Thus at fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
and

\omega\to\big(X(\omega)\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})\big)(x)
belongs to

;
the continuity entails an estimate analogous to (

\sup_{x\in K}\big\|\partial^{\alpha}X\ast(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})(x)\big\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C\sup_{z\in K-L}\sup_{|\beta|\leq m}|\partial^{\alpha+\beta}(\chi(z)\rho_{\varepsilon}(z))|,

showing that conditions (L

\iota:{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}:L^{p}(\Omega))\to{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{p}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})

Example A.1
.

(a) Every (classical) process

with (almost surely) continuous paths defines a random Schwartz distribution via

\langle X(\omega),\varphi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)X(x,\omega)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptx.

(51)

Indeed,
\langle X(\omega),\varphi\rangle
is measurable as a limit of Riemann sums. If
\varphi_{n}\to\varphi
in
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\int\varphi_{n}(x)X(x,\omega)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptx\to\int\varphi(x)X(x,\omega)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptx

(b) If

belongs to
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}:L^{p}(\Omega))

51
) results in a process belonging to
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}:L^{p}(\Omega))

(x,\omega)

\left(\int_{\Omega}\big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)X(x,\omega)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptx\big|^{p}\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptP(\omega)\right)^{1/p}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\int_{\Omega}|X(x,\omega|^{p}\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptP(\omega)\right)^{1/p}|\varphi(x)|\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptx

with a suitable modification for

. It follows that
\varphi\to\langle X,\varphi\rangle
is a continuous map on
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with values in
L^{p}(\Omega)
.

(c) If
Y
is the
\alpha
th distributional derivative of a process

as in (a), (b), that is

\langle Y(\omega),\varphi\rangle=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\langle X(\omega),\partial^{\alpha}\varphi\rangle

it clearly defines a process in

in

(

or

Y_{\varepsilon}(x,\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial^{\alpha}(\chi\rho_{\varepsilon})(x-y)X(y,\omega)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0ptx.

Example A.2
.

(a) Recall that a
d

is a Gaussian stochastic process

\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
(i)

almost surely; (ii)
\mathrm{E}(W(x))=0
for all

E(W(x)W(y))=\prod_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\wedge y_{i}

with an appropriate sign-depending extension to all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

[

has almost surely continuous paths.

The special form of the covariance shows that
\mathrm{E}\big((X(x+h)-X(x))^{2}\big)={\mathcal{O}}(|h|)

belongs to
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}:L^{2}(\Omega))

(b) Taking the derivative of order

\dot{W}=\partial_{x_{1}}\ldots\partial_{x_{d}}W,\qquad\langle\dot{W},\varphi\rangle=(-1)^{d}\langle W,\partial_{x_{1}}\ldots\partial_{x_{d}}\varphi\rangle

Clearly,

Example A.3
.

A

Remark A.1
.

This remark serves to show how the boundedness conditions of Definition
5.1
can be enforced for Colombeau random fields, obtained as embeddedings
of classical or generalized stochastic processes.

(a) Enforcing boundedness: If

is a Gaussian random field with mean zero and unit variance as in Example

, one may transform it to a random field

Y(x)=F^{-1}(\Phi(X(x)))

is a given distribution function and

are distributed according to

will be globally bounded. Further,

inherits the pathwise properties of

, provided

is sufficiently smooth. The embedding (

Alternatively, if

into

via (
50

by a smooth cut-off function

m,&y\leq m\end{array}\right.

to enforce the range

and

(b) Enforcing almost certain local logarithmic type: Replacing the mollifier (

produces asymptotic bounds in terms of negative powers of

Example A.4
.

Here are two counterexamples illustrating the non-embedding properties alluded to in Remark
4.1
.

(a) The map
{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{p^{\prime}}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{G}}_{L^{p}}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d})
need not be injective. This happens already in the case of Colombeau random variables.
We display an element of
{\mathcal{E}}_{M,L^{p^{\prime}}}(\Omega)
which belongs to
{\mathcal{N}}_{L^{p}}(\Omega)
for all
p<p^{\prime}
, but not to
{\mathcal{N}}_{L^{p^{\prime}}}(\Omega)
.
We take
\Omega=[0,\infty)
with the standard exponential distribution. We set

u_{\varepsilon}(\omega)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\omega<{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{p^{\prime}/\varepsilon},\\[2.0pt]
{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{1/\varepsilon},&\omega\geq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{p^{\prime}/\varepsilon}.\end{array}\right.

In particular, at fixed
\omega\geq{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{p^{\prime}/\varepsilon}
,
u_{\varepsilon}(\omega)=0
for
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon(\omega)=p^{\prime}/\log\omega
. (In particular, this shows that
the net
(u_{\varepsilon})
defines an element of
{\mathcal{N}}_{L^{0}}(\Omega)
.) Let
p\geq 1
. Then

\mathrm{E}(u_{\varepsilon}^{p})={\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{p/\varepsilon}\int_{{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{p^{\prime}/\varepsilon}}^{\infty}{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-\omega}\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pt\omega={\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{(p-p^{\prime})/\varepsilon},

which is negligible for
p<p^{\prime}
but not negligible (namely equal to 1) for
p=p^{\prime}
.

(b)
{\mathcal{E}}_{M,L^{1}}(\Omega)\not\subset{\mathcal{E}}_{M,L^{0}}(\Omega)
.
Indeed, take
\Omega=[-1,1]
with the uniform distribution and set

v_{\varepsilon}(\omega)={\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{1/\varepsilon}\mathds{1}_{(-{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-1/\varepsilon},{\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{-1/\varepsilon})}(\omega),\qquad u_{\varepsilon}(\omega)=v_{\varepsilon}\big(\omega-\sin\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\big).

Then

\mathrm{E}(|u_{\varepsilon}|)=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}u_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pt\omega\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}v_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\hskip 1.0pt{\rm d}\hskip 0.0pt\omega=1,

so
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in I}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{M,L^{1}}(\Omega)
. On the other hand, for each fixed
\omega\in[-1,1]
, there is a sequence
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
such that

\omega=\sin\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{k}}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

u_{\varepsilon_{k}}(\omega)=v_{\varepsilon_{k}}\big(\omega-\sin\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon_{k}}\big)=v_{\varepsilon_{k}}(0)={\rm e}\hskip 1.0pt^{1/\varepsilon_{k}},

so
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}\not\in{\mathcal{E}}_{M,L^{0}}(\Omega)
.
