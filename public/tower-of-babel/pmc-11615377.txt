Causal relationship between inflammatory proteins, immune cells, and gout: a Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Rui Lai, Xinmin Deng, Xiaofeng Lv, Yumei Zhong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrior research has documented the association between certain circulating inflammatory proteins/immune cells and gout. However, the reliability of these associations remains contentious due to the constraints of conventional observational methodologies. This investigation seeks to reassess the causative link between circulating inflammatory proteins/immune cells and gout through the application of Mendelian randomization (MR). The study included 3576 individuals of European ancestry with gout, immune cell data from the GWAS summary of 3757 Sardinians, and circulating inflammatory protein data from 14,824 European ancestry participants for MR analysis. The principal approach employed was inverse variance weighted analysis to investigate the causal relationship between exposure and outcomes. The results indicate that CD28 on CD39+ CD4+ T cells may be associated with a reduced risk of gout. Additionally, CD45RA+ CD28- CD8bright T cells may also be associated with a reduced risk of gout. In contrast, DN (CD4-CD8-) T cells and IL-12β may increase the risk of gout. Some inflammatory proteins and immune cells show potential causal associations with gout. Nevertheless, additional experimental verification is warranted to assess the underlying mechanisms and confirm the causative role of these immune factors in gout pathogenesis.Keywords: Mendelian randomization 1, Inflammatory proteins 2, Immune cells 3, Gout 4, Causal relationship 5Subject terms: Gout, Immunology

Introduction

Gout, the predominant type of inflammatory arthritis, has witnessed a steady rise in global burden over the past few decades, with estimated prevalence rates ranging from 1 to 6.8%1. Gout predominantly afflicts men, with a male-to-female ratio of 4:12. Gout prevalence is higher in developed countries due to factors such as genetic susceptibility, socioeconomic conditions, dietary habits, comorbidities like chronic kidney disease, and medications affecting uric acid levels3. Predictions suggest that by 2060, gout mortality rates may surge by 55%4. Gout imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden, with annual direct medical costs per patient ranging from $6870 to $21,4675,6. Compared to those without gout, annual total care costs for gout patients are estimated to surpass $3000, with the total annual cost in the United States potentially reaching tens of billions of dollars7.

Previous research indicates a robust association between the immune system’s inflammatory mechanisms and gout. This relationship is characterized by the upregulation of inflammatory mediators, notably IL-1β, IL-8, and IL-17, alongside the engagement of diverse immune cells such as neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, and lymphocytes8. Monosodium urate (MSU) crystals are pivotal in instigating the immune reaction that is central to gout’s onset, eliciting both innate and adaptive immune responses9. The NLRP3 inflammasome’s role in the activation of pro-inflammatory cytokines is significant, and its involvement in gout’s pathogenesis is well-documented10–12. Within the adaptive immune system, T cells, especially Th1 and Th17 subsets, are implicated in gout’s pathogenesis through their cytokine production, which can exacerbate or alleviate inflammation11. Observations show increased levels of certain T cell subsets in individuals suffering from acute gouty arthritis11. Moreover, a variety of programmed cell death pathways, including pyroptosis, NETosis, necroptosis, and apoptosis, contribute to the pathogenesis of gout13.

Utilizing Mendel’s principles of genetic assortment, Mendelian randomization (MR) serves as an effective method for causal inference in epidemiological research14,15. This approach employs genetic variations as proxies to deduce the impact of risk factors on outcomes. MR stands out from other statistical techniques for its ability to mitigate biases from confounding factors and reverse causation16. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive two-sample MR analysis, integrating datasets encompassing circulating inflammatory proteins and immune cell characteristics. Our objective was to elucidate the causal relationships between these proteins, immune cell attributes, and the onset of gout.

Method

Data sources

Sources of circulating inflammatory protein data

The Olink Targeted Inflammation Panel was utilized to measure data on 91 circulating inflammatory proteins, which were obtained from GWAS data of 11 cohorts. These cohorts collectively included 14,824 participants of European ancestry17.

Sources of immune cell data

The genetic traits of immune cells were investigated through studies on immune cell GWAS data18. Approximately 22 million genetic variants were calculated using data from 3757 Sardinians, aiming to validate the association between immune traits GWAS data and autoimmune diseases.

Sources of gout data

The GWAS summary dataset for gout containing 3576 patients and 203,546 controls (GWAS ID: finn-b-GOUT) is accessible via the ieu open GWAS project (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets).

Gout in the finn-b-GOUT dataset was defined based on hospital discharge data using ICD codes: ICD-10 (M10), ICD-9 (2740), and ICD-8 (2740). Additionally, data from cause of death records with the same codes and KELA reimbursement code 212 were included to ascertain cases of gout.

Given that all data were sourced from publicly available studies, our research was exempt from the need for patient consent or ethical clearance.

Study design

We conducted a two-sample MR analysis to assess the causal relationships between 91 inflammatory proteins/731 immune cell traits and gout. MR uses genetic variation as a proxy for risk factors, necessitating that an instrumental variable (IV) fulfills three pivotal criteria to deduce causality: (1) The genetic variation must be directly associated with the exposure; (2) It should be unassociated with any confounders that could influence the exposure and the outcome relationship; (3) It must influence the outcome exclusively via the exposure, without alternate routes19.

Instrumental variable selection

According to recent studies, we used a cutoff value of P < 5E−06 to select SNPs related to circulating inflammatory proteins20. For SNPs related to immune cell traits, a cutoff value of P < 1E−05 was used21. To maintain independence among selected IVs and minimize linkage disequilibrium (LD) effects, SNP clustering was employed to eliminate LD (r2 = 0.001, kb = 10,000)21. We verified all gout-related SNPs in the LDlink database (https://ldlink.nih.gov/) to identify those related to potential confounders and outcomes. Additionally, palindromic SNPs with ambiguous strands (EAF > 0.42) were corrected. The MR-Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) method was used to eliminate outliers22. The F-statistic was used to assess the statistical strength. The IVs with an F-statistic greater than 10 were considered robust. The F-statistic was calculated as F = (N − K − 1)/K × R2/(1 − R2)23. SNPs with an F-statistic of no less than 10 were selected to ensure validity. Figure 1 for details.

Directed acyclic graph illustrating the Mendelian randomization (MR) framework for examining the causal relationship between exposure and outcome.

MR analysis and sensitivity analysis

We used R (version 4.4.0) and software packages (“TwoSampleMR” and "MR-PRESSO") in the study.

When evaluating the association between circulating proteins/immune cell traits and gout, we used three methods: inverse variance weighted (IVW), weighted median (WM), and Mendelian randomization-Egger (MR-Egger), with IVW as the primary method. The IVW method provides reliable results when all genetic variants are valid IVs24. When invalid IVs are present, WM and MR-Egger provide unbiased causal estimates25.

The Benjamini–Hochberg method adjusts for multiple testing by incorporating the false discovery rate (FDR). A P value < 0.05 and P(FDR corrected) < 0.2 were considered indicative of a causal relationship with gout. A P value < 0.05 and P(FDR corrected) < 0.05 were considered indicative of a significant causal relationship with gout.

In sensitivity analysis, heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test26. Horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated using the MR-Egger regression intercept and the global test of the MR-PRESSO estimator27. Additionally, we plotted funnel plots and scatter plots and conducted leave-one-out analysis.

Through MR analysis of circulating inflammatory proteins and immune cell traits, we identified several biomarkers that exhibit causal relationships with gout. Circulating inflammatory proteins

All circulating inflammatory proteins had F-statistics exceeding 10, indicating that they are all strong instrumental variables. As shown in Fig. 2A. IVW results showed a potential association between IL-12β and gout risk (IVW: OR 1.095, 95% CI 1.015–1.180, P = 0.019, P(FDR corrected) = 0.923), consistent with WM results (WM: OR 1.123, 95% CI 1.009–1.250, P = 0.034), while the MR Egger method did not find a specific association (P = 0.150). The MR-Egger intercept test (p-intercept = 0.946) did not show horizontal pleiotropy.

Immune cells

As shown in Fig. 2B. Through MR analysis of 731 immune cell traits, we identified 45 traits with causal relationships to gout. One trait exhibited horizontal pleiotropy and was thus excluded. Following FDR correction, three immune cell phenotypes had p-values less than 0.2: CD28 on CD39+ CD4+ T cells, CD45RA+ CD28- CD8br %T cells, and DN (CD4-CD8-) T cells. Two of these had p-values less than 0.05: CD45RA+ CD28- CD8br %T cells, and DN (CD4-CD8-) T cells, as shown in Fig. 3.

IVW results showed that CD28 on CD39+ CD4+ T cells were negatively associated with gout risk (IVW: OR 0.912, 95% CI 0.866–0.961, P = 5.91E-04), consistent with WM and MR Egger results (WM: OR 0.916, 95% CI 0.851–0.985, P = 0.019; MR Egger-OR 0.900, 95% CI 0.825–0.982, P = 0.031). The MR-Egger intercept test (p-intercept = 0.707) did not show horizontal pleiotropy (Supplementary Tables S3).

Similarly, IVW results showed that CD45RA+ CD28- CD8bright T cells were negatively associated with gout risk (IVW: OR 0.999, 95% CI 0.998–0.999, P = 5.98E-05), consistent with WM and MR Egger results (WM: OR 0.999, 95% CI 0.998–1.000, P = 0.032; MR Egger: OR 0.999, 95% CI 0.998–0.999, P = 0.002). The MR-Egger intercept test (p-intercept = 0.629) did not show horizontal pleiotropy (Supplementary Tables S3).

DN (CD4-CD8-) T cells were positively associated with gout risk (IVW: OR 1.192, 95% CI 1.093–1.301, P = 7.49E-05), consistent with WM results (WM: OR 1.191, 95% CI 1.050–1.351, P = 0.007). The MR Egger method did not find a specific association (P = 0.112). The MR-Egger intercept test (p-intercept = 0.737) did not show horizontal pleiotropy (Supplementary Tables S3).

Discussion

We conducted a comprehensive analysis of 91 circulating inflammatory proteins and 731 immune cell traits to identify those causally related to gout. From this analysis, we identified one circulating inflammatory protein and three immune cell traits with causal relationships to gout.

This MR study indicates that IL-12β is a risk factor for gout. IL-12β is a common subunit of IL-12 and IL-23. Several studies have shown that polymorphisms in the IL-12β gene are associated with increased susceptibility to gout28,29. Kim et al. 30 discovered that TLR4-mediated IL-12 production enhances the synthesis of IFN-γ and IL-1β via T-bet and pro-IL-1β. An MSU-induced acute inflammation mouse model showed that targeting IL-1β can reduce inflammation and provide therapeutic benefits31. This suggests that IL-12β, by participating in the IL-1β pathway, is a key mediator of the inflammatory response in gout.

Using MR analysis, we found that CD28 on CD39+ CD4+ T cells is a protective factor against gout. CD28 is a co-stimulation molecule that enhances T cell activation and survival32. CD39 is an ectonucleotidase on various immune cells that hydrolyzes ATP and ADP to AMP, which is then converted to adenosine by CD7333. This pathway produces adenosine, which has immunosuppressive effects and can inhibit inflammatory responses 34,35. CD39+ CD4+ T cells are a subpopulation of CD4 T cells with immunostimulatory properties and regulatory functions that help prevent excessive inflammation36. Under certain conditions, CD4 T cells can differentiate into Th1, Th2, Treg, and Th17 lineages37–39. Each subset of CD4 T cells plays a crucial role in immune responses. CD39+ CD4+ T cells have regulatory functions and help prevent excessive inflammation. During gout flares, a decreased Treg/Th17 ratio is associated with increased inflammation40. CD39 is a marker for identifying inhibitory CD4 T cells41. Its expression is associated with T cell exhaustion. However, CD39+ CD4+ T cells can also express activation markers and proliferate upon antigen stimulation, indicating their complex roles in immune regulation and response42,43. Given their regulatory roles, CD39+ CD4+ T cells may become potential therapeutic targets for gout. Enhancing their function or increasing their number may help control inflammation and promote the remission of gouty arthritis34,40.

This MR study indicates that CD45RA+ CD28- CD8bright T cells are protective factors against gout. CD45RA is typically expressed on naive T cells and certain memory T cell subsets, indicating a less differentiated state compared to CD45RO on more differentiated memory T cells44. CD28, a co-stimulatory molecule essential for T cell activation and survival45. T cells progressively lose CD28 expression as they undergo antigen-mediated activation and differentiation46. The absence of CD28 in T cells impacts their activation and function. CD8bright T cells are a unique subset of CD8+ T lymphocytes, representing an activated phenotype of CD8+ T cells47. These cells typically comprise cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) capable of directly eliminating infected or aberrant cells48. Within CD28- T cell subsets, CD8+ CD28-T cells were significantly correlated with age45. Initially observed in elderly individuals, these cells exhibit reduced immune responsiveness and telomere shortening, often associated with age-related replicative senescence49. Alterations in CD8+ CD28-T cells have been documented in autoimmune diseases and nearly all chronic inflammatory conditions49,50. Suciu-Foca et al. 51 initially characterized these cells as a subset of CD8+ T cells with inhibitory functions. Mounting evidence suggests that these cells represent a distinct subset of regulatory T cells with broad-ranging effects, including the suppression of T cell activation, proliferation, secretion of pro-inflammatory factors, and induction of apoptosis of activated T cells in vitro52,53. Adoptive transfer of these cells has significantly attenuated experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), mitigated acute allograft rejection, alleviated allergic diarrhea, and prevented experimental inflammatory bowel disease49,54,55.

In this MR analysis, DN (CD4-CD8-) T cells emerged as a contributing factor to gout risk. DN (CD4-CD8-) T cells, also recognized as double-negative (DN) T cells, represent a rare subset within peripheral T cells. Lacking CD4 and CD8 co-receptors, DN T cells can express either TCRαβ or TCRγδ, while lacking natural killer (NK) T cell markers56,57. Possessing both innate and adaptive immune functionalities, DN T cells modulate the activities of macrophages, CD8 T cells, and B cells58–61. Similar to Treg cells, DN T cells can perform Th functions by producing cytokines (IL-4, IL-17, IFN-γ and TNF-α), thereby exhibiting protective or pathological roles across various infections. Elevated DN T cell levels have been positively associated with disease severity in autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome (ALPS) and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)62,63. In lupus-prone mice, the IL-23/IL-17 axis prominently contributes to the pathogenicity of DN T cells, driving systemic inflammation and organ damage in systemic lupus erythematosus through the production of IL-17 and induction of anti-DNA autoantibody generation62,64,65. The infiltration of IL-17-producing DN T cells in the kidneys is linked to lupus nephritis, a prevalent and severe complication in systemic lupus erythematosus patients, indicating the potential of DN T cells as both biomarkers and therapeutic targets for lupus nephritis64. Vγ9/Vδ2 and Vδ1 constitute the primary subsets of γδT cells66. Studies have demonstrated that Vδ2 T cells from rheumatoid arthritis (RA) can migrate from peripheral blood to the synovium, significantly contributing to RA pathogenesis67. Similarly, Vδ2 T cells infiltrate joints and bolster inflammatory responses by producing cytokines like IL-1768. The study by Di et al.68 proposes that γδT cells, notably Vδ2 T cells, exert a pivotal influence on gouty arthritis pathogenesis. These cells accumulate within affected joints during acute episodes, fueling inflammation through cytokine secretion, particularly IL-17.

These findings provide crucial insights into the roles of proteins and immune cells in gout, aiding the advancement of gout immunotherapy. However, our study has several limitations that should be carefully considered. Firstly, we focused only on the causal relationship between inflammatory proteins, immune cells, and gout, without considering joint tissues. This limitation stems from the dearth of extensive, publicly accessible GWAS datasets pertaining to joint tissues. Additionally, the insufficiency of detailed clinical data for all subjects under scrutiny hindered the execution of further stratified analyses within the study cohort. Furthermore, given that our MR analysis predominantly relies on publicly accessible European ancestry databases, circumspection is warranted when extrapolating our findings to diverse populations. Future research in independent study populations will be required to confirm the reproducibility of these results. The applicability of MR in assessing causal effects in gout is constrained by the use of database analyses and the presence of multiple confounding factors. Consequently, the observed changes in CD39+ CD4+ T cells, CD45RA+ CD28- CD8 bright T cells, and CD28 on DN (CD4-CD8-) T cells may represent physiological responses to gout rather than definitive causal effects. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. Moreover, to comprehensively identify and screen immune features causally related to gout, we used relatively loose thresholds, potentially augmenting the risk of false positives. Our study only demonstrates partial correlations between inflammatory proteins, immune cells, and gout development, lacking empirical evidence to reveal potential mechanisms.

Conclusion

Our study comprehensively evaluates the causal relationship between inflammatory proteins and immune cells in the onset of gout, reinforcing the crucial roles of these elements in gout development and progression. It also provides fundamental insights and new perspectives for prevention, treatment, and novel therapy development. In the future, to further elucidate potential mechanisms with empirical evidence, we will conduct biological experiments to delve deeper into our findings.
