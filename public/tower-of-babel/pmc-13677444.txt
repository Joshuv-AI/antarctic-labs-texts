Evaluating HEART: Student Engagement and Health System Impact in a Person‐Centered Learning Health System Initiative
Authors: Tracey Carr, Stefanie Foster, Phoebe Stobart, Carson Squair, Taylor Spock, Candace Skrapek, Margaret King, Brenda Andreas, Noelle Rohatinsky, Christine Stobart, Caitlin Ward, Tina Zheng, Gerald Farthing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHealthcare Education and Research Training (HEART) is a cocurricular, engaged learning initiative that supported undergraduate students in acquiring and applying healthcare research skills. The project was guided by a learning health system (LHS)‐aligned model focused on the collection, synthesis, and rapid translation of patient‐reported experience data into actionable recommendations. In the 2023/2024 academic year, a cohort of 28 students completed the pilot project to report LHS‐based recommendations to healthcare directors. This paper reports the qualitative evaluation conducted in August 2024 following the pilot to assess the initiative's implementation, participant experience, and healthcare system impacts.MethodsVirtual, semi‐structured interviews were conducted with students who participated in HEART and health system directors from a provincial health authority. Interview guides were developed by the research team in collaboration with Patient Partners. Inductive content analysis (ICA) was used to categorize information significant to the research questions. Final themes were established using a team‐based approach.ResultsStudent interviews indicated that participants found HEART to be a meaningful learning experience that strengthened their understanding of the healthcare system and patient experiences and helped them build applied research skills. Health system directors also expressed strong satisfaction with the project, noting that student‐collected qualitative data identified service gaps, supported patient and community engagement, and contributed to measurable care improvements in at least one clinic. Suggested areas for project improvement included adjusting length of training sessions, increasing clarity around expectations and timelines, streamlining final reporting, and refining the patient interview process to gather more detailed information about specific patient experiences.ConclusionWhile various opportunities for improvement were identified, overall, HEART was determined to be a valuable opportunity for developing student healthcare research skills and was of direct benefit to health system partners as an LHS‐aligned initiative providing actionable data used to identify healthcare gaps and improve patient care. Feedback and recommendations drawn from the evaluation were used to adapt and improve HEART for the following academic year.Keywords: engaged learning project, experiential learning, patient‐oriented research, patient‐reported experience measures, project evaluation

Background

Learning health systems (LHSs) are designed to systematically integrate research data into healthcare delivery, driving decision‐making and informing continuous improvements to patient care, health outcomes, and the overall healthcare system [1]. LHSs create feedback loops where high‐quality health research data generated through routine care can be rapidly analyzed, translated into knowledge, and applied to clinical care, operations, and management, to influence professional practice, healthcare environments, and interventions [2]. In Canada, where healthcare systems continue to face significant pressures and patients encounter barriers to timely and equitable care [3, 4, 5], there is a push for healthcare systems to adopt person‐centered LHS approaches. However, the implementation, uptake, and evaluation of these approaches remain limited [6, 7, 8].

Collecting data in partnership with equity‐deserving groups is a central principle of LHSs [9]. Health inequities in Canada disproportionately affect Indigenous Peoples, racialized communities, people with low incomes, and other marginalized populations, contributing to poorer health outcomes, reduced life expectancy, and a greater prevalence of chronic conditions [10]. Although quantitative patient‐reported outcomes and experiences are increasingly used to inform LHS decision‐making, qualitative patient data remain less consistently integrated into system learning [11]. This gap is particularly important from an equity perspective, as survey‐based approaches may systematically under‐represent the groups whose perspectives are important to health‐system improvement by creating barriers related to digital literacy or the absence of a permanent mailing address, for example [12, 13]. Qualitative approaches can make visible how systemic bias and oppression are experienced in care and provide contextualized, actionable insights into the processes and circumstances contributing to inequitable outcomes that surveys alone may not capture [9, 14, 15]. Despite their potential value, qualitative approaches remain underused in LHS activities because they require time, relationship‐building, and analytic expertise that may exceed organizational capacity [16, 17].

Project Overview

To help address the limited use of qualitative, person‐centered approaches in health system learning, a prairie‐based Canadian university and federated college partnered with provincial patient‐oriented research and health system organizations to develop applied person‐centered research projects for students participating in the Healthcare Education and Research Training (HEART) pilot project. These projects were designed to gather patient experience data from underserved communities while providing meaningful, cocurricular opportunities for students to engage in healthcare research.

The project positioned undergraduate students as engaged student researchers who received training and applied experience in qualitative patient‐oriented research (POR), with emphasis on core skills related to research, evaluation, communication, and knowledge translation [18]. As part of the project, students contributed to the collection and interpretation of Patient‐Reported Experience Measures (PREMs) intended to support a person‐centered LHS in Saskatchewan [19].

HEART launched in Fall 2023 with a cohort of 28 students. The project unfolded in two phases across the 2023/2024 academic year: a training period from September to December 2023, followed by data collection, rapid analysis, and reporting from January to March 2024. In the first phase, students received training in a variety of relevant topics: research ethics; health system privacy and confidentiality policies; POR; Indigenous Health; sex, gender, and equity in healthcare; engaging with equity‐deserving groups; interview and focus group facilitation; and knowledge translation. Personnel from the provincial patient‐oriented research unit offered training in Building Research Relationships with Indigenous Communities (BRRIC). BRRIC aims to inform academic individuals how to respectfully involve Indigenous communities in research to produce the best results that benefit all parties [20]. After completing the training components, student‐led interviewing commenced in January 2024. Together, the pilot cohort completed 35 telephone interviews with patients who had previously responded to a patient experience survey piloted across four health networks and conducted four focus groups with new mothers regarding their experiences with system services for newborns. Participants included individuals from equity‐deserving and underserved communities. Following completion of data collection, students conducted thematic analysis and developed knowledge translation outputs based on the findings. In March 2024, students produced a detailed written report and an infographic summarizing key findings, both of which were shared with health system directors. Project instructors reviewed student‐led analyses and reports before they were shared with partners to support accurate interpretation of the findings and scientific integrity of the final products.

The purpose of this report is to present the results from the evaluation conducted after the completion of the project pilot. It focuses on participating students' personal assessments of their HEART experience, directors' perceptions of the potential benefits of the project to the Saskatchewan healthcare system, and both groups' recommendations for improving HEART for future cohorts. While this article focuses on the evaluation findings, the design and implementation of HEART are addressed in a separate experience report submitted by the team [21].

Evaluation Objectives

A single‐method qualitative evaluation of the HEART pilot took place from May to August 2024, consisting of semi‐structured interviews with the students who completed the project as well as the healthcare directors who had received and reviewed students' final reports. The aims of the evaluation were to (1) gather feedback from participants and healthcare administrators regarding their experience and recommendations for project improvement, (2) assess the implementation process of the project, including recruitment, training, and coordination, and (3) identify facilitators and barriers to the project's scalability and sustainability.

Methods

The HEART evaluation was conducted by a team with extensive experience in POR, LHS concepts and health services evaluation, including three experienced and diverse Patient Partners. Their expertise contributed to developing evaluation interview guides and supporting data collection and analysis. Team members met several times during the evaluation to facilitate data analysis and integrated knowledge translation. HEART's pilot evaluation was deemed ethics exempt by the University of Saskatchewan's Behavioral Research Ethics Board (E514).

Interviews

Students who completed the HEART pilot were invited to participate in either an individual interview or a 90‐min virtual focus group as part of the evaluation and were offered $25 CAD as compensation for their time. HEART pilot leads did not conduct the evaluation interviews; these were facilitated by one or two other researchers and, where possible, a Patient Partner. Four directors were also approached to provide the healthcare administrator perspective of the project and its ability to contribute to an LHS. Both student and healthcare administrator interviewees were provided with the interview questions in advance and signed an informed consent before beginning the interview process.

The student interview guide consisted of two overarching sections: Project Logistics, and Learning Outcomes, with a final open‐ended section for students to share any additional comments. The Project Logistics section focused on recruitment, training, and research phases of the project. Learning Outcomes questions involved individual learning and students' expectations. The interview guide for health system director interviews was divided into three sections: feedback on the reports received from students, feedback on the student‐led patient data collection process, and ideas for future engaged learning opportunities.

Analysis

All interview recordings were transcribed electronically, reviewed for errors such as mistranscription, then de‐identified to protect participant confidentiality. Data was coded with NVivo qualitative analysis software and inductive content analysis (ICA [22]). In the initial open‐coding round, the research team coded student transcript data related to the value of the project as a learning experience and feedback on its implementation, as well as health director interview data related to the project's contribution to a LHS [23]. Additional themes and higher order categories were identified in subsequent rounds. As further rounds of coding proceeded, summary tables were created to present interview data back to the research team in an accessible format, leading to rearrangement and refinement of the codes and their content. Student interview data was ultimately organized by the phases of the project (recruitment, training and data collection/reporting) and learning outcomes. Director interviews were categorized into (1) positive aspects of the project, (2) use of student data, and (3) suggestions for the future of the project.

Results

Sixteen of the 28 students (n = 16/28, 57.1%) involved in the pilot participated in an evaluation interview, 5 in individual interviews, 10 in focus groups, and 1 provided a written response to the interview questions. Student Interview Results

Recruitment

In their interviews, students described factors that motivated them to apply for HEART. Commonly mentioned was a desire to build their research knowledge and skills in the healthcare field, particularly in qualitative data collection and working directly with patients. Participants viewed the project as a way to gain professional competencies, fill gaps in their resumes, and engage in networking opportunities to support their future academic or career goals. Perspectives on the recruitment process were mixed. Some considered the criteria to join reasonable or felt it could be even more selective, focusing on student skills or motivation rather than basing admission on a first‐come, first‐served basis. Others felt they could be loosened to allow for participation from first‐ and second‐year students. Suggestions were made for promoting the project more widely across campus with more descriptive and targeted recruitment ads.

Training Phase

Overall, students felt expectations for the training phase of the project were clear and that they were adequately prepared to conduct POR, specifically citing the value of the more practical sessions such as interview training with Patient Partners. While they expressed appreciation for all training modules, sessions on knowledge translation and research ethics were noted by some to be overly time consuming or irrelevant to the research at hand. Recommendations were made to restructure these sessions and include more relevant, hands‐on learning rather than removing them entirely. Several students reported that including more interview and qualitative thematic data analysis training would have better prepared them for the fieldwork portion of the project.

Data Collection, Analysis, and Reporting

Although they described it as a valuable learning experience, there was a divide between students who felt expectations and timelines for data collection were very clear and those who felt they were not. Recommendations were made to use session time to conduct interviews rather than requiring groups to find additional meeting times to complete data collection tasks; to offer more flexible group schedules; and to provide interview and focus group schedules with more advanced notice. Reporting was a challenge for some participants who felt unclear about the expected structure and content of final reports written for health system partners. Again, suggestions were made to provide more guidance and templates to improve clarity and understanding.

Learning Outcomes

Students felt positively about the project learning outcomes indicating they learned much about various aspects of the healthcare system and developed a variety of professional and personal skills that will benefit their future goals. Several felt more informed about career options previously unknown or unexplored. Having the opportunity to engage in POR and focus on patient outcomes and experiences was cited as being of particular interest, and students highlighted the development of patient interaction skills as a valuable aspect of the experience.

Students also described how collecting experience data from equity‐deserving groups increased their awareness of health inequities and the ways social and community contexts shape experiences of care. In particular, students reflected on the distinct barriers experienced by Indigenous women, people accessing community‐based services, and patients in rural settings. They recognized that these experiences were not necessarily generalizable to the broader population but warranted specific attention within healthcare improvement efforts.

Project Structure and Implementation

Participants felt HEART was a supportive learning environment and were comfortable asking questions of project coordinators and their group. Commonly cited challenges were consistent with those under previous themes such as ambiguous timelines and confusion around the purpose of some training sessions, though opinions on these topics were not homogenous with several students describing the project as “clear” and “well laid out.” Small group dynamics and communication were identified as significant factors contributing to each research team's successes and challenges. For most, the workload was seen as manageable and did not interfere with their academic courses. Suggestions for improvement included setting structured deadlines, adjusting session frequency or length to reduce the amount of unassigned or underutilized time spent in a classroom environment, and distributing the workload more evenly across the length of the project. Health System Director Interview Results

Positive Aspects of the Project

Directors described the project as beneficial, noting that it provided valuable insight into service gaps and patients' experiences within the healthcare system. Collaboration with the university through HEART provided additional capacity to engage community partners and strengthen relationships with patients. Student researchers were described as “boots on the ground,” providing the capacity needed for sites to conduct important qualitative, patient‐centered research in a professional and ethical manner.

Outcomes of Student Data

Data collected by HEART student researchers identified issues which helped system partners to create and implement solutions to improve care delivery. Final reports were shared widely with clinic and nursing staff to directly inform their patient interactions and clinical practice. One director noted that the personal stories of patients in their care (i.e., the qualitative data) gathered by the students was shared with their clinical staff and solutions developed from that data, “(it's) halved our wait times at our clinic… and it's led to some innovations, including a triage list and also using AI in healthcare to help with charting and notes.” Further, student‐collected data led to several occasions where additional nursing staff were added to clinic sites, with one director stating, “we took (the student reported data) and then put in our proposals and that changed the game. We, from that, were able to get a clinic nurse, and we got accepted into Healthcare Excellence Canada. We were the only ones in Saskatchewan to get accepted into that program for improving of Northern remote care.” Two directors tied the data provided by HEART to their decision to add spots for same‐day primary care to their clinic scheduling.

Suggestions for Improvement

Although the student‐produced data reports were deemed helpful, it was mentioned that the formatting could be adjusted for conciseness with more clearly emphasized key themes to strengthen impact and increase uptake by clinicians and community partners. It was noted that, in some cases, the specifics of patient experiences, such as the location (clinic, hospital, home care visit, etc.) of an event, were not available and interviews could be better structured to gather this information. One director expressed an interest in having student recommended solutions included in reports. It was also recommended that more formal “roadmaps” for the project be established, with one director asking, “would there be a possibility of looking at kind of an annual cycle of this work? You know, plan ahead a little bit more being really strategic about where, what, who, how, when, that sort of thing and of course align it with what the student interests and their academic needs… is there an opportunity to look at doing this in a little bit more of a formal way going forward?”

Future Opportunities for Student Engagement

Directors saw many opportunities for embedding patient experience research projects in HEART's programming. Having students conduct a “province‐wide … comparison from one community to another to see… what can we learn from one another that might be working well in one community and for what reason is it working better in some places rather than others,” was one opportunity suggested for future engagement. Opportunities for more targeted interactions with particular patient groups such as those in transitional care, home care clients, or patients with complex needs were also identified. Two directors emphasized ways in which students could help with the tracking and assessment of recently launched quality improvement projects, such as collecting data longitudinally to track changes over time, with one asking, “how cool would it be to work with the students looking at, okay, here's our baseline, here are the improvement strategies that we're working on, how could students enhance this implementation?” Beyond research activities, the recommendation was made to enhance students' understanding of healthcare leadership roles and health system operations by creating opportunities for shadowing of health system leaders ‐ “I have not once had a student precept with me. And I would love that,” remarked one director.

Discussion

The evaluation of HEART demonstrates its effectiveness at engaging students in meaningful, qualitative healthcare research while providing actionable patient experience data back to the health authority.

Student participants described how the project taught them POR skills and improved their overall awareness of healthcare system patient experiences. Hands‐on learning components, including training in interview skills and direct patient engagement, were viewed as especially useful and interesting to students, which highlights the value of creating experiential learning opportunities for student researchers [24]. While there was some reported confusion about project expectations and timelines, overall, the student feedback underscores the idea that these are areas for refinement and clarification rather than factors that detract from the project's overall worth.

From the health system perspective, directors noted the value of student‐collected PREMs, which provided relevant insights into gaps in delivery and care, some of which might not have been effectively captured by quantitative measures. Student‐produced data contributed to tangible changes to patient care including increased nursing allocations and same‐day primary care scheduling. It also contributed to successful grant applications. Directors reported that engaging student researchers extended health system capacity to collect crucial patient experience data. These outcomes align with the principles of a LHS in which ongoing cycles of data collection, translation and application support continuous system improvements and operational decision‐making [1, 9]. However, specific examples of actioned changes were not reported consistently across all directors, and the evaluation was not designed to determine how frequently the findings resulted in changes, the extent of those changes, or whether they were sustained over time. Consequently, it remains unclear whether, and to what degree, the reported changes became embedded within routinized LHS cycles.

The challenges described in student interviews offered avenues for project refinement including opportunities for increased clarity around expectations and timelines. Directors suggested that improvements could be made to the reporting format and more specific details of patient experiences could be gathered during interviews. These insights suggest that creating some standardized components such as data collection and reporting templates may improve student learning and increase uptake of data in the healthcare system. Small group dynamics and team communication were seen by students as factors critical to their success in the project, indicating that there may be benefits to project coordinators composing teams more thoughtfully and providing clearer expectations for small group engagement.

Looking forward, many opportunities for expanding and strengthening HEART were identified by students and directors. These include additional training in interviewing and thematic data analysis, more comparative analysis of patient experiences across communities, targeted engagement of distinct patient populations, and developing opportunities for students to job shadow health system leaders. These types of expansions could enhance the project, enriching student learning and the quality of data used for LHSs in Saskatchewan.

The evaluation findings suggest the HEART pilot was beneficial to students and healthcare organizations and could serve as a model for integrating student‐engaged research into wider healthcare delivery and operations. By addressing the identified challenges and exploring opportunities for scalability and sustainability, HEART has the potential to make healthcare systems more responsive to patient needs while enhancing student engagement and learning outcomes.

HEART aligned with recommendations from a previous evaluation of patient‐reported measurement in Saskatchewan conducted by the first author, which emphasized the need to meaningfully engage Indigenous communities, newcomers, hard‐to‐reach populations, and other underserved groups, and to continuously evaluate and adapt people‐centered measurement initiatives [25]. HEART intentionally prioritized projects involving underserved communities and used qualitative methods to bring their contextualized experiences into system learning. Student reflections suggest that these experiences increased their awareness of health inequities and community‐specific barriers to care. The current evaluation also supports ongoing learning by identifying opportunities to refine future iterations of this novel approach to bringing people‐centered measurement into LHSs. In this way, HEART represents an evolving partnership with the potential to support a sustained, equitable, and person‐centered LHS in Saskatchewan.

Although student‐generated themes and reports underwent review by the instructors before dissemination, the evaluation did not include a formal assessment of the scientific quality of the students' analyses or compare their interpretations with those of experienced qualitative researchers. Given the current evaluation was conducted by members of the HEART instructor team, instructor perspectives on the quality of students' analyses and final products were not collected as a distinct source of data. Future evaluations of projects such as HEART could incorporate an independent assessment of student‐generated analyses and products to examine their credibility and quality.

Determining HEART's longer‐term impact on students' academic, professional, civic, or other pursuits was beyond the scope of this evaluation. As a result, this evaluation cannot determine whether the reported learning outcomes were sustained or translated into subsequent education, employment, research, or community engagement. To inform future follow‐up with HEART alumni, the team is conducting a scoping review of the long‐term impacts of community service‐learning participation among post‐secondary students, with attention to how these impacts are defined and measured in the literature. Findings from this review will guide the development of approaches for assessing longer‐term impacts of participation among HEART alumni.

Conclusion

This evaluation demonstrates that the HEART pilot was perceived by students and healthcare directors as a valuable learning experience with the potential to meaningfully impact healthcare delivery. It offered students opportunities to engage in hands‐on research skills training and patient‐engaged fieldwork while increasing the health system's capacity to collect, analyze, and use qualitative patient experience data. Findings suggest that student‐engaged research can help capture aspects of patient experience that may be absent from decision‐making processes through translating these insights and directly reporting findings to healthcare decision‐makers. To the authors' knowledge, qualitative patient experience data are not routinely collected in primary care and systematically integrated into person‐centered LHS processes.

Areas identified for project improvement, particularly those related to project structure, clarity of expectations and timelines, and opportunities for additional data collection and analysis, offer clear, actionable areas for project refinement. Further, the benefits described by directors, including increased community and patient engagement and measurable improvements to healthcare delivery, indicate that HEART aligns well with the principles of LHS [1, 9].

Further project development, more formal project planning, and responsive alignment with current health system priorities will ensure that HEART has strong potential for scalability and sustainability. As healthcare systems seek continued alignment with LHS principles including the rapid integration of qualitative patient care experience data, engaged learning models such as HEART represent a promising approach to continuous learning and improvement and a reproducible model for training future generations of healthcare researchers [18].
