Cross-field airway management for distal postintubation stenosis: a case of successful reconstruction without ECMO support
Authors: Carlos David Nájera-Acuña, Carlos Alberto Lárraga Díaz, Hugo Arturo Tirapé-Castro, Miguel Alcibíades Llano, Erick Tutín-Miniguano, Gabriel A Molina

Introduction

Postintubation tracheal stenosis (PITS) is an iatrogenic lesion provoked by prolonged pressure on the trachea, which, if not treated, leads to ulceration, inflammation, and ultimate fibrosis and stenosis [1, 2]. Any patient can be affected; therefore, careful surveillance is needed to prevent this troublesome complication [2]. We present a case of a PITS in a rare location near the bifurcation of the bronchus. A cross-field ventilation technique was used to prevent hypoxia and we were able to remove the stenosis without the use of ECMO.

Case report

Patient is a 54-year-old woman with a past medical history of severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) and intracerebral hemorrhage, for which she was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for neuroprotection. She remained in the ICU for over two months with prolonged mechanical ventilation, for which a tracheostomy was required. She had an adequate neurological resolution and fully recovered without any sequelae. She was discharged without the tracheostomy as she was able to eat, drink, breathe, and walk without any issues. Four weeks passed, and she began to suffer from dyspnea on exertion that turned into shortness of breath with stridor while walking. Before coming to the hospital, she presented with severe coughing, and she was unable to move around her own house.

On clinical evaluation, a low incision of the previous tracheostomy was noted in her chest, and audible stridor was detected. Her pulse oximeter reading was between 88 and 93%, and she had some roncus in her right chest. With these findings and her previous intubation history, a bronchoscopy was necessary, which revealed a 1 cm circumferential fibrous stenosis located 2 cm above the carina and 10 cm below the larynx; the width of the trachea in this segment was only 3 mm, and the regular bronchoscope, pediatric bronchoscope or even a guidewire to place a stent could not pass any further than this. (Fig. 1) A thoracic computed tomography also confirmed this finding, as well as left deviation of the trachea secondary to the fibrosis. (Fig. 2) Finally, spirometry (FEV, 49% predicted) and blood gas analysis (PaO2, 82 mmHg with FiO2 0.28) revealed moderate ventilatory compromise.

Bronchoscopy reveals a Cotton Grade 3 stenosis in the trachea

CT reconstruction of the tracheal stenosis

Post-intubation tracheal stenosis was diagnosed, and surgery was planned; however, due to the unavailability of ECMO (Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation) at our center, which is limited by resource constraints and the high cost of this procedure, it was not performed. Instead, a distal intubation with cross-field ventilation was planned. (Fig. 3)

Surgical position with cervical extension the old low tracheostomy and can be seen

After median sternotomy, the distal trachea and main bronchus were exposed by careful dissection, separating the aorta, brachiocephalic trunk, and innominate vein. An intraoperative bronchoscopy was also performed to locate the stenosis and mark it. The trachea was divided at the superior and inferior extent of the stenosis and completely removed. A 7 mm endotracheal tube was placed distally into the left bronchus to secure a distal airway, and selective ventilation was begun. This allowed us to operate comfortably, as keeping a tube in each bronchus would not have allowed us to perform a tension-free anastomosis; therefore, we only performed selective ventilation. (Fig. 4)

Operative field after mediastinal dissection: distal trachea, carina, and main bronchi are exposed with sternal retractor, the stenotic segment is clamped between reference sutures before resection

Lateral dissection was performed to minimize tension in the anastomosis while maintaining blood supply to it. The neck was then flexed, and a new airway was positioned through the proximal trachea into the distal airway, while traction sutures were used to minimize tension on the anastomosis.

The unique challenges posed by this distal stenosis location required several adaptations from conventional tracheal resection techniques. The surgical team elected to use intermittent ventilation through a sterile circuit extension rather than continuous ventilation, allowing for periods of apnea during critical portions of the anastomosis. This approach provided adequate oxygenation while maintaining an optimal surgical field.

The anastomosis was completed using interrupted 4 − 0 polyglactin 910 (Vycryl, Ethicon, NJ) sutures placed posterior to anterior and spaced every 0.4–0.5 cm. Anastomotic sutures were tied anterior to posterior, and the integrity of the anastomosis was tested underwater. Finally, a strap muscle was mobilized and fixed to the anterior anastomosis. A drain was placed, and the surgery was finalized without complications. (Fig. 5)

Tracheal rings including the stenotic segment. The central lumen shows circumferential fibrous thickening

Pathology reported thickening of the mucosa and submucosa. Although the surrounding cartilage was intact, a severe amount of fibrous tissue had undergone hyalinization.

Her postoperative course was uneventful; she was placed in the ICU but didn’t need any additional support, and was then moved to the general ward on her second postoperative day. She remained asymptomatic, without wheezing or cough, the drain was removed on the third postoperative day, due to low and serous production, and she was discharged without complications.

On follow-up, the patient is doing well, without dyspnea or respiratory symptoms, 3 months after the procedure.

Discussion

Postintubation tracheal stenosis (PITS) is an iatrogenic lesion provoked by prolonged pressure on the mucosa by the endotracheal tube or the cuff of the balloon [1, 2]. When the pressure exceeds 30 mm, the mucosa and the underlying cartilages develop ischemia, which, if not treated, leads to ulceration and inflammation of the cartilages [1]. This necrosis will result in the loss of mucosa and structural integrity of the trachea, and since the endotracheal or tracheostomy remains in place, the airway will also remain patent [2]. When the tube is removed, healing of the necrotic region will begin, which leads to fibrosis, scar tissue, and strictures [2, 3]. This typically occurs within 36 weeks after extubation [1, 3]. This was first recognized in the 1940s when intubation replaced external respiratory devices in the poliomyelitis epidemic [1, 4]. At that time, endotracheal tubes were not designed to fit the anatomy of the trachea [5]. Currently, there are newer tubes and cuffs (high volume-low pressure) that allow for a more anatomical conformation of the cuff into the D-shaped section of the trachea, significantly reducing airway injury [1, 5]. Also, constant surveillance to monitor intracuff pressure and maintain it below 30 mm Hg, or as low a level as needed to create an adequate seal for ventilation and to prevent ischemia [3, 4].

Nonetheless, even with these measures, the tube and the cuff can still cause injury and stenosis. (up to 11% of the cases) Other factors include cuff pressure, size of the tube relative to the tracheal lumen, duration of intubation, cardiovascular status during intubation, movement of the tube during intubation, sex, and age [2, 4, 5]. There are many spectrums of lesions associated with the PITS, depending on the location of the stenosis [6]. Cuff stenosis, which is a circumferential stenosis that appears where the sealing cuff is placed and usually within 3–4 cm of the cricoid cartilage [7]. Subglottic stenosis, an eccentric stenosis when there is injury of the endolaryngeal structures distal to the vocal cords [1, 6, 7]. They present as a result of a cricothyroidostomy or an oversized endotracheal tube [1, 2]. Glottic stenosis is related to endotracheal intubation with injury to the posterior vocal cords and arytenoid cartilages when the patient is in a supine position [3, 6].

This case highlights several important considerations in the management of complex tracheal stenosis. The distal location near the carina presented unique technical challenges that required modifications to standard surgical approaches; however, thanks to an imaginative technique to prevent hypoxia, we were able to complete the objective without the use of ECMO.

Nonetheless, although PITS usually presents as a single lesion, multiple intubations, reflux, and aspirations may worsen the original injury and exacerbate the mucosal injury [1, 3]. Patients clinically have a history of intubation and present with symptoms of stridor, tachypnea, the use of accessory muscles, or exertional dyspnea [1, 4]. Stenosis must be less than 30% of the normal diameter of the trachea for patients to be symptomatic at rest [2, 4]. When wheezing occurs, it usually means that the airway diameter is 5 mm or less [5]. Wheezing may be present, and if there is an abnormal voice, it’s suggestive of a glottic or subglottic stenosis [6]. In our case, we believe that the stenosis could have occurred due to the prolonged intubation or the low tracheostomy; nonetheless, as the stenosis worsened, symptoms appeared, and a diagnosis was made.

Chest radiographs typically demonstrate clear lung fields but an altered tracheal air column; however, computed tomography is the ideal imaging method for preoperative planning [2, 3]. Bronchoscopy is an essential tool, as it can be diagnostic and therapeutic for dilating stenosis [5]. Yet, airway dilation will provide a temporary period of palliation for PITS, as surgical therapy will be the definitive treatment [1, 4]. The optimal treatment of PITS is primary resection and reconstruction with preservation of blood supply and without tension, yielding excellent results (up to 87.5% had satisfactory results) with a low mortality rate of 2.4% [2, 7, 8]. Nonetheless, some patients will need a protective tracheostomy to protect the anastomosis and the underlying airway [2, 4]. Intraoperative ECMO support is temporarily performed for patients with cardiopulmonary risk factors for surgery, helping to make the operation more accessible and safer [7].

PTS remains a burden for critically ill patients, as intubation is needed to provide life support for a patient; however, it can create a devastating complication with severe morbidity if left untreated. Therefore, strict surveillance is key.

Airway surgery presents a unique situation in which the surgeon and anesthesiologist must share the airway to provide oxygenation while maintaining a clear view of the trachea [9]. Ever since Grillo et al. described it in the 1960s, multiple techniques have been developed to provide appropriate airway management in airway surgery, such as cross-field ventilation, jet ventilation, nonintubated spontaneous breathing, and Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [10]. Cross-field ventilation is named when the endotracheal tube is placed distally in the airway to maintain control during surgery, while coordinating ventilation and episodes of apnea [9, 10].

This case of postintubation tracheal stenosis near the bronchial bifurcation illustrates that successful reconstruction can be achieved through meticulous surgical technique and adaptive airway management strategies such as cross-field ventilation. The excellent outcome without the use of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation demonstrates that alternative approaches can be effective in resource-constrained settings.

Conclusion

PTS is an iatrogenic lesion that has declined in incidence thanks to the development of new technology and strict surveillance. Nonetheless, it remains a troublesome complication of a critically ill patient that can severely hinder their recovery and quality of life; therefore, the medical team must remain vigilant at all times to prevent these potentially preventable complications.

This case also reinforces the importance of careful preoperative planning and intraoperative decision-making when managing complex airway pathology. Future efforts should focus on protocol development for high-risk patients and standardization of surveillance strategies following prolonged intubation.
