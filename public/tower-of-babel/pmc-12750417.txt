Letter to the Editor: Optimizing Pulmonary Health and Quality of Life in Breast Cancer Survivors: A Randomized Controlled Trial Combining Incentive Spirometry and Aerobic Exercise
Authors: Parth Aphale, Shashank Dokania, Himanshu Shekhar
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

The original research article by Sari et al,1 “Optimizing Pulmonary Health and Quality of Life in Breast Cancer Survivors: A Randomized Controlled Trial Combining Incentive Spirometry and Aerobic Exercise,” is a valuable contribution to oncology rehabilitation. The authors are to be commended for investigating a low-cost, accessible intervention targeting pulmonary impairment and quality of life deficits after radiotherapy. Their finding that a combined approach yields superior improvements is clinically meaningful and deserves attention. However, several methodological and practical issues warrant discussion.

A key limitation lies in the characterization of the control group. Although the authors acknowledge the absence of standardized aerobic protocols for postradiotherapy patients, the comparison of a structured combined program with a generic aerobic-only regimen may have magnified the observed differences. Without precise reporting of aerobic intensity and progression in the control arm, it is difficult to isolate the contribution of incentive spirometry. A 3-arm design, including a sham or placebo spirometry group, could have better clarified whether the observed gains reflect a true synergistic effect rather than “training to the test.”

The 8-week duration provides useful short-term data but leaves questions regarding long-term adherence and sustainability unanswered. Cancer survivors frequently struggle with ongoing symptoms and logistical barriers that reduce program maintenance. Evidence suggests that the benefits of exercise interventions often diminish without structured follow-up.2 Longer-term assessments in unsupervised, real-world settings are essential to confirm the durability of both pulmonary and quality of life improvements.

Another missing dimension is cost-effectiveness. Health systems increasingly require economic evidence before scaling rehabilitation strategies. Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings on the cost-effectiveness of physical activity programs for cancer survivors.3,4 Embedding economic evaluations into clinical trials would provide essential data for policy adoption and implementation.

Finally, while pulmonary outcomes were central, cardiovascular health deserves greater emphasis. Aerobic exercise plays a protective role against cancer therapy-related cardiotoxicity, a leading survivorship concern.5 Evaluating whether incentive spirometry adds incremental cardioprotective benefits alongside aerobic training would strengthen the clinical case for its routine use.

In summary, the study by Sari et al1 provides a timely step toward integrating simple rehabilitation strategies into survivorship care. To ensure real-world translation, future research should standardize comparator interventions, evaluate long-term sustainability, incorporate cost-effectiveness analyses, and explore cardioprotective outcomes.
