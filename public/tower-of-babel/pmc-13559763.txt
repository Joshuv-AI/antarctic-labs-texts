Synthesis, characterization, and adsorption of novel fatty acid curcumin derivatives on cotton fibers pre-mordanted with iron(ii) sulfate heptahydrate
Authors: Evaldir Damião Aparecido Lopes, Bruno Campos da Silva, Sher Ali, Caroline Da Ros Montes D'Oca, Izabel C. Riegel-Vidotti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the effect of fatty acid modification on curcumin by evaluating (i) its photostability through photodegradation kinetics in DMSO under LED irradiation, and (ii) its ability to interact with pre-mordanted cotton fibers. Two novel monoacylated (MCFD) and diacylated (DCFD) curcumin fatty derivatives were synthesized via Steglich esterification using oleic acid as a model compound, achieving yields of 95 and 93.6%, respectively. Structural characterization was performed by FTIR, 1H NMR, and 13C NMR spectroscopy. The photodegradation behavior of curcumin and MCFD under the investigated conditions followed first-order kinetics; however, MCFD exhibited enhanced photostability, with a half-life of 542 min compared to 341 min for curcumin. Adsorption studies on cotton fibers pre-mordanted with FeSO4·7H2O revealed that dye uptake was influenced by both pH and temperature. Under neutral pH conditions, MCFD followed a pseudo-first-order kinetic model and was best described by the Langmuir isotherm, whereas turmeric extract followed a pseudo-second-order kinetic model and was better represented by the Freundlich isotherm. No measurable adsorption was observed for DCFD under the same conditions. Thermodynamic analysis indicated that the adsorption of both turmeric extract and MCFD was spontaneous and exothermic. Fatty acid modification altered the adsorption behavior of curcumin while preserving measurable interactions with pre-mordanted cotton fibers; independently, MCFD exhibited a longer photodegradation half-life than curcumin in DMSO. These results provide proof-of-concept evidence that fatty acid modification can enhance curcumin photostability while retaining its adsorption capacity onto pre-mordanted cotton fibers.

Introduction

The textile industry is confronted with critical environmental challenges, primarily arising from the extensive use of synthetic agents during manufacturing processes,1,2 including bleaching, dyeing, and finishing, which release substantial quantities of organic and inorganic pollutants into the aquatic environment.3–5 A significant concern is the application of synthetic dyes, particularly azo dyes, which are widely recognized for their coloring efficiency but also for their toxic, mutagenic, and carcinogenic potential, posing risks to the environment and human health.6–8 Regulatory bodies worldwide, including those in the United Kingdom, the European Union, and the United States, have imposed stringent restrictions or outright bans on the use of azo dyes.9–11 Consequently, this regulatory shift has intensified the search for safer and more sustainable alternatives focused on pollution reduction and resource conservation.12–14

Natural dyes represent a viable alternative for sustainable dyeing. Primarily derived from plants and minerals, they are abundant and are generally considered more environmentally compatible than many synthetic dyes, while also offering functional properties such as antimicrobial activity, deodorization, and UV protection.15–17 They have long been used for painting, dyeing, and decoration of artifacts.18,19 Among these compounds, curcumin has emerged as a particularly promising candidate due to its availability and multifunctional properties.20–23 Curcumin is a polyphenolic compound extracted from the rhizomes of Curcuma longa L., which contain approximately 2.3–9.2% (w/w) curcuminoids, with curcumin as a predominant component.24,25 Turmeric powder, characterized by its yellow-orange coloration, is commonly used in the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic industries and as a natural colorant.26–29

The application of curcumin as a natural dye is limited by its poor stability, particularly under light exposure, which results in rapid color degradation and low color fastness.30–34 Photodegradation proceeds primarily through photooxidation of the heptadienone chain and the β-diketone moiety, involving reactive oxygen species generation and leading to the formation of colorless phenolic fragments such as vanillin and ferulic acid.30,35–41 Structural modification of curcumin represents a viable approach to overcome these limitations. Although several curcumin derivatives have been synthesized, most studies have focused on enhancing its biological activity for therapeutic applications.42–45 In studies using curcumin as a natural dye, structural modifications have mainly involved the incorporation of UV-absorbing or hydrophilic groups to improve photostability and solubility.46–48

Studies investigating the properties of fatty acid curcumin derivatives remain limited, particularly with regard to adsorption behavior, kinetic and thermodynamic analyses, and photostability. Therefore, this study addresses this gap by evaluating the effect of fatty acid modification on the photostability of curcumin through photodegradation kinetics in DMSO under LED irradiation, as well as its adsorption capacity onto pre-mordanted cotton fibers. The incorporation of fatty acid chains represents a promising and sustainable strategy in this context. Furthermore, the use of fatty acids derived from vegetable oils or industrial by-products is consistent with green chemistry principles and supports the utilization of renewable resources.49–51 In the present work, two novel fatty acid curcumin derivatives were synthesized using oleic acid as a model substrate. Their adsorption behavior on cotton fibers pre-mordanted with iron(ii) sulfate heptahydrate under neutral pH conditions was investigated through kinetic and thermodynamic analyses, whereas the effect of fatty acid modification on the solution-phase photodegradation behavior was evaluated separately (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Chemical reagents

All reagents and solvents were obtained from commercial suppliers and used without further purification unless otherwise specified. Ethyl acetate, hexane, and dichloromethane (CH2Cl2) (all from Sigma-Aldrich) were distilled prior to use. N,N′-Dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC, Sigma-Aldrich) and 4-(dimethylamino)pyridine (DMAP, Sigma-Aldrich) were dried under vacuum before use. The following chemicals were used as received: curcumin (≥65%, Sigma-Aldrich), oleic acid (Sigma-Aldrich), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, analytical grade, Synth), chloroform (CHCl3, analytical grade, Synth), Tween 80 (Êxodo Científica), absolute ethanol (99.5%, analytical grade, Anidrol), monobasic potassium phosphate (99.0%, anhydrous, analytical grade, Synth), sodium hydroxide (99.0%, analytical grade, Biotec), and iron(ii) sulfate heptahydrate (99.0%, FeSO4·7H2O, analytical grade, Biotec).

Synthesis of novel fatty acid curcumin derivatives via Steglich esterification

The synthesis of novel fatty acid curcumin derivatives was carried out using Steglich esterification. Curcumin (383.7 mg, corresponding to 249.4 mg based on a minimum purity of 65%, 0.677 mmol) was dissolved in CH2Cl2 (20 mL) in a round-bottom flask equipped with a magnetic stirrer. Oleic acid (573.7 mg, 2.031 mmol), DCC (139.7 mg, 0.677 mmol), and DMAP (27.3 mg, 0.223 mmol) were sequentially added to the solution. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h, and progress was monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC).

Isolation and purification of curcumin derivatives

After completion of the reaction, the resulting precipitate of N,N′-dicyclohexylurea (DCU) was removed by filtration. The filtrate was subjected to liquid–liquid extraction using 5% aqueous hydrochloric acid (2 × 20 mL) and ethyl acetate (2 × 20 mL). The combined organic layers were dried over anhydrous magnesium sulfate (MgSO4), filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude product was then purified by column chromatography using ethyl acetate : hexane (20 : 80). The same work-up and purification procedure was applied to both MCFD and DCFD.

Characterization

Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectra were recorded using a Cary 60 UV-Vis spectrophotometer over the wavelength range of 300–600 nm. Measurements were performed using either quartz or optical polystyrene cuvettes, with illumination provided by tungsten and deuterium lamps. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were collected on a Bruker Vertex 70 FTIR spectrometer in transmission mode over the range of 4000–400 cm−1, with a resolution of 2 cm−1 and a total of 32 scans per sample. Samples were prepared as KBr pellets and analyzed at room temperature. 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were obtained using Bruker Ascend spectrometers operating at 200, 400, or 600 MHz.

Photodegradation study

Photodegradation experiments were carried out in a controlled irradiation chamber using optical polystyrene cuvettes containing solutions of the dyes in DMSO at an initial concentration of 2.35 × 10−5 mol L−1. Each cuvette held 4.0 mL of solution and was sealed with parafilm to prevent solvent evaporation. Samples were exposed to continuous irradiation from a light-emitting diode (LED) lamp for a total duration of 28 h. The emission spectrum of the lamp spans from approximately 400 to 750 nm, with two main emission bands centered at 461 (sharp peak) and 544 nm (broad band). This spectral profile covers the entire visible absorption range of curcumin, ensuring effective photoexcitation. At regular intervals of 30 min, irradiation was temporarily stopped to allow for the measurement of absorbance using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Kinetic adsorption experiments

Cotton fibers were pre-mordanted by immersing 10 g of fabric in an aqueous solution of FeSO4·7H2O (3% w/v) at 50 °C for 1 h. After mordanting, the fibers were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and air-dried at room temperature, following the procedure described in the literature.52 Dye solutions were prepared by dissolving the synthesized dyes and the dispersing agent Tween 80 in a 1 : 1 (w/v) ratio, followed by the addition of 2–4 mL of absolute ethanol. The mixtures were sonicated until fully homogenized, then dried in an oven at 45 °C. Subsequently, purified water was added to adjust the solutions to the desired concentrations.

Batch adsorption experiments were performed using 125 mg of cotton fibers pre-mordanted with FeSO4·7H2O as the adsorbent and 10 mL of dye solution in amber glass vials, which were hermetically sealed with rubber stoppers and aluminum caps. Results and discussion

1. Synthesis of novel fatty acid curcumin derivatives via Steglich esterification

The synthesis of fatty acid esters of curcumin was performed via Steglich esterification following a modified literature procedure,53 as illustrated in Scheme 1. Curcumin was reacted with oleic acid as a model fatty acid in the presence of DCC and DMAP, and the reaction progress was monitored by TLC using hexane/ethyl acetate (70 : 30, v/v) as the eluent. The esterification is proposed to proceed through DCC-mediated activation of the carboxylic acid group of oleic acid, followed by acyl-transfer catalysis by DMAP. The activated acyl intermediate can then react with one or both phenolic hydroxyl groups of curcumin, yielding MCFD or DCFD, respectively, with DCU formed as the by-product.54,55 This mechanistic pathway explains why the relative formation of MCFD and DCFD was governed by the oleic acid/DCC stoichiometry, as summarized in Table 1.

To achieve selective synthesis of each product, the reaction conditions were systematically optimized by varying the fatty acid and DCC stoichiometries. However, CHCl3 (Entry 3) was used as part of the initial screening, whereas experiments used CH2Cl2 owing to its availability in our laboratory and comparable or improved reaction performance. This substitution did not adversely affect reaction performance and, in some cases, resulted in improved product yields.

Spectroscopic characterization by FTIR, UV-Vis, 1H NMR, and 13C NMR confirmed the successful synthesis of two yellow, pasty compounds. The monoacylated product was identified as oleate of 4-((1E,6E)-7-(4-hydroxy-3-methoxyphenyl)-3,5-dioxohepta-1,6-dien-1-yl)-2-methoxyphenyl, hereafter referred to as the monoacylated curcumin fatty derivative (MCFD). The diacylated product was identified as dioleate of ((1E,6E)-3,5-dioxohepta-1,6-dien-1,7-diyl)bis(2-methoxy-4,1-phenylene), hereafter referred to as the diacylated curcumin fatty derivative (DCFD).

The product distribution was strongly influenced by reagent stoichiometry. When 0.5 equivalents of oleic acid were employed (Entry 1), MCFD was obtained as the major product in 95.0% yield, although a fraction of unreacted curcumin was recovered. Equimolar proportions of curcumin and oleic acid (Entry 2) also favored the formation of MCFD in 72.3% yield, while DCFD was produced in 20.6% yield. Increasing the amount of oleic acid (Entries 3–4) shifted the reaction toward DCFD formation; however, purification became more difficult because of co-elution with excess oleic acid. The similar physicochemical properties of DCFD and oleic acid hindered chromatographic separation and likely contributed to overestimated isolated yields under these conditions.

Further improvements were achieved by increasing the amount of DCC to two equivalents (Entries 5–6), ensuring more efficient activation of the carboxylic acid. Under these conditions, the use of three equivalents of oleic acid enabled the selective synthesis of DCFD in 93.6% yield. These results demonstrate that the degree of acylation can be effectively controlled through reagent stoichiometry, allowing selective access to either MCFD or DCFD. The yields obtained are comparable to, or higher than, those previously reported for curcumin esterification reactions.53,56,57

The FTIR spectrum of curcumin (Fig. 3a) shows a broad absorption band at 3385 cm−1, attributed to the O–H stretching vibrations of phenolic groups. The characteristic CO stretching vibration of the α,β-unsaturated diketone system, overlapping with the CC stretching of the conjugated alkene, appears at 1628 cm−1. A distinct band at 1603 cm−1 corresponds to the aromatic CC stretching vibration. Additionally, the C–O bending vibration associated with phenolic groups is observed at 1281 cm−1, in agreement with previous reports.58–60

The FTIR spectrum of oleic acid (Fig. 3b) shows characteristic symmetric and asymmetric –CH2 stretching vibrations at 2854 and 2926 cm−1, respectively. An absorption band at 3006 cm−1 is assigned to the C–H stretching vibration of the vinyl group. The strong band at 1711 cm−1 corresponds to the CO stretching vibration of the carboxylic acid, while the C–O stretching and O–H bending vibrations are observed at 1285 and 1413 cm−1, respectively. A distinct band at 937 cm−1 is associated with the out-of-plane deformation of the hydroxyl group, characteristic of oleic acid dimers.61,62

The FTIR spectrum of MCFD (Fig. 3c) indicates the formation of a monoacylated product. The appearance of a new absorption band at 1763 cm−1, assigned to the CO stretching vibration of an aromatic ester, supports the esterification of one phenolic hydroxyl group. The persistence of the O–H stretching band at 3412 cm−1 indicates that one hydroxyl group remains unreacted, consistent with the proposed monoacylated structure.

In contrast, the FTIR spectrum of DCFD (Fig. 3d) shows the complete disappearance of the O–H stretching band together with the appearance of a strong ester carbonyl band at 1765 cm−1, indicating esterification of both phenolic hydroxyl groups. These spectral features are consistent with the formation of the proposed diacylated derivative.

NMR spectroscopy of oleic acid, curcumin, MCFD, and DCFD

Structural elucidation of the synthesized derivatives was further supported by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy. All 1H NMR spectra are presented in Fig. 4, and the corresponding signal assignments are discussed below.

1H NMR of (a) oleic acid (200 MHz, CDCl3); (b) curcumin (600 MHz, CDCl3); (c) MCFD (400 MHz, CDCl3); and (d) DCFD (600 MHz, CDCl3).

The formation of MCFD is supported by the splitting of the methoxy proton signals. Monoacylation of one phenolic hydroxyl group breaks the molecular symmetry of curcumin, rendering the two aromatic fragments chemically inequivalent. In curcumin (Fig. 4b), the chemically equivalent methoxy groups appear as a singlet at 3.94 ppm (6H). Upon monoacylation, the loss of molecular symmetry results in two distinct singlets at 3.86 and 3.95 ppm (3H each), as observed in Fig. 4c. In contrast, DCFD (Fig. 4d) regains molecular symmetry following diacylation of both phenolic hydroxyl groups, resulting in a single methoxy proton signal at 3.87 ppm (6H). These changes are consistent with the proposed mono- and diacylated structures.

For oleic acid (Fig. 4a), the α and β methylene protons adjacent to the carbonyl group resonate at 2.35 and 1.63 ppm, respectively. In MCFD and DCFD (Fig. 4c and d), these signals appear at 2.58 and 1.77 ppm, respectively. These downfield shifts are consistent with changes in the electronic environment after esterification, likely arising from the conjugated aromatic system of curcumin.

No significant changes were observed for the remaining oleic acid proton signals: the terminal methyl group appeared at 0.88 ppm, methylene groups at 1.27 and 1.34 ppm, allylic protons at 2.02 ppm, and olefinic protons at 5.36 ppm, in agreement with literature data.63,64 These results indicate that the aliphatic chain and carbon–carbon double bond of oleic acid remained chemically unchanged during the esterification reaction.

The aromatic region of the 1H NMR spectrum also revealed significant changes upon esterification. In curcumin, aromatic proton signals were observed at 6.94 ppm (d, J = 8.2 Hz, 2H), 7.13 ppm (dd, J = 8.2, 1.8 Hz, 2H), and 7.05 ppm (d, J = 1.8 Hz, 2H), while the vinylic protons appeared at 6.48 ppm (d, J = 15.8 Hz, 2H) and 7.59 ppm (d, J = 15.8 Hz, 2H). In MCFD, the loss of molecular symmetry caused by monoacylation resulted in duplication and slight shifts of the aromatic and vinylic proton signals. Aromatic resonances appeared at 6.93 ppm (d, J = 8.2 Hz, 1H), 7.14 ppm (m, 1H), 7.05 ppm (m, 1H), 7.04 ppm (m, J = 8.1 Hz, 1H), 7.12 ppm (m), and 7.11 ppm (m, 1H). Similarly, the vinylic protons were observed at 6.49 and 6.54 ppm (d, J = 15.77, 15.8 Hz, 1H each) and 7.596 and 7.603 ppm (d, J = 15.77, 15.8 Hz, 1H each). The duplication of these signals is consistent with the formation of a monoacylated product, in which the two aromatic rings are no longer chemically equivalent.

In contrast, the 1H NMR spectrum of DCFD showed a simplified aromatic region due to the restoration of molecular symmetry following diacylation. Aromatic signals were observed at 7.05 ppm (d, J = 7.9 Hz, 2H), 7.14 ppm (m, 2H), and 7.12 ppm (m, 2H), while the vinylic protons appeared as two doublets at 6.57 ppm (d, J = 15 Hz, 2H) and 7.62 ppm (d, J = 15 Hz, 2H). The reappearance of equivalent aromatic and vinylic environments provides further evidence supporting the proposed diacylated structure. Collectively, the observed changes in the methoxy, aromatic, and vinylic proton resonances provide strong spectroscopic evidence for the successful formation of MCFD and DCFD.

Structural assignments were further supported by the 13C NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 5). For both products (Fig. 5c and d), the signal corresponding to the carboxylic acid carbonyl carbon of oleic acid at 180 ppm (Fig. 5a) was absent, replaced by new ester carbonyl signals at 171.7 ppm (MCFD) and 171.6 ppm (DCFD), providing unambiguous evidence of ester bond formation. Additional evidence for the proposed structures was obtained from the carbonyl carbon signals of the curcumin skeleton. In MCFD, the loss of molecular symmetry resulting from monoacylation led to the appearance of two distinct signals at 184.5 and 181.9 ppm for the β-diketone carbonyl carbons. In contrast, DCFD exhibited a single signal at 183.1 ppm, reflecting the restoration of molecular symmetry following diacylation.

13C NMR of (a) oleic acid (50 MHz, CDCl3); (b) curcumin (150 MHz, CDCl3); (c) MCFD (100 MHz, CDCl3); and (d) DCFD (150 MHz, CDCl3).

The carbon signals associated with the oleic acid chain remained essentially unchanged in both derivatives. Olefinic carbons were observed at 130 and 129.7 ppm, the terminal methyl carbon at 14.1 ppm, and the methylene carbons between 22.7 and 34 ppm. These results indicate that the aliphatic chain of oleic acid remained structurally preserved during the esterification reaction.

Further evidence of symmetry changes was observed for the methoxy carbon signals. In curcumin (Fig. 5b), the methoxy carbons appeared as a single resonance at 56 ppm. In MCFD (Fig. 5c), this signal split into two distinct resonances at 56 and 55.9 ppm due to the loss of molecular symmetry. Conversely, DCFD (Fig. 5d) displayed a single methoxy carbon signal at 55.9 ppm, consistent with its symmetric structure.

The disappearance of the carboxylic acid carbonyl signal, the appearance of ester carbonyl resonances, and the changes resulting from the loss and restoration of molecular symmetry observed for the carbonyl and methoxy carbons provide strong spectroscopic evidence supporting the formation of the monoacylated and diacylated products. These results are consistent with previous reports on mono and diacylated curcumin analogues.63,65

Photodegradation study of curcumin and MCFD

The photostability in solution of curcumin and MCFD was evaluated through photodegradation kinetics in DMSO under LED irradiation. The maximum absorption wavelengths were determined to be 433 nm for curcumin and 421 nm for MCFD, as shown in Fig. 6a and b. These values were consistent with those previously reported.38,66 The DCFD was excluded from this analysis owing to its insolubility in DMSO.

Photodegradation of (a) curcumin and (b) MCFD in DMSO. Photodegradation kinetics of (c) curcumin and (d) MCFD analyzed using first- and second-order rate laws.

The UV-Vis absorption spectrum of MCFD revealed a hypsochromic shift relative to curcumin, indicating that esterification modified the electronic environment of the chromophore. In addition, a hypochromic effect was observed, with a decrease in molar absorptivity from ε0 = 5.8 × 104 L mol−1 cm−1 for curcumin to ε0 = 3.6 × 104 L mol−1 cm−1 for MCFD, suggesting changes in the extent of π-conjugation resulting from the introduction of the fatty acid chain.

Photodegradation experiments were carried out under continuous LED irradiation in a controlled chamber and UV-Vis spectra were recorded at regular intervals. A gradual decrease in the characteristic absorption bands at 433 nm and 421 nm was observed over time, indicating progressive photodegradation. The absorbance values approached constant levels as irradiation time increased, reaching final absorbances of [A]∞ = 0.034 for curcumin and [A]∞ = 0.012 for MCFD. The photodegradation pathways of curcumin depend on factors such as the irradiation source and solvent system and arise from photooxidation of the heptadienone chain and β-diketone moiety, involving reactive oxygen species generation and leading to fragmentation into smaller phenolic compounds such as vanillin and ferulic acid.30,35–41

The photodegradation kinetics were analyzed using integrated rate laws. First- and second-order kinetic models were evaluated (Fig. 6c and d), and the best fit to the experimental data was obtained using the first-order model for both compounds. The rate constants were determined as k = 2.03 × 10−3 min−1 for curcumin and k = 1.28 × 10−3 min−1 for MCFD. Pearson correlation coefficients were R = −0.998 (curcumin) and R = −0.992 (MCFD), while the corresponding coefficients of determination were R2 = 0.997 and 0.984, respectively, indicating excellent agreement with first-order kinetics. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that the photodegradation of curcuminoids follows a first-order kinetic model under different reaction media and irradiation conditions.35,67,68

Comparison of half-life values revealed that MCFD exhibited improved photostability in solution relative to unmodified curcumin. The calculated half-lives were 341 min for curcumin and 542 min for MCFD, indicating that curcumin degraded approximately 1.6 times faster than its fatty acid derivative under identical conditions. This increase in half-life suggests that esterification contributes to enhanced resistance against photodegradation while preserving the chromophoric properties of curcumin. However, this study demonstrates the photostability of the dyes only in solution. Therefore, further evaluations directly on dyed fabrics, before and after light irradiation, including color-strength values (K/S), CIELAB color coordinates, and light fastness, are required before practical dyeing applications can be considered.

Kinetic study of adsorption

In adsorption studies involving natural dyes and cellulosic substrates, mordants are often employed to promote interactions between dye molecules and the fiber surface.69,70 Iron(ii) sulfate heptahydrate was selected as a pre-mordanting agent for cotton fibers due to its low toxicity and functional benefits, including improved color fastness and antimicrobial properties.71,72 During pre-mordanting, iron species may be retained within the cotton matrix through coordination and/or adsorption involving the hydroxyl-rich cellulose surface. These iron centers can subsequently act as coordination sites for oxygen-donor groups in curcumin and MCFD, including phenolic, carbonyl, and keto–enol moieties, thereby promoting dye fixation on the pre-mordanted fiber. This proposed coordination-mediated interaction is consistent with the role of metal salts as mordants, although direct spectroscopic confirmation of the specific cellulose-Fe-dye coordination environment was not performed in the present study.73,74

The synthesized MCFD and DCFD derivatives were developed for use under neutral pH conditions (pH 7), a choice motivated by sustainability considerations, since operating under neutral pH conditions avoids the need for subsequent neutralization steps. Initial trials performed at pH 5.5 (acidic) and pH 8.5 (basic) resulted in negligible dye uptake for both compounds. However, under neutral pH conditions, MCFD exhibited appreciable adsorption onto cotton fibers, whereas DCFD showed minimal interaction and was therefore excluded from further kinetic investigations. The reduced adsorption of DCFD may be associated with the complete esterification of the phenolic hydroxyl groups and its increased hydrophobic character, which can limit interactions with the pre-mordanted fiber surface.

The adsorption behavior of turmeric extract (as the curcumin source) and MCFD was studied as a function of temperature using cotton fibers pre-mordanted with FeSO4·7H2O under neutral pH conditions. Notably, tests at room temperature showed negligible adsorption for both dyes, indicating that elevated temperatures were required to achieve measurable adsorption. The temperature range of 318, 328, and 338 K was selected to evaluate temperature-dependent adsorption. Although these temperatures are above ambient conditions, they remain within the mild temperature range commonly used for dyeing. Application at ambient temperature was not demonstrated in the present study and may require further optimization, including longer contact times, increased dye concentrations, improved dispersant formulations, or modified mordanting conditions.

As shown in Fig. 7, the maximum adsorption capacity of turmeric extract increased with temperature from 0.841 mg g−1 at 318 K to 0.893 mg g−1 at 328 K and 0.920 mg g−1 at 338 K. Simultaneously, the equilibrium time decreased from 140 to 120 and 100 min, respectively, indicating that higher temperatures accelerated the adsorption process.

The different kinetic behaviors suggest that fatty acid modification influenced the rate-determining step of the adsorption process on cotton fibers pre-mordanted with FeSO4·7H2O. The esterification reaction increased the molecular size and hydrophobic character of curcumin while reducing the number of available phenolic hydroxyl groups, factors that may affect both diffusion toward the fiber surface and interactions with the pre-mordanted substrate. MCFD exhibited adsorption capacities up to 1.5 times greater than those of turmeric extract when expressed on a mass basis (mg g−1, left vertical axis). However, when the adsorption capacity was normalized by the amount of substance (mol g−1, right vertical axis), only a slight decrease was observed for MCFD. These findings suggest that the higher adsorption values observed for MCFD are primarily associated with its greater molar mass, whereas the intrinsic affinity of the adsorbate for the fiber surface remained comparable to that of unmodified curcumin.

Although the adsorption capacities of MCFD and turmeric extract on cotton fibers pre-mordanted with FeSO4·7H2O were relatively modest, the present study demonstrates that fatty acid modification of curcumin is feasible and preserves the ability of the dye to interact with mordanted cellulose. The low uptake may be associated with the hydrophobic nature of the fatty acid chain, which may reduce its affinity for the polar fiber surface. Therefore, the independent experiments show that MCFD exhibited greater photostability than curcumin in DMSO and retained measurable adsorption onto pre-mordanted cotton fibers. Because photodegradation was not evaluated after adsorption, improved fabric-level light fastness cannot be inferred from these results.

Future work should focus on optimizing the mordanting conditions and exploring higher dye concentrations, as well as evaluating alternative mordants. Naturally occurring compounds, such as tannic acid, acetic acid, citric acid, and chitosan, may represent attractive biomordant options for sustainable adsorption systems.79–82 In addition, the synthesis and evaluation of novel fatty acid curcumin derivatives based on elaidic acid, linoleic acid, and linolenic acid may provide further insight into the relationship between molecular structure, adsorption behavior, and photostability.

For MCFD, the Langmuir model provided the best fit to the experimental data, as evidenced by the higher coefficients of determination (R2). In addition, the Langmuir separation factor (RL) remained between 0 and 1 at all temperatures, indicating favorable adsorption. These results suggest that the adsorption of MCFD on cotton fibers pre-mordanted with FeSO4·7H2O is adequately described by a Langmuir-type model, assuming monolayer coverage of the available adsorption sites. The maximum adsorption capacities predicted by the Langmuir model increased from 7.81 mg g−1 at 318 K to 19.88 mg g−1 at 328 K and 55.56 mg g−1 at 338 K, indicating that higher temperatures favored the adsorption process.

In contrast, the adsorption behavior of turmeric extract was better represented by the Freundlich model, as evidenced by the higher coefficients of determination. The Freundlich adsorption intensity parameter (n) remained greater than 1 at all temperatures, indicating favorable adsorption.

These results suggest that adsorption of turmeric extract occurred on a heterogeneous surface containing sites with different affinities for the adsorbate molecules. Furthermore, the relatively small variation in the Freundlich isotherm constant (KF) over the investigated temperature range indicates that the adsorption affinity of turmeric extract remained largely unchanged.

Thermodynamic parameters, including the standard Gibbs free energy change (ΔG), enthalpy change (ΔH), and entropy change (ΔS), were calculated using the van't Hoff equations (eqn (10) and (11)). For this purpose, the Langmuir equilibrium constants were converted into dimensionless thermodynamic constants prior to calculation (eqn (12)).83,84 The calculated parameters are summarized in Table 4.

For the turmeric extract, the negative ΔH value (−14.1 kJ mol−1) indicates an exothermic adsorption process, while the positive ΔS (52.5 J K−1 mol−1) suggests increased disorder at the solid–liquid interface, consistent with displacement of water molecules by dye molecules. The negative ΔG values, −31.0, −30.9, and −32.1 kJ mol−1 at 318, 328, and 338 K, respectively, confirm that adsorption was spontaneous under the conditions studied.

For MCFD, the negative ΔH value (−84.2 kJ mol−1) indicates a strongly exothermic adsorption process, while the negative ΔS (−162.2 J K−1 mol−1) reflects decreased disorder, likely due to the more restricted arrangement of the fatty acid derivative on the fiber surface after adsorption. The negative ΔG values of −32.5, −31.2, and −29.3 kJ mol−1 at 318, 328, and 338 K, respectively, demonstrate that adsorption was spontaneous at all investigated temperatures, with a slight decrease in spontaneity as temperature increased, consistent with the exothermic process.

Overall, the thermodynamic analysis indicates that the adsorption of both turmeric extract and MCFD on cotton fibers pre-mordanted with FeSO4·7H2O was spontaneous and exothermic. The positive ΔS value for turmeric extract suggests increased interfacial disorder during adsorption, whereas the negative ΔS value for MCFD indicates a more ordered adsorbed state, probably associated with the hydrophobic fatty acid chain and restricted molecular mobility upon adsorption.

These findings are consistent with previous studies on the adsorption of turmeric extract and/or curcumin, in which the Freundlich isotherm model provided the best description of the adsorption equilibrium.85–88 The different adsorption behaviors observed for both dyes suggest that fatty acid modification influenced the interaction of curcumin with the pre-mordanted cotton surface, resulting in a shift from heterogeneous adsorption behavior toward a more homogeneous adsorption process.

Conclusion and perspective

The acylation of curcumin with fatty acids commonly found in vegetable oils was successfully achieved under mild conditions, yielding novel mono- and diacylated fatty acid curcumin derivatives. Using oleic acid as a model substrate, the reaction conditions were optimized to control the degree of acylation and enable the selective synthesis of MCFD or DCFD. Structural characterization by FTIR, 1H NMR, and 13C NMR spectroscopy confirmed the successful formation of the target compounds.

Photodegradation kinetics in DMSO under LED irradiation demonstrated that MCFD exhibited enhanced photostability compared with unmodified curcumin, with half-lifes of 542 and 341 min, respectively. This improvement was associated with esterification of the curcumin structure, which altered the electronic environment of the chromophore and induced a hypsochromic shift in the absorption maximum. Both compounds followed first-order photodegradation kinetics.

Adsorption studies on cotton fibers pre-mordanted with FeSO4·7H2O under neutral pH conditions revealed distinct adsorption behaviors for turmeric extract and MCFD. The adsorption of turmeric extract was best described by the pseudo-second-order kinetic model and the Freundlich isotherm, while MCFD followed pseudo-first-order kinetics and was better characterized by the Langmuir model. Thermodynamic analysis indicated that the adsorption of both systems was spontaneous and exothermic. In contrast, DCFD revealed negligible adsorption under the investigated conditions, likely due to its increased hydrophobicity and complete esterification of the phenolic hydroxyl groups.

These independent experiments demonstrate that fatty acid modification increased curcumin photostability in DMSO under the investigated irradiation conditions and preserved the ability of the dye to interact with pre-mordanted cotton fibers. Both turmeric extract and MCFD produced visible yellow coloration on the pre-mordanted cotton fibers, supporting their further evaluation as renewable colorants for dyeing applications.

From an industrial perspective, oleic acid and related fatty acids are attractive because they can be obtained from renewable vegetable oils and potentially from industrial by-products. However, the current DCC/DMAP-mediated Steglich esterification route should be considered a proof-of-concept synthetic approach rather than an optimized large-scale process, since DCC and DMAP increase reagent cost and generate further purification requirements. Future scale-up should therefore focus on lower-cost coupling methods, catalyst replacement or recovery, solvent minimization, and process intensification.

Although iron leaching and interfering ions released during adsorption/desorption were not quantitatively analyzed in the present study, no visible evidence of mordant release was observed during the adsorption experiments under the investigated conditions. Future work should include quantitative elemental analysis of the dye bath and treated fabrics to assess mordant retention and possible ion release. Before practical textile application can be concluded, fabric-level evaluations are required, including color-strength values (K/S), CIELAB color coordinates, washing and light fastness, laundering durability, process reproducibility, dye-bath reuse, scale-up, and environmental release assessment. In addition, the synthesis of derivatives based on other fatty acids, such as elaidic, linoleic, and linolenic acids, may provide further insight into structure–property relationships and support the development of sustainable dye systems based on renewable natural resources.
