Preserving and enhancing cultural heritage through art design using feature pyramid network optimized by modified builder optimization algorithm
Authors: Xing Fu, Navid Razmjooy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCultural heritage continues to be endangered by environmental, social, and political issues requiring new digital preservation methods. This paper proposes a deep learning framework consisting of an Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) and an Modified Builder Optimization Algorithm (MBOA) whose purpose is to classify, restore, and synthesize culturally significant artwork. Using the WikiArt dataset, the model represents the cultural styles of many artistic traditions by fully utilizing FPN’s multi-scale architecture and identifying both fine scale textures as well as global stylistic semantics. The MBOA optimizes hyperparameters through an innovative bio-inspired search strategy, which improves convergence and model performance and improves training efficiency. The framework achieves style classification accuracy of 96.7% in terms of preference, a PSNR of 32.41 dB and SSIM of 0.937 for restoration, and an Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) score of 14.3 for generation, therefore outperforming state-of-the-art CNNs and generative models. The results illustrated the framework’s powerful reconstruction capabilities in restoring degraded works of art, or generating new works inspired by cultural aesthetics. By combining a Feature Pyramid Network, Modified Builder Optimization Algorithm, and Neural Style Transfer, this work further expands the capabilities and role of Generative AI for use in digital preservation. This approach supports not only archival fidelity and quality but forms a creative interaction, enhancing the capabilities of networks and reinterpreting global heritage assets in the digital age as a scalable tool for museums, teachers, and cultural institutions.Keywords: Cultural heritage, Feature pyramid network, Modified builder optimization algorithm, Artistic style classification, Digital restoration, AI-generated artSubject terms: Cultural and media studies, Cultural and media studies, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Cultural heritage is an essential expression of human history, creativity and identity, made up of some tangible things, art, buildings, or manuscripts, and intangible things, customs, language or beliefs. These items provide understanding of values, beliefs, and worldviews of our predecessors; they also play a role in establishing present cultural identity and continuity1.

Heritage preservation aims to allow societies to connect to their roots and for future generations to access their social memory; it also promotes intercultural understanding, respect for subtle (or exaggerated) custom and tradition, and diverse histories2. Essentially, cultural heritage is not only a record of the past, but a living resource that informs education, a sense of community, and global discourse. Figure 1 shows the foundations of cultural heritage.

Foundations of cultural heritage.

Nonetheless, our cultural heritage faces increased risk from a range of global threats. Environmental degradation from climate change continues to threaten outdoor monuments, traditional materials and vulnerable ecological systems. Urbanization continues to destroy or displace heritage sites in favor of urban development and economic growth. Armed conflict continues to threat heritage directly through the destruction and looting of cultural property, frequently in an effort to erase historical memory. And, many cultural heritage assets remain in a condition which is not publicly accessible through either physical or digital means, meaning that education, research and cultural exchange is limited.

A 2023 report released jointly by UNESCO and the International Council on Monuments and Sites (ICOMOS) estimates that thousands of heritage sites are at risk across the globe and that there is unique regional patterning in risks. The report summarizes the degree of threats to heritage by region and the common threats to heritage in a useful format in Table 1, which provides estimates for the number of endangered cultural heritage sites in major regions, and the most common threats to them.

At the same time, the emergence of digital technologies has produced alternatives to preserve and promote cultural heritage. Artificial intelligence (AI), in particular, has developed into a powerful technology that has the capacity to dissect, analyze, respond to, restore, and even recreate cultural expressions. In the context of art and design, the use of AI systems, especially those relying on deep learning, can be trained to identify an artistic style, synthesize a new type of image inspired by historical aesthetics and even assist in preserving an art piece that is deteriorating or fragile3.

One of the most exciting datasets for any of the proposed applications of AI is WikiArt with an enormous online collection of over 80,000 digitized works of art from numerous styles, genres, and artists, and numerous cultures, and is publicly accessible. This collection provides a range of artwork that can help train an AI model to identify the common traits of a visual heritage, simulate traditional cultural aesthetics, and possibly create new forms rooted in cultural history.

Motivation and contribution

This research examines the potential for the field of artificial intelligence to aid in preservation and enhancement of cultural heritage through the context of art design. The techniques of artificial intelligence are used, particular convolutional neural networks (CNNs), generative adversarial networks (GANs), and neural style-transfer techniques on the WikiArt dataset in order to achieve three objectives (1) classify and archive artistic styles as a type of documentation, (2) emulate or restore traditional works of art to contribute to digital preservation, and (3) generate new culturally inspired visuals to enable a new creative engagement with heritage. The study contributes to the interdisciplinary dialogue that is occurring between cultural studies, digital humanities and computational design, where the growing field of artificial intelligence is not just an innovation in technology, but a cultural agent. AI has the potential to preserve the memory of the past and allow for reinterpretation in the present and beyond.

Related works

The use of computational processes to conserve and advance cultural heritage is becoming more and more a multidisciplinary field of research, that intersects artificial intelligence, vision, digital humanities, and other fields. Potential new advances in deeping learning and computer-aided design could be very helpful for giving solutions to many of the complex problems associated with the classification, restoration, and even creative reinterpretation of cultural artefacts.

Depending on the kinds of heritage forms (physical, non-physical, etc.), developing AI-based tools will require that we use not only deep technical models, but also have knowledge of the ‘cultural’ context. This literature review summarizes some examples of the most relevant recent examples of research that use CNNs, genetic algorithms, and 3D reconstructions in the application of cultural heritage preservation and design, in terms of methodological frameworks, strengths, and limitations.

Belhi et al.4 investigated the categorization of digital images as an essential component in the restoration and conservation of cultural heritage (CH). In the field of computer vision, the emphasis has been focused on the classification of cultural heritage asset images according to criteria such as type, artist, genre, and style identification. They observed that CH classification presented numerous obstacles due to the similarities in colors, textures, and shapes among different CH images. Their research sought to investigate and assess the efficacy of pre-trained deep CNNs, including VGG16, VGG-19, ResNet50, and Inception-V3, in the classification of cultural heritage photographs via transfer learning techniques. Rather than constructing CNN-based models from beginning to end up, they employed pre-trained models that had been developed on particular datasets and classes. To evaluate the efficacy of these models, they utilized two image datasets pertaining to CH. The experimental findings demonstrated that the Inception-V3-based model attained higher classification accuracy than the other pre-trained CNN models.

Li et al.5 examined the utilization of deep learning (DL) methods in cultural and creative product design, specifically with recognition of images. Cultural and creative products are described as those influenced by cultural themes, with an emphasis on their growing popularity in modern marketplaces. The study utilized artificial intelligence and deep learning technologies to facilitate the design process of culturally innovative products. Deep learning, as defined, was a machine learning technique that allows computers to learn from examples by imitating human behavior. Their suggested system integrated an image identification technology, defined as the ability of computer systems to detect objects in images. This method employed machine vision technologies with cameras and AI-based software for image analysis that has been often used for autonomous vehicles, image content searching, and robotic vision, using randomized algorithms to optimize the performance of image recognition in the creation of culturally novel items. Their findings showed that their method was producing more precise outputs than traditional methods like LDA, HMMs, or optimization algorithms.

Chen et al.6 proposed a novel method, termed CNN-GA, for the graphic design of traditional residences and historical structures. Their approach integrated CNNs with Genetic Algorithms (GA) to enable the creation of culturally enriched and distinctive architectural graphic designs. The study focused on understanding the essential characteristics of traditional architecture grounded in its historical culture and reintroducing it via a contemporary computational process. The CNN aspect of their model was trained on a mixed collection of architectural images that allowed it to distinguish core things like patterns, textures, and constructs related to different styles of architecture. The CNN acted as a highly developed feature extractor, maintaining an advanced understanding of traditional architecture aesthetics. The GA aspect then took the features extracted from the CNN and began an evolutionary design generation process. The GA allowed for a creative exploration of design alternatives by continually developing and recombining aspects of architecture, retaining an identity in culture while also contributing to reinterpretations. The process design of the CNN-GA in their combined process acted as an automated culturally sensitive process and was able to create graphics that exemplified both stability and diversity of culture. The researchers contended that this approach possesses significant potential to revolutionize architectural graphic design by integrating traditional cultural aesthetics with modern computational design frameworks.

Bai et al.7 evaluated the utilization of computer-aided design (CAD) and 3D reconstruction technologies in the conservation and dissemination of intangible cultural heritage (ICH). Their primary aim was to outline imaginative methods and strategies for curating intangible culture. The study employed a mixed-methods approach in that it included a review of the literature, fieldwork, and case studies to investigate the current condition and concerns about ICH inheritance. Besides, the authors offered a deeper explanation of the relevant conceptualizations and applications of CAD and 3D reconstruction techniques.

Li et al.8 proposed a supervised Modified CNN for automated art classification to overcome the shortcomings of conventional techniques. Their algorithm was trained on a dataset comprising 5000 artworks across five styles: Impressionism, Cubism, Realism, Abstract, and Surrealism. It achieved an accuracy rate of 93.0%, which surpassed models like ResNet50, and VGG16. Visualization tools such as t-SNE, PCA, and Grad-CAM were utilized to help understand the model’s success and incorrect classifications. While the approach was effective, the model had challenges with issues related to data bias and stylistic similarities. The study confirmed the potential of Modified CNNs for reliable art classification for digital curation and cultural heritage applications.

The studies reviewed together show positive trends for the use of artificial intelligence and other computational methodologies in cultural heritage contexts. Pre-trained deep convolutional neural networks are a promising way to overcome the challenges of classification regarding the visual similarities of the cultural heritage images, while custom-built neural networks (such as modifications of CNNs) can further improve accuracy and interpretability in a range of tasks, including the recognition of art styles.

Hybrid applications, such as the combination of CNNs and genetic algorithms, can foster creative pathways to generate culturally informed design outputs that find balance, or conflict, between preservation and design exploration. The recent developments of CAD and 3D reconstruction technologies to include intangible aspects of heritage allow for the consideration of a more integrated and holistic approach to preservation conservation.

Despite the growth in activity using AI in cultural heritage, data bias, stylistic similarities of cultural heritage and culturally aware model design are still issues to be solved. In future research, AI frameworks should further develop using interdisciplinary approaches and contextual perspectives to ensure these technological advancements are relevant and meaningful to the ongoing characteristic preservation of cultural heritage and the innovation of cultural creativity.

Proposed methodology

An explicit and structured process has been proposed for the conservation and enhancement of cultural heritage, through artificial intelligence, computer-driven art design. A framework has been advanced that employs the synergistic collaboration of a deep convolutional architecture, the Feature Pyramid Network (FPN), and a bio-based optimization method, the Modified Builder Optimization Algorithm (MBOA). Three closely related artifact objectives are involved in cultural heritage: (1) the classification and documentation of different artistic styles, (2) the digital re-creation of virtually complete or incomplete artifacts, and (3) the creation of new visual instances that are both stylistically and culturally situated in the past.

The proposed methodology aims at addressing important problems in heritage preservation, including the diversity of artistic form and expression across cultures, the degradation of physical objects, and the need for computationally intelligent systems that are able to learn, respond to, and re-express cultural semantics. By combining multi-scale feature learning with a metaheuristic-driven model optimization approach, the proposed framework offers semantic complexity and architectural efficacy at the same time that covers a wide range of art forms in different domains.

The preferred methodology consists of five interdependent components which create a cohesive pipeline, including (1) Dataset and Preprocessing, ensuring high-quality, diverse, and representational input; (2) Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) Architecture, allowing for hierarchical feature extraction at different spatial scales; (3) Modified Builder Optimization Algorithm (MBOA), a new optimization strategy has been proposed to improve the FPN hyper-parameters with the goal of optimizing learning dynamics; (4) Task-Specific Models, utilizing the tuned FPN into classification, restoration, and generative models; and (5) Training and Optimization Workflow, using MBOA and deep learning to direct the interaction of both algorithms to facilitate the best model convergence and generalization. Figure 2 illustrates the pipeline of the proposed methodology.

The pipeline of the proposed methodology.

This modular-yet-integrated design paradigm enables shared representation learning across tasks while preserving task-specific specialization, leading to both computational efficacy and cultural fidelity in learning. Utilizing FPN as a backbone means it is possible to learn both fine-grained textural features (e.g., brushstrokes in Impressionist paintings or unique geometric patterns in Islamic ontological art) and global compositional structures (e.g., symmetry in Byzantine artifacts or spatial depth in Renaissance constructions).

At the same time, MBOA-enabled learning improves the model’s adaptability in the face of high dimensional hyperparameter space, like varying learning rate schedules, filter dimensions, regularization strengths, and network depth, thus avoiding reliance on solely manual tuning or gradient-based methods, which often find suboptimal local minima.

Each aspect of the methodology is described in the following subsections and substantiated with mathematical frameworks, architectural diagrams, and parameters for reproducibility and scientific rigor. As a result, the framework presented is not just a technical tool but an AI framework with cultural sensitivity, one that has the ability to learn from past, recover that which is lost, and create new forms of artistic expression based on heritage.

The holistic, transformative methodology is a step forward in the application of AI in the cultural preservation space, as it provides an ethical and scalable models for continued research, practice and application supported and transparent at all stages for museums, archives, and education.

Dataset and preprocessing

This research builds on the WikiArt dataset, a publicly available large-scale archive of digitized fine art. The database contains over 80,000 high-resolution images of art from 26 different styles of art. The images were produced by over 1000 artists from a variety of cultural and historical backgrounds. The artwork is also tagged with metadata, including style, genre, artist, and date produced, which allows for supervised learning for classification and style-aware generation. ImageNet statistics is employed in the normalization of the dataset because it has demonstrated itself to be effective in the cases of transfer learning and it is capable of increasing the generalization of the models in the case of a variety of visual fields.

ImageNet, a massive dataset of more than a million images in 1,000 categories, is a widely trained deep learning remotive, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs) model training. Normalization process is the subtraction of the mean and dividing it with the standard deviation of the ImageNet dataset which is used to center the data and scale it to the right size. It has been shown that with this approach, convergence can be accelerated and the models perform better when used on new datasets that are not otherwise linked to ImageNet.

As a more complex and diverse visual feature, the simple scaling and allocation of features are more suitable to the situation when considering cultural heritage preservation, where the variety of artistic styles and the intricacy of visual characteristics is a huge issue.

The empirical validation of this option occurs through other related uses in art classification and restoration where ImageNet-based normalization has helped to improve model performance and reduce training times. When implementing this established normalization tool, the proposed framework will take advantages of the developed knowledge and best practices in the area, and the following steps of feature extraction and model training will have a strong background.

Feature pyramid network (FPN) architecture

The first module is the modified feature pyramid network which produces initial feature maps at various scales and levels, The second module is the deep semantic embedding module which serves to increase the capacity for producing features that are appropriate in semantics, and high in spatial resolutions.

The third module uses a two branch deep feature fusion system which included two branches of deep features called top and down branch respectively, which were specifically created to separate levels of features and allow fusion. The deep features were then fused and used as input for the fourth module for classification. In Fig. 5 a general structure of the proposed approach is illustrated and consists of four main modules.

Enhanced feature pyramid network

Current CNN-based approaches are often formulated as an end-to-end problem and seek to learn an overall image-level representation from the raw image. It is widely accepted , however, that neurons in higher layers respond to the global aspects of the image, while neurons in lower layer or shallow layers respond to local features of the image. This suggests the use of local object-level features previously extracted from lower layers is important to improve the performance of the approach.

To address this shortcoming, a network design has been proposed as a pyramid that can gather local object-level features as well local image-level features. The proposed network model is based on the former FPN (Feature Pyramid Network) model.

The FPN can learn a feature pyramid across levels and scales of resolution. By comparison, the FPN uses bilinear interpolation or nearest neighbor as up sampling methods to create higher resolution feature maps, which causes a loss of high frequency components from the original higher resolution features, introduces discontinuities, and creates distorted object edges that will impact the accuracy of the generated feature maps. This pushes to utilize a more efficient method, deconvolution, for up sampling.

Deconvolution is a worthy operation in several areas including motion deblurring, super-resolution, and semantic segmentation; it is a helpful tool to recover details lost from the convolutional layers in the FPN while also eliminating noise and blur. The pyramid-like architecture proposed for this model will be labeled the enhanced feature pyramid network (EFPN).

It also has a bottom-up pathway, lateral connections, and a top-down pathway. It is important to clarify that in the top-down pathway, as illustrated in Fig. 3, the spatial resolution is increased using deconvolution. A full description of the EFPN will be described next. Deep semantic embedding

As pooling methods and down-sampling by design in the bottom-up path continues, the resolution of the upper channels was diminishing. This decrease in spatial detail also limits their capability to delineate small-scale boundaries. The architecture combines the semantics of features by taking the high responses of bottom features and the strong activations of upper features because high instance responses have greater utility for localizing the objects accurately, and strong semantic activations provide information with a better understanding of the scenes.

So, a simple, lightweight DSE (Deep Semantic Embedding) module was created that merges features from different levels. This DSE module enables efficiently transferring spatial data to the target map without having to traverse numerous layers of the architecture. By merging the rich detail of lower-level features with the weak semantics of high-level features, DSE can utilize both pieces of complementary information and obtain more valid features.

Two-branch deep feature fusion

In an FPN (Feature Pyramid Network), a proposal at one level has been chosen for recognition based on the size of the objects, because object detection is especially about assigning a class to an object. Though it is simple and effective, this is not capable of great performance for some objectives of classification because the goal in some reasons is to recognize many combination features of different discriminative features.

The idea comes from the fact that when experts manually classify the samples, they offer semantic annotations based on the global aspect of the sample, but also the local features of samples as well, so we think that both global and local level features are necessary representation if we want to discriminate those elements or not.

Specifically, the higher level feature maps created by global receptive areas offer similar semantic features, and if lower level features can access them then their capacity for absorbing meaningful contextual information for prediction purposes will improve. Let me explain the converse of the feature maps with lower levels that are created by with local receptive areas that obtain fine level details to help identify samples. These features can help with higher level features replace for their loss of spatial information, which is favorable for classification tasks.

Based on this assessment, a two-branch deep feature fusion methodology has been developed to incorporate features from the lower and higher levels more effectively.

The architecture of two-branch deep feature fusion

The convolutional architecture has two different branches to consider both lower-level and higher-level feature maps. To simultaneously consider different levels and increase the receptive fields for extracting multi-level contextual information in an efficient manner without increasing computation costs, the proposed model will utilize both atrous convolution and convolution.

Atrous (or dilated) convolution has been proven to be an effective strategy for problems that require dense prediction. To mitigate the potential risk of network degradation from increased depth, skip connections have been included in the model. Furthermore, the last components of the model will consist of two Global Average Pooling (GAP) layers that have been used to create the representation features. Modified builder optimization algorithm

The theoretical foundations of the recently established BOA (Builder Optimization Algorithm) are thoroughly explained in the present section. The methodical techniques that builders employ during the building process serve as an inspiration for the BOA. The BOA’s main idea has been founded on 2 main construction techniques.

One of them is making major structural changes in compliance with the design specifications, and the other one is carrying out painstaking adjustments for improving appearance and addressing complex details. Actually, BOA’s theoretical underpinnings are explained through different stages of mathematical modeling: local and global search.

Inspiration of BOA

Building construction is a methodical process that requires careful planning and execution at several stages. Constructors use a systematic procedure to transform raw materials into a finished structure. The present process starts with a building shaping phase and ends with a refinement phase for aesthetics, details, and optimizations. Inspired by the current approach, BOA simulates these two crucial procedures in an optimization environment.

Every population solution in the BOA algorithm represents an ongoing structural design. The iterative improvement of these structures drives the optimization process, with each structure becoming more and more refined through modifications that adhere to an ideal design. The current process’s mathematical model aims to allow BOA to balance local exploitation (small adjustments for optimization) and global exploration (large structural modifications).

Training and optimization workflow

The complete training and optimization workflow follows a two-phase strategy:

Optimization Phase: MBOA is executed to search for the optimal configuration of FPN hyperparameters. In each iteration, a candidate configuration is used to instantiate the FPN, which is trained for a few epochs (e.g., 5) on the training set. The validation performance is recorded and used as the fitness value. After 100 iterations, the bestperforming configuration is selected.

Optimization Phase: MBOA is executed to search for the optimal configuration of FPN hyperparameters. In each iteration, a candidate configuration is used to instantiate the FPN, which is trained for a few epochs (e.g., 5) on the training set. The validation performance is recorded and used as the fitness value. After 100 iterations, the bestperforming configuration is selected.

Training Phase: The FPN is instantiated using the optimized parameters, then it is trained end-to-end on the training set for 100 epochs using the Adam optimizer (initial LR adjusted by MBOA). ReduceLROnPlateau for learning rate scheduling and early stopping (patience = 10) is employed.

Experimental results

This section provides an extensive evaluation of the proposed FPN-MBOA framework for cultural heritage preservation, especially in the context of AI-based art design. The experimental study comprises three main experiments, which include the classification of artistic styles, the digital restoration of degraded works of art, and the generation of culturally relevant images. The experimental study is based on the WikiArt dataset with more than 80,000 high-resolution artworks across 27 artistic styles. A careful experimental design has been followed that includes a 70%-15%-15% train-validation-test split, implementation on a multi-GPU parallel processing platform (NVIDIA A100 with 40 GB VRAM) using MATLAB R2019b., and comparisons against several baseline models.

The proposed FPN-MBOA model is evaluated against ResNet-5012, VGG16-PSO13, and standard FPN + Adam in classification14, DCGAN15 and SRCNN16 in restoration; and StyleGAN217 and ArtGAN18 in generation.

Performance is assessed using domain-specific metrics: classification accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score; restoration quality via PSNR and SSIM; and generative fidelity using Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) and user study ratings. Moreover, the ablation analysis studies the contribution of the modifications of MBOA and the multi-scale feature of FPN. Each analysis indicates that the performance of the model, convergence stability, and cultural expressiveness are significantly improved through the combination of Feature Pyramid Networks and Modified Builder Optimization Algorithm, indicating that the framework works to preserve and reimagine the global art heritage.

Experimental setup

The experiments are conducted on a high-performance computing node equipped with dual Intel Xeon Gold 6330 CPUs, 512 GB RAM, and one NVIDIA A100 GPU, running windows 11OS with MATLAB R2019b. The WikiArt dataset is preprocessed as described formerly, resulting in 56,000 training, 12,000 validation, and 12,000 test images, each resized to 512 × 512.

For classification and restoration, models are trained for 100 epochs with a batch size of 16. The FPN-MBOA model uses MBOA (population size: 30, iterations: 100) to optimize hyperparameters-learning rate, filter dimensions, dropout rate, and L2 regularization-before full training.

The Adam optimizer is used for fine-tuning with an initial learning rate set by MBOA. Baseline models have been trained given the same data and equipment so that each model could be evaluated fairly. For the classification analysis, accuracy, precision, recall, and macro-F1 have been used to report performance.

Restoration quality assessments are carried out with Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), and Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) measurements; generation quality is reported via the FID score (lower is better) and a user study with 20 participants rating realism and cultural authenticity on a 5-point Likert-style scale.

Classification results

The classification performance of the proposed FPN-MBOA model is evaluated on a 20-style subset of WikiArt, including Renaissance, Baroque, Impressionism, Expressionism, Ukiyo-e, Mughal, Byzantine, Cubism, Surrealism, and others. The choice of the proposed FPN-MBOA model to test it on a sub-sample of 20 styles of the WikiArt dataset was motivated by practical and methodological factors. In practice, the 20 styles represented a manageable set to analyze and therefore a more in-depth analysis of the model performance in a varied but controlled set of artistic styles. It had styles like Renaissance, Baroque, Impressionism, Expressionism, Ukiyo-e, Mughal, Byzantine, Cubism, and Surrealism which cover a wide range of western and non-western arts. At the methodological level, such method coincides with other research on the subject, which frequently applies a partial set of styles in order to guarantee the clarity and comparability of findings. Also, the 20-style subset was selected to reduce the effects of the possible class imbalance, since not all styles of the entire WikiArt data are represented equally. The choice of a balanced subset helped to make sure that the performance measures of the model were not biased by imbalanced classes. The given strategy also helped to analyze in greater detail how the model can differentiate stylistically similar categories, i.e. Post-Impressionism and Expressionism, which are similar in some ways yet different in terms of their expression in the art. The outcome showed that FPN-MBOA model was highly accurate and well-generalized throughout this range of styles, which supports the fact that it is effective in classifying artistic styles. These agencies generally work under very limited computational budgets, GPU resources, and a large number of growing collections of digitized artworks such that whichever preservation framework gets implemented must scale gracefully with the data volume.

This analysis assesses the computational efficiency of the proposed FPN-MBOA model by profiling for training time and GPU memory usage, as the dataset size goes from 10,000 to 50,000 samples. While metaheuristic optimization algorithms such as MBOA are sometimes considered to be computationally intensive for their population-based search, our simulations demonstrate that the fast convergence of the MBOA along with the parameter-efficient architecture of the FPN leads, on the one hand, to competitive or sometimes even better scalability, compared to conventional gradient-based training (e.g. Adam).

This analysis also demonstrates how the modular design of the framework allows parallelizable optimization along with memory-efficient multi-scale processing, which is very critical for any real-world adoption of large-scale digitization projects. By quantifying the time and resource demands, this study demonstrates that the FPN-MBOA framework is not only accurate and robust but also operationally feasible for long-term and scalable digital heritage preservation. Such efficiency measures imply that our method is not only accurate but also resource-efficient, rendering it useful to resources-strapped museums and archives.

The MBOA presents a population-based search strategy, which is more computationally expensive to execute in each iteration than the classical gradient-based algorithms, but that is intended to trade off training speed and optimization performance in a number of ways. To begin with, MBOA also has adaptive step control, which is dynamically adjusted to the exploration–exploitation trade-off based on the diversity in solutions.

This enables the algorithm to search the hyperparameter space in a broader way in the initial phase, and then concentrate on the optimization of the optimum solutions as the training is done; thereby speeding up convergence. Secondly, the elitist selection process will maintain the best performing solutions intergenerational, avoiding the waste of good quality setups and minimizing the number of repetitions required to achieve optimal solutions. Also, optional hybrid gradient feedback provided by MBOA can further increase its convergence speed by using lightweight gradient signals of validation batches in the update of continuous variables. Although MBOA has a computational cost associated with its use of a population-based method it is offset by its ability to converge faster, thereby decreasing the overall number of training epochs. The empirical findings indicate that in large datasets and with complex models, MBOA can be as fast or even faster than the traditional Adam-optimized models in terms of overall training times. Moreover, FPN-MBOA framework is modular, which enables MBOA iterations to be parallelized to effectively utilize computational resources and be deployed in resource-constrained settings in practice. With the trade-off between the cost of computation and the speed of training being balanced, high-quality solutions maintained, and parallel computing, MBOA can be used as a feasible means of optimizing deep learning models in the context of cultural heritage applications.

Discussion

In this study, the experimental findings revealed that the proposed FPN-MBOA framework contributes substantially to the advancement of AI-powered preservation and enhancement of cultural heritage. The integration of multi-scale representational capacity of the Feature Pyramid Network with the global search ability of the Modified Builder Optimization Algorithm produced superior outcomes across the three tasks examined, including artistic style classification, digital restoration of degraded artworks, and the generation of culturally inspired visuals.

The quantitative and qualitative findings consistently demonstrated that FPN-MBOA outperformed established baselines in accuracy, perceptual quality, and style fidelity and established its technical efficacy. However, in addition to the performance data, this section will explore broader implications of the results by exploring why the model does well, its inherent limitations, the ethical responsibilities, and real-world applicability of the proposed system. the Technical findings will be explained with cultural, societal and philosophical dimensions, emphasizing the framework not merely as a computational tool, but as a sociotechnical system within the complex reality of cultural heritage stewardship, creative endeavors, and intercultural dialogue.

The FPN-MBOA framework achieved significantly better results due to combining hierarchical feature learning and intelligent optimization. Whereas CNNs tend to work at a particular scale only, the FPN captures fine-grained textural details (e.g., the artist/brushstrokes of Impressionist paintings or the geometric precision of Islamic art) and the global compositional semantics of the image, achieved through its top-down lateral connections.

This multi-scale analysis allows the model to capture fine grained variations in style which is often missed in flat architectures such as ResNet-50 or VGG16. Moreover, the Modified Builder Optimization Algorithm (MBOA) optimizes this ability by exploring the hyperparameter space intelligently (learning rates, filter sizes, and regularization strength) without relying on gradient descent alone. MBOA uses a population-based search which avoids local minima and converges more efficiently than traditional approaches leading to more stable and generalizable models.

In the ablation study, both processes were shown to be necessary as measurable decreases in performance was seen, when either MBOA’s adaptive step control or reduced the depth of the FPN were applied. As such, FPN-MBOA should not be considered a unique innovation, rather the success of the approach is based on the complementary advantages of deep perceptual modeling and bioinspired optimization, which helped create a system that learned richer, frame-discriminative, and culturally aware representations.

The primary advantage of the FPN-MBOA framework lies in its multi-scale, multi-task design, which puts classification, restoration, and generation into a shared representational framework across various types of tasks. This design fosters transfer of knowledge, since features that are learned to recognize style can help enhance restoration fidelity, and restoration improves the realism of generated outputs. The FPN’s ability to preserve spatial detail at multiple resolutions is particularly advantageous for heritage applications, where both macro-composition and micro-texture carry cultural meaning.

Furthermore, the incorporation of MBOA introduces a new level of automation in tuning a model and auto-parameterization, and alleviates dependence upon hyperparameter search, in addition to adding reproducibility. Additionally, the results demonstrate effective generalization across various artistic traditions and cultural styles or genres, including European Renaissance, Asian Ukiyo-e, and Mughal miniatures, which opens the possibility for cross-cultural analysis. Overall, these advantages permit a level of semantic and aesthetic sensitivity that is distinguished from that of conventional deep learning, allowing FPN-MBOA to be used both to preserve culture and to generatively transform the heritage.

Although there are pros to every approach, the FPN-MBOA framework has several limitations, which need consideration. First, MBOA has a significantly greater computational cost than gradient-based optimization for hyperparameter search, even during the hyperparameter search stage of MBOA when multiple short training runs are conducted; as such, it is not scalable to large networks or datasets without high-performance computing support.

Secondly, the use of the WikiArt dataset includes a well-documented bias toward Western art, and little to underrepresentation of Indigenous, African, or other oral art traditions. Therefore, the model might perform poorly at mimicking processes of other non-Western styles, or ultimately recreating the cultural hierarchies found in the training data.

Thirdly, while the visual aspect of the generation component produces a non-trivial output, the generated outputs pose the danger of being visually compelling, while failing to be culturally authentic by copying surface aesthetics which may take into consideration symbolic or spiritual contexts.

Lastly, the model’s performance on actual examples of degraded artifacts (e.g., cracked frescoes or burned manuscripts) will likely not match the results of the synthetically degraded image examples, revealing an important gap in validity between a controlled study and practical application.

Conclusions

Cultural heritage, both tangible and intangible representations of humanity’s creativity and identity, faces unprecedented challenges from climate change and urbanization/dissipation, as well as armed conflict and degradation, requiring new advances in digital preservation. This study introduced a new artificial intelligence approach that combined a Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) with a Modified Builder Optimization Algorithm (MBOA) in order to classify, restore, and generate culturally valuable artworks to support both conservation and reimaginative creativity. By using the WikiArt dataset of over 80,000 digitized paintings in a range of styles across different cultural and historical genres, the FPN-based model employed the FPN’s multi-scale architecture to extract features in layers (fine brushwork at shallow layers, global composition at deeper layers), thus enabling style differentiation and texture preservation. The MBOA optimized hyperparameters (learning rate, filter size, and regularization strength) using a bio-inspired searching mechanism that improved convergence and avoided local minima, unlike conventional gradient search based approaches, and commonly used metaheuristic approaches. In the discipline of classification, the model achieved 96.7% accuracy on a 20-style benchmark. For restoration, a PSNR of 32.41 dB and SSIM of 0.937 are achieved on the synthetically degraded artworks, and for generation, it manages to create culturally coherent images with a Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) of 14.3, better than StyleGAN2 and ArtGAN. These results, the quantitative metrics and qualitative judgment, indicated that the framework is best for digital preservation, artistic restoration, and generative design. Bridging deep feature learning with intelligent optimization from this work fosters the advances of Generative AI and Neural Style Transfer in cultural applications, including a scalable, adaptive, and semantically rich instrument for the protection of global heritage and the nurture of new creative engagements.
