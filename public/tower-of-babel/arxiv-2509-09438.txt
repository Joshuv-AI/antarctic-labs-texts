GrACE: A Generative Approach to Better Confidence Elicitation in Large Language Models
Zhaohan Zhang, Ziquan Liu, Ioannis Patras

Abstract
Assessing the reliability of Large Language Models (LLMs) by confidence elicitation is a prominent approach to AI safety in high-stakes applications, such as healthcare and finance. Existing methods either require expensive computational overhead or suffer from poor calibration, making them impractical and unreliable for real-world deployment. In this work, we propose GrACE, a Generative Approach to Confidence Elicitation that enables scalable and reliable confidence elicitation for LLMs. GrACE adopts a novel mechanism in which the model expresses confidence by the similarity between the last hidden state and the embedding of a special token appended to the vocabulary, in real-time. We fine-tune the model for calibrating the confidence with calibration targets associated with accuracy. Experiments with three LLMs and two benchmark datasets show that the confidence produced by GrACE achieves the best discriminative capacity and calibration on open-ended generation tasks, outperforming six competing methods without resorting to additional sampling or an auxiliary model. Moreover, we propose two strategies for improving test-time scaling based on confidence induced by GrACE. Experimental results show that using GrACE not only improves the accuracy of the final decision but also significantly reduces the number of required samples in the test-time scaling scheme, indicating the potential of GrACE as a practical solution for deploying LLMs with scalable, reliable, and real-time confidence estimation.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have become popular solutions to knowledge-intensive and logically complex problems for their capability of utilizing and reasoning over a huge amount of knowledge they learn
achiam2023gpt
;
dubey2024llama
;
claude3
;
guo2025deepseek
.
However, the reliance on LLMs raises broad concerns about the possibility that LLMs output seemingly correct but factually hallucinated responses, especially in high-stakes domains such as healthcare
singhal2023large
, law
magesh2024hallucination
, and finance
xie2024finben
.
Therefore, it is critical for LLMs to "know what they know"
kadavath2022language
to become more reliable and gain users’ trust in their deployment.

Estimating model confidence is a promising approach to assessing the reliability of model predictions.
A desirable confidence score should exhibit strong separability between positive and negative samples (discrimination) and align closely with actual correctness (calibration).
Although confidence elicitation and calibration have been extensively studied in classification tasks
guo2017calibration
;
desai2020calibration
;
li2025large
, recent attention has shifted to open-ended generation tasks (e.g., question answering) for LLMs.
Nevertheless, as illustrated in Figure
1
, existing methods exhibit limitations in
when
a model expresses confidence and
how well
that confidence is calibrated, which constrains their practical utility in applications such as Test-Time Scaling (TTS)
brown2024large
.
Post-generation methods, which assign the precise confidence score after generation, dominate current confidence elicitation approaches.
They elicit confidence either by prompting the original model again
kadavath2022language
;
zhang2024atomic
or using auxiliary models
ulmer2024calibrating
;
kapoorlarge
.
Such methods require an additional confidence evaluation stage after generation, increasing latency and degrading human-computer interaction.
Verbalized confidence offers a generative alternative by producing numerical values
tian2023just
;
xiongcan
or linguistic expressions (e.g.,most likely, likely, not sure)
band2024linguistic
;
yang2024logu
;
yang2025uncle
during the generation stage.
However, verbal expressions of confidence are inherently vague as the discrete vocabulary fails to capture nuance differences in confidence – for example, the confidence intervals for "most likely", "likely", and "unlikely" are neither standardized nor quantifiable.
Moreover, LLMs tend to be overconfident when expressing verbal confidence, potentially due to too much focus on imitating human expressions
xiongcan
.

Related Works

Post-generation Confidence Elicitation.

Generative language models give their response towards prompts by sampling from the predictive probability.
Unlike model uncertainty, which captures the variability in a model’s responses to questions
kuhnsemantic
;
zhang2025get
, confidence elicitation directly assigns an explicit confidence score to the model’s prediction.
For multiple choice questions where the model only generates a single token, the maximum softmax probability is shown to be aligned with correctness
plaut2024softmax
.
However, the maximum softmax probability becomes a less reliable indicator of correctness when the generation is open-ended.
Post-generation methods follow the generate-then-evaluate paradigm, which first acquires model generations, and then evaluates confidence score with an auxilarity model.
Kadavath et al.
kadavath2022language
proposes
P
(True), which asks the model to self-evaluate its confidence on generation by converting the open-ended task to the multiple choice setting with careful prompting.
Further works
kapoorlarge
calibrate
P
(True) by fine-tuning the model with LoRA using binary labeled data.
As for more efficient solutions

azaria2023internal
;
kossen2024semantic
;
liu2024enhancing
, confidence can be detected from the model’s hidden state with a linear probe.
Post-generation methods are also applicable to black / gray-box models.
Ulmer et al.
ulmer2024calibrating
trains an auxiliary model with semantic-aware targets which predicts model confidence based on generation only.
GrACE differs from post-generation methods by eliminating the separate evaluation stage and producing the confidence score in the generation stage, thereby reducing computational overhead and accelerating the confidence elicitation process.

Verbalized Confidence Elicitation.

Another branch of confidence elicitation aims to teach the models to express their confidence in natural language.
Tian et al.
tian2023just
designs different prompting strategies to lead the model to output a calibrated confidence score.
Some works
band2024linguistic
;
yang2024logu
;
hager2025uncertainty
endow the model with the ability to use confidence-related phrases (e.g., not sure, uncertain) to express confidence as humans do.
Cohen et al.
cohen2024don
asks the model to generate a special token
[idk]
when it feels uncertain about the responses.
Although verbalized confidence is a natural and efficient approach to eliciting model confidence, LLMs are found to be overconfident when verbalizing their confidence
xiongcan
.
Moreover, the confidence in the form of phrases conveys vague and unquantifiable information, restricting its application to broad topics.

Test-Time Scaling.

Test-Time Scaling (TTS)
snell2024scaling
improves the response quality of LLMs with additional computing during the inference stage.
Repeated sampling
brown2024large
with decision-making rules such as Self-Consistency
wangself
is a common practice for selecting high-quality samples for given prompts.
However, this process requires a fixed sample size during TTS, which is not always necessary
chen2024not
.
While some works investigate optimal computational resources allocation
snell2024scaling
;
muennighoff2025s1
, there is research focusing on dynamically adjusting the sample size.
Some attempts
liescape
;
aggarwal2023let
determine when to terminate sampling by pre-sampling and calculating statistical features within the pre-sample set.
Kim et al.
kim2025scaling
applies the LLM-as-Judge technique
zheng2023judging
for evaluating the sample and process quality with reasoning models.
As a metric to evaluate the response quality, confidence is gradually applied to the decision-making process for improving TTS.
Taubenfeld et al.
taubenfeld2025confidence
and Huang et al.
huang2025efficient
optimize the sample size through the lens of confidence and calibration.
However, these works are based on
P
(True) which requires an additional evaluation stage as previously noted.
Concurrent work
fu2025deep
leverages confidence to filter out low-quality reasoning traces but does not account for calibration, resulting in threshold selection without risk guarantees.
Our work enables on-the-fly elicitation of discriminative and well-calibrated confidence, supporting efficient, effective, and risk-controlled TTS.

Methodology

In this section, we introduce the formulation of the generative confidence elicitation problem in §
3.1
and the GrACE framework in §
3.2
, including the form of confidence elicitation in GrACE §
3.2.1
, the calibration target §
3.2.2
, training process §
3.2.3
, and GrACE’s application on test-time scaling §
3.2.4
.

3.1
Problem Formulation

Given a prompt
\bm{x}
and a model
\mathcal{M}
, we define the generative confidence (GenConf)
c
as:

(\bm{y}_{i},{\bm{z}},c_{i})=\mathcal{M}(\bm{x}),\ c_{\text{i}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}

(1)

where
\bm{y}_{i}=\{y_{i}^{1},...,y_{i}^{N}\}
is the
i
-th sample,
{\bm{z}}=\{z_{1},...,z_{N}\}
is the model’s hidden state,
c_{i}
is the confidence score corresponding to the sample
\bm{y}_{i}
.
Note that Eq.
1
requires no auxiliary model and the confidence
c
should be a real number extracted during generation rather than a textual string.
To be effective and trustworthy in downstream applications, the GenConf
c
should exhibit both discrimination and calibration.
Formally:

Discrimination:

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{P}(c_{m}>c_{n}|f(\bm{y}_{m})=1,f(\bm{y}_{n})=0)=1,

(2)

Calibration:

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{P}(f(\bm{y}_{i})=1|c_{i}=p)=p,

(3)

where
f(\cdot,\cdot)
is a function which assigns 1 to the correct response and 0 to the wrong response.
Given these notions, discrimination requires

c
to correctly rank positive samples above negative ones, whereas calibration requires
c
to reflect the actual accuracy of the model’s responses.

3.2
GrACE

We describe the GrACE framework (illustrated in Figure
2
) for teaching the model to produce GenConf in this section.

3.2.1
Confidence Elicitation in GrACE

Ideally, the confidence should be expressed in continuous space during generation as proposed in §
3.1
.
Inspired by the pause token mechanism
goyalthink
;
wangguiding
which prompts the model to "think" by generating a sequence of special, non-semantic tokens before reasoning, we introduce a new token,
<CNF>
, into the vocabulary to encourage the reflective behavior of the model in the latent space.
Specifically, we append
<CNF>
to the end of each question-answer pair.
An example of the revised question answering data is shown below:

Question: Who found the remains of the Titanic?

Answer: Bob Ballard
<CNF>

We train the model to generate
<CNF>
at the end of its response, thereby enabling an additional forward pass in which the model can reflect on its output and estimate confidence in the latent space.
In what follows, we describe how the model expresses its confidence in the latent space.

Let
\bm{e}^{\texttt{<CNF>}}
be the embedding of the new token
<CNF>
, and
\bm{z}_{L}^{\texttt{<CNF>}}
be hidden states in the last layer
L
corresponding to the
<CNF>
.
Then, the confidence is estimated as the similarity between
\bm{e}^{\texttt{<CNF>}}
and
\bm{z}_{L}^{\texttt{<CNF>}}
, that is:

c=\mathrm{sim}(\bm{z}_{L}^{\texttt{<CNF>}},\bm{e}^{\texttt{<CNF>}}).

(4)

In causal language models, the language modeling head (typically a linear projection followed by a softmax) serves as a mechanism to project the last hidden state to the vocabulary.
This projection implicitly evaluates the similarity between the last hidden state and each token embedding in the vocabulary.
We leverage this built-in capability as the foundation for defining function
\mathrm{sim}(\cdot)
, aligning it with the model’s native representation geometry:

c=\mathrm{softmax}(\bm{E}\bm{z}_{L}^{\texttt{<CNF>}})_{\texttt{<CNF>}},

(5)

where
\bm{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{(|\mathcal{V}|+1)\times d}
is the embedding matrix of the vocabulary,
d
is the dimension of token embeddings, the subscript
<CNF>
is the position associated with
<CNF>
.
Note that the position of
<CNF>
is flexible depending on the segment that the users care about, we provide the experiment results with
<CNF>
prepending the response in Ablation Study in §
4.1
.

3.2.2
Setting Target Confidence for GrACE

To guide the model to generate a discriminative and well-calibrated confidence score
c
, it is important to set target confidences for responses
\bm{y}
that are distributionally sharp and numerically aligned with their actual probability of correctness
kuleshov2015calibrated
.
Traditional methods use binary labels as confidence targets, leading to only a sharp distribution with poor calibration
guo2017calibration
.
We propose to smooth the confidence target and ensure calibration by grouping the training data and assigning the empirical accuracy within each group as the supervision target.
The grouping is determined by the outputs of a linear probe trained with binary labels, which helps ensure a sharper distribution.
Specifically, we take the question-answer pair
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
as input to the model and probe an uncalibrated confidence score
s
from the last hidden layer
L
of the model for generation in sequence length
N
with a linear probe parameterized with
\theta
, formulated as:

s={\bm{W}}_{\theta}^{T}\bar{\bm{z}}_{L}+{\bm{b}}_{\theta},\quad\bar{\bm{z}}_{L}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{z}^{i}_{L}}{N}.

(6)

We perform k-fold binning
liu2024enhancing
to align
s
from probing with response accuracy for every training sample.
Specifically, we partition the dataset into
k
folds, iteratively train the linear classifier on
k-1
folds and test on the remaining fold until all the samples in the training data
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
are annotated with
s
.
To convert
s
to response accuracy, we group the data into bins with equal intervals according to
s
and calculate the accuracy
\text{A}_{j}
in each bin
B_{j}
.
Let the target
t_{i}=A_{j}
if the data point
(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i})\in B_{j}
.
The resulting calibration set is defined as:

\mathcal{D}_{c}=\left\{(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{i},t_{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{D}_{c}|}.

In this way, we obtain targets for GrACE that are numerically equal to the accuracies within each group and sharply distributed, as the probe is trained to assess the factuality of the question–answer pairs.

3.2.3
Training Process

We optimize the model to align its self-reflective confidence
c
with the calibration target
t
using the Mean Squared Error (MSE) as calibration loss.
However, fine-tuning the model towards only the calibration objective will sacrifice the model’s overall performance.
To maintain the model utility, we adopt the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) loss on the original generation of the model as a regularizer.
Note that we exclude the
<CNF>
from the calculation of SFT loss to avoid the conflict between the two objectives.
The total loss is formulated as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{T}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{C}}+\gamma\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{c}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{D}_{c}|}(t_{i}-\mathrm{sim}(\bm{z}_{L}^{\texttt{<CNF>}},e^{\texttt{<CNF>}}))^{2}-\gamma\mathrm{log}p_{\theta}(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{x}_{i})),

(7)

where
\gamma
is a hyperparameter for balancing the loss terms
\mathcal{L}_{\text{C}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}
.
Training the model to achieve calibration amortizes the computation cost at the inference stage and is more effective compared with the black-box methods.

3.2.4
Test-Time Scaling with GrACE

As mentioned in §
3.1
, GrACE helps the model to output discriminative and well-calibrated confidence with negligible inference cost.
Building upon this, we incorporate GrACE into the common aggregation and adaptive sampling practices, namely self-consistency
wangself
and early-stopping
liescape
, in TTS for improving the scaling law at inference time without much additional computation for confidence estimation.

Self-Consistency with GrACE (GrACE-SC).

Self-consistency determines the accepted response by taking a majority vote over all samples generated through TTS.
Intuitively, given the variance in sample quality, the voting process should account for this heterogeneity by assigning greater weight to samples with higher confidence.
Thus, we leverage the discrimination of the confidence and reformulate the self-consistency.
Given the sample answer set
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},...,a_{T}\}
where the answer
a_{i}
is the final answer extracted from model response
\bm{y}_{i}
and
T
is the sampling budget, GrACE-SC determines the final answer
a^{*}_{sc}
:

a^{*}_{sc}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\mathbb{I}(a_{i}=a)\cdot c_{i},

(8)

where
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
is an indicator function which returns 1 if the answer extracted from the
i
-th sample
a_{i}
matches
a
else returns 0.

Early Stopping with GrACE (GrACE-ES).

Early stopping terminates the sampling process once the generated samples meet predefined criteria, thereby reducing the number of samples required by the TTS algorithm.
Recent approaches
liescape
;
wan2025reasoningawareselfconsistencyleveraging
;
aggarwal2023let
build upon the concept of self-consistency.
They partition the sampling trajectory into smaller windows and halt further sampling once the consistency within a window exceeds a specified threshold.
However, these methods typically rely on pre-sampling to compute the statistics necessary for evaluating the stopping criterion.
As a sampling-free method, GrACE produces discriminative and calibrated confidence scores in real-time for each sample during inference, which serves as an effective and reliable criterion for early-stopping.
We keep sampling until we obtain a sample with confidence larger than the threshold
\tau
and use this sample as the final prediction.
If the sampling budget runs out and no sample’s confidence reach the threshold, we pick the final answer using GrACE-SC.
The pseudocode for GrACE-ES is in Appendix
A.3
.

4
Experiments

We introduce the experimental settings and the results in this section to demonstrate the effectiveness of GrACE on both 1) open-ended generation and 2) test-time scaling.

4.1
GrACE for Open-ended Generation

Experiment Settings.

We use two popular question-answering datasets, TriviaQA
joshi2017triviaqa
and SciQ
welbl2017crowdsourcing
, for evaluating the quality of confidence produced by GrACE in the open-ended generation task.
Note that in the open-ended generation setting, the model is not provided with candidate answers, unlike in the multiple-choice setting.
Instead, we ask the model to generate answers with greedy decoding.
The correctness of model answers is determined by ROUGE score
lin2004rouge
following the setting in Ulmer et al.
ulmer2024calibrating
.
We select three backbone models with different architectures, scales, and training strategies:
Phi-3-3.8B
abdin2024phi
, Llama-2-7B
touvron2023llama
, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
grattafiori2024llama
, to comprehensively validate the effectiveness of our method.
We only fine-tune a set of LoRA
hulora
and the embedding of
<CNF>
.
The implementation details are in Appendix
A
.

Metrics.

We report the commonly used calibration metrics, including expected calibration error (ECE)
naeini2015obtaining
and Brier score
brier1950verification
, to comprehensively examine the calibration performances of different methods.
ECE measures the average discrepancy between a model’s predicted confidence and its true accuracy across discrete confidence bins.
We choose the number of bins as 10 in all the experiments.
Brier score is the mean squared difference between the predicted confidence scores and the actual correctness.
Additionally, we report AUROC to show how indicative the confidence is of the factuality of the answer.

Comparison Methods.

We choose six strong confidence elicitation and calibration methods as competitors with GrACE.

Seq. Likelihood

malininuncertainty
uses the length-normalized likelihood of generated sequences as predicted confidence.

Platt Scaling

platt1999probabilistic
fits the Seq. Likelihood to minimize the mean squared error on the validation set with scalers
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

P
(True)

kadavath2022language
prompts the LLMs to decide whether or not the generation is true and uses the probability of true as the predictive confidence.

Verbal

tian2023just
directly asks the model to assess its confidence in percentage.

Apricot

ulmer2024calibrating
sets the accuracy of semantic clusters as the calibration target and trains an auxiliary model on the generation to predict confidence.

ActCab

liu2024enhancing
trains a linear probe with the activation from LLMs as the calibrator.
Note that Platt Scaling, Apricot, and ActCab need to train auxiliary models while other comparison methods are training-free.

GrACE produces reliable confidence in open-ended generation.

We report our results in Table
1
and the corresponding reliability diagrams in Appendix
B.1
.
GrACE outperforms one state-of-the-art method (Apricot
ulmer2024calibrating
) by 3.9%, 2.5%, and 5.0% in terms of ECE, Brier and AUROC.
Fine-tuning with GrACE does not affect the model utility as the accuracies on both datsets maintains at the same level with the original model.
Moreover, GrACE achieves the best AUROC among all baselines, indicating that its elicited confidence scores are highly predictive of response factuality.
On TriviaQA with Llama2-7B, ActCab yields the lowest ECE; however, as Figure
6
shows, it pushes most samples toward a confidence around 0.5, offering limited insight into answer correctness.
We find verbalized confidence calibrates the worst.
Most verbalized confidences are in the range of (0.9,1], indicating the model becomes overconfident when expressing verbalized confidence, which aligns with the conclusion of Xiong et al.
xiongcan
.
Our findings further demonstrate that LLMs themselves learn the true distribution of prediction correctness better without any auxiliary model.

Generalization to unseen domain.

We study the generalization ability of confidence elicitation methods that need training (i.e., Apricot, ActCab, GrACE).
We conduct cross-validation using TriviaQA and SciQ, that is, train the model on one dataset and test on another to find out if the method performs consistently well in both in-domain (IND) and out-of-domain (OOD) setting.
The results are reported in Figure
3
.
GrACE exhibits the strongest generalization, with only marginal increases in ECE (+3.3%) and Brier score (+2.4%), and a slight AUROC drop (-3.9%) under domain shift.
Moreover, it achieves the highest absolute performance in the OOD setting, outperforming all baselines by a large margin.
Apricot is prone to domain shift as the AUROC decreases by 16.0% on average, indicating that much reliance on the question semantics provides a shortcut towards calibration with limitations in generalization.
ActCab only trains a linear probe as a calibrator, which is not capable of fully capturing the correspondence between questions and correct answers.
In contrast, GrACE teaches LLM itself the true distribution of the prediction correctness, explicitly encouraging the LLM to internalize the mapping between quesion-answer pairs and the accuracy.
This enables GrACE to generalize more effectively across domains with different semantics.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose GrACE, an approach to eliciting calibrated generative confidence from LLMs.
Unlike post-generation and verbalized confidence elicitation methods, GrACE helps the model to output a confidence score using the real number during generation.
The LLM is trained to express its confidence by internally measuring the similarity between its last hidden state and the embedding of a special token
<CNF>
.
GrACE first constructs calibration targets by probing the self-awareness of the model towards the facuality of the generation and connects the self-awareness to accuracy using k-fold binning.
The model is then fine-tuned to assign a probability mass that correlates with calibration targets to the
<CNF>
at the end of its response.
Extensive experiments show that GrACE outperforms six confidence elicitation methods in terms of open-ended generation calibration, while demonstrating strong domain generalization ability.
Moreover, the calibrated confidence score from the model trained with GrACE improves both the efficacy and efficiency of test-time scaling.

Appendix C
Limitation

Despite the calibration performance and extended applications, GrACE has several limitations.
First, GrACE is only able to provide a single confidence score that reflects the overall confidence for the generation.
It cannot give different confidence scores for every claim in the generation or each step in Chain-of-Thoughts, which will be addressed in our future work.
Second, GrACE focuses only on reflecting the factuality of response through confidence, while other aspects regarding model response, such as completeness, should also be considered in future work.
Finally, GrACE only assesses but does not improve the model confidence.
