Real‐World Evidence for Existing Anti‐Obesity Therapies—The Realities of Treatment Adherence, Tolerability, Access and Patient Satisfaction
Authors: Sameer Kassem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundObesity is associated with substantial cardiometabolic and psychosocial morbidity. Although randomized controlled trials demonstrate the efficacy of anti‐obesity therapies, outcomes in real‐world practice may be attenuated due to limited access, tolerability, adherence and insufficient long‐term support.PurposeThis narrative review aimed to synthesize real‐world evidence on adherence, tolerability, access and patient satisfaction across lifestyle programmes, pharmacotherapy, metabolic bariatric surgery (MBS) and endoscopic procedures, with emphasis on incretin‐based medications.MethodsPubMed/MEDLINE search was conducted through July 2026. Studies reporting real‐world treatment effectiveness, adherence, persistence, discontinuation, tolerability, access or patient satisfaction in adults or adolescents with overweight or obesity were included. Randomized trials and reviews were used to contextualize real‐world findings.FindingsIntensive lifestyle interventions generally achieve approximately 5% weight loss, but attrition and weight regain after programme completion are common. Incretin‐based therapies, including semaglutide and tirzepatide, produce substantial weight loss in trials but real‐world effectiveness is relatively smaller because of non‐initiation, incomplete dose escalation, gastrointestinal adverse effects, treatment interruptions, and financial or insurance barriers. Older medications demonstrate poor persistence, largely related to neurologic, psychiatric or gastrointestinal intolerance, despite lower costs for some agents. MBS provides the greatest and most durable weight loss but remains substantially underused, with racial, socioeconomic and insurance disparities. Endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty offers moderate, durable weight loss with low serious adverse event rates, whereas intragastric balloons are more limited by intolerance, reintervention and weight regain after removal.ConclusionsReal‐world effectiveness is determined not only by biological efficacy but also by treatment tolerability, adherence, access and sustained support. Proactive adverse effect management, individualized behavioural support, streamlined referral pathways and policies that improve affordability and equitable access are essential to narrowing the trial‐to‐practice gap.

Introduction

The global pandemic of overweight and obesity represents an unprecedented public health crisis. As of 2021, it affected approximately 2.11 billion adults worldwide (45% of the global adult population), with 504 million women and 374 million men living with obesity [1]. Among children and adolescents, 18.1% of those aged 5–14 years and 20.3% of those aged 15–24 years had overweight or obesity in 2021, representing 493 million young people worldwide [2]. By 2050, global obesity prevalence is forecasted to increase by an additional 68.3%, with approximately 1 in 3 adults expected to have obesity [1]. The rapidly escalating crisis is driven by several factors, including urbanization, lifestyle changes, unhealthy dietary habits and decreased physical activity [3].

The burden of obesity on healthcare systems is immense, as it is associated with a wide range of comorbidities that significantly impact both individual health and societal resources. Obesity is a well‐established risk factor for hypertension, diabetes and metabolic syndrome, all of which contribute to cardiovascular morbidity [4, 5]. Obesity is closely related to metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), which represents the most common chronic liver disease worldwide [6]. MASLD affects approximately 70% to 80% of those with obesity [7]. Beyond its physical health implications, obesity significantly impacts mental health and psychosocial well‐being, with a complex bidirectional relationship between these conditions [8]. Obesity can contribute to anxiety and depression, often exacerbated by issues related to body image, self‐esteem and social stigma [9].

Thus, addressing obesity requires a comprehensive public health strategy that encompasses more than just weight loss therapies. It should promote healthier lifestyles, improve access to nutritious foods, enhance mental health support and address the social factors contributing to obesity [10]. These efforts are essential for improving individual health and alleviating the burden on global healthcare systems.

Anti‐obesity therapies are vital in the battle against obesity, with advancements in their development playing a key role in breaking the cycle of obesity and the associated physical and mental health challenges faced by many individuals. These therapies include lifestyle and behavioural interventions, pharmacological treatments, surgical options, as well as emerging procedures and medications. Anti‐obesity medications are effective adjunctive therapies that, when combined with lifestyle interventions, produce clinically meaningful weight loss and improve cardiometabolic risk and outcomes [11].

In light of the growing burden of obesity and the introduction of newer anti‐obesity therapies, this narrative review aims to better understand how anti‐obesity therapies perform in real‐world practice beyond the efficacy seen in randomized controlled trials. It will examine real‐world effectiveness, adherence, tolerability, access barriers, persistence and patient satisfaction associated with anti‐obesity therapies currently used in clinical practice.

Methods

This narrative review synthesized real‐world evidence on effectiveness, adherence, persistence, tolerability and patient satisfaction across established anti‐obesity therapies. Interventions were selected according to the Obesity and Weight Management for the Prevention and Treatment of Diabetes section of the 2026 American Diabetes Association (ADA) Standards of Care [12].

A structured search of PubMed/MEDLINE was conducted through July 2026. English‐language publications were considered. For each intervention, combinations of the keywords “obesity” or “overweight” with the relevant treatment were searched. Searches for real‐world evidence additionally included “real‐world evidence” or “real‐world data” or “real‐life evidence” or “real‐life data”. Outcome terms included “efficacy”, “effectiveness”, “weight loss”, “weight reduction”, “adherence”, “persistence”, “discontinuation”, “switching”, “tolerability” and “patient satisfaction”. Randomized controlled trials and review articles were identified using PubMed publication‐type filters. Reference lists of relevant reviews, clinical trials, observational studies and guidelines/consensus statements were also screened. Titles and abstracts were assessed for relevance, followed by full‐text review of potentially eligible studies.

Studies were eligible if they evaluated adults or adolescents with overweight or obesity receiving a weight‐loss intervention and reported at least one relevant outcome. Outcomes of interest included weight loss, adherence, persistence, discontinuation or switching, adverse events and patient satisfaction. Randomized trials and reviews were included primarily to contextualize real‐world findings, particularly where real‐life evidence was limited. Studies involving patients with type 2 diabetes were included when they reported weight‐management outcomes or treatment‐related adherence, tolerability or satisfaction.

Because of the narrative design, no formal risk‐of‐bias assessment or quantitative meta‐analysis was performed. Findings were synthesized descriptively, with comparisons between randomized trial results and real‐world outcomes when available. Differences in study design, populations, healthcare systems, treatment doses, follow‐up duration and definitions of adherence and persistence were considered when interpreting the findings.

Anti‐Obesity Therapies

In accordance with the most recent ADA guideline, the interventions evaluated in this review comprised behavioural lifestyle interventions, FDA‐approved anti‐obesity medications, metabolic bariatric surgery and endoscopic bariatric procedures. The pharmacological therapies included tirzepatide, semaglutide, liraglutide, phentermine‐topiramate, naltrexone‐bupropion and orlistat. These treatments differ substantially in their mechanisms of action, route and frequency of administration, expected weight‐loss efficacy, adverse effect profiles, treatment burden, durability, accessibility and cost.

The therapies are presented thematically according to treatment modality, with emphasis on real‐world treatment effectiveness, adherence, persistence, discontinuation, tolerability, patient satisfaction and access. Randomized clinical trial findings are discussed to contextualize outcomes observed in real life practice, particularly when real world evidence was limited or heterogeneous. Because the included studies varied in population characteristics, treatment duration, dose, healthcare setting, reimbursement environment and definitions of adherence and persistence, findings are summarized descriptively rather than as direct head‐to‐head comparisons. Lifestyle, Nutritional and Behavioural Interventions

Behavioural lifestyle interventions (BLIs) are a cornerstone of obesity management. These interventions combine dietary changes, physical activity, self‐monitoring, goal setting, cognitive restructuring and behavioural support delivered through group, individual or technology‐based formats. Nutritional interventions for obesity commonly prescribe a daily energy deficit of about 500–750 kcal [12]. A systematic review of 31 studies encompassing 42 BLIs in adults with overweight or obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) found that structured programs, characterized by frequent feedback and ongoing support, consistently achieved clinically meaningful weight loss of about 5%, irrespective of the specific dietary, physical activity or behavioural components used [13]. Long‐term data suggest that lifestyle interventions can maintain approximately 5% weight loss at 2 years. Although some weight regain is common, these programs still meaningfully reduce T2DM and cardiovascular risk [14]. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) has concluded that intensive, multicomponent behavioural interventions in adults with obesity produce clinically significant weight loss and lower the incidence of T2DM in those with elevated plasma glucose, offering moderate benefit with little to no harm [15]. Core behaviour‐modification strategies, such as goal setting, self‐monitoring, stimulus control, cognitive restructuring, stress management, problem‐solving and enhancing social support, are central to improving long‐term weight maintenance [16]. Overall, durable weight management requires a comprehensive, individualized approach that integrates dietary change with behavioural and lifestyle support, with a strong emphasis on adherence and on addressing psychological and environmental determinants of obesity [17].

Real‐world evidence highlights significant challenges in treatment adherence, access and implementation for BLIs. Patient satisfaction varies based on program structure and support. A network meta‐analysis of 76 201 participants in UK behavioural weight management services found that better adherence improved outcomes, but high dropout rates persist [18]. Weight regain is common after programs end; in a 10‐year intervention study, over 25% of 3739 participants regained ≥ 2% of weight 2 years after cessation [19]. A qualitative comparison of an online weight program revealed that participants with ≥ 5% weight loss proactively managed stress and sought solutions, while those with < 5% weight loss struggled more with barriers, interpersonal stress and dissatisfaction with their weight targets. Key facilitators included willpower, knowledge, social support and perceived safety, with their absence posing barriers to effective weight loss effort [20].

The implementation of BLIs in real life faces numerous barriers. A systematic review of 28 studies identified nine key themes impacting the implementation of these interventions. Common factors include support systems, the role of general practitioners, program structure, logistics and psychological factors. Essential components for successful implementation are a strong support network and personalized lifestyle interventions [21].

Perceived barriers among patients significantly impact participation in BLIs. A qualitative study of initiators versus non‐initiators identified three key themes: practical factors such as cost and scheduling, anticipated effectiveness and anticipated pleasantness [22]. Program‐specific challenges, including group dynamics and issues with digital platforms, affected both successful and unsuccessful participants. Additionally, food availability created temptations, and social roles could hinder behaviour change, though some found motivation by seeing themselves as role models.

Healthcare system barriers significantly challenge the implementation of BLIs. Healthcare professionals report obstacles such as lack of time, staff shortages, insufficient tools and limited financial resources [23]. A scientific statement from the American Heart Association revealed a gap between evidence and clinical practice in obesity management, with only 16% of healthcare practitioners identifying evidence‐based lifestyle treatments, varying by practice type [24]. Many practitioners lack familiarity with diet and nutrition specialists and struggle with referrals. Approximately 23% of patients never discuss their weight or lifestyle interventions with clinicians, and nearly 60% report that clinicians often do not ask permission before addressing sensitive obesity issues. Only 52% of patients feel their clinician understands the challenges of being overweight or obese, while over 15% avoid seeking care or skip discussions about weight, especially among those with more severe obesity.

Pharmacotherapy

Current FDA‐approved medications for obesity management include glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 Ras, semaglutide and liraglutide), tirzepatide‐ a dual GLP‐1/glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor agonist, phentermine‐topiramate, naltrexone‐bupropion and orlistat. These medications are indicated for adults with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 or BMI ≥ 27 kg/m2 with weight‐related comorbidities and should be used in conjunction with lifestyle modifications [25]. Where real‐world data are available, the observed effectiveness of anti‐obesity medications appears to be more modest in both individuals without T2DM (Table 2) and those with T2DM (Table 3). Tirzepatide

Tirzepatide is a 39‐amino acid synthetic peptide dual GIP and GLP‐1 Ra that is based on the native GIP sequence with a C20 fatty diacid moiety that extends its half‐life to approximately 5 days [44].

Efficacy

In RCTs, tirzepatide demonstrated remarkable weight loss efficacy in both patients with T2DM and without T2DM, ranging from 13% to 21% body weight reduction, representing some of the most substantial results ever achieved with pharmacotherapy for obesity [26, 45].

Real world data on the efficacy of tirzepatide remains limited. In a prospective observational study involving 115 participants without diabetes, who were titrated up to 5 mg weekly, the mean weight reduction at 12 weeks was 7.3% [27]. In an Italian real‐world study of 70 patients with obesity, significant weight reduction and metabolic improvement were observed over 6 months, with mild gastrointestinal side effects; doses ranged from 2.5 to 7.5 mg [46]. The comparison of tirzepatide effectiveness between real‐world studies and RCTs is substantially confounded by dose differences: real‐world cohorts typically received 2.5–7.5 mg, whereas major RCTs used 10–15 mg. Accordingly, the observed difference in weight loss may be largely attributable to dose differences.

Safety, Tolerability and Adherence

Tirzepatide's safety profile in RCTs is generally acceptable for a weight‐loss medication. Gastrointestinal adverse events occur more frequently than with placebo and contribute to higher discontinuation rates, but serious adverse events are similar to placebo [45, 47, 48]. Most side effects are mild to moderate, transient and occur mainly during dose escalation. In the SURMOUNT‐MAINTAIN trial, 91% of participants completed through week 112 [49]. The incidence of new gastrointestinal events declines over time, with long‐term data showing continued reduction over 3 years [45, 47, 48]. Across SURMOUNT trials, 1.0%–10.5% of tirzepatide‐treated participants discontinued due to gastrointestinal adverse events; incidence decreased during treatment and levelled off during maintenance, suggesting improved tolerability beyond the escalation phase [50, 51].

Real‐world persistence and tolerability patterns of tirzepatide and other anti‐obesity medications seem to differ from trials (Table 4). In a study of 8945 Arab patients with type 2 diabetes, only 3686 (41%) completed 40 weeks of follow‐up. Among completers of follow‐up, 288 discontinued treatment due to adverse effects, mainly gastrointestinal. GLP‐1 Ra‐naive patients had greater weight reduction than those previously exposed, and prior metabolic bariatric surgery was associated with greater weight loss [39]. In a six‐month multicenter observational study from Greece of 260 adults with obesity using low to moderate doses, discontinuation occurred in 20%, most commonly during titration, due to goal achievement, cost, non‐response or adverse events. Gastrointestinal events were the most frequent side effects but rarely led to discontinuation [53]. In Indian adults with T2DM, gastrointestinal intolerance accounted for 36% of discontinuations [52]. In a survey of 120 individuals using tirzepatide for weight management in the United Arab Emirates, participants reported high satisfaction and improvements in sleep, energy, mood and confidence. Gastrointestinal effects were usually mild and transient. Higher Weight Efficacy Lifestyle Questionnaire scores were seen among women, employed participants, those with insurance coverage, early in therapy and among those with prior weight‐loss attempts [65].

Comprehensive real‐world adherence data are still limited given tirzepatide's recent approval, but available studies show heterogeneous dosing achievement and nontrivial discontinuation [39, 46, 52, 53, 66]. The concentration of gastrointestinal adverse events during dose escalation may support adherence if clinicians and patients anticipate and manage these effects. However, discontinuation rates in routine care, where support is less intensive, appear higher than in trials.

Cost

The cost of tirzepatide represents a substantial barrier: as of July 2025, its U.S. average wholesale price was approximately $1304 per month (Figure 1) [12]. Real‐world evidence from other countries indicates that the financial burden of the medication directly affects persistence. In Greece, cost was explicitly cited as a reason for discontinuation [53]. Similarly, in India, financial constraints were reported in 32% of discontinuations among adults with type 2 diabetes [52].

Semaglutide

Semaglutide is a selective GLP‐1 Ra with 94% structural homology to native human GLP‐1 that has been modified to achieve a prolonged half‐life of approximately 5–7 days [67]. The 2.4 mg once‐weekly formulation was approved in 2021 [68], and in 2024 semaglutide received an additional indication for reducing major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in adults with established cardiovascular disease and obesity or overweight [69].

Efficacy

In clinical trials, semaglutide produced substantial weight loss. Among adults without diabetes, mean body‐weight reduction was 14.9% versus 2.4% with placebo, and 86%–89% achieved at least 5% weight loss compared with 29%–48% on placebo [28]. In adults with diabetes, mean weight loss was 9.6% with semaglutide versus 3.4% with placebo, and 69% achieved at least 5% weight loss versus 29% with placebo [40, 70].

Oral semaglutide appears to have weight‐reduction efficacy comparable to the injectable formulation. In adults with overweight or obesity but without type 2 diabetes, 50 mg daily resulted in a mean body‐weight reduction of 15.1% over 68 weeks versus 2.4% with placebo [30]. In individuals with type 2 diabetes, body‐weight reduction was 10.7%, though only 34 participants in that study had diabetes [71]. The recently FDA‐approved 25 mg dose achieved a mean body weight reduction of 13.6% versus 2.2% with placebo at 64 weeks and has a safety profile similar to other GLP‐1Ras, with gastrointestinal adverse events the most common. Most events are mild to moderate, transient and occur during dose escalation [31].

Real‐world data specifically on oral semaglutide for weight loss in individuals without diabetes are not yet available given its FDA approval in December 2025. In patients with diabetes, mean body‐weight reduction was 6% after 38 weeks with oral semaglutide 3–14 mg [41].

A large real‐world study examining cardiovascular risk factor changes among patients prescribed semaglutide, stratified by diabetes status, found more modest weight loss than in RCTs among those without diabetes (n = 7386). Interpretation was limited by lack of information on dose and the mixing of injectable and oral formulations [29]. Overall, real‐world data indicate approximately 4%–10% weight loss at 1 year, generally less than the 10%–15% seen in RCTs, reflecting differences in adherence, dose titration, patient selection and support intensity [72, 73].

Safety, Tolerability and Adherence

In RCTs, semaglutide has a generally favourable safety profile. Most adverse events are mild to moderate gastrointestinal symptoms that are transient and occur primarily during dose escalation; serious adverse events are uncommon and occur at rates similar to or slightly higher than placebo, with no increase in mortality [28, 74, 75]. In a systematic review qualitatively analysing 37 completed Phase 4 interventional trials of GLP‐1Ras, including eight focused on semaglutide, gastrointestinal intolerance emerged as a common adverse effect, reported in 1%–30% of older adults with diabetes [76]. Common side effects with semaglutide include nausea (46.9%), headache (18.4%), vomiting (14.3%), fatigue (9.2%) and dizziness (4.8%) [77].

Real‐world use reveals substantial challenges with tolerability, adherence and access that differ from controlled trials. Discontinuation rates are higher than in RCTs. In a large academic weight‐management center, 25% of patients discontinued semaglutide within 1 year, primarily due to patient factors and side effects; 18.6% never started therapy, largely because of access and cost, leaving 56.4% still on treatment at one year [55]. Another cohort found fewer than 45% of patients were persistent or adherent during the first 12 months [56]. In the All of Us program, more than half discontinued within 12 months; oral semaglutide was stopped more quickly than injectable formulations [57]. In France, 13.0% reported early discontinuation of the 2.4 mg dose, with higher rates among younger adults [78]. An Italian study reported early discontinuation of 6.3% at 3 months and 10.5% at 6 months, mainly due to costs, adverse events and perceived inefficacy [79]. Among patients prescribed GLP‐1 receptor agonists for type 2 diabetes, better adherence correlated with older age, female sex and higher socioeconomic status; semaglutide was associated with the lowest discontinuation and switching rates compared with other GLP‐Ras [80, 81].

Cost

Semaglutide imposes a considerable financial burden. For the 2.4 mg weekly dose, the U.S. average wholesale price is approximately $1619 per month, posing a major barrier, particularly for economically disadvantaged and uninsured individuals [12]. Real‐world data underscore the impact of cost on persistence: in one study of patients prescribed semaglutide or tirzepatide for obesity, 47.6% discontinued due to cost or insurance‐related issues, far exceeding adverse effects (14.6%), medication shortages (11.8%) or unsatisfactory weight loss (1.7%) as reasons [82]. National analyses reveal significant income‐based disparities in semaglutide use. Among adults with type 2 diabetes, those with incomes above the median were 61% more likely to use semaglutide than those with lower incomes, and use was significantly higher among individuals with private insurance [58]. A retrospective analysis of the U.S.‐based Epic Cosmos Dataset showed that while prescriptions for semaglutide and tirzepatide increased from 2021 to 2024, substantial disparities persisted across levels of social vulnerability, with patients in the least vulnerable areas receiving more prescriptions and having higher dispensation rates than those in the most vulnerable areas [54]. Collectively, these findings indicate that financial and coverage barriers are primary drivers of non‐initiation and discontinuation, particularly for obesity indications. Policy interventions to reduce financial barriers are essential to improve persistence and ensure equitable access [82, 83].

Liraglutide

Liraglutide is an acylated human GLP‐1Ra with 97% amino acid sequence homology to endogenous human GLP‐1. It was approved for obesity treatment in adults in 2014 and in adolescents aged ≥ 12 years in 2020. It is administered as a once daily subcutaneous injection with a half‐life of approximately 13 h, starting at 0.6 mg daily and titrated weekly in 0.6 mg increments to a target dose of 3.0 mg to mitigate gastrointestinal side effects [84].

Efficacy

In adults with obesity without diabetes, weight loss after 56 weeks was 8% [32]. In adults with obesity and diabetes, mean weight loss at 56 weeks was 6% with liraglutide versus 1.5% with placebo [42]. A meta‐analysis of 18 RCTs including 6321 participants found a mean 4.7% weight reduction with liraglutide [85]. Another meta‐analysis of 25 randomized controlled trials, including 10 593 participants, reported that liraglutide 1.8 mg produced a significantly greater weight loss of −1.93 kg versus placebo, while liraglutide 1.2 mg produced a mean reduction of −0.75 kg versus placebo [86].

Real‐world efficacy data in individuals without diabetes are limited. The Swiss ADDRESS study evaluated liraglutide 3.0 mg in routine practice among adults with obesity or overweight without diabetes and reported a mean 12.4% weight reduction after 10 months in 162 patients who were reimbursed and continued treatment for the full period [87]. However, this study has limitations that reduce generalizability: patients were managed in specialized obesity clinics; accompanying lifestyle interventions were not fully described; and the cohort was enriched for “good responders” who met early weight‐loss targets and thus remained eligible for reimbursement beyond week 16, introducing selection bias. Supporting this, another Swiss retrospective study found that only 13.7% of patients reached the maintenance 3 mg dose, compared with > 90% in the reimbursed ADDRESS cohort [33]. Real‐world studies overall show highly variable outcomes influenced by patient characteristics, adherence, dosing and follow‐up duration. Notably, effectiveness is generally lower in patients with type 2 diabetes than in those without, mirroring RCT findings [72]. Large real‐world studies specifically examining liraglutide 3.0 mg for weight management in diabetes are scarce; most evidence derives from mixed populations and small cohorts. In a large U.S. study of 1671 patients (86% with diabetes), weight loss at 1 year was 2.2% [43].

Safety, Tolerability and Adherence

The tolerability to 3 mg liraglutide in RCT is relatively low compared to other GLP‐1Ras. With a drop out rate of 28% in 56% and gastrointestinal adverse events were the most common (10%) reason for treatment discontinuation [32]. Real‐world evidence highlights major challenges with adherence, tolerability and access, with discontinuation rates far exceeding those in clinical trials. In a population‐based retrospective study from Israel, only 15.1% of patients demonstrated high adherence at 6 months, with mean adherence of 45.3% among continuers. Discontinuation was consistently high, about 50.1% by 2 months and 90% by 6 months, compared with 20%–28% at 1 year in RCTs [59]. Other studies similarly report high non‐persistence: 46% at 6 months in Canada [88], 57.4% at 7 months in Switzerland [33] and 70.1% at 6 months in South Korea [89]. Much real‐world persistence data for liraglutide come from the lower daily doses used for diabetes. In the TROPHIES observational study, 45.2% of patients had switched from daily liraglutide by 24 months, a rate significantly higher than with once‐weekly dulaglutide, indicating poorer persistence with daily regimens [90]. In a population‐based study of 27 596 patients initiating liraglutide for diabetes, about one‐third were switched to another GLP‐1 Ra, predominantly to once‐weekly formulations [81]. In a real‐world analysis of 4066 commercially insured adults without diabetes initiating GLP‐1 therapy, including liraglutide, one‐year persistence with liraglutide was 19.2%, average adherence was 51.0%, with only 27.2% meeting adherence thresholds [60].

Cost

With an average wholesale cost of approximately $1319 per month, liraglutide is a high‐cost anti‐obesity treatment [12]. Financial burden is a major driver of non‐initiation and early discontinuation. In a U.S. study of 853 patients, about 25% of those prescribed liraglutide never initiated therapy, and nearly half of initiators discontinued within 2 years. The most frequently cited reasons for discontinuation were loss to follow‐up with the original prescriber (26.6%), adverse effects (23.7%) and loss of insurance coverage (21.4%) [91]. In a multicenter Turkish study, 42.6% of patients who discontinued identified cost as the primary reason [92]. Similar patterns were observed in France, where liraglutide for obesity was introduced without reimbursement: 26% never initiated treatment, and only 28% of initiators remained on therapy at 6 months. Among non‐initiators, the leading reasons were cost (65%) and fear of side effects (35%); among discontinuers, cost (33%) and adverse effects (31%) predominated [93]. Collectively, these findings highlight cost as a central determinant of liraglutide uptake and persistence.

Phentermine‐Topiramate

The FDA approved the combination of phentermine and topiramate extended release (ER) in 2012 for chronic weight management as an adjunct to lifestyle modification [94]. Phentermine‐topiramate has a complex safety profile characterized by common neurologic side effects, serious warnings related to teratogenicity and neuropsychiatric effects, and multiple contraindications [95]. The most common adverse reactions in adults include paresthesia, dysgeusia, dry mouth, constipation, dizziness, insomnia, disturbance in attention, irritability, hypoesthesia, headache, drowsiness, word aphasia, memory disturbances, anxiety and depression [96].

Efficacy

In clinical trials, patients treated with phentermine‐topiramate lost an average of 10% of initial body weight, approximately 8% more than placebo. Across Phase 3 trials (EQUIP, CONQUER and SEQUEL), phentermine‐topiramate consistently produced statistically significant weight loss versus placebo, with 10.6% loss at 56 weeks on the 15/92 mg dose [34, 61, 62, 63]. In patients with diabetes, mean weight loss at 12 months was 9.4% [97].

Real‐world efficacy data remain limited. In a Korean retrospective study, phentermine–topiramate produced the greatest sustained 12‐month weight loss compared with orlistat and liraglutide [98]. Beyond weight loss, phentermine‐topiramate treatment has been associated with improvements in glucose regulation, lipid profiles and blood pressure [35].

Safety, Tolerability and Adherence

Tolerability appears poorer than with incretin‐based therapies in RCTs. In the CONQUER trial, 38% discontinued study medication by week 56 [61] and in EQUIP, 40% discontinued [34], with adverse events a common cause of withdrawal. Real‐world data indicate that phentermine‐topiramate has among the lowest adherence rates of any anti‐obesity medication in routine practice. Although specific discontinuation reasons are not consistently reported, the high frequency of nervous system adverse events likely contributes substantially to cessation [11]. Despite relative affordability, persistence declines sharply over time, from 36% at 3 months to 13% at 12 months [64]. In a retrospective chart review of 126 adolescents with obesity, 58% had discontinued by 12 months, most commonly due to loss to follow‐up [99]. In a South Korean cohort of 1839 adults with obesity, 48% of patients remained on phentermine‐topiramate at 5–6 months, and only 21% continued treatment at 12 months [35]. An Australian study reported a mean dispensation duration of 10 months and 61 of 103 patients discontinued before the end of data collection, including 41 due to adverse effects [100].

Cost

Phentermine‐topiramate is substantially more affordable than GLP‐1Ras and is the lowest‐cost FDA‐approved branded combination prescription therapy for obesity. The average wholesale price is approximately $238 per month, about 6–7 times lower than semaglutide or liraglutide and 4–5 times lower than tirzepatide, based on 2026 American Diabetes Association data [12].

Naltrexone‐Bupropion

Naltrexone‐bupropion is a fixed‐dose combination of naltrexone (an opioid antagonist) and bupropion (a dopamine and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor). Bupropion stimulates hypothalamic pro‐opiomelanocortin (POMC) neurons to reduce appetite, while naltrexone blocks opioid‐mediated POMC autoinhibition, producing synergistic effects on hypothalamic appetite centers and mesolimbic reward pathways that regulate food intake [101]. The most common adverse effects are nausea, constipation, headache, vomiting, dizziness, insomnia and dry mouth.

Efficacy

The Contrave Obesity Research (COR) program provides the principal efficacy evidence and comprises four Phase III randomized controlled trials (COR‐I, COR‐II, COR‐BMOD and COR‐DM). In COR‐I and COR‐II, approximately 48%–55% of participants achieved ≥ 5% weight loss with naltrexone‐bupropion plus lifestyle intervention [36, 102]. In COR‐DM (patients with type 2 diabetes), about 45% reached the same threshold [103] and in COR‐BMOD (with intensive behavioural modification), two‐thirds achieved ≥ 5% weight loss [104].

Safety, Tolerability and Adherence

Trial dropout rates were high, reaching 50% in COR‐I [36]. The most frequently reported adverse events were nausea, headache, dizziness, vomiting, constipation and dry mouth [105]. Nevertheless, the overall adverse‐effect profile appears less severe than that of phentermine‐topiramate [106]. Naltrexone‐bupropion is associated with a transient increase in blood pressure of about 1.5 mmHg, followed by a decline to roughly 1 mmHg below baseline [36]. The American Association of Clinical Endocrinology recommends monitoring blood pressure and heart rate at initiation and at regular intervals thereafter [84].

Real‐world effectiveness data for weight reduction are limited, and adherence is poor. In a study that evaluated several anti‐obesity medications, only 33.5% of patients remain on naltrexone‐bupropion at 3 months and 9.7% at 12 months‐among the lowest persistence rates for any FDA‐approved anti‐obesity medication. Although specific reasons for discontinuation are not consistently reported, the burden of neuropsychiatric adverse effects likely contributes substantially [64]. In a U.S. MarketScan analysis of 11 660 patients prescribed naltrexone‐bupropion, only 9% remained on treatment at 12 months [107]. Across 37 Veterans Affairs medical centers (n = 311), 16% remained on therapy at 12 months [108]. In a Canadian real‐world cohort, 245 (52%) completed 6 months of treatment, with a mean weight loss of 4% among completers [37]. In a Chinese cohort of 37 patients, only 13 (35%) persisted for 12 months. Among completers, weight loss averaged 10%, while 27.0% discontinued due to adverse effects, primarily neurological and gastrointestinal [109].

Cost

Naltrexone‐bupropion is mid‐priced among anti‐obesity medications, costing roughly three times more than phentermine‐topiramate but about half as much as GLP‐1 Ras [11, 12].

Orlistat

Orlistat was FDA approved in 1999 for the treatment of obesity in adults, making it one of the longest‐standing anti‐obesity medications available. It acts by reversibly inhibiting gastric and pancreatic lipases, reducing absorption of ingested dietary fat by approximately 30% [110].

Efficacy

In a two‐year study, participants receiving orlistat lost more weight initially (8.76 kg) than those on placebo (5.81 kg) and regained less weight in the second year. In obese adults with type 2 diabetes, orlistat also improved glycemic parameters, LDL cholesterol and insulin levels [38]. A European multicenter study found that patients treated with orlistat lost an average of 10.2% of baseline body weight within 1 year, compared with 6.1% in the placebo group; in the second year, those who continued orlistat regained only about half as much weight as those switched to placebo [111]. In patients with diabetes, a systematic review of 12 RCTs demonstrated a mean weight reduction of 4.25 kg at 3–12 months [112].

Because real‐world use of orlistat is extremely low, robust effectiveness data from routine practice are lacking.

Safety, Tolerability and Adherence

Tolerability in clinical trials is relatively low, with only 50% of participants completing 2 years of therapy [38]. Orlistat frequently causes predictable gastrointestinal side effects related to fat malabsorption, including steatorrhea, oily spotting, faecal urgency and flatulence [113]. Because it can reduce absorption of fat‐soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), daily multivitamin supplementation is recommended. Fat malabsorption may also increase urinary oxalate, leading to enteric hyperoxaluria and potentially acute nephropathy or chronic kidney disease, and orlistat has rarely been associated with hepatotoxicity [114]. The American Gastroenterological Association made a conditional recommendation against orlistat use given its modest weight loss and moderate magnitude of harms [101].

Real‐world adherence to orlistat is extremely poor. In a retrospective cohort from a large health system in Ohio and Florida, no patients remained on treatment at 12 months [64]. In a population‐based Israeli study, the mean duration of orlistat use was 2.1 months, and fewer than 2% of patients completed 12 months of therapy [115].

Cost

The 60 mg over‐the‐counter formulation of orlistat is the least expensive FDA‐approved anti‐obesity medication, with an estimated generic manufacturing cost of about $7 per 30‐day course [116]. In contrast, the prescription 120 mg dose is substantially more expensive, with an average wholesale price of approximately $675 per month [12]. Although absolute prices vary across countries and health systems, both formulations remain markedly less costly than most other approved pharmacologic treatments for obesity. However, despite relatively low cost and OTC availability, real‐world use is limited, largely because of characteristic gastrointestinal adverse effects. Additionally, a pharmacovigilance analysis using the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System identified further adverse events not routinely listed in product information, including lipiduria, anal and rectal haemorrhage, hematochezia, sigmoiditis, diverticulitis and muscle spasms [117].

Metabolic Bariatric Surgery (MBS)

The 2022 joint guidelines from the American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery (ASMBS) and the International Federation for the Surgery of Obesity and Metabolic Disorders (IFSO) specify indications for bariatric surgery, including a BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2; a BMI of 30–34.9 kg/m2 with accompanying metabolic diseases, particularly type 2 diabetes; and a BMI ≥ 27.5 kg/m2 for Asian populations, reflecting adjusted thresholds for ethnic differences in obesity‐related risk [118]. In the United States, approximately 252 000 primary bariatric procedures are performed annually, with 61% being laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG) and 17% laparoscopic gastric bypass (LGBP) [119]. By 2020, adjustable gastric banding (AGB) accounted for less than 2% of procedures due to poor long‐term efficacy and high complication rates [120].

Efficacy

Metabolic bariatric surgery is highly effective for weight loss and metabolic outcomes, achieving 25%–31% total body weight loss that is sustained beyond 10 years, with superior glycemic control, diabetes remission rates of 18%–51% at long‐term follow‐up and significant reductions in cardiovascular events, microvascular complications and all‐cause mortality compared to medical therapy [121].

A cross‐sectional analysis of 76 077 patients in a national Medicare claims database (2012–2017) found that LSG was associated with lower one‐year risks of complications, hospitalizations and mortality compared with LGBP. In a separate cohort of 1331 patients, mean percent excess weight loss at 2 years was highest for LGBP, followed by LSG and AGB. The rate of inadequate treatment response (defined as < 25% excess weight loss) at 3 years was 1.0% for LGBP, 25.3% for LSG and 30.2% for AGB [122].

Safety, Tolerability and Satisfaction

Modern bariatric surgery has low perioperative mortality. For primary procedures, 30‐day mortality ranges from 0.03% to 0.2%, with procedure‐specific rates of 0.03%–0.08% for LSG, 0.07%–0.2% for LGBP and 0.01%–0.05% for AGB [123]. The overall rate of adverse events in bariatric surgery is approximately 20%, with laparoscopic approaches resulting in fewer wound complications than open procedures [120].

Patient satisfaction with bariatric surgery is typically very high in the short term. A study involving 11 146 patients 1–2 years after surgery showed that 97% were satisfied with their decision to undergo the procedure [124]. Similarly, a four‐year analysis at a bariatric ambulatory surgery center reported consistently high patient satisfaction scores, averaging between 9.74 and 9.95 out of 10 [125]. However, satisfaction can decline over time. A multicenter study of 1423 participants who underwent gastric bypass surgery revealed that dissatisfaction increased from 15.4% at 3 years to 23.0% at 7 years post‐surgery. Factors linked to lower satisfaction include younger age, lower BMI, poorer physical and mental health, inadequate pre‐surgery social support, insufficient post‐surgery weight loss and worsening mental health along with increased depressive symptoms [126]. Lifelong micronutrient supplementation is crucial after MBS due to altered gastrointestinal anatomy. A survey of 110 adults in India found that 41% of participants had discontinued supplements, primarily due to forgetfulness (35%), cost/safety concerns (22%) and swallowing difficulty (14%) [127].

Accessibility and Barriers

Real‐world data on bariatric surgery reveals significant access barriers and disparities. Despite the well‐established benefits of the procedure, its utilization remains low, with approximately 1% of eligible adults in the U.S. undergoing surgery [128]. Moreover, a concerning trend shows that obesity rates are rising more rapidly than bariatric surgery utilization. Between 2015 and 2018, obesity rates increased by 3.32% per year, while the rate of surgery among the eligible population grew by only 2.47% annually during the same timeframe [129]. This underscores a critical gap between those who could benefit from treatment and those who receive it.

Racial and socioeconomic disparities further exacerbate this issue, with Black and Hispanic/Latino adolescents and adults, as well as individuals with limited social and economic resources, significantly less likely to undergo surgery [24]. A study involving nearly 6000 patients evaluated at bariatric surgery clinics found that those who went on to have surgery were predominantly White, married, full‐time employed and lived in areas with lower poverty rates—demographic patterns that do not represent the populations most affected by obesity. Among those who withdrew from surgery, common barriers included fear of complications, lengthy insurance approval processes and insufficient social support. Many individuals reported a perception that the necessary support for achieving successful outcomes is lacking in under‐resourced communities [130].

An additional barrier to bariatric surgery utilization is the knowledge gap among healthcare providers. Only 16% of healthcare practitioners can identify evidence‐based treatments for obesity, which contributes to low referral rates for surgery [24].

Endoscopic Bariatric Procedures

Currently available endoscopic bariatric procedures include intragastric balloons, endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty, aspiration therapy, transpyloric shuttle, duodenal‐jejunal bypass liner and duodenal mucosal resurfacing. Only intragastric balloons and endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty are FDA‐approved.

Intragastric Balloons (IGB)

Intragastric balloons (IGB) are space‐occupying devices that delay gastric emptying and increase satiety. The first intragastric balloon, the Garren‐Edwards Bubble, was approved by the FDA in 1985 but withdrawn in 1992 because of limited efficacy and significant complications. Modern devices followed, with the ReShape Duo Integrated Dual Balloon System becoming the first of the contemporary IGBs to receive FDA approval in 2015 [131]. The devices are placed via upper endoscopy, filled with saline and removed after 6 to 8 months.

Efficacy

In a clinical trial of 255 patients, lifestyle intervention plus balloon reduced weight by 10.2% at 6 months compared with 3.3% for lifestyle intervention alone. Six months after balloon removal, patients regained some weight, with 7.6% vs. 3.1% total weight loss [132]. Meta‐analyses show mean total body weight loss of 7%–14% at 12 months (compared with 3%–8% with lifestyle intervention) with significant weight regain after device removal [133].

Safety and Tolerability

Adverse effects of the intragastric balloon include abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting and reflux esophagitis, with intolerance often resulting in early balloon removal. Device‐related complications include deflation issues, leakage, rupture and implant failure [134].

Endoscopic Sleeve Gastroplasty (ESG)

ESG involves full‐thickness suturing of the stomach wall to reduce gastric volume by approximately 70% [135]. ESG was first performed clinically in 2013 [136] and was introduced in the American Gastroenterological Association clinical practice updates as an established endoscopic bariatric therapy in 2017 [137].

Efficacy

In RCT of 209 patients, ESG achieved 13.6% weight loss compared with 0.8% with lifestyle modifications alone at 52 weeks [138].

Safety and Tolerability

The procedure is relatively safe, with adverse events ranging between 1.5% and 2.3% [139]. In a meta‐analysis of 1772 patients undergoing ESG, severe adverse events occurred in approximately 2.2% of patients. These included abdominal pain or nausea requiring hospitalization (1.08%), upper gastrointestinal bleeding (0.56%), perigastric leak (0.48%), pulmonary embolism (0.06%) and pneumoperitoneum (0.06%) [140].

Tolerability can be inferred from reintervention and early removal rates. IGB shows consistently higher rates of early reintervention due to intolerance requiring removal or adjustment of the balloon compared to ESG [141]. Since IGB necessitates an additional intervention for device removal at 6 or 12 months, it may be less economically viable than ESG. Thus, ESG may represent a more durable endoscopic bariatric option with advantages in tolerability [142].

Discussion

In this narrative review, we present real‐world data on approved anti‐obesity treatments with emphasis on pharmacotherapy especially the incretin‐based treatments. We discuss limitations and challenges encountered in real‐life practice, which often create an expectation gap relative to findings in RCTs and may often contribute to frustration among both patients and healthcare providers.

We summarize available real‐world evidence and place it in the context of major RCTs. This data should not be interpreted as direct head‐to‐head comparisons. RCTs enrol carefully selected patients, provide close follow‐up, ensure timely dose escalation and maintain uninterrupted treatment. In contrast, real‐world populations are more heterogeneous and may have multimorbidity, financial constraints, limited access to specialist care and psychosocial barriers.

Where evidence is available, we find that real‐world effectiveness is often ameliorated by treatment non‐initiation, switching, early discontinuation, interruptions in therapy, loss to follow‐up and inconsistent behavioural support. Table 5 summarizes real‐world findings on adherence, persistence, patient satisfaction and approximate cost of anti‐obesity therapies. Incretin‐based therapies, including tirzepatide, semaglutide and liraglutide, produce substantial weight loss in clinical trials; however, their real‐world effectiveness may be limited by cost, insurance restrictions, supply interruptions, adverse gastrointestinal effects, inadequate or delayed dose escalation and failure to reach maximal or near‐maximal doses. The use of oral agents such as phentermine‐topiramate, naltrexone‐bupropion and orlistat is also limited, largely because of adverse effects. Real‐world data on the effectiveness persistence of orlistat were not identified. This probably reflects very limited use, potentially related to their tolerability profiles, but this hypothesis requires further confirmation.

Although generally reporting lower effectiveness than RCTs, outcomes of real‐world studies should be carefully evaluated. For example, a real‐world study of phentermine‐topiramate has reported greater weight loss than RCTs in subjects without diabetes (Table 2) [34, 35]. However, the real‐world analysis preferentially included patients who tolerated and persisted with treatment, whereas RCT efficacy was assessed using intention‐to‐treat methodology where considerable drop out was noted. Such findings may therefore be explained by attrition and selection biases and highlight the need for further studies to account for these and other confounders.

In clinical practice, some patients may view medication or a procedure as permission to reduce their attention to healthy behaviours. Therefore, it is important to stress that pharmacotherapy, surgery and endoscopic procedures should complement‐not replace‐behavioural, nutritional and lifestyle interventions. Adequate nutrition with a moderate caloric deficit, regular exercise, good‐quality sleep and other BLIs remain the foundation of obesity prevention and treatment. Therefore, patient education and ongoing behavioural support should accompany every intervention.

Before the initiation of pharmacotherapy, patients should receive counselling regarding the expected benefits and limitations of therapy, the need for dose escalation and long‐term follow‐up, common adverse effects, treatment costs and the likelihood of weight regain after discontinuation. Gastrointestinal symptoms associated with incretin‐based therapies should be anticipated and managed. Dose escalation should be individualized according to the subject's response and tolerability. Patients should be advised to eat slowly, consume smaller meals, avoid high‐fat foods and maintain adequate hydration.

MBS generally provides greater and more durable weight loss but carries risks of perioperative complications, nutritional deficiencies, gastrointestinal symptoms, lifelong supplementation and weight regain. Despite its effectiveness, MBS remains underused because of delayed referral, patient concerns, socioeconomic barriers, limited surgical capacity and inequitable access to multidisciplinary care. Endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty and intragastric balloons offer less invasive alternatives, but long‐term comparative effectiveness relative to pharmacotherapy and surgery remains uncertain.

Differences in insurance coverage and regulations substantially influence real‐world outcomes and limit the generalizability of available evidence. Patients without coverage may discontinue treatment because of its full cost, whereas those with partial coverage may face high deductibles, annual limits or repeated approval requirements; fully insured patients may discontinue because of adverse effects, limited perceived benefit, treatment burden or supply problems. Current evidence does not adequately distinguish non‐initiation and discontinuation related to cost from those caused by other factors. Future studies should report these outcomes separately.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Differences in methodology, inconsistent definitions of effectiveness, adherence, persistence, discontinuation, tolerability, satisfaction and data analysis and reporting are common in real‐world studies. Many are retrospective, short‐term or based on electronic health records that cannot confirm medication use or determine reasons for discontinuation. Comparisons between medications, MBS and endoscopic procedures are further limited by differences in patient selection, baseline demographics, follow‐up duration, treatment intensity and healthcare setting. As a narrative review, this study did not include a formal risk‐of‐bias assessment or quantitative meta‐analysis, and its findings should therefore be viewed as a structured descriptive synthesis rather than an estimate of comparative effectiveness.

Future research should prioritize prospective and pragmatic studies of newer anti‐obesity therapies using standardized definitions of treatment persistence and discontinuation. Comparative studies should assess durability, quality of life, complications, nutritional outcomes and cost‐effectiveness across medical, surgical and endoscopic interventions. They should also examine disparities by socioeconomic and insurance status, race and ethnicity, rurality and language.

Conclusions

Advances in anti‐obesity therapies have substantially expanded treatment options. Nevertheless, real‐world effectiveness depends not only on the biological effect of the treatment and remains constrained by limited access, cost, adverse effects, treatment interruptions and inadequate long‐term support. Only a minority of the growing population with obesity currently receives effective treatment. Healthcare systems and policymakers should promote healthy lifestyle education from an early age, improve access to nutritious foods and facilitate physical activity. Equitable access to comprehensive obesity care should be expanded. And finally, anti‐obesity medications and procedures should be implemented as adjunct and not substitute the behavioural and lifestyle interventions that remain the cornerstone of obesity prevention and treatment.
