Relative Information Gain and Gaussian Process Regression
Hamish Flynn

Abstract
The sample complexity of estimating or maximising an unknown function in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space is known to be linked to both the effective dimension and the information gain associated with the kernel. While the information gain has an attractive information-theoretic interpretation, the effective dimension typically results in better rates. We introduce a new quantity called the relative information gain, which measures the sensitivity of the information gain with respect to the observation noise. We show that the relative information gain smoothly interpolates between the effective dimension and the information gain, and that the relative information gain has the same growth rate as the effective dimension. In the second half of the paper, we prove a new PAC-Bayesian excess risk bound for Gaussian process regression. The relative information gain arises naturally from the complexity term in this PAC-Bayesian bound. We prove bounds on the relative information gain that depend on the spectral properties of the kernel. When these upper bounds are combined with our excess risk bound, we obtain minimax-optimal rates of convergence.

1
Introduction

We consider the model

y_{i}=f^{\star}(x_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}\,,

(1)

with inputs
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathcal{X}
and real-valued responses
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
. The target function
f^{\star}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is an unknown function in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
\mathcal{H}
with reproducing kernel
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. The noise variables are assumed to be independent, centred and
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian.

The model in (
1
) has been extensively studied in the regression setting, in which a sample
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
is used to estimate either
f^{\star}
or the vector of function values
\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}:=[f^{\star}(x_{1}),\dots,f^{\star}(x_{n})]^{\top}

(
Györfi et al. 2002
;
Tsybakov 2009
)
. Kernel ridge regression and Gaussian process regression are among the most popular approaches for the regression problem. For a learning rate
\eta>0
, the kernel ridge regression estimate is defined as

\widehat{f}:=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(x_{i})-y_{i})^{2}+\frac{1}{\eta}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\bigg\}\,.

It is well-known that there is a closed-form solution for
\widehat{f}

(
Schölkopf and Smola 2002
)
, which is

\widehat{f}(x)=\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}(x)(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{\eta}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}\,.

Here,
\mathbf{k}_{n}(x):=[k(x,x_{1}),\dots,k(x,x_{n})]^{\top}
is the vector of kernel comparisons between
x
and the inputs
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
,
\mathbf{K}_{n}:=\{k(x_{i},x_{j})\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
is the usual
n\times n
kernel matrix and
\mathbf{y}_{n}:=[y_{1},\dots,y_{n}]^{\top}
is the response vector. Let us define

\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}:=[\widehat{f}(x_{1}),\dots,\widehat{f}(x_{n})]^{\top}=\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{\eta}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}

as the vector of fitted values. Since each fitted value is a linear combination of the responses
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
, kernel ridge regression is an example of a linear smoother. This means that we can write
\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}=\mathbf{L}_{n}\mathbf{y}_{n}
. In this case,
\mathbf{L}_{n}=\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{\eta}\mathbf{I})^{-1}
. The matrix
\mathbf{L}_{n}
is called the smoothing matrix, and its trace, denoted by
\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{L}_{n})
, is called the effective degrees of freedom
(
Wasserman 2006
)
. We will revisit this quantity shortly.

Gaussian process regression is usually presented as a Bayesian method. It is assumed that
f^{\star}\sim\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}(m(x),k(x,x^{\prime}))
is a random draw from a Gaussian process prior with mean function
m:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and kernel (or covariance) function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. This means that for any fixed sequence of inputs
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, the random vector
\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}
is Gaussian, and in particular,
\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_{n},\mathbf{K}_{n})
, where
\mathbf{m}_{n}:=[m(x_{1}),\dots,m(x_{n})]^{\top}
. If the noise variables are Gaussian with variance
1/\eta
, then the Bayesian posterior (say
Q
) is also a Gaussian process
(
Williams and Rasmussen 2006
)
. In particular, if
f^{\star}
is drawn from a zero-mean prior, i.e.
f^{\star}\sim\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}(0,k(x,x^{\prime}))
, then

Q=\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}(x)(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{\eta}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n},k(x,x^{\prime})-\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}(x)(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{\eta}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{n}(x^{\prime}))\,.

Note that the mean function of
Q
is identical to the kernel ridge regression estimate. It turns out that the covariance function of
Q
, or rather, the covariance matrix of the marginal distribution of
Q
at the points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, is closely related to the effective degrees of freedom
\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{L}_{n})
(cf. Lemma
15
).

The model in (
1
) has also been studied in the bandit setting
(
Srinivas et al. 2010
;
Valko et al. 2013
)
. In kernelised bandits (also known as Gaussian process bandits),
f^{\star}
is called the reward function. The objective is to sequentially maximise the reward function by querying it at a sequence of points
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
and receiving the random rewards
y_{1},y_{2},\dots
, which can be used to inform the selection of future query points. Since the reward function is initially unknown, it must be estimated and maximised simultaneously. For this reason, many kernelised bandit algorithms use kernel ridge regression or Gaussian process regression as a subroutine for estimation.

In both kernel regression and kernelised bandits, there are two widely used notions of complexity, called the effective dimension and the information gain, which are designed to characterise the sample complexity of estimating or maximising the function
f^{\star}
. For any
n\geq 1
and any learning rate
\eta>0
, the effective dimension
(
Zhang 2005
)
is

d_{n}(\eta):=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{\eta}\mathbf{I})^{-1})\,.

Note that
d_{n}(\eta)
is identical to the effective degrees of freedom of the kernel ridge regression estimate. For any
n\geq 1
and any learning rate
\eta\geq 0
, the information gain is defined as

\gamma_{n}(\eta):=\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}+\mathbf{I})\,.

As its name would suggest, the information gain has an appealing information-theoretic interpretation. If in (
1
),
f^{\star}\sim\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}(0,k(x,x^{\prime}))
and
\varepsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/\eta)
, then
\gamma_{n}(\eta)
is equal to the mutual information
I(\mathbf{y}_{n};f^{\star})
between
f^{\star}
and the response vector
\mathbf{y}_{n}
. In other words,
\gamma_{n}(\eta)
is equal to the amount of information that
\mathbf{y}_{n}
provides about
f^{\star}
.

It is known that effective dimension and the information gain are within logarithmic factors of each other
(
Calandriello et al. 2019
;
Zenati et al. 2022
)
. In particular, the growth-rate (in
n
) of the effective dimension is never larger that of the information gain, whereas the growth-rate of the information gain can be larger than that of the effective dimension by a factor of
\log(n)
. Since both quantities can be used to characterise the sample complexity of estimating or maximising
f^{\star}
, it is natural to ask whether there are any other connections between them. One can also ask if there is a single quantity that has both the growth-rate of the effective dimension and an attractive information-theoretic interpretation like that of the information gain. We investigate these questions.

1.1
Contributions

We introduce a new quantity, called the relative information gain, which is the difference between the information gain at two different learning rates. As the smaller of the two learning rates is varied, a scaled version of the relative information gain smoothly interpolates between the effective dimension and the information gain, recovering each of them at the extremes. Moreover, the relative information gain matches the growth-rate of the effective dimension. This reveals that the effective dimension can be interpreted as a measure of how sensitive the information gain is with respect to the variance of the noise in the responses.

We demonstrate that the relative information gain is a reasonably natural notion of complexity for the model in (
1
), and not just an artificial quantity that is designed to interpolate between the effective dimension and the information gain. In particular, we derive a new localised PAC-Bayesian excess risk bound for Gaussian process regression (cf. Theorem
5
) and we find that the relative information gain arises naturally from the complexity term in this PAC-Bayesian bound.

Finally, we show that if
k
is a Mercer kernel, then the relative information gain can be upper bounded based on the rate at which its eigenvalues decay. When these upper bounds are combined with our excess risk bound in Theorem
5
, we obtain minimax-optimal rates of convergence.

1.2
Outline

The rest of this paper is organised as follows. Section
2
describes some related work on rates of convergence for kernel ridge regression and Gaussian process regression, and on PAC-Bayesian bounds for Gaussian processes. In Section
3
, we define the relative information gain and establish some of its properties. In Section 4, we state and sketch the proof of a PAC-Bayesian excess risk bound. In Section 5, we provide upper bounds on the relative information gain and then use them to obtain rates of convergence with explicit dependence on the sample size.

2
Related Work

2.1
Rates of Convergence for Kernel Ridge Regression and Gaussian Process Regression

For the setting we consider, in which
f^{\star}
is a fixed function in the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
, the optimal rates of convergence for kernel ridge regression are well-understood. The rates of convergence that we obtain are the same as those in
(
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
;
Steinwart et al. 2009
;
Dicker et al. 2015
;
Dicker et al. 2017
)
. These works impose similar conditions on the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the kernel. The rates of convergence for Gaussian process regression are also well-understood in this setting. The rate at which the (Bayesian or Gibbs) Gaussian process posterior contracts has been the subject of intense study
(
Seeger et al. 2008
;
Castillo 2008
;
van der Vaart and van Zanten 2008
;
van der Vaart and van Zanten 2011
;
Suzuki 2012
;
Castillo 2014
;
Pati et al. 2015
;
Nickl and Söhl 2017
)
. Several of these results have been used to derive rates of convergence for the excess risk
(
van der Vaart and van Zanten 2011
;
Suzuki 2012
)
.

2.2
PAC-Bayesian Bounds for Gaussian Processes

PAC-Bayesian bounds originate from work by
Shawe-Taylor and Williamson 1997
and
McAllester 1998
. We refer the reader to
Alquier 2024
or
Hellström et al. 2025
for a recent overview. Early work on PAC-Bayesian analysis of Gaussian processes focused on classification
(
Seeger 2002
;
Seeger 2003
)
. More recently, PAC-Bayesian bounds were used to fit Gaussian process predictors for either classification or regression
(
Reeb et al. 2018
)
. The main focus of the aforementioned works was on obtaining numerically tight risk certificates, as opposed to obtaining the best rates of convergence.
Suzuki 2012
derived PAC-Bayesian excess risk bounds for Gaussian process regression which match the optimal rate of convergence under certain conditions. Despite having a similar rate of convergence, these bounds are quite different to ours. In particular, the rate of convergence is determined by the concentration function used in
van der Vaart and van Zanten 2008
;
van der Vaart and van Zanten 2011
, as opposed to a notion of effective dimension or information gain.
Alquier and Ridgway 2020
used PAC-Bayesian bounds to derive rates of convergence for Variational Bayes approximations of Gibbs distributions (such as Gaussian processes). When applied to nonparametric regression over Sobolev ellipsoids, the rate of convergence is optimal up to logarithmic factors.

3
Effective Dimension, Information Gain and Relative Information Gain

We describe a new connection between the effective dimension and the information gain, and we introduce the relative information gain. First, we notice that both the effective dimension and the information gain can be expressed in terms of the eigenvalues
(\lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
of
\mathbf{K}_{n}
. In particular, as we establish in Lemma
10
and Lemma
11
,

d_{n}(\eta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\eta\lambda_{i}}{1+\eta\lambda_{i}}\,,\qquad\gamma_{n}(\eta)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1+\eta\lambda_{i})\,.

(2)

Starting from these expressions, one can easily verify that the effective dimension is related to the derivative of the information gain.

Proposition 1
.

For all
n\geq 1
and
\eta\geq 0
,

d_{n}(\eta)=2\eta\gamma_{n}^{\prime}(\eta)\,.

Proposition
1
gives us an information-theoretic interpretation of the effective dimension. Namely, the effective dimension is a measure of how sensitive the information gain is to the learning rate (or the variance of the noise in the responses). In addition, Proposition
1
suggests that we can obtain a reasonable approximation of the effective dimension by taking the difference between the information gain at two different learning rates. For a sample size
n\geq 1
, and two learning rates
\eta>\beta\geq 0
, we define the relative information gain as

\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta):=\gamma_{n}(\eta)-\gamma_{n}(\beta)\,.

The relative information gain can be interpreted as the additional information that would gained about
f^{\star}
if the variance of the noise in the responses was reduced from
1/\beta
to
1/\eta
. The bottom toast inequality (the bottom half of the sandwich inequality) in Proposition 5 of
Calandriello et al. 2019
states that
d_{n}(\eta)
is bounded by
2\gamma_{n}(\eta)
. We will now show that a scaled version of the relative information gain smoothly interpolates between these two quantities. Using Proposition
1
, and the definition of the derivative, we can express the effective dimension as (the limit of) a scaled version of the relative information gain. In particular, for any learning rates
\eta>\beta>0
,

d_{n}(\eta)=\lim_{\eta_{0}\to\eta^{-}}\frac{2\eta}{\eta-\eta_{0}}\gamma_{n}(\eta,\eta_{0})\approx\frac{2\eta}{\eta-\beta}\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)\,.

(3)

Note that if
\beta=0
, then the right-hand side of (
3
) is equal to twice the information gain. This establishes that the scaled information gain recovers both the effective dimension and the information gain as special cases. The following proposition shows that these are the two extreme cases.

Proposition 2
.

For all
n\geq 1
and
\eta>\beta\geq 0
,

d_{n}(\eta)\leq\frac{2\eta}{\eta-\beta}\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)\leq 2\gamma_{n}(\eta)\,.

The first inequality is sharp in the limit as
\beta
tends to
\eta
from below and the second inequality is sharp when
\beta=0
.

Proof.

The sharpness of each inequality follows from (
3
). For any
\lambda\geq 0
, the mapping
\beta\mapsto\frac{1}{\eta-\beta}\log\frac{1+\eta\lambda}{1+\beta\lambda}
is decreasing on
[0,\eta)
(cf. Lemma
12
). From this and (
2
), it follows that
\beta\mapsto\frac{2\eta}{\eta-\beta}\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)
is also decreasing on
[0,\eta)
. Thus the scaled information gain is bounded between
d_{n}(\eta)
and
2\gamma_{n}(\eta)
for all
\beta
in the interval
[0,\eta)
.
∎

The full sandwich inequality in Proposition 5 of
Calandriello et al. 2019
shows that the effective dimension and the information gain are within logarithmic factors of each other. In particular,

d_{n}(\eta)\leq 2\gamma_{n}(\eta)\leq(1+\log(1+\eta\lambda_{\max}))d_{n}(\eta)\,,

where
\lambda_{\max}=\max_{i}\lambda_{i}
. Since
\lambda_{\max}
is at most of order
n
, this implies that
\gamma_{n}(\eta)=\mathcal{O}(d_{n}(\eta)\log(n))
. We prove that the effective dimension and the relative information gain can form a thinner sandwich.

Proposition 3
.

For all
n\geq 1
and
\eta>\beta>0
,

d_{n}(\eta)\leq\frac{2\eta}{\eta-\beta}\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)\leq\frac{\eta}{\beta}d_{n}(\eta)

Proof.

The first inequality follows from Proposition
2
. Using the inequality
\log(1+x)\leq x
for
x\geq 0
, we obtain

\frac{\eta}{\eta-\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{1+\eta\lambda_{i}}{1+\beta\lambda_{i}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\eta\lambda_{i}}{1+\beta\lambda_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1+\eta\lambda_{i}}{1+\beta\lambda_{i}}\frac{\eta\lambda_{i}}{1+\eta\lambda_{i}}\leq\frac{\eta}{\beta}\sum_{i=1}\frac{\eta\lambda_{i}}{1+\eta\lambda_{i}}\,.

The second inequality now follows from (
2
).
∎

Proposition
3
tells us that the effective dimension and the relative information gain have the same growth-rate whenever
\beta
is positive.

4
Excess Risk Bounds for Gaussian Process Regression

We demonstrate that the relative information gain arises naturally from a localised PAC-Bayesian bound for Gaussian process regression. We use the zero-mean prior
P_{\alpha}=\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}(0,\alpha k(x,x^{\prime}))
with a scale parameter
\alpha>0
. For a learning rate
\eta>0
, we will consider Gibbs distributions
Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}
defined by

\frac{\mathrm{d}{Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}}}{\mathrm{d}{P_{\alpha}}}(f)\propto\exp\bigg(-\eta\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(x_{i})-y_{i})^{2}\bigg)\,.

It is known that
Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}
is also a Gaussian process
(
Williams and Rasmussen 2006
)
. In particular,

Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}=\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}(x)(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\eta\alpha}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n},\alpha k(x,x^{\prime})-\alpha\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}(x)(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\eta\alpha}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{n}(x^{\prime}))\,.

Note that if we set the learning rate to
\eta=1/(2\sigma^{2})
, then
Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}
coincides with the Bayesian posterior for the model in (
1
). We consider the problem of regression with fixed design, which means that the inputs
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
are deterministic and our objective is to estimate the function values
f^{\star}(x_{1}),\dots,f^{\star}(x_{n})
. In this setting, we only need to consider the marginal distributions of
Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}
and
P_{\alpha}
on the inputs
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, which are
n
-dimensional Gaussians. By a small abuse of notation, we also use
Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}
and
P_{\alpha}
to refer to these marginal distributions. It is easy to check that
P_{\alpha}=\mathcal{N}(0,\alpha\mathbf{K}_{n})
and
Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_{n,\eta,\alpha},\mathbf{K}_{n,\eta,\alpha})
, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{n,\eta,\alpha}=\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\eta\alpha}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}\,,\quad\mathbf{K}_{n,\eta,\alpha}=\alpha\mathbf{K}_{n}-\alpha\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\eta\alpha}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}\,.

We assume that the noise variables
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}
are independent and
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian.

Assumption 1
.

The noise variables
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}
are independent. For all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{E}[\exp(\eta\varepsilon_{i})]\leq\exp(\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}/2)
.

For simplicity, we also assume that the inputs
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and the kernel
k
are such that the kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}_{n}
is strictly positive-definite. This assumption is quite mild, since it is satisfied by typical kernels whenever
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
are distinct. It is used to prove some of the auxiliary lemmas in Appendix
B.1
, but we expect that if one has the desire, this assumption can be dropped.

Assumption 2
.

The kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}_{n}
is strictly positive-definite.

In what follows, let us use
\mathbf{g}:=[g_{1},\dots,g_{n}]^{\top}
to denote an arbitrary vector (of function values) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We define the
excess risk

R_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and the
empirical risk

r_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
as

R_{n}(\mathbf{g}):=\frac{1}{n}\|\mathbf{g}-\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\,,\quad r_{n}(\mathbf{g}):=\frac{1}{n}\|\mathbf{g}-\mathbf{y}_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\,.

We want to upper bound the average excess risk
\int R_{n}(\mathbf{g})\mathrm{d}Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}(\mathbf{g})
of
Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}
. Using a well-known PAC-Bayesian bound for sums of independent random variables (cf. Proposition 2.1 in
Catoni and Giulini 2017
), one can derive the following PAC-Bayesian bound. For any
n\geq 1
, any
\delta\in(0,1]
, any
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}})
and any
\alpha>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\int R_{n}(\mathbf{g})\mathrm{d}Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}(\mathbf{g})\leq\inf_{Q}\left\{\frac{\int\eta(r_{n}(\mathbf{g})-r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}))\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})}{\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}}+\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||P_{\alpha})+\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}}{n(\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2})}\right\}\,.

(4)

However, this bound is not completely satisfactory. Lemma
13
tells us that the infimum on the right-hand side is achieved when
Q=Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}
. For this choice of
Q
, the KL divergence
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}||P_{\alpha})
contains the log-determinant term
\frac{1}{2}\log(\det(\alpha\mathbf{K}_{n})/\det(\mathbf{K}_{n,\eta,\alpha}))
. Due to Corollary
7
,

\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\det(\alpha\mathbf{K}_{n})}{\det(\mathbf{K}_{n,\eta,\alpha})}=\frac{1}{2}\log\det(2\eta\alpha\mathbf{K}_{n}+\mathbf{I})=\gamma_{n}(2\eta\alpha)\,.

Therefore, one would expect that the bound in (
4
) is at best of the order
\gamma_{n}(2\eta\alpha)/n
. In fact, one can show that this bound is of the order
[\gamma_{n}(2\eta\alpha)+\|f^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}/\alpha]/n
. Sadly, plugging in the bounds on the information gain from
Vakili et al. 2021
and then optimising the value of
\alpha
does not yield minimax-optimal rates of convergence. The extra (up to) logarithmic factor in the growth rate of
\gamma_{n}(\eta)
(compared to
d_{n}(\eta)
or
\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)
) results in unnecessary log factors in the rate of convergence. To fix this, we use Catoni’s location technique
(
Catoni 2007
)
, which allows one to prove PAC-Bayesian bounds with data-dependent priors. In Proposition
4
, the prior is the Gibbs distribution
Q_{n,\beta,\alpha}
, with a learning rate
\beta<\eta
.

Proposition 4
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
is satisfied. For any
n\geq 1
, any
\delta\in(0,1]
, any
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}})
, any
\beta>0
such that
\eta-2\eta^{2}\sigma^{2}-\beta-2\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}>0
and any
\alpha>0
, w.p. at least
1-\delta
,

\int R_{n}(\mathbf{g})\mathrm{d}Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}(\mathbf{g})\leq\inf_{Q}\left\{\frac{\int(\eta-\beta)(r_{n}(\mathbf{g})-r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}))\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})}{\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}}+\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{n,\beta,\alpha})+2\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}}{n(\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})}\right\}\,.

Using Lemma
13
again, it can be shown that the infimum on the right-hand side is still achieved when
Q=Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}
(cf. Appendix
B.3
). This time, the KL divergence
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}||Q_{n,\beta,\alpha})
contains the log-determinant term
\frac{1}{2}\log(\det(\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}))/\det(\mathbf{K}_{n,\eta,\alpha}))
. Using Corollary
7
again,

\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\det(\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}))}{\det(\mathbf{K}_{n,\eta,\alpha})}=\frac{1}{2}\log\det\frac{2\eta\alpha\mathbf{K}_{n}+\mathbf{I}}{2\beta\alpha\mathbf{K}_{n}+\mathbf{I}}=\gamma_{n}(2\eta\alpha,2\beta\alpha)\,.

One might expect that the bound in Proposition
4
is of the order
[\gamma_{n}(2\eta\alpha,2\beta\alpha)+\|f^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}/\alpha]/n
. We will see shortly that this is indeed the case. We sketch the main ideas of the proof of Proposition
4
here, and refer the interested reader to Appendix
B
. A key idea is to use a prior that assigns higher probability to functions (or vectors of function values) for which the excess risk is small. We define the distribution-dependent prior
\overline{Q}_{n,\beta,\alpha}
by

\frac{\mathrm{d}{\overline{Q}_{n,\beta,\alpha}}}{\mathrm{d}{P_{\alpha}}}(f)\propto\exp\bigg(-(\beta+2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(x_{i})-f^{\star}(x_{i}))^{2}\bigg)\,.

Note that
\overline{Q}_{n,\beta,\alpha}
depends on the distribution of
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
through
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and
f^{\star}
, but it does not depend on the random draw of
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
, and is therefore a valid prior. It is not important for the proof, but one can also notice that
\overline{Q}_{n,\beta,\alpha}
is another Gaussian process and that its marginal distribution at the inputs
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
is

\overline{Q}_{n,\beta,\alpha}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\alpha(\beta+2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n},\alpha\mathbf{K}_{n}-\alpha\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\alpha(\beta+2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n})\,.

In the bound we want to prove, the prior is
Q_{n,\beta,\alpha}
. It turns out that for all
Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the difference between
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||\overline{Q}_{n,\beta,\alpha})
and
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{n,\beta,\alpha})
can be upper bounded with high probability. This is made possible by the following concentration inequality, which is proved using Assumption
1
(cf. Proposition
20
). For any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
and any
\delta\in(0,1]
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{Q}\bigg\{\int\eta(r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n})-r_{n}(\mathbf{g}))+(\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2})R_{n}(\mathbf{g})\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||\overline{Q}_{n,\beta,\alpha})\bigg\}\leq\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}\,.

One can then show that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{n,\beta,\alpha})-\int\beta(r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n})-r_{n}(\mathbf{g}))+(\beta+2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})R_{n}(\mathbf{g})\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})\leq-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||\overline{Q}_{n,\beta,\alpha})+\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}\,.

Using the union bound, one can use both of these inequalities in succession to deduce that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sup_{Q}

\displaystyle\bigg\{\int(\eta-\beta)(r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n})-r_{n}(\mathbf{g}))+(\eta-2\sigma^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})R_{n}(\mathbf{g})\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{n,\beta,\alpha})\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{Q}\bigg\{\int\eta(r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n})-r_{n}(\mathbf{g}))+(\eta-2\sigma^{2})R_{n}(\mathbf{g})\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||\overline{Q}_{n,\beta,\alpha})\bigg\}+\log\tfrac{2}{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq 2\log\tfrac{2}{\delta}\,.

This inequality can be rearranged into the one in Proposition
4
. The
2\log(2/\delta)
term can be improved to
2\log(1/\delta)
by replacing the union bound with Cauchy-Schwarz (cf. Appendix
B.2
). Using Proposition
4
, we obtain the main result of this section.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
are satisfied. For any
n\geq 1
, any
\delta\in(0,1]
, any
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}})
, any
\beta>0
such that
\eta-2\eta^{2}\sigma^{2}-\beta-2\beta^{2}\sigma^{2}>0
and any
\alpha>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\int R_{n}(\mathbf{g})\mathrm{d}Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}(\mathbf{g})\leq\frac{2\gamma_{n}(2\eta\alpha,2\beta\alpha)+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|f^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+2\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}}{n(\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})}\,.

We prove Theorem
5
by evaluating the bound in Proposition
4
at a particular choice of
Q
, and then upper bounding each term by either
\gamma_{n}(2\eta\alpha,2\beta\alpha)
or
\|f^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}
. The gory details can be found in Appendix
B.4
. By Jensen’s inequality,
R_{n}(\mathbf{m}_{n,\eta,\alpha})\leq\int R_{n}(\mathbf{g})\mathrm{d}Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}(\mathbf{g})
. Since
\mathbf{m}_{n,\eta,\alpha}
is identical to the vector of fitted values for kernel ridge regression (with learning rate
2\eta\alpha
), the excess risk bound in Theorem
5
also applies to kernel ridge regression.

5
Rates of Convergence for Mercer Kernels

We provide worst-case bounds on the relative information gain, which, when combined with Theorem
5
, can be used to provide explicit rates of convergence for the excess risk of
Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}
.

5.1
Bounds on the Relative Information Gain

Aside from one or two tricks, the method with which we bound the relative information gain is identical to the method that
Vakili et al. 2021
used to bound the information gain. As a result, we require the same regularity assumptions. A positive-definite kernel
k
on a set
\mathcal{X}
is called a Mercer kernel if
\mathcal{X}
is a compact metric space and the kernel function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous. Due to Mercer’s theorem (cf. Appendix
C.1
), any Mercer kernel can be expressed as an infinite sum of the form

k(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\xi\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{i}(x^{\prime})\,,

where
(\xi_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
and
(\phi_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
are the (non-negative) eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the kernel.

Assumption 3
.

Assume that
k
is a Mercer kernel, and that
\sup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\|\phi_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\psi
.

The rate at which the eigenvalues
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots
of the kernel decay to zero determines the complexity of the corresponding RKHS. The two eigenvalue decay conditions studied by
Vakili et al. 2021
(and many others) are defined as follows.

Assumption 4
.

The polynomial eigenvalue decay condition is satisfied if there exist
C_{p}>0
and
\beta_{p}>1
such that

\xi_{i}\leq C_{p}i^{-\beta_{p}}.

The exponential eigenvalue decay condition is satisfied if there exist
C_{e_{1}},C_{e_{2}}>0
and
\beta_{e}\in(0,1]
such that

\xi_{i}\leq C_{e_{1}}\exp(-C_{e_{2}}i^{\beta_{e}}).

If a kernel is known to satisfy one of these eigenvalue decay conditions, then upper bounds on
\gamma_{n}(\eta)
with explicit dependence on
n
can be given (cf. Corollary 1 in
Vakili et al. 2021
). In particular, under the polynomial eigenvalue decay condition,
\gamma_{n}(\eta)=\mathcal{O}((n\eta)^{1/\beta_{p}}\log^{1-1/\beta_{p}}(n\eta))
. Under the exponential eigenvalue decay condition,
\gamma_{n}(\eta)=\mathcal{O}(\log^{1+1/\beta_{e}}(n\eta))
. A central idea in the method used by
Vakili et al. 2021
is to separate the kernel function into
k_{\parallel}(x,x^{\prime}):=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\xi_{i}\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{i}(x^{\prime})
and
k_{\perp}(x,x^{\prime}):=\sum_{D+1}^{\infty}\xi_{i}\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{i}(x^{\prime})
. It can be seen that
k_{\parallel}
is the reproducing kernel of the subspace of
\mathcal{H}
spanned by
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{D}
and that
k_{\parallel}(x,x^{\prime})
is the reproducing kernel of the subspace of
\mathcal{H}
which is orthogonal to
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{D}
. Let us write
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}
and
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\perp}
for the corresponding kernel matrices. We define

\delta_{D}:=\sum_{i=D+1}^{\infty}\xi_{i}\psi^{2}\,.

The first step is to re-write the information gain as a term depending on the rank
D
kernel matrix
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}
and another term depending on both
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}
and
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\perp}
.
Vakili et al. 2021
show that

\gamma_{n}(\eta)=\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}+\mathbf{I})+\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\mathbf{I}+\eta(\mathbf{I}+\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\perp})\,.

(5)

From here,
Vakili et al. 2021
show that the first term is bounded is by
\frac{1}{2}D\log(1+\frac{\overline{k}n\eta}{D})
, where
\overline{k}=\sup_{x}|k(x,x)|
, and that the second term is bounded by
\frac{1}{2}n\eta\delta_{D}
. One then has the upper bound

\gamma_{n}(\eta)\leq\frac{1}{2}D\log\bigg(1+\frac{\overline{k}n\eta}{D}\bigg)+\frac{1}{2}n\eta\delta_{D}\,.

The relative information gain satisfies a similar, but slightly tighter bound.

Proposition 6
.

For all
\eta>\beta>0
and any integer
D\geq 1
,

\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)\leq\frac{1}{2}D\log\frac{\eta}{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}n\eta\delta_{D}\,.

The full proof of Proposition
6
can be found in Appendix
C.3
. From (
5
), it follows that the relative information gain can be re-written as

\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\det(\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}+\mathbf{I})}{\det(\beta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}+\mathbf{I})}+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\det(\mathbf{I}+\eta(\mathbf{I}+\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\perp})}{\det(\mathbf{I}+\beta(\mathbf{I}+\beta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\perp})}\,.

Since
\log\det(\mathbf{I}+\beta(\mathbf{I}+\beta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\perp})\geq 0
(cf. Lemma
25
), the second term is still upper bounded by
\frac{1}{2}n\eta\delta_{D}
. In Lemma
24
, we show that the first term can be bounded by
\frac{1}{2}D\log\frac{\eta}{\beta}
, saving a factor of
\log(n)
. Using Proposition
6
, one can upper bound the relative information gain based on the spectral decay of the kernel via
\delta_{D}
.

Proposition 7
.

If the polynomial eigenvalue decay condition is satisfied, then

\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)\leq(n\eta C_{p}\psi^{2})^{1/\beta_{p}}\log^{1-1/\beta_{p}}(\tfrac{\eta}{\beta})+\log\tfrac{\eta}{\beta}\,.

If the exponential eigenvalue decay condition is satisfied, then

\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{C_{e,2}}\log(n\eta C_{\beta_{e}})\right)^{1/\beta_{e}}\log\frac{\eta}{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{e\eta}{\beta}\,,

where
C_{\beta_{e}}=\frac{C_{e,1}\psi^{2}}{C_{e,2}}
if
\beta_{e}=1
, and
C_{\beta_{e}}=\frac{2C_{e,1}\psi^{2}}{C_{e,2}\beta_{e}}(\frac{2-2\beta_{e}}{C_{e,2}\beta_{e}})^{1/\beta_{e}-1}\exp(\frac{1-\beta_{e}}{\beta_{e}})
if
\beta_{e}\in(0,1)
.

If
\beta\propto\eta
, then the bound for polynomial decay can be simplified to
\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)=\mathcal{O}((n\eta)^{1/\beta_{e}})
. In this case, the bound for exponential decay can be simplified to
\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)=\mathcal{O}(\log^{1/\beta_{e}}(n\eta))
. The proof is very similar to that of Corollary 1 in
Vakili et al. 2021
, and can be found in Appendix
C.4
.

5.2
Rates of Convergence

For the case of polynomial eigenvalue decay, by plugging the bound in Proposition
7
into Theorem
5
and then choosing suitable values of
\eta
and
\alpha
, we obtain the following rate of convergence.

Corollary 8
.

Suppose that the polynomial eigenvalue decay condition is satisfied,
\eta=\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}
and
\alpha=n^{-\frac{1}{1+\beta_{p}}}
. For any
\delta\in(0,1]
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\int R_{n}(\mathbf{g})\mathrm{d}Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}(\mathbf{g})=\mathcal{O}\bigg(n^{-\frac{\beta_{p}}{1+\beta_{p}}}\bigg)\,.

The proof can be found in Appendix
D.1
. The rate of convergence in Corollary
8
is minimax-optimal
(
Dicker et al. 2017
)
. For instance, for kernels satisfying the polynomial decay condition with
\beta_{p}=2q
, the unit ball of the corresponding RKHS
\mathcal{H}
is a Sobolev space of
q-1
times absolutely continuous and differentiable functions. In this case, we recover the standard rate of
n^{-\frac{2q}{1+2q}}
for nonparametric regression
(
Tsybakov 2009
)
. By tuning
\alpha
according to
\sigma
, we can also match the dependence on
\sigma
in the minimax lower bound for generalised Sobolev ellipsoids in Example 15.23 of
Wainwright 2019
. Note, however, that this example considers regression with random design rather than fixed design. For exponential decay, we obtain the following rate of convergence.

Corollary 9
.

Suppose that the exponential eigenvalue decay condition is satisfied,
\eta=\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}
and
\alpha=1
. For any
\delta\in(0,1]
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\int R_{n}(\mathbf{g})\mathrm{d}Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}(\mathbf{g})=\mathcal{O}\bigg(\frac{\log^{1/\beta_{e}}(n)}{n}\bigg)\,.

The proof can be found in Appendix
D.2
. This rate of convergence for exponential eigenvalue decay is also minimax-optimal
(
Dicker et al. 2017
)
.

6
Discussion

We have introduced a new quantity called the relative information gain, which measures the sensitivity of the information gain with respect to the variance of the noise in the responses. We demonstrated that the relative information gain arises naturally from the complexity term of a PAC-Bayesian excess risk bound for Gaussian process regression. Finally, we proved bounds on the relative information gain. When we combined these bounds with the excess risk bound in Theorem
5
, we recovered minimax-optimal rates of convergence.

There are several questions that could be investigated in future work. One could investigate whether the link between the effective dimension and the derivative of the information gain in Proposition
1
is a coincidence or a sign of some deep connection that has not yet been uncovered. Rather intriguingly, there is a somewhat similar result for Gaussian channels (cf. Chapter 9 in
Cover and Thomas 2006
), which states that the derivative of the mutual information with respect to the signal-to-noise ratio is equal to half the minimum mean-square error
(
Guo et al. 2005
)
. One could also investigate the extent to which the PAC-Bayesian bounds in this paper can be generalised to other regression models. For instance, one could consider regression with random design, misspecified regression or regression with sparse or other approximate Gaussian process predictors.

Q_{\mu,\beta}
by

\frac{\mathrm{d}{Q_{\mu,\beta}}}{\mathrm{d}{P}}(\mathbf{g})\propto\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{f}_{n},-\beta)\right)\,.

Note that while
Q_{\mu,\beta}
depends the unobserved quantities
\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g})
and
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},-\beta)
, it does not depend on the random draw of
(Z_{i}(\mathbf{g}))_{i\in[n],\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}
. We define the empirical approximation
Q_{Z,\beta}
of the localised prior by

\frac{\mathrm{d}{Q_{Z,\beta}}}{\mathrm{d}{P}}(\mathbf{g})\propto\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})\right)

In contrast to
Q_{\mu,\beta}
,
Q_{Z,\beta}
does depend on the random draw of
(Z_{i}(\mathbf{g}))_{i\in[n],\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, but does not depend on any unobservable quantities. The following proposition combines a well-known PAC-Bayesian bound for sums of independent random variables (cf. Proposition 2.1 in
Catoni and Giulini 2017
) with Catoni’s localisation technique (cf. Section 1.3.4 in
Catoni 2007
).

Proposition 20
.

For any
n\geq 1
, any
\delta\in(0,1]
, any
\eta>0
, any
\beta\in[0,\eta)
and any
P\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,
\forall Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\int\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\eta-\beta)(Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g}))-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\eta)-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},-\beta)\right]\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{\beta Z_{n}})\leq 2\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}\,.

The proof is a fairly straightforward combination of the proofs of Proposition 2.1 from
Catoni and Giulini 2017
and some of the derivations in Section 1.3.4 in
Catoni 2007
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary
Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We begin by finding a relationship between
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{\mu,\beta})
and
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{Z,\beta})
. From the definitions of
Q_{\mu,\beta}
and
Q_{Z,\beta}
, we obtain

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{\mu,\beta})

\displaystyle=\int\log\frac{\mathrm{d}{Q}}{\mathrm{d}{Q_{Z,\beta}}}(\mathbf{g})\frac{\mathrm{d}{Q_{Z,\beta}}}{\mathrm{d}{Q_{\mu,\beta}}}(\mathbf{g})\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{f}_{n})

(8)

\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{Z,\beta})

\displaystyle+\int\log\frac{\exp(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta Z_{i}(\mathbf{g}))\int\exp(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},-\beta))\mathrm{d}P(\mathbf{g})}{\exp(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},-\beta))\int\exp(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta Z_{i}(\mathbf{g}))\mathrm{d}P(\mathbf{g})}

\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{Z,\beta})+\int\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\beta\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g})+\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},-\beta)\right]\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})

\displaystyle+\log\frac{\int\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},-\beta)\right)\mathrm{d}P(\mathbf{g})}{\int\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})\right)\mathrm{d}P(\mathbf{g})}\,.

Using Lemma
13
, Tonelli’s theorem and independence, for any
\eta>0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\exp\left(\sup_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\left\{\int\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[\eta(Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g}))-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\eta)\big]\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{\mu,\beta})\right\}\right)\right]

(9)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta(Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g}))-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\eta)\right)\mathrm{d}Q_{\mu,\beta}(\mathbf{g})\right]

\displaystyle=\int\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta(Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g}))-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\eta)\right)\right]\mathrm{d}Q_{\mu,\beta}(\mathbf{g})

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\eta(Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g}))-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\eta)\right)\right]\mathrm{d}Q_{\mu,\beta}(\mathbf{g})

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\exp(\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\eta))}{\exp(\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\eta))}\mathrm{d}Q_{\mu,\beta}(\mathbf{g})=1\,.

Next, using Lemma
13
and Jensen’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\int\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},-\beta)\right)\mathrm{d}P(\mathbf{g})}{\int\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})\right)\mathrm{d}P(\mathbf{g})}\right]

(10)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\exp(\sup_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\{\int\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},-\beta)\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||P)\})}{\exp(\sup_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\{\int\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||P)\})}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int\sum_{i=1}^{n}-\beta(Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g}))-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},-\beta)\mathrm{d}Q_{\mu,\beta}(\mathbf{g})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}-\beta(Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g}))-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},-\beta)\right)\mathrm{d}Q_{\mu,\beta}(\mathbf{g})\right]=1\,.

The last step follows from (
9
) with
\eta=-\beta
. Using (
8
), then the Cathy-Schwarz inequality, and then (
9
) and (
10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\bigg[\exp\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\sup_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\bigg\{\int\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\eta-\beta)(Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g}))-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\eta)-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},-\beta)\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{Z,\beta})\bigg\}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\sup_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\bigg\{\int\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta(Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g}))-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\eta)\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{\mu,\beta})\bigg\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad~\times\exp\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\int\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},-\beta)\right)\mathrm{d}P(\mathbf{g})}{\int\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})\right)\mathrm{d}P(\mathbf{g})}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\exp\bigg(\sup_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\bigg\{\int\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta(Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g}))-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\eta)\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{\mu,\beta})\bigg\}\bigg)\bigg]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\times\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\int\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g})-\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},-\beta)\right)\mathrm{d}P(\mathbf{g})}{\int\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})\right)\mathrm{d}P(\mathbf{g})}\bigg]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 1\,.

The statement now follows from Markov’s inequality.
∎

Notice that if every occurence of
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\eta)
(and
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},-\beta)
) is replaced with an upper bound on
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\eta)
(or
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},-\beta)
), then the proof still goes through. This includes the occurences of
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},-\beta)
in the localised prior
Q_{\mu,\beta}
.

B.3
Proof of Proposition
4

{proofof}

Proposition
4

We set

Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})=(f^{\star}(x_{i})-y_{i})^{2}-(g_{i}-y_{i})^{2}=-(g_{i}-f^{\star}(x_{i}))^{2}+2(g_{i}-f^{\star}(x_{i}))\varepsilon_{i}\,.

With this choice of
Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})
,
\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g})=-(g_{i}-f^{\star}(x_{i}))^{2}
, and for all
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
,

\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\eta)=\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(2\eta(g_{i}-f^{\star}(x_{i}))\varepsilon_{i})]\leq 2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}(g_{i}-f^{\star}(x_{i}))^{2}\,.

(11)

In addition, we choose
P=P_{\alpha}
. From the definition of
Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})
,
Q_{Z,\beta}
is given by

\frac{\mathrm{d}{Q_{Z,\beta}}}{\mathrm{d}{P_{\alpha}}}(\mathbf{g})\propto\exp(n\beta(r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n})-r_{n}(\mathbf{g})))\propto\exp(-n\beta r_{n}(\mathbf{g}))\,.

Namely,
Q_{Z,\beta}=Q_{n,\beta,\alpha}
. Even though we will not need it, we can also determine the expression for the localised prior
Q_{\mu,\beta}
(or rather, for
\mathrm{d}Q_{\mu,\beta}/\mathrm{d}P
). From the bound in (
11
), it follows that
Q_{\mu,\beta}
is given by

\frac{\mathrm{d}{Q_{\mu,\beta}}}{\mathrm{d}{P_{\alpha}}}(\mathbf{g})\propto\exp(-n(\beta+2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})R_{n}(\mathbf{g}))\,.

One can notice that
Q_{\mu,\beta}
assigns higher density to vectors
\mathbf{g}
for which the excess risk is small, whereas
Q_{Z,\beta}
assigns higher density to vectors
\mathbf{g}
for which the empirical risk is small. Next, we substitute the definitions of/bounds on
Z_{i}(\mathbf{g})
,
\mu_{i}(\mathbf{g})
and
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{g},\eta)
into Proposition
20
. In particular, for any
n\geq 1
, any
\delta\in(0,1]
, any
\eta>\beta\geq 0
and any
P\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\int n(\eta-\beta)(r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n})-r_{n}(\mathbf{g}))+n(\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})R_{n}(\mathbf{g})\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{n,\beta,\alpha})\leq 2\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}\,.

If
\eta
and
\beta
are chosen such that
\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}>0
, then we can arrange this inequality to obtain

\int R_{n}(\mathbf{g})\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})\leq\frac{\int(\eta-\beta)(r_{n}(\mathbf{g})-r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}))\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})}{\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}}+\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{n,\beta,\alpha})+2\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}}{n(\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})}\,.

(12)

All that remains is to show that
Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}
minimises the right-hand side of this inequality w.r.t.
Q
. Since
r_{n}
is bounded below (by 0), Lemma
13
tells us that the infimum of the right-hand side is achieved when

\frac{\mathrm{d}{Q}}{\mathrm{d}{Q_{n,\beta,\alpha}}}(\mathbf{g})\propto\exp(-n(\eta-\beta)r_{n}(\mathbf{g}))\,.

For
Q=Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}{Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}}}{\mathrm{d}{Q_{n,\beta,\alpha}}}(\mathbf{g})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}{Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}}}{\mathrm{d}{P_{\alpha}}}(\mathbf{g})\frac{\mathrm{d}{P_{\alpha}}}{\mathrm{d}{Q_{n,\beta,\alpha}}}(\mathbf{g})

\displaystyle\propto\exp(-n\eta r_{n}(\mathbf{g}))\exp(n\beta r_{n}(\mathbf{g}))

\displaystyle\propto\exp(-n(\eta-\beta)r_{n}(\mathbf{g}))\,.

Therefore
Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}
is indeed a minimiser of the right-hand side of (
12
). Since (
12
) holds simultaneously for all
Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, under the same conditions as before, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\int R_{n}(\mathbf{g})\mathrm{d}Q_{n,\eta,\alpha}(\mathbf{g})\leq\inf_{Q}\left\{\frac{\int(\eta-\beta)(r_{n}(\mathbf{g})-r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}))\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})}{\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}}+\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{n,\beta,\alpha})+2\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}}{n(\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})}\right\}\,.

This concludes the proof.

B.4
Proof of Theorem
5

{proofof}

Theorem
5

We define the constrained least squares estimate

\widehat{f}:=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{f\in\mathcal{H},\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\|f^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}}}\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(x_{i})-y_{i})^{2}\bigg\}\,.

For some
\zeta\geq 0
, one can express
\widehat{f}
via

\widehat{f}(x)=\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}(x)(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}\,.

In addition, the vector of fitted values
\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}:=[\widehat{f}(x_{1}),\dots,\widehat{f}(x_{n})]^{\top}
is

\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}=\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}\,.

From the definition of
\widehat{f}
, we have
r_{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n})\leq r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n})
and

\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}=\|\widehat{f}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq\|f^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\,.

(13)

We want to find an upper bound for

\inf_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigg\{\frac{\int(\eta-\beta)(r_{n}(\mathbf{g})-r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}))\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})}{\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}}+\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{n,\beta,\alpha})+2\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}}{n(\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})}\bigg\}\,.

We consider two cases. First, suppose that
\frac{1}{2\beta\alpha}\leq\zeta
. We set
\widehat{Q}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha},\mathbf{K}_{n,\eta,\alpha})
. Since the empirical risk
r_{n}(\mathbf{f}_{n}^{(\zeta)})
of the
\mathbf{f}_{n}^{(\zeta)}:=\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}
is increasing in
\zeta
(cf. Lemma
14
), and since
\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha}=\mathbf{f}_{n}^{(1/(2\beta\alpha))}
, it follows that

r_{n}(\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha})\leq r_{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n})\leq r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n})\,.

Using this inequality, along with Corollary
18
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int(\eta-\beta)(r_{n}(\mathbf{g})-r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}))\mathrm{d}\widehat{Q}(\mathbf{g})

\displaystyle=(\eta-\beta)(r_{n}(\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha})-r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}))+\frac{\eta-\beta}{n}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{K}_{n,\eta,\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\gamma_{n}(2\eta\alpha,2\beta\alpha)\,.

Using Lemma
19
, Corollary
17
and the expression for the KL divergence between two Gaussians (with the same mean), we see that

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widehat{Q}||Q_{n,\beta,\alpha})=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha})-n+\log\frac{\det(\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha})}{\det(\mathbf{K}_{n,\eta,\alpha})}\bigg)\leq\gamma_{n}(2\eta\alpha,2\beta\alpha)\,.

We have shown that, for the case where
\frac{1}{2\beta\alpha}\leq\zeta
,

\displaystyle\inf_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\bigg\{\frac{\int(\eta-\beta)(r_{n}(\mathbf{g})-r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}))\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})}{\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}}+\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{n,\beta,\alpha})+2\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}}{n(\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\int(\eta-\beta)(r_{n}(\mathbf{g})-r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}))\mathrm{d}\widehat{Q}(\mathbf{g})}{\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}}+\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widehat{Q}||Q_{n,\beta,\alpha})+2\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}}{n(\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\gamma_{n}(2\eta\alpha,2\beta\alpha)+2\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}}{n(\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})}\,.

Next, we consider the second case. Suppose that
\frac{1}{2\alpha\beta}>\zeta
. We set
\widehat{Q}=\mathcal{N}(\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n},\mathbf{K}_{n,\eta,\alpha})
. Since
r_{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n})\leq r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n})
, Corollary
18
tells us that

\displaystyle\int(\eta-\beta)(r_{n}(\mathbf{g})-r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}))\mathrm{d}\widehat{Q}(\mathbf{g})

\displaystyle=(\eta-\beta)(r_{n}(\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n})-r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}))+\frac{\eta-\beta}{n}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{K}_{n,\eta,\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\gamma_{n}(2\eta\alpha,2\beta\alpha)\,.

With this choice of
\widehat{Q}
, the KL divergence
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widehat{Q}||Q_{n,\beta,\alpha})
is equal to

\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha})-n+(\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}-\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{-1}(\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}-\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha})+\log\frac{\det(\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha})}{\det(\mathbf{K}_{n,\eta,\alpha})}\bigg)\,.

As previously, the trace (minus
n
) and log-determinant terms can be bounded using Lemma
19
and Corollary
17
. After some slightly unpleasant calculation, one can show that the remaining quadratic term is upper bounded by the squared RKHS norm of
f^{\star}
. First, we expand the quadratic term, and obtain

(\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}-\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{-1}(\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}-\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha})=\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}-2\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha}+\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha}\,.

Using Corollary
7
and (
13
), we see that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}

\displaystyle=2\beta\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta\alpha}\mathbf{I})(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}

\displaystyle=2\beta\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I}+(\tfrac{1}{2\beta\alpha}-\zeta)\mathbf{I})(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}

\displaystyle=2\beta\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}\mathbf{y}_{n}+2\beta(\tfrac{1}{2\beta\alpha}-\zeta)\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}\mathbf{y}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\|f^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\,.

The second term in the quadratic expansion can be re-written using Corollary
7
again. In particular,

\displaystyle 2\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha}

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta\alpha}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}

\displaystyle=4\beta\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta\alpha}\mathbf{I})(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta\alpha}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}

\displaystyle=4\beta\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}\mathbf{y}_{n}\,.

The third term in the quadratic expansion can be re-written in the same way. In particular,

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta\alpha}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta\alpha}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}

\displaystyle=2\beta\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta\alpha}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta\alpha}\mathbf{I})(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta\alpha}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}

\displaystyle=2\beta\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta\alpha}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}\mathbf{y}_{n}\,.

We have now shown that

\displaystyle(\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}-\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{-1}(\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}-\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\|f^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+2\beta\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2\beta\alpha}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}\mathbf{y}_{n}

\displaystyle-2\beta\mathbf{y}_{n}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}\mathbf{y}_{n}\,.

Since
\frac{1}{2\beta\alpha}>\zeta
and the eigenvalues of
(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
are decreasing in
\zeta
, it follows that
(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\frac{1}{2\beta\alpha}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}\preccurlyeq(\mathbf{K}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}
. Therefore,

(\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}-\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{n,\beta,\alpha}^{-1}(\widehat{\mathbf{f}}_{n}-\mathbf{m}_{n,\beta,\alpha})\leq\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\|f^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\,.

For the second case where
\frac{1}{2\beta\alpha}\leq\zeta
, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{Q\in\Delta(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\bigg\{\frac{\int(\eta-\beta)(r_{n}(\mathbf{g})-r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}))\mathrm{d}Q(\mathbf{g})}{\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}}+\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q||Q_{n,\beta,\alpha})+2\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}}{n(\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\int(\eta-\beta)(r_{n}(\mathbf{g})-r_{n}(\mathbf{f}^{\star}_{n}))\mathrm{d}\widehat{Q}(\mathbf{g})}{\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2}}+\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widehat{Q}||Q_{n,\beta,\alpha})+2\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}}{n(\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\gamma_{n}(2\eta\alpha,2\beta\alpha)+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\|f^{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+2\log\tfrac{1}{\delta}}{n(\eta-2\sigma^{2}\eta^{2}-\beta-2\sigma^{2}\beta^{2})}\,.

This concludes the proof.

Appendix C
Bounds on the Relative Information Gain

C.1
Mercer Kernels

A positive-definite kernel
k
on a set
\mathcal{X}
is called a Mercer kernel if
\mathcal{X}
is a compact metric space and the kernel function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous. Mercer’s theorem provides a useful representation for Mercer kernels. Let
\rho
be a non-degenerate Borel measure on
\mathcal{X}
and let
L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\rho)
denote the set of square integrable functions on
\mathcal{X}
. Namely,

L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\rho):=\left\{f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}:\int_{\mathcal{X}}(f(x))^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho(x)<\infty\right\}\,.

Define the linear operator
L_{k}:L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\rho)\to L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\rho)
as

L_{k}(f)(x):=\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(x,y)f(y)\mathrm{d}\rho(y)\,.

Theorem 21

(Mercer’s Theorem)
.

If
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Mercer kernel, then there exist non-negative eigenvalues
\xi_{1}\geq\xi_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0
and corresponding eigenfunctions
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\dots
, such that

L_{k}(\phi_{m})=\xi_{m}\phi_{m},\quad\text{for all }m=1,2,\dots\,.

(14)

In addition, the kernel function has the eigendecomposition

k(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\xi_{m}\phi_{m}(x)\phi_{m}(x^{\prime})\,,

(15)

where the convergence of the infinite series is absolute for each
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and uniform on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
.

C.2
Auxiliary Lemmas

Lemma
22
and Lemma
23
were extracted from the proof of Theorem 3 in
Vakili et al. 2021
. Lemma
22
allows us to re-write the information gain as the sum of a term depending on only
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}
and another term depending on both
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}
and
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\perp}
.

Lemma 22
.

For any
\eta>0
and any kernel
k
that satisfies Assumption
3
,

\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}+\mathbf{I})=\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}+\mathbf{I})+\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\mathbf{I}+\eta(\mathbf{I}+\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\perp})\,.

Lemma
23
provides an upper bound on the second term in Lemma
22
.

Lemma 23
.

For any
\eta>0
and any kernel
k
that satisfies Assumption
3
,

\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\mathbf{I}+\eta(\mathbf{I}+\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\perp})\leq\frac{1}{2}n\eta\delta_{D}\,.

To bound the first term on the right-hand side of the inequality in Lemma
22
, we will consider the log-determinant of a
D\times D
gram matrix. We define a
D
-dimensional feature map
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{D}(x):=[\phi_{1}(x),\dots,\phi_{D}(x)]^{\top}
, an
n\times D
design matrix
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{n,D}:=[\boldsymbol{\phi}_{D}(x_{1}),\dots,\boldsymbol{\phi}_{D}(x_{n})]^{\top}
and a diagonal
D\times D
matrix
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{D}:=\mathrm{diag}(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{D})
. Notice that
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{n,D}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{D}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{n,D}^{\top}
. We define the gram matrix

\mathbf{G}_{n}:=\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{D}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{n,D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{n,D}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{D}^{1/2}\,.

Since we will deal with the relative information gain, we will need to bound the log of the ratio of the determinants of two of these gram matrices. This is handled by Lemma

Lemma 24
.

For any
n\geq 1
,
D\geq 1
and
\eta>\beta>0
,

\log\frac{\det(\eta\mathbf{G}_{n}+\mathbf{I})}{\det(\beta\mathbf{G}_{n}+\mathbf{I})}\leq D\log\frac{\eta}{\beta}\,.

Proof.

Let
(\lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{D}
be the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{G}_{n}
, which are all real and positive. We notice that for any
\zeta\in(0,1]
,

\det(\beta\mathbf{G}_{n}+\zeta\mathbf{I})=\prod_{i=1}^{D}(\beta\lambda_{i}+\zeta)\leq\prod_{i=1}^{D}(\beta\lambda_{i}+1)=\det(\beta\mathbf{G}_{n}+\mathbf{I})\,.

Therefore, we can bound the logarithm of the ratio of determinants as

\displaystyle\log\frac{\det(\eta\mathbf{G}_{n}+\mathbf{I})}{\det(\beta\mathbf{G}_{n}+\mathbf{I})}

\displaystyle=D\log\tfrac{\eta}{\beta}+\log\frac{\det(\beta\mathbf{G}_{n}+\tfrac{\beta}{\eta}\mathbf{I})}{\det(\beta\mathbf{G}_{n}+\mathbf{I})}

\displaystyle\leq D\log\tfrac{\eta}{\beta}+\log\frac{\det(\beta\mathbf{G}_{n}+\mathbf{I})}{\det(\beta\mathbf{G}_{n}+\mathbf{I})}

\displaystyle=D\log\tfrac{\eta}{\beta}\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

We will use the fact that the second term on the right-hand side of the inequality in Lemma
22
is non-negative.

Lemma 25
.

For any
\eta\geq 0
and any kernel
k
that satisfies Assumption
3
,

\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\mathbf{I}+\eta(\mathbf{I}+\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\perp})\geq 0\,.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{K}_{n}=\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}+\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\perp}
, and both
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}
and
\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\perp}
are positive semi-definite, it follows that

\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}+\mathbf{I}\succcurlyeq\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}+\mathbf{I}\,.

Due to monotonicity of the determinant with respect to the Loewner ordering (cf. Corollary 7.7.4 in
Horn and Johnson 2012
),

\log\det(\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}+\mathbf{I})\geq\log\det(\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}+\mathbf{I})\,.

Finally, using Lemma
22
,

\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\mathbf{I}+\eta(\mathbf{I}+\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\perp})=\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}+\mathbf{I})-\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}+\mathbf{I})\geq 0\,.

This concludes the proof.
∎

The next lemma comes from the proof of Corollary 1 in
Vakili et al. 2021
. It provides bounds on
\delta_{D}
under each of the eigenvalue decay assumptions.

Lemma 26
.

If the polynomial eigenvalue decay condition from Assumption
4
is satisfied, then

\delta_{D}\leq C_{p}D^{1-\beta_{p}}\psi^{2}\,.

If the exponential eigenvalue decay condition from Assumption
4
is satisfied and
\beta_{e}=1
, then

\delta_{D}\leq\frac{C_{e,1}\psi^{2}}{C_{e,2}}\exp(-C_{e,2}D)\,.

If the exponential eigenvalue decay condition from Assumption
4
is satisfied and
\beta_{e}\in(0,1)
, then

\delta_{D}\leq\frac{2C_{1,e}\psi^{2}}{C_{e,2}\beta_{e}}\bigg(\frac{2}{C_{e,2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{\beta_{e}}-1\bigg)\bigg)^{1/\beta_{e}-1}\exp\bigg(1-\frac{1}{\beta_{e}}\bigg)\exp\bigg(-C_{e,2}\frac{D^{\beta_{e}}}{2}\bigg)\,.

C.3
Proof of Proposition
6

{proofof}

Proposition
6

Using Lemma
22
and Lemma
25
, we can re-write and then upper bound the relative information gain as

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\det(\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}+\mathbf{I})}{\det(\beta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}+\mathbf{I})}+\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\mathbf{I}+\eta(\mathbf{I}+\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\perp})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\mathbf{I}+\beta(\mathbf{I}+\beta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\perp})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\det(\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}+\mathbf{I})}{\det(\beta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}+\mathbf{I})}+\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\mathbf{I}+\eta(\mathbf{I}+\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\perp})\,.

By the Weinstein–Aronszajn identity,

\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\det(\eta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}+\mathbf{I})}{\det(\beta\mathbf{K}_{n}^{\parallel}+\mathbf{I})}=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\det(\eta\mathbf{G}_{n}+\mathbf{I})}{\det(\beta\mathbf{G}_{n}+\mathbf{I})}\,.

Therefore, from Lemma
24
and Lemma
23
, it follows that

\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)\leq\frac{1}{2}D\log\frac{\eta}{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}n\eta\delta_{D}\,.

This concludes the proof.

C.4
Proof of Proposition
7

{proofof}

Proposition
7

From Proposition
6
,

\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)\leq\frac{1}{2}D\log\frac{\eta}{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}n\eta\delta_{D}\,.

We consider each eigenvalue decay condition separately. If the polynomial eigenvalue decay condition is satisfied, then Lemma
26
tells us that

\frac{1}{2}n\eta\delta_{D}\leq\frac{1}{2}n\eta C_{p}D^{1-\beta_{p}}\psi^{2}\,.

We choose the smallest value of
D
such that

n\eta C_{p}D^{1-\beta_{p}}\psi^{2}\leq D\log\frac{\eta}{\beta}\,.

One can verify that this inequality is satisfied if we choose

D=\big\lceil(n\eta C_{p}\psi^{2})^{1/\beta_{p}}\log^{-1/\beta_{p}}(\tfrac{\eta}{\beta})\big\rceil\,.

With this choice of
D
, the relative information gain satisfies

\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)\leq D\log\tfrac{\eta}{\beta}\leq(n\eta C_{p}\psi^{2})^{1/\beta_{p}}\log^{1-1/\beta_{p}}(\tfrac{\eta}{\beta})+\log\tfrac{\eta}{\beta}\,.

If the exponential eigenvalue decay condition is satisfied and
\beta_{e}=1
, then Lemma
26
tells us that

\frac{1}{2}n\eta\delta_{D}\leq\frac{1}{2}n\eta\frac{C_{e,1}\psi^{2}}{C_{e,2}}\exp(-C_{e,2}D)\,.

This time, we choose

D=\bigg\lceil\frac{1}{C_{e,2}}\log\frac{n\eta C_{e,1}\psi^{2}}{C_{e,2}}\bigg\rceil\,.

With this choice of
D
, the relative information gain satisfies

\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)\leq\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{C_{e,2}}\log\frac{n\eta C_{e,1}\psi^{2}}{C_{e,2}}\log\frac{\eta}{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{e\eta}{\beta}\,.

If the exponential eigenvalue decay condition is satisfied and
\beta_{e}=1
, then Lemma
26
tells us that

\frac{1}{2}n\eta\delta_{D}\leq\frac{1}{2}n\eta\frac{2C_{1,e}\psi^{2}}{C_{e,2}\beta_{e}}\bigg(\frac{2}{C_{e,2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{\beta_{e}}-1\bigg)\bigg)^{1/\beta_{e}-1}\exp\bigg(1-\frac{1}{\beta_{e}}\bigg)\exp\bigg(-C_{e,2}\frac{D^{\beta_{e}}}{2}\bigg)\,.

If we choose

D=\bigg\lceil\bigg(\frac{2}{C_{e,2}}\log\bigg(\frac{2n\eta C_{1,e}\psi^{2}}{C_{e,2}\beta_{e}}\bigg(\frac{2}{C_{e,2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{\beta_{e}}-1\bigg)\bigg)^{1/\beta_{e}-1}\exp\bigg(1-\frac{1}{\beta_{e}}\bigg)\bigg)\bigg)^{1/\beta_{e}}\bigg\rceil\,,

Then the relative information gain satisfies

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{C_{e,2}}\log\bigg(\frac{2n\eta C_{1,e}\psi^{2}}{C_{e,2}\beta_{e}}\bigg(\frac{2}{C_{e,2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{\beta_{e}}-1\bigg)\bigg)^{1/\beta_{e}-1}\exp\bigg(1-\frac{1}{\beta_{e}}\bigg)\bigg)\right)^{1/\beta_{e}}\log\frac{\eta}{\beta}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{e\eta}{\beta}\,.

Therefore, if the exponential decay condition is satisfied with
\beta_{e}\in(0,1]
, the relative information gain satisfies

\gamma_{n}(\eta,\beta)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{C_{e,2}}\log(n\eta C_{\beta_{e}})\right)^{1/\beta_{e}}\log\frac{\eta}{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{e\eta}{\beta}\,,

where
C_{\beta_{e}}=\frac{C_{e,1}\psi^{2}}{C_{e,2}}
if
\beta_{e}=1
, and
C_{\beta_{e}}=\frac{2C_{e,1}\psi^{2}}{C_{e,2}\beta_{e}}(\frac{2-2\beta_{e}}{C_{e,2}\beta_{e}})^{1/\beta_{e}-1}\exp(\frac{1-\beta_{e}}{\beta_{e}})
if
\beta_{e}\in(0,1)
.
