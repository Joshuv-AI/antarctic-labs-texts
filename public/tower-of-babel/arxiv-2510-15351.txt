A Novel Preconditioning Framework for Solving Nonlinear PDEs based on Fenchel-Rockafellar Duality and Transformed Primal-Dual Techniques Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Long Chen, Ruchi Guo, Jingrong Wei, Jun Zou

Abstract
A DualTPD method is proposed for solving nonlinear partial differential equations. The method is characterized by three main features. First, decoupling via Fenchel–Rockafellar duality is achieved, so that nonlinear terms are discretized by discontinuous finite element spaces, yielding block-diagonal mass matrices and closed-form updates. Second, improved convergence is obtained by applying transformed primal–dual (TPD) dynamics to the nonlinear saddle-point system, which yields strongly monotone behavior. Third, efficient preconditioners are designed for the elliptic-type Schur complement arising from the separated differential operators, and multigrid solvers are applied effectively. Extensive numerical experiments on elliptic p p -Laplacian and nonlinear H ⁡ ( curl ) H({\rm curl\,}) problems are presented, showing significant efficiency gains with global, mesh-independent convergence.

We consider the functional minimization problem

The proposed DualTPD algorithm (

) has three main features:

(i)

Decoupling via Fenchel–Rockafellar duality:
the duality framework separates the differential and nonlinear operators,
allowing each to be treated with specialized techniques.
With mixed FEMs, the nonlinear term can be discretized by discontinuous FE spaces,
leading to a nonlinear block-diagonal mass matrix, which allows closed-form Jacobians and inverses.

(ii)

Improved convergence via transformed primal–dual dynamics:

applying the TPD method to the nonlinear saddle-point system produces a dynamical system with better monotonicity properties, which improves convergence.

(iii)

Efficient preconditioners:

the separated differential operators yield an ill-posed, elliptic-type Schur complement.
With suitably designed multigrid-based preconditioners, this term can be treated efficiently.

The nonlinear saddle-point system is traditionally viewed as more difficult to solve. However, our DualTPD method can outperform solvers based on the primal formulation in the case of strong nonlinearity. Compared with the TPD methods developed in
[

We test our algorithm on two types of nonlinear equations: elliptic

2
Primal and Dual Formulations

In this section, we recall the basic settings of the primal and dual formulations for optimization problems at the abstract level and present applications to nonlinear PDEs. We assume
\mathcal{F}
is proper, differentiable, strictly convex, and
\mathcal{G}
is a bounded linear functional.

2.1
The Fenchel–Rockafellar Duality

Introduce a new variable
\gamma=Du
and reformulate the minimization problem (
1
) as the constrained optimization problem

\inf_{u\in\mathcal{V},\gamma\in\Sigma,\,\gamma=Du}\mathcal{I}(u,\gamma):=\mathcal{E}(u,\gamma).

(5)

With a Lagrange multiplier
\sigma
to enforce the constraint
\gamma=Du
,
(
5
) is equivalent to the min–max problem

\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma^{*}}\inf_{u\in\mathcal{V},\gamma\in\Sigma}\Big(\mathcal{F}(\gamma)+\mathcal{G}(u)+\left\langle\sigma,Du-\gamma\right\rangle\Big).

(6)

The convex conjugate (Legendre transform) of
\mathcal{F}

[
56
]
is

\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma):=\sup_{\gamma\in\Sigma}\big(\left\langle\sigma,\gamma\right\rangle-\mathcal{F}(\gamma)\big).

(7)

Since
\mathcal{F}
is strictly convex, the maximum in (
7
) is attained when

\sigma=\nabla\mathcal{F}(\gamma),\qquad\text{equivalently,}\qquad\gamma=\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma),

(8)

see
[
56
, Theorem 23.5]
. One can formally write
\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}=\nabla\mathcal{F}^{-1}
and
\nabla\mathcal{F}=(\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*})^{-1}
.

With the conjugate function, (
6
) is equivalent to the inf-sup problem (
4
),
where the Lagrange multiplier coincides with the dual variable
\sigma
in (
8
).
For a saddle point
(u,\sigma)
of (
6
),
the first-order optimality (KKT) conditions are

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma),\tau\right\rangle-\left\langle Du,\tau\right\rangle

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall~\tau\in\Sigma^{*},

(9a)

\displaystyle\left\langle\sigma,Dv\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla\mathcal{G}(u),v\right\rangle

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall~v\in\mathcal{V},

(9b)

which, from a physical perspective, corresponds to a Hamiltonian system when (
1
) represents a Lagrangian in mechanics
[
49
,
2
]
.

Moreover, using the identity
\left\langle D^{\top}u,\sigma\right\rangle:=\left\langle u,D\sigma\right\rangle
, we have

\begin{split}\inf_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\big(\mathcal{G}(u)+\left\langle\sigma,Du\right\rangle\big)&=-\sup_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\left(\left\langle-D^{\top}\sigma,u\right\rangle-\mathcal{G}(u)\right)\\
&=-\mathcal{G}^{*}(-D^{\top}\sigma),\end{split}

(10)

which yields the equivalence to (
3
) (top-right in
fig.

1
).
This process reflects the Fenchel–Rockafellar duality
[
56
]
.

In summary, the primal and dual formulations are equivalent, as illustrated in Figure
1
(top-left and bottom-right). We refer to (
1
) as the primal problem, whose optimality condition is the Euler–Lagrange equation (
2
), and to (
4
) as the dual problem, whose optimality condition is the KKT system (
9
). Our goal is to develop fast solvers for the nonlinear saddle-point system (
9
).

We close this section by summarizing the results regarding the well-posedness.
Results for the primal problem can be found in
[
34
,
33
]

Here, we focus on the dual problem and recall the argument from
[

]

Throughout the paper, we impose the following assumptions on
\mathcal{F}^{*}
and

Assumption 1
.

The function
\mathcal{F}^{*}
satisfies either global strong convexity with local Lipschitz continuity, or local strong convexity with global Hölder continuity in the Banach space
\Sigma^{*}
. That is, one of the following conditions holds:

(A1)

There exist

and constants
\mu,L>0
such that for all

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma_{1})-\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma_{2}),\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right\rangle\geq\mu\|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\|_{\Sigma^{*}}^{p},

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma_{1})-\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma_{2})\|_{\Sigma}\leq L(\|\sigma_{1}\|_{\Sigma^{*}}+\|\sigma_{2}\|_{\Sigma^{*}})^{p-2}\|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\|_{\Sigma^{*}}.

(A1
′
)

There exist

and constants

such that for all
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma^{*}

\displaystyle(\|\sigma_{1}\|_{\Sigma^{*}}+\|\sigma_{2}\|_{\Sigma^{*}})^{2-p}\left\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma_{1})-\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma_{2}),\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right\rangle\geq\mu\|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\|_{\Sigma^{*}}^{2},

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma_{1})-\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma_{2})\|_{\Sigma}\leq L\|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\|_{\Sigma^{*}}^{p-1}.

.

There are other (weaker) conditions one may consider; see, e.g.,
[

Assumption 3

The linear operator

\inf_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma^{*}\backslash\{0\}}\frac{\left\langle Dv,\sigma\right\rangle_{\Sigma}}{\|v\|_{\mathcal{V}}\,\|\sigma\|_{\Sigma^{*}}}\geq\beta,

(A2)

for some

Theorem 4
(
[

Let

be proper, differentiable, and convex. Under Assumptions

Remark 5

When

\displaystyle=0,~~~~~~\quad\forall~\tau\in[L^{p^{*}}(\Omega)]^{d},

\displaystyle\left\langle\sigma,\nabla v\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle f,v\right\rangle,\quad\forall~v\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega).

2.3
Discretization with Block-diagonal Mass Matrices

In this subsection, we present the discrete system of the dual formulation (in Hamiltonian mechanics) by finite element spaces and show that the nonlinearity leads to block-diagonal mass matrices.
This structure allows the nonlinear terms to be inverted analytically, which significantly simplifies implicit or Newton-type solvers.

The PDE problems are posed on an open domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

and modeled in Sobolev spaces
\Sigma
and
\mathcal{V}
.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}=\bigcup_{i}T_{i}
denote a shape-regular triangulation of
\Omega
.
Since no differential operator is applied to
\sigma
in (
9
),
the space
\Sigma^{*}
requires only minimal regularity and can be discretized by
L^{2}
-subspaces
\Sigma^{*}_{h}
,
for example discontinuous polynomial spaces on the mesh:

\Sigma^{*}_{h}=\{\sigma_{h}\in\Sigma:\;\sigma_{h}|_{T}\in[P_{k}(T)]^{d},~\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

By construction, Assumption
1
holds naturally on
\Sigma^{*}_{h}\subset\Sigma^{*}
.
As this space is fully broken, with no inter-element continuity,
the term
\left\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma),\tau\right\rangle
discretizes to a block-diagonal matrix,
so all nonlinear operations can be performed elementwise.
Moreover, as
\Sigma^{*}_{h}=\Sigma_{h}
in the discrete level, we shall simply use
\Sigma_{h}
in our discussion below.

The discretization space
\mathcal{V}_{h}
for
\mathcal{V}
is chosen so that
Dv_{h}
is well defined for
v_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
.
In particular, we take

\mathcal{V}_{h}:=\{v_{h}\in\mathcal{V}:\;Dv_{h}\in[P_{k}(T)]^{d}\},

(19)

which must satisfy the discrete inf–sup condition corresponding to Assumption
3
:

(A
2_{h}
)

\mathcal{V}_{h}
satisfies the inf–sup property

\inf_{v_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}\sup_{\sigma_{h}\in\Sigma^{*}_{h}\backslash\{0\}}\frac{\left\langle Dv_{h},\sigma_{h}\right\rangle}{\|v_{h}\|_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}\,\|\sigma_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}\geq\bar{\beta},

for some
\bar{\beta}>0
independent of the mesh size
h
.

With these discretization spaces, the discretized problem is to
find
\sigma_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}
and
u_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
satisfying

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma_{h}),\tau_{h}\right\rangle-\left\langle Du_{h},\tau_{h}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=0,~\quad\quad\quad\forall~\tau_{h}\in\Sigma_{h},

(20a)

\displaystyle\left\langle\sigma_{h},Dv_{h}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle g,v_{h}\right\rangle,\quad\forall~v_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h},

(20b)

where
\mathcal{G}(v):=-\left\langle g,v\right\rangle
. Under Assumptions (A1) (or (A1
′
)) and (A
2_{h}
),
the well-posedness of the discretized problem (
20
) is guaranteed.
We remark that, in order for
\mathcal{V}_{h}
to satisfy (A
2_{h}
) in the case of nonlinear Maxwell equations in
example

7
,
we shall employ Lagrange multiplier to impose
\operatorname{div}u=0
in a weak sense,
which results in a larger saddle point system;
see details in Section
5
.

The DualTPD Algorithm

In this section, we derive an algorithm for solving the saddle-point system (

3.1
Transformation and Preconditioning

For clarity, we rewrite the nonlinear saddle-point system (
9
) in matrix form:
find
(\sigma,u)\in\Sigma\times\mathcal{V}
such that

\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}&-D\\
D^{\top}&O\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma\\
u\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
g\end{pmatrix},

where
\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}\sigma:=\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma)
.

Given a linear operator
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}^{-1}
, we consider the transformation

\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}I&O\\
-D^{\top}\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix}}_{:=\mathcal{T}}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}&-D\\
D^{\top}&O\end{pmatrix}}_{:=\mathcal{A}}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma\\
u\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{T}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
g\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
g\end{pmatrix}.

(21)

Since
\mathcal{T}
is invertible, the solution of the transformed system (
21
) is equivalent to that of the saddle-point system (
9
).

In the linear case, when
\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma)=A
for a linear invertible operator
A
, choosing
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}^{-1}=A^{-1}
gives

\mathcal{T}\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}A&-D\\
O&S\end{pmatrix},

an upper triangular system where
S=D^{\top}A^{-1}D
is the Schur complement.
In practice, however,
A^{-1}
is not computable for general nonlinear problems.

With the transformation, we consider the preconditioned system:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{-1}\mathcal{A}:=\mathcal{U}^{-1}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}&-D\\
O&\mathcal{I}_{u}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}I&O\\
-D^{\top}\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}&-D\\
D^{\top}&O\end{pmatrix}

With the decomposition
\mathcal{B}^{-1}=\mathcal{U}^{-1}\mathcal{T}
, the computation only requires the block preconditioners
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}^{-1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{u}^{-1}
.
The dual formulation is crucial for the design of effective preconditioners
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}^{-1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{u}^{-1}
.

3.2
Transformed Primal–Dual Algorithm for Dual System

Based on the preconditioning, we propose the DualTPD algorithm for solving the saddle-point problem (
9
) or (
20
):

Algorithm 8
(DualTPD algorithm).

Given initial value
(\sigma_{0},u_{0})
, preconditioners
\{\mathcal{I}_{\sigma_{k}}^{-1}\}_{k\geq 0},\{\mathcal{I}_{u_{k}}^{-1}\}_{k\geq 0}
and step size
\alpha_{k}>0
, the sequence
\{(\sigma_{k},u_{k})\}_{k\geq 0}
is generated by

\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{k+1}\\
u_{k+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{k}\\
u_{k}\end{pmatrix}-\alpha_{k}\mathcal{B}_{k}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma_{k})-Du_{k}\\
D^{\top}\sigma_{k}-g\end{pmatrix},

(22)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{k}^{-1}:=\mathcal{U}_{k}^{-1}\mathcal{T}_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{I}_{\sigma_{k}}&-D\\
O&\mathcal{I}_{u_{k}}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}I&O\\
-D^{\top}\mathcal{I}_{\sigma_{k}}^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix}.

The iteration is most practical in residual–correction form:

Step 1:
Given
(\sigma_{k},u_{k})
, compute the residuals:

r_{k}^{\sigma}=\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma_{k})-Du_{k},\qquad r_{k}^{u}=D^{\top}\sigma_{k}-g.

Step 2:
Apply the transformation and preconditioners
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma_{k}}^{-1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{u_{k}}^{-1}
to compute the correction:

\delta u_{k}=\mathcal{I}_{u_{k}}^{-1}\big(r_{k}^{u}-D^{\top}\mathcal{I}_{\sigma_{k}}^{-1}r_{k}^{\sigma}\big),\qquad\delta\sigma_{k}=\mathcal{I}_{\sigma_{k}}^{-1}\big(r_{k}^{\sigma}+D\delta u_{k}\big).

Step 3:
Update
(\sigma_{k+1},u_{k+1})
with step size
\alpha_{k}>0
:

\sigma_{k+1}=\sigma_{k}-\alpha_{k}\delta\sigma_{k},\qquad u_{k+1}=u_{k}-\alpha_{k}\delta u_{k}.

Compared with the TPD algorithm in
[
24
,
25
]
, a key difference lies in the derivation of the saddle-point system (
9
) or (
20
) from the dual formulation of the PDEs. We demonstrate that the duality-based framework is essential for effective preconditioning and enhanced computational efficiency. In the primal formulation (
2
), the nonlinear operator and differential operator are inherently coupled, complicating the design of preconditioners capable of addressing both challenges simultaneously. As shown in
[
24
]
, the absence of effective preconditioning leads to mesh-dependent iteration counts, particularly in highly nonlinear regimes.

On the other hand, the dual formulation (
9
) or (
20
) introduces a new potential computational consideration: the evaluation of
\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma_{k})
. While general dual descent algorithms requires solving a nonlinear subproblem
[
7
,
66
]
, our framework enables efficient evaluation of
\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}
by leveraging the elementwise localization of nonlinearity within the discretized space
\Sigma_{h}
. Furthermore, the decoupling of the differential operator and the structure of
\Sigma_{h}
allow for the design of Jacobian-based preconditioners
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma_{k}}^{-1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{u_{k}}^{-1}
, and incorporating Newton-type solvers. By combining these techniques, our proposed algorithm the proposed algorithm achieves global and mesh-independent convergence for a broad class of nonlinear PDEs.

3.3
Connection to Other Algorithms

Despite being derived from the dual formulation, our algorithm applies to general saddle-point systems.
For linear saddle-point problems, triangular preconditioners were first introduced by Bramble and Pasciak
[
14
]

and have since been extensively developed; see
[
11
,
12
,
26
,
68
]
and the references therein.
The motivation comes from the row reduction of saddle-point systems, later formalized as a transformation and extended to nonlinear saddle-point problems
[
25
]
.
A key distinction between linear and nonlinear saddle-point systems lies in the choice of the relaxation parameter (step size):
for linear problems, it is determined by spectral analysis, while for nonlinear problems, it is guided by Lyapunov analysis. In
[
24
]
, we showed through Lyapunov analysis that TPD with variable preconditioners yields a globally convergent scheme.

For nonlinear saddle-point problems, diagonal preconditioners are often considered within the framework of primal–dual methods
[
23
,
67
]

or inexact Uzawa algorithms
[
28
,
60
,
27
,
44
,
45
,
46
]
.
A nonlinear preconditioner
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
for
\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}
can be used if the resolvent or its proximal operator has a closed form
[
23
,
60
,
67
]
,
or if the corresponding subproblem can be solved up to a prescribed accuracy
[
27
,
45
,
46
]
. The primal–dual hybrid gradient method (PDHG)
[
23
]
is effective when the proximal operators of the nonlinear terms are available, and it has been widely applied to imaging problems
[
62
,
59
,
35
]
. However, without effective preconditioners, PDHG often requires thousands of iterations to converge, especially for PDE problems with poor conditioning
[
19
,
29
]
.
Notice that
(\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*})^{-1}=\nabla\mathcal{F}
.
If we use
\sigma_{k}=\nabla\mathcal{F}(Du_{k})
and substitute this into the equation for
u
, the scheme reduces to gradient descent for the primal problem, which is typically slow without an appropriate preconditioner.
In this setting,
\mathcal{I}_{u}
should should serve as an effective preconditioner for the primal problem. Choosing a linear

\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}
corresponds to linearizing the nonlinearity, which often makes construction of
\mathcal{I}_{u}
substantially easier.

Augmented Lagrangian methods (ALM)
[
41
,
57
]
are also widely used for constrained optimization.
By adding an augmented term
\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}+\beta DD^{\top}
for
\beta>0
, the resulting system corresponds to the dual problem (
3
). One can use a scaled identity as the Schur complement preconditioner when
\beta\gg 1

[
25
]
.
In this case, large
\beta
alleviates the difficulty of designing a good
\mathcal{I}_{u}
.
However, constructing an efficient preconditioner for
\nabla\mathcal{F}^{*}+\beta DD^{\top}
with large
\beta
remains challenging.

In particular, if
\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}=\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}^{*}(\sigma_{k})
, the Hessian of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
at
\sigma_{k}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{u}=D^{\top}\mathcal{I}_{\sigma}^{-1}D
, with step size
\alpha_{k}=1
, method (
22
) reduces to Newton’s method.
It is well known that Newton’s method converges locally: quadratic convergence is achieved only when the initial guess is sufficiently close to the true solution, and there is no guarantee of global convergence. While Newton’s method is mainly considered for quasilinear or semilinear cases
[
53
,
54
]
, where the nonlinear term is essentially decoupled from the differential operator, we show that the dual formulation (
9
) and (
20
) decouple the nonlinear term from the differential operator.
This allows Newton-type preconditioners to be adapted either in explicit closed form or by solving simple algebraic equations after finite element discretization.
Most importantly, the computations are local: the associated matrices are elementwise independent, and the inverses involve only
d\times d
blocks in the domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

4
Application I:
p
-Laplacian Equations

In this section, we apply the triangularly preconditioned DualTPD algorithm to the
p
-Laplacian problem.
We refer readers to
example

6
for background.
We show that the design of preconditioners benefits from the dual formulation,
which, combined with duality and the block-diagonal structure, significantly accelerates convergence.

4.1
Discretization and Matrix Form

For computation, we discretize the system using conforming FE spaces and use elementwise constant elements for
\sigma\in\Sigma_{h}=V^{T}_{h}(\Omega)
and linear Lagrange elements with the corresponding boundary condition for
u\in\mathcal{V}_{h}=V^{n}_{h}(\Omega)
:

\displaystyle V^{n}_{h}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\{u\in C^{0}(\Omega):u|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\;u|_{T}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(T),\;\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},

\displaystyle V^{T}_{h}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\{\sigma\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{2}:\sigma|_{T}\in[\mathbb{P}_{0}(T)]^{2},\;\forall~T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

The dimensions of
V^{T}_{h}(\Omega)
and
V^{n}_{h}(\Omega)
are denoted by
N_{T}
and
N_{n}
, respectively.
By choosing bases of
\Sigma_{h}
and
\mathcal{V}_{h}
,
functions in these spaces can be identified with vectors.
We denote the following matrix representations of the related operators:

•

With a fixed weight
\nu
, define
{\bf M}^{\nu}_{\,{\rm d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\,{\rm d}}\times N_{\,{\rm d}}}
as the mass matrix on
V^{n}_{h}(\Omega)
or
V^{T}_{h}(\Omega)
, for
\,{\rm d}=n,T
.

•

The matrix associated with the weak divergence operator is
{\bf D}
.

•

For a fixed
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, define the coefficient
\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}):=|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|^{p^{*}-2}
.

The corresponding matrix form of (
20
) is to find vectors
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
and
{\bf u}
such that

\begin{pmatrix}{\bf M}_{T}^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}&-{\bf D}\\
{\bf D}^{\top}&\bf 0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\\
{\bf u}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
{\bf f}\end{pmatrix},

where for
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}|_{T}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the nonlinear term has the block-diagonal structure

{\bf M}_{T}^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}={\rm diag}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T_{i}})|T_{i}|&0\\
0&\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T_{i}})|T_{i}|\end{pmatrix}\right\}_{i=1}^{N_{T}},\quad{\bf M}_{T}^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\big(\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T_{i}})|T_{i}|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T_{i}}\big)_{i=1}^{N_{T}}.

4.2
Preconditioning

We now discuss the preconditioners
{\bf I}_{\sigma_{k}}^{-1}
and
{\bf I}_{u_{k}}^{-1}
in the algorithm (
22
).
For nonlinear problems, a natural choice for
{\bf I}_{\sigma_{k}}^{-1}
is the mass matrix with nonlinear coefficients.
In particular,
{\bf M}_{T}^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}
is a diagonal matrix for any given
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, and its inverse can be computed directly.
Note that
{\bf M}_{T}^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}
or its inverse becomes degenerate when
\boldsymbol{\sigma}|_{T}=\bf 0
for some
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
. Following
[
47
]
, we adopt the regularization

\gamma_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\begin{cases}\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})+\lambda&\quad p^{*}>2,\\
(\gamma|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|+\lambda)^{p^{*}-2},&\quad p^{*}<2\ \text{and}\ |\boldsymbol{\sigma}|\leq\epsilon_{0},\\
\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),&\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(23)

where
\lambda>0
is a regularization parameter to avoid singular matrix inversion,
and
\epsilon_{0}>0
is the threshold for applying regularization. With the regularized coefficient, we have the first candidate of preconditioner
{\bf I}_{\sigma_{k}}^{-1}=\left({\bf M}_{T}^{\gamma_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k})}\right)^{-1}
. This scheme is referred to as
DualTPD-M
.

Next, we consider a Newton-type preconditioner based on the Jacobian of the nonlinear term. We shall show the Jacobian of
{\bf M}_{T}^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}\boldsymbol{\sigma}
preserves the block structure.

{\bf J}({\bf M}_{T}^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}\boldsymbol{\sigma})={\rm diag}\{{\bf J}_{T_{i}}\}_{i},

(24)

where
{\bf J}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is given by

\displaystyle{\bf J}_{T}

\displaystyle=\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T})|T|{\bf I}_{2}+|T|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}\nabla\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T})^{\top}

\displaystyle=|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}|^{p^{*}-2}|T|{\bf I}_{2}+(p^{*}-2)|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}|^{p^{*}-4}|T|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}^{\top}.

When
|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}|\neq 0
, this special form yields an obvious advantage that its inverse has a closed form by using the Sherman–Morrison formula:

{\bf J}_{T}^{-1}=\frac{|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}|^{2-p^{*}}}{|T|}{\bf I}_{2}-\frac{(p^{*}-2)|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}|^{-p^{*}}}{(p^{*}-1)|T|}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}^{\top},

which shows the inverse of Jacobian (
24
):

[{\bf J}({\bf M}_{T}^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}\boldsymbol{\sigma})]^{-1}={\rm diag}\{{\bf J}_{T_{i}}^{-1}\}_{i}.

As with
\gamma^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T})
and
|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}|^{-1}
, the inverse
{\bf J}_{T}^{-1}
is only applied when both are greater than the threshold
\epsilon_{0}
.
More precisely, our second preconditioner is defined as follows: for a given
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,
{\bf I}_{\sigma}^{-1}={\rm diag}\{{\bf I}_{T_{i}}^{-1}\}_{i=1}^{N_{T}}
where
{\bf I}_{T}^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is defined as

{\bf I}_{T}^{-1}=\begin{cases}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T})|T|&0\\
0&\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T})|T|\end{pmatrix}^{-1},&\quad\text{ if }p^{*}>2,\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T})\leq\epsilon_{0}\text{ or }p^{*}<2,|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}|\leq\epsilon_{0},\\[6.0pt]
{\bf J}_{T}^{-1},&\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(25)

This scheme is referred to as
DualTPD-J
.

With such
{\bf I}_{\sigma_{k}}^{-1}
, the corresponding Schur complement is

{\bf S}_{k}={\bf D}^{\top}{\bf I}_{\sigma_{k}}^{-1}{\bf D},

which is a variable Laplacian matrix with tensor coefficients.
We employ a geometric multigrid V-cycle as the preconditioner, i.e.,

{\bf I}_{\sigma_{k}}^{-1}={\rm MG}({\bf S}_{k},{\rm tol}_{\rm mg},{\rm maxIt}),

where the number of V-cycles is bounded by maxIt or the relative residual is reduced below
{\rm tol}_{\rm mg}
.

If
\alpha_{k}=1
and
{\bf I}_{\sigma_{k}}^{-1}={\bf S}_{k}^{-1}
, the method reduces to Newton’s iteration.

Application II: Nonlinear Maxwell Equations

In this section, we study the dual formulation of nonlinear Maxwell equations.
With suitable preconditioners, we demonstrate that the DualTPD algorithm (
22
) can be effectively applied to solve these problems.

5.1
Augmented Lagrangian formulation

Based on (
18
),
to enforce the divergence-free condition
\operatorname{div}u=0
,
we introduce a Lagrange multiplier
\phi
.
To facilitate the construction of suitable preconditioners,
we adopt the augmented Lagrangian technique by adding the term
\left\langle\phi,\xi\right\rangle
, noting that the solution remains unchanged since
\phi=0
.
Thus, we consider the following modified dual problem, obtained from (
18
): find
\sigma\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{3}
,
\phi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, and
u\in{\bf H}_{0}({\rm curl\,};\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle\left\langle\tfrac{1}{\nu(\Phi^{-1}(|\sigma|))}\sigma,\tau\right\rangle-\left\langle{\rm curl\,}u,\tau\right\rangle

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall~\tau\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{3},

(26a)

\displaystyle\beta\left\langle\phi,\xi\right\rangle-\left\langle u,\nabla\xi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall~\xi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(26b)

\displaystyle\left\langle\sigma,{\rm curl\,}v\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla\phi,v\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathcal{J},v\right\rangle,\quad\forall~v\in{\bf H}_{0}({\rm curl\,};\Omega),

(26c)

where
\beta>0
is a parameter to be specified later.

Although (
26
) involves three variables,
it still fits into the general saddle-point structure (
9
) if
(\sigma,\phi)
is treated as a single variable,
with
\mathcal{F}^{*}
and
D
modified accordingly.
Since
\sigma
and
\phi
are decoupled, and
\phi
enters only through linear terms,
the extension of the algorithm (
22
) and the discussion of preconditioners carry over directly.

5.2
Discretization and Matrix Form

For computation, we use element-wise constant elements for
\sigma
, linear Lagrange elements for
\phi
, and lowest-order Nédélec edge elements for
u
:

\displaystyle V^{T}_{h}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\{\sigma\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{3}:\;\sigma|_{T}\in[\mathbb{P}_{0}(T)]^{3},\;\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},

\displaystyle V^{n}_{h}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\{\phi\in C^{0}(\Omega):\;\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\;\phi|_{T}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(T),\;\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},

\displaystyle V^{e}_{h}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\{u\in{\bf H}_{0}({\rm curl\,};\Omega):\;u|_{K}=a+b\times x,\;a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\;\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}.

With these spaces, the discretized weak form of (
26
) is:
find
(\sigma_{h},\phi_{h},u_{h})\in V^{T}_{h}(\Omega)\times V^{n}_{h}(\Omega)\times V^{e}_{h}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle\left\langle\tfrac{1}{\nu(\Phi^{-1}(|\sigma_{h}|))}\sigma_{h},\tau_{h}\right\rangle-\left\langle{\rm curl\,}u_{h},\tau_{h}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall\tau_{h}\in V^{T}_{h}(\Omega),

(27)

\displaystyle\beta\left\langle\phi_{h},\xi_{h}\right\rangle+\left\langle u_{h},\nabla\xi_{h}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=0,\quad\forall\xi_{h}\in V^{n}_{h}(\Omega),

\displaystyle\left\langle\sigma_{h},{\rm curl\,}v_{h}\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla\phi_{h},v_{h}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle g,v_{h}\right\rangle,\quad\forall v_{h}\in V^{e}_{h}(\Omega).

By selecting
\mathcal{V}_{h}=V^{e}_{h}(\Omega)\cap\{\left\langle v_{h},\nabla p_{h}\right\rangle=0,~\forall p_{h}\in V^{n}_{h}(\Omega)\}
and
\Sigma_{h}=V^{T}_{h}(\Omega)
, one can verify that (
20
) and (
27
) are equivalent.
However, formulation (
27
) is more favorable in computation, as it directly enforces the divergence-free condition and facilitates the design of preconditioners (see the next subsection).

Choosing bases for these spaces identifies all functions with vectors.
Denote the dimensions of
V^{T}_{h}(\Omega)
,
V^{n}_{h}(\Omega)
, and
V^{e}_{h}(\Omega)
by
N_{T}
,
N_{n}
, and
N_{e}
, respectively.
The associated matrix operators are defined as follows:

•

For a weight function
\nu
, let
{\bf M}^{\nu}_{\,{\rm d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\,{\rm d}}\times N_{\,{\rm d}}}
denote the mass matrix on
V^{n}_{h}(\Omega)
,
V^{e}_{h}(\Omega)
, or
V^{T}_{h}(\Omega)
for
\,{\rm d}=n,e,T
.
In particular, if
\nu=1
,
{\bf M}_{\,{\rm d}}
is the standard mass matrix.

•

Let
{\bf G}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{T}}
be the grad matrix,
let
{\bf C}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{e}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N_{T}}
be the curl matrix,
and let
{\bf D}={\bf G}^{\top}{\bf M}_{e}
be the matrix associated with the weak divergence operator.
Denote the matrix
\tilde{\bf C}={\bf M}_{T}{\bf C}
.

•

For a fixed
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, define
\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}):=1/\nu(\Phi^{-1}(|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|))
.

With these definitions, the nonlinear saddle-point system takes the matrix form

\begin{pmatrix}{\bf M}_{T}^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}&\bf 0&-\tilde{\bf C}\\
\bf 0&{\bf M}_{n}^{\beta}&-{\bf D}\\
\tilde{\bf C}^{\top}&{\bf D}^{\top}&\bf 0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\\
\boldsymbol{\phi}\\
{\bf u}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}\\
{\bf J}\end{pmatrix},

(28)

where for
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}|_{T}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the nonlinear term has the block-diagonal structure

{\bf M}_{T}^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}={\rm diag}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T_{i}})|T_{i}|&&\\
&\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T_{i}})|T_{i}|&\\
&&\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T_{i}})|T_{i}|\end{pmatrix}\right\}_{i=1}^{N_{t}},\quad

and

{\bf M}_{T}^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\big(\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T_{i}})|T_{i}|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T_{i}}\big)_{i=1}^{N_{t}}.

5.3
Preconditioning

In this case, we need to construct the preconditioners for the three variables:
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,
\boldsymbol{\phi}
and
{\bf u}
.
First, for
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, since it is piecewise constant, the Jacobian
{\bf J}({\bf M}_{T}^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}\boldsymbol{\sigma})
is block diagonal with
3\times 3
blocks on each element. We denote
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}|_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Direct computation using
\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=1/(\nu(\Phi^{-1}(|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|))
gives that

{\bf J}({\bf M}_{T}^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}\boldsymbol{\sigma})={\rm diag}\{{\bf J}_{T_{i}}\}_{i},

where
{\bf J}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is given by

\displaystyle{\bf J}_{T}

\displaystyle=\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T})|T|{\bf I}_{3}+|T|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}\nabla\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T})^{\top}=\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T})|T|{\bf I}_{3}-t(\boldsymbol{\sigma})|T|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{T}^{\top},

where

t(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\frac{\nu^{\prime}(\Phi^{-1}(|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|))}{\nu^{2}(\Phi^{-1}(|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|)|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|}(\Phi^{-1})^{\prime}(|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|).

With the inverse function theorem and
\Phi(x)=\nu(x)x
, we have

(\Phi^{-1})^{\prime}(|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|)=\frac{1}{\Phi^{\prime}(z)}=\frac{1}{\nu^{\prime}(z)z+\nu(z)},

where
z=\Phi^{-1}(|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|)
. Notice
\Phi(\cdot)
is a smooth function, we can apply Newton’s method on scalar problems to solve for
z
and the coefficient

t(\boldsymbol{\sigma},z)=\frac{\nu^{\prime}(z)}{\Phi^{\prime}(z)\nu^{2}(z)|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|}.

Using Woodbury matrix identity, we can compute

{\bf J}_{T}^{-1}=\frac{\nu(z)}{|T|}{\bf I}_{3}-\frac{t(\boldsymbol{\sigma},z)\nu^{2}(z)}{(t(\boldsymbol{\sigma},z)\nu(z)|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|^{2}-1)|T|}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}

and the preconditioner for
\boldsymbol{\sigma}

{\bf I}_{\sigma}^{-1}=\big({\bf J}({\bf M}_{T}^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}\boldsymbol{\sigma})\big)^{-1}={\rm diag}\{{\bf J}_{T_{i}}^{-1}\}_{i=1}^{N_{t}}.

For the multiplier variable
\boldsymbol{\phi}
, we can apply the mass lumping so that
{\bf M}_{n}
becomes diagonal, and simply take
{\bf I}_{\phi}^{-1}=({\bf M}^{\beta}_{n})^{-1}
.

Next, for
{\bf u}
, we consider the Schur complement

{\bf S}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\tilde{\bf C}^{\top}\big({\bf J}({\bf M}_{T}^{\gamma(\boldsymbol{\sigma})}\boldsymbol{\sigma})\big)^{-1}\tilde{\bf C}+{\bf D}^{\top}({\bf M}_{n}^{\beta})^{-1}{\bf D}.

For any given
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, the first term is a
{\rm curl\,}
–
{\rm curl\,}
matrix with block-diagonal tensor coefficients, and the second term is a
\operatorname{div}
–
\operatorname{div}
matrix.
We use the preconditioned conjugate gradient (PCG) method and denote the preconditioner by

{\bf I}_{u}^{-1}:=\text{PCG}({\bf S}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}),{\rm tol}_{\rm pcg}),

where
{\rm tol}_{\rm pcg}
is the tolerance for terminating the PCG iterations.
For the inner preconditioner within the PCG, we adopt a multigrid method with a modified Hiptmair–Xu preconditioner
[
43
]
.
Specifically, we use the Hiptmair–Xu decomposition with variational auxiliary matrices
[
48
]
to approximate
({\bf S}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}))^{-1}
:

({\bf S}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}))^{-1}\approx{\bf R}+{\bf G}\mathcal{B}_{\rm grad}{\bf G}^{\top}+\boldsymbol{\Pi}\mathcal{B}_{v}\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\top},

where
{\bf R}
is a Jacobi smoother,
\boldsymbol{\Pi}
is the Nédélec interpolation operator for vector functions,
and
\mathcal{B}_{\rm grad}
and
\mathcal{B}_{v}
correspond to multigrid V-cycles for
{\bf G}^{\top}{\bf S}{\bf G}
and
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\top}{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\Pi}
, respectively.
In particular since
{\bf C}{\bf G}=\bf 0
, we have

{\bf G}^{\top}{\bf S}{\bf G}={\bf G}^{\top}{\bf D}^{\top}({\bf M}_{n}^{\beta})^{-1}{\bf D}{\bf G}=({\bf G}^{\top}{\bf M}_{e}{\bf G})^{\top}({\bf M}_{n}^{\beta})^{-1}({\bf G}^{\top}{\bf M}_{e}{\bf G}),

which represents a fourth-order Laplacian operator, independent of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
and unchanged through iterations,
and
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\top}{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\Pi}:{\bf H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\to{\bf H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
is basically a Hodge Laplacian with a
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
-dependent coefficient.
For
{\bf G}^{\top}{\bf S}{\bf G}
, we employ a standard geometric multigrid methods for Laplacians as its preconditioner,
while for
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{\top}{\bf S}\boldsymbol{\Pi}
,
we exploit the multigrid methods for Hodge Laplacians.

6
Conclusion

We have proposed a DualTPD algorithm for nonlinear PDEs based on the Fenchel–Rockafellar duality and transformed primal-dual techniques.
The dual formulation decouples nonlinear terms from differential operators and enables efficient local treatment of nonlinearities and effective preconditioner design.
Applications to
p
-Laplacian and nonlinear Maxwell equations, including
p
-curl problems, have confirmed the efficiency and robustness of the approach.
In both cases, the proposed methods achieves mesh-independent convergence, optimal CPU scaling, and often outperformed primal-based methods.

Future work includes refining preconditioners for strongly nonlinear regimes, incorporating adaptive strategies for step-size and preconditioner updates, and extending the framework to coupled multiphysics systems.
Overall, the DualTPD algorithm provides a flexible and efficient tool for solving a broad class of nonlinear saddle-point problems.
