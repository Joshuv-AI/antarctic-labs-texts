Association between ward structural changes and coercive interventions in psychiatric emergency wards in Japan: a retrospective study
Authors: Takaaki Hirooka, Satoru Oishi, Shoko Miura, Ken Inada
Published: 2026

Background

In psychiatric emergency wards, seclusion and physical restraint (PR) are sometimes necessary to ensure the safety of patients and staff, particularly for individuals at risk of self-harm, harm to others, or severe agitation. However, patients do not always perceive these coercive interventions as therapeutically beneficial [1]. They are associated with adverse outcomes, including traumatic experiences, secondary psychological disorders [2], impaired therapeutic alliance, and reduced treatment adherence [3]. A recent systematic review in adult psychiatry reported that seclusion and PR are associated with deleterious physical and psychological consequences, including non-negligible post-traumatic stress symptoms [4]. Minimizing the use of coercive measures is thus a clinical and ethical imperative, as these interventions directly infringe upon patient rights and dignity. Additionally, physical complications such as venous thromboembolism, aspiration pneumonia, asphyxiation, and ileus have also been reported [5], further underscoring the urgent need for their reduction.

Despite widespread awareness of ethical implications, some countries continue to report increasing trends in coercive interventions. In Japan, the use of seclusion and PR has grown since 2003; by 2016, seclusion cases had doubled, and PR cases had increased approximately 1.4-fold [6]. International data further indicate that Japan has relatively high rates of coercive interventions per capita [7, 8]. A nationwide survey in 1999 identified six key strategies to reduce coercion in psychiatric inpatient care; among these was increasing the availability of private rooms [9]. Recent studies suggest that lower patient density and access to private or communal spaces may decrease behavioural disturbances [10–14]. However, few studies have directly examined the relationship between ward design and the use of seclusion or PR [15, 16]. Accordingly, identifying modifiable determinants of coercive practices remains a priority for improving everyday care in acute psychiatric settings.

One potentially modifiable determinant is the ward environment and architectural design. A rapid systematic review suggested that physical design features of mental health facilities may influence the use of seclusion and restraint [17], and a multicentre study reported that greater private space, better comfort, and improved ward visibility were associated with a lower risk of seclusion [18]. Conceptually, environmental stressors such as crowding, noise, and lack of privacy, as well as poor visibility or delayed staff response, may contribute to agitation and behavioural escalation, whereas environments that support privacy, comfort, and timely observation may facilitate earlier and less restrictive interventions. However, the effectiveness of specific structural features—including private rooms, closed-circuit television (CCTV), and staff station configuration—remains uncertain. Because architectural changes often involve multiple simultaneous elements, and because evidence for video surveillance in psychiatric settings remains limited and ethically complex, observational studies may find it difficult to disentangle the effects of individual components [17, 19].

In April 2020, Hospital A’s psychiatric emergency ward was relocated to Hospital B. Although the total number of beds decreased slightly (from 46 to 42), staffing levels were maintained, and the new ward incorporated significant architectural modifications. These included increasing non-seclusion private rooms from 56.5% to 100%, expanding closed-circuit television (CCTV) units from 9 to 21, and relocating the nursing station from a peripheral to a central position. This study aimed to investigate whether these structural changes were associated with differences in the frequency and duration of seclusion and PR.

Methods

Participants

A total of 823 patients were admitted to the psychiatric emergency ward during the study period: 377 to Hospital A (April 1, 2018 to March 31, 2020) and 446 to Hospital B (April 1, 2020 to March 31, 2022). After excluding seven patients admitted for maintenance electroconvulsive therapy or respite care, 816 patients were included in the final analysis. These admissions were excluded because they are generally short-term, are not primarily intended for the management of acute behavioural disturbance, and rarely involve seclusion or PR.

Facility characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the architectural and staffing features of the psychiatric emergency wards. Total bed count decreased slightly from 46 to 42, while the number of seclusion rooms remained at seven. Private non-seclusion rooms increased from 19 (56.5%) to 35 (100%). Nursing station layouts followed Yokota et al.’s typology [16]: Hospital A used a corner-type layout, while Hospital B adopted a central-type layout. Closed-circuit television (CCTV) cameras increased from 9 to 21 (Fig. 1). The mean annual number of full-time psychiatrists increased from 10.0 in Hospital A to 12.5 in Hospital B during each 2-year study period. However, psychiatrists at Hospital B also covered consultation-liaison psychiatry. Each hospital employed four designated psychiatrists, and decisions regarding seclusion and PR were made by designated psychiatrists. The indication criteria and decision-making process did not differ between the two sites. Nurse and psychiatric social worker staffing remained unchanged.

Data collection and outcome measures

Baseline characteristics were extracted from medical records, including sex, age, type of admission (voluntary vs. involuntary), time of admission (in-hours vs. out-of-hours), Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) score, psychiatric diagnosis (ICD-10), treatment history, and presence of physical comorbidities. Seclusion and PR use during hospitalization were analysed at the patient (admission) level as binary outcomes, indicating whether each patient experienced seclusion or PR at least once during admission. For patients who underwent these interventions, duration-related outcomes were analysed at the episode level. Total intervention time, defined as the interval from initiation to termination, was obtained for each episode, with periods of partial release subtracted to calculate net duration. The number of days from partial release to final termination was also recorded for each episode. Because some patients experienced multiple coercive episodes, patient ID was used as a random effect in the generalized linear mixed models to account for within-patient clustering. Psychotropic medication dosages at the time of coercion initiation were documented: antipsychotics were converted to chlorpromazine equivalents [20], antidepressants were converted to imipramine equivalents [21], and mood stabilizers were reported as lithium or valproate doses.

Seclusion was defined as confinement alone in a locked room without voluntary exit; PR referred to mechanical restraint using cotton belts. Definitions followed the Japanese Mental Health and Welfare Law. Seclusion and PR are generally not applied simultaneously in psychiatric practice, and they were not used concurrently at either study site as a matter of institutional practice. Both interventions were often lifted intermittently for monitoring.

Statistical analysis

Patients were grouped by hospital (A vs. B). Demographic variables, coercive measures, and medication doses were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric data and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Logistic regression (forced-entry method) was used to assess factors associated with coercive interventions, adjusting for hospital, sex, age, admission type and time, GAF score, psychiatric diagnosis, treatment history, and physical comorbidities. Intervention durations were analysed using generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs): gamma distribution (log-link) for seclusion time, log-normal for restraint time, and negative binomial for days from partial release to termination. Patient identification (ID) was included as a random effect; fixed effects included all covariates and psychotropic doses at the time of coercion initiation. Multicollinearity was assessed via variance inflation factors (VIFs).

All analyses were conducted using JMP Pro version 18.0.2 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Two-tailed tests were used, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Hospital B had higher proportions of F1 (substance-related disorders), G40 (epilepsy), and patients with previous psychiatric treatment. No significant differences were found in sex, age, admission type and time, GAF scores, or physical comorbidities.

Characteristics of patients subjected to behavioural restrictions

Seclusion

Seclusion was applied to 158 patients in Hospital A and 155 in Hospital B. Although the absolute difference was small, it was statistically significant (Table 2).

Physical restraint

PR was used for 101 patients in Hospital A and 94 in Hospital B, a statistically significant reduction (Table 2).

In both groups, most patients were involuntarily admitted and had an F2 diagnosis. Restrained patients in Hospital B were significantly older (52.2 ± 19.3 vs. 45.8 ± 15.7 years, p < 0.05), but other characteristics did not differ (Additional file 2). At PR initiation, medication doses were similar, except for significantly lower valproic acid doses in Hospital B (Table 3).

Logistic regression showed significantly lower odds of PR in Hospital B (OR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.46–0.92; Table 4).

Frequency and duration of coercive interventions

Seclusion

Hospital A recorded 189 seclusion events and Hospital B recorded 188. The mean seclusion duration was significantly shorter in Hospital B (184.5 h) compared to Hospital A (268.2 h). Similarly, the mean number of days from initiation of partial release to seclusion termination was shorter in Hospital B (4.6 vs. 8.1 days; Table 3).

A GLMM with a gamma distribution and log link showed significantly shorter seclusion durations in Hospital B (β = − 0.281, standard error [SE] = 0.112, 95% CI: − 0.500 to − 0.061, p < 0.05; Table 5), corresponding to a 24% reduction (exp(–0.281) ≈ 0.76). Model fit was acceptable (χ²/df = 1.021), and patient ID was included as a random effect. Another GLMM with a negative binomial distribution showed a significantly shorter interval from partial release to seclusion termination in Hospital B (β = − 0.386, SE = 0.117, 95% CI: − 0.616 to − 0.157, p < 0.01; Table 6), indicating a 32% reduction (exp(–0.386) ≈ 0.68). Model fit was good (χ²/df = 0.97).

Physical restraint

Hospital A had 130 PR events and Hospital B had 111, a statistically significant reduction. However, no significant differences were found in mean PR duration (434.6 vs. 235.6 h) or days from partial release to termination (4.2 vs. 3.6 days; Table 3).

A GLMM with a log-normal distribution showed no significant difference in PR duration (β = − 0.120, SE = 0.217, 95% CI: − 0.546 to 0.308, p = 0.583; Table 5; χ²/df = 1.51). Similarly, a negative binomial GLMM showed no significant difference in days from partial release to PR termination (β = −0.293, SE = 0.185, 95% CI: −0.658 to 0.071, p = 0.114; Table 6; χ²/df = 0.99). All models accounted for repeated measures using patient ID as a random effect.

Multicollinearity assessment again showed no concerns, with all VIFs below 2.

Discussion

Association between ward structure and seclusion practices in a psychiatric emergency ward

This study found that, even after adjusting for patient characteristics (sex, age, diagnosis, and admission type), Hospital B had significantly fewer seclusion events, shorter seclusion durations, and quicker transitions from partial release to termination compared to Hospital A. These differences remained significant in generalized linear mixed models, suggesting that ward design may influence seclusion practices.

Although few in number, Japanese studies have suggested that higher proportions of private rooms may be associated with reduced seclusion use [14, 15]. One single-site study observed fewer seclusion cases after increasing private room availability [14], while a multicentre study reported less frequent and less aggressive seclusion initiation in wards with more non-seclusion private rooms [15]. In our study, Hospital A had a private room rate of 56.5%, below national averages (58.9% and 68.5%) [15, 22]. This limited availability may have constrained options for patient observation or transitions from seclusion. After relocation, Hospital B achieved 100% private room occupancy, likely contributing to reduced seclusion and smoother post-seclusion transitions. This aligns with research suggesting that private rooms reduce environmental stressors, promote emotional security, and support recovery [23, 24]. They are also commonly used during reintegration for patients needing prolonged seclusion, reinforcing our findings [25].

International literature links several environmental features to reduced seclusion risk, including more private space per patient (OR = 0.88), adjustable temperature and lighting (OR = 0.77), and improved ward visibility (OR = 0.69). In this study, the average private room size in Hospital B was 18 m² larger than the reported average of 13.6 m² and included adjustable temperature settings, likely enhancing comfort [18]. The relocation also improved ward visibility, supporting better patient monitoring. These changes align with existing evidence that ward design influences seclusion practices.

The impact of natural environments remains uncertain. Some studies associate outdoor gardens with increased seclusion risk (OR = 9.09) [18], while others suggest that access to natural elements such as courtyards supports emotional stability and behavioural regulation [26, 27]. These conflicting findings underscore the need for further research to clarify how natural environments influence seclusion use.

CCTV coverage at Hospital B increased post-relocation. While some studies suggest CCTV may reduce patient violence, these effects typically are not statistically significant [28], and a systematic review has not found consistent benefits for self-harm, aggression, or care quality [29]. Notably, no studies have directly examined CCTV’s impact on seclusion rates, indicating a gap in the evidence.

In summary, architectural and environmental changes—particularly increased private rooms, enhanced comfort, and better visibility—may reduce seclusion. However, the effects of natural elements and surveillance technologies remain unclear and warrant further investigation through multicentre, longitudinal studies.

Association between ward design and physical restraint practices and antipsychotic medication prescriptions in acute psychiatric settings

After adjusting for clinical background variables, multivariate analyses showed that both the number of patients subjected to PR and the total restraint episodes were significantly lower at Hospital B. This aligns with previous research identifying factors linked to restraint use, such as male sex, psychiatric history, and diagnoses like F3–F9 [30]. Univariate studies have also associated restraint with older age, after-hours admission, involuntary hospitalization, and comorbid physical conditions [31]. A systematic review has similarly noted predictors such as male sex, younger age, involuntary admission, and schizophrenia spectrum diagnoses [32]. In this study, aside from minor differences in age distribution, the clinical characteristics between hospitals—sex, diagnosis, treatment history, admission type and timing, and comorbidities—were largely comparable. The lower restraint use at Hospital B is thus plausibly linked to differences in ward design.

Before relocation, Hospital A had limited visibility from the staff station, which was up to 54 m from patient rooms. After relocation, Hospital B’s centrally located station shortened staff-to-patient distances, likely improving response times. A previous study suggested that central or dispersed staff stations reduce walking distances and enable quicker, less intrusive interventions, potentially preventing escalation to PR [33]. Although Japanese studies are limited, some support a link between staff station layout and reduced restraint incidence.

However, no significant difference was found between hospitals in PR duration or in the time from partial release to restraint termination. Research examining the association between ward design and PR duration is limited, and one study has suggested that features such as staff station location and the proportion of private rooms may have little influence on PR duration [16]. A recent systematic review of psychiatric ward design and patient outcomes found no studies that examined restraint duration as a primary outcome [34]. Similarly, a review of surveillance technologies, including CCTV, found no evidence linking camera presence to restraint duration or supporting monitoring systems as effective in reducing it [27].

These findings suggest that while architectural features—such as centralized staff stations, more private rooms, and increased CCTV—may reduce the incidence of PR, they have little impact on its duration. This highlights the likely greater importance of clinical and interpersonal factors, such as staff judgment, communication, and therapeutic approach. Future research should explore these influences through multicentre, longitudinal studies assessing both environmental and human factors affecting restraint duration.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective, single-centre observational study, its findings may not be generalizable. The lack of randomization also introduces potential sampling bias and unmeasured confounding, warranting cautious interpretation.

Second, although the two hospital populations were broadly comparable and we adjusted for patient characteristics in the analyses, differences in diagnostic profiles, treatment histories, staffing, and bed capacity may have influenced outcomes. Third, data on staff experience, quality of interdisciplinary care, and non-pharmacological interventions were unavailable, limiting assessment of their impact.

Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths. Prior research on ward design and coercive interventions has often overlooked patient-level clinical factors, risking confounding. In contrast, we adjusted for key variables—sex, age, diagnosis, admission type, and treatment history—allowing a more robust assessment of the association between ward design and the use and duration of seclusion and PR. By also analysing time from partial release to termination, we introduced a clinically meaningful outcome. These findings may inform future psychiatric emergency ward planning to reduce coercion and support patient-centred care.

Conclusion

This study examined how changes in the physical environment of psychiatric emergency wards were associated with the use of seclusion and PR. By comparing data before and after a ward relocation within the same hospital, we found that relocation to a facility with more private rooms, a centrally located staff station, and enhanced CCTV monitoring was associated with significant reductions in the number of patients subjected to seclusion and PR, as well as in the total number of such incidents. Seclusion duration and the time from partial release to termination were also significantly shorter after relocation.

These results suggest that ward design may contribute to reducing the frequency and duration of restrictive interventions and may help inform future efforts to reduce coercive practices and improve acute psychiatric care.
