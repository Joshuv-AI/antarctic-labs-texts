Efficacy and safety of tranexamic acid during bariatric surgery: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of 10 comparative studies
Authors: Ahmed Abu-Zaid, Monirah Khaled Alotaibi, Fajer N. Hasan, Hadeel A. Alkandari, Sara Almutawtah, Bader N. Almutawaa, Mohammad Salem Alajmi, Fahad N. Aladwani, Lulwah A Alselahy, Fatemah A. Ali, Sarah Saqer Alblooshi, Hessa Mohammed Alfayadh, Abdullah M. Alharran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimThis study aimed to conduct an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of comparative studies evaluating the efficacy and safety of tranexamic acid (TXA) during bariatric surgery.MethodsSix databases were searched up to September 2, 2025. The primary endpoint was the rate of postoperative bleeding. Secondary endpoints included transfusion, hematoma, reoperation, thromboembolic events, operative time, and hospital stay. Outcomes were pooled as risk ratios (RR) or mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) using a random-effects model.ResultsTen studies (11 treatment arms; 2,137 patients, mostly undergoing laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy) were included. TXA, usually given as a single intravenous dose at induction, was associated with a lower risk of postoperative bleeding (9 studies; RR 0.57, 95% CI 0.36–0.88), although this effect was not robust in leave-one-out sensitivity analyses. No significant differences were observed for blood transfusion, hematoma, or reoperation. TXA modestly reduced operative time and length of stay, but these differences were not clinically important. No thromboembolic events were reported in TXA-treated patients, and overall risk of bias was judged as low to moderate across most studies.ConclusionTXA may reduce postoperative bleeding, but the effect was not robust in sensitivity analyses; though further high-quality RCTs are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03442-2.

Introduction

According to the most recent Global Burden of Disease study in 2021, approximately 1 billion adult men and 1.11 billion adult women worldwide are living with overweight and obesity, underscoring a critical global health crisis [1]. Bariatric surgery has emerged as one of the most effective and durable treatment strategies for managing severe obesity and its related comorbidities, particularly when lifestyle and pharmacological interventions prove insufficient [2, 3].

As a result, the number of bariatric procedures performed worldwide has increased significantly in recent years, according to a recent report from the International Federation for the Surgery of Obesity and Metabolic Disorders (IFSO) Global Registry [4]. Among the various surgical options available [5], laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG) and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass have become two of the most commonly performed techniques worldwide, due to their relative technical simplicity, shorter operative time, and favorable outcomes in weight reduction and metabolic improvement [6, 7].

Despite the clinical benefits, bariatric surgery carries inherent risks, with perioperative bleeding being a notable complication happening in up to 4% of the cases [8, 9]. Significant intraoperative and postoperative bleeding can lead to adverse outcomes including hemodynamic instability, need for transfusion, reoperation, extended hospital stay, and increased healthcare costs. Therefore, strategies to minimize bleeding during bariatric procedures are of significant clinical interest.

Pharmacological interventions (systemic or topical) are commonly employed to reduce surgical bleeding [10, 11], and among these, tranexamic acid (TXA) has gained attention. TXA is a synthetic lysine analogue that exerts its antifibrinolytic effect by reversibly binding to plasminogen, thereby inhibiting the breakdown of fibrin clots. It has a favorable pharmacokinetic profile, with high oral and intravenous bioavailability, renal excretion, and a plasma half-life of approximately two hours [12]. TXA has been widely used to reduce bleeding across various medical and surgical specialties [13–15], with a well-established safety profile with no significant increase in thromboembolic events [16].

In recent years, several studies have investigated the use of TXA in bariatric surgery, aiming to assess its potential in minimizing blood loss and improving surgical outcomes. Although recent meta-analyses by Mocanu et al., Al-Juhani et al., and Lech et al. [17–19], have already examined TXA in bariatric surgery and suggested a modest reduction in perioperative bleeding with uncertain clinical relevance, they generally included only four to six trials and did not consistently perform subgroup, sensitivity, publication bias, or GRADE-based certainty assessments. Moreover, newer studies (n = 4) with larger sample sizes and more rigorous methodologies have become available [20–23], highlighting the need to update the evidence base to provide a more accurate and reliable synthesis of TXA’s safety and efficacy in this context.

Accordingly, the aim of this study is to conduct an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of 10 studies evaluating the use of TXA in bariatric surgery, designed to extend rather than overturn the conclusions of these earlier reviews. By incorporating recent evidence and performing detailed statistical and methodological assessments, this review seeks to provide the most robust and up-to-date analysis to inform clinical decision-making regarding TXA use in bariatric procedures.

Methods

Study protocol

We conducted this research in accordance with the guidelines outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [24] and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [25]. The research protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with the registration code CRD420251147648. As the study involved only the synthesis of published literature, ethical approval was not required.

Eligibility criteria

We included studies that addressed the following PICOS-based research question: (i) the population comprised patients undergoing bariatric surgery, including laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG), Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), gastric banding, biliopancreatic diversion with duodenal switch (BPD/DS), intragastric balloon placement, or endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty; (ii) the intervention involved the administration of TXA; (iii) the control group received either a matched placebo or no treatment; (iv) the studies reported at least one of the primary or secondary outcomes; and (v) the selected studies employed a comparative interventional design, including both randomized and non-randomized controlled trials. We excluded studies that combined bariatric surgery with other surgical procedures, those in which the intervention group received TXA alongside other active agents, studies that did not reliably report the primary outcome, and non-original publications such as abstracts, reviews, or conference proceedings.

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive literature search across five databases—PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Embase, and Google Scholar—from their inception until September 2, 2025. The search in Google Scholar was limited to the first 100 citations. No filters were applied during the search in terms of patient age, publication date, study location, or language. The general search strategy included the following terms: (“tranexamic acid” OR AMCA OR cyklokapron OR ugurol OR transamin OR amchafibrin OR anvitoff OR spotof OR exacyl) AND (sleeve OR gastrectomy OR bariatric OR “stomach stapling” OR “weight loss surgery” OR “metabolic surgery”). The complete and exact search strategy is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Study selection

After retrieving citations from the databases, we exported them into an Excel spreadsheet and removed duplicates. Titles and abstracts were then screened to exclude irrelevant studies. The full texts of the remaining articles were retrieved and assessed against the predefined eligibility criteria. To ensure comprehensive coverage, we also screened the reference lists of included studies as well as those of recent systematic reviews. Two authors independently conducted the study selection process, any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third author.

Data extraction

We collected data in two main categories. The first category included baseline characteristics of the included studies, such as the study identifier (defined as the last name of the first author and year of publication), study design, type of bariatric surgery performed, patient age, body mass index (BMI), gender distribution, intervention arms, sample size of each arm, and specific details of the interventions. The second category encompassed primary and secondary endpoints. The primary endpoint was the rate of postoperative bleeding. Secondary endpoints included the rate of perioperative blood transfusion, rate of postoperative hematoma, rate of reoperation, rate of postoperative thromboembolic events, mean operative time, and mean length of hospital stay. Supplementary Table 2 shows the study-level definitions of postoperative bleeding. Data were collected using a pre-formatted extraction template. Two pairs of authors independently extracted the data, and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus within each pair.

Assessment of study quality

We assessed the quality of the included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) [26]. Each domain was rated as having a low risk, some concerns, or a high risk of bias, followed by an overall risk of bias judgment for the study. For non-randomized controlled studies, we used the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool [27], with each domain rated as low, moderate, serious, or critical risk of bias, or as having no information. An overall risk of bias judgment was then assigned to each study. Two authors independently conducted the quality assessments, and any conflicts were resolved through consultation with a third author.

Assessment of certainty of evidence

For each outcome, we evaluated the certainty of evidence using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [28]. We assessed five domains (risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias) and rated the overall certainty of evidence as very low, low, moderate, or high.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

One RCT [29] administered TXA either intraoperatively or postoperatively to the experimental group, and each arm was treated as a separate RCT in the meta-analysis. The intraoperative TXA arm was labeled as Sermet et al., 2023-I, while the postoperative TXA arm was labeled as Sermet et al., 2023-P. We conducted meta-analyses using a random-effects model (DerSimonian–Laird method) for outcomes that were pooled from at least three studies [30]. We summarized continuous outcomes using mean difference (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and binary outcomes as risk ratios (RR) with 95% CI. We assessed between-study heterogeneity using the I² statistic and the p-value from the chi-square test, considering heterogeneity significant when I² > 50% and p < 0.1. We generated forest plots to visualize the effect sizes of individual studies as well as the overall pooled effect. We performed a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis to test the robustness of the results by sequentially removing one study at a time and recalculating the pooled effect size based on the remaining studies. We assessed publication bias for outcomes with at least 10 studies, evaluating it qualitatively by inspecting funnel plot asymmetry and quantitatively using Egger’s regression test, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. We conducted a subgroup analysis when applicable (at least 2 studies in each subgroup) based on study design, comparing RCTs to non-RCTs for the primary outcome. For all analyses, we considered statistical significance at p < 0.05. We performed all statistical analyses using STATA version 19 for Windows.

Results

Summary of literature search and study selection

Figure 1 shows the PRISMA flow diagram. A total of 409 records were identified through database searches, of which 176 duplicates were removed. The remaining 233 records were screened, and 222 were excluded based on titles and abstracts. Eleven full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, with one excluded for lacking a control group. Finally, 10 studies with 11 arms were included in the review [20–23, 29, 31–35].

Summary of the included studies and participants

Table 1 presents a summary of the baseline characteristics of the included studies. A total of 11 arms were analyzed, comprising 5 RCTs and 6 non-RCTs. These studies were conducted across seven countries, including Turkey (n = 3), the United Kingdom (n = 2), Poland (n = 2), Kuwait (n = 1), Brazil (n = 1), Israel (n = 1), and the Netherlands (n = 1). The majority of studies (9 out of 11) focused exclusively on LSG procedures, while one study included mixed bariatric surgeries such as LSG, one-anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB), and RYGB, and another combined LSG with omentopexy. Regarding control groups, six studies compared TXA administration to no TXA, while five used placebo controls. TXA was predominantly administered as a single intravenous dose during anesthesia induction or immediately before surgery, with doses ranging from 10 mg/kg to 1.5 g, often delivered as a bolus or short infusion. Follow-up periods varied between 1 and 6 months, with some studies reporting shorter or unspecified durations.

Summary of the quality assessment

Figure 2 presents a summary of the quality assessment of the included RCTs and non-RCTs. Among the RCTs, the overall risk of bias was rated as low in two studies [29, 34], with some concerns in one study [23], and high in one study [31]. In Alhomoud (2016) study [31], randomization based on odd and even patient numbers was predictable and likely introduced selection bias; furthermore, the absence of trial registration increased the risk of selective reporting in Domain 5. In Bieniaszewski et al. (2025) study [23], block randomization with alternating weekly assignments could have introduced predictability, resulting in Domain 1 being rated as having some concerns. For the non-RCTs, the overall risk of bias was judged as low in four studies [20, 21, 33, 35] and as having some concerns in two studies [22, 32]. For the latter two studies [22, 32], Domain 2 was assessed as having a moderate risk of bias, as patients were assigned by the surgeon rather than randomized, raising the possibility of systematic differences in surgical approach related to the operating surgeon.

Meta-analysis of the primary endpoint

The rate of postoperative bleeding was significantly lower in the TXA group compared with the non-TXA group (n = 9 arms, RR = 0.57, 95% CI [0.36, 0.88], p = 0.01), with no evidence of heterogeneity (I² = 0%, p = 0.53). Subgroup analysis by study design showed that postoperative bleeding remained significantly lower in the TXA group for RCTs (n = 3, RR = 0.49, 95% CI [0.26, 0.91], p = 0.03), but not for non-RCTs (n = 6 arms, RR = 0.44, 95% CI [0.15, 1.23], p = 0.12) (Fig. 3). By timing of administration, giving the drug during induction was associated with a larger and statistically significant reduction (RR = 0.34, 95% CI [0.15–0.79]; p = 0.01), whereas administration at the end of surgery was not (RR = 0.69, 95% CI [0.41–1.16; p = 0.16), although the test for subgroup differences was not significant (p = 0.33) (Supplementary Fig. 1). By dose, 1 g was associated with a significant effect (RR = 0.46, 95% CI [0.25–0.84; p = 0.011), while > 1 g was not (RR = 0.84, 95% CI [0.43–1.64; p = 0.610), with no subgroup differences (p = 0.19) (Supplementary Fig. 2). By surgery type, the effect was significant in LSG procedures (RR = 0.60, 95% [CI 0.38–0.94; p = 0.025) but highly imprecise and non-significant in LSG and other procedures (RR = 0.14, 95% CI [0–4.99; p = 0.28); again, subgroup differences were not significant (p = 0.78) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Meta-analysis of secondary endpoints

There was no significant difference between the TXA and control groups in terms of perioperative blood transfusion rates (n = 9 arms, RR = 0.60, 95% CI [0.28, 1.27], p = 0.18), hematoma (n = 6 arms, RR = 0.46, 95% CI [0.13, 1.63], p = 0.23), or reoperation (n = 5 arms, RR = 0.69, 95% CI [0.33, 1.43], p = 0.31). All pooled analyses were homogeneous (I² = 0%, p > 0.1) (Fig. 4).

Fig. 4Meta-analysis of the rates of: A perioperative blood transfusion, B hematoma, and C reoperation

The mean operative time (n = 8 arms, MD = − 3.48 min, 95% CI [–5.65, − 1.30], p < 0.001) and mean hospital stay (n = 5 arms, MD = − 0.11 days, 95% CI [–0.15, − 0.07], p < 0.001) were significantly shorter in the TXA group compared with the control group (Fig. 5). The pooled analysis was heterogeneous for operative time (I² = 74.63%, p < 0.001), and the Galbraith plot identified the study by Chakravartty et al., 2016 [32], as a potential outlier (Supplementary Fig. 4). In contrast, the pooled analysis was homogeneous for hospital stay (I² = 2.82%, p = 0.39).

Only one thromboembolic event was reported in the control group, while none occurred in the TXA group. Meta-analysis showed no significant difference between the two groups (n = 11 arms, RR = 0.92, 95% CI [0.29, 2.92], p = 0.89), and the pooled analysis was homogeneous (I² = 0%, p = 1) (Fig. 6).

Summary of sensitivity analysis and publication bias analysis

Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of all endpoints, except for the rate of postoperative bleeding. The omission of individual studies in this analysis significantly altered the pooled effect size, rendering it statistically insignificant (Supplementary Fig. 5). There was no evidence of publication bias for the rate of thromboembolic events (n = 11 arms), as indicated by the absence of funnel plot asymmetry and a non-significant Egger’s regression test (p = 0.0812, Supplementary Fig. 6).

Summary of certainty of evidence

According to the GRADE assessment, the overall certainty of evidence for all outcomes was very low (Supplementary Table 3). The inclusion of both randomized and non-randomized trials led to a serious risk of bias, while imprecision was common due to small sample sizes, low number of events, or wide confidence intervals. Inconsistency was generally not serious, except for operative time where the between-study heterogeneity was high (I2 > 50%). Publication bias was not formally assessed (< 10 studies) for almost all outcomes, though it cannot be excluded given the limited evidence base. Overall, confidence in the estimated effects remained limited (very low) across all outcomes.

Discussion

Summary of principal findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis included 10 studies (11 treatment arms) involving 2,137 patients undergoing bariatric surgery, primarily LSG. TXA was most commonly administered as a single intravenous dose during anesthesia induction. Among the RCTs, the overall risk of bias was rated as low in two studies, with some concerns in one, and high in another. For non-RCTs, four studies had a low risk of bias, while two had some concerns. For the primary endpoint, the rate of postoperative bleeding was significantly lower in the TXA group. In the RCT subgroup (two trials), this apparent benefit was largely driven by the Sermet et al. study, which contributed most of the statistical weight to the pooled estimate. In leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, exclusion of Sermet et al. rendered the effect non-significant. This fragility indicates that the observed reduction in postoperative bleeding is highly dependent on this single influential trial and should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. TXA was also associated with statistically significant reductions in mean operative time and hospital stay, though these differences were of limited clinical significance. Only one thromboembolic event was reported (in a control group), with no significant difference between groups. However, the very low number of events means that any conclusions regarding thromboembolic safety are based on limited data and should be interpreted with caution.

Interpretation of findings and clinical implications

Bariatric surgery, while offering significant long-term benefits in weight reduction and metabolic improvement [2, 3], is a technically demanding procedure that often carries a heightened risk of peri-operative bleeding [8, 9]. This is primarily due to the dense vascularity of visceral fat, altered anatomy in obese patients, and the complexity of procedures such as sleeve gastrectomy and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass. Although the overall rate of perioperative bleeding in bariatric surgery is relatively low (~ 4%) [8, 9], even minimal bleeding can significantly impair visualization of the surgical field. This distortion can complicate dissection, prolong operative time, and increase the risk of inadvertent injury to surrounding structures, ultimately contributing to higher rates of intraoperative and postoperative complications. Therefore, meticulous control of bleeding is a critical surgical objective, and the use of effective anti-hemorrhagic agents, such as TXA, can be highly beneficial.

A wide range of systemic pharmacologic interventions are employed to support perioperative hemostasis, including vasoconstrictors, hypotensive agents, and antifibrinolytics [11]. Among these, TXA has gained widespread clinical use across many surgical disciplines [13–15]. As a synthetic lysine analog, TXA acts by competitively inhibiting the activation of plasminogen to plasmin, a key enzyme involved in fibrinolysis. This inhibition stabilizes formed fibrin clots, thereby reducing both microvascular and overt surgical bleeding [12]. The utility of TXA as a hemostatic adjunct is supported by a meta-analysis of over 200 RCTs including over 125,000 patients, which showed that prophylactic intravenous TXA significantly reduced both overall and hemorrhage-specific mortality compared to placebo or no treatment. Importantly, this reduction in bleeding risk did not coincide with a significant increase in thromboembolic complications [16]. Our meta-analysis, which specifically focused on the use of TXA in bariatric surgery, is reassuring from a safety perspective, as no thromboembolic events were reported in patients receiving TXA across the included studies. However, the sample size and absolute number of events were small, so these findings should be viewed as suggestive rather than definitive regarding thromboembolic risk. Obesity is already associated with a higher baseline risk for venous thromboembolism, which underscores the need for cautious interpretation and further adequately powered safety studies [36].

These sensitivity findings, particularly the loss of statistical significance when the Sermet et al. trial is excluded, suggest that the evidence for a reduction in postoperative bleeding is compatible with a clinically relevant effect but remains statistically fragile and should be interpreted with caution. TXA was also used across different doses and timings rather than a single standardized protocol, which likely contributed to between-study variability, including the substantial heterogeneity in operative time; although any potential benefit does not appear confined to a single regimen, the current data are insufficient to define an optimal dose or schedule.

The anti-hemorrhagic effects of TXA have been extensively documented in a variety of surgical procedures, including endoscopic sinus surgery [37], head and neck procedures [38], vaginal delivery [39], myomectomy [40], hysterectomy [41], hepatic surgery [42], and shoulder arthroplasty [43]. In each of these, TXA has demonstrated efficacy in reducing blood loss, the need for transfusion, and sometimes operative time and postoperative mortality. Our findings extend the scope of TXA’s utility to bariatric surgery, highlighting its potential role in minimizing perioperative blood loss and improving surgical efficiency in this growing field.

The clinical implications of this review support the use of TXA as an effective and safe agent for blood conservation in bariatric surgery. Given the unique bleeding risks associated with these procedures and the lack of thromboembolic complications observed, TXA should be considered a valuable adjunct in the perioperative management of patients undergoing bariatric operations.

Comparison with previous meta-analyses

The findings of our research are consistent with prior meta-analyses on the use of TXA in bariatric surgery [17–19], which similarly suggested a potential benefit in reducing rate of perioperative bleeding and shorter operative time. However, these earlier studies were limited in scope, typically including only four to six RCTs and lacking critical methodological components such as appraisal of additional outcomes, subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, assessment of publication bias, and evaluation of the certainty of evidence. Furthermore, they did not incorporate several recent high-quality studies (n = 4) with larger sample sizes and improved methodological rigor [20–23]. By including these newer trials and employing a more comprehensive analytical approach, our updated meta-analysis not only expands the evidence base but also strengthens the reliability of the conclusions. These enhancements allow for a more nuanced and robust assessment of TXA’s efficacy and safety in bariatric surgery, providing stronger support for its clinical application.

Strengths and limitations

Our study possesses several notable strengths. It is the most comprehensive and up-to-date systematic review and meta-analysis assessing the efficacy and safety of prophylactic TXA compared with passive controls in bariatric surgery. We included both randomized and non-randomized controlled trials, allowing for a broader synthesis of the available comparative evidence while still maintaining a focus on methodological quality. Accordingly, our primary inferences are based on the randomized trials, while the non-randomized studies are considered as lower-certainty supportive evidence. To account for potential differences in study design, we conducted subgroup analyses based on study type (randomized vs. non-randomized), enhancing the interpretability and applicability of our findings. The majority of included studies demonstrated a low to moderate risk of bias, lending credibility to our results. A wide range of efficacy and safety endpoints were analyzed to provide a well-rounded and clinically meaningful evaluation of TXA’s role in this setting. Furthermore, the robustness of our conclusions was reinforced through rigorous analytical methods, including leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, assessment of publication bias, and evaluation of certainty of evidence.

Nonetheless, our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. One of the primary limitations lies in the relatively small number of included comparative studies—particularly randomized controlled trials—as well as the modest sample sizes within those studies. Accordingly, we could not assess publication bias as all outcomes included less than 10 studies. Additionally, some of the included studies were retrospective in design, which inherently carry risks of bias such as selection bias and confounding that may affect the reliability of their findings. Moreover, several outcomes exhibited notable heterogeneity, likely stemming from variations in perioperative factors such as patient characteristics, surgical techniques, dosing regimens, and routes of TXA administration. These differences may have influenced the pooled effect estimates and should be taken into account when interpreting the results. In addition, key perioperative co-interventions—such as low molecular weight heparin, mechanical thromboprophylaxis, and other hemostatic strategies—were neither standardized nor consistently reported, which limits our ability to isolate the independent effect of TXA. Furthermore, because most included treatment arms (9 of 11) focused on laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy, our findings primarily apply to LSG, and their generalizability to other bariatric procedures remains uncertain.

Future directions

In light of the aforementioned limitations, future research should prioritize large-scale, multicenter, and well-controlled RCTs comparing prophylactic TXA to placebo in patients undergoing bariatric surgery. Given the observed heterogeneity in outcomes—likely influenced by variations in surgical techniques, patient characteristics, TXA dosing, and routes of administration—future studies should aim to standardize these variables and report them transparently to enhance comparability. Subgroup analyses stratified by surgical procedure (e.g., gastric bypass vs. sleeve gastrectomy), TXA administration route (intravenous vs. topical), and patient-specific risk profiles would be particularly informative. In this context, the work by Straatman et al. provides a useful foundation: using data from the Dutch Audit for Treatment of Obesity (2015–2020), they developed a prediction model for postoperative bleeding, identifying significant risk factors such as male sex, age > 45, BMI < 40 kg/m², cardiovascular disease, and sleeve gastrectomy. Their clinical decision rule suggests that patients with three or more of these risk factors are at increased bleeding risk [44]. Incorporating such predictive tools in future TXA trials may help stratify patients and assess differential efficacy in high- versus low-risk groups. Additionally, research could explore the potential synergistic effects of TXA in combination with other pharmacological agents aimed at reducing perioperative bleeding. Identifying which subgroups of bariatric patients derive the greatest benefit from TXA prophylaxis would support more personalized and risk-adapted perioperative strategies.

Conclusion

Among patients undergoing bariatric surgery, prophylactic TXA may be associated with a reduction in postoperative blood loss; however, this effect was not robust in sensitivity analyses. TXA appeared to be generally safe, with no clear increase in thromboembolic events observed. Given the very low certainty of the available evidence, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and further well-designed randomized controlled trials are required to confirm the potential benefits and safety of TXA.
