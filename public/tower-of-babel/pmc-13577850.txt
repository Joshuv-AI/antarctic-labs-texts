Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome following eclampsia in a primigravida at 35 weeks of gestation: A case report
Authors: Anika Chowdhury, Anika Tasnim Khan, Zakia Akther Zahan, Zareen Tabassum, Nafisa Zaman Akhtar, Humayra Tabassum, Md. Deluwar Hussen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) is a reversible neurological disorder most commonly triggered by eclampsia and characterized on imaging by vasogenic edema, typically involving the parieto-occipital regions. We describe a 26-year-old primigravida at 35 weeks of gestation who presented with headache, dyspnea, and a generalized tonic-clonic seizure, with severe hypertension (200/110 mmHg), 3+ proteinuria, serum creatinine (1.1 mg/dL), and platelet count (100,000/mm3) consistent with severe preeclampsia with superimposed eclampsia. She underwent emergency lower-segment cesarean section (LSCS) for delivery of a live neonate, with a second intraoperative seizure controlled with intravenous diazepam and magnesium sulfate. Postoperatively, she developed sudden bilateral visual loss without focal neurological deficit. Brain MRI showed bilateral, symmetric T2- and FLAIR-hyperintense signal in the parieto-occipital white matter without evidence of restricted diffusion on DWI/ADC a pattern of vasogenic edema diagnostic of PRES. She was treated with intravenous magnesium sulfate, antihypertensive therapy, and levetiracetam, with complete clinical recovery and radiological resolution on follow-up imaging. This case highlights eclamptic PRES as a cause of postoperative cortical visual loss and illustrates how the combination of T2/FLAIR signal change with preserved diffusion on MRI distinguishes reversible vasogenic edema from ischemic injury, supporting prompt, targeted management even in resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) is an uncommon but increasingly recognized neurological condition, first described by Hinchey et al. [1]. It typically presents with headache, seizures, altered mental status, confusion, and visual disturbance. Although not restricted to pregnancy, PRES in women of reproductive age is frequently secondary to hypertensive disorders, renal failure, eclampsia, sepsis, cytotoxic chemotherapy, immunosuppression, or autoimmune disease [2]. Diagnosis rests on cross-sectional imaging, most reliably MRI, which demonstrates reversible bilateral vasogenic edema, characteristically in the parieto-occipital white matter [3]. Eclampsia is the most common cause of PRES in pregnant women, accounting for the large majority of obstetric cases [4], and can occur anywhere from 20 weeks of gestation to 4 weeks postpartum [5]. Despite this recognized association, PRES remains underdiagnosed in many obstetric and emergency settings, particularly where access to MRI is limited or clinicians are unfamiliar with its imaging hallmarks. Because the syndrome is, by definition, reversible with prompt blood pressure control and seizure management, timely recognition has a direct bearing on neurological outcome: delayed diagnosis or misclassification as ischemic stroke can lead to inappropriate management and, in severe cases, to irreversible injury such as infarction or hemorrhage [6]. Distinguishing PRES from other postpartum neurological emergencies depends heavily on correct interpretation of the MRI pattern, including the sequence-specific findings that separate vasogenic from cytotoxic edema.

This report describes a 26-year-old primigravida in whom eclampsia led to PRES. As a life-threatening neurological emergency, this case illustrates how neuroimaging can support early diagnosis and prompt, potentially life-saving intervention, particularly in low-resource settings where clinical suspicion must often substitute for readily available advanced imaging.

Clinical presentation

A 26 year old, primigravida patient was admitted at 35 weeks of her pregnancy with complaints of shortness of breath for 2 days, headache from the morning of admission and one episode of generalized tonic–clonic seizure (GTCS) lasting for approximately 2 minutes. On query, she stated that she had fluctuating blood pressure which had been diagnosed after 20 weeks of pregnancy. However, she was nonadherent to antihypertensive medications and was not on regular antenatal check ups. Her urine output was normal and she had no history of epilepsy prior to this episode.

On general examination, Glasgow Coma Scale Score (GCS) was 15, pulse was 95 bpm, blood pressure was 200/110 mmHg and she was mildly anemic with severe bilateral pitting ankle edema.

On obstetric examination, fetal movement was present and fetal heart rate was 140-160 bpm. The height of the uterus was 35 weeks size which corresponded to her period of amenorrhea. Her latest ultrasonography report revealed posterior placenta with no evidence of placenta previa.

Respiratory examination revealed bilateral basal dullness, reduced breath sounds at both lung bases, and fine inspiratory crepitations. These findings were clinically suggestive of bilateral pleural effusion; however, chest imaging could not be obtained prior to emergency cesarean delivery, and the diagnosis therefore remained a clinical impression rather than a radiologically confirmed one.

Investigations showed 3+ proteinuria on bedside urinary dipstick testing, a Bedside Clotting Time (BCT) of 7 minutes, normal capillary blood glucose, a serum creatinine of 1.1 mg/dL, and a platelet count of 100,000 mm3, findings consistent with severe preeclampsia with superimposed eclampsia. Chest radiography was deferred because the patient required emergency lower-segment cesarean section (LSCS).

Following LSCS, a live neonate was delivered with Apgar scores of 7 and 8 at 1 and 5 minutes, respectively. The neonate developed respiratory distress with an inadequate response to oxygen therapy and was transferred to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) for further management. Maternal blood pressure remained elevated after delivery of the baby and placenta, and the patient had a second GTCS intraoperatively, lasting approximately 1 minute, which was controlled with slow intravenous diazepam 10 mg.

On admission, the patient received an intravenous loading dose of magnesium sulfate 4 g, infused over 20 minutes. She was subsequently transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for further management and monitoring. Given the MRI evidence of vasogenic cerebral edema, intravenous dexamethasone 5 mg every 8 hours was added to the treatment. In the ICU, her GCS was 15, blood pressure was initially elevated but was subsequently controlled with slow intravenous bolus of Labetalol 20 mg and a continuous intravenous magnesium sulfate maintenance infusion of 1 g/hour (24 g over 24 hours).

Within hours of hemodynamic stabilization, the patient developed sudden-onset bilateral visual loss with headache, without loss of consciousness, confusion, or focal motor weakness. Ophthalmological and neurological examination showed visual acuity reduced to counting fingers at 1m distance in both eyes, with preserved pupillary light reflexes and no focal neurological deficit.

Discussion

PRES is a rare clinicoradiological disorder characterized by acute neurological deficits including headache, impaired vision, altered mental status, and seizures [7]. Eclampsia is the most common cause (97.9%) [4]. It can be suspected clinically but confirmed by its characteristic neuroradiological finding by MRI [8].

The radiological finding usually shows reversible subcortical vasogenic edema predominantly affecting bilateral posterior white matter regions of the cerebral hemispheres [9]. The exact cause of PRES is still idiopathic with various hypotheses, grossly caused by cerebral autoregulation failure due to perfusion change and endothelial dysfunction due to disturbance in Blood–Brain Barrier. Hypertension with failed autoregulation and hyperperfusion remain as debatable causes for the development of brain edema [10]. Again, Encephalopathy from vasoconstriction which is a compensatory response to hypertension, causes vasogenic and cytotoxic edema and is thought to play a key role in the development of clinical symptoms and neuroradiological findings in patients with eclampsia [11]. After the treatment of hypertension, hyperperfusion is usually seen resolving during subsequent imaging examinations. Another theory suggests that during PRES, there is activation of immune system and increased levels of circulating Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), Endothelin-1 (ET-1) Interleukin-1 (IL-1) that can cause endothelial dysfunction in cerebral arteries [12]. A recently published theory suggests that condition like eclampsia is related to Arginine-Vasopressin hypersecretion which in turn can lead to cerebral vasoconstriction, endothelial dysfunction and ischemia causing cytotoxic edema appearing in the parieto-occipital lobe [13].

In our patient, we encountered headache, shortness of breath, high blood pressure of 200/100 mmHg, bedside urinary dipstick test showed protein +++, BCT 7 minutes, CBG 5.6 mmol/L, thrombocytopenia from recent investigations and GTCS later on with GCS 15 but fetal condition was optimum on admission. Thus an emergency LUCS was performed with a live neonate delivery but during operation, another episode of seizure was noted demanding the patient to be stabilized by appropriate dose of Diazepam, Magnesium Sulfate, and Levetiracetam. For further management and proper monitoring, she was taken to ICU where she faced vision loss with markedly reduced visual acuity with preserved light reflex.

On admission, we performed neurological examination, bed side CBG to exclude ischemic stroke and metabolic cause of seizure subsequently. To exclude other differentials like hypertensive encephalopathy, ischemic stroke, and venous sinus thrombosis we advised MRI which revealed high signal intensity in both parieto-occipital lobes which remained persistent with PRES that helped us to exclude differentials. Although according to definition, PRES is reversible but severe complications like status epilepticus, intracranial hemorrhage, and ischemic stroke can cause irreversible neurological sequelae even mortality requiring ICU support as in case of our patient [6]. In addition, the predilection of posterior area of the brain is possibly due to the regional heterogeneity of intracranial arterioles and better sympathetic auto regulation in the anterior circulation than the posterior part causing the lesion mostly in occipital and parietal lobes. On the contrary, the lesion in radiography can also occur in the brain cortex, frontal lobes, basal ganglia, even in brain stem rather than only the parieto-occipital white matter area making the PRES term a misnomer [[14], [15], [16]]. Therefore, a keen eye for clinical suspicion and crucial early neuroimaging even in a low resource setting for patients with eclampsia is important to start early treatment for a reversible favorable outcome.

Conclusion

PRES should be considered in any woman with eclampsia who develops neurological or visual manifestations. As its name indicates, the condition is reversible, but only if recognized and treated promptly. Management ultimately depends on treating the underlying cause, underscoring the importance of antenatal screening for hypertensive disorders in pregnancy. Rapid treatment initiation improves outcomes and lowers the risk of complications. Prompt diagnosis in resource-limited settings remains challenging, but greater clinician awareness of this diagnosis and its imaging correlates can meaningfully improve maternal neurological outcomes.
