Measurement tools to assess the obesogenic environment in neighborhood: a scoping review
Authors: Yanisa Pumsutas, Hathairat Kosiyaporn, Nisachol Cetthakrikul, Sopit Nasueb, Jiranun Thiphong, Metinee Sathitsorot, Orratai Waleewong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe obesogenic environment encompasses factors that promote overweight and obesity, but few studies integrate both food and physical activity environments with limited details on measurement tools. This study aims to identify measurement tools and methodological approaches used to assess obesogenic environments at the neighborhood level.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review of studies from January 2014 to September 2023. The literature was searched using four primary themes: environment, obesity, physical activity, and food, focusing on studies with measurement tools for evaluating the obesogenic environment from a physical perspective, including Geographic Information Systems (GIS) applications and non-GIS tools such as surveys and audits. Studies in specific population groups and settings such as patients and disadvantaged groups, studies at individual or household levels, and outcomes beyond obesity were excluded. Data were extracted and narratively synthesized, focusing on study characteristics, methodologies, and tools in alignment with the conceptual framework.ResultsSeventy-nine studies were included, predominantly from high-income countries (HICs) (81%), with only one study from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Sixty-four studies were cross-sectional design, with GIS commonly used for measurements (61.0%), followed by combined GIS and other tools (23%), and non-GIS (16%). Accessibility and availability of food and physical activity facilities were mostly identified (49%) such as density or proximity of food or physical activity facilities or calculated using the composite index such as Retail Food Environment Index (RFEI), along with infrastructure for walking, cycling, and transportation as walkability or Walk Score.ConclusionsThis scoping review summarizes tools and methods used to assess obesogenic environments at the neighborhood level. The review points to limited support for GIS tools in LMICs. Combining GIS with surveys and observational audits may help adapt assessment tools across diverse settings. These efforts could inform monitoring and guide actions addressing environmental contributors to non-communicable diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25390-5.Keywords: Obesogenic environment, Measurement, Obesity, Food, Physical activity, Scoping review

Background

Overweight and obesity are metabolic risk factors that increased the risk of Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs), which are the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. As of 2022, 1 in 8 people worldwide were affected by obesity marking a doubling since 1990 (from 8.8% to 18.5% in women and from 4.8% to 14.0% in men) [2, 3]. The causes of overweight and obesity are complex and multifaceted, involving a combination of biological, psychological, environmental, and behavioral factors [4]. This combination of environmental factors, including physical, social, and economic elements that promote unhealthy eating habits and sedentary behaviors, thereby contributing to overweight and obesity, is referred as the obesogenic environment [4].

The concept of the obesogenic environment was introduced by Swinburn in 1999, defining it as the sum of the influences that the surroundings, opportunities or conditions of life have on promoting obesity in individuals and populations [5]. The ANGELO framework is used to analyze obesogenic environments dividing the environment into micro and macro levels across four types: physical, economic, political, and sociocultural [5]. For example, physical environment refers to availability of food services and physical activity facilities, economic environment stands for affordability of foods, and sociocultural environment refers to acceptability of foods and physical activity [5]. According to three systematic reviews, associations between food and physical activity environments with weight outcomes were generally very small or absent, though some factors like fast-food exposure, urbanization, land use mix, and urban sprawl showed more consistent associations with weight status in adult [6–8]. Greater accessibility to supermarkets or less access to takeaway outlets were consistently linked to lower BMI or obesity prevalence [8].

Existing research about instruments for characterizing obesogenic environments are predominantly originated from the high-income countries (HICs) and conducted in English [7, 9], with limited exploration of tools suitable for low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), show the gap in assessing obesogenic environments. Moreover, this literature lacks comprehensive details on the measurements, variables, or proxies specifically tailored to assess the obesogenic environment [9], and many studies predominantly focus on children [10, 11]. Therefore, this study aims to identify an inventory of measurement and tools used for assessing obesogenic environment in neighborhood.

Methods

This study applied scoping review to identify measurements and tools used in assessing obesogenic environment in neighborhood.

Search strategy

The search strategy involved retrieving reviewed articles using online databases from PubMed and Embase because our primary objective was to examine the health-related aspects of obesogenic environments, particularly in the context of public health. Both databases index a variety of journals focused on environmental health including urban health, based on their journal listings [12, 13], and capture more than 95% of relevant literature [14]. The search terms and Boolean operators were applied in both databases. Additionally, we also conducted handsearching and reference searching alongside the e-databases to ensure comprehensive literature search. Filters were applied to include only human studies published in English from 2014 to present.

The search strategy involved four distinct themes: “environment” AND “obesity” AND “physical activity” OR “food”, see Table 1. The specific search terms used for each theme were detailed in Additional file 1.

Eligibility criteria

The studies selected in this scoping review were observational studies such as cross-sectional and cohort studies, with a focus on analyzing the association between obesogenic environment and overweight, obesity, and weight change. The inclusion criteria comprised adults aged 19 to 59 years old. Only peer-reviewed literature published in English published between January 2014 and September 2023 was considered.

Exclusion criteria encompassed participants from disadvantaged groups, such as migrants or residing in rural or low socio-economic areas, as well as individuals with specific health conditions including patients, pregnant/postpartum women, or disease survivors. These groups were excluded in order to focus the review on the general adult population and to reduce heterogeneity that could confound the assessment of obesogenic environments. In addition, studies focusing on treatment, intervention, or program/policy evaluation were excluded to maintain emphasis on observational assessment rather than intervention effects. Furthermore, behavioral outcomes beyond obesity, such as physical activity or eating behaviors, were excluded to align with the review’s objective of examining obesogenic environments specifically in relation to obesity outcomes. Similarly, studies reporting health or disease risk scores, such as those for cardiovascular disease (CVD) or metabolic risks, were also excluded. Moreover, papers addressing the obesogenic environment as confounders or mediators or unquantified attitudes toward the environment were excluded. Additionally, studies involving interpersonal, intrapersonal, and societal levels, or those in educational or workplace settings, were not considered because the study focused on physical environmental aspects at the neighborhood level. Finally, non-peer-reviewed papers, those with inaccessible full texts, literature review, qualitative studies, and experimental studies were removed from the analysis.

Study selection

The process of study selection was managed through Covidence, a systematic review management program, encompassing a thorough review of titles, abstracts, and full texts. A total of six reviewers participated in assessing the articles. Two independent reviewers independently conducted both title/abstract then these were passed to full-text screenings. In full text screening, all the reasons for study exclusion would be recorded. If there was a disagreement among reviewers, a third reviewer (YP and HK) was involved in resolving any conflicts and make a final decision.

Data extraction

Data extraction encompassed characteristics of study such as title, authors’ names, year of publication, country, country classification (such as HICs or LMICs), objectives, study period, participants’ age, sample size, and measurement tools. These tools included study design (cohort study, cross-sectional study, others), data collection (Geographic Information Systems (GIS), registry, observation/audit, survey, others), data measurement and data analysis. Figure 1 presented all domains of obesogenic environments components at neighborhood level for data extraction, adapted from a conceptual framework in previous studies [15–18]. Within the food environment, components were characterized as accessibility and availability of facilities, affordability, accommodation, and acceptability. In the physical activity environment, domains included the accessibility and availability of facilities, accessibility and availability of infrastructure, design/aesthetics, and safety. For example, we assessed the accessibility and availability of facilities by examining the density and proximity of food outlets and physical activity facilities. Similarly, the accessibility and availability of infrastructure were determined by factors like street connectivity and walkability.

Conceptual framework on obesogenic environments (adapted from Lake, 2005; Swinburn, 1999; Glanz, 2005; Caspi, 2012; Giles-Corti, 2016 [5, 15–18])

Data synthesis

Data was narratively synthesized, aligning with the components of the obesogenic framework. Measurements were categorized into specific themes based on the environmental aspects they assessed, such as the availability and accessibility of facilities. Each theme was analyzed in terms of the proportion of studies and the description of characteristics. Missing data was identified as non-assessment variables and was excluded in the synthesis.

ChatGPT-4 was used to paraphrase text and to check the language for grammatical correctness. All content and final revisions were made and verified by the authors, who are responsible for the accuracy and integrity of the manuscript.

Results

From the PRISMA flow chart (Fig. 2), database searches identified 1460 records from Embase and 1256 records from PubMed, with 13 records from citation searching. After the removal of duplicates, a total of 1993 studies were screened. Of these, 234 studies were deemed eligible based on title and abstract and were advanced to full text screening. During the full-text screening, 155 studies were excluded for various reasons. Specifically, papers were excluded due to age criteria, specific groups, intervention/policy evaluation, focusing on behavioral or diseases beyond obesity, or health or disease risk scoring, consideration of the built environment as a confounder or mediator, focusing on individual/interpersonal and societal levels, educational or workplace settings, measurements of unquantified attitude/perception, and being experimental, qualitative, or literature review studies, non-English, non-peer reviewed, and inability to access full text. Finally, a total of 79 papers were included for data extraction.

Study characteristics

A total of 79 studies were published between 2014 and 2023. Publication trends peaked in 2015 (n = 12) and in 2017 and 2019 (n = 10 each), followed by a general decline in the number of publications from 2020 to 2023. The majority of studies were conducted in North America (n = 38, 48.1%), followed by Europe (n = 15, 19.0%), Oceania (n = 9, 11.4%), South America (n = 7, 8.9%), Asia (n = 6, 7.6% including China, South Korea, Japan, and India), and multi-country/multi-region studies (n = 4, 5.0%) (see Table 2 and in Additional file 2 for details). The distribution of studies skewed towards HICs, with 64 out of 79 papers (81.0%) originating from these countries, followed by 10 from upper middle- income countries (UMICs, 12.7%), four studies from mixed HICs and UMICs (5%), and only one from LMICs (1.3%). Cross-sectional studies were the most common study design, comprising 64 out of 79 papers (81.0%), while 15 papers were cohort study. Sample sizes varied: 33 studies had fewer than 5,000 participants, 28 had more than 10,000, and 13 ranged between 5,000 and 10,000 participants. The remaining five used other units of analysis, including households, states, counties, local areas, and communities.

Objective measurements of obesogenic environment

Food environment

The objective measures of accessibility and availability of facilities in the food environment mostly from GIS in addition to registry from public and private organizations such as the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) and the CDC’s Division of Nutrition, Physical Activity, and Obesity, InfoUSA®, and Dun & Bradstreet, see Additional file 3. Data measured to identify density and proximity of food facilities in specific areas. Food facilities were identified such as supermarkets, grocery stores, convenience stores, and fast-food restaurants.

Key measurements related to the accessibility and availability of food facilities can be grouped into density and proximity measures. Density measures highlighted the concentration of food outlets within buffer distances of square miles/kilometers (e.g., 800 or 1,600 m), within straight-line distances of meter or kilometer (e.g. 500 m, or 1 km) around participants’ residential address, and numbers of food outlets per census tract/census block, or per population unit (e.g., per 10,000 population). For example, a study by Mejia et al. (2015), they measured availability of food facilities by the number of food outlets by type lying inside those radii (0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 3.0 miles in radius centered on a respondent’s residence) and also measured the density of food outlets per census tract by number of outlets in census tract per 1,000 inhabitants and in census tract per square mile [31]. Moreover, a study by Hobbs et al. (2018), the density of food outlets was measured by number of food outlets within the 2 km radial buffer around an individual’s home in multiple forms of transport – walking and car [52].

For proximity measures, the average Euclidean distance (straight line) and street network distances to the next food facilities from participants’ address/workplace or area-weighted/population-weighted centroid of specific areas (population), were identified to provide insights into the ease of access for residents. For instance, a study by Aretz et al. (2023) assess the proximity in a person’s surroundings by using GIS to measure the average Euclidean distance to the next food facilities (such as fast food stores, fresh food stores, and supermarkets) [91]. A study by Hobbs et al. (2023) also assessed driving distance (meters) to the closest major chain fast food outlet and large supermarket [93]. In addition, the composite indices, such as the Retail Food Environment Index (RFEI), were used to assess the retail food environment within a particular area by considering both the availability of healthy food options (such as supermarkets and fresh food markets) and unhealthy food options (such as fast-food outlets and convenience stores) [29, 44, 72, 92, 96]. RFEI had a low convergent validity coefficient with shelf space food availability (0.192) and very low with residents’ perceptions of food availability (0.028) [97].

In the mixed domains of affordability and accommodation, the primary tool for measuring the food environment was food store audit followed by store-level food sales data from customer data science companies. Firstly, food prices offered insights for affordability to purchase such as food staple prices (sum of prices of five standard items including egg, whole milk, white bread, high-sugar cereal, and low-sugar cereal), junk food prices, and fruit and vegetable prices, and standardized price index (SPI) (standardized price of food items) [20]. Secondly, the accommodation referred to availability and variety of foods assessed through the number of food sales between healthy (fresh and frozen fruit and vegetables) and unhealthy foods (cakes, biscuits, savoury pies, savoury snacks, and sweetened drinks [98]. Moreover, there was also a mixed tool subjectively measuring multiple domains of food environment. The Nutrition Environment Measures Survey in stores (NEMS-S) used as an audit/observation tool in store to measure availability of food items, affordability or price of each item, and acceptability of foods such as signage and promotion of food items using closed ended questions [49]. In addition, another mixed measurement tool also assessed accessibility of food facilities, affordability and acceptability of foods including street network distances from each respondent’s home to their major food shopping store (the shortest route), food availability (number of food items), food prices (food staple prices, junk food prices, fruit and vegetable prices, SPI), and marketing (total number of in-store store displays and dominant view from the store’s main entrance in each type of foods) [20].

Physical activity environment

In assessing the accessibility and availability of physical activity facilities and infrastructure, it referred to proximity and density of physical activity facilities or neighborhood walkability, respectively. The study commonly employed GIS data sources in addition to registry such as census data, Ordnance Survey, or Sport Scotland, see Additional file 3. Physical activity facilities were identified such as parks, recreational facilities or sport facilities.

Key measurements related to the accessibility and availability of physical activity facilities can be grouped into density and proximity measures. Density measures highlighted the number of physical activity facilities within a radial buffer centered on the home postcode, or the number of accessible physical activity facilities per individual by each mode of transport. For instance, a study by Ellaway (2016) explored the mean number of accessible recreational physical activity facilities per 1,000 individuals considering routes on foot, by bike, by car and by bus from the population weighted centroid of each data zone to each facility [38]. In terms of the accessibility and availability of infrastructure, key measurements commonly included residential density, intersection density, land use mix, and the ratio of retail within buffer area, which could be calculated into the walkability index (Walk Score test–retest reliability correlation coefficient 1.0) [99]. Additionally, public transport stop, business destination and park destination density, street network distance to the nearest public transport stop or station, sidewalk length, were also variables for walkability and street network measurements [21, 25, 54, 78]. For instance, a study by Tarlov (2020) examined a neighborhood walkability index by assessing environmental features, including population density, housing unit density, walking destinations, and street connectivity [78].

Subjective measurements of obesogenic environment

Food environment

The survey tools usually measured mixed domains of perception toward food environment, see Additional file 3. The validated survey tool that assessed the perceptions of food and physical activity environment called ‘Neighborhood Environment Walkability Scale (NEWS)’ was applied from the International Physical Activity and Environment Network (IPEN) Adult Study [26, 62, 76, 79]. The NEWS demonstrated strong construct validity (all F statistic = F1,105 >9.69, P < 0.003) and test-retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficients of all subscales ≥ 0.58; all F1,105 >3.78, P < 0.001) [100]. The ten NEWS subscales included residential density, land-use mix diversity, land-use mix access, street connectivity, infrastructure and safety for walking, aesthetics, safety from traffic, safety from crime, park proximity, and transit proximity [26, 76, 79, 101]. The example of the key questions to assess perceived proximity from home to the nearest supermarket, other food/grocery stores, and restaurants from to 1–5 min to over 30 min walking distance was “How long would it take to get from your home to the nearest businesses or facilities if you walked to them?” [62, 101]. A study from Mackenbach et al. (2019), perceived availability and usage of fast food outlets was measured by a survey which asked about the availability and usage of fast food outlets in their neighborhood with answering options to this single question were “available and used,” “available, not used,” and “not available,” [67]. There was a tool to assess the variety, quality, and price or affordability of fresh fruits and vegetables in their neighborhood, defined as a 10–15 min walk or 5-minute drive, used a scale from 1 (very satisfied) to 4 (not at all satisfied) [51]. In addition, a study by Dubowitz et al. (2015) used the Automated Self-Administered 24-hour Recall (ASA-24) to measure food access and purchasing practices. The key questionnaire included information such as street network distances from each respondent’s home to the retail venues they reported visiting, the types of stores where respondents shopped, shopping frequency, transportation mode, shopping duration, and food expenditures [27].

Physical activity environment

There were mixed domains subjectively assessing physical activity environment including the accessibility and availability of facilities or infrastructure, design/aesthetic, and safety via neighborhood physical environmental perceptions, see Additional file 3. The validated NEWS as mentioned above also included measurements of residential density, land-use mix diversity, land-use mix access, street connectivity, infrastructure and safety for walking, aesthetics, safety from traffic, safety from crime, park proximity, and transit proximity [26, 62, 76, 79]. For instance, subscales of neighborhood satisfaction rated from 1 (strongly dissatisfied) to 5 (strongly satisfied) and sample items related to physical activity environment were “The sidewalks in my neighborhood are well maintained” (walking/cycling facilities), “There is a grass/dirt strip that separates the streets from sidewalks in my neighborhood” (accessibility and availability of facilities or infrastructure), “There are attractive buildings/homes in my neighborhood” (design/aesthetic), and “There is a high crime rate in my neighborhood” (safety) [101].

Other survey tool was the Scale of Perception of the Environment for the Practice of Physical Activity in Cohort of Universities of Minas Gerais (CUME project) which measured proximity to facilities and infrastructure [62]. For example, “In case of going on foot from your home, how long would you take to get to these following places in your neighborhood? (e.g., supermarkets, parks, train station) [89]. It also assessed the perception towards design/aesthetic and safety of physical activity environment that “Are there green areas with trees in the streets near your home?” (design/aesthetic) and “Do the streets near your home have good lighting at night?” (safety) [62].

Discussion

This scoping review aimed to map the range of measurement tools and methodological approaches used to assess obesogenic environments at the neighborhood level. Using systematic methods, we found that most of the studies were conducted in HICs and relied primarily on cross-sectional designs, while research and tool development in LMICs remain limited. GIS was the most frequently used to assess accessibility and availability of food and physical activity environments and infrastructure. Composite indices, such as the retail food environment ratio and walkability index, were also applied. Other domains, including affordability, accommodation, and acceptability, were commonly examined using mixed methods, such as audits, store data, and perception surveys.

Increasing GIS use in HICs and considerations for tool development in LMICs

Most studies on obesogenic environments have been conducted in HICs, particularly North America, regardless of whether they employed questionnaire-based surveys and non-traditional methods such as GIS [9, 102]. In contrast, GIS-based research in LMICs remains limited, despite these countries bearing the majority of global NCD-related mortality and morbidity in 2016 [103]. This gap was largely driven by lack of effective NCD policy tackling with determinants and poor health system under the universal health coverage policy [104]. A systematic review examining the association between food environment and dietary behaviors or obesity found that only about 30% of studies reported a significant link between environmental exposure and increased consumption or obesity [105, 106]; therefore, supporting obesogenic environment research in LMICs is important to identify determinants and guide interventions.

The trends in obesogenic environment research have shifted towards non-traditional data collection methods, particularly GIS. Geographical analysis was the most commonly used method [107, 108], while studies in LMICs relied on surveys, as GIS data are often publicly available and require minimal additional data collection [108, 109]. However, GIS has limitations in construct validity, as it may not fully capture environmental attributes in relation to population health [110, 111]. Agreement between objective GIS measures and subjective perceptions through surveys or interviews is often moderate or low [111]. Therefore, combining both objective and subjective approaches is recommended to improve the validity of obesogenic environment assessments.

Moreover, the effects of spatial exposure may vary depending on food outlet type, dietary outcome and contextual factors. To address these complexities, future studies should consider different population groups and settings by specifying a priori hypotheses relating to the conceptualization [112]. Overall, GIS applications in LMICs need to consider the validity of the tools and the availability of accessible data, in parallel with other measurement approaches.

Accessibility and availability of facilities as key proxies in measuring obesogenic environment

Accessibility and availability of facilities are widely used proxies for obesogenic environments, with data often derived from GIS databases and government registration sources such as the Generalized Land Use Database or Retail Database [72]. Similarly, Baker et al. (2023) reported that geospatial data were obtained from sources such as the Google Street View imagery API or OpenStreetMap, and other GIS-embedded environmental data, licensing data, census data, national surveys and other digital databases from government sectors (e.g., transport, infrastructure, agriculture) [102]. Identifying and developing existing databases is therefore essential to meet new trends in obesogenic environmental research.

The measurement of accessibility and availability depends on both the geographic definition and facility types within neighborhoods. Geographic definitions typically consider distances from reference points (accessibility) and the concentration of locations within a reference area (availability). In the included studies, reference points were selected either from individual address or the center of defined areas, such as radius buffer or census block as neighborhoods [39, 71]. Reference points may be individual addresses or centroids of areas (e.g., centroid or radius sometimes weighted to account for influence between neighborhoods and points of interest), is important to ensure valid results [113]. Neighborhoods are increasingly recognized as a key unit of analysis in obesity research [114], delineated using either predefined administrative boundaries (e.g., census tracts) or perceived areas, such as buffer zones centered on home addresses [115]. For instance, the mean and median of predefined areas were around 1.96 km2 and 1.6 km2 from a study of self-defined residential neighborhoods in five European urban regions [115].

In addition, facilities in the obesogenic environment encompass various types of food and physical activity resources. For the food environment, establishments can generally be classified into three main categories: (1) outlets predominantly of ultra-processed foods, such as fast-food restaurants, takeaway shops, cafeterias, confectionery, ice cream parlors, temporary street food stands, grocery stores, and convenience stores; (2) outlets predominantly sale of natural or minimally processed foods, including farmers’ markets, greengrocers, butcheries, and fish markets; and (3) mixed food establishments, such as bakeries, bars, restaurants, supermarkets, places selling products in bulk, and dairy product vendors [73, 89]. However, food availability and accessibility encompassed a wide range of resources, including facilities such as community gardens and urban farms, which were not limited to the examples of food environments provided in this study, as the specific context significantly influenced these variations. For physical activity facilities, it was defined as public physical activity facilities (athletic fields, football fields, tennis courts, or swimming pools), public parks (green areas for recreation), or public transit (bus stops or subway/train stations) [116]. Accordingly, there are different variables to measure obesogenic environment so the selection should be based on availability of data and representativeness.

In data analysis, obesogenic environment variables are descriptively explained in counts, proportions, and ratios while some were combined in collective variables such as walkability index. In addition, most of the tools are lack of validity and reliability studies. However, the more extensive integration of environmental components including both food and physical activity environments is desirable to better capture the complex variety of exposures [117]. For example, Childhood Obesogenic Environment Index (COEI) is composed of 10 components of the food (density of grocery stores, farmers markets, fast-food restaurants, full‐service restaurants, and convenience stores; percentage of birth at baby‐friendly facilities) and physical activity environments (percentage of population living close to exercise facilities, percentage of population living close to a school, walkability, violent crime percentage) [118]. Although the tool is developed from priori approach (based on previous and existing theory and literature) and expert consultation, it still has limitations in inclusion of other key variables such as quality of physical activity and food resources [118]. Consequently, compiling all relevant exposures using valid and reliable data is essential for robust obesogenic environment assessment.

Other domains of obesogenic environment in terms of affordability, accommodation, and acceptability

Beyond accessibility and availability of facilities, other domains of obesogenic environment included affordability, accommodation, and acceptability (for food environments), as well as design/aesthetic and safety (for physical activity environments). Some studies used mixed measurements or tools to assess the various domains of food or physical activity environments. For instance, Caspi et al. (2012) revealed that mixed tools were necessary to capture the complex and multifaceted nature of food access as GIS-based measures [17].

However, certain tools, such as the Perceived Nutrition Environment Measures Survey (NEMS-P), were not included in this review because studies using NEMS-P were excluded based on specific screening criteria. NEMS-P assesses affordability, accommodation, and acceptability in community and consumer food environments, reporting internal consistency (internal consistency reported as Cronbach’s α values ranging from 0.6 to 0.9, and intra-rater reliability as ICC values ranging from 0.5 to 0.9 for reliability) [100]. Most of the tools retrieve data from survey followed by audit/observation methods which are mostly no validity and reliability of tools. Lytle et al. (2017) noted that internal consistency reliability is particularly relevant for survey or questionnaire tools that use multiple items to measure a specific domain [108]. Although surveys are favored by researchers, the time and resource consuming of this method should be considered. Overall, integrating all dimensions of the obesogenic environment is essential for a comprehensive assessment beyond just accessibility and availability.

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review offers a structured overview of measurement tools and methods used to assess obesogenic environments at the neighborhood level. Its strengths include the use of systematic search strategies which contribute to the transparency and reproducibility of the review process. The inclusion of both GIS and non-GIS tools provides a broad perspective on available methodologies.

However, several limitations remain. Firstly, databases were limited to a few data sources which did not cover specific fields such as GIS. Secondly, the target group included the general adult population, while some specific groups, such as children and other disadvantaged groups, were left behind. Finally, the inclusion criteria selected only English literature so the literature in other languages might be excluded. Future studies could focus on developing tools for specific populations and evaluating their feasibility, validity, and reliability in LMIC contexts.

Conclusion

This scoping review maps a broad range of measurement tools and methodologies used to assess obesogenic environments at the neighborhood level. Studies in LMICs should be promoted, with GIS as a predominant tool for evaluating food and physical activity environments. For researchers, methodological diversity such as combining GIS with surveys, audits, and other approaches may contribute to more comprehensive and contextually relevant assessments. For practitioners and policymakers, these findings could help inform the design of tailored interventions and monitoring systems that take into account the accessibility and availability of food and physical activity facilities. Such policies could potentially enhance monitoring and provide insights to help address environmental determinants of NCDs.
