Association between trajectories of physical activity and depressive symptoms among Chinese older adults with chronic disease: evidence from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey
Authors: Jinghong Huang, Xiaojun Liu, Seung Chun Paek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhysical activity (PA) has been associated with depressive symptoms (DS), yet most evidence relies on single time-point assessments and general populations. It remains unclear whether long-term trajectories of PA are associated with the odds of DS among older adults with chronic disease (participants with at least one chronic disease), including those with multimorbidity (participants with two or more chronic diseases). Therefore, this study aimed to examine the association between trajectories of PA and DS with chronic disease.MethodsA total of 854 older adults (≥ 65 years) with chronic disease (participants with at least one chronic disease) derived from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) in three waves 2011–2012, 2014, and 2017–2018. PA trajectories were identified using group-based trajectory modeling across three waves. Participants who were free of DS at baseline were followed up at the third wave to assess DS status. Multivariable-adjusted binary logistic regression models were applied to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and health-related covariates. Subgroup analyses were conducted among individuals with multimorbidity and specific chronic diseases.ResultsThree distinct PA trajectories were identified: low level and decreasing, moderate level and decreasing, and high level and decreasing. Compared with the low level and decreasing trajectory, both moderate level and decreasing and high level and decreasing trajectories were associated with lower odds of DS. These associations were generally consistent among individuals with multimorbidity. For specific chronic diseases, statistically significant associations were observed in participants with hypertension, lung disease, stroke, and arthritis, while similar trends were noted but did not reach statistical significance in other conditions.ConclusionsMaintaining favorable long-term patterns of PA, even with gradual decline, is associated with reduced odds of DS among older adults with chronic diseases. These findings support PA as a potentially cost-effective strategy for secondary prevention and highlight the importance of sustained behavioral patterns in mitigating mental health risks in aging populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07797-w.

Introduction

With the rapid aging process of the worldwide population, the burden of depressive symptoms (DS) in the older adults on individuals, families, and society is increasing, and high detection of DS exaggerate the loss of healthy life expectancy [1]. Existing evidence indicates DS often typically lead to somatic symptoms of emotional distress, leading to multisystem comorbidity in individuals [2], such as co-occurring physical, psychological, and cognitive multimorbidity [3, 4], and further contributing to functional disability [5]. Therefore, it is essential that effective and modifiable prevention strategies of DS are targeted for older adults.

Physical activity (PA) is defined as nay movement of the body produced by skeletal muscles that results in a measurable increase in energy consumption [6]. Increasing evidence found that individuals who keep PA have a decreased risk of DS. Evidence from Schuch et al. [7] suggested a significant inverse relationship between PA and depression, specifically, a meta-analysis of forty-nine prospective studies found that high PA levels correlate with a 17% lower risk of depression. This conclusion is reinforced by various other meta-analytic reviews that identify physical inactivity as a notable risk factor for the condition [8, 9]. And, small amounts of fragmented PA have still proven effective for the condition [10]. However, existing literature yielded inconsistent findings regarding this correlation; consequently, the link between PA and DS has yet to be firmly established [11, 12]. Moreover, although most prior research found how baseline PA associated with DS [13–18], the role of PA change over time is still unclear. It is as PA always present the dynamic characteristics over time [19–21], and reduces concern about residual confounding for PA methodologically. It is more reasonable to explore the role on DS through identifying the long-term trajectories of PA.

Exploring PA-based intervention strategies to reduce DS is particularly important among older adults with chronic diseases. Compared with the general older population, individuals with chronic diseases are more vulnerable to DS due to accumulated symptom burden, functional limitations, reduced mobility, long-term treatment demands, and health-related stressors. This issue may become even more pronounced among individuals with multimorbidity, where the coexistence of multiple chronic conditions substantially increases physical, psychological, cognitive multimorbidity, and social burden [3, 4]. Moreover, some chronic diseases, particularly those associated with mobility limitation, pain burden, dyspnea, or disability-related symptoms, may make sustained engagement in PA both more challenging and more clinically relevant for psychological well-being. In this context, DS may not only impair quality of life, but also worsen self-management behaviors, treatment adherence, and disease progression, thereby contributing to more complex physical-psychological multimorbidity states [2].

Within current chronic disease management frameworks, secondary prevention aims not only to delay disease progression and reduce complications, but also to preserve psychological well-being and functional capacity. PA has been recognized as a potentially accessible and cost-effective strategy that may provide simultaneous physical and psychological benefits in populations with chronic diseases [22]. However, existing studies have primarily focused on the association between baseline PA and DS in the general population [13–18], leaving uncertainty regarding whether long-term patterns of PA are similarly associated with DS among older adults with chronic condition, including those with multimorbidity and specific chronic conditions.

This evidence gap is particularly relevant in China, where rapid population aging and increasing multimorbidity continue to place substantial pressure on healthcare system and long-term care resource. As chronic diseases progress and accumulate, healthcare needs become increasingly complex and costly. Therefore, identifying accessible and cost-effective behaviors strategies, such as maintaining favorable PA patterns, may help reduce the psychological burden associated with chronic disease and support healthy aging in resource-constrained settings.

To address these research gaps, we used data from a nationally representative survey in China to examine the association between long-term trajectories of PA over 7-year and DS among older adults with chronic diseases (participants with at least one chronic disease). This investigation also responds to ongoing national health initiatives, such as “Healthy China 2030”, which emphasize PA promotion and chronic disease management as key components of healthy aging strategies. By evaluating longitudinal patterns of PA rather than relying solely on single baseline measurements, our study aims to provide more robust evidence to inform targeted and cost-effective intervention strategies for older adults with chronic diseases.

Methods

Study design and participants

This longitudinal study was conducted within the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), which is an ongoing nationally representative population-based survey and covered the countries and cities in 23 provinces in Chinese older adults. Details of study design and sampling have been well-documented in prior research [23]. Briefly, the baseline of CLHLS was performed in 1998 and followed up and further recruitments of new participants in 2000, 2002, 2005, 2008–2009, 2011–2012, 2014, and 2017–2018 [24]. For this current study, we used the 3 waves of CLHLS 2011–2018 data. These waves were selected because comparable measurements of PA were consistently available across the 2011–2012, 2014, and 2017–2018 surveys, enabling the identification of longitudinal PA trajectories over time.

Several exclusion criteria were established: (1) Age < 65 years (baseline); (2) missing formation on PA during CLHLS 2011–2018; (3) participants without chronic diseases (CLHLS 2011–2012, baseline); (4) participants with DS (CLHLS 2011–2012, baseline), and (5) missing formation on DS in CLHLS 2017–2018 (Fig. 1).

In this present study, we included seven types of self-reported physician-diagnosed major chronic diseases from these abovementioned surveys: hypertension, diabetes, cancer, lung disease, heart disease (including heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems), stroke, and arthritis [25–27]. If participants reported 2 or more chronic disease, they are classified as having multimorbidity. Because only 10 participants reported a physician diagnosis of cancer at baseline, cancer-specific analyses were not performed due to insufficient statistical power for reliable estimation.

Exposure

Trajectories of PA were the exposure of interest. The definition of PA follows previous research [24, 28]. PA is assessed at same questionnaire and criteria in CLHLS 2011–2012, 2014, and 2017–2018, respectively.

Specifically, PA was assessed through a combination of nine activity frequencies: regular exercise, housework tasks, personal outdoor activities, gardening, reading, rearing domestic animals/pets, playing cards/mah-jongg, watching television/listening to the radio, and attending social activities. According to the frequency of various physical activities, this study calculated the total PA score, ranging from 0 to 18. The higher PA score presents higher level PA. Details of PA score are provided in Table S1.

Outcome

The outcome of interest is DS. DS is measured at different scales in in CLHLS 2011–2012 and 2017–2018, separately.

For CLHLS 2011–2012, DS was measured through the Phenotypes and eXposures (PhenX) toolkit, which was developed by integrating criteria from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (5th edition; DSM-5) and the Comprehensive International Diagnostic Interview Screening Scale (CIDI-SC) [29, 30]. It utilizes two concise binary (yes or no) self-report items to evaluate participants’ experiences in the past year: (1) persistent feelings of sadness or depression lasting at least 14 days, and (2) a diminished interest in routine activities or hobbies. The presence of DS is identified if a respondent provides a positive response (yes) to either question.

For CLHLS 2017–2018, DS was assessed through the 10-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10), which have been widely applied in Chinese older adults [31] and has excellent validity and reliability in CLHLS [32]. The CESD-10 scale includes 10 items, including 8 negative items were coded as 0 (< 1 day), 1 (1–2 days), 2 (3–4 days), and 3 (5–7 days), while two item (feeling hopeful and happy) were reverse-coded. Based on these calculation criteria, the total score of CESD-10 ranges from 0 to 30, a higher score presents more severe symptoms. According to previous research, the presence of DS is defined as the total score ≥ 10 [33].

Although distinct measurement tools were used to measure DS at baseline and endpoint, the PhenX and the CESD-10 have excellent effectiveness and reliability, and this approach has been widely used in previous studies [34, 35]. Detailed information on the PhenX and the CESD-10 are provided in Table S2.

Covariates

Covariates were assessed at the baseline in this study, including age (continuous), sex (male, female), place of residence (urban, rural), marital status (in marriage, not in marriage), education attainment (< 1 year, 1–6 years, and > 6 years), sufficient financial support (yes, no), occupation before age 60 (professional work, others), coresidence (alone, not alone), diet (healthy, unhealthy), smoking (current non-smoking, others), drinking (non-harmful alcohol consumption, others), body mass index (< 18.5, 18.5–23.9, 24.0-27.9, and ≥ 28.0), and number of chronic diseases (1, ≥ 2).

According to previous research, this study used simplified healthy eating (SHE) index to assess the diet [24, 36]. The SHE includes 9 common food types: fresh fruits, fresh vegetables, legumes, garlic, tea, meat, fish, eggs, and preserved vegetables. The total score of SHE index was calculated through the frequency of consumption of each food types. The total score ranges from 0 to 18, higher score indicates more healthy diet. The distribution of total score of SHE index in the top two-fifth as the healthy diet, otherwise unhealthy diet [28]. Body mass index was calculated through weight (kg) divided by height (m2). Details of covariate definitions were provided in Table S3 and Table S4.

Statistical analyses

In this current study, trajectories of PA were mapped from the CHLHS 2011–2018 using Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) to capture population heterogeneity in PA trend. By treating the data collection years as the time axis and assuming a censored normal distribution for the continuous PA measures, we identified latent subgroups with distinct development paths of PA over time. The analysis was performed in STATA, leveraging the traj package for model fitting and trajplot for visual representation [37, 38].

The optimal number of latent trajectory groups was determined through an interactive process, testing configurations from one to four classes. According to prior studies [39, 40], several selections of the final model and its associated functional forms was guided by multi-dimensional criteria: (1) minimizing the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), aiming for absolute values closest to zero; (2) ensuring a high degree of classification accuracy with an Average Posterior Probability (AvePP) exceeding 70%; and (3) confirming the theoretical and real plausibility of the identified patterns.

The participants’ characteristics were grouped by trajectories of PA and presented as frequencies (proportion) for categorical variables and as the median (interquartile range) for continuous variable. The chi-square tests and analysis of variance (ANONA) was used to assess the differences between trajectory groups, separately for categorical and continuous variables.

Multivariable-adjusted binary logistic regression models were used to estimate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association of trajectories of PA with DS. Two models were considered in this study: (1) Model 1 is the crude model, including the exposure of interest. (2) Model 2 is the adjusted model, additionally adjusting age, sex, place of residence, marital status, education attainment, sufficient financial support, occupation before age 60, living alone, diet, smoking, drinking, body mass index, and number of chronic diseases. The interpretation of the results is based on model 2. These models were not only performed in the whole study sample with chronic disease (regardless of the number of chronic diseases), but also among older adults with multimorbidity, to test whether the protective role of PA for DS in the context of higher burden of chronic disease is still valid. Moreover, we also conducted same analyses among older adults with specific chronic disease (including hypertension, diabetes, cancer, lung disease, heart disease, stroke, and arthritis).

Moreover, sensitivity analyses based on complete-case datasets were performed to examine the robustness of the findings with respect to the imputation approach. Missing values in covariates were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) under the missing-at-random assumption, with five imputed datasets generated. Estimates were subsequently combined using Rubin’s rules to obtain overall effect estimates [41].

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata, version MP18 (StataCorp LLC). Statistical significance is P < 0.05 (2-tailed).

Results

Systematic evaluation of the model selection parameters led to the adoption of a 3-group solution, with each trajectory modeled using a second-order polynomial shape (2 2 2). This configuration achieved the superior balance between statistical rigor and substantive interpretability. The underlying fit statistics supporting this selection are provided in Table S5. Three trajectories of PA were identified and characterized by “Low level and decreasing” (n = 106, 12.41%), “Moderate level and decreasing” (n = 551, 64.52%), and “High level and decreasing” (n = 197, 23.07%) (Fig. 2). Among these trajectories of PA, they have similarity in decreasing trend over time, but the difference is having distinct PA level at baseline.

Table 2 presents the longitudinal association between trajectories of PA and DS among older adults with chronic disease. For the analysis among older adults with chronic disease (including all participants with at least one chronic disease), participants with both moderate level and decreasing and high level and decreasing trajectories had lower odds of experiencing DS, than those with the low level and decreasing trajectory, especially for the high level and decreasing trajectory of PA. Specifically, there were 52% lower odds of DS in the moderate level and decreasing trajectory (OR = 0.48, 95%CI = 0.29–0.80) and 80% lower odds of DS in the high level and decreasing trajectory (OR = 0.20, 95%CI = 0.10–0.41). Meanwhile, we further explored these association in participants with multimorbidity (participants with two or more chronic diseases), and then the similar findings were found. Compared to participants with the low level and decreasing trajectory, those with both moderate level and decreasing (OR = 0.17, 95%CI = 0.06–0.47) and high level and decreasing (OR = 0.12, 95%CI = 0.04–0.41) had lower odds of experiencing DS (Table 2). In the sensitivity analysis of multiple imputation, similar findings were found (Table S6).

Discussion

In this study, three different long-term trajectories of PA were identified. Older Chinese adults with chronic disease (regardless of number) and healthier trajectories (moderate level and decreasing, and high level and decreasing) of PA were less likely to experience DS than low level and decreasing PA trajectory. Similar findings were also found in the older adults with multimorbidity and several specific chronic diseases. The main findings demonstrate that maintaining relatively favorable PA may be important for reducing the odds of DS among older adults with chronic diseases.

Substantial epidemiological evidence has suggested the association between PA at single time point and DS. Short-term (3 months) observational study among 755 adults in the United States indicated that moderate-vigorous PA at baseline significantly correlated with lower DS (r=-0.19) [13], a finding mirrored in multi-year cohort studies across both Western and Asian populations. A 13-year cohort study involving 10,325 adults in Norway found that light (β = 0.11) or moderate-vigorous PA (β = 0.19) played a protective role against psychological problem during follow-up [16], while another 32-year cohort study among 676 women in Gothenburg, Sweden, showed that lower baseline PA levels were associated with higher depression scores [18]. Recent prospective cohort studies conducted in South Korea and China have also found significant associations between high PA levels and lower odds of DS [15, 17]. However, this association has not been universally corroborated, as some observational studies found that the predictive effect of baseline PA diminished over time [11, 12]. These discrepancies may partly reflect the limitations of relying on single baseline assessments of PA, which cannot account for long-term behavioral patterns. By incorporating repeated PA assessments over three survey waves, the present study extends previous evidence by examining sustained PA patterns during older adulthood rather than relying solely on static baseline measures. However, the identified trajectory groups mainly differed in overall PA level rather than in the rate of decline, as all groups showed generally decreasing patterns over time. Therefore, the observed associations may primarily reflect the long-term maintenance of relatively higher PA levels during older adulthood, rather than the independent effect of decline slope. This trajectory-based approach provides additional insight into the association between sustained PA patterns and DS in later life [19–21]. Moreover, given that the potential bidirectional relationships between PA and DS, we followed-up the DS-free population at baseline to strengthen the temporal sequence between trajectories of PA and subsequent DS [31].

Focusing on populations with chronic diseases, particularly those with multimorbidity, is clinically important because these populations often experience greater symptom burden, reduced mobility, functional limitation, and psychological vulnerability. In this context, DS may contribute to poorer self-management, reduced treatment adherence, and worsening health status. Our findings therefore support the potential role of PA as a practical strategy within secondary prevention frameworks for chronic disease management. These findings are also consistent with current public health priorities, including the World Health Organization recommendations and the “Healthy China 2030” initiative, both of which emphasize PA promotion and healthy aging. Importantly, our findings suggest that maintaining achievable and sustainable PA patterns during older adulthood, rather than pursuing high-intensity activity levels, may still provide psychological benefits among individuals living with chronic diseases [42–44].

When examining specific chronic diseases, some heterogeneity in associations was observed. It should be noted that these disease-specific subgroup analyses were not mutually exclusive; therefore, participants with multimorbidity could be included in more than one chronic disease subgroup. In this study, we included seven major physician-diagnosed chronic conditions, including hypertension, diabetes, cancer, lung disease, heart disease, stroke, and arthritis. While the overall direction of association between favorable PA trajectories and lower odds of DS was generally consistent across chronic disease groups, statistically significant associations were primarily observed among individuals with hypertension, lung disease, stroke, and arthritis. Several disease-specific mechanisms may explain these findings. For individuals with hypertension, PA contributes to improved vascular health, better blood pressure control, and reduced systemic inflammation. Regarding respiratory health, PA among those with lung disease may improve exercise tolerance and reduce further adverse health outcome associated with respiratory symptoms. For those managing stroke, PA can facilitate functional recovery, enhance neuroplasticity, and promote social participation, thereby reducing vulnerability to DS. Finally, among individuals with arthritis, PA has been shown to alleviate pain, improve mobility, and strengthen self-efficacy, all of which are closely linked to enhanced psychological well-being [2, 45, 46]. Beyond these biological mechanisms, the larger sample size of these specific subgroups likely provided greater statistical power to detect these associations. In contrast, no statistically significant associations were observed among individuals with diabetes and heart disease after full adjustment. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. The diabetes subgroup was relatively small (n = 69), resulting in wide confidence intervals and limited statistical power. For heart disease, the associations detected in crude analyses were attenuated after multivariable adjustment. This attenuation suggests that demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related factors may partly account for the initial relationship between PA trajectories and DS. Therefore, the absence of statistical significance in these chronic disease groups should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of a lack of association. Furthermore, the reliability and validity of these findings, particularly within the diabetes subgroup, should be evaluated in future studies with larger sample sizes.

Taken together, these findings suggest that maintaining relatively favorable and achievable PA patterns during older adulthood may be associated with lower odds of DS among individuals with chronic diseases. From a public health perspective, promoting sustainable PA engagement may represent a practical strategy to support psychological well-being in aging populations with increasing multimorbidity burden.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, leveraging a large, nationally representative cohort from China enhances the generalizability of our findings to older adults in real-world settings. Second, by adopting a trajectory-based approach to characterize long-term patterns of PA over a 7-year period, this study moves beyond conventional single time-point assessments and captures the dynamic nature of behavioral exposure, thereby providing a more nuanced understanding of its relationship with the odds of DS. Third, the focus on individuals with chronic diseases, including those with multimorbidity, addresses an important yet underexplored high-risk population and aligns closely with current priorities in secondary prevention. In addition, the consistency of findings across multiple subgroups, including several specific chronic conditions, further supports the robustness of the observed associations.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, PA, DS and chronic diseases were assessed based on self-reported measures, which may be subject to recall bias and misclassification. Second, although we accounted for a range of potential confounders and restricted analyses to participants free of DS at baseline to strengthen temporal inference, residual confounding and reverse causation cannot be entirely excluded due to the observational nature of the study. Third, despite the discrepancy in depression scales employed during the 2011–2012 and 2017–2018 waves, the empirical consistency of these measures has been rigorously validated for CLHLS [34, 35]. Fourth, information on the severity and duration of chronic diseases was not available, which may have contributed to heterogeneity in the observed associations across disease groups. Fifth, while trajectory modeling provides valuable insights into long-term patterns, it may still mask within-group variability and does not fully capture short-term fluctuations in PA behavior. Sixth, PA trajectories were evaluated during older adulthood only. Therefore, the identified trajectory patterns may not capture the potential influence of PA exposure during earlier stages of life, such as midlife, which may also contribute to the development of DS in later life. Seventh, covariates were assessed at baseline only, although several socioeconomic, lifestyle, and health-related characteristics may change over time in older adults. Future studies incorporating time-varying covariates are warranted to further clarify these longitudinal associations. Finally, the generalizability of these findings to non-Chinese populations or younger age groups requires further validation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings highlight the importance of considering long-term patterns of PA, rather than single time-point assessments, in understanding the odds of DS among older adults with chronic diseases. Maintaining relatively favorable long-term patterns of PA during older adulthood was associated with lower odds of DS, including among individuals with multimorbidity and several specific chronic diseases. These findings support the importance of sustained and achievable PA engagement as a practical strategy for chronic disease management and healthy aging.
