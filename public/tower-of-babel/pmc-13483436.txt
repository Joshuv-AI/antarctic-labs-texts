Axial compression behavior of square concrete-filled steel tubular columns reinforced with CFRP-ECC hybrid composite
Authors: Ziwang Xiao, Lifeng Wang, Rikang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConcrete-Filled Steel Tube (CFST) columns often encounter challenges such as insufficient compressive capacity, susceptibility to local buckling, and limited ductility. To mitigate these issues, a combined reinforcement approach using Carbon Fiber Reinforced Polymer (CFRP) and Engineered Cementitious Composites (ECC), termed Composite Enhanced Steel-Confined (CESC), is proposed. This paper investigates the axial compressive behavior of CESC through numerical simulation. A refined finite element model is developed and validated against experimental load–displacement curves, failure modes, and ultimate capacities. Based on this validation, a parametric study is conducted to examine the effects of CFRP mesh size, steel tube width-to-thickness ratio, and core concrete strength on the axial compression performance of CESC. Results show that reducing the CFRP mesh size from 40 to 10 mm enhances ultimate capacity by 42.1% and effectively suppresses local buckling. Increasing the width-to-thickness ratio from 31.5 to 80.4 improves bearing capacity by 54.1% and the ductility coefficient by approximately 30%. Raising concrete strength from C30 to C70 increases capacity by 48.8%, though ductility improvement remains limited. Furthermore, multiple linear regression is employed to develop a predictive model for bearing capacity, and a modified theoretical formula is proposed for practical design of CESC columns.Keywords: Carbon fiber reinforced polymer, Engineered cementitious composites, Concrete-filled steel tube column, Numerical simulation, Axial compression behaviorSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Concrete-Filled Steel Tube (CFST) columns, as a representative type of steel–concrete composite structures, have been extensively utilized in building structures owing to their remarkable load-bearing capacity and construction efficiency1–3. Figure 1 demonstrates an application example of CFST columns in bridge engineering. However, with the evolution of building structures towards super-high-rise and large-span designs, the limitations of traditional CFST columns in terms of ultimate bearing capacity, seismic performance, and ductility have become increasingly evident. Specifically, square cross-section CFST columns lack the uniform confinement effect inherent in circular cross-sections, leading to a reduced confining effect of the steel tube on the core concrete. Under axial compression and combined bending loads, square cross-section CFST columns are more prone to failure modes such as local buckling and weld tearing4. Despite this, the majority of current research predominantly focuses on the performance evaluation of circular cross-section CFST columns, while studies on square cross-section CFST columns remain relatively limited5–8. These constraints not only hinder the broader application of CFST columns in complex engineering scenarios but may also result in premature degradation of structural performance. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop innovative reinforcement techniques that can substantially enhance both the load-bearing capacity and ductility of square CFST columns within the context of modern architectural practices.

Application scenarios of CFST columns.

In recent years, high-performance composite materials have emerged as a significant research focus in the field of structural reinforcement, primarily due to the limitations of traditional reinforcement methods concerning construction complexity, durability, and potential damage to the original structure. Among these materials, Engineered Cementitious Composites (ECC) stands out for its exceptional ductility, multi-crack formation characteristics, and strain-hardening behavior. ECC can effectively alleviate the brittle failure of concrete while significantly enhancing the ductility and durability of structures, showcasing considerable application potential in the reinforcement of CFST columns9–12. However, when used independently for reinforcement purposes, ECC’s confining effect on core concrete is relatively limited, thereby failing to fully leverage its superior properties. In contrast, Carbon Fiber Reinforced Polymer (CFRP), characterized by its high tensile strength, lightweight, and excellent corrosion resistance, can provide uniform circumferential confinement to core concrete through a grid configuration. Moreover, the longitudinal fibers of CFRP exhibit high stiffness, effectively suppressing local buckling of steel tubes. This synergy leads to a substantial improvement in overall stability and load-bearing capacity13–17. Consequently, integrating CFRP with ECC for CFST column reinforcement enables the complementary advantages of both materials, further enhancing the comprehensive performance of CFST columns18–21.

The existing research predominantly centers on the impact analysis of single parameters or a limited set of variables, with insufficient systematic investigation into key parameters. Although Huang et al.22 elucidated the influence of CFRP grid spacing on confinement efficiency, their parameter range does not comprehensively address the requirements for refined design. Moreover, they did not thoroughly examine the role of ECC in mitigating crack propagation, leaving unresolved issues regarding stress concentration at corners. The correlation theory proposed by Manigandan et al.23 concerning the width-to-thickness ratio of steel tubes and confinement effects is only applicable to pure CFRP wrapping systems and has not been validated within the context of the CFRP-ECC composite confinement mechanism. This limitation results in a lack of theoretical support for ECC’s compensatory effect on local buckling in thin-walled steel tube reinforcement. Hu et al.24 confined their study of ECC to a unidirectional relationship between material strength and confinement effects, neglecting the enhancement provided by ECC’s high ductility in resisting brittle failure.

Moreover, traditional experimental methods for investigating the axial compressive behavior of reinforced concrete columns frequently encounter challenges such as high costs and significant time consumption. These limitations impede the detailed characterization of complex interface behaviors and multi-parameter coupling effects. In contrast, numerical simulation has exhibited substantial advantages and reliability in structural reinforcement analysis. Ahmed et al.25 validated the feasibility of their numerical model by comparing the simulation results of CFRP-wrapped cylinders with experimental data. Zheng et al.18 further integrated the tensile hardening behavior of ECC into their finite element model, achieving high-precision simulations of multiple crack propagation paths with an error margin below 8%. Through the construction of refined numerical models, it becomes possible to efficiently quantify the interactions among multiple variables and accurately capture material nonlinear responses as well as damage evolution processes, thereby enabling a more comprehensive analysis of complex mechanical behaviors.

The literature review reveals that, despite the widespread application of CFRP and ECC in the reinforcement of CFST columns, research on the combined reinforcement of square CFST columns using both CFRP and ECC remains relatively limited. Moreover, existing studies predominantly concentrate on the analysis of single parameters or a restricted set of variables, lacking a comprehensive investigation into the effects of CFRP mesh size, steel tube width-to-thickness ratio, and core concrete strength on axial compressive behavior. To further explore the axial compressive behavior of square CFST columns, this paper proposes an innovative reinforcement method involving the embedding of CFRP mesh within ECC to jointly reinforce CFST columns, thereby forming a composite reinforced square CFST column (CESC). Initially, numerical models are developed using ABAQUS software, and their reliability is validated by comparing simulation results with experimental data. Based on this foundation, in-depth analyses are conducted focusing on key design parameters: CFRP mesh size, steel tube width-to-thickness ratio, and core concrete strength. Additionally, multiple linear regression techniques are employed to quantify the contribution of each parameter to axial load-bearing capacity. Finally, a load-bearing capacity calculation model specifically tailored for CESC is proposed based on modifications to the theoretical equations.

Elements and boundary conditions

The finite element model for axial compression, developed using ABAQUS software, enables a comprehensive evaluation of the progressive failure process and damage mechanisms of experimental columns, accurately capturing the nonlinear behavior of materials34,35. Specifically, solid elements (C3D8R) are utilized for modeling the core concrete and ECC layer. Shell elements (S4R) are employed for the steel tube, and membrane elements (M3D8R) are assigned to the CFRP mesh to account for its in-plane anisotropic characteristics. In terms of interface simulation, interactions between the steel tube and core concrete, as well as between the steel tube and ECC layer, are defined using a "Surface-Surface" contact approach. The normal contact behavior is characterized as "hard contact," while tangential contact follows Coulomb’s friction law. Detailed design parameters are summarized in Table 6. When the friction coefficient varies within the range of 0.5 to 0.7, the fluctuation of the ultimate bearing capacity is less than 3%, indicating that the model is not sensitive to this parameter. Furthermore, collaborative deformation between the CFRP mesh and ECC is achieved through embedded constraints. Regarding boundary conditions, the base of the column is fully constrained, while an axial load is applied at its top end. The established finite element model is presented in Fig. 5.

Mesh sensitivity analysis

Grid sensitivity is among the critical factors that significantly influence the reliability of results in finite element analysis36. The choice of mesh size has a substantial impact on both the accuracy and computational efficiency of numerical simulations. An excessively coarse mesh may result in inadequate spatial resolution for capturing local stress concentrations and damage evolution, thereby leading to inaccuracies in simulating key mechanical behaviors. Conversely, while a finer mesh can improve simulation accuracy, it also considerably increases computational costs and may potentially induce numerical instability due to element distortion. To systematically assess grid sensitivity, this study employed four distinct mesh sizes: 8 mm, 10 mm, 15 mm, and 20 mm for numerical simulations and conducted a comparative analysis by comparing the simulation results with experimental data4.

The results of the mesh sensitivity analysis are illustrated in Fig. 6. For L2-t2-C40, as shown in Fig. 6(a), when the mesh size is reduced from 20 to 15 mm, the error in the peak load obtained from numerical simulations decreases from 18 to 3%. However, a notable deviation persists in capturing local buckling and concrete crushing processes, with an error of approximately 12% during the descending phase. Further reducing the mesh size to 10 mm optimizes the peak load error to 1.8%, and the descending phase response closely aligns with the experimental curve, effectively characterizing the synergistic damage mechanism between CFRP grid fracture and ECC crack propagation. When the mesh size is further decreased to 8 mm, computational time increases by approximately 150%, significantly impairing computational efficiency.

Results of grid sensitivity analysis: (a) L2-t2-C40; (b) t2-C40.

For t2-C40, as shown in Fig. 6(b), the mesh sensitivity exhibits different characteristics. When the mesh size is reduced from 20 to 15 mm, the peak load error is merely 2.3%, significantly lower than the 18% observed for the CESC specimen, indicating that the CFRP-ECC composite strengthening layer introduces additional material interface nonlinearities and damage evolution pathways, thereby raising the requirement for mesh resolution. Further reducing the mesh size to 10 mm yields a peak load error of 3.1%, and the descending phase response closely aligns with that obtained using the 15 mm mesh, effectively characterizing the evolution of local buckling modes in the steel tube. When the mesh size is further decreased to 8 mm, computational time increases by approximately 280%, while the error only decreases from 3.1% to 3.4%, resulting in significantly lower cost-effectiveness compared to the optimization process for the CESC specimen. Considering both accuracy and efficiency comprehensively, this study adopts a mesh size of 10 mm for model discretization in subsequent analyses.

Results and discussion

Finite element model validation

Load–displacement curve

The comparison of the load–displacement curves for L2-t2-C40EXP and L2-t2-C40FE is presented in Fig. 7(a). The experimental curve comprehensively documents the entire process of CESC, from elastic response through plastic damage to final failure. The OA segment represents the elastic phase, during which no cracking occurs in the CESC specimen. Point A corresponds to a yield load of 1602.1 kN, indicating that the steel tube and core concrete are functioning collaboratively. The AB segment marks the elastoplastic phase, during which the core concrete gradually fragments, with point B reaching a load of 2101.3 kN. In the BC segment, the composite confinement effect of CFRP-ECC becomes significantly pronounced. Point C corresponds to a peak load of 1959.7 kN, demonstrating effective restraint against local buckling of the steel tube. As ECC begins to crack and the core concrete undergoes crushing, the curve progressively declines to point D at approximately 1749.8 kN, signifying the failure of the specimen.

Load–displacement curve: (a) L2-t2-C40; (b) t2-C40.

Figure 7(b) presents the comparison of the load–displacement curves for t2-C40EXP and t2-C40FE. The experimental curve similarly exhibits three-stage characteristics, yet the mechanical behavior differs markedly from that of the CESC specimen. The OA elastic phase is shorter, with a yield load of 1125.3 kN at point A. During the AB elastoplastic phase, core concrete crushing and steel tube local buckling occur almost simultaneously, and the load drops rapidly after reaching a peak load of 1286.4 kN at point B, with no distinct plateau segment. The post-peak softening behavior in the BC segment is extremely steep, with the load plummeting to 856.2 kN at point C with an increase in displacement of only 0.3 mm, exhibiting typical brittle failure characteristics.

The finite element model results generally agree with the experimental trends, albeit with some local discrepancies. For the CESC specimen, the slope of segment OA’ in the L2-t2-C40FE curve is slightly higher than the experimental value. The yield load at point A’ is 1499.5 kN, which is 6.3% lower than the experimental value. The load at point B’ is 1750.3 kN, representing a 7.9% reduction from the experimental value. The ultimate load at point C’ is 1949.8 kN, with an error of only 0.5% compared to the experimental result. These observations indicate that the model reliably captures the composite confinement effect, albeit with a slight underestimation of the initial elastic stiffness. In the descending phase C’D', the load reduction rate predicted by the finite element model is relatively milder, with a load of 1901.1 kN at point D’, corresponding to an error of 8.6%. This discrepancy primarily stems from the model’s insufficient consideration of the stochastic distribution of micro-cracks in the concrete and the potential local delamination of the CFRP grid. Additionally, slight non-uniformities in load application during the experiments, whereas uniform loading is assumed in the numerical simulations, may also contribute to the observed deviations. Despite these minor deviations, the numerical model demonstrates excellent agreement with experimental results in predicting both yield load and peak load. The above findings confirm that the finite element model can effectively simulate the mechanical behavior of CESC.

Failure modes

The crack distribution of L2-t2-C40, as revealed through both finite element simulations and experimental investigations, is vividly depicted in Fig. 8(a), showcasing a remarkable degree of congruence between the two methodologies. Both the numerical analysis and empirical findings illuminate that cracks predominantly coalesce at the corners and mid-edge regions of the column, propagating vertically along its height. As the load escalates, the width and length of these fissures incrementally expand. Notably, the cracks in the corner regions grow increasingly dense, while those in the mid-edge zones retain a relatively sparse arrangement. From the perspective of failure modes, the damage characteristics of the column elucidate that stress concentration phenomena induce fractures within the CFRP mesh at the corners of square columns, which in turn precipitate cracking in ECC within these critical areas. The crack distribution of t2-C40 is presented in Fig. 8(b), also demonstrating strong agreement between simulation and experiment. CFRP mesh size

The influence of CFRP grid size on the axial compressive behavior of CESC was meticulously explored through systematic adjustments to the dimensions of the CFRP grid. The ductility coefficient, yield load, and ultimate load of CESC are comprehensively detailed in Table 9. As illustrated in Fig. 9(b), when compared to CESC-20–80.4–40, an increase in the CFRP grid size leads to a notable reduction in ultimate loads for CESC-30–80.4–40 and CESC-40–80.4–40, diminishing from 1991.2 to 1855.9 and 1809.5 kN respectively, while ultimate displacements contract correspondingly from 2.1 to 2.0 and 1.8 mm. This underscores that the integration of a CFRP grid into CESC significantly elevates its ultimate bearing capacity and deformation capability. Moreover, as the size of the CFRP grid diminishes, this enhancement effect becomes increasingly pronounced. Further analysis reveals that, relative to CESC-20–80.4–40, reducing the CFRP grid size results in a marked increase in the ultimate load for CESC-10–80.4–40, ascending from 1991.2 to 2123.3 kN, with ultimate displacement expanding from 2.1 to 2.4 mm. Simultaneously, a conspicuous strain hardening phenomenon is observed post-peak load on its curve, attributable to the uniform circumferential confinement provided by densely arranged CFRP grids, which effectively mitigates local buckling of steel tubes and crushing failure of core concrete. Additionally, significant disparities in failure characteristics were discerned during the failure stage of specimens, as depicted in Fig. 11: larger CFRP grid sizes correlate with progressively severe damage levels within the core concrete. Specifically, both CESC-10–80.4–40 and CESC-20–80.4–40 exhibit relatively gentle descending segments during their loading decline phase, achieving ductility coefficients as high as 2.7, whereas insufficient local confinement in CESC-30–80.4–40 and CESC-40–80.4–40 induces a precipitous drop-off in loading, resulting in ductility coefficients hovering around 1.5. Comparative results indicate that decreasing CFRP grid sizes can effectively enhance both bearing capacity and deformation ability within CESC. However, further reductions beyond certain thresholds lead to diminishing returns regarding these improvements. This conclusion harmonizes seamlessly with experimental findings reported by Yang et al.37.

Concrete strength

The study delves into the profound impact of adjusting the core concrete strength on the axial compression behavior of CESC. The ductility coefficient, yield load, and ultimate load of CESC are meticulously detailed in Table 10. As vividly illustrated in Fig. 9(c), when compared to CESC-20–80.4–40, an elevation in the core concrete grade from C40 to C50 results in a notable increase in the ultimate load of CESC-20–80.4–50, ascending from 1991.2 kN to 2015.3 kN, while the ultimate displacement expands gracefully from 2.1 to 2.6 mm. This underscores that enhancing core concrete strength markedly elevates both the ultimate bearing capacity and deformation capability of CESC. Moreover, this enhancement effect becomes increasingly pronounced with progressively higher grades of concrete. Further analysis reveals that when the core concrete strength is further augmented to 60 and 70 MPa relative to CESC-20–80.4–40, the ultimate loads for CESC-20–80.4–60 and CESC-20–80.4–70 ascend correspondingly from 1991.2 to 2035.3 and 2041.5 kN respectively, while their respective ultimate displacements evolve gracefully from 2.1 to 2.9 mm and subsequently to 3.1 mm. This suggests that although there remains a discernible improvement in ultimate bearing capacity with further enhancements in core concrete strength, there is a subtle decline in deformation capability at exceedingly high strengths, a phenomenon likely attributable to the inherent brittleness of high-strength concrete, which may compromise ductility post-peak load capacity. Additionally, as depicted in Fig. 12, increasing core concrete strength gradually mitigates damage severity within structures, fostering enhanced structural integrity. In summary, elevating core concrete strength can profoundly enhance the ultimate bearing capacity of CESC. However, its influence on deformation capability exhibits a nonlinear characteristic, where exceedingly high-strength concretes may lead to diminished ductility levels. Therefore, it is imperative for engineering applications to prudently select appropriate grades of concrete based on specific requirements for bearing capacity and ductility so as to harmoniously optimize the overall performance characteristics of CESC.

The proposed finite element model demonstrates strong predictive capability. The simulated load–displacement curve and failure mode of specimen CESC-20–80.4–40 agree well with experimental results, validating the model’s applicability for simulating the composite strengthening system.
