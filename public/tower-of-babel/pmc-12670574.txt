EAP: A versatile cloud-based platform for efficient quantitative analysis of large-scale ChIP/ATAC-seq datasets
Authors: Guangyong Zheng, Haojie Chen, Zhijie Guo, Liangxiao Ma, Anqin Zheng, Tao Huang, Weiran Chen, Shiqi Tu, Yixue Li, Zhen Shao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpigenome profiling techniques such as ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq have revolutionized our understanding of gene expression regulation in many biological processes. The rapidly increasing volume of these data necessitates the development of an integrated platform equipped with powerful computational resources and a versatile suite of analytical tools to facilitate in-depth epigenomic analysis. However, most existing cloud-based analysis platforms still require environment configuration, workflow optimization, or even cloud infrastructure management, posing significant barriers for researchers, particularly experimental biologists. To address this demand, we have developed EAP (Epigenomic Analysis Platform; https://www.biosino.org/epigenetics), a scalable web platform based on cloud technology for efficient and reproducible analysis of large-scale ChIP/ATAC-seq datasets. EAP provides a configuration-free environment and extensive downstream functional analysis capabilities, distinguishing it from existing cloud-based ChIP/ATAC-seq analysis tools. Moreover, EAP integrates a curated collection of computational tools, many of which were recently developed by us, supporting both supervised and unsupervised analyses on heterogeneous datasets. This design enables researchers from diverse backgrounds to perform analyses ranging from data preprocessing to tumor subtyping, therapeutic target discovery, and gaining biological insights into epigenomic dynamics during tissue development and disease progression.Keywords: Large-scale ATAC/ChIP-seq data, Cloud-based platform, Quantitative analysis, Cancer epigenomic subtyping, Inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility

Introduction

The epigenome plays a crucial role in modulating gene expression and cellular states in tissue development and diseases [1], [2]. Deep sequencing-based experiments, including ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq, have been widely adopted to profile the epigenetic landscapes in different cellular contexts [3], [4]. With the data, researchers can systematically identify the epigenetic alterations during specific biological processes as well as the associated transcription factors (TFs), gaining invaluable insights into the regulatory mechanisms driving these processes [1], [5], [6].

In recent years, large consortium projects such as ENCODE [7], Epigenome Roadmap [8], and many disease cohort studies [2] have generated a large volume of ChIP/ATAC-seq data that profiled the epigenomes of many different patients, cell types, and tissue development stages, providing great opportunities to broaden our understanding of epigenetic regulation in tissue development and disease heterogeneity. However, with the continuous growth in the scale of data generation, researchers require increasingly more computational resources to analyze such large-scale datasets. Besides, despite the availability of a large number of computational tools for ChIP/ATAC-seq data analysis [9], it remains a formidable challenge for bench-work biologists to deploy and integrate these tools into a practical pipeline, especially when analyzing data generated from a large disease cohort. In such cases, researchers often need to combine a variety of supervised and unsupervised analyses to systematically understand the epigenetic heterogeneity across patients [10], [11], which greatly adds to the complexity of the task.

While cloud platforms provide scalable and flexible computing resources for large-scale data processing, most cloud-based ChIP/ATAC-seq analysis platforms or pipelines still demand environment configuration (e.g., CloudATAC [12]), workflow optimization (e.g., Galaxy [13]), and cloud infrastructure management (e.g., nfcore/atacseq [14]). These requirements pose significant barriers for researchers, particularly experimental biologists. Hence, a web-based, installation-free analysis platform is highly desirable to facilitate user-friendly and accessible cloud computing for epigenomic studies.

To address the challenge, we have developed EAP (Epigenomic Analysis Platform, https://www.biosino.org/epigenetics), a scalable and customizable ChIP/ATAC-seq data analysis platform. Leveraging cloud technology and state-of-the-art computational algorithms, EAP can transform epigenetic signals from a large panel of ChIP/ATAC-seq samples into biologically meaningful and interpretable results [11], [15], [16]. It provides a series of analytical tools with an interactive web interface to facilitate its use by researchers without programming experience, encompassing data preprocessing, supervised differential peak identification, differential TF motif enrichment, differential inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility analysis, unsupervised hypervariable analysis, clustering analysis, scoring signature genes, and so on. These tools have been specifically tailored to accommodate features of large-scale datasets, especially the considerable epigenetic heterogeneity across cancer patients.

The effectiveness of integrating these computational tools into systematic workflows was demonstrated in two cancer-related case studies, which highlighted their capability to detect global epigenomic alterations between tumor and normal tissues, to delineate cancer subtypes based on epigenetic heterogeneity, and to identify associated transcription factors. In addition, a hematopoiesis case study further illustrated the utility of EAP in capturing epigenetic dynamics and identifying key transcription factors during human hematopoiesis. Collectively, these results indicate that EAP provides a powerful framework for data mining and the discovery of biological insights in epigenomic research.

Results

EAP utilizes cloud technology to create a secure and scalable platform for efficient handling of large-scale ChIP/ATAC-seq datasets

EAP has been specifically tailored for quantitatively analyzing large-scale ChIP/ATAC-seq datasets, such as those from cancer cohort studies. Considering the sensitivity of data privacy, all available analytical procedures on EAP must be conducted with a registered approach. For demonstration purposes, a guest account is also provided, allowing users to explore the platform’s features and functionality (Supplementary Figure 1). After account registration and workspace application (Supplementary Figure 2), users can start data analysis by uploading raw sequencing data files in FASTQ format and a metadata file in CSV format, which provides details regarding the study design and sample description of the uploaded dataset. Prior to data uploading, all data must undergo sufficient anonymization and de-identification to ensure compliance with privacy protection standards. EAP provides a data upload tool that supports breakpoint-resumable transfers and parallel file transmission (Supplementary Figure 3a), addressing the challenge of transferring large files. This tool also incorporates an MD5 checksum functionality to verify the integrity of files, ensuring the absence of corrupted data, inconsistent metadata, or missing files (Supplementary Figure 3b).

It is noteworthy that EAP utilizes private cloud technology to create a secure platform for processing, interpreting, and visualizing large-scale ChIP/ATAC-seq datasets, providing scalable computation power suited to the requirements of various types of tasks. In practice, EAP is capable of handling datasets spanning from gigabytes to terabytes in size. Furthermore, docker container technology [17] has been incorporated to facilitate the establishment of flexible analysis environments and end-to-end pipelines, ensuring the reproducibility of analysis results. With this technology, all the analytical tools on EAP are organized into two primary modules for basic and advanced analyses respectively (Fig. 1a). This modular design allows users to efficiently choose the starting point for conducting personal data analysis.

EAP provides a versatile set of computational tools for analyzing large-scale ChIP/ATAC-seq datasets

In EAP, the Basic Analysis (BA) module is designed as an automated pipeline for routine preprocessing of ChIP/ATAC-seq data, including quality control, read alignment, peak calling, and read counting. It incorporates both Bowtie1 [18] and Bowtie2 [19] aligners, as well as MACS1 [20] and MACS2 peak callers, allowing users to select their preferred tool version for data processing. The pipeline currently supports human (hg19 and hg38) and mouse (mm9 and mm10) genomes and is compatible with both paired-end and single-end sequencing data (Supplementary Table 1). Upon completion, this pipeline generates two output files: a report file that summarizes the quality control of data and the statistics regarding read alignment, and a matrix file with a read count table for subsequent analysis. Samples exhibiting a low mapping rate, a high proportion of duplicate reads, or a low TSS enrichment score are flagged as "Concerning" in the report according to the ENCODE standard (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Figure 4). Intermediate outputs are also available in the output directory, with Supplementary Figure 5 illustrating the intermediate results at each step of the module. Users can utilize these intermediate results for additional analyses and visualization.

The Advanced Analysis (AA) module of EAP carries a carefully curated collection of computational tools, providing solutions for most typical needs in analyzing large-scale ChIP/ATAC-seq datasets (Fig. 1c-d). Most of these tools were recently developed by us for a variety of supervised and unsupervised analyses on heterogeneous data [10], [11], [15], [16]. With these tools, researchers can perform in-depth functional analysis based on the read count table from the BA module. Besides, the AA module provides a user-friendly graphical interface for selecting suitable analytical tools, configuring parameters (details of parameters in each tool are available at Supplementary Table 2), and creating customized pipelines to meet personal research needs (Supplementary Figure 6).

For the analysis of large-scale ChIP/ATAC-seq datasets, there are two typical scenarios, referred to as supervised and unsupervised scenarios respectively. The AA module offers a variety of analytical tools that can be flexibly combined into computational pipelines, providing practical solutions for both scenarios. In the supervised scenario, the involved samples are clearly labelled and can be classified into different groups, such as tumor and normal groups (Fig. 1c). In such cases, users can start the whole analysis by identifying differential ChIP/ATAC-seq signals between different groups of samples. Then, differential TF motif enrichment or differential inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility may be identified to infer the TF motifs associated with the detected differential ChIP/ATAC-seq signals between groups. In the unsupervised scenario, samples have no pre-defined labels that can divide them into groups (e.g., those generated from tumor tissues of different patients with the same cancer type) or are associated with complex labels (e.g., those in pan-cancer studies). Users may start by identifying hypervariable ChIP/ATAC-seq signals across samples (Fig. 1d). Then, these signals can serve as feature variables of the samples and can be used for applying various clustering and dimension reduction methods, with the purpose of revealing the underlying similarity structure among the samples. Hopefully, the samples can be well clustered into different groups after the above procedure, and the users can choose to perform different downstream analyses based on their personal needs. For example, signature gene scoring analysis based on pre-defined functional gene sets can be performed to explore the tissue/cell states of different sample clusters, and TF regulatory potential inference may provide insights into the TFs that preferentially function in specific clusters.

The AA module of EAP has been applied to a number of large-scale ChIP/ATAC-seq datasets generated by previous cancer studies, including the six datasets in Table 1, an ATAC-seq dataset for non-small cell lung cancer [21], a pan-cancer ATAC-seq dataset [2], an H3K27ac ChIP-seq dataset for gastric cancer [22], an H3K27ac ChIP-seq dataset for colorectal cancer [23], an ATAC-seq dataset for leukemia [24], and an H3K4me3 ChIP-seq dataset for NCI-60 human tumor cell lines [25] (Fig. 1e). The associated analysis results from various computational procedures, such as the inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility in different subgroups of tumor samples, have been pre-loaded in EAP and can be accessed via the Data Browser (DB) module (Fig. 1e). This module offers an interactive interface for users to visualize and retrieve information of interest, providing an informative reference of cancer epigenetic regulation for related studies (Supplementary Figure 7).

EAP exhibits stable performance in terms of scalability and reproducibility

To assess the scalability of EAP, we divided the LUAD H3K27ac ChIP-seq dataset and a GBM ATAC-seq dataset into varying sample sizes and applied the BA module to process each, recording CPU·hours, memory·hours, and running time. Both CPU·hours and memory·hours increased linearly with sample size, while running time showed a linear increase for more than 20 samples due to parallelization (Fig. 2a-c). These results demonstrate that EAP efficiently handles large-scale ChIP/ATAC-seq datasets. Furthermore, testing with six large-scale public datasets showed that all could be processed within a reasonable timeframe (Table 1).

Evaluation of the scalability and reproducibility of EAP, (a–c) Three metrics, CPU·hours (a), memory·hours (b), and running time (c) were used to assess the scalability of the BA module across datasets with varying sample sizes. (d–e) Reproducibility assessment of two independent runs of the BA module (d) and the AA module (e). “1st” and “2nd” denote the first and second runs, respectively, and the numbers indicate the total peaks or TFs identified in each run. Differential peak analysis, differential TF motif enrichment analysis, and differential inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility analysis were performed using an adjusted p-value cutoff of 0.01, while hypervariable analysis used an adjusted p-value cutoff of 0.1.

To assess the reproducibility of EAP, each workflow and downstream analysis tool was executed twice independently. The Jaccard index was used to evaluate the consistency of identified peaks (including differential enriched or accessible peaks and hypervariable peaks), as well as differential enriched TF motifs and TFs with differential inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility between the two runs. For clustering results, consistency was measured using the normalized mutual information (NMI), and for signature scores, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated. All workflows and downstream analysis tools exhibited high consistency across repeated runs, demonstrating the strong reproducibility of EAP results (Fig. 2d-e).

Data analysis capability of EAP in epigenetic investigation

In this section, two cancer studies of large-scale ATAC-seq datasets, with and without pre-defined group labels respectively, are presented to demonstrate the practical usefulness of EAP in data analysis and science discovery. Finally, a non-cancer large-scale ATAC-seq dataset is used to demonstrate the practical utility of EAP in non-cancer study.

Case study 1: capturing epigenetic alterations and identifying key transcription factors for thyroid cancer

An ATAC-seq dataset of thyroid cancer [28], comprising 70 primary tumor tissue samples, 74 normal tissue (adjacent to the primary tumor) samples, and 83 metastatic tumor samples, was collected from the GEO database. The BA and AA modules were utilized to preprocess the raw sequencing data and conduct a pairwise differential analysis among the three tissue types. In comparison to the differences between primary and metastatic tumor samples, a much larger number of differentially accessible chromatin sites (absolute M value > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.01) were identified between primary/metastatic tumor samples and normal samples (Fig. 3a). In this supervised scenario, we further performed differential TF motif enrichment analysis to detect motifs that were significantly enriched in specific sets of genomic regions (e.g., enriched in primary tumor sample up-regulated regions compared to normal sample up-regulated ones). TF motifs with an adjusted p-value < 0.01 were considered significantly enriched in a specific tissue type. It was found that key TFs for normal thyroid development, such as FOXE1 and HHEX [29], were significantly enriched in normal sample up-regulated regions, while TFs of the AP-1 family, such as FOS, JUN, FOSL2, JDP2, and BATF [30], were enriched in the up-regulated regions of primary/metastatic tumor samples (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 3–4). The AP-1 family is the main target proteins of MAPKs[31], and previous studies have shown that thyroid cancer can be driven by MAPK signaling pathway [32]. We therefore speculated that the enrichment of AP-1 family in up-regulated regions of primary/metastatic tumor samples was related to the activation of MAPK signaling pathway in thyroid cancer. To compare the activity of MAPK signaling pathway between different tissue types, gene set activity scoring analysis was performed to quantify the activity of MAPK signaling pathway in each individual sample. The results indicated that the activity of MAPK signaling pathway in primary/metastatic tumor tissue was clearly higher than that in normal tissue (Fig. 3c).

We have also performed differential inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility analysis to further confirm the above findings of tissue type-specific TFs. Different from the above differential TF motif enrichment analysis, this analysis identified tissue type specific TFs based on the inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility of each TF in each sample. The t-SNE plot based on the identified HVRs revealed clear distinctions in chromatin accessibility between normal tissues and primary or metastatic tumor tissues (Fig. 3d). Consistent with the above findings, the inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility of normal thyroid development-related TFs was higher in normal tissues, while the inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility of TFs in AP-1 family was clearly increased in primary/metastatic tumor tissues (Fig. 3e-f). It was also found that TFs belonging to the RUNX family had a stepwise increased inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility from normal tissue to primary tumor tissue to metastatic tumor tissue (Fig. 3g), which was consistent with a previous research showing that enhanced regulation activity of RUNX2 is functionally linked to tumor invasion and metastasis in thyroid cancer [33].

Together, these results demonstrated the functional profiling capacity of EAP in analyzing cancer datasets with pre-defined group labels, with respect to the identification of epigenetic alterations and the associated key TFs.

Case study 2: identifying epigenetic subtypes and associated transcription regulators for pancreatic cancer organoids

In practice, most cancer datasets do not have a clear group structure, owing to the considerable heterogeneity across patients and also the complexity of clinical pathology. On this account, EAP has specifically devised a computational workflow for the unsupervised scenario. Here, an ATAC-seq dataset consisting of 44 samples for organoids derived from different pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) patients was used to demonstrate the workflow [27].

First, EAP identified hypervariable regions (adjusted p-value cutoff 0.001) across the 44 samples and used the ATAC-seq signals in these regions as features to hierarchically cluster the samples, resulting in 5 sample clusters (labelled as C1 to C5; Fig. 4a). To evaluate the robustness of the clustering results, we repeated the analysis using adjusted p-value cutoffs of 0.01 and 0.1 for HVR selection. The resulting cluster assignments were largely consistent, with normalized mutual information (NMI) values of 0.755 and 0.660, respectively (Supplementary Figure 8). Then, based on previously defined signature gene sets of four different subtypes of PDAC [34], [35], we performed the gene set scoring analysis and annotated the sample clusters as classical progenitor-like (C1), basal-like (C2), classical immunogenic-like (C3), and aberrantly differentiated endocrine and exocrine (ADEX)-like (C4 and C5; Fig. 4b-c). In line with previous research [5], [36], the basal-like samples were associated with significantly worse prognosis compared to the other samples (p-value=0.045; Fig. 4d).

To further explore the differences in transcriptional regulation between the annotated subgroups of samples, we identified subgroup-specific TFs by performing the differential inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility analysis. Fig. 4e shows the inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility of the top 100 ranked TFs for each subgroup (Supplementary Table 5). TFs that are critical for normal pancreatic development and differentiation, such as GATA6 (ranked first, adjusted p-value=0.265), HNF1A (ranked seventh, adjusted p-value=0.045), and HNF1B (ranked eighth, adjusted p-value=0.050) [37], [38], [39], had significantly higher inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility in the classical progenitor-like subgroup than in the others (Fig. 4e). For the classical immunogenic-like subgroup, HNF4A (ranked thirty-seventh, adjusted p-value=0.032) and HNF4G (forty-fifth, adjusted p-value=0.041) were identified as its specific TFs. These TFs had been found to be associated with the classical subtype of pancreatic cancer and also play an important role in the invasion of it [40]. TFs of the RUNX family (top 3 ranked) were associated with significantly higher inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility in the basal-like subgroup compared to each of the other subgroups (Fig. 4e-f). These TFs have been reported to promote cell migration, invasion, and drug resistance in pancreatic cancer [41], [42], which was consistent with the observation that the basal-like samples were associated with significantly worse prognosis than the others. For the ADEX-like subgroup, TFs related to pancreatic endocrine (e.g., NEUROD2 (ranked tenth, adjusted p-value=0.009) and ASCL1 (ranked fifth, adjusted p-value=0.029) [43]) or exocrine (e.g., Rbpj1 (ranked sixth, adjusted p-value=0.011) [44]) were specifically highly active in it.

To validate these findings, we analyzed an independent PDAC H3K27ac ChIP-seq dataset [5] comprising 22 tumor samples (12 classical and 10 basal subtypes). Using EAP for preprocessing and performing differential inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility analysis, we observed results consistent with the PDAC organoid ATAC-seq dataset: the inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility of GATA6, HNF4A, and HNF4G were significantly upregulated in the classical subtype, whereas RUNX1 and RUNX2 were significantly upregulated in the basal subtype (Fig. 4g). These results further confirm epigenomic transcriptional regulatory differences between PDAC classical and basal subtypes.

Together, these findings exemplified the effectiveness of EAP in revealing cancer epigenetic subtypes and subtype-specific transcriptional regulators when analyzing datasets in the unsupervised scenario.

Case study 3: capturing epigenetic alterations and identifying key transcription factors in hematopoiesis

We further evaluated the utility of EAP using a non-cancer dataset. Specifically, we analyzed a publicly available ATAC-seq dataset of human hematopoiesis comprising 13 distinct cellular populations across the hematopoietic hierarchy [24]. Raw sequencing data were processed and analyzed through EAP. EAP identified hypervariable regions (adjusted p-value cutoff 0.1) across 76 samples and used ATAC-seq signal intensities within these regions as features for principal component analysis (PCA). The PCA plot revealed three major hematopoietic lineages: the lymphoid lineage, the granulocytic-monocytic lineage, and the erythroid lineage (Fig. 5a).

To further dissect lineage-specific regulatory programs, we performed differential inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility analysis among the annotated cell subgroups (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table 6). TFs essential for maintaining hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) quiescence, such as MEIS1 and MEIS2 [45], exhibited significantly higher inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility in HSCs compared with differentiated cells (Fig. 5c). Within the lymphoid lineage, TCF7 was identified as a key regulator in both CD4 and CD8 T cells, consistent with its well-established role in T cell development [46] (Fig. 5d). The inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility of EOMES was elevated in natural killer (NK) cells (Fig. 5e), whereas PAX5 showed upregulation in B cells (Fig. 5f), albeit with marginal significance. These TFs both represent the master regulators of their respective lineages [47], [48]. In the granulocytic-monocytic lineage, SPI1 was highly active in both granulocyte-monocyte progenitors (GMPs) and monocytes (Fig. 5g). It has been reported that this TF is necessary for its function and differentiation. For the erythroid lineage, the GATA1::TAL1 complex was identified as a lineage-specific regulator in both megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitors (MEPs) and erythrocytes [49] (Fig. 5h).

Collectively, this case study demonstrates that EAP effectively captures dynamic epigenetic alterations and identifies lineage-specific regulatory factors in hematopoiesis, highlighting its broad applicability to non-cancer epigenomic datasets.

Comparing the capacity of EAP with existing platforms for ChIP/ATAC-seq data analysis

Although numerous ChIP/ATAC-seq analysis platforms are available, EAP has been specifically designed to provide a broader range of analytical tools for downstream functional analyses, enabling users to more deeply explore data heterogeneity and associated transcriptional regulation. A key advantage of EAP is its ability to handle large-scale ChIP/ATAC-seq datasets, ranging from gigabytes to terabytes, allowing efficient processing and analysis of vast amounts of data.

In contrast to conventional workflows, which primarily focus on upstream preprocessing or isolated analytical modules [50], [51], [52], [53], [54], EAP offers an integrated framework that combines both upstream processing and comprehensive downstream functional analyses (Table 2). Compared with existing cloud-based platforms [12], [13], [14], [55], EAP operates within a fully preconfigured web-based environment, eliminating the need for manual software installation, environment setup, workflow optimization, infrastructure management or reference file configuration. Moreover, EAP distinguishes itself through automated data integrity verification, optimization-free workflows, and a set of downstream functional modules, providing a seamless end-to-end analysis experience (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we presented EAP, a useful computation platform for analyzing large-scale epigenomic datasets based on the cloud technology. Case studies demonstrated the practical utility of EAP in dissecting the heterogeneity across samples in a supervised or unsupervised manner, which can be used for discovering epigenetic subtypes of cancer and the associated transcriptional regulators or potential regulators in cell differentiation. Furthermore, EAP offers advanced computational tools with an interactive interface, allowing end users to iteratively modify parameters to refine analysis results. This feature is especially important for epigenomic data analysis in practical cancer studies, where a customizable computational pipeline is typically highly necessary.

EAP has been successfully applied to several large-scale ChIP/ATAC-seq datasets from previous cancer studies, each of which comprises tens or even hundreds of samples from different patients or cell lines (Fig. 1e). The associated analysis results are available in the Data Browser module of EAP, which provides a valuable reference for future epigenetic studies in cancer and may lead to new biological insights. For example, we noted that, for both cancer types in the two case studies, the regulation activity of the RUNX family was significantly linked with the degree of tumor malignancy, showing much higher inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility of in the tumor subgroup with worse prognosis (Fig. 3g and Fig. 4d-h). Similar patterns were also observed on the other two datasets in Table 1, both of which were associated with pre-defined tumor subtype labels that corresponded to distinct malignancy degrees (Supplementary Figure 7). These findings suggest a potential role for the RUNX family as key regulators associated with cancer progression across multiple tumor types. RUNX2, initially identified for its essential role in osteoblast and chondrocyte differentiation [56], has now been implicated in the metastatic behavior of thyroid [33], pancreatic [41], breast [57], [58] and colon [59] cancers, as well as melanoma [60]. Previous studies have shown that RUNX2 can activate the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [61], an essential regulator of cancer cell growth and metastasis, suggesting that RUNX2 may act as a major driving force underlying tumor progression and aggressiveness.

We also provide the scripts and pre-configured Docker images for the local deployment of EAP, enabling users to run the analysis pipelines without relying on cloud infrastructure. Users can perform analyses on their data using local server.

Overall, as a computational platform designed specifically for large-scale epigenomic studies, EAP is able to provide efficient and reproducible data analysis, which can lead to biologically meaningful findings and can broaden our understanding of epigenetic machinery in tissue development and disease progression.

Methods

Computational framework of EAP

The EAP web server is deployed on a private cloud system equipped with 2637 GB of memory, 568 CPUs, and 200 TB of storage space. These computational resources are provided by the Bio-Med Big Data Center, Shanghai Institute of Nutrition and Health (https://www.biosino.org/bmdc). For each submitted job, computational resources (memory and CPUs) are dynamically allocated. When all available resources are occupied, subsequent jobs are automatically queued until resources become available. The docker technology was utilized to build up analysis environments of EAP, containing necessary software and packages for ChIP/ATAC-seq data analysis, such as FastQC (v0.11.9) for read quality control, Bowtie1 (v1.2.0)/Bowtie2 (v2.2.9) for read alignment, and MACS1 (v1.4.2)/MACS2(v2.1.0) for peak calling. In the cloud system, analysis pipelines, including raw sequencing data preprocessing and downstream data mining, are defined in YAML files specifying configuration parameters and commands to be executed by the scheduler of cloud engine. Notably, user-friendly GUI has been designed to allow researchers without programing skills to carry out customizable analysis on their own ChIP/ATAC-seq datasets. Each authorized user is assigned 2 TB of default storage space. Uploaded data are retained for two weeks; after this period, files within the user’s storage directory are automatically removed to maintain system efficiency.

Workflow of EAP

Data analysis in EAP is conducted in a registration way, considering data privacy and data security. First, users should register an account at https://www.biosino.org/epigenetics/#/user/register and request storage space by submitting an application form to the administrator, which is available at https://github.com/haojiechen94/EAP/blob/main/doc/File_1_Storage_space_application_form.xlsx. Then, the administrator reviews these requirements and creates accounts for the users. After that, they can upload raw sequencing files to the workspace designated to them and can conduct analysis with analytical tools embedded in EAP. The Instruction document and help information are provided on the Home page for an easy use of EAP, which are available at https://github.com/haojiechen94/EAP/blob/main/doc/Help%20document.pdf as well. Given the typically large size of uploaded data, a data client tool has been devised to support breakpoint-resumable transfers and parallel file transmission. Additionally, we offer video tutorials that visually demonstrate the processes, making it easier for users to learn and effectively apply the procedures by logging in with a demo account (Supplementary movie 1, Supplementary movie 2 and Supplementary movie 3, https://github.com/haojiechen94/EAP/tree/main/video_tutorials). A guide page is also available at https://haojiechen94.github.io/my_website/.

Basic analysis module: an automated ChIP/ATAC-seq data preprocessing pipeline

Input

Raw sequencing files in FASTQ format and a textual metadata file describing the phenotypic or group information of all samples. A metadata template is offered on the Home page of EAP, which is also available at https://github.com/haojiechen94/EAP/blob/main/doc/File_2_metadata.csv. Specifying typical bin size to control the size of genomic bins, 1000 by default.

Data processing

First, FastQC (v0.11.9) was used to assess the quality of sequencing reads, and Trim Galore (v0.6.7) was employed to trim adapter sequences and remove low-quality bases. The filtered reads were then aligned to the user-specified reference genome (support both human and mouse genome) using Bowtie1 (v1.2.0) or Bowtie2 (v2.2.9). After alignment, potential PCR duplicates were removed based on read genomic coordinates.

For paired-end ChIP-seq and input samples, each read pair was converted into a single representative read positioned 100 bp upstream of the inferred fragment center, with the fragment center defined as the midpoint between the two 5′ ends of the read pair. For ATAC-seq data, in MACS1, each aligned read was uniformly shifted 100 bp upstream; in MACS2, reads were shifted 75 bp upstream and extended by 150 bp. The remaining reads were used for peak calling with MACS1 (v1.4.2) or MACS2 (v2.1.0). Finally, a read count table was generated using MAnorm2-utils (v1.0.0).

Output

Three count tables are generated, of which the bin count table (NA_profile_bins.xls) is prepared for differential analysis and the other two count tables (proximal_peak_regions_2000bp.txt and distal_peak_regions_2000bp.txt) are prepared for hypervariable analysis. In addition, a summary report is produced, recording information about quality control of sequencing reads, read mapping, peak calling, genomic annotations of peaks, and motif enrichment within peaks. Intermediate outputs are also available in the output directory. Users can utilize these intermediate results for additional analyses and visualization.

Advanced analysis module: a versatile collection of computational tools for ChIP/ATAC-seq data analysis

Differential analysis

Input

The bin count table (NA_profile_bins.xls) and the sample metadata file. Specifying Log 2 fold change cutoff (default 0) and adjusted p-value cutoff (default 0.01) to identify significant differential enriched/accessible peaks.

Data processing

Differential TF motif enrichment analysis

Input

The table resulting from the differential analysis. Specifying Log 2 fold change cutoff (default 0) and adjusted p-value cutoff (default 0.01) to identify significant differential enriched/accessible peaks.

Data processing

A user-specified cutoff of adjusted p-value is used to define significantly up-regulated or down-regulated peak regions. Then, the motifscanR method is utilized to scan for TF motif instances in these regions. Finally, Fisher’s exact test is employed to determine the TF motifs that are significantly enriched in one set of peak regions relative to the other. The Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure was used to adjust p-values for multiple testing.

Output

-A table presenting the results of the differential motif enrichment analysis;

A table presenting the results of the differential motif enrichment analysis;

-A volcano plot highlighting significantly differentially enriched motifs.

A volcano plot highlighting significantly differentially enriched motifs.

Hypervariable analysis

Input

The count tables (proximal_peak_regions_2000bp.txt and distal_peak_regions_2000bp.txt) and the sample metadata file. Specifying adjusted p-value cutoff (default 0.1) to identify significant hypervariable peak regions.

Data processing

M-A normalization is performed in pseudo-reference mode. HyperChIP (v1.2.0) is used to evaluate the significance of signal variability in each peak region. The peak regions with an adjusted p-value below 0.1 (by default) are selected as hypervariable regions (HVRs). Then, principal component analysis (PCA) is conducted based on the signals within these regions and all the samples are visualized in a two-dimensional PCA or t-SNE plot. Considering the inherent mean-variance dependence of ChIP/ATAC-seq signals, EAP also provides an option to apply variance stabilizing transformation (VST) to HVR signals prior to PCA, enabling more robust dimensionality reduction and visualization.

Output

-Hypervariable analysis results exported in both TXT and RData formats;

Hypervariable analysis results exported in both TXT and RData formats;

-Mean-variance scatter plots illustrating the global mean-variance trend in proximal and distal regions, with significant hypervariable regions highlighted;

Mean-variance scatter plots illustrating the global mean-variance trend in proximal and distal regions, with significant hypervariable regions highlighted;

-The two-dimensional PCA or t-SNE plot.

The two-dimensional PCA or t-SNE plot.

Clustering analysis

Input

RData-formatted results of the hypervariable analysis and the sample metadata file. Specifying the number of cluster (default 2) to assignment.

Data processing

Principal component analysis (PCA) is conducted based on signals within hypervariable regions. Then, top-ranked principal components are utilized to calculate the Euclidean distance between each pair of samples. Finally, hierarchical clustering is used to group samples into clusters.

Output

-A new metadata file containing the clustering results, which can be utilized for further analysis;

A new metadata file containing the clustering results, which can be utilized for further analysis;

-A heat map displaying the hierarchical clustering result, providing a visual representation of sample similarity and clustering patterns.

A heat map displaying the hierarchical clustering result, providing a visual representation of sample similarity and clustering patterns.

Signature genes scoring analysis

Input

Hypervariable analysis results of proximal regions in RData format, links between genes and proximal regions and a list of genes of interest in GMT format.

Data processing

First, the ChIP/ATAC-seq signal intensities in proximal regions are transformed into z-scores (centered and scaled across all samples). Then, for each sample, the z-scores of those proximal regions linked to the genes of interest are summed, with the average z-score of all proximal regions in that sample subtracted from the sum to correct for potential biases.

Output

Box plots showing the signature scores associated with different sample clusters.

Differential inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility analysis

Input

Results of the hypervariable analysis in RData format and the sample metadata file. Specifying adjusted p-value cutoff (default 0.1) to identify significant hypervariable peak regions.

Data processing

Peak regions with an adjusted p-value below 0.1 (by default) are defined as hypervariable regions. Then, motifscanR (v0.9.0) is utilized to search for TF motif instances within these regions. For each motif, the ChIP/ATAC-seq signal intensities at the hypervariable regions containing its instances are aggregated separately for each sample (in the same manner as in the signature genes scoring analysis. Finally, t-test is performed to identify TFs associated with significantly increased regulatory potential in each user-specified subgroup of samples. The Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure was used to adjust p-values for multiple testing.

Output

-A table of inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility in all samples;

A table of inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility in all samples;

-A table of t-statistics, p-values and BH-adjusted p-values of the performed t-test;

A table of t-statistics, p-values and BH-adjusted p-values of the performed t-test;

-For each user-specified TF motif, a two-dimensional plot of all samples colored by and the corresponding inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility.

For each user-specified TF motif, a two-dimensional plot of all samples colored by and the corresponding inferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility.

Internal scalability and reproducibility benchmarks

All benchmarking analyses were performed in authorized user mode. To evaluate the scalability of the Basic Analysis (BA) module in EAP, a publicly available ChIP/ATAC-seq dataset was divided into four subsets with varying sample sizes (10, 20, 30, and 40 samples). Each subset was processed independently using the BA module, and computational performance was assessed in terms of CPU·hours, memory·hours, and runtime.

To assess reproducibility, both the BA and Advanced Analysis (AA) modules were executed twice independently under identical conditions. Reproducibility was quantified using the Jaccard index for comparing identified peaks (including differentially enriched or accessible peaks and hypervariable regions), as well as differential enriched TF motifs and TFs with differential iinferred TF motif associated chromatin occupancy/accessibility between the two runs. For clustering results, normalized mutual information (NMI) was used to measure consistency, while Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC) were calculated to assess the reproducibility of signature scores.
