Existence of a robust optimal control process for efficient measurements in a two-qubit system
Ricardo Rodriguez, Nam Nguyen, Elizabeth Behrman, Andrew C. Y. Li, James Steck

Abstract
Using a Hamiltonian of physical interest, we show the existence of a unitary transformation that drives the initial state of a two-qubit system to a designated final state. We implement an application of the above framework by finding an optimal control process that enables us to determine the entanglement of the two-qubit system with a single measurement. We show also that this process is robust to environmental noise. Our results provide mathematical justification for our earlier computational experiments.



I
Introduction

Manipulating physical phenomena for the transmission and processing of information is at the basis of much of our modern technological development. The classical paradigm, formalized by Claude Shannon
Sha 48
, utilizes physical degrees of freedom to represent a classical understanding of logic, probability, and entropy. Quantum information transmission
RHO+ 25
and quantum computing
NC 00
extend this framework by using entanglement and superposition of states. These are quantum properties of physical systems without a classical equivalent.

Entanglement is also an important resource in quantum cryptography and its various manifestations
Mas23b
. For example, quantum key distribution
BB 14

quantum secure direct communications
Ben 92
,
quantum secret sharing
JSBP 19
, and quantum key secure communication
Mas23a
all depend, in a large part, on the sharing of entangled particles. Another novel application is the production of traceable random numbers
KPR+ 24
.

The future quantum internet will then require an industrial production of entangled systems and their quality control. We imagine the need for the sampling and validation of such sources.
Here, we propose a protocol that requires only a single measurement to determine the entanglement of a known two-qubit system. This is just one example of many possibilities. We also demonstrate that this process is robust to environmental noise, a crucial consideration for practical applications.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section

II
Mathematical Framework

II.1
Existence and robustness of the unitary transformations

The time evolution of a quantum system with density matrix
\rho
and Hamiltonian
H
is given by the Liouville-Von Neumann equation:

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[H,\rho].

(1)

Generically, a Hamiltonian is described by a linear combination of terms that correspond to a certain physical set of interactions. For example:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\kappa_{A}\sigma_{x}\otimes\mathds{1}+\kappa_{B}\mathds{1}\otimes\sigma_{x}+\epsilon_{A}\sigma_{z}\otimes\mathds{1}+\epsilon_{B}\mathds{1}\otimes\sigma_{z}+\zeta\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{z}+\nu\sigma_{x}\otimes\sigma_{x}

(2)

is QMA-complete
BL 08
and a generalization of the Hamiltonian used by
BSKW 08
.
\kappa_{i}
are the tunneling amplitudes,
\epsilon_{i}
are the biases, and
\zeta
and
\nu
are qubit-qubit couplings.
H
is a symmetric matrix if the parameters are real. We assume that
\kappa_{i},\epsilon_{i},\zeta,\nu
are functions of time and will represent their time variations by the generic control process
{\mathbf{u}}(t)
.

The system eq.(
1
) is density matrix controllable (DMC)
D’A 22
if, for each pair of unitary equivalent matrices
\rho_{i}
and
\rho_{f}
, there exists a control process
\mathbf{u}
and a time
T>0
such that the solution
\rho(t)
of eq.(
1
) with initial condition equal to
\rho_{i}
at time
t=T
is
\rho_{f}
. This definition means, in our particular case, that given an initial and final state, we can always find real functions
\mathbf{u}(t)=\kappa_{i}(t),\epsilon_{i}(t),\zeta(t),\nu(t)\}
that allow the Hamiltonian to evolve one state to the other. The natural question is to guarantee the existence of the process
\mathbf{u}(t)
.

Definition II.1

(Dynamical Lie Algebra)
.

Let a control Hamiltonian be described by

H(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}(t)H_{j},

where
\{H_{j}\}
are time-independent basis operators (e.g. Pauli strings). The Dynamical Lie Algebra (DLA), denoted as
\mathcal{L}
, is the Lie algebra generated by the set of skew-Hermitian operators

S=\{iH_{1},iH_{2},\cdots,iH_{m}\}.

This is the smallest Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{su}(n)
that contains the set
S
, and is equivalent to the vector space spanned by all possible repeated Lie brackets of the elements of
S
.

Theorem II.1

(Existence of the unitary transformation)
.

The quantum control system for two-qubit system described by the Hamiltonian in Equation
2
is DMC.

Proof.

To show that this quantum system is DMC, we must show that its DLA is the special unitary algebra
\mathfrak{su}(4)
according to the theory of quantum control
D’A 22
. Note that for the Hamiltonian in Equation
2
, the set of six-skew Hermitian basis operators is

S=\{i\sigma_{x}\otimes\mathds{1},i\mathds{1}\otimes\sigma_{x},i\sigma_{z}\otimes\mathds{1},i\mathds{1}\otimes\sigma_{z},i\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{z},i\sigma_{x}\otimes\sigma_{x}\}

The DLA generated can be computed by computing the Lie brackets (commutators) repeatedly. One can easily show that the first round of this calculation yields 7 new, linearly independent operators. The second round yields 2 more linearly independent operators. Thus, after two rounds, we have 15 linear independent operators. Since the dimension of the Lie algebra of
\mathfrak{su}(4)
is 15, we have shown that the DLA of the system is indeed
\mathfrak{su}(4)
. Therefore, by theory of quantum control, this system is DMC.
∎

Having established the controllability (existence of the unitary transformation) in Theorem
II.1
, we now investigate its robustness to common physical noise. In particular, the drift in the Hamiltonian.

Corollary II.1.1

(Robust controllability)
.

The two-qubit control system governed by the ideal Hamiltonian in Equation
2
remains DMC in the presence of drift in the Hamiltonian.

Proof.

The total Hamiltonian of the imperfect (noisy) system is

H_{\text{total}}=H+H_{d}

where
H
is the ideal Hamiltonian, such as the Hamiltonian described in Equation
2
, and
H_{d}
is the drift term and can be characterized as a general Hermitian matrix
MPdR+ 17
. For a two-qubit system, the DLA,
\mathcal{L_{\text{total}}}
, is
\mathfrak{su}(4)
. The set of generators for this system is:

G_{\text{total}}=S\cup\{iH_{d}\}

where
S
is the set of the generators from the ideal Hamiltonian. We have established in Theorem
II.1
that the Lie algebra generated by the set
S
from the Hamiltonian in Equation
2
is the entire algebra. That is,
\mathcal{L_{H}}=\mathfrak{su}(4)
. The skew-Hermitian operator
\{iH_{d}\}
is an element of the algebra
\mathfrak{u}(4)
, and its traceless part lies within
\mathfrak{su}(4)
.

Now, the DLA of the total system,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\text{Lie}(G_{\text{total}})
, is generated by a set of operators that contains the generated set of
\mathcal{L_{H}}
. Thefore,

\mathfrak{su}(4)=\mathcal{L_{H}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}

Since
\mathfrak{su}(4)
is the largest possible DLA for a two-qubit system, we must have
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathfrak{su}(4).

∎

Corollary

shows that the vector space generated by the controls in Equation

II.2
Existence of the Target State

The preceding analysis established that the control Hamiltonian (Equation
2
) is universal. This guarantees that any unitary
U
can be implemented, hence, the system can be driven from an initial state
\rho(0)
to any unitarily equivalent state. The specific goal of quantum control is to find a state within this set of unitarily equivalent states that yields a particular measurement outcome. The existence of such state is not guaranteed. To prove this, we recall the fundamental link between unitary equivalence and the spectrum of a matrix.

Theorem II.2

(Unitary equivalence for two Hermitian matrices
HJ 85
)
.

Two Hermitian matrices,
A
and
B
, are unitarily equivalent (i.e.,
A=UBU^{\dagger}
for some unitary
U
) if and only if they share the same spectrum of eigenvalues.

This theorem allows us to define the set of all possible final states as the collection of all states unitarily equivalent to the initial state. For a given initial spectrum
\lambda
, the following theorem now proves that this set is guaranteed to contain a state that satisfies our measurement condition.

Theorem II.3
.

For any initial two-qubit state
\rho(0)
, there exists a final state
\rho(t_{f})
that is unitarily equivalent to
\rho(0)
and satisfies the condition
\Tr((\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{z})\cdot\rho(t_{f}))=c
, where
c
is the concurrence of the initial state
\rho(0)
.

Proof.

Let
\rho(0)
be a
4\times 4
Hermitian, positive semi-definite matrix with
\Tr(\rho(0))=1
. Without loss of generality, let’s suppose

\lambda(\rho(0))=\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4}\},\quad\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{3}\geq\lambda_{4}.

Let us define

\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}=\{U\rho(0)U^{\dagger}\mid U\in U(4)\}.

(3)

Note that
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
is the set of all states unitarily equivalent to
\rho(0)
. As the continuous image of the compact, connected group
U(4)
,
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
is itself a compact and connected set in the space of Hermitian matrices.
Let us define a real-valued function
f
on this set:

f(\rho)=\Tr((\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{z})\cdot\rho)

Since the trace is a linear and therefore continuous function,
f
is continuous on the compact, connected set
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
. By the Intermediate Value Theorem, the function
f
must attain every value between its minimum and maximum on this set.

The maximum value of
f(\rho)
over the set
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
,
M
, is achieved by aligning the eigenvalues of
\rho
and
\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{z}
in the same sorted order. That is,

\displaystyle M=\max_{\rho\in\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}}f(\rho)

\displaystyle=(1)\lambda_{1}+(1)\lambda_{2}+(-1)\lambda_{3}+(-1)\lambda_{4}

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})-(\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{4}).

And the minimum value
m
is found by aligning them in opposite sorted orders.

\displaystyle m=\min_{\rho\in\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}}f(\rho)

\displaystyle=(-1)\lambda_{1}+(-1)\lambda_{2}+(1)\lambda_{3}+(1)\lambda_{4}

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{4})-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})

Thus, the set of all attainable values for
f(\rho)
is the continuous interval
[m,M]
. Now, note that based on the definition of the concurrence
Woo 98

c=c(\rho(0))=\text{max}\left\{0,\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{4}\right\}
, we have that

m\leq 0\leq c\leq M.

Therefore, there exists a state
\rho(t_{f})\in\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
such that
f(\rho(t_{f}))=c
. This state is unitarily equivalent to
\rho(0)
by definition of
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}
.
∎

Corollary II.3.1
.

For any initial pure state
\rho(0)
of a two qubit system, there exists a unitarily equivalent final state
\rho(t_{f})
such that
\Tr((\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{z})\cdot\rho(t_{f}))=c
for any
c\in[-1,1]
.

Proof.

An initial state
\rho(0)
is pure if and only if it is rank-1. Thus, the sorted eigenvalues are
\lambda=\{1,0,0,0\}
. Therefore,
M=1
and
m=-1
.
∎

Corollary II.3.2
.

For any initial rank-2 mixed state
\rho(0)
of a two-qubit system, there exists a unitarily equivalent final state
\rho(t_{f})
such that
\Tr((\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{z})\cdot\rho(t_{f}))=c
for any
c\in[-1,1]
.

Proof.

An initial state
\rho(0)
is rank-2 if and only if exactly two of its eigenvalues are non-zero. That is,
\lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},0,0\}
with
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
and
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=1
. Therefore,
M=1
and
m=-1
.
∎

III
Optimal control design

The general problem of optimal control
BH 75
is to find a history of the control
vector
\mathbf{u}(t)
for
0\leq t\leq t_{f}
which forces the state from its initial value to its final value (along a trajectory) and, at the same time, maximizes or minimizes the cost functional. The resulting state history
\rho(t)
for
0\leq t\leq t_{f}
is an
optimal trajectory
Ste 94
,
Tri 18
.

Along these lines, we follow
BSKW 08
to define the cost functional, which is a generalization of the Lagrangian.

III.1
Definition of the control functional

Let us begin with the dynamic constraint, the Liouville-Von Neumann equation:

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[H,\rho]

Very often, a scalar algebraic function of the final state is chosen as part of the cost function.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[d-\langle\mathcal{O}(t_{f})\rangle]^{2}

where
\mathcal{O}
is an output measure or some function of this measure
BSKW 08
. For
d
we will use the concurrence
Woo 98
of the initial state and evaluate the quadratic error of some measurement of the final state. The cost function is chosen as:

{\mathcal{J}}=\frac{1}{2}[d-\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle]^{2}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}\,Tr\left(\lambda^{\dagger}\left(\frac{1}{i\hbar}[H,\rho]-\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\right)\right)\,dt+\frac{1}{8}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}\,Tr\left(H^{\dagger}H\right)\,dt

where
Tr
is the trace. This equation can be rewritten in terms of the components of each matrix:

{\mathcal{J}}=\frac{1}{2}[d-\langle\mathcal{O}(t_{f})\rangle]^{2}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}\,\sum_{i,j}\lambda^{*}_{ji}\left(\frac{1}{i\hbar}[H,\rho]-\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\right)_{ij}dt+\frac{1}{8}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{f}}\,\sum_{i,j}H^{*}_{ji}H_{ij}\,dt

\lambda
is a matrix containing the Lagrange multipliers, and we have also added a term that represents a ”control cost.” Observe that each component of the density matrix is considered an independent variable.

We can rewrite the cost function introducing the
control Hamiltonian
:

{\mathcal{J}}=\frac{1}{2}(d-\langle\mathcal{O}(t_{f})\rangle)^{2}+\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885ptt_{0}$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle t_{f}$}}$}}\nolimits\,\left\{{\mathcal{H}}(\rho(t),{\bf u(t)},\lambda(t))-Tr\left(\lambda^{\dagger}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\right)\right\}dt

where

{\mathcal{H}}(\rho(t),{\bf u(t)},\lambda(t))\equiv\frac{1}{i\hbar}\Tr\left(\lambda^{\dagger}[H,\rho]\right)+\frac{1}{8}Tr\left(H^{\dagger}H\right)

Integrating by parts, we obtain the final form of the cost functional:

{\mathcal{J}}=\frac{1}{2}(d-\langle\mathcal{O}(t_{f})\rangle)^{2}+\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885ptt_{0}$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle t_{f}$}}$}}\nolimits\,\left\{{\mathcal{H}}(\rho(t),{\bf u(t)},\lambda(t))+Tr\left(\rho\frac{d\lambda^{\dagger}}{dt}\right)\right\}dt-Tr(\rho(t_{f})\lambda^{\dagger}(t_{f}))

III.2
Equations of motion for the control variables

Now, let us find the Euler-Lagrange equations. Varying over the corresponding variables:

0=\delta{\mathcal{J}}=-(d-\phi(\rho_{f}))\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\rho_{f}}\Delta\rho_{f}+\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885ptt_{0}$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle t_{f}$}}$}}\nolimits\,\left\{\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\bf u}\Delta{\bf u}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\rho}\Delta{\rho}+\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}Tr\left(\frac{\partial\lambda^{\dagger}}{\partial t}\rho\right)\Delta\rho\right\}dt-\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho_{f}}Tr(\lambda^{\dagger}_{f}\rho_{f})\Delta\rho_{f}

We have used
\frac{\partial}{\partial A}\Tr(AB)=B^{T}
and defined
\phi(\rho_{f})=\langle\mathcal{O}(t_{f})\rangle
. Since all variations are independent, we can write:

1.

{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\bf u}=0}

2.

{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\rho}+\frac{\partial\bar{\lambda}}{\partial t}=0}

3.

{\displaystyle-(d-\phi(\rho_{f}))\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\rho_{f}}-\bar{\lambda}_{f}=0}

Let us start with
1

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\bf u}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\Tr\left(\lambda^{\dagger}\left[\frac{\partial H}{\partial\bf u},\rho\right]\right)+\frac{1}{8}Tr\left(\frac{\partial H^{\dagger}}{\partial\bf u}H+H^{\dagger}\frac{\partial H}{\partial\bf u}\right)

Using the fact that

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\Tr\left(\lambda^{\dagger}\left[\frac{\partial H}{\partial\bf u},\rho\right]\right)+\frac{1}{4}Tr\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial\bf u}H\right)

Now, observe that:

\displaystyle u_{l}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}Tr\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial u_{l}}H\right)

And therefore:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial u_{l}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\Tr\left(\lambda^{\dagger}\left[\frac{\partial H}{\partial u_{l}},\rho\right]\right)+u_{l}

Given the structure of the Hamiltonian, we have six equations:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\kappa_{A}}=\kappa_{A}+\frac{1}{i\hbar}\Tr\left(\lambda^{\dagger}\left[\sigma_{x}\otimes\mathds{1},\rho\right]\right),\quad 0=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\kappa_{B}}=\kappa_{B}+\frac{1}{i\hbar}\Tr\left(\lambda^{\dagger}\left[\mathds{1}\otimes\sigma_{x},\rho\right]\right)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\epsilon_{A}}=\epsilon_{A}+\frac{1}{i\hbar}\Tr\left(\lambda^{\dagger}\left[\sigma_{z}\otimes\mathds{1},\rho\right]\right),\ \quad 0=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\epsilon_{B}}=\epsilon_{B}+\frac{1}{i\hbar}\Tr\left(\lambda^{\dagger}\left[\mathds{1}\otimes\sigma_{z},\rho\right]\right)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\zeta}=\zeta+\frac{1}{i\hbar}\Tr\left(\lambda^{\dagger}\left[\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{z},\rho\right]\right),\ \ \quad 0=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\nu}=\nu+\frac{1}{i\hbar}\Tr\left(\lambda^{\dagger}\left[\sigma_{x}\otimes\sigma_{x},\rho\right]\right)

Let’s continue with
2

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\rho}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[H,\bar{\lambda}]

thus:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bar{\lambda}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{i\hbar}[H,\bar{\lambda}]

Taking the complex conjugate on both sides, we finally obtain the dynamic equation for the Lagrange multipliers:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[H,\lambda]

We can work now with the third Euler-Lagrange equation

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}_{f}

\displaystyle=-(d-\phi(\rho_{f}))\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\rho_{f}}

As observable, we choose

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=Tr(\rho_{f}(\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{z}))

So:

\displaystyle\lambda_{f}

\displaystyle=-(d-\phi(\rho_{f}))(\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{z})

We have in our hands a two-point boundary value problem

\displaystyle\rho(t)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(t)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(t)

\displaystyle=exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}H(t-t_{0})\right]

IV
Quantum control algorithm

We will now extend the definition to a sequence of N-time slices where the Hamiltonian of the system is constant:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}[d-\phi(\rho_{N})]^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\,\left\{{\mathcal{H}}(\rho_{k},{\bf u}_{k},\lambda_{k})+Tr\left(\rho_{k}\frac{d\lambda_{k}^{\dagger}}{dt}\right)\right\}-Tr(\rho_{N}\lambda^{\dagger}_{N})

Where:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}(\rho_{k},{\bf u}_{k},\lambda_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}Tr(H^{\dagger}({\bf u}_{k})H({\bf u}_{k}))+\frac{1}{i\hbar}Tr(\lambda^{\dagger}_{k+1}[H({\bf u}_{k}),\rho_{k}])

Following a procedure similar to the one outlined above and using the fact that

{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial u_{l,k}}=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\Tr\left(\lambda_{k+1}\left[\frac{\partial H(u_{l,k})}{\partial u_{l,k}},\rho\right]\right)+u_{l,k}}

2.

{\displaystyle\frac{\partial\lambda_{k}}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[H({\bf u}_{k}),\lambda_{k+1}]}

3.

{\displaystyle-(d-Tr(\rho_{N}\sigma_{zz}))\sigma_{zz}-\lambda_{N}=0}

\frac{\partial\rho_{k}}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[H({\bf u}_{k}),\rho_{k}]

V
Numerical implementation

We implemented the above algorithm in MATLAB/Octave. As it has already been stated, the algorithm takes an initial state represented by a density matrix
\rho(0)
, and returns a well-designed final state
\rho(t_{f})
and a set of controls associated with the transformation. This set of values is known as the control path. For most of the density matrices, four time steps, and so four different Hamiltonians, complete the job of finding the unitary matrix that transforms the initial state.

From the control variables in each time slice
\{{\bf u}_{k};\,\,k=1,\cdots N-1\}
we can build a unitary matrix that represents the control process during this time interval. The sequential product of these matrices is equal to the unitary matrix
\mathcal{U}
responsible of the transformation between the initial and final states.

We tested the software on samples containing density matrices parameterized by the method described in
KT 22
. After sampling uniformly the parameter space, we produced sets containing between 100 to 1000 different matrices with pure and mixed states in them. Typical results can be observed in Fig.

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we have shown that it is possible to evolve any initial state of a two-qubit system to a designated final state, which we can optimally design so as to yield the entanglement with a single measurement. These results provide the mathematical justification for our 2008 results
BSKW 08
. Control and determination of entanglement are at the basis of many applications of quantum communications and quantum computation. It is important to develop efficient methods to certify the quality of the sources of pairs of entangled qubits.

While our results here apply only to a two-qubit system, much of this framework will generalize to an N-qubit system under the
Z^{\otimes N}
or even general measure. Our work in quantum transfer learning
TNBS 20
provides some support for wider applicability, as well. Moreover, while we show here specifically a measurement of entanglement, this is just one example of many possibilities.

It should also be noted that this approach has an additional advantage: it is inherently robust, as shown by Corollary
II.1.1
. This, too, is in line with our previous work
NBS 20
.

Appendix C
Definition of Concurrence

This section follows
MCKB 05
. The original definition of concurrence
BDSW 96
,
HW 97
for a bipartite two-level system is given in terms of a special basis:

|e_{1}\rangle=|\Phi^{+}\rangle,\,\,\,\,|e_{2}\rangle=|\Phi^{-}\rangle\ ,\,\,|e_{3}\rangle=i|\Psi^{+}\rangle,\,\,\,|e_{4}\rangle=|\Psi^{-}\rangle\,\,\,\

where
|\Phi^{\pm}\rangle=(|00\rangle\pm|11\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
and
|\Psi^{\pm}\rangle=(|01\rangle\pm|10\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
are the
Bell
states. Using this particular basis, the concurrence
\mathcal{C}
of a pure state
|\Psi\rangle
is defined as

\mathcal{C}(\Psi)=\left|\sum_{i}\langle e_{i}|\Psi\rangle^{2}\right|

Writing this definition more explicitly,
\mathcal{C}(\Psi)=\left|\sum_{i}\langle\Psi^{*}|e_{i}^{*}\rangle\langle e_{i}|\Psi\rangle\right|
one can obtain after summing over the basis states,

\mathcal{C}(\Psi)=\left|\langle\Psi^{*}|\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y}|\Psi\rangle\right|

Note that
\Psi^{*}
is the complex conjugate of
\Psi
with the conjugation performed in the standard basis
\{|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle\}
. We have to be careful with the above equation. The inner product uses the transpose of the complex conjugate of the state, so the adjoint of
\Psi^{*}
is just the transpose of
\Psi
. Therefore
\langle\Psi^{*}|=\sum_{i,j}\Psi_{ij}\langle ij|
is the transpose and not the adjoint of
|\Psi\rangle=\sum_{i,j}\Psi_{ij}|ij\rangle
. The above definition is the most commonly used.

Definition C.1

(Concurrence for Mixed States)
.

The concurrence of a mixed state is given by using the convex roof construction:

\mathcal{C}(\rho)=\inf_{\{p_{i},\Psi_{i}\}}\sum_{i}\,p_{i}\mathcal{C}(\Psi),\,\,\text{with }p_{i}>0,\,\text{ and }\,\,\,\rho=\sum_{i}\,p_{i}|\Psi_{i}\rangle\langle\Psi_{i}|

An explicit formula for this infimum is known
Woo 98
:

\mathcal{C}(\rho)=\text{max}\left\{0,\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{4}\right\}

where the
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues, in decreasing order, of the Hermitian matrix:

R\equiv\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho}\tilde{\rho}\sqrt{\rho}}

with

\tilde{\rho}=(\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y})\rho^{*}(\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y})

and
\rho^{*}
is the complex conjugate of
\rho
.

This definition is equivalent to
Woo 98
:

\mathcal{C}(\rho)\equiv\text{max}(0,\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}-\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}-\sqrt{\lambda_{3}}-\sqrt{\lambda_{4}})

In which
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4}
are eigenvalues in decreasing order of the non-Hermitian matrix

\rho\tilde{\rho}
.

Observe that the concurrence is a positive number such that
0\leq\mathcal{C}(\rho)\leq 1
.
