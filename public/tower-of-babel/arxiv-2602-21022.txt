Common definitions of the “standard”
LOCAL
model tend to be sloppy and even self-contradictory on one point: do the nodes update their state using an
arbitrary
function or a
computable
function? So far, this distinction has been safe to neglect, since problems where it matters seem contrived and quite different from e.g. typical local graph problems studied in this context.

We show that this question matters even for locally checkable labeling problems (LCLs), perhaps the most widely studied family of problems in the context of the
LOCAL
model. Furthermore, we show that assumptions about computability are directly connected to another aspect already recognized as highly relevant: whether we have any knowledge of
n
, the size of the graph. Concretely, we show that there is an LCL problem
\Pi
with the following properties:

1.

\Pi
can be solved in
O(\regularlog n)
rounds if the
LOCAL
model is uncomputable.

2.

\Pi
can be solved in
O(\regularlog n)
rounds in the computable model if we know
any
upper bound on
n
.

3.

\Pi
requires
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
rounds in the computable model if we do not know anything about
n
.

We also show that the connection between computability and knowledge of
n
holds in general: for any LCL problem
\Pi
, if you have any bound on
n
, then
\Pi
has the same round complexity in the computable and uncomputable models.

1
Introduction

There is no single, universally-agreed-on definition of the “standard”
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
model of distributed computing. And when a paper introduces the
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
model, some aspects are often left unspecified, or the definition might even be self-contradictory.
A case in point is assumptions related to
computability
. By definition, the local state of a node after
T
rounds is some function
f
of its radius-
T
neighborhood—but is this function a computable function, or an arbitrary function?

Many key papers state that one can use any function, without any restrictions related to computability
[
19
,
20
,
15
]
. At the same time, papers often use words such as “processor”
[
19
,
20
]
to refer to individual nodes, as if they would be implementable as real-world computers that are restricted to computable functions. Some papers make it explicit that nodes are computationally bounded
[
17
]
.
Finally, there are some rare papers such as
[
12
]
that explicitly acknowledge that one can define both the computable and uncomputable version of the model.

Many papers leave computability assumptions somewhat vague and open to interpretation, and when explicitly asked about this aspect, there is disagreement among the experts on whether the computable or uncomputable version is the “right” one. Yet what many of our colleagues seem to assume is that this aspect is not particularly relevant from the perspective of problems of interest to them.

The issue gets increasingly important (and confusing) with the emerging area of distributed quantum computing. When we define the quantum version of the
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
model
[
13
,
1
]
, the natural formalization is something equivalent to quantum circuits or quantum Turing machines, which is necessarily computable—there is no quantum analogue of mapping radius-
T
neighborhoods to local outputs. Combining this with a conventional uncomputable definition of the classical
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
model would lead to paradoxical results: quantum-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
would be weaker than classical
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
.

What we show in this work is that assumptions related to computability
are
indeed critical even if we focus on problems and questions commonly studied in the context of the
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
model.

1.1
Locally Checkable Labelings

Perhaps the most popular (and most restrictive) formal definition for a class of “reasonable” graph problems in this context is the family of
locally checkable labeling problems
, or LCLs, first introduced by Naor and Stockmeyer in 1995
[
20
]
. These are problems that can be specified by giving a
finite
set of valid labeled local neighborhoods. There is a long line of research that has culminated in a near-complete classification of LCL problems in the
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
model for general graphs, see e.g.
[
10
,
21
,
19
,
20
,
6
,
11
,
14
,
5
,
8
,
7
,
23
,
2
,
3
,
25
,
9
,
16
]
.

What makes LCL problems particularly nice and clean from the perspective of computability issues is that for any LCL problem
\Pi
, there are Turing machines that solve both of these tasks:

1.

Given a graph
G
and output labeling
f
, decide if
f
is a valid solution of
\Pi
in
G
.

2.

Given a graph
G
, find a feasible solution
f
of
\Pi
in
G
.

In particular, there is no LCL where merely solving the task is uncomputable; if you can solve a task with full information about
G
in uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
, you can do the same in computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
. Intuitively, LCLs should not care about computability issues.

Now what we would like to do is to formalize this intuition and prove that we can safely ignore all issues regarding computability in the context of LCL problems; we would like to show that whatever is the complexity of an LCL in uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
, it is (at least asymptotically) the same in computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
. This would mean that the numerous results on LCLs from prior work are compatible with each other regardless of what the specific paper assumes about computability.
Unfortunately, as we will show in this work, this is not the case!

1.2
Contribution 1: Separation

Our main contribution is this: we show that there exists an LCL problem
\Pi
such that:

•

in computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
, any algorithm that solves
\Pi
requires
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
rounds,

•

in uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
, there is an algorithm that solves
\Pi
in
O(\regularlog n)
rounds.

Our key take-home message is that assumptions on computability are of crucial importance already in the study of LCL problems; such issues cannot be safely ignored, results on the complexity landscape cannot be automatically ported between computable and uncomputable worlds, and this aspect needs to be made explicit. (An equally valid interpretation of our work is that LCL problems are a poor proxy for “reasonable” graph problems, as we run into issues of computability, which we should never encounter in reasonable graph problems. But currently no good alternatives exist.)

1.3
Contribution 2: Equivalences

To fully explain our contributions, it is necessary to draw attention to another subtle issue of the
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
model that tends to get swept under the carpet: what do we know about
n
, the size of the input. We will consider three cases that suffice to make the point:

1.

no auxiliary information,

2.

all nodes receive as input the same value
N
, and we promise that
n\leq N

3.

all nodes receive as input the same value
N

n\leq N\leq f(n)

.

Combining this with models computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}

\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}

This brings some good news: it turns out that numerous papers that have studied LCLs in the

•

computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}

•

computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}

.

In particular, our problem
\Pi
can be solved in

\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}

\Omega(\sqrt{n})

\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
if we do not have such information. This result seems counterintuitive: our upper bound
N
might be arbitrarily high, say, the Ackermann function of
n
, or even the busy beaver
[

of

, which seems unhelpful, yet it can be so valuable that it suffices to lower the round complexity exponentially.

2
Technical Overview

In this section, we give an overview of the techniques and key ideas that we use to prove our main results, and we also provide a road-map to the rest of this paper. Our separation result is presented in Section
4
, and our equivalence results are presented in Section
5
.

2.1
Separation Between Computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
and Uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}

First we discuss the proof for the separation between computable and
uncomputable
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
where we are not given an upper bound
N
on the number of
nodes.
The proof is by a careful construction of an LCL problem
\Pi
that requires

\Omega(\sqrt{n})
locality in computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
but only
\mathcal{O}(\regularlog n)
in its
uncomputable counterpart.

Our separation is obtained by constructing a promise-free LCL
\Pi
, where each node outputs several components (or layers). The early layers can be seen as
structure filters
that either certify that the input graph has the intended backbone structure, or they produce a
locally checkable error certificate
that points to a structural violation. Only the nodes that successfully pass all the structural filters are required to solve the final “hard” layer.

Formally, each structural layer
\Pi_{i}
is split into
\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{good}}
and
\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{bad}}
with disjoint output alphabets. Intuitively

•

\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{good}}
The structure “looks” correct and nodes output a fixed label.

•

\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{bad}}
. The structure is “broken” and nodes output an error pointer that locally routes to a witness or failure.

We then
compose
these layers so that the subsequent layers’ constraints are enforced only inside the region certified as good so far.

The backbone decomposition.

The overall problem is represented as a composition of five layers

\Pi=\Pi_{\mathrm{tree}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{row}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{grid}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{Turing}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{consensus}}

Each layer has a good and bad behavior:

•

\Pi_{\mathrm{tree}}
certifies a tree-like auxiliary structure by marking nodes as
T
, or else outputs a local pointer to a structural error.

•

\Pi_{\mathrm{row}}
certifies nodes marked with
G
form rows that are connected to trees.

•

\Pi_{\mathrm{grid}}
certifies that the rows together form a grid-like structure.

•

\Pi_{\mathrm{Turing}}
ensures that the labels on the grid encode an execution of a Turing machine.

•

\Pi_{\mathrm{consensus}}
is the hard layer which gives us the separation. In effect, the rows of the grid need to predict the outcome of the encoded Turing machine.

The growing grid structure.

The concrete structure that we would like to ensure is what we refer to as a

growing grid
. This is an oriented grid where rows are neatly aligned
(see
Figure

1(a)
) and with the additional property that
consecutive rows differ in length by
exactly
one.
Since (virtually) every row has
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
nodes, to enable
sublinear-time certification inside a row, each row is also equipped with an
attached tree-like structure with
\mathcal{O}(\regularlog n)
diameter (see

Figure

1(b)
).
The tree structure allows each row to operate as a joint computational unit that
is coordinated by the node at the root of the corresponding tree.

The hard problem
\Pi_{\mathrm{Turing}}
.

Inside the certified grid region, input labels encode a computation of a universal Turing machine
U
: each row encodes a configuration (tape symbols, head position and state), and local constraints check that consecutive rows represent valid transitions of
U
. If the encoding is inconsistent, nodes can locally point to an error, partitioning the grid into
valid
regions separated by error regions. The crucial final constraint is a
consistency
requirement: whenever a region contains a halting configuration, nodes in that region must output a globally consistent bit indicating whether the halting state is accepting or rejecting.
A subtle point is that we
must
use a machine
U
that has halting accept
and reject states; see the discussion further below for details.

Uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
solves
\Pi
in
\mathcal{O}(\regularlog n)
rounds.

In uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
we may
assume access to an oracle
H
that, given a configuration of the universal
Turing machine
U
, decides whether it halts or not as well as whether the
halting state is accepting or rejecting.
On a valid grid instance, the root of the tree attached to a row collects the
entire row’s encoding in
\mathcal{O}(\regularlog n)
rounds, determines the configuration
encoded in the row, and queries
H
.
Given the oracle’s answer, the root node sets the output for the nodes it
controls to the final state of
U
or, if the configuration is not halting, to

0
.
Since all the rows access the same oracle and the universal Turing machine is
deterministic, the output of all rows is consistent across the same component of
the input graph.

Computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
needs
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
rounds.

The lower bound relies on a diagonalization argument.
Assume that there is a computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that purportedly solves
\Pi
in

o(\sqrt{n})
rounds.
On our growing-grid instances
G_{k}
with
k
rows (and thus
\Theta(k^{2})

nodes), such a locality is
o(k)
; hence a node in a fixed early layer cannot
see the bottom boundary of the grid.
In fact, we can show that, for large enough values of
k
, nodes in these early
layers must commit to an output in a
constant number of rounds
.
We exploit this by constructing a Turing machine
T_{\mathcal{A}}
that, given an input

x
, simulates
\mathcal{A}
on
G_{k}
for a sufficiently large
k
and produces the
output bit of
\mathcal{A}
on the row with configuration corresponding to
x
.
Notice that
\mathcal{A}
always halts, hence so does
T_{\mathcal{A}}
.
Finally, we feed
T_{\mathcal{A}}
to a diagonalizer
D
that, on input
\langle T_{\mathcal{A}}\rangle
,
outputs the opposite bit whenever
T_{\mathcal{A}}
halts.
Thus, given the input
x=(\langle D\rangle,\langle T_{\mathcal{A}}\rangle)
, the nodes of
\mathcal{A}
in
the row corresponding to
x
will produce the opposite of what is required.
It follows that
\mathcal{A}
fails to solve
\Pi
, implying its complexity in computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
is
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
.

A remark about the specification of the problem output.

Let us take a closer look at the condition for the output bit we ask for in our
problem
\Pi_{\mathrm{Turing}}
:

1.

If
U
halts in the final configuration of the growing grid, then output

0
if it rejects or
1
if it accepts.

2.

Otherwise (i.e., if
U
has not halted by the final configuration), then
output either
0
or
1
consistently.

Interestingly, this condition must be very carefully chosen.
For instance, suppose we leave the problem description as is and only replace
the second point with the following:

2a.

Otherwise (i.e., if
U
has not halted by the final configuration),
then output
0
.

As it turns out,
this breaks the
\mathcal{O}(\regularlog n)
upper bound
:
Of course, with access to an uncomputable oracle, we can still correctly
determine whether
U
halts or not; however, the issue is that we must also
determine
if the input grid is large enough
to contain the entire trace
of
U
until it halts, which inevitably requires inspecting the entire grid.

A much more counterintuitive situation applies to the first of the two
requirements for the output bit.
It is a standard result in computability theory that, without restriction, we
may define Turing machines such that they are only allowed to halt if they are
in an accepting state (and enter an infinite loop whenever an input should be
rejected).
Being aware of this, we might be tempted to replace our
U
that has explicit
accept and reject states with another universal
U^{\prime}
that only halts in an
accepting state.
This simplifies the first of the two items above to the following:

1a.

If
U^{\prime}
halts in the final configuration of the growing grid, then
output
1
everywhere.

Surprisingly, this now
breaks the lower bound
:
Together with the original second item, the resulting problem is trivial since
one can always output
1
no matter what
U^{\prime}
does.
Replacing 2 with 2a does not remedy this as we then run into the same issue with
the upper bound as before (i.e., we fail to obtain an
\mathcal{O}(\regularlog n)
algorithm in
uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
).
Hence it is
crucial that our
U
has both halting accept and reject
states
, unlike what our intuition from computability theory might suggest.

An alternative upper bound in computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
with an
upper bound on
n
.

As an addendum, we also describe a strategy for solving
\Pi
in
computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
with
\mathcal{O}(\regularlog n)
when an upper bound
N
(no matter how bad)
on
n\leq N
is given.
It is true that one can use our equivalence results to obtain such an algorithm,
but it is instructive to come up with a direct strategy to grasp why the
knowledge of
N
makes such a big difference.

The idea is to proceed as in the uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm but replace the
oracle
H
with a computable procedure.
More specifically, using
N
we infer a maximal number of steps
t_{N}
that
U

can run for; otherwise its trace would not fit into the input instance we are
given.
Then we can simulate
U
until
t_{N}
steps have elapsed and, if
U
has not
halted yet, safely output
0
on all nodes.
The point is that, given
N
,
we have a bound on how large our instance
can be
(even if it is arbitrarily bad!), which allows us to circumvent the
computability issues;
without
N
, we cannot know how large our instance
actually is
, and thus it might always be large enough to contain the entire
trace of
U
until it halts—which we cannot tell for sure without inspecting
the whole instance.

2.2
Equivalence Results

Next we discuss our equivalence results.
Recall there are two equivalences that we prove:

1.

between computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
with an upper bound
N\geq n
(where possibly
also
N\leq f(n)
for some computable
f
) and uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
; and

2.

uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
with and without such an upper bound
N
.

Before we turn to these equivalences, we first discuss the concept of a

maximally safe neighborhood
, which is used in both proofs.

Maximally safe neighborhoods.

In the ball-growing view of
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
model, a deterministic algorithm can be described by a function that given the currently gathered rooted neighborhood
N^{t}(v)
and the current radius
t
, either outputs a label or returns a sentinel
\bot
to indicate “keep exploring”; the node halts at the first radius
t
for which the function returns a label. A time bound
T(n)
means that on every
n
-node network, every node must halt by round
T(n)
. A subtle detail is that nodes do not know
n
. Hence, whether a node is allowed to still be running at round
t
must be justified purely from its local view. This motivates a notion of when a radius-
t
view is consistent with a global bound
T
.

1
Informal.

For a network
G
and a node
v\in V(G)
, we say
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is

T
-safe
for
v
if there is no network
G^{\prime}
and
v^{\prime}\in V(G^{\prime})
such that the
t
-neighborhoods rooted at
v
and
v^{\prime}
are
isomorphic and
t>T(\lvert V(G^{\prime})\rvert)
.
In addition,
t
is said to be
maximally
T
-safe
for
v
if it is maximal
with this property (i.e.,
t
is safe but
t+1
is not).

The intuition behind Definition
1
is that if
N^{t}(v)
is
T
-safe, then “still not having halted before round
t
” is not in conflict with the promise that all
n^{\prime}
-node instances must halt by round
T(n^{\prime})
. Indeed, the same local situation
N^{t}(v)
cannot occur in any instance whose time bound
T(n^{\prime})
is already strictly less than
t
. Conversely, if a new view at radius
t
could also occur inside some smaller instance with
T(n^{\prime})<t
, then any rule that keeps running when seeing that view would necessarily violate the time bound on that smaller instance. That is because the node cannot distinguish the two situations.

Maximally
T
-safe neighborhoods are used as a means to define a
stopping view
that depends only on
T
and is independent of the algorithm. Moreover, when
T
is non-decreasing and computable, whether a given rooted neighborhood is maximally
T
-safe is decidable (see Lemma
5.1
), which is what lets us algorithmically enumerate and recognize these views when needed.

From uncomputable to computable with an upper bound on
n
.

The proof of this equivalence uses the following observation:

When nodes know an upper bound
N
on the number of vertices, there are only finitely many maximally
T
-safe neighborhoods that can arise in networks of size at most
N
.

This means that if there is an LCL problem
\Pi
and an uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that has an upper bound
N
on the network size
n
, then we can define a computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm
\mathcal{B}
that solves
\Pi
with the same locality and knowledge of the same upper bound
N
on the number of nodes. The computable algorithm
\mathcal{B}
simply enumerates all these finitely many maximally
T
-safe neighborhoods and searches for a mapping from them to output labels that satisfies the LCL constraints (see Theorem
3
).

Removing the upper bounds on
n
.

Given a family
\{\mathcal{A}_{N}\}
of algorithms that work with knowledge of
N
, we “sandbox” each
\mathcal{A}_{N}
by first growing the view until it becomes maximally
T
-safe, then simulating
\mathcal{A}_{N}
inside that neighborhood, and outputting
\bot
if the simulation ever tries to inspect nodes outside it. The key point is that a maximally safe neighborhood cannot be enlarged without risking a violation of the time bound, so this normalization stays within
T(n)
rounds and makes the later stabilization argument possible (see Theorem
4
).

A final remark.

Our equivalence results combine two different ingredients. The conversion from an uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm to a computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
one given an upper bound is constructive. Indeed, we can enumerate the (finitely many) maximally
T
-safe neighborhoods and search for a correct mapping to output labels. In contrast, removing the need for an upper bound in uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
relies on an existential stabilization step: we pass to an infinite subsequence of bounds on which the sandboxed algorithms agree on each neighborhood (see the Construction step
2
in
Theorem

4
). We do not currently know a direct constructive transformation that replaces this stabilization step.

. For any subset of nodes

. For any nodes

; if

.
The degree of a node

\deg_{G}(v)
. We drop the subscript

Since we are considering distributed models, our definition of the radius-

neighborhood of a node is tailored to match precisely what can be gathered in

r
rounds of a distributed message passing protocol (with unbounded
communication).
It is indeed very similar to the standard graph-theoretic one, but there is a
fine distinction to be made, which we discuss after the definition.

2
Neighborhoods.

Let

Extended to the radius-

We consider the class of all graphs of maximum degree

\mathcal{G}=\left\{G=(V,E)\mid\max_{u\in V}\deg(u)\leq\Delta\right\}.

Such a graph class is
recursively enumerable

, but we stress that choice of the graph family does not matter either that much. We make use of this graph class to show our separation results in
Section

Models of computation.

The
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
model
[
18
]
is a distributed model of computation in which each node in the network executes the same algorithm independently. Computation proceeds in synchronous rounds, and in each round every node may exchange messages with all of its neighbors. There is no bound on the size of the messages (unbounded communication). Every node must eventually terminate and produce an output. The complexity measure in this model is the number of rounds required until all nodes output.

We define the
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
model in the so-called
ball growing
formalism.
The intuition for this model is the following:
Suppose we are considering the execution of our distributed algorithm from the
perspective of a node
v\in V(G)
.
The node has witnessed
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
rounds of the algorithm but has not
committed to an output yet.
Since in
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
we have access to unbounded communication, without restriction
during these
t
rounds the node
v
has gathered complete information about the
rooted neighborhood
\mathcal{N}^{t}(v)
corresponding to it.
At this point, it needs to make a decision:
Does it commit to an output based on what it has seen so far (i.e.,
\mathcal{N}^{t}(v)
),
or does it explore the graph a bit further,
growing

\mathcal{N}^{t}(v)
to

\mathcal{N}^{t+1}(v)
?

6
Ball-growing
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
.

Fix label sets
\Sigma_{\mathrm{in}}
and
\Sigma_{\mathrm{out}}
.
The
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
model in the
ball-growing
formalism is defined by a map

f\colon\mathfrak{N}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\Sigma_{\mathrm{out}}\cup\{\bot\}
, where
\bot\notin\Sigma_{\mathrm{out}}
is a sentinel value.
Given a network
(G,x,\mathtt{deg},\mathtt{id},\{p_{v}\})
as input, the output of a node
v\in V(G)

is the value
f(\mathcal{N}^{t}(v),t)
where
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is minimal such that

f(\mathcal{N}^{t}(v),t)\neq\bot
.
This can be thought of as the result of executing the following iterative
process at each node
v
:

1.

Initialize
t
to zero.

2.

If
f(\mathcal{N}^{t}(v),t)\neq\bot
, output it; otherwise increment
t
and
repeat.

Without restriction, we consider only
“sensible”
algorithms where, if

\mathcal{N}^{t}(v)
has radius
t
, then
f(\mathcal{N}^{t}(v),t^{\prime})=f(\mathcal{N}^{t}(v),t+1)\neq\bot
for
every
t^{\prime}\geq t+1
.

Without further qualifications, the above gives us the uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
model.
If we also insist that
f
is a computable function, then the resulting model
is computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
.
The
complexity
or
locality
of a
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm (in either
case) is the minimal function
T\colon\mathbb{N}_{+}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that, if
G
is a
graph on
n
nodes and
v\in V(G)
is a node in a network
(G,x,\mathtt{deg},\mathtt{id},\{p_{v}\})
,
then there is
t\leq T(n)
for which
f(\mathcal{N}^{t}(v),t)\neq\bot
.

Note that equivalently,
T(n)
can be seen as the worst-case number of rounds required over all graphs of size
n
. It follows that
T
is non-decreasing: if
n_{1}\leq n_{2}
, then the set of graphs with
n_{1}
nodes is contained in the class of graphs with
n_{2}
nodes, and taking the maximum over a larger class cannot yield a smaller value. Hence,
T(n_{1})\;\leq\;T(n_{2})
.

4
Separation Result

In this section, we prove the following result:

1
.

There is a problem
\Pi
that has round complexity
\Omega(\sqrt{n})
in
computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
but only
\mathcal{O}(\regularlog n)
in uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
.

4.1
LCL Backbone

Before we present the definition of our LCL problem, we introduce a few concepts
that are helpful in keeping the description more digestible.
This approach is implicit in many works
[
4
]
; we make
the concept explicit here in order to better structure the presentation.

The first of these concepts is the notion of a
certification LCL problem
.
This is an LCL problem
\Pi=(\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{in}},\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{in}},\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{out}},\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{out}},\mathcal{C})

that actually consists of two sub-problems
\Pi^{\mathrm{good}}
and
\Pi^{\mathrm{bad}}
where the
label sets of
\Pi^{\mathrm{good}}
and
\Pi^{\mathrm{bad}}
are incompatible with each other.
More precisely, we have the following:

•

\Pi^{\mathrm{good}}
and
\Pi^{\mathrm{bad}}
use the same sets
\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{in}}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{in}}
as input
labels.

•

As output labels,
\Pi^{\mathrm{good}}
uses
\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\mathrm{good}}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\mathrm{good}}
while

\Pi^{\mathrm{bad}}
uses
\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\mathrm{bad}}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\mathrm{bad}}
, where we have
\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\mathrm{good}}\cap\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\mathrm{bad}}=\varnothing
and
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\mathrm{good}}\cap\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{out}}_{\mathrm{bad}}=\varnothing
.
The output label sets of
\Pi
, that is,
\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{out}}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{out}}
are the resulting
disjoint unions thereof, respectively.

•

\Pi^{\mathrm{good}}
and
\Pi^{\mathrm{bad}}
specify different constraint sets
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{good}}

and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{bad}}
.
The constraint set
\mathcal{C}
of
\Pi
is the union
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{good}}\cup\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{bad}}
.

Whenever we deal with such an LCL problem
\Pi
, we write
\Pi=(\Pi^{\mathrm{good}},\Pi^{\mathrm{bad}})
to refer to these two parts.
(In this definition as presented we make no distinction between
\Pi^{\mathrm{good}}
and

\Pi^{\mathrm{bad}}
yet; this only appears in connection with the next concept.)

The second concept that we need is the notion of
composing
LCL problems
where one of the problems is a certification LCL problem.
Given a certification LCL problem
\Pi_{1}=(\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{in}}_{1},\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{in}}_{1},\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{out}}_{1},\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{out}}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{1})
and another (general) LCL problem
\Pi_{2}=(\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{in}}_{2},\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{in}}_{2},\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{out}}_{2},\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{out}}_{2},\mathcal{C}_{2})
, we define
\Pi_{1}\otimes\Pi_{2}
to be the LCL problem
\Pi

with the following characteristics:

•

The sets of input labels of
\Pi
are
\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{in}}_{1}\times\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{in}}_{1}\times\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{in}}_{2}
for nodes and edges, respectively.

•

The sets of output labels are
\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{out}}_{1}\times(\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{out}}_{2}\cup\{\bot\})

and
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{out}}_{1}\times(\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{out}}_{2}\cup\{\bot\})
, where
\bot
is a sentinel
value that is not an element of
\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{out}}_{2}
or
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{out}}_{2}
.

•

The constraints of
\Pi
are as follows:

–

An output
(x,y)
of a node or half-edge is only admissible if it is
one of the following:

1.

If
x
is an output label from
\Pi_{1}^{\mathrm{good}}
, then
y
is an output
label from
\Pi_{2}
(i.e.,
y\neq\bot
).

2.

If
x
is an output label from
\Pi_{1}^{\mathrm{bad}}
, then
y=\bot
.

–

If we project the outputs to their first component everywhere, then
the resulting graph satisfies
\mathcal{C}_{1}
.

–

If we project the nodes’ output to their second component everywhere
and disregard nodes and edges that have
\bot
as output (where we disregard
the entire edge if either half-edge is labeled
\bot
), then the resulting
graph satisfies
\mathcal{C}_{2}
.

This definition naturally extends to the composition of multiple problems
\Pi_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\Pi_{k}
(where
\Pi_{i}
is a certification LCL problem for
every
i<k
) in the obvious manner.

The idea behind these two formalisms is to use the certification LCL problems as
a
“filter”
that only allows instances with a structure compatible with
the certification scheme of
\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{good}}
to pass through.
Undesirable structures can be detected and the offending parts certified using
labels from
\Pi_{i}^{\mathrm{bad}}
.
(The latter is required in order for the problem to not be trivially solvable.)
This approach enables us to turn a problem
\Pi_{k}
that gives us a separation
assuming a certain structure into a promise-free separation by embedding the
certification of the structure into the problems
\Pi_{1},\dots,\Pi_{k-1}
.

We now give an overview of the LCL problem that we construct.
Namely our problem is

\Pi=\Pi_{\mathrm{tree}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{row}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{grid}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{Turing}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{consensus}}

where the individual problems are as follows:

1.

In
\Pi_{\mathrm{tree}}
, we certify a complete bipartite tree structure
everywhere the nodes receive as input label a special symbol
\mathrm{T}
.
The certificate comes from the sub-problem
\Pi_{\mathrm{tree}}^{\mathrm{good}}
.
If any connected component formed by nodes with this input does not form a
complete bipartite tree, then we can output a certificate for this (using
labels from
\Pi_{\mathrm{tree}}^{\mathrm{bad}}
) that can be checked with constant radius.
Nodes that are not marked with
\mathrm{T}
are quiescent; any output is valid for them.

2.

\Pi_{\mathrm{row}}
, is similar to
\Pi_{\mathrm{tree}}
in that we are also certifying a structure in nodes marked with
\mathrm{G}
, in this case that an oriented row is properly attached to the leaves of a tree.
In particular, nodes satisfying
\Pi_{\mathrm{row}}^{\mathrm{good}}
form rows that have beginnings and ends, and these match the left-most and right-most nodes of the attached trees.
Also, every row has only one tree attached to it, which means that all nodes of the row are within distance
O(\regularlog n)
of each other.
If this does not hold, then the algorithm can solve
\Pi_{\mathrm{row}}^{\mathrm{bad}}
which certifies that there exists an error in the row.

3.

With
\Pi_{\mathrm{grid}}
, we continue our certification of the structure of nodes marked with
\mathrm{G}
, this time certifying that the nodes form an oriented grid where each row is larger than the previous one by
exactly one node
at the far right end of the row, which we name the
growing grid
.
Again, if the structure is correct, then we can certify it using labels from

\Pi_{\mathrm{grid}}^{\mathrm{good}}
; if the opposite is the case, then we can show this using
labels from
\Pi_{\mathrm{grid}}^{\mathrm{bad}}
.

4.

The problem
\Pi_{\mathrm{Turing}}
embeds the trace of a universal Turing
machine
U
in the growing grid structure evolving in the downward direction.
It contains specifications such that every configuration of
U
corresponds to
a row in the grid.
This includes ensuring that the configuration has a single Turing machine head
(which is checked using the tree structure) and that the configuration of the
respective row arises after one step of
U
from the one in the previous row
(which can be checked locally).
As before, any detected violations can be certified using the sub-problem

\Pi^{\mathrm{bad}}_{\mathrm{Turing}}
.
Otherwise the algorithm may solve
\Pi^{\mathrm{good}}_{\mathrm{Turing}}
.

5.

The last problem
\Pi_{\mathrm{consensus}}
is the one where we get a separation between computable and uncomputable versions of the
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
model.
In every connected component, if the configuration where
U
halts is present,
then we must consistently output a bit corresponding to the accept or reject
state of
U
; otherwise, that is, if
U
has not halted yet in the trace that
we are given, then we are allowed to output any of the two bits as long as it
is consistent across the component.

) specified in

2

It is not hard to check that the resultant labeling satisfies the constraints

{lemma}

2.

\bot

Here is a
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
certification algorithm which runs in

Certification algorithm.

Each node

maintains the explored ball

after round

1.

from

4.2.3
Turing Machine Execution

We now move on to defining a Turing machine on the growing grid.
We start by fixing our definition of Turing machines so that a machine
M
is a tuple
(Q,\Sigma,q_{0},F,\delta)
where:

•

Q
is a set of states;

•

\Sigma
is the set of all symbols that can appear on the tape;

•

F=\{\mathsf{accept},\mathsf{reject}\}
is the set of halting states (where of
course
\mathsf{accept}\neq\mathsf{reject}
);

•

\delta:Q\times\Sigma\rightarrow Q\times\Sigma\times\{\mathtt{L},\mathtt{R},\mathtt{S}\}
is the transition function that describes how a single computation step is performed.
If the machine is either in state
\mathsf{accept}
or
\mathsf{reject}
, then the output Turing machine state must not evolve, that is

\delta(q,b)=(q,b,\mathtt{S})\quad\text{for $q\in\{\mathsf{accept},\mathsf{reject}\}$.}

Note that we consider Turing machines that explicitly either accept or reject the input. With the goal of maintaining a promise free approach, we adopt the following scheme: First, we define constraints on the input labels of
\Pi_{\mathrm{Turing}}
that certify whether the input labeling encodes a valid computation of a universal Turing machine. If this is not the case, then we define constraints that make the output labels point towards a mistake inside a row of the grid. This essentially partitions the graph into regions of valid computations, separated by regions with errors. Then, within the region of valid computations, the output node labels have to satisfy constraints that are dependent on the final state of the Turing-machine computation. In effect, the output
predicts
whether the Turing machine halts in an accepting or a rejecting state. This final step is the key ingredient to show a separation between computable and uncomputable
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
.

Turing LCL.

Here we define the LCL problem
\Pi_{\mathrm{Turing}}
, which will allow us to show the separation between computable and uncomputable
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
. We start by picking a universal Turing machine
U
on binary alphabet, and we denote
\langle M\rangle
the description of Turing machine
M
on the tape of
U
. Note that we aim to encode the computation of a universal Turing machine

{lemma}

Let

Thanks to

, we know that both

be a node in

with input

and let

. We consider two cases.

Case

.

, and all the three Constraints

To satisfy Constraints

We have now shown that

indeed certifies that the input consists of fragments of traces of execution of universal Turing machine

{corollary}

be a labeled graph satisfying

.
Then it consists of components which encode a correct execution of the universal Turing machine

.

We also note that there exists a locality-
O(\regularlog n)
computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm for certifying this structure:

{lemma}

There exists a locality-
O(\regularlog n)
computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm which, given a labeled graph

,

1.

Solves
\Pi_{\mathrm{tree}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{row}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{grid}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{Turing}}
, and

2.

Those regions where the algorithm solves

form a valid execution trace of Turing machine

.

Proof.

By
Section

4.2.2
, we can solve problem
\Pi_{\mathrm{tree}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{row}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{grid}}
with locality
O(\regularlog n)
.
We can concentrate on components which solve
\Pi_{\mathrm{goodGrid}}
; those form a growing grid.
Moreover, the nodes of every row are within distance
O(\regularlog n)
from each other via the trees attached to the rows.

Now the algorithm is as follows:
Every node

collects the whole row
r_{1}
it is on, and the row
r_{2}
south of it; it can do this with locality
O(\regularlog n)
using the trees.
It then checks whether all nodes of

satisfy constraints

; if so, it outputs

.
Otherwise it outputs labels

and

.

It is easy to see that either all nodes of a row output
\bot

\Pi_{\mathrm{goodTuring}}
, or all nodes output error pointers, in which case the whole row solves
\Pi_{\mathrm{badTuring}}
.
∎

4.2.4
Consensus Problem

Finally, we define the consensus LCL problem as follows:

\Pi_{\mathrm{consensus}}=(\{\bot\},\{\bot\},\{\mathsf{accept},\mathsf{reject}\},\{\bot\},\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{consensus}}).

The constraint

Moreover, if the Turing machine halts, then the output will coincide with the halting state of the Turing machine.
Here, like in the definition of

, input labels

,

have input

,

,

4.3
Separation

The final LCL we defined in the last section is

\Pi=\Pi_{\mathrm{tree}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{row}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{grid}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{Turing}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{consensus}}.

Now that
\Pi
is defined, we may present the actual separation result.
First we deal with the upper bound part.

{lemma}

There is an uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm that solves
\Pi
with locality
O(\regularlog n)
.

Proof.

Since we are designing an uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm, we may as well assume that
we have an oracle
H
that, given a configuration
c
of our universal Turing
machine
U
, returns the state
H(c)
in which
U
halts when starting from

c
or some special symbol to indicate
U
does not halt.

By
Section

4.2.3
, we have a computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm that solves

\Pi_{\mathrm{tree}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{row}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{grid}}\otimes\Pi_{\mathrm{Turing}}

with locality
O(\regularlog n)
.

It therefore suffices us to consider here only the components which satisfy
\Pi_{\mathrm{goodTuring}}
; by
Section

4.2.3
, these components form a correct execution of the universal Turing machine
U
.

As all nodes of each row are within distance
O(\regularlog n)
from each other with the help of the trees, we can operate each row as a single entity which is controlled by the west-most node of the row.
From the input labels, the row can then determine the configuration
c
that is encoded in the row and query
H
.
After doing so, it sets the output for the nodes it controls to
b
, where

b=\begin{cases}H(c),&\text{$U$ halts when starting from $c$}\\
\mathsf{reject},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Since all rows use the same oracle
H
and
H(c)=H(c^{\prime})
if
c^{\prime}
is a
predecessor configuration of
c
(and
U
is deterministic), the output of all
rows is consistent across the same connected component: if
U
halts when
starting from the very first configuration
c_{0}
in the component, then all
nodes output
H(c_{0})
; otherwise,
U
does not halt starting from
c_{0}
,
and hence all nodes output
\mathsf{reject}
.
∎

The lower bound part of the separation is as follows:

{lemma}

Any computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm that solves
\Pi
requires
\Omega(\sqrt{n})

locality.

To prove this, we need the following elementary diagonalization strategy:

{lemma}

There is a Turing machine

such that, for every Turing machine
T
, the
following holds:

1.

If
T
does not halt on the input

, then

x_{T}
.

2.

Otherwise
D
and
T
disagree on

, that is, we have
D(x_{T})\neq T(x_{T})
.

Proof.

We describe the operation of a Turing machine
D
.
Using standard techniques from computability theory (i.e., so-called

“quines”
; see, e.g.,
[
24
]
), we may assume
that
D
has access to its own description.
Given
x_{T}
,

simply does the following:

1.

Simulate
T
on
x_{T}
.

2.

If
T
halts, then output
1-T(x_{T})
.

Hence, if
T
does not halt, then so will

not halt.
Otherwise
D
outputs exactly the opposite of what
T
outputs on
x_{T}
.
∎

As usual in computability theory, it might seem at first confusing what results
from executing

on the input

; however, notice that the description is
consistent and this just results in an infinite recursion loop.

The roadmap for the proof is as follows:
First we define a sequence of instances
G_{k}
for

, in particular
setting identifiers on all the nodes.
Then, assuming we have a
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm

that runs in

time and is computable, we can derive a Turing machine
T
that
simulates
\mathcal{A}
and outputs whatever it does.
Finally, using
Section

4.3
,

gives us an adversarial instance
that
\mathcal{A}
fails to solve correctly.

Let us first fix a sequence of instances

We refer to the bundle of a row together with the tree structure on

Hence the

•

•

The inputs are chosen so that all nodes, except for nodes on constantly-many layers close to layers

In addition, we define a binary

Then

For the row nodes in the

For the row nodes that are in a layer subsequent to the

Hence the

Equivalence Results

In this section we prove our equivalence results. First, we show that once nodes are provided with any global upper bound
N
on the network size (and optionally a promise of the form
N\leq f(n)
for a fixed computable

), the gap between computable and uncomputable
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
vanishes: every uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}

\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm with the same locality.
Second, we show that in uncomputable
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
the bound

is in fact unnecessary: from a family

of

-round algorithms that work for all

rounds and agrees with

for a suitable choice of

that we describe in the following subsection.

5.1
Maximally Safe Neighborhoods—and How to Find Them

Here we discuss the concept of maximally safe neighborhood, a useful tool that we will use in our equivalence proofs.
Note that the concept depends only on a given complexity function

and is

independent
of any particular algorithm under consideration.

10
.

Let
T\colon\mathbb{N}_{+}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
be a function and
\mathcal{G}
a class of graphs.
For a network
(G,x,\mathtt{deg},\mathtt{id},\{p_{v}\})

for

and

such that the

are
isomorphic and
t>T(\lvert V(G^{\prime})\rvert)
.
Moreover, we say

is

for

if it is maximal
with this property (i.e.,

is safe but

Without ambiguity, we extend these notions to the rooted neighborhoods
themselves; that is, if

is (maximally)
T

\mathcal{N}^{t}(v)
is also
(maximally)
T
-safe
.

Note that, if we fix

-safe

This is because we may always pick

,

\mathtt{id}^{\prime}=\mathtt{id}

in the condition above, in which case the only non-trivial requirement is

, which must hold for all but finitely many

{lemma}

Let
T
be non-decreasing and computable, and let
\mathcal{G}
be an
enumerable class of graphs.
Then, given a rooted neighborhood
X
of radius
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, it is
decidable if
X
is maximally
T
-safe or not.

Since
T
is non-decreasing, it can be argued that there are only finitely many
counter-examples to test in order to check whether
X
is maximally
T
-safe
or not.
That being said, we must first deal with some technicalities concerning the
function
T
.

Proof.

Let
X
be given.
Since
T
is non-decreasing, there are two options for its behavior relative
to the value
t
:

1.

Either there is
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
such that
t<T(N)
; or

2.

There is a constant
C\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and a value
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
for which,
for every
N^{\prime}\geq N_{0}
,
t=T(N^{\prime})=C
.

In the latter case,
C
is finite information that we can encode in our
decision procedure, so we can assume it to be known and fixed (together with

T
).
For convenience, we can imagine we have
C=\infty
in the former case and
then handle both cases simultaneously.
Moreover, note that, since
T
is computable, it is possible in the former
case to compute a value
N
with said property (i.e.,
t<T(N)
).

Now notice that, if
t
is not maximally
T
-safe, then any network with
n

nodes that proves this (i.e., containing a rooted neighborhood
X^{\prime}

isomorphic to

that violates
t\leq T(n)
) must be such that
n<N
.
Hence we can execute the following strategy:

1.

If
t=C
, directly accept.

2.

Otherwise, enumerate over every graph
G\in\mathcal{G}
on
n<N

nodes and over every node
v\in V(G)
.

3.

Reject if
X
is isomorphic to the radius-
t
neighborhood of
v
in

G
and
t<T(n)
.

4.

If the enumeration is complete and
X
has not been rejected, then
accept.

Clearly, this procedure only rejects if it finds a counter-example to
X

being maximally
T
-safe.
Moreover, as we have just reasoned, any such counter-example must come from a
network on less than
N
nodes (or, if
t=C
, then it is clear such a
counter-example cannot exist).
Hence the procedure correctly decides whether
X
is maximally
T
-safe.
∎

5.2
Equivalence Between Computable and Arbitrary

3
.

Let
\Pi
be an LCL problem, and let
T\colon\mathbb{N}_{+}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
f\colon\mathbb{N}_{+}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
be computable non-decreasing functions.
If there exists an uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}

\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}

\Pi

Given the existence of an uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm solving
\Pi

\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm that also solves
\Pi
with the same locality.
The idea of the computable algorithm is to construct all possible mappings from maximally
T
-safe neighborhoods to output labelings.
There are only finitely many maximally
T
-safe neighborhoods on at-most-
N
-node-graphs, and only finitely many output labels, so our computable algorithm can enumerate over all mappings from
T
-safe neighborhoods to output labels.
As we know that the uncomputable algorithm exists, we know that this process must find at least one valid mapping.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm solving
\Pi
with the knowledge of an upper bound
N
on the size of the instance.
We provide a computable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm that solves
\Pi
with the knowledge of an upper bound
N
on the size of the instance.
The algorithm works in two phases: it first locally finds a valid mapping from input neighborhoods to outputs, and then applies this mapping.

The algorithm starts phase one by constructing the set of all valid input
networks
\mathcal{G}
such that for every
(G,x,\deg,\mathtt{id},\{p_{v}\})\in\mathcal{G}

it holds that
|V(G)|\leq N\leq f(|V(G)|)
.
Set
\mathcal{G}
is finite as we have an upper bound on the size of each network, the input labels come from a finite set, and we have an upper bound on the identifier space.
The algorithm then collects all maximally
T
-safe neighborhoods
\mathcal{M}
from these graphs; this is possible by
Section

5.1
.
Finally, the algorithm enumerates over all possible mappings

from these neighborhoods to output labels; this is possible as the set of neighborhoods and the set of possible output labels is finite.
For each possible mapping, the algorithm tests whether this mapping solves all of the constructed instances and stops once it finds a suitable mapping.
We defer arguing why such mapping
g^{\star}
exists to the end of the proof.

Now every node of the network knows the mapping
g^{\star}
.
In the second phase, every node finds its maximally
T
-safe neighborhood by enumerating over all radii of the neighborhoods until it finds the maximally
T
-safe neighborhood; this is again possible by
Section

5.1
.
Every node then applies mapping

on this neighborhood and halts.
It is clear that this solves
\Pi
as

was chosen such that it works on all graphs that satisfy
|V(G)|\leq N\leq f(|V(G)|)

The only thing left to argue is that such function
g^{\star}
exists in the first place; if such a function exists, then our algorithm will find it by just enumerating over all of the possible functions.
Assume now for contradiction that no such function exists.
Then for every candidate mapping
g
from maximally
T
-safe neighborhoods to output labels there must exist a network
(G,x,\deg,\mathtt{id},\{p_{v}\})\in\mathcal{G}
that

fails to label the graph correctly.
In particular, this holds for the mapping of the uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithm

\mathcal{A}
doesn’t work correctly, or the algorithm produces

to signify that the neighborhood is not large enough.
Consider this neighborhood

for which algorithm
\mathcal{A}
produces
\bot
.
This neighborhood is maximal
T
-safe neighborhood by construction.
Hence there exists some concrete network
(G^{\prime},x^{\prime},\deg^{\prime},\mathtt{id}^{\prime},\{p_{v}^{\prime}\})\in\mathcal{G}
containing
\mathcal{N}
such that the radius of
\mathcal{N}
is
T(|G^{\prime}|)
.
Consider now executing algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on this network:
\mathcal{A}
requires more than locality

\mathcal{A}
being
T
.
Hence we conclude that mapping

must exist.
∎

5.3

n

Here we show that, under our assumptions, and for computable running times, access to an upper bound on the network size does not provide any additional power to deterministic uncomputable-
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}
algorithms. More precisely, we show that whenever an LCL problem
\Pi
can be solved by a family of algorithms

that know an upper bound

on the number of nodes

\mathcal{B}
that solves the same

To this end, fix a computable bound

-safe neighborhoods is enumerable by

5.1
. Let
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},\cdots
be a fixed enumeration of all maximally

-safe neighborhoods.

4
.

Let
\Pi
be an LCL problem,

a family of (possibly uncomputable)

(i)

Then there exists a
\operatorname{\mathsf{LOCAL}}

Proof.

We split the construction of

Construction step 1: sandboxing the upper-bound algorithms.

Based on

4.

Otherwise output the label chosen by

We observe that both steps fit in

{lemma}
