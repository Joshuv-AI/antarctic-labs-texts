Nursing research output and its influencing factors among registered nurses in Zhejiang Province of China
Authors: Yehong Wei, Gaimei Wang, Yujia Gu, Yijing Ling, Owusu Mensah Solomon, Xufeng Chen, Caijiao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNursing research plays an important role in advancing nursing science and improving the quality of care. This cross-sectional study surveyed 512 registered nurses (RNs) in Zhejiang Province, China, to collect their sociodemographic data and examine their research attitudes, capability, and output. Research capability was assessed using the Self-Evaluated Nursing Research Capacity Questionnaire, and research output was defined as publications or patents within the past five years. The results showed that while 86.7% of RNs recognized the importance of research and 62.1% were willing to participate, their median research capability score was 45 out of 120, and the majority (60.7%) had no research output in the past five years. Only 15.8% of RNs were currently involved in or preparing for research. Promotion was the primary motivation for research, whereas lack of knowledge and skills was the main barrier. Logistic regression analysis revealed that higher education level, longer work experience, advanced professional titles, and stronger research capability were significantly associated with greater research output. Due to convenience sampling and the geographic limitation of a developed region of eastern China, the generalizability of these findings warrants caution. Overall, RNs in Zhejiang demonstrated strong interest in research, but their capability and output remain limited. To enhance nurses’ participation and contribution to research, hospital managers should create a supportive environment by providing systematic research training, opportunities for further education, and access to necessary resources, particularly for those with less experience and lower professional titles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51524-9.Keywords: Influencing factor, Nursing research, Registered nurse, Research capability, Research outputSubject terms: Health occupations, Medical research

Introduction

Nursing research is defined as a systematic scientific inquiry aimed at explaining nursing phenomena, generating new knowledge, and providing evidence to inform nursing practice, education, and management1. As a vital component of the nursing discipline, nursing research not only advances nursing science and practice but also fosters nurses’ professional development2,3. Nurses’ research capability and research output are two key indicators for assessing the state of nursing research. The former refers to nurses’ comprehensive competencies in research design, data analysis, literature synthesis, and academic writing, whereas the latter reflects tangible research outcomes, including academic publications, research projects, patents, and software4. Evidence suggests that greater innovation capacity and higher research output among nurses contribute to optimized clinical workflows, enhanced job satisfaction, and improved team performance5. Engagement in research enhances nurses’ decision-making skills, self-efficacy, and professional identity, thereby supporting their sustained career development6.

Given the important role of nursing research in advancing the discipline and supporting individual development, a growing body of literature has examined the state of nursing research both in China and internationally7–9. Overall, the research capability of Chinese nurses has improved steadily, but there remains a persistent gap in both the quantity and quality of research output compared with high-income countries10,11. For instance, between 2011 and 2020, China published 413 articles in the top 10 global nursing journals, substantially fewer than the United States (2382) and Australia (1620)10. At the regional level, Zhejiang Province—a relatively developed region in eastern China with advanced economic and medical resources—has increasingly prioritized the development of nursing research and the strengthening of nurses’ research capability in recent years. According to statistics, nursing research projects funded by the Zhejiang Provincial Medical and Health Science and Technology Program increased from 518 in 2019 to 1797 in 2023, representing a growth rate of 246.91%12. However, despite this significant increase in funded projects, the research capability and output of nurses may remain suboptimal. Notably, nurses in Zhejiang constitute 43.58% of the province’s health technical workforce, while leading only 15.91% of all medical and health research projects12. This disparity reflects a misalignment between the nursing workforce’s size and its research productivity, suggesting considerable room for improvement in nursing research output. Therefore, a thorough understanding of the current status of nursing research output and its influencing factors is needed to inform the development of targeted incentive strategies.

While macro-level bibliometric analyses have provided valuable insights into the overall trends in nursing research development in China, they are inherently limited in revealing the individual-level behaviors, subjective perceptions, and practical barriers that frontline nurses encounter during research engagement. By contrast, cross-sectional surveys are better suited to explore the research capability, output, and underlying influencing mechanisms among clinical nurses. However, existing cross-sectional studies have predominantly focused on research capability or training needs, with limited attention given to directly examining research output13,14. Moreover, these studies have largely relied on self-report questionnaires, which may entail a relative lack of objective indicators15. Furthermore, although the importance of research capability has been widely recognized, empirical evidence supporting its association with research output remains scarce. Investigating the factors that influence research output is essential for identifying key elements beyond research capability that enhance research productivity, moving beyond a narrow focus on enhancing nurses’ research capability.

To address these research gaps, the present study aims to (1) assess the research attitudes, capability, and output of registered nurses (RNs) in Zhejiang Province, with research output measured using objective indicators such as number of publications and patents; and (2) identify factors influencing their research output to inform strategies for enhancing nursing research productivity.

Methods

Study design and theoretical framework

This study employed a multicenter cross-sectional design and was guided by the ability–motivation–opportunity (AMO) model as its theoretical foundation. Widely applied in organizational behavior and performance research, the AMO model posits that individual performance is jointly shaped by three dimensions: ability, motivation, and opportunity16. Drawing on this framework, we examined factors influencing research output across four domains: sociodemographic characteristics, research capability, research facilitators, and research barriers.

Specifically, research capability was classified within the ability dimension. Facilitators of research, which encompassed both internal motivators (e.g., self-improvement) and external motivators (e.g., promotion requirements), were aligned with the motivation dimension, whereas barriers to research, including time constraints, organizational support, and access to resources, were aligned with the opportunity dimension. Sociodemographic characteristics were included as background variables to identify the distribution of research output across different subgroups.

Participants

A convenience sampling method was used to recruit 518 RNs from eight tertiary Grade A hospitals in Zhejiang Province, including six in Hangzhou, one in Ningbo, and one in Shaoxing. Tertiary Grade A hospitals were selected because they serve as regional medical centers with relatively well-established research management systems, funding support, and collaborative platforms. These institutions typically employ nursing staff with higher educational levels and provide more opportunities for research. Thus, this setting is particularly suitable for investigating research output, as it minimizes potential bias that might arise from limited research resources and inadequate sample data in primary healthcare institutions.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) being a frontline clinical nurse with a valid nursing license, and (2) providing informed consent to participate voluntarily. Exclusion criteria were: (1) holding an intern or trainee nursing position, and (2) completing the questionnaire in less than a predefined minimum completion time or providing incomplete responses.

Variables

Based on a review of the literature and consideration of the clinical nursing context, a structured questionnaire was developed consisting of four sections: sociodemographic characteristics, research attitudes, research capability, and research output. Sociodemographic characteristics included gender, age, years of work experience, marital status, number of children, educational background, professional title, employment type, and administrative position. The research attitudes section assessed nurses’ perceptions of the importance and impact of research, as well as their motivations and perceived difficulties in conducting research. Research output was measured by the number of publications and patents obtained within the past five years. Publications were defined as formally published articles in domestic or international peer-reviewed journals, excluding general conference proceedings, reports, and theses. Patents referred only to those that had been officially granted.

Research capability was assessed using the Self-Evaluated Nursing Research Capacity Questionnaire, adapted by Pan et al.17. This instrument was considered the most appropriate tool for evaluating nursing research competence among existing instruments18. It comprises six dimensions: problem identification (3 items), literature review (5 items), research design (5 items), research implementation (6 items), data processing (5 items), and academic writing (6 items). Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (unable to perform) to 4 (fully able to perform), with total scores ranging from 0 to 120. Based on the total score, research capability was categorized into three levels: low (0–40), moderate (41–80), and high (81–120). The scale demonstrated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.861 and a test–retest reliability of 0.902.

Data collection

Data was collected between June and August 2023. Prior to data collection, the survey team received standardized training and used uniform instructions to explain the study purpose and questionnaire completion procedures to participants. After obtaining informed consent, electronic questionnaires were distributed. Participants were given 24 h to complete the questionnaire using a computer or mobile device. All responses were collected anonymously and no personally identifiable information was recorded. Two researchers were involved in data management: one exported the data from the survey platform and constructed the dataset, while the other performed data verification to ensure accuracy and reliability.

Statistical analysis

Data were organized using Excel 2017, and statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 21.0. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables that were not normally distributed were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges. Univariate analyses were performed using chi-square tests or non-parametric tests, as appropriate. Variables that reached statistical significance in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression analysis. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed P < 0.05.

Ethics consideration

This study was granted an exemption from ethical review by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University. All procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and the study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 518 RNs were recruited, and 512 valid questionnaires were returned, yielding an effective response rate of 98.8%. Among the 512 participants, 89.8% were female. The majority were aged between 20 and 39 years (n = 415, 81.1%), and 46.3% (n = 237) had more than 10 years of work experience. Most RNs (88.9%) held a bachelor’s degree. In terms of professional titles, 47.7% (n = 244) had junior titles, and 38.9% (n = 199) held intermediate titles. Research participation and output of RNs

Regarding research training, 69.5% (n = 356) of RNs reported having no systematic research training. Only 15.8% (n = 81) were either currently engaged in or preparing to undertake research projects. In terms of research output, 30.9% (n = 158) had published papers in the past five years, primarily in Chinese journals. Of these publications, the most common article types were review articles (n = 115, 35.1%), followed by research articles (n = 99, 30.2%) and case studies (n = 39, 11.9%). Influencing factors of RNs’ research output

Based on whether they had published papers or obtained patents within the past five years, participants were categorized into a research output group (n = 201, 39.3%) and a non-research output group (n = 311, 60.7%). Univariate analysis revealed that RNs in the research output group had significantly higher age and longer nursing working experience. Significant differences between the two groups were also observed in marital status, number of children, educational background, professional title, employment type, administrative position, and research capability level (P < 0.05, Table 5). These statistically significant factors were subsequently included in a multivariate logistic regression analysis. The results indicated that nursing work experience (OR = 1.832, 95% CI = 1.226–2.737), educational background (OR = 2.946, 95% CI = 1.014–8.55), professional title (OR = 1.775, 95% CI = 1.291–2.441), and research capability level (OR = 1.010, 95% CI = 1.002–1.018) were significant predictors of research output among RNs (P < 0.05, Table 6).

Discussion

Nursing research plays an important and vital role in improving the quality of care19. RNs, as frontline clinicians, often identify numerous specific clinical issues. Their engagement in scientific research helps translate practical experience directly into clinical settings, thereby optimizing care processes and improving patient outcomes20. This study found that 39.3% of nurses had research output in the past five years, a proportion comparable to the 36% reported in Ghana21. Moreover, a study of 2052 clinical nurse specialists in the United States reported a research participation rate of 42%22. Both studies were published nearly a decade ago, suggesting that a gap in research engagement between Chinese nurses and their counterparts in developed countries may persist.

Although only 15.8% of RNs in this study were currently conducting or preparing to conduct research, as many as 62.1% expressed a willingness or strong willingness to engage in research, revealing a “high willingness-low participation” paradox. According to the AMO framework, individual performance is shaped by the interplay of ability, motivation, and opportunity23. Overall, the primary drivers of RN’s research participation were promotion, job requirements, and self-improvement. This suggests that RNs possessed strong internal and external motivation, which may account for the high willingness observed. However, this motivation did not effectively translate into actual research engagement. The reason lies in the fact that motivation requires support from ability and opportunity to take effect. Specifically, the research capability scores of RNs were at a moderate level (bordering on low), consistent with findings by Zhu et al.24 (score: 45 [24, 63] vs. 54.08 ± 24.55; total score range: 0–120). Among the subdimensions, research design, research implementation, and data analysis were identified as core areas of weakness. In addition, lack of adequate time and access to information resources further hindered research participation. These deficiencies in ability and opportunity may prevent motivated RNs from translating their intentions into actual research engagement and output25,26.

Further analysis identified research capability and educational background as two significant predictors of RNs’ research output. RNs with higher educational attainment and stronger research capability were more likely to achieve research outcomes. This finding is related to the nursing education model in China: at the diploma and undergraduate levels, nursing education emphasizes foundational and specialized knowledge, with only basic research training; it is not until the graduate level that the educational focus shifts to the development of research skills27. Nurses with higher educational attainment typically possess stronger research capabilities15. Thus, educational background may influence research output primarily through the ability dimension. Previous research has shown that nurses with a bachelor’s degree often lack sufficient research capability, which limits their ability to produce research output28. In this study, the majority of RNs held a bachelor’s degree. However, nearly 70% of participants reported having never received systematic research training. This finding underscores the urgent need for hospital administrators and nursing leadership to implement targeted interventions.

This study also found that RNs’ research output was closely associated with their professional titles. The survey revealed that 66.2% of nurses in the non-research output group held junior titles, while only 18.9% of those in the research output group did. This aligns with the study by Hu et al.29. One possible explanation is that RNs with lower professional titles face fewer research requirements in their work. As professional titles increase, the evaluation criteria for research output also become more demanding, which serves as both external pressure and intrinsic motivation to engage in research30. Moreover, RNs with higher titles typically assume greater managerial responsibilities and are more likely to participate in hospital-level decision-making and academic activities. These roles provide them with more favorable research conditions, such as dedicated research time, funding support, and collaborative networks31. These advantages facilitate a virtuous cycle of “increased research output—professional title advancement—increased research output.” Therefore, disparities in research output across title levels may stem not only from differences in individual initiative but also from variations in the opportunity dimension. Additionally, this study showed that RNs with longer work experience are more likely to produce research output. From the perspective of the AMO framework, work experience may influence research output through both the ability and opportunity dimensions. Clinically experienced nurses accumulate deeper specialized knowledge and more mature skills in problem identification and resolution32. As their careers advance, they also gain greater access to opportunities for capability enhancement and professional visibility—such as continuing education and participation in research projects—thereby facilitating research output.

To effectively enhance RNs’ research output, synergistic efforts across the ability, motivation, and opportunity dimensions are required. First, establishing a systematic and tiered training framework is crucial for strengthening research capability. Such a framework should be stratified according to nurses’ professional titles and existing skill levels to address their diverse needs33. For nurses with junior titles, foundational training should focus on cultivating research awareness, basic writing skills, and familiarity with research resources. For nurses with intermediate titles, training should emphasize practical competencies such as research design, research implementation, and data analysis. For nurses with senior titles, advanced training in research leadership and interdisciplinary collaboration should be provided, along with opportunities to mentor junior colleagues and lead research teams34. Project-based learning can be employed to engage nurses in addressing real clinical problems through research projects, thereby enhancing their practical skills and teamwork capabilities35. Mentorship and small research group models offer personalized or small-group guidance36. Furthermore, integrating online courses and blended learning formats can overcome temporal and spatial constraints, accommodating nurses’ needs for flexible learning37.

In terms of the motivation and opportunity dimensions, hospital administrators should also provide systematic support, with particular attention to nurses with lower professional titles and less work experience38. Although promotion mechanisms may enhance research motivation to some extent, nurses with lower titles often lack clear incentives due to fewer research requirements, which limits their research engagement. However, this group has not yet developed rigid clinical thinking patterns and possesses strong learning ability and innovation potential. Therefore, differentiated incentive strategies should be adopted to translate their potential strengths into actual motivation39. Specifically, incentive mechanisms tied to research participation (such as performance evaluations, excellence awards, and bonus points for title promotion) can be established. Additionally, structured training, accessible online learning resources, and academic seminars can be offered to reinforce awareness of research value and stimulate intrinsic motivation40,41. Regarding the opportunity dimension, time constraints represent a significant barrier. Hospitals should provide institutional support at the organizational level, such as dedicated research time, flexible scheduling, or research leave, to ensure basic time conditions. Furthermore, nurses with strong potential and enthusiasm may be encouraged to pursue graduate education while maintaining their clinical roles, thereby fostering both individual professional development and the advancement of organizational research capacity42,43.

This study has several limitations. First, due to resource constraints, a convenience sampling method was used to recruit RNs from eight hospitals in Zhejiang Province. The absence of randomization may have introduced uncontrolled confounding variables, potentially biasing the relationships between influencing factors and research output. Second, compared with inland regions in western China, Zhejiang Province has significant economic and educational advantages, providing nurses with greater opportunities for research learning and practice. All participating hospitals were urban tertiary hospitals, with no inclusion of township or lower-level facilities. Consequently, the findings primarily reflect the research status of RNs in eastern China and may not be generalizable to less developed regions or primary care settings. Finally, the definition of research output adopted in this study was relatively narrow, focusing solely on published papers and granted patents, which may have led to an underestimation of nurses’ actual research output. In addition, no distinction was made between publications in common journals and core journals, potentially resulting in the homogenization of research output of varying quality. Future studies should expand the sample to include nurses from different regions and hospital levels, and consider subgroup analyses to improve external validity. A more comprehensive evaluation system for research output should be developed, incorporating diverse forms of research achievements and differentiating their quality to more accurately reflect nurses’ research capabilities. Furthermore, drawing on the AMO model, advanced analytical methods such as structural equation modeling could be employed to explore the mechanisms through which ability, motivation, and opportunity influence research output. Finally, intervention studies are recommended to evaluate the effectiveness of targeted strategies in enhancing nurses’ research capability and output.

Conclusion

In summary, RNs in Zhejiang Province demonstrated a strong willingness to participate in nursing research, but their research capability and output remains suboptimal. Nursing work experience, educational background, professional title, and research capability were identified four important factors influencing research output of RNs. By strategically addressing these factors, healthcare institutions can effectively enhance nurses’ research capability and output, thereby advancing the nursing profession.
