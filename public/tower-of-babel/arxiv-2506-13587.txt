Non-exchangeable mean-field theory for adaptive weights: propagation of chaos and graphon sampling lemma Thanks: This project has received funding from the European Union’s Horizon 2020 research and innovation programme under the Marie Skłodowska-Curie grant agreement No. 101034255.
Datong Zhou

Abstract
. We develop a mean-field theory for large, non-exchangeable particle (agent) systems where the states and interaction weights co-evolve in a coupled system of SDEs. A first main result is a generalization of the propagation of chaos. The weight adaptation in the SDEs makes the classical approach of using a static probability space as the continuum limit for the agent labels inadequate. We address this by introducing a label space endowed with a filtration that captures the stochasticity. While this yields a well-posed McKean-Vlasov SDE, its limit is not easily described by a Vlasov-type PDE. This difficulty, in turn, motivates the introduction of a unified metric that naturally combines the Wasserstein distance for states and the cut norm for weights. In this metric space, we establish the convergence of finite systems to their continuum limit. This result, analogous to the classical convergence of empirical measures, is a subtle consequence of a deep result from dense graph theory, namely the Sampling Lemma.



1.
Overview

1.1.
A class of non-exchangeable multi-agent systems

The aim of the article is to establish a natural formalism of limits for the so-called non-exchangeable multi-agent systems where the interaction between agents is parameterized by an additional weighted
graph
connecting all agents.

We consider systems with the following specifications: Let
\bm{X}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\mathbb{T}^{d}
denote the state of the
i
-th agent, and
\bm{w}_{i,j}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
denote the time-varying interaction weights between the
i
-th and the
j
-th agent.
The individual agent dynamics combine a drift term
\mu:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with addtive noise term
\nu\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{i}(t)
, where
\{\bm{B}_{i}(t)\}
are i.i.d. Brownian motions. Agent interactions are given through an interaction kernel
\sigma:\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and weight adaptation functions
\alpha,\beta:\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{R}
.

This leads to the following general system of SDEs:

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bm{X}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mu(\bm{X}_{i})\mathrm{d}t+\nu\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{i}+\frac{1}{N}\!\sum_{j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}\bm{w}_{i,j}\sigma(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{X}_{j})\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\forall i\in[N],

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bm{w}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\alpha(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{X}_{j})\bm{w}_{i,j}\mathrm{d}t+\beta(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{X}_{j})\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\forall i,j\in[N],\ i\neq j.

Here
[N]=\{1,\dots,N\}
, and this notation is used consistently throughout the article.

A mean-field scaling factor
\frac{1}{N}
is incorporated in the interaction summation in (
1.1
).
One of our key regularity assumptions requires the initial weight configuration to satisfy

\displaystyle\max_{i,j\in[N]}|\bm{w}_{i,j}(0)|\sim O(1),\quad\text{as }N\to\infty,

which is preserved for all
t>0
if one further assumes that
\alpha,\beta
are
L^{\infty}
bounded.
This scaling regime preserves two fundamental characteristics in mean-field analysis: the total interaction exerted on or by any individual agent remains
O(1)
as
N\to\infty
; no single agent dominates the dynamics as pairwise interactions contribute only
O(\frac{1}{N})
.

1.2.
A brief review of classical mean-field theory

The classical study of mean-field limits typically concerns systems of identical agents where interaction weights remain constant (up to the mean-field scaling factor
\frac{1}{N}
). This line of research dates back to the seminal works of
[
13
,
19
,
37
,
38
,
41
]
, where sufficient regularity conditions (e.g., bounded Lipschitz coefficients) ensured rigorous derivation of the mean-field limit. Contemporary research has made significant progress in more singular interaction dynamics, see for example the reviews
[
14
,
15
,
26
,
31
]
.

In the context of exchangeable systems of the form (
1.1a
) with fixed interaction weights
\bm{w}_{i,j}(t)\equiv 1
and bounded Lipschitz coefficients, we briefly outline a few classical conclusions of mean-field theory:

•

Propagation of chaos
: The
N
-particle system becomes asymptotically decoupled as
N\to\infty
, such that under i.i.d. initial conditions, individual agent trajectories converge to i.i.d. copies of the McKean-Vlasov SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\overline{\bm{X}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mu(\overline{\bm{X}}_{i})\mathrm{d}t+\nu\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{i}+\int_{\mathbb{D}}\sigma(\overline{\bm{X}}_{i},y)\overline{f}(t,\mathrm{d}y)\ \mathrm{d}t

characterized by a common law
\overline{f}(t,\cdot)=\mathrm{Law}(\overline{\bm{X}}_{i}(t))
(in Kac’s sense
[
41
]
).
More precisely, for any
t\geq 0
,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[N]}\mathbb{E}|\bm{X}_{i}(t)-\overline{\bm{X}}_{i}(t)|\leq C(t)N^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

•

Vlasov equation
:
The limiting law
\overline{f}(t,\cdot)
in the McKean-Vlasov SDE
satisfies the following Vlasov PDE:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f(t,x)+\nabla\cdot\bigg[\bigg(\mu(x)+\int_{\mathbb{D}}\sigma(x,y)f(t,\mathrm{d}y)\bigg)f(t,x)\bigg]=\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}\Delta f(t,x).

One can directly characterize the convergence of the original system of SDEs (i.e. (
1.1a
) with
\bm{w}_{i,j}(t)\equiv 1
) to the Vlasov PDE by introducing the empirical measure
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\bm{X}_{i}(t)}
. As an example, under mild moment assumptions, the following convergence holds:

(1.4)

\mathbb{E}\bigg[W_{1}\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\bm{X}_{i}(t)}\ ,\ \overline{f}(t,\cdot)\bigg)\bigg]\leq C(t)N^{-\theta},

where
W_{1}
is the Wasserstein-
1
distance.
The exponent
\theta
depends on the spatial dimension
d
and generally satisfies
\theta<\frac{1}{2}
(see, e.g.,
[
18
]
). This phenomenon arises from the use of Wasserstein distance: The distributional convergence with respect to the sample size
N
exhibits slower a priori rates in higher dimensions, as discussed in
[
7
,
20
]
.
For specific dual test functions
\varphi\in\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(\mathbb{D})
, the classical
N^{-\frac{1}{2}}
convergence rate emerges. For example,

(1.5)

\mathbb{E}\left[\ \left|\int_{\mathbb{D}}\varphi(x)\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\bm{X}_{i}(t)}(\mathrm{d}x)-\overline{f}(t,\mathrm{d}x)\right)\right|\ \right]\leq C(t)N^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

One can also establish the stability of empirical measures without assuming initial i.i.d. sampling from a prescribed distribution. For any solution
f
to the Vlasov equation and any particle system
(\bm{X}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N]}
with deterministic initial data
(X_{i}(0))_{i\in[N]}
, the stability estimate

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[W_{1}\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\bm{X}_{i}(t)},f(t,\cdot)\bigg)\bigg]\leq C(t)W_{1}\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{i}(0)},f(0,\cdot)\bigg)+C(t)N^{-\theta}

can be established through a
W_{1}
-stability estimate of the Vlasov PDE combined with an asymptotic independence analysis.
(In the purely deterministic case
\nu=0
where the system reduces to ODEs, the empirical measure’s time evolution exactly satisfies the Vlasov PDE as a distributional solution and the
N^{-\theta}
error term vanishes. See classical results in
[
19
,
37
,
38
]
.)

1.3.
A first intuition: Propagation of independence

In recent years, there has been a growing interest in non-exchangeable systems, where swapping the states of agents
\bm{X}_{i}
and
\bm{X}_{j}
can alter the overall dynamics unless all associated weights are correspondingly adjusted. This difference necessitates a re-examination of classical concepts in mean-field theory.

An intuition that remains valid is that, when the contribution of any single agent is still small (say,
O(1/N)
), the finite-
N
system could exhibit asymptotic independence.
However, a classical propagation of chaos result, where agents
(\bm{X}_{i})_{i\in[N]}
become asymptotically i.i.d., is not expected.

For non-exchangeable systems with non-adaptive weights (e.g., considering (
1.1a
) alone), such independence has been validated to varying degrees in recent works such as
[
4
,
6
,
29
,
35
]
. In our context of fully adaptive weights, this intuition must be formulated via a more complex McKean-Vlasov SDE corresponding to the full system (
1.1
). The auxiliary processes
((\overline{\bm{X}}_{i})_{i\in[N]},(\overline{\bm{w}}_{i,j})_{i,j\in[N]})
computed from this McKean-Vlasov system propagate a form of structured independence, such that, any subcollection of these
N+N^{2}
processes is mutually independent, provided their underlying agent indices are disjoint. For example, the processes
\overline{\bm{X}}_{1},\overline{\bm{X}}_{2},\overline{\bm{w}}_{3,4},\overline{\bm{w}}_{5,6}
are independent, whereas
\overline{\bm{X}}_{1}
and
\overline{\bm{w}}_{1,2}
are necessarily correlated because, in the equation for the weights (
1.1b
), the influence of
\overline{\bm{X}}_{1}
on the dynamics of
\overline{\bm{w}}_{1,2}
is not diluted by a factor of
1/N
.

If both the original SDE system (
1.1
) and this McKean-Vlasov system start from the same initial data, then under suitable assumptions, one can establish the following estimate, which is the direct analogue of the classical propagation of chaos result (
1.2
):

(1.7)

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[N]}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[|\bm{X}_{i}(t)-\overline{\bm{X}}_{i}(t)|\Big]\leq C(t)N^{-\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\sup_{i,j\in[N],i\neq j}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[|\bm{w}_{i,j}(t)-\overline{\bm{w}}_{i,j}(t)|\Big]\leq C(t)N^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

In the following two subsections, we rigorously formulate this structured independence and illustrate the corresponding McKean-Vlasov SDE system.

1.4.
State-weight triplets and independent mixture sampling

We first present the idea of how to construct the structured independence described above. The probability spaces introduced here are technical devices. There could be more than one space in a statement and none of them is directly related to the stochasticity of the SDE system (
1.1
). To emphasize this distinction, we do not use bold font for the functions defined on them.

Definition 1.1

(Extended state-weight triplet)
.

We say that the triplet
(X,w,(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\pi))
constitutes an extended state-weight triplet where:

•

the standard probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\pi)
is the extended label space,

•

the measurable function
X:\Omega\to\mathbb{D}
is the state configuration map,

•

the measurable function
w:\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is the interaction weight function.

To simplify notation, when the underlying probability space is understood from the context, we will often denote the state-weight triplet by
(X,w,\Omega)
or simply
(X,w)
.
We also adopt the notation
X_{\xi}=X(\xi)
and
w_{\xi,\zeta}=w(\xi,\zeta)
for
\xi,\zeta\in\Omega
.

For any array of labels
[\xi]=(\xi_{i})_{i\in[N]}\in\Omega^{N}
, we define the
N
-sampling of the triplet
(X,w,(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\pi))
by
[\xi]
as the pair of maps:

(1.8)

\displaystyle X_{[\xi]}:\Omega^{N}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{D}^{N},

\displaystyle\qquad w_{[\xi]}:\Omega^{N}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

\displaystyle[\xi]

\displaystyle\mapsto(X_{\xi_{i}})_{i\in[N]},

\displaystyle\qquad[\xi]

\displaystyle\mapsto(w_{\xi_{i},\xi_{j}})_{i,j\in[N]}.

If the array
[\xi]=(\xi_{i})_{i\in[N]}
consists of independent random variables, it is clear that the resulting
N+N^{2}
random variables comprising
X_{[\xi]}
and
w_{[\xi]}
exhibit the aforementioned structured independence on subcollections with non-overlapping indices. This argument extends straightforwardly to the case where
X
and
w
are time-dependent processes.

We formalize this observation with the following definition.

Definition 1.2

(Independent mixture space)
.

Given a standard probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\pi)
and
N
probability measures
(\pi_{i})_{i\in[N]}
that are absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi
and satisfy the averaging condition
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}=\pi
, we call the product space

(1.9)

\displaystyle\textstyle\big(\Omega^{N},\mathcal{A}^{\otimes N},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}\big)

an independent mixture space induced by the
(\pi_{i})_{i\in[N]}
. Here,
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}
denotes the product measure and
\mathcal{A}^{\otimes N}
denotes the completed product
\sigma
-algebra.

Given a state-weight triplet
(X,w,(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\pi))
, we say that
((\bm{X}_{i})_{i\in[N]},(\bm{w}_{i,j})_{i,j\in[N]})
is an independent mixture sampling of this triplet if they are random variables on an independent mixture space
(\Omega^{N},\mathcal{A}^{\otimes N},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i})
, such that for any realization of the randomness
[\xi]\in\Omega^{N}
, they satisfy

\displaystyle(\bm{X}_{i})_{i\in[N]}=X_{[\xi]}=(X_{\xi_{i}})_{i\in[N]},\quad(\bm{w}_{i,j})_{i,j\in[N]}=w_{[\xi]}=(w_{\xi_{i},\xi_{j}})_{i,j\in[N]}.

Consider a simple example where
\Omega=[0,1]
with the Lebesgue measure, along with a state map
X:[0,1]\to\mathbb{D}
and a weight kernel
w:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
. The following two distinct procedures both define an independent mixture space and the corresponding sampling
((\bm{X}_{i})_{i\in[N]},(\bm{w}_{i,j})_{i,j\in[N]})
.

(i)

i.i.d. Sampling
: Take
(\xi_{i})_{i\in[N]}
as i.i.d. random variables drawn uniformly from
[0,1]
; i.e., each
\pi_{i}
is the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
.

(ii)

Stratified Sampling
: For each
i\in[N]
, sample
\xi_{i}
independently and uniformly from the sub-interval
\left[\frac{i-1}{N},\frac{i}{N}\right)
; i.e.,
\pi_{i}
is the normalized Lebesgue measure on this sub-interval, with density
N\mathbbm{1}_{\left[\frac{i-1}{N},\frac{i}{N}\right)}
.

In both cases, the averaging condition
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}=\pi
is satisfied.

Of course, we cannot expect the solution of the SDE system (
1.1
) to remain a strict independent mixture sampling for all time. Nevertheless, we can perform a more refined analysis of its independence structure.

Consider initial data follows an independent mixture sampling, meaning there exists a state-weight triplet
(X_{(0)},w_{(0)},(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\pi))
, an independent mixture space
(\Omega^{N},\mathcal{A}^{\otimes N},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i})
and for any realization of the randomness
[\xi]\in\Omega^{N}
,

(1.10)

\displaystyle(\bm{X}_{i}(0))_{i\in[N]}

\displaystyle=(X_{(0)})_{[\xi]},

\displaystyle(\bm{w}_{i,j}(0))_{i,j\in[N]}

\displaystyle=(w_{(0)})_{[\xi]}.

The diagonal weight terms
\bm{w}_{i,i}(0)
, though formally defined here, are absent from the SDEs (
1.1
); we retain them for convenience as their effect is of a negligible order
O(N^{-1})
.

Recall that in (
1.1
),
(\bm{B}_{i})_{i\in[N]}
are
N
i.i.d. Brownian motions, which are also independent of the initial data. To formalize this, we define a canonical filtered probability space for a single Brownian motion, denoted
(\widehat{\Omega},\widehat{\mathcal{F}},\widehat{\pi})
, where
\widehat{\mathcal{F}}=(\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
is its natural filtration. Consequently, the collection of all
N
Brownian motions
(\bm{B}_{i})_{i\in[N]}
is defined on the product space
(\widehat{\Omega}^{N},\widehat{\mathcal{F}}^{\otimes N},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\widehat{\pi})
, and
the solution
((\bm{X}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N]},(\bm{w}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N]})
to the SDE system (
1.1
) can be defined as a random process on the product probability space:

(1.11)

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\big(\Omega^{N},\mathcal{A}^{\otimes N},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}\big)\times\big(\widehat{\Omega}^{N},\widehat{\mathcal{F}}^{\otimes N},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\widehat{\pi}\big)

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle=\big((\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})^{N},(\mathcal{A}\otimes\widehat{\mathcal{F}})^{\otimes N},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}(\pi_{i}\otimes\widehat{\pi})\big).

A key observation is that if we consider the probability space

(1.12)

\displaystyle\big((\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}),(\mathcal{A}\otimes\widehat{\mathcal{F}}),(\pi\otimes\widehat{\pi})\big)

together with the family of measures
(\pi_{i}\otimes\widehat{\pi})_{i\in[N]}
, then the probability space in (
1.11
) is exactly the associated independent mixture space.

This outlines our main strategy: First, we define a McKean-Vlasov SDE whose solution is an evolving state-weight triplet,
(\overline{X}(t),\overline{w}(t))
, on the product space (
1.12
). Then, an auxiliary process,
((\overline{\bm{X}}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N]},(\overline{\bm{w}}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N]})
, defined on the same probability space (
1.11
) as the original system, is then constructed via an independent mixture sampling. We then show that such auxiliary processes approximates the true SDE dynamics at the classical
O(N^{-1/2})
rate, as established in (
1.7
).

1.5.
Two main results

For an initial state-weight triplet
(X_{(0)},w_{(0)},(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\pi))
and one Brownian motion
B(t)
defined on
(\widehat{\Omega},\widehat{\mathcal{F}},\widehat{\pi})
, we consider the following McKean-Vlasov type SDE system defined on the product probability space specified as in (
1.12
):
For a.e.
(\xi,\widehat{\xi})\in\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}
,

(1.13a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\overline{X}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}}

\displaystyle=\mu(\overline{X}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}})\mathrm{d}t+\nu\mathrm{d}B_{\widehat{\xi}}

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}\sim\pi\otimes\widehat{\pi}}\big[\overline{w}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi},\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}\sigma(\overline{X}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}},\overline{X}_{\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}})\big]\mathrm{d}t,

and for a.e.
(\xi,\widehat{\xi}),(\zeta,\widehat{\zeta})\in\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}
,

(1.13b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\overline{w}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi},\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}

\displaystyle=\alpha(\overline{X}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}},\overline{X}_{\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}})\overline{w}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi},\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}\mathrm{d}t+\beta(\overline{X}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}},\overline{X}_{\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}})\mathrm{d}t.

We refer to this as a McKean-Vlasov type SDE as the binary interactions are given by an expectation over all realizations of randomness.
Driving the McKean-Vlasov system by one common Brownian motion
B(t)
is a standard setup in certain mean-field contexts, which naturally causes correlation but does not affect the evolution of the law.

Since
\overline{X}
and
\overline{w}
are defined on different probability spaces, in (
1.13
) we have to introduce an unconventional formulation where the different sources of randomness are written out explicitly. Nevertheless, the well-posedness can be established via a standard Picard iteration argument for stochastic integrals, as detailed in Proposition
1.5
and its proof.

Throughout this article, we impose the following assumptions.

Assumption 1.3

(Regularities)
.

We assume that the coefficients in (
1.1
) satisfy:

•

The coefficients
\mu,\sigma,\alpha,\beta
are bounded and Lipschitz continuous with respect to their relevant arguments.

•

The coefficient
\alpha
exhibits a separable structure, i.e.,
\alpha(x,y)=\alpha_{1}(x)\alpha_{2}(y)
, where both
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are bounded functions.

•

\nu
is a non-negative constant (
\nu\geq 0
).

In addition to these regularity conditions, we require certain moment bounds on the initial data.

Assumption 1.4

(Initial moment bound)
.

We assume that the initial data of the system satisfy the following conditions:

•

For a deterministic
N
-agent system,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}|X_{i}(0)|^{2}<\infty,\quad\text{and}\quad\max_{i,j\in[N]}|w_{i,j}(0)|<\infty.

•

For a general extended state-weight triplet
(X,w,\Omega)

\displaystyle\|X\|_{L^{2}}=\int_{\Omega}|X(\xi)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\pi(\xi)<\infty,\quad\text{and}\quad\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}=\esssup_{(\xi,\zeta)\in\Omega^{2}}|w(\xi,\zeta)|<\infty.

•

When there are multiple systems
(X^{(k)},w^{(k)},\Omega^{(k)})
, the preceding conditions are assumed to hold uniformly in
k
.

We then summarize the well-posedness of (
1.13
) as the following Proposition
1.5
and provide its proof in Section
3.1
.

Proposition 1.5

(Well-posedness of McKean-Vlasov SDEs)
.

Consider the SDE system (
1.1
) whose coefficients satisfying Assumption
1.3
.
Assume that the state-weight triplet
(X_{(0)},w_{(0)},(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\pi))
satisfies Assumption
1.4
.
Let
(B(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
be a Brownian motion defined on a filtered probability space
(\widehat{\Omega},\widehat{\mathcal{F}},\widehat{\pi})
.
Then there exists a unique (in the almost sure sense) pair of stochastic processes:

\displaystyle(\overline{X}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}}(t))_{(\xi,\widehat{\xi})\in\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega},t\in[0,T]}\quad\text{and}\quad(\overline{w}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi},\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(t))_{(\xi,\widehat{\xi}),(\zeta,\widehat{\zeta})\in\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega},t\in[0,T]}

solving (
1.13
) on
[0,T]
with the following properties:

•

Adaptedness:
\overline{X}(t)
is
(\mathcal{A}\otimes\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{t})
measureable and
\overline{w}(t)
is
(\mathcal{A}\otimes\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{t})^{\otimes 2}
measurable.

•

Moment Control: For some constant
C(t)>0
depending on time and on the bounds in Assumptions
1.3
and
1.4
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}}|\overline{X}(t)|^{2}(\mathrm{d}\pi\otimes\mathrm{d}\widehat{\pi})<C(t)\quad\text{and}\quad\esssup_{(\xi,\widehat{\xi}),(\zeta,\widehat{\zeta})\in\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}}|\overline{w}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi},\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(t)|<C(t).

•

Path Regularity: The solutions have continuous sample paths almost surely.

We are now ready to present our first main result in its rigorous form in Theorem
1.6
below. The proof is provided in Section
3.2
.

Theorem 1.6

(Propagation of independence)
.

Consider the SDE system (
1.1
) whose coefficients satisfying Assumption
1.3
.
Assume that the state-weight triplet
(X_{(0)},w_{(0)},(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\pi))
satisfies Assumption
1.4
.
Let
(B(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
be a Brownian motion defined on a filtered probability space
(\widehat{\Omega},\widehat{\mathcal{F}},\widehat{\pi})
.
Denote the solutions of McKean-Vlasov SDEs (
1.13
) in the sense of Proposition
1.5
by
(\overline{X},\overline{w},((\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}),(\mathcal{A}\otimes\widehat{\mathcal{F}}),(\pi\otimes\widehat{\pi})))

For any
N\geq 1
and any independent mixture space

\displaystyle\textstyle\big((\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})^{N},(\mathcal{A}\otimes\widehat{\mathcal{F}})^{\otimes N},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}(\pi_{i}\otimes\widehat{\pi})\big),

there exists a unique solution
((\bm{X}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N]},(\bm{w}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N]})
(in the almost sure sense) to the system of SDEs (
1.1
), such that for any realization of randomness
((\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}))_{i\in[N]}\in(\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})^{N}
, the initial data satisfy

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{i}(0)

\displaystyle=(X_{(0)})_{\xi_{i}},

\displaystyle\forall i\in[N]

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{i,j}(0)

\displaystyle=(w_{(0)})_{\xi_{i},\xi_{j}}

\displaystyle\forall i,j\in[N],\ i\neq j,

and the Brownian motions satisfy

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=B_{\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(t),

\displaystyle\forall i\in[N].

On the same independent mixture space, define
((\overline{\bm{X}}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N]},(\overline{\bm{w}}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N]})
as the independent mixture sampling from
(\overline{X},\overline{w})
, i.e. for any realization of randomness
((\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}))_{i\in[N]}\in(\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})^{N}
,

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{X}}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=(\overline{X}(t))_{\xi_{i}},

\displaystyle\forall i\in[N]

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{w}}_{i,j}(t)

\displaystyle=(\overline{w}(t))_{\xi_{i},\xi_{j}},

\displaystyle\forall i,j\in[N],\ i\neq j.

Then, for some constant
C(t)>0
depending on time and on the bounds in Assumptions
1.3
and
1.4
, the estimate (
1.7
) holds and is restated here:

(1.14)

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[N]}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[|\bm{X}_{i}(t)-\overline{\bm{X}}_{i}(t)|\Big]\leq C(t)N^{-\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\sup_{i,j\in[N],i\neq j}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[|\bm{w}_{i,j}(t)-\overline{\bm{w}}_{i,j}(t)|\Big]\leq C(t)N^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

It is important to clarify that our analysis does not involve an uncountable family of i.i.d. Brownian motions whose measurability issues are known. There is only one
B(t)
driving the McKean-Vlasov system (
1.13
), and a crucial step in our framework is the use of independent mixture space to define the random processes of a finite
N
-agent system.
This ensures that we are always dealing with a well-defined system driven by a finite number of independent Brownian motions,
(\bm{B}_{i})_{i\in[N]}
.

For our non-exchangeable system of SDEs (
1.1
), establishing this propagation of independence is merely the first step towards characterizing the mean-field limit. In the classical setting, propagation of chaos implies that the laws of all agents are identical. Consequently, a single density function evolving according to a Vlasov PDE (such as (
1.3
)) is sufficient to describe the system’s limit. Here, however, the laws of the agents (and the weights) are all distinct, so the path forward is not as straightforward.

For certain special cases, such as systems with non-adaptive weights, efforts have been made to reproduce this familiar Vlasov-type PDE interpretation (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
6
,
16
,
33
]
) by defining an extended density on a product space that encodes the pre-determined weight structure; we elaborate on this in Section
2.1
. However, this is not available in our fully adaptive setting.

These challenges compel a departure from previous approaches that determine the limits of weights and state densities sequentially, motivating a framework that characterizes their joint convergence. To this end, we introduce a distance
\delta_{W_{1},\square}
on the space of state-weight triplets. (We note that this is technically a pseudo-metric, which does not distinguish certain dynamically equivalent limits).

A key insight is that when
[N]
is equipped with the uniform measure, the finite system
((\bm{X}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N]},(\bm{w}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N]},[N])
can be viewed as a (random) state-weight triplet. This perspective allows us to define the mean-field limit directly as the limit of these empirical state-weight triplets with respect to the
\delta_{W_{1},\square}
metric.

Without delving into the precise definition of
\delta_{W_{1},\square}
at this moment, we present our second main result. Its proof is provided in Section
3.3
.

Theorem 1.7

(Stability from empirical data)
.

Consider the SDE system (
1.1
) whose coefficients satisfying Assumption
1.3
. Assume deterministic initial conditions
(X_{i}(0))_{i\in[N]}
,
(w_{i,j}(0))_{i,j\in[N]}
satisfying Assumption

.
Denote
((\bm{X}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N]}
,
(\bm{w}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N]})
the unique solution (in the almost sure sense) to (
1.1
) .

Assume that the state-weight triplet
(X_{(0)},w_{(0)},(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\pi))
also satisfies Assumption
1.4
.
Let
(B(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
be a Brownian motion defined on a filtered probability space
(\widehat{\Omega},\widehat{\mathcal{F}},\widehat{\pi})
.
Denote the solutions of McKean-Vlasov SDEs (

) in the sense of Proposition

by
(\overline{X},\overline{w},((\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}),(\mathcal{A}\otimes\widehat{\mathcal{F}}),(\pi\otimes\widehat{\pi})))
.

Then, there exists a constant
C(t)>0
, depending on time and on the bounds in Assumptions
1.3
and

(1.15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\bigg(\Big((\bm{X}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N]},(\bm{w}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N]},[N]\Big),\Big(\overline{X}(t),\overline{w}(t),(\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})\Big)\bigg)\bigg]

The
\delta_{W_{1},\square}

provides a non-exchangeable counterpoint to the classical Wasserstein stability of empirical measures (

).
Let us remark that this result is not restricted to initial data arising from an independent mixture sampling. In fact, one can directly compare any two solutions,
((\bm{X}^{(k)}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N_{k}]},(\bm{w}^{(k)}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N_{k}]})
for
k=1,2
, of the SDE system (

) without any explicit reference to the McKean-Vlasov SDEs (

).

Another key property is that the
\delta_{W_{1},\square}
metric is independent of agent labeling, allowing convergence without pointwise convergence. Furthermore, under the moment bounds in Assumption
1.4
, the space of state-weight triplets is compact. This guarantees the existence of limit points from any sequence of initial data, bypassing the need for “well-prepared” configurations (additional regularity assumptions) of weights in some literature. For further discussion, see Section
2.1
.

We conclude this overview in the final subsection, which formally defines the metric
\delta_{W_{1},\square}
and presents the remaining key ingredients that bridge our propagation of independence result (Theorem
1.6
) to the stability of empirical state-weight triplets (Theorem
1.7
).

1.6.
Relabeling and unlabeled distance

When two extended state-weight triplets
(X^{(k)},w^{(k)},\Omega)
,
k=1,2
happen to share the same extended label space
\Omega
, their comparison are conceptually straightforward. One can adopt the hybrid norm

\displaystyle\|X^{(1)}-X^{(2)}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\|w^{(1)}-w^{(2)}\|_{\square}

where
\|\cdot\|_{\square}
denotes the cut norm extensively employed in graph limiting theory (see the textbook
[
36
]
), i.e.

\displaystyle\|w^{(1)}-w^{(2)}\|_{\square}\vcentcolon=\sup_{S,T\subset\Omega}\left|\int_{S\times T}(w^{(1)}-w^{(2)})\ \mathrm{d}\pi\otimes\mathrm{d}\pi\right|.

However, the natural need to compare extended state-weight triplets in a manner that is independent of their specific underlying probability spaces
\Omega
necessitates a “relabeling mechanism”. This leads us to revisit the following foundational concept in measure theory:

Definition 1.8

(Measure-preserving map)
.

For probability spaces
(\Omega^{(k)},\mathcal{A}^{(k)},\pi^{(k)})

(k=1,2)
, a map
\phi:\Omega^{(1)}\to\Omega^{(2)}
is measure-preserving if:

•

\phi^{-1}(A)\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
(measurability),

•

\pi_{1}(\phi^{-1}(A))=\pi_{2}(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{A}_{2}
(measure invariance).

This leads us to the following new definitions for comparison across different label spaces:

Definition 1.9

(Relabeling and unlabeled distance)
.

For any extended state-weight triplet
(X,w,\Omega)
and measure-preserving map
\varphi:(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\pi^{\prime})\to(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\pi)
, we define the relabeled system:

\displaystyle X_{\varphi}:\Omega^{\prime}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{D},

\displaystyle w_{\varphi}:\Omega^{\prime}\times\Omega^{\prime}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle\mapsto X_{\varphi(\xi)},

\displaystyle(\xi,\zeta)

\displaystyle\mapsto w_{\varphi(\xi),\varphi(\zeta)}.

The triplet
(X_{\varphi},w_{\varphi},\Omega^{\prime})
is called a relabeling of
(X,w,\Omega)
.

The “unlabeled
W_{1}
-cut distance” between two triplets
(X^{(k)},w^{(k)},\Omega^{(k)})

(k=1,2)
is then defined by optimizing over all possible relabeling:

\displaystyle\delta_{W_{1},\square}\left((X^{(1)},w^{(1)},\Omega^{(1)}),(X^{(2)},w^{(2)},\Omega^{(2)})\right)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\inf_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}\left(\|X^{(1)}_{\varphi_{1}}-X^{(2)}_{\varphi_{2}}\|_{L^{1}}+\|w^{(1)}_{\varphi_{1}}-w^{(2)}_{\varphi_{2}}\|_{\square}\right),

where the infimum ranges over all measure-preserving maps
\varphi_{k}:\Omega\to\Omega^{(k)}

(k=1,2)
with common label space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\pi)
.

It is straightforward to verify that
\delta_{W_{1},\square}
constitutes a pseudo-metric. When
\delta_{W_{1},\square}\left((X^{(1)},w^{(1)}),(X^{(2)},w^{(2)})\right)=0
, we say the two state-weight triplets are weakly isomorphic.
It is straightforward to verify that any
(X,w,\Omega)
is weakly isomorphic to its relabeled version
(X_{\varphi},w_{\varphi},\Omega^{\prime})
under measure-preserving maps.

For exchangeable systems where
w^{(k)}\equiv 1

(k=1,2)
, the interaction term vanishes under relabeling (
w^{(1)}_{\varphi_{1}}-w^{(2)}_{\varphi_{2}}\equiv 0
regardless of chosen
\varphi_{i}
). It is then straightforward to verify that the above definition reduces to the Wasserstein-
1
distance of state distributions (laws):

\displaystyle\inf_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}\|X^{(1)}_{\varphi_{1}}-X^{(2)}_{\varphi_{2}}\|_{L^{1}}=W_{1}(\mathrm{Law}(X^{(1)}),\mathrm{Law}(X^{(2)})).

Conversely, omitting the
L^{1}
term recovers the graphon cut distance

\displaystyle\inf_{\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}}\|w^{(1)}_{\varphi_{1}}-w^{(2)}_{\varphi_{2}}\|_{\square}=\vcentcolon\delta_{\square}(w^{(1)},w^{(2)}).

in graph limiting theory (see
[
36
, Chapter 8, Chapter 13]
).

The following sampling lemma provides the crucial link between the continuum limit and the finite-agent system, justifying why our preceding definitions are formulated exactly as they are. It quantifies how an independent mixture sampling approximates the original state-weight triplet in the large
N
limit. Its proof is provided in Section
3.4
.

Lemma 1.10

(Sampling lemma)
.

Let
(X,w,(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\pi))
be a state-weight triplet. There exists a constant
C_{X,w}>0
, depending only on
\int_{\Omega}|X|^{2}\mathrm{d}\pi
and
\|w\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{2})}
, such that for any independent mixture space
(\Omega^{N},\mathcal{A}^{\otimes N},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i})
and its induced sampling triplet
((\bm{X}_{i})_{i\in[N]},(\bm{w}_{i,j})_{i,j\in[N]},[N])
specified as in Definition
1.2
, the following approximation holds:

(1.16)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\bigg(\Big((\bm{X}_{i})_{i\in[N]},(\bm{w}_{i,j})_{i,j\in[N]},[N]\Big),(X,w,\Omega)\bigg)\bigg]\leq\frac{C_{X,w}}{\sqrt{\log N}}.

This lemma corresponds to the Sampling Lemma in
[
12
]
and has fundamental ties to the Szemerédi Regularity Lemma
[
39
,
40
]
, as well as its weaker, more intuitive formulation
[
21
]
. Although specific assumptions on graph regularity could potentially improve this rate, the
(\log N)^{-1/2}
seems to represent the optimal a priori rate when no further structural properties of the graph are assumed.

One familiar with probability theory or graphon theory may have recognized that Lemma
1.10
could potentially be strengthened to obtain large deviation bounds (e.g., via concentration inequalities like Azuma-Hoeffding). Nevertheless, we present Lemma
1.10
in its current form as it provides the most direct path to our goal of extending classical mean-field theory results to non-exchangeable settings.

In addition, the unlabeled distance
\delta_{W_{1},\square}
, considered as a pseudo-metric on the set of state-weight triplets satisfying
\{(X,w,\Omega):\|X\|_{L^{2}}\leq C,\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\}
, induces a compact quotient space. Lemma
1.10
implies that any such
(X,w,\Omega)
can be approximated by finite-dimensional triplets at a rate of
O(\sqrt{\log N})
. Furthermore, finite-dimensional triplets (subject to appropriate moment bounds) are known to form precompact sets in this topology.

Combining Theorem
1.6
and Lemma
1.10
yields Corollary
1.11
below. This corollary provides a quantitative estimate, acting as a counterpoint to the convergence of the empirical measure for exchangeable systems as stated in (
1.4
). Its proof is provided in Subsection
3.3
.

hold.

, which depends on time

and on the bounds specified in Assumptions

and

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\bigg(\Big((\bm{X}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N]},(\bm{w}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N]},[N]\Big),\Big(\overline{X}(t),\overline{w}(t),(\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})\Big)\bigg)\bigg]

The last piece of our argument is to establish the
\delta_{W_{1},\square}
-stability of the McKean-Vlasov SDEs (

Organization of the article

Our overview of the main results ends here. The remainder of this article is structured as follows:

2.
Discussion

2.1.
Inhomogeneous weights and extended Vlasov PDE

The past decade has witnessed rapid advancements in non-exchangeable mean-field theory. The use of graph limits to derive macroscopic equations (often PDEs) for such systems arguably dates back to works like
[
16
,
33
]
, though the study of heterogeneous agent systems using other mathematical tools may have a longer history.

Using our notation, and taking the system with non-adaptive weights described by (
1.1a
) as an illustrative example, approaches such as those in
[
16
,
33
]
characterize the non-exchangeable limit via a function
f=f(t,x,\xi)
.
In this formulation, consistent with our framework,
\xi\in\Omega
serves as a continuum generalization of the discrete agent labels
i\in[N]
.
For any given time
t\geq 0
and label
\xi\in\Omega
,
f(t,\cdot,\xi)
represents a probability distribution over the state space
\mathbb{D}
(i.e.,
f(t,\cdot,\xi)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{D})
).
This function
f
is then posited to formally satisfy an extended Vlasov-type PDE of the following form:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(t,x,\xi)+\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(\left[\mu(x)+\int_{\mathbb{I}}w(\xi,\zeta)\int_{\mathbb{D}}\sigma(x,y)f(t,y,\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\zeta\right]f(t,x,\xi)\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}\Delta_{x}f(t,x,\xi)=0.

From the perspective of this article, such limit definition benefits from the non-adaptive nature of the interaction weights. This allows for a pre-defined limiting kernel
w(\xi,\zeta)
; the subsequent dynamics for the state distribution
f(t,\cdot,\xi)
(for each label
\xi
) then primarily involve advection (and possibly diffusion) in the state variable
x
, without further evolution in the label
\xi
.

Consequently, with an established graph limit
w(\xi,\zeta)
, justifying the mean-field limit involves showing the stability of
f^{(n)}
(associated with the
N_{n}
-agent system) to the limiting distribution
f
. This is typically done in an
L^{p}
averaged sense over labels
\xi
, using a metric
d_{\mathcal{P}}
(e.g., Wasserstein) on the space of probability measures
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{D})
, i.e.,

(2.2)

\displaystyle d_{F}\left(f^{(n)}(t,\cdot,\cdot),f(t,\cdot,\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\left[\int_{\Omega}\Big(d_{\mathcal{P}}\big(f^{(n)}(t,\cdot,\xi),f(t,\cdot,\xi)\big)\Big)^{p}\mathrm{d}\pi(\xi)\right]^{1/p},

where
\pi
is the measure on
\Omega
. The early works
[
16
,
33
]
extended Neunzert’s method to this
L^{1}
average over
\xi
-fibers (i.e.,
p=1
in (
2.2
)). Their analyses relied on the convergence of weights
w^{(n)}\to w
in
L^{1}
norm. To secure such convergence, these studies constructed the finite-agent system’s weights
w^{(n)}_{ij}
as an ad hoc discretizations of a pre-given limiting kernel
w(\xi,\zeta)
.
This approach was later adapted for alternative graph limit definitions. For example,
[
23
]
analyzed graphops introduced in
[
3
]
, while
[
34
]
considered a notion of digraph measures. Both works established
f^{(n)}\to f
using effectively
p=\infty
(
\esssup
over
\xi
) in (
2.2
), and also described some ad hoc discretizations to ensure stronger convergence of
w^{(n)}\to w
in their respective graphop or digraph measure spaces.

2.2.
Graphon limit for extended Vlasov PDE

It is now a common consideration to establish stability, in the sense of (
2.2
), when
w^{(n)}\to w
in the
\delta_{\square}
unlabeled cut distance. Some examples
[
4
,
6
,
30
]
cover deterministic ODEs
(\nu=0)
, systems with Brownian noise
(\nu>0)
, and types of stochasticity on interactions (such as from Poisson processes) that are not included in (
1.1a
). Another remarkable recent work
[
2
]
considers hypergraphon theory (the corresponding extension of graphon theory to hypergraphs) to characterize mean-field systems with interactions that are not necessarily binary.

Using relabeling (or permutation of indices), additional regularity can be imposed on
w\in L^{\infty}
. As a result, the unlabeled distance
\delta_{\square}
, when viewed as a pseudo-metric on the set
\{w:\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\}
, induces a compact quotient space (see
[
36
]
).
Consequently, for finite agent systems where
N\to\infty
, one can at least discuss the convergence of a subsequence of weights. This ensures a non-empty limit in general, without requiring well-prepared weights (such as those obtained by an ad hoc discretization from a given kernel).
However, to the best of our knowledge, while this compactness has been extended to
L^{p}
graphons
[
10
,
11
]
, its applicability to sparser kernels, such as graph-ops, remains an open question.

A final comment is that for stochastic systems (e.g., (
1.1a
) with
\nu>0
), the topology corresponding to (
2.2
) cannot directly characterize the convergence of empirical data of the form

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}^{(N)}(t,x,\xi)=\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathbbm{1}_{\left[\frac{i-1}{N},\frac{i}{N}\right)}(\xi)\delta_{\bm{X}_{i}(t)}(x).

(In this expression,
\xi\in\Omega=[0,1]
is used primarily to provide a concrete space
\Omega
on which any finite agents can be represented.)
Instead, (
2.2
) characterizes the convergence in law of functions defined as

f^{(N)}(t,x,\xi)=\sum_{i\in[N]}\mathbbm{1}_{\left[\frac{i-1}{N},\frac{i}{N}\right)}(\xi)\mathrm{Law}_{\bar{\bm{X}}_{i}}(x).

For any fixed
t\geq 0
and
\xi\in[0,1]
,
\bm{\rho}^{(N)}(t,\cdot,\xi)
is a Dirac delta measure at
\bm{X}_{i}(t)
if
\xi
is in the
i
-th particle’s designated interval. Yet, for stochastic systems, the limit
f(t,x,\xi)
is not expected to be a Dirac delta for fixed
t
and
\xi
. Therefore, a metric with an
\xi
-fibered structure, such as (
2.2
), generally does not permit the convergence of
\bm{\rho}^{(N)}
to
f
.
(The work
[
30
]
mentioned above is an exception, as their metric is not of form (
2.2
) and corresponds to the weak-* topology in the
\xi
-direction.)

Within our framework, one can of course establish the convergence of empirical data to the solution of the McKean-Vlasov SDE (
1.13a
), as demonstrated in Corollary
1.11
. Moreover, this same limit can be derived from
f
, the solution of the extended Vlasov PDE (
2.1
), by defining for each time
t
an auxiliary state-weight-triplet
(X_{f},w_{f},\Omega_{f}^{(t)})
.
The underlying space for this triplet is
\Omega_{f}^{(t)}\vcentcolon=\mathbb{D}\times[0,1]
, equipped with the probability measure

\pi_{f}^{(t)}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}\xi)\vcentcolon=f(t,\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}\xi).

The state mapping
X_{f}:\Omega_{f}^{(t)}\to\mathbb{D}
and the weight kernel
w_{f}:\Omega_{f}^{(t)}\times\Omega_{f}^{(t)}\to\mathbb{R}
are then defined as

X_{f}(x,\xi)\vcentcolon=x\quad\text{and}\quad w_{f}\big((x_{1},\xi_{1}),(x_{2},\xi_{2})\big)\vcentcolon=w(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}),

where
w
is the underlying interaction kernel that depends on the label components
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in[0,1]
.
(This construction is inspired by
[
42
]
).

For the purpose of relating the
N
-agent system to this limiting structure via our sampling results, we further define a sequence of measures
\{\tilde{\pi}^{(t)}_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
for an independent mixture sampling process. For each
i\in[N]
, the measure
\tilde{\pi}^{(t)}_{i}
on
\mathbb{D}\times[0,1]
is given by:

\tilde{\pi}^{(t)}_{i}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}\xi)\vcentcolon=\mathrm{Law}_{\bar{\bm{X}}_{i}(t)}(\mathrm{d}x)\otimes\left(N\mathbbm{1}_{\left[\frac{i-1}{N},\frac{i}{N}\right)}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi\right).

From Lemma
1.10
(sampling lemma) applied to this construction, we can obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\bigg(\Big((\overline{\bm{X}}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N]},(\overline{\bm{w}}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N]},[N]\Big),\Big(X_{f},w_{f},\Omega_{f}^{(t)}\Big)\bigg)\bigg]\leq\frac{C(t)}{\sqrt{\log N}}.

Then, by applying a propagation of independence argument (such as Theorem
1.6
), we get the direct convergence from empirical data to
f
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\bigg(\Big((\bm{X}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N]},(\bm{w}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N]},[N]\Big),\Big(X_{f},w_{f},\Omega_{f}^{(t)}\Big)\bigg)\bigg]\leq\frac{C(t)}{\sqrt{\log N}}.

Therefore, by incorporating our perspective of matching of both states and weights within a unified metric
\delta_{W_{1},\square}
, the conclusions of the earlier works
[
4
,
6
,
16
,
33
]
established on (
2.2
) can likely be extended, without fundamental difficulty.
This contributes to the understanding of even non-adaptive types of non-exchangeable limits.

2.3.
Adaptive weights

For systems with adaptive weights, one generally cannot expect a mean-field limit described by an extended Vlasov-type PDE like (
2.1
).
This is because agents (density) initially associated with the same label
\xi\in\Omega
can diverge over time as their interaction weights evolve, breaking the initial fiber structure.
Nevertheless, PDE-based limits reminiscent of the exchangeable case are recoverable under special structural assumptions on the weights.
A key example is when the interactions are determined by a property of the target agent alone, i.e., of the form
\bm{w}_{ij}(t)=\bm{m}_{j}(t)
.
Mean-field limits for such systems have been rigorously established in, for example,
[
1
,
5
]
.
We also refer the reader to the comprehensive review
[
2
]
, which discusses mean-field models with both general pairwise (binary) weights and these more structured unary (or node-based) weights.

Extensions of the Neunzert’s approach for systems of ODEs (e.g., when
\nu=0
in (
1.1
)) were explored in
[
24
,
25
]
.
However, the limit object defined therein is a state mapping
X(t,\xi)
rather than a state distribution
f(t,x,\xi)
.
This implies that each state trajectory
X(t,\cdot)
is uniquely indexed by a label
\xi\in\Omega
, precluding a density of states sharing a common label.
This formulation is already conceptually very close to the limit objects we consider in our framework.

Although the specific systems considered in
[
24
,
25
]
differ from ours, applying their approach to our system in the ODE case (
\nu=0
in (
1.1
)) would lead to the following limiting equation. For simplicity, we present its strong form rather than the proper weak formulation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X(t,\xi)=\mu\big(x(t,\zeta)\big)+\int_{\Omega}w(\xi,\zeta)\sigma\big(X(t,\xi),X(t,\zeta)\big)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}w(t,\xi,\zeta)=\alpha\big(X(t,\xi),X(t,\zeta)\big)w(t,\xi,\zeta)+\beta\big(X(t,\xi),X(t,\zeta)\big).

However, such approach do not straightforwardly accommodate stochasticity in the dynamics. Our work addresses this by defining our state-weight-triplet on an explicit filtered probability space
\Omega\times\hat{\Omega}
, which enables a rigorous analysis of the fully stochastic dynamics. This synthesis is what allows us to rigorously define and analyze the mean-field limit for the fully stochastic dynamics.
Moreover, by leveraging graphon theory’s regularity and sampling lemmas, we avoid any need for “well-prepared” initial data for both
\bm{w}^{(n)}
and
\bm{X}^{(n)}
. (See the discussion in Section
2.2
.)

It is important to emphasize that our results do not encompass those in
[
24
,
25
]
.
They considered interaction kernels
w
that are significantly sparser than the
L^{\infty}
objects we consider, such as graphops and digraph measures, which have important practical implications in certain application scenarios.
Due to current limitations in dense graph theory, our approach does not readily extend to comparably sparse settings without compromising properties such as the sampling lemma.

2.4.
Relation to
[
29
,
32
,
42
]

Finally, we wish to discuss several recent works, namely
[
29
,
32
,
42
]
. While these articles do not directly consider adaptive weights, the mathematical structures of the mean-field limits they analyze share significant connections with the framework presented in this paper.

To facilitate this comparison, we first recall the concept of graphon homomorphism density. For a finite oriented graph
F
with vertex set
\mathsf{v}(F)
, edge set
\mathsf{e}(F)
and a kernel
w:\Omega^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, the homomorphism density of
F
in
w
is defined as:

(2.3)

\displaystyle t(F,w)\vcentcolon=\int_{\Omega^{|\mathsf{v}(F)|}}\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathsf{e}(F)}w(\xi_{i},\xi_{j})\prod_{i\in\mathsf{v}(F)}\mathrm{d}\pi(\xi_{i}).

The Counting Lemma and Inverse Counting Lemma provide a fundamental equivalence in dense graph limit theory
[
12
]
. Although originally formulated for undirected graphs and symmetric kernels, the result is known to extend to the oriented, non-symmetric case. It states that for a sequence of kernels
\{w^{(n)}\}
:

\displaystyle t(F,w^{(n)})\to t(F,w),\ \forall\text{ simple oriented graphs }F\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\delta_{\square}(w^{(n)},w)\to 0.

We emphasize that both directions of this equivalence are quantitative, though we present the qualitative statement here for simplicity.
A weaker notion of convergence arises when one considers only tree homomorphisms. This is related to the concept of fractional isomorphism or fractional overlay
[
8
,
17
,
27
]
. In this case, convergence of tree homomorphism densities is equivalent to convergence in a different pseudo-metric, which we denote by
\gamma_{\square}
:

\displaystyle t(T,w^{(n)})\to t(T,w),\ \forall\text{ simple oriented trees }F\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\gamma_{\square}(w^{(n)},w)\to 0.

To define such pseudo-metric
\gamma_{\square}
, we first introduce the adjacency operator
T_{w}
associated with a kernel
w\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times\Omega)
:

T_{w}(\psi)(\xi)\vcentcolon=\int_{\Omega}w(\xi,\zeta)\,\psi(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\zeta),\quad\forall\xi\in\Omega.

It can be verified that

\|w\|_{\square}\leq\|T_{w}\|_{L^{\infty}\to L^{1}}\leq 4\|w\|_{\square}.

In light of this equivalence, we subsequently use the operator norm in place of the original cut norm definition.
Let
\Pi(\Omega^{(1)},\Omega^{(2)})
be the set of all coupling measures between the probability spaces
(\Omega^{(1)},\pi_{1})
and
(\Omega^{(2)},\pi_{2})
. Any such measure
\gamma\in\Pi(\Omega^{(1)},\Omega^{(2)})
induces a coupling operator
T_{\gamma}
defined by

T_{\gamma}(\psi)(\xi)\vcentcolon=\int_{\Omega^{(2)}}\gamma(\xi,\mathrm{d}\zeta)\,\psi(\zeta),\quad\forall\xi\in\Omega^{(1)},

for instance from
L^{\infty}(\Omega^{(2)})
to
L^{\infty}(\Omega^{(1)})
.
The pseudo-metric
\gamma_{\square}
between two kernels
w^{(1)}
and
w^{(2)}
is then defined as the infimum over all such couplings:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\square}(w^{(1)},w^{(2)})

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\Omega^{(1)},\Omega^{(2)})}\bigg[\|T_{w^{(1)}}\circ T_{\gamma}-T_{\gamma}\circ T_{w^{(2)}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{(2)})\to L^{1}(\Omega^{(1)})}

\displaystyle+\|T_{\gamma}^{*}\circ T_{w^{(1)}}-T_{w^{(2)}}\circ T_{\gamma}^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{(1)})\to L^{1}(\Omega^{(2)})}\bigg].

Analogous to how our framework unifies the Wasserstein and unlabeled cut distances into a single metric, the concept of graph homomorphism density can be synthesized with the notion of marginal laws from kinetic theory to describe the non-exchangeable systems. To this end, one can define
tensorized homomorphism densities
(the terminology is adopted from
[
42
]
).
Such definitions no longer produce scalar values, but rather measures on the product state space
\mathbb{D}^{k}
.

This type of definition was first considered in
[
29
]
for tree graphs, where for a solution
f
to an extended Vlasov PDE (similar to (
2.1
)), the following object is defined: For
t\geq 0
and
z=(z_{i})_{i\in\mathsf{v}(T)}\in\mathbb{D}^{\mathsf{v}(T)}
,

\displaystyle\tau(T,w,f)(t,z)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\int_{\Omega^{\mathsf{v}(T)}}\prod_{(k,l)\in\mathsf{e}(T)}{w_{\xi_{k},\xi_{l}}}\prod_{m\in\mathsf{v}(T)}f(t,z_{m},\xi_{m})\mathrm{d}\pi(\xi_{m}).

Using this notion and a hierarchical estimate, they established stability for the extended Vlasov PDE for a class of sparse weight limits termed “extended graphons”, whose assumptions are far weaker than
w\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times\Omega)
.

An extended BBGKY hierarchy, defined directly for the original system of SDEs (such as (
1.1a
)), was established in
[
32
]
as follows:

\displaystyle\tau_{N}(T,w,\bm{X})(t,\mathrm{d}z)\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{N^{|\mathsf{v}(T)|}}\sum_{(i_{j})_{j\in\mathsf{v}(T)}\in[N]^{\wedge\mathsf{v}(T)}}\prod_{(l,m)\in\mathsf{e}(T)}{w_{i_{l},i_{m}}}\ \mathrm{Law}_{(\bm{X}_{i_{j}}(t))_{j\in\mathsf{v}(T)}}(\mathrm{d}z).

This formulation corresponds to the finite agent case where
\Omega=[N]
, and the summation is over tuples of distinct indices which we denote by
[N]^{\wedge\mathsf{v}(T)}
; this does not alter the limit as the contribution from repeated indices is negligible as
N\to\infty
.
Their work accommodates extended graphons and also incorporates stochasticity on the interactions of a Poisson process type.

For dense graphon limits
w\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times\Omega)
, techniques from graphon theory can be more directly leveraged. In
[
42
]
, a more general definition of a tensorized homomorphism density was introduced, applicable to any simple oriented graph
F
. This object, which we denote by
\bm{\tau}(F,w,\bm{X})
, takes values in the tensor product space
(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{\otimes\mathsf{e}(F)}\otimes\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{D})^{\otimes\mathsf{v}^{\prime}(F)}
, where
\mathsf{v}^{\prime}(F)
represents all vertices in
F
that serve as a source for at least one directed edge. The definition is as follows (omitting the time variable for simplicity):

\displaystyle\bm{\tau}(F,w,\bm{X})\vcentcolon=\int_{\Omega^{\mathsf{v}(F)}}\bigotimes_{(k,l)\in\mathsf{e}(F)}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
w(\xi_{k},\xi_{l})\end{pmatrix}\bigotimes_{j\in\mathsf{v}^{\prime}(F)}\delta_{\bm{X}_{\xi_{j}}(t)}\prod_{i\in\mathsf{v}(F)}\mathrm{d}\xi_{i}.

A generalization of the Counting Lemma and Inverse Counting Lemma was then introduced for these tensorized densities. Although the rigorous proof in
[
42
]
was provided only for the case where the state space is the one-dimensional torus (
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}
), the result establishes the following equivalences:

\displaystyle\bm{\tau}(F,w^{(n)},\bm{X}^{(n)})\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\bm{\tau}(F,w,\bm{X}),\ \forall\text{ simple oriented graphs }F

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad\delta_{W_{1},\square}\big((\bm{X}^{(n)},w^{(n)}),(\bm{X},w)\big)\to 0,

and

\displaystyle\bm{\tau}(T,w^{(n)},\bm{X}^{(n)})\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\bm{\tau}(T,w,\bm{X}),\ \forall\text{ oriented trees }T

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad\gamma_{W_{1},\square}\big((\bm{X}^{(n)},w^{(n)}),(\bm{X},w)\big)\to 0.

Here, the weak-
*
convergence on measures can be quantified by a suitable negative Sobolev norm, and the unified metric
\gamma_{W_{1},\square}
(bi-coupling distance in
[
42
]
) is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\gamma_{W_{1},\square}\big((X^{(1)},w^{(1)},\Omega^{(1)}),(X^{(2)},w^{(2)},\Omega^{(2)})\big)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\Omega^{(1)},\Omega^{(2)})}\bigg[\int_{\Omega^{(1)}\times\Omega^{(2)}}|X^{(1)}_{\xi_{1}}-X^{(2)}_{\xi_{2}}|\,\gamma(\mathrm{d}\xi_{1},\mathrm{d}\xi_{2})

\displaystyle+\|T_{w^{(1)}}\circ T_{\gamma}-T_{\gamma}\circ T_{w^{(2)}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{(2)})\to L^{1}(\Omega^{(1)})}

\displaystyle+\|T_{\gamma}^{*}\circ T_{w^{(1)}}-T_{w^{(2)}}\circ T_{\gamma}^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{(1)})\to L^{1}(\Omega^{(2)})}\bigg].

Building upon this equivalence, the work
[
42
]
established the mean-field limit and stability for the system (
1.1a
) using hierarchical estimates. This result can be seen as complementary to the conclusions of the present work:

•

The present work focuses exclusively on results within the stronger
\delta_{W_{1},\square}
topology. In contrast,
[
42
]
characterizes the limits and provides quantitative stability estimates for both the
\delta_{W_{1},\square}
(graph) and
\gamma_{W_{1},\square}
(tree) topologies. (The latter is computationally significant as its corresponding optimization problem is convex).

At this point, it seems to us that quantitative results in the
\gamma_{W_{1},\square}
(tree) topology necessitate the use of hierarchical estimates, and may not be achievable via the Sznitman-like, trajectory-wise estimation methods
[
41
]
employed herein.

•

Nevertheless, the present work establishes a better convergence rate under the stronger topology, and our quantitative estimates maintain a more direct correspondence with those in classical mean-field theory
(specifically, Thm.
1.6
with (
1.2
), Cor.
1.11
with (
1.4
), and Thm.
1.7
with (
1.6
)).

Although a full proof is beyond the scope of this paper, we expect that under the assumptions of Proposition
1.12
, the following holds: for any Lipschitz bounded dual test function
\varphi
on
\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{e}(F)}\times\mathbb{D}^{\mathsf{v}^{\prime}(F)}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int\varphi(z)\left(\bm{\tau}(F,w^{(n)},\bm{X}^{(n)})-\bm{\tau}(F,w,\bm{X})\right)(\mathrm{d}z)\right|\right]\leq C(t)N^{-\theta},

where
\theta
can be any rate less than
1/2
.
This corresponds to the dual formulation of convergence (cf. (
1.5
)), as convergence against all such test functions, when combined with uniform moment bounds, is sufficient to establish the desired mean-field limit. This rate appears beyond the reach of hierarchical methods, such as in
[
42
]
, where finite-
N
errors can propagate in a doubly exponential manner.

•

Additionally, while
[
42
]
presents a general abstract framework, its rigorous proofs are provided only for the case where the state space is the one-dimensional torus (
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{T}
). Extending their methods to higher dimensions or Euclidean spaces appears to require stronger differentiability assumptions on the dynamics (likely related to Sobolev inequalities), as well as moment-based techniques from
[
32
]
. In contrast, our approach is technically more direct, applying to Lipschitz bounded dynamics in any dimension.

•

Finally,
[
42
]
did not consider adaptive weights, although an adaptation of the form (
1.1b
) could formally be written into the hierarchy of equations for the tensorized homomorphism densities. Rigorously justifying the mean-field limit for the fully adaptive system (
1.1
) via tensorized homomorphism densities remains an interesting direction for future work.

2.5.
Future perspectives

We conclude by outlining several directions we wish to explore in future work.

•

A natural first question is the extent to which our results can be generalized to alternative definitions of graph limits. For instance, while we expect it to be technically demanding, adapting our framework to
L^{p}
graphons
[
10
,
11
]
for
p>1
might follow a conceptual roadmap similar to the one presented in this article.

A more substantial and non-trivial extension would be to consider sparser interaction regimes. This includes the graphops analyzed in
[
23
,
24
]
, the digraph measures of
[
34
,
25
]
, or the extended graphons from
[
29
,
32
]
. Our results are fundamentally connected to the sampling lemma, which is best understood in the context of dense graphs. Successfully extending our analysis to these significantly sparser settings is a profound challenge that may first require new foundational insights from graph limit theory itself.

•

Another direction, specific to systems with adaptive weights, is to incorporate more complex sampling schemes and dynamics of weights. A classical result from graphon theory shows that an Erdős-Rényi graph, where each edge exists independently with a fixed probability, converges in the cut-norm to a constant kernel (see
[
36
]
). This suggests that our framework should be robust to additional randomness. For example, one might consider adding an Erdős-Rényi noise term to the initial data, or reinterpreting the weights as probabilities for edge existence.
Theorem
1.7
is directly applicable to these situations.
It is natural to conjecture that the other results in the article would still hold, up to additional remainder terms of order
O(1/\sqrt{\log N})
.

A related, but technically more challenging, question is to add i.i.d. Brownian noise
\bm{B}_{i,j}
in the weight dynamics, i.e., replacing (
1.1
) by

(2.4a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bm{X}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mu(\bm{X}_{i})\mathrm{d}t+\nu\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{i}+\frac{1}{N}\!\sum_{j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}\bm{w}_{i,j}\sigma(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{X}_{j})\mathrm{d}t,

(2.4b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bm{w}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\alpha(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{X}_{j})\bm{w}_{i,j}\mathrm{d}t+\beta(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{X}_{j})\mathrm{d}t+\eta\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{i,j},

where
\eta>0
.
Even this small modification breaks the uniform
L^{\infty}
bound on the weights, connecting this problem to the aforementioned challenge with
L^{p}
extensions. However, heuristics from graphon theory suggest that i.i.d. noise (under the mean-field scaling
1/N
) added to edges often affects the cut-norm only through its mean (which is zero here). This leads us to the following conjecture:

Conjecture 2.1

(Mean-field weight noise does not affect the limit)
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
1.6
hold, but with the dynamics of
(\bm{X}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N]}
and
(\bm{w}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N]}
governed by the SDEs (
2.4
) with weight noise intensity
\eta>0
. Let the limiting processes
(\overline{X}(t),\overline{w}(t))
still be defined as the solution to the original McKean-Vlasov SDEs (
1.13
) (i.e., without the
\eta
term), with initial data drawn from the same independent mixture sampling.

Then, the estimates of this article still hold. In particular, there exists a constant
C(t)>0
, which depends on time
t
and on the bounds specified in Assumptions
1.3
and
1.4
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\bigg(\Big((\bm{X}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N]},(\bm{w}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N]},[N]\Big),\Big(\overline{X}(t),\overline{w}(t),(\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})\Big)\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(t)}{\sqrt{\log N}}.

•

A further related direction is to investigate whether simpler structure can be derived from non-exchangeable systems like (
1.1
) under specific scaling limits. A seemingly straightforward case to consider is the following system:

(2.5a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bm{X}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mu(\bm{X}_{i})\mathrm{d}t+\nu\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{i}+\frac{1}{N}\!\sum_{j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}\bm{w}_{i,j}\sigma(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{X}_{j})\mathrm{d}t,

(2.5b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bm{w}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{-1}\big(-\bm{w}_{i,j}+\beta(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{X}_{j})\big)\mathrm{d}t.

In this system, the weights
\bm{w}_{i,j}
formally evolve according to an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck type process, with the solution given by:

(2.6)

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{i,j}(t)=e^{-t/\epsilon}\bm{w}_{i,j}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\epsilon^{-1}e^{-(t-s)/\epsilon}\beta(\bm{X}_{i}(s),\bm{X}_{j}(s))\mathrm{d}s.

The solution structure indicates that the influence of the initial weights decays exponentially, while the dynamics are increasingly driven by a time average of
\beta
. This leads to a natural conjecture that in the singular limit as
\epsilon\to 0
, the weights are instantaneously determined by the states, i.e.,
\bm{w}_{i,j}(t)\to\beta(\bm{X}_{i}(t),\bm{X}_{j}(t))
. In this limit, the system effectively reduces to:

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bm{X}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mu(\bm{X}_{i})\mathrm{d}t+\nu\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{i}+\frac{1}{N}\!\sum_{j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}\beta(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{X}_{j})\sigma(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{X}_{j})\mathrm{d}t.

Mathematically, we formalize this intuition in the following conjecture.

Conjecture 2.2

(A limit of weight dynamics)
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
1.6
hold. For a parameter
\epsilon>0
, let
(\bm{X}^{(\epsilon)}(t),\bm{w}^{(\epsilon)}(t))
be the solution to the system (
2.5
). Let
(\bm{X}^{(0)}(t))
be the solution to the limiting system (
2.7
) with the same initial data, and define its corresponding weights via
\bm{w}^{(0)}_{ij}(t)\vcentcolon=\beta(\bm{X}^{(0)}_{i}(t),\bm{X}^{(0)}_{j}(t))
.
There exists a constant
C(t)>0
, independent of
\epsilon
but dependent on time
t
and the bounds from Assumptions
1.3
and
1.4
, such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\|\bm{w}^{(\epsilon)}(t)-\bm{w}^{(0)}(t)\|_{\ell^{1}([N]\times[N])}+\frac{1}{N}\|\bm{X}^{(\epsilon)}(t)-\bm{X}^{(0)}(t)\|_{\ell^{2}([N])}\leq\epsilon C(t),

Connecting our adaptive systems to classical models of collective behavior, such as the Cucker-Smale model
[
9
,
22
,
28
]
is a particularly interesting problem. Investigating whether the dynamics similar to (
2.5
) can be viewed as a “delayed” Cucker-Smale system, where the alignment potential adapts based on particle histories, could provide new insights into both non-exchangeable systems and the mechanisms of collective motion.

•

There is one more interesting direction. To build the intuition, it is helpful to consider a toy model:

Let
N\geq 1
be a multiple of 6. For simplicity, we consider only the deterministic version (
\nu=0
) of the system (
2.5
). Consider two systems with identical initial states,
X^{(1)}_{i}(0)=X^{(2)}_{i}(0)
for all
i\in[N]
, which exhibit a periodic structure with period
N/6
:

\displaystyle X^{(1)}_{i}(0)=X^{(1)}_{i+N/6}(0),\quad\forall 1\leq i\leq 5N/6.

The initial weights, however, are given by distinct block matrix structures. Let
J_{1}
be an
N/6\times N/6
matrix.
The matrices
J_{2}
and
J_{3}
are
2\times 2
and
3\times 3
block matrices, respectively, where each block is the matrix
J_{1}
.

–

The first system,
w^{(1)}(0)
, is defined by partitioning the
N
agents into two equal-sized groups, giving a complete bipartite structure:

w^{(1)}(0)=2\begin{pmatrix}0&J_{3}\\
J_{3}&0\end{pmatrix}.

–

The second system,
w^{(2)}(0)
, is defined by partitioning the agents into three equal-sized groups, forming a directed cycle:

w^{(2)}(0)=3\begin{pmatrix}0&J_{2}&0\\
0&0&J_{2}\\
J_{2}&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

One can readily verify that for all
t\geq 0
, the state dynamics of the two systems are identical, i.e.,
X^{(1)}_{i}(t)=X^{(2)}_{i}(t)
for all
i\in[N]
, and

\displaystyle X^{(1)}_{i}(t)=X^{(1)}_{i+N/6}(t),\quad\forall 1\leq i\leq 5N/6.

This occurs because the total interaction experienced by any agent
i\in[N]
is identical in both systems, albeit composed of contributions from different sets of agents. Nevertheless, despite this identical evolution, the initial weight configurations
w^{(1)}(0)
and
w^{(2)}(0)
can be far apart in the cut distance, yet in our
\delta_{W_{1},\square}
. This observation suggests that our metric, while effective for proving convergence, may be too strict to fully capture the notion of dynamical similarity in all cases.
It turns out that the bi-coupling distance
\gamma_{W_{1},\square}
mentioned in Section
2.4
is suitable here, namely

\displaystyle\gamma_{W_{1},\square}\big((X^{(1)},w^{(1)},[N]),(X^{(2)},w^{(2)},[N])\big)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi([N],[N])}\bigg[\int_{[N]\times[N]}|X^{(1)}_{\xi_{1}}-X^{(2)}_{\xi_{2}}|\,\gamma(\mathrm{d}\xi_{1},\mathrm{d}\xi_{2})

\displaystyle+\|T_{w^{(1)}}\circ T_{\gamma}-T_{\gamma}\circ T_{w^{(2)}}\|_{L^{\infty}([N])\to L^{1}([N])}

\displaystyle+\|T_{\gamma}^{*}\circ T_{w^{(1)}}-T_{w^{(2)}}\circ T_{\gamma}^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}([N])\to L^{1}([N])}\bigg],

\displaystyle=0.

The infimum
0
is achieved by taking
\gamma\in\Pi([N],[N])
as

\displaystyle\gamma_{i,j}=\frac{1}{6N}\mathbbm{1}\{i-j\text{ mod }N/6=0\}.

In other words, we couple the
6
“periods” of
[N]
together.

In addition,
a intriguing observation arises from the long-term behavior of the weights. Since the state evolution is identical (
X^{(1)}(t)=X^{(2)}(t)
for all
t
) and maintains the
N/6
periodicity, it is easy to see from the solution form that

w_{i,j}^{(k)}(t)-e^{-t/\epsilon}w^{(k)}_{i,j}(0)=\int_{0}^{t}\epsilon^{-1}e^{-(t-s)/\epsilon}\beta(X_{i}(s),X_{j}(s))\mathrm{d}s

yields a matrix that is independent of
k\in\{1,2\}
and has a
6\times 6
block structure. This heuristically suggests that the difference
w^{(1)}(t)-w^{(2)}(t)
decays to zero as
t\to\infty
, implying convergence in
L^{1}
and in the cut norm.

To formalize the intuition from this toy model that for systems of the form (
2.5
), some heterogeneous weight structures (which are identical only in
\gamma_{W_{1},\square}
) may tend towards partial homogenization (which are identical in
\delta_{W_{1},\square}
) over long time scales, we pose the following conjecture:

Conjecture 2.3

(Partial homogenization)
.

Consider the SDE system (
2.5
) whose coefficients satisfying Assumption
1.3
and
\epsilon=1
. For
k\in N
, assume deterministic initial data
(X^{(k)}_{i}(0))_{i\in[N_{k}]}
and
(w^{(k)}_{i,j}(0))_{i,j\in[N_{k}]}
satisfying Assumption
1.4
.
Moreover, assume

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{k_{1},k_{2}\geq k}\gamma_{W_{1},\square}\bigg(\Big((X^{(k_{1})}_{i}(0))_{i\in[N_{k_{1}}]},(w^{(k_{1})}_{i,j}(0))_{i,j\in[N_{k_{1}}]},[N_{k_{1}}]\Big),

\displaystyle\Big((X^{(k_{2})}_{i}(0))_{i\in[N_{k_{2}}]},(w^{(k_{2})}_{i,j}(0))_{i,j\in[N_{k_{2}}]},[N_{k_{2}}]\Big)\bigg)=0.

Then as
t\to\infty
, the solutions
(\bm{X}^{(k)}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N_{k}]},(\bm{w}^{(k)}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N_{k}]}
to (
2.5
) converge in the stronger sense that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\limsup_{k\to\infty}\sup_{k_{1},k_{2}\geq k}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\bigg(\Big((\bm{X}^{(k_{1})}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N_{k_{1}}]},(\bm{w}^{(k_{1})}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N_{k_{1}}]},[N_{k_{1}}]\Big),

\displaystyle\Big((\bm{X}^{(k_{2})}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N_{k_{2}}]},(\bm{w}^{(k_{2})}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N_{k_{2}}]},[N_{k_{2}}]\Big)\bigg)\bigg]=0.

•

A final direction concerns the self-organization from homogeneous initial data, which is a key feature distinguishing the stochastic system (
1.1
) (
\nu>0
) from its deterministic counterpart. Consider a simple toy model where
X_{i}(0)\equiv 0\in\mathbb{D}
and
w_{i,j}(0)\equiv 1
for all
i,j
. If
\nu=0
, the system remains homogeneous for all time:
X_{i}(t)
and
w_{ij}(t)
stay constant across all indices. In contrast, when
\nu>0
, the individual Brownian motions first cause the states
X_{i}(t)
to diverge, which in turn induces heterogeneity in the weights
w_{ij}(t)
.

Our results, such as Corollary
1.11
, guarantee that as
N\to\infty
, this system converges to a well-defined mean-field limit
(\overline{X}(t),\overline{w}(t))
in the
\delta_{W_{1},\square}
metric. An initial question is whether this emergent limit is non-trivial, i.e., is the limiting kernel
\overline{w}
non-constant? This is not immediately obvious, as a limit of pointwise divergent objects can still be constant (e.g., the convergence of an Erdős-Rényi graph to a constant graphon).

A more profound question concerns the regularity of this self-organized limit
(\overline{X}(t),\overline{w}(t))
. We can possibly investigate this by analyzing the
N\to\infty
convergence rate of the independent mixture sampling error:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\bigg(\Big((\overline{\bm{X}}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N]},(\overline{\bm{w}}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N]},[N]\Big),\Big(\overline{X}(t),\overline{w}(t),(\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})\Big)\bigg)\bigg],

or more simply,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\big((\overline{\bm{w}}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N]},\overline{w}(t)\big)\big].

Lemma
1.10
or the Sampling Lemma in graphon theory guarantees a baseline convergence rate of at least
O(1/\sqrt{\log N})
. However, if the limiting object possesses additional smoothness, for instance, if up to a relabeling,
\overline{w}
is a Lipschitz-bounded kernel on a low-dimensional manifold, one would expect a faster, polynomial rate of convergence, such as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\big((\overline{\bm{w}}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N]},\overline{w}(t)\big)\big]\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{[\xi]}\left[W_{1}\left(\sum_{i\in[N]}\delta_{\xi_{i}},\pi\right)\right]\lesssim N^{-\theta}.

Therefore, encountering a sampling convergence rate better than the logarithmic one should strongly suggest the existence of a non-trivial emergent structure and a degree of regularity in the limit.

3.1.
Proof of Proposition
1.5
and Proposition
1.12

We consolidate the proofs of the two components as they rely on identical estimates on a Picard operator.
For any pair of
(\mathcal{A}\otimes\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{t})
-adapted and
(\mathcal{A}\otimes\widehat{\mathcal{F}}_{t})^{\otimes 2}
-adapted processes

\displaystyle(X_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}}(t))_{(\xi,\widehat{\xi})\in\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega},t\in[0,T]}\quad\text{and}\quad(w_{\xi,\widehat{\xi},\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(t))_{(\xi,\widehat{\xi}),(\zeta,\widehat{\zeta})\in\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega},t\in[0,T]},

we introduce the integral operator
\mathscr{I}
acting on
(X,w)
as

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}(X,w)=(\mathscr{X}(X,w),\mathscr{W}(X,w)),

where the components are given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{X}(X,w)_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}}(t)

\displaystyle=(X_{(0)})_{\xi}+\int_{0}^{t}\mu(X_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}}(s))\mathrm{d}s+\nu B_{\widehat{\xi}}(t)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}\sim\pi\otimes\widehat{\pi}}[w_{\xi,\widehat{\xi},\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(s)\sigma(X_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}}(s),X_{\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(s))]\mathrm{d}s

and

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}(X,w)_{\xi,\widehat{\xi},\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(t)

\displaystyle=(w_{(0)})_{\xi,\zeta}+\int_{0}^{t}\alpha(X_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}}(s),X_{\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(s))w_{\xi,\widehat{\xi},\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(s)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\beta(X_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}}(s),X_{\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(s))\mathrm{d}s

for all
(\xi,\widehat{\xi}),(\zeta,\widehat{\zeta})\in\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}
and
t\in[0,T]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The integral operator
\mathscr{I}(X,w)=(\mathscr{X}(X,w),\mathscr{W}(X,w))
satisfies the moment propagation estimate

\displaystyle\|\mathscr{W}(X,w)(t)\|_{L^{\infty}((\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\|w_{(0)}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{2})}+\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\|\alpha\|_{L^{\infty}}\|w(s)\|_{L^{\infty}((\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})^{2})}+\|\beta\|_{L^{\infty}}\Big)\mathrm{d}s,

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}}|\mathscr{X}(X,w)(t)|^{2}(\mathrm{d}\pi\otimes\mathrm{d}\widehat{\pi})

\displaystyle\leq 4\bigg(\int_{\Omega}|X_{(0)}|^{2}\mathrm{d}\pi+\|\mu\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}t^{2}+\nu^{2}t+\|w\|_{L^{\infty}([0,t]\times(\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})^{2})}^{2}\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}t^{2}\bigg).

Moreover, the operator satisfies the Lipschitz estimate:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}}\big|\mathscr{X}(X^{(1)},w^{(1)})(t)-\mathscr{X}(X^{(2)},w^{(2)})(t)\big|(\mathrm{d}\pi\otimes\mathrm{d}\widehat{\pi})

\displaystyle+\big\|\mathscr{W}(X^{(1)},w^{(1)})(t)-\mathscr{W}(X^{(2)},w^{(2)})(t)\big\|_{\square(\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\big|X^{(1)}_{(0)}-X^{(2)}_{(0)}\big|\mathrm{d}\pi+\big\|w^{(1)}_{(0)}-w^{(2)}_{(0)}\big\|_{\square(\Omega)}+C_{\mu,\sigma,\nu,\alpha,\beta}(t)(1+\|w\|_{L^{\infty}})\bigg(

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}}\big|X^{(1)}(s)-X^{(2)}(s)\big|(\mathrm{d}\pi\otimes\mathrm{d}\widehat{\pi})+\big\|w^{(1)}(s)-w^{(2)}(s)\big\|_{\square(\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})}\mathrm{d}s\bigg)

where the time-dependent constant
C_{\mu,\sigma,\nu,\alpha,\beta}(t)
depends on the regularity of coefficients as specified in Assumption
1.3
, and
\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}:=\max\limits_{k=1,2}\|w^{(k)}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,t]\times(\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})^{2}}
.
The same estimates remain valid when replacing all cut norms
\|\cdot\|_{\square}
with
L^{1}
norms for kernel functions.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

The moment propagation estimates follow directly from Hölder’s inequalities; we omit the detailed derivation.

We now prove the Lipschitz continuity. Regarding the
\mathscr{X}
-term, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}}\big|\mathscr{X}(X^{(1)},w^{(1)})(t)-\mathscr{X}(X^{(2)},w^{(2)})(t)\big|(\mathrm{d}\pi\otimes\mathrm{d}\widehat{\pi})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\big|X^{(1)}_{(0)}-X^{(2)}_{(0)}\big|\mathrm{d}\pi+\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\mu\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}}\big|X^{(1)}(s)-X^{(2)}(s)\big|(\mathrm{d}\pi\otimes\mathrm{d}\widehat{\pi})\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}}\int_{\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}}\Big|w^{(1)}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi},\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(s)\sigma(X^{(1)}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}}(s),X^{(1)}_{\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(s))

\displaystyle-w^{(2)}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi},\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(s)\sigma(X^{(2)}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}}(s),X^{(2)}_{\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(s))\Big|\mathrm{d}\pi(\xi)\mathrm{d}\widehat{\pi}(\widehat{\xi})\mathrm{d}\pi(\zeta)\mathrm{d}\widehat{\pi}(\widehat{\zeta})\mathrm{d}s,

where the last term admits the further bound

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}}\int_{\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}}\Big|w^{(1)}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi},\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(s)\sigma(X^{(1)}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}}(s),X^{(1)}_{\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(s))

\displaystyle-w^{(2)}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi},\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(s)\sigma(X^{(2)}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}}(s),X^{(2)}_{\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}(s))\Big|\mathrm{d}\pi(\xi)\mathrm{d}\widehat{\pi}(\widehat{\xi})\mathrm{d}\pi(\zeta)\mathrm{d}\widehat{\pi}(\widehat{\zeta})\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|w\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times(\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})^{2})}\|\nabla\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}}\big|X^{(1)}(s)-X^{(2)}(s)\big|(\mathrm{d}\pi\otimes\mathrm{d}\widehat{\pi})\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}4\big\|w^{(1)}(s)-w^{(2)}(s)\big\|_{\square(\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})}\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}}\mathrm{d}s.

Next, for the
\mathscr{W}
-component, we have

\displaystyle\big\|\mathscr{W}(X^{(1)},w^{(1)})(t)-\mathscr{W}(X^{(2)},w^{(2)})(t)\big\|_{\square(\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|w^{(1)}_{(0)}-w^{(2)}_{(0)}\big\|_{\square(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}2\|\nabla\alpha\|_{L^{\infty}}\|w\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times(\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})^{2})}\int_{\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}}\big|X^{(1)}(s)-X^{(2)}(s)\big|(\mathrm{d}\pi\otimes\mathrm{d}\widehat{\pi})\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\|\alpha_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\alpha_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}\big\|w^{(1)}(s)-w^{(2)}(s)\big\|_{\square(\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}2\|\nabla\beta\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega}}\big|X^{(1)}(s)-X^{(2)}(s)\big|(\mathrm{d}\pi\otimes\mathrm{d}\widehat{\pi})\mathrm{d}s.

This estimate of the
\mathscr{W}
-component is the only instance where we use the factorization
\alpha(x,y)=\alpha_{1}(x)\alpha_{2}(y)
to simplify estimates. This is justified by properties of the cut distance: for functions
u=u(\xi)
,
w=w(\xi,\zeta)
, and
v=v(\zeta)
, we directly verify that

\displaystyle\|uwv\|_{\square}\leq\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}\|v\|_{L^{\infty}}\|w\|_{\square}.

However, no comparable straightforward estimate exists for general products where both
u=u(\xi,\zeta)
and
w=w(\xi,\zeta)
lack this separable structure.

Combining all estimates and incorporating the coefficients, we consolidate them into the expression
C_{\mu,\sigma,\nu,\alpha,\beta}(t)
.
∎

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.6

The proof strategy of Theorem
1.6
is closely related to the classical propagation of chaos arguments, such as those in
[
41
]
. The core idea typically involves decomposing the error into a term controlled by Lipschitz continuity (often handled via Grönwall’s inequality) and a fluctuation term of order
N^{-1/2}
arising from independence. For our setting, once an appropriate filtered probability space and coupling are defined, the subsequent calculations largely mirror the classical approach.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Recall that the probability measures
(\pi_{i})_{i\in[N]}
are absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi
and satisfy the averaging condition
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}=\pi
. Hence (
1.13a
) can be further decompose as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\overline{X}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}}

\displaystyle=\mu(\overline{X}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}})\mathrm{d}t+\nu\mathrm{d}B_{\widehat{\xi}}

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}\sim\pi\otimes\widehat{\pi}}\big[\overline{w}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi},\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}}\sigma(\overline{X}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}},\overline{X}_{\zeta,\widehat{\zeta}})\big]\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle=\mu(\overline{X}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}})\mathrm{d}t+\nu\mathrm{d}B_{\widehat{\xi}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in[N]}\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}\sim\pi_{j}\otimes\widehat{\pi}}\big[\overline{w}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi},\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}}\sigma(\overline{X}_{\xi,\widehat{\xi}},\overline{X}_{\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}})\big]\mathrm{d}t.

Recall that for an arbitrary realization of the randomness

\displaystyle{((\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}))_{i\in[N]}}\in\big((\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})^{N},(\mathcal{A}\otimes\widehat{\mathcal{F}})^{\otimes N},\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}(\pi_{i}\otimes\widehat{\pi})\big),

one has

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{X}}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=(\overline{X}(t))_{\xi_{i}},

\displaystyle\forall i\in[N]

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{w}}_{i,j}(t)

\displaystyle=(\overline{w}(t))_{\xi_{i},\xi_{j}},

\displaystyle\forall i,j\in[N],\ i\neq j.

Hence, for any
i\in[N]
,

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{i}(t)-\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mu(\bm{X}_{i}(s))\mathrm{d}t+\nu\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{i}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\!\sum_{j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}\bm{w}_{i,j}(s)\sigma(\bm{X}_{i}(s),\bm{X}_{j}(s))\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\mu(\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(s))\mathrm{d}t-\nu\mathrm{d}B_{\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(t)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in[N]}\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}\sim\pi_{j}\otimes\widehat{\pi}}\big[\overline{w}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}}\sigma(\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}},\overline{X}_{\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}})\big]\mathrm{d}t.

Also, for any
i,j\in[N]
,

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{i,j}(t)-\overline{w}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\alpha(\bm{X}_{i}(s),\bm{X}_{j}(s))\bm{w}_{i,j}(s)-\alpha(\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(s),\overline{X}_{\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}}(s))\overline{w}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}}(s)\Big)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\beta(\bm{X}_{i}(s),\bm{X}_{j}(s))-\beta(\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(s),\overline{X}_{\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}}(s))\Big)\mathrm{d}t.

The difference of states can be further decomposed into three terms, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{i}(t)-\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(t)=(\bm{D}_{(1)})_{i}(t)+(\bm{D}_{(2)})_{i}(t)+(\bm{D}_{(3)})_{i}(t)

where

\displaystyle(\bm{D}_{(1)})_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\int_{0}^{t}\mu(\bm{X}_{i}(s))-\mu(\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(s))\mathrm{d}t+\nu\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{i}(t)-\nu\mathrm{d}B_{\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(t)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}\Big(\bm{w}_{i,j}(s)\sigma(\bm{X}_{i}(s),\bm{X}_{j}(s))

\displaystyle-\overline{w}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j}}(s)\sigma(\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(s),\overline{X}_{\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j}}(s))\Big)\mathrm{d}s

measures the difference before time
t
propagated to time
t
,

\displaystyle(\bm{D}_{(2)})_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}\Big(\overline{w}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j}}(s)\sigma(\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(s),\overline{X}_{\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j}}(s))

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}\sim\pi_{j}\otimes\widehat{\pi}}\big[\overline{w}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}}(s)\sigma(\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(s),\overline{X}_{\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}}(s))\big]\Big)\mathrm{d}t

measures the correlation introduced during the propagation, and

\displaystyle(\bm{D}_{(3)})_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\int_{0}^{t}-\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{i},\widehat{\zeta}_{i}\sim\pi_{i}\otimes\widehat{\pi}}\big[\overline{w}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\zeta_{i},\widehat{\zeta}_{i}}(s)\sigma(\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(s),\overline{X}_{\zeta_{i},\widehat{\zeta}_{i}}(s))\big]\mathrm{d}t

measures the
N^{-1}
error introduced by the diagonal entry
(i,i)
in the estimate.

It is straightforward to verify (by triangle inequality and Hölder inequality) that

\displaystyle|\bm{w}_{i,j}(t)-\overline{w}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\alpha\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\overline{w}\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{t}|\bm{X}_{i}(s)-\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(s)|+|\bm{X}_{j}(s)-\overline{X}_{\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j}}(s)|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\|\alpha\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{t}|\bm{w}_{i,j}(s)-\overline{w}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}}(s)|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\|\nabla\beta\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{t}|\bm{X}_{i}(s)-\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(s)|+|\bm{X}_{j}(s)-\overline{X}_{\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j}}(s)|\mathrm{d}s

where we use shorthand notation
\|\overline{w}\|_{L^{\infty}}
for the norm
\|\overline{w}\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times(\Omega\times\widehat{\Omega})^{2})}
, whose a priori bound was previously established (see the proof of Proposition
1.12
).
Similarly, notice that
\nu\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{i}(t)-\nu\mathrm{d}B_{\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(t)=0
almost surely, it is straightforward that

\displaystyle|(\bm{D}_{(1)})_{i}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\mu\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{t}|\bm{X}_{i}(s)-\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(s)|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\|\nabla\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\overline{w}\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{t}|\bm{X}_{i}(s)-\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(s)|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\|\nabla\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\overline{w}\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}|\bm{X}_{j}(s)-\overline{X}_{\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j}}(s)|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}|\bm{w}_{i,j}(s)-\overline{w}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j}}(s)|\ \mathrm{d}s.

The estimation of
(\bm{D}_{(2)})_{i}(t)
follows through a standard application of the conditional independence argument from
[
41
]
. Note that given
i\in[N]
and
(\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i})
,

\displaystyle\overline{V}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j}}(s)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\overline{w}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j}}(s)\sigma(\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(s),\overline{X}_{\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j}}(s))

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}\sim\pi_{j}\otimes\widehat{\pi}}\big[\overline{w}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}}(s)\sigma(\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(s),\overline{X}_{\zeta_{j},\widehat{\zeta}_{j}}(s))\big]

depends solely on
(\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j})
and has mean zero. Hence these terms are conditionally independent for all
j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}\overline{V}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j}}(s)\bigg)^{2}\ \Big|\ \xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}\big(\overline{V}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j}}(s)\big)^{2}\ \Big|\ \xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}\bigg]

as all cross-product terms vanish under conditional expectation.
Therefore, it the straightforward to verify that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[|(\bm{D}_{(2)})_{i}(t)|\ |\ \xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}\big]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}\overline{V}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j}}(s)\ \Big|\ \xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}\bigg]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}\big(\overline{V}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j}}(s)\big)^{2}\ \Big|\ \xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}\bigg]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\overline{w}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}}\frac{t}{\sqrt{N}}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle|(\bm{D}_{(3)})_{i}(t)|\leq\|\overline{w}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}}\frac{t}{N}.

Combine all the estimates above, we have the Gronwall type inequality

\displaystyle G(t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}C_{1}G(s)+\frac{C_{2}}{\sqrt{N}}\ \mathrm{d}s

where

\displaystyle G(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\max\bigg\{\sup_{i\in[N]}\mathbb{E}\Big[|\bm{X}_{i}(t)-\overline{X}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i}}(t)|\Big],

\displaystyle\sup_{i,j\in[N],i\neq j}\mathbb{E}\Big[|\bm{w}_{i,j}(t)-\overline{w}_{\xi_{i},\widehat{\xi}_{i},\xi_{j},\widehat{\xi}_{j}}(t)|\Big]\bigg\}

and the coefficients can be chosen explicitly as

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=2\|\nabla\alpha\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\overline{w}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\alpha\|_{L^{\infty}}+2\|\nabla\beta\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle+\|\nabla\mu\|_{L^{\infty}}+2\|\nabla\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\overline{w}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}},

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=3\|\overline{w}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Consequently by Gronwall inequality,

\displaystyle G(t)\leq\frac{\exp(C_{1}t)C_{2}t}{\sqrt{N}}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.7
and Corollary
1.11

In the proofs, we assume the validity of Lemma
1.10
, whose proof is deferred to the last subsection due to its distinct technical nature.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

We proceed by directly comparing any two solutions to the SDE system (
1.1
), denoted by
(\bm{X}^{(k)}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N_{k}]},(\bm{w}^{(k)}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N_{k}]}
for
k=1,2
, with deterministic initial data. The proof for this case naturally encompasses the one presented in the theorem’s statement.

First, we observe that any deterministic initial data for a
N
-agent system can be viewed as an independent mixture sampling from itself. Specifically, for any
(X_{(0)},w_{(0)},[N])
, we can introduce
[\xi]\sim\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}
where each
\pi_{i}
is the Dirac mass at
i\in[N]
. Then, it is easy to verify that
(X_{0})_{[\xi]}=X_{0}
and
(w_{0})_{[\xi]}=w_{0}
.

For
k=1,2
, let
(\overline{X}^{(k)}(t),\overline{w}^{(k)}(t),([N_{k}]\times\widehat{\Omega}))
denote the state-weight triplet from the McKean-Vlasov SDE (
1.13
) with initial condition
(X^{(k)}(0),w^{(k)}(0),[N_{k}])
constructed above.
Accordingly,
((\bm{X}^{(k)}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N_{k}]},(\bm{w}^{(k)}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N_{k}]},[N_{k}])
can be understood as random state-weight triplets.

Then, the expectation of
\delta_{W_{1},\square}
distance can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\left(((\bm{X}^{(1)}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N_{1}]},(\bm{w}^{(1)}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N_{1}]}),((\bm{X}^{(2)}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N_{2}]},(\bm{w}^{(2)}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N_{2}]})\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{W_{1},\square}\left((\overline{X}^{(1)}(t),\overline{w}^{(1)}(t)),(\overline{X}^{(2)}(t),\overline{w}^{(2)}(t))\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1,2}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\left(((\bm{X}^{(k)}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N_{k}]},(\bm{w}^{(k)}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N_{k}]}),(\overline{X}^{(k)}(t),\overline{w}^{(k)}(t))\right)\bigg]

By Proposition
1.12
, the first term is bounded by

\displaystyle\delta_{W_{1},\square}\left((\overline{X}^{(1)}(t),\overline{w}^{(1)}(t)),(\overline{X}^{(2)}(t),\overline{w}^{(2)}(t))\right)

\displaystyle\leq C(t)\delta_{W_{1},\square}\left((X^{(1)}(0),w^{(1)}(0)),(X^{(2)}(0),w^{(2)}(0))\right)

As for the second term, we can further introduce
((\overline{\bm{X}}^{(k)}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N_{k}]},(\overline{\bm{w}}^{(k)}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N_{k}]})
as the independent mixture sampling from
(\overline{X}^{(k)}(t),\overline{w}^{(k)}(t),([N_{k}]\times\widehat{\Omega}))
with the aforementioned
\pi_{i}
.
This gives the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\left(((\bm{X}^{(k)}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N_{k}]},(\bm{w}^{(k)}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N_{k}]}),(\overline{X}^{(k)}(t),\overline{w}^{(k)}(t))\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\left(((\bm{X}^{(k)}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N_{k}]},(\bm{w}^{(k)}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N_{k}]}),((\overline{\bm{X}}^{(k)}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N_{k}]},(\overline{\bm{w}}^{(k)}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N_{k}]})\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\left(((\overline{\bm{X}}^{(k)}_{i}(t))_{i\in[N_{k}]},(\overline{\bm{w}}^{(k)}_{i,j}(t))_{i,j\in[N_{k}]}),(\overline{X}^{(k)}(t),\overline{w}^{(k)}(t))\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(t)}{\sqrt{N_{k}}}+\frac{C(t)}{{\sqrt{\log N_{k}}}},

where in the last inequality we apply Theorem
1.6
and Lemma
1.10

Combining all the estimates above yields the desired estimate stated in Theorem
1.7
.
∎

3.4.
Proof of Lemma
1.10

The proof presented here is a natural extension of the argument for the Sampling Lemma found in the textbook
[
36
]
. We include it for the completeness of this article.

The first modifications involve our consideration of independent mixture sampling, as defined in Definition
1.2
, rather than solely i.i.d. uniform sampling. Additionally, we need to address the presence of the new term
X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
. The first adjustment does not alter the crucial independence-based large deviation estimates (such as Azuma-Hoeffding or Chernoff-type bounds). The second, while introducing a less favorable coefficient, does not affect the overall asymptotic convergence.

Our current focus is not on replicating the precise convergence rates found in the literature. Instead, to describe estimates that hold with overwhelming probability, we introduce the following notion:

Definition 3.2
.

If, for a sequence of random events
A_{k}
,
k\to\infty
, there exist constants
C_{1}>0,C_{2}>0,
and
\theta>0
such that for all
k
(or for all
k\geq k_{0}
for some initial
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
),

\mathbb{P}(A_{k})\geq 1-C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}k^{\theta}),

we say that the sequence of events
A_{k}
,
k\to\infty
is
k
-certain.

With the definition of
k
-certainty, we can now state our adapted version of the first sampling lemma.

Lemma 3.3

(First Sampling Lemma adapted from
[
36
, Lemma 10.6]
)
.

Let
w\in L^{\infty}(\Omega^{2})
and let
[\xi]=(\xi_{i})_{i\in[N]}\in\Omega^{N}
be an independent mixture sampling distributed according to
[\xi]\sim\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}
. Then, it is
N
-certain that

\Big|\|w_{[\xi]}\|_{\square}-\|w\|_{\square}\Big|\leq\frac{C_{w}}{N^{1/4}},

where
C_{w}>0
is a constant that depends only on
\|w\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{2})}
.

Our proof strategy follows that of
[
36
]
. We begin by considering the positive one-sided cut norm, defined as

\|w\|_{\square}^{+}\vcentcolon=\sup_{S,T\subseteq\Omega}\int_{S\times T}w_{\xi,\zeta}\mathrm{d}\xi\mathrm{d}\zeta.

Given the identity
\|w\|_{\square}=\max\left\{\|w\|_{\square}^{+},\|-w\|_{\square}^{+}\right\}
, the lemma follows if we can show that it is
N
-certain that

\Big|\|w_{[\xi]}\|_{\square}^{+}-\|w\|_{\square}^{+}\Big|\leq\frac{C_{w}}{N^{1/4}},

where the constant
C_{w}
may differ from that in Lemma
3.3
.

To proceed with the proof for the one-sided norm, we first adapt a supporting lemma concerning the selection of a random subset of column indices. Let
B=(B_{ij})_{i,j\in[N]}
be an
N\times N
matrix. For any set
Q_{1}\subseteq[N]
of rows and any set
Q_{2}\subseteq[N]
of columns, we set
B(Q_{1},Q_{2})\vcentcolon=\sum_{i\in Q_{1},j\in Q_{2}}B_{ij}
.
For
Q_{1}\subseteq[N]
a set of rows, let

Q_{1}^{+}\vcentcolon=\{j\in[N]\mid B(Q_{1},\{j\})>0\},

which implies
B(Q_{1},Q_{1}^{+})\geq 0
. The sets
Q_{1}^{-}
,
Q_{2}^{+}
and
Q_{2}^{-}
are defined analogously.

Lemma 3.4

(Random Column Subset, analogous to
[
36
, Lemma 10.7]
)
.

Let
B=(B_{ij})_{i,j\in[N]}
be an
N\times N
matrix.
Let
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq[N]
(where
S_{1}
is a set of rows and
S_{2}
is a set of columns). Let
Q
be a random
q
-subset of
[N]
(representing selected column indices), where
1\leq q\leq N
. Then

B(S_{1},S_{2})\leq\mathbb{E}\left[B((Q\cap S_{2})^{+},S_{2})\right]+\frac{\|B\|_{L^{\infty}}N^{2}}{\sqrt{q}}.

Proof.

We start from the following identity: For any
Q\in[N]
,

\displaystyle B(S_{1},S_{2})

\displaystyle=B((Q\cap S_{2})^{+}\cap S_{1},S_{2})+B((Q\cap S_{2})^{-}\cap S_{1},S_{2})

\displaystyle=B((Q\cap S_{2})^{+},S_{2})

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i\in[N]\setminus S_{1}}B((Q\cap S_{2})^{+}\cap\{i\},S_{2})+\sum_{i\in S_{1}}B((Q\cap S_{2})^{-}\cap\{i\},S_{2}).

Our goal is to show the per-row bounds that for each
i\in[N]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B((Q\cap S_{2})^{-}\cap\{i\},S_{2})\right]\leq\frac{\|B\|_{L^{\infty}}N}{\sqrt{q}},

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\left[B((Q\cap S_{2})^{+}\cap\{i\},S_{2})\right]\leq\frac{\|B\|_{L^{\infty}}N}{\sqrt{q}}.

Taking the expectation for the identity and summing over
i
then yields the desired estimate.

For a fixed row
i\in S_{1}
, let
A_{i}\vcentcolon=\sum_{j\in Q\cap S_{2}}B_{ij}
,
b_{i}\vcentcolon=\sum_{j\in S_{2}}B_{ij}
, and
c_{i}\vcentcolon=\sum_{j\in S_{2}}B_{ij}^{2}
. The expectation term for row
i
is then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B\big((Q\cap S_{2})^{-}\cap\{i\},S_{2}\big)\right]=\mathbb{P}(A_{i}<0)b_{i}.

If
b_{i}\leq 0
, this term is non-positive, satisfying the required bound. Assume
b_{i}>0
.
We have moment estimates
\mathbb{E}[A_{i}]=\frac{q}{N}b_{i}>0
and
\mathbb{E}[A_{i}^{2}]=\frac{q}{N}c_{i}+\frac{q(q-1)}{N(N-1)}(b_{i}^{2}-c_{i})\leq\frac{q}{N}c_{i}+\frac{q^{2}}{N^{2}}b_{i}^{2}
.
By Chebyshev’s inequality, since
\mathbb{E}[A_{i}]>0
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{i}<0)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(|A_{i}-\mathbb{E}[A_{i}]|\geq\mathbb{E}[A_{i}])\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[A_{i}^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}[A_{i}])^{2}}{(\mathbb{E}[A_{i}])^{2}}\leq\frac{N}{q}\frac{c_{i}}{b_{i}^{2}}.

Since
\mathbb{P}(A_{i}<0)\in[0,1]
and
b_{i}>0
,
\mathbb{P}(A_{i}<0)b_{i}\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{P}(A_{i}<0)}b_{i}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[B\big((Q\cap S_{2})^{-}\cap\{i\},S_{2}\big)\right]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{P}(A_{i}<0)}b_{i}\leq\frac{\|B\|_{L^{\infty}}N}{\sqrt{q}}.

This establishes the first per-row bound and a symmetric argument for the second per-row bound completes the proof.
∎

The following lemma gives an upper bound on the one-sided cut norm, using the sampling procedure from the previous lemma.

Lemma 3.5

(One-Sided Upper Bound, analogous to
[
36
, Lemma 10.8]
)
.

Let
B=(B_{ij})_{i,j\in[N]}
be an
N\times N
matrix. Let
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
be independent random
q
-subsets of
[N]
,
(1\leq q\leq N)
. Then

\|B\|_{\square}^{+}\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{1},Q_{2}}\left[\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}R_{i}\subseteq Q_{i}\end{subarray}}B(R_{2}^{+},R_{1}^{+})\right]+\frac{2\|B\|_{L^{\infty}}}{\sqrt{q}},

where
C_{B}>0
is a constant that only depends on
\|B\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

Proof.

Let
S_{1}^{*},S_{2}^{*}\subseteq[N]
be sets such that
N^{2}\|B\|_{\square}^{+}=B(S_{1}^{*},S_{2}^{*})
.
By applying Lemma
3.4
twice, we have

\displaystyle B(S_{1}^{*},S_{2}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Q_{2}}\left[B\left((Q_{2}\cap S_{2}^{*})^{+},S_{2}^{*}\right)\right]+\frac{\|B\|_{L^{\infty}}N^{2}}{\sqrt{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Q_{1},Q_{2}}\left[B\left((Q_{2}\cap S_{2}^{*})^{+},(Q_{1}\cap(Q_{2}\cap S_{2}^{*})^{+})^{+}\right)\right]+\frac{2\|B\|_{L^{\infty}}N^{2}}{\sqrt{q}}.

Dividing by
N^{2}
and taking the supremum over
R_{1},R_{2}
yields the lemma.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

To bound the difference
\|w_{[\xi]}\|_{\square}-\|w\|_{\square}
from below, we first consider its expectation.
For any two measurable subsets
S_{1},S_{2}\subset\Omega
, we have by definition of the empirical cut norm,

\|w_{[\xi]}\|_{\square}^{+}\geq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j:\xi_{i}\in S_{1},\xi_{j}\in S_{2}}w_{\xi_{i},\xi_{j}}.

Taking the expectation over independent mixture sampling
[\xi]\sim\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\pi_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{[\xi]}(\|w_{[\xi]}\|_{\square}^{+})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{[\xi]}\left[\sum_{i,j:\xi_{i}\in S_{1},\xi_{j}\in S_{2}}w_{\xi_{i},\xi_{j}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in[N],\ i\neq j}\int_{S_{1}\times S_{2}}w_{\xi_{i},\xi_{j}}\mathrm{d}\pi_{i}\mathrm{d}\pi_{j}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\in[N]}\int_{S_{1}\cap S_{2}}w_{\xi_{i},\xi_{i}}\mathrm{d}\pi_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{S_{1}\times S_{2}}w_{\xi,\zeta}\mathrm{d}\pi\mathrm{d}\pi-\frac{2\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}}{N}.

Taking the supremum of the right side over all measurable sets
S_{1},S_{2}
we get

\mathbb{E}_{[\xi]}(\|w_{[\xi]}\|_{\square}^{+})\geq\|w\|_{\square}^{+}-\frac{2\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}}{N}.

The
N
-certain bound
\|w_{[\xi]}\|_{\square}-\|w\|_{\square}\geq-\frac{C_{w}}{N^{1/4}}
then follows by a sample concentration argument (such as Azuma’s Inequality), as changing one
\xi_{k}
alters
\|w_{[\xi]}\|_{\square}^{+}
by at most
O(1/N)
and one can construct Doob’s martingale.

To prove the upper bound of
\|w_{[\xi]}\|_{\square}-\|w\|_{\square}
, let
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
be independent random
q
-subsets of
[N]
, where
q=\lfloor\sqrt{N}/4\rfloor
. Lemma
3.5
states that for any given realization
[\xi]
,

\|w_{[\xi]}\|_{\square}^{+}\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{1},Q_{2}}\left[\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}R_{i}\subseteq Q_{i}\end{subarray}}w_{[\xi]}(R_{2}^{+},R_{1}^{+})\right]+\frac{2\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}}{\sqrt{q}},

where
C_{w}
depends only on
\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

For given subsets
R_{i}\subseteq Q_{i}\subseteq[N]
. Let
Q=Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}
. Let
[\xi]_{Q}=(\xi_{i})_{i\in Q}
denote the samples with indices in
Q
.
We define the sets
Y_{1},Y_{2}\subseteq\Omega
as follows:

\displaystyle\textstyle Y_{1}=\{\zeta\in\Omega\mid\sum_{i^{\prime}\in R_{1}}w_{\xi_{i^{\prime}},\zeta}>0\},\quad Y_{2}=\{\xi\in\Omega\mid\sum_{j^{\prime}\in R_{2}}w_{\xi,\xi_{j^{\prime}}}>0\}.

Note that the definitions of
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
depend only on the samples in
[\xi]_{Q}
and not on the remaining samples
[\xi]_{[N]\setminus Q}=(\xi_{i})_{i\in[N]\setminus Q}
.

Therefore, with fixed
R_{i}\subseteq Q_{i}\subseteq[N]
,
[\xi]_{Q}
and
Y_{1},Y_{2}
, one can take the decomposition

\displaystyle w_{[\xi]}(R_{2}^{+},R_{1}^{+})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[N]}w_{\xi_{i},\xi_{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{\xi_{i}\in Y_{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{\xi_{j}\in Y_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j\in[N]\setminus Q}w_{\xi_{i},\xi_{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{\xi_{i}\in Y_{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{\xi_{j}\in Y_{1}}+\sum_{i\in Q\text{ or }j\in Q}w_{\xi_{i},\xi_{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{\xi_{i}\in Y_{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{\xi_{j}\in Y_{1}}.

For the first term one can take the expectation over
[\xi]_{[N]\setminus Q}
and obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{[\xi]_{[N]\setminus Q}}\bigg[\sum_{i,j\in[N]\setminus Q}w_{\xi_{i},\xi_{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{\xi_{i}\in Y_{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{\xi_{j}\in Y_{1}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{i,j\in[N]\setminus Q}\int_{\Omega\times\Omega}w_{\xi,\zeta}\mathbbm{1}_{\xi\in Y_{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{\zeta\in Y_{1}}\ \mathrm{d}\pi_{i}\mathrm{d}\pi_{j}

\displaystyle\quad\leq N^{2}\|w\|_{\square}^{+}.

The second term contains no more than
2N|Q|\leq 4Nq
entries. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{[\xi]_{[N]\setminus Q}}\big[w_{[\xi]}(R_{2}^{+},R_{1}^{+})\big]\leq N^{2}\|w\|_{\square}^{+}+4\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}Nq.

Then, following a similar sample concentration argument we can obtain a
N
-certain bound: Changing one
\xi_{i}\in[N]\setminus Q
alters
w_{[\xi]}(R_{2}^{+},R_{1}^{+})
by at most
4N\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}
. By Azuma’s inequality, with probability at least
1-e^{-1.9q}

\displaystyle w_{[\xi]}(R_{2}^{+},R_{1}^{+})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{[\xi]_{[N]\setminus Q}}\big[w_{[\xi]}(R_{2}^{+},R_{1}^{+})\big]+7.9\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}N\sqrt{Nq}

\displaystyle\leq N^{2}\|w\|_{\square}^{+}+4\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}Nq+7.9\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}N\sqrt{Nq}.

Recall that we take
q=\lfloor\sqrt{N}/4\rfloor
. The number of possible pairs of sets
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
is
4^{q}
, which grows slower than
e^{-1.9q}
. Hence by taking the maximum over
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
one can still obtain
N
-certain bounds

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}R_{i}\subseteq Q_{i}\end{subarray}}w_{[\xi]}(R_{2}^{+},R_{1}^{+})\leq N^{2}\|w\|_{\square}^{+}+4\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}Nq+7.9\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}N\sqrt{Nq}.

This bound is for any fixed realization of
Q_{i}\subseteq[N]
and
[\xi]_{Q}
.

Then by taking the expectation over
Q_{i}
,
i=1,2
, we can obtain
N
-certain bound

\displaystyle\|w_{[\xi]}\|_{\square}^{+}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{1},Q_{2}}\left[\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}R_{i}\subseteq Q_{i}\end{subarray}}w_{[\xi]}(R_{2}^{+},R_{1}^{+})\right]+\frac{2\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}}{\sqrt{q}},

\displaystyle\leq\|w\|_{\square}^{+}+4\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}\frac{q}{N}+7.9\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}\sqrt{\frac{q}{N}}+2\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\|w\|_{\square}^{+}+\frac{C_{w}}{N^{1/4}}.

Finally, by applying the same argument for
\|-w\|_{\square}^{+}
, we conclude the proof.

∎

We now prove Lemma
1.10
using Lemma
3.3
.

Proof of Lemma
1.10
.

Let
m=\lceil N^{1/4}\rceil
. One can construct (following the procedure in
[
36
, Lemma 9.15]
) an equipartition
\mathcal{P}=\{V_{1},\dots,V_{m}\}
of
\Omega
into
m
classes such that

\displaystyle\|w-w_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{\square}\leq\frac{8\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}}{\sqrt{\log N}},\quad\|X-X_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{L^{1}}\leq\frac{8\|X\|_{L^{2}}}{\sqrt{\log N}},

where
w_{\mathcal{P}}
and
X_{\mathcal{P}}
are piecewise constant projection on
\Omega\times\Omega
and
\Omega
, respectively.
By triangle inequality we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\left((X_{[\xi]},w_{[\xi]}),(X,w)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\left((X_{[\xi]},w_{[\xi]}),({X_{\mathcal{P}}}_{[\xi]},{w_{\mathcal{P}}}_{[\xi]})\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\left(({X_{\mathcal{P}}}_{[\xi]},{w_{\mathcal{P}}}_{[\xi]}),(X_{\mathcal{P}},w_{\mathcal{P}})\right)\right]+\delta_{W_{1},\square}\left((X_{\mathcal{P}},w_{\mathcal{P}}),(X,w)\right).

For the last term we have

\displaystyle\delta_{W_{1},\square}\left((X_{\mathcal{P}},w_{\mathcal{P}}),(X,w)\right)\leq\frac{C_{X,w}}{\sqrt{\log N}},

where
C_{X,w}
depends only on
\|X\|_{L^{2}}
and
\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}
. It remains to bound the first two terms on the right hand side.

Since
[\xi]
is an independent mixture sampling, by Lemma
3.3
, it is
N
-certain that

\displaystyle\big|\|w_{[\xi]}-{w_{\mathcal{P}}}_{[\xi]}\|_{\square}-\|w-w_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{\square}\big|\leq\frac{C_{w}}{N^{1/4}},

which implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\|w_{[\xi]}-{w_{\mathcal{P}}}_{[\xi]}\|_{\square}-\|w-w_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{\square}\big|\Big]\leq\frac{C_{w}}{N^{1/4}},

with a possibly larger
C_{w}
which only depends on
\|w\|_{L^{\infty}}
.
It is also straightforward to establish the following bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\|X_{[\xi]}-{X_{\mathcal{P}}}_{[\xi]}\|_{L^{1}}-\|X-X_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{L^{1}}\big|\Big]\leq\frac{C_{X}}{N^{1/2}},

where
C_{X}
depends only
\int_{\Omega}|X|^{2}\mathrm{d}\pi
. In summary,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{W_{1},\square}\left((X_{[\xi]},w_{[\xi]}),({X_{\mathcal{P}}}_{[\xi]},{w_{\mathcal{P}}}_{[\xi]})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\|X_{[\xi]}-{X_{\mathcal{P}}}_{[\xi]}\|_{L^{1}}+\|w_{[\xi]}-{w_{\mathcal{P}}}_{[\xi]}\|_{\square}\big|\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\|X_{[\xi]}-{X_{\mathcal{P}}}_{[\xi]}\|_{L^{1}}-\|X-X_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{L^{1}}\big|\Big]+\|X-X_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\|w_{[\xi]}-{w_{\mathcal{P}}}_{[\xi]}\|_{\square}-\|w-w_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{\square}\big|\Big]+\|w-w_{\mathcal{P}}\|_{\square}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{w,X}}{\sqrt{\log N}}.

Finally, the probability of
\xi_{i}
,
i\in[N]
belonging to
V_{k}
,
k\in[m]
is
1/m
. Hence
