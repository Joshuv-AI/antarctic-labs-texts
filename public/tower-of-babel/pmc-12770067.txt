Therapeutic potential of metformin-based combination therapy in acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Mobina Nakhaei Shamahmood, Abolfazl Miri, Mohammad Javad Yousefi, Fatemeh Mezginejad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) is the most prevalent form of acute leukemia. Despite great efforts, the overall prognosis for patients with AML remains poor, and the mortality rate due to relapse is also considerable. Consequently, the disease poses treatment challenges. Several studies have indicated that metformin may serve as a beneficial adjunct to traditional anti-leukemia medications, especially enhancing their effectiveness against drug resistance; however, it is not effective when used as a standalone treatment. In this context, combination therapies are currently being explored as a promising treatment option.MethodsThis study is a narrative review. A comprehensive literature search across multiple databases, including PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, Embase and Cochrane, yielded 103 relevant articles up to July 2024. From an initial total of 103 articles, we excluded unrelated and duplicate articles, as well as those for which the full text was unavailable and review articles. Ultimately, 32 articles were included in the study. During the database search, we employed search terms such as AML, combination, and metformin to find relevant articles.ResultsAccording to the results of the studies, the combination of metformin with gilteritinib, sorafenib, 6-benzylthionosine, venetoclax, diclofenac, diflunisal, and cytarabine reduces intracellular ATP levels, inhibits glycolysis, halts the cell cycle, diminishes oxidative phosphorylation, increases the expression of anti-apoptotic proteins and increases cytotoxicity. This combination also induces apoptosis, reduces cell growth, and ultimately leads to a significant decrease in the burden of AML and an increase in overall survival for these patients.ConclusionsTherefore, combining these drugs with metformin may enhance the synergistic effect and improve the treatment of AML patients.Keywords: AML, Combination, Metformin

Introduction

Acute leukemia is a group of clonal and heterogeneous hematopoietic disorders characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of immature blast cells. Among acute leukemias, acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most common form in children, whereas AML is the predominant acute leukemia in adults [1]. AML arises from genetic mutations in myeloid progenitor cells that lead to the expansion of abnormal and undifferentiated blasts [2–4]. Clonal hematopoiesis is thought to proceed to AML at a rate of 0.5–1% year over time [5]. The number of mutations per sample, the greater variant allele frequency, and mutations in certain genes, such as DNMT3A, TET2, SRSF2, and ASXL1, are factors that differentiate age-related clonal hematopoiesis from pre-AML [6]. According to estimates, the 5-year OS is 30%4, although it varies significantly by age group, reaching 50% in younger patients and fewer than 10% in those over 60 [7]. Men are slightly more susceptible, while children and young people are less affected [8]. However, given that the FDA has approved 11 new medications or combinations since 2017, these statistics, which are primarily based on older trials, may be improving [9]. The cornerstone of intense chemotherapy is anthracycline and cytarabine-based treatment, which is most frequently referred to as a “7 + 3” regimen that uses 100–200 mg/m2 of cytarabine for seven days and 60–90 mg/m2 of daunorubicin for three days. Other induction regimens, such as CLAG-M, 102 G-CLAM, 103 IA, 104 FLAG-IDA, 105, and lomustine-IA, are in use, nevertheless. It’s uncertain if any of these are “better” than 3 + 7 on their own [9–11]. Relapse is still a major problem, though, since many patients have a return of their condition within a year. The urgent need for innovative therapeutic approaches is highlighted by the present medicines’ low effectiveness [9].

Metformin is one of the most well-known metabolic modifiers for the treatment of type 2 diabetes. It was approved in the United States in 1995 and in England in 1958 [12, 13]. The World Health Organization (WHO) also considered it an essential medication in the treatment protocol for diabetic patients [14]. Reducing hyperglycemia and its associated clinical symptoms is the most well-known effect of metformin [15]. It is currently one of the prescription medications available due to its low cost and effective therapeutic value in managing blood sugar levels safely [16]. Nowadays, Recent studies have identified additional potential applications of this medication, including anti-aging, anti-cancer treatment, cardiovascular protection, neuroprotection, and management of polycystic ovarian syndrome [17]. Evans and colleagues were the first scientists to discover in 1995 that metformin possesses anti-cancer effects without harming healthy tissues, reduces the risk of cancer, and helps regulate blood sugar levels in individuals with diabetes. Subsequent research involving individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes has shown that metformin administration decreases the risk of various cancers, including head and neck cancer, prostate cancer, lung cancer, breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, colorectal cancer, endometrial cancer, ovarian cancer, and liver cancer [18]. Based on epidemiological studies, it has been confirmed that individuals with various types of solid tumors who took metformin experienced lower cancer mortality rates and improved cancer outcomes. Furthermore, earlier research indicates that metformin can target drug-resistant cells or cancer-initiating stem cells when used in combination with cytotoxic chemotherapy [19–23]. Clinical trials highlight the effectiveness of metformin as an anti-tumor agent when used in combination therapy. For example, it was shown that the two-year disease-free survival rate in patients with advanced cervical cancer improved significantly with the combination of metformin and chemotherapy (67% compared to 33%) [24].

Even though metformin has several potential anticancer mechanisms, it inhibits mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I, which in turn inhibits mTOR through TSC1/2 and promotes LKB1/AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase) [25]. In addition to halting fatty acid oxidation, the inhibition of Complex I shifts the metabolic balance in favor of glycolysis as a mechanism for ATP production. Elevated cellular AMP levels lead to numerous metabolic effects, including enhanced glucose absorption and glycolysis, as well as decreased gluconeogenesis [26, 27]. Apoptosis and cell death in prostate and liver cancer cells are induced by AMPK activation and ATP depletion, which results in a AMP/ATP ratio shift [28, 29]. The potential application of metformin in the treatment of AML has been investigated using a similar approach. However, elevated levels of AKT in AML promote glycolysis, which may contribute to metformin resistance [30, 31]. Furthermore, metformin can reduce the NAD+/NADH ratio by inhibiting mitochondrial complex I, which can affect protein expression. This occurs because NAD + serves as a substrate for various enzymes, including histone deacetylases and those involved in the regulation of digestive enzymes. Additionally, Tet1 DNA methylase plays a crucial role in the epigenetic regulation of the expression of several important proteins [32]. furthermore, it has been shown that metformin reduces the expression of stem cell markers in cancer cells, including CD44 and ALDH, and can influence the leukemic properties of stem cells [33] (Fig. 1).

The figure summarizes both AMPK-dependent and AMPK-independent pathways, including regulation of mitochondrial function, hypoglycemia, immune modulation (PDL-1, FOXP3), mTOR signaling (mTORC1/mTORC2), cell proliferation, gene transcription, ROS production, and direct regulation of STAT3, highlighting the multifaceted effects of metformin on cancer cell metabolism and growth.”

The clinical treatment course for AML has remained unchanged for the past 40 years. It typically involves 7 to 10 days of cytarabine administration, followed by 3 days of anthracycline therapy to induce remission [34]. Even though anthracycline and cytarabine combination therapy can lead to complete remission, the overall prognosis for patients with AML remains poor. The majority of patients ultimately succumb to relapse, a condition caused by the proliferation of chemotherapy-resistant leukemic cells [35, 36]. However, research generally indicates that traditional AML treatment is only partially effective, with a cure rate of 30% to 40%. Additionally, the overall five-year survival rate remains approximately 50% [37, 38]. Studies indicate that only approximately 10% of elderly patients and less than half of adult patients aged 60 years or younger receive treatment for AML. As a result, treatment outcomes remain ambiguous and even leukemic cells may develop drug resistance either before or after therapy. Consequently, allogeneic stem cell transplantation is currently the only therapeutic option available for cases of relapse. Therefore, new therapeutic modalities must be developed to address these issues [38, 39].

Numerous studies support the use of metformin as a general adjunct to traditional anti-leukemia medications, and accumulating evidence suggests that this approach may enhance efficacy or overcome drug resistance [40, 41], However, research involving patients with AML and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) found no correlation between metformin use and overall survival or disease-free survival [42]. In any case, the mechanisms by which metformin exerts its anticancer effects, whether used alone or in combination, remain unclear. Although metformin enhances the synthesis of glycolytic ATP as a compensatory mechanism, its overall cytotoxicity against AML cells when administered alone is relatively moderate, even at achievable plasma concentrations (10–20 mM), despite its therapeutic potential. Consequently, the efficacy of metformin in treating AML has been limited. Research has shown that metformin and other mitochondrial inhibitors are not particularly effective when used in isolation, as cancer cells possess glycolytic escape mechanisms that allow them to compensate for reduced mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation [43].Therefore, in addition to administering metformin alone, its anti-leukemic effect can be enhanced when combined with chemotherapy and anti-inflammatory drugs such as gilteritinib, sorafenib, 6-benzylthionosine, venetoclax, diclofenac, difonizal, and cytarabine. Studies have shown that the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapeutic agents in patients with AML are increased when metformin is included in the treatment regimen. To improve the efficacy of novel medications used in the treatment of AML, including metformin, which targets leukemogenic molecules and pathways, it may be necessary to combine chemotherapy with anti-inflammatory agents.

The significance of metformin in AML and cancer treatment in general has been reviewed several times, but none have explicitly looked at how it interacts with anti-inflammatory medications or chemotherapy. By delivering a thorough synthesis of the available data on these interactions, pointing out possible synergistic processes, and providing important insights into their therapeutic implications, this study seeks to close this gap. Our study offers a fresh viewpoint that goes beyond assessments that have already been published by concentrating on these neglected areas.

In this field, several sporadic surveys have been conducted; however, the results have been inconsistent and not always accessible. Notably, a few reviews have evaluated the interaction between metformin and chemotherapy or anti-inflammatory medications. For professionals in this discipline, compiling these disparate studies seems beneficial. Thus, the goal of this research is to evaluate the results of earlier studies, classify drugs that are used with metformin, and explain and analyze those findings. Given the scattered and sometimes inconsistent nature of existing studies, as well as the limited reviews explicitly addressing metformin’s interactions with chemotherapy and anti-inflammatory agents, a comprehensive synthesis is needed. Moreover, combination therapy has emerged as a promising approach in cancer treatment, offering potential benefits such as enhanced efficacy, reduced drug resistance, and minimized toxicity. Therefore, this review not only evaluates and classifies previous findings on metformin-based combinations but also highlights the general therapeutic advantages of combining anticancer agents.

Materials and methods

This narrative review sought to assess the impact of metformin-based combination therapies in the management of AML over the period from 2014 to July 2024. A systematic literature search was performed across multiple databases, including PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, Embase, and Cochrane, employing the search terms “AML,” “combination therapy,” and “metformin.” The search was restricted to publications in English. Eligibility criteria encompassed original research articles and clinical studies investigating the use of metformin in combination regimens for AML treatment. Studies were excluded if they were duplicates, lacked accessible full texts, were published in languages other than English, or were review articles. To ensure comprehensive coverage, abstracts and full texts from pertinent conferences—namely the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO), the American Society of Hematology (ASH), the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), and the European Hematology Association (EHA)—were also examined. Furthermore, an extensive supplementary search via Google was conducted to identify any potentially overlooked publications. From an initial set of 103 articles, 32 met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the final analysis. The selected studies were categorized and evaluated according to the type of combination therapy employed, characteristics of the patient populations, and reported clinical outcomes. Consideration was also given to potential biases, including publication bias and inter-study heterogeneity, during the synthesis of findings. Results

Combination therapy by metformin with chemotherapy drugs

Metformin and gilteritinib

Gilteritinib is a targeted therapy, specifically an oral FLT3 inhibitor, distinct from traditional cytotoxic chemotherapy. Unlike chemotherapy, which non-selectively kills rapidly dividing cells, gilteritinib selectively inhibits FLT3 and related kinases that promote AML cell survival and proliferation. In fact this drug targets several kinases, including c-KIT, AXL, ALK, FLT3 and specifically FLT3-internal tandem duplications (ITD), FLT3-ITD-D835Y, and FLT3-D835Y mutations, which are classified as ITD and tyrosine kinase domain (TKD) mutation [44]. Gilteritinib ultimately induces cell death in cells expressing FLT3-ITD. It is effective in vivo when used alone, in conjunction with chemotherapy, or in combination with other treatments [45]. In phase I/II clinical trials involving refractory or relapsed AML patients with ITD, TKD mutations, and both, gilteritinib at a dosage of 80 mg achieved desired tolerability and overall response rates of 55%, 17%, and 62%, respectively, regardless of prior treatment with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) [46].

Meiling et al. assessed the possible synergistic benefits of metformin (MET) and Gilteritinib (GLT) in the treatment of TKI-resistant AML in vivo using NRGS mice (NOD/RAG1/2−/−IL2Rγ−/−Tg [CMV-IL3, CSF2, KITLG]) xenografted with MOLM13-RES cells. According to the Chou-Talalay [47] Method, FLT3/ITD and MOLM13-RES cells exhibited synergy when their Combination Index (CI) was less than 1, while FLT3/WT cells displayed antagonism (CI >1). Combo1 (GLT 5 nM + MET 5 mM) matched the effects of GLT 10 nM on cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, whereas Combo2 (GLT 10 nM + MET 10 mM) enhanced these effects. Also, through bioluminescence imaging and flow cytometry, the study demonstrated that while individual treatments reduced the leukemia burden, their combination produced a synergistic effect that led to a significant decrease in AML burden. Notably, 90% of the mice receiving the combination treatment survived for more than 120 days, and their leukemia burden remained low, highlighting this combination’s synergistic effect and its promising therapeutic potential for treating FLT3-mutated AML, even in the presence of TKI resistance. In contrast, treatment with either MET or GLT alone resulted in Approximately doubled of overall survival compared to vehicle treatment (56–62 days versus 26 days) [47].

Metformin dramatically increases the sensitivity of FLT3-ITD AML cells to gilteritinib, even those resistant to TKIs, according to another research by Meiling et al. All of the mice in the combinational treatment group survived for more than 70 days with little leukemia burden in FLT3-ITD AML mouse models. This is in sharp contrast to the significantly shorter median survival times in the control (21 days), metformin (46 days), and gilteritinib (52 days) groups. Low-dose metformin and gilteritinib significantly inhibited leukemia progression and extended survival time. Mechanistically, the combination treatment simultaneously inhibited the expression of polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1) and the phosphorylation of FLT3, STAT5, ERK, and mTOR. Although the median survival in the vehicle-, metformin-, and gilteritinib-treated mice was 26, 56, and 62 days, respectively, the combinational treatment demonstrated much more significant therapeutic efficacy, with approximately 90% of the Combo-treated mice surviving over 120 days with minimal leukemia burden. moreover, clinical studies verified that FLT3-ITD AML patients who took metformin had higher survival rates [48] (Fig. 3).

The mechanism of action of the combination of metformin and gilteritinib

In conclusion, the research findings suggest a potential synergistic strategy for treating FLT3-mutant AML that combines GLT and MET. This combination not only provides a viable treatment option for patients with FLT3-mutated AML but also presents an affordable alternative, particularly for individuals with limited financial resources.

Metformin and sorafenib

The FDA has authorized sorafenib [DB00398, Nexavar; Bayer], a first-generation inhibitor of type II FLT3, for clinical use in thyroid, hepatocellular, and renal cell carcinoma. Additionally, it inhibits RAF, vascular endothelial growth factor receptors (VEGFR) -1, -2, and − 3, platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR)-β, KIT, and RET [49]. A subset of individuals with AML has demonstrated encouraging therapeutic success with sorafenib, a kinase inhibitor. Nevertheless, resistance to sorafenib is commonly observed in clinical settings, despite its initial effectiveness. The mechanisms underlying this resistance remain unclear, as do practical methods to overcome it. Research has shown that combining sorafenib with various medications, including metformin, can be an effective therapeutic strategy for AML [50, 51].

In a study by Wang et al. metformin possesses anti-leukemic effects by activating phosphorylated AMP-activated protein kinase (p-AMPK) and synergistically sensitizing FLT3 mutant AML to sorafenib. While sorafenib alone does not significantly alter the autophagy marker light chain 3 (LC3), metformin increases LC3 levels, and the combination of the two drugs further elevates LC3 expression. Both inhibit cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner and induce apoptosis through cell cycle arrest. When metformin and sorafenib were administered together for 48 h, they demonstrated a synergistic effect on apoptosis induction in MV4-11 cells (M4 + S1: 49.95% ± 5.02; M4 + S2: 70.11% ± 8.77%), but did not affect THP-1 cells. Mechanistically, metformin enhances the synergistic anticancer potential of sorafenib by dramatically reducing the protein expression of the mTOR/p70S6K/4EBP1 pathway and elevating LC3 levels without altering the cell cycle. In this study, primary MV4-11 and FLT3-ITD cells exhibited a synergistic effect on cell survival when treated with the combination of metformin and sorafenib for 72 h, as indicated by the combination index (CI) values_ [52].

Furthermore, Yang et al.‘s study showed that in AML patients with a Flt3-ITD mutation, metformin can improve the anti-leukemia effectiveness of the Flt3 inhibitor sorafenib. The mTOR pathway is inhibited to provide this result [53].

According to the work by Ju et al., leukemia cell lines with FLT3-ITD overexpression also showed enhanced lactate generation and glucose absorption. In this case, the commonly used glycolytic inhibitor 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG) increased the anti-leukemia effects of sorafenib. Interestingly, 2-DG plus sorafenib demonstrated a substantial therapeutic effect over sorafenib alone, even while 2-DG alone did not seem to significantly lengthen median (16 days) life when compared to the untreated control (15 days) (2-DG plus sorafenib, 41 days; sorafenib, 32 days; P < 0.0001). From 15 days in the control group to 41 days, the combination significantly increased the median survival by 173% (P < 0.0001). These results imply that in addition to traditional chemotherapy, individuals with FLT3-ITD mutations may benefit from a combination of metabolic therapies, such as the prescription of glucose-lowering drugs like metformin [54].

While there have been unfortunately few investigations into this combination of drugs, overall tests and study results suggest that the combination of metformin and sorafenib may be a viable and appealing therapeutic option for AML with FLT3-ITD mutations.

Metformin and 6-benzylthionosine

As determined by immunophenotyping, morphology, and nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) depletion assays, 6-benzylthionosine (6-BT) is a nucleoside analog that induces monocytic differentiation in various AML cell lines and primary samples from leukemia patients. Consequently, 6-BT can promote the terminal differentiation of leukemic cells. Furthermore, it has been shown that combination therapy of 6-BT with other medications, including metformin, can enhance their efficacy [55].

A study by Sabnis et al. demonstrated that treatment with metformin alone, at tested concentrations and for 48 h, significantly reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) and mitochondrial respiration, enhanced glycolysis, and produced moderate cytotoxicity ranging from 10% to 25% in AML cell lines. In contrast, 6-benzylthioinosine (6-BT) monotherapy induced minimal changes in ROS and mitochondrial respiration but suppressed glucose uptake, decreased glycolysis, and caused ATP depletion. Metformin toxicity alone ranged from 9.8% to 42.7% under these conditions. Importantly, the combination of metformin and 6-BT resulted in substantial cytotoxicity, between 60% and 70%, particularly in monocytic AML cell lines such as MV4-11 and MOLM-14 at 48 h, correlating with suppression of FLT3-ITD-activated STAT5 and reduced expression of c-Myc and GLUT-1 [56] (Fig. 4).

The mechanism of action of the combination of metformin and 6-BT

In a subsequent study by Sabnis et al. the combination treatment at tested doses and timepoints, especially 48 h, led to mild cytotoxicity (10–40%, SD ± 6.3%) across all AML cell lines tested, with less than threefold reduction in cell growth overall. However, in monocytic AML cell lines MV4-11 and MOLM-14, this combination substantially inhibited proliferation by 30- to 50-fold and induced marked cytotoxicity reaching 90–100% (SD ± 8.3%) at 48 h. The combination significantly decreased intracellular ATP levels by 50–70% without notably lowering extracellular glucose, suggesting inhibition of glycolysis. As expected, metformin robustly inhibited oxidative phosphorylation, nearly completely suppressing oxygen consumption rate (OCR) [43].

Consequently, these data indicate that the novel combination of 6-BT and metformin targets this metabolic bypass pathway, leading to restricted glycolysis, increased cell death, and suppression of STAT5. 6-BT is specifically absorbed and phosphorylated by AML cells, ultimately inhibiting glycolysis and inducing cytotoxicity.

Metformin and venetoclax

Adults with AML could be treated with the chemotherapeutic drug Venetoclax, which is marketed under the brand names Venclexta. Venetoclax targets B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2) protein which is expressed at elevated levels in AML cancer cells. This protein plays a crucial role in cell survival and its overexpression contributes to increased risk of treatment resistance in various lymphoid malignancies [57, 58]. Venetoclax block the cancer cell growth by inducing apoptosis through binding to Bcl-2 [51]. As a BH3-mimetic, Venetoclax promotes programmed cell death in AML cells by inhibiting the anti-apoptotic effects of the Bcl-2 protein.

Hua L et al. conducted a study investigating the effects of venetoclax and metformin on AML cell proliferation both in vitro and in vivo. They found that in the Molm13 and THP-1 cell lines, treatment with the combination of venetoclax and metformin significantly reduced leukemia cell growth and induced apoptosis. This effect was accompanied by a marked increase in the expression levels of CHOP, a marker related to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. Importantly, when CHOP was eliminated, the apoptosis induced by the combination treatment was significantly reduced, indicating that the ER stress-CHOP pathway plays a critical role in mediating this apoptotic response. Additionally, the combination therapy demonstrated noteworthy anti-leukemic effects in bone marrow samples obtained from AML patients as well as in xenograft animal models. Overall, this study suggests that the combination of metformin and venetoclax improves anti-leukemic efficacy while maintaining a manageable toxicity profile, proposing a promising therapeutic approach that warrants further clinical investigation in AML treatment [59].

Krastinaite et al. investigated the effects of the combination of metformin and venetoclax on primitive and undifferentiated AML cells to evaluate the potential of this combination as an adjuvant therapy for AML. Their findings revealed while metformin alone affects the number of cells in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle, the combination of metformin and venetoclax results in a more pronounced accumulation of cells in the G2/M phase. This indicates that the combined treatment has a stronger inhibitory effect on cell proliferation compared to either agent alone. Additionally, venetoclax treatment was shown to reduce the clustering tendency of CD34 + KG1a cells, a CD34 + AML cell line, in vitro. Notably, a low-intensity venetoclax regimen demonstrated limited efficacy in refractory or relapsed (R/R) AML, yielding a response rate of 21% and a median survival of 3 months. Moreover, metformin significantly increased the expression of cell surface markers CD9, CD31, and CD105, whereas venetoclax alone increased the expression of HLA-DR and other cell surface markers. These modifications were associated with decreased expression of leukemic stem cell markers, including CD34 and CD44, as well as adhesion molecules. Further, the proliferation and metabolic activity of chemo-resistant CD34 + AML cells were effectively inhibited by combining metformin with a low dose (5 nM) of venetoclax, with higher doses producing comparable effects.The marker changes observed—such as increased expression of CD9, CD31, and CD105, along with decreased levels of CD34, CD44, and HLA-DR—indicate that the cancer cells become less stem-like and less adhesive. This means the cells exhibit reduced characteristics associated with cancer stem cells, which are often responsible for treatment resistance and relapse, and they also show diminished ability to stick to their environment, which can reduce their capacity for invasion and metastasis [60].

Another study by Zhou et al. demonstrated that the combined administration of metformin and venetoclax produced a synergistic increase in apoptosis compared to either treatment alone in both in vitro and in vivo models. This combination exhibited a strong anti-leukemia effect. Notably, venetoclax alone rapidly increased the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Mcl-1 shortly after treatment. In contrast, both the combined treatment of metformin and venetoclax and metformin monotherapy significantly decreased Mcl-1 levels. Additionally, metformin reduced the production of another anti-apoptotic protein, BCL-xL, which was downregulated during the treatments [11].

According to Cao et al., a lower dosage of venetoclax (5 nM) combined with metformin effectively restricts the proliferation and reduces the metabolic activity of CD34 + chemoresistant AML cells. Similar inhibitory effects were observed with higher doses of venetoclax. Their study found that the combination of metformin and venetoclax enhances pro-apoptotic gene expression more effectively than venetoclax alone. Cell cycle analysis after venetoclax treatment revealed an increase in the percentage of cells in the G0/G1 phase, associated with growth inhibition and the activation of apoptosis. Conversely, the combination of venetoclax and metformin results in a combination index (CI) less than 1, indicating synergism, and promotes cell accumulation in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle [56].

To enhance the effectiveness of venetoclax in conjunction with Mcl-1 inhibitors, several studies have focused on combining Bcl-2 mimetics with specific Mcl-1 mimetics. Although these approaches have shown some success, there are significant concerns regarding their potential adverse effects. Interestingly, when metformin is administered alongside venetoclax, it appears to reduce the compensatory overexpression of Mcl-1 and BCL-xL without causing any negative side effects.

Metformin and cytarabine

Cytarabine is a chemotherapeutic drug in the protocol of AML treatment which also known as cytosine arabinoside. Ara-C impedes DNA synthesis by rapidly converting to cytosine arabinoside triphosphate, which disrupts DNA synthesis during the S phase of the cell cycle. So that dividing Cells are most affected, since they require DNA replication to proceed through mitosis. furthermore, cytarabine inhibits the enzymes necessary for DNA synthesis, including ribonucleotide reductase as well as DNA and RNA polymerases. Moreover, Cytarabine was the first in a series of cancer medications that modify the sugar component of nucleosides [61, 62].

In the study by Yuan F et al., the anti-leukemic efficacy of metformin in combination with Ara-C was evaluated using the CCK-8 and apoptosis assays. The mechanisms underlying the combined effects of Ara-C and metformin in AML were also investigated. By inhibiting the mTORC1/P70S6K pathway, metformin has been shown to synergistically sensitize AML cells to Ara-C. Metformin’s assistance for Ara-C, a synergistic anticancer action, was further validated through in vivo testing. Ara-C/metformin therapy demonstrated a stronger anti-proliferation effect on HL-60 and THP-1 cells than either Ara-C or metformin by itself. The combination index (CI) value for the Ara-C/metformin co-therapy was computed using CompuSyn software. According to the findings, CI < 1 was seen in HL-60 cells when Fa (cell proliferation-inhibition rate) >30% and in THP-1 cells when Fa >15%, respectively. Ara-C and metformin demonstrated a positive synergistic effect in AML cells. The synergistic anti-tumor effect was more effective after two AML cells’ total concentrations of Ara-C and metformin increased [63].

Vitkevičienė A et al. demonstrated that co-treatment with cytarabine and metformin reduced oxidative phosphorylation in resistant AML cells and marginally enhanced the treatment’s efficacy. Furthermore, a gene expression study utilizing reverse transcription revealed that cells with higher initial oxidative phosphorylation rates exhibited a slightly greater impact on cell proliferation and cell cycle regulation [64].

In summary, the cytarabine/metformin combination shows a pro-apoptotic synergistic effect on AML cells. Additionally, the results indicate that the Ara-C/metformin combination demonstrates a significant antitumor effect in vivo when compared to Ara-C alone.

Combination therapy of metformin with anti-inflammatory drugs

Combination of metformin with diclofenac and diflunisal

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as diclofenac, are commonly used to treat inflammatory conditions like gout and pain. Diclofenac can be administered through various routes including topical application, oral intake, intramuscular injection, intravenous injection, or as a rectal suppository [57, 58]. This drug plays an effective role in reducing oxidative damage and alleviating inflammation caused by radiation in healthy tissues, especially within the circulatory system. Therefore, diclofenac may have two distinct effects: sensitizing tumor cells to radiation while simultaneously protecting blood vessels from damage [59, 60]. Diflunisal, a derivative of salicylic acid, possesses both anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties [61]. Like other NSAIDs, diflunisal acts by inhibiting the production of prostaglandins, which are hormones associated with pain and inflammation. Additionally, diflunisal can inhibit epigenetic regulators such as CREB-binding protein (CBP) and p300, which modulate proteins involved in cell growth and inflammation [62, 63].

In a study by Renner et al., the effects of metformin on AML cell lines and blasts from primary patients were examined both alone and in combination with the two NSAIDs diclofenac and diflunisal. In three AML cell lines (THP-1, U937, and KG-1), diclofenac reduced lactate production, while diflunisal did not; at low concentrations, both drugs enhanced cellular respiration. Despite these metabolic effects, both NSAIDs had limited effects on tumor cell survival and proliferation up to a dose of 0.2 mM. Diflunisal alone significantly inhibited the growth of AML cell lines and respiration at moderate to high concentrations ranging from 0.4 to 0.8 mM. Metformin, an antidiabetic drug, inhibited mitochondrial respiration but had no effect on AML cell proliferation or survival when used alone. In contrast, the combination of all three drugs produced potent cytotoxic and cytostatic effects on THP-1 cells, substantially reducing their proliferation. Moreover, NSAIDs promoted apoptosis in primary leukemia blasts. When low-dose chemotherapy with cytarabine was combined with low doses of metformin and the two NSAIDs, diclofenac and diflunisal, a synergistic inhibitory effect on AML blast proliferation was observed, likely mediated through obstruction of tumor cell metabolism [65].

Furthermore, primary cells derived from the bone marrow of AML patients and AML cell lines exhibited functional responses to NSAIDs. This response was mediated via transcriptional activation of the GADD45a gene and members of the AP-1 transcription factor family, including c-Jun, JunB, and Fra-2, which contribute to their antiproliferative effects. Activation of GADD45a increases JNK activity, subsequently triggering a caspase-3-dependent apoptotic pathway. GADD45a and AP-1 are critical because they serve as “cell death switches” or “genes that trigger cancer cell self-destruction.” Once activated, these genes initiate cellular pathways leading to programmed cell death (apoptosis), aiding in the elimination of cancer cells. Specifically, GADD45a responds to cellular stress and DNA damage by activating apoptotic pathways, while AP-1 regulates genes related to cell death and survival. Their activation promotes cancer cell suicide and is a key target for therapies aiming to halt tumor growth through inducing cancer cellself-destruction.

However, few studies have investigated the combination of NSAIDs with metformin, and more clinical trials are needed to confirm the safety and efficacy of these combinations. While the initial laboratory results are promising, there is a lack of sufficient data from human studies.

Conclusion

Metformin, a widely utilized antidiabetic medication, has exhibited promising benefits for a range of blood disorders. It exhibits minimal toxicity, rendering it a suitable option for AML treatment especially in elderly patients or those resistant to chemotherapy. Furthermore, metformin can be readily combined with other chemotherapeutic agents, allowing for lower dosages and a reduction in adverse effects. A number of studies indicate that the combination of metformin with chemotherapeutic drugs, including tyrosine kinase inhibitors, FLT3 inhibitors, and nucleoside analogs may yield significant antitumor effects. In conclusion, this narrative review emphasizes the therapeutic promise of combination methods based on metformin in AML, especially when used with anti-inflammatory drugs and chemotherapy. The review is constrained by its narrative nature, the absence of a systematic approach even if the data points to encouraging synergistic effects. To confirm these interactions and identify the best combination regimens for AML patients, future research should concentrate on well-planned preclinical and clinical investigations.
