Surgical Timing and Rehabilitation (STaR) for Multiple Ligament Knee Injuries
Authors: James J. Irrgang, Andrew D. Lynch, Charity G. Patterson, Alexandra B. Gil, Travis C. Burns, Megan Dalzell, Al M.J. Getgood, Christopher D. Harner, Bruce A. Levy, Brett D. Owens, Kathleen M. Poploski, Dustin L. Richter, Robert C. Schenck, Brian R. Waterman, Daniel B. Whelan, Volker Musahl, Robert Arciero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultiple ligament knee injuries (MLKIs) often result in persistent pain, residual disability including post-traumatic osteoarthritis, and high healthcare costs. Although nonrandomized and retrospective research supports operative management, evidence informing the optimal timing of MLKI surgery and subsequent rehabilitation is lacking. In addition, return to preinjury military duty, work, and sports after MLKI is poorly understood. The Surgical Timing and Rehabilitation (STaR) Trial for MLKIs was designed to address these gaps in evidence for surgery and postoperative rehabilitation for military personnel and civilians that have a MLKI. The purpose of this protocol paper is to describe the rationale and methodology for the first randomized trials to investigate the impact of timing of surgery and rehabilitation for individuals with a MLKI on time to return to preinjury levels of military duty, work, and sports. The outcomes of the STaR Trial for MLKIs are of paramount importance from the personal and societal perspective of the impact of MLKIs. The optimal time for surgery and timing of postoperative rehabilitation may also reduce residual knee impairments and complication rates that contribute to the development of long-term disability after MLKI.

Introduction

Background

Multiple ligament knee injuries (MLKIs), including knee dislocations, can negatively affect quality of life, resulting in substantial time away from military duty, work, and sports1-4. Nearly 1 in 5 individuals who sustain a MLKI will be unable to return to work5-7, and over half of military personnel will be unable to return to military duty1,2. For athletes, return to sports rates are near 50%5, of which as few as 20% can return to their preinjury level1,8-10. Even with treatment, MLKIs often lead to complications, including limited range of motion (ROM), residual laxity, and the development of post-traumatic knee osteoarthritis (PTOA)11-15.

The heterogeneity and low incidence of MLKIs contribute to the lack of high-level evidence regarding optimal treatment strategies16. While research supports operative management5,6,17, evidence informing the optimal timing of MLKI surgery is lacking and predominantly based on nonrandomized and retrospective studies17-25. Similarly, the optimal timing of rehabilitation after MLKI surgery has not been adequately investigated.

To address these gaps in evidence, we designed the Surgical Timing and Rehabilitation (STaR) Trial, a multicenter clinical trial to investigate the effects of the timing of surgery and postoperative rehabilitation on outcomes in military personnel and civilians aged 16 to 55 years with a MLKI. Primary outcomes include time to return to preinjury military duty, work, or sport, as well as patient-reported physical function, assessed between 6 and 24 months after randomization. The purpose of this report was to describe the clinical protocol for the STaR Trial.

Methods

Overview

The STaR Trial comprised 2 parallel, unblinded, phase 3 randomized clinical trials. Trial 1 uses a 2 × 2 factorial design to examine the effects of early vs. delayed surgery combined with early vs. delayed postoperative rehabilitation. Trial 2 uses a parallel-group design to evaluate early vs. delayed postoperative rehabilitation alone. Both trials were conducted in accordance with Consolidated Statement of Reporting Trials recommendations26.

The STaR Trial comprised 32 clinical sites across the United States (military and civilian) and Canada (civilian).

Participants

We plan to recruit a total of 690 individuals aged 16 to 55 years with a MLKI (complete grade III injury of 2+ ligaments) with no prior knee ligament surgery. Trial 1 will enroll 392 participants, and Trial 2 will enroll 298 participants. To be eligible for Trial 1, individuals must present for treatment at a time that would permit “early” surgery within 6 weeks of injury. Individuals presenting at a time that precludes early surgery, with an injury that dictates timing of surgery, or those declining surgical randomization are eligible for Trial 2. For both trials, individuals with meniscus injuries and/or cartilage and nerve injuries were not excluded. While these concomitant injuries may affect outcomes, randomization should result in a relatively equal distribution across treatment groups. In addition, these concomitant injuries and associated treatment were thoroughly documented, allowing for secondary analyses to explore the outcomes of these effects. Screening and Baseline Assessment

Potentially eligible patients are identified by the surgeon investigator. Following informed consent and confirmation of eligibility, participants complete surveys to document preinjury activity levels. Military personnel indicate their military duty, including physical demand classification, by completing the Military Occupational Specialty classification27 and Injury Surveillance Survey (ISS). All participants indicate work activity through the Cincinnati Occupational Rating Scale (CORS)28 and employment status/occupation survey, as well as their sports activity and participation through the Marx Activity Rating Scale29 and type and frequency of sports participation survey (Table II).

Randomization and Blinding

Participants in Trial 1 are randomized to both timing of surgery and rehabilitation before surgery, while participants in Trial 2 are randomized to timing of rehabilitation after surgery. The rehabilitation assignment is not disclosed to the surgeon or participant until after surgery in both trials. Randomization was generated by the study biostatistician using permuted blocks with random block sizes stratified by site and knee dislocation (KD) injury pattern in SAS version 9.4.

Injury pattern was classified by the Schenck KD classification, an anatomically based KD classification system (Table III)44,45. The randomization list was uploaded into the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) system46 randomization module for allocation concealment.

Interventions

Early vs. Delayed Surgery (Trial 1)

Early surgery is defined as surgical treatment of the MLKI within 6 weeks of injury. Delayed surgery is defined as surgical treatment 12 to 16 weeks from injury. Given the complex, heterogeneous nature of MLKIs combined with over 60 surgeon investigators, it was impractical to explicitly specify surgical procedures and fixation methods for all combinations of MKLIs. Instead, surgery was performed in accordance with the principles of anatomic repair and/or reconstruction in a manner that allows for early ROM. The decision to repair or reconstruct the damaged ligaments, as well as graft choice, is at each surgeon’s discretion. To account for surgical variability, the approach, method of repair/reconstruction (including graft type, methods of fixation and use of synthetic ligament augmentation devices) are detailed on case report forms. Similarly, meniscus and cartilage surgery is performed as clinically indicated and documented on case report forms. Thromboembolic prophylaxis is at the discretion of the surgeon.

Early vs. Delayed Rehabilitation (Trials 1 and 2)

All participants are instructed to remain non–weight-bearing (NWB) with a knee brace locked in extension and to perform isometric quadriceps exercises and self-patellar mobilization for the first 7 to 10 days postsurgery before transitioning to their assigned rehabilitation allocation.

Early rehabilitation is defined as the transition to weight-bearing as tolerated (WBAT) with an assistive device and unrestricted ROM exercises 7 to 10 days postsurgery. Delayed rehabilitation is defined as remaining NWB with the brace locked in extension and no ROM exercises for a total of 4 weeks postsurgery. After 4 weeks, participants in the delayed rehabilitation group progress to WBAT and unrestricted ROM exercises.

All participants follow the same criterion-based rehabilitation progression with 3 phases—Tissue Protection, Restoration of Motor Control, and Functional Optimization47. If needed, additional tissue-specific rehabilitation modifications can be made to protect the surgical repair or reconstruction. The details of the postoperative rehabilitation program have been previously reported47.

Outcomes

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome is time to return to full, preinjury military duty, work, and/or sports. This is assessed through monthly questionnaires 6 to 24 months postrandomization. Civilians receive a “Full Return to Activity and Participation” designation if and when they return to full, unrestricted preinjury level of work and sports activity based on attainment of their preinjury CORS,28 and Marx Activity Rating Scale29 scores and return to the same preinjury type and frequency of sports. Military personnel achieve a “Full Return to Activity and Participation” designation by meeting the criteria outlined above for civilians and indicating a full, unrestricted return to pre-injury level of military duty as determined by their ability to pass an Annual Physical Fitness Test and are as deployable and mission capable as they were before injury (Rhon DI Personal Communication. 2016).

The Activity Limitations Scale of the MLQoL Questionnaire30 serves as a coprimary outcome and is assessed at 6, 12, and 24 months after randomization. Assuming there is no time by intervention group interaction, the primary focus will be the 24-month follow-up time point.

Secondary Outcomes

Secondary outcomes include additional patient-reported measures of physical function and quality of life, limited ROM, residual laxity, complications/adverse events, reinjury, and additional surgical procedures. Based on the IKDC criteria for assessment of outcome after knee ligament surgery, limited ROM was defined as a >5-degree side-to-side difference in extension and/or >15-degree side-to-side difference in flexion 3 or more months postsurgery48.

To assess secondary outcomes, knee-specific PROs and general health measures are assessed at 6, 12, and 24 months after randomization through surveys. In addition, clinical information is prospectively gathered during standard postsurgical follow-up visits 1, 3, 6, and between 9 to 12 months after surgery. This information includes pain, medication use, joint effusion, wound and neurovascular status, ROM, knee laxity, WB status, bracing, imaging, complications, adverse events, and additional surgeries.

Adverse Events Reporting

Participants are monitored for any notable health events, knee-related problems or additional surgeries that occur throughout their study participation. Adverse events are documented according to standard medical terminology49.

Data Management

The Data Coordinating Center (DCC) at the University of Pittsburgh oversees data management for the study using the REDCap platform. Study data are collected primarily via direct entry or electronic survey. The DCC is responsible for distributing the 6- to 24-month follow-up surveys for all US sites. Canadian sites distribute their own 6- to 24-month surveys before transferring deidentified data to the DCC for monitoring and analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses for both trials will follow the intention-to-treat principle44,50. We will begin the analyses by comparing the time to event curves using Kaplan-Meier estimation and log-rank tests for each trial. For Trial 1, we will assess the proportional hazards assumption for surgery (early vs. delayed) and postoperative rehabilitation (early vs. delayed). If the assumption is not violated, we will proceed using Cox proportional hazards model adjusting for site and KD injury pattern. Although not powered for detecting an interaction, we will test the interaction between timing of surgery and timing of rehabilitation before examining the main effects. We will present results using hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals. If the proportional hazards assumption is violated, we will extend the Cox model to incorporate the group*log(t) term. For Trial 2, we will follow the same strategy to compare early vs. delayed rehabilitation.

The MLQoL Activity Limitations Scale will be analyzed using linear mixed models accounting for repeated measurements with fixed effects for surgery (early vs. delayed), rehabilitation (early vs. delayed), time (baseline, 6, 12, 24 months), and the two-way and three-way interactions controlling for site and KD injury pattern. Assuming the three-way interaction is not significant, we will test the two-way interactions for surgery*time and rehabilitation*time. If significant, we will test treatment effects at 24 months (primary time point) using contrasts.

We will compare participants missing research follow-up on baseline characteristics. If any time point has more than 15% missingness, we will use pattern mixture models under nonignorable missingness to test the impact on the inference of our results.

For the 2 primary outcomes, we will not correct for multiplicity, and each outcome will be tested at the priori alpha of 0.05. In the case where one primary outcome but not the other is significant, we will declare efficaciousness only for that outcome. To conserve statistical power, no interim analyses of the primary aim are planned.

Power and Sample Size

Using the results of a chart review51, we projected to recruit 690 participants across 32 sites over 2 years. For Trial 1, n = 392 complete cases would provide 80%-92% power to detect a 15% absolute difference (α = 0.05) in the rate of return to full preinjury military duty, work, and sports for the main effects (n = 176 for each arm) for timing of surgery or rehabilitation, assuming the delayed arm return rate of 30%-70%, and 82% power to detect a hazard ratio = 0.65 using a log-rank test assuming 2-year accrual, 2-year follow-up for each participant, and 10% dropout and 5% crossover in each arm.

For the MLQoL Questionnaire Activity Limitations Scale, we will have 80% power to detect a 6.2 difference at 24 months for the main effects (2-sided, α = 0.05, standard deviation = 20.8) assuming a 10% attrition rate.

For Trial 2, n = 298 provides 79% to 84% power to detect an absolute difference of 15% between the groups (α = 0.05) assuming the delayed rehabilitation group has a rate of return to pre-injury activity level of 65%-70% and 10% attrition.

Study Oversight

Study oversight is led by the principal investigators and governed by an Executive Steering Committee and 6 subcommittees. In addition to the principal investigators and subcommittees, an independent Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB), the University of Pittsburgh IRB (Single IRB of Record), Canadian REBs, DoD HRPO and an independent research monitor provide safety and study oversight.

Discussion

By encompassing 2 parallel multicenter clinical trials, the STaR Trial will be the first to provide high-level evidence to guide surgical management and postoperative rehabilitation decision making for these often complex and devastating injuries.

About half of military personnel who sustain a MLKI are medically discharged1, suggesting that these injuries can significantly affect a service member’s health and career path while creating a considerable financial burden for the military. Even for those returning to duty, there is likely extensive time lost from work, training, and deployment. Furthermore, many individuals with a MLKI will develop PTOA12-15, which can shorten military career longevity and impact postmilitary quality of life. The results of this study will inform improved, evidence-based treatment strategies for MLKIs in military personnel.

Similarly, MLKIs substantially affect nonmilitary civilians and athletes. Return to work rates among civilians vary from 54% to 92%5,9, with some only able to achieve light duty activities52. Reported return to sports rates are even lower, ranging from 17 to 85%, with a mean of 50%5. The reported average time to return to sports is 5.5 months53 to 14.8 months3. Return to the same level of sports has been reported as low as 29% in high level athletes54. These findings underscore the tremendous personal and societal impact of MLKIs and the need for effective treatment strategies.

The STaR Trial will be the first study to provide Level I evidence on effects of timing of surgery and postoperative rehabilitation after MLKI, which will inform future decision-making for surgeons and rehabilitation specialists. The findings of the STaR trial may also create a framework to further evaluate and potentially mitigate postoperative complications including loss of motion, residual laxity, and PTOA.
