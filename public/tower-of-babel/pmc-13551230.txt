Introduction

Clinical prediction models (CPMs) estimate the probability of health outcomes for individual patients using statistical or machine learning methods.1 They are intended to support clinical decision-making by informing diagnosis, prognosis, treatment selection, and care planning. By providing individualised risk estimates, CPMs have considerable potential to support precision medicine through patient stratification and tailored clinical management.1,2 However, their uptake in routine practice remains limited. This implementation gap is partly attributable to concerns about methodological quality, including inadequate sample sizes, suboptimal predictor handling, insufficient validation, and incomplete reporting.3–5 Consequently, questions remain about whether many published CPMs are sufficiently reliable and reproducible for clinical use.

Traditionally, CPM evaluation has focused on measures of overall predictive performance, particularly discrimination and calibration.6 Although these measures remain essential, they do not fully describe whether a modelling approach produces reproducible individual-level predictions during model development.7,8 Prediction instability occurs when models developed using the same intended approach generate meaningfully different predictions across samples drawn from the same target population. This issue is particularly important for clinical decision-making because unstable individual risk estimates could lead to different decisions for similar patients.7,9 Prediction stability has therefore been increasingly recognised as a distinct and important property of CPM development, complementing conventional measures of model performance.

Several factors may influence the stability of model performance, including the size and heterogeneity of the development sample, the number of candidate predictors, predictor distributions, outcome prevalence, model complexity, and the modelling algorithm used.10–16 Recent work has formalised the assessment of instability of individual predictions and proposed bootstrap-based procedures for evaluating it during model development.7,9 However, evidence remains limited on how specific modelling decisions affect the stability of individual predictions. How continuous predictors are modelled is an important decision in clinical prediction model development.6 Because these predictors often carry substantial predictive information,17 approaches that discard information or differ in their ability to capture non-linear associations can affect model performance.18–21 These modelling choices may also influence prediction stability, particularly when development samples are small.

This study examined how methods for modelling continuous predictors influence prediction stability in clinical prediction model development, using real clinical data as an empirical basis. Rather than identifying a single optimal method, it described patterns in prediction stability across different representations of continuous predictors and varying development sample sizes. By considering simple and more complex approaches, the study aimed to clarify how these choices may affect the reproducibility of individual risk estimates under certain development conditions.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This empirical methodological study investigated how continuous predictor modelling methods influence the stability of individual predictions in clinical prediction model development. Seven methods were compared across five development sample sizes, yielding 35 method-by-size scenarios, each repeated 100 times using independent samples drawn from the full cohort. All methods were fitted to the same development samples, allowing comparisons.

Within each development sample, prediction stability was assessed using 200 bootstrap resamples, following the framework proposed by Riley and Collins.9 Stability reflected the variability of individual predicted probabilities under bootstrap perturbation of the modelling process and was the primary outcome; discrimination and calibration were assessed as supporting measures. Because a stability estimate from a single development sample is itself subject to sampling variability, results were summarised across the 100 repetitions of each scenario. Data Source

Rather than relying on simulated data, we used a real clinical dataset to provide empirical evidence under realistic development conditions. The dataset was obtained from a previously published clinical prediction model developed to predict hospital admission at triage in the emergency department of Lampang Hospital, Thailand, which included 19,418 emergency department patients.23 The original model was developed using logistic regression and incorporated predictors from multiple domains.

Candidate Predictors

Six continuous predictors were pre-selected from the original dataset: age, mean arterial pressure, pulse rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and body temperature. None of the six candidate predictors had missing values; therefore, no imputation was performed.

Scenario Analyses by Sample Size and Modelling Approaches

Scenario analyses were conducted to examine how sample size and continuous predictor modelling methods influenced prediction stability. The base sample size was defined using the minimum sample size approach proposed by Riley et al.24 For a binary prediction model with 12 parameters, assuming a C-statistic of 0.70 and an outcome prevalence of approximately 40%, the required sample size was 871 patients with 349 events, corresponding to approximately 29 events per parameter (EPP). The 12-parameter assumption, equivalent to a maximum of two parameters per predictor, ensured that the sample size was sufficient for modelling approaches requiring two degrees of freedom per continuous predictor, such as tertile categorisation, quadratic terms, or restricted cubic splines.

Five development sample size scenarios were then created by applying multiplicative factors of approximately 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 10 to the base sample size of 871. Each scenario was drawn as a fixed fraction of the source cohort, giving development samples of 437, 874, 1748, 3496, and 8739 patients. The 100 samples of each scenario were drawn at random and without replacement from the source cohort. Within every development sample, seven methods for modelling continuous predictors were compared: dichotomisation at the median (DIC); tertile categorisation using cut-points at the 33rd and 67th percentiles (TER); linear terms (LIN); linear and quadratic terms (QUA); restricted cubic splines (RCS);25 the multivariable fractional polynomial algorithm (MFP);25 and extreme gradient boosting (XGB) as a machine learning comparator. All approaches except XGB were fitted using logistic regression. Every approach was pre-specified in full, including the degrees of freedom per predictor, the parameter count, the elements left to the data, and the software used (Table 2). No hyperparameter was tuned for XGB, and no post hoc recalibration was applied. Cut-points, fractional polynomial powers, knot positions, and tree structures were re-derived within every bootstrap resample, so that variability introduced by selecting the functional form was included in the stability assessment.

Model Evaluation

Prediction stability was the primary focus, with discrimination and calibration assessed as supporting measures. Discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Calibration was assessed using calibration-in-the-large (CITL) and calibration slope.

Internal Validation

Internal validation was performed with 200 bootstrap resamples within each development sample. Resamples were the same size as the development sample, and the full modelling process was repeated within each, including the specified method for handling continuous predictors. Each bootstrap model was then evaluated in both the resample and the development sample to estimate optimism in AUC, CITL, and calibration slope. The mean optimism across resamples was subtracted from the apparent estimates to obtain optimism-corrected performance measures.26 A bootstrap resample contains approximately 63.2% of the distinct observations in the sample it is evaluated against. This correction may be unreliable for a learner such as boosting, which can fit individual observations closely. For this reason, out-of-bag (OOB) AUC and the .632+ estimator were also computed.27

Prediction Stability Assessment

Prediction stability was assessed using the same bootstrap resamples as the internal validation. The model developed in the development sample was used to estimate predicted probabilities for all individuals in that sample. In each bootstrap resample, a new model was developed using the same modelling method and applied to the development sample to generate bootstrap-based predicted probabilities.

For each individual, prediction instability was calculated as the absolute difference between the predicted probability from the bootstrap model and that from the development model. These absolute differences were averaged over the 200 bootstrap resamples to give the mean absolute prediction error (MAPE) for that individual, in percentage points of predicted probability. We also calculated the median MAPE across individuals and the proportion of individuals with MAPE ≤5%. A model was considered to give acceptably stable predictions when this proportion reached at least 90% within a single development sample; this 90% rule is referred to throughout as the stability criterion. In addition, optimism-corrected calibration slope and CITL were used as supporting measures, with values between 0.95 and 1.05 for calibration slope and between −0.05 and 0.05 for CITL considered acceptable. These thresholds were pre-specified as pragmatic reference criteria rather than formal universal standards. They were informed by previous relevant works and by common interpretative principles in clinical prediction modelling.9,28,29

Within a single development sample, the assessment described above estimates the instability of individual predictions from a model developed with a given method in a dataset of that size. This is the quantity the bootstrap framework9 was designed to deliver, and what an investigator with a single dataset can compute. Its value, however, depends on which patients happened to be sampled. Repeating the whole assessment on independently drawn development samples estimates the sampling distribution of that quantity. For every combination of sample size and modelling method, we therefore present the number of development samples in which the stability criterion was met. Each measure is also summarised by its mean across samples, with the 2.5th–97.5th percentile interval and the Monte Carlo standard error.22 The number of repetitions per scenario, R = 100, was fixed in advance, as we expected it to give sufficient Monte Carlo precision for these summaries at a feasible computational cost.

Software and Computational Resources

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Calibration measures were estimated using the CalibrationCurves package. Restricted cubic splines were implemented using the splines package, multivariable fractional polynomial modelling was implemented using the mfp package, and extreme gradient boosting was implemented using the xgboost package through the caret interface. Figures were created using the ggplot2 package. All model fitting was performed using the high-performance computing (HPC) resources of the Faculty of Medicine, Chiang Mai University, including the MedCMU-HPC-RAPTOR cluster. The analysis code that generates every result, table, and figure in this article is openly available at https://github.com/phichayutphinyo/constab/.

Results

Across the five development sample-size scenarios, the 100 development samples drawn from the full cohort included 437 to 8739 patients, with a mean of 176 to 3524 admission events. Outcome prevalence was similar across scenarios, from 0.403 to 0.405 (Supplementary Table S1). The number of parameters was fixed in advance for four of the seven approaches: six for DIC and LIN, and 12 for TER and QUA (Table 2). RCS was specified with 12 parameters and used 12 in all but one development sample, at n = 437, where two knot quantiles coincided and the affected predictor was fitted linearly under the pre-specified fallback. MFP selected between six and 12 parameters, and its mean rose from 6.47 at n = 437 to 10.41 at n = 8739 (Supplementary Table S2).

Baseline characteristics were broadly comparable across sample size scenarios (Supplementary Table S1), with similar marginal distributions of continuous predictors across the full cohort and the development samples (Supplementary Figure S1). The binned predictor–outcome relationships suggested that the main patterns were generally preserved across development samples, although the curves fluctuated more in the smaller scenarios (Supplementary Figure S2).

Prediction Stability Across Modelling Scenarios

Prediction stability increased with larger development sample sizes across all modelling methods (Figures 1 and 2). Median MAPE decreased in every scenario. LIN had the lowest median MAPE throughout, falling from 0.039 at n = 437 to 0.009 at n = 8739, and XGB the highest, falling from 0.077 to 0.020. The proportion of individuals with MAPE ≤5% moved in the same direction. At half the base sample size (n = 437), no method met the pre-specified stability criterion in any development sample. By four times the base size (n = 3496), every method met it in at least 91 of the 100 samples, and at ten times the base size all seven met it in all 100 development samples (Table 3).

Within each sample size scenario, stability differed by modelling method (Table 3 and Figure 2). LIN was the most stable approach at every sample size, and TER and XGB generally had the lowest mean proportions of individuals with MAPE ≤5%. At half the base sample size (n = 437), that proportion ranged from 0.106 for TER to 0.744 for LIN, and no method met the criterion in any development sample. At the base sample size (n = 874), LIN met the criterion in all 100 samples. DIC met it in 79, despite a mean proportion of 0.919, and no other approach met it in any sample. Doubling that size (n = 1748) brought QUA and RCS to all 100 samples and DIC to 99, whereas TER and MFP met the criterion in 37 and 30 samples and XGB in none. Every approach except TER met the criterion in all 100 samples by four times the base size (n = 3496), and TER only at ten times the base size (n = 8739).

Discriminative Performance

Discrimination was generally higher for approaches that kept the continuous predictors continuous than for the categorisation approaches, DIC and TER (Figure 3A). DIC and TER remained at approximately 0.69 in every scenario and did not improve as the development sample size increased. The other regression approaches ranged from approximately 0.71 to 0.73, and QUA, RCS, and MFP were consistently above LIN from twice the base sample size (n = 1748) onwards.

XGB had the highest optimism-corrected AUC in every scenario, falling from 0.769 at half to 0.736 at ten times the base size. The other two estimators did not follow this pattern. Out-of-bag AUC for XGB rose over the same range, from 0.689 to 0.733, and the .632+ estimate was almost flat, from 0.730 to 0.739. The margin between XGB and the best regression approach likewise depended on the estimator: at half the base sample size it was 0.054 under optimism correction and 0.016 under .632+, whereas out-of-bag XGB fell 0.014 below. At ten times the base size the three estimates for XGB lay within 0.006 of one another (Supplementary Table S4). Optimism-corrected AUCs for every approach and scenario are given in Supplementary Table S3.

Model Calibration

In the smallest sample-size scenario, all six regression approaches had calibration slopes below one, ranging from 0.798 for QUA to 0.913 for LIN (Figure 3B). The slopes generally approached one as the development sample size increased. Among these approaches, DIC and LIN remained closest to one and entered the pre-specified acceptable range at the base sample size (n = 874), followed by RCS at twice the base size (n = 1748), and TER, QUA, and MFP at four times the base size (n = 3496). In contrast, XGB showed the opposite pattern, with its calibration slope increasing from 0.947 at n = 437 to 1.157 at n = 8739 and exceeding one at larger sample sizes. Optimism-corrected CITL remained close to zero across all scenarios, with mean values ranging from −0.003 to 0.017, and was within the pre-specified acceptable range in all 35 scenarios (Supplementary Figure S3). Detailed calibration results are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Variation Between Development Samples

All stability values described above are means across the 100 development samples, but between-sample variability differed substantially across methods. At twice the base sample size (n = 1748), median MAPE ranged from 0.019 for LIN to 0.041 for XGB, whereas the stability proportion varied much more widely. Across development samples, it ranged from 0.983 to 0.995 for LIN, 0.812 to 0.930 for MFP, and 0.629 to 0.957 for TER (Table 3). Variability was generally greatest for approaches in which cut-points or functional forms were estimated from the data. Consequently, mean stability proportions and the number of samples meeting the criterion sometimes gave different impressions. At n = 1748, the mean proportions for TER and MFP were below the criterion, although 37 and 30 of the 100 samples, respectively, met it. Conversely, at the base sample size (n = 874), DIC had a mean proportion of 0.919, yet only 79 samples met the criterion. Model complexity also varied across samples: at n = 1748, MFP used six to 10 parameters (median seven), whereas DIC and LIN consistently used six and TER and QUA used 12 (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

As expected, prediction stability improved as the development sample size increased. At half the base size (n = 437), derived from the minimum sample size approach proposed by Riley et al,24 no method met the stability criterion in any of the 100 repetitions. By four times the base size (n = 3496), all methods met the criterion in at least 91 of 100 repetitions. This is consistent with previous evidence that models developed in smaller datasets are more susceptible to instability, optimism, and variability in individual risk estimates. However, methods differed substantially within the same sample-size scenario. Linear terms (LIN) met the criterion in every repetition from the base size onwards, whereas tertile categorisation (TER) did so only at ten times the base size. Thus, the base sample size appeared sufficient for level-2 prediction stability only when continuous predictors were modelled linearly,9 indicating that sample size alone does not determine prediction stability.

Methods that retained continuous predictors on their original scale generally achieved a better balance between prediction stability and predictive performance than categorisation. Although dichotomisation (DIC) met the stability criterion in 79 of 100 repetitions at the base sample size, it had lower discrimination than methods using continuous terms, with an optimism-corrected AUC of approximately 0.69 that did not improve as the development sample size increased. Tertile categorisation (TER) performed less favourably, particularly in smaller datasets, despite having the same nominal number of parameters as quadratic modelling and restricted cubic splines. It was also the only method that did not meet the criterion in every repetition until ten times the base size (n = 8739). These findings are consistent with previous concerns that categorising continuous predictors can reduce information, lower statistical efficiency, and introduce instability through cut-point selection.18,19,30 Therefore, although dichotomisation appeared stable when the sample size was adequate, this should not be taken as evidence that continuous predictors should routinely be categorised.

Among the regression-based approaches, linear modelling (LIN) showed the most consistent stability, meeting the criterion in every repetition from the base sample size onwards and maintaining acceptable or near-acceptable calibration across most scenarios. Quadratic modelling (QUA) and restricted cubic splines (RCS) appeared to require approximately twice the base sample size to achieve comparable stability. At larger sample sizes, both showed slightly higher discrimination than LIN, with optimism-corrected AUCs of 0.727 and 0.728, respectively, at ten times the base size (n = 8739), compared with 0.719 for LIN. Their similar behaviour across scenarios may reflect their comparable complexity, as both used two degrees of freedom per predictor with the functional form specified in advance. These findings suggest that LIN may be preferable when sample size is limited, whereas QUA and RCS may offer a reasonable balance between flexibility, discrimination, and stability in moderately larger datasets.

More flexible approaches required larger development samples to achieve stable individual predictions. At twice the base sample size, MFP met the stability criterion in only 30 of 100 repetitions, compared with all 100 for QUA and RCS, and required four times the base size to meet the criterion consistently. This occurred despite MFP using fewer parameters on average, suggesting that instability arose primarily from data-driven selection of functional forms and transformation powers rather than model size. Previous work has similarly shown that model-building procedures involving selection can be unstable in limited samples.12,13 Local fluctuations in apparent predictor–outcome relationships may have further amplified this instability in smaller samples, despite broadly preserved predictor distributions. Methods whose functional forms, cut-points, or fitted structures are data-driven may therefore be particularly sensitive to sampling variation.31

XGB had the highest optimism-corrected AUC in every scenario, yet did not meet the stability criterion in any repetition until four times the base sample size. This apparent discrimination advantage should be interpreted cautiously because the internal validation estimators diverged for XGB, unlike for the regression approaches. A bootstrap resample contains approximately 63.2% unique observations from the development sample; thus, a flexible learner may be evaluated partly on observations it has already seen, leading optimism correction to underestimate optimism, particularly in smaller samples. At half the base size, for example, an apparent AUC of 0.872 was corrected to 0.769, compared with an out-of-bag estimate of 0.689. With out-of-bag and .632+ estimation, XGB had only a small advantage over the best regression approach across sample sizes, and no advantage at the three smallest sizes using out-of-bag estimation. For boosting, discrimination may therefore be better characterised by out-of-bag or .632+ estimates than by optimism-corrected estimates alone.

Comparisons of this kind are common in applied work, where a single split-sample estimate is often the basis for choosing between approaches. A recent tuberculosis contact-screening model, for example, reported areas under the curve of 0.91 to 0.92 for its best-performing learners against 0.76 for logistic regression, from 23 events among 1277 contacts.32 Our results do not bear on the validity of that model. They do indicate how much of such a margin can rest on the estimator rather than the method: for XGB at half the base sample size, the corrected and out-of-bag estimates differed by 0.080.

Calibration also raised concerns: the slope was near one at half and one times the base size (0.947 and 1.004, respectively) but increased steadily to 1.157 at ten times the base size, while its variability across repetitions narrowed. This systematic pattern suggests a feature of the modelling procedure rather than individual development samples and may reflect the fixed learning budget of 500 boosting rounds at a learning rate of 0.01. Overall, strong discrimination does not necessarily imply stable or well-calibrated individual risk estimates, and boosted models intended for individual risk prediction may require recalibration.33–35

These findings also inform how stability assessments should be interpreted. The bootstrap framework is conditional on the development sample by design, which is appropriate when assessing whether a model developed from the data at hand is reliable.9 However, it does not indicate whether the same conclusion would be reached in another sample from the same population. At moderate sample sizes, this was often not the case. For TER and MFP at twice the base size, the same pre-specified analysis met the criterion in only about one-third of repetitions, showing that a single development sample could plausibly yield either conclusion. Between-sample disagreement was greatest when mean stability was close to the criterion and when cut-points or functional forms were selected from the data, but became negligible once stability was clearly above the threshold. Thus, a within-sample result near the threshold may be better regarded as inconclusive, particularly for strategies involving data-driven selection. In such settings, larger development samples or pre-specified functional forms may provide a more reliable route to stable individual predictions than simply repeating the assessment.

Prediction stability was not determined solely by the number of events per parameter (EPP). Although EPP is commonly used to assess sample size adequacy, it does not account for differences in model form, information preservation, or modelling complexity. This was evident here: methods with the same nominal number of parameters, such as DIC and LIN or TER, QUA, and RCS, differed in both discrimination and stability. These findings support previous arguments that EPP rules are insufficient as a universal basis for sample size planning.24,36,37 The minimum sample size approach proposed by Riley et al24 may provide a more useful starting point, but meeting that minimum did not guarantee stable individual predictions. Apart from LIN, all methods required larger samples before meeting the stability criterion in every repetition. The minimum should therefore be viewed as a basis for planning rather than a guarantee of prediction stability.

The key strength of our study is that seven approaches to modelling continuous predictors were compared across increasing sample sizes within a real clinical dataset, with all approaches fitted to the same 100 independently drawn development samples. Stability was assessed at the level of individual predictions, and repeated sampling allowed results to reflect a distribution of possible outcomes rather than a single development sample. The study therefore provides practical evidence for considering prediction stability alongside conventional predictive performance when selecting how to model continuous predictors.

However, several limitations should be considered. Firstly, the study was based on a real clinical cohort that was resampled rather than simulated from a known data-generating mechanism. This preserved realistic predictor distributions, correlations, and predictor–outcome relationships, but the true functional forms were unknown. Bias could therefore not be assessed, and no modelling approach could be regarded as the correct representation. The use of a single emergency department cohort may also limit generalisability. Secondly, MAPE was calculated for the patients within each development sample, so the same individuals were not evaluated across all repetitions. The reproducibility assessed therefore relates to the study-level stability results rather than to any specific individual prediction. Thirdly, development samples were drawn without replacement from a cohort of 19,418 patients. At the largest sample size, independently drawn samples therefore overlapped, which may have reduced between-sample variability and slightly overstated stability at ten times the base size (n = 8739). Fourthly, only seven commonly used approaches were examined. XGB was fitted using a fixed pre-specified configuration rather than being tuned, so its results should not be interpreted as the best attainable performance of boosting. Other ensemble and boosting-based approaches to individual risk prediction were not examined and warrant further study. Bagged and histogram-based gradient boosting combined with feature selection, and hybrid ensembles that stack several learners, have both been reported to improve discrimination over single classifiers including logistic regression.38,39 Whether that advantage holds at the development sample sizes examined here, and whether it comes at a cost in prediction stability, would be a natural extension of this work. Our examined models were also relatively simple and included only continuous predictors. More complex scenarios involving categorical predictors, interaction terms, missing data, alternative outcome types, or regularisation methods were beyond the scope of this study, although some of these approaches may improve stability in certain settings.16,34 Fifthly, dichotomisation and tertile categorisation used data-driven cut-points, which may not reflect settings where clinically defined thresholds are available. Finally, prediction stability was assessed using bootstrap-based MAPE with pragmatic thresholds. Other aspects of stability, such as calibration and classification instability, were not evaluated.

Conclusion

The present empirical analysis suggests that the way continuous predictors are modelled can influence the stability of individual predictions. LIN reached the stability criterion consistently from the base sample size onwards, whereas QUA and RCS required larger development samples while maintaining a reasonable balance between stability and discrimination. More flexible approaches, including MFP and XGB, generally required even larger samples, and XGB continued to show calibration concerns despite higher discrimination. Categorisation offered no clear advantage, with TER requiring the largest sample size to achieve stability and both DIC and TER showing lower discrimination than methods that retained predictors as continuous. For small or moderate development samples, simpler modelling strategies, particularly LIN, may therefore be preferable when prediction stability is a priority. Because stability assessments from a single development sample are themselves variable, results near the acceptability threshold should be interpreted with caution. Further studies across different clinical settings, outcomes, and predictor structures are needed to confirm the generalisability of these findings.
