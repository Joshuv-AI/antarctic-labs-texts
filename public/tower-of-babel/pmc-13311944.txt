Addition of liposomal bupivacaine in brachial plexus blocks: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Chung Hin Shing, Fengfeng Wang, Pui Ming Lam, Omar Wai Kiu Tsui, Timmy Chi Wing Chan, Stanley Sau Ching Wong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdding liposomal bupivacaine to brachial plexus block may improve postoperative analgesia after upper limb surgery.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the postoperative analgesic efficacy of adding liposomal bupivacaine to brachial plexus block compared with non-liposomal local anesthetic.Evidence reviewA systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, ClinicalTrials.gov, and the Cochrane Library up to June 30, 2024. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that evaluated the postoperative pain scores in patients who received liposomal bupivacaine via brachial plexus block were identified. The primary outcome was resting pain scores (Numerical Rating Scale, 0–10) at 24 hours after nerve block injection derived from the random effects model. A mean difference (MD) of 1/10 was considered to represent a minimal clinically important difference in pain scores. The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework was applied to examine the level of evidence.Findings21 RCTs (1478 participants) were included. Liposomal bupivacaine was associated with statistically significant reduction in resting pain scores at 24 hours after nerve block injection compared with non-liposomal local anesthetic (MD=–1.04, 95% CI=–1.54 to –0.53, p<0.001, 95% prediction interval=–3.05 to 0.98). This was associated with significant heterogeneity (Q=107, p<0.001, I²=81%, tau2=1.0, 95% CI=0.42 to 2.07) and a low-GRADE rating. Liposomal bupivacaine was also associated with a statistically significant reduction in resting pain scores at 48 hours and 72 hours. Opioid consumption at 0–24 hours, 25–48 hours and 49–72 hours was lower in the liposomal bupivacaine group with statistical significance (p values <0.05). Liposomal bupivacaine was associated with a lower incidence of nausea.ConclusionsThere is low-level evidence that liposomal bupivacaine in brachial plexus block may reduce pain intensity after upper limb surgery, but the clinical significance may be limited.Keywords: Meta-Analysis; Anesthesia, Regional; Peripheral Nerves; Brachial Plexus; Nerve Block

Introduction

Regional nerve blocks are an integral component of multimodal analgesia, and brachial plexus blocks are commonly used to provide analgesia and/or surgical anesthesia for upper limb surgery.1 2 They have been shown to improve postoperative pain control for upper limb surgery compared with general anesthesia.3 4 However, rebound pain occurring after peripheral nerve blocks, including brachial plexus blocks, is a significant barrier to achieving good postoperative pain control.5 The analgesic duration of conventional local anesthetics administered via peripheral nerve block does not last more than 24 hours, even when combined with perineural adjuncts such as dexamethasone.6 7 Poorly controlled postoperative pain can delay recovery, decrease patient satisfaction, and prolong length of hospital stay.8

Liposomal bupivacaine is an extended-release formulation of bupivacaine designed to extend the duration of analgesia.9 10 Some randomized controlled trials have demonstrated improved analgesia with the addition of liposomal bupivacaine to brachial plexus blocks for upper limb surgery, but the overall evidence remains unclear.11,13 To the best of our knowledge, there are currently no published systematic reviews/meta-analyses that specifically assess the analgesic efficacy of liposomal bupivacaine in the brachial plexus block. Two previous meta-analyses evaluating the use of perineural liposomal bupivacaine across a variety of surgeries and different nerve blocks showed statistically significant improvement in postoperative pain scores.14 15 However, it was concluded that these differences were not clinically significant.14 15 Another two meta-analyses for shoulder surgery mostly included clinical trials that administered liposomal bupivacaine via local infiltration, rather than specifically evaluating its effect via the brachial plexus block.16 17 The previous meta-analyses did not evaluate dynamic pain scores as well as a number of other pain-related outcomes such as the time to first analgesic request, overall benefit of analgesia score (OBAS), and patient satisfaction. Furthermore, a number of clinical trials on this topic have been completed since the publication of previous meta-analyses.12 18 19

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials to examine the analgesic efficacy of liposomal bupivacaine in brachial plexus block compared with non-liposomal local anesthetics. Our primary outcome was the resting pain scores at 24 hours after nerve block. We chose this time point as the primary outcome because this corresponds to the usual time of rebound pain after brachial plexus blocks.20 21 Secondary outcomes included resting and dynamic pain scores at other time points during the acute postoperative period, postoperative opioid use, the (OBAS, patient satisfaction, time to first analgesic request, length of hospital stay, and incidence of adverse effects.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024559405) and reported according to the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement, as well as the journal guidelines.22 23 The protocol was not published in a peer-reviewed journal. Post hoc changes to this protocol are shown in online supplemental file 1.

Study eligibility

Randomized controlled trials were included if they compared perineural liposomal bupivacaine versus single or continuous infusion of non-liposomal local anesthetics in patients receiving brachial plexus blocks for upper limb surgery in a hospital, with postoperative pain intensity as a study outcome. The studies were included where randomization was implemented either at the individual level or at the group level. Both published and unpublished studies were eligible. For unpublished studies, only clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov were included. Observational studies, preclinical animal trials, narrative review articles, qualitative studies, case reports, abstracts from conference proceedings, master’s theses or dissertations, incomplete studies, studies without available results, studies not reported in English, and studies on pediatric patients were excluded. Studies in which patients received local infiltration of liposomal bupivacaine or compared liposomal bupivacaine to local infiltration analgesia, placebo, or general anesthesia only were also excluded. The primary outcome was resting pain scores at 24 hours after nerve block. Other secondary outcomes included resting and dynamic pain scores at 48 and 72 hours after nerve block, postoperative opioid use, OBAS, patient satisfaction, time to first analgesic request, length of hospital stay, and incidence of adverse effects.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, ClinicalTrials.gov, and the Cochrane Library, which included the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) and Cochrane reviews, from inceptionto June 30, 2024. This search considered both published and unpublished studies to minimize publication bias.24 25 A search strategy was developed by the research team (online supplemental file 2). The title or abstract must include the following keywords: ‘liposomal bupivacaine’ or ‘Exparel’, ‘brachial plexus block’, ‘interscalene block’, ‘supraclavicular block’, ‘infraclavicular block’ or ‘axillary block’, as well as ‘clinical trials’ or ‘randomized’. The search strategy was developed by the research team, and the database search was separately conducted by two investigators (CHS and PML).

Study selection

Two investigators (CHS and PML) independently screened all included articles based on the title, abstract, and full text. Any differences in opinion were resolved through discussion. If a mutual agreement could not be achieved, a third investigator (SSCW) was involved to make the final decision.

Data extraction

Two investigators (CHS and PML) independently extracted data from eligible trials using a standardized data extraction tool. The extracted information included the year of publication, region of enrollment, types of surgery, anesthetic strategy, rescue opioids used, types and techniques of nerve blocks, regimens of nerve blocks, sample size, and study outcomes for both the intervention and control groups. If an article included more study arms, data were extracted only from the relevant arms. Both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses were accepted. If an article contained both types of analyses, intention-to-treat results were selected to maintain the integrity of randomization.26 Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. If mutual agreement could not be achieved, a third investigator (SSCW) was involved to make the final decision. If the mean or SD was not reported in the article, we estimated them from available summary statistics (eg, t-value, median, and IQR) according to recommendations from the Cochrane Handbook and Wan et al.27 28 If estimation was not possible, we contacted the corresponding author twice over 4 weeks to obtain the necessary data.

The following instruments for outcome measurement were considered eligible for assessing pain intensity: Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), Visual Analog Scale (VAS), and other relevant outcome measurement tools. Morphine milligram equivalent (MME) dosage was used for the assessment of interval opioid consumption. The dosage of a specific opioid was transformed into MME dosage using a conversion factor.29 The OBAS, a scale that assesses the composite of opioid symptom distress, pain relief, and patient satisfaction (where a lower score reflects greater effectiveness), was considered eligible for evaluating the benefit of analgesia.30 Rating scales such as the VAS, NRS, and Likert scale, in which higher scores reflect greater satisfaction, were considered eligible for assessing patient satisfaction. Time to the first rescue analgesic request and length of stay in the hospital, as reported in hours, were included. Adverse effects related to opioid use, such as nausea and dizziness, as well as adverse effects related to brachial plexus blocks, such as Horner’s syndrome and hoarseness (recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy), were also documented.31 32

Risk of bias assessment and certainty of evidence

The Revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) was applied to assess the risk of bias of included trials separately for each outcome.33 Compared with the original risk-of-bias tool, RoB 2 allows for a more robust and specific assessment of bias arising from different processes within a trial.34 The tool encompasses five domains of potential bias, including the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Each domain was separately rated as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk” of bias based on Cochrane guidelines and reviewer judgment. An overall low risk of bias was assigned if all five domains were rated as low risk. Alternatively, if at least one domain received a high-risk rating, the overall risk of bias was deemed high. Otherwise, the overall risk of bias was rated as having some concerns.

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework was applied to examine the level of evidence for each meta-analysis outcome.35 The level of evidence was classified into high, moderate, low, or very low certainty, according to the assessment of the following five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias. The evidence level was graded based on the instructions (online supplemental file 3).

Two reviewers (CHS and FW) separately conducted the risk of bias and GRADE assessments for each outcome. Differences in opinion were solved through discussion. If a mutual agreement could not be achieved, a third investigator (SSCW) was involved to make the final decision.

Outcome synthesis and analysis

The primary outcome of this study was the resting pain scores at 24 hours after surgery. Secondary analgesic outcomes included resting pain scores at 48 hours and 72 hours, as well as dynamic pain scores at 24 hours, 48 hours and 72 hours after surgery. We also assessed interval opioid consumption at 0–24 hours, 25–48 hours and 49–72 hours after surgery; the OBAS and patient satisfaction with analgesia at 24 hours, 48 hours and 72 hours after surgery; time to first analgesic request; and length of hospital stay. Additionally, opioid-related and brachial plexus block-related adverse effects were assessed.

Raw mean difference (MD) was used to assess effect size for the study outcomes. Pain scores were converted to a standardized scale ranging from 0 to 10, with 0 representing no pain and 10 representing the worst possible pain. An MD of 1/10 in pain score was considered a minimal clinically important difference (MCID).36 For opioid consumption, MD in MME was reported. MD in OBAS score was measured by the original scale. Patient satisfaction with analgesia was converted to a standardized scale (0–10). For time to analgesic request and length of hospital stay, MD in hours was reported. The OR was used to compare the incidence of adverse effects between the intervention and control groups. A meta-analysis was performed if there were data from at least three comparisons. The restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method combined with the Knapp-Hartung adjustment for the random effects model was selected for estimating the MD and OR.37 This method was recommended over other random effects models due to its superior performance when there was significant inconsistency or a limited number of studies.37 Additionally, a 95% prediction interval was provided, demonstrating the range within which the true effect size of a future study is expected to fall.38 Heterogeneity was evaluated using the Q statistic, I², and tau2 as statistical measures. A multivariate meta-regression analysis was conducted to investigate the relationship between multiple covariates and the effect size, using a mixed-effects model with the REML method. The two covariates—types of nerve block and use of adjunct—were determined a priori based on available evidence.7 39 The Instrument to assess the Credibility of Effect Modification Analyses (ICEMAN) guideline was used to assess the credibility of subgroup differences.40 The Doi plot and Luis Furuya-Kanamori (LFK) index were used to assess for small-study effects (ie, publication bias).41

The leave-one-out (LOO) sensitivity analysis was performed for the primary outcome to assess the robustness of the meta-analysis results. This method systematically removed one study at a time and recalculated the overall effect size with a 95% CI for each iteration to test whether the removal of any single study significantly impacted the overall findings. Additionally, an influence analysis was performed to identify studies that had a disproportionately large impact on the outcome. This included metrics such as Cook’s Distance, DFBETAS, hat values, and covariance ratios (COVRATIO). These metrics could identify studies with high influence or leverage affecting the overall findings. A sensitivity analysis for outliers was further conducted by excluding studies whose 95% CIs for effect sizes did not overlap with the overall 95% CI.

The “metafor” package from R V.4.4.0 was used to perform all the analyses (2024, URL https://www.r-project.org). A two-sided p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

375 records were identified for the overall assessment, with 373 from databases and 2 from other resources (figure 1). After removing duplicates, 274 records were screened based on their titles and abstracts. 49 articles were assessed for eligibility through a review of the full text, resulting in 28 articles being excluded (online supplemental file 4). A total of 21 trials were included in this study.11,1318 19 42 Additional information on the study methodology and outcomes was obtained from the authors of an unpublished trial.56 Two included articles presented multiple relevant comparisons,42 57 leading to a total of 23 comparisons consisting of 1478 participants.

Study characteristics

The characteristics for all the included 21 studies are summarized (online supplemental file 5). All included trials randomized participants at the individual level. Intention-to-treat analysis was performed in 8 trials,1213 19 48 53 55,57 and per-protocol analysis was performed in 13 trials.1118 42,47 49 None of the studies reported results for both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses. 18 trials were conducted in the USA,1318 19 42,55 57 2 trials in Hong Kong,12 56 and 1 trial in Belgium.11 Two studies received industrial funding.11 43 10 trials included patients who underwent total shoulder arthroplasty,1819 43 48 49 51,54 57 5 trials on rotator cuff repair,42 44 46 50 55 4 trials on non-specified shoulder surgery,11 13 45 47 and 2 trials on fracture fixation.12 56 Interscalene brachial plexus block was performed in 19 trials,1113 18 19 42,45 47 while superior trunk block46 and supraclavicular brachial plexus block12 were each performed in one trial. Nerve blocks were ultrasound-guided and performed before surgical incision in all clinical trials. Long-acting local anesthetics were used for control in all the trials, with plain bupivacaine in 17 trials11,1319 42 44 47 and ropivacaine in 4 trials.18 43 45 46 Pain intensity was measured using the NRS in 17 trials11,1319 44 and the VAS in 4 trials.18 42 43 48

All but one comparison used 133 mg of perineural liposomal bupivacaine,11,1318 19 42 while the remaining comparison used a smaller dosage of 88 mg.55 Liposomal bupivacaine was used in combination with plain bupivacaine in 20 comparisons.11,1318 19 42 44 Liposomal bupivacaine was compared with a single-shot nerve block in 17 comparisons11,1319 42 44 46 and to continuous nerve block in 6 comparisons.18 43 45 53 56 57 Perineural adjuvant was included in the control groups for seven comparisons.13 19 42 46 54 57 Dexamethasone was the only adjuvant used in five of these comparisons.13 42 46 57

Meta-analysis for the primary outcome: resting pain score at 24 hours

A total of 21 comparisons consisting of 1404 patients reported resting pain scores at 24 hours after nerve block.11,1318 19 42 Overall analysis revealed a statistically significant reduction in pain scores for liposomal bupivacaine compared with non-liposomal local anesthetics (MD=–1.04, 95% CI=–1.54 to –0.53, p<0.001) (figure 2A). The MD marginally exceeded the predefined MCID. The 95% prediction interval was –3.19 to 1.12. Seven comparisons had a low risk of bias, nine had some concerns, and another five had a high risk of bias (online supplemental file 6). Significant heterogeneity was found for the pooled results (Q=107, p<0.001, I²=81%, tau2=1.0, 95% CI=0.42 to 2.07). A significant small-study effect was detected, as indicated by the Doi plot and LFK test (figure 2B). The GRADE evidence level was graded according to the provided instructions (online supplemental file 3). The pooled result was associated with a low-GRADE rating (table 1 and online supplemental file 6).

Sensitivity analysis

The LOO sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the overall effect size estimates, as they remained consistent despite the sequential removal of each study. The effect sizes varied between –0.84 and –1.11, and the 95% CIs for these estimates consistently did not include zero. This consistency suggests that excluding any single study would not meaningfully impact the overall findings (online supplemental file 7). The influence analysis revealed that no individual study disproportionately affected the overall results. Although one study displayed higher influence metrics,46 its removal did not significantly change the overall findings of the meta-analysis. Further examination through influence plots confirmed that no studies exerted an undue amount of leverage on the overall outcomes (online supplemental file 8).

Among the included studies, four exhibited 95% CIs for effect sizes that did not overlap with the overall 95% CI.18 46 48 53 A sensitivity analysis for outliers was conducted by excluding these four studies. Liposomal bupivacaine was associated with statistically significantly lower resting pain scores at 24 hours (MD=–0.88, 95% CI=–1.23 to –0.52, p<0.001, 95% prediction interval=–1.94 to 0.18). This MD did not reach the threshold of the predefined MCID. The pooled results demonstrated significant heterogeneity (Q=28, p=0.035, I²=45%, tau2=0.22, 95% CI=0 to 0.70). No significant small-study effect was observed, as confirmed by the Doi plot and LFK test. The pooled result was associated with a moderate-GRADE rating (table 1 and online supplemental file 6). Given the difference in reaching the MCID threshold, these four outlier trials were excluded for subsequent subgroup analyses.

Meta-analysis for subgroups

Meta-regression was performed to compare the reduction in pain scores across different types of nerve blocks and the effect with and without adjuncts. A statistically significant difference was found for the effect with and without adjuncts (online supplemental file 9); however, it was rated as having low credibility of effect modification according to the ICEMAN (online supplemental file 9).40 No significant difference was found for the effect between single and continuous nerve block, and this was rated as having very low credibility of effect modification according to ICEMAN. Subgroup analysis was further conducted where there were enough studies (n≥3) to assess the impact of different factors on pain scores.58

There were 12 comparisons where adjuncts were not used,1112 18 43,45 47 and 5 comparisons where dexamethasone alone was used as an adjunct13 42 46 57 in the control group. In the subgroup meta-analysis versus non-liposomal local anesthetic without adjuncts, there was a statistically significant reduction in pain scores in the liposomal bupivacaine group (MD=–1.06, 95% CI=–1.48 to –0.64, p<0.001, 95% prediction interval=–2.21 to 0.09). This MD exceeded the threshold for MCID. Significant heterogeneity was found (Q=20, p=0.045, I²=46%, tau2=0.23, 95% CI=0 to 0.78), and the GRADE rating for this analysis was low (table 1 and online supplemental file 6). The subgroup meta-analysis that evaluated the use of dexamethasone as an adjunct to non-liposomal local anesthetic in the control group did not find a statistically significant difference in pain scores between the liposomal bupivacaine group and the control group (MD=–1.34, 95% CI=–3.43 to 0.75, p>0.05, 95% prediction interval=–7.01 to 4.33). This result was associated with significant heterogeneity (Q=46, p<0.001, I²=89%, tau2=2.61, 95% CI=0.72 to 21.87) and level of evidence was rated as low in the GRADE assessment (table 1 and online supplemental file 6).

13 comparisons studied single-shot nerve block,11,1319 42 44 47 49 and 4 comparisons studied continuous nerve block.43 45 56 57 Subgroup meta-analysis of comparisons on single-shot nerve block showed a statistically significant reduction in pain scores in the liposomal bupivacaine group compared with the control group (MD=–0.98, 95% CI=–1.42 to –0.53, p<0.001, 95% prediction interval=–2.26 to 0.30), but this difference did not reach the threshold of predefined MCID. The pooled result demonstrated significant heterogeneity (Q=26, p=0.012, I²=55%, tau2=0.30, 95% CI=0.02 to 1.09) and a moderate-GRADE rating (table 1 and online supplemental file 6). The subgroup meta-analysis on continuous nerve block did not find statistically significant difference in pain scores between the two groups (MD=–0.50, 95% CI=–1.01 to 0.02, p>0.05, 95% prediction interval=–1.20 to 0.20). The pooled result showed no significant heterogeneity (Q=0.81, p>0.05, I²=0%, tau2=0, 95% CI=0 to 0.88) and was associated with a very low GRADE rating (table 1 and online supplemental file 6).

Pain scores at other time points

Pooled analysis was performed for resting pain scores at 48 hours11,1318 19 42 44 46 and 72 hours11,1318 19 42 44 46 after nerve block. A statistically significant reduction in pain scores was observed at 48 hours in the liposomal bupivacaine group (MD=–1.12, 95% CI=–1.59 to –0.66, p<0.001, 95% prediction interval=–2.76 to 0.51) (online supplemental file 8). This MD exceeded the predefined MCID. Significant heterogeneity was found (Q=56, p<0.001, I²=70%, tau2=0.55, 95% CI=0.18 to 1.63), with a moderate-GRADE rating (table 2 and online supplemental file 6). A statistically significant reduction was found at 72 hours (MD=–0.79, 95% CI=–1.17 to –0.41, p<0.001, 95% prediction interval=–1.92 to 0.34) (online supplemental file 8). This MD was less than the predefined MCID. Significant heterogeneity was found (Q=32, p<0.001, I²=54%, tau2=0.25, 95% CI=0.03 to 0.99), and the GRADE rating was moderate (table 2 and online supplemental file 6).

Postoperative opioid consumption

Pooled analysis was performed for postoperative opioid consumption at 0–24 hours,11,1318 19 42 25–48 hours,11,1318 19 42 44 46 47 50 51 55 56 and 49–72 hours.11,1318 19 42 44 46 47 50 51 55 56 Postoperative opioid consumption was statistically significantly lower in the liposomal bupivacaine group at 0–24 hours (MD=–6.55, 95% CI=–12.21 to –0.88, p=0.027, 95% prediction interval=–28.84 to 15.74). This result was associated with significant heterogeneity (Q=488, p<0.001, I²=98%, tau2=99, 95% CI=47 to 238), and a low-GRADE rating (table 3 and online supplemental file 10). A statistically significant reduction in opioid consumption with liposomal bupivacaine was found at 25–48 hours (MD=–7.89, 95% CI=–12.54 to –3.23, p=0.003, 95% prediction interval=–25.50 to 9.73). Significant heterogeneity was found (Q=333, p<0.001, I²=97%, tau2=61, 95% CI=26 to 144), with a low-GRADE rating (table 3 and online supplemental file 10). A statistically significant reduction in opioid consumption with liposomal bupivacaine was also found at 49–72 hours (MD=–4.96, 95% CI=–8.17 to –1.75, p=0.005, 95% prediction interval=–16.03 to 6.12). Significant heterogeneity was found (Q=137, p<0.001, I²=95%, tau2=24, 95% CI=9 to 73), with a low-GRADE rating (table 3 and online supplemental file 10).

Overall benefit of analgesia

The OBAS score was evaluated at 24 hours, 48 hours, and 72 hours, with each having three comparisons.11 12 46 No statistically significant difference in OBAS was found at 24 hours (MD=–1.05, 95% CI=–2.76 to 0.65, p>0.05, 95% prediction interval=–6.98 to 4.88). No significant heterogeneity was found (Q=3, p<0.05, I²=11%, tau2=0.06, 95% CI=0 to 71), with a low-GRADE rating (online supplemental file 10). There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups at 48 hours (MD=–1.22, 95% CI=–3.87 to 1.42, p>0.05, 95% prediction interval=–12.69 to 10.24). No significant heterogeneity was found (Q=4, p>0.05, I²=49%, tau2=0.44, 95% CI=0 to 89), with a low-GRADE rating (online supplemental file 10). No statistically significant difference was found between the two groups at 72 hours (MD=–0.87, 95% CI=–2.90 to 1.16, p>0.05, 95% prediction interval=–8.49 to 6.75). No significant heterogeneity was found (Q=3, p>0.05, I²=20%, tau2=0.14, 95% CI=0 to 74), with a low-GRADE rating (online supplemental file 1).

Patient satisfaction with analgesia

Pooled analysis was performed for patient satisfaction with analgesia at 24 hours,12 13 56 48 hours12 13 54 56 and 72 hours12 13 47 56 after surgery. The results showed no statistically significant difference in patient satisfaction with analgesia at any of the evaluated time points (p values >0.05). Additionally, no significant heterogeneity was detected (p values >0.05). The GRADE rating was low for 24 hours and 48 hours and was moderate for 72 hours (online supplemental file 10).

Length of hospital stay

Five comparisons including 300 patients reported hospital length of stay.18 19 43 57 Liposomal bupivacaine was associated with statistically significantly shorter length of hospital stay (MD=–1.64, 95% CI=–3.09 to –0.20, p=0.034, 95% prediction interval=–3.30 to 0.01). No significant heterogeneity was detected (Q=2, p>0.05, I²=0%, tau2=0, 95% CI=0 to 68). The GRADE rating for the pooled result was high (online supplemental file 10).

Time to first analgesic request

Five comparisons (275 patients) reported the time to first analgesic request.11 19 55 57 The results showed no statistically significant difference between the two groups (p>0.05). No significant heterogeneity was detected (p>0.05). The GRADE rating was low (online supplemental file 10).

Adverse effects

A total of 17 comparisons consisting of 1103 patients reported adverse effects.11,1319 43 45 The analysis revealed a statistically significant reduction in the incidence of nausea (OR=0.54, 95% CI=0.37 to 0.78, p=0.012) in the liposomal bupivacaine group compared with the control group. However, there was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of dizziness (online supplemental file 11). Additionally, no statistically significant differences were found in the incidence of dyspnea, hoarseness, Horner’s syndrome, numbness, and rash (p values >0.05) (online supplemental file 11). Qualitatively, one study reported no significant difference in terms of vomiting incidence,50 and two other studies reported no difference in the incidence of pruritus between the two groups.12 56

Discussion

Overall findings

In this meta-analysis, we found that the use of liposomal bupivacaine in the brachial plexus blocks was associated with statistically significant reduction in pain scores at rest 24 hours after injection compared with non-liposomal local anesthetics. Furthermore, liposomal bupivacaine was associated with statistically significantly lower pain scores at 48 hours and 72 hours at rest, as well as lower dynamic pain scores at 48 hours after injection. Liposomal bupivacaine also resulted in a statistically significant reduction in opioid consumption at 0–24 hours, 25–48 hours, and 49–72 hours after surgery (less than 8 mg morphine equivalent daily). In addition, liposomal bupivacaine was associated with shorter length of hospital stay and less nausea. No significant differences were found for other secondary outcomes, including OBAS, patient satisfaction with analgesia, and time to the first analgesic request. Apart from nausea, no differences were reported for other adverse effects.

Implication for practice

The MD in resting pain score at 24 and 48 hours was greater than the predefined MCID of 1/10. However, analysis after removing four outlier clinical trials showed an MD of 0.88 for resting pain scores at 24 hours, which is lower than the predefined MCID. A difference of 1/10 appears to be a reasonable threshold for MCID and has also been used in previous meta-analyses of acute postoperative pain.14 15 Nonetheless, the difference in pain scores (eg, 0–10 scale) that represents clinical meaningfulness between treatment groups is not completely known.59 60 The interpretation of clinically significant differences depends on various factors, including the magnitude of difference in pain intensity, other important secondary outcomes, availability of alternative treatments, and adverse effects.59 In this study, liposomal bupivacaine was also associated with lower opioid consumption, but the MD was less than 8 mg of morphine daily. While the clinically meaningful difference in opioid consumption for upper limb surgery is not clear, the magnitude of this difference does not appear to be large. Although liposomal bupivacaine was associated with a small reduction in length of hospital stay and postoperative nausea, there was no difference for a number of secondary outcomes such as OBAS and patient satisfaction. Put together, these findings suggest that the analgesic benefit of liposomal bupivacaine appears to be small, and perhaps marginal in terms of clinical significance. The decision to use liposomal bupivacaine in brachial plexus blocks will likely depend on various factors, including the clinical situation, cost considerations/affordability by the patient, and the efficacy of alternative options. There may perhaps be more clinical benefit in using liposomal bupivacaine in patients at higher risk of experiencing severe postoperative pain.

Given the high cost of liposomal bupivacaine, it is essential to consider the impact on healthcare costs if it was to be widely applied for upper limb surgery. If liposomal bupivacaine was routinely used for brachial plexus block for shoulder surgeries (assuming around 500,000 cases annually in the USA),61 62 medication costs would surpass US$120 million per year (a dose of 133 mg (10 mL) liposomal bupivacaine is priced at around US$241).63 This would be much more expensive than single-shot injections using non-liposomal local anesthetic with or without adjuncts. Therefore, the routine use of liposomal bupivacaine in brachial plexus blocks in public hospitals is unlikely to be feasible. On the other hand, a previous study suggested that liposomal bupivacaine was more cost-effective compared with continuous interscalene brachial plexus block (US$373 vs US$706) by reducing anesthetic consultation fees and the cost of consumables.64 Continuous nerve blocks are also technically more demanding than single-shot injections and associated with complications.65 In our study, there was low-level evidence showing similar levels of analgesia between liposomal bupivacaine and continuous nerve block. Liposomal bupivacaine in the brachial plexus block may be preferable to continuous nerve blocks if postoperative pain control is comparable.

Implication for research

Our study is the first systematic review/meta-analysis to specifically compare liposomal bupivacaine versus non-liposomal local anesthetic in the brachial plexus block for upper limb surgery. Two previous meta-analyses compared both local infiltration and perineural liposomal bupivacaine to non-liposomal local anesthetic for shoulder surgery,16 17 while another two meta-analyses compared perineural liposomal bupivacaine to non-liposomal local anesthetics for various types of nerve and field blocks.15 The primary objective of these meta-analyses was different from our current study. Two previous meta-analyses showed no difference in analgesia between liposomal bupivacaine versus non-liposomal local anesthetics for shoulder surgery.16 17 Unlike our meta-analyses, which only evaluated the use of liposomal bupivacaine in the brachial plexus block, the majority of the clinical studies in the two meta-analyses looked at local infiltration of liposomal bupivacaine rather than administration via the brachial plexus block.16 17 Evidence has shown that local infiltration of liposomal bupivacaine does not improve analgesic outcomes compared with non-liposomal local anesthetics.66 Since the majority of clinical studies were local infiltration of liposomal bupivacaine, it is not unexpected that the previous meta-analyses found no statistical difference in analgesia,16 17 while our study found a statistically significant difference in favor of liposomal bupivacaine. Subgroup analysis specifically for interscalene brachial plexus block showed statistically significant pain score reduction with liposomal bupivacaine in both meta-analyses.16 17 Another difference between our meta-analyses was that the two previous meta-analyses did not include clinical studies on non-shoulder surgeries.12 Furthermore, our meta-analysis included unpublished trials to reduce publication bias,52,57 whereas the two previous meta-analyses did not include unpublished trials.15 16 For efficacy outcomes, our study explored both resting and dynamic pain. Furthermore, our study included a variety of secondary efficacy outcomes, such as opioid consumption, OBAS, patient satisfaction, time to first analgesic request, and length of hospital stay. In contrast, the previous meta-analyses only explored opioid consumption15,17 and hospital length of stay.15 For adverse events, our study comprehensively reviewed the incidence of both opioid-related and nerve block-related adverse events. Previous meta-analyses either did not distinguish between specific adverse events16 17 or focused solely on opioid-related adverse events.15 Methodologically speaking, our meta-analysis aligns with the latest PRISMA and Cochrane risk of bias guidelines. We included a list of excluded studies in the supplemental file and utilized the up-to-date Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool for assessment. In comparison, none of the previous meta-analyses included a list of excluded studies and used the older version for risk of bias assessment. It is essential for future clinical trials and meta-analyses on postoperative pain management to report a comprehensive set of analgesia-related postoperative outcomes while adhering to the most up-to-date guidelines in designing, conducting, and reporting.

Strength and limitations

One strength of this study is that it included data for both resting and dynamic pain scores, providing a more comprehensive evaluation of pain intensity. Dynamic pain scores are important for upper limb surgeries because good control of movement-induced pain facilitates early mobilization and rehabilitation, which are essential for functional recovery.67 A comprehensive set of analgesia-related outcomes was assessed in addition to the primary outcome to provide holistic evaluation of clinical efficacy. This systematic review and meta-analysis has several limitations. First, significant heterogeneity existed in a number of outcomes. This could be attributed to the fact that different clinical trials encompass different patient populations and distinct circumstances. Factors such as trial design, surgical treatment, and analgesic management can all contribute to the observed heterogeneity.68 69 Second, most of the brachial plexus blocks were performed using the interscalene approach, likely because it is the only approach approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). This may impact the generalizability of our findings for other types of brachial plexus blocks. Third, it should be noted that covariates were not randomized across studies, and these should be tested in the original randomized controlled trials. Fourth, given that this was an aggregate data meta-analysis, there is a risk of ecological fallacy. Fifth, since it is not uncommon to add adjuncts like dexamethasone to local anesthetics, the marginal effect size from studies that excluded adjuncts may be less relevant. Finally, this meta-analysis included only clinical trials that were published in English. Consequently, the findings may not fully represent the global body of research on this topic.

Conclusions

There is low-level evidence to show that liposomal bupivacaine provided a marginally clinically significant reduction (MD=1.04) in resting pain scores at 24 hours after surgery. However, clinical significance was no longer present after excluding four outlier clinical trials. Liposomal bupivacaine was also associated with statistically significant reduction in resting pain scores at 48 and 72 hours and postoperative opioid consumption, but the effect size was limited. Overall, our results suggest that there may be a small analgesic benefit with using liposomal bupivacaine in the brachial plexus block compared with non-liposomal local anesthetic.
