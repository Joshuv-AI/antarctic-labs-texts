TDiR: Transformer based Diffusion for Image Restoration Tasks
Abbas Anwar, Mohammad Shullar, Ali Arshad Nasir, Mudassir Masood, Saeed Anwar

Abstract
Images captured in challenging environments often experience various forms of degradation, including noise, color cast, blur, and light scattering. These effects significantly reduce image quality, hindering their applicability in downstream tasks such as object detection, mapping, and classification. Our transformer-based diffusion model was developed to address image restoration tasks, aiming to improve the quality of degraded images. This model was evaluated against existing deep learning methodologies across multiple quality metrics for underwater image enhancement, denoising, and deraining on publicly available datasets. Our findings demonstrate that the diffusion model, combined with transformers, surpasses current methods in performance. The results of our model highlight the efficacy of diffusion models and transformers in improving the quality of degraded images, consequently expanding their utility in downstream tasks that require high-fidelity visual data.

1
Introduction

Image restoration is a fundamental problem in computer vision and image processing, focusing on recovering an original, clean image from a corrupted or degraded version. Corruption can arise from various sources, such as noise, blur, and compression artifacts. Image restoration enhances quality in diverse applications, including medical imaging, satellite imaging, surveillance, and photography. Restoration techniques enable better interpretation, analysis, and decision-making across various fields by reconstructing compromised images.

In medical imaging, for example, the precision and clarity of images are vital for accurate diagnosis and treatment planning. Noise or artifacts in medical images can obscure critical details, potentially leading to misdiagnoses or overlooked conditions. Similarly, restoring clear and precise pictures in satellite imaging is essential for monitoring environmental changes, managing natural disasters, and supporting agricultural practices. High-quality restored images provide more reliable data for researchers and policymakers, facilitating informed decisions based on accurate visual information.

Furthermore, in surveillance and security, image restoration enhances the ability to identify and analyze objects, faces, and activities captured by cameras, particularly in low-light conditions or adverse weather. This capability is crucial for ensuring public safety and effective law enforcement. In the field of photography, both amateur and professional photographers benefit from image restoration techniques that can salvage photos marred by motion blur or low light, preserving valuable memories and artistic works. Overall, the advancement of image restoration methodologies continues to expand the potential and reliability of visual data across a wide range of critical applications.

In this work, we implement a transformer-based U-Net architecture within a diffusion model as a denoiser. This approach is applied to several challenging image restoration tasks, including denoising, deraining, and underwater image reconstruction. Our method demonstrates substantial improvements in image quality, showcasing its potential to advance the field of image restoration.

Literature Review

Among the various image restoration tasks, denoising is a crucial process that aims to reduce or eliminate noise in images. Noise can originate from low-light conditions or errors during image transmission and often appears as graininess, speckles, or random pixel variations that obscure critical visual details. Denoising enhances image clarity and quality while preserving essential features, such as edges and textures. Ren
et al.

Ren et al. 2021
proposed a prior-based deep neural network that consistently adapts to different noise levels. In
Zhang et al. 2017a

et al.

Zhang et al. 2017b

Zhang et al. 2021
applied a residual learning approach with a deep neural network to effectively extract hierarchical features. Zamir
et al.

Zamir et al. 2020
investigated the use of generative models and presented a cycle network for the image denoising task.

Another significant problem in restoration is the image deraining problem, which involves removing rain streaks or raindrops from pictures captured in outdoor environments. Rain significantly degrades visibility and image quality, creating noise-like streaks and reducing contrast. These effects make it challenging to identify or analyze objects accurately, particularly in fields such as autonomous driving and outdoor surveillance. Deraining aims to enhance image clarity by separating and removing rain artifacts while maintaining the integrity of the underlying scene. This task becomes challenging due to the varying shapes, sizes, and intensities of raindrops and streaks.

In
Jose Valanarasu et al. 2022
, the authors introduced a transformer-based architecture reconstructing images disturbed by a rainy effect. A multi-head-tail architecture capable of accomplishing tasks like deraining and denoising effectively is introduced by Chen
et al.

Chen et al. 2021

Li et al. 2020
presents an architectural search approach, particularly for adverse weather conditions like deraining, eliminating the need for prior knowledge. The impressive results in
Li et al. 2020
motivated several works aiming at an inclusive system with higher scalability, where several degradations can be addressed without explicitly notifying the model about the specific type it is dealing with. The authors in
Zhu et al. 2020
developed a complex network composed of three layers to comprehend the rainy effect in rainy images, as well as another layer that guides the learning process. Unsupervised learning also had its share in the literature. Yu
et al.

Yu et al. 2021
tried to combine model-driven and data-driven approaches for deraining tasks. Their proposed method incorporates unsupervised learning with an optimized deep CNN in a bridge-like architecture.

Another image restoration task is underwater image reconstruction. Underwater images are crucial for human exploration and the development of underwater applications. Underwater images are typically affected by light scattering, absorption by suspended particles, and color shift. These effects undermine applications that process underwater images, such as mapping underwater environments, recognition, and detection. Thus, there is a need for underwater image reconstruction methods that retrieve the underwater scene and remove all artifacts introduced by the water.

Underwater image restoration or reconstruction is usually used to refer to the color cast correction problem. This problem has been investigated more than other types of degradation. It can be approached as an inverse problem, where degradation models of the underwater environment are constructed, and the parameters of those models are estimated and then used to obtain higher-quality images. The most commonly used model is the underwater image formation model (IFM). Such models, especially complex ones, require specialized equipment to measure specific parameters. Researchers typically use priors to estimate model parameters and restore degraded images

Drews Jr et al. 2013
. However, using priors for parameter estimation limits such reconstruction techniques to cases where these priors hold. For example, in the case of the dark channel prior, results were inferior under non-uniform light.

Deep learning-based techniques require large datasets and can be applied without the need for handcrafted priors. Using CNNs showed impressive results for the UIR task

However, using those architectures is still not fully addressed for low-level vision tasks such as dehazing, deblurring, denoising, and underwater reconstruction

In the context of computer vision, non-generative methods have demonstrated notable performance in addressing a variety of high-level vision tasks. Nonetheless, their effectiveness in addressing challenges associated with image reconstruction, particularly underwater image reconstruction, does not align with their success in high-level vision tasks. Low-level vision tasks encompass intricate types of image degradation that can significantly hinder these models’ capacity to attain accurate and high-quality visual restoration. The degradation observed in underwater imagery exhibits considerable variability due to lighting conditions, water clarity, and suspended particles, resulting in a scenario wherein a single clear image may plausibly originate from numerous degraded sources.

Generative models are particularly effective in this context due to their ability to generate images from inputs that have undergone substantial degradation. Their success primarily arises from their capability to navigate multimodal distributions. This proficiency is imperative for image reconstruction tasks, where the desired outcome may correspond to multiple potential degraded states. Implementations of these methods have yielded promising results
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
. Diffusion models
Ho et al. 2020
;
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
;
Lu et al. 2023
, a subclass of generative models, have demonstrated encouraging outcomes in image generation tasks. In contrast to traditional approaches, which may fail to fully capture the diversity of degradation modes present in underwater imagery, diffusion models excel at navigating these complex, multimodal distributions. This allows for a more nuanced restoration of underwater images that closely reflects the real-world variability of these challenging environments.

3
Methodology

Diffusion Models convert standard Gaussian distribution samples into empirical data distribution samples through a recurrent denoising process. Conditional variants of these models condition the denoising process based on the input, thereby ensuring that the output does not emerge as a randomly sampled image from the learned distribution. Instead, depending on the specific task, it yields a transformed version of the input, typically an un-degraded restored image.

Diffusion Models consist of a forward diffusion process and a backward denoising stage. The forward operation progressively incorporates Gaussian noise into the input

In the backward denoising process, a denoising network learns to move from the Gaussian distribution to the empirical distribution of the dataset on which it is being trained.

Architectures based upon the attention mechanism, such as Transformers, have attained state-of-the-art results across various natural language processing tasks. Given that the transformer architecture eschews recurrence, it displays computational efficiency and scalability. Nevertheless, in relation to the UIR problem and computer vision in general, convolutional neural network architectures continue to be the preferred option. Preliminary investigations into the transformer architecture within computer vision initially endeavored to integrate hybrid CNN-self-attention architectures, while alternative approaches contemplated the complete removal of convolutions. Nonetheless, these models either exhibited poor scalability or failed to demonstrate efficacy comparable to that of CNNs.

In
Potlapalli et al. 2023
, the authors introduce PromptIR, a Transformer-based U-Net architecture designed for image restoration. The model demonstrates encouraging outcomes in addressing various degradation types without the necessity for distinct models for each instance. It utilizes prompt modules to dynamically adjust to different degradations and attain favorable results in denoising, deraining, and dehazing tasks. In this study, the model used in the PromptIR paper is employed as the denoiser network during the denoising process.

4.1
Training Setting

Since the transformer is reported to be data-hungry, which is typically attributed to the lack of biases inherent in convolutional neural networks, this issue requires a substantial amount of data to learn from. A pre-trained encoder serves as the backbone of the model. Therefore, only part of the decoder is trained in this work, while the encoder parameters remain constant and unchanged. The PromptIR model consists of four levels, with prompt blocks inserted after each decoder stage, leading to three prompt blocks. The baseline is trained with a batch size of 32, and the model was trained on an
\ell_{1}
loss with an Adam optimizer.

4.2
Evaluation Metrics

This study uses four performance metrics to evaluate the quality of the output images generated by the model: two full-reference metrics and two no-reference metrics. For the full-reference measures, we used PSNR and SSIM, while the Underwater Colour Image Quality Evaluation (UCIQE)
Yang and Sowmya 2015
and the Underwater Image Quality Measure (UIQM)
Panetta et al. 2016
are utilized for assessing the model on no-reference datasets. The UCIQE metric extracts the most pertinent statistical features from the CIELab color space that represent underwater image degradations, including color shift and noise induced by suspended particles. In contrast, the UIQM is a linear superposition of three components: colorfulness, sharpness, and contrast. These two metrics are frequently referenced in the literature for evaluating underwater reconstruction methodologies.

4.3
Datasets

In this study, three distinct image restoration tasks are examined, and for each task, various datasets are employed to train and evaluate the effectiveness of the model. The aforementioned datasets are detailed in the list below:

Underwater Reconstruction
. As for the underwater image reconstruction task, the Underwater Image Enhancement Benchmark (UIEB)
Li et al. 2020
dataset is used to train the model. The UIEB contains 890 underwater images with their corresponding
“reference”
images. The reference images were constructed using the following methodology: For each underwater image, several proposed reference images are produced using different underwater image reconstruction methods. Then, some subjects were asked to choose the best image from the proposed images that better captured the underwater scene while removing the color shift effects. While training on this dataset may mean that the model is only as good as the techniques used to produce the references in the UIEB, it is used here because multiple works have utilized and published models trained on it.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we trained and tested a transformer-based diffusion model on three image restoration tasks: denoising, deraining, and underwater reconstruction. Our model demonstrated improvements over existing methods, achieving the highest PSNR and SSIM for underwater reconstruction on the UIEB dataset, as well as the best UIQM on the Test-60 dataset, indicating high fidelity and perceptual quality. In denoising, our model outperformed others at various noise levels. Additionally, our model surpassed other state-of-the-art methods on the Rain100L dataset for image deraining. These results illustrate the model’s effectiveness in restoring image quality across different scenarios.
