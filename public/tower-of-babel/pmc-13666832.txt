Maternal-fetal type I interferon signaling drives TREM2 dysregulation and synaptic dysfunction in neurodevelopmental disorders
Authors: Matteo Bizzotto, Shih Feng You, Matteo Tamborini, Elisa Faggiani, Raffaella Morini, Antonella Borreca, Rui Zhang, Ekaterina Aladyeva, Aasritha Nallapu, Claudio Ferrari, Oscar Harari, Davide Pozzi, Ricardo D'Oliveira Albanus, Celeste M Karch, Michela Matteoli, Fabia Filipello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMaternal infections during gestation cause perturbed neuroimmune interactions and increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders in the offspring. Using a maternal immune activation model with polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (poly(I:C)), we investigated how maternal infections reshape the progeny hippocampal landscape. Poly(I:C) increased maternal type I interferon (IFN-I), disrupting neuronal transcriptional trajectories and excitatory synapse homeostasis in juvenile offspring. Poly(I:C) offspring microglia exhibited reduced expression of triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2), a key regulator of synapse elimination and brain development, accompanied by impaired phagocytosis and suppressed IFN-I-responsive substate. Consistently, a blunted IFN-I signature was observed in postmortem tissues from schizophrenic patients. TREM2 deficiency was associated with aberrant maternal IFN-I and altered neuronal and microglial signatures in poly(I:C) offspring, highlighting its regulatory role in neurodevelopmental pathologies. Blocking maternal IFN-I signaling restored synaptic defects and TREM2 function, suggesting that monitoring IFN-I responses during pregnancy and targeting defective IFN-I cascades in the newborn may represent viable therapeutic approaches to mitigate neurodevelopmental dysfunctions.Keywords: microglia, type I interferon, brain development, schizophrenia, neurodevelopmental disorders, TREM2, maternal immune activation

Introduction

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) encompass a range of conditions that develop early in life and share, as common denominator, alterations in normal brain development and function. At present, the term NDDs includes a wide variety of disorders such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and schizophrenia (SCZ). These conditions present highly heterogeneous features and negatively impact an individual’s cognitive, social, and behavioral abilities throughout life.1,2 The lack of effective treatments for these diseases, together with the increasing rate of diagnosis over the last two decades, pushes for a better understanding of the underlying mechanisms. Besides genetic components, environmental modifiers contribute substantially to NDDs, with maternal infections and immune system dysregulation associated with an increased risk of NDDs in children.3,4,5

Microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS) and active gatekeepers at the neuroimmune interface, are central in maternal and fetal immune responses, by controlling critical developmental steps including neurogenesis, synaptogenesis, and neuronal circuit formation.6,7,8,9,10,11,12 In the healthy brain, these processes are driven by dynamic microglial transcriptomic re-arrangements13,14,15 occurring during development. Also, at these stages, the occurrence of inflammatory conditions profoundly alters microglia signatures and their functional properties, as occurs in the aging brain.13,14,16,17,18 Given the key role of microglia in directing neuronal development and patrolling the brain against pathogen invasions, it is not surprising that these cells represent the most likely targets of prenatal insults.19 Consistently, activation of the maternal immune system induces widespread gene expression changes in the offspring brain, abolished by deletion of microglia,15 with two-hit models of immune activation inducing long-lived blunting of microglial immune responses across development.17,20

Among the genes that heavily control microglia phenotype and function is the triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2). TREM2 is a transmembrane receptor expressed in myeloid cells including peripheral macrophages and CNS microglia.21,22,23 Upon ligand binding and formation of a receptor complex with the adaptor proteins DAP10 and DAP12, TREM2 triggers intracellular signaling cascades that control phagocytosis, cell survival, proliferation, and metabolism.24,25,26,27,28 TREM2 also exists in a soluble form, referred to as soluble TREM2 (sTREM2), generated by alternative splicing of the main transcript or cleavage of the membrane-bound protein by the metalloproteases ADAM10 and ADAM17.29,30,31 Homozygous pathogenic mutations in the TREM2 gene cause a rare neurodegenerative disorder, Nasu-Hakola disease (NHD), also referred to as polycystic lipomembranous osteodysplasia with sclerosing leukoencephalopathy (PLOSL; OMIM 221770, OMIM 605086).32 Heterozygous variants in TREM2 increase the risk for late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (AD)33,34 and other neurodegenerative disorders.35,36,37,38,39,40,41 Besides the receptor’s involvement in critical aspects of aging and neurodegeneration, TREM2 is also actively engaged during physiological neurodevelopment, where it regulates synaptic elimination42,43 and contributes to maintain functional bioenergetics in neurons.44

Here, we investigated whether and through which mechanisms viral infections in pregnant dams affect the transcriptional and functional pathways of microglia and neurons in the offspring. We exploited a model of maternal immune activation (MIA), in which pregnant dams are injected with polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (poly(I:C))—a double-stranded RNA molecule mimicking viral infections45—that recapitulates main features observed in NDDs in humans.4,46,47,48 We found that poly(I:C)-induced MIA triggers widespread transcriptional remodeling of excitatory hippocampal neurons and microglia in juvenile offspring, accompanied by functional impairments. Poly(I:C) offspring exhibited disrupted synaptic homeostasis, pronounced TREM2 dysregulation, and loss of a type I interferon (IFN-I)-responsive microglial substate. Maternal IFN-I signaling was mechanistically linked to TREM2 disruption and directly drove the core synaptic defects observed in poly(I:C) offspring. Notably, a blunted inflammatory response and suppression of IFN-I-related gene expression were also detected in postmortem brain tissues from patients with SCZ, recapitulating key features identified in the poly(I:C) model of NDD.

Results

MIA-induced IFN-I response at maternal-embryo interface underlies the offspring synaptic damage

MIA was induced by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of (10 mg/kg) poly(I:C) in wild-type (WT) pregnant female mice (poly(I:C)) on gestational day 15 (GD15) (Figure S1A), a developmental window corresponding to the late first trimester or early second trimester of human pregnancy.49 Dams injected with saline solution were used as a control (CTRL). It is established that the viral mimetic poly(I:C) induces signaling via multiple inflammatory pathways, eliciting the acute secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-6, IL-17A, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) in pregnant mice.50,51,52 Consistent with the previous evidence, injection of (10 mg/kg) poly(I:C) in pregnant dams at GD15 triggered a robust increase of IL-6 in the plasma of dams 6 h after injection (Figures S1A and S1B). As poly(I:C) mimics a viral infection, it also elicits maternal upregulation of IFN-I as part of the antiviral response.53 Consistent with this, we observed a significant increase in plasma levels of the antiviral cytokine IFN-β (Figure S1C) alongside elevated expression of key IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) in the dam’s hippocampus (Figure S1D), including MX dynamin-like GTPase 1 (Mx1), IFN regulatory factor 7 (Irf7), IFN-induced protein with tetratricopeptide repeats 3 (Ifit3), IFN-induced transmembrane protein 3 (Ifitm3), ISG15 ubiquitin-like modifier (Isg15), the signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (Stat1), and cholesterol 25-hydroxylase (Ch25h) (Figure S1D).

IFN-I response was also aberrantly elevated at the maternal-fetal interface. Indeed, fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis of yolk sac 24 h after poly(I:C) injection in pregnant female at GD15 (Figure 1A) revealed significantly higher levels of membrane Ifitm3, one of the core ISGs,18 in yolk sac macrophages. Both CX3CR1+F480low (Figures 1B and 1C) and CX3CR1+F480+ yolk sac macrophages displayed a significant increase in Ifitm3+ cell percentage (Figures 1B–1D). Thus, poly(I:C) administration in pregnant females at GD15 stimulates IFN-I response and inflammatory processes at the maternal-fetal interface. Consistently, an IFN-I burst was detected in the embryonic brain, becoming detectable as early as 3 h after maternal poly(I:C) injection and reaching significance at 24 h, as evidenced by increased transcription of ISGs, including Irf7 and Ifitm3 (Figures 1E and 1F). Notably, Ifnar1 expression levels remained unchanged in embryos exposed to poly(I:C) for 3 or 24 h (Figures S1E and S1F).

Next, we quantified excitatory synapse density in the hippocampus of offspring born to poly(I:C)-treated dams44,45 at postnatal day (P) 8 and 18 (P8 and P18) (Figure 1G), two critical neurodevelopmental stages characterized by dynamic and ongoing synaptic remodeling.9,10,42,43,54,55,56 At P8, poly(I:C) offspring exhibited a significantly increased density of post-synaptic density protein 95 (PSD95+) puncta in the cornus ammonis 1 (CA1) stratum radiatum, compared with controls (Figures S1G and S1H), indicating an early alteration in excitatory synapse homeostasis. By contrast, at P18, the overall excitatory synapse density was profoundly decreased in poly(I:C) offspring, as assessed with PSD95+ postsynaptic puncta (Figures 1H and 1I) and presynaptic vesicular glutamate transporter-1 (vGluT1)+ (Figures S1I and S1J). Conversely, inhibitory synapses, identified through vGAT immunostaining, were unchanged (Figures S1K and S1L). Importantly, the observed excitatory synaptic decrease at P18 was not attributable to reduced neuronal number or dendritic arborization, as indicated by staining with neuronal nuclei (NeuN) marker to identify neuronal somata and microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2) to label neuronal dendrites (Figure S1M).45,46,47,48

To determine whether these alterations were transient or persisted into adulthood, we next analyzed PSD95+ synapses and neuronal markers in adult offspring. At P90, hippocampal PSD95+ synapse density was comparable between CTRL and poly(I:C) groups (Figures S1N and S1O), indicating that excitatory synapses in the poly(I:C)-induced MIA model are selectively affected during an early developmental window. Consistently, quantification of NeuN+ neuronal nuclei in the pyramidal layer showed no differences between adult CTRL and poly(I:C) offspring (Figure S1P). Together, these data suggest that poly(I:C) offspring exhibit an early overgrowth of excitatory synapses at P8 that is rapidly lost at P18.

To determine whether the maternal IFN-I burst is critical for driving the synaptic damage in the offspring, we tested whether blocking IFN-I signaling prior to poly(I:C) treatment in dams mitigates the effect of MIA in offspring. For this purpose, we used a monoclonal antibody against IFN-α/-β receptor subunit 1 (αIFN-IR) to block IFN-I (IFN-α/-β) responses elicited by poly(I:C) (Figure 1J).20 The effectiveness of the antibody was proved by showing that αIFN-IR prior to poly(I:C) exposure blunts the increase in ISG transcription in both primary murine microglia in vitro (Figure S1Q) and in the hippocampi of dams 6 h after poly(I:C) injection in vivo (Figure S1R). We thus blocked maternal IFN-I signaling by injecting pregnant dams 24 h prior to poly(I:C) treatment (GD14) with αIFN-IR or with immunoglobulin G (IgG) isotype control (Figure 1K). Notably, blocking IFN-I signaling prior to poly(I:C) treatment in dams efficiently attenuated PSD95+ synaptic loss caused in the P18 offspring upon maternal poly(I:C) (Figures 1L and 1M). To further investigate the impact of maternal αIFN-IR administration on offspring synaptic function, we performed whole-cell recordings to assess basal synaptic transmission by simultaneously monitoring miniature excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs and mIPSCs, respectively) in the same CA1 pyramidal neuron from acute brain slices of P18–P20 offspring (Figure 1N). Consistent with the immunofluorescence (IF) data, mEPSCs frequency was significantly reduced in poly(I:C) offspring (Iso poly(I:C)) (Figures 1O and 1P), whereas maternal αIFN-IR pretreatment fully rescued this deficit (αIFN-IR + poly(I:C)) (Figures 1O and 1P). No changes were detected in mEPSC amplitude (Figure 1Q) or in mIPSC frequency and amplitude between poly(I:C) and CTRL offspring (Figures S1S and S1T), supporting a selective impairment of excitatory synaptic transmission at this developmental stage. Furthermore, analysis of the excitatory-to-inhibitory balance (E/I ratio) at the single-cell level corroborated that maternal IFNAR1 blockade restores dysfunctional excitatory currents in poly(I:C) neurons (Figures 1R and S1U). Thus, an antiviral IFN-I burst is elicited in both the dam and the embryo by maternal poly(I:C) injection, and it is crucially involved in the synaptic damage in the offspring.

The IFN-I burst triggered by MIA disrupts expression of the microglial master regulator TREM2

It has been previously shown that maternal upregulation of IFN-I, as part of the antiviral response induced by MIA, interferes with the offspring’s programmed microglial developmental cascade.20 IF analysis of microglia density in the hippocampal region of both P8 and P18 offspring (Figures S2A and S2B), using the myeloid marker ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba1), revealed comparable microglia density upon poly(I:C) treatment. FACS analysis of hippocampal and cortical microglia revealed comparable surface marker expression between poly(I:C) and CTRL P18 offspring, with Cd11b+CD45int cells displaying similar levels of Cd11b; purinergic receptor P2y, G protein-coupled, 12 (P2yr12); surface major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC class II); the B.7 costimulatory molecule CD86; and the scavenger receptor CD36 (Figures S2C–S2K). Given that microglia are specialized phagocytic cells responsible for engulfing synaptic material during early development,9,10,54 we investigated whether maternal poly(I:C) challenge affects microglial phagocytic function in the offspring. Remarkably, poly(I:C) offspring exhibited two opposing waves of synaptic phagocytosis at P8 and P18 (Figure 2A). At P8, microglia showed an enhanced phagocytic profile, compared with controls, with increased PSD95+ synaptic material within CD68+ phagolysosomes in Iba1+ microglia (Figures S2L and S2M). By contrast, microglia from P18 poly(I:C) offspring displayed markedly reduced synaptic engulfment (Figures 2A–2C), despite comparable CD68 volume per Iba1+ cell (Figure S2N).

Since Trem2 is essential in regulating microglial developmental programs,33,34,35,50 including synaptic pruning,12,42,43,44,57 we first asked whether its expression was altered in the poly(I:C)-induced MIA model. We focused on TREM2 because preliminary analysis suggested it could be a key mediator of microglial dysfunction in this context. Trem2 transcript in the embryonic brain and sTREM2 levels in the amniotic fluid (Figures S2O and S2P) remained stable during embryogenesis following MIA but began to decrease postnatally starting at P2 (Figure S2Q). Importantly, Trem2 protein was significantly reduced in hippocampal microglia of poly(I:C) offspring at both P8 (Figures S3A and S3B) and P18 (Figures 2D and 2E), as revealed by IF and 3D reconstruction analysis. FACS analysis confirmed a significant reduction of Trem2 receptor at the surface of Cd11b+CD45int microglia isolated from poly(I:C) offspring, compared with CTRL (Figures 2F–2H). Consistently, exposure of primary murine microglia in vitro to poly(I:C) or IFN-β led to a significant downregulation of Trem2 and Tyrobp transcripts (Figure S3C).

Notably, significantly higher sTREM2 in the blood serum of poly(I:C) P18 offspring was detected by ELISA (Figure 2I). The observed increase in sTREM2 was independent of ADAM17 or ADAM10 expression (Figures S3D–S3H). Interfering with maternal IFN-I signaling prior to poly(I:C) treatment in dams preserved membrane and intracellular Trem2 receptor expression in Iba1+ cells (Figures 2J and 2K) and plasmatic sTREM2 levels in offspring microglia (Figure 2L), indicating that the IFN-I burst induced by MIA can modulate microglia trajectories through the master regulator Trem2. We next tested whether maternal αIFN-IR treatment could prevent the defects in microglial phagocytosis in poly(I:C) offspring at P18. Consistent with the well-established role of the IFN pathway in promoting phagocytosis in myeloid and non-myeloid cells,18,58,59,60 blocking IFN-I signaling did not rescue microglial phagocytic function, as demonstrated by the 3D reconstruction and volumetric analysis showing a comparable amount of PSD95+ synapses in poly(I:C) offspring either pretreated or not with αIFN-IR antibody (Figures S3I and S3J). These results demonstrate a direct link between maternal poly(I:C)-induced IFN-I signaling and TREM2 modulation, which occurs independently of microglial phagocytic dysfunction. Specifically, IFN-I blockade restores both membrane and sTREM2, without correcting the impaired synaptic engulfment in poly(I:C) offspring.

MIA alters neuronal transcriptional trajectories and behavioral phenotypes in the offspring via a TREM2-dependent program

In the last years, Trem2 has emerged as a crucial hub during neurodevelopment, heavily influencing microglial and excitatory neuronal trajectories.12,42,43,44,57 Our finding that poly(I:C) injection in the dams impacts Trem2 expression in the newborn led us to investigate more in detail the possible occurrence of Trem2-dependent transcriptional changes in microglia and excitatory neurons upon MIA. We thus performed single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) on the whole hippocampus of Trem2+/+ and Trem2−/− poly(I:C) and CTRL offspring at P18 (Figure 3A). Unsupervised clustering by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) identified nine cell clusters across all samples (Figure 3B). Based on the expression of known cell-type-specific markers (Table S1), we could distinguish excitatory glutamatergic (Glut) and inhibitory (GABA) neurons, brain macrophages including microglia and likely border-associated macrophages (BAMs), astrocytes, endothelial cells, oligodendrocytes precursors cells (OPCs), and oligodendrocytes (Figure 3B). For all four conditions, all clusters were similarly represented in each of the mice analyzed, and the nuclei had comparable metrics. Proportions of hippocampal cell types were not significantly impacted in poly(I:C) offspring compared with CTRL offspring, as well as across the two genotypes (Figure 3C). Given the alterations in synapse homeostasis and microglial function observed in poly(I:C) juvenile mice (Figure 1), we focused on clusters of excitatory Glut neurons and microglia.

Analyses of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the Glut neuron cluster revealed a total of 2,037 DEGs in Trem2+/+ poly(I:C) vs. CTRL offspring (Figure 3D). Pathways analysis of 1,140 upregulated genes revealed a high association with schizophrenia (false discovery rate [FDR] 3.31E−23), bipolar disorder (FDR 1.31E−17), autistic disorder (FDR 9.23E−12), and synapse organization (FDR 6.16E−25) (Figure 3E). Among 897 downregulated neuronal genes, we found enrichment in pathways associated to synapse organization (6.56E−4) and mRNA metabolic process (FDR 5.14E−4) (Figure 3E; Tables S2 and S3). This finding is consistent with altered synaptic function and dysregulation of genes linked to NDDs in excitatory neurons in the poly(I:C) model. Venn diagram of DEGs in Trem2+/+ and Trem2−/− poly(I:C) vs. CTRL offspring (Figures 3F and S4A) shows 1,228 neuronal genes dysregulated by poly(I:C) only in the presence of Trem2. Also, 881 upregulated genes showed a significant enrichment in pathways associated to SCZ (FDR 2.57E−10), bipolar disorder (FDR 1.36E−6), and autistic disorder (FDR 9.20E−5) (Trem2-dep up; Figures 3E and 3F). Both upregulation and downregulation of genes associated to synapse organization (FDR 1.40E−11; FDR 6.11E−3) was observed in the Trem2-dependent gene set (Trem2-dep up and Trem2-dep down; Figures 3E and 3F; Tables S2 and S3).

To assess whether the Trem2-dependent dysregulation of synaptic gene pathways in the poly(I:C)-MIA model had a structural and functional correlate, we compared PSD95 density in the CA1 region of Trem2+/+ poly(I:C), Trem2−/− poly(I:C), and CTRL offspring at P18. Our previous work has shown that Trem2−/− P18 mice display an increased excitatory synaptic density, compared with Trem2+/+.42 Here, we demonstrated that poly(I:C)-induced MIA did not change PSD95+ synapse density in Trem2−/− offspring (Figures 3G and 3H), which was consistently higher in Trem2−/− hippocampi regardless of poly(I:C) treatment (Figure 3H). MAP2 and NeuN densities were comparable across genotypes and treatments at P18 (Figures S4B and S4C).

Next, we assessed behavioral outcomes in 3-month-old offspring to determine whether Trem2-dependent synaptic alterations translated into functional changes. We found that Trem2+/+ poly(I:C) offspring exhibited increased anxiety,61,62 spending more time in the dark chamber of the dark/light test, compared with Trem2+/+ CTRL (Figure 3J), while no differences were observed in the number of entries or latency time (Figures S4D–S4F). When sensorimotor gating was evaluated using the pre-pulse inhibition (PPI) of the startle reflex,16,63,64,65 Trem2+/+ poly(I:C) offspring displayed a significantly heightened startle response and reduced PPI% (Figures 3K, 3L, and S4G), indicative of SCZ-like traits in the poly(I:C) model. Notably, these behavioral alterations were absent in adult poly(I:C) Trem2-deficient offspring (Figures 3J–3L and S4D–S4G).

These data demonstrate that TREM2 plays a critical role in mediating the synaptic and behavioral consequences of MIA, as its absence attenuates the emergence of specific deficits in adult offspring.

TREM2 deficiency compromises the microglial response to maternal poly(I:C)

We next examined the Trem2-dependent effects of MIA on microglia. In contrast to the reduced synaptic engulfment observed in Trem2+/+ poly(I:C) offspring relative to CTRL (Figures 4A and 4B), microglia from Trem2−/− poly(I:C) and CTRL offspring engulfed comparable amounts of PSD95+ structures, at an extent that was consistently lower relative to Trem2+/+ CTRL42 (Figures 4A and 4B). Analysis of the microglia cluster in our snRNA-seq dataset confirmed that Trem2 transcript is downregulated in Trem2+/+ poly(I:C) offspring at P18 (Figures 4C, S4H, and S4I). Among a total of 234 DEGs in the microglia cluster (Figure 4C), we found 168 genes downregulated in poly(I:C) vs. CTRL offspring. Upregulated genes did not show significant pathways enrichment, whereas downregulated genes were significantly enriched in pathways associated to synapse pruning (FDR 2.45E−5) (Figure 4D), which is in line with the reduced synapse engulfment performed by microglia (Figures 2B, 2C, 4A, and 4B). Additional downregulated pathways included response to cytokines (FDR 156 4.38E−4), response to virus (FDR 1.22E−3), IFN-mediated signaling (FDR 1.35E−3), and increased susceptibility to infection (8.84E−6) (Figure 4D; Tables S2 and S3), suggesting the occurrence of a defective IFN-I signaling and response to virus in microglia from poly(I:C) offspring.

To dissect the role of Trem2 in driving microglial pathways enrichment, we overlapped microglial DEGs in Trem2+/+ and Trem2−/− poly(I:C) vs. CTRL offspring (Venn diagram in Figures 4E and S4J). We identified a total of 161 genes that were modulated by poly(I:C) exclusively in the presence of Trem2 (Figure 4E). Pathway analyses of 117 downregulated genes among this latter group showed that response to cytokines (FDR 2.47E−6), response to virus (FDR 2.24E−5), and IFN-mediated signaling (FDR 1.29E−4) pathways displayed the strongest Trem2-dependency (Trem2-dep down; Figures 4D and 4E; Tables S2 and S3). Consistently, bulk RNA-seq of primary microglia in vitro (Figure 4F) confirmed downregulation of pathways associated to inflammatory response, cell adhesion, response to external stimulus, and response to cytokines in Trem2−/− poly(I:C) microglia, as compared with Trem2+/+ poly(I:C) (Figures 4G and S4K; Table S4). Thus, Trem2 expression is needed for proper microglial responses to poly(I:C) challenge.

Overall, our findings demonstrate that maternal poly(I:C) challenge during gestation induces long-term alterations in the transcriptional trajectories of microglia and Glut neurons in juvenile Trem2+/+ offspring, with both cell types exhibiting transcriptional cell-autonomous defects. The transcriptional and functional changes normally induced by poly(I:C) in WT mice and cells are markedly attenuated in the absence of Trem2 receptor. Thus, Trem2 appears to function as a gate for poly(I:C)-mediated defects. The downregulation of IFN-I and antiviral responses within the microglia cluster was strongly dependent on Trem2, underscoring its critical role in modulating these microglial functions.

An IFN-I-responsive microglial state is lost in poly(I:C) offspring and is tightly connected to Trem2

To further explore the role of Trem2 in controlling microglial features upon MIA, we subclustered the microglial cell population in the snRNA-seq dataset. Microglia could be further classified into four states (Figures 5A and 5B), all of which expressed Trem2 and the canonical microglial markers66,67 (Figures S5 and S6A; Table S5). Changes in the abundances of microglial states were observed in poly(I:C) compared with CTRL offspring and across the two genotypes (Figure 5C). Microglia expressing canonical microglial markers and homeostatic genes (P2ry12, Tmem119)68,69 were annotated as “Mic.0 homeostatic microglia” (Figure 5B). Trem2+/+ poly(I:C) offspring displayed an increased proportion of Mic.0, compared with CTRL, similarly to Trem2−/− CTRL and poly(I:C) offspring (Figure 5C). Mic.2, “inflammatory microglia,” expressed pro-inflammatory cytokine genes such as Ccl3 and Ccl470,71 and the cytokine receptors Cd9 and Cd6372,73,74 (Figure 5B). Mic.3 contained neuronal genes (Robo1, Robo2, Gabrg2, Gabrg3), as previously described in Escoubas et al.18 BAM cluster was identified by the expression of canonical markers (Cd163, Mrc1, Clec10a, Ms4a7)75,76,77 (Figure 5B). Most notable was a microglial substate enriched in IFN-I genes (Ifit3, Irf7, Stat1, Ifitm3), which we renamed as Mic.1 “IFN-I responsive”18,20,60,78,79 (Figures 5A and 5B). A similar microglial substate, defined as IFN-responsive microglia (IRMs), was recently identified as a developmentally regulated state associated to highly phagocytic microglia.18 Trem2+/+ poly(I:C) offspring, as well as Trem2−/− CTRL and poly(I:C) offspring, displayed a lower Mic.1 proportion, compared with Trem2+/+ CTRL (Figure 5C). Also, we found that the expression levels of the top genes enriched in Mic.1 IRMs (Figure S7A) and the percentage of nuclei expressing Mic.1 genes were drastically reduced in Trem2+/+ poly(I:C) offspring (Figures 5D and 5E) and to a lesser extent in CTRL offspring lacking Trem2 (Figures 5D and 5E). Our findings were further validated by constructing a signature score associated with the IRMs detected in Escoubas et al.18 and Kaya et al.78 (Figure S7B). Surprisingly, Trem2−/− poly(I:C) offspring showed a Mic.1 microglial population proportion comparable to that of Trem2+/+ CTRL offspring, along with a strong expression of key Mic.1 signature genes—even exceeding levels observed in Trem2+/+ CTRL offspring (Figures 5D and 5E). To validate our findings at a functional level, we analyzed whether microglia from poly(I:C) offspring displayed altered engulfment of neuronal apoptotic bodies, a function tightly linked to IFN-I signature enrichment.7,18 Consistent with the robust reduction of IFN-I gene expression, microglia from poly(I:C) offspring exhibited significantly fewer phagocytic cups containing neuronal apoptotic bodies, compared with CTRL offspring (Figure S7C). To explore the relationship between Trem2 and the IFN-I response in this context, we examined ISG expression following poly(I:C) exposure at the maternal-fetal interface and in adult Trem2+/+ and Trem2−/− mice (Figure 5F). We observed increased expression of Mx1, Irf7, and Ifitm3 transcripts in Trem2−/− brain embryos 3 h after poly(I:C) injection into pregnant dams (Figure 5G). Similarly, adult Trem2−/− mice exhibited higher expression of Mx1, Irf7, Ifit3, Stat1, and Ch25h transcripts 6 h after direct poly(I:C) administration (Figure 5H). These results indicate that Trem2 deficiency primes mice for an exaggerated ISG response following poly(I:C) challenge, providing a mechanistic explanation for why Trem2−/− poly(I:C) offspring at P18 fail to resolve this response and maintain elevated IFN-I levels.

Maternal IFN-I burst regulates loss of tonic IFN-I signal in poly(I:C) offspring microglia via Cdk8 and pStat1

As we observed decreased expression of several IFN-responsive genes (Mx1, Irf7, Ift3, Stat1, and Ifitm3) in the microglia cluster of poly(I:C) offspring at P18 (Figures 5D and S7A), we sought to determine whether the maternal IFN-I burst contributes to the loss of Mic.1 IFN-I responsive genes. IF analysis revealed that reduced Stat1 protein—a major mediator of the IFN cellular response80—within microglia of poly(I:C) offspring was fully rescued by maternal anti-IFN-I treatment in (αIFN-IR + poly(I:C)) offspring at P18 (Figures 6A and 6B). As expected, Stat1 modulation was also observed in other cell types beyond Iba1+ microglia (Figure S7D).

To identify upstream signals regulating the IFN-I response in P18 poly(I:C) offspring, we focused on cyclin-dependent kinase 8 (CDK8), a key ISG and IFN-I modulator.81 snRNA-seq revealed significant upregulation of Cdk8 transcript in the microglia cluster of poly(I:C) offspring (p adj = 6.72E−5) (Table S2, microglia total genes_upregulated). Western blot analysis confirmed that Cdk8 protein was significantly increased in the cortices of poly(I:C) offspring at P18 (Figures 6C and 6D), whereas no change was detected in brain embryos 24 h after poly(I:C) injection (Figures S7E and S7F). CDK8 mediates STAT1 phosphorylation, and together, they have been reported to modulate the antiviral IFN-I response.81,82 Consistently, pStat1 was increased in poly(I:C) offspring at P18, while it was unchanged in brain embryos at 24 h (Figures 6C, 6E, S7G, and S7H). Notably, blocking the maternal IFN-I burst normalized Cdk8 and pStat1 protein levels in (αIFN-IR + poly(I:C)) offspring to those of (αIFN-IR) controls (Figures 6C–6E). These data underline a role for the Cdk8-pStat11 pathway in modulating IFN-I responses in the context of MIA.

Altered interferon-responsive gene signature in a cohort of schizophrenic patients

To assess whether the phenotypes observed in poly(I:C) mouse offspring are relevant to human NDDs, we compared the dysregulated genes in mouse microglia and neuron clusters with a comprehensive dataset of candidate genes associated with human NDDs, including SCZ, autism, ADHD, epilepsy, and intellectual disability (The NDD genehub: https://nddgenehub.org/).83 We applied Fisher’s exact test to evaluate, for each cell type, the enrichment of human orthologs of the poly(I:C) DEGs within each NDD-associated gene set, determining whether these DEGs overlapped with NDD candidate genes more than expected by chance (Figures 7A and 7B). Notably, a significant number of human orthologs corresponding to DEGs in both microglia (Figure 7A) and neuron (Figure 7B) clusters (Table S6) overlapped with genes harboring loss-of-function or missense mutations linked to one or multiple NDDs (Figures 7A and 7B; Table S6).

Next, we focused on a cohort of patients with SCZ, a psychiatric condition classified within the NDD spectrum. We performed bulk RNA-seq on the superior temporal gyrus (STG) region of 34 schizophrenic patients and 35 healthy controls (CTRL), ranging from 19 to 60 years old, obtained from the Stanley Medical Research Institute brain collection (Figure 7C; Table S7). After adjusting for technical and biological covariates, we identified 507 significantly upregulated and 1,360 downregulated genes in schizophrenic patients compared with controls (p < 0.05; Figure 7D; Table S8). Upregulated genes were not enriched in specific functional pathways. Downregulated genes, however, were enriched in pathways associated with regulation of immune system processes (FDR 3.16E−10), response to cytokines (FDR 228 2.45E−8), positive regulation of immune response (FDR 1.31E−7), inflammatory response (FDR 7.90E−229.07), viral processes (FDR 1.83E−5), and canonical nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signal transduction (FDR 8.54E−4) (Figure 7E). Although we did not observe altered expression of TREM2 or TYROBP in patients with SCZ, we detected significant dysregulation in TREM1L, a gene directly connected to the TREM2 pathway (Table S8).23

We next probed our dataset with a human ISG signature60 (Figures 7F and 7G). As shown in the Venn diagram, we found 86 out of 1,867 DEGs in the patients’ group vs. control subjects overlapping with the human ISG signature (e.g., IFITM3, IFI35, CCL2, IRF1, IRF7, STAT2) (Figures 7F and 7G; Table S9). The majority of these DEGs (78 out of 86) were significantly downregulated in the cohort of schizophrenic patients compared with controls. Importantly, 19 of these genes (including STAT2, IFITM3, IRF7, CCL2, IFI35) were significantly downregulated both in schizophrenic brains and in the microglia cluster from poly(I:C) offspring, after mapping to their human ortholog(s) (Figure 7G; Table S8; STAR Methods section). Importantly, CDK8 was also upregulated in the schizophrenic patient cohort (Table S8), consistent with mouse Cdk8 increased expression in P18 poly(I:C) offspring microglia at both the mRNA and protein levels. This further supports the presence of overlapping molecular pathways between our mouse model and the human data.

Together, these findings indicate that brains from patients with SCZ exhibit an impaired immune response and reduced inflammation, along with downregulation of key IFN-I genes, which mirrors the reduction of IFN-I-responsive Mic.1 microglia observed in our poly(I:C)-induced MIA mouse model.

Discussion

The convergence of genetic predisposition in the progeny and maternal infections during gestation can disrupt the finely tuned maternal-fetal environment, increasing the risk of NDDs, including ASD and SCZ, in newborns.3,4,47,48,84,85,86 Despite growing evidence linking MIA to NDDs, the molecular mechanisms underlying altered neuroimmune communication remain incompletely understood.87 Altered spine density, whether increased or decreased, has been observed in ASD88 and SCZ,89 suggesting that dysregulated synapse formation and elimination are central events in these disorders. Consistent with prior reports of synaptic derangements in MIA models,50,87,90,91,92,93,94,95 transcriptomic analyses of excitatory neurons from poly(I:C) offspring revealed upregulation of pathways associated with SCZ, autistic disorder, and bipolar disorder, along with pronounced dysregulation of synapse organization. Ex vivo analyses confirmed reduced hippocampal excitatory synapse density and activity in juvenile offspring of poly(I:C)-treated dams.

These observations set the stage for our mechanistic dissection, revealing a central role for maternal IFN-I signaling in driving synaptic and Trem2 deficits, dynamic microglial alterations that shape neuronal transcriptional programs, and evidence linking these pathways to molecular signatures observed in human NDDs.

Maternal IFN-I signaling drives synaptic and TREM2 alterations

IFN-Is are key components of the innate antiviral response,96 expressed both in physiological conditions and during neuroinflammation.18,20,58,59,76,78,97 We show that maternal poly(I:C) triggers a robust IFN-I response that propagates across the maternal-fetal barrier, as evidenced by increased Ifitm3 expression in Cx3Cr1+F480+/F480low yolk sac macrophages 24 h post-injection. Aberrant IFN-I signaling has been previously linked to brain network disruptions and cognitive or neuropsychiatric impairments.98,99,100 Moreover, tightly regulated IFN-β signaling at the maternal-fetal interface is essential for a healthy pregnancy, and its dysregulation associated with adverse outcomes including preterm birth and neurodevelopmental defects.101,102

Systemic αIFN-IR treatment by using a monoclonal αIFN-I receptor antibody markedly reduced ISG expression in the hippocampus of dams. Further studies are needed to define the relative contribution of peripheral from central IFN-I signaling inhibition. Importantly, however, maternal IFN-I blockade fully rescued synapse density in poly(I:C) offspring, demonstrating that hyperactivation of maternal IFN-I is a primary driver of synaptic dysregulation. This effect mirrors observations in neurodegenerative models, where IFN-I signaling mediates synapse loss and behavioral deficits.58,59 Notably, the same intervention did not restore microglial phagocytic capacity, revealing a dissociation between synaptic rescue and microglial engulfment. This dissociation indicates that while synapse loss is IFN-I dependent, microglial phagocytic alterations likely arise via distinct or additional signaling pathways, involving molecules and receptors such as complement C1q,103 Mertk, or Axl,59 independently of maternal IFN-I.

Although our data strongly support a combined contribution of maternal and fetal IFN-I responses in the poly(I:C) model, direct genetic approaches, such as the use of IFNAR-knockout and heterozygous dams and/or fetuses, will be required to fully dissect the relative contributions of maternal and fetal IFNAR signaling in mediating the downstream effects.20,104,105

The link between IFN-I signaling and NDDs is further supported by the observation that multiple IFN-I genes dysregulated in microglia and neuron clusters of poly(I:C) offspring—STAT1, STAT2, USP18, ISG15, C1QA, C1QB, C1QC—are implicated in type I interferonopathies,104 including Aicardi-Goutières syndrome, which manifests early with developmental delays and neuropsychiatric features.62,104 Additionally, we observed that elevated Cdk8 expression and phosphorylated Stat1 (S727) in P18 poly(I:C) offspring, were normalized by maternal αIFN-IR pretreatment. Given that CDK8-mediated STAT1 S727 phosphorylation modulates antiviral and IFN-γ-responsive genes,81,105,106 these findings identify a CDK8-STAT1 axis as a key mediator linking maternal IFN-I signaling to tonic IFN-I response regulation in juvenile microglia and a potential therapeutic target for NDDs.

Maternal αIFN-IR blockade restored Trem2 levels in P18 poly(I:C) offspring. This finding highlights a direct link between IFN-I signaling and Trem2 regulation, consistent with recent findings in a model of human microglia infected with herpes simplex virus type 1.107 We hypothesize that the magnitude of the antiviral IFN-I response could influence TREM2 function in brain microglia and, conversely, that reduced TREM2 might enhance IFN-I signaling. Although maternal poly(I:C) does not cross the placenta, it is conceivable that the resulting cytokine and IFN-I surge may epigenetically prime fetal microglia and neural progenitors, potentially inducing long-lasting DNA methylation and chromatin accessibility changes at immune- and synapse-related loci.108,109,110 Such developmental reprogramming might allow microglia to “remember” early-life immune activation, which could contribute to sustained alterations in microglial function, core gene expression (e.g., Trem2), and circuit development, even after IFN-I levels have normalized.

Notably, elevated sTREM2 levels in poly(I:C) offspring occurred without changes in Adam10 or Adam17 expression, suggesting that post-transcriptional or cellular mechanisms may underlie this effect. Future work will be necessary to distinguish between increased sheddase activity,31 peripheral myeloid contributions, or alternative Trem2 splicing as potential drivers of sTREM2 elevation.

Microglial dynamics and neuron-microglia interactions

Poly(I:C) offspring exhibited temporally dynamic microglial responses: prematurely enhanced phagocytic activity at P8, progressing to phagocytic exhaustion by P18, accompanied by transcriptional dysregulation of synaptic genes and altered NDD trajectories.

Notably, synapse loss at P18 did not result from excessive microglial pruning, highlighting neuron-intrinsic synaptic defects. We propose that early MIA-induced perturbations of highly plastic neuronal programs, together with secondary effects of MIA-primed glial cells, converge to drive the pronounced transcriptional remodeling observed in Glut neurons, a finding consistent with prior work showing that microglia, via Trem2, can profoundly shape neuronal transcriptomes.15,44 While contributions from astrocytes or other glial populations to synaptic remodeling cannot be ruled out, additional studies are necessary to disentangle their respective roles in MIA-induced neurodevelopmental defects.

Our work, consistent with previous findings,4,13,17 suggests that disruption of the tightly regulated stepwise microglial differentiation program during gestation may drive developmental reprogramming and subsequent microglial desensitization. This could help explain the reduction in IFN-responsive microglial states despite increased IFN-I exposure, ultimately compromising the neurodevelopmental cascade.

snRNA-seq revealed that a microglial substate enriched in IFN-I genes (Mic.1) was drastically reduced in poly(I:C) offspring. Mic.1 likely corresponds to the highly phagocytic IRMs previously described in neurodevelopment.18 Maternal IFN-IR inhibition restored Stat1 expression, a key ISG within the Mic.1 signature,18,78 thereby linking maternal IFN-I signaling to the loss of the Mic.1 cluster in the offspring.

Our data also indicate that Trem2 is central for the emergence of the Mic.1 substate in poly(I:C) offspring, highlighting that TREM2 controls the emergence of microglial transcriptional signatures not only during aging and neurodegeneration73,74,111 but also in neurodevelopment. Accordingly, maternal poly(I:C) administration caused a marked reduction of Trem2 protein and transcript in offspring microglia, which was restored by maternal αIFN-IR pretreatment. These findings support the notion that maternal IFN-I signaling likely influences TREM2 expression indirectly through microglial developmental reprogramming, consistent with the observation that Trem2 levels are unchanged in embryos but decrease postnatally. Nevertheless, microglial phagocytic activity remained impaired despite Trem2 recovery, highlighting that synaptic rescue is independent of microglial engulfment. These findings suggest that Trem2 deficits induced by MIA may contribute to broader neuronal differentiation and transcriptional remodeling, rather than being solely linked to synapse elimination.44

Translational relevance and human context

There is still a lack of consensus in literature about the inflammatory status of individuals affected by SCZ. While several studies report increased inflammation and elevated cytokines levels in patients,112,113 others observe blunted inflammatory responses and downregulation of NF-κB pathways.114,115 Postmortem transcriptomic analyses of the STG in 35 patients with SCZ revealed downregulation of inflammatory and IFN-I-responsive genes (STAT2, IFITM3, IRF7, CCL2, IFI35), paralleling the reduced Mic.1 substate in poly(I:C) offspring. While patient heterogeneity, long-term antipsychotic treatment, or aging cannot be excluded, these data suggest that impaired IRMs may contribute to the vulnerability to infections and neurodevelopmental perturbations observed in children with NDDs.116,117

Our data suggest that monitoring maternal IFN-I signaling during pregnancy or leveraging anti-inflammatory drugs to counterbalance viral-induced infections could potentially allow therapeutic intervention to prevent or predict the onset of NDDs. Postnatally, stimulating TREM2 signaling via agonistic antibodies offers a potential strategy to enhance microglial support of circuit development. Several anti-TREM2 antibodies are currently in early clinical trials, highlighting the translational importance of understanding IFN-I-TREM2 interactions in neurodevelopment.27,118,119

Conclusions

In summary, maternal IFN-I signaling, microglial dynamics, and TREM2-dependent neuron-microglia interactions converge to shape synaptic and transcriptional trajectories during development. These findings link prenatal immune challenges to long-term neurodevelopmental vulnerability in humans, providing mechanistic insight and potential therapeutic avenues for NDDs.

Experimental model and study participant details

Post-mortem human brain samples

Post-mortem brain samples from the superior temporal gyrus were obtained from the Stanley Medical Research Institute (SMRI) tissue collections, the Array Collection (AC). The diagnosis of schizophrenia (DSM-IV) was established as described in.120 Cases in this study included 34 patients diagnosed with schizophrenia and 35 control subjects, consisting in individuals without psychiatric diagnoses. To control for potential confounding, analyses were adjusted for age, sex, disease profile, age of onset, and post-mortem interval (PMI) (Table S6). All methods for processing and analyzing the brain autopsy tissues followed relevant guidelines and regulations. Demographic and clinical characteristics of the donors of human brain tissues at the time of collection are indicated in Table S6.

Mice

All experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines established by the European Community Council (Directive 2010/63/EU of September 22nd, 2010) and the Italian D.Lg. 26/2014. The study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) (permission number n° 995/2023-PR) of the Humanitas Research Hospital and by the Italian Ministry of Health. Mice were housed in the Specific Pathogen Free (SPF) animal facility of Humanitas Clinical and Research Center in individually ventilated cages under constant temperature (22 ± 1C) and humidity (50%) conditions with a 12-hours light/dark cycle and were provided with food and water ad libitum. All efforts were made to minimize the number of subjects used and their suffering. C57BL/6J Trem2-/-mice, generated as previously described,21 were provided by Bio X Cell-Cosmo Pharmaceutical (Milan, Italy).121 C57BL/6J mice were purchased by Charles River Laboratories.

Maternal Immune Activation (MIA)

According to the experimental need, C57BL/6J wild-type (WT) or Trem2+/- females were mated at the age of 2–6 months with age-matched WT or Trem2+/- males respectively. Timed mating was performed to obtain embryos at defined time points after conception. Pregnant mothers at gestational day 15 (GD15) were intraperitoneally (I.P.) injected with high molecular weight (HMW) Polyinosinic:Polycytidylic acid (Poly(I:C)) (cat. tlrl-pic, Invivogen) [10 mg/kg].16,122 An equivalent volume of saline solution was used as control. CTRL and Poly(I:C) male littermates at P8, P18 or P90 were selected for all the in vivo analyses. 3 or 4 months-old adult female mice were used for some experiments as indicated in the text.

Maternal Treatment with anti-Interferon Type I Receptor (αIFN-IR)

On GD14, WT pregnant females were intravenously (I.V.) injected with a single dose of 400 μg anti-IFN receptor I (αIFN-IR; cat. BP0241, clone MAR1-5A3,123 Bio X Cell, NH, USA) or control IgG (IgG1; cat. BP0083, clone MOPC-21, Bio X Cell, NH, USA), as reported in.20 After 24 hours, on GD15, treated females were injected with Poly(I:C) or saline solution, as described above. WT male offspring were collected at P18 for further analysis.

Primary Microglial Culture

Primary microglia cultures were obtained from mixed glial cultures as previously described.42 Brains from Trem2+/+ and Trem2-/- at P1-P3 mice (mixed sex) were collected and cerebellum, meninges and olfactory bulbs were eliminated. Cortices and hippocampi from both the hemispheres were gently dissected in ice-cold Hanks Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, cat.14180046, ThermoFisher Scientific). After digestion with trypsin (cat.ECB3051D, Euroclone) at 37°, mechanical dissociation and filtration in 70μm cell strainer (BD Biosciences), cells were plated in T-75 flask and maintained at 37° and 5% CO2 in MEM, complemented with Earl’s salts, (cat.M5650, Sigma-Aldrich), 20% heat-inactivated Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, cat.ECS0186L, Euroclone), glucose 0.6%, sodium pyruvate 2mM (cat.ECM9542D, Euroclone) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (cat.15140-122, ThermoFisher Scientific). After two weeks in culture, microglia were harvested by shaking flasks at 245 rpm for 60 minutes and seeded on poly-L ornithine (0.05 mg/mL – cat. P3655, Sigma-Aldrich) pre-coated MW-12 plates with a 300.000 cells/well density. Microglia have been treated for 24 hours with HMW Poly (I:C) [20μg/mL] (cat.tlrl-pic, Invivogen) or for 4 hours with recombinant mouse IFNβ [10ng/mL] (cat. 8234-MB, R&D Systems).

Method details

Behavioral Tests

Dark/Light Chamber

The test was performed according to previous studies.61 The apparatus consisted of a cage divided into a dark compartment (one third) and a brightly illuminated compartment (two thirds). Mice were allowed to freely move for 10 min between the two chambers. The total number of transitions, the time spent in each chamber, the latency to enter the dark chamber and the latency to the first exit from the dark chamber were recorded. After each trial, all chambers were cleaned with ethanol to prevent bias based on olfactory cues.

Pre-Pulse Inhibition (PPI)

Mice underwent the PPI task in a startle response system (PanLab System, Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA). Behavioral testing trials were designed, and data were recorded using PACKWIN V2.0 software (Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA). Sound pressure levels were calibrated using a standard SPL meter (model 407730, Extech, Nashua, NH). Mice were placed on a movement-sensitive platform. Vertical displacements of the platform induced by startle responses were converted into a voltage trace by a piezoelectric transducer located underneath the platform. Startle amplitude was measured as the peak-to-peak maximum startle magnitude of the signal measured during a 1-s window following the presentation of the acoustic stimulation. Prior to any testing session, animals were first handled and acclimatized to the testing chamber, for 10 min. This acclimatization period was used to reduce the occurrence of movement and artifacts throughout testing trials. Following the acclimatization period, an input/output (I/O) assay was performed to test startle reactivity. This I/O test began with the presentation of a 40-ms sound at different intensities (in dB: 70, 80, 90, 100, 110, and 120) every 15 s, in a pseudorandomized order. A total of 35 trials (i.e., 7 sound intensities, each sound presented 5 times) were acquired and quantified. Startle reactivity, derived from this I/O assay, allowed the gain of the movement-sensitive platform to be set. This gain allowed the startle responses to be detected within a measurable range. Once determined, the gain for each experimental subject was kept constant throughout the remaining of the experiment. Mice were presented with nine startle-inducing 120 dB (40 ms) sounds called “pulse-alone” stimulations. These 120 dB sounds were presented every 29 s and were used to achieve a stable baseline startle response. The following PPI test consisted of two different conditions as follows: (1) startling pulse-alone stimulations (for baseline startle amplitude), and (2) combinations of a prepulse (75 dB noise; 20 ms) followed a 120-dB startling pulse (40 ms) at 9 different interstimulus intervals (in ms). PPI analysis was conducted according to previous studies.61,124 The Percentage of PPI is calculated with the following formula 100 × (StartlePulse Only − StartlePrepulse+Pulse) / StartlePulse Only.

Electrophysiological Recordings

WT CTRL and Poly(I:C) male offspring at P18-P20 were sacrificed by cervical dislocation and rapidly decapitated. Brains were removed and placed in ice-cold solution containing the following: 87mM NaCl, 21mM NaHCO3, 1.25mM NaH2PO4, 7mM MgCl2, 0.5mM CaCl2, 2.5mM KCl, 25mM D-glucose, and 7mM sucrose, equilibrated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 (pH 7.4). Coronal hippocampal slices (300 μm thick) were cut with a VT1000S vibratome (Leica Microsystems). Slices were incubated at RT for at least 1 hour, in the same solution as above, before being transferred to the recording chamber. During experiments, slices were superfused at 1–2 mL/min with artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) containing the following: 129mM NaCl, 21mM NaHCO3, 1.25mM NaH2PO4, 1.8mM MgSO4, 1.6mM CaCl2, 3mM KCl, 10mM D-glucose, equilibrated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 (pH 7.4). Cells were visualized with a BX51WI upright microscope (Olympus) equipped with a water immersion differential interference contrast (DIC) objective and an infrared (IR) camera (XM10r Olympus). Neurons were voltage clamped with a Multiclamp 700B patch-clamp amplifier (Molecular Devices, Union City, CA) and recorded at 28–30°C temperature. Micropipettes (4–6 MΩ) were pulled from borosilicate glass capillaries through a vertical puller. The cell capacitance and series resistance were left uncompensated. Experiments in which series resistance changed more than 20% and did not remain below 20 MΩ (an average 10–15 MΩ) were discarded. Signals were low-pass filtered at 2 kHz, sampled at 20kHz and analyzed with Digidata 1550B (Molecular Devices). Recordings were performed in visually identified CA1 pyramidal neurons. Synaptic basal transmission was recorded at -70 mV using the following internal solution: 130mM Cs-Gluconate, 1mM EGTA, 20mM HEPES, 2mM MgCl2, 4mM NaCl, 2mM Mg-ATP, 0.5mM Tris-GTP. The miniature excitatory and inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs and mIPSCs respectively) were simultaneously recorded on the same pyramidal neurons at different membrane potentials: mEPSCs at -70 and mIPSCs at +20 mV. Spontaneous synaptic transmission was recorded in the presence of tetrodotoxin (TTX) to block action potential-dependent activity.

Confocal microscopy and imaging analysis

Confocal images were acquired with a TCS-SP8 (Leica) laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with HC PL APO CS2 40x/1,30, 63x/1,40 or 20x/0,75 oil immersion objectives (Leica). Hybrid detectors were used when necessary and emission filter bandwidths together with sequential scanning acquisition were set up to avoid spectral overlap between fluorophores. Images were acquired as z-stack, with z-step size of 0.35μm (for synaptic signal), 0.5μm (for Trem2 signal) or 1μm (for cell count and IFNAR1 signal). Tilescan acquisition of hippocampus was set up exploiting a DMi8 LASx Thunder Imager (Leica) with HC PL Fluotar 40x/1,30 oil immersion objective, taking advantage of the Leica deconvolving algorithms during the acquisition process. Single tilescans were produced with 10% overlap to ease the merging procedure. Scale bars are reported in the figure legends.

Microglia density analysis

Microglia density was estimated on ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda) performing z-projection (max intensity) of the “Iba1 channel”. Iba1+ cells in the hippocampal CA1 region were counted with the multi-point function and normalized on the CA1 area (field of acquisition).

IFN-I receptor and Stat1 quantification

IFN-I receptor and Stat1 density has been performed on ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda) and determined as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the selected Region of Interest (ROI). For each experiment, fixed threshold values were set across all conditions.

vGluT1, vGAT and PSD95 quantification

vGluT1 and PSD95 density in the stratum radiatum of the hippocampal CA1 region was assessed on ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda) as area fraction of the selected Region of Interest (ROI). For each experiment, fixed threshold values for vGluT1, vGAT and PSD95 signal were set across all conditions.

Map2 quantification

Map2 density in the stratum radiatum of the hippocampal CA1 region was assessed on ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda) as area fraction of the selected Region of Interest (ROI) in the resulting merged image from the tilescan acquisition.

NeuN quantification

Confocal images were analyzed on ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda) to count NeuN+DAPI+ neurons in the hippocampal pyramidal layer of mice at P90 with the multi-point function and normalized on the pyramidal layer area, automatically measured using the wand tracing tool on ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda). The resulting merged image from the tilescan acquisition was analyzed on ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda) to automatically measure the NeuN+ area in the hippocampal pyramidal layer of P18 mice with the wand tracing tool.

Adam10, Adam17 and Stat1 quantification in microglia

3D rendering and volumetric analysis were performed using Imaris Software (Oxford Instruments, v. 9.7.2.) with the built-in Surface module as previously described.43,44,125 Briefly, Iba1 volume was quantified by applying 3D surface rendering of confocal stacks using same settings (fixed thresholds of intensity and voxel) within each experiment. Only Adam10, Adam17 and Stat1 signal co-localizing with Iba1 was considered using the mask function in Imaris. A 3D surface rendering of Adam10 and Adam17 was generated and their signal was quantified as MFI.

Ex vivo synapses engulfment

3D rendering and volumetric analysis were performed using Imaris Software (Oxford Instruments, v. 9.7.2.) as described above. Briefly, CD68 and Iba1 volumes were quantified by applying 3D surface rendering of confocal stacks in their respective channels, using same settings (fixed thresholds of intensity and voxel) within each experiment. For quantification of PSD95 engulfment by microglia, only PSD95+ structures present within microglial CD68+ phagolysosomes were considered. This procedure was used to ensure that only puncta entirely phagocytosed by microglia were included in the analysis. To this aim, a new channel for “engulfed PSD95” was created, by using the mask function in Imaris, masking the PSD95 signal within CD68+ structures. To account for variations in cell size, the amount of ‘engulfed PSD95 in CD68’ was normalized to the total volume of the phagocyte (given by Iba1+ total volume).

Ex vivo microglial-mediated cell engulfment quantification

Iba1+ cells bearing ball-and-chain structures (described as phagocytic cups), originally identified by7 were counted in the subgranular zone (SGZ) in the dentate gyrus (DG) using the multi-point function on ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda). Data are shown as the percentage of Iba1+ cells with phagocytic cups normalized on the total number of Iba1+ cells in the field of acquisition.

Trem2 quantification

3D rendering and volumetric analysis were performed using Imaris Software (Oxford Instruments, v. 9.7.2.) as described above. Briefly, Iba1 volume was quantified by applying 3D surface rendering of confocal stacks using same settings (fixed thresholds of intensity and voxel) within each experiment. Only Trem2 signal co-localizing with Iba1 was considered using the mask function in Imaris. Trem2 was quantified as the volume of the resulting 3D surface render. To account for variations in cell size, the amount of Trem2 was normalized to the total volume of the phagocyte (given by Iba1+ total volume).

Western Blotting

Brain cortices of embryonic day (E) E16 and P18 mice were homogenized (30 Hz for 2 minutes) with the Tissue Lyser system (Qiagen) in lysis buffer (Tris HCl 50 mM pH 7.4, Triton 0.1%, EDTA 2mM, PMFS 1mM, protease inhibitor cocktail). Proteins were then blotted (Fast Blot, Biorad) on nitrocellulose membrane. Upon blocking with 5% BSA in TBS-Tween 0.1% for one hour RT, the following primary antibodies were used: CDk8 (cat. 4101, G398, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000, Rabbit polyclonal), Stat1 (cat.14994, D1K9Y, Cell Signaling Technology, rabbit monoclonal, 1:1000), p-Stat1 (Ser727) (cat. 9177, Cell Signaling Technology, rabbit polyclonal, 1:1000), Actin (cat. sc-1615, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, goat polyclonal, 1:5000), all diluted in 5% BSA in TBS-Tween 0.1% and incubated overnight at 4°C. p-Stat1 antibody was incubated after stripping performed with Restore Plus Western blot stripping buffer (cat. 46430, ThermoFisher Scientific) following the manufacturer instruction. Matching secondary antibodies (cat. 31460, goat α-rabbit HRP, cat. 31402, rabbit α-goat HRP, 1:10000, ThermoFisher Scientific) for one hour RT in 5% BSA in TBS-Tween 0.1%. Chemiluminescent signal was carried out by ECL clarity (cat. 1705061, Biorad) or ECL femto (cat. PI34095, ThermoFisher Scientific). Chemiluminescence was detected by a chemidoc system (Biorad) and the intensity of the signal was quantified by Image Lab software (Biorad).

Flow Cytometry

Microglia

CTRL and Poly(I:C) offspring at P18 were anesthetized with a ketamine (100 mg/kg)/xylazine (20 mg/kg) mixture, transcardially perfused with ice-cold saline solution and the brain was harvested. Hippocampi and cortices from both the hemispheres were dissected on ice and quickly homogenized in Dounce buffer (15 mM HEPES, 0.5% glucose, 1% DNAse-I in HBSS) using a 1-mL or 3-mL Dounce homogenizer. All reagents and instruments were kept at 4 °C and all procedures were performed at <4 °C to avoid cell activation. After filtration through a 70μm cell strainer (BD Biosciences), the homogenates were resuspended in 30% isotonic Percoll and spun down at 1200g for 30 min. The floating myelin layer was removed, the cell pellet was resuspended in HBSS (cat.14180046, ThermoFisher Scientific) and further centrifuged at 500g for 10 minutes at 4 °C. Total cell count was determined, cells were resuspended in FACS buffer (1X PBS, 2% FBS) and immunolabeled for FACS analysis.

Cells were centrifuged at 400g for 5 minutes, resuspended in ice-cold FACS buffer and immunolabeled for flow cytometric analysis.

For mouse plasma collection, pups at P18 and adult mice were anesthetized with a ketamine (100 mg/kg)/xylazine (20 mg/kg) mixture. A small cut was performed in the right atrium and blood was collected in EDTA coated vacutainer (cat. 368856, BD Biosciences) and placed on ice. Plasmatic IL-6 level was assayed by ELISA (cat. DY406-05, R&D Systems) and IFNβ level in blood plasma by ProQuantum assay following the manufacturer protocol (cat. A47435, ThermoFisher Scientific). Plasmatic sTREM2 levels were measured as follows: 96-MWs were coated overnight at 4°C with the capture antibody (Mouse TREM2 antibody, cat. MAB1729, R&D Systems, Monoclonal Rat IgG) diluted in PBS. The ΔΔCt method was applied to determine differences in gene expression levels after normalization to the arithmetic mean of Gapdh as an internal standard. All data are mean of duplicates, and the standard errors of the mean were calculated between duplicates. qPCR was performed using ViiA7 (Applied Biosystems).

Bulk RNA-sequencing (RNAseq) of Schizophrenic patients and controls

Total RNA from the superior temporal gyrus region of 35 control subjects and 34 schizophrenic patients was isolated from brain tissues using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Cat. 74106, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. TapeStation 4200 System (Agilent Technologies) was used to perform quality control of the RNA concentration, purity, and degradation based on the estimated RNA integrity Number (RIN), and DV200. Extracted RNA (10μg) was converted to cDNA by PCR using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcriptase kit (cat. 4368814, Applied Biosystems). Samples were sequenced by an Illumina HiSeq 4000 Systems Technology with a read length of 1×150 bp. STAR (v.2.6.0)126 was used to align the RNA sequences to the human reference genome: GRCh38.p13 (hg38). The quality of RNA reads including the percentage of mapped reads and length of sequence fragments was performed. Aligned sequences were processed using SAMtools (v.1.9).126 Protein coding genes were selected for further analyses.

Gene Orthology Mapping Using Ensembl

For mouse to human orthologs conversion, we used the Ensembl dataset (Ensembl 114), using as a mouse reference genome (GRCm39). A list of 1,918 mouse protein-coding genes was used as input (p-value ≤ 0.05). For each gene, orthologous relationships in human were queried using “Homology” annotations from Ensembl. The mapping was performed by retrieving Ensembl gene IDs for both species. We then probed the list of 86 human genes (p-value ≤0.05), representing the DEGs in common between the SCZ versus CTRL dataset and a published human IFN-I stimulated genes (ISGs) signature, with the mouse ortholog gene table (representing the DEGs in Poly(I:C) vs CTRL in the microglia cluster from snRNAseq), and we obtained 19 shared genes between the two datasets, represented as a heatmap in Figure 7G. In the few instances where the human ortholog had a one-to-many relationship with mouse, we calculated the average fold-change of the mouse orthologs DEGs when reporting them in Figure 7G.

Bulk RNA-sequencing (RNAseq) of mouse microglia in vitro

Trem2+/+ and Trem2-/- primary mouse microglia cultures from P1-3 pups were obtained from 5/6 pups per genotype. Trem2+/+ and Trem2-/- microglia were replated, treated with Poly(I:C) [20 μg/mL] or vehicle for 24h and collected for RNA isolation (n=2 replicates per condition). Total RNA was isolated from microglia using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Cat. 74106, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Total RNA integrity was determined using TapeStation 4200 System (Agilent Technologies). Library preparation was performed with 10ng of total RNA with a Bioanalyzer RIN score greater than 8.0. Double stranded-cDNA was prepared using the SMARTer Ultra Low RNA kit for Illumina Sequencing (Takara-Clontech) per manufacturer's protocol. Fragments were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq-6000 using paired end reads extending 150 bases. RNA-seq reads were then aligned and quantitated to the Ensembl release 101 primary assembly with an Illumina DRAGEN Bio-IT on-premise server running version 3.9.3-8 software. RNA-Seq reads were mapped to the mouse genome (GRCm38, version M12 (Ensembl 87)) with STAR.127 Reads were assigned to genes using FeatureCounts.128 For the differential expression analyses DESeq2124 with default parameters was used. Genes that had less than 5 counts in at least 3 samples were filtered. The gene set enrichment analysis was performed by EnrichR using protein-coding DEG (Padj < 0.05), with specific databases from KEGG 2019 Mouse, GO Biological Process 2023, GO cellular Component 2023, Reactome 2022, MSigDB Hallmark 2020.

Single nucleus RNA sequencing of mouse hippocampi

Nuclei isolation, library preparation and sequencing

Hippocampi from one hemisphere from male offspring at P18 (CTRL Trem2+/+ N=2; Poly(I:C) Trem2+/+ N=4; CTRL Trem2-/- N=2; Poly(I:C) Trem2+/+ N=3) were gently dissected and snap-frozen in dry ice. Nuclei were isolated as previously described.129 In brief, frozen hippocampi were Dounce-homogenized with supplemented homogenization buffer (SHB; Homogenization Buffer, 10% spermine, 10% spermidine; 1% RNASIN and 0.1mM EDTA) for purification of nuclei using density gradient centrifugation with Iodixanol solutions of varying concentrations. The resulting nuclei were visually inspected for appearance and cell lysis using trypan blue, and the concentration was adjusted to 1,000 nuclei/μL for snRNA-seq.

The 10X Genomics Chromium platform was used to construct a snRNA-seq library from the mouse hippocampi. Approximately 10,000 nuclei per sample were loaded onto the Chromium single cell chips (10X Genomics single-nucleus cDNA libraries were constructed and dual-indexed using the Chromium Single Cell 3’ Reagents Kit v3.1 and corresponding user guide (10X Genomics). All samples were pooled in equimolar ratios and sequenced on a NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina) at the sequencing facility of the MGI at Washington University in St. Louis UMCG. Libraries were sequenced to a saturation of >30%.

Single nucleus RNA-seq data analysis

After initial quality control applied to individual samples in CellRanger-arc-2.0.2, DecontX was used to remove ambient RNA contamination. The Seurat 5.0.3 package in R 4.3.3 was used for subsequent analysis. Mitochondrial and ribosomal gene content were calculated per nucleus. To ensure sample quality, doublet removal was performed using DoubletFinder 2.0.4. After doublet removal, nuclei with UMI counts between 300 to 50000 (median = 9769), and the feature counts between 300 to 10000 (median = 3231) were retained. Nuclei with mitochondrial genes higher than 10% were removed from the merged dataset. The merged data was normalized and integrated using SCTransform, regressing out total ribosomal and mitochondrial percentages. The integrated data was clustered with the first 30 PCs and a resolution of 0.2. For cell type annotation, a customized cell type marker set was applied to identified clusters with AddModuleScore, and cluster identities were manually assigned, and the ambiguous clusters were removed. After filtering, a total of 47,878 nuclei remained for downstream analysis. After cell type annotation, the dataset contains nuclei with 22,065 Glutamatergic neurons (Slc17a7, Slc17a6) and 5642 GABAergic neurons (Gad1, Gad2, Dlx2, Dlx6os1), 4283 microglia (Trem2, Tmem119, Iba1, Cxc3r1, Csf1r, and Tyrobp) and 99 border associated macrophages (BAMs) (Mrc1, Ms4a7), 7173 astrocytes (Aqp4, Cldn10, Lcat), 34 endothelial cells (Cldn5, Flt1, Ly6c1), 4122 oligodendrocytes precursors cells (OPCs) (Pdgfra, Cspg4, Lhfpl3) and 4460 oligodendrocytes (Mbp, Mag, Mog, Cldn11). Cell-type proportions were calculated for each mouse (Figure 3C), and pairwise t-tests were conducted to determine whether any conditions were overrepresented. Microglia and border associated macrophages (BAM) were subset from the integrated datasets and re-integrated with Harmony integration, followed by clustering with the first 20 PCs and resolution of 0.2. Microglia/macrophage cluster proportions within each mouse were calculated and tested for overrepresentation. The microglia/BAM markers were identified by FindMarkers function. The cluster markers for BAM are determined by comparing with all remaining microglia subclusters, and the cluster markers for remaining microglia subclusters were compared and identified without BAM (Table S5).

Downstream analyses

Differential expressed genes (DEG) analysis between experimental conditions (Poly(I:C) vs. vehicle) was performed individually in the Trem2+/+ and Trem2-/- groups on the major cell types and microglia subclusters using the FindMarkers function. The mean expression, log2 fold change, and adjusted p-values of DEG (FDR < 0.05) were used to generate volcano plots. The DEG list for Poly(I:C) vs vehicles within Trem2+/+ and Trem2-/- are compared using VennDiagram 1.7.3. The DEGs derived from individual comparison or intersection were taken for following pathway analysis. Pathway analyses were performed separately on up- and down-regulated DEG (FDR < 0.05) within each comparison using ToppGene and EnrichR 3.2, with databases selection of KEGG_2019_Mouse,GO_Biological_Process_2023,GO_Cellular_Component_2023,GO_Molecular_Function_2023, Reactome_2022, MSigDB_Hallmark_2020. The significant pathways (FDR < 0.05) were documented in the supplemental files.

Quantification and Statistical Analysis

Data representation, statistical testing and outliers’ identification were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software). Results are represented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For each graph, the number of mice is indicated as “N”, and the number of observations is indicated as “n”. The distribution of data in each set of experiments was first tested for normality. Normally distributed data were analyzed by two-tailed Unpaired T-test (Student’s T-test) when comparing two groups or by one- or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc multiple comparisons test, when comparing multiple groups. To compare datasets that failed normality test, Mann-Whitney test or Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test were used as indicated in figure legends. Differences were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05 and are indicated with ∗; those at p < 0.01 are indicated with ∗∗; those at p < 0.001 are indicated with ∗∗∗; those at p < 0.0001 are indicated with ∗∗∗∗. Detailed statistics used, p-values and number of biological replicates are reported in each figure legend.
