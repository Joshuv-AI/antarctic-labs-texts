Factors affecting hospital-acquired infections in hospitalized patients in iran: a qualitative study
Authors: Zohreh Alinasab, Mohammad-Ali Jahani, Ghahraman Mahmoudi, Jamshid Yazdani Charati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHospital-acquired infections (HAIs) are a major and complex challenge in healthcare systems, particularly in developing countries, causing significant harm to patients and the community. This study aimed to explore the key factors contributing to HAIs in hospital settings.MethodsUsing a Grounded Theory approach, in-depth semi-structured interviews were conducted in 2025 with 30 experts and specialists in HAIs. Snowball sampling continued until data saturation was reached. Participants were required to have at least one year of relevant experience and provide informed consent.ResultsAnalysis identified three main themes, six subcategories, and 54 key statements. The themes were service recipients (patients), hospital environment, and service providers. Patient-related factors included health status and care processes. Hospital environment factors comprised management, physical structure, and hygiene. Service provider factors involved organizational and managerial practices.ConclusionIdentifying the causes of HAIs can guide hospital managers and policymakers in implementing effective infection control programs. Targeted interventions based on these findings can reduce infection rates, lower healthcare costs, and enhance patient safety in hospital settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07611-7.Keywords: Hospital-acquired infection, Hospital, Hospital care system, Prevention, Hospitalized patients, Qualitative study

Introduction

Hospitals provide a range of diagnostic and therapeutic services to patients [1]. However, these interventions can lead to hospital-acquired infections (HAIs), which may result in mortality [2]. HAIs are infections not present or incubating at admission, developing 48 h after admission, three days post-discharge, or 30 days after surgery [3]. Common types include urinary tract infections, surgical site infections, pneumonia, and bloodstream infections [4]. Globally, HAIs are increasing, contributing significantly to patient mortality [5]. In developed countries, 7–10% of hospitalized patients acquire HAIs, with an average of 4–5 additional hospital days [6]. The World Health Organization recognizes HAIs as a major cause of disability and death, and 27–33% of surgical readmissions are post-operative infections [7]. Patients with HAIs experience organ dysfunction, longer stays, and higher mortality, making HAIs a critical treatment complication [8]. In the United States, HAIs cause approximately 80,000 deaths annually [9], with prevalence ranging from 3.5 to 12% in developed countries and 9.7–19.1% in developing countries [10]. Iran faces similar challenges, with HAIs imposing substantial economic burdens on healthcare systems [11]. Preventing HAIs reduces both mortality and healthcare costs [12]. Studies show that education of staff and patients, post-discharge follow-up [13], adherence to hand hygiene, and use of protective equipment [14] are effective in preventing infections. Awareness among healthcare personnel of infection causes, transmission, and symptoms is crucial for prevention and treatment [15]. HAIs also cause psychological stress, disability, and reduced quality of life [16], while diverting resources from primary and secondary care to infection control [17]. Therefore, prevention is the most cost-effective strategy, requiring staff awareness and proper application of infection control measures. Understanding infection causes is essential for developing and implementing effective hospital control programs [18]. Although many international studies have examined the causes of healthcare-associated infections, most have been conducted in high-income countries with different healthcare systems and cultural contexts. In Iran, these infections remain a major challenge, with a relatively higher prevalence and contributing factors such as overcrowded wards, staff shortages, limited resources, and cultural practices like frequent family presence in hospital wards. Yet, few context-specific studies have explored these determinants in depth. This study addresses this gap and provides evidence to guide locally appropriate infection prevention strategies. Considering the importance of HAIs, this study aimed to identify factors influencing hospital-acquired infections in hospitalized patients in Iran.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study employed a Grounded Theory approach and was conducted in 2025 to identify factors influencing hospital-acquired infections (HAIs).

Interview guide

Semi-structured interview questions were developed based on a literature review and with the assistance of faculty members holding the academic rank of Professor with expertise in infection control and clinical practice.The interview was developed specifically for this study. In this study, professors’ refers to faculty members holding academic positions with the rank of Professor in medical and nursing schools, with expertise in infection control and clinical practice. (see Supplementary File 1).

Participants

The research community consisted of experts and specialists active in healthcare and hospital-acquired infection. To obtain the most valuable samples, a snowball sampling method was utilized. Inclusion criteria required participants to have at least one year of experience in their current role related to hospital infection prevention programs and a voluntary willingness to participate. Unwillingness to participate served as an exclusion criterion.

Data collection

Interviews were transcribed immediately after each session to identify data saturation and ensure accuracy. Each recorded file was listened to multiple times before transcription. To establish interview credibility, the researcher conducted several pilot interviews. Subsequently, for data validity and methodological accuracy, the initial interviews were reviewed by professors. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new themes or categories emerged. Saturation was achieved after 30 interviews. and all interviews were recorded. Interview durations ranged from 35 to 45 min. The interviews utilized exploratory and provocative questions to delve into details and factors influencing HAI etiology.

Focus of interviews

Interviews included exploratory questions such as: “What are the patient-related factors contributing to hospital infections?” “What are the staff-related factors contributing to hospital infections?” “What are the environmental factors related to infection?”

Data analysis

Researchers paid close attention to the credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability of the data. To enhance the study’s credibility, the researcher engaged sufficiently with participants, collecting valid information and confirming it with them. To strengthen data dependability, data collection and analysis steps were repeated by the researcher, and input and review from expert individuals were utilized. To increase data confirmability, review, verification, and, if necessary, feedback from collaborating faculty members were employed. Finally, regarding the transferability of study data, the researchers provided a detailed description to facilitate its examination and application in other contexts. In line with this, the participants’ own audio interview files, along with the written transcripts, were shared with them for direct quotation and to avoid personal interpretation by the researcher, ensuring data accuracy was confirmed.Grounded Theory was used for data analysis, after transcription, the data were analyzed using an expert panel approach. employing open, axial, and selective coding to extract themes, categories, and subcategories. Open coding, the first level of analysis, involved initial data breakdown where data was broken down into its smallest units, i.e., subcategories (items). In axial coding, the second level, existing patterns in the data were identified, and categories were determined. This stage necessitated constant comparison of data and finding similarities and differences to differentiate data and place it into appropriate categories. Selective coding, the third level, was the subsequent step where, immediately upon obtaining recorded interviews, the text was carefully studied multiple times by two members of the research team, and semantic units were marked. In this stage, initial coding used participants’ own words and the researchers’ interpretations of statements and documents. Based on similarities and framework suitability, these were placed into a specific category. In the next stage, these categories were compared with each other, and those with similar characteristics were combined into broader categories, leading to the formation of themes.

Results

Participants in this study included 12 women and 18 men, with work experience ranging from 4 to 37 years (mean = 21.83 ± 8.54 years) (Table 1). The key factors influencing the acquisition of hospital-acquired infections (HAIs) were organized into 3 core themes, 6 categories, and 54 items (statements) (Table 2; Fig. 1). The extracted core themes were service recipient (patient), service provider, and hospital environment.

Classification of factors affecting hospital infections

Service recipient (patient)

The “service recipient” theme included patient health status as well as care and treatment factors. Expert interviews revealed that patients with weakened immune systems, those on corticosteroid therapy, individuals with underlying diseases, high-risk groups, patients with high body mass index (BMI), or malnutrition were more susceptible to HAIs.

Interviewee 1 stated:“There are many factors that cause hospital-acquired infections, such as immune system deficiency, being at both ends of the age spectrum, underlying diseases like diabetes, cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy, patients on long-term corticosteroid therapy, or patients with HIV, as well as hospitalized patients with Foley catheters, those who are intubated, or those using vascular catheters.”

In addition, the delivery of care and treatment significantly influenced HAIs. All participants agreed that prolonged hospital stays, inappropriate antibiotic use, major surgeries and invasive procedures, ICU admissions, and poor oral hygiene or inadequate bed bathing for ICU patients increased infection risk. Specific invasive procedures associated with HAIs included urinary catheterization, Shaldon catheter insertion, intubation, failure to observe sterile techniques, and the use of wound drains.

Hospital environment

The hospital environment was identified as a key factor contributing to HAIs and included the subcategories environmental hygiene and hospital management and physical structure. Regarding management and physical structure, participants highlighted factors such as: multiple patients per room, enclosed or poorly ventilated spaces, ongoing construction, high traffic in restricted areas, visitors in ICUs, absence of separate clean and dirty utility rooms, natural flowers, blocked windows, improper food preparation processes, non-use of isolation rooms, and inadequate laundry facilities.

Interviewee 3 remarked:“Currently, hospitals worldwide are built by engineers specializing in this field. The hospital’s ventilation system, HEPA system, negative-pressure isolation rooms, clean and dirty utility rooms, the distance from the operating room to CSSD, the distance between beds, and even separate restrooms for infectious and non-infectious patients are all extremely important. Construction within hospitals leads to dust, which is a particular issue in developing countries and increases the likelihood of infections.”

Regarding environmental hygiene, participants noted that improper waste disposal, poor cleaning practices, contaminated medical equipment, insufficient disinfection resources, low-quality cleaning agents, unpleasant disinfectant odors, and the presence of resistant bacteria were major contributors to HAIs.

Interviewee 4 commented:“Waste management and segregation are very important. Bins must be pedal-operated and clearly labeled, especially for infectious waste. Temporary waste storage must meet standards, and waste should be disposed of daily. All food entering the hospital must be monitored, kitchen staff must maintain personal hygiene, and clean utensils should be used for distribution.”

Service provider

Service providers were associated with managerial factors and organizational and process-related factors. Regarding organizational and process-related factors, participants highlighted insufficient staff training, high workload and fatigue, use of artificial nails, jewelry, and mobile phones by staff, high bed occupancy rates, teaching hospital status, non-adherence to sterile techniques, non-use of PPE, non-compliance with hygiene protocols, and improper staffing ratios.

Interviewee 6 stated: “Wearing jewelry and accessories, and having long or artificial nails, prevents proper handwashing and increases the risk of hospital-acquired infections. Healthcare staff must have the necessary knowledge to reduce infections by observing hygiene practices, especially in the operating room.”

Interviewee 7 added: “The workload is not commensurate with personnel working hours, meaning that high volume within limited hours leads to actions like handwashing being performed less frequently.” Managerial factors contributing to HAIs included nursing staff shortages, lack of infection control budgets, ineffective communication within treatment teams, unsupervised residents in operating rooms, incomplete staff vaccination, insufficient oversight of hygiene practices, rotation of trained personnel, limited awareness among hospital managers, and inadequate monitoring of cleaning standards.

Interviewee 8 noted: “The CSSD unit is very important in infection control, and trained personnel should be utilized. Unfortunately, untrained staff are sometimes used, which increases the risk of infections.”

Discussion

This qualitative study explored factors contributing to hospital-acquired infections (HAIs) from the perspective of healthcare experts in Iran. The findings highlighted three main categories: patient-related, provider-related, and hospital environment factors. For patients, health status, comorbidities, and type of treatment were key. Immune suppression, malnutrition, prior infection, chronic diseases, and diabetes were repeatedly reported as major risks [17, 19–23]. Older age, high body mass index, prolonged hospitalization, and invasive procedures such as surgery, catheterization, and mechanical ventilation further increased susceptibility [17, 18, 22–26]. Excessive antibiotic use was also linked to HAIs [20]. Conversely, preventive measures such as sterile towels for bed baths or daily bathing reduced infection rates [5, 27]. Overall, patient characteristics, treatment type, and length of hospital stay significantly influence HAI risk, with elderly or immunocompromised patients being the most vulnerable.

The hospital environment, including hygiene practices, management, and physical structure, has a direct effect on patient safety. Many pathogens can survive on surfaces for long periods, making environmental hygiene a critical factor in HAIs [28–30]. Effective disinfection methods, such as gaseous hydrogen peroxide, are cost-effective for C. difficile control [31], and exposure to contaminated rooms significantly increases infection risk [32]. Environmental cleaning also helps prevent infections caused by resistant microorganisms [33]. Contaminated equipment like stethoscopes contributes to pathogen transmission [34], while interventions such as HEPA filters, negative-pressure rooms, and air purification systems reduce infection risks, particularly in vulnerable units [35–38]. Overall, proper cleaning and disinfection of hospital environments are essential to prevent pathogen transmission and protect both patients and Service provider-related factors included managerial, organizational, and process-related issues. Hand hygiene compliance was consistently identified as a critical preventive measure [39–41]. Barriers such as wearing jewelry [41], inadequate numbers of infection control nurses [42], or insufficient knowledge and training among staff increased HAI risk [43, 44]. Conversely, adherence to protocols, use of protective equipment, and continuous education were protective [14, 43, 44]. Creating a strong safety culture, managerial commitment, effective communication, and reducing nurse workload were also emphasized [45–47]. Additionally, surgeries performed by specialists showed lower infection rates than those by assistants [48]. In general, the quality of care including proper infection control practices, staff training, and managerial oversight directly influences HAI risk. Strengthening these aspects through education, staffing, and leadership is key to prevention.

Conclusion

Protecting hospitalized patients’ health depends on identifying the factors that cause and perpetuate HAIs. Understanding patient, provider, and environmental contributors, along with implementing preventive strategies, can reduce infection rates, lower healthcare costs, and optimize resource use. Further research is recommended to develop effective HAI prevention programs, especially in low- and middle-income countries. The innovative aspect of this study is in including a diverse group of participants with managerial backgrounds (nurses, physicians, infection control officers, managers, and policymakers) and identifying managerial and organizational determinants of hospital infections that have received little attention in previous research.

Limitation

This qualitative study explored the factors contributing to hospital-acquired infections in Iran through interviews with a diverse group of participants. While this diversity enriched the data and provided multiple perspectives, several limitations should be noted. First, each participant primarily spoke from their own professional experience, which may limit the comprehensiveness of the findings. Second, the study was conducted in a specific hospital context, which may affect the transferability of the results to other healthcare settings. Third, the reliance on self-reported information may introduce recall bias or social desirability bias. Finally, although the analysis followed rigorous Grounded Theory procedures, the interpretation of qualitative data inherently involves researcher judgment, which may influence the categorization of themes. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the complex factors influencing hospital-acquired infections from multiple perspectives.
