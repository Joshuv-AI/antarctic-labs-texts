Recent progress of NIR light-controlled microneedles and therapeutic applications: A review study
Authors: Nafiseh Farhadian, Reza Abachizadeh, Shaghayegh Kouhestani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransdermal drug delivery presents an appealing alternative to traditional administration methods like oral and injection routes, as it circumvents first-pass metabolism, minimizes pain and infection risks, and enhances patient compliance. Nonetheless, the skin barrier remains a significant obstacle to delivering most medications through the skin. Microneedles (MNs) represent an innovative technology that can address this issue by forming tiny pores in the skin, facilitating the transport of drugs or biomolecules. MNs are manufactured from various materials and come in different designs and classifications. They can also be activated by external stimuli, such as near-infrared (NIR) light, which excels over other light types for this application due to its deeper tissue penetration, allowing for on-demand and precise temporal drug delivery. This review primarily concentrates on NIR light-controlled MNs, which respond to NIR light exposure to release drugs. Here, the fundamental concepts and mechanisms behind NIR light-controlled MNs are introduced, which utilize NIR light-sensitive materials or devices that alter their properties or functions in response to NIR light exposure. Benefits and difficulties associated with NIR light-controlled MNs, such as their extensive penetration capability, low toxicity, and accurate control, are examined. The potential for integrating NIR light-controlled MNs with other therapeutic strategies to boost their efficacy will be investigated. Recent advancements and applications of NIR light-controlled MNs in diverse areas, including diabetes management, cancer therapy, and wound healing, are reviewed. Finally, the future directions for their application will be addressed.

Introduction

Various methods exist for drug delivery into the human body, one of which is the transdermal drug delivery (TDD) method that aims to administer drugs through the skin layers [1]. The skin, being the largest organ in the human body and abundant in body fluids, represents a viable pathway for drug administration [2]. Leveraging the skin as the site for drug delivery is an appealing choice for distributing therapeutics such as vaccines, medications, biomolecules, and challenging small molecules [3]. Although this method offers benefits such as avoiding first-pass metabolism and the severe chemical conditions of the gastrointestinal tract found in oral administration, the application of skin for therapeutic delivery is still not widely adopted due to its notable barrier characteristics [4, 5].

Among the various TDD techniques, Microneedles (MNs) have garnered interest from numerous research organizations and companies [3], and are increasingly recognized for their cost-effective approach and capability to deliver medication into the deeper layers of the skin for pain management [6], which is why they are viewed as effective, painless, and reliable drug delivery systems [7]. The protective, inflammatory, and immune characteristics of the skin make the MN delivery system a compelling alternative to traditional drug delivery methods, addressing their limitations [3]. Controlled release of drugs can ensure prolonged retention times for chronic pain treatment. Hence, the transdermal route also emerges as an option to mitigate drug misuse and provide long-lasting pain relief [8].

Near-infrared (NIR) light-controlled MNs are a special class of MNs that offer controlled drug release by responding to NIR light, and can be used in different applications in disease treatment. These MNs’ interaction with NIR light can offer a promising approach for drug delivery applications.

This review begins by examining MNs, including their definition, types, fabrication methods, and certification standards. Next, it will detail NIR light-controlled MNs, along with a discussion of the materials utilized in these devices, as they play a crucial role in their functionality and efficiency. Furthermore, the importance and applications of NIR light-controlled MNs as a novel drug delivery system will be highlighted. Lastly, their roles in drug delivery for diabetes management, cancer therapy, Parkinson’s disease treatment, wound healing, and the prevention of heart failure will be explored.

What are MNs?

MNs are small needle arrays used for minimally or non-invasive transdermal drug delivery [3, 9]. They typically have conical or pyramidal shapes and are applied to the skin via adhesive patches [10]. Made from materials such as biomaterials, metals, polymers, ceramics, and composites, they are designed to penetrate the stratum corneum for various purposes [11–13]. Their size ranges from 25–2000 µm [14], allowing entry through the stratum corneum and viable epidermis without reaching deep dermal tissue or damaging nerves [15, 16]. MNs reduce or eliminate injection discomfort during drug delivery [14] and effectively transport larger molecules through invasive pathways [6]. They also support continuous drug administration, reducing dosing frequency, such as for insulin in diabetic patients [14, 17]. Additionally, they can be utilized to administer bioactive substances, vaccines, and pharmaceutical compounds, and are also capable of gathering bio-signals and materials from the body with minimal invasiveness [18].

Classification and fabrication of MNs

MNs are typically categorized into five distinct types for diverse applications, including: solid, hollow, coated, hydrogel-forming, and dissolving [19, 20]. Each type has a distinct mechanism for delivering drugs into the skin. Solid MNs, made from materials like metals, silicon, or polymers [1, 12], create micro-incisions to improve permeability, dermal delivery, bioavailability, and transport kinetics [14, 21], and are suitable for vaccines due to durability and strong antibody responses [22]. Hollow MNs enable active drug delivery into the dermal layer via a non-pressurized reservoir [18], delivering solutions or suspensions through precise channels for therapies aimed at specific tissues, while offering higher volume capacity than solid MNs [1, 14]. However, despite allowing controlled release, hollow MNs are difficult to fabricate and risk lumen blockage or needle breakage [23].

Coated MNs consist of a sharp, solid-core needle covered with a drug-containing solid film, where the drug dosage depends on coating thickness [1, 24], and they can both pierce the skin and deliver surface-applied drugs [25]. Nonetheless, their drug-loading capacity is limited, whereas dissolvable MNs can deliver larger doses [14]. Dissolvable MNs created using biodegradable polymers [26, 27] enable rapid macromolecule release [1], enhance the convenience of drug delivery by simplifying drug application, show high biocompatibility and loading capacity, and fully dissolve in the skin. Consequently, the “poke-and-release” approach makes them preferable compared to other methods [28–30]. Hydrogel-forming MNs work similarly, as they penetrate, release the drug, and don’t need disposal [19]. Their hydrophilicity allows absorption of interstitial fluid and swelling in the skin [9], so fabrication requires biocompatible materials with effective swelling behavior [31]. Application of MNs

As illustrated in Fig. 2, MNs have been widely researched for therapeutic applications, with uses spanning vaccine delivery, diagnosis, cosmetics, and drug administration [1, 24]. Needle-free vaccination using MNs addresses limitations of traditional methods, such as pain and fear of needles, and can enhance immune responses through material modification [25, 32, 33]. Various MN types, including dissolvable, hollow, and coated MNs, have been explored for vaccine delivery [28], with dissolvable MNs offering biocompatibility, scalability, and no biohazardous waste in administering vaccines for malaria, diphtheria, influenza, Hepatitis B, HIV, and polio [1, 25].

Graphical demonstration of MNS applications, including vaccine delivery, disease diagnosis, cosmetics, drug delivery, and disease treatment

Traditional fluid-based disease diagnostics methods require specialized tools, personnel, and can cause discomfort. On the other hand, MN-based sensing enables minimally invasive, continuous monitoring of biomarkers, extracting interstitial fluid or blood and avoiding blood-based complications [10, 34–36], with biosensors developed for glucose, lactate, alcohol, and beta-lactam detection [37–41]. Despite challenges like contamination, foreign body reactions, and sensitivity requirements [42], research continues to improve MN biosensors.

Cosmetic applications of MNs focus on enhancing the natural healing process of damaged skin and penetration of cosmeceuticals, leveraging minimally invasive channels to stimulate repair and improve absorption. They are recognized as an effective solution for cosmetic concerns related to aging, skin imperfections, acne, and wrinkles [12, 43]. In drug delivery, MN patches offer a painless, self-administered alternative to hypodermic injections for peptides, proteins, hormones, and natural substances hindered by first-pass metabolism [25], and also improve the efficiency of small-molecule transdermal delivery [10]. Insulin is a leading target for alternative delivery methods, and hollow silicone MNs have shown effective in vivo delivery, producing efficient insulin uptake and measurable glucose response [28, 44]. Various MN types have also been proposed to address the limitations of cancer immunotherapy, offering minimally invasive, controllable, and synergistic treatment options compared to surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy [25, 32].

Light wavelengths

Various types of light can be employed for distinct purposes, each with its own advantages, drawbacks, and wavelengths. The electromagnetic spectrum encompasses the total range of light available [45]. Visible light consists of an electromagnetic wave made up of a range of monochromatic wavelengths, each associated with a specific color, and it is detectable by the human eye, typically spanning from around 400 to 700 nanometers (nm) in wavelength. In contrast, ultraviolet (UV) light possesses a shorter wavelength, ranging from around 10 to 400 nm. Infrared (IR) light has longer wavelengths than visible light, generally extending from about 700 nm to 1 millimeter (mm). Near-infrared (NIR) light is part of the IR spectrum and has wavelengths ranging from approximately 700 to 2500 nm [46, 47].

Photons, the fundamental units of light, have wavelengths that correlate with the energy of the light; photons with shorter wavelengths carry higher energy. Consequently, UV light possesses greater energy than visible light and has sufficient energy to disrupt chemical bonds and facilitate photochemical reactions [46, 48]. For instance, UV light can be utilized in photocatalytic processes [49]. However, UV light is also known to cause harm to biological tissues, emphasizing the importance of shielding the skin from excessive UV exposure. In contrast, IR and NIR light have lower energy levels compared to visible and UV light. Under certain circumstances, IR light can also cause skin damage and accelerate aging [48].

NIR light offers the benefit of penetrating deeper into tissues, making it advantageous for biomedical uses, and it has been applied in various fields, including photothermal therapy (PTT) [50], photodynamic therapy (PDT), and photopolymerization [51].

PTT is a technique for destroying cancer cells by using materials that have high photothermal conversion efficiency to transform light energy into heat energy with the aid of an external light source [52]. In other words, PTT employs light-absorbing materials to produce heat and induce the death of affected cells, while PDT relies on photosensitizing agents to generate oxygen molecules in cells that then create reactive oxygen species (ROS). By incorporating photosensitizing agents or light-absorbing substances into MNs and utilizing NIR light, these MNs can deliver targeted and accurate treatments across various applications. Most agents used in photothermal therapy (PTT) and photodynamic therapy (PDT) absorb light within the visible or NIR spectrum [53].

NIR light is the preferred source, as recent research indicates that it penetrates biological tissues more effectively, hence NIR-absorbing materials for PTT show significant potential for clinical diagnosis and treatment of diseases [53, 54]. This approach allows phototherapies to have decreased toxicity to normal cells and tissues, while requiring a lower light dosage. When exposed to NIR light, phototherapeutic agents that exhibit slight toxicity in darkness become active, selectively destroying cancer cells while minimizing damage to adjacent healthy tissues. The use of photosensitizers with low toxicity levels in the dark and high extinction coefficients may permit the use of greater doses of phototherapeutic agents and NIR light, enhancing the therapeutic index. Through innovative NIR-responsive nanomaterials, PTT and PDT can induce programmed cell death in infected cells via the heat generated or reactive oxygen species (ROS) produced, respectively [55].

Among the various types of light, NIR light offers several benefits that make it a highly suitable option for NIR light-controlled MNs.

i.Enhanced deep tissue penetration: Recent research indicates that NIR light can penetrate biological tissues effectively [50], allowing it to reach greater depths compared to UV [56] or visible light [57].

Although every type of light presents its own advantages and disadvantages, NIR light is regarded as the most effective for use in light-controlled MNs because of its superior tissue penetration capabilities compared to other light wavelengths and its practicality in clinical settings.

NIR Light-controlled MNs

Principles and mechanisms

NIR light-responsive MNs represent an exciting technology for drug delivery purposes. These MNs are engineered to react to NIR light, offering benefits such as noninvasive applications, ease of control, and high precision in space and time [59]. The functioning of NIR light-responsive MNs relies on materials capable of emitting or absorbing NIR light, facilitating the controlled release of drugs or the activation of targeted biological processes. One method to accomplish NIR light-triggered drug release involves utilizing light-sensitive nanoplatforms. These platforms can be crafted to discharge drugs upon exposure to NIR light, allowing for accurate and regulated delivery. Systems that trigger drug release with NIR light often utilize two-photon absorption and photon-up-conversion phenomena. These processes entail the nano platform absorbing NIR light, which then initiates the release of the drug contained within [60, 61].

Research indicates that the penetration capability of the first NIR spectrum (NIR-I, 750–900 nm) is insufficient for reaching solid tumors due to significant tissue scattering. In contrast, the second NIR spectrum (NIR-II, 1000–1700 nm) provides deeper tissue penetration, higher radiation limits, and better tissue tolerance compared to NIR-I, highlighting its greater potential for applications in photothermal conversion [50]. Nonetheless, continuous research and developments in materials science are working to overcome the challenges related to NIR light as a stimulus and enhance the creation of efficient and effective NIR light-controlled MNs.

NIR light-operated MNs have been extensively investigated for a range of applications, including cancer drug delivery [62], diabetes management [63], and wound care [64]. Nonetheless, different uses necessitate the selection of various materials, as these play a crucial role in the efficacy and performance of the MNs. Consequently, numerous studies have proposed different materials based on the specific drug delivery method and desired results. Applications

The utilization of NIR light-controlled MNs varies based on the different diseases and therapeutic approaches. This section reviews various materials employed for NIR light-controlled MNs and their uses in treating various illnesses.

Diabetes treatment

Hu et al. proposed that a device utilizing NIR-triggered MN drug release could significantly benefit diabetes treatment, particularly for patients needing continuous drug administration. In their research, they created an NIR-triggered MN patch array designed for the transdermal delivery of metformin (Met), an anti-diabetic medication, in diabetic rats. They encapsulated Met and indocyanine green (ICG), a photothermal agent, in a polymer vesicle featuring an upper critical solution temperature (UCST) and combined these with a dissolvable polymer matrix to produce the MNs. By embedding the drug-loaded vesicles into the poly(vinyl pyrrolidone)/poly(vinyl alcohol) MNs, they successfully fabricated NIR-triggered MN patches. The study also showed that the polymer vesicles could release Met when subjected to temperature variations caused by NIR exposure, allowing the MNs to penetrate the skin and deliver the drug-loaded vesicles to the subcutaneous tissue [58]. When the temperature increased beyond the phase transition point, the UCST-type polymer transitioned from a contracted state to an extended form [58, 65]. The UCST-type polymer has a clearing point temperature of 41°C, indicating that it remains soluble in water below this temperature but becomes insoluble above it. The drug-loaded polymer vesicles exhibited remarkable NIR-triggered and temperature-responsive drug release in vitro because of the disintegration of polymer vesicles upon NIR exposure. Furthermore, the low cytotoxicity and minimal acute toxicity of the materials facilitated their potential clinical application [58].

In an investigation, Prussian blue nanoparticles (PB NPs) served as the photothermal conversion agent, while metformin was employed as the hypoglycemic agent, both embedded into removable arrowheads made of polyvinyl alcohol and sucrose (PVA/Suc). The MN arrowheads were positioned on soluble solid supporting substrates made from polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP). The surface of the manufactured MNs was coated with lauric acid (LA). When exposed to NIR light, the LA coating melted due to its low melting point and the effective photothermal conversion capabilities of PB NPs. The created MNs enabled the encapsulated metformin to be released in a sequenced and sustained fashion. The hypoglycemic response observed in diabetic rats induced by streptozotocin (STZ) demonstrated that metformin could be released on demand with NIR exposure. Also, no signs of tissue damage or inflammation were observed, confirming the low toxicity and safety of the developed NIR light-controlled MNs [63].

Cancer treatment

Melanoma is the most aggressive and malignant form of skin cancer. Current melanoma treatment methods generally suffer from frequent drug administration as well as difficulty in direct monitoring of drug release. However, in research by Wang et al., a dissolving MN patch with aggregation-induced emission (AIE)-active Poly-AM-TPE-CAA (PATC) microparticles was designed to achieve NIR light-controlled pulsatile chemo-photothermal synergistic therapy of melanoma. The PATC polymeric particles, termed D/I@PATC, encapsulate both of chemotherapeutic drug doxorubicin (DOX) and the photothermal agent indocyanine green (ICG). Upon light illumination, PATC gradually dissociates into smaller particles, causing the release of encapsulated DOX and subsequent fluorescence intensity change of PATC particles, thereby not only enabling direct observation of the drug release process under light stimuli but also facilitating verification of drug release by fluorescence recovery after light trigger. Moreover, encapsulation of ICG in PATC particles displays significant improvement in its photothermal stability both in vitro and in vivo [66]. Figure 3 illustrates some outcomes of this study.

To tackle the high recurrence rate and the challenging hypoxic microenvironment often found in postoperative melanoma wounds, Jia et al. introduced a living photosynthetic MN patch (MA/CM@MN). This groundbreaking strategy uses a functional partitioning design, incorporating alive microalgae (MA) into the MN base (HAMA matrix) and cuttlefish melanin (CM) nanoparticles into the MN tips (HAMA and PEGDA composite hydrogel). The therapeutic strategy involves dual activation; First, NIR-controlled PTT, in which the CM nanoparticles act as the photothermal conversion agent, allowing the MN tips to heat rapidly (stabilizing around) under NIR irradiation (808 nm). This photothermal effect provides strong tumor-killing efficacy against residual melanoma cells. The specific MN structure helps reduce damage to the living MA during the photothermal heating process. Second is in situ sxygenation, where the alive MA in the MN base performs oxygenic photosynthesis under light exposure, continuously generating oxygen. This generated oxygen effectively alleviates tumor hypoxia, demonstrated by notably inferior levels of the hypoxia marker HIF-1 in the MA/CM@MN treated groups compared to controls. As shown in Fig. 4, the synergistic combination of PTT for tumor ablation and oxygenation for microenvironment modulation proved highly effective in vivo. In mice with incomplete tumor resection, the NIR-irradiated MA/CM@MN successfully prevented local tumor recurrence and simultaneously promoted wound healing. Histological analysis confirmed that the patch inhibited tumor cell proliferation (decreased Ki67), stimulated tumor cell apoptosis (increased TUNEL), and mitigated tissue hypoxia, showcasing a promising strategy for postoperative cancer therapy and wound healing [67].

Recognizing the high recrudescence and the resulting aesthetic and functional disorders following the resection of maxillofacial malignant skin tumors, Dong et al. introduced a novel multifunctional sodium nitroprusside and Fe2+ ions loaded microneedle platform (SNP-Fe@MNs). This platform was fabricated using sodium carboxymethylcellulose (SCMC) and was engineered to conquer the therapeutic dilemma of skin malignancies by facilitating tumor cell apoptosis via synergistic photothermal therapy (PTT) and gas therapy, followed by subsequent tissue regeneration in a single step. The PTT functionality is activated by NIR light (808 nm), as the components form Prussian blue analogues in situ, which exhibit a desirable NIR-responsive photothermal effect. The system employs Nitric Oxide (NO) as the gaseous therapeutic agent. The timing and targeted concentration of NO delivery are crucial. The system is designed to provide burst release of NO (triggered by UV radiation) for maximizing anti-tumor effects and inducing apoptosis, while providing a gradual, steady release of NO over the long term for promoting tissue regeneration through angiogenic effects. This synergistic gas-hyperthermia therapy demonstrated the best therapeutic effect in vivo, preventing local tumor recurrence and promoting complete wound closure in murine models. Mechanistically, tumor cell apoptosis was induced by upregulating caspase-3 expression and declining levels of HSP-70, HSP-90, and AKT, supporting the involvement of HSP-90/AKT signaling pathways. Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common and aggressive primary brain tumor. Surgery followed by chemotherapy and radiotherapy remains the standard treatment strategy for GBM patients. However, challenges still exist when surgery is difficult or impossible to remove the tumor completely. To address this challenge, Wang et al. have developed a silk fibroin MN (SMN) patch that allows the integration of three chemotherapeutic agents (thrombin, bevacizumab, and temozolomide) in combination with different drug loads with controlled-release profiles to stop bleeding during the surgical operation, inhibit postoperative angiogenesis, and promote apoptosis of glioblastoma cells. Combined administration of an angiogenic inhibitor and an anti-neoplastic agent effectively regulated the microenvironment of tumor cells, thus dramatically suppressing tumor volume and improving the survival rate of mice in vivo [62].

In a similar study, polycaprolactone MNs containing a photosensitive nanomaterial (lanthanum hexaboride), an anticancer drug (doxorubicin), and a dissolvable poly (vinyl alcohol)/polyvinylpyrrolidone supporting array patch were constructed. After exposing MNs to NIR light, the embedded MN array uniformly heats the target tissue to induce a large thermal ablation area and then melts at 50 °C to release DOX in a broad area, thus destroying tumors [70].

Parkinson treatment

As the population continues to age, the effects of Parkinson’s disease (PD) are expected to become increasingly severe. The common approach to medication management based on the patient’s condition has emerged as a standard treatment for PD around the globe. Nevertheless, traditional oral administration often leads to various digestive issues, is vulnerable to degradation by digestive juices and intestinal microbes, resulting in poor absorption rates, and can significantly delay the drug’s effectiveness. There is an urgent need for alternative methods that can provide lower side effects and improved drug utilization. To tackle these issues, Zhou et al. developed an MN patch (MNP) capable of delivering drugs with controlled timing and spatial precision, activated by NIR light for PD treatment. The MNs utilized Gelatin-methacrylate (GelMA) as the base, and therapeutic drugs (L-DOPA) were encapsulated in the mesopores of up-conversion micron-rods (UCMRs), which can emit blue and UV light when excited by NIR light, dispersing within the GelMA. This MNP can penetrate the skin in a painless, non-invasive, and non-infectious manner. Upon exposure to NIR light, L-DOPA contained in the MNs is released as required and subsequently enters the bloodstream and brain to alleviate PD symptoms. As shown in Fig. 7, in trials involving a Parkinson’s mouse model, this method significantly improved motor functions, and further mechanistic research indicated a reduction in neuroinflammation and a decrease in the death of dopaminergic neurons within the substantia nigra. Additionally, the L-DOPA released directly enters the blood circulation, minimizing gastrointestinal side effects and enhancing the drug’s utilization rate. These noteworthy advantages suggest that the MNP with a controlled drug delivery system could be a promising strategy for treating PD [71].

Wound treatment

In research conducted by Shao et al., a versatile extrusion-based three-dimensional (3D) printing technique was introduced to create MXene and spidroin-integrated MNs scaffolds featuring photothermal responsive and self-healing characteristics to enhance wound healing [72]. 3D printing emerges as a promising technology in tissue engineering, streamlining the production of wound dressings and lowering their manufacturing costs [73–76]. Drawing inspiration from the cactus, the MN scaffold consists of a porous upper layer and MNs, where the inverse opal (IO) photonic crystal (PC) structure and the extensive spaces within the scaffold provide a high capacity for drug loading. Moreover, MXene’s outstanding electrical and photothermal properties enable the MN scaffold to monitor wound movement sensitively and facilitate controlled drug release upon NIR irradiation. Experiments conducted in rat models with wounds demonstrated that drug-loaded MN scaffolds under NIR light can significantly enhance the healing of full-skin wounds [72].

Bacterial biofilm production often complicates the treatment of chronic wounds, and the drug resistance exhibited by bacteria limits the available treatment options, posing challenges for drug delivery. However, a study by Ma et al. developed a painless, broadly effective wound treatment patch, wherein the MN structure breaks the biofilm and directly administers nitric oxide (NO) into the wound. NO serves as an antimicrobial agent with tissue-regenerating capabilities but is hindered by a short half-life and challenges in storage. To address these drawbacks, a novel hydrogel-forming MN (HFMN) was created from polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) hydrogels (a highly biocompatible drug carrier) in combination with S-nitrosoglutathione (GSNO, a NO-releasing component) and graphene oxide (GO) [64]. The integration of MNs with graphene oxide provides multiple benefits, including a wider application range for MNs, significantly improved mechanical strength [77], enhanced moisture resistance [78], antibacterial properties [79], anti-inflammatory effects, and NIR-activated regulated drug release [78, 80]. Research by Ma et al. demonstrated that GSNO-GO-HFMNs release NO at a gradual rate as the temperature increases, and crucially, they can be heated through gentle NIR to expedite the subcutaneous release of NO from the heat-sensitive GSNO [64].

To address the persistent challenges in diabetic wounds, such as excessive exudate, drug-resistant bacteria, and compromised healing environments, Chen et al. introduced a bioinspired, highly mechanically robust system known as the Cellulose Nanofibril-reinforced Bidirectional Janus Photothermal Microneedle array (CBJMN). Inspired by the water-collecting spines of cacti and the hydration strategies of spiny lizards, the CBJMN is engineered to perform the dual function of directional exudate drainage and staggered drug delivery. The device utilizes a NIR-responsive system where the photothermal agent, CNF-PEI-TA, is embedded in a highly tough cellulose nanofibril-polymer matrix. Upon exposure to NIR light, the photothermal effect rapidly increases the local wound temperature. This hyperthermia works synergistically with the therapeutic drug, Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), which is capable of scavenging Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS). The CNF-PEI-TA material exhibits exceptional photothermal stability and reproducibility, maintaining stability through multiple heating/cooling cycles. The CBJMN’s primary innovation is its fluid control: the combined effect of the conical MN tips (which generate a Laplace pressure gradient) and a Janus backing layer (which exhibits a wettability gradient) creates a self-pumping array for autonomous exudate removal. This removal reduces scattering and absorption of NIR light by excess fluid, enhancing photothermal efficacy. The system also achieves controlled drug delivery, where EGCG release is highly dependent on glucose concentration, enabling glucose-responsive release under hyperglycemic conditions. In in vivo infected diabetic rat models, CBJMN treatment significantly accelerated wound closure, reaching up to a 99.5% closure rate by day 14 for wounds infected with ESBL(+) (a multi-drug-resistant bacterium). The synergistic NIR-photothermal action achieved greater than 99.99% antibacterial efficacy against drug-resistant pathogens, including MRSA, ESBL(+), and C. albicans. Histological and immunomolecular analysis confirmed that CBJMN effectively reduced tissue edema, downregulated the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6, promoted angiogenesis by upregulating VEGF, and regulated the wound immune microenvironment by polarizing macrophages from the M1 to the M2 phenotype. The remarkable combination of high mechanical strength, controlled fluid management, and synergistic therapeutic functions positions CBJMN as a transformative material for diabetic wound care [81]. A new investigation outlines a light-activated liquid band-aid utilizing MNs that is designed for antibacterial purposes. A nanozyme derived from metal-organic frameworks, which is incorporated into the MNs, not only utilizes light to generate heat, thereby improving drug absorption, but also breaks down hydrogen peroxide into hydroxyl radicals that possess antibacterial properties. The heat produced by the nanozyme aids in the melting of the MNs to create a liquid band-aid, which helps protect the wound from the surrounding bacteria. Research conducted on a mouse model infected with Staphylococcus aureus indicates that this laser-activated liquid band-aid can significantly diminish skin inflammation and enhance the healing process [83].

Anti-obesity treatment

To address the global epidemic of obesity and the limitations of conventional pharmacotherapy (such as serious adverse effects after long-term use and first-pass metabolism issues), Wang et al. designed and developed a soluble polydopamine-emodin-polyvinylpyrrolidone microneedle patch (PDA-Emodin-PVP-MN). This system delivers emodin, a natural compound with known anti-obesity effects. To overcome emodin’s poor water solubility and low permeability, an Emodin-PVP co-precipitate was first prepared to increase solubility, and then dissolved in a soluble matrix primarily consisting of PVP, silk protein, and hyaluronic acid (HA). Crucially, Polydopamine (PDA) was introduced into the MN matrix to serve as a photothermal conversion agent. Upon exposure to NIR light (808 nm, 0.6 W/cm²), the PDA generates heat, rapidly raising the local temperature (up to 48 °C in rat abdominal tissue within 5 min). This localized hyperthermia serves a dual purpose: it accelerates the dissolution of the MN matrix, leading to an enhanced and accelerated release of emodin into the skin layer (achieving 96.25% cumulative release under NIR in 5 min, compared to 15 min without NIR), and it directly promotes the consumption of body fat. In high-fat diet-induced obese rats, treatment with the NIR-irradiated PDA-Emodin-PVP-MN patch significantly reduced body weight percentage (P < 0.05), lipid-body ratio (P < 0.001), and serum free fatty acids (FFAs) (P < 0.05) compared to the model control group. The synergistic effect of heat and drug delivery proved superior to drug application alone. Mechanistically, the anti-obesity effect may be related to the up-regulation of Hormone-Sensitive Lipase (HSL) and Lipoprotein Lipase (LPL) protein levels in peritesticular adipose tissue. Furthermore, comprehensive safety analysis, including hemolysis and cytotoxicity assays, confirmed the patch’s low toxicity and good biocompatibility, suggesting its potential as a safer alternative to conventional oral obesity medications [84].

Gan et al. developed a novel separable cryo-microneedle patch delivered with capsaicin integrated mesoporous dopamine (mPDA) (TmCNP). The MN patch utilized cryogenic molding of AlgMA-GelMA hydrogel tips to ensure sufficient mechanical strength for skin penetration. A key design feature is the inclusion of a thermosensitive PNIPAm layer that enables the easy separation of the backing layer after application, leaving the drug-loaded tips embedded in the subcutaneous adipose layer for sustained delivery. The therapeutic agent, Cap, was loaded into mPDA nanoparticles (TmCNP), which were specifically functionalized with an adipocyte-targeting peptide (CKGGRAKDC) to enhance delivery precision towards white adipose tissue (WAT). The therapeutic efficacy relies on NIR light-controlled photothermal conversion. Upon exposure to NIR light, the mPDA generates heat, raising the local temperature. This localized, mild hyperthermia achieves a synergistic effect by accelerating the diffusion and release of Cap from the mPDA, while also contributing metabolic benefits through regional hyperthermia therapy. Mechanistically, capsaicin activates the TRPV1 channels, which, combined with the mPDA-mediated photothermal effect, amplify sympathetic nerve stimulation and induce WAT thermogenesis, leading to increased energy expenditure. In high-fat diet (HFD) mice, treatment with the NIR-irradiated MN patch significantly decreased weight gain, resulting in a markedly lower final body weight compared to control animals. The patch alleviated HFD-induced insulin resistance and inhibited hepatic steatosis, as evidenced by reduced liver lipid accumulation. Furthermore, the treatment upregulated markers associated with browning and mitochondrial biogenesis, including TRPV1, PGC1α, UCP1, and Cyto C in adipose tissues. Safety assessments confirmed the cryo-AGMN material exhibited good blood compatibility and negligible toxicity to skin and major organs. This approach highlights the potential of separable cryo-MNs for targeted, controlled obesity management [85].

Corneal crosslinking

Recognizing that the conventional Epithelium-off strategy for corneal crosslinking (CXL) carries risks of epithelial defects, corneal haze, and infections, Hu et al. introduced a responsive porous microneedle system for targeted and highly effective ocular riboflavin delivery. This system aims to provide a minimally invasive CXL protocol without the necessity of epithelial debridement. The MN patch is engineered with three hierarchical components, including a stiff and porous gelatin needle tip for painless corneal penetration, a middle layer composed of Poly (N-isopropyl acrylamide) (PNIPAM) and Graphene Oxide (GO), and a riboflavin-loaded gelatin substrate that serves as a continuous drug reservoir. The core feature is its photothermal-responsive microinjection capability. Upon penetration, the MN tips deliver the riboflavin into the cornea stroma. When subjected to Near-Infrared (NIR) light (808 nm), the highly efficient photothermal agent, GO, generates heat. This local temperature rise triggers the PNIPAM/GO hydrogel to contract (deswell), creating a “pump” that controls and accelerates the flow and release of riboflavin from the backing layer reservoir into the cornea stroma. This pumping cycle ensures rapid and continuous riboflavin delivery, addressing the limitations of previous slow-dissolution MNs. In a rabbit keratoconus model, the responsive porous MNs demonstrated superior riboflavin delivery and enhanced CXL efficacy compared to the conventional epithelial-on protocol, notably strengthening corneal biomechanical properties and accelerating postoperative recovery. Furthermore, the system exhibited excellent biocompatibility with human corneal cells and rapid epithelial recovery in vivo (within 24 h), confirming its potential as a safe and minimally invasive method for clinical ocular drug delivery [86]. MNs for heart failure

A recent study explored the use of NIR light-controlled MNs for treating myocardial infarction (MI), a leading cause of heart failure. A self-unfolding patch made of graphene oxide (GO) and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) that is loaded with vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) was designed and fabricated for minimally invasive surgery (MIS) treatment of MI. The folded MN patch can be easily inserted into the chest cavity through a small incision (4 mm) and quickly revert to its original shape upon exposure to NIR light, owing to its exceptional shape memory effect and rapid shape recovery capabilities. Notably, this MN patch effectively encourages neovascularization, minimizes myocardial fibrosis, and aids in restoring cardiac function, showcasing its promising potential in MIS [87].

Drug-eluting balloon (DEB) angioplasty has emerged as an effective approach for treating cardiovascular and cerebrovascular conditions. However, issues such as distal embolism and late lumen restenosis may arise due to drug loss during DEB manipulation and rapid drug metabolism. In research led by Huang et al., a drug-loaded balloon featuring thermosensitive tip-separable MNs on its surface (MNDLB) was developed, possessing sufficient mechanical strength to penetrate blood vessels and allowing for quick detachment of the MN tips. An integrated NIR ring laser within the catheter’s inner shaft was introduced to activate the biodegradable MN tips. As a result, the drug-loaded tips could embed themselves in the vasculature and gradually release the antiproliferative drug paclitaxel over a period exceeding six months. Other applications

Separating MNs patches

Research by Fan et al. introduces a novel approach for creating controllably separable MNs patches by incorporating photothermal responsive phase-changing microspheres (MPs) into conical hydrogel MNs. These MPs, made from NIR-responsive black phosphorus (BP) and phase-changing gelatin (GT), are produced through a straightforward capillary microfluidic technique. When the composite MN patch is subjected to NIR light, the BP/GT MPs absorb the energy, generate heat, transition into a liquid state, and create voids in the MNs, leading to the easy detachment of the backing layer and needles via shear force. As a result, the needles embedded in the patch can continuously release the drug [89].

Slow drug release improvement

Continuing the topic of materials utilized in NIR light-controlled MNs and their uses, it is important to highlight that the efficiency of drug release from MNs tends to be slower compared to oral administration or hypodermic injections, which limits their broader application. To improve drug release efficiency, Cui et al. have researched a responsive MN characterized by a straightforward manufacturing process, high efficiency, and low cost for transdermal delivery. In their investigation, they developed a photothermal-responsive and temperature-sensitive iron oxide-loaded MN (Fe3O4@MN) patch for controlled transdermal delivery. Photothermal tests indicate that the temperature of the Fe3O4@MN patch rises quickly under NIR exposure, and its photothermal properties can be modified by varying the quantity of Fe3O4 nanoparticles or the duration of irradiation. Additionally, rheological tests thoroughly examine the polymerization process of Fe3O4-loaded gelatin-PEGDA hydrogel (Fe3O4-GP) and how temperature influences the mechanical properties of Fe3O4-GP. Penetration studies reveal that the drug penetration depth achieved by Fe3O4@MN consistently exceeds that of gelatin-PEGDA MN (GP@MN) by 150 to 200 μm under NIR irradiation. These findings demonstrate an improved transdermal delivery capability of Fe3O4@MN compared to conventional GP@MN [90].

Controlling drug release in a laser on/off cycle

Chen et al. integrated silica-coated lanthanum hexaboride (LaB6@SiO2) nanostructures into polycaprolactone MNs, which acted as a NIR absorber. When exposed to NIR light, the LaB6@SiO2 nanostructures facilitated light-to-heat conversion, leading to the melting of the MNs at a temperature of 50 °C. This melting enhanced the mobility of the polymer chains, allowing the drug to be released from the matrix. The drug release from the MNs was assessed over four cycles of laser activation and deactivation. During each cycle, the samples were irradiated until reaching 50 °C for 3 min (laser on), followed by 30 min without laser exposure (laser off). The findings indicated that the light-induced phase transition in the polymer triggered drug release from the liquefied MNs. A gradual drug-release pattern was noted after several NIR light exposure cycles, with no significant drug leakage observed during the off periods. This NIR-light-responsive device demonstrates impressive reproducibility, minimal leakage in the off state, and noninvasive triggering, marking a significant advancement in transdermal drug delivery technology [91].

In a related investigation, Chen et al. introduced an NIR light-controlled MN system for drug administration, aimed at achieving effective pain management through a patient-controlled delivery mechanism that provides analgesia as required. To meet this goal, they developed a photo-activated MN system that facilitates on-demand pain medication delivery to the skin via external NIR light activation. In this setup, polymeric MNs containing both NIR absorbers and analgesics were utilized along with a supportive array made of poly(l-lactide-co-d,l-lactide). The array’s removable design allows for swift implantation of the MNs into the skin, functioning as a drug reservoir, thereby reducing the time needed for patch application. When the implanted MNs are irradiated with NIR light, the absorbers within can capture the light energy, triggering a phase transition in the MNs that initiates drug release. This research demonstrated that the release of lidocaine can be controlled or repeatedly activated by adjusting the irradiation duration and managing the laser’s on-off cycle. Lidocaine delivered through the implanted MNs is quickly absorbed into the bloodstream within 10 min, achieving a bioavailability of at least 95% compared to subcutaneous injections, indicating that this system has the potential to enable rapid pain relief [92].

Conclusion

This review presents a thorough examination of the latest progress and applications of NIR light-controlled MNs in diverse biomedical fields. NIR light-controlled MNs provide precise, on-demand, and spatiotemporal regulation of drug delivery by utilizing materials capable of emitting or absorbing NIR light to trigger drug release. Moreover, NIR light is more advantageous than other wavelengths for light-managed MNs due to its significant penetration capability, low toxicity, and minimal disruption to biological tissues. Several instances of NIR light-controlled MNs for treating diabetes, cancer, Parkinson’s disease, wounds, heart failure, and other uses are discussed, emphasizing their benefits over traditional methods. For example, it was noted that NIR light-controlled MNs can improve glucose regulation in diabetic individuals by administering insulin based on glucose levels; they can also enhance the effectiveness of chemotherapy in cancer patients by releasing doxorubicin in a targeted and controlled manner. Similarly, they can facilitate improved motor function in patients with Parkinson’s disease by delivering dopamine in a targeted manner and expedite the wound healing process by releasing growth factors and antibiotics in a coordinated fashion. Additionally, these MNs can be applied to the body through low-risk, minimally invasive techniques and can ultimately aid in the prevention and management of myocardial infarction, a leading cause of heart failure. However, there are challenges associated with these MNs, including limited tissue penetration depths in certain cases and the requirement for an effective NIR light source. The long-term biosafety of nanoparticles within living organisms is another concerning issue and necessitates thorough investigation. The development of biodegradable or clearable nanoparticles presents a viable solution to this problem. This review also explores the potential integration of NIR light-controlled MNs with other therapies, offering a diverse platform for photothermal treatment. Several promising avenues for future research and development of NIR light-controlled MNs are suggested, including the investigation of new materials and devices responsive to NIR light, with examples that could inspire drug treatments for various therapeutic uses.
