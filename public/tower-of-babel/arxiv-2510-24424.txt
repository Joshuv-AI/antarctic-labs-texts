1.1.
Harmonic analysis of multifractal measures

Given a measure
\mu
on the unit circle identified with
[0,2\pi)
, one can ask whether or not its Fourier coefficients

\hat{\mu}(n)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-2\pi in\theta}\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta)

and form a class lying strictly between those of absolutely continuous and continuous measures (on
[0,2\pi)
). That these inclusions are proper is a consequence of Menšov’s
[
31

famous construction of a singular Rajchman measure, and Wiener’s characterization of continuous measures
[
42

as those for which the
\hat{\mu}(n)
vanish
in mean

[
27
,
26
]
)—and are more generally related to the notion of sets of multiplicity and uniqueness in harmonic analysis
[
21
]
.

In a recent work
[
16
]
, Garban and Vargas initiated the study of the Fourier coefficients of (one-dimensional) GMC, proving in particular that the latter is Rajchman if
\gamma<\sqrt{2}

To be precise, they prove this for the GMC arising from the Gaussian free field on the unit circle.

For instance, define the

This would imply that

Our goal is to provide a first step towards the resolution of this conjecture by proving the following.

1.2.
Main results

In what follows, let
\mu=\mu_{\sqrt{2}}
be the critical Gaussian multiplicative chaos measure arising from a
\star
–scale invariant field
X
on
[0,1]
(defined in Section
2

\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}\theta),\quad\text{ where }\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}\theta):=\sqrt{t}e^{\sqrt{2}X_{t}(\theta)-\mathbb{E}X_{t}(\theta)^{2}}\mathrm{d}\theta,

where the limit is in probability, in the space of Radon measures on
[0,1]
. Let

denote the
n

\mu
. Our main result establishes tightness for
\{(\log n)|c_{n}|\}_{n\geq 1}
, which implies that
c_{n}\to 0
in probability as
n\to\infty
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
K=K(\varepsilon)>0
such that

\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{P}\big\{(\log n)|c_{n}|>K\big\}<\varepsilon.

The
(\log n)^{-1}
scaling stems from what can loosely be described as a freezing phenomenon, which takes effect as soon as
\gamma\geq 1/\sqrt{2}
and contributes logarithmic terms in
n
. This is explained in Section
1.3
, which also contains a heuristic explanation of why we believe our bound to be sharp—that is,
|c_{n}|
is indeed of the order of
1/\log n
.

Our proof is based on a second moment estimate for
c_{n}
on a good event. While the approach is similar in spirit to Berestycki
[
5
]
and Lacoin’s
[
23
]
constructions of subcritical and critical GMC, respectively, the presence of an oscillating factor in the integral defining
c_{n}
here introduces significant complications. We also require a more delicate notion of “good” points than in
[
5
,
23
]
, that uses insights from the study of the maximum of log-correlated Gaussian fields, following the works of Bramson–Ding–Zeitouni
[
7
]
and Madaule
[
28
]
. An overview of the proof is given in Section
1.3
.

By comparison, Garban and Vargas bypass the explicit study of
c_{n}
(when
\gamma>1/\sqrt{2}
) by appealing to the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma. To show that
c_{n}\to 0
almost surely, they note that it suffices by the latter to show that
\mu_{\gamma}^{*d}
is in

for sufficiently large

(where
∗d
denotes
d

.

1.3.
Proof elements

Let
X
be a
\star
–scale invariant field as above, and consider the multiplicative chaos measure
\mu_{\gamma}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mu_{t,\gamma}
associated to

\gamma\in(1/\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2}]

(1.6)

\mu_{t,\gamma}(\mathrm{d}\theta):=\big(\sqrt{t}\cdot\mathbf{1}(\gamma=\sqrt{2})+\mathbf{1}(\gamma\neq\sqrt{2})\big)e^{\gamma X_{t}(\theta)-(\gamma^{2}/2)t}.

Our goal is to estimate
\mathbb{E}[|c_{n,t}|^{2}]

c_{n,t}:=\int e^{in\theta}{\mu_{\sqrt{2},t}(\mathrm{d}\theta)}.

Before this, it is helpful to understand the second moment of the total mass
\int\mu_{\gamma,t}(\mathrm{d}\theta)
for
\gamma<\sqrt{2}
, without the oscillating factor

. Since

is logarithmically correlated, Fubini’s theorem implies that

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(\int\mu_{\gamma,t}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\bigg)^{2}\bigg]\approx\iint\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}}{|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|^{\gamma^{2}}},

which is finite if and only if
\gamma^{2}<1
(the so-called “

phase” of GMC). For larger
\gamma
, the expectation is inflated by points

where

is atypically large at many scales

\theta\in\mathcal{G}

(X_{t}(\theta),t\geq 0)
is well-behaved
2
2

2

This is the main idea in
[
5

23
]

\gamma=\sqrt{2}
, this is achieved by working on the event that
\{\forall\theta\in[0,1]:\theta\in\mathcal{G}\}
—on which the bound

(1.7)

\displaystyle\iint\mu_{\sqrt{2},t}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{1})\mu_{\sqrt{2},t}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{2})\leq\iint\mathbf{1}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{G})\mu_{\sqrt{2},t}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{1})\mu_{\sqrt{2},t}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{2})

holds by monotonicity, and the right-hand side has finite expectation.

An evident hurdle that arises when trying to adapt this approach to

(1.8)

|c_{n,t}|^{2}=\iint e^{in(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})}\mu_{\sqrt{2},t}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{1})\mu_{\sqrt{2},t}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{2})

is that one cannot directly bound as in (

e^{in(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})}
. In particular, the set
\mathcal{G}
must be picked so as to guarantee the positivity and real-valuedness of

(1.9)

\displaystyle\iint e^{in(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})}\mathbf{1}\big(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{G}\big)\mu_{\sqrt{2},t}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{1})\mu_{\sqrt{2},t}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}).

Furthermore, if we hope to get savings from the oscillations in the integral above, we need some control over the regularity of

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{t}(\theta_{1})+X_{t}(\theta_{2}))}\mathbf{1}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{G})\big]

as a function of the gap
\Delta=(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})
. This further limits the choice of
\mathcal{G}
to one for which the event

is relatively simple, while being restrictive enough to make the expectation of (

)).

We now explain where the

\asymp te^{-2t}\iint_{|\Delta|\in[n^{-100},n^{-1}]}e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{t}(\theta_{1})+X_{t}(\theta_{2}))}\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}.

Since

1.4.
Conjectures for subcritical chaos

\gamma\in(1/\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2})

(X_{t}^{(r)}(\theta))_{\theta\in[0,1]}
is
\star-
scale invariant with kernel
k^{(r)}(x):=k(e^{r}x)
since

\mathbb{E}[X_{s}^{(r)}(\theta_{1})X_{s}^{(r)}(\theta_{2})]=\bigg(\int_{1}^{e^{s+r}}-\int_{1}^{e^{s}}\bigg)\frac{k(u(x-y))}{u}\mathrm{d}u=\int_{1}^{e^{s}}\frac{k^{(r)}(u(x-y))}{u}\mathrm{d}u.

Our argument will require some control on the large values of
X_{r_{n}}
and
(X_{t}^{(r_{n})})_{t\geq 0}
, where
r_{n}
is a carefully chosen time depending on the frequency
n
of the Fourier coefficient being studied (see Proposition
4.3
). Following
[
13
,
28
]
, the typical size of
\max_{\theta\in[0,1]}X_{t}(\theta)
fluctuates around

(2.7)

\displaystyle m(t):=\sqrt{2}t-\frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}}\log t

as made precise by the following proposition.

Proposition 2.3

(Theorem 1.1 in
[
1
]
)
.

Let
m(t)
be as in Equation (
2.7
). Then the family

\Big(\max_{\theta\in[0,1]}X_{t}(\theta)-m(t)\Big)_{t\geq 1},

is tight.

This is what one expects from any log–correlated field (see, e.g.
[
4
]
).
In fact, one can typically go further and show that the paths
s\mapsto X_{s}(\theta),s\leq t
of points
\theta
for which
X_{t}(\theta)
is near the maximum cannot fluctuate too wildly. Instead, they display almost linear growth while remaining under a suitably chosen barrier function. This phenomenon was first leveraged by Bramson
[
9
]
to study the maximal particle of branching Brownian motion, and underpins most of the works that followed on the extrema of other log–correlated fields
[
7
,
8
,
2
,
11
]
.

In what follows, we will use the following upper bound essentially due to Lacoin
[
23
]
.

Proposition 2.4

(Proposition 2.4 in
[
23
]
)
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be defined as in Equation (
2.1
). Then almost surely,

\sup_{t\geq e}\sup_{\theta\in[0,1]}\bigg(X_{t}(\theta)-\sqrt{2}t+\frac{\log t}{2\sqrt{2}}-\frac{4\log\log t}{\sqrt{2}}\bigg)<\infty.

It follows that with high probability, the fields
(X_{t}(\theta))_{t\geq 0}
remain below

(2.8)

\displaystyle U(t):=\begin{cases}\sqrt{2}t-\frac{\log t}{2\sqrt{2}}+\frac{4\log\log t}{\sqrt{2}}&\quad\text{if }s\geq e,\\
\infty&\quad\text{if }s<e.\end{cases}

at every point
\theta\in[0,1]
in the following sense.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
U(t)
be defined as in Equation (
2.8
). Then

\mathbb{P}\Big(\forall\theta\in[0,1],\forall t\geq 0,X_{t}(\theta)\leq U(t)+A\Big)\underset{\text{$A\to\infty$}}{\longrightarrow}1.

2.2.
Brownian estimates

We record the following estimates for the probability that a Brownian motion stays under a barrier starting at time
e
(in light of (
2.8
)). Such events will arise from restricting to samples
\omega
for which the event in Corollary
2.5
holds.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
B_{t}
be a standard linear Brownian motion and
a>0
. Then for any
t>e
,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{s\in[e,t]}B_{s}\leq a\Big)\ll\frac{(a+1)}{\sqrt{t}+1}.

Furthermore, if
a,b>0
and
\mathbb{P}_{(0,a)}^{(t,b)}
denotes the law of a Brownian bridge of length
t>e
from
a
to
b
, then

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{(0,a)}^{(t,b)}\Big(B_{s}\geq 0,\forall s\in[e,t]\Big)\ll\frac{(a+1)(b+1)}{t+1}.

Proof.

Both inequalities are a direct consequence of the reflection principle after integrating over the value of
B_{e}
.
∎

3.
Proof of the main theorem

Let
X
be a
\star-
scale invariant field with approximations
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, and define its associated (critical) GMC measure
\mu
as

\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}\theta),\quad\text{ where }\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}\theta):=\sqrt{t}e^{\sqrt{2}X_{t}(\theta)-\mathbb{E}X_{t}(\theta)^{2}}\mathrm{d}\theta.

This limit holds in probability, and it follows that

(3.1)

c_{n,t}=\int_{0}^{1}e^{in\theta}\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}\theta)\overunderset{\mathbb{P}}{t\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}c_{n}=\int_{0}^{1}e^{in\theta}\mu(\mathrm{d}\theta).

We want to show that
\{(\log n)\cdot|c_{n}|\}_{n\geq 1}
is tight, which we do by proving an upper bound of the correct order for the expectation of

(3.2)

|c_{n,t}|^{2}=\iint_{[0,1]^{2}}e^{in(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})}\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{1})\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}).

on a good event
\mathcal{E}_{n,t}^{(A)}
. This is the content of Proposition
3.1
below. As a byproduct of its proof, we identify the set of pairs
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\in[0,1]^{2}
over which the integral in (
3.2
) yields a leading order contribution, and those for which the contribution is negligible in the limit (see Remark
4.4
).

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
A>0
and
0<\delta<1/4
. Let
r_{n}:=\delta\log n
.
For any
t\geq r_{n}
, consider the event

\mathcal{E}^{(A)}_{n,t}:=\big\{\forall\theta\in[0,1],\,\theta\in\mathcal{G}_{n,t}^{(A)}\big\},

where

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{n,t}^{(A)}=\left\{\theta\in[0,1]:\,\,\begin{aligned} &X_{s}^{(r_{n})}(\theta)\leq U(s)+A,\,\forall s\in[0,t-r_{n}]\\
&X_{r_{n}}(\theta)\leq m(r_{n})+A\end{aligned}\right\}

and
m(t),U(t)
are defined in Equations (
2.7
), (
2.8
) respectively.
Then there exists an integer
N>2
such that for any
n\geq N
,

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big[|c_{n,t}|^{2};{\mathcal{E}_{n,t}^{(A)}}\Big]\ll_{A}(\log n)^{-2},

where the implicit constant is uniform in
n
.

The choice of good event
\mathcal{E}_{n,t}^{(A)}
will be explained in Section
4
. For the time being, we show how Theorem
1.1
follows straightforwardly from this proposition.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
assuming Proposition
3.1
.

Noting that
|c_{n}|\leq\mu([0,1])
is almost surely finite, it suffices to prove that
\{|c_{n}|\log n\}_{n\geq N}
is tight where
N
is the constant from Proposition
3.1
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
and let
K>0
be arbitrary for now. Then by (
3.1
) and the Portmanteau theorem, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(|c_{n}|(\log n)>K\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(|c_{n,t}|(\log n)>K\Big)

for any
n>0
. Furthermore, by definition of
\mathcal{E}_{n,t}^{(A)}
, the pre-limit quantity on the right-hand side is bounded above by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n,t}^{(A)}}

\displaystyle|c_{n,t}|(\log n)>K\Big)+\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists\theta\in[0,1]:X_{r_{n}}(\theta)>m(r_{n})+A\Big)

(3.4)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists s\in[0,\infty),\exists\theta\in[0,1]:X_{s}^{(r_{n})}(\theta)>U(s)+A\Big),

for any
A>0
. We can then make the second and third terms in (
3
) arbitrarily small in a way that is uniform in
n
, by taking
A
large enough. Indeed, for any
n
,
(X_{t}^{(r_{n})})_{t\geq 0}
(defined in (
2.6
)) is a sequence of cutoff approximations to a
\star
–scale invariant field with variance
t
at every point. Corollary
2.5
and Proposition
2.3
thus imply that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\sup_{n>2}\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists s\in[0,\infty),\exists\theta\in[0,1]:X_{s}^{(r_{n})}(\theta)>U(s)+A\Big)<\varepsilon/3,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sup_{n>2}\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists\theta\in[0,1]:X_{r_{n}}(\theta)>m(r_{n})+A\Big)<\varepsilon/3,

for sufficiently large
A
. For such an
A
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq N}\mathbb{P}\Big(|c_{n}|(\log n)>K\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{n\geq N}\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n,t}^{(A)}}|c_{n,t}|(\log n)>K\Big)+2\varepsilon/3.

Using Chebyshev’s inequality and Proposition
3.1
, we can then write

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq N}\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n,t}^{(A)}}|c_{n,t}|(\log n)>K\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{n\geq N}\frac{(\log n)^{2}}{K^{2}}\limsup_{t\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}\big[|c_{n,t}|^{2};\mathcal{E}_{n,t}^{(A)}\big]}\ll_{A}\frac{1}{K^{2}}.

Tightness follows by picking
K=K(A)
to make the left-hand side
<\varepsilon/3
.
∎

What’s left is to prove Proposition
3.1
, which is the main difficulty here.

4.
Proof of Proposition
3.1

4.1.
Initial reductions

To bound
|c_{n,t}|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n,t}^{(A)}}
, we first want to extract a local condition (meaning a condition on each pair of points
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
) in (
3.2
) from the global event
\mathcal{E}_{n,t}^{(A)}
, noting that one cannot directly upper bound the integrand due to the presence of the oscillating factor
e^{in\Delta}
. We instead note the following pointwise inequality on the sample space

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n,t}^{(A)}}|c_{n,t}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n,t}^{(A)}}\iint_{[0,1]^{2}}e^{in(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})}\mathbf{1}\Big\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{G}_{n,t}^{(A)}\Big\}\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{1})\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{[0,1]^{2}}e^{in(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})}\mathbf{1}\Big\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{G}_{n,t}^{(A)}\Big\}\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{1})\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}).

We used the fact that the indicator in the integrand is redundant in the first line and positivity of the double integral on the second, noting that it equals the modulus squared of the
n
–th Fourier coefficient of

\tilde{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}\theta):=\mathbf{1}\Big\{\theta\in\mathcal{G}_{n,t}^{(A)}\Big\}\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}\theta).

It therefore suffices to prove the analogous claim (to Proposition
3.1
) for the Fourier coefficients of
\tilde{\mu}_{t}
.

To that end, fix
0<\delta<1/4
and partition
[0,1]^{2}
into the following two ranges:

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{(I)}

\displaystyle=\{(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}):|\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}|\in[\Delta_{n},1]\}

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{(II)}

\displaystyle=\{(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}):|\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}|\in[0,\Delta_{n})\},

where

\Delta_{n}=:en^{-\delta},\text{ noting that }\log\Delta_{n}^{-1}=r_{n}-1.

Denote the contribution to (
3.2
) coming from each of these regions by

(4.4)

\mathcal{C}_{n,t}(\#):=\iint_{(\mathrm{\#})}e^{in(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})}\mathbf{1}\Big\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{G}_{n,t}^{(A)}\Big\}\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{1})\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}\theta_{2})

for
\#\in\{\mathrm{I},\mathrm{II}\}
.

The restriction on
\delta
is technical, and is necessary for our bound for the contribution from (I) (see (
4.17
)) to be of the correct order. On (II), we will ignore the
e^{in(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})}
by the triangle inequality. While this may seem excessively inefficient—since
\delta<1
,
e^{in(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})}
will still oscillate with
n
—it will become apparent that any
\delta>0
yields the same bound (due to the observation in Remark
4.4
).

4.2.
The oscillatory range

We first study the contribution from
(\mathrm{I)}
where
e^{in\Delta}
oscillates very rapidly.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
A>0
. Then

(4.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{C}_{n,t}(\mathrm{I})\big]=o_{A}\big((\log n)^{-2}\big)

for large enough
n
.

Proof.

Recall that
r_{n}=\delta\log n
where
0<\delta<1/4
. By properties (2) and (3) in Section
2
, we know that for any pair
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathrm{(I)}
, the processes

(4.6)

X_{s}^{(r_{n})}(\theta_{1})\text{ and }X_{s}^{(r_{n})}(\theta_{2})\text{ for }{s\leq t-r_{n}}

are independent, identically distributed, and independent from the
\sigma
–algebra generated by the field
X_{s}
up to time
s\leq r_{n}
. Furthermore, the indicator
\mathbf{1}\big\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{G}_{n,t}^{(A)}\big\}
factors into

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}\Big\{X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{i})\leq m(r_{n})+A,i\in\{1,2\}\Big\}

\displaystyle\times\mathbf{1}\Big\{X_{s}^{(r_{n})}(\theta_{i})\leq U(s)+A,\forall s\in[0,t-r_{n}],i\in\{1,2\}\Big\}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}}\mathcal{I}_{>r_{n}}

where the
\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}}
is measurable with respect to (the
\sigma-
algebra generated by)
X_{r_{n}}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{>r_{n}}
with respect to
(X^{(r_{n})}_{s})_{s\in[0,t-r_{n}]}
.

It follows that for any
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{t}(\theta_{1})+X_{t}(\theta_{2}))}\mathbf{1}\Big\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{G}_{n,t}^{(A)}\Big\}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{1})+X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{2}))}\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}}\Big]\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{2}e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{t}(\theta_{i})-X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{i}))}\mathcal{I}_{>r_{n}}\bigg]

and crucially, for
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\in(\mathrm{I})
the rightmost factor is
independent of
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
since
-\log|\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}|\leq r_{n}-1\leq r_{n}
. By translation invariance of the law of
(X_{s}^{(r_{n})})_{s\geq 0}
, the factor in question equals

\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\sqrt{2}X_{t-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(0)}\mathcal{I}_{>r_{n}}\Big]^{2}.

Using
U(s)\leq\sqrt{2}s
,

\mathcal{I}_{>r_{n}}\leq\mathbf{1}\big\{X_{s}^{(r_{n})}(0)\leq\sqrt{2}s+A,\forall s\in[0,t-r_{n}]\big\},

and we conclude by Fubini’s theorem that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}_{n,t}(\mathrm{I})]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-2r_{n}}\iint_{(\mathrm{I})}e^{in(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})}\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{1})+X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{2}))}\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}}\Big]\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}

(4.8)

\displaystyle\times{t}e^{-2t+2r_{n}}\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\sqrt{2}X_{t-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(0)}\mathbf{1}\big\{X_{s}^{(r_{n})}(0)\leq\sqrt{2}t+A,\forall s\in[0,t-r_{n}]\big\}\Big]^{2}.

We begin by showing that the factor in (
4.2
) is bounded in
t
and
n
. Under the measure
\mathbb{Q}
defined through

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}={e^{\sqrt{2}X_{t-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(0)-\mathbb{E}[X_{t-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(0)^{2}]}},

the mean of
(X_{s}^{(r_{n})}(0),s\in[0,t-r_{n}]
) is shifted by
\sqrt{2}s
by Girsanov’s theorem. The quantity on the second line in (
4.2
) is thus bounded by

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}t\cdot\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{s}^{(r_{n})}(0)\leq A,\,\forall s\in[e,t-r_{n}]\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\ll_{A}\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{t}{t-r_{n}}=O_{A}(1),

by Lemma (
2.9
).

It remains to show that

\limsup_{t\to\infty}e^{-2r_{n}}\iint_{(\mathrm{I})}e^{in(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})}\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{1})+X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{2}))}\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}}\Big]\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}=o_{A}\big((\log n)^{-2}\big).

For fixed
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
, consider the change of measure given by

(4.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}_{\Delta}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}=e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{1})+X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{2}))-\mathbb{E}[(X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{1})+X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{2}))^{2}]}.

The mean of
X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{1})
under
\mathbb{Q}_{\Delta}
is then shifted by

(4.10)

\sqrt{2}\cdot\text{Cov}\Big(X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{1}),X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{1})+X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{2})\Big)=\sqrt{2}\big(r_{n}+K(\Delta)\big)

where
\Delta=\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}
and
K=K_{r_{n}}
(cf. Equation (
2.1
). The same is true for
X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{2})
since
K(\Delta)=K(|\Delta|)
.
Noting that

\mathbb{E}[(X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{1})+X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{2}))^{2}]=2r_{n}+2K_{r_{n}}(|\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}|),

the change of variables
(\theta,\Delta)=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})
thus yields

\displaystyle e^{-2r_{n}}\iint_{(\mathrm{I})}e^{in(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})}\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{1})+X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{2}))}\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}}\Big]\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}

\displaystyle=2\int_{\Delta_{n}}^{1}e^{in\Delta+2K(\Delta)}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{Q}_{\Delta}\mathbf{(}\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}})\mathrm{d}\theta\mathrm{d}\Delta

\displaystyle=2\int_{\Delta_{n}}^{1}e^{in\Delta+2K(\Delta)}\mathbb{Q}_{\Delta}\mathbf{(}\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}})\mathrm{d}\Delta

where
\Delta_{n}=en^{-\delta}
.

We integrate by parts to exploit the cancellation coming from
e^{in\Delta}
. Doing so yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta_{n}}^{1}

\displaystyle e^{in\Delta}e^{2K(\Delta)}\mathbb{Q}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}})\mathrm{d}\Delta

(4.11)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{in\Delta}e^{2K(\Delta)}\mathbb{Q}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}})}{in}\bigg|^{1}_{\Delta_{n}}-\int_{\Delta_{n}}^{1}\frac{e^{in\Delta}\partial_{\Delta}\big(e^{2K(\Delta)}\mathbb{Q}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}})\big)}{in}\mathrm{d}\Delta.

Noting that
\mathbb{Q}_{\Delta}
is a probability measure, the first term is

\ll\frac{e^{2K(\Delta_{n})}+e^{2K(1)}}{n}\ll\frac{e^{2r_{n}}}{n}\leq n^{2\delta-1}=o\big((\log n)^{-2}\big).

By triangle inequality, the second term in (
4.11
) is

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{n}\sup_{\Delta\in[\Delta_{n},1]}\Big(|K^{\prime}(\Delta)|e^{2K(\Delta)}+e^{2K(\Delta)}\partial_{\Delta}\mathbb{Q}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}})\Big)

(4.12)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{e^{2K(\Delta_{n})}}{n}\bigg(\sup_{\Delta\in[\Delta_{n},1]}|K^{\prime}(\Delta)|+\sup_{\Delta\in[\Delta_{n},1]}\partial_{\Delta}\mathbb{Q}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}})\bigg).

Using the fact that
e^{2K(\Delta_{n})}\leq n^{2\delta}
and that
K^{\prime}
is uniformly bounded on
[\Delta_{n},1]
by

(4.13)

|K^{\prime}(\Delta)|\leq\int_{1}^{e^{r_{n}}}|{k^{\prime}(u\Delta)}|\mathrm{d}u\leq e^{r_{n}}||k^{\prime}||_{\infty}\ll n^{\delta},

(cf. Equation (
2.1
)) we conclude that (
4.12
) is

(4.14)

\displaystyle\ll n^{3\delta-1}+n^{2\delta-1}\sup_{\Delta\in[\Delta_{n},1]}\partial_{\Delta}\mathbb{Q}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}}).

Recalling the mean shift in Equation (
4.10
),

\mathbb{Q}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}})=\mathbb{P}\Big(X_{i}\leq-\sqrt{2}K(\Delta)-\frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}}\log r_{n}+A,\,\forall i\in\{1,2\}\Big)

where
X_{1},X_{2}
are centered Gaussian random variables with mean
0
, variance
r_{n}
and covariance
K(\Delta)
.
Equivalently, by Fubini’s theorem, this equals

\int_{-\infty}^{H(\Delta)}\int_{-\infty}^{H(\Delta)}p_{\Delta}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}

where
p_{\Delta}
is the density of
(X_{1},X_{2})
and
H(\Delta)=-\sqrt{2}K(\Delta)-\frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}}\log r_{n}+A
for simplicity. We differentiate by repeated applications of Leibniz’s rule, whose validity follows from the uniform bounds established later in the proof. This yields

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(\Delta)\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{H(\Delta)}p_{\Delta}\big(x_{1},H(\Delta)\big)\mathrm{d}x_{1}+\int_{-\infty}^{H(\Delta)}p_{\Delta}\big(H(\Delta),x_{2}\big)\mathrm{d}x_{2}\bigg)

(4.15)

\displaystyle+\iint_{(-\infty,H(\Delta)]^{2}}\partial_{\Delta}p_{\Delta}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}.

The boundary terms in (
4.2
) are equal by symmetry, and are bounded by
|H^{\prime}(\Delta)|=\sqrt{2}|K^{\prime}(\Delta)|
since

\int_{-\infty}^{H(\Delta)}p_{\Delta}\big(x_{1},H(\Delta)\big)\mathrm{d}x_{1}=\frac{e^{-H(\Delta)^{2}/2r_{n}}}{\sqrt{2\pi r_{n}}}\mathbb{P}\big(X_{1}\leq H(\Delta)\big|X_{2}=H(\Delta)\big)\leq 1

uniformly over
\Delta
.
As for the double integral in (
4.2
), differentiating the bivariate Gaussian density and using the triangle inequality gives

\displaystyle\ll|K^{\prime}(\Delta)|\bigg(\frac{|K(\Delta)|}{(r_{n}^{2}-K(\Delta)^{2})}\mathbb{P}\big(X\leq H(\Delta)\big)+\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{1}X_{2}|K(\Delta)\mathbf{1}\big\{X\leq H(\Delta)\big\}\big]}{(r_{n}^{2}-K(\Delta)^{2})}

(4.16)

\displaystyle+\frac{|K(\Delta)|}{(r_{n}^{2}-K(\Delta)^{2})^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{1}\big\{X\leq H(\Delta)\big\}(K(\Delta)|X_{1}X_{2}|+r_{n}|X_{1}|+r_{n}|X_{2}|)\big]\bigg)

where
X=\max(X_{1},X_{2})
. We prove that the right-hand side in (
4.2
) is bounded by a constant times
r_{n}^{2}|K^{\prime}(\Delta)|
using rather crude bounds. To begin with, we can safely ignore the
(r_{n}^{2}-K(\Delta)^{2})^{-1}
terms since

r_{n}^{2}-K(\Delta)^{2}\geq r_{n}^{2}-(r_{n}-1)^{2}\geq 1,\quad\text{ if $|\Delta|>en^{-\delta}=e^{-r_{n}+1}$},\,r_{n}\geq 1.

Secondly, we bound the probability term in (
4.2
) as well as the indicators in the expectations therein by one. Combined with the fact that
K(\Delta)\ll r_{n}
in this range, this yields the simple bound

\ll r_{n}^{2}|K^{\prime}(\Delta)|\cdot\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{1}X_{2}|\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{1}|\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{2}|\big]\Big).

The remaining expectations can be estimated using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\mathbb{E}[|X_{1}X_{2}|]\ll r_{n},\quad\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}|]\ll\sqrt{r_{n}},\,i\in\{1,2\}.

By combining the estimates above, we may conclude that

\sup_{\Delta\in[\Delta_{n},1]}\partial_{\Delta}\mathbb{Q}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}})\ll\sup_{\Delta\in[\Delta_{n},1]}\Big(r_{n}^{3}|K^{\prime}(\Delta)|+|K^{\prime}(\Delta)|\Big)\leq n^{\delta}r_{n}^{3}

(where we used (
4.13
) to bound
|K^{\prime}(\Delta)|
). It follows that from (
4.14
),

(4.17)

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta_{n}}^{1}e^{in\Delta}e^{2K(\Delta)}\mathbb{Q}_{\Delta}(\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}})\mathrm{d}\Delta\ll r_{n}^{3}n^{3\delta-1}=o\big((\log n)^{-2}\big),

and in turn that

e^{-2r_{n}}\iint_{(\mathrm{I})}e^{in(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})}\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{1})+X_{r_{n}}(\theta_{2}))}\mathcal{I}_{\leq r_{n}}\Big]\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}=o\big((\log n)^{-2}\big).

∎

4.3.
Dominant contribution

Having shown that
\mathbb{E}\mathcal{C}_{n,t}(\mathrm{I})
can be discarded, it remains to bound the contribution coming from
\mathbb{E}\mathcal{C}_{n,t}(\mathrm{II})
. We prove the following bound.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
A>0
and
\mathcal{E}_{n,t}^{(A)}
be as in the statement of Lemma
4.2
. Then

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{C}_{n,t}\mathrm{(II)}\big]\ll_{A}\frac{1}{(\log n)^{2}}

for large enough
n
.

We begin by introducing the auxiliary function

(4.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{n,t}(\Delta)=te^{-2t}\cdot\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}\big\{0,\Delta\in\mathcal{G}_{n,t}^{(A)}\big\}e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{t}(0)+X_{t}(\Delta))}\Big]

noting that Fubini’s theorem and the triangle inequality yield

(4.19)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{C}_{n,t}(\mathrm{II})\big]\ll\int_{0}^{\Delta_{n}}\mathcal{F}_{n,t}(\Delta)\mathrm{d}\Delta.

It therefore suffices to prove that the right-hand side is
\ll(\log n)^{-2}
as
t\to\infty
.
The bulk of the work lies in proving the following uniform upper bound for
\mathcal{F}_{n,t}
whose proof is deferred to Section
4.3
.

To state it, we introducing the
branching time

{r_{\Delta}}=t\land\big(\log|\Delta|^{-1}\lor r_{n}\big),\quad\text{ for }\Delta\in[e^{-t},\Delta_{n}]

This should essentially be viewed as
\log|\Delta|^{-1}
on a first reading. The minimum and maximum taken in the definition only serve to make
r_{\Delta}
well-behaved near the “boundary” (namely
\Delta\approx e^{-t}
and
\Delta\approx\Delta_{n}
).

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{n,t}
be defined as in Equation (
4.18
). Then for large enough
t,n>0
, there exists a constant
C(A)=C>0
such that
\mathcal{F}_{n,t}(\Delta)
satisfies the bound

(4.20)

\mathcal{F}_{n,t}(\Delta)\leq C\frac{1}{r_{n}^{2}}\frac{t}{(t-{r_{\Delta}})+1}\bigg(\frac{\log(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})^{5}}{(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})^{2}}\land 1\bigg)\frac{1}{\Delta},\quad\text{ for }\Delta\in[e^{-t},\Delta_{n}]

uniformly in
\Delta
in said range, and

(4.21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{n,t}(\Delta)\leq Cf(n)\frac{e^{t}}{\sqrt{t}},\quad\text{ for }\Delta\in[0,e^{-t})

for some positive function
f
of
n
.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
assuming Proposition
4.3
.

For large enough fixed
n>0
, we can use Proposition
4.3
to bound the integral in (
4.19
), beginning with the range
[0,e^{-t}]
where

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{e^{-t}}\mathcal{F}_{n,t}(\Delta)\mathrm{d}t\ll_{A}\lim_{t\to\infty}f(n)\frac{e^{t-t}}{\sqrt{t}}=0\quad\text{(by \eqref{eq:micro})}.

On
[e^{-t},\Delta_{n}]
, we use the bound in (
4.20
) to write

\displaystyle\int_{e^{-t}}^{\Delta_{n}}\mathcal{F}_{n,t}(\Delta)\mathrm{d}\Delta\ll_{A}\frac{1}{{r_{n}}^{2}}\int_{e^{-t}}^{\Delta_{n}}\frac{t}{(t-{r_{\Delta}})+1}\bigg(\frac{\log(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})^{5}}{(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})^{2}}\land 1\bigg)\frac{1}{\Delta}\mathrm{d}\Delta.

We first take care of the range where
r_{\Delta}
is close to
r_{n}
. On
[\Delta_{n}/e^{3},\Delta_{n}]
, say, we can bound the integral by

\ll\frac{1}{r_{n}^{2}}\frac{t}{t-r_{n}}\int_{\Delta_{n}/e^{3}}^{\Delta_{n}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta}{\Delta}\ll\frac{1}{r_{n}^{2}}\int_{r_{n}-1}^{r_{n}+2}\mathrm{d}\log(\Delta^{-1})\ll\frac{1}{r_{n}^{2}}.

Using the substitution
u=r_{\Delta}
, we split the remaining range into
u\in[r_{n}+2,t/2)
and
u\in[t/2,t)
. The contribution from the latter is

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{r_{n}^{2}}\int_{t/2}^{t}\frac{t}{(t-r_{\Delta})+1}\frac{\log(u-r_{n})^{5}}{(u-r_{n})^{2}+1}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\ll t\log(t)^{5}\int_{t/2}^{t-1}\frac{1}{u^{2}(t-u)}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\log(t)^{6}}{t}\to 0\text{ as }t\to\infty.

On the former,
t/(t-u)\leq 2
and we conclude that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\int_{e^{-t}}^{\Delta_{n}}\mathcal{F}_{n,t}(\Delta)\mathrm{d}\Delta

\displaystyle\ll_{A}\frac{1}{r_{n}^{2}}\int_{r_{n}+2}^{t/2}\frac{\log(u-r_{n})^{4}}{(u-r_{n})^{2}}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\ll_{A}\frac{1}{r_{n}^{2}}\Big(1+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\log(x)^{4}}{x^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\Big).

∎

Remark 4.4
.

For any
a>b>\delta
, the same argument shows that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{n^{-a}}^{n^{-b}}e^{in\Delta}\mathcal{F}_{n,t}(\Delta)\mathrm{d}\Delta\ll\frac{1}{r_{n}^{2}}\int_{b\log n}^{a\log n}\frac{\log(u-r_{n})^{4}}{(u-r_{n}+1)^{2}}\mathrm{d}u\ll\frac{1}{(\log n)^{2}}

whereas for any
s_{1}(n)\leq s_{2}(n)
both tending to
+\infty
in
n
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{n^{-s_{2}(n)}}^{n^{-s_{1}(n)}}e^{in\Delta}\mathcal{F}_{n,t}(\Delta)\mathrm{d}\Delta=o\bigg(\frac{1}{(\log n)^{2}}\bigg).

This suggests that the main contribution to
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}_{n,t}\mathrm{(II)}]
(and in turn
\mathbb{E}[|c_{n,t}|^{2};\mathcal{E}_{n,t}^{(A)}]
) comes from pairs
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
for which
\Delta\approx n^{-a}
for
all
scales
a>\delta
.

5.
Upper bound for
\mathcal{F}_{n,t}(\Delta)

This section proves Proposition
4.3
. To begin with, by independence of increments, the expectation in the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{n,t}
splits into

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{n,t}(\Delta)=te^{-2t}\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{r_{n}}(0)+X_{r_{n}}(\Delta))}\mathbf{1}\big\{X_{r_{n}}(*)\leq m(r_{n})+A,*\in\{0,\Delta\}\big\}\Big]

\displaystyle\times\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{t-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(0)+X_{t-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(\Delta))}\mathbf{1}\big\{X_{s}^{(r_{n})}(*)\leq U(s)+A,\forall s\in[0,t-r_{n}],*\in\{0,\Delta\}\big\}\Big].

By Girsanov’s theorem, the first of the two expectations is bounded above by

e^{2K_{r_{n}}(\Delta)+2r_{n}}\mathbb{Q}_{\Delta}\big(X_{r_{n}}(0)\leq m(r_{n})+A\big),

where
\mathbb{Q}_{\Delta}
is a probability measure under which the mean of
X_{r_{n}}(0)
is shifted by
\sqrt{2}(r_{n}+K_{r_{n}}(\Delta))
(see Equation
4.9
). Since the latter is also Gaussian with variance
r_{n}
, it follows from that the above is

(5.2)

e^{2K_{r_{n}}(\Delta)+2r_{n}}\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathcal{N}\big(0,r_{n}\big)\leq-\sqrt{2}K_{r_{n}}(\Delta)-\frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}}\log(r_{n})+A\Big).

For
\Delta\leq\Delta_{n}
,
K_{r_{n}}(\Delta)=r_{n}+O(1)
(cf. Equation (
2.2
)) and (
5.2
) is therefore bounded by

(5.3)

\ll e^{4r_{n}}\int_{-\infty}^{O_{A}(1)-\sqrt{2}r_{n}-\frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}}\log r_{n}}\frac{e^{-u^{2}/(2r_{n})}}{\sqrt{r_{n}}}\mathrm{d}u\ll_{A}\frac{e^{3r_{n}}}{r_{n}^{2}}

for large enough
n\geq 0
.

We now move on to bounding

(5.4)

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\sqrt{2}(X_{t-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(0)+X_{t-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(\Delta))}\mathbf{1}\big\{X_{s}^{(r_{n})}(*)\leq U(s)+A,\forall s\in[0,t-r_{n}],*\in\{0,\Delta\}\big\}\Big]

beginning with another application of Girsanov’s theorem, by which the above equals

(5.5)

\displaystyle e^{2(t-r_{n})+2(K_{t}(\Delta)-K_{r_{n}}(\Delta))}

\displaystyle\times\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\Big(X_{s}^{(r_{n})}(*)\leq A+{U}(s)-2\sqrt{2}s-\sqrt{2}\big(K_{s+r_{n}}(\Delta)-K_{r_{n}}(\Delta)\big),\begin{subarray}{c}\forall s\in[0,t-r_{n}],\\
*\in\{0,\Delta\}\end{subarray}\Big).

The goal will be to prove that this quantity satisfies the bound

\ll_{A}\frac{e^{2(t-r_{n})+(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})}\cdot\log(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})^{5}}{(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})^{2}\big((t-r_{\Delta})+1\big)},

which, combined with (
5.3
) yields the desired estimate for
\mathcal{F}_{n,t}(\Delta)
, namely

\mathcal{F}_{n,t}(\Delta)\ll_{A}\frac{1}{r_{n}^{2}}\frac{1}{\Delta}\cdot\frac{\log(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})^{5}}{(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})^{2}}\frac{t}{\big((t-r_{\Delta})+1\big)}.

To estimate the probability in (
5.5
), we argue similarly to the proof of Proposition 2.6 in
[
23
]
but require a slightly sharper estimate, which we obtain by utilizing the lower order terms in
U(s)
.
In particular, we will need the following “linearized” version of
U(s)
.

Recall that

r_{\Delta}:=t\land\big((\log|\Delta|^{-1})\lor r_{n}\big),\quad\text{$r_{\Delta}\in[r_{n},t]$ for $\Delta\in[0,\Delta_{n}]$}.

Whenever
r_{\Delta}-r_{n}\geq e
, we define the
slope

\alpha_{\Delta}=\alpha=\bigg(\sqrt{2}-\frac{\log(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})}{2\sqrt{2}(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})}\bigg),

so that

(5.6)

U(s)\leq\alpha s+\frac{4}{\sqrt{2}}{\log\log(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})}\,\text{ for all $s\in[e,r_{\Delta}-r_{n}]$},

with equality at
s=r_{\Delta}-r_{n}
.

We are now equipped to bound the probability in (
5.5
), assuming for now that
(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})\geq e
so that
U(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})
is well-defined. Observe first that by symmetry, we can add the restriction
X_{r_{\Delta}-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(0)\leq X_{r_{\Delta}-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(\Delta)
to the event in (
5.5
) since doing so can only change its probability by a factor of
1/2
. Secondly, we can use the estimate

K_{s+r_{n}}(\Delta)-K_{r_{n}}(\Delta)=s\land(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})+O(1),

in (
5.5
), which follows from Equation (
2.2
). It follows that there exists a constant
C(A)=C
for which the intersection of
\{X_{r_{\Delta}-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(0)\leq X_{r_{\Delta}-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(\Delta)\}
with the events in (
5.5
) is contained in
E_{1}\cap E_{2}\cap E_{3}
, where

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{X_{s}^{(r_{n})}(0)\leq C+\overline{U}(s),\forall s\in[0,r_{\Delta}-r_{n}]\Big\}

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{X_{s}^{(r_{\Delta})}(0)\leq C+\overline{U}(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})-X_{r_{\Delta}-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(0),\forall s\in[0,t-r_{\Delta}]\Big\}

\displaystyle E_{3}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{X_{s}^{(r_{\Delta})}(\Delta)\leq C+\overline{U}(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})-X_{r_{\Delta}-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(0),\forall s\in[0,t-r_{\Delta}]\Big\}

and
\overline{U}(s):=U(s)-2\sqrt{2}s
.

Note that to get the inequality in
E_{3}
, we used (and subsequently dropped) the restriction
X_{r_{\Delta}-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(0)\leq X_{r_{\Delta}-r_{n}}^{(r_{\Delta})}(\Delta)
. We have also used the fact that
\overline{U}
is decreasing on
[e,\infty)
.

By Equation (
2.9
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(E_{2}\cap E_{3}\,\Big|\,X_{r_{\Delta}-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(0)=z\Big)\ll\frac{(C+\overline{U}(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})-z+1)^{2}}{((t-r_{\Delta})+1)}.

Furthermore, by (
5.6
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(E_{1}\Big|X_{r_{\Delta}-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(0)=z\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\bigg(X_{s}^{(r_{n})}(0)\leq C+\frac{4}{\sqrt{2}}{\log\log(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})}+(\alpha-2\sqrt{2})s,

\displaystyle\hskip 200.75pt\forall s\in[e,r_{\Delta}-r_{n}]\Big|X_{r_{\Delta}-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(0)=z\bigg),

and we can estimate the right-hand side using Equation (
2.10
). Indeed, it is bounded by the probability that

Z_{s}:=C+\frac{4}{\sqrt{2}}\log\log(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})+\bigg(\alpha-2\sqrt{2}-\frac{z}{(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})}\bigg)s-X_{s}^{(r_{n})}(0)+\frac{z}{(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})}s,

which is a Brownian bridge from
C+(4/\sqrt{2})\log\log(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})
to
(C+\overline{U}(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})-z)
, remains positive at all times
s\in[e,r_{\Delta}-r_{n}]
. This gives

\displaystyle\ll\frac{(C+\log(r_{\Delta}-r_{n}))(C+\overline{U}(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})-z+1)}{((r_{\Delta}-r_{n})+1)},

where we bounded the iterated logarithm by
\log(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})
for simplicity.

By integrating over the value of
C+\overline{U}(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})-X_{r_{\Delta}-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(0)
, we can then bound the quantity in (
5.5
) by

\displaystyle e^{2(t-r_{n})+2(K_{t}(\Delta)-K_{r_{n}}(\Delta))}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\mathbf{1}_{E_{1}}\mathbb{P}\Big(E_{2}\cap E_{3}\,\Big|\,X_{r_{\Delta}-r_{n}}^{(r_{n})}(0)\Big)\bigg]

\displaystyle\ll e^{2(t-r_{n})+2(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{(x-C-\overline{U}(r_{\Delta}-r_{n}))^{2}}{2(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})}}}{\sqrt{r_{\Delta}-r_{n}}}\frac{(C+\log(r_{\Delta}-r_{n}))(x+1)(x+1)^{2}}{\big((r_{\Delta}-r_{n})+1\big)\big((t-r_{\Delta})+1\big)}\mathrm{d}x

(5.7)

\displaystyle\ll_{A}e^{2(t-r_{n})+2(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})}\frac{\log(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})e^{-(\overline{U}(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})+C)^{2}/2(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})}}{(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})^{3/2}\big((t-r_{\Delta})+1\big)}.

Recalling that

\overline{U}(s)=-\sqrt{2}s-\frac{\log s}{2\sqrt{2}}+\frac{4}{\sqrt{2}}\log\log s,

expanding the square in the exponential in Equation (
5
) gives

\displaystyle\ll_{A}\frac{e^{2(t-r_{n})+(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})}\cdot\log(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})^{5}}{(r_{\Delta}-r_{n})^{2}\big((t-r_{\Delta})+1\big)},

If
r_{\Delta}-r_{n}<e
, repeating the argument while omitting
E_{1}
yields the desired bound, noting that
e^{r_{\Delta}}\asymp e^{r_{n}}
in this range. Lastly, the bound in (
4.21
) follows immediately from the above by noting that
r_{\Delta}=t
when
\Delta\leq e^{-t}
, by definition.
