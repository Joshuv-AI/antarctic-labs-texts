Early Effects of the Serious Accidents Punishment Act on Occupational Health Outcomes in Korea: A Nationwide Difference‐in‐Differences Study (2017–2023)
Authors: Hyewon Park, Misong Woo, Wanhyung Lee
Published: 2026

Introduction

Work‐related injuries and occupational diseases remain critical challenges for worker health and safety globally. Despite long‐standing regulatory frameworks such as the Occupational Safety and Health Act (enacted in 1981), Korea continues to report comparatively high rates among OECD countries of industrial accidents and fatalities [1, 2]. To strengthen corporate accountability for worker safety, the Korean government enacted the Serious Accidents Punishment Act (SAPA) in 2021, effective January 2022 [3]. The law seeks to establish managerial responsibility for occupational safety and prevent serious accidents through compliance‐based safety governance.

SAPA's implementation was phased rather than universal. In its initial stage, the law applied to businesses with ≥ 50 regular workers and to construction projects exceeding 5 billion KRW (US$3.5 million) in contract value [4]. Small enterprises—which account for most occupational accidents in Korea—remained outside its scope until workplaces with ≥ 5 employees were included in January 2024. This partial enforcement reflects regulatory pragmatism and capacity constraints, and it creates an opportunity to begin to test differential impacts between covered and uncovered enterprises.

Although studies have examined SAPA effects [5], evidence stratified by enterprise size and industry remains limited. Because safety conditions and compliance capacity vary by enterprise size and industry, early evaluation of the selective enforcement phase is crucial [6, 7]. Analyzing sectoral responses to stricter legal accountability can clarify how legislation influences occupational health outcomes and safety culture.

We evaluated SAPA's early effects on short‐term patterns of injury, disease, and fatality rates across Korean industries, contrasting enterprises subject to versus exempt from the law during the initial enforcement period. Using national compensation records from 2017 to 2023 and a difference‐in‐differences (DID) analytical design, we assessed how accountability reforms translate into measurable changes in worker health and safety at the national level.

Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

We used a DID design to evaluate early SAPA effects on injury, disease, and fatality rates in South Korea. DID compares pre‐ and post‐policy changes between affected and unaffected enterprises, estimating the average treatment effect while accounting for time‐invariant confounders. We used national industrial accident statistics from the Occupational Safety and Health Research Institute (OSHRI) and the Korea Workers' Compensation and Welfare Service (KCOMWEL). These data constitute the official national record of injuries, diseases, and fatalities across insured enterprises and are publicly available via the OSHRI Open Statistics Portal (https://oshri.kosha.or.kr/english/index.do) for 2017–2023. We defined pre‐implementation (2017–2021) and post‐implementation (2022–2023) periods corresponding to SAPA's commencing enforcement in January 2022.

Study Population and Grouping

Enterprises were categorized into two groups based on SAPA's statutory scope:

1.Treatment group—enterprises with ≥ 50 full‐time employees or construction projects with contract values ≥ 5 billion KRW, to which SAPA applied beginning in 2022.

2.Control group—enterprises with < 50 employees, to which the Act did not apply during the study period.

Industry classifications followed the International Standard Industrial Classification to enable sector‐stratified analyses (e.g., manufacturing, construction, transportation).

Outcome Variables

We analyzed four occupational health and safety outcomes, defined according to international standards from the International Labour Organization (ILO, 16th ICLS) [8] and the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (https://www.bls.gov/iif/definitions/occupational-safety-and-health-definitions.htm). Occupational injury and disease rates were calculated as the number of compensated cases divided by the total number of workers and multiplied by 100. The work‐related fatality rate was calculated as the number of deaths divided by the total number of workers and multiplied by 10,000.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in three stages. First, descriptive analyses summarized annual counts (N) and corresponding rates of occupational injury and disease combined, occupational injury, occupational disease, and work‐related fatalities from 2017 to 2023. Results were stratified by enterprise size (total, < 50 employees, and ≥ 50 employees).

Second, to assess temporal trends prior to policy evaluation, linear regression models were fitted with the annual outcome rate as the dependent variable and calendar year as the independent variable. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to enterprise size (< 50 employees and ≥ 50 employees). Industry classification was included as a categorical covariate. Analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Robust standard errors addressed heteroskedasticity and clustering within enterprise strata. Parallel trends were assessed using pre‐policy data (2017–2021) to evaluate DID validity [10].

Results

As reported in Table 1 and Figure 1, the total occupational injury and disease rate rose from 0.48% in 2017 to 0.66% in 2023, with a steeper increase for occupational diseases, which nearly doubled from 0.05% to 0.11%. The work‐related fatality rate fluctuated slightly but declined after 2022, decreasing from 1.10 to 0.98 deaths per 10,000 workers in 2023.

Discussion

We evaluated the early impact of SAPA on occupational injury, disease, and fatality outcomes in South Korea using national data from 2017 to 2023. During the limited enforcement period (2022–2023), when only enterprises with ≥ 50 employees were covered, overall rates of occupational injuries, diseases, and fatalities did not decline significantly. By contrast, the construction industry showed a significant reduction in occupational disease rates following SAPA enforcement. These findings underscore the incremental nature of policy effects and the importance of contextual factors, including industry‐specific hazards, regulatory enforcement, and enterprise size.

Over the study period, the steady increase in reported occupational injuries and diseases likely reflects multiple, overlapping influences rather than a simple deterioration in workplace safety. First, demographic and labor market changes, including aging of the workforce and expansion of employment in high‐risk sectors, may have gradually elevated susceptibility to both acute injuries and chronic conditions [11]. Second, improvements in coverage of workers' compensation, recognition criteria for occupational diseases, and awareness of claim procedures could have increased the likelihood that cases are reported and officially compensated [12]. Finally, enhanced surveillance and policy attention to occupational health may have strengthened reporting incentives among employers, particularly in sectors under closer regulatory scrutiny [13, 14]. While these mechanisms cannot be formally tested with the available data, they align with existing knowledge on occupational reporting and risk and provide a useful context for understanding the upward trends in national statistics.

The lack of an immediate nationwide decline in occupational incidents accords with international experience: newly implemented legal regimes often require several years before measurable workplace safety gains emerge. Early effects tend to elevate compliance awareness and institutional accountability rather than produce direct outcome changes [15, 16]. Korea's introduction of criminal liability for work‐related deaths marks a paradigm shift, but its deterrent effect hinges on administrative readiness and managerial adaptation [3]. Large enterprises that already had established safety systems likely showed limited marginal gains, whereas smaller enterprises remained outside SAPA's legal scope during this period [7]. Most firms also needed time to interpret the new obligations and allocate compliance resources [17, 18].

The observed reduction in occupational disease rates in the construction industry should be interpreted cautiously in light of disease heterogeneity. While some occupational diseases involve long latency periods and cumulative exposure, others, such as work‐related musculoskeletal disorders, may respond more rapidly to changes in workplace practices, reporting, or regulatory compliance. Therefore, short‐term changes following SAPA implementation may reflect early shifts in reporting or compliance rather than immediate reductions in long‐latency disease incidence.

The construction industry exhibited a significant post‐law reduction in occupational disease rates, consistent with recent reports that identify enhanced dust control, noise reduction, and ergonomic hazard management as key drivers [18, 19]. Similar patterns have been noted in process‐industry analyses, indicating that focused government inspection and investment in safety resources accelerate chronic disease prevention more than accident reduction [20]. This pattern may suggest that enforcement‐linked interventions—such as mandatory safety‐officer appointments and digital monitoring—may yield early gains in health outcomes rather than accident metrics [21].

This difference may reflect the distinct responsiveness of occupational disease and injury outcomes to regulatory and workplace changes. Occupational diseases, particularly those related to musculoskeletal strain or repeated exposure, may be more sensitive to early improvements in reporting, compliance, or preventive practices [22]. In contrast, occupational injuries often depend on acute events and may require longer‐term structural or behavioral changes to show measurable reductions [22, 23].

The absence of similar patterns in other sectors, such as transportation, may reflect differences in baseline risk profiles, enforcement intensity, and sector‐specific operational characteristics. These factors may influence both the implementation of safety measures and the detectability of short‐term changes following SAPA enforcement [24, 25].

Despite SAPA's initial success in certain industries, the persistent disparity between large and small enterprises underscores enduring inequities in Korea's occupational safety infrastructure. Enterprises with fewer than 50 workers—still accountable for the majority of occupational fatalities—were not legally covered by SAPA during the study period [7, 26]. Consequently, national statistics likely underrepresent the Act's broader potential effects. The 2024 expansion of SAPA to workplaces employing five or more workers is expected to narrow this structural gap and test the law's scalability [3]. International experience likewise indicates that accountability measures alone are insufficient without concurrent capacity‐building for small businesses [27, 28].

Cross‐regional analyses show that the effectiveness of occupational safety legislation depends on a balance between deterrence and institutional support [3, 29]. In Korea, SAPA has refocused accountability on corporate leadership, yet enforcement success rests on integrating complementary mechanisms—technical training and consistent inspection—to sustain compliance [30]. Policy environments that emphasize administrative learning and safety‐culture development typically precede tangible reductions in occupational hazards [16, 20]. Thus, the evidence here suggests that SAPA's promise lies less in short‐term punishment than in the long‐term institutionalization of preventive governance.

Although initial enforcement of SAPA did not produce broad, immediate reductions in occupational injury or fatality rates, its observed impact on construction‐related diseases suggests early progress in risk prevention. The results indicate that punitive regulation must be paired with supportive measures that build preventive capacity, especially among smaller firms and subcontracted industries. The 2024 expansion phase presents a critical opportunity to evaluate whether broader coverage and enhanced compliance assistance can accelerate improvements in worker health nationwide. Policymakers should therefore emphasize balanced approaches that integrate long‐term regulatory consistency, managerial education, and proactive health surveillance to realize SAPA's full public‐health potential.

This analysis benefits from a comprehensive national data set of all compensated occupational incidents in Korea, enabling population‐level inference. The DID model effectively controlled for temporal and structural confounders, strengthening causal inference. Nonetheless, several limitations remain. The primary constraint lies in the data source: KCOMWEL's national industrial accident statistics are based on the approval date of compensation claims rather than the actual date of occurrence. Thus, for the study period beginning in 2022, the data set may include incidents that occurred before SAPA enforcement. Additionally, the data are available only annually and cannot be disaggregated monthly, complicating precise alignment with policy timing; some pre‐implementation incidents may have been inadvertently included in the post‐implementation period. Despite these constraints, this study offers an initial step toward understanding how SAPA has influenced occupational health outcomes during its early enforcement. Ongoing improvements in data transparency and in linking occurrence and compensation systems will be essential to enable more precise causal analyses. Furthermore, the post‐SAPA observation window was limited to two years, restricting assessment of delayed effects. Firm‐level covariates such as internal hazard assessments or compliance investments were also unavailable. Because small enterprises were not yet covered by legal enforcement, the findings may underestimate the Act's full potential scope. An additional limitation relates to the use of enterprise size to define treatment and comparison groups. Because SAPA was implemented based on a statutory threshold of 50 employees, this classification reflects the policy design rather than an analytically equivalent comparison. However, enterprises above and below this threshold may differ systematically in baseline safety capacity, regulatory compliance, and implementation of occupational safety and health practices. Although the DID framework accounts for time‐invariant differences between groups, residual structural differences may remain. The industry‐stratified analyses partially address this limitation by capturing sector‐specific heterogeneity and represent a key strength of the present study. In addition, the wide confidence intervals observed in some sector‐specific DID estimates should be interpreted in light of methodological constraints. The post‐enforcement period was limited to two years, which restricts the stability of post‐policy trend estimation. Furthermore, the analysis was based on a limited number of annual time points (2017–2023), limiting the amount of temporal information available for regression‐based DID estimation. The interaction term used to estimate the DID effect is inherently subject to greater variability, particularly when combined with sector‐stratified analyses that further reduce variation within each subgroup. As a result, statistical precision may be limited despite relatively large point estimates. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary and indicative of potential patterns rather than definitive causal effects.

Conclusion

This study examined the initial effects of SAPA on occupational health outcomes in South Korea using national industrial accident compensation data for 2017–2023. During the early enforcement period (2022–2023), when SAPA applied only to enterprises with 50 or more workers, we observed no statistically significant nationwide reduction in overall occupational injury or fatality rates. However, a measurable decrease was observed in occupational disease rates within the construction sector. Given that this was the only statistically significant sector‐level finding, it should be regarded as a preliminary, construction‐specific signal that requires confirmation with longer follow‐up rather than as definitive evidence of sector‐specific benefits. Together, these findings indicate that although SAPA has strengthened managerial accountability and corporate responsibility in occupational safety, the short time frame and limited enforcement scope constrain its immediate measurable impact. Persistent disparities between large and small enterprises further highlight the structural limitations of partial policy coverage. As SAPA extends to businesses with five or more workers from 2024 onward, continued monitoring will be critical to evaluate long‐term effects on comprehensive worker safety. Overall, early evidence suggests that SAPA marks a turning point in Korea's occupational safety governance. Its success, however, will depend on sustained enforcement, equitable inclusion of small enterprises, more granular data systems, and integration of proactive measures such as technical training and consistent inspection. As these complementary mechanisms mature, SAPA can evolve from a primarily punitive framework into a comprehensive, preventive model for worker health and corporate safety accountability.
