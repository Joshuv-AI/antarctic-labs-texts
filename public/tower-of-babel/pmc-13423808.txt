Long-term outcomes in adults born preterm with symptomatic patent ductus arteriosus
Authors: Alysha Joseph, Alvin Chandra, Gregory P Barton, Nataly J Sanchez-Solano, Jarett D Berry, Kara N Goss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare outcomes in young adults born prematurely with and without symptomatic patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) during infancy.Study designYoung adults born preterm (≤32 weeks gestation; n = 107) were recruited from the Parkland Health Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) Registry and compared based on presence of symptomatic PDA during their NICU stay (n = 23 with PDA), defined as clinical evidence of large left-to-right shunt or congestive heart failure.ResultsSystolic blood pressure was similar, but the PDA group exhibited higher resting diastolic blood pressure (80 ± 10 versus 74 ± 12 mmHg, p = 0.02). Biventricular systolic function was similar. However, tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) velocities at the lateral/septal aspect of the mitral annulus were lower and RV end-diastolic diameter larger in the PDA group.ConclusionsIndividuals born preterm with a symptomatic PDA exhibited elevated diastolic blood pressure, slightly lower diastolic function, and larger right ventricular size in early adulthood.Subject terms: Outcomes research, Cardiovascular diseases, Congenital heart defects

Introduction

Prematurity impacts cardiovascular health throughout the lifetime. Various studies demonstrate that adults born prematurely have smaller cardiac chambers, while others demonstrate potential for cardiac hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction, and myocardial fibrosis [1, 2]. There is also evidence that these patients are at risk for pulmonary hypertension [3] and heart failure later in life [4, 5]. In addition to hemodynamic and structural changes, this population has higher incidence of metabolic syndromes [6] and lifetime adverse cardiovascular outcomes compared to a general term born adult population [7].

Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) is a problem of prematurity, with higher morbidity and increased incidence in very preterm infants due to immature responses to constrictive mechanisms [8]. Over the past 30 years, management of PDA has shifted from standard indomethacin and early/prophylactic PDA ligation to more conservative strategy of waiting for spontaneous closure or evidence of hemodynamic significance [9]. Sustained volume load due to PDA leads to early cardiac remodeling, an effect more pronounced with longer exposure [10, 11]. However, there is a critical knowledge gap regarding outcomes specifically in adults who had symptomatic PDA as preterm infants, as most long-term studies do not stratify by neonatal PDA status. Available evidence primarily addresses childhood outcomes rather than adult outcomes [12].

The long-term consequences of neonatal patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) in adults who were born premature remains unknown. We aimed to determine whether individuals born prematurely and diagnosed with symptomatic PDA during infancy have altered cardiac structure and function in late adolescence or early adulthood.

Methods

Participants who were born moderately to extremely premature (≤32 week gestation or <1500 g birth weight, current age 12–40 years born from 1986 to 2009) were recruited from the Parkland Health Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) Registry (Dallas, TX) [13]. The enrolled preterm participants were similar to the overall registry population with respect to neonatal characteristics. Exclusion criteria for all participants included inability to complete study procedures and BMI > 40. Neonatal data for preterm participants were available from the Parkland Health NICU registry. All adult participants provided written informed consent and adolescent participants provided assent along with parent/guardian consent before enrollment in the present study. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (STU- 2020-1332) at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This is an observational study based on retrospective data and sample size was based on available data.

For all participants, height, weight, vitals, echocardiography, pulmonary function tests, and labs were performed. Data comparing term versus preterm participants has previously been published [14–17]. Echocardiography was performed by certified cardiac sonographers masked to birth history. Study-specific imaging protocol was performed using a dedicated Philips Epiq ultrasound system (Andover, MA) at the University of Texas Southwestern echocardiography core laboratory (Dallas, TX). All studies were read at the core laboratory by the same echocardiography board-certified cardiologist (A.C.) with advanced training in echocardiography. The reader was also masked to participant birth history. Intraobserver variability was tested using intraclass correlation coefficient and exhibited an intraclass coefficient range between 0.93 and 0.99. Global longitudinal strain and left atrial (LA) strain were measured using speckle-tracking echocardiography. Images were analyzed offline using TomTec-Arena version 2.41 (TomTec, Unterschleißheim, Germany).

To measure LV ejection fraction and volumes, modified biplane Simpson’s rule was used. LV mass was derived from LV linear dimensions and indexed to body surface area per American Society of Echocardiography-recommended formula. Peak early diastolic tissue velocity (e’) was measured using tissue Doppler imaging from the septal and lateral aspects of the mitral annulus. Mitral inflow velocity was measured from the apical 4-chamber view using pulsed-wave Doppler. LA volume was assessed from apical 2- and 4-chamber views at end-systole using the modified biplane Simpson’s method and was indexed to body surface area. LA reservoir function, which represents LA filling during LV systole, was measured using peak strain during ventricular systole.

Data were initially grouped by presence or absence of symptomatic PDA during their NICU stay. This was defined as clinical evidence of large left-to-right shunt or congestive heart failure. Clinical evidence of large left-to-right shunt was defined as having at least 4 of the following criteria: 1) resting heart rate >150 bpm, 2) hyperdynamic precordium, 3) bounding pulses, 4) characteristic murmur, 5) cardiomegaly by chest radiograph, 6) enhanced pulmonary vasculature by chest radiograph, or 7) abnormal echocardiogram. Clinical evidence of congestive heart failure was defined as having at least 2 of the following items: 1) gallop rhythm, 2) pulmonary edema, 3) hepatomegaly, 4) apnea and bradycardia not explained by prematurity, or 5) hypercapnia. This approach to defining symptomatic PDA reflects real-world neonatal practice during the study period, prior to widespread adoption of standardized echocardiographic frameworks. Baseline demographic and anthropometric data were compared using unpaired t test for continuous variables and Fischer’s exact test for categorical variables. Least squares regression was used to determine the main effect of PDA after adjusting for age, sex, and gestational age. Linear regression was used to analyze the goodness of fit between diastolic blood pressure and tissue Doppler indexed e’ septal and lateral. For the patients with PDA closed surgically, date of surgical closure was used to assess the relationship between timing of closure and effect on blood pressure and diastolic function in adulthood using linear regression. Date of medical closure post-indomethacin was unavailable in the Parkland NICU Registry. The statistical analyses were performed using Prism GraphPad (Version 10, GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA).

Results

Of the 107 preterm participants, 23 had symptomatic PDA in infancy. Patients with PDA were born at earlier gestational ages (27.5 ± 2.0 versus 30.1 ± 2.4 weeks, p < 0.001) with lower birth weights (995 ± 286 versus 1409 ± 379 grams, p < 0.001) and longer hospital stays. As adults, the 2 groups were similar in terms of current age (26 years), sex (35% male), and BMI (32 kg/m2). All participants had normal fasting glucose and no chronic kidney disease at baseline. After adjustment for gestational age, sex, and current age, preterm participants with symptomatic PDA in infancy had non-significantly higher systolic blood pressure (125 ± 12 versus 121 ± 15 mmHg group, p = 0.06) but higher resting diastolic blood pressure (80 ± 10 versus 74 ± 12 mmHg, p = 0.002). Left ventricular structure and systolic function were similar between groups, including global longitudinal strain. The aortic root diameter indexed was similar, measured at end diastole. Tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) e’ septal was lower in the PDA group (9.5 ± 1.75 versus 10.7 ± 1.9 cm/s, p = 0.003). TDI e’ lateral was similarly lower (13.2 ± 2.5 versus 14.3 ± 2.5 cm/s, p = 0.04). Other markers of diastolic function including E/e’ ratio, E/A ratio, left atrial volume index and strain were similar. The PDA group also had larger RV end-diastolic diameter (34.5 ± 4.5 versus 33.0 ± 5.3 mm, p = 0.02) when adjusted via least squares regression for above factors. Markers of right ventricular function were similar between groups. Pulmonary function tests and markers of renal function were similar (Table 2).

The relationship between diastolic blood pressure and diastolic function was assessed via simple linear regression, which showed a statistically significant but weak association for e’ septal (R2 = 0.187, P < 0.001) and e’ lateral (R2 = 0.15, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1A, B). Of the 23 patients with symptomatic PDA, 2 required no treatment, while 21 were treated either medically (indomethacin, n = 12) and/or surgically (n = 9). For those treated surgically, the day of life at surgical closure was plotted versus blood pressure and diastolic function in adulthood. PDA surgical closure occurred on average at 19 days of life. The time to closure in days ranged from 6 to 32 days after birth. Earlier timing of closure was strongly associated with increased diastolic BP later in life (R2 0.64, p = 0.005), but not significantly associated with systolic blood pressure (R2 0.31, p = 0.09), and not related to diastolic function (Fig. 1C, D).

Discussion

Our study is the first description of long-term outcomes of symptomatic PDA in premature infants with respect to cardiovascular outcomes in adulthood. Of note, the findings must be interpreted within the context of an era-specific PDA phenotype, which is based on clinical criteria rather than contemporary standardized echocardiographic definitions of hemodynamically significant PDA but represents infants with clinically meaningful ductal disease. Neonates with symptomatic PDA in this study were in general younger gestational age, lower birth weight, and overall sicker (with lower APGARs, greater ventilation days), which are expected as these are risk factors for symptomatic PDA. The adult characteristics were well-matched between the groups. Here we demonstrate that after adjustment for current age, sex, and gestational age, individuals born preterm with a symptomatic PDA exhibited elevated diastolic blood pressure, lower diastolic function, and larger right ventricular size in early adulthood. There were no significant differences in renal and pulmonary outcomes between groups.

Those who had a history of symptomatic PDA in infancy had higher resting diastolic blood pressures, and this difference became more significant when adjusted for age, sex, and gestational age. Diastolic hypertension in young adults has been linked to multiple adverse outcomes, including increased risk of cardiovascular disease events, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and chronic kidney disease [18]. While the mean elevation in diastolic blood pressure in the PDA group was not high enough to be classified as hypertension, prior studies link subclinical elevation in diastolic blood pressure in young adults to abnormalities in systolic and diastolic ventricular function, particularly at ranges near the upper limit of normal [19]. Cumulative exposure to prolonged elevation in blood pressure in young adults, even at levels within normal range, has been linked to left ventricular systolic and diastolic dysfunction later in life [20].

Tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) velocities at the lateral and septal aspects of the mitral annulus were lower in the group with symptomatic PDA. TDI is a noninvasive assessment of LV relaxation, which is an important component of diastolic function [21]. Lateral e’ velocity can be 20 cm/s in healthy young adults and these decline with age. In adults over 30 years old, lateral e’ >12 cm/s is associated with normal LV diastolic function. In both preterm-born groups, the absolute values of the TDI velocities are normal, but overall lower than would be expected for the young age of study participants, and more significantly decreased in the group with history of symptomatic PDA. This signifies less robust cardiac relaxation and left ventricular filling.

The larger RV end-diastolic diameter (adjusted p-value 0.02) in the PDA group and shorter PA acceleration times (though not significant), could be early signs of increased pulmonary vascular resistance. It is possible that pulmonary over-circulation due to hemodynamically significant PDA leads to long-lasting impacts on pulmonary vascular development and thus increases lifetime risk for pulmonary arterial hypertension. This merits further attention in longer follow-up studies.

Simple linear regression showed a weak but statistically significant association between diastolic blood pressure and diastolic function. For the PDA treated surgically, earlier closure was associated with higher diastolic blood pressure later in life. This must be interpreted with caution given the small sample size. However, it fits favorably with current practice to delay closure. Alternatively, those closed earlier may have been more ill at an earlier age with greater symptoms attributed to PDA. While longer duration of significant PDA is associated with worse outcomes in prior studies, it is possible that there may be a sweet spot for age of closure, and that closure in the first 7 days of life was “too early” [22].

The incidence of PDA varies considerably between centers but can be as high as 60% in all preterm infants and 80% in infants with very low birth weight (<1500 g) [23]. A prior study from our institution postulated that reasons behind this lower incidence of PDA in some studies could be due to institution-specific factors like conservative fluid management strategies and differences in diagnostic criteria [24]. The gold standard for diagnosing a hsPDA is transthoracic echocardiography that uses a multiparametric assessment to evaluate the degree of pulmonary overcirculation and systemic hypoperfusion, in addition to basic parameters such as PDA size and shunt direction [25].

There has been wide practice pattern variation over the past several decades in management of PDA in preterm infants. Persistent PDA in preterm infants, particularly those with low gestation age or birth weight and decreased pulmonary vascular resistance, can lead to increased pulmonary blood flow, leading to edema and volume loading on the left heart. Ductal steal from systemic circulation may lead to adverse consequences in preterm infants, although a clear relationship has not been well defined [26]. Furthermore, therapies for closing PDA may also have side effects. Overall, there has been a worldwide trend towards less medical/surgical treatment of PDA in preterm infants over the past decade and more conservative management. Even in our institution, implementation of conservative management guidelines in 2010 led to a decreased frequency of medical and surgical intervention after this period [27]. The PDA-TOLERATE trial suggests that for preterm infants <28 weeks with a moderate-to-large PDA after the first week of life, an early and aggressive treatment strategy may not be beneficial and could be harmful [28]. Most trials looked at early versus later timing of intervention and showed no difference in outcomes, but there is a lack of randomized data comparing intervention to waiting for spontaneous closure (ongoing PIVOTAL study; NCT05547165). While there has overall been a trend toward less aggressive treatment of PDA in preterm infants, the pendulum has more recently swung towards identifying infants at highest risk for complications from PDA and focusing efforts on intervention in these groups, such as in extremely preterm and very low birth weight infants as spontaneous closure in this group is particularly low at 15% [29]. There is clear evidence that prolonged exposure to PDA is associated with negative outcomes as well [30], which further suggests that there is a subgroup of patients who should be treated for PDA. The long-term outcomes for these patients remain to be studied.

Closure of PDA immediately resolves the issue of left-to-right shunting, pulmonary over-circulation, and systemic hypoperfusion. In the immediate post-closure period, mean arterial pressure and systemic vascular resistance increases, and LV output decreases as preload decreases. In resetting the Starling curve, there may be a brief period of decreased contractility and need for increased support in a minority of patients [31]. However, the majority of studies in infancy and early childhood have not shown an association between PDA and lasting effects on cardiac structure and function [12, 32]. Importantly, in a recently published study, prolonged hemodynamically significant PDA (hsPDA) exposure beyond 21 days in the neonatal period did not appear to cause major additional cardiac alterations compared to shorter shunt durations in a preterm-born cohort at follow up of age 6.5 years [32]. However, a recent metanalysis suggests that prolonged exposure to PDA may be associated with increased risk of pulmonary vascular disease (related to bronchopulmonary dysplasia) in extremely preterm infants [33]. There is a lack of longer outcomes data beyond childhood on PDA in premature infants.

Limitations of the study include the retrospective observational nature of the data, which has intrinsic downsides including inability to control multiple possible confounding variables. Additionally, the small sample size, which is innately part of the difficulty of studying long-term outcomes in this patient population, can lead to increased variability in the data and potentially biased conclusions. In this study, it was not possible to quantify shunt volume or shunt burden, which would provide a more accurate measure of hemodynamic significance and true disease state. It is possible that the observed associations in this study may be attenuated by the inclusion of patients with low-volume shunts, though all patients met criteria for symptomatic PDA. Without neonatal echocardiographic data, we were unable to verify factors that are now considered to be associated with more hemodynamically significant PDA (size of shunt, left ventricular dilation). It is also impossible to tell how much of the differences between groups are due to extreme prematurity versus symptomatic PDA. While it is reassuring that similar results were confirmed with multivariate analysis adjusting for factors like gestation age, residual confounding remains possible and it is difficult to determine whether the observed adult cardiovascular differences reflect the direct effects of PDA physiology, the consequences of extreme prematurity, or an interaction between the two.

Another limitation is that there was no data available on confirmation of PDA closure. PDA treatment is not equivalent to shunt elimination. It is possible that there may have been infants in the intervention arm with continued ductal patency. Although the diagnosis of symptomatic PDA in this study was based on criteria agreed upon by clinicians involved in patient care, the subjectivity of decision making could create challenges in interpretation of data between the group of infants with or without symptomatic PDA. This also limits interpretation of the exploratory analysis examining timing of surgical closure and association with higher diastolic blood pressure, which was based on a very small subset of patients and may reflect confounding by indication, as infants undergoing earlier PDA ligation were likely the most clinically unstable. As a result, there is likely heterogeneity within the symptomatic PDA group, and misclassification bias cannot be excluded. Finally, this study identifies associations between symptomatic PDA in infancy and subclinical cardiovascular differences in early adulthood but does not establish causality. Nevertheless, the findings are clinically relevant, as the cohort represents infants with ductal disease severe enough to influence clinical management. These results support further prospective studies using standardized echocardiographic phenotyping and longitudinal assessment of cardiovascular outcomes.

In summary, adolescents and adults born preterm with symptomatic PDA exhibit elevated diastolic blood pressure, reduction in diastolic function, and larger right ventricular size. While there are several limitations with interpretation of these findings, this observational study raises important hypothesis-generating questions about the long-term cardiovascular implications of symptomatic PDA in preterm infants. The findings reinforce the need for future studies that prospectively define PDA using standardized echocardiographic criteria, quantify shunt exposure over time, and disentangle the effects of PDA from those of prematurity itself. Linking neonatal PDA phenotypes to adult clinical outcomes, rather than subclinical echocardiographic measures alone, will be essential to inform contemporary debates surrounding indications and timing of PDA intervention, as well as need for long-term follow up of our highest risk preterm infants.
