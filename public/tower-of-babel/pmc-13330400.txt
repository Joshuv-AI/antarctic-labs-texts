Myectomy with secondary chordal resection in hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy: the mid-term outcomes
Authors: Zhuheng Wu, Zedong He, Lin Xie, Songbo Zhang, Hong Qian, Yajiao Li, Yanlin Yang, Changping Gan, Ke Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt is not clear whether septal myectomy with secondary chordal resection (SM-SCR) can achieve similar postoperative outcomes among hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM) patients exhibiting different severity of septal hypertrophy.MethodsThis was a single-center retrospective cohort study with a nested case-control study. HOCM patients received SM-SCR between 2014 and 2023 were retrospectively included and were grouped based on their septal thickness. The main outcome was the relief of the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction. Besides, predictors for recurrent mitral regurgitation (MR) were also explored.ResultsA total of 88 patients were included, with 63 in the significant hypertrophy group (SH group) and 25 in the mild hypertrophy group (MH group). Background

Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM) is a hereditary cardiovascular disease characterized by septal hypertrophy and dynamic left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction [1–2]. Heart failure and malignant arrhythmia are the main clinical manifestations in HOCM patients [1]. Current treatment of HOCM mainly consists of pharmacotherapy and septal reduction therapy (SRT) [3]. SRT, which includes septal myectomy, alcohol septal ablation, and radiofrequency septal ablation, provides both LVOT obstruction relief and symptom improvements, and septal myectomy is still recommended as the standard surgical treatment for HOCM patients [3–5].

Systolic anterior motion (SAM) is the main cause of the LVOT obstruction in HOCM patients, and mitral valve abnormalities contribute to both SAM and LVOT obstruction [6–8]. Abnormalities of the mitral valve can be observed on the leaflets, chords and papillary muscles [8–9]. Many surgical strategies aiming to correct these mitral valve abnormalities have been proposed, but whether and how the mitral valve procedures should be performed during septal myectomy are still controversial [8, 10–14]. Septal myectomy with secondary chordal resection (SM-SCR) was first reported by Ferrazzi et al., with satisfactory outcomes achieved among patients with mild septal hypertrophy [13]. Further investigations pointed out that SM-SCR could also produce good results among unselected HOCM patients [15–16]. However, due to the lack of direct comparisons, there is currently little evidence that SM-SCR can achieve comparable outcomes between patients with significant and mild septal hypertrophy. Besides, around 2% – 5% of the HOCM patients experienced recurrent mitral regurgitation (MR) after SM-SCR, but the predictors are still unclear [15].

This study compared the postoperative outcomes after SM-SCR among patients with significant and mild septal hypertrophy, aiming to provide evidence that the procedure can achieve similar results for patients with different severity of septal hypertrophy. Meanwhile, we also tried to explore the predictors for recurrent MR after SM-SCR.

Methods

Patient population and study design

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study with a nested case-control study (Fig. 1). The cohort study included HOCM patients who received SM-SCR in our center from December 2014 to July 2023. The exclusion criteria were: (1) History of mitral valve surgery; (2) Active or a history of infective endocarditis; (3) Received mitral valve repair techniques other than secondary chordal resection. The included patients were divided into two groups according to the thickness of the interventricular septum (IVS) measured by preoperative echocardiography, namely, the significant hypertrophy group (SH group, IVS > 18 mm) and the mild hypertrophy group (MH group, IVS ≤ 18 mm) [24]. After the surgery, clinical and echocardiographic follow-up was carried out regularly via our outpatient clinic. The main outcome of the retrospective cohort study was the relief of the LOVT obstruction, reflected by the IVS and the LVOT gradients during the last follow-up.

In the following nested case-control study, cases were defined as patients with recurrent MR (new-onset MR ≥ moderate) during follow-up in both groups, and controls were selected from the remaining patients by propensity score matching (PSM). Among the patients been selected into the case-control study, immediate postoperative anterior mitral leaflet-annulus angle (AML-annulus angle), posterior mitral leaflet-annulus angle (PML-annulus angle), coaptation depth, and the left ventricular sphericity index (SI) were retrospectively measured. AML-annulus angle and PML-annulus angle was defined as the angle between the annular plane and a line that joins the anterior/posterior annulus and coaptation point at early systole (Fig. 2). The coaptation depth was defined as the distance between the coaptation point and the annular plane (Fig. 2), and the SI was calculated by dividing the left ventricle end-systolic short-axis dimension by the long-axis dimension. All the measurements mentioned above were obtained in the 4-chamber view.

Surgical procedure

At our center, the diagnosis of HOCM is established by integrating evidence from patient’s history, electrocardiography, resting or provoked echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, and genetic test. Specifically, an IVS ≥ 15 mm (≥ 13 mm for patients with a positive family history) in conjunction with a LVOT gradient ≥ 30 mmHg serves as the primary diagnostic criteria. The surgical indication for HOCM was the resting or provoked LVOT peak gradients ≥ 50 mmHg and symptoms refractory to maximum tolerated medical therapy [3]. The surgical procedure has been well described by previous studies [13, 15–16]. Abnormal secondary chords were defined as those significantly thickened and/or deformed the AML close to the IVS (Fig. 3a). After myectomy, distal and proximal parts of the abnormal secondary chords were cut, and the fibrotic tissue attached on the AML was also resected (Fig. 3b).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR), and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied for comparison. Categorical variables were presented as frequency (percentage), and Chi-Square test or the Fisher’s exact test was chosen for comparison. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to assess the recurrent MR, and log-rank test was applied to evaluate the significance. For our main outcome, sensitivity analysis was performed via covariates adjustment, with age, gender, pre-op LVOT gradients, and pre-op IVS thickness been sequentially adjusted in a linear regression model. To address non-normal distributions and adhere to model assumptions, right-skewed continuous variables underwent square-root transformation, whereas left-skewed variables were squared (covariates skewness can be seen in Fig. 4).

In the following nested case-control study, 1:2 PSM was conducted, with age, gender, IVS thickness, and follow-up time being the covariates, and the matching caliper was chosen as 0.2. After PSM, univariate conditional logistic regression was performed to identify potential predictors for recurrent MR. The significance level α was chosen as 0.05, and all P-values were 2-sided. Data analysis was performed with R version 4.3.1(R Development Core Team). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board [2023(2408)], and patients’ consents were waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total number of 88 patients were included, with 63 in the SH group, and 25 in the MH group. The baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. The median age of the included patients was 47.0 (33.0–54.0) years, and 43 (48.9%) of them were male. The overall median IVS was 21.0 (18.0–24.0) mm, with the septal hypertrophy more pronounced in the SH group (P < 0.001). The overall median LVOT gradient was 73.5 (55.0–93.0) mmHg, with no difference between groups (P = 0.224). Before surgery, 43 (48.9%) patients had MR ≥ moderate, and the preoperative MR severity was comparable between groups (P = 0.756).

There were no differences in the bypass time and clamp time between groups (P = 0.131 and 0.155 respectively), and the postoperative intensive care unit (ICU) stay was also similar (P = 0.846). The overall median follow-up time was 54 (33–79) months, with follow-up durations being similar between groups (P = 0.601). At the last follow-up, the overall median of IVS was 14.0 (12.0–16.0) mm, and the overall median LVOT gradient was 9.0 (6.0–12.5) mmHg. The IVS seemed slightly thinner in the MH group (P = 0.016 before adjustment), while the IVS reduction was more significant in the SH group (P < 0.001). The postoperative LVOT gradient was comparable between groups (P = 0.556 before adjustment), and the LVOT gradient reduction was also similar between groups (P = 0.212). In the sensitivity analysis, after covariates adjustment (Table 3), both the LVOT gradients and IVS thickness were similar between groups in the full model (P = 0.606 and 0.502 respectively). There are 5 (5.7%) patients had recurrent SAM during follow-up, and 1 of them had the LVOT gradient of 57 mmHg, who was closely followed at our center. Improvement in MR was observed in both groups, with 92.0% of the patients had MR ≤ mild at the last follow-up. A total of 7 (8.0%) patients developed recurrent MR during follow-up, with 5 (7.9%) in the SH group and 2 (8.0%) in the MH group (log-rank P = 0.879, Fig. 5). 83 patients (94.3%) were in NYHA functional class I – II during the last follow-up, with no difference between groups (P = 0.876).

Predictors for recurrent mitral regurgitation

In the nested case-control study, 7 patients with recurrent MR were defined as cases, and 14 patients were matched as controls. The characteristics for the 21 included patients are presented in Table 4. Preoperative LVOT gradient and postoperative SI were the only 2 variables with between groups significance (P = 0.046 and 0.003 respectively). Preoperative MR seemed to be more severe in the case group, but it failed to exhibit statistical difference (P = 0.126). Results of the conditional logistic regression are presented in Table 5, with an Odds Ratio (OR) > 1 indicating risk for recurrent MR. Based on the results, we failed to identify any predictors for recurrent MR.

Discussions

Septal myectomy is still the standard surgical treatment for patients with HOCM [3]. However, due to the high prevalence of mitral valve abnormalities among HOCM patients, concomitant mitral valve procedures are also common [8, 9, 17, 18]. Abnormalities of the mitral valve include leaflet elongation, increased leaflet laxity, leaflet retraction caused by secondary chords, hypertrophy and abnormal positioning of the papillary muscle and its direct insertion into the leaflets [8]. As a result, different surgical procedures targeting the leaflets, chords and papillary muscles have been proposed [10–13]. However, it’s worth noting that compared with septal myectomy alone, some of the mitral valve procedures may not only lengthen the bypass time but also increase the surgical complexity, besides, the effectiveness of some mitral valve procedures (such as leaflet plication and resection) is still controversial [8, 14, 23, 19, 20].

Secondary chordal resection in patients with HOCM was first described by Ferrazzi et al., originally applied to patients with mild septal hypertrophy [13]. The presence of abnormal secondary chordae tends to displace the mitral leaflets closer to the IVS, subjecting them to aberrant flow field within the LVOT, which in turn, exacerbates SAM and LVOT obstruction [8, 27]. Secondary chordal resection aimed to allow the tethered mitral leaflet to fall away from the septum, thereby eliminating SAM and LVOT obstruction. Subsequent studies have shown that SM-SCR can also yield good outcomes among unselected HOCM patients [15, 16]. Compared with myectomy alone, SM-SCR would not significantly increase the bypass time and might provide a greater relief of the LVOT obstruction [15]. Moreover, secondary chordal resection can also restore the mitral leaflets and annulus to the near-normal physiological position [13, 15]. The evidence mentioned above suggests that SM-SCR was a potentially better procedure for HOCM patients when compared with myectomy alone. However, none of the studies compared the surgical outcomes between patients with different severities of septal hypertrophy, leaving an important piece of evidence missing.

Based on our clinical experience, abnormal secondary chordae are relatively prevalent within our patient population. Thus, SM-SCR is our routine surgical strategy. The intraoperative identification of these abnormal chordae is based on two key criteria: 1. Morphological abnormalities: Chords thickening, attached to the septum, and the presence of a ‘doming’ deformity of the anterior leaflet, visible either via echocardiography or under direct visualization. 2. Tension testing: For chordae with normal or borderline morphology, we use forceps to displace the anterior leaflet away from the septum. If there are secondary chords become abnormally tense during this maneuver—specifically if their tension develops prior to that of the primary chords—it is also classified as abnormal.

In our included patients, expect for the IVS thickness, other baseline characteristics were all similar between groups, indicating that thinner IVS didn’t mean less severe LVOT obstruction. After SM-SCR, similar postoperative outcomes were observed among HOCM patients in the two groups, with IVS thickness and LVOT gradients markedly reduced, while MR and NYHA functional class improved. The overall postoperative IVS was 14.0 (12.0–16.0) mm in our study and the overall LVOT gradient was 9.0 (6.0–12.8) mmHg, which were similar to the previous results [13, 15, 16].

The safety profile was relatively satisfactory for both groups. There was no in-hospital death or stroke in our study, and our re-exploration for bleeding rate seemed to be lower than the previous results [15, 24]. Our pacemaker implantation rate was 3.4% (3/88), similar to the existed studies [15, 24]. Besides, we observed that the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) experienced a significant reduction in both groups after SM-SCR (P < 0.001 for both groups), but not a single individual had a postoperative LVEF less than 50%. We hypothesized two possible mechanisms for this: (1) Since MR had improved after the surgery, the LVEF was no longer overestimated, thus might explain the reduction. (2) The excision of a significant septal muscle mass might also contribute to this reduction.

In normal individuals, the secondary chords help to preserve ventricular shape and function during ejection [25]. Though secondary chordal resection has been used in valve repair among patients with ischemic MR, concerns have been raised about this technique [26]. However, current evidence suggests that secondary chordal resection in patients with HOCM is relatively safe and effective, without increasing the risk of recurrent MR [13, 15, 16]. Resection of the secondary chords can decrease the mitral valve tenting area and AML-annulus ratio, while it can also increase the aorto-mitral angle [13, 15]. These changes in the mitral valve geometry can not only enlarge the outflow tract but also make the mitral leaflet away from the abnormal ejection flow. A total number of 7 (8.0%) patients experienced recurrent MR in our study, which was slightly higher than the 2% – 5% rate reported by others [15, 24], warranting further exploration.

We assumed that the recurrent MR may be related to incomplete or over-resection of the secondary chords, which might result in residual tethering or valve prolapse respectively. Besides, we thought that recurrent MR might also be the result of morphological changes of the left ventricle, leading to additional tethering [21]. Moreover, preoperative severity of MR, preoperative left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV), postoperative AML-annulus angle, and postoperative coaptation depth were previously reported to be correlated with recurrent MR after mitral valve repair [21, 22]. However, we failed to identify any predictors for recurrent MR in the nested case-control study, which was consistent with the results reported by Lapenna et al. [24]. We assumed that this might be related to the difficulty in obtaining homogeneous results with such a small sample size in the case of diverse regurgitation mechanisms. In our findings, SI was the most significantly variable between the Case and Control groups. Although SI did not reach statistical significance in the subsequent regression model, this disparity suggests that patients in the Case group might exhibit more pronounced left ventricle remodeling. We hypothesize that left ventricle remodeling in HOCM patients could alter the geometric relationship between the subvalvular apparatus and the mitral valve leaflets, potentially inducing secondary tethering and contributing to subsequent recurrent MR. In all, considering the limited sample size of this study and the low possibility of recurrent MR after this procedure, perhaps large-sample and multi-center studies are needed to explore the predictors.

Currently, cardiac myosin inhibitors have demonstrated excellent short- and mid-term outcomes among HOCM patients [28, 29]. During the patient inclusion period of this study (2014–2023), cardiac myosin inhibitors were not a routinely available therapeutic option at our center; consequently, they did not significantly influence our clinical decision-making for HOCM patients during that timeframe. However, as clinical adoption expands and the evidence base grows, these inhibitors may serve as a valuable adjunct or even an alternative to surgical intervention. Notably, there is currently a paucity of head-to-head comparative studies between cardiac myosin inhibitors and surgical correction. High-quality randomized controlled trials or target trial emulations are still required to provide robust evidence in this evolving field.

There are several limitations to this study. Firstly, this was a single-center retrospective study, the results still await further confirmations. Secondly, the sample size of the study was relatively limited, which might influence the statistic power and restrict further detailed analysis. Thirdly, the number of patients with recurrent MR was relatively small, so it would be difficult to identify any specific predictors.

Conclusions

Based on our single-center retrospective data, for HOCM patients with different severity of septal hypertrophy, the mid-term outcomes after SM-SCR were comparable. Further investigations with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up durations are needed to confirm our results and to identify the predictors for recurrent MR.
