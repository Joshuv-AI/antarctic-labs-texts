Letter on "The cuneus vein: initial anatomical description and preservation technique for posterior interhemispheric approaches"—what defines an anatomic structure?
Authors: Helbert de Oliveira Manduca Palmiero, Eberval Gadelha Figueiredo
Published: 2026

It is interesting the article by Gurses et al., “The Cuneus Vein: Initial Anatomical Description and Preservation Technique for Posterior Interhemispheric Approaches,” published in Acta Neurochirurgica [3]. In a field where, since Rhoton’s foundational anatomical descriptions, genuinely novel venous nomenclature has remained rare, the authors demonstrate laudable anatomical curiosity. However, several points merit rigorous academic discussion.

Among 303 posterior interhemispheric approaches (PIAs) performed over 20 years, the authors identified “a previously unreported venous variation” — a cortical vein extending from the cuneus to the falx cerebri — in 12 cases (4%), which they designate the cuneus vein. This raises a fundamental question: what criteria must a structure meet to be granted an anatomical designation? Anatomical eponymy and nomenclature carry lasting implications for surgical education and operative communication. The central sulcus, for instance, is identifiable in virtually all human brains. At a 4% prevalence, the cuneus vein occupies a very different epidemiological tier. The authors candidly acknowledge they considered but rejected the term “occipital vein” due to pre-existing terminological conflicts. While this reasoning is appreciated, the proposed name itself warrants scrutiny: the cuneus is a broad, [3] wedge-shaped gyrus spanning the medial occipital lobe between the calcarine and parieto-occipital fissures, and the study does not clearly delineate whether this vein consistently originates from a specific subregion of the cuneus, from cortex overlying the lingual gyrus, or from the depth of the calcarine sulcus across cases. A more anatomically precise descriptor, corroborated by postoperative MR venography in successfully preserved cases, would substantially strengthen the nomenclatural claim.

One of the study’s most intriguing findings is the marked left-sided predominance: the cuneus vein was encountered in 10 of 146 left-sided PIAs (6.8%) versus only 2 of 157 right-sided approaches (1.3%). The authors acknowledge this asymmetry but offer no mechanistic explanation. We invite deeper reflection: could this reflect genuine neuroanatomical lateralization, perhaps related to the well-established leftward torque of the brain and the known rightward deviation of the superior sagittal sinus? [5] Alternatively, might a systematically different operative trajectory, retraction vector, or corridor inspection on the left bring a normally present but less prominent vein into operative view more consistently? Without cadaveric or large-cohort MR venographic data stratified by side, this lateralization remains a compelling but unexplained observation. The posterior interhemispheric approach, as detailed by Chi and Lawton [2], exploits gravity retraction of the ipsilateral occipital lobe. Whether systematic differences in gravity-assisted lobe displacement between left- and right-sided approaches alter vein exposure in the corridor deserves dedicated investigation.

Regarding clinical applicability: the authors report that the cuneus vein could not be reliably identified on preoperative MR venography in any of the 12 cases, even after retrospective review. They propose that a thinner-than-expected parieto-occipital vein may serve as an indirect radiologic clue. However, this surrogate marker is neither validated nor quantified. The paper would benefit considerably from a systematic analysis of parieto-occipital vein caliber across all 303 cases, comparing the 12 cuneus vein cases with the 291 without. If a caliber threshold could be established, it would represent a genuinely actionable preoperative finding. The potential utility of 7-Tesla MRI for improved detection, as suggested by the authors, is a promising direction meriting prospective validation, particularly given the functional stakes. The posterior interhemispheric approach is also routinely used for falcotentorial meningiomas and other deep lesions [1, 4], in which meticulous preoperative venous mapping is already considered mandatory. A validated preoperative signature for the cuneus vein would integrate naturally into existing surgical planning frameworks for these corridors.

The authors report preservation of the cuneus vein in 11 of 12 patients. In the single case of inadvertent injury, a patient with hydrocephalus, they report no hemorrhagic or ischemic morbidity. However, formal visual field testing could not be completed in this patient due to cognitive impairment. Therefore, the assertion of morbidity-free sacrifice rests on an incomplete functional assessment; subclinical or untested visual field defects cannot be excluded. This point deserves explicit acknowledgment, given that the striate cortex, comprising the cuneus and lingual gyrus along the calcarine fissure, is the primary visual cortex, and venous compromise in this territory carries a known risk of hemianopsia [3]. Rates of permanent visual field loss following surgery in this region range from 0 to 17% across published series [2]. A prospective protocol incorporating standardized automated perimetry before and after all PIAs would be invaluable for establishing the true functional consequences of this variant’s sacrifice.

The entire dataset derives from a single highly experienced neurosurgeon at a single institution, a design that ensures technical consistency but inevitably introduces ascertainment bias. A 4% prevalence may underestimate the true rate: might less experienced surgeons, operating more rapidly or without the same attentive corridor inspection, encounter and injure this vein without recognizing it? As with bridging vein considerations in the interhemispheric precuneus retrosplenial transfalcine approach, where preoperative venous mapping directly determines the operative side and corridor [1], multi-center corroboration or a prospective cadaveric anatomical study is needed to establish true population prevalence and confirm whether the cuneus vein represents a consistent anatomical variant or a rare surgical curiosity. As Nishizaki et al. noted in their description of the occipital interhemispheric approach for trigone meningiomas, special attention to bridging veins entering the vein of Galen for safe corridor navigation [4], and the same vigilance must now be extended to include the possibility of the cuneus vein.

In summary, Gurses et al. have made a thoughtful contribution to posterior interhemispheric surgical anatomy. The study merits commendation for its intellectual curiosity. However, returning to the cadaveric anatomy laboratory for systematic dissection of medial cortical venous structures across a large specimen series remains indispensable for definitively characterizing the cuneus vein as a true anatomical entity. Verification of its prevalence, topographic consistency, and drainage territory will be essential before this structure can be incorporated into the canonical venous anatomy of the posterior interhemispheric corridor.
