Interpretable wrapper-based machine learning framework for predicting patellofemoral pain syndrome using minimal clinical tests
Authors: Rajasekar Sannasi, Praveen Kumar Kandakurti, Thompson Stephan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClinical screening for patellofemoral pain syndrome (PFPS), a leading cause of anterior knee pain in athletes and physically active populations, often involves numerous physical tests. Such lengthy examinations are further compounded when considering the regional interdependence model. This study assessed whether near-full predictive accuracy could be achieved with a much smaller, clinician-interpretable subset of variables. We analyzed data from 70 participants with 29 biomechanical features. Nine classifiers were evaluated using stratified nested cross-validation. Eight population-based wrapper optimizers each performed ten runs of 100 iterations, and a stable subset was identified via weighted voting of feature-selection frequencies. Logistic regression, the top baseline model, was retrained on this subset. It was compared with its full-feature version using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Model behavior was explained with explainable AI techniques. The full-feature logistic model achieved 99.7% accuracy, and the 16-feature model maintained 97.1% accuracy (AUC = 0.998) with Holm-adjusted for all metrics, indicating no significant loss. Four mobility angles were dominant in global SHAP scores: trunk rotation, lumbar extension, hip internal rotation, and lumbar lateral flexion. The reduced test battery is expected to shorten the assessment time. These findings demonstrate that wrapper-guided feature reduction preserves predictive performance while enhancing transparency and efficiency, offering a practical, low-cost decision aid for PFPS screening and a reproducible approach to interpretable modeling in small biomechanical datasets.Keywords: Patellofemoral pain syndrome, Feature selection, Wrapper optimization, Classification, Interpretability, Explainable AI, Sports medicine, Metaheuristic, Musculoskeletal screeningSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome (PFPS), often referred to as “runner’s knee,” is a frequent condition among athletes, especially runners and participants in jumping or cutting sports, characterized by pain around or behind the patella1,2. PFPS is a prevalent musculoskeletal condition with an estimated lifetime prevalence of 23% to 29%, commonly affecting young active adults, elite athletes, and military personnel3. Among those affected, 12% are male and 15% are female4. PFPS is characterized by non-traumatic, diffuse pain in the anterior, retropatellar, or peripatellar regions of the knee, often aggravated by activities that increase joint loading, such as squatting, stair navigation, running, or prolonged sitting5,6. Clinical symptoms frequently include anterior knee pain, crepitus, sensations of giving way (commonly attributed to quadriceps dysfunction), occasional stiffness, and mild swelling5.

The multifactorial etiology of PFPS has complicated efforts to establish reliable screening and assessment protocols. Contributing biomechanical risk factors include abnormal patellofemoral joint loading, altered hip and trunk kinematics, and deficits in neuromuscular control6–8. In addition, muscle imbalances and kinetic chain dysfunctions across the trunk, hip, knee, and ankle influence patellofemoral joint stress during functional activities9,10.

Clinical management has traditionally emphasized quadriceps strengthening. However, growing evidence supports proximal-focused rehabilitation strategies that involve hip and core musculature, which have shown superior outcomes11,12. In addition, the Regional Interdependence model13 highlights the influence of remote biomechanical dysfunction, particularly lumbopelvic and hip impairments, on distal joint health. Lumbopelvic asymmetry, anterior pelvic tilt, dynamic knee valgus, and reduced trunk control have been documented in PFPS populations14–16. Core dysfunction and sacroiliac joint malalignment may further affect pelvic mechanics17.

To our knowledge, no studies have objectively quantified the association between pelvic asymmetry, lumbopelvic mobility, and PFPS. Establishing these relationships would provide further justification for integrating proximal assessments and targeted interventions into routine clinical management18,19. Therefore, we address a pivotal question: whether PFPS can be detected with near-maximal accuracy using a sparse, transparent, and clinically interpretable subset of biomechanical variables.

Clinical and biomechanical context

PFPS is characterized by diffuse retropatellar or peripatellar pain that worsens during activities such as running, stair climbing, or prolonged sitting. Although pain presentation is heterogeneous, abnormal patellofemoral joint loading, altered hip and trunk kinematics, and deficits in muscular control have been repeatedly implicated as contributing factors20. For example, Brancati et al. used a data mining framework on treadmill running trials to show that symptomatic runners could be distinguished from pain-free controls by combinations of knee flexion angle, hip abduction moment, and gluteus maximus activity21. Earlier work by Lai and colleagues demonstrated that ground-reaction forces (GRFs) alone can support automated recognition of PFPS gait via support-vector machines (SVMs) with leave-one-out accuracy approaching 85%22. Collectively, these studies highlight that discriminative signal exists, but it is distributed across multiple sensor domains and may evolve over the course of sustained activity21.

Rise of machine learning in musculoskeletal screening

Modern machine learning approaches have been increasingly applied to PFPS and related musculoskeletal problems, using combinations of gait kinematics, ground-reaction forces, and surface electromyography to distinguish symptomatic from asymptomatic individuals20–25. These studies report accuracies ranging from about 75% to the low 90% range and demonstrate that discriminative information is distributed across multiple sensor modalities. However, most models rely on complex architectures or data sources that are difficult to implement in routine clinical practice. The following integrated background and literature review (Sect. 1.7) summarizes these contributions in more detail and motivates our focus on a compact, interpretable screening panel built from low-cost clinical and goniometric tests.

Feature selection, optimization, and interpretability

High-dimensional biomedical datasets are prone to overfitting, particularly when sample sizes are small, as is common in biomechanics. Techniques such as recursive feature elimination, embedded regularizers, and swarm-intelligence search heuristics aim to identify compact subsets that preserve discriminative power. Combined pruning strategies tailored for nested cross-validation were recently shown to cut training loads by over 80% while safeguarding promising hyper-parameter trials26. In addition, ensemble feature-selection frameworks have proved effective in miRNA biomarker discovery for rare genetic diseases27, which underscores the transferability of rigorous selection pipelines. In imaging domains, isolation-forest variants28 and artificial bee-colony hybrids29 have simultaneously selected features and tuned neural-network parameters, realizing near-perfect classification accuracy. Similarly, optimization methods such as the spike-statistical flower-pollination algorithm have powered IoT-enabled seizure-detection pipelines30. Therefore, these advances show that multi-objective optimization can balance accuracy, subset size, and computational efficiency. Evidence outside medicine supports this premise, for example intelligent strategy selection in massive concrete cooling31 and sustainability-driven concrete mixture design32.

Gaps in current PFPS detection research

Notwithstanding promising accuracies, recent PFPS models exhibit three notable limitations. First, many rely on deep architectures whose decision boundaries are difficult to audit. While interpretability extensions such as attention maps or post hoc SHAP values offer insight, linear or shallow models with inherently transparent parameters remain preferable in clinical contexts. Second, few studies systematically examine whether their high accuracies can be maintained with fewer, more clinician-friendly predictors. Exceptions include the random forest reduction by Shi et al.23 and the FBI-GCN variable ablation of Xiong et al.25, yet both retained sensor channels impractical for routine examinations. Third, some validation procedures do not strictly separate feature selection, hyper-parameter tuning, and performance estimation, which can inflate reported metrics, a concern emphasized in reviews of injury-prediction ML studies33. Indeed, Dolatabadi et al. demonstrated the need for nested cross-validation to compare discriminative and generative gait classifiers fairly34. Beyond PFPS, shape-analysis work on patellofemoral instability (PFI) reinforces the promise of combining three-dimensional imaging with ML, and it also underscores the necessity of rigorous validation (SVM accuracy 0.91 with cross-validated AUC 0.94)35.

Study objectives

Guided by these observations, we adopt a transparent logistic-regression baseline and ask whether wrapper-based metaheuristics can locate a minimal subset of clinical tests and goniometric ranges that retains full-model accuracy. The workflow integrates: (i) repeated stratified nested cross-validation for unbiased performance estimation, following best practices outlined in combined-pruning research; (ii) eight population-based optimizers inspired by swarm intelligence and evolutionary computation, mirroring optimization successes in imaging and EEG seizure monitoring; (iii) weighted voting to assess feature stability, similar to ensemble biomarker discovery techniques; and (iv) a multi-level explainability framework that ensures the resulting model is interpretable and can be trusted by clinicians, and aligns with calls for explainable machine learning in sports medicine. By positioning feature reduction, statistical validation, and interpretability as coequal goals, we seek to move from mere classification accuracy toward deployable, clinician-centered decision support.

Background and literature review

Patellofemoral pain syndrome (PFPS) occupies a pivotal place in musculoskeletal research because it is both highly prevalent and biomechanically complex. Over the past two decades, investigative strategies have moved from isolated kinematic observations toward integrative machine-learning (ML) frameworks capable of digesting heterogeneous sensor streams. The following review synthesizes that evolution across five thematic pillars: gait and kinematic studies, data-driven PFPS classifiers, methodological advances in feature selection29 and optimization, explainable ML for sports-injury prediction, and cross-domain innovations that inform the present study.

Gait, kinematics, and traditional statistical analyses

Initial attempts to characterize PFPS relied on discrete biomechanical variables obtained during overground or treadmill walking. Lai et al. demonstrated that ground-reaction force (GRF) waveforms and rear-foot kinematics contained sufficient information to distinguish symptomatic from healthy gaits, achieving leave-one-out accuracies of 85.15% when GRF features were used in isolation and 88.89% after hill-climbing feature selection combined kinetic and kinematic measures22. These findings established two premises: PFPS manifests measurable deviations in lower-limb mechanics, and multivariate modeling enhances discrimination beyond univariate thresholds.

Brancati et al. later took a data-mining approach, applying principal component analysis (PCA) to high-dimensional running data and then training a logistic-regression classifier. Their model, trained on minute-one treadmill data, reached 82.76% accuracy with features centered on knee flexion angle, hip abduction moment, and gluteus maximus activation21. When the same runners were evaluated after twenty minutes, classification required a broader feature set, which implies that biomechanical adaptations unfold with fatigue or pain progression. Together, these studies illustrate both the richness of raw gait signals and the risk of information overload when feature counts escalate.

Machine-learning classifiers tailored to PFPS

Recognizing the curse of dimensionality, Shi et al. compared random-forest importance ranks against analysis-of-variance scores, then fed the six most salient running variables into a multilayer perceptron (MLP). They reported 75% accuracy, virtually identical to the full-variable baseline while reducing inputs by 40%23. The same research group subsequently introduced a dual-channel multi-input convolutional neural network (MI-CNN) that processed standardized and normalized time-series streams concurrently, boosting running-data sensitivity to 97.6% and overall accuracy to 89.0%24. A complementary one-dimensional CNN that incorporated an attention mechanism and focal loss achieved 92.4% accuracy on the same dataset, which underscores the benefit of addressing class imbalance and providing post hoc interpretability of channel salience20.

Graph-based deep learning has also entered the field. Xiong et al. proposed a fused-biometrics information graph convolutional network (FBI-GCN) that encoded subjects as nodes and demographic as well as gait features as weighted edges; after optimal variable selection, the model delivered 92.45% mean accuracy, tolerating a 40% reduction in test variables with only minimal performance loss25. While this approach captured inter-subject commonalities absent from earlier CNN pipelines, its clinical deployment is constrained by the need to construct graphs spanning multiple patients, an organizational overhead less suited to point-of-care screening.

Imaging-centered efforts offer parallel evidence that shape-based descriptors benefit from ML. Using three-dimensional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), Nagawa et al. quantified femoral trochlear morphology in patellofemoral instability (PFI) and reached 0.909 accuracy with a linear support-vector machine35. Although PFI and PFPS are distinct clinical conditions, the study validates the premise that subtle geometric cues, once distilled into statistical shape components, can power accurate classifiers.

Feature selection, optimization, and computational efficiency

High-dimensional biomedical datasets, especially those with small sample sizes typical of biomechanics studies, motivate careful feature selection and hyper-parameter tuning. May et al. addressed the computational burden of nested cross-validation by combining asynchronous successive-halving pruners with two domain-aware strategies, trimming up to 81.3% of model evaluations while preserving optimal solutions26. Their combined-pruning design informs the present study’s emphasis on time-efficient optimization. In addition, swarm-intelligence algorithms have proved adept at balancing exploration and exploitation in feature-selection landscapes. For example, Punitha et al. integrated Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) search with region-growing segmentation to classify COVID-19 lung CT scans, obtaining 92.37% accuracy36. A parallel investigation from the same authors hybridized ABC with immune-system and bacterial-foraging heuristics, achieving 99.54% classification accuracy in breast-cancer diagnosis while simultaneously reducing neural-network complexity29. Although these domains differ from PFPS, they show how bio-inspired metaheuristics can co-optimize feature subsets and model parameters, a strategy directly translatable to musculoskeletal screening.

The Flower-Pollination Algorithm (FPA) has likewise surfaced in Internet-of-Things (IoT) health monitoring. Yedurkar et al. deployed a spike-statistical FPA to derive spectral-verge features from EEG signals, pairing them with a convolutional neural network that reached 98.48% seizure-detection accuracy30. The success of FPA in high-frequency biomedical time series strengthens the case for its inclusion among the metaheuristics considered in the present work. Ensemble feature-selection frameworks further reinforce the value of aggregated importance metrics. Thelagathoti et al. required a minimal panel of ten miRNAs to differentiate Usher syndrome from controls with 97.7% accuracy, which illustrates that majority-vote convergence among multiple algorithms can stabilize biomarker discovery27. Such weighted-voting logic underpins the score aggregation adopted in our pipeline.

Explainable machine learning in sports and rehabilitation

Explainability is increasingly mandated for ML systems that influence clinical decisions. Calderón-Díaz et al. predicted hamstring strain injuries in professional soccer using tree-based ensembles and SHAP-derived importance rankings, ultimately identifying muscle strength and stiffness as principal risk factors37. Moustakidis et al. surveyed risk factors among Greek CrossFit practitioners, employing AdaBoost ensembles to achieve an area-under-the-curve (AUC) of 77.93% while clarifying the relative influence of six selected variables38. A recent scoping review encompassing 38 ML injury-prediction studies concluded that tree-based solutions, particularly Random Forest and XGBoost, dominated high-performing models, yet transparency remained an open challenge33. These findings motivate the present study’s choice of logistic regression as a transparent baseline coupled with a full battery of post hoc explainability tools.

Running-related injury prediction provides additional methodological cues. Han et al. integrated gradient-boosted decision trees, long short-term memory networks, and SVMs into an ensemble that analyzed wearable-sensor data from 84 runners, attributing 88.37% prediction accuracy chiefly to GRF, foot pressure, and stride-length features39. This pipeline validates the practical value of combining time-domain and frequency-domain metrics, a consideration echoed in surface-EMG-based PFPS classifiers20.

Studies on pathological gait detection beyond PFPS further attest to the necessity of nested cross-validation and feature-importance auditing. Dolatabadi et al. compared k-nearest neighbors and Gaussian-process latent variable models for stroke gait classification, emphasizing that discriminative models attained F1-scores above 0.95 under repeated folds, whereas generative approaches yielded micro-averaged error advantages34. Their granular cross-validation design informs the performance-estimation protocol employed herein.

Cross-domain insights on multi-objective optimization

Outside biomechanics, multi-objective optimization has matured into a robust paradigm capable of balancing accuracy, efficiency, and resource constraints. Ma et al. introduced an intelligent optimization model governing cooling strategies in massive concrete structures, simultaneously improving safety by 42% and cutting costs by 20.6%31. In construction materials, Wani et al. reviewed machine-learning applications for sustainable concrete and concluded that ML markedly accelerates mixture optimization while respecting environmental constraints32. These outcomes underscore the broader feasibility of Pareto-based decision-making, a principle mirrored in our stability-selection elbow rule that trades subset size against predictive fidelity.

Swarm-optimized artificial-intelligence frameworks also reveal productivity gains relevant to healthcare. Sharmila et al. designed a hybrid isolation-forest pipeline for knee-joint CT images, reaching 98.31% accuracy with reduced feature burdens and computational time28. Likewise, Punitha et al. showed that Artificial Bee Colony optimizers can simultaneously segment and classify medical images, which reinforces the adaptability of metaheuristics across imaging modalities36. These precedents justify including multiple population-based algorithms, Bat, Cuckoo Search, Differential Evolution, Firefly, Flower Pollination, Genetic Algorithm, Grey Wolf Optimizer, and Particle Swarm, in the present wrapper framework.

Methodological gaps and rationale for the present study

The surveyed literature collectively provides robust accuracies, yet it also exposes methodological gaps that impede clinical translation. First, deep-learning models often require large sample sizes, yet PFPS datasets typically contain fewer than 50 participants, which raises concerns about overfitting. Second, only a minority of studies perform rigorous nested cross-validation or Holm-adjusted statistical testing, steps crucial for unbiased performance estimation26,34. Third, feature-selection practices vary widely, and few works explicitly quantify the stability of selected variables across optimization runs, although ensemble biomarker studies highlight its importance27. Fourth, interpretability remains uneven: while SHAP or attention-based visualizations appear in recent papers, fully transparent baselines that can substitute deep networks without performance loss are seldom explored.

Addressing these issues, we design a six-stage pipeline that: (i) benchmarks nine classifiers on all 29 variables; (ii) employs eight metaheuristics to search feature subsets under nested cross-validation; (iii) aggregates inclusion frequencies via weighted voting, inspired by ensemble feature-selection precedents27; (iv) selects an elbow-knee threshold, a pragmatic surrogate for Pareto selection; (v) rebenchmark classifiers to verify ranking consistency; and (vi) subject the top logistic model to a comprehensive explainability audit aligned with best practices in sports-injury ML33,37. By merging high-fidelity performance with parsimony and transparency, we aim to fill the methodological gap between complex black-box networks and clinician-friendly decision tools.

Methodology

Overview of analytical workflow

We followed a six-stage pipeline designed to progress from a comprehensive baseline benchmark toward a clinically interpretable predictive model while maintaining statistical rigor at each decision point. In Stage 1, we trained and statistically ranked nine candidate classifiers on the full variables to establish an empirical upper bound on attainable performance. In Stage 2, we embedded a wrapper-based feature-selection (FS) procedure within the same nested cross-validation scheme (Table 3) to explore the combinatorial space of feature subsets. Eight population-based metaheuristic optimizers, Bat, Cuckoo Search, Differential Evolution, Firefly, Flower Pollination, Genetic Algorithm, Grey-Wolf Optimizer, and Particle Swarm, each executed runs of iterations with population size , yielding 10 Pareto-optimal subsets per optimizer. In Stage 3, we aggregated those candidate solutions by computing normalized selection frequencies and weighted ensemble scores as defined in Eq. 1, then applied an elbow + threshold to produce a single stable subset of 16 predictors. In Stage 4, we rebenchmarked the original classifier pool on to assess whether relative algorithmic rankings changed when the dimensionality was reduced. In Stage 5, we quantified pairwise differences between full- and trimmed-feature models via Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Eq. 2) with Holm correction and rank-biserial effect sizes (Eq. 3), confirming statistical equivalence for all metrics of interest. Finally, in Stage 6, we fit a logistic-regression model, the top performer in the prior stages, on and subjected it to a comprehensive battery of global and local explainability analyses.

As shown in Fig. 2, the analysis begins with the full 29-variable biomechanical dataset and passes it through eight independent metaheuristic wrappers to generate candidate feature subsets. A weighted-voting stability module then transforms those subsets into a single 16-predictor panel, upon which a fixed-hyperparameter logistic regression is trained and evaluated; downstream components compute metrics and a suite of explainability artifacts, culminating in a consolidated XAI report. Figure 2 highlights three methodological transitions of interest. First, wrapper optimization is clearly decoupled from stability voting, which ensures that subset selection is validated before model fitting. Second, the explainable model development block feeds its coefficients and predictions to both performance evaluation and layered explanation paths, underscoring the dual emphasis on accuracy and transparency. Finally, the converging arrows into the XAI findings node illustrate how global attributions and local case analyses were synthesized jointly, which reinforces the pipeline’s capacity to deliver clinician-readable insights alongside robust statistical evidence. This sequential architecture ensured that every downstream decision, subset selection, algorithm choice, and interpretability focus, was made only after quantitatively validating the preceding stage, thereby minimizing the risk of information leakage and overfitting. In addition, by keeping all hyperparameters fixed across full and trimmed experiments, we isolated the specific contribution of dimensionality reduction to model behavior. The explicit record of fold indices, random seeds, and optimizer trajectories, saved alongside result logs, permits full reproducibility and independent audit of each intermediate artifact.

Dataset description and preprocessing

We analyzed an anonymized cross-sectional matrix paired with a binary response vector . Column semantics, physical units, and clinical interpretations are documented in Table 1. Variables span four thematic blocks: demographics (AGE, GEN, BMI); questionnaire-derived activity (IPAQ); standard orthopedic provocation tests (SFT, SiFT, PSIS, PKF, SuLSI, ShT, GT); and quantitative range-of-motion goniometry (LF, LE, LLF, TR, HIR, HER), supplemented by pelvic asymmetry (PAsym). Side-specific variables (for example LLF_R/LLF_L and TR_R/TR_L) are coded by anatomical side (right vs. left) rather than by symptomatic vs. asymptomatic limb. For participants with unilateral PFPS, the painful side may therefore correspond to either the right or left measurement, and we did not recode features relative to the symptomatic limb. This design choice preserves the original clinical measurement workflow but may introduce additional noise into side-to-side comparisons, which is one reason why stability analyses and ensemble feature selection were emphasised in the later stages of the pipeline. The class label GRP encodes presence (A) or absence (B) of patellofemoral pain syndrome (PFPS), which was subsequently mapped to for model compatibility. Participants completed a structured battery of orthopedic provocation tests and range-of-motion (ROM) assessments targeting the sacroiliac joint, lumbopelvic control, and hip mobility, as summarized in Table 1. Sacroiliac and lumbopelvic clinical tests included the standing flexion test (SFT_R/L), sitting flexion test (SiFT_R/L), posterior superior iliac spine mobility test (PSIS_R/L), prone knee flexion (PKF_R/L), supine long-sitting test (SuLSI_R/L), Shimpi test (ShT_R/L), and Gillet test (GT_R/GT_RL). Goniometric ROM measures comprised lumbar flexion and extension (LF, LE), lumbar lateral flexion (LLF_R/L), trunk rotation (TR_R/L), hip internal and external rotation (HIR_R/L, HER_R/L), and pelvic asymmetry (PAsym). All ROM measurements were obtained using a standard clinical goniometer or tape-based procedures that are consistent with prior reliability work on trunk and pelvic motion40–44. Manual palpation and mobility tests for the sacroiliac joint followed published clinical descriptions of Shimpi and cluster-based sacroiliac test procedures45,46. For each test, the examiner recorded dichotomous outcomes (positive/negative) for provocation tests and continuous angles (degrees) for ROM measures. Raw measurements were documented on a structured case-report form and then transcribed into the analysis dataset, with range and consistency checks performed before statistical processing.

Figure 3 shows that the predictors do not exhibit extreme pairwise collinearity. All off-diagonal Pearson coefficients remain below about , and the largest absolute correlations are observed between PKF_R and GT_R (), GT_R and HER_R (), and ShT_R and LLF_L (). Most remaining pairs fall in the weak to moderate range (), which indicates that the clinical tests and goniometric measures capture partly distinct aspects of lumbopelvic and hip function. Side-specific versions of the same test show positive but not excessive associations, consistent with shared underlying constructs but clinically plausible left–right variability. Global variables such as BMI and AGE display only moderate correlations with each other () and with IPAQ (), and they are weakly related to most biomechanical measures. The pelvic asymmetry measure (PAsym) also shows only low to moderate links with other predictors, which suggests that it contributes largely independent information. Taken together with the high variance inflation factors observed for some variables, this pattern implies that multicollinearity arises from combinations of predictors rather than from any single pair with near-perfect correlation, so no individual predictor was removed solely on the basis of pairwise correlations. For the linear models, we used these diagnostics purely descriptively and relied on the wrapper-based selection to downweight redundant predictors rather than enforcing a hard VIF threshold, so that potentially informative but correlated tests were not excluded a priori. In the case of Gaussian Naive Bayes, we regarded the conditional-independence assumption as an approximation and interpreted its performance as a baseline comparator rather than a fully specified generative model.

Experimental design

Rigorous evaluation required a protocol that: (i) provides unbiased estimates of generalization error; (ii) supplies repeatable optimizer feedback for the wrapper phase; and (iii) yields paired score vectors suitable for nonparametric significance testing. Therefore, we adopted a nested, stratified cross-validation (CV) architecture, with control variables listed in Table 3. The outer loop comprised folds repeated times, yielding 25 statistically independent train–test partitions. Stratification preserved the PFPS/non-PFPS ratio within each split, which prevented prevalence drift that can inflate apparent accuracy in imbalanced scenarios. The inner loop () wrapped a grid search around each candidate classifier, so that hyperparameter tuning remained strictly nested and no information leaked from the test fold.

Nested cross-validation protocol

For each outer repetition r and fold index f, the dataset was partitioned into a training set (80%) and a holdout test set (20%). Within , the inner loop optimized algorithm hyperparameters by exhaustively enumerating discrete grids; candidate settings were scored by mean validation accuracy across the three inner folds. All preprocessing operations, including standardisation of continuous predictors and fitting of the logistic models used to score candidate hyperparameters, were refit from scratch within each inner training fold and then applied to the corresponding validation fold. No information from any outer-test or inner-validation partition was used to compute transformations for the folds in which performance was evaluated, which minimises the risk of inadvertent data leakage. The best-scoring configuration, with ties resolved by lower model complexity, was retrained on the full and then evaluated on . All scalar metrics were recorded, which produced a matrix per metric (rows = outer splits, columns = algorithms).

This design offers two advantages. First, the outer repeats attenuated “lucky split” variance, which is particularly salient in small-sample biomechanics studies. Second, the independence between inner validation and outer testing prevented hyperparameter overfitting, thereby securing an honest estimate of real-world performance. At the same time, the total sample size remains modest (), so even a carefully stratified nested design cannot eliminate all sampling variability. As a consequence, the reported performance estimates should be interpreted as proof-of-concept results, and some degradation of accuracy and calibration must be expected when the model is applied to new cohorts or to settings with different case-mix distributions.

Hyperparameter optimization strategy

Hyperparameter spaces were intentionally conservative to reflect clinically feasible deployment requirements (fast inference, low memory footprint). For instance, logistic regression traversed , while random forest searched , max_features , and min_samples_leaf . Each grid contained combinations, which capped inner-loop runtime at manageable limits on commodity hardware (4-core CPU (2.20 GHz), x86_64 architecture, and 12 GB RAM). To maintain comparability across algorithms, any setting that induced parameters was pruned before execution. Random seeds were fixed at to ensure determinism without sacrificing stochastic diversity across splits.

Performance metrics and statistical endpoints

Accuracy served as the optimizer objective and headline figure, and six supplementary metrics, precision, recall, F1, AUC, Cohen’s , and MCC, were logged to triangulate performance. Because the PFPS and control groups were exactly balanced (35 vs. 35 participants), we selected accuracy as a simple scalar objective for the wrappers and cross-validated benchmarking. This choice avoids the implicit weighting of classes that arises when prevalence is skewed. At the same time, we examined AUC, F1-score, , and MCC for every outer split, and the relative ranking of classifiers was consistent across these metrics (Table 4 and Fig. 5), which indicates that optimising for accuracy did not conceal poor performance on other key criteria. Because none of these measures follows a known analytic sampling distribution under stratified CV, we adopted the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (2) for pairwise algorithm comparisons. Holm step-down adjustment controlled the family-wise error rate at . The rank-biserial effect size (3) translated the W statistic into an interpretable scale, where was deemed a “large” effect in clinical-decision science.

Wrapper-based feature-selection strategy

The wrapper framework sought to maximise external validation accuracy while implicitly encouraging smaller feature sets; this was operationalised through the scalar fitness function J (Eq. 4) together with the minimum subset size constraint. Feature-selection masks were evaluated exclusively on inner-loop training and validation data: for each outer split, the wrappers operated on the training partition , and the selected subset was then refit and assessed on the held-out test set . At no point were test labels or test-fold feature statistics used during the search, ensuring that the reported outer performance reflects genuine generalisation. Weighted voting ensemble for feature stability

The wrapper stage generated candidate subsets per optimizer. Individually, these masks solve the optimization problem yet provide limited insight into which predictors show persistent discriminative value. To distill a consensus, we adopted a weighted-voting scheme that combines inclusion frequencies with optimizer reliability, thereby mitigating bias from any single heuristic. We adopted a weighted-voting scheme for two reasons. First, the eight metaheuristics differ in their exploration strategies and final accuracies, so giving slightly higher weight to optimisers with better outer-test performance discourages unstable features that arise mainly from weaker search dynamics. Second, the knee-based threshold on approximates a Pareto trade-off between subset size and stability: features that are both frequently selected and supported by several strong optimisers lie above the knee, whereas infrequently chosen or algorithm-specific predictors fall into the long tail and are excluded. This construction is consistent with general stability-selection principles, while remaining simple enough to reproduce in clinical ML pipelines.

Post-selection explainable-AI pipeline

Having shown that the 16-variable subset preserves classification fidelity, we then ran an interpretability-centered workflow. The pipeline comprised seven sequential modules. First, the trimmed data matrix was loaded and preprocessed: continuous features were z-scored using means and standard deviations from the outer training folds, while categorical factors retained their binary codes. A single stratified five-fold split of the full data provided train–test sets reserved exclusively for the explainability analysis, thus maintaining independence from earlier cross-validation. Second, we fit a logistic regression model (hyperparameters in Table 9) on the training partition; class imbalance was negligible (52.4% vs. 47.6%), so no adjustment was needed. The final coefficients and intercept were saved. Third, we computed global performance metrics, Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1, AUC, Cohen’s , and MCC, on the test partition and exported a confusion matrix. A 10-bin calibration curve was also generated, with its Brier score reported. Fourth, we obtained global feature attributions by running KernelSHAP to derive mean values, a beeswarm plot, and a horizontal bar chart; and by fitting a surrogate CART tree of depth 3 on the model logits, exporting its rules. Fifth, we produced local explanations for three representative test instances selected via k-medoids in SHAP space: SHAP waterfall plots of additive log-odds contributions, LIME tabular explanations over discretized feature intervals, and force plots. Sixth, for each numeric predictor we defined a 50-point grid across its observed range, plotted individual conditional expectation (ICE) curves, and overlaid their average partial dependence profile with 95% bootstrap confidence ribbons.

Results and discussion

This section presents the main quantitative findings of the study and places them in a clinical and methodological context. We first summarize the performance of the nine candidate classifiers and describe how metaheuristic wrapper methods identify a compact subset of biomechanically coherent predictors for patellofemoral pain syndrome (PFPS). We then compare the full and reduced models, examine their equivalence across multiple evaluation metrics, and discuss the clinical implications, limitations, and future research directions that follow from these results.

Classification and feature-selection findings

We begin by reporting baseline classification performance for all algorithms trained on the full 29-variable feature set, which establishes a reference for subsequent comparisons. We then describe the behavior of the metaheuristic wrapper procedures, including their convergence patterns, typical subset sizes, and agreement on selected predictors. On this basis, we derive the final 16-feature panel, evaluate its performance against the full model using nested cross-validation and Wilcoxon-based equivalence tests, and assess whether dimensionality reduction preserves accuracy, discrimination, and stability at a clinically acceptable level.

Across all seven evaluation criteria the linear pair, logistic regression (LogReg) and linear discriminant analysis (LDA), achieved the highest performance among the tested models, each exceeding 0.98 accuracy and 0.99 AUC. In agreement with those findings, Cohen’s () and Matthews correlation () approached their theoretical maxima, signaling almost flawless concordance beyond chance.

LDA followed closely with 0.9829 accuracy and an AUC of 0.9992, indicating that its Gaussian class-conditional assumption, though idealized, captures sufficient structure when all variables are present. The modest gap of in accuracy vs. LogReg translates into only extra misclassifications over the 25 outer folds, a difference too small to alter clinical decision pathways. Ensemble techniques partitioned into two strata. The upper stratum comprised Random Forest (RF) and Explainable Boosting Machine (EBM), each converging near 0.957 accuracy. Their robust performance stems from the ability to memorize axis-aligned splits while mitigating variance through bagging (RF) or additive constraints (EBM). Nevertheless, their deficit of percentage points relative to LogReg equates to one additional misclassification every 25 samples, a figure that may influence treatment allocation in borderline cases. The lower stratum, AdaBoost, XGBoost, LightGBM, and the depth-constrained decision tree (DT_small), fell below 0.91 accuracy and scored negative ranks in at least five of the seven Wilcoxon columns, underscoring systematic underperformance rather than sporadic fold-level misfortune.

Two methodological insights emerge. First, high dimensionality does not invariably necessitate complex nonlinear architectures; on this dataset linear boundaries suffice, likely because many clinical tests partition the feature space via threshold-like cutoffs that align naturally with logistic log-odds. Second, the AUC exhibited by LogReg hints at potential class separation margin that could be preserved with fewer variables, a hypothesis tested in Sec. 3.1. From an operational perspective, linear models enjoy superior inference speed ( ms per patient) and require minimal memory, qualities desirable for real time clinic deployment or embedding in mobile decision support apps.

In summary, the full-feature benchmark establishes a strong reference line: any dimensionality-reduced alternative must achieve accuracy and keep and MCC above 0.94 to claim parity. The next subsection evaluates whether the wrapper-selected subset satisfies these stringent criteria.

The bubble map shows consistently high frequencies for sacroiliac tests (ShT_L/R, SuLSI_L/R) and key mobility angles (LF, TR_R), which confirms their algorithm-independent importance and justifies keeping them in the final 16-feature panel selected for downstream modeling.

Classification performance on the optimal subset

Having fixed (Table 8), we re-executed the nested CV protocol on all nine algorithms to quantify performance in the reduced 16-dimensional space. As shown in Fig. 5, the left panel visualizes seven evaluation metrics for each classifier trained on either the full 29-variable set (“Full-”) or the 16-variable optimal subset (“Optimal-”), normalized to the 0–1 range for color consistency. The right panel translates these raw metrics into Wilcoxon signed-rank dominance scores and the resulting rank order, thereby providing a compact summary of how dimensionality reduction affected each algorithm’s relative standing. Inspection of the color gradients shows that logistic regression retains high performance after trimming (accuracy , AUC ), while LDA and Random Forest also preserve high values across all metrics. In contrast, LightGBM and the depth-limited decision tree occupy the lowest bands for both feature sets, indicating limited capacity to exploit either the full or reduced space. The Wilcoxon summary confirms these patterns: Optimal-LogReg attains the highest dominance score (38) and rank 1, narrowly ahead of Optimal-LDA (37), whereas both LightGBM variants remain bottom-ranked (). These findings confirm that feature reduction leaves the performance hierarchy essentially unchanged and establish the trimmed logistic model as the most reliable, interpretable choice for PFPS screening.

Relative to the baseline (Table 4), the trimmed logistic model forfeited only 2.6% in accuracy and 5.2% in MCC, both within the 95% confidence intervals of outer-fold variability. Precision increased by 0.9%, likely because removing noisy predictors reduced false positives. LDA retained an almost perfect AUC (), which indicates that separability remains intact along linear discriminants even after dimensionality reduction.

Wilcoxon dominance tallies echo the full-feature hierarchy: LogReg retains the top rank (aggregate 38) followed by LDA (37). Random Forest, while improving its absolute metrics relative to EBM, remains third (). The decision tree and LightGBM continue to underperform, which suggests that their depth and leaf constraints are too restrictive to exploit the reduced yet still heterogeneous feature set.

A trimmed logistic model that misclassifies fewer than three additional patients per hundred compared with its full-feature counterpart offers an attractive trade-off: halving input dimensionality reduces assessment time (fewer manual tests) and measurement error, therefore clinic throughput and adherence increase. From a computational standpoint, working with 16 rather than 29 predictors reduces both memory usage and training time in direct proportion to the number of features, but even the full logistic model is inexpensive to train and deploy on standard hardware. The main motivation for feature reduction in this study was therefore improved interpretability and a shorter, more feasible clinical examination, with computational gains regarded as a secondary but favourable consequence. In addition, the retained variables are readily obtainable without sophisticated equipment, which enhances feasibility of deployment in resource-limited settings.

Surrogate tree interpretation

We fitted a depth-3 surrogate CART (Fig. 10), which replicated 93.5% of logistic-model predictions on the test set and yielded the following rule set:

IF TR_R > 42 AND LE > 20 THEN PFPS (probability = 0.91).

ELSE IF ShT_R 1 AND HIR_R > 34 THEN PFPS (probability = 0.82).

ELSE non-PFPS.

As shown in Fig. 10, the surrogate decision tree compresses the logistic model’s probability surface into three hierarchical splits: lumbar extension (LE), pelvic asymmetry (PAsym), and trunk rotation right (TR_R), with a subsidiary threshold for hip internal rotation right (HIR_R). The left branch (LE ) routes all 24 observations to the PFPS class without error, while the right branch applies PAsym and TR_R gates to separate the remaining samples. This pattern confirms earlier SHAP and LIME findings: low lumbar extension alone is sufficient for PFPS assignment, whereas higher extension requires additional evidence, either low pelvic tilt or elevated axial rotation, to overcome the base rate. The Gini indices indicate that each node contributes substantial purity gains (Gini at the root), and the overall 93.5% fidelity shows that these four thresholds capture the dominant decision logic. Clinically, the tree offers a rapid bedside heuristic: limited extension suggests PFPS. If extension is normal, clinicians should next check pelvic symmetry and axial rotation before concluding.

Across global, local, and rule-based lenses, the same quartet of mechanical ranges (TR_R, LE, HIR_R, LLF_L) emerged as principal drivers, flanked by sacroiliac mobility tests that register asymmetry signals (PSIS_L, SuLSI_L/R, ShT_L/R). This redundancy in explanatory evidence supports clinical adoption: clinicians need to measure only a dozen straightforward goniometric angles and manual tests to achieve near-expert classification accuracy, and they can see how each measurement affects the predicted probability.

Biomechanical interpretation of selected features

The wrapper-based machine learning pipeline identified four mobility-related predictors, lumbar extension, trunk rotation, lumbar lateral flexion, and hip internal rotation, together with pelvic asymmetry and sacroiliac joint (SIJ) test outcomes, as the most stable and discriminative features for patellofemoral pain syndrome (PFPS). Interpreting these variables within a biomechanical framework helps to explain why they contribute strongly to classification.

Pelvic asymmetry emerged as a strong predictor in the feature-selection analysis. In the parallel case–control dataset, participants with PFPS showed greater iliac crest height asymmetry and a higher prevalence of SIJ hypomobility than controls. These proximal dysfunctions can disturb load transfer through the lumbopelvic–hip complex and may promote dynamic knee valgus and altered patellofemoral joint (PFJ) stress. Prior work has reported that malalignment and SIJ restrictions can change trunk–hip coordination and increase patellofemoral loading during weight-bearing tasks47. The importance of pelvic asymmetry and SIJ findings in the model is therefore consistent with existing biomechanical theory.

Lumbar spine mobility also played a central role. Restricted lumbar flexion, extension, and lateral flexion were observed among PFPS participants in the clinical dataset, and the same parameters dominated the SHAP importance scores in the machine learning model. This pattern suggests that reduced spinal mobility is not only statistically relevant but also mechanistically meaningful. Limited lumbar motion may reduce the ability of the trunk to absorb and redistribute ground reaction forces, so abnormal loads are redirected to the knee14,19.

Trunk axial rotation was another influential predictor. Reduced trunk rotation has been linked to compensatory femoral internal rotation and knee valgus collapse, both of which increase PFJ contact forces15,16. In the present analysis, trunk rotation remained important across multiple feature-selection runs, which shows that its contribution is both biomechanically plausible and statistically stable.

Hip rotation findings further support the “hip theory” of PFPS. Limited hip external and internal rotation were present in the case–control dataset, which agrees with the idea that weakness or stiffness of the hip rotator muscles contributes to medial collapse of the femur and higher PFJ stress48. The prominence of hip internal rotation in the machine learning model strengthens the view that altered hip mechanics are a key discriminator between PFPS and non-PFPS participants.

Taken together, these results show that objective biomechanical impairments, pelvic asymmetry, SIJ hypomobility, and restricted lumbopelvic–hip mobility, align closely with the features that the data-driven pipeline selected. This agreement reinforces the regional interdependence model and supports the conclusion that proximal dysfunction is central to PFPS pathomechanics. By integrating standard clinical measurements with explainable AI outputs, the study provides converging evidence that these proximal factors should be prioritized in both screening and rehabilitation strategies for PFPS.

Limitations and future directions

The main limitations of this study are the modest sample size () and the single-center recruitment. Both factors may limit the generalizability of the findings and make the results more dependent on the specific mix of PFPS and control participants in our cohort. Although repeated nested cross-validation attenuates “lucky split” effects, near-perfect accuracies obtained on small datasets can still be optimistic, particularly when class boundaries are well separated. Future work should therefore prioritise external validation on independent samples drawn from different clinical settings and activity levels, including both recreational and elite athletes, and should quantify the stability of the 16-feature subset and the calibration of predicted probabilities across such cohorts. In addition, we did not explicitly model measurement error or inter-rater variability in the clinical and goniometric tests; all predictors were treated as exact inputs. In practice, examiner technique, patient effort, and instrument positioning can introduce random and systematic error into goniometric and sacroiliac assessments, which may attenuate true effect sizes and reduce the reproducibility of the learned coefficients. Future studies should therefore incorporate inter- and intra-rater reliability assessments and, where feasible, use statistical models that propagate measurement uncertainty into confidence intervals for predicted risk. Goniometry and sacroiliac provocation tests have known measurement uncertainty, which may attenuate true effect sizes and could influence the robustness of the learned decision boundary. Subsequent studies should incorporate repeated measurements or reliability studies to characterise variability in these assessments and, where feasible, propagate this uncertainty into the modelling framework. Subsequent research could explore more aggressive sparsity-inducing criteria or reinforcement-learning searches to determine whether a still smaller panel of, for example, 8–10 tests can preserve most of the predictive signal without sacrificing stability.

Conclusion

We set out to determine whether a smaller set of clinical measures can match the predictive accuracy of the full 29-variable model for patellofemoral pain syndrome (PFPS) while remaining interpretable. We found that a wrapper ensemble combined with weighted stability voting reduced dimensionality by to a 16-feature subset. Logistic regression accuracy decreased by 2.6 percentage points to , and no metric differed significantly after Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon tests. The retained variables cluster around sacroiliac mobility, lumbopelvic ranges, and hip rotation, which aligns with current biomechanical theories and reinforces their clinical relevance. Because all selected tests rely on low-cost goniometry or manual palpation, clinics can shorten assessments without sacrificing predictive performance. In addition, the model’s coefficients, SHAP plots, and a depth-3 surrogate tree together provide transparent case-level explanations. In summary, the proposed pipeline unites parsimony, accuracy, and explainability, offering a practical decision support tool that can be deployed in routine musculoskeletal screening while advancing understanding of PFPS biomechanics.
