The CoinAlg Bind: Profitability-Fairness Tradeoffs in Collective Investment Algorithms
Andrés FábregaCornell Tech, IC3, James AustgenCornell Tech, IC3Samuel BreckenridgeCornell Tech, IC3Jay YuPantera CapitalAmy ZhaoAva Labs, IC3Sarah AllenFlashbots, IC3Aditya SarafCornell Tech, IC3Ari JuelsCornell Tech, IC3

Abstract
Co llective In vestment Alg orithms (CoinAlgs) are increasingly popular systems that deploy shared trading strategies for investor communities. Their goal is to democratize sophisticated—often AI-based—investing tools. We identify and demonstrate a fundamental profitability-fairness tradeoff in CoinAlgs that we call the CoinAlg Bind : CoinAlgs cannot ensure economic fairness without losing profit to arbitrage. We present a formal model of CoinAlgs, with definitions of privacy (incomplete algorithm disclosure) and economic fairness (value extraction by an adversarial insider). We prove two complementary results that together demonstrate the CoinAlg Bind. First, privacy in a CoinAlg is a precondition for insider attacks on economic fairness. Conversely, in a game-theoretic model, lack of privacy, i.e., transparency , enables arbitrageurs to erode the profitability of a CoinAlg. Using data from Uniswap, a decentralized exchange, we empirically study both sides of the CoinAlg Bind. We quantify the impact of arbitrage against transparent CoinAlgs. We show the risks posed by a private CoinAlg: Even low-bandwidth covert-channel information leakage enables unfair value extraction.

Introduction

This paper initiates the study of tradeoffs in what we call
Co
llective
In
vestment
Alg
orithms
(
CoinAlgs
)—algorithms shared by a community of users to dictate or execute financial transactions on their behalf in pursuit of profitable investment strategies.

CoinAlgs have long existed in traditional finance. For example, quantitative strategies at firms like BlackRock Systematic or Renaissance Technologies execute trades across many client portfolios. Variants also exist in the form of robo-advisers, e.g., Wealthfront, that use shared AI engines for financial planning.

CoinAlgs have recently gained traction outside traditional financial firms.
AI-based trading software packages for individual investors have become available for both crypto and traditional financial assets, e.g.,
[
26
,
33
]
.
In decentralized finance (DeFi), ElizaOS (token AI16Z), “one of the first known AI-controlled venture capital funds,” achieved a peak market capitalization of $2+ billion
[
7
,
5
]
, while Truth Terminal, an AI agent that drives community trading with tweets on X, initiated the launch of Goatseus Maximus (GOAT), a memecoin with a peak market capitalization of $1+ billion
[
34
,
31
]
.

CoinAlgs in general promise to place institutional-grade investment strategies or specialized trading intelligence within the reach of retail investors. Newly emerging ones, though—direct-to-consumer trading tools and AI-based crypto platforms—lack the investor protections of regulated intermediaries. We therefore ask: What design tradeoffs and risks are inherent in CoinAlgs?

In this work, we prove the existence of a fundamental dilemma for CoinAlgs’ designers and users. A CoinAlg’s trading strategy must be at least partially
private
or else
transparent
. Both options, though, place investor profits at risk:

•

If
private
, a CoinAlg can leak information to insiders (through, e.g., a covert channel), enabling unfair and potentially undetectable insider
value extraction
from investors.

•

If
transparent
, a CoinAlg leaks information publicly, exposing it to arbitrage and degraded profits—what we term a
cost of transparency
.

We call this tension the
CoinAlg Bind
: Any CoinAlg must sacrifice either economic fairness or profitability.

1.1
Our Results

Our work represents the first formal treatment of the tradeoff expressed by the CoinAlg Bind.
It comprises three main results, on the privacy, transparency, and empirical investigation of CoinAlgs.

CoinAlg privacy and economic (un)fairness.

We define a CoinAlg as
private
if there is a non-negligible statistical distance between a probability distribution over expected trades induced by a public view of CoinAlg and the actual, executed trades of the CoinAlg. In other words, the public view is insufficient to predict its transactions. We present a formal definition of CoinAlg privacy in our work.

We also present a definition of
fairness
as a measure of
value extraction
by an adversary that uses inside knowledge to exploit (e.g., sandwich) the transactions of a CoinAlg. This definition is an insider variant of similar concepts in decentralized finance (e.g., “miner / maximal extractable value” (MEV))
[
1
,
8
,
27
,
36
]
.

Our main result here (
Theorem

1
) is that
economic unfairness implies privacy
. Intuitively, privacy is necessary to give an advantage to an adversarial insider. (The converse doesn’t always hold: Privacy need not mean that an adversary has actionable insider information.)

Transparency in CoinAlgs.

We also explore CoinAlgs that are not private, but
transparent
. Public access to a transparent CoinAlg—e.g., open-sourcing its algorithm—enables advance knowledge of its transactions. We demonstrate that this exposes a CoinAlg to arbitrage by an adversary
{\cal A}
. Our transparency model in practice relies only on the weak assumption that
{\cal A}
can simulate the CoinAlg; it doesn’t require
{\cal A}
to reverse-engineer the CoinAlg.

Modeling the interaction between
{\cal A}
and a transparent CoinAlg as an ultimatum game, we show that
{\cal A}
can use a
grim-trigger
strategy—the threat of invalidating the CoinAlg’s transactions—to force CoinAlg exposure to arbitrage by
{\cal A}
(Theorem
4
). The ideas behind our model may be of independent interest, as they involve a
repeated game
where
intentional transaction invalidation
forms the basis of a trigger strategy.

Experiments.

Using historical transactions in a decentralized exchange (Uniswap V3) on Ethereum for a period of over one year (June 2024 to July 2025), we simulate a profitable CoinAlg that correctly predicts future asset prices. We study both sides of the CoinAlg Bind. We demonstrate that even minimal information leakage from a private CoinAlg enables significant value extraction. We show conversely that even moderate defensive strategies against arbitrage incur a significant cost of transparency and thus eroded profits.

While we focus on crypto CoinAlgs for concreteness, our basic results apply more generally. For example, they
emphasize why the investor protections of traditional finance are important to protect against value-extraction attacks in private CoinAlgs.

2
What are CoinAlgs?

Two primary characteristics define CoinAlgs. They are:

1.

Collective
: Users in a CoinAlg are bound by a
shared
trading logic that drives investments.

2.

Algorithmic
: The trading logic of a CoinAlg is
algorithmic
in nature.

In other words, CoinAlgs are
algorithms that drive collective investment actions
. These two defining properties are what give relevance to the CoinAlg Bind: collectivity introduces fiduciary and thus fairness obligations, while the algorithmic nature introduces the potential for transparency or privacy.

Categories of CoinAlgs.

A number of seemingly unrelated financial services—across both traditional finance and Web3—come under the umbrella of CoinAlgs. Key among these are:

Traditional-finance algorithmic trading.
Collective management of investor assets is a well-established practice in traditional finance (a.k.a TradFi), including at high-frequency trading (HFT) firms, hedge funds, and quantitative investment firms. Their platforms are often explicitly CoinAlgs, pooling investors’ assets and executing trades algorithmically on their behalf.

Other examples of TradFi CoinAlgs are more subtle, involving investors sharing trading algorithms but acting independently. Robo-advisors—offered by many established TradFi firms (Vanguard, Schwab, etc.)—are an example, although customized to individual goals. eToro, a popular social-trading platform, allows explicit investor-to-investor replication of trading algorithms.

Direct-to-investor platforms.

Direct-to-investor algorithmic-trading platforms—such as Composer and QuantConnect for equities and Zignaly for crypto—enable individual investors to develop, share, or license trading algorithms while retaining independent control of their assets. As they allow communities of investors to share trading logic, they constitute CoinAlgs.

AI-powered investment DAOs and communities.
In DeFi, there has been a flurry of interest in coupling AI-powered investment algorithms with Decentralized Autonomous Organizations (DAOs)—smart contracts in which investors pool funds. Prominent examples of such CoinAlgs include AI16Z and AI-XBT. A less obvious form of CoinAlg is Terminal of Truths, described in
Section

1
.

Among these three types of CoinAlgs, those in
TradFi are
custodial
, meaning that firms hold investors’ funds as broker-dealers and are subject to investor-protection regulations. Direct-to-investor and DeFi CoinAlgs are typically
non-custodial
, placing them outside traditional investor-protection regimes.

Our definition of CoinAlgs excludes many popular financial services. For example, index funds in TradFi are not CoinAlgs: they do not operate algorithmically, so there is no opportunity for transparency.

Properties of CoinAlgs.

As our examples illustrate, CoinAlgs can differ along two dimensions.

First, the way in which
user assets are managed
. This can be
consolidated
, which means that assets are pooled together in a central location (e.g., an AI-powered DAO, quantitative investment firm, etc.); or it can be
fragmented
, which means that assets are traded individually (e.g., robo advisors).

The second dimension is the way in which the CoinAlg’s
trading algorithm is accessed
. This can once again be consolidated or fragmented: the former refers to when the algorithm is in a single, central location (e.g., robo-advisors); and the latter to when multiple copies are distributed (e.g., open-source trading bots).

CoinAlgs in TradFi vs. DeFi

While CoinAlgs exist in both TradFi and DeFi, we focus on the latter in this work for two reasons. First, DeFi CoinAlgs lend themselves to
crisper modeling
—blockchains provide natural abstractions which are much simpler than those of traditional markets—and are more amenable to formal study. Second, the open nature of DeFi provides a natural testbed for
empirical studies
of CoinAlgs (see, e.g., Section
6
), due to better data availability supporting more realistic experiments and simulations.

We stress, however, that our results point to a fundamental tension in CoinAlgs that applies broadly, including to TradFi. In TradFi, the CoinAlg Bind is addressed via legally required
investor protections
, which allow CoinAlgs to be opaque in order to safeguard profits. Non-custodial CoinAlgs raise strong concerns because they lack these protections.

Formal Model for CoinAlgs

To study the CoinAlg Bind theoretically, we first present a formal model for CoinAlgs more broadly.
We introduce a general model for blockchain systems (drawing inspiration from prior work
[
1
,
13
]
), followed by our definition for CoinAlgs in the context of this model, including a formal definition of privacy for CoinAlgs.

System abstraction.

We denote the
state
of the system (blockchain) by
\mathcal{S}
, and the universe of all possible states by
\mathbb{S}
.
Valid
state transitions
are defined by the function
{\textsf{transition}}:\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{S}\to\{0,1\}
, which returns 1 if and only if the second input state is reachable from the first. We call a finite sequence of states
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{S}_{1},...,\mathcal{S}_{s})
a
transcript
, and say that a transcript is
valid
if
{\textsf{transition}}(\mathcal{S}_{i},\mathcal{S}_{i+1})=1
for every
i\in[q)
.

The system is composed of a set of
players

{\mathcal{P}}=\{P_{1},...,P_{p}\}
, which hold positions on a set of tradable assets
\mathbb{A}=\{A_{1},...,A_{a}\}
. A particular system state is thus defined by the balance of all players across all assets.

We denote trades via tuples of the form

\mathcal{S}

The state transition as a result of trade

{\textsf{action}}:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{S}\cup\{\bot\}
, which returns
\bot

The number of units acquired during the trade is a function of current
asset prices
, which we model via function
{\textsf{price}}:\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}

State transitions are triggered at fixed intervals (e.g., approximately 12 seconds for Ethereum), which execute (a subset of) all trades accumulated after the previous state transition. Given the present state
\mathcal{S}_{0}
and a

{\textsf{action}}(\tau_{q},\mathcal{S}_{q-1})

\bot

\bot

The trades that are included in a block, and their order, are determined by a system-specific protocol.

CoinAlgs model.

In our system abstraction, a CoinAlg
\mathcal{C}
is a player in the system that makes trades according to an
investment strategy

\textsf{ALG}:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{T}\cup\{\bot\}
, which is an algorithm that returns a trade to be made for a given input state (or
\bot
, if no trades would be made). We denote the space of all possible investment strategy functions by
\mathbb{F}
. Critically, the behavior of
\mathcal{C}
is fully determined by
ALG
, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}
will always make a trade output by
\textsf{ALG}(\mathcal{S})
in state
\mathcal{S}
.

The trading algorithm
ALG
may be deterministic or randomized. To capture this, we model
ALG
as consisting of two logical subroutines: (1)
\textsf{ALG}_{\textsf{dist}}(\mathcal{S})
, which (deterministically) outputs a probability distribution
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}
over
\mathbb{T}
; followed by (2)
\textsf{ALG}_{\textsf{sample}}(\pi_{\mathcal{S}})
, which samples a specific trade
\tau
from probability distribution
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}
. So, for example, a
deterministic
CoinAlg is one for which
\textsf{ALG}_{\textsf{dist}}(\mathcal{S})
always outputs a degenerate distribution.

Privacy of CoinAlgs.

We now formally define privacy for CoinAlgs, a necessary precondition for the principled analysis of the CoinAlg Bind.

At a high level, we characterize the privacy of
\mathcal{C}
in terms of
the ability of players to predict
\mathcal{C}
’s trades in advance
. Notice that our privacy notion is framed in terms of
capabilities
of players (i.e., predict trades) as opposed to specific
properties
of
\mathcal{C}
(e.g., whether
\mathcal{C}
’s code is open-source or not). As we show in future sections, predicting trades is at the core of the CoinAlg Bind. We thus focus directly on the impact of (lack of) privacy, while abstracting away the mechanisms of how information is disclosed.

For instance, say that
\mathcal{C}
is fully transparent, so players can perfectly predict (distributions of) trades in advance. This could stem from the specification of
ALG
being open-source (i.e., players having white-box access to
ALG
), or from players having just black-box, but unfettered, query access to
ALG
. For both, what matters is that players can accurately predict
trades (which, as we show in Section
5
, undermines
\mathcal{C}
’s profits)
trades, and not the mechanics of
how
this information is obtained.

To model this formally, we introduce the notion of
public functions
, which “flatten”
ALG
to capture properties of future trades that are revealed to the public. (Without loss of generality, we assume, for now, that all players share the same knowledge of
ALG
.

(1)

Full privacy

(2)

\displaystyle(\textsf{ALG},\mathcal{S})=\big\{

Unlike
\mathcal{L}_{\textsf{asset}}

\mathcal{L}_{\textsf{dir}}

(4)

Public functions serve as theoretical abstractions to model the behavior of CoinAlgs revealed to other system players. For example, as mentioned earlier,
\mathcal{L}_{\textsf{transp}}
could arise from white- or black-box access to

ALG
, or even more contrived scenarios like past trades from
\mathcal{C}
leaking future trades that will be made.
Also notice that, depending on whether
ALG
is deterministic or randomized, so is
\mathcal{L}
.

Equipped with public functions, we can now define the privacy of
\mathcal{C}
as the “distance” between
\mathcal{L}(\textsf{ALG},\cdot)
and
ALG
itself: a distance of 0 implies that
\mathcal{C}
is fully transparent, while a maximum distance implies
\mathcal{C}
is fully private. We proceed to make this definition concrete.

First, note that
\mathcal{L}(\textsf{ALG},\mathcal{S})
induces a probability distribution
\pi^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{S}}
over
\mathbb{T}
for state
\mathcal{S}
, which represents the player’s belief of the trade
\mathcal{C}
may make at a given state.
For example, in the fully opaque case,
\pi^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{S}}
is uniformly distributed over all possible trades
\mathcal{C}
can make given the current market conditions and state of
\mathcal{C}
(assets held, etc); in the asset transparency case,
\pi^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{S}}
is uniformly distributed only over the trades that involve the two assets that are revealed; and so on. Based on this, we can define the privacy of
\mathcal{C}
as the
statistical distance
between distributions
\pi^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{S}}
and
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}\leftarrow\textsf{ALG}_{\textsf{dist}}(\mathcal{S})
(which, recall, is
\mathcal{C}
’s “true” distribution of trades).
More formally:

Definition 1

(Privacy of CoinAlgs)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a CoinAlg with associated public function
\mathcal{L}
,
\pi^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{S}}
be the probability distribution over
\mathbb{T}
induced by
\mathcal{L}
for a state
\mathcal{S}
, and
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}\leftarrow\textsf{ALG}_{\textsf{dist}}(\mathcal{S})
be the real distribution over
\mathbb{T}
for
\mathcal{C}
for a state
\mathcal{S}
. We say that
\mathcal{C}
is
\epsilon
-private
if, for any finite subset of states
\tilde{\mathbb{S}}\subseteq\mathbb{S}
,
it follows that

\frac{1}{|\tilde{\mathbb{S}}|}\sum_{\mathcal{S}\in\tilde{\mathbb{S}}}\delta(\pi^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{S}},\pi_{\mathcal{S}})\geq\epsilon

where
\delta
denotes the total variation distance between the pair of input random variables.

In other words, the “farther apart” public information is from real information, the more private
\mathcal{C}
is.
We note that, while we use total variation distance, our definition of privacy is compatible with other standard statistical distance measures.

4
Private CoinAlgs: Threats to Fairness

Equipped with our formal model for CoinAlgs, we now begin our analysis of the CoinAlg Bind.

As noted in Section
2
, CoinAlgs in practice tend to be
private
.
This makes sense, since it is natural to keep investment strategies private in order to protect profits.
However, in this section, we will show that private CoinAlgs pose
economic risks
to its investors. Intuitively, private CoinAlgs lead to
information asymmetry
: privileged entities (e.g., a CoinAlg’s creator) may have full access to the CoinAlg’s investment strategy, which can be leveraged to
extract value
from the CoinAlg’s trades.

Key Insight 1
.

Investors in a
private
CoinAlg
\mathcal{C}
—one whose investment strategy is not publicly disclosed—are vulnerable to unfair
value extraction
by an adversary
\mathcal{A}
with insider knowledge of
\mathcal{C}
’s strategy.

A core element of our analysis will be to frame economic (un)fairness in CoinAlgs as a form of value extraction, similar to MEV
[
1
,
8
,
27
,
36
]
in other areas of decentralized finance, from which economic risks naturally follow.

4.1
Economic Fairness Definition for CoinAlgs

We begin by introducing our definition for economic (un)fairness of CoinAlgs.
Alongside our privacy definition from Section
3
, this definition will let us show that privacy is tied to economic risk.

As described in Section
3
, players have partial knowledge of the trades
\mathcal{C}
will make, which we model via privacy function
\mathcal{L}
. To define economic fairness, we now assume that there is some priviledged, adversarial player
\mathcal{A}
with
additional
knowledge of
\mathcal{C}
’s trades (formally, with a distribution over trades that is closer to
\pi
than
\pi^{\mathcal{L}}
). This could correspond to, for example, the creators of
\mathcal{C}
. We assume that
\mathcal{C}
is
fully transparent
to
\mathcal{A}
, so
\mathcal{A}
can predict the distribution of trades in advance;
\mathcal{A}
’s only source of uncertainty may come from
\mathcal{C}
’s randomness (e.g., sampled inside a TEE).

Economic fairness definition.

Our goal is to define what it means for
\mathcal{C}
to be “fair”, i.e., that no player can extract outsized benefits from
\mathcal{C}
’s trades. To build intuition, we start with a straw definition, which shows important elements of a robust notion of economic fairness.

A first attempt could be to say that
\mathcal{C}
’s trades cannot be manipulated maliciously. So, for example,
\mathcal{C}

cannot siphon funds to an adversarial asset, nor can
be steered to make trades that would benefit a particular player. While a legitimate threat, this definition only captures
directly benefiting
from
\mathcal{C}
’s trades, and does not consider
external capabilities and knowledge
of players, which may allow for
indirect
benefit in the form of insider trading. For example, if
\mathcal{A}
knows that
\mathcal{C}
will make a particular trade at a certain time,
\mathcal{A}
could sandwich the
trade to steal profits from
\mathcal{C}
.
Instead, we want our definition to capture the
total value
that can be obtained (directly and indirectly) from
\mathcal{C}
’s trades.
Even if
\mathcal{C}
is still profitable, an adversary should not be able to leverage privileged information to make outsized gains.
This leads to the core idea of our definition, which is to cast it as
value extraction problem
.

Formally, we model economic fairness via security game

\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{A}

P
(using
\mathcal{L}

\mathcal{C}
to produce the next state. Note that, after
\mathcal{S}_{0}

over CoinAlg

(with associated public function

Definition 2

(Economic Fairness of CoinAlgs)
.

A CoinAlg

In other words, our fairness definition characterizes the power of
\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{O}
in terms of how it allows
\mathcal{A}
to extract additional profits.

As mentioned earlier in the section, we are most interested in the case where
\mathcal{O}

perfectly predicts

, which is a special case of Definition
2
. We refer to the value that
\mathcal{A}
can extract with a perfect prediction oracle as the
full knowledge extractable value
for a given starting state and set of parameters.

Definition
2
models the (un)fairness of
\mathcal{C}
in a parametrized way, since it is stated in terms of a particular threshold difference of value extraction and an adversarial success probability So, more accurately, it captures a
snapshot
of
\mathcal{C}
’s overall fairness. Therefore, our definition is meant to be used in a flexible way, with multiple applications of our definition for various

. Conceptually, this yields a
fairness curve
, which is the broader characterization of
\mathcal{C}
’s fairness.

4.2
Fairness vs Privacy in CoinAlgs

We now proceed to the main result of this section, arguing that privacy in CoinAlgs is in tension with economic fairness. In particular, we show that the existence of a meaningful information oracle (i.e., one which allows the adversary to extract additional value) implies that
\mathcal{C}
must be private, and thus
privacy is a precondition for unfairness
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a CoinAlg that is
(\alpha,t)
-unfair with respect to perfect-prediction oracle
\mathcal{O}
for some
\alpha,t>0
. Then, there exists some
\epsilon>0
such that
\mathcal{C}
is
\epsilon
-private.

We defer the proof of this theorem to Appendix
A
. Intuitively, if
\mathcal{A}
is able to extract additional value using
\mathcal{O}
over any other
P
using
\mathcal{L}
, it must be the case that there is an information asymmetry between
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{L}
that leads to this difference in value, which, by definition, implies that
\mathcal{C}
is private. More formally, if
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{O}}
extracts additional value over
P^{\mathcal{L}}
, a
distinguisher

Dist
attempting to differentiate
\mathcal{O}
from
\mathcal{L}
(which is an equivalent formulation of Definition
1
) can run both
\mathcal{A}
and
P
as subroutines, using the difference in extracted value to determine which oracle it is interacting with.

Note that Theorem
1
is a
directional
result. An interesting question for future work is to study fine-grained variants, quantifying the degree to which privacy degrades for specific parameter values of Definition
2
.

Does privacy imply unfairness?

A natural question to ask next is whether the converse of Theorem
1
also holds, i.e., whether privacy always leads to economic unfairness. This, however, need not always be true: additional information
does not always lead to additional value extraction
, since information is not always useful from an economic perspective.
The converse of Theorem
1
is therefore not true in general.

However, if it
is
the case that
\mathcal{C}
is private in such a way that privileged information is economically useful, then this directly leads to economic unfairness. This implication is by design, since our
Fair
game is defined to precisely model the financial utility of information oracles. Therefore, an oracle from which more value can be extracted will (tautologically) lead to a larger value gap in our
Fair
game.

Let us now formalize this idea. We model the
financial utility
of an information oracle as the
percentage of the full knowledge extractable value
(as defined earlier) that it enables. By definition, the perfect-prediction oracle
\mathcal{O}
has a financial utility of 100%. We then have the following simple result.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a CoinAlg with associated public function
\mathcal{L}
with a financial utility of
u
. Assume that
\mathcal{C}
is
\epsilon
-private with respect to perfect-prediction oracle
\mathcal{O}
for some
\epsilon>0
. Then, there exists some
\alpha,t>0
such that
\mathcal{C}
is
(\alpha,t)
-unfair with respect to perfect-prediction oracle
\mathcal{O}
.

As mentioned earlier, this result holds by construction: if
\mathcal{L}
has a financial utility of
u
,
P^{\mathcal{L}}
will be able to extract at most
uT
value in
Fair
, where
T
is the full knowledge extractable value of
ALG
. On the other hand,
\mathcal{A}^{\mathcal{O}}
will be able to extract the value of
T
in
Fair
.

We note that the precise value of information is highly context-dependent. Therefore, deriving more general, fine-grained bounds on the degree to which (useful) information degrades economic unfairness is likely not feasible.

5
Transparent CoinAlgs: Threats to Profitability

Given our results in the previous section on the risks of economic unfairness in
private
CoinAlgs, a better alternative would seem for CoinAlgs to be
transparent
.
Transparency is, after all, a key property for ensuring the trustworthiness of systems ranging from open-source software to smart contracts.

In this section we show, however, that full transparency in a CoinAlg—i.e., public knowledge of
ALG
—
comes at the expense of profitability
. The reason is intuitive: Transparency means signaling trades publicly to the market. Arbitrageurs can profit from this advance information at the expense of the CoinAlg. Broadly, our work in this section provides evidence for:

Key Insight 2
.

A CoinAlg
\mathcal{C}
whose strategy
ALG
is
transparent
—known to an arbitraging adversary
{\cal A}
—incurs a
cost of transparency
: It yields lower profit than an equivalent
private
CoinAlg
\mathcal{C}^{*}
.

By equivalent
private
CoinAlg
\mathcal{C}^{*}
, we mean that
\mathcal{C}^{*}
executes the same strategy
ALG
as
\mathcal{C}
, but with
ALG
unknown to
{\cal A}
and thus not subject to frontrunning / sandwiching.
\mathcal{C}
’s
cost of transparency
is the amount by which its profit is lower than that of
\mathcal{C}^{*}
.
(Details follow below.)

Even a small cost of transparency can place
\mathcal{C}
at a competitive disadvantage. This disadvantage can lead to the market failure of
\mathcal{C}
, as investors gravitate toward the higher returns of a private alternative. As a result, Key Insight
2
strongly motivates the appeal of private CoinAlgs for profit maximization. Combined with our results in
Section

4
regarding the risks of private CoinAlgs (Key Insight
1
), it is clear that CoinAlgs present significant risks however they are designed.

5.1
Adversarial Strategies

An adversary
{\cal A}
poses two distinct strategic threats to a transparent CoinAlg
\mathcal{C}
:

Threat 1 (Strategy theft)
: If
\mathcal{C}
is about to execute a trade
\tau_{1}
,
{\cal A}
can
copy and frontrun
it, executing an equivalent trade
\tau_{0}
prior to
\tau_{1}
. In this way,
{\cal A}

steals

\mathcal{C}
’s investment strategy.

Example:

Predicting a price rise for TOK from 10 now to 15 tomorrow,
\mathcal{C}
prepares to buy 500 TOK in trade
\tau_{1}
.
{\cal A}
exploits
\mathcal{C}
’s transparency by executing
\tau_{0}=\tau_{1}

before

\tau_{1}
—profiting if
\mathcal{C}
’s prediction was correct and potentially eroding the profit of
{\cal A}
, as
\tau_{0}
will raise the price of TOK paid by
\mathcal{C}
.

Threat 2 (Sandwiching)
:
{\cal A}
can exploit
\mathcal{C}
’s transparency by
sandwiching

\mathcal{C}
’s trade
\tau_{1}
—executing paired
frontrunning
and
backrunning
trades. In this way,
{\cal A}
profits from advance knowledge of price movement induced by
\tau_{1}
while degrading
\mathcal{C}
’s profit.

Example:
Given the same
\tau_{1}
as above (buy 500 TOK),
{\cal A}
exploits
\mathcal{C}
’s transparency by executing
\tau_{0}
, buying 5 TOK before
\tau_{1}
, and selling 5 TOK in
\tau_{2}
after
\tau_{1}
.
{\cal A}
profits because
\tau_{1}
raises the price of TOK before
{\cal A}
sells, while
\tau_{0}
raises the price of TOK that
\mathcal{C}
pays in
\tau_{1}
, degrading
\mathcal{C}
’s profit.

Strategy theft is simple for an adversary to execute, as it just involves copying. However, it entails a hard-to-quantify risk for
{\cal A}
, as
\mathcal{C}
’s prediction may be inaccurate and thus its strategy may or may not be profitable over any given time horizon. We perform experiments in
Section

6
to evaluate the impact of strategy theft.

We focus in the remainder of this section on Threat 2. Sandwiching strategies are more amenable to analytic study than strategy theft. They can be
risk-free
, in the sense that they do not depend on the soundness of
\mathcal{C}
’s strategy and instead yield immediate profit for
{\cal A}
. Sandwiching is for this reason a widespread strategy today as a means of obtaining MEV
[
1
,
8
,
35
,
36
]
.

Does sandwiching affect only transparent CoinAlgs?
Sandwiching—and broader MEV extraction—can degrade the profit of any transaction exposed to arbitrageurs, whether in public or private mempools. So even a private CoinAlg
could
be vulnerable. A private CoinAlg
can
, though, take advantage of sandwiching protections: services such as Flashbots Protect
[
10
]
or Cowswap
[
6
]
, or side agreements with validators. Such protections are
not
possible if
\mathcal{C}
is transparent, as the trades executed will leak to
{\cal A}
.

5.2
Model Details

We first introduce a formal model for sandwich attacks against transparent CoinAlgs, extending our formalism from Section
3
. This model will form the basis for our results that follow.

Market model.

We consider a simple market in which two assets trade within a single, constant-product AMM. For the sake of concreteness, we denote these assets by TOK and USD (e.g., ether and a generic USD stablecoin). We let
t
denote logical time for the AMM—a counter on the number of trades it executes. The state of the AMM prior to execution in timestep
t
(or after timestep
t-1
) is represented by a trading-pair balance
\textsf{bal}^{\textsf{AMM}}_{t}=\big[p_{t}r_{t}\mbox{ USD},r_{t}\mbox{ TOK}\big]
. In a constant-product AMM, this reflects a market TOK-USD price of
(p_{t}r_{t})/r_{t}=p_{t}
. Our model of time matches that in
Section

4
, but assumes that trades involve only the TOK-USD pair on this single AMM. In what follows, we use the term
transaction
to denote the full, digitally signed payload that executes a
trade

\tau
at the application layer, meaning an AMM operation.

Trades are of two types: they either buy or sell TOK against USD. To simplify notation from Section
4
, we let
{\textsf{buy}(x)}
denote purchase of
x
TOK and
{\textsf{buy}(-x)}
denote the sale of
x
TOK. We assume AMM or smart-contract support for
conditional trade execution
based on the TOK price in the AMM, i.e., support for limit orders. We denote a limit order with limit
{p_{\textsf{lim}}}
by
{\textsf{buy}(x)_{\mathrel{\downarrow}{p_{\textsf{lim}}}}}
, meaning that the trade will be executed as long as TOK’s price is at or below
{p_{\textsf{lim}}}
.
4
4

4

AMM trades generally specify not price limits, but
slippage
limits. Price limits simplify our analysis and can easily be enforced via smart contract, as in our experiments in
Section

6
.

Model for
\mathcal{C}
’s strategy.

We are interested in algorithms
ALG
for
\mathcal{C}
with market-beating performance—motivating investor interest in the CoinAlg. We consider a simple model in which an internal

{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}[\mathcal{S}_{i}]

issues a prediction of the TOK-USD price

\hat{p}
for some future time
\hat{t}
.
5
5

5

We let

We assume for simplicity here that
\textsf{ALG}^{{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}}
is

\mathcal{C}

•

Execute

Looking ahead, our empirical analysis in Section

For simplicity, we assume that sale of TOK perfectly realizes

Transparency model.

As defined in
Section

3
,
\mathcal{C}
is

if every player, including

\mathcal{L}_{\textsf{transp}}(\textsf{ALG}^{\hat{\mathcal{O}}},\cdot)=\textsf{ALG}^{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}(\cdot)

A consequence—given our assumption that
\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{A}
can
predict trades from

\textsf{ALG}^{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}

prior to their execution
.
Exactly what “prior to” means will depend on the specific scenario considered in our game definitions and experiments. For example, looking ahead, our game
Sdwch

{\cal A}
can learn

in time to mount a successful sandwich attack against

at the same time as
\mathcal{C}
or even slightly later, but position its own transaction executing trade
\tau_{0}
earlier in a block, as happens in MEV extraction today. This capability is a direct consequence of transparency, as

can just execute
\textsf{ALG}^{\hat{\mathcal{O}}}

Model for adversary

We model

could represent a collection of cooperating or independent arbitrageurs. We are interested in how

\mathcal{C}
by pushing the AMM to
\mathcal{C}

{p_{\textsf{lim}}}

\tau_{0}
.
6
6

6

In an MEV supply chain,
\mathcal{C}

We denote
{\cal A}
’s portfolio prior to the execution of timestep

by
\textsf{bal}^{{\cal A}}_{t}=\big[c_{t}\mbox{ USD},d_{t}\mbox{ TOK}\big]

To gauge how a sandwich attack affects the profits of (transparent)

\mathcal{C}^{*}
also executes the same strategy

Definition 3

(Cost of transparency)
.

Let
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}

\Pi_{\mathcal{C}^{*}}-\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}

That is, we use the setting with no sandwiching as ground truth, and compare

Blockchain model.

Block construction and infrastructure and markets around it vary considerably across blockchains and design evolutions. For generality, we abstract away block construction and just assume advantageous ordering of transactions by

’s transparency, etc.

Remarks on Modeling

Before proceeding to our results, we make a few clarifying remarks about our model.

Remark 1

(Trade vs. transaction leakage)
.

The

Remark 2

.

In practice, under

6
Empirical Evaluation

In this section, we conduct experiments demonstrating the impact of frontrunning on backdoored CoinAlgs in a realistic setting using historical blockchain data. Through these experiments, we learn the extent to which frontrunning a CoinAlg is profitable even with limited, imprecise information. All experiment source code can be found at
https://git.disroot.org/verge24/calgs
.

Motivation.

We demonstrated the theoretical risk of profit capture in CoinAlgs in Section
5
. We now aim to discover the potential relative magnitude of such capture under real market conditions. Are CoinAlgs actually at risk? Can a frontrunner profit with limited information?

Methodology overview.

Following our model from Section
5
, we design a synthetic CoinAlg with perfect knowledge of future market conditions and simulate its trades on historical block data. It represents the ideal CoinAlg: its transactions are submitted privately, and investors cannot lose money as long as it is not blocked from trading. A simple frontrunner attempts to take advantage of its trades. These simulations reflect the liquidity conditions and fees present in an actual token market while showcasing the risk of frontrunning even for a best-case CoinAlg.

The experiments in this section complement the preceding theoretical results, to provide evidence for the following:

Key Insight 3
.

A CoinAlg that is profitable as a result of accurate market predictions can suffer significant losses in profit in the presence of even a low-bandwidth channel that leaks trade information to an arbitraging adversary.

As we unpack later in the section, using a special testing harness, we perform a variety of experiments to test this hypothesis across configurations of variables reflective of real-world considerations: multiple types of assets (stable and volatile), CoinAlg designs (transparent and partially private), adversarial strategies (strategy theft and sandwiching), and more. Broadly, our experiments show that, regardless of starting conditions, a CoinAlg that reveals even a small amount of information is at risk of profit loss.

6.1
Experimental Setup and Methodology

CoinAlg strategy.

The CoinAlg trades on a predetermined schedule and will trade up to the future market price predicted at the time of its next scheduled trade to ensure a profit, using as many funds as it can. Our simulated CoinAlg uses a more sophisticated strategy than that reflected in our game-theoretic model of Section
5
, which is simplified for analytic tractability. This CoinAlg uses continually updated information: it adjusts its strategy at every trading period using the next predicted price and does not necessarily sell the assets it purchased in the last trade. For example, if the CoinAlg predicts the price of ETH will increase by $50 today, it will try to buy ETH now; if tomorrow the CoinAlg predicts another increase of $70, it will try to buy more ETH tomorrow and will not sell its current ETH holdings. It submits its trades through a private transaction relay to protect against ordinary sandwiching attacks (i.e., the frontrunner does not observe its transactions before they are included in blocks).

A core goal of our experiments is to understand the extent to which a CoinAlg’s profit loss depends on the
amount
of information that is leaked: Is even a small amount of leakage sufficient for value extraction? We thus experiment with several variants that represent different types of leakage: (1) a fully transparent trading strategy that trades on a fixed interval, (2) a private strategy trading on a fixed schedule that leaks only a binary signal to the frontrunner (“buy/sell”), and (3) a transparent strategy with secret trading times but a known trading time distribution; in particular, for the third type, the CoinAlg places its trades according to a Poisson process, aiming to trade twice per day on average (
\lambda=3{,}600\text{ blocks}
). The aim of these three settings is to test how several plausible
degrees
of information leakage affect a CoinAlg’s profitability.

Using these three CoinAlg types as a starting point, we further vary lower-level details of the starting conditions to test whether these also bear an impact on our results. In particular, we experiment with different asset types, starting balances, times of day trades are executed, and the overall window of time in which the CoinAlg runs. See
Figure

8
for a summary of the parameters we vary and the values we consider. Our goal is to cast a wide net over CoinAlg types: those that trade volatile vs. stable assets, have large vs. small balances, execute for short vs. long periods of time, etc. As we will see, varying these parameters does not affect the directionality of our results.

Adversarial strategies.

Turning to adversaries, we similarly consider multiple strategies, as motivated by the threats from earlier sections: (1) strategy theft, where the frontrunner copies the CoinAlg’s trading strategy; and (2) sandwiching, where the adversary, doubting the profitability of the CoinAlg’s strategy, frontruns and backruns the CoinAlg’s trades.

For sandwich attacks, an important consideration is the amount of capital that the adversary should use in its transactions. While one might expect the best strategy is to use as much available capital as possible, this is not optimal, for two reasons: (1) larger trades incur higher pool fees, and (2) larger trades risk increasing asset prices above the CoinAlg’s price limit. Therefore, given a particular CoinAlg trade to frontrun, an attacker’s estimated sandwich profit is instead a
unimodal function
of its trade input, with a peak that is determined by the two aforementioned conditions. We show an example of this in Figure
9
, where we quantify the relationship between an attacker’s profit and its sandwich input size.

Because this function is strictly unimodal, we can use a
golden-section search
over frontrun trade sizes to optimize the frontrunner’s trade input
[
18
]
. We use this technique to derive the frontrunner’s sandwich transactions from its available capital.

Similar to the CoinAlg, we consider multiple values for the adversary’s starting balance, in order to analyze the feasibility of profit extraction for both well-resourced and under-resourced adversaries. See
Figure

8
for a summary of the values we consider. As we discuss later, our simulations show that adversaries of both types are able to profit.

Simulation details.

We conduct experiments on two real-world Uniswap V3 token pools (USDC/ETH, 0.05% fee and PEPE/ETH, 0.3% fee) and target a trading period between Ethereum blocks 20,000,000 and 23,000,000 (June 1, 2024 to July 26, 2025). Using an archive node, we simulate trades by running a multi-transaction call at the top of a target block whenever a player wants to submit a transaction. The multi-call (1) funds the CoinAlg’s and frontrunner’s simulated accounts by transferring assets from burn addresses (ETH, PEPE) or using an authorized token minter (USDC), (2) approves and trades tokens on the target market, and (3) returns the transaction results and reports the final balances of the players.

To prevent these transactions from disrupting historical trading activity in the simulation, historical state is restored after each simulated block. We incorporate a third “ghost” player who places a trade at the beginning of every multi-call to compensate for the market movements caused by the CoinAlg and frontrunner in previous blocks.
We eliminate these market corrections after the CoinAlg has traded and the frontrunner has had the option to react to that trade.
This ensures the CoinAlg’s price predictions remain accurate and token prices interpretable. Note that this negates the impact of a frontrunner’s back-run: the back-run does not negatively affect the CoinAlg. Our experimental results thus show an upper bound on CoinAlg profits; frontrunning impact may be greater.
Our simulations also differ from our model in that the CoinAlg is given a finite amount of funds.

Notice that, for a fixed set of starting conditions (i.e., selecting one value per column in Figure
8
), the results of a simulation run are
deterministic
. Therefore, a single run per parameter set is sufficient to obtain our results. The only exception is our experiments in which the CoinAlg decides when to trade according to a Poisson distribution. In this case, we performed 16 experimental runs with different random seeds for every combination of parameters.

6.2
Results

We ran our testing harness across all 3,072 combinations of parameters shown in Figure
8
. The output of all experimental runs can be found at
https://git.disroot.org/verge24/calgs/src/branch/main/results
.

The primary takeaway from our experiments is that, across all parameter combinations,
the presence of an adversary leads to profit loss for the CoinAlg
, confirming our hypothesis that a CoinAlg that reveals information (to either the public or to an insider through a backdoor) incurs a cost. While the specific amount of profit loss depends on the starting conditions (particularly the initial balances), the behavior is directionally consistent across all instances.

We now discuss a few lower-level takeaways elucidated by our experiments.

Both adversarial strategies are viable.

Our experiments confirm that, as expected, both strategy theft and sandwiching are profitable strategies for the adversary.

For strategy theft, the frontrunner copies each CoinAlg trade and submits a transaction one block before the CoinAlg does, getting a better price and reducing the remaining liquidity available within the desired price limit. We exemplify this in
Figure

10
, which shows the result of one of our experimental runs that consider a transparent CoinAlg and a strategy-theft adversary. As we can see, the frontrunner captures nearly all the profit the CoinAlg would have captured in the absence of frontrunning.
With sufficient capital, its larger market trades consume available liquidity and reach the target price limit, leading to more variable profit for the frontrunner and significantly hampered trading ability for the CoinAlg. The CoinAlg is blocked from trading when the frontrunner is able to move the price to the price limit.

Traditional sandwiching activity usually involves positioning trades around a victim trade in the same block. CoinAlgs using anti-frontrunning best practices, such as private mempools, can still be frontrun with sandwich attacks if their trades can be predicted. As exemplified in
Figure

11
, frontrunner sandwich attacks reduce the profits of our transparent CoinAlg by 27.6%. Our ideal CoinAlg is still profitable despite sandwich activity because the price limits it uses guarantee that trades are profitable and are not based on “slippage” relative to the market price; furthermore, frontrunners cannot impact the CoinAlg when its action is to hold assets rather than trade them.

Note that the frontrunner’s profit and the CoinAlg’s profit without frontrunning in the graph are nearly identical.

Even partially-private CoinAlgs are vulnerable.

Our experiments also show that full knowledge of the CoinAlg is not required for the adversary to extract profits, and even a limited amount of information can lead to value loss.

We consider this setting by modifying our CoinAlg so that its trade times are secret: rather than trading on a fixed schedule, it places its trades according to a Poisson process. Unlike traditional sandwich attacks, the frontrunner must now assume risk, since it will hold the CoinAlg’s position for an extended time. We give the frontrunner a simple algorithm: immediately after seeing a trade from the CoinAlg, it calculates the expected time of the next CoinAlg trade (
3{,}600\text{ blocks}
in the future) and attempts to frontrun a trade produced by the CoinAlg’s algorithm for this expected target block. After the CoinAlg finally trades, it places its backrun transaction. Ultimately, as exemplified in
Figure

12
, the frontrunner still profits substantially.

Figure 12:
Mean profit across 256 runs for CoinAlg (blue) starting with 300 WETH trading WETH/USDC using randomized, hidden trade times, a frontrunner (red) starting with 200 WETH / 400,000 USDC engaging in long-range sandwich attacks, and the same CoinAlg (green) if a frontrunner were not present. Bands show the range between the 10th and 90th percentiles.

Figure 13:
Profit comparison for private CoinAlg (blue) trading WETH/USDC leaking only trade directions to a privileged frontrunner (red) engaging in sandwich attacks estimating 1% price movements per trade, and the same CoinAlg (green) if there were no frontrunner.

We further consider an even more limited adversary, where the CoinAlg is now private, except for a low-bit covert channel that the adversary has access to. We do not detail the construction of this channel but posit that it may be realized, for example, through an encrypted timing side channel related to the CoinAlg’s processing. Ten blocks prior to its next trading time, the CoinAlg over this channel communicates to the frontrunner a single bit containing the anticipated direction of its next trade. The channel does not communicate the exact trade amount or price limit, or even whether the CoinAlg will submit a trade, which may not happen if placing a trade would not be profitable (e.g., when the market price is too close to its limit price). The frontrunner runs a modest sandwiching strategy against the CoinAlg, blindly expecting the CoinAlg to make a fixed price impact of 1%.

As shown in
Figure

13
, the frontrunner remains profitable with this limited information and decreases the CoinAlg’s profits by 17.8%. Though a number of the frontrunner’s individual trades were unprofitable, its successful ones more than offset the losses. The impact of this frontrunner on CoinAlg profits is more than half the impact of a frontrunner on the fully transparent CoinAlg.

Collective trades with little market impact are not affected by frontrunning.

In our experiments, when the cost of a trade to the frontrunner exceeded the value which could be extracted from a sandwich trade, the frontrunner opted not to trade. For example, for the 0.05% USDC/WETH pool, the cost of running a sandwich attack on a liquid pool is at least
0.05\%\times 2=0.1\%
of the input. This cost dominates when available liquidity is high relative to the CoinAlg’s trade size, as also observed in
[
35
]
.

7
Heuristic Guardrails

Our results in this work show that the CoinAlg Bind is
inescapable; no mitigation will address it entirely. CoinAlgs (particularly
private
ones) would seem an inevitable part of the investment landscape, however.
Thus, finding
guardrails
that limit the impact of the CoinAlg Bind in private CoinAlgs is crucial. In this section, we sketch a few practical, heuristic guardrails, which we hope motivate future research.

Randomizing wrappers.

Randomization
of a CoinAlg
\mathcal{C}
’s trades can make it more challenging for an adversary to extract value from them. In particular,
\mathcal{C}
can select its next trade u
sing private randomness
generated by a
transparent
mechanism that ensures
fairness
, i.e., no leakage to or biasing by an adversary. We call such a mechanism a
randomizing wrapper
.

A randomizing wrapper hybridizes a private CoinAlg with a transparent guardrail, bringing the profitability benefits of a private trading strategy while limiting opportunities for exploitation through information leakage to an insider.

Recall from Section
3
that
\mathcal{C}
selects its trade in state
\mathcal{S}
by running: (1) trade-generation algorithm
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}\leftarrow\textsf{ALG}_{\textsf{dist}}(\mathcal{S})
, and (2) sampling algorithm
\tau{\>{\leftarrow{\hskip-3.0pt\raisebox{0.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\$$}}}\>}\textsf{ALG}_{\textsf{sample}}(\pi_{\mathcal{S}})
. The idea is to replace (2) and implement a new private CoinAlg
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
with these two components:

1.

A
private
algorithm
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}\leftarrow\textsf{ALG}_{\textsf{dist}}(\mathcal{S})
and

2.

A
transparent
randomizing wrapper
RW
.

Here,
\textsf{RW}=(\textsf{RW}_{\$},\textsf{RW}_{e})
, where
\tau{\>{\leftarrow{\hskip-3.0pt\raisebox{0.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\$$}}}\>}\textsf{RW}_{\$}(\pi_{\mathcal{S}})
selects a transaction using fair, private coins, and
\textsf{RW}_{e}
then executes
\tau
.
RW
is transparent in the sense that its code is published and its properties can be publicly verified. (Its
state
, however, is not transparent during execution.) By running
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
in a TEE, it is possible to enforce privacy for
\textsf{ALG}_{\textsf{dist}}(\mathcal{S})
and the distribution of trades
\pi_{\mathcal{S}}
. At the same time, the TEE can ensure correct execution of
RW
with fair, secret, TEE-generated random coins.

In summary,
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
executes a private, randomized trading strategy where
RW
can be publicly verified by users to ensure that it selects trades using fair randomness that is not leaked to an adversary.
While randomizing wrappers cannot completely eliminate attacks, they can help minimize value extraction.

We note that, in some cases, randomizing wrappers can lead the CoinAlg to perform suboptimal trades, since the wrapper may sample a transaction that is not the most profitable one. However, there
are
cases where randomization does not come at the expense of profits. For example, if a CoinAlgs is performing long-term forecasting, the wrapper can randomize the
time
that trades takes place, effectively acting as a timing jitter. In this case, an adversary would not be able to leverage the instantaneous price increase required for a profitable sandwich attack.

Protected training pipelines.

Randomizing wrappers modify the
runtime
behavior of a CoinAlg to minimize value extraction. An alternative approach is to modify a CoinAlg at
creation
time. As a concrete example, if the
data sources
used to train an AI-based CoinAlg were public, this would provide assurance that the model has not been engineered in malicious ways during the training process. Even if the list of data sources is transparent, however, a key technical challenge is guaranteeing that the model has actually been trained (only) on data from these sources. To achieve this, CoinAlgs can be trained using
protected pipelines

[
16
]
which securely attest to the data sources that AI-models have been trained on.

Bug bounty systems.

Complementary to technical countermeasures is the approach of
disincentivizing
exploitation of the CoinAlg Bind via the
economic
means of a
bug bounty
system.
The idea is to enable users to prove the existence of value-extraction backdoors and claim monetary rewards, incentivizing close scrutiny of CoinAlgs or even whistleblowing. In the context of CoinAlg vulnerabilities, such a bug-bounty system can be made publicly verifiable—even as a smart contract
[
4
]
. A claimant submits a sequence of correct predictions relating to a CoinAlg
\mathcal{C}
’s trades. With enough prediction entropy, the sequence constitutes a sound proof of access to a covert (or side) channel.

As a simple, concrete example, consider a bounty system
\mathcal{B}
for trade-information leakage from a randomizing wrapper
RW
in the construction above. Suppose a prover / whistleblower
P
claims to learn a predicate
F(\tau)
prior to
\mathcal{C}
’s execution of any trade
\tau
.
The proof system then works as follows.
P
sends
F
to
\mathcal{B}
. Then, in each of a sequence
i\in[1,t]
of epochs,
P
sends to
\mathcal{B}
a prediction
x_{i}
of
F(\tau_{i})
prior to execution of
\tau_{i}
.
In epoch
t+1
,
\mathcal{B}
checks two
conditions:

1.

Predictions are correct:

\forall i\in[1,t]:x_{i}=F(\tau_{i})
.

2.

Soundness threshold is met:
Prediction sequence
\{x_{i}\}
yields a proof with soundness
1-2^{\theta}
for system parameter
\theta
(e.g.,
\theta=128
).

Let
X_{i}=F\big(\tau_{i}{\>{\leftarrow{\hskip-3.0pt\raisebox{0.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\$$}}}\>}\textsf{RW}_{\$}(\pi_{\mathcal{S}_{i}})\big)
denote a random variable over the private coins of
\textsf{RW}_{\$}
. To verify condition (2),
\mathcal{B}
passes
(F,t,\theta)
to an API—integrated into
RW
to support the bounty system—which checks
that:

\sum_{i=1}^{t}H_{\infty}(X_{i})\geq\theta.

Related Work

CoinAlgs.

While not termed as such, CoinAlgs in TradFi have been studied extensively, as they encompass various financial instruments (see Section
2
). For example, previous work examines the efficient-market hypothesis (i.e., market-beating performance is impossible using only public information
[
29
,
23

); and risks from opaque instruments have motivated research on investor protection and fiduciary duties
[
19

28

, including for robo-advisors
[
14

. However, no prior work has jointly studied privacy
and

transparency or their inherent tension.

Literature on Web3 CoinAlgs is limited, focusing mostly on AI-powered DAOs. Notably, Patlan et al.
[
25
]
conduct a security analysis of ElizaOS and expose prompt-injection vulnerabilities. These results are orthogonal and complementary to ours, as they show different concerns of AI-powered DAOs.

Sandwich attacks.

Sandwich attacks have received substantial prior study. Most relevant to our work, several papers analyze sandwich attacks game-theoretically, as we do in Section
5
. Heimbach and Wattenhofer
[
13
]
introduce a sandwich game to formally analyze such attacks, but, critically, their analysis is one-shot while we consider the infinitely-repeated variant. Zhou et al.
[
35
]
similarly formalize sandwich attacks, but limit their analysis to attackers, omitting victim-side considerations such as slippage limit adjustments.
Other works study sandwich attacks empirically, similar to Section
6
. Many papers
[
27
,
30
,
35
]
quantify value extracted via sandwiching and other MEV attacks, but our experiments add new market participants rather than merely observing historical data, since private CoinAlgs are not observable and truly transparent CoinAlgs do not yet exist.

Repeated games.

A core component of the results in Section
5
is the analysis of the
repeated
variant of our sandwiching game.
Repeated games are surprisingly sparse in DeFi literature, as most works consider one-shot or finite-horizon games (e.g.,
[
13
,
20
]
). Yet, DeFi involves repeated, strategic interactions between players, and so repeated games are highly relevant. A few works do consider repeated games. For example, Daian et al.
[
8
]
model “priority gas auctions” with successive adaptive
bidding; while their analysis is one-shot, each iteration resembles a repeated game. Similarly, Judmayer et al.
[
15
]
explore MEV definitions using repeated games to model blockchain assets.

Crypto and AI.

Our paper contributes to the emerging intersection of crypto and AI, such as on “DePINs” for AI computations
[
21
]
, payments for agentic AI
[
24
]
,
“trustless”
agent-to-agent protocols
[
9
]
, authenticated ML pipelines
[
16
]
, and more. Crypto and AI are meeting at a brisk pace, and the CoinAlg Bind is at the core of this convergence, as any AI-based financial instrument involving multiple users could be in the scope of our results.

9
Conclusion

This work begins the study of the CoinAlg Bind, the tradeoff between profitability and economic fairness in CoinAlgs, a broad class of financial instruments. We argue that CoinAlgs can either be transparent, and risk losing profits; or be private, and open the door for unfair value extraction by insiders. Through extensive experiments, we show that this threat is not merely a theoretical concern—even in scenarios where a CoinAlg may seem benign, subtle backdoors can be inserted that lead to profits from adversaries. Regardless of this fundamental tension, however, CoinAlgs are an inevitable part of the financial landscape, and thus the search for principled guardrails remains an important avenue for future work.
