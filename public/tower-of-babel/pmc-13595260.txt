Procedural and Clinical Outcomes of Second-generation Robotic-assisted versus Manual Percutaneous Coronary Intervention: A 1-year Follow-up Study
Authors: Bambang Dwiputra, Yutaka Tadano, Shoichi Kuramitsu, Takuro Sugie, Daitaro Kanno, Tsutomu Fujita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Robotic-assisted percutaneous coronary intervention (rPCI) offers potential benefits, including improved lesion assessment, optimised stent deployment and reduced radiation exposure. However, comparative data, particularly in Japanese populations, remain limited.Aim:To compare procedural and clinical outcomes between second-generation rPCI and manual percutaneous coronary intervention (mPCI) in patients with chronic coronary syndrome and acute coronary syndrome over a 1-year follow-up period.Methods:This retrospective cohort study included 176 patients who underwent rPCI using the second-generation CorPath GRX Vascular Robotic System. Following 1:1 propensity score matching at the lesion level, 176 patients were identified in the mPCI group. The co-primary endpoints were 1-year all-cause mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events, defined as a composite of cardiac death, non-fatal MI, target lesion revascularisation, stent thrombosis and stroke.Results:Both groups had similar baseline characteristics. Compared with mPCI, rPCI was associated with shorter total procedural time (26 minutes [19–37] versus 71 minutes [52–90]; p<0.01), fluoroscopy time (14 minutes [10–18] versus 16 minutes [10–26]; p=0.04), lower contrast volume (99 [82.8–129.0] versus 141 ml [116–172]; p<0.01) and lower dose area product (3,628.3 [2,552.1–5,352.3] versus 6,349.1 [4,080.0–8,382.3]; p<0.01). At 1 year, the co-primary endpoints of all-cause mortality (p=0.33) and major adverse cardiovascular events (p=0.79) were comparable between groups.Conclusion:Compared with mPCI, rPCI was associated with shorter procedural and fluoroscopy times, lower contrast volume and lower dose area product, while demonstrating similar clinical outcomes at 1 year.

In the past decade, the interventional cardiology field has witnessed considerable advances in robotic-assisted percutaneous coronary intervention (rPCI). This innovative approach has emerged as a promising tool to overcome challenges posed by manual percutaneous coronary intervention (mPCI), including radiation exposure and ergonomic injuries among the operators.1,2

The safety and efficacy of rPCI with the first-generation robotic platform have been previously reported in the multicentre PRECISE and CORA-PCI trials.3,4 Several countries have successfully implemented the use of rPCI.

In Switzerland, Haner et al. have proven in the early adoption phase of the percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) robotic program that rPCI may be safely completed, with approximately 80% of procedures conducted completely robotically or partially with assistance, and without clinically relevant robot-associated problems.5 Successful introduction of rPCI has been reported in Japan.6 China also managed to safely implement the use of rPCI, effectively treating all the lesions without manual interruption or in-hospital major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). Although these Asian countries have been able to use rPCI, both countries only performed small-size, single-centre studies.6,7

The lack of rPCI studies in the Asian population necessitates further investigation into its applicability in scientific understanding. Therefore, to address this gap, this study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of second-generation rPCI by comparing patients receiving rPCI versus mPCI, and describing the differences in procedural and clinical outcomes within the 30-day and 1-year follow-up periods.

Methods

Study Design

In this retrospective cohort, we compared the outcomes of rPCI with mPCI in patients with coronary artery disease. Data were collected from the electronic medical records for all patients who underwent a clinically successful rPCI between January 2021 and May 2023 at the Sapporo Cardiovascular Centre, Japan. The study included patients aged ≥18 years with stable coronary artery disease, unstable angina or acute MI who had rPCI using the second-generation CorPath GRX System and completed a 1-year follow-up. Patients with cardiogenic shock, haemodynamic instability from any cause, diagnosis of ST-elevation MI (STEMI) and severely calcified lesions were excluded. The mPCI cohort was identified using the same inclusion criteria from the electronic medical records within the same period. Propensity score matching at a 1:1 ratio was used to create a comparable control group, resulting in 176 patients in each group.

All procedures were conducted with informed consent according to local regulations, with approval from the local ethics committee and in adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki. Additionally, patients were thoroughly informed about the aspects of rPCI during consultations to ensure they could make informed decisions. Regarding patient and public involvement in the research process itself, patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this study. Given the retrospective and observational nature of this chart review, it was not feasible to incorporate active patient participation in the development of the research questions or outcome measures.

Robotic-assisted and Manual Percutaneous Coronary Intervention

This study used a previously documented rPCI technique.8 In summary, the procedure involved using the second-generation CorPath GRX Vascular Robotic System (Corindus/Siemens Healthineers), controlled from a shielded interventional cockpit. Unlike first-generation platforms, the GRX system features active guide catheter control allowing for precise millimetre adjustments and automated robotic movements (TechnIQ) for complex wire manipulation. A scrubbed-in assistant at the patient’s bedside operated the robotic arm, handling guidewires and catheters, positioning them in the coronary arteries, and administering contrast medium.

Due to the bulky profile of current intravascular imaging catheters (intravascular ultrasound [IVUS] and optical coherence tomography [OCT]), robotic trackability can be limited. Therefore, our standard workflow mandates that the bedside assistant manually delivers and performs the pullback for all IVUS and OCT catheters, while the primary operator remains at the console. The choice between OCT and IVUS was driven by operator preference and lesion morphology. Furthermore, because the GRX system is incompatible with rotational atherectomy, patients with severe calcification requiring planned atherectomy were scheduled directly for manual PCI. Two experienced interventional cardiologists alternated roles as primary and secondary operators for all rPCI procedures.

For mPCI, a team of interventional cardiologists performed these procedures, manually accessing blood vessels, guiding catheters into the coronary arteries, and manoeuvring guidewires, balloons and stents to treat blockages. For both the rPCI and mPCI groups, interventional cardiologists determined the treatment approach for each coronary lesion, adhering to standard clinical guidelines.9

Lesion Characteristics Assessment

Coronary artery disease severity was categorised based on the number of major epicardial vessels with significant stenosis (>50% diameter narrowing) or a history of PCI, using a grading system of single, double or triple vessel disease. Two interventional cardiologists analysed digitally recorded coronary angiograms to assign each coronary lesion an American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association lesion morphology classification (type A, B1/B2 or C).10 Clinical success was defined as achieving angiographic success, which entailed a final stenosis of <30% with thrombolysis in MI flow grade 3, without death, target vessel MI or the need for repeat target vessel revascularisation during hospitalisation.

Total procedural time was defined as the duration from the initial arterial puncture to the complete removal of the access sheath for both the rPCI and mPCI groups. PCI complications, defined as coronary dissections (graded by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute classification) and perforations (categorised by the Ellis classification), were identified through angiogram reviews by the same cardiologists. Bifurcation lesions were classified according to the Bifurcation Academic Research Consortium criteria, where significant stenosis (>50%) in both the main vessel and side branch was defined as true bifurcation.11

Follow-up and Outcome

The co-primary outcomes of this study are 1-year all-cause mortality and MACE. The composite of the MACE outcome was cardiac death, target lesion revascularisation, non-fatal MI, stent thrombosis and non-fatal stroke. MI was defined in accordance with the Fourth Universal Definition of MI. These criteria require the detection of a rise and/or fall in cardiac biomarker values with at least one value above the 99th percentile upper reference limit, accompanied by clinical or electrocardiographic indicators of acute myocardial ischaemia.9

All outcome measures were recorded in two different follow-up timeframes: 30 days and 1 year. Follow-up was conducted through telephone and mail interviews, using a standardised questionnaire to ensure consistency and reliability in data collection. All incident endpoints were validated against medical records and were carried out by physicians who were blinded to the initial procedure.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0 software (IBM). Propensity score matching was used to create a balanced control group. This method used the Mahalanobis distance metric in a 1:1 ratio, with the rPCI cohort designated as the cases. Matching variables included patient demographics (age, sex and BMI), medical history (diabetes, active smoking, dyslipidaemia and prior coronary artery bypass grafting) and lesion characteristics according to the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association classification.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the data. Categorical variables are presented as absolute numbers and percentages, with comparisons performed using Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables are expressed as medians with 25th to 75th percentiles or means with standard deviations, with comparisons made using either the t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test depending on normality of distribution.

Kaplan–Meier curves for all-cause mortality, MACE and each individual MACE endpoint were plotted, with survival curve differences assessed by the log-rank test and event rates calculated using the Kaplan–Meier estimator. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value of <0.05.

Results

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

Between January 2021 and May 2023, 176 patients (median age 74 years [25th–75th quartile 67–81]; 75% men) underwent a clinically successful rPCI in the Sapporo Cardiovascular Centre. Within the same timeframe, 2,994 patients underwent mPCI, and after using propensity matching, a total of 176 patients (median age 76 years [25th–75th quartile 69–83 years]; 70.2% men) were included in this analysis. All cardiovascular risk factors and previous history of diseases were comparable between the two groups. Both groups were indicated for PCI, mostly because of stable angina (83.5% in rPCI versus 88.6 in mPCI; Table 1).

Lesion and Procedural Characteristics

For both rPCI and mPCI, the left anterior descending artery (39.8% versus 40.3; p=0.91) and the right coronary artery (42.0 versus 32.4%; p=0.06) were the most common. Complex lesions, according to the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association lesion morphology classification, were comparably prevalent in both groups (Type B2 60.8 versus 65.9%; p=0.58; Type C 15.9 versus 11.4%; p=0.21). The prevalence of chronic total occlusion (CTO) was fully balanced after propensity matching (rPCI 0.0% versus mPCI 0.6%; p=1.000). A comparable number of bifurcation lesions were treated in both groups (22.7 versus 19.3%; p=0.43; Table 2). Transradial access was used for most patients in both cohorts, but more frequently in the rPCI group (95.5 versus 75.6%; p<0.00). There was no difference in the number of stents used (1.05 ± 0.21 versus 1.01 ± 0.10; p=0.10) or the stent length (median 24 [20.0–33.0] versus 24 [24–34]; p=0.90).

The rPCI group had significantly less total procedural time, fluoroscopy time, contrast agent dose and dose area product compared with the mPCI group (Table 3). The mPCI group underwent rotational atherectomy and IVUS more frequently, while the use of OCT was more frequent in the rPCI group. Manual assistance in the rPCI group was required in 58.5% of all procedures, particularly for the manual delivery and manipulation of intracoronary imaging catheters (IVUS or OCT), while all therapeutic balloon and stent deployments were performed robotically. Only one case (0.6%) needed manual conversion due to extreme proximal vessel tortuosity. Both groups experienced a similar number of PCI complications (rPCI 1.1% [n=2] versus mPCI 2.3% [n=4]; p = 0.410). The two complications in the rPCI cohort were non-flow-limiting edge dissections that were successfully recognised via imaging and immediately treated with additional robotic stent deployment; neither was attributed to robotic hardware malfunction.

Clinical Outcomes

There were no statistical differences in the co-primary endpoints of both 1-year all-cause mortality (rPCI 6.3% versus mPCI 4.0%; plog-rank=0.33) and 1-year MACE (rPCI 4.0% versus mPCI 4.5%; plog-rank=0.79; Figure 1A and B). At 30 days after discharge, the all-cause mortality was 1.1% for both rPCI and mPCI (p=1.00), with MACE occurring in 1.1% of rPCI and 1.7% of mPCI cases (p=0.56). Specifically for each MACE component in both groups at 30 days: four cardiac deaths (rPCI 1.1% versus mPCI 1.1%; p=1.00) and one stent thrombosis (0 versus 0.6%; p=0.32) occurred, while no target lesion revascularisation, non-fatal MI and non-fatal stroke were recorded (Table 4). At 1 year, the individual MACE components for both rPCI and mPCI were 10 cardiac deaths (2.8% versus 2.8%; plog-rank=0.74), two MIs (0% versus 1.1%; plog-rank=0.16), four target lesion revascularisations (0.6% versus 1.7%; plog-rank=0.32), one stent thrombosis (0% versus 0.6%; plog-rank=0.32) and two strokes (0.6% versus 0.6%; plog-rank=1.00; Figure 1C–G).

Discussion

The present propensity-matched cohort study is the first to investigate the differences in procedural characteristics and outcomes for patients undergoing second-generation rPCI compared with mPCI in a Japanese single-centre cohort. Our findings indicate that the rPCI group showed significantly shorter total procedural and fluoroscopy times. Additionally, the contrast agent dose and dose area product were significantly lower in the rPCI group. There were no significant differences in all-cause mortality and MACE at 30-day and 1-year follow-up.

Numerous robot systems have been introduced since 2002, with the first robotic system, CorPath 200 (Corindus Vascular Robotics), receiving its Food and Drug Administration approval to be commercially available in 2012.12 The PRECISE trial in 2013 was the first large multicentre study to validate the safety and feasibility of rPCI with the CorPath 200 system. Of 164 patients recruited, device technical success was achieved in 98.8% of patients, and clinical procedural success was achieved in 97.6% of cases. Moreover, the primary operator’s radiation exposure was reduced by 95.2% compared with the levels in the conventional mPCI settings. However, the majority of lesions treated in this trial were short, non-type C lesions.3 To address these concerns, the CORA-PCI trial evaluated the feasibility and technical success of the CorPath 200 system in patients with complex coronary artery lesions and significant comorbidities. The trial demonstrated no significant differences in clinical success, stent use, and fluoroscopy time between rPCI and mPCI groups.4 The latest second-generation CorPath GRX Vascular Robotic System (Corindus/Siemens Healthineers) was used in our study.13

In this single-centre investigation, lesion and procedural characteristics were largely comparable between the two groups. Complex lesions (classified as type B2/C) dominated both the rPCI and mPCI groups, where we achieved procedural success while also demonstrating lower procedural and fluoroscopy times, contrast agent dose, and dose area product. While more complex lesions, such as CTO, were absent in the rPCI group of our study, a study by Hirai et al. showed comparable procedural time, contrast dose and in-hospital major adverse events in 49 CTO lesion patients treated with a partially manual rPCI procedure compared with mPCI.14 Given these positive outcomes, rPCI holds significant promise for the future direction of treating complex lesions.

One of the main advantages of using rPCI is the reduced radiation exposure for the operator. Cardiologists performing >15 PCIs per year have been shown to have a higher risk of cancer than those performing fewer PCIs.15 A recent meta-analysis of 2,230 patients demonstrated that rPCI significantly reduces chest-level operator radiation exposure, fluoroscopy time and amount of contrast used compared with the mPCI group.16 These findings, in corroboration with our study, support the use of rPCI in addressing the risk of radiation hazards.

The advantages offered by rPCI are not restricted to only the operator, but also to the patients. We have shown that the rPCI group demonstrated significantly less fluoroscopy time, contrast agent dose and dose area product than the mPCI group, as previously confirmed in two meta-analyses.17,18 This could be explained by the improved visualisation provided by the high-definition monitors in the operator’s cockpit, allowing precise balloon and stent positioning, therefore, minimising the risk of longitudinal geographic miss. Robotic systems also allow for better operator control over contrast media injectors, reducing the amount of contrast media needed.

The favourable procedural metrics observed in our study, including reduced contrast volume, are likely augmented by the advanced features of the second-generation CorPath GRX. The addition of active robotic guide catheter control allowed operators to maintain stable coaxial alignment during deep seating or dynamic respiration, thereby minimising contrast waste during injections. Total procedural time in this study was defined as the duration from arterial puncture to access sheath removal, which was significantly shorter in the rPCI group. This pronounced efficiency in non-fluoroscopic time is primarily driven by a specific institutional workflow rather than the robotic technology itself.

During standard mPCI, operators frequently required intraprocedural pauses to consult with the senior supervising interventional cardiologist regarding treatment strategy, which prolonged the total time. Conversely, rPCI cases were performed directly by the senior operator, eliminating these intraprocedural consultation delays. The fluoroscopy time, which demonstrated a much narrower difference between the groups (14 versus 16 minutes), may serve as a more accurate and objective metric of true procedural efficiency. This narrower margin aligns more closely with previous studies, where rPCI generally resulted in longer total procedural times compared with mPCI.19,20

Additionally, the significantly higher usage of rotablation in the mPCI group (16.5 versus 1.7%; p<0.001) reflects a known hardware limitation. The CorPath GRX system lacks compatibility with current rotational atherectomy devices. Consequently, patients with severe, tortuous calcifications requiring upfront atherectomy were preferentially triaged to the manual cohort. Finally, accumulating 176 successful rPCI cases at a single centre indicates that our operators have largely surpassed the initial learning curve associated with robotic PCI.

Procedural time is a particularly significant finding for treating acute coronary syndrome patients. While many have reported the safety and technical and procedural success in unstable angina pectoris and non-STEMI cases, the same cannot be said for STEMI.8,21–23 Prolonged door-to-balloon time has been the primary reason for large-scale studies to exclude patients presenting with STEMI, since rPCI generally resulted in longer total procedural time compared with mPCI.19,20 Faster procedural time, therefore, might warrant further studies to include a wider spectrum of acute coronary syndrome cases. To prevent prolonged procedures, it is vital to implement more frequent training sessions with the robotic system. It has been shown that advanced-experience cases demonstrated significantly shorter procedure duration, and after performing at least three rPCI cases, interventional cardiologists showed the ability to perform rPCI faster with lower radiation exposure, without jeopardising safety.24

The usage rates for IVUS and rotablation in our study are notably high. This is due to the PCI approach at our centre being typically guided by IVUS. In other countries, as reported in existing studies, the use of IVUS may not be as prevalent as it is at our centre or in Japan in general. The robust usage of intravascular imaging in our study aligns with emerging consensus regarding complex PCI. For instance, a recent large-scale analysis by Roh et al. demonstrated that IVUS-guided PCI significantly reduced the 3-year risk of target lesion failure compared with angiography-guided PCI in highly complex patient subsets, specifically those presenting with acute MI and concomitant chronic kidney disease.25 Patients with renal impairment frequently present with extensive, heavily calcified coronary anatomies where IVUS is indispensable for accurate morphological assessment and optimal stent expansion. Therefore, the frequent requirement for manual assistance to deliver imaging catheters in our rPCI cohort is a necessary reflection of prioritising optimal clinical outcomes over purely robotic execution. IVUS-guided PCI has been deemed to be beneficial in accurately determining the optimal position of the major axis during stenting, resulting in improved treatment outcomes. A retrospective observational study of 102 IVUS-guided rPCI cases reported safe procedure execution in all cases, satisfactory 30-day outcomes and few procedural complications.23

Consistently throughout our follow-up, both groups demonstrated similar MACE and mortality rates, validating the long-term safety and clinical outcomes of rPCI, which are comparable to the manual approach. The safety of rPCI has been previously reported by Walters et al. in the US population at 6- and 12-month follow-up.26 Similarly, a prospective matched-cohort analysis in the European population revealed no difference between the two groups regarding all-cause mortality and MACE at 12 months.27 To our knowledge, our study is the first of its kind in an Asian population, enhancing the generalisability of the findings for a broader application across diverse demographic groups.

Limitations

Avoiding bias in this study has proven difficult due to the non-randomised nature of the study. Despite using propensity matching to account for potential confounders, differences in baseline and lesion characteristics were still found. The number of affected vessels and presence of CTO were significantly higher in the mPCI group, indicating a more complex cohort. Combined with a more experienced operator in the rPCI group, all these factors could have affected both study outcome and procedure. The single-centre nature of this study also potentially limits transferability of the study findings.

Conclusion

Second-generation rPCI demonstrated a comparable performance in procedural efficacy versus mPCI. Outcomes within the 30-day and 1-year follow-up period between the two groups were also comparable in terms of MACE and mortality. However, to definitively determine if rPCI is truly on par with manual PCI in everyday clinical practice, more research is needed. This could involve conducting even more rigorous clinical trials with random assignment to each method.

Clinical Perspective

Second-generation robotic-assisted percutaneous coronary intervention is safe and highly feasible for routine clinical practice, demonstrating 1-year major adverse cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality rates that are comparable to conventional manual percutaneous coronary intervention.
