When defaults cannot be hedged: XVA calculations via Local Risk-Minimization
Francesca Biagini, Alessandro Gnoatto, Katharina Oberpriller

Abstract
. We consider the pricing and hedging of counterparty credit risk and funding when there is no possibility to hedge the jump to default of either the bank or the counterparty. This represents the situation which is most often encountered in practice, due to the absence of quoted corporate bonds or CDS contracts written on the counterparty and the difficulty for the bank to buy/sell protection on her own default. We apply local risk-minimization to find the optimal strategy and compute it via a BSDE.

1.
Introduction

In this paper we study pricing and hedging of counterparty credit risk and funding in absence of hedging possibilities against the default of the bank or the counterparty. We tackle the market incompleteness due to the presence of possible defaults with the well-known local risk-minimization approach extended to a multi-curve setting. We describe the optimal strategy via the solution of a BSDE and use this result to derive a decomposition of the price in terms of value adjustments.

The 2007-2009 financial crisis provided the motivation for a critical review of several assumptions regarding the valuation of financial products. According to the market practice and the academic literature the value of a product should account for the possibility of default of any agent involved in the transaction. The presence of multiple interest rate curves for funding also represents a significant shift with respect to a classical single-curve asset pricing theory. Such aspects are captured by value adjustments (xVA), which are added or subtracted to an idealized reference price, in order to account for the aforementioned frictions. The first contribution on counterparty risk is
Duffie & Huang (1996)
. Before the 2007–2009 financial crisis,
Brigo & Masetti (2005)
and
Cherubini (2005)
studied the
credit value adjustment
(CVA). Subsequently, a
debt value adjustment
(DVA) was introduced in order to account for the default of the trader in, among others,
Brigo et al. (2011)
and
Brigo et al. (2014)
.

By funding costs/multiple curves we mean that nowadays it is recognized, in line with realistic market conditions, that the trader can fund her trading activity by means of a multitude of sources of funding involving different interest rates. In a Black-Scholes economy without default risk
Piterbarg (2010)
provides valuation formulas in presence or absence of collateral agreements, introducing the so-called
funding value adjustment
(FVA). Such results are generalized in
Bielecki & Rutkowski (2015)
and, in a cross currency setting in
Fujii et al. (2010)
,
Fujii et al. (2011)
, and
Gnoatto & Seiffert (2021)
. Further discussions on the FVA are

Pallavicini et al. (2011)
and
Brigo & Pallavicini (2014)
where the role of central counterparties is discussed as well.

Funding and counterparty risk have been simultaneously considered afterwards, giving rise to the so-called xVAs, where the x represents a placeholder for different valuation adjustments. In a series of papers, Burgard and Kjaer generalize the classical Black-Scholes replication approach to include many effects,
see
Burgard & Kjaer (2011)
and
Burgard & Kjaer (2013)
. However, their results are based on replication arguments. The discounting approach of Brigo and co-authors does not assume market completeness, see
Brigo et al. (2019)
,
Brigo et al. (2022)
and, for a mathematical formalization of the involved pricing equations
Brigo et al. (2024)
. The inclusion of xVAs results in general in non-linear pricing equations satisfying
backward stochastic differential equations
(BSDEs). Such an approach is studied in
Crépey (2015a)
,
Crépey (2015b)
,

Bichuch et al. (2018)
,
Bichuch et al. (2020)
and
Biagini et al. (2021)
.

In practice it is usually not possible to hedge against the default of the bank or the counterparty. However, the vast majority of the literature assumes a complete market setting. This is particulary evident in the initial contributions of Burgard and Kjaer, focusing on extensions of the classical Black-Scholes-type replication argument. In concrete terms, computing e.g. CVA under market completeness is based on the assumption that there exists some traded asset such as a corporate bond or a CDS, such that the trading desk can hedge the jump to default of the counterparty. This assumption is justified only for a very limited set of bigger corporates. For most counterparties instead there are no hedging instruments against the jump to default. This has led to the introduction of different methodologies for the construction of artificial term structure of risk neutral default probabilities, for example
Epperlein et al. (2013)
or even the application of historical default probabilities. However such approaches do not offer any protection against default events.

Regarding the hedging of the bank’s default this is also problematic from a practical point of view: in concrete cases banks have been proxy-hedging their DVA by trading the CDS of peers i.e. of other investment banks. Such a practice can lead to an approximate hedge before default but does not offer protection against the jump to default. For a bank, buying protection on her own default could even be prohibited under certain jurisdictions.

Looking again at the existing literature, the aforementioned discounting approach of Brigo does not require market completeness: only the martingale property of certain cumulative discounted cashflows is postulated. A similar route that does not postulate replication has been followed by Crépey and co-authors from 2017 onward. In a series of papers, an analysis of xVA from the point of view of a stylized model of the balance sheet of the bank has been proposed in
Albanese et al. (2017)

Albanese et al. (2021)

Crépey et al. (2020)

Crépey (2022)
. An interesting approach based on local risk-minimization, limited to CVA, is performed in
Bo & Ceci (2020)
.

To summarize, default risk is in general difficult to hedge in concrete settings, up to the point where counterparty credit risk is essentially similar to an actuarial risk that is warehoused by the bank, in a fashion similar to an insurance company: the CVA is charged as an insurance policy premium to the counterparty and the default events erode a risk margin.

In this paper we study xVA valuation under market incompleteness by means of the local risk-minimization approach. The first study applying local risk-minimization to counterparty risk is
Bo & Ceci (2020)
, however the study is limited to CVA in a single curve framework. In our approach, we obtain a pricing-hedging BSDE in a full multiple-curve setting with realistic collateral specifications and bank defaultability, thus providing a full xVA framework. The system of possibly high-dimensional BSDEs we obtain is amenable to numerical treatment by means of deep learning-based approaches, see
Han et al. (2018)
,
E et al. (2017)
,
Gnoatto et al. (2023)
and
Gnoatto et al. (2024)
. While in the present paper we consider the pricing of a single contingent claim, our approach can be readily extended to account for the presence of a portfolio of derivatives between the bank and the counterparty, as done in
Biagini et al. (2021)
, where xVA under arbitrary aggregation levels (margin sets and netting sets) is analyzed. Our xVA metrics consider only two agents, the bank and the counterparty, in line with, among others,
Bichuch et al. (2018)
or
Brigo et al. (2024)
and the current market practice. We deliberately adopt the perspective of a ring-fenced trading desk pricing a transaction at the netting-set level with a specific counterparty. We acknowledge the ongoing debate in the literature regarding whether valuation adjustments should be evaluated at the level of the bank’s entire portfolio to account for embedded nonlinearities or wealth transfers between shareholders and bondholders as in
Andersen et al. (2019)
. A fully holistic approach requires modeling the bank’s central treasury explicitly, accounting for the Treasury CVA and Treasury DVA incurred when borrowing externally to fund the trading desk. Depending on the treasury’s net funding position, these treasury-level adjustments can offset the trading desk’s DVA or reclassify it as a pure funding benefit. However, incorporating these macro-level treasury dynamics and global debt networks shifts the focus from pricing a bilateral derivative to solving a corporate finance and capital allocation problem. Because our primary objective is to isolate and manage the unhedgeable risk of counterparty and bank default jumps, we restrict our scope to the bilateral netting set. This reflects the market reality that a counterparty’s xVA charge cannot depend on the bank’s opaque, firm-wide netting sets with third parties.

The paper is outlined as follows: in Section
2
we introduce the financial setting. Here we make the explicit choice of presenting a canonical complete financial market model in the multiple curve setting in order to isolate the fact that incompleteness is introduced only by means of the default events. Section
3
presents local risk-minimization in the multiple curve setting as a tool to solve the pricing problem, whereas Section
4
connects local risk-minimization to BSDEs and derives our all-inclusive price decomposition in terms of an idealized clean price and valuation adjustments. Section
6
finally provides a tentative definition of KVA arising from our approach.

Any

Remark 2.2

For

2.2.
Repo-trading

In line with the existing literature, we assume that the trading activity on the risky assets
S
,
introduced in Subsection
2.1

is

This means that borrowing and lending activities related to risky securities are financed via security lending or

see

To model this market we introduce for every risky asset
S^{i},

i=1,...,d,
a repo account

(2.7)

dB_{t}^{i}=r_{t}^{i}B_{t}^{i}dt,\quad t\in[0,T],

where

Since transactions on the repo market are collateralized by the risky assets, repo rates are lower than unsecured funding rates.
As argued in
Crépey (2015a)

(2.8)

\displaystyle\xi_{t}^{i}S_{t}^{i}+\psi_{t}^{i}B_{t}^{i}=0,\quad t\in[0,T],\,i=1,...,d,

where
\xi^{i}

and the repo account
B^{i}
, respectively. We refer to (

It is worth noting that

may be either positive or negative. Here

means that we are in a long position, which has to be financed by collateralization. On the other hand,

implies that the
i
-th asset is shorted, so that the whole amount of collateral is deposited in the riskless asset.

\widetilde{S}\in\mathcal{S}_{\textnormal{d}}^{2}(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
and thus
for every
i=1,...,d
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{B^{i}_{u}}{B^{f}_{u}}\right)^{2}d\langle M^{\widetilde{S}^{i}}\rangle_{u}+\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left|\frac{B^{i}_{u}}{B^{f}_{u}}dA^{\widetilde{S}^{i}}_{u}\right|\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{B^{i}_{u}}{B^{f}_{u}}\right)^{2}\left(\widetilde{S}_{u}^{i}\sigma^{i}(u,S_{u})\right)^{2}du+\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left|\frac{B^{i}_{u}}{B^{f}_{u}}\widetilde{S}_{u}^{i}\left(\mu^{i}(u,S_{u})-r_{u}^{i}\right)du\right|\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C(K_{r})\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(\widetilde{S}_{u}^{i}\sigma^{i}(u,S_{u})\right)^{2}du+\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left|\widetilde{S}_{u}^{i}\left(\mu^{i}(u,S_{u})-r_{u}^{i}\right)du\right|\right)^{2}\right]<\infty,

where we use (
2.5
).

For
i=1,...,d
the dynamics of the stopped process
X^{\tau,i}
are given by

\displaystyle X_{t}^{\tau,i}

\displaystyle=X_{0}^{i}+A_{t}^{X^{\tau,i}}+M_{t}^{X^{\tau,i}}

(2.17)

\displaystyle=X_{0}^{i}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{S_{u}^{\tau,i}}{B_{u}^{f}}\left(\mu^{i}(u,S_{u}^{\tau})-r_{u}^{i}\right)du+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{S_{u}^{\tau,i}}{B_{u}^{f}}{\sigma}^{i}(u,S_{u}^{\tau})dW^{\tau,f,i}_{u},\quad t\in[0,\tau\wedge T].

As
X
is an element in
\mathcal{S}^{2}_{\textnormal{d}}(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
it follows by Remark
2.2
and as
X\in\mathcal{S}_{\textnormal{d}}^{2}(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})

that the stopped process
X^{\tau}=(X_{\tau\wedge t})_{t\in[0,\tau\wedge T]}
is an element in
\mathcal{S}^{2}_{\textnormal{d}}(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{P})
.

2.5.
Financial contract and collateralization

In the following, we assume that the bank enters into an over-the-counter derivative contract at time
0
with a counterparty in the market.

Definition 2.6
.

The payment stream of a
non-defaultable and uncollateralized financial contract
is represented by an
{\mathbb{F}}
-adapted càdlàg process of finite variation
A=(A_{t})_{t\,\in\,[0,T]}.

We use the notation
\Delta A_{t}:=A_{t}-A_{t-}
for the jumps of
A
.
Note that
A_{\tau-}
represents the last payment before default, see also
Brigo & Morini (2010)
.

The cash flow of
A
does not take into account the risk factors resulting from collateralization and the possible default of the bank or the counterparty.

For what concerns financial contracts, collateralization is a method to minimize losses due to default
of the counterparty by using margins. In the financial jargon, a
margin
represents an economic value, either in the form of cash or risky securities,
exchanged between the counterparties of a financial transaction, in order to reduce their risk exposures.
To keep the notation simple we do not distinguish between initial margin and collateral (or variation margin). This means that the collateral process
C=(C_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
represents the overall collateralization. For a detailed study of the two different margins we refer to
Biagini et al. (2021)
.

A collateral is posted between the bank and the counterparty to mitigate counterparty risk.
The collateral process
C
is assumed to be
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{F}}
-adapted.
We follow the convention
of
Brigo et al. (2022)
:

•

If
C_{t}<0,
we say that the bank is the
collateral provider.
It means that the counterparty measures a positive exposure
towards the bank, so it is a potential lender to the bank, hence the bank provides/lends collateral to reduce its exposure.
The bank is remunerated at interest rate
r^{c,l}=(r^{c,l}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
.

•

If
C_{t}>0,
we say that the bank is the
collateral taker.
It means that the bank measures a positive exposure towards
the counterparty, so it is a potential lender to the counterparty, hence the counterparty provides/lends collateral to reduce
its exposure.
In this case, the bank has to pay instantaneous interest rate
r^{c,b}=(r^{c,b}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
.

In this case, the effective collateral accrual rate
\bar{r}^{c}=(\bar{r}_{t}^{c})_{t\in[0,T]}
equals

\bar{r}_{t}^{c}:=r_{t}^{c,l}\textbf{1}_{\left\{C_{t}<0\right\}}+r_{t}^{c,b}\textbf{1}_{\left\{C_{t}\geq 0\right\}},\quad t\in[0,T].

If there is a collateral agreement (or a multitude of agreements) between the bank and the counterparty, in evaluating
portfolio dynamics we need to make a distinction between the value of the portfolio and the wealth of the bank, the two
concepts being distinguished since the bank is not the legal owner of the collateral (prior to default).

In this paper collateral is always posted in the form of cash, in line with standard
current market practice
. Moreover, we assume

rehypothecation,
meaning that the holder of collateral can use the cash to finance her trading activity.
This is the opposite of
segregation,
where the received cash collateral must be kept in a separate account and
cannot be used to finance the purchase of assets.

We simply set
\bar{r}^{c}=r^{c,l}=r^{c,b}
in case there is no bid-offer spread in the collateral rate.
Possible choices for the collateral rate are e.g. €STR for EUR trades, SOFR for USD and SONIA for GBP trades.
Such rates are overnight rates with a negligible embedded risk component. The choice of such approximately risk-free rates
as collateral rates is motivated by market consensus. However, two counterparties might enter a collateral agreement
that involves a remuneration of collateral at any other risky rate of their choice.
Here we do not assume any requirements on collateral rates. This allows us to cover the quite common situation where the
collateral rate agreed between the two counterparties in the CSA is defined by including a real valued
spread over some market publicly observed rate, e.g. €STR
-50\,bps,
where
bps
stands for
basis points.

In case of default, cashflows are exchanged between the surviving agent and the liquidators of the defaulted agent.
Here we use the term
agent
as a placeholder for the bank or for the counterparty. Due to the exchange of
cashflows at default time, agents need to perform a valuation of the position at a random time

represented by the close-out condition, see
Bichuch et al. (2018)
Section 3.4
.
The object of the analysis can be the value in the absence of counterparty risk (referred to in the literature as

risk-free close-out
) or the value of the trade including the price adjustments due to counterparty risk and
funding (
risky close-out
), see e.g.
Brigo & Morini (2010)
.
A risky close-out condition guarantees that the surviving counterparty can ideally
fully substitute the transaction with a new trade entered with another counterparty with the same credit quality.
This comes at the price of a significant increase of the complexity of the valuation equations. Current
market practice and the existing literature mainly focus on the case of risk-free close-out value.

We now introduce an auxiliary artificial interest rate process
r=(r_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
which is assumed to be right-continuous,
\mathbb{F}
-adapted and bounded, i.e. (
2.5
) holds. The associated bank account is denoted by
B^{r}=(B_{t}^{r})_{t\in[0,T]}
.
Following the approach of
Biagini et al. (2021)
, we now introduce the definition of the clean value and choose the minimal measure
\mathbb{Q}
as a reference probability in this setting, as it refers to the assets only and leaves the dynamics of the process associated to
\tau
unaffected.

Definition 2.7
.

The
clean value process

\mathcal{V}=(\mathcal{V}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
of a financial contract
A
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{t}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[-\int_{]t,T]}\frac{B_{t}^{r}}{B_{u}^{r}}\ dA_{u}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[-\int_{]t,T]}{\mathscr{Z}}_{u}\frac{B_{t}^{r}}{B_{u}^{r}}\frac{1}{{\mathscr{Z}}_{t}}dA_{u}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right],\quad t\in[0,T].

The
close-out valuation process

Q=(Q_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
of the financial contract
A
is defined by

\displaystyle Q_{t}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\int_{[t,T]}\frac{B_{t}^{r}}{B_{u}^{r}}\ dA_{u}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\int_{[t,T]}{\mathscr{Z}}_{u}\frac{B_{t}^{r}}{B_{u}^{r}}\frac{1}{{\mathscr{Z}}_{t}}dA_{u}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=-\mathcal{V}_{t}+\Delta A_{t},\quad t\in[0,T].

Remark 2.8
.

(i)

Note that it holds for
t\in[0,T]

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[-\int_{]t,T]}{\mathscr{Z}}_{u}\frac{B_{t}^{r}}{B_{u}^{r}}\frac{1}{{\mathscr{Z}}_{t}}dA_{u}\Big|\mathcal{G}_{t}\right]\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\int_{[t,T]}{\mathscr{Z}}_{u}\frac{B_{t}^{r}}{B_{u}^{r}}\frac{1}{{\mathscr{Z}}_{t}}dA_{u}\Big|\mathcal{G}_{t}\right].

This follows directly by Hypothesis
2.1
and as
\int_{[t,T]}{\mathscr{Z}}_{u}\frac{B_{t}^{r}}{B_{u}^{r}}\frac{1}{{\mathscr{Z}}_{t}}dA_{u}
is
\mathcal{F}_{T}
-measurable, see e.g. Lemma 3.2.1 in
Bielecki et al. (2009)
.

(ii)

On the event
\{{\tau\leq T\}}
we have

(2.19)

\displaystyle Q_{\tau}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\int_{[{\tau},T]}{\mathscr{Z}}_{u}\frac{B_{\tau}^{r}}{B_{u}^{r}}\frac{1}{{\mathscr{Z}}_{\tau}}dA_{u}\Big|\mathcal{G}_{\tau}\right]=-\mathcal{V}_{\tau}+\Delta A_{\tau}=-\mathcal{V}_{\tau},

where we use (
2.18
) and that
\Delta A_{\tau}=0
by Lemma 2.1 in
Crépey (2015b)
.

In the intensity-based setting of this paper,
\Delta A_{\tau}=0
. This would be restrictive in situations where the link between credit exposures and default probabilities, known in practice as wrong-way risk, is significant. An example of such situation would be the pricing of xVA for credit derivatives. This problem has already been addressed in the literature via a variety of approaches:
Brigo & Vrins (2018)
tackle wrong-way risk by means of measure change techniques, while
Jeanblanc & Vrins (2018)
;
Vrins (2026)
consider models where the Azéma supermartingale features a martingale part. This last approach is explicitly linked in
Vrins (2026)
to the dynamic copula models used in
Crépey & Song (2016)
.
However, the main purpose of our article is to study the impact of market incompleteness due to the fact that hedging instruments against the jump to default do not exist in practice in most cases, and for this purpose it is sufficient to use the standard reduced-form approach.

We set
C_{t}:=g(\mathcal{V}_{t}),\,t\,\in\,[0,T],
where
g:\,\mathbb{R}\,\rightarrow\,\mathbb{R}
is a Lipschitz function, which allows to cover realistic collateral specifications, see e.g.
Lichters et al. (2015)
and
Ballotta et al. (2019)
. Moreover, note that

(2.20)

\displaystyle C_{\tau\wedge T}=C_{T}\textbf{1}_{\{\tau>T\}}+C_{\tau}\textbf{1}_{\{\tau\leq T\}}=C_{T}\textbf{1}_{\{\tau>T\}}+C_{\tau-}\textbf{1}_{\{\tau\leq T\}},

as the collateral is not updated at the time of default.

Next, we provide the definition of a defaultable, collateralized contract.

Definition 2.9
.

A
defaultable, collateralized contract

(A,R,C,\tau)
is a contract with the promised cashflow process
A
, collateral account
C
, and a close-out payoff
\vartheta_{\tau}(R,C)
occurring at the first default time
\tau
given by

(2.21)

\displaystyle\vartheta_{\tau}(R,C):=R_{\tau}+C_{\tau-},

where the recovery payoff
R_{\tau}
is defined on the event
\{\tau\leq T\}
by

(2.22)

\displaystyle R_{\tau}

\displaystyle:=\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{C}<\tau^{B}\}}}(R^{C}\mathcal{Y}^{+}-\mathcal{Y}^{-})+\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{B}<\tau^{C}\}}}(\mathcal{Y}^{+}-R^{B}\mathcal{Y}^{-}),

with
\mathcal{Y}:=Q_{\tau}-C_{\tau-}
and the recovery rates
0<R^{j}<1,j\in\{B,C\},
of the bank and the counterparty, respectively.
Equation (
2.21
) is known as
close-out condition
.

Note that we assume the recovery rates to be constant. However this can be extended to the more general case of stochastic rates by imposing some additional measurability and integrability conditions so that the following results continue to hold.

Remark 2.10
.

We can rewrite the close-out condition
\vartheta_{\tau}(R,C)
in Definition
2.21
with
\mathcal{Y}=Q_{\tau}-C_{\tau_{-}}
as follows

\displaystyle\vartheta_{\tau}(R,C)

\displaystyle=R_{\tau}+C_{\tau-}

\displaystyle=\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{C}<\tau^{B}\}}}(R^{C}\mathcal{Y}^{+}-\mathcal{Y}^{-})+\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{B}<\tau^{C}\}}}(\mathcal{Y}^{+}-R^{B}\mathcal{Y}^{-})+C_{\tau-}

\displaystyle=\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{C}<\tau^{B}\}}}(R^{C}\mathcal{Y}^{+}+\mathcal{Y}-\mathcal{Y}^{+})+\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{B}<\tau^{C}\}}}(\mathcal{Y}+\mathcal{Y}^{-}-R^{B}\mathcal{Y}^{-})+C_{\tau-}

\displaystyle=-\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{C}<\tau^{B}\}}}(1-R^{C})\mathcal{Y}^{+}+\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{B}<\tau^{C}\}}}(1-R^{B})\mathcal{Y}^{-}+\mathcal{Y}\left(\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{C}<\tau^{B}\}}}+\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{B}<\tau^{C}\}}}\right)+C_{\tau-}

(2.23)

\displaystyle=-\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{C}<\tau^{B}\}}}(1-R^{C})(Q_{\tau}-C_{\tau-})^{+}+\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{B}<\tau^{C}\}}}(1-R^{B})(Q_{\tau}-C_{\tau-})^{-}+Q_{\tau}-C_{\tau-}+C_{\tau-}

(2.24)

\displaystyle=Q_{\tau}-\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{C}<\tau^{B}\}}}(1-R^{C})(Q_{\tau}-C_{\tau-})^{+}+\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{B}<\tau^{C}\}}}(1-R^{B})(Q_{\tau}-C_{\tau-})^{-},

where we use in (
2.23
) that
\mathbb{P}(\tau^{B}=\tau^{C})=0
and thus
\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{C}<\tau^{B}\}}}+\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{B}<\tau^{C}\}}}=1

\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

The cashflow process associated to a defaultable, collateralized contract
(A,R,C,\tau)
, denoted by
A^{R,C}=(A_{t}^{R,C})_{t\in[0,\tau\wedge T]}
, is given by

(2.25)

\displaystyle A_{t}^{R,C}:={A}_{t}^{C}+\textbf{1}_{\{t\geq\tau\}}R_{\tau},\quad t\in[0,\tau\wedge T],

with

(2.26)

\displaystyle{A}_{t}^{C}=\textbf{1}_{\{t<\tau\}}A_{t}+\textbf{1}_{\{t\geq\tau\}}A_{\tau-}+\textbf{1}_{\{t<\tau\}}C_{t}+\textbf{1}_{\{t\geq\tau\}}C_{\tau-}-\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge t}\bar{r}_{u}^{c}C_{u}du,\quad t\in[0,\tau\wedge T],

and the recovery payoff
R_{\tau}
is defined in (
2.22
).

2.6.
Portfolio and value process

We assume that the bank enters in a defaultable, collateralized contract
(A,R,C,\tau)
at time
0
and invests over the time horizon
[0,\tau\wedge T]
all its wealth fully collateralized in the risky assets and the treasury account
B^{f}
. In particular, the contract
A
can only be entered at time
0
and ends at time
\tau\wedge T.

Definition 2.11
.

We denote by

\varphi=(\xi,\psi,\psi^{f})=(\xi_{t},\psi_{t},\psi^{f}_{t})_{t\in[0,\tau\wedge T]}

the
portfolio/strategy
of the bank where

(i)

\xi=(\xi^{1},...,\xi^{d})
is an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued,
\mathbb{G}
-predictable process, representing the number of basic traded assets
S^{1},...,S^{d}
,

(ii)

\psi=(\psi^{1},...,\psi^{d})
is an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued,
\mathbb{G}
-predictable process, representing the shares of the repo accounts
B^{1},...,B^{d}
and
\psi
is uniquely determined by
\xi
due to the repo-trading constraint (
2.8
),

(iii)

\psi^{f}
is an
\mathbb{R}
-valued,
\mathbb{G}
-adapted process denoting the shares in the treasury account
B^{f}
.

Definition 2.12
.

The
value process
of a portfolio
\varphi
for a defaultable, collateralized contract
(A,R,C,\tau)
, denoted by
V^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C})=(V_{t}^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C}))_{t\in[0,\tau\wedge T]}
, is given by

(2.27)

V_{t}^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C}):=\psi_{t}^{f}B_{t}^{f}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\psi_{t}^{i}B_{t}^{i}+\xi_{t}^{i}S_{t}^{i}\right)=\psi_{t}^{f}B_{t}^{f},\quad t\in[0,\tau\wedge T],

where we use the repo-trading constraint in (
2.8
).

Note that while the value process
V^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C})
does not depend on the contract
(A,R,C,\tau)
, the dynamics of
V^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C})
do, see also
Brigo et al. (2022)
.

We now want to hedge a defaultable, collateralized contract
(A,R,C,\tau)
with a suitable strategy
\varphi
.

Definition 2.13
.

The
value process

V(\varphi,A^{R,C})=(V_{t}(\varphi,A^{R,C}))_{t\in[0,\tau\wedge T]}
of the bank’s wealth associated to a portfolio
\varphi
for a defaultable, collateralized contract
(A,R,C,\tau)
is given on the event
\{{t<\tau\}}
for every
t\in[0,\tau\wedge T]
by

\displaystyle V_{t}(\varphi,A^{R,C}):=V_{t}^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C})-C_{t}=V_{t}^{p}(\varphi,A^{C})-C_{t},

and on the event
\{{\tau\leq T\}}
by

\displaystyle V_{\tau}(\varphi,A^{R,C}):=V_{\tau}^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C})=V_{\tau}^{p}(\varphi,{A}^{C})+R_{\tau}.

Remark 2.14
.

Note that we assumed boundedness for all interest rates for the sake of simplicity in order to guarantee existence and uniqueness of the BSDEs we are going to consider in the sequel. Boundedness can be dropped by resorting to the results of e.g.
El Karoui & Huang (1997)
,
Yong (2006)
or
Gashi & Li (2019)
.
Concerning the deep-learning-based numerical algorithms mentioned in
the Introduction, we remark that boundedness of the interest rates is not necessary to ensure convergence.
For example, if a short rate is modeled by a
Vasicek model, the corresponding BSDE generator remains Lipschitz in the
solution variables, but the associated Lipschitz coefficient becomes stochastic
rather than uniformly bounded. Well-posedness and continuous dependence for
BSDEs under stochastic Lipschitz conditions are available under suitable
integrability assumptions; see
Li et al. (2023)
. This suggests a natural
localization procedure. More precisely, for
r_{t}^{x,N}:=(-N)\vee r_{t}^{x}\wedge N
,
the BSDE corresponding to the truncated rate
r^{x,N}
has a uniformly bounded
rate-dependent Lipschitz coefficient and, subject to the remaining assumptions,
falls within the uniformly Lipschitz setting covered by existing convergence
results for deep BSDE schemes, such as
Han & Long (2020)
. The general stability
results of
Papapantoleon et al. (2023)
then provide a framework,
under suitable convergence and integrability conditions, for passing
from the localized BSDEs to the original stochastic-Lipschitz BSDE as

N\to\infty
. In this way, the numerical error can be separated into the deep
BSDE approximation error for the localized problem and the localization error
controlled by BSDE stability.

3.
Local risk-minimization in a multi-curve setting

Our goal is to address the market incompleteness due to the presence of possible defaults via local risk-minimization extended to a multi-curve setting. According to this approach, also in the case of an incomplete market a contingent claim or a payment stream is perfectly replicated via a portfolio admitting a cost.
In the sequel we define the cost process of a financial contract
A
, which is then used to introduce the definition of a locally risk-minimizing strategy and a Föllmer-Schweizer decomposition in the presence of multi-curves. We then link the existence of a locally risk-minimizing strategy to a Föllmer-Schweizer decomposition.

3.1.
Locally risk-minimizing strategies and Föllmer-Schweizer decomposition in a multi-curve setting

Before providing the definition of a pseudo-locally risk-minimizing strategy in this setting we introduce some further notation.

Notation 3.1
.

(i)

We denote by
\Theta_{X}^{\mathbb{G},\tau}
the space of all
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued,
\mathbb{G}
-predictable processes
\xi=(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,\tau\wedge T]}
such that for all
i=1,...,d
the integral
\int_{0}^{\cdot\wedge\tau}\xi_{u}^{i}\frac{B_{u}^{i}}{B_{u}^{f}}d\widetilde{S}_{u}^{i}
is well-defined and in
\mathcal{S}^{2}_{\textnormal{d}}(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{P})
. Equivalently,
\Theta_{X}^{\mathbb{G},\tau}
contains all
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued,
\mathbb{G}
-predictable processes
\xi=(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,\tau\wedge T]}
such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge T}\xi^{\top}_{u}d\langle M^{X}\rangle_{u}\xi_{u}+\left(\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge T}\left|\xi^{\top}_{u}dA^{X}_{u}\right|\right)^{2}\right]<\infty.

(ii)

\Theta_{X}^{\mathbb{F},\tau}
is the space of all
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued,
\mathbb{F}
-predictable processes
\xi=(\xi_{t})_{t\in[0,\tau\wedge T]}
such that (
3.1
) holds.

Locally risk-minimizing strategies are not self-financing. However, we rely on Definition 7 in
Brigo et al. (2022)
of a self-financing strategy to define the cost process.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that a trading strategy
\varphi=(\xi,\psi,\psi^{f})
with
\xi\in\Theta_{X}^{\mathbb{G},\tau}
is a
self-financing strategy
inclusive of a defaultable, collateralized contract
(A,R,C,\tau)
, if the value process
V^{p}_{t}(\varphi,A^{R,C})=\psi_{t}^{f}B_{t}^{f}
satisfies on
[0,\tau\wedge T]

(3.2)

\displaystyle dV_{t}^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C})

\displaystyle=\psi^{f}_{t}dB_{t}^{f}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\psi_{t}^{i}dB_{t}^{i}+\xi_{t}^{i}dS_{t}^{i}\right)+dA_{t}^{R,C}.

For a self-financing strategy
\varphi
inclusive of a defaultable, collateralized contract
(A,R,C,\tau)
it holds

(3.3)

\displaystyle dV_{t}^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C})

\displaystyle=\psi^{f}_{t}dB_{t}^{f}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\psi_{t}^{i}dB_{t}^{i}+\xi_{t}^{i}dS_{t}^{i}\right)+dA_{t}^{R,C}

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\psi^{f}_{t}dB_{t}^{f}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{t}^{i}S_{t}^{i}(B_{t}^{i})^{-1}dB_{t}^{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{t}^{i}dS_{t}^{i}+dA_{t}^{R,C},

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\psi^{f}_{t}B_{t}^{f}\frac{dB_{t}^{f}}{B_{t}^{f}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{t}^{i}B_{t}^{i}\frac{1}{B_{t}^{i}}\left(dS_{t}^{i}-r_{t}^{i}S_{t}^{i}dt\right)+dA_{t}^{R,C},

(3.6)

\displaystyle=V_{t}^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C})r_{t}^{f}dt+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{t}^{i}B_{t}^{i}d\widetilde{S}_{t}^{i}+dA_{t}^{R,C},

where we use the repo-trading constraint
\psi_{t}^{i}=-(B_{t}^{i})^{-1}\xi_{t}^{i}S_{t}^{i}
in (
3.4
). Moreover, (
3.5
) follows by (
2.12
). In (
3.6
) we use that
V^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C})
satisfies (
2.27
) and
dB_{t}^{f}=r_{t}^{f}B_{t}^{f}dt
. By setting

(3.7)

\displaystyle\widetilde{V}_{t}^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C}):=\frac{V_{t}^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C})}{B_{t}^{f}},\quad t\in[0,\tau\wedge T],

we conclude with (
3.6
)

\displaystyle d\widetilde{V}_{t}^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B_{t}^{f}}dV_{t}^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C})-\frac{V_{t}^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C})}{B_{t}^{f}}r_{t}^{f}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B_{t}^{f}}\left(V_{t}^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C})r_{t}^{f}dt+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{t}^{i}B_{t}^{i}d\widetilde{S}_{t}^{i}+dA_{t}^{R,C}\right)-\frac{V_{t}^{p}(\varphi,A^{R,C})}{B_{t}^{f}}r_{t}^{f}dt

(3.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B_{t}^{f}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{t}^{i}B_{t}^{i}d\widetilde{S}_{t}^{i}+dA_{t}^{R,C}\right).

This motivates the following definition of the cost process of a portfolio.

Definition 3.3

5.
Two-Step valuation of xVAs

In this section we show that a slight modification of the assumptions underlying our approach based on local risk-minimization provides a foundation to a version of the two-step valuation approach proposed in
Pelsser & Stadje (2014)
,
Delong et al. (2019)
and
Barigou et al. (2023)
. This allows us to obtain a formula for CVA (and similarly for other valuation adjustments) which is particularly convenient in applications.

We consider
W^{f}=(W_{t}^{f})_{t\in[0,T]}
and
W^{\perp}=(W_{t}^{\perp})_{t\in[0,T]}
to be
d
- resp.
d^{\prime}
-dimensional Brownian motions on
(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P})
and
\mathbb{F}^{W^{f}}=(\mathcal{F}^{W^{f}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
and
\mathbb{F}^{W^{\perp}}=(\mathcal{F}^{W^{\perp}}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
their associated natural filtrations. We set
\mathbb{F}:=\mathbb{F}^{W^{f}}\otimes\mathbb{F}^{W^{\perp}}
, that is
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\mathcal{F}^{W^{f}}_{t}\otimes\mathcal{F}^{W^{\perp}}_{t}
for every
t\in[0,T]
. Let the stopping times
\tau^{B},\tau^{C},\tau
and the associated filtrations be as introduced in Section
2
. On
(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P})
we consider the filtration
\mathbb{G}=(\mathcal{G}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{t}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{F}_{t}\vee\mathcal{H}^{B}_{t}\vee\mathcal{H}^{C}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{W^{f}}_{t}\otimes\mathcal{F}^{W^{\perp}}_{t}\vee\mathcal{H}^{B}_{t}\vee\mathcal{H}^{C}_{t},\quad t\in[0,T],

and set
\mathcal{G}:=\mathcal{G}_{T}.
All filtrations are required to satisfy the usual hypotheses of completeness and right-continuity.

In addition to the assumptions in Section
2
, we assume that all coefficients
\mu^{i},\sigma^{i}
, repo rates
r^{i}
for
i=1,...,d,
collateral and funding rates are
\mathbb{F}^{W^{f}}
-adapted, so that completeness is preserved for the clean market. With respect to the previous sections, we assume that the close-out valuation process
Q
is
\mathbb{F}^{W^{f}}
-adapted by setting

\displaystyle Q_{t}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\int_{[t,T]}\frac{B_{t}^{r}}{B_{u}^{r}}\ dA_{u}\Big|\mathcal{F}^{W^{f}}_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\int_{[t,T]}{\mathscr{Z}}_{u}\frac{B_{t}^{r}}{B_{u}^{r}}\frac{1}{{\mathscr{Z}}_{t}}dA_{u}\Big|\mathcal{F}^{W^{f}}_{t}\right]\quad t\in[0,T].

Similarly, we also assume that the collateral process
C
is
\mathbb{F}^{W^{f}}
-adapted.

We allow the default intensities to be
\mathbb{F}
-adapted, which introduces an additional dependence on the Brownian motion
W^{\perp}
. Recalling our previous assumption that intensity rates are bounded, a concrete example that satisfies all the assumptions of the present setting is given by the Jacobi diffusion

\displaystyle d\lambda^{j}_{t}=\kappa^{j}(\theta^{j}-\lambda^{j}_{t})dt+\sqrt{(\lambda_{t}-\lambda^{j}_{max})(\lambda_{t}-\lambda^{j}_{min})}\left(\rho^{j}dW^{f,1}_{t}+\sqrt{1-(\rho^{j})^{2}}dW^{\perp,j}\right),\quad t\in[0,T],

where
\lambda^{j}_{max},\lambda^{j}_{min}
are the upper and lower bound for the intensity respectively,
\kappa^{j},\theta^{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\rho^{j}\in(-1,1)
for
j\in\{B,C\}
. In this setting, let us consider the CVA which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\textnormal{CVA}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{B_{t}^{r}}{\mathscr{Z}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\textbf{1}_{\{\tau\leq T\}}\textbf{1}_{\{{\tau^{C}<\tau^{B}\}}}\frac{\mathscr{Z}_{\tau}}{B_{\tau}^{r}}(1-R^{C})(Q_{\tau}-C_{\tau-})^{+}\Big|\mathcal{G}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\textbf{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\frac{B_{t}^{r}}{\mathscr{Z}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[(1-R^{C})\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\mathscr{Z}_{u}}{B_{u}^{r}}e^{-\int_{t}^{u}\lambda^{C}_{s}+\lambda^{B}_{s}ds}\lambda^{C}_{u}(Q_{u}-C_{u})^{+}du\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right],\quad t\in[0,T],

by using Proposition 5.1.1 in
Bielecki & Rutkowski (2004)
. Next, following
Pelsser & Stadje (2014)
, we condition on the full information on the market risk factors up to maturity and consider the two-step valuation

\displaystyle\textnormal{CVA}_{t}

\displaystyle=\textbf{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\frac{B_{t}^{r}}{\mathscr{Z}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[(1-R^{C})\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\mathscr{Z}_{u}}{B_{u}^{r}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[e^{-\int_{t}^{u}\lambda^{C}_{s}+\lambda^{B}_{s}ds}\lambda^{C}_{u}\Big|\mathcal{F}^{W^{f}}_{T}\otimes\mathcal{F}^{W^{\perp}}_{t}\right](Q_{u}-C_{u})^{+}du\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right],

where we apply Fubini’s theorem. We introduce the notation

\displaystyle\Lambda_{t,u}^{C}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[e^{-\int_{t}^{u}\lambda^{C}_{s}+\lambda^{B}_{s}ds}\lambda^{C}_{u}\Big|\mathcal{F}^{W^{f}}_{T}\otimes\mathcal{F}^{W^{\perp}}_{t}\right],\quad u\in[t,T],

to represent this term that can be computed based on historical information regarding the defaults and then switch to the risk-neutral measure to finally obtain

\displaystyle\textnormal{CVA}_{t}=\textbf{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}B_{t}^{r}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[(1-R^{C})\int_{t}^{T}\frac{1}{B_{u}^{r}}\Lambda_{t,u}^{C}(Q_{u}-C_{u})^{+}du\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right].

This allows the bank to calculate in a first step
\Lambda_{t,u}^{C}
conditional on the market-factor information and on
t
-conditional historical information from the internal rating system, as in the outlined setting the bank cannot fall back on the dynamics of the intensity rates under
\mathbb{Q}
. In a second step, the usual approximation of the CVA can be performed either via American Monte Carlo or more advanced deep learning methods. In particular, the local risk-minimization approach of the present paper provides a sound mathematical foundation to this appealing computational strategy. Similar calculations hold also for the remaining valuation adjustments.

6.
Capital Value Adjustment (KVA)

In recent years, there has been a debate regarding the need to take into account the funding cost generated by capital requirements on derivative transactions. According to Basel III a bank that is holding a derivative portfolio must set aside capital that can be determined according to different approaches: this could involve a standard model defined by the regulator or an internal model. A first approach that includes the cost of capital in the valuation of derivatives can be found in
Green & Kenyon (2014)
. Later studies include, among many
Kjaer (2017)
and
Kjaer (2018)
, who consider the balance sheet model of
Andersen et al. (2019)
or
Castagna (2019)
. There are other approaches in the literature that see capital requirements as a consequence of market incompleteness, see
Brigo et al. (2017)
,
Albanese et al. (2017)
,
Arnsdorf (2020)
,
Crépey et al. (2020)
and
Crépey (2022)
.
The capital is provided by the shareholders, who require in return a remuneration for the regulatory capital that they are providing, which is typically termed
hurdle rate
. In line with other value adjustments, the discussion has been about the possibility to charge such costs to the client by means of a suitable value adjustment. In the following, we provide a tentative definition of a capital valuation adjustment that arises from the approach presented in this work. We first consider the variation of the cost process of the local risk-minimizing strategy given by

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{C}_{t}^{\varphi,A^{R,C}}:=\mathcal{C}_{t}^{\varphi,A^{R,C}}-\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\varphi,A^{R,C}}.

This process measures the variation of the cash capital that needs to be put aside or withdrawn. We assume that the capital requirement at a certain point in time
t
is given by the expected shortfall with confidence level
\alpha
given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{ES}^{\mathbb{P}}_{\alpha}\left[\left.\mathcal{C}_{t+\Delta t}^{\varphi,A^{R,C}}-\mathcal{C}_{t}^{\varphi,A^{R,C}}\right|\mathcal{G}_{t}\right]

with
\Delta t
a fixed time horizon, e.g. 1 year. When the shareholders provide such capital, they are remunerated by means of a hurdle rate
h
. When applied to the whole lifetime of the transaction, this gives rise to the following definition

\displaystyle\textnormal{KVA}_{t}:=B^{r}_{t}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\left.\int_{t}^{\tau\wedge T}\frac{1}{B^{r}_{u}}h_{u}\mathbb{ES}^{\mathbb{P}}_{\alpha}\left[\left.\mathcal{C}_{u+\Delta t}^{\varphi,A^{R,C}}-\mathcal{C}_{u}^{\varphi,A^{R,C}}\right|\mathcal{G}_{u}\right]du\right|\mathcal{G}_{t}\right].

With this definition the KVA is then charged as a mark-up to the customer on top of the price represented by
{V}_{t}(\varphi,{A}^{R,C})
. In case the market is complete, then we clearly have that the cost process is a.s. constant,
\mathcal{C}_{t}^{\varphi,A^{R,C}}-\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\varphi,A^{R,C}}\equiv 0

d\mathbb{P}\otimes dt
-a.s., so that
\textnormal{KVA}_{t}\equiv 0
and the client is charged the price
{V}_{t}(\varphi,{A}^{R,C})
. In summary, the bank charges the amount
V^{full}_{t}(\varphi,{A}^{R,C})
given by

\displaystyle V^{full}_{t}(\varphi,{A}^{R,C}):=V_{t}(\varphi,{A}^{R,C})+\textnormal{KVA}_{t}.

This market practice in the banking sector essentially mimics the traditional insurance valuation, where pricing is performed under the historical measure with the addition of a risk-charge, whose role is taken by the KVA. Clearly, our valuation is performed under a risk neutral setting since we assumed that market risk factors can be hedged. As already claimed in
Crépey (2022)
the idea of adding a KVA mimics the valuation approach of Solvency II where the cost of capital is added to the best estimate of the liability.

Remark 6.1
.

In the introduction, we mentioned that all xVA metrics in the present paper are computed at the level of the portfolio of claims between the bank and the counterparty, under the standing principle of
bilateral contestability
. We notice that capital requirements, under a bilateral contestable framework are more conservative, provided that these are defined through a coherent risk measure. In fact, let
\rho
be a coherent risk measure, and let
(N_{i})_{i}
denote the exposures associated with different netting sets. By subadditivity, one has

\rho\left(\sum_{i}N_{i}\right)\leq\sum_{i}\rho(N_{i}).

Thus, computing capital requirements at the level of individual netting sets and summing the results yields an upper bound on the portfolio-level capital.

These observations show that restricting attention to bilateral netting sets does not lead to an underestimation of risk. On the contrary, it produces a robust upper bound on capital adjustments by deliberately ignoring diversification effects that are not bilaterally verifiable. In this sense, the bilateral framework can be interpreted as a prudent approximation under information constraints.

(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{P})
-semimartingale with decomposition
Y=Y_{0}+A^{Y}+M^{Y}.
We say that
Y
satisfies the
structure condition (SC)
if

(i)

Y\in\mathcal{S}^{2}_{\textnormal{loc,d}}(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{P}),

(ii)

A^{Y}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t. the bracket process
\langle M^{Y}\rangle=(\langle M^{Y^{i}},M^{Y^{j}}\rangle)_{i,j=1,...,d}
, i.e. there exists a
\mathbb{G}
-predictable process
\lambda=(\lambda_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
such that
A_{t}^{Y^{i}}=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}{\lambda}_{u}^{j}d\left\langle M^{Y^{i}},M^{Y^{j}}\right\rangle_{u}
, for
i=1,\ldots,d
and
t\in[0,T],

(iii)

the
mean-variance tradeoff
process
K^{Y}=(K_{t}^{Y})_{t\in[0,T]}

(A.1)

\displaystyle{K}_{t}^{Y}:=\int_{0}^{t}{\lambda}_{u}^{\top}{d}\left\langle M^{Y}\right\rangle_{u}{\lambda}_{u}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{u}^{i}\lambda_{u}^{j}d\langle M^{Y^{i}},M^{Y^{i}}\rangle_{u}

satisfies
K_{T}^{Y}<\infty

\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

It is well-known that there exists a link between no arbitrage conditions and the structure condition for continuous semimartingales. Denote by
\mathcal{P}^{\textnormal{loc}}_{\mathbb{G}}(Y)
the set of all equivalent local
\mathbb{G}
-martingale measures
\mathbb{Q}
of
Y
w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}
.
Then the following result holds, see Theorem 3.5 in
Schweizer (2001)
and Theorem 3.4 in
Monat & Stricker (1995)
.

Theorem A.2
.

Let
Y
be a continuous process in
\mathcal{S}^{2}_{\textnormal{loc,d}}(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{P})
and
N
a random variable in
L^{2}(\mathcal{G}_{T},\mathbb{P})
.

(i)

If
\mathcal{P}^{\textnormal{loc}}_{\mathbb{G}}(Y)\neq\emptyset,
then
Y
satisfies (SC).

(ii)

If
Y
satisfies (SC) and the mean-variance tradeoff process
K^{Y}
is uniformly bounded, then
N
admits a unique
\mathbb{G}
-Föllmer-Schweizer decomposition.
