Deep cross-modal affective memory networks with adaptive multi-source heterogeneous transfer learning in speech emotion recognition
Authors: Xiaofen Zhao, Jingchao Liu, Lei Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An innovative Deep Cross-Modal Emotional Memory Network (DCM-EMNet) and an Adaptive Multi-source Heterogeneous Transfer Learning Framework (AMS-HTLF) are proposed in this paper. The multimodal data fusion and multi-source heterogeneous data migration problems in speech emotion recognition are effectively solved by this method.In DCM-EMNet, multi-level feature fusion, dynamic affective memory mechanism and cross-modal consistency constraints are adopted to make full use of the bimodal information of speech and text descriptions, and the accuracy of emotion recognition is thereby significantly improved. Meanwhile, through the AMS-HTLF framework, adaptive feature alignment and heterogeneous label mapping are implemented, heterogeneous data from multiple different source domains are effectively migrated, and the generalization ability of the model on the target domain is significantly enhanced. Experimental results show that significant performance improvement is achieved by the proposed method on multiple speech emotion recognition datasets, and its effectiveness and practicality are fully verified. This study not only provides new research perspectives and methods in the field of speech emotion recognition, but also expands new ideas in the field of multimodal learning and transfer learning.

Introduction

In the domain of artificial intelligence (AI) and human–computer interaction (HCI)1,affective computing has emerged as a critical technology to bridge the gap between machine cognition and human emotions2, which play a fundamental role in human communication, Emotions are often conveyed through speech signals. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the two-dimensional Arousal-Valence (V-A)3,4 emotion space model categorizes speech emotions into negative, positive, and neutral states, with varying intensity levels (low to high).Speech signals encode rich emotional information, however decoding these patterns remains a challenging task for machines. Accurate speech emotion recognition (SER) is essential not only for enhancing machines’ understanding of human intent but also for improving the overall HCI experience, enabling more natural and intuitive communication .Despite recent advancements in deep learning-based SER systems, real-time emotion recognition in noisy environments continues to pose significant challenges due to variability in speech patterns and cultural differences in emotional expression.Fig. 1the two-dimensional Arousal-Valence emotion space model.

the two-dimensional Arousal-Valence emotion space model.

However, speech emotion recognition technology faces many challenges in practical applications5. First, the diversity of emotional expressions makes it difficult for machines to accurately capture and recognize them6,7. Each person’s emotional expression is unique and is affected by multiple factors such as personal experience, cultural background and specific situations. This diversity requires a high degree of flexibility and adaptability for machines to accurately recognize and understand the emotional expressions of different individuals in different situations.Second, the scarcity of data labeling is also an important factor that restricts the development of speech emotion recognition technology 8,9. High-quality labeled data is the basis for training machine learning models, but in the field of emotion computing, the acquisition of labeled data is exceptionally difficult. This is mainly because emotion is a subjective and complex psychological phenomenon, which is difficult to be accurately measured and labeled by objective standards10. Therefore, how to effectively acquire and label emotion data has become an urgent problem.In addition, noise interference in complex environments is also a major challenge for speech emotion recognition technology 11,12. In practical applications, speech signals are often interfered by various noises, such as environmental noise, equipment noise, etc.. These noises not only reduce the clarity of the speech signal, but also mask the emotional information, which makes it difficult for the machine to accurately recognize. Therefore, how to extract effective emotional information under noise interference has become a technical challenge to be overcome.

In response to these challenges, scholars have conducted in-depth research and achieved a series of important results.A novel streaming active learning approach for real-world scenario speech emotion recognition is proposed in References13. This method integrates the MLOps concept with the human-assisted annotation mechanism to construct an adaptive data screening system. The system can identify and prioritize high-value speech samples in real-time, which significantly reduces the manual annotation workload while continuously improving the training effect of deep neural networks.A multimodal emotion recognition algorithm based on K-mean clustering and kernel-typical correlation analysis has been proposed in References14, which optimizes intermodal heterogeneity and improves recognition performance by fusing the time–frequency domain features of facial expressions and speech.An optimized isovariant subconvolutional neural network based speech emotion recognition method is proposed in References15, which achieves accurate classification of emotional states such as neutral, happy, sad, angry and fear by Bellman filter preprocessing and single-candidate optimizer feature selection using the RAVDESS database, which provides a new technological solution for emotion recognition in human–computer interaction. A Coordinated Attention-based Transformer model (CAT) has been proposed in References16 ,which is based on a dual-attention paradigm that dynamically coordinates the importance of intra- and inter-modal factors by inferring the passage rates of self-attention and trans-attention layer by layer. The results show that the method achieves state-of-the-art performance level under the same conditions.A semi-supervised domain adaptive network (SDAN)-based approach for cross-corpus speech emotion recognition is proposed in References17. A supervised feature dictionary-based domain adaptation method was devised to reduce the differences between the feature representations of the source and target domains under different emotions. Each dictionary is associated with the extracted feature representation of a particular domain and independently aggregates samples under different emotions to obtain performance improvement.References18 uses classifier combination and attention-based feature fusion for speech emotion recognition, and the results show that it can improve the performance of recognition to some extent.Previous studies have extensively investigated speech emotion recognition from diverse perspectives. For instance, References19 and20 have made significant contributions to the field of affective computing by establishing solid theoretical frameworks and developing innovative technical approaches. These pioneering works have not only advanced the fundamental understanding of SER mechanisms but also facilitated practical applications in emerging domains such as intelligent customer service systems, mental health monitoring platforms, and autonomous driving technologies.The remainder of this paper is organized as follows:

Related work

Research on speech emotion recognition methods

Speech emotion recognition is achieved by machine processing of speech signals with the aim of recognizing the emotions embedded in the speech. The complete process of speech emotion recognition is shown in Fig. 2. First, the input speech is converted into a digital signal; subsequently, the emotion features are extracted from the signal; finally, the classification results are calculated by the model.Fig. 2Process of speech emotion recognition.

Process of speech emotion recognition.

In traditional methods, emotion classification mainly relies on hand-designed acoustic features21,22. These features usually include Mel Frequency Cepstrum Coefficient (MFCC), fundamental frequency F0, resonance peaks, etc. Emotion-related information can be extracted from these features. When these handcrafted features are combined with machine learning algorithms such as Support Vector Machines (SVM)23, K Nearest Neighbors (KNN)24, and Decision Trees, the task of sentiment classification is accomplished.However, the effectiveness of the application of this approach is limited by several factors. When dealing with complex emotions, these nuances are often not fully captured by handcrafted features due to the mixing of multidimensional sentiment features that may be involved. In addition, cross-language emotion recognition faces challenges due to the large differences in speech features across languages25,26. Furthermore, the ability of manual features to generalize across datasets is often compromised due to differences in datasets in terms of acquisition environments and annotation standards.

In recent years, with the rapid development of deep learning technology27, models such as convolutional neural networks (CNN)28,29, recurrent neural networks (RNN)30,31 and their variants have gradually been established as mainstream methods in the field of speech emotion recognition. Effective feature representations can be automatically learned from raw speech signals by these deep learning models without the need for manual feature design. Through multi-layer nonlinear transformations, the high-level semantic information in the speech signal can be captured by the deep learning models, thus significantly improving the performance of sentiment recognition. Especially in complex emotion processing and cross-linguistic tasks, stronger generalization capabilities are demonstrated by the deep learning models.However, the application of deep learning models in the field of speech emotion recognition still faces some challenges32. First, the reliance on large-scale labeled data is considered an important issue for deep learning models. In practical application scenarios, the labeling of emotion data usually takes a lot of manpower and time, which makes model training difficult. Second, the complexity of feature extraction has also become one of the difficulties in current research33. Although feature representations can be automatically learned by deep learning models, how to design effective network structures and optimization algorithms to extract the most representative sentiment features is still a challenge to be overcome. Finally, the lack of cross-domain generalization capability is also a focus of current research. Since there are often significant differences between different datasets, how to make the model still perform well on another dataset after completing training on one dataset is still an unsolved problem34. To address these challenges, various approaches are being explored by researchers, such as data augmentation, transfer learning, semi-supervised learning, etc., aiming to improve the performance of deep learning models in speech emotion recognition.

Research on multimodal learning methods

Multimodal learning is regarded as a powerful technique in which the enhancement of system performance is realized through the integration of information from different modalities35. In the field of speech emotion recognition, multimodal approaches are widely used, in which multiple information sources, such as speech signals, facial expression features, and text descriptions, are often combined to achieve a more comprehensive capture and parsing of emotional expressions. The core advantage of this approach is that the complementary nature of different modalities can be fully utilized, thus significantly improving the accuracy and robustness of emotion recognition.However, the application of multimodal learning in speech emotion recognition faces many challenges. The first challenge is the synchronization and consistency of multimodal data. In practice, due to the differences in acquisition equipment, environmental conditions, and user behaviors, it is often difficult to keep the data from different modalities completely synchronized, and temporal misalignment or content inconsistency is often observed. Such inconsistencies have been demonstrated to significantly impair the efficacy of multimodal fusion, ultimately resulting in substantial degradation of recognition performance.

Feature fusion strategies are generally recognized as significantly difficult in current research. In order to achieve effective fusion of multimodal information, various methods have been proposed by researchers, mainly including feature-level fusion, decision-level fusion, and hybrid fusion strategies.

In feature-level fusion, the feature vectors of different modalities are usually directly spliced in the data preprocessing stage or integrated after specific mathematical transformations, and finally used as input data for classifiers. Decision-level fusion, on the other hand, is implemented in such a way that each modality first completes sentiment recognition independently, and then the recognition results of each modality are combined through methods such as voting mechanism or weighted averaging. The advantages of the hybrid fusion strategy are fully demonstrated, which is implemented both at the feature level for fusion operations and at the decision level for result integration, thus maximizing the complementary use of multimodal information.However, these fusion methods have been shown to have significant limitations in dealing with the heterogeneity, redundancy, and complex relationships among features in multimodal data. Heterogeneity is mainly manifested in the observed variability of different modal data in terms of representation and distributional characteristics, which makes direct fusion operations difficult to be effectively implemented. The problem of redundancy is reflected in the fact that repetitive or similar information may be simultaneously included in different modal data, a phenomenon that leads to an unnecessary increase in the dimensionality of the fused feature vectors, a consequent increase in computational complexity, and a significant amplification of the risk of noise interference. To further complicate matters, complex relationships such as nonlinearity and nonsmoothness are found to exist between different modal features, which makes the design of fusion strategies even more difficult.

Aiming at the above challenges, various solutions are being actively explored and researched. First, data heterogeneity and redundancy can be effectively reduced by data preprocessing and feature selection techniques. Second, deep learning methods are widely used in the automatic learning process of feature representation and fusion strategies. In addition, advanced methods such as transfer learning and semi-supervised learning are introduced to enhance the generalization ability and adaptability of the model. Through these technical approaches, the potential of multimodal learning in speech emotion recognition is expected to be further explored, and important breakthroughs and advances are expected to be realized.

Transfer Learning Process.

In the field of speech emotion recognition, the importance of transfer learning has been particularly emphasized. This is mainly due to the fact that the process of collecting and labeling high-quality sentiment data is usually accompanied by high cost consumption and significant time investment, which leads to the commonly observed scarcity of training data. To address this problem, a variety of transfer learning methods have been successfully applied in speech emotion recognition, mainly in the form of feature-based migration, model-based migration, and instance-based migration.

Feature-based migration approaches are typically implemented in two key steps: first, feature representations with generalization are extracted from the source domain38; subsequently, these features are applied to task processing in the target domain. The core challenge of the approach is that feature space mappings that can span across different domains need to be efficiently constructed such that similarity in the distribution of source and target domain data in feature space is ensured. However, when the difference in data distribution between the source and target domains is observed to be more significant, direct feature migration operations may lead to the introduction of noise, which ultimately has a negative impact on the recognition performance39.The model-based migration approach is usually implemented as the following process: first, the model parameters trained on the source domain are used as the initial parameters for model training in the target domain; subsequently, the model parameters are adapted to the data characteristics of the target domain by means of fine-tuning and other technical means. The main advantage of this method is that the source domain knowledge can be fully migrated and utilized, while the amount of training data required for the target domain can be significantly reduced.However, the migration effectiveness of the approach was found to be constrained by two key factors: first, the degree of correlation between the source and target domain tasks was shown to have a decisive impact on the migration effectiveness; second, the generalization ability of the model architecture itself was also shown to be an important factor affecting the success of the migration. When the above conditions are not adequately met, negative migration phenomena may be observed, leading to a less-than-expected model performance on the target domain.

The instance-based migration approach is mainly realized through the following mechanism: instances in the source domain are re-weighted or selected to improve the similarity of their data distributions with the target domain, thus providing auxiliary support for learning tasks in the target domain. The method has been shown to be significantly effective in cases where the data distributions of the source and target domains are similar but not identical.However, the method still faces two key challenges in practical application: first, the similarity assessment criteria between instances and target domains have not been fully established; second, the technical challenge of how filtered instances can be effectively integrated into the target-domain learning framework has not yet been completely solved40,41. These constraints limit the effectiveness of instance-based migration methods in complex scenarios, and related optimization schemes still need to be further explored and investigated.

In the field of speech emotion recognition, many critical issues have still not been fully solved, especially in multimodal data fusion and cross-domain transfer learning. First, the effective fusion mechanism of multimodal information has not been fully established, which makes the comprehensive capture of emotion cues difficult to realize. Second, the problem of distributional differences among heterogeneous data from multiple sources has not been properly addressed, which directly affects the effectiveness of migration effects. In addition, more efficient transfer learning strategies have yet to be devised to enhance the generalization ability of the model.

Based on the above research gaps, the innovative approach of this paper is proposed, and its main contributions are reflected in 1) a new multimodal fusion framework is constructed; 2) an optimization algorithm for cross-domain distributional differences is developed; and 3) an efficient transfer learning strategy is designed. Through these technological breakthroughs, the research ideas in the field of speech emotion recognition are expanded and a new methodological system is established.

Deep cross-modal emotional memory network (DCM-EMNet)

Network architecture overview

DCM-EMNet is proposed as an innovative deep cross-modal emotional memory network with the model architecture shown in Fig. 5. In this architecture, advanced methods such as multilevel feature extraction and fusion techniques, dynamic affective memory mechanisms, and cross-modal consistency constraints are systematically integrated.Specifically, the raw speech signal is first processed by a CNN, and low-level acoustic features are thus extracted. Subsequently, high-level semantic and emotional features are further acquired through the application of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) and its variants including LSTM and GRU. These multi-level features are subsequently effectively fused in the dynamic emotional memory module, where not only the intrinsic connections between different modalities are captured, but also the dynamic updating process of human emotional memory is modeled.In particular, the introduction of cross-modal consistency constraints allows the model to be guided to learn feature representations with inter-modal consistency that reflect the intrinsic consistency of different modal data in expressing the same emotion. Based on this comprehensive design, the accuracy and robustness of the model in multimodal emotion recognition tasks are significantly improved.Fig. 5DCM-EMNet network structure diagram.

DCM-EMNet network structure diagram.

Multi-level feature integration

The full utilization of multimodal information is achieved by a multilevel feature fusion strategy, which is adopted by DCM-EMNet. In the feature extraction stage, low-level acoustic features are extracted from the speech signal, and this process is accomplished by CNNs. CNNs have been established as the core technology for extracting low-level acoustic features due to their powerful feature extraction capabilities. This section details how these features are efficiently extracted from speech signals by CNNs.First, a series of preprocessing steps need to be performed. These steps include: background noise is removed by the noise reduction process, continuous signals are cut into short time segments (about 20ms per frame) by the frame splitting process, and window functions such as the Hamming window are applied to reduce the discontinuities at the edges of the frames. With these preprocessing steps, the signal quality is optimized, thus making it more suitable as an input to the CNN.Subsequently, the preprocessed speech signal is transformed into a Mel spectrogram. The Mel Spectrogram is able to visualize the frequency and energy distribution of the speech signal and is therefore selected as the preferred input format for CNN processing of speech signals.

A CNN model consisting of 5 convolutional layers is employed in this study. Each convolutional layer is configured with 64 convolutional kernels whose dimensions are set to 5 × 5. In our model, the LSTM processes the frame-level acoustic features extracted by the preceding CNN. As the sequence unfolds, the network accumulates emotional cues—such as prosody, rhythm, and stress captured in the gating dynamics—to build a high-level semantic representation. This representation encapsulates both the linguistic content and the paralinguistic emotional state of the speaker, providing rich, contextualized information for the final emotion classification.

To enable more flexible fusion of features extracted by CNNs and LSTM networks, we introduce an attention mechanism. This mechanism dynamically assigns distinct attention weights to CNN-derived and LSTM-derived features based on current task requirements, thereby highlighting critical features and suppressing irrelevant ones. Through this attention mechanism, the model learns to more effectively integrate the shallow acoustic features from CNNs and the deep semantic features from LSTMs, ultimately enhancing overall performance.

In SER, LSTMs process speech signals frame by frame: each frame’s acoustic features serve as input, and emotional information is incrementally accumulated via their internal memory units. As the speech sequence progresses, LSTMs gradually construct high-level semantic representations that not only encode the textual content of speech but also implicitly capture the speaker’s emotional state. For instance, after capturing acoustic features such as intonation, speech rate, and stress, the speaker’s emotional tendency (e.g., happiness, sadness, anger) can be effectively inferred. Additionally, LSTMs extract fine-grained emotional features (e.g., sentiment intensity)—a capability particularly critical for fine-grained sentiment analysis tasks. Through sequential processing, LSTMs fully unveil the high-level semantic and emotional features in speech signals, providing comprehensive information for subsequent sentiment classification or regression tasks.

As illustrated in Fig. 6, the multi-level feature fusion process of speech signals is clearly visualized: CNNs extract shallow-level features of the speech signal, while LSTMs extract deep-level features. The attention mechanism then facilitates the adaptive fusion of these heterogeneous features, ensuring the model prioritizes information that contributes most to emotion recognition.Fig. 6Multi-level feature fusion process of speech signal.

Cross-modal consistency constraints

After the multilevel features have been successfully extracted, to further enhance the accuracy of emotion recognition, these features can be cross-modally fused with the features extracted from the text content. As shown in Fig. 7, cross-modal fusion aims to achieve a more comprehensive capture of emotional expression through the comprehensive utilization of different modal information. Adaptive multi-source heterogeneous transfer learning framework(AMS-HTLF)

Overview of the framework

AMS-HTLF, as an innovative adaptive multi-source heterogeneous transfer learning framework, is designed to integrate multiple functional modules such as adaptive feature alignment, heterogeneous label mapping, and multi-task learning mechanism. Through this framework, heterogeneous data from multiple different source domains can be effectively migrated, thus the generalization ability of the model on the target domain is significantly improved.As shown in Fig. 8, in the AMS-HTLF framework, the source domain features are first projected to the target domain feature space through the adaptive feature alignment module to effectively reduce the distributional differences; subsequently, the source domain labels are converted to the target domain label space through the heterogeneous label mapping module to ensure that the label consistency can be maintained; ultimately, through the introduction of the multi-task learning mechanism, the auxiliary tasks are synchronously executed in the migration and learning process. Finally, through the introduction of the multi-task learning mechanism, the learning of auxiliary tasks during the transfer learning process is executed synchronously, and the generalization performance of the model is further optimized.Fig. 8Block diagram of AMS-HTLF framework for speech emotion classification.

Adaptive feature alignment

In the SER task, to mitigate distributional discrepancies between the source domains (i.e., diverse speech emotion datasets) and the target domain (i.e., the speech emotion scenario to be recognized), we propose an Adaptive Multi-source Transfer Learning Framework (AMS-TLF). Within this framework, a dedicated Adaptive Feature Alignment Module (AFAM) is designed to dynamically adjust the correspondence of source domain features. By learning a feature transformation matrix, AFAM projects source domain features into the target domain feature space, thereby reducing distributional differences and improving recognition performance.

In AFAM, the Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence is adopted as a key metric to quantify the discrepancy between feature distributions of the source and target domains. As an effective symmetric metric, JS divergence smooths and reflects the difference between two probability distributions. To model feature data as probability distributions, we apply the Gaussian Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) method to approximate the probabilistic distribution of features.

Specifically, let the source domain feature set be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{X}}_{s}\in {\mathrm{R}}^{{n}_{s}\times {d}_{s}}$$\end{document},where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${n}_{s}$$\end{document} denotes the number of samples in the source domain and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}_{s}$$\end{document} denotes the source domain feature dimension. The target domain feature set is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{X}}_{T}\in {R}^{{n}_{t}\times {d}_{t}}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${n}_{t}$$\end{document} is the number of target domain samples and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${n}_{t}$$\end{document} is the target domain feature dimension. To map source domain features to the target domain feature space, we introduce a feature transformation matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf{W}\in {R}^{{d}_{t}\times {d}_{s}}$$\end{document}. The optimal W is learned by minimizing the JS divergence between the transformed source domain features and the target domain features, formulated as:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{min}\_\mathbf{W}\mathrm{JS}({\mathrm{P}}_{\mathrm{SW}}\parallel {\mathrm{P}}_{\mathrm{T}})$$\end{document}

We solve this objective using gradient-based optimization. The gradients of the JS divergence with respect to the transformation matrix W are computed, enabling the model to iteratively adjust W to reduce the distributional discrepancy.

Heterogeneous label mapping

In the Adaptive Multi-Source Heterogeneous Transfer Learning Framework (AMS-HTLF), a Heterogeneous Label Mapping Module (HLMM) is introduced in order to solve the problem of label space inconsistency between the source and target domains. The core task of this module is set as the learning of mapping functions from source domain labels to target domain labels to ensure that the target domain data can be accurately classified.To achieve this goal, a label embedding space is constructed by the HLMM, through which the intrinsic connections between source and target domain labels are captured. Specifically, tag semantic information is first extracted and subsequently projected into the shared embedding space through a nonlinear transformation. In this process, a similarity metric between source and target domain labels is employed, thus ensuring that semantically similar labels are mapped to similar regions in the embedding space.

Due to the possible inconsistency in the label space of the source and target domains, i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Y}_{s}\ne {Y}_{T}$$\end{document}, the operation of direct label mapping proves to be more difficult.

To address this problem, a label embedding space is constructed by a heterogeneous label mapping module. In this space, each label in the source and target domains is learned to obtain the corresponding embedding vector representation. Within the label embedding space, an intrinsic connection between the embedding vectors of the labels in the source domain and the embedding vectors of the labels in the target domain is expected to be established. This potential connection can be efficiently captured by the learning process of the mapping function .Specifically, the design objective of the mapping function f is determined as mapping the embedding vectors of the source domain labels to the neighboring regions of the embedding vectors of the target domain labels, thus ensuring that accurate classification of the target domain data is achieved. In this process, the label semantic similarity metric is employed and the distance of semantically similar labels in the embedding space is minimized. Meanwhile, cross-domain label alignment loss function is introduced to supervise the optimization process of the mapping function.

Experimental validation and performance evaluation

Datasets

To comprehensively evaluate the performance and generalization capability of the proposed method, we conduct experiments on three benchmark speech emotion recognition datasets: IEMOCAP42, CREMA-D43, and RAVDESS44. The inclusion of multiple datasets, which vary in collection methodology, emotional categories, and acoustic properties, allows for a more robust assessment of our model’s effectiveness and its ability to adapt to diverse speech characteristics.

The IEMOCAP dataset was recorded by the Interactive Multimedia Laboratory at the University of Southern California and contains 10 sessions, each consisting of a dialog between two actors. Rich emotion labels were labeled in the dataset, including basic emotions such as happiness, sadness, anger, and neutrality, as well as several compound emotions. To ensure that the experiments are focused and comparable, multiple emotions were selected for the recognition task from each dataset. The CREMA-D dataset was recorded by the Laboratory for Emotion and Intelligence at Columbia University and contains 7441 video clips. Six emotions (happiness, sadness, anger, fear, disgust, and surprise) were labeled in each clip. Compared to IEMOCAP, the CREMA-D dataset focuses more on the recognition of subtle emotional expressions.

The RAVDESS (Ryerson Audio-Visual Database of Emotional Speech and Song) dataset is a high-quality, multimodal dataset featuring 24 professional actors (12 female, 12 male). They vocalize two lexically-matched statements in a neutral North American accent, producing expressions across eight emotions (neutral, calm, happy, sad, angry, fearful, surprise, and disgust), each at two levels of emotional intensity (normal and strong). For this study, we utilize the speech-only modality to evaluate the model’s performance across a range of prototypical emotions. The dataset’s high-fidelity recordings and controlled, factorial design make it particularly valuable for rigorously testing a model’s ability to recognize prototypical emotions.

Conclusion

The dynamic affective memory bank enhanced the model’s capacity to capture the temporal dynamics of emotional states. Furthermore, the adaptive multi-source transfer framework substantially improved the model’s adaptability and robustness in cross-domain scenarios through its feature alignment and label mapping strategies.This research provides a new methodological framework for multimodal fusion and cross-domain transfer in SER. The dynamic affective memory bank enhanced the model’s capacity to capture the temporal dynamics of emotional states. Furthermore, the adaptive multi-source transfer framework substantially improved the model’s adaptability and robustness in cross-domain scenarios through its feature alignment and label mapping strategies.

Outlook

The proposed cross-modal affective memory and adaptive transfer learning framework opens up several promising research avenues. Future work will focus on integrating additional modalities, such as physiological signals (EEG, ECG), to construct a more comprehensive and robust affective computing system. Exploring the cognitive and neurological foundations of our affective memory model could lead to more empathetic and human-like AI for human–computer interaction. In practical terms, this technology holds significant potential for clinical assistive devices, such as tools for continuous emotional monitoring in mental healthcare or for aiding neurodivergent individuals in social interaction. Further applications can be extended to auditory rehabilitation training, cross-cultural language education, and developing accent-invariant tutoring systems.
