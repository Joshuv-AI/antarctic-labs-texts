Monitoring Variant Allele Fraction in VEXAS Syndrome: A Comparison of Digital PCR and Next‐Generation Sequencing
Authors: Alba Exposito‐Bey, Marco Antonio Montes‐Cano, Salvador Payán‐Pernía, José Raúl García‐Lozano, María Francisca González‐Escribano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundVEXAS syndrome is an adult‐onset, X‐linked autoinflammatory disorder resulting from somatic variations in the UBA1 gene.AimTo evaluate the adequacy of the digital PCR (dPCR) to follow up the variant allele frequency (VAF) on response to the treatment in patients with VEXAS.Patient and MethodsThis study is a 1‐year follow‐up of a 76‐year‐old male VEXAS patient treated with azacitidine. The VAF of UBA1 was serially quantified using next‐generation sequencing (NGS) and dPCR.ResultsThere was a high concordance between the two methods. Azacitidine treatment results in a progressive clinical improvement, normalisation of haematologic parameters and a marked reduction in VAF.ConclusionThese findings support dPCR as a sensitive and easy‐to‐manage tool for monitoring therapeutic responses in VEXAS syndrome, providing a method with a quick and cost‐effective response.Trial RegistrationThe authors have confirmed clinical trial registration is not needed for this submissionKeywords: digital PCR, next‐generation sequencing, somatic variants, UBA1 gene variants, VEXAS syndrome

Introduction

VEXAS syndrome (vacuoles, enzyme E1, X‐linked, autoinflammatory, somatic) is a recently identified adult‐onset, X‐linked recessive autoinflammatory disorder [1]. Somatic mutations in the UBA1 gene, which encodes the E1 enzyme essential for ubiquitination in the proteasome degradative pathway, cause this disease [2].

Patients present with clinical manifestations that bridge rheumatologic and haematologic conditions, not fitting into traditional classifications of immune‐mediated diseases and highlighting the importance of genetic analysis in diagnosis [3]. Current treatments aim to block cytokine‐driven inflammation or eliminate the affected haematopoietic clone, with hypomethylating agents primarily used in cases associated with myelodysplastic syndrome [4]. Although there is no biomarker of response to treatment, previous studies have followed haematological parameters, such as platelet and haemoglobin concentration or mean corpuscular volume, to assess the improvement of thrombocytopenia and macrocytic anaemia. Additionally, inflammatory parameters, such as C‐reactive protein, have been considered [5].

Given the complexity of VEXAS syndrome treatment, it is essential to advance the development of biomarkers that facilitate the monitoring of therapeutic response. In this sense, a suggested follow‐up biomarker is the quantification of the variant allele fraction (VAF) [6]. Indeed, some studies have reported cases of complete molecular remission in patients with non‐detection of the mutated fraction of the allele [7]. In these studies, next‐generation sequencing (NGS) is the approach used in the quantification.

Here, we describe VAF follow‐up in a patient with VEXAS syndrome treated with azacitidine by comparing NGS with digital PCR (dPCR) as a cost‐effective alternative.

Patient and Methods

Patient

The propositus is a 76‐year‐old male patient referred to the haematology department for macrocytic anaemia (haemoglobin, Hb: 11.1 g/dL; mean corpuscular volume, MCV: 115 fL). For 2 years, he had recurrent symptoms consistent with an uncharacterised autoinflammatory syndrome, with persistently elevated acute‐phase reactants: arthritis, myalgia, erythematous papules/plaques, low‐grade fever/fever and asthenia. He was on methotrexate and a variable dose of prednisone, to which symptoms responded when increased (dose ranging from 5 to 15 mg/day). MCV had progressively increased over the past years and was already elevated (without anaemia) at the onset of the clinical syndrome. Bone marrow aspiration revealed vacuolisation in granulocytic and erythroid progenitors with no other abnormalities. Cytogenetic and molecular analysis (Oncomine Myeloid Research Assay, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) revealed no alterations. Suspecting VEXAS, Sanger sequencing revealed the most prevalent UBA1 VEXAS variant, c.122T>C (p.Met42Thr).

The patient remained stable for a year post‐diagnosis but developed progressive anaemia requiring transfusions as well as a mild thrombocytopenia (> 100/mm3). Bone marrow reassessment revealed no myelodysplastic syndrome. Other causes of anaemia were unidentified, and the worsening could not be attributed to increased inflammation, as both inflammatory manifestations and acute‐phase reactants had remained stable. High‐dose epoetin was ineffective, and an increase in VAF was detected compared to diagnosis. The decision made was to initiate treatment with azacytidine to reduce the clone.

Sample Collection and DNA Extraction

Blood samples were collected in EDTA tubes at diagnosis (February 2022) and before each azacitidine cycle (14 from January 2024 to April 2025). To extract genomic DNA, we used the QIamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Barcelona, Spain), following the manufacturer's instructions. To check DNA concentration and quality, we used a NanoDrop spectrophotometer.

Sanger Sequencing

At diagnosis, Exon 3 of UBA1 was analysed by Sanger sequencing using primers generated with Thermo Fisher NGS Sanger Confirmation Software on Ion Reporter, and the BigDye Direct Cycle Sequencing Kit, following the manufacturer's instructions [8].

Next‐Generation Sequencing

DNA samples were quantified in a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer and diluted to 0.67 ng/µL. We used a custom‐designed primer system (AmpliSeq software, Ion Torrent, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) targeting coding regions and flanking intronic sequences of the UBA1 gene (NM_003334) in the NGS. The panel included 37 amplicons, with 100% coverage and an average of 1000 reads. In variant calling, we used the 5.0 version of the Ion Reporter software, which implemented the somatic mutation detection and quantification pipeline. The human genome, hg19, was used as a reference to align the readings. Variants reported by Ion Reporter were verified and filtered by visual inspection to check missense variants and to perform a confirmation of the reading alignments using the Integrative Genomics Viewer IGV v. 2.8.13, Broad Institute.

dPCR

For each sample, the reaction mix prepared contained 8 µL of DNA (20 ng/µL), 2 µL of Absolute Q DNA dPCR Master Mix (5X) (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 0.25 µL of TaqMan primer/probe mix (Applied Biosystems). We used the Custom TaqMan Assay Design Tool to design the primers and probes. One probe targeted the wild‐type gene (labelled with VIC), and another probe targeted c.122T>C (labelled with FAM). For amplification, we used a total of 9 µL of each sample reaction mix and 15 µL of QuantStudio Absolute Q Isolation Buffer (Applied Biosystems) loaded in a cartridge QuantStudio Absolute Q MAP16 Digital PCR Kit 12‐pack (Applied Biosystems), running in a QuantStudio Absolute Q (Applied Biosystems) with the following schedule: 96°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 96°C for 5 s and 60°C for 15 s. Absolute VAF quantification of each sample was calculated from the number of positive counts per panel using the Poisson distribution, as previously described [9]. To report VAF, we used the ratio of mutated molecules out of the total as a percentage (copies/µL) (Figure S1). Based on previous validations for similar assays, the theoretical limit of detection (LOD) was set at 0.1%, allowing for the reliable quantification of low‐frequency variants [10].

Statistics

We used two parameters to evaluate the correspondence between the NGS and the dPCR. First, the correlation between the observed and expected values using simple linear regression, calculating the coefficient of determination R 2, and second, comparison of the two measurements with a Bland–Altman plot or difference plot. In all statistical tests, we used the GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Clinical Response to Azacitidine

Figure 1 displays the evolution of different haematological parameters with the treatment. The anaemia, detected at diagnosis, was resolved with red blood cell transfusion. Macrocytosis persisted, accompanied by a decrease in the mean MCV. Neutrophil and platelet counts reached a nadir after June (67,000/mm3 and 0.78 × 10⁹/L, respectively), likely due to toxicity, but subsequently improved. Systemic manifestations also meliorate, allowing methotrexate and prednisone tapering after June (to 5 mg/week and 5 mg/day, respectively, at the time of data analysis). Given the favourable response and patient preference, the azacitidine dose was reduced after the eighth cycle (from 7 to 5 days) with a cycle extension to 35 days, thereby maintaining clinical and VAF stability.

VAF Monitoring Using NGS

Samples obtained from the patient at diagnosis, as well as before each cycle of azacitidine, were used to assess VAF using NGS until October (Table S1). A peak preceded treatment initiation (correlating with clinical worsening), followed by a decline across treatment cycles until reaching a plateau between June and July (Figure 2C). These findings suggest a dynamic response to treatment, where the initial steep reduction in VAF correlates with clinical improvement; nevertheless, the subsequent plateau indicates that further response is required to achieve a complete molecular remission.

Comparison of NGS and dPCR

We performed serial dilutions mixing DNA from one male patient with the same variant as the propositus and with 85% VAF by NGS with DNA from another male without the mutation, comparing the expected values with those observed by NGS and dPCR (Table S2). Calibration curves showed excellent correlation (R 2 = 0.9916 for NGS, R 2 = 0.9996 for dPCR, Figure 2A). Samples assessed by NGS during treatment were also analysed by dPCR, obtaining similar results (Table S3, Figure 2C,D). Due to this, only dPCR was performed from October 2024 to April 2025. In addition to this high correlation, the Bland–Altman plot (Figure 2B) revealed a non‐significant bias and acceptable limits of agreement between both methods.

Discussion

In the present study, we suggest dPCR as an interesting approach to follow up the VAF response to the treatment in patients with VEXAS.

Our study demonstrated a perfect correlation between dPCR and NGS, the approach used in most previous studies, in the VAF quantification. The dPCR assay has a sensitivity similar to NGS, with a simplified design and management; dPCR is less time‐consuming for processing samples and has a lower cost than NGS. dPCR is not adequate for diagnosis, a situation requiring an approach providing a broad mutational profile. Nevertheless, it is satisfactory in the follow‐up when we can design the specific dPCR probes after identifying the particular mutation by NGS. Droplet Digital PCR (ddPCR) has been introduced lately in monitoring [11]. However, the chip‐based technology of dPCR has the advantage of a more homogeneous and controlled distribution of the sample in the chip, making the quantification process more robust and faster [12].

Since the discovery of VEXAS syndrome, many studies have described cases worldwide. Finding an effective treatment to manage patients appropriately is crucial, so having tools that help us monitor the effectiveness of the chosen drug during the process is of great importance. In our patient, the increase in VAF, coinciding with the development of transfusion‐dependent anaemia, was a key factor in initiating azacitidine treatment to reduce the clone, even in the absence of myelodysplastic syndrome, both in controlling clinical symptoms and in reducing the VAF, a marker of treatment effectiveness, which correlated with progressive clinical improvement in our patient. Subsequently, VAF reduction paralleled clinical and laboratory improvement, reinforcing azacitidine's therapeutic role. After plateau and reduction of dosage, the VAF remained stable. This case, as previous studies, illustrates how VAF analysis can inform treatment decisions and monitoring.

In conclusion, dPCR is a reliable method for monitoring VAF response in VEXAS patients and may serve as a cost‐effective alternative to NGS in treatment follow‐up.
