Shear-Sensing by C-Reactive Protein: Linking Aortic Stenosis and Inflammation

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:CRP (C-reactive protein) is a prototypical acute phase reactant. Upon dissociation of the pentameric isoform (pCRP [pentameric CRP]) into its monomeric subunits (mCRP [monomeric CRP]), it exhibits prothrombotic and proinflammatory activity. Pathophysiological shear rates as observed in aortic valve stenosis (AS) can influence protein conformation and function as observed with vWF (von Willebrand factor). Given the proinflammatory function of dissociated CRP and the important role of inflammation in the pathogenesis of AS, we investigated whether shear stress can modify CRP conformation and induce inflammatory effects relevant to AS.METHODS:To determine the effects of pathological shear rates on the function of human CRP, pCRP was subjected to pathophysiologically relevant shear rates and analyzed using biophysical and biochemical methods. To investigate the effect of shear on CRP conformation in vivo, we used a mouse model of arterial stenosis. Levels of mCRP and pCRP were measured in patients with severe AS pre- and post-transcatheter aortic valve implantation, and the presence of CRP was investigated on excised valves from patients undergoing aortic valve replacement surgery for severe AS. Microfluidic models of AS were then used to recapitulate the shear rates of patients with AS and to investigate this shear-dependent dissociation of pCRP and its inflammatory function.RESULTS:Exposed to high shear rates, pCRP dissociates into its proinflammatory monomers (mCRP) and aggregates into large particles. Our in vitro findings were further confirmed in a mouse carotid artery stenosis model, where the administration of human pCRP led to the deposition of mCRP poststenosis. Patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation demonstrated significantly higher mCRP bound to circulating microvesicles pre-transcatheter aortic valve implantation compared with post-transcatheter aortic valve implantation. Excised human stenotic aortic valves display mCRP deposition. pCRP dissociated in a microfluidic model of AS and induces endothelial cell activation as measured by increased ICAM-1 (intercellular adhesion molecule 1) and P-selectin expression. mCRP also induces platelet activation and TGF-β (transforming growth factor beta) expression on platelets.CONCLUSIONS:We identify a novel mechanism of shear-induced pCRP dissociation, which results in the activation of cells central to the development of AS. This novel mechanosensing mechanism of pCRP dissociation to mCRP is likely also relevant to other pathologies involving increased shear rates, such as in atherosclerotic and injured arteries.

The development of AS is linked to inflammation and ultimately leads to significant hemodynamic disturbance through the stenotic valve. An emerging body of data now demonstrates that shear stress regulates endothelial and immune cell function. However, whether shear stress alters the conformation of circulating proteins that have inflammatory function has not been explored. Here, we present CRP as a mechanosensitive protein, which undergoes dissociation in response to pathological shear rates as seen in AS. Pathological shear rates dissociate CRP from a pentameric form (pCRP [pentameric C-reactive protein]) to a monomeric form (mCRP [monomeric C-reactive protein]), with mCRP deposited on the valves of patients with AS. Patients with severe AS exhibited elevated levels of mCRP on circulating microvesicles, comparing blood taken before and after transcatheter aortic valve implantation. Functionally, mCRP possesses important proinflammatory and prothrombotic properties, which results in an upregulation of ICAM-1 (intercellular adhesion molecule 1) and P-selectin expression on endothelial cells and TGF-β secretion from activated platelets. These findings present a novel mechanosensing mechanism of CRP and link altered shear stress and inflammation, both important mediators of AS pathogenesis.

Shear stress plays an integral role in regulating multiple aspects of cardiovascular physiology, for example, endothelial, platelet, and leukocyte function.1,2 This biomechanical factor mediates physiological processes, including hemostasis and vascular development, and has been shown to influence the development of pathological conditions such as atherothrombosis.3,4 Critically, shear stress can also regulate protein structure and function. In this regard, a large body of evidence demonstrates that the important plasma protein vWF (von Willebrand factor) responds to changes in shear, which, in turn, regulates its hemostatic function and defines vWF as a mechanosensitive protein.5,6 Under pathological shear stress, as observed in patients with aortic valve stenosis (AS), the high shear stress environment facilitates the premature proteolysis of vWF and leads to the selective loss of high molecular vWF multimers, thus leading to acquired von Willebrand disease with associated multiple bleeding problems.7,8

Akin to vWF, the acute phase protein CRP (C-reactive protein) also exhibits a shift in its biological function as a consequence of conformational change. The pentameric protein (pCRP [pentameric CRP]) harbors strong proinflammatory and prothrombotic potential; however, these are only unveiled after conformational change. Initially, the intersubunit interactions weaken (pCRP*), and the pentamer begins to dissociate, exposing proinflammatory linear neoepitopes normally buried at the monomer-monomer interface. Then, exposed neoepitopes enable the interaction with complement C1q (complement component 1q) and Fc-gamma receptors.9–11 Recent in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo studies revealed several biological mechanisms underlying this conformational change. Typically, the process occurs after the interaction of pCRP with phosphocholine head groups exposed on inflamed cell surfaces, and the deposition of mCRP (monomeric CRP) generated through this process accelerates localized inflammation.10–13

Importantly, a large body of data now exists highlighting the effects of shear stress on mediating endothelial inflammation.1 In addition, recent data from our group have demonstrated that the elevated shear stress associated with AS activates monocytes via the mechanosensitive ion channel PIEZO1.2 Interestingly, the development of AS is marked by endothelial injury and subsequent inflammatory response, followed by fibrosis and calcification.14

Given the significant body of data highlighting inflammation as a driver of AS pathogenesis in conjunction with the observation that CRP levels are elevated in patients with AS and have been shown to be deposited on valves from patients with AS,15 we hypothesized that shear stress occurring at a stenosed aortic valve could alter the conformation of CRP, thereby unmasking its proinflammatory function and contribute to the development of AS.

Utilizing a model of extensional flow in conjunction with biophysical and biochemical methods, we demonstrate that pCRP dissociates into its monomeric isoform. We translate our in vitro findings into a relevant in vivo model of arterial stenosis in mice. Next, we confirmed our findings in human ex vivo samples by demonstrating a significant reduction in circulating mCRP after removal of AS-induced high shear stress by transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) and immunohistological detection of dissociated CRP on stenotic valves. Finally, we validated our findings with a microfluidic model, which recapitulates the peak shear rates of severe AS and demonstrates that shear-dissociated CRP induces the upregulation of endothelial adhesion molecules and the activation of platelets and their TGF-β expression; the latter is a potential contributor to AS.2 Thus, we report a novel mechanosensing mechanism of shear-induced CRP dissociation, which facilitates thromboinflammatory reactions important to the development of AS and likely relevant to a broad spectrum of cardiovascular diseases and vessel injuries in general.

Results

Shear Stress Induces Dissociation of Human pCRP In Vitro

To directly investigate the effects of shear stress on CRP conformation, human pCRP was subjected to shear in an extensional flow model, as previously described.16 This extensional flow apparatus was constructed by connecting 2 1-mL gas-tight syringes over a capillary tube, producing a defined increase in velocity and a sharp contraction at the transition point (Figure 1A and 1B). Using computational fluid dynamics simulations (see details in Table S1), we first visualized the flow streamlines inside the extensional flow apparatus and calculated the shear rate exerted on a CRP solution. Abrupt contraction at the interface of the infusing syringe and capillary tube led to the convergence of flow streamlines and an increase in flow velocity inside the capillary tube (Figure 1C, left). In contrast, the sudden expansion at the interface of the capillary tube and the withdrawing syringe led to the formation of a large vortex inside the withdrawing syringe (Figure 1C, right). The flow velocity profile was parabolic inside the capillary tube with a maximum velocity at the centerline of the tube. The shear rate was proportional to the distance from the centerline of the capillary tube, reaching a peak close to the walls of the tube. The shear rate profile remained steady along the length of the capillary tube (Figure 1D) and was proportional to the flow rate of the CRP solution. This allowed us to generate customized shear rate values by simply adjusting the plunger velocity. The CRP solution moving close to the walls of the capillary tube (r=0.17 mm) experienced a peak shear rate of 29 600 s−1 when setting the plunger velocity to 8 mm s−1 (mean shear rate, 19 700 s−1), corresponding to a flow rate of 8 mL min−1 (Figure 1D).

The pCRP protein solution was stressed for 200 passes with the protein conformation subsequently analyzed using native sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), which allowed the distinction between pCRP and mCRP.17 Unsheared pCRP served as the control (red arrowhead, Figure 2A, second lane). In contrast to the unsheared protein, sheared pCRP samples contained an additional isoform of CRP running at ≈23 kDa (black arrowhead, Figure 2A, third lane), corresponding to the molecular weight of the CRP monomer (mCRP control; Figure 2A, sixth lane). The 2 CRP entities were separable by centrifugation at 22 000 g. Strikingly, after centrifugation, sheared pCRP samples, but not unsheared pCRP, formed visible pellets (not shown), indicating a decrease in aqueous solubility of the protein, as previously described for disordered mCRP.18 To further characterize the conformation of the CRP in the supernatant and pellet, samples were analyzed using SDS-PAGE (Figure 2A, lanes 4 and 5) and Western blot, probing with the mCRP conformation-specific antibody 8C10 (Figure 2B; control mCRP is shown in Figure 2D, second lane).19 The protein size from the supernatant of the sheared pCRP was consistent with the pCRP control, while the protein size from the resuspended pellet was consistent with the mCRP control. Importantly, the band at 23 kDa from the CRP samples subjected to shear stress was detected by the mCRP-specific antibody clone 8C10 (Figure 2B).

Dissociation of pCRP Correlates With Exposure Time to Shear Stress

To verify the observations in greater detail, human pCRP samples at 2.5 mg/mL were exposed to shear stress in a second experiment for different time periods (passes), that is, 50, 200, and 1000 passes (Figure 2C and 2D). In these experiments, protein samples were not centrifuged to allow for better comparability of the amounts of both pentameric and monomeric CRP. With increasing numbers of passes, the amount of protein found at 23 kDa increased proportionally (black arrowhead; Figure 2D). In contrast, the amount of protein as detected by Coomassie staining at the size of the unsheared pCRP decreased with the number of passes (red arrowhead; Figure 2C). To further exclude dissociating effects of the 1/20 SDS-PAGE, as described before,17 a dot blot assay using clone 8C10 was performed, confirming the presence of dissociated (unfolded) CRP in the sheared samples (Figure 2E).

CRP Exposed to Shear Stress Forms Hydrophobic Aggregates

We further investigated the configuration of pCRP under static conditions and under shear stress by transmission electron microscopy (Figure 2F). While pCRP contained in the flow apparatus under static conditions (pCRP control, left images) showed a pentameric configuration with a diameter of ≈10 nm,20 pCRP sheared by increasing passes showed a correlation with the formation of larger aggregates (1000 passes, right images). To verify our findings in a quantitative assay, we performed mass photometry comparing pCRP after increasing passes and a static control (Figure 2G). While the static pCRP control demonstrated a mean of 116 kDa in mass photometry, increasing passes through the flow apparatus correlated with an increased mean mass of CRP aggregates (100 passes: 681 kDa; 300 passes: 1302 kDa; 600 passes: 2013 kDa; and 1000 passes: 2153 kDa).

Biophysical Measurements Confirm Shear-Induced Conformational Change of pCRP

To further characterize the protein structure of CRP after exposure to shear stress, intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence, 8-anilino-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid (ANS) fluorescence, and dynamic light scattering were used, as previously described.16,21 We identified changes in the tertiary structure of pCRP by both intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence and ANS fluorescence after shear stress. While pCRP yielded significantly higher intensity of tryptophan emission fluorescence (λ=370 nm) compared with mCRP,21 pCRP samples exposed to shear stress demonstrated decreasing intrinsic fluorescence after shear, in direct correlation to the number of passes (Figure 3A and 3B). ANS fluorescence was used to further verify the conformational change in pCRP.21 The conformational change of pCRP to mCRP was accompanied by a significant loss in aqueous solubility.18 The fluorescent probe ANS noncovalently interacted with hydrophobic sites, increasing its fluorescence yield. Shear-dissociated pCRP increased the ANS fluorescence and, finally, reached similar emission spectra (Figure 3C) and peak values (Figure 3D) as mCRP. As demonstrated by dynamic light scattering, both the size and dispersity of pCRP solutions changed with shear stress. For pCRP, a single peak was observed (hydrodynamic radius [Rh]=12.26±0.63 nm [mean±SD]; Figure 3E). pCRP subjected to 200 passes and 1000 passes, however, showed multiple peaks, with 200 passes still expressing a peak at the size of pCRP and an additional peak at Rh=677.87±100.2 nm, while 1000 passes showed only a minor peak (≈2%) at Rh of pCRP (Figure 3E). Accordingly, the Z-average after pCRP was subjected to 200 or 1000 passes, demonstrating significant increases in particle size compared with the unsheared pCRP (Figure 3F). Correlograms of various pCRP samples initially exhibited uniform sigmoidal patterns with fast decay times, indicating consistent and small sizes (left plot, Figure 3G). However, with increasing numbers of passes of CRP, the plots became nonuniform with multiple shoulders and longer decay times, suggesting the evolution of pCRP into a mixture of larger and smaller structures (middle and right plots, Figure 3G). The polydisperse index changed accordingly from a monodisperse protein solution of 0.21±0.02 (pCRP) to 0.6±0.12 (200 passes) and 0.87±0.26 (1000 passes), indicating a broad particle size distribution.

Shear-Dissociated mCRP Does Not Bind to Phosphocholine but Binds to C1q

To assess the accessibility of the phosphocholine binding site of the CRP isoforms produced by shear, we incubated sheared pCRP, untreated pCRP, and control mCRP with immobilized PC-KLH (phosphorylcholine keyhole limpet hemocyanin), as previously described.22 As expected, native pCRP bounds to PC-KLH in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure 3H, purple curve). Likewise, the supernatant of sheared pCRP was found to bind to PC-KLH (Figure 3H, light blue curve). mCRP, however, does not express a functional phosphocholine binding site18 and, accordingly, did not bind to PC-KLH (Figure 3H, dark blue curve). The resuspended pellet of sheared CRP was found to bind only minimally to PC-KLH (Figure 3H, green curve), which was interpreted as an unfolding of the protein and significant loss of the phosphocholine binding site. We next investigated the interaction of shear-dissociated CRP with complement C1q using a sandwich ELISA.23 Although dissociation of pCRP to mCRP by shear was suggested by previous assays, we did not find significant binding activity of sheared pCRP to C1q (Figure 3I, 200 passes). We hypothesized that shear-generated mCRP would form large aggregates due to increased hydrophobicity18 and, thereby, would decrease the accessibility of C1q binding sites (Figure 3J, right). Interestingly, the anionic surfactant SDS in a concentration of 0.1% disrupted the protein aggregates into individual neoepitope-expressing CRP molecules (Figure 3I, 200 passes+SDS), thereby unmasking the binding site for C1q (Figure 3J, middle). Thus, the sheared pCRP samples containing SDS showed significantly higher C1q binding than the samples without SDS (Figure 3I, 200 passes+SDS versus 200 passes). In contrast, SDS alone in the utilized concentrations did not dissociate pCRP in the unsheared controls (pCRP+SDS versus pCRP).

Human pCRP Dissociates in an In Vivo Mouse Stenosis Model

Having demonstrated that shear stress applied in vitro can dissociate pCRP, we next investigated whether we could detect CRP dissociation using an in vivo model of arterial stenosis (Figure 4A through 4C). Briefly, an 80% stenosis of the right carotid artery was induced in mice followed by the intravenous administration of human pCRP. Longitudinal sections of the stenotic carotid arteries were then stained with conformation-specific anti-CRP antibodies. Demonstrated by staining with the mCRP conformation-specific antibody, clone 9C9, mCRP was found to be deposited directly poststenosis and bound to the vessel wall but not prestenosis (Figure 4D, red arrowheads). The blinded analysis of mCRP staining demonstrated a significant deposition in vessels with stenosis compared with those without stenosis (n=5 each; Figure 4E). The pCRP-specific antibody, clone 1D6, showed no significant staining comparing stenosed and nonstenosed arteries (Figure 4F and 4G). Together, these results support the notion that high shear stress in vivo can lead to the conformational change of pCRP to mCRP, and thus, these data confirm the in vivo relevance of our in vitro findings.

mCRP Bound to Microvesicles Correlate With Shear in Stenotic Aortic Valves

To further investigate pCRP dissociation due to high shear stress in humans, we examined whether patients with AS (Figure 5A) have higher levels of mCRP, utilizing the unique opportunity to obtain mCRP measurements in patients before and after TAVI. mCRP levels in patients with severe AS (overall cohort of 49 patients; Table S2) were analyzed at baseline (pre-TAVI) and then 1 month post-TAVI. After TAVI, an increased aortic valve area led to a reduced aortic mean pressure gradient (Figure 5B) and a reduced mean shear rate (Figure 5C), as measured 1 month post-TAVI compared with pre-TAVI. The pCRP concentration was not significantly different before and 1 month after TAVI (Figure 5D). Importantly, the high-sensitivity CRP assay measures the circulating isoform (ie, pCRP) and does not detect epitopes specific to mCRP.24 Previously, we described the detection of proinflammatory mCRP on the surface of circulating microvesicles.25 We used super-resolution microscopy to demonstrate the binding of mCRP (antibody 9C9) to microvesicles (using CD63 as a marker)26 from peripheral blood in patients with AS undergoing TAVI (representative example shown in Figure S1). For quantitative flow cytometry, microvesicles were gated based on forward and sideward scatter (Figure S2).25 The overall percentage of mCRP-positive microvesicles was significantly reduced after TAVI (Figure 5E). In line with this finding, the overall deposition of mCRP on these microvesicles was reduced after the reduction of shear stress by the TAVI procedure (Figure 5F). In contrast, we observed only a minor reduction at a low level of mean fluorescence intensity of pCRP-positive microvesicles and no significant differences in the percentage of pCRP-positive microvesicles, as measured by the binding of antibody 1D6, in patients before and after TAVI (Figure 5G and 5H).

mCRP Deposits Are Found on Stenotic Human Aortic Valves

In accordance with our flow cytometry data, human stenotic aortic valves obtained from patients with severe AS were assessed for the presence of CRP isoforms using immunohistochemistry. Slides were incubated with either conformation-specific antibodies 9C9 (mCRP) or 1D6 (pCRP; Figure 5I and 5J). The quantification of CRP staining in excised stenotic aortic valves from 8 patients demonstrated a significant deposition of mCRP, mainly at the edges of the valve cusps, but not of pCRP (Figure 5K).

Stenosis-Mimicking Microfluidic Device Confirms pCRP Dissociation Under Shear

To investigate whether the shear stress encountered in the setting of AS is sufficient to lead to CRP conformational change in the absence of cells, we utilized a microfluidic model, as previously described.2 This model has a fixed stenosis and generates shear rates similar to those observed in patients with AS. Here, pCRP was subjected to different peak shear rates representing states of patients with AS before TAVI (Figure 5L, upper) and after TAVI (lower). Importantly, we found an increase in mCRP (at 23 kDa) with increased shear rates (Figure 5M, right). In concert with the increase in mCRP, the amount of pCRP decreased with increasing shear stress consistent with the notion that increased shear rates lead to pCRP dissociation (Figure 5M, left).

Shear Stress Generates mCRP and Activates Human Valvular Endothelial Cells and Platelets

To gain insights into the biological activity of shear-dissociated CRP, we developed a microfluidic device that incorporates the microfluidic stenosis model described above, in series with a microfluidic chip containing a confluent monolayer of human aortic valve endothelial cells, as illustrated in Figure 6A. This model allowed us to directly investigate the downstream functional effects of pCRP after experiencing a high shear level akin to severe AS in the presence of valvular endothelial cells to try and recapitulate the conditions of AS in vitro. In addition, the presence of cell membranes also avoids the self-aggregation of sheared CRP. Static controls were performed with endothelial growth media supplemented with or without 50 µg/mL of either pCRP or mCRP. These studies demonstrated that pCRP induced no significant upregulation of ICAM-1 (intercellular adhesion molecule 1) or P-selectin compared with control. In contrast, mCRP produced a significant upregulation of ICAM-1 and P-selectin expression (Figure 6B through D, left graphs). To then investigate the effects of shear-dissociated CRP on endothelial cells, endothelial growth media supplemented with or without 50 µg/mL of pCRP were perfused through the microfluidic device (80% stenosis; peak shear, 5000 s−1) using a peristaltic pump continuously for 16 hours. Strikingly, sheared pCRP induced a significant increase in ICAM-1 and P-selectin expression in human aortic valve endothelial cells compared with the control group, thus demonstrating that pCRP dissociated by shear stress has immediate proinflammatory effects on endothelial cells (Figure 6B through D, right graphs).

To further investigate the relevance of dissociated CRP to the pathogenesis of AS, we investigated platelet functional responses after exposure to mCRP and pCRP. For these assays, we used recombinant mCRP because we observed the formation of large aggregates in the absence of cognate-binding partners. Platelet-rich plasma was stimulated with 50-µg/mL mCRP for 30 minutes, and PAC-1 binding and P-selectin expression were measured by flow cytometry. mCRP significantly increased both integrin activation (PAC-1 binding) and alpha granule exocytosis (P-selectin expression) compared with pCRP in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure 6E). Given that TGF-β (transforming growth factor beta) is abundant within platelet granules and has previously been described to intensify fibrosis in AS, we next investigated TGF-β expression on mCRP-activated platelets (Figure 6F).27 Accordingly, mCRP induced a significant increase in platelet TGF-β expression compared with pCRP and unstimulated control, while pCRP showed no significant effect on TGF-β expression.

Discussion

Here, we describe a novel mechanism of pCRP dissociation to mCRP by shear stress and its potential role in AS, as summarized in the following findings: (1) pCRP dissociates into its monomeric subunits under high shear rates, as demonstrated by the decreased aqueous solubility of sheared CRP and the subsequent formation of aggregates, reactivity of sheared CRP with anti-mCRP antibodies (8C10 or 9C9), and alteration of tryptophan fluorescence and ANS fluorescence of sheared CRP consistent with dissociation; (2) human pCRP dissociates in a murine model of pathological shear where we detected and quantified dissociated CRP downstream of an arterial stenosis; (3) mCRP was detected and quantified on valves from patients with AS ex vivo, and increased levels of mCRP on circulating microvesicles were detected in patients with severe AS before TAVI compared with post-TAVI, with the ability of shear stress to dissociate CRP recapitulated in a microfluidic model of AS; and (4) mCRP directly induces the upregulation of the adhesion molecules, ICAM-1 and P-selectin, on human aortic valvular endothelial cells and induces platelet activation and increased expression of platelet TGF-β.

We found pCRP to be relatively stable under physiological shear. Physiological bulk shear rates of in vivo circulating blood in healthy arteries (300–800 s−1), veins (15–200 s−1), and microvessels (450–1800 s−1) did not dissociate pCRP.28,29 However, shear rates under pathological conditions can be much higher compared with these physiological rates, and shear rates up to 5000 s−1 have been demonstrated in aortic stenoses.2 Even higher shear rates have been found at the edges of wounds in vessels; for example, median shear rates up to 25 000 s−1 were demonstrated in a mouse model of hemostasis.30 In keeping with previous data, our cohort of patients with severe AS had a mean calculated shear rate at the stenotic valve of 3527 s−1. In this patient cohort, we detected significant increases in mCRP on circulating microvesicles pre-TAVI and on the endothelium downstream of a stenotic point in a mouse model where the calculated peak shear rate of 5000 s−1 approximated the shear rates in our patient cohort. These data could not exclude the effects of inflamed membranes on the conformation of CRP, given that the binding of phosphocholine head groups on disturbed and inflamed cell membranes represents a classic mechanism for pCRP binding and dissociation. However, importantly, in our in vitro microfluidic assay, which generated a peak shear akin to severe AS, we observed a correlation between shear and pCRP dissociation in a cell-free environment, thus strengthening the argument that the principal driver of pCRP dissociation in vivo is indeed shear. Nevertheless, we cannot exclude the contributing effects of surface interactions on pCRP with our in vitro devices, despite these studies being performed with strict blocking protocols to minimize such effects.

Understanding the details of how shear stress can disrupt the pentameric CRP structure at the molecular level is complex and will require further research; however, our evidence that this disruption is occurring is strong. As we have previously demonstrated, pCRP and mCRP have different secondary structures, with mCRP significantly more disordered than pCRP.9,31 The intrinsic fluorescence of tryptophan residues in the protein is distinctly different in the CRP isoforms, and therefore, it has been utilized to monitor the dissociation of pCRP to mCRP.9,18,32 Tryptophan residues become more solvent-exposed in the fibrillary mCRP than in the packed globular conformation of pCRP, resulting in reduced fluorescence intensity in the unfolded mCRP.33 Here, we found sheared pCRP to present significantly lower intrinsic fluorescence than unsheared pCRP, supporting the evidence for conversion of pCRP to mCRP under shear stress. The hydrophobic fluorescent probe ANS is frequently used to identify conformation changes in proteins through its ability to bind to hydrophobic regions,34 causing a change in quantum yield and a blue shift in emission maxima.35 Hydrophobic regions in mCRP were demonstrated to bind to ANS more readily than those of pCRP.21 ANS was used here to measure changes in the tertiary structure of CRP under shear stress and again demonstrated that dissociation of the pCRP tertiary structure was occurring. Analysis of the particle size produced when pCRP was subjected to high shear was performed using dynamic light scattering and indicated that a broad range of high molecular weight CRP aggregates were formed. Taken together, our in vitro data are consistent with stable, properly folded pCRP dissociating to mCRP under shear stress and the unfolded mCRP subsequently forming large aggregates.

Our finding that shear stress can dissociate pCRP prompts the interesting question of how this may contribute to the pathogenesis of AS. We, and others, have previously demonstrated that while pCRP does not exhibit significant proinflammatory properties, mCRP displays potent prothrombotic and proinflammatory activities.12 There is growing evidence that the development of AS is driven by a chronic inflammatory response, which is initiated by endothelial inflammation, propagated by the infiltration of immune cells, and ultimately results in fibrosis and calcification of the valve leaflets. Importantly, we demonstrate that shear-dissociated CRP directly upregulates the adhesion molecules ICAM-1 and P-selectin, which are critical to facilitating immune cell recruitment. Indeed, ICAM-1 has been demonstrated to be present on stenotic aortic valves ex vivo, and soluble ICAM-1 levels have been demonstrated to be increased in patients with AS and have, thus, been postulated to be an important mediator of AS.36,37 In addition to endothelial cells, platelets are increasingly recognized as an important arm of the innate immune system and have been demonstrated to be central to the pathobiology of AS.38,39 Therefore, we investigated the effects of mCRP on platelet function. These studies demonstrated that mCRP induces important aspects of platelet activation linked to the development of AS, namely, the expression of platelet TGF-β. Elevated levels of TGF-β have recently been demonstrated to induce valvular endothelial Smad 2 signaling, which induces their transformation to collagen-producing myofibroblasts and, thus, increased fibrosis and accelerated AS progression in mice.38

The present study focused on patients with severe AS; however, in light of our findings, future studies are required to investigate how shear-dissociated CRP may play a role in the progression of AS, especially at the early stages of AS. Previous studies have demonstrated that patients with less severe forms of AS still display significant flow disturbance through the stenotic aortic valve.31 Moreover, previous data have demonstrated that patients with AS who have the most significant hemodynamic disturbance exhibit a faster progression of AS and also linked CRP as a biomarker in those who demonstrate more rapid AS progression.40 Thus, we postulate that the described mechanosensing mechanism of shear-induced dissociation of pCRP initiates a positive feedback loop, whereby AS progression is accelerated, which begets further effects on CRP. However, future studies will be required to investigate this further and demonstrate a direct role of CRP in the progression of AS. Moreover, how CRP may modulate other cell types, such as monocytes, which contribute to AS, as shown by us previously,2 requires further investigation.

In summary, we have demonstrated a novel mechanosensing mechanism of shear-induced pCRP dissociation exerting proinflammatory effects with relevance to AS and a potential role in the inflammation underlying subsequent disease progression.
