Bioinformatic insights into sugar signaling pathways in sugarcane growth
Authors: Lauana Pereira de Oliveira, João Pedro de Jesus Pereira, Bruno Viana Navarro, Marina C. M. Martins, Diego Mauricio Riaño-Pachón, Marcos Silveira Buckeridge
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The SnRK1, hexokinase, and TORC1 (TOR, LST8, RAPTOR) are three pivotal kinases at the core of sugar level sensing, significantly impacting plant metabolism and development. We retrieved and analyzed protein sequences of these three kinase pathways from seven sugarcane transcriptome and genome datasets, identifying protein domains, phylogenetic relationships, sequence ancestry, and in silico expression levels. Additionally, we predicted HXK subcellular localization and assessed its enzymatic activity in sugarcane leaves and culms along development in the field. We retrieved 11 TOR, 23 RAPTOR, 55 LST8, 95 SnRK1α, 98 HXK, and 14 HXK-like putative full-length sequences containing all the conserved domains. Most of these transcripts seem to share a common origin with the three ancestral species of sugarcane: Saccharum officinarum, Saccharum spontaneum, and Saccharum barberi. We accessed the expression profile of sequences from one sugarcane transcriptome. We found the highest enzymatic activity of HXK in culms in the first month, which, at this stage, provides carbon (sucrose) and nitrogen (amino acids) for initial plant development. Our approach places novel sugar sensing sequences that work as a guideline for further research into the underlying signaling mechanisms and biotechnology applications in sugarcane.

Introduction

Extreme events, such as high or low temperatures, drought, flooding, or attacks from pathogens, are major yield-limiting factors that can lead to a reduction in crop productivity of more than 50%1. To deal with these events, plants activate protective mechanisms to tolerate stress and survive, often at the expense of growth and other developmental processes2. Two serine-threonine protein kinases, the sucrose non-fermenting-1-related protein kinase 1 (SnRK1) and the target of rapamycin (TOR), are central sensors of the plant’s energy status linking nutrient availability and growth2–5. These kinases have antagonistic functions and are typically organized in protein complexes that work to phosphorylate specific amino acid residues on their target molecules6,7.

In plants, the target of rapamycin complex 1 (TORC1, composed of the TOR kinase itself, the regulatory-associated protein of TOR—RAPTOR, and the Lethal with Sec Thirteen protein 8—LST8, all essential to the signaling function of the complex8–10) is activated in energy-rich conditions, including nutrient availability, growth factors, and cellular energy status. A single gene encodes TOR in Arabidopsis thaliana, and its knockout is embryo-lethal11. The TOR protein is composed of five domains: HEAT repeat, FAT, FKBP12/rapamycin binding domain (FRB), FATC, and a catalytic domain phosphatidylinositol 3- and 4-kinase (PI3-PI4 kinase); the latter has serine and threonine phosphorylation sites9. Two genes in Arabidopsis encode LST8, but only LST8-1 is functional12. LST8 binds to the C-terminus region of TOR, which contains several WD40 repeats that contribute to protein–protein interactions9,13. RAPTOR, also encoded by two 14,15, anchors in the N-terminus region of TOR having a Raptor N-terminus Conserved (RNC) domain, a HEAT domain, and WD40 repeats16.

Conversely, under sugar deprivation and hypoxia, SnRK1 responds to declining energy levels, leading to remobilization of reserves2,17,18. SnRK1 is a heterotrimeric enzyme composed of an alpha (α) catalytic subunit and two regulatory subunits, called beta (β) (acts as scaffolding) and beta-gamma (βγ) (for nucleotide-binding)19. In general, SnRK1α has three domains: a regulatory ubiquitin-associated (UBA) in the C-terminal20, a kinase-associated (KA1), and a serine/threonine kinase at its N-terminus, which inactivates TORC1 through phosphorylation of the RAPTOR protein6. Although some studies have demonstrated that sugars (e.g., trehalose-6-phosphate, glucose-6-phosphate, and glucose-1-phosphate) allosterically inhibit SnRK1, how this kinase responds to the availability of carbohydrates in plants is not clear6,20. In A. thaliana, enzymes involved in the primary metabolism, such as sucrose-phosphate-synthase (SPS) and nitrate reductase, have been identified as putative SnRK1 targets6,21.

Another master sugar regulator is hexokinase (HXK), the first sugar sensor identified in plants22. HXK belongs to a multigene family with six members in A. thaliana, 10 in Oryza sativa, and nine in Zea mays23–25, consistent with the idea that some copies may act as metabolic drivers and others as sugar sensors26. All HXKs display two domains, a large C-terminal catalytic domain and a small N-terminal regulatory domain. In A. thaliana, only three HXKs show catalytic activity (HXK1, HXK2, and HXK3), whereas the others are known as hexokinase-Like (HXK-Like 1, HXK-Like 2, and HXK-Like 3) without enzymatic activity and probably have a regulatory function. Four major HXK subfamilies (type A, B, C, and D) can be distinguished based on their subcellular localization and function. Subfamily A is chloroplastic, while subfamilies B and D contain different mitochondrial HXKs, and subfamily C consists of cytosolic HXKs (for review see 27).

Sugarcane can accumulate up to 42% of the plant’s dry weight in the form of sucrose28, and its average productivity is approximately 71 tons/ha, with most countries falling within this range. However, it is estimated that the theoretical maximum yield could range between 100 to 120 tons/ha29. The expansion of cultivated areas primarily drives the increase in global production. Nevertheless, this approach is becoming increasingly impractical given the climate change scenarios. Therefore, despite the inherent challenges in sugarcane breeding, such as its complex genome, extended growth cycle, and vulnerability to various biological and environmental pressures, the imperative for innovative advancements in biotechnological mechanisms aimed at enhancing productivity is becoming increasingly crucial29.

Environmental conditions primarily drive sugarcane (Saccharum spp. cv. SP80–3280) growth during the first three months of development in the field, and the role of sugars becomes more prominent from six months onwards30. Accumulation of sucrose in the culm is a net product of the sugar synthesized in source leaves31, which is stored in the parenchyma in a concentration of approximately 650 mM32. So, understanding the sensors involved in the carbon (C) partition into sucrose will prove a real milestone in developing future varieties, especially under the projected scenarios of extreme weather events such as the accumulation of 20% more sucrose in elevated CO2 condition33.

Here, we identified the sequences of sugarcane TORC1, SnRK1α, and HXK in different publicly available datasets and performed phylogenetic analysis, functional protein domain characterization, sequence ancestry analysis, and in silico expression levels for all classes of proteins. We also predicted subcellular localization of HXK and monitored HXK enzymatic activity in sugarcane leaves and culms throughout the developmental cycle (01, 03, 06, and 12 months) in the field. This systemic approach establishes a strong and promising foundation for future biotechnological exploration of the essential role of these three kinases in regulating plant energy metabolism.

Results

Identification of TORC1, SnRK1α, and HXK orthologous groups (OGs) in sugarcane

The protein sequences of TORC1, SnRK1α, and HXK from A. thaliana, Z. mays, and O. sativa were used as queries to identify the OGs in the EggNOG database v4.5.1 and v5.034,35. Subsequently, the EggNOG database was used to annotate the publicly available transcriptomic and genomic datasets from sugarcane36. Assembling all the OGs obtained from the model species and sugarcane resulted in 14 distinct OGs: one for each TORC1 component (TOR, RAPTOR, and LST8), two for SnRK1α, and nine for HXK, of which three were HXK-like (Supplementary Table S1). Recovery of TORC1, SnRK1α, and HXK putative sequences in datasets of sugarcane and parental analysis

The functional activity of a protein reflects its structure, including the domains. Due to the lack of information available for sugarcane, we applied a stringent cut-off of ≥ 80% similarity of protein domains with their respective models. Although we recovered 60 putative TOR, each domain has distinct functions (i.e., FAT and FATC are related to protein–protein interactions, and the PI3-PI4 kinase domain has kinase activity)12,37. We categorized 11 sequences of TOR as filtered, which had the Domain of Unknown Function (DUF3385), FAT, FRB, PI3-PI4 kinase, and FATC domains. RAPTOR had RNC/Raptor N and WD40 repeats, while LST8 had only WD40 repeat domains. HXK showed Hexokinase 1 and Hexokinase 2 domains. For SnRK1α, most sequences had the Protein kinase (with a predicted active site) domain and kinase-associated domain 1, while the minority (eight sugarcane sequences) contained the UBA/TS-N domain (Supplementary Table S1: S2-S15). We found 23 RAPTOR, 55 LST8, 95 SnRK1 alpha, 98 HXK, and 14 HXK-Like sequences showing all the core protein domains. This was the first step in removing truncated or incomplete sequences. Further, 30 LST8, 45 SnRK1α, 29 HXK, and five HXK-like sequences presented 100% of identity (highlighted in Supplementary Table S2) and therefore we considered only one of these sequences for further analyses.

Phylogenetic classification and subcellular localization prediction of HXKs in sugarcane

To assess the phylogenetic relationship among homo(eo)logous sequences from Saccharum spp. and other species, phylogenetic trees were built for each target (TOR, RAPTOR, LST8, SnRKα, and HXK). As expected, sequences of monocots like sugarcane, rice, Sorghum bicolor, and maize were phylogenetically close, while eudicot Arabidopsis was generally more distant (Supplementary Table S3).

To classify filtered sugarcane sequences of HXK, we included sequences from Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Consensus on clade nomenclature for HXK is lacking in the literature24,25,45,46, and we did not find a clear resolution in our results. In general, clades were distinguished with bootstrap values ​​above 80% for most of them (Fig. 3), but some subclades are not well defined. Sugarcane sequences were grouped in the clades II, III, IV, V, and VI. Clades II, IV, and V contain only monocot species, while clades I, III, and VI have monocot and eudicot sequences. AtHXK3 and OsHXK4 formed a distinct but not closely related sub-clade (clade I), as previously reported by24. Additionally, AtHXK-like 3 was phylogenetically distant from the other clade and therefore was not assigned to any clade. The subcellular localization of HXK isoforms may relate to their modes of action. However, this information remains unclear for sugarcane. Thus, we evaluated the putative localization of HXK proteins of sugarcane, A. thaliana, rice, and maize using three different software. According to WOLF PSORT, most sugarcane HXKs were predicted to be in the cytoplasm and chloroplast, while the TargetP program classified most sequences as ‘other’ (i.e., not targeted to any cellular compartment) (Supplementary Table S4). As most of the predictions for model species (A. thaliana, rice, and maize) in Plant-mPLoc corroborated previous studies27, we considered this the most robust software. However, all sequences of sugarcane were predicted to be in mitochondria and/or chloroplast based on Plant-mPLoc software (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S4). All sequences of clade VI were putatively localized in mitochondria, while for other clades, sequences from chloroplast or mitochondria were found.

HXK activity in sugarcane organs

To accurately quantify the activity of HXK in culms and leaves from sugarcane SP80-3280 grown in the field and harvested throughout the developmental cycle30, we used a highly sensitive cycling assay47. The highest activity was found in culms in the first month, which decreased in month 03 and remained constant until the end of the experiment. In leaves, HXK activity was more constant during the development. Discussion

In plants, sucrose is the main photoassimilate transported over long distances and the primary source of C skeletons48. Sucrose and its breakdown product glucose (Glc) also act as signaling molecules that regulate gene expression through sugar-sensing pathways, which can be affected by many environmental factors49–51. Although sugarcane stores an excess of photoassimilates as sucrose instead of starch, which is the most common storage carbohydrate in plants52, little is known about how C flow is regulated and how sugar sensors, which link sugar availability with growth and development, act on sugarcane metabolism53.

TORC1, SnRK1, and HXK are three sugar master regulators encoded by conserved gene families that have already been identified in several plants, most notably in C3 species26,54. Together, they regulate different aspects of metabolism, including sugars, amino acids, and organic acids55–58, as well as developmental transitions59, which influence plant performance. In sugarcane, only a few studies have identified genes of SnRK160–63, HXK46,64, and TOR65, but not RAPTOR and LST8. In most cases, the number of identified sequences was relatively low, even considering multigenic families like HXK, for which three67 and a single gene46.

The complex genotype of modern sugarcane cultivars, characterized by polyploidy and interspecific hybridization, has created a challenge for acquiring a comprehensive and annotated genome42. The sugarcane variety SP80-3280, has been used as a model for genomic, genetic, physiological, and transgenic studies30,66. In this work, apart from retrieving sugarcane sequences with a more stringent approach, we aimed to ensure they are closer to biological functionality. Therefore, we have attributed a high cut-off score (≥ 80% similarity) of protein domains, performed phylogenetic inferences of the filtered sugarcane sequences, and associated them with expression values. We expect these analyses to help extract more information about gene copy numbers representing homo(eo)logous sequences, alternative allelic versions of gene products, splicing variants, and possible paralogs. However, despite using different databases, the reduction in the available number of sequences suggests that many of them are still truncated or incomplete. On the other hand, as a commercial hybrid cultivar, sugarcane has already experienced different genetic selection pressures directly impacting the features of its gene families65,67. Moreover, balanced gene expression and achieved homeostasis are still poorly understood.

Additionally, we assayed HXK activity throughout the cultivation cycle of sugarcane plants in the field. We found that HXK levels were highest in culms during the first month of growth when they function as a source providing C (sucrose) and nitrogen (amino acids) for seedling establishment68–70. Consequently, high HXK activity can be expected, as well as a greater demand for fine-tuning sugar utilization and partitioning.

Physiologically, TORC1 is a crucial regulator of photosynthesis. Many studies with TOR dysfunctions/inhibition have shown severe defects in chloroplasts and broad regulation of plant photosynthesis-related genes71–73. Practically no information about TORC1 is available in sugarcane. 65 described seven haplotypes with a putative single-copy TOR gene, each sequence showing a different expression level in leaves of the R570 cultivar. Some of these haplotypes reflected a probable S. spontaneum and S. officinarum ancestry, and genes related to growth, such as TOR, were described to be under purifying selection and likely functional in all the haplotypes65. In the present work, we found that most of the sequences of TOR have a common ancestrally with S. spontaneum, S. officinarum, and S. barberi (Fig. 2), reinforcing the idea that TORC1 is strongly conserved among plants because of their crucial role in maintaining homeostasis.

TOR signaling occurs in all studied plants when associated with RAPTOR and LST8. We observed an increase in the number of homo(eo)logous putative RAPTOR (23) and LST8 (55) sequences compared to those of A. thaliana, rice, and maize (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S1—S3-S4). RAPTOR assists TORC1 in substrate binding via the RNC domain74, and AtRAPTOR sequences also have HEAT motifs followed by WD40 repeats15. The latter, which may be involved in the formation of reversible protein complexes75, is also responsible for the circularized beta-propeller protein that is implicated in a wide range of functions, including signal transduction and vesicular trafficking76.

Protein domain analysis employing HMMERscan with profile hidden Markov models (HMMs) integrated with PFAM77 and the gathering cut-offs revealed the RNC/RAPTOR_N (PF14538) and WD40 (PF00400) domains in sugarcane sequences of RAPTOR (Additional file 1: Table S3). In contrast, only the RNC/RAPTOR_N domain was detected in Arabidopsis. However, when using the superfamily selection option, HEAT and WD40 domains were also detected (data not shown). Although AtLST8 contains seven WD40 repeats12, our analysis with HMMERscan identified only three. Like RAPTOR, all the LST8 sugarcane sequences exhibited a range of two to five WD40 repeats, but not all displayed a domain model match coverage above 80% (Supplementary Table S1—S4). This result confirms the selective filter’s efficiency in excluding incomplete sequences from sugarcane. It reflects the hierarchy of the Structural Classification of Protein (SCOP), where superfamily is not detectable by sequence similarity but only from shared structural features associated with a large group of distantly related proteins78,79. Of the two AtLST8 genes, only LST8-1 is functional and expressed at significant levels12. Eight were not expressed in leaves out of the 25-sugarcane filtered LST8 sequences. We likely have the presence of more than one homo(eo)logous sequence in sugarcane that may perform more than one function.

SnRK1 is known to be activated in response to low energy levels, which are generally associated with stress17, but is inhibited by sugars such as trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P)80 and glucose-6-phosphate (G6P)81. Recently, 53 demonstrated that, under optimal conditions, SnRK1 plays a role in the homeostasis of sucrose and transcriptional regulation in the leaves of A. thaliana, partly determined by diel fluctuations in T6P accumulation that impact SnRK1 activity. In an engineered sugarcane cell line (Saccharum officinarum L. interspecific hybrids) accumulating up to twice as much sucrose compared to the genotype Q117, the transgenic line showed significantly lower expressions of SnRK1 during the days of faster sucrose accumulation. The authors demonstrated for the first time that T6P is also a negative regulator of SnRK1 activity in sugarcane, suggesting its role in energy management and activation of critical enzymes for significant accumulation of sucrose82.

At the transcription level, only three and two SnRK1 sequences were identified60,62. SnRK1 is suggested to regulate C metabolism at the gene expression level. Additionally, SnRK1α is constitutively expressed in sugarcane leaves and stem internodes63. It is speculated that the decrease in SnRK1 expression in mature internodes, where sucrose accumulates more, may result from its down-regulation on SPS, an enzyme that synthesizes sucrose62. Few studies have investigated the SnRK1 gene expression profile in sugarcane. Our bioinformatic analysis expands the number of sequences (we report 95 sequences of SnRK1α with different expression levels Fig. 1 and 5) in the commercial cultivar SP80-3280. It also highlights a wider functional diversity of SnRK1 in sugarcane and underscores the need for more studies to better understand and control sugar production in this essential bioenergy crop.

HXKs, which are in different subcellular compartments and differ in structure (e.g., introns and exons disposition), are considered moonlighting proteins. This means they have more than one distinct and independent function, which should not affect each other83.

HXK is an essential enzyme for Glc metabolism in plants, catalyzing the phosphorylation of Glc to G6P using adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Despite this importance, few members of HXK have been characterized, even in model species28. In sugarcane, only a few HXK sequences have been found, isolated, and characterized in previous studies46,64. Our study extends these findings by reporting 98 HXK and 14 HXK-like sequences, which were classified into five distinct clades (II, III, IV, V, and IV) (Fig. 4)25,84. These clades reflect minor differences among the HXK sequences. For example, AtHXK-like 1 and AtHXK-like 2 are localized in clade VI, indicating that they have 6–10 amino acid insertions or deletions at the adenosine binding domain (N-terminus) in contrast to AtHXK-like 385. Therefore, the separation in clades may lead to a sequence-function association among HXK85; for instance, ZmHXK 6 and ZmHXK 9 (Clade VI) are localized in the mitochondria, and their localization relies on the first 30 amino acids of the N-terminal domain86. At the same time, ZmHKX 7 in the Clade V can have high catalytic efficiency for Glc, fructose, and mannose86. Clades IV, V, and VI contain the sugarcane sequences with the highest expression values, indicating their primary expression in leaves. Previous studies have shown that after treatment with Glc or fructose, OsHXK2, OsHXK5, and OsHXK6 in clades IV and V were upregulated in leaves. At the same time, OsHXK7 was downregulated, suggesting their potential involvement in sugar detection and signaling in rice24,87. The sugarcane sequences in clades IV and V can have similar functions described for the rice and corn. Most sequences in clades IV and V have a putative dual-localization, chloroplast, and/or mitochondria, whereas all sequences in clade VI are putatively mitochondrial. Interestingly, the mitochondrial HXK or HXK sequences from model species in this clade have been the most investigated, with multiple functions attributed to them, including sensing and metabolic enzymatic roles27.

During bud sprouting, the culms undergo a sink-to-source transition in the first few months of development, providing energy and nutrients for the growing tissues88. In this study, we analyzed sugarcane plants from the second ratoon cycle, in which underground buds are attached to a functioning root system, resulting in a faster sprout and emergence89. High HXK activity is expected in very young growing tissues that use glycolysis to obtain high energy fluxes and precursors for critical biosynthetic processes. This could explain the high HXK activity observed in the first month in the culm.

Previous studies, such as those by Hoepfner and Botha64, identified three hexokinases (HK1, HK2, and HK3) with varying enzymatic activities in sugarcane culm, suggesting distinct roles in glucose metabolism and hexose phosphorylation. Additionally, Zhang et al90 reported a 24% increase in HXK activity in transgenic sugarcane overexpressing phosphomannose isomerase. However, sucrose accumulation remained unchanged, aligning with kinetic models indicating that manipulating HXK activity alone is ineffective for enhancing sucrose content in sugarcane culm31. These findings highlight the complexity of HXK regulation in sugarcane metabolism and suggest that while HXK activity plays a significant role in early growth, its manipulation is unlikely to increase sucrose accumulation in the culm directly.

One of the significant transitions in plant development is the shift from heterotrophic to photoautotrophic growth, such as when plants emerge from the soil and form aboveground and underground organs91. In this case, high levels of HXK activity may provide metabolic intermediates that aid in this transition, especially concerning plant establishment, including cell wall synthesis23. Moreover, Glc and light are required for TOR meristem activation during organogenesis in both roots and shoot meristems in A. thaliana92. Currently, there is no direct assay to quantify TOR activity55, and information on the TOR pathway in sugarcane is limited. Changes in the source-sink relationship are responsible for developmental transitions in all plants. Each organism requires an adequate supply of C, which can be sensed through TORC1, SnRK1, and HXK, with interactions among them and with environmental factors59. This supports the hypothesis that sugar-sensing mechanisms are complex and highly relevant to increasing crop yield. However, field experiments are crucial for assessing how sugarcane responds to natural variations in this context and the contribution of each sensor.

Our work is pioneering in the recovery of sequences from all the members of the complex TORC1 (TOR, RAPTOR, and LST8). We found that most of the sequences from one sugarcane transcriptomic dataset showed varying expression levels in leaves. Regarding HXK, we observed a decline in its activity in culms after the first month, indicating increased sugar production and plant growth. The main advantage of using different databases for our analyses was the ability to obtain a more significant number of sugarcane sequences. This is particularly important for a complex polypoid crop with a highly duplicated genome. However, the mixture of homo(eo)logous sequences together with a high repeat content reinforces the hypothesis that the genetics of sugarcane is more complex than other species (Fig. 6) and makes it difficult to understand how the genome functions. Altogether, our findings of the gene sequences, transcript expression patterns, and the variation in HXK activity during plant development in the field highlight the importance of the sugar sensing mechanism as an essential control step in constructing sucrose storage in sugarcane culms. These discoveries will impact future biotechnology development regarding sugar and bioethanol production from sugarcane.

Methods

Identification of TORC1, SnRK1α, and HXK sequences in sugarcane

Gene identification was performed following the method described by36. Briefly, gene sequences that encode proteins associated with TORC1, SnRK1α, and HXK from A. thaliana, Z. mays, and O. sativa were used as queries to identify orthologous groups (OG) using the EggNOG v4.5.134 and v5.0 platforms35 (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de/). These OGs were then utilized to identify the genes from these protein kinases in sugarcane’s genome and transcriptome databases38–44.

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analyses of TORC1, SnRK1α, and HXK in sugarcane

To determine clusters and/or redundant sequences (with 100% identity) among all retrieved proteins from distinct sugarcane databases, we performed a global pairwise analysis using EMBOSS v6.0.0. suite93. We also generated multiple sequence alignments for the protein sequences of TORC1, SnRK1α, and HXK via MAFFT. We then performed phylogenetic inference using IQ-TREE v1.6.994 with an automatic evolutionary model, Shimodaira–Hasegawa approximate likelihood ratio test (SH-like aLRT)95, and Ultrafast bootstrap (UFboot)96. Lastly, we reconstructed the phylogenetic trees of filtered HXK sugarcane sequences based on previous works24,25,45,46.

Characterization of protein domains

Protein sequences of A. thaliana, Sorghum bicolor, and sugarcane were subjected to domain analysis by HMMER scan v2.41.297,98. Sequences with at least 80% of the functional domain model(s) coverage were considered filtered. The following core domains in their respective sequences should be present: TOR – FAT (PF02259), FRB (PF08771), PI3-PI4 kinase (PF00454), and FATC (PF02260); RAPTOR – RNC/RAPTOR_N (PF14538); LST8 – WD40 (PF00400); SnRK1 alpha – protein kinase domain (PF00069); and HXK – Hexokinase 1 (PF00349) and Hexokinase 2 (PF03727). The best model of each sequence, consisting of the more extended sequence containing all the predicted domains, was selected and illustrated using Illustrator for Biological Sequences v1.099.

In silico expression patterns

The filtered sequences of sugarcane were used to determine the expression values of TORC1, SnRK1α, and HXK as described in 36 using in the transcriptome the hybrid SP80-328039.

Subcellular localization prediction of sugarcane HXKs

The filtered HXK protein sequences of A. thaliana, maize, rice, and sugarcane were used for subcellular localization prediction using WoLF PSORT (version not specified) (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp), TargetP v.2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/service.php?TargetP-2.0), and Plant-mPLoc v.2.0 (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/plant-multi/).

Plant material and HXK activity

The plants of Saccharum spp. (cv. SP80–3280) were grown in the field in Piracicaba, São Paulo, Brazil (22o42′01.7″ S,47o41′55.9″ W) and harvested during the second ratoon cycle30. Green fully expanded leaf (leaf + 1) and culm (internodes representing the mature portion) were collected at 10:00 h in 01, 03, 06, and 12 months of sugarcane development. They were then immediately placed in liquid nitrogen, ground to a fine powder, and stored at − 80 °C until use.

The protein extraction and maximal catalytic activity of HXK (E.C. 2.7.1.1) were assayed as described by 47 using an NADP+ cycling assay. Briefly, 10 mg aliquots (leaves and culms) were extracted by the addition of 10 mg (w/v) polyvinylpolypyrrolidone and vigorously shaking in 1 mL of ice-cold extraction buffer. The crude extract was centrifuged for 5 min at 20,000g at 4 °C, and the supernatant was desalted with Sephadex® G-25 (GE Healthcare) resin. Extracts and glucose-6P standards (ranging from 0 to 80 µM) were incubated in a medium containing 100 mM Tricine/KOH, pH 8.0, 5 mM MgCl2, 2 mM glucose, 0.4 mM NADP+, and 1 unit 0.1 mL−1 glucose-6P dehydrogenase. The reaction was started by the addition of ATP to a final concentration of 0 (blank) or 0.8 mM (maximal activity) and proceeded for 20 min at 25 °C. The reaction was stopped with 20 µL of 0.5 M NaOH. The microplates were sealed using an adhesive aluminum foil and heated at 95 °C for 5 min to destroy the NADP+. After cooling down, pH was adjusted to 9 with 20 µL of 0.5 M HCl containing 0.1 M Tricine/KOH, pH 9.0. NADPH was measured in the presence of 3 units mL−1 G6PDH grade I, 100 mM Tricine/KOH, pH 9.0, 5 mM MgCl2, 4 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM PES, 0.6 mM MTT, and 3 mM glucose-6P. The absorbance was read at 570 nm until the rates were stabilized, and the reaction rates were calculated as the absorbance increase in mOD min−1 (n = 4 biological replicates composed of 2 plants each).

Data analysis

The data on HXK activity were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the Tukey test (p < 0.05) using SigmaPlot version 11.0. Graphs were created also with SigmaPlot and heatmaps were generated using Excel.
