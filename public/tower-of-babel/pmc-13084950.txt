Epidemiology, aetiology, care and prevention strategies for pelvic floor disorders in young nulliparous female athletes in athletics: a scoping review
Authors: Estelle Gignoux, Julie Mougeot, Aïda Cadellans-Arróniz, Pascal Edouard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveYoung nulliparous female athletes do not present all of the usual pelvic floor disorders (PFDs) risk factors (eg, ageing, menopause, childbirth), and thus constitute a population for whom management strategies could differ from the general population. We aimed to map the available evidence and gap about epidemiology, aetiology, care and preventive strategies of PFDs in young nulliparous female athletes in athletics.DesignScoping review.Data sourcesPubMed, Pedro, Embase and Web of Science were searched from inception to 12 January 2026.Eligibility criteria for selecting studiesAll types of peer-reviewed articles on PFDs in young nulliparous female athletes in athletics.Results29 articles were included: 14 reviews and 15 original studies. According to the Translating Research into Injury Prevention Practice framework, 24 (83%) articles were classified in stage 1, 23 (79%) in stage 2, 11 (38%) in stage 3, 1 (3%) in stage 5 and none in stages 4 and 6. Prevalence of urinary incontinence ranged from 18.2% to 58.3%. The mechanisms and risk factors of PFDs were complex and inter-related, including a variety of intrinsic factors (eg, eating disorders, low energy availability, behaviour and knowledge), extrinsic factors related to sports practice and environmental factors (eg, relationships with staff, access to education). A significant gap was identified: there are currently no articles nor trials evaluating the efficacy of care or prevention of PFDs.ConclusionPFDs affect a large number of young nulliparous female athletes, with population-specific risk factors and mechanisms, which suggest population-specific care and prevention strategies.

Introduction

Pelvic floor disorders (PFDs) encompass a wide variety of symptoms, such as urinary or anal dysfunction, pelvic muscle or connective tissue weakness, or prolapse.1 The most common PFD is urinary incontinence, defined by the involuntary loss of urine, with a prevalence of 25.0–49.7% of women, varying with age.2 3 Pelvic organ prolapses, corresponding to the descent of one or more pelvic organs into the vagina, represent the second most common PFD with a prevalence of 30.8% in the general female population.4 The main reported risk factors for PFDs were childbirth, menopause and ageing.5

In sport contexts, recent studies point out that high-impact and/or high abdominal pressure can increase the prevalence of urinary incontinence.6 This would mean that such sporting activities can play an important role in PFD occurrences. The IOC has identified pelvic floor health in sports as an area of surveillance and aimed to improve care and prevention strategies.7 This aligns with the opinions of female athletes, who place pelvic floor health, including urinary incontinence, among the key topics of their perceived health needs.8

Among sports, athletics (track and field) is an Olympic sport category including several disciplines: sprints, hurdles, jumps, throws, combined events and race walking, as well as different running disciplines (middle-distance and long-distance track running, marathon, road running (including 5 km, 10 km and half-marathon), cross country, mountain and trail running and ultra-running) (https://worldathletics.org). Athletics activities induce high and repeated impacts on the ground, as well as high abdominal pressures, which may affect the pelvic floor function.9 Since almost half of the 600 million people who run in the world are women,10 11 a considerable number of people could potentially be concerned by these potential disorders of the pelvic floor function. Among them, young nulliparous female athletes, with urinary incontinence prevalence rates reaching 80% in certain disciplines,12 represent a specific population who do not present any of the usual PFD risk factors (eg, childbirth, menopause and ageing) and for whom the athletic discipline they are practising could itself contribute to PFDs. Analysing this population makes it possible to isolate the specific effects of athletic discipline on PFDs, which in turn could be of help to develop care and preventive strategies specific to this population of young nulliparous female athletes.

In this context, we aimed, through this scoping review, to map the available evidence and gaps with regards to epidemiology, aetiology, care and preventive strategies of PFDs in young nulliparous female athletes in athletics.

Methods

Protocol and registration

According to Munn et al13 guidance and our aims to identify the types of available evidence in a given field or to identify and analyse knowledge gaps, we chose to conduct a scoping review. We conducted a scoping review, using a rigorous and transparent scoping review method. We followed the framework proposed by Levac et al14. The protocol was registered in Open Science Foundation (doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/DK7HM). The present scoping review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews.15

Information sources and search

We searched the following databases from inception to 12 January 2026: PubMed, Pedro, Embase and Web of Science. Bramer et al16 reported that Embase and MEDLINE combined with Web of Science was the combination of database providing the greatest number of unique references by finding 95.9% of overall references.

The search strategy comprised the Boolean operators that combined the following terms:

Selection of sources of evidence

We imported all references found in the four databases into Rayyan software (https://www.rayyan.ai/), and we manually removed duplicates. Then, two independent reviewers (EG and JM) screened all references identified from titles and abstracts according to the inclusion/exclusion criteria. Three independent reviewers (EG, AC-A and PE) then selected full text articles, EG for all three languages, AC-A for texts in English and Spanish and PE for texts in French, also according to the inclusion/exclusion criteria. For each step, a consensus was required between the reviewers. An additional reviewer was planned to resolve any disagreement that could not be resolved. Furthermore, the references cited by included articles, and especially the reviews, were manually screened to identify any additional relevant articles.

Synthesis of results

We performed a descriptive analysis of the characteristics of all included articles.

Since we aimed to map available evidence and gap about epidemiology, aetiology, care and preventive strategies of PFDs, we categorised the articles and their extracted data according to the six stages of the Translating Research into Injury Prevention Practice framework (TRIPP), considering the management of PFDs in a sports injury prevention approach17:

For Stage 1, we presented PFD prevalence and their impact on quality of life. The impact on quality of life was considered in our approach as a symptom of PFDs, and therefore related to epidemiology.

For stage 2, we present a thematic analysis of the risk factors associated with PFDs on one hand, and a separate presentation of the mechanisms associated with PFDs on the other.

For all stages, we performed a descriptive analysis of the extracted data, and we reported data as they were presented in the included articles, with as many details as possible (eg, ratio, percentage, CI, verbatim). To avoid duplication and ensure an accurate representation of the available evidence, only data from original studies were included in these analyses, and data reported solely in reviews were not used.

We also categorised the data extracted from each stage according to the biopsychosocial model, according to their biological, psychological or environmental source, to highlight the complexity of the interactions.18

This allowed us to (1) map the evidence and key concepts available on combined events and performance and/or health, (2) summarise existing research findings and (3) identify research gaps in the existing literature.19

Equity, diversity and inclusion statement

Our study included only young women, nulliparous, from adolescence until before menopause, between 13–40 years old, practising athletics (including running) regardless of their level of performance, without restriction on the countries, race/ethnicity/culture, socio-economic status or representation from marginalised groups (except neurological or oncological diseases, pregnancy, perimenopause and old age), except writing language (only studies published in English or French or Spanish were included).

The authors of this article include three women and one man, two early-career and two senior researchers, three physiotherapists and one physician, from two countries of Europe (France and Spain).

Prevalence

Prevalence data specific to athletics was reported in five included articles,37 38 43 45 48 four of which concerned urinary incontinence and one reporting other PFDs (urinary incontinence, anal incontinence, overactive bladder, pain and pelvic organ prolapse). The prevalence of urinary incontinence by athletics disciplines was detailed only in two articles.37 48 The levels and volumes of practice of the populations varied, from the ‘physically active woman’ to the ‘elite athlete’ and were not clearly defined in each article.

Regarding our specific population of young nulliparous female athletes in athletics, the prevalence data concerned a total of 498 young female athletes, with average ages ranging from 18.0 to 21.1 years old (online supplemental material 6). Prevalence of urinary incontinence ranged from 18.2% to 58.3%. Results per article are presented by taking into account the sample size in figure 3.

Quality of life

Hagovska et al38 used Incontinence Quality of Life Questionnaire and showed a global score of 94.8 over a maximum of 100 (which corresponds to no reduction in quality of life). Alves et al46 used the King’s Health Questionnaire (KHQ) and showed a decrease in quality of life in all areas (ie, incontinence impact, role limitations, physical limitations, social limitations, personal relationships, emotions, sleep/energy and severity measures), with the exception of perception of general health.

Three articles35 45 48 reported athletes’ feelings of embarrassment and anxiety. Two reported35 48 negative effects on sports performance, such as reduced coordination, clumsiness, distraction, perceived impairment of movement and a perceived decrease in power output.

Stage 2: establish aetiology and mechanisms of injury

Characteristics of the included articles

A total of 23 articles (79%) reported information that could be considered as related to TRIPP stage 2: 9 reviews (29%)2324 26 27 2933 and 14 original studies (48%)3548 (9 cross-sectional studies, 1 case-control study, 2 cohort studies, 1 exploratory study and 1 crossover clinical trial) (table 1 and figure 2).

Data extracted on aetiology and mechanisms were classified into three subgroups: perineal function and mechanisms (such as incontinence triggers or muscle activity during sports activity), intrinsic risk factors and extrinsic risk factors.

Perineal function and mechanisms

Perineal electromyogram (EMG) during running showed preactivation of the perineum and maximum activation that can exceed maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) at 11 km/h (106.1% MVC±10.8%).40 49 There were no significant differences between minimalist and traditional shoes on EMG pelvic floor activity, whereas significant correlations between minimalist shoes and lumbar extensor activation and internal oblique activation were found at 6, 9 and 11 km/hour.47

Athletes who trained frequently and intensively at high impact showed significant differences in pelvic floor anatomy. Kruger et al42 reported that there was a greater descent of the bladder neck (22.7 mm vs 15.1 mm, p=0.03), a greater hiatal area during the Valsalva manoeuvre (21.53 vs 14.91 cm2, p=0.013) and a higher mean diameter of the pubovisceral muscle (0.96 cm vs 0.70 cm, p<0.01).

Triggers have been identified, with jumping and running tasks being the most likely to cause urinary incontinence.45 Proposed hypotheses were muscle fatigability or imbalance between high intra-abdominal pressure and the support function of the perineum.45

Intrinsic risk factors

One article36 focused on eating disorders and reported that athletes suffering urinary incontinence were 2.15 times more likely to have risk behaviours for eating disorders. The impact of body mass index was uncertain; however, no significant association was reported between body mass index and urinary incontinence in three of the four articles studying this factor.36 37 44 48 Female athletes with low energy availability had 1.7 times the odds (OR=1.73, 95% CI 1.22 to 2.45, p=0.002) of having urinary incontinence compared with those with adequate energy available.37

The role of constipation was unclear in included articles. One article identified constipation as a risk factor for urinary incontinence: the proportion of urinary incontinence was higher in constipated athletes (81.8%) than in athletes without constipation (68.5%).44 A second article did not find any increased prevalence for stool consistencies usually associated with the presence of constipation.39

Elleuch et al44 reported 79.3% stress urinary incontinence in athletes who had enuresis in childhood compared with 59.5% in athletes who did not have.

Inappropriate voiding behaviours were reported among nulliparous sportswomen, including increased urinary frequency, systematic voiding before the event, rushing to the toilet (18/42), (18/42) or delayed voiding (7/42).43

A lack of specific knowledge on PFDs was observed among the athletes.41 48 Parmigiano et al41 reported that 89% of the athletes were not aware of the fact that they could be exposed to increased risk of urinary incontinence.

Extrinsic risk factors

A lack of individual knowledge may also be influenced by environmental factors.43 One article showed the lack of education on pelvic floor muscle of athletes: 80.8% of participants (n=172) did not receive any information.43 The taboo limiting communication was found in two articles.35 45 Half of the athletes discussed their urinary incontinence with a teammate, less than 5% discussed it with a coach, caregiver or family member.

The results concerning the impact of sports practice methods were quite disparate, particularly with regard to the number of years and volume of sports practice.39 44 46 Regarding disciplines, there seemed to be more urinary incontinence in long-distance and hurdlers/sprinters and jumpers than middle-distance.37 45 48

Discussion

The main findings of this scoping review were that (1) a significant proportion of young nulliparous female athletes in athletics were affected by PFDs, especially urinary incontinence, (2) the mechanisms and risk factors of PFDs in young nulliparous female athletes in athletics were complex and interrelated, including a variety of intrinsic, extrinsic and environmental factors; and (3) a major gap has been identified in stage 3–6 of the TRIPP framework, as we did not find any article evaluating the efficacy or the implementation of PFD treatments or preventive measures.

Large-scale phenomenon

The prevalence data obtained from scientific peer-reviewed articles through our systematic search mostly concerned urinary incontinence (93%). The results showed the importance of this disorder, with a prevalence ranging from 18% to 58%. These results in young nulliparous female athletes in athletics are higher than the prevalence observed in the general population of comparable age (10%).5052 The study conducted at the 2024 World U20 Athletics Championships reported that 41.2% of participating females reported symptoms of PFDs during activity (urinary incontinence, overactive bladder syndrome, pelvic organ prolapse, anal incontinence and pelvic pain).48 These results confirm the noticeable prevalence of urinary incontinence and PFDs in general. This confirms the need to develop specific strategies for this population.

Sport-specific mechanisms

From a biomechanical perspective, two types of force seem to be at the root of these symptoms: (1) direct forces, such as pushing or the increase in intra-abdominal pressure when carrying weights or doing certain abdominal muscle strengthening exercises, and (2) forces linked to so-called ‘impact tasks’ (running and jumping).45 46 However, there is no consensus on the categorisation of what should be considered as ‘impact practices’, and these categorisations vary from one article to another. The acceleration components of sports movements and their effects on pelvic structures do not appear to have been explored and should be in the future to better understand the mechanisms underlying PFDs.

The stresses associated with sporting activity appeared to lead to anatomical changes, increasing the size of the urogenital hiatus and a lowering of the height of the bladder neck.42 These results were supported by the findings of a study confirming that morphological changes after 37 min running appeared to predominantly affect the passive support system.53 According to De Lancey’s hammock theory, laxity or hypermobility of the sub-urethral layer reduced the transmission of abdominal pressure to the urethra closure and contributed to stress urinary incontinence.54 In addition, early urinary incontinence reported by young elite athletes was a good predictor of urinary incontinence later in life.55 This can suggest that connective tissue lesions acquired early in life persist into adulthood and should be considered in prevention programmes. Preservation of pelvic structures appears to be a central element in the maintenance of continence. The morphological changes in the structures were associated with an increase in the diameter of the puborectal muscle.42 This muscle hypertrophy could be compatible with an adaptive process. The increase in muscle size was mainly the result of an increase in the diameter of muscle fibres, which improved force production capacity.56 Pelvic floor training, which was already used in the management of constituted incontinence, seemed to be a relevant means of prevention by increasing the adaptive process.57

Holistic approach to health

Regarding urinary and defecatory functions, findings on the relationship between constipation and urinary incontinence were contradictory in young athletes.39 44 One article found an increase of urinary incontinence during sport in constipated athletes compared with athletes without constipation,44 while another article did not find an increased prevalence for stool consistencies usually associated with the presence of constipation,39 so no conclusion can be drawn here on this point. However, in the older general population, constipation was significantly associated with the risk of urinary and anal incontinence, as well as pelvic organ prolapse, and should therefore be prevented.58 Thus, future studies exploring this association in young athletes should be addressed.

The higher prevalence of urinary incontinence in athletes who had experienced enuresis during childhood suggested the interest of considering lower urinary tract classically observed in children and adolescents, such as voiding postponement, overactive bladder and dysfunctional voiding.59 60 A recent study supports this hypothesis, showing hypertrophy of the pelvic floor muscles and striated urethral sphincter (median: 2.5 mm vs 1.8 mm, p=0.016) in symptomatic athletes compared with asymptomatic athletes.61 In addition, the symptomatic subgroup had more severe urinary difficulties as assessed by the Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction Research Network Symptom Index 29 (LURN SI-29) questionnaire (section C) (5.0 (3.0–5.0); 2.0 (1.0–3.5); p=0.031).61

According to our findings regarding overall health, there seemed to be a significant association between eating disorders and urinary incontinence.36 Similarly, women with low energy availability were more likely to experience urinary incontinence.37 Thus, these results implied that urinary incontinence symptoms should be detected and taken into account to ensure efficient management of athletes’ overall health.

Understanding perceptions

Using the KHQ, Alves et al,46 by reported that, in the presence of urinary incontinence, the condition was worse in all areas except the domain of ‘general health perception’.46 This is an interesting point, because while the impact on performance and quality of life was significant, athletes seemed not to consider urinary incontinence as a health problem. Lack of attention to this problem could worsen the management of it. The use of questionnaires did not provide a detailed understanding of how athletes feel. A qualitative approach could help better ascertain athletes’ perceptions in order to better manage them and to increase their adherence to future care or prevention programmes.

Knowledge of pelvic floor disorders

Athlete representations of PFDs may be related to their level of knowledge and beliefs. Inadequate or lack of knowledge regarding pelvic floor function has been observed,41 43 and this seemed to influence the degree of incontinence. Access to awareness-raising resources was low, 80% of athletes had not received any information on pelvic floor muscle training.43 This reinforces the importance of considering educational programmes regarding PFDs targeting these athletes. There are currently programmes for athletes of all ages and parities, or programmes for adolescent girls regardless of their sporting status,62 63 but no specific programmes for young nulliparous female athletes. Evaluating the effect of prevention programmes in this population (which does not present the usual risk factors) would identify the specific effects on the practice of sport.

In addition, a lack of knowledge was also observed among the professionals supervising the athletes. Dakic et al64 reported that one-third of the accompanying professionals acknowledged a lack of training in this area.64 Half of them would not feel comfortable managing PFD, if they were detected.64 Most professionals (87%) were interested in receiving training or accessing additional information.64 In addition, better education for athletes and managers, enabling them to share the same level of knowledge, would also help to remove taboos and improve the communication. The interest of multidisciplinary prevention strategies is reinforced in this article by the number of environmental risk factors we take into account. Reducing risk factors in sport requires close collaboration between athletes, carers and coaches, in order to strike a balance between performance needs and health needs.

Limitations

A critical evaluation of individual sources of evidence was not carried out, but this is not mandatory for scoping reviews.15 Our inclusion and exclusion criteria may have limited the exhaustiveness of the results. We limited the language of the articles. We chose to include only peer-reviewed articles to ensure robust results, which meant excluding grey literature. Only one reviewer performed the data extraction. For stages 2–6, it was not possible to distinguish the information specific to athletics athletes from information pertaining to athletes in general in the data extracted from included articles. The older articles only partially reflected current sports training practices.

Clinical implications and research perspectives

These findings will be of great interest to help to orient future research and future development of care and prevention strategies. Efforts must consequently be made to develop and evaluate care and prevention strategies for young nulliparous athletes, by considering all the intrinsic factors (eg, eating disorders, low energy availability, behaviour and knowledge), extrinsic factors related to sports practice and environmental factors (eg, relationships with staff, access to education) in a holistic approach. In addition, qualitative exploration of the context, beliefs, barriers and levers of all those involved seems necessary to improve the development and implementation of prevention programmes. The collaboration of athletes, coaches and carers in the design and application of prevention programmes seems relevant.

Conclusions

The results of this scoping review show that PFDs affect a significant proportion of young nulliparous female athletes in athletics. The mechanisms involved are not yet fully understood. This review of the available literature has illustrated the complexity and interdependence of the mechanisms and risk factors involved in pelvic floor dysfunction. Our findings showed that there is currently a major gap in care and prevention strategies, with a lack of evaluation of potential programmes.
