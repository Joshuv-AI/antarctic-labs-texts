Probing the quantum motion of a macroscopic mechanical oscillator with a radio-frequency superconducting qubit
K. Gerashchenko, R. Rousseau, L. Balembois, H. Patange, P. Manset, T. Briant, P.-F. Cohadon, A. Heidmann, W. C. Smith, A. Tilloy, Z. Leghtas, E. Flurin, T. Jacqmin, S. Deléglise

Abstract

Long-lived mechanical resonators like drums oscillating at MHz frequencies and operating in the quantum regime are a powerful platform for quantum technologies and tests of fundamental physics
[
1
]
.
Yet quantum control of such systems remains challenging, owing to their low energy scale and the difficulty of achieving efficient coupling to other well-controlled quantum devices. Here, we demonstrate repeated coherent interactions between a 4 MHz suspended silicon nitride membrane and a resonant superconducting heavy-fluxonium qubit
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. The qubit is initialized at an effective temperature of 21 µK and read out
with 77% single-shot fidelity. During the 6 ms lifetime of the membrane the two systems swap excitations more than 300 times. After each interaction, a
state-selective qubit detection is performed, implementing a stroboscopic series of weak measurements that provide information about the mechanical state. The accumulated records reconstruct the position noise spectrum of the membrane, revealing both its thermal occupation
\bm{n_{\mathrm{th}}\approx 47}
at 10 mK and the qubit-induced back-action. By preparing the qubit either in its ground or excited state before each interaction, we observe an imbalance between the emission and absorption spectra, proportional to
\bm{n_{\mathrm{th}}}
and
\bm{n_{\mathrm{th}}+1}
respectively, a hallmark of the non-commutation of phonon creation and annihilation operators. Since the predicted Diósi–Penrose gravitational collapse time
[
5
,
6
]
is comparable to the measured mechanical decoherence time, our architecture enters a regime where gravity-induced decoherence could be tested directly
[
7
]
.

Mechanical resonators, such as vibrating membranes or beams, are essential components of quantum technologies, serving as high-precision sensors
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
and transducers between distinct quantum systems
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
.
They also provide a platform for probing fundamental physics at the intersection of quantum mechanics and gravity
[
7
]
.
For instance, owing to their long lifetime and large zero-point fluctuations, state-of-the-art drums oscillating at MHz frequencies have been identified as good candidates to test the the gravitationally induced wavefunction collapse mechanism postulated by Diósi
[
5
]
and Penrose
[
6
]
.
Measuring and controlling the quantum state of these systems remains challenging because it requires coupling them to a resonant two-level system
[
15
]
. In circuit Quantum Acoustodynamics (cQAD)
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
, this is typically achieved with superconducting qubits that operate in the GHz range.
Significant progress has been made toward bridging the frequency gap with macroscopic mechanical resonators. For instance, mechanical squeezing was demonstrated in a 22 MHz aluminum drum dispersively coupled to a 4 GHz Cooper-pair box
[
19
]
.
However, coherent interaction and efficient qubit readout are hindered by the short coherence times of this qubit architecture.
More recently, techniques from cQAD have been extended to observe coherent exchange dynamics between a 700 MHz piezoelectric resonator and a fluxonium qubit
[
20
]
.
However, owing to the resonator’s extremely small mass (only a few picograms), the gravitational decoherence time predicted by the Diosi–Penrose model is six orders of magnitude longer than the environmental decoherence time.

In this paper, we report an experimental implementation of an idealized mechanical-cavity quantum electrodynamics system, where a mechanical resonator is repeatedly interrogated by a resonant two-level system.
The mechanical resonator is a suspended silicon nitride membrane oscillating at
\omega_{m}/2\pi\sim 4.4
MHz.
It is coupled to a heavy-fluxonium circuit, which features simultaneously a low frequency qubit transition
[
3
,
4
]
, resonant with the MHz mechanical mode, and GHz transitions to higher excited states that can be used to read out and prepare the qubit state deterministically (see
Fig.
1
(a)
).
We rely exclusively on the binary outcomes of qubit measurements to infer the mechanical state.
Owing to the long mechanical lifetime (
T_{1}^{\mathrm{m}}\sim 6
ms), the qubit interacts with the membrane over 300 times during that interval,
extracting information bit-by-bit about its state.
This process realizes a periodic weak measurement of the mechanical mode, from which we reconstruct the noise spectrum
S_{xx}(\omega)
of the dimensionless membrane position
\bm{x}
under the influence of the thermal environment of occupation
n_{\mathrm{th}}
.
By letting the membrane interact with the qubit either prepared in its ground, or excited state, we probe separately the emission and absorption noise spectra around
\pm\omega_{m}
.
This measurement reveals the fundamental quantum asymmetry between emission and absorption processes, proportional to
n_{\mathrm{th}}
and
n_{\mathrm{th}}+1
respectively.
While similar signatures have already been observed through sideband asymmetries under dispersive coupling to microwave
[
21
]
or optical
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
cavities, this is the first time, to our knowledge, the asymmetric mechanical noise spectrum is measured via coupling to a two-level system, as initially proposed theoretically
[
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
.
Most importantly, given the mass of our system (
M\sim 5.3
ng), the estimated Diósi-Penrose gravitational collapse time
1
1

1

This estimate relies on several debated assumptions; for a detailed discussion see
App.
B.2
, Ref.
[
7
]
and references therein.

\tau_{G}\sim 0.5
ms is comparable to the mechanical decoherence time
\tau_{\text{th}}=2T_{1}^{m}/n_{\textrm{th}}\sim 0.3
ms, positioning our experiment in a regime where gravitational effects could become experimentally relevant (see Methods). More generally, by bringing cQAD into the MHz regime, this platform opens the way to interferometric tests of objective-collapse models in the phenomenologically relevant parameter range (see Methods).

Our device (
Fig.
1
) comprises two chips assembled via a flip-chip technique. The fluxonium qubit is fabricated on intrinsic silicon (see Supplementary Information) and features two coplanar capacitor pads shunting a small Josephson junction (
E_{J}/h=4.82(6)
GHz) in parallel with a large superinductance formed by 360 junctions (
E_{L}/h=0.128(7)
GHz). The mechanical resonator is a clamped
110\times 140\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}
, 90-nm-thick SiN membrane under
\gtrsim 800
MPa tensile stress, coated with a patterned Al pad (see Supplementary Information). The qubit couples predominantly to the membrane’s mode at
\omega_{m}/2\pi=4.4
MHz—antisymmetric about the
Y
-axis (
Fig.
1
(b)-(c)
)—via two vacuum-gap capacitors, yielding the total capacitance
E_{C}/h=0.418(9)
GHz. A large bias electrode holds the membrane at potential
V_{b}
, while capacitors
C_{g}
reference the fluxonium island to ground, enabling motion-to-charge transduction.

In the heavy-fluxonium regime
[
3
,
4
]
(
E_{J}\gg E_{C}>E_{L}
), the circuit is highly anharmonic, with a qubit transition frequency
\omega_{q}/2\pi=2.35\,
MHz, while the transition to the next excited state occurs at 3.45
GHz
. This permits treating
\{|g\rangle,|e\rangle\}
as an almost ideal two-level system, with higher levels used for fast reset and dynamic tuning. A two-photon preparation protocol yields fidelities
\eta_{g}=99.5(1.7)\%
and
\eta_{e}=99.2(5)\%
(see Supplementary Information), while single-shot dispersive readout achieves measurement fidelities
F_{g}=84.8(1.8)\%
and
F_{e}=69.1(1.2)\%
(see Supplementary Information). By driving the circuit near the higher excited state transitions at the flux sweet spot
\varphi_{\text{ext}}=\pi
, we dynamically tune
\omega_{q}
via the AC Stark effect to bring it into resonance with the 4.4 MHz membrane. Coherence times are
T_{1}=22.4~
µs and
T_{2}=17.1~
µs, decreasing to
T_{1}=7.4~
µs and
T_{2}=4.2~
µs when tuned to
\omega_{m}
via AC-Stark shift (see Supplementary Information).

A membrane mode of frequency
\omega_{m}
can be modeled as a harmonic oscillator of Hamiltonian
\bm{H}_{m}=\hbar\omega_{m}(\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}+1/2)
, where
\bm{a}
and
\bm{a}^{\dagger}
are the ladder operators (
Fig.
1
(a)
).
Projecting the system dynamics in the
\{|g\rangle,|e\rangle\}
manifold and applying the rotating wave approximation yields the Jaynes-Cummings Hamiltonian

\bm{H}_{\!\!J\!C}/\hbar=\omega_{m}\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}+\frac{\omega_{q}}{2}\bm{\sigma}_{z}+i\frac{\Omega}{2}\left(\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{\sigma}-\bm{a}\bm{\sigma}^{\dagger}\right),

(1)

where
\bm{\sigma}
represents the lowering operator for the qubit and the coupling strength is given by (see Supplementary Information)

\Omega=\omega_{q}|\langle g|\bm{\varphi}|e\rangle|\frac{\partial C_{m}^{+}}{\partial x}\beta\frac{V_{b}-V_{\text{offset}}}{2e}.

(2)

Here,
V_{b}
denotes the membrane bias voltage,
\beta\sim C_{g}/(C_{g}+C_{m})\sim 1/2
is a dilution factor resulting from the capacitive biasing scheme, and
V_{\mathrm{offset}}=Q_{2}/2C_{g}
is an offset voltage resulting from the static charge
Q_{2}
confined on the floating fluxonium island.
With the maximum effective voltage of
|V_{b}-V_{\mathrm{offset}}|\sim 11.5
V practically achievable in our setup, we measure
\Omega/2\pi\sim 1.50(8)
kHz (see Supplementary Information).

In absence of AC-Stark effect, the qubit and the membrane modes are out-of-resonance: the large detuning
|\Delta|\gg\Omega
suppresses direct energy exchange between the two systems.
Instead, the qubit undergoes a frequency shift proportional to the phonon population of the membrane
[
30
,
31
]

\chi\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}\bm{\sigma}_{z}
, where
\chi=\Omega^{2}/4\Delta
.
This effect is detected via a Ramsey spectroscopy experiment (see
Fig.
2
(a)
).
The qubit is initialized in the
|+\rangle=(|g\rangle+|e\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
state and evolves for a duration
\tau
with the membrane in a coherent state, during which it accumulates a phase proportional to the phonon number.
The experiment alternates between measuring the
\bm{\sigma}_{x}
and
\bm{\sigma}_{y}
components of the superposition over multiple realizations.

Fig.
2
(a)
shows the qubit frequency shift as a function of the membrane drive frequency and applied DC bias voltage
V_{b}
. The data reveal a quadratic decrease in the mechanical frequency with
V_{b}-V_{\rm offset}
, explained by the electrostatic spring-softening effect
[
32
]
(see supplementay information).

The relaxation time of the mechanical resonator
T_{1}^{m}
, is extracted by preparing the membrane in a coherent state and measuring the exponential decay of its phonon population.
This decay manifests as a time variation of the qubit frequency, tracked through repeated Ramsey measurements.
An exponential fit to the data, shown in
Fig.
2
(b)
, yields
T_{1}^{m}=5.9~\mathrm{ms}
.
The corresponding quality factor,
Q_{m}=\omega_{m}T_{1}^{m}=1.62\cdot 10^{5}
, is consistent with similar studies on silicon nitride membranes
[
33
]
.

We now focus on the resonant interaction regime.
In these experiments, the qubit is prepared and measured at its bare resonance frequency
\omega_{q}
, while the resonant interaction, is activated for a duration
\tau
by tuning the qubit frequency
\omega_{q}^{\rm st}=\omega_{m}+\Delta
close to the membrane one, governed by the evolution operator
\bm{U}_{\!I}=e^{-i\bm{H}_{\!\!J\!C}.\tau/\hbar}
.
When the membrane is initialized in a large enough coherent state
\alpha
, characteristic Rabi oscillations emerge at a rate
\Omega|\alpha|\gg 1/T_{1}
(see
Fig.
2
(c)
).
In this limit,
\bm{U}_{\!I}
describes a two-level system undergoing a rotation by an angle
|\alpha|\theta_{1}
about the equatorial axis defined by
i\alpha^{*}
under the influence of a classical drive.

When the membrane is in a thermal state, the resonant interaction induces a qubit precession of typical angle
\theta_{1}\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{th}}}
, where
\theta_{1}=\Omega\tau\sim 47
mrad is the angle induced by a single phonon.
Though small, this precession is detectable through sequential qubit measurements performed within a single mechanical lifetime
[
34
,
4
]
.
Starting from the prepared qubit state (either
|g\rangle
or
|e\rangle
), we turn on the interaction for a duration
\tau=5~
µs. We then project the qubit onto
\bm{\sigma}_{x}
by applying a
\sqrt{X}
rotation,
followed by a
\bm{\sigma}_{z}
measurement.
This sequence is periodically repeated with the qubit reinitialized in the same state (
Fig.
3
(c)
).

To build physical intuition, we first treat the degrees of freedom of the membrane—its position
\bm{x}
and momentum
\bm{p}
—classically.
In the qubit drive frame, rotating at
\omega_{q}^{\rm st}
, the complex mechanical amplitude
\alpha(t)=\tilde{\alpha}(t)e^{i\Delta t}
appears as a signal oscillating at frequency
\Delta
, modulated by a slowly varying envelope
\tilde{\alpha}(t)
that diffuses in phase space at a characteristic rate
\kappa_{m}
, as shown in
Fig.
3
(a)
.
This amplitude is sampled at discrete times
t_{k}=k\times T
by the sequential qubit measurements of
\bm{\sigma}_{x,k}
, where
T=15.5~
µs. In the small angle limit
|\alpha|\theta_{1}\ll 1
, the rotation
\bm{U}_{\!I}
maps
the phase space coordinates
(\mathrm{Re}(\alpha(t_{k})),\mathrm{Im}(\alpha(t_{k}))
of the mechanical oscillator onto the qubit’s transverse components
(\bm{\sigma}_{x,k},\bm{\sigma}_{y,k})
(
Fig.
3
(b)
). Therefore,

\langle\bm{\sigma}_{x,k}\rangle=\theta_{1}x(t_{k}),

(3)

where
x(t_{k})=\mathrm{Re}(\alpha(t_{k}))
is the dimensionless membrane position.
By operating the qubit in a frame detuned by
\Delta>\kappa_{m}
, the free evolution between interaction pulses continually rotates the measurement axis. Consequently, successive
\bm{\sigma}_{x,k}
measurements sweep through all mechanical quadrature angles—probing both
\mathrm{Re}(\tilde{\alpha}(t_{k}))
and
\mathrm{Im}(\tilde{\alpha}(t_{k}))
—in direct analogy with a heterodyne detection.

Our goal is to characterize the steady-state noise spectra
S_{mm}^{g/e}(\omega)
of the qubit readout record
m_{k}
. In practice see Methods), we segment the time series into equal-length batches, compute the discrete Fourier transform of each, and average the squared magnitudes (see
Fig.
3
(d)-(e)
).
In a semiclassical approximation that neglects qubit backaction on the membrane, the trajectory
x(t)
acts as an independent external drive, and the two-point correlator factorizes:

\mathcal{C}_{k}\equiv\overline{\langle\sigma_{x,k}\,\sigma_{x,0}\rangle}=\overline{\langle\sigma_{x,k}\rangle}\,\overline{\langle\sigma_{x,0}\rangle},

(4)

for
k\neq 0
, with
\mathcal{C}_{0}=1
.
Recalling
Eq. (
3
)
, and Wiener–Khinchin theorem, linking the measured spectrum to the Fourier-transform of
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, we have

S_{mm}^{g/e}(\omega)=\frac{T}{N}\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{C}_{k}]=\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{4}S_{xx}(\omega)+T.

(5)

where
S_{xx}
is the position noise spectrum and
T
is the constant background arising from the sampling noise due to the binary nature of the qubit measurements.
In our experimental data (see
Fig.
3
(f)
), the spectra
S_{mm}^{g/e}
display a Lorentzian peak centered at
\omega_{m}
that reflects the thermal fluctuations of the membrane, with the sampling noise background
T
indicated by a dashed line.
Although
Eq. (
5
)
predicts identical spectra regardless of the initial qubit state, we observe significant differences in both the area and the linewidth of the thermal peaks.

These discrepancies arise because our simple model neglects the energy exchange between the membrane and the qubit during their interaction. In particular, when the qubit is prepared in
|g\rangle
it absorbs energy from the membrane, whereas
when prepared in
|e\rangle
, it emits energy to the membrane (see
Fig.
3
(a)
).
On average, the qubit energy is lowered or raised on the Bloch sphere by
\Delta E_{g,e}/\hbar\omega_{q}^{\text{st}}=\theta_{1}^{2}n_{\mathrm{th}}/4
due to the interaction.
Because of energy conservation, the membrane must experience a qubit-induced relaxation or amplification at a rate
\kappa=\theta_{1}^{2}/4T
.
The mechanical linewidth is thus modified (broadened or narrowed) according to
\kappa^{\prime}_{g/e}=\kappa_{m}\pm\kappa
.
Furthermore the qubit being reinitialized in a pure state before each interaction, it provides a 0-occupation bath for the membrane, leading to an occupation
n^{\prime}_{\mathrm{th},i}=\kappa_{m}n_{\mathrm{th}}/\kappa^{\prime}_{i}
.
This qubit-induced cooling (or heating) mechanism is analogous to the dynamical back-action effects observed in cavity optomechanics
[
35
,
36
]
.

Lorentzian fits to the thermal peaks yield linewidths of
\kappa^{\prime}_{g}=2\pi\times(28.5\pm 1.4)
Hz for
|g\rangle
preparations and
\kappa^{\prime}_{e}=2\pi\times(23.7\pm 0.7)
Hz for
|e\rangle
preparations.
The inferred linewidth
\kappa_{m}=(\kappa^{\prime}_{e}+\kappa^{\prime}_{g})/2=2\pi\times(26.1\pm 0.8)~{\rm Hz}
is in agreement with the previously measured
1/T_{1}^{m}=2\pi\times(27.16\pm 2.6)
Hz, confirming that the membrane exhibits no additional dephasing beyond its Fourier-limited linewidth.
Moreover, the dynamical back-action rate
\kappa=2\pi\times(2.4\pm 0.8)
Hz agrees with a full experimental model that includes finite qubit preparation and readout fidelities.
To account for these imperfections, the spectrum is normalized using a calibration peak generated by a weak continuous tone applied on the qubit charge drive port (see
Fig.
3
(f)
).
The normalized, background-subtracted spectra are displayed in
Fig.
3
(g)
.
The experimentally obtained area ratio
A_{e}/A_{g}=1.42\pm 0.11
is in a reasonable agreement with the expected value
n^{\prime}_{\mathrm{th},e}/n^{\prime}_{\mathrm{th},g}=\kappa^{\prime}_{g}/\kappa^{\prime}_{e}=1.22\pm 0.07
.
Here the dynamical backaction is moderate due to the relatively low cooperativity
\kappa/\kappa_{m}\sim 1/10
.
Nevertheless, we expect that ground-state cooling will be within reach in future experiments—either by further increasing the mechanical lifetime
[
37
,
38
]
or by boosting the qubit–membrane coupling strength via a larger DC bias.

In the full quantum treatment, each interaction cycle constitutes a weak measurement of the membrane state
\bm{\rho}_{m}
, described by

\bm{M}_{\pm}^{i}=\langle\pm|\,\bm{U}_{I}\,|i\rangle,\quad i\in\{g,e\},

where
\bm{U}_{I}
entangles qubit and membrane and “
\pm
” labels the projective qubit outcome. This measurement-induced backaction steers the membrane into one of two conditional states, imprinting information that survives for its mechanical lifetime
T_{1}^{m}
. As a result, for qubit prepared in
|g\rangle
(resp.
|e\rangle
), the two-point correlator to second order in
\theta_{1}
becomes:

C_{k}^{g}=\theta_{1}^{2}\,\text{Re}\bigl\langle\bm{a}^{\dagger}(t_{k})\,\bm{a}(0)\bigr\rangle,\qquad C_{k}^{e}=\theta_{1}^{2}\,\text{Re}\bigl\langle\bm{a}(t_{k})\,\bm{a}^{\dagger}(0)\bigr\rangle,

where
\bm{a}(t)
and
\bm{a}^{\dagger}(t)
are the annihilation and creation operators in the Heisenberg picture. Fourier transforming these correlators yields the emission (
g
) and absorption (
e
) spectra of the mechanical mode (see Methods).

In
Fig.
3
, the difference between
S_{xx}^{g}(\omega_{m})
and
S_{xx}^{e}(\omega_{m})
is largely due to classical backaction–cooling for
|g\rangle
and amplification for
|e\rangle
.
In other words, the equilibrium occupation
\langle\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}\rangle
is different in the two experimental sequences.
To mitigate this effect, and isolate the small quantum asymmetry, we implemented an experimental sequence where the qubit is alternatively prepared in
|g\rangle
and
|e\rangle
(see
Fig.
4
(b)
). In this manner, the average energy exchanged with the qubit cancels out in a coarse-grained approximation.
The spectra

To conclude, we have demonstrated, for the first time, direct resonant coupling between a superconducting qubit and a macroscopic mechanical membrane oscillating at 4 MHz—over two orders of magnitude below previous realizations

20

Exploiting the membrane’s long lifetime and periodic weak measurements, we reconstruct its noise spectrum and reveal a clear asymmetry between emission and absorption—a direct signature of the non-commutativity of phonon creation and annihilation operators.

Importantly, operating a
5.3

[
7

Looking ahead, this platform naturally lends itself to quantum and adaptive feedback protocols—using membrane drives or conditional qubit preparations informed by real-time readout—to achieve active stabilization, state steering, and generation of non-classical mechanical states
[

Several optimizations could improve the device performance. Phononic-crystal engineering should boost the electromechanical cooperativity by three orders of magnitude. B
Fundamental tests on massive quantum superpositions

According to general relativity, mass–energy curves spacetime, so the rate at which time flows is not universal but depends on the distribution of nearby massive objects.
On the other hand, in quantum mechanics, the superposition principle allows a system to occupy several distinct configurations at once.
This leads to a conceptual tension: how does time flow for a massive object in a spatial superposition? In Schrödinger’s equation, time appears only as an external parameter, independent of the quantum state.

Concomitantly, while the superposition principle has been verified extensively at the microscopic scale, it is in stark contrast with our everyday experience where an object can only be in one specific state at a time.
The emergence of a classical macroscopic world is not derived from the Schrödinger equation but introduced by the measurement postulate: during a measurement the unitary evolution ceases and the state is projected onto a single outcome.
Neither the moment at which unitary evolution gives way to projection nor the reason why measuring devices are singled-out is specified by the theory; this is the quantum measurement problem.

Spontaneous-collapse models resolve this problem by replacing the measurement postulate with a modification of Schrodinger’s equation. Added nonlinear noise terms steer spatial superpositions continuously toward localized states, with a strength that scales with the system’s mass or complexity. Consequently, the quantum dynamics of microscopic systems is practically unchanged, while macroscopic superpositions are suppressed on experimentally negligible timescales. Such modifications would explain the quantum–to-classical crossover within one unified dynamical law.
Importantly, the collapse dynamics, controlled by a few model parameters, differs from that of standard quantum mechanics, such that it can be verified experimentally. Among these models, the Diósi–Penrose proposal
[
6
,
5
]
plays a central role by explicitly linking collapse to gravity, with self-gravity setting the timescale for the decay of spatial superpositions. In other words, massive superpositions are dynamically localized so that spacetime remains effectively classical.

Efforts to bound collapse-model parameters follow two complementary strategies.
Interferometric
tests are the most direct: they monitor the loss or persistence of fringe visibility as ever more massive objects are prepared in spatial superpositions.
Non-interferometric
tests, by contrast, search for indirect signatures of the same localization noise—momentum diffusion, excess heating, or anomalous radiation—at the cost of additional assumptions about environmental couplings, noise spectra, and electromagnetic response.

So far, the strongest interferometric bounds have come from a bottom-up program in molecular and cold-atom matter-wave interferometry, reaching object masses of order
10^{4}
atomic mass units (amu).
In parallel, stringent non-interferometric limits have been obtained in solid-state platforms by monitoring excess Brownian noise, from cryogenic cantilevers to the mirrors of gravitational-wave detectors. For such optomechanical systems, however, the main obstacle to a direct interferometric test has been the preparation of genuine quantum superpositions. Recent progress in circuit quantum acoustodynamics (cQAD) changes this outlook, enabling a top-down route in which macroscopic solid-state resonators can be prepared in nonclassical motional states, with masses beyond
10^{15}
amu.
In particular, a 16 µg resonator has recently been prepared in a Schrödinger-cat state
[
1
]
.
Up to now, however, most cQAD platforms have operated in the hundreds-of-MHz to GHz range, which directly constrains the accessible spatial separation. For a Schrödinger-cat state

|\Psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}(|\alpha\rangle+|-\alpha\rangle),\qquad(\mathcal{N}\approx\sqrt{2}),

(6)

the distance between the two coherent components of amplitude
\alpha
is

d=2|\alpha|\,X_{\rm zpf},\qquad\text{with}\quad X_{\rm zpf}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega_{m}}},

so at fixed
m
and
\alpha
one has
d\propto\omega_{m}^{-1/2}
.
With the parameters of Ref.
[
1
]
and
|\alpha|^{2}=2.6
, the corresponding separation is
d\approx 1
am.
In our MHz regime
X_{\rm zpf}\approx 1~\mathrm{fm}
, allowing separations of a few femtometres for

|\alpha|^{2}\sim 1-10
. These femtometre-scale separations match nuclear dimensions—the scale first invoked in collapse proposals—and bring such models within direct interferometric reach.
We review below the main collapse models, namely the Diosi-Penrose and CSL frameworks and assess the bounds on the model parameters achievable with our platform.

B.1
cQAD tests of objective collapse models

Although collapse models are often formulated as stochastic, nonlinear modifications of the Schrödinger equation for state vectors, it is convenient to describe the averaged dynamics at the density-matrix level. In this picture, the collapse appears as an additional decoherence term in the Lindblad master equation:

\dot{\bm{\rho}}\;=\;\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm QM}[\bm{\rho}]\;+\;\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm collapse}[\bm{\rho}].

(7)

Requiring linearity, complete positivity, translation invariance, and no superluminal signaling tightly constrains its form. A general Markovian expression is

\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm collapse}[\bm{\rho}]=\,\!\int d^{3}\vec{r}\,d^{3}\vec{r}^{\prime}\;\mathcal{D}(\vec{r}-\vec{r}^{\prime})\,\big[\bm{M}(\vec{r}),\,[\bm{M}(\vec{r}^{\prime}),\,\bm{\rho}]\big],

(8)

where
\bm{M}(\vec{r})
denotes the mass-density operator and
\mathcal{D}
encodes the strength and spatial correlations of the localization field. The double commutator suppresses off-diagonal matrix elements of

\bm{\rho}
between states with different mass-density profiles. The kernel
\mathcal{D}(\vec{r})
specifies the overall strength and spatial resolution of this collapse (only mass-density differences on scales larger than this width are efficiently resolved). Different collapse proposals correspond to different choices of
\mathcal{D}
. We will specify these choices below for the Diósi–Penrose and CSL models.

In an interferometric cQAD test, a mechanical Schrödinger-cat state
|\Psi\rangle
with calibrated separation
d
[
Eq. (
6
)
] is prepared and its coherence is tracked in time. On this platform, the mechanical phonon-number parity, provides a direct readout of fringe visibility, which—under Markovian conditions—decays exponentially with rate

\Gamma_{\rm meas}(d)=\Gamma_{\rm QM}(d)+\Gamma_{\rm collapse}(d;\theta).

(9)

Here
\Gamma_{\rm QM}(d)
denotes the standard decoherence rate predicted by quantum mechanics from environmental couplings (e.g. thermal noise and damping, in the absence of any collapse mechanism), while
\Gamma_{\rm collapse}(d;\theta)
is the model-dependent excess;
By comparing the measured decoherence rate
\Gamma_{\rm meas}(d)
to the independently calibrated value
\Gamma_{\rm QM}(d)
, one can bound
\Gamma_{\rm collapse}(d;\theta)
and thus exclude some regions of the parameter’s model
\theta
.
A key advantage of cQAD is that the cat separation
d
is precisely set by state preparation, allowing controlled scans in
d

d

\Gamma_{\rm collapse}
differs from that of
\Gamma_{\rm QM}
.

For a cat state
|\Psi\rangle
subject only to a thermal Markovian bath (no additional dephasing)—with mechanical energy–relaxation time

\Gamma_{\rm QM}(d)\;\approx\;\frac{(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)}{2T_{1}^{m}}\left(\frac{d}{X_{\rm zpf}}\right)^{\!2}.

(10)

Although this background rate can in principle be calibrated independently—for instance via temperature sweeps and

\Gamma_{\rm collapse}
constitutes a sizeable fraction of the background
\Gamma_{\rm QM}

In the following, we compare the model-dependent collapse rate

\Gamma_{\rm collapse}(d;\theta)
of the CSL and Diósi–Penrose models to this calibrated thermal baseline.

B.3
Exclusion bounds on the Continuous Spontaneous Localization (CSL) model

Several assumptions underlie the Diósi–Penrose proposal—how to coarse-grain the mass density, how strong the putative gravitational noise is, and whether gravity plays any role at all in state-vector reduction. To remain open to alternatives, it is convenient to adopt a theory-agnostic, phenomenological description: Continuous Spontaneous Localization (CSL). Introduced by Pearle and completed in 1990 by Ghirardi, Pearle, and Rimini, CSL assumes a Gaussian correlator without committing to a specific physical origin.

\mathcal{D}_{\rm CSL}(\vec{r})=\frac{\lambda}{m_{0}^{2}}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{|\vec{r}|^{2}}{4r_{C}^{2}}\Big)

(15)

with
m_{0}
the nucleon mass. The two parameters of the CSL model,
\lambda
and
r_{C}
are respectively the localization rate and spatial correlation length. Since the Kernel
\mathcal{D}_{\rm CSL}(\vec{r})
is finite at short distances, one may treat the solid as a sum of pointlike nuclei with mass density
\mu(\vec{r})=\sum_{n}m_{a}\delta(\vec{r}-\vec{r}_{n})
, where
m_{a}
is the nuclear mass and
\vec{r}_{n}
the position of the
n^{\rm th}
nucleon in the lattice.
Under these assumptions, the CSL master equation takes the form
[
9
]
:

\mathcal{L}_{\rm CSL}[\bm{\rho}]=-\,\frac{\lambda}{2\,\pi^{3/2}r_{C}^{3}m_{0}^{2}}\int d^{3}\!\vec{r}\,\big[\bm{m}(\vec{r}),\,[\bm{m}(\vec{r}),\bm{\rho}]\big],

(16)

with the Gaussian-smeared mass operator

\bm{m}(\vec{r})=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\rm nuc}}m_{a}\,\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{|\vec{r}-\vec{\bm{r}}_{n}|^{2}}{2r_{C}^{2}}\Big),

(17)

where
\vec{\bm{r}}_{n}
is the position operator of the
n
-th nucleus.

Phenomenological studies of CSL commonly consider a sub-micron to micron correlation length, with the canonical benchmark
r_{C}=10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}
(GRW) and, more broadly,
r_{C}\gtrsim 100~\mathrm{nm}
. In this regime,
r_{C}
exceeds the ångström-scale lattice spacing of solid-state devices by many orders of magnitude, so a coarse-grained continuum mass density is well justified. Moreover, the relevant displacements—zero-point fluctuations
X_{\rm zpf}
, thermal rms motion, and the cat separations
d
considered below—satisfy
X,d\ll r_{C}
. A small-displacement expansion then yields
[
10
]
:

\mathcal{L}_{\rm CSL}[\bm{\rho}]=\;\lambda\,\frac{\alpha_{\rm geo}(r_{C})}{r_{C}^{2}}\,[\bm{X},[\bm{X},\bm{\rho}]],

(18)

where
\alpha_{\rm geo}(r_{C})
is a geometry factor quantifying how much of the resonator’s mass distribution falls within the localization volume.

Writing the displacement in terms of ladder operators
\bm{X}=X_{\rm zpf}(\bm{a}+\bm{a}^{\dagger})
, and discarding counter-rotating terms,
Eq. (
18
)
reduces to an
effective phase-insensitive (infinite-temperature) bath:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm CSL}[\bm{\rho}]

\displaystyle=\kappa\big(\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{a}]+\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{a}^{\dagger}]\big),

(19a)

\displaystyle\text{with\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,}\kappa

\displaystyle=2\,\lambda\,\alpha_{\rm geo}(r_{C})\,\frac{X_{\rm zpf}^{2}}{r_{C}^{2}},

(19b)

and
\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{L}]\bm{\rho}=\bm{L}\bm{\rho}\bm{L}^{\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{L},\bm{\rho}\}
.
The associated heating (increase of effective temperature) is the standard observable in optomechanical, non-interferometric CSL tests
[
6
]
.

In a cQED scenario, the cat state of
Eq. (
6
)
subject to the CSL bath of
Eq. (
19a
)
looses coherence at rate
2\kappa|\alpha|^{2}
. Using
d=2|\alpha|X_{\rm zpf}
, we obtain

\Gamma_{\rm CSL}(d)=\lambda\,\alpha_{\rm geo}(r_{C})\!\left(\frac{d}{r_{C}}\right)^{2}\qquad(d\ll r_{C}).

(20)

In this small separation regime, the CSL dynamics effectively appears as an excess-temperature bath. As a result,
\Gamma_{\rm CSL}
and the thermal background
\Gamma_{\rm QM}
have the same
d^{2}
scaling, so the CSL contribution can only be isolated via a careful calibration of the thermal background (e.g., temperature dependence). Quantitatively, if
\epsilon
denotes the fractional uncertainty of the calibrated background, the experiment excludes the CSL parameter region where

B.4
Conclusion

Advancing cQAD into the MHz regime with long

and large

enables
interferometric
tests of objective-collapse dynamics.
For the Diósi–Penrose (DP) model,
nuclear-scale
spatial separations become directly accessible, offering a strong, model-specific experimental signature.
For Continuous Spontaneous Localization (CSL), the experiment probes the
small-separation
regime
d\!\ll\!r_{C}
, where the collapse field manifests as an effectively phase-insensitive excess-temperature bath.
Importantly, these measurements would provide the first tests using genuine quantum superpositions entering the relevant parameter range.

S1.1
Qubit design and fabrication

The fluxonium qubit is fabricated on a 280 µm intrinsic Si wafer. All superconducting wiring (except for junctions) is defined from a sputtered 200 nm Nb film by wafer-scale UV-laser lithography (S1805 resist) and dry etching. Aluminum spacers (600 nm tall) set the nominal flip-chip gap and are patterned via a bilayer resist (LOR20B/AZ 5214E), UV lithography, Al evaporation, and lift-off. Josephson junctions are formed using a Dolan-bridge bilayer (MMA EL13/PMMA A3) and double-angle Al evaporation (
\pm 22^{\circ}
) followed by NMP liftoff.
After fabrication, the wafer is diced, and the chips are wire-bounded to a printed-circuit board. Qubit control is achieved via a capacitively coupled waveguide (cyan in
Fig.
1
(b)
); readout employs a
\lambda/4
resonator (5.59 GHz, light pink in
Fig.
1
(b)
) in series with an inductively coupled
\lambda/4
Purcell filter (5.92 GHz); flux biasing uses an on-chip flux line (brown in
Fig.
1
(b)
) for fine tuning near
\varphi_{\rm ext}=\pi
plus an external coil for coarse adjustments.

S1.2
Membrane design and fabrication

The mechanical resonator is fabricated from a 350 µm-thick (100) Si wafer coated on both sides with 100 nm of high-stress SiN (
\gtrsim 800
MPa). On the backside, a rectangular opening is defined in the SiN by UV lithography (AZ 5214E) and RIE, and the silicon is then anisotropically etched in 30 % KOH at 85 °C until the membrane is released.
On the front side, SiN is removed by UV lithography (AZ 5214E) and RIE everywhere except around the membrane and bias-pad. A second KOH etch (same chemistry, timed to remove
\approx
25 µm of silicon) recesses the substrate everywhere except in this zone, reducing the area of close proximity between the two chips and mitigating the risk of a dust particle compromising the flip-chip process.
The wafer is then immersed in 10 % HF for 5 min to remove surface oxide and uniformly thin down the released SiN to 90 nm. Finally, UV lithography and evaporation of 30 nm Al (followed by lift-off) define the central membrane electrode and the square bias pad (
Fig.
S6
). Room-temperature Michelson interferometry (prior to metallization) reveals the fundamental mode at 2.54 MHz, the first asymmetric mode at 3.75 MHz, and the second asymmetric mode, which is used in this work, at 4.30 MHz. For this mode, we observed a room-temperature quality factor before metallization of
Q\approx 6\times 10^{4}
.

S1.3
Flip-Chip Assembly

The flip-chip assembly is performed using an
MJB4
mask aligner for micropositioning and a custom-built holder.
The chips are aligned with an estimated accuracy of 5 µm and brought into contact.
Bonding is achieved using drops of
Dymax OP-67-LC
UV-curing glue at the corners of the membrane chip.
The aluminum spacers on the qubit chip nominally set the inter-chip gap to 600 nm.
While room-temperature Fizeau interferometry on unmetalized test samples confirmed this spacing, cryogenic qubit spectroscopy consistently reveals a gap of 1–3 µm, which we attribute to differential bending under thermo–mechanical stress.

S2
Hamiltonian derivation

S2.1
Effective mass

In this section, we derive the effective mass of the vibrational mode.
The membrane is a rectangular suspended film of width
L_{y}=110~\upmu\mathrm{m}
, length
L_{z}=140~\upmu\mathrm{m}
, and thickness
t=90~\mathrm{nm}
.
Including the added mass of the
30~\mathrm{nm}
-thick aluminum electrode with area
90~\upmu\mathrm{m}\times 120~\upmu\mathrm{m}
, the physical mass
M=5.3~\mathrm{ng}
is obtained using the mass densities of silicon nitride
\rho_{\text{SiN}}=3200~\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{m}^{3}
and aluminum
\rho_{\text{Al}}=2700~\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{m}^{3}
.
The membrane supports a family of out-of-plane vibrational modes, indexed by the number of antinodes
(m,n)
in the lateral and longitudinal directions, with the displacement profile
u_{m,n}(y,z)
given by

\lambda\epsilon(y,z)\sin\left(m\pi\frac{y\!-\!L_{y}/2}{L_{y}}\right)\sin\left(n\pi\frac{z\!-\!L_{z}/2}{L_{z}}\right).

(S22)

Here,
\epsilon(y,z)
is a regularization function which, for thin films under high tensile stress
\sigma_{\text{SiN}}
, equals unity everywhere except in an exponentially small region near the clamping edges
[
33
]

y=\pm L_{y}/2
and
z=\pm L_{z}/2
. While this factor is crucial to capture mechanical damping in the membrane
[
33
]
, it has negligible effect on the motional mass and will henceforth be set to unity.
As the mode profiles (
S22
) are solutions of the linear elasticity equations, there is a freedom in choosing the normalization constant
\lambda
, leading to an ambiguity in the motional mass
m_{\lambda}
.
The kinetic energy of a single (2, 1)-mode with amplitude
X
is given by

T=\frac{1}{2}\dot{X}^{2}\iint\rho_{\text{s}}\,u_{2,1}^{2}(y,z)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}z,

(S23)

where
\rho_{\text{s}}
is the surface mass density of the membrane.
Identifying to the harmonic oscillator kinetic energy
T=\frac{1}{2}m_{\lambda}\dot{X}^{2}
leads to the effective mass

m_{\lambda}=\iint\rho_{\text{s}}\,u_{2,1}^{2}(y,z)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}z=M\,\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}.

(S24)

While any normalization factor
\lambda
is admissible provided the effective mass is redefined according to

Eq. (
S24
)
, a natural choice, given the electrostatic coupling scheme employed in this work, is to choose
\lambda
such that

\iint_{\mathcal{A}}u_{2,1}(y,z)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}z=\mathcal{A},

(S25)

where
\mathcal{A}
denotes the area of overlap in each of the capacitors
C_{m}^{\pm}
. This choice validates the parallel-plate approximation for small displacements
X
, yielding

C_{m}^{\pm}(X)\simeq C_{m}\bigl(1\mp X/d\bigr).

(S26)

Indeed,

\displaystyle C_{m}^{\pm}(X)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathcal{A}}\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\,d\pm X\,u_{2,1}(y,z)\,}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}z

(S27)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\epsilon_{0}\mathcal{A}}{d}\left(1\mp\frac{X}{d\mathcal{A}}\iint_{\mathcal{A}}u_{2,1}(y,z)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}z\right).

With this normalization one finds
\lambda=1.3
and hence

m_{\lambda}=M\,\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4}=2.3~\mathrm{ng}.

This effective mass corresponds to the mass of the vibrational mode as seen by the fluxonium electrodes.
The second-order term in the expansion (
S27
) differs from the parallel-plate approximation by
5.5\%
and will be neglected in further analytical calculations.

S2.2
Circuit quantization

Figure S7:

Equivalent electrical circuit of the flip-chip device.

Schematic diagram showing the equivalent circuit and defining the component symbols and node labels used throughout the text.

Qubit–membrane coupling is achieved via the two capacitors
[

]

C_{m}^{+}

The fluxonium qubit consists of a floating superconducting loop that is symmetrically coupled to ground through two identical capacitors
C_{g}
.
The electrical schematic is shown in
Fig.
S7
, where any additional parasitic capacitances have been neglected.
The dynamics are described by the membrane displacement coordinate
X
and the vector of flux-node coordinates

\vec{\Phi}^{T}=\bigl(\Phi_{1},\;\Phi_{2},\;\Phi_{3}\bigr),

(S28)

where
\Phi_{1}
is the flux across the qubit, and
\Phi_{2,3}
denote the fluxes at the coupling nodes (see
Fig.
S7
).

We express the total Lagrangian as the sum of mechanical and electrical contributions:

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mec}}(X,\dot{X})+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{elec}}(\Phi_{1},\dot{\vec{\Phi}},X)\,,

(S29)

where overdots indicate time derivatives.
The membrane Lagrangian is that of a harmonic oscillator:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mec}}=\frac{1}{2}\,m\,\dot{X}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\,m\,\omega_{m}^{2}\,X^{2},

(S30)

with
m
the effective mass of the membrane and
\omega_{m}
its resonance frequency.
The electrical Lagrangian is split into kinetic and potential parts,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{elec}}=T_{\mathrm{elec}}-V_{\mathrm{elec}}
:

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{elec}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{g}}{2}\Bigl(\dot{\Phi}_{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\Phi}_{1}\Bigr)^{2}+\frac{C_{g}}{2}\Bigl(\dot{\Phi}_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\Phi}_{1}\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{C_{m}^{+}}{2}\Bigl(\dot{\Phi}_{3}-\dot{\Phi}_{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\Phi}_{1}\Bigr)^{2}+\frac{C_{m}^{-}}{2}\Bigl(\dot{\Phi}_{3}-\dot{\Phi}_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\dot{\Phi}_{1}\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{C_{b}}{2}\bigl(V_{b}-\dot{\Phi}_{3}\bigr)^{2}+\frac{C_{q}}{2}\,\dot{\Phi}_{1}^{2},

(S31)

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{elec}}

\displaystyle=-E_{J}\cos\bigl(2\pi\Phi_{q}/\Phi_{0}\bigr)+\frac{\Phi_{1}^{2}}{2L}.

(S32)

Here,
C_{g}
is the ground capacitance,
C_{b}
the membrane bias capacitance,
C_{q}
the qubit shunt capacitance,
V_{b}
the bias voltage,
E_{J}
the Josephson energy,
\Phi_{0}=h/2e
the flux quantum, and
L
the inductance of the fluxonium.

For the Legendre transform we rewrite
T_{\mathrm{elec}}
in matrix form:

T=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}\dot{\Phi}_{i}\,M_{ij}(X)\,\dot{\Phi}_{j}-C_{b}\,V_{b}\,\dot{\Phi}_{3},

(S33)

with the position-dependent capacitance matrix

M(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{1}{2}\,(C_{g}+C_{m}^{\Sigma}+2C_{q})&C_{m}^{\Delta}&-C_{m}^{\Delta}\\[4.0pt]
C_{m}^{\Delta}&2\,(C_{g}+C_{m}^{\Sigma})&-2\,C_{m}^{\Sigma}\\[4.0pt]
-C_{m}^{\Delta}&-2\,C_{m}^{\Sigma}&C_{b}+2\,C_{m}^{\Sigma}\end{pmatrix},

with
C_{m}^{\Sigma}\equiv(C_{m}^{+}+C_{m}^{-})/2
and
C_{m}^{\Delta}\equiv(C_{m}^{+}-C_{m}^{-})/2
are symmetric and antisymmetric combinations of the membrane-qubit capacitances.
The canonical flux-momenta are defined by
Q_{k}=\partial T/\partial\dot{\Phi}_{k}
, where
k=1,2,3
.
This yields

\dot{\Phi}_{k}=\sum M_{ki}^{-1}\,\widetilde{Q}_{i}\,,\text{ where }\widetilde{Q}_{i}=Q_{i}+C_{b}\,V_{b}\,\delta_{i3}

(S34)

Here
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta.
The membrane momentum is simply
P_{X}=m\,\dot{X}
.
Performing the Legendre transform,
H=\sum_{i=1}^{3}Q_{i}\,\dot{\Phi}_{i}+P_{X}\,\dot{X}-\mathcal{L}
, yields the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}\widetilde{Q}_{i}\,M^{-1}_{ij}\,\widetilde{Q}_{j}-E_{J}\cos\bigl(2\pi\Phi_{1}/\Phi_{0}\bigr)+\frac{\Phi_{1}^{2}}{2L}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{P_{X}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}\,m\,\omega_{m}^{2}\,X^{2}.

(S35)

Since
\mathcal{L}
[
Eq. (
S29
)
] does not depend on
\Phi_{2}
or
\Phi_{3}
, these coordinates are cyclic and their conjugate charges are strictly conserved by Noether’s theorem.
Physically, these conserved quantities correspond to the static charges on the fluxonium and membrane islands, and may therefore be treated as fixed scalar parameters in the subsequent analysis.

We quantize the system by introducing the canonical variables for the qubit phase
\varphi
and Cooper pair number
n
, along with the dimensionless canonical variables for the membrane position
x
, momentum
p
and
\bm{a}
(
\bm{a}^{\dagger}
) the mechanical annihilation (creation) operators:

\displaystyle\bm{\varphi}

\displaystyle=2\pi\frac{\bm{\Phi}_{1}}{\Phi_{0}},

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{X}}{2\,X_{\mathrm{zpf}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{a}+\bm{a}^{\dagger}}{2},

(S36)

\displaystyle\bm{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{Q}_{1}}{2e},

\displaystyle\qquad\bm{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bm{P}}{2P_{\mathrm{zpf}}}

\displaystyle=i\frac{\bm{a}^{\dagger}-\bm{a}}{2},

where
X_{\mathrm{zpf}}=\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega_{m})}
and
P_{\mathrm{zpf}}=\sqrt{\hbar m\omega_{m}/2}
are the membrane zero-point fluctuations of position and momentum, respectively.
These variables satisfy the commutation relations
[\bm{\varphi},\bm{n}]=i
and
[\bm{x},\bm{p}]=i/2
, and operators from different sets commute.
The capacitance matrix depends on the membrane position,
\bm{M}^{-1}_{ij}=\bm{M}^{-1}_{ij}(\bm{x})
, and must therefore be treated as an operator.
In this basis the Hamiltonian assumes the familiar form for a fluxonium coupled to a harmonic mode:

\displaystyle\bm{H}

\displaystyle=4\,\bm{E}_{C}(\bm{x})\bigl[\bm{n}+\bm{n}_{g}(\bm{x})\bigr]^{2}-E_{J}\cos\bm{\varphi}_{q}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,E_{L}\,\bm{\varphi}_{q}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\hbar\omega_{m}\bigl(\bm{p}^{2}+\bm{x}^{2}\bigr)+\bm{F}_{m}(\bm{x}),

(S37)

where

\displaystyle E_{L}=\left(\frac{\Phi_{0}}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{L},\quad\bm{E}_{C}(\bm{x})=\frac{e^{2}}{2}\,\bm{M}^{-1}_{11}(\bm{x}),

\displaystyle\bm{n}_{g}(\bm{x})=\frac{e}{4\,\bm{E}_{C}(\bm{x})}\sum_{i=2}^{3}\widetilde{Q}_{i}\,\bm{M}^{-1}_{1i}(\bm{x}).

This form of the Hamiltonian shows that the coupling between the fluxonium and the membrane induces an additional charge
\bm{n}_{g}(\bm{x})
on the fluxonium.
The membrane also experiences an additional electrostatic potential
\bm{H}_{\mathrm{ms}}(\bm{x})
, which, as will be shown later, leads to a reduction of the membrane frequency.
The general form of
\bm{H}_{\mathrm{ms}}
is given by

\bm{H}_{\mathrm{ms}}=4\bm{E}_{C}\bm{n}_{g}^{2}+\bm{F}_{m}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=2}^{3}\widetilde{Q}_{i}\,\bm{M}^{-1}_{ij}\,\widetilde{Q}_{j}.

(S38)

S2.3
Qubit–membrane coupling

For small membrane displacements we perform a first-order expansion of the coupling Hamiltonian in the dimensionless coordinate
\bm{x}
, yielding

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{coupling}}

\displaystyle=8\,\bm{E}_{C}(\bm{x})\,\bm{n}_{g}(\bm{x})

(S39)

\displaystyle\approx\;4E_{C}\,\frac{\partial C_{m}^{+}}{\partial x}\,\frac{V_{\text{eff}}(V_{b})}{e}\,\bm{n}\,\bm{x}\,,

Here,
E_{C}=e^{2}/(C_{g}+C_{m}+2C_{q})
is the unperturbed capacitive energy of the fluxonium (neglecting all terms beyond linear order in
x
), and

\frac{\partial C_{m}^{\pm}}{\partial x}=2\,X_{\mathrm{zpf}}\,\frac{\partial C_{m}^{\pm}}{\partial X}

relates the derivative with respect to the dimensionless coordinate
\bm{x}
back to the physical membrane displacement
X
.
The effective bias,
V_{\text{eff}}(V_{b})
, is given by:

\displaystyle V_{\text{eff}}(V_{b})

\displaystyle=\frac{\,C_{g}\,V_{b}-Q_{2}/2+\,(C_{g}/C_{b})\,Q_{3}}{C_{g}+C_{m}+2\,(C_{g}\,C_{m}/C_{b})}

(S40)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\;\frac{C_{g}\,V_{b}-Q_{2}/2}{C_{g}+C_{m}}\,,

where the last expression indicates the limit
C_{g}/C_{b}\!\ll\!1
.
This indicates that trapped charges,
Q_{2}
and
Q_{3}
, act as an effective bias on the qubit.
Introducing the dilution factor
\beta=C_{g}/(C_{g}+C_{m})
and the offset voltage
V_{\text{offset}}=Q_{2}/2C_{g}
, we have

V_{\text{eff}}(V_{b})=\beta(V_{b}-V_{\text{offset}})

(S41)

Projecting onto the lowest two levels of the fluxonium and invoking the rotating-wave approximation yields the Jaynes–Cummings Hamiltonian,

\bm{H}_{JC}/\hbar=\omega_{m}\,\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}+\frac{\omega_{q}}{2}\,\bm{\sigma}_{z}+i\,\frac{\Omega}{2}\,\bigl(\bm{a}^{\dagger}\,\bm{\sigma}_{-}-\bm{\sigma}_{+}\,\bm{a}\bigr),

(S42)

where the effective vacuum Rabi frequency is

\Omega=\omega_{q}\,\bigl|\langle g|\bm{\varphi}|e\rangle\bigr|\,\frac{dC_{m}^{+}}{dx}\,\frac{V_{\text{eff}}}{2e}\,.

(S43)

Here,
\omega_{q}
is the qubit transition frequency and
\bm{\sigma}_{\pm}

S2.4
Electrostatic spring softening

A bias voltage
V_{b}
on the membrane produces an electrostatic potential
\bm{H}_{ms}
that effectively softens the mechanical spring and lowers the resonant frequency.
To capture this effect, we expand
\bm{H}_{\mathrm{ms}}
to second order in the dimensionless displacement
\bm{x}
, express it in terms of the ladder operators and retain only energy-conserving terms.
The membrane Hamiltonian then becomes

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{\mathrm{membrane}}

\displaystyle=\hbar\,\omega_{m}\bigl(\bm{a}^{\dagger}\,\bm{a}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)\;+\;\bm{H}_{ms}

(S45)

\displaystyle=\hbar\,\bigl(\omega_{m}+\delta\omega_{m}\bigr)\bigl(\bm{a}^{\dagger}\,\bm{a}+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)\,,

where the frequency shift is

\delta\omega_{m}=-\zeta(V_{b}-V_{\text{offset}})^{2}

(S46)

with

\zeta=2\,C_{m}\,(C_{g}+2C_{q})\,\frac{E_{C}}{\hbar e^{2}}\,\frac{X_{\mathrm{zpf}}^{2}}{d^{2}}\,\beta^{2}.

(S47)

Here we have used the parallel-plate model
C_{m}^{\pm}(X)={C_{m}}/(1\pm X/d)
to calculate the derivatives
{dC_{m}^{\pm}}/{dX}
and
{d^{2}C_{m}^{\pm}}/{dX^{2}}
.

The electrostatic force thus acts as a “negative spring”, lowering the membrane’s resonance frequency.
In
Fig.
2
(a)
we plot this softening and extract a tuning coefficient

\zeta/2\pi=-0.980\,\mathrm{Hz}/\mathrm{V}^{2}.

By combining
Eq. (
S47
)
with the capacitance
C_{m}(d)
extracted from our ANSYS Q3D simulations and the gap
d
, independently determined (with its uncertainty) in
Sec.
S2.6
, we obtain

\zeta_{\rm th}/2\pi=-1.14(42)\,\mathrm{Hz}/\mathrm{V}^{2}.

Because
\zeta\propto d^{-3}
, this parameter-free agreement between
\zeta
and
\zeta_{\rm th}
provides a stringent consistency check on our membrane–qubit separation.

S2.5
Offset voltage discussion

In our measurements, we consistently observed a residual static charge on the membrane, which offsets the voltage bias required to cancel the electrostatic force by an amount
V_{\mathrm{offset}}
(see Fig 2a).
This offset arises from trapped charges
Q_{2}
and
Q_{3}
on the islands between the capacitors (see
Eq. (
S40
)
).
Although the qubit remains operational for a DC-bias voltage up to
|V_{b}|\lesssim 9\,\mathrm{V}
, the effective coupling strength depends on the difference
V_{b}-V_{\mathrm{offset}}
.
Therefore, the presence of this offset can be used to increase the coupling strength.

We found that
V_{\mathrm{offset}}
slowly drifts toward the applied bias over a timescale of weeks.
To restore the maximal coupling range, we deliberately “reset” the offset by applying a large negative bias of
V_{b}=-20\,\mathrm{V}
to the membrane for several days.
While this high voltage temporarily disables measurements, it reliably shifts
V_{\mathrm{offset}}
closer to
-9\,\mathrm{V}
.
After this procedure, we verify the new offset value using Ramsey-type membrane spectroscopy (see
Fig.
2
(a)
) and ensure that it lies within the desired measurement range.

3

This bosonic mode, at

\displaystyle\bm{H}

\displaystyle=4E_{C}\,\bm{n}^{2}-E_{J}\cos\bigl(\bm{\varphi}-\varphi_{\mathrm{ext}}\bigr)+\tfrac{1}{2}E_{L}\,\bm{\varphi}^{2}

(S49)

\displaystyle+\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{chain}}\,\bm{b}^{\dagger}\bm{b}+\hbar g_{qc}\,(\bm{b}^{\dagger}+\bm{b})\,\bm{n}.

\{|g\rangle,|e\rangle\}\to\{|f\rangle,|h\rangle\}

\{|g\rangle,|e\rangle\}\to\{|f\rangle,|h\rangle\}

Although incorporating additional chain modes could further refine the theory-experiment agreement, we have chosen to keep a single chain-mode in the fit to keep the model tractable.
We also provide a best fit, where the
|g\rangle\to|e\rangle
transition frequency is constrained around
2.35~\mathrm{MHz}
(dashed lines in

The lowest
|g\rangle\!\leftrightarrow\!|e\rangle
transition of our fluxonium lies at
\omega_{q}/2\pi=2.35\text{ MHz}
, while the mechanical mode sits at
\omega_{m}/2\pi=4.4\text{ MHz}
.
By applying a continuous drive at
\omega_{d}/2\pi=3.463\text{ GHz}
—exactly midway between the
\omega_{\text{ef}}
and
\omega_{\text{gh}}
lines—we induce an AC–Stark shift that pulls the dressed qubit into resonance with the mechanics, at the cost of an increased dephasing rate (see
Sec.
S3.5
).
To describe this driven system we therefore add a time-dependent charge-drive term to the fluxonium Hamiltonian:

H=H_{\text{fluxonium}}+\hbar\Omega_{d}\cos(\omega_{d}t)\bm{n},

(S50)

where
\bm{H_{\text{fluxonium}}}
is the undriven fluxonium Hamiltonian and
\Omega_{d}
is the coupling of the charge drive to the qubit.
We restrict our attention to transitions near
\omega_{d}
and retain only the lowest four fluxonium levels,
\{|g\rangle,|h\rangle,|e\rangle,|f\rangle\}
.
Projecting the driving term of the Hamiltoninan into this four-level subspace, and considering only non-zero matrix elements at
\varphi_{\mathrm{ext}}=\pi
yields

\bm{H}_{\mathrm{Stark}}=\hbar\Omega_{d}\cos{\omega_{d}t}\begin{pmatrix}0&n_{\text{gh}}&n_{\text{ge}}&0\\
n_{\text{gh}}^{*}&0&0&0\\
n_{\text{ge}}^{*}&0&0&n_{\text{ef}}\\
0&0&n_{\text{ef}}^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}

(S51)

where
n_{ij}=\langle i|\bm{n}|j\rangle
is the charge matrix element.
We then transform into the frame rotating at
\omega_{d}
via the unitary transformation

\bm{U}=\mathrm{diag}\!\begin{pmatrix}1&e^{i\omega_{d}t}&e^{i\omega_{\text{ge}}t}&e^{i(\omega_{d}+\omega_{\mathrm{ge}})t}\end{pmatrix}.

(S52)

In the rotated frame, dropping all fast rotating terms (beyond or close to
\omega_{d}
), the Hamiltonian writes

\bm{H}^{\text{int}}=\hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&\Omega_{d}n_{\text{gh}}/2&0&0\\
\Omega_{d}n_{\text{gh}}^{*}/2&\Delta_{\text{gh}}&0&0\\
0&0&0&\Omega_{d}n_{\text{ef}}/2\\
0&0&\Omega_{d}n_{\text{ef}}^{*}/2&\Delta_{\mathrm{ef}}\end{pmatrix},

(S53)

with
\Delta_{\text{gh}}=\omega_{\text{gh}}-\omega_{d}
and
\Delta_{\text{ef}}=\omega_{\text{ef}}-\omega_{d}
.
This Hamiltonian is block-diagonal, such that it can be diagonalized separately in the two subspaces
\{|g\rangle,|h\rangle\}
and
\{|e\rangle,|f\rangle\}
.
The two lowest energy dressed eigenstates are

\displaystyle|\tilde{g}\rangle

\displaystyle=\cos{\frac{\Theta_{\text{gh}}}{2}}|g\rangle-\sin{\frac{\Theta_{\text{gh}}}{2}}|h\rangle\,,

(S54)

\displaystyle|\tilde{e}\rangle

\displaystyle=\cos{\frac{\Theta_{\text{ef}}}{2}}|e\rangle-\sin{\frac{\Theta_{\text{ef}}}{2}}|f\rangle\,,

with mixing angles defined by
\tan\Theta_{\text{gh}}=\Omega_{d}n_{\text{gh}}/|\Delta_{\text{gh}}|
and
\tan\Theta_{\text{ef}}=\Omega_{d}n_{\text{ef}}/|\Delta_{\text{ef}}|
.
The eigenerergies are given by

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{g}/\hbar

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\text{sign}\bigl(\Delta_{\rm gh}\bigr)\,\bigl(|\Delta_{\rm gh}|-\sqrt{\Delta_{\rm gh}^{2}+\Omega_{d}^{2}\,n_{\rm gh}^{2}}\bigr)\,,

(S55)

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{e}/\hbar

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\text{sign}\bigl(\Delta_{\rm ef}\bigr)\,\bigl(|\Delta_{\rm ef}|-\sqrt{\Delta_{\rm ef}^{2}+\Omega_{d}^{2}\,n_{\rm ef}^{2}}\bigr)\,.

For the circuit at half-flux,
\varphi_{\mathrm{ext}}=\pi
, one finds
n_{\text{gh}}\simeq n_{\text{ef}}\equiv n_{\text{up}}
, furthermore, we have chosen a drive frequency precisely midway between
\omega_{\text{gh}}
and
\omega_{\text{ef}}
yielding
\Delta_{\text{gh}}=-\Delta_{\text{ef}}\equiv\Delta_{\text{st}}
, and
\Theta_{\text{gh}}\simeq\Theta_{\text{ef}}\equiv\Theta
.
In this approximation, the AC Stark shift is given by

\displaystyle\delta\omega_{q}

\displaystyle\equiv\tilde{E}_{e}-\tilde{E}_{g}

(S56)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\Delta_{\text{st}}^{2}+\Omega_{d}^{2}n_{\text{up}}^{2}}-\Delta_{\text{st}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\Omega_{d}^{2}n_{\text{up}}^{2}}{2\Delta_{\mathrm{st}}}\left(1-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\Omega_{d}^{2}n_{\text{up}}^{2}}{\Delta_{\mathrm{st}}^{2}}\right).

In the last line, we expanded the expression to fourth order in
\Omega_{d}/\Delta_{\mathrm{st}}
.

Since the absolute attenuation between source and sample is unknown, we calibrate the AC–Stark shift by Ramsey interferometry (
Fig.
S12
(a)
).
Beginning at the fluxonium sweet-spot frequency
\omega_{q}/2\pi\approx 2.35
MHz, we apply a continuous tone at
\omega_{d}/2\pi=3.463
GHz with varying source power
P
.
A
\pi/2
–Ramsey sequence maps the drive-induced phase into
\sigma_{z}
, from which we extract the dressed qubit frequency.
Plotting this frequency versus
P
reveals a primarily linear Stark pull plus a small quadratic correction (
Fig.
S12
(c)
).
Because
Eq. (
S56
)
fixes the ratio of the linear and quadratic terms, only one free parameter—the scaling factor between
P
and the on-chip Rabi rate
\Omega_{d}
—is required to fit all the data.
Over the accessible power range the qubit frequency tunes continuously from 2.35 MHz up to
\sim
5 MHz (more than one octave), enabling resonance with the 4.4 MHz mechanical mode.
The resulting single-parameter fit (solid line,
Fig.
S12
(c)
) quantitatively reproduces both slope and curvature of the Stark shift.

states into the lower dressed states—an effect analogous to dressed-state spontaneous emission in optical tweezers (see
Eq. (
S54
)
). If the bare-manifold decay rates are
\Gamma_{1}^{ge}
and
\Gamma_{1}^{fh}
, then the dressed-state relaxation rate is

\Gamma_{1}(\delta\omega_{q})=\cos^{2}\!\bigl(\tfrac{\Theta}{2}\bigr)\,\Gamma_{1}^{ge}+\sin^{2}\!\bigl(\tfrac{\Theta}{2}\bigr)\,\Gamma_{1}^{fh}\,.

For small mixing angle
\Theta\approx\delta\omega_{q}/\Delta_{\rm st}
, expanding to first order gives

\Gamma_{1}(\delta\omega_{q})\approx\Gamma_{1}^{ge}+\frac{\Gamma_{1}^{fh}}{2\,\Delta_{\rm st}}\,\delta\omega_{q}\,.

(S57)

The dashed blue line in
Fig.
S13
plots
Eq. (
S58
)
using the independently measured value
\Gamma_{1}^{fh}/2\pi=90
kHz, leaving only
\Gamma_{1}^{ge}
as a fit parameter.

We attribute the enhanced dephasing rate
\Gamma_{2}^{*}(\omega_{q}^{\mathrm{st}})
to amplitude fluctuations of the Stark drive
\Omega_{d}
, yielding

\Gamma_{\phi}=\frac{d(\delta\omega_{q})}{d\Omega_{d}}\,\delta\Omega_{d}\simeq 2\,\delta\omega_{q}\,\frac{\delta\Omega_{d}}{\Omega_{d}}\,,

(S58)

where the approximation holds for
\Omega_{d}\ll\Delta_{\rm st}
. Assuming classical amplitude noise (i.e. constant
\delta\Omega_{d}/\Omega_{d}
), fitting this expression to the data (yellow dashed line in
Fig.
S13
) gives
\delta\Omega_{d}/\Omega_{d}\approx 0.6\%
, in good qualitative agreement with the
\sim 0.2\%
specification of our Anapico source.

S4
Spectrum analyzer theory

S4.1
Quantum harmonic oscillator spectrum

The mechanical system is modeled as a harmonic oscillator, with annihilation operator
\bm{a}
, coupled to a thermal bath at rate
\kappa_{m}
yielding a mean thermal phonon number
n_{\mathrm{th}}
.
The Hamiltonian and loss operators capturing the system dynamics can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{m}/\hbar=\left(\omega_{m}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a},

\displaystyle\bm{L}_{m,\downarrow}=\sqrt{\kappa_{m}(n_{th}+1)}\,\bm{a},\quad\bm{L}_{m,\uparrow}=\sqrt{\kappa_{m}\,n_{th}}\,\bm{a}^{\dagger}.

The evolution of the system is modeled by the Lindblad equation
\dot{\bm{\rho}}=\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m}\bm{\rho}
, with

\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m}\cdot=-i[\bm{H},\cdot]+\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{L}_{m,\downarrow}]\cdot+\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{L}_{m,\uparrow}]\cdot

(S59)

the Lindbladian.

Closely following the reasoning of ref.
[
25
]
, we’ll discuss in this part the quantum noise spectrum.
Similarly to classical physics, the noise spectrum of the Heisenberg picture position operator
\bm{x}(t)=\bm{x}e^{\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m}t}
can be written as

\mathcal{S}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dt\;e^{i\omega t}\langle\bm{x}(t)\bm{x}(0)\rangle.

(S60)

In general, for
t\neq 0
, the commutator
[\bm{x}(t),\bm{x}(0)]
is a complex non-zero quantity.
This reflects the fact that the measurement of
\bm{x}(t)
is necessarily impacted by the back-action of the measurement of
\bm{x}(0)
.
Consequently, the complex-valued auto-correlator
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}(t)=\langle\bm{x}(t)\bm{x}(0)\rangle
is a purely mathematical construction that cannot be evaluated by a simple continuous measurement of
\bm{x}(t)
.
Indeed, in a practical measurement setting, the added quantum noises ensure that the measured quantities
\bm{x}(t)
commute with each other at all times
t
, resulting in real-valued correlators.
Nevertheless, the complex-nature of
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}(t)
, which results in a frequency asymmetric spectrum
S_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}(\omega)
, has a profound physical meaning.
We can gain insights by developing
\bm{x}(t)=(\bm{a}^{\dagger}+\bm{a})/2
, in it’s elementary components and dropping vanishing terms

\langle\bm{x}(t)\bm{x}(0)\rangle=\frac{1}{4}\Bigl(\langle\bm{a}(t)\bm{a}^{\dagger}(0)\rangle+\langle\bm{a}^{\dagger}(t)\bm{a}(0)\rangle\Bigr).

(S61)

The first correlator
\langle\bm{a}(t)\bm{a}^{\dagger}(0)\rangle
can be understood as the amplitude of probability in a fictitious experiment where an excitation would be recovered at time
t
after being added to the oscillator at time
0
.
Therefore, the noise spectrum associated to this correlator is the absorption spectrum of the mechanical mode.
Similarly, the noise spectrum associated to
\langle\bm{a}^{\dagger}(t)\bm{a}(0)\rangle
is the emission spectrum of the mechanical mode, where an excitation is removed from the oscillator at time
0
and re-injected at time
t
.
Since the mechanical mode is coupled to a bath, both correlators tend to
0
exponentially as the excitation can be lost in the interval.
Computing this correlators explicitly, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\bm{x}(t)\bm{x}(0)\rangle=\frac{1}{4}\Bigl(

\displaystyle\langle\bm{a}^{\dagger}(0)\bm{a}(0)\rangle e^{-i\omega_{m}t}+

(S62)

\displaystyle+\langle\bm{a}(0)\bm{a}^{\dagger}(0)\rangle e^{i\omega_{m}t}\Bigr)e^{-\frac{\kappa_{m}}{2}|t|}.

Which in turn yields the spectra

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}(\omega)=\mathcal{S}_{\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}}(\omega)+\mathcal{S}_{\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\dagger}}(\omega)

(S63)

with

\displaystyle S_{\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{\mathrm{th}}\kappa_{m}}{(\omega-\omega_{m})^{2}+(\kappa_{m}/2)^{2}},

(S64)

\displaystyle S_{\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\dagger}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{(n_{\mathrm{th}}+1)\kappa_{m}}{(\omega+\omega_{m})^{2}+(\kappa_{m}/2)^{2}},

where the negative and positive frequency parts corresponds to the emission and absorption spectrum, respectively.
Crucially, the total spectrum
\mathcal{S}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}(\omega)
is not symmetric-in-frequency and cannot be obtained from the correlator of a real-valued time-dependent observable
\langle\bm{A}(t)\bm{A}(0)\rangle
.
Instead, we separately measure the real part of the autocorrelation functions
\mathrm{Re}(\mathcal{C}_{\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}})
and
\mathrm{Re}(\mathcal{C}_{\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\dagger}})
, the Fourier transform of which yields the symmetrized spectra
\overline{\mathcal{S}}_{\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}}
and
\overline{\mathcal{S}}_{\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\dagger}}
, by inspecting the correlation between successive measurements where the qubit is repeatedly prepared in
|g\rangle
or
|e\rangle
respectively.

S4.2
System definition

In this section we explain how to experimentally measure the emission and absorption spectrum from periodic weak measurements of the membrane with a qubit.
The system is composed of a Fluxonium, modeled as a qubit with frequency
\omega_{q}
and the membrane mode.
The two systems interact with a Jaynes-Cummings Hamiltonian
\bm{H_{c}}/\hbar=i\tfrac{\Omega}{2}(\bm{a}+\bm{a}^{\dagger})(\bm{\sigma}-\bm{\sigma}^{\dagger})
, yielding the system Hamiltonian

\bm{H}=\frac{\hbar\omega_{q}}{2}\bm{\sigma_{z}}+\bm{H}_{m}+\bm{H}_{c}.

In the spectrum analyzer experiment, the qubit frequency is regularly Stark-shifted, close to the membrane frequency, to
\omega_{m}-\Delta
.
In the frame rotating at the Stark-shifted qubit frequency for both the membrane and the qubit, after a rotating-wave approximation, the Hamiltonian simplifies to
\bm{H}/\hbar=\Delta\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}+i\tfrac{\Omega}{2}(\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{\sigma}-\bm{a}\bm{\sigma}^{\dagger})
.
When the qubit frequency is not shifted, we can neglect the effect of the coupling.
The Lindbladian superoperator of the membrane free evolution is
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m}\bm{\rho}=-i[\Delta\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a},\bm{\rho}]+\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{L_{m,\downarrow}}]\bm{\rho}+\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{L_{m,\downarrow}}]\bm{\rho}
.
For the sake of simplicity, we will consider for now an ideal qubit and we will treat the qubit imperfections in
App.
S4.5
.

S4.3
Semi-classical treatment

S4.3.1
Autocorrelation function

In the semi-classical hypothesis, we treat classically the mechanical mode degrees of freedom,
\bm{x}
and
\bm{p}
.
In the frame of the qubit, the mode complex amplitude
\alpha(t)
is slowly oscillating at rate
\Delta
with random phase and amplitude fluctuations over a characteristic time-scale
1/\kappa_{m}
.
As the interaction time is short compared to the mechanical mode time-scales,
\alpha(t)
can be considered constant over one interaction.
Moreover, the interaction Hamiltonian can be simplified to
\bm{H}_{c}/\hbar=i\tfrac{\Omega}{2}(\alpha^{*}\bm{\sigma}-\alpha\bm{\sigma}^{\dagger})
, which is a classical drive with amplitude
\alpha
on the qubit.
Expanding the terms, we obtain that
\bm{H}_{c}/\hbar=\tfrac{\Omega}{2}\left(\mathrm{Im}(\alpha)\bm{\sigma}_{x}+\mathrm{Re}(\alpha)\bm{\sigma}_{y}\right)
.
The unitary evolution after an interaction time
\tau
can be written as

\bm{U}_{I}=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\bm{H}_{c}\tau\Bigr)=\exp\Bigl(-i\frac{\Omega}{2}|\alpha|\vec{n}\cdot\vec{\bm{\sigma}}\tau\Bigr)\\

(S65)

where

\vec{n}=\frac{1}{|\alpha|}(\mathrm{Im}(\alpha),\mathrm{Re}(\alpha),0)
and

\vec{\bm{\sigma}}=(\bm{\sigma}_{x},\bm{\sigma}_{y},\bm{\sigma}_{z})
.
Comparing with the standard
SU(2)
rotation matrix
\exp(-i\tfrac{\theta}{2}\vec{n}\cdot\vec{\bm{\sigma}})
shows that the qubit undergoes a rotation by
\theta=\Omega|\alpha|\tau
about the axis
\vec{n}
in the Bloch-sphere equatorial plane, whose complex direction is
i\alpha^{*}
.
Therefore, measuring
\bm{\sigma}_{x}
of the qubit, at time
t_{k}
, performs a measurement of the position of the mechanical mode as

\langle\bm{\sigma}_{x}(t_{k})\rangle=\theta_{1}x(t_{k}).

(S66)

In this semi-classical treatment, where the position
x(t)
is an external parameter which is not subject to qubit backaction, the measurement outcomes at different times are independent quantum observables. We thus have
\langle\bm{\sigma}_{x,k}\bm{\sigma}_{x,{k^{\prime}}}\rangle=\langle\bm{\sigma}_{x,k}\rangle\langle\bm{\sigma}_{x,{k^{\prime}}}\rangle
for
k\neq k^{\prime}
. It thus follows from
Eq. (
S66
)
that

\mathcal{C}_{k}\equiv\overline{\langle\bm{\sigma}_{x,k}\bm{\sigma}_{x,{0}}\rangle}=\theta_{1}^{2}\overline{x(t_{k})x(0)},

(S67)

where the overline denote the averaging over the different trajectories of the harmonic oscillator.
Moreover, in the case where
k=0
, the autocorrelation reads
\mathcal{C}_{0}=1
as
\sigma_{x}^{2}=\mathbb{I}
.
This proves
Eq. (
4
)
of the main text.

S4.3.2
Wiener-Khintchine theorem

Let us define the discrete Fourier transform of a signal
\{m_{k}\}_{0\leq k<N}
sampled at a frequency
1/T
as
\mathcal{F}[\{m_{k}\}](\omega_{r})=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}m_{n}e^{-i\omega_{r}n}
, where
\omega_{r}=2\pi r/NT
for
0\leq r<N
.
These discrete time frequencies maps unambiguously to continuous time frequencies
\omega
on the first Nyquist zone
0\leq\omega<\pi/T
. We thus drop the subscript
r
in the following.
We define the power spectral density as

S_{mm}(\omega)=T\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\overline{|\mathcal{F}[\{m_{k}\}](\omega)|^{2}}.

(S68)

The averaged squared modulus of the Fourier transforms can be expressed as

\displaystyle\overline{|\mathcal{F}[\{m_{k}\}](\omega)|^{2}}=

\displaystyle\overline{\Bigl(\mathcal{F}[\{m_{k}\}](\omega)\Bigr)^{*}\mathcal{F}[\{m_{k}\}](\omega)}

(S69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\overline{m_{k}m_{l}}e^{-i\omega(k-l)}

By stationarity of the measurement signal
\overline{m_{k}m_{l}}=\overline{m_{k-l}m_{0}}\equiv\mathcal{C}_{k-l}
, defining
\mathcal{C}_{r}
as the autocorrelation function at time
t_{r}=rT
.
Changing in
Eq. (
S69
)
the sum index
k
to
r=k-l
, we obtain

\overline{|\mathcal{F}[\{m_{k}\}](\omega)|^{2}}=\sum_{r=-N+1}^{N-1}\sum_{l=f(r)}^{g(r)}\mathcal{C}_{r}e^{-i\omega r},

where
f(r)=\mathrm{max}(0,r)
and
g(r)=f(r)+N-1-|r|
.
The second sum is composed of
N-|r|
terms independent of
l
.
Taking the limit of this sum leads to the result

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\overline{|\mathcal{F}[\{m_{k}\}](\omega)|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=-N+1}^{N-1}\mathcal{C}_{r}e^{-i\omega r}\left(1-\frac{|r|}{N}\right)

(S70)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\sum_{r=-N+1}^{N-1}\mathcal{C}_{r}e^{-i\omega r}.

The inversion of the sum and limit in the last line is valid in the hypothesis of an absolute summable autocorrelation, i.e. a power spectral density that does not diverge.
This proves the Wiener-Khintchine theorem, linking the power spectral density to the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation function.

S_{mm}(\omega)=T\mathcal{F}[\{\mathcal{C}_{r}\}](\omega).

(S71)

Using
Eq. (
S67
)
, this theorem is used to prove
Eq. (
5
)
of the main text.

S4.4
Quantum treatment

S4.4.1
Measurement operators

The qubit being initialized in the pure state
|g\rangle
or
|e\rangle
before each interaction, we can safely assume that the initial joint qubit-membrane state is described by the factorized density operator
\bm{\rho_{m}}\otimes|i\rangle\langle i|
(with
i\in\{g,e\}
).
In the rotating frame, neglecting the small qubit-membrane detuning
\Delta
and decoherence during the short interaction time
\tau
, the evolution operator
\bm{U}_{I}=e^{-i\theta_{1}/2(\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{\sigma}-\bm{a}\bm{\sigma}^{\dagger})}
writes
[
5
]

\bm{U_{I}}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\left(\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\sqrt{\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}+1}\right)&-\bm{a}\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\sqrt{\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}}\right)}{\sqrt{\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}}}\\
\bm{a}^{\dagger}\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\sqrt{\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}+1}\right)}{\sqrt{\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}+1}}&\cos\left(\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\sqrt{\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}}\right)\end{pmatrix},

(S72)

where the matrix is written in the qubit basis
\{|e\rangle,|g\rangle\}
. During the interaction, the system state evolves according to:

\bm{\rho}(t_{k})\longrightarrow\bm{\rho}(t_{k+1})=\bm{U}_{I}\left(\bm{\rho}_{m}(t_{k})\otimes|i\rangle\langle i|\right)\bm{U}_{I}^{\dagger}.

(S73)

In the final state
\bm{\rho}(t_{k+1})
, we perform a projective measurement of the qubit in the
|\pm\rangle
basis. The probability to measure the qubit in state
|+\rangle
or
|-\rangle
is then

p_{\pm,i}(t_{k+1})=\text{Tr}[\bm{\rho}(t_{k+1})|\pm\rangle\langle\pm|]=\text{Tr}[\bm{M}_{\pm,i}\bm{\rho}_{m}(t_{k})\bm{M}_{\pm,i}]
, where we defined the measurement operators

\displaystyle\bm{M}_{\pm,i}

\displaystyle=\langle\pm|\bm{U}_{I}|i\rangle

(S74)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{cases}\mathbb{I}\mp\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\bm{a}-\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{8}\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}&\text{if }i=g,\\
\mathbb{I}\pm\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\bm{a}^{\dagger}-\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{8}\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\dagger}&\text{if }i=e.\end{cases}

The final expression is calculated up to second order in
\theta_{1}
, and we removed an irrelevant global phase term in the
i=e
case.
The set of measurement operators describes a Positive Operator-Valued Measure which fulfills
\bm{M}_{+,i}^{\dagger}\bm{M}_{+,i}+\bm{M}_{-,i}^{\dagger}\bm{M}_{-,i}=\mathbb{I}
up to second order in
\theta_{1}
.
Moreover, since
\bm{M}_{\pm,i}
are close to the identity for
\theta_{1}\ll 1
, this process describes more specifically a weak measurement.
Upon qubit measurement, the membrane density matrix is projected to

\bm{\rho}_{m}^{\pm,i}(t_{k+1})=\frac{\bm{M}_{\pm,i}\bm{\rho}_{m}(t_{k})\bm{M}_{\pm,i}^{\dagger}}{p_{\pm,i}}\equiv\frac{\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{\pm,i}\bm{\rho}_{m}(t_{k})}{p_{\pm,i}}.

(S75)

Here, we defined the measurement Kraus map
\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{\pm,i}
.
Introducing the dissipation and measurement superoperators as

\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{L}]\,\bm{\rho}=\bm{L}\,\bm{\rho}\,\bm{L}^{\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{\bm{L}^{\dagger}\bm{L},\bm{\rho}\},\qquad\bm{\mathcal{M}}[\bm{L}]\,\bm{\rho}=\bm{L}\,\bm{\rho}+\bm{\rho}\,\bm{L}^{\dagger},

the Kraus map can be expressed, up to second order in
\theta_{1}
, as

\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{\pm,i}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\mathbb{I}\pm\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\,\bm{\mathcal{M}}[\bm{L}_{i}]+\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{4}\,\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{L}_{i}]\Bigr],

(S76)

where the weakly measured membrane operators, for each qubit state preparation are

\bm{L}_{i}=\begin{cases}-\bm{a},&i=g,\\
\bm{a}^{\dagger},&i=e.\end{cases}

(S77)

The term proportional to
\bm{\mathcal{M}}[L]
in
Eq. (
S76
)
is the outcome-dependent “informational” (or quantum) backaction, which steers the mechanical state based on the measurement result, while the
\bm{\mathcal{D}}[L]
contribution describes a measurement-independent, qubit-induced damping channel, directly analogous to dynamical backaction in cavity optomechanics
[
1
]
.

S4.4.2
Dynamical back-action

When the measurement outcomes are disregarded, the membrane evolves under the average Kraus map

\bar{\bm{\mathcal{K}}}_{i}=\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{+,i}+\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{-,i}=\mathbb{I}+\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{4}\,\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{L}_{i}].

(S78)

During the interval
T
between two successive qubit measurements, the membrane undergoes free-evolution and dissipation to the thermal bath, described by the Lindbladian

\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m}=-i[\Delta\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a},\cdot]+\kappa_{m}n_{\mathrm{th}}\,\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{a}]+\kappa_{m}(n_{\mathrm{th}}+1)\,\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{a}^{\dagger}].

(S79)

Interleaving the average Kraus map (
S78
) with the membrane’s free evolution
e^{\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m}\,T}
yields

\bm{\rho}(t_{k}+T)=e^{\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m}\,T}\;\bar{\bm{\mathcal{K}}}_{i}\,\bm{\rho}(t_{k})\approx e^{\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m,i}\,T},

(S80)

where, in a coarse-grained approximation, the effective Lindbladian becomes

\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m,i}=\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m}+\kappa\,\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{L}_{i}]
, with the dynamical-backaction rate
\kappa=\theta_{1}^{2}/4T
.
This coarse-grained evolution is identical to that induced by the coupling at a rate
\kappa_{m,i}^{\prime}
to an effective environment with thermal occupation
n_{\mathrm{th,i}}^{\prime}
, where

\kappa^{\prime}_{m,i}=\begin{cases}\kappa_{m}+\kappa,&i=g,\\
\kappa_{m}-\kappa,&i=e,\end{cases}\quad n^{\prime}_{\text{th,i}}=\frac{\kappa_{m}n_{\rm th}+\delta_{e,i}\,\kappa}{\kappa^{\prime}_{m,i}}.

Here
\delta_{e,i}=1
if
i=e
(zero otherwise). In other words, dynamical back-action modifies both the damping rate and the apparent thermal occupation by factors
1\pm C
, where
C=\kappa/\kappa_{m}
is the cooperativity, in line with conventions commonly used in optomechanics.
The last additive term in the occupation
\kappa/\kappa_{m}^{\prime}=C/(1-C)
appearing when the qubit is prepared in state
|e\rangle
corresponds to the additional fluctuations resulting from quantum backaction. In our experiment where the cooperativity is on the order of 10 %, we can safely neglect this contribution in the area of the spectrum
S_{mm}^{e}(\omega)
.

Finally, if we alternate ground and excited preparations each cycle, the net evolution over a full cycle is generated by

\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m,ge}=\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m}+\kappa\,\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{a}]+\kappa\,\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{a}^{\dagger}],

(S81)

so that

\kappa^{\prime}_{m,ge}=\kappa_{m},\qquad n^{\prime}_{\text{th},ge}=n_{\rm th}+C.

Thus the alternating protocol cancels the first-order dynamical back-action, leaving only a negligible residual heating of order
C\ll n_{\rm th}
.
Finally, note that here, the interval between two identical preparation steps
T
is effectively doubled (see
Fig.
4
(b)
) compared to the previous protocol, which halves both the back-action rate
\kappa
and the cooperativity
C
.

S4.4.3
Emission and absorption spectra

The full Kraus operators factor into the mean-evolution map and a pure backaction part:

\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{\pm,i}=\bar{\bm{\mathcal{K}}}_{i}\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{\pm,i}^{\prime}\;,\text{ with }\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{\pm,i}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{I}\pm\frac{\theta_{1}}{2}\bm{\mathcal{M}}[\bm{L}_{i}]\right).

(S82)

One can then calculate the joint probability to obtain outcomes
m_{0}
and
m_{k}
separated by
k
full cycles becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[m_{k},m_{0}|\bm{\rho}_{m},i]\simeq\mathrm{Tr}[\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{m_{k},i}^{\prime}e^{\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}_{m}kT}\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{m_{0},i}^{\prime}\bm{\rho}_{m}],

(S83)

where
\bm{\mathcal{\widetilde{L}}}_{m}
denotes the effective Lindbladian for the chosen preparation sequence—either
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m,g}
or
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m,e}
for identical preparations, or
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m,ge}
for alternating ground/excited protocols.

We then express the discrete-time autocorrelation of the measurement outcomes as

\mathcal{C}_{k}^{i}\;=\;\sum_{m_{0},m_{k}=\pm 1}m_{k}\,m_{0}\;\mathbb{P}[m_{k},m_{0}\mid\bm{\rho}_{m},i],

so that for
k=0
trivially
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{i}=1
. For
k\neq 0
, inserting the expression (
S83
), and using

\sum_{m=\pm 1}m\,\bm{\mathcal{K}}^{\prime}_{m,i}=\tfrac{\theta_{1}}{2}\,\bm{\mathcal{M}}[L_{i}]

yields

\mathcal{C}_{k}^{i}=\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{4}\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{M}}[\bm{L}_{i}]\,e^{\tilde{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}_{m}\,kT}\,\bm{\mathcal{M}}[\bm{L}_{i}]\,\bm{\rho}_{m}\bigr].

Defining the Heisenberg picture operator
\bm{O}(t)
as

\bm{O}\bm{\rho}(t)=\bm{O}e^{\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}_{m}t}\bm{\rho}=\bm{O}(t)\bm{\rho}
,
the measurement correlator reads

\mathcal{C}_{k}^{i}\big|_{k\neq 0}=\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{4}\,\text{Re}\bigl\langle\bm{L}_{i}^{\dagger}(t_{k})\,\bm{L}_{i}(0)\bigr\rangle,

The discrete-time spectrum then follows by Fourier transform.

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{mm}^{g}(\omega)

\displaystyle=T+\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{4}\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}}(\omega),

(S84)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{mm}^{e}(\omega_{k})

\displaystyle=T+\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{4}\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\dagger}}(\omega),

where
\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}}(\omega)
and
\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\dagger}}(\omega)
are the one-sided emission and absorption mechanical spectra, in the actual protocol.

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\bm{L}_{i}^{\dagger}\bm{L}_{i}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\tilde{n}_{\text{th}}+\delta_{ei})\tilde{\kappa}_{m}}{(\omega-\Delta)^{2}+(\tilde{\kappa}_{m}/2)^{2}},

(S85)

where
i\in\{g,e\}
and
(\tilde{n}_{\text{th}},\tilde{\kappa}_{m})=(n_{\text{th},p}^{\prime},\kappa_{m,p}^{\prime})
with
p\in\{g,e,ge\}
based on the used protocol.

S4.5
Effect of the qubit imperfections

In this section, we account for imperfect state preparation and readout, as well as intrinsic qubit decoherence.
We assume the qubit is initialized in
|g\rangle
and
|e\rangle
with preparation fidelities
\eta_{g}
and
\eta_{e}
, respectively.
The qubit’s energy relaxation/excitation (at rate
\kappa_{1}/2
) and pure dephasing (at rate
\kappa_{\phi}
) are described by the Lindblad jump operators

\bm{L_{q,\downarrow}}=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{1}}{2}}\,\bm{\sigma},\quad\bm{L_{q,\uparrow}}=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{1}}{2}}\,\bm{\sigma}^{\dagger},\quad\bm{L_{q,\phi}}=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{\phi}}{2}}\,\bm{\sigma_{z}}.

(S86)

The relevant hierarchy in our experimental regime is
\Delta,\kappa_{m}n_{th}\ll\Omega\sqrt{n_{th}}\ll\kappa_{1},\kappa_{\phi}
.
Therefore, we can perform a perturbation treatment on the losses of the qubit.
The Lindblad super-operator,
\bm{\mathcal{L}}
and the joint density matrix,
\bm{\rho}
, can be vectorized using the column-stacking convention on the qubit space.
The total Hilbert space is
\mathcal{H}_{q}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{m}
, then the vectorized density matrix reads
\vec{\bm{\rho}}=(\bm{\rho_{ee}},\bm{\rho_{eg}},\bm{\rho_{ge}},\bm{\rho_{gg}})^{T}
, where each elements is an operator on
\mathcal{H}_{m}
.
An operator
\bm{O}
acting on the left (resp. right) of the oscillator density matrix is written
\bm{{O}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}\!
(resp.
\bm{{O}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow}\!
).
Using these conventions, the super-operators can be written as,
\bm{\mathcal{L}}=\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}+\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{1}+\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m}
, with

\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}-\kappa_{1}/2&0&0&\kappa_{1}/2\\
0&-\kappa_{2}&0&0\\
0&0&-\kappa_{2}&0\\
\kappa_{1}/2&0&0&-\kappa_{1}/2\end{pmatrix},

capturing the qubit loss operators and

\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{1}=\frac{\Omega}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&\bm{{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}\!^{\dagger}&\bm{{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow}\!&0\\
-\bm{{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}\!&0&0&\bm{{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow}\!\\
-\bm{{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow}\!^{\dagger}&0&0&\bm{{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}\!^{\dagger}\\
0&-\bm{{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow}\!^{\dagger}&-\bm{{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}\!&0\end{pmatrix}

capturing the qubit membrane coupling.

S4.5.1
Perturbative treatment

As
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
acts only on the qubit and
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m}
only on the mechanical mode,
[\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0},\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m}]=0
.
Under the approximation of a weak coupling, the evolution super-operator can be approximated by the truncated Dyson series

\displaystyle e^{\bm{\mathcal{L}}t}\simeq

\displaystyle e^{(\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}+\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m})t}+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!dt_{1}\;e^{(\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}+\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m})(t-t_{1})}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{1}e^{(\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}+\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m})t_{1}}+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!dt_{2}\!\!\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\!\!dt_{1}\;e^{(\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}+\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m})(t-t_{2})}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{1}e^{(\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}+\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m})(t_{2}-t_{1})}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{1}e^{(\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}+\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m})t_{1}}

(S87)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle e^{\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m}t}\left(e^{\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}+\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\;e^{\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}(t-t_{1})}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{1}e^{\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t_{1}}+\int_{0}^{t}dt_{2}\int_{0}^{t_{2}}dt_{1}\;e^{\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}(t-t_{2})}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{1}e^{\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}(t_{2}-t_{1})}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{1}e^{\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t_{1}}\right),

where we used both that
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
and
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m}
commutes and the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula to factorize
e^{\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m}t}
.
Upon exponentiation of
\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
, we get

e^{\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}t}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1+e^{-\kappa_{1}t}}{2}&0&0&\frac{1-e^{-\kappa_{1}t}}{2}\\
0&e^{-\kappa_{2}t}&0&0\\
0&0&e^{-\kappa_{2}t}&0\\
\frac{1-e^{-\kappa_{1}t}}{2}&0&0&\frac{1+e^{-\kappa_{1}t}}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(S88)

The first integral in
Eq. (
S87
)
, can then be computed as

\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(t)\equiv\frac{\Omega}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&\bm{a}_{+}^{\dagger}&\bm{a}_{-}&0\\
-\bm{a}_{+}&0&0&\bm{a}_{-}\\
-\bm{a}_{-}^{\dagger}&0&0&\bm{a}_{+}^{\dagger}\\
0&-\bm{a}_{-}^{\dagger}&-\bm{a}_{+}&0\end{pmatrix},

(S89)

where we defined
\bm{a}_{\pm}=\tau_{\Sigma}\bm{a}_{\Sigma}\pm\tau_{2}\bm{a}_{\Delta}
, with

\displaystyle\tau_{\Sigma}(t)=\frac{\kappa_{1}\tau_{1}(t)-\kappa_{2}\tau_{2}(t)}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}},

\displaystyle\tau_{1}(t)=\frac{1-e^{-\kappa_{1}t}}{\kappa_{1}},\quad\tau_{2}(t)=\frac{1-e^{-\kappa_{2}t}}{\kappa_{2}},

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{\Sigma}=\frac{\bm{{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}\!+\bm{{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow}\!}{2},\quad\bm{a}_{\Delta}=\frac{\bm{{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}\!-\bm{{a}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow}\!}{2}.

Notably,
\lim_{\kappa_{2}\to\kappa_{1}}\tau_{\Sigma}(t)=te^{-\kappa_{1}t}
and
\lim_{\kappa_{1}\to 0}\tau_{1}(t)=\lim_{\kappa_{2}\to 0}\tau_{2}(t)=t
.
Due to the matrix structure of this term, it captures the entanglement of the membrane and qubits states, but it doesn’t capture the population transfer responsible for heating or cooling of the membrane.
In order to capture such effect, we have to consider the second order perturbation term of
Eq. (
S87
)
, that can be written as

\bm{\mathcal{I}}_{2}(t)=\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{V}_{1,1}&0&0&\bm{V}_{1,4}\\
0&\bm{V}_{2,2}&\bm{V}_{2,3}&0\\
0&\bm{V}_{3,2}&\bm{V}_{3,3}&0\\
\bm{V}_{4,1}&0&0&\bm{V}_{4,4}\end{pmatrix}

(S90)

where
\{\bm{V}_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in[\![1,4]\!]}
terms are not explicitly written for simplicity.

S4.5.2
Kraus operators

The Kraus operators acting on the membrane degree of freedom can be computed as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{\pm,i}\bm{\rho}_{m}=\mathrm{Tr}_{q}[|\pm\rangle\langle\pm|e^{\bm{\mathcal{L}}\tau}(\bm{\rho}_{m}\otimes\bm{\rho}_{q,i})]

(S91)

\displaystyle\text{with }\bm{\rho}_{q,i}=\begin{pmatrix}1-p_{i}&0&0&p_{i}\end{pmatrix}^{T}

where
p_{g}=(1+\eta_{g})/2
and
p_{e}=(1-\eta_{e})/2
.
They can be approximated using
Eq. (
S87
)
as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{\pm,i}=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{m}\tau}}{2}\Bigl(\mathbb{I}\pm\frac{\Omega}{4}((2p_{i}-1)\tau_{\Sigma}+\tau_{2})\bm{\mathcal{M}}[\bm{a}]

(S92)

\displaystyle\pm\frac{\Omega}{4}((2p_{i}-1)\tau_{\Sigma}-\tau_{2})\bm{\mathcal{M}}[\bm{a}^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle+\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{\tau-\tau_{2}}{\kappa_{2}}+(2p_{i}-1)\frac{\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}}\right)\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{a}]

\displaystyle+\frac{\Omega^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{\tau-\tau_{2}}{\kappa_{2}}-(2p_{i}-1)\frac{\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}}\right)\bm{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{a}^{\dagger}]\Bigr)

Due to the limited qubit lifetime, the action of a qubit prepared in
|g\rangle
(resp.
|e\rangle
also weakly measures the
\bm{a}^{\dagger}
(resp.
\bm{a}
) operator.

To account for the readout infidelity, we’ll write the readout confusion matrix in terms of the single-shot assignment error probabilities
\varepsilon_{g}=1-F_{g}
and
\varepsilon_{e}=1-F_{e}
.
As the state is readout in the
|\pm\rangle
basis with a
\sqrt{\bm{X}}
pulse, the readout errors on state
|+\rangle
(resp.
|-\rangle
) are the one of
|e\rangle
(resp.
|g\rangle
), defining
\varepsilon_{+/-}=\varepsilon_{e/g}
.
The effective Kraus operator acting on the membrane can be written as

\bar{\bm{\mathcal{K}}}_{\pm,i}=(1-\varepsilon_{\pm})\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{\pm,i}+\varepsilon_{\pm}\bm{\mathcal{K}}_{\mp,i}.

(S93)

S4.5.3
Emission and absorption spectra

We can compute all the previous steps to compute the autocorrelation we applied to the lossless qubit, except for the case where
k=0
.
Indeed, as the readout of the qubit is asymmetric, the autocorrelation at
k=0
is modified to
\mathcal{C}_{k}^{i}=1-(\varepsilon_{g}-\varepsilon_{e})^{2}
.
Computing the spectra from this autocorrelations, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{mm}^{g}(\omega_{k}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon_{g}-\varepsilon_{e})^{2}\eta_{g}\frac{\Omega^{2}\tau_{\Sigma}^{2}}{4}\Bigl(\frac{\eta_{g}+\tau_{2}/\tau_{\Sigma}}{2}\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}}(\omega_{k}^{\prime})+

(S94)

\displaystyle\frac{\eta_{g}-\tau_{2}/\tau_{\Sigma}}{2}\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\dagger}}(\omega_{k}^{\prime}))\Bigr)+T(1-(\varepsilon_{g}-\varepsilon_{e})^{2}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{mm}^{e}(\omega_{k}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon_{g}-\varepsilon_{e})^{2}\eta_{e}\frac{\Omega^{2}\tau_{\Sigma}^{2}}{4}\Bigl(\frac{\eta_{e}-\tau_{2}/\tau_{\Sigma}}{2}\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\bm{a}^{\dagger}\bm{a}}(\omega_{k}^{\prime})+

\displaystyle\frac{\eta_{e}+\tau_{2}/\tau_{\Sigma}}{2}\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\dagger}}(\omega_{k}^{\prime}))\Bigr)+T(1-(\varepsilon_{g}-\varepsilon_{e})^{2}).

Using the amplitude of the measured thermal peak for the preparation in
|g\rangle
at 10 mK in
Fig.
4
, we estimate
\Omega/2\pi=1.50(8)
kHz.

The normalized spectra are expressed in units of a calibration peak amplitude resulting from a weak drive on the qubit, the resulting sinus cardinal amplitudes in the spectra are proportionate to
(1-\varepsilon_{g}-\varepsilon_{e})^{2}\eta_{i}^{2}
.
The area of the normalized Lorentzian peaks therefore reads

\displaystyle A_{g}=

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega^{2}\tau_{\Sigma}^{2}}{4}\left(n_{\mathrm{th}}+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\tau_{2}}{2\eta_{g}\tau_{\Sigma}}\right)

(S95)

\displaystyle A_{e}=

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega^{2}\tau_{\Sigma}^{2}}{4}\left(n_{\mathrm{th}}+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\tau_{2}}{2\eta_{e}\tau_{\Sigma}}\right)

Performing a linear fit of the areas as a function of the cryostat temperature, we are able to express the amplitude in term of phonon number,

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{g}=

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{th}}+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\tau_{2}}{2\eta_{g}\tau_{\Sigma}}

(S96)

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{e}=

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{th}}+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\tau_{2}}{2\eta_{g}\tau_{\Sigma}}

The expected asymmetry of the spectrum used in
Fig.
4
writes

\bar{n}_{e}-\bar{n}_{g}=\frac{\tau_{2}}{\tau_{\Sigma}}\frac{\eta_{g}+\eta_{e}}{2\eta_{g}\eta_{e}}.

(S97)

S4.6
Data analysis of the spectrum analyzer

In order to estimate the noise spectral density
S_{mm}^{g/e}
of the qubit telegraphic signal, we first group the measurement outcomes
\{m_{k}\}
into batches of
N=10\,000
samples (see
Fig.
3
(d)
).
For each batch, we compute the discrete Fourier transform,
\mathcal{F}[m_{k}]_{r}
, where the frequency bins are defined as

\omega_{r}=2\pi r/NT\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq r<N.

(S98)

We then average the squared magnitudes over all batches to obtain the noise spectral density

S_{mm}(\omega_{r})=\overline{|\mathcal{F}[m_{k}]_{r}|^{2}}T/N,

(S99)

with the overline indicating an average over batches corresponding to distinct classical trajectories
\{x(t_{k})\}_{0\leq k<N}
(see
Fig.
3
(e)
).
At our sampling rate, the aliasing-free frequency span is
\omega_{N/2}\approx 2\pi\times 32~
kHz.
We therefore choose a small qubit-membrane detuning
\Delta/2\pi=5~
kHz to ensure the oscillations of
x(t)
remain entirely within the Nyquist zone.

The spectra displayed in
Fig.
3
(e–f)
and
Fig.
4
(c)
are obtained from
N_{\rm batches}\sim\mathrm{1000}
and
\mathrm{4\times 10^{5}}
Fourier-transformed batches.
To determine the statistical error on the estimated spectrum, we use a bootstrap approach: the averaging described by
Eq. (
S99
)
is repeated over 1000 different datasets.
Each dataset is obtained from the original data by drawing a resample with replacement of the
N_{\rm batches}
Fourier-transformed batches.
The 1000 different spectra are then accumulated in histograms, represented by the shaded regions in
Fig.
3
(e–f)
and
Fig.
4
(c)
.
The quoted errors on the fitted linewidths
\kappa_{g}^{\prime}
,
\kappa_{e}^{\prime}
and peak areas
A_{g}
,
A_{e}
, as well as the error bars in
Fig.
4
(d–e)
, are the standard deviations of those 1000 fits.

Since the mechanical peak area scales as
V_{\text{offset}}^{2}
, any slow drift in
V_{\text{offset}}
during a monotonic temperature sweep (see Sec. (S2.5) of supplementary information) would introduce a spurious temperature dependence in the sideband-asymmetry data of
Fig.
4
(c)-(d)
.
To avoid this, we interleaved the measurements by cycling the cryostat through each setpoint (2 h per point) for 16 complete cycles, so that any residual offset drift acts uniformly across all temperatures.

S5
Experimental Setup

The experimental setup is shown in
Fig.
S14
.
RF signals are controlled and measured using a heterodyne detection scheme, implemented via an FPGA-based controller (
OPX, Quantum Machine
) combined with a microwave source (
Anapico APUASYN20-4
). The magnetic flux through the qubit is tuned using a
Yokogawa 7651
DC source, which supplies current to a superconducting coil placed directly to the chip enclosure. Fast flux tuning is achieved via an on-chip flux line.
Signal amplification is performed using a traveling-wave parametric amplifier (TWPA), a high-electron-mobility transistor (HEMT) amplifier, and a room-temperature amplifier. The DC membrane bias is controlled by a
Yokogawa GS200
source and filtered through a commercial 10 kHz low-pass filter (
QDevil
) to suppress high-frequency noise.
