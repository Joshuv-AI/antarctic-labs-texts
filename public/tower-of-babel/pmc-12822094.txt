Association between advanced lung cancer inflammation index and postoperative mortality in lung cancer patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Lingzi Su, Xi Li, Qilin Yang, Qin Wang, Yabin Gong, WenXiao Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related death. Surgical resection of early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is associated with a suboptimal survival rate and significant adverse outcomes. The advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI), which integrates body mass index (BMI), albumin level, and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), comprehensively assesses nutritional and inflammatory status, potentially improving prognostic precision in surgical patients.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 2447 patients who underwent lung cancer resection (INSPIRE database, South Korea, 2011–2020). The ALI (BMI × Albumin/NLR) was calculated preoperatively. Patients were stratified into high (≥ 37.22) and low (< 37.22) ALI groups (receiver operating characteristic curve-determined cut-off). Associations between ALI and mortality (primary: 90-day; secondary: 180-day, in-hospital) were assessed using multivariate regression after adjusting for demographics, surgical factors, and key laboratory parameters.ResultsThe low ALI group (n = 805) was older (64.2 vs 63.2 years, p = 0.018), had more males (63.2% vs. 51.2%, p < 0.001), and exhibited significantly poorer laboratory profiles (e.g., lower hematocrit and hemoglobin levels; higher white blood cell count and C-reactive protein levels; all p < 0.001) than the high ALI group (n = 1642). Mortality rates were significantly higher in the low ALI group (90-day: 1.6% vs 0.1%; 180-day: 3.2% vs 0.5%; in-hospital: 2.0% vs 0.4%; all p < 0.001). After full adjustment, compared with the low ALI group, the high ALI group had an 82% lower risk of 90-day mortality (HR 0.18, 95% CI 0.04–0.81) and a 70% lower risk of 180-day mortality (HR 0.30, 95% CI 0.13–0.70). Each 1-unit increase in ALI was linked to a 4% reduction in 90-day mortality risk (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.93–0.99). High ALI was also associated with an 66% lower odds of in-hospital mortality (OR 0.34, 95% CI 0.12–0.96).ConclusionsOur study shows that ALI is significantly associated with poor clinical prognosis after lung cancer resection. Background

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with over 2.2 million new cases and 1.8 million deaths reported in 2020 [1]. While surgical resection is the primary treatment for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), five-year postoperative survival rates remain relatively low at approximately 60% [2]. Notably, nearly 30% of patients undergoing surgery experience poor outcomes owing to occult metastases or postoperative complications [3], highlighting the urgent need for reliable prognostic tools. Emerging evidence indicates that systemic inflammatory responses can significantly impact postoperative trajectories by remodeling the tumor microenvironment and facilitating immune evasion [4], suggesting that multiparametric indices integrating both nutritional and inflammatory status may yield superior prognostic precision.

Currently available prognostic biomarkers in lung cancer often focus on isolated pathophysiological processes rather than providing a comprehensive assessment of systemic metabolic condition. Examples include the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), which are associated with postoperative survival by reflecting disturbances in immune homeostasis [5]; as well as the Glasgow Prognostic Score (GPS), which combines C-reactive protein and albumin levels to predict postoperative complications [6]. In contrast, the advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI) uniquely integrates body mass index (BMI), albumin levels, and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, thereby providing a composite measure of nutritional status, inflammatory burden, and metabolic reserves [7]. Although preliminary studies have demonstrated the ALI's independent prognostic value in gastric [8] and colorectal cancers [9], its role in surgical lung cancer populations remains underexplored.

To address these gaps, this study retrospectively established a surgical cohort (n = 2447) to systematically evaluate the associations between ALI and short-term (90- and 180-day) as well as in-hospital mortality. The study was designed to address three research questions: (1) Does ALI independently predict postoperative outcomes beyond conventional prognostic indicators? (2) Does it exhibit superior predictive accuracy compared to existing inflammatory biomarkers? (3) What is its potential clinical utility in perioperative risk stratification?

Methods

Data sources

The data for this retrospective cohort study were obtained from the INSPIRE database, a publicly available resource in perioperative medicine. It includes details of approximately 130,000 surgical procedures performed at an academic institution in South Korea between 2011 and 2020. The last date of follow-up for mortality ascertainment was December 31, 2020. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH) approved the use of the INSPIRE database for the study (IRB No. H-2210-078-1368). Given the retrospective design, the IRB waived the requirement for informed consent. This cohort study adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [10].

Study population

Participants were included if they met the following criteria: (1) histologically confirmed diagnosis of lung cancer and (2) age ≥ 18 years. Exclusion criteria were: (1) ASA physical status classification > 4; (2) missing key laboratory data required to calculate ALI; and (3) absence of lung cancer surgical resection. Lung cancer diagnoses were verified using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10).

The primary exposure variable was the advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI), calculated as ALI = BMI (kg/m2) × albumin (g/dL)/neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR). All values used to compute ALI were drawn from the last preoperative blood sample taken within 24 h before surgery. Based on receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, an optimal cut-off value of 37.22 was identified to categorize patients into high (≥ 37.22) and low (< 37.22) ALI groups (Figure S1 for ROC curve; Table S1 for full metrics).

Data extraction

To adjust for potential confounding, the following covariates were included: (1) demographic characteristics: age and sex; (2) surgical parameters: emergency surgery status (yes/no), ASA physical status (I–IV), and operative approach; (3) laboratory values: hemoglobin (HGB, g/dL), white blood cell count (WBC, × 10⁹/L), platelet count (PLT, × 1012/L), hematocrit (HCT, %), C-reactive protein (CRP, mg/dL), serum creatinine (Scr, mg/dL), blood glucose (mg/dL), serum sodium (mmol/L), and lactate (mmol/L). Covariate selection was guided by biological plausibility and prior research. Age and sex are established prognostic indicators, while ASA classification reflects overall health and operative risk. Emergency status and surgical approach directly influence perioperative outcomes. The included hematological and biochemical parameters collectively reflect systemic inflammation, nutritional status, and multiorgan functional reserve—all recognized determinants of surgical prognosis. All laboratory analyses were performed in accredited clinical laboratories under standardized quality control protocols.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was 90-day postoperative mortality. Secondary outcomes encompassed 180-day mortality, 365-day mortality, and in-hospital mortality. Mortality data were collected through institutional electronic medical records, follow-up documentation, and linkage to the Korean National Death Registry. Post-discharge deaths were confirmed via structured telephone follow-up and cross-verified with registry data. All outcomes were recorded as binary variables (death/survival), with time-to-event calculated from the date of surgery to either death or last follow-up. Although outcome assessment was unblinded due to the study’s retrospective nature, mortality as an objective endpoint is minimally prone to subjective bias. All mortality events were adjudicated independently using standardized protocols [11].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data, or median (IQR) for skewed data, with comparisons made using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as counts (percentages) and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’ exact test.

Multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate associations between ALI and 90-, 180-, and 365-day mortality, with results expressed as hazard ratios (HRs). Logistic regression models assessed the association between ALI and in-hospital mortality. Covariates included in multivariate models were selected based on clinical relevance or if their inclusion altered the effect estimate by more than 10%. In Model I, no covariates was adjusted. Model II adjusted for age, sex, ASA classification, emergency surgery, and operative approach. Model III added laboratory parameters to the covariates in model II, including HGB, WBC, PLT, HCT, CRP, Scr, sodium, glucose, and lactate.

We used a smoothed curve fit to assess the association between ALI and 90-day mortality. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used to compare survival probabilities between ALI groups, with the log-rank test applied for significance testing. Cox proportional hazards models assessed the association between ALI and mortality at 90 and 180 days postoperatively, with results reported as HRs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Logistic regression analyses were conducted to evaluate in-hospital mortality, with results presented as odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% CIs. ROC curve analysis was applied to evaluate whether ALI could improve the predictive value of 90-day mortality. The proportional hazards assumptions was checked using the Schoenfeld residuals.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (http://www.r-project.org, The R Foundation) and Free Statistics software (version 2.1). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of our primary findings, we conducted both subgroup and sensitivity analyses. First, we performed subgroup analysis based on operative time to assess for potential effect modification, with statistical interaction evaluated using the likelihood ratio test. Additionally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding patients who underwent emergency surgery, in order to examine the consistency of the associations in a more homogeneous elective surgery population.

Results

Study participants and baseline characteristics

As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 3247 lung cancer patients were identified from the INSPIRE database. After excluding individuals with missing anthropometric data (weight/height) and those who did not undergo surgical resection (n = 796), 2447 patients were included in the final analysis. Baseline demographic characteristics of all participants are presented in Table 1. The overall mean age was 63.5 ± 10.3 years, with 1349 (55.1%) males and 1098 (44.9%) females. In the fully adjusted model (Model III), each 1-unit increase in ALI was associated with a 4% reduction in the risk of 90-day mortality (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.93–0.99, p = 0.009), indicating a dose-dependent protective effect. When analyzed categorically, the high ALI group (≥ 37.22) had an 82% lower risk of 90-day mortality compared to the low ALI group (HR 0.18, 95% CI 0.04–0.81, p = 0.025).

Similarly, ALI was inversely associated with 180-day mortality. Each 1-unit increase in ALI was linked to a 4% reduction in 180-day mortality risk (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.94–0.98, p = 0.001). Categorical analysis showed a 70% lower risk of 180-day mortality in the high ALI group (HR 0.30, 95% CI 0.13–0.70, p = 0.006).

Comparable results were observed for 365-day mortality. In continuous analysis, each 1-unit increase in ALI was linked to a 1% reduction in 365-day mortality risk (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.98–1, p = 0.048), However, categorical high/low ALI grouping did not reach statistical significance in the fully adjusted model (p = 0.082). Additionally, logistic regression analysis (Model III) showed that the high ALI group had an 66% lower odds of in-hospital mortality compared to the low ALI group (OR 0.34, 95% CI 0.12–0.96, p = 0.042).

Receiver operating characteristic analysis

To further evaluate the predictive value of ALI, NLR, PLR, albumin level in patients for 90-day mortality, we constructed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves (Figure S2; Table S1). The results showed that the area under the ROC curve (AUC) (95% CI) for ALI, NLR, PLR and albumin were 0.8 (0.73,0.89), 0.76 (0.76,0.86), 0.77 (0.67,0.87) and 0.78(0.64,0.91), respectively.

Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis

Subgroup analysis stratified by operative time reveals a clinically meaningful pattern in the association between ALI and mortality. While the test for interaction was not statistically significant (p for interaction = 0.663), the protective effect of a high ALI was markedly more pronounced in patients undergoing longer procedures (HR 0.11, 95% CI 0.01–0.95) compared to those with shorter operative times (HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.06–6.30). This suggests that the prognostic value of ALI may be particularly strong and statistically significant in patients subjected to more complex or prolonged surgery, who likely endure greater physiological stress (Figure S5).

Sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the study results. After removal of patients with Emergency surgery, sensitivity analyses showed that the association between ALI and mortality in different models remained strong (Table S2).

Discussion

This study suggests a clinically relevant association between preoperative ALI and short- and mid-term mortality following lung cancer resection. In this retrospective cohort of 2447 patients, ALI was independently associated with survival following pulmonary resection after multivariable adjustment. Patients with low ALI levels (< 37.22) had significantly higher mortality at 90 days (1.6% vs. 0.1%), 180 days (3.2% vs. 0.5%), and during hospitalization (2.0% vs. 0.4%). In the fully adjusted model, the high ALI group (≥ 37.22) was associated with substantially lower hazard for 90-day (HR 0.18, 95% CI 0.04–0.81) and 180-day mortality (HR 0.30, 95% CI 0.13–0.70), and lower odds of in-hospital mortality (OR 0.34, 95% CI 0.12–0.96). These results support the potential utility of ALI as a pragmatic prognostic indicator in the preoperative assessment of patients undergoing lung cancer surgery. ROC curves showed that ALI had good predictive power for 90-day mortality in patients following lung cancer resection.

Originally introduced by Jafri et al. [7] for prognostic assessment in advanced NSCLC, the ALI has primarily been investigated in non-surgical lung cancer cohorts and in other malignancies. For instance, Zhang et al. [8] reported that lower ALI was associated with poorer overall survival following gastrectomy for gastric cancer. In contrast, this study focuses on patients undergoing pulmonary resection, extending the use of ALI to surgically managed lung cancer populations. Three key limitations characterize current research in this area: First, conventional biomarkers often rely on unidimensional indicators, which fail to capture the complexity of postoperative recovery [12]. Second, most ALI studies in lung cancer have been conducted in palliative care settings [13], lacking prospective validation in surgical cohorts. Third, prior investigations frequently overlook the multi-layered impact of surgical stress on inflammation and metabolism. Consistent with findings by Shibutani et al. [9] in colorectal cancer, our study confirms ALI's predictive value for short-term postoperative outcomes [14].

Importantly, while Wen et al. identified a cut-off value of 262.7 for ALI in hepatocellular carcinoma, our study determined an optimal threshold of 37.22 for surgical lung cancer patients, highlighting cancer-specific and treatment-specific differences. ALI's integration of both inflammatory and nutritional markers provides a more comprehensive systemic assessment than standalone indices such as NLR or PLR [15]. A meta-analysis by Xin et al. [16] further supports this, demonstrating that reduced ALI consistently predicts poorer survival across various malignancies. These findings reinforce ALI’s role as a valuable, cancer prognostic biomarker.

The prognostic value of ALI lies in its integrated evaluation of nutritional status (BMI and albumin) and systemic inflammation (NLR). A low ALI score reflects chronic inflammation and malnutrition, both of which are associated with poor cancer outcomes. First, systemic inflammation plays a crucial role in tumor development, progression, and metastasis [17]. An elevated NLR signifies a pro-inflammatory state associated with immunosuppression within the tumor microenvironment. Neutrophils contribute to tumor invasion and angiogenesis through the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and proteolytic enzymes [18], while lymphocytes—particularly CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells—are essential for anti-tumor immunity. Reduced lymphocyte counts suggest impaired immune surveillance and weakened host defense [19]. Second, albumin, synthesized in the liver, serves as a marker of both nutritional status and hepatic function. Hypoalbuminemia is commonly associated with malnutrition and chronic inflammation. McMillan et al. [20] proposed that inflammation, rather than nutrition alone, may be the primary cause of hypoalbuminemia. Inflammatory responses can suppress hepatic albumin synthesis and increase vascular permeability, contributing to albumin loss into extravascular spaces and resulting in reduced serum levels. Third, BMI reflects overall nutritional reserves. Cancer-related weight loss and cachexia can lead to decreased BMI, which has been associated with increased postoperative complications and poorer survival outcomes [21]. However, BMI interpretation requires context, as elevated BMI may paradoxically predict adverse outcomes in certain cancer types. By synthesizing these elements, ALI offers a comprehensive evaluation of systemic status, thereby capturing inflammatory burden, immune competence, and nutritional reserve. These interconnected physiological domains influence perioperative resilience, recovery potential, and long-term prognosis.

In our study, we found the association between ALI (as a categorical variable) and 365-day mortality was attenuated in the fully adjusted model (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.42–1.05, p = 0.082). We posit that this phenomenon is best explained by the shifting dominance of risk determinants over the postoperative course. ALI, which integrates nutritional and inflammatory markers, primarily reflect a patient's physiological capacity to withstand surgical stress, and thus shows stronger predictive power for short-term mortality, which is predominantly driven by postoperative complications and the immediate sequelae of the operation. As follow-up extends to 1 year, survival is increasingly determined by factors such as tumor biology, the occurrence of recurrence or metastasis, and the efficacy of subsequent oncological therapies. We acknowledge in the limitations that our database lacks detailed information on postoperative recurrence, metastasis, and specific adjuvant treatments.. The inability to fully adjust for these potent, long-term prognostic drivers in Model III likely contributes to the observed attenuation of the ALI effect. Consequently, ALI appears most applicable for short-to-medium term risk stratification and perioperative management, rather than as an independent predictor of long-term survival.

In the present study, we observed that the area under the ROC curve of ALI was superior to that of NLR or albumin alone for predicting 90-day mortality, suggesting its higher discriminative value. This finding can be explained by the fundamental premise that ALI, as a composite index, provides a more holistic reflection of the patient's preoperative physiological state than its individual components. First, while NLR is a recognized marker of systemic inflammation, it fails to capture the critical dimension of nutritional reserve. An elevated NLR indicates a pro-inflammatory and immunosuppressive state, but it does not account for the patient's metabolic capacity to withstand the catabolic stress of surgery. Conversely, albumin, though a robust indicator of nutritional status and chronic illness, may not fully reflect the acuity and intensity of the systemic inflammatory response. ALI integrates these two distinct yet interconnected pathophysiological pathways. By mathematically combining NLR with albumin and BMI, ALI simultaneously quantifies the inflammatory burden and the nutritional-metabolic reserve, creating a more balanced and comprehensive profile of the patient's overall fitness for surgery.

Our study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the retrospective design carries an inherent risk of residual confounding and selection bias, despite multivariable adjustment and sensitivity analyses. More importantly, the absence of key clinical variables in the INSPIRE database—such as clinical and pathological tumor stage, details on neoadjuvant therapy, specific histologic subtypes, and postoperative complications—markedly restricts the comprehensiveness of our adjustment for potential confounders. The inability to account for tumor stage and preoperative treatments is particularly critical, as these factors may directly influence both ALI values and mortality outcomes. In addition, the lack of cause-of-death data precluded any analysis of cancer-specific versus non-cancer-related mortality; thus, all reported outcomes represent all-cause mortality, and any inference regarding shifts in the predominant cause of death over time remains speculative. Although data on steroid use or specific inflammatory diagnoses were also unavailable, we addressed potential confounding from acute inflammation by adjusting for laboratory markers of systemic inflammation (CRP, WBC, lactate) in our models. Given these constraints, our findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Future well-designed, prospective multicenter studies incorporating detailed staging, treatment, complication, and cause-of-death data are warranted to validate and extend our conclusions.

Conclusions

Our study shows that a low ALI is significantly associated with increased postoperative mortality in lung cancer patients, suggesting its potential as a novel biomarker for perioperative risk stratification.
