Enhanced Electrochemical Performance and Cycling Stability of High-Nickel NCM90 Cathodes via g‑C3N4 Surface Coating
Authors: Pangyu Kim, Jihoon Choi, Unseock Kang, Jeeyoung Yoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
High-nickel layered oxide cathodes, such as LiNi0.9Co0.05Mn0.05O2 (NCM90), represent promising options for next-generation lithium-ion batteries owing to their high specific capacity and reduced cobalt content. Nevertheless, structural degradation, transition metal (TM) dissolution, and electrolyte decomposition at elevated voltages (>4.1 V vs Li/Li+) significantly constrain their long-term performance. In this investigation, we apply a graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) coating on NCM90 particles through a straightforward two-step thermal annealing process, with melamine as the precursor. The nitrogen-rich g-C3N4 layer enhances the surface structural stability of NCM90, thereby mitigating transition metal (TM) dissolution and facilitating the formation of a stable cathode–electrolyte interphase. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy revealed a decrease in charge transfer resistance (R ct) from 220.2 Ω to 144.3 Ω. Following 100 charge–discharge cycles at 0.5C, the g-C3N4-coated NCM90 retained a specific capacity of 179.67 mAh g– 1 (corresponding to 81% retention), compared to 74.32 mAh g– 1 (34%) for the pristine sample. Furthermore, the coated sample demonstrated enhanced high-rate performance, with 182.96 mAh g– 1 at 5C. Notably, in full-cell evaluations for practical cycle life, the g-C3N4-coated NCM90 retained a discharge capacity of 120.16 mAh g– 1 (83% retention) after 100 cycles at 2C, whereas the pristine sample exhibited only 83.51 mAh g– 1 (57%). These findings illustrate that g-C3N4 surface coatings effectively alleviate interfacial degradation and enhance electrochemical performance, providing a scalable approach for stabilizing high-voltage Ni-rich cathodes.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of the electric vehicle (EV) industry and the increasing reliance on portable electronics have heightened the demand for high-performance lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) that provide high energy density, extended cycle life, and enhanced safety. , Among the components of LIBs, the cathode material plays a pivotal role in determining the overall energy storage capacity and long-term reliability. High-nickel layered oxide cathodes, particularly LiNi0.9Co0.05Mn0.05O2 (NCM90), have garnered significant attention owing to their high specific capacity and improved cost-effectiveness achieved through cobalt reduction. However, the elevated Ni content also exacerbates inherent structural and chemical instabilities, particularly under high cutoff voltages (>4.1 V vs Li/Li+). −

At high voltages, Ni2+ is oxidized to Ni4+, which accelerates electrolyte decomposition and generates HF through LiPF6 degradation. HF reacts aggressively with cathode surfaces, causing the dissolution of transition metals (TMs) such as Ni, Co, and Mn into the electrolyte. Simultaneously, repeated lithium extraction induces cation disorder, oxygen loss, and irreversible phase transitions (e.g., layered-to-rock-salt), leading to increased interfacial resistance, surface reconstruction, and significant capacity degradation. , These degradation mechanisms, combined with intergranular cracking and breakdown of the cathode–electrolyte interphase (CEI), ultimately undermine the electrochemical stability and power capability of the battery. ,

To circumvent these challenges, surface coating strategies have become prominent as a practical and scalable method for improving the interface stability of high-Ni cathodes. − Among various options, graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) has emerged as a promising coating material because of its robust chemical structure, easy synthesis, and multifunctionality. Structurally, the g-C3N4 layer, composed of sp2 -bonded C and N atoms connected through CN and C–N linkages, features a conjugated π-electron system that imparts partial electronic conductivity. The delocalized π-electron network facilitates interfacial charge transport between the coating and the NCM surface, thereby decreasing the charge-transfer resistance (Rct) and mitigating polarization during cycling. Moreover, the extended conjugation within the heptazine framework allows π-electron delocalization through CC and C–N bonds, establishing an electronic percolation pathway that supports coupled Li+–electron transport at high rates. This conductive network not only improves the rate capability but also stabilizes the local electronic potential distribution across the cathode surface. − Simultaneously, g-C3N4 acts as a chemically robust passivation layer that prevents direct contact between the electrolyte and the active surface. By suppressing parasitic reactions and surface reconstruction, the coating enhances both structural and interfacial stability, effectively inhibiting transition-metal dissolution during repeated cycling. ,,

In contrast to previous studies that mainly investigated g-C3N4 coatings on NCM811 systems under moderate voltage ranges, the present work focuses on a more industrially relevant high-nickel composition, LiNi0.9Co0.05Mn0.05O2 (NCM90). This composition poses greater structural and interfacial challenges due to its higher Ni content and stronger tendency toward phase transitions, making it a more stringent platform for evaluating the effectiveness of g-C3N4 coatings. In addition, to fully exploit the intrinsic semiconducting and electron-conductive properties of g-C3N4, the conductive carbon content in the cathode slurry was reduced from 10 wt % (as commonly reported in previous studies) to 5 wt % without compromising electronic percolation. Moreover, electrochemical assessments were carried out within an extended voltage window of 2.7–4.4 V (vs Li/Li+), beyond the conventional 2.6–4.2 V range, to rigorously examine the interfacial and structural stability of the coated cathode under high-voltage stress. Furthermore, to gain deeper insights into the degradation mechanisms, transition-metal dissolution after cycling was systematically evaluated both qualitatively and quantitatively using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS). In parallel, cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to observe the formation and propagation of microcracks within secondary particles, providing a direct correlation between surface stability and mechanical integrity. These design modifications underscore the distinctiveness of this study, which validates the multifunctional role of g-C3N4 under more demanding and practically relevant conditions for next-generation high-Ni cathodes.

In this study, we present a straightforward and scalable thermal annealing method for depositing g-C3N4 onto NCM90 particles using melamine as a precursor. The effects of the coating on the structural integrity, thermal stability, and electrochemical performance of the cathode were thoroughly evaluated. Particularly, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) revealed a notable decrease in charge transfer resistance (R ct) from 220.2 Ω (pristine) to 144.3 Ω (coated), suggesting enhanced interfacial kinetics. Following 100 charge–discharge cycles at 0.5C, the g-C3N4-coated NCM90 maintained a specific capacity of 179.67 mAh g– 1 (81% capacity retention), surpassing the pristine sample (74.32 mAh g– 1, 34% retention). Moreover, the coated sample exhibited a specific capacity of 182.96 mAh g– 1 at 5C, demonstrating enhanced rate capability. After 100 cycles at 2C in full-cell testing, the coated NCM90 preserved a higher discharge capacity of 120.16 mAh g– 1 (83% retention), while the unmodified sample suffered a rapid decline to 83.51 mAh g– 1 (57%). These findings highlight the efficacy of g-C3N4 coatings in addressing interfacial instability and performance decline in high-Ni cathodes, thereby facilitating the advancement of robust, high-energy LIBs.

Experimental Section

Materials

The active material utilized in this study was LiNi0.90Co0.05Mn0.05O2 (NCM90). For the production of the cathode electrode, NCM90 powder, conductive carbon (Super P, TIMCAL), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVdF, HSV 900, Kynar) were mixed in a weight ratio of 90:5:5 (wt %) in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, Sigma-Aldrich, anhydrous, 99.5%) to form a uniform slurry. Silicon anode was prepared by dispersing mSi powder (Daejoo), Super P, and poly­(acrylic acid) (PAA, MTI korea) at 70:20:10 (wt %), was combined with NMP solvent. The slurry was homogenized and defoamed using a Thinky mixer (AR-100, MD BROS) at 800 rpm for 30 min in a 12 mL vessel filled with zirconia balls.

The prepared slurry was applied using a precision coating machine (SINOPRO) with the doctor blade gap set to 125 μm. The cathode was coated onto a 23 μm-thick aluminum foil (MTI Korea), while the anode was coated onto a 9 μm-thick copper foil (MTI Korea). The electrodes were then dried in a convection oven at 80 °C for 3 h to eliminate the solvent, followed by vacuum drying at 80 °C for an additional 12 h to remove residual moisture. The final dried thicknesses were approximately 30–35 μm for the cathode and 28–30 μm for the anode. All electrodes were kept in an Ar-filled glovebox (KOREA KIYON, Ar purity >99.999%) with O2 and H2O levels below 0.1 ppm to maintain an inert, moisture-free environment.

Melamine (Daejungchem) served as the g-C3N4 precursor. The powder underwent vacuum drying at 80 °C for 12 h before being used to remove moisture and ensure consistent synthesis. The dried melamine was evenly mixed with NCM90 powder and then underwent a two-step calcination process in an Ar atmosphere. Initially, the mixture was heated to 350 °C for 2 h to vaporize melamine and deposit it onto NCM90 particles. Subsequently, a second calcination at 550 °C for 2 h was conducted to enhance polymerization and carbonization, resulting in the formation of a stable and uniform g-C3N4 coating. Following the coating process, the NCM90@g-CN powders were stored in a sealed container to shield them from humidity, thereby maintaining their chemical stability and reactivity.

Material Synthesis

The synthesis method of g-C3N4-coated NCM90 (referred to as NCM90@g-CN) is depicted in Figure . To enhance reactivity and reduce moisture disruption, both NCM90 and melamine powders underwent vacuum drying at 80 °C for 12 h. Subsequently, the two powders were combined using an agate mortar and further mixed at high speed in a Thinky mixer (AR-100, MD BROS) to achieve a uniform distribution of melamine on the surface of NCM90 particles.

Schematic illustration of the synthesis process of NCM90@g-CN cathode materials.

The mixed powders were transferred to an alumina crucible and subjected to thermal treatment in a box furnace (C-A14DF-85, HANTECH) under a nitrogen (N2) atmosphere to prevent oxidation. Initially, the temperature was raised to 350 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min and maintained for 2 h to facilitate melamine vaporization and ensure uniform surface exposure. Subsequently, the temperature was increased to 550 °C and maintained for an additional 2 h to promote the polymerization and condensation of melamine, leading to the development of a thin, uniform g-C3N4 layer. ,

Following calcination, the crucible was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature for over 6 h to prevent thermal stress and maintain the structural integrity of the coated particles. The color evolution of melamine during this processfrom white (pristine) to pale yellow at 450 °C, light yellow at 500 °C, and deep yellow at 550–600 °Cwas visually observed and is illustrated in Figure S1, confirming the gradual transformation to graphitic carbon nitride. −

Characterization

To examine the structural and compositional stability of the synthesized materials, a range of physicochemical characterization techniques were utilized. X-ray diffraction (XRD, EMPYREAN, Panalytical, Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.5406 Å) was conducted across a 2θ range of 10°–90° to ascertain the crystal structure and confirm the phase purity post-g-C3N4 coating. Rietveld refinement was employed to assess alterations in lattice parameters. The findings validated that the g-C3N4 coating maintained the layered α-NaFeO2 structure of NCM90 without introducing additional phases or structural deformations.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA-DSC, Discovery SDT 650, Auto TGA) was conducted under a N2 atmosphere to evaluate the thermal stability of pristine g-C3N4, pristine NCM90, and NCM90@g-CN. The samples were heated from room temperature to 1000 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min. The g-C3N4 coating demonstrated thermal stability up to approximately 550 °C without notable decomposition, confirming its durability under standard battery operating conditions.

The nanoscale morphology and coating uniformity were analyzed using field-emission transmission electron microscopy (FE-TEM, Titan G2 ChemiSTEM Cs Probe, FEI). Powder samples were dispersed in ethanol and drop-cast onto carbon-coated copper grids. High-resolution TEM and selected area electron diffraction images were captured to assess the crystallinity of the g-C3N4 layer. Elemental distribution was additionally examined by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), confirming a conformal coating with a thickness of approximately ∼9–10 nm.

Particle morphology and surface features pre- and postcycling were examined through field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, SU8230, Hitachi). Cross-sectional images of the electrodes were obtained postion milling (Ar Blade 5000, HITACHI) to assess internal structural deterioration and crack development. These assessments facilitated a direct comparison of microstructural changes in the unaltered and coated electrodes after prolonged cycling.

The level of TMs (Ni, Co, Mn) dissolution from the cathode into the lithium anode after 100 cycles was measured using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, NexION 2000, PerkinElmer). The lithium anodes were digested in diluted nitric acid, filtered, and analyzed to evaluate TM movement. These data provided a quantitative measure of the effectiveness of the g-C3N4 coating in reducing TM release and improving interfacial stability.

Laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS, J200, Applied Spectra, 266 nm Nd:YAG laser) was used to further investigate TM redistribution within the cathode. Depth profiling was performed by ablating 9 successive layers from the surface toward the electrode core. At each depth, a 7 × 7 grid mapping was conducted at a laser energy of approximately 4 mJ/pulse, with three accumulations per spot. To reduce matrix effects and ensure signal consistency, oxygen was used as an internal standard (isO normalization). To evaluate the degree of transition metal dissolution before and after cycling, LIBS depth profiling was conducted across nine ablation layers, starting from the electrode surface (first layer) and progressing toward the bulk. The ninth layer corresponds to the region near the bottom of the electrode, effectively representing the bulk interior. Figures h,i and S8 present both 3D and cross-sectional 2D LIBS elemental maps, which visualize the depth-dependent distribution of transition metals. These maps revealed that the pristine NCM90 electrode exhibited extensive transition metal dissolution from the surface to the bulk interior, while the NCM90@g-CN electrode maintained a significantly suppressed dissolution profile throughout the electrode depth after cycling. This observation clearly highlights the effectiveness of the g-C3N4 coating in suppressing TM migration and surface degradation during electrochemical cycling.

Electrochemical Measurements

CR2032 coin cells were constructed in an Ar-filled glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm, KOREA KIYON) using lithium metal foil (thickness: 100 μm, SHIN HYUNG E&T Co.) as the counter/reference electrode and NCM90 or NCM90@g-CN as the working electrode. The cathode slurry was prepared by blending the active material, Super P, and PVdF in a weight ratio of 90:5:5, followed by dispersion in NMP. The slurry was coated onto 23 μm thick aluminum foil (MTI Korea), dried, and punched into 14 mm circular discs. A polyethylene separator (Celgard 2400) and 80 μL of electrolyte (1 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate/diethyl carbonate, 1:1 v/v, Enchem) were utilized in each cell. The anode slurry was prepared by blending the active material, Super P, and PAA in a weight ratio of 70:20:10, followed by dispersion in NMP.

The slurry was coated onto 9 μm-thick copper foil (MTI Korea), dried, and punched into 14 mm circular discs. The areal mass loading of the cathode and anode was approximately 5.6 and 1.4 mg cm– 2, respectively, corresponding to a balanced capacity ratio of N/P ≈ 1.2. For full-cell assembly, a silicon-based anode was used instead of lithium metal, while all other conditions were identical to those of the half-cell configuration.

Galvanostatic charge–discharge experiments were conducted using a battery cycler (WBC 3000L, WonATech) within the voltage range of 2.7 to 4.4 V (vs Li/Li+) at a temperature of 30 °C. The cells were initially activated at a rate of 0.1C for five cycles and then cycled at 0.5C for 100 cycles to evaluate long-term cycling stability. For the full-cell tests, cycling was conducted at 30 °C within a voltage range of 2.7–4.4 V (vs Li/Li+). Following an initial activation step at 0.1C for three cycles, the cells were evaluated for long-term cycling stability at 2C for 100 cycles. Rate capability was evaluated at different current densities of 0.1C, 0.2C, 0.5C, 1C, 2C, and 5C, each for five cycles.

Prior to cycling using a potentiostat (SP-300, BioLogic) across a frequency range of 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz with an amplitude of 10 mV, EIS measurements were conducted. The Nyquist plots were assessed employing an equivalent circuit model to derive the bulk resistance (R b) and charge transfer resistance (R ct), facilitating a comparison of interfacial resistance between pristine and g-C3N4-coated cathodes. The obtained values were further supported by voltage gap analysis from charge–discharge curves and differential capacity (dQ/dV) plots, offering insights into the polarization behavior and redox kinetics throughout repeated cycling.

All electrochemical measurements were conducted under temperature-controlled conditions (30 ± 1 °C) to maintain consistency and reduce environmental variability.

Figure a illustrates the XRD patterns of melamine and its thermally treated products. Below 450 °C, characteristic peaks of crystalline melamine are evident. As the temperature increases to 450 °C, the diffraction peaks broaden and decrease, indicating a shift to intermediate products such as melem and melon. At 550 °C, two distinct peaks emerge at 13.2° and 27.4°, corresponding to the (100) and (002) planes of g-C3N4, respectively, signifying in-plane ordering and interlayer stacking of tri-s-triazine units. Crystallinity marginally increases at 600 °C; however, excessive crystallinity could impede defect-related lithium-ion pathways. Therefore, 550 °C was determined as the optimal calcination temperature. ,

Figure b illustrates the XRD patterns of pristine NCM90 and NCM90@g-CN powders postcoating at 550 °C. Both samples exhibit a layered α-NaFeO2 structure (space group R3̅m) without impurity phases, indicating that the g-C3N4 coating process maintains the bulk structure. According to Table S1, Rietveld refinement shows minimal differences in lattice parameters between the two samples: the c/a ratio remains at 4.932, and the unit cell volume slightly increases from 101.91 Å3 to 102.10 Å3 after coating, suggesting insignificant structural distortion. The I (003)/I (104) ratios1.59 for pristine and 1.57 for coatedfurther validate the preservation of cation ordering.

Figure c shows the TGA profiles of pristine g-C3N4, pristine NCM90, and NCM90@g-CN. The pristine NCM90 sample shows a minimal weight loss of approximately 0.685% near 690.3 °C. In contrast, the NCM90@g-CN sample remains thermally stable up to approximately 550 °C, beyond which it shows a 1.073% weight loss, corresponding to the decomposition of the g-C3N4 coating layer. Pure g-C3N4 shows significant degradation onset at approximately 631.3 °C. These thermal behaviors, detailed in Table S2, confirm that the coating is stable under conventional LIB operating conditions.

The TEM and high-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) images in Figure d,e present direct evidence of the coating morphology. Pristine NCM90 particles (Figure d) display clean and smooth surfaces. The corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern exhibits well-defined diffraction spots, which are indicative of a highly ordered layered structure, consistent with the α-NaFeO2-type crystal structure of NCM90. In contrast, the coated NCM90@g-CN (Figure e) clearly exhibits island-like g-C3N4 coating domains approximately ∼9–10 nm thick. The thickness of the coating layer was determined from the intensity–distance line profile using the Gatan Digital Micrograph software (Figure S4). The contrast transition corresponding to the coating region extended from approximately 8–21 nm along the line profile, yielding an estimated thickness of ≈13 nm. Considering the interface gradient and projection angle, the effective coating thickness was evaluated to be ∼9–10 nm on average. The coating exhibits a primarily amorphous structure, as indicated by the broad, diffuse ring in the FFT pattern of the coated region (Figure e), characteristic of amorphous carbon. In addition, localized lattice fringes originating from crystalline g-C3N4 domains reveal partial ordering, which indicates a hybrid structure that can improve both ion transport and interfacial stability. These microstructural features provide compelling evidence for the successful formation of a coating layer on the NCM surface. It is crack-free and well-adhered, maintaining particle integrity during high-temperature operation.

High-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) and EDS elemental mapping images (Figure f) confirm the consistent presence of carbon and nitrogen elements on the particle surface, originating from g-C3N4. Quantitative EDS results (Table S3) show that NCM90@g-CN contains 3.30 wt % C and 0.64 wt % N, whereas the pristine sample contains only 0.13 wt % C and undetectable N. This validates the effective development of a nitrogen-rich protective layer.

In summary, the g-C3N4 coating, derived from melamine, forms on the NCM90 surface without altering its bulk crystal structure. This coating provides extra thermal stability and interfacial protection, crucial for sustaining long-term performance in high-voltage LIBs.

EIS was conducted before the cycling to assess the initial interfacial resistance (Figure a). The pristine NCM90 exhibited a charge transfer resistance (R ct) of 220.2 Ω and bulk resistance (R b) of 0.86 Ω, whereas NCM90@g-CN exhibited notably lower R ct (144.3 Ω) and R b (0.68 Ω), as outlined in Table S4. The decrease in interfacial impedance can be attributed to the conductive and chemically stable nature of the g-C3N4 coating, enabling quicker charge transfer at the cathode–electrolyte interface. A magnified Nyquist plot highlighting the Rb variation is presented in Figure S5.

Rate capability was assessed across different current densities ranging from 0.1C to 5C (Figure b). Although both samples performed similarly at 0.1C and 0.2C, the coated electrode demonstrated higher capacities from 0.5C onward. At 5C, NCM90@g-CN achieved a capacity of 182.96 mAh g– 1, surpassing the pristine counterpart (167.68 mAh g– 1), suggesting that the coating improves ionic/electronic conductivity and maintains structural integrity during high-rate cycling.

Cycling performance at 0.5C for 100 cycles (Figure c) clearly demonstrates the improved durability due to g-C3N4 modification. Pristine NCM90 showed a rapid decline in specific capacity to 74.32 mAh g– 1, whereas NCM90@g-CN maintained 179.67 mAh g– 1, indicating a 2.2-fold increase in capacity retention. This enhancement is attributed to the protective role of the coating, which mitigates surface side reactions and structural degradation.

Figure d,e illustrates the differential capacity (dQ/dV) curves of pristine NCM90 and NCM90@g-CN from the first to 100th cycles at 0.5C. The pristine electrode exhibits distinct redox peaks near 4.2 V, which become more pronounced with cycling, suggesting the H2 → H3 phase transition and structural instability. , In contrast, NCM90@g-CN maintains broader, more consistent peaks with limited growth, indicating decreased polarization and mitigated phase transition. These findings highlight the role of the g-C3N4 layer in enhancing structural stability during repetitive cycling.

Figure S6a,b illustrates the progression of the average voltage difference between the first and 100th cycles. The pristine sample exhibited an increase from 0.05 to 0.71 V, whereas NCM90@g-CN exhibited a significantly smaller increment from 0.03 to 0.21 V. This decrease in polarization confirms that the g-C3N4 layer aids in preserving interfacial stability during cycling, indicating reduced voltage hysteresis and diminished internal resistance accumulation over cycles. Furthermore, Figure S6c,d exhibit the discharge voltage profiles of both electrodes at 0.5C, revealing that NCM90@g-CN maintained a more consistent voltage plateau compared to the irregular curves observed in the pristine sample, further substantiating its enhanced electrochemical stability.

Figure S7 illustrates comprehensive full-cell evaluations conducted under a stringent 2C charge–discharge regime for 100 consecutive cycles, aimed at further validating the practical cycle life performance. The g-C3N4-coated NCM90 electrode sustained a discharge capacity of 120.16 mAh g– 1, corresponding to 83% retention of its initial capacity. In stark contrast, the pristine counterpart exhibited a pronounced fading behavior, maintaining only 83.51 mAh g– 1 with 57% retention under identical conditions. This clear disparity provides compelling evidence that the g-C3N4 modification effectively mitigates interfacial degradation and structural instabilitykey factors that typically accelerate capacity decay in high-Ni cathodes when operated in practical full-cell configurations. These results not only reaffirm the beneficial role of the coating in enhancing electrode durability but also highlight its potential applicability for next-generation high-energy lithium-ion batteries where long-term cycling stability is critical. Furthermore, the Nyquist plots in Figure S7b demonstrate a noticeably smaller semicircle for the NCM90@g-CN electrode compared to the pristine counterpart, indicating a substantially lower charge-transfer resistance (R ct). This reduction reflects an improved Li+ transport and enhanced interfacial charge kinetics, attributed to the conductive and chemically stable nature of the g-C3N4 coating. In addition, the voltage profiles shown in Figure S7c,d reveal reduced polarization and stable voltage plateaus throughout repeated cycles for the coated electrode, further confirming that the coating suppresses resistive layer growth and maintains electrode integrity under high-rate cycling conditions.

These results collectively confirm that g-C3N4 coating substantially improves the electrochemical performance of high-Ni NCM90 cathodes by stabilizing the surface interface, reducing polarization, and improving both cycling stability and rate capability.

To evaluate the structural and compositional stability of the electrodes following extended cycling, postmortem analyses were conducted using SEM, inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), and LIBS (Figures and S8). SEM images of the powder and cross sections (Figure a–f) reveal that pristine NCM90, after 100 cycles at 0.5C, displays significant microcrack formation and internal fracture, indicating severe mechanical degradation. In contrast, the NCM90@g-CN sample retained a more intact structure with minimal crack propagation, confirming that the g-C3N4 coating effectively mitigates particle-level mechanical damage during repetitive cycling. Furthermore, the cross-sectional SEM images provide more direct evidence of the degradation behavior after 100 cycles at 0.5C. As highlighted by the red dashed circles in Figure c,f, the pristine NCM90 electrode exhibits pronounced intergranular cracks, which occur at the grain boundaries between adjacent primary particles. These intergranular cracks serve as pathways for electrolyte penetration, thereby accelerating parasitic reactions and promoting severe transition metal dissolution during cycling. In contrast, the NCM90@g-CN electrode maintains a coherent microstructure without noticeable crack formation between the primary grains. Instead, only clear grain boundaries can be distinguished, indicating that the g-C3N4 coating effectively suppresses microcrack propagation and preserves the integrity of the primary particles after long-term cycling.

The mitigation of degradation is further supported by quantitative ICP-MS analysis (Table S5), indicating a significant decrease in the levels of Ni, Co, and Mn dissolved into the electrolyte postcycling in the coated sample. Specifically, Ni dissolution decreased from 33.95 ppb in pristine NCM90 to 19.01 ppb in NCM90@g-CN, with similar reductions observed for Co and Mn. This indicates that the coating functions as a chemical barrier, restricting the dissolution of TMsa key factor contributing to interfacial instability and capacity decline.

To provide spatial insights into the depth-wise distribution of TMs within the cathode, LIBS depth profiling was performed across 9 layers from the electrode surface to the core. Oxygen served as an internal standard for normalizing signal intensity and enhancing comparability. Figures h,i and S8 illustrate elemental distribution maps of Ni, Co, and Mn from the electrode surface (first layer) to the bulk interior (ninth layer). Both the pristine NCM90 and NCM90@g-CN electrodes exhibited a uniformly distributed transition metal profile from the surface to the bulk prior to cycling. But, after cycling, the pristine NCM90 electrode exhibited relatively weak transition metal signals not only on the surface but also deep within the bulk. Notably, among the widely reported transition metal dissolution phenomena in NCM cathodes, the pronounced leaching of Ni is particularly evident. This trend is consistently observed in both the ICP-MS results and the LIBS depth profiling data. In contrast, the NCM90@g-CN electrode exhibited weaker TM signals near the surface but stronger and more uniform signals in the inner bulk, suggesting that metal ion redistribution and TM dissolution were significantly suppressed. While LIBS yields semiquantitative outcomes, the observed patterns in TM redistribution clearly support the protective function of the g-C3N4 layer.

Collectively, these results confirm that the g-C3N4 coating enhances both the mechanical and chemical robustness of high-Ni cathodes. By effectively mitigating intergranular cracking and suppressing TM dissolution and migration, the coating contributes to the long-term stability and reliability of the NCM90 cathode, even under high-voltage operation. These enhancements provide a robust foundation for the subsequent performance gains discussed in the following section.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a melamine-derived g-C3N4 surface coating effectively enhances both the structural integrity and electrochemical performance of high-nickel NCM90 cathodes. The g-C3N4 layer (∼9–10 nm thick) functions as a multifunctional interfacial barrier, simultaneously suppressing HF-driven electrolyte decomposition, mitigating transition-metal (TM) dissolution, inhibiting intergranular microcrack propagation, and stabilizing the cathode/electrolyte interface during high-voltage cycling.

Structural and thermal analyses (XRD and TGA) confirmed that the coating process preserved the layered crystal structure and thermal robustness of NCM90. Morphological characterizations (HR-TEM and EDS mapping) revealed a crack-free g-C3N4 layer tightly adhered to the surface. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) verified a marked reduction in charge-transfer resistance and a slower increase in interfacial impedance upon cycling, indicating improved interfacial charge transport and surface stability. Consequently, the coated cathode achieved superior electrochemical durability, maintaining a high discharge capacity of 179.67 mAh g– 1 with 81% retention after 100 cycles at 0.5 C and excellent rate capability up to 5 C.

Postcycling analyses using ICP-MS and LIBS depth profiling revealed substantially reduced TM dissolution and a more homogeneous elemental distribution across the electrode depth, confirming the coating’s dual chemical and mechanical protection mechanisms. Furthermore, full-cell evaluations demonstrated that the g-C3N4-coated NCM90 retained 83% of its initial capacity after 100 cycles at 2 C, accompanied by reduced voltage polarization and stable impedance evolution, underscoring its practical durability under high-rate operating conditions.

Overall, g-C3N4 serves as a comprehensive protective layer that simultaneously provides chemical passivation, mechanical reinforcement, and interfacial stabilization, offering a scalable and practical surface-engineering strategy for high-energy-density, long-life lithium-ion batteries.
