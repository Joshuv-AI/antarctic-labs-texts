Beyond Eager Encodings: A Theory-Agnostic Approach to Theory-Lemma Enumeration in SMT
Emanuele Civini, Gabriele Masina, Giuseppe Spallitta, Roberto Sebastiani

Abstract
Lifting Boolean-reasoning techniques to the SMT level most often requires producing theory lemmas that rule out theory-inconsistent truth assignments. With standard SMT solving, it is common to “lazily” generate such lemmas on demand during the search; with some harder SMT-level tasks —such as unsat-core extraction, MaxSMT, 𝒯 \mathcal{T} -OBDD or 𝒯 \mathcal{T} -SDD compilation— it may be beneficial or even necessary to “eagerly” pre-compute all the needed theory lemmas upfront. Whereas in principle “classic” eager SMT encodings could do the job, they are specific for very few and easy theories, they do not comply with theory combination, and may produce lots of unnecessary lemmas. In this paper, we present theory-agnostic methods for enumerating complete sets of theory lemmas tailored to a given formula. Starting from AllSMT as a baseline approach, we propose improved lemma-enumeration techniques, including divide&conquer, projected enumeration, and theory-driven partitioning, which are highly parallelizable and which may drastically improve scalability. An experimental evaluation demonstrates that these techniques significantly enhance efficiency and enable the method to scale to substantially more complex instances.

Contributions.

In this paper, we address this problem explicitly, and we present

Background

Notation & terminology.

We assume the reader is familiar with the basic syntax, semantics, and results of propositional and first-order logics.
We adopt the following terminology and notation.

A propositional formula is either a Boolean constant (
\top
or
\bot
representing “true” and “false”, respectively), a Boolean atom, or a formula built from propositional connectives (
\neg,\land,\lor,\to,\leftrightarrow
) over propositional formulas. Propositional satisfiability (SAT) is the problem of deciding the satisfiability of propositional formulas.
Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) extends SAT to the context of first-order formulas modulo some background theory
\mathcal{T}
, which provides an intended interpretation for constant, function, and predicate symbols
[
1
]
. We restrict to quantifier-free formulas.
A
\mathcal{T}
-formula is a combination of theory-specific atoms (
\mathcal{T}
-atoms) and
Boolean atoms (
\mathbb{B}
-atoms) via Boolean connectives
(“atoms” denotes
\mathcal{T}
- and
\mathbb{B}
-atoms indifferently).
For instance, the theory of linear real arithmetic (
\mathcal{LRA}
) provides
the standard interpretations of arithmetic operators (
+
,
-
,

\cdot
) and relations (
=
,
\leq
,
<
,
>
) over the reals.
\mathcal{LRA}
-atoms are linear (in)equalities over
rational variables.
An example of
\mathcal{LRA}
-formula is
((x-y\leq 3)\vee(x=z))
, where
x,y,z
are
\mathcal{LRA}
-variables, and
(x-y\leq 3),(x=z)
are
\mathcal{LRA}
-atoms.
Other theories of interest include equalities (
\mathcal{E}
), equalities with uninterpreted functions (
\mathcal{EUF}
), bit-vectors (
\mathcal{BV}
), arrays (
\mathcal{AR}
), and combinations thereof.

{\cal T}2{\cal B}
is a bijective function (“theory to Boolean”),
called

abstraction
,
which maps Boolean atoms into themselves,

\mathcal{T}
-atoms into fresh Boolean atoms,

The function
{\cal B}2{\cal T}
(“Boolean to theory”), called

{\cal T}2{\cal B}
.
(For instance

{\cal T}2{\cal B}(\{((x-y\leq 3)\vee(x=z))\})=\{(A_{1}\vee A_{2})\}

The symbol

{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\scalebox{.7}{def}}}}{{=}}\{{\alpha_{i}}\}_{i}
,
possibly with subscripts or superscripts, denotes
a set of atoms.
We represent truth assignments as conjunctions of literals.
We
denote by

{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.
The symbols

is drastically smaller than
P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\varphi)
, typically in AllSMT most of the search effort is devoted to rule out the assignments in
H_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\varphi)
.

We also remark that
partial
AllSMT may be dramatically faster than total AllSMT, since a single
\mathcal{T}
-satisfiable partial assignment represents up to
2^{k}

\mathcal{T}
-satisfiable total assignments, where
k
is the number of unassigned atoms.
For the same reason,
projected
AllSMT may be dramatically faster than non-projected AllSMT, as a single
\mathcal{T}
-satisfiable assignment on
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\prime}
represents up to
2^{k}

\mathcal{T}
-satisfiable assignments on
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.

As a final note, we also remark that the results presented in the rest
of the paper also hold for projected AllSMT algorithms which are not based on blocking clauses, e.g.,
[
17
]
. For simplicity, we carry out the discussion taking
Algorithm

1
as a reference.

3
\mathcal{T}
-Lemma Enumeration Techniques

We present four theory-agnostic methods for enumerating a set
Cl_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\varphi)
of
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas to rule out
P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\varphi)
(see
Definition

1
).
In §
3.1
we describe the method used in
[
15
]
, which we will use as a baseline.
The method is simple to implement, but relies on basic, total AllSMT enumeration, which is often intractable on real-world instances due to the combinatorial explosion of total assignments.
To address this issue, we introduce three orthogonal improvements that
drastically enhance scalability.
First, in §
3.2
we describe a divide-and-conquer strategy that can be naturally parallelized. Second, in §
3.3
we prove that projecting the enumeration onto the
\mathcal{T}
-atoms guarantees finding a complete set of
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas, thereby enabling more efficient enumeration.
Finally, in §
3.4
we prove that when the atoms of
\varphi
can be partitioned into disjoint sets,
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas can be enumerated for each partition separately. This results in a dramatic improvement in the efficiency of enumeration.
Notably, these three improvements are orthogonal and can be combined.

Example 2

Let

Algorithm

{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

A possible run may proceed as follows.

The SMT solver sets

(x=1)

\eta[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\scalebox{.7}{def}}}}{{=}}\neg(x=0)\wedge(x=1)

\mathcal{T}

(line

and returns the partial assignment
\mu_{1}[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\scalebox{.7}{def}}}}{{=}}(x=1)

(line

\mathcal{T}
-conflict is exposed, and
no
\mathcal{T}
-lemma is generated. The enumerator blocks the returned cube by
adding
\neg(x=1)
(line

). On the next iteration,

\neg(x=1)
is enforced, and unit propagation forces
(x=0)
.
The resulting total assignment

\eta_{2}[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\scalebox{.7}{def}}}}{{=}}(x=0)\wedge\neg(x=1)
is again
\mathcal{T}
-consistent
(line

Along this run, no
\mathcal{T}
-conflict is encountered, hence no
\mathcal{T}
-lemma is generated. This is because the SMT solver never explored the
\mathcal{T}
-inconsistent total assignment

This example shows that naive partial AllSMT enumeration

14

17

without exhaustive enumeration, while still leveraging compact partial assignments.

Although partial enumeration alone is not sufficient to find a set of

\mathcal{T}

This decomposition is inspired to the cube-and-conquer approach in SAT
[
12

, recently used also for model counting
[
22
]
.

To exploit this decomposition, we propose a divide-and-conquer
approach to enumerate
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas, outlined in

Algorithm

3
.
We consider first the case where
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.

First, the algorithm performs a
partial
AllSMT enumeration on
\varphi[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]

{M_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{p}=\{\mu_{1}[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}],\dots,\mu_{k}[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]\}

\mathcal{T}

\mathcal{T}
-lemmas

to prevent the SMT solver from rediscovering them in the residual subproblems (line

Overall, rather than paying once for one large total AllSMT run
on
\varphi[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]

smaller total AllSMT runs on restricted residual formulas, whose
overall cost is typically lower than one monolithic total enumeration on
\varphi[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]

Moreover, the residual enumeration tasks (line

\mu_{i}
to a process and finally merging the
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas produced by the processes.
In our architecture, processes execute in isolation, and distinct processes may independently discover identical or redundant lemmas.
In practice, in §

we show empirically that the number of lemmas found is comparable to that of the AllSMT baseline.

The correctness of this approach follows from the fact that overall, in the different iterations, all the total truth assignments are explored, thus for each
\rho[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]\in P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\varphi)
, a
\mathcal{T}
-lemma is eventually generated that rules it out.

We remark that, while our algorithm guarantees that the
\mu_{i}[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]
are
disjoint,
\mathcal{T}
-satisfiable, and propositionally satisfy
\varphi[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]

latter fact is not a strict requirement for the correctness of the approach.
Indeed, this is only one of the possible decompositions of the search space, and any family of partial assignments that covers all propositional assignments of
\varphi
would suffice. This opens up the possibility of designing alternative heuristics to decompose the search space.

Enumerating all total truth assignments, even when parallelized, is often infeasible on real-world instances. In this section, we spotlight that this is often unnecessary to find a complete set of
\mathcal{T}

Truth assignments explored during an AllSMT enumeration, whether via the classic or the divide-and-conquer approach, consist of two components: an assignment to
\mathcal{T}
-atoms and an assignment to
\mathbb{B}
-atoms.
We note that the
\mathcal{T}
-inconsistency of a truth assignment is determined exclusively by its projection onto the
\mathcal{T}
-atoms.
Consequently, a standard AllSMT procedure may inefficiently enumerate multiple truth assignments that share an identical
\mathcal{T}
-consistent projection but differ in their Boolean assignments. This redundancy is computationally wasteful, as variations in the Boolean assignment do not generate new
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas.

We can address this inefficiency by performing an AllSMT
enumeration
projected on the
\mathcal{T}
-atoms of
\varphi
. The correctness of this approach follows from the following theorem (proof in
Section

0.A.1

We now present a further improvement for the enumeration of
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas, for the
case where the set of atoms of

\varphi[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]
can be partitioned into two or more disjoint sets with respect to variables and uninterpreted symbols.
We first recall the following property, which characterizes the partitioning of atoms that we will use in the following results.

Property 1

Consider a set
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}{}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\scalebox{.7}{def}}}}{{=}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}{}\cup{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}{}
of atoms s.t. no atom in
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}
shares variables or uninterpreted symbols with any atom in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}
.
Then, for any truth assignment

\eta[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\scalebox{.7}{def}}}}{{=}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\eta_{1}}[{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}]{}\wedge{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\eta_{2}}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}]{}
,

\eta[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]
is
\mathcal{T}
-satisfiable iff both
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\eta_{1}}[{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}]{}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\eta_{2}}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}]{}
are
\mathcal{T}
-satisfiable.

Consequently, if the set of atoms of
\varphi
can be partitioned according to the conditions of
1
, then in order to rule out a
\mathcal{T}
-inconsistent truth assignment
\rho[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\scalebox{.7}{def}}}}{{=}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\rho_{1}}[{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}]\wedge{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\rho_{2}}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}]\in P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\varphi)
, a
\mathcal{T}
-lemma must rule out
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\rho_{1}}[{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}]
or
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\rho_{2}}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}]
.

This suggests a decomposition strategy: an AllSMT enumeration projected on
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}
, will find a set of
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas that rule out all
\rho[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]\in P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\varphi)
such that
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\rho_{1}}[{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}]
is
\mathcal{T}
-inconsistent.
Dually, an AllSMT enumeration projected on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}
, will find a set of
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas that rule out all
\rho[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]\in P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\varphi)
such that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\rho_{2}}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}]
is
\mathcal{T}
-inconsistent.
Thus, the enumeration of
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas can be decomposed into two separate enumerations.

This intuition is formalized in the following theorem (proof in
Section

0.A.2
).

Theorem 3.2

Let
\varphi[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]
be a
\mathcal{T}
-formula, s.t.
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}{}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\scalebox{.7}{def}}}}{{=}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}{}\cup{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}{}
and no atom in
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}
shares variables or uninterpreted symbols with any atom in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}
.

Let
\mathit{BC}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}}(\varphi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\scalebox{.7}{def}}}}{{=}}\{{\neg{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\eta_{1}}[{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}]{}\mathrm{\ s.t.\ }{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\eta_{1}}[{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}]\wedge{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\eta_{2}}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}]{}\in H_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\varphi)}\}
, and let
Cl_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
be a set of
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas s.t.
\mathit{BC}_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}}(\varphi)\cup Cl_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}{}
rules out
P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\varphi)
.

Let
\varphi^{\prime}[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\scalebox{.7}{def}}}}{{=}}\varphi\wedge\bigwedge_{C\in Cl_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}{}}C
.

Let
\mathit{BC}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}}(\varphi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\scalebox{.7}{def}}}}{{=}}\{{\neg{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\eta_{2}}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}]\mathrm{\ s.t.\ }{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\eta_{1}}[{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}}]\wedge{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\eta_{2}}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}]{}\in H_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\varphi^{\prime}[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}])}\}
, and let
Cl_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}^{\prime}
be a set of
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas s.t.
\mathit{BC}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}}(\varphi)\cup Cl_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}^{\prime}{}
rules out
P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\varphi^{\prime}[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}])
.

Then
Cl_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}{}\cup Cl_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}^{\prime}{}
rules out
P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\varphi)
.

Note that this result can be easily generalized to the case where the
set of atoms can be partitioned into more than two sets. Indeed, if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}}{}
can be further partitioned, we can apply the same
reasoning recursively to it.

This result naturally leads to the enumeration strategy in
Algorithm

5
.
The algorithm first partitions the set of atoms of
\varphi[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}]
as in
1
, obtaining
\{{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{n}}\}
(line
2
).
This step can be performed efficiently, e.g., using a union-find data
structure to compute the connected components of the graph of
atoms, where two atoms are directly connected if they share a variable or an uninterpreted symbol.
Note that, in principle, every
\mathbb{B}
-atom can be considered as belonging to a separate, singleton partition.
In practice, we assume that all the
\mathbb{B}
-atoms belong to the same partition, and can be handled via projection as discussed in §
3.3
.

Then, for each partition
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i}
, the algorithm performs an
AllSMT-based
\mathcal{T}
-lemma enumeration projected on
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{i}

(line
4
), and accumulates the
enumerated
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas in
Cl_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}

(line
4
). Note that, as in

Theorem

3.2
, we conjoin the returned
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas
to the input formula in each enumeration, so as to rule out
\mathcal{T}
-inconsistent assignments that have already been ruled out by previous enumerations.
Finally, the algorithm returns the accumulated set
Cl_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
of
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas (line
7
), which, by
Theorem

3.2
, rules out
P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\varphi)
.

Similar considerations as in
Remark

2
apply in
this case. Indeed, if
n>1
then the improvement could be dramatic, as
we decompose a single total enumeration step into
n
projected enumerations on
subsets
of atoms.

We considered two sets of benchmarks.
The first set consists of all the 450
\text{SMT}(\mathcal{LRA})
synthetic instances from
[
15

.
These problems were generated randomly by nesting Boolean operators up to a fixed depth varying from 4 to 8, involving 10 Boolean variables and 10 real variables.

We also removed the goal constraints to increase the number of solutions and make the problems more challenging for

On temporal planning problems (
Fig.

2
), we can see a similar trend, with some differences. While
D&C
is much faster than the baseline, solving 21 more problems within the timeout, the addition of projection does not yield any improvement. This is likely due to the fact that in planning problems, the assignment on
\mathcal{T}
-atoms is a deterministic consequence of the assignment on
\mathbb{B}
-atoms, so that projecting does not reduce the number of
\mathcal{T}
-satisfiable truth assignments explored by the solver.
On the other hand, here, partitioning is extremely effective, dramatically reducing the enumeration time.
In fact, in these temporal planning problems, we have 5 partitions of
\mathcal{T}
-atoms: atoms encoding time constraints, atoms with constraints on the values of the fluents, and three partitions with constraints on the parameters of three different actions.
By reasoning separately on these partitions, we achieve a speedup of several orders of magnitude, solving problems up to horizon 5 that were previously intractable.

Finally, we analyze the number of
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas enumerated by the different strategies, as shown in the scatter plots in
Figs.

4
and
4
for the synthetic and temporal planning benchmarks, respectively.
As for the time plots, also here we present pairwise comparisons of incremental improvements. We remark that we only show points for problems solved by both the compared strategies, so that in temporal planning problems, we only have a limited number of points.

Overall, we note that there is no large difference in the number of enumerated
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas between the different strategies. We can, however, observe some trends.

First,
D&C
tends to enumerate more
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas than the baseline. This is expected as we said in §
3.2
, since the parallelization of the enumeration of total truth assignments can lead to the discovery of redundant
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas.

Second, both projection and partitioning help in reducing the number of enumerated
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas.
This fact can have multiple explanations.

One is that early pruning to the
\mathcal{T}
-solver
[
1
]
may cause the SMT solver to enumerate
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas that rule out also
\mathcal{T}
-unsatisfiable assignments that do not propositionally satisfy the formula. By narrowing the search space, both projection and partitioning help mitigate this undesired behavior.

Additionally, for the divide-and-conquer strategy without projection, two parallel processes can find two
\mathcal{T}
-inconsistent assignments sharing the same
\mathcal{T}
-literals and different
\mathbb{B}
-literals, inevitably generating two redundant
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas. With projection, instead, the partial assignments fed to different processes differ only on
\mathcal{T}
-literals, thereby mitigating this issue.

Lastly, as discussed in the next paragraph, partitioning can help enumerate smaller
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas, which have stronger pruning power and thus reduce the number of enumerated
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas.

Results on the size of
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas.

As a further insight into the
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas that have been enumerated by the different
strategies, we analyze the median size (i.e., the number of literals)
of the generated
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas. The results are shown in
Figs.

6
and
6
for synthetic and planning problems, respectively.

We observe that different techniques do not consistently improve or worsen the size of
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas, with a notable exception for partitioning, which, on planning problems, allows for finding much smaller
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas.
We conjecture that this may be due to the fact that, with projected
enumeration, the literal selection heuristics of the SMT solver may
prefer selecting projection atoms first.
Hence, early-pruning
calls
[
1
]
to the
\mathcal{T}
-solver are more likely
to cut
\mathcal{T}
-inconsistent branches involving only projection atoms.
This hinders the generation of bigger
\mathcal{T}
-lemmas with atoms from
different partitions, which are
intrinsically redundant by
1
.

Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper, we presented several theory-agnostic techniques for enumerating a set of theory-lemmas that rule out all theory-inconsistent truth assignments for a given SMT formula.
Starting from a baseline method based on total AllSMT enumeration proposed in
[
15
]
, we proposed three orthogonal, composable, techniques to enhance efficiency.

We experimentally evaluated our techniques on SMT problems
from
[
15
]
, and on a new set
of SMT formulas encoding temporal planning
problems
[
20
]
. Our
techniques drastically improve the efficiency of lemma enumeration
with respect to the baseline technique, allowing us to scale to much more complex instances.

This work is planned to play a crucial role in our ongoing research in
knowledge compilation modulo theories
, which we
introduced in
[
15
]
for
decision diagrams and are currently investigating for more general
d-DNNF formulas.
Since in
[
15
]
the
enumeration of theory lemmas was the
major bottleneck in the compilation process, our novel lemma-enumeration techniques pave
the way for scaling to complex, real-world instances.
