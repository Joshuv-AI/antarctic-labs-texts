Noise pollution during pregnancy and congenital issues: systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mahboubeh Hojati, Omid Iravani, Roya Riahi, Fariba Pashazadeh, Elham Koohkan, Pegah Maghzi, Sayed Mohsen Hosseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study provides a systematic review of the literature on the association between noise pollution during pregnancy and congenital malformations, including neural tube defects, orofacial clefts, cardiac defects, and hearing dysfunction.Materials and methodsThe search used the keywords “noise pollution during pregnancy” and “congenital issues.” Two independent investigators extracted studies from Web of Science, Medline (PubMed), Embase, and Scopus through July 2024. After removing duplicates, a systematic review of 11 studies and a meta-analysis of 9 studies were conducted, involving 1,522,346 participants. The review followed PRISMA guidelines and utilized the JBI critical appraisal checklist. Data were extracted in Excel and analyzed using STATA 17.ResultsNoise pollution during pregnancy was significantly associated with congenital anomalies (OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.25–2.27, I² = 69.77%). However, no significant association was established between noise pollution and heart defects (OR = 1.48, 95% CI : 0.97–2.15).ConclusionsThis study concludes that women exposed to noise pollution during pregnancy are at an increased risk of congenital anomalies, emphasizing the need for further research on its effects on pregnancy outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-08857-0.Keywords: Noise Pollution, Pregnancy, Congenital Issues, Systematic Review, Meta-Analysis

Introduction

Noise pollution is a widespread environmental stressor with documented adverse effects on mental and physical health, including hearing impairment, cardiovascular disease, and elevated stress levels. Pregnant women and their fetuses represent a particularly vulnerable population, as occupational and environmental noise exposure during pregnancy may lead to fetal stress and developmental disruptions [1–5].

In high-income countries, working during pregnancy is common, and the fetuses of pregnant mothers are one of the most sensitive populations in society, who are inadvertently exposed to noise pollution [6]. Unlike the general population, pregnant women are exposed to noise during a critical developmental window for the fetus, where even attenuated sounds may influence organogenesis and long-term outcomes. Although the uterine environment attenuates the intensity of noise reaching the fetus, the immature fetal auditory system is highly susceptible to damage, and emerging evidence suggests that stress-mediated pathways have broader effects across multiple systems [7–10].

Congenital anomalies are structural or functional abnormalities that arise during intrauterine life. They are a leading cause of fetal mortality, long-term disabilities, and childhood complications, imposing high individual and societal costs [11]. Over the past 25 years, due to maternal age, environmental factors, and better prenatal diagnosis, the prevalence of abnormalities has increased [12]. Causes include genetic factors, chemical exposures, micronutrient deficiencies, and environmental pollutants such as air pollution and noise. Noise pollution is an emerging and understudied risk factor [13, 14]. Noise can change the physiology of the ear and the development process by affecting the gene expression system [15] It also affects the immune system [16, 17].

Noise exposure acts as a potent stressor that activates the maternal hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to elevated cortisol levels that can cross the placenta and disrupt fetal development [18–24]. This neuroendocrine response induces oxidative stress, free radical formation, and tissue ischemia, which may interfere with critical processes of organogenesis and contribute to structural or functional congenital anomalies [25–29]. Maternal stress from noise also elevates fetal cortisol, potentially affecting long-term developmental outcomes [30–34]. Furthermore, chronic noise can compromise the integrity of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) by releasing stress hormones and inflammatory cytokines that disrupt endothelial tight junctions, increasing permeability to harmful substances and plausibly exacerbating neurodevelopmental anomalies in the fetus [35–40].

While some epidemiological studies suggest an increased risk of congenital malformations with prenatal noise exposure [14, 41–45], others report inconsistent or null findings [46–48], highlighting the need for updated synthesis. Emerging evidence also links prenatal noise and stress to broader developmental consequences, including neurodevelopmental and mental health problems in children [49] and cognitive impairments associated with maternal cortisol timing [50]. Prior systematic reviews and guidelines [14, 30, 42, 51, 52] up to 2022 have examined associations between noise exposure during pregnancy and reproductive outcomes, but they lack a comprehensive meta-analysis incorporating recent studies, heterogeneity, and methodological advancements .This gap underscores the importance of an updated quantitative synthesis to clarify the evidence base and inform public health strategies. This study investigates the correlation between noise pollution and the incidence of congenital anomalies through a systematic review and meta-analysis. Addressing noise exposure in pregnancy aligns with life-course approaches to promote healthy starts for children. By gaining insights into this relationship, we can develop more effective policies and practices to protect the health of both mothers and fetuses.

Materials and methods

This review followed the JBI methodology for etiological evidence [53] and the PRISMA guidelines for reporting [54], combining JBI’s focus on appraisal of observational studies with PRISMA’s transparency standards.

Registration and protocol

The review protocol was not registered on the PROSPERO database.

Inclusion criteria

Participant

The inclusion criteria consisted of pregnant women with no age limit. No exclusions were applied for multiple pregnancies or pre-existing conditions unless reported in the included studies.

Exposure

Pregnant women exposed to noise (objective or subjective, and both occupational and residential) during pregnancy were classified as the exposure group and compared with unexposed women. Noise exposure was categorized as occupational (e.g., > 80 dB) or environmental (e.g., road traffic), assessed via objective (measurements) or subjective (self-report) methods.

Outcome

Children with congenital anomalies (such as neural tube defects, orofacial clefts, heart defects, and hearing dysfunction) born to women who were exposed to noise during pregnancy were considered the outcome variable. Outcomes were ascertained from medical records, registries, or clinical assessments.

Study design

Analytical observational studies, including prospective and retrospective cohort studies and case-control studies, were included to assess the relationship between noise exposure and congenital anomalies.

Search strategy

The comprehensive systematic search was conducted under the guidance of a biomedical search specialist using PICO-based keywords (Medical Subject Headings and free-text terms) to search electronic bibliographic databases, including Web of Science, Medline (PubMed), Embase, and Scopus, for retrieving English relevant studies available until the end of July 2024 to ensure inclusion of the most recent publications (English-language restriction applied to enhance feasibility and reproducibility; no other language limitations were imposed beyond this). In addition, Google Scholar was used to find gray and unpublished studies. Finally, the reference lists of all articles selected for critical appraisal were searched for additional studies. The full search strategy for Web of Science, Medline (PubMed), Embase, and Scopus is provided in Appendix I.

Assessment of risk of bias

Two independent reviewers assessed the potential for systematic bias in the included studies. The JBI critical appraisal tools were used according to their study designs to report observational studies and to prepare a standard table for assessing risk of bias [53]. Studies that qualified more than half of the checklist criteria were considered for inclusion (threshold justified by JBI guidelines for etiological evidence to balance inclusivity and quality). Through discussion, any disagreements regarding the scoring process were resolved.

Data extraction

The review involved two independent reviewers extracting data from the studies. The extracted data included the primary author’s name, study year, research design, sample size, exposure definition and assessment, outcomes (congenital anomaly classifications), crude and adjusted effect size estimates, and adjustments for confounding variables. The primary outcome was any congenital anomaly (general or specific). Specific anomalies (e.g., polydactyly, heart defects, neural tube defects) were extracted separately where reported to enable subgroup analyses. Any inconsistencies identified in the case record forms were addressed by referencing the original articles. If disagreements arose between the reviewers, they were resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer. Additionally, the authors of the papers were contacted to request missing or additional data. Missing data were handled by contacting authors; where unavailable, studies were excluded from quantitative synthesis.

Statistical analysis

We extracted odds ratios and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from studies, focusing on the variable congenital anomaly. Where statistical pooling was not possible, the findings were presented narratively, with tables used to present data where appropriate. A forest plot was employed to investigate the association between noise pollution during pregnancy and congenital anomalies. Depending on the presence or absence of significant heterogeneity, both fixed and random-effects models were utilized.

To assess heterogeneity among the included studies, we employed Cochran’s Q statistic. The inconsistency index (I2) was then calculated to quantify heterogeneity. When statistically significant heterogeneity was observed (substantial heterogeneity defined as I2 > 50%), we used the random-effects model. Subgroup analyses of cardiovascular defects were conducted when sufficient data were available. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the decisions regarding study inclusion and model selection.

We evaluated the possibility of publication bias using funnel plots, specifically employing both Begg’s and Egger’s tests. Statistically significant publication bias was indicated if the p-value from both Begg’s and Egger’s tests was less than 0.05 or if the funnel plot exhibited an asymmetrical shape. Given the small number of studies, these tests have limited statistical power. The analysis was performed using Stata software (version 17).

Results

Upon completing the search, all identified citations were imported into EndNote version 21, and duplicate entries were subsequently removed. Subsequently, two independent expert reviewers screened citations based on titles and abstracts against the predetermined inclusion criteria. Subsequently, the full texts of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and assessed, with exclusion of those that did not meet the stipulated criteria. Exclusion criteria included: review articles, commentaries, editorials, case reports, correspondence, animal studies, cross-sectional studies, articles lacking sufficient information, incomplete full-text versions, and studies not addressing selected noise exposure or outcomes. Articles lacking sufficient information, as well as those with incomplete full-text versions and review papers, were excluded. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion. Ultimately, 11 articles were included for the critical appraisal step (see Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the articles included in this review

After a thorough screening process, we excluded review articles, commentaries, editorials, case reports, correspondence, animal studies, and cross-sectional studies, as shown in Fig. 1. This review included the studies conducted from 1979 to 2023. Eleven studies were included in this study. They included 1,522,346 participants; among all included studies, five were cohort designs [45, 48, 55–57] and six were case-control designs [47, 58–62]. Cohort studies contributed the majority of participants (e.g., large registries with > 1 million births). The characteristics of all included studies are shown in Table 1.

Methodological quality

Eleven studies were critically appraised using the JBI appraisal tools for cohort studies (Supplementary Table S1) and case-control studies (Supplementary Table S2) to assess the risk of bias. All studies met more than half of the checklist criteria and were included (APPENDIX II). Common strengths included valid outcome measurement and adjustment for confounders; weaknesses included potential exposure misclassification.

Meta-analysis results

The association between noise pollution during pregnancy and congenital anomaly was significant (OR = 1.68, 95% CI = 1.25 to 2.27) (Fig. 2). This OR indicates a 68% increased odds of anomalies, though heterogeneity suggests caution in interpretation.

Forest plot of the association between noise exposure during pregnancy and risk of congenital anomalies (random-effects model)

The heterogeneity was significant (I2 = 69.77%; p-value < 0.05). Subgroup analysis by noise type (occupational vs. environmental/residential) was limited by the small number of environmental studies and high heterogeneity.

The analysis revealed evidence of publication bias as indicated by the funnel plot (Fig. 3). Asymmetry in the funnel plot was noted; however, the statistical tests yielded nonsignificant results (Egger’s p = 0.07; Begg’s p = 0.60), which is likely attributable to the limited number of studies included in the review.

Funnel plot for publication bias

Based on the results of leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, the magnitude of the overall effect was considerably changed after excluding Gong et al.‘s study (OR = 1.44, 95%CI: 1.12 to 1.84), and also after excluding Shi et al.‘s study (OR = 1.45, 95%CI: 1.12 to 1.87) (Fig. 4). The overall conclusion remained robust.

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis

The association between noise pollution during pregnancy and congenital heart defects was not significant (OR = 1.48, 95% CI = 0.97 to 2.15) (Fig. 5). This subgroup was prioritized due to sufficient data availability. The heterogeneity was significant (I2 = 59.48%; p-value < 0.05). There was evidence of publication bias based on the funnel plot and Egger’s test (for all studies: p = 0.00), while Begg’s tests revealed no evidence of publication bias (p = 0.29) (small number of studies limits interpretation).

Discussion

Principal findings

The meta-analysis results showed that noise pollution during pregnancy was significantly associated with an increased risk of congenital anomalies (OR = 1.68, 95% CI = 1.25–2.27). However, no significant association was found with heart defects (OR = 1.48, 95% CI = 0.97–2.15). Overall, evidence suggests an increased risk of malformations, such as orofacial clefts and polydactyly, with noise exposure. This is consistent with some studies [45, 60, 63] but contradicts others [46, 47, 59]. The OR of 1.68 indicates a moderate public health impact, implying that pregnant women in noisy environments may have a 68% higher risk of fetal anomalies, warranting targeted screening and noise-reduction policies.

Biological mechanisms

Noise exposure acts as a potent stressor that activates the maternal hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to elevated cortisol levels that can cross the placenta and disrupt fetal development [18–24]. This neuroendocrine response induces oxidative stress, free radical formation, and tissue ischemia, which may interfere with critical processes of organogenesis and contribute to structural or functional congenital anomalies [25–29]. Maternal stress from noise also elevates fetal cortisol, potentially affecting long-term developmental outcomes [30–34]. Furthermore, chronic noise exposure can compromise the integrity of the BBB by inducing stress hormones and inflammatory cytokines that disrupt endothelial tight junctions, increase permeability to harmful substances, and plausibly exacerbate neurodevelopmental anomalies in the fetus [35–40].

Comparison with existing literature

The discrepancy between this meta-analysis and other studies reporting no significant associations with congenital anomalies highlights the variability in study methodologies and populations. Environmental exposure outcomes can vary by geography and population demographics, as documented in numerous epidemiological studies. Discrepancies may arise from variations in exposure assessment (e.g., self-report vs. modeling); differences in outcome classification (specific vs. grouped anomalies); and demographic characteristics (e.g., occupational vs. residential settings).

Alignments with studies such as Pedersen et al. [50] may stem from robust registry data and confounder adjustment, whereas discrepancies with Hjortebjerg et al. [51] could arise from lower exposure levels or incomplete ascertainment of anomalies in smaller cohorts. Focusing on specific anomalies (e.g., polydactyly) offers mechanistic insight but reduces power; grouping them enhances statistical power at the cost of specificity. Some studies specifically examine the association between occupational noise exposure and congenital malformations, contrasting it with the association between environmental noise pollution and congenital malformations. The safety of pregnant women in the workplace may account for this observed trend, which has been reinforced by global occupational safety regulations. Occupational noise often exceeds environmental levels [23], and stricter workplace regulations may mitigate risks, which may explain the lower associations observed in regulated settings compared with uncontrolled residential exposures. Furthermore, as highlighted by Nieuwenhuijsen and colleagues [23], occupational noise pollution is often at higher levels than environmental noise pollution. This disparity may account for the greater emphasis on investigating occupational noise pollution in research studies than on environmental noise pollution.

Meta-analysis of other specific anomaly categories (e.g., neural tube defects or orofacial clefts) was not feasible due to insufficient studies and data. It is important to emphasize that the registry does not account for most cases of congenital malformation due to miscarriage and abortion, which could further reduce the incidence of this rare malformation [6]. This may bias toward null findings by missing miscarriages.

There is a clear need for more longitudinal studies that capture noise exposure throughout pregnancy, with a focus on critical windows. Future longitudinal studies should focus on critical windows (e.g., first-trimester organogenesis) and include objective noise measurements, adjustment for confounders, and multi-exposure assessments. Research exploring the interplay between noise pollution and other environmental factors, such as air quality and maternal health, will be vital to understanding the multifactorial nature of congenital anomalies.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include large sample size (> 1.5 million participants), rigorous JBI bias assessment, and robust sensitivity analyses that confirmed findings.This research is based on existing studies and incorporates new research and data from previously reviewed sources. We conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis and calculated adjusted odds ratios to provide more conservative results reflecting the true impact of noise pollution on pregnancy outcomes. However, there are several significant limitations to consider when interpreting the results. This review was not prospectively registered in databases such as PROSPERO due to time and resource constraints.

Diverse designs, populations, and methodologies introduce substantial variability, impacting result consistency and comparability. Inconsistent measurement methods and insufficient detailed exposure information pose challenges to accurately assessing the impact on fetal development. Noise pollution often coexists with other environmental factors, such as air pollution, and inadequate control for these can introduce bias. Variations in study focus and combining data from occupational and residential environments may raise concerns due to differences in noise effects.

Selection and publication biases skew the overall conclusions, and incomplete capture of congenital malformations in registry data leads to underestimation of true incidence. While publication bias was not statistically significant, its potential presence reduces confidence in the pooled estimates; pre-registering studies and including unpublished data in future reviews could improve reliability. Additionally, potential missed studies due to search strategy limitations and language bias, as well as subjectivity in data extraction and varying quality of included studies, affect the reliability of findings. To reduce publication bias, researchers should register their studies and outcomes in advance, and journals should publish all results, including negative ones. Supporting open access journals and requiring clear reporting of findings can improve reliability. Systematic reviews that incorporate unpublished studies can provide a balanced perspective, while promoting a culture that shares all results can help lessen the stigma associated with negative findings.

Implementation

Given the increased odds ratio for congenital anomalies linked to noise exposure, it is imperative to prioritize screening for women residing in noisy environments and to establish prospective cohorts to validate causal relationships. This entails initiating prospective cohort studies to clarify causality, investigating specific noise sources in relation to particular types of congenital anomalies, and conducting more in-depth exploration of the underlying biological mechanisms. The incorporation of noise-exposure assessments into routine prenatal care, along with educating expectant mothers on strategies to minimize exposure, is essential. Furthermore, it is advisable to establish guidelines for safer work environments for pregnant employees, implement stricter noise regulations in residential areas, and conduct longitudinal studies to monitor noise exposure throughout pregnancy.

Conclusions

The results of this study confirm a higher risk of congenital anomalies in women exposed to noise during pregnancy. Given heterogeneity and limitations, findings should be interpreted cautiously. Therefore, congenital anomaly screening programs could be prioritized for women who work or live in noisy environments. Additionally, further research is needed to address remaining questions and uncertainties.
