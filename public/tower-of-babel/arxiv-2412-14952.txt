The impact of rotation on the stochastic excitation of stellar acoustic modes in solar-like pulsators
L. Bessila, A. Deckx van Ruys ,, V. Buriasco, S. Mathis, L. Bugnet, R.A. García, S. Mathur

Abstract
Context. Recent observational results from asteroseismic studies show that an important fraction of solar-like stars do not present detectable stochastically excited acoustic oscillations. This non-detectability seems to correlate with a high rotation rate in the convective envelope and a high surface magnetic activity. At the same time, the properties of stellar convection are affected by rotation and magnetism. Aims. We investigate the role of rotation in the excitation of acoustic modes in the convective envelope of solar-like stars, to evaluate its impact on the energy injected in the oscillations. Methods. We derived theoretical prescriptions for the excitation of acoustic waves in the convective envelope of rotating solar-like stars. We adopted the rotating mixing-length Theory to model the influence of rotation on convection. We used the MESA stellar evolution code and the GYRE stellar oscillation code to estimate the power injected in the oscillations from our theoretical prescriptions. Results. We demonstrate that the power injected in the acoustic modes is insensitive to rotation if a Gaussian time-correlation function is assumed, while it can decrease by up to 60 % for a Lorentzian time-correlation function, for a 20 ​ Ω ⊙ \displaystyle 20\Omega_{\odot} rotation rate. We show that the modification of the excitation rate by rotation depends not only on the rotation rate but also on the radial and angular orders of the considered oscillation mode. This result can allow for better constraints on the properties of stellar convection by studying observationally acoustic mode excitation. Conclusions. These results demonstrate how important it is to take into account the modification of stellar convection by rotation when evaluating the amplitude of the stellar oscillations it stochastically excites. They open the path for understanding the large variety of observed acoustic-mode amplitudes at the surface of solar-like stars as a function of surface rotation rates.

The power contained in acoustic oscillations results from a balance between the energy injection rates by turbulent Reynolds stresses

In this paper, we aim to understand how p-mode amplitudes are affected by the rotation of the star, through the modification it induces on turbulent convection that excites pressure waves. Indeed, we know from fundamental fluid mechanics studies
(
Chandrasekhar 1961
;
Julien et al. 2012b
, e.g.)
and dedicated studies as a function of stellar types
(
Brun et al. 2017
;
Brun & Browning 2017
;
Brun et al. 2022
, e.g.)
that turbulent convection is strongly modified by rotation. Here, as a first step, we focus on the theoretical study of the impact of rotation on the excitation of acoustic modes and do not study the effect of rotation on the damping of these modes. We do not take into account magnetic effects; magnetic effects on the excitation and damping of the modes in rotating and magnetised stars are out of the scope of this first article and will be investigated in follow-up studies.

Within this context, we used the mixing-length theory   (hereafter MLT) to assess the heat transport by convection
(
Böhm-Vitense 1958
;
Gough 1977
, e.g.)
.
Stevenson 1979
introduced the rotating-mixing length theory   (hereafter R-MLT), which describes the modification of the root-mean-square (r.m.s. velocity) and scale of turbulent convection by rotation for the mode that maximises the heat transport.
Barker et al. 2014
and
Currie et al. 2020
validated the theory and tested it with high-resolution three-dimensional non-linear hydrodynamical simulations of convection in a Cartesian box assuming the Boussinesq approximation.
Augustson & Mathis 2019
generalised this theory by taking into account heat and viscous diffusions and deriving a prescription for the convective overshoot, which has been successfully compared to 3D non-linear spherical numerical simulations
(
Korre & Featherstone 2021
)
. These prescriptions also provide interesting results to understand light-elements mixing in late-type stars
(
Dumont et al. 2021
)
and convective core boundary properties in early-type stars
(
Michielsen et al. 2019
)
. Their model was then used to quantify the efficiency of the stochastic excitation of gravito-inertial modes, which are gravity modes influenced by rotation
(
Augustson et al. 2020
)
. However, the impact of rotating convection on the stochastic excitation of acoustic modes has never been studied before.

In Section
2
, we present the theoretical model of acoustic mode excitation in a rotating convective envelope. We then introduce the R-MLT, presented in Section
3
, to assess how rotation affects the stochastic excitation model. Finally, we implement the obtained theoretical prescription to estimate the energy injected into acoustic oscillations in typical rotating Sun-like stars. We model such stars using the Modules for Experiments in Stellar Astrophysics
(
Paxton et al. 2011
;
Paxton et al. 2013
;
Paxton et al. 2015
;
Paxton et al. 2018
;
Paxton et al. 2019
;
Jermyn et al. 2023
, MESA;)
in order to obtain their structure and evolution, along with GYRE
(
Townsend & Teitler 2013
;
Townsend et al. 2014
)
to constrain the computation of their oscillations in Section
4
. We present the main results of our work, and we conclude on its impact on the understanding of observed acoustic-mode amplitudes in rotating solar-like stars in Section
5
.

2
Turbulent stochastic mode excitation in rotating stars

2.1
The inhomogeneous wave equation

Following the method of
Samadi & Goupil 2001
and
Belkacem et al. 2009
, we derive the inhomogeneous wave equation by taking into account the Coriolis acceleration. We neglect in the present work the action of the centrifugal acceleration and magnetic fields
(see
Bessila & Mathis 2024
, for the impact of the magnetic field)
. We use the usual spherical coordinates
\displaystyle(r,\theta,\varphi)
and the corresponding unit vector basis (
\displaystyle{\bm{e_{r}}},{\bm{e_{\theta}}},{\bm{e_{\varphi}}}
). In the inertial reference frame, the fluid velocity field

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}
is expanded into two components:

{\bm{v}}(r,\theta,\varphi,t)={\bm{u}}(r,\theta,\varphi,t)+r\sin{\theta}\Omega(r,\theta)\bm{e}_{\varphi}.

(1)

Here,
\displaystyle{\bm{u}}
is the velocity field in the rotating frame: it is then associated with both turbulent convective motion and stellar oscillation velocities. We denote
\displaystyle\bm{w}=r\sin{\theta}\Omega(r,\theta)\bm{e}_{\varphi}
the velocity field associated with rotation, where
\displaystyle\Omega(r,\theta)
is the rotational angular frequency. Then, assuming an axisymmetric rotation where the rotation direction is along the
\displaystyle\theta=0
axis, the angular velocity vector is
\displaystyle{\bm{\Omega}}(r,\theta)=\Omega(r,\theta)\cos{\theta}{\bm{e_{r}}}-\Omega(r,\theta)\sin{\theta}{\bm{e_{\theta}}}
.

The mass conservation equation is written as:

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot{(\rho{\bm{u}})}=0.

(2)

The momentum equation, taking into account the Coriolis acceleration, boils down to
(see e.g.
Unno et al. 1989
, for the rotation part)
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\rho{\bm{u}})}{\partial t}+

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}:\left(\rho{\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}\right)+\rho\left[\Omega\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial\varphi}+2{\bm{\Omega}}\times{\bm{u}}+r\sin{\theta}\left({\bm{u}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\right)\Omega{\bm{e_{\varphi}}}\right]

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho\bm{g}-\bm{\nabla}P,

where
\displaystyle\rho
is the density,
\displaystyle P
the pressure, and
\displaystyle\bm{g}
the gravitational field.

To study the source term due to the turbulent background, we break down each physical quantity
\displaystyle f
into an equilibrium component
\displaystyle f_{0}
, and an Eulerian perturbation
\displaystyle f_{1}
. Using the Cowling approximation
\displaystyle\bm{g}_{1}=0

(
Cowling 1941
)
and assuming that the equilibrium quantities verify the hydrostatic equilibrium equation, we obtained the perturbed form of equations (
2

\frac{\partial\rho_{1}}{\partial t}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left[{(\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}){\bm{u}}}\right]=0,

(4)

\frac{\partial[(\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}){\bm{u}}]}{\partial t}+\bm{\nabla}:\left[(\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}){\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}\right]+(\rho_{0}+\rho_{1})\left[\Omega\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial\varphi}+2{\bm{\Omega}}\times{\bm{u}}+r\sin{\theta}\left({\bm{u}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\right)\Omega{\bm{e_{\varphi}}}\right]=\rho_{1}\bm{g}_{0}-\bm{\nabla}P_{1}.

(5)

The perturbed equation of state, using a second-order expansion, is written as:

P_{1}=c_{s}^{2}\rho_{1}+\alpha_{s}s_{1}+\alpha_{\rho_{p}\rho}\rho_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{ss}s_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{\rho s}\rho_{1}s_{1},

(6)

where:

\displaystyle\alpha_{s}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial s}\right)_{\rho},\alpha_{\rho s}=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}P}{\partial\rho\partial s}\right),

\displaystyle\alpha_{ss}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}P}{\partial s^{2}}\right)_{\rho},\alpha_{\rho\rho}=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}P}{\partial\rho^{2}}\right)_{s},

\displaystyle c_{s}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{1}P_{0}/\rho_{0}.

We have introduced
\displaystyle c_{s}
, the sound waves speed, and
\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}=\left(\frac{\partial\ln P}{\partial\ln\rho}\right)_{s}

\displaystyle S
denotes the macroscopic entropy. We assumed that the oscillations follow an adiabatic evolution so that the Lagrangian entropy fluctuation is exclusively due to turbulence:
\displaystyle s_{1}=s_{t}

\displaystyle\delta s_{t}
the Lagrangian turbulent entropy fluctuations and
\displaystyle s_{t}

\frac{d\delta s_{t}}{dt}=\frac{\partial s_{1}}{\partial t}+\Omega\partial_{\varphi}{(s_{0}+s_{1})}+\left(\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\right)(s_{0}+s_{1}).

(7)

Combining the time-derivative of Eq.(

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P_{1}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=c_{s}^{2}\partial_{t}\rho_{1}+\alpha_{s}\frac{d\delta s_{t}}{dt}-\alpha_{s}\bm{u}\cdot\nabla s_{0}-\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{s}s_{1}\bm{u})+s_{1}\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\alpha_{s}

(8)

\displaystyle+\alpha_{\rho s}\rho_{1}\frac{\partial s_{1}}{\partial t}-\alpha_{s}\Omega\frac{\partial s_{0}}{\partial\varphi}-\alpha_{s}\Omega\frac{\partial s_{1}}{\partial\varphi}.

To evaluate the amplitude of stellar oscillation modes excited by turbulence, we considered that the velocity field is the superposition of turbulent convective motions and stellar oscillation velocities (respectively,
\displaystyle{\bm{U_{\rm t}}}
and
\displaystyle{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}

\bm{u}={\bm{U_{\rm t}}}+{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}+r\sin\theta\Omega\bm{e}_{\varphi}.

(9)

As in previous works by
Samadi & Goupil 2001

Belkacem et al. 2009

\frac{\partial\rho_{t}}{\partial t}=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left[(\rho_{0}+\rho_{t}){\bm{U_{\rm t}}}\right],

(10)

\frac{\partial\rho_{\rm osc}}{\partial t}=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left(\rho_{0}\ {\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}\right).

(11)

Differentiating (

\rho_{0}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}-\mathcal{L}\right){\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}+\mathcal{D}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial t}.

(12)

One may break down this equation into several terms.
\displaystyle\partial^{2}{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}/\partial t^{2}-\mathcal{L}{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}
is the operator that governs the linear dynamics of stellar oscillations under the combined action of compressibility, buoyancy, and the Coriolis acceleration. It contains all the terms that are linear in
\displaystyle{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}
is the damping term, which describes the non-adiabatic interactions between stellar oscillations and turbulence. It includes the cross-terms with oscillating quantities
\displaystyle{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}
and turbulent quantities
\displaystyle{\bm{U_{\rm t}}}
. We excluded in the present formulation the radiative damping.
\displaystyle\partial\mathcal{S}/\partial t
is the source term that accounts for the forcing of the modes by the turbulent background. Only the terms that contain combinations of the turbulent quantities
\displaystyle s_{t}
,
\displaystyle\rho_{t}
,
\displaystyle{\bm{U_{\rm t}}}
, are part of it.
There is a strong analogy between this inhomogeneous equation and a mechanical forced mass-spring system:
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}
is homologous of a mechanical damping, while
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
is an excitation source which forces the oscillations.

We found the following expressions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\bm{\nabla}\left[\alpha_{s}{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}s_{0}+c_{s}^{2}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left(\rho_{0}{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}\right)\right]-\bm{g}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left(\rho_{0}{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle-\rho_{0}\bm{\Omega}\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}}{\partial t\partial\varphi}-2\rho_{0}\bm{\Omega}\times\frac{\partial{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}}{\partial t}-\rho_{0}r\sin\theta\frac{\partial{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}}{\partial t}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\bm{\Omega}\bm{e}_{\varphi},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\frac{\partial\left(\rho_{\mathrm{t}}{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}\right)}{\partial t}+2\bm{\nabla}:\left(\rho_{0}{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}{\bm{U_{\rm t}}}\right)+\rho_{\mathrm{t}}\bm{\Omega}\frac{\partial{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}}{\partial\varphi}\right.

(14)

\displaystyle+2\rho_{\mathrm{t}}\bm{\Omega}\times{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}+\rho_{\mathrm{t}}r\sin\theta\left({\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\Omega\right)\bm{e}_{\varphi}

\displaystyle\left.+\bm{\nabla}\left(\alpha_{s}{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}s_{1}+c_{s}^{2}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left(\rho_{\mathrm{t}}{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}\right)\right)\right].

2.2
Source terms

We focus now on the excitation source for the oscillations. One can break down the dominant source term
\displaystyle\partial\mathcal{S}/\partial t
into several components to identify the various origins of the energy injected into the modes:

\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{R}+\mathcal{S}_{S}+\mathcal{S}_{\Omega}+\mathcal{S}_{N},

(15)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{R}
is the Reynolds stresses source terms expressed as:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}_{R}}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Bigg[\nabla:\Big(\rho{\bm{U_{\rm t}}}{\bm{U_{\rm t}}}\Big)\Bigg];

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{S}
is the entropy fluctuations source term,

\mathcal{S}_{S}=-\nabla\left(\alpha_{s}\frac{d\delta s_{t}}{dt}-\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{s}s_{t}{\bm{U_{\rm t}}})+s_{t}{\bm{U_{\rm t}}}\cdot\nabla\alpha_{s}\right);

(17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\Omega}}
is the source term due to rotation, and its gradient,

\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}_{\Omega}}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\rho_{t}\Omega\frac{\partial{\bm{U_{\rm t}}}}{\partial\varphi}+2\rho_{t}\bm{\Omega}\times{\bm{U_{\rm t}}}+\rho_{t}r\sin\theta({\bm{U_{\rm t}}}\cdot\nabla)\Omega\hat{\bm{e}}_{\varphi}\right].

(18)

Finally, one has

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}_{N}}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\left[c_{s}^{2}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left(\rho_{\mathrm{t}}{\bm{U_{\rm t}}}\right)\right]-\bm{g}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left(\rho_{\mathrm{t}}{\bm{U_{\rm t}}}\right)

(19)

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\left(\rho_{\mathrm{t}}{\bm{U_{\rm t}}}\right)+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{t}},

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}
contains the linear terms that do not contribute to the excitation. Previous studies
(
Samadi et al. 2008
, e.g.)
have shown that the contribution of entropy fluctuations accounts for less than
\displaystyle 10\%
of the total power injected in the modes in solar-like stars. We neglected the contribution of the entropy fluctuation source term
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{S}
in this work. Moreover,
Belkacem et al. 2009
showed that
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\Omega}\sim\mathcal{M}\mathcal{S}_{R}
where
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
is the Mach number. The rotational contribution
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\Omega}
is then negligible compared to the Reynolds stresses term
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{R}
in the subsonic regime, which is valid inside the Sun and solar-like pulsators.

Finally, as shown in
Samadi & Goupil 2001
,
\displaystyle\partial\mathcal{S}_{N}/\partial t
does not significantly contribute to the stochastic excitation. Indeed, the terms contained are either negligible compared to the Reynolds stresses term
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{R}
, or linear with respect to turbulent quantities. As detailed in
Samadi & Goupil 2001
in their section 4.1, linear terms in turbulent quantities vanish when integrated over the convective envelope to compute the resulting injected power.
From this point on, we then consider that the excitation source terms are reduced to the Reynolds stresses term
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{R}
alone.

2.3
Mode amplitudes with rotation

Using the inhomogeneous wave equation, we sought the mean square amplitude of
\displaystyle{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}
, which is directly related to the power
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
injected into each mode of a given radial, latitudinal, and azimuthal order
\displaystyle(n,\ell,m)
. Following
Samadi & Goupil 2001
and
Belkacem et al. 2009
, we obtained:

\mathcal{P}=\eta\langle\lvert A\rvert^{2}\rangle I\omega_{0}^{2},

(20)

where
\displaystyle\eta
is the damping rate of the waves and
\displaystyle\omega
is the mode frequency in the inertial reference frame. We introduce the mode inertia
\displaystyle I=\iiint_{\mathcal{V}}\rho_{0}d^{3}r\left(\bm{\xi}^{\star}\cdot\bm{\xi}\right)
, where
\displaystyle\bm{\xi}
is the mode displacement,
\displaystyle\bm{\xi}^{\star}
denotes its complex conjugate and we perform the integral over the whole star volume
\displaystyle\mathcal{V}
. In the case of acoustic modes studied here, we can neglect the inertia correction introduced in the rapid rotation regime by
Neiner et al. 2020
.

The pulsational displacement
\displaystyle\delta\bm{r_{\rm osc}}
and the velocity
\displaystyle{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}
are written using the adiabatic eigendisplacement
\displaystyle\bm{\xi}(\bm{r},t)
, that is with no forcing, and an instantaneous amplitude
\displaystyle A(t)
due to the turbulent forcing
(
Unno et al. 1989
)
:

\delta\bm{r}_{\rm osc}=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(A(t)\bm{\xi}(\bm{r})e^{i\omega_{0}t}+\rm{c.c.}\bigg),

(21)

where
\displaystyle\rm c.c.
indicates the complex conjugate.
In this framework,
\displaystyle\bm{\xi}(\bm{r})
is expanded on the vectorial spherical harmonics basis
(
Unno et al. 1989
, e.g.)
:

\mathbf{\xi}(\mathbf{r})=\left(\xi_{r;\ell,m,n}\mathbf{e}_{r}+\xi_{h;\ell,m,n}\mathbf{\nabla}_{H}+\xi_{T;\ell,m,n}\mathbf{\nabla}_{H}\times\mathbf{e}_{r}\right)Y_{\ell,m}(\theta,\varphi),

(22)

where
\displaystyle\xi_{r},\xi_{H}
, and
\displaystyle\xi_{T}
are the radial, horizontal, and toroidal functions of the displacement eigenfunction, respectively. Then, the oscillation velocity
\displaystyle{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}
is given by

{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}=A(t)\bigg[i\widehat{\omega}_{0}\bm{\xi}(\bm{r})-(\bm{\xi}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{\nabla}\Omega)r\sin\theta\bm{e}_{\varphi}\bigg]\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t},

(23)

where
\displaystyle\widehat{\omega}_{0}=\omega_{0}+m\Omega
, and
\displaystyle\omega_{0}
is the wave frequency in an inertial reference frame. In the latter expression, the temporal variation of the instantaneous amplitude
\displaystyle A(t)
was neglected, as the oscillation periods are much shorter than their lifetime:
\displaystyle d\ln A(t)/dt\ll\omega_{0}

(
Samadi & Goupil 2001
, see e.g.)
.
In the framework of this paper, we restrict ourselves to uniform rotation as a first step, such that
\displaystyle{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}
becomes

{\bm{u}_{\rm osc}}=A(t)i\widehat{\omega}_{0}\bm{\xi}(\bm{r},t))\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{0}t}.

(24)

Moreover, for acoustic waves studied here
\displaystyle\omega_{0}\gg\Omega
. We then assume that
\displaystyle\widehat{\omega}_{0}\approx\omega_{0}
. The mean squared amplitude is then
(
Samadi & Goupil 2001
;
Belkacem et al. 2009
, e.g.)
:

\left\langle|A|^{2}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{8\eta\left(\omega_{0}I\right)^{2}}C_{R},

(25)

where
\displaystyle C_{R}
is the Reynolds-stress contribution:

C_{R}=\int d^{3}x_{0}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d^{3}rd\tau e^{-i\omega_{0}\tau}\Bigg\langle(\mathbf{\xi}\cdot\mathcal{S_{R}})_{1}(\mathbf{\xi}\cdot\mathcal{S_{R}})_{2}\Bigg\rangle.

(26)

Here the operator
\displaystyle\langle(.)(.)\rangle
denotes the statistical average performed on an infinite number of independent realisations. Subscripts 1 and 2 represent the values taken at spatiotemporal positions
\displaystyle(\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{r}/2,-\tau/2)
and
\displaystyle(\bm{x}_{0}+\bm{r}/2,\tau/2)
, respectively.
To compute the contribution
\displaystyle C_{R}
, we follow the formalism from
Samadi & Goupil 2001
which is built on three main assumptions:

1.

It is assumed that the wavelength of the eigenfunctions is large compared to the typical lengthscale of turbulence: this assumption is called the separation of scales. To apply this formalism, one must then compare the oscillation characteristic lengthscale
\displaystyle\ell_{\rm osc}
to the turbulent characteristic length of the eddies
\displaystyle\lambda
. Only the turbulent eddies such that
\displaystyle\lambda\lesssim\ell_{\rm osc}
contribute to the stochastic excitation (see illustration in Figure
2
, and
Samadi & Goupil 2001
for more details). Using the softwares MESA and GYRE, we find that this scale-separation assumption is valid for acoustic modes in solar-like stars. Indeed, the injection scale verifies
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\lesssim\ell_{\rm osc}
.

2.

The turbulent background is considered homogeneous: thermodynamical quantities and the oscillations vary on scale lengths much larger than the contributive turbulent eddies
(
Goldreich & Kumar 1990
;
Samadi & Goupil 2001
, see e.g.)
.

3.

Finally, isotropic turbulence is assumed.
Although rotating turbulence is anisotropic
(
Aurnou et al. 2015
, e.g.)
, this simplification is necessary to combine the stochastic excitation formalism by
Samadi & Goupil 2001
and Rotating MLT, which reproduces well the properties of the convective mode that transports the most heat in rotating anisotropic convective flows
(
Barker et al. 2014
;
Currie et al. 2020
;
Vasil et al. 2021
, e.g.)
. Tackling anisotropic turbulence with rotation is subject to forthcoming developments.

\displaystyle\lambda_{d}

. The size of an arbitrary eddy is denoted
\displaystyle\lambda
. All the eddies in the cascade contribute to the stochastic excitation.

Moreover, as underlined in
(
Belkacem 2008
)
, modes with frequencies between 1 mHz and 5 mHz and low angular degree
\displaystyle\ell
are essentially radial that is
\displaystyle\xi_{h}\ll\xi_{r}
. This is the case for the solar p-modes considered in the present study. We also use the plane-parallel approximation for acoustic modes: it is justified in the present case by the fact that excitation occurs in the uppermost part of the convection zone
\displaystyle(r/R\approx 1)
. Such an approximation is valid when the condition
\displaystyle rk_{\mathrm{\rm osc}}\gg 1
is fulfilled in the excitation region,
\displaystyle k_{\text{osc }}
being the local wavenumber
(
Belkacem et al. 2008
, e.g.)
. In this framework, one can write the Reynolds stresses contribution as in
Samadi & Goupil 2001
:

C_{R}=\frac{16}{15}\pi^{3}\int_{\mathcal{V_{CZ}}}d^{3}x_{0}\rho_{0}^{2}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\xi_{r}}{\mathrm{\penalty\ d}r}\right|^{2}Y_{\ell,m}Y^{*}_{\ell,m}\hat{S}_{R}\left(r,\theta,\omega_{0}\right),

(27)

where we have introduced the spherical harmonics
\displaystyle Y_{\ell,m}
and their complex conjugates
\displaystyle Y_{\ell,m}^{*}
. The integral is performed over the whole convective zone volume
\displaystyle\mathcal{V_{CZ}}
. We also introduced the source function

\hat{S}_{R}\left(r,\theta,\omega_{0}\right)=\int\frac{dk}{k^{2}}E^{2}(k)\int d\omega\chi_{k}\left(\omega+\omega_{0}\right)\chi_{k}(\omega).

(28)

Here,
\displaystyle E(k)
is the spatial kinetic energy spectrum in turbulence, and
\displaystyle\chi_{k}(\omega)
is the associated eddy time-correlation function following
Stein 1967
. With rotation, these kinetic energy and time-correlation spectra depend both on the radius and the colatitude of the studied location inside the convective zone. For this reason, Eq. (
27
) is now a multi-dimension integral where we cannot separate the radial part from the latitudinal one. In the non-rotating case, where the source function only depends on the radius we recover the result by
Samadi & Goupil 2001
.

It must also be noted that even if rotation does not add any significant source term in equation (
12
), it indirectly influences mode excitation:

1.

Waves are affected by the rotation so that they become inertio-acoustic waves. This changes both the frequencies of the modes
\displaystyle\omega_{0}
, which are shifted, and the eigenfunctions of the displacement
\displaystyle\bm{\xi}
. For acoustic modes in main-sequence solar-like stars, this effect is treated as a first-order perturbation
(
Reese et al. 2006
, e.g.)
so we neglect it in the present work.

2.

Convective properties strongly affect the source terms. In the MLT theoretical framework, this happens through a change of the convective wavenumber
\displaystyle k_{0}
and the convective r.m.s. velocity
\displaystyle v_{0}
. For such an approach, rotation tends to weaken convection
(
Stevenson 1979
;
Barker et al. 2014
;
Augustson & Mathis 2019
)
, for the convective mode that transports the most energy. Rotation also affects turbulent convection through a change in the kinetic energy spectrum
(
Zhou 1995
;
Mininni & Pouquet 2010
)
. This effect is paramount in the present model and discussed in Section
3
.

3.

The stellar structure is modified by the centrifugal acceleration. The star’s thermodynamic quantities (density, pressure, entropy) profiles change as well as the stellar oscillation mode resonant cavity and as a consequence the mode eigenfrequencies
(
Espinosa Lara & Rieutord 2013
;
Reese et al. 2021
;
Mombarg et al. 2023
)
. This is the so-called indirect effect on stellar oscillations
(
Gough & Thompson 1990
)
. We neglect this effect in the present work.

illustrates these interdependencies.

2.4
Turbulent spectrum

One has to proceed with the derivation of
\displaystyle\hat{S}_{R}
. We used the descriptions of temporal and spatial properties of the turbulent spectra.

2.4.1
Temporal spectrum

Various choices for the eddy time-correlation function
\displaystyle\chi_{k}(\omega)
exist in the literature
(
Goldreich & Keeley 1977
;
Belkacem et al. 2010
, e.g.)
. Some theoretical formulations assume a Gaussian function
(
Goldreich & Keeley 1977
;
Balmforth 1992
;
Houdek et al. 1999
;
Samadi & Goupil 2001
, see e.g.)
:

\chi_{k}^{G}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\omega_{k}}e^{-\frac{\omega^{2}}{2\omega_{k}^{2}}},

(29)

where
\displaystyle\omega_{k}
is the frequency of the turbulent eddy associated with the wavenumber
\displaystyle k
, defined in Eq. (
34
).
Other works find that a Lorentzian gives a better agreement with solar observations and numerical simulations
(
Samadi et al. 2003
;
Belkacem et al. 2010
;
Philidet & Gizon 2023
, e.g.)
:

\chi_{k}^{L}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\pi\omega_{k}}\frac{1}{1+\left(\frac{\omega}{\omega_{k}}\right)^{2}},

(30)

where we normalise this function to 1:

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\omega\chi_{k}=1.

(31)

We then define the frequency component of the integral (
45
):

\mathcal{I}(\omega_{0},k)\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\chi_{k}(\omega)\chi_{k}(\omega+\omega_{0})d\omega,

(32)

where
\displaystyle\chi_{k}=\chi_{k}^{G}
for a Gaussian time-correlation, and
\displaystyle\chi_{k}=\chi_{k}^{L}
for a Lorentzian time-correlation function. The comparison between those functions is illustrated in Fig.
4
.

) and a Gaussian time-correlation function (Eq.

), both with

. (Centre) Comparison between a Lorentzian eddy-time correlation function and a Gaussian for the integrand

of

and

. As rotation modifies the lower integration bound of
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
for low wavenumbers, it only has a significant influence on the Lorentzian eddy-time correlation. (Right) Influence of the choice for the eddy-time correlation on

. Rotation only affects this term when a Lorentzian is chosen.

2.4.2
Kinetic energy spectrum

The last step to compute
\displaystyle\hat{S}_{R}
following Eq. (
28
) is to proceed with the integral over the wavenumber
\displaystyle k
, taking into account the kinetic energy spectrum:

\hat{S}_{R}\left(r,\theta,\omega_{0}\right)=\int_{k_{c}}^{+\infty}\frac{dk}{k^{2}}E^{2}(k)\mathcal{I}(\omega_{0},k).

(33)

The eddies turnover frequency is
(
Balmforth 1992
)
:

\omega_{k}=ku_{k},

(34)

where
\displaystyle u_{k}
is the characteristic velocity of the eddy with wave number
\displaystyle k
. It is related to the kinetic energy spectrum
\displaystyle E(k)

(
Stein 1967
)
:

u_{k}^{2}=\int_{k}^{2k}E(k)dk.

(35)

We first derive the expression for the kinetic energy spectrum. For
\displaystyle k>k_{c}
, where
\displaystyle k_{c}
is the injection scale (
\displaystyle k_{c}
corresponds to the largest eddy), we use a Kolmogorov energy spectrum
(
Kolmogorov 1941
)
:

E(k)=\gamma\left(\frac{k}{k_{c}}\right)^{\alpha},

(36)

where
\displaystyle\gamma
is a normalisation factor. The turbulent cascade conserves the overall kinetic energy:

\int_{k_{c}}^{+\infty}\gamma E(k)=\frac{\phi}{2}w^{2},

(37)

where
\displaystyle w
is the vertical r.m.s. velocity and
\displaystyle\phi
is the anisotropy factor as defined by
Gough 1977
:

\phi\equiv\frac{\langle u_{c}^{2}\rangle}{\langle w_{c}^{2}\rangle},

(38)

\displaystyle w_{c}
being the vertical convective velocity and
\displaystyle u_{c}
the convective velocity.
In the following, we work within the isotropic framework of the rotating mixing-length theory so that the convective velocity is the same in every direction:
\displaystyle\phi=3
, and
\displaystyle w_{c}=u_{c}
the r.m.s. convective velocity. The normalisation factor
\displaystyle\gamma
is then:

\gamma=\frac{(-\alpha-1)\phi u_{c}^{2}}{2k_{c}}.

(39)

We also introduce the reduced wavenumber:

K\equiv\frac{k}{k_{c}}.

(40)

Eq. (
35
) gives

u_{k}=\sqrt{\beta}K^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}},

(41)

where

\beta=\frac{\phi}{2}u_{c}^{2}(1-2^{1+\alpha}).

(42)

One can deduce the turnover frequency for the eddy of wavenumber
\displaystyle k
, following Eq.(
34
):

\omega_{k}=\delta K^{\frac{3+\alpha}{2}},

(43)

where:

\delta=k_{c}\sqrt{\beta}.

(44)

Finally, we compute the integral over the wave number
\displaystyle k
, assuming a Lorentzian eddy time-correlation spectrum defined by Eq. (
30
):

\hat{S}_{R}\left(r,\theta,\omega_{0}\right)=\int_{k_{c}}^{+\infty}\frac{E(k)^{2}}{k^{2}}\frac{dk}{2\pi\omega_{k}\left[1+\left(\frac{\omega_{0}}{2\omega_{k}}\right)^{2}\right]}.

(45)

Equations (
27
) and (
45
) enable us to compute the contribution of the Reynolds stresses to the stochastic excitation for a given acoustic mode. One can choose to model the turbulent kinetic energy spectrum with a given slope
\displaystyle\alpha
. The choice for this parameter depends on the turbulent regime and is influenced by rotation (see Sect.
3.3
for more details). The important physical quantities are the values of the convective rms velocity and wavenumber, usually computed in 1-D stellar modelling codes using the Mixing-Length Theory.

However, as shown in Figure
3
, rotation affects convection, which in turn influences the stochastic excitation of modes through the Reynolds stresses source term we compute. Rotation indeed influences convection, leading to a lower (resp. higher) value for
\displaystyle v_{c}
(resp.
\displaystyle k_{c}
) in the Rotating Mixing-Length Theory compared to the non-rotating case. To model the impact of rotation on the stochastic excitation of modes, we thus require a robust model and prescription for rotating convection.

3
Rotating convection

3.1
The state of the art

To model rotating convection in stellar or planetary interiors, the most realistic approach is to use direct numerical simulations (DNSs), solving the underlying equations of fluid dynamics. Progress has been made, following the increase of computing power. For instance, the community has computed large sets of Large Eddy Simulations of the 3D non-linear dynamics of stellar rotating convective zones in spherical geometry
(
Brun & Browning 2017
;
Smolarkiewicz & Charbonneau 2013
;
Brun et al. 2022
;
Käpylä et al. 2023
, we refer the reader to)
. To tackle specific phenomena, local DNS is also often used instead of global simulations, neglecting the body’s curvature and using Cartesian boxes, while pushing the Reynolds number to its maximum possible value
(
Julien & Knobloch 1998
;
Julien et al. 2012b
;
Currie et al. 2020
;
Vasil et al. 2021
, e.g.)
. However, these simulations are very demanding and they cannot systematically explore large regions of the parameters space yet.

To study stellar structure and long-term evolution in astrophysics, heat transport by convection is most of the time quantified using MLT
(
Böhm-Vitense 1958
;
Gough 1977
, e.g.)
. This unidimensional theory is broadly implemented in stellar structure and evolution codes, such as MESA
(
Paxton et al. 2011
;
Paxton et al. 2013
;
Paxton et al. 2015
;
Paxton et al. 2018
;
Paxton et al. 2019
;
Jermyn et al. 2023
)
. Despite its simplicity, it has proven to be relevant in modelling stars and planets along their evolution. MLT is based on the maximisation of the heat flux transported by convection, following the principle of
Malkus 1954
. The convective MLT length scale and the convective velocity are governed by the linear mode that maximises the convective heat flux. As such, the MLT is thus a local and monomodal theory.

However, it omits many important effects, including rotation and magnetic fields. To address these simplifications, a theory of the MLT that takes into account rotation and magnetism has been proposed by
Stevenson 1979
and extended in the case of rotation by
Barker et al. 2014
and
Augustson & Mathis 2019
. This R-MLT has not been widely implemented in 1D stellar evolution codes yet, one exception being
Ireland & Browning 2018
(see also
Michielsen et al. 2019
and
Dumont et al. 2021
for the specific study of convective penetration with good agreement with asteroseismology of early-type stars and observations of light-elements abundances in low-mass stars, respectively).
Ireland & Browning 2018
show that the entropy gradient in low-mass stars can be modified by rotation, leading to structural and evolutionary changes, although such changes are found negligible. The same conclusion has been obtained in the case of giant gaseous planets by
Fuentes et al. 2023
and
Fuentes et al. 2024
. The authors suggest that planetary rotation is another factor contributing to the longevity of primordial composition gradients in Jupiter, as observed by the Juno spacecraft. Furthermore, the prescriptions of
Stevenson 1979
have been successfully confronted with nonlinear numerical simulations in Cartesian geometry
(
Barker et al. 2014
;
Currie et al. 2020
)
. In these simulations, the R-MLT formalism established in
Stevenson 1979
holds well for three decades in Rossby number (defined in Eq.
47
), which compares the inertia of convective flows to their Coriolis acceleration. The prescription for the convective overshoot with rotation derived by
Augustson & Mathis 2019
in the R-MLT framework has also been successfully compared with numerical simulations
(
Korre & Featherstone 2021
)
. Finally, the R-MLT theory has been used to model the stochastic excitation of gravito-inertial stellar oscillation modes by turbulent convection in rapidly rotating stars with predictions in agreement with asteroseismic observations of rapidly rotating early-type stars
(
Neiner et al. 2012
;
Neiner et al. 2020
)
. This last study shows a high interest in using this theory to study the impact of rotation on the stochastic excitation of stellar oscillation modes in rotating solar-type pulsators.

3.2
Prescriptions for rotating mixing-length theory

In the present work, we make use of the prescription of
Augustson & Mathis 2019
to account for the modification of convection by rotation, generalising the model by
Stevenson 1979
. This latter provides prescriptions for the asymptotic regimes of slow and rapid rotations while
Augustson & Mathis 2019
prescriptions can be applied for any rotation rate (and Rossby number as defined in Eq.
47
). Both follow the heat flux maximisation principle proposed by
Malkus 1954
: the convective flow is dominated and modelled by the mode that transports the most heat.

Stevenson 1979
gives a modulation factor in each case. The convective velocity (resp. convective wavenumber) with rotation
\displaystyle u_{c}
(resp.
\displaystyle k_{c}
) is expressed with respect to the convective velocity (resp. convective wavenumber) without rotation
\displaystyle u_{0}
(resp.
\displaystyle k_{0}
):

\displaystyle u_{c}=\tilde{U}(\mathcal{R}o)u_{0},

(46)

\displaystyle k_{c}=\tilde{K}(\mathcal{R}o)k_{0},

where we use the effective convective Rossby number introduced by
Stevenson 1979
computed as a function of the non-rotating characteristic velocity and the convective scale:

\mathcal{R}o=\frac{u_{0}k_{0}}{2\Omega\cos{\theta}}.

(47)

Here
\displaystyle\theta
is the colatitude. The convective velocity without rotation
\displaystyle u_{0}
, as well as the convective wavenumber without rotation
\displaystyle k_{0}
are outputs from the MESA stellar structure and evolution code (see Sec.
4
for the detailed method and results).

3.3
Turbulent spectrum in rotating turbulence

Rotation also affects the turbulent kinetic energy spectrum defined in Eq. (
36
), which in turn influences the stochastic excitation of acoustic modes (see Fig.
3
). Numerical simulations and experiments have highlighted a wide panel of behaviours in rotating convection or free turbulence. Theoretical, experimental, and numerical studies provide scalings for the kinetic energy spectrum
(
Julien et al. 2012a
)
.
Zhou 1995
;
Mahalov & Zhou 1996
propose
\displaystyle E(k)\propto k^{-2}
, in a phenomenological study. When the turbulence is forced at a scale
\displaystyle k_{f}
,
Smith & Waleffe 1999
find with numerical simulations
\displaystyle E(k)\propto k^{-3}
for
\displaystyle k<k_{f}
, and
\displaystyle E(k)\propto k^{-5/3}
for
\displaystyle k>k_{f}
.
Mininni & Pouquet 2010
also find a scaling
\displaystyle E(k)\propto k^{-5/3}
, similar to the Kolmogorov spectrum scaling for forced turbulence. Nevertheless, rotating turbulence is strongly anisotropic: for a more accurate description of the turbulent cascade, one must generally distinguish the wavenumber parallel to the rotation axis
\displaystyle k_{\parallel}
, and the orthogonal component
\displaystyle k_{\perp}
. We neglect this anisotropy in the present study as a first step because we are considering the isotropic formalism of R-MLT. Following Eq. (
36
), the turbulent kinetic energy spectrum scales like
\displaystyle E(k)\propto k^{-\alpha}
and we explore the dependency of the stochastic excitation with the slope
\displaystyle\alpha
of this spectrum. More details regarding the turbulent spectra in rotating turbulence can be found for example in
Godeferd & Moisy 2015
;
Alexakis & Biferale 2018
.

We use a Solar-like star 1D model, computed with the stellar evolution code MESA for quantities like sound speed, convective velocity or density profiles. We use in combination the stellar oscillation code GYRE to compute stellar acoustic modes eigenfunctions
\displaystyle\bm{\xi}
and their eigenfrequencies.
For each mode, we use the following methodology:

1.

For a given rotation rate
\displaystyle\Omega
, we compute the effective Rossby number using a profile from MESA. Next, we calculate the convective velocity modified by rotation, following the prescription from

. We refer the reader to Appendix

\displaystyle\theta<\pi/2
.

2.

We compute the oscillations eigenfunctions
\displaystyle\xi_{r}(r)
and eigenfrequencies
\displaystyle\omega_{0}
with GYRE.

3.

At each radial position, we compute the integral
\displaystyle I(r,\omega_{0})
from Eq. (
32
) over all the eddies scales.

4.

Finally, we integrate over the whole star’s convective envelope, using Eq. (
27
).

This process, which is summarised in Fig.
6
, is then repeated for any stellar model, for different modes
\displaystyle(\ell,m,n)
and values for the rotation
\displaystyle\Omega
.

\displaystyle\Omega_{\odot}=28\textsc{ }\rm days

(
Thompson et al. 2003
;

)
.

4.1
Influence of the turbulent eddy-time correlation

As explained in Section 2, one can choose either a Lorentzian or a Gaussian turbulent time-correlation function. First, we examine the impact of either of these choices on mode excitation. Using
Augustson & Mathis 2019
model for rotating convection, the lower integration bound
\displaystyle k_{c}
in
\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{S}}(r,\theta)
increases when the rotation increases. However, only a Lorentzian time-correlation function is significantly modified by rotation, the Gaussian time-correlation function being too steep for low-
\displaystyle k
values. We plotted in Figure
4

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(k)
the integrand of
\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{S}}(r,\theta)

5
Conclusion and perspectives

5.1
Discussion

In this work, we generalise the formalism modelling of the stochastic excitation of stellar acoustic oscillation modes by the bulk turbulence in uniformly rotating convective stellar regions. We build on the work by
Samadi & Goupil 2001
and
Belkacem et al. 2009
, taking the modification of convection by rotation into account. To do so, we use the prescription of the Rotating Mixing-Length Theory
(
Stevenson 1979
;
Augustson & Mathis 2019
)
, which is a monomodal approach considering the mode that transports the most heat.

We applied this theoretical formalism to stellar models, using a combination of MESA and GYRE numerical codes. This semi-analytical approach enables us to obtain the following conclusions.

First, the choice for the eddy-time-correlation function is paramount. As shown in Sec.
3.3
, a Lorentzian eddy-time correlation function significantly impacts the stochastic excitation of acoustic modes in the presence of rotation, while for a Gaussian function, there is no impact on the injected power. Studying the influence of the rotation on the mode amplitudes from an observational point of view can help better constrain the time-correlation spectrum in the convective zone
(
Samadi et al. 2015
, for more details about the previous works, we refer the reader to the review in)
. Noticing modulations in the mode amplitudes as a function of rotation as reported by
Mathur et al. 2019
corroborates a Lorentzian function for
\displaystyle\chi_{k}
according to our model, in agreement with
Samadi et al. 2003
and
Philidet & Gizon 2023
. Indeed,
Mathur et al. 2019
have shown that the mode detection are related to the rotation periods, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. A systematic study of mode amplitudes in stars with surface rotation period
\displaystyle P_{\rm rot}
determination will help confirm it. It constitutes a key constraint on the coupling between turbulent convection and stellar oscillation modes in rotating stars.

Second, rotation significantly diminishes the power injected into acoustic modes by turbulent convection. In a realistic solar-like star, the injected power can decrease by up to
\displaystyle 60\%
for a
\displaystyle 20\Omega_{\odot}
rotation rate, compared to the non-rotating case. This is an indirect effect, due to the impact of rotation on convection that we modelled using the Rotating Mixing-Length Theory
(
Stevenson 1979
)
. This is in agreement with the
Kepler
observations of rapidly rotating stars in the study by
Mathur et al. 2019
, among which fewer detections of acoustic modes are observed in rapidly-rotating stars. It demonstrates how stellar rotation
(
Bessila & Mathis 2024
, and magnetic field, see)
must become key parameters when predicting the observability of stellar oscillation modes
(see e.g.
Goupil et al. 2024
, for the PLATO mission)
.

Using stellar modelling and oscillation numerical codes MESA and GYRE, we also find that the modes are not equally affected by rotation: the injected power is more strongly inhibited in low
\displaystyle n
and low
\displaystyle\ell
modes. For a given
\displaystyle\ell
parameter, low-frequency modes (i.e. low
\displaystyle n
modes) are more inhibited by rotation. However, when looking at the influence of
\displaystyle m
or
\displaystyle\ell
on the excitation, no clear tendency emerges because of the complex behaviour of the source term. (see Figures
8
and
9
).

The choice for the kinetic energy spectrum slope
\displaystyle\alpha
in the theoretical prescription does not influence how rotation diminishes the power injected into the modes compared to the non-rotating case. However, the resulting power differs: the steeper the slope, the higher the power injected into the acoustic modes. Studying more precisely amplitudes of the stochastically excited acoustic modes can thus provide constraints on the turbulent kinetic energy spectrum inside the convective zone.

Finally, stellar structure and fundamental parameters are paramount to assessing the power injected into acoustic modes. For a fixed stellar mass and age, metallicity plays a key role. The lower-metallicity model we computed presents a higher excitation rate, nearly
\displaystyle 10^{2}
times higher than the
\displaystyle 10
times more metal-rich corresponding model. The main parameter when it comes to excitation is the velocity near the photosphere, where the modes are mostly excited by convection. The higher this velocity, the higher the power injected into the modes. This result needs to be further compared with observations. As highlighted in
Yu et al. 2018
, the effect of metallicity is difficult to disentangle from other stellar parameters such as effective temperature, mass, and luminosity. Moreover, metallicity also has an impact on rotation
(
Amard & Matt 2020
)
: at ages superior to 1 Gyr, stars with higher metallicity are expected to spin down more efficiently. This work corroborates that in Solar-like stars, mode amplitudes are very sensitive to the mass and evolutionary stage. Therefore, to accurately assess the robustness of the present model, an extended study comparing observations to theoretical amplitudes computed using 1-D stellar models with different metallicities and rotation rates is required.

5.2
Perspectives

Our study opens the way to a better understanding of how acoustic modes are excited by convection, and how rotation influences the excitation.

However, the mode amplitudes are a balance between driving and damping in the framework of stochastic excitation
(
Samadi et al. 2015
)
. The present work focuses exclusively on the driving of such modes. Modelling the damping rate
(
Houdek & Dupret 2015
, see e.g.)
, as well as the influence of rotation on the damping of the modes is key to fully assessing the impact of rotation on the mode amplitudes. To our knowledge, such a study of this phenomenon has never been done in the literature.

Furthermore, stellar convection zones are observed and predicted to be differentially rotating
(
Thompson et al. 2003
;
García et al. 2007
;
Barnes & Kim 2010
;
Brun & Browning 2017
;
Benomar et al. 2018
;
Brun et al. 2022
, e.g.)
, while we considered in this study a uniform mean rotation. The effects of differential rotation on the acoustic mode excitation rates are currently under investigation (Biscarrat et al., in prep.). One can expect a different behaviour depending on the differential rotation profile (solar or anti-solar), and the differential rotation rate.

The present study also highlights how important the stellar structure is when it comes to mode excitation: we considered five different stellar models and witnessed changes of several orders of magnitude for the power injected into the modes. Such effect should not be ignored: in the near future, these prescriptions should be extended to a wide range of stellar models across the HR diagram. In addition, future works should also tackle PMS stars in order to select some interesting targets. Moreover, there is a strong need for better predictions of mode detection for missions such as TESS and PLATO. For example,
Campante et al. 2016
and
Goupil et al. 2024
studied acoustic mode detection probability, taking into account the properties of the target stars. Such studies could be extended by taking rotation into account.

Moreover, our semi-analytical formalism can be adapted to tackle other types of waves. For gravity waves and gravito-inertial modes, the present work has to be extended to take into account the horizontal and toroidal displacements
(
Samadi et al. 2010
;
Belkacem et al. 2019
;
Neiner et al. 2020
)
. Such waves are stochastically excited at the base of the convective zone, which is where the Rossby number is small and the rotation effects are dominant, as demonstrated in Cartesian models by
Mathis et al. 2014
and
Augustson et al. 2020
. One can then expect rotation to play an even more important role for gravity and gravito-inertial waves than for acoustic modes. One can also apply our formalism to study the impact of rotation on the stochastic excitation of Rossby waves
(
Philidet & Gizon 2023
;
Blume et al. 2024
, e.g.)
. These waves have been recently observed in the Sun
(
Löptien et al. 2018
;
Gizon et al. 2021
)
, and are driven by rotation. They constitute a complementary probe with acoustic modes to sound rotating stellar convection zones.

Finally, as shown observationally by
Mathur et al. 2019
and theoretically by
Bessila & Mathis 2024
, the magnetic field also has an impact on the mode excitation. Rotation strongly influences the magnetic field, which is generated by the dynamo action inside convective zones
(
Brun & Browning 2017
, see e.g.)
and thus they are closely linked. Addressing the magnetic field effect on mode excitation is thus key to fully understanding the excitation of mode amplitudes. It should be examined first on its own and then combined with the effects of rotation.

Appendix A
Rotating mixing-length theory from
Augustson & Mathis 2019

We detail here the Rotating Mixing-Length Theory (R-MLT) formalism from
Augustson & Mathis 2019
, which we use in our stochastic excitation modelling to take into account the modification of the convection properties by rotation.

In this framework, we consider a local Cartesian model, which allows us to characterise the local dynamics of rotating convection around a given radius and latitude in a star or in a planet. In
Augustson & Mathis 2019
the fluid dynamics is studied within a simplified Rayleigh-Bénard set-up. The key point to underline here is that scaling laws obtained within this simplified geometry have been recovered in numerical simulations of rotating stellar convection in global spherical geometry
(
Vasil et al. 2021
;
Korre & Featherstone 2021
)
. Building on this, we also apply these scaling laws to model the stochastic excitation of acoustic oscillation modes in rotating solar-like pulsators.

A.1
Linear Boussinesq equations

Augustson & Mathis 2019
considered an infinite layer of fluid with a local rotation vector
\displaystyle\bm{\Omega}
, inclined with an angle
\displaystyle\theta
relatively to the vertical direction. They also used the Boussinesq approximation, which neglects density fluctuations except for the buoyancy force. This means that we focus here on small-scale motions (and eddies) which have properties linked to the local conditions. The fluid has a small thermal expansion coefficient
\displaystyle\alpha=-(\partial\ln\rho/\partial\ln T)_{P}
, where
\displaystyle\rho
is the density,
\displaystyle T
the temperature and
\displaystyle P
the pressure. The fluid is confined between two infinite impenetrable plates with different temperatures and separated by a distance
\displaystyle\ell_{0}
. In this framework, the Navier-Stokes equation within the Boussinesq approximation with rotation is written as:

\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial t}+({\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla){\bm{u}}+2{\bm{\Omega}}\times{\bm{u}}=\frac{-1}{\rho}\nabla P-\bm{g}\alpha\Theta+\nu\nabla^{2}{\bm{u}},

(53)

where
\displaystyle{\bm{u}}
is the fluid velocity in the rotating frame,
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle\Theta
are the fluctuations of pressure and temperature respectively caused by convection around their hydrostatic background values. We introduce the molecular diffusivity
\displaystyle\nu
and the thermal diffusivity
\displaystyle\kappa
.
The thermal diffusion equation is:

\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial t}-\kappa\nabla^{2}\Theta=\beta u_{z}-{\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla\Theta,

(54)

where
\displaystyle\beta
is the thermal gradient:

\beta=\frac{d\theta}{dz}+\frac{c_{p}}{g},

(55)

with
\displaystyle c_{p}
being the specific heat capacity at constant pressure.
In addition, the velocity field in the Boussinesq approximation is solenoidal:

\nabla\cdot{\bm{v}}=0.

(56)

Linearising and combining the previous equations Eqs. (
53
)-(
56
), one obtains a single equation for the vertical component of the velocity
(see e.g.
Chandrasekhar 1961
, for more details regarding its derivation)
:

\begin{gathered}\left(\partial_{t}-\kappa\nabla^{2}\right)\left(\partial_{t}-\nu\nabla^{2}\right)^{2}\nabla^{2}u_{z}+g\alpha_{T}\beta\nabla_{\perp}^{2}\left(\partial_{t}-\nu\nabla^{2}\right)u_{z}\\
+4\Omega\cdot\nabla\left[\Omega\cdot\nabla\left(\partial_{t}-\kappa\nabla^{2}\right)u_{z}\right]=0.\end{gathered}

(57)

Furthermore,
Augustson & Mathis 2019
assume impenetrable boundary velocity conditions and stress-free boundary temperature conditions, which require that the vertical wavenumber is
\displaystyle k_{z}=n\pi/\ell_{0}
, with
\displaystyle n
an integer. We neglect in our study all diffusive processes that is
\displaystyle\kappa=\nu=0
. Indeed, including such diffusive processes does not qualitatively change the results in R-MLT. The vertical velocity behaves as
\displaystyle\exp({st})
, where
\displaystyle s
is a growth rate for the convective instability
Eq. (
57
) then yields the dispersion relation that links
\displaystyle s
to the wavevector
\displaystyle\bm{k}
:

\hat{s}^{2}+\mathcal{O}^{2}\cos^{2}(\theta)-\frac{(z^{3}-1)}{z^{3}}=0,

(58)

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle N_{*}^{2}=\lvert g\alpha\beta\rvert,

(59)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{s}=\frac{s}{N_{*}},

(60)

\displaystyle\displaystyle z^{3}=1+a^{2}=\frac{k^{2}}{k_{z}^{2}},

(61)

\displaystyle\displaystyle a^{2}=\frac{k_{x}^{2}}{k_{z}^{2}}+\frac{k_{y}^{2}}{k_{z}^{2}}=a_{x}^{2}+a_{y}^{2},

(62)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{O}^{2}=\frac{4\Omega^{2}}{N_{*}^{2}}.

(63)

Moreover, to compare the results from the R-MLT and the standard MLT as implemented in stellar evolution codes, we need to compute the characteristic convective velocity
\displaystyle v_{0}
without rotation.
\displaystyle v_{0}
is derived from the growth rate and maximising wavevector in the non-rotating and non-diffusive case. This leads to
\displaystyle s_{0}^{2}=3/5\left|g_{0}\alpha\beta_{0}\right|
, where
\displaystyle\beta_{0}
and
\displaystyle g_{0}
are the thermal gradient and effective gravity in the non-rotating case, respectively. From Eq. (36) in
Stevenson 1979
, we have
\displaystyle k_{0}^{2}=5/2k_{z}^{2}
and:

v_{0}=\frac{s_{0}}{k_{0}}=\frac{\sqrt{6}}{5}\frac{N_{*,0}}{k_{z}}=\frac{\sqrt{6}}{5\pi}\ell_{0}N_{*,0}.

(64)

We can then define the convective Rossby number
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}o
:

\mathcal{R}o=\frac{v_{0}}{2\Omega_{0}\ell_{0}\cos\theta}=\frac{\sqrt{6}N_{*,0}}{10\pi\Omega_{0}\cos\theta}.

(65)

It implies that:

\mathcal{O}=\frac{2\Omega_{0}}{N_{*}}=\frac{u_{0}}{N_{*}\mathcal{R}o\ell_{0}}=\frac{\sqrt{6}N_{*,0}\cos\theta}{5\pi N_{*}\mathcal{R}o}.

(66)

We then consider the variation of the superadiabaticity, which is given by
\displaystyle\epsilon=H_{P}\beta/T
. With this definition,
\displaystyle N_{*}^{2}=\left|g\alpha T\epsilon/H_{P}\right|
, where
\displaystyle H_{P}
is the pressure scale height. We then introduce
\displaystyle N_{*,0}=\left|g_{0}\alpha\beta_{0}\right|
in the non-rotating case. So far, all quantities have been normalised with respect to
\displaystyle N_{*}
, which includes the impact of rotation on
\displaystyle\beta
and
\displaystyle g
. We introduce the ratio of superadiabaticities as an additional unknown:

q=N_{*,0}/N_{*}.

(67)

Therefore, one has:

\mathcal{O}=q\frac{\sqrt{6}}{5\pi\mathcal{R}o}=q\cos\theta\mathcal{O}_{0},

(68)

where:

\mathcal{O}_{0}=\frac{4\Omega^{2}}{N_{*,0}^{2}}.

(69)

A.2
Heat-flux maximisation

The method defined by
Malkus 1954
relies on the heat-flux maximisation principle: it assumes that the linear mode that maximises the total convective heat flux is dominant. With Mixing-Length Theory, it amounts to selecting this maximising mode to compute the characteristic convective velocity and wavenumber for the flow. In the absence of any diffusive process, the convective heat-flux writes
(
Stevenson 1979
, see e.g.)
:

\mathcal{F}=\frac{\mathcal{F}_{0}}{q^{3}}\frac{\hat{s}^{3}}{z^{3}},

(70)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{0}=\langle\rho\rangle c_{P}N_{*,0}^{3}/\left(g\alpha k_{z}^{2}\right)
is the heat-flux in the absence of rotation. When maximised for a positive real value of
\displaystyle\hat{s}
, Eq. (
70
) implies that

\frac{d\hat{s}}{dz}=\frac{\hat{s}}{z}.

(71)

The derivative of the dispersion relationship yields another expression for
\displaystyle d\hat{s}/dz
:

\frac{d\hat{s}}{dz}=\frac{-3z^{2}\hat{s}(\hat{s}^{2}-1)}{1+\mathcal{O}_{o}^{2}q^{2}+z^{3}(3\hat{s}^{2}-1)}.

(72)

Moreover,
Augustson & Mathis 2019
used a heat flux invariance approximation. Indeed, the total heat flux is assumed to remain identical with and without rotation (see section 2.3 from
Augustson & Mathis 2019
for more details). This condition yields:

\frac{\max[\mathcal{F}]}{\max[\mathcal{F}]_{0}}=\frac{25}{6}\sqrt{\frac{5}{3}}\frac{\hat{s}^{3}}{q^{3}z^{3}}=1,

(73)

which implies:

\hat{s}=\tilde{s}qz,

(74)

where
\displaystyle\tilde{s}=2^{1/3}3^{1/2}5^{-5/6}
.
One then has the convective velocity and wavenumber modulation, depending only on the maximising value for
\displaystyle z
:

\frac{k}{k_{0}}=\frac{k_{z}z^{3/2}}{k_{0}}=\sqrt{2/5}z^{3/2},

(75)

\frac{u}{u_{0}}=\frac{k_{0}}{s_{0}}\frac{s}{k}=\left(\frac{5}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{6}}z^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(76)

Finally, only the maximising wavenumber
\displaystyle z
is needed. Equating the right-hand sides of Eqs. (
71
) and (
72
) then applying (
74
), we find:

2z^{5}-5z^{2}-\frac{18}{25\pi^{2}\mathcal{R}o^{2}\tilde{s}^{2}}=0,

(77)

which is Eq.(46) from
Augustson & Mathis 2019
. Together with Eqs. (
75
) and (
76
), one can recover the convective velocity (resp. convective wavenumber) modified by rotation in the framework of R-MLT.
