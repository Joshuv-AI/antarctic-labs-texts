Multicentre evidence for an independent association between third-trimester hypoaldosteronism and preeclampsia
Authors: Mei Yang, Menghui Wang, Qing Zhu, Nanfang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study aimed to investigate the association of plasma aldosterone concentration (PAC) on admission before delivery with preeclampsia, admission blood pressure, and pregnancy outcomes. In this multicenter observational study (May 2023-December 2024), 886 pregnancies (125 preeclampsia, 761 controls) from six hospitals were analyzed. Associations were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression, Spearman’s correlation, and restricted cubic spline models, with sensitivity analysis employing Winsorization. Piecewise regression was employed to identify breakpoint and examine threshold effect. Preeclamptic women had significantly lower PAC than controls (16.73 [11.03, 32.59] vs. 28.16 [15.99, 52.82] ng/dL, P < 0.001). Lower PAC was independently associated with preeclampsia (adjusted OR: 0.98; 95% CI 0.98–0.99; P < 0.001) and correlated with higher systolic blood pressure (ρ = −0.170, P < 0.001) and adverse outcomes (P < 0.05). A significant nonlinear relationship was observed (P < 0.001), with a breakpoint at 11.74 ng/dL where each 1-unit increase reduced preeclampsia odds by 30% below it (adjusted OR 0.70; 0.57–0.87) and only by 2% at or above it (adjusted OR 0.98; 0.97–0.99). Introduction

Preeclampsia, a severe pregnancy-specific hypertensive disorder, is a leading cause of global maternal and neonatal mortality and morbidity1, accounting for a prevalence of 2–8%2 and approximately 76,000 maternal and 500,000 fetal and neonatal deaths annually3. The condition is multifactorial4, involving placental dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and angiogenic imbalance5. As its precise etiology remains unclear and no curative treatment exists, identifying key modifiable factors is crucial for risk stratification and novel therapeutic strategies.

Aldosterone, a key mineralocorticoid hormone, is central to blood pressure and electrolyte homeostasis6,7. Outside of pregnancy, elevated aldosterone is a well-established driver of cardiovascular pathology, promoting hypertension8, fibrosis, and cardiac remodelling or hypertrophy9. It can also cause cardiac arrhythmias10 and glucose intolerance11. Elevated plasma aldosterone concentration (PAC) is an independent risk factor in cardiovascular disease12. Paradoxically, emerging evidence suggests that in the context of pregnancy, physiological elevation of aldosterone may be essential for successful adaptation, supporting plasma volume expansion and possibly placental development13,14. This dichotomous role positions aldosterone as a molecule of particular interest in pregnancy-specific disorders like preeclampsia.

Previous studies have found that PAC is lower in women with preeclampsia compared to those with normotensive pregnancies15,16. However, their association remains controversial. To clarify this, our multicenter observational study employed multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic spline models, and piecewise regression to explore the association between PAC and preeclampsia. We further analyzed the associations of PAC with maternal blood pressure and adverse pregnancy outcomes, to comprehensively evaluate the potential role of aldosterone in the pathophysiology of preeclampsia.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This multicenter observational study was conducted across six medical institutions in Xinjiang, China, between May 1, 2023, and December 31, 2024. The participating centers were: the People’s Hospital of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, the People’s Hospital of Tacheng Region, the People’s Hospital of Ermin County, the People’s Hospital of Wusu City, the People’s Hospital of Tuoli County, and the People’s Hospital of the Ninth Division. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the People’s Hospital of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region (approval number: KY2023042001; date: April 20, 2023).

Study population

A total of 1,100 pregnant women provided informed consent and contributed discarded clinical blood samples. To minimize the influence of potential confounders on PAC and clinical outcomes, strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. Inclusion criteria were: (1) gestational age > 20 weeks; (2) singleton pregnancy; (3) natural conception. Exclusion criteria included: (1) incomplete clinical records; (2) use of diuretics or dexamethasone within the preceding 2 weeks; (3) a history of chronic hypertension, autoimmune disorders, diabetes, hyperthyroidism, Cushing’s syndrome, glomerular or tubulointerstitial disease, renovascular disease, confirmed adrenal hyperplasia or mass, confirmed primary aldosteronism, or hematological disorders (except iron-deficiency anemia); (4) pregnancy-related complications, including gestational hypertension, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, chorioamnionitis, or fetal structural/chromosomal abnormalities; (5) regular uterine contractions at the time of blood sampling; (6) alanine aminotransferase (ALT) or aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels > 3 times the upper limit of normal; (7) serum creatinine level above the upper limit of normal.

Data collection

Data on demographics, medical history, clinical characteristics, and laboratory results on admission were extracted systematically from a dedicated cloud-based platform for maternal and child health data and electronic medical records.

Collected variables included: maternal age, body mass index (BMI), gravidity, parity, admission blood pressure, ALT, AST, blood glucose, serum creatinine, gestational age on admission and delivery, as well as neonatal birth weight and the 1-minute Apgar score (Apgar-1). We also assessed maternal outcomes, including gestational diabetes (GDM), preeclampsia, hypothyroidism, placental abruption, and postpartum hemorrhage; and fetal/neonatal outcomes, including stillbirth, fetal distress, neonatal asphyxia, preterm birth, and fetal growth restriction (FGR).

Definition and diagnostic criteria

Preeclampsia was defined as the presence of new-onset hypertension and proteinuria or other end-organ damage with or without proteinuria occurring after 20 weeks’ gestation17. Its diagnostic criteria were based on the guidelines recommended by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists17. In this study, adverse fetal outcomes comprised stillbirth, fetal distress, neonatal asphyxia, preterm birth, and FGR. Adverse maternal outcomes included GDM, preeclampsia, placental abruption, and postpartum hemorrhage. The diagnosis and classification of all outcomes were based on established international clinical guidelines18–22.

Blood sample collection and hormone assay on admission before labor onset

Venous blood was drawn after admission for delivery and before labor onset. Specifically, blood samples were collected from the antecubital fossa into EDTA tubes after the participants had rested in a seated position for 30 min. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 15 min at room temperature and stored at -80 °C until all samples were collected. All specimens were transported on dry ice and analyzed in a single batch by trained personnel in the hypertension research laboratory. Plasma aldosterone and renin concentration were measured quantitatively using chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay (CMIA) technology with commercially available CLIA kits and an automated analyzer (Autobio Diagnostics Ltd., Zhengzhou, China), strictly adhering to the manufacturer’s standardized protocols.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0) and R software (version 4.2.2). Continuous variables of the study were expressed as median (interquartile range) because they did not follow a normal distribution, while categorical variables were presented as number (percentage). Group comparisons were made using the independent samples t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. The association between PAC and preeclampsia was assessed by logistic regression. Given the multicenter design of the study, PAC values were normalized using the Z-score method. Results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Associations between PAC and clinical parameters were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation. Potential nonlinearity of the PAC–preeclampsia relationship was examined with restricted cubic splines (RCS). If nonlinearity was confirmed, piecewise regression was further employed to identify the breakpoint and examine the threshold effect of PAC. The primary R packages utilized in this study were ‘rms’ and ‘segmented’. For the few missing values (< 2% of all data), mean imputation was applied to continuous variables. All tests were two-sided, with a P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 886 eligible women were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). These women were aged 18–41 years, with BMI ranging from 20.00 to 46.07 kg/m². All women were in the third trimester of pregnancy at admission, and 771 (86%) were at term gestation. Of these, 125 women were diagnosed with preeclampsia and 761 women were without hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (Non-HDP). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of BMI, gravidity, parity, ALT, and blood glucose (all P > 0.050). However, women with preeclampsia had higher systolic blood pressure (SBP) (136 [120, 145] vs. 120 [110, 120] mmHg, P < 0.001), diastolic blood pressure (DBP) (85 [70, 96] vs. 70 [70, 74] mmHg, P < 0.001), AST (18.00 [15.00, 23.00] vs. 17.00 [14.00, 20.00] U/L, P < 0.001) and serum creatinine (51.00 [43.00, 63.65] vs. 47.70 [43.00, 53.40] umol/L, P < 0.001) compared to women without HDP. PAC was significantly lower in women with preeclampsia (16.73 [11.03, 32.59] vs. 28.16 [15.99, 52.82] ng/dL, P < 0.001). A significant inverse trend in the prevalence of preeclampsia was observed across increasing PAC quartiles (Q1 [< 15.03 ng/dL]: 41.6% [52/125]; Q2 [15.03–25.94 ng/dL]: 26.4% [33/125]; Q3 [25.94–48.69 ng/dL]: 18.4% [23/125]; Q4 [≥ 48.69 ng/dL]: 13.6% [17/125]; P < 0.001). Similarly, plasma renin concentration was significantly lower in women with preeclampsia compared to women without HDP (31.06 [11.46, 66.17] vs. 53.07 [29.38, 82.13] pg/ml, P < 0.001). And, the prevalence of preeclampsia was higher in the low-renin group than in the high-renin group (65.6% vs. 34.4%, P < 0.001) (Table 1). Additionally, GDM (21 [16.8%]vs. 71 [9.3%], P = 0.011), placental abruption (6 [4.8%] vs. 3 [0.4%], P < 0.001), FGR (13 [10.4%] vs. 2 [0.3%], P < 0.001), preterm birth (42 [34.4%] vs. 35 [4.6%], P < 0.001), and neonatal asphyxia (11 [9.0%] vs. 6 [0.8%], P < 0.001) were more prevalent in the preeclampsia group (see Supplementary Table S1 online). There were 3 stillbirths, and 2 pregnancies were terminated due to persistent loss of umbilical artery flow.

Association between PAC and preeclampsia

As a continuous variable, each 1 ng/dL increase in PAC was associated with a 2% reduction in the odds of preeclampsia (OR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.98–0.99, P < 0.001), and this negative association was consistent across all multivariable-adjusted models (all P < 0.001). When analyzed as a standardized variable, each standard deviation increase in PAC was associated with a 50% reduction in the odds of preeclampsia (OR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.36–0.70, P < 0.001) in the unadjusted model; after full adjustment for age, BMI, gravidity, parity, serum creatinine, renin, gestational age, and GDM, the corresponding odds ratio was 0.40 (95% CI 0.27–0.59, P < 0.001) (Table 2).

When PAC was categorized into quartiles, a graded negative association with preeclampsia was observed. In the unadjusted model (model 1), compared with the lowest quartile (Q1, reference), the odds of preeclampsia were lower in Q2 (OR = 0.56, 95% CI 0.35–0.91, P = 0.019), Q3 (OR = 0.37, 95% CI 0.22–0.64, P < 0.001), and Q4 (OR = 0.27, 95% CI 0.15–0.48, P < 0.001). After sequential adjustment for covariates in models 2–4, these negative associations remained significant in the highest quartiles. In the fully adjusted model (Model 4), the odds ratios for Q3 and Q4 were 0.34 (95% CI 0.19–0.63, P < 0.001) and 0.18 (95% CI 0.09–0.37, P < 0.001), respectively. A significant negative dose-response relationship was observed across all models (P for trend < 0.001), and the associations remained robust to multivariable adjustment (Table 2).

Stratified analysis by BMI

To explore potential effect modification by BMI, the study population was stratified into two groups: BMI < 28.00 kg/m² group (n = 335) and BMI ≥ 28.00 kg/m² group (n = 551). After adjusting for age, gravidity, parity, serum creatinine, renin, gestational age, and GDM, the association between aldosterone and preeclampsia remained significant in both subgroups (P = 0.001 and P = 0.002, respectively). In the BMI < 28 kg/m² group, each 1 ng/dL increase in PAC was associated with 3% lower odds of preeclampsia (adjusted OR = 0.97, 95% CI 0.96–0.99, P = 0.001). A similar magnitude of association was observed in the BMI ≥ 28 kg/m² group, with a 2% reduction in the odds of preeclampsia per 1 ng/dL increase in PAC (adjusted OR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.97–0.99, P = 0.002). The interaction between aldosterone and BMI category was not statistically significant (P for interaction = 0.428), indicating that the negative association between aldosterone and preeclampsia was consistent across BMI strata, although the point estimate was slightly stronger in the lower BMI group (see Supplementary Fig. S1 online).

Association of PAC with clinical outcomes

Correlation analysis revealed a significant negative association between PAC and admission SBP (ρ = -0.170, P < 0.001). Similarly, PAC was negatively correlated with adverse maternal outcomes (ρ = -0.146, P < 0.001). Regarding fetal outcomes, a significant but weak negative association was observed between PAC and adverse fetal outcomes (ρ = -0.080, P = 0.017). For neonatal outcomes, PAC showed a weak positive association with the Apgar-1 scores (ρ = 0.082, P = 0.014). No significant associations were observed between PAC and admission DBP (ρ = -0.007, P = 0.843), neonatal birth weight (ρ = 0.031, P = 0.366) and gestational age at delivery (ρ = 0.035, P = 0.295) (see Supplementary Table S2 online).

Sensitivity analyses

Data characteristics and outlier handling

To mitigate the influence of extreme values, PAC was winsorized at the 5th and 95th percentiles (pre-winsorized range: 6.34-312.09 ng/dL; post-winsorized range: 8.49-127.93 ng/dL). Women with preeclampsia exhibited significantly lower median PAC compared to women without HDP (16.73 ng/dL vs. 28.16 ng/dL; P < 0.001). Additionally, a significant inverse trend in preeclampsia prevalence was observed across increasing aldosterone quartiles (< 15.03 ng/dL: 41.6%, 52/125; 15.03–25.94 ng/dL: 26.4%, 33/125; 25.94–48.69 ng/dL: 18.4%, 23/125; ≥ 48.69 ng/dL: 13.6%, 17/125, P < 0.001).

Association between Winsorized PAC and preeclampsia

Following Winsorization of PAC at the 5th and 95th percentiles, a consistent negative association with preeclampsia was observed across all analytical models. In the unadjusted model (model 1), each 1 ng/dL increase in PAC was associated with a 2% reduction in the odds of preeclampsia (OR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.97–0.99; P < 0.001). This association remained stable in models 2, 3 and 4 after sequential adjustment for different sets of covariates (all ORs = 0.98, all P < 0.001). A similarly consistent negative association was observed for standardized PAC (all P < 0.001). When winsorized PAC was analyzed in quartiles, a significant negative dose-response relationship was observed (P for trend < 0.001), as evidenced by progressively lower odds ratios across the ascending quartiles groups (see Supplementary Table S3 online).

Association of Winsorized PAC with Clinical Outcomes

Correlation analysis showed a significant negative association between winsorized PAC and admission SBP (ρ = -0.169, P < 0.001), maternal adverse outcomes (ρ = -0.145, P < 0.001) and fetal adverse outcomes (ρ = -0.080, P = 0.018). There was also a weak but statistically significant positive association between PAC and neonatal Apgar-1 scores (ρ = 0.082, P = 0.014). These findings are consistent with the primary results presented earlier (see Supplementary Table S4 online).

Nonlinear relationship between aldosterone and preeclampsia

To explore the potential relationship between PAC and preeclampsia, RCS with 3 knots selected by minimizing the Akaike Information Criterion was utilized. The knots were positioned at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the aldosterone distribution. To account for potential confounding effects, the model was adjusted for age, BMI, gravidity, parity, serum creatinine, renin, gestational age, and GDM. RCS analysis showed that the overall association between PAC and preeclampsia was statistically significant (P overall < 0.001) and a non-linear relationship was suggested (P non-linear = 0.043) (Fig. 2).

Threshold effect of PAC on preeclampsia

A piecewise regression model automatically identified the breakpoint for the association between PAC and preeclampsia at 11.74 ng/dL. The difference in the strength of association on either side of this threshold was statistically significant (P for the log-likelihood ratio test = 0.003). After adjusting for age, BMI, gravidity, parity, serum creatinine, renin, gestational age on admission, and GDM, the results showed that when PAC was below 11.74 ng/dL, each 1‑unit increase in PAC was associated with a 30% reduction in the odds of preeclampsia (adjusted OR: 0.70; 95% CI 0.57–0.87; P = 0.001). When PAC was at or above 11.74 ng/dL, each 1‑unit increase in PAC was associated with only a 2% reduction in the odds of preeclampsia (adjusted OR: 0.98; 95% CI 0.97–0.99; P < 0.001). The odds of preeclampsia exhibited a decreasing trend when PAC was below the breakpoint and leveled off after the breakpoint (see Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. S2).

Discussion

Aldosterone is the major mineralocorticoid hormone that regulates body electrolyte and fluid homeostasis23 and is crucial for sustaining normal pregnancy. Clinical studies indicate that women with preeclampsia exhibit significantly lower PAC compared to those with normal pregnancies15,16, suggesting a potential link between reduced PAC and preeclampsia. However, the nature of this association remains controversial. For instance, a longitudinal cohort study by Birukov et al.24 involving 569 pregnant women at 29 weeks’ gestation found no significant correlation between PAC and preeclampsia. Given that aldosterone synthase, encoded by Cytochrome P450 Family 11 Subfamily B Member 2 (CYP11B2), is the key enzyme for aldosterone synthesis, Todkar et al.25 examined the role of aldosterone in preeclampsia using synthase-deficient mouse models and found that its absence did not induce the condition. Conversely, other studies present divergent findings. Bogacz et al.26 analyzed the CYP11B2 gene in peripheral blood of 165 pregnant women (59 with preeclampsia and 109 healthy controls) and identified that the − 344 C > T polymorphism may be associated with the development of preeclampsia, potentially by affecting aldosterone synthesis. Furthermore, Hiten D. Mistry et al.27 demonstrated through placental perfusion experiments that elevated PAC promote the production of anti-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic factors, suggesting a beneficial role in pregnancy health. Despite these findings, definitive clinical evidence establishing a link between PAC and preeclampsia has been lacking. Our clinical research contributes to clarifying this relationship by demonstrating a significant non-linear association between lower PAC and preeclampsia. Notably, a breakpoint was identified at 11.74 ng/dL, offering further evidence of a complex dose-response pattern. Below it, PAC showed a stronger inverse association with preeclampsia, whereas at or above it, the association weakened markedly. These findings suggest that a concentration-specific breakpoint may exist in the PAC–preeclampsia relationship, providing a novel perspective on aldosterone’s potential role in its pathophysiology. Besides, the identified breakpoint could also offer a preliminary biological reference for future studies to evaluate the clinical relevance of PAC monitoring. We also further observed a significant negative association between PAC and adverse maternal-fetal outcomes, indicating that aldosterone may play a beneficial role in relation to these complications. We revealed a modest yet statistically significant negative association between PAC and SBP, supporting its potential involvement in moderating hypertensive conditions. Additionally, we found that higher PAC showed a positive association with indicators of fetal well-being, as evidenced by a weak positive association with Apgar-1 scores. These associations remained stable in sensitivity analyses, providing additional support for the observed relationships.

Aldosterone, a key mineralocorticoid hormone, plays divergent roles depending on the pathophysiological context. In hypertensive disorders, its excess is pathogenic, promoting sodium retention, potassium excretion, increased blood volume, and vasoconstriction, which collectively elevate blood pressure and cardiovascular stress. Furthermore, aldosterone induces vascular inflammation, fibrosis, and endothelial dysfunction, aggravating hypertension and its complications. In contrast, aldosterone plays an active role during normal pregnancy. Maternal adaptation to pregnancy involves systemic vasodilation and a substantial increase in blood volume, facilitated by activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS)28. Compared to non-pregnant women, pregnant women exhibit elevated levels of circulating RAAS components29, and aldosterone, essential for blood volume expansion, also shows increased expression during pregnancy30. However, PAC is notably lower in women with preeclampsia than in those with normotensive pregnancies15,16. This suggests that relative aldosterone deficiency may be associated with the disease pathogenesis. Our findings align with this view, indicating that insufficient RAAS activation and reduced PAC may be associated with hypovolemia and diminished placental perfusion, which could potentially correlate with the development of preeclampsia. Moreover, aldosterone exerts important extra-renal effects that may support placental and fetal development by promoting trophoblast proliferation and placental growth31,32. Experimental evidence from mice models suggests that genetic ablation of the CYP11B2 results in diminished litter size, reduced fetal weight, and an increased incidence of placental necrosis, thereby compromising placental development25. Human placental perfusion experiments further demonstrate that aldosterone upregulates anti-inflammatory and proangiogenic factors critical for healthy pregnancy27. Additional evidence indicates that aldosterone may induce the expression of placental growth factor, thereby supporting placental function. Together, these observations underscore the beneficial role of aldosterone in normal pregnancy, though the precise mechanisms warrant further investigation33.

This study demonstrates substantial clinical significance: First, PAC levels could serve as a potential biomarker for assessing preeclampsia risk. Targeted monitoring of pregnant women with lower PAC may provide a reference for formulating preventive strategies and clinical interventions. Second, the further elucidation of aldosterone’s role in preeclampsia offers new insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms of the disorder and suggests its potential as a therapeutic target. Third, the observed inverse association suggests that monitoring PAC could provide a basis for predicting adverse maternal-fetal outcomes, thereby facilitating early management of related complications. This study also has several limitations. First, although statistical adjustments were made for known confounders, the influence of residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Second, measuring PAC at a single time point may not adequately capture its dynamic physiological changes throughout pregnancy. Future research should adopt multicenter, large-scale prospective designs to better delineate the temporal relationship between PAC and the onset of preeclampsia, complemented by mechanistic investigations to clarify the underlying pathological processes. Furthermore, serial measurements of PAC across different gestational stages would help to more comprehensively characterize its trajectory during pregnancy, thereby enhancing the reliability and validity of the conclusions.

Conclusion

Lower PAC shows an independent, nonlinear association with preeclampsia, characterized by a threshold effect around 11.74 ng/dL, and is linked to elevated systolic blood pressure and adverse maternal-fetal outcomes, suggesting that maintaining adequate PAC may be beneficial for pregnancy health.
