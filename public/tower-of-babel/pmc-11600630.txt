Global, regional, and national burden of gout in elderly 1990–2021: an analysis for the global burden of disease study 2021
Authors: Yilan Li, Zhaopeng Chen, Baijie Xu, Gengmao Wu, Qiongyu Yuan, Xiaoqian Xue, Yue Wu, Yudan Huang, Shouqi Mo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGout, an inflammatory arthritis, disproportionately affects the elderly due to hyperuricemia, leading to significant health-related quality of life impairments and escalating healthcare costs. However, a comprehensive global analysis focusing on the elderly population is needed to inform effective interventions.MethodsUtilizing data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, this study assessed the prevalence, incidence, and Years Lived with Disability (YLDs) of gout among individuals aged ≥ 55 years in 204 countries from 1990 to 2021. We also evaluated the impact of high body mass index (BMI) and kidney dysfunction as key risk factors.ResultsThe study identified 37,230,366 cases of gout globally among the elderly, with an age-standardized prevalence rate of 2505.4 per 100,000 population. There was a notable increase in prevalence with an Estimated Annual Percentage Change (EAPC) of 1.08. Similarly, the age-standardized incidence and YLD rates increased, with EAPCs of 0.83 and 1.06, respectively. High-income regions, particularly Australasia and High-income North America, exhibited the highest rates, while Central Latin America and the Caribbean reported the lowest. Males had a higher burden of gout than females. High BMI and kidney dysfunction were significant contributors to YLDs, with their impact more pronounced in regions with higher Socio-Demographic Index (SDI).ConclusionThe study found a growing gout burden among the elderly, with substantial regional and gender disparities. It underscores the urgent need for targeted public health interventions, particularly in high SDI regions, to address modifiable risk factors like high BMI and kidney dysfunction and to curb the rising trend of gout prevalence and disability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20799-w.Keywords: Gout, Global burden of disease, Elderly, Body mass index, Kidney dysfunction

Introduction

Gout is the most common inflammatory arthritis [1], with a notably impact on the elderly population [2]. Gout arises from the deposition of monosodium urate crystals within the joints of individuals suffering from hyperuricemia [3]. This pathological process leads to impaired physical function [4], diminished health-related quality of life [5], escalated healthcare expenditures attributed to gout [6], and an augmented risk of comorbidities and mortality [7]. The burden of gout varies across different countries and ethnicities [8]. In 2020, high-income North America and Australasia reported the highest age-standardized prevalence rates of gout globally, while Tropical Latin America, the Caribbean, and Central Latin America exhibited the lowest rates [9].

Among the elderly, the burden of gout presents unique challenges, such as the presence of multiple co-morbidities, the use of multiple medications, and physiological alterations associated with aging [10]. These factors collectively amplify the intricacy of managing gout effectively. Furthermore, the increasing number of elderly people makes gout management a challenge for countries around the globe. The management of gout is less than ideal in numerous countries, as only a third to half of those diagnosed with the condition actually undergo urate-lowering therapy (ULT) [11, 12]. Moreover, less than half of these patients consistently follow the necessary long-term treatment regimen [11, 12]. Existing research on gout has highlighted the importance of modifiable risk factors such as high body mass index (BMI) and kidney dysfunction in the development and progression of the disease [13, 14]. However, the extent to which these risk factors contribute to the global burden of gout, particularly among the elderly, remains incompletely understood. An in-depth analysis of the temporal dynamics, regional disparities, and risk factors associated with gout among the elderly is imperative for the development of effective public health strategies and targeted interventions.

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study 2021, spearheaded by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, offers an extensive repository of data on a spectrum of health conditions, including gout. This dataset is instrumental in delineating the contours of the global health panorama and pinpointing pivotal areas for intervention. Currently, there is no detailed GBD study focusing on the global burden and trends of gout among individuals aged ≥ 55 years. The objective of this study was to analyze the prevalence, incidence, and Years Lived with Disability (YLDs) of gout among individuals aged ≥ 55 years in 204 countries and territories, spanning from 1990 to 2021, utilizing data from the GBD study 2021.Additionally, the study assessed the impact of BMI and kidney dysfunction as risk factors on the burden of gout.

Methods

Study population and data collection

In our comprehensive examination of the GBD Study 2021, we have accessed a series of datasets from the Global Health Data Exchange (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool), which includes the global burden of 371 diseases and injuries, such as gout, and 88 risk factors, including elevated BMI and impaired kidney function. The data spanned across 21 regions and 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021. The study focused on a demographic subset of individuals with gout who were 55 years of age and older. This included location-specific, age-specific, and sex-specific prevalence, incidence, and YLDs (with corresponding 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs)). YLDs is a health metric used to measure the non-fatal impact of a disease or injury. It calculates the total number of years of life that are lost due to a disability, taking into account the severity and duration of the health condition. Additionally, we identified the YLDs attributable to each risk factor. The analytical approach adopted in the GBD Study 2021 has been thoroughly detailed in prior works [15, 16]. Given that the study was based on de-identified and publicly accessible epidemiological datasets, it was exempt from the requirement for ethical review, in accordance with Article 32 of the Declaration of Helsinki. Similarly, obtaining patient consent was not a prerequisite for the collection and analysis of data from the GBD study.

According to the GBD study, gout is a rheumatic disease characterized by the deposition of monosodium urate crystals in the synovial fluid of joints and in other tissues, leading to inflammation. The formation of these crystals is caused by elevated urate levels in extracellular fluids. Case definitions of Gout used in GBD study could be found in supplementary file (Supplementary File Section 1). The GBD study 2021 utilized DisMod-MR 2.1, a Bayesian meta-regression tool for disease modeling, to generate coherent estimates of prevalence and incidence of gout, stratified by age, sex, year, and location [15]. Since gout is primarily a condition that affects the quality of life without being a direct cause of death, it is considered non-fatal in the GBD framework. YLDs were estimated by employing a microsimulation approach, which integrates the prevalence of non-fatal health outcomes across a spectrum of diseases and injuries with their respective disability weights for each health condition [15]. The GBD framework categorized gout into three severity levels, with each level receiving a unique set of disability weights that accurately reflect the varied impacts on an individual's health and well-being. The input data and a comprehensive overview of the methodology pertaining to gout can be found in supplementary file (Supplementary File Section 2, 3 and 4).

GBD study 2021 further evaluated the Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) for each nation, an amalgamated measure reflecting the societal and economic factors that shape health outcomes in various regions. This index is derived from the geometric mean of three indicators, ranging from 0 to 1, which include the total fertility rate for people under 25, the average years of schooling for those aged 15 and above, and the income distribution per capita with a lag. Here, a score of 0 indicates the lowest level of education, the lowest income per person, and the highest fertility rate. The 204 countries and territories were categorized into five groups based on SDI: low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high.

Risk factor analysis

In previous iterations of the GBD study, systematic reviews have been conducted to ascertain the potential risk factors linked to gout. The methodology for quantifying the impact of these various risk factors on the overall disease burden has been described in previous study [16]. The proportional attributable fraction for high BMI and kidney dysfunction is a measure of how much the prevalence of gout could theoretically decrease if these risk factors were reduced to the minimum risk level. In the context of the GBD study, high BMI and kidney dysfunction are identified as significant risk factors for the onset of gout. By estimating the proportional decrease in gout that would result from reducing these risk factors to the theoretical minimum risk exposure level, researchers can attribute a portion of the YLDs for gout to these specific risk factors. Further details on the definitions and measurement of the risk factors can be found in supplementary file (Supplementary File Section 5).

Statistical analysis

Count and age-standardized rate (ASR) per 100,000 population with 95% UIs were used to quantify the burden of gout among the elderly. The ASR was calculated using a standard demographic formula to ensure comparability across different age structures: ASR=∑i=1Aaiwi∑i=1Awi×100,000. In this formula, ai represents the age-specific rate of LBP within the i th age group, and wi corresponds to the proportion of the standard population for the i th age group. A denotes the total count of age groups. The 95% UIs were established by identifying the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the ordered draws, which were based on 1000 draw-level estimates for each parameter (GBD’s algorithm). A 95% UI that does not include zero was interpreted as indicative of statistical significance.

The trend of the ASR over a certain period was monitored using the Estimated Annual Percentage Change (EAPC) [17]. A linear regression equation was applied, with the natural logarithm of the rates related to the calendar year as the independent variable, expressed as y = α + βx + ɛ, where x denotes the calendar year and y represents ln (ASR). The EAPC was then determined by the formula 100 × (10^β − 1), and its 95% Confidence Interval (CI) was derived from the regression analysis. If both the EAPC and its 95% CI were below zero, the ASR was viewed as declining. Conversely, if both the EAPC and its 95% CI were above zero, the ASR was considered to be increasing. All statistical analyses were conducted using the R program version 4.0.3.

Results

Global level

The study demonstrated a global prevalence of 37,230,366 (95% UI 28,535,182 to 48,686,032) cases of gout among the elderly. This corresponded to an age-standardized prevalence rate of 2505.4 (95% UI 1920.3 to 3276.3) per 100,000 population. Notably, from 1990 to 2021, this rate exhibited a significant upward trend, with a EAPC of 1.08 (95% CI 0.99 to 1.17). The global new cases of gout in 2021 were 5,508,692 (95% UI 4,074,755 to 7,472,967), with an age-standardized incidence rate of 370.7 per 100,000 population (95% UI 274.2 to 502.9). This incidence rate similarly showed an increase over the same period, with an EAPC of 0.83 (95% CI 0.76 to 0.9). Additionally, gout contributed to 1,117,447 (95% UI 734,147–1,609,315) YLDs globally, with an age-standardized YLD rate of 75.2 (95% UI 49.4 to 108.3) per 100,000 population. The age-standardized YLD rate also experienced an increase from 1990 to 2021, with an EAPC of 1.06 (95% CI 0.96 to 1.15) (Table 1).

Age and sex patterns

In 2021, age-standardized prevalence rate of gout among individuals aged ≥ 55 years was higher in males and increased with age, peaking at the 95 + age groups (Fig. 1). The pattern of age-standardized incidence and YLD rates by sex across the age groups were relatively similar to the age-standardized prevalence rate (Fig. 1).

Age-specific numbers and rates of prevalence (A), incidence (B), and Years Lived with Disability (YLDs) (C) of gout among individuals aged ≥ 55 years in 2021 by sex. Dotted and dashed lines indicate 95% upper and lower uncertainty intervals, respectively

Between 1990 and 2021, the age-standardized prevalence, incidence and YLD rates of gout among individuals aged ≥ 55 years increased for both males and females worldwide (Table 1). The increase in prevalence and YLD of gout was more pronounced among males compared to females. However, the EAPC of the age-standardized rate of incidence rate was consistent in males and females. Across all age groups within the older population worldwide, there was an increase in the age-standardized prevalence, incidence and YLD rates of gout. Specifically, the age group 70–74 years exhibited the most significant growth trend in the age-standardized prevalence and YLD rates, while the age group 65–69 years showed the most significant growth trend in the age-standardized incidence rate (Table 1).

Burden of gout by SDI

The age-standardized prevalence, incidence and YLD rates of gout were significantly lower in countries with low SDI levels (Table 1 and Fig. 2). Between 1990 and 2021, the age-standardized prevalence, incidence and YLD rates increased across all SDI levels, with the most pronounced rise observed in high SDI countries (Table 1, Figs. 2 and S1-2). During this period, a positive correlation was observed between the age-standardized prevalence, incidence and YLD rates and the SDI (Fig. 3).

Regional level

In 2021, the age-standardized prevalence rate of gout among individuals aged ≥ 55 years was highest in Australasia [6295.7 (95% UI 4687.4 to 8307) per 100,000 population], followed by High-income North America [6153.8 (95% UI 4863.7 to 7761.1) per 100,000 population]. In contrast, Central Latin America [574.8 (95% UI 436.6 to 759.1) per 100,000 population], and Caribbean [827.4 (95% UI 641.3 to 1066.6) per 100,000 population] reported the lowest rates (Fig. 2 and Table S1). With the exception of Western Sub-Saharan Africa, there was a rising trend in the age-standardized prevalence rate from 1990 to 2021. High-income North America experienced the most substantial increase, with an EAPC of 2.79 (95% CI 2.42 to 3.17) (Fig. 2 and Table S1).

In 2021, Australasia notably exhibited the highest age-standardized incidence and YLDs rates (Fig. S1-2 and Table S1). Conversely, Central Latin America was characterized by the lowest age-standardized incidence and YLDs rates in the same year (Fig. S1-2 and Table S1). Over the period from 1990 to 2021, the most pronounced increase in these metrics was observed in the High-income North America (Fig. S1-2 and Table S1).

National level

Australia [6296.3 (95% UI 4664.5 to 8320.8) per 100,000 population], New Zealand [6292.3 (95% UI 4686.7 to 8260.6) per 100,000 population] and United States of America [6215.8 (95% UI 4940.4 to 7814.9) per 100,000 population] had the highest age-standardized prevalence rate gout among the elderly in 2021 across 204 countries and territories (Fig. 4 and Table S1). Most of the 204 countries demonstrated increasing trend of age-standardized prevalence rate from 1990 to 2021. The top three were United States of America [EAPC = 3.04 (95% CI 2.61 to 3.48)], China ([EAPC = 1.3 (95% CI 1.15 to 1.44)] and Australia [EAPC = 1.23 (95% CI 1.14 to 1.32)] (Fig. 4 and Table S1).

In 2021, New Zealand exhibited the highest age-standardized incidence and YLDs rates. Risk factors

In 2021, globally, the burden of gout among the elderly was significantly influenced by high BMI and kidney dysfunction, which accounted for 35.1% and 16.5% of the YLDs, respectively (Fig. 5). The proportion of YLDs attributable to high BMI increased with SDI levels, peaking at 42.8% in high SDI. The regional attributable risk fraction of high BMI varied significantly, from 19.8% to 48.6%, with the highest proportion noted in high-income North America (48.6%). The impact of high BMI on YLD was also age-dependent, with the most pronounced effect observed in the age group 60–64 years, accounting for 36.5% of YLDs (Fig. 5).

The proportion of Years Lived with Disability (YLDs) of gout among individuals aged ≥ 55 years attributable to high body mass index and kidney dysfunction

Similarly, the YLDs attributable to kidney dysfunction was most pronounced in regions with high SDI, where it constituted 21.1% (Fig. 5). The regional risk fraction for kidney dysfunction varied from 8.2% to 23.5%, with high-income North America again showing the highest proportion at 23.5%. The proportion of YLDs due to kidney dysfunction also increased with age, culminating in the 95 + age group, where it represented 45.1% of YLDs.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study was the first to describe the gout burden and its changing trends among individuals aged ≥ 55 years from 1990 to 2021 at the global, regional and national levels, stratified by age, sex and SDI. The results indicated a significant increase in the prevalence, incidence, and YLDs of gout among the elderly worldwide, with notable variations across age, sex, SDI levels, regions, and countries. These findings underscore the critical need for heightened awareness, early detection, and effective management strategies to mitigate the impact of gout on aging populations worldwide.

The increase in the age-standardized prevalence, incidence, and YLD rates from 1990 to 2021, with EAPCs of 1.08, 0.83, and 1.06 respectively, indicated a steady upward trend. The simultaneous increase in prevalence, incidence, and YLD rates suggested that the prevention and treatment targeted at elderly patients with gout were highly unsuccessful. Urate-lowering therapy (ULT) is recommended for people with recurrent acute attacks of gout [18]. However, adherence to and persistence with ULT are often compromised among older patients, who may also have contraindications to long-term prescription of ULT medications [10], potentially leading to increased disability and impaired quality of life due to recurrent acute attacks. Furthermore, among the elderly population, chronic gout can exacerbate heart disease and kidney conditions [10, 19], thereby increasing the overall mortality rate and organ-related complications. Given the current challenges in gout prevention, screening, diagnosis, treatment, and adherence among the elderly globally [2], these results underscore the urgent need for focused strategies to address the needs of ageing populations and older gout patients, the rational allocation of healthcare resources, and the development of targeted guidelines. The findings are significant for health practitioners and future research, offering encouraging evidence for all gout patients, particularly those diagnosed at a young age.

Our findings revealed that gout was more prevalent among males, with the age-standardized rate of prevalence peaking in the oldest age groups (95 + years). This sex disparity is consistent with the current understanding that hormonal differences, genetic predispositions, and lifestyle factors contribute to a higher prevalence of gout in males [20–22]. However, in the present study, the EAPC of the age-standardized rate of incidence rate was consistent in males and females. This could be partly attributed to the global trend of an ageing population. Estrogen has a uricosuric effect, and for elderly women, after menopause, they lose the protective effect of estrogen [20]. Consequently, due to population ageing, the burden of gout in females is expected to increase over the coming decades. Additionally, the age-specific patterns highlighted the significant burden of gout in the elderly, with the most substantial growth in prevalence and YLDs observed in the 70–74 age group and in incidence in the 65–69 age group. The elderly often have multiple comorbidities, such as hypertension, diabetes, and kidney disease, which can increase the risk of developing gout [2, 10]. Gout may be underdiagnosed or undertreated in the elderly, leading to a higher prevalence and more severe disease manifestations [23]. It's a call to action for the medical community to craft strategies that not only prevent gout but also ensure early detection and effective management, especially for the elderly.

The study also found significant disparities in the burden of gout across different SDI levels. Countries with higher SDI levels exhibited higher age-standardized prevalence, incidence, and YLDs rates. At the regional level, Australasia had the highest age-standardized prevalence, incidence, and YLDs rates, with the United States showing the most significant increase in these rates. This positive correlation between SDI and gout burden may be linked to lifestyle factors such as dietary habits, higher prevalence of obesity and kidney dysfunction, and greater access to healthcare leading to better diagnosis and reporting [24]. In addition, regional disparities in gout prevalence may be partially attributed to the influence of genetic and racial determinants. Previous research has shown that the incidence of gout is significantly higher in certain racial/ethnic groups, such as African Americans, Hmong Chinese in the United States of America, and New Zealand Maoris, compared to individuals of European descent [8]. The increased rates in countries with higher SDI may stem from more robust healthcare systems that facilitate accurate detection and recording of cases. Conversely, this pattern implies that less developed areas with lower SDI might see a hidden burden of gout due to inadequate healthcare infrastructure. This relationship between SDI and the capacity to diagnose and manage gout underscores the need for public health strategies in lower SDI regions, focusing on bolstering healthcare systems to guarantee the accurate identification and treatment of gout sufferers.

The management of gout has seen significant advancements with the introduction of ULT and attack-reducing drugs [24]. However, treatment of this disease remains suboptimal worldwide [24]. Even in countries with well-established healthcare systems such as the United States of America, the United Kingdom, and Australasia, the proportion of gout patients who adhere to ULT is quite low [25–27]. The suboptimal prescription and adherence to ULT can be largely attributed to insufficient patient education by healthcare providers and misconceptions regarding treatment goals and outcomes [11]. Elderly patients often grapple with multiple co-morbidities that require multiple medications, which can lead to difficulties in managing and adhering to ULT [23]. Additionally, the potential for adverse drug reactions may cause patients to discontinue their medication, especially if they are not adequately informed about the importance of consistent treatment for gout [23]. Interventions led by pharmacists and nurses have demonstrated efficacy in enhancing gout management, with studies showing improved adherence to ULT and achievement of treatment goals [11]. Patient-targeted education and technology-based tools like mobile applications are being explored to further support self-management and adherence, although the development in this area is still emerging [28]. For elderly patients with comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, or heart disease, a thorough review of medications is essential, as the drugs used to treat these conditions may lead to increased uric acid levels in the blood or complicate the treatment of hyperuricemia. It is recommended to prioritize the prescription of medications that have a neutral effect on uric acid levels [23].

We observed that High BMI was major contributors to the burden of gout among the elderly. Previous studies have established a positive correlation between a High BMI and the risk of gout [14, 29, 30]. Specifically, individuals with a BMI of 30 kg/m2 or higher showed a 2.24 times increased risk of developing gout [14]. Even after controlling for serum uric acid levels, a robust and statistically significant dose–response correlation is observed between an elevated BMI and the increased prevalence of gout [31]. The impact of high BMI on the burden of gout increased with higher levels of SDI. High-income North America had the highest YLDs attributable to high BMI. In the United States of America, the prevalence of a BMI of 25 kg/m2 is approximately 57.5% among individuals aged 20 to 39 years, however, this rate increases to around 70% among those in their 40 s and older [32]. The prevalence of obesity is also high in many Western countries [33]. The prevalence of obesity is rising progressively, even among older age groups [34]. Lifestyle interventions, which include a well-balanced diet, behavioral therapy, and multi-component exercise, are recommended for individuals with gout who are obese [35].

Kidney dysfunction was another significant contributor to the burden of gout, especially in high SDI regions. Kidney dysfunction can impair the excretion of uric acid, thereby contributing to hyperuricemia and subsequently gout [13]. Patients (aged ≥ 66 years) with a lower level of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) had a higher 3-year cumulative incidence of gout [36]. The proportion of YLDs attributable to kidney dysfunction in individuals with gout showed a significant increase with age, with the highest proportion observed in the 95 + age group. The management of gout poses a significant challenge, particularly among the elderly who often experience renal impairment. This demographic requires a heightened level of caution due to the increased risk of adverse effects associated with their compromised kidney function [37]. It's important for individuals with gout and kidney dysfunction to work closely with their healthcare provider to develop a treatment plan that considers their overall health, kidney function, and the specific needs of their condition.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the accuracy of the data depends on the quality and completeness of health records from various countries, which may vary significantly. Secondly, the use of age-standardized rates, while helpful for comparison, may obscure important age-specific trends. Thirdly, the attribution of YLDs to specific risk factors such as high BMI and kidney dysfunction is based on population-level data and may not account for individual variability. Lastly, the study did not explore the impact of treatment strategies and healthcare access, which could influence the burden of gout. We also recognize that our study did not assess the comparative efficacy of a Treat-to-Target versus a Treat-to-Symptom approach in managing gout, which could be a valuable area for future research. Future research should aim to address these limitations by incorporating more comprehensive data and exploring the interplay of various risk factors on gout burden.

Conclusion

This study indicated the substantial and growing burden of gout among the elderly globally, with significant regional, age, and sex variations. Recognizing the diverse burden of gout, region-specific guidelines are essential, considering local risk factors and healthcare access. In lower SDI regions, improving healthcare access and timely diagnosis should be prioritized. Particularly in high SDI regions, where high BMI and kidney dysfunction were prevalent, there is a critical need for targeted interventions focusing on obesity management and early kidney disease screening. Additionally, further research is needed to understand the long-term impacts of gout treatments on the elderly, especially in relation to comorbidities like kidney disease, to foster a more integrated approach to care. Continued research and targeted public health efforts are essential to mitigate the impact of gout globally.

Addressing key risk factors such as high BMI and kidney dysfunction, especially in high SDI regions, is crucial for mitigating the impact of gout in aging populations. Future research should focus on identifying and addressing underlying causes, improving diagnostic and treatment approaches, and developing targeted prevention strategies to reduce the global burden of gout.
