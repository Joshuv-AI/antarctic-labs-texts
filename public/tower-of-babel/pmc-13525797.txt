Oral Health Behavior Management in Patients with Periodontitis: A COM-B-Guided Qualitative Study
Authors: Jianuo Bao, Yuan Li, Lei Zhang, Shuying Li, Fanli Meng, Yingxue Huang, Rui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to explore barriers and support needs related to oral health behaviors among patients with periodontitis guided by the Capability–Opportunity–Motivation–Behavior (COM-B) model in a Chinese clinical context.Patients and MethodsSemi-structured qualitative interviews were conducted with 17 patients diagnosed with stage II–IV periodontitis who attended the Department of Stomatology at a tertiary hospital in Chengde, China, between December 2025 and January 2026. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling. Interviews were conducted in person, and data were analyzed using qualitative content analysis guided by the COM-B model. Themes and sub-themes were identified through an iterative and collaborative coding process conducted by two trained researchers.ResultsThree main themes and eleven sub-themes were identified, reflecting barriers and support needs related to oral health behaviors. Within the capability domain, barriers included insufficient disease knowledge and information literacy, inadequate skills in using oral hygiene tools, and physical limitations. Within the opportunity domain, barriers included constraints related to objective resources and life circumstances and unsupportive social norms, while participants expressed support needs for professional and social support. Within the motivation domain, barriers included negative behavioral experiences, concerns about social judgment, and lack of self-efficacy, whereas perceived benefits and disease awareness and the desire to preserve oral health supported sustained oral health behaviors.ConclusionPatients with periodontitis experience interconnected barriers and support needs across capability, opportunity, and motivation domains. These qualitative findings provide evidence to inform the development of tailored oral health behavior interventions.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a prevalent chronic inflammatory disease and a major public health concern worldwide, imposing substantial burdens on oral health, general health, and quality of life.1,2 The 2021 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) analysis reported that severe periodontitis had a global age-standardised prevalence of 12.5%, affecting approximately 1.07 billion people worldwide in 2021.3 In China, the prevalence of periodontitis among adults aged over 35 years has also been reported to be substantial, reaching approximately 62.3%.2 In addition to causing periodontal tissue destruction and tooth loss, periodontitis has been associated with impaired chewing function, psychosocial distress, and increased risks of systemic conditions such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes.4,5 As the burden of chronic oral diseases continues to increase, improving oral health has become an important component of health promotion and chronic disease management. Dental plaque biofilm is the primary initiating factor for periodontitis, and studies have shown that behavior management targeting plaque control combined with professional supragingival scaling can effectively reduce plaque accumulation and improve periodontal outcomes.1,6 Although plaque control remains a central component of periodontal self-management, long-term periodontal stability also depends on a broader range of sustained oral health behaviors, including regular dental attendance, healthy dietary and exercise habits, smoking cessation, reduced alcohol consumption, adequate sleep, and other lifestyle behaviors.7–9 Such behaviors influence periodontal health through their effects on disease-related processes, such as plaque accumulation and inflammation, while also shaping patients’ engagement in long-term periodontal self-management. Among these modifiable behaviors, smoking represents an important behavioral target within comprehensive periodontal behavior management because it is a major preventable risk factor for periodontal destruction and poorer treatment outcomes.10 Substantial evidence has demonstrated that tobacco use is associated with increased periodontal tissue destruction and impaired treatment response.11,12 However, smoking cessation is a complex behavior change process that can be affected by smokers’ personal conditions, social and environmental contexts.13 Therefore, these modifiable behaviors should be considered important targets for periodontal behavior management, and understanding the factors influencing their maintenance and modification is essential for developing context-sensitive behavioral interventions. However, inadequate oral health behaviors remain highly prevalent among patients with periodontitis and are closely associated with poor disease management, disease recurrence, and reduced quality of life.14 Therefore, promoting sustainable oral health behaviors among patients with periodontitis is not only critical for individual disease control but also an important public health strategy for reducing the long-term burden of oral diseases.

Previous qualitative studies have explored patients’ perceptions and understanding of periodontitis, treatment experiences, psychosocial impacts, and support needs, providing important insights into how patients experience periodontal disease and its management.15–17 Recent qualitative research has further explored patients’ perspectives on digital support for periodontal care, highlighting patients’ expectations and perceived needs regarding additional support for maintaining oral health behaviors.18 Collectively, these studies have advanced understanding of patients’ perspectives on periodontitis and identified a range of factors that may influence oral health behaviors. However, they have primarily focused on describing patients’ experiences and perceived influences on behavior, with relatively limited attention to the behavioral mechanisms through which these factors interact to influence the initiation and long-term maintenance of recommended oral health behaviors.

Furthermore, maintaining oral health behaviors is a complex process influenced not only by individual factors but also by the social and cultural environments in which patients live. Sociocultural factors, including traditional health beliefs regarding disease causation, patients’ interpretations of symptoms, and health-related decision-making influenced by family and social networks, may shape how individuals understand periodontal disease and engage in self-management behaviors.19 Therefore, exploring these sociocultural influences may provide a more contextually grounded understanding of how patients perceive, adopt, and maintain oral health behaviors within the Chinese context.

Beyond sociocultural influences, patients’ behavioral experiences are also shaped by the broader social and environmental contexts in which oral health behaviors are maintained. Within this broader context, tertiary clinical settings in China provide an important setting for examining periodontal behavior management, as patients receiving specialist periodontal care are provided with professional treatment, oral health guidance, and recommendations for long-term self-management.20,21 However, periodontal management extends beyond clinical care, as sustained disease control depends on patients’ ability to integrate these recommendations into daily life and maintain them over time. This process may be influenced by both the individual factors of the patients and the social environmental factors, highlighting the necessity to explore these interacting factors within the Chinese clinical context.

The Capability–Opportunity–Motivation–Behavior (COM-B) model proposes that behavior results from the interaction between individual factors (capability and motivation) and environmental factors (opportunity), providing a theoretical framework for explaining how multiple determinants interact to shape behavior and identifying potential targets for behavior change interventions.22 The COM-B model has been widely applied to understand barriers and facilitators of health-related behaviors across different populations and clinical settings, providing a systematic framework for explaining how individual and environmental factors contribute to behavior change.23,24 Given that long-term oral health behavior maintenance involves interactions among individual and environmental factors, this model is particularly suitable for exploring patients’ behavioral experiences and support needs related to sustained behavior change.

Therefore, this study employs a qualitative descriptive research approach based on the COM-B model to explore, from the patients’ perspective, the barriers and support needs related to oral health behavior management among patients with periodontitis. The findings will provide a theory-informed understanding of how capability, opportunity, and motivation shape patients’ behaviors and inform the development of patient-centred behavioral interventions.

Materials and Methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Chengde Medical University (No. CYFYLL2025443). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, which included permission for the publication of anonymized responses and direct quotes, and all patient data were analyzed anonymously.

Research Design

Participants were recruited between December 2025 and January 2026 from the Department of Stomatology at a tertiary hospital in Chengde, China. Purposive sampling with maximum variation was employed to capture diverse patient experiences across differences in age, sex, educational background, and stages of periodontitis. Eligible participants met the following inclusion criteria: (i) aged 18 years or older; (ii) newly diagnosed with stage II–IV periodontitis according to the 2018 classification of periodontal and peri-implant diseases and conditions;25 (iii) able to communicate effectively and participate in interviews; (iv) possessing at least 16 natural teeth;26 and (v) willing to participate and provide written informed consent. Patients were excluded if they had hearing or visual impairments that could affect participation in interviews, or severe psychiatric or systemic conditions that might interfere with communication or study participation.

The study was designed and reported in accordance with the 32-item Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Health Research (COREQ).27

Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently. Following each interview, transcripts were transcribed, preliminarily coded, and continuously compared with previously analyzed data. No formal saturation documentation was maintained; however, saturation was considered throughout the iterative analytical process. Saturation was assessed by the research team at the level of themes and conceptual categories. Data Collection

Eligible patients were initially identified by a periodontist in the Department of Stomatology according to the study eligibility criteria. Patients who expressed interest in participating were referred to the first author. Before each interview, the first author introduced the study, explained its purpose and procedures, and obtained written informed consent together with permission for audio recording. Data were collected through face-to-face semi-structured interviews conducted in a quiet and private setting free from interruptions. Each interview lasted approximately 15–30 min. Interviews were conducted by the first author, a master’s student in nursing with four years of clinical nursing training, including one year of clinical experience in stomatology nursing. The interviewer had received formal training in qualitative research methods and interviewing techniques. The interviewer was not involved in the participants’ periodontal treatment and had no prior therapeutic or personal relationship with any participant before recruitment. No interviews were conducted by the participants’ treating periodontists. Participants were informed that the interviewer was independent of their clinical treatment and care, that participation was entirely voluntary, and that their decision to participate or withdraw would not affect their treatment.

During the interviews, participants were encouraged to openly describe their experiences and perceptions related to oral health behaviors. The interviewer maintained an open and non-judgmental stance throughout the interviews to reduce the influence of personal expectations on participants’ responses. Interview techniques such as open-ended questions, probing, clarification, and reflective listening were used to facilitate in-depth discussion. In addition, field notes documenting participants’ nonverbal expressions and body language and contextual observations were recorded during the interviews to enrich contextual understanding of the data.29 Given the interviewer’s clinical background in stomatology nursing, reflective discussions with the second author were conducted during the analytical process to critically examine potential researcher influence on data interpretation.

The multidisciplinary research team included nursing researchers and periodontists. The periodontists provided clinical expertise in the study design, development and refinement of the interview guide, and discussion of the findings, but were not involved in participant recruitment, interviewing, transcription, translation, or data coding.

Data Analysis

Audio recordings and field notes were transcribed verbatim within 24 hours after each interview by the first and second authors, who had received formal training in qualitative research methods. When ambiguities arose during transcription, participants were contacted by telephone for clarification to ensure that the transcripts accurately reflected their intended meanings. All transcripts were anonymized and systematically organized before analysis. Quotations presented in this manuscript were translated into English by the first and second authors and independently reviewed by the corresponding author. Any discrepancies in translation were resolved through discussion to ensure semantic equivalence with the original Chinese transcripts.

Data were analyzed using directed content analysis guided by the COM-B model with the assistance of NVivo 12 software. The first and second authors repeatedly read the transcripts to achieve immersion in the data and identify meaningful statements related to oral health behaviors. Initial coding was conducted inductively from participants’ narratives without assigning data directly to predefined COM-B domains. Similar codes were compared and grouped into categories through repeated review and refinement of the coded data. Coding definitions, category structures, and theme titles were iteratively refined throughout the analytical process to improve conceptual consistency and distinguish between closely related categories. Only after categories had been inductively developed were they mapped onto the relevant COM-B domains to facilitate theoretical interpretation, from which the final themes and subthemes were generated.

The first and second authors independently coded all transcripts. Coding results were compared, and discrepancies regarding code assignment and category development were discussed between the two researchers. When consensus could not be reached, disagreements were resolved through further discussion within the research team until agreement was achieved. Key analytical decisions, including revisions to coding definitions, coding categories, and themes, were documented throughout the analytical process. Final themes were agreed upon only after repeated review of the original transcripts to ensure that they accurately reflected participants’ experiences.

Trustworthiness

To enhance the trustworthiness of the study, strategies addressing credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability were implemented throughout the research process.

Credibility was enhanced through familiarity with the data, verbatim transcription of audio recordings, and integration of field notes documenting participants’ non-verbal expressions, body language, and contextual observations. During the interviews, the interviewer used open-ended questions, probing, clarification, reflective listening, and summarising to facilitate in-depth discussion and confirm mutual understanding with participants. When ambiguities arose during transcription, participants were contacted by telephone to clarify their intended meanings and ensure that the transcripts accurately reflected their experiences.

Dependability was strengthened through an iterative and transparent analytical process. Two researchers independently coded the transcripts, compared coding results, and resolved disagreements through discussion until consensus was achieved. Key analytical decisions were documented throughout the analytical process.

Confirmability refers to the extent to which findings are derived from the data rather than researchers’ subjective interpretations. In this study, reflexivity and regular discussions within the research team were used to enhance confirmability. Interpretations were repeatedly compared with participants’ accounts to ensure consistency with the interview data. Representative quotations were used to support the interpretation of findings.

Transferability was supported by providing detailed descriptions of the study setting, participant characteristics, recruitment procedures, data collection, and analytical process, enabling readers to judge the applicability of the findings to other contexts.

Capability Factors

Insufficient Disease Knowledge and Information Literacy

Many participants demonstrated limited understanding of periodontitis and had difficulty recognizing early symptoms of the disease. Misinterpretation of symptoms reduced patients’ perception of disease risk and delayed preventive actions. I never really paid attention to dental problems before. Even if something was wrong, I probably wouldn’t notice it myself. (P7) Sometimes my gums felt uncomfortable, but I couldn’t tell what was going on. Then I got busy and kept putting it off, and it gradually became worse. (P2) I didn’t know it was a periodontal problem. Since there wasn’t any pain, I thought everything was fine. By the time I realized it was serious, it was already too late. (P17)

Some participants reported difficulty selecting and using interdental cleaning tools, and repeated unsuccessful attempts discouraged continued use. Opportunity Factors

Constraints Related to Objective Resources and Life Circumstances

External environmental constraints may reduce opportunities for patients to initiate and maintain oral health behaviors. Competing demands from work, family, and academic responsibilities often led patients to prioritize immediate daily obligations over preventive oral care. Rural participants reported greater difficulty accessing dental services and oral hygiene products. Frequent postponement and disruption of oral health behaviors prevented their integration into daily routines, hindering the development and long-term maintenance of sound oral hygiene habits. Taking care of my child, going to work, and doing housework take up the whole day. By the end of the day, I’m so exhausted that I just want to lie down and sleep. (P11) Work is always extremely busy. I kept putting off dealing with my teeth, and the problems gradually became worse. (P3)

Professional and Social Support Needs

Participants consistently expressed the need for ongoing professional and social support rather than one-time health education. Regular feedback and interpersonal support enabled patients to confirm whether they were performing oral health behaviors correctly, reducing uncertainty during self-management. This continuous reinforcement promoted confidence in behavioral performance and facilitated the long-term maintenance of recommended oral health practices. I wish the dentist could show me in person how to do it. (P12) If I could have regular follow-ups and someone point out what I’m not doing well, I’d know where I still need to improve. Motivational Factors

Negative Behavioral Experiences

When oral health behaviors were associated with pain, discomfort, or the possibility of causing further harm, patients were more likely to perceive these behaviors as threatening rather than beneficial. This shift in behavioral appraisal promoted avoidance as a means of preventing anticipated discomfort, even when patients recognized the importance of oral health management. Using dental floss sometimes makes my gums bleed, which makes me feel resistant to using it. (P13) The last time I had scaling done, it hurt so much that I broke out in a sweat. Since then, I’ve been a bit afraid of dental treatment. (P5) Every time I think about dental treatment, I feel nervous. (P10) I’m always worried that brushing incorrectly will make my teeth even worse. (P11)

Concerns About Social Judgment

When oral health behaviors were perceived as socially inappropriate or likely to attract negative attention, patients became more concerned with avoiding embarrassment than maintaining recommended behaviors. As a result, anticipated social discomfort reduced their willingness to perform oral hygiene practices in situations where these behaviors might be visible to others. I always feel like people are watching me and thinking I’m being overly particular. (P14) Using dental floss in public feels especially awkward and inappropriate. (P11)

Lack of Self-Efficacy

Older participants more frequently expressed reduced confidence in improving their oral health, whereas some younger participants underestimated their susceptibility to periodontal disease. Limited self-efficacy weakened patients’ confidence in overcoming barriers encountered during daily oral health management and hindered their ability to translate intentions into consistent oral health behaviors. (P7) I’ve smoked for many years. I know it’s not good, but habits are hard to change. (P9) Sometimes after drinking alcohol, I just want to rest when I get home, and whether I brush my teeth depends on my mood. (P14)

Perceived Benefits and Disease Awareness

Participants who had experienced noticeable disease consequences or received explanations about the relationship between periodontitis and systemic health were more likely to recognize the benefits of maintaining oral health behaviors. Clear communication about disease-related risks helped patients link daily self-care with meaningful health outcomes, thereby strengthening motivation to sustain these behaviors over time. I always feel uncomfortable in my mouth and notice an odor, so I keep brushing to make it better. (P2) If doctors clearly explained the consequences to me, I would take it much more seriously. (P1) The doctor told me my periodontitis was related to diabetes, so I knew I couldn’t ignore it. (P3) The more I can maintain things now, the less suffering and expense I’ll have later. (P10)

Desire to Preserve Oral Health

Expectations of favorable health and social outcomes increased the perceived value of oral health behaviors. When patients recognized that consistent self-care could preserve natural teeth, improve quality of life, and reduce social embarrassment, they were more willing to invest effort in maintaining these behaviors over time. Interactions Among Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation

The findings indicated that capability, opportunity, and motivation were not independent determinants of oral health behaviors but were factors that interacted dynamically to influence patients’ ability to initiate and sustain oral health behaviors. First, insufficient oral health knowledge limited patients’ understanding of periodontal disease progression and the consequences of inadequate self-care, thereby reducing risk perception and weakening motivation to engage in preventive behaviors. Second, inadequate professional guidance and the lack of continuous feedback affected patients’ ability to perform oral hygiene practices correctly. Uncertainty regarding the effectiveness of their behaviors gradually undermined confidence in maintaining these behaviors over time. Third, opportunity-related barriers interacted with motivational factors to further restrict behavioral performance. Although some patients recognized the importance of interdental cleaning, concerns about social judgment, together with limited opportunities to perform these behaviors comfortably in daily environments, reduced their willingness to use interdental cleaning tools. Finally, maintaining oral health behaviors required more than health education alone. For patients experiencing individual functional or practical difficulties in performing oral hygiene behaviors, individualized guidance and appropriate selection of oral hygiene tools may better support sustained behavioral performance than information provision alone.

Discussion

The present study provides a comprehensive understanding of the behavioral determinants influencing oral health behavior management among patients with periodontitis within the COM-B model. The findings suggest that psychological and physical capability, physical and social opportunity, and reflective and automatic motivation interact dynamically to influence the initiation and long-term maintenance of oral health behaviors, rather than acting as independent determinants. Behavioral capability enables patients to acquire and apply disease-related knowledge and oral hygiene skills; however, these capabilities may not translate into sustained behaviors without supportive physical and social environments. Likewise, favorable opportunities alone are unlikely to maintain behavioral change if patients lack confidence in their ability to influence periodontal outcomes or remain constrained by established habits and misconceptions. Although the three COM-B domains are interdependent, our findings indicate that strengthening behavioral capability may represent the most feasible initial target for clinical intervention, as deficiencies in disease understanding, health information appraisal, and oral hygiene skills directly limited patients’ ability to perform appropriate self-care behaviors. Nevertheless, sustainable behavior change is unlikely to be achieved without simultaneously improving external support systems and strengthening long-term motivation.

Strengthening Psychological and Physical Capability to Translate Oral Health Knowledge into Practice

The present findings suggest that inadequate psychological and physical capability represents a fundamental barrier preventing patients from translating oral health knowledge into sustained self-care practices. Within the COM-B model, behavioral capability encompasses not only the ability to understand disease-related information but also the practical skills required to perform oral hygiene behaviors. Participants experienced difficulties in recognizing periodontal symptoms, critically appraising online health information, and selecting and using oral hygiene devices appropriately, indicating that deficiencies in disease understanding and behavioral skills jointly limited the adoption of recommended oral health behaviors. Older adults, individuals with lower educational attainment, and those living in rural areas experienced greater difficulties in acquiring and applying oral health knowledge, suggesting that behavioral capability is influenced by both individual characteristics and the health information environment. Similar associations between age, educational level, living environment, and oral health literacy have been reported by Li and Gero.30,31 An important contextual finding was that some participants interpreted gingival bleeding as a manifestation of “internal heat” (shang-huo), a common health belief in Chinese culture. This interpretation may influence patients’ understanding of periodontal disease and reduce their awareness of the need for timely professional care. In addition, without the ability to critically appraise online health information, patients may adopt inaccurate or misleading advice, resulting in inappropriate self-management practices or delayed professional treatment.

Therefore, improving behavioral capability should extend beyond knowledge dissemination and prioritize the development of practical behavioral skills and the ability to critically appraise oral health information. Dental professionals should incorporate structured chairside demonstrations, hands-on practice, individualized selection of interdental cleaning devices, teach-back methods, and repeated feedback into routine periodontal care to improve patients’ competence in performing oral hygiene behaviors. Oral health education should also acknowledge patients’ existing health beliefs while providing clear biomedical explanations of periodontal disease, thereby helping patients distinguish common cultural interpretations from periodontal disease symptoms. Furthermore, healthcare professionals should recommend reliable health information sources and guide patients in evaluating the credibility of online information, enabling them to make informed decisions regarding oral health behavior management.32

Optimizing Physical and Social Opportunities to Sustain Oral Health Behaviors

The present findings suggest that maintaining oral health behaviors depends not only on individual capability but also on the availability of supportive physical and social opportunities. Participants described multiple barriers, including limited access to oral hygiene products, financial burden, travel distance, competing work and family responsibilities, and insufficient professional follow-up, all of which constrained the long-term maintenance of recommended behaviors. These findings indicate that even when patients possess adequate knowledge and behavioral skills, sustained behavior change may be difficult to achieve if the surrounding environment does not adequately support behavioral implementation. Rural residence appeared to further exacerbate these challenges because reduced accessibility to dental services and limited healthcare resources restricted opportunities for continuous periodontal care, which is consistent with the findings of Parmar.33 In addition, specialized periodontal treatment in China is mainly provided by tertiary general hospitals and tertiary stomatological hospitals. Although these institutions provide specialized periodontal treatment, patients, particularly those living in rural areas, may experience substantial barriers to regular periodontal maintenance visits because of travel distance, time commitment, and associated costs. These barriers may reduce continuity of professional follow-up and limit opportunities for ongoing behavioral reinforcement, thereby compromising the long-term maintenance of oral health behaviors. Limited family involvement may reduce reminders, supervision, and emotional encouragement for maintaining oral hygiene behaviors, thereby weakening long-term behavioral reinforcement after patients leave the clinical setting.34

Therefore, interventions should strengthen supportive environments alongside individual behavioral capability. Healthcare organizations should establish structured follow-up systems that provide regular behavioral reinforcement and personalized feedback after periodontal treatment. Digital health platforms21,35 may complement routine clinical care by delivering remote follow-up, behavioral reminders, and individualized guidance. Such platforms can reduce geographical and time-related barriers while maintaining continuity of professional support.36,37 Policymakers and public health authorities should prioritize oral health education resources for underserved populations, particularly older adults, individuals with lower educational attainment, and residents of rural areas, to improve equitable access to continuous behavioral support.38 Long-term oral health behavior management should not rely solely on periodontal clinics. Strengthening collaboration among dental professionals, primary healthcare institutions, and families may facilitate continuous oral health education, behavioral monitoring, and follow-up support after periodontal treatment.39

Strengthening Reflective and Automatic Motivation to Sustain Oral Health Behaviors

The present findings suggest that sustained oral health behaviors are influenced by both reflective and automatic motivation. Reflective motivation was primarily shaped by patients’ risk perception, beliefs about periodontal disease, and confidence in their ability to influence oral health outcomes. Younger participants often underestimated the risks associated with periodontal disease because symptoms were mild or inconspicuous during the early stages, whereas some older participants regarded gingival bleeding, tooth mobility, and eventual tooth loss as inevitable consequences of aging. These beliefs may weaken patients’ confidence that their own behaviors can influence periodontal outcomes, thereby reducing motivation to maintain long-term oral health behaviors. Automatic motivation was influenced by immediate treatment experiences and established lifestyle habits. Positive experiences, such as improved oral comfort and fresher breath, provided immediate reinforcement for continued oral hygiene behaviors, whereas unpleasant treatment experiences and dentin hypersensitivity discouraged behavioral adherence. This suggests that patients often place greater emphasis on immediately perceived benefits and costs than on long-term health outcomes when making decisions about oral health behaviors.40 Similarly, smoking and alcohol consumption may become habitual behaviors that compete with oral health goals, making long-term behavioral change more difficult.41 Recent evidence has also demonstrated that smoking is strongly associated with poorer periodontal outcomes, further highlighting smoking cessation as an important component of periodontal behavior management.12

Therefore, strengthening motivation should involve strategies targeting both reflective and automatic motivational processes. Dental professionals should provide individualized risk communication to correct misconceptions regarding periodontal disease and aging while emphasizing that disease progression can be effectively controlled through appropriate treatment and sustained self-care. Periodic feedback on periodontal status and behavioral progress should also be incorporated into routine periodontal care to strengthen patients’ confidence in managing their oral health and reinforce positive behavioral changes. Positive treatment experiences should be used to help patients recognize the immediate benefits of oral hygiene behaviors and enhance behavioral reinforcement. For patients with smoking- or alcohol-related behaviors, healthcare organizations should integrate motivational interviewing, behavioral counseling, and referral to smoking cessation services into routine periodontal care to facilitate sustainable lifestyle changes.42

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited from a single tertiary hospital in China, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other healthcare settings and populations. Second, participants were recruited from individuals who actively sought periodontal care and may therefore have had greater health awareness or motivation for behavior change than individuals who do not access dental services regularly. Consequently, the experiences of patients with lower levels of healthcare engagement may not have been fully captured. Finally, although data saturation was achieved, the relatively small sample size and qualitative design mean that the findings should be interpreted as context-specific qualitative evidence rather than statistically generalisable conclusions or evidence of intervention effectiveness. Future research should include patients from diverse regions, healthcare settings, and levels of healthcare engagement to enhance the transferability of findings.

Conclusions

Guided by the COM-B model, this study identified interconnected behavioral determinants, including barriers and support needs, across the domains of capability, opportunity, and motivation related to oral health behavior management among patients with periodontitis, highlighting the complexity of sustaining oral health behaviors. The findings suggest that future interventions should prioritise strengthening patients’ oral health knowledge and practical self-care skills, while addressing social and resource barriers, enhancing self-efficacy, leveraging positive motivational factors such as disease awareness and the desire to preserve oral health, and addressing modifiable risk behaviors, such as smoking cessation, where relevant. These findings may inform the development of tailored oral health behavior management strategies by prioritizing capability enhancement as an initial intervention target while integrating supportive opportunities and motivational strategies into routine periodontal care. The findings may also inform oral health education and the development of healthcare policies and services that support long-term behavior management for patients with periodontitis. Future research should focus on developing and evaluating tailored behavior management approaches to address patients’ evolving barriers and support needs.
