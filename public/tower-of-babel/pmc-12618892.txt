The evolving landscape of antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) for treatment of prostate cancer
Authors: Claire Lin, Matthew J. Hadfield, Ariana Santopietro, Galina Lagos, Liang Cheng, Wafik S. El-Deiry, Rahul Aggarwal, Anthony Mega, Elias Hyams, Benedito A. Carneiro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Advances in antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) technology and identification of new antigen targets are repositioning ADCs as a promising therapeutic platform for metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC). This review summarizes recent progress in ADC payload and linker chemistry, highlights membrane antigens in prostate cancer that have demonstrated encouraging antitumor activity in clinical trials, and addresses strategies to mitigate off-target toxicity. These advances position ADCs to transform the therapeutic landscape of mCRPC.

Introduction

Prostate cancer remains a significant health problem worldwide, with nearly 60% of men over the age of 65 diagnosed with prostate cancer1. In the United States, it is the most common cancer among men; 1 in 8 will be diagnosed with prostate cancer in their lifetime1. Prognosis is poor for patients diagnosed with advanced disease: metastatic prostate cancer has a 5-year survival rate of 34.1%, and current therapies only offer temporary control of disease without curative ability2. Moreover, relapse occurs in 20-50% of patients treated for early-stage disease3. Advanced tumors often become castration-resistant and refractory to conventional androgen-deprivation therapy (ADT)4. Recent incorporation of PARP inhibitors and radioligands such as Lutetium-177 has improved the outcomes of castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC). However, CRPC remains a lethal disease, urging the development of novel therapeutic strategies to provide durable disease control and improve survival in patients. Advances in the development of antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) have translated into improved clinical outcomes for patients with multiple tumor types and fostered interest in ADCs as a therapeutic platform for prostate cancer.

ADCs are a novel class of biotherapeutics linking the specificity of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) with the cytotoxicity of small molecule payloads5. These antibodies target cell surface antigens overexpressed primarily on cancer cells, connected to payloads via linkers designed with varying degrees of stability in the systemic circulation, to release the payload in response to intracellular triggers or antibody degradation at the target site5,6. By using mAbs to enhance tumor cell selection, ADCs were designed to improve the therapeutic index of cytotoxic agents6,7. However, clinical experience has shown that linker instability and side effects from systemic exposure to ADC payloads remain a significant challenge, with both off-target and on-target toxicities frequently resembling those of the unconjugated cytotoxic agents6,7. Regardless, ADCs have continued to be explored, improved upon, and approved for clinical use, heralding a new era of precision-targeted treatment modalities6.

ADCs first showed clinical activity in hematological malignancies, given their well-described tumor-specific cell membrane antigens readily assessable for circulating antibodies8. In 2000, gemtuzumab ozogamicin became the first ADC to receive regulatory approval for treating acute myeloid leukemia8. Later, improvements in conjugation technology helped increase the stability and efficacy of ADCs, leading to the development of ADCs with better therapeutic indices and expanded indications. In 2013, trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1) became the first ADC approved for solid tumors8. Thirteen FDA-approved ADCs are currently on the market, with over 200 ADCs in various stages of clinical development (Table 1)6,8,9. Within the past six years alone, four ADCs have been approved for urogenital malignancies: enfortumab vedotin for urothelial carcinoma, tisotumab vedotin for cervical cancer, and mirvetuximab soravtansine for ovarian and fallopian tube cancers8. ADC cytotoxic payloads

Important considerations when selecting payloads include: (1) adequate potency, to account for limited tumor-specific antigens and antibody-antigen internalization; (2) adequate stability, since ADCs can have a long half-life in the circulation; (3) functional group modifiability for conjugation to the antibody; (4) ability to induce bystander killing effect to kill tumors with heterogenous antigen expression; (5) and water solubility for payload release in the proper physiological conditions10. The most commonly used mechanisms of cytotoxicity in ADC payloads include microtubule disruption, topoisomerase inactivation, and DNA alkylation11.

Microtubule-disrupting agents: Auristatin, Maytansinoid, and Tubulysin

Disruption of microtubule-facilitated movement and segregation of chromosomes during mitosis induces mitotic arrest and apoptosis in rapidly dividing cancer cells10,12. Taxanes (docetaxel, cabazitaxel), which enhance microtubule polymerization and prevent depolymerization, are the most active class of chemotherapy agents used in mCRPC13. Taxanes have also been reported in preclinical models of prostate cancer to inhibit nuclear translocation of the androgen receptor (AR), thereby decreasing the pro-tumorigenic transcriptional activity of AR14,15. However, nearly all tumors eventually develop resistance to taxanes, mediated by mutations in tubulin, decreased microtubule bundling, and development of drug efflux pumps (e.g. P-glycoprotein)16,17. Several features of microtubule-carrying ADCs may be able to overcome taxane resistance: for example, ADCs employ payloads with different mechanisms and binding sites from conventional taxanes, overcoming resistance caused by tubulin mutations or isotype switching18.

Microtubule-disrupting payloads (auristatins, maytansinoids, and tubulysins) approved in various ADCs have therapeutic potential in prostate cancer. Auristatins, such as monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE), monomethyl auristatin F (MMAF), and Duostatin-5, inhibit tubulin polymerization; approved ADCs with an MMAE payload include brentuximab vedotin, polatuzumab vedotin, enfortumab vedotin, disitamab vedotin, tisotumab vedotin, and telisotuzumab vedotin10,19. Maytansinoids (DM1, DM4), which also inhibit tubulin polymerization, are payloads found in trastuzumab emtansine (DM1) and mirvetuximab soravtansine (DM4)20,21. Tubulysin is another anti-microtubule peptide that inhibits tubulin polymerization, triggering G2/M cell cycle arrest and apoptosis10. While these microtubule-disrupting agents are highly efficacious at killing cancer cells when delivered via ADCs, their clinical use is limited by adverse effects such as neurotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, and cardiotoxicity10. MMAE-based ADCs in particular are known to cause peripheral neuropathy, an important side effect in patients previously treated with chemotherapy agents such as platinum and taxanes that also cause peripheral neuropathy22. Strategies to manage peripheral neuropathy include close monitoring, dose adjustments and interruptions, use of medications such as duloxetine, acetyl-L-carnitine, amifostin, and mecobalamin to alleviate symptoms, and non-medical interventions such as cryotherapy, compression therapy, and nerve blocker injections23. The systemic toxicity of microtubule-disrupting agents are likely due to non-specific uptake by normal cells and premature delivery, so advances in conjugation technology to prevent non-specific endocytosis and payload release may help overcome these challenges24.

Topoisomerase I inhibitors: camptothecin (CPT) derivatives

Topoisomerase I (TOPI) inhibitors such as camptothecin (CPT) derivatives have been investigated as cytotoxic payloads in ADCs25,26. CPT binds to and inactivates the TOPI-DNA complex, promoting SSB formation and inducing apoptosis27,28. Inhibition of TOPI by CPT also suppresses HIF-1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α protein accumulation, downregulating transcription of HIF-1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α-regulated genes involved in angiogenesis, glucose metabolism, immortalization, immune evasion, invasion and metastasis, drug resistance, and cancer stem cell (CSC) maintenance and proliferation29,30. CPT derivatives irinotecan (CPT-11) and topotecan have improved solubility compared to CPT, while exatecan mesylate (DX-8951) and deruxtecan (DXd) have enhanced TOPI inhibition31. The therapeutic success of trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd), whose 8:1 drug-to-antibody ratio delivers high concentrations of deruxtecan to target breast cancer cells, has validated the clinical feasibility and efficacy of TOPI inhibitor-based ADCs21,32.

Approximately 20-25% of mCRPC cases display molecular alterations causing homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), such as BRCA1/2 or ATM mutations33. Tumors with HRD rely on alternative DNA repair mechanisms for survival, so agents that inhibit DNA single-strand break (SSB) repair or induce more SSBs trigger genomic instability and apoptosis34. PARP inhibitors (PARPi; e.g., olaparib, talazoparib, rucaparib) that block SSB repair are currently approved for mCRPC patients with HRD33,35. TOPI inhibitor-induced SSBs require tyrosyl-DNA phosphodiesterase 1 (TDP1)-mediated excision repair and homologous recombination pathways for repair, so mCRPC tumors harboring HRD may exhibit increased sensitivity to TOPI inhibitor-carrying ADCs36,37. As the understanding of resistance mechanisms to TOPI inhibitors emerges (i.e., TOPI mutations and loss of Schlafen 11 (SLFN11), these agents could be deployed to a molecularly selected group of patients as monotherapy or in combinations with PARP inhibitors38,39. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential and the complexity of resistance mechanisms associated with TOPI inhibitor ADCs, underscoring the importance of biomarkers to determine sensitivity.

Other synergistic therapeutic strategies may involve combining TOPI inhibitor-based ADCs with androgen receptor inhibitors (ARi) or HIF-1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α inhibitors. ARi resistance is frequently mediated by upregulation of DNA repair pathways and alternative survival mechanisms40. Inhibition of topoisomerases can thus disrupt AR-driven transcription and impair DNA repair, potentially overcoming resistance to AR-targeted therapies41,42. For example, studies have shown that topoisomerase II inhibitors can suppress AR signaling and sensitize CRPC cells to the ARi enzalutamide, supporting the concept of co-targeting DNA topoisomerase and androgen receptor pathways to enhance antitumor efficacy41,42. TOPI inhibitor-based ADCs may also demonstrate synergy with HIF-1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α inhibitors, given that TOPI is an upstream upregulator of HIF-1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α: inhibiting both TOPI and HIF-1α to decrease expression of HIF-1α target genes may reduce prostate tumor progression, increase sensitization to therapy, as well as modulate the tumor microenvironment by reducing immunosuppressive myeloid cells and increasing cytotoxic T cell infiltration43,44. Preclinical studies in hepatocellular carcinoma cells have also demonstrated that high HIF-1α levels could confer resistance to TOP1 inhibitors, but that pharmacologic HIF-1α inhibition could restore sensitivity and produce synergistic cytotoxicity, increased apoptosis, and impaired DNA repair when combined with TOP1 inhibitors45.

DNA alkylating agents: pyrrolobenzodiazepine (PBD) dimers and duocarmycin (DCM)

DNA alkylation distorts the DNA helix and promotes insertion of incorrect nucleotides, impairing DNA repair, replication, transcription, and ultimately inducing apoptosis46. This mechanism underlies the cytotoxic effects of two ADC payloads: pyrrolobenzodiazepine (PBD) dimers and duocarmycin (DCM). PBD dimers bind to the minor groove of DNA, where covalent aminal cross-linking between DNA guanine and PBD forms an adduct that stalls replication forks and arrests tumor cells at the G2/M phase47. Additionally, PBD-DNA adducts minimally distort DNA, allowing for evasion of DNA damage repair processes and decreasing the risk of emergent drug resistance48. DCMs also bind to the minor groove of DNA; however, they alkylate the adenine in DNA, resulting in DNA strand breaks and ultimately apoptosis49,50. Cancer cells with a hypoxic, chemoresistant, and stemness phenotype have shown sensitivity to DCM, and many analogs are active against multiple drug-resistant cancer cell lines49,50. PBD and DCM also have a wide spectrum of activity: they can induce apoptosis in both dividing and non-dividing cells, unlike other payloads that kill predominantly proliferating cancer cells50. Loncastuximab tesirine, which carries the PBD SG3199, is currently the only approved ADC with a DNA alkylating payload and it is utilized for the treatment of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma21. However, despite promising preclinical antitumor activity, the clinical development of many PBD- and DCM-based ADCs has been constrained by safety and efficacy challenges, leading to over 20 discontinued PBD-based ADCs and no approved DCM-based ADCs to date50,51. Therefore, while these novel DNA alkylating ADC payloads may offer advantages over classic DNA alkylating agents like cyclophosphamide and estramustine used in salvage regimens, their therapeutic index and tolerability require careful optimization and further clinical validation52,53.

ADC linker technology

Linkers are engineered chemical moieties that attach the cytotoxic payload to the antibody, with their design critically influencing both the stability of the ADC in systemic circulation and the efficiency and specificity of payload release at the target site6,54. There are two types of linkers, non-cleavable and cleavable, with different mechanisms of payload release. Non-cleavable thioether linkers are unaffected by differences in the environment, with the payload complex released upon internalization and complete proteolytic degradation of the antibody component within the lysosome of the target cell55. Non-cleavable linkers are specifically engineered to resist enzymatic, hydrolytic, and chemical cleavage under physiological conditions, lacking a designated weak point for protease or pH-mediated breakdown56. The goal of this design was to ensure that the cytotoxic payload remained conjugated to the antibody during systemic circulation, minimizing premature drug release and off-target toxicity57. However, full catabolism of ADCs with non-cleavable linkers structurally modifies the payload, which can negatively affect the activity of the payload6. Thus, only trastuzumab emtansine features a non-cleavable linker, with greater focused on the development of cleavable linkers.

In contrast, unstable cleavable linkers release unmodified payloads with unchanged cytotoxic activity6. Cleavable linkers consist of chemical (hydrazone, carbonate, silyl ether, disulfide, etc.) or enzyme (glucuronide or peptide) bonds that are cleaved when the ADC encounters a tumor environment different from the systemic circulation. Hydrazone, carbonate, and silyl ether linkers are pH-sensitive, hydrolyzed in the acidic pH of lysosomes and endosomes upon target cell internalization54. However, hydrazone (t1/2 = 24 h) and carbonate (t1/2 = 36 h) linkers both suffer from instability in the plasma, with hydrolysis also occurring at physiological pH and contributing to off-target toxicity due to premature drug release54,58,59. By contrast, silyl ether linkers have been engineered to remain more stable in the plasma (t1/2 > 7 days), providing more controlled, site-specific drug release than hydrazone or carbonate linkers59,60. Beyond pH-sensitive hydrolysis, chemical linkers also include disulfide linkers cleaved by reductive glutathione, which is found in higher concentrations in cancer cells than in the blood59,61. Enzyme-based linkers are susceptible to lysosomal proteases such as cathepsin B, cathepsin H, glycosidase, phosphatase, sulfatase, and beta-glucuronidase that are overexpressed in cancer cells. At the same time, protease inhibitors in the blood also help to prevent premature payload release62,63. Enzyme-based linkers are utilized most commonly in ADCs: 8 out of 13 FDA-approved ADCs use a protease-cleavable linker, including the ADCs enfortumab vedotin and trastuzumab deruxtecan that have transformed the standard of care for metastatic urothelial and breast carcinomas54. However, regardless of chemical or enzyme linker, ADCs with cleavable linkers face issues with either linker–drug instability, in which only the drug is released, or antibody–linker instability, in which the linker and drug are both released, over time in the systemic circulation before the ADC’s intended site of metabolism6. While instability may be contributing to off-target toxicity, linker stabilization needs to be carefully optimized, as unconjugated free drugs achieving pharmacologically relevant blood levels may be contributing to the antitumor effect, and shorter half-lives of ADCs may decrease exposure to and metabolism of ADCs in healthy tissues6,64,65.

Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA)

PSMA is a transmembrane protein found predominantly in prostate cells, and its expression increases as prostate malignancy progresses67,68. In particular, PSMA expression increases following ADT—the cornerstone of prostate cancer treatment—making PSMA a prominent target for the treatment of CRPC67. PSMA also has an internalization signal via its MXXXL cytoplasmic tail motif: upon binding, antibody-PSMA complexes undergo internalization from the cell surface into endosomal compartments via clathrin-coated pits, where the unique microenvironment facilitates the release of ADC payloads69.

PSMA-targeting ADCs have been in clinical development over the past two decades. MLN2704, an ADC targeting the extracellular domain of PSMA with the antimicrotubule agent DM1 as the payload, was the first PSMA-based ADC to complete a phase I clinical trial in 23 patients with mCRPC70. At a 4-week dosing schedule, MLN2704 was found to have dose-dependent pharmacokinetics and no measurable immunogenicity70. The only dose-limiting toxicity observed was a grade 3 febrile neutropenia in 1 patient; other mild to moderate adverse events (AEs) included fatigue, nausea, diarrhea, neuropathy, leukopenia, lymphopenia, and reversible elevations of transaminases70. MLN2704 demonstrated antitumor activity: of the 9 patients treated at the highest two doses, 2 (22%) had a > 50% decrease in prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and 1 (11%) had measurable regression in tumor size (NCT00052000)70. This was then followed by a phase I/II trial in which only 5 out of 62 patients with mCRPC (8%) had a decrease in PSA and PSA stabilization lasting ≥90 days following treatment, suggesting limited efficacy of MLN270471. Moreover, this trial found that disulfide linker lability and rapid deconjugation in MLN2704 led to significant neurotoxicity, with 44 (71%) patients experiencing dose-limiting peripheral neuropathy halting clinical development71. Thus, MLN2704 was ultimately discontinued71.

PSMA ADC, an ADC conjugating the antimicrotubule agent MMAE to a human IgG1 antibody targeting PSMA via a dipeptide linker, was the next PSMA-based ADC to enter clinical development. A phase I trial including 52 patients with mCRPC demonstrated a favorable toxicity profile and preliminary antitumor activity72. Two dose-limiting toxicities (neutropenia and reversible elevations of transaminases) occurred, and 16 (31%) patients reported peripheral neuropathy73. Durable reductions in PSA or circulating tumor cells (CTCs) were documented in 50% of patients72,73. The following phase II trial enrolled 119 patients with mCRPC (83 taxane-experienced and 36 chemotherapy-naïve; all patients had disease progression after abiraterone/enzalutamide) and showed that 19/113 (14%) patients had PSA declines ≥50%; 60/78 (78%) patients with measurable CTCs had CTC declines ≥50%74. Of the 31 patients with RECIST-measurable disease, 4 (13%) had partial response, 19 (61%) stable disease, and 8 (26%) progressive disease74. Despite the encouraging antitumor activity, the development of PSMA ADC was discontinued due to off-target toxicity from premature deconjugation, leading to significant rates of ≥ grade 3 neurotoxicity (24%), neutropenia (8%) and three patients died of sepsis-related death (2.6%)72–74.

Another PSMA-based ADC investigated for the treatment of mCRPC, MEDI3726, also had similar issues with delivering its pyrrolobenzodiazepine (tesirine, SG3249) payload to the tumor without off-target effects due to heavy-light chain dissociation in the antibody75. A phase I clinical trial investigated MEDI3726 among 33 patients with mCRPC after disease progression on abiraterone/enzalutamide and taxane therapy76. While MEDI3726 showed some antitumor efficacy at high doses, clinical responses were not durable, and AEs led to treatment discontinuation in 11 (33%) patients. AEs such as skin toxicity and effusions occurred in 30 (91%) patients76. MEDI3726 clinical development was thus halted.

The first-in-human phase I trial APEX-01 is investigating ARX517, a PSMA-targeting antibody conjugated to the auristatin amberstatin 269 (AS269) that inhibits microtubule polymerization. ARX517 uses oxime conjugation to attach the non-cleavable PEG linker carrying AS269 to site-specific synthetic amino acids in the antibody77. The oxime chemistry is stable at physiological pH but labile in acidic environments such as the lysosome after ADC internalization, reducing premature payload release77,78. A total of 24 patients with mCRPC were enrolled in the dose-escalation phase. Mild to moderate AEs included xerostomia (41.7%), fatigue (33.3%), diarrhea (20.8%), and thrombocytopenia (20.8%)79. At the highest dose tested (2.8 mg/kg), no dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) or treatment-related serious adverse events (SAEs ≥ grade 4) were reported, and ≥50% PSA and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) decline were observed79. RECIST responses were seen in 2/7 patients with measurable soft tissue disease79. Enrollment in the expansion cohort is ongoing (NCT04662580).

While many PSMA-targeted ADCs have entered the clinical pipeline—including SHR-4394, which carries an undisclosed payload and is currently recruiting patients for its first phase I clinical trial (NCT06783829)—many have failed to advance beyond phase I/II clinical trials due to modest PSA response rates, inadequate survival benefit, and significant toxicity80,81. However, PSMA remains an important target, especially with the successful development of PSMA positron emission tomography (PET) and the PSMA-targeting radioligand therapy lutetium-177 vipivotide tetraxetan (177Lu-PSMA-617)82,83. PSMA-PET, which has high sensitivity and specificity for detecting PSMA-expressing metastatic lesions in prostate cancer, is the standard for selecting patients for PSMA-targeted therapies such as 177Lu-PSMA-61784. 177Lu-PSMA-617 gained FDA approval in 2023 for patients with PSMA-positive mCRPC after progression on ARi and taxane-based chemotherapy, following the phase III VISION trial demonstrating that the combination of 177Lu-PSMA-617 with best standard-of-care (BSoC) therapy improved median overall survival and radiographic progression-free survival compared to BSoC alone (NCT03511664)83. While 177Lu-PSMA-617 currently demonstrates superior efficacy and a more favorable toxicity profile compared to PSMA-targeting ADCs, further development and refinement of PSMA-targeting ADCs could lead to improved efficacy and establish them as an important component of the PSMA-targeted therapeutic landscape80.

B7H3

Another promising target for ADCs in prostate cancer is B7H3, a costimulatory molecule commonly found on the surface of prostate cells that is overexpressed in prostate tumor cells85. B7H3 has been shown to promote tumor metastasis and invasion via suppression of both CD8+ and CD4 + T cell proliferation and activity86,87. As a result, overexpression of B7H3 is associated with disease spread, including increased extracapsular extension and seminal vesicle invasion, as well as worse outcomes, including higher risk of metastatic recurrence or subsequent development of CRPC88. B7H3-based ADCs for the treatment of CRPC and other malignancies have been in clinical development89.

Vobramitamab duocarmazine (MGC018) is an ADC composed of a duocarmycin payload conjugated to an anti-B7H3 antibody via a cleavable linker. Phase I dose escalation in 29 patients with advanced solid tumors showed two-dose limiting toxicities: grade 4 neutropenia and grade 3 fatigue90. Results from the expansion cohort in mCRPC (n = 40) demonstrated that 4/7 patients had reductions in target lesions and 11/22 evaluable patients had ≥50% PSA reduction91. This study was terminated due to high rates of dose modifications and early treatment discontinuation (NCT03729596). A phase II study investigated MGC018 at a lower dose (2.0 and 2.7 mg/kg) and enrolled 182 patients with mCRPC (NCT05551117)92. Adverse effects included asthenia (40.7%), nausea (27.7%), fatigue (20.3%), decreased appetite (19.2%), anemia (17.5%), constipation (16.4%), and diarrhea (14.7%). The study was ultimately discontinued after five patients experienced fatal adverse events, including acute myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, pneumonitis, and pleural effusion92–94. Another phase I, dose-escalation, cohort-expansion study investigated MGC018 in combination with lorigerlimab (MGD019), an anti-PD-1 x CTLA-4 checkpoint inhibitor, in patients with advanced solid tumors including mCRPC, although it has since been paused (NCT05293496)91,93.

Ifinatamab deruxtecan (I-DXd, DS-7300) is another ADC with an anti-B7H3 antibody linked to the TOPI inhibitor DXd. Findings from a Phase I/II dose-escalation and -expansion study in patients with advanced solid tumors demonstrated manageable safety profile and encouraging antitumor activity95. Data from 73 heavily pre-treated mCRPC patients showed that 15/59 evaluable patients had a partial or complete response per RECIST, and the average duration of overall response (DOR) and progression-free survival (PFS) were 6.4 months and 4.8 months, respectively95. The most common side effects reported were nausea, fatigue, vomiting, and chills, and grade ≥3 treatment-related SAEs included anemia, neutropenia, dehydration, diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting96. I-DXd illustrates the potential of TOPI inhibitor payloads in the treatment of CRPC, in contrast to the limited activity of irinotecan, topotecan, and other camptothecins as chemotherapy agents in CRPC97–99. Enrollment in the trial for I-DXd in mCRPC is ongoing (NCT04145622).

YL201 contains a highly hydrophilic protease-cleavable linker-TOPI inhibitor payload (TMALIN®) with increased stability in the circulation and payload release in both the extracellular tumor environment and intracellular lysosomal compartment100. Preliminary data from a phase I trial evaluating YL201 in 312 patients with advanced solid tumors, including 2 with prostate cancer, demonstrated overall promising antitumor activity: across all the tumor types, YL201 had a 40.8% objective response rate and 83.6% disease control rate100. However, 97.1% of patients experienced TRAEs, most commonly hematological toxicities such as leukopenia (66.3%), anemia (64.7%), and neutropenia (61.5%); 36.2% of patients also experienced treatment interruptions while 5.4% discontinued treatment due to TRAEs (NCT05434234)100. Despite the frequency, TRAEs for YL201 were similar to those of other B7H3 ADCs, and YL201 had a manageable safety profile95,100,101. A phase I/II trial evaluating YL201 in patients with advanced solid tumors including mCRPC (NCT06057922), as well as a phase II trial in patients with mCRPC (NCT06241846), are both currently recruiting subjects100,102.

DB-1311/BNT324 is a B7H3-targeting ADC carrying a proprietary TOPI inhibitor payload that was granted fast-track designation by the FDA for mCRPC patients who have progressed on or after standard systemic regimens in 2024103. Preliminary data from a phase I/II study including 73 patients with pre-treated CRPC showed encouraging efficacy and a manageable safety profile104,105. Of the 52 patients with RECIST evaluable tumors, partial response was seen in 22 patients (objective response rate 42.3%) and stable disease was seen in 25 patients (disease control rate 90.4%), with a 6-month radiographic progression free survival rate of 67.7%104,105. Common TRAEs included gastrointestinal and hematologic toxicities; 31 patients (42.5%) experienced ≥grade 3 adverse events, and only 7 patients (9.6%) discontinued treatment due to toxicity104,105. Enrollment of post-177Lu-PSMA-617 CRPC and taxane-naïve CRPC patients is currently ongoing (NCT05914116).

Other B7H3-targeting ADCs are also in clinical development. HS-20093, a TOPI inhibitor (HS-9265)-carrying ADC, was shown to have an acceptable safety profile and promising antitumor activity in a phase I study in patients with advanced solid tumors (NCT05276609), and is currently undergoing a phase II trial in mCRPC patients (NCT06001255)106,107. MHB088C carries a TOPI inhibitor payload, SuperTopoi™, with 5 to 10 times greater potency than DXd; a phase I/II trial in 36 patients with heavily-pretreated mCRPC demonstrated a 14.3% objective response rate, a 95.2% disease control rate, and improvements in PSA levels, as well as a manageable safety profile with cytopenias as the most common ≥ grade 3 TRAE (CTR20231298)101. MGC026, which carries the TOPI inhibitor/exatecan payload SYNtecan E™, was shown to have potent antitumor activity in B7H3+ patient-derived xenograft models of prostate cancer, and is currently undergoing a phase I clinical trial in patients with advanced solid tumors, including CRPC (NCT06242470)108,109.

STEAP1

STEAP1 (six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of the prostate 1) is another promising target for ADCs in prostate cancer. STEAP1 is a member of the STEAP1-4 family of metalloreductases, which are highly expressed in PCa tumors; STEAP1 overexpression has been detected in 90% of prostate cancers110. Higher STEAP1 expression is associated with increased tumor proliferation, progression, invasiveness, risk of relapse, and Gleason score110. One possible mechanism for this oncogenic overexpression is STEAP1’s function as an intercellular communication channel for small molecules that promote stromal cell recruitment and proliferation, enhancing tumor survival and growth. STEAP1 upregulation has been seen in both PCa primary tumor cells and stroma cells in the tumor microenvironment111–114. Notably, STEAP1 expression is sustained in even PSMA-negative prostate tumors and metastatic sites, providing an opportune target for patients with mCRPC.

STEAP1 forms homotrimers and STEAP1/2 heterotrimers on the plasma membrane surface of prostate cancer cells115. STEAP2 has previously been established as a therapeutic target antigen abundantly expressed in all stages of prostate cancer and directly correlated with the Gleason score. AZD0754, a STEAP2 CAR-T cell therapeutic agent, has demonstrated potent and specific cytotoxicity against STEAP2-expressing prostate cancer cells, validating STEAP2 as a therapeutic target in mCRPC116. Recent studies of AMG 509, a STEAP1-directed T cell engager molecule under development for the treatment of prostate cancer, have also shown that targeting STEAP1 homodimers and STEAP1/2 heterotrimers leads to T cell-dependent cellular cytotoxicity115.

Vandortuzumab vedotin (DSTP3086S, MSTP2109A, RG7450) is a humanized anti-STEAP1 IgG1 linked to MMAE that was evaluated in a phase I trial with 77 mCRPC patients117. Dose-limiting toxicities included grade 3 transaminitis in 2 patients and grade 3 hyperglycemia and grade 4 hypophosphatemia in one patient, and common AEs, including fatigue, peripheral neuropathy, nausea, constipation, anorexia, diarrhea, and vomiting, occurred in >20% of participants117. Early clinical efficacy was also observed: 11/62 (18%) patients who received doses >2 mg/kg had a ≥ 50% decline in PSA, and 2/36 (6%) of evaluable patients achieved radiographic partial responses. 16/27 (59%) of patients with unfavorable CTCs (≥5, a predictor of adverse prognosis) also saw conversion to favorable CTCs (<5, a predictor of favorable prognosis), suggesting prolonged disease stability117. While DSTP3086S displayed evidence of antitumor activity with an acceptable safety profile, further optimization of DSTP3086S is necessary before any further clinical study. However, despite its discontinuation, DSTP3086S did highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting STEAP1 with ADC to treat mCRPC117.

Three other STEAP1-targeting ADCs are under clinical investigation: ADRX-0405, ABBV-969, and DXC-008. Preclinical studies of ADRX-0405, which carries a TOPI inhibitor payload, have demonstrated good tolerability, potent bystander activity, and robust antitumor efficacy in prostate tumors with both high and low levels of target expression, and a phase I clinical trial investigating ADRX-0405 in patients with mCRPC and other advanced solid tumors is ongoing (NCT06710379)118,119. ABBV-969 and DXC-008 are dual-targeting PSMAxSTEAP1 ADCs carrying a TOPI inhibitor or tubulysin B analog payload, respectively; both are also currently undergoing phase I clinical trials in patients with prostate cancer (NCT06318273 and NCT06926283, respectively)120–122.

TROP2

Trop2 (trophoblast antigen 2), a membrane-bound glycoprotein, has also been investigated as a therapeutic target for ADCs in prostate cancer. Trop2 promotes prostate cancer growth, metastasis, and treatment resistance, and its expression is associated with inferior outcomes123–125. Trop2 may also play a role in the neuroendocrine differentiation phenotype, a well-characterized mechanism for ADT-resistance in prostate tumors123,124.

Sacituzumab govitecan (SG, IMMU-132) is an ADC linking a humanized anti-Trop-2 antibody (hRS7) to an SN-38 payload approved for the treatment of triple-negative breast cancer. Building upon preliminary activity from the phase I trial in CRPC, a phase II study is investigating SG in mCRPC (NCT03725761)126. Preliminary results from 20 participants showed no PSA50 responses127. Another phase I trial investigated the combination of SG and an ATR (ataxia telangiectasia and Rad-3 related) inhibitor berzosertib in 12 patients with DNA damage response (DDR) gene-mutated or high-replication stress solid tumors; no dose-limiting toxicities were observed, and 2 patients with neuroendocrine prostate cancer showed partial responses to the drug combination128.

ESG401 is another ADC composed of an anti-TROP2 antibody conjugated to the TOPI inhibitor SN-38. Preliminary results from a phase I clinical trial in patients with pre-treated solid tumors (including prostate cancer) demonstrated a favorable safety profile and promising antitumor activity129. ESG401 was found to have improved systemic stability and a prolonged half-life compared to SG, resulting in better tolerability: ESG401 had 11% less rates of grade ≥3 neutropenia than SG, with most cases of neutropenia unperceivable and easily manageable129. ESG401 also had an overall response rate of 34.2%, a disease control rate of 65.8%, and a clinical benefit rate of 50%129. Enrollment in the trial is still ongoing (NCT04892342).

Datopotamab deruxtecan (Dato-DXd) is another anti-Trop-2 ADC consisting of the payload DXd, a highly potent TOPI inhibitor. Selective delivery of Dato-DXd is enhanced by the plasma-stable linker cleaved by tumor cell-enriched lysosomal enzymes130. In the TROPION-PanTumor01 and TROPION-PanTumor02 phase I clinical trials, Dato-DXd demonstrated meaningful clinical activity and a manageable safety profile in advanced/metastatic HR + /HER2– and triple-negative breast cancers, urothelial carcinoma, and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (NCT03401385, NCR05460273)131–133. While common AEs included nausea and stomatitis, Dato-DXd had an improved benefit-risk profile compared to SG, with lower incidences of neutropenia and diarrhea131,134. Results from the Phase III TROPION-Breast01 and TROPION-Lung01trials demonstrated greater progression-free survival on Dato-DXd compared to standard-of-care therapy, leading to the FDA approval of Dato-DXd in metastatic HR + /HER2– breast cancer and EGFR + NSCLC135,136. The ongoing TROPION-PanTumor03 Phase II clinical trial is assessing the safety, tolerability, and antitumor activity of Dato-DXd in patients with various advanced/metastatic solid tumors, including mCRPC137. No results have been published yet (NCT05489211).

MHB036C is a TROP2-targeted ADC with an undisclosed TOPI inhibitor payload currently being investigated in a phase I/II trial of patients with advanced solid tumors, including mCRPC (NCT06373406)138. This builds upon preclinical data demonstrating superior potency of MHB036C in comparison to other anti-TROP2 ADCs such as DS-1062a currently being explored for NSCLC, as well as preliminary data from a first-in-human phase I/II trial in 26 patients with locally advanced or metastatic solid tumors showing a 33% partial response rate and manageable safety profile (CTR20231246)139.

CD46

CD46 is a cell-surface antigen highly expressed in advanced prostate cancer140. FOR46 is an ADC composed of an anti-CD46 antibody conjugated to an MMAE payload. A recently completed phase I study demonstrated that FOR46 has an acceptable safety profile at clinically active doses in patients with mCRPC. Among the 33 patients enrolled in the dose-escalation phase, 9 patients achieved PSA50 decline, 1 out of 8 patients with measurable disease had a partial response, and 6 patients had stable disease lasting up to 39 weeks141. The dose-limiting toxicity was grade 4 neutropenia141. Ten patients were subsequently enrolled in the dose-expansion at RP2D (2.7 mg/kg) with improved safety profile: neutropenia (77%, Gr 3/4), infusion reactions (37%, all < Gr 2), fatigue (31%, all < Gr 2) and peripheral neuropathy (24%, all < Gr 2)142. The summary of both cohorts showed that 14 out of 31 (45%) patients with evaluable PSA achieved a PSA50 response with a median duration of >16 weeks; and of the 18 patients with measurable tumor lesions, 8 (44%) had tumor regression, with a median duration of >14 weeks142.

Preclinical models have shown that enzalutamide-induced androgen receptor blockade increases CD46 expression and has an additive effect in combination with FOR46, supporting the ongoing trial evaluating FOR46 in combination with enzalutamide143. Dose-limiting toxicities include Gr 4 neutropenia and hyponatremia and Gr 3 elevated transaminases, and the most common AEs are fatigue (64.7%), peripheral sensory neuropathy (47.1%), elevated transaminases (17.6%), alopecia (23.5%), decreased appetite (41.2%), neutropenia (17.6%), and weight loss (17.6%)143. Decline in PSA levels was observed in 13/16 evaluable patients, and stable disease for ≥6 months was seen in 41.2% of patients143. Moreover, 89Zr-DFO-YS5-PET imaging demonstrated tumor-specific uptake of FOR46. Enrollment for this study is ongoing (NCT05011188).

Tissue factor (TF)

Tissue factor (TF) is a transmembrane protein aberrantly expressed in tumor cells and the tumor vasculature in many solid tumors, including cervical, bladder, and prostate cancer144. Acting as the main initiator of the extrinsic coagulation pathway, TF forms a complex with activated factor VII (FVIIa) to induce clot formation, contributing to tumor-associated thrombosis145. Additionally, TF triggers intracellular signaling cascades via protease-activated receptor 2 (PAR-2), promoting the production of proangiogenic factors, cytokines, and adhesion molecules, thereby fueling tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis144. Experimental models have demonstrated that interference with TF expression or activity reduces tumor outgrowth and metastatic potential, while clinically, overexpression of TF has been found to be associated with disease progression and poor prognosis in solid tumors146,147. Enrichment of TF in prostate cancer has supported the development of ADCs in CRPC148.

JEWEL-101 is a first-in-human phase I study investigating XB002, a TF-targeting ADC carrying an auristatin-based payload with a protease-cleavable linker, in patients with advanced solid tumors148. The auristatin-based payload, zovodotin, has lower off-target payload deconjugation and improved tolerability compared to other auristatins such as MMAE. Preliminary results from the dose-escalation stage have demonstrated that XB002 is well-tolerated, with only low-grade ocular toxicity and no bleeding events or treatment-related peripheral neuropathy149. No efficacy results have been published, and cohort expansion was planned to continue in patients with mCRPC (NCT04925284)148. However, XB002 was discontinued due to the inability to compete against the existing TF-targeting ADC tisotumab vedotin150.

Tisotumab vedotin, which carries an MMAE payload, is FDA-approved as a second-line treatment for recurrent or metastatic cervical cancer, with potential indication in prostate cancer as well. A phase I/II and phase II trial of tisotumab vedotin in patients with solid tumors, including CRPC has been completed, but no CRPC-specific data have been reported (NCT03245736 and NCT02001623)151,152. However, across all tumor types, 15.6% of patients achieved an objective response, and the safety profile was manageable with the most common TRAEs being epistaxis (69%), fatigue (56%), nausea (52%), alopecia (44%), and conjunctivitis (43%)153.

Delta-like ligand 3 (DLL3)

The expression of delta-like ligand 3 (DLL3), a notch inhibitory ligand expressed on neuroendocrine carcinomas (NEC), correlates with aggressive clinical features and poor prognosis154,155. DLL3 has limited expression in benign prostate tissue and localized prostate cancer but is expressed in CRPC with neuroendocrine features and de novo neuroendocrine prostate cancer (NEPC)155.

Rovalpituzumab tesirine (Rova-T), a DLL3-targeting ADC linked to PBD payload, demonstrated antitumor activity in NECs including NEPC156. A phase I/II study enrolled 101 patients with NECs, of which 21 were NEPC patients. Of the 69 NEC patients ultimately treated with the phase II dose, 9 had partial responses with a duration of response of 3.1 months; however, specific data for NEPC was not available. Serious adverse events at a dose of 0.3 mg/kg included pleural and pericardial effusions, photosensitivity reaction, dyspnea, tumor lysis syndrome, rhabdomyolysis, and kidney injury (NCT02709889)156. Following two phase III trials in small cell lung cancer (SCLC) patients, MERU and TAHOE, development of Rova-T was ultimately discontinued due to toxicity, limited efficacy and lack of survival benefit compared to standard-of-care therapies157.

Despite issues with safety and efficacy halting development of Rova-T, DLL3 still remains a relevant anticancer target in prostate cancer. A recent phase Ib study of tarlatamab, a bispecific T-cell engager targeting DLL3 and CD3, in 40 NEPC patients demonstrated a manageable safety profile with promising antitumor activity, including partial responses in 22.2% of DLL3 + NEPC patients158. This highlights the validity of DLL3 as a therapeutic target in NEPC (NCT04702737). DLL3-targeted ADCs currently under investigation for other indications may also have potential in prostate cancer, including ZL-1310, which has shown a favorable safety profile with significant antitumor activity and high response rate in patients with extensive stage SCLC (NCT06179069)159.

SLC44A4 (CTL4)

SLC44A4 (soluble carrier family 4 member 4 or CTL4) is a bicarbonate transporter involved in regulating the acidic tumor microenvironment, with increased expression in epithelial cancers and low expression in normal tissue160. It is upregulated in 75% of primary prostate tumors, and higher SLC44A4 expression correlates with higher Gleason score161. SLC44A4 was also identified as a driver of ADT resistance in CRPC162.

ASG-5ME is an ADC consisting of an anti-SLC44A4 antibody conjugated to MMAE via a protease-cleavable valine-citrulline linker161. A phase I trial investigated ASG-5ME in 46 patients with mCRPC. Of the 40 patients with measurable PSA, 11(25%) had a PSA decline >50%163. Among 16 patients with RECIST evaluable tumors, 2 (12%) had a partial response, 7 (41%) had stable disease, and 7 (41%) had progressive disease163. Ten patients (22%) terminated the study early due to AEs. Common AEs included fatigue (46%), diarrhea (24%), cardiotoxicity, ophthalmic toxicity, neurotoxicity, renal toxicity, and metabolic dysregulation163. Two treatment-related deaths occurred: one due to ileus, severe hypotension, and renal failure, and the other due to sepsis post-hospitalization for hyperglycemia163. Due to the significant toxicity and limited antitumor activity ASG-5ME development was discontinued.

HER2

Overexpression of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), a transmembrane protein, is an oncogenic driver in many tumors, such as breast cancer164. Moreover, HER2 is enriched in quiescent prostate cancer cells, which contribute to tumor progression, metastasis, and relapse165. While no prostate cancer-specific clinical data are available for HER2-targeting ADCs, HER2-targeting ADCs have been extensively studied in breast cancer. Trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1) was approved first as standard second-line therapy for HER2+ metastatic breast cancer, followed by the approval of trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) for both HER2+ and HER2-low breast cancers with significantly improved progression-free survival and overall response rates compared to T-DM1 or standard chemotherapy166–168. T-DXd has a high drug-to-antibody ratio and membrane-permeable payload, allowing a bystander effect even in tumors with heterogenous or low HER2 expression169. This is especially relevant in prostate cancer, where low level HER2 overexpression has been associated with rapid tumor cell proliferation and poor prognosis170. A case report of a patient with HER2-expressing NEPC with rapid progression on seven different lines of therapy demonstrated substantial response on off-label treatment with T-DXd, including a 57% overall reduction in tumor volume across sites of metastases and improved performance status171.

A166 is another HER2-based ADC, containing the antimicrotubule Duostatin-5 payload conjugated via a stable protease-cleavable valine citrulline linker. A recently completed, first-in-human phase I/II study of A166 in 49 patients with relapsed/refractory cancers expressing HER2 antigen or amplified HER2 gene, including mCRPC, indicated safety and clinical efficacy172. Preliminary data from 35 patients in the dose-escalation phase demonstrated manageable toxicity, with common AEs being corneal epitheliopathy, keratitis, decreased appetite, dry eye, and blurry vision but no dose-limiting toxicities or drug-related deaths172. Moreover, of the 27 evaluable patients, 9 had stable disease while 7 achieved partial response, resulting in a 59% disease control rate. However, no specific data for the mCRPC cohort has been published (NCT03602079)172.

HER3

Human epidermal growth factor receptor 3 (HER3) is a membrane-bound protein that activates downstream PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling to promote tumor growth, survival, and therapeutic resistance after dimerization with other ERBB family members (e.g., HER2)173,174. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that HER3 is frequently overexpressed in lethal prostate cancer, where it is associated with more aggressive disease, reduced time to castration resistance, and poor prognosis175. In the tumor microenvironment, secretion of NRG1 by stromal myelomonocytic cells can also activate HER3 and induce ADT resistance, further supporting the role of HER3 in disease progression175,176. Patritumab deruxtecan (HER3-DXd; U3-1402) and DB-1310 are two TOPI inhibitor-based anti-HER3 ADCs currently undergoing development for solid tumors, although data in prostate cancer patients is currently lacking177. Following demonstration of potent and sustained antitumor activity against a HER3-expressing CRPC patient-derived xenograft model, a phase II trial for HER3-DXd in relapsed/refractory locally advanced/metastatic solid tumors is currently enrolling prostate cancer patients (NCT06172478)175,177. An ongoing phase I/II trial of DB-1310 in advanced/metastatic solid tumors is also enrolling patients with prostate cancer (NCT05785741), after DB-1310 was found to exhibit significant cytotoxicity and tumor growth inhibition, synergy with the EGFR inhibitor osimertinib, and the ability to overcome osimertinib in prostate cancer patient-derived xenograft models178,179.

Nectin-4

Nectin-4 is a transmembrane cell adhesion molecule that is minimally expressed in normal adult tissues but is overexpressed in several malignancies, including subsets of prostate cancer such as prostate ductal adenocarcinoma180. However, while canine models of metastatic prostate cancer highly express Nectin-4, Nectin-4 expression in human mCRPC is generally low and heterogenous, with only a minority of metastatic lesions (notably in lung and peritoneum) showing moderate membranous expression181,182.

Enfortumab vedotin (EV) is an Nectin-4-targeting ADC carrying a MMAE payload that has been approved in combination with pembrolizumab in patients with locally advanced or metastatic urothelial carcinoma, and is now undergoing investigation in prostate cancer183. Preliminary data from a phase II study of EV in 11 heavily-treated refractory mCRPC patients demonstrated an acceptable safety profile and promising efficacy with a 64% overall response rate: no grade 5 TRAEs were reported, 5/11 patients achieved PSA decline ≥ 50%, 3/4 patients had an objective response, 4/7 had declines in CTCs, and 2/11 had stable disease for ≥6 months184. Enrollment in the trial is ongoing (NCT04754191)184.

LY4101174 and LY4052031 are two next-generation Nectin-4-targeting ADCs that have demonstrated robust antitumor activity across a range of Nectin-4 expression levels and in a Nectin-4-MMAE ADC-resistant tumor model in the preclinical setting185,186. LY4101174, which carries an exatecan payload, is currently undergoing a phase I trial in patients with advanced/metastatic solid tumors, including prostate cancer (NCT06238479)186. A phase I study investigating LY4052031, which carries the camptothecin payload LSN3889710, in patients with advanced/metastatic solid tumors expressing Nectin-4 also recruited patients with prostate cancer, but was suspended before any data had been published (NCT 06465069)185.

5T4

Trophoblast glycoprotein (TPBG; 5T4) is a cell surface oncofetal antigen that is highly expressed in prostate cancer but rarely found in normal adult tissues187,188. 5T4 expression is associated with increased prostate tumor progression, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and invasion and metastasis via potentiation of CXCL12/CXCR4 chemotaxis and favoring of the non-canonical Wnt pathway189. Moreover, 5T4 is very efficiently endocytosed, making it an ideal target for ADCs189. TUB-030 is a 5T4-targeting ADC carrying an exatecan payload that has demonstrated sub-nanomolar affinity to 5T4, efficient bystander activity against target-negative cells, high stability in plasma, and long-lasting antitumor activity in preclinical cancer models; TUB-030 is currently undergoing a phase I/II clinical trial in patients with advanced solid tumors, including prostate cancer (NCT06657222)190,191.

KAAG1

Kidney associated antigen 1 (KAAG1) is a cell surface antigen highly and selectively expressed in prostate cancer192. KAAG1 is also rapidly internalized and trafficked by lysosomes upon antibody binding, making it an attractive target for ADCs192. ADCT-901 is an anti-KAAG1 ADC carrying a PBD dimer payload, SG3199, that completed a phase I trial in patients with advanced solid tumors, including prostate cancer; however, no definitive clinical data is available yet (NCT04972981)192.

ASCT2

Expression of alanine–serine–cysteine transporter-2 (ASCT2; SLC1A5) is increased in primary and recurrent prostate cancer compared to normal tissue193. Inhibition of ASCT2-mediated glutamine uptake and metabolism in prostate tumor cells has also been implicated in the reduction of cell cycle progression, cell proliferation, and metastasis, highlighting ASCT2 as a potential therapeutic target in prostate cancer193. MEDI7247 is an anti-ASCT2 ADC carrying a PBD payload that has completed a phase I trial in patients with various solid tumors, including mCRPC; however, no definitive clinical data are available yet (NCT03811652)194.

LYPD3

LYPD3 (C4.4a) is a cell surface protein with limited expression on normal tissue but high expression in tumor cells, including in prostate cancer195. LYPD3 overexpression is correlated with a more malignant phenotype and poor prognosis, and has been implicated in tumor invasion and metastasis195. Moreover, LYPD3 is rapidly internalized, making it an attractive target for ADCs195. Lupartumab amadotin is a LYPD3-targeting ADC with an auristatin payload; however, a phase I clinical trial in patients with advanced solid tumors (including prostate cancer) has been terminated due to insufficient participant accrual (NCT02134197)196.

TAG-72

Tumor-associated glycoprotein 72 (TAG-72) is a cell surface mucin-like glycoprotein expressed in a high proportion of human prostatic adenocarcinomas, particularly those with a Gleason grade of six or higher197. Expression is predominantly localized to the cytoplasm and cell membrane of malignant cells, with minimal or absent staining in benign prostatic epithelium197. TGW101, an MMAE-based, TAG-72-targeting ADC is currently being explored in a phase I clinical trial in patients with advanced solid tumors, including CRPC198.

TM4SF1

TM4SF1 is a direct AR target gene that is overexpressed in prostate cancer tissues compared to benign prostatic hyperplasia199. TM4SF1 is highly expressed by both tumor cells and tumor-associated vascular endothelial cells, and has been implicated in cancer progression, angiogenesis, metastasis, and chemoresistance200,201. Delivery of TM4SF1-directed ADCs to both the vascular and cancer cell compartments of a tumor may be able to activate tumor immune surveillance, deprive the tumor of its blood supply, and directly kill tumor cells202. AGX101, whose anti-TM4SG1 antibody delivers its maytansinoid payload directly to the target cell nucleus, is currently undergoing a phase I trial in patients with advanced solid tumors, including prostate cancer (NCT06440005)202.

ROR1

Receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 1 (ROR1) is overexpressed in prostate cancer and associated with worse prognosis and more aggressive disease biology203. ROR1 mediates oncogenic signaling through the Wnt5a/ROR1 axis, activating pathways such as the PI3K/AKT, MAPK, NF-κB, STAT3, and Hippo pathways to promote cancer cell survival, proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and resistance to apoptosis204,205. Moreover, ROR1 has been shown to be reactivated in a subset of bone-metastatic prostate cancer cells, highlighting ROR1 as a potential therapeutic target in mCRPC206. CS5001 is a ROR1-targeting ADC carrying a PBD dimer payload currently undergoing a phase I clinical trial in patients with advanced solid tumors and lymphomas, including prostate cancer; preliminary results have shown encouraging antitumor activity and no dose-limiting toxicities so far (NCT05279300)207.

P-cadherin (CDH3)

Upregulation of P-cadherin (CDH3), a cell-to-cell adhesion molecule, has been observed and implicated in the development and progression of various solid tumors, including prostate cancer208. In particular, CDH3 expression can promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition and increase invasion and metastasis of tumor cells, whereas reduced metastasis burden has been seen with anti-CDH3 ADCs in mouse models of prostate cancer209,210. BC3195, which carries an MMAE payload, is the only CDH3-targeting ADC in clinical trials for patients with advanced solid malignancies (NCT05957471)211.

Prostate-specific cancer antigen (PSCA)

Prostate-specific cancer antigen (PSCA) is a cell surface protein frequently overexpressed in primary, metastatic, and castration-resistant prostate cancer, with limited expression in normal tissue212. PSCA expression level is correlated with tumor progression, higher Gleason score, and worse prognosis, and is particularly elevated with more advanced prostate cancers such as those with bone metastases or neuroendocrine differentiation213,214. PSCA-targeted modalities, including radiolabeled antibodies and CAR T cell therapy, have shown encouraging antitumor activity in both the preclinical and clinical settings, indicating the feasibility of PSCA as a target for ADCs215,216. While there have been no PSCA-targeting ADCs in clinical development to date, the first reported ADC based on a fully human anti-PSCA antibody has shown efficient internalization and antitumor efficacy with its MMAE payload in a xenograft mouse model of prostate cancer217.

Future perspectives

The clinical development of ADCs for the treatment of mCRPC has been challenged by off-tumor toxicity as well as limitations in payload delivery related to target antigen selection and the histological features of mCRPC5,218. In particular, the histological architecture of mCRPC—characterized by dense stroma, low vascularity, and high between-tumor heterogeneity—has limited ADC penetration and distribution within tumor tissue219–222. Future advancements in ADC development to improve safe and sufficient payload delivery and increase antitumor efficacy.

Improvements in linker, antibody, and payload technology, the utilization of precision medicine to identify markers of sensitivity and resistance in tumors, and combination therapy with radioligands all represent avenues for the future of ADCs.

While linker instability is a common characteristic of currently-approved ADCs and may actually contribute to antitumor activity and decreased normal tissue metabolism of ADCs, off-tumor toxicity from payload release in the systemic circulation has been a limiting factor in the development of prostate cancer ADCs, especially PSMA-based ADCs plagued with problems of peripheral neuropathy6,74. Thus, novel linker strategies have been under investigation. For example, linkers that release payloads independently of endogenous enzyme-mediated cleavage, such as those incorporating a click-to-release system upon administration of a subsequent exogenous chemical activator, have shown promising preclinical efficacy223. These ADCs can be applied to a greater breadth of non-internalizing tumor antigens and stroma proteins, expanding the number and specificity of ADC targets223,224. Extracellular cleavage of the ADCs may also result in more homogenous drug distribution throughout the tumor, enhancing the bystander effect and even potentially reaching cancer stem cells225. Moreover, temporally controlling drug delivery with a separate activator administered after the ADCs have arrived at the target site may reduce off-target toxicity223. Other strategies include tandem-cleavage linkers—where requiring two sequential enzymatic cleavage events for payload delivery has been shown to improve ADC stability and tolerability226–228.

Another obstacle to ADC approval in mCRPC remains in target selection, with heterogenous target antigen expression a main driver of ADC failure229. The most studied targets in mCRPC—PSMA, STEAP1, TROP2, CD46, and B7H3—are not exclusive to nor uniformly or highly expressed across all tumor cells, impairing ADC binding and internalization, reducing effective payload delivery, and increasing the risk of on-target, off-tumor toxicity5. Heterogenous and often low antigen density may be overcome with bispecific ADCs; for example, STEAP1 expression is often sustained in PSMA-low tumors, suggesting the utility of dual PSMAxSTEAP1-targeting ADCs118. Bispecific ADCs have increased selectivity and internalization, as well as may limit ADC binding to normal tissue, reducing on-target off-tumor toxicity5,230. For example, ABBV-969 is a novel PSMAxSTEAP1-directed bispecific ADC carrying a TOPI inhibitor payload with broader activity than ADCs targeting only PSMA or STEAP1 in preclinical mCRPC models, and is undergoing a phase I trial in mCRPC patients (NCT06318273)120. DXC-008, which carries a tubulysin B payload, is another PSMAxSTEAP1-directed ADC with potent dual-target antitumor activity in preclinical studies, currently undergoing a phase I trial in patients with prostate cancer and other solid tumors (NCT06926283)121,231. GenSci143 is a novel PSMAxB7H3-directed bispecific ADC carrying a TOPI inhibitor that has shown superior antitumor efficacy, pharmacokinetic control, and therapeutic index than single-target ADCs in in vivo models of mCRPC232. DXC014, another PSMAxB7H3-directed bispecific ADC carrying a TOPI inhibitor, has also demonstrated a similar favorable antitumor efficacy and safety profile in preclinical PCa models233.

ADCs with dual payloads represent another strategy to improve anti-tumor activity234. The combination of MMAE, with superior cell permeability and bystander effect, and MMAF, with less susceptibility to multidrug resistance (MDR) efflux pumps, may promote a synergistic cytotoxic effect and reduce the risk of drug resistance235. Combining payloads with different mechanisms of action may also overcome resistance: for example, preclinical studies of an ADC carrying both TOPI and tubulin inhibitor payloads have demonstrated potent antitumor activity in TOPI inhibitor-resistant tumors236. However, combining payloads may also increase the toxicity profile, and care must be taken to reconcile the effective and toxic doses of the drugs incorporated234. Two dual payload ADCs have entered clinical development: KH815 and IBI3020. KH815, a TROP2-directed ADC conjugated to both a TOPI inhibitor and RNA polymerase II inhibitor, simultaneously inhibits RNA synthesis and induces DNA double-stranded breaks (DSB)237. KH815 has been shown to produce a more potent antitumor effect than single TOPI inhibitor-TROP2-targeting ADCs as well as the ability to overcome drug resistance in preclinical cancer models, and is currently being investigated in a phase I clinical trial in patients with advanced solid tumors (NCT06885645)237. IBI3020, a CEACAM5-targeting ADC with an undisclosed dual payload, has also shown robust antitumor activity, notable bystander effect, and a manageable safety profile in preclinical tumor models, and has just entered a phase I clinical trial in patients with unresectable, locally advanced, or metastatic solid tumors (NCT06946446)238.

In recent years, ADC targets have also gradually expanded from antigens overexpressed on tumor cells to include antigens in the tumor microenvironment, such as components of the neovascular system, subendothelial extracellular matrix, and tumor matrix54,239. By reducing angiogenesis and the concentration of growth factors produced by the tumor matrix, ADCs targeting the tumor microenvironment may increase cancer cell apoptosis, broadening their efficacy in heterogenous tumors239. Moreover, non-cancerous matrix cells may have more stable genomes, reducing the risk of drug resistance towards ADCs240. Recent preclinical studies investigating huB12-MMAE, an ADC targeting cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAPs) implicated in promoting cancer growth and survival, angiogenesis, drug resistance, and immunosuppression, in a prostate cancer model have found that targeting and eliminating CAPs with huB12-MMAE induces both proinflammatory and antitumor immune responses241. PYX-201, an auristatin-carrying ADC targeting the extra-domain B splice variant of fibronectin (EDB + FN, an extracellular matrix protein abundantly expressed in the tumor microenvironment), is currently being evaluated in a phase I monotherapy trial (NCT06795412) and a phase I/II combination trial with pembrolizumab (NCT05720117) in patients with advanced solid tumors242. Preliminary data from the phase I trial demonstrated clinical responses with an acceptable tolerability profile in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), ovarian cancer, NSCLC, HR + /HER2- breast cancer, triple negative breast cancer, and sarcoma, and PYX-201 monotherapy was granted FDA fast-track designation for patients with metastatic or recurrent HNSCC243,244. Thus, ADCs that target stromal components may be particularly effective against cancers with high stromal-tumor ratios, such as prostate cancer11.

Another consideration for ADC use in patients is the sequencing of therapies, particularly in the context of ADCs that employ payloads with mechanisms of action shared by chemotherapeutics that are standard-of-care in treating prostate cancer245. Prior exposure to taxanes during treatment of mCRPC can induce resistance mechanisms, including upregulation of efflux pumps or mutations in tubulin, that may compromise the efficacy of microtubule inhibitor-based ADCs246,247. Thus, the timing of ADC administration, ideally before the emergence of resistance to analogous cytotoxics, may significantly impact therapeutic outcomes. Furthermore, the increasing availability of multiple ADCs within a single disease space also necessitates strategic sequencing based on payload diversity to mitigate cross-resistance and optimize efficacy245. For example, in patients with metastatic breast cancer, TOPI mutations have emerged as a mechanism of acquired resistance after treatment TOPI inhibitor-based ADCs, conferring resistance to a second ADC with a similar TOPI inhibitor payload39. On the other hand, switching to an ADC with a different payload class has been shown to overcome resistance and increase antitumor efficacy: in patients with HER2+ metastatic breast cancer, switching from a TOPI inhibitor-based ADC to a microtubule inhibitor-based ADC or vice versa yielded significantly higher objective response rates and progression-free survival than sequencing two ADCs with the same payload class248,249. Further investigation into expanding payload diversity to overcome resistance mechanisms is also warranted—especially since current ADC payloads primarily fall into three main classes while many other classes of cytotoxic agents still remain250. As novel payloads are explored for various cancers, future clinical trial designs should incorporate sequencing hypotheses and consider the utility of ADCs in earlier lines of therapy.

Factoring in tumor biomarkers when selecting ADC payloads may also play a role in enhancing therapeutic precision. For example, ADCs armed with TOPI inhibitors, which induce double-stranded DNA breaks that BRCA2-deficient cancer cells cannot effectively repair, may lead to enhanced cytotoxicity in prostate tumors with the BRCA2 mutation25,251. This rationale is supporting clinical trials investigating the safety and anti-cancer activity of T-DXd or Dato-DXd with the PARPi AZD5305 in patients with advanced solid malignancies, including prostate cancer (NCT 04644068)218. In B7H3–targeting ADCs armed with a PBD payload that disrupts replication forks, loss-of-function alterations in RB1 and TP53—which promote replication stress—have been identified as predictors of therapeutic response252. Other genomic and phenotypic markers positively predicting sensitivity to B7H3-PBD-ADCs include loss of/mutations in DNA repair proteins CHD1 and ATR, as well as expression of SLFN11, a DNA/RNA helicase that inhibits stressed replication fork repair252. Beyond assessment of HRD status and other molecular vulnerabilities, tumor profiling to assess for antigen expression should also be considered, especially since the efficacy of ADCs may be highly dependent on the presence and density of the antigen target on tumor cells, with heterogenous or low expression liming therapeutic benefit80. Thus, evaluation of tumor antigen target expression—by immunohistochemistry or molecular imaging—could be considered before patient selection5. For example, use of PSMA-PET, which is already standard for staging, restaging, and eligibility assessment for PSMA-targeted radioligand therapy, can be extended to PSMA-targeting ADCs253,254. Radiolabeled antibodies against STEAP1 in development can potentially guide patient selection for ADCs targeting STEAP1255. Ultimately, genomic and molecular profiling along with evaluation of tumor antigen expression when selecting patients for ADCs may optimize the precision and impact of ADCs in prostate cancer.

The role of radioligand therapies in mCRPC expanded with the approval of 177Lu-PSMA-617, which delivers beta-particle radiation to PSMA-expressing cells and the surrounding microenvironment256. Both radioligands and ADCs can deliver their cytotoxic effects via selective targeting of PSMA, with possible synergy if used in combination. Radiation-induced DNA double-strand breaks could sensitize tumors to ADCs with DNA-damaging payloads, while ADCs may potentiate death in radioresistant tumor cells and induce immune cell filtration of tumors257. Moreover, combination therapy with non-PSMA-targeting ADCs may be able to overcome resistance to 177Lu-PSMA-617. Disease progression on 177Lu-PSMA-617 is inevitable even in patients who are strongly PSMA-positive on imaging, with patients either having no response or progressing long-term despite having an excellent initial response258. One possible mechanism for this primary and acquired resistance may be the presence of PSMA-negative disease, which may proliferate and become the predominant clone after elimination of PSMA-expressing cancer cells with 177Lu-PSMA-617259. Thus, a therapeutic opportunity may lie in that these PSMA-negative clones may be sensitive to ADCs that target other antigens expressed on their cell surface, such as STEAP1. However, despite the potential for an increased antitumor response, there may also be increased risk of toxicity in healthy tissues with combination therapy257. Thus, maximizing the safety profile must be considered when designing combination strategies. Overall, the current challenges faced in ADC development and strategies to address them emphasize the importance of choosing the right combination of specific target antigen, active linker, and appropriate payload for optimal efficacy in the tumor environment.

Conclusion

As the field of oncology continues to advance towards personalized and targeted approaches, ADCs hold promise as a therapeutic strategy against prostate cancer. This manuscript reviewed some of the most relevant cytotoxic payloads, molecular targets, and ADCs that have demonstrated therapeutic potential in prostate cancer. These ADCs combine potent microtubule disruptors, topoisomerase inhibitors, and DNA alkylators to antibodies targeting antigens overexpressed on the surface of prostate cancer cells, including PSMA, B7H3, STEAP1, TROP2, CD46, TF, DLL3, SLC44A4, HER2, HER3, Nectin-4, 5T4, KAAG1, ASCT2, LYPD3, Tag-72, TM4SF1, CDH3, ROR1, and PSCA. However, due to issues with safety and specificity, no ADCs have yet been approved for the treatment of prostate cancer. Thus, further development and innovation are needed, both to refine current ADCs in the pipeline as well as develop novel ADCs with optimal targets, linkers, and/or payloads. As ADC technology continues to advance in the realm of prostate cancer, ADCs offer renewed hope for improved clinical outcomes and enhanced quality of life for patients battling this prevalent malignancy.
