Achievable Burning Densities of Growing Grids
Jordan Barrett, Karen Gunderson, JD Nir, Paweł Prałat

Abstract
Graph burning is a discrete-time process on graphs where vertices are sequentially activated and burning vertices cause their neighbours to burn over time. In this work, we focus on a dynamic setting in which the graph grows over time, and at each step we burn vertices in the growing grid G n = [ − f ⁡ ( n ) , f ⁡ ( n ) ] 2 G_{n}=[-f(n),f(n)]^{2} . We investigate the set of achievable burning densities for functions of the form f ⁡ ( n ) = ⌈ c ​ n α ⌉ f(n)=\lceil cn^{\alpha}\rceil , where α ≥ 1 \alpha\geq 1 and c > 0 c>0 . We show that for α = 1 \alpha=1 , the set of achievable densities is [ 1 / ( 2 ​ c 2 ) , 1 ] [1/(2c^{2}),1] , for 1 < α < 3 / 2 1<\alpha<3/2 , every density in [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] is achievable, and for α = 3 / 2 \alpha=3/2 , the set of achievable densities is [ 0 , ( 1 + 6 ​ c ) − 2 ] [0,(1+\sqrt{6}c)^{-2}] .

Introduction

Graph burning is a discrete-time process that models the spread of influence in a network. Vertices are in one of two states: either
burning
or
unburned
. In each round, a burning vertex causes all of its neighbours to become burning and a new
fire source
is chosen: a vertex whose state is changed to burning regardless of its neighbours and of its previous state. The updates repeat until all vertices are burning. The
burning number
of a graph
G
, denoted
b(G)
, is the minimum number of rounds required to burn all of the vertices of
G
.

Graph burning first appeared in print in a paper of Alon
[
1
]
, motivated by a question of Brandenburg and Scott at Intel, and was formulated as a transmission problem involving a set of processors. It was then independently studied by Bonato, Janssen, and Roshanbin
[
6
,
7
,
13
]
who, in addition to introducing the name
graph burning
, gave bounds and characterized the burning number for various graph classes. The problem has since received wide attention (e.g.
[
2
,
3
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
), with particular focus given to the so-called Burning Number Conjecture that every connected graph on
n
vertices requires at most
\lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil
rounds to burn.

The concept of burning density, first introduced by Bonato, Gunderson, and Shaw
[
5
]
, replaces a static network with a growing sequence of graphs. In this paradigm, there may not be a step in which every vertex is burning. Instead, we consider the proportion of burning vertices to the total number of vertices.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(G_{n},n\geq 1)
be a sequence of connected graphs with the property that
G_{n}
is an induced subgraph of
G_{n+1}
for all
n\geq 1
. Next, let
\mathcal{V}=(v_{n},n\geq 1)
be a sequence of vertices such that
v_{n}\in V(G_{n})\cup\{\emptyset\}
for all
n\geq 1
. We consider the following
burning process on
\mathcal{G}
with
activator sequence
\mathcal{V}
. Beginning with the null graph
G_{0}
and the empty set
B_{0}
of burning vertices, for
n\geq 1
,

1.

add vertices and edges to
G_{n-1}
to construct
G_{n}
,

2.

set
B_{n}
to be the union of
B_{n-1}
and each vertex in the neighbourhood of
B_{n-1}
in
G_{n}
, and finally

3.

if
v_{n}\neq\emptyset
, add
v_{n}
to
B_{n}
.

We think of this as a process in time where, at each time interval, the graph grows, the fire spreads, and a new vertex is burned; we often refer to the process at time
t
as
turn
t
. We do not insist on
v_{t}
being unburned when it is activated, nor do we insist that
v_{t}\notin\{v_{1},\dots,v_{t-1}\}
. Likewise, we allow an activator sequence to “pass” on turn
t
by setting
v_{t}=\emptyset
. The only requirement we insist on is that
v_{t}\neq\emptyset
for
at least one

t
. See Figure
1
for an example of a burning process.

For a graph sequence
\mathcal{G}
and activator sequence
\mathcal{V}
, define the
lower burning density
and the
upper burning density
of
\mathcal{V}
in
\mathcal{G}
, respectively, as

\underline{\delta}\left(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{V}\right):=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{|B_{n}|}{|V(G_{n})|}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\overline{\delta}\left(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{V}\right):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{|B_{n}|}{|V(G_{n})|}\,.

Trivially,
0\leq\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{V})\leq\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{V})\leq 1
. If
\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{V})=\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{V})
for a pair
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{V})
, then we call this value the
burning density
of
\mathcal{V}
in
\mathcal{G}
and denote it
\delta(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{V})
. Note that if an activator sequence
\mathcal{V}=(v_{n},n\geq 1)
has
v_{n}=\emptyset
for all
n
, then
\delta(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{V})=0
. To avoid this trivial case, we assume that activator sequences contain at least some vertices, and call such sequences non-empty. Finally, write
P(\mathcal{G})\subseteq[0,1]
for the set of obtainable burning densities in
\mathcal{G}
, defined as

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle:=\left\{\rho\in[0,1]:\text{there is a non-empty activator sequence }\mathcal{V}\text{ such that }\delta(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{V})=\rho\right\}.

In this paper we consider sequences
\mathcal{G}
of grid graphs on the integer lattice. We write
G=[a,b]\times[c,d]
when
G
is the grid graph with vertex set
[a,b]\times[c,d]
and with edges joining pairs of vertices at
L_{1}
-distance one from one another. We write
[a,b]^{2}
as shorthand for
[a,b]\times[a,b]
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
be a strictly increasing function, set
G_{n}=[-f(n),f(n)]^{2}
, and define
\mathcal{G}(f)=\big(G_{n},n\geq 1\big)
. Bonato, Gunderson, and Shaw proved:

Theorem

(
[
5
]
)
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
be a non-decreasing function.

(a)

If
f(n)=\lceil cn\rceil
for some

, then
P(\mathcal{G}(f))=[1/(2c^{2}),1]
.

(b)

If
f(n)=\lceil cn^{3/2}\rceil

, then there exists an activator sequence

such that
\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V})>0
.

(c)

If

, then

.

Our primary contribution is an extension of their result in which we determine the set of achievable burning densities in

for all functions of the form

\alpha\geq 1
and

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix positive real numbers

and
\alpha
and set

.

(a)

If
\alpha=1

, then

.

(b)

If

.

(c)

.

(d)

If

Note that Bonato, Gunderson, and Shaw proved case (

) and proved the strongest possible extension of case (

) of

, namely if

. Our contribution to Theorem

for a broader class of functions. In the cases (

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix positive real numbers
c
and

and suppose

is strictly increasing and satisfies
\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-\alpha}f(n)=c\,.

(a)

If
\alpha=1
and

, then
P(\mathcal{G}(f))=[1/(2c^{2}),1]
.

(c)

If
\alpha=3/2
, then

.

In the remaining case of
Theorem

1.1
, case (

), we prove a similar extension, though we require an additional assumption. We say a function

satisfies the

for every function

.

Let

and

We also provide examples (see

2
Preliminary results

We present a series of lemmas that, when combined, allow us to modify a burning process in quite substantial ways whilst maintaining the same lower and upper burning densities. Although not all of the results in this section are required to prove the three main theorems, we include them all as they offer a set of tools for analyzing burning processes.

For a burning process
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{V})
, write
B_{t}[i]
for the subset of
B_{t}
that would still be burned if only the
i^{\text{th}}
vertex in
\mathcal{V}
was activated during the process. Note that

B_{t}=\bigcup_{i\in[t]}B_{t}[i]\,,

and also note that
B_{t}[i]\cap B_{t}[j]
is not necessarily empty. Finally, note that
B_{t}[i]
is not necessarily the ball of radius
t-i
centred at
v_{i}
in
G_{t}
. For example (see Figure
1
), if
f(1)=f(2)=1
,
f(3)=3
, and vertex
(1,1)
is activated on turn 1, then
B_{1}[3]
is not the ball of radius of 2 centred at
(1,1)
. However, if
f
is strictly increasing, then
B_{t}[i]
is in fact the ball of radius
t-i
centred at
v_{i}
in
G_{t}
.

2.1
Key lemmas part 1: manipulating the activator sequence

In this first collection of three lemmas, we will show how an activator sequence can be delayed, trimmed at the front, and point-wise perturbed without altering the lower and upper burning densities. For these lemmas, we assume
f
has controlled growth.

Lemma 2.1
.

Fix
k\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{O}=(v_{n},n\geq 1)
be an activator sequence on
\mathcal{G}(f)
and let
\mathcal{D}=(u_{n},n\geq 1)
be a modified activator sequence with
u_{1}=\dots=u_{k}=\emptyset
and
u_{k+i}=v_{i}
for all
i\geq 1
. Then
\underline{\delta}\left(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{D}\right)=\underline{\delta}\left(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O}\right)
and
\overline{\delta}\left(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{D}\right)=\overline{\delta}\left(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O}\right)
.

For ease of readability, we refer to
(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O})
and
(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{D})
, respectively, as the
original
and
delayed
burning processes.

Proof.

Write
B^{\mathcal{O}}_{t}
and
B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t}
for the respective sets of burning vertices in the original and delayed processes after time
t
. Now let
v_{i}\in\mathcal{O}
and note that
v_{i}=u_{i+k}\in\mathcal{D}
. Thus,
v_{i}
is activated
k
rounds earlier in the original process than in the delayed process, meaning
v_{i}
burns no more in the delayed process than in the original one, i.e.,

B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t}[i+k]\subseteq B^{\mathcal{O}}_{t}[i]\,.

Moreover, since
B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t}[1]=\dots=B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t}[k]=\emptyset
we have that

B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t}=\bigcup_{i\in[n]}B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t}[i]\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in[n]}B^{\mathcal{O}}_{t}[i]=B^{\mathcal{O}}_{t}\,,

establishing that
\underline{\delta}\left(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{D}\right)\leq\underline{\delta}\left(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O}\right)
and
\overline{\delta}\left(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{D}\right)\leq\overline{\delta}\left(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O}\right)
.

For the other direction, we compare the burning sets
B^{\mathcal{O}}_{t}[i]
and
B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t+k}[i+k]
. If
t=i
, then
B^{\mathcal{O}}_{i}[i]=\{v_{i}\}
and
B^{\mathcal{D}}_{i+k}[i+k]=\{u_{i+k}\}
and these sets are equivalent. Now assume that
B^{\mathcal{O}}_{t}[i]\subseteq B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t+k}[i+k]
for some arbitrary
t\geq i
and consider the two burning processes at their respective next steps. For the original process,
B^{\mathcal{O}}_{t+1}[i]
is constructed from
B^{\mathcal{O}}_{t}[i]
by adding all adjacent vertices in
G_{t+1}
, then adding
v_{t+1}
. Similarly for the delayed process,
B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t+k+1}[i+k]
is constructed from
B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t+k}[i+k]
by adding all adjacent vertices in
G_{t+k+1}
, then adding
u_{t+k+1}=v_{t+1}
. Thus, since
G_{t+1}\subseteq G_{t+k+1}
and
B^{\mathcal{O}}_{t}[i]\subseteq B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t+k}[i+k]
, it follows that
B^{\mathcal{O}}_{t+1}[i]\subseteq B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t+k+1}[i+k]
. By induction, we have established that

B^{\mathcal{O}}_{t}[i]\subseteq B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t+k}[i+k]\,,

for all
t\geq i
. Therefore,

\frac{|B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t+k}|}{|V(G_{t})|}\geq\frac{|B^{\mathcal{O}}_{t}|}{|V(G_{t})|}\,.

Finally, as
f(n)
has controlled growth, for
t\to\infty
and
k
fixed we have that
|V(G_{t+k})|=(1+o(1))|V(G_{t})|
, implying that

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{|B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t+k}|}{|V(G_{t+k})|}=\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{|B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t+k}|}{|V(G_{t})|}\geq\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{|B^{\mathcal{O}}_{t}|}{|V(G_{t})|}\,,

and similarly for the limit superior. This inequality establishes that
\underline{\delta}\left(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{D}\right)\geq\underline{\delta}\left(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O}\right)
and
\overline{\delta}\left(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{D}\right)\geq\overline{\delta}\left(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O}\right)
, finishing the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Fix
k\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{O}=(v_{n},n\geq 1)
be an activator sequence on
\mathcal{G}(f)
with
v_{i}\neq\emptyset
for some
i>k
, and let
{\mathcal{T}}=(u_{n},n\geq 1)
be the modified activator sequence with
u_{1}=\dots=u_{k}=\emptyset
and
u_{i}=v_{i}
for all
i>k
. Then
\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),{\mathcal{T}})=\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O})
and
\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),{\mathcal{T}})=\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O})
.

For ease of readability, we refer to
(\mathcal{G}(f),{\mathcal{T}})
as the
trimmed
burning processes.

Proof.

Write
B^{\mathcal{O}}_{t}
and
B^{{\mathcal{T}}}_{t}
for the respective burning sets in the original and trimmed processes after time
t
. Note that
\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),{\mathcal{T}})\leq\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O})
and
\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),{\mathcal{T}})\leq\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O})
is immediate, as
B^{{\mathcal{T}}}_{t}\subseteq B^{\mathcal{O}}_{t}
for all
t\geq 1
.

For the other direction, let
\ell>k
satisfy
V(G_{k})\subseteq B^{{\mathcal{T}}}_{\ell}
. Such an
\ell
exists as there is some time
s\geq k
when
B^{{\mathcal{T}}}_{s}
is non-empty, and after time
s+2f(s)+1
all of
G_{s}
(and hence all of
G_{k}
) is burning. Let
\mathcal{D}
be the activator sequence that activates
v_{i}
on round
i+\ell
for all
i\geq 1
and activates nothing beforehand, i.e.,
(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{D})
is the delayed process in Lemma
2.1
with
k
replaced by
\ell
. Then, writing
B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t}
for the set of burning vertices in the delayed process after time
t
, we have that

\displaystyle B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t}[\ell+i]

\displaystyle\subseteq\bigcup_{j\in[\ell]}B^{{\mathcal{T}}}_{t}[j]\hskip 28.45274pt\text{for }1\leq i\leq k\text{, and}

\displaystyle B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t}[\ell+i]

\displaystyle\subseteq B^{{\mathcal{T}}}_{t}[i]\hskip 50.07669pt\text{for }i>k\,,

the first containment coming from the fact that
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in V(G_{k})
are already burning in the trimmed process before time
\ell+1
, and the second containment due to the fact that
v_{i}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
activator vertex in
{\mathcal{T}}
and the
(\ell+i)^{\text{th}}
activator vertex in
\mathcal{D}
. Therefore,
B^{\mathcal{D}}_{t}\subseteq B^{{\mathcal{T}}}_{t}
so
\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),{\mathcal{T}})\geq\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{D})
and
\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),{\mathcal{T}})\geq\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{D})
. Furthermore, by Lemma
2.1
we have that
\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{D})=\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O})
and
\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{D})=\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O})
. In combination, we get that
\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),{\mathcal{T}})\geq\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O})
and
\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),{\mathcal{T}})\geq\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O})
, finishing the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Fix
d\geq 1
and let
\mathcal{O}=(v_{n},n\geq 1)
and
\mathcal{P}=(u_{n},n\geq 1)
be activator sequences on
\mathcal{G}(f)
with the property that
\mathrm{dist}_{G_{n}}(v_{n},u_{n})\leq d
for all
n\geq 1
. Then
\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{P})=\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O})
and
\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{P})=\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O})
.

In this case, we call
(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{P})
the perturbed process.

Proof.

By symmetry, it suffices to prove that
\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O})\leq\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{P})
and
\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O})\leq\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{P})
. Let
\mathcal{D}
be the activator sequence that activates
v_{i}
at time
i+d
for all
i\geq 1
and activates nothing beforehand, i.e., the delayed process. Then any vertex in
\mathcal{D}
, at the time of activation, was already burning in the perturbed process by some vertex that (a) is at distance at most
d
, and (b) was activated
d
turns before. Therefore,
\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{D})\leq\underline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{P})
and
\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{D})\leq\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{P})
, and once again in combination with Lemma
2.1
this finishes the proof.
∎

In summary, Lemmas
2.1
,
2.2
, and
2.3
tell us that an activator sequence can be delayed, trimmed, and perturbed without altering the lower and upper burning densities.

2.2
Restricting our attention

Two of the three main objectives, Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
, involve functions that are
almost
strictly increasing. We take this moment to restrict our attention to functions that are, in fact, strictly increasing. The purpose is to ignore certain corner cases where
f
might not grow at every step, preventing burning sets to grow outwards in all four directions at a constant rate (e.g., Figure
1
). We provide a simple lemma justifying our restriction.

Lemma 2.4
.

Fix
n_{0}\geq 1
and let
f
be such that (a)
f(n)\geq n
for all
n\geq n_{0}
, and (b)
f(n+1)-f(n)>0
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Then there is a strictly increasing function
g
such that (a)
f(n)=g(n)
for all sufficiently large
n
, and (b)
P(\mathcal{G}(f))=P(\mathcal{G}(g))
.

Proof.

Let
\rho\in P(\mathcal{G}(f))
and let
\mathcal{O}
be an activator sequence for
\mathcal{G}(f)
such that
\delta(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V})=\rho
. Define the activator sequence
{\mathcal{T}}
by replacing the first
n_{0}
entries of
\mathcal{V}
with
\emptyset
. By Lemma
2.2
,
\delta(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{O})=\delta(\mathcal{G}(f),{\mathcal{T}})
. Then define
g
by
g(n)=n
for
n<n_{0}
and
g(n)=f(n)
for
n\geq n_{0}
. Because
\mathcal{G}(g)=\mathcal{G}(f)
on each round after
n_{0}
, the burning sets of
(\mathcal{G}(f),{\mathcal{T}})
are identical to those of
(\mathcal{G}(g),{\mathcal{T}})
so
\delta(\mathcal{G}(g),{\mathcal{T}})=\rho
. By a symmetric argument, if
\mathcal{O}
achieves
\delta(\mathcal{G}(g),\mathcal{O})=\rho
then there exists
{\mathcal{T}}
such that
\delta(\mathcal{G}(f),{\mathcal{T}})=\rho
.
∎

An immediate benefit of this restriction is seen in the next lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
f
be strictly increasing. For any activator sequence
(v_{n},n\geq 1)
on
\mathcal{G}(f)
,

(a)

B_{t}[i]
is the ball of radius
t-i
centred at
v_{i}
in
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
, and

(b)

|B_{t}|\leq\frac{2t^{3}+t}{3}
.

Proof.

Statement (a) follows immediately from the fact that if
f
is strictly increasing and
v_{i}\in G_{i}
then all points in
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
at distance
k
from
v_{i}
must be in
G_{i+k}
.

For statement (b), we have that
|B_{t}[i]|=(t-i+1)^{2}+(t-i)^{2}
if
v_{i}\neq\emptyset
, and
|B_{t}[i]|=0
otherwise. Hence,

\displaystyle|B_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}|B_{t}[i]|=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\Big((t-i+1)^{2}+(t-i)^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\Big(j^{2}+(j-1)^{2}\Big)=\sum_{j=1}^{t}j^{2}+\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}j^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{t(t+1)(2t+1)}{6}+\frac{(t-1)t(2t-1)}{6}

\displaystyle=\frac{2t^{3}+t}{3}\,,

which finishes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Note that Lemma
2.5
immediately implies Theorem
1.1
case (d), as the number of burning vertices is always
O(t^{3})
and the number of vertices in this case is
\omega(t^{3})
.

2.3
Key lemmas part 2: manipulating the growth function

The first collection of key lemmas were all for the goal of modifying activator sequences. We now turn to lemmas that allow us to modify growth functions. Thanks to Lemma
2.4
, we may assume growth functions are strictly increasing in the proofs of these lemmas.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
f,g
be increasing functions such that at least one of
f,g
has controlled growth and
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)=1
. Then

(a)

f
and
g
both have controlled growth, and

(b)

P(\mathcal{G}(f))=P(\mathcal{G}(g))
.

Proof.

Beginning with (a), since
f
and
g
are increasing they both satisfy condition (i) of the controlled growth requirement. Now suppose
f
has controlled growth and let
\epsilon:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
satisfy
\epsilon(n)=o(n)
. Then

\displaystyle g(n+\epsilon(n))

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))f(n+\epsilon(n))

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))f(n)

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))g(n)\,.

Therefore,
g
satisfies condition (ii) of the controlled growth requirement.

Now fix
c>0
and let
d>0
be such that for sufficiently large
n
,
f(n+cn)\geq(1+d)f(n)
. Then

\displaystyle g(n+cn)

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))f(n+cn)

\displaystyle\geq(1+o(1))(1+d)f(n)

\displaystyle=(1+d+o(1))g(n)\,.

Thus, for sufficiently large
n
,
g(n+cn)\geq(1+d/2)g(n)
, meaning
g
satisfies condition (iii) of the controlled growth requirement. Therefore, if
f
has controlled growth then so does
g
and, by a symmetrical argument, if
g
has controlled growth then so does
f
.

Continuing with (b), write
\mathcal{G}(f)=(G^{(f)}_{n},n\geq 1)
and
\mathcal{G}(g)=(G^{(g)}_{n},n\geq 1)
. Let
\rho\in P(\mathcal{G}(f))
and let
\mathcal{V}=(v_{n},n\geq 1)
be an activator sequence on
\mathcal{G}(f)
such that
\delta(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V})=\rho
. Define the activator sequence
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}=(v_{n}^{\prime},n\geq 1)
on
\mathcal{G}(g)
inductively as follows. First, if
v_{1}\in V(G^{(g)}_{1})
then set
v_{1}^{\prime}=v_{1}
, and otherwise set
v_{1}^{\prime}=v_{0}:=(0,0)
. Then, for
n\geq 1
and
k\geq 0
, if
v_{n}^{\prime}=v_{n-k}
, set
v_{n+1}^{\prime}=v_{n-k+1}
if
v_{n-k+1}\in V(G^{(g)}_{n+1})
, and otherwise set
v_{n+1}^{\prime}=\emptyset
. In words,
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
burns the same vertices as
\mathcal{V}
in the same order, except that
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
“waits” until it is able to burn the correct vertices in
\mathcal{G}(g)
. We claim that
\underline{\delta}\big(\mathcal{G}(g),\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\big)=\overline{\delta}\big(\mathcal{G}(g),\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\big)=\rho
.

Firstly, note that
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
is a well defined activator sequence on
\mathcal{G}(f)
as well as on
\mathcal{G}(g)
. Moreover, after any turn
t
, the number of vertices burned by
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{G}(f)
is at most the number of vertices burned by
\mathcal{V}
. Thus,
\overline{\delta}\big(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\big)\leq\delta\big(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V}\big)=\rho
. Next, since
|V(G^{(g)}_{n})|=(1+o(1))|V(G^{(f)}_{n})|
, and since the sets of burning vertices in
(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
and in
(\mathcal{G}(g),\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
are identical at all times, we get that

\overline{\delta}\big(\mathcal{G}(g),\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\big)=\overline{\delta}\big(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\big)\leq\delta\big(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V}\big)=\rho\,.

We are left to show that
\underline{\delta}\big(\mathcal{G}(g),\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\big)\geq\rho
. For each
n\geq 1
, define
\epsilon(n)
to be the smallest non-negative integer that satisfies
g(n+\epsilon(n))\geq f(n)
. We claim that
\epsilon(n)=o(n)
. Suppose, to the contrary, that there exists a constant
c>0
and an increasing sequence
(n_{i},i\geq 1)
such that
\epsilon(n_{i})\geq cn_{i}
for all
i\geq 1
. Then, for each
i\geq 1
, by the minimality of
\epsilon
we have
g(n_{i}+\epsilon(n_{i})-1)<f(n_{i})
. By condition (ii) of the controlled growth requirement, this inequality implies that
g(n_{i}+\epsilon(n_{i}))=(1+o(1))f(n_{i})
, implying further that

g(n_{i}+cn_{i})\leq g(n_{i}+\epsilon(n_{i}))=(1+o(1))f(n_{i})=(1+o(1))g(n_{i})\,.

However, by condition (iii) of the controlled growth requirement, we can find a constant
d>0
such that for sufficiently large
n
,
g(n+cn)\geq(1+d)g(n)
. Therefore, the assumption that
\epsilon(n)\neq o(n)
leads to a contradiction, and thus
\epsilon(n)=o(n)
.

Lastly, by the definition of
\epsilon
, the number of unique activator vertices in
(v_{i},i\in[n])\subseteq\mathcal{V}
that are not in
(v_{i}^{\prime},i\in[n])
is at most

\epsilon_{\max}(i):=\max_{i\in[n]}\epsilon(i)\,,

and thus the number of burning vertices in
(\mathcal{G}(g),\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
by the end of turn
t
is at least the number of burning vertices in
(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V})
by the end of turn
t-\epsilon_{\max}(t)=(1-o(1))t
. Letting
B_{t}
and
B_{t}^{\prime}
be the respective number of burning vertices in
(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V})
and
(\mathcal{G}(g),\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
by the end of turn
t
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{|B_{t}^{\prime}|}{|V(G^{(g)}_{t})|}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|B_{t-\epsilon_{\max}(t)}|}{|V(G^{(g)}_{t})|}

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\frac{|B_{t-\epsilon_{\max}(t)}|}{|V(G^{(f)}_{t})|}

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\frac{|B_{t-\epsilon_{\max}(t)}|}{|V(G^{(f)}_{t-\epsilon_{\max}(t)})|}\,,

the last equality holding since
f
satisfies condition (i) of the controlled growth requirement. Therefore,

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{|B_{t}^{\prime}|}{|V(G^{(g)}_{t})|}\geq\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{|B_{t}|}{|V(G^{(f)}_{t})|}\,,

implying
\underline{\delta}\big(\mathcal{G}(g),\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\big)\geq\delta\big(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V}\big)=\rho
.

Therefore,
\delta\big(\mathcal{G}(g),\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\big)
is well defined and equals
\rho
. By symmetry, we have shown that
P(\mathcal{G}(f))=P(\mathcal{G}(g))
, and this concludes the proof.
∎

The next lemma is our key to converting an activator sequence
\mathcal{V}
on
\mathcal{G}(f)
with
\delta(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V})=\rho
into a new activator sequence
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{G}(f)
with
\delta(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V}^{\prime})=c^{2}\cdot\rho
for any
c\in(0,1)
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
f,g:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
be strictly increasing functions such that
f\leq g
(point-wise) and
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)/g(n)=c
for some
c\in(0,1)
. Then
\rho\in P(\mathcal{G}(f))
implies
c^{2}\rho\in P(\mathcal{G}(g))
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be an activator sequence on
\mathcal{G}(f)
such that
\delta(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V})=\rho
and consider
\mathcal{V}
as an activator sequence on
\mathcal{G}(g)
. Then
\mathcal{V}
is well defined as
[-f(n),f(n)]^{2}\subseteq[-g(n),g(n)]^{2}
for all
n\geq 1
. Furthermore, as
f
and
g
are both strictly increasing, the set of burning vertices
B_{t}
by the end of turn
t
is identical in
(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V})
and in
(\mathcal{G}(g),\mathcal{V})
. Therefore, writing
\mathcal{G}(f)=(G^{(f)}_{n},n\geq 1)
and
\mathcal{G}(g)=(G^{(g)}_{n},n\geq 1)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{|B_{t}|}{|V(G^{(f)}_{t})|}

\displaystyle=\frac{|B_{t}|}{(2f(t)+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{|B_{t}|}{(2g(t)+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{|B_{t}|}{|V(G^{(g)}_{t})|}\,,

implying that

\delta(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V})=c^{2}\delta(\mathcal{G}(g),\mathcal{V})=c^{2}\rho\,,

and this concludes the proof.
∎

2.4
The need for controlled growth

We provide an example showing that at least
some
form of restriction on the growth of
f
is necessary for attaining burning densities on
\mathcal{G}(f)
.

Example 2.8
.

Let
g(n)=\lceil n^{4/3}\rceil
and define
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
as
f(1)=g(1)=1
and

f(n)=\begin{cases}g(n)&\text{if }n=2^{k}\text{ for some }k\in{\mathbb{N}}\text{, and}\\
f(n-1)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Fix
n
and let
k
be the largest integer such that
2^{k}\leq n
. Then

\lceil n^{4/3}\rceil=g(n)\geq f(n)=(2^{k})^{4/3}=\frac{1}{2^{4/3}}(2^{k+1})^{4/3}\geq\frac{1}{2^{4/3}}n^{4/3},

so
f(n)=\Theta\left(n^{4/3}\right)
and thus
f(n)=\omega(n)
and
f(n)=o(n^{3/2})
. However, for any activator sequence
\mathcal{V}
in
\mathcal{G}(f)
, there are infinitely many turns
t
with the property that

\frac{|B_{t}|}{|V(G_{t})|}\geq\frac{(2^{4/3})^{2}|B_{t}|}{|V(G_{t+1})|}+o(1)\geq\frac{(2^{4/3})^{2}|B_{t+1}|}{4|V(G_{t+1})|}+o(1)=\frac{2^{2/3}|B_{t+1}|}{|V(G_{t+1})|}+o(1)\,,

the second inequality coming from the fact that
|B_{t+1}|\leq 4|B_{t}|+1
, as every vertex burning by turn
t
can burn at most 4 other vertices in turn
t+1
. Therefore, either
\frac{|B_{t}|}{|V(G_{t})|}
diverges or converges to 0, and in either case we do not have
P(\mathcal{G}(f))=[0,1]
.

As
Example

2.8
demonstrates, without condition (ii) of the controlled growth requirement,
f
may have sudden “jumps” corresponding to
|B_{t}|/|V(G_{t})|
fluctuating infinitely often. However, we do not claim that the controlled growth requirement is necessary for Theorem
1.3
, Lemma
2.6
, or Lemma
2.7
. We discuss this topic further in the concluding section of the paper.

Proof of Theorem
1.3

We will make use of the following result of Mitsche, Prałat, and Roshanbin
[
10
]
regarding the burning number of fixed grid graphs. Note that this result has since been generalized to higher dimensions in
[
4
]
.

Theorem 3.1

(Mitsche, Prałat, Roshanbin,
[
10
]
)
.

b([m]\times[n])=\begin{cases}(1+o(1))\sqrt[3]{3mn/2},&n\geq m=\omega(\sqrt{n})\\
\Theta(\sqrt{n}),&m=O(\sqrt{n})\end{cases}

We begin the proof of
Theorem

1.3
by demonstrating how to achieve density 1.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
satisfy the controlled growth requirement and
f(n)=o(n^{3/2})
. Then there exists an activator sequence
\mathcal{V}
on
\mathcal{G}(f)
such that
\delta(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V})=1
.

Proof.

Regardless of which vertices were activated up to step
t
, Theorem
3.1
tells us we can burn all of the vertices of
G_{t}
in at most
\Theta(f(t)^{2/3})
steps. This fact motivates the following strategy.

1.

Write
(t_{i},i\geq 1)
for the sequence defined inductively as
t_{1}=1
and
t_{i+1}=t_{i}+\tau_{i}
where
\tau_{i}
is the length of time required to burn
G_{t_{i}}
as per Theorem
3.1
.

2.

For each
i\geq 1
, choose some activator sequence
\mathcal{V}_{i}=(v_{t},t_{i}<t\leq t_{i+1})
that burns all of
G_{t_{i}}
.

3.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be the concatenation of
(\mathcal{V}_{i}),i\geq 1
.

This
\mathcal{V}
gives

\frac{|B_{t_{i+1}}|}{|V(G_{t_{i+1}})|}\geq\frac{|V(G_{t_{i}})|}{|V(G_{t_{i+1}})|}=\frac{(2f(t_{i})+1)^{2}}{(2f(t_{i}+\tau_{i})+1)^{2}}=(1+o(1))\left(\frac{f(t_{i})}{f(t_{i}+\tau_{i})}\right)^{2}\,.

By Theorem
3.1
, combined with the fact that
f(n)=o(n^{3/2})
, we know that
\tau_{i}=\Theta(f(t_{i})^{2/3})=o(t_{i})
. Thus, as
f
satisfies condition (i) of the controlled growth requirement, we have that

\frac{|B_{t_{i+1}}|}{|V(G_{t_{i+1}})|}\geq(1+o(1))\left(\frac{f(t_{i})}{(1+o(1))f(t_{i})}\right)^{2}=(1+o(1)),

and so

\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{|B_{t_{i}}|}{|V(G_{t_{i}})|}=1\,.

This proves that
\mathcal{V}
contains a subsequence on which the burning density converges to
1
. Finally, for any
t_{i}<t<t_{i+1}
we have

\frac{|B_{t}|}{|V(G_{t})|}\geq\frac{|V(G_{t_{i-1}})|}{|V(G_{t_{i+1}})|}

and we also have

|V(G_{t_{i-1}})|=(2f(t_{i-1})+1)^{2}=(2(f(t_{i+1})-\tau_{i}-\tau_{i+1})+1)^{2}=(2f(t_{i+1})+1)^{2}-O(f(t_{i+1})^{5/3})\,,

meaning

\frac{|B_{t}|}{|V(G_{t})|}\geq(1-o(1))\frac{|V(G_{t_{i+1}})|}{|V(G_{t_{i+1}})|}\to 1\,,

and this completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

1.3
as a straightforward consequence of Lemmas
2.7
and
3.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
be a strictly increasing function with controlled growth such that

and

. Then

\mathcal{V}=(v_{1},n\geq 1)

and therefore

.

Now fix
\rho\in(0,1]
and let

g(n+1)>g(n)
for all sufficiently large

. For example, if
f(n+1)=f(n)+1
for infinitely many

, then
g(n+1)=g(n)
infinitely often. Thus, we define
g^{+}(n)
recursively as
g^{+}(0)=g(0)
and

,

2.7

, and this concludes the proof.

Lemma 3.3

.

(a)

there exists an activator sequence

4.1
Upper bound

We begin with the proof of Lemma
4.1

Proof of
Lemma

4.1
(b).

Let
\mathcal{V}=(v_{n},n\geq 1)
be an activator sequence on
\mathcal{G}(f)
, fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and let
t=(1+\varepsilon)n
. Define
B_{t}^{(\text{old})}\subseteq B_{t}
to be the set of vertices in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}B_{t}[i]
and define
B_{t}^{(\text{new})}\subseteq B_{t}
to be the set of vertices in
\bigcup_{i=n+1}^{t}B_{t}[i]
. Note that
B_{t}=B_{t}^{(\text{old})}\cup B_{t}^{(\text{new})}
and so
|B_{t}|\leq|B_{t}^{(\text{old})}|+|B_{t}^{(\text{new})}|
.

To bound
|B_{t}^{(\text{old})}|
, note that the centres of balls
B_{t}[1],\dots,B_{t}[n]
are all in
V(G_{n})
, meaning
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}B_{t}[i]
covers at most every vertex in
V(G_{n})\subseteq V(G_{t})
plus every vertex of distance at most
t-n=\varepsilon n
from
V(G_{n})
. Since the length of the perimeter of
G_{n}
is
\Theta(n^{3/2})
, we get that

|B_{t}^{(\text{old})}|\leq|V(G_{n})|+O(n^{5/2})=4c^{2}n^{3}+O(n^{5/2})\,.

For
|B_{t}^{(\text{new})}|
, we have the immediate bound

|B_{t}^{(\text{new})}|\leq\sum_{i=n+1}^{t}|B_{t}[i]|=\frac{2(t-n)^{3}+(t-n)}{3}=\frac{2(\varepsilon n)^{3}}{3}+O(n)\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle|B_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq\left(4c^{2}+\frac{2\varepsilon^{3}}{3}\right)\,n^{3}+O(n^{5/2})\,,

and so

\frac{|B_{t}|}{|V(G_{t})|}\leq f(c,\varepsilon)+O(n^{-1/2}),\qquad\text{with}\ f(c,\varepsilon)=\frac{4c^{2}+\frac{2\varepsilon^{3}}{3}}{4c^{2}(1+\varepsilon)^{3}}.

Since

f^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(c,\varepsilon)=\frac{-6c^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}{2c^{2}(1+\varepsilon)^{4}},

letting
\varepsilon=\sqrt{6}c
to minimize the upper bound for
|B_{t}|/|V(G_{t})|
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\overline{\delta}(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V})\leq\frac{4c^{2}+\frac{2\varepsilon^{3}}{3}}{4c^{2}(1+\varepsilon)^{3}}=\frac{1+\frac{2(\sqrt{6}c)^{3}}{12c^{2}}}{(1+\sqrt{6}c)^{3}}=\frac{1+\sqrt{6}c}{(1+\sqrt{6}c)^{3}}=\frac{1}{(1+\sqrt{6}c)^{2}},

and the proof is finished.

4.2
Lower bound

In this section we prove Lemma
4.1
(a) by describing the construction of an activator sequence
\mathcal{V}
in
\mathcal{G}(f)
such that
\delta(\mathcal{G}(f),\mathcal{V})=(1+\sqrt{6}c)^{-2}
. As suggested during the proof of Lemma
4.1
(b), our strategy ensures the balls surrounding newly activated vertices stay mostly disjoint while the balls surrounding older activated vertices, which must necessarily overlap, have burned almost all vertices that were added up to the point that these older vertices were activated.

At the heart of the strategy is an explicit description of how to burn a rectangle of a specified height and width using a set number of activation vertices. While describing this strategy, we temporarily describe activator vertices using real numbers which may not be integers. We then describe how to recursively apply this rectangle burning strategy to burn
\mathcal{G}(f)
. Finally, we analyze the burning density by estimating the contributions of the new and old activator vertices.

Let us justify using real numbers as activator vertices. For an increasing function
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
, consider the following burning process on the sequence of real compact spaces
(S_{n}(f),n\geq 1)
, where
S_{n}(f)=[-f(n),f(n)+1]^{2}
, and a sequence of activator points
((x_{n},y_{n}),n\geq 1)
where
(x_{n},y_{n})\in S_{n}(f)\cup\{\emptyset\}
. Note that, exclusively in this description, we are writing
[-f(n),f(n)+1]^{2}
as the real compact space and not the integer lattice. For each time
t
,

1.

the space grows from
S_{t-1}(f)
to
S_{t}(f)
,

2.

if every point in the unit square
[a,a+1]\times[b,b+1]
is burning then every point in the four squares
[a-1,a]\times[b,b+1]
,
[a+1,a+2]\times[b,b+1]
,
[a,a+1]\times[b-1,b]
, and
[a,a+1]\times[b+1,b+2]
changes its state to burning, and finally

3.

every point in the unit square
[x_{t},x_{t}+1]\times[y_{t},y_{t}+1]
changes its state to burning.

If a unit square is not fully in
S_{t}
, we simply do not burn any of the square. The burning density is defined as the limiting fraction of the space that is burning.

It is clear that, by choosing
((x_{n},y_{n}),n\geq 1)
as integer pairs, this burning process is identical to the burning process on the integer lattice. Moreover, similar to how we proved Lemma
2.1
, we can see that the burning density on
\mathcal{V}=((x_{n},y_{n}),n\geq 1)
is identical to the burning density on
\mathcal{V}^{*}=((\lfloor x_{n}\rfloor,\lfloor y_{n}\rfloor),n\geq 1)
: on the one hand we can delay
\mathcal{V}
by one turn and then everything burning due to
\mathcal{V}
is also burning due to
\mathcal{V}^{*}
, and on the other hand we can delay
\mathcal{V}^{*}
by one turn for the inverse effect. Thus, we are justified in choosing real coordinates instead of integer coordinates in our coming strategy.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
G=[0,w]\times[0,\ell]
, fix
t>0
, let
r=\sqrt{w\ell/2t}
, and let
S
be the set of points
(r\cdot i,r\cdot j)
such that
0\leq i\leq w/r
,
0\leq j\leq\ell/r
, and
i+j\equiv 1\mod 2
. Now suppose
\mathcal{V}
is an activator sequence for
G
that (a) activates all of
S
in an arbitrary order, (b) does not activate any vertex outside of
S
, and (c) ends on turn
\tau\geq t
. Then

(a)

on turns
n
with
1\leq n\leq r
, the burning balls are all disjoint, and

(b)

on turns
n
with
n\geq r+\tau
, there is a
1+O\left(\frac{r(w+\ell)}{w\ell}\right)
fraction of
V(G)
that is burning.

Proof.

Starting with (a), the minimum distance between any two vertices in
\mathcal{V}
is
2r
. Moreover, at the end of turn
\lfloor r\rfloor
, all of the balls have radius at most
\lfloor r\rfloor-1
. Thus, the balls are all disjoint.

Continuing with (b), after turn
\lceil r\rceil+\tau
all of the balls have radius at least
r
. For any
(x,y)\in[0,w-r]\times[0,\ell-r]
, let
i,j
be the unique non-negative integers satisfying
r\cdot i\leq x<r\cdot(i+1)
and
r\cdot j\leq y<r\cdot(j+1)
.

Case 1:

Suppose that
i+j\equiv 0\mod 2
. Then
(r\cdot i,r\cdot j)
and
(r\cdot(i+1),r\cdot(j+1))
are both activator vertices. Moreover, in the
L^{1}
norm,

||(x,y)-(r\cdot i,r\cdot j)||_{1}+||(x,y)-(r\cdot(i+1),r\cdot(j+1))||_{1}=2r,

meaning
(x,y)
is at most distance
r
away from one of the two activator vertices.

Case 2:

Suppose that
i+j\equiv 1\mod 2
. Then
(r\cdot i,r\cdot(j+1))
and
(r\cdot(i+1),r\cdot j)
are both activator vertices. Moreover, in the
L^{1}
norm,

||(x,y)-(r\cdot i,r\cdot(j+1))||_{1}+||(x,y)-(r\cdot(i+1),r\cdot j)||_{1}=2r,

meaning
(x,y)
is at most distance
r
away from one of the two activator vertices.

Therefore,
[0,w-r]\times[0,\ell-r]
is covered after time
r+\tau
, meaning at most
r\ell+rw+r^{2}
vertices are not covered. Since

\frac{r\ell+rw+r^{2}}{w\ell}=O\left(\frac{r(w+\ell)}{w\ell}\right)\,,

this completes the proof.
∎

and let

\displaystyle\ell_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{3c}{2}(t_{i+1}-t_{i}-1)t_{i}^{1/2}+O\left(\frac{(t_{i+1}-t_{i}-1)^{2}}{t_{i}^{1/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=6c\,i^{5}(1+O(1/i)),

\displaystyle\geq t_{i}+\left(\frac{w_{i}\ell_{i}}{2i^{3}}\right)^{1/2}-1

\displaystyle=t_{i}+(1+O(1/i))\left(\frac{\left(2c\,i^{6}\right)\left(6c\,i^{5}\right)}{2i^{3}}\right)^{1/2}-1

5
Further Directions

A natural extension of this work is to study the attainable densities on growing
d
-dimensional grids. To this end, we pose the following conjecture.

Conjecture 5.1
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
be a strictly increasing function, let
G_{n}=[-f(n),f(n)]^{d}
for some
d>2
, and let
\mathcal{G}(f)=(G_{n},n\geq 1)
.

(a)

If
f(n)=\lceil cn\rceil
for some
c\geq 1
then
P(\mathcal{G}(f))=[1/(2c^{d}),1]
.

(b)

If
f(n)=\omega(n)
and
f(n)=o(n^{(d+1)/d})
then
P(\mathcal{G}(f))=[0,1]
.

(c)

If
f(n)=\lceil cn^{(d+1)/d}\rceil
for some
c>0
then
P(\mathcal{G}(f))=[0,\phi(c,d)]
for some constant
\phi(c,d)
depending on
c
and
d
.

(d)

If
f(n)=\omega(n^{(d+1)/d})
then
P(\mathcal{G}(f))=\{0\}
.

In a recent work by Blanc and Contat
[
4
]
, they study random burning on the
d
-dimensional Euclidean lattice, and their results could give insights as to the correct function
\phi(c,d)
in case
(c)
. Moreover, their generalization of Theorem
3.1
from Mitsche, Prałat and Roshanbin
[
10
]
might allow for a generalization of our proof of Theorem
1.1
(b).

Another direction for future work is to find necessary and sufficient growth conditions for the function
f(n)
so that
P(\mathcal{G}(f))\neq\emptyset
, or so that
\rho\in P(\mathcal{G}(f))
for some
\rho\in(0,1)
. We showed via example that at least
some
restriction on the growth of

is necessary to obtain a burning density on
\mathcal{G}(f)
, though we do not claim that controlled growth is the weakest possible restriction.
