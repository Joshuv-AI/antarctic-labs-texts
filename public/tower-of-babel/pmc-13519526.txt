Shared Spectral Parameters Between EEG and Basal Ganglia LFP Distinguish Sleep‐Specific Oscillations in Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Lisa Hirt, Ruby Martini, Siqun Tang, Michael Summers, Lesile West, Clete Kushida, Stephen V. Gliske, Dulce Maroni, Younghoon Nho, Casey H. Halpern, Aviva Abosch, John A. Thompson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIn Parkinson's disease (PD), sleep‐related oscillatory dynamics between basal ganglia and cortical nodes may inform sleep dysfunction. The subthalamic nucleus (STN) is highly interconnected with human sleep circuitry and is a current target of interest for sleep neuromodulation.MethodsTo investigate correlated cortical–subcortical activity, we recorded simultaneous PSG and STN local field potentials (LFP) in 19 patients undergoing deep brain stimulation (DBS) for PD. We evaluated the correlation in spectral power between STN‐LFP and EEG during polysomnogram (PSG)‐defined stages of sleep. In addition, we analyzed aperiodic 1/f spectral parameters (exponent and offset), which have been identified as markers of arousal across sleep stages. Finally, we trained classifiers on aperiodic features to test whether 1/f structure alone could recover PSG‐defined sleep stages from EEG and STN‐LFP.ResultsResults revealed a significant interaction between recording modality and sleep stage. During wakefulness and REM sleep, STN‐LFP exhibited a significantly steeper spectral slope compared to scalp EEG. However, this dissociation diminished with sleep depth; slopes appeared more similar during N2 and reversed during N3, where EEG became steeper than LFP. Across subjects, the aperiodic component showed robust state dependence and modality differences with STN‐LFP maintaining higher exponents overall. STN‐LFP aperiodic features alone classified sleep stages above chance and outperformed scalp EEG, approaching the combined‐modality ceiling.ConclusionThese findings highlight distinct subcortical electrophysiological signatures in sleep‐dependent regulation of neural arousal across brain networks in PD. These insights offer potential biomarkers for both invasive and non‐invasive forms of closed‐loop neuromodulation strategies targeting sleep dysfunction.Trial Registration ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04620551

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor symptoms such as rigidity, tremor, and bradykinesia. However, non‐motor symptoms, including anosmia, neuropsychiatric and gastrointestinal disturbances, cognitive impairment, autonomic dysfunction, pain, and sleep disorders, often emerge up to a decade earlier and may be more disabling [1]. Sleep disorders such as insomnia, rapid‐eye‐movement sleep behavior disorder (RBD), excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS), restless legs syndrome (RLS), sleep‐related breathing disorders, circadian rhythm sleep–wake disorders, and excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) are highly prevalent among people with PD [2], affecting up to 98% of those with the diagnosis [3]. Individuals with PD typically exhibit reduced total sleep time, increased wake after sleep onset (WASO) and non‐REM stage N1 sleep, and decreased N2, REM sleep, and overall sleep efficiency [4, 5].

Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) of the subthalamic nucleus (STN) is a standard intervention for treating the motor symptoms of PD and may also improve sleep by increasing total sleep time [6, 7, 8, 9] and reducing WASO [6, 8, 10, 11], and generally results in improved sleep metrics (meta‐analysis [12, 13]), although the mechanisms remain unclear. While polysomnography (PSG) remains the clinical gold standard for sleep staging, on‐going efforts using intracranial electrophysiology in PD have sought to use STN local field potentials (LFP) to characterize sleep dysfunction within the pathophysiological basal ganglia nodes [14, 15, 16, 17, 18] for use in adaptative DBS control schemes [19, 20]. Emerging research suggests that STN‐LFP features may, in fact, predict sleep stages and could serve as targets for adaptive neuromodulation [21, 22, 23] aimed at improving sleep, yet rigorous comparisons between scalp EEG and intracranial LFPs remain limited and warrant further investigation.

Recent findings suggested that broad band spectral dynamics (i.e., scale free dynamics—1/f) reliably differentiate between wakefulness from those under general anesthesia, deep sleep, and REM sleep [24, 25, 26]. Notably, the spectral slope distinguishes REM sleep from wakefulness even though REM EEG appears desynchronized and resembles awake by conventional oscillatory metrics [26]. The physiological mechanisms underlying the oscillatory components of the 1/f features remain under investigation. A predominant view suggests that the slope reflects shifts in global network excitability or balance between inhibition and excitation (E/I): steeper (more negative) slopes are hypothesized to correspond to states of greater inhibition or reduced excitation, matching lower arousal states [25, 26, 27]. Because of this relationship, the aperiodic slope serves as a practical and temporally precise electrophysiological marker of arousal even when oscillatory markers can be ambiguous. Additionally, the differences in the spectral slope during sleep may identify the amount of arousal changes with temporal accuracy across a wide range of time series [26] due to the spectral slope representing the overall firing rate [27, 28]. More generally, the spectral slope has been proposed as a proxy for local E/I balance, with a more negative slope indicating increased inhibition [25].

Within the STN there is a mixture of excitatory and inhibitory synaptic terminals [29], which may affect the expression of an abnormal E/I ratio in PD that would be reflected through 1/f slope activity in the STN. Recent studies found that aperiodic exponent, the slope of the 1/f component, and broadband aperiodic power (in higher frequency ranges) tracked spiking activity and the inferred E/I balance: higher exponents corresponded to lower STN neuronal firing and more inhibition, while lower exponents corresponded to higher firing (hyperactivity) and disinhibition. Reduced dopaminergic tone in PD causes has been linked to an increase in excitation within the STN as well as a decrease in overall STN inhibition [30], it is not yet clear how this could affect the E/I ratio within the STN. In human PD patients, the exponent increased (i.e., steeper 1/f drop‐off) when patients had taken dopaminergic medication and transitioned to the ON state or following DBS, consistent with restored inhibition or reduced pathological over‐activity of STN [30]. These findings suggest that aperiodic metrics reflect physiologically meaningful changes in basal‐ganglia circuit excitability, and make the aperiodic exponent (or related broadband power) a promising candidate marker for adaptive DBS in PD.

A recent publication investigated STN LFPs in PD patients and found that there were specific 1/f LFP characteristics that were related to sleep fragmentation [18]. However, the relationship between 1/f activity in the STN and scalp EEG during sleep in PD needs to be explored. With respect to both sleep and PD, aperiodic measurements are key as indexing features of network excitability, E/I ratio and large‐scale spiking/background activity. In sleep, the 1/f slope becomes more negative in lower arousal states, consistent with increased inhibition or reduced excitation; in PD, aperiodic exponents of STN LFP move in expected directions with dopamine replacement medications or DBS and with STN firing, linking PD pathophysiology to altered E/I and overall activity. Thus, aperiodic parameters derived from STN LFP, such as E/I balance, may differentiate physiological (sleep/arousal) and pathological (PD) states. If so, these characteristics could be used to enhance therapeutic interventions for PD DBS. We sought to address this gap by simultaneously recording scalp EEG and STN‐LFP during overnight sleep in PD DBS patients, directly comparing cortical and subcortical electrophysiological dynamics across sleep stages. Initially we examined the contralateral–ipsilateral hemispheric electrophysiological dynamics between STN‐LFP and scalp EEG. Our results demonstrate that subject‐specific hemispheric biases became more consistently detectable when aggregating across contiguous stage blocks. Apart from hemispheric variances, our findings highlight that EEG–LFP coupling, spectral slope, aperiodic exponent, and aperiodic offset each vary systematically with sleep stage, yet diverge meaningfully between recording modalities. The largest cortical–subcortical dissociation consistently observed during wakefulness and REM, and a progressive convergence across NREM stages. We further tested whether aperiodic features alone, from EEG, STN‐LFP, or both, could classify PSG‐defined sleep stages using a leave‐one‐participant‐out framework. These results suggest that STN‐LFP captures sleep‐stage‐dependent changes in network excitability that are partially, but not fully, reflected in scalp EEG.

Methods

Patient Population

The study cohort consisted of 19 patients with Parkinson's disease enrolled at three clinical sites: 13 subjects from the University of Nebraska Medical Center (UNMC), two subjects from Stanford University (STAN), and four subjects from the University of Pennsylvania (UPENN). Data was collected in the context of a larger clinical trial, and all subjects who completed the in‐hospital polysomnography were included in the current study. Inclusion criteria included those who were approved for STN DBS surgery by the respective institutional patient care conference. All individuals were approved for DBS surgery based on standard clinical evaluation, including Movement Disorders Society‐Sponsored Revision of Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS‐UPDRS) testing on versus off PD medications, neuroimaging assessment, and formal neuropsychological testing by a certified neuropsychologist at each clinical site. Exclusion criteria included a diagnosis of a personality or mood disorder that would interfere with study requirements, a pre‐existing implanted stimulation system or prior intracranial surgery, history of or current drug or alcohol abuse, and/or sleep apnea requiring nightly use of a continuous positive airway pressure machine (CPAP). This study was approved by the UNMC Institutional Review Board, in accordance with the National Institute of Health single IRB policy. All participants underwent bilateral DBS implantation targeting the STN following standard clinical procedures. Baseline sleep disturbances were assessed via validated sleep surveys including Parkinson's Disease Sleep Scale (PDSS‐2), Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS), the REM Behavior Disorder 1 Question (RDB1Q), and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). Data Collection

Prior manuscripts related to subsets of these data overlap with some of the methods described below [31, 32]. Video PSG were collected at least 4 weeks (6.53 ± 2.41 weeks) after DBS quadripolar lead implantation to allow for resolution of surgery‐related STN edema, but prior to implantation of the neurostimulator (implanted programmable generator) and extension cables. In all participants we chose to externalize via sterile implantation of a percutaneous extension cable, the DBS lead contralateral to the most affected side of the body, which in 13/19 participants with staged lead surgeries, was also the brain hemisphere initially implanted in each subject with an STN lead and therefore the implantation site with the greatest interval for resolution of post‐operative edema since lead implantation surgery (6/19 received a single bilateral surgery). While implanting a single percutaneous cable limited recording from the STN in only 1 hemisphere, this approach was chosen to limit subject risks. After implantation of the percutaneous extension cable, patients were transferred, in the OFF‐medication state (final dose administered on the previous evening), to an inpatient neurosurgical monitoring unit. No complications resulted from the externalized lead procedure. A movement disorders trained neurologist then determined unilateral STN‐DBS stimulation parameters to optimally mitigate motor symptoms and avoid side effects in the off state, using an external stimulation source (Medtronic, model 37022, Minneapolis, Minnesota) connected to the percutaneous extension cable. Stimulation frequency varied between 130 and 185 Hz, and pulse width was set to either 60 or 90 μs, as determined by symptom amelioration and mitigation of side effects. Patients were then returned to routine, at‐home levels of PD medications during recovery, at least 6 h prior to the start of the first nocturnal PSG recording, and remained at their routine out‐patient doses throughout the recording sessions. Patients remained on necessary out‐of‐hospital medications (e.g., hypertensives). Patients were weaned off sleep medications (e.g., sedative hypnotics) at least 2 weeks prior, except a few who remained on stable levels of oral melatonin. Patients received intravenous antibiotics (e.g., vancomycin) for post‐operative prophylaxis and due to externalization of the percutaneous extension cable and were allowed hydrocodone and/or acetaminophen for pain control.

Subjects took their routine PD medications, documented by nursing staff, as per their home routine, for the duration of this in‐hospital investigation. Video PSG were recorded for all night sessions. For the purposes of this study, we only examined recording nights that did not include stimulation. Video PSG recordings included nine electroencephalogram (EEG) channels (F3, Fz, F4, C3, Cz, C4, O1, Oz, O2), bilateral mastoid contacts for later EEG re‐referencing (A1 and A2), two electrooculogram (EOG) channels (E1, E2), and three chin electromyogram (EMG) channels. Scalp electrodes were placed by EEG technologists in accordance with the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) standards [33]. All signals (LFP, EEG, EOG and EMG) were recorded using the Neuro Omega system equipped with EEG headboxes (sampled at 1375 Hz). Video recordings were obtained with an infrared camera connected through a laptop and synchronized to PSG recordings via a digital input signal. The motor MDS‐UPDRS (Part III) was assessed by a trained research staff member in the evening prior to and at the morning of each night of sleep, on medications but off stimulation. Levodopa equivalent doses ranged from 400 to 1200 mg across patients, with a median of 730 mg.

Sleep Stage Scoring

Sleep stage scoring was conducted independently by four experts (a registered sleep technologist and three sleep medicine physicians), using a custom sleep staging software created for the purpose of this study, and described in a previous publication [32]. Arousals were not scored as our focus was on sleep macro‐architecture. Consensus scoring for discordantly scored epochs was determined by two to four of the sleep experts after reviewing the four sets of scoring along with the corresponding PSG data, as described previously [32]. Classification agreement averaged 83% on initial scoring and reached 96.6% on secondary consensus scoring. The few epochs for which experts did not reach consensus after this review process were labeled “undecided” and these epochs were excluded from subsequent analyses.

Selection of Lead Recording Location for Local Field Potential Analysis

Neuroimaging datasets including a pre‐operative 3T brain MRI with a T1‐weighted 3D volumetric sequence and a post‐operative thin‐slice CT were fused and used to reconstruct and localize DBS electrodes within the borders of STN. Lead‐DBS (v3.0) was used for imaging transformations and visualization. Pre‐operative and post‐operative imaging datasets were normalized into MNI152Lin2009bAsym space based on the pre‐operative T1 sequence using the SyN registration approach implemented using Advanced Normalization Tools. Co‐registration and normalization were accomplished in five steps, consisting of: a two‐stage linear registration (rigid followed by affine), a non‐linear (whole brain) SyN‐registration stage, and two non‐linear SyN‐registrations that focused on a subcortical area of interest defined by subcortical masks. In addition, brain shift was corrected on post‐operative CT by applying a refined affine transform calculated between pre‐ and post‐operative acquisitions that were restricted to a subcortical area of interest as implemented in the brain‐shift‐correction module of Lead‐DBS. Using the post‐operative CT datasets, all individual DBS contacts were automatically localized using the PaCER algorithm [34]. The Middlebrooks DBS atlas was used to visualize and verify contact localization within the STN [35]. Visual confirmation of STN localization and monopolar assessment conducted by the movement disorders neurologist via the external stimulation source connected to the percutaneous extension cable were used to determine which bipolar contact pair to use for LFP analyses.

EEG Preprocessing and Spectral Analyses

EEG PSG data were organized into 30 s epochs corresponding to their sleep stage classification labels, separated by channel and night of recording for each subject. Our analysis focused on hemispheric derivations of channel configurations: Left sided scale electrodes (F3, C3, O1) referenced to A1 and Right sided (F4, C4, O2) were referenced to A2. For each epoch and channel, the active lead was re‐referenced to the mastoid reference from the same epoch (reference − signal) with respect to hemisphere. Bipolar referenced EEG data were downsampled to 100 Hz. For every re‐referenced epoch, we estimated the power spectral density (PSD) using a 1–50 Hz passband and a target frequency resolution of 2 Hz. The resulting frequency vector (Hz) was retained, and power was stored in decibels (dB). We computed a log–log spectral slope from 30 to 45 Hz. This frequency range was used based on prior work by [25, 26], demonstrated that this frequency range correlates best with changes in arousal across species (rodents, monkey, and humans). In addition, this frequency range avoids the predominant low oscillatory frequency bands in sleep EEG that could affect the linear fit. For the linear fit, spectra were obtained with a multitaper method (pmtm function in MATLAB, time–bandwidth product NW = 3, fs = 100 Hz). To separate aperiodic and periodic components, we applied the Spectral Parameterization method (specparam algorithm; formerly known as FOOOF [36]) to each epoch over the frequency range 1–50 Hz, using default settings (peak width min = 0.5, peak width max = 12, minimum peak height = 0, peak threshold = 2, and aperiodic mode = fixed). Inputs were row vectors of frequency (Hz) and linear‐scale power. Stored outputs included aperiodic parameters (offset, exponent), peak parameters for identified Gaussians (center frequency, amplitude, standard deviation), and model error (mean‐squared error).

LFP Preprocessing and Spectral Analyses

Similar to the EEG data, STN LFP data were organized into 30 s epochs. Each epoch contained simultaneously recorded raw voltage data from the four DBS contacts (0–3). Contacts were bipolar referenced resulting in three recording configurations (0–1, 1–2, and 2–3). The bipolar contact pair optimally localized to the dorso‐lateral posterior region of STN and corresponding to the stimulation configuration resulting in optimal clinical benefit for the patient's motor symptoms was selected for analysis (see Table 1 for participant contact configurations identified for analysis). LFP data were downsampled to 100 Hz to standardize sampling with EEG. PSD, spectral slope and spectral parameterization were calculated using the same approach as for EEG.

EEG and LFP Epoch Artifact Rejection

For each EEG and LFP bipolar recording, we computed the across‐epoch mean and standard deviation by frequency bin. Frequency‐wise thresholds were defined as mean ± 1.5 SD. Epochs were replaced with not‐a‐number (NaN) if > 25% of frequency bins exceeded the thresholds. For LFP, the process was repeated on the epochs that were not rejected in the first pass. In the second pass, the threshold was the mean ± 2 SD and any epoch with > 45% of bins outside the threshold limits was additionally rejected (replaced with NaN). Following artifact rejection, PSD power values were z‐scored across epochs within channel based on normalization over the entire night.

EEG and LFP Hemispheric Comparison

For each sleep stage over the course of a night, the selected LFP recording was separately compared to the ipsilateral and contralateral EEG recordings. At the epoch level, the STN localized bipolar LFP recording was compared to the EEG data, averaged for the three unilateral referenced contacts, using a cross correlation for each epoch.

EEG and LFP Hemispheric Comparison by Contiguous Sleep Stage Blocks

Contiguous sleep stage blocks (or bouts) were defined using the consensus hypnogram. For each sleep stage, uninterrupted segments were demarcated using a minimum block length of 3 contiguous sleep stage epochs (~1.5 min; not interrupted by a stage transition). Undecided epochs were excluded from the analysis. Like the epoch‐based hemispheric comparison for each sleep stage within each night, the selected LFP recording was separately compared to the ipsilateral and contralateral EEG recordings. At the block level, the STN‐localized bipolar LFP recording was compared to the EEG data, averaged for the three unilateral referenced contacts using both a cross correlation for each block.

Leave‐One‐Participant‐Out Classification Analysis for Sleep Stage Classification Using Aperiodic Features

To evaluate whether 1/f spectral features alone could recover PSG‐defined sleep stages by we trained multinomial classifiers using only the aperiodic exponent and offset estimated per epoch. Three feature variants were compared: EEG‐only (contralateral exponent and offset), STN‐LFP‐only (exponent and offset from the clinically‐selected bipolar contact pair), and combined (all four features). For each variant, classification performance was estimated using leave‐one‐participant‐out (LOPO) cross‐validation across all 19 subjects. In each fold, one subject's epochs were held out as the test set and the classifier was trained on the remaining 18. To prevent inter‐subject offset differences from confounding within‐state structure, features were centered within each subject using that patient's own mean (computed without reference to test‐fold labels), and a global scale was estimated from training‐fold patients only and applied to both train and test data. Multinomial logistic regression was implemented as an error‐correcting output ensemble with one‐versus‐one coding (linear with L2 regularization). Inverse‐frequency class weights were computed from training‐fold label counts to mitigate the strong stage imbalance characteristic of our observed PD sleep architecture (N2‐dominant, sparse N3 and N1). Performance was reported as overall accuracy, balanced accuracy (mean per‐class recall), and per‐class F1 score. To establish a chance‐level reference that respects the subject‐wise structure of the data, a permutation null distribution (n = 100) was generated by shuffling stage labels within each patient prior to LOPO, with reported p‐values computed as the proportion of permutations yielding accuracy at or above the observed value.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed in MATLAB (MathWorks) and significance was assessed at α = 0.05. Epochs labeled “undecided” were excluded. To quantify unilateral STN LFP–EEG coupling, we computed per‐epoch Pearson correlations between the LFP‐derived spectral profile and the corresponding EEG‐derived spectral profile, separately for ipsilateral and contralateral scalp EEG relative to the recorded STN. Pearson r values were modeled directly and summary estimates (means/medians) are reported in the native correlation units unless noted. For within‐subject laterality analyses, correlations were aggregated across nights within each subject and compared using a two‐factor linear model with fixed effects of sleep stage (W, N1, N2, N3, REM) and EEG laterality (ipsilateral vs. contralateral), including their interaction. Post hoc pairwise contrasts were performed to test ipsilateral versus contralateral differences within each sleep stage and sleep‐stage differences within each laterality condition, with family‐wise error control (Tukey–Kramer). For sleep‐period (block or bout) analyses, contiguous runs of the same sleep stage were grouped into periods; coupling metrics were computed per period and the same stage by laterality framework was applied. When stage‐specific period counts were insufficient for a given subject, that subject was excluded from that stage‐specific contrast and denominators are reported accordingly. To evaluate spectral slope and parameterized aperiodic metrics (aperiodic exponent and offset), we computed per‐epoch values for EEG and LFP. For EEG, contralateral channels were averaged per epoch for primary analyses (after verifying similar ipsilateral and contralateral profiles), and LFP was analyzed using a predefined bipolar contact pair based on optimal clinical benefit. Group‐level effects of recording modality (EEG vs. LFP) and sleep stage, including their interaction, were tested using linear mixed‐effects models with random intercepts for subject. Models were fit with awake as the reference level when interpreting stage coefficients, and post hoc contrasts were corrected for multiple comparisons (FDR). For aperiodic offset, values were additionally expressed relative to each subject's awake baseline to compare state‐dependent shifts between modalities; within‐stage modality differences were assessed using nonparametric tests at the subject level and confirmed using mixed‐effects modeling across subjects.

Results

Spectral Dynamics Within EEG and STN LFP During Sleep in PD by Epoch

Comparing the ipsilateral and contralateral EEG correlation with unilateral LFP aggregated across nights within subject, we found that 5/19 (26.3%) exhibited significant differences in ipsilateral versus contralateral EEG relative to STN. For those subjects with significant differences in I–C correlations, the contralateral correlation was greater than the ipsilateral correlation in 2/5 cases (p < 0.01) and 3/5 subjects exhibited a greater ipsilateral correlation (p < 0.01). Spectral Dynamics Within EEG and STN LFP During Sleep in PD by Sleep Period

Aggregating sleep periods by combining contiguous sleep stage epochs increased the differences in comparing between hemispheric EEG and unilateral STN‐LFP. For sleep stage periods as compared to individual sleep stages, comparing the ipsilateral and contralateral EEG correlation with unilateral LFP aggregated across nights within subject, we found that 19/19 (100%) exhibited significant differences in ipsilateral versus contralateral EEG relative to STN. For those subjects with significant differences in I–C correlations, the contralateral correlation was greater than the ipsilateral correlation in 11/19 cases (p < 0.01) and 8/19 subjects exhibited a greater ipsilateral correlation (p < 0.05). Out of the 19 subjects, both representative sleep stage periods and significance (p < 0.05) for sleep stage and laterality, the following subject subsets exhibited significant differences based on the interaction between laterality and specific sleep stages based on post hoc tests: 8/17 (N1), 5/19 (N2), 0/4 (N3), 12/19 (W), 3/11 (R). Denominators vary by stage due to insufficient period counts.

STN Spectral Slope Exhibits Relative Differences by Sleep Stage

We analyzed per‐epoch spectral slope (EEG and LFP) of the power spectrum across all nights, excluding the epochs with “undecided” classification. Given the largely similar findings for ipsilateral and contralateral results in the correlation with unilateral LFP, we conducted the remaining analyses on the contralateral EEG. On a per subject basis, analysis of sleep stage slope differences for each recording modality demonstrated that 18/19 subjects exhibited significant slope differences for sleep stage across the averaged EEG channels (p < 0.05) and 19/19 subjects exhibited significant differences for slopes in LFP recording data (p < 0.05). To compare across subjects, we evaluated the relationship between recording modality and sleep stage for the aperiodic exponent (AE) across subjects using a linear mixed‐effects model with awake set as the reference baseline (Figure 3c). The analysis revealed a significant interaction between recording modality and sleep stage (p < 0.001). EEG exhibited lowest exponent estimate during awake (β = 1.07). Transitioning into sleep resulted in a progressive increase in AE with significant elevations observed in N1 (β = 0.27 increase over awake), REM (β = 0.54 increase over awake), N2 (β = 0.69 increase over awake), and peaking during N3 (β = 0.95 increase over awake; all p < 0.001). Compared to contralateral EEG, STN‐LFP signals consistently exhibited higher AE across all arousal states. However, the magnitude of this dissociation was strongly sleep stage dependent. The difference between recording modalities was most pronounced during awake epochs, where LFP AE exceeded EEG by 0.59 (p < 0.001). The difference between recording modalities was far less during sleep stages, indicated by negative interaction coefficients relative to the awake baseline. While the differences were minimal in N1 (interaction β = 0.04 (decreased compared to awake), p = 0.02), the EEG‐LFP difference decreased substantially during deeper sleep stages, including N2 (β = 0.24 less than awake, p < 0.001) and N3 (β = 0.27 less than awake, p < 0.001). REM sleep exhibited the greatest reduction between EEG and LFP (β = 0.37 relative to awake, p < 0.001).

Aperiodic Features for Detecting Sleep Stage

To address whether 1/f spectral features alone could recover PSG‐defined sleep stages, we trained multinomial classifiers using only the aperiodic exponent and offset estimated per epoch. STN‐LFP aperiodic features alone correctly classified sleep stages at 0.47 (balanced accuracy 0.42; permutation p < 0.001), exceeding scalp EEG‐only performance (0.35/0.36) and approaching the combined‐modality ceiling (0.5/0.47). Confusion‐matrix structure (Figure 5d) indicated that Awake and N3 sleep stages, the states most relevant for sleep‐targeted adaptive DBS, were the most reliably distinguished by intracranial features, while N1 and REM remained the principal sources of error across all variants.

Discussion

Sleep disruption is among the most prevalent and debilitating non‐motor symptoms of PD, emerging often years before diagnosis [37, 38]. PSG in PD patients consistently shows reduced sleep efficiency, increased N1 sleep and WASO, decreased N2 and REM sleep, and fragmented sleep architecture [2, 3]. Recent work has emphasized the importance of aperiodic (1/f‐like) electrophysiological structure, specifically spectral slope and aperiodic exponent, as markers of excitation‐inhibition (E/I) balance where slope can differentiate sleep stages based on features such as robustness angle [26, 39]. Building on work showing that aperiodic spectral features track arousal across awake, NREM, and REM, we compared scalp EEG and STN‐LFP aperiodic dynamics across PSG‐defined sleep stages in PD patients recorded with externalized leads. We found across participants (1) consistent coupling between STN‐LFP spectral structure and scalp EEG, with subject‐specific hemispheric biases that became more consistently detectable when aggregating across contiguous stage blocks, (2) a robust stage‐dependent dissociation between modalities that was greatest in awake and REM but diminished with NREM depth and inverted in N3, and (3) convergent but dissimilar sleep stage effects across a band‐limited high‐frequency slope metric and model‐based aperiodic feature estimates. While epoch‐level analyses revealed modest inter‐hemispheric differences, aggregating contiguous sleep periods enhanced detection of network dynamics, yielding significant inter‐hemispheric differences across all participants, suggesting longer timescale measures may better capture cortical–subcortical coupling during sleep.

Aperiodic slope and exponent varied reliably with sleep stage in both modalities, but the magnitude and direction of changes differed between EEG and STN. In EEG, slopes flattened during wake, steepened progressively through the NREM stages and were more negative in REM than in awake. STN‐LFP slopes were steeper overall than EEG, reflecting greater low‐frequency dominance, and showed the most pronounced modality differences during awake and REM. As sleep deepened, the EEG–LFP dissociation diminished, and in N3 the relationship inverted, with EEG showing steeper slopes than LFP. Aperiodic exponents followed similar trends: EEG exponents increased from wake into deeper NREM and peaked in N3, whereas STN‐LFP exponents remained consistently higher than EEG across all stages but converged during NREM. Together, these findings show that while both cortical and STN aperiodic features track arousal state, they do so with distinct signatures that vary with sleep depth (Figure 6). The STN shows disproportionately steep slope and large aperiodic exponent during wakefulness and REM, while deeper NREM sleep reduces these differences and reveals more synchronized cortical–subcortical dynamics. The dissociation between LFP and EEG during awake and REM states, which differ fundamentally from the synchronized, inhibitory‐biased physiology of NREM sleep, may reflect basal‐ganglia hyperactivity, dopamine‐dependent modulation, or STN's role in cortical gating states [17, 18, 30]. Few studies have explicitly tested whether there are interhemispheric differences in cortico‐subcortical coherence. One study investigated bilateral motor cortical EEG and STN LFP coherence while subjects were awake at rest (instructed to remain awake in supine position with eyes closed). In a series of studies conducted immediately post DBS implant, in patients at rest, mostly in the absence of PD medication, through externalized leads, cortico‐subcortical coherence appears strongest for midline EEG contacts across a broad frequency range, in addition to frequency‐dependent differences in coherence for EEG signals ipsilateral and contralateral to STN [40, 41]. These studies did not quantify the contribution of the predominantly affected hemisphere, which has been noted to exhibit greater frequency‐dependent coherence [42]. Beyond intracranial studies in PD, ample evidence from sleep EEG studies demonstrates a significant increase in interhemispheric coherent activity during several sleep stages in comparison to wakefulness [43, 44]. Limitations in our study design do not permit adequate characterization of the contributions from dopaminergic tone and underlying neurodegeneration that may influence the observations of interhemispheric differences in cortico‐subcortical LFP correlations.

These results convey several implications. First, they reinforce the utility of aperiodic spectral features as physiologically meaningful markers of arousal, extending beyond conventional oscillatory metrics [45, 46, 47, 48]. Second, the demonstration that STN‐LFPs exhibit distinct aperiodic dynamics relative to EEG suggests that subcortical recordings capture complementary aspects of sleep physiology, potentially reflecting basal‐ganglia contributions to sleep regulation and fragmentation in PD [17, 18, 36, 45, 49]. Third, the strong sleep‐stage dependence of LFP–EEG correspondence provides a foundation for targeting adaptive neuromodulation strategies [19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Closed‐loop DBS systems require interpretable biomarkers of behavioral or physiological state; our data suggest that spectral slope or exponent may serve as viable state markers for detecting arousal, monitoring sleep fragmentation by differentiating sleep stage transitions. These spectral features may be incorporated into control schemes for tuning stimulation amplitude in relation to sleep‐state transitions. The current work used sleep expert consensus to establish ground truth sleep stage classification for the aperiodic feature comparison. This study did not explicitly test whether aperiodic components, as a single spectral feature set, would effectively differentiate sleep stage across subjects, as even sub‐band power analyses demonstrate moderate success on an individualized basis. However, our findings suggest that these features may augment or complement power based features in real‐time sleep stage classification [20, 21, 50]. Our leave‐one‐participant‐out classification analysis demonstrated that all three modalities (EEG‐only, STN‐LFP only and combined EEG + STN‐LFP) all demonstrated greater than chance accuracy in sleep stage class prediction. The STN‐LFP only model outperformed the EEG only model but was only moderately less accurate than the combined model. Of note for the STN‐LFP only model, the highest accuracy was obtained for classifying Awake and N3 epochs which is significant considering that N3 epochs are often a challenge for power based approaches (see Figure 5).

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cohort size was modest, and variability in PD severity, medication, and surgical approaches may have influenced results. Recordings were limited to one hemisphere due to the requirement for lead externalization, potentially resulting in underestimation of bilateral dynamics. Analyses focused on spectral slope and exponent within a defined frequency range, possibly overlooking brief transitions relevant to sleep fragmentation. Finally, causal links between basal ganglia excitability, cortical arousal, and sleep quality remain unclear. Future work should expand this effort to larger cohorts with bilateral STN recordings and chronic sensing‐enabled DBS to track dynamics across sleep, medication states, disease progression, and in the more naturalistic home environment. Incorporating arousal scoring and finer sleep microarchitectural measures will allow more detailed analyses. Mechanistic studies linking dopaminergic state, DBS parameters, and aperiodic features could clarify neuromodulation's role. Ultimately, translational studies should test whether adaptive DBS guided by aperiodic markers can improve sleep outcomes and extend therapeutic benefits beyond motor control.

In summary, this study provides the first systematic comparison of aperiodic EEG and STN‐LFP dynamics during sleep in PD. Both modalities exhibit robust, stage‐dependent variations in spectral slope and aperiodic exponent, but the magnitude and direction of these changes differ in ways that reflect underlying neurophysiology. These findings highlight aperiodic structure as a shared but distinct marker of arousal across cortical and subcortical networks and underscore its potential utility for future adaptive DBS strategies aimed at addressing both motor and non‐motor symptoms, particularly the pervasive problem of sleep fragmentation in PD.
