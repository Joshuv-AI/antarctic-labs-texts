Impact of multi-frequency ultrasound processing with different treatment times on the structural quality of frozen wheat dough
Authors: Zahoor Ahmed, Bin Xu, Umar Farooq, Muhammad Faisal Manzoor, Mohamed F. Awad, Jawad Ashraf, Tabussam Tufail, Gholamreza Abdi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Frozen dough technology has become indispensable because it ensures processing consistency and simplicity. This technology is critical for ensuring consistent product quality, lowering labor costs, and prolonging shelf life. It allows bakers to streamline operations and efficiently meet consumer demands [1]. Frozen dough is a mainstay in current baking techniques since recent developments have improved its performance and usefulness [2]. After years of development, frozen dough technology is widely recognized for its ability to facilitate industrial production and is commonly used in baking products.

Freezing can negatively affect the dough quality, reducing poor water-holding capacity and volume and shorter shelf life. Freezing causes proteins in the dough to break down, resulting in a disordered structure and decreased foaming capacity and loaf volume [3]. These adverse effects are primarily caused by mechanical damage from ice crystals. Freezing storage temperature fluctuations influence the uneven water distribution in the frozen dough, with a critical temperature range of −1°C to −5°C [4]. The longer the dough remains within this temperature range, the larger the ice crystals become, and ice nucleation occurs through primary and secondary nucleation. Primary nucleation is further divided into homogeneous and heterogeneous nucleation. While numerous studies have examined the changes in frozen dough throughout the freezing process, few have specifically focused on the zone of maximum ice crystal formation [5]. Therefore, it is necessary to improve the quality of final frozen dough-based products to enhance their edibility. These benefits, freezing treatments cause disturbance of the gluten network structure, starch granule structure, and yeast action by the formation and recrystallization of ice crystals. These ice crystals can burst yeast cell membranes, lowering the dough's rising power. Moreover, depolymerization of gluten macro polymers (GMP results in a secondary structure disordered and a decrease in disulfide bonds) [6]. In the case of starch, freezing compromises granular integrity, resulting in surface holes and pits. This process includes forming short-range ordered structures, multi-scale structures, crystalline structures, and layered structures that become disordered, although digestibility improves [7]. These altered physicochemical characteristics result in a disrupted starch-gluten matrix, leading to increased hardness, reduced specific volume, and diminished sensorial quality [8]. Consequently, research focused on enhancing frozen dough quality has garnered significant interest from the scientific community.

Recent research has concentrated on various strategies for enhancing the quality of frozen dough, primarily through modifying flour constituents, incorporating additives, and advancements in physical freezing technologies. Among these approaches, physically assisted methods, such as ultrasound and the application of electric and magnetic fields, have attracted considerable attention due to their mild and environmentally benign reaction conditions [8]. Ultrasound technology, in particular, has shown promise in food technologies for enhancing vegetable and fruit storage and shelf life due to its durable perviousness and non-contact nature [9]. For instance, Köprüalan Aydın, Yüksel Sarıoğlu, Dirim and Kaymak-Ertekin [10] demonstrated that ultrasound could delay ice crystal formation under the Lorentz force, which is affected by the electronic polar water molecules' spin. This mechanism reduces the self-diffusion coefficient of water by weakening or breaking hydrogen molecules in water clusters [11]. It forms smaller, stronger inter-cluster connections shielding the food matrix from ice crystal damage [12].

Additionally, Parandi, Pero and Kiani [13] concluded that static ultrasound shortens the time needed to pass the formation of ice crystals, inhibiting the transformation into free water of bound water and reducing the proportion of freezable water. Jiang, Zhang, Mujumdar and Chen [14] reported that a permanent ultrasound with a frequency of 35 kHz efficiently enhanced the frozen quality of blueberries by decreasing the average ice crystal by 34.1 %. Despite these promising results, the ultrasound (US) application for efficient frozen dough quality remains underexplored.

This study investigated the impact of various ultrasound frequencies and durations on frozen wheat dough compositional characteristics, rheological behavior, texture, and microstructure. Advanced techniques such as FTIR, RAMAN, and XRD were utilized to analyze the functional properties and structural changes in the dough. The findings elucidate the mechanisms driving these effects but also offer valuable insights into optimizing ultrasound treatment for frozen wheat dough. These results pave the way for enhancing dough quality and could significantly expand the industrial application of ultrasound technology in the processing of frozen wheat-based products.

Materials and methods

Materials

Wheat flour was sourced from (Wudeli Flour Group Co., Ltd Handan, China,) with moisture, carbohydrate, and protein contents 12. %, 24 % and 16. %, respectively.

Preparation dough

Dough samples were prepared according to [15], using a formulation of 500 g of wheat flour, 4 g of salt, and 295 g of deionized water. The ingredients were blended in an (AM-CG108 mixer North American Electric, Zhuhai, China) for 22 min, then proofed at 30 °C and 80 % humidity for 30 min in an XF-16A proofer (Hongling Electric Heating Equipment, Guangzhou, China). After proofing, the dough was cut into 50-gram pieces, placed in self-sealing bags, and subjected to multi-frequency ultrasound treatment.

Ultrasound pretreatment

For pretreatment, 50 g of dough were placed in a WKS1800/7S ultrasonication reaction tank in Jiangsu, China. Three ultrasound frequency settings were investigated: single-frequency 20 kHz, dual-frequency 20/40 kHz, and triple-frequency 20/40/60 kHz. Sonication times varied single-frequency S1: 10-minutes S2: 20- minutes S3: 30-minutes dual frequency D1: 10-minutes D2: 20-minutes D3: 30-minutes and triple frequency T1: 10-minute T2: 20-minutes T3: 30-minutes. The 45 W/L power density was maintained, and the ultrasonic temperature was kept between 2–8 °C (Table 1). After treatment, samples were stored in a −80 °C freezer for 7 days and dried using a freeze-dryer. Texture properties

The dough's characteristics were analyzed by a texture analyzer with a P/100 type probe [16]. The settings for the compression test are the auto-trigger force of 5 g, the pretest speed of 1 mm/s, the post-test speeds of 5 mm/s, the strain of 70, and the compression interval of 5 s. For every sample, at least three repetitions of each measurement were made.

Rheological behavior

A rheometer measured the dynamic rheological properties of dough treated with different ultrasound frequencies and duration. According to the Instruments of TA USA Delaware [17] the dough samples were compressed between plates to a 1.0 mm gap, with excess dough gently removed. The range at 0.05 strain, 25 °C of angular frequencies from 1.0 to 100.0 rad/s, and the magnitude of six data points were recorded between each order. The frequency of the loss modulus Gʹʹ storage modulus Gʹ and tan δ GʹʹGʹ were plotted as a function.

Rapid viscosity analysis (RVA)

To determine the pasting properties, use a Rapid Visco Analyzer (TecMaster Perten Instruments North Ryde BC NSW Australia following). Briefly, 3 g of the sample was mixed with 25 g of deionized water in an RVA canister. The analysis time was 13 min, with the slurry mixed at 960 rpm for 10 s and then maintained at 160 rpm. The initial temperature was 50 °C for 1 min, increased to 95 °C at 6 °C/min, held at 95 °C for 5 min, at 6 °C/min cooled to 50 °C, and held at 50 °C for 2 min. The Parameters studied included peak viscosity (PV), trough viscosity (TV), breakdown, final viscosity (FV), and setback.

Fourier Transform infrared spectral analysis

The infrared spectra of treated dough samples were obtained using an FTIR USA Perkin Elmer Instruments. Spectrum collected over 600 to 4000 cm−1 at 4 cm−1 resolution. Secondary structure changes were analyzed between 1600 and 1700 cm−1 amide I band using OMNIC. β-sheets random coils α-helices and β-turns corresponded to wavelengths 1615–1637 cm−1, 1637–1646 cm−1, 1646–1660 cm−1 and 1660–1700 cm−1, respectively [18].

Raman spectra analysis

The spectrum was recorded with a DXR2 Raman spectroscopy Nicolet Inc, MA, USA. The 400–4000 cm−1 range for each spectrum included 512 exposures, a 10-second exposure time, and a laser wavelength of 785 nm. The Peak Fit software was used to fit and analyze the secondary structure components of each protein sample [19].

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

A D8 Advance AXS X-ray powder diffractometer Bruker Karlsruhe Germany was used to study the frozen dough of dried samples with around 10 % moisture content. Measurements were conducted at 40 mA and 40 kV using Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 0.1542 nm. The tightly packed starch powder was in a rectangular cell glass and scanned from 4° to 40° 2θ at a speed of 2°/min with a 0.033° step size.

Microstructure analysis

The samples were lyophilized using a Biocool Experimental freeze-dryer (Beijing, China). After freeze-drying, a gold–palladium alloy was used to coat the dough and analyzed with a 500 Gemini scanning electron microscope (Oberkochen, Germany) at magnifications of × 2000 and × 300 with 2.0 kV an accelerating voltage. This process was adapted from the method outlined by Yang et al. (2021) with certain modifications.

Data analysis

Data were processed using SPSS version 18.0 and plotted using Origin version 8.0. All results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation n = 3. Significant differences were determined at the 95 % confidence level using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Duncan's test procedure.

Results and discussion

Texture (TPA)

Texture is a critical factor influencing consumer choice in food products [20]. Hardness is a particularly significant indicator for assessing the wheat flour quality of products as it significantly impacts the structural integrity of frozen dough [21]. Table 1 presents the variation in textural characteristics of frozen dough subjected to various ultrasound treatment conditions (frequencies and time duration). The T3 treatments significantly impacted the dough's chewiness, hardness, elasticity, cohesion, and resilience p < 0.05. As Table 1 depicts, the hardness of frozen dough decreased when the frequency and time duration changed. The minimum hardness recorded was 52.6 T3 at an ultrasonic frequency of 20/40/60 kHz and 30 min, reflecting a 52.1 % reduction associated with the control frozen dough. The frozen dough hardness decreased with changes in the ultrasound frequencies and duration. The reduction in hardness at different frequencies can be attributed to ultrasound breaking large down hydrogen bonds between protein polymers, resulting in protein aggregates in smaller soluble and looser dough structures.

Conversely, the increase in hardness at higher power densities may be due to the denaturation of proteins, which leads to gluten protein a tighter network due to the exposure of hydrophobic groups. When the ultrasonic frequencies are lower, the hardness resembles the untreated dough. It indicates that ultrasound treatment can mitigate the detrimental effects of freezing, enhance food stability, and modify the impact of gluten networks and ice crystals on the dough's structure. Additionally, the triple-frequency (T3) ultrasonic treatment increases the dough's water content, enhancing water binding and preventing the formation of a typical gluten matrix, thereby reducing the dough's hardness. Moreover, the resilience and springiness values provide insight into changes in the dough's elasticity. The resilience and springiness were slightly lower, aligning with previous research findings. It suggests that ultrasonic treatment improves frozen dough's texture and structural integrity and maintains its elastic properties better than conventional freezing methods [22].

As shown in Table 1, the frozen dough springiness increased with rising ultrasonic frequencies and duration like T3 by 39 % compared to the control. A study found a positive correlation between the dough matrix's mechanical and protein network quality. Literature indicates that ultrasonic treatment can significantly enhance the resilience and springiness of frozen dough by efficiently enhancing the water-holding capacity and improving the textural properties. For instance, an increase in ultrasonic power density to 66 W/L led to a 14.9 % increase in springiness and reduced hardness by 7.8 %, improving the dough's overall quality and cooking characteristics [23]. It demonstrates that ultrasound is an effective method for improving dough texture and elasticity.

Another study reported that the gas concentration was reduced by ultrasound treatment by about 1 % in the dough due to air bubbles in a deeper distribution and increased elasticity [24]. This observation suggests that ultrasound treatment can effectively counteract the adverse effects of freezing, thereby enhancing food stability. Specifically, ultrasound can modify the gluten networks and ice crystals, significantly impacting the frozen dough's structure. Furthermore, the triple-frequency (T3) ultrasonic treatment has a notable effect on the dough's water content by increasing the water content. T3 treatment strengthens water binding within the dough, which hinders the formation of a conventional gluten matrix. This disruption in the gluten matrix formation reduces the dough's overall hardness, making it more pliable. The resilience and springiness values of the dough provide important insights into its elasticity and structural integrity. In this case, these values were slightly lower, consistent with previous findings. This consistency reinforces that freezing alone can diminish the dough's elastic properties. However, applying ultrasonic treatment appears to preserve these properties more. It also improves the dough's water-binding capacity and disrupts the formation of a traditional gluten matrix, reducing hardness. Moreover, the treatment preserves the dough's resilience and springiness, ensuring better elasticity and overall quality than conventional freezing methods.

Ultrasound treatment impacts dough in several ways related to its structural and mechanical properties. Ultrasound can facilitate molecular rearrangement, increase the bound-water content in the dough, and reinforce hydrogen bonding within the dough matrix. These effects help to enhance the gluten network structure by improving the interaction between gluten proteins and starch granules [25]. Additionally, ultrasound treatment can cause the structure to unfold the protein, expose more hydrophobic groups, and break hydrophobic bonds to the solvent. This can lead to a more open and flexible dough structure, improving overall quality by enhancing hydration elasticity and rheological properties.

Pasting properties

Ultrasound treatment significantly affects the pasting properties of wheat flour. Several studies have investigated how different frequencies and durations of ultrasound treatment impact the rheological and pasting characteristics. Peak viscosity, which characterizes the swelling power of starch, results in gelatinization and disrupts the internal network structure of starch granules. Previous studies have stated that ultrasonication treatment improves the peak viscosity of waxy corn starch of potato millet [26]. This improvement in viscosity is likely due to physical changes to starch granules enhancing water penetration and hydration.

Additionally, mechanical cavitation during ultrasonic processing oscillation can cause the breakdown of long polymer chains and increase the interaction forces among starch granules, thereby improving viscosity [27]. Rheology

The dough's rheological properties, influenced by its ingredient structure and content, are crucial for industrial production, particularly regarding dough deformation and flow. Fig. 1 illustrates the loss modulus G“ and storage modulus G' loss tangent tan δ of frozen dough samples as functions of frequency oscillation. The storage modulus G' represents the energy stored in reversible deformation, indicating dough elasticity. In contrast, the loss modulus G” represents energy lost due to irreversible deformation indicating dough viscosity. The loss tangent tan δ ratio is the G“ to G'. Throughout the frequency scan, the energy storage modulus, the loss modulus G” was lower than the G', i.e., tanδ < 1, suggesting the dough exhibits solid-like viscoelastic behavior. As shown in Fig. 1, it was consistently lower than G' across all frequency ranges, confirming normal viscoelastic properties and solid-like behavior due to cross-linking within the polymer network, a feature of viscoelasticity [33]. Increased G' values suggest weak dough extensibility and strength but increased interaction elasticity [32]. Associated with the control frozen dough and ultrasonication treatments, both G“ and G' values increased in dough frozen. Interestingly, frozen dough with various ultrasound treatments had higher G' and G” values than frozen control dough. The increase in G' and G“ suggests enhanced gluten strength due to unfolding protein during sonication as acoustic cavitation, leading to conformational and structural changes. These changes result in a more uniform distribution of starch-granule molecules within the dough network, absorbing water to form a more extensive gel network. A coherent structure of the gluten network is essential for a dough viscoelastic system, and the observed increase in gluten strength implies that ultrasound frequencies and duration can mitigate the formation of a large risk of clumps [34]. The rheological behavior of dough treated with different ultrasonic treatments induces cavitation effects and mechanical altering of the gluten structure to enhance the dough's viscoelastic system. Tan δ, the ratio of G” to G' is a critical indicator reflecting dough viscoelasticity [35]. This study's tan δ values ranged from 0.22 to 0.99, typical of pseudogel systems [36]. This range indicates that the dough behaves more like a solid elastic than a viscous liquid, suggesting that T3 treatment dough develops a more robust and resilient structure during the frequency range consistent with the pragmatic dough textural properties.

Fourier Transform infrared (FTIR)

The structural changes in wheat flour frozen dough samples can be effectively monitored using Fourier Transform Infrared FTIR spectroscopy, which provides valuable information on short-range molecular sequencing. FTIR spectra of wheat flour frozen dough samples are presented in Fig. 2. The gluten protein molecular configuration is crucial in determining the frozen dough quality, with changes in the secondary structure significantly influencing the gluten protein configuration. Microstructure

The results showed significant variation in the rheological properties and hydration of the dough due to ultrasound treatments. To better understand these effects, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to examine the microstructure of the composite dough subjected to different ultrasound treatments. This analysis provides valuable insights into the interactions between compounds, such as starch granules and gluten protein. The SEM images of the control dough in Fig. 5 revealed a gluten network that was consistently present, with large lenticular starch granules (SG) that appeared loosely connected. Smaller granules were arranged necklace-like, exhibiting flat oval shapes with smooth surfaces devoid of pinholes, fractures, or cracks. Ultrasonic treatment causes notable changes in the wheat dough through mechanical and physical pressures. High-frequency sound waves create micro-jets, shear waves, and turbulence when passing through the dough, producing a combined localized solid pressure. These forces upset van der Waals connections and hydrogen bonds that hold the polypeptide chains of protein molecules in place. The partial unfolding of the protein molecules as these weak non-covalent bonds break reveals reactive regions, encouraging fresh cross-linking between proteins [46]. This method lets gluten proteins reorganize and stretch, altering their secondary structure and spatial conformation. The expanded and linked gluten network enhances the dough's solubility, elasticity, and swelling power, among other physicochemical properties. These improvements improve the dough's functional characteristics, which raises its adaptability for various cooking purposes and guarantees better product quality and yield in food processing [47].

Through the use of ultrasonic treatment, the protein structures in wheat dough can be significantly altered. High-frequency sound waves generate strong mechanical pressures that disturb the hydrophobic bonds responsible for maintaining the normal structure of proteins, leading to the unfolding process. Normally, these hydrophobic groups are hidden within the protein to prevent interaction with water. However, when these bonds break down, the hydrophobic groups come into contact with the surrounding solvent, increasing the interaction between the protein and water [48].

The unfolding and reconfiguration of protein structures caused by ultrasonic vibrations also impact the dough's microstructure. These changes affect the hydration properties of the dough, as the proteins can now bind more water, thereby influencing the overall moisture content and distribution. Additionally, the altered protein network affects the rheological properties of the dough, including its elasticity, viscosity, and resistance to deformation. As a result, the dough becomes more porous and flexible due to the larger and modified protein network [49] taken together; these microstructural changes explain the observed alterations in hydration, rheological, and structural properties of frozen dough following ultrasonic treatment. Consequently, this treatment can improve processing parameters and enhance the quality of the final baked product. When considered collectively, these microstructural changes explain the observed changes in frozen dough's hydration, rheological, and structural properties after ultrasonic treatment, potentially increasing processing parameters and the quality of the finished baked product.

Conclusion

The application of ultrasound in food processing, particularly in freezing, has significantly improved efficiency and quality. Studies indicate that ultrasound frequencies and the duration of exposure play crucial roles in these enhancements. Multi-frequency ultrasound has been found to accelerate freezing rates, improve the microstructure, maintain the quality attributes of frozen products, reduce drip loss and retain firmness and food constituents. When applied for varying durations and at different frequencies, ultrasound treatments can influence the structure properties of frozen products. This study explored the effects of varying ultrasound frequencies and durations on the compositional characteristics, rheological behavior, texture, pasting properties, and microstructure of frozen dough. Utilizing FTIR, RAMAN, and XRD techniques, the functional properties and structural changes in the dough were thoroughly analyzed. The results highlight the potential of optimizing ultrasound-assisted freezing to improve energy efficiency and food quality preservation significantly. These findings offer valuable insights for the food industry, presenting ultrasound treatment as a promising tool for enhancing the quality and processing of frozen dough products.
