Engineering a new tripartite split-ccGFP system from Corynactis californica for detecting protein–protein interactions
Authors: Thomas M Groseclose, Erin Kober, Jennifer Zupancic, Claire K McLelland, Lexy A Lujan, Yuliya A Kunde, Sarah C Mozden, Nileena Velappan, Antonietta M Lillo, Emilia Solomon, Jacob Yoder, Geoffrey S Waldo, Hau B Nguyen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein-protein interactions (PPIs) are critical to a range of biological processes and, consequently, aberrant interactions are implicated in many disorders. The study of the complex networks of PPIs promises to elucidate undiscovered roles in cellular processes and the mechanisms of disease. To accomplish this, tools to effectively sense PPIs are necessary. Effective PPI sensors must rapidly detect interactions in real-time with high sensitivity without perturbing the proteins of interest (POIs) under study. Split fluorescent proteins have previously been used to successfully monitor PPIs, in part due to the small size of the tags. Here, we developed an optimized tripartite split GFP system based on Corynactis californica GFP (ccGFP) to detect PPIs in vitro. In this sensor system, ccGFP fragments ccGFP10 and ccGFP11 are tagged to two POIs. PPIs can then be detected via fluorescence by complementation to the third fragment, ccGFP1-9, which reconstitutes functional ccGFP. The optimized ccGFP system shows improved detection kinetics and pH and temperature stability compared to a previous system. We then validated the sensor by monitoring PPIs in two model systems: attractive/repulsive coiled-coils and rapamycin-inducible FRB/FKBP heterodimerization. Finally, we developed an anti-tripartite ccGFP single-chain variable fragment (scFv), which could enable versatile detection of identified protein-protein complexes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24657-6.Keywords: Protein tagging, Protein detection, Protein-protein interactions, Split fluorescent protein, Green fluorescent protein (GFP), Protein fragment complementation, Antibody, Single-chain variable fragment (scFv), Protein engineering, Directed evolutionSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Computational biology and bioinformatics

Introduction

Protein-protein interactions (PPIs) play important roles in many critical biological processes. These processes include: DNA replication; transcription/translation; signal transduction and receptor binding; cellular organization, growth, division, and death; and metabolism1–3. As such, disruption of these PPIs are implicated in many diseases and disorders2,4, which include infectious diseases5, cancer6, and neurodegeneration7. Increased awareness of the effects of aberrant PPIs has motivated the study of the complex network of the protein-protein “interactome”1,8 and developing therapeutics targeted toward them5,6,9,10. Consequently, the development of new tools for detecting and measuring the affinities of PPIs can enable the identification of new and uncharacterized interactions, provide valuable information to the growing human protein interactome, accelerate complex proteomics studies, and foster the discovery of PPI modulators to act as therapeutics1,9,11.

Several methods have been developed to detect protein-protein complexes in living cells12, such as Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET)12–14, fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM)13,15, bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET)16,17, and fluorescence (cross-) correlation microscopy (FCM/FCCM)12,18, which each rely on imaging the luminescence signal generated by the tags attached to the proteins coming into proximity of one another via energy transfer or release of a fluorophore. Other methods have used protein fragment complementation assays (PCAs), whereby PPIs are detected as protein fragments tagged on proteins of interest reconstitute functional proteins when brought in proximity of one another19. This has been performed with a range of enzymes20–22, with enzyme activity on a substrate serving as a proxy for interacting protein partners.

The application of PCAs to fluorescent proteins (FPs) is termed bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC)12,23. BiFC images fluorescence as fragments of FPs, tagged to proteins of interest (POIs), reconstitute functional fluorescent proteins. A key property of BiFC is its irreversibility: functional FPs can accumulate, allowing detection at low concentrations and affinities, and are permanently tethered to protein-protein complexes, enabling spatial tracking12,24,25. Early BiFC systems, however, experienced issues with the solubility and folding of the large, bulky protein fragments, in addition to high background signals25–27. Using smaller fragments as tags, however, can reduce propensity for aggregation and interference with the proteins of interest28–30. Based on their original work with split GFP–where the expression and solubility of a protein of interest could be readily measured by tagging with a small fragment of GFP, GFP11, and detecting with the remaining part, GFP1-1028,29–Cabantous, et al., developed a tripartite split GFP protein-protein interaction sensor25. The system used three GFP fragments: two small fragments: GFP10 and GFP11 tags, which were each tethered to a POI, and GFP1-9, which served as the detector25. They then demonstrated the utility of the tripartite split GFP system by characterizing the PPIs in two model systems, as well as verified localization of protein-protein complexes in mammalian cells.

FPs have, more generally, been used to visualize and label proteins and cellular components in living cells and tissues14,27,31. Commonly, FPs, such as GFP, are fused to POIs to report on gene expression and protein localization and interactions14,27,31. Detection of FPs is routine using standard laboratory equipment (e.g., plate reader, fluorescent microscope), however the effects of imaging fluorescence can be damaging to samples32 and some conditions may render fluorescence detection difficult33. The use of anti-FP antibodies facilitates the detection and manipulation of proteins using this fluorescent protein “handle.” Anti-GFP antibodies, when fused to other FPs or enzymes that generate a signal orthogonal to green fluorescence, can be used to amplify detection signals, or provide for alternative means of detection, of the protein fused to GFP34. Antibodies characteristically have high affinity and specificity toward their targets, increasing the signal-to-noise ratio. This is particularly important in conditions in which GFP fluorescence is not optimal, such as under anaerobic33 or acidic conditions and with certain fixing techniques35, or when there is dominant background or autofluorescence36. Anti-GFP antibodies have general utility in protein quantification (i.e., Western blots, ELISAs), immunofluorescence, immunoprecipitation, and immunohistochemistry. Recently, several groups have created and used anti-GFP and anti-FP antibody fragments, such as nanobodies and single-chain variable fragments (scFvs). These antibody fragments consist of only the antigen-binding domains, and they can be readily used in detection applications due to their small size and flexible expression hosts37–41.

Recently, Nguyen, et al., developed a two-body split fluorescent protein system based on the green fluorescent protein from a strawberry anemone, Corynactis californica, referred to as ccGFP42. This system functioned similarly to the two-body split GFP system from the jellyfish, Aequorea victoria, (GFP) developed by Cabantous, et al.28,29 though was demonstrated to have three-fold faster complementation kinetics (the rate at which the constituent fluorescent protein fragments associate and the chromophore matures to generate fluorescence), as well as to not cross-react with other fluorescent proteins, allowing for multiplexed labeling in cells with a palette of fluorescent colors. We present here the engineering of a new tripartite PPI sensor based on the ccGFP and two-body split ccGFP system developed by Nguyen, et al.42. We show that the new tripartite split ccGFP sensor has improved complementation kinetics and stability compared to tripartite split GFP28 and can detect and monitor PPIs in two model systems, charged coiled-coil peptides43 and the rapamycin-mediated heterodimerization of FK506 binding protein (FKBP) and FKBP12-rapamycin binding domain (FRB)44. We then present the engineering of highly-specific anti-tripartite ccGFP scFvs, which allow secondary detection of PPIs via ccGFP and direct integration into existing workflows using anti-FP antibodies. We anticipate that this tripartite split ccGFP sensor system, with its utility further enabled by the anti-tripartite ccGFP scFvs, will serve as a robust tool to detect, track and amplify PPIs using orthogonal signals, contributing to proteomics studies and the growing protein interactome.

Results

Engineering a tripartite split ccGFP system for improved solubility and complementation

Following a similar strategy we used to engineer the tripartite split GFP system25 from A. victoria, our goal was to develop a new, orthogonal tripartite split fluorescent protein sensor for PPIs, based on the two-body split ccGFP system developed by Nguyen, et al.42 (Fig. S1a-b). Our previous work showed that the two-body split ccGFP did not cross react with the two-body split GFP system, thus, together with our existing tripartite split GFP, a new tripartite split ccGFP system has great potential for multiplexed and cascade labeling.

The tripartite split ccGFP system was envisioned to function similarly to the tripartite split GFP system, whereby two POIs, A and B, are each tagged with either ccGFP10 or ccGFP11, respectively (Fig. 1). Incubating the two fusion proteins, A-ccGFP10 and B-ccGFP11 together and adding the detector, ccGFP1-9, will elucidate any existing PPIs, with the strength of the interaction correlating with fluorescence signal. If protein A and protein B interact, the ccGFP10 and ccGFP11 tags are brought in proximity of one another; following complementation with ccGFP1-9, functional ccGFP will be reconstituted, creating a fluorescent signal (Fig. 1, top). However, if protein A and protein B do not interact, ccGFP10 and ccGFP11 will not appreciably be brought into proximity of one another, resulting in low/no fluorescent signal (Fig. 1, bottom). An important consideration is that individual ccGFP10, ccGFP11, and ccGFP1-9 fragments do not fluoresce; it is only when all three components reconstitute to form a complete β-barrel structure that the complex fluoresces (Fig. 1, right)25,28,42.

A schematic diagram showing the principle of the tripartite split ccGFP system in detecting protein-protein interactions similar to the previously reported tripartite split GFP25. Proteins A and B are fused to ccGFP10 and ccGFP11 fragments, respectively. If proteins A and B interact, upon complementation with the detector fragment, ccGFP1-9, they will reconstitute functional ccGFP, causing a fluorescent signal (top). If proteins A and B do not interact, there will be no fluorescent signal, as functional ccGFP will not associate together (bottom). Individual β fragments (represented throughout as cylinders) do not individually fluoresce. Only when the 11 β-strands of ccGFP associate into a β-barrel does the complex generate fluorescence (right).

We first sought to develop a ccGFP1-9 detector fragment with high complementation efficiency. Toward this end, we began with the ccGFP_7 full length protein45 (Fig. S1a) that is comprised of the ccGFP1-10 v242 (residues 1-202) and ccGFP11 v142 (residues 205–221) fragments. Next, we tested different junction points to create a feasible pair of ccGFP1-9 and ccGFP10-11 fragments (Fig. S1a). These putative junction points were V186, V187, and K188, chosen based on sequence homology to the A. victoria GFP1-9 fragment28. These junction point variants (ccGFP1-91−186, ccGFP1-91−187, ccGFP1-91−188) were initially tested with their corresponding ccGFP10-11 fragments (ccGFP10-11187−221, ccGFP10-11188−221, ccGFP10-11189−221) fused to the C-terminus of a soluble protein, sulfite reductase (SR, a protein from Pylobacterium aerophilum46, and co-expressed in Escherichia coli (E. coli). All three cognate pairs had bright fluorescence; however, ccGFP1-91−188 and its cognate partner, ccGFP10-11189−221, had slightly brighter fluorescence intensities at the colony level than the other two pairs (Fig. S2a).

To evaluate the complementation efficiency closer to a tripartite split system, we then spaced ccGFP10 and ccGFP11 away from each other by inserting SR in between them via flexible linkers (GTDVGSGGGS and GSGGGSGGGSTS). Hence, we created a “sandwich” ccGFP10-linker-SR-linker-ccGFP11 (ccGFP10-SR-ccGFP11) protein fusion. The three ccGFP1-9 junction point variants (ccGFP1-91−186, ccGFP1-91−187, ccGFP1-91−188) were tested for in vivo complementation with their corresponding ccGFP10-SR-ccGFP11 constructs (ccGFP10187–204, ccGFP10188–204, ccGFP10189–204) and ccGFP11 (residues 205–221)43 fragments. In vivo co-expression of the two components in E. coli showed that all three pairs had similar faint fluorescence (Fig. S2b). Therefore, we selected the ccGFP1-91−188 variant (hereafter referred to as ccGFP1-9 WT) as a candidate for further engineering.

Seeking to increase the complementation efficiency and kinetics, we performed 3 rounds of directed evolution on ccGFP1-9 WT, selecting for variants that developed enhanced fluorescence intensity in shorter times when complemented with the ccGFP10-SR-ccGFP11 protein construct in vivo in E. coli. DNA shuffling was used to introduce and combine mutations after each round of evolution, as discussed in the Methods section. The resulting variant, named ccGFP1-9 v1, contained 3 mutations compared to the starting point ccGFP1-9 WT construct: M3I, R85Q, and V109A (Fig. S1b).

We next sought to optimize the ccGFP10 and ccGFP11 fragments for improved complementation efficiency with the ccGFP1-9 v1 construct. We first constructed a ccGFP10189–204-linker-ccGFP11205–221 fusion by inserting a long linker (GTDVGSGGGSHMGGGSGSGGGSGGGSTS, where HM is the NdeI and GS is the BamHI site for cloning) between the C-terminus of ccGFP10 and the N-terminus of ccGFP11 (i.e., the “sandwich” ccGFP10-SR-ccGFP11 construct as above, without the SR protein). We then performed 3 rounds of directed evolution on the construct, again selecting for variants that had enhanced fluorescence brightness in shorter development times, when complemented with ccGFP1-9 v1, as compared to the starting ccGFP10-linker-ccGFP11 construct. The resultant variant, ccGFP10 OPT, contained an S202Y mutation (relative to the original C. californica GFP sequence), while the resultant ccGFP11 contained no mutations compared to the starting sequence obtained from the previous studies42,45 (Fig. S1b). Interestingly, four of the mutations obtained for the ccGFP10-linker-ccGFP11 construct are linker residues, suggesting that the linker sequence may have strong effect on the complementation efficiency. This new linker sequence, along with the new S202Y mutation obtained for ccGFP10 OPT, was used as a complement construct to further optimize ccGFP1-9 v1, with SR cloned in between ccGFP10 OPT and ccGFP11 via NdeI and BamHI sites in the linker sequence, as mentioned above. After two rounds of directed evolution, we obtained the final version of ccGFP1-9, named ccGFP1-9 OPT, that contained four mutations compared to the ccGFP1-9WT: M3N, L44V, R85Q, and V109A (Fig. S1b).

The DNA and protein sequences of the engineered ccGFP fragments used in this study are shown in Tables S1 and S2, respectively.

Characterizing an engineered tripartite split CcGFP system

Following the optimization of the tripartite split ccGFP system using the stepwise engineering campaign, we sought to characterize its performance. We first tested our engineered ccGFP system (ccGFP1-9 OPT, ccGFP10 OPT, ccGFP11) against the wild-type system (i.e., the starting system containing ccGFP1-9 WT, ccGFP10 WT, and ccGFP11, see Fig. S1c), measuring the development of fluorescence over time for the two systems in which the ccGFP10 and ccGFP11 fragments were tethered in a ccGFP10-SR-ccGFP11 “sandwich” fusion and incubated with the respective ccGFP1-9 detector (Fig. 2a). We saw that the engineered system had substantially higher fluorescence signal generation compared to the wild-type system, which showed very low fluorescence over 15 h. Notably, the engineered system had rapid signal development (Fig. 2a, inset), with observable fluorescence within 15 min and reaching half of its maximum fluorescence in less than 1 h. We additionally compared the results of the engineered ccGFP system against the tripartite A. victoria GFP system28, using a similar experimental setup, with the GFP10 and GFP11 fragments fused in the GFP10-SR-GFP11 “sandwich” fusion and complemented with the corresponding GFP1-9 detector fragment (Fig. 2b). We saw that our engineered ccGFP system had overall improved complementation kinetics compared to the GFP system. While the GFP system appeared to have a slightly faster complementation rate in the first ~ 10 min, the ccGFP system had ~ 3-fold higher normalized fluorescence compared to GFP system by 1 h (Fig. 2b, inset) and reached its maximum fluorescence by approximately 7 h, while GFP system did not appear to reach its maximum fluorescence value by 15 h (Fig. 2b).

We additionally compared the performance and function of the tripartite sensor systems at a range of pH and temperature conditions (Fig. S3). Testing the ccGFP and GFP systems using the GFP10-SR-GFP11 fusions complemented with the corresponding GFP1-9 at varying conditions and measuring fluorescence generated, we found that the engineered ccGFP system was more tolerant to more extreme pH and temperature conditions than the GFP system. Specifically, regarding temperature, the GFP system had an over 2-fold reduction in fluorescence at 37 °C compared to its maximum at 25 °C, and substantially decreased fluorescence at temperatures at 40 °C and above (~ 3.5% of the maximum value at 40 °C, ~ 1.3% at 50 °C), whereas ccGFP retained 85% of its maximum fluorescence value (also observed at 25 °C) at 37 °C, 53% at 40 °C, and over 9% at 50 °C (Fig. S3a). Regarding pH, neither ccGFP nor GFP showed much fluorescence at pH 5, though ccGFP showed ~ 75% of its maximum fluorescence (observed at pH 8) at pH 6, while GFP showed less than 5% of its maximum fluorescence (also observed at pH 8) at pH 6 (Fig. S3b). Both ccGFP and GFP showed appreciable fluorescence generation at pHs of 7 to 9 (Fig. S3b).

We next sought to evaluate how the engineered ccGFP10 OPT and ccGFP11 tags would affect the solubility of the proteins that they are fused to. We created a set of 8 fusion proteins, whereby 8 test POIs from P. aerophilum46 were inserted between the C-terminus of ccGFP10 and N-terminus of ccGFP (i.e., a ccGFP10-POI-ccGFP11 “sandwich”). These proteins were previously tested with two- and three-body split GFP and two-body split ccGFP, and it was determined that both GFP11 and ccGFP11 caused no significant change of protein solubility after tagging25,29,42. Our solubility tests were performed similarly to as reported previously29. See the bottom key of Fig. 2c for the determined solubilities of the 8 POIs.

The first test of the system was performed using each of the ccGFP10-POI-ccGFP11 protein fusions with the ccGFP1-10 v2 developed by Nguyen, et al.42 (ccGFP1-10 v2) in E. coli colonies (Fig. 2c, top). Co-expression of the two protein components, giving a measurement of the amount of POI expressed, generally showed fluorescent colonies (Fig. 2c, top). Expressing the two components sequentially, whereby the POI protein fusions were first expressed, then expression was halted and protein was allowed to remain soluble or aggregate, followed by expression of the ccGFP1-10 detector, however, showed that the partially soluble and insoluble POIs (e.g., POIs #1, #6, and #8) caused little to no fluorescence in their colonies and a decrease from the co-expression condition (Fig. 2c, top). This was as expected, as co-expression can allow fluorescent protein fragments to associate prior to POI aggregation, and is similar to results performed previously with split two- and three-body GFP systems25,29, particularly with the two-body ccGFP system42. On the other hand, soluble and partially soluble POIs such as #2, #3, and #5 showed a maintained fluorescence (Fig. 2c, top). We believe this indicates that the ccGFP10 and ccGFP11 tags do not significantly affect POI solubility. We do additionally note that while the ccGFP11 tag stays the same, the ccGFP10 tag used in this study differs from the one used by Nguyen, et al. by a single mutation, in similar experiments with the two-body ccGFP system42. See Fig. S1 for these sequences.

We next tested the utility of the engineered tripartite ccGFP system using a three-body complementation setup. Here, the ccGFP10-POI-ccGFP11 protein fusions were complemented with ccGFP1-9 OPT in E. coli cells (Fig. 2c, bottom). Using a similar experimental procedure as above, we first co-expressed the two complementation components and saw, as above, that many of the POIs showed successful complementation, with fluorescent colonies (Fig. 2c, bottom). Conducting the expression of the components sequentially (as above), to allow the POI complex to remain soluble or aggregate, we again saw similar results. Several of the insoluble POIs (such as POIs #1, #6, and #8) saw diminished fluorescence, while essentially all of the soluble or partially soluble POIs showed fluorescent colonies (e.g., #2, #3, #4, and #5) (Fig. 2c, bottom). All said, we believe this further demonstrates the ability of our engineered tripartite split ccGFP system to act as a PPI sensor without significantly affecting the solubilities of the POIs tagged with ccGFP10 and ccGFP11.

Validating the protein-protein interaction sensor with a coiled-coil model system

To validate the ability of the developed tripartite ccGFP sensor system to detect PPIs, we chose to test it on a model coiled-coil system43. Here, two pairs of oppositely-charged coiled coils, E1 and K1, were chosen to be fused to either ccGFP10 or ccGFP11. Based on the charges of the two interacting coils, there would be either attractive interactions (i.e., between E1 and K1) or repulsive interactions (i.e., between E1 and E1)25,43. The particular coil system was chosen due to fast association of the oppositely charged coils and low rates of association between E1 coils25,43. Hence, by combining E1-ccGFP11 with either ccGFP10-E1 or ccGFP10-K1, and complementing the fragments with ccGFP1-9 OPT, we could validate the use of tripartite split ccGFP sensor in detecting the interactions between these coiled-coils.

The constructs 6xHis-E1-ccGFP11, ccGFP10-E1-6xHis, ccGFP10-K1-6xHis, and ccGFP1-9 OPT (without a 6xHis tag) were expressed in E. coli cells, then were purified and quantified as indicated in the Methods. A short linker (ETDVGSGVDSGS) was inserted between the GFP tags and the K1 or E1 coils. In our initial test, we mixed E1-ccGFP11 with either ccGFP10-E1 or ccGFP10-K1 and an excess of the detector, ccGFP1-9 OPT. Measuring the fluorescence of the two systems, either E/E or K/E, over time, demonstrated that the tripartite split ccGFP system was able to detect and quantify the preferential association of the oppositely charged K/E system vs. the E/E system (Fig. 3a). Within 15 min, the K/E system began to show fluorescence, rapidly detecting the PPI interaction (Fig. 3a, inset), whereas the E/E system showed comparatively less fluorescence over 15 h (Fig. 3a), likely due to background fluorescence as a result of tripartite self-assembling.

We next varied the amount of the E1-ccGFP11, while keeping the ccGFP1-9 OPT detector and ccGFP10-E1 or ccGFP10-K1 fusion concentrations constant, to evaluate the sensitivity of the detection system. As shown in Fig. 3b (inset), the system was able to detect concentrations of E1 as low as 12.5 nM as it associated with the K1 coiled-coil after 1 h. The fluorescence generated by the detection system increased significantly (with a generally linear response) with increasing E1 coil concentration, showcasing the ability of the system to detect PPI over a range of concentrations, though it was also able to detect latent E/E homodimerization, especially at high E coil concentrations (where these interactions may be more prevalent) (Fig. 3b).

We validated a second iteration of the K/E coil system, where we used a longer linker length (29 residues: ETDVGSGVDSHMGGGSGSGGGSGGESTSG) between the E1/K1 coil and the ccGFP10/ccGFP11 tags. Performing a second round of experiments, we observed generally similar results as above (Fig. S4). Kinetically, within 15 min, the sensor was able to detect and quantify the association between the K1 and E1 coils, and significant fluorescence signal developed within 1 h compared to the non-preferential E/E interaction (Fig. S4a). However, the second iteration of the system was less sensitive, requiring 100 nM of E1 coil for detection (Fig. S4). Hence, choice of linker length may be an important consideration in detecting PPIs, to ensure that the POIs are able to effectively associate.

We further used the K/E coil system to test how the kinetics of this model system would change when the detector fragment, ccGFP1-9 OPT, was not in excess. Holding ccGFP10-K1 and E1-ccGFP11 constant, at 2 µM and 1 µM, respectively, (Fig. S5a) and at 6 µM and 3 µM, respectively (Fig. S5b), we saw both kinetic rate of detection and maximum fluorescence decreased as ccGFP1-9 OPT was decreased from 8 µM to 0.0625 µM. This agrees with our expectations that as ccGFP1-9 OPT becomes more limiting, the rate at which it complements to the ccGFP10-K1/E1-ccGFP11 complex decreases. Further, there becomes a point at which ccGFP1-9 OPT is the stoichiometric limiting reagent and completely saturated, resulting in decreased maximum fluorescence due to free ccGFP10-K1/E1-ccGFP11 complex. However, the kinetics of the association appears predictable. For example, increasing the concentrations of ccGFP10-K1 and E1-ccGFP11 by 3-fold and maintaining ccGFP1-9 OPT in excess results in ~ 3-fold higher maximum fluorescence (Fig. S5).

Validating the protein-protein interaction sensor with an inducible heterodimerization model system

To further validate our tripartite split ccGFP PPI sensor system, we tested it on the rapamycin (RAP) inducible interaction between (i) a domain of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) kinase, the FKBP12-rapamycin binding domain (FRB), and (ii) the FK506 binding protein (FKBP12), hereafter referred to as the FRB/FKBP12 system44. We created two fusion proteins, ccGFP10 fused to FRB (i.e., ccGFP10-FRB) and ccGFP11 fused to FKBP (i.e., FKBP12-ccGFP11), which could be detected with the ccGFP1-9 OPT fragment. Using the FRB/FKBP12 system, we sought to detect and monitor the RAP-mediated interaction between the two proteins, demonstrating the ability of the tripartite split ccGFP sensor to measure protein interactions, specifically in response to a small molecule modulator. In this model system, the FRB and FKBP12 proteins would associate in the presence of RAP, forming the FRB-FKBP12 complex and allowing the ccGFP10 and ccGFP11 fragments to be brought into proximity. Upon complementation with ccGFP1-9 OPT, fluorescence would be generated (Fig. 4a, top). However, without exogenous RAP, the FRB/FKBP12 complex would not preferentially form, preventing functional ccGFP from forming, resulting in no fluorescent signal generated (Fig. 4a, bottom).

Without rapamycin ligand binding, ccGFP does not reconstitute, resulting in limited fluorescence signal (bottom). (d) Rapamycin dose response curve (0.1 to 2500 nM) for FRB/FKPB12 binding in vitro, measured as final fluorescence after 1 h. The black line shows a model fit to the Hill equation. As appropriate, points/bars show average of n = 2 measurements, with error bars showing ± 1 S.D.

We expressed the fusion proteins and incubated them with ccGFP1-9 OPT in the presence of 150 nM RAP or in the absence of RAP (adding pure DMSO only). Monitoring the fluorescence over 15 h, we saw that the fluorescence signal generated by the (+) RAP condition was rapid, while the fluorescence of the (-) RAP condition remained at low, basal levels (Fig. 4b). Within 15 min, the PPI sensor was able to detect the RAP-mediated formation of the FRB/FKBP12 complex (Fig. 4b, inset) and fluorescence continued to increase up to at least 15 h for the (+) RAP condition, whereas the (-) RAP condition showed a slow plateauing, indicating non-preferential complex formation. Increasing the concentration of the ccGFP10-FRB and FKBP12-ccGFP11 constructs, we observed an enhancement in the dynamic range between the (+) RAP and (-) RAP conditions, with the highest, a 17.6-fold increase, at 320 nM of each (Fig. 4c). This agrees with our expectations, as at higher concentrations of ccGFP10-FRB and FKBP12-ccGFP11, there will be more functional ccGFP association in the presence of RAP, generating higher fluorescence. However, in the absence of RAP, this association appeared non-preferential, which resulted in only slight increases in fluorescence as fragment concentration was increased. We posit that this is primarily attributed to increased probability of collisions between fragments at higher concentrations of tripartite self-assembling.

To further characterize the system, we sought to test the sensor’s ability to monitor highly-sensitive, dose responsive interactions between the interacting protein partners. Maintaining the concentrations of ccGFP10-FRB, FKBP12-ccGFP11, and ccGFP1-9 OPT and measuring the fluorescence after 1 h of incubation with varying concentrations of RAP verified the expected, dose responsive behavior of the RAP-mediated PPI (Fig. 4d). Increasing the RAP concentration from 0 to 10 nM appeared to result in little FRB/FKBP12 complex formation, while beyond 10 nM, complex formation appeared highly-efficient as fluorescence exponentially increased. Around 1000 nM, however, the fluorescence plateaued, presumably because of limited free FRB and FKBP12 fragments as the complex became saturated with RAP.

Single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) for split ccGFP

Prior to development and analysis of single-chain variable fragments (scFvs), we first assembled the full-length tripartite ccGFP (ccGFPtri) construct by combining the sequences of ccGFP1-9 OPT with ccGFP10 OPT and ccGFP11. We then purified ccGFPtri by size exclusion chromatography (SEC). Chromatograms before and after SEC purification are shown in Fig. S6. To develop scFvs that had high affinity and specificity for ccGFPtri, we pursued a selection process using a combination of phage and yeast display, beginning with a previously-reported naïve human library47,48. We first proceeded through three rounds of phage selection. In the first two rounds, 300 nM of biotinylated ccGFPtri were used for selection, whereas in the third round, selection for higher affinity was increased by reducing the ccGFPtri concentration to 100 nM. Following isolation of scFvs selected from the phage library, amplified sequences were then transformed into yeast via homologous recombination into a surface display vector. After growth and induction, the yeast display library was incubated with biotinylated ccGFPtri. Two rounds of yeast display selection were used, with the first round including incubation with 100 nM ccGFPtri and the second using 30 nM ccGFPtri. Following staining for scFv display (anti-PE signal) and antigen binding (streptavidin AlexaFluor 633), the cells were sorted. Plasmids were isolated, transformed into E. coli, and sequenced, with selected scFvs (C4 and E1) produced as scFv Fc fusion proteins (Biointron) for analysis. Both resultant scFvs produced using the selection scheme had similar sequences (Fig. S7, Table S4).

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) was used to characterize the kinetics of antibody binding to ccGFPtri (see Methods). Both anti-ccGFPtri fusion proteins showed high specificity and affinity for ccGFPtri, with little interaction with off-target proteins, based on ELISA results (Fig. S8). C4 and E1 both showed specific interactions with ccGFPtri and not with tripartite GFP (GFPtri) or myoglobin. As a control, we tested GBP237,41, which we found to be specific to GFPtri, as expected. At low scFv concentrations (up to 20 nM, Fig. S8a, b), C4 and E1 did not show affinity for ccGFP1-9 OPT, but at concentrations of 300 nM, we observed off-target binding to ccGFP1-9 OPT by both scFvs (Fig. S8c). The ka, kd, and KD values of the C4 and E1 anti-ccGFPtri scFv Fc fusion proteins interacting with ccGFPtri are shown in Table 1. Representative sensorgrams from the Carterra LSA system are shown for C4 and E1 in Fig. S9a and Fig. S9b, respectively.

Discussion

We describe here the successful engineering of a tripartite split ccGFP PPI sensor based on the GFP from C. californica. We were motivated by previous successes with engineering two- and three-body split GFP systems, where self-assembly of functional fluorescent proteins was enabled by the tagging of small β-strands to POIs coupled with the use of a larger detector fragment consisting of the remaining β-strands of GFP25,28,42. This is in contrast to other BiFC systems, which have historically been prone to aggregation and interference with the folding of POIs due to the large, bulky tags used25–27. Our two- and three-body split fluorescent protein sensor systems have used small tags, less than 20 amino acids in length, mitigating their effects on the folding of the proteins to which they are fused25,28,42. The small tags allowed the sensor systems to find widespread application in measuring protein expression, solubility, and folding; tracking protein localization; and, as discussed, detecting protein-protein interactions27,45. This has included applications in detecting protein-RNA49,50 and virus-host cell51 interactions; monitoring cell signaling52, protein trafficking and compartmentalization53–55, formation of neuron gap junctions56, virus formation57, and entry of exogenous molecules into cells58; labeling proteins in live cells30,59; facilitating creation of assembled nanostructures60 and protein crystals61; and quantifying POIs without needing laborious protein purification, enabling the development of high-throughput screening assays62,63. This repertoire of applications for split fluorescent proteins reinforces our engineered tripartite split ccGFP system to function as a robust PPI sensor in vitro and in live cells, in a variety of systems and hosts.

We demonstrated here the applicability of the tripartite split GFP system to a range of protein of interest targets. As with other engineered split fluorescent protein systems25,28,42 (and in contrast to the issues experienced by other BiFC systems25–27, the ccGFP10 and ccGFP11 tags did not appear to significantly interfere with or alter the solubility of the proteins of interest. The 8 test proteins originated from a host other than E. coli and spanned a range of protein molecular weights, solubilities, expression levels, etc. (Table S3); in other words, they were representative of potential POIs. Further, we demonstrated utility of the tripartite split GFP to function in two model systems, detecting static and dynamic interactions, namely as a PPI sensor with the attractive and repulsive interactions between charged E and K coil-coiled domains43 and the chemical-mediated heterodimerization of FRB and FKBP1244. The sensor detected PPIs rapidly and with high sensitivity, down to ~ 10 nM. While not all inclusive, we posit that this breadth of successful uses with the ccGFP system indicates that it could be employed in many other systems off-the-shelf, with minimal experimentation and troubleshooting required.

Toward creating a new, orthogonal tripartite split GFP PPI sensor, we chose the C. californica GFP scaffold due to previous findings that the engineered two-body split ccGFP sensor showed three-fold improved complementation kinetics compared to two-body split A. victoria GFP system, as well as notable tolerance at lower pHs42. We saw these properties reflected in the tripartite systems, as well, with the tripartite split ccGFP system having greater rate of fluorescence development compared to the A. victoria tripartite GFP system (Fig. 2b) and higher tolerance to pHs (Fig. S3b) and temperatures (Fig. S3a). Kinetically, for assays requiring maximal fluorescence signal, the same results that would require 15 h or more with the GFP system could be accomplished in 1–2 h with the ccGFP system (Fig. 2b). A PPI sensor that can function at a range of conditions is particularly advantageous, as the detection could be reliably performed with proteins and expression systems that may be required to be at more extreme pHs and temperatures or can be performed in vitro in the field without disruption from environmental conditions. Currently, the original tripartite split GFP system’s function declines significantly at temperatures of 37 °C (physiological temperature) and above, whereas the tripartite split ccGFP system retains much of its function at 37 °C, with a less dramatic decline at higher temperatures (Fig. S3a). With that said, further engineering could be pursued to develop a tripartite system that is functional at more extreme conditions, depending on the desired application, potentially using a ccGFP scaffold that has improved stability and tolerance to denaturation45.

The development of the anti-tripartite ccGFP scFvs enhances the flexibility and utility of the split ccGFP system. By coupling the scFv with the PPI sensor system, interacting protein complexes can be visualized and tracked by readouts that do not rely only on the fluorescence generated by the reconstituted ccGFP. This allows for signal amplification and/or switching the detection method to one more favorable for the system and experimental conditions, such as pH/acidity64, presence of oxygen33, and fixing/staining reagents35. The anti-tripartite ccGFP scFv allows the reconstituted tripartite ccGFP to be used as a versatile molecular “handle,” which could aid in such applications as recovery or pulldown of PPI complexes and quantification of ccGFP-PPI complexes using ELISAs. We posit that the anti-tripartite ccGFP scFv provides users a means to more fully study and manipulate the interacting partners in detected PPIs and opens opportunities for future developments.

Further, we demonstrate that the anti-ccGFP scFvs reported here have high specificity. Even at high concentrations (Fig. S8c), these scFvs do not show any interaction with other fluorescent proteins (tripartite GFP) or unrelated proteins (myoglobin). These results suggest that these scFvs can be used to detect or enhance the signal from ccGFP in complex systems, such as cellular systems or protein mixtures. The ability to detect tripartite ccGFP in preference to ccGFP1-9 OPT would provide direct interrogation into the presence and kinetics of split ccGFP complementation. While some binding of the scFv Fc fusion proteins to ccGFP1-9 OPT was observed, we note that this interaction was observed mainly at high scFv concentrations. At lower concentrations of scFv Fc fusion protein, the scFvs reported here show the desired preference for full-length tripartite ccGFP.

The tripartite split ccGFP system is readily able to be adapted as a high-throughput method to rapidly test large libraries of proteins, protein interfaces, and domains for interactions65. Coupled with its versatility to screen in vitro at a range of conditions, the sensor system has the potential to have broad impact in proteomics and the construction of the protein-protein interactome, as well as in related applications such as screening small molecule PPI inhibitors for therapeutic applications. As we demonstrated that ccGFP did not experience crosstalk with other fluorescent protein sensors42, use of the tripartite split ccGFP system could potentially facilitate multiplexed characterization of protein interactions in live cells66 and/or be made ultra-high-throughput using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)67.

Methods

Engineering of tripartite split-ccGFP

Using ccGFP_7 construct45 that was previously created by assembling ccGFP1-10 v2 and ccGFP11 v1 fragments42 as starting scaffold, we first created three different constructs for ccGFP 1–9 ending at three different junction points (amino acids: 1-186, 1-187, 1-188) with three corresponding ccGFP10-11 constructs (amino acids: 187–221, 188–221, 189–221). ccGFP10-11 constructs were fused to the C-terminus of a soluble protein, sulfite reductase (SR) with a linker (GSDGGSGGGSTS) in between. SR-ccGFP10-11 was ligated into our in-house engineered pTET-SpecR with ColE1 origin, (which expresses the tetR regulator), where expression is regulated by addition of anhydrotetracycline (AnTET). ccGFP 1–9 constructs were ligated into our modified p15A vector inducible with isopropyl-β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) with kanamycin resistance marker. In vivo co-expression assays in E. coli cells were performed by inducing colonies with both 350 ng/ml AnTET and 1 mM IPTG.

SR protein was inserted between ccGFP10 and ccGFP11 with a linker before (GTDVGSGGGS) and a linker after (GSGGGSGGGSTS) amino sequence to create ccGFP10-SR-GFP11 construct. In vivo co-expression assays in E. coli cells were performed as mentioned above. The ccGFP1-9 wildtype (residues 1-188) was selected to be evolved to improve solubility and folding by directed evolution. The DNA coding for ccGFP1-9 WT was PCR amplified using vector flanking primers and was subjected to DNA fragmentation and shuffling using published protocols, generating library of ~ 105 variants per round of evolution. The library of DNA plasmids was transformed into E. coliBL21 (DE3) gold (Novagen) competent cells containing the pTET plasmid with ccGFP10-SR-GFP11 construct cloned in for co-expression testing. The 1.0 OD600nm cell stock frozen library was diluted by two sequential 400-fold dilutions and 1 ml plated on each of 5 supported nitrocellulose membranes resting on 150 mm diameter Bauer plates containing Luria–Bertani (LB) agar supplemented with 50 µg kanamycin/ml and 75 µg spectinomycin/ml. Colonies with brighter fluorescence were visualized and selected after co-induction with 350 ng/ml AnTET and 1 mM IPTG using an Illumatool Lighting System (LightTools Research) equipped with a 488 nm excitation filter and a colored glass filter (520 nm long pass, LightTools Research). Throughout the course of directed evolution, increasing selection pressure, in this case, a shorter time period from induction to colony selection was applied. After 3 rounds of directed evolution, we obtained ccGFP1-9 v1 containing 3 mutations compared to ccGFP1-9 WT: M3I, R85Q, and V109A.

ccGFP10-ccGFP11 construct was engineered to improve complementation efficiency with ccGFP1-9 v1. A long linker (GTDVGSGGGSHMGGGSGSGGGSGGGSTS) was inserted between the C-terminus of ccGFP10 and the N-terminus of ccGFP11 and the construct was cloned into pTET-SpecR vector. The DNA coding for ccGFP10-linker-ccGFP11 was PCR amplified using vector flanking primers and was subjected to DNA fragmentation and shuffling using published protocols. The library of DNA plasmids was transformed into E. coliBL21 (DE3) gold (Novagen) competent cells containing the pET p15 plasmid with ccGFP1-9 v1 construct cloned in for co-expression testing. Colonies with brighter fluorescence compared to the starting templates were selected after co-induction with 350 ng/ml AnTET and 1 mM IPTG. After 3 rounds of directed evolution, we selected ccGFP10 OPT containing one mutation compared to ccGFP10 WT: S202Y, and a new linker sequence containing four mutations compared to the original linker: ETDVGSGVDSHMGGGSGSGGGSGGESTS.

ccGFP1-9 v1 was subjected to two more rounds of directed evolution against the new sequence of ccGFP10 OPT and ccGFP11. SR was cloned in between ccGFP10 OPT and ccGFP11 with one linker (ETDVGSGVDSH) before and one linker (GSGGGSGGESTS) after its sequence and the construct was cloned in pTET-SpecR vector. The DNA coding ccGFP1-9 v1 was PCR amplified and was subjected to DNA fragmentation, shuffling, and library transformation as indicated above. Colonies with brighter fluorescence were selected and after two rounds of directed evolution, we obtained the final version of ccGFP1-9, ccGFP1-9OPT, that contains four mutations compared to the ccGFP1-9 WT: M3N, L44V, R85Q, and V109A (Fig. S1c).

Expression and refolding of ccGFP1-9 OPT

The ccGFP1-9 OPT protein fragment was isolated using an adapted protocol previously described for GFP1-1025,28. ccGFP1-9 OPT was transformed into BL21(DE3) E. coli competent cells and grown in 4 mL selective LB media with 50 µg/mL kanamycin. The cell culture was incubated overnight at 30 °C while shaking and used to inoculate 1 L of selective LB media. The 1 L culture for ccGFP1-9 OPT was grown at 37 °C while shaking until reaching an OD600 of 0.6–0.8. Cells were then induced with 1 mM IPTG for protein expression and incubated for an additional 5 h at 37 °C. Cell pellets were harvested by centrifugation following expression. To collect inclusion bodies containing the ccGFP1-9 OPT protein, the cell pellets were resuspended in 20 mL of TNG, sonicated for 8 min at 4 °C, and centrifuged for 30 min at 15,500 rcf. Inclusion bodies were washed, resuspended, and aliquoted to obtain 75 mg/mL pellet samples according to previously described method29. The protein from prepared inclusion bodies was refolded using 9 M urea in TNG buffer and then diluted with 25 mL TNG buffer and filtered. The refolded protein was quantified using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoScientific) and diluted to obtain a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL.

Expression and purification of proteins fused to ccGFP tags

ccGFP10 and ccGFP11 tags were fused to N-terminus or C-terminus of K1-coil and E1-coil with either a short (ETDVGSGVDSGS) or a long (ETDVGSGVDSHMGGGSGSGGGSGGESTSG) linker between them43. These linker sequences were selected based on this study and previous work25. ccGFP10-K1, ccGFP10-E1 fusion proteins were cloned into a pET vector containing a C-terminal 6-His tag while E1-ccGFP11 construct was cloned into a pET vector with a N-terminal 6-His tag. These protein constructs were then expressed and purified using Talon metal affinity resin as previously reported29,45. The purified proteins were eluted from the column with 250 mM imidazole in TNG buffer and was buffer exchange into TNG buffer using a centrifugal filter device (Millipore). The final protein concentration was quantified using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoScientific).

Similarly, FRB was expressed as C-terminal fusion to ccGFP10 tag and cloned into a pET vector containing a C-terminal 6-His tag while FKBP12 was fused to the N-terminus of ccGFP11 and cloned into a pET vector with a N-terminal 6-His tag. A short linker (ETDVGSGVDSGGGGS) was inserted between the protein and ccGFP tags. These proteins were expressed, purified, and exchanged to TNG buffer using published protocols45. Protein concentration was quantified as indicated above.

In vitro complementation assay for tripartite split-ccGFP and split-GFP

In vitro complementation assays were carried out for the final optimized split-ccGFP and split-GFP systems using previously described protocols28. Briefly, a 96-well white microplate (Nunc-Immuno plate, Nunc) was first blocked with 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in TNG for 10 min. 180 µL of refolded ccGFP1-9 OPT or GFP1-9 OPT in TNG buffer, pH 7.4 (normalized to the same concentration of 0.5 mg/mL) was added to 20 µL of the SR constructs (either ccGFP10-SR-ccGFP11 obtained in this study or GFP10-SR-GFP11 from previous work25 at [0.5 mg/ml]. Fluorescence kinetics (488 nm excitation/520 nm emission) were monitored with a Tecan Microplate Fluorescence Reader (TECAN) at 3 min intervals for 15 h. The background fluorescence of a blank sample (20 µl of 0.5% BSA in TNG buffer, 180 µl of 0.5 mg/ml ccGFP1-9 OPT or GFP1-9 OPT in TNG buffer) was subtracted from the final fluorescence values.

Following a previously described protocol for split-GFP25, for K1/E1 and E1/E1 protein interactions, 25 µL of ccGFP10-K1 or ccGFP10-E1 (at 6.25 µM) was mixed with 50 µL of E1-ccGFP11 prepared at a range of concentrations (from 0.4 nM to 800 nM) and, immediately following, 150 µL of ccGFP1-9 OPT was added to each well. Fluorescence kinetics (488 nm excitation/520 nm emission) were monitored with a Tecan Microplate Fluorescence Reader at 3 min intervals for 15 h. To study how different concentrations of GFP1-9 OPT affect the kinetic assay, two-fold serial dilutions of GFP1–9 OPT were added to the protein mixtures containing ccGFP10-K1 and E1-ccGFP11 at the same final concentration of 1 µM each25.

Inducible FRB/FKBP protein interactions were performed using protocols previously published25. Briefly, 20 µL of ccGFP10-FRB was mixed with 20 µL of FKBP-ccGFP11 at 0.5 mg/ml concentrations and either 10 µL of TNG buffer or rapamycin diluted in DMSO was added to the final concentration of 150 nM. Fluorescence kinetics (488 nm excitation/520 nm emission) using a Tecan Microplate Fluorescence Reader at 3 min intervals for 15 h were started right after the addition of a large excess of 150 µL of ccGFP1-9 OPT at 0.5 mg/ml. The background fluorescence of a blank sample (50 µL of TNG buffer + 150 µL of ccGFP1-9 OPT at 0.5 mg/ml) was subtracted from the final fluorescence values. To study how sensitive the fluorescence signals are at various protein concentrations, ccGFP10-FRB/FKBP-ccGFP11 were assayed at 320, 160, and 40 nM/each with an addition of rapamycin to a final concentration of 150 nM. Fluorescence kinetics were recorded as mentioned above after the addition of 150 µL of ccGFP1-9 OPT at 0.5 mg/ml. In addition, rapamycin dose curve was also investigated at a wide range of concentration from 0.1 to 2500 nM measured as fluorescence after 1 h of incubation with ccGFP10-FRB and FKBP-ccGFP11 at 400 nM each.

In vivo solubility screen of 8 control proteins using tripartite split-ccGFP

Eight control proteins from P. aerophilum46 were cloned into our in-house pTET vector with N-terminal ccGFP10 and C-terminal ccGFP11 fragments to create GFP10-POI-GFP11 constructs. The fusion proteins were transformed into BL21(DE3) E. coli competent cells containing either the ccGFP1-9 OPT or ccGFP1-10 v2 in pET p15 plasmid and expressed in LB media with 50 µg/mL kanamycin and 75 µg/mL spectinomycin. Two consecutive 400-fold dilutions were prepared for cell cultures at OD600 = 1, plated on selective LB agar with a nitrocellulose membrane, and grown overnight at 30 °C. Cell colonies were then either sequentially or co-induced according to methods previously described for split-GFP25. After induction, plates were illuminated using an Illumatool Lighting System (LightTools Research) with a 488 nm excitation filter and imaged with a D3500 digital camera (Nikon) and 520 nm long-pass filter.

Temperature and pH dependent studies for tripartite split-ccGFP

To measure the effects of pH on in vitro complementation, refolded ccGFP1-9 OPT and GFP1-9 OPT were dialyzed against buffers covering a range of pH values (5.0, 6.0, 7.0, 7.4, 8.0, and 9.0). The concentrations were normalized to 0.25 mg/mL after dialysis. At each pH variation of ccGFP1-9 OPT or GFP1-9 OPT solutions, 180 µL of refolded protein was added to 20 µL of the respective ccGFP10-SR-ccGFP11 fragment (~ 0.5 mg/ml).

Temperature dependent in vitro complementation assays were set up using the same protocol described for pH studies. After combining 180 µL of ccGFP1-9 OPT or GFP1-9 OPT refolded in TNG pH 7.4 buffer (0.5 mg/mL) with 20 µL of the corresponding S10-SR-S11 fusion protein (0.5 mg/mL), complementation reactions were incubated overnight (18 h) at 4 °C, 25 °C, 30 °C, 37 °C, 40 °C, 45 °C, and 50 °C. For both pH and temperature dependent complementation assays, fluorescence (488 nm excitation/520 nm emission) was measured using a Tecan Microplate Fluorescence Reader after samples incubated overnight for 18 h.

Size-exclusion chromatography

Prior to use in single-chain variable fragment (scFvs) selections and analysis, tripartite ccGFP was further purified by size exclusion chromatography (SEC). SEC was performed using an AKTA GO (Cytiva 29383015) connected to a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva 28990944). Purification was performed in a running buffer of 1x PBS supplemented with 200 mM arginine at a flow rate of 0.5 mL / min. Fractions with a volume of 2 mL were collected. Protein fractions corresponding to monomer were determined via comparison to molecular sizing standards (BioRad 1511901). Monomeric tripartite ccGFP was then buffer exchanged into TNG buffer using Zeba Spin Desalting Columns 7 K MWCO (Thermo Scientific 89892). Tripartite ccGFP was biotinylated using EZ-Link NHS-LC-LC Biotinylation Kit (Thermo Scientific #21342). Tripartite ccGFP and biotinylated ccGFP were aliquoted, snap frozen with liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C until use.

Selection of ccGFP specific ScFv

Single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) specific for ccGFP were selected using a combination of phage and yeast display from a previously reported naïve human library47. Three rounds of phage selection were performed. In the first two rounds, 300 nM biotinylated tripartite ccGFP was used, and in the third round, 100 nM biotinylated tripartite ccGFP was used. Phage were first blocked with 2% BSA for approximately 30 min. Biotinylated tripartite ccGFP was then added to a final concentration of 300 nM or 100 nM. Phage were incubated with end-over-end rotation at room temperature for approximately 30 min and then incubated without rotation for approximately 90 min. Streptavidin Dynabeads (Invitrogen 11047) were prepared by washing twice with PBS + 0.1% TWEEN (PBST), incubating for 30 min with 2% BSA, and resuspending in PBST. Beads were then added to phage incubations using a KingFisher Sample Purification System (ThermoFisher #5400000). Automated bead washing was then performed using the KingFisher. Beads were washed twice with PBST and three times with PBS. Bound phage were then eluted through incubation of the beads with either 3 µM (rounds 1 and 2) or 1 µM (round 3) tripartite ccGFP (not labeled with biotin) or 0.1 N HCl. One Shot OmniMax 2 T1 chemically competent cells (Invitrogen C8540-03) were cultured at 37 °C in 2XYT medium supplemented with 3% glucose and 0.015 µg/mL tetracycline to an OD600 of approximately 0.5. Eluted phage were incubated with OmniMax at 37 °C for 30 min. Infected OmniMax cells were then centrifuged at 3750 rpm for 10 min. OmniMax cells were then resuspended and plated on 2XYT agarose plates supplemented with 3% glucose and 0.05 µg/mL carbenicillin. Plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C. OmniMax cells were collected. Following the first and second selections, enriched phage were harvested from OmniMax and used for the subsequent round of selection. Following the third selection, plasmid DNA was isolated using a QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (Qiagen 27104).

Enriched scFv sequences were amplified by PCR. Amplified scFv sequences were then transformed into EBY100 along with linear yeast surface display vector. Transformed yeast were cultured in SDCAA media at 30 °C for 2 d. Two additional selections were performed by yeast surface display. Prior to each selection, yeast cells were grown in SDCAA media at 30 °C for 1 d and induced in SGRCAA media at 20 °C for 2 d. For each selection, 107 yeast cells were washed twice with PBS supplemented 0.5% BSA and 2 mM EDTA (YWB). Cells were then incubated with biotinylated tripartite ccGFP for approximately 3 h at room temperature with end-over-end mixing. For the first selection, 100 nM tripartite ccGFP was used, and for the second selection 30 nM tripartite ccGFP was used. Cells were then washed once with YWB. Cells were incubated at 1:1000 anti-SV5 PE and 1:200 streptavidin AlexaFluor 633 (Invitrogen S21375) at 4 °C for 30 min. Cells were washed once. The cells were then sorted using a Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX SRT Cell Sorter (Beckman Coulter C37808AC). Sorted cells were grown at 30 °C for 2 d. Following the final selection, plasmid DNA was extracted using a Zymoprep Yeast Plasmid Miniprep II kit (Zymo Research D2004). Isolated plasmids were transformed into XL1-Blue Electroporation Competent Cells (Agilent 200228) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Individual colonies were selected and grown in LB media supplemented with carbenicillin at 0.05 µg/mL at 37 °C overnight. Plasmids were extracted using a QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (Qiagen 27104). The sequences of selected scFvs were determined by Sanger sequencing. Selected scFv sequences were produced as scFv Fc fusion proteins by Biointron for further analysis.

Measurement of scFv Fc fusion affinity for ccGFP by surface plasmon resonance

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) capture kinetics experiments were conducted using the Carterra LSA system equipped with an HC30M sensor chip (30 nm linear polycarboxylate surface, Carterra #4279). For surface preparation, both the single flow cell (SFC) and 96-channel printhead (96PH) were primed with activation HBSTE buffer (10 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM EDTA, and 0.05% Tween-20).

Capture surfaces were generated within the SFC via standard amine coupling of goat anti-human IgG Fc (SouthernBiotech #2047-01), creating a uniform “lawn” across the chip. The sensor surface was first activated by injecting a freshly prepared 1:1:1 (v/v/v) mixture of 0.4 M EDC (Carterra #9094), 0.1 M N-hydroxysulfosuccinimide (SNHS; ThermoFisher Scientific #24510), and 0.1 M MES (pH 5.5; Carterra #3625) for 7 min. Goat anti-human IgG Fc was then diluted to 50 µg/mL in 10 mM sodium acetate (pH 4.5; Carterra #3622) and coupled for 10 min. Remaining active esters were quenched with a 5-minute injection of 150 mM ethylenediamine-borate (pH 8.5; Sigma #E1521, ThermoFisher Scientific #28341).

Anti-ccGFP scFv Fc fusion proteins were prepared at concentrations ranging from 6.8 ng/mL to 5 µg/mL in 1x HBSTE running buffer and captured onto individual spots for 15 min using the 96PH. Samples were printed two times to produce a ligand array, yielding 14 spots per monoclonal antibody (mAb).

Subsequently, the SFC was docked over the array, and the running buffer was supplemented with 2 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA) for the interaction analysis, conducted at 25 °C. Recombinant tripartite ccGFP was prepared at concentrations of 0, 0.9, 3.9, 15.6, 62.5, 250, and 1000 nM and injected as analytes for 5 min each, followed by a 20-minute dissociation phase. Analytes were injected in ascending concentration order without regeneration between injections.

Binding signals from local reference spots (interspots coated with only the capture reagent) were subtracted from active spot signals (Regions of Interest, ROIs), and nearest buffer blank injections were used for double referencing. Double-referenced sensorgrams were globally fit to a simple 1:1 Langmuir binding model using Carterra’s Kinetics software to determine the association rate constant (ka), dissociation rate constant (kd), and affinity constant (KD = kd/ka). Sensorgrams from ligand spots yielding Rmax values between 15 and 100 RU were included and treated as replicates for final kinetic parameter reporting.

Measurement of scFv Fc fusion specificity for ccGFP by ELISA

Proteins of interest (ccGFPtri, ccGFP 1–9, GFPtri, and myoglobin) were loaded onto a black Maxisorp plate (NUNC #437111) at a total mass of 1 µg per well. Proteins were allowed to immobilize overnight at 4 °C. All wells were then blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for approximately 1 h. The plate was then washed three times with PBS. Antibody dilutions (C4, E1, and GBP2) were prepared in PBS and then added to the plate. Antibodies were incubated at room temperature for approximately 3 h. The plate was then washed three times with PBST (PBS + 0.1% TWEEN-20) and three times with PBS. A 1:1000 dilution of anti-human Alexa Fluor 582 antibody (Invitrogen) was then prepared in PBS and incubated in all wells for approximately 1 h. The plate was then washed three times with PBST and three times with PBS. PBS was added to all wells and fluorescent signal was measured using an Infinite 200 Pro Fluorescence Reader (TECAN) with excitation at 560 nm and emission measured at 610 nm. Two replicates were performed.
