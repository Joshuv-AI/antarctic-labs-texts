An Efficient Variant of One-Class SVM with Lifelong Online Learning Guarantees
Joe Suk, Samory Kpotufe

Abstract
We study outlier (a.k.a., anomaly) detection for single-pass non-stationary streaming data. In the well-studied offline or batch outlier detection problem, traditional methods such as kernel One-Class SVM (OCSVM) are both computationally heavy and prone to large false-negative (Type II) errors under non-stationarity. To remedy this, we introduce SONAR, an efficient SGD-based OCSVM solver with strongly convex regularization. We show novel theoretical guarantees on the Type I/II errors of SONAR, superior to those known for OCSVM, and further prove that SONAR ensures favorable lifelong learning guarantees under benign distribution shifts. In the more challenging problem of adversarial non-stationary data, we show that SONAR can be used within an ensemble method and equipped with changepoint detection to achieve adaptive guarantees, ensuring small Type I/II errors on each phase of data. We validate our theoretical findings on synthetic and real-world datasets.



1  Introduction

We study the problem of outlier detection (a.k.a., novelty or anomaly detection) in a one-pass streaming setting where data arrive in a sequential or online manner. A key motivating example here is the real-life application of “Internet of Things” (IoT), where data consists of network traffic encodings from smart devices which carry signals of unusual or malicious activity.
In such applications, one aims to detect anomalous activity for security and threat identification.
For deployment of algorithms in the IoT problem, a common goal is to design memory and computation efficient algorithms, without needing to store or analyze large datasets, as typical smart devices are resource constrained.

In real-life applications, the underlying environment changes over time due to seasonalities or because streamlined software needs to be deployed across different modalities.
This necessitates procedures which adapt to non-stationary environments, or ensure
lifelong learning
, transferring knowledge across different data distributions.
While supervised transfer learning and non-stationary online learning have been well-studied in the literature, the particular problem of
non-stationary online outlier detection
has received less attention.
Additionally, the outlier detection problem poses unique challenges beyond supervised classification settings as the learner may not have access to outlier data, which can be adversarial in nature and change as the normal data distribution changes.

As is standard in works on outlier detection, our goal is to design procedures which adhere to the Neyman-Pearson framework: ensure small Type I error, or error of misclassifying normal data, while also maintaining small Type II error on unknown outlier data.
Our work rigorously proves such guarantees for a broad class of normal and outlier data distributions with supporting verifications of our theory on both synthetic and real-world IoT datasets.

We focus on a class of procedures based on the One-Class Support Vector Machine (OCSVM)
(
Schölkopf et al. 1999
)
which is a common technique for offline outlier detection inspired by Support-Vector-Machines (SVMs) from supervised learning.
In particular, OCSVM has found widespread usage for outlier detection in IoT settings
(
Shilton et al. 2015
;
Lee et al. 2016
;
Mahdavinejad et al. 2018
;
Al Shorman et al. 2020
;
Razzak et al. 2020
)
.

A first attempt at handling the streaming problem is to note OCSVM’s objective function has a penalty-based formulation amenable to stochastic gradient descent (SGD).
However, since decision boundaries between normal and outlier data are unlikely to be linear for complex datasets, it’s standard to use a kernel trick within OCSVM to embed the data into an infinite-dimensional reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS).
This in turn poses several challenges for the penalty-based formulation, as the gradient updates of SGD depend on the gram matrix of the entire dataset, which is inaccessible in a streaming setting.

Instead, using Random Fourier Features
(
Rahimi and Recht 2007
)
for kernel approximation allow us to work in a finite-dimensional Euclidean approximation of the RKHS, where gradient updates can be made with one-pass streaming.

However, an additional challenge for theoretical analysis is that the objective function of OCSVM is not strongly convex, disallowing standard convergence analyses of the SGD iterates to the population minimizer.
This makes it tricky to conduct a rigorous analysis of the Type I and II errors of the associated SGD iterates.

We resolve this issue by introducing a new strongly convex variant of the OCSVM objective which is also amenable to SGD when using Random Fourier Features.
Going further, we find that our new objective’s solution in fact admits stronger lifelong learning guarantees than the original OCSVM objective, even under unknown changing data distributions.

2  Related Works

Outlier Detection in Changing Environments.

Prior approaches to outlier detection in non-stationary data streams consist of sliding-window techniques, ensemble methods, and data weighting
(
Tan et al. 2011
;
Krawczyk and Woźniak 2015
;
Salehi and Rashidi 2018
)
.
However, theoretical guarantees largely remain unknown for this challenging setting.

In the offline setting, recent works have studied the Neyman-Pearson framework for outlier detection under distribution shift, establishing theoretical guarantees under various types of shifts
(
Kalan and Kpotufe 2024
;
Kalan et al. 2025a
;
Kalan et al. 2025b
)
.
However, these works assume knowledge of source and target data, obviating the need for changepoint detection.

Works on OCSVM.

For theoretical guarantees,
Schölkopf et al. 1999
showed the first generalization error bounds for OCSVM, appealing to the classical margin-based analysis of SVM
(
Bartlett and Shawe-Taylor 1999
)
.
Later,
Vert and Vert 2005
showed consistency results (i.e., that OCSVM asymptotically learns a level set), for a Gaussian kernel with sample-dependent bandwidth.
Related to our work,
Yang et al. 2022
also study the use of kernel data compression (via Nyström sketches) to reduce the time and space evaluation complexity of OCSVM.

OCSVM in
d
-dimensional Euclidean space is also equivalent to the
Minimum Enclosing Ball (MEB) problem

(
Megiddo 1983
)
, a well-studied computational geometry problem.
In the one-pass setting,
Zarrabi-Zadeh and Chan 2006
achieve a
3/2
approximation ratio on the radius of the enclosing ball.
This approximation factor was later improved with accompanying lower bounds showing any algorithm using
\poly(d)
space must incur a constant approximation ratio
(
Agarwal and Sharathkumar 2010
;
Chan and Pathak 2014
)
.
In multi-pass streaming settings, works on the broader online coreset problem showed tighter approximation factors are possible
(
Bădoiu and Clarkson 2008
)
.

Krivosija and Munteanu 2019
study a
probabilistic MEB
problem in an RKHS, where the goal is to minimize expected distance to heterogeneous randomly sampled datapoints, achieving a
1+\varepsilon
approximation ratio using
O(dn\poly(1/\varepsilon))
total training time for multi-pass streaming data.

We note that in the one-pass streaming setting, a constant approximation ratio on the radius of the enclosing ball
yields a more conservative decision boundary and thus a larger Type II error than OCSVM.
To contrast, our method (
SONAR
) achieves a smaller Type II error than OCSVM for alternatives near the decision boundary.

3  Setup and Background on OCSVM and Random Fourier Features

Figure 1:
OCSVM maps data points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
as
\varphi(x)

\mathcal{H}
, inducing linear separation between normal points (gray) and yet unseen novel points (in red).

3.1  Preliminaries

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{T}
be our streaming data over
T
steps, lying in domain
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
We use
\mathcal{H}
to denote an RKHS with kernel
K(\cdot,\cdot)
and kernel embedding
\varphi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}
.
We assume the kernel is shift-invariant
K(x,y)=K(x-y)
and that
K(x,x)=\langle\varphi(x),\varphi(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
is a constant, as is the case for a Gaussian kernel.
This means
\varphi(x)
lies in the unit sphere in
\mathcal{H}
with respect to the RKHS norm.

Definition 1

(Classification Criterion)
.

A unit-norm RKHS element
w\in\mathcal{H}
and threshold
c_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
are associated to a hyperplane in
\mathcal{H}
.
A test point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
is deemed an
outlier
if
\varphi(x)
and the origin are on the same side of the hyperplane, or if
\langle w,\varphi(x)\rangle=w(x)<c_{0}
.
This induces a classifier
h_{w}(x):=\boldsymbol{1}\{w(x)<c_{0}\}
on outlier detection.

3.2  Background on OCSVM

Defining the OCSVM Objective.

We first review the OCSVM objective, as originally formulated in
Schölkopf et al. 1999
;
Schölkopf et al. 2001
.
OCSVM finds the maximum margin hyperplane separating the observed embedded data
\{\varphi(X_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
from the origin in the unit sphere of the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
.
This is equivalent to finding the minimal diameter spherical cap containing the data or:

\min_{r>0,w_{c}\in\mathcal{H}}r^{2}\hskip 5.0pt\text{ s.t. }\hskip 5.0pt\forall t\in[T]:\|\varphi(X_{t})-w_{c}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq r^{2}\,,

(1)

where
w_{c}
denotes the center of the spherical cap in question.
We can also phrase the above as a margin maximization problem
1
1

1

The radius
r
in
Equation

1
corresponds to
\sqrt{2-2\rho}
in terms of the offset
\rho
in
Equation

2
.
:

\max_{w,\rho}\,\rho\hskip 5.0pt\text{ s.t }\hskip 5.0pt\forall t\in[T]:\langle w,\varphi(X_{t})\rangle\geq\rho,\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1,

(2)

where
w
is the unit-norm normal vector to the separating hyperplane and
\rho
is the threshold on the inner product for normal data.
The optimizer
w^{*}
solving
Equation

2
geometrically corresponds to a hyperplane of maximal distance from the origin enclosing the data as depicted in
Figure

1
.

Schölkopf et al. 2001
also consider the simpler objective without a norm constraint on
w
:

\min_{w,\rho}\frac{1}{2}\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-\rho\hskip 5.0pt\text{ s.t. }\hskip 5.0pt\forall t\in[T]:\langle w,\varphi(X_{t})\rangle\geq\rho\,.

(3)

In fact the above
is equivalent to
Equation

2
, as the distance of a hyperplane induced by a pair
(w,\rho)
from the origin is
\rho/\|w\|

(
Schölkopf et al. 2001
, Prop 2)
.
The reader is referred to Section 2.1 of
Tax and Duin 2004
for further discussion on the equivalence of
Equation

3
and
Equation

2
.

As is the case for SVMs, we can also formulate the soft version of
Equation

3
with slack variables:

\displaystyle\min_{w,\rho,\xi}\frac{1}{2}\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-\rho+\frac{1}{\lambda\cdot T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\xi_{t}\hskip 5.0pt\text{ s.t. }\hskip 5.0pt\langle w,\varphi(X_{t})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\geq\rho-\xi_{t},\forall t\in[T]:\xi_{t}\geq 0\,.

(4)

Here, the parameter
\lambda
is user-set and, in theory, an upper bound on the fraction of outliers as we expect outliers to violate the constraint
\langle w,\varphi(X)\rangle\geq\rho
a
\lambda
proportion of the time.
Proposition 4 of
Schölkopf et al. 2001
shows that
\lambda

is asymptotically equal to the proportion of outliers with i.i.d. continuous data.

Putting the OCSVM Objective in ERM Form.

We note the optimal slack values in
Equation

4
are

\xi_{t}=(\rho-\langle w,\varphi(X_{t})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})_{+}\,,

meaning we can simplify
Equation

4
using a penalty term:

\min_{w,\rho}\frac{1}{2}\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-\rho+\frac{1}{\lambda\cdot T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\rho-\langle w,\varphi(X_{t})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right)_{+},

(5)

Now, we’ve arrived at an objective which is in an empirical risk minimization form and is thus amenable to SGD in the streaming setting.
However, a major issue is that for infinite-dimensional RKHS’s (e.g., that of a Gaussian kernel) we don’t have access to the Hilbert space norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
.

In the batch setting, to get around this the kernel OCSVM objective is instead typically solved by either (a) using the dual formulation of
Equation

5
or (b) using the representer theorem to constrain our attention to solutions in a finite dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}
.
However, both of these approaches pose the same challenge in the streaming setting.

Challenge of Streaming Setting.

Going into more detail on the second approach, the representer theorem gives
w(x)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\beta_{t}\cdot K(x,X_{t})
for coefficients
\beta_{t}
so that
\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\sum_{i,j}^{T}\beta_{i}\beta_{j}K(X_{i},X_{j})

(
Chapelle 2007
)
.
Then, the objective
Equation

5
becomes:

\min_{\beta,\rho}\frac{1}{2}\beta^{T}\textbf{K}_{T}\beta+\frac{1}{\lambda T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\rho-\textbf{k}_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta})_{+}-\rho,

(6)

where
\textbf{K}_{T}=(K(X_{i},X_{j}))_{1\leq i,j\leq T}
is the
T\times T
gram matrix,
\textbf{k}_{t}
is the
t
-th column of
\textbf{K}_{T}
, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}:=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{T})
.
While
Equation

6
is also amenable to SGD, it is no longer sensible in the streaming setting as
\textbf{K}_{T}
and
\textbf{k}_{t}
depend on future datapoints, disallowing unbiased single-sample subgradient estimates.
The approach of solving
Equation

5
via the dual introduces an identical issue.
While we could solve
Equation

6
at each timestep
T=t
thus reducing to the offline problem, but this would require prohibitive memory and runtime.

To get around this data dependency issue, we first curiously note this key issue does not arise for the simpler linear OCSVM problem with a Euclidean kernel.
In such a case, the primal
Equation

5
simplifies to:

\min_{w,\rho}\frac{1}{2}w^{T}w-\rho+\frac{1}{\lambda\cdot T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\rho-w^{T}X_{t})_{+}\,.

(7)

Here, we have unbiased single-step subgradient estimate
(\rho-w^{T}X_{t})_{+}
only depending on the datapoint
X_{t}
.

Now, a non-linear kernel can be linearized using Random Fourier Features, allowing us to obtain an objective similar to
Equation

7
for infinite-dimensional RKHS’s.
We next review the details of Random Fourier Features (RFF).

Review of Random Fourier Features.

RFF approximates the feature map
\varphi(x)
of a kernel
K(\cdot,\cdot)
by means of a Euclidean projection
z:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
z(x)^{T}z(y)\approx K(x,y)
.
We choose to use cos-sin pair RFF formulation of
Rahimi and Recht 2007
, which is defined by:

\displaystyle z(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{d}}\left(z_{\omega_{1}}(x),\ldots,z_{\omega_{2d}}(x)\right)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{d}}\left(\sin(\omega_{1}^{T}x),\cos(\omega_{1}^{T}x),\ldots,\sin(\omega_{d}^{T}x),\cos(\omega_{d}^{T}x)\right),

where
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d}
are i.i.d. from the Fourier transform of
K(\cdot,\cdot)
(which, recall is shift-invariant).
Then, the approximate kernel map is:

\displaystyle z(x)^{T}z(y)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\cos(\omega_{j}^{T}(x-y))\approx\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[\cos(\omega^{T}(x-y))]=K(x,y)\,,

where the last equality holds by
K(x,y)=K(x-y)=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[\exp(i\omega^{T}(x-y))]
with expectation taken over the Fourier transform probability distribution of the kernel.

Now, the primal OCSVM objective
Equation

5
from before can be written as:

\min_{w,\rho}\frac{\lambda}{2}\cdot w^{T}w-\lambda\cdot\rho+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\rho-w^{T}z(X_{t})\right)_{+}\,.

(8)

This is a linear OCSVM problem for (projected) data
\{z(X_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
and the SGD update rules w.r.t. learning rate
\eta
are:

\displaystyle w_{t+1}

\displaystyle\leftarrow w_{t}-\eta\left(\lambda\cdot w_{t}-z(X_{t})\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho_{t}\geq w_{t}^{T}z(X_{t})\}\right)

\displaystyle\rho_{t+1}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\rho_{t}-\eta\left(-\lambda+\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho_{t}\geq w_{t}^{T}z(X_{t})\}\right).

However, the optimization problem
Equation

8
is not strongly convex in the parameter
(w,\rho)
(see
Section

B.1
), making fast convergence guarantees unavailable.
Without such guarantees, it is unclear how to establish guarantees on Type I and II errors of the SGD iterates for
Equation

8
.
To get around this, we define a new strongly convex variant of
Equation

8
, which admits rigorous guarantees on both Type I and II errors.

4  A New Strongly Convex Objective for Online Outlier Detection

Since we choose to use RFF’s to approximate our kernel, for simplicity we’ll assume going forward that the data lie in the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and satisfy the following margin assumption (which we show in
Appendix

A
is justified for a Gaussian kernel for a sufficient number of RFF’s
d
).

Assumption 1
.

Suppose the data domain
\mathcal{X}
lies in the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and has margin from the origin of at least
1/2
, i.e.
r^{*}:=\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\langle x,u\rangle\geq 1/2
.

We now present a strongly convex modification of
Equation

8
.
For a probability measure
P_{X}
on
\mathcal{X}
, we define objective:

F(w,\rho):=\frac{\|w\|_{2}^{2}+\rho^{2}}{2}-\lambda\cdot\rho+\Exp\limits_{X\sim P_{X}}[(\rho-w^{T}X)_{+}].

(9)

We next prove some basic properties of the optimizer to
Equation

9
.
The proofs of all presented results going forward are found in
Appendix

B
.
First, we assert this objective is indeed strongly convex.

Proposition 2
.

F(w,\rho)
is
1
-strongly convex in
(w,\rho)
.

Next, we show the optimizer of
Equation

9

has small Type I error, controlled by
\lambda
similar to the OCSVM objective
Equation

4

(
Schölkopf et al. 2001
, Prop. 4)
.

Theorem 3

(Small Type I Error)
.

Let
\lambda\in[0,1]
.
The solution
(w_{\lambda},\rho_{\lambda})
to
Equation

9
satisfies

\mathbb{P}_{X\sim P_{X}}(\langle w_{\lambda},X\rangle<\rho_{\lambda})<\lambda\,.

On the other hand, our new objective
Equation

9
gives rise to a solution whose margin or distance of the separating hyperplane to the origin
r_{\lambda}:=\frac{\rho_{\lambda}}{\|w_{\lambda}\|}
is maximal, and in fact larger than that of OCSVM.
This implies that, in terms of margin, the solution to
Equation

9
generally has a larger rejection region for outliers.

Theorem 4

(Large Margin Property)
.

The solution
(w_{\lambda},\rho_{\lambda})
to
Equation

9
satisfies
r_{\lambda}\geq r^{*}
.

Thus, put together, our new objective’s solution has the same Type I error bound as OCSVM, but a tighter acceptance region for inliers allowing for smaller Type II error against alternatives near the decision boundary.

Lemma 5

(SGD Convergence Guarantee)

Suppose the data

4.2  Comparing Complexity and Efficiency of SONAR vs Reduction to Batch OCSVM

We show the training time, evaluation time (to classify a point), and memory of
SONAR
is superior to solving batch kernel OCSVM without RFF’s at each step.
All our comparisons are done for the Gaussian kernel.

Training Time.

Each SGD update in
SONAR
takes
O(d)
time for a total training time of
O(Td)
.
For the theoretically recommended choice of
d=O(D\log(D/\delta))
(as justified in
Appendix

A
), this gives a worst-case total runtime of
O(TD\log(D/\delta))
.

In contrast, solving the offline OCSVM at step
t
using the seen data incurs higher train time.
Solving the dual convex quadratic program to
Equation

5
takes worst-case
O(t^{3})
time for a total time of
O(T^{4})
.
Also, computing the gram matrix requires time
O(T^{2}D)
.
If one uses SGD to solve the primal
Equation

6
, a total train time of at least
\Omega(T^{2}D)
is still incurred just for gradient updates.
Using SMO to solve OCSVM also incurs
\Omega(T^{2}D)
time to check KKT violations
(
Platt 1998
)
.

Evaluation Time.

SONAR
requires
O(d)

For the recommended choice of

at step

Space Complexity.

Meanwhile,

5  SONAR’s Transfer Learning Guarantees

We now tackle the more challenging non-stationary setting where the normal data distribution
P_{X_{t}}
changes over time.
We first show that
SONAR
(
Algorithm

1
) benefits from the past under favorable distribution shifts, without the need for any resets in learning.
We express such results as generalization error bounds and margin lower bounds akin to
Section

4.1
and
8
, in terms of the quality of the iterate
(w_{t_{0}},\rho_{t_{0}})
at a changepoint
t_{0}
.

Theorem 9

(Transferring Quality of Past Iterates)
.

Suppose the data
X_{t_{0}},\ldots,X_{t}
over period
[t_{0},t]
are i.i.d. from some distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Let
(w_{t},\rho_{t})
be the
t
-th iterate of
Algorithm

1
using step sizes
\eta_{t}:=1/(t+1)
.
Then, we have for steps
t>t_{0}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\langle w_{t},X_{t}\rangle<\rho_{t})\leq\underbrace{\mathbb{P}\left(\langle w_{t_{0}},X_{t}\rangle<\rho_{t_{0}}+\lambda\cdot\frac{t-t_{0}}{t_{0}}\right)}_{\text{\bf Benefit of Past}}\land\underbrace{\mathbb{P}\left(\langle w_{\lambda},X_{t}\rangle<\rho_{\lambda}+\tilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{t-t_{0}}}\right)\right)}_{\text{\bf Agnostic Guarantee}}\,.

(10)

Additionally, the
t
-th iterate’s margin

satisfies:

\displaystyle r_{t}:=\frac{\rho_{t}}{\|w_{t}\|_{2}}\geq r_{t_{0}}+\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}\frac{\lambda-Z_{s}\cdot(1+\lvert r_{s-1}\rvert)}{(s-1)\cdot\|w_{s-1}+Z_{s}X_{s}/(s-1)\|_{2}}\,.

(11)

The generalization error bound

10

, we benefit from the generalization error of
(w_{t_{0}},\rho_{t_{0}})
, while for larger

, we can default to the agnostic guarantee of

.

From the margin lower bound in
Equation

11
, we see that
\lambda
controls a tradeoff in Type I and II errors.
For steps where there is no misclassification
Z_{s}=0
(i.e., low Type I error), the margin

increases by an amount proportional to
\lambda
, thus reinforcing smaller Type II error for alternatives near the decision boundary.
On the other hand, misclassification

(more likely for large

Additionally, the lower bound

“transfers” the margin from step

to

to

Thus, if the margin of the past iterate

Additionally,

1
to
t
.
Letting

and
8
, depending on the quality of the initializer
(w_{0},\rho_{0})
.

However, in the face of adversarial distribution shifts, past iterates may not confer any benefits on Type I or II error, necessitating changepoint detection and restarts in learning.

6  Adaptive Guarantees under Non-Stationarity

Next, we design a procedure which adapts to any non-stationary environment, by ensuring similar-order small Type I error (
Section

4.1
) and large margin property (
8
) within each stationary phase of time.
In particular, our aim as in other works on ensemble non-stationary online learning
(
Herbster and Warmuth 1995
;
Haussler et al. 1998
;
Auer et al. 2002
;
Hazan and Seshadhri 2007
;
Daniely et al. 2015
)
is to mimic the performance of an oracle restarting variant of

which resets its learning rate at changepoints.

For simplicity, let’s suppose we know the total number of steps
T
(we can circumvent this with a time-varying learning rate and doing the analysis along doubling periods).
The high-level idea is to run multiple copies of
SONAR
which independently reset their learning rates at different schedules.
We call each such instantiation a
base algorithm
.
We detect a change in the environment using the iterates of different base learners.
Intuitively, if the population minimizer
(w_{\lambda},\rho_{\lambda})
remains fixed, then the iterates of all base learners will converge to
(w_{\lambda},\rho_{\lambda})
.
Thus, we detect a change in the environment if there is large discrepancy in the iterates beyond the SGD convergence threshold of
Section

4.1
.

Algorithm

2
captures the overall procedure.

Input

T
, anticipated outlier proportion
\lambda

\delta

.

for

do

1

and update each of the bases

for

Let

denote the most recent “final iterate” of
{\textsf{{Base}}}(m)
before resetting its learning rate.

If

\displaystyle\|(w_{t},\rho_{t})-(w_{t,m},\rho_{t,m})\|_{2}^{2}\geq C\cdot\frac{\log(T)\cdot\log(1/\delta)}{2^{m}}\,,

(12)

then restart the whole procedure with time horizon
T-t
.

with
C

We present two main theoretical guarantees for

We first show a

(w_{\lambda},\rho_{\lambda})
.

Theorem 10

(Safety of CPD)

If

2

2

Our next result shows

Crucially,

Additionally,

7  Conclusion/Discussion

We introduced
SONAR
, an efficient SGD-based algorithm for streaming one-class classification that achieves provable guarantees on both Type I and II errors.
A key finding is that
SONAR
admits favorable transfer learning properties under benign distribution shifts – that is, past iterates confer benefits on both Type I error and margin without requiring explicit knowledge of source and target domains.
For general non-stationarity,
SONARC
extends
SONAR
with a principled changepoint detection mechanism that adapts to unknown distribution shifts while avoiding unnecessary restarts.

While our Type II error guarantees are expressed through margin bounds, future work could derive direct Type II error bounds for various outlier models.
It is also left open to study how labeled outlier feedback could be incorporated in
SONAR
’s design principles and further improve guarantees.

Appendix A
A Lower Bound on the Number of Needed Random Fourier Features

Here, we investigate
1
more closely and derive a lower bound
r_{\min}
on the margin separating the support from the origin.
We first study the RBF kernel without the use of Random Fourier Features.
For an RBF kernel, we have that if the untransformed data
x
lie in a set of diameter at most
B
, then:

K(x,x^{\prime}):=\exp\left(-\gamma\cdot\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\geq\exp(-\gamma\cdot B^{2})\geq r_{\min}\iff B\leq\sqrt{\gamma^{-1}\log(r_{\min}^{-1})}\,.

In particular, for untransformed data lying in a ball of diameter
1
and
\gamma=0.5
, the above second inequality is satisfied for
r_{\min}=1/2
.
Then,
K(x,x^{\prime})\geq r_{\min}=1/2>0
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\supp(P_{X})
implies the support of the transformed data lies in the interior of a fixed hemisphere since the angle between points in
\mathcal{H}
is lower bounded by
1/2
.

We next show that
r_{\min}
lower bounds the margin of the population OCSVM problem
Equation

2
.
Let
w^{*}
be the normalized solution to
Equation

2
.
We then have:

r^{*}=\min_{x\in P_{X}}\langle\varphi(x),w^{*}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\geq\min_{x,x^{\prime}\in P_{X}}\langle\varphi(x),\varphi(x^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\geq r_{\min}\,,

where the first inequality above holds because
w^{*}
lies in the convex hull of the support vectors.

We next generalize these claims to the Random Fourier Feature approximation of the RBF kernel.
For RFF map
z:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
approximating the kernel embedding
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}
, we desire accuracy

|z(x)^{T}z(x^{\prime})-K(x,x^{\prime})|\leq r_{\min}/2,

(13)

to ensure that the transformed data in the RFF RKHS also lies in a spherical cap with angle at least
r_{\min}/2
or:

\min_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}z(x)^{T}z(x^{\prime})\geq r_{\min}/2\,.

This will ensure there is a similar order margin in the RFF RKHS.
By a uniform convergence argument on the randomness of selecting features for
z

(
Sutherland and Schneider 2015
, Proposition 1)
,
Equation

13
will hold w.p.
1-\delta
for
d=\Omega\left(\frac{D}{r_{\min}^{2}}\log\left(\frac{D}{\delta\cdot r_{\min}^{2}}\right)\right)
features where recall
D
is the ambient dimension of the untransformed data
\mathcal{X}
.

B.1  Proof that OCSVM ERM-Form Objective is Not Strongly Convex

We show the OCSVM objective of
Equation

8
may in general not be strongly convex.
Consider a point-mass distribution at
X
.
We simplify the objective as:

f(w,\rho):=\frac{\lambda}{2}w^{T}w-\lambda\rho+(\rho-w^{T}X)_{+}\,.

When
\rho\neq w^{T}X
this objective is smooth in the parameters and has Hessian

\nabla^{2}f=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda\cdot\Id_{d\times d}&\boldsymbol{0}_{d}^{T}\\
\boldsymbol{0}_{d}&0\end{pmatrix}\,,

which is not uniformly positive definite.
This means the objective
f(w,\rho)
is not strongly convex in
(w,\rho)
.
\blacksquare

B.2  Proof of Strong Convexity of
SONAR
Objective
Equation

9
(
Section

4
)

It suffices to show
(w,\rho)\mapsto-\lambda\cdot\rho+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{X}}[(\rho-w^{T}X)_{+}]
is convex.
Because this latter function is the sum of an affine function and the function
(w,\rho)\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{X}}[(\rho-w^{T}X)_{+}]
, it suffices to show this expectation is convex in
(w,\rho)
.
To wit, note
(\rho,w)\mapsto(\rho-w^{T}X)_{+}
is convex being the composition of a non-decreasing function with an affine (and thus convex) function.
Then, since expectations preserve convexity, the proof is finished.
\blacksquare

B.3  Proof that Minimizer of
SONAR
Objective

has Small Type I Error (
3
)

First, we note a zero-norm choice of

turns the objective

which has a minimal value of

at

for

Then, we can rule out

(w,\rho)

Let’s suppose

We reparametrize the variables as

We then rewrite the objective

This is a convex quadratic in

F(r,v):=-\frac{(-\lambda r+L(r,v))^{2}}{2+2r^{2}}\,.

Otherwise,

It remains to minimize

over

such that

First, note that

Next, we claim the subderivative

The above is found by computing the left and right derivatives of

For the right-derivative, by bounded convergence theorem:

\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{L(r+h,v)-L(r,v)}{h}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{X}}\left[\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{(r+h-v^{T}X)_{+}-(r-v^{T}X)_{+}}{h}\right]=\mathbb{P}(v^{T}X\leq r)\,.

Similarly, for the left-derivative, again by bounded convergence:

Now, let

be the minimizer of

for

Next, note the set of

0\in\partial_{r}F(r_{\lambda},v_{\lambda})\implies\exists g\in[\mathbb{P}(v_{\lambda}^{T}X<r_{\lambda}),\mathbb{P}(v_{\lambda}^{T}X\leq r_{\lambda})]:g-\lambda=\frac{(L(r_{\lambda},v_{\lambda})-\lambda r_{\lambda})\cdot r_{\lambda}}{1+r_{\lambda}^{2}}<0\,.

This means we must have

.
\blacksquare

B.7  Proof of SGD Convergence Guarantee (

)

The subgradient inequality for strongly convex function

(namely, that
\boldsymbol{0}\in\partial F(w_{\lambda},\rho_{\lambda})
) gives us

\|(w_{T},\rho_{T})-(w_{\lambda},\rho_{\lambda})\|^{2}\leq 2\cdot(F(w_{T},\rho_{T})-F(w_{\lambda},\rho_{\lambda}))\,.

Thus, it suffices to bound the objective value gap of the iterate
(w_{T},\rho_{T})
using the results of
Harvey et al. 2019

We first verify that our optimization problem satisfies the setting/assumptions of
Harvey et al. 2019
.
We claim that the noise of the subgradient estimate of

used in the update rules of

1
have bounded norm.

Subgradient Estimates are Unbiased and have Bounded Noise.

Define
H_{X}(w,\rho):=(\rho-w^{T}X)_{+}
, which is the hinge-like risk appearing in the definition of

for a single datapoint
X
.
We then have

-X\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho\geq w^{T}X\}\in\partial_{w}H_{X}(w,\rho)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho\geq w^{T}X\}\in\partial_{\rho}H_{X}(w,\rho)\,,

are subgradients of
H_{X}(w,\rho)
.
Then, we claim
g_{w}:=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{X}}[-X\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho\geq w^{T}X\}]
and
g_{\rho}:=\mathbb{P}_{X\sim P_{X}}(\rho\geq w^{T}X)
are subgradients of
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{X}}[H_{X}(w,\rho)]
.
This follows from the definition of subgradient:

\displaystyle H_{X}(w,\rho)-H_{X}(w^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho\geq w^{T}X\}\cdot(X,1)^{T}(w-w^{\prime},\rho-\rho^{\prime})\implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{X}}[H_{X}(w,\rho)-H_{X}(w^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{X}}[\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho\geq w^{T}X\}\cdot(X,1)]^{T}(w-w^{\prime},\rho-\rho^{\prime})\,.

Additionally,
\|g_{w}+X\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho\geq w^{T}X\}\|_{2}^{2}+\lvert g_{\rho}-\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho\geq w^{T}X\}\rvert^{2}
is bounded by a constant free of
w,\rho
since
X
has unit norm.
Thus, the subgradient estimates used in Lines
1
and
1
of
Algorithm

1
are unbiased and have noise with bounded norm.

Bounding Lipschitz Constant of Objective.

Next, we claim
F(w,\rho)
is Lipschitz in
(w,\rho)
with
O(1)
Lipschitz constant.
We first analyze the Lipschitz constant of the objective defined for a point-mass at
X
:
F_{X}(w,\rho):=\frac{1}{2}(\|w\|_{2}^{2}+\rho^{2})-\lambda\cdot\rho+(\rho-w^{T}X)_{+}
.
We first claim that so long as the initial iterate
(w_{0},\rho_{0})
satisfies
\|w_{0}\|_{2}\leq 1
and
\lvert\rho_{0}\rvert\leq 1
, the SGD iterates
(w_{t},\rho_{t})
stay within the convex set
\Theta:=\{(w,\rho):\|w\|_{2}\leq 1,\lvert\rho\rvert\leq 1\}
.
This follows from writing the update rules:

\displaystyle w_{t}

\displaystyle=w_{t-1}-\eta_{t-1}(w_{t-1}-X_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho_{t-1}\geq\langle w_{t-1},X_{t}\rangle\})=(1-\eta_{t-1})\cdot w_{t-1}+\eta_{t-1}\cdot X_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho_{t-1}\geq\langle w_{t-1},X_{t}\rangle\}

\displaystyle\rho_{t}

\displaystyle=\rho_{t-1}-\eta_{t-1}(\rho_{t-1}-\lambda+\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho_{t-1}\geq\langle w_{t-1},X_{t}\rangle\})=(1-\eta_{t-1})\cdot\rho_{t-1}+\lambda\eta_{t-1}-\eta_{t-1}\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho_{t-1}\geq\langle w_{t-1},X_{t}\rangle\}\,,

whence we observe
w_{t}
is a convex combination of vectors in the closed unit ball and
\rho_{t}
is a convex combination of scalars in the interval
[-1,1]
.
Hence, the iterates
w_{t}
and
\rho_{t}
have norm at most
1
throughout.

Then, it suffices to bound the subgradient norm on this compact parameter space
\Theta
.
The subdifferential of
F(w,\rho)
is

\partial F_{X}(w,\rho)=(w,\rho-\lambda)+(-X,1)\cdot\begin{cases}\{0\}&\rho-w^{T}X<0\\
[0,1]&\rho-w^{T}X=0\\
\{1\}&\rho-w^{T}X>0\end{cases}\,,

with norm:

\displaystyle\|\partial F_{X}(w,\rho)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{c\in[0,1]}\|(w-c\cdot X,\rho-\lambda+c)\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{c\in[0,1]}\sqrt{\|w-c\cdot X\|_{2}^{2}+(\rho-\lambda+c)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{c\in[0,1]}\sqrt{1+1+2\cdot((\rho-\lambda)^{2}+c^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2+2\cdot((1+\lambda)^{2}+1)}\,.

This last bound is at most
\sqrt{12}
for
\lambda\in[0,1]
.
This shows
F_{X}(w,\rho)
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
\sqrt{12}
.
It then follows
F(w,\rho)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{X}}[F_{X}(w,\rho)]
has the same Lipschitz constant since by Jensen’s inequality:

\lvert F(w,\rho)-F(w^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\rvert\leq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{X}}[\lvert F_{X}(w,\rho)-F_{X}(w^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\rvert]\leq\sqrt{12}\cdot\|(w,\rho)-(w^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})\|_{2}\,.

Then, by Theorem F.1 of
Harvey et al. 2019
, we have w.p.
1-\delta
:

F(w_{T},\rho_{T})-F(w_{\lambda},\rho_{\lambda})\leq O\left(\frac{\log(T)\log(1/\delta)}{T}\right).

\blacksquare

B.11  Proof of Transfer Guarantees of

(

We first show the Type I error bound of

First, we note that subgradients of the objective function

can be chosen as:

\displaystyle\nabla_{w}F(w,\rho,X)

\displaystyle=w-X\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho\geq w^{T}X\}

\displaystyle\nabla_{\rho}F(w,\rho,X)

\displaystyle=\rho-\lambda+\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho\geq w^{T}X\}.

Then, we write update rules:

\displaystyle w_{t}

\displaystyle=w_{t-1}-\eta_{t-1}\cdot(w_{t-1}-X_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho_{t-1}\geq\langle w_{t-1},X_{t}\rangle\})=(1-\eta_{t-1})\cdot w_{t-1}+\eta_{t-1}\cdot X_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho_{t-1}\geq\langle w_{t-1},X_{t}\rangle\}

\displaystyle=\rho_{t-1}-\eta_{t-1}\cdot(\rho_{t-1}-\lambda+\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho_{t-1}\geq\langle w_{t-1},X_{t}\rangle\})=(1-\eta_{t-1})\cdot\rho_{t-1}+\lambda\eta_{t-1}-\eta_{t-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho_{t-1}\geq\langle w_{t-1},X_{t}\rangle\}\,.

For shorthand, let

Let

Inducting, we have closed-form formulas:

\displaystyle w_{t}

\displaystyle=H_{t}\cdot w_{0}+\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}X_{s}\cdot\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho_{s-1}\geq\langle w_{s-1},X_{s}\rangle\}\cdot J_{s,t}

(15)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}

\displaystyle=H_{t}\cdot\rho_{0}+\lambda\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}J_{s,t}-\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}\boldsymbol{1}\{\rho_{s-1}\geq\langle w_{s-1},X_{s}\rangle\}\cdot J_{s,t}.

(16)

Next, letting

\displaystyle\langle w_{t},X\rangle

\displaystyle<\rho_{t}\iff

\displaystyle H_{t}\cdot\langle w_{0},X\rangle+\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}\langle X,X_{s}\rangle\cdot Z_{s}\cdot J_{s,t}

\displaystyle<H_{t}\cdot\rho_{0}+\lambda\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}J_{s,t}-\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}Z_{s}\cdot J_{s,t}\iff

\displaystyle\langle w_{0},X\rangle

\displaystyle<\rho_{0}+\lambda\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}\frac{J_{s,t}}{H_{t}}-\sum_{s=t_{0}+1}^{t}\frac{J_{s,t}}{H_{t}}\cdot Z_{s}\cdot(1+\langle X,X_{s}\rangle)\implies

We have by telescoping product

Thus, we conclude

\langle w_{t},X\rangle<\rho_{t}\implies\langle w_{0},X\rangle<\rho_{0}+\lambda\cdot\frac{t-t_{0}}{t_{0}}.

We next show the margin lower bound for

Using the closed-form formulas for the iterates

\displaystyle r_{t}-r_{t-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{t-1}-\frac{\eta_{t-1}}{1-\eta_{t-1}}\cdot(Z_{t}-\lambda)}{\|w_{t-1}+Z_{t}\cdot X_{t}\cdot\frac{\eta_{t-1}}{1-\eta_{t-1}}\|_{2}}-\frac{\rho_{t-1}}{\|w_{t-1}\|_{2}}\,.

When

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{t-1}-\frac{\eta_{t-1}}{1-\eta_{t-1}}\cdot(1-\lambda)}{\|w_{t-1}+X_{t}\cdot\frac{\eta_{t-1}}{1-\eta_{t-1}}\|_{2}}-\frac{\rho_{t-1}}{\|w_{t-1}\|_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{t-1}\cdot\|w_{t-1}\|_{2}-\frac{1}{t-1}\cdot(1-\lambda)\cdot\|w_{t-1}\|_{2}-\rho_{t-1}\cdot\|w_{t-1}+X_{t}/(t-1)\|_{2}}{\|w_{t-1}\|_{2}\cdot\|w_{t-1}+X_{t}/(t-1)\|_{2}}

Next, we have by triangle inequality and the fact that

Lemma 13
.

The population minimizer
(w_{\lambda},\rho_{\lambda})
of
Equation

9
satisfies
r_{\lambda}:=\frac{\rho_{\lambda}}{\|w_{\lambda}\|_{2}}\leq 1
.

Proof.

Recall the objective

F(w,\rho)=\frac{1}{2}(\|w\|_{2}^{2}+\rho^{2})-\lambda\rho+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{X}}[(\rho-w^{T}X)_{+}]\,.

We claim that for any
(w,\rho)
such that
\rho>\|w\|_{2}
, the parameter
(w,\|w\|_{2})
has strictly smaller objective value.
First, note since
\|X\|_{2}=1
for any
X
, by Cauchy-Schwarz:

\rho-w^{T}X\geq\rho-\|w\|_{2}>0\,.

Thus,
(\rho-w^{T}X)_{+}=\rho-w^{T}X
and also
(\|w\|_{2}-w^{T}X)_{+}=\|w\|_{2}-w^{T}X
.
Then,

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{X}}[(\rho-w^{T}X)_{+}-(\|w\|_{2}-w^{T}X)_{+}]=\rho-\|w\|_{2}\,.

Then,

F(w,\rho)-F(w,\|w\|_{2})=\frac{1}{2}(\rho^{2}-\|w\|_{2}^{2})-\lambda(\rho-\|w\|_{2})+(\rho-\|w\|_{2})=(\rho-\|w\|_{2})\cdot\left(\frac{\rho+\|w\|_{2}}{2}+1-\lambda\right)\,.

This last expression is strictly positive for
\lambda\leq 1
.
∎

Lemma 14
.

Let
a,b,x,\varepsilon>0
with
a\leq b
and
\varepsilon<1
.
Then,

x\leq b\cdot\varepsilon/2\implies\frac{a-x}{b+x}\geq\frac{a}{b}-\varepsilon\,.

Proof.

Since
a\leq b
, we have

a+b\cdot(1-\varepsilon)\leq b\cdot(2-\varepsilon)\,.

Then,

\frac{b^{2}\varepsilon}{a+b\cdot(1-\varepsilon)}\geq\frac{b^{2}\varepsilon}{b\cdot(2-\varepsilon)}=\frac{b\varepsilon}{2-\varepsilon}\geq\frac{b\varepsilon}{2}\geq x\,.

This gives

-ax-bx+b\varepsilon x\geq-b^{2}\varepsilon\implies ab-bx\geq ab-b^{2}\varepsilon+ax-b\varepsilon x\implies b(a-x)\geq(a-b\varepsilon)\cdot(b+x)\,.

Rearranging this last inequality, we arrive at
\frac{a-x}{b+x}\geq\frac{a}{b}-\varepsilon
.
∎

Appendix E
Experiments on Real-World Data

We apply
SONAR
,
SONARC
, and OCSVM applied to
Equation

5
to two real-world datasets, each of which has streaming data sorted in time and includes labelled outlier data.
We use the same setup as
Section

D.1
with different choices of
\lambda
for each dataset.

Error Metrics.

We focus on assessing the (normalized)
online cumulative Type I error
which is measured, at round
t
, as the normalized mistake count
\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\boldsymbol{1}\{\langle w_{s},\varphi(X_{s})\rangle<\rho_{s}\text{ and $X_{s}$ is normal datapoint}\}
, where
N_{t}
is the number of normal data points seen till time
t
..
We also assess the
online cumulative Type II error
which is defined similarly:
\frac{1}{t-N_{t}}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\boldsymbol{1}\{\langle w_{s},\varphi(X_{s})\rangle\geq\rho_{s}\text{ and $X_{s}$ is an outlier}\}
.
Note that labeled outlier datapoints are
not
used for training.

In the non-stationary streaming setting, these online Type I and II error metrics are appropriate as the learner must adapt on-the-fly to sequential data and changing distributions.

The SKAB repository consists of multivariate time series data collected in 2020 from sensors installed on a water circulation loop testbed

.
We concatenated the
valve1
and

datasets from SKAB, which both have

features.
We set
\lambda=0.005
(or

using running estimates of the mean and variance up to time

We chose the threshold

from

From the first two plots of

The improvement in Type II error is also reflected in the comparison of margin iterates

Comparison with Other Procedures.

The official repository for SKAB reports a Kaggle leaderboard with batch Type I and II errors on held-out test datasets
(
Katser and Kozitsin 2020
)
.
The state-of-art consists of deep learning-based methods such as LSTM and Conv-AE as well as non-parametric methods such as MSET and Isolation Forest.
None of these methods are, without further modification, applicable to our one-pass streaming setting.
In spite of this, we find that our experiment’s
SONARC
has final online Type I error of
7.2\times 10^{-4}
and online Type II error of
0.551
, which compare favorably to many of the reported arts.

We do caution that this is not a full apples to apples comparison as the error metrics are computed differently.
Arguably, our online Type I and II errors are akin to test errors because evaluation is done at each step using an unseen datapoint.
In another sense, our online error metrics could be considered more challenging than simply computing errors on a test holdout dataset as the online metrics average over all steps, including earlier steps where convergence is slow.

The
final iterate’s offline Type I or II error
on the entire dataset for
SONAR
is, respectively,
38\%
and
26.6\%
, which yields an F1-score of
0.6
for this problem.
This is also within the range of the state-of-the-art in the leaderboards.
