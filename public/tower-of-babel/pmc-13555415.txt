The transformative impact of stem cell core facilities in biomedical research
Authors: Barbara Corneo, Giovanni Faga, Inês Figueira, Jacopo Zasso, Wenli Yang, Katrin Neuman, Pia A. Johansson, Lyn Healy, Laurence M. Daheron, Melanie Generali, Ole Pless, Ras Trokovic, Laurent David, Mehrnaz Gazhvini, Laura Batlle-Morera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOver the past three decades, advances in human pluripotent stem cell (hPSC) technologies, including induced pluripotent stem cells, gene editing, and 2D/3D models, have transformed biomedical research. These powerful tools have revolutionized disease modeling, drug discovery, and the development of advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs), while driving the establishment of stem cell core facilities. By providing specialized expertise, standardized workflows, and access to advanced technologies, these facilities support both fundamental and translational research, promote rigor and reproducibility, and foster collaboration. This manuscript highlights their role as hubs of excellence and discusses current challenges and future opportunities for the global stem cell community.

Introduction to stem cell core facilities and their foundational technologies

The derivation of the first human embryonic stem cell (hESC) lines from blastocysts in 1998 marked a transformative milestone in biomedical research (Thomson et al., 1998). These human hPSCs introduced a wave of optimism within the global scientific community due to their unparalleled ability to generate nearly every cell type of the human body. Their potential to revolutionize developmental biology, disease modeling, and drug discovery sparked intense excitement, alongside considerable ethical debate. Due to their origin, the use of hESCs prompted restrictions and outright bans in several countries, igniting political, moral, and philosophical controversy. Nevertheless, despite this challenging landscape, their scientific value was rapidly acknowledged and multiple research groups across the globe derived hESC lines, catalyzing international interest in their therapeutic and research potential.

In this dynamic environment, several global initiatives emerged with the support of international agencies. These efforts yielded regulations based on ethical questions, advanced the investigation of the characteristics and functional properties of hESCs, and led to the development of guidelines for scientists working in this rapidly growing field. The original derivation laboratories became hubs of expertise, establishing foundational knowledge on how to derive, culture, bank, and utilize hESCs.

Following the derivation of hESCs, the field was impacted profoundly by Shinya Yamanaka’s landmark discovery that demonstrated that somatic cells from the mouse could be reprogrammed into a pluripotent state using a specific set of transcription factors, a finding soon replicated in human cells (human induced pluripotent stem cells [hiPSCs]), offering the possibility to have hPSCs retaining the genomic background of a living donor, therefore enabling phenotype-genotype studies in humans (Takahashi and Yamanaka, 2006; Takahashi et al., 2007; Yu et al., 2007). Moreover, the emergence of genome editing technologies, most notably CRISPR/Cas9, enabled targeted, precise genome modifications (Cho et al., 2013; Cong et al., 2013; Jinek et al., 2013; Mali et al., 2013). Together with hiPSC technology, genome editing accelerated the generation of in vitro human models of greater complexity and physiological relevance. Finally, the advent of three-dimensional (3D) differentiation systems for the generation of organoids, enabled ex vivo production of tissue-like structures that recapitulate key aspects of human organ architecture and function (Sato et al., 2009; Lehmann et al., 2019). The integration of hiPSCs, CRISPR-based genome editing, and organoid systems allows the creation of advanced human cell models, opening unprecedented opportunities to study development, dissect disease mechanisms, and evaluate novel therapeutic strategies.

These advancements concurrently enabled laboratories, repositories, and commercial organizations to assemble dedicated scientific teams to generate, maintain, cryopreserve, and distribute hPSC lines (Stem Cell Research | Pluripotent stem cell banking for research and specialist applications | ScienceDirect.com by Elsevier). The adoption of standardized quality control (QC) parameters across institutions introduced greater rigor and reproducibility to stem cell workflows. Within a few years, academic institutions recognized the strategic value of embedding this expertise within stem cell core facilities, centralized units that provided specialized skills, economies of scale, and critical support for diverse stem cell research activities. These facilities have evolved into centers of excellence, often developing unique strengths in specific domains of stem cell biology (Dahéron et al., 2021).

Considering these advances and challenges, a critical question arises: what makes stem cell core facilities not merely valuable, but essential to advancing biomedical research and to shaping an institute’s strategic vision, operational efficiency, and scientific leadership?

Foundation, role, and benefits of stem cell core facilities

Biomedical scientific core facilities have existed since the mid-20th century, originally established to meet the growing need for centralized resources and, later, for specialized expertise and standardized infrastructure as biomedical research became increasingly complex, multidisciplinary, and technology-intensive. Stem cell core facilities represent a more recent evolution. While WiCell is the first hESC line distribution core (1999) followed by the UK Stem Cell Bank (2003), the first formal examples of core facilities dedicated to hESC research emerged around 2005, led by pioneering institutions such as Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston, MA, and Mount Sinai School of Medicine in New York, NY, followed in 2007 by Memorial Sloan Kettering (also in New York, NY) and Harvard Stem Cell Institute in 2008 in Cambridge, MA.

Today, we count close to 100 stem cell core facilities worldwide embedded within academic institutions, hospitals, and research centers as centralized infrastructures that provide the technical foundation and intellectual support necessary to carry out complex stem-cell-based studies. These facilities specialize in the generation, maintenance, and manipulation of hPSCs and their downstream applications in differentiation, genome editing, and disease modeling. Their primary mission is to distribute qualified resources to researchers, while streaming access to these powerful but technically demanding technologies by offering standardized protocols, validated workflows, and expert guidance. Typical services include the derivation and banking of new hiPSC lines from donor/patient material, routine culture and QC of PSCs, CRISPR/Cas-mediated gene editing to introduce mutations, generation of isogenic controls or reporter lines, and directed differentiation into specific cell or tissue types. A cornerstone of a stem cell core activity is the rigorous characterization of pluripotent stem cells and their derivatives, an essential requirement for ensuring the quality, identity, and functional potential of these complex biological systems, as recommended by the International Society for Stem Cell Research (ISSCR) (Ludwig et al., 2023). While many of these assays, such as expression profiling, karyotyping, trilineage differentiation, and identity confirmation, are technically available to researchers, their implementation is often non-trivial and resource-intensive. Stem cell core facilities address these challenges by supporting standardization of stem cell derivation and QC methods, as well as of gene editing approaches and differentiation protocols. This way, researchers are spared the burden of developing, optimizing, and maintaining complex QC workflows that require years of experience or seeking fragmented support through academic collaborations or fee-for-service companies. By embedding standardized QC checkpoints throughout the pipeline, from iPSC generation to downstream differentiation, stem cell core facilities ensure consistency, reduce technical variability, provide and distribute qualified resources to the research field, and therefore support the long-term reproducibility of stem-cell-based research (Selfa Aspiroz et al., 2025).

Stem cell core facilities serve as critical platforms where advanced methodologies are not only maintained and optimized but also offered in a streamlined, interoperable fashion, enabling researchers to generate, modify, and differentiate patient-derived cells within a single pipeline. In addition, beyond developing and enforcing standard operating procedures (SOPs) to ensure research consistency and reproducibility across the field and being critical in the adoption of best practices and their dissemination to the field, these facilities must also pursue methodological sophistication, tailored services, and collaborative approaches, including the design and execution of specific projects in collaboration with users. A defining feature is the level of expertise required: facility personnel are not only proficient in executing protocols but are also equipped with deep biological understanding, enabling them to critically engage with each project’s scientific rationale. From the outset, service delivery is highly collaborative and bespoke: core staff work closely with researchers to refine objectives, assess feasibility, and co-develop experimental strategies. This initial phase, while time-intensive, ensures alignment between the experimental plan and the biological questions being addressed. The execution phase, whether focused on genome editing or organoid derivation, is similarly complex, requiring iterative optimization and the integration of intermediate QC steps. Final deliverables, such as engineered cell lines or differentiated tissues, are biologically sophisticated and demand careful handling and validation.

An emerging role for some stem cell core facilities lies in their direct involvement with pre-clinical/clinical studies and Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) production of stem cell therapies as demonstrated by (1) Harvard Stem Cell Institute Core Facility involvement in bringing VX-880, a stem-cell-derived pancreatic islet cell replacement therapy for type 1 diabetes and for the development of a GMP-grade iPSC; (2) Memorial Sloan Kettering Core Facility involvement in the Development of MSK-DA01, now known as bemdaneprocel, in a clinical trial for Parkinson disease; and (3) NeuraCell at the Neural Stem Cell Institute for their role in supporting the development of research-grade cells subsequently manufactured to GMP grade in the (4) Cedars Sinai Stem Cell Core for a clinical trial of RPE cell therapy for dry age-related macular degeneration (Hulspas et al., 2025; Rao et al., 2025; Reichman et al., 2025; Tabar et al., 2025). GMP-compliant stem cell core facilities ensure that cell products are manufactured under stringent conditions that meet regulatory standards for safety, identity, purity, and potency for use in clinical trials or compassionate treatments. Collaborating closely with clinical researchers, regulatory bodies, and industry partners, GMP-compliant stem cell core facilities bridge the gap between bench research and bedside therapies by enabling reliable and standardized cell production, thereby accelerating the delivery of Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMPs).

In addition to the scientific and technical advantages, stem cell core facilities offer a clear economic benefit. They enable access to complex workflows, such as genome editing, organoid generation, and advanced characterization, that would be costly for most individual labs to implement reliably on their own. Establishing these capabilities independently, for example by hiring dedicated personnel such as postdoctoral researchers, represents a significant financial challenge for individual labs. In contrast, stem cell core facilities allow researchers to pay only for the time and services they actually use, rather than bearing the full cost of a salaried employee. Stem cell core facilities offer additional significant benefits to the scientific community by concentrating specialized expertise, validated protocols, and robust QC frameworks within a centralized infrastructure. Through iterative optimization and built-in checkpoints, they help mitigate the risk of failure while maximizing the reliability and reproducibility of outcomes. Altogether, accessing core facilities is markedly cost-efficient, allowing institutions to pool resources and avoid redundant expenditures.

Equally important is the conceptual benefit: stem cell cores enable researchers to focus on their primary scientific questions without the need to become experts in every technical domain. For example, a researcher studying iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes does not need to master the intricacies of reprogramming or genome editing. Stem cell core facilities act as enablers, providing the necessary tools and expertise so that investigators can concentrate on hypothesis-driven research rather than technical implementation. This division of labor not only enhances efficiency but also accelerates discovery by allowing each party to operate at the pinnacle of their specialization.

To accommodate diverse research needs, stem cell cores often operate under flexible access models: either providing end-to-end support managed by core staff, or offering user-access systems, where researchers are trained and perform experiments using facility resources under expert oversight. Importantly, stem cell core facilities provide comprehensive training for internal staff and external researchers alike. This training extends beyond technical procedures to include conceptual frameworks that are essential for working responsibly with stem cell technologies under the highest standards in the field. Successful stem cell experimentation requires not only adherence to protocols but also informed decision-making. By delivering this kind of training, stem cell core facilities propagate best practices in stem cell research, in turn enhancing reproducibility across the field.

The power of stem cell consortia

Stem cell core facilities have evolved into pivotal hubs within research institutions, offering not only essential services but also actively shaping the trajectory of stem cell science. As the field continues to advance at a rapid pace, cross-institutional collaboration has become increasingly critical to maintain and advance state-of-the art technologies. The formation of stem cell core consortia networks has accelerated adoption of these new technologies.

These consortia foster open, continuous exchange of expertise among stem cell core facilities, enabling efficient protocol development, troubleshooting, and the dissemination of best practices. Such collaborative culture helps avoid redundancy and enhances the overall quality, reproducibility, and consistency of services across participating centers. Moreover, these networks provide a unique platform for the early adoption and coordinated implementation of emerging technologies and benchmarking of specific procedures and workflows, accelerating validation and accessibility across multiple sites to benefit the broader research community.

Two well-established networks dedicated to advancing collaboration between stem cell core facilities include Stem Cell COREdinates, Inc. and CorEuStem. Stem Cell COREdinates (https://coredinates.org/) is an international consortium founded in 2009 by Sunita DSouza (then the director of the stem cell core at Mount Sinai School of Medicine, NY) and Mark Tomishima (then the director of the stem cell core at Memorial Sloan Kettering, NY); CorEuStem (https://coreustem.eu/) is the European network for stem cell core facilities, founded in 2021 by Laura Batlle Morera (Head of the Tissue Engineering Unit at the Centre for Genomic Regulation in Barcelona, Spain) and supported by a 4-year COST Action program (European Cooperation in Science and Technology, a funding organization by the European community) grant.

As a result of both networks’ efforts, at the time of the submission we have connected to total of 75 stem cell core facilities or labs providing similar services (Figure 1A). Detailed information on individual stem cell core facilities, including their specific area of expertise, has been compiled in a searchable table available at https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1yOzJtw-3yFT69DD4pC1oSFlK4THPYU7cV3U7mOd-dNI/edit?usp=sharing. A key objective of this ongoing effort is to establish a comprehensive, inclusive, and continuously updated global network of stem cell core facilities. By connecting facilities worldwide and documenting their expertise, capabilities, and geographical distribution, we aim to develop and maintain an accurate global map of stem cell core resources. Such a resource will facilitate collaboration, knowledge exchange, the identification of regional gaps, and more equitable access to stem cell technologies across the international research community. Recognizing that some regions remain underrepresented in the current map and dataset, we are actively expanding our outreach to institutions in Africa, India, Brazil, Canada, Japan, and Korea, among others. The two consortia, CorEuStem and Stem Cell COREdinates, collaborate closely, with working groups focused on gene editing, reprogramming, differentiation, automation and more. They additionally engage with major industry stakeholders, partnering with R&D teams from companies that develop and supply stem cell products to provide feedback, share user insights, and contribute to pre-commercial beta testing of reagents and kits. Another notable example of this collaboration is the invaluable feedback that members of the consortia provided to the launch of the “ISSCR standards for the use of human stem cells in basic research,” an effort led by Tenneille Ludwig (director of WiCell Stem Cell Bank, Wisconsin, USA) (Ludwig et al., 2023). CorEuStem also translated those standards in Spanish, Portuguese, and Italian. Since 2010, Stem Cell COREdinates has been organizing and hosting the annual ISSCR Focus Session “Tools for Basic and Applied Stem Cell Biology,” to facilitate in depth coverage of specific topics and promote the dissemination of cutting edge information to the field, an event attended on average by more than 200 scientists (ISSCR 2025: 243 attendees; ISSCR 2024: 130 attendees; ISSCR 2023: 240 attendees; ISSCR 2022: 265 attendees; ISSCR 2019: 300 attendees [2020–21 virtual only]). Moreover, ongoing collaborations exist between the international members of the consortia: for example, the UK Stem Cell Bank (UKSCB) and the Harvard Stem Cell Institute (HSCI) have collaborated on several joint workshops and training courses focusing on iPSC derivation and culture techniques, offering a blend of theoretical lectures and hands-on laboratory experience. Many studies have been published with contributions from authors within multiple stem cell core facilities, highlighting their collaborative spirit and shared mission of stem cell knowledge dissemination (CorEuStem, no date; Daheron and D’Souza, 2012; Yang et al., 2014; Schlaeger et al., 2015; Dahéron et al., 2021; Selfa Aspiroz et al., 2025). Many core directors are members of the editorial board of journals, such as Stem Cell Reports, Current Protocols in Stem Cell Biology, Stem Cell Research Lab Resources, or StemBook, reflecting their recognized expertise in the field. Their involvement helps shape the direction of scientific publishing, ensures high standards of quality and rigor, and facilitates the dissemination of best practices and emerging technologies in stem cell research. A remarkable initiative is the listserve (with over 4,377 email threads since 2012) that connects members of the two international consortia: common requests, such as questions about reagents, troubleshooting help, or protocol sharing, often receive rapid responses, as someone is always awake somewhere in the world. This is also an exceptional resource for the users of each stem cell core facility, as they can count not only on the expertise of their local team but also on the collective knowledge and experience shared across the broader international network.

Beyond operational benefits, consortia also amplify the visibility of individual stem cell core facilities by providing a collective voice within the international stem cell community. Through joint outreach efforts, conference contributions, and coordinated training initiatives, they raise the profile and influence of academic infrastructure at a global scale and can benchmark workflows and common activities and evaluate impact provided to the research community.

To better understand the functioning of each stem cell core facility and the actual impact provided to their biomedical community, in March 2026, we conducted a survey among stem cell core facilities, retrieving a total of 75 independent replies. Most stem cell core facilities in our consortia are located within academic institutions (61/75, 81%), with 13 non-profit (17%) and 1 private (1%). Services are delivered within their respective institutions or universities, with 74 cores (98.7%) operating internally. The survey also highlighted the significant impact of stem cell core facility activities on the international stem cell research landscape. Collectively, the 75 cores have made substantial contributions: 4,036 new hPSC lines have been registered on hPSCreg.eu (Figures 2C and 2E); 7,351 new gene-edited hPSC lines have been produced (Figures 2C and 2E); and 398 training sessions have been hosted annually, reaching 828 scientists worldwide (Figures 2D and 2E). Each year, stem cell cores receive service requests from 2,279 laboratories, 652 institutions, and 186 companies (Figures 2D and 2E), highlighting both the dedicated efforts of the cores and the high demand for their expertise. These outputs are not just numbers: they are unlocking research questions that were previously impossible to address, reshaping disease modeling strategies, and accelerating translational pipelines across academia and industry. The impact of stem cell core facilities extends far beyond technology. Each facility is a hub for training the next generation of scientists, an invaluable resource in such a dynamic and rapidly evolving field.

Looking ahead, stem cell core consortia will continue to play a vital role in shaping the future of the field by fostering synergy, ensuring quality, and enabling innovation.

Challenges and solutions

Stem cell core facilities are critical enablers of cutting-edge research, yet they face persistent operational and strategic challenges. Balancing innovation with service delivery, securing sustainable funding, and retaining specialized talent are ongoing struggles that threaten their long-term impact. Addressing these issues is essential to ensure these facilities continue driving advances in the stem cell field.

Balancing innovation and service delivery: Breadth versus focus

A fundamental challenge for stem cell core facilities is finding the right balance between service provision and continuous innovation. In fast-moving fields such as stem cell biology and genome editing, staying at the forefront requires the proactive adoption and development of new technologies. These efforts demand significant personnel and financial resources. This challenge is particularly strong for stem cell core facilities, as compared to other equipment-based cores, as they rely heavily on human resources. Yet, the costs of research, innovation, and development are rarely covered by fee-for-service models, requiring dedicated funding through grants or institutional investment, resources that are often limited or unsustainable over time. In our survey, cost recovery of 30/75 (40%) stem cell core facilities is based on fee-for-service, with 6/75 (8%) based on collaboration and 39/75 (52%) on a hybrid model. Most of the surveyed cores (97.3%) reported that they have the ability of developing new technologies and applications but lack a dedicated R&D budget as a major limitation: out of these 75, 20 (26.7%) do not have a R&D budget, hindering their ability to develop new techniques and explore emerging scientific findings; 33.3% (25/75) relies on grants for R&D; 9.3% (7/75) relies on fees for R&D; and 28.0% (21/75) are funded by institution for R&D.

Stem cell core facilities typically offer a wide array of services, including reprogramming, gene editing, and differentiation. While some processes, like reprogramming, are increasingly standardized, others, especially differentiation and genome editing, require specialized, protocol-specific knowledge.

Implementing a new differentiation protocol for a stem cell core facility can involve extensive R&D time, without a guaranteed return on investment if the service is rarely requested. In addition, there is an inherent risk of failure due to the fact that even when using validated protocols and careful planning, certain cell lines may not respond as expected, edits may disrupt viability, or differentiation may fail. Unlike academic labs, core facilities operating under a fee-for-service model must navigate the tension between client expectations and inherent uncertainty of scientific work. Implementing cost-risk strategies in these cases is challenging and, if applied, must be implemented carefully. The commonly used fee-for-service models in stem cell core facilities rarely provide sufficient revenue for full cost recovery. While they provide a necessary baseline for operational funding, they often fail to account for the true costs of maintaining high-quality reagents and equipment, supporting highly trained personnel, implementing new technologies, and driving innovation. While cost-efficiency and fiscal responsibility are important, strict self-sufficiency mandates can be counterproductive. They limit the capacity of core facilities to engage in proactive research and development, invest in emerging technologies, or pilot new protocols that could significantly benefit the broader scientific community. Moreover, these constraints may discourage collaboration, reduce access for early-career investigators, and lead to increased service fees that can become a barrier to entry for less well-funded labs.

This presents a strategic dilemma: whether to focus on a limited number of iPSC-derived lineages where a deep expertise already exists in house or to expand the core’s service offerings in response to broad user demands. Striking the right balance between service quality and breadth, and between user needs and available resources, remains a central challenge.

Structural funding limitations

While some national or state funding agencies support the establishment of centralized infrastructures such as stem cell cores (i.e., National Roadmap Large-Scale Scientific Infrastructure of the Netherlands Organization for Scientific Research; Carl Zeiss Stiftung; FEDER; IBISA or BIOGENOUEST; California Institute of Regenerative Medicine [CIRM]; Massachusetts Life Sciences Center; and NIH P30 grants, among others, or generous gifts from Foundations or programs such as the now defunct New York State NYSTEM), this support often ends after initial setup. Thereafter, the responsibility for operational costs—particularly personnel salaries, which are the largest expense—falls entirely on the host institution. Unlike equipment-based facilities, stem cell work is labor-intensive and requires continuous staffing, including coverage on weekends, holidays, and during staff absences. In addition, some national funding agencies’ reimbursement policies often exclude core facility labor from allowable project costs for internal users. Directly hiring staff onto project grants is often infeasible, particularly for small-scale projects. This leads to difficulties maintaining long-term expert staffing with the consequent accumulated knowledge lost.

In financially challenging times, such as the current climate, where education and research budgets are being reduced in many countries, this dynamic places stem cell core facilities in a precarious position. Rather than being recognized as valuable assets that enhance cost-effectiveness, efficiency, and quality of stem cell research, they are often perceived as costly investments. Given these challenges, it is important that more institutions recognize R&D as a foundational function of stem cell core facilities and provide dedicated internal funding to support it. Moreover, to foster a sustainable innovative stem cell field, funding agencies must reconsider their policies, allowing stem cell core facilities to have access to targeted R&D support in future funding mechanisms. This investment is essential to ensure the long-term innovation, sustainability, and success of these shared resources.

Talent retention and recognition

Stem cell core operations rely on highly skilled specialists who contribute far beyond technical execution. In our survey, we found that 77.3% (58/75) of facilities’ heads often have other responsibilities: 72.4% (42/58) also dedicate their time to research, 56.9% (33/58) to administrative chores, and 62.1% (36/58) to academic/teaching responsibilities. In 37.3% (28/75) of core facilities, over half of the staff hold a PhD (data not shown). These professionals support experimental design, adapt technologies, ensure data quality, and uphold research integrity. Despite their critical roles, core staff often lack clear career paths, recognition, and long-term job security.

Hiring and retaining such talent is increasingly difficult. Core positions typically carry academic lab titles such as “technician” or “postdoc,” despite requiring years of experience and advanced expertise. Salaries often lag behind industry standards, and limited institutional budgets restrict the ability to offer competitive compensation or professional development.

Consequently, many experienced professionals migrate to industry, where higher pay, stability, and advancement opportunities are more readily available. This turnover disrupts continuity, increases core facility costs, and undermines team stability and optimized performance.

Institutions should consider establishing dedicated job classifications for core facility staff, with defined progression tracks and appropriate compensation. Recognizing, rewarding, and stabilizing these specialists is vital to the long-term viability of the stem cell core ecosystem and the impact in stem cell research field.

Beyond financial and staffing challenges, facilities must address additional pressures, including navigating strict ethical and regulatory landscapes, ensuring reproducibility of stem cell lines, supporting interdisciplinary collaboration, and promoting trust through responsible research practices.

Stem cell core facilities are not only important but also essential to biomedical research and to an institute’s strategic vision, operational efficiency, and scientific standing.

Discussion; call to action and future outlook

Human-stem-cell-based biomedicine is a comparatively young discipline: widespread adoption began in the early 2000s and has expanded exponentially since. Stem cell core facilities arose to meet escalating demands for high-quality, reproducible experimentation and rigorous training in a rapidly evolving field. Since the field is so recent, and has evolved so fast, stakeholders are now articulating the foundations of a sustainable stem cell ecosystem. A persistent constraint on progress toward clinical translation and drug development is the limited, and often insufficient, funding, where budgets frequently fail to cover the complete intended scope of proposed stem cell studies and downstream analyses. Stem cell core facilities are mitigating this bottleneck by optimizing shared resources, widening access to advanced methodologies, and training new users under high-quality standards.

A notable trend in the field is the shift toward being able to evaluate “population-scale” genetic variance for specific diseases. Panels comprising hundreds of donor-derived iPSC line models or organoids are increasingly used to capture genetic, sex, and ancestry diversity, thereby improving predictions of efficacy and toxicity and enabling the generation of population-level benchmarks and reference datasets (DeBoever et al., 2017; Ramos et al., 2021; Pantazis et al., 2022). Executing studies at this scale will depend critically on core facilities with automation and high-throughput capacity.

Further to that, regulatory frameworks are accelerating demand for human-relevant in vitro systems. Pharmaceutical pipelines seek earlier human validation to reduce late-stage attrition, and regulators increasingly encourage or mandate non-animal approaches. Recent policy changes, such as the US FDA Modernization Act 2.0, the European Parliament’s 2024 resolution to phase out animal testing, and the more recent NIH announcement to no longer fund projects focused exclusively on animal models of human disease, formalize a shift toward methodologies that are more human-relevant, ethically sound, and scientifically robust (FDA Announces Plan to Phase Out Animal Testing Requirement for Monoclonal Antibodies and Other Drugs | FDA (no date); Directive - 2010/63 - EN - EUR-Lex, no date; Regulatory acceptance of new approach methodologies [NAMs] to reduce animal use testing | European Medicines Agency (EMA), no date; Sen. Paul, 2022). This is in line with the momentum behind New Approach Methodologies (NAMs), including hiPSCs and organoid technologies, that holds an unprecedented opportunity for disease modeling, toxicology, and therapeutic screening, taking advantage of stem cell field current and evolving bio-models (Regulatory acceptance of new approach methodologies (NAMs) to reduce animal use testing | European Medicines Agency [EMA], no date; Adler et al., 2008; Dekkers et al., 2016; Vlachogiannis et al., 2018; Shinozawa et al., 2021; Herpers et al., 2022; Pierson et al., 2025). In that sense, stem cell core facilities provide a critical role for stem-cell-based NAMs’ success by (1) implementing, validating, and disseminating hiPSC- and organoid-based platforms to reduce or replace animal use in preclinical studies; (2) maximize resource utilization under current budget constraints; (3) collaborate with institutions and regulators to establish validation frameworks that ensure scientific and translational credibility; and (4) deliver continuous education that empowers users across academia, industry, and translational settings to adopt scalable, predictive, and reproducible human-relevant systems. However, this scientific transition has not been matched by adequate funding mechanisms to implement and validate such models. Stem cell core facilities occupy a pivotal position in driving this transformation, yet their potential will remain underutilized without targeted support.

Funding initiatives should therefore prioritize the expansion and modernization of existing facilities, not only the creation of new ones. For example, the use of Dedicated Requests for Applications (RFAs)—similar to the CZI Imaging Scientists Award (https://chanzuckerberg.com/rfa/chan-zuckerberg-initiative-imaging-scientists/), but focused on stem cell core facilities infrastructure upgrades, providing support for technical staffing, and R&D/innovation, would strengthen research capacity in stem cell core facilities and accelerate technology transfer across centers like our consortia.

The consortia mentioned here represent an existing, well-established, highly active, and globally connected network of shared resource laboratories for stem-cell-based modeling. Leveraging this existing infrastructure, which was recently recognized by ISSCR with the 2026 ISSCR Public Service Award, is far more efficient than building parallel networks, ensuring maximum return on investment and greater scientific impact.

In summary, stem cell core facilities occupy a unique and essential niche in the biomedical research ecosystem. By centralizing advanced technologies, democratizing access to stem cell technologies, delivering tailored services, and nurturing a skilled research community, they not only empower scientists to tackle complex questions but also ensure that discoveries are reliable, reproducible, and impactful. As the field of stem cell biology continues to advance, these facilities will remain at the forefront, integrating emerging technologies into the wider landscape of biomedical innovation. In doing so, they serve not merely as service providers, but as key drivers of discovery, enablers of translational progress, and custodians of best practices.
