Can platelet to neutrophil ratio (PNR) serve as a viable alternative tool for monitoring sickle cell disease patients on hydroxyurea in low income countries?
Authors: Efobi Chilota Chibuife, Nri-Ezedi Chisom Adaobi, Franklin Odini, Habib Darbari Deepika, Nana-Bilkisu Habib, Campbell Andrew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuantification of foetal haemoglobin (HbF) via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) remains a critical parameter for monitoring therapeutic response to hydroxyurea (HU) in patients with sickle cell disease (SCD). However, its routine application is constrained in low-income settings such as Nigeria due to limited accessibility. The platelet-to-neutrophil ratio (PNR), an emerging inflammatory biomarker, has demonstrated prognostic relevance in several disease conditions. This study investigates the potential utility of PNR as a surrogate monitoring tool in SCD patients receiving HU therapy. This was a retrospective analysis of data derived from the Children’s National Hospital Natural History Study of Sickle Cell Disease, conducted in Washington, D.C., USA. Medical records of patients with SCD were reviewed from 1 September 2013 to 14 September 2023. Associations between PNR, HU use, HbF concentration, and reticulocyte count were evaluated. T-tests was used for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Regression analysis was used to evaluate the predictors of hydroxyurea use. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Of 437 patient records reviewed, 222 (50.8%) were male, and 312 (71.4%) were receiving HU. The majority (81%) had no history of transfusion. Patients on HU exhibited significantly higher platelet counts, HbF levels, and PNR values (p = 0.0008, p < 0.0001, and p < 0.0001, respectively). In multivariate logistic regression, both HbF (OR: 1.121; 95% CI: 1.051–1.196; p < 0.001) and PNR (OR: 1.014; 95% CI: 1.005–1.024; p = 0.0022) were independently associated with HU use. No significant associations were observed between HU use and age group, haemoglobin phenotype, transfusion history, or BMI (p > 0.05). The PNR demonstrates a significant association with HU use and HbF levels, indicating its potential as a low-cost, accessible biomarker for monitoring HU therapy in SCD. Although this analysis was based on data from a high-resource setting, the findings underscore the relevance of PNR as a feasible monitoring alternative in resource-constrained environments. Further validation in low-income contexts is warranted to establish population-specific reference intervals and inform clinical practice.Keywords: Sickle cell disease, Hydroxyurea, Platelet-to-neutrophil ratio, Foetal haemoglobin, Low-resource settings

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a hereditary haemolytic anaemia and multisystem disorder arising from a single point mutation in the β-globin gene, which results in the production of sickle haemoglobin (HbS). Upon deoxygenation, HbS polymerises, distorting erythrocyte morphology and triggering vaso-occlusive events [1]. These pathophysiological processes give rise to a spectrum of acute and chronic complications, which contribute to significant morbidity and mortality.

Inflammation is a critical mediator in the clinical expression of SCD and has been extensively documented in the literature [2–4]. Polymerised HbS initiates a cascade of inflammatory responses through endothelial activation, impaired blood flow, and leukocyte recruitment. Sickled erythrocytes adhere to the vascular endothelium, obstructing microcirculation and facilitating the activation and aggregation of neutrophils and platelets [5]. Neutrophilia has been independently associated with disease severity and adverse outcomes in individuals with SCD [6, 7]. Platelet activation further exacerbates this pathology by promoting adhesion to leukocytes, sickled erythrocytes, and the endothelium, thereby contributing to thrombotic complications such as pulmonary hypertension [8–10]. Additionally, elevated levels of platelet-monocyte and platelet-neutrophil aggregates have been identified in individuals with SCD, implicating these cellular interactions in the pathogenesis of vaso-occlusive crises [11, 12].

Hydroxyurea (HU) remains the cornerstone of disease-modifying therapy in SCD. Its cytoreductive properties lead to reductions in leukocyte and platelet counts, while simultaneously inducing the production of foetal haemoglobin (HbF), which possesses antisickling properties that improve clinical outcomes [13–16]. Monitoring HU adherence and therapeutic response typically involves quantifying HbF levels, with studies indicating that levels exceeding 20% are associated with fewer hospital admissions in paediatric cohorts [17]. In addition to other parameters like number of painful crises, hospital admissions and blood transfusions are also used to monitor hydroxyurea intake since changes in Hb F levels may not be significant in some patients. However, in low-resource settings such as Nigeria, routine HbF measurement is hindered by limited accessibility and cost constraints, necessitating alternative monitoring strategies.

The platelet-to-neutrophil ratio (PNR) has emerged as a potential inflammatory biomarker across a range of clinical conditions [18–20]. Efobi et al. [21] demonstrated an inverse association between PNR and SCD severity, suggesting that higher PNR values may correlate with milder disease phenotypes. Despite this promising evidence, there remains a paucity of research exploring the utility of PNR in monitoring HU response among patients with SCD. A related investigation reported a negative correlation between the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and HbF concentration in patients receiving HU therapy [22].

Given that platelet and neutrophil counts are readily obtainable from routine complete blood count (CBC) analyses, PNR may represent a cost-effective and accessible proxy for treatment monitoring in SCD. Considering the central role of these cell lines in SCD pathophysiology and their modulation by HU, it is imperative to explore the potential of PNR as a surrogate marker for HbF in resource-constrained environments.

This study, therefore, aims to assess the association between PNR and hydroxyurea use among paediatric and adolescent patients with SCD. It also seeks to evaluate whether PNR may serve as a feasible and affordable alternative to HbF assay for monitoring therapeutic response in low-income countries such as Nigeria, where laboratory infrastructure is often limited.

Methodology

Study design

This was a retrospective study of a subset of SCD patients followed up at Children’s National Hospital in Washington DC, USA. Medical records were extracted and reviewed from 1 st of September 2013 to 14th of September 2023 as part of the Children’s National Hospital Natural History Study of Sickle Cell Disease. The data included various demographic variables such as age, sex distribution, and hemoglobin phenotype, and other parameters such as use of hydroxyurea, history of blood transfusion, laboratory parameters like hemoglobin concentration, white blood cells count, neutrophil count, platelets, foetal hemoglobin, reticulocyte count, and key clinical variables like height and weight, which were used to calculate body mass index (BMI). The data was collected while the patients were in their steady state.

Retrospective data collection

The initial dataset comprised 2294 observations, representing a substantial data pool. Within this dataset, there were 527 unique patients, each associated with a variety of variables. To ensure data quality and relevance, a rigorous data cleaning process was performed. This involved the exclusion of patients with missing variables related to hydroxyurea intake, as well as those with a history of splenectomy or bone marrow transplantation using the trimming method. Furthermore, to maintain a focused patient population, individuals under 5 years of age were excluded, leaving a sample size of 437.

PNR (Platelet-Neutrophil Ratio) was calculated by dividing the platelet count by the absolute neutrophil count. An important consideration in data preparation was the treatment of outliers in the Platelet-Neutrophil Ratio (PNR).

Data analysis

These data processing steps were conducted to ensure the robustness and reliability of our subsequent statistical analyses, allowing for a more focused exploration of the relationship between platelet-neutrophil ratio and hydroxyurea intake in children with sickle cell disease.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the basic features of the data, including mean, median, standard deviation, and interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate, for continuous variables. Frequency and percentage distributions were used for categorical variables.

To assess the impact of hydroxyurea treatment, group comparisons were made between patients on hydroxyurea (Yes) and those not on treatment (No). This was done using appropriate statistical tests, such as t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Regression analysis was used to evaluate the predictors of hydroxyurea use. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Sex distribution was nearly equal, with 50.8% males and 49.2% females. The most common phenotype was HbSS (69.8%), followed by HbSC (20.8%), while the rest constituted a smaller proportion (9.4%). Discussion

Given the central role of neutrophils and platelets in SCD pathogenesis and the known cytoreductive effects of HU, assessing PNR as a surrogate monitoring tool is both timely and necessary. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the impact of HU on PNR among SCD patients—both those on treatment and those not—while exploring the feasibility of using PNR as an alternative to fetal haemoglobin (HbF) assay for resource-constrained contexts such as Nigeria.

In our cohort, 312 (71.4%) of the participants were on HU, and the majority (305; 69.8%) had the HbSS genotype. This aligns with existing guidelines that recommend initiating HU from as early as six months in infants diagnosed with SCD [23, 24]. However, this contrasts sharply with the situation in sub-Saharan Africa, where HU uptake remains low due to factors such as limited availability, cost, and low awareness among healthcare providers [25, 26].

When we compared haematological parameters between patients on HU and those not receiving the drug, we observed a lower total white blood cell (WBC) count among the HU group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.1216). However, there was a statistically significant reduction in neutrophil count (p = 0.0020), consistent with the cytoreductive properties of HU and in agreement with prior studies [27, 28]. Interestingly, patients on HU had significantly lower haemoglobin levels (p < 0.0001), a finding that contrasts with the literature [27, 28]. This discrepancy may be explained by the assumption that patients not on HU were enrolled in chronic transfusion programmes, which could have elevated their haemoglobin concentrations but our knowledge is limited by the retrospective nature of this study. One inherent limitation of our study is its retrospective design, which precluded us from accounting for baseline haemoglobin levels prior to HU initiation, as we only analysed the most recent laboratory values available.

Expectedly, total WBC and neutrophil counts were reduced in patients on HU. However, contrary to earlier reports [27, 28], we found that platelet counts were significantly elevated in this group (p = 0.0008). It is unclear why platelet count was higher amongst patients on hydroxyurea considering its cytoreductive effect. However, a study performed on SCD patients in intensive care showed that higher platelet counts was associated with improved survival. [29]. In as much as the study is not related to ours because we studied patients in steady state, there may be a need for further research on the role of platelet count on clinical outcome of SCD patients.

Additionally, our analysis revealed a five-fold increase in HbF levels among patients on HU compared to those not on treatment (p < 0.0001), consistent with established evidence supporting the efficacy of HU in inducing HbF production [17, 27, 28]. Hydroxyurea has also been reported to decrease the rate of transfusion, hospitalization, spleen size and significantly increased Hb, RBC indices. [30]

Notably, PNR was significantly higher in patients on HU, regardless of haemoglobin phenotype (p < 0.0001). Efobi et al. [21] had earlier reported a negative correlation between PNR and disease severity in SCD, with higher PNR values corresponding to lower severity scores and fewer clinical complications. They further found that PNR was a superior marker of disease severity compared to other commonly used ratios such as the platelet-to-lymphocyte and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios. While their study population was not on HU, our findings which showed a higher PNR in patients on HU (85.60 (59.83-118.97) vs. 60.40 (44.20–88.20) aligned with theirs in that elevated PNR appears to reflect clinical stability—particularly in the setting of HU therapy. This appears that PNR may serve a dual role of monitoring hydroxyurea intake as well as disease severity. Unfortunately, we did not assess the relation between PNR and disease severity in this study.

Quantification of foetal haemoglobin remains the standard method for monitoring HU adherence and response [31]. Although several Nigerian studies have demonstrated HU’s effectiveness in improving clinical outcomes in SCD [32–34], few have discussed patient monitoring in detail. This gap is not surprising, given the limited availability and high cost of HbF testing in most healthcare settings across Nigeria. As such, our study sought to explore a more accessible alternative. While the PNR was elevated for all SCD patients with HbSS and HbSC hemoglobin phenotypes, the same could not be said for the fetal hemoglobin, which was found to be elevated only in the HbSS patients. This makes one wonder if hydroxyurea is mostly effective in HbSS patients when compared to the others. It is interesting that only HbSS patients had a striking result with hydroxyurea use. There is no general agreement on HU treatment for other SCD variants, such as HbSC disease.[35] Unlike HbSS, hydroxyurea use is not routinely recommended for individuals with HbSC because of the perceived high-risk to benefit ratio as they tend to have milder disease. [36]

Few studies have been done on the efficacy of hydroxyurea on HbSC patients. Lutchman-Jones and his colleagues reported a reduction in neutrophil, white cell and reticulocyte counts in HbSC patients on HU therapy but recommended a multicentered placebo controlled trial to determine the efficacy of hydroxyurea in HbSC patients. [37] Contrary to our findings, Miller et al. [38] reported an elevated fetal hemoglobin level in HbSC patients on hydroxyurea. It is possible that the HbSC patients in our study did not have a severe disease because HbSC disease is not usually as severe as that of HbSS patients, and therefore not on the maximum dose required to elicit a significant laboratory response. Our knowledge is also limited by the retrospective nature of this study.

We found that both HbF (OR: 1.121; 95% CI: 1.051–1.196; p < 0.001) and PNR (OR: 1.014; 95% CI: 1.005–1.024; p = 0.0022) were significantly associated with HU use in multivariate analysis adjusted for transfusion status and other clinical covariates. There were no significant associations between HU use and age, haemoglobin phenotype, transfusion history, or BMI (p > 0.05). Because this study is a new concept, there is insufficient literature available for comparison with our findings.

Although the data for this study were obtained from a high-income setting where patients receive standardised, comprehensive care, we believe the findings are relevant to low-income countries. One could argue that there is a lot of disparity in the health care system between where the data used for this study was generated and the low income countries. The fact that a local study has also confirmed the PNR is associated with less severe disease, [21] it can be tailored to the peculiarities of the low income country and then used to monitor patients on hydroxyurea, as well as disease severity. In such contexts, PNR—calculated from routinely available full blood count parameters—could serve as a practical alternative for monitoring HU response. While it is not a replacement for HbF quantification, PNR offers valuable clinical insights and could help inform decisions on dose adjustments, particularly in settings where HbF measurement is not feasible.

Limitation of the study

This study was limited by its retrospective design, which restricted our ability to assess real-time changes in haematological parameters and precluded access to baseline values prior to HU initiation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that PNR is a viable and effective metric for assessing HU treatment response in patients with SCD. The significant association between PNR and HU use— YES (85.60 (59.83-118.97) vs. NO(60.40 (44.20–88.20) p = 0.0001, alongside its correlation with HbF levels (p = 0.0022**) suggests that PNR could serve as a low-cost alternative monitoring tool in resource-limited settings.
