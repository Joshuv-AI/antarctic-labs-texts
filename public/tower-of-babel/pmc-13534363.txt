Experts’ Perspectives on Psychosocial Needs of Couples Following Traumatic Brain Injury: A Qualitative Study from a Tertiary Care Centre
Authors: Karthik Shetty, Thiruchengodu Raju Kanmani, Subhas Kanti Konar, Srikanth Pallerla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraumatic brain injury (TBI) has an immense impact on families, particularly spouses, who face significant challenges such as changes in roles and responsibilities, relational dynamics and financial instability. The study aims to explore the psychosocial needs and concerns of couples coping with TBI from experts across various fields.PurposeThe study purpose was to explore the psychosocial needs and concerns of couples coping with TBI from experts across various fields.MethodsThe current study adopted an exploratory qualitative approach, involving in-depth, face-to-face interviews with experts. Nine experts from six professional backgrounds were selected based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligibility criteria included a minimum of 5 years of expertise in TBI-related fields, proficiency in English and willingness to provide informed consent. The interviews were audio-recorded with the participants’ consent.ResultsExperts included neuropsychologists, psychiatric social workers, neurosurgeons, neuro-rehabilitation experts, physiotherapists and clinical nurses. A total of five themes have been identified, namely education needs, emotional needs, family needs, financial needs and support needs.ConclusionThe study on the psychosocial needs of couples from experts’ perspectives in India is unique in nature. The results of this study will support the development and implementation of tailored psychosocial interventions specifically designed for couples in India. Various stakeholders engaged in the management of TBIs emphasised the critical importance of addressing the needs of these couples and ensuring the timely provision of personalised psychosocial support.Keywords: India, persons living with traumatic brain injury, psychosocial needs, traumatic brain injury

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (2021), traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a significant global health concern. 1 This can occur from direct contact to the head or when the brain is shaken within the skull, such as from a blast or whiplash during a car accident. Affected individuals may experience memory loss before or after the injury. The severity of TBI is determined at the time of the injury and is based on the length of loss of consciousness, the length of either memory loss or disorientation and how responsive the individual was after the injury. 2 Therefore, it has been called a silent epidemic and causes more death and disability. 3 The consequences of TBI not only cause problems for individuals with the injury but can also greatly affect their relationship with spouses. As a result of this, there will be a shift in roles and responsibilities within the relationship.4, 5 The caring of an injured patient is not limited; it is a continuous process in which patients may need help for the rest of their lives. The long-term care required for individuals with TBI can also lead to a decline in the health-related quality of life of their caregivers. 6 Once discharged from the hospital, patients frequently depend on family members for both emotional and practical support in daily activities. 7

It is estimated that 50% of family members suffer from some kind of psychological disorder due to caregiver overload. 8 Families are often unprepared for the sudden changes caused by TBI, leading to disruptions in family dynamics and unhealthy functioning. Researchers have documented a significant and lasting increase in unhealthy family functioning following TBI.9, 10 This can lead to neglect of the patient’s needs6, 8 and heightened physical, emotional and psychological strain among immediate caregivers. 11 TBI is a complex pathology that requires multidisciplinary management by a large number of professionals. 12 However, this structure is notably fragmented in clinical practice and, although communication between all these teams is beginning to improve, there is still a long way to go towards unified management. 13

Healthcare systems play a crucial role in addressing the burden of long-term caregiving, particularly for patients with ongoing dependencies. 14 Identifying caregiver needs through educational and psychosocial interventions is essential for improving the well-being of both caregivers and patients, ultimately leading to better outcomes in TBI management. 15 All these factors need to be considered when designing a clinical study. These considerations were integral to the design of the current study, which sought insights from experts across various fields regarding the psychosocial needs and concerns of couples coping with TBI.

Methods

Design

The study used a qualitative method to evaluate the study variables, as it explores and provides deeper insights into real-world problems. 16 In India, psychosocial needs, issues, concerns and experiences of couples in which one spouse has experienced TBI have not been studied extensively from experts’ perspectives. Consequently, an exploratory qualitative approach was adopted in the current study, involving in-depth, face-to-face interviews with experts. Since no structured framework exists in this area in India, an exploratory approach was used, as it was appropriate for generating new insights and guiding future interventions.

Setting and Participants

This study was conducted in the Department of Neurosurgery at a tertiary care centre in South India. Nine experts from six professional backgrounds were selected based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligibility criteria included a minimum of 5 years of expertise in TBI-related fields, proficiency in English and willingness to provide informed consent. Experts uninvolved in clinical or academic activities were excluded. Convenience sampling was used to recruit experts. The study was conducted from July to September 2023.

Data Collection

Semi-structured Questionnaire

The researcher developed a semi-structured questionnaire to collect the socio-demographic details of the experts, such as educational qualifications, area of specialisation, years of experience and department.

Interview Guide

A semi-structured in-depth interview guide was prepared by the researcher based on the existing review of literature. Six professionals with experience in TBI patient care and caregiving reviewed and validated the guide. The professionals who validated the interview guide were not involved in the in-depth interview. The data collection was initiated after the completion of the validation process. The experts were selected based on their previous expertise in working with TBI and their caregivers, and each of them provided insight into qualitative research approaches. In-depth Interviews

The interview was conducted face-to-face in the experts’ respective departments. A prior appointment was taken, and an initial introduction to the research purpose was explained to the experts. After obtaining consent, the researcher started the interview using the validated interview guide and used appropriate probes to assist with the collection of the required information (Table 2). Experts were neuropsychologist (1), psychiatric social workers (2), neurosurgeon (1), physiotherapist (1), clinical nurses (3) and neuro-rehabilitation expert (1). Data collection was stopped after the ninth interview, as saturation had been reached. It was assessed through ongoing analysis, and no new codes emerged beyond this point, confirming the adequacy of the data. The interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed into English and had an average duration of 1 h and 15 min, with the shortest interview lasting 1 h.

Analysis of Data

Shortly after each interview, a summary of the data was compiled and transcribed into Word. Braun and Clarke’s (2006) 17 thematic analysis utilising an inductive approach was used to analyse the data manually. The researcher reviewed the data several times to ensure consistency. To ensure uniformity, two transcribed Word documents and audio recordings were provided to two experts who were not involved in the study. Initial coding was performed, and codes were iteratively refined and categorised into hierarchies. The final themes were reviewed and discussed with three peer scholars for consistency and verification. Upon reaching a consensus, the final themes were established.

Results

The socio-demographic profile of the experts is described in Table 2. The interview included nine experts. Six (66.7%) of them were male consultants, and three (33.3%) were female consultants who worked in the emergency and head injury unit. Experts include neuropsychologists, psychiatric social workers, neurosurgeons, neuro-rehabilitation experts, physiotherapists and clinical nurses. Although the experts’ age range was 38–55, the average age was between 48 and 49. Many of the specialists had substantial professional experience (15+ years), with experience ranging from 6 to 20+ years. Theme 1: Educational Needs

According to experts, one of the primary needs of couples is education about the patient’s condition; therefore, education is crucial since there is a need. Clear information about brain injury, its types, causes, short- and long-term effects, expectations about recovery, prevention strategies, medication management, rehabilitation requirements and legal education for benefit eligibility must be provided to patients, their spouses and their families. Couples should have thorough information and comprehension of all these aspects, as it will help them understand the patient’s condition, provide better care and cope with the situation. Unmet educational needs can be addressed by providing education to couples, leading to better patient outcomes. Hence, they expressed that one element of the intervention should be family education, which should be considered while preparing an intervention package in the future to enhance couples’ well-being.

Educating About Head Injury

They expressed that educating couples about head injury is a crucial element in providing care. This theme stresses the importance of education in terms of improving the knowledge of couples, to strengthen their relationship and to have better psychosocial outcomes. A comprehensive educational training programme is essential.

I feel they need to know about the traumatic brain injury, like how it happens, the prognosis of this illness, how it recovers and how it is going to impact them later. This is more important. This itself will solve many of their doubts, problems. (Expert 1: Psychiatric Social Work Consultant)

Meeting Other Professionals

According to experts, this subtheme describes the importance of multidisciplinary team treatment for couples. They should know the importance of various other healthcare professionals, such as neurologists, neuropsychologists, occupational therapists, speech therapists and social workers. If they are informed about the roles played by different healthcare providers, this would help them in terms of reaching them during any health emergency.

It is not always true that only one specialist can help the patient. It is important to work with other multidisciplinary team where all of them help patient together. Couples should know that they will have to meet other doctors on need basis such as occupation, speech therapist, social workers and rehabilitation experts. They can easily meet them in an emergency if they are properly informed about this. (Expert 2: Physiotherapist)

Medication Management

This subtheme focuses on how important it is to enlighten couples about the medication. They have stated that couples should be provided with information regarding the medicines, possible adverse reactions, the value of following recommended dosage schedules and the significance of routine medication reviews. Couples can be empowered to actively participate in decisions regarding medication, reduce problems and improve treatment efficacy with this knowledge through proper education.

Both the couple’s should be aware of the medication, especially the one who supervise the medication must know all the medicines which his/her spouses take, dosage, its side effects, when to stop medicines. (Expert 3: Neurosurgery Consultant)

Recovery and Follow-up

This subtheme reflects on the importance of knowing about recovery and follow-up services. Experts viewed recovery from TBI not only as physical healing but also as psychological and social healing. They expressed that the recovery trajectory is continuous. They also highlighted the gap in follow-up. Couples tend to miss follow-ups consistently, which is not a good sign of recovery; hence, stressing on such component would be required for couples when we educate them on TBI.

I have seen couples frequently missing follow-ups, and when they turn up to OPD for consultation, they report of physical and psychological issues, probably due to lack of resources, distance from hospitals or financial constraints, families often discontinue follow-up. I would always encourage including this component in the psychosocial management. (Expert 03, Neurosurgery Consultant)

Home-based Skills

Experts highlighted the importance of teaching basic routines, such as meal planning, medication management and wound management to couples. These skills not only enhance their knowledge and skills but also reduce dependence on external help and empower the couple to manage together.

I think home-based skills should be taught to couples, and this is important component of education. It may not be possible to teach everything for us in the hospital due to shortage of time; however, including this component in the module would be a great source for couples. (Expert 4: Neuro-rehabilitation Consultant)

Theme 2: Emotional Needs

As experts reported, couples may frequently encounter various issues or problems within their relationship and may struggle to solve them because one partner has a TBI. They need to be trained in adaptive strategies and problem-solving techniques to overcome the obstacles they face and re-establish confidence in their ability to make appropriate decisions. Anger is one of the common emotions reported by many TBI patients and their spouses, according to experts. Emotional difficulties are caused by TBI, and couples should be educated about the deviations of emotions, how to identify early warning signs, how to control their anger and how to seek professional help. In order to help them get back on track as a family, the intervention package should include information on coping approaches, communication skills, patience and understanding.

Problem-solving Skill

As experts reported, the dynamics of problem-solving in a relationship can change, and couples may frequently come up with various issues or problems within them. They may struggle to solve problems because one partner has TBI. They are required to be trained in adaptive strategies and problem-solving techniques to overcome the problems they are facing and re-establish confidence in their ability to make appropriate decisions.

Couples should be well prepared about the all possible problems that would arise due to patient’s traumatic brain injury. This would impact on their quality of relationship they share; therefore they should be taught how particular problems can be solved being a spouse and being a PLwTBI within them. (Expert 5: Clinical Nursing)

Anger Management

This subtheme emphasises the need to deal with emotions, especially anger. Experts have expressed the importance of developing constructive techniques in order to properly handle anger in a relationship. This should also include how persons living with TBI (PLwTBI) has to identify their early warning signs of anger and how to control anger. Non-injured spouses have to know how to respond to injured spouses’ anger; as per experts, there is a need to provide proper management to prevent family discord.

Anger is commonly reported problems by the patient and their family members. They are supposed to know all the changes in the personality is brought by TBI. One step above this is training patient and their spouses how to manage anger is essential. Teach them, being a patient how he/she can deal with it and being a spouse what she can do at the time. I think this must be part of the intervention manual. (Expert 4: Neuro-rehabilitation Consultant)

Theme 3: Family Needs

One of the other main needs that couples frequently have is family/personal needs, as per experts. According to the majority of experts, sexual problems and changes in roles and responsibilities are common among them. Couples may not be able to engage in sexual activity, or the frequency of sexual encounters might be reduced compared to before. In terms of roles and responsibilities, the injured spouse may no longer be able to take on the same roles and responsibilities they once did, and additional roles may also fall on the non-injured spouse, such as taking care of the household, children, finances, decisions of the family, shopping responsibility, dropping off and picking up children from school and taking care of in-laws. An intervention programme for couples should include sex education, such as how to restart sexual activity, methods to resume sexual activity like before and ways to manage new roles and responsibilities to maintain a healthy family relationship.

Management of Sexual Issues

As experts expressed, this subtheme recognises that intimate relationships and sexual functioning among couples will be affected following one’s TBI. It highlights that it is important to focus on these issues in order to enhance couples’ healthy family life. Less attention has been given to this, but this is the most affected area, which should be handled through proper psychosocial intervention.

India being a very cultural country, we don’t talk much about sexual aspect, but that is most challenging part for the couples. Like some patient with the frontal lobe injury, they become hypersexualised and become more demanding for sex with her partner or some may completely lose interest over sex. These two are very commonly reported problems. This needs to desensitised to them and give proper intervention. (Expert 6: Neuropsychologist)

Roles and Responsibilities

Experts have said that focusing on reassessing and reassigning the roles and responsibilities is important in the context of the family and the couple’s relationship. Therefore, both of them are required to be adaptable and flexible in terms of managing the family. Developing an intervention to structure their family roles and responsibilities is important, as experts have identified.

There may be complete role reversal in the family due to injury in one of the spouses. The role and responsibilities which patient was taking care is now would fall on non-injured spouse, for example, dropping the children to school, getting grocery or financial role. There may be a role reversal due to injury. They may not be aware about it, though they are aware, they might not know how to solve this issue; therefore, it is important to tackle the issue to prevent further relationship breakdown. (Expert 6: Neuropsychologist)

Religiousness

Experts have stressed that religiousness can serve as an important coping resource for couples and families facing the long-term challenges of TBI. They perceived that shared religious practices, such as praying together at home or engaging in rituals together, often strengthen marital and family bonds, as well as help families to find hope, trust and meaning in difficult circumstances in their lives. This component will make it more culturally sensitive for couples.

Our society is more cultural base. I have seen couples talking more in religious ways. Most of the time they say that “I trust god, he will help us”. This reflects the connection with spirituality. I would want you to add this in the module as one of the components. (Expert 9: Psychiatric Social Work Consultant)

Theme 4: Financial Needs

Subject experts highlighted that financial need is one of the most frequently mentioned issues and requirements. Experts opined that financial stability is one of the key predictors of a healthy family. The family’s main breadwinner may become bedridden after an injury, and other family members may not be able to provide financial support or may not provide support at all. On the other hand, the spouse may struggle to manage the household, and children’s needs may arise. Additionally, the injured spouse may require long-term medication, follow-up hospital visits, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), physiotherapy, occupational therapy and so on. Hence, social work intervention should include welfare counselling, disability benefits and awareness about overall financial resources for couples.

Financial Support for Treatment

The subtheme describes the importance of financial resources and knowledge in order to lessen the financial burden. It is observed that when the main head of the family is hospitalised, it obviously causes problems in meeting their medical expenses, such as long-term medication, taking them to the hospital for follow-up, MRI, physiotherapy and occupational therapy. All these are important for better health outcomes of PLwTBI. Therefore, the intervention should include welfare counselling, disability benefits and awareness about overall financial sources for couples.

Financial is the major concern which they all going through. They would have saved the patient by taking lakhs of rupees loan from someone; they would have sold whatever property they had; now they may be struggling to repay the loan as main earning member is bedridden, cannot go for work. Taking care of injured person’s medical expenses is not so easy. Wife or the husband may be trying to adjust money for his further treatment in hospitals or other expenses. (Expert 6: Neuropsychologist)

Financial Support like Disability Card and Pension

This particular subtheme emphasises the importance of long-term financial stability through external financial sources. As they noted, most couples in which one person has TBI are not aware of the UDID card and its importance. Hence, they should be aware of having a UDID card, pensions, disability cards and other welfare benefits available in the hospitals. The intervention should cover where and how they can enrol for financial support, which can act as a vital safety net for their finances.

It can be difficult to predict whether patient will have temporary or permanent disability, but in most of the cases, patients will be disabled and dependent on someone. That is the reason it is better to educate the spouse with injury and spouse without injury about the same. If it lasts more than, they can be availed UDID card, so that they will get some amount which can be used for patient’s treatment. (Expert 8: Clinical Nursing)

Theme 5: Support Needs

According to subject experts, following a brain injury, there will be changes in family dynamics within the relationship, and both spouses need some kind of help, either in the form of emotional or financial support. Following a brain injury to one of the spouses, they may possibly lose all support from friends and relatives. Couples may not receive financial or emotional support from their extended family or other relatives as they did before. Since they believe no one is supporting them, this can make them feel more anxious and tense. They will be able to overcome family dysfunction brought on by TBI if they have greater social support, as they would feel more secure and better able to handle the situation, including marital conflict, role strain, emotional distancing, financial difficulties and unequal responsibilities. This shows that well-being depends on having a strong support system. Therefore, social work interventions need to focus on helping them enhance their support system by enhancing their knowledge, strengthening their family connection, networking with community support groups and building skills to maintain emotional, practical and financial support.

Support Groups for Couples

Support groups help couples deal with challenges by offering practical guidance and emotional support. Experts have suggested that all affected spouses and their families should have a proper support group to meet once in a while and talk about their difficulties. It is important to have interaction with people who are having similar experiences and exchange their coping mechanisms, ideas and suggestions, so that they can implement all these in their lives. Altogether, it will relax their mood as well.

The groups for couples are very important, I feel. Like in other Western countries, the same consistent groups should be there for this problematic population to discuss their own problems within the groups. This will help them feel that they are no longer alone in facing problems. I have heard couples reporting of loss of support or no support at all; therefore, creating a group like support groups would really help the couples. (Expert 7: Clinical Nursing)

Socialisation and Participation

This reflects how couples frequently face social isolation. Experts expressed that it is important to have social connections and participation to avoid isolation. This places importance on methods for overcoming obstacles to couples’ mental health and quality of life. They are advised to be a part of social activities, family functions and community gatherings; this will enhance their overall well-being. The question of ‘how’ should be addressed through the psychosocial intervention.

One of the many changes brought by TBI is social isolation and less socialisation due to their personal reasons or lack of interest, but they are supposed to be the part of society to have adequate socialisation. Being a therapist, you must address the issue and encourage them to do so. Gradually, their well-being will be up. (Expert 9: Psychiatric Social Work Consultant)

Discussion

The aim of the current study was to explore the psychosocial needs and concerns of couples from experts’ perspectives. To the best of our knowledge and based on a comprehensive search of PubMed, Scopus and Google Scholar, no previous studies have explicitly explored the psychosocial needs and concerns from experts’ perspectives globally. Considering this fact, the current study results were discussed in relation to the perspectives of patients and spouses identified in existing literature. The first emerging need from the experts is educational needs. They stated that couples should know about brain injury, its causes, effects, follow-up, the importance of meeting other professionals and medication management. According to spouses in one of the earlier research, many spouses expressed a great need for information and explanation, and uncertainty regarding the prognosis and expected impact of the injury was distressing.18, 19 According to one of the studies, spouses may believe that they are not receiving all the information they require, that they are not being sufficiently involved in decision-making and that their knowledge of the injured person is not being appropriately taken into consideration. Clinicians may make them feel emotionally abandoned. 19 Different professionals’ inconsistent recommendations can be perceived as incompetence and cause patients to lose faith in the hospital staff’s capacity to deliver the best treatment. 20 It is well known that spouses and experts have noted that couples require education.

The experts also identified emotional needs, considering anger control and problem-solving techniques. These are the two main things that the intervention should cover. Anger in injured spouses is one of the major problems.21, 22 This behaviour has a negative impact on interpersonal relationships; spouses and family members report losing their pre-injury romantic relationships and being especially afraid of their survivors’ anger.23–25 In comparison to other health problems, caregiver stress is particularly high for those caring for survivors of acquired brain injury (ABI), and uncontrollable anger is a significant contributor to this burden.21, 26 This burden might cause uncontrollable emotions in non-injured spouses.

The other concern that couples usually express is sexual difficulties, as per experts. According to a previous study, many patients reported experiencing problems with their sexual performance, such as decreased sexual desire, difficulty getting an erection, difficulty reaching orgasm and decreased frequency of intercourse. 27 Conversely, the desire for sexual connection may lessen for the spouse who is not injured. With someone who no longer loves, who feels alienated, who seems detached and uncaring or who is prone to violence, intimacy might be challenging.28, 29

In addition to altering roles and duties, the injured individual may need support with a number of daily tasks, such as mobility and self-care. 30 The spouse may need to take over some of the everyday tasks that were previously handled by the injured individual, or they may need to be more involved. In order to prevent additional family problems, experts have stated that one of the interventions should be the management of their sexual functioning and management of their duties and responsibilities. As per the experts, these needs should be included in the couple’s well-being module when developing by including psychoeducation, problem-solving, decision-making skills, communication, sex education, enhancement of social support and so on.

According to the experts, financial need is another need. When their primary provider is bedridden, couples often express this. A previous study discovered that stress might be caused by financial difficulties. The injured and/or their spouse may have to switch to a lower-paying job or quit their job entirely. Additional expenses might also be incurred for ongoing medical and rehabilitation requirements.28, 31 According to experts, long-term patients’ medical and non-medical care can put a heavy financial burden on their families. Hence, those families should be identified and given proper interventions to address their financial issues through various government welfare schemes and hospital schemes, so that this would at least lessen the financial burden within the hospital.

Another important need that couples require is support, as they expressed that they might lose social support after one’s TBI. This loss of support would have an impact on the overall family dynamics. A study conducted in the past concluded that improving social support is a crucial component of treating mental and neurological diseases. 32 Consequently, nearly all specialists have stated that strengthening their support network would enable them to manage the family in a healthy manner. One of the interventions for couples may be to strengthen their support network. A recent systematic review concluded that developing rehabilitation programmes for persons with TBI and identifying psychosocial factors is essential for their quality of life. 33

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

According to our best understanding, this is the first study in India to explore the psychosocial needs of persons living with TBI and their non-injured spouses using in-depth interviews from experts’ perspectives. The study used convenience sampling, which may limit the representativeness of the sample and the generalisability of the findings.

Implications

The present study examined the perceived psychosocial needs of the injured and non-injured spouses from experts’ perspectives post-hospitalisation. These insights from the experts will help us develop comprehensive psychosocial interventions for the couples to enhance their overall well-being.

Recommendations

The experts’ perspectives and experiences of the needs and concerns of the couples in which one spouse has sustained a TBI would be considered while developing psychosocial interventions. The intervention module can be tested for feasibility or effectiveness later with couples as part of the research. The intervention programme would be used in day-to-day clinical activities in terms of managing both the injured and non-injured spouse.

Conclusion

The study on the psychosocial needs of couples from experts’ perspectives in India is unique in nature. The results of this study will support the development and implementation of tailored psychosocial interventions specifically designed for couples in India. Various stakeholders engaged in the management of TBIs emphasised the critical importance of addressing the needs of these couples and ensuring the timely provision of personalised psychosocial support.
