Ganglion Impar Neurolysis With 60% Alcohol for Refractory Post‐Traumatic Coccygodynia: A Case Report With Sustained Relief
Authors: Pradeep Kumar, Ashish Dahal, Sujan Ghimire, Ritika Bhatta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFor patients with post‐traumatic coccygodynia refractory to conservative measures, fluoroscopy‐guided ganglion impar neurolysis using 60% alcohol is a highly effective and minimally invasive intervention. This technique should be considered a viable treatment option prior to pursuing more invasive surgical procedures, offering the potential for significant and sustained pain relief.Keywords: alcohol ablation, coccygodynia, ganglion impar, interventional pain management, neurolysis, refractory pain

Introduction

Coccygodynia, or pain localized to the coccyx and surrounding area, is a debilitating condition that accounts for approximately 1% of musculoskeletal disorders [1]. The pain often worsens with sitting, prolonged immobility, or direct pressure, significantly impacting patients' quality of life. While the etiology varies, ranging from trauma (e.g., falls, childbirth) to idiopathic causes, the underlying mechanism frequently involves inflammation, instability, or neural irritation of the coccygeal region [2].

First‐line management typically includes conservative measures such as oral analgesics (NSAIDs, acetaminophen), ergonomic adjustments (coccygeal cushions), and physical therapy. However, 20%–30% of patients experience refractory symptoms despite these interventions, necessitating advanced approaches [3]. In such cases, interventional techniques targeting the ganglion impar a sympathetic ganglion supplying the coccyx have demonstrated efficacy. Ganglion impar block (with local anesthetics or steroids) and neurolysis (using chemical agents like 60%–100% alcohol or phenol) are well‐documented options for sustained pain relief [4].

This report presents a case of chronic coccygodynia unresponsive to conservative therapy, successfully managed with ganglion impar neurolysis using 60% medical alcohol. The discussion highlights procedural nuances, outcomes, and evidence supporting this intervention in refractory cases.

Case Presentation

Informed consent for publication of this case report was obtained from the patient. A 30‐year‐old female presented with a 3‐year history of gradually worsening coccygeal pain following a fall injury. The pain was described as a deep, dull ache with intensity fluctuating between 3 and 8 on the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), significantly aggravated by sitting and partially relieved upon standing or lying down. There was no prior history of inflammatory arthritis, autoimmune disorders, or chronic back pain. Despite receiving two sessions of saddle block injections (unknown medication) at a local center 2 years prior, she experienced no meaningful pain relief.

On examination, her vitals were stable, with no signs of pallor, icterus, or dehydration. The musculoskeletal assessment revealed localized tenderness over the coccyx without swelling or deformity, while lumbar spine range of motion remained intact. Provocative tests, including FABER, FAIR, and straight leg raise (SLR), were negative, ruling out sacroiliac joint dysfunction or radiculopathy. Neurological examination showed no deficits in motor strength, reflexes, or sensation in the lower limbs.

Laboratory investigations, including ESR, uric acid, anti‐CCP, and RA factor, were within normal limits, excluding inflammatory or metabolic etiologies. An MRI of the lumbosacral spine revealed a central disc prolapse at L5–S1 without neural compression, along with a displaced coccygeal fracture, likely post‐traumatic in origin. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of post‐traumatic coccygodynia refractory to conservative management was established.

Initial treatment included naproxen (500 mg twice daily) and duloxetine (30 mg once daily) for central sensitization, along with activity modification and a coccygeal cushion. However, after 3 months, her pain persisted (NRS 6–8), prompting consideration of interventional therapy. She subsequently underwent fluoroscopy‐guided ganglion impar neurolysis with 60% medical alcohol. Fluoroscopic images confirming needle placement were obtained but are not available for publication due to institutional data management protocols. Under aseptic precautions, 5 mL of 1% lignocaine was first injected around the coccyx for local anesthesia, followed by precise needle placement confirmation using contrast dye. A total of 4 mL of 60% alcohol was then injected into the ganglion impar. The procedure was well‐tolerated, with only mild transient discomfort.

At her 1‐month follow‐up, she reported significant pain reduction (NRS 2–3) and improved functional capacity, allowing her to resume daily activities without limitation. By 3 months post‐procedure, her pain relief remained sustained, reinforcing the efficacy of chemical neurolysis in refractory coccygodynia.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case demonstrates that fluoroscopy‐guided ganglion impar neurolysis with 60% alcohol can provide substantial and sustained relief for patients with refractory post‐traumatic coccygodynia. The mechanism of action targeted chemical ablation of sympathetic nociceptive fibers offers a durable solution when conservative treatments fail. The success of this intervention hinges on precise technique under image guidance and careful patient selection based on clinical and radiological findings. This minimally invasive procedure should be considered a key step in the treatment algorithm before resorting to more invasive surgical options, offering a favorable risk–benefit profile for suitable candidates.

Discussion

Coccygodynia is a debilitating condition characterized by pain in and around the coccyx, affecting less than 1% of the population [5]. The etiology is multifactorial, with trauma, obesity, predisposing coccygeal morphology, and hypermobility being common contributors [6]. Women are five times more likely to develop this condition due to anatomical differences, childbirth‐related stress, and hormonal influences on joint laxity. Patients typically present with localized pain exacerbated by sitting, often worsening during menstruation due to increased pelvic floor tension. Physical examination usually reveals tenderness over the coccyx, particularly in post‐traumatic cases, while associated symptoms like dyspareunia further complicate the clinical picture [7].

Diagnosis relies on a thorough clinical assessment supplemented by imaging. Functional X‐rays, CT scans, or MRI can identify structural abnormalities such as fractures, dislocations, or degenerative changes. In this case, MRI confirmed a coccygeal fracture with dislocation, aligning with the patient's history of trauma.

Conservative management remains the first‐line approach, including NSAIDs, physiotherapy (e.g., levator ani and rectal massage), and ergonomic adjustments like donut pillows [8]. Adjuvants such as tricyclic antidepressants or anticonvulsants may be added for refractory pain [1]. However, only 25% of patients achieve significant relief with these measures [9].

For persistent cases, interventional options like ganglion impar block or neurolysis offer promising outcomes. Ideal candidates for this intervention are those with well‐localized coccygodynia, confirmed refractoriness to a minimum 3‐month course of conservative management, and, ideally, radiological correlation of a structural anomaly, as seen in our patient. The ganglion impar, a sympathetic ganglion innervating the perineal region, is a critical target for pain modulation [10]. A UK‐based study of eight patients with treatment‐resistant coccygodynia reported 75% experiencing relief (four complete, two partial) after ganglion impar block at 6‐month follow‐up [11]. Similarly, chemical neurolysis using agents like alcohol or phenol has demonstrated efficacy in refractory cases [12].

The choice of a neurolytic agent involves weighing efficacy, safety, and availability. While phenol is a common agent, we selected 60% alcohol for this procedure as it provides a potent neurolytic effect while potentially mitigating the risks of tissue irritation associated with higher concentrations (e.g., 90%–100%) in this anatomically compact region. This aligns with a growing body of evidence supporting the use of intermediate‐concentration alcohol for peripheral neurolysis [13]. Other interventional options for refractory cases include pulsed radiofrequency ablation, which offers a nonneurolytic, neuromodulatory approach, and coccygectomy, which is reserved as a last resort due to its invasive nature and associated risks like infection and poor wound healing [3].

While highly effective, ganglion impar neurolysis is not without potential complications. These can include transient neuritis, infection, rectal puncture, or partial efficacy. A thorough understanding of the sacrococcygeal anatomy and the use of fluoroscopic guidance are paramount to minimizing these risks.

In this patient, conservative measures failed to alleviate her chronic pain. Fluoroscopy‐guided ganglion impar neurolysis with 60% alcohol was performed, resulting in significant symptom reduction. This aligns with existing evidence, reinforcing the procedure's role in managing refractory coccydynia.

This report has limitations inherent to its design as a single case study. The promising results at the 3‐month follow‐up are encouraging but do not guarantee long‐term efficacy. Furthermore, the outcomes from a single patient cannot be generalized to all populations. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts and longer follow‐up periods are necessary to establish the definitive role and long‐term outcomes of 60% alcohol neurolysis for coccygodynia.
