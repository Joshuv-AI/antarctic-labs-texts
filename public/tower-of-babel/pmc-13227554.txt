HIV/AIDS-Related Awareness and Misconceptions Among Grade 10 Students in Muttrah Wilayat, Muscat, Oman: A descriptive, cross-sectional study
Authors: Rahma Abdullah Al Riyami, Balqees Ahmed Al-Maawali, Nabila Hamood Al Wahaibi, Mariam Rashid Al Rashdi, Fatma Ahmed Al Hinai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:Despite Oman having a low HIV prevalence, stigma and lack of knowledge among young people may hinder prevention efforts. This study aimed to assess HIV/AIDS-related knowledge and misconceptions among Grade 10 students in Muttrah wilayat in Oman.Methods:This cross-sectional study was conducted in February 2025 and included Grade 10 students from 4 governmental schools in Muttrah selected via proportional stratified sampling. The sample size was calculated depending on the number of students in Grade 10. Data collection was performed during face-to-face interviews using a validated Arabic-language questionnaire.Results:A total of 271 Grade 10 students were included in this study. Only 11.8% of students showed good knowledge of HIV/AIDS (≥70% correct responses), while 48.7% showed poor knowledge (<50%). Male students were more likely to show poor knowledge than female students (59.7% versus 24.7%; P <0.001). Similarly, female students held low levels of misconceptions, defined as answering <50% of misconception items incorrectly compared to male students (82.4% versus 37.6%; P <0.001). Age was not associated with knowledge (P = 0.198); however, 14–15-year-olds held fewer misconceptions compared to 16–17-year-olds (P = 0.016). Common misconceptions included the possibility of transmission through mosquito bites or casual contact and existence of a vaccine or cure.Conclusion:The students showed inconsistencies in knowledge and persistent misconceptions. This suggests that structured HIV/AIDS education interventions in Omani schools might be beneficial, especially to target gender differences and myths. Further research is suggested to explore other elements linked to behavioural change, such as perceived susceptibility to contracting infection, attitudes and intentions to adopt preventive behaviours.Keywords: Health Knowledge, Attitudes, Practice, Students, Adolescent, Awareness, HIV, Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome, Health Education, Oman

1. Introduction

Worldwide, HIV/AIDS still represents a public health challenge. In 2021, there were an estimated 38.6 million people living with HIV/AIDS globally and approximately 1.5 million new cases reported.1,2 According to international research, adolescents often have inadequate knowledge about HIV/AIDS, with disparities linked to age, gender and educational level.3–6 Studies from India, Rwanda and Ghana have highlighted persistent misconceptions, including beliefs about transmission through casual contact or the existence of a cure.7–11 This indicates that knowledge gaps are common among school-aged adolescents across diverse sociocultural contexts.

Researchers from the Middle East have published similar findings. University and secondary school students in the UAE and other Arabian countries show moderate knowledge levels (61–70%), alongside varying attitudes including a sense of reluctance to interact with HIV-infected individuals.12,13 This suggests that although basic HIV/AIDS awareness may be relatively high in certain populations, stigma and misinformation remain widespread in the region.

In Oman, the prevalence of HIV in adults has remained below 0.1%; however, newly reported cases have gradually increased, with over 140 new diagnoses in the past 4 years.14–16 An estimated 2,800 individuals were living with HIV in 2023, 73% of whom were receiving antiretroviral therapy.17 Despite this relatively low burden, HIV-related knowledge among Omani youth appears limited, with a notable gap between knowledge and attitudes.18,19 This suggests that knowledge alone may not translate into acceptance or empathic behaviour.

The relationship between HIV awareness, attitudes and behaviour can be interpreted through various theoretical lenses. According to the Health Belief Model (HBM), individuals are more likely to adopt preventive behaviours when they perceive themselves to be susceptible to a health threat, recognise the severity of the condition and consider that the protective steps to be taken are both feasible and effective in reducing their risk.20,21 In the context of HIV, accurate knowledge of transmission routes and prevention measures is therefore essential for shaping positive attitudes and reducing discriminatory behaviours.22,23

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no published research has examined HIV/AIDS awareness among high-school students in Oman. This study aimed to evaluate cognitive knowledge of HIV/AIDS among Grade 10 students in Muttrah wilayat, Oman, and to identify common misconceptions that might hinder effective prevention. This research was undertaken as part of a health-protection campaign in schools. Teachers and school nurses sought to assess students’ current level of knowledge in order to identify gaps and guide future educational interventions.

2. Methods

This descriptive, school-based cross-sectional study was conducted in February 2025and included high-school students attending four schools in Muttrah wilayat, Muscat Governorate, Oman.. All four schools in Muttrah wilayat were included, therefore a census approach was applied at the school level. Within the included schools, eligible students were selected using random sampling.

The sample size was calculated based on the number of students in Grade 10; a statistical formula to calculate the optimal sample size was used. Epidata, Version 4.7.0 (EpiData Association, Denmark, Europe) was used and the sample size was calculated with a 5% margin of error, resulting in a total of 246 participants. A 10% margin was added, bringing the total to 270. The sample was stratified into 4 groups (representing 4 schools) based on the percentage of Grade 10 students in each school relative to the total number of Grade 10 students in Muttrah wilayat. The teachers selected the required number of students from among those with varying academic achievement, as requested by the researchers.

The inclusion criteria consisted of all Arabic-speaking students of both genders, aged 14–17 years who were enrolled in Grade 10 at 1 of the 4 selected schools and present on the day of data collection. Although all participants were in Grade 10, their ages ranged from 14 to 17 years, reflecting the differences in school entry and progression. Participants were grouped into 14–15 and 16–17 years to enable comparison between relatively younger and older adolescents within the same grade, while maintaining adequate numbers in each group for analysis. Students absent during the data collection period, those enrolled in other grades, those outside the eligible age range or those who declined to participate were excluded. Questionnaires with incomplete or missing key responses were excluded from the final analysis.

Structured face-to-face interviews took place during the students’ regular classes. Trained members of the research team provided uniform instructions to all students to ensure privacy and minimise interviewer bias. A 24-item, structured self-administered, Arabic-language questionnaire was used to collect information relating to the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, HIV/AIDS knowledge and misconceptions. The questionnaire was adapted from a previously published and validated tool.7 Expert review was conducted to ensure content validity. Internal consistency was acceptable (as indicated by a KR-20 reliability value of 0.676).

In the first section of the questionnaire, there were 20 questions covering a variety of aspects related to HIV/AIDS knowledge, including transmission, prevention and common misconceptions. Some examples included the possibility of transmitting the infection by casual contact or mosquito bite. It should be noted that, in this study, HIV awareness was operationalised primarily in terms of cognitive knowledge and misconceptions. Broader elements of behavioural change considered in the HBM, such as perceived susceptibility, attitudinal openness or behavioural intentions (e.g., condom use or HIV testing), were deemed beyond the scope of the questionnaire. Stigma was defined as negative attitudes or behaviours towards people with HIV, including fear, blame, avoidance, or moral judgment. Attitudes and stigma were indirectly assessed through items such as willingness to interact with an HIV-positive person. Basic demographic data, including age and gender, were also collected.

Students completed the questionnaire under the supervision of trained focal point teachers and school nurses, all of whom had attended a prior HIV awareness workshop. This study was pilot tested with 7 students of similar age to ensure that all questions were clear. Minor phrasing adjustments were made based on feedback. The average completion time was approximately 3 minutes.

Knowledge scores were calculated by assigning 1 point for each correct response and 0 for incorrect or ‘don't know’ answers, yielding a maximum score of 20 points (100% of questions answered correctly). Based on these scores, knowledge levels were classified as good (≥70% correct responses), moderate (50–69% correct responses) or poor (<50% correct responses), in line with similar research assessing HIV-related knowledge, attitudes and practices.24,25 A set of key misconception items was identified and each student's misconception score was calculated based on the number of these items answered incorrectly. Misconceptions levels were similarly categorised as low (<50% of items answered incorrectly), moderate (50–69% of items answered incorrectly), or high (≥70% of items answered incorrectly).

Data were analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), Version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). Frequencies and percentages were used to summarise categorical variables such as knowledge and misconception levels. Bivariate analyses were conducted to assess associations between demographic characteristics and knowledge or misconception levels. Chi-squared tests were used for categorical comparisons, with the significance level set at P <0.05. Prior to conducting t-tests, the normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, along with visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Knowledge levels were found to be approximately normally distributed (P >0.05), supporting the use of parametric tests. Where appropriate, independent-sample t-tests were performed to compare mean knowledge scores between groups. All statistical tests were two-tailed.

3. Results

A total of 668 Grade 10 students were eligible across the 4 public schools in Muttrah. Using proportional stratified sampling, 271 students were approached and all agreed to participate, completed the questionnaire, and were included in the final analysis (response rate: 100%). None of the students declined participation or were excluded due to incomplete data.

Of the included students, 186 were male (68.6%) students recruited from 2 boys’ schools and 85 (31.4%) were female students recruited from 2 girls’ schools. The mean age was 15.5 ± 0.92 years (range: 15–17 years). Most students were 15 years old (n = 163, 60.1%), followed by those aged 16 years old (n = 58, 21.4%) [Table 1].

Overall knowledge of HIV/AIDS knowledge was low. Mean and median knowledge scores were 9.46 ± 3.39 and 10 (interquartile range [IQR]: 7–12). Only 32 students (11.8%) demonstrated good knowledge (defined as ≥70% questions answered correctly), while 107 (39.5%) had moderate knowledge (50–69% questions answered correctly) and 132 (48.7%) scored poorly (<50% questions answered correctly). Misconceptions were also common, with mean and median scores of 51.12 ± 20.22 and 45.45 (IQR: 36.36–63.64), indicating that students, on average, endorsed half of the myths presented. A total of 140 students (51.7%) exhibited low levels of misconceptions, while 77 (28.4%) and 54 (19.9%) showed moderate and high levels of misconceptions, respectively.

Significant gender differences were observed in terms of knowledge levels. A greater frequency of female students (21.2%) achieved good knowledge scores when compared the male students (7.5%). In turn, poor scores were noted in 59.7% of male students compared with 24.7% of female students; this was significant (P <0.001) [Table 2]. The female students’ mean knowledge scores were also higher (55.53 ± 15.96 versus 43.58 ± 16.06; P <0.001). When it came to misconception levels, similar findings were seen, suggesting that male students were more likely to believe in incorrect HIV/AIDS-related myths. Only 37.6% of male students had low misconception levels compared to 82.4% of females (P <0.001) [Table 3]. There was no significant relationship between age and knowledge levels. For the younger age group (14–15 years), 13.2% had good knowledge while 45.3% had poor knowledge. This was slightly higher than the slightly older age group (16–17 years) where 8.6% had good knowledge while 56.8% had poor knowledge. This, however, was not significant (P = 0.198) [Table 2]. The mean knowledge score was also comparable at 49.08 ± 16.39 versus 43.21 ± 17.58 (P = 0.009). On the other hand, there was a significant association with misconception levels. Mean misconception scores in these age groups were 49 ± 19.67 and 56.12 ± 20.72 (P = 0.008). More of the young students (57.4%) had low levels of misconceptions than the slightly older age group (44.4%; P = 0.015) [Table 3].

A total of 10.7% of students were aware that HIV and AIDS are not the same thing. Similarly, only 36.5% knew that there is currently no vaccine against HIV and 57.9% knew that it is not curable. Myths about the modes of transmission were also common. For example, whether HIV can be spread through mosquito bites (41.3%), urine (27.7%), sharing food (36.5%) or drink (88.6%), or swimming (57.9%). In terms of important legal and social misconceptions, 49.1% provided incorrect answers, did not know or were unsure whether people living with HIV were prohibited from marrying in Oman [Table 4].

4. Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive examination of the levels of knowledge of HIV/AIDS prevalent in a sample of adolescents attending school in Muttrah wilayat, Oman. The results highlight both positive aspects along with areas that need further action. Almost all respondents had heard of HIV/AIDS and knew of at least a few key details. However, only 11.8% showed a good level of understanding of the subject (which was defined as ≥70% correct responses). Under half of the respondents failed answers even half of the questions on the survey correctly; therefore, this reflects that this group may not know basic information about HIV/AIDS. Some students also seemed to endorse incorrect convictions relating to HIV, especially regarding how the disease spreads to others or availability of a vaccine or cure. The authors of this article speculate that these should be viewed as signs of possible gaps in their education, in which more focused interventions addressing these myths might be beneficial.

Other studies conducted in the region have reported consistent trends. For example, according to a recent review, school-aged populations in the Arabian Peninsula have moderate HIV knowledge (60–70%).13 The results of the current study are in line with these regional figures which indicate that adolescents or teenagers usually have lower levels of knowledge than adults or those with university education. Furthermore, it is important to note that, Oman's national youth HIV knowledge barometer was recorded at less than 10% according to UNAIDS in 2023.17 This potentially indicates that there have been very few improvements in this indicator in adolescents, thereby suggesting an educational gap.

Gender disparities in terms of both knowledge and misconceptions were also seen in the current study. Compared to boys, girls had considerably significantly fewer misconceptions and were more likely to have good knowledge. This is encouraging, as it supports the notion that young school-going females in Oman are receiving and retaining accurate information related to this topic. This is not the case within some other research contexts, such as in India, where girls were less informed owing to cultural barriers against sexual health.8 The authors speculate that girls may be more involved or pay better attention in health classes or they might learn more from classes that are segregated by gender, although this hasn't been empirically established in this study. In contrast, boys could be less engaged or involved due to embarrassment, peer dynamics or discomfort when they discuss sensitive issues. Oman-specific cultural factors, such as family discussion dynamics, openness or differences in media consumption, might also serve to explain this gap.

These trends are largely in line with the findings reported in a meta-analysis of 17 countries, including Oman, conducted in the Middle East and North Africa region.26 Generally, the results showed that adolescent girls tend to participate in more health-promoting behaviour, such as hygiene practices, emotional regulation and cooperative behaviours at school, while boys are more likely to engage in risky behaviour such as physical aggression, tobacco and injuries.26 Therefore, it might be possible that the gendered socialisation and cultural norms in this region of the world may also explain the different levels of health knowledge and beliefs seen in the current study.

These explanations, however, should be regarded as speculative, since the current study did not actually assess the underlying causes of the gender gap that was observed. Further qualitative and mixed-methods research in Oman and similar societies should be conducted to better understand how gender shapes adolescents’ access to and engagement with health education efforts. Regardless, the differences shown in the current study support the potential benefit of implementing gender-tailored education to improve knowledge and reduce misconceptions, particularly in male students who demonstrated lower knowledge and held more misconceptions in the current study. Therefore, school-based programmes should be enhanced by targeting males. This could involve, among other strategies, targeted correction, peer engagement activities and interactive workshops. This could help to make HIV prevention programmes more relevant and effective.

In the current study, age was not significantly related to knowledge levels. The common assumption that older adolescents would be more informed was not proven in this study. In fact, most students aged 16–17 involved in the current study did slightly worse than their younger counterparts, although the difference was not statistically relevant. This is not especially surprising since all the participants were in the same grade and typically following the same curriculum. Instead, it is consistent with the notion that knowledge may not automatically improve with age in the absence of reinforcement. However, younger students were significantly less likely to hold misconceptions than their older counterparts, which may reflect more recent exposure to informal educational content, greater receptiveness to learning or educational continuity, while some older students may have repeated a grade.

In mainstream Omani schools, HIV/AIDS topics are not formally included in the curriculum, so students must rely on informal sources such as social media, peers or occasional school health activities for HIV information. In contrast, studies from other contexts have typically shown a positive association between age and knowledge. However, it is important to note that these studies often include wider age ranges than the current study (e.g., 10–19 or 15–19 years), which may contribute to the larger age-related differences observed. For example, Rwandan and Indian studies reported higher knowledge scores among older adolescents.7,8 The lack of such a trend in this sample suggests the potential benefit of implementing age-appropriate, evidence-based HIV education throughout lower secondary schooling to support consistent understanding and reduce misconceptions across all students.

A particularly concerning finding of this study was the persistence of myths and stigma-related beliefs. A significant proportion of students believed that HIV can be transmitted through mosquito bites, shared food or swimming. The prevalence of such superstitions among Grade 10 students in Oman, suggests that the health education currently provided is either not sufficient or is not adequately tailored to correct misinformation derived from other student peers or mass media. Such beliefs might contribute to social stigma that could lead to people with HIV being avoided or discriminated against. Similar trends have been recorded in the region. A study in the UAE found that 85% of university pupils held at least one misconception or had a negative attitude towards people living with HIV.12 This comparison is not meant to imply a direct parallel between a school-going and university population but rather furnish broader context regarding knowledge and misconceptions across educational stages.

HIV myths are likely introduced by obtaining information from various sources with differing levels of accuracy. Apart from the formal education provided in schools, students might also obtain information that could be either harmful or true regarding HIV through digital, social media, platforms and peer networks. Sources of information, however, were not directly evaluated in the current study. Furthermore, as with other Arab countries, the conservative societal norms in Oman as well as the religious and cultural taboos on open discussion of sexuality in public or family settings have left adolescents with fewer opportunities to acquire accurate knowledge related to HIV.27 Previous research has indicated that formal provision of HIV/AIDS education in the Gulf is in short supply, with common sources of information being derived from TV, films, the internet and friends.27,28 Getting a better understanding of how these factors influence HIV knowledge and misconceptions will enable researchers and health educators to target interventions more appropriately in the future.

Among the misconceptions that were most concerning, was that almost half of the respondents believed that HIV-positive individuals are not allowed to get married in Oman. Despite the fact that the legal reality in Oman does not reflect this belief, it may imply the very real social stigma surrounding HIV and also the perception that people regard HIV as a condition that limits normal life. In the context of a conservative society such as Oman, this could stem from the concern over obligatory premarital testing and fears of social exclusion. An earlier study among Omani university students found that 65.4% expressed reluctance to care for someone living with HIV despite being aware of the facts.19 Globally, studies demonstrate that there is persistent stigma toward individuals living with HIV among adolescents, even in some Western countries, which emphasises the potential value for educational programmes based not only on biomedical facts but also on the importance of empathy and compassion.29

The current study examined some stigma-related beliefs, but this only approximates stigma because responses were assessed indirectly via questions such as willingness to interact with people living with HIV. The utilisation of validated and standardised scales such as Berger's HIV Stigma Scale is suggested to carry out a more thorough evaluation of HIV-related stigma among Omani youth.30 Future studies should include these measures in order to provide a more complete view of stigma that is multidimensional in nature, which includes fear, blame, avoidance and moral judgment. Moreover, future studies could employ either longitudinal or pre-post interventional designs to check whether educational interventional efforts improve knowledge and lower stigma.

Therefore, the current study emphasises the need for the introduction of comprehensive, targeted interventions that will bolster HIV/AIDS education efforts in schools in Oman. The educational content should specify the respective routes of transmission involved, not just what does not spread HIV to debunk myths, but also up-to-date information on treatment so that the students will not assume that HIV is always fatal or, on the contrary, curable. The gender gap identified by this study shows that it may be pertinent to implement interventions directed specifically towards the boys, who might be guided by male educators and be actively encouraged to participate. The introduction of interactive activities such as workshops by healthcare professionals could also help correct misconceptions in a non-judgmental environment. The overall performance of females can serve as the building block for other initiatives. For instance, the girls’ schools can be used as pilot sites for teaching the improved curriculum, which later can be extended to the boys’ schools. Peer education can also be introduced to normalise accurate knowledge, correct any misinformation and challenge stigma in the students’ community.

The situation in Muttrah wilayat serves as a reflection of broader global patterns. On the whole, adolescent HIV literacy remains patchy and inconsistent, despite global efforts towards increasing awareness. Even in countries with a high HIV burden, only 30–50% of youth have comprehensive knowledge.9,10 In the current study, 11.8% of students showed good knowledge of HIV/AIDS which is lower in absolute terms, but the threshold for determining comprehensive knowledge is stricter and the setting is low-prevalence. In high-prevalence countries, the urgency of the HIV epidemic compels far more vigorous educational campaigns, which Oman could utilise for direction; high-income countries face similar dilemmas with reported knowledge gaps, such as those related to newer prevention strategies such as pre-exposure prophylaxis.31,32 This supports the argument that an ongoing update of HIV education to fit scientific developments and evolving social situations may be beneficial.

Most importantly, this is the first study of its kind which investigates HIV/AIDS knowledge at the high-school level in Oman. Previous research, which was conducted nearly 2 decades ago among university students, indicated similar problems of misinformation and stigma.19 While both studies exhibit common concerns over misinformation and stigma, the current study's findings suggest that these problems are present in adolescence or even before. As such, high school may represent an important site for intervention. This study contributes valuable information to support introducing structured HIV/AIDS education in high schools in Oman, rather than waiting until students advance to higher education settings. It is necessary to highlight that this study was intended only for research and educational purposes to evaluate the students’ awareness and knowledge about HIV/AIDS. The study was not used for any clinical diagnosis, nor for the screening of individual students or for healthcare decision-making.

In terms of recommendations based on the results of this study, educational authorities should prioritise integrating structured HIV education modules into secondary school curricula, with a strong focus on interactive learning, targeted outreach to male students and reinforcement of facts through media and community support to amplify the impact. Myth-busting should be a priority to address common misconceptions. Demonstrations, analogies or testimonials can be used to refute myths and present accurate information in a compelling and impactful way. The Ministry of Health and Ministry of Education should work jointly to design culturally appropriate and scientifically accurate education programmes, involving healthcare professionals in delivering some of the content. School teachers should receive training on the latest HIV information so they can confidently teach and answer questions. Schools should involve parents through informational letters or workshops, so that correct information is reinforced at home and parents can support school discussions. Finally, social media, school websites and online content can be utilised to reach adolescents outside the classroom, perhaps through short videos, quizzes or even infographics.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causal relationships between variables. Second, the study was conducted among Grade 10 students in government schools within a single wilayat, which may limit the generalisability of the results to students of different age groups, private schools or other regions. Third, data were collected using a face-to-face self-administered questionnaire, which may be subject to reporting bias or social desirability bias. Fourth, the study did not include an a priori sample size or effect size estimation because a census approach was used. Although this allowed coverage of the full accessible population, future studies with larger and statistically powered samples may provide more generalisable estimates. Finally, the instrument used did not comprehensively assess attitudes. In particular, HIV awareness was measured mainly as factual knowledge and misconceptions, without including broader behavioural or perceptual elements such as perceived susceptibility, attitudinal openness or behavioural intentions. Since the HBM highlights the importance of these perceptions in shaping health behaviours, future studies should consider incorporating these elements.20,21 A qualitative approach may also be needed to explore the roots of HIV-related stigma among Omani youth.25

Despite these limitations, the findings provide valuable insights into the level of HIV/AIDS awareness and misconceptions among adolescents. The results can serve as a resource for the age-appropriate development of school-based health education programmes and to provide guidance for decision-makers and teachers on incorporating HIV/AIDS awareness into Omani high school curricula.

5. Conclusion

This study shows a disturbing lack of knowledge and the enduring existence of misconceptions about HIV/AIDS among Grade 10 students in Muttrah, especially apparent among adolescent boys. These findings support the potential value of integrating comprehensive, age-relevant and gender-sensitive HIV education in schools in Oman.
