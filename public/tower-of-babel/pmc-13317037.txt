Global prevalence of internet gaming disorder in children and adolescents: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Joshuan J Barboza, César Bonilla Asalde, Oriana Rivera-Lozada, Maria Mihaela Iuga, Betty R Valdivia, Gabriel Hernández-Juárez, Carlos A Canelo, Roberto A Molina Campuzano, Jonathan C Aguilar-Bueno, Rubén Aguirre-Ipenza, Luis Angel Kong-Lozano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInternet Gaming Disorder (IGD) has emerged as a significant behavioral concern among youth, yet global prevalence estimates remain inconsistent due to methodological variability. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine the global pooled prevalence of IGD in children and adolescents.MethodsFollowing PRISMA 2020 guidelines and prospective registration in PROSPERO (CRD420251156483) prior to data extraction and analysis, comprehensive searches were conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and PsycINFO up to September 2025. Eligible observational studies assessed IGD prevalence in individuals aged 6–18 years using standardized diagnostic instruments based on DSM-5 or ICD-11 criteria. A random-effects meta-analysis (DerSimonian and Laird method) with Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation was used to pool prevalence estimates. Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic, and publication bias was assessed using funnel plot asymmetry and Egger’s regression test.ResultsNineteen studies involving 251,037 participants and 26,868 IGD cases met the inclusion criteria. The pooled global prevalence of IGD among children and adolescents was 10% (95% CI: 6–15%). Individual study estimates ranged from approximately 1% in some European populations to over 25% in studies conducted in Middle Eastern and Latin American settings. Heterogeneity was extreme (I² = 99.7%), and smaller studies tended to report higher prevalence.ConclusionsApproximately one in ten children and adolescents worldwide meet criteria for IGD, underscoring its growing significance as a global mental health concern. Given the substantial heterogeneity observed, the pooled estimate should be interpreted as an average across diverse contexts rather than a uniform global rate. These findings highlight the need for standardized diagnostic criteria, culturally sensitive preventive interventions, and early identification strategies to mitigate long-term psychosocial impact.Keywords: Internet gaming disorder, Prevalence, Children, Adolescents, Epidemiology, Systematic review

Introduction

Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) has garnered significant attention in recent years as a pertinent public health issue, particularly impacting children and adolescents, who are among the most frequent users of interactive digital technology. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) characterizes Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) as a recurrent and enduring gaming behavior that results in considerable impairment in academic, social, and personal functioning, designating it as a condition necessitating further investigation [1]. Subsequently, the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) officially recognized “gaming disorder” as a behavioral addiction unrelated to substance use, thus affirming its status as a mental and behavioral ailment [2].

Global investigations have revealed substantial variation in the reported prevalence of IGD across regions. In East and Southeast Asia, pooled estimates have frequently ranged between 8% and 15%, with some country-specific studies reporting rates exceeding 20% among male adolescents [3]. In contrast, European studies have generally documented lower prevalence, often between 1% and 5%, particularly in nationally representative samples from Germany and the Czech Republic. Studies from the Middle East have reported markedly higher estimates, frequently surpassing 15%, while evidence from Latin America remains limited but suggests similarly elevated rates in certain adolescent populations [4]. These regional discrepancies may reflect differences in cultural norms surrounding gaming, parental supervision practices, technological accessibility, and the diagnostic instruments employed. However, the magnitude and consistency of these regional differences remain uncertain due to methodological heterogeneity across studies.

Previous reviews have emphasized that methodological variability—particularly regarding the diagnostic criteria and instruments used—has a major impact on prevalence estimates. Mihara and Higuchi highlighted that the use of non-standardized tools directly contributes to discrepancies across studies [6]. In contrast, Fam conducted a meta-analysis focused on adolescents and reported a pooled global prevalence of 4.6% [7], while Stevens et al. found a prevalence of 8.6% after analyzing data from more than 640,000 participants across multiple regions [8]. These results illustrate the lack of epidemiological consensus and the need for updated estimates that incorporate recent evidence.

From a theoretical perspective, several models have been proposed to explain the mechanisms underlying IGD. Davis’s cognitive-behavioral model suggests that maladaptive cognitions, such as distorted beliefs about achievement or social validation through gaming, interact with reinforcement processes to perpetuate problematic use [9]. Neurobiological frameworks, including Volkow’s reward circuit model, emphasize dopaminergic sensitization and altered executive control mechanisms that may increase vulnerability to compulsive gaming behaviors [10]. These models collectively imply that IGD prevalence may vary according to developmental stage, sociocultural reinforcement patterns, and exposure intensity to gaming environments. Consequently, a global epidemiological synthesis is necessary not only to estimate prevalence but also to examine how contextual and structural factors may shape the manifestation of IGD across regions. By quantifying regional variability, this meta-analysis indirectly contributes to evaluating the ecological validity of these theoretical models at the population level.

Despite advances in this field, significant gaps remain. Previous meta-analyses have often combined adult and adolescent populations, relied on heterogeneous diagnostic thresholds, or were conducted prior to the widespread digital intensification observed during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Furthermore, inconsistencies in the operationalization of DSM-5 and ICD-11 criteria, as well as variability in sampling strategies, have limited cross-cultural comparability. The present systematic review and meta-analysis addresses these limitations by (1) focusing exclusively on children and adolescents aged 6–18 years (2), restricting inclusion to studies using standardized instruments aligned with DSM-5 or ICD-11 criteria (3), incorporating recent evidence up to 2025, and (4) explicitly exploring sources of heterogeneity across geographical regions and diagnostic tools. By applying a rigorous methodological framework and updated statistical synthesis, this study aims to provide a more precise and context-sensitive global estimate of IGD prevalence in youth populations.

The objective of this study was to determine the global prevalence and associated characteristics of Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) in children and adolescents, incorporating evidence from observational studies across diverse geographical and cultural settings.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (registration number CRD420251156483) prior to data extraction and statistical analysis.

Searches

The selection of databases was guided by the need to ensure comprehensive coverage of medical, psychological, and multidisciplinary literature. PubMed and Embase were selected to capture biomedical and clinical research; Scopus and Web of Science were included for their broad indexing of multidisciplinary and international journals; and PsycINFO was incorporated to enhance sensitivity in retrieving psychiatric and behavioral science studies specifically addressing addictive behaviors and adolescent mental health.

The core search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH and Emtree terms) and free-text keywords related to “Internet Gaming Disorder,” “Gaming Disorder,” “video game addiction,” “children,” and “adolescents,” using Boolean operators (AND/OR) and truncation strategies to maximize sensitivity. A representative PubMed search string was as follows: (“Internet Gaming Disorder” OR “Gaming Disorder” OR “video game addiction”) AND (“children” OR “adolescents” OR “youth”) AND (“prevalence” OR “epidemiology”).

In addition to database searches, the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible studies. The complete search strategies for all databases are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

All retrieved records were consolidated into a single reference management file for organization and deduplication. Duplicate records were identified and removed prior to screening. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment to determine eligibility according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) observational design (cross-sectional or longitudinal); (2) assessment of IGD prevalence in individuals aged 6 to 18 years; (3) use of standardized diagnostic or screening instruments aligned with DSM-5 or ICD-11 criteria; and (4) sufficient data to extract or calculate prevalence estimates and sample size.

Clinical populations (e.g., treatment-seeking individuals, psychiatric inpatients, or samples recruited from addiction clinics) were excluded to avoid overestimation of prevalence and to ensure that the pooled estimate reflected general population samples. Studies that included both adults and adolescents without disaggregated data were also excluded.

The age range of 6–18 years was selected to capture the developmental period spanning middle childhood to late adolescence. Studies focusing exclusively on participants older than 18 years or younger than 6 years were excluded to maintain developmental consistency and due to lack of validated instruments in early childhood.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was the point prevalence of IGD in the general population of children and adolescents. Prevalence was defined as the proportion of participants meeting diagnostic criteria for IGD at the time of assessment, based on the respective cut-off scores of the instruments used.

Data extraction

Following deduplication, all records were screened in two stages (title/abstract and full-text review) by two independent reviewers using Rayyan. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (JJB).

Data extraction was performed using a pre-designed Excel form, capturing variables including author name, year of publication, country, study design, population characteristics (mean age, age range, sex distribution), sample size, diagnostic instrument, cut-off used, reported prevalence, and reported correlates of IGD. When studies reported multiple timepoints or subgroups, only baseline data or the earliest complete dataset were included to avoid duplication.

A pilot calibration exercise was conducted on a random subset of 50 records to ensure consistency in eligibility assessment. Inter-reviewer agreement was evaluated, and discrepancies were resolved through consensus. Regular meetings were held to maintain methodological consistency throughout the review process.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). This tool evaluates three core domains: selection of participants (maximum of 4 stars), comparability of study groups (maximum of 2 stars), and outcome assessment (maximum of 3 stars), with a total possible score of up to 9 stars.

Although the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale was originally developed for cohort and case-control studies, it was applied in this review due to its structured framework for evaluating key methodological aspects of observational research, including representativeness of the sample, comparability across groups, and validity of outcome measurement.

Two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias for all included studies according to predefined criteria. Discrepancies in scoring were resolved through discussion, and when consensus was not achieved, a third reviewer adjudicated the final decision.

Each study was assigned a total NOS score and categorized as low risk of bias (7–9 stars), moderate risk (4–6 stars), or high risk (0–3 stars). The assessment focused on key methodological elements, including sampling representativeness, adequacy of the sampling strategy, reliability of the diagnostic instruments used to assess Internet Gaming Disorder, and completeness of outcome reporting.

Risk of bias assessments were not used as exclusion criteria; instead, they informed the interpretation of the pooled prevalence estimates and were considered in sensitivity analyses.

Data synthesis

Prevalence estimates were pooled using a random-effects meta-analysis (DerSimonian and Laird method), accounting for between-study variability. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), employing the “meta” package.

The metaprop function was used to pool prevalence estimates, applying the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation to stabilize variance, particularly in studies reporting very low or very high prevalence values. Pooled estimates obtained under the transformed scale were back-transformed to the original proportion scale using the inverse Freeman–Tukey method, as implemented within the meta-analysis framework.

Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic. Cochran’s Q test was also calculated to assess the presence of heterogeneity beyond chance. Given the expected variability across regions and diagnostic approaches, subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Results

Selection of studies

A total of 5,568 records were found via database searches. After eliminating 2,266 duplicates, 3,302 titles and abstracts were evaluated in Rayyan. Of these, 3,251 records failed to satisfy the eligibility requirements and were consequently excluded. The remaining 51 full-text articles were assessed in detail, leading to the exclusion of 32 studies (3 conference abstracts, 12 with non-eligible designs, 2 with ineligible age groups, 9 with unrelated outcomes, and 6 duplicates). As a result, 19 studies fulfilled the inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review [11–30] (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the included studies

A total of 19 studies published between 2015 and 2025 were included in this systematic review, encompassing a broad geographical representation that primarily concentrated in Asia—especially China, South Korea, and Saudi Arabia—while additional data came from Europe (Germany, Czech Republic, France, Slovenia), South America (Brazil), and the Middle East (Iran, Tunisia) (Table 1). The majority of studies (88%) were cross-sectional, whereas a smaller proportion adopted longitudinal or cohort designs, such as the two-year follow-up studies [17, 20]. Sampling procedures varied widely, ranging from nationally representative stratified cluster designs [22, 23] to convenience or school-based cluster sampling, which was the most common strategy overall. Nearly all studies were conducted in academic settings, and data were predominantly collected through anonymous, self-administered paper questionnaires during classroom sessions, occasionally supplemented by online surveys to enhance accessibility [12, 24].

Characteristics of included studies

Regarding participant demographics (Table 2), the combined population comprised children and adolescents aged 6 to 18 years, with mean ages across studies ranging between 10.2 and 16.9 years. Most samples were balanced in sex distribution, though several studies reported a predominance of male participants [17, 26], reflecting the higher gaming engagement typically observed in males. School level was consistently primary or secondary education, and almost all data were obtained from school-based populations, confirming the literature’s adolescent focus. Inclusion criteria generally required active school enrollment and parental consent, while exclusion criteria often addressed incomplete questionnaires, lack of consent, or absence during data collection. Only a few studies explicitly excluded participants with pre-existing psychiatric or neurological conditions [14, 29].

The diagnostic assessment of Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) showed notable heterogeneity across tools, frameworks, and reference periods (Table 3). Across studies, the Internet Gaming Disorder Scale–Short Form (IGDS9-SF) emerged as the most frequently used instrument, appearing in approximately one-third of included studies [16, 23, 26]. Other validated measures included the IGD-20 Test, Game Addiction Scale (GAS), Gaming Disorder Test (GDT), and Video Game Dependency Scale (CSAS). Diagnostic alignment was predominantly with DSM-5 criteria (84% of studies), whereas a smaller subset utilized ICD-11 definitions [18, 29]. The most frequently applied recall period was 12 months, consistent with the DSM-5 diagnostic window. Sample sizes ranged widely, from 391 participants [12] to over 200,000 [18], reflecting considerable variability in study scope and statistical power.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the 19 observational studies included in this meta-analysis was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), which evaluates three domains: selection (maximum 4 stars), comparability (maximum 2 stars), and outcome/exposure (maximum 3 stars). Two reviewers independently conducted the evaluations, resolving discrepancies through discussion and consensus. Studies were classified as low risk (7–9 stars), moderate risk (4–6 stars), or high risk (0–3 stars).

Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies was acceptable, though with some heterogeneity. Specifically, 6 studies (31.6%) were rated as low risk of bias, 9 studies (47.4%) as moderate risk, and 4 studies (21.0%) as high risk. Most studies achieved high scores in the selection and outcome/exposure domains, indicating well-defined samples, appropriate exposure ascertainment, and the use of validated assessment instruments. However, greater variability was observed in the comparability domain, as several studies did not adequately control for potential confounders such as socioeconomic status, psychiatric comorbidities, or baseline psychological traits.

In summary, although a subset of studies demonstrated robust methodological rigor, the predominance of moderate-risk ratings suggests that the pooled estimates of this meta-analysis should be interpreted with caution, given potential confounding influences. A sensitivity analysis was conducted excluding studies classified as high risk of bias (NOS score ≤ 3). The pooled prevalence estimate remained within a similar range, indicating that high-risk studies did not disproportionately inflate the overall estimate. Prevalence of internet gaming disorder

A total of 19 studies, comprising 251,037 participants and 26,868 identified cases of Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD), were included in the quantitative synthesis, with study characteristics detailed in Tables 1, 2 and 3. The pooled prevalence of IGD among children and adolescents was 10% (95% CI: 6%–15%) under a random-effects model (Fig. 2). Individual prevalence estimates demonstrated substantial variability, ranging from 1% in European cohorts [22, 29] to over 25% in studies from Latin America and the Middle East [11, 13, 15].

Meta-analysis of prevalence

Regional trends were observed, with lower prevalence rates typically reported in European samples and considerably higher rates documented in Middle Eastern and Latin American populations. Most studies employed DSM-5–based instruments, particularly the IGDS9-SF, while a smaller number utilized ICD-11 criteria, which may have contributed to the heterogeneity of findings.

Between-study heterogeneity was extremely high (I² = 99.7%, τ² = 0.0155, p < 0.001), indicating that nearly all variability stemmed from methodological and contextual differences rather than sampling error. The prediction interval was wide (0%–31%), suggesting that true prevalence estimates in future studies may range from negligible to nearly one-third of the adolescent population, depending on the diagnostic approach and cultural context.

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted according to geographical region, diagnostic framework, and study design.

When stratified by region, pooled prevalence was lower in European samples (approximately 2–4%) compared to Middle Eastern and Latin American studies (often exceeding 15%), while Asian samples demonstrated intermediate but variable estimates. This pattern suggests that sociocultural norms surrounding gaming, parental monitoring practices, and digital infrastructure accessibility may contribute substantially to observed variability.

Stratification by diagnostic framework revealed that studies using DSM-5–based instruments tended to report higher prevalence estimates than those applying ICD-11 criteria, possibly reflecting broader operational definitions and lower impairment thresholds in DSM-5–aligned screening tools.

Furthermore, studies employing large nationally representative samples generally reported lower prevalence compared to single-site or convenience-based school samples, indicating that sampling strategy may partially explain heterogeneity.

Despite these subgroup patterns, substantial residual heterogeneity persisted within strata, suggesting that IGD prevalence is influenced by complex contextual and methodological factors rather than a single explanatory variable.

Publication bias assessment

The funnel plot displays the relationship between study size (represented by the standard error on the y-axis) and prevalence estimates of Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) (x-axis) (Fig. 3). In the absence of publication bias or small-study effects, one would expect the studies to be symmetrically distributed around the pooled estimate, forming an inverted funnel shape.

Funnel plot of studies assessed

In this plot, however, there is marked asymmetry. Several smaller studies (with higher standard errors) are concentrated on the right-hand side, reporting higher prevalence estimates, while fewer small studies appear on the left-hand side, reporting lower prevalence estimates. This imbalance suggests the potential presence of publication bias or small-study effects, in which studies showing higher prevalence may have been more likely to be published or included, while those reporting low prevalence may be underrepresented.

The observed funnel plot asymmetry suggests the presence of small-study effects, whereby smaller studies tended to report higher prevalence estimates. This pattern may reflect selective publication of studies reporting elevated IGD prevalence; however, in prevalence meta-analyses, asymmetry may also arise from genuine heterogeneity, as smaller studies are often conducted in specific high-risk populations or localized settings with distinct gaming behaviors.

Egger’s regression test indicated statistical asymmetry; nevertheless, given the extremely high heterogeneity (I² > 99%), interpretation of publication bias indicators should be approached cautiously, as statistical tests for asymmetry may overestimate bias under substantial between-study variability.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides one of the most comprehensive syntheses of the global prevalence of Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD) among children and adolescents. Based on 19 studies including 251,037 participants, the pooled prevalence was 10% (95% CI: 6%–15%), indicating that approximately one in ten youths meet diagnostic criteria for IGD. This finding highlights IGD as a relevant and growing behavioral health concern across diverse sociocultural contexts [5].

However, the interpretation of this estimate must be contextualized in light of the extreme between-study heterogeneity (I² = 99.7%). Under such conditions, the pooled prevalence should not be interpreted as a universal global rate but rather as a summary measure of central tendency across highly heterogeneous populations and methodological frameworks. The wide prediction interval further reinforces that true prevalence is expected to vary substantially across settings, underscoring the importance of contextual interpretation rather than reliance on a single aggregate estimate.

The observed heterogeneity is likely multifactorial and reflects both methodological and contextual variability. Differences in diagnostic instruments and frameworks, particularly between DSM-5–based and ICD-11–based approaches, may lead to variation in case identification and threshold definitions. DSM-5–aligned instruments often apply broader screening criteria, whereas ICD-11 emphasizes functional impairment and clinical relevance, potentially yielding more conservative prevalence estimates. In addition, heterogeneity in sampling strategies—from nationally representative surveys to convenience-based school samples—may further contribute to variability. Sociocultural and environmental factors, including access to digital technologies, parental monitoring practices, and normative patterns of gaming behavior, also likely play a significant role. Collectively, these findings support the interpretation of IGD as a context-dependent phenomenon rather than a fixed global parameter.

The pooled prevalence identified in this study is higher than that reported in earlier meta-analyses. Fam et al. [7] estimated a global prevalence of 4.6%, while Stevens et al. [5] and Mihara and Higuchi [6] reported 8.6% and 5.5%, respectively. This apparent increase may reflect a combination of temporal, methodological, and geographical factors rather than a simple rise in incidence. Notably, many studies included in the present analysis were conducted after 2020, a period characterized by increased digital engagement during the COVID-19 pandemic. Prolonged screen exposure and reliance on online gaming for social interaction may have contributed to heightened vulnerability among adolescents.

In addition, methodological evolution may partially explain differences with earlier estimates. Recent studies more frequently employ standardized instruments such as the IGDS9-SF, which may capture a broader spectrum of problematic gaming behaviors, including subthreshold cases. Furthermore, earlier meta-analyses were predominantly based on East Asian populations, whereas the present synthesis incorporates data from more diverse regions, including the Middle East and Latin America, where sociocultural dynamics and patterns of digital use may differ substantially.

Regional differences in prevalence warrant further consideration. Higher estimates observed in Middle Eastern and certain Latin American studies may reflect rapid digital expansion combined with limited regulatory frameworks and lower levels of digital literacy or parental supervision. In contrast, lower prevalence rates reported in European countries may be associated with stricter monitoring practices, more established digital health policies, and greater awareness of problematic gaming behaviors. Differences in sampling methodology and diagnostic thresholds may also contribute to these regional patterns.

From a developmental perspective, longitudinal evidence suggests that IGD may persist or worsen over time in a subset of adolescents, supporting the conceptualization of IGD as a potentially chronic and relapsing behavioral condition [17]. This dynamic nature complicates prevalence estimation and highlights the need for longitudinal designs to better understand disease trajectories.

The presence of funnel plot asymmetry suggests potential small-study effects, with smaller studies tending to report higher prevalence estimates. However, in prevalence meta-analyses, such asymmetry may reflect genuine contextual differences rather than publication bias alone. Given the extreme heterogeneity observed, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

The overall prevalence of 10% has important clinical and public health implications. IGD has been consistently associated with depression, anxiety, sleep disturbances, impaired academic performance, and reduced social functioning [26, 28]. Early identification through school-based screening and the use of validated instruments such as the IGDS9-SF may facilitate timely intervention. At the public health level, these findings highlight the need for preventive strategies that promote healthy digital behaviors, enhance digital literacy, and support parental engagement. Integrated interventions combining cognitive-behavioral therapy, motivational approaches, and family-based strategies may offer additional benefits [8, 23]. Multisectoral collaboration between healthcare providers, educators, and policymakers will be essential to mitigate long-term consequences.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the predominance of cross-sectional designs limits causal inference, preventing conclusions about the directionality of associations between IGD and psychosocial outcomes. Second, most studies relied on self-reported measures, which are subject to recall bias, social desirability bias, and potential misclassification [31, 32]. Third, the extreme heterogeneity (I² = 99.7%) limits the interpretability of the pooled estimate, which should be understood as a central tendency rather than a uniform global rate. Fourth, variability in diagnostic instruments and operational definitions may reduce comparability across studies. Fifth, most studies were conducted in school-based populations, potentially underrepresenting out-of-school or vulnerable adolescents, and certain regions remain underrepresented. Finally, although small-study effects were suggested, these findings must be interpreted cautiously in the context of substantial heterogeneity.

Meta-regression was not performed due to the limited number of studies within specific strata and the substantial clinical and methodological variability, which would have limited the robustness and interpretability of such analyses.

Implications for future research

Future research should prioritize longitudinal and cross-cultural designs to better characterize developmental trajectories and contextual determinants of IGD. Harmonization of diagnostic criteria between DSM-5 and ICD-11 frameworks is essential to improve comparability and reduce variability in prevalence estimates.

Conclusions

In summary, this systematic review and meta-analysis indicates that IGD affects approximately one in ten children and adolescents worldwide, although prevalence varies considerably across contexts. The extreme heterogeneity observed reinforces that IGD should be understood as a context-dependent phenomenon rather than a uniform global condition. Advancing methodological standardization and conducting longitudinal, cross-cultural research will be essential to generate more reliable and clinically meaningful estimates.
