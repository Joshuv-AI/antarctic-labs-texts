Generics and Default Reasoning in Large Language Models
James Ravi Kirkpatrick, Rachel Katharine Sterken

Abstract
This paper evaluates the capabilities of 28 large language models (LLMs) to reason with 20 defeasible reasoning patterns involving generic generalizations (e.g., Birds fly , Ravens are black ) central to non-monotonic logic. Generics are of special interest to linguists, philosophers, logicians, and cognitive scientists because of their complex exception-permitting behaviour and their centrality to default reasoning, cognition, and concept acquisition. We find that while several frontier models handle many default reasoning problems well, performance varies widely across models and prompting styles. Few-shot prompting modestly improves performance for some models, but chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting often leads to serious performance degradation (mean accuracy drop -11.14%, SD 15.74% in models performing above 75% accuracy in zero-shot condition, temperature 0). Most models either struggle to distinguish between defeasible and deductive inference or misinterpret generics as universal statements. These findings underscore both the promise and limits of current LLMs for default reasoning. Keywords. artificial intelligence (AI); default reasoning; defeasible reasoning; generic generalizations; large language models; non-monotonic reasoning

Introduction

Default reasoning and generic generalizations are central to human cognition. Default reasoning is a form of non-monotonic reasoning: it allows us to draw plausible, defeasible conclusions on the basis of general rules that may have exceptions in situations involving incomplete or uncertain information. It is non-monotonic in the sense that additional information may force us to withdraw earlier conclusions by showing us that the case is exceptional. Default reasoning often relies on generic generalizations (e.g.,

We find that several frontier models perform impressively high on our set of default reasoning patterns, although results vary considerably across models. we find that language models struggle to distinguish between defeasible and deductive reasoning contexts, as well as between generic and universal generalizations.

Background and Related Work

2.1
Default Reasoning

In the AI literature, default reasoning has usually been studied under the term ‘non-monotonic reasoning’ (
McCarthy 1980
;
McDermott and Doyle 1980
;
Reiter 1980
;
Reiter 1987
;
Moore 1985
; see
Ginsberg 1987
, for an overview). Formally, a system is
non-monotonic
if it violates the monotonicity property that governs classically deductive reasoning:

Monotonicity.

If
\Gamma\mathrel{|}\joinrel\sim\phi
, then
\Gamma\cup\Delta\mathrel{|}\joinrel\sim\phi
,

where
\Gamma,\Delta
are sets of sentences,
\phi
is a sentence, and
\mathrel{|}\joinrel\sim
is an entailment relation. Under Monotonicity, conclusions are robust under information addition: once
\phi
follows from
\Gamma
, it follows from any superset of
\Gamma
. Non-monotonic frameworks, by contrast, allow for
retraction
:
\phi
may cease to follow in light of new information. While ‘non-monotonic reasoning’ refers to this feature, we prefer ‘default reasoning’ to remain agnostic about the underlying formal architecture of reasoning. Our focus is on informal reasoning patterns: drawing defeasible conclusions from incomplete information.

Default reasoning should be distinguished from
commonsense reasoning
, which draws on broad background knowledge (e.g., spatial relational, causal, scientific, social) to draw defeasible inferences, like inferring from
John put his hand in fire
that
John’s hand is hurt

(
Speer et al. 2018
)
. Although such inferences are often defeasible, commonsense reasoning draws on rich worldly knowledge not given by the premise set. In contrast, we target
pure
default reasoning: inferences drawn strictly from given premises, isolating reasoning patterns for controlled evaluation. To this end, we adapt the experimental design of
Holliday et al. 2024
, who examine the capacities of LLMs to distinguish between pure logically correct and logically incorrect reasoning patterns involving modals and conditionals.

Research in non-monotonic reasoning has identified many inference patterns that are defeasibly acceptable but deductively invalid, often using generic generalizations to capture default rules tolerant of exceptions (as in Defeasible Modus Tollens introduced in §
1
). AI researchers developed non-monotonic logics to define a notion of consequence aimed at capturing these inferences, grounding a symbolic approach to default reasoning
(
McCarthy 1980
;
McDermott and Doyle 1980
;
Reiter 1980
;
Reiter 1987
;
Moore 1985
)
, although this notion of consequence should not be confused with classical validity.
Furthermore, while these inference patterns are widely accepted in AI, their psychological status remains debated and human subjects often resist default inferences about individuals known to violate other default rules
(
Elio and Pelletier 1993
;
Pelletier and Elio 1997
;
Pelletier and Elio 2005
)
. In this study, we test LLMs on a broad set of such patterns, from the widely accepted to the contested.
We evaluate performance against expert judgments rather than human data, without taking a stand on their normative status.

2.2
Generic Generalizations

Linguists distinguish two types of generic sentences:
reference-to-kind
generics, where genericity comes from the determiner phrase as in
(1)
, and
characterizing
generics, where genericity is a feature of the whole sentence as in
(2)
.

(1)

a.

The potato was first cultivated in South America.

b.

Potatoes were first cultivated in South America.

(2)

a.

Potatoes contain vitamin C.

b.

A potato contains vitamin C.

c.

The potato contains vitamin C.

d.

Sean has potato with dinner.

This paper focuses on characterizing generics, which express generalizations about kinds or their members without overt quantifiers (e.g.,
every
,
some
,
normally
,
typically
, etc.).

A hallmark of generics is their tolerance to exceptions; although identifying systematic patterns in how they tolerate exceptions has been proven challenging
(
Carlson 1977
;
Krifka et al. 1995
;
Pelletier and Asher 1997
;
Cohen 1999a
;
Leslie 2007
;
Leslie 2008
)
. Generic generalizations vary in strength, ranging from exceptionless definitional statements (e.g.,
Squares have four sides
,
The lion is mammal
), to majority characteristic statements holding of a majority (e.g.,
Birds fly
) or within a restricted subclass (e.g.,
Ducks lay eggs
), to striking property generics true only of a small number (e.g.,
Pit bulls attack children
). Crucially, their truth is not reducible to majority statistics:
Canadians are right-handed
is false despite being true of most Canadians.

Generics in English are expressed with bare plurals (
potatoes
), indefinite singulars (
a potato
), definite singulars (
the potato
), and mass nouns (
potato
), as seen in
(2)
. The expression of genericity varies cross-linguistically, though no known language contains a dedicated phonological marker for genericity
(
Krifka et al. 1995
;
Dahl 1995
;
Chierchia 1998
;
Gerstner-Link 1998
;
Dayal 1999
;
Dayal 2004
;
Behrens 2005
;
Farkas and de Swart 2007
)
. Here we focus on default reasoning with English bare plural generics.

Generics subjects may be natural kinds (
birds
), artifacts (
tables
), and social kind (
Quakers
).
Generics support stative predicates (
contains vitamin C
) and habituals (
fly
), but not episodics (
are outside my tent
). Some predicates, like
fly
, are ambiguous between ‘dispositional’ and ‘episodic’ readings, as well as between different modes of propulsion (self-powered flight vs being transported by air). This yields “doubly generic” interpretations, as in like
Birds fly
, that generalize over both kinds and events.

Following
Krifka et al. 1995
, we treat characterizing generics as having a tripartite structure involving a variable-binding
generic operator
‘Gen’, a
restrictor clause
defining the domain, and a
matrix clause
expressing the predicated property. For example,
A lion has a tail
can be formalized as:

(3)

\textrm{Gen}[x]\big(\textrm{lion}(x);\exists y[\textrm{tail}(y)\wedge\textrm{has}(x,y)]\big)

which roughly means: if
x
is a lion, then, typically,
x
has a tail. More generally, the logical form of a generic sentence is as follows:

(4)

\textrm{Gen}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{i}]\big(\textrm{Restrictor}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i});\exists y_{1},\ldots,y_{j}\textrm{Matrix}[(\{x_{1}\},\ldots,\{x_{i}\},y_{1},\ldots,y_{j})]\big)

where
\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{x}_{i}
are bound by
Gen
,
\accentset{\rightharpoonup}{y}_{j}
are existentially bound in the matrix, and
\phi[\ldots\{x_{m}\}\ldots]
indicates that
x_{m}
may occur free in
\phi
. For readability, we abbreviate this to
\ulcorner Gen(\phi,\psi)\urcorner
, where
\phi
and
\psi
are the restrictor and matrix clauses respectively, suppressing variables as necessary. Determining the appropriate logical form of a generic involves partitioning linguistic material between the restrictor and matrix. This partitioning is highly context-sensitive; it is shaped by discourse topic and prosody structure
(
Rooth 1985
;
Rooth 1995
;
Krifka 1995
;
Cohen 1999b
)
. In the absence of such cues, we assume subjects map to the restrictor and verb phrases to the matrix.

Related Work

Recent work on generics and LLMs has focused on their relation to commonsense knowledge
(
Gajbhiye et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
, training models for generic sentence generation
(
Bhagavatula et al. 2023
)
, and constructing knowledge bases of generics, focusing on exceptions and instantiations
(
Bhakthavatsalam et al. 2020
;
Allaway et al. 2023
;
Allaway et al. 2024
)
. Other work evaluates LLMs on classical and non-monotonic reasoning
(
Parmar et al. 2024
;
Patel et al. 2024
)
, or models the relationship between generics and other forms of quantification
(
Ralethe and Buys 2022
;
Collacciani et al. 2024
)

(
Allaway et al. 2024

Our study differs systematically testing how LLMs handle one-step

(diOSMIn) (e.g., “From ‘Quakers are pacifists’ together with ‘Republicans are not pacifists’, can we reasonably infer ‘Quakers who are Republicans are pacifists’?”;
Correct answer
: no). Overall, 21 inference patterns were tested in total (20 patterns from §
3
plus diOSMIn).

To test reliance on world knowledge gained from training data, we created 20 matched instances of each inference pattern using nonsense kind terms (e.g., “From ‘Zarpies live in the ocean’ together with ‘Sue is a zarpie’, can we reasonably infer ‘Sue lives in the ocean’?”). These versions are denoted with an ‘x’ suffix (e.g., diDMPx).
To test order effects in two-premise inferences, we reversed premise order. These versions are denoted with an ‘o’ suffix (e.g., diDMPo). Combined conditions (both nonsense terms and switched premise order) used ‘ox’ suffix (e.g., diDMPox). (See Table
3
in Appendix B for a summary of the prefix–suffix system with examples.)

To test sensitivity to defeasible vs. deductive reasoning contexts and generic vs. universal generalizations, we compared three matched versions of 8 patterns (BDR, DDMP, DI, DMP, DMT, DRDD, DRII, and FI): a standard defeasible version with generics, a deductive version with generics (e.g., “From ‘Cats are cute’ together with ‘Chuck is a cat’, does it deductively follow ‘Chuck is cute’?”), and a deductive version with universals (e.g., “From ‘All cats are cute’ together with ‘Chuck is a cat’, does it deductively follow ‘Chuck is cute’?”). The latter two versions are denoted by a ‘v’ and ‘va’ prefix (e.g., vDMP and vaDMP) respectively. None of the reasoning patterns are deductively valid when their generic premises are interpreted correctly, but they
become
deductively valid if the generic premise is erroneously interpreted as a universal generalization. Conversely, if the model is misinterpreting the generic premise as a universal one, we would expect it to answer ‘yes’. Therefore, models sensitive to these distinctions should answer ‘no’ to the v- variants, but ‘yes’ to the va-variants.

4.3
Evaluation

For each of the 20 instances of a given inference pattern and for each LLM, we posed each instance under three conditions — zero-shot, few-shot, or zero-shot chain-of-thought — at temperature 0 and 1 for zero-shot and few-shot conditions (see Appendix C for condition samples). At temperature 1, we asked the question repeatedly to obtain an empirical distribution for ‘yes’ and ‘no’ answers. We used an early stopping rule (10 consecutive identical answers or maximum 20 repetitions; for expensive models — Claude 3 Opus, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini 1.5 Pro, GPT-4o (2024-08-06), GPT-4o (2024-05-13), GPT-4 Turbo (2024-04-09), GPT-4 Turbo (1106), GPT-4 (0613), and GPT-4 (0314) — the repetition range was reduced to a minimum of 5 and a maximum of 10 times).

To establish a baseline of performance, we evaluated model performance on 15 uncontroversial inference patterns (DMP, DMT, DDMP, ND, PP, DT, DSA, I, BDR, DRII, DRDD, FI, DI, OSMI, and its negation OSMIn, as well as the x, o, and ox variants of these inferences). Responses were generated with greedy decoding (at temperature = 0) and constrained to the single-token outputs ‘yes’ or ‘no’, ensuring chance-level performance corresponds to 50% accuracy.

To validate the adequacy of using 20 instances of each inference pattern, we tested sensitivity of model responses to the form of reasoning pattern by looking at the Pearson correlation coefficients between the ‘yes’-response frequencies on the 20 sensical instances of each of the 15 core inference patterns vs the matched nonsense versions (average correlation 0.95). This confirmed sensitivity to logical structure over lexical associations. An ablation study randomly sampled 5, 10, 15, and 20 items per inference pattern (with matched x versions), computed the correlation in model ‘yes’-response frequencies across these pairs, and repeated this procedure 100 times per sample size. The average correlation increased steadily with more items (5 items: M=0.716, SD=0.076; 10 items: M=0.717, SD=0.057; 15 items: M=0.718, SD=0.051; 20 items: M=0.723, SD=0.044), but never approached the correlation we observed with the full dataset. This suggests 20 items per pattern were necessary for stable correlations.

We also tested robustness under word variations (e.g., ‘can we defeasibly conclude/deduce’, ‘is it reasonable to conclude’, ‘can we reasonably infer’) and premise presentations (conditional; string of sentences) for BDR, using appropriate prefix/suffix labels (Table
3
, Appendix B).

–

Model tier/size relationships are non-linear.

Despite general trends favouring larger models, notable exceptions exist. Gemma 2 9B outperforms Gemma 2 27B and both Gemini models; Gemini 1.5 Flash outperforms Gemini 1.5 Pro; smaller Llama models outperform their larger counterparts.

Code Llama models perform poorly in zero-shot (6.48% for the 34B model), but show dramatic improvements under CoT (>62%). Conversely, Yi Chat 34B and Llama 2 Chat 70B experience steep CoT performance drops, indicating substantial differences in how models encode default reasoning patterns.

•

Chain-of-thought prompting degrades performance.
Surprisingly, many of the top-performing models show significant performance degradation under CoT prompting (mean accuracy drop -11.14%, SD 15.74% in models performing above 75% accuracy in zero-shot condition, temperature 0). Claude 3 Sonnet drops >16% and GPT-4 (0613) drops >12%, with similar or greater declines observed across many other models. This contradicts expectations that CoT enhances reliability and accuracy.
Importantly, however, CoT prompting does not always lead to performance degradation; GPT-4o models improve under CoT (GPT-4o (2024-05-13) 17.44%; GPT-4o (2024-08-06) 4.98%).

•

Few-shot prompting yields heterogenous effects.
Weaker models substantially benefit from few-shot examples (GPT-4o (2024-05-13): +16.27%; Llama 2 Chat 7B, +28.74%; Claude 3 Haiku, +25.08%), suggesting that contextual anchoring calibrates inferential behaviour. However, high-performing models show minimal effects, the exception being Claude 3 Opus whose performance dropped in few-shot condition (-6.81%). These results show that high-performing models do not benefit much from additional context; indeed, additional examples may introduce noise that reduces performance. But other models that performed worse may benefit from additional context that clarifies the notion of default reasoning through examples.

•

Prompt wording robustness varies considerably.
Testing alternative formulations of BDR revealed high correlations for minor changes (aBDRdc: 0.99; aBDRdd: 0.99; aBDRsdi: 0.88), but moderate-to-weak correlations for others (aBDRc: 0.54; aBDRnq: 0.43; BDR: 0.47). The aBDRnqc variant showed no correlation (-0.05). These results highlight how subtle shifts in phrasing significantly impact LLM behaviour the importance of careful prompt formulation.

5.1
Chain-of-Thought Performance Degradation

Qualitative error analysis under CoT prompting.
To better understand CoT performance degradation, we manually inspected incorrect responses from three representative models with varying CoT deltas (Claude 3.5 Sonnet: -0.33; GPT-4 (0613): -12.62; Gemini 1.5 Flash: -18.77). We identified four common error patterns (see Table
4
, Appendix D for a summary with examples).

1.

Generic
\rightarrow
Universal
: Treating generic claims as universals (e.g., explicitly treating exceptions as counterexamples).

2.

Defeasible vs. Deductive Confusion
: Reasoning deductively where defeasible reasoning applies, misconstruing concept of defeasibility, or insisting on deductive logic.

3.

Overly Statistical
: Using frequency/statistical information as decisive factors, even where defeasible endorsement of a generic would be appropriate (e.g., rejecting acceptable generics because they may not be overwhelmingly true).

4.

Step-Consistent, Bad Conclusion
: Locally coherent intermediate reasoning, but conflicting final conclusions.

These errors suggest two compatible explanations for CoT performance degradation.

•

CoT prompting causes “overthinking”.
Research shows verbal thinking and deliberation can hurt performance on certain tasks in humans
(
Schooler and Engstler-Schooler 1990
;
Dijksterhuis 2004
;
van den Bos and Poletiek 2008
,
inter alia
)
, with similar effects observed in LLMs
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
. CoT prompting may encourage overly analytic processing that misrepresents the defeasible character of default reasoning and generic generalizations. In turn, this could lead to decreased performance in our tasks.

•

CoT prompting reveals conceptual competence gaps.
Our tasks required models to distinguish between defeasible and deductive consequences of premise sets and recognize the exception-permitting behaviour of generics. Error analysis shows models sometimes erroneously applying these concepts, failing to recognize whether it is reasoning defeasibly or deductively, and misinterpreting generic premises as universal generalizations. We examine these behaviours further in §
5.6
.

5.3
Logical Complexity and Length of Arguments

LLMs have been found to struggle with negation contextually complex single inference rules
(
Parmar et al. 2024
)
. Our results partly align with these findings, but suggest a more nuanced picture.

Negation.
Among models with overall performance <90% (as shown in Figure
1
), performance on negation-involving inferences (ND, DMT, DDMP, BDR, DRII, DRDD, OSMI) was highly variable in zero-shot conditions. Code Llama models performed well on ND and DMT, but fell below chance performance on DDMP, BRD, DRII, DRDD, and OSMI. Gemini 1.5 Flash model (87.87% overall) performed well on DMT, DDMP, DRDD, and OSMI, but poorly on BRD and DRII. These inconsistencies suggest model-specific idiosyncrasies rather than a general failure tied to negation. In contrast, models with >90% performance generally handled negation robustly in both zero- and few-shot conditions (temperature 0), with exceptions: GPT-4o models dropped to at/below chance on ND, BDR, and DRDD in zero-shot; Claude 3 Opus showed decreased performance on BDR despite otherwise being a top performer.

Argument Length.
Inference patterns with more than 3 premises (ND, BDR, DRII, DRDD) did not elicit systematically lower performance among models with >90% accuracy. These models remained stable across zero-shot and few-shot conditions. Lower-performing models (<90%) showed variable performance on longer patterns, but no consistent degradation was observed from argument length alone.

In summary, neither negation nor argument length systematically undermined performance across all models. While lower-performing models showed fluctuating performance across these dimensions, top-tier models maintained stable performance.

5.4
Premise Order Effects

For two-premise inferences (DMP, DMT, DT), we investigated whether premise order affected performance.

Defeasible Modus Ponens.
Zero-shot performance typically improved when specific premises were presented first. This trend reversed in few-shot, where models often performed better when generic premises were presented first.

Defeasible Modus Tollens.
Results varied considerably. In zero-shot (temperature 0), GPT-4o models, Code Llama 13B, and Mistral 7B performed better with specific premise first (the latter two models improving from below chance). However, GPT-4 Turbo models showed opposite trends: performance dropped with specific-first, with GPT-4 Turbo (1106) falling below chance. Similar patterns occurred in few-shot conditions.

Defeasible Transitivity.
Zero-shot performance typically improved when the specific premise came first.
Code Llama 13B’s performance improved from 0% to
\sim 80\%
. GPT-4 Turbo again declined under this ordering. While standard prompt variants improved from zero-shot to few-shot, no such improvement was observed for any model family in the reordered (o) variant, which actually declined slightly in few-shot compared to the standard variants.

These order effects are unexpected: order should not affect the validity or plausibility of an inference, yet surface presentation clearly guides LLM inference. This suggests that internal representations are sensitive to input structure in ways that diverge from logical norms.

5.5
Reasoning with Contradictory Information

Previous research reports that LLMs struggle to reason with inconsistent or contradictory information
(
Kazemi et al. 2024
)
.
Two of our inference patterns involved potentially inconsistent or contradictory information: ND and DDMP.
Performance with ND varied considerably among high-performing models in zero-shot (temperature 0). Claude family models performed well (>80%), while the GPT-4 family models varied from below chance (GPT-4 Turbo (1106) to 100% (GPT-4o (2024-05-06). In contrast, performance with DDMP was uniformly high. This suggests that high-end models do have some capacity for correct default reasoning with generic and contradictory information.

6
Conclusions and Future Work

The ability to defeasibly draw conclusions from generic generalizations is crucial for real-world applications of language models. We evaluated 28 large language models on 20 benchmark default reasoning problems across two temperature settings (0 and 1) and three prompting conditions (zero-shot, few-shot, zero-shot CoT). Performance varied widely across and within model families, though several models (Claude 3 Opus, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4, GPT-4 Turbo, Gemma 2 9B Instruct) exceeded 90% accuracy in zero-shot condition. Few-shot prompting sometimes improved performance, but chain-of-thought prompting often led to serious performance degradation. Most models struggled to maintain a distinction between defeasible and deductive reasoning contexts, and between generics and universals, raising concerns about the capacity for non-monotonic reasoning.

Our findings also bear on the broader contrast between connectionist and symbolic systems, specifically whether connectionist systems replicate or diverge from inference patterns traditionally modelled by the symbolic systems. Whereas symbolic systems implement rules as explicit instructions, connectionist systems embed them as patterns of activation and weighted connections within a neural network. Rules are not stored or hard coded as discrete statements, but emerge as learned dispositions to generate certain outputs given certain inputs. While frontier models sometimes display dispositions consistent with expert default reasoning, these behaviours are fragile under prompting conditions and styles. This highlights the limitations of connectionist systems as substitutes for symbolic reasoning.

There are several natural paths for future work. First, LLM behaviour should be compared with actual human behaviour on the inferences we tested. We used reported expert human judgements as a benchmark for performance, although there is some evidence that experimental human subjects draw different inferences
(
Elio and Pelletier 1993
;
Pelletier and Elio 1997
;
Pelletier and Elio 2005
)
. Second, there are many more default inferences involving generics and other logical operators to explore. Third, reasoning with generics is connected with probabilistic and inductive inference, and so this could provide another perspective on LLM reasoning capabilities. Fourth, future evaluations of default reasoning should test new models (OpenAI’s o1, o3, and GPT-5; Anthropic’s latest Claude models; Google’s Gemini 2.5 Pro; Deepseek’s V3). Testing these models for default reasoning capabilities would shed light on the kinds of reasoning tasks in which they excel. Finally, future work should look at the performance of hybrid models that blend connectionist and symbolic approaches.

7
Limitations

Local open-weight models ran in quantized form, which may have reduced their performance.

Our 15 baseline inference patterns excluded social and artefact kinds, due to LLM bias and guardrails against stereotyping, and naming artefacts is unnatural in English. However, results showed no bias between sensical vs. non-sensical kind terms, which suggests this restriction was not limiting. We also focused on default reasoning with bare plural generics. Future work should test social and artefact generics, as well as indefinite or definite singular generics.

Inference patterns were based on non-monotonic reasoning benchmark problems, raising possible data contamination. Although we minimized risks (e.g., using pure inference pattern prompts), we cannot rule out overlap with training data.

All experiments were conducted in English.
Future research should evaluate default reasoning and generics in other languages.

Universal

Generic treated as universal; exceptions explicitly treated as counterexamples

Model
: GPT-4 (0613)

Prompt Class
: diOSMInx

Prompt
: From ‘Snerfs are happy’ together with ‘Quandles are not happy’, can we defeasibly infer ‘Snerfs who are quandles are happy’?

Response
:
Explanation: The statement ‘Snerfs are happy’ implies that all Snerfs, regardless of any other characteristics they may have, are happy. The statement ‘Quandles are not happy’ implies that all Quandles are not happy. However, the question asks about ‘Snerfs who are Quandles’. This is a subset of Snerfs that also have the characteristic of being Quandles. Given the information provided, we have a contradiction. On one hand, if they are Snerfs, they should be happy. On the other hand, if they are Quandles, they should not be happy. Therefore, we cannot make a definitive inference about the happiness of Snerfs who are also Quandles based on the information provided. Answer: no

Defeasible vs. Deductive Confusion

Deductive reasoning used, not defeasible reasoning; concept of defeasibility misconstrued or misapplied

Model
: Gemini 1.5 Flash

Prompt Class
: diDRII

Prompt
: From ‘Hummingbirds drink nectar’ together with ‘Henry is a hummingbird’ and ‘Frank is a hummingbird’ and ‘Frank doesn’t drink nectar’ and ‘Henry is quick’, can we defeasibly infer ‘Henry drinks nectar’?

Response
: Explanation: We are given that hummingbirds drink nectar. We know Henry is a hummingbird. However, we are also told that Frank is a hummingbird but Frank *doesn’t* drink nectar. This contradicts the general statement that hummingbirds drink nectar. The existence of Frank, a hummingbird who doesn’t drink nectar, weakens the defeasible inference that all hummingbirds drink nectar. Therefore, while it’s *likely* Henry drinks nectar based on the general rule, the exception presented by Frank makes the inference defeasible – meaning it could be false. The fact that Henry is quick is irrelevant to whether he drinks nectar. Answer: no

Over-Statistical

Frequency or statistical information taken as decisive, even where defeasible endorsement of a generic would be appropriate

Model
: Gemini 1.5 Flash

Prompt Class
: diDSA

Prompt
: From ‘Spiders spin webs’, can we defeasibly infer ‘Brave spiders spin webs’?

Response
: Explanation: The statement “Spiders spin webs” describes a characteristic behavior of spiders. The statement “Brave spiders spin webs” adds an attribute – bravery – to the spiders that spin webs. While it’s possible that spiders exhibit bravery in spinning webs (perhaps facing dangers while doing so), the original statement doesn’t provide any information about their bravery. The inference is not logically guaranteed. We can’t defeasibly infer bravery from the act of web-spinning. The connection is not established. Therefore, the inference is invalid.

Step-Consistent, Bad Conclusion

Intermediate reasoning is locally coherent but conclusion contradicts those steps

Model
: Gemini 1.5 Flash

Prompt Class
: diDSA

Prompt
: From ‘Hummingbirds drink nectar’, can we defeasibly infer ‘Happy hummingbirds drink nectar’?

Response
: Explanation: The statement “Hummingbirds drink nectar” describes a general characteristic of hummingbirds. It doesn’t specify anything about their emotional state. The statement “Happy hummingbirds drink nectar” adds a condition (happiness) that is not present in the original statement. While it’s *possible* that happy hummingbirds drink nectar, it’s not necessarily true. A hummingbird could be unhappy and still drink nectar. Therefore, the inference is defeasible; it could be proven false by finding a counterexample (a sad hummingbird drinking nectar).
