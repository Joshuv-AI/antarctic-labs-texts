Abstract
. The non-isomorphic locus of a general projection from an embedded smooth projective variety to a hypersurface moves in a linear system of an effective divisor which we call the double point divisor. David Mumford proved that the double point divisor from outer projection is always base point free, and Bo Ilic proved that it is ample except for a Roth variety. The first aim of this paper is to show that the double point divisor from outer projection is very ample except in the Roth case. This answers a question of Bo Ilic. Unlike the case of outer projection, the double point divisor from inner projection may not be base point free nor ample. However, Atsushi Noma proved that it is semiample except when a variety is neither a Roth variety, a scroll over a curve, nor the second Veronese surface. In this paper, we investigate when the double point divisor from inner projection is base point free or big.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
be a non-degenerate smooth projective variety such that it is not a hypersurface.

(1)

For a point

, the set
S_{\operatorname{out}}
of geometric sections separates tangent directions at

if and only if

\mathscr{C}(X)=\varnothing

separates all the tangent directions.

(2)

The subspace
V_{\operatorname{out}}
of

spanned by geometric sections separates all the tangent directions. In particular, if
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is not a Roth variety, then the linear system

is very ample, and hence, the divisor

is very ample.

Note that a subset

to
X
at
x
, we can construct a geometric divisor

such that

. Note that

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
be a non-degenerate smooth projective variety of dimension
n\geq 2
, codimension
e\geq 2
such that it is not a Noma’s exceptional variety. Suppose that
\mathscr{C}(X)\neq\varnothing
and
Y:=\overline{\pi_{x,X}(X\setminus\{x\})}
is smooth for a point
x\in\mathscr{C}(X)
. Then

except when

is a quadric surface (cf. Example
5.2
).

In the situation of Theorem
1.2
, Noma’s result
[
19
, Theorem 4]
says that the blow-up
\widetilde{X}
of

at
x
with the exceptional divisor
E
is a smooth member of
\lvert\mathscr{O}_{F}(\mu)\otimes\pi^{*}L\rvert
for
\mu=\deg\pi_{x,X}
, where

with the canonical projection
\tau_{F}\colon F\to Y
for

. Note that

if and only if the restriction map

is surjective. We show the surjectivity of the restriction map through cohomological arguments using the geometry of

and

. If one sets up the problem well, then it can be easily solved when

is not Noma’s exceptional variety. However, in the case of Noma’s exceptional variety, a careful case-by-case analysis is needed.
We are not aware of any example where

is not base point free other than Noma’s example where

(Example
5.2
). It would be exceedingly interesting to study what happens when

is singular.

Recall from
[
9
, Corollary 5.3]
that
D_{\operatorname{out}}
is big if and only if
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is not the Segre embedding of
\mathbf{P}^{1}\times\mathbf{P}^{n-1}
. It is also worth to study when
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is big. Here we keep assuming that
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is not a Noma’s exceptional variety. If

is not big, then

is base point free (Proposition

). We expect that there are only a few cases where

is not big, and in each case, the morphism given by

would have a special structure like the adjunction mapping. We have three examples where

is not big (Example

): unless

is a del Pezzo variety (equivalent to

is not a del Pezzo variety,

is big in the following cases:

(1)

-K_{X}
and

are proportional. This holds whenever

by Barth–Larsen theorem (cf.
[

(2)

Our result in this direction is the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
be a non-degenerate smooth projective variety of dimension
n\geq 2
, codimension
e\geq n+1
, and degree
d
. Assume that
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is neither a Noma’s exceptional variety nor a del Pezzo variety. If

then
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is big except when

induces a linear fibration

(cf. Example

).

If

, then the degree condition in Theorem
1.3
becomes

, which is automatically satisfied (see Corollary

). In other words,

becomes big as soon as

is large enough. Conversely, when

and

is not big, we have

, so

belongs to a bounded family once we fix the dimension

. It would be possible to classify all such cases. On the other hand, we also derive the following consequence asserting that the Iitaka dimension
\kappa(D_{\operatorname{inn}})
is large. This suggests that the image of the morhpism given by

might be big in contrast to the adjunction mapping.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
be a non-degenerate smooth projective variety of dimension
n\geq 2
, codimension
e\geq 2
, and degree
d
. Assume that

\kappa(D_{\operatorname{inn}})\geq(3n-1)/4
.

To prove Theorem
1.3
, it is enough to show
D_{\operatorname{inn}}^{n}>0
because

is nef, but it is not easy to control the intersection numbers

. Instead, we utilize the technique developed by the second author with Sijong Kwak in

15
]

): if

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Roth varieties

A
Roth variety

X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is an isomorphic projection of a divisor on a rational normal scroll
\overline{S}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{N}
which is the image of the birational embedding
\psi
of
S:=\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}^{\oplus 2}\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}(a_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}(a_{n-1}))
with
1\leq a_{1}\leq\cdots a_{n-1}
given by
\lvert\mathscr{O}_{S}(1)\rvert
such that
X
is a general smooth member of
\lvert\mathscr{O}_{S}(b)\otimes\pi^{*}\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}(1)\rvert
for an integer
b\geq 1
, where
\pi\colon S\to\mathbf{P}^{1}
is the canonical projection. Here
N=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n-1}+n
and
\deg\overline{S}=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n-1}
. The birational morphism
\psi\colon S\to\overline{S}
contracts
\Gamma:=\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}^{\oplus 2})\subseteq S
to a line
\ell\subseteq\overline{S}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{N}
. Note that
\psi|_{X}\colon X\to\mathbf{P}^{N}
is an embedding and
\ell=\psi(\Gamma)=\psi(X\cap\Gamma)\subseteq X
. Here
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{N}
is non-degenerate and linearly normal, and
\deg X=b(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n-1})+1
. If
b=1
, then the Roth variety
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a rational scroll. See
[
9
, Section 3]
for more details.

2.2.
Segre loci

In this subsection, we do not assume that
X
is smooth. The
outer Segre locus
of
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is defined as

\mathscr{B}(X):=\{u\in\mathbf{P}^{r}\setminus X\mid\operatorname{length}(X\cap\langle x,u\rangle)\geq 2~\text{ for general $x\in X$}\}.

Note that
u\in\mathscr{B}(X)
if and only if the outer projection
\pi_{u,X}\colon X\to\mathbf{P}^{r-1}
centered at
u
is nonbirational. Keep in mind that we always assume
e\geq 2
. Then we have
\dim\mathscr{B}(X)\leq\min\{n-1,\dim\operatorname{Sing}X+1\}
(
[
17
, Theorem 4]
). When
X
is smooth, we put
\dim\operatorname{Sing}X=-1
. The
inner Segre locus
of
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is defined as

\mathscr{C}(X):=\{u\in X\mid\operatorname{length}(X\cap\langle u,x\rangle)\geq 3~\text{for general $x\in X$}\}.

Note that
u\in\mathscr{C}(X)
if and only if the inner projection
\pi_{u,X}\colon X\dashrightarrow\mathbf{P}^{r-1}
centered at
u
is nonbirational. As
e\geq 2
, we have
\dim\mathscr{C}(X)\leq\min\{n-1,\dim\operatorname{Sing}X+2\}
(
[
17
, Theorem 5]
). When
X
is smooth,
\dim\mathscr{C}(X)\leq 1
and the equality holds if and only if
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a Roth variety but not a rational scroll. We refer to
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
for more details.

2.3.
Birational divisors on conical scrolls

We assume again that
X
is smooth. If
\mathscr{C}(X)
is a nonempty finite set and
x\in\mathscr{C}(X)
, then the inner projection
\pi_{x,X}\colon X\dashrightarrow\overline{Y}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r-1}
centered at
x
is not birational. Note that the cone

\operatorname{Cone}(x,X):=\overline{\bigcup_{u\in X\setminus\{x\}}\langle x,u\rangle}

is a conical scroll over
\overline{Y}
. There is a non-degenerate birational embedding
\nu\colon Y\to\overline{Y}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r-1}
from a smooth projective variety
Y
of dimension
n
such that for
F:=\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{O}_{Y}\oplus\mathscr{O}_{Y}(1))
with
\mathscr{O}_{Y}(1)=\nu^{*}\mathscr{O}_{\overline{Y}}(1)
, a linear subsystem of
\lvert\mathscr{O}_{F}(1)\rvert
gives a birational embedding
\sigma_{F}\colon F\to\mathbf{P}^{r}
with
\overline{F}:=\sigma_{F}(F)=\operatorname{Cone}(x,X)
contracting
\Gamma:=\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{O}_{Y})\subseteq F
to
x\in\overline{F}
. If
\tau_{F}\colon F\to Y
is the canonical projection, then
\mathscr{O}_{F}(\Gamma)=\mathscr{O}_{F}(1)\otimes\tau_{F}^{*}\mathscr{O}_{Y}(-1)
. By
[
19
, Theorem 4]
,
X
is a
birational divisor of type
(\mu,L)
on a conical scroll
\overline{F}
: there is a prime divisor
\widetilde{X}\in\lvert\mathscr{O}_{F}(\mu)\otimes\tau_{F}^{*}L\rvert
for some
\mu\geq 2
and
L\in\operatorname{Pic}(Y)
with
H^{0}(Y,L)\neq 0
and
L.\mathscr{O}_{Y}(1)^{n-1}=1
such that
\sigma:=\sigma_{F}|_{\widetilde{X}}\colon\widetilde{X}\to X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a non-degenerate birational embedding and
\mu=\deg\pi_{x,X},d=\mu\deg\overline{Y}+1
. If
Y
is smooth, then we may take
\nu=\operatorname{id}_{Y}
. In this case,
\sigma
is the blow-up of
X
at
x
, and
E:=\Gamma\cap\widetilde{X}
is the exceptional divisor. Using
[
19
, Theorem 5]
and Bertini theorem, one can construct smooth birational divisors on conical scrolls (see
[
19
, Example 4.2]
and Example
5.12
).

2.4.
Double point divisors from outer projection

Let
\Lambda
be an
(e-2)
-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathbf{P}^{r}
with
X\cap\Lambda=\varnothing
, and consider the outer projection

\pi_{\Lambda,X}\colon X\longrightarrow\overline{X}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+1}

centered at
\Lambda
. Notice that
\pi_{\Lambda,X}
is isomorphic in a neighborhood of
x\in X
if and only if
\Lambda\cap\operatorname{Cone}(x,X)=\varnothing
. Thus if
\Lambda\cap\operatorname{Cone}(x,X)=\varnothing
for some
x\in X
, then
\pi_{\Lambda,X}
is finite and birational. Suppose that
\pi_{\Lambda,X}
is finite and birational. Note that
\deg\overline{X}=d
. By birational double–point formula (cf.
[
16
, Lemma 10.2.8]
), there exists an effective divisor
D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})
on
X
such that

D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})\sim-K_{X}+\pi_{\Lambda,X}^{*}K_{\overline{X}}=-K_{X}+(d-n-2)H=D_{\operatorname{out}}.

Note that
\operatorname{Supp}(D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X}))
is the non-isomorphic locus of
\pi_{\Lambda,X}
. If
S_{\Lambda,X}
is the cone over
\pi_{\Lambda,X}(D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X}))
with vertex at
\Lambda
, then scheme-theoretically

D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})=X\cap S_{\Lambda,X}
.
Recall that the linear subsystem
\lvert V_{\operatorname{out}}\rvert
generated by geometric divisors is base point free (
[
2
, Technical Appendix 4]
). Furthermore,
\lvert V_{\operatorname{out}}\rvert
separates two distinct points and hence
D_{\operatorname{out}}
is ample unless
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a Roth variety (
[
9
, Theorem 4.2]
). When
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a Roth variety,
D_{\operatorname{out}}.\ell=0
for the line
\ell=\psi(\Gamma)\subseteq X
and thus
D_{\operatorname{out}}
is not ample (
[
9
, Proposition 3.8]
).

2.5.
Double point divisors from inner projection

Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{e-1}\in X
be general points, and consider the inner projection

\pi_{\Lambda,X}\colon X\dashrightarrow\overline{X}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+1}

centered at
\Lambda:=\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{e-1}\rangle\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
. By the general position lemma (cf.
[
18
, Lemma 1.2]
),
\Lambda
is an
(e-2)
-dimensional linear subspace in
\mathbf{P}^{r}
.
Let
\sigma\colon\widetilde{X}\to X
be the blow-up of
X
at
x_{1},\ldots,x_{e-1}
. There exists a birational morphism
\widetilde{\pi}_{\Lambda,X}\colon\widetilde{X}\to\overline{X}
. We have the following commutative diagram

\textstyle{\widetilde{X}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sigma}
\scriptstyle{\widetilde{\pi}_{\Lambda,X}}
\textstyle{X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{\Lambda,X}}
\textstyle{\mathbf{P}^{r}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\overline{X}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathbf{P}^{n+1}.}

Noma
[
18
, Theorem 3]
proved that if
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is neither a scroll over a smooth projective curve nor the second Veronese surface, then
\widetilde{\pi}_{\Lambda,X}\colon\widetilde{X}\to X
does not contract any divisor. In this case,
\deg(\overline{X})=d-e+1
. By the birational double–point formula, there exists an effective divisor
\widetilde{D}(\widetilde{\pi}_{\Lambda,X})
on
\widetilde{X}
such that

\widetilde{D}(\widetilde{\pi}_{\Lambda,X})\sim-K_{\widetilde{X}}+\widetilde{\pi}_{\Lambda,X}^{*}K_{\overline{X}}.

Then

D_{\operatorname{inn}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})=\sigma_{*}\widetilde{D}(\widetilde{\pi}_{\Lambda,X})\sim-K_{X}+(d-n-e-1)H=D_{\operatorname{inn}}.

Note that
\operatorname{Supp}(D_{\operatorname{inn}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X}))
is the divisorial part of the closure of the non-isomorphic locus of
\pi_{\Lambda,X}
. Recall that
\operatorname{Bs}\lvert D_{\operatorname{inn}}\rvert\subseteq\mathscr{C}(X)
(
[
18
, Theorem 1]
). When
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is not a Roth variety,
\mathscr{C}(X)
is at most a finite set. Thus if
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is not a Noma’s exceptional variety (a Roth variety, a scroll over a smooth projective curve, or the second Veronese surface), then Fujita–Zariski theorem (cf.
[
16
, Remark 2.1.32]
) implies that
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is semiample (
[
18
, Theorem 4]
).

2.6.
Castelnuovo’s genus bound

Let
C\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
be a non-degenerate smooth projective curve of genus
g
, codimension
e
, and degree
d
. Castelnuovo’s genus bound (
[
1
, page 116]
) says that

(2.1)

g\leq\frac{(d-1-\epsilon)(d+\epsilon-e-1)}{2e}=\frac{(d-e-2)d+(\varepsilon+1)(e+1-\varepsilon)}{2e},

where
m:=\lfloor(d-1)/e\rfloor
and
\varepsilon:=(d-1)-me
. Note that
m\geq 1
and
0\leq\varepsilon\leq e-1
. When the equality holds,
C\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is projectively normal (
[
1
, page 117]
). If furthermore
d\geq 2e+3
, then
C
is a smooth divisor on a rational normal scroll except when
e=4
and
d=2k
, in which case,
C\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{5}
is the image of the plane curve of degree
k
under the second Veronese embedding
v_{2}\colon\mathbf{P}^{2}\to\mathbf{P}^{5}
(
[
1
, Theorem 2.5]
).

3.
Very ampleness of
S_{\operatorname{out}}

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1

(1)
. We first show some lemmas, which are certainly well-known, but we write down the detailed proofs for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
L
be a line in
\mathbf{P}^{r}
passing through a point
x\in X
. If
n\geq 3
and
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n-2}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
are general hyperplanes containing
L
, then
X_{i}:=X\cap H_{1}\cap\cdots\cap H_{i}
is smooth for each
i=1,\ldots,n-2
.

Proof.

By Bertini theorem, we may assume
X_{i}
is smooth at the points in
X_{i}\setminus L
. We claim by induction on
i
that
X_{i}
is smooth at the points of
L\cap X_{i}
. For
i=0
, clearly
X_{0}:=X
is smooth. For
i\leq n-3
, if
X_{i}
is smooth at the points of
L\cap X_{i}
, then for each
y\in L\cap X_{i}
, the projective tangent space
\mathbf{T}_{y}X_{i}
has codimension
r-n
in
\mathbf{P}^{r-i}=H_{1}\cap\cdots\cap H_{i}
.
The hyperplanes containing
\mathbf{T}_{y}X_{i}
form a linear subspace of dimension
r-n-1
in
(\mathbf{P}^{r-i})^{\vee}
. Since
\dim(L\cap X_{i})\leq 1
, the locus of hyperplanes containing at least one of
\{\mathbf{T}_{y}X_{i}\}_{y\in L\cap X_{i}}
is of dimension at most
r-n
in
(\mathbf{P}^{r-i})^{\vee}
. On the other hand, the hyperplanes containing
L
form a linear subspace of dimension
r-i-2\geq r-n+1
. Hence we may pick
H_{i+1}
which does not contain any of
\mathbf{T}_{y}X_{i}
. Then
X_{i+1}=X_{i}\cap H_{i+1}
is smooth at the points of
X_{i+1}\cap L
.
∎

By the above lemma, we can always take a smooth surface section
S\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r-n+2}
of
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
such that
L
is still contained in
\mathbf{P}^{r-n+2}
. To attain a curve section
C\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r-n+1}
such that
L
is contained in
\mathbf{P}^{r-n+1}
, we should sacrifice the smoothness when
L\subseteq S
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
L
be a line in
\mathbf{P}^{r}
passing through a point
x\in X
, and
C:=X\cap H_{1}\cap\cdots\cap H_{n-1}
for general hyperplanes
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n-1}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
containing
L
.

(1)

If
L\not\subseteq X
, then
C
is smooth.

(2)

If
L\subseteq X
, then
C
is the union of
L
and a smooth curve
C^{\prime}
with
x\not\in C^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
,
S:=X\cap H_{1}\cap\cdots\cap H_{n-2}
is a smooth projective surface. To prove (1), assume
L\not\subseteq S
. The hyperplanes in
\mathbf{P}^{r-n+2}=H_{1}\cap\cdots\cap H_{n-2}
containing at least one of
\{\mathbf{T}_{y}S\}_{y\in L\cap S}
form a family of dimension at most
r-n-1
, while the hyperplanes in
\mathbf{P}^{r-n+2}
containing
L
form a linear space of dimension
r-n
. Thus there exists a hyperplane
H_{n-1}
not containing any of
\mathbf{T}_{y}S
. This proves (1).
To prove (2), assume
L\subseteq S
. The image of the restriction map
H^{0}(\mathbf{P}^{r-n+2},\mathscr{I}_{L|\mathbf{P}^{r-n+2}}(1))\rightarrow H^{0}(S,\mathscr{I}_{L|S}(1))
gives rise to a base point free linear subsystem of
\lvert\mathscr{O}_{S}(1)\otimes\mathscr{O}_{S}(-L)\rvert
. Then (2) follows from Bertini theorem.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
L
be a projective tangent line to
X
at a point
x\in X
. Then
\mathbf{T}_{y}X\cap L=\varnothing
for a general point
y\in X
.

Proof.

To derive a contradiction, suppose that
\mathbf{T}_{y}X\cap L\neq\varnothing
for every
y\in X
. Consider a general curve section
C\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{e+1}
of
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
such that
\mathbf{T}_{x}C=L
. By Corollary
3.2
,
C
is smooth when
L\not\subseteq X
or
C=L\cup C^{\prime}
such that
C^{\prime}
is smooth with
x\not\in C^{\prime}
when
L\subseteq X
. Consider the projection
\pi_{p,C}\colon C\dashrightarrow\overline{C}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{e}
centered at a general point
p\in L
. Notice that all projective tangent lines to
\overline{C}
pass through one point, i.e,
\overline{C}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{e}
is a strange curve. However, there is no strange curve other than a line in characteristic zero, so we get a contradiction.
∎

We are ready to prove Theorem
1.1

(1)
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1

(1)
.

First, consider the case when
\mathscr{C}(X)\neq\varnothing
and
x\in\mathscr{C}(X)
. Let

\pi_{\Lambda,X}\colon X\rightarrow\overline{X}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+1}
be an outer projection centered at an
(e-2)
-dimensional linear subspace
\Lambda
in
\mathbf{P}^{r}
such that
D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})
is defined and
x\in\operatorname{Supp}(D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X}))
.
We claim that

\operatorname{mult}_{x}D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})\geq 2,

which implies that
S_{\operatorname{out}}
does not separate any tangent directions at
x
. For a general
(e+1)
-dimensional linear subspace
M
in
\mathbf{P}^{r}
containing
\Lambda
and
x
, consider the curve section

C:=X\cap M\subseteq M=\mathbf{P}^{e+1}

of
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
. By Bertini theorem,
C
is smooth. Note that

D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})|_{C}=D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,C})~~\text{and}~~\operatorname{mult}_{x}D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})=\operatorname{mult}_{x}D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,C}).

Since
\pi_{\Lambda,C}^{-1}(\pi_{\Lambda,C}(x))=C\cap\langle\Lambda,x\rangle
and
\operatorname{length}(C\cap\langle\Lambda,x\rangle)\geq 3
, it follows that
\operatorname{mult}_{\pi_{\Lambda,C}(x)}\overline{C}\geq 3
.
Now, take a log resolution
f\colon S\to\mathbf{P}^{2}
of
(\mathbf{P}^{2},\overline{C})
, and write
f^{*}\overline{C}=C+F
for some
f
-exceptional divisor
F
. Then

-(K_{S/\mathbf{P}^{2}}-F)|_{C}=-\big((K_{S}+C)-f^{*}(K_{\mathbf{P}^{2}}+\overline{C})\big)|_{C}=-K_{C}+f^{*}K_{\overline{C}}=D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,C}).

As
\operatorname{mult}_{\pi_{\Lambda,C}(x)}\overline{C}\geq 3
, we obtain
\operatorname{mult}_{x}D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,C})\geq 2
.

Next, consider the case when
x\in X\setminus\mathscr{C}(X)
. For any projective tangent line
L
to
X
at
x
, we show that there is a section in
S_{\operatorname{out}}
separating the tangent direction at
x
corresponding to
L
. By Lemma
3.3
,
\mathbf{T}_{y}X\cap L=\varnothing
for a general point
y\in X
. As
x\not\in\mathscr{C}(X)
, we may assume that
X\cap\langle x,y\rangle=\{x,y\}
as a scheme. Let
H
be a general hyperplane in
\mathbf{P}^{r}
such that
\mathbf{T}_{y}X\subseteq H,~x\in H
, and
L\not\subseteq H
. We can take a general
(e-2)
-dimensional linear subspace
\Lambda
in
H
such that
\Lambda\cap\mathbf{T}_{y}X=\varnothing
,
\Lambda\cap\langle x,y\rangle=\Lambda\cap\operatorname{Cone}(x,X)=\{p\}

as a scheme for a point
p\not\in X
, and
\Lambda\cap\operatorname{Cone}(q,X)=\varnothing
for a general point
q\in X
. In particular,
\Lambda\cap X=\varnothing
. Note that
\Lambda\cap\mathbf{T}_{x}X\subseteq\Lambda\cap\operatorname{Cone}(x,X)=\{p\}
and
p\in\langle x,y\rangle
. If
\Lambda\cap\mathbf{T}_{x}X=\{p\}
, then
\langle x,p\rangle=\langle x,y\rangle\subseteq\mathbf{T}_{x}X
, which is a contradiction. Thus
\Lambda\cap\mathbf{T}_{x}X=\varnothing
. Suppose that there is a point
u\in\langle\Lambda,x\rangle\cap X
with
u\neq x,y
. Since
X\cap\langle x,y\rangle=\{x,y\}
, it follows that
u\not\in\langle x,y\rangle
. Then
\langle x,u\rangle
intersects
\Lambda
at some point
v\neq p
so that
v\in\Lambda\cap\operatorname{Cone}(x,X)
, which is a contradiction to that
\Lambda\cap\operatorname{Cone}(x,X)=\{p\}
. Thus
\langle\Lambda,x\rangle\cap X=\{x,y\}
as a set. However,
\Lambda\cap\mathbf{T}_{x}X=\varnothing
and
\Lambda\cap\mathbf{T}_{y}X=\varnothing
, so
\langle\Lambda,x\rangle\cap X=\{x,y\}
as a scheme. Now, as
\Lambda\cap\operatorname{Cone}(p,X)=\varnothing
for a general point
p\in X
, the outer projection

\pi_{\Lambda,X}\colon X\longrightarrow\overline{X}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+1}

centered at
\Lambda
is finite birational and hence
D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})
is defined. We have

\pi_{\Lambda,X}^{-1}(\pi_{\Lambda,X}(x))=\langle\Lambda,x\rangle\cap X=\{x,y\}.

Notice that
L\not\subseteq\langle\Lambda,\mathbf{T}_{y}X\rangle
since
\langle\Lambda,\mathbf{T}_{y}X\rangle\subseteq H
but
L\not\subseteq H
. Thus
\pi_{\Lambda,X}(L)\not\subseteq\pi_{\Lambda,X}(\mathbf{T}_{y}X)
. As
\pi_{\Lambda,X}(L)\subseteq\pi_{\Lambda,X}(\mathbf{T}_{x}X)
, we see that
\pi_{\Lambda,X}(\mathbf{T}_{x}X)
and
\pi_{\Lambda,X}(\mathbf{T}_{y}X)
meet transversally. Note that

\pi_{\Lambda,X}(L)\not\subseteq\pi_{\Lambda,X}(\mathbf{T}_{x}X)\cap\pi_{\Lambda,X}(\mathbf{T}_{y}X)=\mathbf{T}_{\pi_{\Lambda,X}(x)}\pi_{\Lambda,X}(D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})).

Hence
L\not\subseteq\mathbf{T}_{x}D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})
. This means the section of
D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})
separates the tangent direction at
x
corresponding to
L
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Suppose that
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a smooth projective curve.
In this case, it was claimed in
[
9
, Proof of Proposition 2.7]
that
S_{\operatorname{out}}
separates tangent vectors at each point
x\in X
. This contradicts Theorem
1.1

(1)
. In fact, the proof in
[
9
]
overlooks the possibility that
\mathscr{C}(X)
could be nonempty (see Example
4.18

(1)
for explicit examples of
\mathscr{C}(X)\neq\varnothing
). However, the statement of
[
9
, Proposition 2.7]
that
D_{\operatorname{out}}
is very ample is quite correct because one can easily check that

using Castelnuovo’s genus bound (

. Here we want to stress that the tangent separation problem of

rather than

is not easy at all even for the curve case. Most part of the next section is devoted to proving that
\lvert V_{\operatorname{out}}\rvert
separates tangent vectors in the curve case.

4.
Very ampleness of
\lvert V_{\operatorname{out}}\rvert

4.1.
Overall framework

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1

(2)
. More specifically, we prove that the linear system
\lvert V_{\operatorname{out}}\rvert
separates tangents vectors at each point
x\in X
. This implies that
\lvert V_{\operatorname{out}}\rvert
is very ample when
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is not a Roth variety since
\lvert V_{\operatorname{out}}\rvert
separates distinct two points by
[
9
, Theorem 4.2]
.
Let
\Lambda^{e-2}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
be a general linear subspace of codimension
r-(e-2)=n+2
from which the linear projection induces a finite birational morphism
\pi_{\Lambda,X}\colon X\to\mathbf{P}^{n+1}
. By definition,
\lvert V_{\operatorname{out}}\rvert
is the linear subsystem of
\lvert D_{\operatorname{out}}\rvert=\lvert-K_{X}+(d-n-2)H_{X}\rvert
spanned by the divisors
D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})
, where
D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})
is the double point divisor of
\pi_{\Lambda,X}
. By Theorem
1.1

(1)
,
D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})
is always singular at
x
whenever it passes through
x
. Thus to prove Theorem
1.1

(2)
, it is necessary to manipulate the linear combinations of geometric sections in the vector space
H^{0}(X,D_{\operatorname{out}})
.

One of the key ingredients is the identity (
1.1
) by Mumford (
[
2
, Technical Appendix 4]
). For the reader’s convenience, we recall the notations here. Let
\Lambda^{\prime e-1}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
be a linear subspace of codimension
r-(e-1)=n+1
which does not intersect
X
. The linear projection from
\Lambda^{\prime}
induces a dominant morphism
\pi_{\Lambda^{\prime},X}\colon X\to\mathbf{P}^{n}
. Let
\operatorname{Ram}(\pi_{\Lambda^{\prime},X})
be the ramification divisor of
\pi_{\Lambda^{\prime},X}
. Let
\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda^{\prime}
be a linear subspace of codimension one such that the projection from
\Lambda
induces a finite birational morphism
\pi_{\Lambda,X}\colon X\to\overline{X}_{\Lambda}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{n+1}
. The morphism
\pi_{\Lambda^{\prime},X}\colon X\to\mathbf{P}^{n}
factors as

X\xrightarrow{\,\pi_{\Lambda,X}\,}\overline{X}_{\Lambda}\xrightarrow{\,\pi_{R,\overline{X}_{\Lambda}}\,}\mathbf{P}^{n},

where the point
R\in\mathbf{P}^{n+1}
is the projection image of
\Lambda^{\prime}\setminus\Lambda
. Fix a homogeneous coordinates
[Z_{0},\dots,Z_{n+1}]
of
\mathbf{P}^{n+1}
and let
f_{\Lambda}\in\mathbf{C}[\mathbf{P}^{n+1}]=\mathbf{C}[Z_{0},\dots,Z_{n+1}]
be the defining equation of
\overline{X}_{\Lambda}
. For a point
Q=[q_{0},\dots,q_{n+1}]\in\mathbf{P}^{n+1}
, define the differential operator
\partial_{Q}
as follows:

\partial_{Q}:=Q\cdot\nabla=q_{0}\frac{\partial}{\partial Z_{0}}+q_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial Z_{1}}+\dots+q_{n+1}\frac{\partial}{\partial Z_{n+1}}.

We remark that
\partial_{Q}
is defined only up to multiplications by nonzero constants. The projection
\pi_{\Lambda,\mathbf{P}^{r}}\colon\mathbf{P}^{r}\dashrightarrow\mathbf{P}^{n+1}
induces
\pi_{\Lambda,\mathbf{P}^{r}}^{*}\colon\mathbf{C}[\mathbf{P}^{n+1}]\hookrightarrow\mathbf{C}[\mathbf{P}^{r}]
, and it maps
\partial_{R}f_{\Lambda}
to the polynomial
F_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}}:=\pi_{\Lambda,\mathbf{P}^{r}}^{*}(\partial_{R}f_{\Lambda})
. Now, the identity (
1.1
) says that the divisor
\operatorname{Ram}(\pi_{\Lambda^{\prime},X})+D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})
on
X
equals to the scheme-theoretic intersection
V(F_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}})\cap X
.

To obtain a linear combination of geometric sections, we take codimension one linear subspaces
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}\subseteq\Lambda^{\prime}
and consider linear combination of
a_{1}F_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda^{\prime}}-a_{2}F_{\Lambda_{2},\Lambda^{\prime}}
for
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbf{C}
. The hypersurface
V(a_{1}F_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda^{\prime}}-a_{2}F_{\Lambda_{2},\Lambda^{\prime}})\cap X
in
X
is the divisor
\operatorname{Ram}(\pi_{\Lambda^{\prime},X})+D
on
X
, where
D\in\lvert V_{\operatorname{out}}\rvert
. The proof of Theorem
1.1

(2)
consists of the following steps:

(1)

fix a point
x
and a projective tangent line
L\subseteq\mathbf{T}_{x}X
;

(2)

take
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}\subseteq\Lambda^{\prime}
such that
x\not\in V(F_{\Lambda_{i},\Lambda})
for
i=1,2
;

(3)

find suitable
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbf{C}
such that
V(a_{1}F_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda^{\prime}}-a_{2}F_{\Lambda_{2},\Lambda^{\prime}})
contains
x
but is not tangent to
L
at
x
.

Even though the purpose of each step appears simple and straightforward, verifying it requires rather involved procedure. The initial step is to reduce the dimension and codimension, which allows us to assume
X^{n}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a space curve (namely,
(n,r)=(1,3)
).

4.2.
Reduction to space curves

We fix
x\in X
and a tangent line
L\subseteq\mathbf{T}_{x}X
. We will reduce
\dim X
to
1
by taking hyperplane sections containing
L
. If
n=\dim X\geq 3
, then we can choose a general smooth surface section
S\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
with
L\subseteq\mathbf{T}_{x}S
by Lemma
3.1
. Thus we may reduce the problem to the surface case. If
L\not\subseteq S
, then a general curve section
C\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
with
L=\mathbf{T}_{x}C
is still smooth. However, if
L\subseteq S
, then a general curve section
C\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
with
L=\mathbf{T}_{x}C
is the union of
L
and a smooth projective curve
C^{\prime}
with
x\not\in C^{\prime}
(see Corollary
3.2
).

To justify the reduction procedure, we also need to compare
V_{\operatorname{out}}
of
X
with that of its hyperplane sections.

Proposition 4.1
.

Notations
X,\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime},R,f_{\Lambda},F_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}}
as in Subsection
4.1
. Let
H\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
be a hyperplane and
H_{X}:=X\cap H
. We assume further that
\Lambda^{\prime}\subseteq H\setminus X
and that
\pi_{\Lambda,H_{X}}\colon H_{X}\to\mathbf{P}^{n}
is finite birational. Let
R_{H}\in\mathbf{P}^{n}
be the projection of
\Lambda^{\prime}
under
\pi_{\Lambda,H}\colon H\dashrightarrow\mathbf{P}^{n}
. For the polynomial
f_{\Lambda,H}\in\mathbf{C}[\mathbf{P}^{n}]
defining
\pi_{\Lambda,H_{X}}(H_{X})
, we associate
F_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime},H}\in\mathbf{C}[H]
with
\partial_{R_{H}}f_{\Lambda,H}
via the inclusion
\pi_{\Lambda,H}^{*}\colon\mathbf{C}[\mathbf{P}^{n}]\hookrightarrow\mathbf{C}[H]
. Then we have

V(F_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime},H})=V(F_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}})\cap H.

Proof.

Let
[Z_{0},\dots,Z_{r}]
be the homogeneous coordinates for
\mathbf{P}^{r}
. Assume
H=V(Z_{r})
,
\Lambda^{\prime}=V(Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{n},Z_{r})
, and
\Lambda=V(Z_{0},Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n},Z_{r})
. The target of the projection
\pi_{\Lambda,X}
is
\mathbf{P}^{n+1}
with the homogeneous coordinates
[Z_{0},Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n},Z_{r}]
, and the morphism
\pi_{\Lambda,H_{X}}\colon H_{X}\to\mathbf{P}^{n}
is simply the restriction of
\pi_{\Lambda,X}
to
H=V(Z_{r})
. It follows that
\pi_{\Lambda,H_{X}}(H_{X})=\pi_{\Lambda,X}(X)\big|_{H}
, or equivalently,

f_{\Lambda,H}(Z_{0},Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})=f_{\Lambda}(Z_{0},Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n},0).

By descriptions of
\Lambda^{\prime}
and
\Lambda
, the coordinates of
R=\pi_{\Lambda,\mathbf{P}^{r}}(\Lambda^{\prime})
is
[1,0,\dots,0]\in\mathbf{P}^{n+1}
. Similarly, the coordinates of
R_{H}
is
[1,0,\dots,0]\in\mathbf{P}^{n}
. This shows that both
\partial_{R}
and
\partial_{R_{H}}
are
\partial/\partial Z_{0}
. It follows that
\partial_{R_{H}}f_{\Lambda,H}(Z_{0},\dots,Z_{n})=\partial_{R}f_{\Lambda}(Z_{0},\dots,Z_{n},0)
holds, and consequently,

F_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime},H}(Z_{0},Z_{1},\dots,Z_{r-1})=F_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}}(Z_{0},Z_{1},\dots,Z_{r-1},0).

This proves the proposition.
∎

Proposition
4.1
implies the following. If
H
is a hyperplane containing
L
and if
V(a_{1}F_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda^{\prime},H}-a_{2}F_{\Lambda_{2},\Lambda^{\prime},H})
intersects
L
transversally at
x
, then so does
V(a_{1}F_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda^{\prime}}-a_{2}F_{\Lambda_{2},\Lambda^{\prime}})
. This justifies our reduction procedure. We want to emphasize that the smoothness of
H_{X}
is not necessary here. In particular, the reduction procedure works until the last step at which
H_{X}
is a (possibly reducible) curve. This is one of the main reasons why we are working with the polynomials
F_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}}
instead of the divisor
\operatorname{Ram}(\pi_{\Lambda^{\prime},X})+D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{\Lambda,X})
.

So far we have reduced the dimension of
X
to one. To have a space curve, we need to reduce to
e=2
. We use outer projections to achieve it.

Proposition 4.2
.

Notations
X,\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime},R,f_{\Lambda},F_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}}
as in Subsection
4.1
. Assume further that
e\geq 3
(so that
\dim\Lambda=e-2\geq 1
). For a point
T\in\Lambda\setminus X
, let
\pi_{T,X}\colon X\to\mathbf{P}^{r-1}
be the projection morphism. Let
X_{T}=\pi_{T,X}(X)
and let
\Lambda_{T},\,\Lambda^{\prime}_{T}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r-1}
be the projection images of
\Lambda,\,\Lambda^{\prime}
under
\pi_{T,\mathbf{P}^{r}}\colon\mathbf{P}^{r}\dashrightarrow\mathbf{P}^{r-1}
respectively. For the inclusion morphism
\pi_{T,\mathbf{P}^{r}}^{*}\colon\mathbf{C}[\mathbf{P}^{r-1}]\hookrightarrow\mathbf{C}[\mathbf{P}^{r}]
, the following holds:

\pi_{T,\mathbf{P}^{r}}^{*}(F_{\Lambda_{T},\Lambda_{T}^{\prime}})=F_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}}.

Proof.

Since
\pi_{\Lambda,\mathbf{P}^{r}}=\pi_{\Lambda_{T},\mathbf{P}^{r-1}}\circ\pi_{T,\mathbf{P}^{r}}
, we have

F_{\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}}=\pi_{\Lambda,\mathbf{P}^{r}}^{*}(\partial_{R}f_{\Lambda})=\pi_{T,\mathbf{P}^{r}}^{*}(\pi_{\Lambda_{T},\mathbf{P}^{r-1}}^{*}(\partial_{R}f_{\Lambda}))=\pi_{T,\mathbf{P}^{r}}^{*}F_{\Lambda_{T},\Lambda_{T}^{\prime}}.\qed

From Proposition
4.2
, it is immediate to see that if
T\in\Lambda\setminus(X\cup L)
and if
V(a_{1}F_{(\Lambda_{1})_{T},\Lambda^{\prime}_{T}}-a_{2}F_{(\Lambda_{2})_{T},\Lambda_{T}^{\prime}})\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r-1}
intersects
L
trasversally at
x
, then so does
V(a_{1}F_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda^{\prime}}-a_{2}F_{\Lambda_{2},\Lambda^{\prime}})
.

In summary, to prove Theorem
1.1

(2)
, it suffices to consider the following two cases:

(1)

X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{3}
is smooth curve and
x\in X
, or

(2)

X
is a union of
\mathbf{T}_{x}X
and a smooth curve
C^{\prime}\not\ni x
.

As we mentioned in Remark
3.4
, in the case
(1)
, it was known that
D_{\operatorname{out}}
is very ample, but it was not known that
\lvert V_{\operatorname{out}}\rvert
is very ample.

4.3.
Separation of tangents

From now on, we assume
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{3}
is either a smooth curve or a union of
\mathbf{T}_{x}X
and a smooth curve
C^{\prime}\not\ni x
. Let
\Lambda^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{3}\setminus X
be a linear space of dimension
e-1=1
(i.e. a line) and let
P=\Lambda_{1},\,Q=\Lambda_{2}\in\Lambda^{\prime}
be distinct points. We need some generosity assumptions on the points
P
and
Q
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
x
be a point of
X
. We call a point
P\in\mathbf{P}^{3}
a
birational center of
(X,x)
if it fulfills the following two conditions:

(1)

P\not\in\mathscr{B}(X)=\bigl\{Q\in\mathbf{P}^{3}\setminus X\,\mid\,\operatorname{length}(\langle P,Q\rangle\cap X)\geq 2\ \text{for general }P\in X\bigr\}
.

(2)

P\not\in\mathscr{C}_{x}=\operatorname{Cone}(x,X)=\overline{\bigcup_{y\in X\setminus\{x\}}\langle x,y\rangle}
.

In addition, a pair
P,Q
of birational centers of
(X,x)
is said to be
admissible
if it satisfies the following two conditions:

(3)

\langle P,Q\rangle\cap X=\varnothing
.

(4)

\langle P,Q\rangle\cap\mathbf{T}_{x}X=\varnothing
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
P
be a birational center of
(X,x)
. Then, the projection morphism
\pi_{P,X}\colon X\to\mathbf{P}^{2}
is finite and birational. Moreover,
x\not\in D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{P,X})
. If
P,Q
is an admissible pair of birational centers of
(X,x)
, then the morphism
\pi_{\langle P,Q\rangle,X}\colon X\to\mathbf{P}^{1}
is finite and
x\not\in\operatorname{Ram}(\pi_{\langle P,Q\rangle,X})
.

Proof.

The proposition immediately follows from the definition.
∎

It is important to point out that an admissible pair always exists in our situation.

Proposition 4.5
.

An admissible pair of birational centers of
(X,x)
exists.

Proof.

We need to consider two cases, that is, either
X
is smooth or
X=\mathbf{T}_{x}X\cup C^{\prime}
with
C^{\prime}
smooth and
x\not\in C^{\prime}
. Suppose
X
is smooth. The closure
\bar{\mathscr{B}}(X)
of
\mathscr{B}(X)
is a union of linear spaces of dimension at most
n-1=0
(see
[
19
, Theorem 1]
), and
\dim\mathscr{C}_{x}=2
. Hence,
P\not\in\mathscr{B}(x)
and
P\not\in\mathscr{C}_{x}
for general
P
. This shows that the general point in
\mathbf{P}^{3}
is a birational center of
(X,x)
. Moreover, both
X
and
\mathbf{T}_{x}X
are of codimension
2
in
\mathbf{P}^{3}
, a general line does not intersect neither
X
nor
\mathbf{T}_{x}X
. This shows that for general
P,Q
, we have
\langle P,Q\rangle\cap X=\langle P,Q\rangle\cap\mathbf{T}_{x}X=\varnothing
.

Now, assume
X=\mathbf{T}_{x}X\cup C^{\prime}
, and
Q\in\mathscr{B}(X)
. For general
P\in C^{\prime}
, the line
\langle P,Q\rangle
does not intersect
\mathbf{T}_{x}X
; otherwise,
C^{\prime}\subseteq\langle Q,\mathbf{T}_{x}X\rangle
, i.e.
X
is degenerate. Hence,
\operatorname{length}(\langle P,Q\rangle\cap C^{\prime})\geq 2
for general
P\in C^{\prime}
. This shows
\mathscr{B}(X)\subseteq\mathscr{B}(C^{\prime})
. We invoke
[
19
, Theorem 1]
to see that
\mathscr{B}(X)
is zero-dimensional. In particular, the condition (1) holds for general
P\in\mathbf{P}^{3}
. To verfiy (2) for general
P
, we observe that
\mathscr{C}_{x}=\operatorname{Cone}(x,X)=\operatorname{Cone}(x,\mathbf{T}_{x}X)\cup\operatorname{Cone}(x,C^{\prime})=\mathbf{T}_{x}X\cup\operatorname{Cone}(x,C^{\prime})
. Since
\dim sC_{x}=2
, a general
P
is not contained in
\mathscr{C}_{x}
. Conditions (3) and (4) follows immediately from the dimension counting.
∎

From now on, we assume that
P,Q\in\Lambda^{\prime}
are admissible pair of birational centers of
(X,x)
. Using notations in Subsection
4.1
, we have

\operatorname{Ram}(\pi_{\Lambda^{\prime},X})+D_{\operatorname{out}}(\pi_{P,X})=V(F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}})\cap X.

Let
a_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}},\,a_{Q,\Lambda^{\prime}}\in\mathbf{C}
be complex numbers such that

\frac{a_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}}{a_{Q,\Lambda^{\prime}}}=\frac{F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}}{F_{Q,\Lambda^{\prime}}}(x),

regarding
F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}/F_{Q,\Lambda^{\prime}}
as a rational function on
\mathbf{P}^{3}
. If we put

F_{P,Q}:=a_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}F_{Q,\Lambda^{\prime}}-a_{Q,\Lambda^{\prime}}F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}},

then
V(F_{P,Q})\cap X=\operatorname{Ram}(\pi_{\Lambda^{\prime},X})+D
, where
D\in\lvert V_{\operatorname{out}}\rvert
.

We need to investigate when the divisor
D
is tangent to
\mathbf{T}_{x}X
at
x
. Parametrize
\mathbf{T}_{x}X
by
\{x+tv\}_{t\in\mathbf{P}^{1}}
, where
v\in\mathbf{T}_{x}X\setminus\{x\}
. Then,

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{$D$ is tangent to $\mathbf{T}_{x}X$ at $x$}\ \ &\Longleftrightarrow\ \ (v\cdot\nabla)F_{P,Q}(x)=0\\
&\Longleftrightarrow\ \ v\cdot\bigl(a_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}\nabla F_{Q,\Lambda^{\prime}}-a_{Q,\Lambda^{\prime}}\nabla F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}\bigr)(x)=0.\end{split}

In above, the products are the standard inner product of vectors. The last condition in (
4.1
) can be reinterpreted as follows. Let
H_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{3}
be the plane defined by the equation

\sum_{i=0}^{3}Z_{i}\frac{\partial F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}}{\partial Z_{i}}(x)=0.

The plane
H_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}
intersects
\mathbf{T}_{x}X
at
x+tv
(
t\in\mathbf{P}^{1}
) if and only if

\sum_{i=0}^{3}(x_{i}+tv_{i})\frac{\partial F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}}{\partial Z_{i}}(x)=(\deg F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}})F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}(x)+t(v\cdot\nabla)F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}(x)=0.

Note that
F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}(x)\neq 0
, so the above equation must have a unique solution
t=t_{0}
. Since
\deg F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}=\deg X-1=\deg F_{Q,\Lambda^{\prime}}
, the last condition in (
4.1
) is equivalent to
H_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}\cap\mathbf{T}_{x}X\neq H_{Q,\Lambda^{\prime}}\cap\mathbf{T}_{x}X
. Consequently, we have

(4.2)

\text{$D$ is not tangent to $\mathbf{T}_{x}X$ at $x$}\ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ H_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}\cap\mathbf{T}_{x}X\neq H_{Q,\Lambda^{\prime}}\cap\mathbf{T}_{x}X.

4.4.
Proof of the right hand side of (
4.2
)

Fix coordinates in
\mathbf{P}^{3}
such that
P=[1,0,0,0]\in\mathbf{P}^{3}
and that
\mathbf{T}_{x}X
contains a point
T=[1,1,0,0]\neq x
. If
Q=[1,q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}]
, then

F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}=\pi_{P,\mathbf{P}^{3}}^{*}\bigl(\partial_{Q_{P}}f_{P}\bigr)=q_{1}\frac{\partial f_{P}}{\partial Z_{1}}+q_{2}\frac{\partial f_{P}}{\partial Z_{2}}+q_{3}\frac{\partial f_{P}}{\partial Z_{3}}.

Here, the pullback
\pi_{P,\mathbf{P}^{3}}^{*}
is suppressed in the notation since
\pi_{P,\mathbf{P}^{3}}^{*}
is nothing but the natural inclusion
\mathbf{C}[Z_{1},Z_{2},Z_{3}]\hookrightarrow\mathbf{C}[Z_{0},Z_{1},Z_{2},Z_{3}]
.

The plane
H_{P,L}
passes through
T
if and only if

(4.3)

(T\cdot\nabla)F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}(x)=\frac{\partial F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}}{\partial Z_{1}}(x)=0,

or equivalently,

(4.4)

q_{1}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{P}}{\partial Z_{1}^{2}}(x)+q_{2}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{P}}{\partial Z_{1}\partial Z_{2}}(x)+q_{3}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{P}}{\partial Z_{1}\partial Z_{3}}(x).

The condition (
4.4
) is a single linear condition on
Q=[1,q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}]
unless
\dfrac{\partial^{2}f_{P}}{\partial Z_{1}\partial Z_{i}}(x)=0
for all
i=1,2,3
.

From now on, we denote by
\mathcal{L}_{P,T}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{3}
the set of points
[q_{0},q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}]
defined by the equation (
4.3
). In general,
\mathcal{L}_{P,T}
is a plane and parametrizes the points
Q
satisfying
T\in H_{P,\langle P,Q\rangle}
. In Proposition
4.8
and Proposition
4.9
, we will provide a geometric illustration of the condition
\mathcal{L}_{P,T}=\mathbf{P}^{3}
. Also, (
4.4
) suggests to define
\mathcal{L}_{P,x}=\langle P,\mathbf{T}_{x}X\rangle
.

Assume there exist admissible birational centers
P,Q
such that

(4.5)

Q\in\mathcal{L}_{P,T},\ \text{but\ }P\not\in\mathcal{L}_{Q,T}.

Then, the right hand side of (
4.2
) follows immediately. From now on, we assume

(4.6)

\text{if $P,Q$ are admissible birational centers, then}\ \ Q\in\mathcal{L}_{P,T}\Longleftrightarrow P\in\mathcal{L}_{Q,T},

and finally we will derive a contradiction and hence verify the right hand side of (
4.2
).

Before getting into the technical proof, we give some basic observation about
\mathcal{L}_{P,T}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

The condition (
4.4
) is nontrivial for general
T\in\mathbf{T}_{x}X\setminus\{x\}
. Moreover, the condition is trivial at most one point of
\mathbf{T}_{x}X\setminus\{x\}
, and in such a case all the other
\mathcal{L}_{P,T}
are
\langle P,\mathbf{T}_{x}X\rangle
.

Proof.

Assume that (
4.4
) is trivial at the point
T
, and pick another
T^{\prime}=T+tx
where
t\neq 0
. Then, (
4.4
) with respect to
T^{\prime}
is written as

\partial_{T^{\prime}}F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}(x)=(\partial_{T}+t\partial_{x})F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}(x)=0

Here,
\partial_{T}F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}(x)=\dfrac{\partial F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}}{\partial Z_{1}}(x)
is the left hand side of (
4.4
), and

\partial_{x}F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}(x)=(\deg F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}})F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}(x)=(\deg F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}})\biggl(q_{1}\frac{\partial f_{P}}{\partial Z_{1}}(x)+q_{2}\frac{\partial f_{P}}{\partial Z_{2}}(x)+q_{3}\frac{\partial f_{P}}{\partial Z_{3}}(x)\biggr).

This is nontrivial since the curve
\overline{X}_{P}=\pi_{P,X}(X)
is nonsingular at
\bar{x}_{P}=\pi_{P,X}(x)
. It follows that the condition
\partial_{T^{\prime}}F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}(x)=0
differs from
\partial_{T}F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}(x)=0
, hence cannot be trivial. The last statement follows from the fact that
\partial_{x}F_{P,\Lambda^{\prime}}(x)=0
defines
\pi_{P,\mathbf{P}^{3}}^{*}(\mathbf{T}_{x_{P}}X_{P})=\langle P,\mathbf{T}_{x}X\rangle
.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

x\in\mathcal{L}_{P,T}
.

Proof.

Let
x=[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
. It suffices to prove that
(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3})=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
is a solution to (
4.4
). Indeed,

\displaystyle x_{1}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{P}}{\partial Z_{1}^{2}}(x)+x_{2}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{P}}{\partial Z_{1}\partial Z_{2}}(x)+x_{3}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{P}}{\partial Z_{1}\partial Z_{3}}(x)

\displaystyle{}=((x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\cdot\nabla)\frac{\partial f_{P}}{\partial Z_{1}}(x)

\displaystyle{}=(\deg f_{P}-1)\cdot\frac{\partial f_{P}}{\partial Z_{1}}(x),

and the latter term is zero since
\dfrac{\partial}{\partial Z_{1}}
is a tangent vector of
\overline{X}
at
\bar{x}
.
∎

The following proposition interprets Lemma
4.6
geometrically.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
P\in\mathbf{P}^{3}
be a birational center, and let
\overline{X}_{P}=\pi_{P,X}(X)
and
\bar{x}_{P}=\pi_{P,X}(x)
. Then, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\bar{x}_{P}
is an inflection point of
\overline{X}_{P}
;

(2)

there exists a point
T_{P}\in\mathbf{T}_{x}X
such that
\mathcal{L}_{P,T_{P}}=\mathbf{P}^{3}
.

Moreover, if (2) holds, then such
T_{P}
is unique.

Proof.

Let
f_{P}\in\mathbf{C}[Z_{0},Z_{1},Z_{2}]
be the equation of
\overline{X}_{P}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{2}
, and let
\mathbf{H}:=\operatorname{Hess}(f_{P})_{\bar{x}_{P}}
be the Hessian matrix of
\bar{f}_{P}
evaluated at
\bar{x}_{P}
. We assume that
\bar{x}_{P}\in\overline{X}_{P}
is an inflection point, and show that
\operatorname{rank}\mathbf{H}=2
. Taking suitable coordinates, we assume
\bar{x}_{P}=[1,0,0]
and
\mathbf{T}_{\bar{x}_{P}}\overline{X}_{P}=(Z_{2}=0)
. Let
\gamma\colon\Delta\to\overline{X}_{P}
,
t\mapsto[\gamma_{0}(t),\gamma_{1}(t),\gamma_{2}(t)]
be a local parametrization of
\overline{X}_{P}
at
\gamma(0)=\bar{x}_{P}
. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{P}}{\partial Z_{i}}(\bar{x}_{P})=0\Longleftrightarrow i\neq 2

since
\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{\bar{x}_{P}}\overline{X}_{P}=\Bigl(\sum_{i=0,1,2}Z_{i}\frac{\partial f_{P}}{\partial Z_{i}}(\bar{x}_{P})=0\Bigr)=(Z_{2}=0)
. By Euler’s theorem,

\sum_{j=0,1,2}\gamma_{j}(0)\frac{\partial^{2}f_{P}}{\partial Z_{j}\partial Z_{i}}(\bar{x}_{P})=(\deg f_{P}-1)\frac{\partial f_{P}}{\partial Z_{i}}(\bar{x}_{P}),\quad\forall i\in\{0,1,2\},

and the left hand side of the above equation reads
\displaystyle\gamma_{0}(0)\frac{\partial^{2}f_{P}}{\partial Z_{j}\partial Z_{i}}(\bar{x}_{P})
. It follows that

\frac{\partial^{2}f_{P}}{\partial Z_{j}\partial Z_{i}}(\bar{x}_{P})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&i=0,1\\
\neq 0&i=2.\end{array}\right.

Since the matrix
\mathbf{H}
is singular,
\mathbf{H}
is of the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&\neq 0\\
0&0&?\\
\neq 0&?&?\end{array}\right),

showing that
\operatorname{rank}\mathbf{H}=2
. Let
\bar{T}\in\mathbf{T}_{\bar{x}_{P}}\overline{X}_{P}\setminus\{\bar{x}_{P}\}
be a point, and let
T\in\mathbf{T}_{x}X
be the unique point which maps to
\bar{T}
along
\pi_{P,X}
. Then,
\mathcal{L}_{P,T}
is the pullback of the line defined by the matrix equation

\{[Z_{0},Z_{1},Z_{2}]\in\mathbf{P}^{3}\mid(\ Z_{0}\ Z_{1}\ Z_{2}\ )\,\mathbf{H}\,(\ \bar{T}_{0}\ \bar{T}_{1}\ \bar{T}_{2}\ )^{\sf T}=0\},\quad\text{(cf. Eq.~\eqref{eq: H thru T})}

where
\bar{T}=[\bar{T}_{0},\bar{T}_{1},\bar{T}_{2}]
and
{({}\mathrel{\cdot}{})}^{\sf T}
denotes the matrix transpose. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{P,T}=\mathbf{P}^{3}\

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\ \mathbf{H}(\ \bar{T}_{0}\ \bar{T}_{1}\ \bar{T}_{2}\ )^{\sf T}=0

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\ (\ \bar{T}_{0}\ \bar{T}_{1}\ \bar{T}_{2}\ )^{\sf T}\in\ker\mathbf{H},

and there is a unique
T=T_{P}
satisfying the above equivalent conditions since
\dim\ker\mathbf{H}=1
. This shows
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
.
Conversely, if (2) is true, then
T_{P}
induces the nontrivial vector of
\ker\mathbf{H}
, hence (1) follows. Such
T_{P}
is unique since
\dim\ker\mathbf{H}=1
.
∎

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
\Xi=\mathbf{P}^{2}
be a projective plane with a homogeneous coordinates
[\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\xi_{2}]
, and let
\mathbf{T}\subseteq\Xi
be the line
(\xi_{2}=0)

\mathcal{R}:=\{R_{1},\dots,R_{r}\}\subseteq\Xi
be a finite set of points satisfying the following properties.

(1)

\mathcal{R}\not\subseteq\mathbf{T}
;

(2)

\mathcal{R}\cap(\xi_{1}=0)=\varnothing
.

Let
P\in\Xi
be a general point and for each

, let
P_{i}=[\alpha_{i}(P),1,0]
be the intersection of the line
\langle P,R_{i}\rangle

\mathbf{T}
. Then,
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}(P)
varies over
P
.

Proof.

Assume the contrary that
P\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}(P)
is constant on an open subset of
\Xi
. Let us write
R_{i}=[\zeta_{i},1,\eta_{i}]
and

for
\eta\not\in\{\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{r}\}
. The line
\langle P,R_{i}\rangle
intersects

\left[\zeta_{i}\eta-\eta_{i}\zeta,\ \eta-\eta_{i},\ 0\right],

hence
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}(P)=\frac{\zeta_{i}\eta-\eta_{i}\zeta}{\eta-\eta_{i}}
. Consider the index set
\mathcal{I}=\{i:\eta_{i}\neq 0\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,r\}

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\alpha_{i}(P)=\sum_{i\not\in\mathcal{I}}\zeta_{i}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\zeta_{i}\eta-\eta_{i}\zeta}{\eta-\eta_{i}}.

Let

be the (homogeneous) polynomial

\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(\eta-\eta_{i})\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\zeta_{i}\eta-\eta_{i}\zeta}{\eta-\eta_{i}}

for general

, but

5.

\lvert D_{\operatorname{inn}}\rvert

In this section, we investigate when

is base point free, and prove Theorem

5.1.
The curve case

If

is not a Noma’s exceptional variety and

is not base point free for a general curve section

is not base point free. We first consider the curve case in order to find examples where

is not base point free.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{e+1}
be a non-degenerate smooth projective curve of genus
g
, codimensnion
e\geq 2
, and degree
d
, and
H
be its hyperplane section. Then

-K_{C}+(d-e-2)H
is not base point free if and only if
C\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{e+1}
is a rational normal curve or
C\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{3}
is linearly normal with
g\geq 2
and
C
is a birational divisor of type
(m,\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}(1))
for
m\geq 2
on a conical scroll
\overline{F}
over
\mathbf{P}^{1}
which is obtained by the birational embedding of
F:=\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}(2))
by
\lvert\mathscr{O}_{F}(1)\rvert
.

Proof.

We put
D:=-K_{C}+(d-e-2)H
. As the assertion is easy to check when
g=0
or
1
, we assume that
g\geq 2
and
d\geq e+3
. Suppose that
d=e+3
. By Riemann–Roch theorem and Clifford theorem,
g=2
and
C\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{e+1}
is linearly normal. If
e\geq 3
, then
\deg D=e+1\geq 4=2g
so that
D
is base point free. If
e=2
, then
\deg D=3
. Thus
D
is not base point free if and only if
D=K_{C}+x
(equivalently,
H=2K+x
) for
x\in C
.
In this case,
C
is a birational divisor of type
(2,\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}(1))
on a conical scroll
\overline{F}
over
\mathbf{P}^{1}
which is obtained by the birational embedding of
F:=\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}(2))
by
\lvert\mathscr{O}_{F}(1)\rvert
.

Suppose that
d\geq e+4
. To show that
D
is base point free, it suffices to check that

-2g+2+(d-e-2)d=\deg D\geq 2g,

which can be restated as

(5.1)

\frac{(d-e-2)d+2}{2}\geq 2g

By Castelnuovo’s genus bound (
2.1
), we have

\frac{(d-e-2)d+(\varepsilon+1)(e+1-\varepsilon)}{e}\geq 2g,

where
m:=\left\lfloor(d-1)/e\right\rfloor
and
\varepsilon:=(d-1)-me
with
m\geq 1
and
0\leq\varepsilon\leq e-1
. Thus (
5.1
) can be deduced from

\frac{(d-e-2)d+2}{2}\geq\frac{(d-e-2)d+(\varepsilon+1)(e+1-\varepsilon)}{e}

which is equivalent to

(5.2)

(e-2)(d-e-2)d+2e\geq 2(\varepsilon+1)(e+1-\varepsilon).

First, consider the case of
e\geq 3
.
As
d\geq e+4
, we obtain

(e-2)(d-e-2)d+2e\geq\displaystyle 2(e-2)(e+4)+2e\geq 2\left(\frac{e+2}{2}\right)^{2}\geq 2(\varepsilon+1)(e+1-\varepsilon).

Next, consider the case of
e=2
(in this case, (
5.2
) does not hold). Keep in mind that
d\geq 6
. Castelnuovo’s genus bound (
2.1
) says

\frac{(d-4)d+(\varepsilon+1)(3-\varepsilon)}{2}\geq 2g.

If the strict inequality holds, then (
5.1
) holds so that
D
is base point free. Suppose that the equality holds. If
d=2m
is even, then
C\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{3}
is a complete intersection of type
(2,m)
(see e.g.,
[
1
, page 119]
). In this case,
D=K_{C}
is very ample since
d\geq 6
. Suppose that
d=2m+1
is odd (
\varepsilon=0
). As
((d-4)d+3)/2\geq 2g
, we have
\deg D\geq 2g-1
. Thus
D
is not base point free if and only if
D=K_{C}+x
(equivalently,
2(K_{C}-(m-2)H)=H-x
) for
x\in C
. In this case, the equality holds in Castelnuovo’s genus bound, so
C\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{3}
is projectively normal and
\lvert K_{C}-(m-2)H\rvert
gives a branch covering

of degree
m
(
[
1
, Corollary 2.6]
). Since
H-x=2(K_{C}-(m-2)H)
and

, it follows that

gives a morphism

This means that

and
C
is a birational divisor of type
(m,\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}(1))
for
m\geq 3
on a conical scroll
\overline{F}
over
\mathbf{P}^{1}
which is obtained by the birational embedding of
F:=\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}(2))
by
\lvert\mathscr{O}_{F}(1)\rvert
. It is easy to check that in this case

is not base point free.

. Notice that
\dim f^{-1}(f(z))\geq n-\kappa(D)\geq b+1
. Thus we can take a point
z^{\prime}\in f^{-1}(f(z))\setminus\operatorname{Bs}\lvert D\rvert
. Now, there is an effective divisor
D^{\prime}\in|D|
such that
z^{\prime}\not\in\operatorname{Supp}(D^{\prime})
. On the other hand,
z\in\operatorname{Supp}(D^{\prime})
. Note that
mD^{\prime}=f^{*}A
for some nonzero effective divisor
A
on
W
. We have
f(z)\in\operatorname{Supp}(A)
. Then

w^{\prime}\in f^{-1}(f(z))\subseteq f^{-1}(\operatorname{Supp}(A))=\operatorname{Supp}(mD^{\prime})=\operatorname{Supp}(D^{\prime}),

which gives a contradiction. Hence
\kappa(D)\geq n-b
.
∎

Proposition 6.2
.

If
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is not big, then
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is base point free.

Proof.

Suppose that
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is not base point free. In particular,
D_{\operatorname{inn}}\neq 0
. We know that
\operatorname{Bs}\lvert D_{\operatorname{inn}}\rvert\subseteq\mathscr{C}(X)
and
\dim\mathscr{C}(X)\leq 0
, and thus,
\dim\operatorname{Bs}\lvert D_{\operatorname{inn}}\rvert=0
. By Lemma
6.1
,
\kappa(D_{\operatorname{inn}})\geq n
, i.e.,
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is big.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

Proposition
6.2
also follows from Noma’s recent result
[
22
, Theorem 1.2 (2)]
asserting that if
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is not a Roth variety and
\mathscr{C}(X)\neq\varnothing
, then
D_{\operatorname{inn}}^{n}\geq\lvert\mathscr{C}(X)\rvert
.
Indeed, if
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is not big, then
D_{\operatorname{inn}}^{n}=0
and thus
\mathscr{C}(X)=\varnothing
. Since
\operatorname{Bs}\lvert D_{\operatorname{inn}}\rvert\subseteq\mathscr{C}(X)
, it follows that
\lvert D_{\operatorname{inn}}\rvert
is base point free.

As for adjunction mappings, it would be an interesting problem to explore the structure of the morphism
\varphi\colon X\to Y
given by
\lvert D_{\operatorname{inn}}\rvert
when
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is not big. Here we exhibit three examples where
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is not big. We are not aware any other examples.

Example 6.4
.

(1)
By
[
15
, Lemma 2.5]
,
D_{\operatorname{inn}}=0
if and only if
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a
del Pezzo variety
, by which we mean a linearly normal smooth projective variety with
d=e+2
. It is well-known that if
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is a del Pezzo variety, then
-K_{X}=(n-1)H
.

(2)
Let
S:=\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}\oplus\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}(1)^{\oplus n})
for
n\geq 3
with the tautological divisor
H_{S}
and a fiber
F_{S}
. Take a general smooth member
X\in|2H_{S}|
, and put
H:=H_{S}|_{X},F:=F_{S}|_{X}
. Note that
\lvert H\rvert
gives an embedding of
X
into
\mathbf{P}^{2n}
. Thus
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{2n}
is a non-degenerate smooth projective variety of dimension
n
, codimension
n
, and degree
d=H_{S}^{n}.2H_{S}=2n
. Clearly,
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{2n}
is not a Noma’s exceptional variety.
Note that

K_{S}=-(n+1)H_{S}+(n-2)F_{S}~~\text{ and }~~K_{X}=(K_{S}+2H_{S})|_{X}=-(n-1)H+(n-2)F.

Thus

D_{\operatorname{inn}}=-K_{X}-H=(n-2)(H-F).

Since

D_{\operatorname{inn}}^{n}=(n-2)^{n}(H_{S}-F_{S})^{n}.2H_{S}=2(n-2)^{n}(H_{S}^{n+1}-nH_{S}^{n}.F_{S})=0,

it follows that
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is not big. The morphism induced by
\lvert D_{\operatorname{inn}}\rvert
is a conic fibration over
\mathbf{P}^{n-1}
.

(3)
Let
S:=\mathbf{P}(\mathscr{O}_{\mathbf{P}^{1}}(1)^{\oplus n+1})
for
n\geq 4
with the tautological divisor
H_{S}
and a fiber
F_{S}
. Take a general smooth member
X\in|2H_{S}-2F_{S}|
, and put
H:=H_{S}|_{X}
and
F:=F_{S}|_{X}
. Note that
|H|
gives an embedding of
X
into
\mathbf{P}^{2n+1}
. Thus
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{2n+1}
is a non-degenerate smooth projective variety of dimension
n
, codimension
n+1
, and degree
d=H_{S}^{n}(2H_{S}-2F_{S})=2n
. Clearly,
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{2n+1}
is not a Noma’s exceptional variety.
Note that

K_{S}=-(n+1)H_{S}+(n-1)F_{S}~~\text{ and }~~K_{X}=(K_{S}+2H_{S}-2F_{S})|_{X}=(-n+1)H+(n-3)F.

Thus

D_{\operatorname{inn}}=-K_{X}-2H=(n-3)(H-F).

Since

D_{\operatorname{inn}}^{n}=(n-3)^{n}(H_{S}-F_{S})^{n}(2H_{S}-2F_{S})=2(n-3)^{n}(H_{S}^{n+1}-(n+1)H_{S}^{n}F_{S})=0,

it follows that
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is not big. The morphism induced by
\lvert D_{\operatorname{inn}}\rvert
is a linear fibration over
Q^{n-1}
. In fact,
X=\mathbf{P}^{1}\times Q^{n-1}
.

6.2.
Overall framework

In the remaining, we show that
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is big in most cases. We assume that
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is neither a Noma’s exceptional variety nor a del Pezzo variety (cf. Example
6.4

(1)
). In particular,
d\geq e+3
. Recall that if
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is not linearly normal, then
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is ample. As our aim is to show
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is big under some assumptions, we may henceforth assume that
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is linearly normal. Now, we introduce a technique developed in
[
14
,
15
]
for proving
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is big. Let

g:=\frac{1}{2}(K_{X}.H^{n-1}+(n-1)H^{n})+1

be the sectional genus of
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
.

Lemma 6.5
.

If

(6.1)

\frac{(d-e-2)d+(\varepsilon+1)(e+1-\varepsilon)}{e}<\frac{(d-e-2)d+2n}{n},

then
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is big.

Proof.

As
d\geq e+3
, the divisor
D:=(d-e-2)H
is very ample. By
[
10
, Theorem 1]
,
E:=K_{X}+(n-1)H
is base point free under our assumption. By Siu’s bigness criterion (cf.
[
16
, Theorem 2.2.15]
), if

(6.2)

nD^{n-1}.E<D^{n},

then
D-E=D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is big . As
2g=K_{X}.H^{n-1}+(n-1)H^{n}
, we may rewrite (
6.2
) as

(6.3)

2g<\frac{(d-e-2)d+2n}{n}.

Applying Castelnuovo’s genus bound (
2.1
), we see that (
6.1
) implies (
6.3
).
∎

Lemma 6.6
.

If
d\leq r
, then
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is big except when either
n\geq 3
,
d=2n
,
e=n
and
\lvert D_{\operatorname{inn}}\rvert
induces a conic fibration
X\to\mathbf{P}^{n-1}
(cf. Example
6.4
), or
n\geq 4
,
d=2n
,
e=n+1
and
\lvert D_{\operatorname{inn}}\rvert
induces a linear fibration
X\to Q^{n-1}
(cf. Example
6.4
).

Proof.

By Ionescu’s classification
[
12
]
of smooth projective varieties
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
of degree
d\leq r
, one can easily check the assertion.
∎

Proposition 6.7
.

Suppose that one of the following hold:

(1)

e\geq n+1
and either
\varepsilon\geq n-1
or
\varepsilon\leq e-n+1
, or

(2)

e=n
and
\varepsilon=0
.

Then
(
6.1
)
holds, i.e.,
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is big, unless
n\geq 4,d=2n,e=n+1
and
\lvert D_{\operatorname{inn}}\rvert
induces a linear fibration
X\to Q^{n-1}
(cf. Example
6.4
).

Proof.

We may rewrite (
6.1
) in Lemma
6.5
as

(6.4)

n(\varepsilon-1)(e-\varepsilon-1)<(e-n)(d-e-2)d.

If the condition
(2)
holds, then (
6.4
) holds. Now, suppose that the condition
(1)
holds.
First, assume that
\varepsilon\geq n-1
. Then
e-\varepsilon-1\leq e-n
. In this case,
\epsilon-1\leq d-e-2
and
n\leq e<d
so that (
6.4
) holds.
Next, assume that
\varepsilon\leq e-n+1
. Then
\varepsilon-1\leq e-n
. If
m\geq 2
, then
e-\varepsilon-1\leq d-e-2
and
n\leq e<d
so that (
6.4
) holds. Suppose that
m=1
. Then
d=e+\varepsilon+1
. We may assume that
\varepsilon\leq n-2
(if not, (
6.4
) holds). Then
d\leq r-1
, so the assertion follows from Lemma
6.6
.
∎

6.3.
Proofs of Theorems
1.3
and
1.5
and Corollary
1.4

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

By Lemma
6.6
, we may assume that
d\geq r+1
. We need to show that if

(6.5)

d\geq\left\lceil\frac{2n-e-2}{2e-2n}\right\rceil e+1,

then
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is big. We rewrite (
6.1
) in Lemma
6.5
as

(6.6)

q(\varepsilon):=\varepsilon^{2}+\big(2m(e-n)-e\big)\varepsilon+m(m-1)(e-n)e+2n-e-1>0.

The assumption (
6.5
) means that

m\geq\frac{2n-e-2}{2e-2n},

which is equivalent to

(6.7)

e-n+1\geq-\frac{1}{2}\big(2m(e-n)-e\big).

Proposition
6.7
says (
6.1
) holds when
\varepsilon=e-n+1
. As (

) is equivalent to (
6.6
), we have

(6.8)

q(e-n+1)>0.

Notice that the quadratic function
q(\varepsilon)
in

is increasing when

\varepsilon\geq-1/2\big(2m(e-n)-e\big)
.
Then (
6.7
) and (
6.8
) imply that

for
\varepsilon\geq e-n+1
. On the other hand, by Proposition
6.7
, we have
q(\varepsilon)>0
for

. Hence (
6.6
) holds for all
\varepsilon
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

(1)
Assume
n=2
. By Proposition
6.7
, we only have to consider the case of
e=2
and
\varepsilon=1
.
By Castelnuovo’s genus bound (
2.1
), we have

2g\leq\frac{1}{2}d^{2}-2d+2.

If the inequality is strict, then (
6.3
) holds so that
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is big. Assume that the equality holds, i.e.,
2g=(1/2)d^{2}-2d+2
. Then a general curve section
C\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{3}
of

is projectively normal and contained in a quadric surface. Since

is even, it follows that

is a complete intersection (see e.g.,
[
1
, page 119]
). Thus
S\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is also a complete intersection. In this case, since
S\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
is not a linearly normal del Pezzo surface, it follows that
m\geq 2
, i.e.,
d\geq 6
. Then
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is very ample.

(2)
Assume
n=3
. If
e\geq 4
or
e=3
and
\varepsilon=0
, then
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is big by Proposition
6.7
. Suppose
e=3
and
\varepsilon=1
or
2
. Then (
6.4
) is the equality, so (
6.1
) is the equality. If (
2.1
) is the strict inequality, then (
6.2
) holds so that
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is big by Siu’s bigness criterion. Thus we assume that (
2.1
) is also the equality, i.e.,
6g=d^{2}-5d+6=(d-2)(d-3)
. If
d\leq 8
, then
(g,d)=(2,6)
or
(5,8)
. By Ionescu’s classification
[
10
,
11
]
of smooth projective varieties
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
of degree
d\leq 8
, we can easily check the assertion for
d\leq 8
. Assume that
d\geq 9
. Since a general curve section
C\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{4}
of

is a Castelnuovo curve, it follows that
X
is a smooth divisor on a rational normal scroll (see
[
1

). As

(actually, we may assume
\mathscr{C}(X)=\varnothing
by Proposition

, but we don’t need this),
[

shows that
X\in|aH_{S}|
for some integer

, where

with the tautological divisor

. Here

and

, where

is the restriction of a fiber of

over
\mathbf{P}^{1}
to

. Then

so

Hence

is big.

Finally, consider the case of
e=2
. Notice that
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{5}
is linearly normal and

. If
X
is contained in a quadric hyperusrface in
\mathbf{P}^{5}
, then

is a complete intersection or a Roth variety by

and

. In the former case,

is very ample. We assume that
X
is not contained in a quadric hypersurface in
\mathbf{P}^{5}
. If

is a general curve section of

is not contained in a quadric hypersurface in
\mathbf{P}^{3}

(6.9)

where

is the genus of

. Note that

is base point free (see
[

is contained in a quadric hypersurface in

Corollary 6.8
.

If
e\geq(4n-2)/3
, then
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is big except when
n=4,5
,
e=n+1
,
d=2n
and
\lvert D_{\operatorname{inn}}\rvert
induces a linear fibration
X\to Q^{n-1}
(cf. Example
6.4
).

Proof.

When
n\leq 3
, the assertions follow from Theorem
1.5
. We henceforth assume that
n\geq 4
. Note that
e\geq(4n-2)/3
implies
e\geq n+1
when
n\geq 4
and
e=n+1
if and only if
n=4,5
. Furthermore,
e\geq(4n-2)/3
is equivalent to
(2n-e-2)/(2e-2n)\leq 1
. Then the corollary is a consequence of Theorem
1.3
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.4
.

If
e\leq n-2
, then Barth–Larsen theorem implies that
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is very ample. Thus we assume that
e\geq n-1
. By Lemma
6.6
, the assertion holds when
d\leq r
. Thus we also assume that
d\geq r+1
. Now, since
D_{\operatorname{inn}}
is semiample, it follows that
\kappa(D_{\operatorname{inn}})=\nu(D_{\operatorname{inn}})
, where
\nu(D_{\operatorname{inn}})
is the numerical Iitaka dimenson. It suffices to show that
D_{\operatorname{inn}}^{n-k}\not\equiv 0
for
n-k\leq(3n-1)/4
. If
Y\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r-k}
is a general
(n-k)
-dimensional section of
X\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{r}
, then Corollary
6.8
implies that

is big as soon as
n-1\geq(4(n-k)-2)/3
, which is equivalent to
(3n-1)/4\geq n-k
. Thus we can conclude that
D_{\operatorname{inn}}^{n-k}\not\equiv 0
for
(3n-1)/4\geq n-k
.
∎
