Genome-wide characterization of Solanum tuberosum UGT gene family and functional analysis of StUGT178 in salt tolerance
Authors: Yu Ma, Jiafeng Song, Suao Sheng, Daijuan Wang, Tongtong Wang, Nan Wang, Airu Chen, Lixia Wang, Yaxuan Peng, Yuhan Ma, Zhaoyan Lv, Xiaobiao Zhu, Hualan Hou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractUDP-glycosyltransferases (UGTs) widely exist in plants and play essential roles in catalyzing the glycosylation reaction associated with metabolic processes. UGT gene family has been identified in many species to date. However, the comprehensive identification and systematic analysis have not been documented yet in the latest potato genome. In this study, a total of 295 UGT members (StUGT) were identified and found to be unevenly distributed on twelve chromosomes of potato. All StUGT genes were classified into 17 groups (A-P, R) and the UGT genes within the same groups have similar structural characterization. Tandem duplication was the major driving force for the StUGT gene expansion. The prediction of cis-acting elements showed that the development process, light, phytohormone, and abiotic stress-responsive elements generally existed in StUGT promoter regions. Analysis of spatial and temporal expression patterns demonstrated that StUGT genes were widely and differentially expressed in various tissues. Additionally, to investigate the salt stress-responsive genes, we analyzed the expression profiles of the StUGT genes under salt treatment. A total of 50 and 20 StUGT genes were continuously up- and down-regulated, respectively, implicating that these genes were involved in the regulation of salt tolerance. Among them, the StUGT178 gene, which was significantly induced by salt stress and contains salt-responsive element, was considered as one of the most relevant candidate genes. Transient transformation of the StUGT178 promoter in tobacco revealed that the transcriptional activation activity of the StUGT178 gene was strengthened under salt treatment. Furthermore, the heterologous expressions of the promoter and coding protein of the StUGT178 gene in Arabidopsis further demonstrated that the StUGT178 gene significantly responds to salt treatment, and enhanced salinity tolerance by regulating antioxidant enzyme activity and H2O2 accumulation. These results provide comprehensive information for a better understanding of the StUGT genes and offer a foundation for uncovering their function associated with salt stress in potato.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-11140-1.Keywords: Potato, UGT gene family, Expression profile, Salt stress, StUGT178

Introduction

The glycosyltransferase family (GTs, EC 2.4.x.y) is widely discovered in archaea, bacteria, viruses, and eukaryotes, and is responsible for catalyzing the transfer of sugar moieties to a broad array of acceptor molecules, including nucleic acids, proteins, lipids, sugars, antibiotics and other small molecules [1]. The glycosyltransferases were classified into 116 families (GT1-GT116) by the latest carbohydrate-active enzyme database (CAZy, http://www.cazy.org/GlycosylTransferases.html, accessed on 5 March 2023) according to the existence of conserved motifs, catalytic activity, substrate specificity, and the similarities of protein sequences. Different GT families were highly species-specific to some extent, for example, genes in the GT11, GT14, and GT92 families are specific to C. elegans, while those in GT2, GT8, and GT47 are plant-specific, and those in GT29 are specific to human [2]. Therefore, different GT families may take unique roles in the metabolism of distinct organisms, for example, the enzymes belonging to GT2, GT8, and GT47 play important roles in plant cell wall biosynthesis [3–5].

The uridine diphosphate (UDP)-glycosyltransferases (UGTs) belong to the biggest GT family in the plant kingdom. UGTs play critical roles in regulating plant hormones and in the biosynthesis of natural products, including steroids, terpenoids, phenylpropanoids, and flavonoids [6]. In plants, UGTs contain a relatively conserved protein sequence, consisting of 44 amino acid residues, which was designated as plant secondary product glycosyltransferase (PSPG) box ([F/W]-[2X]-Q-[2X]-[L/I/V/M/Y/A]-[L/I/M/V]-[4-6X]-[L/V/G/A/C]-[L/V/F/Y/A]-[L/I/V/M/F]-[S/T/A/G/C/M]-[H/N/Q]-[S/T/A/G/C]-G-[2X]-[S/T/A/G]-[3X]-[S/T/A/G/L]-[L/I/V/M/F/A]-[4X]-[P/Q/R]-[L/I/V/M/T]-[3X]-[P/A]-[3X]-[D/E/S]-[Q/E/H/N]) and participated in the binding of the enzyme with acceptor substrates, at the C-terminal end [7, 8]. Along with the rapid development in plant genomics and bioinformatics, UGT family genes have been widely identified and analyzed in a variety of plants [2, 9, 10]. Generally, > 100 UGT members were identified in each plant species [10]. For example, 120 UGTs were found in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana [11], 165 in Medicago trunculata [12], 147 in Zea mays [13], 179 in Triticum aestivum [14], 212 in Glycine max [15], 155 in Broussonetia papyrifera [16], 409 in Alfalfa [17], and 152 in Morella rubra [18]. However, a comprehensive investigation of UGT family genes in the latest potato genome has not been reported to date.

The UGTs are extensively involved in plant growth and respond to biotic and abiotic stresses by regulating the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites [16, 19]. For example, in Carex rigescens, CrUGT87A1 gene significantly increased salt tolerance by accumulating flavonoids for antioxidation in Arabidopsis thaliana [20]. So far, several glucosyltransferases have been verified as UGTs involved in flavonoid glycosylation. For example, the flavonol glycoside compositions and total flavonol contents of Arabidopsis were significantly affected by AtUGT78D1 and AtUGT78D2, which encoded two flavonol-specific glycosyltransferases [21, 22]. In Glycine max (soybean), UGT88A13 exhibited activity toward epicatechin, while UGT88E19 and UGT73C20 displayed activity toward flavonol and iso-flavone aglycones, respectively, and UGT92G4, UGT72 × 4 and UGT72Z3 are flavonol-specific UGTs [15]. In Morella rubra, the MrUGT78R1 and MrUGT78R2 function as rhamnosyltransferases, while MrUGT78W1 as a galactosyltransferase, participated in the glycosylation of flavonols [18]. Furthermore, the UGT genes MdUGT71B1 [23], CsUGT78A14 [24], FaGT6, FaGT7 [25], and AtUGT78D2 [26] function as glucosyltransferases, while MdUGT75B1 [23], CsUGT78A15 [24], and F3GalTase [27] function as galactosyltransferases, involved in flavonol glycosylation [23–27]. In addition, the UGTs could regulate the growth and development, and adapt to changing environments in plants, for instance, UGT03300 and UGT76B1 have roles in plant defense to pathogens [28, 29]. In Arabidopsis, the UGT71B6 gene, which encode an ABA-specific glycosyltransferase, could reduce water loss from leaves under conditions of water deficit [30], while down-expression or mutation of the UGT71C5 gene enhanced drought resistance [31]. SlUGT75C1 from tomato was validated to influence the drought responses and fruit ripening by glycosylating ABA [32]. The over-expression of a 5-UGT cDNA increased the plant’s defense against pathogen infection in potato tuber [33]. In cotton, silencing of the GHUGT116 gene delayed dark-induced leaf senescence [34]. Furthermore, several UGT genes involved in salt stress were also identified. For example, overexpression of OsUGT3 gene enhanced drought and salt tolerance through modulating ABA synthesis and scavenging ROS in rice [35], while OsUGT2 gene was regulated by transcription factor bZIP23 to play a crucial role in salt tolerance [36].

Potato (Solanum tuberosum), a member of the Solanaceae, is the fourth food crop followed by rice, wheat, and maize. Potato has been widely cultivated owing to its significant value in global food security and nutrient supply. Potato plants are characterized by relatively weak tolerance to abiotic stresses and its quality and yield is severely compromised by ongoing climate change [37]. Salinity is one of the main abiotic stresses and thought to be a serious threat to crop yield via affecting the Na+ and Cl− homeostasis, which have a direct effect on the integrity of cellular membranes, the mineral and water uptake, and the efficiency of photosynthesis [37]. Furthermore, salt stress can induce anthocyanin biosynthesis, which leads to the reduction of ionic and osmotic damage and the scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [38, 39]. Potato is regarded as a moderately salt-sensitive crop, and its growth and yield is seriously influenced by salt stress, which reduces the economic output of potato crops and impedes human food supply [40]. As a result, how to improve salt tolerance is one of the important research fields of potato. As mentioned above, the UGT family genes have been shown to be extensively involved in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, growth and development, and stress response in different species. However, the potential functions of UGT family genes on salt stress remain unclear in potato.

In this study, 295 StUGT genes were identified from potato based on the latest potato genome. The chromosome locations, phylogenetic relationship, conserved motifs, gene structure, cis-acting regulatory elements and collinearity were systematically analyzed to broaden the understanding of the characteristics and potential functions of the StUGT genes. The transcript profiles of the 295 StUGT genes in different tissues and the candidate StUGT genes related to salt stress were analyzed and determined. The potential roles of the StUGT178 gene in salt stress were further investigated by heterologous expression of the StUGT178 gene in Arabidopsis. Our study provided useful information for understanding the characterization of the StUGT genes and identification of functional UGTs involved in salt stress, which contribute to reveal the molecular mechanism of the StUGT genes responsesive to salt tolerance in potato.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Potato (Solanum tuberosum Group Phureja DM) test-tube plantlets were preserved in our lab and grown in a light incubator under 16-h light (22 °C)/8-h dark (17 °C) cycles. The whole plants of 4-week-old test-tube plantlets were sampled and frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at -80 °C before DNA and RNA extraction.

Nicotiana benthamiana seeds were sown in pots filled with peat soil: vermiculite = 1:1, and the seedlings were transplanted into individual pots after emerging 3–4 cotyledons. The growth conditions were as follows: 16-h light (24 °C)/8-h dark (18 °C), 70% relative humidity. The four-week-old tobacco leaves were used for subcellular localization of the StUGT178 protein and transient transformation of StUGT178 promoter.

The seedlings of Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Col-0 were grown in a plant growth chamber with 16-h light/8-h dark cycles at 23 °C and light intensity of 150 µmol m− 2 s− 1 (white fluorescent lamps) until flowering.

Identification of UGT family members

The latest genome and protein files of S. tuberosum were retrieved from the potato database (PGSC, v6.1) (http://spuddb.uga.edu/index.shtml) [41]. The protein sequences of Arabidopsis UGTs accessed from the Arabidopsis database (TAIR, https://www.arabidopsis.org/) served as queries to identify candidate UGT sequences in the potato genome. Additionally, the hidden Markov model (HMM) profile of the conserved domain of UGT proteins (PF00201) downloaded from the Pfam database (https://pfam.xfam.org/) was used for searching protein sequences containing conserved UDPGT domain using native software HMMER 3.0. The candidate UGT genes identified simultaneously using the above two methods were retained and the redundant genes were removed. The protein sequences of candidate StUGTs were extracted from the protein file of potato. Subsequently, we further verified the UGT proteins to obtain the final members of the UGT family in potato using SMART, InterProScan, and NCBI Batch CD-Search tools.

Physicochemical property and subcellular location prediction

The biophysical and chemical properties of StUGT proteins, including protein lengths, molecular weights (MWs), grand average hydropathy and isoelectric point (pIs), were surveyed by the online ExPASy website (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). The WoLF PSORT tool (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/) was used to predict the subcellular location of the StUGT proteins.

Chromosome organization and tandem duplication events of StUGT genes

The genome annotation files downloaded from the potato database were used for chromosome location analysis of the StUGT genes. The StUGT genes were named in order according to their distributions on the chromosome from Chr01 to Chr12. The allocation of the StUGT genes on chromosomes and duplication events were displayed by TBtools software (v1.120) [42]. The Ka/Ks values of duplicate gene pairs of UGT family were calculated by the Simple Ka/Ks Calculator program of TBtools.

Phylogenetic analysis

The UGT protein sequences of Arabidopsis were downloaded from the TAIR website. The UGT protein sequences of Zea may, Camellia sinensis, and Solanum lycopersicum were obtained from previous reports [13, 24, 43]. The representative members from different groups of UGT gene family were used for phylogenetic analysis. Multiple sequence alignment for StUGT proteins of potato and representative UGT proteins from other plants was performed using the ClustalW program, and the unrooted phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method (1000 bootstrap replicates) in MAGE 11 software.

Conserved motifs, gene structure and cis-elements analysis

The conserved motifs of StUGT proteins were predicted by the Multiple EM for Motif Elicitation (MEME) website with the parameters: highest motif number of 20, and motif occurrences distribution of zero or one per sequence. The conserved PSPG motif, consisting of 44 amino acids in each StUGT protein was analyzed and visualized using MEME software [44]. Intron-exon structures of the StUGT genes were investigated by the Gene Structure Display Server 2.0 website (http://gsds.gao-lab.org/index.php). Results of phylogenetic tree, conserved motifs and intron/exon composition were visualized by TBtools [42].

The up-stream 1500 bp DNA sequences of the ATG transcription start site of the StUGT genes were retrieved from the potato genome file (.gff) and were used for the cis-elements analysis by the PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) online website. The results of promoter information of the StUGT genes were visualized using the TBtools software.

Syntenic analysis of the UGT gene family

To recognize the evolutionary relationship and gene duplication events of UGT genes, collinearity analysis of the StUGT gene family was conducted with three diverse species, i.e., maize (Zea may, monocotyledon), Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana, model plant in dicots), and tomato (Solanum lycopersicum, Solanaceae). The genome data of maize, Arabidopsis, and tomato was retrieved from Gramene (http://www.maizesequence.org/index.html), TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org/) and phytozome (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/) databases, respectively. One Step MCScanX program with default parameters and Dual Systeny Plot program available at the TBtools software were used to investigate and draw the synteny relationship [42].

Expression profiles of the StUGT genes

The expression values of the StUGT genes in various tissues were obtained from the potato database according to the public transcriptome data. To investigate the potential StUGT genes involved in salt stress, the transcript abundances of the StUGT genes were analyzed using our previous transcriptome data of DM plantlets with 200-mM NaCl treatment (PRJNA882516) [45]. The StUGT genes significantly induced by NaCl were selected as candidate genes related to salt stress and were used for further functional verification.

Construct development and subcellular localization

The full length StUGT178 cDNA without termination codon was amplified from the DM cDNA library using polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and the amplified PCR product was cloned into the pMD-19T vector. The StUGT178 cDNA fragment was then inserted into the linearized pRI101 vector digested with BamH I and Sal I restriction endonuclease using the homologous recombination method. The recombinant plasmid StUGT178-pRI101 was transferred into the Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. One-month-old leaves of N. benthamiana (tobacco) were co-injected with the suspension (OD600 = 0.7–0.8) of Agrobacterium cells containing 35 S::StUGT178-GFP and mCherry-labeled nuclear-marker at a 1:1 ratio using a 1-mL sterile syringe. After dark culture for 24 h, the A. tumefaciens inoculated tobacco plants were transferred to normal growth condition for 24–36 h, and then the fluorescent signals were observed and photographed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus FV1000, Japan). The excitation wavelength with 488 nm and 580 nm was used for GFP and RFP observation, respectively.

Construction of the StUGT178 promoter::GUS construct and transient transformation

The up-stream 1,500-bp promoter sequence of the ATG translation start codon of the StUGT178 gene was amplified from the DM genomic DNA library and incorporated between Hind III and BamH I sites of the pBI121 vector to replace the CaMV 35 S promoter to generate the StUGT178::GUS reporter construct, and then transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101. The resulting construct plasmid was infiltrated into tobacco leaves by Agrobacterium-mediated transient transformation as mentioned above. To analyze the response of the StUGT178 gene upon salt stress, transiently transformed tobacco plantlets were grown in nutritional soil, that were irrigated with nutrient solution (as control) or 200-mM NaCl solution for 2 days.

Arabidopsis transformation

The full-length CDS and 1,500-bp promoter sequence of the StUGT178 gene were transformed into the Arabidopsis Col-0 genome, respectively, using the Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated floral dip method [46]. The kanamycin resistant and GUS staining was used for the identification of positive transgenic plants of the StUGT178 promoter. The T2 generation lines were used for the GUS activity analysis, which was assayed as described above. To analyze the response of the StUGT178 promoter upon salt stress, steadily transformed seedlings (7-day-old) were grown in 1/2 MS medium with 0 mM NaCl (as control) or 200 mM NaCl treatment for 24 h. The positive transgenic plants overexpressing the StUGT178 gene were prescreened on MS medium containing 50 mg/L kanamycin and then selected using semi-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). PCR was performed by specific primers for the StUGT178 gene and GFP sequence on genomic DNA extracted from 4-week-old leaves as a template. The T3 generation lines with higher transcript levels were employed for follow-up assays.

Histochemical staining of GUS activity

β-Glucuronidase (GUS) activity was examined as previously described [47, 48] in transiently transformed tobacco leaves and transgenic Arabidopsis plantlets overexpressing the proStUGT178::GUS chimeric gene. In brief, the whole tobacco leaves or Arabidopsis plants were incubated in the GUS assay buffer with the following components: deionized water, 0.1 M K3[Fe(CN)6], 0.1 M K4[Fe(CN)6], 0.5 M Na2EDTA (pH = 8.0), 0.2 M PBS (0.2 M Na2HPO4 and 0.2 M NaH2PO4), 20% Triton X-100 and 2.5% 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-β-D-glucuronide. The fresh samples of different tissues were harvested and immediately placed in the petri dish containing 25 mL GUS staining solution, and then incubated in a thermostatic oven at 37℃ for 24 h. The decoloration reaction was performed to remove the chlorophyll (for visual clarity) using 70% ethanol several times.

Total RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

The total RNA of DM and Arabidopsis samples were extracted by RNAprep Pure Plant Plus Kit (TIANGEN, China), respectively, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cDNA was synthesized by the reverse transcription of 1 µg RNA using the PrimeScript™ first strand cDNA synthesis kit (TaKaRa, China). qRT-PCR assay was performed using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq II Kit (Takara, Kyoto, Japan) on a CFX96 Touch instrument (Bio-Rad, USA). The qRT-PCR procedure was as follows: 95℃ for 30 s, and 41 cycles of 95℃ 10 s, 60℃ 20 s, and 72℃ 30 s. The StActin97 gene was used as an internal reference gene. Each sample has three biological replicates and three technical replicates. The relative expression levels were quantified by the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Analysis of seed germination and root length

To observe the seed germination rate, sterilized seeds of transgenic lines were sown on a solid 1/2 MS medium supplemented without or with different concentrations of NaCl. Following stratification at 4 °C refrigerator for 3 d, the plates were moved to an incubator with a 16-h light/8-h dark cycle at 22 °C. The germination rates of seeds treated for one day to seven days were calculated.

For root elongation assays, the wild-type and transformed plantlets after 4 days grown on 1/2 MS medium were transferred to 1/2 MS medium supplemented with 0, 100, 150, and 200 mM NaCl. The root lengths were photographed and measured after growing for another 7 days under normal growth conditions.

Measurements of proline and MDA content

To evaluate the effect of salt treatment, seeds of wild type (WT) and transgenic lines were grown on 1/2 MS medium for two weeks and then transplanted into soil and grew for a further two weeks. Then, forty plants for each line were classified into two groups and irrigated with water or 200 mM NaCl solution for 5 d under normal conditions. The performances of the plants were observed and photographed. The proline content was detected as previously described [49]. The MDA content, CAT activity, and POD activity were determined according to the Yang et al. methods [50].

NBT and DAB staining

To assess the H2O2 and O2− activity, leaves of wild type (WT) and the StUGT178-OE Arabidopsis plants were immersed in 1 mg/mL 3,3-diaminobenzidine (DAB) and 0.5 mg/mL nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) staining solution, respectively. The samples were incubated at 28℃ overnight and then were decolorized in a boiling water bath for 10 min.

Results

Identification and molecular characteristics of UGT family members in potato

The candidate genes of the UGT family in potato were identified using both the HMM profile of the conserved domain of UGT proteins (PF00201) and the 120 Arabidopsis UGT genes as queries, and 342 and 803 UGT genes were obtained by HMMER and Blastp search, respectively. Then, 317 common StUGT genes were further confirmed using SMART, InterProScan, and CD-Search databases. Among them, 22 StUGTs were deleted due to their protein sequence without the completely conserved PSPG-box. Finally, a total of 295 putative UGTs with the UDPGT domain were identified in the whole genome of potato and were designated as StUGT1 to StUGT295 based on their chromosomal locations from chromosome 1 (Chr01) to chromosome 12 (Chr12) (Table S1).

The protein sequences encoded by StUGT genes varying from 80 (StUGT29) to 1031 (StUGT225) amino acids, with an average of 417 amino acids per gene. The molecular weights (MWs) ranged from 9.31 kDa (StUGT29) to 117.55 kDa (StUGT225) and the theoretical isoelectric point (pI) varied from 4.44 (StUGT45) to 9.22 (StUGT209) with a mean of 5.75. The instability index (II) of these StUGT proteins were between 25.57 (StUGT128) and 68.55 (StUGT248), with 99 implicated stable proteins (II < 40). The hydrophilicity analysis revealed that 95.25% (281) of the 295 StUGT proteins were hydrophilic, while 4.75% were hydrophobic.

The prediction of subcellular localization showed that the majority of StUGT proteins were localized in the cytoplasm, chloroplast, and nucleus, with only a small number of them in other organelle. Among them, 224 StUGT proteins were distributed in the cytoplasm; 197 and 155 StUGT were located in the chloroplast and nucleus, respectively; 48, 28, 26, and 25 StUGTs were localized in the extracellular region, vacuole, mitochondria, and endoplasmic reticulum, respectively; while only a few of StUGT proteins were located in the plasma membrane (11), peroxisome (9), and golgi apparatus (7). The detailed physicochemical properties of all StUGT proteins are given in Table S2.

Chromosomal position and duplication analysis of StUGT genes

All 295 StUGT genes were scattered throughout the 12 chromosomes based on the latest potato genome annotation information (DM v6.1). As shown in Fig. 1, most of the StUGT genes were clustered at both terminals of chromosomes, and the highest density of the StUGT genes was located at a particular site on chromosome 11. The number of StUGT genes ranged from a minimum of 14 genes on both chromosome 7 and chromosome 8 to a maximum of 54 on chromosome 11. There were 35, 29, 25, and 24 StUGT genes on chromosomes 4, 10, 3, and 2, respectively, and 22 genes on both chromosome 1 and chromosome 12. In addition, chromosomes 9, 5, and 6 had 21, 20, and 15 StUGT genes, respectively.

Gray oval on the chromosome bar represents centromere position for each chromosome. The gene pairs are linked by red lines. Numbers of chromosomes were labeled on the left of each chromosome

The clusters of StUGT genes presented on individual chromosomes indicated that they might go through gene duplication events during evolution. A total of 108 tandem duplication gene pairs were identified in StUGT family (Fig. 1, Table S3). Furthermore, 23 segmental duplication gene pairs were existed in StUGT family (Fig. 2, Table S4). These results revealed that tandem duplication played a major role in the expansion of the StUGT family in potato. The Ka/Ks ratio analysis shown that the Ka/Ks values were < 1 in all duplicate gene pairs (Table S5).

Collinear analysis of the StUGT and UGT genes from several different species

The analysis of duplication events contributes to further understanding the evolution of UGT genes. To investigate the evolutionary relationships of StUGT, syntenic maps were plotted between S. tuberosum and three typical plant species, including Z. mays (monocotyledon), Arabidopsis (dicotyledon, model plant), and S. lycopersicum (Solanaceae). A total of 7, 50, and 114 homologous UGT gene pairs were exhibited between S. tuberosum and Z. mays, Arabidopsis, S. lycopersicum, respectively (Fig. 3, Table S6). The results implicated that S. tuberosum has a closer evolutionary relationship with Arabidopsis and S. lycopersicum than Z. mays.

Phylogenetic analysis of StUGT genes in potato

To further investigate the phylogenetic relationship of StUGT genes, an unrooted tree was constructed. The 295 StUGT genes, and 17, 20, 16 and 31 representative UGT genes from maize, Arabidopsis, Camellia sinensis and tomato, respectively, were used for phylogenetic analysis, and the results showed that all UGT members were classified into 18 groups (A-R) (Fig. 4, Table S7). 295 StUGT genes can be clustered into 17 groups (A-P, R) with a lack of phylogenetic group Q, which only existed in maize genome. Among them, the members of group A had the highest proportion (64, 21.69%). The distributions of other StUGT genes among different groups were as follows: O (57, 19.32%), L (39, 13.22%), D (26, 8.81%), E (23, 7.80%), G (19, 6.44%), P (12, 4.07%), C (10, 3.39%), H (9, 3.05%), M (8, 2.71%), K (8, 2.71%), J (8, 2.71%), B (7, 2.37%), I (2, 0.68%), R (1, 0.34%), N (1, 0.34%), and F (1, 0.34%).

Analysis of conserved motifs and gene structure of StUGT genes

To explore the characteristics of StUGT members, the conserved motifs and gene structure were analyzed with the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 5). For conserved motifs analysis, twenty motifs with 15–50 amino acids were identified among the 295 StUGT proteins using MEME online website (Fig. S1). Among them, StUGT29 proteins contain only two motifs (motif 1 and motif 16), while StUGT12 contain 17 motifs. Most of StUGT members contained 6–16 conserved motifs, and motif 1 could be found in all StUGT proteins, demonstrating that motif 1 was the high conserved domain of the UGT superfamily (Fig. 5B). Meanwhile, several motifs were existed in specific groups, for example, motif 20 was uniquely found in group J, and motif 19 was widely presented in StUGT proteins of group O, suggesting that these special motifs play essential roles in different groups. Interestingly, with the extension of the length of StUGT225 protein, the conserved motifs occurred tandem duplication, and it can be speculated that this gene likely to be a new gene constituted by the combination of different UGT genes.

The phylogenetic relationship, conserved motif, and gene structure analysis of deduced StUGT genes. A, The phylogenetic tree of 295 StUGTs was generated by MAGE 11 software using the ML method. The different colored blocks mean different phylogenetic groups. B, The conservative motif arrangement of StUGT proteins. C, The prediction of conserved domain of UGT superfamily. D, The exon-intron structure of StUGT genes

Analysis of exon-intron structure displayed that the exon numbers of StUGT genes varied from 1 to 4. Most of StUGT genes (176, 59.66%) had one exon, followed by 104 (35.26%) and 12 (4.07%) StUGT genes possessed two and three exons, respectively, while StUGT225, StUGT266, and StUGT280 contained four exons (Fig. 5D).

Prediction of cis-acting regulatory elements of StUGT genes

To investigate the potential functions of the StUGT genes, the cis-acting regulatory elements of 1,500-bp promoter sequence of the StUGT genes were characterized by PlantCARE database (Fig. 6, Table S8). The common cis-acting element (CAAT-box) and promoter core elements (TATA-box) were present in all StUGT genes. Except for the CAAT-box and TATA-box elements, all identified cis-regulatory elements could be assigned into four functional categories: development regulation elements, light responsive elements, phytohormone-responsive elements, and stress responsive elements. Among them, the largest number of elements is light-responsive cis-acting elements (2539, 49.34%). A total of 1,404 cis-acting regulatory elements associated with hormone responses, including abscisic acid (ABA), auxin, methyl jasmonate (MeJA), gibberellin, and salicylic acid (SA), were identified. 273 out of 295 StUGT genes had at least one hormone-responsive element. In addition, 265 StUGT genes contained cis-regulatory elements that participated in abiotic and biotic stress responses, such as anaerobic stress (ARE), pathogen defense (TC-rich repeat), drought (MBS), low-temperature (LTR and DRE), and wounding (WUN-motif) (Fig. S2). Taken together, the cis-element analysis demonstrated that the StUGT genes could be regulated by environmental factors and several hormones.

Transcript profiles of StUGT genes in various tissues

To obtain a better understanding of the functions of the StUGT genes, their tissue-specific expression profiles were analyzed using RNA-seq data of DM potato. FPKM values were utilized to estimate the expression pattern of the StUGT genes. As shown in Fig. 7, the StUGT genes were distinctly expressed in different tissues. For example, StUGT32, StUGT101, StUGT198, and StUGT221 showed the highest expression levels in leaves. StUGT187, StUGT197, StUGT288, and StUGT295 had significant high expression levels in roots. StUGT87, StUGT146, StUGT187, and StUGT295 were highly expressed in shoot. StUGT75, StUGT87, StUGT100 and StUGT146 had significantly high expression levels in sepal. A total of 16 StUGT members had similar tissue expression profiles and were highly expressed in stamen, especially the StUGT32 and StUGT178 genes, while only one gene, StUGT13 or StUGT159, had the highest expression level in tubers or immature fruit, respectively, suggesting that StUGT13 and StUGT159 play essential roles in tubers formation and fruit development, respectively. StUGT32 was highly expressed in leaves, sepals, stamens, mature flowers, petioles, petals and carpels, but expressed at low levels in tubers, stolons, callus and mature fruits, indicating that StUGT32 may play an important role in the development of leaf and flower organs. StUGT100 was highly expressed in sepal, stamen, mature flower, petal, and carpel, and relatively low or no expressed in other tissues, indicating that StUGT100 may play an important role in flower development. StUGT52 and StUGT159 were only highly expressed in mature fruit and immature fruit, respectively, while lowly or no expressed in other tissues, suggesting that StUGT52 and StUGT159 have essential roles in different stages of fruit ripening. In addition, several StUGT genes were lowly expressed or not expressed in different tissues, such as StUGT3, StUGT6, StUGT7, StUGT24, StUGT89 etc.

Expression profile of the StUGT genes in response to salt treatment

Salt stress is an important environmental factor for plant growth and development. Therefore, we performed salt treatment (DM plantlets with 200-mM NaCl treatment) for identification of candidate StUGT genes involved in the salt stress, and their expression profiles were investigated by RNA-seq. A total of 74 and 44 StUGT genes were significantly up- and down-regulated (log2FC > 1, p < 0.01), respectively, by salt treatment according to transcriptomic analysis (Fig. 8). 65, 64, 67 and 58 StUGT genes were increased after 3, 6, 12 and 24 h NaCl treatment, respectively, and 50 StUGT genes were continuously induced by salt treatment after 3, 6, 12, and 24 h. Among them, the transcript levels of genes StUGT16, StUGT20, StUGT187 and StUGT288 reached the peak at 1 h, respectively, while StUGT52 reached the maximum level at 6 h, StUGT177 and StUGT178 at 12 h, and StUGT19 at 24 h, suggesting that these StUGT genes have important roles in salt tolerance. In addition, the transcript levels of 39, 31, 33 and 24 StUGT genes were decreased after 3, 6, 12, and 24 h NaCl treatment, respectively, and 20 StUGT genes were consecutively inhibited by salt treatment after 3, 6, 12, and 24 h. Among them, the expression levels of genes StUGT32, StUGT97, and StUGT104 decreased significantly at 3 h, StUGT200 decreased to the minimum level at 6 h, while genes StUGT26, StUGT74, and StUGT109 showed a decreasing trend, and reached the lowest levels at 12 h, and then increased at 24 h. In order to test the accuracy of transcriptome data, the relative expression of four up-regulated (StUGT13, StUGT53, StUGT177 and StUGT178) and two down-regulated StUGT genes (StUGT104 and StUGT175) were analyzed by real time PCR assay. Results indicated that six genes were significantly induced or inhibited under salt treatment, which consistent with the transcriptome data (Fig. S3). To further confirm the function of candidate StUGT genes associated with salt stress, the StUGT178 gene significantly up-regulated under salt stress was selected for further validation.

StUGT178 gene expression was significantly induced by salt treatment.

To explore the biological function of the candidate StUGT178 gene, the upstream 1507 bp promoter sequence of the StUGT178 was amplified from DNA library of DM potato and subcloned into pBI121 vector. The Agrobacterium harbored empty vector (35 S::GUS) and recombinant plasmid (proStUGT178::GUS) were injected into tobacco leaves, respectively. As shown in Fig. 9, the GUS signals were weakly detected in tobacco leaves (0 mM NaCl) containing proStUGT178::GUS, indicating that the StUGT178 promoter had weak transcriptional activation activity before NaCl treatment. However, the GUS signals of tobacco leaves infiltrated with proStUGT178::GUS were evidently enhanced after NaCl treatment (200 mM NaCl) (Fig. 9C), while the GUS signals have no evident change in tobacco leaves infiltrated with empty vector (35 S::GUS). These results were well consistent with the expression profile of StUGT178 gene under salt treatment. Thus, we speculated that StUGT178 played an important role in salt stress. As shown in Fig. 9D, the GUS activities in roots of proStUGT178 transgenic plants were significantly enhanced by NaCl treatment as compared to those of transgenic plants without NaCl treatment, implicating that the expression of StUGT178 was positively associated with salt tolerance.

The GUS activity analysis demonstrated that the StUGT178 gene had strong expression in young roots and flower organs, while almost have no expression in seeds, leaves, and siliques (Fig. 10A). Subcellular localization analysis revealed that StUGT178 protein was likely localized in the cytoplasm and nucleus. Ectopic expression of the StUGT178 gene enhanced the salt tolerance

The salt-inducible expression of the StUGT178 gene prompted us to validate its potential role in salt tolerance. To further assess the potential contribution of the StUGT178 gene to salt tolerance, we generated StUGT178 overexpression (StUGT178-OE) lines harboring the 35 S::StUGT178-GFP construct in the Col-0 genetic background. A total of 11 independent transgenic lines were obtained and three lines (OE3, OE4, and OE6) with the highest expression levels were selected for further assays (Fig. S5).

Under normal conditions, the germination rates of the StUGT178-OE lines were similar to that of the wild-type (WT) plants. However, the germination rate significantly lower in the wild-type (WT) plants after NaCl treatment than those in the StUGT178-OE lines (OE3, OE4, and OE6). In addition, the WT plants were more sensitive to salt stress than the StUGT178-OE lines as they displayed shorter root length than transgenic lines under salt treatment (Fig. 11). These results revealed that overexpression of the StUGT178 gene increased salt tolerance.

To investigate the physiological mechanism, we measured the related physiological indexes and enzyme activity. The physiological index analysis showed that the proline content of the wild-type plants was similar to those of the StUGT178-OE lines under normal conditions (Fig. 12). In addition, DAB and NBT staining indicated that overexpression of StUGT178 gene contributed to reducing the H2O2 accumulation and promoting the integrity of cell membrane. Taken together, these results were indicative of a role for the StUGT178 gene in the salt tolerance.

Discussion

Glycosylation play essential roles in the reactivity, storage, transport, stability and bioactivity of the sugar acceptors [51]. Glycosyltransferases (GTs) have prominent roles in transferring sugar moieties onto a broad array of small molecules and regulating numerous metabolic processes. GTs can be divided into at least 116 families and several GT families have been identified in different species, for example, GT43 member was identified in rice and charophycean green algae, while GT8 was characterized in 15 plant species [52–54]. UDP-dependent glycosyltransferase (UGT) is the biggest family of GT superfamily, widely distributed in bacteria, fungi, viruses, animals and plants. The sugar donors used for UGTs commonly include UDP-rhamnose (UDP-Rha), UDP-galactose (UDP-Gal), and UDP-glucose (UDP-Glc) [23, 55]. At present, the UGT family has been identified in various plant species, including rice [56], Arabidopsis [10], kiwifruit [57], grape [58], Glycine max [15], and Alfalfa [59]. Potato is the most important tuber crop, which plays an important role in world food security. However, the members of the UGT family in the latest potato genome have not been comprehensively identified and analyzed. In the present study, we identified 295 StUGT genes in potato, which were unevenly distributed on the 12 chromosomes and mainly distributed on Chr04, Chr10, and Chr11. The ORF lengths of the StUGT genes ranged from 240 to 3093 bp. The number of putative StUGT genes was significantly larger than 120, 137, 147, 149, and 168, respectively, from Arabidopsis thaliana [60], Linum usitatissimum [61], Zea mays [13], Glycine max [62], and Prunus persica [63]. The occurrence of duplication events was the primary driving force for gene expansion during evolutionary history. In this study, we obtained 108 pairs of tandem duplications containing 166 StUGT genes and 23 pairs of segmental duplications containing 23 StUGT genes accounting for 56.27% and 7.8% of the total StUGT genes in potato, respectively. Therefore, we speculated that tandem duplication was the major approach to UGT gene expansion in potato, which was consistent with the previous study [64]. The Ka/Ks ratio analysis showed that the Ka/Ks values were < 1 in all duplicate gene pairs, suggesting that the UGT gene family undergone purifying selection during evolutionary process in potato. In addition, a total of 7, 50, and 114 homologous UGT gene pairs were found between S. tuberosum and Z. mays, Arabidopsis, S. lycopersicum, respectively, suggesting that S. tuberosum has a closer evolutionary relationship with Arabidopsis and S. lycopersicum than Z. mays, which coincident with the common evolutionary processes.

The number of UGT clades in distinct plant species varied greatly. In Triticum aestivum, 179 putative UGT genes were classified into 16 major groups without group K. Similarly, UGT genes could be categorized into 17 (A-Q) and 14 (A-N) distinct phylogenetic groups in Zea mays [13] and Brassica species [65], respectively. Based on phylogeny, all 295 StUGT genes were divided into 17 distinct groups and defined as groups A-P and R according to the classification of UGT genes in other species, including Arabidopsis, maize, Camellia sinensis, and tomato. Among them, 14 conserved groups (A-N) were found in all species. Group Q, have O-glycoside-forming activities on multiple flavonoid substrates, was absent from the potato. With the exception of potato, group Q UGTs were also lacking in Cucumis sativus (Cucurbitaceae) and Brassicales, while were widely found in the rosids (fabids, malvids, and Vitales) [64]. Previous studies exhibited that group E has the maximum UGT members in several plant species. For example, a total of 35, 22 and 17 UGT genes were clustered into group E of maize, flax and Arabidopsis, respectively, suggesting that group E had a gene expansion in different species [10, 13]. Furthermore, several UGT gene members of group E have been functionally identified, such as the biosynthesis of anthocyanins and flavonoid glycosides, which demonstrates that group E has an essential role in the glycosylation of secondary metabolites [66, 67]. Here, the highest number of StUGT genes was clustered in group A, which accounted for 21.69% (64) of the total UGT genes identified in potato, while group E was the fifth largest group, containing 23 members.

Most of studies have shown that the sequences at the C-terminal region of UGTs are well conservative and unique, which contained a PSPG-box comprised of 44 amino acids, presented in all UGTs across plant species. On the other hand, the sequences are highly distinct, which is responsible for the recognition of diverse substrates, at the N-terminus of UGTs [2, 8]. Here, a total of 20 motifs were identified in StUGT sequences. All StUGT proteins contain motif 1, which was the highly conserved PSPG box in the UGT family. StUGT proteins within the same group have similar motif components, indicating that these proteins share closely evolutionary relationships and similar functions. The insertion position of introns and the intron loss or gain events are commonly regarded as key clues for understanding the evolution or diversification of the gene family [34]. The analysis of exon-intron structure exhibited that the number of introns of StUGT genes was different and the majority of UGT genes in the same group have a similar gene structure, which was analogous to the structures in previously studied plants [10, 13, 14, 68]. Intron location of 295 StUGT genes demonstrated that most of the StUGT members (176, 59.66%) have no intron, which was consistent with maize (60%), Arabidopsis (58%), and flax (55%) [10, 13, 57, 64]. In addition, 104 (35.26%), 12 (4.07%), and 3 (1.02%) StUGT genes possessed two, three, and four exons, respectively. Furthermore, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis exhibited that the regulatory functions of StUGT genes were divided into three categories: biological process, molecular function, and cellular component. For molecular function, all 295 StUGT genes were annotated to “UDP-glycosyltransferase activity (GO:0008194)”, “glycosyltransferase activity (GO:0016757)”, “catalytic activity (GO:0003824)”, and “transferase activity (GO:0016740)” (Fig. S6).

Generally, the gene function was determined by the cis-regulatory elements of promoter regions to a certain extent. In previous reports, several UGT genes involved in the glycosylation of hormones, such as auxin [69], cytokinin [70], brassinosteroids [71] and salicylic acid [72], have been identified. In the present study, several light-, hormone-, and stress-related cis-elements were distributed in the promoters of the StUGT genes in potato, which was similar to the results reported for Arabidopsis and rice, suggesting that these UGT genes possibly play essential roles in response to diverse biotic and abiotic stresses. Among them, abiotic stress response elements were primarily related to low temperature, drought, and salt stress. In previous studies, several stress-related UGT genes have been identified. For example, UGT79B2 and UGT79B3 participated in cold stress response under the regulation of CBF1, which encodes a transcriptional activator [73]. In Arabidopsis, ectopic expression of the UGT76C2 gene, which is described as a cytokinin glycosyltransferase, enhanced tolerance to drought stress [74]. The UGT71C5 gene is involved in the drought stress response through mediating ABA glucosylation [75]. The UGT74E2 gene, as a hydrogen peroxide-responsive UDP-glucosyltransferase, plays an important role in the regulation of water deficit via its activity toward the auxin indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) [69]. Additionally, the potential function of UGT genes in fiber and seed development has been surveyed by RNA-seq data in flax, cotton, and soybean [61, 62, 76]. Nevertheless, the biological function of the identified UGTs in response to salt stress is largely unknown.

The growth and development of potato plants is susceptible to high salinity, which has a negative impact on tuber yield and market value [40]. Therefore, The development of new cultivars with higher resistance to unfavorable external environment has become one of the primary objectives of potato breeding. The introduction of exogenous genes is the most general strategy for environmental stress resistance currently used [77–79]. However, the number of verified potato genes that play important roles in salt tolerance is relatively scarce. In this study, transcriptome sequencing was performed using potato seedlings with salt stress treatment to identify salt stress-related UGT genes. Gene expression analyses showed that a total of 50 StUGT genes were continuously induced under NaCl treatment, especially the genes StUGT16, StUGT19, StUGT20, StUGT52, StUGT177, StUGT178, StUGT187 and StUGT288 were significantly up-regulated, demonstrating these genes have crucial roles in salt tolerance. To better understand the salt responsiveness of candidate genes, the StUGT178 gene, which was significantly induced by salt stress and contained salt-responsive element, was selected for further study. The results of the transient transformation of tobacco and genetic transformation of Arabidopsis indicated that the GUS activities of tobacco leaves and transgenic Arabidopsis seedlings with proStUGT178::GUS construct were significantly induced under NaCl treatment, which was similar to the results of expression analysis. Furthermore, the StUGT178 overexpression lines exhibited salt-tolerance phenotypes of higher seed germination rates and root lengths, lower MDA contents and H2O2 accumulation, and higher proline contents, CAT and POD activities under salt treatment as compared to WT plants, suggesting the StUGT178 gene had a positive role in salt tolerance. Therefore, we speculated that the StUGT178 gene could be used as a potential tool for the modification of potato plants, especially regarding tolerance to salt stress. In addition, to our knowledge, the StUGT178 gene is the first UGT gene family member characterized that play an essential role in salt tolerance in potato.

Conclusion

In summary, a total of 295 StUGT genes were identified using the latest genomic information of potato. In the present study, the chromosome distribution, gene structure, conserved motifs, evolutionary relationship, and expression profiles of the StUGT genes were systematically analyzed in potato. Furthermore, several candidate StUGT genes involved in salt-tolerance were identified, and the StUGT178 gene was mainly localized in the cytoplasm, which was consistent with the prediction of subcellular localization. Transient transformation of StUGT178 promoter demonstrated that StUGT178 significantly respond to salt stress. Meantime, ectopic expression of the StUGT178 promoter and protein further verified that StUGT178 functioned as a positive regulator of salt stress (Fig. S7). These results provide a foundation for further study on the molecular mechanism of the StUGT genes responsive to salt tolerance in potato.
