Small gaps of GSE
Renjie Feng, Jiaming Li, Dong Yao

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the smallest gaps for the Gaussian symplectic ensemble (GSE). We prove that the rescaled smallest gaps and their locations converge to a Poisson point process with an explicit rate. The approach provides an alternative proof for the GOE case and complements the results in [ 8 ] . By combining the main results from [ 2 , 8 , 9 ] , the study of the smallest gaps for the classical random matrix ensembles C β \beta E and G β \beta E for β = 1 , 2 , \beta=1,2, and 4 4 is now complete.



1.
Introduction

In random matrix theory, one of the main concerns is the distributions of eigenvalues and the gaps between consecutive eigenvalues. Back in the 1950s, Wigner predicted that the spacings between the spectrum of a heavy atom’s nucleus should resemble the spacings between the eigenvalues of certain Gaussian random matrices. This prediction is confirmed by experiments in nuclear physics
[
14
]
. Wigner also predicted the distribution of average gaps, which follow the Gaudin-Mehta distribution
[
4
,
14
]
. In fact, the Gaudin-Mehta distribution has been proved to be true even for a single gap in the bulk of the semicircle law of GUE
[
16
]
and GOE
[
6
]
, which is universal for more general random matrices. Other than the average gaps and the single gap, another fundamental quantity of interest is the extreme spacings.

To state the main results, we first recall two types of point processes studied intensively in random matrix theory. The first is the Gaussian
\beta
-ensemble (G
\beta
E) for
\beta>0
: Given
n
points
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}
with the joint density

(1.1)

J(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,n}}{\prod_{k=1}^{n}e^{-\frac{\beta n}{4}\lambda_{k}^{2}}}{\prod_{i<j}\left|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}\right|^{\beta}},

where by the Selberg integral,

Z_{\beta,n}=(2\pi)^{n/2}\left(\frac{\beta n}{2}\right)^{-\frac{n(n-1)\beta}{4}-\frac{n}{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(1+j\frac{\beta}{2})}{\Gamma(1+\frac{\beta}{2})}.

In particular,
\beta=1,2
and
4
correspond to the joint density of eigenvalues of the classical random matrices of GOE, GUE and GSE, respectively. A remarkable fact is that GUE is a determinantal point process, while GOE and GSE are Pfaffian point processes. The limit of the global distribution of the point processes of G
\beta
E is given by the Wigner semicircle law as
n\to\infty
:

(1.2)

\rho_{\text{sc}}(x)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-x^{2}},\quad x\in[-2,2].

Another point process is the circular
\beta
-ensemble (C
\beta
E) for
\beta>0
: Given
n
points
e^{i\theta_{1}},\cdots,e^{i\theta_{n}}
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the eigenangles
\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{n}
have the joint density

J(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{n})=\frac{1}{C_{\beta,n}}\prod_{i<j}\left|e^{i\theta_{j}}-e^{i\theta_{i}}\right|^{\beta},

where by the Selberg integral again,

C_{\beta,n}=(2\pi)^{n}\frac{\Gamma(1+\beta n/2)}{(\Gamma(1+\beta/2))^{n}}.

In particular,
\beta=1,2
and
4
correspond to the joint density of eigenangles of classical random matrices of COE, CUE and CSE, respectively. Similarly, CUE has a determinantal structure, while COE and CSE have Pfaffian structures. The global distribution of the eigenangles of C
\beta
E tends to the uniform measure on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
as
n\to\infty
.

We first summarize the existing results on the smallest gaps for both the circular ensembles and Gaussian ensembles. In
[
17
]
,
Vinson first obtained limiting distributions of rescaled smallest gap of CUE and GUE. In
[
15
]
, Soshnikov studied the smallest gaps of determinantal point processes with translation invariant kernels. In
[
2
]
, Ben Arous-Bourgade employed Soshnikov’s method to further derive the limiting joint density of
k
smallest gaps of CUE and GUE for
k\geq 1
, along with the locations where these gaps occur. Note that the proofs in
[
2
,
15
,
17
]
rely heavily on the determinantal structures.

In
[
8
]
, the authors derived the smallest gaps for GOE, addressing the problem beyond the determinantal structures. Note that
[
8
]
only proves the limiting distribution of the smallest gaps of GOE, without providing information on their locations. The intuitive idea behind the method in
[
8
]
is based on the following observation from a statistical physics perspective: for a pair of two particles with charge 1 forming the smallest gap, these two particles will stick together to form one ‘double particle’ with charge 2 in the limit. That is, the original system of one-component log-gas will become a new system of two-component log-gas. To prove the main results, it is necessary to study the limit of the ratios of the partition functions of these two systems, which can be analyzed using Selberg-type integrals. In
[
9
]
, using the same method, the authors derived the smallest gaps of C
\beta
E with integer-valued
\beta
, with results holding particularly for the classical random matrices of COE, CUE, and CSE.

However, one of the main obstacles with this method is that the Selberg-type integrals are intractable in most cases, such as GSE. In the current paper, we derive the smallest gaps of GSE and the locations where they occur. Our approaches relies on combinatorial arguments to bound certain quantities arising from the Pfaffian structure. We expect our computations to provide new insights into the study of various Pfaffian point processes. In particular, it provides an alternative proof for GOE, where the previously omitted location information will be included. This, together with the main results in
[
2
,
8
,
9
]
, will complete the study of the smallest gaps and their locations for the classical random matrices of C
\beta
E and G
\beta
E with
\beta=1,2
and
4
.

Regarding the largest gaps, only a few results are known. In
[
2
]
, Ben Arous-Bourgade determined the decay order of the largest gaps for both the CUE and GUE (within the interior of the semicircle law). Additionally,
[
10
]
proves that these largest gaps converge to Poisson point processes. Consequently, the fluctuations of the largest gaps are shown to follow Gumbel distributions in the limit. Again, the determinantal structures of the CUE and GUE play a crucial role in the analyses in
[
2
,
10
]
. It is worth noting that the decay orders and fluctuations of the largest gaps for other ensembles, such as the COE and CSE, remain unknown.

The works
[
3
,
13
]
proved that the results in
[
2
,
8
,
10
]
are universal,
i.e., they hold for Wigner ensembles with general distributions. Figalli-Guionnet also derived the smallest gaps for a several-matrix model in
[
7
]
.

1.1.
Main results

Let
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots<\lambda_{n}
be sampled from G
\beta
E with joint density given in (
1.1
). For any fixed small
\epsilon>0
, consider the two-dimensional point process

\Upsilon^{(\beta)}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\delta_{\left(n^{\frac{\beta+2}{\beta+1}}(\lambda_{i+1}-\lambda_{i}),\,\,\,\lambda_{i}\right)}\cdot\mathbbm{1}[\lambda_{i}\in(-2+\epsilon,2-\epsilon)].

The main result is

Theorem 1.1

For
\beta=1,2
and
4
,
\Upsilon^{(\beta)}_{n}
converges to a Poisson point process
\Upsilon^{(\beta)}
on
(0,+\infty)\times(-2+\epsilon,2-\epsilon)
with intensity

\mathbb{E}\Upsilon^{(\beta)}(A\times I)=\left(\dfrac{1}{c_{\beta}}\int_{A}u^{\beta}\mathrm{d}u\right)\int_{I}\left(2\pi\rho_{\text{sc}}(x)\right)^{\beta+2}\mathrm{d}x,

where
\rho_{sc}
is the semicircle law (
1.2
),
A
is any bounded measurable set in
(0,+\infty)
,
I
is any interval in
(-2+\epsilon,2-\epsilon)
, and

\displaystyle c_{1}={48\pi},\quad c_{2}={48\pi^{2}},\quad c_{4}={540\pi^{2}}.

As we mentioned earlier, the case for
\beta=2
has been proven in
[
2
]
. For
\beta=1
,
[
8
]
only studies the 1-dimensional point process of the smallest gaps
\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\delta_{n^{{3/2}}(\lambda_{i+1}-\lambda_{i})}
, where the location information is missing. In this paper, we prove Theorem
1.1
for
\beta=4
in detail, and then sketch the proof for GOE.

As a direct corollary of Theorem
1.1
, we obtain the limiting distribution of the
k
-th smallest gap in the bulk of the semicircle law.

Corollary 1.2

Let
I
be any interval in
(-2+\epsilon,2-\epsilon)
, and
t_{k}^{(\beta)}(I)
be the
k
-th smallest gap for eigenvalues falling in
I
, we define the
normalized gap

{\tau}_{k}^{(\beta)}(I):=\left(\dfrac{1}{(\beta+1)c_{\beta}}\int_{I}\left(2\pi\rho_{\text{sc}}(x)\right)^{\beta+2}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta+1}}n^{\frac{\beta+2}{\beta+1}}t_{k}^{(\beta)}(I).

Then for
\beta=1,2
and
4
, we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left({\tau}_{k}^{(\beta)}(I)\in A\right)=\int_{A}\frac{\beta+1}{(k-1)!}x^{k(\beta+1)-1}e^{-x^{\beta+1}}\mathrm{d}x

for any bounded interval
A\subset(0,+\infty)
.

Now we outline the main steps to prove Theorem
1.1
.
Given the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots<\lambda_{n}
of GSE. For given
\epsilon>0
small,
consider the point processes consisting of the eigenvalues of GSE in the bulk
(-2+\epsilon,2-\epsilon)
,

\xi_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\lambda_{i}}\mathbbm{1}[\left|\lambda_{i}\right|<2-\epsilon].

Now we construct an auxiliary point process
\widetilde{\xi}_{n}\subset\xi_{n}
as follows.
For any bounded measurable set
A\subset(0,+\infty)
, let
A_{n}=n^{-6/5}A
. The process
\widetilde{\xi}_{n}
is selected from
\xi_{n}
such that the following two conditions hold:

-

|\xi_{n}\left(\lambda_{k}+A_{n}\right)|=1.

-

There does not exists
\lambda_{\ell}\neq\lambda_{k}
such that
|\xi_{n}\left(\lambda_{\ell}+A_{n}\right)|=1

and
\left|\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{\ell}\right|<\log^{-1}n
.

Theorem
1.1
is a direct consequence of the following two lemmas. We first have

Lemma 1.3

The two point processes
\Upsilon_{n}^{(4)}(A\times\cdot)
and
\widetilde{\xi}_{n}(\cdot)
are asymptotically equivalent, i.e., for any bounded interval
I\subset(-2+\epsilon,2-\epsilon)
, the cardinalities satisfy

\left|\Upsilon_{n}(A\times I)\right|-|{\tilde{\xi}_{n}(I)}|\to 0\quad\mbox{in probability as $n\to\infty$}.

Next, we prove that
\widetilde{\xi}_{n}
converges to a Poisson point process by the moment method.

Lemma 1.4

For any fixed positive integer
k
, one has the following convergence of the
k
-th factorial moment

(1.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{|\widetilde{\xi}_{n}(I)|!}{\left(|\widetilde{\xi}_{n}(I)|-k\right)!}\right)=\left(\dfrac{1}{540\pi^{2}}\int_{A}u^{4}\mathrm{d}u\right)^{k}\left(\int_{I^{k}}\left(2\pi\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda)\right)^{6}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right)^{k}.\end{aligned}

We now provide a brief overview of the key steps of the proof. The starting point is the Pfaffian structure of GSE, where the correlation function is the Pfaffian of an anti-symmetric matrix (see (
3.1
) and (
3.2
)). This matrix can be expressed in terms of a scalar kernel
S_{n}
, along with its derivative and antiderivative (see (
3.3
)).

There are several significant challenges in analyzing the correlation functions of Pfaffian processes compared to determinantal processes. For instance, because the kernel matrices for Pfaffian processes are anti-symmetric, the Hadamard-Fischer inequality for positive symmetric matrices, as used in
[
2
,
15
]
, can no longer be applied to estimate their correlation functions. Additionally, the growth orders of different entries in the anti-symmetric matrix are mixed and interwoven (see Lemma
3.2
below).

In Lemma
5.1
, our first observation is that the integral of the product of the Pfaffians of the on-diagonal
4\times 4
blocks provides the leading order term for the factorial moments. Therefore, one of the main tasks is to demonstrate that the contributions from the off-diagonal
4\times 4
blocks are negligible in the limit. To address this, in Section
5.2
, we will first perform two rounds of transformations on the anti-symmetric kernel matrix with its Pfaffian unchanged, so that the orders of different entries become more balanced (e.g., Lemma
5.2
). Then, the negligibility of the contribution from off-diagonal blocks (e.g., Lemma
5.3
) follows from the combinatorial counting arguments (e.g., Lemma
5.4
). In Section
6
, the similar arguments are applied to prove the negligibility of two error terms (
E_{1,k,n}
and
E_{2,k,n}
in (
4.6
)) appearing when estimating the correlation functions. This approach provides an alternative proof for GOE, which is sketched in Section
7
. We anticipate that these computations, particularly the two rounds of transformations and combinatorial counting arguments, can be applied to study the intricate structures of other Pfaffian point processes.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Correlation functions

First, we review some basic concepts
regarding the factorial moments and the correlation functions of a point process.
Let

\displaystyle\xi=\sum_{i}\delta_{X_{i}}

be a simple point process on
\mathbb{R}
. For any
k\geq 1
, we can construct a new point process
\xi_{k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
via

\xi_{k}=\sum_{X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{k}}\textrm{ pairwise distinct}}\delta_{(X_{i_{1}},\cdots,X_{i_{k}})}.

The
k
-point correlation function of
\xi

is a function
\rho_{k}
on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that for any bounded Borel sets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}|\xi_{k}\left(B_{1}\times\cdots\times B_{k}\right)|=\int_{B_{1}\times\cdots\times B_{k}}\rho_{k}(x_{1},...,x_{k})\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k}.

In particular, for any bounded Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}
, one has

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{|\xi(B)|!}{(|\xi(B)|-k)!}\right)=\int_{B^{k}}\rho_{k}(x_{1},...,x_{k})\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k},

which is the
k
-th factorial moment of the cardinality
|\xi(B)|
.

2.2.
Pfaffian

Now we recall the definition of the Pfaffian of an anti-symmetric matrix. Let
\mathbf{M}=\left(\mathbf{M}(i,j)\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq 2N}
be a
2N\times 2N
anti-symmetric matrix, where
N
is a positive integer. Then the Pfaffian of
\mathbf{M}
is defined by

\text{Pf }\mathbf{M}:=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{2N}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\mathbf{M}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\mathbf{M}(\sigma_{3},\sigma_{4})\cdots\mathbf{M}(\sigma_{2N-1},\sigma_{2N}),

where
\mathcal{P}_{2N}
consists of all permutations
\sigma
of
\{1,2,...,2N\}
such that

\sigma_{1}<\sigma_{3}<\cdots<\sigma_{2N-1},\text{ and }\sigma_{1}<\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}<\sigma_{4},...,\sigma_{2N-1}<\sigma_{2N}.

For any
2N\times 2N
matrix
A
, we have

\text{Pf }\left(A^{T}\mathbf{M}A\right)=(\det A)(\text{Pf }\mathbf{M}).

This identity implies that
\text{Pf }\mathbf{M}
is invariant under congruent transformations when
A
has unit determinant, which in particular includes the case of the
product of elementary matrices.

2.3.
Hermite polynomials

The monic orthogonal polynomials on
\mathbb{R}
for the weight

\mathrm{d}v(x)=w(x)\mathrm{d}x,\quad w(x)=\frac{e^{-x^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}

are called Hermite polynomials, which are given by

H_{n}(x):=(-1)^{n}e^{x^{2}/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}\left(e^{-x^{2}/2}\right)

for integer
n\geq 0
.
The
L^{2}
-normalized Hermite wave functions

\varphi_{n}(x):=\frac{e^{-x^{2}/4}H_{n}(x)}{(2\pi)^{1/4}\sqrt{n!}}

are orthonormal, i.e.,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi_{n}(x)\varphi_{m}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\delta_{n,m}.

For any
\theta\in(0,\pi)
, we set

b(\theta)=\theta-\frac{1}{2}\sin 2\theta,\quad R_{n}(\theta)=\sin\left(nb(\theta)+\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{\theta}{2}\right),

Q_{n}(\theta)=\frac{3}{16}(\sin\theta)^{-2}\sin\left(nb(\theta)-\frac{3}{4}\pi-\frac{5}{2}\theta\right)+\frac{5}{96}(\sin\theta)^{-3}\sin\left(nb(\theta)-\frac{5}{4}\pi-\frac{7}{2}\theta\right).

Then we have the following classical Plancherel-Rotach formula (see
[
5
]
),

(2.1)

\begin{split}&\varphi_{n}\left(2\sqrt{n+1}\cos\theta\right)\\
&=(\pi\sin\theta)^{-{1}/{2}}n^{-{1}/{4}}\left(R_{n+1}(\theta)+\frac{1}{n}\left(Q_{n+1}(\theta)-\frac{5}{24}R_{n+1}(\theta)\right)+O\left(n^{-2}\right)\right).\end{split}

Here, for any
\epsilon>0
, the error bound
O\left(n^{-2}\right)
is uniform for
\theta\in(-\pi+\epsilon,\pi-\epsilon)
. The Plancherel-Rotach formula will imply the following

Lemma 2.1

Given any
\epsilon>0
, for
x\leq y
such that
x,y\in(-2+\epsilon,2-\epsilon)
, one has uniform estimates,

(2.2)

\int_{\sqrt{n}x}^{\sqrt{n}y}\varphi_{n}(t)\mathrm{d}t=O\left(n^{-3/4}\right)

and

(2.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\sqrt{2n}x}^{+\infty}\varphi_{2n-1}(t)\mathrm{d}t=O\left(n^{-1/4}\right).

Proof.

Let
0<\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<\pi
be such that

2\sqrt{n+1}\cos\theta_{1}=\sqrt{n}y,\,\,2\sqrt{n+1}\cos\theta_{2}=\sqrt{n}x.

Since
(x,y)\in(-2+\epsilon,2-\epsilon)
,
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
are also bounded away from 0 and
\pi
for large
n
.
By a change of variable
t=2\sqrt{n+1}\cos\theta
and
using (
2.1
), we have

(2.4)

\begin{split}\int_{\sqrt{n}x}^{\sqrt{n}y}\varphi_{n}(t)\mathrm{d}t&=2\sqrt{n+1}\int_{\theta_{1}}^{\theta_{2}}\varphi_{n}\left(2\sqrt{n+1}\cos\theta\right)\sin\theta\mathrm{d}\theta\\
&=2\sqrt{n+1}\pi^{-1/2}n^{-1/4}\int_{\theta_{1}}^{\theta_{2}}R_{n+1}(\theta)(\sin\theta)^{1/2}\mathrm{d}\theta+O\left(n^{-3/4}\right).\end{split}

The integrand
R_{n+1}(\theta)(\sin\theta)^{1/2}
can be written as
g_{1}(\theta)\sin\left((n+1)b(\theta)\right)+g_{2}(\theta)\cos((n+1)b(\theta))
for some
C^{1}(0,\pi)
functions
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, where
b(\theta)=\theta-\sin(2\theta)/2
belongs to
C^{1}(0,\pi)
and satisfies

b^{\prime}(\theta)=1-\cos(2\theta)>c>0
on
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}).

Integration by parts will simply imply

(2.5)

\left|\int_{\theta_{1}}^{\theta_{2}}R_{n+1}(\theta)(\sin\theta)^{1/2}\mathrm{d}\theta\right|\lesssim n^{-1}.

This will prove (
2.2
) by (
2.4
) and (
2.5
).
By identities (7.376) in
[
11
]
, we can derive

(2.6)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-x^{2}/4}H_{2n}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=(2n-1)!!\sqrt{\pi},\quad\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-x^{2}/4}H_{2n+1}(x)\mathrm{d}x\lesssim(2n)!!\sqrt{\pi}.

By Stirling’s formula, the integral of the Hermite wave functions satisfies

(2.7)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\varphi_{2n-1}(t)\mathrm{d}t\lesssim&\dfrac{(2n-2)!!}{\sqrt{(2n-1)!}}\lesssim\dfrac{\sqrt{4(n-1)}\left(\frac{2n-2}{e}\right)^{n-1}}{\left(2\pi(2n-1)\right)^{1/4}\left(\frac{2n-1}{e}\right)^{n-1/2}}\lesssim n^{-1/4}.\end{split}

By the decomposition

\int_{\sqrt{2n}x}^{+\infty}\varphi_{2n-1}(t)\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\cdots-\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2n}x}\cdots
,
(
2.3
) follows directly from (
2.2
) and (
2.7
). ∎

3.
Kernel estimates of GSE

GSE is a Gaussian measure on the space of Hermitian self-dual matrices
[
14
]
. The eigenvalues of GSE are real, and the joint density is given by (
1.1
) with
\beta=4
. The eigenvalues of GSE form a Pfaffian point process. Specifically, for any integer
k\geq 1
, the
k
-point correlation function can be expressed as the Pfaffian of a
2k\times 2k
anti-symmetric matrix (see Section 3.9 in
[
1
]
)

(3.1)

\rho_{k}(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{k})=\text{Pf }\left(JK_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq k},

where

J=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{bmatrix},

and
K_{n}(x,y)
is the kernel given by

(3.2)

K_{n}(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}S_{n}(x,y)&V_{n}(x,y)\\
J_{n}(x,y)&S_{n}(y,x)\end{bmatrix}.

Here,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S_{n}(x,y)&=\frac{\sqrt{2n}}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\varphi_{j}\left(\sqrt{2n}x\right)\varphi_{j}\left(\sqrt{2n}y\right)-\frac{n}{2}\varphi_{2n}(\sqrt{2n}x)\int_{\sqrt{2n}y}^{\infty}\varphi_{2n-1}(t)\mathrm{d}t,\\
V_{n}(x,y)&=-{\partial_{y}}S_{n}(x,y),\\
J_{n}(x,y)&=\int_{y}^{x}S_{n}(t,y)\mathrm{d}t.\end{aligned}

The
2\times 2
matrix
JK_{n}(x,y)
is anti-symmetric where

(3.4)

\displaystyle V_{n}(x,y)=-V_{n}(y,x),\quad\int_{y}^{x}S_{n}(t,y)\mathrm{d}t=\int_{y}^{x}S_{n}(t,x)\mathrm{d}t.

For any given
x_{0}\in(-2,2)
, we define the following rescaled GSE kernel

(3.5)

\displaystyle\widehat{K}_{n}(x,y):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})}}&0\\
&\sqrt{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})}\end{bmatrix}K_{n}(x,y)\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})}}\end{bmatrix}.

We further denote it by

\displaystyle\widehat{K}_{n}(x,y):=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{S}_{n}(x,y)&\widehat{V}_{n}(x,y)\\
\widehat{J}_{n}(x,y)&\widehat{S}_{n}(y,x)\end{bmatrix}.

Let
K_{\sin}
be the sine kernel

(3.6)

K_{\sin}(t):=\frac{\sin(\pi t)}{\pi t}.

We have the following uniform estimates regarding the rescaled GSE kernel.

Lemma 3.1

For any
\epsilon>0
, uniformly over
x_{0}\in(-2+\epsilon,2-\epsilon)
and
x,y\in[-1,1]
,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{S}_{n}\left(x_{0}+\frac{x}{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})},x_{0}+\frac{y}{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})}\right)-K_{\sin}(2(x-y))\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},

(3.8)

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{J}_{n}\left(x_{0}+\frac{x}{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})},x_{0}+\frac{y}{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})}\right)-\int_{0}^{x-y}K_{\sin}(2t)\mathrm{d}t\right|\lesssim\frac{\left|x-y\right|}{\sqrt{n}},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{V}_{n}\left(x_{0}+\frac{x}{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})},x_{0}+\frac{y}{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})}\right)-{\partial_{x}}[K_{\sin}(2(x-y))]\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{n}.

Proof.

Let
K_{n}^{(2)}(x,y)
be the kernel of the GUE. It can be expressed in terms of Hermite wave functions as follows (e.g., Lemma 3.2.2 in
[
1
]
):

K_{n}^{(2)}(x,y)=\sqrt{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\varphi_{i}(\sqrt{n}x)\varphi_{i}(\sqrt{n}y).

One first has the uniform estimates (e.g., Theorem 1 in
[
12
]
)

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in\left[-1,1\right]}\left|\frac{1}{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})}K_{n}^{(2)}\left(x_{0}+\frac{x}{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})},x_{0}+\frac{y}{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})}\right)-K_{\sin}(x-y)\right|

(3.10)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n},

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in\left[-1,1\right]}\left|{\partial_{x}}\left(\frac{1}{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})}K_{n}^{(2)}\left(x_{0}+\frac{x}{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})},x_{0}+\frac{y}{n\rho_{\text{sc}}(x_{0})}\right)-K_{\sin}(x-y)\right)\right|

(3.11)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n}.

By (
3.3
), we have the relation

(3.12)

\displaystyle S_{n}(x,y)=\frac{K_{2n}^{(2)}(x,y)}{2}-\frac{n}{2}\varphi_{2n}(\sqrt{2n}x)\int_{\sqrt{2n}y}^{\infty}\varphi_{2n-1}(t)\mathrm{d}t.

The estimate (
3.7
) then follows from the bound
O\left(n^{-1/4}\right)
for
\varphi_{2n}
(see (
2.1
)) and the bound
n^{-1/4}
for the integral term (see (
2.3
)).
We also obtain (
3.8
) by integrating both sides of (
3.7
). Taking the derivative with respect to
y
on (
3.12
), by (
2.1
) again, we get

(3.13)

\displaystyle\left|{\partial_{y}}S_{n}(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}{\partial_{y}}K^{(2)}_{2n}(x,y)\right|=\left|\frac{\sqrt{2}n^{3/2}}{2}\varphi_{2n}(\sqrt{2n}x)\varphi_{2n-1}(\sqrt{2n}y)\right|\lesssim n.

Then, (
3.9
) follows from the estimate (
3.11
).

∎

Subsequently, we need to control the behavior of the kernel at different scales. To achieve this, we provide the following uniform upper bounds.

Lemma 3.2

Let
d:=d(x,y)=\left|x-y\right|
. For any
\epsilon>0
and
(x,y)\in(-2+\epsilon,2-\epsilon)
,

(3.14)

\displaystyle\left|S_{n}(x,y)\right|\lesssim\min\left\{\frac{1}{d},n\right\}+n^{1/2},

(3.15)

\left|\partial_{x}S_{n}(x,y)\right|\lesssim n\min\left\{\frac{1}{d},n^{2}d\right\}+n^{3/2},

(3.16)

\left|V_{n}(x,y)\right|=\left|\partial_{y}S_{n}(x,y)\right|\lesssim n\min\left\{\frac{1}{d},n^{2}d\right\}+n,

(3.17)

\left|\partial_{x}V_{n}(x,y)\right|+\left|\partial_{y}V_{n}(x,y)\right|\lesssim n^{2}\min\left\{n,\frac{1}{d}\right\},

(3.18)

\left|\partial^{2}_{xy}V_{n}(x,y)\right|+\left|\partial^{2}_{yx}V_{n}(x,y)\right|+\left|\partial^{2}_{yy}V_{n}(x,y)\right|\lesssim n^{3}\min\left\{n,\frac{1}{d}\right\},

(3.19)

\left|\int_{x}^{y}S_{n}(t,w)\mathrm{d}t\right|\lesssim\log n,\quad\forall w\in(-2+\epsilon,2-\epsilon).

Proof.

We first prove the following estimates for the GUE kernel
K_{n}^{(2)}
:

(3.20)

\displaystyle\left|K^{(2)}_{n}(x,y)\right|\lesssim

\displaystyle\min\left\{\frac{1}{d},n\right\},

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{x}K^{(2)}_{n}(x,y)\right|+\left|\partial_{y}K^{(2)}_{n}(x,y)\right|\lesssim

\displaystyle n\min\left\{\frac{1}{d},n^{2}d\right\}+n.

When
d\leq 1/n
, (
3.20
) directly follows from (
3.10
) and (
3.11
).
For
d\geq 1/n
,
by Christoffel-Darboux formula, we can rewrite (e.g., Lemma 3.2.5 in
[
1
]
)

(3.21)

K_{n}^{(2)}(x,y)=\sqrt{n}\frac{\varphi_{n}(\sqrt{n}x)\varphi_{n-1}(\sqrt{n}y)-\varphi_{n}(\sqrt{n}y)\varphi_{n-1}(\sqrt{n}x)}{x-y}.

By Plancherel-Rotach formula (
2.1
), we have

\left|\varphi_{n}(\sqrt{n}x)\right|+\left|\varphi_{n-1}(\sqrt{n}x)\right|\lesssim n^{-1/4}.

This proves the first inequality in (
3.20
). We note the relation (e.g., Lemma 3.2.7 in
[
1
]
)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(\varphi_{n}(\sqrt{n}x)\right)=n\varphi_{n-1}(\sqrt{n}x)-\frac{nx}{2}\varphi_{n}(\sqrt{n}x).

The second inequality in (
3.20
) follows by differentiating (
3.21
) with respect to
x
together with the estimate

(3.22)

\begin{split}\left|\frac{\textrm{d}}{\textrm{d}x}\left(\varphi_{n}(\sqrt{n}x)\right)\right|\leq n\left(\left|\varphi_{n-1}(\sqrt{n}x)\right|+\left|\varphi_{n}(\sqrt{n}x)\right|\right)\lesssim n^{3/4}.\end{split}

In fact, performing further differentiations, we have

(3.23)

\begin{split}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}\left(\varphi_{n}(\sqrt{n}x)\right)\right|&\lesssim n^{7/4},\\
\sum_{i+j=2,i,j\geq 0}\left|\partial^{i}_{x}\partial^{j}_{y}K_{n}^{(2)}(x,y)\right|&\lesssim n^{2}\min\left\{\frac{1}{d},n\right\},\\
\sum_{i+j=3,i,j\geq 0}\left|\partial^{i}_{x}\partial^{j}_{y}K_{n}^{(2)}(x,y)\right|&\lesssim n^{3}\min\left\{\frac{1}{d},n\right\}.\end{split}

Now we are ready to prove (
3.14
) - (
3.19
).
To prove (
3.14
), by (
2.1
), (
2.2
), (
2.3
), (
3.12
) and (
3.20
), we get

(3.24)

\begin{split}\left|S_{n}(x,y)\right|\leq&\frac{1}{2}\left|K_{2n}^{(2)}(x,y)\right|+\frac{n}{2}\left|\varphi_{2n}\left(\sqrt{2n}x\right)\right|\left|\int_{\sqrt{2n}y}^{\infty}\varphi_{2n-1}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right|\\
\lesssim&\min\left\{\frac{1}{d},n\right\}+n^{1/2}.\end{split}

To prove (
3.15
), by (
2.3
), (
3.20
) and (
3.22
), we get

(3.25)

\begin{split}\left|\partial_{x}S_{n}(x,y)\right|&\lesssim\frac{1}{2}\left|\partial_{x}K_{2n}^{(2)}(x,y)\right|+\frac{n}{2}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(\varphi_{2n}(\sqrt{2n}x)\right)\right|\cdot\left|\int_{\sqrt{2n}y}^{\infty}\varphi_{2n-1}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right|\\
&\lesssim n\min\left\{\frac{1}{d},n^{2}d\right\}+n^{3/2}.\end{split}

Now, (
3.16
) follows directly from (
3.13
) and the second part of (
3.20
). And (
3.17
) and (
3.18
)
can be proved similarly, thanks to (
3.23
). It remains to show (
3.19
). We have

(3.26)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{x}^{y}S_{n}(t,w)\mathrm{d}t\right|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left|\int_{x}^{y}K_{2n}^{(2)}(t,w)\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle+\frac{n}{2}\left|\int_{x}^{y}\varphi_{2n}(\sqrt{2n}t)\mathrm{d}t\right|\cdot\left|\int_{\sqrt{2n}w}^{\infty}\varphi_{2n-1}(t)\mathrm{d}t\right|.

By (
3.20
), we can bound the first term in (
3.26
) as follows:

(3.27)

\int_{x}^{y}\left|K_{n}^{(2)}(t,x)\right|\mathrm{d}t\lesssim\left(\int_{0}^{1/n}n\mathrm{d}t+\int_{1/n}^{4}\frac{1}{t}\mathrm{d}t\right)\lesssim\log n.

By (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), we can bound the second term in (
3.26
) by

Cn\cdot n^{-5/4}\cdot n^{-1/4}=Cn^{-1/2},
which completes the proof of
(
3.19
).

∎

4.
Main lemmas

In this section, we introduce two main lemmas that will imply Lemma
1.3
and Lemma
1.4
, and thus Theorem
1.1
for GSE. Recall in Section
1
that for any bounded measurable set
A\subset(0,+\infty)
, we set
A_{n}=n^{-6/5}A
. First, Lemma
1.3
is the direct consequence of the following

Lemma 4.1

Suppose
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},...,\lambda_{n}
are the eigenvalues of GSE. For any interval
I\subset(-2+\epsilon,2-\epsilon)
, define
G_{n}
to be the union of the following two sets:

\displaystyle\left\{(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i+1},\lambda_{i+2})\in I^{3}:1\leq i\leq n-2,\lambda_{i+1}-\lambda_{i}\in A_{n}\text{ and }0\leq\lambda_{i+2}-\lambda_{i}\leq 2\sup\left(A_{n}\right)\right\},

and

\displaystyle\Big\{(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i+1},\lambda_{j},\lambda_{j+1})\in I^{4}:

\displaystyle 1\leq i<j\leq n-1,\lambda_{i+1}-\lambda_{i}\in A_{n},\lambda_{j+1}-\lambda_{j}\in A_{n},

\displaystyle\text{and }2\sup\left(A_{n}\right)<\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}<\log^{-1}n\Big\},

respectively.
Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|G_{n}\right|>0\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|G_{n}\right|\right]\rightarrow 0\,\,\,\text{as}\,\,\,n\to\infty.

Recall the definitions of the point processes
\xi_{n}
and
\widetilde{\xi}_{n}
from Section
1
. Let
\rho_{k}
and
\widetilde{\rho}_{k}
denote the
k
-point correlation functions of
\xi_{n}
and
\widetilde{\xi}_{n}
, respectively. To prove Lemma
1.4
, we will derive some upper and lower bounds for the correlation functions
\widetilde{\rho}_{k}
and show that these two bounds match up to the leading order. Then we will prove that the integral of the leading order term yields the limit in (
1.3
).

Specifically, we define the set

(4.1)

\Omega_{k}:=\left\{(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\in(-2+\epsilon,2-\epsilon)^{k}:\min_{i\neq j}\left|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\right|>\log^{-1}n\right\}.

By the definition of
\widetilde{\xi}_{n}
, we have

(4.2)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}_{k}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\equiv 0\quad\text{for}\quad(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\in\Omega_{k}^{c}.

On
\Omega_{k}
we first have the upper bound

(4.3)

\begin{split}\widetilde{\rho}_{k}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\leq&\int_{\lambda_{1}+A_{n}}\cdots\int_{\lambda_{k}+A_{n}}\rho_{2k}(\lambda_{1},x_{1},...,\lambda_{k},x_{k})\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k}\\
:=&L_{k,n}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}).\end{split}

Given any
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\in\Omega_{k}
, we define two sets

(4.4)

T_{1,k,n}=T_{1,k,n}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}):=\cup_{i=1}^{k}\left(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i}+2\sup\left(A_{n}\right)\right),

and

(4.5)

\begin{split}T_{2,k,n}=&T_{2,k,n}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\\
:=&\cup_{i=1}^{k}\left\{(x,y):y\in x+A_{n},2\sup\left(A_{n}\right)<\left|x-\lambda_{i}\right|\leq\log^{-1}n\right\}.\end{split}

Then on
\Omega_{k}
we have the lower bound

(4.6)

\begin{split}&\widetilde{\rho}_{k}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\\
\geq&\int_{\lambda_{1}+A_{n}}\cdots\int_{\lambda_{k}+A_{n}}\rho_{2k}(\lambda_{1},x_{1},...,\lambda_{k},x_{k})\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k}\\
-&\int_{\lambda_{1}+A_{n}}\cdots\int_{\lambda_{k}+A_{n}}\int_{T_{1,k,n}}\rho_{2k+1}(\lambda_{1},x_{1},...,\lambda_{k},x_{k},z)\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k}\\
-&\int_{\lambda_{1}+A_{n}}\cdots\int_{\lambda_{k}+A_{n}}\int_{T_{2,k,n}}\rho_{2k+2}(\lambda_{1},x_{1},...,\lambda_{k},x_{k},z_{1},z_{2})\mathrm{d}z_{1}\mathrm{d}z_{2}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k}\\
:=&L_{k,n}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})-E_{1,k,n}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})-E_{2,k,n}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}).\end{split}

In the following, we will simply write these terms as
L_{k,n},E_{1,k,n}
, and
E_{2,k,n}
.
The main task of the article is to prove the following lemma to control the integration of the leading order term
L_{k,n}

and the error terms
E_{1,k,n}
and
E_{2,k,n}
.

Lemma 4.2

For any interval
I\subset(-2+\epsilon,2-\epsilon)
, we have the convergence

(4.7)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{I^{k}\cap\Omega_{k}}L_{k,n}\mathrm{d}\lambda_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\lambda_{k}=\left(\dfrac{1}{{540\pi^{2}}}\int_{A}u^{4}\mathrm{d}u\right)^{k}\int_{I^{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(2\pi\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})\right)^{6}\mathrm{d}\lambda_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\lambda_{k}.

We also have the negligibility of the error terms

(4.8)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{I^{k}\cap\Omega_{k}}E_{1,k,n}\mathrm{d}\lambda_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\lambda_{k}=0,

and

(4.9)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{I^{k}\cap\Omega_{k}}E_{2,k,n}\mathrm{d}\lambda_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\lambda_{k}=0.

Lemma
4.1
is a direct consequence of (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) for the special case
k=1
, which, in turn, implies Lemma
1.3
as mentioned earlier at the beginning of this section.

Recall the relation between the correlations function and the factorial moment, we have

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{|\widetilde{\xi}_{n}(I)|!}{\left(|\widetilde{\xi}_{n}(I)|-k\right)!}\right)=\int_{I^{k}}\widetilde{\rho}_{k}d\lambda_{1}\cdots d\lambda_{k}=\int_{I^{k}\cap\Omega_{k}^{c}}\cdots+\int_{I^{k}\cap\Omega_{k}}\cdots.

This together with (
4.2
), (
4.3
), (
4.6
) and Lemma
4.2
will imply Lemma
1.4
.

Therefore, to prove Theorem
1.1
for GSE, it is sufficient to prove Lemma
4.2
. We will
prove (
4.7
) in Section
5
.
The limits (
4.8
) and (
4.9
)
will be established in Sections
6.1
and
6.2
, respectively.

5.
Estimates of the leading order term

In this section, we prove (
4.7
). Recall that

L_{k,n}=\int_{\lambda_{1}+A_{n}}\cdots\int_{\lambda_{k}+A_{n}}\rho_{2k}(\lambda_{1},x_{1},...,\lambda_{k},x_{k})\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k}.

By the Pfaffian structure of GSE in (
3.1
), the correlation function is

\rho_{2k}\left(\lambda_{1},x_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k},x_{k}\right)=\text{Pf }\mathbf{M}_{4k}.

Here,

\mathbf{M}_{4k}
is an anti-symmetric
4k\times 4k
matrix:

(5.1)

\mathbf{M}_{4k}:=\left(M_{i,j}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq k},\quad M_{i,j}=\begin{bmatrix}JK_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})&JK_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{j})\\
JK_{n}(x_{i},\lambda_{j})&JK_{n}(x_{i},x_{j})\end{bmatrix},

where each block
M_{i,j}
is a
4\times 4
matrix.

Recall the definition of
\mathcal{P}_{4k}
in (
2.2
),
we define its
diagonal subset

\mathcal{D}_{4k}:=\left\{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{4k}:\lceil\sigma_{2i-1}/4\rceil=\lceil\sigma_{2i}/4\rceil,i=1,...,2k\right\},

where
\lceil a\rceil
is the smallest integer greater than or equal to
a
.
We can thus decompose

(5.2)

\text{Pf }\mathbf{M}_{4k}:=\text{Pf}_{D}\mathbf{M}_{4k}+\text{Pf}_{O}\mathbf{M}_{4k},

where

\text{Pf}_{D}\mathbf{M}_{4k}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{4k}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\mathbf{M}_{4k}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\mathbf{M}_{4k}(\sigma_{3},\sigma_{4})\cdots\mathbf{M}_{4k}(\sigma_{4k-1},\sigma_{4k})=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\text{Pf }M_{i,i}

and

\text{Pf}_{O}\mathbf{M}_{4k}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{4k}\backslash\mathcal{D}_{4k}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\mathbf{M}_{4k}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\mathbf{M}_{4k}(\sigma_{3},\sigma_{4})\cdots\mathbf{M}_{4k}(\sigma_{4k-1},\sigma_{4k}).

5.1.
On-diagonal part
\text{Pf}_{D}\mathbf{M}_{4k}

For the on-diagonal part, we will prove

Lemma 5.1

Uniformly in
(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{k})\in I^{k}
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\int_{\lambda_{i}+A_{n}}\text{Pf }M_{i,i}\mathrm{d}x_{i}=\left(\dfrac{1}{540\pi^{2}}\int_{A}u^{4}\mathrm{d}u\right)^{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(2\pi\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})\right)^{6}.

Proof.

For the diagonal block
M_{i,i}
, by (
3.3
), its Pfaffian reads

(5.3)

\begin{split}\text{Pf }{M}_{i,i}=&S_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i})S_{n}(x_{i},x_{i})-S_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i})S_{n}(x_{i},\lambda_{i})+J_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i})V_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i})\\
=&S_{n}(x_{i},x_{i})\int_{\lambda_{i}}^{x_{i}}V_{n}(\lambda_{i},t)\mathrm{d}t-S_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i})\int_{\lambda_{i}}^{x_{i}}V_{n}(x_{i},t)\mathrm{d}t\\
&+J_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i})V_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i}).\end{split}

By Lemma
3.1
and the bound
\left|x_{i}-\lambda_{i}\right|\lesssim n^{-6/5}
, we have the uniform estimates,

S_{n}(x_{i},x_{i})=n\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})+O(n^{1/2}),

and

\displaystyle\int_{\lambda_{i}}^{x_{i}}V_{n}(\lambda_{i},t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x_{i}-\lambda_{i}}n\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})\Big(\partial_{t}\left[K_{\sin}(-2n\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})t)\right]+O\left(1\right)\Big)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=n\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})(K_{\sin}(2u_{i})-1)+O\left(n^{-1/5}\right),

where
u_{i}:=n\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})(x_{i}-\lambda_{i})\lesssim n^{-1/5}
and thus

n\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})(K_{\sin}(2u_{i})-1)\lesssim n^{3/5}
.
This will yield the estimate for the first term in (
5.3
) as follows,

\displaystyle S_{n}(x_{i},x_{i})\int_{\lambda_{i}}^{x_{i}}V_{n}(\lambda_{i},t)\mathrm{d}t-n^{2}\rho^{2}_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})\left(K_{\sin}(2u_{i})-1\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle n\cdot n^{-1/5}+n^{1/2}\cdot n^{3/5}+n^{1/2}\cdot n^{-1/5}\lesssim n^{11/10}.

The estimates of the other two terms in (
5.3
) can be derived in the same way. In the end we obtain the following uniform estimate

\text{Pf }M_{i,i}=n^{2}\rho_{\text{sc}}^{2}\left(\lambda_{i}\right)\left(1-K^{2}_{\sin}(2u_{i})+\partial_{u_{i}}[K_{\sin}(2u_{i})]\int^{u_{i}}_{0}K_{\sin}(2t)\mathrm{d}t\right)+O\left(n^{11/10}\right).

Note that the Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}({A_{n}})
is
O\left(n^{-6/5}\right)
. Recall the sine kernel in (
3.6
), if we further apply the Taylor expansion

K_{\sin}(t)=1-\frac{\pi^{2}t^{2}}{6}+\frac{\pi^{4}t^{4}}{120}+O\left(t^{6}\right)\mbox{ as }t\to 0,

we will complete the proof of the lemma as follows,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\int_{\lambda_{i}+A_{n}}\text{Pf }M_{i,i}\mathrm{d}x_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left[\int_{n^{-1/5}\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})A}n\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})\left(\dfrac{16\pi^{4}u_{i}^{4}}{135}+O\left(u_{i}^{5}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}u_{i}+O\left(n^{-1/10}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\int_{A}\dfrac{u^{4}}{540\pi^{2}}\mathrm{d}u\right)^{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(2\pi\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})\right)^{6}.

∎

5.2.
Two rounds of transformations

In order to bound the off-diagonal part
\text{Pf}_{O}\mathbf{M}_{4k}
, we need to bound all its entries. Note that Pfaffian is invariant under the congruent transformations,
we can perform the following
Round 1 transformation
to reduce the order of the entries of
\mathbf{M}_{4k}
without changing its Pfaffian.

-

Calculate
(\lambda_{i+1}-x_{i+1})
times the
(4i+2)
-th row, and add the result to the
(4i+1)
-th row for
i=0,...,k-1
. Then perform the same operations to the columns.

-

Then subtract the
(4i+1)
-th row from the
(4i+3)
-th row, and subtract the
(4i+2)
-th row from the
(4i+4)
-th row for
i=0,...,k-1
. Then perform the same operations to the columns.

We shall use a superscript
(R)
to denote the matrix after Round 1 transformation. Now we have,

\displaystyle\text{Pf }\mathbf{M}_{4k}

\displaystyle=\text{Pf }\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(R)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{4k}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(R)}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(R)}(\sigma_{3},\sigma_{4})\cdots\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(R)}(\sigma_{4k-1},\sigma_{4k}).

Since off-diagonal
4\times 4
blocks have no influences on diagonal
4\times 4
blocks during the transformation, we have

\text{Pf}_{D}\mathbf{M}_{4k}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\text{Pf }M_{i,i}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\text{Pf }M^{(R)}_{i,i}=\text{Pf}_{D}\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(R)}.

Consequently,

\text{Pf}_{O}\mathbf{M}_{4k}=\text{Pf }\mathbf{M}_{4k}-\text{Pf}_{D}\mathbf{M}_{4k}=\text{Pf }\mathbf{M}^{(R)}_{4k}-\text{Pf}_{D}\mathbf{M}^{(R)}_{4k}=\text{Pf}_{O}\mathbf{M}^{(R)}_{4k}.

As will be clear later, it will be more convenient to bound
\text{Pf}_{O}\mathbf{M}_{4k}
if we make the following
Round 2 transformation
for
\mathbf{M}^{(R)}
: by multiplying certain factors, we define

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(F)}(i,j)=:\left\{\begin{aligned} n^{-6/5}\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(R)}(i,j),&\quad&&\text{for $i,j$ both even,}\\
n^{6/5}\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(R)}(i,j),&\quad&&\text{for $i,j$ both odd,}\\
\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(R)}(i,j),&\quad&&\text{for all other $i,j$.}\end{aligned}\right.

For any
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{4k}
,
we have

\displaystyle|\{1\leq\ell\leq 2k:\sigma_{2\ell-1}\mbox{ and }\sigma_{2\ell}\mbox{ are both odd}\}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\{1\leq\ell\leq 2k:\sigma_{2\ell-1}\mbox{ and }\sigma_{2\ell}\mbox{ are both even}\}|.

And thus

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(R)}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(R)}(\sigma_{3},\sigma_{4})\cdots\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(R)}(\sigma_{4k-1},\sigma_{4k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(F)}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(F)}(\sigma_{3},\sigma_{4})\cdots\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(F)}(\sigma_{4k-1},\sigma_{4k}).

This further implies that

(5.5)

\text{Pf}_{D}\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(F)}=\text{Pf}_{D}\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(R)}=\text{Pf}_{D}\mathbf{M}_{4k},\quad\text{Pf}_{O}\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(F)}=\text{Pf}_{O}\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(R)}=\text{Pf}_{O}\mathbf{M}_{4k}.

Hence, it suffices to bound
\mathbf{M}^{(F)}_{4k}
. For this purpose we need the following lemma, which will be used to control the integral of
\text{Pf}_{O}\mathbf{M}_{4k}
in Subsection
5.3
. We first define the following subset of
I^{k}
,

(5.6)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}=:\left\{(\lambda_{1},x_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k},x_{k})\in I^{2k}:\begin{aligned} \left|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\right|>&\log^{-1}n\text{ for }1\leq i<j\leq k;\\
x_{i}\in&\lambda_{i}+A_{n}\text{ for }i=1,...,k\end{aligned}\right\}.

Then we have

Lemma 5.2

For
\lambda_{i}\in I
and
x_{i}\in\lambda_{i}+A_{n}
for
i=1,...,k
, the diagonal block
M_{i,i}^{(F)}
has the upper bound,

(5.7)

\displaystyle\left|M_{i,i}^{(F)}\right|\lesssim\begin{bmatrix}0&n&n^{3/5}&n^{3/5}\\
n&0&n^{3/5}&n^{3/5}\\
n^{3/5}&n^{3/5}&0&n^{1/5}\\
n^{3/5}&n^{3/5}&n^{1/5}&0\end{bmatrix}.

On
\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}
the off-diagonal part
M^{(F)}_{i,j}(i<j)
can be controlled by

(5.8)

\displaystyle\left|M^{(F)}_{i,j}\right|\lesssim\begin{bmatrix}n^{6/5}\log n&n^{1/2}&n^{3/10}&n^{3/10}\\
n^{1/2}&n^{-1/5}\log n&n^{-2/5}\log n&n^{-2/5}\log n\\
n^{3/10}&n^{-2/5}\log n&n^{-1/5}\log n&n^{-2/5}\log n\\
n^{3/10}&n^{-2/5}\log n&n^{-2/5}\log n&n^{-3/5}\log n\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

Let
M_{i,j,11}^{(R)},M_{i,j,12}^{(R)},M_{i,j,21}^{(R)},M_{i,j,22}^{(R)}
be the upper left, upper right, lower left and lower right
2\times 2
blocks of
M^{(R)}_{i,j}
after the Round 1 transformation, respectively.

We first prove (
5.7
). Recall (
5.1
), the original on-diagonal blocks are

\displaystyle{M}_{i,i}=\begin{bmatrix}0&S_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i})&J_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i})&S_{n}(x_{i},\lambda_{i})\\
-S_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i})&0&-S_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i})&-V_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i})\\
J_{n}(x_{i},\lambda_{i})&S_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i})&0&S_{n}(x_{i},x_{i})\\
-S_{n}(x_{i},\lambda_{i})&-V_{n}(x_{i},\lambda_{i})&-S_{n}(x_{i},x_{i})&0\end{bmatrix}

After the Round 1 transformation, the (1,1)-entry of
M_{i,i,12}^{(R)}
is

\displaystyle\int_{x_{i}}^{\lambda_{i}}S_{n}(t,x_{i})\mathrm{d}t-(\lambda_{i}-x_{i})S_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i})=

\displaystyle(\lambda_{i}-x_{i})S_{n}(\xi,x_{i})-(\lambda_{i}-x_{i})S_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\lambda_{i}-x_{i})(\lambda_{i}-\xi)\partial_{\gamma}S_{n}(\gamma,x_{i}),

where
\xi\in[\lambda_{i},x_{i}]
and
\gamma\in[\lambda_{i},\xi]
. By (
3.15
) and (
3.16
) we have

\sup_{\left|x-y\right|\leq\sup(A_{n})}\left|\partial_{x}S_{n}(x,y)\right|=O\left(n^{9/5}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{\left|x-y\right|\leq\sup(A_{n})}\left|V_{n}(x,y)\right|=O\left(n^{9/5}\right).

This implies that the (1,1)-entry of
M_{i,i,12}^{(R)}
is of order
O\left(n^{-3/5}\right)
. The (1,2)-entry of
M_{i,i,12}^{(R)}
is given by

\displaystyle S_{n}(x_{i},\lambda_{i})-S_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i})-(\lambda_{i}-x_{i})V_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i})=(x_{i}-\lambda_{i})\partial_{\xi}S_{n}(\xi,\lambda_{i})+O\left(n^{3/5}\right),

which is of order
O\left(n^{3/5}\right)
. Similar analysis works for the (2,1) and (2,2) entries of
M_{i,i}^{(R)}
, and we
get the estimate

\displaystyle\left|M^{(R)}_{i,i,12}\right|\lesssim\begin{bmatrix}n^{-3/5}&n^{3/5}\\
n^{3/5}&n^{9/5}\end{bmatrix}.

Due to the anti-symmetry of
M_{i,i}^{(R)}
,
M^{(R)}_{i,i,21}
has the same order estimate as above. For
M^{(R)}_{i,i,22}
, its (1,2)-entry is

\displaystyle S_{n}(x_{i},x_{i})+S_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i})-S_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i})-S_{n}(x_{i},\lambda_{i})+(x_{i}-\lambda_{i})V_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i})

(5.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\lambda_{i}}^{x_{i}}V_{n}(x_{i},t)\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\lambda_{i}}^{x_{i}}V_{n}(\lambda_{i},t)\mathrm{d}t+(x_{i}-\lambda_{i})V_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i}).

By the estimate (
3.9
), we have

\displaystyle\left|V_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{i})-n^{2}\rho^{2}_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}[K_{\sin}(2u)]\right|=O\left(n\right),

where
u:=n\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})(\lambda_{i}-x_{i})\lesssim n^{-1/5}
.
Then (
5.9
) can be written as

\displaystyle-2n\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})\int_{0}^{u}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}[K_{\sin}(2t)]\right)\mathrm{d}t+n\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})u\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}[K_{\sin}(2u)]+O\left(n^{-1/5}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2n\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})\left(1-K_{\sin}(2u)+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}[K_{\sin}(2u)]\right)+O\left(n^{-1/5}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{4n\rho_{\text{sc}}(\lambda_{i})u^{4}}{15}+O\left(n^{-1/5}\right).

Hence we get

\displaystyle\left|M_{i,i,22}^{(R)}\right|\lesssim\begin{bmatrix}0&n^{1/5}\\
n^{1/5}&0\end{bmatrix}.

Combining above estimates, we have the following bound for the diagonal block
M_{i,i}^{(R)}

(1\leq i\leq k)
after the Round 1 transformation,

(5.10)

\displaystyle\left|M_{i,i}^{(R)}\right|\lesssim\begin{bmatrix}0&n&n^{-3/5}&n^{3/5}\\
n&0&n^{3/5}&n^{9/5}\\
n^{-3/5}&n^{3/5}&0&n^{1/5}\\
n^{3/5}&n^{9/5}&n^{1/5}&0\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore, (
5.7
) follows from (
5.10
)
and the definition of Round 2 transformation (
5.4
)

Now we prove (
5.8
).
Recall (
5.1
) again, the original off-diagonal blocks are

\displaystyle M_{i,j}=\begin{bmatrix}J_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})&S_{n}(\lambda_{j},\lambda_{i})&J_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{j})&S_{n}(x_{j},\lambda_{i})\\
-S_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})&-V_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})&-S_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{j})&-V_{n}(\lambda_{i},x_{j})\\
J_{n}(x_{i},\lambda_{j})&S_{n}(\lambda_{j},x_{i})&J_{n}(x_{i},x_{j})&S_{n}(x_{j},x_{i})\\
-S_{n}(x_{i},\lambda_{j})&-V_{n}(x_{i},\lambda_{j})&-S_{n}(x_{i},x_{j})&-V_{n}(x_{i},x_{j})\end{bmatrix}.

We consider the upper left
2\times 2
block
M_{i,j,11}^{(R)}
of
M^{(R)}_{i,j}
after the Round 1 transformation.
Its entries are

\displaystyle M_{i,j,11}^{(R)}(1,1)=

\displaystyle J_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})-(\lambda_{i}-x_{i})S_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})+(\lambda_{j}-x_{j})S_{n}(\lambda_{j},\lambda_{i})

\displaystyle-(\lambda_{j}-x_{j})(\lambda_{i}-x_{i})V_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}),

\displaystyle M_{i,j,11}^{(R)}(1,2)=

\displaystyle S_{n}(\lambda_{j},\lambda_{i})-(\lambda_{i}-x_{i})V_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}),

\displaystyle M_{i,j,11}^{(R)}(2,1)=

\displaystyle-(\lambda_{j}-x_{j})V_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})-S_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}),

\displaystyle M_{i,j,11}^{(R)}(2,2)=

\displaystyle-V_{n}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}).

The requirement that
x_{i}\in\lambda_{i}+A_{n}
implies

\left|\lambda_{i}-x_{i}\right|\leq\sup(A_{n})(=\sup\{x:x\in A_{n}\})\lesssim n^{-6/5}.

In addition,
using (
5.6
) where
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\in\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}
, for all
i\neq j
, we have

\min\left\{\left|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}\right|,\left|\lambda_{j}-x_{i}\right|,\left|x_{j}-\lambda_{i}\right|,\left|x_{i}-x_{j}\right|\right\}\geq\log^{-1}n-2\sup(A_{n})\geq\frac{1}{2}\log^{-1}n.

Then by the estimates (
3.14
), (
3.16
) and (
3.19
),
we get

\displaystyle\left|M_{i,j,11}^{(R)}\right|\lesssim\begin{bmatrix}\log n&n^{1/2}\\
n^{1/2}&n\log n\end{bmatrix}.

Other parts of
M^{(R)}_{i,j}
can be bounded in the same manner; therefore, we omit the details. This leads us to the final estimate,

(5.11)

\displaystyle\left|M^{(R)}_{i,j}\right|\lesssim\begin{bmatrix}\log n&n^{1/2}&n^{-9/10}&n^{3/10}\\
n^{1/2}&n\log n&n^{-2/5}\log n&n^{4/5}\log n\\
n^{-9/10}&n^{-2/5}\log n&n^{-7/5}\log n&n^{-2/5}\log n\\
n^{3/10}&n^{4/5}\log n&n^{-2/5}\log n&n^{3/5}\log n\end{bmatrix}.

Now (
5.8
) follows from (
5.11
) and the definition of the Round 2 transformation (
5.4
).

∎

5.3.
Off-diagonal part
\text{Pf}_{O}\mathbf{M}_{4k}

Next, we prove the following lemma, which indicates that the contribution from the off-diagonal blocks is negligible.

Lemma 5.3

Uniformly in
(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{k})\in\Omega^{k}
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\lambda_{1}+A_{n}}\cdots\int_{\lambda_{k}+A_{n}}\left|\text{Pf}_{O}\mathbf{M}_{4k}\right|\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k}=0.

We first construct a
4k\times 4k
matrix
\mathbf{U}_{4k}
in the block form

\mathbf{U}_{4k}=\left(U_{i,j}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq k},

where
U_{i,i}
and
U_{i,j}
are
4\times 4
upper bound matrices given by the right-hand side of (
5.7
) and (
5.8

|\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(F)}(i,j)|\lesssim\mathbf{U}_{4k}(i,j).

For example,

\mathbf{U}_{4k}(i,j)=n^{6/5}\log n
if
i\equiv j\equiv 1
(mod 4) and
i\neq j.

We set

(5.12)

Let
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}
be a 0-1 matrix of size

\sigma

(5.13)

\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(i,j)=1\Leftrightarrow\exists\,\ell\mbox{ s.t. }(\sigma_{2\ell-1},\sigma_{2\ell})=(i,j).

We rewrite
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}=(\Lambda_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq k}
where each

is a

\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}
into diagonal regimes (denoted by a letter ‘
D
’) and off-diagonal regimes (denoted by a letter ‘

as described in (
5.7
) and (
5.8
). The diagonal part contains 4 regimes:

\displaystyle D_{1}:=

\displaystyle\left\{(i,j)\in[4k]\times[4k]:\lceil i/4\rceil=\lceil j/4\rceil,\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(i,j)=1,i\equiv 1(\text{mod }4),\,j\equiv 2(\text{mod }4)\right\},

\displaystyle D_{2}:=

\displaystyle D_{3}:=

\displaystyle\left\{(i,j)\in[4k]\times[4k]:\lceil i/4\rceil=\lceil j/4\rceil,\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(i,j)=1,i\equiv 2(\text{mod }4),\,j\equiv 3,4(\text{mod }4)\right\},

\displaystyle D_{4}:=

\displaystyle\left\{(i,j)\in[4k]\times[4k]:\lceil i/4\rceil=\lceil j/4\rceil,\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(i,j)=1,i\equiv 3(\text{mod }4)\right\},

where

.

, providing the information on which nonzero entries belong to each

where

(5.15)

\begin{split}\text{Ord}_{1}:=&|D_{1}|+\dfrac{3}{5}|D_{2}|+\dfrac{3}{5}|D_{3}|+\dfrac{1}{5}|D_{4}|\\

Lemma 5.4

For any
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{4k}\backslash\mathcal{D}_{4k}
, it holds that

(5.16)

\text{Ord}_{1}<6k/5.

Assuming Lemma
5.4
, we can complete the proof of Lemma
5.3
.

Proof of Lemma
5.3
.

Recall the set
\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}
in (
5.6
), its Lebesgue measure satisfies

\mathcal{L}({\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}})\lesssim\mathcal{L}({A_{n}})^{k}\lesssim n^{-6k/5}.

This, together with (
5.16
), implies that for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{4k}\backslash\mathcal{D}_{4k}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}}\left|\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(F)}(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(F)}(\sigma_{3},\sigma_{4})\cdots\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(F)}(\sigma_{4k-1},\sigma_{4k})\right|\mathrm{d}\lambda_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\lambda_{k}\mathrm{d}x_{k}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}}U_{4k}(\sigma)\mathrm{d}\lambda_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}\lambda_{k}\mathrm{d}x_{k}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\left(\log^{C}n\right)n^{-6k/5+\text{Ord}_{1}},

which converges to 0 as
n\to\infty
.
Here, we have used the fact that

\left|\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(F)}(i,j)\right|\lesssim\mathbf{U}_{4k}(i,j)
for all
1\leq i,j\leq 4k
. This implies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\lambda_{1}+A_{n}}\cdots\int_{\lambda_{k}+A_{n}}\left|\text{Pf}_{O}\mathbf{M}_{4k}^{(F)}\right|\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k}=0,

which completes the proof by (
5.5
).
∎

Now we prove Lemma
5.4
.

Proof of Lemma
5.4
.

The starting point of the proof is to derive some identities which hold for all
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{4k}
. Since the two sets
\{\sigma_{i},1\leq i\leq 4k\}
and
[4k]
are the same, if we group
\sigma_{i}^{\prime}s
according to their congruence classes modulo 4, then for each
\ell=0,1,2,3
,

(5.17)

\left|\{i\in[4k]:\sigma_{i}\equiv\ell(\text{mod }4)\}\right|=\left|\{i\in[4k]:i\equiv\ell(\text{mod }4)\}\right|=k.

We can also rewrite the left hand hand of (
5.17
) via

(5.18)

\begin{split}\left|\{i\in[4k]:\sigma_{i}\equiv\ell(\text{mod }4)\}\right|=&\left|\{(i,j):\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(i,j)=1,i\equiv\ell,j\not\equiv\ell(\mathrm{mode}\,4)\}\right|\\
&+\left|\{(i,j):\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(i,j)=1,i\not\equiv\ell,j\equiv\ell(\mathrm{mode}\,4)\}\right|\\
&+2\left|\{(i,j):\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(i,j)=1,i\equiv j\equiv\ell(\mathrm{mode}\,4)\}\right|.\end{split}

Consider first the case
\ell=1
. By the definition of the sets
D_{i}^{\prime}s
and
O_{i}^{\prime}s
, we see that sum of the first and second lines on the right hand side of
(
5.18
) is equal to
\left|D_{1}\right|+\left|O_{2}\right|+\left|O_{3}\right|
, while the third lines are
2\left|O_{1}\right|
. It follows from this observation and (
5.17
) (with
\ell=1
) that

(5.19)

2|O_{1}|+|O_{2}|+|O_{3}|+|D_{1}|+|D_{2}|=k.

Similarly, for
\ell=2
, we get

(5.20)

|O_{2}|+2|O_{4,1}|+|O_{5,1}|+|D_{1}|+|D_{3}|=k.

Now, combining the cases of
\ell=3
and
\ell=4
, we get

(5.21)

|O_{3}|+2|O_{4,2}|+|O_{5,1}|+2|O_{5,2}|+2|O_{6}|+|D_{2}|+|D_{3}|+2|D_{4}|=2k.

We will need one more identity. Since the number of nonzero
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(i,j)^{\prime}s
is
2k
, we have

(5.22)

\left|D_{1}\right|+\left|D_{2}\right|+\left|D_{3}\right|+\left|D_{4}\right|+\left|O_{1}\right|+\left|O_{2}\right|+|O_{3}|+\left|O_{4}\right|+\left|O_{5}\right|+\left|O_{6}\right|=2k.

By computing the linear combination

\frac{3}{5}\times\eqref{cons1}+\frac{3}{5}\times\eqref{cons2}+\frac{1}{5}\times\eqref{cons3}-\frac{1}{5}\times\eqref{cons4},

we find that

(5.23)

\begin{split}\frac{6k}{5}=&|D_{1}|+\frac{3}{5}|D_{2}|+\frac{3}{5}|D_{3}|+\frac{1}{5}|D_{4}|+|O_{1}|+|O_{2}|\\
&+\frac{3}{5}|O_{3}|+\left|O_{4,1}\right|+\frac{1}{5}|O_{4,2}|+\frac{3}{5}|O_{5,1}|+\frac{1}{5}|O_{5,2}|+\frac{1}{5}|O_{6}|.\end{split}

Comparing (
5.15
) and (
5.23
), we see that the assertion
\text{Ord}_{1}<6k/5
for
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{4k}/\mathcal{D}_{4k}
is equivalent to

(5.24)

\left|O_{1}\right|<\frac{3}{2}\left|O_{3}\right|+2\left|O_{4,2}\right|+\frac{5}{2}\left|O_{2}\right|+3\left|O_{5,2}\right|+4\left|O_{6}\right|+5\left|O_{5,1}\right|+6\left|O_{4,1}\right|.

Actually, we shall prove a stronger conclusion for
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{4k}
:

(5.25)

\left|O_{1}\right|\leq\left|O_{2}\right|+|O_{3}|+\left|O_{4}\right|+\left|O_{5}\right|+\left|O_{6}\right|.

Indeed, assuming that (
5.25
) holds, then (
5.24
) follows by combining (
5.25
) and the trivial fact
\sum_{i=1}^{6}\left|O_{i}\right|>0
for
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{4k}/\mathcal{D}_{4k}
.

We now provide an intuitive explanation of (
5.25
). Suppose that
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(1,5)=1
, i.e.,
(1,5)\in O_{1}
, then there must exist at least one element
b\in\{2,3,4\}
and
b^{\prime}>5
such that
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(b,b^{\prime})=1
, leading to
(b,b^{\prime})\in\cup_{i=2}^{6}O_{i}
. This suggests that the right hand side of (
5.25
) should dominate
\left|O_{1}\right|
.

To prove (
5.25
) formally, we observe that, by the construction of
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}
, for each
1\leq i\leq 4k
,

(5.26)

\sum_{j:j\neq i}\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(i,j)+\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(j,i)\right)=1.

Summing this equality over all
4\ell-3\leq i\leq 4\ell
for some
1\leq\ell\leq k
, and using the fact that
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(i,j)=0
for
i\geq j
, we get

(5.27)

2\sum_{\lceil i/4\rceil=\lceil j/4\rceil=\ell}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(i,j)+\sum_{\lceil i/4\rceil=\ell<\lceil j/4\rceil}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(i,j)+\sum_{\lceil j/4\rceil<\lceil i/4\rceil=\ell}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(j,i)=4.

We now define a function
h_{\sigma}
as follows:

(5.28)

h_{\sigma}(\ell):=\sum_{\lceil i/4\rceil=\ell<\lceil j/4\rceil}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(i,j)+\sum_{\lceil j/4\rceil<\lceil i/4\rceil=\ell}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(j,i),\,1\leq\ell\leq k.

By (
5.27
),
h_{\sigma}(\ell)
is an even integer for all
\ell
. In particular, we have the implications

(5.29)

\begin{split}&\forall\,1\leq\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}\leq k,(4\ell_{1}-3,4\ell_{2}-3)\in O_{1}\Rightarrow\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(4\ell_{1}-3,4\ell_{2}-3)=1\\
&\Rightarrow\min\{h_{\sigma}(\ell_{1}),h_{\sigma}(\ell_{2})\}\geq 1\Rightarrow\min\{h_{\sigma}(\ell_{1}),h_{\sigma}(\ell_{2})\}\geq 2,\end{split}

where we used the evenness of
h_{\sigma}(\ell_{1})
and
h_{\sigma}(\ell_{2})
in the last step.
Consider the set

(5.30)

\widehat{O}_{1}:=\{1\leq\ell\leq k:\exists\ell^{\prime}\in[1,k],\mbox{ s.t. }(4\min\{\ell,\ell^{\prime}\}-3,4\max\{\ell,\ell^{\prime}\}-3)\in O_{1}\}.

It follows from (
5.28
) and (
5.29
) that

(5.31)

\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq k}h_{\sigma}(\ell)\geq\sum_{\ell\in\widehat{O}_{1}}h_{\sigma}(\ell)\geq 2\left|\widehat{O}_{1}\right|=4|O_{1}|.

On the other hand, we also have

(5.32)

\begin{split}\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq k}h_{\sigma}(\ell)&=\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq k}\sum_{\lceil i/4\rceil=\ell<\lceil j/4\rceil}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(i,j)+\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq k}\sum_{\lceil j/4\rceil<\lceil i/4\rceil=\ell}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(j,i)\\
&=\sum_{\lceil i/4\rceil<\lceil j/4\rceil}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(i,j)+\sum_{\lceil j/4\rceil<\lceil i/4\rceil}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{4k}(j,i)=2\sum_{j=1}^{6}\left|O_{j}\right|.\end{split}

Now (
5.25
) follows by combining (
5.31
) and (
5.32
). And thus we completes the proof of Lemma
5.4
.
∎

7.
The case of GOE

7.1.
Pfaffian structure of GOE

GOE is a Gaussian measure on the space of Hermitian matrices. The joint density of eigenvalues of GOE is given by (
1.1
) with
\beta=1
. In this section, we use a superscript (1) to denote various quantities regarding GOE.
The
k
-point correlation function of eigenvalues of GOE is
[
14
]

(7.1)

\displaystyle\rho^{(1)}_{k,n}(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{k})=\text{Pf }\left(JK_{n}^{(1)}(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq k},

where

(7.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} J=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{bmatrix},\quad K_{n}^{(1)}(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}S^{(1)}_{n}(x,y)+\alpha_{n}(x)&V^{(1)}_{n}(x,y)\\
J^{(1)}_{n}(x,y)&S^{(1)}_{n}(y,x)+\alpha_{n}(y)\end{bmatrix}.\end{aligned}

Here,

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}(x)=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sqrt{2m+1}\varphi_{2m}\left(\sqrt{2m+1}x\right)\big/\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{2m}(t)\mathrm{d}t,&&\quad\text{if }n=2m+1,\\
&0,&&\quad\text{if }n=2m,\end{aligned}\right.

and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S_{n}^{(1)}(x,y)&=\sqrt{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\varphi_{j}\left(\sqrt{n}x\right)\varphi_{j}\left(\sqrt{n}y\right)+\frac{n}{2}\varphi_{n-1}\left(\sqrt{n}x\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varepsilon(\sqrt{n}y-t)\varphi_{n}(t)\mathrm{d}t,\\
V_{n}^{(1)}(x,y)&=-\frac{\partial}{\partial y}S_{n}^{(1)}(x,y),\\
J_{n}^{(1)}(x,y)&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varepsilon(x-t)S^{(1)}_{n}(t,y)\mathrm{d}t-\varepsilon(x-y)+\gamma_{n}(x)-\gamma_{n}(y),\end{aligned}

where

\gamma_{n}(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varepsilon(x-t)\alpha_{n}(t)\mathrm{d}t,

and

\varepsilon(x)=(1/2)\operatorname{sign}(x)
is
1/2
,
-1/2
or
0
according as
x>0
,
x<0
or
x=0
,

Let
\widehat{K}^{(1)}_{n}
be the rescaled version of

defined as in (

).
As in the proof of Lemma
3.1
, using the result in

n^{-1/2}&n^{-1/2}\end{bmatrix}
