Succinate Dehydrogenase Pathogenic Variants Among Older Adults With Head and Neck Paragangliomas
Authors: Nikhil Bellamkonda, Anne Naumer, Luke O. Buchmann, Hilary C. McCrary, Mana Espahbodi, Neil S. Patel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe objective of this study was to determine the proportion of patients over the age of 50 who have HNPGL related to an SDH PV.MethodsPatients seen at a single, tertiary‐care academic medical center diagnosed with a new HNPGL over the age of 50 were included and analyzed.ResultsOne hundred and eleven individuals (155 tumors) were included. Introduction

Paragangliomas (PGLs), neuroendocrine tumors arising from sympathetic and parasympathetic ganglia, present in the head and neck up to 70% of the time [1]. The most common of these are carotid body tumors (CBT), though other frequent presentations include jugular paraganglioma (JP), glomus tympanicum (GT) (middle ear paragangliomas), and vagal paragangliomas (VP) [2]. PGLs can be caused by mutations in various genes known to be associated with these tumors. Up to 40% of PGLs have predisposing germline mutations, and 25%–30% are secondary to somatic mutations [3]. These genes are separated into two clusters: cluster 1 comprising genes associated with the pseudohypoxic pathway (succinyl dehydrogenase (SDH) subunits, VHL, FH) and cluster 2 with genes that encode elements of the kinase‐signaling pathway (NF1, TMEM127, MAX) [4, 5]. In certain individuals these can be inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, resulting in a phenomenon known as hereditary paraganglioma/pheochromocytoma syndrome (PGL/PCC). Certain mutations, such as SDHD and SDHAF2, are almost exclusively paternally inherited [6].

Given the propensity for mutations to be related to PGLs, genetic testing has become a critical component of the work up and management of head and neck PGLs (HNPGL). However, age‐related testing recommendations are highly variable. The National Cancer Institute website recommends against testing in patients over the age of 50, and in fact Erlic et al. found that patients with pheochromocytoma less than 45 years old were five times more likely to have a genetic mutation compared to patients over 45 years old [7, 8]. Williams found that hereditary PGLs tend to present around a decade earlier than sporadic tumors [9]. Another study found that among patients with SDHB, SDHC, and SDHD mutations, 38%–60% develop PGLs by 35 years old [10]. However, a prior Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline on PGL management did not set a cutoff age for genetic testing, and more recent guidelines from Europe suggest testing of all patients [8, 11].

With this variability in genetic testing recommendations, decision making in older patients presenting with HNPGLs is difficult. There is minimal data on the incidence of SDH mutations in older individuals specifically, which limits evidence‐based counseling in older patients diagnosed with HNPGL. Thus, the objective of this study was to determine the proportion of patients over the age of 50 who have a head and neck PGL related to an SDH pathogenic variant (PV), to better define this cohort and inform genetic testing and counseling guidelines in this population.

Methods

This was a single institution, retrospective review conducted at a tertiary care academic medical center. All patients referred to a familial cancer assessment clinic between 2010 and 2024 were reviewed. All patients equal to or over the age of 50 years old diagnosed with their first HNPGL were included. Demographic data, clinical and radiographic data, and genetic testing reports were abstracted for analysis.

Germline genetic testing results were obtained from reports from external Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments–approved commercial or academic laboratories, or from the Huntsman Cancer Institute Clinical Cancer Registry Database. “Single gene testing” indicated single gene or single site gene analysis. Any broad genetic test evaluating 10 or more genes with utilization of next‐generation sequencing was defined as a “multigene panel”. All germline variants and classifications were reviewed by a certified genetic counselor. Patients were categorized as positive for a PV if they had any pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant regardless of a concomitant variant of unknown significance (VUS). Germline test results that had VUS, had benign/likely benign variants, or were negative for mutations were considered to have a negative result and were characterized as “sporadic” [12].

Approval for this study was granted by the University of Utah Institutional Review Board. Descriptive and comparative analyses (Mann–Whitney U tests and binary logistic regression analyses) were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.

Results

A total of 111 individuals diagnosed with their first HNPGL over the age of 50 were included in this study. There were 23 (20.7%) males and 88 (79.3%) females. The median age at first tumor diagnosis was 65 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 58–71). The majority of patients identified as white (n = 102, 91.9%) and two (1.8%) as Hispanic.

Among all patients, 81 (73%) had genetic data available. Among these, 70 underwent multigene panel testing, and 10 had site‐specific testing conducted. One patient had a presumed PV due to known familial PV and his clinical history. The remaining patients either declined testing or were unable to complete testing due to insurance coverage reasons. Of individuals with genetic data available, 21 (25.9%) had a PV associated with HNPGL. This included SDHA in one patient, SDHB in 11, SDHC in 5, SDHD in 3, and FH in one (Table 1). The rate of SDH PVs in the tested group was approximately 25% (20/81). The median age at first diagnosis of patients with a genetic PV was 60 years (IQR: 54–67), while the median age of patients with negative genetic testing was 67 (IQR: 59–71) (p = 0.009). Within tested patients, younger age was correlated with having a known genetic PV (OR: 0.917, 95% CI: 0.856–0.982, p = 0.013). Among the 21 patients with a HNPGL‐associated PV, two were between 50 and 59 years of age, seven between 60 and 69, eight between 70 and 79, three between 80 and 89, and one between 90 and 99 (Figure 1).

Among all patients, 155 tumors were included. Eighty‐six tumors were diagnosed due to tumor‐related symptoms, while the remainder were diagnosed incidentally on imaging or on screening/surveillance scans. This consisted of 112 (72.3%) CBTs, 26 (16.8%) JPs, 13 (8.4%) VPs, one (0.6%) GT, and three (1.9%) atypical tumors (these included PGL found on the facial nerve, spinal nerve, and lateral neck) (Figure 2). Only seven (4.5%) tumors were found to be metastatic. The median number of tumors per patient was one (IQR: 1–2). There were 33 (29.7%) patients who had greater than one tumor. There were 38 (24.5%) tumors found in patients identified with a PV in PGL‐associated genes. There was not a significant association between tumor location and the presence of a PV. Among patients who underwent genetic testing, the presence of a PGL‐associated PV was not related to having more than one tumor (OR: 0.938, 95% CI: 0.293–2.996, p = 0.91).

Distribution of tumor location.

The majority (n = 80, 51.6%) of tumors were observed. There were 66 (42.6%) treated surgically, six (3.9%) with radiation therapy, one (0.6%) with both surgery and radiation therapy, one (0.6%) with chemotherapy, and one (0.6%) with surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy. Among tumors found in patients with a known genetic PV (n = 38), 16 (42%) were observed.

Discussion

In this study investigating the frequency of PGL‐related PVs in patients over the age of 50 presenting with HNPGL, 25.9% of patients (n = 81) had an associated PV. Patients with a predisposing PV were significantly younger than those without, with a median age of 60 compared to 67. Over 70% of patients presented with a CBT. Currently, there are variable recommendations on an upper age limit for genetic testing in PGL patients. The Physician Data Query Adult Treatment Editorial Board summary on Pheochromocytoma and Paraganglioma treatment, published on the National Cancer Institute website, recommends against testing in patients presenting over the age of 50 years old [7]. However, per the findings of this study, a quarter of these patients may have an SDH mutation and family members at risk for developing PGL/PCC.

Identification of a PV can trigger predictive testing for at‐risk family members. Penetrance of SDH‐related PGL is highly variable and varies across PVs, with published rates between 8% and 37% for SDHB and 38% and 64% in SDHD [13]. Our clinic follows almost 300 HNPGL patients; among patients who have had genetic testing completed, approximately 35% have an SDH PV. In Muth et al.'s genetic testing and surveillance guidelines for PGL/PCC, it was recommended that first‐degree relatives of patients with a PV in SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, TMEM127, or MAX undergo targeted DNA testing from peripheral blood or saliva. In SDHB patients, due to the increased risk of malignancy, they recommend considering testing second‐degree relatives. Due to maternal imprinting of SDHD and SDHAF2, testing of both first‐ and second‐degree relatives was recommended [14]. At our interdisciplinary PGL/PCC clinic, genetic testing is currently offered during the initial work up of a PGL, regardless of age. With a median age of 60 in patients found to have a PGL associated PV in this cohort, the age of 50 certainly should not be used as a cut‐off for genetic testing referral.

In addition, early diagnosis of possible PGL/PCC is key for initiating active surveillance. In this cohort, almost 30% of patients were diagnosed with multiple tumors over the course of their follow up. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network recommends starting surveillance imaging and lab testing starting between 6 and 15 years old, depending on specific mutations. This includes plasma and/or urine metanephrine testing and whole body imaging [15]. Early detection of tumors can allow for intervention when tumors are smaller in size, which can lower rates of complications and preserve cranial nerve function [16]. In a recent international consensus statement, experts also felt that earlier diagnosis was associated with better long term prognosis [13]. Buffet et al. found that PGL patients who underwent genetic testing had significantly better 5‐year survival rates than those who did not, attributed to improved follow up and management tailored according to patients' genetic risk profile [17]. In particular, patients diagnosed with SDHB are at higher risk for development of malignant disease, and thus early detection can facilitate earlier surgical intervention [18].

It is true that a large proportion (42%) of tumors found in patients with a genetic PV were observed in this cohort. This may lead some to the conclusion that earlier diagnosis of a genetic predisposition is not as critical given that many tumors found in older individuals are observed regardless of whether they are sporadic or familial. However, several points warrant mention. First, this statistic represents an overall shift toward conservatism in the management paradigm of most HNPGLs, particularly those associated with an SDH mutation. Second, many HNPGLs, since they are rarely functional, are clinically silent for years prior to diagnosis. So, it is quite likely that a 60‐year‐old diagnosed with an HNPGL has harbored the tumor for several years prior to its discovery. Lastly, earlier identification in the proband leads to earlier identification of PVs in descendants, which can have significant benefits on their morbidity and survival.

Cost can be a barrier to genetic testing. However, the development of next generation sequencing has brought down costs significantly [19]. Familial testing can be done at a significantly lower cost as well. Some centers, such as our own, subsidize genetic testing costs for patients as well to make this diagnostic tool more accessible. To our knowledge, no formal cost–benefit analyses have yet been conducted for genetic testing in the context of PGL management—this may be a valuable area of future investigation.

This study had several strengths and limitations. HNPGLs are rare tumors, which limits the size of our study cohort. However, our center does follow one of the largest cohorts of paraganglioma patients in the United States and serves a large population and catchment area, providing a real‐world example of management of this condition [12, 18]. One limitation of this cohort is its ethnic homogeneity (91.9% patients identified as white), which may limit generalizability to more diverse populations. Also, almost 30% of patients who fit inclusion criteria did not follow through with genetic testing, limiting the power of this study. As commercial testing was not widely available for these genes until around 2010, patients with more recent diagnoses were more likely to be tested.

Conclusion

This study found that in patients over the age of 50 with at least one HNPGL, the rate of SDH PVs or other PVs known to be associated with hereditary PGL/PCC was 25.9%. These findings suggest that age over 50 does not negate the possibility of a PV known to be associated with hereditary PGL/PCC. Therefore, older patients diagnosed with HNPGL and their families still benefit from genetic testing as this may inform their management and aid in the diagnosis of at‐risk related individuals.
