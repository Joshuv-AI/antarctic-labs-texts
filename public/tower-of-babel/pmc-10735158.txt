Analysis of competing risks of cardiovascular death in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma: A population-based study
Authors: Lizhi Cao, Xiaoying Wang, Yuzhong Yan, Zhongping Ning, Linlin Ma, Yanfei Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Clinical data has shown that cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) have emerged as a prominent cause of mortality in individuals with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). This research aimed to reveal the comorbid effects of CVDs in patients with HCC. The cardiovascular mortality of patients diagnosed with HCC between 2000 and 2014 was compared to that of the general US population. Standardized mortality ratios were calculated to quantify the relative risk of cardiovascular mortality in HCC patients. The cumulative incidence of cardiovascular death (CVD) was estimated using Fine-Gray testing, and independent risk factors for CVD were determined using competing risk models. The results were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier analysis. The overall SMR for CVD in HCC patients was 11.15 (95% CI: 10.99–11.32). The risk of CVD was significantly higher in patients aged < 55 years (SMR: 56.19 [95% CI: 54.97–57.44]) compared to those aged ≥ 75 years (SMR: 1.86 [95% CI: 1.75–1.97]). This study suggests that patients with HCC are at significant risk of developing CVD. Competing risk analyses indicated that age, grade, tumor size, surveillance, epidemiology, and end results stage, and surgical status were independent risk factors for CVD in patients with HCC. Therefore, patients with HCC require enhanced preventive screening and management of CVDs during and after treatment to improve patient survival.

1. Introduction

Liver cancer continues to be a significant contributor to the global cancer burden,[1] ranking as the 6th most common cancer worldwide and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality.[2] Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the predominant form of primary liver cancer, comprising 70% to 90% of all liver cancer cases.[3] According to recent research, the number of new HCC cases diagnosed globally in 2020 was 905,677, with 830,180 HCC-related deaths recorded.[4] Cardiovascular death (CVD), which includes conditions such as ischemic heart disease, aortic aneurysm, peripheral arterial disease, and myocarditis, is the leading cause of death globally, posing a significant medical and economic burden on society.[5] Patients with HCC frequently succumb to various factors, including tumor progression, treatment-related complications, and infectious complications, ultimately leading to mortality.[6] Nevertheless, recent studies have shown that CVDs may also play a significant role in the mortality of patients with HCC.[7,8] Consequently, it has become increasingly crucial to explore the association between HCC and CVDs, and to discern the pertinent risk factors.

Cancer patients, including those with HCC, have been observed to have an increased risk of CVDs compared to the general population.[9,10] Additionally, risk factors that contribute to the development of hepatocellular carcinomas, such as diabetes and obesity, are also recognized as increasing the likelihood of developing CVDs.[11,12] Moreover, research has shown that specific medications administered to manage HCC,[13,14] such as adriamycin,[15] epirubicin,[16] sorafenib,[17] and rituximab,[18] have been associated with potential cardiotoxicity. Recent evidence suggests a molecular link between HCC and CVDs, wherein impaired DNA repair mechanisms can result in genomic instability and mutations, which are hallmarks of HCC.[19,20] Furthermore, the impact of DNA damage and its consequent response is crucial in the pathogenesis of CVDs.[21] Chronic inflammation and fibrosis are recognized as established factors that drive HCC development,[22] while inflammation also plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of CVDs, including atherosclerosis.[23] Additionally, Fuster JJ has proposed that clonal hematopoiesis is common in cancer patients and may be strongly linked to CVDs, such as ischemic heart disease, atrial fibrillation, and stroke.[24]

Despite previous studies shedding some light on the association between HCC and CVDs, numerous unresolved questions still necessitate further investigation. Hence, it is imperative to identify the risk factors associated with CVD in HCC patients to enhance patient prognosis and develop targeted interventions. This study aims to explore the potential risk factors linked to CVDs in HCC patients through the analysis of the final cause of death data retrieved from the surveillance, epidemiology, and end results (SEER) database. The findings of this research will contribute to the advancement of cardiovascular oncology and provide valuable insights for the clinical management and prognostic evaluation of HCC patients.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data source and study cohort

The SEER database contains information on cancer diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up for approximately 30% of the US population. The SEER database is a large and comprehensive population-based dataset that provides valuable information for studying the impact of cancer treatment and prognosis. It encompasses diverse demographics, geographic regions, and time periods, thus rendering it a vital resource for cancer research. Moreover, the SEER database is freely available to the public and does not require informed patient consent or institutional review board approval.[25] The data for this research was obtained from the SEER database downloads (version 8.4.0.1) using the SEER*Stat software, which included information on HCC patients diagnosed between 2000 and 2014. This investigation extracted cardiovascular mortality data for the general population from 2000 to 2014 from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and then compared with cardiovascular mortality for patients with HCC to calculate standardized mortality rates (SMRs: [number of observed deaths/expected deaths] × 100%).[26] Until 2007, there were no approved drugs for the treatment of HCC. Sorafenib was the first drug approved for HCC treatment in that year. Therefore, we conducted a separate analysis of the SMRs from 2007 to 2014.

This research included HCC patients with a positive pathology diagnosis between 2000 and 2014. This research included patients with histological codes 8170 to 8175 from the SEER database, which was classified according to the International Classification of Diseases of Oncology, Third Revision. Patients identified only by autopsy or death certificate, as well as those with incomplete data on certain variables such as age, sex, months of survival, and cause of death, were excluded (Fig. 1). CVD was the primary endpoint of interest and defined in the SEER database as the following 6 cardiovascular diseases: Cardiac disease; Hypertension; Cerebrovascular disease; Atherosclerosis; Aortic aneurysm, and dissection; Other diseases of the arteries, small arteries, and capillaries. This study defined competing events as death from HCC, other cancers, and other noncancerous diseases. Patients who survived until the last follow-up visit or lost to follow-up before the end of the observation period were considered censored data.

Patient inclusion criteria and screening protocol design.

2.2. Study variables

The definitions and information on experiment variables in this research were as follows: age at diagnosis (0–54 years, 55–64 years, 65–74 years, 75 + years), sex (male, female), year of diagnosis (2000–2007, 2008–2014), summary stage (local, regional, distal, unknown), surgery (underwent surgery or did not undergo surgery), marital status (married, unmarried [unmarried or cohabiting partner, single]), radiotherapy (received radiotherapy or unknown), chemotherapy (received chemotherapy or unknown), tumor size ( ≤ 2 cm, 3–5 cm, ≥ 5cm), household income (<$65,000, ≥ $65,000), cause of death, and duration of follow-up.

2.3. Statistical analysis

The SMRs were calculated as the ratio of observed deaths to expected deaths. We used the exact method to calculate 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for all SMRs. This research used the Fine-Gray test and competing risk models to assess the risk of CVD in different subgroups of HCC patients. This study conducted univariate and multivariate competing risk analyses to identify the risk factors associated with CVD and calculate the cumulative risk rate of CVD. Finally, we used Kaplan–Meier survival curves to evaluate the survival rate of patients with CVD. This research implemented all statistical methods using R software (version 4.2.1, https://cran.r-project.org/bin/windows/base/old/).

3. Results

3.1. Cohort characteristics

For this investigation, a total of 49,942 patients diagnosed with HCC between 2000 and 2014 were identified. The majority of the identified HCC patients were over 55 years old (75.97%), male (76.99%), and diagnosed between 2008 and 2014 (59.86%). Regarding annual income, more than half of the patients (54.36%) had an income of ≥$65,000, and the majority of patients (75.59%) were married. Among the patients, 51.24% had a SEER Stage of Localized, and 50.09% had a tumor size of > 5cm. Out of the total patient population, 3154 (6.31%) patients underwent radiotherapy, 14,052 (28.13%) underwent surgery, and 20,487 (41.02%) underwent chemotherapy. The highest number of deaths occurred within the first year of follow-up (49.44%), followed by the 1- to 3-year follow-up period (22.21%). Out of the 42,688 patients who died during the 5-year follow-up period, 1550 patients experienced CVD, with the primary cause being heart disease (74.52%), followed by cerebrovascular disease (17.68%), hypertension (5.03%), atherosclerosis (0.97%), aortic aneurysm and dissection (0.51%), and other diseases of the arteries, arterioles, and capillaries (1.29%), as shown in Table 1. Out of the total number of HCC patients, 24,693 patients died within 1 year after diagnosis, including 18,394 (74.49%) deaths from HCC, 4355 (17.64%) deaths from other non-cancer diseases, 1305 (5.29%) deaths from other cancers and 639 (2.59%) deaths from CVD. As shown in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/L106, the proportion of deaths from HCC decreased gradually over time, with the majority of deaths occurring within the first year after diagnosis. On the other hand, the proportion of deaths from CVD increased gradually over time.

3.2. Patients with HCC have a higher risk of CVD

This research employed SMRs to assess the risk of CVD in individuals diagnosed with HCC. The overall SMRs for CVD were 11.15 (95% CI: 10.99–11.32). Table 2 displays the baseline characteristics and SMR of CVD in HCC patients, stratified by sex and age. The most significant increases in SMRs across subgroups were for ages 0 to 54 (56.19 [54.97–57.44]). Additionally, the SMRs of patients decreased gradually with increasing age. The highest increase in SMR was observed for hypertension (18.98 [18.06–19.94]), followed by other diseases of the arteries, arterioles, and capillaries (12.86 [11.44–14.46]), cerebrovascular diseases (11.62 [11.24–12.01]), diseases of the heart (10.89 [10.71–11.07]), atherosclerosis (9.33 [7.97–10.91]), and aortic aneurysm and dissection (3.88 [2.87-5.26]) as shown in Table 3. In summary, this research found that patients diagnosed with HCC have a significantly higher risk of CVD compared to the general population. As shown in Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/L108 and Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/L109, HCC patients diagnosed after 2007 exhibited a slightly higher risk of CVD compared to those diagnosed before 2007. However, the results still indicate a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular diseases among patients diagnosed with HCC, when compared to the general population.

3.3. Cumulative mortality assessment of the high-risk subgroup for CVD

To investigate the primary risk factors linked with CVD in patients with HCC, we employed the Fine-Gray test to compute the cumulative mortality rate for CVD in diverse subgroups of HCC patients. Figure 4 and Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/L107 demonstrate an increased risk of CVD with longer follow-up periods in different subgroups of HCC patients, with the following subgroups exhibiting a higher risk of CVD: those aged over 75 years (5.10%), those with grade I/II tumors (3.40%), and those with tumors size ≤ 2 cm (3.49%). The cumulative risk analysis revealed that the risk of CVD in HCC patients increased with age at diagnosis. Moreover, patients diagnosed with early tumor progression, such as those with tumor size ≤ 2 cm, grade I/II, and localized tumor, had a significantly higher risk of CVD than those with other tumor stages. Patients who did not receive or had unknown radiotherapy had a greater risk of experiencing CVD than those who received radiotherapy. Similarly, the risk of CVD in patients who received chemotherapy was similar to those who received radiotherapy, which might be due to incomplete records in the SEER database. Furthermore, patients who underwent surgery had a higher risk of developing CVD than those who did not receive surgery.

3.4. Predictors of CVD

This research identified several risk factors associated with higher risks of CVD in HCC patients using multifactorial competing risks regression analysis (Table 4). These included age over 75 years, not receiving or having unknown radiotherapy or chemotherapy, and having undergone surgery. The hazard ratios (HR) with 95%CI for these factors were 3.223 (2.817–3.689), 1.552 (1.328–1.812), 1.203 (1.099–1.316), and 1.239 (1.121–1.368). On the contrary, we found that the following indicators were independently associated with a lower risk of CVD: Grade III/IV (HR [95% CI]: 0.741 [0.620–0.885]), tumor size > 5 cm (HR [95% CI]:0.788 [0.696–0.893]), and distant summary stage (HR [95% CI]: 0.506 [0.425–0.603]). Notably, our findings suggest that HCC patients at a higher risk of CVD are frequently diagnosed at the early stages of cancer, which aligns with the cumulative mortality results. Moreover, a 1-way competing risk analysis from 2007 to 2014 (Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/L110) similarly showed that patients with early stages of cancer (Grade I/II, SEER Stage was Localized) and who underwent surgery had a high CVD risk.

3.5. Survival rate for HCC patients at high CVD risk

To investigate the factors contributing to the elevated risk of CVD in HCC patients diagnosed at an early-stage, this research conducted a Kaplan–Meier analysis on subgroups stratified by CVD risk factors identified from a multifactorial competing risk analysis. Figure 5A shows the results of the Kaplan–Meier analysis of patients who died from CVDs and those who died from non-CVDs, showing that the survival rate of patients with CVD was significantly better than that of non-CVD. Figure 5B–E present the KM analysis of HCC patients in each subgroup, indicating that patients who had undergone surgery, had Grade I/II tumors, had SEER stage Localized, and had tumor size ≤ 2cm exhibited higher survival rates compared to the other subgroups. These findings further support the results of the overall KM analysis. In conclusion, the survival rates of patients with different stages of HCC varied, which may clarify why patients with early-stage HCC have a greater risk of CVD than those with advanced stages. Additionally, patients who underwent surgery had a higher risk of CVD compared to those who did not, which could be attributed to the better prognosis associated with surgical treatment.

4. Discussion

The research is the first large population-based cohort study to investigate and independently identify predictive risk factors for CVD risk in patients with HCC using the SEER database. The results of the study showed that HCC patients have a significantly elevated risk of CVD, 11.15 times higher than the general U.S. population, especially hypertension. Notably, despite the increasing number of patients with HCC each year, there was an overall downward trend in mortality from both HCC and CVDs. This trend may be attributed to the current advancements in treatment strategies for HCC patients, as well as the improved treatment and emergency measures for CVDs, as previously reported in the literature.[27–30] Additionally, our study identified age, tumor differentiation, tumor size, surgical status, and SEER stage as independent predictors of CVD in patients with HCC. These findings suggest that cardiovascular disease staging of HCC patients should be done in advance in the treatment and care of HCC patients in order to develop a rational therapeutic regimen thereby reducing the risk of cardiovascular mortality and improving the quality of life.[31] This research had essential clinical implications and underscored the need for healthcare providers to consider CVD risk factors when developing treatment plans for HCC patients.

The prevalence of CVD among HCC patients in this study showed an increasing trend each year, with the incidence in year 5 being more than double that of year 1. This trend may have suggested a possible oversight of cardiovascular toxicity during drug usage or therapeutic regimens for HCC treatment,[32,33] resulting in an elevated risk of CVD in patients after their recovery. The predominance of cardiac disease, cerebrovascular disease, and hypertension as the primary causes of CVD-related deaths may have been attributed to the coexistence of hepatic insufficiency, malnutrition, and tumor-related inflammation in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma.[34] These factors may have potentially contributed to the development of cardiovascular issues in these patients. There may be underlying mechanisms for the interaction between cardiovascular disease and HCC; for example, it has been shown that nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, hepatitis C, hepatitis B, and cirrhosis, which are all etiologic factors of HCC, may contribute to the development of CVD in patients with HCC.[35–38] These findings may suggest that healthcare providers should have considered the cardiovascular status (especially heart disease, and hypertension) of patients with HCC during treatment, and taken proactive measures to prevent and treat cardiovascular disease to reduce the risk of mortality.

Compared with patients in other age groups, patients aged ≥ 75 years tend to choose to forgo surgery. Elderly patients have increased sensitivity to anesthetics and are often comorbid with a variety of diseases, which seriously affects the reserve function of vital organs, making them less tolerant to anesthesia and surgery and increasing the risk of surgery,[39,40] as well as the possibility of some age-based medical decision making.[41–43] In addition, age is also associated with the incidence of CVD, with studies showing a higher rate of cardiovascular death with age. In addition, although surgery in patients in all age strata, Grade I/II, Tumorsize ≤ 2 cm, significantly reduces the risk of death directly from HCC, the risk of CVD is significantly higher, possibly due to the impact of the surgery itself on the patient’s physical function and the fact that surgical treatment may require certain operations such as anesthesia and incisions, thus increasing the risk of CVD.[44,45] Taken together, these findings suggest that a thorough assessment and risk management are needed before surgical treatment for patients with HCC to ensure maximum effectiveness and reduce the risk of postoperative CVD.

In the present research, the SMR for CVD risk was significantly higher in patients with HCC than in the general population. This is consistent with recent studies reporting increased CVD risk in bladder cancer, osteosarcoma, gallbladder cancer, colorectal cancer, and lymphoma.[25,30,46–48] Although the SMR was lowest in the elderly group, it was still higher 7th an in the general elderly population. The reason for the higher SMR in the elderly group may be attributed to their poorer health, reduced immunity, and increased prevalence of comorbidities, including infections.[49,50] Furthermore, out of the 6 cardiovascular disease-related causes of death, patients with HCC exhibited the highest SMR for hypertension. This finding could potentially be attributed to the use of HCC-related drugs such as bevacizumab, ramucirumab, sorafenib, and sunitinib,[51] all of which have been associated with an elevated risk of hypertension in patients with HCC.[52] Cumulative incidence from CVD increased with age at diagnosis, with multifactorial competing risk analysis showing that older patients were more likely to develop CVD (HR: 3.223 [95% CI: 2.817–3.689]). Interestingly, patients aged 0 to 54 years had the highest SMR for CVD (54.72 [95% CI: 53.51–55.95]), which was similar to the results of Soohwan Kim et al.[53] Younger patients often receive more intense cancer treatments such as radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and surgery, which may cause damage to the cardiovascular system and increase the risk of cardiovascular events.[54] Overall, these findings suggest that the potential impact of cardiovascular toxicity on patients is not sufficiently taken into account in clinical drug development and treatment. Although mortality rates may decrease after treatment, the risk of CVD increases significantly in HCC patients. Hence, greater emphasis on CVD screening and preventive measures is needed to improve the prognosis of HCC patients.

Studies have demonstrated that radiotherapy and chemotherapy drugs, such as anthracyclines and trastuzumab, can produce cardiotoxic effects.[55,56] However, in this research, patients who underwent radiotherapy and chemotherapy did not demonstrate a significant advantage in terms of cumulative mortality and risk ratio for CVDs. This might be due to the lack of detailed information on chemotherapy and radiotherapy in the SEER database, which may have skewed the results.[57,58] It highlighted the need to enhance the prevention of cardiovascular events in patients with HCC, both before and after surgery. A multifactorial competing risk analysis showed that patients with early-stage HCC (stage I/II cancer differentiation, tumor size ≤ 2 cm, SEER stage localized had a higher risk of CVD compared to patients with advanced HCC. In addition, patients who underwent surgery had a higher risk of CVD than those who did not. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that patients with early-stage HCC had a significantly better prognosis than patients with advanced HCC. In addition, patients who underwent surgery had a better prognosis and longer life expectancy, offering patients with early-stage HCC a greater likelihood of developing age-related CVD. Therefore, establishing proactive CVD screening and follow-up strategies for HCC patients can contribute to better patient outcomes.

In our study, we found that patients with HCC face a significantly increased risk of cardiovascular diseases. This finding serves as a warning for the patients themselves and provides profound insights for clinical practice. Firstly, our study highlights the need for a more proactive focus on cardiovascular health during HCC treatment. Physicians should pay closer attention to the cardiovascular condition of patients, especially when undergoing intensive treatments. When devising treatment plans, it is crucial to consider the patient’s cardiovascular risk comprehensively to avoid triggering serious cardiovascular events. Secondly, our research calls for clinical practitioners to improve prevention and treatment strategies for cardiovascular diseases in HCC patients. Maintaining cardiovascular health in HCC patients should be considered equally important as cancer treatment. This might involve regular cardiovascular health checkups, stricter lifestyle interventions, and close monitoring of cardiovascular indicators during the treatment process. Additionally, our research results provide crucial clues for future intervention measures. Personalized prevention and intervention plans can be developed targeting specific cardiovascular risk factors in HCC patients. This may include pharmaceutical interventions for hypertension, high cholesterol, and other cardiovascular risk factors, as well as more personalized lifestyle recommendations such as customized exercise programs and dietary guidance. In summary, our research findings are not just academic discoveries but also valuable guidance for clinical practice. By better understanding and managing the cardiovascular risks in HCC patients, we can offer more comprehensive and personalized medical services, enhancing the quality of life and survival rates for these patients.

This research also provided important references for the development of more effective interventions. However, there were some limitations to this study. Firstly, the study design was retrospective, utilizing a large US cancer population database, and despite the strict screening criteria applied, potential biases could not be completely eliminated. Secondly the SEER database does not contain information on the various susceptibility factors associated with cardiovascular disease, such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and etiologic factors contributing to HCC (e.g., hepatitis C, hepatitis B, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, etc), which may affect the accuracy of the study results. Additionally, the database lacked complete information on the radiotherapy and chemotherapy treatments that patients underwent, including the dosages used. Lastly, the SEER database did not provide data related to sequencing, which could have provided more insight into the progression of CVD in HCC patients.

5. Conclusion

In summary, this research demonstrates that patients with HCC have a significantly higher risk of CVD than the general population, with all types of CVD showing an elevated full standardized mortality rate. These findings underscore the importance of timely cardiovascular disease screening in patients with HCC. Research finds tumor differentiation, tumor size, SEER stage, and surgical status as independent predictors of CVD in HCC patients. Additionally, study revealed the reasons behind the higher CVD risk in patients with early-stage cancer and those who underwent surgery, which can inform clinicians in developing treatment plans and follow-up strategies for patients at different stages of HCC. Finally, this study contributes to the emerging field of oncological cardiology and provides new insights into CVD prevention in patients with HCC.
