H2O2-propelled Janus nanomotor for synergistic treatment of drug-resistant biofilm infections
Authors: Daifeng Li, Lurui Yang, Liuliang He, Bowen Liu, Yuhui Yuan, Zhichao Wang, Jinjin Shi, Yi Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn contemporary antibacterial treatment, the inability to effectively penetrate biofilm structures, coupled with the rising prevalence of antimicrobial resistance, significantly compromise the clinical efficacy of antibiotic-based therapies. Herein, we propose a hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)-propelled Janus nanomotor designed to co-deliver vancomycin (Van) and silver ions (Ag⁺), thereby enabling synergistic therapy against refractory biofilm infections. The nanomotor is fabricated by self-assembling Van with FDA-approved biocompatible poloxamer 188 (F68) and tannic acid (TA) to create a Van-loaded nanocarrier, with silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) subsequently deposited on one side to generate the final structure (F68@TA/Van@Ag). Within the acidic biofilm microenvironment, oxygen generated through the catalytic decomposition of H2O2 by asymmetrical AgNPs facilitates autonomous movement, allowing efficient biofilm penetration and achieving a 3.9-fold increase in the effective concentration of Van. Concurrently, Ag⁺ released during decomposition induces oxidative stress within bacterial cells, disrupts membrane integrity, and interferes with DNA replication; these actions collectively enhance biofilm eradication in conjunction with Van treatment. Notably, F68@TA/Van@Ag reprograms macrophages toward pro-inflammatory polarization, thus activating robust antibacterial innate immune responses. Introduction

Bacterial infections, particularly those caused by drug-resistant bacteria, represent a significant threat to global public health [1]. In contemporary medical practice, standard clinical therapies mainly focus on combating bacterial infections with antibiotics. However, the formation of biofilms and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) pose major challenges for clinical anti-infection treatment [2–6]. Biofilms are implicated in approximately 80% of bacterial infections and significantly impede the intracellular penetration of antibiotics [7, 8]. Research has demonstrated that the diffusion coefficient of vancomycin (Van) within Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) biofilms is merely 0.2 µm2/s, which is over 1000 times slower than its diffusion rate in water [9]. Consequently, bacteria residing within biofilm shelters exhibit a 10- to 1000-fold reduction in susceptibility to various antimicrobial agents compared to their planktonic counterparts [10, 11]. In addition to the physical barrier presented by biofilms, the escalating issue of AMR further complicates the management of bacterial infections [12, 13]. Therefore, it is imperative to explore safe and effective strategies that not only facilitate antibiotic penetration into biofilms but also enhance bacterial sensitivity to antibiotic therapy.

Currently, various strategies have been investigated to improve the antibiotic delivery within biofilms [14, 15]. For example, optimizing properties of nanocarriers — such as nanoparticle size, surface charge, and hydrophilicity — can enhance antibiotic diffusion through dense extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix [16–18]. Nevertheless, these passive strategies are often constrained by their dependence on Brownian motion and diffusion processes, which limit deep penetration into biofilms [19, 20]. Recently, nanomotors have demonstrated significant potential in applications involving the traversal of complex physiological or pathological barriers as well as in antibacterial therapies, owing to their superior capability for controllable autonomous motion [21–24]. Research indicates that catalyzing local microenvironmental substances into gases or introducing exogenous gas-generating agents can produce gas as a propulsive force for nanomotors [25–27]. In the biofilm microenvironment (BME), elevated levels of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) have been well-documented in multiple studies [28, 29]. Given this property, the catalytic conversion of endogenous H2O2 into oxygen (O2) has emerged as a promising approach to power nanomotors. On one hand, nanomotors utilize self-propelled random motion to penetrate biofilms [24]; on the other hand, O2 generation alleviates the hypoxic biofilm microenvironment and restores the bactericidal function of macrophages impaired by hypoxia [4, 30]. Consequently, a nanomotor system endowed with autonomous movement capability is anticipated to effectively traverse biofilm barriers and enhance antibiotic delivery within these structures.

In addition, AMR exhibits non-transient characteristics, persisting within bacterial populations even after biofilm disruption [5]. This sustained resistance primarily arises from either genomic mutations or the acquisition of resistance determinants via horizontal gene transfer (HGT) [31–33]. However, the multi-target action mechanism complicates bacteria’s ability to develop drug resistance through single gene mutations [34–36]. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), recognized for their broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties, have received clinical approval for use [37]. Their antibacterial mechanism predominantly involves the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can induce an imbalance of oxidative stress and lead to cell membrane disruption and DNA damage [38]. Research indicates that following continuous exposure over 30 generations, the minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) value of AgNPs increases only 2–4 fold — significantly less than the 100–1000 fold increase observed with β-lactam antibiotics [39]. The combination of AgNPs with antibiotics can restore sensitivity of resistant bacterial strains (FIC index < 0.5 indicating a synergistic effect) [40]. Moreover, AgNPs can catalyze excess H2O2 within BME to O2 to propel nanomotors and silver ions can perform immunomodulatory function which can potentially promote the pro-inflammatory polarization of macrophages and its endocytosis. Therefore, integrating AgNPs into an antibiotic-loaded nanomotor system presents a promising approach to enhance antibiotic delivery within biofilms while simultaneously addressing AMR and reversing immuno-silence.

Herein, we proposed a H2O2-propelled Janus nanomotor designed to co-deliver Van and silver ions (Ag⁺), thereby enabling synergistic therapy against S. aureus and Methicillin-resistance Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) biofilm infections (Scheme 1). The nanomotor was constructed through the self-assembly of Van with FDA-approved biocompatible poloxamer 188 (F68) and tannic acid (TA), resulting in Van-loaded nanocarrier F68@TA/Van. Subsequently, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) were deposited on one side to generate the final structure, named F68@TA/Van@Ag. Within the acidic BME, oxygen generated through the catalytic decomposition of H2O2 by asymmetrical AgNPs facilitates autonomous motion, which enhanced effective biofilm penetration efficiency and achieved a 3.9-fold increase in the effective concentration of Van. Simultaneously, Ag⁺ released during decomposition induced oxidative stress within bacterial cells, compromised bacterial cell membrane integrity, and interferes with DNA replication, thereby synergistically augmenting bacterial eradication in combination with Van. The in vitro antibiofilm experiments demonstrated that F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 achieved biofilm biomass clearance rates of 97.4% against S. aureus and 99.3% against MRSA, respectively. Notably, this nanomotor also promoted macrophage reprogramming toward pro-inflammatory phenotype, thereby activating robust antibacterial immune responses. Overall, our H2O2-propelled Janus nanomotor F68@TA/Van@Ag demonstrates remarkable antibiofilm properties and immunomodulatory effects while maintaining biocompatibility, offering a promising therapeutic strategy for addressing refractory biofilm infections — particularly those drug-resistant biofilm infections.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of H2O2-propelled Janus-structured nanomotor F68@TA/Van@Ag

To address the limited biofilm penetration efficacy of antibiotics and its insufficient antibacterial treatment in combating AMR, we proposed a therapeutic strategy that integrated AgNPs into an antibiotic-loaded Janus-structured nanomotor. This approach was designed to enhance antibiotic delivery within biofilms while simultaneously addressing AMR challenges. Initially, the Van-loaded nanocarrier was constructed through the self-assembly of Van with FDA-approved biocompatible poloxamer 188 (F68) and tannic acid (TA), named F68@TA/Van. Subsequently, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) were deposited on one side of F68@TA/Van to generate the final structure, designated as F68@TA/Van@Ag (Fig. 2a). Relevant investigations were further conducted to verify the characterizations of these nanostructures. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed that F68@TA/Van exhibited a spherical structure with a diameter of approximately 100.7 ± 2.2 nm, whereas F68@TA/Van@Ag displayed an increased diameter of 172.2 ± 31.2 nm due to the in-situ deposition of AgNPs (Fig. 2b). Additionally, size distributions and zeta potentials were analyzed using dynamic light scattering (DLS, Fig. 2c and d), indicating good dispersibility in aqueous solutions. The particle size and Zeta potential of F68@TA/Van@Ag were systematically characterized in both PBS and a biomimetic medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) to confirm its colloidal stability under physiologically relevant conditions. Over a 7-day incubation period with measurements performed every two days, no significant changes in nanoparticle size or surface charge were observed in either environment (Fig. S1a, b). The favorable dispersion properties of the nanomaterials were further validated through measurement of the polydispersity index (PDI) of the nanoparticles (Table S1), indicating a homogeneous size distribution. These results demonstrate that F68@TA/Van@Ag exhibits excellent stability in simulated physiological conditions, supporting its potential for biological applications. Elemental mapping images for F68@TA/Van@Ag (Fig. 2e) further demonstrated that Ag was predominantly localized on one side of the nanostructure, while nitrogen (N), carbon (C), and oxygen (O) were uniformly distributed throughout, confirming the asymmetric structure as a Janus nanomotor F68@TA/Van@Ag. As shown in Fig. 2f, the Ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy (UV-Vis) for F68@TA/Van remained unchanged after Van loading; however, F68@TA/Van@Ag exhibited a distinct absorption peak at approximately 470 nm, confirming the successful incorporation of AgNPs into the structure. Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) provided additional evidence for both Ag and Van presence within this composite material (Fig. 2g and h). Moreover, the loading capacities for Van and Ag within F68@TA/Van@Ag were quantified via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), yielding values of 10.2% and 9.4%, respectively (Fig. 2i). Together, these findings confirm successful synthesis of Janus-structured nanomotor F68@TA/Van@Ag.

Biofilm penetration and release of Van and Ag+ of F68@TA/Van@Ag

Gas serves as a key driving force of self-propelled nanomotor. AgNPs in F68@TA/Van@Ag can catalyze the decomposition of H2O2, thereby generating O2 to propel the movement of the nanomotor (Fig. 3a). Initially, the catalytic reaction between AgNPs and H2O2 was examined. TEM results indicated that AgNPs were nearly completely dissolved after incubation with 10 mM H2O2 for 3 h (Fig. 3b). As illustrated in Fig. 3c, the concentration of Ag⁺ released from F68@TA/Van@Ag increased with higher concentrations of H2O2, reaching approximately 800 µg/mL at 10 mM H2O2. Concurrently, significant color changes in the solution and the generation of O2 bubbles further confirmed the catalytic activity of AgNPs (Fig. 3d and e). Additionally, we tracked the movement of F68@TA/Van@Ag nanomotors over time; their trajectory in a solution containing 10 mM H2O2 was depicted over a duration of up to 30 s in Fig. S2. Typical tracking trajectories for various concentrations of H2O2 were also recorded (Fig. 3f). In the absence of exogenous H2O2 as fuel, nanomotors predominantly exhibit Brownian motion; with increasing H2O2 concentration, their movement transitions gradually toward a more directional zigzag trajectory. Theoretically, higher H2O2 concentrations should promote increasingly linear and continuous motion. However, in actual physiological solution environments, nanomotors are subject to fluid shear forces, local disturbances, and microenvironmental heterogeneity, which can induce rotational flipping or orientation changes—particularly in Janus-structured particles with unilateral Ag NP distribution, where directional adjustments lead to asymmetric force distribution, resulting in irregular zigzag motility. The F68@TA/Van@Ag nanomotor exhibited movement speeds measuring at approximately 0, 5, and 10 mM concentrations; specifically, speeds were found to be 2.5 ± 1.0, 12.6 ± 1.3, and 15.1 ± 1.4 μm/s, respectively (Fig. 3g). Furthermore, both the mean squared displacement (MSD) and diffusion coefficient demonstrated an increasing trend correlating with rising levels of H2O2 (Fig. 3h and Fig. S3). These observations confirm that asymmetrically produced O2 bubbles generate differential thrust necessary for self-propulsion.

Given that biofilms serve as protective shields for bacteria against treatment strategies, assessing biofilm penetration by this nanomotor is crucial. The penetration performance was evaluated using a transwell model (Fig. 3i). Biofilms were cultivated in the apical chamber, followed by the introduction of rhodamine B-labeled nanoparticles (F68@TA/Van or F68@TA/Van@Ag). After co-incubation for 30 min, the fluorescence intensity of nanoparticles that penetrated the basal chamber was quantitatively measured and results indicated significant penetration by F68@TA/Van@Ag nanomotors into S. aureus and Escherichia coli (E. coli) biofilm matrices (Fig. 3i and Fig. S4). Correspondingly, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images validated these observations (Fig. 3j). Biofilms treated with F68@TA/Van exhibited weak fluorescence signals, whereas treatment with F68@TA/Van@Ag showed markedly enhanced fluorescence signals. The increased signal intensity is attributed to nanomotor movement, which facilitate deeper penetration into dense extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix within biofilms. To further evaluate the biofilm penetration ability of the nanomotors during the long-term dynamic process, the penetration effect at 1 h and 3 h was detected. The results showed that after 1 h of incubation, the nanomotors had widely penetrated into most of the matrix regions of the biofilm; by 3 h, the penetration depth had further expanded, achieving significant penetration throughout the entire biofilm layer, fully demonstrating their excellent autonomous motion-driven penetration performance (Fig. S5).

Subsequently, we investigated the pH-dependent swelling behavior and BME-responsive Van release characteristics from F68@TA/Van. TEM images depicting F68@TA/Van NPs incubated in PBS solutions across varying pH levels (7.4, 6.5, and 5.0) in darkness at room temperature for 1 h were presented in Fig. S6. In PBS solution at pH 7.4, F68@TA/Van NPs displayed spherical morphology which subtly altered when exposed to PBS solution at pH 6.5. Conversely, in conditions where pH reached 5.0, the F68@TA/Van NPs appeared swollen and partially fragmented under certain circumstances. These findings illustrate that F68@TA/Van NPs exhibit sensitivity toward variations in pH, paving pathway toward effective Van release within acidic BME. Simultaneously, the quantity of Van released from F68@TA/Van was determined by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). After 10 h incubation at pH levels of 7.4, 6.5, and 5.0, the cumulative release rates of Van were approximately 12.3 ± 2.0%, 41.8 ± 5.0%, and 80.3 ± 2.4%, respectively (Fig. S7). To further evaluate the bioavailability of Van within the biofilm matrix, the actual Van concentrations following treatment with either free Van or F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 were measured. The HPLC results demonstrated that the F68@TA/Van treatment group achieved a 3.9-fold increase in effective Van concentration compared to the free Van treatment group (Fig. S8). This pattern establishes foundational support for effective Van delivery mechanisms within biofilm shelters while achieving BME-responsive Van release; this approach further enhances the therapeutic efficacy of Van whilst potentially mitigating the side effects associated with high-dose administration.

Antibiofilm activity of H2O2-propelled Janus nanomotor F68@TA/Van@Ag in vitro

This study systematically investigated the chemical properties of BME to identify the optimal conditions for enhancing the interaction between F68@TA/Van@Ag and BME. The pH and H2O2 concentration of BME were characterized (Fig. S9a, b), revealing that the MRSA biofilm culture medium exhibited a pH of 5.4 and an H2O2 concentration of 69.6 µM. This distinct chemical microenvironment provides a foundation for the functional activity of F68@TA/Van@Ag. Based on the outstanding performance of F68@TA/Van@Ag nanomotors in penetrating biofilms and responding to BME release, we further investigated the anti-biofilm efficacy of each group of materials. Relying solely on endogenous H2O2 as a fuel source would limit their propulsion efficiency, thereby affecting their penetration ability into the deep regions of biofilms [41, 42]. Therefore, in subsequent experiments, we enhanced the motility and penetration depth of the nanomotors by exogenously adding H2O2 (1 mM). This strategy not only effectively improved the motor-driven performance but also was consistent with the local use of H2O2 in actual clinical procedures such as wound disinfection and debridement, demonstrating good application compatibility and translational potential [43, 44]. To further assess its antibacterial potential, a comparative study was performed between the F68@TA/Van@Ag group and the F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 group. Absorbance measuring at 600 nm (OD600) after 12 h of co-incubation with the biofilm indicated that F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 group exhibited a significant reduction in OD600 values in both S. aureus and MRSA biofilms culture media. (Fig. 4a and b). In contrast, the Van-only treatment group exhibited limited efficacy in disrupting biofilms, primarily due to the restricted penetration of Van within the biofilm matrix. The results revealed that the level of endogenous H2O2 was insufficient to significantly enhance the antibacterial efficacy. Exogenous H2O2, a commonly used disinfectant in wound debridement, is already well-established in clinical practice. Therefore, in the management of biofilm-associated wound infections, the concurrent application of exogenous H2O2 and F68@TA/Van@Ag not only aligns with standard clinical debridement protocols but also markedly enhances the anti-infective outcome.

Quantitative analysis using crystal violet staining further revealed that the majority of biofilm biomass remained intact in the Control, H2O2, Van, F68@TA/Van@Ag, and F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 groups. Conversely, treatment with the F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 formulation resulted in a marked reduction in biomass (Fig. 4c and d). The clearance rates of biofilm biomass reached 96.1% for S. aureus and 97.4% for MRSA, respectively, demonstrating superior efficacy of F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 in eradicating bacterial biofilms. Colony-forming units (CFU) of different treatment groups were further assessed using standard plate method (SPM), revealing colony inhibition rates of 97.4% against S. aureus and 99.3% against MRSA for the F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 group, underscoring its potent antibiofilm activity (Fig. 4e-h). The in vitro anti- E. coli biofilm efficacy was systematically assessed through OD600 measurements, crystal violet staining, and plate colony counting assays (Fig. S10a-c). These analyses revealed that F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 achieved approximately 64.0% clearance of E. coli biofilms—lower than its efficacy against MRSA biofilms (99.3%)—owing to the inherent mechanism of action of vancomycin, which predominantly targets Gram-positive bacteria. The three-dimensional architecture of biofilms along with residual bacteria was examined via CLSM. Biofilms treated with F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 displayed severe structural disruption accompanied by intense red fluorescence indicative of extensive bacterial death; conversely, remaining groups maintained largely intact three-dimensional structures, suggesting sustained bacterial viability and activity (Fig. 4i and j). Collectively, these findings highlight the efficient antibiofilm effects of autonomously moving nanomotor F68@TA/Van@Ag in vitro; particularly combating those drug-resistant biofilm infections. In addition to the desired therapeutic effects, the biocompatibility of nanomotors plays a critical role in their further biomedical applications. Initially, we performed hemolysis assay utilizing red blood cells (RBCs) to evaluate the potential cytotoxic effects. A low hemolysis rate indicated minimal toxicity and no significant RBC hemolysis (less than 5%) was observed after incubation with F68@TA/Van@Ag, even at the highest concentration of 256 µg/mL. In contrast, the positive control group treated with 1% (v/v) Triton X-100 induced complete hemolysis (100%) (Fig. S11). CCK-8 assays were also performed to assess the cytotoxicity on NIH 3T3 and RAW264.7 cell lines. The findings illustrated that F68@TA/Van@Ag exhibited excellent biocompatibility, as evidenced by cell viabilities exceeding 90% across all tested concentrations following a 24-h incubation period (Fig. S12a and b). The safety of various H2O2 concentrations was evaluated using cytotoxicity assays. The results demonstrated that a 1 mM concentration maintained a cell viability of 98% without obvious toxicity (Fig. S13).

Antibacterial mechanism of H2O2-propelled Janus-structured nanomotor F68@TA/Van@Ag

As a transition metal ion, Ag+ can catalyze the Fenton reaction, leading to an increased production of ROS [45, 46]. This process exacerbates oxidative stress within bacterial cells, ultimately resulting in membrane rupture and cell damage [47, 48]. Based on this premise, we hypothesized that the nanomotor could effectively deliver Ag+ within biofilms after catalyzation, and significantly enhance oxidative stress levels in bacterial cells. To investigate this hypothesis, we observed changes in cell membrane morphology following various treatments under scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The cell membranes of S. aureus and MRSA treated with nanomotor F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 exhibited increased roughness, more pronounced deformation, and more extensive rupture compared to those treated with nanoparticles from other groups (Fig. 5a). Notably, Van weakens the bacterial cell structure by inhibiting peptidoglycan polymerization in the cell wall, thereby enhancing membrane permeability and facilitating increased intracellular influx of silver ions. To quantify this effect, we compared silver ion uptake in bacterial biofilms across different treatment groups—PBS, F68@TA@Ag + H2O2, and F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2—using ICP-MS analysis. The F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 group exhibits a significantly higher intracellular silver concentration compared to the control and single-agent groups, demonstrating that vancomycin enhances the internalization of silver ions (Fig. S14). Subsequently, the levels of ROS in S. aureus and MRSA were quantified using carboxy-H2DCFDA (carboxylated H2DCFDA) via CLSM. The results demonstrated that S. aureus and MRSA treated with F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 exhibited markedly elevated ROS levels compared to those treated with other nanoparticles (Fig. 5b), indicating a critical role of Ag+ in promoting ROS generation, which was further enhanced by Van’s synergistic effect. Bacterial protein leakage and alterations in membrane permeability were assessed using the BCA leakage test and the o-nitrophenyl-β-D-galactoside (ONPG) assay. The results indicated significant increases in both parameters (membrane permeability and cytoplasmic leakage) under treatment with F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 for both S. aureus and MRSA (Fig. 5c and d). Furthermore, total bacterial DNA from each experimental group was quantified utilizing a bacterial DNA extraction kit (Fig. 5e). Remarkably low amounts of intact DNA were detected only in the F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 treatment group, suggesting severe compromise to bacterial DNA integrity due to ROS storm induced by this treatment regimen. Figure 5f elucidated a plausible bactericidal mechanism for H2O2-propelled Janus-structured nanomotor F68@TA/Van@Ag. Owing to its autonomous motion capability, this nanomotor can effectively penetrate biofilms while co-delivering Van and Ag+. The synergistic action of these two agents induces oxidative stress in bacterial cells, thereby altering membrane permeability, damaging bacterial DNA, and ultimately causing bacterial death.

Immunomodulatory effects of H2O2-propelled Janus-structured nanomotor F68@TA/Van@Ag on macrophages

Macrophages play a crucial role as the host’s primary defense mechanism against infection by recognizing, engulfing, and eliminating invading bacteria [49, 50]. Due to their high plasticity, macrophages can rapidly adapt to the complex inflammatory microenvironment through polarization [51, 52]. Increasing evidence indicates that ROS facilitate macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype with bactericidal properties. Moreover, macrophages employ ROS-mediated oxidative bursts to eliminate invading pathogens [53, 54]. Although F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 has demonstrated significant peroxidase-like activity in vitro, its specific impact on intracellular ROS levels, phenotypic modulation, and overall macrophage function remains have yet to be fully elucidated. To investigate this further, immunofluorescence staining was employed to evaluate ROS production in F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2-treated macrophages (Fig. 6a and Fig. S15). The results revealed that treatment with F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 significantly increased intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 6b). Furthermore, flow cytometric analysis of phenotypic shifts in macrophages indicated a substantial increase from 1.61 ± 0.37% to 12.5 ± 0.92% in the proportion of M1-type macrophages following treatment; this suggests that F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 promotes M1 polarization via induction of intracellular ROS (Fig. 6c and d). Given that immunosuppressive BME is dominated by the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, we further investigated the reprogramming effect from the anti-inflammatory M2 to the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. The results demonstrated that F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 group modulated the shift from CD206 (marker of M2 macrophages) to CD86 (marker of M1 macrophages), effectively reversing the immunosuppressive BME and enhancing the bactericidal immune response (Fig. S16). Real-time Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) results showed marked upregulation of M1-type marker genes (TNF-α, iNOS, IL-6, and CCR7), further supporting the conclusion that F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 effectively enhances macrophage polarization toward M1 pro-inflammatory phenotype (Fig. 6e). Finally, we evaluated both phagocytic capacity and bacterial elimination of macrophages, which were pretreated with Control, H2O2, F68@TA/Van@Ag or F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 before co-incubation with RFP-labeled S. aureus. The results demonstrated that those treated with F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 exhibited significantly enhanced phagocytosis of S. aureus compared to any of other groups (Fig. 6f and g; Fig. S17). Together, these findings highlight that this nanomotor F68@TA/Van@Ag also promote macrophage reprogramming toward pro-inflammatory phenotype, thereby activating robust antibacterial immune responses to further eradicate biofilm infections.

In vivo antibiofilm effect of H2O2-propelled Janus nanomotor F68@TA/Van@Ag

Given its excellent biocompatibility coupled with potent antibiofilm activity against drug-resistant biofilms observed through vitro studies, we further evaluated the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of nanomotor F68@TA/Van@Ag employing MRSA biofilm-infected wound mice models. In this study, local topical administration was employed in the in vivo biofilm-infected wound model to closely simulate the clinical wound debridement and disinfection procedure [24, 55]. After 48 h incubation of MRSA (10 µL containing 108 CFU/mL concentration) [56], mature biofilm was formed and wounds were treated with various formulations. Photographs of wound area were recorded, and wound tissues were collected on day 7 (Fig. 7a). Although initial weight loss was observed due to the infection, mice treated with F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 began to regain body weight starting from day 3 (Fig. 7b). In the PBS, H2O2, and Van groups, significant bacterial deposition and abscess formation were observed throughout the study period. In contrast, the F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 group exhibited marked wound healing by the 7th day, whereas wounds in the other groups remained unhealed due to persistent biofilm infection (Fig. 7c and d). Subsequently, the bacterial load at wound site was quantitatively determined using SPM method on the 7th day following treatment. The results demonstrated that the F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 group (G5) exhibited a significantly reduced bacterial load compared to other treatment groups, indicating enhanced antibiofilm efficacy (Fig. 7e). We further investigated the phenotypic modulation of primary macrophages in a murine model to strengthen the robustness and broader applicability of our conclusions. Flow cytometric analysis of primary macrophages isolated from infected draining lymph nodes across treatment groups revealed that the F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 regimen induced a significant shift in macrophage polarization—from CD206-positive (M2 marker) toward CD86-positive (M1 marker)—demonstrating its capacity to effectively reprogram the immunosuppressive microenvironment and potentiate bactericidal immune response (Fig. 7f, g). Histological and immunohistochemical fluorescence analyses revealed that F68@TA/Van@Ag + H2O2 group (G5) not only efficiently eradicated biofilm infection, but also significantly mitigated inflammatory responses (hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining) and enhanced neovascularization (CD31 staining) (Fig. 7h and i; Figs. S18 and 19). These findings suggest that this H2O2-propelled nanomotor F68@TA/Van@Ag, capable of penetrating biofilm matrix barrier to co-deliver Ag+ and Van, represents a promising therapeutic strategy for addressing biofilm infections — particularly those drug-resistant biofilm infections. Additionally, blood and tissue samples were collected for routine blood tests following the completion of treatment. As illustrated in Fig. S20, all routine blood indices were within normal ranges. Therefore, all these findings collectively confirm the excellent biocompatibility of F68@TA/Van@Ag, with no significant damage to blood parameters.

Conclusion

In summary, our H2O2-propelled Janus nanomotor F68@TA/Van@Ag is synthesized through the self-assembly of Van with FDA-approved F68 and TA, followed by the deposition of AgNPs on one side, thereby achieving co-delivering Van and Ag⁺. The asymmetrical distribution of AgNPs within the BME facilitates the catalytic decomposition of H2O2, resulting in O2 generation that provides sufficient autonomous movement to penetrate biofilms, thereby achieving a 3.9-fold increase in Van concentration within bacterial cells. Concurrently, Ag⁺ released during this decomposition process directly eradicates bacteria and activates a pro-inflammatory antibacterial immune response; these combined actions significantly enhance biofilm eradication in conjunction with Van treatment. Overall, this H2O2-propelled Janus nanomotor represents an innovative therapeutic approach for addressing biofilm infections — particularly those drug-resistant biofilm infections.
