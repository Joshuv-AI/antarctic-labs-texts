The impact of 1-hour plasma glucose on the metabolic characteristics and pregnancy outcomes in polycystic ovary syndrome
Authors: Xiu Huang, Nan Lu, Hui Yu, Fan Yang, Lihua Wang, Meili Cai, Diliqingna Dilimulati, Yuqin Zhang, Jiayi Lu, Changbin Li, Shen Qu, Lan Fang, Hongwei Ji, Manna Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the impact of 1-hour plasma glucose (1 h-PG) on the metabolic characteristics and pregnancy outcomes in polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS).MethodsThis multicenter study analyzed 970 PCOS patients (2019–2025), including 289 undergoing assisted reproductive technology (198 successful deliveries). Participants were stratified by glucose tolerance: Group 1 (normal: fasting PG [FPG] ≤ 6.1, 1 h-PG < 8.6, 2-hour PG [2 h-PG] <7.8 mmol/L); Group 2 (isolated 1 h-prediabetes: 8.6 ≤ 1 h-PG < 11.6, FPG ≤ 6.1, 2 h-PG < 7.8 mmol/L); Group 3 (traditional 2 h-prediabetes: 7.8 ≤ 2 h-PG ≤ 11.1, FPG ≤ 6.1 mmol/L). Data included anthropometrics, metabolic biomarkers, sex hormones, and pregnancy outcomes were compared across three groups.ResultsPCOS with isolated 1 h-prediabetes (Group 2) had a more unfavorable metabolic profile with regard to metabolic traits, but were not significantly different from those of the traditional 2 h-prediabetes (Group 3). The adjusted odds ratios (ORs) for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, metabolic syndrome (MetS), and hyperuricemia in PCOS with Group 2 were 1.451 (1.013–2.079, P = 0.042), 1.706 (1.188–2.450, P = 0.004), 2.957 (1.755–4.981, P < 0.001), 1.890 (1.327–2.692, P < 0.001), respectively. For pregnancy outcomes, PCOS in Group 2 were more likely to progress to gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) than those of Group 1, which was similarly observed in Group 3. The adjusted OR for GDM in the Group 2 was 4.065 (1.530–10.800, P = 0.005).ConclusionsOur study demonstrated that similar to 2 h-PG, elevated 1 h-PG was associated with different metabolic disorders and GDM. Therefore, 1 h-PG may serve as an additional marker of adverse metabolic status in women with PCOS.Subject terms: Endocrinology, Risk factors

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most common endocrine disorders in women of reproductive age, with a reported prevalence of 6–20% [1]. While traditionally characterized by irregular menstruation and hyperandrogenism (HA), PCOS is now recognized as a multisystem metabolic disorder with lifelong health implications [2]. A hallmark feature is insulin resistance (IR), present in 50–75% of cases, which drives hyperinsulinemia and predisposes to metabolic syndrome (MetS), dysglycemia (including prediabetes and type 2 diabetes mellitus [T2DM]) [3], hypertension [4], hyperuricemia [5], and obstetric complications [2]. Notably, women with PCOS more frequently require assisted reproductive technology (ART) for conception, and face heightened pregnancy risks such as gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) [6], which may adversely affect both maternal and offspring outcomes [7].

Plasma glucose during oral glucose tolerance testing (OGTT) may serve as an early metabolic marker. The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) recently proposed diagnostic thresholds for prediabetes (intermediate hyperglycemia, IH; 1-h plasma glucose (1 h-PG) ≥ 8.6 mmol/L) and T2DM (1 h-PG ≥ 11.6 mmol/L), which could enable earlier identification of high-risk individuals while reducing OGTT testing time and costs [8]. Substantial evidence links 1 h-PG ≥ 8.6 mmol/L with IR [9], abdominal obesity [10, 11], MetS [10–12] and elevated uric acid (UA) [13]. However, data on 1 h-PG abnormalities in PCOS remain limited. Ezeh et al. revealed that women with amenorrheic PCOS had a significantly higher 1 h-PG level and higher risk of developing abnormal glucose tolerance when compared to other types of hyperandrogenic women with PCOS (ovulatory- eumenorrhea, anovulatory-eumenorrhea, and oligomenorrhea) [3]. Zhao et al. noted that women with PCOS with sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) levels of <51.90 nmol/L had higher 1 h-PG levels [14]. Notably, the effects of elevated 1 h-PG on metabolic profiles, especially pregnancy outcomes, in patients with PCOS are unclear.

In this multicenter study, we stratified patients with PCOS into three glycemic categories: normal plasma glucose (NPG), isolated 1 h-prediabetes (1 h-PG 8.6–11.6 mmol/L), and traditional 2 h-prediabetes (2-hour plasma glucose [2 h-PG] 7.8–11.1 mmol/L). Through comprehensive analysis of clinical biochemical parameters and metabolic profiles across these groups, we aimed to: (1) characterize distinct metabolic phenotypes associated with different prediabetes classifications; (2) evaluate their associations with pregnancy outcomes; and (3) explore whether 1 h-PG may provide additional information regarding metabolic and reproductive characteristics in patients with PCOS.

Materials and methods

Study population

This multicenter study consecutively recruited a total of 1587 participants attending the Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital, Shanghai Renji Hospital, and Chenzhou First People’s Hospital during 2019–2025 with the following inclusion criteria: (1) with a definite diagnosis of PCOS according to the 2003 Rotterdam diagnostic criteria [oligo- or anovulation, clinical and/or biochemical signs of HA, polycystic ovaries and exclusion of other etiologies (congenital adrenal hyperplasia, androgen-secreting tumors, Cushing’s syndrome), 2 out of 3] [15], (2) standardized OGTT after 75 g glucose load with complete data of fasting plasma glucose (FPG), 1 h-PG and 2 h-PG was performed. The exclusion criteria included hyperprolactinemia, adrenal tumor, congenital adrenal hyperplasia; abnormal thyroid function; hypereosinophilic syndrome, severe infections, severe anemia, and other hematologic disorders; organic ovarian cysts; severe psychological disorders; history of medications in the past three months, such as estrogen, antiandrogens, anti-diabetic drugs, weight-loss drugs, and lipid-lowering drugs. Patients with pre-existing diabetes were also excluded to avoid: (1) confounding by glucose-lowering treatments, and (2) metabolic ceiling effects—wherein advanced diabetes stabilizes at a pathological plateau, masking progressive IR and early metabolic deterioration. After screening for the above requirements, 970 patients were finally enrolled in this study. The data distribution of patients for each center and the screening strategy for enrolled patients were illustrated in Fig. 1, and the locations of Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital, Shanghai Renji Hospital, and Chenzhou First People’s Hospital were shown in S-Fig. 1.

Protocol

Baseline study

All patients were required to fill out a standardized questionnaire containing information about their age, menstrual history, and medication history. And they also received a complete physical examination in a fasted state. Height was measured twice by two researchers using a calibrated electronic height meter without shoes, accurate to 0.1 cm; weight was measured twice by two researchers using a calibrated electronic scale, accurate to 0.01 kg; then body mass index (BMI) was calculated, waist circumference (WC) was measured twice by two researchers at the level of iliac crest, accurate to 0.01 cm. After sitting quietly in a fasting state for 10 min, with a 5-min interval, blood pressure (BP) was measured twice by two researchers, then the mean of two measurements was further analyzed. Two researchers applied a modified Ferriman-Gallwey (mF-G) scoring system to screen for hirsutism, and a mF-G score of ≥4 indicated the presence of hirsutism [16, 17].

OGTT test after 75 g glucose load was performed following an 8-hour overnight fast. Fasting venous blood was drawn for the analysis of FPG and fasting insulin, as well as liver and kidney function related indexes and sex hormones; then counting from the time of the first sip of sugar water, venous blood was drawn at 1 h for the analysis of 1 h-PG and 1 h-insulin; similarly, venous blood was drawn at 2 h from the time of the first sip of sugar water for the analysis of 2 h-PG and 2 h-insulin.

In addition, either transvaginal ultrasound or transanal ultrasound were conducted to assess ovarian morphology. According to the 2003 Rotterdam consensus on diagnostic criteria related to PCOS, 12 or more follicles with a diameter of 2–9 mm per ovary and/or increased ovarian volume (>10 ml) was defined as polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM) [18]. Meanwhile, HA included biochemical HA and clinical HA. Biochemical HA referred to an elevated level of total testosterone (TT) (TT > 1.67 nmol/L); clinical HA was defined as the presence of hirsutism alone, or the presence of female-pattern hair loss and acne isolation (without hirsutism) [16]. Ovulatory dysfunction (OD) was defined as a menstrual cycle of <21 days or > 35 days, or <8 periods per year [16]. Then, according to the 2003 Rotterdam diagnostic criteria and the 2012 National Institutes of Health (NIH) PCOS phenotyping criteria [18, 19], PCOS individuals were divided into four different types: HA + OD + PCOM (A); HA + OD (B); HA + PCOM (C); OD + PCOM (D).

All of the above data were recorded and retrieved from the corresponding hospital’s electrical medical system, and all data were double-checked by another researcher. According to the Third Report of the National Cholesterol Education Program (NCEP), a total cholesterol (TC) level ≥5.17 mmol/L, a total triglyceride (TG) level ≥1.7 mmol/L, a low-density lipoprotein (LDL) level ≥3.36 mmol/L, or a high-density lipoprotein (HDL) level <1.29 mmol/L (females) was defined as hyperlipidemia [20]. According to the definition of the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists (AACE), a diagnosis of MetS requires the presence of high risk of IR or BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 or waist ≥ 88 cm (women) with two of the following four factors: TG ≥ 1.7 mmol/L, HDL < 1.3 mmol/L (females), systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 130 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 85 mm Hg and FPG ≥ 6.1 mmol/L or 2 h-PG ≥ 7.8 mmol/L [21, 22]. In the MetS definition, elevated BP was considered as one component and defined as SBP ≥ 130 mm Hg or DBP ≥ 85 mmHg. However, in 2017, American College of Cardiology (ACC) hypertension guidelines had proposed that a BP ≥ 130/80 mmHg as the diagnostic threshold for hypertension [23] and women with PCOS exhibited an adverse cardiometabolic profile early in life [24–26], thus, hypertension defined in women with PCOS as SBP ≥ 130 mm Hg or DBP ≥ 80 mm Hg may be of greater clinical value. In addition, blood UA level ≥360 µmol/L was defined as hyperuricemia [27].

Follow-up study

Of the 970 patients with PCOS enrolled, 289 underwent ART. From the time they received ART, a phone-call follow-up was performed every two weeks to confirm whether they conceived successfully. Among the patients who were successfully conceived, face-to-face visits were conducted every three months until they delivered. Maternal pregnancy outcomes included premature labor (delivery after 28 weeks but before 37 weeks of gestation) [28] or full-term labor, cesarean delivery or natural birth, gestational weight gain, gestational hypertension (BP ≥ 140/90 mmHg after 20 weeks of gestation) [28], and GDM (FPG ≥ 5.1 mmol/L or 1 h-PG ≥ 10.0 mmol/L or 2 h-PG ≥ 8.5 mmol/L) [29]. Offspring outcomes included birth weight and birth length. After successful delivery, patients were followed up by telephone every 6 months to record weight information of their offspring.

Eventually, 74 had failed embryo transfer, 13 had not yet transferred, and 4 underwent spontaneous or induced abortions, thus, a total of 198 individuals with PCOS ultimately had successful deliveries. The maternal and offspring outcomes were analyzed in these 198 patients. In accordance with the World Health Organization (WHO) child growth standards, we utilized WHO Anthro software (Version 3.2.2.1) to standardize the weight of their offspring, which was expressed as weight-for-age Z-score (WAZ) [30]. The paradigm of consultation and follow-up for this group of patients was displayed in S-Fig. 2.

Statistical analysis

In order to investigate the effect of 1 h and 2 h-PG on metabolic characteristics and pregnancy outcomes in patients with PCOS, we divided these patients into three groups according to their 0, 1 and 2 h-PG: Group 1 (NPG): FPG ≤ 6.1 mmol/L, 1 h-PG < 8.6 mmol/L, 2 h-PG < 7.8 mmol/L; Group 2 (isolated 1 h-prediabetes): FPG ≤ 6.1 mmol/L, 8.6 mmol/L ≤ 1 h-PG < 11.6 mmol/L, 2 h-PG < 7.8 mmol/L; Group 3 (traditional 2 h-prediabetes): FPG ≤ 6.1 mmol/L, 7.8 mmol/L ≤ 2 h-PG ≤ 11.1 mmol/L. The clinical characteristics and pregnancy outcomes were statistically described. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and non-normally distributed variables were summarized as median (interquartile range, IQR), while categorical variables were expressed as count (percentages). One-way ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis H test were used to compare intergroup differences for continuous variables as appropriate. Chi-Square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare intergroup differences for categorical variables. Nonlinear regression or binary logistic regression was used to analyze the effect of 1 h-PG and 2 h-PG on various metabolic indicators and pregnancy outcomes. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves with 3 knots (at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles) were fitted using logistic regression models to explore the dose-response relationship between 1 h-PG/2 h-PG and the risk of metabolic disorders and GDM. In sensitivity analyses, we utilized chained equations involving an iterative series of predictive mean matching, logistic regression, and polytomous regression models to perform multiple imputations of key variables missing from the original data [31]. The multiple imputation model included variables presented in Table 1 (including age, PCOS classification, BMI, BP, TC, TG, LDL, HDL, UA, HbA1C, fasting insulin, 1-h insulin, 2-h insulin) to generate five imputed datasets. Following multiple imputations, five complete datasets were generated and each of them was analyzed individually. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. All data analysis was performed using SPSS software (Version 27.0.1) and R package (Version 4.4.1).

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics in patients with PCOS

Table 1 presents the characteristics of 970 patients with PCOS stratified by glycemic status, revealing progressive metabolic deterioration from Group 1 to Group 2 (isolated 1 h-prediabetes) and Group 3 (traditional 2 h-prediabetes). Group 3 showed significantly higher age, BMI, and WC compared to Groups 2 and 1, respectively (P for all<0.001). Prevalence of metabolic comorbidities increased progressively from Group 1 to 3: hypertension, hyperlipidemia, MetS, and hyperuricemia (P for all <0.001). While testosterone (T) was comparable, luteinizing hormone (LH), LH/follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and anti-mullerian hormone (AMH) decreased from Group 1 to 3 (P for all <0.05). PCOS phenotypes were similarly distributed. Glucose parameters between the three groups are shown in S-Fig. 3.

Association between 1 h-PG and hypertension, hyperlipidemia, MetS, and hyperuricemia in patients with PCOS

The RCS showed that the odds ratios (ORs) for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, MetS, and hyperuricemia increased with increasing 1 h and 2 h-PG, and the overall ORs were statistically significant expect for 2 h-PG and hyperlipidemia (Fig. 2A–H). As outlined in the “materials and methods”, five datasets were generated following multiple imputation, with the major findings summarized in Table 2. In binary logistic regression, when compared with Group 1, patients with PCOS in Group 2 were more likely to have higher incidences of hypertension (unadjusted OR: 1.699, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.210–2.387, P = 0.002), hyperlipidemia (unadjusted OR: 1.804, 95% CI: 1.264–2.575, P = 0.001), MetS (unadjusted OR: 3.281, 95% CI: 2.116–5.089, P < 0.001) and hyperuricemia (unadjusted OR: 2.103, 95% CI: 1.506–2.938, P < 0.001). After adjustment for age, BMI and PCOS classification, the ORs of Group 2 for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, MetS, and hyperuricemia were 1.451 (1.013–2.079, P = 0.042), 1.706 (1.188–2.450, P = 0.004), 2.957 (1.755–4.981, P < 0.001), 1.890 (1.327–2.69, P < 0.001), respectively (Table 2). Similarly, the higher incidences of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, MetS, and hyperuricemia were demonstrated in the Group 3 when compared with Group 1 (Table 2). These results were consistent with those from the original dataset that without multiple imputations and the remaining 4 datasets that with multiple imputations. Meanwhile, the directionality of the findings from all datasets was in line with the major analysis (S-Tables 1–5).

Pregnancy outcomes

Of all 970 patients enrolled in this study, 289 patients underwent ART to conceive, and 198 of them eventually delivered successfully. Data on pregnancy outcomes were summarized in Supplementary Table 6. Compared with Group 1, patients with PCOS both in Group 2 and Group 3 had a higher incidence of GDM (9.09% vs. 27.91% vs. 30.36%, P = 0.001). There were not significantly difference in delivery type, delivery mode, weight gain during pregnancy, gestational hypertension, birth weight, and birth length of the fetus, WAZs of their offspring among the three groups (Supplementary Table 6).

Association between 1 h-PG and GDM in patients with PCOS who received ART

The RCS showed that the risk for GDM increased with increasing 1 h-PG, whereas the same trend was not observed in 2 h-PG (Fig. 2I, J). Compared with Group 1, Group 2 tended to develop GDM with an unadjusted OR of 3.871 (1.488–10.067, P = 0.006). After adjustment for age, BMI, and PCOS classification, the OR of Group 2 for developing GDM was 4.065 (1.530–10.800, P = 0.005) (Table 2). Similarity, compared with Group 1, patients with PCOS in Group 3 were more likely to progress to GDM during pregnancy with an unadjusted OR of 4.359 (1.788–10.626, P = 0.001). After adjustment for age, BMI and PCOS classification, the OR of Group 3 for developing GDM remained statistically significant.

Discussion

This large multicenter study showed that elevated 1 h-PG levels (8.6–11.6 mmol/L)—the newly proposed prediabetes threshold, were associated with adverse metabolic characteristics in women with PCOS, similar to those observed among women with traditional 2 h-PG-defined prediabetes (7.8–11.1 mmol/L). Both dysglycemic subgroups showed significantly higher prevalence of hypertension, MetS, hyperlipidemia, and hyperuricemia than normoglycemic women with PCOS, with particular clinical relevance for women with PCOS underwent ART who showed parallel susceptibility to GDM. These findings carry three key implications: First, these findings suggest that elevated 1 h-PG may serve an early metabolic warning signal in PCOS, potentially preceding 2 h-PG abnormalities. Second, the results indicate that the potential role of incorporating 1 h-PG into screening strategies for women with PCOS needs further prospective evaluation. Third, in the ART-treated subgroup, elevated 1 h-PG was associated with GDM, suggesting that further risk assessment investigation is needed for this subgroup in clinical practice.

Recently, the IDF issued a statement clarifying that 1 h-PG in the OGTT ≥ 8.6 mmol/L, which was defined as 1 h-prediabetes, could occur before impaired glucose tolerance, and was associated with various metabolic disorders [10, 12, 32–34] and an increased risk of T2DM [8].

In this multicenter cohort study, similar to the traditional 2 h-prediabetes group, PCOS with 1 h-PG ≥ 8.6 mmol/L had a significantly higher prevalence of hyperlipidemia than those with normal glucose, suggesting that elevated 1 h-PG was associated with a less favorable lipid profile in PCOS. Pareek et al. conducted a 37-year follow-up investigation of participants from the Malmö Preventive Project (1974–1992) and found that elevated 1 h-PG (≥8.6 mmol/L) was associated with dyslipidemia (higher TG and TC levels) [34]. Similarly, Andreozzi et al. found that 1 h-PG ≥ 8.6 mmol/L was associated with increased TG levels and decreased HDL levels in 1011 subjects with non-diabetes from the CAtanzaro MEtabolic RIsk factors Study (CATAMERIS) [32]. Neves et al. stated in 2022 that in patients with prediabetes with elevated 1 h-PG, the same intensive approach to dyslipidemia also needed to be considered as recommended for diabetes [35]. In contrast, Bergman et al. utilized a cohort from the Israel Study of Glucose Intolerance, Obesity and Hypertension (The Israel GOH Study) and concluded that 1 h-PG ≥ 8.6 mmol/L was not associated with lipid levels in the study population [36]. The reasons for the inconsistency in the results of these studies may be due to the differences in the inclusion and exclusion criteria for the enrolled participants, as well as the differences in the gender, age, and ethnicity of the populations that were ultimately included in each study. To our knowledge, evidence regarding the association between 1 h-PG and dyslipidemia among women with PCOS remains limited. A key mechanism underlying this association may involve IR [37]. Specifically, hyperglycemia can disrupt lipoprotein lipase activity, which in turn contributes to the development of secondary hypertriglyceridemia [38].

MetS was a major risk factor for cardiometabolic disorders in patients with diabetes and prediabetes. Park et al. followed up a group of 5389 Koreans with non-diabetes, and pointed out that 1 h-PG ≥ 8.6 mmol/L increased the incidence of MetS, and it was superior than 2 h-PG in predicting MetS [12]. Similarly, Haverals et al. conducted a cross-sectional study of 2439 Caucasians with obesity and revealed that subjects with 1 h-PG ≥ 8.6 mmol/L had an increased risk of MetS [10]. However, few studies had been conducted in a population with PCOS. Consistent with previous findings, our study demonstrated that 1 h-PG ≥ 8.6 mmol/L was associated with an increased risk of MetS in the population with PCOS.

Early in 2011, Perticone et al. conducted a study on 955 patients with uncomplicated hypertension from the CATAMERIS and showed that a 1 h-PG ≥ 8.6 mmol/L was associated with increased UA levels [13]. Subsequently, Fiorentino and Cefalo et al., utilizing the same study cohort (CATAMERIS), also came to consistent conclusions [39, 40]. And our study also revealed for the first time that 1 h-PG ≥ 8.6 mmol/L was also associated with an increased incidence of hyperuricemia in populations with PCOS. Previous studies indicated that hyperuricemia is a possible side effect of IR; and in turn, UA can contribute to IR through oxidative stress, inflammatory processes and impaired endothelial function [41, 42].

Our study also revealed that in populations with PCOS, those with elevated 1-PG levels have a significantly elevated risk of hypertension when compared to those with normal blood glucose. Similarly, a retrospective study of 996 patients with non-diabetes by Pramodkumar et al. showed that those with elevated 1 h-PG levels had a significantly higher risk of developing hypertension compared to those with normal blood glucose [11].

PCOS was one of the leading causes of anovulatory infertility in women of childbearing age; therefore, patients with PCOS were more likely to undergo ART to conceive [7]. Numerous studies had shown that patients with PCOS tended to exhibit more adverse pregnancy and offspring outcomes, such as greater pregnancy weight gain, preterm birth, lower birth weight, shorter birth length, pregnancy-induced hypertension, preeclampsia, GDM, cesarean section, and so on [7, 43–46]. However, few studies investigated the relationship between 1 h-PG and adverse pregnancy outcomes in a population with PCOS yet.

Our findings suggested that in populations with PCOS, elevated 1 h-PG was associated only with GDM and not with other adverse pregnancy outcomes. In 2016, Temming et al. conducted a retrospective analysis of 5983 women with pregnancy and showed that elevated 1 h-PG was associated with GDM, pregnancy-induced hypertension, cesarean section, macrosomia, and preterm labor [47]. While in 2019, in a follow-up study of 1976 women with high-risk pregnancy, Zheng et al. showed that 1 h-PG was significantly elevated in patients with GDM, and that the risk of other adverse pregnancy outcomes such as preterm labor, macrosomia, cesarean section, and pre-eclampsia rised with each one unit increase for 1 h-PG [48]. In our study population, the limited number of patients who underwent ART and eventually delivered successfully may have resulted in limited statistical efficacy of their pregnancy outcomes, which may explain the discrepancy between our findings and other previous findings.

Compared to previous studies, our study had some advantages. It was a multicenter population-based study of PCOS cohort with a relatively large sample size; According to FPG, 1 h-PG and 2 h-PG, the study population was strictly grouped into 3 groups (normal blood glucose, isolated 1 h-prediabetes, traditional 2 h-prediabetes); we also encapsulated a certain amount of pregnancy follow-up data in the present study. Our results demonstrated that both isolated 1 h-prediabetes and traditional 2 h-prediabetes were independently associated with an increased risk of hypertension, dyslipidemia, hyperuricemia, and MetS. In addition, elevated 1 h-PG and 2 h-PG were also correlated with GDM susceptibility in the relevant subgroup analysis. In clinical practice, 1 h-PG may have potential practical advantages such as improving patient compliance, saving the time of OGTT and making patients screening more convenient; however, given the observational nature of this study, and we did not directly assess these clinical implications, this hypothesis requires further prospective evaluation.

Overall, our study was the first multicenter research to explore the association of 1 h-PG with metabolic characteristics and pregnancy outcomes in populations with PCOS. Inevitably, our study also had some limitations. First, this study was a multicenter cross-sectional study with inconsistent data completeness across centers, which resulted in a certain percentage of missing data overall; second, the extended follow-up period necessary to capture pregnancy outcomes resulted in finite number of completed pregnancies available for analysis at this time. This may compromise the statistical efficacy of the pregnancy outcomes, as evidenced by the wide confidence intervals for certain estimates (e.g., GDM in Group 2), and these findings should be interpreted with caution. Thus, the analysis of pregnancy outcomes should be considered as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and future prospective cohorts or larger multi-center studies are needed to confirm these observations. What is more, we chose a BMI cut off of 25 kg/m2 for the definition of MetS according to the guidelines, which may have reduced sensitivity for identifying a small subset of individuals with high-risk in our study population because lower thresholds are often used in Asian population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, elevated 1 h-PG was associated with adverse metabolic characteristics (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, MetS, and hyperuricemia) and adverse pregnancy outcomes (GDM) in women with PCOS, which was similar to that observed for traditional 2 h-PG-defined prediabetes. These findings suggest that 1 h-PG may serve as an additional marker for metabolic risk assessment, although its clinical utility requires further validation in prospective studies.
