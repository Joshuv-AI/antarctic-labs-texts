A multiscale analysis of compressive stress-enhanced carbide precipitation in deep cryogenic treated martensitic steel for shovel teeth
Authors: Pejman Hajipour, Waris Nawaz Khan, Jack Cahn, Shaofeng Sun, Leijun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
It is well known that the deep cryogenic treatment (DCT) hardens a steel alloy by generating more martensite, yet we find that, for a martensitic steel, the cryogenic treatment has resulted in little more martensite but an improved strain hardening, better elongation, a slight decrease in hardness, and an enhanced abrasive wear resistance. This paper reports a mechanistic study into the rationale for these unconventional results. It is shown that the DCT at − 180 °C followed by tempering at 200 °C significantly altered the kinetics and pathways of carbide precipitation by accelerating carbon diffusion under compressive stresses, enabling η-carbide that preceded M₇C₃ and M₂₃C₆ carbides during tempering. Dilatometric analysis revealed a marked drop in the apparent activation energy for η-carbide formation, from 59 kJ mol⁻¹ in the pre-DCT to 12 kJ mol⁻¹ post-DCT, indicating enhanced nucleation possibly driven by lattice contraction and defect redistribution on-heating. X-ray diffraction, electron microscopy, and dynamic light scattering indicated the formation of η-carbide during DCT, which served as precursors for a refined dispersion of M₇C₃ and M₂₃C₆ carbides during tempering. Thermo-Calc calculations indicated that the driving force for η-carbide nucleation remains favorable after DCT and at low temperatures. Finite element simulations showed that the cryogenic thermal cycle generates compressive surface stresses, part of which is retained as residual stress after DCT. XRD measurements confirmed a reduction in surface stress, while EBSD analysis revealed decreased lattice strain and GND density after DCT, with a slight increase following low-temperature tempering. Therefore, the cryogenically induced carbon diffusion under compressive stresses may have enhanced fine carbide precipitations that result in an improved mechanical performance for a martensitic steel widely used in the mining industry.

Introduction

Martensitic steels continue to play a crucial role in industrial applications, particularly in the mining and energy sectors. These steels are engineered to endure significant mechanical loads, primarily in the form of wear, toughness, and ductility. The performance of martensitic shovel teeth during excavation activities is pivotal, given their exposure to abrasive materials and high-impact forces encountered during operations1. Wear resistance is especially critical for shovel teeth, as they are constantly subjected to abrasion by soil and rock. This wear can lead to premature shovel teeth failure if not adequately managed. One of the primary methods to enhance wear resistance is through the optimization of the steel’s microstructure, which can be achieved by introducing hard carbides, such as those created during the tempering process2,3. These secondary carbides can impede dislocation movements, resulting in a strong yet ductile matrix capable of withstanding dynamic loads4.

Deep Cryogenic Treatment (DCT), subjecting materials to very low temperatures (down to − 196 °C), facilitates several significant transformations and modifications that enhance the mechanical properties, wear resistance, and toughness performance5,6. One of the primary mechanisms of DCT is the transformation of retained austenite into martensite. Studies by Yao and Zhou7 demonstrate that DCT effectively reduces the volume fraction of retained austenite, transforming it into hard martensite. This transformation enhances mechanical properties, including hardness and wear resistance. DCT relieves pre-existing tensile residual stresses that originate from quenching and machining while simultaneously introducing beneficial compressive residual stresses, which improved wear resistance8. Reducing residual stresses is particularly important in applications where materials are subjected to cyclic loads, as compressive residual stresses help to counteract the effects of tensile work stresses that can lead to fatigue crack initiation and propagation. Another critical mechanism for DCT is the refinement of carbide precipitation and distribution. Research indicated that DCT could promote a uniform precipitation of fine secondary carbides9. The precipitation of these carbides was discussed in studies by Zare et al.10 and Jimbert et al.11, who highlighted how a more uniform distribution of carbides had improved strength and reinforced the steel matrix.

The impact of DCT on mechanical properties, specifically the contributions by carbide evolution during the subsequent tempering process, warrants a deeper investigation. Baldissera and Delprete12 discussed how DCT could facilitate the precipitation of carbides. Their findings indicated that the conditions of DCT significantly affected the effectiveness of carbide refinement and distribution within the steel matrix. Firouzdor13 provided insights into the beneficial effects of tempering after cryogenic treatment of the M2 high-speed steel. Their results showed that low-temperature tempering could facilitate the precipitation of fine carbides. Calzada et al.14 systematically investigated how low temperature tempering affected carbides in carburized steels following cryogenic treatment and found a significant increase in the volume fraction of precipitated carbides during the temper. The findings of Li and Wu15 revealed how tempering at specific temperatures post-DCT could influence carbide evolution. They observed both micro-sized and nano-sized carbides, indicating that DCT might have set the stage for further carbide refinements during subsequent tempering.

While enhanced martensitic transformation has been accepted as the main mechanism for DCT, potential new mechanisms of carbide formation and evolution during the tempering phase require further quantification. This study aims to measure the multiscale effects of deep cryogenic treatment on tempering behavior and carbide formation in steels used for mining operations, particularly shovel teeth.

Materials and experimental procedures

Martensitic steel shovel-tooth samples were investigated in this study. The components were sourced in their industrially manufactured, as-received condition. These shovel teeth are produced by casting followed by quenching and tempering to obtain a hardened, tempered-martensite microstructure with very low retained austenite. Coupons extracted from these components served as the baseline microstructure for assessing the effects of deep cryogenic treatment. The chemical composition of the steel was determined using Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES) and shown in Table 1. The cryogenic treatment was performed using two sets of procedures, each targeting a different minimum temperature: −150 °C and − 180 °C. For both procedures, the treatment followed a three-stage thermal cycle. First, samples were cooled from room temperature to the target cryogenic temperature over a period of 8 h. Once the target temperature was reached, the samples were held isothermally for 8 h to ensure thermal equilibrium. Then, the samples were returned to room temperature through a controlled ramp-up over 4 h. All treatments were conducted in a temperature-programmable cryogenic chamber to maintain precise control over cooling and heating rates and to ensure reproducibility of the procedure. The complete temperature–time profiles for the applied cryogenic and tempering treatments are presented in Fig. 1, detailing the DCT stages at − 150 °C and − 180 °C and subsequent tempering at 200 °C, 300 °C, and 400 °C. This figure also defines the sample naming convention used throughout the study based on the specific treatment conditions.

Phase transformation kinetics were examined using dilatometry. Cylindrical samples with a diameter of 5 mm and a length of 10 mm were subjected to both non-isothermal and isothermal heat treatments in a quench deformation dilatometer (DIL L78 RITA, Linseis Messgeraete GmbH, Germany). Thermal cycles were applied using controlled heating and cooling rates of 50 °C/min, 10 °C/min and 20 °C/min, during which dimensional changes were continuously recorded. These measurements were used to determine the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) as well as the start temperatures of phase transformations. Isothermal dilatometry tests conducted at selected temperatures provided insight into the kinetics of carbide nucleation and growth at given temperatures. Activation energy values for these transformations were further estimated using the Kissinger’s method16.

In-situ X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed using a Bruker D8 Discover diffractometer (Bruker, USA) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). The sample was placed in a TC-DOME low-temperature chamber equipped with a beryllium dome to enable measurements during the thermal cycle. Diffraction patterns were collected over a 2θ range of 30–100° with a step size of 0.02° at four stages of the thermal cycle: room temperature (as-received), − 180 °C after equilibration, 200 °C after reheating, and room temperature after completion of the DCT cycle. XRD analysis was also conducted on electrolytically extracted carbide particles to identify crystalline carbide phases. Carbides were isolated by electrolytic dissolution in a solution of 10% HCl in ethanol, followed by collection, rinsing, and drying. The extracted carbide powders were scanned over a 2θ range of 20–70° at a scan rate of 1°/min using the same diffractometer and radiation source. Residual stresses were also quantified using X-ray diffraction in accordance with ASTM E2860-12. Measurements were conducted on a Proto iXRD system equipped with a Cr Kα radiation source (λ = 2.291 Å). The {211} ferrite diffraction peak was selected, and data were collected using a series of psi tilts (± 30.00, ± 23.46, ± 16.32, ± 11.80, and 0°) to obtain strain from the d–sin²ψ slope. Each scan consisted of 10 one-second exposures, yielding a total collection time of approximately 8 min per point. Peak positions were determined using a Gaussian fitting routine with linear background subtraction. The instrument’s X-ray elastic constant (XEC) of 20,200 ksi for ferritic steel was applied. To complement the structural data, Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) measurements were conducted using an ALV / CGS-3 system (ALV-Laser Vertriebsgesellschaft mbH, Germany) to determine the particle size distribution of the extracted carbides, enabling assessment of particle refinement and dispersion induced by the thermal treatments.

Thermodynamic calculations were carried out using Thermo-Calc software (Thermo-Calc Software AB, Sweden) with the TCFE10 steel database. Equilibrium phase and Scheil solidification diagrams were generated to predict phase formation sequences during solidification, solid-state cooling, and thermal treatment. The chemical driving force and interfacial energy for phase nucleation were calculated across a wide temperature range. Nucleation energy values were then determined using a classical nucleation theory17, based on the computed thermodynamic parameters.

Microstructural characterization was performed using optical microscopy and field emission scanning electron microscopy. Samples were mechanically polished and etched with 2% Nital to reveal the microstructure. High-resolution imaging was carried out using a Zeiss Sigma FESEM (Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) equipped with an Oxford EDX system (Oxford Instruments, UK) to observe carbide morphology and distribution in both bulk specimens and electrolytically extracted carbides. Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analysis was performed on polished cross-sections using an Oxford EBSD detector (Oxford Instruments, UK) mounted on a ThermoFisher Helios Hydra PFIB/SEM system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). EBSD data were processed for grain boundaries, grain size, phases, misorientation angles, and kernel average misorientation (KAM).

Mechanical properties were evaluated through tensile testing, hardness measurements, and abrasive wear testing. The tensile tests were conducted according to ASTM E8/E8M using a universal testing machine (MTS Model C45, MTS Systems Corporation, USA). Hardness was measured on the Rockwell C scale in accordance with ASTM E384 using an AFFRI 206 EX hardness tester (AFFRI, Italy). Five indents were placed on each sample, and the average value with standard deviation was reported. Abrasive wear resistance was assessed using ASTM G65-16e1 Procedure, where the mass loss and wear volume were measured to quantify material performance under dry sand–rubber wheel conditions. The wear test was done using a neoprene rubber wheel and standardized dry silica sand (AFS 50–70). The applied load was 130 N, with a rubber wheel rotation speed of 200 rpm and a sand feed rate of 300–400 g/min. Each test lasted 6000 wheel revolutions, corresponding to approximately 15 min of abrasion under constant load.

Finite Element Analysis (FEA) was conducted using Abaqus CAE software to simulate the thermal stress evolution during the cryogenic treatment cycles. A 3D model of the shovel tooth coupon was created, incorporating experimentally determined or ThermoCalc calculated temperature-dependent material properties, including thermal conductivity, specific heat, and coefficient of thermal expansion.

The activation energy quantifies the barrier for carbide nucleation under different treatment conditions. A progressive reduction in energy with lower cryogenic temperatures indicates a more favorable environment for carbide formation. This trend likely reflects enhanced carbon mobility and increased number of nucleation sites due to cryogenic-induced stress and microstructural rearrangements22. The energy barrier for carbide precipitation is controlled by the interplay between the thermodynamic driving force for carbide formation (ΔG), the interfacial energy between the carbide and matrix (γ), and the diffusion rates and nucleation site availability. Factors such as alloy composition, microstructural defects, applied stresses, and temperature influence these parameters, collectively determining the ease and rate of carbide nucleation23. Because the transformation peak corresponds to transition carbide precipitation, the process is primarily controlled by the formation of new nuclei rather than by long-range growth or coarsening. At Stage II, diffusion distances are short, and the interfacial energy term dominates the transformation kinetics, making the apparent activation energy closely representative of the nucleation barrier.

Isothermal dilatometric tests were conducted for 2 h of tempering to further investigate the kinetics of carbide nucleation, growth, and coarsening at constant temperatures. These tests were performed at 200 °C, 300 °C, and 400 °C, which correspond to key points within the previously identified transformation stages. Specifically, 200 °C represents a temperature below the peak of Stage II, where early transition carbide formation is expected to dominate; 300 °C is near the peak of Stage II, where the maximum rate of precipitation occurs; and 400 °C corresponds to Stage III, where coarsening and partial dissolution of carbides become significant. Figure 4 presents the isothermal dilatometry curves for the three samples, AR, DCT (− 150), and DCT (− 180), at 200 °C, 300 °C, and 400 °C.

Figure 4a shows the isothermal dilatometry of the three samples at 200 °C. The AR sample shows a small initial decrease in ΔL/L₀, indicating a dominant carbide dissolution process or carbon diffusion into the lattice. After 30 min, the slope of the increase in ΔL/L₀ reduces, and a plateau forms, indicating size equilibrium between small carbide dissolution and growth. The DCT (− 150) 200 °C and especially the DCT (− 180) 200 °C samples exhibit a decrease in ΔL/L₀ at the beginning of the tempering procedure, suggesting that transition carbide formation or carbide growth processes dominate. The amount of this drop in ΔL/L₀ is larger in the sample treated at the lower temperature. After the initial drop, an increase in size for both DCT-treated samples can be seen, with plateauing towards the end, indicating that carbide growth and dissolution approach a dynamic equilibrium.

The dilatometric response observed during tempering reflects the net redistribution of carbon between the tempered martensitic matrix and the evolving carbide phases. Under these conditions, precipitation, growth, coarsening, and partial dissolution of metastable carbides may occur simultaneously, depending on the local carbon concentration and diffusion kinetics. Consequently, the measured changes in ΔL/L₀ represent the combined volumetric response of these concurrent processes rather than a strictly sequential precipitation–dissolution transformation. With this framework, the dilatometric behavior observed at constant temperature can be interpreted based on changes in ΔL/L₀ over time. An increase in ΔL/L₀ suggests that carbon is diffusing into the martensite lattice as carbides dissolve, leading to lattice expansion. In contrast, a net decrease in ΔL/L₀ indicates that carbides are forming and growing, drawing carbon out of the matrix and resulting in lattice contraction. A plateau in ΔL/L₀ over time suggests a dynamic equilibrium between carbide growth and dissolution, with no significant net change in the sample’s length24.

This interpretation is consistent with the phenomenological model originally developed by Li et al.25 for phase transformation-induced dilation. Under isothermal conditions, thermal expansion remains negligible, so any observed change in sample length ΔL/L0 ​reflects microstructural changes associated with carbon redistribution between the matrix and carbide phases. Microstructural Evolution of Tempered Martensite – Microstructure evolution from the as-received (AR), to DCT-treated pre-tempering (DCT (− 180)), and DCT-treated post-tempering at 200 °C (DCT (− 180) T200) is presented next. Figure 5a and b show the microstructure of in the as-received condition, with martensitic laths of varying thicknesses. Thin laths (< 0.5 μm) contribute to hardness and strength due to a higher dislocation density. Coarser laths (> 0.5 μm), formed during the original tempering, improve the ductility and impact toughness26. Figure 5c and d show the microstructure of the DCT (− 180) sample. Despite deep cryogenic treatment, no significant microstructural changes are evident compared to the as-received sample, suggesting minimal martensitic refinement or coarsening due to DCT alone.

In Fig. 5e and f, the DCT (− 180) T200 sample displays clear evidence of martensitic coalescence, where fine laths merge into coarser structures. Martensitic coalescence typically occurs through mechanisms associated with either high-temperature tempering or plastic deformation. During tempering, this process is driven by carbon diffusion, which promotes the redistribution of carbon atoms from the supersaturated martensitic boundaries, allowing adjacent laths to merge and form coarser structures. This boundary refinement reduces internal stresses and overall system energy, resulting in a coarser martensitic structure that enhances ductility and improves impact resistance. Plastic deformation promotes martensitic coalescence through mechanical mechanisms, where the accumulation of dislocations at lath boundaries leads to pile-up and stress concentrations that force boundary migration and merging of laths. Dislocation tangling and shear band formation can further contribute, especially in high-carbon martensitic steels. These mechanisms typically require significant plastic strain to drive lath coalescence, leading to localized hardening and potentially increased brittleness due to residual stresses27. However, in this case, martensitic coalescence is observed at a relatively low tempering temperature (200 °C) without extensive plastic deformation. This suggests that DCT increases the diffusivity of carbon at lower tempering temperatures, enabling this phenomenon and other diffusion-controlled effects to occur at conditions not typically associated with such changes.

EDS line scans for both the AR and DCT (− 180) T200 samples (Fig. 5g and h) show a consistent increase in carbon counts at martensite lath boundaries. Although EDS cannot quantify carbon with high accuracy, the relative rise at these interfaces confirms that the bright boundary contrast observed in FESEM corresponds to carbon-enriched lath boundaries.

Carbide Precipitation and Stress Relaxation–EBSD phase mapping (Fig. 6a–c) revealed that the matrix of the steel samples in all conditions remained predominantly martensitic, with more than 95% identified as Martensite. Minor phases including retained austenite and carbides (Fe₃C, Cr₇C₃, and Cr₂₃C₆) were detected by EBSD phase indexing. The measured fractions should be interpreted as relative indicators of phase presence and evolution between conditions, rather than absolute phase fractions, due to the fine size and heterogeneous distribution of carbide particles. In the as-received (AR) condition, the carbide fraction was low, with Fe₃C, Cr₇C₃, and Cr₂₃C₆ present in concentrations of 0.43%, 0.49%, and 0.32%, respectively. The retained austenite content was approximately 0.31%. Following deep cryogenic treatment at − 180 °C, the total carbide content showed a slight increase, particularly in chromium-rich carbides. Upon subsequent tempering at 200 °C, the fraction of Cr₇C₃ and Cr₂₃C₆ increased further to 0.75% and 0.60%, respectively, suggesting enhanced carbide nucleation and growth driven by the tempering process.

The observed increase in carbide content after DCT and tempering is consistent with previously reported phenomena in cryogenically treated martensitic steels, where carbon redistribution and carbide precipitation are thermodynamically favored during tempering after cryogenic saturation of the matrix21. The enrichment of Cr-rich carbides such as Cr₇C₃ and Cr₂₃C₆ is particularly relevant, as these phases are known to improve wear and corrosion resistance in high-alloy martensitic steels28. The presence of retained austenite was slightly reduced following DCT and remained stable after tempering, indicating partial transformation to martensite or decomposition during the thermal cycles.

Dislocation-related metrics obtained from EBSD including Kernel Average Misorientation (KAM), Geometrically Necessary Dislocation (GND) density, and Grain Reference Orientation Deviation (GROD) were used to assess the evolution of lattice strain and defect structures (Fig. 7). In the AR condition, the mean KAM was 0.728°, and the GND density reached 14.72 × 1014 m−2, reflecting a highly strained martensitic structure with a dense dislocation network resulting from rapid quenching. The corresponding GROD mean value of 3.683° further indicated significant internal misorientations and residual strain.

Following DCT at − 180 °C, the KAM and GND mean values decreased to 0.649° and 12.09 × 1014 m−2, respectively. This reduction indicates partial dislocation rearrangement or annihilation, consistent with cryogenically induced stress relaxation and potential substructural recovery. The GROD mean also declined to 2.5°, supporting the hypothesis of residual stress relief through thermally driven lattice accommodation mechanisms at low temperatures29. This agrees with the development of compressive surface stress during the warming phase of DCT.

Characteristics and Size Distribution of Precipitated Carbides – To better understand the crystallographic response of martensitic shovel-tooth steel during DCT, in-situ X-ray diffraction experiments were performed during the complete thermal cycle. Diffraction patterns were collected at four stages: the as-received condition at room temperature, after cooling to − 180 °C, after reheating to 200 °C, and after returning to room temperature following the DCT cycle.

During the cryogenic measurements, the sample was enclosed in a beryllium dome to enable low-temperature operation. Consequently, several reflections originating from the beryllium window appear in the diffraction patterns. These reflections were identified and excluded from further analysis. Only the diffraction peaks corresponding to α′-martensite were used for lattice parameter calculations.

Three characteristic reflections of the α′-Fe martensitic phase were analyzed, namely the (110), (200), and (211) planes. These reflections provide sufficient information to determine the lattice parameters of the body-centered tetragonal (BCT) martensite structure. The diffraction results reveal clear peak shifts during the thermal cycle. Upon cooling to − 180 °C, the (110) and (200) reflections shift slightly toward lower diffraction angles, indicating an increase in the corresponding lattice spacings. In contrast, the (211) reflection shifts toward higher angles, indicating a decrease in interplanar spacing for that crystallographic direction. The opposite shifts observed for different reflections indicate an anisotropic lattice response. While the crystal lattice undergoes overall thermal contraction during cooling, the diffraction peaks shift differently depending on crystallographic orientation due to anisotropic lattice strain.

The calculated lattice parameters show that the martensitic lattice undergoes a strongly anisotropic distortion during the cryogenic cycle. The lattice parameter a increases slightly during cooling and heating, rising from 2.871 Å in the as received condition to a maximum of approximately 2.884 Å at 200 °C. In contrast, the c parameter decreases significantly from 2.986 Å to approximately 2.887 Å. Because the contraction along the c-axis is larger than the expansion along the a-axis, the overall unit-cell volume decreases during the thermal cycle.

The unit-cell volume decreases from 24.615 Å³ in the as-received condition to 24.125 Å³ after cooling to − 180 °C and further to 24.015 Å³ at 200 °C. After the complete DCT cycle and subsequent tempering at 200 °C, the unit-cell volume partially recovers but remains lower than in the as-received state (24.105 Å³). This indicates that part of the lattice distortion introduced during cryogenic exposure is retained after the thermal cycle.

A notable consequence of these changes is the strong reduction in tetragonality. The c/a ratio decreases from 1.0399 in the as-received martensite to nearly unity at 200 °C. This suggests that the cryogenic treatment and subsequent heating alter the tetragonal distortion of the martensitic lattice, likely through a combination of defect rearrangement, internal stress evolution, and redistribution of interstitial carbon.

Importantly, the peak shifts observed in the diffraction patterns demonstrate that the structural response cannot be described as a uniform lattice expansion or contraction. Instead, the martensitic lattice experiences directional changes in lattice spacing, producing an anisotropic distortion that ultimately results in a net reduction in unit-cell volume. The persistence of this contracted lattice after returning to room temperature suggests that the cryogenic cycle introduces a residual stress state within the martensitic structure.

A distinct peak observed at approximately 22.5° 2θ, corresponding to a d-spacing of ~ 3.97 Å, was identified as FeCl₂·4 H₂O, a residue from the electrolytic extraction process using hydrochloric acid and ethanol. This phase was excluded from carbide phase analysis, as it represents a preparation artifact rather than a carbide phase.

The diffraction patterns are dominated by reflections consistent with cementite-type carbides (M₃C), while several additional peaks occur in regions where Cr-containing carbides such as M₇C₃ and M₂₃C₆ are commonly reported. These peaks appear in all three conditions with varying peak widths and intensities.

Comparison of the three conditions reveals noticeable differences in peak width and intensity, particularly after deep cryogenic treatment. Several reflections exhibit peak broadening after DCT, followed by partial peak narrowing after tempering. The most pronounced change occurs at ~ 32.2°, where the FWHM increases from 0.264° in the AR condition to 0.576° after DCT, before decreasing to 0.216° after tempering. Similar behavior is observed for several additional reflections, including 33.7° (0.192° → 0.240° → 0.192°), 41.05° (0.168° → 0.192° → 0.168°), 42.4° (0.240° → 0.288° → 0.264°), 52.6° (0.216° → 0.288° → 0.192°), and 67.4° (0.240° → 0.288° → 0.192°).

The 32–34° (2θ) region corresponds to reflections frequently associated with Cr-rich carbides, particularly Cr₇C₃ and Cr₂₃C₆. The pronounced peak broadening observed in this region after DCT suggests the formation of very fine carbide precipitates during cryogenic treatment. After tempering at 200 °C, the reduction in FWHM indicates particle growth and partial coarsening, resulting in a narrower diffraction profile.

A similar trend is observed near ~ 42.4° (2θ), where the FWHM increases from 0.240° in the AR condition to 0.288° after DCT and decreases to 0.264° after tempering. This peak lies in a region where reflections from cementite, Cr-rich carbides, and transition carbides may overlap. In particular, several reflections reported for η-type carbides (Fe₂C) occur in the ~ 42–44° (2θ) range when Cu Kα radiation is used. The broadening observed in this region after DCT may therefore reflect the presence of very fine transition carbides, which are commonly reported to precipitate during early stages of tempering or carbon redistribution in martensitic steels. Such η-type carbides typically form as nanoscale precipitates prior to the development of larger cementite particles and may therefore contribute to peak broadening in overlapping diffraction regions.

The subsequent reduction in FWHM after tempering suggests carbide growth and stabilization, consistent with the transition from a fine carbide population toward coarser carbide particles during low-temperature tempering34,35.

Effect of Thermal Straining on Carbide Precipitation—A transient thermomechanical finite element analysis (FEA) was conducted to evaluate the internal stress evolution during deep cryogenic treatment of martensitic steel. A 3D model was developed in Abaqus/CAE using temperature-dependent material properties for the investigated steel, including thermal expansion coefficient, Young’s modulus, and thermal conductivity. The experimentally recorded DCT temperature profile was applied as a time-dependent thermal boundary condition.

The simulation results revealed the formation of significant residual stresses throughout the thermal cycle. During the ramp-up stage (from cryogenic temperatures to room temperature), compressive axial stresses developed at the surface, while tensile stresses accumulated in the core due to thermal gradients (Fig. 14a). These FEA predictions were compared with experimental residual stress measurements obtained via X-ray diffraction (XRD) in accordance with ASTM E2860-12. Six DCT-treated samples were analyzed across five surface positions before and after treatment to quantify changes in residual stress as seen in Fig. 14b. A reduction in longitudinal surface stress was observed after DCT, agreeing with the FEA predictions. The development of compressive surface stress during the warming phase of the cryogenic cycle was captured in the simulation and aligned with previous studies that reported similar stress evolution trends in martensitic steels subjected to DCT. The position-dependent stress reduction pattern also agreed qualitatively with the XRD data, further supporting the validity of the modeled thermal stress response.

The observed increase in carbide volume fraction after deep cryogenic treatment, as observed in XRD and EBSD, particularly when tempering follows the DCT ramp-up, can be attributed to stress-assisted enhancement of carbon diffusivity within the martensitic matrix. The foundational work by Maugis et al.36 provides a detailed mean-field elastodiffusion model demonstrating that compressive stresses significantly reduce the migration energy of carbon atoms, thus accelerating their diffusion. The acceleration of carbon diffusion caused by compressive stress during DCT not only enhances the nucleation rate of carbides but also increases the number of nucleation sites that are thermodynamically and kinetically viable. As a result, tempering following DCT leads to a greater fraction of finely dispersed carbides. This corelates to the reduction of carbide formation energy barrier during tempering that was observed in our nonisothermal dilatometry investigations. This interpretation partially aligns with the increased carbon diffusivity theory, which attributes carbide formation to contraction-induced carbon migration toward dislocations37,38. While the model acknowledges the role of volumetric contraction, the present study does not rely on dislocation pinning as a prerequisite, instead emphasizing stress-assisted diffusion. In contrast, other theories suggest that the plastic deformation during DCT generates new dislocations which can attract carbon atoms14,39,40, is not supported by our findings, because no evidence of deformation-induced dislocation accumulation was observed. Partial agreement also exists with the third theory, which attributes transition carbide formation to anisotropic lattice distortion and low-temperature carbon segregation41,42; our model focuses on isotropic compressive stress and general carbide nucleation rather than transition carbide formation along the carbon-rich bands during stress relieving and tempering.

The volumetric driving force was obtained from Thermo-Calc simulations, providing the chemical potential difference per unit volume for the formation of M₇C₃, M₂₃C₆, M₃C, and MC (η-carbide). Interfacial energy (γ) values were also extracted from Thermo-Calc where available; however, the database lacked data for η-carbide at the temperatures of interest (below ~ 500 °C). To overcome this limitation, interfacial energy values for η-carbide were extrapolated from higher-temperature data using a smooth trend assumption (shown in dashed line in Fig. 15e). The resultant nucleation energy calculations (Fig. 15f) indicate that η-carbide exhibits the lowest nucleation barrier at lower temperatures, followed by M₃C, M₂₃C₆, and M₇C₃. This supports the hypothesis that fine η-carbides preferentially form during DCT due to favorable nucleation conditions and a high driving force, before coarsening into stable phases such as M₇C₃ and M₂₃C₆ upon tempering.

All discussions suggest a stress-assisted diffusion pathway for carbide precipitation. Non-isothermal dilatometry revealed that the apparent activation energy for Stage II precipitation drops from 59 kJ mol⁻¹ in the as-received state to 12 kJ mol⁻¹ after DCT, signalling a greatly reduced nucleation barrier. Thermo-Calc calculations ranked η-carbide with the highest chemical driving force at cryogenic temperature, and finite-element analysis identified axial compression during reheating. According to the elastodiffusion model of Maugis et al., compressive stress lowers the migration enthalpy for carbon and channels diffusion along the stressed axis. Enhanced mobility results in a distribution of transition carbides that, during tempering, grow and transform into M₇C₃ and M₂₃C₆ without excessive coarsening.

Conclusions
