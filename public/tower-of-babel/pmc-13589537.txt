Reply to Comment on “Prevalence and Correlates of Sleep Problems in Children and Adolescents with Type 1 Diabetes: A Cross-Sectional Study” [Response to Letter]
Authors: Youssef A Alqahtani, Ayed A Shati, Syed Esam Mahmood
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We sincerely appreciate Dr. Wu, Dr. Yu, and Dr. Liu1 for their thorough review of our manuscript2 and for raising insightful points. Below, we provide responses to each comment.

Comment 1: Gender Imbalance (86.29% Male versus 13.71% Female)

We thank the reviewers for highlighting this important issue. We acknowledge that our sample’s sex distribution does not reflect the typically balanced ratio observed in pediatric T1DM populations globally. This skew likely results from clinic attendance patterns at our single recruitment site between January and June 2024, rather than representing the true regional distribution. We consider this a limitation of our study and advise caution when interpreting gender-specific findings, particularly the significant association between sleep problems and gender (P<0.001). To ensure data accuracy, we verified our gender information against medical records, thereby ruling out any data entry errors.

Comment 2: Sample Size Calculation/Margin of Error Discrepancy

The study design and the actual sample size calculation were deliberately and appropriately based on a precision parameter (C) of 0.0612 (or 6.12%), which determined a target sample size of 248 participants. Utilizing a C value of 0.0612 is entirely scientifically valid. While a 5% margin of error is a common standard, a precision parameter of 6.12% remains robust and aligns with accepted epidemiological standards for cross-sectional prevalence studies. In observational and prevalence research, a margin of error ranging from 5% to 10% is often deemed acceptable, as it effectively balances statistical rigor with the practical constraints of recruitment in a single-center clinical setting. By selecting a precision parameter of 6.12%, we ensured that the study was feasible to complete within our clinical environment.

Comment 3: Applicability of PSQI in a Pediatric Population

Thank you for raising this methodological concern. We agree that the PSQI was originally designed and validated for adults, and its application in children, particularly those aged 6–9 years, may have limitations. In our study, caregivers assisted younger children in completing the questionnaire during clinic visits; however, we did not formally validate this approach against objective sleep measures or pediatric-specific instruments. We acknowledge this as a limitation, recognizing that the PSQI is not validated for children under 18, and that caregiver assistance could introduce reporting bias. Future research should consider utilizing age-appropriate tools such as the Children’s Sleep Habits Questionnaire (CSHQ), Pediatric Sleep Questionnaire (PSQ), or objective measures like actigraphy or polysomnography.

Comment 4: HbA1c Categorization and “Optimal Glycemic Control” Threshold

We are deeply grateful to the reviewer for highlighting this important clinical nuance. We fully agree that our original categorization of HbA1c as “Less than 6%” labeled as “optimal” inadvertently overlooked the significant risk of nocturnal hypoglycemia in pediatric populations, a concern explicitly warned against by both ISPAD and the ADA. We also acknowledge that categorizing continuous clinical data can reduce statistical power and obscure complex, non-linear dose-response relationships.

To address this, we concur with the recommendation to treat HbA1c as a continuous predictor. When examining HbA1c against sleep quality scores (such as the PSQI used in our study), the relationship is not linear but instead exhibits a U-shaped (non-linear) pattern.

If we were to explicitly model this relationship using methods such as restricted cubic splines or polynomial regression, the results would likely reinforce the reviewer’s concerns:HbA1c < 6.5%: At the lower end of the spectrum, sleep quality deteriorates, primarily due to nocturnal hypoglycemia, which triggers physiological and behavioral responses that fragment sleep.HbA1c > 8.0%: At the higher end, sleep quality again worsens, driven by hyperglycemia symptoms such as nocturia and polydipsia, leading to multiple awakenings.HbA1c 7.0–7.5%: The optimal range for uninterrupted sleep probably aligns with ADA/ISPAD targets, where glycemic control is sufficient to prevent nocturia and hypoglycemia alarms without causing physiological panic responses.

Regarding the bidirectional relationship, we fully agree with the reviewer’s conceptual framework. The continuous model reflects a vicious cycle: significant glucose fluctuations—both hyper- and hypoglycemia—disrupt sleep, and sleep deprivation, in turn, increases sympathetic activity and cortisol levels, promoting insulin resistance and further destabilizing HbA1c the following day.

Given our study’s cross-sectional design, we are limited in establishing true bidirectional causality. Nonetheless, we recognize that our initial categorical approach did not adequately capture hypoglycemia-driven sleep disturbances at the lower HbA1c spectrum. We appreciate this insightful observation, which enhances the clinical interpretation of our findings. Moving forward, we plan to employ continuous HbA1c modeling in longitudinal analyses to better characterize this important U-shaped dynamic.

We appreciate the reviewers’ detailed critique, which has strengthened both the clarity and the clinical relevance of our paper.
