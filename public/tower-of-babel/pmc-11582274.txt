Omadacycline for Diverse Infections in China: A Real-World Analysis of Efficacy and Safety
Authors: Weiwen Gao, Jian Yang, Xiangwang Zhang, Lei Tian, Dong Xu, Shuyun Xu, Dong Liu, Yan He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe efficacy and safety of omadacycline have been primarily documented through Phase III clinical trials; however, there are limited data from real-world clinical settings. This study aims to explore the real-world use of omadacycline in China and identify the factors associated with its efficacy.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective review of medical records for patients treated with omadacycline at a single center from March 2022 to March 2024. We analyzed demographic characteristics, laboratory results, antibiotic regimens, and clinical outcomes. Logistic regression was employed to identify risk factors associated with clinical treatment failure or failure of microbial clearance.ResultsA total of 183 patients were included in the final analysis. Clinical success was achieved in 71.0% (130/183) of patients, with a bacterial clearance rate of 61.9% (26/42). Renal impairment was observed in 20.8% (38/183) of patients, with 39.5% (15/38) of these patients receiving nephrotoxic antibiotic treatments. Noteworthy adverse drug reactions were rare during the course of the treatment. Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified several independent factors associated with treatment failure: moderate to severe liver damage (OR: 3.073, 95% CI 1.345–7.021, p = 0.008), admission to the respiratory department (OR: 2.573, 95% CI 1.135–5.834, p = 0.024), and a duration of omadacycline therapy of less than 7 days (OR: 3.762, 95% CI 1.626–8.706, p = 0.002).ConclusionsOur study demonstrates that omadacycline treatment can achieve favorable clinical success and bacterial clearance, with positive safety and tolerability outcomes. However, high-quality randomized controlled trials are needed to validate these initial findings.Keywords: Omadacycline, Clinical outcomes, Efficacy, Safety, Real-world study

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Omadacycline’s efficacy and safety have primarily been evaluated in phase III clinical trials, with limited real-world data, especially in China. There is a need to assess its performance and safety in a broader, real-world clinical setting.Bacterial resistance to antibiotics remains a significant global challenge, and novel treatments like omadacycline offer potential benefits due to their ability to address drug-resistant bacteria and provide a convenient oral administration option.This study aimed to evaluate the real-world efficacy, safety, and factors influencing the clinical outcomes of omadacycline in Chinese patients, including assessing its effectiveness against various bacterial infections and its safety profile. What was learned from the study? Omadacycline demonstrated a clinical success rate of 71.0% and a bacterial clearance rate of 61.9%. Key factors associated with treatment failure included moderate to severe liver impairment, admission to the respiratory department, and a treatment duration of less than 7 days.The study supports omadacycline’s efficacy and favorable safety profile in treating diverse infections, including those caused by resistant pathogens. However, it highlights the importance of managing liver function and ensuring adequate treatment duration to optimize outcomes. The findings call for further high-quality randomized controlled trials to confirm these results and refine treatment protocols.

Introduction

Bacterial resistance to a wide range of antibiotics has emerged as a major global public health issue [1]. Since the introduction of tetracyclines in the late 1940s, their efficacy has been increasingly compromised by the evolving resistance of bacteria. The latest generation of tetracyclines, including eravacycline, tigecycline, and omadacycline, has been developed to counteract the most prevalent acquired resistance mechanisms, making them critical in the fight against drug-resistant bacteria [2]. However, tigecycline and eravacycline are not available in oral form due to poor intestinal absorption, which limits their use in outpatient or homecare settings. Additionally, tigecycline is associated with a high incidence of nausea and vomiting [3], and an increased risk of all-cause mortality has been observed in patients with ventilator-associated pneumonia treated with standard doses of tigecycline [4, 5]. Conversely, eravacycline shows enhanced in vitro antimicrobial potency and a favorable tolerability profile. However, its metabolism is predominantly mediated by the liver CYP3A4 enzyme system, necessitating dosage adjustments when co-administered with potent CYP3A4 inducers like rifampicin to maintain therapeutic efficacy [6].

Omadacycline, a novel semi-synthetic aminomethyl tetracycline antibiotic, offers several advantages over tigecycline and eravacycline. Approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in October 2018 for the treatment of acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections (ABSSSI) and community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) in adults [7]. Omadacycline features unique chemical modifications at positions C7 and C9, and these modifications help it evade efflux pump resistance mechanisms and ribosome protection strategies, resulting in potent activity against traditional tetracycline-resistant bacteria [8, 9]. Research has shown that omadacycline is effective against a broad spectrum of microorganisms, including Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, multidrug-resistant strains, and certain anaerobic and atypical pathogens [10–13]. It also holds promise for a switch from intravenous to oral administration, with potential for once-daily dosing, which could enhance patient convenience and treatment efficacy [14]. Importantly, omadacycline does not require dosage adjustments based on body mass index, age, sex, or renal and hepatic function, allowing physicians to focus less on inter-patient variability [15–17].

While the efficacy and safety of omadacycline have been primarily reported in Phase III clinical trials, there are limited data from real-world clinical settings [18–20]. Real-world studies, despite potential confounding factors such as off-label prescribing, salvage therapy, co-medication, and comorbidities, provide valuable insights into a drug’s performance in clinical practice. As the largest real-world clinical study published to date evaluating omadacycline for the treatment of various infections in Chinese patients, our objective is to enhance the existing clinical experience and to provide a reference for physicians’ decision-making.

Study Design

Our retrospective analysis focused on inpatients who received omadacycline treatment at Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Hubei, China, between March 2022 and March 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients were over 18 years old and had received at least 72 h of omadacycline treatment. Patients who had previously undergone multiple courses of omadacycline were also included, provided at least 60 days had passed since their last dose. Exclusion criteria included missing vital data or infections caused by fungal or virus. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, which determined that informed consent from patients was not necessary.

Patient data were collected from the electronic medical record system and included demographic characteristics, comorbidities, laboratory results, microbiological features, antimicrobial strategies, clinical and microbiological responses, and adverse reactions.

Evaluations and Definitions

Infections were defined as conditions identified by positive bacterial cultures at specific anatomical sites or in conjunction with clinical signs and symptoms indicative of infection. The determination of a patient’s infection or colonization status was managed by infectiologists.

The primary outcome was clinical response, categorized as either clinical success or clinical failure. Secondary outcomes included microbial eradication and the occurrence of adverse events.

Clinical success was defined as patient survival upon discharge, along with improvement in symptoms, and radiological or laboratory evidence of infection resolution, provided that salvage antimicrobial therapy was not used. Symptom improvement was defined as an improvement in at least one of the symptoms (e.g., temperature, cough, sputum production) when two or more symptoms were assessed.

For patients receiving targeted treatment, the microbiology efficacy was classified as eradication (negative bacterial culture results), hypothetical eradication (clinically effective but not confirmed due to the inability to obtain specimens), replacement (the original bacteria were clear but other bacteria were detected), and re-infection (the original bacteria were still detected in the culture). The bacteriological clearance rate is calculated as (number of eradication cases + hypothetical eradication cases + number of replacement cases)/total number of cases multiplied by 100% [21]. Duplicated instances (several isolates in the same patient) were not considered. Patients who underwent empirical treatment were excluded from the analysis of microbial clearance.

Hepatic impairment was classified using the Child–Turcotte–Pugh scoring system [22]. A grade B or C examination indicates moderate to severe liver damage, whereas a grade A evaluation suggests mild liver damage.

Acute kidney injury was defined by a serum creatinine level of 1.5 times the baseline or 0.3 mg/dL after omadacycline treatment. Nephrotoxic antibiotics included vancomycin, amphotericin B, aminoglycosides, and colistin.

Antibiotic lines were defined as the number of changes in the types or quantities of antimicrobial regimens during hospitalization.

Available safety laboratory data were graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE).

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 26.0), and the results are presented as n (%) or median interquartile range (IQR). In the analysis of categorical variables, the chi-squared test (χ2) or Fisher’s exact test was utilized. Logistic regression was used to identify risk factors associated with clinical treatment failure or microbial clearance failure, and a two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Between March 2022 and March 2024, we identified 233 inpatients who received omadacycline treatment. After excluding 50 patients for various reasons, 183 patients met our inclusion criteria (see Fig. 1).

Flowchart of patients included and excluded for this study

The demographic characteristics of the 183 eligible patients are summarized in Table 1. Of these patients, 62.8% (115/183) were male and the median age was 66 years (range 18–98). A significant 85.2% (156/183) of the patients had comorbidities. Among them, 71 patients had moderate to severe liver impairment, and 48 had moderate to severe renal impairment. The median Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was 2 (range 0–8). The most common site of infection was the lung, with 163 cases, followed by 22 abdominal infections, 7 acute skin and soft tissue infections, and 5 bloodstream infections. Additionally, 25 patients had concurrent infections at multiple sites, and 54 patients progressed to sepsis.

Targeted therapy was administered to 42 patients, from whom 48 bacterial strains were isolated. These included 19 strains of Klebsiella pneumoniae (KP), 11 strains of Acinetobacter baumannii (AB), 6 strains of Escherichia coli (ECO), and 4 strains of Staphylococcus aureus (SA). Notably, the isolated strains included 17 carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP), 10 carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB), 5 carbapenem-resistant E. coli (CRECO), and 4 methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). Specimens were collected from various sources.

Treatment Regimens

Patients received 100 mg of intravenous omadacycline tosilate per day. As shown in Table 1, treatment regimens varied significantly prior to switching to omadacycline. Initially, 109 patients were treated with monotherapy, the antimicrobials prescribed including β-lactams/β-lactamase inhibitors, carbapenems, fluoroquinolones, tigecycline, and colistin. For 48 patients, the pre-treatment regimen consisted of two or more antibiotics; among these, 27 patients were diagnosed with CAP and 14 with hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP). Additionally, 26 patients received three or more antibiotics in their pre-treatment regimen, with 13 of these developing sepsis.

Patients frequently experienced changes in their anti-infective regimen before starting omadacycline, with a median of 1 antibiotic line change (range 0–4). Due to the ineffectiveness of systemic antibiotics, 126 patients (68.9%) were switched to omadacycline for salvage therapy. After transitioning to omadacycline, monotherapy was utilized in 68 cases (37.2%), while a combination of omadacycline and other antibiotics was utilized in 115 cases (62.8%). The median duration of omadacycline therapy was 6 days (range 2–20), and the median time from infection onset to omadacycline initiation was 5 days (range 1–97). Omadacycline was initiated within 48 h of infection onset in 30 cases (16.4%). Prior to susceptibility test results, 141 patients (77.0%) received omadacycline as empirical therapy, and 49 patients (26.8%) received a loading dose of omadacycline (Table 1).

Factors Affecting Clinical Outcome

Overall, clinical response was achieved in 71.0% (130/183) of patients, with varying success rates among different treatment groups: 72.2% (91/126) for those on salvage therapy, 72.5% (102/141) for those on empirical therapy, and 64.3% (27/42) for those on targeted therapy. Additionally, the efficacy rate was 71.4% (15/21) among patients whose pre-treatment regimen included tigecycline. The efficacy rates for different infection types were as follows: abdominal infections 90.9% (20/22), CAP 69.8% (90/129), HAP 70.6% (24/34), bloodstream infections 40.0% (2/5), acute skin and soft tissue infections 71.4% (5/7), and urinary infections 66.7% (8/12) (see Table 1, Fig. 2A).

Logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors affecting clinical outcomes. In univariate analysis, abdominal infection and a 7-day course of omadacycline were found to be protective factors against treatment failure. Conversely, risk factors for treatment failure included admission to the respiratory department, catheter placement, moderate to severe liver function impairment, an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 prior to rescue therapy, a diagnosis of KP infection, pre-treatment with three or more antibiotic lines, a pre-treatment regimen containing three or more antibiotics, and use of colistin.

Multivariate analysis identified the following independent risk factors for treatment failure (Table 2): Moderate to severe liver function impairment (OR: 3.073, 95% CI 1.345–7.021, p = 0.008); Admission to the respiratory department (OR: 2.573, 95% CI 1.135–5.834, p = 0.024); Less than 7 days of omadacycline therapy (OR: 3.762, 95% CI 1.626–8.706, p = 0.002). Despite medical interventions, two patients unfortunately died. The first was a 91-year-old female with severe pneumonia who was bedridden and required mechanical ventilation. Her bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) tested positive for MRSA. Initially treated with omadacycline and later switched to carbapenems, she died after only 5 days of treatment. The second patient, a 75-year-old male diagnosed with CAP, had a BALF culture positive for CRKP after 6 days of carbapenem treatment. He was subsequently treated with a combination of omadacycline and colistin, but his condition worsened and he died 11 days later.

In the empirical therapy subgroup, the only independent risk factor identified was failure to complete a 7-day course of omadacycline (OR: 3.981, 95% CI 1.526–10.387, p = 0.005).

Microbiological Outcomes

Among the 42 patients who received targeted therapy, the overall microbial clearance rate was 61.9% (26/42). The eradication rates of the bacterial subgroups were (Fig. 2B):

Klebsiella pneumoniae: 47.4% (9/19), Acinetobacter baumannii: 63.6% (7/11), Escherichia coli: 50.0% (3/6), Staphylococcus aureus: 50.0% (2/4), Staphylococcus epidermidis: 100% (3/3), Enterococcus: 100% (2/2), Stenotrophomonas maltophilia: 100% (1/1), Proteus mirabilis: 100% (1/1), and Mycobacterium abscessus: 0% (0/1). For multidrug-resistant bacteria, the eradication rates were as follows: CRKP: 47.1% (8/17), CRAB: 70.0% (7/10), CRECO: 40.0% (2/5), and MRSA: 50.0% (2/4).

Among patients whose pre-treatment regimen included tigecycline, 90.9% (10/11) of the pathogens were eradicated (see Fig. 2B). This included the following cases: 1 case of MRSA, 1 case of Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, 3 cases of CRAB, 1 case of CRECO, 1 case of Enterococcus, and 5 cases of CRKP. Only 1 case of CRKP was not cleared.

Factors potentially associated with pathogen clearance failure included the exclusion of tigecycline from the pre-treatment regimen, liver function classified as Child–Pugh B or C, renal function with an eGFR below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2, hypersensitive C-reactive protein (hsCRP) levels above 55 ng/mL, and a diagnosis of HAP. Given the sample size, further research is needed to confirm these findings.

Safety

Records of adverse drug reactions during treatment were extremely rare, indicating that no serious adverse reactions were noteworthy. Furthermore, it could not be definitively concluded that the adverse reactions were directly related to omadacycline. In this study, six patients discontinued omadacycline after only two days, with two of these patients reporting intolerable diarrhea as an adverse reaction.

Renal impairment was observed in 20.8% (38/183) of patients (see Table 1), with 39.5% (15/38) of these patients being on nephrotoxic regimens. The proportion of renal injury was significantly lower in patients not receiving nephrotoxic antibiotics compared to those on regimens with nephrotoxic drugs (16.4% vs. 53.6%, p = 0.009).

Discussion

This study provides significant insights into the real-world use of omadacycline in 183 Chinese patients. The clinical cure rate associated with omadacycline treatment was 71.0% (130/183). Notably, some patients received omadacycline for off-label indications such as HAP, bloodstream infections, abdominal infections, and urinary infections, even though it is currently approved only for adult CAP. In vitro studies show that omadacycline has robust activity against a range of common pathogens associated with HAP. Pharmacokinetic studies suggest that omadacycline concentrations in epithelial lining fluid and alveolar macrophages exceed those in plasma [23], which may enhance its efficacy against pathogens commonly found in HAP. Our data indicated that omadacycline achieved a clinical efficacy rate of 70.6% (24/34) in treating HAP, suggesting it could be a viable treatment option for this condition.

Omadacycline is extensively distributed across various tissues and demonstrates favorable tissue penetration, which may be advantageous for treating patients with bacteremia [24]. A post hoc analysis compared omadacycline with other treatments in patients with acute bacterial skin and ABSSSI and CAP with secondary bacteremia, finding clinical response rates of 61.5% (8/13) and 66.6% (10/15) in these groups, respectively [25]. In our cohort, omadacycline had a clinical efficacy rate of 40.0% (2/5) for patients with concurrent bloodstream infections. These results should be interpreted with caution due to the reliance on post hoc analysis and the small sample size.

Omadacycline demonstrated potent antimicrobial activity against a range of aerobic and anaerobic organisms in mouse peritonitis models [26, 27]. Despite these promising preclinical results, clinical evidence for omadacycline’s efficacy in treating abdominal infections in humans is limited. A previous study of oral omadacycline for drug-resistant Gram-negative pathogens reported a clinical success rate of 33.3% (1/3) for abdominal infections [28]. In contrast, our study found a clinical efficacy rate of 90.9% (20/22) for treating abdominal infections. The difference in clinical efficacy rates between our study and previous research may be due to differences in sample sizes, with our study including a larger cohort (22 vs. 3 patients).

Although omadacycline has shown in vitro activity against extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacteriaceae, it has not yet received FDA approval for urinary tract infections (UTIs). A Phase 1b trial found that omadacycline achieved significant clinical response rates in female patients with cystitis, with final treatment success rates of 94.0% and follow-up success rates of 84.0% [29]. Our study reported an efficacy rate of 66.7% (8/12) for treating UTIs with omadacycline. However, the small sample size of 12 cases limits the generalizability of these findings, highlighting the need for further research to confirm these preliminary results.

Our study identified moderate to severe liver function impairment as an independent risk factor for treatment failure. Research on the impact of liver dysfunction on omadacycline efficacy is currently limited to individual case reports [30–32], indicating a need for further investigation. Although omadacycline is not metabolized by the liver [33], several factors might contribute to the observed treatment differences: (1) variations in patients’ physiological and pathological status: patients with liver impairment may experience hepatic microcirculatory disturbances, systemic inflammatory responses, immune dysfunction, and gut microbiome dysbiosis, all of which could affect anti-infective therapy efficacy; and (2) heterogeneity of clinical interventions: potential interactions between medications used to manage liver injury and omadacycline should be explored, as well as the impact of procedures like ascites drainage on omadacycline’s pharmacokinetics. Additionally, the susceptibility of these patients to nosocomial infections or atypical pathogens should be considered.

In our cohort, a treatment duration of less than 7 days was identified as an independent risk factor for treatment failure. This finding aligns with clinical practice guidelines from the Infectious Disease Society of America (IDSA) and the American Thoracic Society (ATS), which recommend a minimum antimicrobial therapy duration of 5 days for clinically stable CAP [34]. Adequate treatment duration is crucial for achieving optimal outcomes. Therefore, ensuring sufficient antimicrobial exposure when treating CAP with omadacycline is essential to minimize the risk of treatment failure and to promote clinical cure. The median duration from the commencement of treatment to the achievement of disease remission is a critical determinant in shaping the therapeutic strategy. Nevertheless, precise acquisition of these data is unattainable through the existing case records. Consequently, the clinical infectiologists recommended that the efficacy of omadacycline be evaluated within the first 5 days post-treatment completion, when treatment efficacy can be thoroughly assessed using infection indicators such as PCT and CRP, as well as the patient's symptom improvement.

In vitro studies suggest potential cross-resistance between omadacycline and tigecycline against Gram-negative bacteria [35]. However, to our knowledge, there have been no reports of cross-resistance in Gram-positive infections. Our study found that omadacycline effectively eliminated Gram-positive bacteria resistant to tigecycline, and the clearance rate for Gram-negative bacteria remained high, which contrasts with some earlier findings. This discrepancy may be due to the complexity of real-world treatment regimens. For example, among five patients with CRKP infections, omadacycline’s efficacy was influenced by the activity of concomitant antibiotics. Two patients received ceftazidime–avibactam, one received levofloxacin, and one received fosfomycin, all responding effectively. Notably, among the 12 CRKP-infected patients not treated with tigecycline, the microbial eradication rate fell to 33.3% (4/12), suggesting that tigecycline might act as a protective factor. These observations underscore the complexities of real-world treatment settings and highlight the need for further research into factors influencing omadacycline’s efficacy.

In real-world settings, low pathogen culture yields often necessitate reliance on empirical therapy. Determining the best salvage treatment for infections unresponsive to multiple rounds of empirical therapy presents a significant clinical challenge. In our study, the clinical response rate with empirical therapy was 72.5% (102/141), despite 65.7% (67/102) of patients having received multiple lines of antibiotic therapy. This suggests that omadacycline could be a valuable treatment option. Additionally, our findings revealed that the clinical response rate was 42.1% (8/19) among patients who had been treated with more than three lines of antibiotics, compared to 74.4% (122/164) in those with fewer treatment changes (see Fig. 2A). The timing of omadacycline administration relative to infection onset did not significantly impact the clinical response rate (p = 0.389). These findings indicate that, while frequent antibiotic changes should be approached with caution, omadacycline remains a viable treatment option regardless of its timing.

Phase 3 clinical trials reported that intravenous omadacycline was associated with adverse reactions such as nausea (14.9%), vomiting (8.3%), and elevated transaminase levels (5.4%) [36, 37]. In our study, omadacycline generally showed good tolerability. However, six patients discontinued the medication within 2 days due to intolerable diarrhea. The adverse reactions observed in our study were consistent with those reported in Phase 3 trials. Additionally, 20.8% (38/183) of patients experienced renal impairment, with 39.5% (15/38) on nephrotoxic medications. Although omadacycline-induced nephrotoxicity is reported as extremely rare, with only 0.5% (2/382) of CAP patients and no cases among 691 ABSSSI patients [38], our study found a higher incidence of renal impairment. This discrepancy may reflect the complexities of real-world medication use, with a focus specifically on antibiotics associated with kidney injury and excluding other potentially nephrotoxic drugs. Further research is needed to validate these findings and to assess omadacycline’s impact in broader clinical contexts.

Despite its valuable contributions, our study has limitations. The relatively small sample size limits the generalizability of the findings. The extensive spectrum of infections and the complex interplay of variables, particularly the drug combinations, may have significantly constrained the analysis's scope. Additionally, retrospective studies face challenges such as difficulties in controlling confounding factors and independently analyzing the influence of variables on outcomes.

Conclusions

This study is the largest real-world clinical study published to date evaluating omadacycline for the treatment of various infections. The findings suggest that omadacycline is promising in terms of safety and tolerability and is effective against infections at different anatomical sites, including those caused by drug-resistant pathogens. To validate our preliminary findings and strengthen our conclusions, further studies with larger sample sizes, comparative analyses, and prospective clinical trials are needed.
