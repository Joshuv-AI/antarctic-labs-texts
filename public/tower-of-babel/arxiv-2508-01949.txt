Embedding ample semigroups as ( 2 , 1 , 1 ) (2,1,1) -subalgebras of inverse semigroups Thanks: This research is being supported by the Estonian Research Council grant PRG1204.
Nasir Sohail, Aftab Hussain Shah, Kristo Väljako

Abstract
. The problem of embedding an ample semigroup in an inverse semigroup as a ( 2 , 1 , 1 ) (2,1,1) -type subalgebra is known to be undecidable. In this article, we investigate the problem for certain classes of ample semigroups. We also give examples of semigroups that are left (respectively, right) but not right (respectively, left) ample.

1.
Motivation and preliminaries

Let
X
be a (possibly empty) set. By a
partial bijection
of
X
we mean a bijection
\beta:A\longrightarrow B
such that
A
and
B
are subsets of
X
, called, respectively, the
domain
and
codomain
of
\beta
. We shall denote
A
and
B
by
Dom\beta
and
Im\beta
, respectively. The set of all partial bijections of
X
is denoted by
\mathcal{I}_{X}
. The empty map (which has empty domain and codomain) may be viewed as a special element of
\mathcal{I}_{X}
.
Throughout this article, we shall be following the convention of writing maps to the right of their arguments. Also, parentheses around arguments will be dropped if there is no chance of ambiguity. Given
\beta,\gamma\in\mathcal{I}_{X}
, we define

\beta\gamma=\beta|_{(Im\beta\cap Dom\gamma)\beta^{-1}}\circ\gamma,

(1.1)

where
\circ
denotes the ordinary composition of (full) maps. The binary operation defined by (
1.1
) turns
\mathcal{I}_{X}
into a semigroup, called the
symmetric inverse semigroup over
X
. Because the
empty map
acts as the zero element of
\mathcal{I}_{X}
, we shall denote it by
0
. Note that for every
\beta\in\mathcal{I}_{X}
there exists a unique partial bijection
\beta^{-1}\in\mathcal{I}_{X}
, such that
\beta\beta^{-1}
and
\beta^{-1}\beta
are identities on
Dom\beta
and
Im\beta
, respectively. Particularly,
\beta\beta^{-1}\beta=\beta
and
\beta^{-1}\beta\beta^{-1}=\beta^{-1}
.

In general, an element
a
of a semigroup
S
is said to be
invertible
if there exists a unique element
a^{-1}\in S
, called the
inverse
of
a
, such that
aa^{-1}a=a
and
a^{-1}aa^{-1}=a^{-1}
. We call
S
an
inverse semigroup
if all of its elements are invertible. The semigroup
\mathcal{I}_{X}
, discussed above, is a classical example of an inverse semigroup. From now on, the letter
T
, possibly with subscripts, will be reserved to only denote inverse semigroups.

A
morphism

h:T_{1}\longrightarrow T_{2}
of inverse semigroups is just a semigroup homomorphism from
T_{1}
to
T_{2}
.
Monomorphisms
and
isomorphisms
of (inverse) semigroups are, respectively, the injective and bijective homomorphisms. The Wagner-Preston representation (see Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
) asserts that every inverse semigroup can be embedded in a symmetric inverse semigroup.

Theorem 1.1
.

Given an inverse semigroup
T
the map

\rho_{x}:Txx^{-1}\longrightarrow Tx^{-1}x,

defined by

(a)\rho_{x}=ax,\,\,\forall\,a\in Txx^{-1},

is a bijection for all
x\in T
. Furthermore, the map

\rho_{{}_{T}}:T\longrightarrow\mathcal{I}_{T},\text{ given by }\,x\longmapsto\rho_{x},\,\,\forall\,x\in T,

is a monomorphism.

Proof.

See, for instance,
[
5
]
Theorem 5.1.7.
∎

Theorem
1.1
may also be presented in the following dual form.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
T
be an inverse semigroup. Then, the map

{\lambda_{x}}:x^{-1}xT\longrightarrow xx^{-1}T,

given by

(a){\lambda_{x}}=xa,\,\,\forall\,a\in x^{-1}xT,

is a bijection for all
x\in T
. Moreover, the map

\lambda_{T}:T\longrightarrow\mathcal{I}_{T},\text{ defined as }\,x\longmapsto{\lambda_{x}},\,\,\forall\,x\in T,

is a monomorphism.

Proof.

See, for instance,
[
8
]
Theorem 1.5.1.

Clearly, for any inverse semigroup
T
the map
\vartheta:(T)\rho_{{}_{T}}\longrightarrow(T)\lambda_{T}
, defined by
\rho_{x}\mapsto{\lambda_{x}}
, for all

, is an isomorphism, where

Ttt^{-1}=Tt^{-1},\;Tt^{-1}t=Tt,\;t^{-1}tT=t^{-1}T,\text{ and }\;tt^{-1}T=tT.

(1.2)

As usual, the set of idempotents of a semigroup
S
will be denoted by
E(S)
. For an inverse semigroup
T
, we have

E(T)=\{aa^{-1}:a\in T\};

moreover, the latter is a subsemilattice of the former.
Identifying
T
with its isomorphic copy in
\mathcal{I}_{T}
we see that the idempotents
xx^{-1},x^{-1}x\in E(T)
are, respectively, the identities on the ‘domain’ and ‘codomain’ of
x\in T
. This fact may be used to verify that

\forall\,x,y\in T,\,x\leq y\text{ iff }x=xx^{-1}y

(1.3)

defines a partial order on
T
—interpret (
1.3
) as
x\leq y
if and only if the domain of
x
is a subset of the domain of
y
and the latter agrees with the former on the restricted domain. It is an easy exercise to show that Condition (
1.3
) is equivalent to the following one:

\forall\,x,y\in T,\,x\leq y\text{ iff }\,\exists\,e\in E(T),\text{ such that }x=ey.

(1.4)

We call
\leq
the
natural partial order
on
T
. In fact, for any semigroup
S
the set
E(S)
comes equipped with the partial order:

e\preccurlyeq f\,\text{ iff }\,ef=fe=e,\;\forall\,e,f\in E(S).

(1.5)

In an inverse semigroup, the natural partial order is an extension of the partial order
\preccurlyeq
. From now on,
order
on an inverse semigroup will mean the natural partial order, as defined by (
1.3
).
Given an inverse semigroup
T
, let us further recall (
[
6
]
, Remark 4.3) that for all
x\in T
,
xx^{-1}
and
x^{-1}x
are the minimum idempotents such that
x=xx^{-1}x
, i.e. for all
e,f\in E(T)
,
ex=x
implies that
xx^{-1}\leq e
and
xf=x
implies that
x^{-1}x\leq f
.

A semigroup
S
is called
left ample
if it can be embedded in an inverse semigroup
T
such that
(x\phi)(x\phi)^{-1}\in S\phi
for all
x\in S
, where
\phi
is the embedding of
S
into
T
. Identifying
S
with its isomorphic copy in
T
, we shall henceforth write
xx^{-1}=[(x\phi^{\prime})(x\phi^{\prime})^{-1}]\phi^{\prime-1}
, where
\phi^{\prime}:S\longrightarrow(S)\phi
is the isomorphism defined by
x\mapsto(x)\phi
for all
x\in S
. So, we can say that a left ample semigroup comes equipped with a unary operation
x\mapsto x^{+}
, defined by
x^{+}=xx^{-1}
. Similarly, a semigroup
S
is called
right ample
if there exists an inverse semigroup
T
admitting a monomorphism
\phi:S\longrightarrow T
such that
(x\phi)^{-1}(x\phi)\in S\phi
. We then define
x^{-1}x=\left[(x\phi^{\prime})^{-1}(x\phi^{\prime})\right]\phi^{\prime-1}\in S
for all
x\in S
. A right ample semigroup comes endowed with the unary operation
x\mapsto x^{*}
, given by
x^{*}=x^{-1}x
. We shall refer to
T
as an
associated inverse semigroup
of the (right, left) ample semigroup
S
. One may easily verify that
S
is left (respectively, right) ample in
T
if and only if
(S)\rho_{{}_{T}}
is left (respectively, right) ample in
\mathcal{I}_{T}
, where
\rho_{{}_{T}}
is the monomorphisms given by Theorem
1.1
. This is also true for
(S)\lambda_{T}
, with
\lambda_{T}
being the monomorphism given by Theorem
1.2
. Note that, a left or a right ample semigroup may be viewed as an algebra of type
(2,1)
. A semigroup
S
is called
ample
if it has (possibly different) associated inverse semigroups
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
making it, respectively, into a right and a left ample semigroup. An ample semigroup may be considered a
(2,1,1)
-type algebra. If
S
is a left (respectively, right) ample subsemigroup of an associated inverse semigroup
T
then we shall say that
S
is
left
, (respectively,
right
)
ample in

T
. Similarly,
S
will be called ample in
T
if it is both left and right ample in
T
. Obviously, every inverse semigroup is ample (in itself), and is, as such, a
(2,1,1)
-type algebra. Also, a subsemigroup
S
of an inverse semigroup
T
is ample in
T
if
E(T)\subseteq S
, that is, if
S
is
full
in
T
. The converse is not true; for example,
\mathbb{N}
is ample but not full in the multiplicative monoid
\mathbb{Q}
. For more examples of (left, right) ample semigroups the reader is referred to
[
3
]
and
[
6
]
and the references contained therein.

Problem 1.3
.

Let
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
be different inverse semigroups containing isomorphic copies, say
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, of a non-inverse semigroup
S
. Let
S_{1}
be left ample in
T_{1}
and
S_{2}
be right ample in
T_{2}
. Then, can we find an (associated) inverse semigroup
T
making (an isomorphic copy of)
S
into a left as well as right ample semigroup? In the language of universal algebra, this amounts to embedding
S
in an inverse semigroup as a
(2,1,1)
-type subalgebra.

The following results imply that, in general, Problem
1.3
is undecidable even for finite ample semigroups.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
4
]
, Theorem 3.4)
.

Let
S
be an ample semigroup. Then
S
is a
(2,1,1)
-type subalgebra of an inverse semigroup if and only if
S
is a full subsemigroup of an inverse semigroup.

Corollary 1.5

(
[
4
]
, Corollary 4.3)
.

It is undecidable whether a finite ample semigroup embeds as a full subsemigroup of a finite inverse semigroup, or of an inverse semigroup.

In Section 2, we shall consider Problem
1.3
for certain classes of (finite) semigroups. Also, to the knowledge of the authors, there exists no example of a left (respectively, right) ample semigroup that is not right (respectively, left) ample. A class of such semigroups will be provided in Section 3. We shall also consider Problem
1.3
for (left, right) rich ample semigroups that were introduced in
[
6
]
. For the convenience of the reader, their definitions have been reproduced below.

For a subsemigroup
S
of an inverse semigroup
T
let us define
S^{\prime}=\{s^{-1}\in T:s\in S\}
.
We say that
S
is
rich left ample
(respectively,
rich right ample
) in
T
if for all
x,y\in S
one has
xy^{-1}\in S\cup S^{\prime}
(respectively,
x^{-1}y\in S\cup S^{\prime}
). Alternatively (see
[
6
]
, Proposition 4.8),
S
is
rich left ample in

T
if

(1L)

S
is left ample in
T
, and

(2L)

for all
x,y\in S
,
xy^{-1}=ayy^{-1}
, where
a\in S\cup S^{\prime}
.

Similarly, the conjunction of,

(1R)

S
is right ample in
T
, and

(2R)

for all
x,y\in S
,
x^{-1}y=x^{-1}xb
, where
b\in S\cup S^{\prime}
,

is equivalent to
S
being
rich right ample in

T
(
[
6
]
, Proposition 4.9). Obviosly, in Conditions (2L) and (2R), one may replace
yy^{-1}
and
x^{-1}x
by
y^{+}
and
x^{*}
, respectively. We call
S

rich left
(
right
)
ample
if it can be embedded, as such, in an (associated) inverse semigroup. We say that
S
is (two-sided)
rich ample
if it is rich left as well as rich right ample—again, the associated inverse semigroups need not be the same. If the element
a
in Condition (2L) is determined uniquely then
S
is said to be
ultra rich left ample
.
Ultra rich right
and
ultra rich ample
semigroups are defined analogously.

Remark 1.6
.

Let a semigroup
S
be left (respectively, right) ample in an inverse semigroup
T_{1}
, and
T_{2}
be an inverse semigroup admitting a morphism
f:T_{1}\longrightarrow T_{2}
. Then one can easily verifiy that
(S)f
is left (respectively, right) ample in
T_{2}
. The remark also holds for rich (left, right) ample semigroups. However, ultra rich (left, right) ample subsemigroups are only preserved by monomorphisms.

From any ample semigroup
S
one may obtain an amalgam
(S;T_{1},T_{2};\phi_{1},\phi_{2})
, see
[
6
]
for details, where
T_{i},1\leq i\leq 2,
are (associated) inverse semigroups admitting monomorphisms
\phi_{i}:S\longrightarrow T_{i}
such that
S_{1}=(S)\phi_{1}
is left ample in
T_{1}
and
S_{2}=(S)\phi_{2}
is right ample in
T_{2}
. Let
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
be the inverse hulls of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
in
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, respectively (see
[
6
]
).
Also, assume that the isomorphism
\psi=\phi_{1}^{-1}\circ\,\phi_{2}:(S)\phi_{1}\longrightarrow(S)\phi_{2}
extends to a homomorphism, say
\psi^{\prime}
, from
V_{1}
to
V_{2}
.
Now, because every element of
V_{1}
may be written as
x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{n}
, where for each

either
x_{i}
or
x_{i}^{-1}
belongs to
S_{1}
,
\psi^{\prime}
must be defined by

(x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{n})\psi^{\prime}=(x_{1})\overline{\psi}(x_{2})\overline{\psi}\dots(x_{n})\overline{\psi},

(1.6)

where

(x_{i})\overline{\psi}=\begin{cases}(x_{i})\psi,&\quad\text{if }x_{i}\in S_{1},\\
\left((x_{i}^{-1})\psi\right)^{-1},&\quad\text{if }x_{i}^{-1}\in S_{1}.\\
\end{cases}

Because
(V_{1})\psi^{\prime}
is an inverse subsemigroup of
V_{2}
containing
S_{2}
, we may further assert that
\psi^{\prime}
is necessarily surjective. This implies by Remark
1.6
that
S_{2}=(S_{1})\psi^{\prime}
is right as well as left ample in
V_{2}
, and hence in
T_{2}
.
Furthermore, there exists a (retract) morphism
\psi^{\prime\prime}:V_{2}\longrightarrow V_{1}
such that
\psi^{\prime\prime}\circ\psi^{\prime}
is identity on
V_{2}
, see, for instance,
[
9
]
§5.6. Because, by a similar argument,
\psi^{\prime\prime}
is also surjective, it follows that
\psi^{\prime\prime}=(\psi^{\prime})^{-1}
, implying that
\psi^{\prime}
is an isomorphism. This implies that
S_{1}
is also left as well as right ample in
V_{1}
, equivalently, in
T_{1}
.
Condition (1) of the following proposition recapitulates the above discussion.

2.
Answering problem
1.3
for certain classes of ample semigroups

Let
S
be a subsemigroup of an inverse semigroup
T
. Following
[
1
]
, a subset
Y
of
T
will be called
right
S
-invariant
if

Im(\rho_{s}|_{Dom\rho_{s}\cap Y})\subseteq Y,\hskip 4.0pt\text{for all }s\in S,

where
\rho_{s}:Tss^{-1}\longrightarrow Ts^{-1}s
is the partial bijection defined in Theorem
1.1
.
Denoting
\rho_{s}|_{Dom\rho_{s}\cap Y}
, equivalently
\rho_{s}|_{Tss^{-1}\cap Y}
, by
\sigma_{s}
, we introduce a map:

\hat{\rho}_{{}_{Y}}:S\longrightarrow\mathcal{I}_{Y}\text{ by }(s)\hat{\rho}_{{}_{Y}}=\sigma_{s}.

Clearly,
\hat{\rho}_{{}_{Y}}
is well-defined. Also, it is known to be a homomorphism when
S
is finite; see
[
1
]
. The following lemma shows that
\hat{\rho}_{{}_{Y}}
is a homomorphism even if
S
is not finite.

Lemma 2.1
.

With the notations defined above,
\hat{\rho}_{{}_{Y}}
is a homomorphism.

Proof.

We need to show that
\sigma_{s}\sigma_{t}=\sigma_{st}
, for all
s,t\in S
. By Theorem
1.1
we have
\rho_{st}=\rho_{s}\rho_{t}
, also requiring that

Dom\rho_{st}=Dom(\rho_{s}\rho_{t})=(Ts^{-1}s\cap Ttt^{-1})\rho^{-1}_{s}.

Consequently,

\sigma_{st}=\rho_{st}|_{Dom\rho_{st}\cap Y}=(\rho_{s}\rho_{t})|_{(Ts^{-1}s\cap Ttt^{-1})\rho^{-1}_{s}\cap Y}.

On the other hand, one has

\sigma_{s}\sigma_{t}=\left(\rho_{s}|_{Tss^{-1}\cap Y}\right)\left(\rho_{t}|_{Ttt^{-1}\cap Y}\right).

whence,

(x)\sigma_{st}=(x)(\rho_{s}\rho_{t})=(x)(\sigma_{s}\sigma_{t}),\,\forall\,x\in Dom(\sigma_{s}\sigma_{t})\cap Dom(\sigma_{st}).

So, the proof will be accomplished if we show that
Dom(\sigma_{s}\sigma_{t})=Dom(\sigma_{st})
, that is,

[(Tss^{-1}\cap Y)\rho_{s}\cap(Ttt^{-1}\cap Y)]\rho^{-1}_{s}=(Ts^{-1}s\cap Ttt^{-1})\rho^{-1}_{s}\cap Y.

To this end, observe that

\begin{split}x&\in[(Tss^{-1}\cap Y)\rho_{s}\cap(Ttt^{-1}\cap Y)]\rho^{-1}_{s}\\
\iff x\rho_{s}&\in(Tss^{-1}\cap Y)\rho_{s}\cap Ttt^{-1}\cap Y\\
\iff\hskip 10.0ptx&\in Tss^{-1}\cap Y,\,\,x\rho_{s}\in Ttt^{-1}\cap Y\\
\iff\hskip 10.0ptx&\in Tss^{-1},\,\,x\in Y,\,\,x\rho_{s}\in Ttt^{-1},\,\,x\rho_{s}\in Y\\
\iff x\rho_{s}&\in Ts^{-1}s,\,\,x\rho_{s}\in Ttt^{-1},\,\,x\in Y,\,\,x\rho_{s}\in Y\\
\iff x\rho_{s}&\in(Ts^{-1}s\cap Ttt^{-1}),\,\,x\in Y,\,\,x\rho_{s}\in Y\\
\iff\hskip 10.0ptx&\in(Ts^{-1}s\cap Ttt^{-1})\rho^{-1}_{s}\cap Y,\\
\end{split}

where the ‘if’ part of the last implication follows by observing that

x\in(Ts^{-1}s\cap Ttt^{-1})\rho^{-1}_{s}\implies x\rho_{s}\in Ts^{-1}s\implies x\in Tss^{-1}\implies x\in Dom\rho_{s}.

This implies that
Dom(\sigma_{s}\sigma_{t})=Dom(\sigma_{st})
, and the proof is complete.
∎

In particular, the subsemigroup
S
is a right
S
-invariant subset of
T
, giving a homomorphism
\hat{\rho}_{{}_{S}}:S\longrightarrow\mathcal{I}_{S}
, as defined above.

Proposition 2.2
.

If
S
is left ample in
T
, then
\hat{\rho}_{{}_{S}}:S\longrightarrow\mathcal{I}_{S}
is a monomorphism. Moreover,
(S)\hat{\rho}_{{}_{S}}
is left ample in
\mathcal{I}_{S}
.

Proof.

To prove the first part of the proposition, we only need to show that
\hat{\rho}_{{}_{S}}
is injective. Assume, for this purpose, that

\sigma_{s}=(s)\hat{\rho}_{{}_{S}}=(t)\hat{\rho}_{{}_{S}}=\sigma_{t},\;\text{i.e. }\,\rho_{s}|_{Tss^{-1}\cap S}=\rho_{t}|_{Ttt^{-1}\cap S},

for some
s,t\in S
. Then,
ss^{-1}\in Tss^{-1}\cap S
and
tt^{-1}\in Ttt^{-1}\cap S
, because
S
is left ample, and we may calculate from
Tss^{-1}\cap S=Ttt^{-1}\cap S
(viz. the equality of domains of
\sigma_{s}
and
\sigma_{t}
) that

(ss^{-1})\rho_{s}=(ss^{-1})\rho_{t}\;\text{ and }\;(tt^{-1})\rho_{s}=(tt^{-1})\rho_{t}.

This gives

s=ss^{-1}s=ss^{-1}t\;\text{ and }\;t=tt^{-1}t=tt^{-1}s.

Consequently, we have
s\leq t\leq s
in
T
,
yielding
s=t
. Hence,
\hat{\rho}_{{}_{S}}
is an injection.

As for the second part of the proposition,
let us first notice that

Sxx^{-1}\subseteq Txx^{-1}\cap S,\ \forall\,x\in S.

Then observe that, for all
y\in T,\,x\in S
, we have

y(xx^{-1})\in Txx^{-1}\cap S\implies y(xx^{-1})=y(xx^{-1})(xx^{-1})\in Sxx^{-1}.

Thus, for every
x\in S
, one has

Sxx^{-1}=Txx^{-1}\cap S.

(2.1)

This implies that the domain and codomain of
\sigma_{s}
are indeed

Sss^{-1}\text{ and }Sss^{-1}s=Ss\,\,(\subseteq Ss^{-1}s\cap S),

(2.2)

respectively. Let us next note that

(\rho_{{}_{T}})^{-1}\circ\hat{\rho}_{{}_{S}}:(S)\rho_{{}_{T}}\longrightarrow(S)\hat{\rho}_{{}_{S}}

defined by

\rho_{s}\longmapsto\rho_{s}|_{Dom\rho_{s}\cap S}=\sigma_{s},\,s\in S,

is an isomorphism. Now, because
\sigma_{x}\sigma_{x}^{-1}
is the identity on
Sxx^{-1}=Txx^{-1}\cap S
and
\rho_{xx^{-1}}
is the identity on
Txx^{-1}
(
=Dom\rho_{x}
), where
x^{-1}
is the inverse of
x
in
T
, we have

\sigma_{x}\sigma_{x}^{-1}=\rho_{xx^{-1}}|_{Dom\rho_{xx^{-1}}\cap S}.

Finally, since
xx^{-1}\in S

3.
Examples concerning left (right) ample semigroups

A semigroup

If a semigroup

5

In this article we shall consider completely

Let

eB
, such that
u^{-1}u\in eB
(if no such inverse semigroup exists then we are done). Then, after effectuating necessary identifications, one can easily verify from
uu^{-1}u=u\neq 0
that
u^{-1}u=(l,1,l)
. Because
I
is not a singleton, we can choose
u
such that
l\neq\lambda
. But then,

\begin{split}\hat{\lambda}_{u}&=(l,1,l)(eB)\longrightarrow(\lambda,h,l)(eB)\\
&=(l,1,l)(\lambda,1,\lambda)B\longrightarrow(\lambda,h,l)(\lambda,1,\lambda)B\\
&=0B\longrightarrow 0B\\
&=\hat{\lambda}_{0}\end{split},

implying the
\hat{\lambda}_{S}
is not a monomorphism.
∎

The following example provides a left ample semigroup of a Brandt semigroup that is not contained in any of the non-zero idempotent generated ideals.

Example 3.6
.

Let
\mathbb{Q}^{+}
be the multiplicative group of positive rationals,
\mathbb{N}_{1}
the multiplicative semigroup of natural numbers starting from 1 and
I=\{1,2,3\}
. Consider the Brandt semigroup
B=(I\times\mathbb{Q}^{+}\times I)\cup\{0\}
and its subsemigroup
S=(\{1,2\}\times\mathbb{N}_{1}\times I)\cup\{0\}
. Subsemigroup
S
is left ample, because for any
n\in\mathbb{N}_{1}
,
j\in\{1,2\}
and
i\in I
, we have
(j,n,i)(i,n^{-1},j)=(j,1,j)\in S
, but not right ample, because
(3,n^{-1},j)(j,n,3)=(3,1,3)\not\in S
. The non-zero idempotent generated ideals are
(i,1,i)B=(\{i\}\times\mathbb{Q}^{+}\times I)\cup\{0\}
. Clearly
S
is not contained in any of them.

Remark 3.7
.

The construction used in the above example works for any group (including finite ones). Let
G
be a group and
I=\{1,2,3\}
. Consider the the Brandt semigroup
B=(I\times G\times I)\cup\{0\}
and the subsemigroup
S=(\{1,2\}\times G\times I)\cup\{0\}
. (Actually we may take
S=(\{1,2\}\times H\times I)\cup\{0\}
, where
H
is any submonoid of
G
.) Now we have, for any
g\in G
,
j\in\{1,2\}
and
i\in I
,
(j,g,i)(i,g^{-1},j)=(j,1,j)\in S
, but
(3,g^{-1},j)(j,g,3)=(3,1,3)\not\in S
. Hence
S
is strict left ample. On the other hand
S\not\subset(\{i\}\times G\times I)\cup\{0\}
for any
i\in I
, but all principle ideals are of the form
(\{i\}\times G\times I)\cup\{0\}
. The reason for the previous example to work is that the strict ample subsemigroup (also a right ideal) is generated by two idempotents
(1,1,1)
and
(2,1,2)
.

Problem 3.8
.

Can we prove that Theorem
3.5
does not apply to the above example and remark?

Let
B=(I\times G\times I)\cup\{0\}
be a Brandt semigroup. Then clearly every subsemigroup of
B
with zero is of the form
S=(I^{\prime}\times H\times I^{\prime\prime})\cup\{0\}
where
I^{\prime}
and
I^{\prime\prime}
are subsets of
I
and
H
is a subsemigroup of the group
G
; a subsemigroup without zero is of the form
I^{\prime}\times H\times I^{\prime\prime}
.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
S=(I^{\prime}\times G\times I^{\prime\prime})\cup\{0\}
be a non-trivial subsemigroup of a Brandt semigroup
B=(I\times G\times I)\cup\{0\}
. Then
S
is left (respectively, right) ample in
B
if and only if
H
is a left (respectively, right) ample submonoid of the group
G
.

Proof.

If
S
is left ample in
B
then for any non-zero element
s=(\lambda,a,\mu)\in S
we have
ss^{-1}=(\lambda,a,\mu)(\mu,a^{-1},\lambda)=(\lambda,1,\lambda)\in S
. This implies that
aa^{-1}=1\in H
for all
a\in H
, whence
H
is a left ample submonoid of the group
G
.

Conversely if
H
is a left ample submonoid of the group
G
then for every
a\in H
and its inverse
a^{-1}\in G
,
aa^{-1}\in H
. So
ss^{-1}=(\lambda,a,\mu)(\mu,a^{-1},\lambda)=(\lambda,aa^{-1},\lambda)\in S
, that is
S
is left ample. A similar argument applies if ‘left ample’ is replaced by ‘right ample’.
∎

Theorem 3.10
.

A subsemigroup
S=(I^{\prime}\times H\times I^{\prime\prime})\cup\{0\}
of a Brandt semigroup
B=(I\times G\times I)\cup\{0\}
is rich left (right) ample in
B
if and only if
H
is a subgroup of
G
.

Proof.

Suppose that
S
is rich left ample in
B
. Take any
x=(i,a,\lambda),~y=(j,b,\mu)\in S
. Now
xy^{-1}\in S
implies
(i,a,\lambda)(\mu,b^{-1},j)\in S
. Without loss of generality we may assume that
\lambda=\mu
and thus
(i,ab^{-1},j)\in S
. This implies
ab^{-1}\in H
. Because
a
and
b
can be arbitrarily chosen, it follows that
H
is a subgroup of
G
.

Conversely assume that
H
is a subgroup of
G
. So
ab^{-1}\in H
for every
a,b\in H
. Take any
x=(i,a,\lambda),~y=(j,b,\mu)\in S
. Then,
xy^{-1}
is either
0\in S
or equals
(i,ab^{-1},\mu)\in S
. Hence
S
is rich left ample. The left version of the theorem can be proved similarly.
∎

4.
Connection with dominions

Given a subsemigroup
S
of semigroup
T
, an element
d\in T
is said to be
dominated
by
S
if
f|_{S}=g|_{S}
implies
df=dg
for all pairs of semigroup homomorphism
f,g:T\longrightarrow W
. The
dominion
of
S
in
T
, denote by
Dom_{T}S
, is the set of all elements of
T
dominated by
S
. In fact,
Dom_{T}S
is a subsemigroup of
T
and oversemigroup of
S
. Let
x\in Dom_{T}S
for some inverse oversemigroup
T
of
S
, and
W
be a semigroup admitting homomorphisms
f,g:T\longrightarrow W
, such that
f|_{S}=g|_{S}
. Then, using the fact that
Imf
and
Img
are inverse subsemigroups of
W
, we have,

(x^{-1})f=(xf)^{-1}=(xg)^{-1}=(x^{-1})g.

This implies that
x^{-1}\in Dom_{T}S
. Hence
Dom_{T}S
is an inverse subsemigroup of
T
.

Remark 4.1
.

Let
S
be a subsemigroup of an inverse semigroup
T
, and
\langle S\rangle_{T}
denote the inverse subsemigroup of
T
generated by
S
. Then
\langle S\rangle_{T}\subseteq Dom_{T}S
.

The following remark connects ampleness with dominions.

Remark 4.2

(
[
7
]
, Proposition 1)
.

If
S
be an ample subsemigroup of an inverse semigroup
T
then
\langle S\rangle_{T}=Dom_{T}S
.

The next example demonstrates that the above condition is not sufficient.

Example 4.3
.

Consider the inverse monoid
T=\mathbb{Q^{+}}
of all positive rational numbers with usual multiplication. Then
S=\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{1\}
is a non-ample subsemigroup of
T
;
in fact,
T=\langle S\rangle_{T}
. Let
\alpha,\,\beta
be homomorphisms from
T
into a monoid
W
, such that
\alpha|_{S}=\beta|_{S}
. Then, one has, for all
n\in S
,

\begin{split}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\alpha&=\left(2\cdot\frac{1}{2n}\right)\alpha=\left(2\right)\alpha\left(\frac{1}{2n}\right)\alpha=(2)\beta\left(\frac{1}{2n}\right)\alpha=\left(\frac{2n}{n}\right)\beta\left(\frac{1}{2n}\right)\alpha\\
&=\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\beta(2n)\beta\left(\frac{1}{2n}\right)\alpha=\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\beta(2n)\alpha\left(\frac{1}{2n}\right)\alpha=\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\beta\left(\frac{2n}{2n}\right)\alpha\\
&=\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\beta\left(1\right)\alpha=\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\beta\left(1\right)\beta=\left(\frac{1}{n}\cdot 1\right)\beta=\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\beta.\end{split}

Because
\alpha
and
\beta
agree on its generators, it follows that they agree on
\langle S\rangle_{T}
.
Hence
Dom_{T}S=\langle S\rangle_{T}
, as required.

Example 4.4
.

Take
T=I_{X}
, where
X
is an infinite set and let
S
be its subsemigroup of full injective mappings on
X
. Clearly
T
is non-group inverse semigroup and the subsemigroup
S
is not ample in
T
. In fact
S
is left ample but not right ample in
T
: For any
s\in S
,
ss^{-1}
is the identity function on
X
so is in
S
but
s^{-1}s
is not in
S
as its domain is not necessarily whole of
X
. We show that
Dom_{T}(S)=S
i.e.,
S
is closed in
T
by using the zigzag argument. Take any
d\in Dom_{T}(S)\setminus U
and let let
d=a_{0}y_{1}=x_{1}a_{1}y_{1}=x_{1}a_{2}y_{2}=x_{2}a_{3}y_{2}=x_{2}a_{4}
, where
a_{i}\in S,x_{i},y_{i}\in T
, be a zigzag in
T
over
S
with value
d
(this is a zigzag of length 2 but the argument can be extended for a zigzag of any length). Since
a_{0}=x_{1}a_{1}
so
X=dom(a_{0})=dom(x_{1})
it follows that
x_{1}\in S
and thus
x_{1}a_{1}=x_{2}a_{2}\in S
. Therefore
dom(x_{2}a_{2})=X
which implies that
dom(x_{2})=X
so
x_{2}\in S
. Hence
d=x_{2}a_{4}\in S
, showing that
S
is closed in
T
. Thus
Dom_{T}(S)=S\subset\langle S\rangle_{T}.
