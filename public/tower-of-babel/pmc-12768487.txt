Mapping transcriptomic signatures downstream of mitochondrial superoxide dismutase SOD2 in OVCA433 multicellular aggregates
Authors: Amal Taher Elhaw, Priscilla W Tang, Shriya Kamlapurkar, Sarah Al-Saad, Sneha Srinivasan, Danyang Li, George C Tseng, Adam C Straub, Nadine Hempel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring metastatic spread, tumor cells adapt to evade anchorage-independent cell death by upregulating mitochondrial antioxidant systems. We previously showed that ovarian cancer cells upregulate mitochondrial manganese superoxide dismutase (SOD2) following detachment and multicellular aggregate (MCA) formation. SOD2 scavenges mitochondrial superoxide and manipulates cellular hydrogen peroxide levels, both functions necessary for metastasis. Here, we investigated SOD2′s metastatic function by assessing its transcriptomic effects in OVCA433 ovarian cancer cells cultured in low-attachment conditions that induce MCAs, mimicking anchorage-independent states in malignant ascites. SOD2 siRNA-mediated knockdown effects in MCAs were compared to adherent culture conditions. RNA sequencing and pathway analysis revealed that SOD2 lies upstream of pro-metastatic pathways, including PI3K/AKT signaling. Notably, in MCAs, cytokine and immune cell signaling pathways were more significantly enriched following SOD2 knockdown. We previously identified SIRT3 as an important SOD2 activity regulator in MCAs. While SIRT3 knockdown resulted in minor transcriptional changes, we identified that FOXO3 and ELF4 transcription factors, important for stress response and immune regulation, are downregulated by both SIRT3 and SOD2 knockdown. Comparing SOD2 knockdown transcriptional changes to the Cancer Genome Atlas, we found SOD2 expression strongly associates with pro-tumorigenic immune signaling in serous ovarian cancer specimens, including genes identified downstream of SOD2 from our siRNA screen. Moreover, SOD2 expression correlated with signatures related to pro-tumorigenic neutrophil and T-regulatory cell populations. Our data suggest SOD2 positively regulates pro-metastatic pathways, and those identified in MCAs more closely reflect gene expression profiles associated with SOD2 expression in patient tumors.Keywords: Ovarian cancer, Metastasis, SOD2, PI3K/AKT

Introduction

Manganese containing superoxide dismutase (SOD2) localized within the mitochondrial matrix is responsible for the dismutation of superoxide anions (O2.−) to hydrogen peroxide H2O2. Loss of SOD2 has been shown to contribute to tumor initiation due to the buildup of O2•− and resultant oxidants, including hydroxyl radicals (HO•), that can contribute to DNA damage. Conversely, SOD2 expression often increases during tumor progression and metastasis (reviewed in [1]). SOD2 is frequently upregulated as a pro-survival adaptation during these advanced tumor stages as cells escape from the primary tumor and need to face intrinsic and extrinsic sources of stress including metabolic changes and new tumor microenvironments. SOD2 undergoes extensive stress-responsive transcriptional regulation through factors like Nrf2, Foxo3A, and NF-κB [1]. In addition, we previously demonstrated that SOD2 is regulated at multiple levels in detached conditions, an early step in metastasis, which includes regulation of its activity via the upregulation of SIRT3, and rapid translation by the mRNA binding protein HuR [2,3].

The resultant consequences of SOD2 upregulation in tumor cells are multi-faceted. O2•−scavenging maintains optimal mitochondrial function, necessary for metastatic tumor cells. In addition, the antioxidant function of SOD2 is thought to promote chemotherapy and radio-resistance [4]. We previously showed that SOD2 expression supports ovarian cancer metastasis, and that SOD2 dependent O2.− scavenging is necessary for anoikis resistance of ovarian cancer cells [2]. Our prior work and that of others also suggests that H2O2, the product of the dismutation reaction catalyzed by SOD2, contributes to redox-signaling in tumor cells. For example, SOD2 expression can drive H2O2-dependent migration [5], and lead to oxidation of phosphatases, including PTEN, thus resulting in activation of pro-tumorigenic signaling of the Phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT pathway [6,7]. Others have also shown that Sod2 can act as a peroxidase when highly expressed in tumor cells [8,9].

Here we set out to further elucidate the function of SOD2 by focusing on how SOD2 affects the transcriptome in OVCA433 tumor cells. Given that SOD2 transcription, translation and activity are strongly induced in anchorage-independent ovarian cancer cells [2,3], we compared the effects of SOD2 siRNA-mediated knock-down on transcriptional changes in both adherent cell culture conditions and in ultra-low attachment plates. The latter promotes 3D multicellular aggregate (MCA) formation, which better recapitulates the anchorage-independent metastatic cell clusters observed in malignant ascites of late-stage ovarian cancer patients. Our results confirm that SOD2 lies upstream of transcriptional pathways regulated by PI3K/AKT signaling and reveals context-specific transcriptomic changes associated with adherent and MCA culture conditions. By comparing transcriptional changes elicited by SOD2 knock-down to transcriptomics data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) we find that the MCA model reveals several SOD2-dependent transcriptional pathways that are also associated with high SOD2 expression in serous adenocarcinoma specimens from TCGA, most prominently a novel association with pro-tumorigenic immune signatures.

Materials & methods

Cell culture conditions

OVCA433 cells were generously gifted by Dr. Susan K. Murphy (Duke University) and maintained in RPMI 1640 medium with 10 % FBS at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. 3D multicellular aggregates (MCAs) were generated by culturing cells in ultra-low attachment suspension culture conditions. Suspension cell culture was performed by growing cells until 75 % confluency, followed by treatment with 0.25 % Trypsin-EDTA for detachment. Following, cells were then seeded into 6-well ultra-low attachment plates (Corning 3471) at 250,000 cells/2 mL of growth medium per well and left at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 for 24 h. For control adherent conditions, OVCA433 cells were seeded at the same density in standard cell culture-treated 6-well plates. Since we previously demonstrated that SOD2 knock-down leads to vast OVCA433 cell death in anchorage-independent conditions at 72 hrs [2], we carried out RNA sequencing on RNA extracted from MCAs at an earlier time point (24 h).

siRNA-mediated knock-down

Knockdown by siRNA for non-targeting control, SOD2 and SIRT3 was achieved by ON-TARGETplus siRNA SMARTPools (non-targeting control: d-001,810–10–05; SIRT3: l-004,827–01–0005; SOD2: l-009,784–00–0005). Cells were incubated with siRNA against either non-targeting, SIRT3 or SOD2 for 24 h, followed by replacement of fresh growth medium for 24 h prior to seeding in either attached or 3D conditions for RNA collection.

Protein isolation and immunoblotting

To confirm efficacy of SOD2 and SIRT3 knockdown at the protein level prior to RNA-sequencing submission, cells from either adherent or MCA suspension culture conditions were collected in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific 89,901) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific 78,443). Samples were incubated on end-over rotation for 30 min at 4 °C, followed by centrifugation at 15000RPM for 30 min at 4 °C. Lysate concentrations were acquired by the Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific 23,225). 20μg of protein from each sample was utilized for SDS-PAGE in 4–20 % Mini-PROTEAN TGX precast gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes through the Trans-Blot Turbo™ transfer system. Membranes were blocked in 5 % milk in TBS with 0.1 % Tween-20. The following antibodies were utilized for primary: SIRT3 (Cell Signaling Technology 5490 s, 1:1000), SOD2 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology A-2, sc-133,134, 1:500) and β−actin (Thermo Fisher AM4302, 1:10,000); and HRP-linked secondary detection: GE Healthcare anti-mouse (NA931V), anti-rabbit (NA934V). Proteins were visualized with SuperSignal West Femto maximum sensitivity chemiluminescence substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific 34,096) and the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP system.

RNA extraction and isolation

Adherent cells were rinsed with 1X PBS and then directly scraped off the cell culture plates in TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen 15,596,026). For MCA suspension cultures, cells were collected into 15 mL tubes and centrifuged at 1500 RPM for 5 min at room temperature (RT). Cells were then transferred to 1.5 mL tubes in 1X PBS and centrifuged for an additional 1500 RPM for 5 min at RT. Supernatants were discarded and cell pellets were resuspended in TRIzol reagent. Direct-zol RNA mini prep (Zymo Research, R2052) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

RNA sequencing and ingenuity pathway analysis

Isolated RNA from OVCA433 cells treated with siControl, siSOD2 or siSIRT3 in adherent or MCA conditions were processed by Novogene for RNA-sequencing using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform and sequencing alignments were done via the Hisat2 mapping tool. Four biological replicates were used for each experimental condition. Normalizations and differential expression (DE) analysis were done utilizing DESeq2. DE analysis was performed between siSOD2 versus siControl (non-targeting), and siSIRT3 versus siControl in adherent and suspension conditions. Following Benjamini-Hochberg correction, genes containing adjusted p-values of 0.05 or less were deemed significantly differentially expressed and further utilized for Qiagen’s Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA). Metabolism, reactome, and signaling pathway collections were utilized for DE comparisons and ranked on p-values via the right-tailed Fisher’s Exact Test. Positive z-score values signify an increased activity between the gene expression profile with the predicted IPA direction of activity, whereas negative z-scores represent decreased activity. Heatmaps were generated from z-score transformed FPKM values in GraphPad Prism following hierarchical clustering (average linkage; Euclidean distance) using https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus/.

Publicly available data analysis

cBioportal.org was used to analyze genes co-expressed with SOD2 in TCGA Pan Cancer ovarian cancer specimens (n = 300 specimens with RNA sequencing data). An RSEM (RNA-Seq by Expectation-Maximization) z-score above or below 0 was used to segregate specimens into high (n = 90) and low SOD2 (n = 210) populations. Pre-ranked gene lists with significant positive and negative correlations to SOD2 expression were further analyzed using pathway analysis in DAVID (Davidinformatics.nih.org). Timer2.0 was used to carry out to assess correlation of SOD2 expression with cell signatures in TCGA samples using CIBERSORT and TIMER analysis. Immune cell correlations were assessed separately using gene expression signatures established by Tamborero et al. [10] and deriving Pearson’s correlation relative to SOD2 expression from TCGA specimens using GraphPad Prism software. The Tumor Immune Single Cell Hub 2 (TISCH2) was used to explore SOD2 expression in single cell RNA sequencing data from publicly available data sets GSE151214 [11], GSE130000 [12], and GSE154600 [13].

Results & discussion

SOD2 knock-down results in common and divergent transcriptional changes in adherent and MCA cultures

In this study we chose OVCA433 cells, an ovarian cancer cell line with high SOD2 expression and activity that further increases when cells are cultured as MCAs [2,14]. After successful knock-down of SOD2 in OVCA433 cells using an siRNA smart pool, as validated by western blotting and SOD zymography (Supplemental Figs. 1 and 2), cells were cultured in adherent culture conditions (2D) or as MCAs (3D) in ULA plates for 24 hrs, followed by RNA extraction and RNA sequencing.

SOD2 knock-down resulted in 894 significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in 2D adherent cells and 612 DEGs in 3D MCAs (p.adj. <0.05, Fig. 1A; Supplemental Table 1). In both conditions, SOD2 knock-down resulted in more genes being downregulated than upregulated (63 % down in adherent; 62 % down in MCAs; Fig. 1B), suggesting that SOD2 supports the upregulation of gene expression in OVCA433 cells more than being repressive. 39 % of downregulated genes and 20 % of upregulated genes following SOD2 knock-down overlapped in 3D MCAs and 2D adherent conditions (Fig. 1B). This indicates that there are unique transcriptional changes dependent on SOD2 expression in MCA and adherent cultures.

To further elucidate the functional roles of SOD2 regulated transcriptional changes in 2D adherent and 3D MCA cultures, we carried out Qiagen Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (Figs. 1and 2, Supplemental Table 2). Compared to the relatively limited set of signaling and reactome pathways upregulated after SOD2 knockdown, the number of significantly downregulated pathways was substantially greater, underscoring SOD2 as a positive regulator of transcriptional programs (Figs. 1C and 2A). This analysis also revealed that several pathways downstream of SOD2 are shared between cell culture conditions, albeit their significance being weighted differently (Figs. 1D and 2B). Shared pathways that decreased following SOD2 knock-down included PI3K/AKT (Fig. 1E) and ERK/MAPK signaling, senescence (Supplemental Fig. 3A), and embryonic stem cell pluripotency pathways (Supplemental Fig. 3B). Although significantly different in both conditions following SOD2 knock-down, pathways associated with proliferation, such as cell cycle checkpoints (Fig. 2A) were more obviously affected by SOD2 knock-down in adherent conditions (Supplemental Fig. 3C). This reflects our observations that 3D MCAs slow proliferation and switch to a cell survival mode. Notably, PTEN signaling was increased, while PI3K/AKT signaling was decrease following SOD2 knock-down, in both adherent and MCA cultures (Fig. 1C). This fits with the pro-survival function of this pathway in MCAs [15,16], and our previous observations that high SOD2 expression is associated with H2O2-dependent inactivation of PTEN and activation of PI3K/AKT signaling [17,18] and that loss of SOD2 reduces MCA survival (Supplemental Fig. 4), which is exacerbated in long-term anchorage independent culture, as previously reported by us [2].

Interestingly, the genes driving these shared pathways downstream of SOD2 were not always the same in the two different culture conditions, as evident by gene expression changes within these pathways. For example, in MCAs PI3K pathway changes are driven primarily by the down regulation of PIK3CG, GDF15, GAB1, IFNAR1, INPP5D, FOXO3, FOXO1, NFKBIA, IKBKE, and RAP2B following SOD2 knock-down, while this was driven by changes in RAF1, CCND1, MAPK1, IL17RD, PPP2CB, EIF4E, YWHAB, YWHAQ, CHUK and INPP5F downregulation in 2D conditions following SOD2 knock-down (Fig. 1E).

The cyclophilin pathway, which regulates migration, IL-6 signaling and proliferation, as well as HER2 signaling (Fig. 1C), and Rho GTPase cycle (Fig. 2A) were more significantly negatively enriched in adherent 2D conditions. Of note, genes within the RHO GTPase signature (Fig. 2C) that were downregulated in response to SOD2 knock-down primarily have roles in regulation of GTPase activation (ARHGAP35, ARHGAP19, ARHGAP22, CDC42EP1, ARHGEF39, CDC42SE2, PREX1), actin and microtubule organization (DAAM1, ABI2, DBN1, FMNL3, CAPZB, TWF1, CKAP4, LMNB1, TPM3, TPM4, VIM), cell adhesion and membrane dynamics (GIT1, BAIAP2L2, AKAP12, CAVIN1), and membrane trafficking (FAF2, FAM91A1, MTMR1, DLC1). These data suggest SOD2 regulates transcription of genes responsible for reorganization of the cytoskeleton and cell migration.

In MCA conditions pro-metastatic pathways such as the sheddase pathway were more enriched following SOD2 knock-down (Fig. 1Cii and F). Given the established roles of sheddases in releasing growth factors, cytokines, and adhesion molecules, and in shaping metastatic niches, this downregulation may have particular relevance to the biology of MCAs [19–23]. The above findings correlate with prior work from the Melendez lab demonstrating that SOD2 drives transcription of MMPs in tumor cells in a H2O2-depdented manner via MEK/ERK [24–26], which is important for tumor cell invasion. In endothelial cells, SOD2 deficiency has been shown to impair cellular adhesion and migration, accompanied by dysregulation of integrin expression and disruption of barrier permeability through defective fibronectin processing caused by impaired dimerization [27]. The influence of SOD2 on matrix-associated gene expression in ovarian cancer cells appears to parallel these endothelial findings.

In addition, pro-survival pathways such as the NGF-stimulated transcription (Fig. 2D), which canonically acts through PI3K/AKT signaling, and several cytokine and immune related pathways were more significantly affected by SOD2 knockdown in MCAs, including IL-8 signaling (Fig. 1Cii), and neutrophil degranulation (Fig. 2Aii). Interestingly, several genes in the IL-8 signaling pathway that are upregulated in MCAs compared to adherent conditions (Fig. 1G) include subunits of the Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase, Protein Kinase C and phospholipase A, such as PIK3C3, PLCB2 and PRKCA. These genes are all necessary upstream regulators of AKT and ERK signaling, suggesting that IL-8 cytokine signaling under the control of SOD2 feeds into AKT signaling in MCAs. The above indicate that SOD2 knock-down more strongly affects downregulation of pro-metastatic and immune related pathways in 3D MCA conditions. The predominance of downregulated pathways following SOD2 knock-down, particularly those governing kinase activity, survival and immune signaling underscores the role of SOD2 as a positive upstream regulator of broad signaling programs associated with metastasis. Whether these changes are directly due to SOD2-mediated H2O2 signaling, or a consequence of decreased superoxide scavenging remains to be determined in future studies.

Pathways that were upregulated following loss of SOD2 included PPARα/RXRα activation (Fig. 1Ci, Supplemental 3D) and PTEN signaling (Fig. 1C). Enriched pathways in response to SOD2 knock-down in adherent 2D cultures included cholesterol biosynthesis (Fig 2A and E), while extracellular matrix organization was more strongly enriched in 3D MCAs, alongside shared induction of lipid-related pathways seen in 2D conditions (Fig. 2A). Many of these pathways are activated by oxidants or cellular redox imbalance, suggesting that increased mitochondrial superoxide following SOD2 loss could drive activation of these oxidative stress–responsive signaling cascades [28–33].

Collectively, these findings reveal that SOD2 loss induces conserved signaling changes across the two different culture conditions as well as context-specific adaptations potentially shaped by the constraints of each culture condition. For example, MCAs slow their proliferation and enhance their survival adaptations. This is due in part to changes in receptor signaling associated with matrix and cell adhesion receptors that are differentially engaged upon detachment from adherent conditions, as well as metabolic changes associated with culture in anchorage independent conditions. In addition to changes in energy demands due to a slowing in proliferation and enhanced focus on survival, cells within MCAs maintain high respiration, thus requiring adequate superoxide scavenging.

SOD2 knockdown results in context-dependent metabolic pathway transcriptional alterations across adherent and MCA cultures

To assess if SOD2 affects metabolic pathways directly in OVCA433 cells, metabolic pathway enrichment analysis was carried out (Fig. 3A). Downregulated pathways revealed a strong overlap between the two culture conditions, with six metabolic pathways significantly suppressed in both 2D and 3D cultures following SOD2 knock-down (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, these shared pathways all converged on the inositol phosphate signaling network (Fig. 3A and C). These govern the synthesis and turnover of inositol phosphates and phosphoinositides, such as PI (4,5)P2 and PI(3,4,5)P3, which are essential for activating PI3K/AKT, PLC/Ca2+, and actin cytoskeletal remodeling pathways [34]. Downregulation of this network would therefore be expected to limit PI3K-driven signal transduction, consistent with our signaling pathway analysis showing suppression of PI3K/AKT signaling following SOD2 knockdown (Fig. 1). This again agrees with prior studies (including our own data) that show PI3K/AKT signaling is up-regulated in 3D cultures and that this pathway is redox-sensitive and downstream of SOD2 [6,18, 35]. In 3D MCA cultures, repression of the phospholipase pathway following SOD2 knock-down (Fig. 3Aii), which cleaves PI(4,5)P2 to generate DAG and IP3, further suggests a connection to inositol phosphate-driven calcium and PKC signaling in MCAs. These data identify the inositol phosphate network as a major metabolic hub maintained by SOD2 in both 2D and 3D cultured ovarian cancer cells.

Notably, several metabolic pathways were uniquely downregulated only in 2D cultures, including the pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP) salvage pathway, critical for amino acid metabolism and neurotransmitter synthesis [36], and the pyrimidine ribonucleotide salvage pathway (Fig. 3Ai and D), important for maintaining nucleotide pools under metabolic stress [37,38]. These changes suggest that, under adherent conditions, SOD2 is essential for preserving cofactor and nucleotide recycling systems critical for amino acid metabolism, DNA synthesis, and cell proliferation. The absence of these effects in 3D cultures may reflect the lower proliferative rate and reduced anabolic demand of multicellular aggregates.

Metabolic pathway analysis again demonstrated that siRNA against SOD2 induced upregulation of cholesterol biosynthesis pathways in 2D adherent conditions (Fig. 3A). These lipid metabolic changes fit with upregulation of PPAR and cholesterol signaling pathways observed above (Figs. 1and 2). In contrast, no metabolic pathways were significantly upregulated in 3D MCAs (Fig. 3Aii), underscoring that this transcriptional response to SOD2 loss is context dependent and implies that under adherent conditions the presence of SOD2 may be inhibitory to cholesterol synthesis or that increased superoxide levels drive cholesterol and PPAR signaling, an observation that warrants further investigation. Together, these findings suggest a novel role of SOD2 as a metabolic regulator with overlapping and context-specific roles adapting to the structural and metabolic demands imposed by different tumor cell growth conditions.

Effect of knock-down of SIRT3 on the MCA transcriptome

The mitochondrial deacetylase SIRT3 is an important regulator of SOD2, and we previously demonstrated that SOD2 activity in MCAs is at least partially dependent on elevated SIRT3 expression [2]. Thus, we sought to explore if siRNA mediated SIRT3 knock-down (Supplemental Fig. 1) results in similar effects on transcription as SOD2. However, compared to SOD2, SIRT3 knock-down resulted only in minor changes in transcriptional changes in both adherent and MCA conditions (Fig. 4A&B) and shared limited gene expression changes with SOD2 (Fig. 4C, Supplemental Table 3). Consequently, pathway analysis resulted in few enriched signatures in response to SIRT3 knock-down (Fig. 4B) and demonstrates that SIRT3 knock-down does not fully recapitulate the effects on transcription elicited by SOD2 knock-down. However, ERK, PIP3-activated AKT signaling, and the RHO GTPase cycle pathways were significantly negatively enriched following SIRT3 knock-down in adherent conditions (Fig. 4B), sharing some similarity with the effects of SOD2 loss (Figs. 1and 2). Assessing genes that are commonly regulated by both, highlights that a major transcription factor downstream of PI3K/AKT signaling, FOXO3, is significantly downregulated by both SOD2 and SIRT3 knock-down in attached conditions (Fig. 4C). In addition, ELF4, a transcription factor important for immune cell signaling, such as IL-8 transcription is also regulated by both. It remains to be seen if the SIRT3-dependent transcriptional changes are elicited by its function as an SOD2 deacetylase and directly related to SOD2-dependent changes in cellular superoxide/H2O2 balance, or due to effects on other deacetylation target proteins. Although requiring further study, the minor effects of SIRT3 knock-down on MCA transcriptional changes may be reflected by the fact that SIRT3 knock-down lead to a more muted decrease in SOD2 activity compared to SOD2 knock-down at the 24-hour time point (Supplemental Fig. 2). Based on our prior work in MCAs cultured for 72 h, SIRT3 may be more important for preserving SOD2 superoxide scavenging activity and cell survival in long-term anchorage-independent conditions.

SOD2 expression in patient tumors is associated with immune signature identified through SOD2 knock-down in MCAs

We next sought to assess if any of the transcriptional changes observed following SOD2 knock-down in adherent and MCA culture conditions are associated with SOD2 expression in patient tumors. For this we assessed the expression of SOD2 using bulk RNA sequencing data from 300 serous adenocarcinoma specimens available through The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Reactome pathway analysis demonstrated that SOD2 high tumors are associated with gene expression signatures related to immune signaling (Fig. 5A), including neutrophil degranulation, a top Reactome signature identified to be the strongest negatively enriched pathway downstream of SOD2 knock-down in MCAs (Fig. 2A). A heatmap of expression changes of genes within this pathway illustrates that a majority of these genes are upregulated in MCAs compared to adherent conditions and under the control of SOD2 in MCAs (Fig. 5B). Importantly, we assessed the correlation of these genes with SOD2 expression in TCGA patient tumors and found highly significant positive correlation with all but two of these genes (Fig. 5C).

MCAs suggest that SOD2 expression can manipulate transcription of cytokine and other immune effector genes within tumor cells that may affect the recruitment and function of immune and stromal cells (Figs. 1and 2). Using TIMER analysis to identify cell populations from bulk RNA sequencing data of TCGA tumors, we found that SOD2 expression in serous ovarian tumors are strongly associated with neutrophils, myeloid dendritic cells and CD4 positive T-cells (Fig. 5D). Further analysis using CIBERSORT demonstrated that T regulatory cells (Tregs), macrophages and resting myeloid dendritic cell signatures were also significantly associated with SOD2 expression (Fig. 5D). These data suggest that high SOD2 expression in tumors is associated with the presence of CD4+ T-cells, specifically Tregs, and myeloid cells, including neutrophils, dendritic cells and macrophages. This strong association with neutrophils, Tregs and macrophages was confirmed using the signatures developed by Tamborero et al. [10]. In contrast, Helper T cells that activate cytotoxic T cells, and follicular helper T-cells that aid B cells in tumor antibody production had low association with SOD2 expression in tumors (Fig. 5E). The high Treg association was also reflected by high FOXP3 expression in SOD2-high tumors (Fig. 5F). The above data suggest that SOD2 expression in serous ovarian tumors is associated with the enrichment of immune cells that are generally considered to have pro-tumor functions, including neutrophils, which support the tumor niche, promote tumor progression and suppress T-cell activation, and Tregs, major suppressors of T-cell anti-tumor defenses. The highly significant correlation of SOD2 with PD-L1 and PD-1 levels in ovarian tumors further suggests that SOD2 expression is associated with an immune suppressive environment (Fig. 5F). Interestingly, a recent study found that besides its immune suppressive role, PD-L1 can regulate the expression of SOD2 in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma cells as a mechanism to enhance ferroptosis resistance [39].

Our observations that SOD2 expression is upstream of several cytokine and immune signaling transcriptional programs in ovarian cancer cells, suggests that tumor intrinsic SOD2 affects the recruitment and/or function of tumor associated immune and stromal cells, including neutrophils and Tregs. Given the strong association of SOD2 expression with gene expression of these cell populations signatures in ovarian cancer tumors from bulk RNA sequencing data (TCGA; Fig. 5D and E), an alternative hypothesis is that SOD2 expression is high in tumor associated cell populations within the tumor stroma. To address this, we explored publicly available singe cell RNA seq data from ovarian cancer patients, which demonstrated that SOD2 expression is found predominantly within mono/macrophage, fibroblast, endothelial and malignant cell populations (Supplemental Fig. 5). Although the scRNA data did not include neutrophil populations, the low association with CD4+ T-cells at least suggests that the positive correlation between SOD2 and Tregs is not due to high SOD2 Treg expression.

Conclusion

We provide a valuable data set to further elucidate the role of SOD2 in tumor cells under two different culture conditions. These findings set the stage to further explore the role of SOD2 on the tumor microenvironment given our findings that high SOD2 expression is strongly associated with Tregs and neutrophils in ovarian cancer tumors and that some of the cytokine pathways associated with this may be directly associated with intrinsic expression of SOD2 in tumor cells. Since these cell populations are involved in tumor immune evasion mechanisms, these data warrant further study as to how tumor intrinsic increases in SOD2 manipulate the recruitment, activation and exhaustion of immune cell populations. The relatively modest response of serous ovarian tumors to immune therapy requires better understanding of the mechanisms of resistance to this therapy and identification of biomarkers that can predict positive response [40]. In this context, understanding the link between SOD2 expression and immune evasion will be of interest.

This work also opens several questions as to how SOD2 is regulating these pathways. Since we predominantly saw downregulation of transcription following SOD2 knock-down, this suggests that high SOD2 expression in tumors predominantly drives the upregulation of transcription. A question requiring further exploration is whether this is due to removal of any repressive effects of superoxide on transcription or due to H2O2-mediated induction of transcription. Since our work and that of others has suggested that high SOD2 expression can also lead to H2O2-dependent signaling in tumor cells, this is a possible scenario to further explore using approaches that specifically scavenge H2O2, such as use of catalase targeting to the mitochondria, which would also help to rule out any potential changes due to indirect mechanisms unrelated to SOD2-mediated changes in mitochondrial oxidants. If H2O2-dependent signaling is observed, additional unbiased approaches such as cysteine oxidation proteomics screens will be needed to further identify the direct targets of oxidant signaling in response to SOD2 manipulation in ovarian cancer cells. Alternatively, removal of O2.− by SOD2 can shift the NO balance within tumor cells [41]. It is thus possible that consequential increases in protein nitrosation or resultant cysteine disulfide changes lie upstream of the transcriptional changes observed [42,43].

Priority pathways that are of high interest to further explore include the role of SOD2 in inositide and ribonucleotide metabolic pathways, as these are strongly involved in tumor biology and necessary for signaling, proliferation and metastasis. In particular, given prior work demonstrating that MCAs are enriched in PI3K/AKT signaling [15,16] and prior work demonstrating that SOD2 regulates AKT signaling in cancer cells via its regulation of H2O2-dependent PTEN oxidation [17,18], these data provide and additional pathway that may contribute to the regulation of AKT signaling via IP3 synthesis pathway regulation.

In conclusion, our study provides an initial transcriptomic framework for understanding how SOD2 shapes tumor cell biology in attached and anchorage-independent contexts. By linking SOD2 expression to both conserved and context-specific signaling changes, these findings highlight SOD2 as a central regulator of transcriptional programs with potential consequences for tumor–immune interactions and metabolic adaptation. Together with prior evidence that SOD2-driven redox signaling influences key oncogenic pathways, our results underscore the importance of further dissecting the redox-dependent and independent mechanisms by which SOD2 reprograms tumor cells. Ultimately, integrating transcriptomic analyses with functional studies of redox signaling, immune modulation, and metabolic rewiring will be essential to fully delineate the contribution of SOD2 to ovarian cancer progression.
