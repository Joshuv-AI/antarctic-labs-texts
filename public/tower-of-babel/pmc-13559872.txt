Synthesis and Hepatotoxicity Evaluation of Paeonol Clofibrate
Authors: Haoyu Tu, Ling Ding, Jing Zheng, Yichen Yang, Xin Chen, Yumiao Song, Xinyi Shi, Chuwen Wang, Xinya Xu, Jiping Liu, Yundong Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClofibrate (CF) is an effective lipid‐lowering drug, but its clinical use is restricted by hepatotoxicity. Molecular hybridization with paeonol may improve hepatic safety while preserving efficacy. To synthesize CF‐Paeonol and compare its lipid‐lowering activity and hepatic safety with CF. CF‐Paeonol was prepared by esterifying clofibric acid with paeonol. Its lipid‐lowering activity was assessed in Triton WR‐1339‐induced hyperlipidemic mice, while hepatic safety was evaluated after 30 days of administration in normal mice. Plasma lipids, liver function, bile acids, oxidative stress, inflammation, histopathology, molecular docking, and hepatic YY1/FXR expression were examined. CF‐Paeonol dose‐dependently reduced triglycerides and total cholesterol. At the high dose, levels decreased by 30.51% and 33.78%, respectively, versus the model group, while the equimolar dose showed efficacy comparable to CF. Compared with CF, CF‐Paeonol significantly lowered AST and ALT and reduced the liver histopathological score from 11 to 3. It also improved bile acid, oxidative stress, and inflammatory indices without obvious pathological changes in other organs. Docking predicted interactions with PPAR‐α and YY1, and Western blot showed decreased YY1 and increased FXR expression. CF‐Paeonol retained lipid‐lowering activity with less hepatic injury than CF, warranting further pharmacokinetic, metabolic, and causal mechanistic studies for confirmation.Keywords: clofibrate, hepatotoxicity evaluation, hypolipidemic effect, paeonol, YY1/FXR

Introduction

With the ongoing shifts in modern lifestyles and dietary compositions in recent years, lipid metabolism disorders have emerged as a pressing global public health challenge [1, 2]. The incidence rate of hypertriglyceridemia (HTG) has been consistently rising, and this condition is widely regarded as a crucial risk factor for various clinical disorders, including atherosclerosis, coronary heart disease, ischemic stroke, acute pancreatitis, and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [3, 4]. HTG represents an abnormal physiological status featuring excessive accumulation of triglycerides within peripheral blood plasma [5, 6]. It facilitates atherosclerotic development by promoting the accumulation of triglyceride‐rich lipoproteins and their remnants in the vascular wall, causing endothelial dysfunction, and intensifying oxidative stress and inflammation. At the same time, it markedly elevates the susceptibility to acute pancreatitis, liver damage, and metabolic syndrome [7]. Consequently, the research and development of safe and highly efficient triglyceride‐lowering drugs are clinically significant in preventing and treating diseases induced by lipid metabolic abnormalities [8, 9, 10]. Fibrates are clinically important agents for the management of HTG. Among currently available fibrates, fenofibrate is generally preferred, particularly when combined with statins, because of its relatively low risk of drug interactions and myopathy [8]. In contrast, clofibrate (CF) is a first‐generation fibrate whose clinical application is limited by hepatotoxicity. Nevertheless, CF was selected as a proof‐of‐concept scaffold because its active metabolite, clofibric acid, contains a well‐characterized PPAR‐α pharmacophore and a carboxyl group that can be readily conjugated with paeonol. CF lowers plasma triglyceride (TG) and total cholesterol (TC) levels by activating PPAR‐α, thereby promoting fatty acid oxidation and increasing lipoprotein lipase activity [11]. Moreover, its reproducible hepatotoxicity, including liver enlargement, hepatocyte injury, inflammatory infiltration, and bile acid dysregulation [12, 13, 14], provides a suitable model for evaluating whether molecular hybridization can retain lipid‐lowering activity while reducing hepatic injury. Beyond these manifestations, emerging evidence indicates that drug metabolism and transporter‐mediated processes play a critical role in the pathogenesis of drug‐induced liver injury (DILI), affecting both the disposition of hepatotoxic drugs and the elimination of endogenous toxic metabolites [15]. The hepatotoxicity of CF considerably restricts its clinical application. Recent studies have indicated that its mechanism of hepatotoxicity is closely associated with oxidative stress, inflammatory reactions, and dysregulated bile acid metabolism [13, 16]. Recent studies have reported that the DILI induced by CF is related to the activation of YY1 protein in the liver [17]. Earlier investigations from our research team also demonstrated that liver damage resulting from CF administration is correlated with elevated pro‐oxidative and pro‐inflammatory responses [18, 19]. Compared with formulation optimization, which primarily improves drug delivery without fundamentally altering the intrinsic toxicity of the parent compound, and combination therapy, which may introduce additional drug–drug interactions and compliance concerns, structural modification can integrate lipid‐lowering and hepatoprotective properties into a single molecule. Therefore, it may provide a more direct and controllable strategy for reducing CF‐induced hepatotoxicity while retaining its pharmacological activity. Thus, introducing natural active moieties with anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and bile acid metabolism‐modulating effects to reduce hepatotoxicity while maintaining the lipid‐lowering activity of CF may serve as a pivotal approach for the structural modification of fibrates.

Natural active ingredients serve as an important source of lead compounds for innovative drug development [20]. With diverse structures, high safety, and broad pharmacological activities, they provide high‐quality moieties for drug molecular design and modification [21, 22]. The principle of molecular hybridization involves linking structural fragments with distinct pharmacological activities via covalent bonds to construct novel compounds with synergistic effects and reduced toxic side effects, representing an important strategy for drug molecular optimization [23]. Recent evidence further supports this strategy, as He et al. demonstrated that graveoline, a natural quinolinone alkaloid, alleviated D‐GalN/LPS‐induced acute liver injury by regulating inflammatory cytokines and suppressing the JAK1/STAT3 signaling pathway, highlighting the potential of natural compounds for the prevention of inflammation‐associated liver injury [24]. Paeonol is a phenolic active ingredient extracted from traditional Chinese medicines such as Moutan Cortex and Cynanchum paniculatum [25]. It exerts hepatoprotective effects by attenuating oxidative stress and inflammation and maintaining bile acid homeostasis [26, 27]. Paeonol was selected over other hepatoprotective phytochemicals because it combines these pharmacological effects with a small and structurally simple scaffold containing a single phenolic hydroxyl group, which enables its direct and selective esterification with clofibric acid. This structural compatibility facilitates molecular hybridization while minimizing unnecessary structural complexity.

Previous studies have shown that conjugating fibrate pharmacophores with natural bioactive fragments may preserve lipid‐lowering activity while reducing hepatic adverse effects. Hydroxytyrosol‐, carvacrol‐, and vanillin‐CF hybrids exhibited hypolipidemic activity with improved hepatic safety, whereas the paeonol‐based fibrate derivative T9 alleviated hyperlipidemia‐associated liver injury through regulation of PPAR‐a / HMG‐CoA‐related proteins, oxidative stress, and inflammation [18, 19, 28, 29]. Although paeonol‐fibrate hybridization has been reported, CF‐Paeonol is structurally distinct because clofibric acid is esterified directly with the phenolic hydroxyl group of paeonol without an additional linker. This study therefore examined whether this structure could preserve lipid‐lowering activity while reducing chronic CF‐induced liver injury.

Based on these considerations, CF‐Paeonol was designed using clofibric acid as the lipid‐lowering pharmacophore and paeonol as the hepatoprotective fragment, as schematically illustrated in Figure 1. We hypothesized that, at equimolar doses, CF‐Paeonol would retain or improve the lipid‐lowering activity of CF, as reflected by reductions in plasma TG and TC, while exhibiting improved hepatic safety. The expected safety advantages were defined as lower liver index, AST, ALT, plasma and hepatic TBA, MDA, IL‐6, and TNF‐α levels; higher SOD, GSH, and CAT activities; and attenuated hepatic histopathological injury compared with CF. We further hypothesized that these effects would be associated with modulation of the YY1/FXR signaling pathway and restoration of bile acid homeostasis. Accordingly, the hypolipidemic activity and hepatic safety of CF‐Paeonol were systematically evaluated in mouse models.

Results

Chemistry

The synthetic route for CF‐Paeonol is shown in Figure 2. CF was first dissolved in 1,4‑dioxane, upon which a 6 mol/L NaOH aqueous solution was slowly introduced dropwise under stirring conditions. The mixture was subjected to hydrolysis at 60°C over a period of 4 h to yield clofibric acid. After the reaction was completed, the mixture was acidified to pH 2 with 2 mol/L hydrochloric acid, resulting in the precipitation of clofibric acid as a white solid. Upon dissolution in DMF, clofibric acid and paeonol underwent a coupling reaction in the presence of EDCI and DMAP catalytic system. Reaction progression throughout the experiment was analyzed and monitored by means of TLC analysis. Following the termination of the reaction, acidification of the mixture was adjusted using 2 mol/L hydrochloric acid. After extraction and rotary evaporation, the crude product was obtained. Subsequent purification and isolation afforded the desired compound CF‑Paeonol, which was achieved by silica gel column chromatography using petroleum ether–ethyl acetate (20:1) as the eluent. The molecular structure of the target molecule was fully characterized through the combined spectroscopic data obtained from 1H‑NMR, 1 3C‑NMR, and high‐resolution mass spectrometry (HR‐MS).

Bioactivity Assessment

Hypolipidemic Activity

The Triton WR‐1339‐induced hyperlipidemia model is a classical animal model widely used to evaluate the hypolipidemic activities of compounds. [30, 31]. To construct hyperlipidemia animal models, mice were injected with Triton WR 1339 (400 mg/kg) through the tail vein, and the hypolipidemic capacity of CF‑Paeonol was subsequently assessed in this study. The three dose levels of CF‐Paeonol were determined based on our preliminary experiments. The medium dose (0.267 mmol/kg) was set to be equimolar to the positive control, CF, while the low and high doses were set at one‐half and twofold of the medium dose, respectively, to evaluate the dose–response relationship [19]. As illustrated in Figure 3, the serum concentrations of TG and TC in the model group were remarkably upregulated when contrasted with those of the control group (p < 0.01), which verified that the hyperlipidemia animal model had been successfully constructed. Based on the above findings, CF‑Paeonol significantly ameliorated the blood lipid profiles of model mice. In reference to the model group, the serum triglyceride levels in low, medium, and high dosage groups of CF‑Paeonol were remarkably lowered by 9.86% (p > 0.05), 19.31% (p < 0.01), and 30.51% (p < 0.01), respectively. Accordingly, TC levels were reduced by 11.42% (p > 0.05), 23.27% (p < 0.01), and 33.78% (p < 0.01), respectively. The positive control group treated with CF showed a 15.70% reduction in TG (p < 0.05) and a 22.41% decrease in TC (p < 0.01). Compared with the CF group, the medium‑ and high‑dose groups of CF‑Paeonol exhibited further reductions in TG by 4.28% (p > 0.05) and 17.56% (p < 0.05), respectively, and in TC by 1.11% (p > 0.05) and 14.65% (p < 0.05), respectively. Taken together, these experimental findings reveal that CF‑Paeonol displays remarkable lipid‐lowering effects depending on dose levels, and its lipid‐lowering activity is gradually enhanced with increasing dosage. When administered at equimolar doses, the medium‐dose CF‑Paeonol group exhibited stronger TG‐lowering effects than the positive control CF and also significantly decreased TC levels.

Preliminary Molecular Docking Analysis Related to the Hypolipidemic Effect

In view of the remarkable hypolipidemic effects exerted by CF‐Paeonol, this study further used molecular docking technology to initially predict and evaluate its lipid‐lowering mechanism. According to prior research findings, CF achieves its TG‐lowering function primarily through the activation of PPAR‐α [32]. Therefore, PPAR‐α was selected as the target protein in this study to carry out molecular docking research. First, under the Windows 11 system, the molecular docking module of AutoDock software was used to complete the operation. PPAR‐α protein crystal structure was downloaded from the PDB database (PDB ID: 6KB1), and the parent compound CF was used as the control ligand. As shown in the results of Table 1, the binding energy between CF‐Paeonol and PPAR‐α was −6. 96 kcal/mol, and the binding energy of CF was −6. 02 kcal/mol. The corresponding three‐dimensional conformation of molecular docking is shown in Figure 4. The ester carbonyl group on the phenoxyalkanoate of CF‐Paeonol formed a hydrogen bond with Phe‐423 of PPAR‐α, with a distance of 2.1 Å between them. In the CF structure, the oxygen atom on the ester moiety established hydrogen bonding interactions with Tyr‐334, with a distance of 2.4 Å between them; a hydrogen bond was established between Asn‐219 and the carbonyl oxygen atom of the ester group, with a distance of 2.1 Å between them. These docking results represent static computational predictions and should be interpreted only as preliminary structural hypotheses.

Study on the Hepatotoxicity of CF‐Paeonol

Given the significant hypolipidemic activity of CF‑Paeonol, this study further explored its potential hepatotoxicity. Normal KM mice were employed as the animal model in this experiment to assess the hepatic effects of the compound. In toxicological research, elevated liver weight and liver coefficient are commonly regarded as key indicators of liver injury, hepatocellular hypertrophy, inflammation, or edema [33]. As presented in Figure 5, body weight exhibited no obvious statistical differences between the control group and the mice treated with CF or CF‑Paeonol. Statistical results revealed that an obvious elevation in liver weight was found in the CF group in contrast to the control group(p < 0.01). In contrast, liver weight in the CF‑Paeonol treatment group was remarkably reduced compared with the CF group(p < 0.05), and there was no statistically distinct difference from the control group (p > 0.05). Regarding the liver coefficient, it was notably higher in the CF group than in the control group (p < 0.01). Meanwhile, the liver coefficient in mice treated with CF‐Paeonol was notably decreased compared with the CF group (p < 0.01), exhibiting no remarkable discrepancy compared to the normal control group (p > 0.05). All of the aforementioned findings jointly reveal that CF‑Paeonol exhibits notably milder hepatotoxicity than CF under the present experimental conditions.

AST and ALT are sensitive indicators reflecting hepatocyte injury. When hepatocytes are damaged due to inflammation and other reasons, they will be released into the blood, resulting in an increase in blood test values [34]. The detection results of AST and ALT indicators are shown in Figure 6. After 30 consecutive days of administration, the plasma AST and ALT contents of mice from the CF group were markedly elevated in comparison with the control group (p < 0.01), indicating that sustained CF administration leads to evident liver impairment in mice. In Figures A and B, mice in the CF‐Paeonol group exhibited notably lower plasma AST concentrations when compared with the CF‐treated group (p < 0.05); meanwhile, the plasma ALT activity also presented an extremely prominent decline (p < 0.01), without notable statistical discrepancies relative to the control animals (p > 0.05). The aforementioned results demonstrate that compared with CF, CF‐Paeonol can significantly alleviate DILI and has better liver safety.

In contrast to the control group, mice from the CF group exhibited notably lower plasma concentrations of SOD, GSH, and catalase (CAT), whereas the concentration of malondialdehyde (MDA), an indicator of lipid peroxidation, presented notable upregulation (p < 0.01), suggesting that chronic CF treatment can lead to prominent oxidative stress responses in mice. These data demonstrate that CF can induce oxidative stress damage in experimental mice, while CF‐Paeonol can significantly improve the oxidative stress response.

It has been reported that inflammatory responses are tightly correlated with oxidative stress in the progression of drug‐induced hepatotoxicity [35]. Based on this, this study used the ELISA kit method to detect the contents of pro‐inflammatory factors IL‐6 and TNF‐α within hepatic tissues of mice. According to the data presented in Figure 9, compared to the control group, the intrahepatic expression of IL‐6 and TNF‐α was greatly enhanced in the CF group (p < 0.01); in contrast, the levels of the above two inflammatory factors in the CF‐Paeonol group presented an obvious decline (p < 0.05). Taken together, the present results reveal that CF can significantly activate the hepatic inflammatory response, while CF‐Paeonol can effectively improve and inhibit the CF‐induced hepatic inflammation.

To further verify that CF‐Paeonol has reduced liver toxicity compared with CF, we took photos of the liver immediately after sampling and stained liver tissue sections to observe pathological changes. The obtained findings are displayed in Figure 10. The liver of the control group was dark reddish‐brown, with a smooth, moist and shiny surface, clear five lobes and sharp edges (Figure 10A); the liver of the CF group showed obvious dark red or congestion‐like changes in local areas, and some lobes were slightly swollen or deformed (Figure 10B); liver congestive pathological changes were notably attenuated in the CF‐Paeonol group compared to the CF‐treated group, and the liver weight was also reduced compared with the CF group (Figure 10C). Pathological section observation showed that the hepatocytes of the control group were arranged regularly with clear boundaries, clear cell nuclei, no vacuoles, clear sinusoidal structure, and no obvious inflammatory cell infiltration or necrotic foci (Figure 10D). On the contrary, the hepatocytes of the CF group showed obvious swelling and necrosis, loose cytoplasm, degenerative changes, and karyolysis of cell nuclei, alongside remarkable infiltration and substantial clustering of inflammatory cells (Figure 10E). Within the liver of the CF‐Paeonol intervention group, no congestion of hepatic central veins or massive infiltration of inflammatory cells was found, but slight swelling of some hepatocytes could still be observed (Figure 10F). Studies by Zhao et al. have shown that YY1 is the target of DILI caused by CF [17]. Collectively analyzing the above results, it is speculated that CF‐Paeonol has milder damage to the mouse liver, and its mechanism may be related to inhibiting the YY1/FXR signaling pathway, reducing bile acid levels, while suppressing oxidative damage and ameliorating inflammatory reactions. Thus, this study preliminarily explored the potential mechanisms involved. The expression levels of YY1 and FXR proteins in mouse liver following drug administration were determined via Western blot. Figure 11 demonstrates the detected protein expression data. When contrasted with the control group, the relative expression of YY1 protein in liver tissue was significantly elevated (P < 0.01), accompanied by remarkable downregulation of FXR protein (P < 0.01). In contrast to CF‐treated mice, the YY1 protein level in liver tissue of the CF‐Paeonol intervention group was notably decreased (P < 0.01), along with markedly elevated FXR protein expression (P < 0.05). The experimental results indicate that CF‐Paeonol may inhibit the YY1 transcription factor, thereby elevating the transcriptional activity and protein abundance of FXR, and ultimately alleviating liver damage.

While evaluating liver injury, we evaluated the biological safety of multiple vital organs in mice, including the heart, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and brain. As shown in Figure 12, histological sections of multiple organs displayed no remarkable variations between the control group, CF‐treated group, and CF‐Paeonol intervention group, all maintaining normal physiological structures. The specific manifestations are as follows: The cardiac tissue sections showed that the myocardial fibers were arranged neatly and densely, the myocardial cell contours were clear, the nuclei were centrally located, the blood vessels in the myocardial interstitium were evenly distributed, and no obvious edema, inflammatory infiltration, or fiber rupture was observed. The splenic tissue exhibited well‐defined separation of red and white pulp under histological observation; the white pulp was formed by dense lymphocyte aggregation, and the red pulp area had a uniform distribution of red blood cells. The lung tissue sections showed clear alveolar structures, thin and intact alveolar walls, regular alveolar cavity morphology, and a small amount of physiological fluid. The kidney tissue sections showed intact glomerular structures, clear capillaries, and neatly arranged renal tubular epithelial cells with normal morphology. The brain tissue sections showed clear and intact neuronal cellular structure, homogeneous distribution of nerve fibers, and unaltered ventricular structures. The aforementioned results reveal that sustained intragastric treatment with CF and CF‐Paeonol over 30 days exerted no distinct pathological damage to vital visceral organs in mice, and all organs maintained normal tissue morphology.

Molecular Docking Study on Binding Affinity Between CF‐Paeonol and YY1 Protein

Existing studies have shown that CF may induce DILI by activating the YY1 target in the liver [17]. Therefore, we took YY1 as the target protein to carry out molecular docking research. The 3D crystal structure of the YY1 protein was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDBID: 1UBD), and the parent compound CF was used as the control ligand. As shown in the results of Table 3, the binding energy between CF‐Paeonol and YY1 was −4. 89 kcal/mol, and the binding energy of CF was −6. 17 kcal/mol. The corresponding three‐dimensional conformation of molecular docking is shown in Figure 13. In the docking with YY1, a hydrogen bond interaction was observed between Lys‐339 and the carbonyl oxygen within the ketone fragment of CF‐Paeonol, and the distance between them was 2.3 Å; the phenoxy moiety and ester carbonyl oxygen atom of CF could respectively establish hydrogen bonds with the Arg‐342 residue, and the distances between them were 3.1 and 2.7 Å, respectively.

Discussion

The principal finding of this study is that molecular hybridization of the clofibric acid pharmacophore with a paeonol‐derived moiety produced a derivative that retained lipid‐lowering activity while causing less pronounced hepatic alterations than equimolar CF under the present experimental conditions. This finding is of particular significance because the therapeutic value of fibrates depends not only on their lipid‐regulating efficacy but also on their long‐term hepatic safety. The present results therefore support molecular hybridization as a potentially useful strategy for improving the efficacy–safety balance of fibrate derivatives. From a structure–activity perspective, the lipid‐lowering effect of CF‐Paeonol suggests that introduction of the paeonol moiety did not abolish the pharmacological function of the clofibric acid pharmacophore. Meanwhile, the attenuation of hepatic functional, metabolic, inflammatory, oxidative, and histopathological alterations suggests that the structural incorporation of the paeonol‐derived fragment may contribute to reducing the hepatotoxic potential associated with CF. The observed differences in PPAR‐α binding, YY1 expression, and FXR expression provide preliminary mechanistic clues linking the structural modification to the biological effects of CF‐Paeonol. However, these findings remain associative and require further validation through targeted functional experiments.

Previous natural‐product–CF hybrids, including hydroxytyrosol–, carvacrol–, and vanillin–CF conjugates, retained lipid‐lowering activity while reducing hepatic injury, mainly through antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory effects [18, 19, 29]. The paeonol‐based fibrate derivative T9 similarly improved lipid profiles and hepatic injury through PPAR‐α/HMG‐CoA‐related regulation [28], whereas bis‐3,4‐dimethoxybenzene–fibrate derivatives represent an alternative structural optimization strategy [31]. Compared with these compounds, CF‐Paeonol directly links paeonol to the complete clofibric acid pharmacophore and was evaluated against equimolar CF during prolonged administration, with additional assessment of bile acid accumulation and YY1/FXR expression. However, its hepatic safety was examined only in normal mice, and the respective contributions of intact CF‐Paeonol, its hydrolysis products, and YY1/FXR signaling remain unclear. Therefore, further pharmacokinetic and mechanistic validation is required.

Consistent with previous studies, the lipid‐lowering effect of fibrate drugs mainly depends on the activation of PPAR‐α. [11]. In this study, the binding energy of CF‐Paeonol to PPAR‐α (−6.96 kcal/mol) was higher than that of CF (−6.02 kcal/mol). Molecular docking showed that its carbonyl group formed a stable hydrogen bond with Phe‐423, which provided a structural basis for its enhanced lipid‐lowering activity. It is worth noting that the introduction of the paeonol fragment did not interfere with the ability of clofibric acid to activate PPAR‐α; instead, it enhanced the binding affinity by increasing the hydrophobic interaction of aromatic rings and hydrogen bond sites. This result supports the feasibility of the “pharmacophore + natural active fragment” conjugation strategy in optimizing fibrate drugs.

Regarding liver toxicity, long‐term administration of CF can lead to increased liver weight, elevated ALT/AST levels, oxidative stress damage, and inflammatory factor secretion in experimental mice, which is consistent with the literature reports [12, 13, 14, 16]. From a broader perspective, maintaining hepatic homeostasis and limiting persistent liver injury are important for preventing chronic liver disease progression [37, 38]. CF‐Paeonol significantly reversed the above changes. Recent studies have pointed out that YY1 is a key transcription factor for CF‐induced DILI, and its activation can inhibit FXR expression, thereby weakening the negative regulatory effect of FXR on bile acid metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammation. [17, 39, 40, 41]. In addition to the YY1/FXR pathway, emerging evidence suggests that noncoding RNA‐mediated inflammatory regulation may also contribute to acute liver injury, as Wang et al. identified lncRNA TTN‐AS1 as a potential regulatory target in sepsis‐associated acute liver injury [42]. Against this broader mechanistic background, the present study showed in vivo that CF‐Paeonol significantly downregulated YY1 expression and restored FXR levels, simultaneously reduced the TBA content in plasma and liver, and alleviated oxidative stress (elevating SOD, GSH, and CAT levels while reducing MDA content) and inflammatory factors (IL‐6, TNF‐α) [43]. Molecular docking predicted that CF‐Paeonol bound less strongly to YY1 (−4.89 kcal/mol) than CF (−6.17 kcal/mol), suggesting a weaker interaction with YY1 and, consequently, reduced YY1‐mediated liver injury. Based on these findings, we propose that CF‐Paeonol may exert hepatoprotective effects through a sequential mechanism. Its relatively weak interaction with YY1 may attenuate YY1‐mediated transcriptional repression of FXR, thereby increasing FXR expression. Enhanced FXR signaling may subsequently improve bile acid homeostasis, reduce oxidative stress and inflammation, and ultimately alleviate liver injury.

There are several limitations in the current study. First, the evaluation of the lipid‐lowering effect adopted a hyperlipidemia model induced by Triton WR1339. This model is suitable for rapid screening but differs significantly from the pathophysiological state of clinical chronic hyperlipidemia. Second, the study on hepatotoxicity and its mechanism was limited to in vivo animal experiments and molecular docking; causal verification by overexpressing or knocking down YY1/FXR in hepatocyte lines (such as HepG2 and primary mouse hepatocytes) was not performed, resulting in limited depth of the mechanism. Third, the pharmacokinetic and metabolic characteristics of CF‐Paeonol remain unclear. CF‑Paeonol is linked via an ester bond directly connecting clofibric acid and paeonol. This ester bond is susceptible to enzymatic hydrolysis under physiological conditions, and therefore the compound is likely to undergo enzymatic hydrolysis in vivo. Notably, our previous in vitro metabolism study on the structurally similar CF‑Carvacrol hybrid has confirmed the hydrolytic behavior of this class of ester‑linked molecules—the formation of clofibric acid, carvacrol, and intact CF‑Carvacrol was clearly detected, suggesting that the parent compound and its hydrolysis products may jointly contribute to the pharmacological effects [19]. Based on this prior experimental evidence, the effects observed in the present study are likely to arise from intact CF‑Paeonol, its hydrolysis products (clofibric acid and paeonol), or their combined actions. Future studies should systematically evaluate the stability of CF‑Paeonol in plasma and liver microsomes, and simultaneously quantify CF‑Paeonol, clofibric acid, and paeonol using LC‑MS/MS, in order to determine its bioavailability, tissue distribution, elimination half‑life, and the true pharmacologically active forms. Fourth, the toxicity evaluation only used a single equimolar dose (equivalent to the maximum clinical dose of CF), and no multi‐dose gradient or long‐term low‐dose administration observation was carried out, so the toxicity threshold remains unclear.

Future studies should first evaluate CF‐Paeonol in a high‐fat diet‐induced chronic hyperlipidemia mouse model. Its lipid‐lowering efficacy should be quantified by measuring serum TG, TC, LDL‐C, and HDL‐C levels, whereas hepatic safety should be assessed using the liver index, ALT, AST, TBA, oxidative stress markers, inflammatory cytokines, and histopathological scores. Second, the involvement of the YY1/FXR pathway should be validated in HepG2 cells and primary mouse hepatocytes using YY1‐ and FXR‐overexpression models. Changes in YY1 and FXR mRNA and protein levels, intracellular bile acid accumulation, oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokines, and cell viability should be quantitatively assessed. Third, pharmacokinetic studies should be conducted using LC‐MS/MS to determine the Cmax, Tmax, AUC, elimination half‐life, clearance, and tissue distribution of CF‐Paeonol. In parallel, plasma and liver microsomal stability assays should be performed to quantify the extent of CF‐Paeonol hydrolysis into clofibric acid and paeonol.

Conclusion

CF‑Paeonol exhibited lipid‑lowering activity comparable to that of CF, while causing less pronounced hepatic alterations in normal mice. Its reduced hepatotoxic potential may be associated with modulation of the YY1/FXR pathway and improvements in bile acid metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses. These findings support molecular hybridization as a promising strategy for improving the efficacy–safety balance of fibrate derivatives; however, the acute hyperlipidemia model, toxicity assessment at a single dose level, and the lack of causal mechanistic and pharmacokinetic validation limit the generalizability of the current conclusions. Future studies should evaluate CF‑Paeonol in chronic hyperlipidemia models, further elucidate its YY1/FXR‑dependent mechanisms, and conduct molecular dynamics simulations, pharmacokinetic, and metabolic stability assessments.

Methods

Synthesis Of the Target Compound CF‐Paeonol

Synthesis of 2‐(4‐Chlorophenoxy)‐2‐Methylpropionic Acid (CA)

CF (10.00 g, 41.20 mmol) was dissolved in 1,4‐dioxane (250 mL), and 6 mol/L NaOH solution (20 mL) was added dropwise with stirring. The reaction mixture was stirred at 60°C for 4 h and monitored by TLC. After completion of the reaction, the mixture was adjusted to pH 2 with 2 mol/L hydrochloric acid and extracted three times with ethyl acetate (100 mL each). The combined organic layers were dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate and concentrated under reduced pressure to afford clofibric acid as a white solid (6.42 g, 72.6%).

Synthesis of CF‐Paeonol

Dissolve paeonol (5.09 g, 30.63 mmol), clofibric acid (6.42 g, 29.99 mmol), EDCI (6.91 g, 36.05 mmol), and DMAP (0.742 g, 6.07 mmol) in DMF (65 mL), and stir at ambient temperature for 8–10 h. Once the reaction had finished, acidify the solution to pH 1–2 employing 2 mol/L hydrochloric acid solution, then extraction was conducted and the organic layer was separated with 100 mL ethyl acetate‐water mixture for three times, followed by sequential washing with 50 mL saturated sodium carbonate solution in triplicate, dry it adopting anhydrous sodium sulfate, and the ethyl acetate solvent was evaporated off by employing a rotary evaporator, thereby yielding the crude product. Afterwards, column chromatography was adopted for the isolation and purification of the crude mixture, using petroleum ether‐ethyl acetate at a volume ratio of 20:1, and a pale yellow oily compound was finally acquired, CF‐Paeonol (7.44 g), with a yield of 68.6%. Spectral analysis was employed to characterize the structure of the target compound, including 1H‐NMR, 13C‐NMR, and HR‐MS detection. Molecular Docking

Selection of Target Proteins

Retrieve relevant literatures by searching for “PPAR‐α + molecular docking” and “YY1 + molecular docking” in the Web of Science database, comprehensively screen and select the most commonly used protein crystal structures in the studies, and determine PPAR‐α (PDB ID: 6KB1) and YY1 (PDB ID: 1UBD) as the target proteins for this molecular docking study [44]; download the original PDB format files of the above two proteins from the RCSB PDB protein database, which serve as the receptor proteins for the docking experiment.

Construction and Processing of Ligand Molecules

Use ChemDraw chemical drawing software to accurately draw the two‐dimensional chemical structures of CF‐Paeonol and CF with reference to the verified chemical structures. After structural optimization by the software, export the structures as PDB format files, which serve as the ligand molecules for the docking experiment; import the ligand files into AutoDock molecular docking software, and perform preprocessing such as hydrogenation, charge assignment, and conformational optimization to eliminate structural redundancy and ensure the accuracy of docking.

Software Parameter Setting Before Docking

Import the pretreated PPAR‐α, YY1 receptor proteins, CF‐Paeonol and CF ligand molecules into AutoDock software respectively to complete the construction of the receptor‐ligand system; refer to the literature reports of the corresponding proteins in the RCSB PDB database to locate and determine the core active sites of PPAR‐α and YY1 proteins, and set this region as the effective binding region for molecular docking; set key parameters such as docking grid parameters, conformational search algorithms, and energy optimization standards, with the grid center accurately covering the protein active sites to ensure that the ligand molecules can fully bind and conduct conformational search within the effective region [45].

Molecular Docking and Result Screening

Start the molecular docking program according to the set parameters, allowing the ligand molecules to perform flexible conformational search for active site regions on receptor proteins, and automatically calculate the binding energy under different binding conformations; after the docking is completed, take the binding energy as the core screening criterion, combined with the rationality of the conformation, and select the optimal docking conformation for each ligand–receptor combination (the lowest binding energy and the highest matching degree between the conformation and the active site) [46].

Result Visualization and Analysis

Import the screened optimal docking conformation files into PyMOL molecular visualization software for 3D structure modeling and rendering, clearly display the interaction modes such as hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions between ligand molecules and receptor proteins, and label the key binding amino acid residues and interaction distances; sort out the binding energy data of different ligands with the same receptor, conduct comparative analysis, and clarify the binding ability and binding characteristics of CF‐Paeonol, CF with PPAR‐α and YY1.

Evaluation of the Hypolipidemic Activity of CF‐Paeonol

Animal Experiments

Select healthy SPF‐grade male KM mice with a body weight controlled at 20 ± 2 g. First, adaptively feed them for 3 days, assign a continuous number to each mouse, and then randomly select numbers to divide them into six groups (n = 8): normal control group, model group, low‐dose CF‐Paeonol group, medium‐dose CF‐Paeonol group, high‐dose CF‐Paeonol group, and positive drug group. The gavage treatment plan for each group is as follows: both the normal control and model groups are given 0. 5% sodium carboxymethylcellulose (CMC‐Na) solution; the low‐, medium‐, and high‐dose CF‐Paeonol groups are dosed at 0.1335, 0.267, and 0.534 mmol/kg, respectively; the positive drug CF group is dosed at 0. 267 mmol/kg. Each group was subjected to intragastric dosing with a constant volume of 10 mL per kg of body weight, with continuous gavage for 7 days. During the entire experimental period, mice in each group had unrestricted access to feed and water ad libitum and were fed ordinary maintenance feed. 18 h before the last administration, except that the control subjects were treated via caudal venous injection with the same dosage volume of physiological saline, acute hyperlipidemia was induced in other experimental animals by caudal vein administration of Triton WR1339 at 400 mg/kg body weight. At 6 h post‐final administration, the mouse whiskers were removed to avoid hemolysis, and blood was collected from the orbital venous plexus. After the blood is left standing for 30 min, a low‐temperature centrifuge is used to centrifuge and separate the serum at 4°C, 4500 r/min for 15 min, which is then stored at −80°C for later use.

Kit Detection

Take the serum stored at −80°C and thaw it on ice, and take out the reagents of the TC and TG kits and place them on ice. First, add the sample serum, add the reaction solution after all samples are added, and finally incubate at 37°C for 10 min. After incubation, a microplate reader was employed to detect the absorbance value of the reacted solution at 500 nm. Each sample is tested in three replicate wells, and the average value is taken. Calculate the results according to the formula provided in the instruction manual, and import the data into GraphPad Prism software to export the bar chart.

Hepatotoxicity Evaluation of CF‐Paeonol

Animal Experiments

Select male KM mice with a body weight of 20±2 g. After 3 days of adaptive feeding, each mouse was continuously numbered, and then the mice were randomly partitioned into three groups with eight individuals in each group. Among them, the control group received an equivalent‐volume 0.5% CMC‐Na solution, while the CF and CF‐Paeonol groups were dosed at 1.65 mmol/kg, respectively. The applied dosing level was designed in accordance with the maximum clinical dose of CF, and CF‐Paeonol was treated at an equal molar concentration relative to CF. All mice were given intragastric administration continuously for 1 month, with unrestricted access to food and drinking water throughout the whole period. After the end of intragastric administration, the mouse whiskers were removed to avoid hemolysis, and blood was harvested from the orbital venous plexus. After the blood was left standing for 30 min, a low‐temperature centrifuge was used to centrifuge and separate the serum at 4°C, 4500 r/min for 15 min, which was then preserved at −80°C for subsequent experiments. The mice were then sacrificed and dissected, and tissues such as heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain were removed, rinsed with normal saline to remove residual blood, and connective tissues were removed. For the liver tissue, the surface moisture needs to be blotted dry with absorbent paper and then weighed, and a part of the tissue is placed in 4% paraformaldehyde for fixation, which is used for subsequent histopathological detection; another part of the liver tissue is stored at ‐80°C for later use.

Kit Detection

Take the serum and tissues stored at −80°C and thaw them on ice. The serum operation is the same as above. For the tissue samples, add them to a centrifuge tube at a ratio of sample: normal saline = 1:9. Cut the tissues into as small pieces as possible with ophthalmic scissors, then centrifuge with a low‐temperature centrifuge at 4°C, 4500 rpm for 10 min. Take the supernatant into a new tube, determine the protein concentration with a protein quantification kit, and finally detect the biochemical indicators of TNF‐α, IL‐6, and TBA. The specific operation of indicator detection shall be strictly in accordance with the kit instructions for different indicators. Import the data into GraphPad Prism software to export bar charts (Figures 6, 7, 8, 9).

Hematoxylin‐Eosin staining

After fixation, the tissues were rinsed under running water for 30 min to remove residual fixative. The tissues were dehydrated by sequential immersion in 70% ethanol for 60 min, 80% ethanol for 60 min, 90% ethanol for 60 min, 95% ethanol twice for 60 min each, and absolute ethanol twice for 45 min each. This graded dehydration procedure gradually replaced the water in the tissues and minimized tissue shrinkage. The dehydrated tissues were cleared in xylene at room temperature until they became transparent, indicating that the ethanol had been completely replaced. The tissues were then infiltrated with molten paraffin at 60°C–62°C and oriented in molds containing molten paraffin according to the predetermined sectioning plane. The molds were placed on a cold stage until the paraffin solidified, after which the blocks were removed from the molds and trimmed. The paraffin blocks were mounted on a microtome and sectioned continuously at a uniform thickness of 4–5 µm. The section ribbons were flattened in a 42°C–45°C water bath and collected on poly‐L‐lysine‐coated slides. The slides were incubated at 60°C–65°C for 2 h to promote tissue adhesion and melt residual paraffin. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene until fully transparent and then rehydrated by sequential immersion in absolute ethanol twice for 2 min each, 95% ethanol for 2 min, 85% ethanol for 2 min, 75% ethanol for 2 min, and distilled water for 2 min. For hematoxylin‐eosin staining, the sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min and rinsed under running water for 2 min to remove excess stain. They were then differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol for 1 s and immediately rinsed under running water to stop the reaction. Next, the sections were immersed in weakly alkaline water for 5 min until the nuclei appeared clearly blue‐purple, rinsed under running water, and counterstained with 0.5% eosin for 2 min. The stained sections were dehydrated in 85% ethanol for 1 min, 95% ethanol for 2 min, and absolute ethanol twice for 3 min each. After clearing in xylene, the sections were mounted with neutral resin under coverslips while avoiding air bubbles and were air‐dried at room temperature before histopathological examination (Figures 10 and 12).

Western Blotting

Liver tissues stored at −80°C were thawed on ice, rinsed with precooled PBS to remove blood and other impurities, and blotted dry with filter paper. A 100 mg tissue sample was weighed, transferred to a centrifuge tube on ice, and minced thoroughly with ophthalmic scissors. Inhibitors and lysis buffer were added at a sample‐to‐lysis‐buffer‐to‐inhibitor volume ratio of 5:50:3. The tissue was fully homogenized with a tissue grinder, and the homogenate was incubated on ice for 30 min to ensure complete lysis. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, after which the supernatant was transferred to a new tube. The total protein concentration was determined using a protein quantification assay kit. All samples were adjusted to the same protein concentration with lysis buffer to ensure a uniform loading volume. The samples were heated in a boiling‐water bath at 100°C for 5 min to denature the proteins and expose the epitopes. After cooling, a portion of each sample was aliquoted and stored at ‐80°C, while the remainder was kept on ice for immediate analysis. Equal amounts of protein were loaded onto the gel and separated by electrophoresis at 210 V and 400 mA for 90 min. The proteins were then transferred to a membrane at 210 V and 400 mA for 45 min. The membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 60 min and incubated overnight with the internal reference antibody diluted 1:7500. After incubation, the membrane was washed three times with 1×TBST and exposed for signal detection. The membrane was then washed three additional times with 1×TBST and incubated overnight with the primary antibody diluted 1:3000. After three washes with 1×TBST, the membrane was incubated with the secondary antibody diluted 1:7500 for 60 min. Finally, the membrane was washed three times with 1×TBST and exposed for signal detection (Figure 11).
