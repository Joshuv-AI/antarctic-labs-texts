Meta-heuristic and machine learning based functional capacity prediction using gait parameters in patients with heart failure
Authors: Aylin Tanriverdi Eyolcu, Ayşe Doğru, Selim Buyrukoğlu, Sema Savci, Buse Ozcan Kahraman, Mehmet Birhan Yilmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to predict functional capacity using wearable sensor-based spatiotemporal and kinematic gait parameters, and meta-heuristic-based machine learning approaches in patients with heart failure (HF). This cross-sectional study included 70 patients with HF and assessed functional capacity and gait parameters using the six-minute walk test and a wearable inertial sensor, respectively. The Synthetic Minority Over-Sampling Technique for Regression was employed to increase the number of instances. The optimisation of four machine learning models (XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest, CatBoost) was implemented using Simulated Annealing, Genetic Algorithms, Particle Swarm Optimisation (PSO) and Bayesian Optimisation. The model performance was evaluated via 10-fold cross-validation through the R-squared coefficient (R²), root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and mean squared error (MSE). The interpretability of the optimal model was investigated using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to explain individual feature contributions. The hybrid PSO-CatBoost model provided better predictive accuracy (R2 = 0.9456; RMSE = 17.2891; MAE = 10.9230; MSE = 298.9114) as compared to other hybrid optimisation-model configurations. SHAP ranked right stride length and gait speed as the most important features. The hybrid PSO-CatBoost demonstrated high predictive accuracy in estimating functional capacity in patients with HF using gait parameters, but its potential as a clinical decision-support tool requires further validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56217-x.

Introduction

A universal definition of heart failure (HF) is that ‘HF is a clinical syndrome with symptoms and/or signs caused by a structural and/or functional cardiac abnormality and corroborated by elevated natriuretic peptide levels and/or objective evidence of pulmonary or systemic congestion’1. The prevalence of HF is 56 million people on a global scale, and rises with age in both women and men, with high mortality and morbidity rates2,3. This condition is progressive if properly unmanaged, as the inability of the heart to pump efficiently causes inadequate blood flow to organs and tissues, stimulating compensatory mechanisms that can worsen the condition over time4. Orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, fatigue, ankle swelling, reduced exercise tolerance, and inability to exercise are symptoms frequently encountered in clinical trials and used in risk scoring in patients with HF1.

Patients with HF experience poor functional capacity caused by the coupling of reduced cardiac and pulmonary reserve, skeletal muscle and peripheral dysfunction, impaired autonomic regulation and existing comorbidities5. The 6-minute walk test (6MWT) is a widely applied tool to evaluate the functional capacity of HF patients6. 6MWT has been determined to be a safe, simple and reliable tool in this population7–9. 6MWT predicts mortality and morbidity in patients with HF10. Additionally, functional impairment assessed using 6MWT provides additional prognostic information over recognised risk markers in patients with HF11. Furthermore, the 6MWT distance (6MWD) provides prognostic information similar to that of peak oxygen consumption and the ventilatory efficiency obtained through cardiopulmonary exercise testing, a gold standard for measuring exercise capacity, in this population12.

Gait speed has an incremental value in predicting mortality and hospitalisation in older patients with HF13. Gait parameters obtained from wearable sensors provide a large amount of data on human movement, such as gait speed, stride length, and cadence, regardless of whether those assessments are performed in laboratory or clinical settings14,15. Nowadays, machine learning (ML) approaches using parameters of gait analysis are widely investigated16–18. ML is one subfield of artificial intelligence that focuses on developing algorithms that allow computers to automatically learn patterns and make predictions or decisions based on data19. The applications of ML in patients with HF are becoming widespread. ML algorithms can be utilised for predicting HF risk, diagnosing HF, classifying HF phenotypes, and predicting outcomes following HF diagnosis20. Meta-heuristic algorithms are essential in enhancing the performance of ML models, as they effectively search a complex and multidimensional search space and avoid local minima. Their adaptive optimisation potential allows for optimising model hyperparameters with fine accuracy, which results in improved predictive accuracy, robustness and extrapolation to a wide variety of datasets21. By determining the feasibility of ML applications that estimate functional capacity using wearable sensor-based gait parameters, these models would have the potential to provide practical and accurate assessment and to optimise exercise prescription in cardiac rehabilitation, leading to better disease management in patients with HF. Therefore, this study aimed to predict functional capacity using wearable sensor-based spatiotemporal and kinematic gait parameters and meta-heuristic-based ML approaches in patients with HF and to determine the optimal gait parameters.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a single-centre, retrospective and cross-sectional study. Ethical approval of this this study was provided by Çankırı Karatekin University Ethics Commission (decision code: f4e68ddf5d3f451f, decision date: 09.07.2025). All participants in this study provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients with heart failure and reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), defined according to international guideline, comprised the study population22. Inclusion criteria consisted of being clinically stable, being between 35 and 85 years, and being in New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class II or III. Exclusion criteria consisted of neurological or orthopaedical disease affecting mobility, significant chronic respiratory or kidney disease or diabetic neuropathy.

Functional capacity assessment

The 6MWT was performed to evaluate functional capacity following the international statement23. Participants were instructed to walk as quickly as possible along a 20-meter flat corridor for 6 min. The standard phrases of encouragement were used every minute during the test. The distance covered during 6MWT was noted as 6MWD.

Gait assessment

Gait assessment was performed using the reliable G-WALK® gait analysis system (BTS Bioengineering S.p.A., Italy)24. A wearable inertial sensor (G-SENSOR®) comprising a triaxial accelerometer (16 bit/axes; 8 g), a triaxial magnetometer (13 bit; 1200 µT), a triaxial gyroscope (16 bit/axes; 250°/s), and a global positioning system receiver was positioned at the S1 and S2 vertebrae and fastened with a semi-elastic strap25. Each participant was asked to walk along an 8-meter path at their preferred walking speed. Obtained data, sampled at 100 Hz, were wirelessly sent to a computer via Bluetooth, then processed using software (BTS G-STUDIO, version 3.5.25.0)25. In total, 37 gait parameters, automatically analysed by the software, were used for model development (see Supplementary, Table S1 online).

Data augmentation and preprocessing

The dataset used in this research was expanded via the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique for Regression (SMOGN), which boosted the number of total observations to 41026–28.

In contrast to standard oversampling, SMOGN uses a relevance function to determine which areas of the continuous target variable are underrepresented and increases the number of observations of the training sample to 41026. The strategy aims at increasing the capacity of the model to acquire the uncommon pathological gait patterns without affecting the general data distribution. The final sampling size chosen at 410 observations was calculated assessing that they will give the meta-heuristic optimization process enough statistical power and reduce the risk of overfitting that commonly comes with defining too much synthetic data creation.

The modeling pipeline was designed in such a way that it all possibilities of data leakage and ensure methodological rigor. First, 80% training set and a 20% independent testing set were created out of the original data (n = 70). More importantly, all preprocessing operations such as data augmentation (SMOGN) and Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) were carried out in the training folds. The test set, made up of 14 original patients, was completely untouched by any data generation or feature selection processes used to create a synthetic data set. This will guarantee that the model test is based on real clinical generalizability on unseen data (see Fig. 1 to see the detailed pipeline).

In order to make the performance estimates of the small dataset (10-fold cross-validation) stable and reliable, a 10-fold cross-validation framework was applied on the development set (80% of the original data). This strategy, together with an independent hold-out test set (20%), is a strong evaluation of how the model can be applied to unobserved clinical cases.

Recursive feature elimination

Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) is a feature selection method which removes and keeps the most relevant features in a recursive manner, training a model and removing the least significant features29. The model is fitted to the data at every iteration, and features are ranked by their importance or contribution to the predictive performance. The most insignificant feature(s) are eliminated, and the process continues until the number of features required is achieved.

RFE was used to reduce the noise and increase the interpretability of the models. It was necessary to optimize the feature space, which was achieved by selecting the 25 important gait parameters out of the initial 37. Besides, the SMOGN algorithm was incorporated into the pipeline to take care of data imbalance. SMOGN was effective in enhancing the learning ability of the model because of complex biomechanical interactions by having a relevance function that concentrated on underrepresented target values.

Meta-heuristic algorithms for hyperparameter optimisation

The optimisation of hyperparameters is an essential stage in ensuring the overall performance of an ML model is optimised. In the present research, the traditional ML methods were supplemented by meta-heuristic and Bayesian algorithms, which provide more advanced and efficient optimisation strategies21. These algorithms are characterised by the possibility to explore a wide parameter space, locate global optima and minimise the probability of local optima entrapment. Simulated Annealing (ASA) automatically changes the cooling schedule and the sampling strategy in the parameter space based on the nature of the problem30. The adaptive mechanism allows the algorithm to find the global optimum faster and more efficiently and decreases the chances of local optima trapping. It is especially useful in high-dimensional, nonlinear optimisation problems. Genetic algorithm (GA) is powerful, nature-inspired optimization tool that use evolutionary principles to solve complex problems across diverse domains. Their adaptability and ability to search large, complex spaces make them valuable for both theoretical and practical applications31. In a GA, mutation and crossover operations are used on the entire new generation. Hybrid Particle Swarm Optimisation (HPSO) is a hybrid algorithm that is built upon the Particle Swarm Optimisation (PSO)32. HPSO aims to address the issue of premature convergence and enhance the process of global solution discovery by keeping the quick convergence property of PSO and exploiting the advantage of other algorithms. HPSO usually uses the global search power of PSO with the local search or exploration powers of other complementary algorithms. This combination allows the algorithm to both perform a wide-ranging search and perform more detailed searches in promising areas. Bayesian Optimisation is based on the development of a probabilistic model of the objective function, typically a Gaussian Process33. The model has already been assessed to create a pre-belief regarding the behaviour of the function. A function of acquisition is then used to decide the next most promising point to consider. The acquisition function is a trade-off between exploration (searching areas where the model is uncertain) and exploitation (searching in areas that are likely to give good results). This is done iteratively until a predetermined number of iterations has been completed or until a convergence criterion has been reached.

Machine learning algorithms

In the present work, a collection of ML algorithms that are typically used in regression problems and which exemplify various learning paradigms was used. These algorithms are famous in terms of performance and effectiveness in different spheres of application34. Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) is a faster and more efficient type of the gradient boosting model with a specific optimised implementation35. XGBoost works by repeatedly building the weak learners, usually decision trees, by gradient of the loss function, with each new tree designed to reduce the errors made by the previous learners. Regularisation techniques are also used in this method to control overfitting, thus turning XGBoost into an effective and robust modelling tool. Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), unlike the classic gradient boosting algorithms, builds trees in a leaf-wise manner instead of level-wise36. The approach enables more complex trees to be built and thus becomes more accurate but can also run the risk of overfitting. LightGBM also offers rapid training times, histogram-based algorithms and parallel learning features. Random forest (RF) works through the pooling of several decision trees, each of which has been trained on a randomly chosen subset of the dataset37. Moreover, the random subset of features is taken into account at every split. This added randomness also minimises overfitting and increases the generalizability of the model. RF is an excellent option when it comes to classification and regression tasks because it can be highly effective and interpreted. CatBoost uses ordered boosting to avoid target leakage, symmetric (oblivious) trees to create balanced trees, and boosting to build computationally efficient trees38. Other sampling techniques, such as minimal variance sampling, stabilise the gradient estimation and enhance convergence and generalisation. These properties allow CatBoost to enjoy good predictive accuracy, high efficiency in training on a large sample, and high performance on heterogeneous and categorical attributes.

Hyperparameter tuning

The process of hyperparameter optimisation is an essential part of the process of ML optimisation39. In this work, a wide hyperparameter range was specified in relation to each of the ML models. The hyperparameters set aside in the individual models are provided in Supplementary, Table S2 online.

The best hyperparameter settings were found within such ranges using meta-heuristic algorithms like SA, GA, PSO and Bayesian Optimisation.

In order to fully replicate the results, the particular optimization parameters, such as the iterations, the population sizes, and convergence criterion of each algorithm, are clearly written in Supplementary Table S3. The last optimized settings are given in the same table as well. Computational experiments were performed all on Google Colab platform with a Tesla T4 to provide a standard of processing power and disclosure of computational cost.

Explainability via SHapley Additive exPlanations

Lundberg and Lee proposed a game theory-based interpretability tool called SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), which is designed to explain the black-box behaviour of ML models34. This technique measures the amount that each feature adds to the prediction of the model in a just way, according to the concept of Shapley values; that is, it measures the effect of a feature on the model prediction relative to the baseline.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation values, whereas those with alternative distributions are presented as median and interquartile range values. Categorical variables are presented as the number (percentage) of patients with the corresponding attribute. Moreover, comparisons between the training and testing datasets were performed using the Student t-test (Welch t-test) with continuous variables that are normally distributed, the Mann-Whitney U test with non-normally distributed continuous variables, and the Fisher exact test with categorical variables. p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Python (version 3.12) was used for ML and statistical analyses.

Figure 2 displays the bias-variance decomposition for the top-performing models from each algorithm family on the non-augmented and augmented datasets. The results indicate that all models suffer from high Mean Squared Error (MSE), with values ranging from 6269 for SA-XGBoost to 7512 for PSO-CatBoost. A significant portion of this error is attributable to high bias, suggesting that the models were underfitting and unable to capture the underlying complexity of the original dataset. Recognizing the limitations of the original dataset, data augmentation was employed to enhance model training. There is a dramatic reduction in MSE for all models; for example, the MSE for the PSO-CatBoost model decreased from 7512 to 560.46. This improvement is largely due to a significant reduction in the bias component, indicating that the augmented data enabled the models to learn the target function more effectively and overcome the initial underfitting problem.

Discussion

The main findings of the present study were (1) the RFE-RF model, when applied to inertial sensor-based gait parameters, had the best potential to predict functional capacity measured using 6MWT without data augmentation, (2) when data were augmented, the PSO-CatBoost model was superior compared to other ML models for the prediction of functional capacity in patients with HF, (3) left pelvic obliquity and pelvic tilt ranges were the most important features for predicting functional capacity without data augmentation, in contrast, right stride length and gait speed were the most important features for predicting functional capacity with data augmentation. This analysis provides initial insights into the key biomechanical parameters driving the predictions.

Our findings demonstrate that data augmentation was a critical and transformative step in this modelling task. While the combination of the RFE-RF was the strongest performer on the limited original dataset, its advantage disappeared when the models were trained on the richer, augmented data. The overall high performance of CatBoost, regardless of the optimisation strategy used, is an indication of its strength and flexibility, which makes it a valid option in predictive modelling in augmented, complex datasets. Additionally, the augmentation process effectively mitigated the high bias (underfitting) observed initially, allowing the gradient boosting models, particularly CatBoost, to excel. Data augmentation led not only to more accurate models but also to more stable and reliable ones. Furthermore, the combination of the PSO-CatBoost for hyperparameter tuning yielded the best predictive performance, achieving an outstanding R2 score of 0.9456. This demonstrates a high degree of accuracy and reliability for the model’s predictions on unseen data. The present study also underscores the importance of a sufficiently large and diverse dataset for training robust ML models and highlights the effectiveness of CatBoost for this particular regression problem.

A variety of related studies have been proposed in the literature, and they are presented in Table 3. De Cannière et al. found that a support vector machine (SVM) model using chronotropic response and effort as input features predicted 6MWD with an overestimation of 42.8 m in the cardiac rehabilitation population41. The present study demonstrated that hybrid PSO-CatBoost was the best model predicting 6MWD with an 10.9 m overestimation in patients with HF. This variation in distance is less than 32 m and 25 m, which is considered a clinically meaningful change in overall disease status in patients with HF and in assessing the effect of cardiac rehabilitation, respectively42,43. These findings indicate that hybrid PSO-CatBoost applied to gait parameters achieves a more accurate prediction for functional capacity in patients with HF.

In the literature, several studies identified different ML models using gait parameters for classification or prediction16–18. Zhou et al. investigated three different ML methods, including Kernel principal component Analysis (KPCA) in combination with Support Vector Machine (SVM), RF and Artificial Neural Network (ANN), to classify healthy young-middle-aged adults, healthy old adults, and geriatric patients based on gait outcomes16. Authors reported that KPCA in combination with SVM, RF, and ANN, when applied to dynamic gait outcomes, are capable of discriminating between age groups with the accuracy of 89%, 73.6% and 90%, respectively. Additionally, cross-entropy between medial-vertical and vertical acceleration, vertical direction, maximal Lyapunov exponent of the vertical acceleration, gait speed and the variability of the accelerations in anterior-posterior and vertical direction have been found to be the most important gait outcomes for classification16. Lim et al. reported that the LightGBM applied to sensor-based gait parameters is superior to predict falls with an accuracy of 96%17. Mizugucci et al. aimed to develop a ML–based automatic rating model of the Clinical Frailty Scale using gait analysis from smartphone video recordings for patients with HF18. The author demonstrated that the LightGBM automatic rating model achieved a good performance in estimating Clinical Frailty Scale, with Shapley analysis identifying 15 primary gait parameters, including gait speed, total gait time, stance phase time, and ankle swing speed, as key contributors to the Clinical Frailty Scale automatic rating system18. In the present study, global SHAP analysis revealed that left pelvic obliquity and pelvic tilt ranges were the most influential; however, when data were augmented, right stride length and gait speed emerged as the strongest features of functional capacity. This suggests that the augmented data allowed the model to uncover more robust and significant relationships between the spatiotemporal and kinematic gait parameters and functional capacity. Gait speed appears to be a key contributor across the studies, underpinning its prominence in prognosis in patients with HF13.

ML model (PSO-CatBoost) and wearable sensor-based gait parameters may offer a promising approach for estimating functional status over time, optimising exercise prescription and assessing the effectiveness of cardiac rehabilitation to increase functional capacity in patients with HF. Gait speed and stride length emerged as the most influential variables in the best model, indicating their role in estimating functional capacity in patients with HF. Identifying declines in these gait parameters may reflect reduced functional capacity in patients with HF15. From a clinical perspective, monitoring changes in gait speed and stride length may support clinical decision-making, particularly when 6MWT is not feasible.

While data limitations remain a challenge, our findings provide a methodological improvement by combining augmentation, feature elimination, and optimization in a unified pipeline. Our study was based on a single-center, cross-sectional design, which may limit the generalizability and clinical applicability of the findings, given the absence of external validation, longitudinal validation, and evaluation of clinical decision impact. Therefore, longitudinal and multicentre studies are required to confirm the generalizability of the model and to better define its role in clinical decision support.

The present study advances the application of ML in clinical prediction tasks by systematically integrating feature selection, data augmentation, and optimisation techniques. Compared to earlier studies, our study applied recursive feature elimination and advanced meta-heuristic algorithms, alongside SMOGN-based data augmentation, to build robust predictive models using state-of-the-art learners. The method is in line with the recent literature, including the study by Trabassi et al. that stated that data balancing needs to be combined with generative AI to discover rare pathological patterns in small clinical gait datasets44. By leveraging PSO-CatBoost as the optimal model, our study achieved competitive predictive efficiency, outperforming several previous studies that lacked either feature selection or optimisation strategies16–18,41,45,46. This contribution demonstrates that the integration of meta-heuristic algorithms significantly enhances predictive accuracy and robustness compared to conventional approaches.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that meta-heuristic-based ML models are useful for predicting functional capacity based on gait parameters in patients with HF. Augmentation through the use of SMOGN worked to enhance data representativeness and lower underfitting, whereas PSO was successful in hyperparameter fine-tuning and the resulting eventual capacity in prediction. CatBoost turned out to be significantly more accurate and more stable with PSO, which proves the power of such a combination to capture the intricate relationships between gait parameters. These results highlight the strength of CatBoost that is PSO-enhanced as an effective and feasible model of predictive analytics. Further studies should evaluate the findings on a much larger dataset in different HF trajectories.
