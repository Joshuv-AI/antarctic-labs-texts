Summary of the Clinical Problem, Operative Strategy, Hybrid Bailout, and Outcome in a Patient Undergoing Open Thoracoabdominal Aortic Repair After Prior EVAR

A thoracoabdominal pseudoaneurysm developed adjacent to the suprarenal bare stent after EVAR, and direct clamping of the prior endograft was considered difficult. Balloon-assisted distal occlusion using bilateral femoral sheaths enabled distal control and distal perfusion during thoracoabdominal graft replacement. A composite graft was created to address diameter discrepancy, and intraluminal aortic cuff deployment provided effective hybrid bailout for distal anastomotic bleeding. The patient had no operative mortality or major morbidity, and 12-month follow-up CT showed no pseudoaneurysm recurrence or endoleak. CT = computed tomography; EVAR = endovascular aortic repair.

Thoracoabdominal graft replacement after endovascular aortic repair (EVAR) remains technically challenging because the distal clamp site is limited by the presence of a stent graft, and diameter mismatch often complicates reconstruction. Open conversion after EVAR is associated with increased operative complexity and morbidity, particularly when distal clamping must be performed over a previously implanted stent graft.1, 2, 3 Mechanical clamping may deform the endograft or injure the aortic wall, highlighting the need for alternative distal control strategies. We describe a hybrid technique that resolves these issues and promotes safe distal control during thoracoabdominal repair.Take-Home Messages•Balloon-assisted distal control provides a practical alternative to mechanical clamping over a stent graft.•Hybrid readiness ensures rapid endovascular reinforcement when unexpected bleeding occurs.

Case Summary

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and any accompanying images or videos. A 76-year-old man previously underwent EVAR for a 69-mm abdominal aortic aneurysm. Follow-up computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a 56-mm thoracoabdominal pseudoaneurysm associated with the suprarenal bare stent. There were no clinical, laboratory, or imaging findings suggestive of graft infection, and no endoleak had been detected after the initial EVAR (Figure 1, Video 1).Figure 1Preoperative Computed Tomography FindingsContrast-enhanced CT demonstrated a thoracoabdominal pseudoaneurysm after prior EVAR. Because no endoleak had been identified after EVAR, complete explantation of the prior endograft followed by thoracoabdominal graft replacement was considered excessively invasive. Direct clamping of the prior endograft was also avoided because it was considered likely to deform the stent graft and potentially create a new endoleak. Therefore, thoracoabdominal graft replacement was performed with balloon-assisted distal occlusion for distal control.

The total operative time was 437 minutes, cardiopulmonary bypass time was 248 minutes, distal balloon occlusion time was 60 minutes, and estimated blood loss was 2,400 mL. The patient stayed in the intensive care unit for 5 days and was transferred for rehabilitation on postoperative day 20, without operative mortality or major morbidity (neurological deficits or lower limb edema).

At the 12-month follow-up, contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated no anastomotic pseudoaneurysm, no endoleak, and preserved graft configuration. The patient remained free from reintervention and had no lower limb or neurologic sequelae.

Potential Pitfalls

Balloon migration can compromise occlusion and cause hemodynamic instability. Incomplete balloon expansion can result in back-bleeding. Direct aortic clamping over the stent graft risks graft deformation and aortic wall injury. Prolonged distal occlusion without adequate distal perfusion may lead to spinal cord or visceral ischemia.

Tips for Avoiding Pitfalls

Fluoroscopic monitoring is essential to ensure stable balloon positioning and prevent overinflation. Distal perfusion through the side branches mitigates ischemia. Maintaining hybrid readiness facilitates immediate deployment of additional endovascular devices for bleeding control if needed.

Bleeding occurred from the distal anastomotic interface rather than from balloon-related injury or balloon seal failure. We interpreted this as resulting from fragility and structural complexity at the junction between the prior endograft-bearing aorta and the surgical graft. Prompt intraluminal cuff deployment served as an effective hybrid bailout.

Study Limitations

First, it describes a single case and therefore does not allow any conclusions regarding superiority over other distal control strategies. Second, the effectiveness of distal perfusion was assessed primarily on intraoperative findings and postoperative clinical course rather than a predefined protocol of perfusion monitoring. Third, the need for adjunctive cuff deployment indicates that complex anastomotic issues may still occur despite balloon-assisted control. Nevertheless, the case illustrates a feasible technical option in a setting in which conventional distal clamping may be problematic.

Conclusions

Balloon-assisted distal occlusion may be a feasible adjunctive technique for thoracoabdominal graft replacement after EVAR when conventional clamping is difficult. Further experience is needed to determine its reproducibility, safety profile, and broader applicability.
