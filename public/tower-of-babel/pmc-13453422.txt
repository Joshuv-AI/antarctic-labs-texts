Solvent-Free One-Pot Preparation of Lignin-Ferric Nanoparticles for Enhanced Antibacterial Therapy
Authors: Xiuliang Dai, Ruitong Liu, Yujie Zhou, Tingting Fan, Cheng Wang, Hongbin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global rise of antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections has intensified the need for scalable and biocompatible antimicrobial nanomaterials. Lignin is an abundant renewable biopolymer with intrinsic antimicrobial activity, but its limited aqueous processability and modest antibacterial efficacy restrict its direct application.MethodsLignin–ferric nanoparticles (LS-Fe) were prepared through an aqueous one-pot process using sodium lignosulfonate (SLS) as the lignin precursor and Fe3⁺ as the coordination component, without the use of organic solvents. The nanoparticles were characterized for size, dispersity, surface charge, morphology, Fe incorporation, and batch-to-batch reproducibility. Their antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli, effects on bacterial growth, biofilm biomass, membrane integrity, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), and lipid peroxidation were evaluated. Fe/ROS-modulation experiments were performed using deferoxamine, thiourea, catalase, and H2O2. Exploratory in vivo efficacy and safety were assessed in a small mouse model of S. aureus-infected wounds.ResultsLS-Fe formed stable colloidal nanoparticles with a hydrodynamic diameter of 126.63 nm, a polydispersity index of 0.19, and a zeta potential of −44.7 mV. In preliminary in vitro assays, LS-Fe showed a descriptive trend toward greater antibacterial activity than pristine SLS against both bacterial strains. LS-Fe treatment was associated with increased intracellular ROS and lipid peroxidation, bacterial membrane damage, inhibition of biofilm formation, and reduced residual biomass of preformed biofilms. The attenuation of LS-Fe-mediated antibacterial activity by deferoxamine, thiourea, and catalase, together with its enhancement by exogenous H2O2, supported a possible Fe-mediated oxidative antibacterial contribution. In the exploratory infected-wound model, topical LS-Fe treatment was associated with faster wound closure, reduced bacterial burden, and improved histological features of wound repair, while no obvious histopathological abnormalities were observed in major organs at the tested dose.ConclusionAqueous one-pot preparation of LS-Fe provides a simple organic-solvent-free strategy for integrating lignin nanoparticle formation with ferric functionalization. The preliminary findings support further investigation of LS-Fe as a sustainable antibacterial nanomaterial for infected-wound management, while larger confirmatory studies and more comprehensive mechanistic and safety evaluations remain necessary.

Introduction

The global rise of antimicrobial resistance, together with increasing concerns over the toxicity and environmental persistence of some conventional antimicrobial agents, has created a pressing need for safe, effective, and sustainable antibacterial materials.1–5 Natural antibacterial substances derived from plants, animals, and microorganisms are attractive candidates because of their renewable origins, structural diversity, and generally favorable biocompatibility.6–8

Nevertheless, many natural antibacterial agents are limited by modest antimicrobial potency, poor aqueous solubility, insufficient stability, and difficulties in scalable production.9–11 These limitations restrict their direct use in high-performance antimicrobial systems. Engineering natural substances into nanostructured or hybrid materials is therefore an important strategy to improve antibacterial efficacy while preserving the sustainability advantages of natural materials.12–19

Lignin, the most abundant renewable aromatic biopolymer on Earth, is a three-dimensional phenylpropanoid polymer rich in phenolic, methoxy, sulfonate, and carboxyl groups. These functional groups can contribute to antioxidant and antimicrobial effects through bacterial membrane perturbation, redox activity, and ROS-related damage.20–22 However, native lignin generally suffers from high structural heterogeneity, limited water dispersibility, and relatively weak standalone antibacterial activity, making further modification necessary for high-performance applications.23,24 Previous studies have shown that converting lignin into nanoparticles can enhance its bioactivity by increasing specific surface area, improving contact with bacterial membranes, and enriching accessible surface functional groups.25–27 Hybridization with functional inorganic or metal components can further improve antimicrobial performance.28–30 These studies indicate that lignin can serve not only as a natural antimicrobial material but also as a renewable platform for constructing antibacterial nanohybrids.

However, conventional lignin nanosizing methods, including antisolvent precipitation, mechanical disintegration (ultrasonication or high-pressure homogenization), and electrospinning, still present important practical limitations.31–34 Antisolvent precipitation is facile but commonly involves large solvent volumes, multistep processing, particle-size heterogeneity, and solvent-recovery costs. Mechanical disintegration requires high energy input and specialized equipment, while harsh processing may alter lignin linkages or surface functionalities. Electrospinning generally has low throughput and often requires carrier polymers. These factors hinder scalable and environmentally benign preparation. Therefore, a one-pot, solvent-minimized or solvent-free route capable of inducing lignin self-assembly and functionalization within the same process would be highly desirable.

In this study, we designed a green one-pot strategy to prepare lignin-ferric nanoparticles (LS-Fe) using sodium lignosulfonate (SLS) as the lignin precursor and Fe3⁺ as the coordination component. We hypothesized that Fe3⁺ coordination would drive nanoparticle formation while providing redox-active sites capable of amplifying oxidative antibacterial stress through a Fenton-like process. This design was intended to address two major limitations of lignin-based antimicrobial systems: the solvent dependence and scalability constraints of conventional nanosizing methods, and the relatively weak antibacterial activity of pristine lignin. The resulting LS-Fe nanoparticles were characterized and preliminarily evaluated for antibacterial activity, biofilm regulation, oxidative stress induction, and exploratory wound-healing performance in an S. aureus-infected wound model.

Previous lignin-based antibacterial hybrids have incorporated ZnO, silver, or tellurium to enhance antimicrobial performance.28–30 These studies establish the feasibility of inorganic-component-assisted lignin antibacterial materials. However, representative systems have involved hydrothermal preparation followed by polymer grafting and composite-film fabrication, solvent-exchange preparation of lignin nanoparticles before silver incorporation, or sonochemical synthesis of tellurium–lignin nanoparticles. In contrast, the present LS-Fe formulation integrates particle formation and ferric functionalization within a simple aqueous one-pot process using SLS itself as the coordinating lignin precursor. Specifically, this study tested whether Fe3⁺ incorporation could be achieved within a solvent-free SLS-based one-pot process, whether Fe-associated LS-Fe particles would show greater antibacterial and antibiofilm activity than pristine SLS, and whether the antibacterial activity would be accompanied by Fe/ROS-related oxidative stress. Because the biological experiments were designed as exploratory proof-of-concept studies, the findings were interpreted cautiously and are intended to guide subsequent confirmatory work with larger sample sizes and expanded mechanistic validation.

Experimental Section

Materials

Sodium lignosulfonate (SLS, 95%, approximately 30,000 Da) was purchased from Tianjin Zhonglian Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). Ferric chloride was obtained from Shanghai Easen Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Unless otherwise stated, all other reagents were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of LS-Fe

A 500 μL aliquot of ferric chloride solution (10 mg/mL) was rapidly injected into 5 mL of SLS solution (10 mg/mL), followed by vortexing at 1500 rpm for 5 min. Subsequently, 20 μL of ammonia solution (NH3·H2O, 25–28%) was added to the mixture. The ammonia solution was used to mildly adjust alkalinity, promote deprotonation of SLS functional groups, and facilitate Fe-associated coordination/aggregation during nanoparticle formation. The resulting suspension was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min and then allowed to stand at room temperature for 2 h. After removal of the supernatant, the precipitate was resuspended in 1 mL of ultrapure water by pipetting. Finally, the suspension was subjected to ultrasonic dispersion in an ice bath (60% power, 3 s on/3 s off cycles) to obtain lignin-ferric nanoparticles (LS-Fe).

Hydrodynamic Size and Zeta Potential Measurements of LS-Fe

The LS-Fe dispersion was diluted 100-fold with ultrapure water. A 1 mL aliquot of the diluted suspension was then collected for hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential measurements using a Malvern Zetasizer (Malvern Panalytical, Worcestershire, UK).

Transmission Electron Microscopy and Physicochemical Characterization of LS-Fe

The morphology of LS-Fe was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) operated at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV. For sample preparation, 20 μL of the LS-Fe dispersion was deposited onto a carbon-coated copper grid and allowed to stand for 1 min to permit particle adsorption. The grid was then dried overnight at room temperature under ambient conditions before TEM observation (HT7800, Hitachi, Japan).

To determine Fe incorporation and formulation reproducibility, three independently prepared LS-Fe batches were analyzed for nanoparticle yield, Fe content, Fe loading efficiency, hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential. Fe content was quantified by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, 5110, Agilent, USA) after acid digestion, and Fe loading efficiency was calculated as the mass of Fe retained in purified LS-Fe divided by the initial Fe input. Nanoparticle yield was calculated as the dry mass of purified LS-Fe divided by the total initial input of SLS and ferric chloride.

To obtain spectroscopic evidence of Fe–SLS interactions, dried SLS and LS-Fe samples were analyzed by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet iS 10, Thermo Fisher, USA) over the wavenumber range of 500–4000 cm−1. Changes in the positions and relative intensities of bands associated with hydroxyl groups, aromatic skeletal vibrations, C–O groups, sulfonate groups, and low-wavenumber Fe–O-associated vibrations were evaluated.

Bacterial Culture

A single colony of S. aureus or E. coli was picked from agar plates and transferred into test tubes containing 10 mL of culture medium. The tubes were incubated in a temperature-controlled shaker at 37 °C and 250 rpm for 12 h. To obtain bacteria in an active growth state with good viability, the resulting cultures were appropriately diluted and subcultured twice under the same conditions. The bacterial suspensions prepared through this procedure were used for subsequent experiments.

Antibacterial Assays of LS-Fe and SLS

The antibacterial activities of LS-Fe and sodium lignosulfonate (SLS) against S. aureus and E. coli were evaluated using a colony-counting assay. Mid-logarithmic-phase bacterial suspensions were adjusted to 1×108 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL by gradient dilution. The S. aureus suspension was incubated with LS-Fe or SLS at final concentrations of 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 mg/mL, whereas the E. coli suspension was incubated with LS-Fe or SLS at final concentrations of 1, 2, 4, and 8 mg/mL. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was used as the control for both strains. The concentration ranges were selected on the basis of preliminary screening, which indicated lower susceptibility of E. coli than S. aureus under the present assay conditions. Accordingly, a concentration range of 0.5–4 mg/mL was used for S. aureus, whereas 1–8 mg/mL was used for E. coli. For each treatment system, 100 μL of bacterial suspension was mixed with 300 μL of the LS-Fe or SLS solution and incubated at 37 °C and 250 rpm for 1 h. After incubation, the samples were serially diluted 2×104-fold with sterile PBS, and 100 μL of each diluted sample was uniformly spread onto tryptic soy agar (TSA) plates. The plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C, after which colonies were counted manually and photographed. Relative bacterial viability was calculated by normalizing the colony number in each treatment group to that of the PBS control, which was defined as 100%. The assay was performed as an exploratory experiment with three experimental repetitions where applicable.35 The resulting data were summarized descriptively and plotted using Origin software (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). No formal inferential statistical analysis was performed for groups with fewer than five independent observations.

Bacterial Growth-Curve Assay

The growth-inhibitory effects of LS-Fe and SLS were further assessed by monitoring optical density at 600 nm (OD600) using a microplate reader (Synergy NEO, BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). In each well of a 96-well plate, 100 μL of bacterial suspension (105 CFU/mL) was mixed with 100 μL of LS-Fe or SLS solution. The final concentrations were 4 mg/mL for S. aureus and 8 mg/mL for E. coli. PBS was used as the control. The plate was incubated at 37 °C and 250 rpm for 10 h, and OD600 values were recorded hourly to monitor bacterial growth.35,36 Growth-curve data were summarized descriptively, and no formal inferential statistical analysis was performed for groups with fewer than five independent observations.

Detection of Intracellular ROS in Bacteria Treated with LS-Fe and SLS

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were measured using a ROS detection kit containing the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe (KGA7308-100, KeyGEN BioTECH, China). After entering cells, DCFH-DA is deacetylated to nonfluorescent DCFH, which can be oxidized by ROS to fluorescent 2ʹ,7ʹ-dichlorofluorescein (DCF); therefore, DCF fluorescence intensity reflects intracellular ROS levels. Bacterial suspensions (S. aureus or E. coli, 1×108 CFU/mL) were first incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min to allow probe loading. The bacteria were then washed three times with sterile ultrapure water to remove unbound probe and reduce background interference. Subsequently, the bacteria were incubated with PBS, LS-Fe, or SLS for 20 min. The final concentrations were 4 mg/mL for S. aureus and 8 mg/mL for E. coli, consistent with the antibacterial assays. DCF fluorescence was quantified using a microplate reader at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm. The ROS assay was conducted as an exploratory experiment with three experimental repetitions where applicable, and the data were summarized descriptively without formal inferential statistical testing.37

To further examine Fe/ROS involvement in LS-Fe-mediated antibacterial activity, S. aureus was treated with LS-Fe in the presence of deferoxamine mesylate (DFO, 0.5 mM), thiourea (100 mM), catalase (100 U/mL), or H2O2 (100 μM). The final LS-Fe concentration was 4 mg/mL, consistent with the S. aureus antibacterial assays. After 1 h incubation at 37°C, bacterial survival was evaluated by colony counting as described above. The resulting CFU survival data were used to assess whether LS-Fe-mediated antibacterial activity was attenuated by Fe chelation, hydroxyl-radical scavenging, or H2O2 removal and whether it was enhanced by exogenous H2O2. In parallel, intracellular ROS levels under the same Fe/ROS-modulation conditions were measured using DCFH-DA as described above (Figure S3).

Detection of Intracellular MDA in Bacteria Treated with LS-Fe and SLS

Malondialdehyde (MDA), a representative biomarker of lipid peroxidation (LPO), was measured to evaluate oxidative membrane damage in bacteria. An MDA standard curve was first established using an MDA assay kit (KeyGEN BioTECH, China). Briefly, a 100 μM MDA stock solution was prepared with ultrapure water and serially diluted to generate standard solutions with final concentrations of 1, 2.5, 10, 25, 50, and 100 μM. For each standard, 100 μL was transferred into a centrifuge tube, mixed thoroughly with 200 μL of MDA detection working solution, and incubated in a 100 °C water bath for 15 min to allow the chromogenic reaction. The reaction was terminated by immediately placing the samples on ice for 10 min. After the samples returned to room temperature, they were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant (200 μL) was then transferred to a 96-well plate, and absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a microplate reader.

For the experimental groups, 1 mL aliquots of S. aureus and E. coli suspensions (1×109 CFU/mL) were separately incubated with LS-Fe or SLS. The final concentrations were 4 mg/mL for S. aureus and 8 mg/mL for E. coli, and incubation was performed at 37 °C and 250 rpm for 30 min. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C to collect the bacterial pellets, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellets were resuspended in lysozyme-containing lysis buffer, vortexed thoroughly, and incubated on ice for 10 min to achieve bacterial lysis. Next, 200 μL of MDA detection working solution was added to the bacterial lysate, and the mixture was heated at 100 °C for 15 min for color development. The samples were then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Finally, 100 μL of the resulting supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate, and absorbance at 532 nm was measured using a microplate reader. MDA concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve.37 Because the number of independent observations was below five per group where applicable, MDA data were summarized descriptively without formal inferential statistical testing.

Bacterial Live/Dead Staining Assay

The viability of S. aureus and E. coli after treatment with LS-Fe or SLS was evaluated using a Live/Dead staining assay. Mid-logarithmic-phase bacterial suspensions (1×108 CFU/mL) were incubated with LS-Fe or SLS at final concentrations of 4 mg/mL for S. aureus and 8 mg/mL for E. coli. Incubation was performed at 37 °C and 250 rpm for 2 h. After treatment, the bacterial suspensions were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was carefully removed to retain the bacterial pellets.

The bacterial pellets were stained with 20 μL of Live/Dead Bacterial Viability Kit working solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) and vortexed thoroughly to ensure homogeneous contact between the staining reagent and bacteria. Staining was performed for 20 min under light-protected conditions. Finally, 10 μL of the stained bacterial suspension was placed onto a clean glass slide, covered with a coverslip while avoiding air bubbles, and observed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM; Ti2, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Bacterial viability was evaluated according to the fluorescence signals captured by CLSM imaging.38 For quantitative image analysis, three representative regions of interest per group were analyzed using ImageJ. The integrated fluorescence densities of the red and green channels were measured under identical threshold settings. The red/green fluorescence ratio was calculated when the green signal was detectable, and the dead fluorescence fraction was calculated as red fluorescence intensity divided by the sum of red and green fluorescence intensities. Because the green fluorescence signal was nearly absent in LS-Fe-treated regions, the dead fluorescence fraction was used as the primary quantitative descriptor for these groups.

SEM Observation of Bacterial Micromorphology

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Regulus-8100, Hitachi, Japan) was used to observe micromorphological changes in S. aureus and E. coli before and after treatment with LS-Fe or SLS. Briefly, 2 mL aliquots of overnight-cultured, mid-logarithmic-phase S. aureus or E. coli suspensions (1×108 CFU/mL) were collected into centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully removed, and the bacterial pellets were resuspended in 3 mL of PBS, LS-Fe, or SLS solution. The final LS-Fe/SLS concentrations were 4 mg/mL for S. aureus and 8 mg/mL for E. coli, consistent with the previous assays. After thorough mixing, the samples were incubated at 37 °C and 250 rpm for 30 min.

After incubation, the bacteria were washed three times with sterile PBS to remove residual LS-Fe or SLS and then centrifuged again at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the bacterial pellets were fixed with 200 μL of 4% glutaraldehyde solution (v/v, prepared in PBS) at room temperature for 1 h. After fixation, the bacteria were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the fixative was removed. Sequential dehydration was performed using ethanol solutions of increasing concentrations (70%, 90%, and 100%, v/v), with each dehydration step lasting 10 min. After the final dehydration, the samples were freeze-dried to remove residual moisture. Finally, 10 μL of the reconstituted bacterial suspension was dropped onto a clean silicon wafer placed in a 6-well plate and dried overnight at room temperature. The dried samples were observed by SEM to analyze bacterial micromorphology.

Biofilm Formation Inhibition and Short-Exposure Preformed-Biofilm Biomass Reduction Assays

To evaluate inhibition of biofilm formation by LS-Fe and SLS, 50 μL aliquots of mid-logarithmic-phase S. aureus or E. coli suspension (1×108 CFU/mL) were added to each well of a 96-well plate and mixed with 150 μL of LS-Fe or SLS solution. The final concentrations were 4 mg/mL for S. aureus and 8 mg/mL for E. coli. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 48 h to allow biofilm formation. Subsequently, 100 μL of crystal violet solution was added to each well and incubated for 10 min to stain the biofilm. The wells were rinsed 2–3 times with PBS to remove unbound dye. Then, 200 μL of 80% ethanol (v/v) was added to dissolve the bound crystal violet, and the plate was shaken at 37 °C and 250 rpm for 2 h to ensure complete dye extraction. Absorbance at 600 nm was measured using a microplate reader to quantify biofilm biomass.

To assess short-exposure reduction of residual biomass in preformed biofilms, equal volumes of sterile medium (100 μL) and mid-logarithmic-phase bacterial suspension (100 μL, 1×108 CFU/mL) were added to each well of a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h to establish stable biofilms. LS-Fe or SLS solution (100 μL) was then added to each well to reach final concentrations of 4 mg/mL for S. aureus and 8 mg/mL for E. coli. After incubation at 37 °C for 1 h as a rapid topical-contact model, the supernatant was removed, and the wells were rinsed 2–3 times with PBS to eliminate residual LS-Fe or SLS. Crystal violet staining and absorbance measurement were then performed as described above. Biofilm assay data were summarized descriptively, and no formal inferential statistical analysis was performed for groups with fewer than five independent observations.39

Experimental Animals

Male BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old; body weight, 19–24 g) were used in this study. All mice were purchased from and housed at Cavens Co., Ltd. (Changzhou, China) under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA), applicable institutional animal-care regulations, and relevant veterinary euthanasia guidelines, including the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals where applicable. The animal study protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Changzhou University (ethics approval No. 2022030122).

Establishment of a Mouse Model of S. aureus-Infected Wounds

A mouse model of S. aureus-infected wounds was established to evaluate the in vivo antibacterial and wound-healing effects of LS-Fe and SLS. Mice were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane according to the approved animal protocol, and dorsal hair was removed using depilatory cream to expose a smooth skin surface. Surgical instruments, including punchers, scissors, and forceps, were sterilized by wiping with 75% ethanol followed by ultraviolet irradiation. A full-thickness oval wound (major axis, 1.0 cm; minor axis, 0.8 cm) was created on the dorsal midline using a sterile puncher. Excess marginal skin was excised, and residual blood on the wound bed was absorbed with sterile cotton swabs.

Subsequently, 50 μL of mid-logarithmic-phase S. aureus suspension (1×108 CFU/mL) was uniformly applied to the wound. After air drying for 5 min to allow bacterial adhesion, the inoculation step was repeated three times to ensure sufficient bacterial loading. The inoculation procedure was performed for two consecutive days. Successful establishment of local infection was indicated by redness, swelling, and exudation.

Treatment of S. aureus-Infected Mouse Wounds with LS-Fe and SLS

After infection was established, mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 3 per group): PBS control, LS-Fe treatment, and SLS treatment. The animal experiment was designed as an exploratory proof-of-concept study to obtain preliminary evidence of topical antibacterial and wound-healing activity while minimizing animal use in accordance with the reduction principle of animal research. Topical administration was performed by applying 100 μL of sterile PBS, 100 μL of LS-Fe solution (4 mg/mL), or 100 μL of SLS solution (4 mg/mL) to the wound once daily for 5 consecutive days. During the 11-day observation period, wound morphology was photographed daily, and body weight was recorded to assess general systemic status. Wound area was measured using ImageJ software (v1.8.0), and the relative wound area was calculated as follows: relative wound area (%) = (wound area on day n/mean wound area on day 0) × 100%. Because the group size was below five independent observations per group, the in vivo data were summarized descriptively and were not subjected to formal inferential statistical testing.

Plate-Coating Assay for Wound Skin Tissues

To descriptively evaluate in vivo bacterial burden, mice were euthanized on day 11 by deep inhalation anesthesia with isoflurane overdose followed by cervical dislocation as a secondary physical method to confirm death, in accordance with the approved animal protocol and applicable veterinary guidelines. Wound-centered skin tissues, including the wound bed and 0.5 cm of surrounding normal skin, were then harvested. Each tissue sample was immersed in 1 mL PBS and incubated at 37 °C and 250 rpm for 1 h to elute bacteria. The eluate was serially diluted 10-fold, and 100 μL aliquots of the 10−3 and 10−4 dilutions were spread onto TSA plates. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 12 h and photographed.

Pathological Staining of Wound Skin Tissues

For histological analysis, wound tissues collected on day 11 were fixed in pre-labeled tubes containing 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h. Fixed samples were subjected to gradient dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning into 5 μm slices. Three staining procedures were performed: hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to assess inflammation and re-epithelialization, Masson staining to evaluate collagen deposition, and CD31 immunohistochemistry to examine angiogenesis. Stained sections were scanned using a digital slide scanner (Pannoramic 250), and images were saved for subsequent descriptive histological assessment.

Visceral Safety Evaluation

To evaluate in vivo biocompatibility, major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were collected from mice euthanized on day 11 using the same method as mentioned above. The organs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, processed into 5 μm sections, and stained with H&E. Sections were scanned, and histological integrity, including inflammatory infiltration, necrosis, and structural abnormalities, was evaluated by a pathologist in a blinded manner.

Descriptive Data Presentation

Several experimental groups in this exploratory proof-of-concept study included fewer than five independent observations. Therefore, in accordance with the journal’s statistical guidance for studies with very small group sizes, no formal inferential statistical analyses were performed. Data are presented descriptively as mean ± standard deviation (SD) where applicable. No p values, statistical significance thresholds, or post hoc comparisons were reported. GraphPad Prism 9 and Origin software were used only for data organization, descriptive summaries, and graph preparation. The findings should be interpreted as preliminary observations requiring confirmation in future studies with larger independent sample sizes.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of LS-Fe

LS-Fe was prepared through a one-pot synthetic route, yielding a reddish-brown colloidal suspension. Upon irradiation with a laser pointer, the LS-Fe suspension exhibited the Tyndall effect, confirming the formation of a colloidal dispersion (Figure 1a). The physicochemical properties of LS-Fe were then systematically characterized by dynamic light scattering (DLS), zeta potential analysis, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). DLS analysis showed that LS-Fe had an average hydrodynamic diameter of 126.63 nm and a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.19 (Figure 1b), indicating a relatively narrow size distribution and good dispersibility. The zeta potential was −44.70 mV (Figure 1c), suggesting strong electrostatic repulsion and favorable colloidal stability in aqueous dispersion. TEM images further revealed that LS-Fe mainly consisted of well-dispersed nanoscale particles with predominantly spherical morphology, although some particles displayed irregular polygonal features (Figure 1d). Collectively, these characterization results confirm the successful preparation of LS-Fe with suitable structural and colloidal properties for subsequent antibacterial evaluation. LS-Fe showed a nanoparticle yield of 67.0 ± 1.4%, Fe content of 3.98 ± 0.09 wt%, and Fe loading efficiency of 85.2 ± 3.6%. The hydrodynamic diameter, PDI, and zeta potential remained within narrow ranges among batches, suggesting acceptable batch-to-batch reproducibility (Table S1). FTIR analysis showed that the principal absorption band in the 1600–1630 cm−1 region shifted from approximately 1632 cm−1 in SLS to 1603 cm−1 in LS-Fe. The band near 1115 cm−1 shifted to approximately 1122 cm−1, accompanied by pronounced absorption features at approximately 966 and 621 cm−1 in LS-Fe. These changes in the oxygen- and sulfonate-associated fingerprint regions provide preliminary evidence of Fe–SLS interactions during nanoparticle formation (Figure S1).

In vitro Antibacterial Activity of LS-Fe

The in vitro antibacterial activities of LS-Fe and SLS against S. aureus and E. coli were evaluated using a solid agar plate colony-counting assay, and bacterial viability was quantified by colony enumeration.

For S. aureus, descriptive differences in antibacterial activity were observed between LS-Fe and SLS in this preliminary dataset (Figure 2a–d). At a concentration of 4 mg/mL, SLS treatment reduced S. aureus viability to 59%, whereas LS-Fe at the same concentration reduced viability to 15%. These observations suggest that ferric coordination may enhance the antibacterial performance of SLS-derived lignin nanoparticles.

Effects of LS-Fe and SLS on Bacterial Growth Curves

As shown in Figure 3a and b, both SLS and LS-Fe showed measurable growth-inhibitory activity against S. aureus and E. coli in this preliminary dataset. LS-Fe-treated groups showed lower OD600 values than the SLS-treated groups throughout the 10 h monitoring period. Together with the colony-counting observations in Figure 2, these growth-curve data provide preliminary support for the antibacterial activity of LS-Fe and its potential for controlling bacterial proliferation. Biofilm Formation Inhibition and Short-Exposure Reduction of Preformed-Biofilm Biomass by LS-Fe and SLS

Bacterial biofilms are structured, surface-attached communities of cells embedded in a self-produced extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix. In the absence of intervention, planktonic bacteria can adhere, proliferate, and develop into mature biofilms, which display high tolerance to conventional antibacterial treatments.40,41 Crystal violet, a cationic dye, stains bacterial cells and EPS components within biofilms, thereby enabling indirect quantification of total biofilm biomass.

To evaluate the biofilm-modulating capacity of LS-Fe and SLS, both biofilm-formation inhibition and short-exposure preformed-biofilm biomass reduction assays were performed against the two bacterial strains.

As shown in Figure 3c and d, LS-Fe treatment showed the lowest biofilm biomass among the tested groups in this preliminary dataset. The absorbance values, which reflect biofilm biomass, were lower in the LS-Fe group than in the PBS control and SLS groups, suggesting that LS-Fe may suppress biofilm initiation and maturation. Consistently, Figure 3e and f show that LS-Fe-treated groups also displayed lower absorbance values in the short-exposure preformed-biofilm biomass reduction assay compared with the other groups.

These preliminary findings suggest that LS-Fe may have dual biofilm-modulating activity, including inhibition of biofilm formation and rapid reduction of residual biomass in preformed biofilms. This observation is consistent with the colony-counting and growth-curve results and provides preliminary support for further investigation of LS-Fe in biofilm-associated bacterial infections. Because crystal violet staining measures total attached biomass, including bacterial cells and extracellular polymeric substances, these data should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of reduced residual biofilm biomass rather than definitive proof of complete mature-biofilm eradication.

Live/Dead Staining Assay of LS-Fe and SLS

To directly visualize bacterial viability after treatment, a dual-fluorescence live/dead staining assay using SYTO 9 and propidium iodide (PI) was performed. SYTO 9 can penetrate bacteria with intact membranes and produces green fluorescence, whereas PI mainly enters bacteria with compromised membranes and produces red fluorescence.42 This staining strategy enables simultaneous visualization of live and dead bacterial populations.

Representative fluorescence images of S. aureus and E. coli are shown in Figure 4c and d, respectively. Consistent with the colony-counting, growth-curve, and SEM observations, LS-Fe-treated samples showed more red fluorescence and less green fluorescence than PBS- and SLS-treated samples in this preliminary assessment. These observations suggest membrane damage and bacterial death at the single-cell level. ImageJ-based fluorescence quantification further supported these observations (Figure S2). In S. aureus, the dead fluorescence fraction increased from almost zero in the control group to 11.16% after SLS treatment and reached almost 100.00% after LS-Fe treatment. In E. coli, the corresponding values were about 0.37%, 6.91%, and 100.00% in the control, SLS, and LS-Fe groups, respectively. Because the green fluorescence signal was nearly absent in LS-Fe-treated regions, the red/green fluorescence ratio was not calculated for these groups. Therefore, the dead fluorescence fraction was used as the primary quantitative descriptor of membrane-compromised bacterial populations.

Intracellular ROS and MDA Levels in Bacteria

The Fenton reaction is an iron-mediated process in which Fe2⁺ catalyzes the decomposition of H2O2 to generate highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (•OH). In Fe-containing antibacterial nanosystems, Fe3⁺ can be reduced to Fe2⁺ by endogenous reducing species, and the resulting Fe2⁺ may react with local H2O2 to generate ROS near bacterial cells. Such oxidative stress can damage lipids, proteins, DNA, and membranes, thereby contributing to bacterial inactivation.43,44

To examine whether oxidative stress contributed to LS-Fe-mediated antibacterial activity, intracellular ROS levels were measured using the DCFH-DA probe, which is oxidized to fluorescent DCF in the presence of ROS.45 As shown in Figure 5a and b, LS-Fe treatment increased intracellular ROS levels in both S. aureus and E. coli compared with the PBS and SLS groups. Fe/ROS-modulation controls further supported this interpretation in S. aureus (Figure S3). In this assay, CFU survival in the PBS control was defined as 100.00%, whereas the SLS and LS-Fe groups showed survival values of 59.31% and 16.67%, respectively. Conclusion

This exploratory study developed a facile, solvent-free one-pot strategy for preparing lignin-ferric nanoparticles (LS-Fe) from sodium lignosulfonate (SLS) and ferric ions. This approach may help address key limitations of conventional lignin nanosizing methods, including solvent dependence, multistep processing, and scalability constraints. Physicochemical characterization supported the formation of nanoscale LS-Fe colloidal particles with a narrow size distribution, favorable aqueous stability, and appropriate morphology for preliminary antibacterial evaluation.

Compared with pristine SLS, LS-Fe showed a descriptive trend toward greater antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive S. aureus and Gram-negative E. coli in preliminary in vitro experiments. Descriptive observations suggested that LS-Fe inhibited bacterial growth, suppressed biofilm formation, reduced residual biomass in preformed biofilms, and induced bacterial membrane damage. Exploratory mechanistic assays further indicated that LS-Fe treatment was associated with increased intracellular ROS and MDA levels, suggesting that oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation may contribute to its antibacterial activity, consistent with a possible Fe-mediated Fenton-like contribution. In a small exploratory in vivo model, topical LS-Fe treatment was associated with reduced bacterial burden, faster wound closure, reduced inflammation, and histological features consistent with re-epithelialization, collagen deposition, and angiogenesis. No obvious histological abnormalities were observed in major organs at the tested dose.

The main contribution of this work lies in integrating green synthesis and antibacterial functionalization within a single lignin-based nanosystem. The solvent-free one-pot protocol simplifies preparation and may facilitate scale-up, while Fe3⁺ coordination may improve antibacterial performance without introducing highly toxic synthetic additives. The combination of preliminary in vitro antibacterial activity, biofilm regulation, and exploratory in vivo wound-healing observations suggests that LS-Fe warrants further investigation for infected-wound management.

Several limitations should be addressed in future studies. The main limitation is the small number of independent observations in several experimental groups. Because some groups included fewer than five independent observations, no formal inferential statistical analysis was performed, and the results should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. First, the antibacterial spectrum was evaluated only against S. aureus and E. coli; additional clinically relevant pathogens, such as methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, should be investigated. Second, larger confirmatory studies with appropriate sample-size planning are required to validate the antibacterial and wound-healing observations. Third, longer-term biosafety, pharmacokinetic behavior, local retention, degradation, and iron-release profiles require systematic evaluation. Fourth, the Fe3⁺ loading ratio and reaction parameters should be optimized to further improve antibacterial activity, stability, and formulation reproducibility. The current crystal-violet biofilm assay primarily reflects total residual biomass; future studies should include longer exposure times, additional concentrations, and viability-based mature-biofilm assays to define antibiofilm activity more comprehensively. Finally, the 11-day observation period permitted only an early exploratory assessment of wound closure and histological repair. It was insufficient to evaluate complete healing, late tissue remodeling, or scar formation; future studies should therefore extend the follow-up period to 21–28 days.

In summary, solvent-free one-pot synthesized LS-Fe represents a potentially sustainable lignin-based antibacterial nanoplatform. The present preliminary findings suggest that this strategy may expand the utility of natural antimicrobial nanomaterials and justify further confirmatory investigation for infected-wound management and other antibacterial applications.
