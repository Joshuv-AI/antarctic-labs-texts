Recognisability for generalised hierarchical patterns and pattern spaces of finite local complexity
James J. Walton

Abstract
. We explore an extended notion of a tiling of translational finite local complexity (FLC) being substitutional, or hierarchical, to Euclidean ‘patterns’ and define, more generally, a similar notion for spaces of patterns. We prove recognisability results for these, relating injectivity of substitution to aperiodicity, with no minimality requirements. More precisely, for a suitable power of the substitution map, we determine the size of fibre over any pattern to be the index of its inflated (translational) periods in its whole group of periods. This answers an open question of Cortez and Solomyak, on whether non-periodic tilings necessarily have unique pre-images under substitution: they do, even for a wider notion of pattern and being substitutional; conversely, for an appropriate power of the substitution, discretely periodic points necessarily have multiple pre-images. Our results cover examples of FLC pattern spaces, such as of uniformly discrete but non-relatively dense point sets, that contain elements with non-discrete groups of periods.

Introduction

Many aperiodically ordered patterns exhibit a form of repeating hierarchy. Just as self-similar fractals have recurring features when zooming in, hierarchical patterns can have recurring structures when zooming out, so are like ‘upside down fractals’; this goes further than analogy, see for instance
[

,

]

]

[

,

]

[

Recognisability results equate a form of injectivity of substitution to aperiodicity. In Symbolic Dynamics, Mossé’s Theorem
[

In this context, recognisability is also called ‘unique composition’, since it is equivalent to there being a unique supertiling — the tiling that decomposes to the original — being unique and, moreover, defined by a local rule grouping tiles into supertiles. This has been extended to a reasonably wide class of non-primitive (non-repetitive) admissible stone inflations
[
CS11

Let

-sub pattern space. There exists

In

They note the following open question: more generally, is it still true that non-periodic elements of a tiling space generated by an FLC stone inflation rule necessarily have unique pre-images under substitution? Since such systems are covered by Theorem
4.8
, we have shown that this indeed holds, even in the wider framework of
L
-sub pattern spaces. Moreover, we have a converse: discretely periodic patterns necessarily have multiple pre-images, at least under some power of the substitution. This power (described in the full statement of Theorem

By

is disconnected (see Definition

This indicates a further sense in which our results go beyond tilings, to patterns that even may lack ‘return discreteness’ (see Definition

-sub, in terms of the local indistinguishability
\cP\LI\cQ

Corollary
4.10
.

Let
\cP
be an FLC and return discrete pattern in the Euclidean space
E
and
L\colon E\to E
a linear expansion. Suppose that
\cP
is
L
-sub, that is, there is a pattern
\cP^{\prime}
for which
\cP^{\prime}\LI\cP
and
L\cP^{\prime}\LD\cP
. Then
\cP^{\prime}\LIs\cP
. We have that
\cP\LD L\cP^{\prime}
, equivalently
\sigma\colon\Omega_{\cP}\to\Omega_{\cP}
is injective, if and only if
\cP
is aperiodic, that is,

contains no periodic elements.

The above definition of
L
-sub gives a conveniently versatile sense in which a pattern
\cP
is substitutional: when it permits some ‘superpattern’
L\cP^{\prime}
. In more detail, we need a so-called predecessor pattern
\cP^{\prime}\LI\cP
, read
\cP^{\prime}
is locally indistinguishable from
\cP
. This means that
\cP^{\prime}
contains only patches, modulo translation, that are already in
\cP
; this is equivalent to
\cP^{\prime}\in\Omega_{\cP}
, the translational hull of
\cP
(Definition
6.4
) which, as usual, can be considered as an orbit closure
\Omega_{\cP}=\overline{\cP+E}
(see Lemma

). We then need the inflation

of this predecessor to satisfy
L\cP^{\prime}\LD\cP
, read
\cP
is locally derivable from
L\cP^{\prime}
. This is an analogue, in the geometric setting, of having a sliding block code from
L\cP^{\prime}
to
\cP
— one may imagine a subdivision rule on inflated tiles, for instance. Compare this idea to our description of the hierarchical nature of the Penrose tilings at the start of this introduction. For Penrose tilings, if
\cP^{\prime}\LI\cP
we automatically have
\cP^{\prime}\LIs\cP
, that is,
\cP\LI\cP^{\prime}
too, by repetitivity (see Proposition
6.19
). Under reasonable conditions, this always holds for predecessors of
L
-sub patterns, it need not be assumed even in the non-repetitive case (Proposition
3.4
).

This definition has several advantages. Firstly, it has a straightforward definition in terms of the fundamental notions of local indistinguishability and derivability
[
BG13
,
BSJ91
]
. This makes it easier to reason about than, for example, being ‘generated from an inflate, subdivide rule’ (which, to the author’s knowledge, is not even systematically laid out in full generality for non-stone tile substitution rules). And, indeed, its definition was developed in the collaboration
[
HKW25
]
, on classifying when cut and project sets with Euclidean internal space are substitutional. It is quickly seen to always hold for standard cases of patterns generated by ‘inflate, replace’ rules, but it is also very closely related. In the case of repetitive tilings, being
L
-sub corresponds to
\cP
being in a hull of tilings defined by iterating an ‘inflate by
L
, replace’ rule, up to perhaps adding locally derivable labels to tiles (the ‘replace’ rule can quickly be determined by how the supertiles of
L\cP^{\prime}
lie over the patches of
\cP
, up to a suitable collaring of information). It is also a slight weakening of being pseudo-self affine
[
Sol07
]
, that is,
L\cP\LD\cP
, although this instead captures the idea of a pattern being the substitution of
itself
, rather than being generated or ‘admitted’ by some substitution.

In this paper, we use a very relaxed notion of being a
pattern
, as simply some function
\cP\colon E\to S
(where
S
has no further structure, here, and is not necessarily finite). Thus, a pattern can be thought of as any ‘colouring’ of the ambient Euclidean space
E
. This easily covers usual examples such as tilings and Delone sets (see Examples
2.2
). Somewhat surprisingly, this notion of pattern is enough to define the machinery needed throughout the paper, a major component being Section
6
, where we define generalised
pattern spaces
, to which we extend some classical constructions and results from the translational dynamics of tiling spaces. We always use the ‘local topology’, whereby patterns are considered ‘close’ if they agree to a large radius about the origin, up to a small translation. Thus, most results here are only relevant to pattern spaces where this is the natural topology, which usually means when we have FLC (but not for translationally non-FLC pinwheel-type examples
[
Rad94
]
). However, one motivation for our detailed treatment of translational pattern spaces (including full proofs for results such as equivalence of FLC and compactness), in addition to being a self-contained reference for these results, is that the techniques and application to recognisability likely can, through appropriate adjustment, be adapted to some pinwheel-type examples, classes of substitutions on compact alphabets
[
DOP18
,
MRW25
]
or maybe
S
-adic systems in the geometric setting
[
ST21
,
GM13
]
.

Special attention is paid to local derivation (LD)
maps
between pattern spaces, analogues of sliding block code factor maps of subshifts, which are key to our definition of
L
-sub. Although similar definitions have appeared before, we provide a more thorough coverage (as necessitated by our new setting), precisely outlining the functional quality of LDs; as usual, from an LD
\cP\LD\cQ
of individual patterns, we get an induced LD map
f\colon\Omega_{\cP}\LDmap\Omega_{\cQ}
of hulls. Dropping return discreteness occasionally adds some new complications, although we are still able to recover results such as the inverse of an LD map also being LD (in the compact Hausdorff case; see Proposition
6.40
).

Applying this to an
L
-sub pattern
\cP
, with hull
\Omega=\Omega_{\cP}
and predecessor
\cP^{\prime}\in\Omega
, a surjective LD map
S\colon\Omega_{L\cP^{\prime}}\LDmap\Omega_{\cP}=\Omega
is induced from
L\cP^{\prime}\LD\cP
. As
\cP^{\prime}\LIs\cP
(Proposition

This is a convenient setup, for example, for tiling spaces generated by non-primitive substitutions, where the space of admitted tilings may fail to be topologically transitive (and thus will not be a hull). Under familiar circumstances — loosely speaking, of pattern spaces
\Omega

Organisation of paper

In Section

Our main recognisability results are stated and proved in Section

In Section

Finally, in Section

u\in U
.

In short,
\cP[x,U]=\cP[y,V]
if and only if
U=V
and the
U
-patches (or ‘patches of shape
U
’) centred at
x
and
y
agree. The square brackets indicate how an equivalence relation has been so introduced. We have that
\cP[x,r]=\cP[y,r]
if and only if the patches of tiles within radius
r
of
x
and
y
agree, up to a translation from
x
to
y
.

Analogously, for
\cP
defined by a Delone (multi)set, we may identify
\cP[x,U]
with the the shape
U
along with the (finite, if
U
is compact) set of labelled points of
\cP-x
restricted to
U
.

\Diamond

Example 2.3
.

An alternative way to represent a tiling (with tiles labelled in
\cL
) as a pattern is to let
\cP[x]=B\notin\cL
if
x
belongs to a tile
boundary
, and
\cP[x]=\ell\in\cL
if
x
belongs to the interior of a tile with label
\ell
. When tiles are connected, this defines an MLD pattern (Definition
2.11
) to the one defined as above (if tiles are not connected, some minor modifications can still ensure this), where
S=\mathcal{L}\cup\{B\}
is now a finite set if
\mathcal{L}
is finite. This representation is preferable when considering rotational aspects of tilings (see Remark
6.10
).

\Diamond

Remark 2.4
.

An alternative, but closely related notion of a ‘discrete structure’ can be found in
[
BG13
, Definition 5.1]
, although our definition is more minimal. Another general setting is described in
[
Nag19
]
.

We should warn, however, that to go beyond translational dynamics of FLC patterns (to also keep track of rotational aspects), more structure to Definition
2.1
may be required. For instance, given a tiling, its associated pattern (as discussed above) is essentially unchanged upon adding any symmetry breaking decorations, such as a frame of basis vectors decorating each tile. Again, we expand upon this in Remark
6.10
.

\Diamond

The following axiom-like properties, of
restriction
(1),
transformation
(2),
shifting
(3) and
glueing
(4) of patches, are obvious but frequently used, especially (3), which we will often refer to as
shifting by
z
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For patterns
\cP
,
\cQ\colon E\to S
,

(1)

if
\cP[x,V]=\cQ[y,V]
and
U\subseteq V
then
\cP[x,U]=\cQ[y,U]
;

(2)

if
A(x)=L(x)+z
is an affine automorphism then
\cP[x,V]=\cQ[y,V]
if and only if
(A\cP)[Ax,LV]=(A\cQ)[Ay,LV]
. For
z\in E
, we have that

\cP[x,V]=\cQ[y,V]\text{ if and only if }\cP[x+z,V-z]=\cQ[y+z,V-z];

(3)

if
\cP[x,V]=\cQ[y,V]
and
U+z\subseteq V
then
\cP[x+z,U]=\cQ[y+z,U]
;

(4)

if
V=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}U_{i}
then
\cP[x,V]=\cQ[y,V]
if and only if
\cP[x,U_{i}]=\cQ[y,U_{i}]
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
.

Proof.

By definition,
\cP[x,U]
is just the restriction of the function
\cP[x,V]
to
U\subseteq V
, so (1) is obvious. For (2), for this proof only, we will denote
\cP[x,V]\cdot v\coloneqq(\cP[x,V])(v)=\cP[x+v]
. Note that
A^{-1}(x)=L^{-1}(x-z)
and
A(x+v)=L(x+v)+z=A(x)+L(v)
so, by definition of
A\cP
,

(A\cP)[Ax,LV]=\left(LV\ni Lv\mapsto(A\cP)[Ax+Lv]=(A\cP)[A(x+v)]=\cP[x+v]=\cP[x,V]\cdot v\right)

Analogously,
(A\cQ)[Ay,LV]=(Lv\mapsto\cQ[y,V]\cdot v)
so, since
L
is invertible, the first claim of (2) follows. For the second, given
z\in E
, we have

\cP[x+z,V-z]=\left(V-z\ni v-z\mapsto\cP[(x+z)+(v-z)]=\cP[x+v]=\cP[x,V]\cdot v\right)

and analogously for
\cQ
, so the result follows. For (3), we may just combine (1) and (2). Indeed, by (2), we have
\cP[x+z,V-z]=\cQ[y+z,V-z]
, which we may then restrict to
U
using (1) and
U\subseteq V-z
. Finally, by definition,
\cP[x,V]=\cQ[y,V]
if and only if
\cP[x+v]=\cQ[y+v]
for all
v\in V
, from which (4) quickly follows.
∎

2.2.
Properties of patterns and their hulls

In this paper, our main results will only apply to patterns of translational finite local complexity (FLC). For (locally finite) tilings and Delone sets, this means that, for each
r\geq 0
, there are only finitely many patches
\cP[x,r]
, where
x
belongs to a set of ‘punctures’ for each translation-class of tile, or on the support of a Delone (multi)set. This is generalised by the following (see
[
BG13
, Definition 5.10]
for a similar definition):

Definition 2.6
.

A pattern
\cP
has (
translational
)
finite local complexity
(or is
FLC
) if, for all compact
U\subset E
, there exists some compact
K(U)\subset E
for which, for all
x\in E
, we have
\cP[x,U]=\cP[v,U]
for some
v\in K(U)
.

Recall that a tiling is repetitive if every patch that appears somewhere, in fact appears within a bounded distance of anywhere, up to translation. We generalise as follows:

Definition 2.7
.

A pattern
\cP
is
repetitive
if, for all compact
U\subset E
, there exists some compact
R(U)\subset E
so that, for all
x
,
y\in E
, we have
\cP[x,U]=\cP[y+v,U]
for some
v\in R(U)
.

Taking
K(U)=R(U)
and
y=\mathbf{0}
in the above, clearly a repetitive pattern is FLC. The following definition will be used in our notion of a pattern being substitutional, as well as in defining the hull of a pattern:

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\cP
,
\cQ\colon E\to S
be patterns. We say that
\cQ
is
locally indistinguishable from

\cP
, and write
\cQ\LI\cP
, if for all compact
U\subset E
and
x\in E
there exists some
y\in E
for which
\cQ[x,U]=\cP[y,U]
(that is, every patch of
\cQ
is a patch in
\cP
). If
\cQ\LI\cP
and
\cP\LI\cQ
, then we say that
\cP
and
\cQ
are
locally isomorphic
and write
\cP\LIs\cQ
. We let
\mathrm{LI}(\cP)
denote the
LI-class
of
\cP
, that is, the set of all patterns locally isomorphic to
\cP
.

Remark 2.9
.

Often (e.g., in
[
BG13
]
), the terms ‘locally indistinguishable’ and ‘locally isomorphic’ are used interchangeably, for the symmetric equivalence relation. However, the non-symmetric relation is also very useful (for instance, in defining the hull) and the phrase ‘locally indistinguishable
from
’ rather suggests a one-sided relation, the convention we use here.

\Diamond

Given a pattern
\cP
, its (
continuous
or
translational
)
hull

\Omega=\Omega_{\cP}
is the set of patterns locally indistinguishable from
\cP
, equipped with the ‘local topology’ (see
[
Sad08
,
BG13
]
and Section
6
). This is often defined by a metric, although we will show in Section
6
how the whole theory can be efficiently developed from a uniformity instead. It is well-known that the hull
\Omega
is then compact if and only if
\cP
is FLC, see
[
Rob04
,
Rud89
]
(and Corollary
6.15
later in the current framework). Given
\cQ\in\Omega
, we have that
\mathrm{LI}(\cQ)\subseteq\Omega
and the LI-classes partition the hull. For an FLC pattern,
\mathrm{LI}(\cP)=\Omega_{\cP}
if and only if
\cP
is repetitive, see Lemma
6.19
.

Remark 2.10
.

Without further conditions,
\Omega
may fail to be Hausdorff (see Example
6.21
). Henceforth, until we re-examine the general situation in Section
6
, we will assume that
\Omega
is Hausdorff. In the FLC case this is equivalent (see Proposition
6.27
and Lemma
6.25
) to a simple property on the pattern
\cP
, namely of being well-separated (Definition
6.23
).

\Diamond

Clearly, for any pattern
\cQ
and
x\in E
, we have
\cQ\LIs\cQ+x
, so
E
acts on
\Omega
by translation (continuously, see Lemma
6.9
in general). Another standard result, for tiling spaces, is that the dynamical system
(\Omega_{\cP},E)
is minimal if and only if
\cP
is repetitive. We extend this later (see Lemma
6.18
and Proposition
6.19
).

The notion of an LD is an analogue of a sliding block code from Symbolic Dynamics:

Definition 2.11
.

For a pattern
\cP\colon E\to S
we say that a function
f\colon E\to X
(for any set
X
) is
pattern equivariant
or
locally defined
(
by
\cP
) if there exists some
c\geq 0
for which, whenever
\cP[x,c]=\cP[y,c]
for
x
,
y\in E
, then
f(x)=f(y)
. Given another pattern
\cQ\colon E\to S^{\prime}
, we say that
\cQ
is
locally derivable
(
LD
)
from

\cP
, and write
\cP\LD\cQ
, if the function
f(x)\coloneqq\cQ[x]
is locally defined by
\cP
. That is,
\cP\LD\cQ
if there exists some
c\geq 0
, called a
derivation radius
, for which

\cP[x,c]=\cP[y,c]\text{ implies that }\cQ[x]=\cQ[y].

If
\cP\LD\cQ
and
\cQ\LD\cP
then we say that
\cP
and
\cQ
are
mutually locally derivable
(
MLD
) and write
\cP\MLD\cQ
.

The idea is that the whole pattern
\cQ
can be derived from
\cP
, at each point
x\in E
, by a rule that only observes
\cP
to a bounded radius
c
about
x
. For example, recolouring all white tiles black in an infinite chequerboard pattern is a (non-mutual) LD, but recolouring a single tile is not (since arbitrarily large patches about that tile cannot distinguish it from any others of the same colour). Notably, the operation of replacing the inflated tiles of a supertiling with tiles of the original scale, according to a substitution rule, is an example of an LD. Note that the ‘
0
’ of
\cQ[x,0]\equiv\cQ[x]
(and
\cQ[y]\equiv\cQ[y,0]
) implicit in the definition is somewhat arbitrary, in the sense we could replace the requirement
\cQ[x]=\cQ[y]
with
\cQ[x,r]=\cQ[y,r]
for any fixed
r\geq 0
, by increasing
c
by
r
(see Lemma
6.31
).

The notation
\cQ\LI\cP
suggests the containment finding local indistinguishablity: the set of patches found in
\cQ
are
contained
in those for
\cP
. Moreover,
\cQ\LI\cP
if and only if
\Omega_{\cQ}\subseteq\Omega_{\cP}
. Similarly, the notation
\cP\LD\cQ
indicates a ‘lifting’ of the notion to hulls. There is an induced factor map
f\colon\Omega_{\cP}\LDmap\Omega_{\cQ}
, that is a
local derivation map
(see Definition
6.30
and Proposition
6.36
). In particular, we have
\cU\LD f(\cU)
for all
\cU\in\Omega_{\cP}
, also with derivation radius
c
. Also as suggested by these notations, local indistinguishability and derivation can be composed (they are pre-orders, see Lemmas
6.2
and
6.33
).

Definition 2.12
.

For a pattern
\cP
in
E
we let
\cK=\cK_{\cP}
denote the Abelian group of (translational)
periods
of
\cP
, that is
\cK=\{x\in E\mid\cP=\cP+x\}
. We say that
\cP
is
non-periodic
if
\cK=\{\mathbf{0}\}
, otherwise it is
periodic
.

Clearly,
u\in\cK_{\cP}
if and only if
\cP[x]=\cP[x+u]
for all
x\in E
. It is not hard to show (see Lemmas
6.3
and
6.34
) that
\cK_{\cQ}\leq\cK_{\cP}
if
\cP\LI\cQ
or
\cQ\LD\cP
, in particular
\cK_{\cQ}=\cK_{\cP}
if
\cP\LIs\cQ
or
\cP\MLD\cQ
. Following
[
BG13
]
, we say that
\cP
(and its hull
\Omega_{\cP}
) is
aperiodic
if all elements of
\Omega_{\cP}
are non-periodic. This holds if
\cP
is non-periodic and repetitive, since then
\Omega=\mathrm{LI}(\cP)
.

For standard examples of patterns coming from tilings or Delone sets,
\cK\cong\mathbb{Z}^{k}
for some
0\leq k\leq d
, as
\cK
is discrete. More generally,
\cK
is discrete when
\cP
is
return discrete
, see Definition
6.22
and Lemma
6.26
. But not all patterns are return discrete. However, as we assume the hull is Hausdorff,
\cK
is at least a closed subgroup of
E
, see Proposition
6.27
. This is important later, as we will use that the index
[L\cK:\cK]
, of a certain inflated copy
L\cK
inside
\cK
, is finite. The fact required for the following definition, on closed Euclidean subgroups, is well-known:

Definition 2.13
.

Let
G\leqslant E
be a closed subgroup of
E\cong\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then we may write
G\cong V\oplus K
, where
V\leqslant G
is the vector subspace given by the connected component of
G
that contains the origin, and
K=G/V\cong\mathbb{Z}^{k}
for some
0\leq k\leq d
. We say that
G
has
trivial discrete component
if
K
is the trivial group, that is, when
\cK
is connected.

Of course, if
G
is discrete then it has trivial discrete component if and only if
G=\{\mathbf{0}\}
is itself trivial. We will allow for patterns that have non-discrete groups of periods
\cK
, in which case having trivial discrete component is typically the suitable replacement of
\cK
being itself trivial (i.e., non-periodicity) for our recognisability results.

Lemma 2.14
.

For an affine automorphism
A(x)=L(x)+z
, we have
\cK_{A\cP}=L\cK_{\cP}
.

Proof.

We have
A(x+g)=A(x)+L(g)
. Thus, by the definition of the distorted pattern,
\cP[x]=\cP[x+g]
is equivalent to
(A\cP)[A(x)]=(A\cP)[A(x)+L(g)]
. Now,
\cP[x]=\cP[x+g]
for arbitrary
x\in E
is equivalent to
g\in\cK_{\cP}
, and similarly
(A\cP)[A(x)]=(A\cP)[A(x)+L(g)]
for arbitrary
A(x)\in E
is equivalent to
L(g)\in\cK_{A\cP}
. It follows that
\cK_{A\cP}=L\cK_{\cP}
, as required.
∎

3.
Substitutional patterns and recurrence of patches

In this section we define a natural notion of a pattern being hierarchical with respect to a linear expansion and derive some important properties of these patterns. The definition aims to simultaneously cover standard definitions including stone and non-stone inflation tilings, substitution Delone (multi)sets
[
LW03
]
and more. As noted in the introduction, the definition here stems from the collaboration
[
HKW25
]
but is a slight relaxation of the reasonably well-known notion of pseudo self-affine, see
[
Sol07
]
. An extension of this to pattern spaces will be given in Section
6.6
. We also give some results, in this section, on certain short return vectors necessarily being periods, useful to the proof of Theorem
4.2
on unique composition.

3.1.
Substitutional patterns and notation

Throughout, from now up until Section
6
, we assume that the hull
\Omega
of our pattern
\cP
is compact and Hausdorff, equivalently
\cP
is FLC and well-separated (see Definition
6.23
). Thus,
\cP
need not be defined by a tiling or Delone set, and it may even have a non-discrete group of periods (see the examples in Section
5
). However, on a first reading, one may just consider the case that
\cP
is an FLC (but not necessarily repetitive) tiling and the right intuition will hold throughout.

Definition 3.1
.

A linear map
L\colon E\to E
is
expansive
if all eigenvalues of
L
are strictly larger than
1
in modulus.

It can be shown that
L
being expansive is equivalent to there existing some norm on
E
and
\lambda>1
with
\|L(x)\|\geq\lambda\|x\|
for all
x\in E
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
L\colon E\to E
be a linear automorphism. A pattern
\cP
of
E
is
L
-substitutional
(or
L
-sub
, for short) if there is another pattern
\cP^{\prime}
of
E
with
\cP^{\prime}\LI\cP
and
L\cP^{\prime}\LD\cP
. We call
\cP^{\prime}
a
predecessor
for
\cP
. If
L
is expansive, we say that
\cP
is
expansive
L
-sub
.

Intuition for the above definition is given below the
statement of
Corollary
4.10
in the introduction. One might consider allowing
L
to be a general affine automorphism (a linear automorphism followed by a translation), but this essentially offers no more generality, since we may always translate
\cP^{\prime}
instead (as it is locally isomorphic to
\cP^{\prime}
).

Remark 3.3
.

It is easily checked that the property of being
L
-sub is invariant (and with a canonically defined predecessor) under local isomorphism and MLD equivalence. On the latter, see a similar statement for
L
-sub pattern spaces in Lemma
6.50
.

\Diamond

To keep the conditions of this definition as primitive as possible, we do not even demand
\cP^{\prime}\LIs\cP
, merely
\cP^{\prime}\LI\cP
. However, it turns out that in the case of interest here — namely, when
L
is expansive — this holds automatically:

Proposition 3.4
.

If
\cP
is FLC and well-separated (equivalently,
\Omega_{\cP}
is compact and Hausdorff) and expansive
L
-sub with predecessor
\cP^{\prime}
, then
\cP^{\prime}\LIs\cP
, equivalently,
\Omega_{\cP}=\Omega_{\cP^{\prime}}
.

The above will be proved in Section
6.6
. Henceforth in this section, we fix an expansive
L
-sub pattern
\cP
in
E
with predecessor
\cP^{\prime}
. We let
c\geq 0
denote a derivation radius of
L\cP^{\prime}\LD\cP
and fix a norm of
E
so that
\|L(x)\|\geq\lambda\|x\|
, with
\lambda>1
(the choice of norm, otherwise, does not affect any results).

It will be important to control agreement of patches fairly tightly, which may be pushed to larger and larger regions by substitution, as will be explained later. A minor annoyance here is that, due to the derivation radius, some information near the boundary of a region is potentially lost under each application. This can be addressed in a few ways quite easily, using that
L
is expansive. Here, we choose to slightly modify our ‘level-
n
ball’, using the following simple lemma. Throughout, given
X\subseteq E
and
t\geq 0
, we let
X_{+t}
denote the set of points within
t
of
X
, and
X_{-t}
the set of points with
t
-neighbourhoods wholly in
X
. More precisely,
X_{+t}=\{y\in E\mid\|y-x\|\leq t\text{ for some }x\in X\}=X+B_{t}
and
X_{-t}=\{x\in E\mid B(x,t)\subseteq X\}
. It is trivial to check that
X_{-t}\supseteq Y
if and only if
X\supseteq Y_{+t}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

There exists
\kappa\geq 0
for which, for all
X\subseteq E
, we have
(L(X_{+\kappa}))_{-c}\supseteq(LX)_{+\kappa}
.

Proof.

Let
B\coloneqq B_{1}
. The lemma holds if
\kappa LB\supseteq(\kappa+c)B
. Indeed, in this case,

L(X_{+\kappa})=L(X+\kappa B)=LX+\kappa LB\supseteq LX+(\kappa+c)B=(LX+\kappa B)+cB=((LX)_{+\kappa})_{+c},

as required. To show
\kappa LB\supseteq(\kappa+c)B
for some
\kappa\geq 0
, recall that
LB\supseteq\lambda B
for
\lambda>1
(by expansivity), so
\kappa LB\supseteq\kappa\lambda B\supseteq(\kappa+c)B
provided
\kappa\lambda\geq\kappa+c
, which holds for
\kappa\geq\frac{c}{\lambda-1}\geq 0
.
∎

Notation 3.6
.

We now fix
\kappa\geq 0
satisfying Lemma
3.5
and define
V^{k}\coloneqq(L^{k}B)_{+\kappa}
. Thus,
L(V^{k})_{-c}\supseteq V^{k+1}
since, applying the lemma to
X=L^{k}B
,

L(V^{k})_{-c}=(L(X_{+\kappa}))_{-c}\supseteq(LX)_{+\kappa}=(L^{k+1}B)_{+\kappa}=V^{k+1}.

If
c=0
(for instance, for a tiling stone inflation) we may take
\kappa=0
and then
V^{k}=L^{k}B
. Generally, we just add a small ‘fringe’ to these inflated balls.

3.2.
Substitution and hierarchy

Given an
L
-sub pattern
\cP_{0}\coloneqq\cP
, by definition it has a predecessor
\cP_{1}\coloneqq\cP^{\prime}
. In fact, we may construct an infinite hierarchy of predecessors. Firstly, recall that
L\cP^{\prime}\LD\cP
induces an LD map of hulls
S\colon\Omega_{L\cP^{\prime}}\LDmap\Omega_{\cP}=\Omega
, denoted
S
for
subdivision
(mirroring the notation in
[
Sol98
]
), see Proposition
6.36
. Intuitively, this map simply ‘applies the same local rule’ to all elements in the hull of
\cP^{\prime}
. Since
\cP\LIs\cP^{\prime}
(Proposition
3.4
), we have
\Omega_{\cP^{\prime}}=\Omega_{\cP}
so subdivision
S\colon L\Omega\LDmap\Omega
is in fact defined over the entire (inflated) hull; note that the elements of
\Omega_{L\cP}
are precisely the inflates, by
L
, of the elements of
\Omega
, hence the notation
L\Omega=\{L\cQ\mid\cQ\in\Omega\}=\Omega_{L\cP}=\Omega_{L\cP^{\prime}}
(see Lemma
6.9
). We may thus define a map, called
substitution
, by

\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Omega,\ \ \sigma(\cQ)\coloneqq S(L\cQ),

the composition
S\circ L
of the inflation map
L\colon\Omega\to L\Omega
, given by
\cQ\mapsto L\cQ
, followed by subdivision. Since
S
is an LD map, we have that
L\cQ\LD S(L\cQ)
for each
L\cQ\in L\Omega
. Since
S
is a factor map,
\sigma(\cQ+x)=S(L(\cQ+x))=S(L(\cQ)+Lx)=\sigma(\cQ)+Lx
for all
\cQ\in\Omega
and
x\in E
. Since inflation is continuous (Lemma
6.9
) and subdivision is continuous (Lemma
6.36
), so is substitution. Substitution and subdivision are also surjective, by the same lemmas. Thus, by taking forward- and pre-images of
\cP
under
\sigma
, we may thus always construct a
hierarchy
:

Definition 3.7
.

Let
\cP
be
L
-sub with predecessor
\cP^{\prime}
and let
(\cP_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a bi-infinite sequence of patterns satisfying
\sigma(\cP_{i+1})=\cP_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, with
\cP_{0}=\cP
and
\cP_{1}=\cP^{\prime}
. We call
(\cP_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
a
hierarchy
for
\cP
(with respect to expansion
L
and predecessor
\cP^{\prime}
).

Proposition 3.8
.

All
\cP_{i}
in a hierarchy are locally isomorphic to each other and each
\cP_{i}
is
L
-sub, with predecessor

.

Proof.

By the definition of an LD map (Definition

L\cP_{i+1}\LD S(L\cP_{i+1})=\cP_{i}
for each

for all

For

Remark 3.9
.

The above result does
not

are locally isomorphic to each other, which will happen if and only if

is repetitive (see Proposition

\Diamond

Surjectivity of
\sigma
, equivalently
S
, may be interpreted as saying that, for each
\cQ\in\Omega
, there is some ‘super-pattern’
L\cQ^{\prime}\in L\Omega
with
S(L\cQ^{\prime})=\cQ
. Henceforth, given an
L
-sub pattern
\cP
, we fix some hierarchy
(\cP_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
. It is sometimes useful to visualise a hierarchy with the rescaled patterns:

\cdots\LD L^{3}\cP_{3}\LD L^{2}\cP_{2}\LD(L\cP_{1}=L\cP^{\prime})\LD(\cP_{0}=\cP)\LD L^{-1}\cP_{-1}\LD L^{-2}\cP_{-2}\LD\cdots.

In the above, the local derivations come from the LD map
S\colon L\Omega\LDmap\Omega
induced by
L\cP^{\prime}\LD\cP
and the ‘distortions’
S\colon L^{n}\Omega\LDmap L^{n-1}\Omega
of this, see Proposition
6.39
. The following, using expansivity of
L
, explicitly details the obvious fact that substitution increases the size of agreement between (sufficiently large) patches, and will be used frequently:

Lemma 3.10
.

Given
\cU
,
\cV\in\Omega
, suppose that
\cU[x,U]=\cV[y,U]
. Then
(\sigma\cU)[Lx,(LU)_{-c}]=(\sigma\cV)[Ly,(LU)_{-c}]
. In particular, for
r\geq c/\lambda
,

\mathrm{if}\ \cU[x,r]=\cV[y,r]\ \mathrm{then}\ (\sigma\cU)[Lx,\lambda r-c]=(\sigma\cV)[Ly,\lambda r-c]

and, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\mathrm{if}\ \cU[x,V^{k}]=\cV[y,V^{k}]\ \mathrm{then}\ (\sigma\cU)[Lx,V^{k+1}]=(\sigma\cV)[Ly,V^{k+1}].

Proof.

By Lemma
2.5
(2), given
\cU[x,U]=\cV[y,U]
we have that
(L\cU)[Lx,LU]=(L\cV)[Ly,LU]
. Given
z\in(LU)_{-c}
, we have
B(z,c)\subseteq LU
and thus
(L\cU)[Lx,B(z,c)]=(L\cV)[Ly,B(z,c)]
, by Lemma
2.5
(1). Shifting by
z
, using Lemma
2.5
(3), we have
(L\cU)[Lx+z,B_{c}]=(L\cV)[Ly+z,B_{c}]
. Applying the LD map
S
of hulls (which also has derivation radius
c
, see Lemma
6.36
), we thus have
(S(L\cU))[Lx+z]=(S(L\cV))[Ly+z]
, that is,
(\sigma\cU)[Lx+z]=(\sigma\cV)[Ly+z]
. Since this holds for all
z\in(LU)_{-c}
, we have
(\sigma\cU)[Lx,(LU)_{-c}]=(\sigma\cV)[Ly,(LU)_{-c}]
, as required.

Applying the above to
U=B_{r}
derives the second claim, using that
(LB_{r})_{-c}\supseteq(\lambda B_{r})_{-c}=(B_{\lambda r})_{-c}=B_{\lambda r-c}
, where
\lambda r-c\geq 0
for
r\geq c/\lambda
. The third claim follows from applying the above to
V^{k}
since
L(V^{k})_{-c}\supseteq V^{k+1}
, see Notation
3.6
.
∎

3.3.
Repetitivity and periods from short return vectors

The following gives a generalised notion of a repetitivity function:

Definition 3.11
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}
denote the set of compact subsets of
E
and let
\rho\colon D\to\mathcal{Z}
with some domain
D\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
. We say that
\cP
is
\rho
-repetitive
if, for any
x
,
y\in E
and
U\in D
, there exists some
v\in\rho(U)
with
\cP[x,U]=\cP[y+v,U]
. That is, given
U\in D
, if a
U
-patch occurs somewhere, it appears within
\rho(U)
of anywhere.

Note that, by Definition
2.7
,
\cP
is repetitive precisely when it is
\rho
-repetitive for some function
\rho
with domain
D=\mathcal{Z}
. For flexibility, we allow for a smaller domain in the above definition. Clearly,
\cP
is repetitive if and only if it is
\rho
-repetitive for some
\rho
whose domain is final, in the sense that every compact
U\subset E
satisfies
U\subseteq V
for some
V\in D
, for example,
D=\{B_{r}\mid r\geq 0\}
or
D=\{V^{k}\mid k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
when working with an
L
-sub pattern.

Example 3.12
.

Take any function
f\colon\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and define the corresponding repetitivity function
\rho\colon\{B_{r}\mid r\geq 0\}\to\mathcal{Z}
by
\rho(B_{r})=B_{f(r)}
. Then
\cP
is
\rho
-repetitive if (the centre of) every
r
-patch appears within radius
f(r)
of anywhere, so this corresponds to the standard notion of a repetitivity function in this case. A pattern is repetitive if and only if it is
\rho
-repetitive for a function
\rho
of this form.

\Diamond

Definition 3.13
.

We call an
L
-sub pattern
\cP

primitive
if its
V^{0}
-patches appear relatively densely, that is, there exists some compact
X\subset E
for which, for all
x
,
y\in E
, there exists some

with
\cP[x,V^{0}]=\cP[y+z,V^{0}]
.

Example 3.14
.

The name ‘primitive’ is motivated by FLC tile stone substitutions. Such a substitution is primitive if there exists some
k\in\mathbb{N}
so that the
k
-fold substitution of any prototile contains at least one translated copy of each prototile
[
AP98
]
. It is easy to show that tilings admitted by a primitive substitution are also primitive in the above sense. Conversely, if all
V^{0}
-patches appear relatively densely, then by taking a high enough supertile level so that all supertiles contain a translated copy of

, we see that the substitution is primitive in the usual sense (after, perhaps, discarding any prototiles that do not appear in the tiling).

\Diamond

Notation 3.15
.

Given
x\in E
and

, we denote
x^{k}\coloneqq L^{k}x
.

Lemma 3.16
.

Suppose that
X\subset E
meets the centres of all

-patches in
\cP=\cP_{0}

, we have that

-patches in
\cP_{-k}

\cP_{-k}[x,V^{k}]=\cP_{-k}[y,V^{k}]

In particular, suppose that
\cP
is primitive, with

for which
\cP

Proof.

This is essentially a corollary of Lemma

So now suppose that there exists

∎

Example 3.17
.

Suppose that
\cP

-ball for some fixed

\Diamond

A central observation in Solomyak’s proof of unique composition is that patches of repetitive tilings that repeat too closely, relative to their size and the repetitivity function, are necessarily displaced by a period. As noted in
[
Sol98
]
, this is itself a higher dimensional analogue of an main insight in Mossé’s result
[
Mos92
,
Mos96
]
in the symbolic setting (being
N
-power free for sufficiently large
N
). The below is a quantitative statement of these in our setting:

Lemma 3.18
.

Suppose that
\cP
is
\rho
-repetitive. Take any
U\in\mathrm{dom}(\rho)
with
\mathbf{0}\in U
. Suppose there exists some
x\in E
and
u\in U
for which
\cP[x,\rho(U)]=\cP[x+u,\rho(U)]
. Then
u\in\cK
.

Proof.

Take any
y\in E
and denote
V\coloneqq\rho(U)
. By the definition of
\rho
-repetitivity, there exists
v\in V
with
\cP[x+v,U]=\cP[y,U]
. In particular, since
\mathbf{0}\in U
, we have
\cP[x+v]=\cP[y]
. Moreover, since we assume
\cP[x,V]=\cP[x+u,V]
, and
v\in V
, we have
\cP[x,\{v\}]=\cP[x+u,\{v\}]
, that is,
\cP[x+v]=\cP[x+u+v]
. Combining these,
\cP[x+u+v]=\cP[x+v]=\cP[y]
. From
\cP[y,U]=\cP[x+v,U]
and
u\in U
, we similarly have
\cP[y+u]=\cP[x+v+u]
and hence
\cP[y]=\cP[y+u]
. Since
y
was arbitrary,
u\in\cK
.
∎

Example 3.19
.

Consider the function
\rho
from Example

of a repetitive pattern
\cP
. By the above, if we ever have patches about

.

\Diamond

.

By Lemma

, if
\cP
is primitive then there exists some

(which, by the proof, depends only on

-patches) satisfying the following: if

for some

, then

. In the self-similar case, by Example

, there exists some

so that if

[

in this case.

\Diamond

The above lemma is of no use for non-repetitive patterns. However, we observe that whilst some large patches may appear with relatively short displacements in a non-repetitive, non-periodic tiling, certain patches (those containing all patches of some smaller size) cannot:

Lemma 3.21
.

Let
U\subset E
with
\mathbf{0}\in U
. Take some compact
V\subset E
so that all
U
-patches occur with centre within
V
of a point
x
. That is, suppose that for all
y\in E
, there exists some
v\in V
so that
\cP[x+v,U]=\cP[y,U]
. Then, if
\cP[x,V]=\cP[x+u,V]
for some
u\in U
, we have
u\in\cK
.

Proof.

The proof is identical to that of Lemma
3.18
, except now the existence of the
v\in V
with
\cP[x+v,U]=\cP[y,U]
is assumed at a particular
x\in E
rather than being derived from repetitivity at all
x\in E
.
∎

By FLC, for any compact
U
and
x\in E
, all
U
-patches occur with centre within some bounded distance from
x
. For non-repetitive patterns this distance will be arbitrarily large, depending on
x
. Nonetheless, once such a region has been fixed, we can obtain a similar property for larger patches by substitution, using Lemma
3.16
, allowing us us to push the property of seeing all patches of a certain size down the hierarchy, to larger patches over larger (but controlled) regions. We also need to go up the hierarchy, ensuring that a point ‘near’ all
V^{0}
-patches has the same property in the level above. For this, we introduce the following lemma, restricted over the origin for simplicity:

Lemma 3.22
.

There exists some compact
Y\subset E
for which, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have that every
V^{0}
-patch of
\cP
appears in
\cP_{i}
, with centre in
Y
. That is, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
x\in E
, there exists some
y\in Y
with
\cP[x,V^{0}]=\cP_{i}[y,V^{0}]
.

Proof.

It may be helpful to the reader to consider the case of FLC tile substitutions. For each tile in
\cP
, repeated substitution of it either eventually generates all tile types in
\cP
(which are the same as those appearing in each
\cP_{i}\LIs\cP
), a so-called ‘generator’ tile, or it does not, a ‘non-generator’. If, arbitrarily high up the hierarchy, the origin lies (say, near the middle of) non-generators, under iterated substitution we will not see all tile types in
\cP
to larger and larger radii (namely, in larger and larger supertiles), contradicting that we see all tiles to a bounded radius in
\cP
. Since there are only finitely many tiles (FLC), the generators generate all tiles in a bounded number
N
of applications of substitution, so we should be able to find all tiles in
\cP_{i}
within the (bounded)
N
-supertile defined by the tile of
\cP_{i+N}
over the origin which, by above, should be generating; technically, to avoid issues near (or on) tile boundaries, we need to talk about (non-) generating small
V^{0}
-patches, but the argument is similar. We now make this argument precise, and extend it to general
L
-sub patterns.

Given a patch
p=\cP_{i}[x,V^{k}]
, by Lemma
3.10
we have that
\cP_{i-1}[Lx,V^{k+1}]
depends only on
p
and not on
x
or

(also note that all such patches occur somewhere in
\cP=\cP_{0}
, by Proposition
3.8
), so denoting this by
\sigma p\coloneqq\cP_{i-1}[Lx,V^{k+1}]
is well-defined (that is, it depends only on
p
and not on
x
,
i
or
k
). Moreover, the collection of sub-patches of

, that is, the patches
\cP_{i-1}[x^{\prime},V^{k^{\prime}}]
for

and
V^{k^{\prime}}+x^{\prime}\subseteq V^{k}+x
, depends only on
p
. So, for each patch
p=\cP_{i}[x,V^{n}]
, we may define the number
f(p)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
that is the minimum for which
\sigma^{f(p)}(p)
contains a copy of each
V^{0}
-patch (with
f(p)=\infty
if
\sigma^{k}(p)
fails this for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
). By FLC, there exists some compact
K\subset E
containing all
V^{0}
-patches, so
\sigma^{n}q
contains all
V^{0}
-patches in
\cP_{0}
with

chosen so that

and

. In particular,

for some

-patches (and, of course,

-patches too, but later we want

to be a
V^{1}
-patch).

Let

be a compact set containing, in
\cP_{0}
, centres of all
V^{2}
-patches in
\cP_{0}
(equivalently, in any
\cP_{i}\LIs\cP
); that is, for all

and

for some

. Let

and

. Choose a finite set

that is

there exists

with

. For each

, let

be defined by

. Let

and

. Without loss of generality, by the previous paragraph, we may suppose that

(by including a centre of the patch

in
S
). Let

-patches, where

, the patch

-patches. By the end of the previous paragraph, this holds for

(even restricting

from

). Given

. Then

. Indeed,

∎

We combine some of the above results in a useful form for our proof of Theorem

:

\cP

\cP_{-\eta}

\cP[x,V^{i+\eta-\ell}]=\cP_{-\eta}[y,V^{i+\eta-\ell}]

,

\cP_{i+\eta-\ell}\LIs\cP\LIs\cP_{i-\ell}
, so
p\coloneqq\cP_{{i+\eta-\ell}}[x^{-(i+\eta-\ell)},V^{0}]=\cP_{i-\ell}[y,V^{0}]
for some
y\in E
; in fact, we may take
y\in L^{\ell}B
by Lemma
3.22
, since
p
also appears in
\cP
(and
i-\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
). Substituting
i+\eta-\ell
times with Lemma
3.10
,
\cP[x,V^{i+\eta-\ell}]=\cP_{-\eta}[y^{i+\eta-\ell},V^{i+\eta-\ell}]
. Since
y^{i+\eta-\ell}\in L^{i+\eta-\ell}(L^{\ell}B)=L^{i+\eta}B\subseteq V^{i+\eta}
, the result follows.
∎

4.
Recognisability for
L
-sub pattern spaces

In this section we state and prove our main recognisability results. The key result is Theorem
4.2
, for individual
L
-sub patterns, whose proof is delayed to the end of this section. Recognisability results (e.g., on injectivity of substitution) for hulls and general pattern spaces then quickly follow from this and more fundamental results on pattern spaces, which are covered later, in Section
6
. In particular, Theorem
6.51
is used to ensure that some power of substitution fixes LI-classes, for general
L
-sub pattern spaces.

4.1.
Recognisability results

Our main aim is to show the following property always holds, under reasonable conditions:

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\cP
be
L
-sub, with corresponding substitution map
\sigma\colon\Omega_{\cP}\to\Omega_{\cP}
. Let
\cK\coloneqq\cK_{\cP}
. We say that
\cP
satisfies
unique composition modulo

\cK
, or UC
/\cK
, for short, if for all
\cU
,
\cV\in\Omega_{\cP}
with
\sigma(\cU)=\sigma(\cV)=\cP
, we have

L\cU=L\cV+g\text{ for some }g\in\cK.

The latter is of course equivalent to
\cU=\cV+L^{-1}(g)
for some
g\in\cK
(Lemma
2.14
). The main structural theorem in this section, from which we will derive many more particular results on the degree of injectivity of substitution
\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Omega
, is the following:

Theorem 4.2
.

Any FLC, well-separated and expansive
L
-sub pattern satisfies UC
/\cK
.

Remark 4.3
.

There is a trivial converse statement to UC
/\cK
: if
\sigma(\cV)=\cP
and
L\cU=L\cV+g
for some
g\in\cK_{\cP}
then
\sigma(\cU)=\cP
too. Indeed, since subdivision
S
is a factor map,

\sigma(\cU)=S(L\cU)=S(L\cV+g)=S(L\cV)+g=\sigma(\cV)+g=\cP+g=\cP.

The content of Theorem
4.2
is that
all
(inflated) pre-images of
\cP
under substitution are related by a translational period of
\cP
.

\Diamond

Remark 4.4
.

If
\cP
is non-periodic,
\cK_{\cP}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
so Theorem
4.2
says that
\cP
has a unique pre-image under substitution, which of course is its predecessor
\cP^{\prime}\in\Omega_{\cP}
, assumed to exist by the definition of
\cP
being
L
-sub.

\Diamond

Remark 4.5
.

Note that
L\cK
is a subgroup of
\cK=\cK_{\cP}
, see Lemma
6.43
and Proposition
3.4
, where
\cK
is closed in
E\cong\mathbb{R}^{d}
(as
\Omega_{\cP}
is Hausdorff, see Lemma
6.24
). Since
L\cK\cong\cK
, they have identical connected component containing the origin. So the index is equal to that of the image of the induced inclusion of the discrete component of
L\cK
into that of
\cK
; these are free Abelian of equal, finite rank (see Definition
2.13
), so
[L\cK:\cK]<\infty
.

\Diamond

Compare the statement of Theorem
4.2
with
[
Sol98
, Theorem 1.2]
. The latter applies to all elements in the hull of an FLC and repetitive stone inflation tiling, whereas Theorem
4.2
, as currently stated, only holds for the particular pattern
\cP
(although it can now be a general
L
-sub pattern and need not be repetitive). So before proving it, we will show how the result extends over the whole hull and, in fact, to general so-called
L
-sub pattern spaces (Definition
6.48
). To avoid too many technicalities before stating our main theorems, this concept will only be fully developed later, in Section
6.6
. However, in short, a pattern space
\Omega
being
L
-sub (Definition
6.4
) means
\Omega
is equipped with a surjective LD map
S\colon L\Omega\LDmap\Omega
(Definition
6.30
). This allows the following results to be applied, for example, to non-transitive spaces of tilings generated by non-primitive tile substitutions. Of course, the hull of an (FLC, well-separated)
L
-sub pattern
\cP
is an example, see Example
6.49
.

Firstly, the classical case of hulls of repetitive stone inflation tilings (and more) are easily dealt with, recovering a mild generalisation of the main result of
[
Sol98
]
:

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\cP
be a well-separated, repetitive and expansive
L
-sub pattern, with
\cK_{\cP}
discrete. Then
\cP
is non-periodic (equivalently, aperiodic) if and only
\sigma\colon\Omega_{\cP}\to\Omega_{\cP}
is injective. In this case, the inverse
S^{-1}\colon\Omega_{\cP}\LDmap\Omega_{L\cP}
is also an LD map.

Proof.

Since
\cP
is repetitive, all elements of the hull are locally isomorphic (see Proposition
6.19
). In particular, all elements of the hull are
L
-sub (where the predecessor of any
\cQ\in\Omega
is given by any
\cQ^{\prime}\in\sigma^{-1}(\cQ)
, by surjectivity, see Lemma
6.44
). Thus, Theorem
4.2
applies to all elements
\cQ\in\Omega=\Omega_{\cP}=\Omega_{\cQ}
.

Suppose that
\cP
, and thus every
\cQ\in\Omega
, is non-periodic, so
\cK_{\cQ}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
for all
\cQ\in\Omega
. Then UC
/\cK
states: if
\sigma(\cU)=\sigma(\cV)=\cQ
then
L\cU=L\cV+\mathbf{0}=L\cV
, so
\cU=\cV
and
\sigma
is injective. Conversely, supposing that
\sigma
is injective, take any
\cU_{0}=\cU\in\Omega
and any
g\in\cK=\cK_{\cU}
, so
\cU=\cU+g
. There exists some
\cU_{1}\in\Omega_{\cP}
with
\sigma(\cU_{1})=\cU_{0}=\cU_{0}+g=\sigma(\cU_{1}+L^{-1}(g))
. Since
\sigma
is injective, it follows that
\cU_{1}=\cU_{1}+L^{-1}(g)
. We may repeat this argument, showing that
\cU_{n}=\cU_{n}+L^{-n}(g)
for arbitrarily large
n
and
\cU_{n}\in\Omega
, so
L^{-n}(g)\in\cK
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
L
is expansive but
\cK
is discrete,
g=\mathbf{0}
. Since
g\in\cK
was arbitrary, it follows that
\cK=\{\mathbf{0}\}
and all elements of the hull are non-periodic.

Finally, given that the subdivision map
S\colon L\Omega\LDmap\Omega
induced by
L\cP^{\prime}\LD\cP
is injective, from Corollary
6.41
the inverse
S^{-1}\colon\Omega\LDmap L\Omega
is also the LD map induced by
\cP\LD L\cP^{\prime}
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

An equivalent statement, in the above, is to replace
\cP
being well-separated and each
\cK_{\cP}
is discrete with simply
\cP
is ‘return discrete’ (see Definition
6.22
and Proposition
6.28
). This condition cannot be dropped here. Indeed, otherwise, we have counter-examples such as a ‘Fibonacci barcode’ or the trivial void pattern, see Section
5
. But it applies to the standard examples of FLC, repetitive tilings and Delone sets, which are return discrete. Instead, this can be repaired by replacing ‘non-periodic’ with ‘
\cK
has trivial discrete component’, as we will see in more general results below.

\Diamond

We now give recognisability results that go well beyond minimal examples and even beyond
hulls
, to
L
-sub pattern spaces (Definition
6.48
), as mentioned above. We will see, in Theorem
6.51
, that some power
\sigma^{n}
of substitution on an FLC and well-separated (equivalently, compact and Hausdorff)
L
-sub pattern space
\Omega
fixes LI-classes and is such that
\cP
is
L^{n}
-sub for each
\cP\in\Omega
. Note that it is not necessarily true that each
\cP\in\Omega
is
L
-sub without taking a power, even for hulls (see Example
5.7
). We emphasise that the following does not require a return discreteness condition and thus applies, for example, to spaces of uniformly discrete but possibly non-relatively dense point sets.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
\Omega
be an FLC, well-separated and expansive
L
-sub pattern space. Take
n\in\mathbb{N}
for which
\sigma^{n}
fixes LI-classes, and thus each
\cP\in\Omega
is
L^{n}
-sub. Such an
n\in\mathbb{N}
exists. Then, for each
\cP\in\Omega
, we have
\#\sigma^{-n}(\cP)={[L^{n}\cK_{\cP}:\cK_{\cP}]}
. Thus, for each
\cP\in\Omega
, we have that
\cK_{\cP}
has trivial discrete component if and only if
\sigma^{-n}(\cP)
is a singleton. In particular, if
\cP\in\Omega
is nonperiodic, then
\sigma^{-1}(\cP)
is a singleton.

Proof.

Take
n\in\mathbb{N}
as in Theorem
6.51
, so that
\sigma^{n}\colon\Omega\to\Omega
preserves LI-classes and each element of
\Omega
is
L^{n}
-sub. Thus, for any
\cP\in\Omega
, we have that
\sigma^{-n}(\cP)\subseteq\mathrm{LI}(\cP)\subseteq\Omega_{\cP}\subseteq\Omega
. Since
\Omega
is FLC and well-separated (equivalently, compact Hausdorff), so is
\Omega_{\cP}\subseteq\Omega
(see also Proposition
6.29
). Thus, we may restrict the problem to finding the number of pre-images of the restriction
\sigma^{n}\colon\Omega_{\cP}\to\Omega_{\cP}
, to which Theorem
4.2
applies directly to the
L^{n}
-sub pattern
\cP
.

Substitution
\sigma^{n}
is surjective (Lemma
6.44
), so
\sigma^{-n}(\cP)\neq\emptyset
. Given
\cV\in\sigma^{-n}(\cP)
, by Theorem
4.2
(and Definition
4.1
), every other
\cU\in\sigma^{-n}(\cP)
is given by
\cU=\cV+L^{-n}(g)
for some
g\in\cK_{\cP}
. There are precisely
[L^{n}\cK_{\cP}:\cK_{\cP}]
distinct such translates. To see this, note that
\cV+L^{-n}(g_{1})=\cV+L^{-n}(g_{2})
if and only if
\cV+L^{-n}(g_{1}-g_{2})=\cV
, that is,

, equivalently
g_{1}-g_{2}\in L^{n}\cK_{\cV}=L^{n}\cK_{\cP}
, using that
\cV\LIs\cP
by our choice of
n
. Thus, the number of distinct translates
\cV+L^{-n}(g)
over
g\in\cK_{\cP}
is given by the number of elements in the quotient
\cK_{\cP}/L^{n}\cK_{\cP}
, that is, by the index
[L^{n}\cK_{\cP}:\cK_{\cP}]
, so the first claim on
\#\sigma^{-n}(\cP)
is proved.

The next claim follows directly from the above, and that
[L^{n}\cK_{\cP}:\cK_{\cP}]>1
if and only if
\cK_{\cP}
has non-trivial discrete component for
L
expansive. Finally, by the previous claim, if
\cK_{\cP}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
then
\#\sigma^{-n}(\cP)=1
so
\#\sigma^{-1}(\cP)=1
, since
\sigma
is surjective.
∎

If one is only interested in the dichotomy between periodicity and aperiodicity (versus injectivity or not of
\sigma
), the following general recognisability result applies:

Theorem 4.9
.

For an FLC, well-separated (equivalently, compact Hausdorff) and expansive
L
-sub pattern space
\Omega
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Omega
is injective, and thus a homeomorphism;

(2)

\cK_{\cP}
has trivial discrete component for all
\cP\in\Omega
.

When (1) holds,
S^{-1}\colon\Omega\LDmap L\Omega
is an LD map. If
\cK_{\cP}
is discrete for each
\cP\in\Omega
(equivalently
\Omega
is return discrete), (1) and (2) are equivalent to:

(3)

\Omega
is aperiodic, that is, every element of
\Omega
is nonperiodic.

Proof.

Injectivity of
\sigma^{n}
, for the appropriate
n\in\mathbb{N}
, is equivalent to
[L^{n}\cK_{\cP}:\cK_{\cP}]=1
for all
\cP\in\Omega
, by Theorem
4.8
. In turn, the latter is equivalent (since
L
is expansive) to each
\cK_{\cP}
having trivial discrete component. Clearly
\sigma
is injective if and only if
\sigma^{n}
is, so (1) and (2) are equivalent. If each
\cK_{\cP}
is discrete (equivalently,
\Omega
is return discrete, by Proposition
6.28
), having trivial discrete component is equivalent to triviality, establishing equivalence with (3). When
\sigma
(and thus
S\colon L\Omega\LDmap\Omega
) is injective,
S^{-1}
is also an LD map by Proposition
6.40
.
∎

Breaking down the definitions, the following gives another version of the above dichotomy result in the case of an individual return discrete case and FLC pattern:

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
\cP
be an FLC and return discrete pattern in the Euclidean space
E
and
L\colon E\to E
a linear expansion. Suppose that
\cP
is
L
-sub, that is, there is a pattern
\cP^{\prime}
for which
\cP^{\prime}\LI\cP
and
L\cP^{\prime}\LD\cP
. Then
\cP^{\prime}\LIs\cP
. We have that
L\cP^{\prime}\MLD\cP
, equivalently
\sigma\colon\Omega_{\cP}\to\Omega_{\cP}
is injective, if and only if
\cP
is aperiodic, that is,
\Omega_{\cP}
contains no periodic elements.

Proof.

If
\cP
is FLC and return discrete, then so is its hull
\Omega_{\cP}
(Lemmas
6.25
and
6.12
). We have that
S\colon\Omega_{L\cP^{\prime}}\LDmap\Omega_{\cP}
(which is the induced map of
L\cP^{\prime}\LD\cP
, see Proposition
6.36
) is injective if and only if
L\cP^{\prime}\MLD\cP
, see Corollary
6.41
, and
S
is injective if and only if
\sigma=S\circ L
is injective, which by Theorem
4.9
holds if and only if
\cP
is aperiodic.
∎

4.2.
Proof of Theorem
4.2

We now turn to the proof that UC
/\cK
holds. Throughout this section, we denote
\Omega\coloneqq\Omega_{\cP}
. The following is an analogue of
[
Sol98
, Lemma 2.5]
, a sufficient ‘local’ condition for UC
/\cK
, although modified so as to fix consideration at the origin. As well as simplifying matters (by removing one variable), this is crucial: a two-pointed version fails in the non-repetitive case, where we replace
\mathbf{0}
with an arbitrary
y\in E
and
y^{-1}\in E
in the left- and right-hand equations of Property
\mathrm{P}_{f}
below (recall that
y^{-1}\coloneqq L^{-1}(y)
here). Moreover, its statement is natural in light of the origin-centric Corollary
3.23
, needed later.

Lemma 4.11
.

Given
f\colon\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, define the following property:

(
\mathrm{P}_{f}
)

for all
x\in E
and
R\geq 0
, if
\cP[\mathbf{0},f(R)]=\cP[x,f(R)]
then
\cP^{\prime}[\mathbf{0},R]=\cP^{\prime}[x^{-1}+g^{-1},R]
for some

.

If
\mathrm{P}_{f}
holds for
\cP
(with predecessor
\cP^{\prime}
) then so does UC
/\cK
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathrm{P}_{f}
holds and take any
\cU\in\Omega
for which
\sigma(\cU)=\cP
. Set an arbitrary sequence
R_{n}\to\infty
(e.g.,
R_{n}=n
). Without loss of generality, in the below,
f(R_{n})\geq R_{n}
, since increasing
f(R_{n})
only makes the first equality of
\mathrm{P}_{f}
stronger (thus,
\mathrm{P}_{g}
is weaker, for

\cU\in\Omega=\Omega_{\cP^{\prime}}

(4.1)

\cU[\mathbf{0},f(R_{n})]=\cP^{\prime}[x^{-1}_{n},f(R_{n})]

for some
x_{n}\in E
. Then, substituting and using

for sufficiently large

since, by Lemma
3.10
, substitution only increases size of agreement of patches. By

, we have
\cP^{\prime}[\mathbf{0},R_{n}]=\cP^{\prime}[x^{-1}_{n}+g_{n}^{-1},R_{n}]

in

(where we recall that

Thus,
\cP^{\prime}[\mathbf{0},R_{n}]=\cP^{\prime}[x^{-1}_{n}+g_{n}^{-1},R_{n}]

and using Equation

once

One may readily (at least in the return discrete case) prove a converse to the above: if UC

\mathrm{P}_{f}
for some

, demonstrating necessity as well as sufficiency of some

5.
Examples

In this section we give examples demonstrating the range of patterns that our main recognisability results may be applied to.

Example 5.1

(Standard substitution tilings)
.

For any FLC and primitive stone inflation (with inflation
L
), such as the chair substitution (see Figure

), we may generate the usual tiling space

of tilings admitted by the substitution. As is well-known,

for any

for all
\cP\in\Omega
. Then the subdivision

-sub pattern space, and any

-sub, so the above results of course cover this simple case.

FLC but non-stone substitutions (such as the Penrose rhomb or kite and darts) are also covered, although the admitted space of tilings is not so obvious to define in full generality. Moreover, primitivity (or minimality of

\Diamond

Example 5.2

(Half and half patterns)

This example demonstrates how our results operate when applied to two very simple non-minimal patterns, the first return discrete and the second not. Take the tiling

Example 5.4

(Fibonacci bar codes)

Example 5.5

(Trivial patterns)
.

Consider the ‘void’ pattern
\cP\colon E\to\{\emptyset\}
with
\cP[x]\coloneqq\emptyset
for all

i.e., a pattern that looks the same everywhere. One may consider this as a ‘point set’ that is devoid of any points, and indeed it occurs in any hull of a non-relatively dense point set. In this case,

-sub for any linear automorphism

In the other extreme, consider the pattern

if and only if

-sub for any linear automorphism (in particular, expansion)

in the pattern

, the point at

6.
Generalised translational pattern spaces

Recall the notion of a generalised pattern in Definition

6.1.
Local indistinguishability and pattern spaces

Recall the notion of local indistinguishability (see Definition
2.8
). Of course, this defines a reflexive and transitive relation, as noted in the lemma below.

Notation 6.1
.

We write
K\cpt E
to mean that
K
is a compact subset of
E
, we write
B\nhd E
to mean that
B
is a neighbourhood of
\mathbf{0}\in E
and
B\cptn E
if
B
is a compact neighbourhood of
\mathbf{0}\in E
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Local indistinguishability is a preorder. That is, for any pattern,
\cP\LI\cP
and for any patterns
\cP_{1}
,
\cP_{2}
and
\cP_{3}
, if
\cP_{1}\LI\cP_{2}\LI\cP_{3}
then
\cP_{1}\LI\cP_{3}
.

Proof.

Given arbitrary
U\cpt E
and
x\in E
, we have
\cP[x,U]=\cP[y,U]
for
y=x
, so
\cP\LI\cP
. Supposing
\cP_{1}\LI\cP_{2}\LI\cP_{3}
, since
\cP_{1}\LI\cP_{2}
there exists some
y\in E
for which
\cP_{1}[x,U]=\cP_{2}[y,U]
. From
\cP_{2}\LI\cP_{3}
, we have
\cP_{2}[y,U]=\cP_{3}[z,U]
for some

. Thus,
\cP_{1}[x,U]=\cP_{3}[z,U]
, so
\cP_{1}\LI\cP_{3}
, as required.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

If
\cQ\LI\cP
then
\cK_{\cQ}\geq\cK_{\cP}
.

Proof.

It follows directly from the definitions that
g\in\cK_{\cP}
if and only if
\cP[x]=\cP[x+g]
for all
x\in E
(and similarly for
\cQ
). Suppose that
g\in\cK_{\cP}
. Then
\cQ[x]=\cQ[x+g]
since, for the two-point patch,
\cQ[x,\{\mathbf{0},g\}]=\cP[y,\{\mathbf{0},g\}]
for some

(as
\cQ\LI\cP
) and thus
\cQ[x]=\cP[y]=\cP[y+g]=\cQ[x+g]

was arbitrary,
\cK_{\cQ}\geq\cK_{\cP}
.
∎

In the FLC and translational dynamics setting, patterns are usually equipped with the ‘local topology’. This is most naturally defined using a uniformity; we recommend
[
Wil04
]
for a reader unfamiliar with uniformities, although our notation and terminology throughout should remind the reader of similar arguments with a metric. The properties required for a uniformity base (and its intuitive interpretation) are given shortly after the following definition.

Definition 6.4
.

For a fixed vector space
E\cong\mathbb{R}^{d}
and set
S
, let
\Omega
be a set of patterns
\cP\colon E\to S
. We write
\cQ\LIin\Omega
if, for all
x\in E
and
U\cpt E
, we have that
\cQ[x,U]=\cR[y,U]
for some
\cR\in\Omega
and
y\in E
. We call
\Omega
a (
translational
)
pattern space
if
\Omega\neq\emptyset
and is such that, for all patterns
\cQ\colon E\to S
, if
\cQ\LIin\Omega
then
\cQ\in\Omega
.

We define a base
\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
for the
local uniformity

\mathscr{U}
on any set
\Omega
of patterns
\cP\colon E\to S
by

\mathscr{U}^{\prime}\coloneqq\{\Phi(B,U)\mid B\nhd E,\ U\cpt E\},\text{ where}

\Phi(B,U)\coloneqq\{(\cP,\cQ)\in\Omega\times\Omega\mid\cP[x,U]=\cQ[y,U]\text{ for some }x,y\in B\}.

This defines the uniformity
\mathscr{U}
on
\Omega
, as usual, by letting
e\in\mathscr{U}
whenever
e\supseteq e^{\prime}
for some
e\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
. Elements
e\in\mathscr{U}
are subsets
e\subseteq\Omega\times\Omega
called
entourages
.

Given a pattern
\cP
, we define its (
translational
)
hull

\Omega_{\cP}
as the pattern space with
\cQ\in\Omega_{\cP}
if and only if
\cQ\LI\cP
.

The condition required to be a pattern space
\Omega
(colloquially:
if you’re LIin, you’re in
) ensures completeness, see Lemma
6.14
. It may be thought of as saying
\Omega
is closed under the relation of being ‘locally indistinguishable from the collection
\Omega
’ (as opposed to individual patterns of
\Omega
). This implies, but is generally stronger than,
\Omega
being closed under local indistinguishability, as shown in the following lemma and (non-) example.

Lemma 6.5
.

If
\Omega
is a pattern space,
\cP\in\Omega
and
\cQ\LI\cP
, then
\cQ\in\Omega
. In particular,
\Omega
is closed under translations by
E
.

Proof.

Taking
\cR=\cP
in Definition
6.4
, since
\cQ\LI\cP
, for arbitrary
x\in E
and
U\cpt E
, we have that
\cQ[x,U]=\cR[y,U]
for some
y\in E
, so
\cQ\LIin\Omega
, thus
\cQ\in\Omega
, as required. Of course,
\cP\LIs\cP+x
for any
x\in E
, so it follows that
\cP+x\LIin\Omega
, thus
\cP+x\in\Omega
, for all
\cP\in\Omega
and
x\in E
.
∎

Example 6.6
.

Consider the set
X
of patterns defined by tilings of
E=\mathbb{R}^{1}
, of unit interval tiles where all tiles are labelled
a
, except for at most a finite string of
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
consecutive
b
tiles. Then

is closed under local indistinguishability: whenever
\cQ\LI\cP
for some
\cP\in X
, we also have
\cQ\in X
(indeed,
\cQ
must be a translate of
\cP
in this case, or an all

tiling, so
\cQ\in X
). However,

is
not
a pattern space. Indeed, consider the tiling
\cQ
of all

tiles. Then
\cQ\LIin X
but
\cQ\notin X
. And note that

is not complete with the local uniformity (so allowing

.

\Diamond

We choose to define the geometry on a pattern space using a uniformity, rather than merely a topology, as it will be useful to have a notion of uniform continuity and Cauchy sequences/nets. The idea is that each
e\in\mathscr{U}
provides a uniform notion of ‘closeness’ across
\Omega
: we say that
\cP
and
\cQ
are
e
-close
if
(\cP,\cQ)\in e
. Note that
\cP[x,U]=\cQ[y,U]
, in defining each
\Phi(B,U)
, is equivalent to
(\cP-x)[\mathbf{0},U]=(\cQ-y)[\mathbf{0},U]
i.e., the translations
\cP-x
and
\cQ-y
agree precisely on
U
. We may consider
\cP
and
\cQ
to be particularly close if they are
e
-close for
e=\Phi(B,U)
with very small
B
and
U
containing a large ball centred at the origin. That is, up to small translations (in the small set
B
), they agree to a large distance about the origin (on the large set
U
). This is analogous to how one defines the standard tiling metric, except we omit the rather arbitrary step of converting these closeness relations into an explicit number in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. But we may still define, for instance, the ‘ball’ of points
e
-close to some
\cQ\in\Omega
, by defining, for
e\in\mathscr{U}
,

B(\cP,e)\coloneqq\{\cQ\in\Omega\mid(\cP,\cQ)\in e\};

note that, in the literature, this is usually denoted by
e[\cP]
; we use the above ‘ball notation’ as this will be more familiar to readers used to tiling metrics, and it suggests the correct analogy. Given
e\in\mathscr{U}
, there is always some
e^{\prime}=\Phi(B_{\varepsilon},B_{r})\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
with
e^{\prime}\subseteq e
, and thus whenever
\cQ
is a pattern that agrees with
\cP
to radius
r
, up to shifting
\cP
and
\cQ
by translations of norm at most
\varepsilon
, then we have
\cQ\in B(\cP,e)
. Thus, such balls always contain all patterns that, up to sufficiently small shift, agree with
\cP
to a sufficiently large distance about the origin.

Recall that a collection
\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
of entourages
e\subseteq\Omega\times\Omega
is a
base of a uniformity
on
\Omega
when:

(a)

if
e\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
then
\Delta\subseteq e
, where
\Delta=\{(\cP,\cP)\mid\cP\in\Omega\}
;

(b)

if
e
,
e^{\prime}\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
then
f\subseteq e\cap e^{\prime}
for some
f\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
;

(c)

if
e\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
then
f\circ f\subseteq e
for some
f\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
, where for subsets
u
,
v\subseteq X\times X
(i.e., ‘relations’
u
and
v
on
X
), we denote
u\circ v\coloneqq\{(x,z)\in X\times X\mid(x,y)\in u,\ (y,z)\in v\}
;

(d)

if
e\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
then
f^{-1}\subseteq e
for some
f\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
, where for a relation
u\subseteq X\times X
we denote
u^{-1}\coloneqq\{(y,x)\mid(x,y)\in u\}
;

Such a base defines a uniformity
\mathscr{U}
by letting
e\in\mathscr{U}
if
e\supseteq e^{\prime}
for some
e^{\prime}\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
, see
[
Wil04
, Definition 35.2]
for further details. The local uniformity defines a local topology on
\Omega
, as usual. Here, the easiest approach is the equivalent definition of a topology by a neighbourhood system: a set
A\subseteq\Omega
is a neighbourhood of
\cP\in\Omega
if
B(\cP,e)\subseteq A
for some
e\in\mathscr{U}
(equivalently, some
e=\Phi(B,U)\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
). The set
A
is open if it is a neighbourhood of all of its points. So the closure of some
A\subseteq\Omega
is
\overline{A}=\{\cP\in\Omega\mid B(\cP,e)\cap A\neq\emptyset\text{ for all }e\in\mathscr{U}\}
.

Lemma 6.7
.

The set
\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
is a well-defined uniformity base for any set
\Omega
of patterns
\cP\colon E\to S
.

Proof.

Clearly (a) holds, since for all
\Phi(B,U)\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
and
\cP\in\Omega
we have
\cP[\mathbf{0},U]=\cP[\mathbf{0},U]
and thus
(\cP,\cP)\in\Phi(B,U)
(since
\mathbf{0}\in B
). Next, let
e=\Phi(B,U)
,
e^{\prime}=\Phi(B^{\prime},U^{\prime})\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
. Define
K\coloneqq B\cap B^{\prime}
,
V\coloneqq U\cup U^{\prime}
and
f\coloneqq\Phi(K,V)\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
. If
(\cP,\cQ)\in f
then
\cP[x,V]=\cQ[y,V]
for
x
,
y\in K
. Since
x
,
y\in K\subseteq B
,
B^{\prime}
, and
U
,
U^{\prime}\subseteq V
, we have
(\cP,\cQ)\in e
and
e^{\prime}
, so
e\cap e^{\prime}\supseteq f
, proving (b).

Given
e=\Phi(B,U)\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
, take any
B^{\prime}\cptn E
satisfying
B^{\prime}-B^{\prime}\subseteq B
and let
U^{\prime}=U-B^{\prime}
. Suppose that
(\cP,\cQ)
,
(\cQ,\cR)\in f\coloneqq\Phi(B^{\prime},U^{\prime})
. Thus, there exist
x_{1}
,
y_{1}
,
x_{2}
,
y_{2}\in B^{\prime}
for which

\cP[x_{1},U-B^{\prime}]=\cQ[y_{1},U-B^{\prime}]\ \text{ and }\cQ[x_{2},U-B^{\prime}]=\cR[y_{2},U-B^{\prime}].

Shifting the first equation by
-y_{1}
and the second by
-x_{2}
(see Lemma
2.5
(3)), we have

\cP[x_{1}-y_{1},U]=\cQ[\mathbf{0},U]=\cR[y_{2}-x_{2},U].

Since
x_{1}-y_{1}
,
y_{2}-x_{2}\in B^{\prime}-B^{\prime}\subseteq B
, we have
(\cP,\cR)\in\Phi(B,U)=e
and thus
f\circ f\subseteq e
, showing (c). Finally, by definition each

is symmetric i.e.,
(\cP,\cQ)\in e
if and only if
(\cQ,\cP)\in e
, confirming (d).
∎

The uniformity
\mathscr{U}
has a countable base, for example by the entourages
e_{n}=\Phi(B_{1/n},B_{n})
, thus, the hull
\Omega
is (pseudo)metrisable, see
[
Wil04
, Theorem 38.3]
(this can also be shown directly, by construction, as one does for the tiling metric), and metrisable when
\Omega
is Hausdorff (of which, see Section
6.4
later). Thus, concepts including closures, continuity and compactness can be replaced with sequential closures, sequential continuity and sequential compactness, respectively. Pattern spaces, in almost all cases of imaginable interest (namely when
\Omega
is a countable union of hulls
\Omega_{\cP}
for
\cP\in\Omega
), are second-countable (the proof is left as an exercise, as it is not used elsewhere). As usual — for instance, by considering the pattern space
\Omega
of
all
possible patterns
\cP\colon E\to S
or some suitable, perhaps Hausdorff, sub-pattern space of this — hulls are orbit closures:

Lemma 6.8
.

Given any pattern space
\Omega
and
\cP\in\Omega
, we have that
\cQ\in\overline{(\cP+E)}
if and only if
\cQ\LI\cP
. Thus, in particular, for any
\cP\in\Omega
we have an equality of uniform spaces
\Omega_{\cP}=\overline{\cP+E}
, the latter with uniformity induced as a subspace of
\Omega
.

Proof.

Firstly, suppose that
\cQ\LI\cP\in\Omega
(so
\cQ\in\Omega
too, by Lemma
6.5
) and take an arbitrarily small neighbourhood
B(\cQ,e)
of
\cQ
, where
e=\Phi(B,U)\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
. As
\cQ\LI\cP
, there exists some
y\in E
for which
\cQ[\mathbf{0},U]=\cP[y,U]=(\cP-y)[\mathbf{0},U]
. Since
\mathbf{0}\in B
, we have
(\cQ,\cP-y)\in e
, that is,
\cP-y\in B(\cQ,e)
, so
(\cP+E)\cap B(\cQ,e)\neq\emptyset
hence
\cQ\in\overline{\cP+E}
.

Conversely, suppose that
\cQ\in\overline{(\cP+E)}\subseteq\Omega
and take arbitrary
U\cpt E
,
x\in E
. Since
\cQ\in\overline{\cP+E}
, we must have that
(\cQ,\cP+z)\in e\coloneqq\Phi(B_{1},(U+x)+B_{1})
for some
z\in E
i.e.,
\cQ[b,(U+x)+B_{1})=(\cP+z)[b^{\prime},(U+x)+B_{1}]
for
b
,
b^{\prime}\in B_{1}=B(\mathbf{0},1)
. By Lemma
2.5
(2),
\cQ[x+b,U+B_{1}]=(\cP+z)[x+b^{\prime},U+B_{1}]=\cP[x+b^{\prime}-z,U+B_{1}]
and thus
\cQ[x,U]=\cP[x+(b^{\prime}-b)-z,U]
by Lemma
2.5
(3). Hence,
\cQ[x,U]=\cP[y,U]
for
y=x+(b^{\prime}-b)-z
. Since the patch
\cQ[x,U]
in
\cQ
was arbitrary and appears (up to translation) in
\cP
, we have
\cQ\LI\cP
, as required.

Finally, by the definition of the hull,
\cQ\in\Omega_{\cP}
if and only if
\cQ\LI\cP
, which by the above is equivalent to
\cQ\in\overline{\cP+E}
, where the uniformity on
\Omega_{\cP}
is, again by definition, simply the subspace uniformity of
\Omega
restricted to
\Omega_{\cP}
.
∎

Next we show that translation acts continuously on pattern spaces, making them dynamical systems. We also record the relatively obvious fact that dilation by an affine map defines a (uniform) homeomorphism between pattern spaces:

Lemma 6.9
.

The action of
E
on a pattern space
\Omega
, given by
x\cdot\cQ\coloneqq\cQ+x
, defines a continuous group action
E\times\Omega\to\Omega
of
E
on
\Omega
. For any affine automorphism
A\colon E\to E
, the map
A\colon\Omega\to A\Omega=\{A\cQ\mid\cQ\in\Omega\}
, given by
\cQ\mapsto A\cQ
, is a uniformly continuous homeomorphism of pattern spaces. In the particular case of a hull, this is a homeomorphism of hulls
A\colon\Omega_{\cP}\to\Omega_{A\cP}
.

Proof.

Clearly translation is a group action as
\Omega
is closed under translation (Lemma
6.5
). To see that it is continuous, let
x\in E
and
\cP\in\Omega
be arbitrary. To define an arbitrarily small neighbourhood of
x\cdot\cP=\cP+x\in\Omega
, take an arbitrary
e=\Phi(B,U)\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
. Take any
B^{\prime}\nhd E
with
B^{\prime}+B^{\prime}\subseteq B
and define
e^{\prime}\coloneqq\Phi(B^{\prime},U^{\prime})
with
U^{\prime}\coloneqq U-x
. We will show that, for all
y\in x+B^{\prime}
and
\cQ\in B(\cP,e^{\prime})
, we have
y\cdot\cQ=\cQ+y\in B(\cP+x,e)
, establishing continuity.

Indeed, if
\cQ\in B(\cP,e^{\prime})
, then there exist
b_{1}
,
b_{2}\in B^{\prime}
with
\cP[b_{1},U^{\prime}]=\cQ[b_{2},U^{\prime}]
. For arbitrary
y=x+b^{\prime}\in x+B^{\prime}
this gives (by definition of the translated pattern) the middle equality of

(\cP+x)[b_{1},U^{\prime}+x]=(\cP+x)[b_{1}+x,U^{\prime}]=(\cQ+x)[b_{2}+x,U^{\prime}]=(\cQ+x)[b_{2},U^{\prime}+x],

where the outside equalities use Lemma
2.5
(2). From
U=U^{\prime}+x
then
x=y-b^{\prime}
, we have

(\cP+x)[b_{1},U]=(\cQ+x)[b_{2},U]=((\cQ+y)-b^{\prime})[b_{2},U]=(\cQ+y)[b_{2}+b^{\prime},U].

Since
b_{1}\in B^{\prime}\subset B
and
b_{2}+b^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}+B^{\prime}\subseteq B
, we have that
\cQ+y\in B(\cP+x,e)
, as required.

Next, let
A(x)=L(x)+z
and
e=\Phi(B,U)\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
. Let
e^{\prime}\coloneqq\Phi(L^{-1}(B),A^{-1}(U))\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
. We claim that, if
\cQ\in B(\cP,e^{\prime})
, then
A\cQ\in B(A\cP,e)
. Indeed,
\cP[L^{-1}b_{1},A^{-1}U]=\cQ[L^{-1}b_{2},A^{-1}U]
for some
b_{1}
,
b_{2}\in B
. By Lemma
2.5
,
(A\cP)[AL^{-1}b_{1},LA^{-1}U]=(A\cQ)[AL^{-1}b_{2},LA^{-1}U]
. Now,
AL^{-1}x=x+z
and
LA^{-1}x=x-z
. Thus,
(A\cP)[b_{1}+z,U-z]=(A\cQ)[b_{2}+z,U-z]
so
(A\cP)[b_{1},U]=(A\cQ)[b_{2},U]
by Lemma
2.5
, hence
A\cQ\in B(A\cP,e)
, showing that
\cP\mapsto A\cP\in A\Omega
is uniformly continuous.

Note that
A\Omega
is a pattern space. Indeed, by Lemma
2.5
(2), for arbitrary patterns
\cQ
and
\cR
we have that
(A\cQ)[x,U]=(A\cR)[y,U]
if and only if
\cQ[A^{-1}x,L^{-1}U]=\cR[A^{-1}y,L^{-1}U]
. It quickly follows that an arbitrary pattern
A\cQ
satisfies
A\cQ\LIin A\Omega
if and only if
\cQ\LIin\Omega
, thus
\cQ\in\Omega
and
A\cQ\in A\Omega
, as required. Since
A\colon\Omega\to A\Omega
and
A^{-1}\colon A\Omega\to\Omega
are continuous, by the above, they are homeomorphisms of pattern spaces. Similarly,
\cQ\LI\cR
if and only if
A\cQ\LI A\cR
. Thus, in the case of a hull,
A\Omega_{\cP}=\{A\cQ\mid\cQ\in\Omega_{\cP}\}=\{A\cQ\mid\cQ\LI\cP\}=\{A\cQ\mid A\cQ\LI A\cP\}=\Omega_{A\cP}
.
∎

Remark 6.10
.

) between pattern spaces, our general version is sufficient. Note that, for patterns defined by Delone sets (or the alternative definition of a pattern from a tiling, in Example
2.3
), the action
f
on labels is the identity and no adjustment is even needed. These considerations may be of interest in generalising to settings that take into account rotational structure, and perhaps to non-FLC pinwheel-like patterns.

\Diamond

6.2.
FLC and compactness

In this section we extend a standard result, that compactness is equivalent to FLC, for pattern spaces. For compactness, we use the equivalent condition of being complete and totally bounded (
[
Wil04
, Theorem 39.9]
). Recall that a uniform space is
totally bounded
if, for all
e=\Phi(B,U)\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
, there exists some finite cover
\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\}
of
\Omega
such that, for each
i
, we have
A_{i}\times A_{i}\subseteq e
. In other words, it is a covering of
\Omega
by finitely many
e
-small sets. To extend to pattern spaces, rather than just hulls (or orbit closures) of single patterns, we define the notion of FLC also for pattern spaces:

Definition 6.11
.

A pattern space
\Omega
is of
finite local complexity
(or is
FLC
) if, for each
U\cpt E
, there are finitely many patterns
\cP_{1}
,
\cP_{2}
, …,
\cP_{n}
(
n
possibly depending on
U
) and compact subsets
K_{1}(U)
,
K_{2}(U)
, …,
K_{n}(U)\cpt E
for which, for any
\cQ\in\Omega
and any
x\in E
, there exists some
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
y\in K_{i}(U)
with
\cQ[x,U]=\cP_{i}[y,U]
.

The above may be read as: all patches of all patterns in
\Omega
appear within a bounded distance of the origin in at least one of the patterns
\cP_{1}
, …,
\cP_{n}
. Thus, for a pattern space of tilings (in-radii of all tiles positively bounded from below), this is readily seen to be equivalent to the following familiar property: for each
r\geq 0
, only finitely many patches
\cQ\sqcap B_{r}
, up to translation, over all
\cQ\in\Omega
(which, in turn, follows from just the
r=0
case i.e., if there are only finitely many tiles and finitely many ways for them to meet at a point). The value of
n
in Definition
6.11
may need to depend on
U
(such as for
\Omega
in Example
6.6
). Of course, for hulls, this notion is consistent:

Lemma 6.12
.

A pattern
\cP
is FLC if and only if its hull
\Omega_{\cP}
is FLC.

Proof.

If
\cP
is FLC then, trivially, so is
\Omega_{\cP}
. Indeed, for
U\cpt E
, we may take
n=1
,
\cP_{1}\coloneqq\cP
and
K_{1}(U)\coloneqq K(U)
from Definition
6.11
. Conversely, suppose that
\Omega_{\cP}
is FLC and
U\cpt E
. Let
\cP_{1}
, …,
\cP_{n}\in\Omega_{\cP}
,
K_{1}=K_{1}(U)
, …,
K_{n}=K_{n}(U)
be as in Definition
6.11
. Since each
\cP_{i}\LI\cP
, there exist
z_{i}\in E
for which
\cP[z_{i},K_{i}+U]=\cP_{i}[\mathbf{0},K_{i}+U]
. Let
K=K(U)\coloneqq\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}(K_{i}+z_{i})\cpt E
, which we claim satisfies the requirement of Definition
2.6
. Indeed, given
x\in E
, by Definition
6.11
there exists some
y_{i}\in K_{i}
with
\cP[x,U]=\cP_{i}[y_{i},U]
. Since
\cP[z_{i},K_{i}+U]=\cP_{i}[\mathbf{0},K_{i}+U]
, shifting by
y_{i}\in K_{i}
we have
\cP[y_{i}+z_{i},U]=\cP_{i}[y_{i},U]=\cP[x,U]
. Since
y_{i}+z_{i}\in K_{i}+z_{i}\subseteq K
and
\cP[y_{i}+z_{i},U]=\cP[x,U]
was an arbitrary
U
-patch in
\cP
, we have verified Definition
2.6
.
∎

Lemma 6.13
.

A pattern space
\Omega
is totally bounded if and only if it is FLC.

Proof.

Suppose that
\Omega
is totally bounded and let
U\cpt E
be arbitrary. Fix any
B\cptn E
, say,
B=B(\mathbf{0},1)
. By total boundedness, there is a finite covering
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
\Omega
with each
A_{i}\times A_{i}\subseteq e\coloneqq\Phi(B,U-B)
. Without loss of generality, each
A_{i}\neq\emptyset
, so choose any
\cP_{i}\in A_{i}
for each
i
. We claim that taking each
K_{i}(U)\coloneqq B-B
satisfies the requirement for FLC in Definition
6.11
. Indeed, take any
\cQ\in\Omega
and
x\in E
. Since the
A_{i}
cover
\Omega
, we have that
\cQ-x\in A_{i}
for some
i
, thus
(\cQ-x,\cP_{i})\in\Phi(B,U-B)
so that
(\cQ-x)[b,U-B]=\cP_{i}[b^{\prime},U-B]
for some
b
,
b^{\prime}\in B
. By Lemma
2.5
, shifting by
-b\in-B
,

\cQ[x,U]=(\cQ-x)[\mathbf{0},U]=\cP_{i}[b^{\prime}-b,U].

Since
b^{\prime}-b\in B-B=K_{i}(U)
, we have shown that
\Omega
is FLC.

Conversely, suppose that
\Omega
is FLC and take an arbitrary
e=\Phi(B,U)\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
where, without loss of generality (taking
B
smaller if needed),
B
is compact. Apply Definition
6.11
to
(U+B)
-patches, defining patterns
\cP_{1}
, …,
\cP_{n}
and
K_{1}=K_{1}(U+B)
, …,
K_{n}=K_{n}(U+B)\cpt E
. Each
K_{i}
may be covered with finitely many translates
-B+z_{i}^{1}
, …,
-B+z_{i}^{n_{i}}
of
-B
. For
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,n_{i}\}
, we define

A_{i}^{j}\coloneqq\{\cQ\in\Omega\mid\cQ[b,U]=\cP_{i}[z_{i}^{j},U]\text{ for some }b\in B\}.

We claim that the
A_{i}^{j}
cover
\Omega
. Indeed, given
\cQ\in\Omega
, by definition of the
K_{i}
we have
\cQ[\mathbf{0},U+B]=\cP_{i}[y,U+B]
for some
y\in K_{i}
. By our definition of the
z_{i}^{j}
we have
y=-b+z_{i}^{j}\in-B+z_{i}^{j}
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,n_{i}\}
and
b\in B
. Thus, shifting by
-y+z_{i}^{j}=b\in B
, we have
\cQ[b,U]=\cP_{i}[z_{i}^{j},U]
so that
\cQ\in A_{i}^{j}
, showing the
A_{i}^{j}
cover
\Omega
. Moreover, each
A_{i}^{j}\times A_{i}^{j}\subseteq e
since, given
\cQ_{1}
,
\cQ_{2}\in A_{i}^{j}
, we have
\cQ_{1}[b_{1},U]=\cP_{i}[z_{i}^{j},U]=\cQ_{2}[b_{2},U]
for
b_{i}\in B
, so
(\cQ_{1},\cQ_{2})\in\Phi(B,U)
, as required.
∎

Lemma 6.14
.

Any pattern space
\Omega
is complete.

Proof.

Let
(\cQ_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
be a Cauchy net in
\Omega
, for a directed set
\mathcal{I}
(in fact, it is sufficient to only consider Cauchy sequences, see the comments below Lemma
6.7
, but the argument is essentially identical nonetheless). We wish to show that
(\cQ_{i})_{i}
is convergent. Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
e_{n}\coloneqq\Phi(B_{2^{-(n+1)}},B_{n+2}))\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
. Since
(\cQ_{i})_{i}
is Cauchy, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we may find
\overline{n}\in\mathcal{I}
with
(\cQ_{u},\cQ_{v})\in e_{n}
for all
u
,
v\geq\overline{n}
. We can obviously arrange that
\overline{m}\geq\overline{n}
for
m\geq n
. Then, in particular, we have
(\cQ_{\overline{n}},\cQ_{\overline{n+1}})\in e_{n}
, thus
\cQ_{\overline{n}}[x_{n},B_{n+2}]=\cQ_{\overline{n+1}}[y_{n},B_{n+2}]
for
x_{n}
,
y_{n}\in B_{2^{-(n+1)}}
. Shifting by
-y_{n}\in B_{1}
,

(6.1)

\cQ_{\overline{n}}[x_{n}-y_{n},B_{n+1}]=\cQ_{\overline{n+1}}[\mathbf{0},B_{n+1}].

Define
z_{n}\coloneqq x_{n}-y_{n}
and
z_{\geq n}\coloneqq\sum_{i=n}^{\infty}z_{n}
, which exists since each
\|z_{n}\|\leq 2^{-n}
. Define
\cQ\in\Omega
by setting
\cQ[\mathbf{0},B_{n}]=P_{n}\coloneqq\cQ_{\overline{n}}[z_{\geq n},B_{n}]
i.e.,
\cQ[u]\coloneqq\cQ_{\overline{n}}[z_{\geq n}+u]
once
n\geq\|u\|
. Firstly, we check that this is a well-defined element of
\Omega
. This just requires checking that the patches are nested (that is, the restriction of
P_{n+1}
to
B_{n}
is
P_{n}
). Indeed, if these are nested, then we clearly have a well-defined pattern
\cQ\colon E\to S
. To show
\cQ\in\Omega
, take any patch
\cQ[x,U]
and
n
sufficiently large so that
U+x\subseteq B_{n}
. For
\cR=\cQ_{\overline{n}}\in\Omega
, we have that
\cQ[\mathbf{0},B_{n}]=P_{n}=\cR[z_{\geq n},B_{n}]
and, since
U+x\subseteq B_{n}
, we have
\cQ[\mathbf{0},U+x]=\cR[z_{\geq n},U+x]
, equivalently
\cQ[x,U]=\cR[z_{\geq n}+x,U]
. So the arbitrary patch
\cQ[x,U]
appears in
\cR\in\Omega
, hence
\cQ\LIin\Omega
and thus
\cQ\in\Omega
.

Next, we show that
\cQ_{i}\to\cQ
. Indeed, let
e=\Phi(B,U)\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
be arbitrary and
n\geq 1
be such that
B_{2^{3-n}}\subseteq B
and
B_{n}\supseteq U
. By definition of
\overline{n}\in\mathcal{I}
, for
i\geq\overline{n}
we have
(\cQ_{i},\cQ_{\overline{n}})\in e_{n}
, that is,

\cQ_{i}[x,B_{n+2}]=\cQ_{\overline{n}}[y,B_{n+2}]

for
x
,
y\in B_{2^{-(n+1)}}\subseteq B_{2^{1-n}}
, where
z_{\geq n}\in B_{2^{1-n}}
too, so
z_{\geq n}-y\in B_{2^{2-n}}\subseteq B_{2}
. Thus, shifting by this vector,

\cQ_{i}[x+z_{\geq n}-y,B_{n}]=\cQ_{\overline{n}}[z_{\geq n},B_{n}]=P_{n}=\cQ[\mathbf{0},B_{n}].

Since
x+z_{\geq n}-y\in B_{2^{3-n}}\subseteq B
and
B_{n}\supseteq U
, we have
\cQ_{i}\in B(\cQ,e)
. Since
e\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
and
i\geq\overline{n}
were arbitrary, it follows that
\cQ_{i}\to\cQ
, as required.
∎

Because a uniform space is compact if and only if it is complete and totally bounded, we obtain the following from Lemmas
6.13
and
6.14
(and Lemma
6.12
for hulls):

Corollary 6.15
.

A pattern space
\Omega
is compact if and only if it is FLC. The translational hull
\Omega_{\cP}
of a pattern
\cP
is compact if and only if
\cP
is FLC.

Example 6.16
.

If
\cT
is defined (as in Example
2.2
) for a pinwheel tiling, which is not FLC (with respect to translations), the translational hull
\Omega_{\cT}
(as defined here, with the local uniformity) is complete but not totally bounded or compact. Of course, in this case the local topology on
\Omega_{\mathcal{T}}
is unnatural, it requires close tilings to have perfect agreement to a large radius up to a small

6.3.
Minimality of pattern spaces

Below, we give recover the statement in the generalised pattern space setting that repetitivity corresponds to minimality.

Definition 6.17
.

A pattern space
\Omega
is
repetitive
if, for all
U\cpt E
, there exists some
R(U)\cpt E
for which, for all
\cP
,
\cQ\in\Omega
, there exists some
v\in R(U)
with
\cP[\mathbf{0},U]=\cQ[v,U]
.

This simply says that any patch from a pattern of
\Omega
appears with bounded gaps in all patterns of
\Omega
. Trivially, if
\Omega
is repetitive then it is FLC. Indeed, just take
n=1
and any
\cP_{1}\in\Omega
for all
U\cpt E
in Definition
6.11
, with
K_{1}(U)=R(U)
.

Lemma 6.18
.

A pattern
\cP
is repetitive if and only if
\Omega_{\cP}
is repetitive.

Proof.

Suppose that
\cP
is repetitive and let
R(U)
be as in Definition
2.7
. Let
\cU
,
\cV\in\Omega=\Omega_{\cP}
be arbitrary, so
\cU
,
\cV\LI\cP
. Then there exist
x
,
y\in E
with
\cU[\mathbf{0},U]=\cP[x,U]
and
\cV[\mathbf{0},U+R(U)]=\cP[y,U+R(U)]
. Since
\cP
is repetitive,
\cP[x,U]=\cP[y+v,U]
for some
v\in R(U)
. Shifting by
v
, we have
\cV[v,U]=\cP[y+v,U]=\cP[x,U]=\cU[\mathbf{0},U]
, so
\Omega
is repetitive.

Suppose, instead, that
\Omega
is repetitive,
U\cpt E
and
R(U)
is as in Definition
6.17
. Let
x
,
y\in E
be arbitrary. Since
\cP-x
and
\cP-y\in\Omega
, there exists
v\in R(U)
with
(\cP-x)[\mathbf{0},U]=(\cP-y)[v,U]
, equivalently
\cP[x,U]=\cP[y+v,U]
, so
\cP
is repetitive.
∎

Proposition 6.19
.

Let
\Omega
be a pattern space. Then (1) implies (2) and (2) is equivalent to (3). If
\Omega
is FLC, then (1) and (2) (and thus all three) are equivalent.

(1)

\Omega
is repetitive;

(2)

for all
\cP
,
\cQ\in\Omega
, we have that
\cP\LIs\cQ
;

(3)

for all
\cP
,
\cQ\in\Omega
, we have that
\cP\in\overline{(\cQ+E)}
, that is, all orbits are dense, or
\Omega
is
minimal
.

Proof.

Suppose (1) and let
R(U)
be as in Definition
6.17
. Take arbitrary
\cP
,
\cQ\in\Omega
,
x\in E
and
U\cpt E
. Then, by repetitivity,
\cP[x,U]=(\cP-x)[\mathbf{0},U]=\cQ[v,U]
for some
v\in R(U)
. Since
\cP[x,U]
was an arbitrary patch in
\cP
, we have
\cP\LI\cQ
, with
\cQ\LI\cP
proved analogously. Given (2), if
\cP\LIs\cQ
then
\cP\LI\cQ
so that
\cP\in\overline{(\cQ+E)}
by Lemma
6.8
, establishing (3). Given (3), we have that
\cP\LI\cQ
from
\cP\in\overline{(\cQ+E)}
and
\cQ\LI\cP
from
\cQ\in\overline{(\cP+E)}
, again using Lemma
6.8
, so (2) holds.

So we just need to show that (2) implies (1) when
\Omega
is FLC. Suppose that all elements of
\Omega
are locally isomorphic but that
\Omega
is not repetitive. Then there exists
U\cpt E
for which, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there are
\cP_{n}
,
\cQ_{n}\in\Omega
with
\cP_{n}[\mathbf{0},U]\neq\cQ_{n}[v,U]
for all
v\in B_{n}
. Using compactness, find a subsequence so that
(\cP_{n},\cQ_{n})\to(\cP,\cQ)
converges (with
\cP
,
\cQ\in\Omega
). We will show that
\cP\LI\cQ
fails. Indeed, otherwise,
\cP[\mathbf{0},U]=\cQ[y,U]
for some
y\in E
, say
y\in B_{N}
. Let
n\geq N+4
be such that
\cP_{n}[b_{1},U+B_{3}]=\cP[b_{1}^{\prime},U+B_{3}]
for
b_{1}
,
b_{1}^{\prime}\in B_{1}
and
\cQ_{n}[b_{2},U+B_{N+3}]=\cQ[b_{2}^{\prime},U+B_{N+3}]
for
b_{2}
,
b_{2}^{\prime}\in B_{1}
. Since
\cP_{n}\to\cP
and
\cQ_{n}\to\cQ
on a subsequence, such an

exists. Then, shifting the equation for
\cP
by
-b_{1}^{\prime}\in B_{1}
and the equation for
\cQ
by

, we have

\cP_{n}[b_{1}-b_{1}^{\prime},U+B_{2}]=\cP[\mathbf{0},U+B_{2}]=\cQ[y,U+B_{2}]=\cQ_{n}[y+b_{2}-b_{2}^{\prime},U+B_{2}].

Shifting by
-(b_{1}-b_{1}^{\prime})\in B_{2}

\cP_{n}[\mathbf{0},U]=\cQ_{n}[v,U]
for

Remark 6.20
.

Repetitive pattern spaces are hulls. Indeed, if
\Omega
is repetitive, then by (2) (and Lemma

) we have that
\Omega=\Omega_{\cP}
is the hull of any
\cP\in\Omega
.

We cannot drop the assumption of FLC in the proof of (2) implying (1). The non-FLC (thus non-repetitive) ‘trivial’ pattern
\cP
where all points are inequivalent (see Example

) has hull

, so
\cQ=\cP+x\LIs\cP
for all

.

\Diamond

6.4.
Separation properties

So far in this section it has not been prohibited — and nor has it complicated matters — that our pattern spaces could be non-Hausdorff. Since we usually need Hausdorff (e.g., throughout Section
4
), we give simple conditions for it in basic terms of patterns. Of course, for all standard examples such as tilings and Delone sets (or even merely uniformly discrete point sets), their hull will be Hausdorff. But the following absurd example has not been excluded from any definitions so far in this section:

Example 6.21
.

Let
\cP\colon\mathbb{R}\to\{B,W\}
be defined by
\cP[x]=B
if
x\in\mathbb{Q}
and
\cP[x]=W
if
x\notin\mathbb{Q}
. We may think of it as the ‘decoration’ of
E=\mathbb{R}
by marking the rationals. Clearly
\cP
has periods
\cK=\mathbb{Q}
. In particular,
\cP
is FLC: every
U
-patch has centre contained in
K
, for any
K\cpt\mathbb{R}
with non-empty interior. All patterns locally indistinguishable from
\cP
are translates of
\cP
and
\Omega_{\cP}\cong\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}
, with the non-Hausdorff trivial (or ‘indiscrete’) topology.

\Diamond

Wild objects like the above are not of interest here (although, amusingly,
\cP
above
is

L
-sub for any
L(x)=qx
with non-zero
q\in\mathbb{Q}
). We first introduce a strong notion of separation that will force the hull to be Hausdorff, and groups of periods of patterns of the hull all being discrete. Then we give a weaker notion, which is equivalent to the hull being Hausdorff in the FLC case. Both notions are also extended to general pattern spaces.

Definition 6.22
.

A pattern
\cP
is
return discrete
if there exists
B\nhd E
and
V\cpt E
for which, for all
x\in E
, if
\cP[x,V]=\cP[x+b,V]
for
b\in B
then
b=\mathbf{0}
.

Similarly, a pattern space
\Omega
is
return discrete
if there exists some
B\nhd E
and
V\cpt E
for which, for all
\cU\in\Omega
, if
\cU[\mathbf{0},V]=\cU[b,V]
for
b\in B
then
b=\mathbf{0}
.

In words, return vectors between sufficiently large patches are bounded from below in norm. A pattern space of tilings (with in-radii of tiles all positively bounded from below) is return discrete. Similarly, a pattern space of Delone sets (whose uniform discreteness constants are bounded from below and relatively dense constants are bounded from above e.g., for the hull of a Delone set) is easily shown to be return discrete. A notable class of examples that are not return discrete are uniformly discrete but non-relatively dense point sets. These are nonetheless sufficiently behaved to be be covered by many of our main results and satisfy the following:

Definition 6.23
.

A pattern
\cP
is
well-separated
if there exists
V\cpt E
for which, for all
x
,
y\in E
, if
\cP[x]\neq\cP[y]
then there exists
B\nhd E
with
\cP[x+b,V]\neq\cP[y+b^{\prime},V]
for all
b
,
b^{\prime}\in B
.

Similarly, a pattern space
\Omega
is
well-separated
if there exists
V\cpt E
for which, for all
\cU
,
\cV\in\Omega
, if
\cU[\mathbf{0}]\neq\cV[\mathbf{0}]
then there exists
B\nhd E
for which
\cU[b,V]\neq\cV[b^{\prime},V]
for all
b
,
b^{\prime}\in B
.

Note that the neighbourhood
B
of
\mathbf{0}
in the above definition can depend on the local patches in question (but is fixed in the definition of return discrete). A simple consequence of a pattern space being Hausdorff (c.f. Example
6.21
) is the following:

Lemma 6.24
.

For a Hausdorff pattern space
\Omega
, for all
\cP\in\Omega
we have that
\cK_{\cP}
is closed in
E
.

Proof.

Let
\cP\in\Omega
and
(g_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be any sequence in
\cK\coloneqq\cK_{\cP}
with
g_{n}\to g\in E
convergent in
E
. By Lemma
6.9
, translation
E\times\Omega\to\Omega
is continuous, so
\cP+g_{n}\to\cP+g
. Moreover,
\cP+g_{n}=\cP
for all
n
since each
g_{n}\in\cK
, so
\cP+g_{n}=\cP\to\cP
. If
\Omega
is Hausdorff, the limit of
(\cP+g_{n})_{n}
must be unique, so
\cP=\cP+g
and thus
g\in\cK
too. Since
(g_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
was an arbitrary sequence in
\cK
that converges in
E
, we have that
\cK\leqslant E
is topologically closed, as required.
∎

Lemma 6.25
.

A pattern
\cP
is return discrete if and only if
\Omega_{\cP}
is return discrete. Similarly,
\cP
is well-separated if and only if
\Omega_{\cP}
is well-separated.

Proof.

Let
\cP
be return discrete and take
B\nhd E
and
V\cpt E
, as in Definition
6.22

Lemma 6.26
.

Suppose
\Omega
is a return discrete pattern space. Then it is well-separated. Moreover, for

as in Definition
6.22
, for each
\cQ\in\Omega

\cK_{\cQ}\cap B=\{\mathbf{0}\}
. In particular, each
\cK_{\cQ}
is discrete.

Proof.

Let
V\cpt E
and
B\nhd E
be as in Definition
6.22
. Without loss of generality,
\mathbf{0}\in V
and we may take
B
compact (by taking
V
larger and
B

\cU
,
\cV\in\Omega
with
\cU[\mathbf{0}]\neq\cV[\mathbf{0}]

b_{n}
,

\cU[b_{n},V+B]=\cV[b_{n}^{\prime},V+B]

Now suppose that

Proposition 6.27
.

If
\Omega
is a well-separated pattern space then
\Omega
is Hausdorff. Conversely, if
\Omega
is FLC and
\Omega
is Hausdorff, then
\Omega
is well-separated.

Thus,
\Omega
is compact Hausdorff if and only if it is FLC and well-separated.

Proof.

Let
\Omega
be well-separated and
V\cpt E
be as in Definition
6.23
. Take arbitrary
\cU
,
\cV\in\Omega
with
\cU\neq\cV
. Thus,
\cU[x]\neq\cV[x]
for some
x\in E
. By the definition of being well-separated, there exists
B\nhd E
with
(\cU-x)[b,V]\neq(\cV-x)[b^{\prime},V]
for all
b
,
b^{\prime}\in B
. Equivalently,
\cU[b,V+x]\neq\cV[b^{\prime},V+x]
for all
b
,
b^{\prime}\in B
. Thus,
\cU
and
\cV
are not
e
-close, for
e=\Phi(B,V+x)\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
. Since the uniformity separates
\cU
and
\cV
, we have that
\Omega
is Hausdorff.

Conversely, suppose that
\Omega
is FLC (equivalently, compact) and Hausdorff. For a contradiction, suppose that
\Omega
is not well-separated. Equivalently, for arbitrarily large
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we may find
\cU_{n}
,
\cV_{n}\in\Omega
satisfying the following:
\cU_{n}[b_{1},n]=\cV_{n}[b_{2},n]
for arbitrarily small
b_{1}
,
b_{2}\in E
, yet
\cU_{n}[\mathbf{0}]\neq\cV_{n}[\mathbf{0}]
. Using compactness, take a subsequence for which
\cU_{n}\to\cQ\in\Omega
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\cQ[\mathbf{0},N]
wholly contains all
4
-patches in
\cQ
, that is, for all
x\in E
there exists some
y\in B_{N-4}
with
\cQ[x,4]=\cQ[y,4]
. Such an

exists, since
\cQ
is FLC (which is easily shown directly from
\cQ\in\Omega
for
\Omega
FLC, or by the proof of Proposition
6.29
). There exists an index
n\geq N+5
for which

(6.2)

\cU_{n}[\mathbf{0},N+5]=\cQ[b,N+5],

for some
b\in E
, which we can make arbitrarily small by convergence
\cU_{n}\to\cQ
, but in particular
b\in B_{1}
. Moreover, by assumption, we have that

(6.3)

\cU_{n}[b_{1},n]=\cV_{n}[b_{2},n]

for arbitrarily small
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
, but also with
\cU_{n}[\mathbf{0}]\neq\cV_{n}[\mathbf{0}]
. Fix some particular

and
v=b_{2}
in Equation
6.3
, with
u
,

. Thus,
\cV_{n}[\mathbf{0},n-1]=\cU_{n}[u-v,n-1]
. Since

and

6.2
and then the last equality,

\cQ[b+(u-v),N+3]=\cU_{n}[u-v,N+3]=\cV_{n}[\mathbf{0},N+3].

Now, from Equation
6.3
, we have other

,
b_{2}\in B_{\varepsilon}
for

\varepsilon\leq 1
) satisfying
\cV_{n}[\mathbf{0},N+4]=\cU_{n}[b_{1}-b_{2},N+4]
and thus, by the above,

(6.4)

\cQ[b+(u-v),N+3]=\cV_{n}[\mathbf{0},N+3]=\cU_{n}[b_{1}-b_{2},N+3]=\cQ[b+(b_{1}-b_{2}),N+3],

where the final equality comes from shifting Equation
6.2
by
b_{1}-b_{2}\in B_{2}
. Now, since

, we have that
\cQ[b+(u-v),N+3]
contains a (translated) copy of
\cQ[\mathbf{0},N]
, which by assumption wholly contains all
4
-patches in
\cQ
, so the same is true for

. The displacement from this patch to itself in Equation

is

. It follows from Lemma

(in short: we have a sufficiently short return vector of a patch containing all
4
-patches, which is thus a period) that

, where we recall that

can be arbitrarily small. Since we assumed that

we have that

is closed, so

too. But then,

\cU_{n}[\mathbf{0},N+3]=\cQ[b,N+3]=\cQ[b+(u-v),N+3]=\cV_{n}[\mathbf{0},N+3]

where the first equality is from Equation

, the second from

and the third from the left-hand equality in Equation

∎

Note that the above applies to individual patterns, as well as pattern spaces, by Lemma
6.25
. That is, if
\cP
is well-separated, then so is its hull, thus the hull is Hausdorff. Conversely, for
\cP
FLC, if

is Hausdorff then
\cP
is well-separated. The assumption that

The following shows that, for a compact pattern space, return discreteness is simply a matter of whether any elements have non-discrete groups of periods. In particular, if
\cP
is FLC and aperiodic (i.e.,
\Omega_{\cP}
contains no periodic elements) then
\cP
is return discrete and thus
\Omega_{\cP}
is Hausdorff.

Proposition 6.28
.

For a pattern space
\Omega
, (1) implies (2):

(1)

\Omega
is return discrete;

(2)

for all
\cQ\in\Omega
, we have that
\cK_{\cQ}
is discrete.

Conversely, if
\Omega
is also FLC, (2) implies (1).

Proof.

That (1) implies (2) is shown in Lemma
6.26
(in fact, the
\cK_{\cQ}
are ‘uniformly’ discrete across all
\cQ\in\Omega
). For the converse, suppose that
\Omega
is FLC (equivalently, compact) but not return discrete. Thus, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we may find non-zero

and
\cP_{n}\in\Omega
with

(6.5)

\cP_{n}[\mathbf{0},n]=\cP_{n}[b_{n},n].

Using compactness, take a convergent subsequence
\cP_{n}\to\cQ\in\Omega
. We claim that
\cK_{\cQ}
is not discrete.

Indeed, take
N\in\mathbb{N}
large enough so that every
1
-patch of
\cQ
occurs with centre in
B_{N}
, that is, for all
x\in E
, there exists

with
\cQ[x,1]=\cQ[y,1]
. There exists such an

, since
\cQ
is FLC (Proposition
6.29
). Since the subsequence
\cP_{n}\to\cQ
, we have that

(6.6)

\cP_{n}[b,N+1]=\cQ[\mathbf{0},N+1]

for some sufficiently large

and

. Shifting Equation
6.5
by
b\in B_{1}
,

\cP_{n}[b,N]=\cP_{n}[b+b_{n},N],

since

. Shifting Equation
6.6
by

,

\cP_{n}[b+b_{n},N]=\cQ[b_{n},N].

Combining the last two equations, along with Equation
6.6
restricted to
B_{N}
,

\cQ[\mathbf{0},N]=\cP_{n}[b,N]=\cP_{n}[b+b_{n},N]=\cQ[b_{n},N].

By Lemma
3.21
,
b_{n}\in\cK_{\cQ}
since

and
\cQ
contains centres of all

-patches. Since this holds for arbitrarily large

, with

and

∎

The following shows that the discussed properties are inherited by inclusion of pattern subspaces and membership of patterns, and similarly for local indistinguishability between patterns.

Proposition 6.29
.

If
\Omega
is FLC, well-separated, return discrete or repetitive then, respectively, so is every pattern subspace
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega
, and every
\cP\in\Omega
. If
\Omega
is repetitive, then
\Omega^{\prime}=\Omega
.

Given patterns
\cQ\LI\cP
, if
\cP
is FLC, well-separated, return discrete or repetitive then, respectively, so is
\cQ
. If
\cP
is repetitive, then
\cP\LIs\cQ
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\Omega
is FLC. Then it is totally bounded (Lemma
6.13
), hence so is
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega
. Since
\Omega^{\prime}
is also complete (by Lemma
6.14
) it is compact, thus FLC by Corollary
6.15
. That
\Omega^{\prime}
is well-separated or return discrete, if
\Omega
has the same, follows trivially from the definitions.

Suppose that
\Omega
is repetitive. By (2) of Proposition
6.19
, all elements of
\Omega
are locally isomorphic. Take some
\cP\in\Omega^{\prime}
(by assumption, pattern spaces are non-empty). For any other
\cQ\in\Omega
, we have
\cP\LIs\cQ
, thus
\cQ\in\Omega^{\prime}
too, by Lemma
6.5
. So
\Omega=\Omega^{\prime}
and
\Omega^{\prime}
is also repetitive, as required.

Take any
\cP\in\Omega
and consider its hull
\Omega_{\cP}\subseteq\Omega
. By the above,
\Omega_{\cP}
inherits any of the properties of FLC, well-separated, return discrete or repetitive from
\Omega
. Then
\cP
also has those properties, respectively, by Lemmas
6.12
,
6.25
(twice) and
6.18
. Finally, if
\cQ\LI\cP
then
\Omega_{\cQ}\subseteq\Omega_{\cP}
, so the corresponding statement for patterns follows from that for pattern spaces (although we note that it can also be quite easily proved directly from definitions). When
\cP
is repetitive, that
\cP\LIs\cQ
is already shown in Proposition
6.19
.
∎

6.5.
Local derivations of pattern spaces

In this section we define LD maps between translational pattern spaces, and relate this to LDs of individual patterns, via induced LD maps of hulls. Recall the notion of a local derivation of individual patterns from Definition
2.11
.

Definition 6.30
.

Let
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
be pattern spaces (of patterns over the same Euclidean space
E
), and
f\colon\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
. We call
f
a
local derivation
(
LD
)
map
, and write
f\colon\Omega\LDmap\Omega^{\prime}
, if there exists
c\geq 0
(called a
derivation radius
) for which, for all
\cP
,
\cQ\in\Omega
and
x
,
y\in E
, if
\cP[x,c]=\cQ[y,c]
then
(f\cP)[x]=(f\cQ)[y]
.

We have the following trivial but often used lemma:

Lemma 6.31
.

Suppose that
f\colon\Omega\LDmap\Omega^{\prime}
, with derivation radius
c
. Then, for all
U\cpt E
,
\cP
,
\cQ\in\Omega
and
x
,
y\in E
, if
\cP[x,B_{c}+U]=\cQ[y,B_{c}+U]
then
(f\cP)[x,U]=(f\cQ)[y,U]
. In particular, if
\cP[x,r+c]=\cQ[y,r+c]
then
(f\cP)[x,r]=(f\cQ)[y,r]
.

Similarly, suppose
\cP\LD\cQ
has derivation radius
c
. Then, for all
U\cpt E
, if
\cP[x,B_{c}+U]=\cP[y,B_{c}+U]
then
\cQ[x,U]=\cQ[y,U]
. In particular, for all
r\geq 0
, if
\cP[x,c+r]=\cP[y,c+r]
then
\cQ[x,r]=\cQ[y,r]
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\cP[x,B_{c}+U]=\cQ[y,B_{c}+U]
. For
u\in U
, since
B_{c}+u\subseteq B_{c}+U
, we have
\cP[x+u,B_{c}]=\cQ[y+u,B_{c}]
(Lemma
2.5
) and hence, by the LD property,
(f\cP)[x+u]=(f\cQ)[y+u]
. As this holds for all
u\in U
, by definition
(f\cP)[x,U]=(f\cQ)[y,U]
, as required. The second claim follows immediately, taking
U=B_{r}
. The final pair of claims follow with an analogous argument.
∎

Lemma 6.32
.

If
f\colon\Omega\LDmap\Omega^{\prime}
, then
f
is uniformly continuous, and a factor map, that is,
f(\cP-x)=(f\cP)-x
for all
\cP\in\Omega
and
x\in E
.

Conversely, if
f\colon\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
is a factor map, then
f\colon\Omega\LDmap\Omega^{\prime}
is LD with derivation radius
c
if and only if, for all
\cP
,
\cQ\in\Omega
, if
\cP[\mathbf{0},c]=\cQ[\mathbf{0},c]
then
(f\cP)[\mathbf{0}]=(f\cQ)[\mathbf{0}]
.

Proof.

Given
e=\Phi(B,U)\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
, define
e^{\prime}=\Phi(B,U+B_{c})\in\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
. If
\cP
,
\cQ\in\Omega
are
e^{\prime}
-close then
\cP[b,U+B_{c}]=\cQ[b^{\prime},U+B_{c}]
for
b
,
b^{\prime}\in B
. By Lemma
6.31
, applying the local derivation,
(f\cP)[b,U]=(f\cQ)[b^{\prime},U]
, thus
f\cP
and
f\cQ
are
e
-close so
f
is uniformly continuous.

Given
\cP\in\Omega
,
x\in E
and
r\geq 0
, we have
\cP[x,r+c]=(\cP-x)[\mathbf{0},r+c]
. By Lemma
6.31
,
(f\cP)[x,r]=(f(\cP-x))[\mathbf{0},r]
, thus
((f\cP)-x)[\mathbf{0},r]=(f(\cP-x))[\mathbf{0},r]
. Since this holds for all
r\geq 0
, we have
(f\cP)-x=f(\cP-x)
, as required.

Now suppose that
f\colon\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
is a factor map and take arbitrary
\cP
,
\cQ\in\Omega
and
x
,
y\in E
with
\cP[x,c]=\cQ[y,c]
, equivalently,
(\cP-x)[\mathbf{0},c]=(\cQ-y)[\mathbf{0},c]
. Assuming the final stated property of the lemma then gives the middle equality below:

(f\cP)[x]=(f(\cP)-x)[\mathbf{0}]=(f(\cP-x))[\mathbf{0}]=(f(\cQ-y))[\mathbf{0}]=(f(\cQ)-y)[\mathbf{0}]=(f\cQ)[y],

as required. The converse direction, assuming
f
is LD, is similarly trivial, as it is just the restriction of the LD property of Definition
6.30
to
x=y=\mathbf{0}
.
∎

The following shows that patterns over a fixed Euclidean space
E
, with LDs as morphisms (as a pre-order) define a category, and similarly for individual patterns.

Lemma 6.33
.

For any pattern space
\Omega
, the identity map
\id\colon\Omega\LDmap\Omega
is LD. Given
f\colon\Omega\LDmap\Omega^{\prime}
and
g\colon\Omega^{\prime}\LDmap\Omega^{\prime\prime}
, we also have that
g\circ f
is an LD map.

LD for patterns is a preorder. That is, for any pattern,
\cP\LD\cP
and for any patterns
\cP_{1}
,
\cP_{2}
and
\cP_{3}
, if
\cP_{1}\LD\cP_{2}\LD\cP_{3}
then
\cP_{1}\LD\cP_{3}
.

Proof.

If
\cP[x,c]=\cQ[y,c]
then
\cP[x]=\cQ[y]
, so the identity map is trivially a local derivation (for any derivation radius
c\geq 0
). Suppose that
f
has derivation radius
c
and
g
has derivation radius
c^{\prime}
. If
\cP[x,c+c^{\prime}]=\cQ[y,c+c^{\prime}]
then
(f\cP)[x,c^{\prime}]=(f\cQ)[y,c^{\prime}]
, by Lemma
6.31
. Applying
g
, we have
(gf\cP)[x]=(gf\cQ)[y]
, so
g\circ f
has derivation radius
c+c^{\prime}
. The proof for individual patterns (which is also a counterpart result to Lemma
6.2
) is very similar and we omit it.
∎

Lemma 6.34
.

If
\cP\LD\cQ
then
\cK_{\cP}\leq\cK_{\cQ}
.

Proof.

Let
g\in\cK_{\cP}
, so
\cP[x,c]=\cP[x+g,c]
for all
x\in E
. Then, applying the LD,
\cQ[x]=\cQ[x+g]
for all
x\in E
and thus
g\in\cK_{\cQ}
.
∎

LD maps of pattern spaces preserve the pre-order of local indistinguishability:

Lemma 6.35
.

If
f\colon\Omega\LDmap\Omega^{\prime}
and
\cU\LI\cV
for
\cU
,
\cV\in\Omega
, then
f\cU\LI f\cV
.

Proof.

Let
f
have derivation radius
c
and take arbitrary
x\in E
,
U\cpt E
. Then, since
\cU\LI\cV
, we have
\cU[x,U+B_{c}]=\cV[y,U+B_{c}]
for some
y\in E
. By Lemma
6.31
,
(f\cU)[x,U]=(f\cV)[y,U]
. So the arbitrary patch
(f\cU)[x,U]
of
f\cU
occurs in
f\cV
, hence
f\cU\LI f\cV
.
∎

The above shows, in particular, that an LD map
f\colon\Omega\LDmap\Omega^{\prime}
induces a map
f\colon\mathrm{LI}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{LI}(\Omega^{\prime})
of the LI-classes contained in the pattern spaces. By definition, for any LD map

, we have that
\cP\LD f(\cP)
, with derivation radius

, for all
\cP\in\Omega
. The below gives a kind of converse for hulls, defining an

from an LD of patterns:

Proposition 6.36
.

Suppose that
\cP\LD\cQ

(which also has derivation radius

\Omega_{\cQ}
is Hausdorff,

is the unique factor map satisfying
f(\cP)=\cQ

\cP
is additionally FLC, then
f\colon\Omega_{\cP}\to\Omega_{\cQ}

We define
f
explicitly, as follows. Given
\cU\in\Omega_{\cP}
, we let
f(\cU)=\cU^{\prime}
be the pattern defined by
\cU^{\prime}[x]\coloneqq\cQ[z]
where

is any point for which
\cP[z,c]=\cU[x,c]
. Since
\cU\in\Omega_{\cP}
, equivalently
\cU\LI\cP
, such a

\cU^{\prime}[x]
does not depend on the choice of

is

\cQ[z^{\prime}]=\cQ[z]
. Clearly,

(taking

\cU[x,c]=\cV[y,c]

(f\cU)[x]=(f\cV)[y]
, as both are equal to
\cQ[z]
for any

\cP[z,c]=\cU[x,c]=\cV[y,c]

The above defines, for each
\cU\in\Omega_{\cP}

f\cU
on

and with

for all
\cU\in\Omega_{\cP}

and

\cU\in\Omega_{\cP}

, as required.

To show that

is unique, let

be another LD map, with derivation radius

, and with

. For any
\cU\in\Omega_{\cP}
and

For any

is Hausdorff, this limit point is unique, so

is determined by the images

from just the factor map property and

∎

The following shows that the operation of taking a pattern to its translational hull is functorial and commutes with applying affine transformations:

.

Taking induced LD maps of local derivations is functorial, in the following sense: firstly, the induced map of

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega_{\cP}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega_{\cQ}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega_{A\cP}}

The following is relatively simple in the standard return discrete setting but needs more work in the full generality here:

Proposition 6.40
.

If
f\colon\Omega\LDmap\Omega^{\prime}
is bijective,
\Omega
is FLC and
\Omega^{\prime}
is Hausdorff (equivalently,
\Omega^{\prime}
is well-separated) then

is also an LD map.

Since
\Omega
is FLC, it is compact. Thus, since

is bijective and continuous (Lemma
6.32
),
\Omega^{\prime}=f(\Omega)
is also compact, thus FLC. Hence,
\Omega^{\prime}
is Hausdorff if and only if it is well-separated (Proposition
6.27
), as stated. In this case,
f
is a continuous map from a compact space to a Hausdorff space, so
\Omega^{\prime}
is also compact Hausdorff and

has (uniformly) continuous inverse

is an LD map.

We claim that, for sufficiently large

we have the following: for all
\cP\in\Omega
, if
(f\cP)[\mathbf{0},n]=(f\cP)[b,n]
for some

then
\cP[\mathbf{0}]=\cP[b]
. Indeed, suppose otherwise, so that for arbitrarily large
n\in\mathbb{N}
we may find

and

\cQ_{n}[\mathbf{0},n]=\cQ_{n}[b_{n},n]

\cP_{n}[\mathbf{0}]\neq\cP_{n}[b_{n}]

\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
are compact, we may find a subsequence of the above

for which both
\cP_{n}\to\cP\in\Omega
and
\cQ_{n}\to\cQ\in\Omega^{\prime}

\cQ

\cP\LD\cQ
, with induced LD map

S_{i}\colon\Omega_{L^{i+1}\cP^{\prime}}\LDmap\Omega_{L^{i}\cP}
is the LD map between inflated hulls defined by Proposition
6.39
, and substitution is given by
S^{\prime}\circ L^{n}=\sigma^{n}\colon\Omega_{\cQ}\to\Omega_{\cP}
.

Proof.

Let
\Omega\coloneqq\Omega_{\cP}
and
S_{i}\colon L^{i+1}\Omega_{\cP^{\prime}}\LDmap L^{i}\Omega_{\cP}
denote the distortions of
S=S_{0}\colon L\Omega_{\cP^{\prime}}\to\Omega_{\cP}
, which are surjective. We have
S_{i}\circ L=L\circ S_{i-1}
so that
S^{\prime}\circ L^{n}=(S_{0}\circ L)^{n}=(S\circ L)^{n}=\sigma^{n}
. Take any
\cQ\in\sigma^{-n}(\cP)
, that is,
S^{\prime}(L^{n}\cQ)=\cP
. Since
\cQ\in\Omega
, we have
\cQ\LI\cP
and since
S^{\prime}(L^{n}\cQ)=\cP
, with
S^{\prime}
an LD map (Proposition
6.33
), we have
L^{n}\cQ\LD\cP
, showing that
\cP
is
L^{n}
-sub. As usual, the subdivision map is the one induced by
L^{n}\cQ\LD\cP
, which agrees with
S^{\prime}
since
S^{\prime}(L^{n}\cQ)=\cP
(by uniqueness, from Proposition
6.36
), at least upon restriction to
S^{\prime}\colon L^{n}\Omega_{\cQ}\to\Omega_{\cP}
, which is well-defined by Lemma
6.35
. By the above, substitution is given by
\sigma^{n}=S^{\prime}\circ L^{n}\colon\Omega_{\cQ}\to\Omega_{\cP}
.
∎

In the above, of course
\Omega_{\cQ}=\Omega_{\cP}
if
\cP\LIs\cP^{\prime}
. We now wish to remove the awkward possibility that this is not the case, at least in the case of
L
expansive i.e., to prove Proposition
3.4
. We begin with a simple lemma, that for an FLC pattern space
\Omega
and
U\cpt E
there are only finitely many possible ‘configurations’ of different sets of
U
-patches that can appear together for patterns in
\Omega
.

Lemma 6.46
.

For a pattern
\cP
and
U\cpt E
, let
W_{U}(\cP)
be the set of
U
-patches in
\cP
, that is

W_{U}(\cP)\coloneqq\{\cP[x,U]\mid x\in E\}.

If
\Omega
is an FLC pattern space then, for each
U\cpt E
, the set
\{W_{U}(\cP)\mid\cP\in\Omega\}
of possible ‘configurations’ of
U
-patches is finite.

Proof.

Define
\cP_{1}
, …,
\cP_{n}\in\Omega
and
K_{1}(U+B_{2})=K_{1}
, …,
K_{n}(U+B_{2})=K_{n}\cpt E
as in Definition
6.11
of FLC. Take finite sets
S_{i}\subseteq K_{i}
that are
1
-dense, that is, for each
x\in K_{i}
there exists some
s\in S_{i}
with
s-x\in B_{1}
. We define maps
F_{i}\colon\Omega\to 2^{S_{i}}
by

F_{i}(\cP)\coloneqq\{s\in S_{i}\mid\cP_{i}[s,U+B_{1}]=\cP[x,U+B_{1}]\text{ for some }x\in E\},

i.e., the points of
S_{i}
at the centres of
(U+B_{1})
-patches appearing in
\cP
. Each
S_{i}
is finite, so
F_{i}(\cP)
also has finitely many possibilities. Thus, the result follows if the
F_{i}(\cP)
fully determine
W_{U}(\cP)
.

Indeed, given
\cP
,
\cQ\in\Omega
, suppose that
F_{i}(\cP)=F_{i}(\cQ)
for each
i
and take an arbitrary
U
-patch
\cP[x,U]\in W_{U}(\cP)
. There exists some
i
and
y\in K_{i}
with
\cP[x,U+B_{2}]=\cP_{i}[y,U+B_{2}]
. Since
S_{i}
is
1
-dense in
K_{i}
, there exists
s\in S_{i}
with
y+b=s\in S_{i}
for some
b\in B_{1}
. Shifting by
b
, we thus have
\cP[x+b,U+B_{1}]=\cP_{i}[s,U+B_{1}]
, so
s\in F_{i}(\cP)
. Since
F_{i}(\cP)=F_{i}(\cQ)
, we also have
s\in F_{i}(\cQ)
, thus
\cP_{i}[s,U+B_{1}]=\cQ[z,U+B_{1}]
for some
z\in E
. Shifting by
-b\in B_{1}
, we have
\cP_{i}[s-b,U]=\cQ[z-b,U]
. Since
s-b=y
and
\cP_{i}[y,U]=\cP[x,U]
, we have thus shown that the arbitrary
U
-patch
\cP[x,U]
of
\cP
appears in
\cQ
. The converse is proved analogously, so
W_{U}(\cP)=W_{U}(\cQ)
, as required.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

Let
\cP
be
L
-sub, FLC and well-separated, with predecessor
\cP^{\prime}
and
L
expansive. Recall that Proposition
3.4
states that
\cP\LIs\cP^{\prime}
.

By Lemma
6.44
,
\sigma\colon\Omega_{\cP^{\prime}}\to\Omega_{\cP}
is surjective. Let
\cP_{0}\coloneqq\cP
,
\cP_{1}\coloneqq\cP^{\prime}
and iteratively choose pre-images under
\sigma
to define
\cP_{i}\in\Omega_{\cP}
with
\sigma(\cP_{i+1})=\cP_{i}
and
\cP_{i+1}\LI\cP_{i}
, equivalently,
\Omega_{\cP_{i+1}}\subseteq\Omega_{\cP_{i}}
. This may all be done, by definition of
\sigma
, with each
\sigma(\Omega_{\cP_{i+1}})=\Omega_{\cP_{i}}
. Indeed, by the above, this holds for the base case of
i=0
. So let
\sigma(\cP_{i+1})=\cP_{i}
,
\Omega_{\cP_{i+1}}\subseteq\Omega_{\cP_{i}}
and
\sigma(\Omega_{\cP_{i+1}})=\Omega_{\cP_{i}}
for some
i\in\mathbb{N}
. By the latter two equations, we may choose
\cP_{i+2}\in\Omega_{\cP_{i+1}}
(equivalently,
\cP_{i+2}\LI\cP_{i+1}
) with
\sigma(\cP_{i+2})=\cP_{i+1}\in\Omega_{\cP_{i+1}}
. So we just need to check that
\sigma(\Omega_{\cP_{i+2}})=\Omega_{\cP_{i+1}}
, equivalently,
S(\Omega_{L\cP_{i+2}})=\Omega_{\cP_{i+1}}
. Clearly the restriction of
S
to
\Omega_{L\cP_{i+2}}
remains an LD map with
S(L\cP_{i+2})=\cP_{i+1}
. Note that
S\colon\Omega_{L\cP_{i+2}}\LDmap\Omega_{\cP_{i+1}}
is well-defined, since if
L\cQ\in\Omega_{L\cP_{i+2}}
, equivalently
L\cQ\LI L\cP_{i+2}
, then
SL\cQ\LI SL\cP_{i+2}=\cP_{i+1}
(Lemma
6.35
), that is,
SL\cQ\in\Omega_{\cP_{i+1}}
, as required. Since all hulls involved are compact Hausdorff, this must agree with the LD map induced by
L\cP_{i+2}\LD\cP_{i+1}
(Proposition
6.36
), which is surjective, as required.

To summarise, we may construct patterns
\cP_{i}\in\Omega\coloneqq\Omega_{\cP}
with
\sigma(\cP_{i+1})=\cP_{i}
and
\cP_{i+1}\LI\cP_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Using the notation of Lemma
6.46
(and Notation
3.6
), define
W_{n}(\cQ)\coloneqq W_{V^{n}}(\cQ)
. By this lemma,
W_{n}\coloneqq\{W_{n}(\cQ)\mid\cQ\in\Omega_{\cP}\}
is finite. We claim that, for
\cU
,
\cV\in\Omega
, if
W_{n}(\cU)\subseteq W_{n}(\cV)
then
W_{n}(\sigma\cU)\subseteq W_{n}(\sigma\cV)
. Indeed, let
x\in E
be arbitrary. By assumption,
\cU[L^{-1}x,V^{n}]=\cV[y,V^{n}]
for some
y\in E
. By Lemma
3.10
,
(\sigma\cU)[x,V^{n+1}]=(\sigma\cV)[Ly,V^{n+1}]
. In particular, the arbitrary
V^{n}
-patch
(\sigma\cU)[x,V^{n}]
of
\sigma\cU
occurs in
\sigma\cV
(at
Ly)
, thus
W_{n}(\sigma\cU)\subseteq W_{n}(\sigma\cV)
.

The above shows that substitution induces a map
\sigma_{n}\colon W_{n}\to W_{n}
, given by
\sigma(W_{n}(\cU))\coloneqq W_{n}(\sigma\cU)
, as it depends only on
W_{n}(\cU)
and not on
\cU\in\Omega
. Let
w_{i}\coloneqq W_{n}(\cP_{i})\in W_{n}
. Since
\cP_{i+1}\LI\cP_{i}
, we have a decreasing chain
w_{0}\supseteq w_{1}\supseteq w_{2}\supseteq\cdots
that must stabilise to some

by finiteness of

. Moreover,
w_{i}=\sigma(w_{i+1})
for each

, thus
\sigma(w)=\sigma(w_{k+1})=w_{k}=w
and, iterating,
w_{0}=\sigma^{k}(w_{k})=\sigma^{k}(w)=\sigma^{k-1}(w)=w_{1}
, that is,
W_{n}(\cP)=W_{n}(\cP^{\prime})
. Since this holds for all

, every
V^{n}
-patch of
\cP
occurs in
\cP^{\prime}
, and vice versa, so
\cP\LIs\cP^{\prime}
, as required.
∎

One would expect that we cannot drop the requirement that
L
is expansive in Proposition
3.4
. So it is natural to try to find, say, in the simplest case of the non-expansion
L=\id
, FLC and well-separated patterns
\cP
and
\cP^{\prime}
with
L\cP^{\prime}=\cP^{\prime}\LD\cP
and
\cP^{\prime}\LI\cP
but
not

\cP\LI\cP^{\prime}
. Specialising further, one is led to the following simply-stated question in the symbolic setting:

Question 6.47
.

Is there a transitive subshift
X\subseteq A^{\mathbb{Z}}
, for a finite set

, that contains a proper transitive subshift
Y\subset X
admitting a surjective factor map

?

The author is unaware of any such examples, although consensus appears to be that they should exist. In this case, expansivity of

cannot be dropped in Proposition

.

In the literature, sometimes spaces of substitution patterns are considered that are not hulls (that is, they need not be transitive), in the context of non-primitive substitutions. To cover such examples in full possible generality, we introduce the following:

Definition 6.48
.

Let
\Omega
be a pattern space and
L\colon E\to E
be a linear automorphism (usually an expansion). We say that
\Omega
is
L
-substitutional
(or
L
-sub
, for short) if it is also equipped with a surjective LD map of pattern spaces
S\colon L\Omega\LDmap\Omega
, which we call

. We define

to be the map
\sigma\colon S\circ L\colon\Omega\to\Omega
.

Example 6.49
.

Let
\cP
be FLC, well-separated and
L
-sub, with
L
expansive. Then its hull
\Omega_{\cP}
is
L
-sub, with the subdivision map constructed induced, as usual, by
L\cP^{\prime}\LD\cP
. Indeed,
\cP^{\prime}\LIs\cP
by Proposition
3.4
, so
\Omega_{L\cP^{\prime}}=\Omega_{L\cP}
. Then

is surjective by Lemma

and, by Proposition

, is the unique factor map satisfying

.

\Diamond

Being

Suppose that

is a bijective LD map, with

The LD map

, by Lemma

. By Proposition

The following is vital to several results in Section
4
, providing an appropriate power of substitution fixing LI-classes:

Theorem 6.51
.

If
\Omega
is
L
-sub then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it is also
L^{n}
-sub, with subdivision
S^{\prime}=S_{0}\circ S_{1}\circ\cdots\circ S_{n}
, where
S_{i}=L^{i}\circ S\circ L^{-i}
is the distortion of subdivision
S\colon L\Omega\LDmap\Omega
to
S_{i}\colon L^{i+1}\Omega\LDmap L^{i}\Omega
, for which substitution is given by
S^{\prime}\circ L^{n}=\sigma^{n}
.

If
\Omega
contains finitely many LI-classes, then for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have that
\sigma^{n}
acts as the identity on the LI-classes of
\Omega
, in which case each element of
\Omega
is
L^{n}
-sub.

If
\Omega
is FLC, well-separated and
L
-sub, with
L

Proof.

The distorted subdivision maps
S_{i}
are LD maps, by Lemma
6.38
. Their composition is also LD (Lemma
6.33
). Since each
S_{i}=L\circ S_{i-1}\circ L^{-1}
is surjective, so is
S^{\prime}
. Moreover, using the identity
S_{i}\circ L=L\circ S_{i-1}
, we have
\sigma^{n}=(S\circ L)^{n}=(S_{0}\circ L)^{n}=S^{\prime}\circ L^{n}
. Thus,
\Omega
is
L^{n}
-sub, with subdivision
S^{\prime}
and substitution
\sigma^{n}
.

Suppose that
\cU\LIs\cV
in
\Omega
. Then
L\cU\LIs L\cV
and

too (Lemma
6.35
) i.e.,
\sigma(\cU)\LIs\sigma(\cV)
. Thus,
\sigma
gives a well-defined map on LI-classes of
\Omega
, which is surjective (since
\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Omega
is surjective). Hence, if there are finitely many LI-classes in
\Omega
, we must have that
\sigma
is a permutation on this finite set and an appropriate power
\sigma^{n}
is the identity on LI-classes. Take any
\cP\in\Omega
. Since
\sigma^{n}
is surjective, take any
\cP^{\prime}\in\Omega
with
\sigma^{n}(\cP^{\prime})=\cP
. Thus,
S^{\prime}(L^{n}\cP^{\prime})=\cP
. Since

is a local derivation, we have
L^{n}\cP^{\prime}\LD\cP
. Since we also have
\cP=\sigma^{n}(\cP^{\prime})\LIs\cP^{\prime}
, we have that
\cP
is
L^{n}
-sub, as required.

Now we show that if
\Omega
is FLC and well-separated, and

is expansive, then
\Omega
contains finitely many LI-classes. For
\cP\in\Omega
, let
W_{0}(\cP)\coloneqq W_{V^{0}}(\cP)
, with notation as in Lemma
6.46
. The same lemma shows that the set

of all possibilities for ‘configurations’ of

-patches of patterns of
\Omega
is finite. We claim that the LI-class of any
\cP\in\Omega
is determined by

, from which the result immediately follows.

Indeed, as in the
proof
of Proposition
3.4
, substitution induces a well-defined map
\sigma_{0}\colon W_{0}\to W_{0}
, by
\sigma_{0}(W_{0}(\cP))\coloneqq W_{0}(\sigma(\cP))
, which is surjective by surjectivity of
\sigma\colon\Omega\to\Omega
. Hence,
\sigma_{0}^{n}=\mathrm{id}
for some power
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Take arbitrary
\cP
,
\cQ\in\Omega
with
W_{0}(\cP)=W_{0}(\cQ)
and an arbitrary
V^{k}
-patch
\cP[x,V^{k}]
of
\cP
. Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
n\ell\geq k
and choose
\cP^{\prime}
,
\cQ^{\prime}\in\Omega
with
\sigma^{n\ell}(\cP^{\prime})=\cP
and
\sigma^{n\ell}(\cQ^{\prime})=\cQ
. Since
\sigma^{n}_{0}=\mathrm{id}
, we also have

, so there exists some

with

. Using Lemma

. Thus, the arbitrary

∎

As standard, even in the non-minimal case, some power of substitution has fixed points:

Proposition 6.53
.

If
\Omega
is FLC, well-separated and
L
-sub with
L
expansive, then there exists some
\cP\in\Omega
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
\sigma^{n}(\cP)=\cP
. Thus,
\Omega
contains pseudo self-affine points for some power of
L
, that is, points
\cP\in\Omega
with
L^{n}\cP\LD\cP
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

First, using Theorem
6.51
, we assume that
\sigma
fixes LI-classes of
\Omega
(otherwise, we begin by passing to a power that does). Take any
\cQ\in\Omega
and, using FLC, let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be such that all
V^{1}
-patches occur with centre in
B_{N}
, that is, for all
x\in E
there exists some
y\in V^{1}
so that
\cQ[x,V^{1}]=\cQ[y,V^{1}]
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, pick some
\cQ_{n}\in\sigma^{-n}(\cQ)
. Take
x_{n}\in B_{N}
with
\cQ[x_{n},V^{1}]=\cQ_{n}[\mathbf{0},V^{1}]
. For
y\in E
small enough that
V^{0}+y\subseteq V^{1}
(for the second equality below), by Lemma
2.5
we have

\displaystyle(\cQ_{n}-y)[\mathbf{0},V^{0}]

\displaystyle=\cQ_{n}[y,V^{0}]=\cQ[x_{n}+y,V^{0}]=(\cQ-(x_{n}+y))[\mathbf{0},V^{0}]

\displaystyle=(\sigma^{n}(\cQ_{n}-L^{-n}(x_{n}+y)))[\mathbf{0},V^{0}].

For sufficiently large
n
, we may take
y
sufficiently so small (as required for above) and also satisfying
y=L^{-n}(x_{n}+y)
. Indeed, equivalently,
y=(L^{n}-\id)^{-1}x_{n}
and, since
L
is expansive,
\|(L^{n}-\id)^{-1}\|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, whilst
\|x_{n}\|\leq N
remains bounded. So we have shown that there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
and some
\cQ^{\prime}=\cQ_{n}-y\in\Omega
satisfying
\cQ^{\prime}[\mathbf{0},V^{0}]=(\sigma^{n}\cQ^{\prime})[\mathbf{0},V^{0}]
.

Define
\cP_{i}\coloneqq\sigma^{ni}(\cQ^{\prime})
. Thus,
\cP_{0}[\mathbf{0},V^{0}]=\cP_{1}[\mathbf{0},V^{0}]
. Applying
\sigma^{n}
, from Lemma
3.10
, we have
\cP_{1}[\mathbf{0},V^{1}]=\cP_{2}[\mathbf{0},V^{1}]
(in fact, even
\cP_{1}[\mathbf{0},V^{n}]=\cP_{2}[\mathbf{0},V^{n}]
). Iterating, we have
\cP_{i}[\mathbf{0},V^{i}]=\cP_{i+1}[\mathbf{0},V^{i}]
. Thus,
(\cP_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy sequence, so
\cP_{i}\to\cP
as
i\to\infty
, by compactness (or completeness) of
\Omega
. By continuity of
\sigma^{n}
and
\Omega
being Hausdorff, it follows that

\sigma^{n}(\cP)=\sigma^{n}\lim\cP_{i}=\lim\sigma^{n}\cP_{i}=\lim\cP_{i+1}=\cP,

so
\sigma^{n}(\cP)=\cP
, as required. Hence,
L^{n}\cP\LD\cP
, so
\Omega
contains pseudo self-affine points.
∎

6.7.
L
-sub pattern spaces from iterated inflate, replace rules

Let us further motivate the
L
-sub pattern space definition by explaining how they naturally arise in familiar cases of iterating substitution rules on ‘discrete structures’ (see Figures
1
–
3
). Covering all possibilities with one definition appears to necessarily devolve into too many technicalities, so we mostly only sketch the main procedures in broad terms, but ones hopefully easily enacted in practice.

Typically, one starts with a finite set of ‘atoms’ (say, a finite set of prototiles or coloured points
[
LW03
]
) and a rule
S
that replaces the inflation
L
of an atom by a patch of (translated) atoms. This ‘inflate, replace’ rule
\sigma=S\circ L
may be iterated, defining
generated patches
. If (some of) these grow appropriately, they can be used to define patterns ‘admitted’ by substitution, those whose finite patches are (in an appropriate sense) sub-patches of generated patches, defining a pattern space
\Omega=\Omega_{\sigma}
of admitted patterns.

When
\sigma
is FLC — loosely, if it only generates a finite number of ‘small patches’, such as patches of two adjacent tiles for tile substitutions, modulo translation — then
\Omega
being FLC should quickly follow, as well as
\Omega\neq\emptyset
, by building patches of increasing ‘support’ about the origin, appropriately centred, and using a Cantor diagonalisation argument. That
\Omega
is Hausdorff (and often return discrete) will typically be clear. And, by the nature of its construction,
\Omega
will satisfy the pattern space property i.e., if
\cP\LIin\Omega
then
\cP\in\Omega
. Indeed, if all finite patches of some pattern
\cP
can be found in ones of
\Omega
, since those only admitted patches, this shows that
\cP
does too.

The replacement rule
S
should be defined on full patterns
L\cP\in L\Omega
(by replacing each translated and inflated atom by the corresponding translate of its replacement) from which
S
being LD should be immediate, with domain
L\Omega
. Under appropriate conditions,
S(L\Omega)\subseteq\Omega
as each patch
p
of
\sigma(\cP)
will be contained in the substituted image of another patch of
\cP
, which is itself admitted (so
p
is contained in one further iterate of this and so is also admitted).

Thus, one expects the main technicality to be in checking that
S\colon L\Omega\LD\Omega
is surjective. But this should also tend to be straightforward, using the definition of
\Omega
of when patterns are admitted along with the following ‘local’ version of surjectivity:

Proposition 6.54
.

Let
\Omega
be an compact Hausdorff pattern space and
S\colon L\Omega\LDmap\Omega
. Then
S
is surjective if and only if we have the following: for each
r
-patch
p
in
\Omega
(that is,
p=\cP[\mathbf{0},r]
for some
\cP\in\Omega
and
r\geq 0
), there exists some
\cP^{\prime}_{r}\in\Omega
with
(\sigma\cP^{\prime}_{r})[\mathbf{0},r]=p
.

Proof.

Take any
\cP\in\Omega
and, for each
r\geq 0
, take
\cP^{\prime}_{r}\in\Omega
satisfying the stated condition. Define
\cP_{r}\coloneqq\sigma(\cP^{\prime}_{r})=S(L\cP^{\prime}_{r})
. Since
\cP_{r}[\mathbf{0},r]=p=\cP[\mathbf{0},r]
, we have that
\cP_{r}\to\cP
in
\Omega
as
r\to\infty
. We also have
\cP^{\prime}_{r}\to\cP^{\prime}\in\Omega
for some subsequence, by compactness. On this subsequence, by continuity of
S
and
\Omega
being Hausdorff,

S(L\cP^{\prime})=S(\lim L\cP^{\prime}_{r})=\lim(S(L\cP^{\prime}_{r}))=\lim\cP_{r}=\cP,

so
S
is surjective, as required. The converse is trivial (taking
\cP_{r}^{\prime}
as any pre-image of
\cP
).
∎

Informally, the above says that, for any admitted patch
p
that appears in an element of
\Omega
, we just need to find another admitted cluster
p^{\prime}
(appearing in some
\cP^{\prime}\in\Omega
which, in practice, one should be able to construct from
q
and Cantor diagonalisation) whose substitute contains
p
.

By way of concrete example, take the space
\Omega
of tilings admitted by an FLC stone inflation rule. Given a patch
p=\cT\sqcap U
of some tiling
\cT\in\Omega
, as
\cT
is admitted we may write
p=\sigma^{n}(t)\sqcap U
for some tile
t
. Similarly, since the patch of tiles within any given radius
R\geq 0
in
\cT
has the same property, we can find
n_{R}\in\mathbb{N}
and tiles
t_{R}
with
p\subseteq\sigma^{n_{R}}(t_{R})
but the latter also covering all points within radius
R
of
p
. Consider the sequence of patches
q_{n}\coloneqq\sigma^{n_{R}-1}(t_{R})
, so
p
is contained in each
\sigma(q_{n})
. Using FLC and a Cantor diagonalisation argument (by sieving the sequences of restricted supertiles
q_{n}\sqcap B
to be consistent over increasingly large balls
B
), we may construct a limiting tiling
\cT^{\prime}_{p}
that is admitted, by construction, and whose substitution contains
p
, as required. This does not require primitivity of substitution, so general admissible stone inflations
[
CS11
]
are covered, for instance.
