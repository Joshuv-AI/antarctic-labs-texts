Photoinitiated Regulated Silver-Releasing Zwitterionic Hydrogel Coating for Antibacterial, Antifouling, and Thrombus-Resistant PVC Catheters
Authors: Qinsheng Lu, Zhenyu Ren, Qianyun Zhang, Mian Xu, Qi Feng, Yutong Ding, Aiying Chen, Bin He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Blood-contacting catheters are susceptible to bacterial colonization, nonspecific protein adsorption, and thrombus adhesion, which may lead to catheter-related bloodstream infections and catheter dysfunction. To address these challenges, a photoinitiated silver-loaded zwitterionic hydrogel coating, denoted as pSBMA-A, was constructed on PVC catheter surfaces by integrating a PDA/BP interfacial layer, a poly­(sulfobetaine methacrylate) (pSBMA) hydrogel network, and silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs). The PDA/BP layer enhanced interfacial anchoring, while the pSBMA network provided a hydrated antifouling interface and served as a matrix for Ag NP incorporation. The small-sized Ag NPs (15.99 ± 0.11 nm) endowed the coating with broad-spectrum antibacterial activity. The pSBMA-A coating achieved antibacterial efficiencies of up to 99% against both Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus and Gram-negative Escherichia coli at a low Ag concentration of 0.05 mM. ICP-MS analysis showed that Ag was gradually released from the coating over 1–11 days, indicating regulated Ag release rather than rapid Ag loss. In addition, the pSBMA-containing coatings significantly improved surface hydrophilicity and reduced nonspecific BSA adsorption, supporting the antifouling role of the zwitterionic hydration layer. Compared with bare PVC, the pSBMA-A coating reduced thrombus adhesion by approximately 77% and showed acceptable hemocompatibility and cytocompatibility, with a hemolysis ratio below 2% and cell viability above 80%. Furthermore, subcutaneous implantation showed reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and lower IL-6 and CD68 expression around the pSBMA-A samples. This study provides a promising surface engineering strategy for improving the anti-infective, antifouling, and thrombus-resistant performance of blood-contacting PVC catheters.

Introduction

With the rapid development of interventional therapy, blood purification, critical care medicine, and long-term vascular access technologies, blood-contacting poly­(vinyl chloride) (PVC) catheters, including vascular access catheters, hemodialysis catheters, and infusion catheters, have been widely used in clinical practice. However, blood-contacting PVC catheters still suffer from three major challenges. (1) High risk of thrombosis. After catheter implantation, platelet and fibrinogen adhesion and aggregation are rapidly induced, resulting in a thrombosis incidence as high as 33–66%. , This process not only leads to catheter occlusion but may also cause life-threatening complications such as pulmonary embolism. Moreover, thrombus formation provides a favorable substrate for subsequent microbial colonization. , (2) Lack of antibacterial efficacy. Thrombi formed on catheter surfaces significantly enhance bacterial adhesion and aggregation, facilitating biofilm development. Biofilms endow pathogens with strong resilience against host immune responses and antimicrobial agents, becoming a persistent source of catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs), while further accelerating thrombus progression. The infection rate within 7 days after implantation can still reach 20–40%, forming a vicious cycle of “thrombosis-infection”. , (3) Poor tissue compatibility. Catheters made of bioinert polymeric materials such as silicone rubber and polyurethane continuously exert physicochemical and mechanical stimulation on the vascular endothelium, inducing chronic inflammation and delaying endothelial healing, thereby further increasing the risk of thrombosis. , These issues severely limit the clinical performance and service life of medical catheters.

Currently, antithrombotic and antibacterial surface modification strategies for medical catheters can be generally divided into two types: active bactericidal approaches and passive antifouling approaches. Active bactericidal strategies involve incorporating antimicrobial components (e.g., antibiotics, antimicrobial peptides, silver nanoparticles, and anticoagulants) onto device surfaces, where these agents exert antibacterial effects or inhibit platelet activation through in situ release. Among these, silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) are considered ideal candidates for catheter antibacterial modification due to their wide-ranging antibacterial activity, multitarget mechanisms, and relatively low tendency to induce bacterial resilience. Representative nanosilver antibacterial coating systems include the polyphenol-reduction and coordination-deposition-based silver nanoparticle coatings developed by He and coworkers. However, such systems also face critical limitations: silver-based coatings mainly provide a “bactericidal” function but are insufficient in suppressing thrombus adhesion and initial bacterial attachment. Once a fouling layer forms, bacteria can still adhere and develop biofilms on top of it. In addition, some silver coatings exhibit inadequate adhesion strength under dynamic fluid shear conditions, leading to particle detachment or inactivation, which compromises long-term stability and clinical reliability. Furthermore, concerns regarding silver-induced cytotoxicity and biosafety necessitate a precise balance between antibacterial efficacy and biocompatibility.

In contrast to active bactericidal strategies, zwitterionic hydrogel coatings have gained increasing interest in medical catheter research due to their outstanding antifouling performance. Typical zwitterionic materials include sulfobetaine methacrylate (SBMA), carboxybetaine methacrylate (CBMA), and phosphorylcholine (PC). These molecules contain equal amounts of positively charged and negatively charged groups, which can form a dense hydration layer at the material interface via electrostatic interactions. This hydration layer not only provides physical steric hindrance but also significantly reduces interfacial free energy, thereby resisting thrombus and bacterial adhesion. Moreover, hydrogel coatings consist of three-dimensional porous networks formed by cross-linked hydrophilic polymer chains, which enhance surface hydrophilicity while enabling physical encapsulation of active substances. For example, Ke et al. developed robust zwitterionic coatings based on poly­(phosphorylcholine) (pMPC), achieving effective resistance against fibrinogen and bacterial adhesion. Yao et al. further constructed a purely zwitterionic hydrogel coating by reinforcing poly­(sulfobetaine) with poly­(carboxybetaine) microgels (pCB/pSB), which not only mitigated foreign body responses but also prevented in vitro clot formation in rat and rabbit circulation without the use of blood thinners. Despite their excellent antifouling properties and biocompatibility, the real-world use of hydrogel coatings is largely limited by their adhesion strength to substrates, which is a key determinant of clinical reliability. Most medical consumables (e.g., silicone rubber and polyurethane) possess chemically inert, highly hydrophobic surfaces with few reactive functional groups, resulting in weak physical attachment of hydrogels and a high risk of delamination. Common improvement strategies involve introducing interfacial bridging molecules (such as silane coupling agents or polydopamine) to enhance adhesion through covalent bonding. Among these, photoinitiated polymerization techniques can rapidly induce in situ cross-linking of monomers on substrate surfaces under ultraviolet or visible light, forming hydrogel coatings with strong adhesion and tunable thickness, and have therefore been widely investigated. Nevertheless, most zwitterionic coating systems function as “passive antibacterial” materials, primarily reducing contamination by preventing adhesion. Their intrinsic advantage lies in “anti-adhesion” rather than “bactericidal” activity. Consequently, under high bacterial load conditions or after coverage by a fouling layer, their antifouling performance is significantly compromised. For bacteria that have already adhered or are rapidly proliferating, antifouling alone is often insufficient to completely prevent infection.

Herein, a photoinitiated silver-loaded poly­(sulfobetaine methacrylate) hydrogel coating, denoted as pSBMA-A, was fabricated on PDA/BP-pretreated poly­(vinyl chloride) catheters. As illustrated in Scheme , the PDA/BP interfacial layer was introduced to enhance coating adhesion; SBMA was polymerized to form a zwitterionic hydrogel network with strong hydration capability; and Ag NPs were incorporated to provide broad-spectrum antibacterial activity. The rationale for combining Ag NPs with a pSBMA hydrogel matrix is to integrate active antibacterial activity with zwitterionic hydration-mediated antifouling within one coating system. Compared with a pure pSBMA coating, the incorporation of Ag NPs introduces active bactericidal functionality and compensates for the limited killing ability of passive antifouling surfaces. Compared with conventional silver-based coatings, the cross-linked pSBMA hydrogel network not only provides a hydrated antifouling interface but also serves as a diffusion-regulating matrix for Ag species, thereby helping to maintain antibacterial activity at low silver loading while reducing rapid silver loss and potential cytotoxicity. Compared with physically deposited coatings, the PDA/BP-assisted photoinitiated in situ polymerization strategy was designed to strengthen interfacial anchoring on the hydrophobic PVC substrate and improve the short-term coating stability under the tested aqueous washing conditions. Therefore, the technical advancement of this coating lies in the balanced integration of low-silver-loading antibacterial activity, zwitterionic-hydration-mediated antifouling performance, reduced thrombus adhesion, regulated silver release, and improved short-term interfacial stability under aqueous washing conditions. In this work, the coating structure, hydrophilicity, Ag NP stability, Ag release behavior, antibacterial activity, protein adsorption, thrombus adhesion, short-term coating stability, hemocompatibility, cytocompatibility, and preliminary in vivo tissue response were systematically evaluated. This study provides a surface engineering strategy for improving the anti-infective and thrombus-resistant performance of blood-contacting poly­(vinyl chloride) catheters.

Results

Structure of Silver Nanoparticles

The UV–vis absorption spectrum of the silver nanoparticle solution is shown in Figure a, where a single surface plasmon resonance peak appears at 404 nm. The typical dipolar resonance peak for nearly spherical silver nanoparticles is observed at around 400 nm, suggesting that the prepared Ag NPs are primarily spherical in shape. Figure b shows that transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed that the prepared Ag NPs have a uniform spherical structure. Figure c presents the particle size profile, which follows a log-normal distribution, having an average particle size of 15.99 ± 0.11 nm. The nanoparticles are mainly concentrated in the narrow distribution interval of 10–25 nm. The frequency distribution and cumulative frequency results indicate that the D10, D50, and D90 values are 11.89, 14.97, and 18.12 nm, respectively. Moreover, the D50 value is almost identical to the average particle size. After D90, the cumulative frequency distribution curve flattens and grows slowly, which can be well fitted using the Boltzmann distribution function. This distribution suggests that larger particles are present in a minor proportion and do not significantly affect the overall average particle size. The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) photograph in Figure d displays clear lattice fringes, with an interplanar spacing of approximately 0.2074 nm, corresponding to the (200) plane of the silver face-centered cubic structure. , The intensity profile of the lattice fringes in Figure e exhibits a regular sinusoidal periodic oscillation, reflecting the stable periodic arrangement of atomic layers in the crystal, indicating that the synthesized Ag NPs possess good crystallinity. Figure f shows digital photographs of the Ag NPs solution stored at room temperature for 1–30 days and the UV–vis spectrum recorded after 30 days. No obvious color change was observed, and the characteristic absorption peak of Ag NPs remained at approximately 404 nm without obvious peak shift, peak broadening, or new absorption bands, indicating that no apparent impurity formation or severe aggregation occurred in the Ag NPs dispersion.

Hydrogel Coating Structure

As illustrated in Figure S1, the surfaces of PDA, pBDA, pSBMA, and pSBMA-A turned brown due to the deposition of polydopamine (PDA). Compared with the PDA and pBDA samples, the pSBMA and pSBMA-A samples exhibited a lighter surface color, which can be attributed to the hydrogel coating layer deposited on top of the PDA layer. The surface morphology of the coatings was observed by SEM. As shown in Figure a, the untreated PVC surface was smooth and flat. In contrast, the surface of the pBDA coating in Figure b exhibits a rough, granular morphology, which results from the deposition and polymerization of dopamine (DA) onto the surface. After UV curing, the hydrogel coating was deposited on the PVC surface. As shown in Figure c and d, both the pSBMA and pSBMA-A coatings have smoother and more uniform surfaces compared to the pBDA coating, with the SBMA coating effectively masking the DA particle features, confirming the successful formation of the hydrogel coating. The transverse structure depicted in Figure e–h clearly indicates the thickness and structure of the coating. The thickness of the pSBMA-A coating was 1.63 ± 0.18 μm. Elemental mapping (Figure i–l) demonstrates the uniform distribution of elements in the pSBMA-A coating. The C signal originates from the PVC substrate, SBMA, MBA, and dopamine; the N signal comes from SBMA, MBA, and dopamine; the S signal is from the sulfonic groups (−SO3 –) of sulfobetaine; and the Ag signal originates from the Ag NPs within the hydrogel network. These results not only confirm the effective deposition of the hydrogel coating on the PVC surface but also indicate that the Ag NPs are uniformly dispersed within the hydrogel network.

(a–d) SEM images of the blank PVC, pBDA, pSBMA, and pSBMA-A coatings. (e–h) Cross -sectional morphology of the blank PVC, pBDA, pSBMA, and pSBMA-A coatings, respectively. (i–l) Elemental mapping of the pSBMA-A coating.

The FTIR results shown in Figure S2a indicate a common strong broad peak at 3435 cm–1 in the pBDA, pSBMA, and pSBMA-A coating samples, which corresponds to the O–H stretching vibration and N–H bending vibration of dopamine (DA). Additionally, two distinct absorption peaks at 1038 cm–1 and 1197 cm–1 were detected in the pSBMA and pSBMA-A coatings. These peaks correspond to the symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of the sulfonate groups (−SO3 –), respectively, supporting the presence of SBMA-related structures in the hydrogel coating. Together with the SEM and elemental mapping results, the FTIR spectra indicate the successful formation of an SBMA-containing hydrogel layer on the PVC surface. The Raman spectra shown in Figure S2b indicate that, with the introduction of the hydrogel coating, the C–H stretching vibration from the PVC substrate at 3070 cm–1 gradually weakens and even disappears. Additionally, the peaks at 1387 cm–1 and 1588 cm–1 in the pBDA, pSBMA, and pSBMA-A samples correspond to the D and G bands of dopamine. The Raman spectra confirm the successful incorporation of the PDA coating and the construction of the hydrogel coating on the substrate.

Figure a shows the full-scan spectrum, revealing characteristic peaks for Ag 3d and S 2p, corresponding to the silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) encapsulated in the hydrogel network and the sulfur element in SBMA, respectively. In Figure b, the C 1s spectrum exhibits peaks at 284.80 eV (C–C) and 286.35 eV (C–O), which originate from the PVC substrate and dopamine (PDA) coating, with dopamine (PDA) contributing to the formation of these peaks. Figure d presents the O 1s spectrum, where a peak at 531.02 eV is assigned to the SO bond, derived from the sulfonic acid group in sulfobetaine. In Figure e, the high-resolution S 2p spectrum displays a pair of spin–orbit split peaks corresponding to SO3 2p3/2 (167.51 eV) and SO3 2p1/2 (168.77 eV), further supporting the presence of sulfobetaine groups in the coating. Antibacterial Properties of the Hydrogel Coating

During the use of medical catheters, bacteria initially adhere to the substrate via van der Waals forces and specific interactions, subsequently colonize the surface, and ultimately lead to infection. In order to assess the antibacterial properties of the pBDA, pSBMA, and pSBMA-A hydrogel coatings, the representative Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Gram-negative Escherichia coli (E. coli) were selected as model bacteria. As illustrated in Figure a, the pBDA coating demonstrated only limited antibacterial activity, with a large number of colonies still observed on the agar plates. Quantitative analysis of colony-forming units (CFU) (Figure b) revealed that the average CFU values for E. coli and S. aureus were 1.70 × 104 CFU/cm2 and 1.43 × 104 CFU/cm2, respectively, corresponding to an antibacterial rate of about 40%. In contrast, owing to its zwitterionic hydrophilic nature, the pSBMA coating significantly reduced bacterial adhesion, resulting in average CFU values of 8.29 × 103 CFU/cm2 for E. coli and 6.46 × 103 CFU/cm2 for S. aureus, with the antibacterial efficiency increased to around 70%. Notably, the pSBMA-A coating demonstrated the best performance, with average CFU values of 0.24 × 102 CFU/cm2 and 0.48 × 102 CFU/cm2 for E. coli and S. aureus, respectively, achieving antibacterial efficiencies as high as 99% against both bacterial strains.

(a) Colony images of E. coli and S. aureus cultured on the surfaces of PVC, pBDA, pSBMA, and pSBMA-A. (b) Statistical results of colony-forming units against E. coli and S. aureus for different materials.

The pSBMA and pSBMA-A hydrogel coatings were immersed in artificial urine for 3 days and then removed for antibacterial reevaluation using Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus as representative bacterial strains. Artificial urine was used here as an ion-rich simulated physiological medium to evaluate short-term antibacterial durability rather than to define urinary catheters as the primary application scenario. As shown in Figure a, only a small number of bacterial colonies were observed on the surface of the pSBMA-A coating, whereas a significantly higher number of colonies appeared on the pSBMA coating, indicating that the pSBMA-A coating retained antibacterial activity in a complex, simulated physiological environment.

(a) Plate colony culture results of E. coli and S. aureus after 3 days of immersion in artificial urine on PVC, pSBMA, and pSBMA-A hydrogel coatings. (b) Analysis of colony-forming unit results. (c) SEM images of pSBMA-A before and after PBS immersion/flushing; S and Ag elemental mapping of pSBMA-A after treatment. (d) ICP-MS Ag release profile from pSBMA-A in PBS over 1–11 days.

Quantitative analysis of the data in Figure b demonstrated that, after immersion, the number of colonies on the pSBMA-A coating surface was markedly lower than that on the pSBMA coating. Specifically, the average CFU values on the pSBMA-A coating were 4.17 × 103 CFU/cm2 for E. coli and 4.79 × 103 CFU/cm2 for S. aureus, corresponding to an antibacterial efficiency of about 80%, while the pSBMA coating exhibited average CFU values of 1.11 × 104 CFU/cm2 and 1.77 × 104 CFU/cm2 for E. coli and S. aureus, respectively, with an antibacterial efficiency of around 50%. These results indicate that the pSBMA-A hydrogel coating retained antibacterial activity after short-term immersion in artificial urine. Figure c shows that the coating did not exhibit large-area delamination after immersion and flushing, and S and Ag signals remained detectable on the treated surface, supporting short-term coating retention under the tested immersion/flushing conditions. ICP-MS analysis in Figure d showed that Ag was gradually released over 1–11 days. By day 11, the Ag concentration in the release medium reached 22.89 ± 1.77 μg/L, corresponding to an area-normalized cumulative Ag release of approximately 0.0509 ± 0.0039 μg/cm2. However, because the total Ag loading retained in the coating was not determined by complete digestion of the coated samples, the cumulative release percentage could not be accurately calculated in this study. Although an initial Ag release was observed within the first day, the release amount continued to increase during the subsequent immersion period, suggesting that the cross-linked pSBMA hydrogel network could regulate Ag diffusion and reduce rapid Ag loss.

Hydrophilicity of the Hydrogel Coating

As shown in Figure a, because of the different degrees of bending stress on the inner and outer surfaces of the medical catheter, the distance over which the droplet spreads on its surface also varies. Under the influence of the pSBMA and pSBMA-A hydrogel coatings, the droplet spreads significantly more on the outer surface than on the PVC, PDA, and pBDA samples, while on the inner surface, the pSBMA and pSBMA-A coatings nearly flatten the water droplet completely.

(a) Morphological images of water droplets on the convex and concave surfaces of PVC, PDA, pBDA, pSBMA, and pSBMA-A coatings. (b) Statistical results of the height-to-width ratio (H/W) of water droplets on the convex surface of different materials. (c) Statistical results of the height-to-width ratio (H/W) of water droplets on the concave surface of different materials.

In the analysis of the convex and concave droplet height-to-width ratios (H/W) shown in Figure b–c, the vertical distance from the highest point of the droplet to the sample surface and the ratio of its spreading width on the sample surface gradually decreased with the presence of the hydrogel coating. The convex surface height-to-width ratio decreased from 0.70 ± 0.13 to 0.18 ± 0.02, and the concave surface ratio decreased from 0.52 ± 0.0 to 0.08 ± 0.01, indicating a significant improvement in the hydrophilicity of the catheter surface. This improvement is primarily due to the zwitterionic structure of sulfobetaine, which contains quaternary ammonium cations and sulfonate anions that strongly engage with water molecules via electrostatic and hydrogen bonding interactions, forming a hydration layer on the surface and enhancing the coating’s hydrophilicity. This demonstrates a significant enhancement of the hydrophilicity of the catheter surface.

Reduced Thrombus Adhesion and Antifouling Properties of the Hydrogel Coating

In the in vitro blood circulation experiment, the pictures of the samples after circulation are presented in Figure a. Both the blank PVC and pBDA-coated catheters exhibited a significant amount of blood clot deposition on their surfaces after circulation. In contrast, the blood clot deposition on the surfaces of pSBMA and pSBMA-A hydrogel-coated catheters was markedly reduced. The corresponding SEM images of the areas in Figure a reveal that the blank PVC and pBDA-coated surfaces have a large accumulation of red blood cells, whereas the pSBMA and pSBMA-A hydrogel-coated surfaces show significantly fewer red blood cells, with a smoother and denser surface, and nearly no visible aggregation of red blood cells. This result further supports the effective antifouling properties of the pSBMA and pSBMA-A hydrogel coatings. Compared to the blank PVC and pBDA coatings, the pSBMA and pSBMA-A hydrogel coatings demonstrated a significant reduction in thrombus adhesion after the blood circulation experiment, according to the results in Figure b, decreasing from 52.40 ± 15.74 mg to 12.03 ± 0.47 mg (approximately 77% reduction). This result suggests that the hydrophobic surface of PVC promotes coagulation, leading to thrombus adhesion, whereas the high hydrophilicity induced by the bonding of SBMA to the PVC catheter surface significantly enhances the antifouling properties of the material. This may be attributed to the favorable hydrophilicity and hydration capability of the sulfobetaine groups, which form a hydrated interfacial layer on the substrate surface and thereby reduce thrombus adhesion and aggregation on the catheter surface. Protein adsorption was evaluated by the BCA assay to assess the antifouling ability of the coatings. As shown in Figure c,d, bare PVC showed much higher protein adsorption and absorbance at 562 nm, whereas both pSBMA and pSBMA-A significantly reduced protein adsorption. No significant difference was observed between pSBMA and pSBMA-A, indicating that the antifouling effect mainly originated from the pSBMA hydrogel layer. The zwitterionic sulfobetaine groups can form a hydrated interfacial layer, which resists protein and erythrocyte adhesion, as illustrated in Figure e. These results provide mechanistic support for the reduced thrombus adhesion on the coated catheter surfaces.

Biocompatibility

Biocompatibility for use in the human body is considered a critical requirement for biomedical applications. As shown in Figure S4, representative images of the samples after incubation with the CCK-8 reagent are presented. After incubation, the wells corresponding to blank PVC, pBDA, pSBMA, and pSBMA-A all appeared to be yellow. This is because WST-8 in the CCK-8 reagent can be reduced by mitochondrial dehydrogenases in viable cells to produce a water-soluble orange-yellow formazan product, the amount of which is positively correlated with cellular metabolic activity and the number of viable cells. − Figure a shows the cell viability of blank PVC catheters and catheters coated with pBDA, pSBMA, and pSBMA-A hydrogels. The CCK-8 assay results indicate that compared with the positive control, the pSBMA and pSBMA-A hydrogel-coated catheters exhibited cell viabilities higher than 80% after 24 h of incubation. According to ISO 10993-5:2009, the prepared pSBMA-A hydrogel-coated catheters can therefore be regarded as noncytotoxic. Figure b presents the optical density (OD) values measured at 450 nm, showing no statistically significant differences between the blank PVC catheter, the pBDA-, pSBMA-, and pSBMA-A-coated catheters, and the negative control.

(a) Cell viability of different samples (Positive, PVC, pBDA, pSBMA, and pSBMA-A) after coculture with cells. (b) Optical density (OD) values measured at 450 nm. (c) Hemolysis ratios of different samples; the inset photographs show the hemolysis results for the positive control, negative control, and various catheter samples. The green dashed line indicates the nonhemolytic threshold (2%). (d) Optical density (OD) values measured at 545 nm.

Figure c illustrates the hemolysis ratios of human red blood cells (HRBCs) induced by the blank PVC catheter and the pBDA, pSBMA, and pSBMA-A coated catheters. The hemolysis rates of the blank PVC, pBDA, pSBMA, and pSBMA-A hydrogel-coated catheters were 0.26 ± 0.15%, 0.35 ± 0.15%, 0.39 ± 0.11%, and 0.47 ± 0.10%, respectively. All values were below 2% (ASTM F756-17, 2025) and can thus be considered nonhemolytic. Figure d shows the OD values measured at 545 nm. The absorbance values of the blank PVC catheter and the pBDA, pSBMA, and pSBMA-A coated catheters were all below 0.1 and exhibited statistically significant differences compared with the positive control. Overall, both the hemolysis and cytotoxicity results demonstrate that the pSBMA-A hydrogel coating is suitable for biomedical applications.

In Vivo Tissue Response Evaluation of the Hydrogel Coating

To evaluate the in vivo anti-inflammatory performance of the hydrogel coating, blank PVC and pSBMA-A samples were simultaneously implanted into the subcutaneous tissue of SD rats, followed by H&E staining and histological analysis. A schematic illustration of the implantation procedure and sample locations is shown in Figure a. The results showed that the tissues surrounding the blank PVC samples exhibited obvious redness, swelling, and inflammatory responses, whereas the tissues around the pSBMA-A samples maintained a largely normal morphology with no apparent inflammatory response. This may be attributed to the favorable hydrophilicity and mechanical flexibility of the pSBMA-A hydrogel coating. As shown in Figure b, compared with the blank PVC samples, the pSBMA-A samples induced significantly less inflammatory cell infiltration in the surrounding tissues, along with lower expression levels of the macrophage marker CD68 and the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6. Quantitative analysis in Figure c further demonstrated that relative to the blank PVC group, the expression levels of IL-6 and CD68 in the pSBMA-A group were reduced by approximately 48.3% and 26.5%, respectively. These results indicate that the pSBMA-A samples possess good in vivo biocompatibility and anti-inflammatory properties.

Discussion

Ag NP-Mediated Antibacterial Activity and Regulated Ag Release

The antibacterial performance of the pSBMA-A coating can be attributed to the combined effects of Ag NPs and the zwitterionic pSBMA hydrogel network. The Ag NPs synthesized in this study had a small average size of 15.99 ± 0.11 nm and were incorporated into the hydrogel coating. Compared with pBDA, which lacks the zwitterionic SBMA network, pSBMA mainly reduced bacterial adhesion through hydration-mediated antiadhesion behavior. However, this passive antifouling effect alone may be insufficient under complex physiological conditions. In contrast, pSBMA-A integrated the passive antiadhesion effect of the pSBMA hydration layer with the active antibacterial effect of Ag NPs, achieving high antibacterial activity against both E. coli and S. aureus at a low Ag concentration of 0.05 mM. It should be noted that the Ag concentration of 0.05 mM refers to the Ag feed concentration in the coating precursor rather than the absolute Ag loading immobilized in the final coating.

The antibacterial durability of pSBMA-A after immersion should not be simply attributed to the complete retention of Ag NPs within the hydrogel network. ICP-MS results showed that Ag was gradually released from the pSBMA-A coating over 1–11 days. Although an initial Ag release was observed within the first day, the release amount continued to increase during the subsequent immersion period. This suggests that the cross-linked pSBMA hydrogel network can regulate the diffusion of Ag species, reducing rapid Ag loss while allowing sustained Ag release. Therefore, the retained antibacterial activity after immersion is more reasonably explained by the combined contribution of surface-associated Ag NPs and the sustained release of Ag species.

Hydration-Mediated Antifouling and Reduced Thrombus Adhesion

The reduced thrombus adhesion of pSBMA and pSBMA-A is closely associated with the zwitterionic hydration layer formed by the sulfobetaine groups. This hydration layer can reduce nonspecific protein adsorption and weaken protein conditioning layer formation, thereby decreasing subsequent bacterial adhesion and thrombus deposition. The BSA adsorption assay further confirmed that pSBMA-containing coatings significantly suppressed nonspecific protein adsorption compared to bare PVC, providing mechanistic support for the reduced thrombus adhesion observed in the in vitro blood circulation experiment.

It should be noted that the present thrombus-related evaluation mainly supports reduced thrombus adhesion/deposition on the coated surfaces rather than a complete anticoagulant or antithrombotic function. Further platelet adhesion assays, platelet activation marker assays, and coagulation assays such as PT, APTT, and TT will be required in future studies.

Integrated Mechanism, Short-Term Coating Stability, and Limitations

Overall, pSBMA-A should be understood as an integrated interfacial system rather than a simple combination of Ag NPs and pSBMA. The PDA/BP layer enhances coating adhesion, pSBMA provides hydration-mediated antifouling, and Ag NPs provide antibacterial activity through surface-associated Ag and sustained Ag release. Short-term coating stability was further evaluated by PBS immersion for 3 days, followed by PBS flushing. SEM and elemental mapping showed that pSBMA-A retained a continuous morphology without obvious delamination or large-area peeling, and S/Ag signals remained detectable on the treated surface, supporting the short-term interfacial stability under the tested aqueous washing conditions.

As summarized in Table S1, compared with reported silver-containing catheter coatings, pSBMA-A achieves a balance among low-silver antibacterial activity, antifouling behavior, reduced thrombus adhesion, Ag release regulation, short-term coating stability, and acceptable biocompatibility. Meanwhile, the subcutaneous implantation model mainly reflects local tissue compatibility and the inflammatory response and cannot fully reproduce the actual blood-contacting catheter environment. Therefore, vascular implantation or extracorporeal circulation models will be needed in future studies to further validate the long-term anti-infective, thrombus-resistant, and biosafety performance of the coating.

Conclusions

In this study, a photoinitiated silver-loaded zwitterionic hydrogel coating, denoted as pSBMA-A, was constructed on blood-contacting PVC catheter surfaces by integrating a PDA/BP interfacial layer, a pSBMA hydrogel network, and Ag NPs. The PDA/BP layer enhanced interfacial anchoring, while the pSBMA network formed a hydrated antifouling interface and served as a matrix for Ag NPs’ incorporation. The resulting coating formed a continuous layer on the PVC surface, with an average thickness of 1.63 ± 0.18 μm.

The pSBMA-A coating exhibited high antibacterial activity against both E. coli and S. aureus at a low Ag concentration of 0.05 mM. ICP-MS analysis showed gradual Ag release over 1–11 days, suggesting that the pSBMA hydrogel network regulated Ag release and reduced rapid Ag loss. In addition, the pSBMA-containing coatings improved the surface hydrophilicity and reduced nonspecific BSA adsorption, supporting the antifouling role of the zwitterionic hydration layer. The pSBMA-A coating also reduced thrombus adhesion by approximately 77% compared to bare PVC, indicating thrombus-resistant surface properties.

Furthermore, the pSBMA-A coating showed acceptable hemocompatibility and cytocompatibility, with a hemolysis ratio below 2% and cell viability above 80%. Subcutaneous implantation further showed reduced levels of inflammatory cell infiltration and lower IL-6 and CD68 expression. After 3 days of PBS immersion followed by flushing, the coating retained its surface morphology and S/Ag elemental distribution, supporting its short-term stability under the tested aqueous washing conditions. Overall, this pSBMA-A coating provides a promising surface engineering strategy for improving the anti-infective, antifouling, and thrombus-resistant performance of blood-contacting PVC catheters.
