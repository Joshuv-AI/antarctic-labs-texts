Abstract
. We provide a stochastic analysis of an overlapping-generations model under incomplete markets. By casting individual optimization with idiosyncratic income risk into a forward–backward stochastic differential-equation (FBSDE) system, we (i) establish existence and uniqueness of the dynamic general-equilibrium interest rate; (ii) derive analytical and semi-explicit formulas for both the equilibrium interest-rate path and the “natural” borrowing limit—defined as the discounted expected shortfall of future income; and (iii) show how to compute closed-form derivatives of these equilibrium objects with respect to key model parameters. Our FBSDE-based approach yields tractable policy functions and equilibrium mappings without relying on high-dimensional PDE methods, offering clear insights into how income dynamics and demographic structure drive interest-rate fluctuations and credit constraints. MSC2020 Subject Classification: 91B70, 91B74, 93E20, 65K10, Keywords: Heterogeneous agent models, microeconomic modelling, stochastic optimal control, overlapping generations, numerical optimisation.



1.
Introduction

Recent demographic shifts, most notably declining fertility rates, combined with rapid technological innovation, have far-reaching effects on economic inequality. These trends impact cohorts and income groups unevenly, underscoring the critical need for models that explicitly incorporate agent heterogeneity.
Since
[
Samuelson, 1958
]
and
[
Diamond, 1965
]
, the overlapping-generations (OLG) framework has been pivotal in macroeconomics, particularly for analyzing the distributional effects of demographic and technological changes. OLG models capture intergenerational dynamics in finite-lived economies, where agents optimally allocate consumption and savings over time and interact through aggregate markets.

[
Ríos-Rull, 1996
]
and
[
Huggett, 1996
]
further enriched the framework by introducing idiosyncratic income risks and market incompleteness. In such settings, agents cannot fully insure against individual shocks, preventing perfect risk-pooling. Consequently, higher-income or older cohorts self-insure more effectively than lower-income or younger cohorts, generating persistent cross-sectional dispersion in wealth and consumption. Transitory labor-income shocks thus give rise to enduring differences in lifetime welfare.

It is worth noting that overlapping-generations models can yield theoretical predictions absent in infinitely lived-agent frameworks.
[
Galor and Ryder, 1989
]
,
[
Cass, 1972
]
, and
[
Shell, 1971
]
highlight equilibrium multiplicity and efficiency concerns in OLG settings.
[
Rao Aiyagari, 1985
]
shows that, under specific conditions, aggregate outcomes in OLG models coincide with those in corresponding infinitely lived-agent models. Moreover,
[
Yaari, 1965
]
and
[
Blanchard, 1985
]
formulate infinite-horizon representations of OLG economies with stochastic lifespans in a deterministic setting. To our knowledge, there is little work analyzing equilibrium in stochastic OLG economies under incomplete markets and heterogeneous agents, as the resulting equilibrium conditions give rise to highly nonlinear systems of PDEs that complicate equilibrium characterization.

In this paper we introduce a novel approach to the rigorous study of continuous-time OLG models with heterogeneous agents by combining tools from
stochastic control theory
, notably the Pontryagin maximum principle, and
forward–backward stochastic differential equations (FBSDEs)
. We derive explicit and semi-explicit expressions for economic quantities of interest in these models, in particular the
optimal consumption policy
,
equilibrium interest rates
,
natural borrowing limits
and functional derivatives of equilibrium interest rates with respect to capital supply. These results furnish new insights into the impact of demographic structure and income dynamics on macroeconomic outcomes. Our method represents a significant advance over existing PDE-based techniques, which typically impose restrictive assumptions such as wealth or income bounds or equilibrium stationarity
[
Achdou et al., 2021
,
Huggett, 1993
,
Aiyagari, 1994
]
.

We make contributions to the economic literature in providing analysis of equilibrium interest rates in continuous time OLG models. In
[
Demichelis and Polemarchakis, 2007
]
and
[
Edmond, 2008
]
the model is cast with fully deterministic endowments (no idiosyncratic risk).
[
Demichelis and Polemarchakis, 2007
]
construct a finite
-
horizon, log
-
utility example in which there is (up to time
-
shift) a unique non
-
stationary equilibrium price path in continuous time.
[
Edmond, 2008
]
shows that, under log utility, the equilibrium problem can be written as a linear integral equation for the inter-temporal interest rate function, which admits a unique globally stable solution and is straightforward to approximate numerically. Analogous existence-and-uniqueness results for OLG models with idiosyncratic income shocks and incomplete markets have not been established.

There exists work showing existence, and in certain instances, uniqueness of recursive equilibria in heterogeneous-agent models with incomplete markets. The vast majority of these results pertain to discrete-time economies (e.g.,
[
Acemoglu and Jensen, 2015
]
).
[
Pröhl, 2024
]
contributes to this literature by incorporating aggregate risk: leveraging the monotonicity of equilibrium correspondences and tools from convex analysis, she establishes that the generalized Euler operator is maximally monotone, which yields both a convergent solution algorithm and a unique recursive equilibrium in a Bewley–Aiyagari-style growth model subject to aggregate shocks. Building on these methods,
[
Cao, 2020
]
demonstrates the existence of extended recursive equilibria, in which policy and price functions also depend on the value function.
[
Brumm et al., 2017
]
further extend the analysis by proving the existence of sunspot-dependent recursive equilibria within finite-agent approximations. Our work adds to this strand of literature by studying a continuous-time overlapping-generations model under incomplete markets, enabling us to apply stochastic-analysis tools, specifically a forward–backward stochastic differential-equation (FBSDE) system, to establish existence and uniqueness of the dynamic general-equilibrium interest rate.

A second key result of our work is the derivation of an
endogenous natural borrowing limit
for individuals. In classical OLG and heterogeneous-agent models, borrowing limits are often required to prevent Ponzi schemes
[
Aiyagari, 1994
]
and remain identical for all households. In contrast, our framework shows that a heterogeneous, natural borrowing limit arises
endogenously
from agents’ optimization behaviour and terminal wealth constraints. We prove that the natural borrowing limit depends on the conditional expectation of future income. This result reveals that the borrowing limit depends on the present value of expected future income, discounted by the equilibrium interest rate path. Notably, this borrowing limit is
heterogeneous
across agents, as it reflects their individual income processes and wealth trajectories. This stands in sharp contrast to exogenous limits assumed in many existing models, making our framework more flexible for economic applications. Deriving model implications such as the heterogeneous natural borrowing limit is a particular advantage of our framework.

From a mathematical perspective, our work contributes to the growing literature on probabilistic methods for dynamic equilibrium analysis. While continuous-time formulations of models such as
[
Aiyagari, 1994
]
and
[
Huggett, 1993
]
have been considered using PDE approaches
[
Achdou et al., 2021
,
Achdou et al., 2014
,
Ambrose, 2021
]
, we adopt a purely stochastic formulation. In particular, we derive optimal consumption policies using solutions to coupled forward-backward stochastic differential equations. This approach avoids the boundary constraints required in PDE methods and provides semi-explicit solutions for consumption and wealth dynamics.
By treating the equilibrium interest rate as a functional of aggregate distributions, we establish a novel fixed-point argument for solving the dynamic general equilibrium problem.
We provide analytical expressions for the natural borrowing limit and prove its stability under perturbations in income processes or interest rate paths. This stability is essential for numerical implementations and economic interpretations.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the overlapping generations model in mathematical terms and describes the main objects we will work with in the remainder of the paper. Section
3
contains the main workhorse result of the paper, Theorem
3.4
, which gives an FBSDE representation of the optimal consumption policy in the life-cycle model. In addition, Section
3
contains a presentation of the deterministic life-cycle model, presentation of an a priori natural borrowing limit and proofs of existence and uniqueness of an optimal solution to the stochastic life-cycle model under suitable assumptions. Section
4
demonstrates important stability results on the optimal consumption policy in the life-cycle model and as a result demonstrates existence and uniqueness of a general equilibrium interest rate in the life-cycle case. Finally, Section
5
contains the most economically important results of the paper, combining the work of previous sections to analyse the overlapping generations model. Here we provide proofs of existence and uniqueness of the optimal consumption policies for each generation as well as existence and local uniqueness of a general equilibrium interest rate under certain reasonable assumptions on the coefficients and model parameters. Section
6
presents numerical simulations of partial equilibria in the life-cycle model.

Given a probability space

denote the space of all Borel measurable maps
X:\Omega\to\mathcal{X}
such that
\|X\|_{\mathscr{L}^{p}_{\omega}}<\infty

\mathscr{L}^{0}(\Omega;\mathcal{X})\coloneqq\{X:\Omega\to\mathcal{X}\,:\,X\,\text{ is Borel measurable }\}.

Similarly, given an interval

\|f\|_{\mathscr{L}^{p}_{I}}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\left(\int_{I}\|f(t)\|^{p}_{\mathcal{X}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}},&p\in[1,+\infty),\\

and set
\mathscr{L}^{p}(I;\mathcal{X})

for the space of bounded continuous maps

(1.1)

\|f\|_{\text{Lip}_{I}}\coloneqq\|f\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{I}}+\esssup_{t\neq s\,\in I}\frac{\|f(t)-f(s)\|_{\mathcal{X}}}{|t-s|}.

2.
Model setup

In this section we outline a continuous time overlapping generations general equilibrium model where agents live for a given finite interval of time and face stochastic and independent income shocks. Markets are incomplete in the sense that households can only self-insure against bad shocks by trading a single risk-free bond. In equilibrium, aggregate savings must by equal to an exogenous capital supply.

We fix a lifespan
L>0
, a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
which carries an uncountable family of Brownian motions
\{B^{b}\}_{b\in\mathbb{R}}
where each
B^{b}
is shifted so that
B^{b}_{b}=0
. Let
\{\mathcal{F}^{b}_{t}\}_{t\in[b,b+L]}
be the augmented natural filtration associated to
B^{b}|_{[b,b+L]}
. Then, for a given continuous path

\mathbb{R}\ni t\mapsto r_{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

for each
b\in\mathbb{R}
we describe a household by its wealth and income which are modelled according to the dynamics

(2.1)

\displaystyle w^{b}_{t}(r)

\displaystyle=w^{b}_{b}+\int_{b}^{t}r_{s}w^{b}_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{b}^{t}\eta^{b}_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int_{b}^{t}c_{s}^{b}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,\quad t\in[b,b+L]

(2.2)

\displaystyle\eta^{b}_{t}

\displaystyle=\eta^{b}_{b}+\int_{b}^{t}\mu_{s}(\eta^{b}_{s})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{b}^{t}\sigma_{s}(\eta^{b}_{s})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B^{b}_{s},\quad t\in[b,b+L]

where
\eta^{b}_{b}\sim\rho^{\eta}_{b}
,
w^{b}_{b}\sim\rho^{w}_{b}
for families of laws
\{\rho^{\eta}_{b}\}_{b\in\mathbb{R}}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\,\{\rho^{w}_{b}\}_{b\in\mathbb{R}}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
and

\mu:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R},

are suitable coefficients such that for each
b\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a unique strong solution
\eta^{b}
to (
2.2
).

Each realisation

Each household chooses their consumption

where

\mathcal{A}^{b}\coloneqq\left\{c^{b}:[b,b+L]\to\mathbb{R}\,:\,\text{progressively }\{\mathcal{F}^{b}_{t}\}_{t\in[b,b+L]}\text{-measurable and continuous}\right\}.

Remark 2.1

(CRRA Utility Functions)
.

A common example of utility functions to keep in mind are those known as the
constant-relative-risk-aversion (CRRA)
utility functions. For
\gamma>0
, these are given by the expression

(2.5)

u(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{x^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma},\quad\mathrm{for}\quad&x\geq 0,\\
-\infty\quad&x<0.\end{cases}

These functions are convex with unbounded derivative at
x=0
, this models an individuals strong desire to consume something rather than nothing (or hold any positive amount of wealth over no wealth). This unbounded gradient, however, poses inevitable mathematical challenges. In subsequent sections we discuss approximations to (
2.5

3.18

2.3

\gamma_{1},\,\gamma_{2}>0

When each individual optimizes the discounted payoff function given in (

c^{b}

(2.6)

v^{b}_{L}\big(t,w^{b},\eta^{b}\big)=\sup_{c^{b}\in\mathcal{A}^{b}}\,\mathcal{J}^{b}_{L}\big(t,w^{b},\eta^{b}|c\big),\quad t\in[b,b+L].

Furthermore, we define

(2.7)

c^{b;*}_{t}=\argsup_{c^{b}\in\mathcal{A}^{b}}\,\mathcal{J}^{b}_{L}\big(t,w^{b},\eta^{b}|c^{b}\big),\quad t\in[b,b+L],

as the optimal path of consumption for the agent born at

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w_{t}^{b;*}=\big(r_{t}w_{t}^{b;*}-c_{t}^{b;*}+\eta^{*}_{t}\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,\quad w^{b;*}_{b}\sim\rho^{w}_{b}.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that finding the optimal consumption
c^{b;*}
for each
b\in\mathbb{R}
as prescribed by (

) gives a functional pathwise dependence of
c^{b;*}
on
w^{b}
and the interest rate
r
. In principle it also depends on the income process
\eta^{b}
but this dependence is encoded by

. Furthermore, since the wealth dynamic also depends on the interest rate path, we can re-write (
2.8

(2.9)

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w_{t}^{b;*}=\big(r_{t}w_{t}^{b;*}-c_{t}^{b;*}\big(w^{b;*}(r),r\big)+\eta^{*}_{t}\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,\quad w^{b;*}_{b}\sim\rho^{w}_{b}.

We will later give a semi-explicit expression for this functional dependence, which reflects the known explicit solution to the related life-cycle model with deterministic income, Section
3.1
.

Since total capital is fixed in our model, the interest rate must be chosen to keep aggregate wealth constant. In the OLG model this requires us to integrate total wealth over a probability measure describing the chance of being born at each time. To this end we introduce the notion of a
flow of demographic measures

(2.10)

\mathbb{R}\ni t\mapsto\nu_{t}\in\mathcal{P}([t-L,t]),

where for any Borel set
A\in\mathcal{B}([t-L,t])
the quantity
\nu_{t}(A)\in[0,1]
describes the probability that a randomly chosen individual alive in the economy at time
t\in\mathbb{R}
was born in
A
. Hence, the aggregate wealth, income and consumption are written as follows, taking account of the functional dependencies described in Remark
2.2
,

(2.11)

\displaystyle W^{L}_{t}(r)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{t-L}^{t}w_{t}^{b}(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}(b)

(2.12)

\displaystyle C^{L}_{t}(w,r)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{t-L}^{t}c^{b}_{t}(w^{b},r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}(b),

(2.13)

\displaystyle N^{L}_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{t-L}^{t}\eta^{b}_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}(b).

In this setting, the notion of a general equilibrium is defined as follows.

Definition 2.3
.

Given a
lifespan

L>0
and a capital supply
K\in C(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
we say that the associated overlapping generations model (
5.1
)-(
5.3
) and (
2.11
)-(
2.13
) is in
general equilibrium
if the interest rate
r:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is such that

(2.14)

\mathbb{W}^{L}_{t}(r)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[W^{L}_{t}(r)\right]=K_{t}\quad\text{for all}\,\,t\in\mathbb{R}.

For brevity we sometimes say that
r
is a
general equilibrium interest rate
for the associated overlapping generations model.

Remark 2.4
.

The path
t\mapsto K_{t}
represents the capital supply of the economy at time
t
and is given exogenously in the current paper for simplicity, and is often set to
0
in many applications. It provides a relationship with the amount of capital that is borrowed and the amount that must be saved. In economic theory, the capital supply
K
is often determined endogenously through a production technology, see e.g.
[
Aiyagari, 1994
]
and
[
Krusell and Anthony A. Smith, 1998
]
for details on this. For simplicity of presentation we keep the production function abstract here, simply positing an exogenous capital flow, but plan to investigate the combined setting in more detail in future works.

Remark 2.5
.

As noted in Remark
2.2
, the optimal consumption is a pathwise functional of the wealth and interest rate processes. Thus, the closure condition (
2.14
) exhibits a dependence of the
general equilibrium
interest rate on both the law of the family of processes
\{w^{b}\}_{b\in\mathbb{R}}
and the
flow of demographic measures
. In this regard, our general equilibrium problem is closely related to the setting of mean field control. Agents do not select their consumption policy
c^{b;*}
in order to advantageously affect the interest rate
r
, however, the interest rate must be set in order to attain a desired distribution of wealth. However, our model does not encode a notion of optimal wealth distribution, as would be common in a genuine mean field control setup. Instead, the general equilibrium condition is simply a fixing of the scenario and at important economic output of the model. As discussed in
[
Carvalho et al., 2016
]
there is evidence that demographic shift has a measurable impact on the real interest rate. This is something that we aim to investigate computationally.

Solving the general equilibrium problem for (
2.1
)-(
2.4
) is equivalent to the following procedure:

i)

Fix a deterministic interest rate
r\in C(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})

ii)

For each
b\in\mathbb{R}
, solve the optimal control problem

\begin{cases}\sup_{c^{b}\in\mathcal{A}^{b}}\mathcal{J}^{b}_{L}(w^{b},\eta^{b}|c^{b})\\
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w^{b}_{t}=(r_{t}w^{b}_{t}-c^{b}_{t}+\eta^{b}_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,\quad w^{b}_{b}\sim\rho^{w}_{b},\\
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\eta^{b}_{t}=\mu_{t}(\eta^{b}_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{t}(\eta^{b}_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{t},\quad\eta^{b}_{b}\sim\rho^{\eta}_{b}.\end{cases}

iii)

View the optimal solution
(w^{*},c^{*})
as a functional
r\mapsto(w^{*}(r),c^{*}(r))
and show that for a given flow of demographic measures
t\mapsto\nu_{t}
, there exists a unique continuous path
\bar{r}
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t-L}^{t}w^{b;*}_{t}(\bar{r})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}(b)\right]=K_{t},\quad\text{for all }\,t\in\mathbb{R}.

We complete these three steps under a number of reasonable regularity and structural assumptions on the coefficients involved as well as a restriction to
local
uniqueness of the general equilibrium interest rate in the space of all continuous paths. As already mentioned, along the way we also derive explicit or semi-explicit expressions for a number of economically relevant aggregates and outputs of the model.

Since Step
ii)
is identical (up to shifting the domain) for each
b\in\mathbb{R}
, our first focus will be on the so-called
life-cycle
model which considers an economy of one generation living in isolation on
[0,L]
where each household solves the same optimal control problem.

3.2.
The Stochastic Maximum Principle and BSDEs

We now move to the stochastic life-cycle problem (
3.2

is through the dynamic programming principle, leading to an Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation. An alternative is to use the stochastic maximum principle, by analogy with the deterministic case discussed above. In this article we choose the latter and illustrate how this approach provides insightful representation of the economic system of heterogeneous agents of interest. Furthermore, since the noise in (

[
Ambrose, 2021
]
. In contrast, the stochastic maximum principle approach allows us to employ fixed point arguments at the price of making some regularity assumptions on the utility functions. We will subsequently show that these regularity assumptions are satisfied by suitable approximations to the commonly used CRRA functions, see Example
3.18

The following theorem is the main technical result of our paper, which shows that solving the stochastic optimal control problem (

3.4.
Natural Borrowing Limits and Wealth Asymptotic

A finite time horizon with both an incentive to consume and an incentive to bequeath encodes a strong disciplinary effect on the individuals. Since, in the idealized model, individuals receives an infinitely negative payoff for ending up with negative wealth at time
L
, the optimal savings/consumption policy must be such that the the terminal wealth at time
L
is non-negative.
This creates a natural borrowing limit for the individuals; a debt limit such that even borrowing up to this limit, individuals expect to repay their debt before time
L
. A distinct advantage of the FSBDE approach to describing the life-cycle dynamics is that we can express the natural borrowing limit as an analytic function, which we will see is a natural generalisation of the deterministic counterpart.

In the economic literature
[
Aiyagari, 1994
]
introduced the concept of natural borrowing limit in an infinite horizon economy with finite income states. In a stationary equilibrium with interest rate
r>0
, the natural borrowing limit is defined as
-\eta^{\frac{1}{r}}
where
\eta^{1}
is the lowest income.
In a continuous-time model with a discrete two-state income process
[
Achdou et al., 2014
]
, in which
\eta_{t}\in\{\eta^{1},\eta^{2}\}
for all
t\in[0,L]
, the natural borrowing limit at any point in time can be proven to be the net present value of all future income - in the
low
income state. That is, suppose
\eta^{1}<\eta^{2}
, then the borrowing limit (equivalently lower bound of wealth
\underline{w}
) at time
t\in[0,L]
will be

4
4

4

When
r
is constant we get
\underline{w}=-{\frac{\eta^{1}}{r}}\left(e^{-rt}-e^{-rL}\right)
. Now let
t\to 0
and
L\to\infty
to obtain Aiyagari’s natural borrowing limit.
.

\underline{w}=-\eta^{1}\int_{t}^{L}e^{-\int_{s}^{L}r_{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

The reason for this borrowing limit, even for people in the high income state, is that at any point in time there is a greater than
0
probability that you will jump to, and remain in, the lowest income state for the rest of you life (until time
L
). Therefore, if you borrow more than relative to the lowest possible income, there will be a positive probability that you end up with
-\infty
in your value function which is sub-optimal.

The same thought experiment can be extended to a continuous time model with discrete state space
\eta_{t}\in\{\eta^{1},\eta^{2},\ldots,\eta^{N}\}
for all
t\in[0,L]
, as long as there is a positive probability of jumping from your current income level to the lowest, at any point in time.
However, if we assume the stochastic income process to be a random walk, in which the state space is given by
\{\eta^{1},\ldots,\eta^{N}\}
with
\eta^{i}\leq\eta^{j}
for all
i\leq j
, then at each point in time the income process may jump one level up or one level down. Thus, the natural borrowing limit at time
t
depends on the state of the income process at time
t
. In particular, if
\eta_{t}=\eta^{i}
, then the maximal amount you are willing to borrow will be given in terms of the lowest income state possible in the next period, i.e. given that
\eta_{t}=\eta^{i}

\underline{w}_{t}=-\eta^{i-1}\int_{t}^{L}e^{-\int_{s}^{L}r_{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

The reason for this is that the consumption process may be updated accordingly in the case of a bad income shock. Note in particular that since the income process may jump up and down, the borrowing limit in this model is depending on time as well as the current income level at that time. The natural borrowing limit therefore becomes heterogeneous in income. This gives a much more generous natural borrowing limit for individuals in high income states.

Just as a random walk can approximate a Brownian motion, the next proposition will show that when income is an Itô diffusion we also recover a heterogeneous natural borrowing limit in the case of CRRA utility functions, conditioned on having a unique solution to the associated optimal control problem.

Proposition 3.11

(Lower bound for the natural borrowing limit)
.

Let
\gamma_{1},\,\gamma_{2}>0
,

and

). Then for all

Our first observation is to note that since

is assumed to be an optimal control, the lower bound for

must correspond to a situation where

, since any positive amount of consumption is strictly preferred.

Secondly, we note that formally, for

Hence,

\displaystyle\infty>c_{t}(w)=

\displaystyle\,(u^{\prime}_{1})^{-1}\bigg(\lambda\exp\bigg(\int_{t}^{L}(r_{s}-\delta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\mathbb{E}\left[u^{\prime}_{2}(w_{L})|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\bigg)\geq 0.

Furthermore, since

is the optimal consumption policy it cannot be the case that

\displaystyle c_{t}(w)=

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\lambda^{-\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}}\int_{t}^{L}(r_{s}-\delta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\bigg)\,\mathbb{E}\left[w_{L}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]^{\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\gamma_{1}}},

where in the last line we used the conditional Jensen inequality applied to the convex function

3.5.
Asymptotic Values of
r\mapsto\mathbb{E}[w_{t}(r)]

Using the specific structure of the optimal consumption and wealth, we can provided quantitative bounds for the asymptotic limits of expected wealth,
\mathbb{E}[w_{t}]
as a function of
r\in\mathbb{R}
. As expected, when
r\rightarrow\infty
, we can show that aggregate wealth
\mathbb{E}[w_{t}]
also tends to infinity. From the lower bound of wealth found in Proposition
3.11
it is reasonable to expect that wealth diverges to
-\infty
as
r\rightarrow-\infty
. However, proving this seems to be challenging. For our purpose, however, it is sufficient to prove that this limit is non-positive. We prove these facts in the next proposition.

Proposition 3.15
.

Assume
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are such that

(u^{\prime}_{1})^{-1}(u^{\prime}_{2}(x))\leq x\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R},

and that the inverse
(u^{\prime}_{1})^{-1}
is homogenous of degree
-\alpha
for some
\alpha\geq 1
.
Suppose further that there exists a pair
(w,c)
which solves (
3.24
).
Then, for all
t\in[0,L]
it holds that

\lim_{r\mapsto\infty}\mathbb{E}[w_{t}(r)]=\infty,\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\lim_{r\mapsto-\infty}\mathbb{E}[w_{t}(r)]\leq 0.

Proof.

By variation of constants formula, we can write

\mathbb{E}[w_{t}]=e^{rt}\left(\mathbb{E}[w_{0}]+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-rs}(\mathbb{E}[\eta_{s}]-\mathbb{E}[c_{s}(r)])\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right).

To analyse the asymptotic behaviour when
r\rightarrow\infty
or
-\infty
we need to investigate the behaviour of the final integral term in the above representation, namely

A(r):=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-rs}\left(\mathbb{E}[\eta_{s}]-\mathbb{E}[c_{s}(r)]\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Using that
(u^{\prime}_{1})^{-1}
is convex and homogenous of degree
-\alpha
, and that
u^{\prime}_{2}
is such that

(u^{\prime}_{1})^{-1}(u^{\prime}_{2}(x))\leq x\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R},

it follows from Jensen’s inequality that

\mathbb{E}[c_{t}(r)]\leq\lambda^{-\alpha}e^{-\alpha r(L-t)}\mathbb{E}[w_{L}].

Note that

\mathbb{E}[w_{L}]=e^{rL}\bigg(\mathbb{E}[w_{0}]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{L}e^{-rs}\big(\eta_{s}-c_{s}(r)\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\Big]\bigg)\leq e^{rL}\bigg(\mathbb{E}[w_{0}]+\int_{0}^{L}e^{-rs}\mathbb{E}[\eta_{s}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\bigg).

Combining these estimates, we see that

\mathbb{E}[c_{t}(r)]\leq\lambda^{-\alpha}e^{(1-\alpha)rL}e^{\alpha rt}\bigg(\mathbb{E}[w_{0}]+\int_{0}^{L}e^{-rs}\mathbb{E}[\eta_{s}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\bigg)

Using this, we see that

A(r)\geq\int_{0}^{t}e^{-rs}\mathbb{E}[\eta_{s}]-\lambda^{-\alpha}e^{(1-\alpha)rL}e^{(\alpha-1)rs}\bigg(\mathbb{E}[w_{0}]+\int_{0}^{L}e^{-rl}\mathbb{E}[\eta_{l}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}l\bigg)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Recall that
t\leq L
and
\kappa\geq 1
. Then we see that

\lambda^{-\alpha}e^{(1-\alpha)rL}e^{(\alpha-1)rs}\rightarrow 0\quad{\mathrm{as}}\quad r\rightarrow\infty,

and thus it follows that

A(r)\geq 0\quad\mathrm{as}\quad r\rightarrow\infty.

Using this, together with the assumption that
\mathbb{E}[w_{0}]>0
and that

\mathbb{E}[w_{t}]\geq e^{rt}(w_{0}+A(r)).

we see that for all
t\in[0,L]

\mathbb{E}[w_{t}]\rightarrow\infty\quad\mathrm{as}\quad r\rightarrow\infty.

We conclude by proving that
\lim_{r\to-\infty}\mathbb{E}[w_{t}]\leq 0
for all
t\in[0,L]
. But this follows from an easier estimate; by assumption,
c_{t}\geq 0
for all
t
. Then clearly

\mathbb{E}[w_{t}]\leq e^{rt}\bigg(\mathbb{E}[w_{0}]+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-rs}\mathbb{E}[\eta_{s}]\bigg)

From this it is clear that
\mathbb{E}[w_{t}]\leq 0
as
r\rightarrow-\infty
for all
t
.
∎

Remark 3.16
.

In the context of CRRA utilities with
u_{1}(x)=u_{2}(x)=\frac{x^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}

(u^{\prime}_{1})^{-1}(x)=x^{-\frac{1}{\gamma}}

Remark 3.17
.

In principle, if we know that a function
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is continuous and that
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}f(r)=\infty
and
\lim_{r\rightarrow-\infty}f(r)\leq 0
,
then we can conclude that for any
K\geq 0
there exists an
r\in\mathbb{R}
such that
f(r)=K
. That is, in our context we conclude that for each
t\in[0,L]
there exists an
r\in\mathbb{R}
(depending on

) such that

. Therefore, this does not allow us to conclude that there exists a general equilibrium interest rate path in the life-cycle case. To see this, recall that at any time

, wealth

is a functional of the entire path of the interest rate, not just the interest rate at time

. Concretely, it is not clear, from Proposition

that there exists a continuous selection

to give

on

. However, in the OLG setting analysed in subsequent sections, we can use this technique to prove existence in stationary economies.

Existence and uniqueness of life-cycle SDE dynamics

Theorem

\displaystyle(u_{1}^{\prime})^{-1}\left(\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\int_{t}^{L}(r_{s}-\delta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)u^{\prime}_{2}(w_{L})|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\right),

on
[0,L]
, where we see the consumption
c
as a functional of the continuous paths

.
Note that by variation of constants, we can rewrite the equation for wealth as

w_{t}=e^{-\int_{0}^{t}r_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}w_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}r_{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\right)\left(c_{s}(w,r)+\eta_{s}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,\quad t\in[0,L].

The purpose of this section is to show that under our standing Assumption
2.6
there exists a unique solution to (
3.16
).
The following example illustrates an approximation to the commonly used CRRA utility functions which satisfy Assumption
2.6
.

Example 3.18
.

The well-studied CRRA utility functions do not satisfy Assumption
2.6
, since for
\gamma>0
one has

u(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{1-\gamma}x^{1-\gamma},&x>0,\\
-\infty,&x\leq 0,\end{cases}\quad\Rightarrow\quad u^{\prime}(x)=\begin{cases}x^{-\gamma},&x>0,\\
-\infty,&x\leq 0.\end{cases}

The structure of these utility functions is such that
u(|x|)\gg u(-|x|)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
; in other words, positive inputs always return higher outputs than a negative input. In practical terms, this models the idea that agents would always rather consume any positive quantity than any negative quantity. With this in mind, for practical purposes, we can suitably approximate
u
by a sufficiently regular function, retaining the property of preferential consumption at the level of machine tolerance, along with concavity.

For
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
and any
p\geq 1
, let us define

u_{\varepsilon,p}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{1-\gamma}x^{1-\gamma},&x\geq\varepsilon,\\
-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{p}}x^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p-1}}\right)x-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{p-2}}+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\varepsilon^{1-\gamma},&x\leq\varepsilon.\end{cases}

The quadratic polynomial is chosen so that the function
x\mapsto u_{\varepsilon,p}(x)
remains convex while satisfying Assumption
2.6
and is continuous at
x=\varepsilon
. To wit, taking the first derivative we find

u^{\prime}_{\varepsilon,p}(x)=\begin{cases}x^{-\gamma},&x\geq\varepsilon,\\
-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p}}x+\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p-1}}\right),&x\leq\varepsilon,\end{cases}

and so

u^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon,p}(x)=\begin{cases}-x^{-(\gamma+1)},&x>\varepsilon,\\
-\infty,&x=\varepsilon,\\
-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p}},&x<\varepsilon.\end{cases}

Hence,
x\mapsto u_{\varepsilon,p}(x)
is globally concave. In addition, we have

(u^{\prime}_{\varepsilon})^{-1}(y)=\begin{cases}y^{-\gamma},&y\geq\varepsilon,\\
-\varepsilon^{p}y-\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p-1}}\right),&y\leq\varepsilon.\end{cases}

So that one readily checks that Assumption
2.6
is satisfied.

\eta\in\mathscr{L}^{0}(\Omega;C_{L})
given stochastic process. Then, for any given
w,\,v\in\mathscr{L}^{0}(\Omega;C_{L})
and
r,\,h\in C_{L}
, it holds that

(3.22)

\sup_{w\in\mathscr{L}^{0}(\Omega;C_{L})}\|\Theta_{L}(w,r)\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\leq(1+L)\exp(\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}})\left(|w_{0}|+\|\eta\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\kappa\right),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

and

\displaystyle\|\Theta_{L}(w,r)-\Theta_{L}(v,r)\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}

\displaystyle\leq LC\kappa\exp(\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}L)\|w-v\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}},\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

\displaystyle\|\Theta_{T}(w,r)-\Theta_{L}(w,h)\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}

\displaystyle\leq LC\kappa\exp(2(\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\|h\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}})L)\|r-h\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}},\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

Proof.

From the definition of
\Theta_{L}
in (
3.21
), we begin by observing that

(3.23)

\|\Theta_{L}(w,r)\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\leq\exp(L\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}})|w_{0}|+L\exp(L\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}})\left(\|\eta\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\|c(w,r)\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\right).

Invoking the bound from (

\|\Theta_{L}(w,r)\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\leq\exp(L\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}})|w_{0}|+L\exp(L\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}})\left(\|\eta\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\kappa\right),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

From this estimate, we collect terms and conclude that (
3.22
) holds.

We continue on to prove
stability. To this end, let us first fix two random paths
w,\,v
in
\mathscr{L}^{2}(\Omega;C_{L})
, and consider the difference

\Theta_{L}(w,r)_{t}-\Theta_{L}(v,r)_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}r_{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\right)(c_{s}(w,r)-c_{s}(v,r))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Using the stability in wealth obtained for the consumption, see (

\|\Theta_{L}(w,r)-\Theta_{L}(v,r)\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\leq L\lambda\kappa^{2}\exp(2\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}L)\|w-v\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}},\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

At last we consider the stability of the mapping

. Again, we observe that given
r,\,h\in C_{L}
, one has

\displaystyle\Theta_{L}(w,r)_{t}-\Theta_{L}(w,h)_{t}=

\displaystyle\,\int_{0}^{t}\left[\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}r_{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\right)-\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}h_{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\right)\right]w_{s}

\displaystyle\,+\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}h_{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\right)\left[c_{s}(w,r)-c_{s}(w,h)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Now applying the stability in interest rate of the consumption policy, (

\|\Theta_{L}(w,r)-\Theta_{L}(w,h)\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\leq L\lambda\kappa\exp(2(\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\|h\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}})T)\|r-h\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}},\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

which concludes the proof.
∎

Combining the a-priori estimates and stability results above we obtain existence and uniqueness of solutions to the SDE system (

). This is simply a consequence of choosing

sufficiently small in Proposition

4.
General Equilibria of the Life-Cycle Model

We show that a unique general equilibrium interest rate exists for the life-cycle model.
While a life-cycle model with finite lifespans in itself is not a particularly economically relevant, the mathematical results obtained on the way allow us to obtain existence and uniqueness of a general equilibrium in the overlapping generations model.

4.1.
Dynamics of the equilibrium interests rates

We define the notion of general equilibria in the life-cycle model.

Definition 4.1

(General Equilibrium in the life-cycle Model)
.

Let
\{w_{t}^{*}\}_{t\in[0,L]}
be an optimal wealth process solving (
3.2
). Given a continuous path
K\in C([0,L];\mathbb{R})
we say that an interest rate path
r\in C([0,L];\mathbb{R})
is a
general equilibrium interest rate
for the associated life-cycle model if,

(4.1)

\mathbb{E}[w_{t}^{*}]=K_{t},\quad\forall t\in[0,L].

Our first step is to formulate a fixed point equation that must be satisfied by any general equilibrium interest rate for the life-cycle model. To this end, we view the consumption and resulting wealth processes as functionals of the interest rate path
C([0,L];\mathbb{R})\ni r\mapsto(w(r),c(w(r),r))
, see Remark
2.2
. Corollary
3.21
shows that this map is well defined for sufficiently small lifespans
L\in(0,L^{*})
with
L^{*}
obtained therein. The following proposition obtains an a priori functional identity which is in one-to-one correspondence with the property of being a general equilibrium rate.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
L>0
,
\eta\in\mathscr{L}^{0}(\Omega;C_{L})
be a given income process, and consider a function
K\in C^{1}([0,L];\mathbb{R})
. Suppose there exists a pair
(w,c)
which solve (
3.24
) on
[0,L]
such that
\mathbb{E}[w_{0}]=K_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, there exists a general equilibrium according to Definition
4.1
for capital allocation
t\mapsto K_{t}
, if and only if there exists a continuous path
r\in C([0,L];\mathbb{R})
which satisfies the following functional equation

(4.2)

K_{t}r_{t}=\dot{K}_{t}+\mathbb{E}[c_{t}(r)]-\mathbb{E}[\eta_{t}],\quad\forall\,t\in[0,L].

In particular, if
K_{t}\equiv 0
for all
t
, then
r
must solve the following functional equation

\mathbb{E}[c_{t}(r)]=\mathbb{E}[\eta_{t}].

Proof.

Assume first that there exists an
r\in C([0,L];\mathbb{R})
such that the economy is in general equilibrium according to Definition
4.1
for a path
t\mapsto K_{t}
, i.e. that
\mathbb{E}[w_{t}(r)]=K_{t}
for all
t\in[0,L]
. It follows that the expected wealth must satisfy the following equation

\mathbb{E}[w_{t}]=K_{t}=K_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}K_{s}r_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[c_{s}(r)]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\eta_{s}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,\quad\text{for all }\,t\in[0,L]

Differentiating this equation with respect to
t
and then rearranging, it follows that in equilibrium the interest rate path
r:[0,L]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
must satisfy the functional equation

K_{t}r_{t}=\dot{K}_{t}+\left(\mathbb{E}[c_{t}(r)]-\mathbb{E}[\eta_{t}]\right),\quad\forall\,t\in[0,L].

On the other hand, if
r\in C([0,L];\mathbb{R})
satisfies (
4.2
), we know that the wealth dynamics satisfy:

w_{t}=w_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}r_{s}w_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}c_{s}(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\eta_{t}.

Taking expectations on both sides, using the assumption that
\mathbb{E}[w_{0}]=K
, and inserting the relation that
\mathbb{E}[c_{t}(r)]-\mathbb{E}[\eta_{t}]=r_{t}K_{t}-\dot{K}_{t}

\mathbb{E}[w_{t}]=K_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}r_{s}\mathbb{E}[w_{s}]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}\left(r_{s}K_{s}-\dot{K}_{s}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

By rearranging the terms, and defining
Z_{t}=\mathbb{E}[w_{t}]-K_{t}
we see that this equation can be formulated in terms of
Z
as the following equation

(4.3)

Z_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}r_{s}Z_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

It is clear that the only solution to this linear equation is
Z_{t}\equiv 0
for all
t
, and thus
\mathbb{E}[w_{t}]=K_{t}
if the interest rate
r
satisfies (
4.2
). This concludes the proof.
∎

The identity (
4.2
) has a natural economic interpretation. If
K_{t}=0
for all
t\in[0,L]
then thinking of
r
as the price of consumption, aggregate consumption must equal aggregate income at all times to keep zero net wealth in the economy.
If
K_{t}\neq 0
then aggregate consumption must exceed aggregate income by a factor proportional to the interest rate in order to meet the capital requirements. Note that, we implicitly set the depreciation rate to zero in our case.

With the above dynamics of the equilibrium interest rate, we can also analyse the impact of a change in the capital supply
K
on the interest rate. The following proposition gives this expression in terms of the Fréchet derivative of consumption with respect to the interest rate.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
(w,c)
be a solution to (
3.24
)
K_{\,\cdot\,}\equiv K\in\mathbb{R}
and
r(K)\coloneqq\in C([0,L];\mathbb{R})
be an interest rate path satisfying (
4.2
). Then, for any
t\in[0,L]
, it holds that

(4.4)

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}K}r_{t}(K)=\begin{cases}(\mathbb{E}[D_{r}c_{t}(r)]-K\mathbb{I})^{-1}r_{t},&K\neq 0,\\
\mathbb{E}[D_{r}c_{t}(r)]^{-1}0,&K=0.\end{cases},

where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity operator,
D_{r}c_{t}(r)
denotes the Fréchet derivative of
c_{t}(r)
with respect to the path
r
and the formula holds whenever this exists and the required operator is invertible.

Proof.

Starting from (
4.2
), we first assume that
K\neq 0
and compute the derivative of
r_{t}
with respect to
K
, using the chain rule to see that we must have

r_{t}+K\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}K}r_{t}=\mathbb{E}[D_{r}c_{t}(r)]\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}K}r_{t}\quad\iff\quad\left(\mathbb{E}[D_{r}c_{t}(r)]-K\mathbb{I}\right)\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}K}r_{t}=r_{t}.

So that rearranging we obtain the claimed expression when
K\neq 0
.

When
K=0
, we start again from (
4.2
) to see that one must have

\mathbb{E}[D_{r}c_{t}(r)]\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}K}r_{t}=0,

which again gives the claimed expression after taking the inverse of
\mathbb{E}[D_{r}c_{t}(r)]
on both sides.
∎

4.2.
Marshallian stability in the life-cycle

The general equilibrium interest rate is found at the balance between aggregate consumption and aggregate income, and it confirms that in order for the interest rate to stay positive aggregate consumption must be greater than aggregate income.
Furthermore, under the assumption that the income process
t\mapsto\eta_{t}
is a
(pathwise) continuous process, the wealth process must be a (pathwise) continuous process, and thus the interest rate is a continuous path
t\mapsto r_{t}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
L>0
,
r,\,\tilde{r}\in C([0,L];\mathbb{R})
be two continuous deterministic interest rate processes,
w_{0},\,\tilde{w}_{0}\in\mathscr{L}^{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
and
\eta\in\mathscr{L}^{0}(\Omega;C_{L})
be a given income process. Then, letting
(w(r),c(w(r),r))
and
(w(\tilde{r}),c(w(\tilde{r}),\tilde{r}))
be associated solutions to the life-cycle equation (
3.24
) on
[0,L]
, with initial data
w(r)|_{t=0}=w_{0}
and
w(\tilde{r})|_{t=0}=\tilde{w}_{0}
, one has

(4.5)

\begin{aligned} \|w(r)\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}&\leq(1+L)e^{\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}}\left(|w_{0}|+\|\eta\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\kappa\right),\end{aligned}\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

Furthermore, the following bounds hold

(4.6)

\displaystyle\|w(r)-w(\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\lesssim\,A\bigg(

\displaystyle|w_{0}-\tilde{w}_{0}|

\displaystyle+L\bigg((1+L)\left(|w_{0}|+\|\eta\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\kappa\right)+\lambda\kappa\,\bigg)\|r-\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\bigg),

and

(4.7)

\|c(w(r),r)-c(w(\tilde{r}),\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\lesssim\,\lambda\kappa(1+\kappa)Ae^{L\|\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}}\bigg(|w_{0}-\tilde{w}_{0}|\\
+L\bigg((1+L)\left(|w_{0}|+\|\eta\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\kappa\right)+\lambda\kappa\,\bigg)\|r-\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\bigg).

Here we have used the shorthand notation

A\coloneqq A(r,L,\lambda,\kappa)\coloneqq\exp\left(L\Big(\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\lambda\kappa^{2}e^{(1+L)\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}}\Big)\right).

Proof.

The first bound, (
4.5
) follows directly by applying (
3.22
) to the fixed point
\Theta(w,r)=w(r)
, where
\Theta
was defined in (
3.21
). To show (
4.6
), first note that for all
0\leq s<t\leq L
, we have

w_{s,t}(r)-w_{s,t}(\tilde{r})=w_{s}(r)-w_{s}(\tilde{r})\\
+\int_{s}^{t}(r_{u}-\tilde{r}_{u})w_{u}(r)+\tilde{r}_{u}\left(w_{u}(r)-w_{u}(\tilde{r})\right)+\left(c_{u}(w(r),r)-c_{u}(w(\tilde{r}),\tilde{r})\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

So that using (
3.18
), (
3.19
) and (
4.5
) yields

\displaystyle\|w(r)-w(\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{[s,t]}}\leq

\displaystyle|w_{s}(r)-w_{s}(\tilde{r})|+|t-s|\|w(r)\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\|r-\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+|t-s|\|\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\|w(r)-w(\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{[s,t]}}

\displaystyle+|t-s|\|c(w(r),r)-c(w(r),\tilde{r}))\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{[s,t]}}

\displaystyle+|t-s|\|c(w(r),\tilde{r})-c(w(\tilde{r}),\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{[s,t]}}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle|w_{s}(r)-w_{s}(\tilde{r})|+|t-s|(1+L)e^{\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}}\left(|w_{0}|+\|\eta\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\kappa\right)\|r-\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}

\displaystyle+|t-s|\|\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\|w(r)-w(\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{[s,t]}}

\displaystyle+|t-s|\lambda\kappa e^{L\left(\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\|\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\right)}\|r-\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}

\displaystyle+|t-s|\lambda\kappa^{2}e^{L\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}}\|w(r)-w(\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{[s,t]}}.

Defining the quantities,

\mathfrak{A}(w_{0},\eta,\kappa)\coloneqq|w_{0}|+\|\eta\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\kappa\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{B}(L,r,\tilde{r})\coloneqq e^{L\left(\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\|\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\right)}

and rewriting the above estimate, gives the following bound

\displaystyle\|w(r)-w(\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{[s,t]}}\leq

\displaystyle\,|w_{s}(r)-w_{s}(\tilde{r})|

\displaystyle+|t-s|\left((1+L)e^{\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}}\mathfrak{A}(w_{0},\eta,\kappa)+\lambda\kappa\mathfrak{B}(L,r,\tilde{r})\,\right)\|r-\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}

\displaystyle+|t-s|\bigg(\|\tilde{r}\|_{L}+\lambda\kappa^{2}e^{L\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}}\bigg)\|w(r)-w(\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{[s,t]}}.

So that defining

L_{1}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\bigg(\|\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\lambda\kappa^{2}\exp\left(L\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\right)\bigg)}

and rearranging the proceeding inequality for any
L\in(0,L_{1}]
and
0\leq s<t\leq L
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|w(r)-w(\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{[s,t]}}\leq

\displaystyle\,2|w_{s}(r)-w_{s}(\tilde{r})|

\displaystyle+2|t-s|\bigg((1+L)e^{\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}}\mathfrak{A}(w_{0},\eta,\kappa)+\lambda\kappa\mathfrak{B}(L,r,\tilde{r})\,\bigg)\|r-\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}.

In particular, taking
s=0
and
t=L_{1}
,

\displaystyle\|w(r)-w(\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{[0,L_{1}]}}\leq

\displaystyle\,2|w_{0}-\tilde{w}_{0}|

\displaystyle+2L_{1}\bigg((1+L)e^{\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}}\mathfrak{A}(w_{0},\eta,\kappa)+\lambda\kappa\mathfrak{B}(L,r,\tilde{r})\,\bigg)\|r-\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}.

and furthermore, since
|w_{L_{1}}(r)-w_{L_{1}}(\tilde{r})|\leq\|w(r)-w(\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{[0,L_{1}]}}
,

\displaystyle|w_{L_{1}}(r)-w_{L_{1}}(\tilde{r})|\leq

\displaystyle\,2|w_{0}-\tilde{w}_{0}|

\displaystyle+2L\bigg((1+L)e^{\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}}\mathfrak{A}(w_{0},\eta,\kappa)+\lambda\kappa\mathfrak{B}(L,r,\tilde{r})\,\bigg)\|r-\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}.

So that iterating the bound, we find that for any
L>0
,

\displaystyle\|w(r)-w(\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\leq

\displaystyle\,2^{N_{L}}|w_{0}-\tilde{w}_{0}|

\displaystyle+2^{N_{L}}L_{1}\bigg((1+L)e^{\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}}\mathfrak{A}(w_{0},\eta,\kappa)+\lambda\kappa\mathfrak{B}(L,r,\tilde{r})\,\bigg)\|r-\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}},

where

N_{L}\coloneqq\left\lceil 2L\bigg(\|\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\lambda\kappa^{2}\exp\left(L\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\right)\bigg)\right\rceil

is the number of intervals of length
L_{1}
required to cover
[0,L]
. This concludes the proof of (
4.6
).

To see that (
4.7
) also holds, we apply (
3.18
) and (
3.19
) along with the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|c(w(r),r)-c(w(\tilde{r}),\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\leq\,

\displaystyle\|c(w(r),r)-c(w(r),\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\|c(w(r),\tilde{r})-c(w(\tilde{r}),\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\lambda\kappa e^{L(\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\|\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}})}\|r-\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\lambda\kappa^{2}e^{L\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}}\|w(r)-w(\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\lambda\kappa e^{L(\|r\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\|\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}})}\left(\|r-\tilde{r}\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}+\kappa\|w(r)-w(\tilde{r})\|_{\mathscr{L}^{\infty}_{L}}\right).

Applying (
4.6
) and consolidating terms completes the proof of (
4.7
).
∎

As in Section
3.6
, the stability result of Lemma
4.4
allows us to obtain existence and uniqueness of a market clearing interest rate in the life-cycle model. However, since our main focus is general equilibria in the overlapping generations model we do not give a detailed proof.

Corollary 4.5

(General Equilibria in the life-cycle Model)
.

Let
L>0
,
K>0
,
\eta\in\mathscr{L}^{0}(\Omega;C_{L})
be a given income process and suppose there exists a pair
(w,c)
which solve (
3.24
) on
[0,L]
such that
\mathbb{E}[w_{0}]=K\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Then, for a bequest motive
\lambda>0
and/or parameter
\kappa>0
sufficiently small, depending on
L
and
\eta
, there exists a unique market clearing interest rate
\bar{r}\in C([0,L];\mathbb{R})
for the life-cycle equations
3.24
.

Sketch of Proof.

The result follows from Banach’s fixed point theorem applied to the map

r\mapsto\frac{1}{K}\left(\mathbb{E}[c(r)]-\mathbb{E}[\eta]\right).

Recall that this expression comes from choosing a constant capital allocation
K_{t}=K
in Proposition
4.2
. Using the stability estimate (
4.7
), we can choose
\lambda
and/or
\kappa
sufficiently small to obtain a contraction.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

In our analysis we have assumed that the capital supply is constant, i.e.
K_{t}=K\in\mathbb{R}
for all
t\in[0,L]
. This assumption simplifies the fixed point formulation for the equilibrium interest rate, making it easier to convey the techniques and result. Nevertheless, the approach can be straightforwardly generalized to the case of a time-varying capital by introducing some more notational complexity, provided that
K_{t}\neq 0
for all
t
.

5.
Overlapping generations

So far we have focused on the life-cycle of a heterogeneous population of individuals all
born
at time
0
and
ceasing economic activity
at time
L>0
. In this section we extend this model to an economy of overlapping generations. At each moment in time, a new generation with infinitely many individuals are born, with the same
lifespan

L>0
as the previous generation. A flow of probability distributions will describe the age distribution in the total population, which we refer to as a
flow of demographic measures
.

The wealth, consumption and income at time
t\in\mathbb{R}
of an individual born at date
b\in\mathbb{R}
under interest rate path
r
and income modelled as an Itô process can be written, for
b\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in[b,b+R]
,

(5.1)

\displaystyle w^{b}_{t}(r)

\displaystyle=w^{b}_{b}+\int_{b}^{t}r_{u}w^{b}_{u}(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u+\int_{b}^{t}\eta^{b}_{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u-\int_{b}^{t}c_{u}^{b}(w^{b}(r),r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u,

(5.2)

\displaystyle c_{t}^{b}(w^{b},r)

\displaystyle=(u_{1}^{\prime})^{-1}\left(\lambda\exp\left(\int_{t}^{L+b}(r_{u}-\delta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\right)\mathbb{E}\left[u^{\prime}_{2}\left(w_{L+b}^{b}\right)|\mathcal{F}_{t}^{b}\right]\right),

Letting

Hence, we can delineate the following simple cases:

(1)

Globally Stationary Populations
are described by the condition that the chance of being born at any time prior to the current time is constant; i.e. a population is globally stationary if

\mathbb{P}(Y_{t}\in[t-a,t])=\mathbb{P}(Y_{s}\in[s-a,s])\quad\text{for all}\,t,\,s\in\mathbb{R}\,\,\text{and}\,\,a\in[0,L].

This can be reformulated as a condition on the age of a random member of the population,

\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in[0,a])=\mathbb{P}(X_{s}\in[0,a])\quad\text{for all}\,\,t,\,s\in\mathbb{R}\,\,\text{and}\,\,a\in[0,L].

These conditions can be written at the level of a
flow of regular demographic measures
by requiring

(5.9)

n(t,b)=n(s,b-(t-s))\quad\quad\text{for all}\,\,t,\,s\in\mathbb{R}\,\,\&\,\,b\in[t-L,t].

So that in particular

n(t,b)=n(0,b-t)\quad\text{for all}\,\,t\in\mathbb{R}\,\,\&\,\,b\in[t-L,t].

(2)

Locally Stationary Populations
are described by the condition that being born today or at any infinitesimally prior time are equal; i.e. we say that a population is locally stationary at time
t\in\mathbb{R}
if

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a}\mathbb{P}(Y_{t}\in[t-a,t])=0.

(3)

Locally Increasing Populations
are described by the condition that being born today is more likely than any, infinitesimally close previous time; i.e. we say that a population is increasing at time
t\in\mathbb{R}
if

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a}\mathbb{P}(Y_{t}\in[t-a,t])>0.

(4)

Locally Decreasing Populations
are described by the condition that being born today is less likely that at any infinitesimal previous time; i.e. we say that a population is locally decreasing at
t\in\mathbb{R}
if

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}a}\mathbb{P}(Y_{t}\in[t-a,t])<0.

(5.12)

\displaystyle N^{L}_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{t-L}^{t}\eta^{b}_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}(b).

In this setting, we redefine the market clearing condition in terms of the aggregate expected wealth.

6.
Numerical Experiments

We provide numerical results on the partial equilibrium case in the life cycle model (
3.4
) where we hold the interest rate to be a constant through the life cycle. We train a neural network to approximate the optimal consumption policy given in
(
3.2
). In contrast to solving the HJB equation using PDE solver based on finite-elements method, our approach using deep learning method does not require imposing boundary conditions and is generative by nature which makes it easy to interpolate the consumption policy. It is worth mentioning that our goal is to showcase how we can numerically solve a stochastic control problem using neural networks and we do not focus on finding the best neural network architecture or training procedure that achieve the lowest loss possible. We will leave the more general case of finding equilibrium interest rates and simulating the OLG models using neural networks for future work. Besides providing validation to the theories we have developped in this work, we believe that our approach using neural networks might be of independent interest to researchers working on building economic models using dynamical systems.

We now describe our loss function, training and evaluation of the consumption policy network, following the notation used in Section
3.6
. Our loss function comes from approximating the total utility given in (
3.2
) by choosing a number of time steps so that the integral inside the expecation can be approximated by a finite sum. More precisely, we take the discretised stochastic income with geometric Brownian motion in the following recursive form:

\eta_{t+1}=\eta_{t}+\mu\eta_{t}\Delta t+\sigma\eta_{t}z,\ \ z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(\Delta t)^{2}),

where
\Delta t:=L/M
is the length of the time step given the life span
T
and the number of time steps
M
.

We define the utility function
u
using the CRRA function:

u(x,\gamma)=\begin{cases}\frac{x^{1-\gamma_{1}}}{1-\gamma_{1}},&\text{if }\gamma_{1}\neq 1,\,x>0\\
\log(x),&\text{if }\gamma_{1}=1,\,x>0,\\
\end{cases}

and the terminal utility:

e^{-\rho L}\lambda u(w_{L},\gamma_{2}).

To ensure numerical stability when we differentiate
u
when
x
is small, we replace
x
by a preset threshold which is a small positive number when
x
is smaller than that threshold. We also add a quadratic penalty for terminal utility when terminal wealth
w_{L}
is negative. This helps the training to stay away from solutions that give rise to negative terminal wealth.

We define our consumption network
\alpha
to take three inputs: time point
t
, wealth level
w_{t}
and income level
\eta_{t}
. We compute the wealth at time
t
recursively by the following expression using our consumption policy network
\alpha
:

(6.1)

w_{t}=w_{t-1}+rw_{t-1}\Delta t+\eta_{t}\Delta t-\alpha(t-1,w_{t-1},\eta_{t-1})\Delta t.

The loss function is defined as an approximation to the expectation of the accumulated utilities over the income process:

(6.2)

J(\alpha):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N}J^{(i)}(\alpha),

where

J^{(i)}(\alpha):=-\sum_{t=0}^{L}e^{-\rho t}u(\alpha(t,w^{(i)}_{t},\eta_{t}^{(i)}),\gamma_{1})\Delta t+e^{-\rho L}\lambda u(w^{(i)}_{L},\gamma_{2})

that captures the utility based on income process for agent
i
. In the implementation, we simulate different income processes by varying the random seed for sampling
z
from Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,(\Delta t)^{2})
for each agent
i,\,1\leq i\leq N
. We allow the initial income
y^{(i)}_{0}
to be sampled from either a uniform or a log-normal distribution, and the initial wealth
w^{(i)}_{0}
from a uniform or a Pareto distribution.

We adopt a simplified ResNet architecture
[
He et al., 2016
]
for our neural network which consists only of convolutional layers and shortcut connections with one projection layer at the top and a projection head at the bottom. The number of residual blocks is set to be a model parameter that can be chosen according to the problem. We have chosen the ResNet architecture due to its simpleness and its capability to mitigate gradient vanishing during training. To ensure that the output of the consumption network is non-negative, we choose the Softplus activation function in the final layer. In doing so, we avoid having to impose a hard constraint in the optimisation.

We find it helpful for convergence of training to pretrain the consumption network for a certain of epochs to minimise the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the consumption trajectory of the stochastic model and that given by the deterministic closed-form solution. We choose the AdamW optimiser
[
Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019
]
and apply learning rate decay. We train the consumption policy network on
4000
agents and then apply the trained policy to datasets obtained from sampling initial income and wealth from different distributions. We set the pretraining epochs to
400
and the main training epochs to
500
. The learning rate decay step is taken to be
150
with a decay factor of
0.6
. We set the number of residual blocks in our neural network to
2
and the number of hidden units in each fully connected layer to
25
. We apply gradient clipping with norm
1.0
.

Appendix A
Explicit solutions to linear BSDEs

We recall here a result providing an explicit solution to certain linear BSDEs. It is this representation that plays a central role in the derivation of the optimal consumption profile.
The following proposition is a special case formulation of
[
Pham, 2009
, Prop. 6.2.1]
.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\{\beta_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
be a Brownian motion supported on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P})
, suppose
a
is bounded and progressively measurable processes valued in
\mathbb{R}
, and let
\xi\in\mathscr{L}^{2}_{\Omega}
. Then the unique solution
(y,z)
to the linear BSDE

-\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y_{t}=a_{t}y_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{t}+z_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{t},\quad y_{T}=\xi,

is given by

y_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\Gamma_{T}\Gamma_{t}^{-1}\xi|\mathcal{F}_{t}],

where
\Gamma:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
solves the (random) ODE

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{t}=\Gamma_{t}a_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,\quad\Gamma_{0}=1.

As illustrated in the next section, the so called stochastic maximum principle guarantees that the solution to the HJB equation is tightly connected to the the solution of certain BSDEs. Thus, when the Hamiltonian in the HJB equation is linear (to be specified later) it turns out that the optimal control can be explicitly found through the solution to the above BSDE.
