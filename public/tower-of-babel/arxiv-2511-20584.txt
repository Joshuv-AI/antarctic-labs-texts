A Tale of Two Geometries: Adaptive Optimizers and Non-Euclidean Descent
Shuo Xie, Tianhao Wang, Beining Wu, Zhiyuan Li

Abstract
Adaptive optimizers can reduce to normalized steepest descent (NSD) when only adapting to the current gradient, suggesting a close connection between the two algorithmic families. A key distinction between their analyses, however, lies in the geometries, e.g., smoothness notions, they rely on. In the convex setting, adaptive optimizers are governed by a stronger adaptive smoothness condition, while NSD relies on the standard notion of smoothness. We extend the theory of adaptive smoothness to the nonconvex setting and show that it precisely characterizes the convergence of adaptive optimizers. Moreover, we establish that adaptive smoothness enables acceleration of adaptive optimizers with Nesterov momentum in the convex setting, a guarantee unattainable under standard smoothness for certain non-Euclidean geometry. We further develop an analogous comparison for stochastic optimization by introducing adaptive gradient variance, which parallels adaptive smoothness and leads to dimension-free convergence guarantees that cannot be achieved under standard gradient variance for certain non-Euclidean geometry.

Introduction

Adaptive optimizers such as Adam have been indispensable for training large-scale machine learning models
(
5
;
12
;
48
;
43
)
.
Their dominance in training efficiency, however, has recently been challenged by the surprising effectiveness of simpler Normalized Steepest Descent (NSD)-type methods such as Muon and Lion
(
20
;
7
;
39
;
32
;
37

.
Behind this competition of two family of optimizers, a broader consensus has begun to emerge: their superior performance is critically related to their ability to exploit non-Euclidean geometry of the loss landscape
(
3
;
44
;
49
;
35
)
.

Recent studies have rigorously characterized how adaptive optimizers exploit non-Euclidean geometry.
For example,
33
and
46
show that AdaGrad and Adam benefit from exploiting the
\ell_{\infty}
-geometry of loss functions, and a one-sided variant of Shampoo has been shown to leverage the geometry induced by the matrix spectral norm
(
47
;
2

.
Notably,
4

For example, without EMA, Adam coincides with NSD under the
\ell_{\infty}
norm, and Shampoo coincides with NSD under the matrix spectral norm, which is proposed to be an independent algorithm Muon.
Yet, beyond these connections, there is no formal result that systematically characterizes the relationship between the two families of algorithms.
This naturally motivates the following question:

Q1.

Do adaptive methods (like Adam, Shampoo) and their corresponding non-Euclidean descent (like Lion, Muon) exploit the non-Euclidean geometry of loss landscape in the same way?

To address this question, we adopt a theoretical perspective and focus on comparing different types of smoothness assumptions that underpin the analysis of these methods.
In fact, even under the same geometry, two distinct notions of smoothness arise.
The first is the standard smoothness under a general norm (cf.
Definition

2.3
), which governs the convergence rate of NSD.
The second is called the
adaptive smoothness
(cf.
Definition

2.4
), introduced by
47
and shown to govern the convergence rate of adaptive optimizers in the convex case.
Indeed, a main contribution of our work is to show that adaptive smoothness also characterizes the convergence rate of adaptive optimizers in the nonconvex setting.
Therefore, while both adaptive optimizers and NSD can exploit non-Euclidean geometry, they rely on fundamentally different smoothness assumptions.

This difference is not merely terminological but quantitative: adaptive smoothness is always no smaller than the standard smoothness under the same geometry.
In other words, from the standpoint of technical conditions, the adaptive smoothness represents a stronger assumption.
This in turn motivates our second question:

Q2.

Does the stronger smoothness assumption in adaptive methods offer any optimization benefits?

We answer this question affirmatively.
In particular, we show that by leveraging Nesterov acceleration, adaptive optimizers can attain an accelerated
O(T^{-2})
rate under adaptive smoothness in the convex setting.
In sharp contrast, it has been shown by
18
that the convergence rate of any optimizer is no better than
\Omega(T^{-1})
under the standard
\ell_{\infty}
smoothness assumption.
This establishes a clear separation: adaptive smoothness enables adaptive optimizers to achieve acceleration under non-Euclidean geometry, while the standard smoothness fails.
Therefore, the stronger adaptive smoothness assumption indeed translates into concrete optimization benefits, showing its difference from the standard smoothness.

In fact this difference has a direct and interesting analogy in terms of the noise assumption in the stochastic setting.
When gradient noise is present, its variability can be measured in two distinct ways:
the standard variance considers gradient variation under a fixed norm, whereas the
adaptive variance
(cf.
Definition

4.1
) measures noise in a more stringent but also more adaptive way that requires uniform control over the geometry prescribed by each preconditioner under consideration.
By construction, adaptive variance is always no smaller than standard variance, directly paralleling the relationship between adaptive and standard smoothness.
Analogous to adaptive smoothness that enables acceleration under a stronger requirement, adaptive variance can likewise yield benefits despite being larger.
We demonstrate this through a careful analysis of NSD under two types of noise assumptions: adaptive variance enables a dimension-free rate, which is not attainable in the worst case under the standard variance condition.

Taken together, our results demonstrate that adaptive smoothness and adaptive variance are different from their standard counterparts as adaptive smoothness enables an acceleration rate and adaptive noise enables a dimension-free rate. These findings reveal an intricate interplay between adaptivity and non-Euclidean geometry, deepening our theoretical understanding of adaptivity in optimization.

2.1
Adam and SignGD can exploit

We start by discussing a specific pair of algorithms, Adam and SignGD, to illustrate the problem of interest.
It is known that SignGD can be viewed as Normalized Steepest Descent (NSD) under the
\ell_{\infty}
norm and its convergence rate for deterministic nonconvex functions admits the following form

46

\displaystyle\min_{t\in[T]}\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\|_{1}\leq O\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_{0}L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty}}(f)}{T}}\bigg)

where

2.3

Note that SignGD can also be viewed as a special case of Adam with
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=0

However, the convergence rate of Adam for general

(

Notably, this diagonal adaptive smoothness is always no smaller than

(
3
)

This suggests that though both SignGD and Adam admit convergence guarantees for the

This distinction motivates the following question:

How does the diagonal adaptive smoothness

emerge as an

To address this question, let us consider the convergence rate of NSD under any norm

4.6
):

\displaystyle\min_{t\in[T]}\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\|_{{\bm{H}},*}=O\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_{0}L_{\|\cdot\|_{\bm{H}}}(f)}{T}}\bigg)

(2)

where

is the dual norm of
\|\cdot\|_{\bm{H}}

Minimizing both sides of (

\displaystyle\inf_{\text{diagonal }{\bm{H}}\succeq 0,\Tr({\bm{H}})\leq 1}\min_{t\in[T]}\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\|_{{\bm{H}},*}=O\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_{0}}{T}\inf_{\text{diagonal }{\bm{H}}\succeq 0,\Tr({\bm{H}})\leq 1}L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{{\bm{H}}}}\!\left(f\right)}\bigg)=O\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_{0}L_{\mathrm{diag}}(f)}{T}}\bigg)

where the equality can be checked by the definition of

Now the right-hand side matches the aforementioned convergence rate of Adam.
The adaptivity of Adam is then demonstrated by its ability to automatically identify and adapt to the best diagonal matrix-induced norm for any given loss function, without the need of knowing
{\bm{H}}

Importantly, we point out that the left-hand side of (

This is because

\displaystyle\sup_{\text{diagonal }{\bm{H}}\succeq 0,\Tr({\bm{H}})\leq 1}\|\cdot\|_{\bm{H}}=\|\cdot\|_{\infty},\qquad\inf_{\text{diagonal }{\bm{H}}\succeq 0,\Tr({\bm{H}})\leq 1}\|\cdot\|_{{\bm{H}},*}=\|\cdot\|_{1}.

Lemma 2.2

Standard and adaptive smoothness.

We proceed to introduce the adaptive smoothness associated with any well-structured preconditioner set

We first review the standard smoothness notion under a general norm

Definition 2.3
.

For a loss function

When

In contrast, the adaptive smoothness associated with

Definition 2.4

(Adaptive Smoothness,

.

The adaptive smoothness of a function

is defined as the smallest smoothness of

\|\cdot\|_{{\bm{H}}}
for

with
\Tr({\bm{H}})\leq 1

\Lambda_{{\mathcal{H}}}\left(f\right):=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{H}}\in\mathcal{H}\\
\Tr({\bm{H}})\leq 1\end{subarray}}L_{\|\cdot\|_{\bm{H}}}(f)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bm{H}}\in\mathcal{{\mathcal{H}}}\\
\forall{\bm{x}},-{\bm{H}}\preceq\nabla^{2}f({\bm{x}})\preceq{\bm{H}}\end{subarray}}\Tr({\bm{H}}).

(5)

In the deterministic convex setting, it has been shown by

is of order
O(\Lambda_{\mathcal{H}}(f)\left\|{\mathcal{X}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}/T)

In

Comparison between two smoothness notions.

For any
{\bm{H}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
with

Technical Contribution: A Novel Matrix Inequality

Previous theoretical results for one-sided Shampoo/ASGO (

;
2
;
27
)

In the nonconvex regime, existing convergence analyses apply essentially when the preconditioner set

In contrast, we provide the first unified convergence analysis that applies to any general well-structured preconditioner sets

A central difficulty in our analysis is the extension from diagonal preconditioners to a general preconditioner set
{\mathcal{H}}

In the diagonal case, the proof basically decomposes to entry-wise analyses, and scalar telescoping readily yields the desired bounds.
However, for general

Our resolution of this challenge yields a key technical contribution, formalized in the following

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\epsilon\geq 0
and
\beta\in(0,1]
.
For any
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
, consider
any sequence of vectors

{\bm{g}}_{0},\ldots,{\bm{g}}_{T-1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Let
{\bm{M}}_{-1}=0
, and recursively define
{\bm{M}}_{t}=\beta{\bm{M}}_{t-1}+{\bm{g}}_{t}{\bm{g}}_{t}^{\top}

Then for any

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|{\bm{V}}_{t}^{-1}{\bm{g}}_{t}\|_{\bm{H}}^{2}\leq\Tr({\bm{H}})\left\|{\bm{S}}_{T}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\quad\text{where }{\bm{S}}_{T}=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\bm{V}}_{t}^{-1}\left({\bm{V}}_{t}^{2}-\beta{\bm{V}}_{t-1}^{2}\right){\bm{V}}_{t}^{-1}.

Moreover, there exists an absolute constant

, independent of

,
\epsilon

and

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{S}}_{T}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq C_{1}\left(1+\log\bigg(1+\frac{d}{\epsilon}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|{\bm{g}}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}+d^{2}(1-\beta)T\bigg)\right)\left(\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}T+\log\|{\bm{V}}_{T-1}^{2}/\epsilon\|_{\mathrm{op}}\right)+C_{2}.

In the special case when
{\mathcal{H}}
is commutative, the above bound can be further improved to

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{S}}_{T}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq(1-\beta)T+\log\|{\bm{V}}_{T-1}^{2}/\epsilon\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

Specializing
{\bm{g}}_{t}
’s to be the gradients in the weighted adaptive algorithm,

provides a general upper bound on the sum of second-order terms.
Meanwhile, it highlights the essential gap between diagonal and general preconditioner sets: noncommutativity introduces an additional
\log d
factor, making the dependence strictly worse than in the diagonal case.
Nevertheless, this is the first bound that applies to arbitrary well-structured preconditioner sets, and it plays a central role in extending convex analyses to the nonconvex setting.

The proof of
Lemma

3.3
can be found in
Appendix

B
.
A key step in the proof is to establish a novel matrix inequality that relates the difference between two positive definite matrices to the difference between their logarithms.
To illustrate this, note that in
Lemma

3.3
, we need to bound the spectral norm of
{\bm{S}}_{T}
, which admits the following form when
\beta=1
:

\displaystyle{\bm{S}}_{T}=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\bm{V}}_{t}^{-1}({\bm{V}}_{t}^{2}-{\bm{V}}_{t-1}^{2}){\bm{V}}_{t}^{-1}.

When
{\bm{V}}_{0},\ldots,{\bm{V}}_{T-1}
commute, it can be shown that
{\bm{V}}_{t}^{-1}({\bm{V}}_{t}^{2}-{\bm{V}}_{t-1}^{2}){\bm{V}}_{t}^{-1}\preceq\log({\bm{V}}_{t}^{2})-\log({\bm{V}}_{t-1}^{2})
, and thus we can apply telescoping to obtain
{\bm{S}}_{T}\preceq\log({\bm{V}}_{T-1}^{2})-\log({\bm{V}}_{0}^{2})
.
However, this argument breaks down when the matrices do not commute.
To address this, we need to pay some extra cost to control the noncommutativity, which is captured by the following matrix inequality.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any positive definite matrices
{\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
such that
{\bm{Y}}\preceq{\bm{X}}
, it holds for any
0\leq c\leq C
that

\displaystyle{\bm{X}}^{-1/2}({\bm{X}}-{\bm{Y}}){\bm{X}}^{-1/2}\preceq\frac{3(\log C-\log c)}{\pi^{2}}(\log{\bm{X}}-\log{\bm{Y}})+\bigg(\frac{12cd}{\pi^{2}\lambda_{\min}({\bm{X}})^{2}}+\frac{12C^{-1}d}{\pi^{2}}\bigg)\Tr({\bm{X}}-{\bm{Y}})\cdot{\bm{I}}_{d}.

Roughly speaking, the second term on the right-hand side represents the curse of noncommutativity.
However, thanks to the logarithmic dependence on
c,C
in the first term, we can choose
c=1/\mathrm{poly}(d)
and
C=\mathrm{poly}(d)
to ensure that
\log C-\log c=O(\log d)
, while keeping the second term small.
This is the root of the
\log d
factor in
Lemma

3.3
for general well-structured preconditioner sets.
We believe the techniques developed here may be of independent interest.

4
Benefit of Adaptive Geometry

We have shown in
Section

3
that the nonconvex convergence rate of adaptive optimizers relies critically on the adaptive smoothness of the loss, and the bound is worse than that of the corresponding NSD.
This naturally raises the concern posed in question
1
: does the stronger assumption of adaptive smoothness lead to stronger results?
In this section, we address this question from two complementary angles:

1.

Under the adaptive smoothness assumption, adaptive optimizers can achieve faster convergence rates on convex functions via Nestrov acceleration.

2.

The distinction between standard smoothness and adaptive smoothness mirrors a parallel separation in the assumptions on gradient noise.

At a high level, these two angles share the same underlying mechanism:
Under non-Euclidean geometry, averaging might not be effective in reducing the norm
.
We illustrate such ineffectiveness in the subsequent sections.

4.1
Adaptive Variance: An Analogue of Adaptive Smoothness

Our main results in this section are concerned about accelerated adaptive algorithms for convex functions and NSD in the nonconvex setting.

4.3.2
Standard gradient variance yields dimension-dependent rate

We first present the upper bound on the convergence rate of NSD under the standard gradient variance assumption.

Theorem 4.8
.

For any
\epsilon\geq 0
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\eta>0
, and

Conclusion

We extend the unified analysis of adaptive optimizers to nonconvex functions, establishing convergence rate depending on the adaptive smoothness. It strengthens the comparison between smoothnesses that adaptive optimizers and NSD use in the convex settings. We further show the benefit of adaptive smoothness by showing the accelerated rate of adaptive optimizers with Nesterov momentum. The benefit of adaptive geometry is also justified by comparing two kinds of noise.

{\bm{A}}
, and thus we first consider
{\bm{A}}={\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}^{\top}
for any unit vector
{\bm{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
We define the following two quantities

\displaystyle f({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle={\bm{v}}^{\top}\partial\log({\bm{X}})[{\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}^{\top}]{\bm{v}}={\bm{v}}^{\top}\int_{0}^{\infty}({\bm{X}}+z{\bm{I}}_{d})^{-1}{\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}^{\top}({\bm{X}}+z{\bm{I}}_{d})^{-1}\mathrm{d}z{\bm{v}},

(14)

\displaystyle g({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle={\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{-1/2}{\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{-1/2}{\bm{v}}.

(15)

Now suppose that the SVD of
{\bm{X}}
is
{\bm{X}}={\bm{U}}{\rm diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}){\bm{U}}^{\top}
where
{\bm{U}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
is an orthogonal matrix.
Then
{\bm{X}}^{-1/2}={\bm{U}}{\rm diag}(\lambda_{1}^{-1/2},\ldots,\lambda_{d}^{-1/2}){\bm{U}}^{\top}
and
({\bm{X}}+z{\bm{I}})^{-1}={\bm{U}}{\rm diag}(1/(\lambda_{1}+z),\ldots,1/(\lambda_{d}+z)){\bm{U}}^{\top}
.
Writing
\tilde{\bm{u}}={\bm{U}}{\bm{u}}
and
\tilde{\bm{v}}={\bm{U}}{\bm{v}}
, then
f({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})
becomes

\displaystyle f({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{\bm{v}}^{\top}{\rm diag}\bigg(\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}+z},\ldots,\frac{1}{\lambda_{d}+z}\bigg)\tilde{\bm{u}}\tilde{\bm{u}}^{\top}{\rm diag}\bigg(\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}+z},\ldots,\frac{1}{\lambda_{d}+z}\bigg)\tilde{\bm{v}}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}},(1/(\lambda_{1}+z),\ldots,1/(\lambda_{d}+z))\rangle^{2}\mathrm{d}z.

Similarly,
g({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})
becomes

\displaystyle g({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\bm{v}}^{\top}{\rm diag}(\lambda_{1}^{-1/2},\ldots,\lambda_{d}^{-1/2})\tilde{\bm{u}}\tilde{\bm{u}}^{\top}{\rm diag}(\lambda_{1}^{-1/2},\ldots,\lambda_{d}^{-1/2})\tilde{\bm{v}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}},(1/\lambda_{1}^{-1/2},\ldots,1/\lambda_{d}^{-1/2})\right\rangle^{2}.

Now applying the fact that
\lambda^{-1/2}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{-1/2}}{\lambda+z}\mathrm{d}z
for any
\lambda>0
, we have

\displaystyle g({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}},(z^{-1/2}/(\lambda_{1}+z),\ldots,z^{-1/2}/(\lambda_{d}+z))\right\rangle\mathrm{d}z\bigg)^{2}.

To further bound
g({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})
, we split the integral into three parts:
[0,c]
,
[c,C]
, and
[C,\infty)
where
c>0
and
C>0
are constants to be determined later.
That is,

\displaystyle g({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{c}\left\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}},(z^{-1/2}/(\lambda_{1}+z),\ldots,z^{-1/2}/(\lambda_{d}+z))\right\rangle\mathrm{d}z\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\bigg(\int_{c}^{C}\left\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}},(z^{-1/2}/(\lambda_{1}+z),\ldots,z^{-1/2}/(\lambda_{d}+z))\right\rangle\mathrm{d}z\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\bigg(\int_{C}^{\infty}\left\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}},(z^{-1/2}/(\lambda_{1}+z),\ldots,z^{-1/2}/(\lambda_{d}+z))\right\rangle\mathrm{d}z\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=:g_{1}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})+g_{2}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})+g_{3}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

where we apply the triangle inequality and denote the three terms on the right-hand side by
g_{1}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}),g_{2}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}),g_{3}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})
respectively.
We control each term separately.

First, for
g_{1}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})
,

\displaystyle g_{1}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{c}\left\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}},(1/(\lambda_{1}+z),\ldots,1/(\lambda_{d}+z))\right\rangle z^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}z\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{c}\|\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}}\|_{2}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{i}+z)^{2}}\bigg)^{1/2}z^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}z\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\|\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}}\|_{2}\sqrt{d}}{\min_{i\in[d]}\lambda_{i}}\int_{0}^{c}z^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}z\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{12\|\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot cd}{\pi^{2}\cdot\min_{i\in[d]}\lambda_{i}^{2}}

where the first inequality follows from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and in the second inequality we apply
\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}+z}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}
as each
\lambda_{i}
is positive.
Next, for the integral over
[c,C]
, we have

\displaystyle g_{2}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\bigg(\int_{c}^{C}\left\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}},(1/(\lambda_{1}+z),\ldots,1/(\lambda_{d}+z))\right\rangle z^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}z\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\bigg(\int_{c}^{C}\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}},(1/(\lambda_{1}+z),\ldots,1/(\lambda_{d}+z))\rangle^{2}\mathrm{d}z\bigg)\bigg(\int_{c}^{C}z^{-1}\mathrm{d}z\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3(\log C-\log c)}{\pi^{2}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}},(1/(\lambda_{1}+z),\ldots,1/(\lambda_{d}+z))\rangle^{2}\mathrm{d}z\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{3(\log C-\log c)}{\pi^{2}}f({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

where the first inequality follows from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and in the second inequality we relax the domain of the integral to
[0,\infty)
, which exactly gives us
f({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})
.
Finally, for
g_{3}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})
,

\displaystyle g_{3}({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\bigg(\int_{C}^{\infty}\left\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}},(1/(\lambda_{1}+z),\ldots,1/(\lambda_{d}+z))\right\rangle z^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}z\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\bigg(\int_{C}^{\infty}\|\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}}\|_{2}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{i}+z)^{2}}\bigg)^{1/2}z^{-1/2}\mathrm{d}z\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\bigg(\int_{C}^{\infty}\|\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}}\|_{2}\sqrt{d}z^{-3/2}\mathrm{d}z\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{12\|\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot C^{-1}d}{\pi^{2}}

where the first inequality follows from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and in the second inequality we apply
\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}+z}\leq\frac{1}{z}
as each
\lambda_{i}
is positive.
Collecting the above bounds, we obtain

\displaystyle g({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3(\log C-\log c)}{\pi^{2}}f({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})+\frac{12\|\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot cd}{\pi^{2}\cdot\min_{i\in[d]}\lambda_{i}^{2}}+\frac{12\|\tilde{\bm{u}}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot C^{-1}d}{\pi^{2}}.

(16)

Now for any general positive semi-definite matrix
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
with eigendecomposition
{\bm{A}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\top}
, we apply the bound (
16
) to each
{\bm{u}}_{i}
in the eigendecomposition and sum over all
i
to get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}g({\bm{u}}_{i},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3(\log C-\log c)}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}f({\bm{u}}_{i},{\bm{v}})+\frac{12cd}{\pi^{2}\cdot\min_{i\in[d]}\lambda_{i}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\|\tilde{\bm{u}}_{i}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{12C^{-1}d}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\|\tilde{\bm{u}}_{i}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}}\|_{2}^{2}.

Note that
\|\tilde{\bm{u}}_{i}\odot\tilde{\bm{v}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\tilde{\bm{u}}\|_{2}^{2}\|\tilde{\bm{v}}\|_{2}^{2}=1
as both
\tilde{\bm{u}}
and
\tilde{\bm{v}}
are unit vectors.
Therefore, we further have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}g({\bm{u}}_{i},{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3(\log C-\log c)}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}f({\bm{u}}_{i},{\bm{v}})+\frac{12cd}{\pi^{2}\cdot\min_{i\in[d]}\lambda_{i}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}+\frac{12C^{-1}d}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{3(\log C-\log c)}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}f({\bm{u}}_{i},{\bm{v}})+\frac{12cd}{\pi^{2}\cdot\min_{i\in[d]}\lambda_{i}^{2}}\Tr({\bm{A}})+\frac{12C^{-1}d}{\pi^{2}}\Tr({\bm{A}}).

Recall the definition of
f({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})
and
g({\bm{u}},{\bm{v}})
in (
14
) and (
15
).
Then we have shown that for any unit vector
{\bm{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{-1/2}{\bm{A}}{\bm{X}}^{-1/2}{\bm{v}}\leq\frac{3(\log C-\log c)}{\pi^{2}}{\bm{v}}^{\top}\partial\log({\bm{X}})[{\bm{A}}]{\bm{v}}+\frac{12cd}{\pi^{2}\cdot\min_{i\in[d]}\lambda_{i}^{2}}\Tr({\bm{A}})+\frac{12C^{-1}d}{\pi^{2}}\Tr({\bm{A}}).

Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle{\bm{X}}^{-1/2}{\bm{A}}{\bm{X}}^{-1/2}\preceq\frac{3(\log C-\log c)}{\pi^{2}}\partial\log({\bm{X}})[{\bm{A}}]+\bigg(\frac{12cd}{\pi^{2}\lambda_{\min}({\bm{X}})^{2}}+\frac{12C^{-1}d}{\pi^{2}}\bigg)\Tr({\bm{A}})\cdot{\bm{I}}_{d}.

∎

By triangle inequality,
\left\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\left\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}+2\left\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{t}-\bar{\bm{g}}_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}
, and thus we only need to bound the distance
\bar{\bm{g}}_{t}-\bar{\bm{g}}_{0}
.
To this end, since
-{\bm{H}}^{*}\preceq\nabla^{2}f({\bm{x}})\preceq{\bm{H}}^{*}
for all
{\bm{x}}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{t}-\bar{\bm{g}}_{0}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{0})\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|{\bm{H}}^{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\left\|{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{x}}_{0}\right\|_{2}\leq\Lambda_{\mathcal{H}}(f)\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\|{\bm{x}}_{s+1}-{\bm{x}}_{s}\|_{2}.

(37)

Moreover, we can control each
\|{\bm{x}}_{s+1}-{\bm{x}}_{s}\|_{2}
as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{x}}_{s+1}-{\bm{x}}_{s}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\eta^{2}\left\|{\bm{V}}_{s}^{-1}{\bm{g}}_{s}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\eta^{2}\Tr({\bm{g}}_{s}^{\top}{\bm{V}}_{s}^{-2}{\bm{g}}_{s})

\displaystyle=\eta^{2}\Tr(P_{\mathcal{H}}({\bm{M}}_{s})^{-2}{\bm{g}}_{s}{\bm{g}}_{s}^{\top})

\displaystyle=\eta^{2}\Tr(P_{\mathcal{H}}({\bm{M}}_{s})^{-2}P_{\mathcal{H}}({\bm{g}}_{s}{\bm{g}}_{s}^{\top})^{2})

where the last equality holds by
Lemma

A.2
.
Then since
{\bm{M}}_{s}\succeq{\bm{g}}_{s}{\bm{g}}_{s}^{\top}
, we have
P_{\mathcal{H}}({\bm{M}}_{s})\succeq P_{\mathcal{H}}({\bm{g}}_{s}{\bm{g}}_{s}^{\top})
by
Lemma

A.3
, and it follows that

\displaystyle\|{\bm{x}}_{s+1}-{\bm{x}}_{s}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\eta^{2}\Tr({\bm{I}}_{d})=d\eta^{2}.

Plugging this back into
Equation

37
, we get
\left\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{t}-\bar{\bm{g}}_{0}\right\|_{2}\leq\eta t\Lambda_{\mathcal{H}}(f)\sqrt{d}
, so
\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+2\eta^{2}t^{2}\Lambda_{\mathcal{H}}(f)^{2}d
.
Now applying this to
Equation

36
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|{\bm{g}}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq T\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(2\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+2\eta^{2}t^{2}\Lambda_{\mathcal{H}}(f)^{2}d)

\displaystyle\leq 2(\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\left\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\Lambda_{{\mathcal{H}}}\left(f\right)^{2}d\eta^{2})T^{3}.

(38)

Finally, we can bound
\|V_{T-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{V}}_{T-1}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}=\left\|{\bm{V}}_{T-1}^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\left\|P_{\mathcal{H}}({\bm{M}}_{T-1})^{2}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\Tr(P_{\mathcal{H}}({\bm{M}}_{T-1})^{2})=\Tr({\bm{M}}_{T-1})

where we apply
Lemma

A.2
in the last equality.
Note that
\Tr({\bm{M}}_{T-1})=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|{\bm{g}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\epsilon d
, so it follows from (
38
) that

\displaystyle\|{\bm{V}}_{T-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\sqrt{2(\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\left\|\bar{\bm{g}}_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\Lambda_{{\mathcal{H}}}\left(f\right)^{2}d\eta^{2})T^{3}+\epsilon d}.

This completes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle\kappa

Theorem

C.8
.

As mentioned in
Section

3
,
Algorithm

6
is equivalent to

7
with
\epsilon/(1-\beta)

.

\displaystyle=\tilde{O}\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}\sqrt{1-\beta}}{\eta}+\bigg(\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{1-\beta}}\Lambda_{{\mathcal{H}}}\left(f\right)+d\left\|\bm{\Sigma}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{1/2}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}T+\log d\bigg)\log d\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}},*}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\eta T}+\frac{L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|}(f)\eta}{2}+\frac{2}{\alpha T}{\mathbb{E}}\left\|{\bm{m}}_{0}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{0})\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}},*}+\frac{2(1-\alpha)\eta}{\alpha}L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|}\!\left(f\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{2}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg\|\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\alpha)^{t-i}\alpha({\bm{g}}_{i}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i}))\bigg\|_{{\mathcal{H}},*}.

(50)

We only need to bound
{\mathbb{E}}\left\|{\bm{m}}_{0}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{0})\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}},*}
and
{\mathbb{E}}\bigg\|\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\alpha)^{t-i}\alpha({\bm{g}}_{i}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i}))\bigg\|_{{\mathcal{H}},*}
. For notational simplicity, we define
\omega_{i}=(1-\alpha)^{t-i}\alpha
and
\bm{\delta}_{i}={\bm{g}}_{i}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i})
.
By
4.3
, we know that
{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{\delta}_{j}|\bm{\delta}_{i}]=0
for
j>1
.
Then we have that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\alpha)^{t-i}\alpha({\bm{g}}_{i}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i}))\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}},*}^{2}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{t}\omega_{i}\bm{\delta}_{i}\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}},*}^{2}={\mathbb{E}}\min_{{\bm{H}}\in{\mathcal{H}},\Tr({\bm{H}})\leq 1}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{t}\omega_{i}\bm{\delta}_{i}\Big){\bm{H}}^{-1}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{t}\omega_{i}\bm{\delta}_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{{\bm{H}}\in{\mathcal{H}},\Tr({\bm{H}})\leq 1}{\mathbb{E}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{t}\omega_{i}\bm{\delta}_{i}\Big){\bm{H}}^{-1}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{t}\omega_{i}\bm{\delta}_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle=\min_{{\bm{H}}\in{\mathcal{H}},\Tr({\bm{H}})\leq 1}{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\omega_{i}^{2}\bm{\delta}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{H}}^{-1}\bm{\delta}_{i}

\displaystyle=\min_{{\bm{H}}\in{\mathcal{H}},\Tr({\bm{H}})\leq 1}{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\omega_{i}^{2}\left\|\bm{\delta}_{i}\right\|_{{\bm{H}}^{-1}}^{2}.

Now by the condition on
\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}
and the definition of the adaptive gradient variance in
Definition

4.1
, we know that
\min_{{\bm{H}}\in{\mathcal{H}},\Tr({\bm{H}})\leq 1}{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\bm{\delta}_{i}\right\|_{{\bm{H}}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{2}
for each
i\in[t]
.
Therefore, we further have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\alpha)^{t-i}\alpha({\bm{g}}_{i}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i}))\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}},*}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\omega_{i}^{2}\leq\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{2}\alpha

(51)

where the second inequality holds by the definition of
\omega_{i}
and the condition that
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Similarly, we also have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\|{\bm{m}}_{0}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{0})\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}},*}^{2}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left\|{\bm{g}}_{0}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{0})\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}},*}^{2}\leq\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{2}.

(52)

Plugging in
Equation

51
and
Equation

52
into
Section

E.1
, we can get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}},*}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\eta T}+\frac{\eta L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}}\!\left(f\right)}{2}+\frac{2\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}}}}{\alpha T}+\frac{2(1-\alpha)\eta}{\alpha}L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}}\!\left(f\right)+2\sqrt{\alpha}\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\eta T}+\frac{2\eta}{\alpha}L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}}\!\left(f\right)+\frac{2\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}}}}{\alpha T}+2\sqrt{\alpha}\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}}}.

(53)

Next, we will choose appropriate hyperparameters
\eta
and
\alpha
to achieve the optimal rate.

For any fixed
\alpha<1
, to balance the first two terms on the right-hand side of (
53
), we choose

\displaystyle\eta=\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_{0}\alpha}{2TL_{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}}\!\left(f\right)}}.

Also, for simplicity, denote
a_{0}=\sqrt{\Delta_{0}L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}}\!\left(f\right)}/\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}}}
.
Then the convergence rate becomes

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}},*}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{2\Delta_{0}L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}}\!\left(f\right)}{\alpha T}}+\frac{2\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}}}}{\alpha T}+2\sqrt{\alpha}\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2}\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}(f)\bigg(\frac{a_{0}}{\sqrt{\alpha T}}+\frac{1}{\alpha T}+\sqrt{\alpha}\bigg).

(54)

We further stratify the values of
\alpha,a_{0}
and
T
to minimize the right-hand side:

•

When
\alpha\leq T^{-2/3}
,
\frac{1}{\alpha T}\geq\sqrt{\alpha}
. We only need to minimize
\frac{1}{\alpha T}+\frac{a_{0}}{\sqrt{\alpha}\sqrt{T}}
, which is achieved at
\alpha=T^{-2/3}
because it is a monotone decreasing function in
\alpha
.
So the convergence rate is always no better than that in the case of
\alpha\geq T^{-2/3}
.

•

When
\alpha\geq T^{-2/3}
,
\frac{1}{\alpha T}\leq\sqrt{\alpha}
. We only need to minimize
\sqrt{\alpha}+\frac{a_{0}}{\sqrt{\alpha T}}
. Then
\frac{a_{0}}{\sqrt{T}}
is the global minimizer but there are constraints
T^{-2/3}\leq\alpha\leq 1
.

–

When
a_{0}\leq T^{-1/6}
, we have that
\frac{a_{0}}{\sqrt{T}}\leq T^{-2/3}
. Then we need to choose
\alpha=T^{-2/3}
and the rate is
\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}}}T^{-1/3}+\sqrt{\Delta_{0}L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}}\!\left(f\right)}T^{-1/6}\leq 2\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}}}T^{-1/3}
.

–

When
a_{0}\geq\sqrt{T}
, we have that
\frac{a_{0}}{\sqrt{T}}\geq 1
. Then we need to choose
\alpha=1
and the rate is
\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}}}+\sqrt{\Delta_{0}L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}}\!\left(f\right)}T^{-1/2}\leq 2\sqrt{\Delta_{0}L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}}\!\left(f\right)}T^{-1/2}
.

–

When
T^{-1/6}\leq a_{0}\leq\sqrt{T}
, we have that
T^{-2/3}\leq\frac{a_{0}}{\sqrt{T}}\leq 1
. We can choose
\alpha=\frac{a_{0}}{\sqrt{T}}
. The rate becomes
(\Delta_{0}L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}}\!\left(f\right))^{1/4}\sqrt{\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}}}}T^{-1/4}
.

This completes the proof.
∎

E.2
Convergence rate under general-norm assumption

Definition E.3

(Distortion of norms)
.

For any target norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|
and a reference norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathsf{ref}}
on a finite-dimensional vector space, the corresponding distortion of
\left\|\cdot\right\|
with respect to
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathsf{ref}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\psi(\left\|\cdot\right\|,\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathsf{ref}})\coloneqq\sup_{{\bm{x}}\neq 0}\frac{\|{\bm{x}}\|}{\|{\bm{x}}\|_{\mathsf{ref}}}\cdot\sup_{{\bm{x}}\neq 0}\frac{\|{\bm{x}}\|_{\mathsf{ref}}}{\|{\bm{x}}\|}.

(55)

Since all norms on the finite-dimensional space are equivalent,
\psi(\left\|\cdot\right\|,\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathsf{ref}})
is always finite.

See
4.8

Proof of
Theorem

4.8
.

Since
{\mathbb{E}}[\|\bm{m}_{0}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{0})\|_{*}]\leq\sigma_{\|\cdot\|_{*}}
, it follows from
Proposition

E.2
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\right\|_{*}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\eta T}+\frac{L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|}(f)\eta}{2}+\frac{2\sigma_{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{*}}}{\alpha T}+\frac{2(1-\alpha)\eta}{\alpha}L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|}\!\left(f\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{2\alpha}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg\|\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\alpha)^{t-i}({\bm{g}}_{i}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i}))\bigg\|_{*}.

(56)

There are two ways to control the last terms, and we discuss them separately below.

For the first approach, we directly apply triangle inequality to get

\displaystyle\alpha\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\bigg\|\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\alpha)^{t-i}({\bm{g}}_{i}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i}))\bigg\|_{*}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\alpha(1-\alpha)^{t-i}\left\|{\bm{g}}_{i}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i})\right\|_{*}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}\alpha(1-\alpha)^{t-i}\big[{\mathbb{E}}\left\|{\bm{g}}_{i}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i})\right\|_{*}^{2}\big]^{1/2}\leq\sigma_{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{*}}.

(57)

For the second approach, we first convert
\|\cdot\|_{*}
to
\|\cdot\|_{2}
and get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\bigg\|\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\alpha)^{t-i}({\bm{g}}_{i}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i}))\bigg\|_{*}^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sup_{\bm{x}}\frac{\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{*}}{\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}}\right)^{2}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\bigg\|\sum_{i=1}^{t}\alpha(1-\alpha)^{t-i}({\bm{g}}_{i}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i}))\bigg\|_{2}^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\left(\sup_{\bm{x}}\frac{\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{*}}{\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}}\right)^{2}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{t}\alpha^{2}(1-\alpha)^{2(t-i)}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}[\left\|{\bm{g}}_{i}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sup_{\bm{x}}\frac{\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{*}}{\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}}\right)^{2}\left(\sup_{\bm{x}}\frac{\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|{\bm{x}}\right\|_{*}}\right)^{2}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{t}(1-\alpha)^{2i}\alpha^{2}{\mathbb{E}}[\left\|{\bm{g}}_{i}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i})\right\|_{*}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\psi(\left\|\cdot\right\|_{*},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2})^{2}\cdot\sigma_{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{*}}^{2}\cdot\alpha.

Using Jensen’s inequality, this further implies that

\displaystyle\alpha\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\bigg\|\sum_{i=1}^{t}(1-\alpha)^{t-i}({\bm{g}}_{i}-\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{i}))\bigg\|_{*}\leq\alpha^{1/2}\psi(\|\cdot\|_{*},\|\cdot\|_{2})\cdot\sigma_{\|\cdot\|_{*}}.

(58)

Now, by taking the minimum over the two bounds in (
E.2
) and (
58
), we obtain from (
E.2
) that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\right\|_{*}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\eta T}+\frac{L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|}(f)\eta}{2}+\frac{2\sigma_{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{*}}}{\alpha T}+\frac{2(1-\alpha)\eta}{\alpha}L_{\left\|\cdot\right\|}\!\left(f\right)+2\sigma_{\|\cdot\|_{*}}\cdot\min\Big(1,\alpha^{1/2}\psi(\|\cdot\|_{*},\|\cdot\|_{2})\big).

This completes the proof.
∎

E.3
Dimension dependent lower bound for NSD

In this subsection, we construct the example for the lower bound in
Theorem

4.9
.

Lemma E.4
.

Fix any
\epsilon>0
.
For any distinct points
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}
where
T+1\leq\frac{1}{4\epsilon^{2}}
, there exists a function
p:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, such that its derivative
p^{\prime}
is
1
-Lipschitz,
p(x_{0})-\inf_{x}p(x)\leq 1
and
p^{\prime}(x_{t})=-\epsilon
for
t=0,1,\dots,T
.

Proof of
Lemma

E.4
.

We use
g(x)
to denote
p^{\prime}(x)
in the proof.
For any set of points
\{x_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
, we will construct
g(x)
such that
g(x)=-\epsilon
for
x<x_{0}
and
g(x+\frac{1}{\epsilon})=g(x)
for
x\geq x_{0}
.
We first explain what it requires to construct this function
g
:
For such a
g
, because of the periodicity, we can always find
x_{0}=y_{0}\leq y_{1}\leq\cdots\leq y_{T^{\prime}}<x_{0}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}
for
T^{\prime}\leq T
so that for any
x_{t}\geq x_{0}
, there is a
y_{s}
such that there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
x_{t}=y_{s}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\cdot n
and therefore
g(x_{t})=g(y_{s})
.
For any
x_{t}<x_{0}
,
g(x_{t})=-\epsilon
by definition.
Also for
x\leq x_{0}
, it holds that
p(x_{0})\leq p(x)
and
g(x)
is
1
-Lipschitz.
Then it suffices to construct
g(x)
in the interval
[x_{0},x_{0}+\frac{1}{\epsilon})
to satisfy the following conditions

•

g(x)
is
1
-Lipschitz in
[x_{0},x_{0}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}]
.

•

For any
x>x_{0}
, it can be written as
x=x_{0}+\delta+n\epsilon^{-1}
for some
\delta\in[0,\epsilon^{-1})
and non-negative integer
n
, and thus
p(x)-p(x_{0})=\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\delta}g(x)\mathrm{d}x+n\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\epsilon^{-1}}g(x)\mathrm{d}x
.
Therefore, to ensure that
p(x_{0})-\inf_{x}p(x)\leq 1
, we require
p(x_{0})-p(x_{0}+\delta)=\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\delta}g(x)\mathrm{d}x\geq-1
for any
0\leq\delta\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}
and
\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}}g(x)\mathrm{d}x\geq 0
.

•

g(y_{s})=-\epsilon
for
s=0,1,\ldots,T^{\prime}
.

We will further define
y_{T^{\prime}+1}=x_{0}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}
in the following construction.

For each
t=0,1,\ldots,T^{\prime}
, we construct
g
on the interval
[y_{t},y_{t+1}]
as follows:

\displaystyle g(x)=\begin{cases}-\epsilon+(x-y_{t}),&x\in[y_{t},(y_{t}+y_{t+1})/2];\\
-\epsilon-(x-y_{t+1}),&x\in((y_{t}+y_{t+1})/2,y_{t+1}].\end{cases}

It is straightforward to see that
g
is
1
-Lipschitz on
[x_{0},x_{0}+\epsilon^{-1}]
and
g(y_{t})=-\epsilon
for
t=0,1,\dots,T^{\prime}+1
.
Also, note that
g(x)\geq-\epsilon
for all
x\in[x_{0},x_{0}+\epsilon^{-1}]
.
Thus, for any
0\leq\delta\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}
, it always holds that
\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\delta}g(x)\mathrm{d}x\geq-\epsilon\delta\geq-1
.
Then it only remains to show
\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}}g(x)\mathrm{d}x\geq 0
.
By direct calculation, we have

\displaystyle\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}}g(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{T^{\prime}}\int_{y_{t}}^{y_{t+1}}g(x)\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{t=0}^{T^{\prime}}\left(-\epsilon(y_{t+1}-y_{t})+\frac{(y_{t+1}-y_{t})^{2}}{4}\right)

\displaystyle=-1+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{t=0}^{T^{\prime}}(y_{t+1}-y_{t})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq-1+\frac{1}{4}\frac{(\sum_{t=0}^{T^{\prime}}(y_{t+1}-y_{t}))^{2}}{T^{\prime}+1}

\displaystyle=-1+\frac{1}{4\epsilon^{2}(T^{\prime}+1)}

where the inequality holds by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
Since
T^{\prime}+1\leq T+1\leq\frac{1}{4\epsilon^{2}}
, it immediately follows that
\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{0}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}}g(x)\mathrm{d}x\geq 0
.
This completes the proof.
∎

f_{t}
as

\displaystyle f_{t}({\bm{x}})=f({\bm{x}})+\langle{\bm{\delta}}_{t},{\bm{x}}\rangle+\frac{\epsilon}{d}\langle\bm{1}_{d},{\bm{x}}\rangle.

(62)

Its gradient is given by

\displaystyle\nabla f_{t}({\bm{x}})=\nabla f({\bm{x}})+{\bm{\delta}}_{t}+\frac{\epsilon}{d}\bm{1}_{d}.

By the previous construction of
{\bm{\delta}}_{t}
, it is clear that
{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla f_{t}({\bm{x}})]=\nabla f({\bm{x}})
, and it follows from (
E.3
) that the variance of
\nabla f_{t}({\bm{x}})
under
\|\cdot\|_{1}
satisfies
{\mathbb{E}}[\left\|\nabla f_{t}({\bm{x}})-\nabla f({\bm{x}})\right\|_{1}^{2}]={\mathbb{E}}[\left\|{\bm{\delta}}_{t}-{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{\delta}}_{t}]\right\|_{1}^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
.
Therefore, the stochastic loss functions
f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{T-1}
satisfy the condition
(b)
.
Next, we proceed to establish the lower bound for
{\mathbb{E}}[\min_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\|_{1}]
, where
{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{T-1}
are obtained by running
Algorithm

3
on the constructed loss functions
f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{T-1}
with
{\bm{x}}_{0}=0
.

Lower bound on gradient norm.

First note that by the construction of the loss functions and the choice
{\bm{x}}_{0}=0
, the coordinate-wise dynamics have the same distribution across coordinates, i.e.,
(x_{0,i},x_{1,i},\ldots,x_{T-1,i})
has the same distribution as
(x_{0,j},x_{1,j},\ldots,x_{T-1,j})
for all
i,j\in[d]
.
Utilizing this observation, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\min_{t\in[T]}\left\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\right\|_{1}\Big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\min_{t\in[T]}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}|p^{\prime}(x_{t,i})|\bigg]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\min_{t\in[T]}|p^{\prime}(x_{t,i})|\Big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Big[\min_{t\in[T]}|p^{\prime}(x_{t,1})|\Big].

(63)

So it suffices to focus on the dynamics in the first coordinate.

By the construction of the function
p
in the previous step, we have
p^{\prime}(0)=-\epsilon
.
Therefore, when
x_{t,1}=0
, we have
[\nabla f_{t}({\bm{x}})]_{1}=p^{\prime}(x_{1})/d+\delta_{t,1}+\epsilon/d=\delta_{t,1}
.
Consequently, since
x_{0,1}=0
, the first coordinate of
{\bm{x}}_{t}
will remain to be zero until at some step
\delta_{t,1}\neq 0
, in which case we must have
\delta_{t,1}=-C
.
This implies that

\displaystyle\min\{t\in[T]:x_{t,1}\neq 0\}-1=\min\bigg\{t\in[T]:[\nabla f_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})]_{1}=-C\bigg\}=:\tau,

and this stopping time
\tau
follows a geometric distribution with parameter
\theta=\frac{5\epsilon^{2}}{d\sigma^{2}}
again by the distribution of
\bm{\delta}_{t}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\bigg[\tau\geq\frac{1}{\theta}\bigg]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\bigg[\tau\geq\lceil\frac{1}{\theta}\rceil\bigg]=(1-\theta)^{\lceil\frac{1}{\theta}\rceil}

\displaystyle\geq\exp\bigg(-\theta\lceil\frac{1}{\theta}\rceil\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq e^{-1-\theta}\geq e^{-2}.

Now conditioned on the event that
\tau\geq\frac{1}{\theta}
, we can show that

\displaystyle\tau+N\geq\frac{1}{\theta}+\frac{1}{4\epsilon^{2}}\geq 2\sqrt{\frac{1}{\theta}\cdot\frac{1}{4\epsilon^{2}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\theta}\epsilon}=\frac{\sqrt{d}\sigma}{\sqrt{5}\epsilon^{2}},

which is no smaller than
T
by the choice of
\epsilon
in
Equation

59
. Therefore, we have
x_{t,1}=0
for all
t=0,1,\ldots,\tau
and there are at most
T-\tau
distinct points among
x_{0,1},x_{1,1},\ldots,x_{T,1}
.
Then since
|x_{t+1}-x_{t}|
is either 0 or
\eta
by the update rule of
Algorithm

3
with
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, we see that
x_{0,1},x_{1,1},\ldots,x_{T,1}\in\{0,\eta,2\eta,\ldots,(N-1)\eta\}
on the event
\tau\geq\frac{1}{\theta}
, in which case we have
p^{\prime}(x_{t,1})=-\epsilon
for all
t=0,1,\ldots,T
by our construction of
p
.
Leveraging this, it follows from
Equation

63
that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\min_{t\in[T]}\left\|\nabla f({\bm{x}}_{t})\right\|_{1}\Big]\geq{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\min_{t\in[T]}|p^{\prime}(x_{t,1})|\Big]

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}\Big[\tau\geq\frac{1}{\theta}\Big]\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\min_{t\in[T]}|p^{\prime}(x_{t,1})|\,\Big|\,\tau\geq\frac{1}{\theta}\Big]

\displaystyle\geq e^{-2}\epsilon

This gives the desired lower bound and completes the proof.
∎
