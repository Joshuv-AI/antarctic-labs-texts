Hands-on summer research programs at Indiana university Simon comprehensive cancer center
Authors: Elizabeth S Yeh, Shannon M Hawkins, Jessica Byram, Daenique H A Jengelley, Gayle A Buck, J Adam Scribner, Harikrishna Nakshatri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer treatment has improved dramatically over the last thirty years, with mortality rates dropping by 34% from 1991 to 2022. Continued innovations in cancer treatment require an energized workforce. To support workforce development in cancer research, the Indiana University Simon Comprehensive Cancer Center initiated its first summer research program in 2002. Over the first 22 years of the program, more than 300 participants participated, averaging 14 per summer. The intent of our program participants is to continue careers in health-related fields, thus contributing positively to the program goals. This small, yet sustained program, initially funded by the National Cancer Institute (NCI) Continuing Umbrella of Research Experiences (CURE) training program, transformed into three nationally-funded programs: the NCI R25-funded Educational Pathways in Cancer Research (EPCR) and Summer Program for Academic Research in Cancer (SPARC) programs and an American Cancer Society (ACS) sponsored Center for Innovation in Cancer Research Training program. The programs aim to increase the number of biomedical science researchers with an emphasis on cancer research, with each program targeting subtly different learners. Here, we report the outcomes of the previous summer interns, the organizational structure of the new programs, and the early data on the latest expansion of the programs. While early programs focused on high school and early undergraduate students, these new programs add innovative opportunities to middle school students, high school science teachers, and medical students. These programs offer innovative educational opportunities for young learners to build skills and gain experience, offering interns the potential to excel, grow, and develop into impactful members of the biomedical workforce.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44217-025-00982-w.

Introduction

Cancer is a disease of aging, and with increasing life expectancy, the burden of cancer will likely increase, with two out of five Americans affected by 2050 [1]. Unfortunately, the number of cancer care providers and researchers is not adequate [2, 3]. The National Institutes of Health (NIH), American Association of Cancer Research (AACR), American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), American Cancer Society (ACS), and the National Cancer Institute (NCI), have recently articulated several workforce challenges that have to be overcome to improve the nation’s health including reducing the burden of cancer [4]. These challenges can be overcome by engaging students in cancer research at an earlier age. Literature suggests that providing research opportunities in middle or high school can help foster future interest in biomedical careers. For example, summer internships and subsequent long-distance mentoring programs for middle and high school students increase interest in health careers [5]. These programs are reported to improve students’ ability to navigate disciplinary skills, research design, information or data analysis skills, information literacy, self-confidence, communication, and professional advancement [6–8]. Furthermore, skills gained in taking responsibility for student-led projects had an effect on downstream academic and career success even after accounting for parental income, intelligence quotient (IQ), and other factors that influence achievement [9–11]. In addition, it is crucial to encourage middle-level and high school teachers to participate in research programs that incorporate hands-on laboratory research and professional development, which can also significantly improve their students’ achievement in science [12]. Thus, efforts that engage students and their teachers in biomedical research may help in supporting future workforce development. Given the evidence that there are durable effects in the frequency of research jobs held and a higher levels of productivity from providing early exposure to research to trainees [13], it is expected that programs engaging individuals in cancer research at early educational stages would have positive effects on empowering future generations to participate in medical and research careers that impact global change.

To address these needs, NCI implemented the Cancer Education Grants Program under the R25 mechanism. The goal of these programs is to support cancer education by reaching and engaging individuals in cancer training experiences at early stages to enhance and nurture the next generation of the cancer workforce. These programs aim not only to support participants in future professional roles in the cancer field but also to support the current faculty who mentor and educate trainees [14]. The success of these programs requires that participating cancer centers develop and implement programs that support trainee career development and adopt data-driven approaches, using rigorous quantitative and qualitative assessments of training outcomes [15]. These steps are necessary for the continuous refinement of the programs and to provide accurate data that demonstrate return on investment.

The Indiana University Simon Comprehensive Cancer Center (IUSCCC) is the only NCI-designated cancer center in the state of Indiana, and Indiana University School of Medicine (IUSM) is the largest public medical school in the country enrolling ~ 360 medical students per year. IUSM and IUSCCC are poised to make significant contributions to developing the cancer-related scientific workforce. Indiana students face significant education challenges. Only three out of 10 Indiana students who enroll in a four-year college graduate on time, and only half finish within six years [16]. This contrasts with a national average of 62.4% graduating within six years [17]. While the reasons for this graduation gap in Indiana are multifaceted, improving educational opportunities at the earliest possible time and aiding retention by maintaining personal ties with student researchers would at least partially help to mitigate these problems. The goals of IUSCCC are to help Indiana overcome these educational challenges and to encourage future careers in cancer-related fields. Through the summer research programs, including two NCI-sponsored R25 programs Educational Pathways in Cancer Research (EPCR) and Summer Program for Academic Research in Cancer (SPARC), and an ACS-sponsored summer research program called the Center for Innovation in Cancer Research Training (CICRT), the goal is to maintain a significant footprint in cancer education by training individuals at an early stage and to increase the development of the future workforce in cancer-related careers through continued interactions.

The IUSCCC Summer Research Programs (SRPs) are unique because they engage trainees at an earlier stage of the educational spectrum, including middle-level and high school students through engagement of middle school and high school teachers in curriculum development. IUSCCC SRPs also reach undergraduate and first-year medical students. Through community engagement efforts, the cancer center’s education programs aimed to further enhance SRP’s impact by facilitating effective and far-reaching recruitment in local and rural communities. Furthermore, to provide accurate and objective assessments, we collaborated with education experts to objectively evaluate our SRP activities. The model system we developed here may be applicable to states with fewer medical schools and limited opportunities for students to learn about biomedical and cancer research.

Methods

Historical program description

The IUSCCC received a NCI Continuing Umbrella of Research Experiences (CURE) training program grant as part of its cancer center support grant starting in 2002 and ending with the program’s sunset in 2019. This program was named “Summer Research Program” (SRP). Alongside SRP in 2013, a feeder program called “Future Scientist Program” (FSP) was started. The recruitment process, participant information, and programming have been described [18]. Briefly, for SRP, the target population was late high school and early undergraduate students. FSP recruited only high school juniors from Marion County public high schools in Indiana. The program did not have a housing option. Therefore, all participants were from a local high school or college students who were home for the summer. SRP and FSP offered hands-on, mentored cancer research within the IUSCCC, as well as didactic professional development courses, for six to eight weeks during the summer. Starting with the 2010 cohort, program alumni receive an annual post-program survey to query academic and professional data.

Contemporary programs description

At the IUSCCC, the NCI CURE training program or SRP transformed into three nationally-funded programs: the NCI R25-funded EPCR and SPARC programs and an ACS sponsored Center for Innovation in Cancer Research Training program. FSP remains in place through philanthropic support, alongside a pilot outreach program collaborating with Jackson State University. The programs all aim to increase the number of biomedical science researchers with an emphasis on cancer research. However, each summer program targets different and sometimes overlapping learners (Table 1). Since the summer programs share overlapping time line, their recruitment, programming, and evaluation processes utilize similar methods. The non-summer portions of EPCR are described below.

Summer recruitment and trainee selection

There are four groups of learners recruited into the summer research programs: high school students, undergraduate students, medical students, and high school science teachers. Middle school teachers attend cancer-related workshops. Each learner group has a unique online application. Undergraduates use a common application portal for all programs. High school students and teachers have different personal statement prompts. Medical students from Indiana University or other medical or osteopathic schools apply through the Indiana University Medical Student Program for Research and Scholarship (IMPRS) online application portal. All learners provide a transcript, answer prompts about why they want to do cancer research and provide a letter of recommendation (except teachers).

Each learner group has unique recruitment efforts. FSP is advertised on Exploradoor, an Indiana nonprofit corporation that offers an online service connecting teens to summer paid internships. Long-standing relationships with science teachers at Marion County public high schools contribute to high FSP application rates from this high school student population. Outreach programs have included visits to local high schools, contacting school superintendents, and advertising FSP at the annual Hoosier Association of Science Teachers meeting. Career events aimed at high school and undergraduate students include advertising at the Indiana State Fair, attending local university science events, visiting universities, and participating in national scientific conferences. Opportunities for medical students are advertised through the American Association of Medical Colleges (AAMC) and the Student National Medical Association (SNMA) websites.

Applications undergo initial screening to determine qualification for the programs. Each application, blinded to demographic information, is holistically reviewed by three program members. These recommendations are based on academic performance, a reference letter, a personal statement, demonstrated interest in cancer research, an area of research interest that aligns with the available mentor pool, and career plans. Through further review by the entire program staff, applications are ranked, and selected applicants are notified. The number of contemporary program applications has exceeded 400.

Summer mentor recruitment and mentor matching

The IUSCCC Summer Research Programs typically need 70 + mentors, which is one of the most challenging tasks of the program. Because of a long-standing priority of mentor satisfaction, the IUSCCC is fortunate to have a core list of faculty members who have agreed to mentor students through the summer programs. Additionally, IUSCCC program leaders set an example by taking summer interns, highlighting faculty who take students, and enthusiastically recruiting new mentors. The program encourages near-peer mentoring, supporting mentors who will take two or three students. Because some mentors cannot provide appropriate support for children (summer interns under age 18 are considered children), mentors are allowed to request their learner level. Based on the topic of interest expressed by the applicant, every effort is made to match an applicant with an appropriate mentor. All faculty members have access to and are encouraged to participate in school sponsored mentor training programs.

Summer research internships for high school, undergraduate, and medical students

The IUSCCC summer program comprises two main components: hands-on cancer research and a didactic curriculum. The immediate goals for the summer are to engage in hands-on lab activities and produce a deliverable (i.e., present a poster). Summer interns perform hands-on cancer research under the guidance of a mentor. Additionally, most interns are directly supervised by an in-lab mentor, such as a graduate student, postdoctoral fellow, or lab manager. These in-lab relationships enable near-peer mentoring, support for the intern (i.e., real-time experimental troubleshooting), and an opportunity for in-lab mentors to gain mentoring experience. Interns attend lab meetings, journal clubs, special seminars, and perform literature searches. The interns are taught how to do lab-specific techniques for experiments, collect and analyze the data, write an abstract, and present the results and conclusions in a poster symposium at the end of the summer.

Orientation is an integral part of the summer curriculum as it sets expectations for lab safety, professionalism, and program requirements. During orientation, interns undergo training in biological safety, bloodborne pathogens, and NIH guidelines for DNA recombination. Additionally, expectations on timeliness, professional attire, and lab engagement and etiquette are discussed. The weekly didactic curriculum offers professional development, training in the responsible conduct of research, guidance on searching the research literature, and inspirational and career-focused seminars led by IUSCCC faculty. Additionally, career exploration topics are provided, including a medical simulation event, graduate and medical student networking events, and pathology lab tours. Summer research internships for high school science teachers

The EPCR program is unique in that it offers integrated laboratory research experience and curriculum development opportunities for high school science teachers. After selection, high school science teachers are paired with a research mentor. The teachers undergo a similar orientation and participate in the didactic curriculum in Table 2. Teachers spend four weeks in the lab. The deliverables for the teachers include biology and cancer-related instructional modules that apply knowledge gained through their laboratory experience. These modules will then be used in the classroom and/or disseminated to other classrooms.

Cancer research experience for middle school teachers

A significant and separate aim of EPCR is to prepare middle-level teachers to equip their students with the prerequisite knowledge and dispositions necessary for medical research. Middle-level teachers were recruited from public schools across the state of Indiana. This cohort of teachers participated in a workshop dedicated to project-based activities that teachers can facilitate with their students focused on [1] what is cancer; [2] what are the causes of cancer; [3] what are the impacts of cancer on our communities; and [4] how can advances in cancer research mitigate cancer’s severity and impact? Evaluation methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at Indiana University (IRB protocol #1110007280). Participants provided consent to participate using the evaluation methods and surveys, which were previously published [18]. At the beginning of the summer research experience, interns were invited to complete a pre-survey that collected demographic information and the Research Preparation Scale that evaluated the degree to which interns felt prepared and able to conduct research. Further, using free responses, interns were asked what they hoped to learn from the program and to discuss any concerns about the summer research experience. The free responses were analyzed using the Framework Method of thematic analysis [19]. Interns completed a post-survey at the end of the program that included the same Research Preparation Scale as well as items evaluating the overall research and educational experience and evaluation of the research mentor. Free responses collected data about what interns learned from the research experience, future career goals, and their overall perception of the summer research experience. Finally, interns completed a 27-item quiz at the beginning and end of the program to assess their baseline level of knowledge on oncology topics and to determine whether their knowledge improved at the end of the program.

Surveys were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools. REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) data collection projects rely on a thorough study-specific data dictionary defined in an iterative self-documenting process by all members of the research team. The iterative development and testing process results in a well-planned data collection strategy for individual studies. IU REDCap servers are securely housed in on-site, limited access data centers managed by University Information Technology Services at Indiana University. The web-based interface for IU REDCap encrypts all data transmitted to and from IU REDCap, and IU REDCap data are stored on a private, firewall-protected network. Data are backed up regularly, and those backups are stored at separate data centers in Indianapolis, IN and Bloomington, IN. All users are given individual user ids and passwords and their access is restricted on a role-specific basis. REDCap was developed specifically around HIPAA Privacy and Security guidelines, and is implemented and maintained at Indiana University according to NIST 800 − 53 and Indiana University guidelines. REDCap software is licensed by over 6,500 academic/non-profit consortium partners in more than 150 countries, with millions of users worldwide [20, 21].

Demographic information and responses to the post-survey are reported as descriptive statistics. An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed in R to evaluate evidence of construct validity of the Research Preparation Scale (R Core Team, 2025). A polychoric correlation matrix was produced as the ratings were on an ordinal (Likert) scale and a parallel analysis was performed on the polychoric matrix to choose the factors for the EFA. A Cronbach’s α was performed in SPSS v.29 (IBM Corp, Armonk NY) on the Research Preparation Scale to evaluate internal consistency reliability of the scale. A linear mixed-effects model by REML (restricted maximum likelihood) was performed in R to analyze the effects of time (pre-post responses) and demographics on responses to the research preparation scale. In the model, individuals were the random effect, time, race/ethnicity, and gender were fixed effects, and education-level was a covariate (R Core Team, 2025). A Paired T Test was used to compare interns’ responses on the oncology topics quiz in SPSS v.29 (IBM Corp, Armonk NY). Significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Historical program results

Early outcomes from SRP have been highly positive, as measured by college enrollment, graduation, entry into or completion of doctoral programs, and careers in science-related fields. Data from 2010 to 2015 showed that out of 79 SRP and FSP participants, all completed an undergraduate degree, and nearly 30% (23/79) pursued a doctoral level degree in a scientific or biomedical field of study. In 2015, the program set out to contact post-program alumni annually to provide updated education and career information. Between 2015 and 2024, 224 alumni were identified. Of those alumni who attended during this period and responded to our inquiry, 41 alumni had participated in the program more than once with 2 participating more than twice. In terms of the biomedical workforce, 41 alumni were enrolled in or have completed an advanced degree program (7 PhD, 9 MD, 1 MD/PhD, 20 Masters, 3 DMS, PharmD, or Nursing). Of the 30 alumni who did not pursue an advanced degree program, 19 have a science-related job (i.e., therapy assistant, research technician, engineer, or operations manager); 9 program alumni did not pursue a science-related job, and 2 are unknown. Longer-term outcomes of SRP have shown significant deliverables (i.e., scientific publications). Program alumni have contributed to scholarship by publishing a total of 14 journal articles in PubMed — some as outcomes of summer research projects and others from ongoing academic research [22–35]. These positive historical program results provide a baseline for continued outcomes that contribute to the biomedical workforce in contemporary programs.

A total of 50 interns (72%) completed the pre-survey. In free-text responses, interns discussed what they hoped to learn from the summer research program and how they thought it would influence their career choices. The responses reflect an interest in gaining practical laboratory skills, understanding the research process, and improving scientific communication. Collaboration, mentorship, and career exploration are also important to these students, with a notable focus on cancer research. Students further discussed how the research experience may inform career decisions, particularly in the fields of cancer research and clinical medicine. Program outcomes: summer interns

A total of 56 interns (81%) completed the post-survey. Most students agreed or strongly agreed that the program increased their appreciation for research, confidence in themselves and their research abilities, and was an overall benefit to their professional development (Fig. 1).

Results of post-experience survey of students (n = 56)

The interns overwhelmingly enjoyed the summer research program. They enjoyed being able to do hands-on work in the laboratory, network with other students, mentors, and professionals, learning and applying new skills, and seeing the fruits of their experiments at the end of the program. One student described the most memorable aspects of the Summer Research experience:

I was able to communicate and befriend many amazing, educating, and inspiring people. Along with my mentor, seeing all the different experiences and words of guidance allowed me to best evaluate what I wish to do in the future as a future scientist, and ongoing student.

Despite overall positive perceptions of the program, the students did highlight several barriers to their summer experience and provided recommendations to improve the program. Many discussed time constraints, both in terms of time to complete experiments and collect data, and that there were times a mentor was unavailable due to a busy schedule. Finally, some students, particularly those still in high school, discussed a steep learning curve in the beginning of the program due to their limited background knowledge on the topic.

Some recommendations included giving the students more control over the types of labs they are placed in and adjusting the timing of required program elements to the beginning or end of the day so that they interrupted time in the laboratory less. Finally, students would like to see the science communication lectures tailored to give the students more practical skills for presenting their posters.

Most interns agreed or strongly agreed that their mentors were available to answer questions, provide advice, feedback, and resources to complete their research project (Fig. 2). A total of 44 students (80%) rated their experience with mentoring staff as excellent, followed by 12% rating the experience as good, and only 7% rating it as fair.

Student responses to post-experience evaluation of mentors (n = 56)

Interns further described how mentors supported them in the summer research experience by making themselves available to answer questions, provide feedback, and being overall encouraging, patient, and understanding. Others described how their mentors were able to create a supportive learning environment. One intern stated:

My mentor was there every step of the way, and if I made a mistake or did something wrong it wasn’t the end of the world and I was encouraged to try again. All of the people in my lab were positive and very supportive, they all also encouraged asking questions! I learned a lot in this lab and it was no doubt because of the amazing people in it!

The Research Preparation Scale was included on both the pre- and post-surveys to evaluate interns’ perceptions of their understanding of and ability to conduct research after completing the program (Table 5). The EFA produced a single factor structure with acceptable goodness of fit indices and communalities ranging from 0.306 to 0.645. The linear mixed-effects model found that time had the only significant effect on the model. As such, interns (n = 48 or ~ 70% of the total intern pool in 2024) reported significantly higher scores on all but two items at the end of the summer research experience (i.e., I am prepared to conduct research methods; I am able to plan my schedule to work independently; Fig. 3). Cronbach’s α revealed the scale had excellent reliability (α = 0.91). The full results of the EFA and linear mixed-effects model can be found in Supplement A.

Program outcomes as assessed by mentors

At the end of the program, mentors were also sent a REDCap survey. Ten mentors (22%) submitted evaluations for 14 mentees (range of 1–2 mentees). Although response rate from mentors is low, it mirrors national trend for online surveys of 2–30% [36]. Mentors agreed or strongly agreed their mentees learned research techniques, were respectful, accepted feedback, and were well-prepared for activities (Fig. 4). Only one mentor felt that their intern spent too much time on other program activities, and 77% agreed/strongly agreed that program requirements enhanced the experience. Finally, 79% (n = 11) rated their experience with their mentee as excellent and three rated their experience as good.

Evaluation of Summer Research interns by mentors; results of post-program survey of n = 10 mentors

In free responses, mentors discussed the factors that promote success in mentees and highlighted proactivity and willingness to find information independently, good time management skills, and motivation and curiosity to learn beyond the basics. They described the best aspects of their programs for their mentees as giving them hands-on research experiences and exposure to real-life research, interaction with peers and mentors, and opportunities to explore various career paths. When discussing how the program could be improved, mentors described how the duration of the program was prohibitive and how there was limited time for interaction within groups and with teachers. Suggestions included expanding the program duration by at least one week and reducing the number of deadlines related to abstracts and poster drafts.

Discussion

Students who participate in research opportunities early have the potential for increased interest in biomedical careers in the future [5]. To address the consistent increase in cancer health burden, a robust pipeline of individuals interested in pursuing cancer-related occupations is needed. Implementing programs, such as the IUSCCC SRP is a pro-active approach to the future goal of increasing the workforce that is needed to support overall healthcare needs [4] and prior studies show short-term cancer research educational programs help students to refine and solidify future career plans, often in cancer-related fields [37, 38]. While our current analysis does not address this outcome because we do not have enough data collected to date, the intention is to follow the trajectory of the interns who participated. The IUSCCC has developed a database to track these measures which will be assessed for the out comes in the future. This tracking system has so far revealed 97% graduation rate of program participants.

Overall, based on the assessments from mentors and students who completed the survey, findings indicate that students enjoyed being able to do hands-on work in the laboratory, network with other students, mentors, and professionals. They also enjoyed learning and applying new skills, as well as seeing the fruits of their experiments at the end of the program, which culminates each year in a closing ceremony where interns present their research. Parents and family members were invited to attend the poster session. Importantly, they valued working with the mentors and the program, which created a supportive learning environment.

Free responses indicated that mentors highlighted characteristics of mentees that supported their success in the summer program. These mentee characteristics included proactivity and willingness to find information independently, good time management skills, and motivation and curiosity to learn beyond the basics. Mentors indicated that the best aspects of the program were giving the mentees hands-on research experiences and exposure to real-life research, interaction with peers and mentors, and opportunities to explore various career paths. Overall, feedback from participating mentors and mentees were positive.

Finally, these successful summer research programs have benefited from a cohort of local educators. Their input helped in the smooth implementation and evaluation of the programs. Those cancer centers looking to establish programs should consider involving local high school educators in recruitment, selection, implementation, and advisory boards.

Limitations and future directions

The formal assessment of outcomes prior to 2024 is limited to retrospective surveys, which rely on alumni to respond. While stratifying data across different educational cohorts may provide important subgroup differences, a limitation of the current analysis is the limited number of survey responses. To improve response, future events will invite alumni to network with current summer interns on an annual basis and at scientific meetings. Additionally, alumni may only report positive outcomes. Automatic trainee tracking methods are being developed, incorporating cross-referencing of outcomes with online presence (e.g., PubMed, LinkedIn).

The current program evaluation has limitations to note as well. First, all evaluation instruments, including the Research Preparation Scale and Oncology Topics Quiz, were locally developed for our summer research programs and have not undergone rigorous evaluation for validity evidence. While the current exploratory factor analysis provides some evidence of construct validity, the analysis was likely underpowered and the results should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, the psychometric properties of the Oncology Topics Quiz were not evaluated. As such, a future direction of this work is to provide additional validity evidence for these assessments to ensure their suitability to measure research preparedness and understanding of foundational topics in oncology.

The impact of our programs on middle and high school curriculum is too early to formally assess. However, high school teachers who have participated in SRPs so far have expressed great satisfaction with the program and are actively developing their curriculum. One of these teachers arranged a campus visit for his students, which helped IUSCCC to further engage with the community. The anticipated outcome from the middle-level teachers is that these efforts will result in 15- to 20-hours of medical research curriculum for formal learning environments in middle-level classrooms. Implementation of educational assessment involve mastery of cancer topics by middle level students but also satisfaction of middle-level teachers.

Overall, these programs offer early medical, undergraduate, and high school students the opportunity to perform hands-on mentored research. A major goal is to provide interns with a foundational understanding of cancer biology that has the potential to inform participants toward future approaches to patient care and contribute to this process as they progress in their career journeys. Although the ability to quantitate the long term effects of these programs on workforce innovation has not been reached yet, it is expected that training programs such as the one described in this manuscript will have an effect on global health outcomes by empowering future generations to participate in activities leading to global change. Future assessments of alumni will focus on the impact of cancer biology-specific endeavors on workforce quality.
