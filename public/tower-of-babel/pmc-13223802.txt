Diagnostic utility of tumor volume doubling time in ciliated muconodular papillary tumor/bronchiolar adenoma mimicking invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma: A case report
Authors: Tomonari Oki, Shuhei Iizuka, Yoshiro Otsuki, Toru Nakamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCiliated muconodular papillary tumor (CMPT), recently categorized under bronchiolar adenoma (BA), is a rare benign peripheral lung lesion. Due to its mucin-rich nature and radiological appearance, it frequently mimics invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma (IMA), leading to potential over-treatment. While its indolent nature is known, quantitative data regarding its growth kinetics, specifically tumor volume doubling time (VDT), are lacking. We present a case of CMPT/BA with a 3-year observation period and evaluate its diagnostic utility via VDT calculation. A 72-year-old female with a history of smoking and treated cecal adenocarcinoma presented with an enlarging 1.2 cm irregular, cavitary nodule in the right middle lobe. The lesion had been monitored for 3 years, showing a gradual growth of approximately 0.3 mm every 6 months. Suspecting primary lung cancer or metastasis, a diagnostic resection was performed. Intraoperative findings showed a glistening, mucinous lesion suggestive of IMA, prompting a right middle lobectomy. However, histopathology revealed a tri-layer epithelial composition including ciliated columnar, mucinous, and basal cells. Immunohistochemistry confirmed a continuous basal cell layer (p63/p40 positive) and thyroid transcription factor-1 positive surface epithelium, diagnostic of CMPT/BA. Retrospective 3-dimensional computed tomography volumetry determined the VDT to be 2526 days. CMPT/BA poses a significant diagnostic challenge as a radiological and intraoperative mimic of IMA. In this case, the VDT (2526 days) was markedly longer than the reported mean for IMA (approximately 440 days). Identifying such prolonged doubling times through serial imaging and volumetry can assist clinicians in suspecting benign entities like CMPT/BA, potentially facilitating appropriate treatment strategies.Keywords: Ciliated muconodular papillary tumor, Bronchiolar adenoma, Invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma, Tumor volume doubling time, Differential diagnosis

Introduction

Ciliated muconodular papillary tumor (CMPT) is an extremely rare, benign peripheral lung tumor. Recently, the term “bronchiolar adenoma” (BA) has been proposed to encompass both CMPT and related lesions. Differentiating CMPT/BA from invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma (IMA) is often challenging due to their similarities in both radiological and histopathological features, despite their markedly different prognoses. Due to its rarity, CMPT/BA remains under-recognized among thoracic surgeons, pulmonologists, and pathologists, underscoring the importance to establish a precise and well- defined understanding of this entity. Although the indolent growth pattern of this tumor is widely recognized, there are no detailed reports describing the tumor volume doubling time (VDT). Herein, we report a case of CMPT/BA with a documented 3-year natural history and discuss its clinicopathological characteristics, including VDT, and diagnostic strategies.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old woman with a history of smoking (190 pack-years) was referred for evaluation of an enlarging right middle-lobe pulmonary nodule. Her past medical history was notable for cecal adenocarcinoma (pT3N1aM0, Stage IIIa) treated 3 years earlier with a laparoscopic right hemicolectomy followed by 4 cycles of adjuvant capecitabine plus oxaliplatin for 3 months. Chest computed tomography (CT) for follow-up of the cecal cancer revealed a 1.2 cm irregular nodule with a small internal cavitation in segment 5 of the right middle lobe (Fig. 1A). The nodule had already been present at the time of the colectomy 3 years earlier, measuring 1.0 cm without cavitation (Fig. 1B). Because pulmonary metastasis was considered less likely due to the irregular morphology of the nodule, resection of the cecal cancer was prioritized at that time. The pulmonary nodule was monitored with chest CT every 6 months and showed gradual enlargement, with a growth rate of approximately 0.3 mm every 6 months. Tumor markers were within normal limits, and serum fungal antigen tests were negative. Primary lung cancer or pulmonary metastasis was suspected given the lesion’s interval growth. However, the lesion was not accessible via bronchoscopy, and a diagnostic surgical resection was therefore planned.

Computed tomography (CT) findings. (A) Follow-up CT 3 years after the right hemicolectomy revealing a 1.2 cm irregular nodule with internal cavitation in the right middle lobe (white arrow). (B) CT image from 3 years prior showing the same nodule (white arrowhead) measuring 1.0 cm.

Intraoperatively, the lesion was well-circumscribed and exhibited a glistening, mucin-rich appearance suggestive of IMA (Fig. 2). Given these findings, along with the relatively small volume of the middle lobe (approximately 10% of the total lung capacity), a right middle lobectomy was performed. Gloss finding of the cut surface revealed a 1.2 cm, well-circumscribed, mucin-filled nodule (Fig. 3). Histologically, papillary proliferation of epithelial tumor cells in a mucinous background was observed (Fig. 4A). Tumor cells exhibited minimal nuclear atypia and comprised a mixture of ciliated columnar cells and mucinous cells (Fig. 4B). Immunohistochemistry demonstrated that the basal cells were positive for p63 and p40 (Fig. 4C), the papillary surface epithelium was positive for thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1) (Fig. 4D), and the lesion was partially positive for MUC5AC and MUC6, while it was negative for caudal type homeobox 2. There was no evidence of invasive growth beyond the basal cell layer. These features supported a diagnosis of CMPT/BA. The postoperative course was uneventful: the chest drain was removed on postoperative day (POD) 1, and the patient was discharged on POD 2.

Discussion

CMPT/BA is an extremely rare benign tumor arising in the lung periphery and typically affects older adults around the age of 60, with most cases being asymptomatic and detected incidentally [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. The most significant clinical issue associated with CMPT/BA is that, despite its benign nature, its radiological and histopathological features closely resemble those of lung adenocarcinoma, particularly IMA, which makes a differential diagnosis challenging [1,3,5].

Radiological findings of CMPT/BA may vary and most commonly present as a peripheral ground-glass or part-solid nodule occasionally with a cavitary. The size is usually small (commonly 0.3-1.4 cm), and it is frequently mucus-filled, resembling IMA [[2], [3], [4]]. Macroscopic findings of CMPT/BA are typically well-circumscribed and non-endobronchial lesions and demonstrate a jelly-like or mucinous cut surface; these features overlap with those of IMA [1,3,6]. Given the difficulty in distinguishing CMPT/BA from IMA based on radiological and macroscopic findings alone, pathological evaluation is essential.

The pathological diagnostic hallmark of CMPT/BA is the tri-layer epithelial composition, consisting of ciliated columnar cells, mucin-producing cells, and basal cells, often accompanied by a papillary architecture and abundant extracellular mucin [1,3]. Key features for diagnosis include the presence of ciliated columnar epithelial cells and a continuous basal cell layer. In contrast, this entity lacks overt cellular atypia, significant mitotic activity, necrosis, and invasive growth [5,7,8]. Immunophenotypically, the ciliated cells are positive for TTF-1, whereas the basal cells are positive for basal markers such as p40 and p63 [1,5]. However, caution is warranted as IMA can also be composed of columnar cells with abundant intracellular mucin, potentially mimicking CMPT/BA pathologically [9,10].

An accurate intraoperative frozen section diagnosis of CMPT/BA is challenging, with reported diagnostic accuracy of only 16.7%-21.3%. Many cases are misdiagnosed as adenocarcinoma, IMA, or other forms of non-small cell lung cancer [5,7]. This difficulty largely reflects the fact that recognizing the histological hallmarks of CMPT/BA—such as ciliated columnar cells, a continuous basal cell layer, and the absence of nuclear atypia, necrosis, or mitoses—is often challenging in frozen sections. Accordingly, cautious interpretation of frozen section findings is warranted, and evaluation of permanent sections with immunohistochemical analysis should be performed when the preoperative or intraoperative diagnosis is uncertain.

Surgical resection remains the only definitive approach for both diagnostic and treatment of CMPT/BA. Given the favorable postoperative outcomes reported to date, limited resections such as wedge resection or segmentectomy may be sufficient to cure [[1], [2], [3],11]. By contrast, IMA more often necessitates lobectomy because of its tendency for mucus extension, multifocal involvement, and a higher risk of intrapulmonary recurrence [12]. Although the overall survival rate after surgical resection is comparable to that of non-mucinous adenocarcinoma, intrapulmonary recurrence occurs approximately 2 to 2.5 times more frequently. Notably, the presence of mucinous spread is associated with a significantly increased risk of recurrence and a worse prognosis [12].

In the present case, the lesion presented as a small, peripheral, irregular nodule with focal cavitation. Although its mucin-filled appearance at surgery was consistent with previous descriptions of CMPT/BA, this extremely rare entity was not included in the differential diagnosis, and IMA was deemed more likely. Based on these findings, the nodule could not be confidently characterized as benign at the time of evaluation. However, in retrospect, the lesion exhibited a relatively long VDT, which might have prompted consideration of a benign etiology during diagnostic work-up. To further evaluate this finding, we retrospectively performed 3-dimensional CT volumetry to calculate the VDT. VDT, defined as the time required for a tumor to double in volume, is commonly used to distinguish between benign and malignant nodules [13]. The VDT was calculated using the modified Schwartz formula (t2-t1)*log(2)/log(V2/V1), where t2-t1 represents the time interval between scans, and V2 and V1 denote the later and initial tumor volumes [14]. Tumor volumetry (Fig. 5) was performed using a 3-dimensional image analysis system, SYNAPSE VINCENT, Version 6.8.0037 (FUJIFILM Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The calculated VDT in this case was 2526 days, indicating a markedly slower growth than the reported mean VDT of 440.7 days for IMA [15]. Had CMPT/BA been suspected preoperatively, lobectomy might have been avoided. Given the limited use of an intraoperative frozen section in this setting, preoperative suspicion of CMPT/BA is therefore of critical importance. We propose that an indolent growth pattern, reflected by a prolonged VDT, represents a key clinical feature suggestive of CMPT/BA.

Conclusion

We report a rare case of CMPT/BA with a longitudinal observation over a 3-year period prior to surgery. Although CMPT/BA closely mimics IMA in radiological, intraoperative, and histopathological findings, their biological behaviors and prognoses are markedly different. Serial imaging enabled assessment of tumor growth kinetics, revealing an extremely long VDT compared to those reported for IMA. When encountering a mucus-filled small peripheral pulmonary nodule with such indolent growth, CMPT/BA may be considered in the differential diagnosis. While a definitive preoperative diagnosis remains challenging, long-term growth assessment may provide supportive information to guide appropriate treatment strategies, particularly regarding the extent of resection, in selected patients.
