Doping of a Borexino-like Liquid Scintillator with Tellurium-Diols
and Michael Wurm

Abstract
One of the most promising approaches for future neutrinoless double beta decay ( 0 ​ ν ​ β ​ β 0\nu\beta\beta ) searches is to incorporate a candidate isotope into the liquid scintillator of a next-generation neutrino detector. In this study, a sample of the high-performance 1,2,4-Trimethylbenzene-based liquid scintillator from the Borexino detector was loaded with different concentrations of Te-diols. Therefore, a novel and completely water-free synthesis in a non-acidic organic environment at room temperature was used. Key parameters of the loaded samples were analyzed and compared with those of the pure Borexino liquid scintillator. Both the emission spectrum and optical transmission remained nearly unchanged, even at high doping levels. The reduction in light yield was moderate: approximately 8,400 photons were emitted for a 1 MeV energy deposition by an electron at 1% tellurium-loading. The time profile of the light emission induced by alpha particles was also investigated, revealing that the scintillation response becomes significantly faster with increasing tellurium concentration.

For the synthesis, the orthotelluric acid was first ground in a mortar to obtain a fine powder. The telluric acid was then added to a round flask and neutralized with N,N-Dimethyldodecylamine at room temperature while stirring constantly. Benzene was then added to the mixture. To form the Te-diol complexes in the liquid 1,2-Butanediol was slowly added using a burette. During this process, water is produced in the reaction, which is removed by continuously bubbling dried nitrogen through the liquid. Typically within hours the milky and cloudy liquid clears up and becomes fully colorless and transparent while stirring continuously. For the synthesis, the molar ratios of the compounds (similarly to
[
7
]
) are 0.5:1:2 (DDA:TeA:BD). A tiny excess of a few milligrams of TeA ensures that no BD remains, which would have a negative effect on the transparency of the final scintillator, as it is almost insoluble in PC. To remove potential TeA crystals from the liquid filtration by means of a Büchner funnel equipped with ash-free filter paper (retention: 2 µm) was performed. However, it should be mentioned that only negligible amounts of telluric acid remain in the filter paper. In the final step, the benzene is evaporated at room temperature with constant stirring, leaving behind a colorless, transparent, and highly viscous liquid.

It should be noted that benzene was deliberately chosen to enable evaporation of the organic carrier medium at room temperature and atmospheric pressure. This ensures the removal of the by-product of the reaction (water) by means of an azeotropic distillation in a simple manner. It is clear that benzene could be replaced by toluene if necessary, and that azeotropic distillation could be carried out using a rotary evaporator and low pressure at similarly low temperatures. Despite its toxicity, benzene has proven to be ideal and particularly easy to use for the small quantities of Te-diols required here, while toluene should be preferred for later, larger applications.

To load the 1,2,4-Trimethylbenzene-based LS it is added to the Te-diols in a quantity that ensures a loading of 2% (by mass) of Tellurium in final liquid. Note that the mass-loading fraction with the Te-diol product is higher by a factor of about 2.67. Typically the dopant dissolves in the LS completely within seconds. The lower concentrations also described in the later chapters of this publication are produced by dilution of this concentrated 2% mixture with additional Borexino scintillator. All scintillators are extensively flushed with dried nitrogen gas and stored in light-tight glass bottles under protective atmosphere.

3
Characterization of fundamental Scintillator Properties

In recent decades, the requirements for scintillators in the field of neutrino physics have become increasingly demanding. Of particular importance are high transmission for the light emitted by the scintillator, particularly high light yields with spectra matching well the spectral response function of conventional photomultipliers, and a well-characterized and sufficiently fast time profile of light emission. The pseudocumene-based scintillator used in the Borexino experiment has set new standards in this regard, in addition to its outstanding radio purity
[
8
,
11
]
. In this section, the Borexino sample is compared with regard to the properties of spectral light emission, spectral absorbance, light yield, and time profile for alpha particle interaction with mixtures of varying Te-loading in order to evaluate the influence of increasing amounts of Te-diols on these key properties of the scintillator.

detector), the samples with different amounts of Te-diols were examined and compared with that of the unloaded mixture of PC and PPO using an Edinburgh FS5 spectro-fluorometer. To take into account the self-absorption of the samples, they were measured pure and undiluted in a

3.3
Light Yields

The amount of scintillation photons produced for a specific energy deposition by a specific particle species, the LS light yield (LY), is a crucial parameter greatly affecting the energy resolution and vertex reconstruction capabilities of a scintillation medium. In a two component scintillator (solvent + fluor) the LY drastically increases with increasing fluor loadings from approximately 1 g/l
-
10 g/l. Here, internal losses in the solvent (mainly due to impurities) compete with the energy transfer from the solvent to the fluor. At the critical concentration (typ. <1 g/l) for the solvent-fluor combination, about half the maximum light yield is reached
[
12
,
13
]
. For scintillation cocktails with effective energy transfers between solvent and fluor the critical concentration is lower, leading to higher light yields with lower amounts of fluor.

Absolute measurements of the amount of scintillation photons emitted for a given energy deposition in a scintillator liquid are very difficult and prone to mismeasurements. Therefore, most light yields are determined relative to a reference scintillator with a known LY
[
14
]
. For the LY studies presented here, this method was also applied with the undoped Borexino LS as a reference.

To evaluate the light yield of the liquid scintillators, the setup shown in
Figure 3
and described in detail in
[
15
]
was used. A 10.9 ml sample of LS is filled under protective N
2
atmosphere into a highly reflective PTFE cell with 2 mm thin UV-transparent glass windows such that the entire volume is free of gas bubbles. Before and after the filling the sample was saturated with inert gas by extensively bubbling N
2
through it. To protect the sample, the cell is sealed under moderate over-pressure. For the readout of the scintillation light, two conventional 1.13 inch PMTs (ETEL9128B) are coupled to the cell windows. During the measurements the sample is irradiated by mono-energetic 662 keV gammas from a
137
Cs source (activity:
\sim

3

3.4
Light Emission Time Profile

The pulses recorded by photomultiplier tubes (PMTs) of a scintillation detector are shaped not only by the detector hardware but also, more fundamentally, by the intrinsic fluorescence time profile of the scintillator itself. This time profile is determined by both the composition of the scintillator and the differential energy loss
dE/dx
of particles passing through it. For theoretical background and additional empirical data, see references
[
18
,
19
,
20
]

4
Conclusions

Metal-loaded liquid scintillators are expected to play an important role in the search for neutrino-less double beta decay in the future. Experiments such as SNO+ and KamLAND-Zen have already achieved initial success in this area. In this work, a sample of the scintillator from the Borexino experiment was successfully loaded with Te-diols. To realize this, the techniques of the SNO+ experiment were reviewed and modified towards a completely water-free synthesis in a non-acidic organic environment. The process enables a Te-diol synthesis at room temperature thus not requiring heating the reactants. Key parameters of the loaded sample were examined and compared with the initial sample. The emission spectrum and transmission were virtually unaffected by even high doping factors. The decline in light yield is also moderate, meaning that even with 1% tellurium, approximately 8,400 photons for 1 MeV energy deposition by an electron are emitted. The time profile of light emission by alpha particles was also studied. It was shown that the speed of scintillation increases significantly when the tellurium-loading is increased. This effect was expected, as the addition of large atoms or molecules to scintillators usually leads to chemical quenching effects that accelerate light emission while reducing the absolute light yield. Overall, it was demonstrated that the loading technique used here is suitable even for pseudocumene-based scintillators, which are known to be very sensitive.
