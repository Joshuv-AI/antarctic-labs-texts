Antibacterial Activity, Antiadherence Activity, Antioxidant Activity, and Molecular Docking Analysis of Arab Rose (Rosa Damascena) Extract on Periodontopathic Bacteria: Porphyromonas Gingivalis and Aggregatibacter Actinomycetemcomitans - An In vitro Study
Authors: Syed Wali Peeran, Manohar Murugan, Hytham Fageeh, Wael Ibraheem, Hammam Ibrahim Fageeh, Syed Nahid Basheer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Rosa damascena extracts were prepared, and their antibacterial and antibiofilm assays were investigated against two different periodontopathic bacteria (Porphyromonas gingivalis and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans). The aim was to determine whether the extracts could restrict the growth of these dental pathogens and to investigate the molecular docking between the myricetin of flower extracts and virulence proteins of dental pathogens.Materials and Method:The antibacterial activity of ethanol and methanol extracts of Rosa damascena was determined using the standard agar well diffusion method. Among them, methanol extracts showed more activity against dental pathogens. Methanol extracts (300 and 400 μg/mL) showed inhibitory zones of about 13.3 ± 1.05 mm and 16.3 ± 1.05 mm against Porphyromonas gingivalis; Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans showed inhibitory zones of about 13.9 ± 0.57 and 15.3 ± 1.05 mm.Result:Minimum inhibitory concentration studies revealed that 400 μg/mL of methanol extracts inhibited the turbid growth in the broth tubes. Antiadherence test results showed that Rosa damascena extracts reduced the number of organisms adhering to the glass specimens against the test cultures significantly. The molecular docking report revealed −2.53 kcal/mol of binding energy between myricetin and mfa1 of Porphyromonas gingivalis. Myricetin and target protein, cdtA of Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans revealed a binding energy of −3.46 kcal/mol.Conclusion:These findings conclude that Rosa damascena extracts can be considered an additive in natural antidental biofilm oral health products.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontitis is a prevalent inflammatory disease induced by periodontopathic bacteria growing in a dental biofilm on tooth root surfaces, causing progressive irreversible destruction of tooth-supporting structures with concomitant tooth loss.[12] It is also reasoned that this chronic inflammatory disease progresses not just by the mere increase in bacterial count but rather by dysbiotic interaction between the bacterial microbiome and the host immune response.[3] This dysbiotic state also manifests a shift toward an increase in the number of Gram-negative anaerobic periodontopathic bacteria, including Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans. Thus, these periodontopathogens are detected frequently in increased numbers in subjects suffering from periodontitis. Moreover, research has also indicated that periodontal therapy outcome is better if specific periodontopathic pathogens, including Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, are no longer detectable post therapy.[4]

Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans is a Gram-negative commensal bacterium associated with normal oral flora. It is also associated with dental biofilm formation and periodontal disease.[5] Infective endocarditis is an infection of the heart’s lining, usually affecting valves, caused by corrosion and exposure of subendothelium tissue to bacterial invasion.[6] Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans has also been shown to cause infective endocarditis in adults with poor dentition and underlying valvular heart disease.[78] Moreover, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, the periodontopathic bacteria, have also been known to upregulate cytokines and promote inflammation in host tissues.[910] These bacteria are of enormous significance in the context of oral health, being associated with both dental biofilm and periodontal diseases and can adversely affect the systemic health of an individual. Therefore, it is important to focus on and generate potential targets for interventions that prevent dysbiosis in dental biofilm and limit the load of these bacteria in the oral cavity.

Porphyromonas gingivalis, a Gram-negative anaerobic bacterium, has been extensively studied in relation to periodontitis, an inflammatory disease that affects the tissues supporting the teeth.[11] This bacterium is considered a major etiologic agent contributing to periodontitis.[12] Porphyromonas gingivalis is known to secrete potent proteases called gingipains, which play a role in the pathogenesis of periodontal disease.[13] Studies have shown that Porphyromonas gingivalis is often found in individuals suffering from periodontitis.[14] Furthermore, there is a suggested association between Porphyromonas gingivalis and rheumatoid arthritis (RA).[15] Research indicates that RA patients with severe periodontitis have higher antibody titers against Porphyromonas gingivalis, although this does not seem to affect the presence of the bacterium in dental plaque.[16] Despite differences in etiology, both RA and periodontitis share similarities in their pathogenesis, involving chronic inflammation driven by pro-inflammatory cytokines, connective tissue breakdown, and bone erosion.[17] Additionally, Porphyromonas gingivalis is known to disrupt the epithelial barrier and impair cellular function, expressing various virulence factors that are involved in the pathobiology of periodontitis.[18]

Moreover, scientific literature insists on developing natural therapies for dental biofilm bacteria instead of regular administration of systemic and conventional antimicrobials (fluoride, iodine compounds, and quaternary ammonium salts) to avoid multidrug-resistant microorganisms and side effects caused by conventional antimicrobials.[192021] Hence, to overcome the development of multidrug-resistant and virulent bacteria in dental biofilm, natural products derived from plants with bioactive compounds of significance can be employed.[2223] These natural products would also have the advantage of being less expensive and can be employed with no adverse effects.

Rosa damascena, commonly called various names such as Arab rose, damask rose, and Gole Mohammadi, has been extensively studied for its various biological activities, including its antibacterial properties. Research has shown that Rosa damascena flower extracts exhibit antibacterial and antioxidant activities.[24] The essential oils derived from Rosa damascena have been found to possess redox-modulating capacity as well as antineoplastic activity.[25] Furthermore, studies have highlighted the variability in the chemical composition of essential oils of Rosa damascena from different regions, affecting their antimicrobial activity.[26] The hypolipidemic effects of Rosa damascena extract have been explored in diabetic rats, emphasizing its antioxidant activity.[27] Rosa damascena extract has also been reported to exhibit SOD-like activity and cytotoxic effects on certain cell lines.[28] Additionally, the extract has shown promise in mitigating high-fat diet-induced nonalcoholic fatty liver.[29]

In conclusion, the antibacterial activity of Rosa damascena, particularly in the form of flower extracts and essential oils, has been a subject of interest in various studies. The plant’s diverse bioactive compounds contribute to its antibacterial properties, making it a valuable natural resource with potential applications in antimicrobial therapies. Therefore, with this background, the study aims to investigate the antibacterial and antiadherence properties of AR (Rosa damascena) extracts against Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans and Porphyromonas gingivalis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Procurement and extraction of Rosa damascena (Lin et al., 2012)[30]

The farmhouse at Tamil Nadu Agricultural University in Coimbatore, Tamil Nadu, India, was the source of the rosa damascena flower powders [Figure 1]. A conventional Soxhlet extraction device was used to do solvent extractions of the floral powders using ethanol. The extracted materials were scraped and stored at room temperature in airtight brown amber containers after being individually transferred to petri dishes and dried at 50°C [Figure 2].

Procurement of test bacteria

For the investigation, highly significant test bacteria with etiological traits linked to periodontal infections were chosen. The selected bacteria were as follows: Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans and Porphyromonas gingivalis.

Determination of MIC of Rosa damascena (Al-Madi et al.., 2019)[31]

To determine the MIC of test organisms, for every test organism, a set of five Mueller-Hinton broth tubes was prepared in sterile circumstances. About 100 μL of extracted Rosa damascena at different concentrations (100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 μg/mL) were added to each Mueller-Hinton broth tube. Subsequently, approximately 10 μL of the test culture suspension was added to the broth in isolation, and its growth was watched for turbidity. The tubes were incubated for a full day at 37°C under both anaerobic and aerobic conditions. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of Rosa damascena was determined to be the least dilution that inhibited the test organism from growing.

A loopful of extract sample was taken from the assigned MIC tube of the respective bacteria and inoculated onto MHA plates to determine the minimal bactericidal concentration of the Rosa damascena extracts (composition in g/L: acid hydrolysate of casein: 17.5 g; agar: 17.0 g, sodium chloride: 5.0 g, starch: 1.5 g; final pH 7.0 ± 0.2). To calculate the minimal bactericidal concentration (MBC) and minimal fungicidal concentration (MFC) of the chosen AR extracts, all of the plates were incubated and checked for an absence of growth.

Antimicrobial activity of Rosa damascena (Anita et al. 2015)[32]

Antimicrobial activity of Rosa damascena extracts was determined against the test organisms (Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans and Porphyromonas gingivalis) using the well-diffusion method. A sterile nutrient broth (composition in g/L: beef extract: 3 g, peptone: 5 g, sodium chloride: 5 g, yeast extract: 5 g; final pH: 7.0 ± 0.2) was utilized to inoculate each test culture, after which they were allowed to proliferate for a period of 24–48 hours. Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA) plates were prepared and left to set. In each case, 0.1% inoculum suspensions of the test organism were uniformly spread to the agar surface by using swabs. Sterility was maintained while 6-mm wells were cut on the agar surface of each plate. Each well received the chosen MIC of Rosa damascena extracts (20 and 30 μg/mL). In contrast, normal streptomycin was added at a rate of 4 μg/mL. A negative control was DMSO. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 hours, and then any significant inhibitory zones were looked for.

Antiadherence activity of Rosa damascene (Brandão et al., 2006)[33]

Each Rosa damascena extract sample was added to 9-mm test tubes along with 0.37 g of dehydrated culture medium (BHI broth plus 10% sucrose). Subsequently, standardized glass specimens measuring 2 mm in diameter and 5 cm in length were placed inside these tubes and subjected to autoclave sterilization. Next, 1 mL of 24-hour test cultures (Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans and Porphyromonas gingivalis) was added to each tube, and it was then incubated for 90 minutes at 37°C to assess the bacterial adherence. Following the incubation period, the glass specimens were moved into tubes filled with pH 7.2 buffered phosphate saline. A similar experimental setup was done for distilled water instead of AR extract samples in another set of tubes. After all of the tubes were shaken, dilutions of 10-1 and 10-2 were made in sterile NaCl (0.85%) saline solution for the initial suspension. Next, each dilution was plated in aliquots of 0.1 mL on BHI agar, and the mixture was cultured for 48 hours at 37°C. Following this, the number of colonies was enumerated and the log CFU/mL value was computed.

Antioxidant activity (Diphenyl-1-Picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay)

Three different standard concentrations (10, 20, and 30 μg/mL) of Rosa damascena solvent extract were prepared using DMSO. Each concentration of samples was mixed with an equal volume of DPPH (0.3 mM). For 30 minutes, the mixture was left to react at room temperature in dark environment. The standard control was ascorbic acid. For every test sample, three replicates were created. The absorbance (A) was measured at 518 nm after 30 minutes, and the following equation was used for converting the absorbance (A) into the percentage of antioxidant activity:

I (%) =100× (A0-A1)/Ao

where A1 denotes absorbance when the extract sample is present, and A0 denotes absorbance of the control.

The standard antibiotic demonstrated marginally better efficacy activity than the ethanol extracts of Rosa damascena, indicating that a higher concentration is essential to achieve comparable inhibitory zone size. Among the ethanol and methanol extracts, methanol showed more activity against all the dental pathogens. Hence, methanol extract was used for further studies.

MIC of Rosa damascena extracts

The micro broth dilution method was used in this study to assess the MIC of Rosa damascena methanol extract against the test organisms at various concentrations: 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 μg/mL. The test cultures (Porphyromonas gingivalis) exhibited turbidity in the broth dilution method at the first three concentrations (100, 200, and 300 μg/mL); however, no growth was seen at the concentrations of 400 and 500 μg/mL. Hence, the MIC was 400 μg/mL of Rosa damascena extracts for Porphyromonas gingivalis [Table 3 and Figure 4a].

CONCLUSION

Rosa damascena, commonly known as Arab rose, is reported to contain different biological compounds attributing to various properties, including antibacterial activity, antifungal activity, antioxidant activity, and antibiofilm activity. In the present research, solvent extracts of the flowers were investigated for their antimicrobial activity, antioxidant activity, and antiadherent activity against two significant periodontopathic bacteria that are highly associated with various forms of periodotitis. A bioactive compound called myricetin was investigated against different virulent proteins of the pathogens by using molecular docking analysis. The results showed good binding energy values between the compound and target proteins. The investigation thus contributes to the available scientific literature and makes it a significant natural resource with potential applications in antimicrobial therapies against the studied pathogens. The findings conclude that Rosa damascena extracts can be promisingly considered as a viable additive to various antidental biofilm therapeutic formulations.
