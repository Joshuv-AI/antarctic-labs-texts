Immunologic changes during treatment with romidepsin, azacitidine, and lenalidomide in relapse/refractory T-cell lymphoma
Authors: Max J Gordon, Logan Cook, Milos D Miljkovic, Rahul Lakhotia, Christopher Melani, Mark Roschewski, Jagan Muppidi, Irini Sereti, Louis M Staudt, Wyndham H Wilson, Samuel Ng, Joseph Rocco
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR:

Empiric chemotherapy is ineffective in relapsed/refractory (R/R) T-cell lymphoma (TCL).1 Immune dysfunction and mutations in epigenetic regulators are biologic hallmarks of R/R TCL and could represent rational therapeutic targets.2

Natural killer (NK)–cell expansion and activation can be effectively induced by administration of recombinant human interleukin-15 (rhIL-15),3 and when rhIL-15 is combined with monoclonal antibodies, it synergistically induces antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), leading to clinical responses in patients with R/R TCL.4, 5, 6

The epigenetic modifiers romidepsin (HDAC inhibitor) and azacitidine (DNMT inhibitor) have response rates of ∼30% as single agents and 60% in combination in patients with R/R TCL.7, 8, 9 In a phase 1 trial of patients with R/R TCL treated with RAdR (romidepsin, azacitidine, dexamethasone, and lenalidomide), the objective response rate was 89% at the maximum tolerated dose, but median progression-free survival was only 4 months.10 Interestingly, similar to patients treated with rhIL-15, NK-cell expansion was noted in responding patients. Both lenalidomide and romidepsin can expand and activate NK cells.11,12 Azacitidine has also been shown to increase NK-cell cytotoxicity.13 In myeloid malignancies, azacitidine induces CD70 expression, a promising immunotherapy target in TCL.14, 15, 16

Here, we characterize circulating immune cell composition in patients with R/R TCL treated with RAdR. We hypothesize that in addition to direct tumor cell killing, targeted therapies may enhance effector cell function and induce expression of immunotherapy targets, which could be therapeutically exploited to achieve more durable remissions in patients with R/R TCL.

We studied patients with R/R TCL treated on a prospective phase 1 trial.10 Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were collected before each cycle. Patients received a 7-day window of single-agent lenalidomide followed by up to 6 cycles (21-28 days) of combination therapy with RAdR (n = 10). First response assessment was performed after cycle 2 of combination therapy.10 Multiparameter flow cytometry was performed at baseline and before each cycle. PBMCs were stained with a 26-color panel modified from the optimized multicolor immunofluorescence panel–069.17 Data were acquired on a Cytek Aurora flow cytometer. Analysis was performed using FlowJo 10.5.3 and the cloud-based computational platform OMIQ.ai (Omiq, Santa Clara, CA). Unsupervised clustering was performed, and differential abundance analyses were used to generate volcano plots (supplemental Methods). Heat maps were generated to represent the relative expression of each immune marker.

We analyzed 64 PBMC samples taken from 10 patients with R/R TCL. Patient characteristics are shown in supplemental Table 1. We identified 11 distinct immune clusters (Figure 1A). These included 4 CD8+ T-cell populations (metacluster 3 [MC3], MC4, MC5, and MC11), 3 NK-cell populations (MC6-MC8), 2 CD4+ T-cell populations (MC1 and MC2), and 2 monocyte populations (MC9 and MC10; Figure 1B).

Patients who subsequently had a response to RAdR (n = 6) had significantly higher levels of CD56+CD16+ activated cytotoxic NK cells (MC8) at baseline compared with patients who did not respond (n = 4; Figure 1C). Responders had significantly lower pretreatment levels of activated classical monocytes (MC9) and CD56highCD16−CD70+ cytokine-producing NK cells (MC6) compared with nonresponders.

RAdR responders had significantly higher baseline levels of terminal effector CD8+ T cells (MC5) and lower levels of CD56highCD16lowCD57+ NK cells (MC7) and CD56+CD16+ activated NK cells (MC8) compared with healthy controls (n = 10). Both cytotoxic MC7 and MC8 NK-cell populations were significantly increased in healthy controls compared with RAdR nonresponders, similar to responders. RAdR nonresponders had higher levels of activated classical monocytes (MC9), CD56highCD16−CD70+ NK cells (MC6), activated CD4+ T cells (MC2), and early activated CD8+ T cells (MC3) at baseline compared with healthy controls (supplemental Figure 1).

During treatment, the absolute number and proportion of NK cells increased in RAdR responders, whereas absolute numbers of unselected CD3+/CD8+ cells did not change significantly (Figure 2A-B; supplemental Figures 2 and 3). We further evaluated dynamic changes in immune clusters during treatment (Figure 2C). Comparing baseline with the postlenalidomide window, we found significant increases in CD8+ activated memory T cells (MC3) and activated classical monocytes (MC9; Figure 2D). Increases in the abundance of CD56highCD16−CD70+ NK cells (MC6) and activated effector CD8 T cells (MC11) were observed after cycles 2 and 3 of RAdR. Activated classical monocytes (MC9) were increased throughout cycles 1 to 3 of RAdR, with greater abundance of CD8+ activated memory T cells (MC3) after cycle 2. Both populations were also increased after the lenalidomide window (Figure 2E; supplemental Figure 4). CD56+CD16+ activated NK cells (MC8) were increased from baseline after 3 cycles of RAdR and, notably, were also increased at baseline in responders compared with nonresponders (Figures 1C and 2E). Notably, at baseline, the total NK-cell expression of activation markers NKG2D or CD38 was lower in responders vs nonresponders (Figure 2F). NKG2D expression consistently increased on NK cells during treatment in responders, whereas it notably decreased in nonresponders, although these trends did not reach statistical significance (Figure 2G). Activated NK cells (MC8) significantly increased after cycle 3 of RAdR compared with levels after the lenalidomide window (supplemental Figure 5). In contrast, there were no significant changes in immune clusters in RAdR nonresponders, although MC8 (activated NK cells) increased after cycle 1 of RAdR (supplemental Figure 6).

Additionally, in patients with circulating tumor consisting of >10% of lymphocytes, individual gating on TCL cells was performed to evaluate CD70 expression, which could be targeted by clinically available antibodies (supplemental Table 2). There was a statistical increase in mean fluorescence intensity of CD70 on lymphoma cells after 1 cycle of RAdR (P = .13; supplemental Figure 7). CD38 expression on tumor cells did not change during RAdR treatment.

This study identified important immunologic differences in patients with R/R TCL treated with RAdR. At baseline, there was greater abundance of cytotoxic NK cells in RAdR responders, whereas cytokine-producing NK cells and activated monocytes were higher in RAdR nonresponders. Overall, nonresponders had higher expression of NK activation markers at baseline, an inflammatory state that may have hampered clinical benefits because the immune activating effects of RAdR could have been less pronounced. Given the small number of patients and lack of mechanistic studies, these findings should be considered as hypothesis generating.

In RAdR responders, early activation of memory CD8+ T cells and activated monocytes was observed after the lenalidomide window, which remained elevated throughout the treatment. Greater activation after RAdR initiation was first observed in cytokine-producing and then cytotoxic NK cells, along with activated effector CD8+ T cells. Only cytotoxic NK cells were significantly increased after RAdR treatment compared with postlenalidomide levels. Increases in NK-cell abundance were observed in most responding patients throughout the treatment course.

Lenalidomide, romidepsin, and azacitidine have all been shown to activate and expand NK cells and other cell types involved in ADCC.11, 12, 13 Regardless of the exact mechanism(s) by which RAdR modulates immune composition, our study suggests RAdR-like therapies may improve the efficacy of tumor-directed antibodies, for example CD70, by enhancing ADCC.

This study is limited by the small and heterogeneous population, and therefore, these findings should be considered as hypothesis generating. Additional limitations include a paucity of data regarding the individual contributions of the drugs in RAdR or whether the observed immunologic changes are an epiphenomenon related to responding lymphoma rather than treatment. Notably, we have not observed immune cell changes with other targeted agents in patients with R/R TCL.18 We were also unable to assess cytokines or immune cells within the tumor microenvironment.

Overall, the results of this study highlight that a RAdR-like regimen can affect antitumor responses through NK/T-cell activation and expansion, and, if confirmed, its efficacy could be further improved by combining it with tumor-directed antibodies. Mechanistically, this would occur by simultaneously inducing direct tumor cell killing, increasing tumor specific antigen expression, and enhancing tumor cell clearance by promoting ADCC (supplemental Figure 8). Targeted immunotherapy combinations warrant exploration for the treatment of R/R TCL.
