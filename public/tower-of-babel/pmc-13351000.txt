Biomimetic-voltage nitride-enhanced memristor arrays for femtojoule in-memory biosignal processing
Authors: Zijian Wang, Zhejia Zhang, Guobin Zhang, Xuemeng Fan, Pengtao Li, Baicheng Zhu, Yi Tong, Panpan Zhang, Dawei Gao, Bin Yu, Jiuren Zhou, Qing Wan, Yishu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Edge audio recognition demands extreme energy efficiency and noise resilience. Conventional separate denoising and recognition modules incur high overhead and latency, hindering deployment on resource-constrained edge devices. Inspired by biological audition, here we show a memristor-based neuromorphic system that monolithically integrates on-chip audio denoising and recognition. Our Pt/IGZO/SiNx/Ta memristor achieves a 19 mV biomimetic voltage and high endurance of 106 cycles. Fabricated into a uniform 1-kb crossbar array, the system supports partitioned processing: a volatile-switching denoising region enables real-time noise suppression, activating based on input signal strength without erasure steps, while a non-volatile region performs high-precision classification. This end-to-end solution attains 100% accuracy on 10-class audio signals (500 samples) after 15 training epochs with post-denoising, consuming 1.44 fJ per denoising operation, outperforming non-denoised approaches in convergence speed and robustness. Confusion matrices confirm >90% class-specific accuracy under noise, establishing a pathway for miniaturized, energy-scalable edge hardware.

Introduction

Rapid advancements in the Internet of Things (IoT) and edge computing have intensified demands1–3 for hardware capable of intelligent perception, particularly for processing faint biosignals prevalent in healthcare monitoring and human-machine interfaces4,5. Such signals, typically ranging from microvolts to millivolts, require ultralow voltage operation to avoid distortion from amplification stages. Conventional CMOS-based mixed-signal systems struggle to meet this need, suffering from limited energy efficiency (>nJ/operation), inadequate noise resilience, and the ability with biomimetic potentials6–8. These limitations fundamentally constrain real-time processing of weak physiological and audio signatures in miniature edge devices.

Memristors have emerged as promising candidates for integrated storage-computing architectures, offering dynamic adaptability, built-in fault tolerance, and suitability for high-density integration9–12. Their ultralow-power characteristics (<pJ/operation) and analog switching capabilities position them uniquely for biosignal-compatible hardware. However, prevailing memristor technologies face critical barriers: prohibitively high operating voltages (>100 mV), poor thermal stability, and device variability that preclude direct weak-signal processing11,13,14.

In this study, we report an intrinsic ion-migration-induced nitride-enhanced memristor composed of silicon nitride (SiNx) and indium gallium zinc oxide (IGZO) with the Pt/IGZO/SiNx/Ta structure engineered specifically for biomimetic voltage operation. The device achieves a 19 mV switching voltage, matching neural action potentials, and 72 ns switching speed, coupled with endurance over 106 cycles and state retention for 106 seconds (s). These metrics meet the biomimetic-voltage criterion and support the potential for direct interfacing with millivolt-level cellular signals without front-end amplification15–19. We attribute this reseult to interfacial ion dynamics vacancy (Si dangling bonds20–22) pathways between SiNx23,24 and IGZO layer, as resolved by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Utilizing this device, we demonstrate a 1-kb (32 × 32) crossbar array implementing partitioned in-memory computing: a volatility-based denoising unit for real-time biosignal enhancement and a non-volatile convolutional classifier. This co-optimized system achieves 100% accuracy for 500 diverse audio samples (10-class identification) within 15 training epochs post-denoising, consuming 1.44 fJ (volatile switching) and 248.2 fJ (non-volatile switching) per operation—outperforming non-denoised approaches in convergence speed and robustness. Confusion matrices confirm >90% accuracy across all classes under noise, with the synergy of steep switching dynamics (32 µV/decade) and noise-robust amplification enabling precision in challenging acoustic environments. Our work establishes a pathway toward miniaturized, energy-scalable edge-computing hardware.

Results

Ultralow Voltage Memristor for Biosignal Audio Processing

Conventional mixed-signal systems often grapple with high power consumption, inadequate integration, and vulnerability to noise, particularly in resource-constrained environments such as wearable devices or remote sensors25–27. These limitations impede the seamless handling of weak biosignals, including audio inputs, which require ultralow-voltage operation to preserve signal integrity without distortion from amplification28–30. To address the challenges, there is a pressing need to construct on-chip neuromorphic architectures leveraging adaptive, low-power memristors, which integrate denoising and recognition functions into a monolithic structure for direct processing of faint signals. Drawing inspiration from biological systems, such architectures can realize robust, scalable edge intelligence8,31,32.

We propose a memristor array architecture inspired by the human auditory system, specifically the synergistic roles of cochlear active gain33 and parvalbumin-positive (PV + ) neuron-mediated suppression34, which together enable the ‘cocktail party effect’35,36—the selective extraction of target sounds from noisy acoustic environments. In biological hearing, active gain from outer hair cell motility captures weak auditory cues, while PV+ interneurons suppress irrelevant background noise through inhibitory neural pathways, enabling precise auditory scene analysis. Figure 1a (upper panel) illustrates this process: an input audio stream undergoes selective amplification and suppression to isolate meaningful components, for example, distinguishing ‘four’ from ‘five’ in rapid speech under noisy conditions. Our hardware implementation (Fig. 1a, lower panel) translates these biological principles into a memristor-based signal processing pipeline. Incoming signals are processed via ultralow voltage operation, high-speed switching, and a self-resetting binary spectra integrative denoising (SR-BSID) methods, enabling simultaneous noise suppression and recognition within the same device array. Key to this performance is the memristor’s high sensitivity to inter-audio similarities, facilitated by accumulation effects at low compliance currents (CC), alongside sub-microsecond switching and biomimetic-voltage that yields biologically comparable energy efficiency37–39. In the biological auditory pathway (Fig. 1b), target signals often appear amidst strong background noise. Neuronal circuits handle this by balancing two processes: forming weak synaptic connections to suppress noise, and establishing strong, active connections to detect target signals. This mechanism allows the cochlea to extract faint acoustic features while rejecting interference. Our memristor array (Fig. 1c) emulates this behavior at the hardware level. With its intrinsic ultralow-voltage sensitivity, the device selectively responds to weak embedded signals while maintaining suppression of continuous noise. The self-resetting function ensures immediate recovery after signal detection, preventing the performance degradation that plagues conventional memristors under sustained noisy inputs. Unlike typical devices, which accumulate bias-induced drift under noise, our architecture maintains signal selectivity over extended operation. By physically implementing cochlea-inspired noise-suppression dynamics, we achieve high-fidelity weak-signal extraction with robust noise immunity, enabling efficient on-chip neuromorphic sensing and resilient edge computation.

The integrated weak biosignal processing workflow (Fig. 2a) begins with signal conversion via SR-BSID algorithm, which leverages the array’s volatile switching mode under 1 μA CC. In this mode, high-intensity input pulses that represent salient features switch targeted cells into low-resistance states (LRS), preserving critical information. Low-intensity noise pulses remain below threshold and are therefore rejected, while authentic weak biosignals reach activation through cumulative pulse effects. Critically, activated cells autonomously return to a high resistance state (HRS) without external bias, eliminating the need for explicit erase operations and cumulative activation of noise. We implement SR‑BSID on a 32 × 32 passive crossbar array consisting of 1024 Pt/IGZO/SiNx/Ta cells (Fig. 2b). Fabrication employed photolithography and lift-off processes (Supplementary Fig. 1), beginning with Pt bottom electrodes and SiNx isolated layer patterned on a SiO2/p⁺-Si substrate. A 30-nm IGZO layer was deposited by radio frequency (RF) magnetron sputtering (120 W), followed by a 10-nm SiNx (90 W RF), and capped with Ta top electrodes (see Device Fabrication and Encapsulation and Characterization). Structural and compositional analyses confirm the high quality of the multilayer stack. Cross-sectional TEM images (Fig. 2c) reveal continuous films with atomically sharp interfaces and the amplified section of the crosspoint positions of the passive array is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping and spectra (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 3) show uniform distribution of Pt, Zn, Ga, In, O, N, Ta, and Si, as well as the defined SiNx/IGZO composition. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements indicate ultralow root-mean-square (RMS) surface roughness (0.286 nm for SiNx and 0.317 nm for IGZO; Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 4), minimizing leakage currents and variability. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra of SiNx (Fig. 2f) exhibit >90% Si-N bonding with controlled intrinsic vacancies, while IGZO analysis (Fig. 2g) reveals 46.2% oxygen vacancies (VO) versus 53.8% lattice oxygen. This engineered vacancy concentration facilitates ionic transport, enabling uniform, stable resistive switching across the array. These material characteristics are critical enablers for high-density neuromorphic and in-memory computing, ensuring the reproducible, volatile switching required for SR‑BSID. Selective addressing of individual cells is achieved by applying voltage pulses to word lines (WLs) and bit lines (BLs), supporting robust array‑level operation for integrated weak‑signal denoising and classification.

Reconfigurable volatility and underlying mechanisms

We have systematically evaluated the electrical performance of the Pt/IGZO/SiNx/Ta device, demonstrating stable, uniform resistive switching suitable for low-power neuromorphic and in-memory computing (Fig. 3). Consecutive current-voltage (I-V) sweeps over 150 cycles exhibit reproducible bipolar resistive switching with ultralow set and reset voltages of 19 mV and −96 mV, respectively (Fig. 3a). The variable coefficients of set/reset voltage distribution are limited to 14.6% and 7.2% (Fig. 3b), indicating exceptional operational uniformity. The initial electroforming step requires 280 mV and exhibits a steep current surge (Supplementary Fig. 5). Finer voltage-step characterization corresponding to three different scan rates (SRs) is shown in Fig. 3c, allowing precise voltage control and selecting a convenient voltage step. Thermal stability tests (300–350 K) show only minor changes in HRS and negligible variation in LRS, with the variable coefficients limited to 11.8% and 5.3%, confirming robust switching under temperature fluctuations (Fig. 3d). Pulse programming achieves on and off times of 72 ns and 90 ns, respectively, using 50 mV (set) and −120 mV (reset) pulses with 1 μs width, critical for high-speed edge computing (Fig. 3e). Endurance testing verifies reliable cycling beyond 106 switching events, maintaining an on/off ratio of over 106 under pulsed operation (Fig. 3f). Retention tests demonstrate stable non-volatile states for over 106 s at room temperature (Fig. 3g). Sub-threshold voltage sweeps (from 0 to 16 mV) (Supplementary Fig. 6) reveal gradual conductance modulation prior to the set transition, indicative of an accumulation switching process. This gradual modulation indicates that the internal vacancies are rearranging, leading to an increase in conductivity that is not attributed to capacitive effects but rather to intrinsic material changes40,41, supporting the notion of a dynamic and evolving hysteresis phenomenon in our device’s operation. This behavior is further confirmed under sub-threshold pulsed excitation (Supplementary Fig. 7). The device activates continuously at 30 mV (Supplementary Fig. 8a), intermittently at 18 mV (Supplementary Fig. 8b), and sparsely at 15 mV (Supplementary Fig. 8c) with the CC of 0.5 μA, reflecting progressive ionic/electronic stable buildup toward the switching threshold. This gradient response mechanism enables the memristor to effectively detect weak signals, which is crucial for neuromorphic applications. Such a graded response enables the detection of weaker stimuli and contributes to neuromorphic integration by capturing low-feature signal components that conventional devices might disregard. CC modulation demonstrates a controllable transition from non-volatile to volatile switching below 1 μA with a corresponding loss of long-term retention (Fig. 3h). The corresponding volatile I-V curves for 100 cycles under 1μA CC are shown in Supplementary Fig. 9. At CC of 1 μA the device exhibits volatile switching under 40 mV, 500 ns write pulses, and 10 mV, 500 ns read pulses, where CC of 10 μA produces stable non-volatile operation (Fig. 3i) (see Device Characterization in Methods section for pulse test). And Fig.S10 further illustrates the device’s performance of resistance state retention after switching to LRS under different CC. At high CC (å 1 μA), the non-volatile retention time reaches 1000 s for LRS and HRS, respectively, while in the volatile state (CC ≤ 1 μA), the device switches directly from LRS to HRS upon removing the hold voltage. Statistical analysis across 100 devices (Supplementary Fig. 11) shows a 7 mV safety margin between the maximum hold voltage and minimum set voltage, minimizing inadvertent switching during hold operations (see Controlling Voltage Fluctuations in Methods section for voltage fluctuations analysis). Power analysis yields minimal energy consumption of 1.44 fJ for volatile switching and 248.2 fJ per recognition event, though operation at reduced voltages and currents increase the number of required pulses and total energy (Supplementary Fig. 12). Benchmarking against reported bulk resistive memories (Fig. 3j)42–52 and the resistive switching mechanisms of devices employing varying operational principles are differentiated using distinct colors for valence change memory (VCM), electrochemical metallization (ECM), and charge trap memristor (CTM), which confirms our device achieves competitive metrics, the low set voltage (19 mV) and a high on/off ratio (>106), establishing its strong potential for next-generation, low-power neuromorphic hardware. To elucidate the resistive switching behavior of Pt/IGZO/SiNx/Ta memristor, we investigate its charge transport characteristics and develop a physical switching model supported by experimental and simulation data (Fig. 4). Electrical analysis reveals two dominant conduction modes: Ohmic conduction, in which the I-V response is linear, and Schottky emission, where electrons are injected over a metal-semiconductor barrier. In LRS, ohmic conduction dominates, indicating that a continuous conductive filament composed of vacancy paths is formed and stabilized, providing a low-resistance pathway for electron transport. This aligns with the device switching mechanism dominated by the VCM. In the HRS, the ruptured filament leaves a nanoscale gap dominated by the SiNx energy barrier, suppressing current conduction, and the transport mechanism follows Schottky emission characteristics (Fig. 4a). Least-squares fitting to the Schottky emission model applied to HRS I–V characteristics is shown in Fig. 4b; over the full negative bias range the linearized Schottky plot yields a Pearson coefficient (R²) exceeding 0.996, confirming field-assisted thermionic emission as the governing transport mechanism. Corresponding Ohmic conduction fitting for the LRS data in Fig. 4c delivers an R² of 0.998, demonstrating that carrier transport proceeds solely through the metallic filament. The HRS-to-LRS transition under forward bias is governed by the migration and co-aggregation of VO in the IGZO layer and nitrogen vacancies (VN) in the SiNx layer. High-resolution TEM imaging directly identifies vacancy-condensed filaments bridging the electrodes in the ON state (Fig. 4d, e; Supplementary Fig. 13). Additional pathways located in the cross-sectional area of the slices, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 14, where multiple conductive pathways with diameters ranging from 0.7 to 1 nm and forming filaments can be observed. And we also obtain multiple sets of atomic-resolution cross-sectional images focused on the IGZO region (Supplementary Fig. 15), in which high-contrast filamentary structures extending along current-conduction paths are clearly identified at the nanometer scale. Comparative evidence from structural and cross-sectional characterization and device performance jointly supports a VO–VN composite percolation channel at the IGZO/SiNx interface; this coupling lowers the formation threshold and explains the low switching voltage (19 mV).The key role of SiNx layer in vacancy modulation is underscored by control devices lacking this layer, which exhibit a reduced switching ratio (~103) and higher operating voltage (~1 V) (Fig. 4f).

We propose a reversible switching model (Fig. 4g): positive bias facilitates the co-aggregation of VO and VN, forming a conductive path (LRS), while negative bias ruptures this path, restoring the insulating HRS. Interestingly, the filament composition—and thus device volatility—is tuned by the CC. Higher CC (>1 μA) promotes the growth of robust, VN -rich filaments in SiNx, supporting stable non-volatile switching. In contrast, lower CC ( ≤ 1 μA) favors metastable, VO-dominated filaments in IGZO, which dissipate more readily, yielding volatile operation (details shown in Supplementary Fig. 16). Limited by the current achievable spatiotemporal resolution of in-situ TEM, we are unable to perform deeper atomic-scale real-time observations of the dynamic formation and rupture of conductive filaments; consequently, we carried out device simulations to further help validate the mechanism. Technology computer-aided design (TCAD) of the Pt/IGZO/SiNx/Ta memristor further reveals that the vertical heterostructure formed by the isolation layer wrapping the functional layer creates a localized strong vertical electric field at the device center, which facilitates controlled oxygen-vacancy migration and conductive-filament formation—critical for stable resistive switching behavior. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 17, this mechanism converts the typical randomness of filament creation into a more deterministic process and enhances the formation of vertical conductive paths, improving device switching uniformity (see Technology Computer Aided Design Model Structure in Methods section and the distribution of internal electric fields in the device under different isolation layers is shown in Supplementary Fig. 18). And the low magnification TEM cross-sectional image of the fabricated device is shown in Supplementary Fig. 19, which matches the design in TCAD simulations. We also employed density-functional theory (DFT) to quantify how the formation energy of a conductive filament varies, under an applied electric field, between an aligned state (ON, with vacancies arranged regularly) and a misaligned state (OFF, with vacancies randomly distributed) (see Supplementary Fig. 20a). DFT shows that when a reverse field is applied, the formation energy of the ordered state rises while that of the disordered state falls. Consequently, the originally ordered vacancies lose stability and revert to a disordered, misaligned configuration, rupturing the filament (see Supplementary Fig. 20b). The opposite process holds under forward bias. Thus, the essence of our low-voltage operation lies in the DFT-revealed coupling between the vacancy formation energy and the external electric field, which enables the vacancy array within the filament to switch reversibly between disordered and ordered configurations at extremely low voltages, yielding ultra-low-voltage resistive switching (see Density functional theory calculations in the Methods section).

Array-level electrical performance and uniformity

Array-level characterization of the reconfigurable Pt/IGZO/SiNx/Ta memristor confirms stable, controllable switching between volatile and non-volatile states, providing a hardware foundation for monolithic integration of denoising and recognition functions. The programming protocol (Fig. 5a) employs a 1/2 operation voltage bias scheme (Supplementary Fig. 21a) that applies the full operating voltage only to the selected cell, while half-selected cells receive sub-threshold bias (Supplementary Fig. 21b). Volatility control is achieved through dynamic modulation of CC of programming pulses across the 32 × 32 array (Fig. 5b). By carefully tuning the current compliance level, we can systematically adjust the switching behavior of individual memristors, enabling fine-grained control over their volatile and non-volatile characteristics. At 10 μA, devices exhibit stable non-volatile behavior, where at lower CC (100 nA, 10 nA), then consistently operate in a volatile mode suitable for transient signal processing. Statistical evaluation across 100 devices confirms this CC-driven state transition (Fig. 5c), underscoring the reliability of current-controlled reconfigurability. Dynamic uniformity is assessed by collecting I–V characteristics from all 1,024 cells under representative CC settings - 5 μA (non-volatile) and 0.5 μA (volatile) (Fig. 5d, e). Array-wide statistical analysis reveals distinctly separated LRS and HRS distributions with minimal intra-array resistance variation (Fig. 5g, h), a prerequisite for robust binary memory operation. The array’s complex-data storage capability is demonstrated by encoding arbitrary patterns—e.g., alphanumeric sequences—through coordinated set/reset operations at predefined WL/BL intersections, followed by non-destructive reads to recover the programmed HRS (‘0’) and LRS (‘1’) states (Fig. 5i). Collectively, the demonstrated half-bias programming, CC-controlled volatility tuning, array-level switching uniformity, and reliable binary data storage/decoding confirm the high performance, stability, and scalability of the 32 × 32 memristive crossbar for advanced memory and neuromorphic computing applications.

Hardware implementation for audio denoising and recognition

Building on the developed ultralow voltage memristor, we constructed an integrated, partitionable 32 × 32 crossbar array with bio signal acquisition for end-to-end, on-chip audio signal denoising and recognition (Fig. 6). To efficiently operate the 32 × 32 memristor crossbar array used in this work, we designed a dedicated PCB, as shown in Fig. 6a. This read–write board is equipped with a dual 8-channel ADC, which effectively partitions the array structure into two synchronized 16-column modules. Figure 6b shows the bio-audio signal acquisition board, which is used to achieve high-precision acquisition and processing of audio signals in our experimental platform (see Array Testing via Custom Printed Circuit Board Platform in Methods section for details). The array operates in dual modes (Fig. 6c): volatile mode for real-time denoising and non-volatile mode for weight storage in recognition. In volatile mode with low CC (0.1 μA), devices switch to LRS under a programming pulse and self-reset to HRS at the holding voltage. In non-volatile mode with high CC (10 μA), LRS persists during the hold phase. The self-reset phenomenon occurring in volatile mode originates from the selected devices having holding voltages between 10 and 12 mV. The 10 mV holding voltage can be applied more stably while remaining insufficient to maintain the LRS of these devices, enabling automatic state clearing, which is crucial for denoising functionality. For audio recognition, the array is partitioned into two 16 × 32 sub-arrays (Supplementary Fig. 26). Discussion

We have demonstrated a monolithic, on-chip architecture for energy-efficient, noise-tolerant audio recognition, enabled by an integrated ultralow voltage Pt/IGZO/SiNx/Ta memristor technology. The device achieves a low operating voltage (19 mV), near-ideal switching steepness of 32 µV/decade, and current-controlled volatility, while delivering high endurance (>106 cycles) and long-term retention (>106 s). The device is integrated into a highly uniform 1-kb crossbar array that supports dual-mode operation. It employs volatile SR-BSID for real-time signal enhancement and non-volatile FCNN for in-memory classification. This heterogeneous operation enables 10-class audio recognition with 100% accuracy after only 15 training epochs post-denoising, consuming a 1.44 fJ (volatile switching) and 248.2 fJ (non-volatile switching) per operation. Notably, on-chip denoising enhances the SNR by an average of 21.4 dB and ensures robust performance (>90% accuracy) in noisy environments, surpassing conventional non-denoised approaches in convergence speed and resilience. These results highlight the potential of low-voltage memristor arrays for scalable, ultralow-power edge intelligence, paving the way for integrated sensory processing and adaptive decision-making in next-generation miniaturized hardware.

Methods

Device fabrication

The device was fabricated on a 4-inch p-type silicon wafer with a 200 nm-thick thermal silicon oxide layer on its surface. Initially, the wafer underwent a standard cleaning process to remove organic substances, metallic ions, and particulate contaminants, ensuring substrate cleanliness.

Sputtering system parameters

All sputtering depositions were conducted using high-purity argon gas (99.999% purity) as the sputtering medium with a flow rate of 37 sccm, controlled by a mass flow controller (±0.1 sccm accuracy). The deposition chamber was pumped down to a base pressure better than 5 × 10-6 Torr, with a pump-down time of approximately 3 h. The ignition pressure during sputtering was maintained at 99 mTorr throughout all deposition processes.

Bottom electrode formation

A lift-off process was utilized as the core fabrication method for the 32 × 32 memristive array. Photoresist was spun on the wafer, exposed using UV photolithography, and developed to define the electrode pattern. Subsequently, a 50 nm thick Pt film was deposited as the bottom electrode through magnetron sputtering at room temperature.

Insulating layer deposition

A second lithography process defined the device area, followed by the deposition of a 40 nm thick SiNx insulating layer using RF magnetron sputtering. The Si3N4 target (99.9% purity, 4-inch diameter, 6.35 mm thickness) was sputtered under a nitrogen atmosphere (N2 flow: 15 sccm). The deposition rate was calibrated to 2.5 nm/min and verified by spectroscopic ellipsometry.

Active layer formation

A 30 nm thick IGZO semiconductor film was deposited via radio-frequency magnetron sputtering at 120 W RF power, with the substrate maintained at 150 °C. The In2O3:Ga2O3:ZnO target (atomic ratio 1:1:1:4, 99.99% purity, 4-inch diameter) was used under argon atmosphere, achieving a deposition rate of 3.5 nm/min.

Functional layer

A 8 nm thick SiNx film was subsequently sputtered as the core functional layer using reduced RF power of 90 W, maintaining the same nitrogen atmosphere and target specifications as the insulating layer.

Top electrode completion

Finally, a 30 nm thick Pt film was deposited using electron beam evaporation, followed by a third lithography and lift-off process to complete the 32 × 32 cross-array structure fabrication.

Device specifications

Each unit cell in the crossbar array features precisely defined lateral device dimensions of 1 μm × 2.5 μm, with an active switching area of 2.5 μm2 at each crosspoint.

Encapsulation and characterization

To preserve the intrinsic chemical states of IGZO thin films and prevent oxygen vacancy annihilation by environmental contamination, a comprehensive sample transfer protocol was implemented throughout the fabrication and characterization workflow.

Substrate preparation and film deposition

Prior to film sputtering, substrates underwent rigorous surface preparation following a standardized three-step cleaning procedure: sequential immersion in acetone, isopropanol, and anhydrous ethanol, each for 10 minutes under ultrasonic agitation. Following chemical cleaning, samples were subjected to final blow-drying with high-purity nitrogen gas (99.999% purity) at 2.5 bar to ensure complete solvent evaporation and removal of particulate contaminants. All IGZO film depositions were conducted under controlled atmospheric conditions with chamber base pressure maintained below 1 × 10-7 Torr.

Vacuum storage system

Immediately upon completion of IGZO film preparation, sample surfaces were cleaned using high-pressure nitrogen gas under ultra-clean conditions, followed by immediate transfer to dedicated vacuum storage chambers. The storage chambers maintained internal pressure consistently below 0.01 psi (6.9 × 10-4 atm), with continuous monitoring systems verifying vacuum level stability within ±6% variation over extended periods exceeding 30 days. This sealed storage environment effectively prevented sample exposure to atmospheric oxygen and moisture.

XPS sample transfer protocol

For X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy characterization, samples were retrieved from vacuum storage exclusively within glove boxes purged with high-purity nitrogen gas. Upon removal, samples were immediately sealed in light-proof, nitrogen-filled containers to maintain inert atmospheric conditions during transport. The total atmospheric exposure time from storage removal to XPS chamber loading was minimized to less than 5 minutes.

Surface preparation and characterization

Prior to XPS analysis, sample surfaces underwent standardized Ar⁺ ion beam sputtering (3 keV, 1 μA/cm², 60 seconds) to remove potential surface contamination and native oxidation layers. XPS spectra were subsequently acquired under ultra-high vacuum conditions (<10-9 Torr) to ensure measurement of intrinsic oxygen vacancy signals from the film interior rather than environmentally-induced surface species59,60.

Device characterization

Using the pulse measurement unit (PMU) module of the Keithley 4200A-SCS semiconductor parameter analyzer, we characterized the device's high-frequency pulsed electrical properties. Initially, test devices were connected to the appropriate ports of the analyzer using a probe station. Subsequently, we configured the CC in the terminal settings of the analyzer. Specifically, the CC setting was adjusted by modifying the threshold in the device interface, ensuring that the maximum output current within the device was limited during the application of pulse signals. Based on the results obtained from DC testing, we established several typical CC, such as 0.1 μA, 0.5 μA, and 1 μA, to correspond with the volatile characteristics of different devices. The rise and fall times were set to one-tenth of the total pulse width to minimize signal distortion caused by parasitic capacitance and inductance. Each experimental data set was subjected to over 1000 repeated tests under the established CC and high current threshold conditions to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the data.

Controlling voltage fluctuations

In modern CMOS processes, voltage fluctuation is a controllable parameter: although Within-Die (WID) voltage fluctuation can reach 3.5% of standard operating voltage in 90 nm processes, and even rise to 6.6% in 45 nm processes61,62, through careful circuit design and layout optimization, voltage fluctuation can be completely controlled within 0.7%63. This means that at a 50 mV operating voltage, the actual voltage deviation is only 0.35 mV, providing sufficient stability assurance for device operation. Meanwhile, the low-resistance state (LRS ~ 1 MΩ) of our device is several orders of magnitude larger than the parasitic wire resistance. For a 32 × 32 array, the resistance of a platinum metal crossbar electrode that is 50 nm thick is about 110 ohms, so the resulting voltage drop can be neglected. This minimal deviation ensures operational fidelity and accurate state sensing even in large arrays. Therefore, the IR drop relative to the device’s own resistance is almost negligible and will not substantially affect normal read and write operations.

Signal noise separation based on statistical ratio optimization

The array must balance two seemingly contradictory requirements to improve its ability to capture feature signals: reliably capturing continuous feature signals, which requires signals to lie in the lower-percentile tail of the distribution; and robustly blocking strong noise, which requires noise pulses to have sufficient amplitude and fall within an appropriate percentile. This trade-off is directly reflected in the shape of the distribution, and the value of σ/μ — as a key measure of dispersion — must be tuned to a precise range to satisfy both requirements simultaneously.

Guided by practical use cases, we analyze two representative operating conditions. In the first, the strength of consecutive feature signals is set at the 20th percentile of the distribution, whereas sporadic noise pulses are 1.3–1.5 times stronger and must lie at or below the 50th percentile. Under the standard-normal assumption, the 20th percentile corresponds to z = –0.8416, so the feature-signal strength is xc = μ – 0.8416σ. The noise-pulse strength is xd = k·xc (k = 1.3–1.5), and simultaneously, as the distribution median, xd = μ. Equating the two expressions gives μ = k(μ – 0.8416σ). Rearranging yields μ – kμ = –k·0.8416σ, hence σ/μ = (k – 1)/(k·0.8416). For k = 1.3, σ/μ = 0.3/(1.3 × 0.8416) ≈ 0.274; for k = 1.5, σ/μ = 0.5/(1.5 × 0.8416) ≈ 0.396. The appropriate σ/μ range is therefore approximately 0.27–0.40. Values above this range imply excessive dispersion, causing a large fraction of devices to lie outside the functional voltage window.

In the second condition, consecutive feature signals must lie at the 5th percentile, while sporadic noise pulses are 1.3–1.5 times stronger and must lie at or below the 80th percentile. The 5th percentile gives z = –1.6449, so xc = μ – 1.6449σ; the 80th percentile gives z = 0.8416, so xd = μ + 0.8416σ. Combining with xd = k·xc yields μ + 0.8416σ = k(μ – 1.6449σ). Rearranging gives μ – kμ = –0.8416σ – 1.6449kσ, hence σ/μ = (k – 1)/(0.8416 + 1.6449k). For k = 1.3, σ/μ = 0.3/(0.8416 + 1.6449 × 1.3) ≈ 0.101; for k = 1.5, σ/μ = 0.5/(0.8416 + 1.6449 × 1.5) ≈ 0.151. The appropriate σ/μ range is therefore approximately 0.10–0.15.

These calculated ranges carry clear physical meaning. When σ/μ falls below the lower bound (< 0.10), the distribution is too tight, leaving insufficient separation between feature and noise pulses and precluding effective noise blocking. When σ/μ exceeds the upper bound (> 0.40), the distribution is too broad, pushing the set voltages of many devices beyond the operational window and causing functional failure. Only within the derived ranges can consecutive feature signals be reliably captured while sporadic noise pulses are simultaneously blocked.

Array testing via custom printed circuit board platform

To comprehensively evaluate the electrical and computational performance of the fabricated memristor arrays at the system level, we developed a custom-designed printed circuit board (PCB) platform to enable automated and high-throughput testing. This dedicated PCB integrates 8-channel array measurement capabilities, high-speed analog multiplexers for row/column addressing, and onboard current-to-voltage converters for low-noise signal readout. A dedicated control unit is responsible for coordinating device programming, state readout, and all timing and communication with the host computer via standard interfaces.

The testing procedure consists of several stages. First, the static electrical characteristics of each cell in the array are automatically acquired through row-by-row and column-by-column scanning—for example, resistance or conductivity mapping under a small read voltage—with the data synchronized through a high-precision analog-to-digital converter (ADC). This approach enables spatial uniformity assessment and defect analysis for large-scale arrays. Second, in situ vector–matrix multiplication operations are validated by applying a sequence of input voltage pulses and measuring the corresponding column-wise output currents. In addition, endurance and retention performance are further evaluated through cycling protocols (such as repeated array write/read cycles) and long-term stability measurements.

The ADC model we are using is AD7928BRUZ-REEL7. This ADC features a sampling rate of up to 250 kSPS, 16-bit resolution, and low power consumption, making it highly suitable for the high-precision, high-speed demands of our project. Its input voltage range is from 1 V to VREF (up to 5 V), which helps maintain flexibility and compatibility across various application scenarios.

In our design, the model of the multiplexer (MUX) adopted is ADG804YRMZ-REEL7. This product is an analog switch with four channels, characterized by low power consumption and high precision. The signal bandwidth of the ADG804 reaches up to 1 MHz, enabling the transmission of high-frequency signals, while its low ON resistance in the switching state ensures signal integrity. Furthermore, the maximum switching voltage of this MUX is ±15 V, making it suitable for a wide range of application needs.

For the digital-to-analog converter (DAC), the model we are using is AD5328BRUZ-REEL7, which has an 8-bit resolution output capability, ensuring fine control of the signals. The maximum sampling rate of this DAC is 2.5 MSPS, supporting a wide range of output voltages and meeting the real-time response requirements of our system. For testing a 32 × 32 array, the read/write board is equipped with two 8-channel ADCs, and the columns are divided into two 16-column modules. A selector activates the target module, while a JFET switch places the inactive module in an off state. All modules share a 16-bit enable for synchronous operation.

For the audio sensor and signal acquisition frontend, we employed the ADS127L01 IEPE-N1 high-performance low-noise vibro-acoustic acquisition module as the core audio signal acquisition unit. This module is based on a 24-bit high-precision ADC design and features a wide-range sampling rate configuration capability of 2-512 kSPS, equipped with a low temperature drift and low jitter clock system that enables precise control of arbitrary sampling rates, ensuring high accuracy and stability of audio signal acquisition. The audio acquisition module supports both DC and AC coupling modes, with the AC coupling mode providing a -3dB analog bandwidth covering 0.8 Hz to 250 kHz, ensuring spectral flatness within the passband. The module is equipped with a switchable IEPE constant current source that provides standard excitation for IEPE-type accelerometers and acoustic sensors, while achieving excellent dynamic range performance under input short-circuit conditions. Audio signals first enter the ADS127L01 module through a BNC interface, undergo processing through frontend coupling circuits and a programmable gain amplifier, and then are digitized by the integrated 24-bit ADC. The converted digital data is transmitted to the main controller via SPI interface, where the controller monitors the DRDY (data ready) signal to determine the availability of new sampling data, subsequently reading the digitized data through standard SPI timing protocols, thus achieving complete conversion from analog audio signals to digital data streams.

Array weight update

A custom-designed 32 × 32 memristor array control PCB is used, connected to the host computer via USB to perform weight update operations. In the system configuration, the FPGA controller parses update commands issued by the computer and generates standardized pulse sequences: the set pulse is specified as +50 mV amplitude and 10 ms pulse width, and the reset pulse is specified as −100 mV amplitude and 5 ms pulse width. The pulse rise and fall times are both set to 15% of the pulse width to minimize signal distortion caused by parasitic capacitance and inductance. Weight adjustment is achieved by controlling the number of pulses applied to the target device: set pulse sequences are applied to increase the weight, and reset pulse sequences are applied to decrease the weight.

The entire update process uses a row-scanning timing sequence, activating each memristor cell in the array sequentially, with conductance state readout verification performed immediately after each pulse operation. To ensure data reliability and reproducibility, weight update operations under each set of experimental parameters are repeated more than 500 times under identical conditions. Multiple typical current-limiting thresholds, such as 0.1 μA, 0.5 μA, and 1 μA, have been established to accommodate the volatile characteristics requirements of different devices.
