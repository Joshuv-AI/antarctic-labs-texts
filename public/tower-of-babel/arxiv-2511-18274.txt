Clinician-Directed Large Language Model Software Generation for Therapeutic Interventions in Physical Rehabilitation
Edward Kim, Yuri Cho, José Eduardo E. Lima, Julie Muccini, Jenelle Jindal, Alison Scheid, Erik Nelson, Seong Hyun Park, Yuchen Zeng, Alton Sturgis, Caesar Li, Jackie Dai, Sun Min Kim, Yash Prakash, Liwen Sun, Isabella Hu, Hongxuan Wu, Daniel He, Wiktor Rajca, Cathra Halabi, Maarten Lansberg, Bjoern Hartmann, Sanjit A. Seshia

Abstract
Digital health interventions are increasingly used in physical and occupational therapy to deliver home exercise programs via sensor-equipped devices such as smartphones, enabling remote monitoring of adherence and performance. However, in the current digital intervention paradigm, treatments in the form of software are typically programmed before clinical encounters by software engineers as libraries of parametrized exercise modules targeting broad impairment categories. Consequently, at the point of care, clinicians can only select modules and adjust a narrow set of parameters such as duration and repetitions. Patient-specific needs that emerge during encounters—such as distinct movement limitations, personal goals, or home and work constraints—are rarely reflected in the software, limiting personalization and contributing to lower adherence and reduced therapeutic benefit. In this study, we propose a new digital intervention paradigm that uses large language models (LLMs) to translate clinicians’ exercise prescriptions into intervention software. In a prospective single-arm feasibility study with 20 licensed physical and occupational therapists and a standardized patient, clinicians created 40 individualized upper extremity programs (398 instructions) that were automatically translated into executable software. We used a standardized patient because the safety of LLM-generated intervention software has not previously been studied. Our results show that the proposed paradigm yields a 45% increase in the proportion of personalized prescriptions that can be implemented as software compared with the status quo, with unanimous consensus among therapists on its ease of use. The LLM-generated software correctly delivered 99.78% (397/398) of instructions as prescribed and monitored performance with 88.4% (352/398) accuracy; 90% (18/20) of therapists judged it safe to interact with patients, and 75% (15/20) expressed willingness to adopt it in their clinical practices. To our knowledge, this is the first prospective evaluation of clinician-directed intervention software generation with an LLM in healthcare, demonstrating feasibility and motivating larger trials to assess clinical effectiveness and safety in real-patient populations.

Digital health interventions (DHIs)

Such software delivers step-by-step exercise instructions and, critically, can objectively capture adherence and performance using on-device sensors (e.g., camera-based motion tracking on phones or inertial sensing in wearables). As a result, it can quantify whether exercises were completed and how they were performed—such as range of motion, repetition count, tempo, and posture—and return concise summaries to clinicians for timely adjustment of therapy (e.g., modifying difficulty or introducing new exercises). This capability represents a practical advance over standard paper exercise worksheets, which cannot capture how patients perform prescribed exercises at home between visits.

We posit that advances in large language models (LLMs), particularly their ability to generate software from natural language instructions
17
, can offer a viable solution to this limitation. While LLMs have already been used in several healthcare settings
8

, their use for generating patient-facing software remains nascent. Early work has applied LLM-based software generation to data retrieval
35

In this paradigm, clinicians design exercises during clinical encounters, tailoring them to each patient’s physical impairments, rehabilitation goals, and home environment, and the LLM translates these prescriptions into intervention software. By freeing clinicians from pre-defined software templates, this approach allows the generated software to be structurally aligned with individualized patient needs. The resulting intervention software is then deployed to patients’ devices, where it delivers step-by-step instructions, monitors completion of each instruction, and transmits adherence and performance reports back to clinicians.

Here, we report a prospective, single-arm study involving 20 licensed physical and occupational therapists and a standardized patient to assess the feasibility of clinician-directed, LLM-based software generation from clinicians’ perspectives. We employed a standardized patient because the safety of LLM-generated intervention software had not previously been investigated, and we sought an initial feasibility and safety assessment before enrolling real patients. To our knowledge, the prospective feasibility of clinician-directed LLM software generation has not been evaluated previously in any domain of healthcare. In our study (Fig.

These exercises were immediately translated into intervention software by the LLM. Following the session, therapists observed the software instruct the patient, monitor completion of each instruction, and report monitored results back to them for evaluation. Therapists evaluated the LLM-based digital prescription paradigm across five dimensions: (i) usability of intervention software generation via the LLM, (ii) flexibility in accommodating personalized exercise design compared with the existing digital intervention paradigm, (iii) generated software’s accuracy of instruction delivery and monitoring, (iv) perceived safety in patient use, and (v) clinician acceptance of LLM-based software generation.

Clinician-generated intervention software delivered 45% more personalized interventions than the status quo paradigm.

In the status quo digital intervention paradigm, physical and occupational therapists prescribe exercises by selecting from a library of intervention software that has been programmed prior to the clinical encounter. By contrast, we propose a paradigm in which therapists generate intervention software after the encounter by writing free-form, patient-specific exercise descriptions that are then compiled into executable programs (Fig.
1
). We hypothesized that allowing clinicians to directly generate intervention software, informed by their current understanding of the patient’s needs, would enable a larger fraction of personalized prescriptions to be represented in software than the status quo template-based approach. To validate this hypothesis, we analyzed whether each personalized exercise prescribed after the standardized patient session could be translated into intervention software under (i) our proposed LLM-based paradigm and (ii) existing digital rehabilitation interventions (Sec.
4.6.2
).

Our proposed paradigm successfully translated 100% (40/40) of personalized exercise prescriptions into intervention software, whereas the existing paradigm could represent only 55% (22/40). This corresponds to an absolute increase of 45 percentage points in the proportion of personalized prescriptions that can be delivered as software under our paradigm compared with the status quo. Table
1
summarizes the personalization features that could not be encoded within the existing paradigm (the analysis data are provided in Supplement
9
). The most frequent limitation was procedural variation, where therapists substantially modified exercise instruction sequence, movement quality, or goals (37.5%, 15/40; for example, replacing simple shoulder abduction–adduction with a functional cube-transfer task). Other common limitations included intensity customization via new equipment (15%, 6/40; for example, adding a rubber band to increase resistance during finger abduction–adduction) and novel contingency logic beyond predefined progressions (15%, 6/40; for example, progressing difficulty only when new criteria were met rather than based on fixed repetitions). Finally, several prescriptions specified compensatory strategies that the template could not represent (10.0%, 4/40; for example, instructing the patient to rotate the trunk or use shoulder adduction to facilitate task performance).

Together, these findings suggest that clinician-driven software generation substantially expands the expressive capacity of digital interventions, enabling a larger share of clinically meaningful personalization to be preserved when translating therapists’ prescriptions into intervention software.

Instruction and Monitoring Accuracy of Software

While LLM enables therapists to flexibly personalize the intervention software, we also observed some hallucinations in the generated software.

After generating intervention software using the LLM, therapists evaluated the fidelity of all 398 software-delivered instructions against their original descriptions, assessing both correctness and completeness (Fig.

Correctness
was defined as the proportion of instructions that exactly matched the content and sequence of the therapist’s descriptions.

There was no generation of arbitrary instructions.

88.4% of prescribed instructions were accurately monitored by software.

The LLM-generated intervention software guided the prescribed exercises and monitored the completion of each instruction. Therapists then reviewed the software-generated performance report to assess its monitoring accuracy across all 398 instructions (Fig.
1
(iii), and refer to Sec
4.6
). The software’s monitoring accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were 88.4% (95% CI, 0.843–0.915), 88.6% (95% CI, 0.836–0.923), and 87.5% (95% CI, 0.809–0.921), respectively. Confidence intervals were estimated using a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM)
5
to account for the hierarchical structure of the experiment, in which each exercise goal (e.g., shoulder flexion; exerciseID) was assigned to a therapist (clinicianID), and each therapist created multiple instructions (stepID) for that goal. The GLMM incorporated potential intra-class correlations (ICCs) among exercises, therapists, and instructions when computing accuracy (proportion of correct classifications), sensitivity (proportion of actual successes correctly identified), and specificity (proportion of actual failures correctly identified). To assess the impact of ICCs, we also calculated Wilson score intervals

7
, which do not account for correlations. As shown in Fig.
2
, the similarity of the confidence intervals between the two methods indicates that the effects of correlations were minimal. No syntax error was encountered with any LLM-generated software. The observed monitoring inaccuracies arose from two sources: hallucinated conditions in the LLM-generated software and errors in visual movement detection. Upon manual inspection, we identified hallucinations in the monitoring logic for 10 of 398 instructions, where the generated code checked for conditions that were not specified in the therapist’s prescribed exercise instructions. The remaining inaccuracies predominantly reflected limitations of the computer vision models used to interpret the patient’s movements, leading to incorrect classifications of whether exercises were performed as instructed.

2.4
Perceived Safety of Intervention Software in Patient Use

90% of therapists perceived software’s interactions with patients to be safe.

As shown in Fig.
1
(iv), therapists observed the LLM-generated intervention software articulating and monitoring each instruction to assess the perceived safety of interaction between the software and patients. 90% (18/20) therapists reported that exercising with the software delivered via patients’ personal electric devices posed no greater risk of injury than using traditional paper worksheets which are the most common form of exercise prescription in standard practice (Fig.
3
c). However, 10% (2/20) disagreed with this statement, citing concerns that discrepancies in pacing instructions (ranging from 1 to 20 seconds before proceeding to the next instruction) might place undue physical demand on some patients.

Pacing refers to the software’s ability to adaptively wait for patients to complete each instruction before advancing to the next, and it is inherently dependent on the monitoring accuracy of individual instructions. During the software’s interaction with patients, therapists evaluated and flagged the instructions with inadequate pacing. Therapists judged 92.8% (369/398) of instructions as appropriately paced. These inadequate pacing resulted from the aforementioned hallucinations in monitoring logic in the software and inaccuracies in the computer vision models. False negatives (i.e., the software failed to detect a completed movement) led to pacing delays, while false positives (i.e., the incomplete movement was incorrectly judged as complete) caused the software to advance prematurely.

Therapists noted that pacing discrepancies, whether too slow or too fast, could lead to unnecessary exertion. For instance, if the software delayed progression while verifying completion of an instruction such as “hold an object in the air,” patients might hold the position longer than needed, risking fatigue or discomfort.

2.5
Clinician Acceptance of LLM software Generation

75% of therapists expressed willingness to adopt our clinician-generated intervention software paradigm in their clinical practice.

When asked about their willingness to adopt LLM software generation in clinical practice (Fig.
1
(v)), 75% (15/20) of therapists agreed or strongly agreed as shown in Fig.
3
d). Many highlighted ease of use and time efficiency, noting that real-time generation of intervention software during clinical encounters allowed them to continue interacting with the patient. A therapist explained,
“It saves time and effort. . . because I could just talk to the patient normally, I’m doing my treatment. . . it’s not eating into productivity time. And then at the same time, it’s creating the home exercise program (software) for me afterwards.”
Others valued the flexibility for generating personalized software, particularly in situations where exercise worksheets or software were not available:
“A lot of the time, for the type of therapy I do, there are no pre-existing handouts. . . So I’m having to kind of create things a lot… I like the fact that this can be customized quickly and easily.
”

Among therapists who were hesitant to adopt, the most frequent feedback concerned the monitoring function of the intervention software. Some wanted greater accuracy:
“They didn’t get all the success or failures correct. . . I don’t know how much I would trust it to be correct when they’re doing it at home when I can’t look at it.”
Others cited the the binary (complete or incomplete) classification did not capture movement quality or compensatory movements, and recommended adding granularity in performance reports about how patients failed, as well as what compensatory strategies were used during successful completions. There were also comments that multi-task instructions required more context-sensitive reporting than a simple pass/fail label:
“If your instruction step is asking multiple things to do and then they fail. . . I would just want more info.”

3
Discussion

We propose a novel digital intervention paradigm in which clinicians use LLMs to translate their exercise prescriptions into intervention software that can remotely instruct and monitor patients’ exercises. In our study, the generated software was executed on a standardized patient’s device to provide step-by-step guidance, monitor performance, and return monitored outcomes to clinicians for review. Our findings demonstrate that clinician-driven intervention software generation with LLM is technically feasible and acceptable to therapists in a standardized-patient setting, suggesting potential for future clinical adoption pending evaluation in real-patient trials.

The proposed digital intervention paradigm enabled a substantially larger fraction of personalized prescriptions to be delivered as software than the existing template-based paradigm, with a 45 percentage point increase in the proportion of prescribed exercises that could be implemented (100% vs. 55%). This finding is consistent with our paradigm’s design: therapists translate exercise prescriptions—step-by-step instructions for specific exercises—
after
clinical encounters. During these encounters in the study, therapists elicited individual needs (for example, physical deficits, home and work environments, rehabilitation goals) and crafted prescriptions accordingly. By contrast, under the status quo, intervention software is implemented
prior to clinical encounters
, and personalization is largely limited to selecting software and adjusting a narrow set of parameters (for example, repetitions or duration) while the overall exercise instruction remains fixed. Our paradigm enabled forms of personalization not previously supported by the generalized DHI template, such as incorporating patient-specific household objects used in activities of daily living and providing contingency instructions when patients had difficulty following particular motions.

Within this paradigm, the LLM-generated intervention software achieved 99.7% accuracy in delivering exercise instructions (397/398 instructions delivered as prescribed). Importantly, therapists preserved their role as clinical decision makers. As in standard practice, they reviewed the patient chart (here, a clinical vignette summarizing physical deficits), interacted with the standardized patient to elicit needs, and prescribed personalized exercises. The LLM functioned solely as a translator and did not participate in clinical decision-making. As specified in the system prompt (Fig.
1
), the LLM was instructed to deliver exercise instructions
verbatim as prescribed
, permitting only minor edits (for example, resolving ambiguous pronouns such as “this” or “that” based on prior context). Because therapists determined the intervention design, this approach leverages the LLM’s strength in converting text description into structured logic
17
while limiting its role to code generation. Our use of LLMs is therefore distinct from prior applications that rely on more complex clinical reasoning—such as diagnosis
21
, treatment planning
32
, or risk management
25
. Therapists’ feedback further indicated that this constrained, code-generation role was easy to integrate into their existing clinical workflow and, in some cases, perceived as enhancing efficiency.

While our proposed paradigm equipped clinicians with a tool to prescribe personalized intervention software, we also observed monitoring inaccuracies arising from both hallucinations in the generated software logic and limitations of the computer vision models used in this study. In existing template-based DHIs, libraries of intervention software are typically manually implemented, inspected, and tested by software engineers before deployment, which may, in principle, support higher monitoring reliability than an automatically generated program. By contrast, our current implementation did not include a manual validation step to mitigate suboptimal monitoring logic. Despite this limitation, we observed an 88.4% monitoring accuracy (352/398 instructions; 95% CI, 0.84–0.92), which we consider a reasonable initial outcome that warrants further investigation of LLM-based software generation for digital interventions. Moreover, the commercial LLM and computer vision models used in our study were not fine-tuned for this specific rehabilitation context. Fine-tuning these models on domain-specific data, incorporating more robust pose-estimation and activity-recognition pipelines, is likely to improve monitoring performance, especially as underlying LLM and computer vision capabilities continue to advance.

Regarding patient safety, 90% (18/20) of therapists judged the LLM-generated intervention software to be safe in its patient interactions in this standardized-patient setting. Because the safety of LLM-generated intervention software had not previously been evaluated, we deliberately used a standardized patient rather than real patients in this first feasibility study. This initial safety signal is encouraging, but it should be interpreted as preliminary and will require confirmation in trials involving real patients and longer-term use. Therapists observed the software articulate each prescribed instruction and wait until monitoring confirmed completion or time-to-completion expired before proceeding. This monitoring enabled adaptive pacing aligned with patient performance, making sessions more interactive than a patient reading step-by-step instructions on a screen. Most therapists considered the pacing adequate; however, two raised safety concerns, noting premature or delayed instruction transitions that could pose risks—for example, delayed detection of completing a task such as “hold an object in the air” could lead to fatigue or muscle strain. Improving monitoring accuracy through targeted fine-tuning may reduce premature transitions caused by false positives, while delayed transitions may be mitigated by carefully selecting sensor data processing models—exposed to the LLM as APIs in the system prompt (Fig.
1
)—with explicit consideration of processing latency.

To broadly support physical and occupational therapy with the proposed LLM-based paradigm, our study found that iterative co-design of the system prompt (Fig.
1
) between software developers and clinicians was essential. The prompt specifies guidelines for the LLM to generate intervention software, detailing desired functionalities and guardrails aligned with clinical needs. Because authoring such prompts currently requires programming expertise, AI researchers or software engineers remain necessary contributors to developing this class of tools, as with other LLM-based clinical systems. In this study, however, the prompt only partially met therapist expectations, tempering adoption. Some therapists sought finer control over instruction pacing, while others expected monitoring beyond binary labels (complete vs. incomplete). Although 75% (15/20) of therapists indicated willingness to adopt the LLM software-generation tool, with unanimous consensus (20/20) on its ease of use, these unmet needs contributed to hesitancy, underscoring the importance of early needs-finding and iterative prompt refinement to ensure alignment with clinical practice.

Our findings may have implications for other clinical domains adopting DHIs. Template-based DHIs in mental health
33
, chronic disease management
15
, pain management
12
, and speech–language pathology
10
exhibit similar limitations in personalization. As an illustrative (untested) example, a study of exposure and response therapy for obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD)
19
did not permit clinicians to introduce new task-specific contingencies (for example, “proceed to Step 3 only if hands remain unwashed and visible for 10 minutes”) beyond predefined instructions. In contrast, our approach allow clinicians to encode such contingencies directly in the intervention software (Table
1
). Prospective studies are needed to validate whether the benefits of LLM-based software generation observed in this study extend to other areas of healthcare.

This study has several limitations. First, we evaluated feasibility solely from clinicians’ perspectives using a standardized patient, without input from real patients. Second, although we hypothesized that enhanced personalization would improve treatment relevance and effectiveness—consistent with prior literature
14
;
16
;
41
—this requires confirmation in longitudinal prospective trials. Third, the study focused on a single patient profile and a small therapist cohort; broader inclusion of diverse conditions and a larger clinician sample will be needed to assess whether LLMs can faithfully translate heterogeneous treatment strategies into software with appropriate monitoring logic. Finally, in the absence of benchmark data, we conducted a post hoc comparison against a simulated external benchmark (a generalized software template). Demonstrating superiority in personalization will require a prospective, two-arm trial directly comparing LLM-generated interventions with existing digital rehabilitation programs.

In summary, we introduce a digital prescription paradigm in which clinicians use an LLM to generate intervention software
after clinical encounters
, informed by individual patients’ needs. This contrasts with the prevailing approach, in which clinicians retrofit pre-programmed software libraries authored
prior to clinical encounters
. To our knowledge, the prospective feasibility of clinician-directed, LLM-based software generation has not been evaluated previously in any healthcare domain. Our results demonstrate feasibility and therapist acceptability in a standardized-patient setting and suggest applicability beyond rehabilitation. These findings merit evaluation in larger, more diverse clinical settings with real patients and longer-term outcomes.

Methodology

4.1
Participants

We recruited 20 physical and occupational therapists through emails to rehabilitation centers or clinics within the United States. Inclusion criteria were that participants be registered and licensed physical or occupational therapists in the United States and have practiced for at least two years. Written informed consent was obtained before enrollment. This study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board (UC Berkeley IRB protocol ID: 2025-01-18162). The study lasted for 1 hour per therapist. Each therapist received USD $50 in compensation.

4.2
Standardized Patient Profile

A standardized patient was employed because the safety of LLM-generated intervention software had not been previously evaluated. The patient presented with right upper extremity weakness characterized as muscle strength graded 3 out of 5 on manual muscle testing, following the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale
28
for the shoulder, elbow, wrist, and fingers. Strength was preserved to the extent that the patient could move against gravity, but not against resistance. Active right elbow range of motion was limited to 80–120° by a hinged elbow brace with adjustable ROM settings. Cognitive, visual, sensory, and auditory functions were intact. These impairments were simulated using an orthotic splint designed to replicate nonspecific movement limitations commonly observed in outpatient rehabilitation. The clinical vignette was developed in collaboration with licensed therapists and neurologists on the study team to ensure plausibility without attributing the presentation to a specific diagnosis. This approach allowed for consistent presentation across sessions while enabling therapists to tailor exercises to realistic motor constraints without biases related to specific pathologies.

4.3
LLM Software Generation

As shown in Fig.
1
, we provided the following inputs to the LLM in addition to the therapist’s exercise descriptions: (i) documentation on syntax and semantics of the programming language, (ii) an API library, (iii) a coding guideline for software generation, and (iv) example programs pairing sample exercise prescriptions with corresponding exemplary software.

Programming Language Documentation.
We utilized Scenic
11
, a domain-specific language optimized for modeling and generating physical scenarios; however, this framework is adaptable to any programming language suitable for the targeted electronic devices.

API Library.
The library of APIs provided function calls to onboard sensors (e.g., camera, inertial measurement unit) and actuators (e.g., speaker) as well as external computation processes (e.g., camera visual data processing for body pose estimation).

Coding Guideline.
We prompted the LLM to write software in a structured manner such that it iteratively instructs exercises, monitors movements, and logs the patient’s monitored outcomes.

Example programs.
A curated set of exercise prescriptions and software example pairs was provided to the LLM as a reference on how to write software.

4.4
Model Selection

To identify the most suitable model for software generation, we benchmarked four state-of-the-art LLMs available at the time of our experiment (April 2025): Google Gemini 2.5 Pro, OpenAI o4-mini, Claude Sonnet 3.7, and Grok 3.0 Beta. We constructed a dataset comprising 14 distinct instructions and prompted an LLM for software generation, with the inputs aforementioned in Sec.
4.3
. We manually evaluated the generated software from each model. Grok 3.0-beta demonstrated the highest performance, accurately specifying logic to monitor the completion of all instructions. For this reason, Grok 3.0-beta was used for LLM software generation in our study.

4.5
Experiment Setup

Exercise goals
. We prepared 10 upper extremity exercise goals, each with a corresponding worksheet adapting from Shirley Ryan Ability Lab’s upper extremities exercises (e.g.,
36
), in consultation with licensed therapists and neurologists on the study team (refer to Appendix
6
). Each therapist was randomly assigned 2 goals, resulting in 4 therapists per goal (refer to Appendix
8
). This study design ensured that all 10 goals were covered and allowed us to examine potential variations in personalization of exercises across therapists.

Web portal interface.
We developed a web portal for this study in which therapists conducted live video call sessions with the patient, input exercise descriptions, and evaluated the monitoring capability of the LLM-generated intervention software. Fig.
4
shows the interface used for this evaluation of monitoring accuracy.

Patient device.
An augmented reality headset (Meta Quest 3) was used. We developed an AR application which executed the intervention software on the headset. The patient wore the headset and followed the verbal instructions from it. The AR application used the speakers as well as the sensors, i.e. RGB cameras and inertial measurement units, to instruct and monitor the patient.

4.6
Study Design

A prospective single-arm feasibility study was conducted. To mirror clinical practice, therapists reviewed the standardized patient’s clinical vignette and conducted a remote therapy session to design two personalized upper extremities exercises based on their assigned goals: one by revising a prepared worksheet and the other by designing an exercise from scratch. The LLM-generated intervention software then delivered the prescribed exercises and produced a performance report for the therapists.

4.6.1
Pre-labeled Evaluation for Monitoring Accuracy

To evaluate the intervention software’s monitoring capability, therapists conducted a pre-labeled assessment. Before observing the intervention software interacting with the standardized patient, each therapist was presented with a table (Fig.
4
, left) listing the sequence of instructions that the software would deliver. To rigorously assess monitoring accuracy, therapists pre-labeled which instructions the patient should complete and which should remain incomplete. This approach ensured that the evaluation reflected the software’s actual monitoring capability and prevented artificially inflated results. For example, if the patient happened to complete all steps, the software could achieve 100% accuracy by marking every instruction as complete without truly monitoring. Pre-labeling also created a balanced and known distribution of outcomes, enabling the calculation of accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. The proportion of instructions that therapists pre-labeled as incomplete was 36.3%.

4.6.2
Comparison of Existing vs Proposed DHI Paradigms

In the existing digital intervention paradigm for remotely instructing and monitoring patients, physical and occupational therapists typically select exercise treatments from libraries of parametrized intervention software that are programmed prior to clinical encounters. These software modules encode exercise instructions derived from standard exercise worksheets, while exposing a limited set of parameters (for example, number of repetitions) that clinicians can adjust for individual patients.

In our study, we constructed a proxy for this status quo paradigm by preparing ten exercise worksheets corresponding to ten common exercise goals (Sec.
4.5
). The instructions contained in these ten worksheets were taken to represent the fixed instruction sets used in intervention software. In this paradigm, the textual instructions remain fixed, and therapists can only adjust predefined parameters. Because there is no standardized schema for how existing DHIs parametrize exercises, we derived a generalized parameter set by aggregating the parameters used in digital rehabilitation trials in physical and occupational therapy over the past ten years. .

After each clinical encounter with the standardized patient, we asked therapists to prescribe personalized exercises for their assigned exercise goals (Appendix
6
,
8
); each exercise goal was assigned to four different therapists to observe variations in personalized exercises (Sec.
4.5
). We then examined, for each of the 40 personalized prescriptions, whether its exercise instructions could be represented within the existing paradigm by (i) selecting one of the ten worksheet-based instruction sets and (ii) adjusting only the parameters allowed by the generalized DHI template. If a therapist’s prescription could not be expressed under these constraints, we considered it not translatable into intervention software under the existing template-based paradigm.

Exercise Goal 1: Shoulder abduction and adduction

1.

Sit at a table with pink, blue, and yellow post-its.

2.

With your left hand, place the pink post-it on the table to the left of you by about 10 inches.

3.

Place the blue post-it in the middle.

4.

Place the yellow post-its on the right side by about 10 inches.

5.

Position your right hand on the blue post-it.

6.

Move your hand across the body to touch the pink post-it.

7.

Move your hand to the side to touch the yellow post-it.

8.

Move your hand across the body to touch the pink post-it.

9.

Move your hand to the side to touch the yellow post-it.

10.

Return your hand to the blue post-it.

11.

Relax your right hand.

Exercise Goal 2: Elbow extension using two objects

1.

Sit at a table with a pink and a yellow post-it in front of you.

2.

Rest your right hand on the table.

3.

With your left hand, place the pink post-it at the edge of the table away from you.

4.

With your left hand, place the yellow post-it at the edge of the table right in front of you.

5.

Position your right hand on the yellow post-it.

6.

Reach forward with your right hand to touch the pink post-it by extending your elbow.

7.

Return your hand to the yellow post-it.

9.

Return your hand to the yellow post-it.

10.

Rest your hand on the table.

Exercise Goal 3: Forearm supination and pronation

1.

Prepare a scoop in front of you.

2.

Sit comfortably with your right hand on the table.

3.

Turn your palm facing down.

4.

Slowly rotate your forearm to turn your palm facing up.

5.

Rotate your forearm again to turn your palm facing down.

6.

Grab a scoop with your right hand.

7.

Rotate your forearm to turn your palm up while holding the scoop.

8.

Rotate your forearm to turn your palm down while holding the scoop.

9.

Place the scoop on the table.

10.

Rest your right hand.

Exercise Goal 4: Thumb opposition

1.

Sit comfortably with your right hand resting on a table.

2.

Open your right hand with fingers relaxed and slightly spread.

3.

Touch the tip of your right thumb to the tip of your index finger.

4.

Release the thumb and index fingers.

5.

Touch the tip of your thumb to the tip of your middle finger.

6.

Release the thumb and middle fingers.

7.

Touch the tip of your thumb to the tip of your ring finger.

8.

Release the thumb and ring fingers.

9.

Touch the tip of your thumb to the tip of your pinky.

10.

Release the thumb and the pinky.

11.

Rest your right hand.

Exercise Goal 5: Finger abduction/adduction

Rest your right hand on the table with your palm facing down.

2.

Spread apart your index and middle fingers as much as you can.

3.

Bring them back together.

4.

Spread apart your middle and ring fingers.

5.

Bring them back together.

6.

Spread apart your ring and little fingers.

7.

Bring them back together.

8.

Spread apart your thumb and index fingers.

9.

Bring them back together.

10.

Rest your right hand.

Exercise Goal 6: Move fruits out of a bowl

Place your right hand on the table to take some rest.

8.

Pick up the banana using your right hand.

9.

Place the banana on the table.

10.

Rest your right arm.

Pick up the fork with your right hand.

10.

Place the fork into the container.

11.

Rest your right arm.

Exercise Goal 8: Remove coins from a wallet

Sit at a table with a wallet placed in front of you.

2.

Place your right hand near the wallet.

Hold the wallet with your left hand.

4.

Use your right hand to unzip the wallet slowly.

Place the coin on the table.

Place the coin on the table.

Place the wallet on the table.

Rest your right arm.

Exercise Goal 9: Stack three cubes

Sit at the table with orange, blue, and green cubes in front of you.

Use your right hand to pick up the orange cube.

3.

Place the orange cube on the table as the base.

Pick up the blue cube with your right hand.

5.

Stack the blue cube on top of the orange cube.

6.

Pick up the green cube with your right hand.

7.

Stack the green cube on top of the blue cube.

Grab the green cube with your right hand.

9.

Place the green cube on the table.

10.

Grab the blue cube with your right hand.

11.

Place the blue cube on the table.

12.

Rest your right hand.

Exercise Goal 10: Transport beads from a bowl to a towel

1.

Sit at the table with a bowl containing three colored spheres—yellow, red, and blue—, a towel, and a tong in front of you.

Pick up the tongs with your right hand.

Use the tongs to pick up the red bead from the bowl.

6.

Move the red bead onto the towel and release it.

8.

Place the blue sphere on the towel.

9.

Place the tongs on the table.

10.

Rest your right hand.
