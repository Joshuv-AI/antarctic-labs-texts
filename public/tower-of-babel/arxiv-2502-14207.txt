Quantum stick-slip motion in nanoscaled friction
Dai-Nam Le, Pablo Rodriguez-Lopez, Lilia M. Woods

Abstract
Friction in atomistic systems is usually described by the classical Prandtl-Tomlinson model suitable for capturing the dragging force of a nanoparticle in a periodic potential. Here we consider the quantum mechanical version of this model in which the dissipation is facilitated by releasing heat to an external bath reservoir. The time evolution of the system is captured with the Liouville-von Neumann equation through the density matrix of the system in the Markov approximation. We examine several kinetic and dissipative properties of the nanoparticle by delineating classical vs quantum mechanical effects. We find that that the Landau-Zener tunneling is a key factor in the overall reduction of the frictional dissipation when compared to the classical motion in which such tunneling is absent. This in-depth study analyzes the interplay between velocity, strength of interaction, and temperature to control the frictional process and provide useful guidelines for experimental data interpretation.



I
Introduction

The relative motion between objects in close contact causes friction as a result of energy dissipated in this dynamical process. Friction often leads to reduced efficiency and reliability of machines and devices. Although friction has been extensively studied, the governing mechanisms are not completely understood yet
Volokitin and Persson 2007
;
Vakis et al. 2018
. Computational studies using first principles methods have examined adhesion, corrugation, and van der Waals interaction energies for various materials
Wolloch et al. 2019
;
Cahangirov et al. 2012
;
Barik and Woods 2024
[another]. These directly related to static friction properties are instrumental in identifying materials with targeted tribological applications
Wolloch et al. 2018
;
Torche et al. 2022
.

The dynamical side of frictional processes is important in force microscopy applications
Szlufarska et al. 2008
and typically, it is studied with continuum approaches that are variations of the Prandtl–Tomlinson model
Tomlinson 1929
;
Prandtl 1928
. This is essentially a classical approach in which a moving particle without internal degrees of freedom experiences stick-slip motion above a molecular chain represented as an oscillatory-like periodic potential. The dynamics of the process is modeled semi-empirically with materials-dependent parameters for the corrugation, velocity, and particle-chain interaction. The dynamical frictional properties of atomic/molecular chains in relative motion have also been studied within the Frenkel-Kontorova model
Krajewski and Müser 2004
;
Krajewski and Müser 2005
;
Xu et al. 2007
, which was also used to understand dislocations, nonlinear effects and topological defects in relative chain displacements. Numerical methods, such as path integrals and quantum Monte Carlo simulations have shown the role of quantum and thermal fluctuations of the forced motion of an atom above a finite discrete chain. However, many-body collective phenomena at the quantum mechanical level are difficult to access via such algorithms due to numerical limitations associated with the size of the studied system.

A basic quantum mechanical effect important for the dynamics of frictional motion is the mixing between the energy states of the system creating conditions for tunneling. This is captured by the most basic assumptions in the Landau-Zener (LZ) theory
Landau 1932
;
Zener 1932
in which the energy difference between two adiabatic states is violated by creating avoided level crossings. The LZ theory has found applications in dissipative and multilevel systems
Pokrovsky and Sinitsyn 2002
;
Huang and Zhao 2018
;
Zanca et al. 2018
. Curiously, a dissipative LZ two level system coupled to a bath of harmonic oscillator shows that the environmental temperature can actually enhance the probability of the system remaining in its ground state leading to quantum annealing
Arceci et al. 2017
.

In this paper, we investigate the quantum mechanical Prandtl–Tomlinson model in the presence of an external bath reservoir into which heat is released in the frictional dissipation. Several energetic, kinetic, and dissipative properties are defined and examined by using the density matrix of the Liouville-von Neumann equation in the Markov approximation. To better understand the quantum mechanical nature of the motion, we also investigate the quantum mechanical Prandtl–Tomlinson model in the absence of an external bath as well as the classical limit with and without an external bath using the stochastic Newton law. The released power and lateral force, in particular, give a quantitative representation of the frictional process and can be directly associated with experimental measurements.

II
Model System

The system under consideration consists of a nanoscaled particle with mass
M
at a distance
d
above a one-dimensional infinitely long atomic chain with lattice period
a
, as shown in Fig.
1
. The particle moves with a constant velocity
v
parallel to the chain. To maintain this motion, it is assumed that
M
is in an optical trap modeled by a harmonic potential. The particle also experiences the influence of a short-ranged interaction from the overlap of its atomic orbitals and the atomic orbitals of the chain. Additionally, there is a long-ranged van der Waals coupling originating from the exchange of electromagnetic fluctuations between
M
and the chain. The optical trap balances these different interactions by maintaining a motion parallel to the chain at a constant velocity.

This system can be modeled by a 1D Hamiltonian given as

\hat{H}_{S}(t)=\frac{1}{2M}\hat{p}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}M\Omega^{2}\left(x-vt\right)^{2}+U_{0}(d)\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{a}x\right)+\Delta_{0}(d),

reflect the combined effect of short-ranged interatomic and long-ranged van der Waals interactions. Assuming that

U_{0}(d)=A_{s}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi dd_{s}}{a^{2}}}e^{-\frac{d}{d_{s}}-\frac{2\pi^{2}dd_{c}}{a^{2}}}-\frac{3\pi C_{6}}{8ad^{2}}e^{-\frac{2\pi d}{a}}

C_{6}

A_{s}e^{-r/d_{s}}-C_{6}r^{-6}

Stone 2013
(see section S-I in the Supplementary Information
sup

\Delta_{0}(d)

x_{c}=vt

The above Hamiltonian can be re-written in a more convenient form,

\hat{H}_{S}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\overline{x}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\overline{x}-\overline{v}\overline{t}\right)^{2}+u_{0}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right),

(2)

where
u_{0}=\frac{U_{0}(d)}{\hbar\Omega}
,
\overline{x}=\frac{x}{\ell}
,
\overline{t}=\frac{t}{\tau}
and
\overline{v}=\frac{v}{\nu}
are unitless quantities expressed in terms of a characteristic length
\ell=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{M\Omega}}
, period of motion
\tau=\frac{1}{\Omega}
, and characteristic velocity
\nu=\frac{\ell}{\tau}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\Omega}{M}}
. This rescaling is equivalent to setting
\hbar=M=\Omega=1
.

The above Hamiltonian is a quantum mechanical version of the classical Prandtl-Tomlinson model of a particle driven by a spring above a sinusoidally corrugated potential. This model has found many applications in the analysis of dynamic friction, especially in the understanding of experiments using atomic force microscopes
Meyer et al. 1998
. It has also been utilized in capturing temperature in sliding frictional processes
Müser 2011
. Nevertheless, this is a classical approach, and here we expand its applicability at the quantum mechanical level.

II.1
Dynamics of the moving quantum particle: unitary evolution

To investigate the dynamical evolution of this closed system, we first solve the eigenvalue problem of the Schrödinger equation,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{S}(\overline{t})\left|n(\overline{t})\right>=E_{n}(\overline{t})\left|n(\overline{t})\right>.

(3)

H_{p}

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{p^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}(H_{S})_{p,p^{\prime}}(\overline{t})c_{n,p^{\prime}}(\overline{t})=E_{n}(\overline{t})c_{n,p}(\overline{t}).

(4)

\displaystyle\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}+\sin\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{v}\overline{t}\right)&\text{when }|n-n^{\prime}|\text{ is odd}\\

5

II.1

\mathcal{V}_{n,n}(\overline{t})
is controlled by the parameters
\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}
and the velocity
\overline{v}=\dfrac{v}{\nu}
. In the case of

a\ll\ell

e^{-\frac{1}{4}(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a})^{2}}\to 0

H_{S}

E_{n}(\overline{t})\approx n+\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{u_{0}}{2}

In the case of
a\gg\ell
, the
\mathcal{V}_{n,n}(\overline{t})

\left(\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\right)^{|n-n^{\prime}|}\to 0
and only the diagonal terms matter. As a result, the eigenenergies become oscillatory according to
E_{n}(\overline{t})\approx n+\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{u_{0}}{2}-\dfrac{u_{0}}{2}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{v}\overline{t}\right)

\left(\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\right)\to 0
or
\left(\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\right)\to\infty

When the ratio
\left(\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\right)\sim 1
, however, the off-diagonal elements play a vital role for the particle as the eigenstates now experience the influence of different
\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>

;
Zener 1932

II.2
Dynamics of the moving quantum particle with coupling to the environment

The dynamics of the system described by the Hamiltonian in Eqs. (
1
)-(
2

The forced motion of the particle, however, results in energy removed by dissipation. To model this we consider that the paricle/atomic chain system is coupled to an external thermostat taken as a harmonic bath in the Caldeira–Leggett model

;
Weiss 2012

(11)

where

, where
\omega_{c}
is a cutoff frequency separating the low-frequency regime for which
J(\omega)\propto\omega
and the high-frequency regime specified by a decaying Ohmic damping. The dimensionless constant
\alpha\ll 1
specifies the coupling between the particle/chain system and the harmonic bath. Notice that if
\alpha=0
, then
J(\omega)=0
for all frequencies and
c_{i}=0
i.e. there is no coupling between the system and the bath and
\hat{H}_{int}=0
.

To continue further, the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
11
) is represented by separating the degrees of freedom of the nanoparticle from the degrees of freedom of the harmonic bath, such that

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{S}^{\prime}(\overline{t})\otimes\hat{1}_{B}+\hat{A}\otimes\hat{B}+\hat{1}_{S}\otimes\hat{H}_{B},

(12)

with a renormalization operator
\hat{A}=\sin\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right)
and
\hat{H}_{S}^{\prime}(\overline{t})=\hat{H}_{S}(\overline{t})+u_{rn}\hat{A}^{2}
act in the subspace of the particle, while
\hat{B}=-\sum\limits_{i}c_{i}x_{i}
and
\hat{H}_{B}=\sum\limits_{i}\left(-\frac{1}{2m_{i}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}m_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}x_{i}^{2}\right)
operate in the subspace of the harmonic bath. The term
u_{rn}\hat{A}^{2}
captures the dissipative coupling between the moving particle and the heat bath. The renormalization constant can be determined via the spectral density function
u_{rn}=\sum\limits_{i}\frac{c_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}}=\sum\limits_{i}\frac{c_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}\omega_{i}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\omega_{i}^{-1}\delta(\omega-\omega_{i})d\omega=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\omega^{-1}J(\omega)d\omega=2\alpha\omega_{c}
, as also shown in Ref.
Zanca et al. 2018
. This renormalization potential in the total Hamiltonian (
11
) ensures that the system maintains its global minimum regardless of the bath
Weiss 2012
.

Assuming weak coupling consistent with the Born-Markov approximation, the quantum Liouville - von Neumann master equation for the density matrix is written as
Yamaguchi et al. 2017

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\overline{t}}\hat{\rho}_{S}(\overline{t})=-i\left[\hat{H}_{S}(\overline{t})+2\alpha\omega_{c}\hat{A}^{2},\hat{\rho}_{S}(\overline{t})\right]-\left\{\left[\hat{A},\hat{S}(\overline{t})\hat{\rho}_{S}(\overline{t})\right]+\text{Hermitian conjugate}\right\},

(13)

where the bath-convoluted operator
\hat{S}(t)
is defined as
\hat{S}(t)\approx\sum_{m,n}\left<n(\overline{t})\middle|\hat{A}\middle|m(\overline{t})\right>\Gamma\left(E_{m}-E_{n}\right)\left|n(\overline{t})\right>\left<m(\overline{t})\right|
in which the bath-induced transition rate
\Gamma\left(E\right)
is simply the Fourier transform of the bath correlation function
C(\tau)
for which
\Gamma(E)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}d\tau C(\tau)e^{i(E+i0^{+})\tau}
. Taking into account that the harmonic bath is in a thermal equilibrium obeying Bose-Einstein distribution
f_{BE}(\omega)=\left(e^{\omega/k_{B}T}-1\right)^{-1}
, the bath correlation function can be expressed in term of the spectral function
C(\tau)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{i\omega\tau}f_{BE}(\omega)J(\omega)d\omega
. The bath-induced transition rate can further be simplified as
\Gamma\left(E\right)=\frac{1}{2}\gamma(E)+i\sigma(E)
where
\gamma(E)=\frac{4\pi\alpha Ee^{-|E|/\omega_{c}}}{1-e^{-|E|/{k_{B}T}}}
and
\sigma(E)=\mathcal{P}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{2\alpha\xi e^{-|\xi|/\omega_{c}}d\xi}{(E+\xi)(e^{-\xi/k_{B}T}-1)}
. For
E=0
,
\gamma(0)=4\pi\alpha k_{B}T
and
\sigma(0)=-2\alpha\omega_{c}
.

Similar to the case of the moving particle with no external bath, Eq. (
13
) can be expressed in the basis set of the time-dependent harmonic oscillators
\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>=\varphi_{n}^{HO}(\bar{x}-\bar{v}\bar{t})
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\overline{t}}\rho_{S}(\overline{t})=-i\left[H_{S}(t)+2\alpha\omega_{c}A^{2}(\overline{t})-\sigma_{t}(\overline{t}),\rho_{S}(\overline{t})\right]-\left\{\left[A(\overline{t}),S(\overline{t})\rho_{S}(\overline{t})\right]+\text{Hermitian conjugate}\right\},

(14)

where the matrix elements of
\hat{A}

\displaystyle S_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,m^{\prime},k,k^{\prime}}c_{m,n}(\overline{t})c^{*}_{m,k}(\overline{t})A_{k,k^{\prime}}(\overline{t})c_{m^{\prime},k^{\prime}}(\overline{t})c_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}}^{*}(\overline{t})\Gamma\left[E_{m^{\prime}}(\overline{t})-E_{m}(\overline{t})\right].

(18)

II.3
Dynamics of the moving particle within the classical Prandtl-Tomlinson model

In order to understand better the quantum mechanical effects in the frictional dissipation process, we also examine the motion of the particle within the classical Prandtl-Tomlinson model. From the classical version of the Hamiltonian in (
11
), the stochastic dynamics of the particle is captured through the Hamilton’s equations of motion with coupling to an external bath via a function
f_{bath}

Weiss 2012

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\dfrac{d^{2}\overline{x}}{d\overline{t}^{2}}+\left(\overline{x}-\overline{v}\overline{t}\right)+\dfrac{u_{0}\pi\ell}{a}\sin\left(\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right)&=&f_{bath}(\overline{x},\overline{t}),\\
\dfrac{d^{2}x_{i}}{d\overline{t}^{2}}+\omega_{i}^{2}x_{i}&=&\dfrac{c_{i}}{m_{i}}\sin\left(\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right)\end{array}\right.,

\displaystyle f_{bath}(\overline{x},\overline{t})=\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\left(\sum\limits_{i}x_{i}c_{i}\right)\cos\left(\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right)-\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\left(\sum\limits_{i}\dfrac{c_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}}\right)\sin\left(\dfrac{4\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right).

(22)

To solve the above system of equations,
the bath degrees of freedom
x_{i}
are first resolved by using the Green function of the harmonic bath
G(\overline{t},\overline{t}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\omega_{i}}\Theta(\overline{t}-\overline{t}^{\prime})\sin\left[\omega_{i}(\overline{t}-\overline{t}^{\prime})\right]
and taking into account the initial conditions
x_{i}(0),v_{i}(0)
of the external bath. This is followed by a summation of bath degrees of freedom using the spectral function
J(\omega)=\sum\limits_{i}\frac{c_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}\omega_{i}}\delta\left(\omega-\omega_{i}\right)=2\alpha\omega e^{-|\omega|/\omega_{c}}
and the classical damping correlation
\lim\limits_{\omega_{c}\to\infty}\frac{4\alpha\omega_{c}}{1+\omega_{c}^{2}\overline{t}^{2}}=4\pi\alpha\delta(\overline{t})
following standard procedure
Weiss 2012

\alpha
denotes the coupling parameter with the external bath and
\omega_{c}
is a cutoff frequency.

The bath coupling function can now be conveniently written as
f_{bath}\left[\overline{x}(\overline{t}),\overline{t}\right]=f_{vis}\left[\overline{x},\dfrac{d\overline{x}}{d\overline{t}}\right]+f_{ran}\left(\overline{t}\right)

\displaystyle f_{vis}\left[\overline{x},\dfrac{d\overline{x}}{d\overline{t}}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{8\alpha\pi^{3}\ell^{2}}{a^{2}}\cos^{2}\left(\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right)\dfrac{d\overline{x}}{d\overline{t}},

(23)

\displaystyle f_{ran}\left(\overline{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\left\{\sum\limits_{i}c_{i}\left[x_{i}^{homo}(\overline{t})-\sum\limits_{i}\dfrac{c_{i}}{m_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}}\sin\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(0)\right]\cos\left(\omega_{i}\overline{t}\right)\right]\right\}\cos\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t})\right].

(24)

Rytov et al. 1989

The dissipative evolution of the particle/atomic chain system is further studied by examining its kinematic properties, as given in Table

By comparing the different regimes in Fig.
3

For driving velocities larger than the first Landau-Zener velocity

n=0\to n=1

makes the state of the particle mainly distribute to the first excited state whose center moves from the second well back to the first well of the potential surface at
t/T=0.5

After the initial stick-slip motion, the particle is in a superposition state and it experiences a series of stick-slip oscillations with a frequency in the order of
\Omega
. Higher driving velocities involve contributions from higher states, which makes the stick-slip oscillations have more complex oscillatory patterns. Since the period of motion
T

While the
\left<x\right>

IV
Conclusions

Friction at the nanoscale is a complex process, and here we offer a comprehensive study delineating quantum mechanical vs classical effects in the Prandtl-Tomlinson model, one of the most popular models in tribology. By focusing on the stick-slip regime (also captured in the classical corrugation parameter), it is found that Landau-Zener tunneling at multiple eigenenergy level anti-crossings is a quantum mechanical distinguishing factor. It is responsible for allowing the particle to permeate through the potential barriers, which are essentially impenetrable for the classical particle. As a result, the stick-slip quantum mechanical motion experiences reduced friction as compared to the classical case. The quantum mechanical effects in the nanoscaled friction are expected to play a reduced role at higher temperatures. As
T
is increased, higher states will become important leading to more pronounced classical features in the motion of the particle.

The theoretical understanding is made possible by presenting the full description of the quantum mechanical and classical methodlogies of Prandtl-Tomlinson model by distinguishing quantum vs classical vs dissipative aspects of the nanoscaled friction. A variety of properties are defined and examined in order to track the time dynamics and velocity functionality of the frictional process. Dissipative heat, dissipative thermal power, and lateral force can serve as a theoretical base for possible experimental probing of quantum effects in stick-slip motion. Moving cold atoms and ions in optical lattices and time-resolved Landau-Zenner tunneling in periodic potentials, already achieved in the laboratory
Gangloff et al. 2015
;
Counts et al. 2017
;
Bonvin et al. 2024
;
Zenesini et al. 2009
, may be a useful platform to demonstrate the reduction of friction in the quantum mechanical regime.

Then the total short-range overlap interaction between the moving particle and one-dimensional chain is given as

\displaystyle V_{sr}(x)\;

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{n=-\infty}^{n=+\infty}Ae^{-\frac{\sqrt{d^{2}+(x-na)^{2}}}{d_{c}}}\approx Ae^{-\frac{d}{d_{c}}}\sum\limits_{n=-\infty}^{n=+\infty}e^{-\frac{a^{2}}{2dd_{c}}\left(\frac{x}{a}-n\right)^{2}}=Ae^{-\frac{d}{d_{c}}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi dd_{c}}{a^{2}}}\vartheta_{3}\left(\frac{\pi x}{a},e^{-\frac{2\pi^{2}dd_{c}}{a^{2}}}\right),

(S-1)

where
\vartheta_{3}(u,q)=1+2\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}q^{n^{2}}\cos\left(2nu\right)
is the third theta function. Here we consider the scenario when
d\gg a
; thus, above potential becomes cosine one

\displaystyle V_{sr}(x)\approx A\sqrt{\frac{2\pi dd_{c}}{a^{2}}}e^{-\frac{d}{d_{c}}}\left[1+2e^{-\frac{2\pi^{2}dd_{c}}{a^{2}}}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)\right].

(S-2)

S-VI.2
Long-range vdW interaction

For simplicity, we consider only
C_{6}
vdW interaction between the moving particle and a particle inside one-dimensional lattice; thus, the total long-range vdW interaction between the moving particle and one-dimensional chain is given as

\displaystyle V_{lr}(x)\;

\displaystyle=-\sum\limits_{n=-\infty}^{n=+\infty}\dfrac{C_{6}}{\left[d^{2}+\left(x-na\right)^{2}\right]^{3}}=\dfrac{C_{6}}{d^{6}}\sum\limits_{n=-\infty}^{n=+\infty}\dfrac{1}{\left[1+\dfrac{a^{2}}{d^{2}}\left(\dfrac{x}{a}-n\right)^{2}\right]^{3}}

(S-3)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{C_{6}}{d^{6}}\times\left\{\frac{3\pi d}{8a}\dfrac{\sinh\left(\frac{2\pi d}{a}\right)}{\cosh\left(\frac{2\pi d}{a}\right)-\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)}+\frac{3\pi^{2}d^{2}}{4a^{2}}\dfrac{\cosh\left(\frac{2\pi d}{a}\right)\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)-1}{\left[\cosh\left(\frac{2\pi d}{a}\right)-\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)\right]^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\left.+\frac{\pi^{3}d^{3}}{2a^{3}}\dfrac{\sinh\left(\frac{2\pi d}{a}\right)\left[\cosh\left(\frac{2\pi d}{a}\right)\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)+\cos^{2}\left(\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)-2\right]}{\left[\cosh\left(\frac{2\pi d}{a}\right)-\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)\right]^{3}}\right\}.

When the distance between the moving particle and one-dimensional chain is large
d\gg a
, the long-range vdW interaction approximately becomes a cosine potential

V_{lr}(x)\approx-\frac{3\pi C_{6}}{8d^{5}a}\left[1+2e^{-\frac{2\pi d}{a}}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)\right].

(S-4)

Combining the repulsive short-range overlap and attractive long-range vdW interaction given in Equations (
S-2
) and (
S-4
), the interaction arising from one-dimensional chain of atoms is approximately given as

\displaystyle V(x)=V_{sr}(x)+V_{lr}(x)\approx\left[A\sqrt{\frac{2\pi dd_{c}}{a^{2}}}e^{-\frac{d}{d_{c}}}-\frac{3\pi C_{6}}{8d^{5}a}\right]+2\left[A\sqrt{\frac{2\pi dd_{c}}{a^{2}}}e^{-\frac{d}{d_{c}}-\frac{2\pi^{2}dd_{c}}{a^{2}}}-\frac{3\pi C_{6}}{8d^{5}a}e^{-\frac{2\pi d}{a}}\right]\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right).

(S-5)

Denote
U_{0}(d)=4\left[A\sqrt{\frac{2\pi dd_{c}}{a^{2}}}e^{-\frac{d}{d_{c}}-\frac{2\pi^{2}dd_{c}}{a^{2}}}-\frac{3\pi C_{6}}{8d^{5}a}e^{-\frac{2\pi d}{a}}\right]
and
\Delta_{0}(d)=\left[A\sqrt{\frac{2\pi dd_{c}}{a^{2}}}e^{-\frac{d}{d_{c}}}-\frac{3\pi C_{6}}{8d^{5}a}\right]-\frac{1}{2}U_{0}(d)
, then the resulting potential by one-dimensional chain of atom can be represented in a more compact from

\displaystyle V(x)=\Delta_{0}(d)+\frac{U_{0}(d)}{2}+\frac{U_{0}(d)}{2}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right)=\Delta_{0}(d)+U_{0}(d)\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi x}{a}\right).

(S-6)

S-VII
Representation in moving harmonic oscillator basis set of wavefunctions

S-VII.1
Basis set of moving harmonic oscillator

To perform any calculations in this study, we need to choose a basis set of wavefunctions where any arbitrary state can be expressed as a superposition of these basis wavefunctions. In our model’s Hamiltonian
H_{S}(\overline{t})
, the periodic potential has a finite height, while a moving harmonic trap potential has an infinite height. Thus two most natural choice of basis set are the eigenstates of the periodic potential and the eigenstates of the harmonic oscillator. Both choices have pros and cons. Indeed, since the periodic potential is time independent, its eigenstates are time independent and orthonormalized. However, the harmonic trap potential is more dominant when the particle is far from the center of the trap and consequently, the particle wavefunction will tend to localized inside the harmonic trap. This localization property cannot be satisfied by eigenstates of periodic potential because these eigenstates obey Bloch theorem. One will need to Wannierized these eigenstates in order to control the convergence of the calculations; for an exeample, see Ref.
Zanca et al. 2018
. The eigenstates of the harmonic oscillator, on the other hand, will automatically obey the required localization property. But since the harmonic trap is moving, we need to pick the eigenstates of a moving harmonic oscillator (The upper index
{...}^{(0)}
stands for solutions without periodic potential i.e
u_{0}=0
.):

E_{n}^{(0)}=n+\frac{1}{2},\quad\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>=\varphi_{0}^{HO}(\overline{x}-\overline{v}\overline{t})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi}}}e^{-\frac{(\overline{x}-\overline{v}\overline{t})^{2}}{2}}H_{n}\left(\overline{x}-\overline{v}\overline{t}\right),

(S-7)

where
H_{n}
is
n
-th Hermite polynomials
Sakurai and Napolitano 2020
. Noticeably, this basis set of wavefunctions relates to the basis set of wavefunctions of the static harmonic oscillator
\left|n^{(0)}(0)\right>=\varphi_{0}^{HO}(\overline{x})
by the following expansions

\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>=\sum\limits_{p=0}^{+\infty}c_{np}^{(0)}(\overline{t})\left|n^{(0)}(0)\right>,\quad\left|n^{(0)}(0)\right>=\sum\limits_{p=0}^{+\infty}c_{pn}^{(0)*}(\overline{t})\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>,

(S-8)

where
c_{np}^{(0)}(\overline{t})=e^{-\frac{\overline{v}^{2}\overline{t}^{2}}{4}}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\min(n,p)}\frac{(-1)^{n-m}}{m!(n-m)!(p-m)!}\sqrt{\frac{n!p!}{2^{n+p-2m}}}\left(\overline{v}\overline{t}\right)^{n+p-2m}
.

S-VII.2
Matrix element of the periodic potential

Next, we need to evaluate the matrix elements of the periodic potential in the representation of the moving harmonic oscillator basis set. To do so, there are two necessary integrals need to be given
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2014

\displaystyle I_{pp^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{p+p^{\prime}}p!p^{\prime}!\sqrt{\pi}}}\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\overline{x}^{2}}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right)H_{p}(\overline{x})H_{p^{\prime}}(\overline{x})

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{when }|p-p^{\prime}|\text{ is odd}\\
\frac{(-1)^{\frac{|p-p^{\prime}|}{2}}}{\sqrt{2^{|p-p^{\prime}|+2}}}\sqrt{\frac{\min(p,p^{\prime})!}{\max(p,p^{\prime})!}}\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\right)^{|p-p^{\prime}|}e^{-\frac{1}{4}(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a})^{2}}\mathcal{L}_{\min(p,p^{\prime})}^{|p-p^{\prime}|}\left[\frac{1}{2}(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a})^{2}\right]&\text{when }|p-p^{\prime}|\text{ is even}\end{array}\right..

and

\displaystyle J_{pp^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{p+p^{\prime}}p!p^{\prime}!\sqrt{\pi}}}\int\limits_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\overline{x}^{2}}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right)H_{p}(\overline{x})H_{p^{\prime}}(\overline{x})

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{(-1)^{\frac{|p-p^{\prime}|-1}{2}}}{\sqrt{2^{|p-p^{\prime}|+2}}}\sqrt{\frac{\min(p,p^{\prime})!}{\max(p,p^{\prime})!}}\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\right)^{|p-p^{\prime}|}e^{-\frac{1}{4}(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a})^{2}}\mathcal{L}_{\min(p,p^{\prime})}^{|p-p^{\prime}|}\left[\frac{1}{2}(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a})^{2}\right]&\text{when }|p-p^{\prime}|\text{ is odd}\\
0&\text{when }|p-p^{\prime}|\text{ is even}\end{array}\right..

Now, rewriting the periodic potential as

\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right)-\frac{1}{2}=-\frac{1}{2}u_{0}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\left[\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{v}\overline{t}\right)\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\left(\overline{x}-\overline{v}\overline{t}\right)\right)-\sin\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{v}\overline{t}\right)\sin\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\left(\overline{x}-\overline{v}\overline{t}\right)\right)\right],

(S-15)

then using (
S-7
) to obtain matrix elements of
\hat{V}_{cos}
:

\mathcal{V}_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})=-\left[\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{v}\overline{t}\right)I_{nn^{\prime}}-\sin\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{v}\overline{t}\right)J_{nn^{\prime}}\right]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}+\sin\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{v}\overline{t}\right)J_{nn^{\prime}}&\text{when }|n-n^{\prime}|\text{ is odd}\\
-\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{v}\overline{t}\right)I_{nn^{\prime}}&\text{when }|n-n^{\prime}|\text{ is even}\end{array}\right.,

(S-16)

where
I_{nn^{\prime}}
and
J_{nn^{\prime}}
are explicitly given in Equations (
S-VII.2
) and (
S-VII.2
). Based on known matrix elements
\mathcal{V}_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})
, the matrix representation of the original Hamiltonian is finally obtained

\displaystyle(H_{S})_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})=\left<n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right|\hat{H}_{S}(t)\left|n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right>=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}u_{0}\right)\delta_{n,n^{\prime}}+u_{0}\mathcal{V}_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t}).

(S-17)

S-VIII
Quasi-static eigenvalues and quasi-static eigenstates of our model

Now we are looking for the solution of the quasi-static a.k.a time-independent Schrödinger equation where time
t
plays a role as a parameter

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{S}(t)\left|n(\overline{t}\right>=E_{n}(t)\left|n(\overline{t})\right>.

(S-18)

Utilizing
\varphi_{n}^{(0)}(\overline{x},\overline{t})
as basic set of wavefunctions, our eigenstates can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\left<\overline{x}\middle|n(\overline{t})\right>=\psi_{n}(\overline{x},\overline{t})=\sum\limits_{p^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}c_{n,p^{\prime}}(\overline{t})\varphi_{p^{\prime}}^{(0)}(\overline{x},\overline{t}),\quad\sum\limits_{p^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\left|c_{n,p^{\prime}}(\overline{t})\right|^{2}=1.

(S-19)

Substituting (
S-19
) into (
S-18
), then multiplying both sides with
\varphi_{p}^{(0)}(\overline{x},\overline{t})

\overline{x}
from

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{p^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}(H_{S})_{p,p^{\prime}}(\overline{t})c_{n,p^{\prime}}(\overline{t})=E_{n}(\overline{t})c_{n,p}(\overline{t}),

S-IX
Quantum dynamical evolution without environment

Dynamical evolution of a closed system is governed by the following quantum Liouville–von Neumann equation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\left[\hat{H}_{S}(t),\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)\right],

(S-21)

where density matrix operator
\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)
captures all dynamical information about our quantum system. Noticeably that we are working in dimensionless Hamiltonian by setting
\hbar=1
and replacing
t
by
\overline{t}
; therefore, dimensionless form of quantum Liouville–von Neumann equation is

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\overline{t}}\hat{\rho}_{S}(\overline{t})=-i\left[\hat{H}_{S}(\overline{t}),\hat{\rho}_{S}(\overline{t})\right],

(S-22)

In the basis
\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>
, the matrix representation of density matrix operator
\hat{\rho}_{S}(\overline{t})
is given as

\displaystyle\left(\rho_{S}\right)_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})=\left<n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right|\hat{\rho}_{S}(\overline{t})\left|n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right>,

(S-23)

while the time derivative operator
i\dfrac{d}{d\overline{t}}
is represented by the following matrix

\displaystyle\left(\sigma_{t}\right)_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})=i\left<n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right|\left(\dfrac{d}{d\overline{t}}\left|n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\right).

(S-24)

We will determine the explicit expression of matrix
\left(\sigma_{t}\right)_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})
later. According to the orthonormal of the chosen basis
\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>
, we can easily show that

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{d\overline{t}}\left<n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\middle|n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right>=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\left<n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right|\left(\dfrac{d}{d\overline{t}}\left|n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\right)+\left(\dfrac{d}{d\overline{t}}\left<n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right|\right)\left|n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right>=0

(S-25)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow i\left(\dfrac{d}{d\overline{t}}\left<n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right|\right)\left|n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right>=-\left(\sigma_{t}\right)_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t}).

From (
S-22
) we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{d\overline{t}}\left\{\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\left(\rho_{S}\right)_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left<n^{\prime(0)}\right|\right\}=-i\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}{\left[H_{S}(\overline{t}),\rho_{S}(\overline{t})\right]_{n,n^{\prime}}\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left<n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right|}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\dfrac{d\left(\rho_{S}\right)_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})}{d\overline{t}}\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left<n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right|+\sum_{n^{\prime\prime},n^{\prime}}\left(\rho_{S}\right)_{n^{\prime\prime},n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})\left(\dfrac{d}{d\overline{t}}\left|n^{\prime\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\right)\left<n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right|

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\sum_{n,n^{\prime\prime}}\left(\rho_{S}\right)_{n,n^{\prime\prime}}(\overline{t})\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left(\dfrac{d}{d\overline{t}}\left<n^{\prime\prime(0)}\right|\right)=-i\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}{\left[H_{S}(\overline{t}),\rho_{S}(\overline{t})\right]_{n,n^{\prime}}\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left<n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right|}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\dfrac{d\left(\rho_{S}\right)_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})}{d\overline{t}}\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left<n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right|+\sum_{n,n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}\left(\rho_{S}\right)_{n^{\prime\prime},n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})\left[\left<n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right|\left(\dfrac{d}{d\overline{t}}\left|n^{\prime\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\right)\right]\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left<n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right|

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\left(\rho_{S}\right)_{n,n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}(\overline{t})\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left[\left(\dfrac{d}{d\overline{t}}\left<n^{\prime\prime(0)}\right|\right)\left|n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\right]\left<n^{\prime(0)}\right|=-i\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}{\left[H_{S}(\overline{t}),\rho_{S}(\overline{t})\right]_{n,n^{\prime}}\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left<n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right|}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\dfrac{d\left(\rho_{S}\right)_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})}{d\overline{t}}\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left<n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right|-i\sum_{n,n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}\left(\sigma_{t}\right)_{n,n^{\prime\prime}}(\overline{t})\left(\rho_{S}\right)_{n^{\prime\prime},n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left<n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right|

\displaystyle\quad\quad+i\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\left(\rho_{S}\right)_{n,n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}(\overline{t})\left(\sigma_{t}\right)_{n^{\prime\prime},n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left<n^{\prime(0)}\right|=-i\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}{\left[H_{S}(\overline{t}),\rho_{S}(\overline{t})\right]_{n,n^{\prime}}\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left<n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right|}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\dfrac{d\left(\rho_{S}\right)_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})}{d\overline{t}}\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left<n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right|-i\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\left[\sigma_{t}(\overline{t}),\rho_{S}(\overline{t})\right]_{n,n^{\prime}}\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left<n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\;=-i\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}{\left[H_{S}(\overline{t}),\rho_{S}(\overline{t})\right]_{n,n^{\prime}}\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left<n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right|}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\dfrac{d\left(\rho_{S}\right)_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})}{d\overline{t}}\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left<n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right|=-i\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}{\left[H_{S}(\overline{t})-\sigma_{t}(\overline{t}),\rho_{S}(\overline{t})\right]_{n,n^{\prime}}\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\left<n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right|}.

(S-26)

or as the first-order ordinary differential equation of matrix:

\dfrac{d}{d\overline{t}}\rho_{S}(\overline{t})=-i\left[H_{S}(\overline{t})-\sigma_{t}(\overline{t}),\rho_{S}(\overline{t})\right].

(S-27)

S-IX.1
Explicit matrix elements
\left(\sigma_{t}\right)_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})
in the basis set of moving harmonic oscillator

First of all, we have to notice that if denote
\overline{x}^{\prime}=\overline{x}-\overline{v}\overline{t}
then

\displaystyle i\dfrac{d}{d\overline{t}}\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>=i\dfrac{d}{d\overline{t}}\varphi_{n}^{HO}(\overline{x}-\overline{v}\overline{t})=\overline{v}\left(-i\dfrac{d}{d\overline{x}^{\prime}}\varphi_{n}^{HO}(\overline{x}^{\prime})\right)=\overline{v}\left(\hat{\overline{p}}^{\prime}\varphi_{n}^{HO}(\overline{x}^{\prime})\right).

(S-28)

The action of momentum operator on an eigenstate of a harmonic oscillator can be found in any quantum mechanics textbook
Sakurai and Napolitano 2020

\hat{\overline{p}}^{\prime}\varphi_{n}^{HO}(\overline{x}^{\prime})=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\sqrt{n+1}\varphi_{n+1}^{HO}(\overline{x}^{\prime})-\sqrt{n}\varphi_{n-1}^{HO}(\overline{x}^{\prime})\right]=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\sqrt{n+1}\varphi_{n+1}^{(0)}(\overline{x},\overline{t})-\sqrt{n}\varphi_{n-1}^{(0)}(\overline{x},\overline{t})\right],

(S-29)

thus, Equation (
S-28
) becomes

\displaystyle i\dfrac{d}{d\overline{t}}\varphi_{n}^{(0)}(\overline{x},\overline{t})=\frac{i\;\overline{v}}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\sqrt{n+1}\varphi_{n+1}^{(0)}(\overline{x},\overline{t})-\sqrt{n}\varphi_{n-1}^{(0)}(\overline{x},\overline{t})\right],

(S-30)

or

\displaystyle i\dfrac{d}{d\overline{t}}\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>=\frac{i\;\overline{v}}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\sqrt{n+1}\left|(n+1)^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>-\sqrt{n}\left|(n-1)^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>\right].

(S-31)

Plugging (
S-31
) in (
S-24
), we finally find matrix elements
\left(\sigma_{t}\right)_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})
explicitly as

\displaystyle\left(\sigma_{t}\right)_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})=\frac{i\;\overline{v}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{n^{\prime}+1}\delta_{n,n^{\prime}+1}-\sqrt{n^{\prime}}\delta_{n,n^{\prime}-1}\right)=\frac{i\;\overline{v}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{n^{\prime}+1}\delta_{n,n^{\prime}+1}-\sqrt{n+1}\delta_{n+1,n^{\prime}}\right)\equiv\left(\sigma_{t}\right)_{n,n^{\prime}}.

(S-32)

Hence
\sigma_{t}(\overline{t})
is a time-independent Hermitian tridiagonal matrix with zero main diagonal

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}=\sigma_{t}^{\dagger}=\frac{\overline{v}}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\sqrt{1}&0&0&0&\ldots\\
i\sqrt{1}&0&-i\sqrt{2}&0&0&\ldots\\
0&i\sqrt{2}&0&-i\sqrt{3}&0&\ldots\\
0&0&i\sqrt{3}&0&-i\sqrt{4}&\ldots\\
0&0&0&i\sqrt{4}&0&\ldots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}

(S-33)

S-IX.2
Forth order Runge-Kutta solver for (
S-27
)

To solve the first-order ordinary differential equation of matrix (
S-27
), we utilize a forth order Runge-Kutta method (RK4) by dividing time domain from
\overline{t}=0
to
\overline{t}=\overline{t}_{max}
by even grid spacing by
\Delta\overline{t}
. Then density matrix at
\overline{t}+\Delta\overline{t}
is obtained by

\displaystyle\rho_{S}\left(\overline{t}+\Delta{\overline{t}}\right)=\rho_{S}\left(\overline{t}\right)+\frac{1}{6}\left(\kappa_{1}+2\kappa_{2}+2\kappa_{3}+\kappa_{4}\right)\Delta\overline{t},

(S-34)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\kappa_{1}=&-i\left[H_{S}(\overline{t})-\sigma_{t}(\overline{t}),\rho_{S}(\overline{t})\right]\\
\kappa_{2}=&-i\left[H_{S}(\overline{t}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\overline{t})-\sigma_{t}(\overline{t}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\overline{t}),\rho_{S}(\overline{t})+\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{1}\Delta\overline{t}\right]\\
\kappa_{3}=&-i\left[H_{S}(\overline{t}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\overline{t})-\sigma_{t}(\overline{t}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\overline{t}),\rho_{S}(\overline{t})+\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{2}\Delta\overline{t}\right]\\
\kappa_{4}=&-i\left[H_{S}(\overline{t}+\Delta\overline{t})-\sigma_{t}(\overline{t}+\Delta\overline{t}),\rho_{S}(\overline{t})+\kappa_{3}\Delta\overline{t}\right]\end{array}\right..

From a given initial density matrix
\rho_{S}(0)
, (
S-34

(S-86)

where

S-XI
Classical dynamical evolution with enviroment: the case of Caldeira-Leggett model

In order to emphasize on quantum effects in our system, we also examine the classical motion whose dynamics is governed by stochastic Newton second law. Starting from the Hamiltonian (10) in the manuscript, the Hamilton’s equations for the whole system including harmonic bath are given as
Weiss 2012

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\dfrac{d^{2}\overline{x}}{d\overline{t}^{2}}+\left(\overline{x}-\overline{v}\overline{t}\right)+\dfrac{u_{0}\pi\ell}{a}\sin\left(\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right)&=&f_{bath}(\overline{x},\overline{t}),\\
\dfrac{d^{2}x_{i}}{d\overline{t}^{2}}+\omega_{i}^{2}x_{i}&=&\dfrac{c_{i}}{m_{i}}\sin\left(\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right),\end{array}\right.,

where the force coming from the harmonic bath is

\displaystyle f_{bath}(\overline{x},\overline{t})=\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\left(\sum\limits_{i}x_{i}c_{i}\right)\cos\left(\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right)-\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\left(\sum\limits_{i}\dfrac{c_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}}\right)\sin\left(\dfrac{4\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right).

(S-100)

Now we need to solve for the dynamical evolution of the bath degrees of freedom
x_{i}
in order to determine the bath force. Because the Green function of the harmonic bath is known as
G(\overline{t},\overline{t}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\omega_{i}}\Theta(\overline{t}-\overline{t}^{\prime})\sin\left[\omega_{i}(\overline{t}-\overline{t}^{\prime})\right]
, the dynamical equation for the degree of freedom of the heat bath can be easily solved

\displaystyle x_{i}(\overline{t})=x_{i}^{homo}(\overline{t})+\dfrac{c_{i}}{m_{i}\omega_{i}}\int\limits_{0}^{\overline{t}}\sin\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t}^{\prime})\right]\sin\left[\omega_{i}(\overline{t}-\overline{t}^{\prime})\right]d\overline{t}^{\prime}

(S-101)

where the homogeneous solution is determined via initial positions and velocities of bath oscillators
x_{i}(0),v_{i}(0)
as
x_{i}^{homo}(\overline{t})=x_{i}(0)\cos(\omega_{i}\overline{t})+\dfrac{v_{i}(0)}{\omega_{i}}\sin(\omega_{i}\overline{t})
. Integrating by part the second term of Eq. (
S-101
) gives us

\displaystyle x_{i}(\overline{t})=x_{i}^{homo}(\overline{t})

\displaystyle+\dfrac{c_{i}}{m_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}}\sin\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t})\right]-\dfrac{c_{i}}{m_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}}\cos\left(\omega_{i}\overline{t}\right)\sin\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(0)\right]

(S-102)

\displaystyle-\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\dfrac{c_{i}}{m_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\overline{t}}\cos\left[\omega_{i}(\overline{t}-\overline{t}^{\prime})\right]\cos\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t}^{\prime})\right]\dfrac{d\overline{x}(\overline{t}^{\prime})}{d\overline{t}^{\prime}}d\overline{t}^{\prime}.

As
x_{i}(\overline{t})
is found, the force coming from harmonic bath is simplified as

\displaystyle f_{bath}\left[\overline{x}(\overline{t}),\overline{t}\right]=

\displaystyle\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\left\{\sum\limits_{i}c_{i}\left[x_{i}^{homo}(\overline{t})-\sum\limits_{i}\dfrac{c_{i}}{m_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}}\sin\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(0)\right]\cos\left(\omega_{i}\overline{t}\right)\right]\right\}\cos\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t})\right]

(S-103)

\displaystyle-\dfrac{4\pi^{2}\ell^{2}}{a^{2}}\cos\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t})\right]\int\limits_{0}^{\overline{t}}\left[\sum\limits_{i}\dfrac{c_{i}^{2}}{m_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}}\cos\left[\omega_{i}(\overline{t}-\overline{t}^{\prime})\right]\right]\cos\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t}^{\prime})\right]\dfrac{d\overline{x}(\overline{t}^{\prime})}{d\overline{t}^{\prime}}d\overline{t}^{\prime}.

The summation over bath degrees of freedom appeared in the second term can be rewritten in term of spectral function
J(\omega)=\sum\limits_{i}\frac{c_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}\omega_{i}}\delta\left(\omega-\omega_{i}\right)=2\alpha\omega e^{-|\omega|/\omega_{c}}
. Such terms are known as classical damping factor
Weiss 2012
:

\displaystyle\overline{\gamma}(\overline{t})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i}\dfrac{c_{i}^{2}}{m_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}}\cos\left(\omega_{i}\overline{t}\right)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\sum\limits_{i}\dfrac{2\cos\left(\omega_{i}\overline{t}\right)}{\omega_{i}}\dfrac{c_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}\omega_{i}}\delta\left(\omega-\omega_{i}\right)d\omega

(S-104)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\dfrac{2\cos\left(\omega\overline{t}\right)}{\omega}\sum\limits_{i}\dfrac{c_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}\omega_{i}}\delta\left(\omega-\omega_{i}\right)d\omega=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\dfrac{2\cos\left(\omega\overline{t}\right)}{\omega}J(\omega)d\omega=\dfrac{4\alpha\omega_{c}}{1+\omega_{c}^{2}\overline{t}^{2}}\xrightarrow{\omega_{c}\to\infty}4\pi\alpha\delta(\overline{t}).

Noticeably, the second term of the bath force depends on the velocity of the particle
d\overline{x}/d\overline{t}
and does not depends on the initial conditions of the bath degrees of freedom, therefore, can be interpreted as environmental viscous force
Weiss 2012
. On the other hand, the first term of the bath force, which is in
\left\{...\right\}
bracket depends on the initial conditions of both bath degrees of freedom
x_{i}(0),v_{i}(0)
and of particle
\overline{x}(0)
which will be pointed out as random force due to dissipation
Weiss 2012
. Thus, the bath force (
S-103
) is separated into viscous and random force
f_{bath}\left[\overline{x}(\overline{t}),\overline{t}\right]=f_{vis}\left[\overline{x},\dfrac{d\overline{x}}{d\overline{t}}\right]+f_{ran}\left(\overline{t}\right)
where

\displaystyle f_{vis}\left[\overline{x},\dfrac{d\overline{x}}{d\overline{t}}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{4\pi^{2}\ell^{2}}{a^{2}}\cos\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t})\right]\int\limits_{0}^{\overline{t}}\overline{\gamma}(\overline{t}-\overline{t}^{\prime})\cos\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t}^{\prime})\right]\dfrac{d\overline{x}(\overline{t}^{\prime})}{d\overline{t}^{\prime}}d\overline{t}^{\prime}=-\frac{8\alpha\pi^{3}\ell^{2}}{a^{2}}\cos^{2}\left(\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right)\dfrac{d\overline{x}}{d\overline{t}},

(S-105)

\displaystyle f_{ran}\left(\overline{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\left\{\sum\limits_{i}c_{i}\left[x_{i}^{homo}(\overline{t})-\sum\limits_{i}\dfrac{c_{i}}{m_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}}\sin\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(0)\right]\cos\left(\omega_{i}\overline{t}\right)\right]\right\}\cos\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t})\right].

(S-106)

Unlike viscous force, the random force depends on initial configuration of the bath degrees of freedom
x_{i}(0),v_{i}(0)
. Statistically, if the bath is kept at thermal equilibrium state obeying canonical Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution
Weiss 2012

\rho_{bath}=Z^{-1}\exp{\left\{-k_{B}^{-1}T^{-1}\sum_{i}\left[\frac{m_{i}v_{i}^{2}(0)}{2}+\frac{m_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}}{2}\left(x_{i}(0)-\frac{c_{i}}{m_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(0)\right)\right)\right]\right\}},

(S-107)

we can show that the statistics of random force is governed by the following relation
Weiss 2012

\displaystyle\left<f_{ran}(\overline{t})\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(S-108)

\displaystyle\left<f_{ran}(\overline{t})f_{ran}(\overline{t}^{\prime})\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{B}T\frac{4\pi^{2}\ell^{2}}{a^{2}}\cos\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t})\right]\cos\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t}^{\prime})\right]\sum_{i}\frac{c_{i}^{2}}{m_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}}\cos(\omega_{i}\overline{t})\cos(\omega_{i}\overline{t}^{\prime})

(S-109)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{B}T\frac{4\pi^{2}\ell^{2}}{a^{2}}\cos\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t})\right]\cos\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t}^{\prime})\right]\sum_{i}\frac{c_{i}^{2}}{m_{i}\omega_{i}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\left[\cos(\omega_{i}(\overline{t}-\overline{t}^{\prime}))+\cos(\omega_{i}(\overline{t}+\overline{t}^{\prime}))\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{B}T\frac{2\pi^{2}\ell^{2}}{a^{2}}\cos\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t})\right]\cos\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t}^{\prime})\right]\left[\gamma(\overline{t}-\overline{t}^{\prime})+\gamma(\overline{t}+\overline{t}^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{B}T\dfrac{8\alpha\pi^{3}\ell^{2}}{a^{3}}\cos^{2}\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t})\right]\delta(\overline{t}-\overline{t}^{\prime}).

This indicates that the random force obeys fluctuation-dissipation theorem because it originally comes from the thermal fluctuation of the harmonic bath
Rytov et al. 1989
.

Finally the motion of the driven particle is governed by the following stochastic Newton second law

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\dfrac{d\overline{x}}{d\overline{t}}&=&\dot{\overline{x}}\\
\dfrac{d\dot{\overline{x}}}{d\overline{t}}&=&-\left(\overline{x}-\overline{v}\overline{t}\right)-\dfrac{u_{0}\pi\ell}{a}\sin\left(\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right)-\frac{8\alpha\pi^{3}\ell^{2}}{a^{2}}\cos^{2}\left(\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right)\dot{\overline{x}}+f_{ran}(\overline{t}),\end{array}\right.,

where the random force coming from thermal fluctuation is considered as Wiener process governed by the following probability density function

\displaystyle P\left[f_{ran}\Delta\overline{t}\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{16k_{B}T\alpha\pi^{4}\ell^{3}}{a^{3}}\cos^{2}\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t})\right]\Delta\overline{t}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\left(f_{ran}\Delta\overline{t}\right)^{2}}{\frac{16k_{B}T\alpha\pi^{3}\ell^{3}}{a^{3}}\cos^{2}\left[\dfrac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}(\overline{t})\right]\Delta\overline{t}}\right\}.

(S-113)

Above equation can be numerical solved by forth-order Runge-Kutta method as given in Ref.
Wilkie and Çetinbaş 2005
.

S-XII
Geometry phase

As generalization from the concept of geometric phase introduced by Pancharatnam in classical optics and later by Berry in quantum cyclic evolution, the geometric phase of a mixed state quantum system under nonunitary evolution has been developed by Tong et al
Tong et al. 2004
.

If density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{S}(\overline{t})
can be decomposed into eigenvectors
\left|\phi_{k}(\overline{t})\right>
and eigenvalues
\xi_{k}(\overline{t})
as

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{S}(\overline{t})=\sum_{k}\xi_{k}(\overline{t})\left|\phi_{k}(\overline{t})\right>\left<\phi_{k}(\overline{t})\right|,

(S-114)

the geometry phase is given as

\displaystyle\gamma_{phase}=\gamma(T),

(S-115)

where
T
is the period of evolution without dissipation while
\gamma(\overline{t})
is the following function

\displaystyle\gamma(\overline{t})=\textrm{arg}\left[\sum_{k}\sqrt{\xi_{k}(0)\xi_{k}(\overline{t})}\left<\phi_{k}(0)\right.\left|\phi_{k}(\overline{t})\right>\exp{\left(-\int_{0}^{\overline{t}}\left<\phi_{k}(\overline{t})\middle|\frac{d\phi_{k}}{d\overline{t}}(\overline{t})\right>d\overline{t}\right)}\right].

(S-116)

In the basis
\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>
, the matrix representation of density matrix operator
\hat{\rho}_{S}
is given as (
S-23
). Then Eq. (
S-114
) reads

\displaystyle\left(\rho_{S}\right)_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})=\sum_{k}P_{n,k}(\overline{t})\xi_{k}(\overline{t})P^{\dagger}_{k,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})

(S-117)

where
P_{n,k}(\overline{t})
is an unitary matrix

\displaystyle P_{n,k}(\overline{t})=\left<n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\middle|\phi_{k}(\overline{t})\right>\Leftrightarrow\left|\phi_{k}(\overline{t})\right>=\sum_{n}P_{n,k}(\overline{t})\left|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>.

(S-118)

Then the phase function is explicitly presented as

\displaystyle\gamma(\overline{t})=\textrm{arg}\left[\sum_{k}\sqrt{\xi_{k}(0)\xi_{k}(\overline{t})}\left[P^{\dagger}(0)c(\overline{t})P(\overline{t})\right]_{kk}\exp{\left(-\int_{0}^{\overline{t}}\left[P^{\dagger}(\overline{t})\frac{dP}{d\overline{t}}(\overline{t})-iP^{\dagger}(\overline{t})\sigma_{t}(\overline{t})P(\overline{t})\right]_{kk}d\overline{t}\right)}\right].

(S-119)

Here we use the fact that
\left<n^{\prime(0)}(0)\middle|n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\right>=c^{(0)}_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})
and
\left<n^{(0)}(\overline{t})\middle|\frac{d}{d\overline{t}}n^{\prime(0)}(\overline{t})\right>=-i\left(\sigma_{t}\right)_{n,n^{\prime}}(\overline{t})
.

S-XIII
Tunneling and slipping of quantum quasi-static eigenstates and classical trajectory

According to classical mechanics, if driving the particle with a very small driving velocity
\overline{v}
, our particle keeps staying at the first local minimum of the potential surface i.e.
\overline{x}(\overline{t})\approx\overline{x}_{min,1}(\overline{t})
. Since the minimum obeys
\frac{\partial V(\overline{x},\overline{t})}{\partial\overline{t}}=0
, we find that

\overline{x}-\overline{v}\overline{t}+\frac{u_{0}}{2}\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\right)\sin\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right)\approx 0\Leftrightarrow F_{L}\approx\frac{\pi U_{0}}{a}\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\right)^{2}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\overline{x}\right)\leq\frac{\pi U_{0}}{a},

(S-120)

which yields to the maximal lateral force at classical limit
(F_{L})_{max}=\frac{\pi U_{0}}{a}
as given in Refs.
Socoliuc et al. 2004
;
Gnecco et al. 2012
. Correspondingly, the lateral force reaches its maximum when
\overline{x}=\frac{a}{4\ell}
from which we can determine the slipping time

\frac{a}{4\ell}-\overline{v}\overline{t}_{slip,cl}+\frac{u_{0}}{2}\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\right)\approx 0\Rightarrow\frac{t_{slip,cl}}{T}\approx\frac{1}{4}+\frac{u_{0}}{4\pi}\left(\frac{2\pi\ell}{a}\right)^{2}\approx\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\eta}{2\pi}.

(S-121)

Since above examination based on the assumption that our particle keeps staying at the first local minimum of the potential surface i.e.
\overline{x}(\overline{t})\approx\overline{x}_{min,1}(\overline{t})
, it is only valid for small enough driving velocity
\overline{v}
.

t_{slip,n}
. For each quasi-static eigenstate
\left|n(\overline{t})\right>
, we evaluate the statistical average of relative displacement between the particle and the center of the harmonic trap
\left<\overline{x}_{n}\right>-\overline{v}\overline{t}
within the first period. Results for the first 5 eigenstates
n=0,1,2,3,4
are given in Figs.
S-3
(a,b,c,d,e). The slipping time of each quasi-static eigenstate

is then determined as the moment when
\left<\overline{x}_{n}\right>-\overline{v}\overline{t}
reaches its minimum (circled) and
t_{slip,n}
should be in the period when the potential surface forms a double-well shape (
0.32T\leq t\leq 0.68T
). Our calculation finds that
t_{slip,n=0}=0.485T
(Fig.
S-3
(a,g)),
t_{slip,n=1}=0.519T
(Fig.
S-3
(b,i)),
t_{slip,n=2}=0.578T
(Fig.
S-3
(c,k)) while there are no slipping time corresponding to the higher quasi-static eigenstates. Furthermore, Figs.
