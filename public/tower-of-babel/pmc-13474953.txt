Exploring nurses’ experiences of the presence of a wound care specialist: a qualitative study
Authors: Ziba Farzi, Saba Farzi, Nahid Madhani, Masoud Sharifian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWound care is a critical aspect of nursing practice, yet nurses often face challenges related to limited knowledge, lack of structured training, and insufficient organizational support. The integration of specialist wound care nurses has been suggested to enhance clinical outcomes, continuity of care, and professional competence among nursing staff. This study aimed to explore nurses’ experiences with a specialist wound care nurse in a hospital setting and to identify the impact of this role on clinical practice and team performance.MethodsA qualitative descriptive–exploratory study was conducted in 2024–2025 at a specialized surgical and trauma care center in Iran. Ten participants, including staff nurses, head nurses, a specialist wound care nurse, and a nursing instructor, were recruited through purposeful sampling with maximum variation. Data were collected via semi-structured interviews (average duration: 35 min) and field notes, and analyzed using Graneheim and Lundman’s qualitative content analysis framework. Trustworthiness was ensured through credibility, dependability, transferability, and confirmability strategies.ResultsFour main categories emerged: Specialized Wound Care, Continuity of Wound Care, Empowerment of Nurses, and Challenges and Strategies for Improvement. Specialist nurses enhanced evidence-based decision-making, improved care quality, accelerated wound healing, and reduced complications. They facilitated continuous, coordinated care across wards and empowered other nurses through clinical education and experiential learning. Barriers included staff shortages, undefined organizational positions, and insufficient managerial support, highlighting the need for structural and policy interventions.ConclusionThe presence of a specialist wound care nurse positively influences patient outcomes, care continuity, and professional development of nursing staff. For sustainable implementation, organizational support, formalized positions, adequate staffing, and Interprofessional education are essential. Integrating this role into hospital systems can foster safe, evidence-based, and patient-centered wound management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04845-z.

Background

Wound care is one of the essential and routine clinical activities performed by nurses, encompassing tasks such as dressing changes, patient education, and the prevention of wound occurrence [1]. Several studies focusing on nurses’ competencies in wound management have indicated that the level of knowledge and skills among nurses—and even nursing students at the point of graduation—remains limited and This issue is primarily attributed to insufficient educational preparation and the lack of consistent, standardized curricula within undergraduate nursing programs [2–5]. Therefore, to ensure the quality of care and enhance patient safety, the design and implementation of supportive and educational programs aimed at empowering nurses in wound management are imperative [5].

Improving wound care globally requires the adoption of innovative care models and the optimal utilization of healthcare providers’ capacities, particularly given the increasing burden of chronic and complex wounds. Nurses with advanced competencies and specialization in wound management can play a pivotal role in bridging care gaps and promoting evidence-based practice [6]. Findings from Sili et al. demonstrated that nurse-led management of chronic wounds was associated with faster healing rates, shorter treatment durations, and reduced hospital readmissions compared to standard care [7]. It is recommended that nursing managers assign clinical pathways for chronic wound management to specialist nurses to improve patient outcomes and prevent recurrent hospitalizations [8]. Recent systematic review also indicate that nurses specialized in wound, ostomy, and continence (WOC) care not only enhance care quality and patient outcomes but also play a critical role in educating and empowering other nurses, reducing healthcare costs, providing advanced treatments, and engaging in research and leadership. Nevertheless, high-quality studies with advanced designs are needed to demonstrate sustained effects and reduce bias, highlighting the importance of developing supportive and educational programs for specialized wound nursing [9]. Innes-Walker et al. (2018) further showed that a nurse-led, collaborative wound clinic model, providing continuous training and coaching to primary healthcare staff, increased their confidence and competence in assessing and managing various wound types, improved patient outcomes, enhanced knowledge, and led to higher patient satisfaction [10]. Collectively, this evidence underscores the critical role of specialist wound nurses and nurse-led clinics in improving care quality and empowering other healthcare providers.

In line with this evidence and considering the gap between theoretical knowledge and clinical practice in wound care, one of the main challenges identified in our hospital, based on the research team’s observations, was the low quality of wound care, particularly in the management of chronic wounds. To address this issue, an action research study was designed and implemented in 2022, during which a wound clinic—including a general surgeon, an infectious disease physician, and a specialist wound care nurse—was established, and the specialist nurse provided care across both the hospital wards and the clinic. The subsequent phase of the project aimed to evaluate the effectiveness and efficiency of the wound clinic and the specialist nurse, facilitating necessary adjustments and improvements.

One component of the evaluation phase involved understanding nurses’ experiences. To assess the effectiveness of this intervention from the perspective of clinical nurses, a qualitative study was designed to examine their experiences interacting with the specialist wound care nurse. Analyzing these experiences can help identify barriers, educational needs, and opportunities for improvement within the wound care system, thereby providing a foundation for developing more effective care models in the future. Therefore, the question of this study was “what is nurses’ experiences regarding the presence of a specialist wound care nurse in the hospital?”

Methods

Study design

This study was part of a larger research project (Project No. 13971993009). It employed a qualitative design with a descriptive–exploratory approach and was conducted between September 2024 and January 2025 to explore nurses’ experiences regarding the presence of a specialist wound care nurse in the hospital.

Setting

The study was conducted in a specialized surgical and trauma care center located in Lorestan, Iran, which includes 4 intensive care units, 1 CCU, and surgical wards including neurosurgery, urology, ENT, reconstructive & plastic surgery, general surgery, burn, internal medicine, infectious diseases, and emergency. Since 2022, the wound care clinic provides care to all patients with acute and chronic wounds during hospitalization, after discharge, and in outpatient visits. The specialist wound care nurse, every morning (except one day), first visits patients with acute and chronic wounds for whom the attending physician has requested a consultation. Together with In charge nurse ward nurse, the wound care nurse takes patient history, performs assessments, and if needed coordinates with the surgical and infectious disease physicians and the wound care team to plan and implement wound care. After visiting inpatients, the wound care nurse attends the wound clinic and provides care to outpatients.

Participants

The participants included ten nurses (one specialist wound care nurse, three nursing managers or supervisors, five staff nurses, and one faculty member specializing in nursing) who were involved in the hospital’s wound care program. Purposeful sampling with maximum variation was employed to ensure representation from different wards and levels of professional experience. The inclusion criteria were holding a relevant clinical position, having at least five years of work experience, and a willingness to participate in the study. Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants were free to withdraw from the study at any stage without any consequences.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews (average duration: 35 min) and field notes. The interviews focused on participants’ experiences, perceived benefits and challenges, and educational needs related to the presence of the specialist wound care nurse (see Interview Guide). To ensure the clarity and comprehensibility of the interview questions, a pilot interview was conducted solely to test the interview guide; the data from this pilot were not included in the final analysis. All interviews were conducted after obtaining informed consent and ensuring confidentiality. With participants’ permission, interviews were audio-recorded. The time and location of each interview were arranged in coordination with the participants in the hospital clinical environment, including inpatient wards and the wound care clinic. All interviews were carried out by the corresponding author, who possesses experience in qualitative research as well as clinical nursing practice, and is skilled in conducting semi-structured interviews and performing conventional content analysis.

Regarding saturation, data were collected until no new concepts or codes emerged in the most recent interviews; thus, the analysis continued to ensure that all categories and dimensions of participants’ experiences were fully identified.

Data analysis

Data in this study were analyzed using Qualitative Content Analysis followed the framework proposed by Graneheim and Lundman (2004) [11]. All 10 interviews were fully transcribed verbatim. The initial independent coding of all interviews was conducted by SF and ZF. The unit of analysis comprised the entire interview texts. The interview texts were initially divided into 402 meaning units, which consisted of words, sentences, or paragraphs related to a central meaning or topic. These units were then condensed into 282 condensed meaning units to preserve the core meaning while making the data manageable for analysis. The condensed meaning units were subsequently coded, resulting in 54 final codes. These codes were compared and discussed in group meetings among the researchers until a consensus was reached. Next, the codes were organized into 12 subcategories, which were further grouped into 4 main categories. These categories represented the manifest content of the texts, reflecting the clear and observable topics within the interviews. The importance of reflexivity in qualitative research was carefully considered. The corresponding author (SF) was directly present in the clinical setting and collaborated with faculty members during the implementation of the action research project establishing the wound care clinic. Potential preconceptions and biases of the researcher—such as the perceived value of having a specialist wound care nurse—were documented prior to data analysis and discussed in group sessions with MSH, NM and ZF to minimize their influence on the analysis. These measures included maintaining reflexive notes, cross-checking codes, and engaging in group discussions to reach consensus.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Lorestan University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.LUMS.REC.1403.170). Prior to data collection, written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Throughout the study, the principles of confidentiality, anonymity, and voluntary participation were strictly observed. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its later amendments. To protect participant anonymity, all data were recorded without any personal identifiers. Audio files were destroyed after transcription, and the transcribed texts were stored only on a single, password-protected computer accessible to the corresponding author for one year. Consequently, participants’ identities were not revealed at any stage of the analysis or reporting.

Rigor

To ensure the rigor and trustworthiness of the findings, the four criteria proposed by Guba and Lincoln—credibility, dependability, transferability, and confirmability—were applied [12]. Credibility was established through member checking and peer debriefing to validate the researchers’ interpretations. Dependability was ensured by maintaining a detailed audit trail of the coding process and keeping comprehensive analytical memos. Transferability was enhanced by providing a rich and detailed description of the study context, the care setting, and the participants’ characteristics, enabling readers to judge whether the findings could be applied to other settings. Finally, confirmability was achieved through independent review of the research process and documentation by external researchers. In this study, the evaluation of credibility, dependability, transferability, and confirmability was conducted with the involvement of multiple researchers (two researchers independently at the beginning of the analysis and, subsequently, three researchers). All coding, categorization, and theme development were discussed and reviewed in group sessions among the researchers, leading to final consensus. This multi-researcher approach, combined with feedback and review methods, enhanced the trustworthiness and transparency of the qualitative content analysis and ensured that the interpretations aligned with the data.

Results

The findings of this study represent part of a larger research project (13971993009). The demographic characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. Data analysis yielded four main categories and several related subcategories, each reflecting an aspect of the impact of the wound care specialist nurse on clinical practice and the nursing team. The first category, “Specialized Wound Care,” included subcategories such as the utilization of specialized knowledge and skills, improving quality and accelerating wound healing, and Reducing complications and wound-related infections. The second category, “Continuity of Wound Care,” encompassed continuous and regular patient follow-up, interdepartmental coordination in care delivery, and strengthening team communication and continuity of treatment. The third category, “Empowerment of Nurses,” comprised in-service training and clinical learning, enhancing professional competence and evidence-based decision-making, and Increasing awareness for prevention and early detection of chronic wounds. Additionally, the study identified several challenges and improvement strategies, including Staff shortage and high patient load, Lack of defined organizational structure and position, and Need for managerial support and Interprofessional training (Table 2). Categories and subcategories

Specialized wound care

This category reflects the pivotal role of the wound care specialist nurse (WCSN) in providing advanced clinical care. Participants emphasized the utilization of specialized knowledge and skills by these nurses. They noted that WCSNs, drawing on up-to-date scientific knowledge, were able to accurately assess wound type, select appropriate dressings, and perform effective clinical interventions.There is a big difference when a wound nurse performs the assessment. She knows exactly what stage the wound is in and which dressing should be used. (P2)

There is a big difference when a wound nurse performs the assessment. She knows exactly what stage the wound is in and which dressing should be used. (P2)

Improving the quality of care and accelerating wound healing also emerged as key subcategories. Participants reported that with the involvement of WCSNs, the quality of care improved, and wound healing occurred more rapidly.Previously, patients’ wounds would take weeks to heal, and for diabetic foot ulcers or pressure injuries grade 3 and above, there was little noticeable improvement. But now, with the wound nurse’s guidance, diagnosis and treatment progress much faster. (P7)

Previously, patients’ wounds would take weeks to heal, and for diabetic foot ulcers or pressure injuries grade 3 and above, there was little noticeable improvement. But now, with the wound nurse’s guidance, diagnosis and treatment progress much faster. (P7)

From the participants’ perspectives, these interventions also contributed to a reduction in wound-related complications and infections. In many cases, careful dressing control and adherence to antiseptic principles by WCSNs prevented hospital-acquired infections.Usually, nurses have difficulty identifying wound infections in the early stages, but the wound nurse, because of her specialized knowledge and greater experience, can easily recognize them. (P3)

Usually, nurses have difficulty identifying wound infections in the early stages, but the wound nurse, because of her specialized knowledge and greater experience, can easily recognize them. (P3)

Continuity of wound care

This category highlights the WCSN’s role in establishing consistent and sustainable care. Continuous and regular patient follow-up was recognized as one of the key elements in maintaining continuity. Participants explained that WCSNs monitored patients from the time of diagnosis, throughout hospitalization, and even after discharge, providing consultation and re-evaluation when necessary.The wound nurse doesn’t abandon patients after discharge. She calls them, asks for wound photos, and follows up on their progress. (P4)

The wound nurse doesn’t abandon patients after discharge. She calls them, asks for wound photos, and follows up on their progress. (P4)

Another subcategory was interdepartmental coordination in care delivery and strengthening team communication and continuity of treatment. Participants emphasized that WCSNs acted as liaisons between members of the healthcare team—surgeons, infectious disease specialists, and physiotherapists—enhancing Interprofessional collaboration and cohesion within the nursing team.When the wound nurse coordinates with the physicians, everything moves faster, and a more effective treatment plan is developed and implemented. (P5)

When the wound nurse coordinates with the physicians, everything moves faster, and a more effective treatment plan is developed and implemented. (P5)

Empowerment of nurses

This category represents the educational and developmental role of the WCSN within the nursing team. The first subcategory, in-service training and clinical learning, was evident in participants’ accounts. Nurses explained that through direct collaboration with WCSNs, they acquired new hands-on skills in wound assessment and dressing techniques.Now nurses learn specialized wound care while working alongside the wound nurse; each time they learn something new that isn’t taught in workshops, and because it happens in the clinical setting, the learning is more lasting. (P6)

Now nurses learn specialized wound care while working alongside the wound nurse; each time they learn something new that isn’t taught in workshops, and because it happens in the clinical setting, the learning is more lasting. (P6)

This subcategory focuses on enhancing professional competence and promoting evidence-based clinical decision-making. Through guidance and instruction from WCSNs, as well as access to scientific resources, nurses learn to base their decisions on empirical evidence and established standards rather than solely on personal experience or intuition. Moreover, ongoing interactions with WCSNs —who frequently references recent publications and specialized seminars during training and discussions—encourage nurses to engage with up-to-date books, articles, and resources, thereby fostering a culture of self-directed learning within the team.The wound nurse regularly attends wound care seminars and shares new information with us. Now our decisions are based on evidence, not just guesswork or experience, because we’ve learned to review scientific sources. (P8)

The wound nurse regularly attends wound care seminars and shares new information with us. Now our decisions are based on evidence, not just guesswork or experience, because we’ve learned to review scientific sources. (P8)

The third subcategory, increasing awareness toward prevention and early detection of chronic wounds, showed a shift in nurses’ perspectives from a purely treatment-oriented to a preventive approach, particularly concerning pressure injuries.Before, we only intervened once a wound had developed, but now we’ve learned to recognize the early signs and prevent wounds from occurring. (P9)

Before, we only intervened once a wound had developed, but now we’ve learned to recognize the early signs and prevent wounds from occurring. (P9)

Challenges and strategies for improvement

Despite the multiple benefits of the WCSN role, participants identified several significant barriers. The first subcategory, staff shortage and high patient load, was frequently mentioned. They reported that the number of WCSNs was insufficient compared to the large volume of patients.With this patient load, one or two wound nurses are not enough. Many times, there isn’t enough time to visit every patient. (P10)

With this patient load, one or two wound nurses are not enough. Many times, there isn’t enough time to visit every patient. (P10)

The second subcategory, lack of a defined organizational structure and position, referred to the absence of an officially recognized role for wound care nurses within hospital systems. Participants believed this lack of formal recognition negatively affected motivation and limited the scope of practice.The absence of an official position in the organizational chart causes problems with salary, benefits, and authority—and over time, it may reduce motivation for people to take on this role. (P7)

The absence of an official position in the organizational chart causes problems with salary, benefits, and authority—and over time, it may reduce motivation for people to take on this role. (P7)

Finally, the third subcategory, need for managerial support and Interprofessional training, pointed to systemic solutions. Participants emphasized that managerial support—such as providing adequate wound care supplies, negotiating insurance coverage, and organizing joint educational programs among nurses, physicians, and other health professionals—could facilitate the sustainability and development of this specialized role.If managers support this role and Interprofessional education is implemented, the overall quality of wound care across the hospital will improve. (P1)

Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrate that the presence of wound care specialist nurses plays a critical and multidimensional role in enhancing the quality, continuity, and safety of patient care. Participants emphasized that these nurses are “not merely dressing providers,” highlighting their broader contributions, including the empowerment of other nurses, coordination with physicians in developing treatment plans, and application of evidence-based knowledge for informed clinical decision-making.

Analysis of participants’ experiences revealed that each professional group focused on distinct aspects of the specialist’s role. Clinical nurses primarily highlighted improvements in care quality and opportunities for experiential learning; nurse supervisors emphasized team coordination and operational management; and faculty members, along with the specialist herself, concentrated on structural and organizational dimensions of the clinic. These varying perspectives illustrate the multifaceted impact of wound care specialists and underscore the importance of a comprehensive understanding of their role in both clinical practice and education. Such insights enrich the study’s findings and support their transferability to other hospital settings with similar specialized nursing roles.

The findings indicated that wound care specialist nurses, by utilizing their specialized knowledge and conducting thorough assessments of wound characteristics (such as tissue type, exudate, and the extent of necrosis or granulation), make more scientific and evidence-based clinical decisions. This aligns with the findings of Lee and Chang (2023), who, in their qualitative study, demonstrated that experienced wound nurses employ analytical interpretation of tissue and physiological signs to select treatment strategies both intuitively and systematically [7].

Furthermore, the results of Lommi et al. (2023) showed that the implementation of specialized wound care by trained nurses led to improvements in nursing indicators, reduced infection rates, and shorter wound healing durations. These findings underscore the importance of specialized training and evidence-based decision-making in minimizing clinical errors and enhancing care quality [13]. In addition, patients have reported that Advanced Practice Registered Nurses (APRNs) spend more time engaging in dialogue, providing individualized education, and reviewing treatment options with them [14]. Nurse-led models (e.g., the utilization of APRNs and standardized nursing teams) and transitional care models can strengthen relational and managerial continuity, thereby creating more coherent care pathways for patients with chronic wounds. Evidence indicates that the active role of APRNs in patient transition and follow-up reduces unnecessary visits and enhances the patient experience [15].

In addition to the findings of the present study, recent international evidence indicates that nurse-led wound care clinic models can have significant clinical impacts. A study found that clinics led by nurse practitioners achieved higher wound healing rates, increased access to care, better continuity of treatment, and improved clinical outcomes for underserved populations [16]. These findings are consistent with other empirical research, showing that nurse-led care can reduce treatment duration and lower readmission rates [8], as well as reports highlighting high patient satisfaction with specialized consultations and support in these models [17].

The findings of the present study similarly demonstrate that the presence of a wound care specialist in the clinical setting improves care quality, enhances nurses’ practical skills, and increases the use of evidence-based practice in clinical decision-making. Combined with global evidence from nurse-led clinics, this suggests that such care models can be effectively implemented even in resource-limited settings, producing satisfactory outcomes in wound management. Therefore, the results of the present study are not only justified within the national context but are also generalizable within the framework of international advanced nursing models.

Continuity of care encompasses relational, informational, and management aspects, ensuring that patients receive coordinated, connected, and consistent care over time. Relational continuity refers to a stable, ongoing relationship between patients and their care providers; informational continuity ensures that relevant clinical information is accurately shared across care encounters; and management continuity guarantees that care plans are coherent and consistently implemented across providers and settings. Evidence indicates that strong continuity of care is associated with improved patient outcomes and reduced hospitalizations, particularly for older adults and patients with complex or chronic conditions [18]. In the present study, regular patient follow-up, accurate reporting, and effective communication among patients, nurses, the wound care nurse, and physicians were identified as key factors contributing to continuity of care. Furthermore, the wound care nurse acted as a link between different wards, transforming wound care from a fragmented and isolated process into an integrated and continuous care pathway. Fitzpatrick (2022) emphasized that establishing a single point of contact for wound care and vascular surgery needs increased coordination within the multidisciplinary team and enabled comprehensive patient care. These findings confirm that weekly continuous care and effective communication among nurses, advanced practice nurses, and surgeons can prevent unnecessary hospitalizations and improve patient outcomes [17]. Collectively, these results indicate that, at the clinical level, coordinated and structured management by specialist nurses can be highly effective.

Studies have shown that nurses’ knowledge and skills in wound care remain insufficient, with the diversity and lack of structured training identified as major barriers [5]. The presence of a specialist wound care nurse has significant educational and empowering effects on other nurses. Nurse empowerment refers to providing nurses with the professional authority, knowledge, and resources necessary to act independently and effectively in specialized areas of care. In the context of wound care, this empowerment encompasses specialized clinical education, access to protocols and decision-making pathways, organizational support for clinical decisions, and capacity-building for participation in multidisciplinary interventions. Evidence indicates that nurse-led care in community-oriented settings can be cost-effective and improve outcomes for patients with chronic wounds, including faster healing, reduced treatment duration, and increased patient satisfaction [19].

Participants’ this study emphasized that in-service training, observing the specialist’s performance, and receiving effective clinical feedback are among the most important forms of learning. Active, experiential learning in real care environments can significantly enhance nurses’ skills [20]. This aligns with the key nursing concept of work-based learning, which is defined as informal, experiential learning occurring during routine clinical activities. A Systematic review have shown that work-based learning occurs when nurses engage in continuous interaction with colleagues, perform clinical tasks, and exchange experiences, such that learning emerges from doing the work rather than solely from formal instruction [21]. This type of learning provides opportunities to strengthen practical skills, improve decision-making, and foster professional development, especially when the clinical environment’s organizational culture supports experience-sharing and encourages interaction and feedback [21, 22].

Despite the evident benefits of the specialist wound care nurse role, participants in this study reported several key barriers, including staff shortages, the absence of a clearly defined organizational position, and weak managerial support. The lack of a transparent organizational structure and insufficient personnel has been associated with reduced adherence to standard guidelines, difficulties in team coordination, delayed wound healing, and increased costs. Therefore, merely developing guidelines without providing adequate organizational infrastructure, managerial support, and sufficient staffing is insufficient for the effective and sustainable implementation of the specialist nurse role.

Moreover, in many hospitals, wound care lacks an independent and specialized place within the organizational structure. One study noted that “the wound care community is highly fragmented and lacks a center of excellence capable of organizing care” [23]. The absence of a formal organizational position can lead to role overlap and reduced efficiency, whereas establishing an official role and providing structural support are essential prerequisites for the development and sustainability of this role. Accordingly, institutionalizing the specialist wound care nurse role requires organizational support, Interprofessional education, and formal recognition within the nursing structure [5, 23].

The implementation of specialized nursing roles in hospital settings is a complex, multi-stage, and context-dependent process. Scoping review studies have reported that implementing the Clinical Nurse Specialist (CNS) role in hospital environments requires attention to structural elements, care processes, and outcome evaluation, and that this process may vary across clinical contexts depending on organizational resources, managerial support, and the availability of practical guidance [24]. Furthermore, evidence indicates that implementing specialized nursing roles involves the stages of needs assessment, role design, role implementation, and role evaluation, and that structured frameworks can assist organizations in effectively managing this process [25]. Reviews of advanced nursing roles in Arab countries have also shown that the main challenges in implementing these roles include role ambiguity, lack of legal and policy-level support, and limitations in human resources, all of which hinder the stabilization and sustained integration of these roles into practice [26]. The findings of the present study revealed that the absence of a formal organizational position for the specialist wound care nurse is one of the key challenges in establishing and sustaining the specialized role of these nurses. This finding aligns with the international literature on Advanced Practice Nursing, as global frameworks, such as the International Council of Nurses (ICN) guidelines, emphasize that formal role definition, clear delineation of responsibilities, and organizational support are essential features of advanced nursing roles, and their absence can lead to role ambiguity and implementation barriers [27]. Other qualitative and comparative studies have also shown that unclear structural and supportive role frameworks, legal and organizational arrangements, and the lack of clear guidelines at policy and organizational levels are major obstacles to implementing advanced nursing roles within healthcare systems [28].

The diversity of participants in the present study also strengthens the interpretive depth and transferability of the findings. Purposive sampling with maximum variation in this study, involving clinical nurses, nurse supervisors, and faculty members from different units and with varying levels of professional experience, enabled an in-depth and multidimensional exploration of experiences related to the role and functions of the specialist wound care nurse. This diversity of participants facilitated the identification of similarities and differences in perceptions of the specialist wound care nurse’s roles and responsibilities, ensuring that the findings were not confined to a single unit or context. Consequently, common patterns extending beyond individual settings were identified, enhancing the transferability of the findings to similar clinical contexts, particularly hospitals in which the role of the specialist wound care nurse is emerging or evolving.

Clinical significance of the study

The findings of this study indicate that the presence of a wound care specialist nurse plays a meaningful role in improving the quality and continuity of care, reducing wound-related complications and infections, and enhancing evidence-based clinical decision-making within the nursing team. Beyond providing direct patient care, this specialized role functions as an educational and supportive resource at the bedside, contributing to the development of nurses’ professional competence and increasing their sensitivity toward wound prevention and early detection. These findings highlight the importance of structurally integrating specialized nursing roles into routine care pathways to promote patient safety and care coherence.

Limitations and recommendations for future research & clinical practice

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, it was conducted in a single teaching hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other healthcare settings with different organizational structures, resources, or patient populations. Second, the study focused exclusively on nurses’ experiences with the specialist wound care nurse, without directly incorporating perspectives from patients or other healthcare providers, which could have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the intervention’s impact. Finally, the findings are context-specific, reflecting the implementation of a nurse-led wound care clinic within a particular cultural and healthcare environment. Therefore, caution is warranted when attempting to transfer these results to different settings or countries.

Future studies are recommended to employ longitudinal and mixed-methods designs to examine the clinical, organizational, and economic outcomes of wound care specialist nurse roles, while also incorporating patients’ perspectives. In clinical practice, the formal definition of this role within organizational structures, accompanied by managerial support and Interprofessional education, is essential to ensure its sustainability and effectiveness. At the policy level, recognizing specialized nursing roles in clinical guidelines and workforce planning may facilitate the advancement of evidence-based, patient-centered wound care.

Conclusion

The findings of this study underscore the pivotal role of wound care specialist nurses in ensuring continuity of care, and empowering other nurses within the healthcare team. Beyond performing direct patient care, these nurses act as knowledgeable decision-makers, coordinators of multidisciplinary teams, and facilitators of experiential learning for staff. The study highlights that specialized wound care, integrated care processes, and nurse empowerment are interdependent elements that contribute to improved patient safety, and more efficient care pathways. Nevertheless, the successful integration and sustainability of this role require organizational support, clearly defined professional positions, sufficient staffing, and Interprofessional educational initiatives. Implementing such structural and policy measures can institutionalize the wound care specialist nurse role, leading to safer, evidence-based, and patient-centered wound management in hospital settings.
