Curves in hyperspaces obtained by intersection of r r -neighborhoods with a fixed subset
Arsen Galstyan, Alexey Tuzhilin

Abstract
The present paper generalizes the result from one of the papers by Galstyan. Namely, we consider two nonempty subsets A A and B B of a metric space X X , and construct one-parametric family F r F_{r} of subsets obtained by intersection between B B and closed r r -neighborhood of A A , where r r is bigger than the infimum distance between the sets A A and B B . In the case where B B is compact, we show that this intersection, considered as a mapping, is right semicontinuously on r r in the topology generated by Hausdorff distance. Moreover, if A A and B B are convex subsets of a normed space X X , then we prove that F r F_{r} depends continuously on r r in such topology if and only if the Hausdorff distance between different sets F r F_{r} is finite. We also show that for normed spaces X X of dimension 2 2 or less, the latter condition is automatically fulfilled. For dimension 3 3 and hence for bigger ones, we construct an example in which the Hausdorff distance between different F r F_{r} is always infinite.

Introduction

Let
A
and
B
be nonempty subsets of a metric space
X
. We put
F_{r}(A,B)=B_{r}(A)\cap B
, where
B_{r}(A)
is the closed
r
-neighborhood of the set
A
(see Definition
2
). In this work, we study in which cases the mapping

defines a curve in the hyperspace over
X
.

Here, the hyperspace over
X
is understood as the set
\mathcal{P}_{0}(X)
of all nonempty subsets of
X
, endowed with the Hausdorff distance
d_{H}
(see Definition
3
), and a curve in the hyperspace is a continuous mapping from an interval to this hyperspace.

In metric geometry, many standard functionals like the distance between points or from a point to a set, are
1
-Lipschitz, that guarantees their continuity. However, in our case, the mapping
F_{r}
might not possess this property. Indeed, consider the case of the plane with the Euclidean norm. When
r
is changed by a small positive value
\delta>0
, the Hausdorff distance between
F_{r}
and
F_{r+\delta}
may change by a significantly larger value than
\delta
, see Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
An example where
d_{H}\bigl(F_{r},F_{r+\delta}\bigr)>\delta

This figure shows two subsets
A
and
B
. The first is a closed rectangle, the second is a closed triangle. In this example,
F_{r+\delta}\not\subset B_{\delta}\bigl(F_{r}\bigr)
, and thus
d_{H}\bigl(F_{r},F_{r+\delta}\bigr)>\delta
. Moreover, the mapping
F_{r}
can be discontinuous, and there are many different reasons of that, see Examples
2
,
3
, and
4
. However, it turns out that the mapping

is right semicontinuous in an arbitrary metric space provided the subset

is compact, see Theorem

. Notice that the compactness condition of

is essential in Theorem

as it is illustrated in Examples

and

. However, the compactness condition of

does not imply left semicontinuity even in Euclidean plane, see Example

B
.

Assume now that
A
and
B
are convex subsets of a normed space
X
. Is it always true that
F_{r}
depends continuously on
r
? We show that the only obstacle for that is the possibility of infinite distance between different
F_{r}
, see Theorem
20
. Moreover, the infinity of this distance can really occur as we show in Example

. However, it is amazing that for

of dimension
2
or less, the distance between different

is always finite, see Theorem
21

follows from more strict Theorem

is Lipschitz on each closed interval where the finiteness occurs.

Initially, the mapping
F_{r}
arose as a byproduct of the theory of extremal networks in hyperspaces
Gals_1
. Many results in this theory use various deformations of sets of the form
B_{r}(A)\cap B
. However, the mapping
F
turned out to be interesting in itself, so the proof of continuity of this mapping in the case of compact convex subsets of a finite-dimensional normed space was published separately
Gals_0
. The present paper is devoted to generalization of Theorem 3.2 from
Gals_0
to the cases of arbitrary metric spaces, arbitrary normed spaces, and for sets

and

Note that the intersection of an
r
-neighborhood with a subset appear, for example, in
(
Alimov
, pp. 53–54)
, whose author discusses under what circumstances the set
B_{r}(x)\cap B
is a retract of the ball
B_{r}(x)
, where

is a point in a real normed space.

On one hand, as we mentioned above, the present work relates to the theory of extremal networks, which constitutes a large part of metric geometry
Burago
. This theory investigates various variational problems, which have interested specialists for centuries. A detailed historical overview and a summary of modern results can be found in
Branching
;
Cieslik
;
Review
;
Hwang
.

On the other hand, this article investigates the properties of mappings in hyperspaces. The theory of hyperspaces dates back to the works of F. Hausdorff and L. Vietoris at the beginning of the 20th century. This theory gained popularity due to the paper
Kelley
by J. Kelley. A historical excursion on the theory of hyperspaces can be found, for example, in
Nadler
. Mappings to hyperspaces are also called set-valued mappings. The properties of such mappings are studied by a branch of mathematics called set-valued analysis

.

The present work also investigates the geometric structure of convex sets. Such sets and their various transformations arise in functional analysis, probability theory, information theory, and linear programming. Indeed, any system of linear inequalities in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
generates some convex (possibly non-compact) polyhedron. Such systems are widely used, for example, in optimization problems, from planning and organizing production
Kant
to scheduling the work of computer processors

2.1.3
Definition and properties of
r
-neighborhoods

Most statements in this subsection are well known, see for example
TuzLections
.

Definition 1
.

For any subset
A
of a metric space
X
and any point
p\in X
, the distance from
p
to
A
is the value

|p\,A|=\inf\bigl\{|p\,a|:a\in A\bigr\}.

In particular, for empty
A
we have
|p\,A|=\infty
.

Remark 1
.

It is well known that for a nonempty subset
A
of a metric space
X
, the mapping
x\mapsto|x\,A|
is continuous.

Definition 2
.

Let
A
be a subset of a metric space
X
and
0\leq r<\infty
. The subsets

B_{r}(A)=\{p\in X:|p\,A|\leq r\},\ \ U_{r}(A)=\{p\in X:|p\,A|<r\}

are called, respectively, the
closed
and the
open ball with center at
A
and radius
r
or the
closed
and the
open
r
-neighborhood of
A
.

Remark 2
.

According to Definition
1
, for any
0\leq r<\infty
,

B_{r}(\emptyset)=U_{r}(\emptyset)=\emptyset.

In the case
A=\{a\}
, the notations
B_{r}\bigl(\{a\}\bigr)
and
U_{r}\bigl(\{a\}\bigr)
will be replaced for brevity by
B_{r}(a)
and
U_{r}(a)
, respectively.

Lemma 4
.

Let
A
be a subset of a metric space
X
. Then for any
0\leq r<\infty
, the set
B_{r}(A)
is closed, and
B_{0}(A)=\operatorname{Cl}A
.

Proof.

If
A=\emptyset
, then according to Remark
2
,
B_{r}(A)=\emptyset
is closed. For
A\neq\emptyset
, it follows immediately from continuity of the function
x\mapsto|x\,A|
.
∎

Remark 3
.

Notice that
\cup_{a\in A}B_{r}(a)\subset B_{r}(A)
, although equality is not always achieved. For instance, let
A=\{1/n\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\{-1\}\cup\{1/n\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=:X\subset\mathbb{R}
, then
B_{1}(A)=X
, but
\cup_{a\in A}B_{1}(a)=X\setminus\{-1\}
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
A
be a subset of a metric space
X
. Then for any
0<r<\infty
, we have
U_{r}(A)=\bigcup\limits_{a\in A}U_{r}(a)
.

Proof.

If
A=\emptyset
, then according to Remark
2
,
U_{r}(A)=\emptyset
. On the other hand,
\bigcup\limits_{a\in\emptyset}U_{r}(a)=\emptyset
.

For nonempty
A
, we have

x\in U_{r}(A)\Leftrightarrow|x\,A|=\inf_{a\in A}|x\,a|<r\Leftrightarrow\exists a\in A,|x\,a|<r\Leftrightarrow\\
\exists a\in A,x\in U_{r}(a)\Leftrightarrow x\in\cup_{a\in A}U_{r}(a),

which completes the proof.
∎

From Lemma
5
we get

Corollary 6
.

For any subset
A
of a metric space
X
and any
0<r<\infty
, the set
U_{r}(A)
is open.

Lemma 7
.

Let
A
be a nonempty convex subset of a real normed space
X
. Then for any
0<r<\infty
,
U_{r}(A)=\operatorname{Int}B_{r}(A)
.

Proof.

According to Definition
2
and Corollary
6
,
U_{r}(A)\subset\operatorname{Int}B_{r}(A)
. Assume
\operatorname{Int}B_{r}(A)\setminus U_{r}(A)\neq\emptyset
and take an arbitrary
x\in\operatorname{Int}B_{r}(A)\setminus U_{r}(A)
, then
|x\,A|=r
. Since
x\in\operatorname{Int}B_{r}(A)
, there exists
s>0
such that
B_{s}(x)\subset B_{r}(A)
. Take an arbitrary
a\in A
, then the segment
[a,x]
is not degenerate. Extend the segment over the point
x
up to some
y
such that
|x\,y|=s
. Then
y\in B_{s}(x)\subset B_{r}(A)
and
[a,y]\subset B_{r}(A)
because
B_{r}(A)
is convex by Lemma
8
. In addition,
|y\,a|=|x\,a|+s
. Consider an arbitrary
b\in A
, then
[a,b]\subset A
because
A
is convex.

Let
f
be the homothety with center at
a
and coefficient
k=|x\,a|/|y\,a|<1
. Then
f(y)=x
and we put
c=f(b)
. Since
[a,b]\subset A
and
0<k<1
, we have
c\in A
. Notice that we can choose
a
and
b
independently and such that
|x\,a|=r+\alpha
, where
\alpha
is non-negative and arbitrary close to
0
, and for any
\beta>0
one can choose
b
such that
|y\,b|\in[0,r+\beta)
. We get

|x\,c|=k\,|y\,b|=\frac{r+\alpha}{r+\alpha+s}\,|y\,b|\leq\frac{r+\alpha}{r+\alpha+s}\,(r+\beta).

For
\alpha\to 0
and
\beta\to 0
, the right hand side of the previous equation tends to
\frac{r}{r+s}\,r<r
, hence we can choose
\alpha
and
\beta
such that
|x\,c|<r
, a contradiction with
|x\,A|=r
.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Let
A
be a nonempty convex subset of a real normed space
X
. Then for
0\leq r<\infty
, the sets
B_{r}(A)
and
U_{r}(A)
are convex.

Proof.

To start with, we prove that
U_{r}(A)
is convex. Take arbitrary
x,y\in U_{r}(A)
then, by Lemma
5
, there exists
a,b\in A
such that
|a\,x|<r
and
|b\,y|<r
. Then for any
\lambda\in[0,1]
, we have
(1-\lambda)a+\lambda b\in A
due to convexity of
A
, and

\|(1-\lambda)a+\lambda b-(1-\lambda)x+\lambda y\|\leq(1-\lambda)|a\,x|+\lambda|b\,y|<r,

hence
(1-\lambda)x+\lambda y\in U_{r}(A)
.

To prove the result for
B_{r}(A)
, it suffices to change
a
and
b
with sequences
a_{i}\in A
and
b_{i}\in A
such that
|a_{i}\,x|\to|x\,A|\leq r
and
|b_{i}\,y|\to|y\,A|\leq r
, proceed in the same way as above and pass to the limit.
∎

Applying Lemma
8
for
r=0
, we get the following

Corollary 9
.

In a real normed space
X
, the closure of a nonempty convex set is convex.

The next lemma is standard, see
(
TuzLections
, p. 22)

Lemma 10
.

Let
A
be a nonempty subset of a metric space
X
, and
r,r^{\prime}\in[0,\infty)
. Then
B_{r}\bigl(B_{r^{\prime}}(A)\bigr)\subset B_{r+r^{\prime}}(A)
.

Remark 4
.

In general metric spaces, we cannot change the inclusion to equality in Lemma
10
. For example, let
A=\{-1\}\subset\{-1\}\cup\{1/n\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=:X\subset\mathbb{R}
, then
B_{1}(A)=A
, hence
B_{1}(B_{1})(A)=A
. However,
B_{2}(A)=X
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
A
be a nonempty subset of a real normed space
X
, and
r,r^{\prime}\in[0,\infty)
. Then
B_{r}\bigl(B_{r^{\prime}}(A)\bigr)=B_{r+r^{\prime}}(A)
.

Proof.

Lemma
10
implies
B_{r}\bigl(B_{r^{\prime}}(A)\bigr)\subset B_{r+r^{\prime}}(A)
. Let us prove the converse inclusion. Consider an arbitrary
p\in B_{r+r^{\prime}}(A)
, then for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
a\in A
such that
|p\,a|<r+r^{\prime}+\varepsilon
. If
|p\,a|\leq r^{\prime}
, then we put
b=p
, hence we got
b\in B_{r^{\prime}}(A)
and
|b\,p|=0<r+\varepsilon
. If
|p\,a|>r^{\prime}
, we choose
b\in[p,a]
such that
|a\,b|=r^{\prime}
, hence
b\in B_{r^{\prime}}(A)
and
|b\,p|<r+\varepsilon
. Since we can choose
\varepsilon>0
in arbitrary way, we got
\bigl|p\,B_{r^{\prime}}(A)\bigr|\leq r
, thus
p\in B_{r}\bigl(B_{r^{\prime}}(A)\bigr)
.
∎

Proposition 12
.

Let
A
be a nonempty convex subset of a real normed space
X
,
x\not\in\operatorname{Cl}A
, and
p\in A
. Then the ray

\ell:=(1-\lambda)p+\lambda x,\ \ \lambda\in[0,\infty),

does not lie in any ball
B_{r}(A)
for all
0\leq r<\infty
.

Proof.

Since
x\not\in\operatorname{Cl}A
, we have
r:=|x\,A|>0
and hence
|p\,x|\geq r>0
. Choose an arbitrary
k>1
and
y_{k}\in\ell
such that
|p\,y_{k}|=k|p\,x|
. Due to Corollary
9
, the set
\operatorname{Cl}A
is convex. Then
y_{k}\neq\operatorname{Cl}A
, otherwise

. Then
|y_{k}\,A|=:\rho>0
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, let
b\in A
be such that
|y_{k}\,b|<\rho+\varepsilon
. Consider homothety
f
with center

and coefficient
1/k
, then
f(y_{k})=x
. Let

, then

is convex. Hence

2.1.4
Rays in convex set

Lemma 13
.

Let
X
be a real normed space, and let for a sequence
\{x_{i}\}\subset X
and a point
p\in X
, it holds
\|x_{i}-p\|\to\infty
and
\frac{x_{i}-p}{\|x_{i}-p\|}\to y\in X
as
i\to\infty
. Then for any point
q\in X
, we have
\|x_{i}-q\|\to\infty
and
\frac{x_{i}-q}{\|x_{i}-q\|}\to y
as
i\to\infty
.

Proof.

Choose a new coordinate system by taking
p
as the new origin. Since the shift
x\mapsto x-p
is an isometry, it suffices to prove the lemma in the new coordinates, i.e., without loss of generality we can assume that
p=0
. Moreover, starting from sufficiently large
i
, we can also assume that both
x_{i}
and
x_{i}-q
do not vanish for all
i
.

Notice that
\|x_{i}-q\|/\|x_{i}\|\to 1
and thus
\|x_{i}\|/\|x_{i}-q\|\to 1
as
i\to\infty
, because

1-\frac{\|q\|}{\|x_{i}\|}\leq\frac{\|x_{i}-q\|}{\|x_{i}\|}\leq 1+\frac{\|q\|}{\|x_{i}\|}

for each
i
. Hence

\frac{x_{i}-q}{\|x_{i}-q\|}=\frac{\|x_{i}\|}{\|x_{i}-q\|}\biggl(\frac{x_{i}}{\|x_{i}\|}-\frac{q}{\|x_{i}\|}\biggr)\to y,

that completes the proof.
∎

.

Let

be a nonempty convex subset of a real normed space

, and suppose that some ray

emitted from a point

belongs to

. Then for any

To start with, we consider the case

with the vertex

and the base

contains each ray

. Since

, it is contained in

, together with

.

Now, assume that

is closed and take

. Consider an arbitrary

, and put

. Choose a sequence

2.1.5
Hausdorff distance

For any set
X
, denote by
\mathcal{P}_{0}(X)
the collection of all nonempty subsets of
X
.

Definition 3
.

Given a metric spaces
X
, we endow
\mathcal{P}_{0}(X)
with the
Hausdorff distance
d_{H}
defined as follows: for any
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(X)
, we put

d_{H}(A,B)=\inf\bigl\{r:A\subset B_{r}(B),\,B\subset B_{r}(A)\bigr\}.

Notice that
d_{H}
is non-negative, symmetric, and satisfies the triangle inequality, but can be zero for
A\neq B
and can be infinite.

Proposition 15

(
(
TuzLections
, Corollary 5.30)
)
.

For an arbitrary metric space
X
and an arbitrary decreasing sequence of non-empty subsets
A_{1}\supset A_{2}\supset\ldots
of
X
, where
A_{1}
is compact, we have
A_{k}\xrightarrow{d_{H}}\bigcap\limits_{n}\operatorname{Cl}A_{n}\neq\emptyset
.

2.2
Main part

Let
X
be a metric spaces and
\mathcal{P}_{0}(X)
be endowed with the Hausdorff distance
d_{H}
. Then, for any
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(X)
and any
r\in\bigl(|A\,B|,\infty\bigr)\subset\mathbb{R}
, we put
F_{r}(A,B)=B_{r}(A)\cap B
. Note that it holds
F_{r}(A,B)\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(X)
. Thus, henceforth we will measure the distance between sets
F_{r_{1}}(A,B)
and
F_{r_{2}}(A,B)
using the Hausdorff distance
d_{H}
, and we will understand continuity of
F_{r}(A,B)
in

with respect to
d_{H}
.

2.2.1
Continuity of
F_{r}
in metric spaces

We start from the first part of our main result. If
X
is a metric space, we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{comp}}(X)
the subfamily of
\mathcal{P}_{0}(X)
consisting of all compact sets.

Theorem 16
.

Let

2.2.2
Continuity of
F_{r}
in normed spaces

If
X
is a real normed space, we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{conv}}(X)
the subfamily of
\mathcal{P}_{0}(X)
consisting of all convex sets.

Proposition 17
.

Let
X
be real normed space, and
A,\,B\in\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{conv}}(X)
. Let the mapping
f\colon X\times\bigl(|A\,B|,\infty\bigr)\to[0,\infty)
be defined by the rule
f(x,r)=\bigl|x\,F_{r}(A,B)\bigr|
. Then
f
is convex in
r
.

Proof.

For brevity, put
F_{\rho}:=F_{\rho}(A,B)
. Fix
x\in X
and set
r=(1-t)r_{1}+t\,r_{2}
, where
r_{1},\,r_{2}\in\bigl(|A\,B|,\infty\bigr)
. Upto the end of this paragraph, all statements that include
i
are valid for both
i=1,\,2
. Take any
\varepsilon>0
. There exists
\widetilde{y}_{i}\in F_{r_{i}}
such that
|x\,\widetilde{y}_{i}|<f(x,r_{i})+\varepsilon/2
. Since
r_{i}\in\bigl(|A\,B|,\infty\bigr)
, we have
U_{r_{i}}(A)\cap B\neq\emptyset
. Let
z_{i}\in U_{r_{i}}(A)\cap B
. Since
A
and
B
are convex, Lemmas
8
,
7
, and
1
imply
[z_{i},\widetilde{y}_{i})\subset U_{r_{i}}(A)\cap B
. So, there exists
y_{i}\in U_{r_{i}}(A)\cap B
such that
|\widetilde{y}_{i}\,y_{i}|<\varepsilon/2
. By triangle inequality, we get
|x\,y_{i}|<f(x,r_{i})+\varepsilon
. In addition, since
y_{i}\in U_{r_{i}}(A)
, there exists
a_{i}\in A
such that
|y_{i}\,a_{i}|<r_{i}
.

Let
y=(1-t)y_{1}+t\,y_{2}
and
a=(1-t)a_{1}+t\,a_{2}
. By the convexity of
A
and
B
, we have
y\in B
and
a\in A
. Further,
|y\,a|\leq(1-t)|y_{1}\,a_{1}|+t|y_{2}\,a_{2}|<(1-t)r_{1}+t\,r_{2}=r
. From this we obtain
y\in U_{r}(A)
, and therefore,
y\in F_{r}
.

Thus, we have

|x\,y|\leq(1-t)|x\,y_{1}|+t|x\,y_{2}|<(1-t)f(x,r_{1})+t\,f(x,r_{2})+\varepsilon,

therefore,

f\bigl(x,(1-t)r_{1}+t\,r_{2}\bigr)=|x\,F_{r}|\leq|x\,y|<(1-t)f(x,r_{1})+t\,f(x,r_{2})+\varepsilon.

From the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon>0
it follows that the mapping
f(x,r)
is convex in
r
.
∎

Theorem 18
.

Let
X
be a real normed space, and
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{conv}}(X).
Suppose that for some
(Q,T)\subset\bigl(|A\,B|,\infty\bigr)
and any
s_{1},s_{2}\in(Q,T)
, it holds
d_{H}\bigl(F_{s_{1}}(A,B),F_{s_{2}}(A,B)\bigr)<\infty.
Then, for any
q,t\in(Q,T)
, there exists
M>0
such that the mapping
F_{r}(A,B)
is
M
-Lipschitz in
r
on
[q,t]
.

Proof.

For brevity, put
F_{\rho}:=F_{\rho}(A,B)
. Choose arbitrary
r,r^{\prime}\in(Q,T)
,
r<r^{\prime}
, and
q,q^{\prime},t\in(Q,T)
such that
Q<q^{\prime}<q\leq r<r^{\prime}\leq t<T
, then
d:=d_{H}(F_{q^{\prime}},F_{t})<\infty
. Since for each
\rho\in[q^{\prime},t]
we have
F_{q^{\prime}}\subset F_{\rho}\subset F_{t}
, it holds
d_{H}(F_{q^{\prime}},F_{\rho})=\sup_{x\in F_{\rho}}|x\,F_{q^{\prime}}|\leq d
.

Since
r\in(q^{\prime},r^{\prime})
, there exists and unique
\delta\in(0,1)
such that
r=(1-\delta)r^{\prime}+\delta q^{\prime}
, hence
\delta=(r^{\prime}-r)/(r^{\prime}-q^{\prime})<(r^{\prime}-r)/(q-q^{\prime})
. Put
M=d/(q-q^{\prime})
.

By Proposition
17
, for each
x\in F_{r^{\prime}}
it holds

|x\,F_{r}|\leq(1-\delta)|x\,F_{r^{\prime}}|+\delta|x\,F_{q^{\prime}}|=\delta|x\,F_{q^{\prime}}|\leq\delta d<\frac{d}{q-q^{\prime}}(r^{\prime}-r)=M(r^{\prime}-r).

Hence, for any
r,r^{\prime}\in[q,t]
,
r<r^{\prime}
, we obtain
d_{H}(F_{r},F_{r^{\prime}})=\sup_{x\in F_{r^{\prime}}}|x\,F_{r}|\leq M(r^{\prime}-r)
, i.e.,
F_{r}
is
M
-Lipschitz in
r
on
[q,t]
.
∎

Theorem

directly implies the following

Corollary 19
.

Let

be a real normed space, and

. Suppose that for some

and any

.

This leads to the following continuity criterion.

Theorem 20

(Continuity Criterion of
F_{r}
in normed spaces)
.

Let
X
be a real normed space, and
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{conv}}(X)
. Then the mapping
F_{r}(A,B)
is continuous in
r
on some interval
(Q,T)\subset\bigl(|A\,B|,\infty\bigr)
if and only if for any
s_{1},s_{2}\in(Q,T)
, it holds

d_{H}\bigl(F_{s_{1}}(A,B),F_{s_{2}}(A,B)\bigr)<\infty.

Proof.

If for any
s_{1},s_{2}\in(Q,T)
it holds
d_{H}\bigl(F_{s_{1}}(A,B),F_{s_{2}}(A,B)\bigr)<\infty
, then the mapping
F_{r}
is continuous in
r
on
(Q,T)
by Corollary
19
. Thus, it remains to prove the converse statement.

For brevity, put
F_{\rho}=F_{\rho}(A,B)
. Let
F_{r}
be continuous on
(Q,T)
. This means that for each
s\in(Q,T)
and any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta_{s}
such that
I_{s}:=(s-\delta_{s},s+\delta_{s})\subset(Q,T)
and for any
r\in I_{s}
it holds
d_{H}(F_{s},F_{r})<\varepsilon
. This implies that for each
s\in(Q,T)
, the function
f_{s}(r)=d_{H}(F_{s},F_{r})
is finite on the interval
I_{s}
.

Now we take an arbitrary
s_{1},s_{2}\in(Q,T)
, say
s_{1}<s_{2}
, and consider the open covering
\{I_{s}\}_{s\in[s_{1},s_{2}]}
. By compactness of
[s_{1},s_{2}]
we can take a finite subcovering
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}
such that
I_{k-1}\cap I_{k}\neq\emptyset
for each
k=2,\ldots,n
, and there exists a sequence
s_{1}=t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{n}=s_{2}
with
t_{k}\in I_{k}
. Since consecutive
I_{k}
intersect each other, we get
d_{H}(F_{t_{k-1}},F_{t_{k}})<\infty
, and, by the triangle inequality, we obtain
d_{H}(F_{s_{1}},F_{s_{2}})<\infty
, that completes the proof.
∎

Note that, in general, the convexity of the sets

for any

Example 4
.

Fix in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
the standard basis
e_{1}=(1,0,0)
,
e_{2}=(0,1,0)

We show that

Now we need to show that for any

. Thus, we get

Conclusion

This work studies the continuous dependence on
r\in\bigl[|A\,B|,\infty\bigr)
of the mapping
F_{r}(A,B)=B_{r}(A)\cap B
in metric and normed spaces, where
A
and
B
are nonempty subsets of these spaces. Such a mapping arises in geometric optimization problems with the Hausdorff metric, for example, when searching for subsets that minimize the sum of distances to given elements of the hyperspace
\mathcal{H}(X)
over the space
X
, see
Gals_1
.

Furthermore, it would also be interesting to investigate the operation
F_{r}(A,B)
with respect to continuous dependence on the subsets
A
and
B
. Specifically, fix a radius
r\in\bigl(|A\,B|,\infty\bigr).
The question is: will the mapping
F_{r}(A,B)
be continuous at the point
(A,B)\in\mathcal{H}(X)\times\mathcal{H}(X)
, and if so, under what conditions and in what spaces? This question is the subject of possible future research.
