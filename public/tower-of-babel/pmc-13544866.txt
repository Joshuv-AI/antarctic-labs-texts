Knowledge, Attitude, and Practices of General Neurologists and Headache Experts Toward Migraine Treatment: A Cross-Sectional Study in India
Authors: Sanjith Aaron, Ashish Bondia, Ishan Patel, Vikas Madaan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Migraine, a disabling neurological disorder, poses a significant global burden, especially among women of reproductive age. In India, inconsistent diagnoses and the underutilization of evidence-based therapies present substantial challenges. This study evaluated the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of general neurologists and headache experts in India regarding migraine diagnosis and treatment, identifying barriers and opportunities for improvement in clinical care.Methods:A cross-sectional online survey was conducted among 137 clinicians in India (125 general neurologists and 12 headache experts). The questionnaire assessed diagnostic approaches, preferences for pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments, utilization of guidelines, and readiness to adopt novel therapies. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics.Results:Headache experts reported a higher patient burden, with 9 (75.00%) seeing 25 or more headache patients per week, compared to 39 (31.2%) general neurologists. Migraines comprised over 50% of cases for 7 (58.33%) of the experts. The use of International Classification of Headache Disorders (3rd edition) criteria was markedly higher among headache experts (91.67% vs. 38.71%), as was the Migraine Disability Assessment (66.67% vs. 24.20%). Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and triptans were the most commonly prescribed therapies. Although triptans provided only moderate relief for most patients, newer agents, such as ditans, were rarely used (0–20%). Nonpharmacological interventions were widely adopted, with approximately 90% usage in both groups. Barriers included limited diagnostic standardization, therapeutic inertia, low familiarity with newer agents, and operational challenges such as time constraints.Conclusions:Both general neurologists and headache experts acknowledge the burden of migraine and practice holistic management. However, gaps remain in standardized diagnosis, optimal triptan use, and the adoption of novel therapies. Enhancing clinician education, promoting validated diagnostic tools, developing expert consensus, and increasing community awareness are crucial to optimizing outcomes and reducing therapeutic inertia.

Introduction

Migraine is a debilitating neurological disorder characterized by episodes of moderate to severe headaches that typically last 4 to 72 hours, accompanied by symptoms such as photophobia, phonophobia, nausea, and vomiting.[1] Over 1.1 billion individuals globally are affected by migraine. Between 1990 and 2019, the global age-standardized prevalence of migraine increased by 1.7% (95% uncertainty interval [UI]: 0.7%–2.8%), indicating a rising public health burden. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2019, migraine is the second leading cause of years lived with disability (YLDs) worldwide and the primary cause among women aged 15 to 49 years, accounting for 88.2% of the 46.6 million headache-related YLDs, which represents 5.4% of the total global YLDs.[23] In low- and middle-income countries, including India, migraine management is constrained by limited neurological resources, with only 0.1 neurologists per 100,000 population compared to 7.1 in high-income countries.[4] In India, migraine presents a substantial public health challenge, with prevalence rates ranging from 14.1% to 25.2%, and higher disability observed in women and rural populations (median of 24 headache days per year).[56] Affected individuals lose an average of 17.3 productive days annually, leading to a substantial economic burden.[7]

Diagnostic challenges arise from symptom overlap and limited training, resulting in nearly half of migraines going unrecognized. This leads to prolonged suffering, increased disability, and substantial economic burdens. Targeted clinician training is essential to improve diagnostic accuracy and patient outcomes.[8] To ensure accurate diagnosis and appropriate management, migraines are classified according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3), 3rd edition, developed by the International Headache Society (IHS).[9] Adherence to these standardized diagnostic criteria enhances diagnostic precision and reduces misclassification in both clinical and research settings.

Conventional acute treatments, such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), triptans, and ergotamines, offer moderate efficacy but carry risks of gastrointestinal adverse effects, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), and medication-overuse headaches. Many patients experiencing inadequate relief continue to suffer from debilitating migraine attacks, underscoring the need for targeted therapies.[10] Novel targeted therapies, such as gepants and monoclonal antibodies, address the underlying pathophysiology of migraines by modulating the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) pathway. Gepants (e.g. rimegepant) have demonstrated significant efficacy and favorable safety profiles in acute migraine treatment.[11] These options provide individualized strategies to address unmet treatment needs, highlighting the clinical value of new CGRP receptor antagonists in migraine management.

This study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of general neurologists and headache experts in India regarding migraine diagnosis and management. It includes the perceived burden of migraine, awareness of diagnostic tools, treatment guidelines, management strategies, and barriers to optimal care, with the goal of informing strategies to enhance awareness and improve patient outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

A quantitative, cross-sectional, questionnaire-based survey was conducted by Christian Medical College (CMC) Vellore, in collaboration with the Consortium of Accredited Healthcare Organizations (CAHO), to assess the KAP of general neurologists and headache experts on migraine burden, diagnosis, and treatment in India. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines in reporting cross-sectional observational research.[12]

Sample size and recruitment

Convenience and snowball sampling techniques were used to recruit participants from varied clinical settings. A total of 500 general neurologists and 20 headache specialists were invited; 125 general neurologists and 12 headache specialists completed the survey, yielding an overall response rate of 26.3% (25% and 60%, respectively). The unequal distribution reflects the low number of headache subspecialists in routine clinical practice in India. Comparisons between general neurologists and headache experts were descriptive and exploratory, aimed at identifying gaps in migraine care rather than drawing definitive inferential conclusions.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using the formula:

n = Z2×p (1-p)/d2

where Z = 1.96 (for a 95% confidence level), p = the estimated proportion, and d = the margin of error (0.09). Among the KAP components, the maximum required sample size (n = 111 for practice) was considered, and a target of 110 participants was set. However, 137 complete responses were received and included in the final analysis.

Questionnaire design and validation

The structured questionnaire was developed based on a literature review of migraine management practices. It included items on demographics, diagnostic criteria, treatment preferences, and clinical practice patterns (Supplementary Material—Questionnaire). The tool was reviewed and validated by subject experts and pilot-tested on ten general neurologists at CMC, Vellore. Revisions were made based on face validity to ensure clarity and content validity.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected through an online survey (Google Forms and SurveyMonkey®) from October 2024 to April 2025. Participants were invited via email, which included an introductory message and the survey link. The first page contained study information and an electronic informed consent form. The questionnaire consisted mainly of predefined, checkbox-based items, with an “Others (please specify)” option allowing free-text responses where applicable. Only participants who provided consent were able to access the survey. Only complete, anonymized responses were analyzed, and duplicate entries were identified and removed.

Ethical considerations

The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of CMC, Vellore, India (IRB Min. No. 2410122, dated October 16, 2024). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the ICH-GCP guideline,[13] ICMR National Ethical Guidelines (2017),[14] and NDCT Rules (2019).[15] Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participation was voluntary, confidentiality was strictly maintained, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequences.

Statistical analysis

Data were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Missing data were handled using pairwise deletion.

Results

General neurologists were primarily affiliated with corporate hospitals (54; 43.20%), while headache experts (4; 33.33%) were more commonly associated with teaching hospitals or medical colleges. Experts also reported managing a higher weekly volume of migraine patients (10–25 patients/week: general neurologists 72 [57.60%] vs > 25 patients/week: headache experts 9 [75.00%]).

Among general neurologists (n = 125), more than half (68; 54.40%) reported that 10%–25% of patients visiting them presented with headaches as their chief complaint. Similarly, 7 (58.33%) headache experts (n = 12) reported this range, while 5 (41.67%) reported an even higher proportion (25%-50%) of patients presenting with headache [Supplementary Figure 1].

Most general neurologists (50; 40.00%) reported that 10%–15% of their headache patients had migraine, whereas 7 (58.33%) headache experts indicated that more than 20% of headache patients had migraine [Figure 1]. These findings reflect a substantial burden of migraine, with respondents managing a considerable number of patients weekly.

Proportion of headache patients diagnosed with migraine

Most general neurologists (106; 85.00%) reported low aura prevalence (<40% of patients), whereas headache experts (11; 91.67%) more often observed aura in approximately 20% of patients. Episodic migraine (<15 days/month) was most frequently reported in 41%–60% of patients by general neurologists (41; 33.00%) and in 61%–80% by headache experts (7; 58.00%). Chronic migraine (>15 days/month) was commonly reported in 0–20% of patients by general neurologists (43; 34.00%) and in 21%–40% by headache experts (6; 50.00%) [Supplementary Table 1].

Among migraine-associated symptoms, the majority of general neurologists (110; 88.00%) and headache experts (9; 75.00%) reported that patients experienced a combination of symptoms such as nausea, photophobia, and phonophobia. Nausea alone, photophobia alone, and phonophobia alone were reported by 3 (2.40%), 1 (0.80%), and 2 (1.60%) general neurologists, respectively, and by 1 (8.33%), 1 (8.33%), and none of the headache experts, respectively [Figure 2].

Common symptoms associated with migraine

In terms of migraine triggers, stress was the most commonly reported trigger (general neurologists 74 [59.20%]; headache experts 7 [58.33%]), followed by sleep deprivation (58 [46.40%]; 9 [75.00%]), sunlight exposure (38 [30.40%]; 8 [66.67%]), and food-related factors (46 [36.80%]; 3 [25.00%]). Travel and fasting were reported less frequently, while additional triggers were noted by 22 (17.60%) general neurologists and 6 (50.00%) headache experts [Supplementary Figure 2].

Regarding family history of migraine, most general neurologists (47; 37.60%) reported that 41–60% of patients had a positive family history, whereas 7 (58.33%) headache experts reported a prevalence of 21%–40%. Across both groups, mothers were the most commonly affected family members, followed by sisters, brothers, and fathers [Supplementary Table 1].

Adherence to ICHD-3 criteria was markedly higher among headache experts (11; 91.67%) than general neurologists (48; 38.71%). In contrast, general neurologists more frequently relied on non-standardized diagnostic approaches, such as clinical judgment (13; 10.48%) or other methods (26; 20.97%). Disability assessment tools such as Migraine Disability Assessment (MIDAS, 29; 23.39%) were primarily used to assess disease impact rather than for migraine diagnosis.

Non-pharmacological advice was consistently high and comparable in both groups (general neurologists: 113 [90.40%]; headache experts: 11 [91.67%]). Yoga, exercise, and meditation were the most frequently recommended interventions, followed by stress avoidance, dietary adjustments, lifestyle modifications, and other measures. Moderate-to-high NSAID prescribing predominated in both groups, with general neurologists (41; 32.80%) most often reporting use in 61–80% of patients and headache experts (5; 41.67%) in 41%–80%. Very high use (>80%) was observed only among general neurologists. Ergot alkaloids and ditans were infrequently prescribed, with most respondents reporting use in 0–20% of patients [Supplementary Table 2a].

Triptan use was more common at low patient proportions (<20.00%) among headache experts (8; 66.67%) compared to general neurologists (44; 35.20%), while moderate-to-high use (41%–80% of patients) was more common among general neurologists (60; 48.00%). High effectiveness in pain relief was the primary reason for prescribing triptans, reported by 98 (78.40%) general neurologists and 9 (75.00%) headache experts. Among headache experts, the availability of multiple triptan formulations was another key factor (6; 50.00% vs general neurologists 19; 15.2%). Oral tablets remained the preferred route in both groups (general neurologists: 114 [91.20%]; headache experts: 8 [66.67%]), followed by nasal sprays and oral dispersible (wafer) formulations, which were more commonly used by headache experts. Sumatriptan and rizatriptan were the most frequently prescribed agents in both groups (general neurologists: 92 [73.60%] and 75 [60.00%]; headache experts: 8 [66.67%] for both). Eletriptan was not reported in expert practice, while zolmitriptan was used only by headache experts (1; 8.33%).

Pain relief rates clustered in the 50%–75% range for both groups (general neurologists: 56 [44.80%]; headache experts: 5 [41.67%]) [Figure 3]. Headache experts (5; 41.67%) more often advised switching triptans after ≥4 migraine episodes, whereas general neurologists (59; 47.20%) mostly switched after three episodes.

Patients experiencing pain relief with triptan therapy

Use of triptan monotherapy was low in both groups (≤20% of patients: general neurologists 70 [56.00%]; headache experts 7 [58.33%]), and combination therapy with NSAIDs was also generally low (0–20%: headache experts 8 [66.67%] vs general neurologists 51 [40.80%]) [Table 2].

Both groups typically tried two triptans or formulations before deeming the response insufficient (general neurologists: 85 [68.00%]; headache experts: 9 [75.00%]). Contraindications such as coronary artery disease (CAD) (general neurologists: 98 [78.40%]; headache experts: 12 [100%]) and cerebrovascular disease (CVD) (general neurologists: 94 [75.20%]; headache experts: 11 [91.67%]) strongly influenced treatment avoidance. Insufficient response to triptans was reported in >30% of patients in headache expert practice (5; 41.67%), whereas it was most commonly reported in 10%–20% of patients by general neurologists (49; 39.20%) [Figure 4]. Self-medication before consultation was common, especially among patients of headache experts (6; 50.00%). Disability scales were used more frequently by headache experts (8; 66.67%) than general neurologists (30; 24.20%), with time constraints cited as a major barrier by general neurologists who did not use them (76; 80.90%). A small proportion also reported non-standardized approaches, such as migraine diaries, nerve testing, and IHS-related clinical methods, highlighting practice gaps and the need for focused medical education on standardized assessment tools. Comorbidities limited treatment options in 10%–15% of patients, as reported by 52 (41.60%) general neurologists and 4 (33.30%) headache experts [Supplementary Tables 2a and 2b].

Triptan inadequate response or triptan resistance

Experience-based stratification of general neurologists

General neurologists with >10 years of practice more frequently employed combination and non-oral treatment strategies and reported a higher burden of triptan resistance and complex management needs. In contrast, clinicians with <10 years of experience demonstrated greater reliance on NSAIDs and oral formulations, and conducted fewer triptan trials before defining insufficient response. These findings reflected experience-dependent differences in prescribing patterns, treatment response, and the impact of comorbidities on therapeutic decision-making [Supplementary Tables 3a and 3b].

Discussion

This cross-sectional study assessed the KAP of general neurologists and headache experts in India regarding migraine management, providing insights into clinical experience, practice settings, patient burden, diagnostic approaches, and treatment strategies.

Headache experts provided more comprehensive care in specialty settings, whereas general neurologists in corporate hospitals often faced time constraints that limited detailed assessments. Headache experts demonstrated substantially higher adherence to the ICHD-3 diagnostic criteria (91.67%) compared with general neurologists (38.71%). In contrast, general neurologists more frequently relied on non-standardized approaches, including clinical judgment and disability assessment tools such as the MIDAS scale, which are intended to quantify disease impact rather than establish diagnosis. Approximately 23% of general neurologists reported using MIDAS as a diagnostic tool. Although this reflects self-reported practice rather than the intended role of MIDAS, it indicates a meaningful knowledge and implementation gap. Using a disability assessment tool instead of standardized diagnostic criteria may lead to misclassification, inconsistent treatment decisions, and variability in clinical practice, potentially affecting optimal patient care. Similar diagnostic gaps have been reported previously; a study in eastern India found that 25% of migraine cases were misdiagnosed by neurologists, emphasizing the need for standardized diagnostic training.[16] These findings highlight the need to strengthen awareness of the distinct roles of ICHD-3 criteria for diagnosis and tools such as MIDAS for disability assessment, particularly among general neurologists. Previous studies also indicate that a majority of neurologists (91%) correctly diagnose migraine based on clinical vignettes.[17] Standardized frameworks such as ICHD-3 improve diagnostic accuracy when combined with structured patient interviews and guide tailored acute treatment, appropriate prophylaxis, and integration of trigger management alongside non-pharmacological strategies to support comprehensive, patient-centered care.[18]

Stress was the most frequently cited trigger (59.20% of general neurologists and 58.33% of headache experts), followed by sleep deprivation and sunlight exposure, consistent with previous studies identifying stress and sleep disturbances as key migraine triggers.[19] Nonpharmacological advice was provided at high rates in both groups, highlighting the importance of lifestyle modification and trigger avoidance in migraine management.[20]

Pharmacological treatment patterns varied between groups. NSAID prescribing was common in both groups, with moderate-to-high use (41%–80%) most frequent, while very high use (>80%) was observed only among general neurologists. A study from a tertiary care hospital in Bhubaneswar, India, reported frequent NSAID use, with naproxen being the most commonly prescribed, followed by ibuprofen and etoricoxib.[21] Triptans were prescribed at moderate-to-high levels more often by general neurologists (48.00% for 41%–80% of patients) compared to headache experts, among whom low use (0–20%) was more common (66.67%). This could reflect differences in patient severity, as headache experts may reserve triptans for specific cases due to contraindications.[22] Oral tablets and nasal sprays were the most commonly preferred formulations; however, the higher use of wafer formulations by headache experts (25.00% vs. 8.00%) suggests greater familiarity with alternative delivery methods. Studies evaluating the efficacy and safety of triptans have predominantly focused on oral formulations, including tablets and orally disintegrating tablets.

Contraindications such as CAD and CVD were major reasons for avoiding triptans, particularly among headache experts. A study showed that ≥20% of patients have a CVD that specifically contraindicates triptan use.[23] No general neurologists reported avoiding triptans in patients with peripheral arterial disease, indicating a critical knowledge gap in recognizing cardiovascular contraindications and the need for improved guideline awareness and risk stratification. Triptan resistance was more prevalent in headache expert practice (41.67% reporting >30%) than among general neurologists (39.20% reporting 10%–20%), possibly due to the higher proportion of chronic migraine and heavily pre-treated cases seen by headache experts. General neurologists used the MIDAS scale selectively rather than routinely due to time constraints, whereas higher rates of self-medication among patients of headache experts highlight the need for better patient education to prevent medication overuse. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Rashid et al.[24] reported a high prevalence of self-medication (53.57%), with headache as a common indication and NSAIDs among the most frequently used drugs. Although triptans are widely used for acute migraine, the absence of standardized national guidelines in India leads to variability in defining treatment response.[25] Most general neurologists (68.00%) and headache experts (75.00%) required trials of two triptans or formulations before defining a patient as a “non-responder,” while smaller proportions did so after just one trial (10.40% vs. 16.67%). This inconsistency may contribute to triptan overuse and suboptimal outcomes in patients with inadequate relief, frequent relapse, or treatment-limiting adverse effects.

International guidelines offer standardized criteria for assessing triptan efficacy. The European Headache Federation (2022) defines triptan responders as those achieving effective relief in ≥3 of 4 attacks, whereas the IHS (2024) evaluates response over three attacks and considers treatment ineffective if no meaningful benefit occurs in ≥2 attacks. Both recommend trying up to three triptans before switching classes.[2627]

In contrast to these international recommendations, many Indian neurologists continue prescribing triptans beyond 3–4 episodes before switching, potentially prolonging patient suffering. In the present study, 47.20% of general neurologists continued triptans for three episodes and 20.80% for four episodes, compared with 25.00% and 41.67% of headache experts, respectively. With increasing experience, neurologists tend to adopt more targeted prescribing, optimize counseling and formulation selection, and trial multiple formulations before defining non-response, helping distinguish true resistance from suboptimal use. To address these challenges, a consensus framework developed with Indian headache experts is essential to standardize definitions of triptan response, non-response, and resistance. Clear and uniform criteria would help general neurologists identify non-responders early, optimize treatment duration, and guide systematic cycling across triptans. Such standardization would reduce undertreatment, avoid inconsistent switching, and prevent prolonged ineffective triptan use, supporting more rational, evidence-based, and patient-centered migraine management in India.[28]

The addition of novel therapies such as CGRP antagonists, including gepants, offers a promising, targeted advancement in migraine management compared with conventional treatments. Evidence shows that these agents reduce attack frequency and symptom burden in patients who are unresponsive or intolerant to standard options. Integrating these agents into treatment regimens can improve disease control, enhance quality of life, and reduce reliance on acute medications, thereby lowering the risk of medication overuse and related complications.[29]

Overall, the observed differences in diagnostic and treatment practices highlight the need for improved training, wider use of ICHD-3 criteria, and better resource allocation for migraine care in India. Strengthening Continuing Medical Education (CME) programs, expanding headache services, and promoting structured assessments may enhance timely and effective management.

Limitations

The findings related to headache experts were compared with those of general neurologists to identify gaps in current migraine care in the country. However, voluntary online recruitment and self-reported data may introduce selection and reporting bias, thereby limiting generalizability. Furthermore, important migraine triggers such as menstruation[30] were not assessed in the survey. Additionally, attitudes toward migraine management were not assessed using validated Likert-scale instruments, limiting insights into clinician decision-making. Future studies should include larger, more balanced samples and longitudinal designs to evaluate evolving practice patterns.

Conclusions

This study highlights that both general neurologists and headache experts in India are aware of the burden of migraine and report using a combination of pharmacological and non-pharmacological strategies. However, differences exist in the use of diagnostic tools, treatment preferences, and adoption of newer therapies, including triptans. These findings highlight the need to enhance awareness and clinical familiarity with emerging migraine therapies. Continuous medical education programs and updated clinical resources may help bridge existing gaps and support evidence-based migraine management among physicians in India.
