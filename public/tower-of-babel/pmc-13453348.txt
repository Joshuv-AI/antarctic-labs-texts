Nighttime Sleep Symptoms and Sleep-Relevant Medication Documentation Among Adults Receiving Buprenorphine for Opioid Use Disorder
Authors: Austin M Jones, Michelle Eglovitch, Pablo Soto, Amber R Green, Madison M Marcus, Joseph M Dzierzewski, Maha Alattar, Caitlin E Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep-related symptoms are common among people receiving medications for opioid use disorder (OUD), including buprenorphine. In outpatient addiction treatment, nighttime sleep complaints are often evaluated alongside active medication lists and recent substance exposures. This short report described patient-reported nighttime sleep symptoms and sleep-relevant medication class documentation among adults receiving buprenorphine for OUD.MethodsThis cross-sectional study used survey data and medical-record abstraction from adults receiving buprenorphine for OUD at an academic addiction clinic from February 2022 to September 2023. Nighttime sleep symptoms were characterized using Insomnia Severity Index items 1–3: difficulty falling asleep, difficulty staying asleep, and waking too early. Participants were categorized as No-Sx, Single-Sx, or Multi-Sx. Sleep-relevant medications documented on the active outpatient medication list were grouped into eight classes. Sample characteristics, nighttime symptom groups, medication class documentation, and urine drug testing results within 30 days were summarized descriptively.ResultsAmong study participants (n = 136), 72.1% endorsed at least one nighttime sleep symptom. Symptom group distribution was No-Sx (27.9%), Single-Sx (11.0%), and Multi-Sx (61.0%). In the Multi-Sx group, gabapentinoids were most frequently documented (34.9%), followed by sedating antidepressants (26.5%), sedating antihistamines (22.9%), and adrenergic agents (22.9%). Cannabinoids were detected in 46.2% of participants with available urine drug testing results.ConclusionNighttime sleep symptoms and sleep-relevant medication documentation were common in this outpatient buprenorphine-treated OUD sample. Findings support routine nighttime sleep symptom assessment, together with medication review and consideration of recent substance exposure, to help identify clinically relevant sleep concerns and inform safer outpatient buprenorphine care.

Introduction

Sleep disturbance is highly prevalent (51–75%) among people with opioid use disorder (OUD), including individuals receiving medications for OUD such as buprenorphine.1–4 These symptoms may complicate outpatient treatment through associations with symptom burden, co-occurring pain and psychiatric symptoms, and recovery-related functioning.1–3 In office-based buprenorphine treatment, clinicians often evaluate nighttime sleep complaints while also reconciling active medications and reviewing recent substance use. Because sleep complaints are managed within this broader addiction-care context, describing patient-reported nighttime symptoms alongside documented sleep-relevant or potentially sedating medications may provide clinically useful information for medication review and safety in outpatient OUD care.5

Nighttime sleep concerns are commonly identified through patient-reported symptoms such as difficulty falling asleep, difficulty staying asleep, and waking too early. These symptoms can be assessed using validated self-report measures such as the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI).6 Pharmacotherapy for sleep complaints is also encountered in routine care, but medication review is especially important among patients receiving buprenorphine because sedating medications and other central nervous system depressants can increase risks of sedation and respiratory depression when combined with opioids.7–10 In addition, cannabis use is common among people receiving medications for OUD, and sleep-related concerns are among the reasons some individuals report cannabis use.11–13 Recent substance exposures may therefore provide clinically relevant context that is not captured by the active medication list alone.

Prior claims-based work examined sleep-related medication prescriptions among buprenorphine-treated adults with OUD using diagnostic codes for insomnia.14 However, coded diagnoses, prescription claims, active medication lists, and patient-reported nighttime symptoms do not always align in routine care, and active outpatient medication lists typically do not specify prescribing rationale.15–17 Accordingly, this study aimed to: (1) estimate the prevalence of patient-reported nighttime sleep symptoms among adults receiving buprenorphine for OUD; (2) describe sleep-relevant medication class documentation on the active outpatient medication list; and (3) describe how medication class documentation varied across nighttime symptom groups.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This cross-sectional analysis used survey data and medical-record abstraction from adults receiving buprenorphine for OUD at an academic addiction clinic.18 Clinical data were abstracted from participants’ electronic medical records as part of a larger study investigating sleep in OUD recovery. All study procedures were approved by the Virginia Commonwealth University Institutional Review Board (HM20023390) and were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants

The analytic sample included 136 adults enrolled between February 2022 and September 2023. Participants were recruited during routine outpatient treatment visits. Eligible individuals were 18 to 65 years old and had been stabilized on a buprenorphine prescription for at least six weeks before enrollment. Individuals unable to complete informed consent procedures or with serious comorbid cognitive or psychiatric impairment were excluded.

Nighttime Sleep Symptoms

Nighttime sleep symptoms were assessed using the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI), a seven-item self-report measure scored from 0 to 4 per item, with higher scores indicating greater insomnia symptom severity.6 To focus on nighttime sleep symptoms rather than the broader ISI total-score construct, this analysis used ISI items 1–3 as individual variables: difficulty falling asleep, difficulty staying asleep, and waking too early. As an example, ISI item 1 asks participants to rate the current (ie, last 2 weeks) severity of “Difficulty falling asleep” on a 0–4 scale from “None” to “Very Severe”. For each item, responses were dichotomized as not endorsed (0–1) versus endorsed (2–4). Participants were categorized into three mutually exclusive nighttime symptom groups based on the number of endorsed nighttime symptom items: No-Sx (0 items), Single-Sx (1 item), and Multi-Sx (≥2 items).

Medication and Substance-Exposure Measures

Medication information was obtained from the active outpatient medication list in the electronic medical record at the time of abstraction (EPIC). Documented medications were grouped into eight sleep-relevant or potentially sedating medication classes informed by clinical insomnia guidance: benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, melatonin-related agents, sedating antihistamines, gabapentinoids, sedating antidepressants, antipsychotics, and adrenergic agents.7,8 Medication class assignment was conducted through manual review of active medication names and therapeutic classes, with classification decisions guided by clinical insomnia guidance and reviewed by the study team for consistency. Medication class documentation was defined as the presence of one or more active medications within a class. Because active medication lists do not confirm prescribing indication, adherence, or timing of use, medication classes were interpreted as documentation patterns rather than confirmed sleep-directed treatment. Routine urine drug testing (UDT) results documented in the medical record within 30 days of study assessment were summarized descriptively to characterize recent substance detection beyond the active medication list.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the sample, nighttime sleep symptoms, medication class documentation, and recent UDT results. Means and standard deviations were used for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages were used for categorical variables. Medication class documentation was summarized overall and by nighttime symptom group. Missing values were reported descriptively, and analyses used available data. Responses to ISI items 1–3 were partially missing for three participants; no participant was missing all three items. For nighttime symptom group classification, partially missing ISI item responses were treated as not endorsed.

Based on ISI items 1–3, 72.1% of participants (n = 98) endorsed at least one nighttime sleep symptom. Nighttime symptom group distribution was No-Sx (n = 38, 27.9%), Single-Sx (n = 15, 11.0%), and Multi-Sx (n = 83, 61.0%). Individual nighttime symptoms were also common and not mutually exclusive: difficulty falling asleep was endorsed by 51.5% of participants, difficulty staying asleep by 60.3%, and waking too early by 61.8%. Specific symptom patterns within the Single-Sx group are shown in Supplemental Table S1.

Routine clinical UDT results were available for 130 participants (95.6%), including 35 in the No-Sx group, 15 in the Single-Sx group, and 80 in the Multi-Sx group. Overall, cannabinoids were detected in 46.2% of participants with available UDT results, followed by cocaine (17.7%), benzodiazepines (10.0%), and methamphetamine metabolites (6.9%). By nighttime symptom group, cannabinoid detection was 48.6% in the No-Sx group, 53.3% in the Single-Sx group, and 43.8% in the Multi-Sx group. Cocaine, benzodiazepine, and methamphetamine metabolite detection were less frequent and were observed in 17.1%, 11.4%, and 5.7% of the No-Sx group; 33.3%, 13.3%, and 20.0% of the Single-Sx group; and 15.0%, 8.8%, and 5.0% of the Multi-Sx group, respectively.

Discussion

In this outpatient sample of adults receiving buprenorphine for OUD, nighttime sleep symptoms and documented sleep-relevant medication classes were common. Most participants endorsed at least one nighttime symptom, and more than half endorsed multiple nighttime symptoms. This symptom burden was higher than insomnia prevalence reported in claims-based analyses using ICD diagnosis codes, which is consistent with evidence that patient-reported symptoms and diagnostic coding may not fully align in routine care.1,14,18,19 Clinically, these findings support routine assessment of nighttime sleep complaints in buprenorphine-treated adults, particularly because sleep concerns may arise alongside medication burden, psychiatric symptoms, pain, and recent substance exposure.

Medication class documentation varied across nighttime symptom groups, but these patterns should be interpreted as markers of real-world clinical context rather than evidence of insomnia-specific treatment selection. Higher documentation of gabapentinoids, sedating antidepressants, sedating antihistamines, and adrenergic agents among participants in the Multi-Sx group may reflect greater overall symptom burden and clinical complexity, including co-occurring pain and psychiatric symptoms that commonly accompany sleep disturbance among individuals receiving medication for OUD.3,4 Conversely, sleep-relevant medication classes documented in the No-Sx group may reflect non-sleep indications, prior nighttime sleep disturbance that is currently controlled, or sedating pharmacologic effects unrelated to the reason for prescribing. Because the active medication list does not identify prescribing rationale, these findings are best understood as documentation patterns relevant to medication review, not as evidence of sleep-directed pharmacotherapy.

These documentation patterns also differ from prior administrative claims work. Martin et al (2023) identified substantially higher benzodiazepine and Z-drug prescription receipt among buprenorphine-treated individuals classified as having insomnia using diagnostic codes. In contrast, Z-drugs were infrequently documented in the present clinical sample, and benzodiazepines were documented in a minority of participants. Gabapentinoids and sedating antidepressants were more commonly documented, particularly among participants endorsing multiple nighttime symptoms. These differences may reflect variation in how sleep problems are identified across data sources, including ICD-coded diagnoses, patient-reported symptoms, prescription claims, and active medication lists, as well as broader shifts in sedative prescribing patterns over time.15,16,20 Importantly, neither claims data nor active medication lists can determine prescribing indication with certainty.17

Recent substance detection further situates sleep-related symptom assessment within the broader substance-use context of outpatient OUD care. Nearly half of participants with available UDT data had cannabis detected. Cannabis use is common among people receiving medications for OUD, and sleep-related concerns are among the reasons some individuals report cannabis use.11–13 However, this study did not assess reason for cannabis use, timing, frequency, product characteristics, or route of administration. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that participants were using cannabis specifically to manage sleep symptoms. Instead, the high prevalence of cannabis detection suggests that evaluation of nighttime sleep symptoms and medication documentation may require consideration of recent substance exposures beyond the active medication list alone.

These findings have practical implications for outpatient buprenorphine treatment. Nighttime sleep complaints may coexist with documented benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, sedating antidepressants, antihistamines, antipsychotics, adrenergic agents, and other sedating medications. Medication reconciliation may therefore be useful when patients report sleep concerns, especially given official safety communications from the US Food and Drug Administration regarding sedation and respiratory depression risks when opioids are combined with benzodiazepines or gabapentinoids.9,10 In routine care, assessment of nighttime sleep symptoms may be most clinically useful when paired with review of the active medication list and consideration of recent substance exposure.

Several limitations should be considered. This was a descriptive, cross-sectional analysis from a single outpatient addiction clinic, and findings should not be interpreted as causal or generalizable to all OUD treatment settings. Medication classes were abstracted from the active outpatient medication list, which may not reflect prescribing indication, adherence, timing of medication use, or whether medications were taken for sleep-related reasons. The Single-Sx group was modest in size, and several medication classes were infrequently documented; therefore, inferential comparisons across symptom groups were not performed. UDT results provided additional clinical context but did not fully characterize non-prescribed substance use, including frequency, timing, product characteristics, route of administration, or reasons for use. Future longitudinal and intervention studies could address these limitations by evaluating whether sleep symptom assessment, paired with medication and substance-exposure review, improves identification of sleep-related concerns, medication safety, and OUD treatment outcomes.

Conclusions

Among adults receiving buprenorphine for OUD in an outpatient addiction clinic, nighttime sleep symptoms were common and sleep-relevant medication classes were frequently documented on the active medication list. Incorporating routine nighttime sleep symptom assessment into outpatient buprenorphine care, alongside careful medication review and consideration of recent substance-exposure context, may help identify clinically relevant sleep concerns and inform safer OUD care.
