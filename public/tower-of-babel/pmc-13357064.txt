Ailanthone as a novel AKT1 inhibitor that induces mitochondrial apoptosis and exerts anti-tumor activity in cervical cancer
Authors: Shaofeng Huang, Yanlun Song, Yun Zhao, Xin Zhang, Yannan Jiao, Jin Zhang, Yuehua Huang, Junli Wang, Haimei Qin, Fenglian Yang, Rong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs a significant global burden on women’s health, cervical cancer necessitates the exploration of novel therapeutic strategies driven by the limitations inherent in the current treatment options. Ailanthone (AIL), a natural compound derived from Ailanthus altissima, has significant inhibitory effects against various tumor types. However, its function in cervical cancer and associated molecular mechanisms are yet to be elucidated. This study aims to explore the regulatory effect of AIL on cervical cancer and clarify its potential value in anti-tumor therapy.MethodsWe evaluated the antitumor activity of AIL in cervical cancer through a series of in vitro cellular experiments using the human cervical cancer cell line HeLa and in vivo animal models using female BALB/c mice. We also investigated the mechanism of action by using network pharmacology and molecular docking techniques. Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA) was then performed to validate the findings.ResultsThe in vitro experiments conducted in HeLa cells have shown that AIL effectively suppressed the proliferative, migratory, and invasive capacities of HeLa cells. Furthermore, it triggered cell cycle arrest in the G0/G1 phase and promoted apoptosis. Xenograft models in nude mice further confirmed the potent inhibitory effects of AIL on tumor growth. Network pharmacology and molecular docking techniques predicted the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-protein kinase B (AKT) signaling pathway as a potential key pathway and identified AKT1 as a core target protein. The CETSA demonstrated that, under gradually increasing temperature, the thermal stability of AKT1 in the AIL-treated group was stronger than that in the dimethyl sulfoxide-treated control group, verifying the previous prediction.ConclusionsOur findings from the cervical cancer cell model indicate that the anti-tumor effect of AIL against cervical cancer may be dependent on AKT1 protein regulation and intracellular apoptotic pathway activation.

Introduction

Cervical cancer represents a significant global health burden among gynecological malignancies, largely due to persistent infection with oncogenic types of human papillomaviruses (HPV), such as HPV16 and HPV18 (1). According to the GLOBOCAN 2022 report of the International Agency for Research on Cancer (2), new cases of cervical cancer account for 6.5% of all female malignancies globally, with a relative mortality rate of 11.3%. A substantial proportion of cervical cancer patients (around 67%) were initially diagnosed at an advanced stage of the disease. Notably, the overall survival rate of patients with cervical cancer remains relatively low despite receiving standardized comprehensive treatment (3). Therefore, the development of innovative treatment strategies with high efficacy and low toxicity is an unmet need in oncology.

Natural compounds have significant research value and broad application in drug development, particularly for the discovery of anti-tumor agents (4). Ailanthone (AIL), a natural active component isolated and purified from Ailanthus altissima, exhibits diverse biological activities, including anti-inflammatory (5), antiviral (6), and anti-tumor effects (7,8). In recent years, growing evidence has suggested that the anti-tumor activity of AIL involves multiple mechanisms. For instance, Ding et al. (9) demonstrated that AIL effectively inhibits colon cancer cell viability primarily by mediating the STAT3 signaling pathway and initiating the caspase cascade through the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. In osteosarcoma, AIL potently inhibits cellular proliferation, migration, and invasion through modulation of the serine biosynthesis pathway, concomitant with the induction of G0/G1 phase cell cycle arrest and promotion of apoptosis (10). Additionally, AIL can induce ferroptosis by elevating lipid peroxides and Fe2+ levels, promoting GPX4 degradation, and reducing antioxidant enzyme activity (11). Moreover, AIL exhibits notable anti-proliferative effects in HL-60 cells, likely through the downregulation of LC3-I and p62 expression and activation of autophagy-related pathways, thereby triggering apoptosis (12). These findings collectively indicate that AIL has promising potential as an anti-tumor agent; however, its therapeutic potential in cervical cancer remains unexplored.

Network pharmacology is a research paradigm based on systems biology. It integrates multi-omics data and analytical bioinformatics to systematically analyze the complex interaction networks between drugs, targets, and diseases, providing a theoretical basis and methodological framework for elucidating the multi-target synergistic mechanisms of drugs (13). In this study, we conducted cell-based experiments and animal models to assess the antitumor activity of AIL in cervical cancer and used the network pharmacology approach to investigate its mechanism of action. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR and ARRIVE reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0508/rc).

Methods

Cell lines and drugs

The human cervical cancer cell line HeLa was provided by BDBIO Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. They were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. AIL with a purity of 99.93%, sourced from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China), was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO).

Cell viability assay

Cells were seeded at a concentration of 5,000 cells per well in a 96-well dish and were left to attach for a 12-hour period. The next step involved exposing the cells to varying concentrations of AIL for 48 hours. Post-treatment, the medium was removed and substituted with a serum-devoid medium including 10% CCK-8, followed by a 2-hour incubation. The extent of proliferation was assessed numerically by the absorbance readings at 450 nm, taken per well with the microplate reader.

Clone formation experiments

HeLa cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells/well. After complete attachment, the cells were treated with 50 nM and 100 nM of AIL and cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 14 days. During the culture period, the medium was replaced every 3–4 days with fresh medium containing the corresponding drug concentrations. After treatment with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 minutes, the cells then underwent staining with crystal violet for an extra 10 minutes. Stained samples were rinsed with water, air-dried, and photographed to evaluate their ability to form colonies.

Cell-cycle analysis

HeLa cells were plated at 5×105 per well in a 6-well dish and left overnight to allow attachment. The following day, the cells were exposed to various concentrations of AIL for 48 hours. After treatment, cells were collected by enzymatic detachment, fixed in 75% ethanol, and stored at 4 °C for 24 hours. Any remaining ethanol was removed by centrifugation, and 500 µL of propidium iodide (PI) staining solution was added. The samples were incubated in the dark for 30 minutes to ensure proper staining. To evaluate how AIL influences cell-cycle progression, the samples were analyzed on an Attune NxT flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and cell-cycle distribution was determined using ModFit LT 5.0 software.

Scratch experiment

HeLa cells were seeded into culture inserts (ibidi GmbH, Gräfelfing, Germany) at a density of 3×104 cells per well and cultured overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. When the cells reached nearly 100% confluence, the inserts were carefully removed. The medium was then replaced with 1% fetal bovine serum-supplemented medium containing AIL. Microscopic images were captured immediately after creating the wound (0 h) and again at 24 hours post-treatment from the same field of view to monitor and document cell migration. The results were analyzed to evaluate the effects of AIL on the migratory capacity of HeLa cells.

Transwell experiments

HeLa cell suspensions were prepared in a basal medium containing AIL. Subsequently, 4×104 cells were loaded into the upper chamber of Matrigel-precoated Transwell inserts. The lower chamber was supplemented with 700 µL of complete medium as a chemoattractant. After a 24-hour incubation (37 °C, 5% CO2), non-invading cells remaining on the upper surface of the membrane were gently scraped off with a cotton swab. Subsequently, cells that had migrated to the lower surface were immobilized with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes and then stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for an additional 15 minutes. Following staining, the membrane inserts were carefully rinsed with distilled water, air-dried, and imaged by microscopy to quantify the suppressive effect of AIL on cellular invasion.

Cell-apoptosis analysis

HeLa cells were plated at 5×105 cells per well in a 6-well plate and left to incubate overnight under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). Following 48 hours of AIL treatment, the cells were trypsinized, collected, and resuspended in 500 µL of 1× binding buffer per sample. To each suspension, 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 µL of PI were added, and the mixtures were incubated in the dark for 10 minutes. Apoptosis levels were measured by flow cytometry, and the results were analyzed with FlowJo v10.8.1 to evaluate the pro-apoptotic impact of AIL on cervical cancer cells.

Network pharmacological analysis

The molecular structure of AIL was submitted to the PharmMapper database (https://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/) to predict potential target proteins. Using “cervical cancer” as the keyword, relevant therapeutic targets were retrieved from the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com/) databases. Results were filtered based on the “Relevance Score ≥30” criterion provided by the GeneCards database. Subsequently, the nomenclature of selected target proteins was standardized using the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/), and their information was unified. Standardized AIL potential targets and cervical cancer-associated targets were imported into the Venn diagram tool (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/) to identify and extract common targets between the two groups. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The overlapping targets were then submitted to the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) with species restricted to “human” and a confidence threshold of ≥0.7 to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network. The PPI network was visualized using Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1), and its topological parameters were analyzed with built-in network analysis tools. Node size and color intensity were adjusted based on degree values to visually represent each node’s importance within the network. Finally, Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the common target gene set using an online bioinformatics platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/) to clarify potential biological roles and principal signaling pathways.

Molecular docking and visualization

The key protein AKT1 was selected as the receptor, and its three-dimensional structure file in PDB format was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). The obtained protein structure was preprocessed using the PyMOL software, including the removal of water molecules, addition of hydrogen atoms, and elimination of small-molecule ligands. The initial structure of AIL in mol2 format was retrieved from the TCMSP database and preprocessed through the addition of hydrogen atoms with AutoDockTools 1.5.7. Computational docking was then carried out employing AutoDock Vina, and the resulting docking poses were subjected to visualization and analysis using PyMOL.

Western blot analysis

After 48 hours of incubation with AIL, cells were lysed with RIPA buffer to extract proteins supplemented with 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were then assessed using the BCA assay kit. For each sample, 30 µg of protein were resolved by SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto a PVDF membrane. Membranes were blocked in 5% bovine serum albumin at room temperature for 1 hour and then exposed overnight at 4 °C to the following primary antibodies: β-actin (66009-1-Ig, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) (10205-2-AP, Proteintech), cyclin D1 (ET1601-31, HUABIO, Hangzhou, China), cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4) (ET1612-23, HUABIO), E-cadherin (20874-1-AP, Proteintech), protein kinase B (AKT) (10176-2-AP, Proteintech), phosphorylated AKT (T40067S, Abmart, Shanghai, China), N-cadherin (22018-1-AP, Proteintech), Bcl-xL (ET1603-28, HUABIO), Cytochrome c (ET1610-60, HUABIO), Cleaved Caspase-9 (ER60008, HUABIO), Cleaved Caspase-3 (ET1602-47, HUABIO), and cleaved poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1) (BM3941, Boster, Wuhan, China). The following day, secondary antibodies, diluted as instructed, were mixed with the membranes. The presence of immunoreactive strips was detected via an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system.

Establishment of xenograft tumor models and drug intervention experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Youjiang Medical University for Nationalities (Approval No. 2024081601) and conducted in accordance with the “Regulations on the Management of Experimental Animals” and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. Female Bagg Albino (BALB/c) mice (5 weeks old, SPF grade) were procured from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) (Batch No.: 44829700037597). Following 1 week of acclimatization under standard housing conditions, each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of 100 µL of phosphate-buffered saline suspension containing 5×106 HeLa cells into the left axillary region. When tumor volumes reached 100–200 mm3, the experimental mice were randomly divided into two groups (n=5 in each) using a computer-generated random number method: the treatment group received daily intraperitoneal injections of AIL (5 mg/kg in 200 µL vehicle) (14), whereas the control group received an equal volume of vehicle alone. The administration continued for 21 days, with body weight and tumor volume recorded every 3 days. The maximum allowable tumor burden in this study was defined as 1,500 mm3, and no experimental animals had tumors exceeding this threshold. At the end of the treatment period, all animals were euthanized via cervical dislocation under 4% isoflurane anesthesia. Tumor tissues, along with liver and kidney samples, were collected for subsequent histopathological examination.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining and immunohistochemistry

Fresh tissue specimens were promptly fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and processed into paraffin blocks. Five-micron sections were cut and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological assessment under light microscopy.

For immunohistochemistry, consecutive 5 µm sections were dewaxed and rehydrated through a graded alcohol series. Antigen retrieval was performed with citrate buffer (pH 6.0) under heat. To curb nonspecific binding, sections were blocked with bovine serum albumin at room temperature. Primary antibody incubation was carried out overnight at 4 °C. Following washes, sections were incubated with species-matched secondary antibodies for 30 minutes at room temperature. Detection used diaminobenzidine as the chromogen, and nuclei were lightly counterstained with hematoxylin. All stained slides were then imaged and evaluated using bright-field microscopy.

Statistical analyses

Cell experiments included three biological replicates per group, whereas animal experiments included five mice per group. All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0) was used for all the statistical analyses. Intergroup differences were assessed using an unpaired Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons, whereas a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test was used for multiple comparisons between each treatment group and the control group. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

Results

AIL suppresses the growth of cervical cancer cells

The molecular structure of AIL is shown in Figure 1A. After treating HeLa cells with different concentrations of AIL, CCK-8 assays revealed that AIL markedly reduced HeLa cell viability in a concentration-dependent fashion, exhibiting a half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 63.92±7.73 nM after 48 hours of treatment (Figure 1B,1C). Examination under an inverted microscope revealed that HeLa cells treated with AIL for 48 hours exhibited typical changes, such as cell shrinkage, reduced volume, and sparse arrangement (Figure 1D). To assess the long-term anti-proliferative effect of AIL, a colony formation assay was conducted on HeLa cells, which revealed dose-dependent suppression of colony formation (Figure 1E). Finally, western blotting was performed to examine the expression of the cell proliferation marker PCNA, which was significantly downregulated after drug treatment (Figure 1F).

AIL induced cell cycle arrest in cervical cancer cells

To investigate the mechanisms underlying the antiproliferative effects of AIL, we analyzed the cell cycle distribution in HeLa cells following AIL treatment. After 48 hours of AIL exposure, a notable increase in the population of cells arrested in the G0/G1 phase was observed (Figure 2A). To further investigate the underlying mechanism controlling cell cycle progression in this phase, we assessed the expression of key regulatory proteins associated with the G0/G1 phase using western blotting. The results indicated that AIL treatment markedly upregulated the protein expression of P21 while significantly downregulating the expression of CDK4 and Cyclin D1 relative to the control (Figure 2B).

The proportions of cells in each phase were quantified using ModFit software. (B) The protein expression levels of P21, Cyclin D1, and CDK4 were detected by western blotting. ***, P<0.001. AIL inhibited the migration and invasion of cervical cancer cells

Building upon the verified antiproliferative effects of AIL on cervical cancer HeLa cells, we systematically investigated its inhibitory effects on cell migration and invasion. A wound healing assay demonstrated that after 24 hours of treatment with AIL, AIL-treated HeLa cells exhibited a significantly impaired ability to close the wound compared with the control (Figure 3A). To further evaluate the invasive potential, a Transwell Matrigel invasion assay was performed and it revealed that AIL treatment inhibited the transmembrane ability of cervical cancer cells in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 3B). Collectively, these findings indicated that AIL significantly suppressed the migratory and invasive phenotypes of HeLa cells. To explore the molecular mechanisms involved, we evaluated the expression of critical epithelial-mesenchymal transition-associated molecules. Western blotting showed that AIL treatment dose-dependently increased E-cadherin expression and markedly downregulated N-cadherin expression in HeLa cells (Figure 3C). These molecular changes suggest that AIL effectively inhibits the metastatic potential of cervical cancer cells by reversing the epithelial-mesenchymal transition.

AIL induced apoptosis in cervical cancer cells

In previous experiments, AIL markedly suppressed the proliferation of HeLa cells through the induction of G0/G1 phase cell cycle arrest. Concurrently, the morphological changes observed in AIL-treated cells were consistent with those typically observed during apoptosis. To further determine whether this anti-proliferative effect was associated with apoptosis, we performed an apoptosis analysis using Annexin V-FITC/PI dual staining and analyzed the apoptosis levels using flow cytometry. The results indicated that the apoptotic rate of HeLa cells increased significantly in a dose-dependent manner following AIL treatment (Figure 4A). Western blot analysis was conducted to assess the expression of key apoptosis-associated proteins. Compared with control, AIL treatment significantly downregulated the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-xL and markedly upregulated the expression of the pro-apoptotic proteins Bax, cytochrome c, cleaved caspase-3, cleaved caspase-9, and cleaved PARP1 (Figure 4B).

Network pharmacology analysis and molecular docking results

To map the potential mechanisms of action, the signaling pathways and molecular targets of AIL were systematically predicted using a network pharmacology-based approach. A total of 53 common targets related to AIL and cervical cancer were identified (Figure 5A). A PPI network of these potential targets was generated using the STRING database. Visualization and topological analysis to identify the core nodes were performed using Cytoscape (Figure 5B). The results indicated that AKT1 had the highest degree value of 56 interaction edges, suggesting it as a key target of AIL in cervical cancer. Further GO functional and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the common targets. As shown in Figure 5C,5D, GO enrichment analysis revealed that the potential targets were significantly enriched in biological processes including epithelial cell proliferation and extrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway, while KEGG pathway enrichment analysis suggested that the anti-cervical cancer activity of AIL is likely mediated through regulation of the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-AKT signaling cascade. Notably, molecular docking studies (Figure 6A) confirmed a specific binding affinity between AIL and AKT1, with a calculated binding energy of −7.74 kcal/mol, and a corresponding inhibition constant of 2.11 μM, indicating strong binding affinity and stability between the two molecules.

AIL exerts its anti-cervical cancer effects by targeting AKT1

Network pharmacological predictions suggested that AIL likely inhibits cervical cancer by modulating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, with AKT1 identified as a key potential target. To validate this, we examined the expression level of AKT1 in HeLa cells following AIL treatment by western blotting. The results showed a significant decrease in the ratio of p-AKT1 to total AKT1 (Figure 6B). To further confirm this mechanism, we performed a Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA) (Figure 6C), which demonstrated that under increasing temperatures, the thermal stability of AKT1 was significantly higher in the AIL treatment group than that in the DMSO control group. Overall, these results indicate that AIL may inhibit the initiation and development of cervical cancer by regulating the expression of AKT1.

AIL inhibited tumor growth in xenograft mouse models

In vivo, xenograft tumors exhibited significantly decreased volume and weight in response to AIL treatment (Figure 7A-7D). Regarding safety, body weight remained comparable between the experimental and control groups at any point during the experiment (Figure 7E). Histopathological analysis via H&E staining demonstrated reduced tumor cellularity and expanded intercellular spaces in AIL-treated tumors relative to control specimens, and revealed no significant histopathological changes in the liver and kidney tissues under the current experimental conditions (Figure 7F). Furthermore, immunohistochemical analysis indicated that AIL treatment markedly suppressed Ki-67 expression in xenograft tumors (Figure 7G). Taken together, these results demonstrate that AIL effectively inhibits the growth of cervical cancer in vivo and exhibits a good safety profile under the tested dose.

Discussion

Cervical cancer is the fourth most commonly diagnosed cancer, posing a significant burden on global health, disproportionately affecting women. The disease will account for an estimated 660,000 new cases and 340,000 deaths in 2022 (2). The incidence and mortality rates of cervical cancer exhibit significant geographical disparities; around 85% of instances and fatalities are found in nations with low to moderate economic status (15). With widespread early screening and HPV vaccination, the incidence of cervical cancer has declined (16). However, in low- and middle-income countries with insufficient screening and limited medical resources, a disproportionately high proportion of patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, which contributes to a suboptimal 5-year survival rate (17). Current clinical management of cervical cancer primarily relies on comprehensive treatment strategies, including surgical resection, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy (18). Despite these approaches, clinical practice continues to face multiple challenges such as increased risks of postoperative recurrence and distant metastasis (19), development of resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy (20-22), and a generally low response rate to immunotherapy (23), all of which significantly inhibit long-term patient survival outcomes.

In this study, we assessed the antitumor activity of AIL in cervical cancer and investigated its mechanism of action. Our in vitro findings showed that AIL suppressed the proliferative, migratory, and invasive capacities of cervical cancer HeLa cells, triggered cell cycle arrest in the G0/G1 phase, and induced apoptosis. In animal models, AIL significantly suppressed tumor growth in xenograft mice, with good safety profiles. Network pharmacology analysis suggested that AIL may regulate the AKT signaling pathway, and molecular docking simulations were employed to validate the potential binding mode. Furthermore, the CETSA results supported the hypothesis that AIL directly binds to the AKT1 protein, indicating that AIL may inhibit cervical cancer growth by suppressing the activity of the AKT1 protein, thereby promoting apoptosis, suggesting its potential as a candidate therapeutic agent for the treatment of cervical cancer.

AIL is a natural triterpenoid extracted from Ailanthus altissima. In recent years, numerous studies have reported the mechanisms and potential of triterpenoids in the treatment of cervical cancer. Notoginsenoside R1 has been shown to enhance its deleterious effect on cervical cancer HeLa cells by downregulating the nucleolar protein PHF6 expression and inducing DNA damage, thereby significantly inhibiting cell viability (24). However, its inhibitory effect is relatively weak, with an IC50 value of only 0.41 mM at 48 h. Zheng et al. (25) found that triptolide exhibits strong cytotoxicity against cervical cancer cells, demonstrating significant antiproliferative activity both in vitro and in vivo. This mechanism may involve the activation of endoplasmic reticulum stress and redox regulatory pathways, thereby inducing immunogenic cell death in cervical cancer cells. However, several studies have indicated that triptolide can cause toxic reactions in various organs, including the liver and kidneys (26). Compared with these compounds, AIL demonstrated stronger anti-cervical cancer activity and better safety. Furthermore, pharmacokinetic studies by Hu et al. (27) revealed that AIL possesses favorable metabolic stability and a low clearance rate, suggesting a prolonged duration of action in vivo. A high free drug fraction of up to 83.4% also indicates high bioavailability, which may contribute to enhanced pharmacological efficacy. Based on our in vitro experiments, compared with cisplatin (IC50: 3.14±0.07 µmol/L; tumor growth inhibition rate at 3 mg/kg dose: 85.40%±6.09%) and 5-fluorouracil (IC50: 176.36±29.50 µmol/L; tumor growth inhibition rate at 10 mg/kg dose: 69.69%±6.43%) (28), the inhibitory effect of AIL on HeLa cell proliferation was significantly stronger, with an IC50 of 63.92±7.73 nM. However, in experiments utilizing the HeLa cell xenograft mouse model, the tumor growth inhibition rate of AIL was only 49.38%±7.36% after administration at a dose of 5 mg/kg. This was lower than the corresponding values of cisplatin and 5-fluorouracil. These results indicate that AIL exhibits notable anti-tumor activity in vitro compared with existing clinical drugs, but its anti-tumor effect in vivo is limited.

Our results demonstrate that AIL markedly suppresses HeLa cell growth mainly through triggering cell cycle arrest and enhancing apoptosis. Specifically, AIL treatment induced a G0/G1 phase cell cycle blockade in HeLa cells. It also triggers apoptosis by activating the caspase cascade. Apoptosis, a major form of programmed cell death, is a genetically controlled, active cellular process accompanied by distinctive morphological changes and activation of biochemical pathways, such as those mediated by caspases (29). Mitochondria, which serve as central hubs for cellular energy metabolism, are also critical in the regulation of apoptosis, playing an essential role in programmed cell death (30,31). The fundamental mechanism centers on increased permeability of the mitochondrial outer membrane, triggering the release of apoptotic factors, including cytochrome c, which subsequently initiates the caspase cascade (32). This process is precisely and bidirectionally regulated by the Bcl-2 family (33).

The PI3K/AKT pathway is an evolutionarily conserved intracellular signaling cascade that specifically responds to extracellular ligand stimulation. Phosphorylation cascades regulate metabolic processes, including glycolysis and protein synthesis, and coordinate key biological events such as cell proliferation, survival, and programmed cell death (34). Growing evidence shows the PI3K/AKT pathway critically suppresses cervical cancer development and progression by regulating apoptotic processes. For example, cinnamaldehyde induces the ubiquitination-mediated degradation of HPV oncoproteins E6/E7 by targeting RPS16, thereby inhibiting the PI3K/AKT pathway and initiating apoptosis (35). Curcumin suppresses proliferation and migration while promoting apoptosis in cisplatin-resistant HeLa cells by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT pathway and inducing autophagy activation (36). Andrographolide demonstrates substantial anti-tumor efficacy in both laboratory and living organisms, potentially through the suppression of NF-κB and COX-2 expression and the interference with PI3K/AKT signaling, which promotes apoptosis (37). This study revealed that AIL activates the mitochondria-dependent apoptotic pathway by suppressing AKT phosphorylation. The molecular mechanism involves a significant increase in the ratio of pro-apoptotic Bax to anti-apoptotic Bcl-xL, facilitation of the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, promotion of cytochrome c release into the cytoplasm, and subsequent activation of caspase-9 and its downstream effector, caspase-3, culminating in programmed cell death. PARP1 cooperatively regulates apoptosis by cleaving its substrate proteins.

This study has some limitations. First, all in vitro experiments were performed using HPV18-positive HeLa cells, and the findings were not confirmed in HPV16-positive and HPV-negative cervical cancer cell lines. Second, neither in vitro nor in vivo experiments included positive control drug groups. Finally, while no obvious histopathological changes were identified in the liver and kidneys, we did not evaluate serum biochemical indicators. Future studies should include cell lines with different HPV status and positive control groups and incorporate serum biochemical analyses to further verify the efficacy and safety of AIL against cervical cancer.

Conclusions

In conclusion, AIL exhibited significant anti-tumor activity against cervical cancer both in vitro and in vivo. Network pharmacology analysis, supported by CETSA results, suggested that AIL suppresses cervical cancer cell proliferation by modulating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, and AKT1 may be its direct target. Taken together, AIL demonstrated promising potential as an anticancer drug candidate by markedly suppressing cervical cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.
