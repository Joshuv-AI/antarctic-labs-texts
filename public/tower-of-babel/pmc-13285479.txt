Utilizing generative artificial intelligence to create simulated patient for history taking in gynecology
Authors: Lingling Gao, Li Zhang, Yanxin Zhang, Min Wu, Jianbo Xu, Dan Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a generative artificial intelligence based simulated patient model in improving gynecological history-taking skills among medical students, compared with traditional clinical teaching methods.MethodsA prospective randomized controlled trial was conducted involving 40 fourth-year medical students during their gynecology clerkship. Students were randomly assigned to either an AI group (n = 20) or a control group (n = 20). Both groups received identical theoretical instruction on history taking. The control group practiced interviews with instructor-led standardized patients, while the AI group conducted simulated interviews with a large language model configured to behave as a real patient using predefined gynecological cases. Outcomes were assessed using a gynecological history-taking content checklist, Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) Score, and the Mini-Clinical Evaluation Exercise (Mini-CEX). Statistical analyses were performed using t-tests, chi-square tests, or Mann-Whitney U tests.ResultsBaseline characteristics did not differ significantly between the two groups. Students in the AI group achieved a significantly higher history-taking checklist completion rate than those in the control group and missed fewer key history items. The AI group achieved significantly higher total OSCE scores compared to the control group. Mini-CEX results showed that the AI group performed significantly better in medical interviewing skills and organization/efficiency. No significant differences were observed in professionalism, clinical judgment, counseling skills, or overall clinical competence.ConclusionsThe use of a generative AI–simulated patient significantly enhanced the information completeness and organizational quality of gynecological history taking among medical students. AI-based simulation appears to be an effective and scalable adjunct to traditional standardized patients, offering educational benefits for reinforcing systematic clinical inquiry. However, these findings are preliminary as a single-center study with a limited sample size. Further large-scale, multi-center research is required to confirm the generalizability of these results across diverse educational settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09219-2.

Introduction

Effective history taking is a foundational clinical skill in medical education and is particularly critical in gynecology, where clinicians must collect sensitive, complex, and highly contextual information related to menstrual patterns, obstetric history, sexual health, and reproductive concerns. Mastery of gynecological history taking requires both structured cognitive frameworks and the ability to communicate with empathy and professionalism. However, medical students often experience challenges in gathering complete and well-organized gynecologic histories, especially when addressing intimate or stigmatized topics [1], resulting in omission of key clinical information during patient encounters.

Traditional instructional methods such as bedside teaching, role-play, and standardized patients remain essential components of clinical training. But these approaches have some limitations [2], such as inconsistent patient availability, variability in learning opportunities, and high faculty workload. Moreover, standardized patient programs, although effective, can be resource-intensive and may not provide sufficient repetition for students to achieve fluency and confidence in gynecologic interviewing [3]. As a result, there is increasing interest in exploring supplemental modalities that can provide scalable, standardized, and readily accessible practice environments for medical students.

Recognizing its critical role, medical courses worldwide prioritize history taking training, traditionally relying on communication-focused courses and clinical clerkships [2]. Students often struggle with the transition from theoretical knowledge to clinical practice, leading to disorganized inquiries and missed critical information. Furthermore, the sensitive nature of gynecological complaints (e.g., sexual history, menstrual irregularities) requires a degree of privacy and comfort that makes medical students difficult to practice.

Advances in generative artificial intelligence (AI) offer new opportunities to enhance simulation-based education. Large language models (LLMs), such as GPT systems, can generate dynamic, contextually appropriate, and conversationally responsive simulated patients capable of mimicking a wide range of clinical scenarios [4, 5]. Recent studies have highlighted the potential of LLMs to support clinical reasoning, generate patient narratives, and even demonstrate performance approaching passing thresholds on medical licensing examinations [6, 7]. Early educational research suggests that AI-generated virtual patients may improve learners’ engagement [8, 9], provide unlimited practice opportunities [10–12], and support consistent exposure to rare or sensitive clinical presentations.

Despite these developments, limited evidence exists regarding the use of generative AI to create simulated patients specifically for gynecological history-taking training. Gynecology presents unique interpersonal and content-specific challenges, and it remains unclear whether AI-generated simulated patients can offer sufficient realism, structure, and educational value to improve learners’ performance. The present study addresses this gap by examining the impact of generative AI–created simulated patients on medical students’ gynecological history-taking performance. By comparing AI-assisted teaching with traditional instruction, this study aims to determine whether generative AI can enhance the completeness, organization, and clinical interviewing skills demonstrated by learners. The findings may offer insights into the role of AI-driven simulation as a scalable and pedagogically meaningful tool for competency-based medical education.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This study was designed as a prospective, randomized controlled trial conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were recruited among fourth-year medical students rotating in the gynecology clerkship at Northern Jiangsu People’s Hospital Affiliated to Yangzhou University. Sample size calculation was performed using G*Power. The calculation was based on a two-tailed independent samples t-test with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a desired statistical power (1 − β) of 0.80. Based on previously published studies evaluating AI-simulated patients in medical education, a large effect size was assumed (Cohen’s d = 0.92). The analysis indicated that a minimum of 38 participants would be required to detect a statistically significant difference between the two groups, corresponding to 19 participants per group with an allocation ratio of 1:1. To account for a potential dropout rate of approximately 5%, the planned sample size was increased to 40 participants, with 20 students assigned to each group. Ethical approval and permission to conduct the study were obtained from the Ethics Committee of Northern Jiangsu People’s Hospital Affiliated to Yangzhou University (No. 2026KY007). Informed consent was collected from all participants. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Study procedures

All participants first received standard theoretical lectures on medical history-taking skills together, including basic principles, key points, and communication techniques. Then participants were randomly assigned into two groups by an independent researcher using a computer-generated random number sequence: the experimental group (AI Group, n = 20) and the traditional group (Control Group, n = 20). The allocation was disclosed by a study coordinator only after the participants had provided informed consent and completed the baseline theoretical instruction. Due to the nature of the experimental setup (AI-based text interface vs. human standardized patient), neither students nor faculty examiners could be blinded to group assignment during the history collection or performance assessment. Students in the control group underwent traditional medical history taking process. The instructor acted as a standardized patient, allowing students to practice interviewing and complete the process of medical history collection. To ensure consistency and minimize variability in the control group, the same instructor conducted all standardized patient encounters. To ensure comparability with the AI condition, the control group’s instructor-led simulation followed a strictly standardized protocol. Prior to the study, the instructor underwent a formal calibration process based on the identical illness scripts used for the AI group. These scripts provided a structured framework for the chief complaint, history of present illness, and specific clinical cues to be withheld unless prompted by student inquiry. By adhering to a fixed response logic, the instructor minimized unconscious cueing and ensured that the educational challenge was comparable to the AI-driven simulation. Students in the AI group participated in simulated patient interviews using an AI-based conversational model (ChatGPT-5.2, accessed via the web-based interface on 2026-01-23). Several typical gynecological cases were designed, such as ectopic pregnancy which was uploaded as a supplementary file. Each script contained detailed clinical information such as chief complaints, history of present illness, past medical history, personal history, and family history. These standardized case scripts were preloaded into the AI system prior to each simulation session. Students then conducted text-based simulated interviews with the model via a text-based interface to collect medical histories, assuming the role of doctors. The simulated interviews were conducted in real-time, mimicking the conversational flow of a physical clinical encounter. Students accessed the AI platform independently in a quiet learning environment, allowing for self-paced and self-directed practice without direct faculty supervision. This independent setup was designed to evaluate the tool’s potential as a scalable, 24/7 accessible educational resource. Each simulated history-taking session typically lasted between 15 and 30 min, depending on the complexity of the specific case and the student’s inquiry speed, which closely approximates the duration of a standard gynecological outpatient consultation. While the AI’s output was not monitored in real-time during student encounters to simulate an independent environment, all interaction logs were recorded for post-intervention analysis. The limitations of the AI system, including knowledge cutoff constraints and the absence of non-verbal clinical cues, were acknowledged and mitigated through the use of standardized scripts and controlled prompt engineering. To ensure reproducibility and consistency of the AI-generated responses, predefined technical parameters and structured prompt controls were implemented. Specifically, the model’s temperature was set at 0.7 to balance conversational realism with response consistency, while the top-p parameter was fixed at 0.9. The model was not fine-tuned. Additional behavioral constraints were embedded within the system prompt to regulate response characteristics. These included: the model was restricted from providing medical advice or deviating from its assigned patient role; The model was instructed to use lay language typical of real patients rather than medical terminology; and diagnostic clues were withheld unless elicited through appropriate student questioning.

Outcome measures

To comprehensively evaluate the differences in history-taking performance between the two groups, three distinct assessment tools were employed post-intervention: History-Taking Content Checklist, Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) Score, and the Mini-Clinical Evaluation Exercise (mini-CEX). The History-Taking Content Checklist was developed by a panel of three senior clinical educators in our university based on the Calgary-Cambridge Guide to ensure a standardized evaluation of clinical communication. To ensure clinical specificity, the panel adapted the guide to include essential gynecological components, such as menstrual, obstetric, and contraceptive histories. The final instrument comprised 44 items categorized into core domains including the chief complaint, history of present illness, menstrual history, marital and reproductive history, past medical history, personal history, and family history. To minimize subjective bias, no weighting was applied. Each checklist item was scored dichotomously as either completed or not completed by two independent faculty examiners, and the final score was calculated as the percentage of completed items out of the total number of items. Examiners were strictly instructed to evaluate students based on the scoring criteria of the History-Taking Content Checklist, OSCE, and mini-CEX, rather than the medium of interaction or stylistic nuances. To ensure the reliability of the assessment, the two faculty examiners independently graded a subset of 10% of the encounters prior to the full evaluation. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s κ, yielding a coefficient of 0.83, indicating ‘almost perfect’ agreement between the raters.

Two faculty examiners also graded performance independently using a station-specific OSCE checklist. This checklist evaluates the process of the interview, including the introduction, presenting complaint, history of presenting complaint, menstrual history, marital and reproductive history, past medical history, personal history, family history, and closing of the interview.

In addition to checklist-based assessment, students’ clinical competence was also evaluated by these two independent faculty examiners using the mini-CEX. A gynecology-specific mini-CEX form was developed to assess students’ gynecological history-taking performance. Students’ performance was evaluated across multiple domains, including history-taking skills, communication skills, clinical judgment, medical knowledge application, professionalism, and organization and efficiency, using a standardized 9-point scale where scores of 1–3 represented unsatisfactory performance, 4–6 represented satisfactory performance, and 7–9 represented superior performance. An overall global rating was also recorded for each student.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data distribution was evaluated using Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables. The composition ratio was statistically analyzed using the chi-square test. The independent sample t-test was used to compare the continuous variables between the AI Group and the Control Group. If the data were not normally distributed Mann-Whitney U test was used. To supplement null-hypothesis significance testing, effect sizes were calculated for primary outcomes using Cohen’s d. Effect sizes were interpreted as small (0.2 ≤ d < 0.5), medium (0.5 ≤ d < 0.8), or large (d > 0.8) to assess the educational magnitude of the AI intervention. Statistical analysis was performed using STATA 15.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 40 medical students were recruited and randomly assigned to either the AI-simulated patient group (n = 20) or the control group (n = 20). As shown in Table 1, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of baseline characteristics. The gender distribution was comparable between groups (p = 0.744), and the mean age did not differ significantly between the AI group (22.60 ± 0.60 years) and the control group (22.50 ± 0.51 years, p = 0.5737), indicating that the two groups were well balanced at baseline.

Performance on history-taking content checklist

Students in the AI group demonstrated significantly better performance on the gynecological history-taking content checklist compared with the control group (Table 2). The AI group demonstrated a significantly higher checklist completion rate compared to the control group (93.07 ± 3.93 vs. 88.98 ± 4.05, p = 0.0025, Cohen’s d = 1.03). Furthermore, the AI group missed significantly fewer key items during the history-taking process. The mean number of missed key items was 3.05 ± 1.73 in the AI group, whereas the control group missed an average of 4.85 ± 1.78 items (p = 0.0025, Cohen’s d = 1.03). These findings suggest that the generative AI simulation was particularly effective in reinforcing the comprehensiveness of information gathering.

These results suggest that AI-based training specifically enhances the performance of medical students in history taking process.

Assessment of clinical competencies by Mini-CEX

The results of the Mini-CEX are detailed in Table 4. Students in the AI group achieved significantly higher scores in medical interviewing skills compared with the control group (7.70 ± 0.66 vs. 7.25 ± 0.64, p = 0.0342, Cohen’s d = 0.69). The AI group also demonstrated superior organization and efficiency during gynecological history-taking encounters (7.60 ± 0.50 vs. 7.10 ± 0.64, p = 0.0092, Cohen’s d = 0.88). This indicates that AI training likely helped students structure their interviews more logically and conduct them more efficiently. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in the domains of “Humanistic qualities/professionalism” (p = 0.3572), “Clinical judgment” (p = 0.6391), “Counseling Skills” (p = 0.6041), or “Overall Clinical Competence” (p = 0.7739). This suggests that while AI simulation effectively improved procedural and organizational skills, students in the AI group demonstrated comparable performance to those in the traditional group in fostering empathy and clinical reasoning, with no statistically significant differences observed.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that generative AI simulations significantly improved medical students’ gynecological history-taking performance, particularly in completeness and organizational efficiency. Gynecological history-taking requires systematic coverage of sensitive and complex domains such as menstrual history, obstetric history, sexual and contraceptive history, which students often omit or approach inconsistently. The relatively controlled and repeatable nature of AI-based simulations may allow learners to focus on these core elements without the cognitive and emotional pressures associated with real or human stimulated patient encounters. This finding aligns with previous studies demonstrating that virtual patients and simulation-based education enhance learners’ ability to organize clinical information and reduce omission errors during data gathering [13]. Besides, unlike human role-playing, where fatigue or variability may occur, the AI system offers repeated, high-fidelity practice opportunities [9, 11, 12], allowing students to master the checklist elements of history taking through deliberate practice [14]. The consistency between the OSCE scores and the History-Taking Checklist results further reinforces the educational value of generative AI. By providing a structured and repeatable environment, the AI intervention allowed students to refine their competence, as evidenced by the superior OSCE scores.

Mini-CEX results further support the complementary role of AI-generated simulated patients in clinical skills training. Students exposed to AI-based simulation demonstrated significantly better medical interviewing skills and organization and efficiency. This improvement can be attributed to the structured nature of the AI-based interaction. To elicit accurate information from a generative AI model, students must formulate clear, logical, and specific questions, which inherently trains them to organize their thought processes. However, no significant differences were observed between the groups in clinical judgment or humanistic qualities. The lack of disparity in clinical judgment suggests that while AI is excellent at training students how to gather data, the ability to interpret that data requires more complex cognitive processing that short-term simulation may not immediately alter. Clinical reasoning is a higher-order skill that typically develops longitudinally with clinical experience and case exposure, rather than through single simulation intervention [15]. Similarly, the comparable scores in humanistic qualities indicate that while AI training yielded similar outcomes to traditional methods, it did not demonstrate superiority in fostering empathy within the current study framework. This is likely because current text-based AI interfaces lack the non-verbal cues, such as eye contact, grimacing in pain, or body posture which are essential for triggering empathetic responses in real-world doctor-patient interactions [16]. Future iterations of AI simulations incorporating multimodal avatars may be necessary to better address these soft skills.

The lack of difference in overall mini-CEX global ratings highlights an important consideration for curricular integration. Generative AI should be viewed as a complementary educational tool rather than a replacement for traditional bedside teaching with real patients. Beyond its role in clerkship training, generative AI simulation offers significant potential for longitudinal integration within medical curricula. In the pre-clinical phase, AI-generated simulated patients can serve as a scaffolded transition, allowing students to practice foundational history-taking in a low-stakes, text-based environment before advancing to real patient interactions. Furthermore, this technology provides a scalable solution for remediation. Students who demonstrate deficiencies in specific clinical competencies can engage in unlimited, independent practice to master checklist elements without additional faculty burden. While the current study focuses on the effectiveness of AI as a conversational simulated patient, its potential as an interactive evaluator remains to be fully utilized. In our subsequent research, we will further explore the role of generative AI in providing active assessment and formative feedback to create a more dynamic and personalized learning loop for medical students. AI simulations may offer real-time, standardized feedback throughout the academic year, ensuring that learners achieve a consistent baseline of proficiency before undergoing summative OSCEs. This longitudinal approach supports a competency-based model where students progress at their own pace while maintaining high standards of clinical preparation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This study involved a relatively small sample size from a single institution, which may limit generalizability of the results to other medical curricula or regions with different training standards. Besides, the relatively small sample size may have limited our ability to detect significant differences in certain humanistic and cognitive domains of the Mini-CEX, such as professionalism or clinical judgment. These findings should be interpreted with caution, as the study may have been underpowered to capture subtle variations in these competencies, potentially leading to a Type II error. Consequently, the observed improvements should be viewed as preliminary evidence of the potential of generative AI in medical education. Future studies employing multi-center designs and larger, more diverse student populations are essential to validate the scalability and broader applicability of AI-driven simulation tools. Secondly, the study lacked blinding of the examiners. Because the AI and control group interventions involved different interaction modalities, examiners were aware of the group assignments during the evaluation process. This lack of blinding may introduce observer bias. However, the use of structured, validated checklists helped standardize the scoring process. Furthermore, an inherent imbalance in standardization between the two groups must be acknowledged. While the human standardized patient was rigorously calibrated to a fixed script, human-led simulations are subject to subtle variations in emotional nuance and response timing. In contrast, the generative AI provides a level of absolute procedural consistency that is difficult for human standardized patient to replicate over multiple sessions. This difference in standardization may have influenced the perceived realism of the encounters and should be considered when interpreting the comparative improvement in organizational efficiency. Additionally, the short-term nature of the assessment did not allow evaluation of skill retention or transfer to real clinical settings. Future studies should include longitudinal follow-up, large-scale, multicenter designs, and qualitative exploration of learners’ perceptions to better confirm the generalizability of these results across diverse educational settings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that AI-simulated patients serve as an effective pedagogical tool for improving the structural performance of gynecological history-taking in medical students. By providing a repeatable and standardized environment, the AI intervention helped students master the systematic retrieval of clinical data. However, these findings are preliminary as a single-center study with a limited sample size. Further large-scale, multi-center research is required to confirm the generalizability of these results across diverse educational settings.
