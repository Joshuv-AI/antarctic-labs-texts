Weak Adversarial Neural Pushforward Mappings for Fokker-Planck Equations
Andrew Qing He, Wei Cai

Abstract
This paper presents a novel method for solving Fokker-Planck equations by learning neural samplers via a weak adversarial framework. We represent the solution distribution through a neural pushforward map, bypassing the limitations of density-based methods. A key innovation is our use of computationally efficient plane-wave test functions, whose derivatives are explicitly computed—a treatment distinct from prior work. This approach handles distributions without densities and naturally enforces probability conservation.

Introduction

In recent years, significant attention has been directed towards solving differential and integral equations using deep learning techniques, such as Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)
[
1
]
, the Deep Ritz method
[
8
]
, Weak Adversarial Networks (WAN)
[
6
]
, and others. A common element across these frameworks is the representation of the solution by a
pointwise evaluation neural network
—a deep neural network that takes a spatial or spatio-temporal coordinate as input and outputs an estimate of the solution’s value at that point. This offers a natural and intuitive way to represent a function.

However, this approach is not always optimal. The intrinsic nature of the solution can sometimes suggest more suitable representations. This paper examines one such case: when the solution is a probability density function (PDF). Here, a pointwise evaluation network is often suboptimal for two primary reasons:

1.

The solution may not be absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(e.g., a Dirac
\delta
distribution), in which case a classical PDF does not exist.

2.

It is exceedingly difficult to guarantee conservation of probability (a volume-preserving property), as exactly evaluating the integral of a neural network over an arbitrary domain is generally intractable.

In such scenarios, a more natural strategy is to represent the solution distribution directly. We propose representing the solution via a
neural pushforward mapping

F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
(parameterized by
{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
), which transforms samples from a simple reference distribution (e.g., uniform or standard Gaussian) of potentially different dimensionality into the target solution distribution through a pushforward operation.

A similar methodology is employed in
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
. However, these works typically employ a PINN-style loss that requires the existence and regularity of the PDF. This, in turn, constrains the network
F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
to be invertible and Jacobian-friendly (e.g., Real NVP
[
13
]
, as used in normalizing flows), and incurs a high computational cost from calculating second-order derivatives of the density (which involves Jacobian determinant computations). In contrast, our method is based on the
weak
formulation of transport and Fokker-Planck equations. This allows us to employ more general deep neural networks for the mapping
F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
. We adopt the weak adversarial framework
[
6
,
5
]
, which reformulates the equation in its weak form. A test function in this formulation is parameterized as an adversarial network (acting as a “discriminator”, similar to GANs
[
14
]
), and the pushforward mapping
F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
is trained adversarially to satisfy the equation.

In our framework,
F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
can be any neural network that maps from a convenient base distribution (of arbitrary dimension) to the target space, with output dimension matching that of the target distribution. The trained network acts as an efficient sampler from the solution distribution. While the framework does not inherently provide an explicit expression for the probability density function, users who require one—and can ensure its existence—may still instantiate
F_{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
as an invertible, Jacobian-friendly network like Real NVP. Nevertheless, this only serves as an optional choice within our framework, not a requirement.

The adversarial test function is treated in a distinct manner. Aligning with the mathematical philosophy of weak formulations, the test function should exhibit greater regularity and be more amenable to differential operators than the solution itself. In PDE theory, this implies the test functions should be smooth and exhibit suitable decay. But for numerically, this translates to a requirement for computational efficiency; the cost of differentiating the network should not substantially exceed the cost of evaluating it. To satisfy this condition, we select a specific, computationally efficient form for the test function: the plane wave function. This choice allows its partial derivatives to be computed explicitly and with the same complexity as evaluating the function itself, significantly enhancing the efficiency of the training process. It is worth noting that while plane waves have been employed as test functions in other works
[
2
,
3
]
, their treatment was fundamentally different as the requisite differential operators were not applied to them. Our method’s explicit computation of these operators on the test function is a novel and innovative aspect of this framework.

2
Methods

2.1
The Steady-State Fokker-Planck Equation

We consider the steady-state Fokker-Planck equation (FPE) on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which describes the probability density function
f(\mathbf{x})
of a stochastic process at equilibrium. The equation is given by:

\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}f(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\left[a_{ij}(\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x})\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left[b_{i}(\mathbf{x})f(\mathbf{x})\right]=0,

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
a_{ij}(\mathbf{x})
are the components of the state-dependent diffusion tensor
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x})
, and
b_{i}(\mathbf{x})
are the components of the drift vector
\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x})
. We assume the diffusion tensor
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x})
is such that the differential operator
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
is elliptic (or possibly hypo-elliptic), ensuring the well-posedness of the problem. Furthermore, we seek a probability density function, which imposes the natural normalization constraint:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=1,\quad\text{and}\quad f(\mathbf{x})\geq 0.

(2)

2.1.1
Weak Formulation and Adversarial Formulation

A key challenge in solving (
1
) directly is that the solution
f
may be a generalized function (e.g., a Dirac delta distribution) lacking a classical density. To circumvent this, we employ the weak formulation of the PDE. Multiplying (
1
) by a smooth test function
\rho(\mathbf{x})
and applying integration by parts twice (assuming appropriate decay conditions at infinity of
f
), we obtain the weak form:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(\mathbf{x})\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial^{2}\rho(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial\rho(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0.

(3)

This must hold for all test functions
\rho
in a suitable class of smooth, bounded functions. Critically, this formulation does not require
f
to be differentiable. The weak form can be interpreted as an expectation under the probability measure associated with
f
:

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim f}\left[\mathcal{L}\rho(\mathbf{x})\right]=0,\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{L}\rho(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial^{2}\rho}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial x_{i}}.

(4)

This expectation can be approximated efficiently via Monte Carlo sampling if one has access to samples from the distribution
f
.

2.1.2
Neural Parametrization of the Solution and Test Functions

Our method parametrizes two core components: the solution
f
and the test functions
\rho
.

Parametrizing the Solution (
f
)

Instead of representing the density
f
directly, we represent the solution distribution implicitly via a pushforward map.We define a neural network
F_{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
, parameterized by weights
{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
, that transforms samples from a simple base distribution (e.g., uniform or Gaussian) of dimension
d
into samples from the target distribution
f
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The dimensions
d
and
n
need not be equal, providing flexibility in choosing the most convenient base distribution for sampling and optimization. If
\mathbf{r}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\text{base}}
, then
\mathbf{x}=F_{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}(\mathbf{r})
is a sample such that
\mathbf{x}\sim f
. This approach naturally enforces the normalization constraint and can represent distributions without a density.

Parametrizing the Test Functions (
\rho
)

Following the rationale in the introduction, we choose test functions that are computationally efficient and facilitate easy computation of their derivatives. A highly effective choice is the
plane-wave
function of the form:

\rho(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(k)})=\varphi\left(\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b^{(k)}\right),

where
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(k)}=\{\mathbf{w}^{(k)},b^{(k)}\}
are the parameters for the
k
-th test function,
\mathbf{w}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a weight vector,
b^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}
is a bias, and
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
C^{2}
activation function. The derivatives of this function have a simple, closed-form expression. For a given test function
\rho
, the operator
\mathcal{L}\rho
evaluates to:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\rho(\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(\mathbf{x})w_{i}\right)\varphi^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(\mathbf{x})w_{i}w_{j}\right)\varphi^{\prime\prime}\left(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b\right).

(5)

A common and effective choice is
\varphi(\cdot)=\sin(\cdot)
, leading to:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\rho(\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(\mathbf{x})w_{i}\right)\cos\left(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(\mathbf{x})w_{i}w_{j}\right)\sin\left(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}+b\right).

(6)

This form is computationally efficient, particularly when the diffusion matrix
(a_{ij})_{n\times n}
possesses favorable low-rank or sparsity structures. In such cases, the quadratic form
\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(\mathbf{x})w_{i}w_{j}
can be evaluated in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time, ensuring that the overall cost of computing
\mathcal{L}\rho
remains comparable to that of evaluating
\rho
itself. It is worth noting, however, that this efficiency is problem-dependent. For instance, when
(a_{ij})_{n\times n}
is a constant multiple of the identity matrix—effectively reducing the second-order term to a Laplacian—the computation remains highly efficient. In the general case of a fully dense diffusion matrix, the double summation requires
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
operations, leading to significantly increased computational cost in high dimensions. This scalability challenge is inherent to numerical methods for second-order partial differential equations and is not unique to the present approach.

Flexibility in Base Distribution Choice

A key advantage of our approach is the flexibility in choosing the base distribution
\mathcal{P}_{\text{base}}
. Unlike methods that require the base and target distributions to have matching support or dimensionality, our framework allows
F_{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}
to map from any convenient
m
-dimensional base distribution to the
n
-dimensional target space. This flexibility enables:

•

Using higher-dimensional base distributions to provide richer representational capacity for complex target distributions

•

Employing computationally efficient base distributions (e.g., uniform or standard normal) regardless of target complexity

•

Leveraging the universal approximation properties of neural networks to bridge arbitrary distributional gaps

2.1.3
Adversarial Training Algorithm

The training objective is to find parameters
{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
for the pushforward network such that the weak formulation (
4
) holds for a rich set of test functions. This is achieved by adversarially training the pushforward network
F_{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}
against a suite of
K
parameterized test functions
\{\rho(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(k)})\}_{k=1}^{K}
. We denote
\boldsymbol{\eta}:=\{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
as the ensemble of all the parameters of the test functions.

The algorithm proceeds as follows:

1.

Sampling:
Draw
M
samples
\{\mathbf{r}^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^{M}
from the base distribution
\mathcal{P}_{\text{base}}
and push them through the network to generate samples
\{\mathbf{x}^{(m)}=F_{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}(\mathbf{r}^{(m)})\}_{m=1}^{M}
.

2.

Test Function Evaluation:
For each of the
K
test functions with parameters
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(k)}=\{\mathbf{w}^{(k)},b^{(k)}\}
, compute the empirical mean of
\mathcal{L}\rho^{(k)}
over the batch:

\hat{\mathbb{E}}^{(k)}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(k)})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{L}\rho^{(k)}(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}).

3.

Loss Function:
The overall loss function measures the squared violation of the weak form across all
K
test functions:

{L}_{\text{total}}[{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\eta}]=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[\hat{\mathbb{E}}^{(k)}({\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\eta}^{(k)})\right]^{2}.

(7)

4.

Adversarial Optimization:
We solve the following min-max optimization problem:

\min_{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}\max_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}{L}_{\text{total}}[{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}},\boldsymbol{\eta}].

In practice, this is done by alternating gradient updates:

•

Update
{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
(Generator):
Take a gradient
descent
step on
{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
to minimize
{L}_{\text{total}}
, thus improving the pushforward map to satisfy the weak form for the current set of test functions.

•

Update
\boldsymbol{\eta}
(Adversary):
Take a gradient
ascent
step on each
\boldsymbol{\eta}
to maximize
{L}_{\text{total}}
, thus refining the test functions to better detect violations of the weak form by the current pushforward map.

This adversarial training process ensures that the learned distribution
f
, represented by
F_{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}
, converges to a solution that satisfies the Fokker-Planck equation in its weak form against a broad spectrum of test functions.

3
Results

3.1
A One-Dimensional Steady-State Problem

To validate the proposed methodology, we first apply it to a canonical one-dimensional problem with a known analytical solution. This allows for a precise quantitative evaluation of the algorithm’s accuracy and convergence properties.

3.1.1
Problem Specification

We consider the steady-state Fokker-Planck equation (
1
) on
\mathbb{R}
with the following parameters:

•

Dimension:
n=1

•

Drift coefficient:
b(x)=-\theta(x-\mu)

•

Diffusion coefficient:
a(x)=\sigma^{2}/2
(
\sigma
is a constant)

The corresponding PDE is therefore:

-\frac{\sigma}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[(-\theta(x-\mu))f\right]=0.

(8)

For this test, we set the parameters to
\sigma=\sqrt{2}
,
\theta=1
, and
\mu=2
, simplifying the equation to:

-\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial((x-2)f)}{\partial x}=0.

(9)

The analytical solution to this equation, satisfying the normalization constraint of a probability density function, is the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2}/2\theta)
:

f_{\text{true}}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left({-\frac{(x-2)^{2}}{2}}\right).

(10)

Thus, the target solution is a normal distribution with mean
\mu_{\text{true}}=2
and standard deviation
\sigma_{\text{true}}=1
.

3.1.2
Training Configuration

The neural pushforward mapping
F_{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}
was implemented as a deep neural network (DNN) with architecture
(1,10,10,1)
, using smooth activation functions (e.g., SiLU or Tanh) to ensure differentiability. This network maps samples from a simple base distribution to samples approximating the target distribution. In this experiment, the base distribution was chosen as the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
, i.e.,
r^{(m)}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
.

The adversarial test functions were parameterized as
\rho^{(k)}(x)=\sin(w^{(k)}x+b^{(k)})
. A bank of
K=100
such test functions was constructed. Notably, for this initial proof-of-concept, the parameters
\eta=\{w^{(k)},b^{(k)}\}
were not trained adversarially. Instead, the weights
w^{(k)}
were initialized by sampling from a standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and the biases
b^{(k)}
were sampled from
\mathcal{U}(0,2\pi)
; these parameters remained fixed throughout the training process. This simplification tests the method’s ability to find a solution that satisfies the weak form against a fixed, random set of modes. The batch size was set to
M=1000
samples.

The model was trained by minimizing the loss function
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}
(defined as (
7
)) using the Adam optimizer, performing gradient descent
only
on the parameters
{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}
of the pushforward network
F_{{\boldsymbol{\vartheta}}}
.

3.2
A One-Dimensional Double-Peak Distribution

To further demonstrate the versatility and robustness of our method, we apply it to a more challenging problem involving a bimodal distribution arising from a double-well potential. This example showcases the framework’s ability to handle complex, multimodal probability distributions that are difficult to represent with traditional density-based approaches.

3.2.1
Problem Specification

We consider a steady-state Fokker-Planck equation with a double-well potential
V(x)=(x^{2}-1)^{2}
, which creates two symmetric minima at
x=\pm 1
. The corresponding drift and diffusion coefficients are:

•

Dimension:
n=1

•

Drift coefficient:
b(x)=-\frac{dV}{dx}=-4x^{3}+4x=-4x(x^{2}-1)

•

Diffusion coefficient:
a(x)=\sigma^{2}
(constant)

For this experiment, we set
\sigma=0.6
. The resulting Fokker-Planck equation is:

-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}[(-4x^{3}+4x)f]=0.

(11)

The steady-state solution corresponds to the Boltzmann distribution:

f_{\text{true}}(x)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{2V(x)}{\sigma^{2}}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{2(x^{2}-1)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right),

(12)

which exhibits two pronounced peaks near
x=\pm 1
when the noise level
\sigma
is sufficiently small.

3.2.2
Training Configuration

The neural pushforward mapping was implemented as a deeper network with architecture
(8,100,1)
using Tanh activation functions, taking 8-dimensional input from a multivariate standard normal base distribution:

d=8,\quad\mathcal{P}_{\text{base}}=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d\times d}).

This higher-dimensional input space provides the network with greater flexibility to model the complex bimodal structure.

The adversarial test functions maintained the same plane-wave parameterization
\rho^{(k)}(x)=\sin(w^{(k)}x+b^{(k)})
, with
K=100
test functions. Unlike the previous example, we implemented full adversarial training, alternating between:

•

Generator updates: minimizing the loss with respect to the pushforward network parameters

•

Adversary updates: maximizing the loss with respect to test function parameters, performed 5 times per generator update every 10 epochs

The model was trained for 16,000 epochs with a batch size of 1,000 samples, using Adam optimizer with learning rate
2\times 10^{-3}
for the generator and SGD with learning rate
1\times 10^{-2}
for the test functions.

The quality of the learned solution is remarkable, as demonstrated in Figure

x=\pm 1

This result represents a significant advancement over the single-peak Gaussian example, as bimodal distributions pose substantial challenges for density-based neural methods due to their complex topology and the difficulty of ensuring proper normalization across disconnected modes. Our pushforward approach naturally handles this complexity by learning the transformation directly, without requiring explicit density computations or connectivity constraints.

The success of this experiment validates several key aspects of our framework: (1) the effectiveness of full adversarial training between generator and test functions, (2) the ability to handle complex multimodal distributions, and (3) the robustness of plane-wave test functions in capturing intricate distributional features. This establishes a strong foundation for extending the method to higher-dimensional multimodal problems where traditional approaches often fail.

4
Conclusion

We have introduced a weak adversarial framework that solves Fokker-Planck equations by learning a neural pushforward map, offering a powerful alternative to density-based methods. Our approach’s core innovations are: (1) a weak formulation that permits arbitrary network architectures, and (2) the use of plane-wave test functions with explicit derivative computation for efficient adversarial training. The method successfully learned a target distribution in a 1D benchmark, confirming its validity. This work provides a foundation for tackling high-dimensional problems and complex distributions, with future directions including full adversarial training of test functions and extension to time-dependent equations.
