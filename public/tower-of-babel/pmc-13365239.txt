Experimental investigation of heat transfer and friction factor in laminar, transition and turbulence region for water flow in mini-micro channels
Authors: Beytullah Erdoğan, Güneyhan Taşkaya, Hicret Akbaba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, heat transfer and friction factor were experimentally investigated for laminar, transitional, and turbulent flow regimes in circular mini-micro channels carrying pure water flow. Unlike many studies in the literature, a systematic experimental study was conducted under a constant surface temperature condition, encompassing multiple hydraulic diameters (381, 500, 750, and 1000 μm) and a wide range of Reynolds numbers (from 228 to 3120, with different flow rate 0–50 mL/min). The experimental setup consisted of a piston pump with precise flow and pressure control, a pressure regulator, heating–cooling baths, and high-accuracy temperature and pressure sensors. Thermo-hydraulic performance, including heat transfer, friction factor, pumping power, and entropy generation, were determined by simultaneously measuring the inlet and outlet temperatures and the pressure drop in the mini-micro channel. The obtained results were found to be in good agreement with the experimental data studied in the literature with similar hydraulic diameters. Based on the results at a flow rate of 50 mL/min, the maximum heat transfer was 45.96 W at a hydraulic diameter of 750 μm, while the minimum heat transfer at the same flow rate was 37.20 W at a hydraulic diameter of 381 μm. In terms of pressure drop, the highest was 16.358 bar at a flow rate of 50 mL/min and a hydraulic diameter of 381 μm, while the lowest was 0.089 bar at a flow rate of 50 mL/min and a hydraulic diameter of 1000 μm. These findings reveal the balance between heat transfer performance and pumping power requirements in mini-micro channel systems, offering comprehensive and original contributions to the literature on operational performance and efficiency optimization.Keywords: Mini-micro channel, Entropy generation, Heat transfer, Pressure dropSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Physics

Introduction

With the advancement of modern technologies, heat exchangers have become key components governing heat transfer performance in increasingly demanding cooling systems1–4. The reliable operation and extended lifetime of continuously miniaturized, highly integrated electronic devices depend on effective heat-dissipation mechanisms5,6. However, conventional heat exchanger designs are often inadequate to meet the extremely high heat fluxes of approximately 5 MW/m² encountered in integrated circuits, thereby motivating the development of innovative thermal management solutions7,8. Reducing the hydraulic diameter (Dh) in heat exchangers enhances the surface-to-volume ratio and compactness, enabling efficient heat transfer within limited volumes. This has promoted the widespread adoption of mini- and micro-scale channels in advanced technological fields such as energy storage systems, aerospace applications, microelectronic devices, and next-generation energy vehicles9–11. Nevertheless, the lack of a clear understanding of the validity limits of conventional heat transfer and friction factor correlations at reduced hydraulic diameters raises concerns about their applicability in the design of mini- and microchannel heat exchangers. This uncertainty complicates the selection of optimal channel geometries for specific operating conditions9. Consequently, comprehensive experimental investigations of heat transfer and pressure drop characteristics in mini- and microchannels over a wide range of diameters and across all flow regimes are required.

Yu et al.12 conducted water-flow experiments under laminar conditions in a rectangular microchannel with a depth (Dh) of 104 μm. They reported that, contrary to classical macro-scale theory, the Nusselt number (Nu), which is independent of flow velocity for fully developed internal flows, exhibited a linear increase with Reynolds number in microchannels. Lalagi et al.13 experimentally investigated the effects of various nanofluids and concentration ratios on heat transfer and pressure drop in microchannel heat exchangers under laminar flow conditions (Re = 100–500), reporting up to a 37% enhancement in heat transfer performance. Yang et al.14, through numerical simulations of two microchannel heat exchangers with Dh=260 μm operating over Re = 500–1500, demonstrated that increasing the Reynolds number and nanofluid concentration significantly increased the friction factor, leading to up to a 15% increase in pumping power requirements. Tam and Ghajar15 examined heat transfer characteristics in the laminar–turbulent transition region for smooth horizontal channels with a diameter-to-length ratio of 385 under constant wall heat flux conditions. They proposed correlations applicable to forced and mixed convection and developed a transition map that defines flow-regime boundaries for horizontal channels under various inlet conditions.

In a subsequent experimental study, Ghajar et al.16 investigated the friction factor behavior of single-phase water flow in the transition region using 12 stainless-steel mini–microchannels with diameters ranging from 337 μm to 2083 μm. Their results revealed that the friction factor profile remained essentially unchanged for diameters between 2083 μm and 1372 μm but became increasingly sensitive as the diameter decreased below 1372 μm. They further reported that the Reynolds number range corresponding to the transition regime narrows with decreasing channel diameter and that both the onset and completion of transition are diameter-dependent. Meyer and Hallquist17, experimentally studied water flow in a smooth channel with an inner diameter of 8 mm under constant wall heat flux conditions (1409, 3354, 5009, and 6881 W/m²) and Reynolds numbers ranging from 500 to 8800. They observed that, in the turbulent regime, the experimental results deviated by approximately 10% from the Ghajar correlation at Re = 5000; however, this deviation can be attributed to flow instabilities near the transition–turbulent boundary. At Re = 7000, good agreement was achieved. The laminar–turbulent transition was reported to occur in the range Re = 2100–2300, with increasing heat flux delaying the transition onset from Re = 2050 to 2280.

Tam et al.18 investigated the effects of different inlet geometries and heating conditions on friction behavior and heat transfer in a horizontal channel with an inner diameter of 1.48 cm under uniform average wall heat fluxes ranging from 4.3 to 8.9 kW/m². Their findings indicated that friction factor data tended toward fully developed turbulent behavior around Re = 3000 and decreased thereafter, whereas heat transfer continued to increase up to Re = 8000. Comparisons with existing correlations clearly highlighted the need for new correlations applicable to entrance and fully developed regions in these regimes. In their analysis, the thermophysical properties of water were obtained from the literature as temperature-dependent functions, evaluated at the mean fluid temperature, and subsequently used to calculate the corresponding Reynolds number. Dirker et al.19, whose work shares similarities in terms of operating with different flow types and hydraulic diameters, developed a series of friction factor correlations based on experimental data obtained for water flow in rectangular microchannels with hydraulic diameters of 0.57 mm, 0.85 mm, and 1.05 mm under laminar and transitional flow conditions. The proposed correlations predict between 94% and 100% of the experimental data within an error band of ± 10%, depending on the inlet geometry. They also demonstrated that bell-mouth and vortex-generating inlets provided greater heat transfer enhancement relative to the associated increase in friction factor than sudden contraction inlets during transition flow. Erdoğan and Zengin20, based on experimental results obtained in a microchannel with an inner diameter of 381 μm and a length of 20 cm operating at flow rates between 10 and 50 mL/min, reported laminar flow for Re < 2000, turbulent flow for Re > 3100–3500, and a transitional regime within 2000 < Re < 3500. More recently, Ma et al.21 numerically investigated the thermo-hydraulic performance of Ag, SiO₂, and Al₂O₃ nanofluids in rectangular, trapezoidal, and omega-shaped microchannels under a constant heat flux of 100 W/cm². Their results showed that increasing the Reynolds number significantly enhanced the Nusselt number and the convective heat transfer coefficient, while simultaneously increasing the pressure drop and the pumping power requirements. Additionally, total entropy generation decreased with increasing Reynolds number, indicating reduced thermal and flow irreversibilities within the system. Recent studies by Ayegba et al.22–25 have provided comprehensive insights into the hydrodynamics, interfacial friction factor, and heat transfer characteristics of annular flow boiling in millimetric channels, including both normal and microgravity conditions. These works highlight the strong coupling between flow structure and thermal performance, emphasizing the need for accurate modelling approaches to predict heat transfer behavior in advanced thermal systems.

Despite extensive research, in Table 1. the majority of studies in the literature focus on heat transfer and pressure drop in mini–micro channels for a single hydraulic diameter and a single flow regime, predominantly laminar flow. In contrast, the distinctive contribution of the present study lies in its systematic investigation of circular mini-micro channels with multiple hydraulic diameters (381, 500, 750, and 1000 μm) under constant wall temperature conditions over a wide flow-rate range (0–50 mL/min), encompassing laminar, transitional, and turbulent regimes. Within this framework, critical thermo-hydraulic performance, including heat transfer performance, pressure drop, friction factor, and entropy generation, are comprehensively analyzed. Furthermore, the effect of hydraulic diameter on pumping power requirements and its trade-off with heat transfer enhancement is evaluated, providing substantial and original contributions to the literature on performance efficiency optimization for mini-micro channel applications.

Materials and methods

Experimental setup

The experimental setup shown in Fig. 1 was designed to investigate the heat transfer and friction factor characteristics of pure water flow in circular mini-micro channels under laminar, transitional, and turbulent flow regimes at constant wall temperature. The working fluid is initially supplied from a deionized water reservoir (1) and delivered to the system using a flow-rate-controlled piston pump (Gilson 305, 0–50 mL/min, 0–140 bar) (2). The inlet pressure conditions are precisely adjusted using a pressure regulator (Gilson 806 manometric pressure module, up to 320 bar) (3). Before entering the test section, the fluid inlet temperature is regulated to approximately 30 °C using a heating bath (JSR JSIB-11T, 5–99 °C, 12 L/min, 250 mbar) (5). The fluid then passes through a double-pipe stainless steel heat exchanger (outer diameter: 9 mm, length: 100 cm) (4), where a cooling bath (JSR JSRC-22 C, − 20 to 120 °C, 12 L/min, 250 mbar) (6) is employed to maintain a constant wall temperature condition of approximately 10 °C. Subsequently, the conditioned fluid flows through the mini-micro channel (Supplied by Upchurch Scientific®, hydraulic diameters of 381, 500, 750, and 1000 μm) (8), which is mounted inside a polymer-body heat exchanger (7). The fluid temperatures at the channel inlet (T1), outlet (T2), and at the cold-side inlet and outlet locations (T3 and T4) of the mini–micro channel were measured using high-precision thermistors (Thermometrics Amphenol, 10 kΩ, − 50 to 150 °C). The pressure drop across the test section is measured with a differential pressure sensor (Keller PR-21Y) (9), allowing the friction factor to be determined. All temperature and pressure data are acquired simultaneously using a data acquisition system (LNL, AYA-Logger) (11) and processed in a computer environment (12). This configuration allows the heat transfer coefficient and hydrodynamic performance to be determined reliably and reproducibly under constant wall temperature conditions over a wide range of Reynolds numbers. Results and discussion

The selection of equations for comparing experimental and theoretical results depends on the fluid’s flow regime and the conditions for its hydrodynamic and thermal development. Therefore, the fluid’s development conditions were particularly considered in this study. The Edwards equation can be applied to circular pipes with a constant surface temperature for hydrodynamically developed but thermally developing laminar flow; the Sieder–Tate equation can be used to both hydrodynamically and thermally developing laminar flow26. Figure 3 presents the change in the Nusselt number as a function of the Reynolds number, comparing experimental data with predictions from the Edwards and Sieder-Tate equations. The experimentally obtained Nusselt numbers were found to be lower than those predicted by the Sieder–Tate equation and Edwards correlation correlations, primarily due to the dominance of entrance region effects, increased axial heat conduction, and enhanced relative surface roughness effects in mini–micro channels, which are not fully accounted for in conventional correlations developed for macroscale channels. Morever, the experimental results show an increasing trend with increasing Reynolds number and are generally consistent with both theoretical relationships. At higher Reynolds numbers, the increased scatter in the experimental Nusselt data can be attributed to the stronger influence of the entrance region, where undeveloped velocity and thermal boundary layers cause local fluctuations in heat transfer behavior within the mini–micro channel. At low Reynolds numbers, in the laminar flow region, the experimental data follow the Sieder–Tate equation more closely, while with increasing Reynolds number in the turbulent flow region, a tendency to approach the Edwards equation is observed. However, at high Reynolds numbers, experimental Nusselt numbers are observed to remain slightly above theoretical values, and these deviations can be attributed to microchannel and inlet-region effects.

Experimental results

In this study, experiments were conducted at different flow rates ranging from 10 to 50 mL/min, different hydraulic diameters from 381 to 1000 μm, a fluid inlet temperature of approximately 30 °C, and a constant surface (cold-side) temperature of 10 °C. The results were examined and compared for heat transfer, pressure drop, friction factor, and entropy generation.

The experimental results for pressure drop (ΔP) as a function of Reynolds number are presented in Fig. 5. As shown in the figure, ΔP increases with Reynolds number for all channel diameters, following a nonlinear trend, with smaller diameters exhibiting higher pressure drops. For instance, the 400 μm channel reaches a pressure drop of 16.358 bar at a Reynolds number of 3120 (at 50 mL/min), while the 1000 μm channel shows only 0.089 bar at a Reynolds number of 1177 (at the same flow rate of 50 mL/min). The corresponding table highlights the measured ΔP values at different flow rates (10–50 mL/min) for channels of diameters 400, 500, 750, and 1000 μm, along with the calculated Reynolds numbers. Smaller channels consistently show higher Reynolds numbers at equivalent flow rates due to the reduced hydraulic diameter, and the results demonstrate the expected increase in ΔP with both flow rate and decreasing diameter. Overall, these data illustrate the strong dependence of pressure drop on channel size and flow conditions in mini-micro channel systems.

Figure 6 illustrates the variation of the friction factor with Reynolds number for mini- and micro-channels with different hydraulic diameters. In the legend, the values 381, 500, 750, and 1000 represent the channel hydraulic diameters in micrometers (µm), with each color corresponding to a specific diameter. The fitted curves shown in Fig. 5 were generated using fifth-order polynomial functions selected to provide the maximum coefficient of determination (R²) for the experimental data. At a given Reynolds number, smaller hydraulic diameters tend to exhibit larger friction factors. For example, in the 381 μm channel, the measured values in the range Re = 1800–2300 are up to about four times higher than those observed for the larger diameters (e.g., 500 μm). This difference can be attributed to enhanced wall effects and increased flow resistance as the characteristic dimension decreases. While the separation between the curves is more evident at lower Reynolds numbers, they approach each other as Reynolds number increases, suggesting a gradual change in flow structure and reduced sensitivity to diameter. Nevertheless, this interpretation is limited to the experimental conditions considered in the present study.

Figure 7 presents the variation of total entropy generation (Sgen) with volumetric flow rate (10–50 mL/min) based on the experimental data. Within this interval, the highest entropy levels are generally obtained for the 381 μm channel, followed by 500, 1000, and 750 μm. At 50 mL/min, the maximum value reaches 112.918 × 10⁻⁷ W/K for the 381 μm diameter, whereas the minimum value is observed for the larger channels within the tested range. This tendency may be related to the growth of velocity gradients and pressure losses, which contribute to higher irreversibilities at elevated flow conditions. Smaller hydraulic diameters tend to exhibit greater entropy production under identical flow rates, possibly due to enhanced wall influence and frictional effects. As the characteristic channel dimension decreases, the increased surface-area-to-volume ratio intensifies viscous shear stress at the wall, leading to greater flow resistance and consequently higher friction factor values. Moreover, the separation between the curves becomes more visible toward the upper end of the investigated flow-rate range, implying a stronger sensitivity of thermodynamic performance to geometry. Nevertheless, these interpretations should be considered in light of the specific experimental conditions and uncertainties of the present study.

Figure 8 illustrates the variation of heat transfer with volumetric flow rate (10–50 mL/min) for mini–microchannels having different hydraulic diameters. The measurements indicate an approximately linear increase in heat transfer with increasing flow rate for all tested geometries. Within the investigated range, the highest values are generally observed for the 750 μm channel, followed by 1000, 500, and 381 μm. For instance, at 50 mL/min the heat transfer reaches 45.96 W for the 750 μm diameter, whereas the lowest value, 37.20 W, is recorded for the 381 μm diameter. This behavior may be associated with the increase in mean velocity and the resulting enhancement of convective transport as the flow rate rises. Under identical flow-rate conditions, larger hydraulic diameters tend to provide higher heat transfer levels. Although the separation between the curves is relatively small at low flow rates, the influence of diameter becomes more noticeable toward the upper end of the tested range. These findings suggest that geometric parameters can significantly affect heat transfer performance; however, this interpretation should be considered within the specific operating conditions and uncertainties of the present experiments.

Figure 9 presents the variation of the performance ratio (Pη), defined as the ratio of heat transfer rate to pumping power, with volumetric flow rate for mini–micro channels of different hydraulic diameters. Here, the pumping power is defined as Wp = ΔP×, where ΔP is the pressure drop along the channel and , is the volumetric flow rate. The measurements show that Pη increases with increasing flow rate for all tested diameters, which may be associated with the rise in fluid velocity and the accompanying pressure losses. The validity of the selected performance ratio Pη (defined as the ratio of heat transfer rate to pumping power) should be interpreted within the investigated range of hydraulic diameters and finite flow rates. In principle, as the hydraulic diameter increases significantly, the increase in flow cross-sectional area may dominate over the heat transfer surface enhancement, potentially leading to a decrease in Pη. Similarly, as the flow rate approaches zero, both heat transfer rate and pumping power tend toward zero, causing the ratio to lose physical significance. Therefore, the present results should be considered as a comparative performance metric within the studied operating range, where the observed trends remain physically meaningful and relevant for engineering applications. To provide a more detailed analysis based on diameters in this specific study, smaller hydraulic diameters (e.g., 381 μm) tend to yield higher Pη values, suggesting that the relative contribution of frictional effects becomes more pronounced under these conditions. In contrast, larger diameters (750–1000 μm) generally produce lower Pr values within the same flow-rate interval. These observations highlight that the choice of hydraulic diameter can noticeably influence thermo-hydraulic performance; however, the extent of this influence should be interpreted within the specific experimental range, surface characteristics, and operating conditions considered in the present study.

Conclusion

This study experimentally investigated the thermo-hydraulic performance of water flow in circular mini–microchannels with hydraulic diameters of 381, 500, 750, and 1000 μm under constant wall temperature conditions, covering laminar, transitional, and near-turbulent flow regimes over a volumetric flow rate range of 10–50 mL/min. The main novelty of this work lies in the integrated evaluation of heat transfer and pumping power through the performance ratio (Pη), enabling the identification of optimal channel diameter and operating conditions at the microscale.

The results demonstrate that decreasing the channel diameter enhances the heat transfer rate; however, this improvement is accompanied by a substantial increase in pressure drop and, consequently, pumping power. In particular, the 381 μm channel provides the highest heat transfer performance, but at the expense of significantly elevated pumping power requirements. Conversely, the 1000 μm channel exhibits the lowest pumping power demand but comparatively weaker heat transfer capability.

The results demonstrate that decreasing the channel diameter enhances the heat transfer rate; however, this improvement is accompanied by a substantial increase in pressure drop and, consequently, pumping power. In particular, the 381 μm channel provides the highest heat transfer performance, but at the expense of significantly elevated pumping power requirements. Conversely, the 1000 μm channel exhibits the lowest pumping power demand but comparatively weaker heat transfer capability.

The performance ratio (Pη), defined as the ratio of heat transfer rate to pumping power, reveals that smaller diameters do not necessarily yield superior overall performance. Instead, an optimal trade-off emerges within an intermediate diameter range. Under the investigated conditions, channels with diameters in the range of approximately 500–750 μm provide a more balanced thermo-hydraulic performance. Furthermore, increasing the flow rate leads to simultaneous increases in both heat transfer rate and pumping power; however, their non-proportional growth results in a nonlinear variation of the performance ratio.

The performance ratio (Pη), defined as the ratio of heat transfer rate to pumping power, reveals that smaller diameters do not necessarily yield superior overall performance. Instead, an optimal trade-off emerges within an intermediate diameter range. Under the investigated conditions, channels with diameters in the range of approximately 500–750 μm provide a more balanced thermo-hydraulic performance. Furthermore, increasing the flow rate leads to simultaneous increases in both heat transfer rate and pumping power; however, their non-proportional growth results in a nonlinear variation of the performance ratio.

Comparisons with classical macro-scale correlations indicate that the Sieder–Tate correlation remains reasonably accurate in the laminar regime, whereas deviations become more pronounced in the transitional regime due to microscale effects such as surface roughness, entrance effects, and incomplete flow development. In this context, Edwards-type correlations provide improved predictive capability. Additionally, friction factors in microchannels are found to be consistently higher than those predicted by conventional smooth-tube theory, highlighting the importance of scale-dependent effects in design considerations.

Overall, this study provides a quantitative framework for evaluating the trade-off between thermal performance and hydraulic efficiency in microchannels, demonstrating that maximizing heat transfer alone is not sufficient for optimal design. The use of the performance ratio (Pη) as a unified metric offers a more realistic basis for determining optimal operating conditions and channel dimensions. These findings contribute experimentally to the literature by clarifying the role of hydraulic diameter across multiple flow regimes and by identifying an optimal performance window within the microscale range. Nevertheless, the conclusions are limited to the tested fluid, geometries, and operating conditions; further studies involving different fluids, extended Reynolds number ranges, and alternative microchannel configurations are recommended to generalize the results and support robust design methodologies.

Uncertainty analysis is applied to evaluate the reliability and significance of results obtained from experimental studies, to determine the extent to which measured or calculated values may deviate from the actual value, and to reveal the expected range of the exact value. This analysis method differs depending on whether the quantity for which the uncertainty is to be determined is a directly measured variable or an indirect variable calculated using directly measured variables28.
