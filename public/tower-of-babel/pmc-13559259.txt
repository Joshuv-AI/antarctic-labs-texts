Simplifying intrapleural fibrinolytic therapy in pleural infection: the need for risk-stratified patient selection
Authors: Tongwei Xiang, Tao Yu
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the recent studies by Chong et al. (1) and Yong et al. (2) in the Journal of Thoracic Disease, which explored whether intrapleural fibrinolytic therapy (IPFT) for pleural infection could be delivered in a simpler manner. These studies are timely and clinically relevant, as any strategy that reduces treatment complexity while preserving efficacy and safety would be highly valuable in routine practice.

The important issue, however, may not be whether IPFT can be simplified in general, but rather in whom simplification is most appropriate. Pleural infection is a heterogeneous condition, and patients differ substantially in bleeding risk, radiographic complexity, clinical severity, and the likelihood of requiring escalation of care. In this context, a simplified regimen may be reasonable for selected patients with less complex disease and lower bleeding risk, whereas patients with extensive septations, higher bleeding risk, or more severe illness may still benefit from more individualized or standard protocols.

From this perspective, the current evidence should perhaps be interpreted as supporting feasibility, but not yet as defining universal applicability. Although both studies provide important real-world and comparative data, neither appears to have fully addressed effect modification across clinically relevant risk strata. In particular, subgroup analyses according to baseline bleeding risk, radiographic loculation, or established severity scores such as RAPID (Renal, Age, Purulence, Infection source, Dietary factors) score (3) may help clarify whether the observed equivalence applies uniformly across patient subsets or is driven by lower-risk populations. Without such stratification, an overall comparison may mask clinically meaningful heterogeneity.

This issue is not merely methodological; it has direct implications for bedside decision-making. A risk-stratified approach may help identify patients in whom simplification is most likely to preserve benefit while minimizing harm. Future prospective studies should therefore move beyond the question of whether simplified IPFT is feasible and ask more precisely which patients are best suited for it.

We commend the authors for advancing this important field. Their work provides a valuable foundation for further refinement of IPFT strategies. The next step, in our view, is to integrate simplification with patient selection, so that treatment can be tailored more precisely to disease complexity and individual risk.
