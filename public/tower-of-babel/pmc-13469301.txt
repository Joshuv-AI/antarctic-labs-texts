Antiviral activity of Saponin C from Liriope muscari against influenza A virus and SARS-CoV-2
Authors: Yuhan Gong, Junwen Liu, Wenxuan Ding, Yuxing Yan, Xue Hu, Gary Wong, Huaijie Jia, Zhengxiang Wang, Xin Li, Jiayi Chen, Xin Zhang, Chao Shan, Shuai Xu, Xiaoxia Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Saponin C (also known as DT-13) is the dominant ingredient extracted from the tuber of Liriope muscari (Decne.) L. H. Bailey, which exhibits multiple pharmacological activities, including anti-tumor, anti-thrombotic, cardioprotective and anti-inflammatory effects. However, its antiviral potential remains unexplored. This study aimed to investigate the inhibitory activity and underlying mechanisms of Saponin C against both influenza A virus (IAV) and SARS-CoV-2. In vitro experiments demonstrated that Saponin C dose-dependently inhibited IAV replication in MDCK and A549 cells. Time-of-addition assay revealed that Saponin C targeted the post-entry stages of IAV replication by impairing viral ribonucleoprotein (vRNP) function. This was evidenced by the suppression of viral polymerase activity, reduction of viral RNA synthesis, and delayed nuclear export of vRNP. The in vivo efficacy was further validated in a lethal H1N1 mouse model, where both prophylactic and therapeutic administration of Saponin C significantly improved the survival rate of infected mice, reduced viral loads in lung tissues, and attenuated virus-induced pulmonary inflammation in a dose-dependent manner. Beyond IAV, Saponin C also effectively inhibited SARS-CoV-2 infection by blocking viral entry, specifically through interfering with the binding of viral spike protein to the human ACE2 receptor and suppressing spike-mediated membrane fusion. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Saponin C possesses antiviral potential against IAV and SARS-CoV-2, and may thus serve as a promising therapeutic agent for the treatment of respiratory viral infections.

Introduction

Respiratory infections pose significant threats to the global health, due to their widespread prevalence, substantial morbidity and mortality. Among emerging respiratory viruses, influenza viruses and coronaviruses (CoVs) are two of the most high-profile pathogens. Since the 1990s, over 17 emerging respiratory viruses have been documented on a global scale, with eight influenza viruses (H5N1, H9N2, H7N7, H1N1 swine flu, H3N2 swine flu, H1N1pdm09, H7N9 and H5N6) and five CoVs (SARS-CoV, CoV-NL63, CoV-HKU1, MERS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2) (Al-Tawfiq et al., 2014; Luo et al., 2023). These pathogens circulate around the world, and pose a threat to individuals in all age groups (Brody, 2019; Salzberger et al., 2021). Moreover, co-infections with influenza and SARS-CoV-2 is inevitable, which may result in higher levels of severe lung damage and more serious clinical symptoms (Swets et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022).

Compared to antibiotics for bacterial infections, the range of drugs available to treat viral respiratory infections remains very limited. Most viral infections are treated symptomatically, and the emergence of drug-resistant strains can curtail the efficacy of antiviral drugs. Current anti-influenza drugs include M2 ion channel blockers (e.g., amantadine and rimantadine), neuraminidase (NA) inhibitors (e.g., oseltamivir, zanamivir and peramivir), and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) inhibitors (e.g., favipiravir, baloxavir marboxil and Onradivir) (Kumari et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025). However, most circulating influenza strains are resistant to M2 blockers, rendering them clinically obsolete (Baranovich et al., 2015). Resistance to oseltamivir, the most widely used anti-influenza compound, have also been reported continuously worldwide (Lackenby et al., 2011; Renaud et al., 2011). Moreover, resistance to baloxavir, a recently approved anti-influenza compound, has already been observed in clinical trials (Hayden et al., 2018; Takashita et al., 2019; Hirotsu et al., 2020). It remains critical to develop highly effective and broad-spectrum antivirals for the prevention and treatment of viral infections.

Natural products, especially phytochemicals, have long been served as the major source for drug discovery, attributable to their inherent chemical and structural diversity, as well as biological relevance (Koehn and Carter, 2005; Newman and Cragg, 2020). As a prominent class of phytochemicals, saponins are widely distributed in medicinal and edible plants, displaying diverse pharmacological activities—from anti-inflammatory to antiviral effects (Mieres-Castro and Mora-Poblete, 2023; Zheng et al., 2025). As reported previously, saponins from Panax ginseng ginsenoside rk1 and rg5 exhibited significant antiviral effects against H1N1, H5N1 and H3N2 in vitro (Yang et al., 2023). Saikosaponin A, from the root of Bupleurum chinense DC, not only inhibited influenza A virus (IAV) replication but also alleviated virus-induced inflammatory responses in C57BL/6 mice (Chen et al., 2015). Anemoside B4, the predominant triterpenoidal saponin in Pulsatilla chinensis, has been shown to suppress both IAV- and SARS-CoV-2-induced pneumonia, potentially by inhibiting viral entry and modulating inflammatory pathways (He et al., 2020; Xiao et al., 2024). Given their structural diversity, investigating the antiviral potential of saponins derived from different plants may be promising for discovering a wider range of novel antiviral agents.

Liriope saponin C (also known as DT-13) is the dominant ingredient extracted from the tuber of Liriope muscari (Decne.) L. H. Bailey (Chinese name: Duanting Shan-maidong) with anti-tumor, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities (Khan et al., 2018), but the role played by Saponin C in infectious diseases is unknown. In this study, we investigated whether Saponin C possesses antiviral activity against IAV and SARS-CoV-2 in vitro and in vivo, and characterized the underlying mechanisms.

Results

Saponin C exhibits antiviral activity against IAV in vitro

In this study, we focused on Saponin C from L. muscari (Fig. 1A). To quantify the anti-IAV potential of this compound, we used a previously constructed recombinant virus H1N1-HiBiT, in which the flexible linker region (amino acids 77 to 84) in the NS1 protein of PR8 was replaced by HiBiT, an 11-amino acid peptide derived from the NanoLuciferase (NanoLuc), and viral replication could be monitored by the Nano-Glo assay system (Wang, Z. et al., 2025). First, the cells were infected with H1N1-HiBiT followed by Saponin C treatment, then the luciferase activity in cell lysates were measured at 24 h post-infection. The result showed that Saponin C inhibited viral replication in MDCK cells in a dose-dependent manner, with an IC50 value of 1.10 μM and a CC50 value of 54.92 μM, yielding a selectivity index (SI) of 50.02 (Fig. 1B). Consistently, Saponin C also inhibited H1N1-HiBiT infection in A549 cells, with an IC50 value of 2.38 μM and a CC50 value of 25.04 μM, resulting in a SI of 10.50 (Fig. 1C). As a control, oseltamivir phosphate inhibited H1N1-HiBiT infection in MDCK cells with an IC50 of 21.1 μM, which is consistent with previously reported values (Supplementary Fig. S1) (Huang et al., 2023). Additionally, the expression of NP protein was detected in MDCK cells by immunofluorescence analysis at 24 h post H1N1 (PR8) virus infection. Saponin C interferes with the post entry stages of IAV replication

To uncover the mechanism behind Saponin C-mediated inhibition, we first performed attachment and entry experiments in MDCK cells. The cells were co-treated with Saponin C and H1N1-HiBiT at 4 °C for 1 h to allow the virus to attach to the cell surface (adsorption), and then followed by 1 h incubation at 37 °C to allow for virus entry (internalization). After PBS washing, the number of virus particles associated with the cells was quantified by the luciferase assay. Similar levels of IAV particles were detected with or without Saponin C treatment, suggesting that the compound did not affect viral attachment or entry (Fig. 3A and B). Meanwhile, the inhibition of Saponin C on IAV-induced aggregation of chicken erythrocytes was evaluated using a hemagglutination inhibition (HI) assay. The results showed that even at a concentration as high as 100 μM, Saponin C failed to inhibit the agglutination of IAV to chicken erythrocytes (Fig. 3C), suggesting that the compound did not interfere with virus-receptor binding. Subsequently, to verify the steps in IAV life cycle impacted by Saponin C, we performed a time-of-addition assay (Fig. 3D). The viral life cycle was divided into six intervals, and Saponin C (10 μM) was added at the beginning of each interval and maintained throughout the specified time periods during H1N1-HiBiT infection (-2–0, 0–2, 2–4, 4–6, 6–8, 8–10, and -2–24 h). As shown in Fig. 3E, the most pronounced inhibitory effect was observed when Saponin C was presented at the viral replication stage (2–4 or 4–6 h post-infection). In contrast, when Saponin C was added prior to infection (-2–0 h), during viral entry (0–2 h) or release stage (8–10 h), no significant inhibitory activity was observed. These results suggest that Saponin C mainly impairs the IAV life cycle after viral entry.

Saponin C inhibits viral RNA synthesis by targeting vRNP

To further determine the action of Saponin C on viral RNA synthesis, MDCK cells were infected with PR8 virus for 1 h and then treated with various concentrations of Saponin C (1.25–20 μM) for 6 h. Then, the total RNA was extracted, and subjected to qRT-PCR to quantify vRNA, mRNA, and cRNA copies of viral NP gene. As expected, the accumulation of viral genomic RNA (vRNA) and viral transcripts (mRNA) was substantially decreased in the presence of Saponin C in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4A and B). However, the significant decrease of viral cRNA was only observed when Saponin C was present at higher concentrations (10 μM and 20 μM) (Fig. 4C). Since the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) in the context of viral ribonucleoprotein (vRNP) complexes is critical for the transcription and replication of the viral RNA genome (Fodor and Te Velthuis, 2020), we next examined the effect of Saponin C on RdRp activity through mini-replicon assay in 293T cells. The polymerase activity was shown to be considerably decreased with increasing amounts of Saponin C; and at the final concentration of 5 μM or above, a nearly 90% reduction of the enzymatic activity was observed (Fig. 4D).

We also employed a time-course immunofluorescence assay to localize and examine the nucleocytoplasmic shuttling of vRNP complex, which is also vital for viral replication. Saponin C treatment was shown to delay the export of vRNP from the nucleus and retained vRNP in the nucleus 6–8 h after IAV infection (Fig. 4E). Additionally, the binding affinities (ΔG, kcal/mol) of Saponin C against the individual subunits of RdRp were summarized in Supplementary Table S1, and the docking diagrams were shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. Saponin C exhibited strong binding to influenza polymerase subunits, with the highest affinity for PA (−8.6 kcal/mol), followed by PB2 (−8.1 kcal/mol) and PB1 (−8.1 kcal/mol). Collectively, these results indicate that Saponin C may directly target the vRNP complex and impair RdRp activity, which is essential for viral RNA synthesis.

As the main component of the vRNP complex, NP protein mediates the nuclear transport of vRNP (Eisfeld et al., 2015). Thus, nuclear retention of the vRNP complex may result from impaired nuclear export of NP. To test this possibility, we analyzed the effect of Saponin C on NP nuclear export in cells transfected with the plasmid encoding NP gene. The indirect immunofluorescence assay showed that the subcellular localization of NP protein was not altered by Saponin C (5–20 μM) treatment (Fig. 5A). Separately, we examined the effect of Saponin C on the binding between NP and host protein CRM1, which is involved in the nuclear export of NP (Yu et al., 2012). Co-immunoprecipitation assays revealed that NP immunoprecipitated CRM1 equally in the presence or absence of Saponin C, indicating that the compound did not disrupt the NP-CRM1 interaction (Fig. 5B). To further investigate whether Saponin C directly binds to NP or CRM1, we performed a cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA). The Tagg values of NP and CRM1 were measured in the absence or presence of Saponin C (40 μM) at temperatures ranging from 35 to 75 °C. As shown in Fig. 5C–F, Saponin C failed to enhance the thermal stability of either NP or CRM1, suggesting that it does not bind to these two proteins. Saponin C protects mice from lethal IAV infection

To further assess the anti-IAV potential of Saponin C in vivo, we utilized a lethal H1N1-PR8 mouse model. Briefly, BALB/c mice were intranasally infected with a high dose (105 EID50) of PR8 virus and monitored daily for weight loss and mortality over a 14-day period. Three treatment doses of Saponin C (12.5, 25 and 50 mg/kg per day) were selected and administered once per day from one day before infection for six consecutive days via intraperitoneal (IP) injection. In addition, oseltamivir was administered via IP injection at a dose of 15 mg/kg per day (Fig. 6A). The results showed that mice except the ones receiving high-dose Saponin C (50 mg/kg per day) began to lose weight on day 3 post-infection, and the mice treated with medium-dose Saponin C (25 mg/kg per day) and oseltamivir regained weight starting on day 8 and day 12 post-infection, respectively (Fig. 6B). In addition, the acute lung injury, as examined on day 3 and day 5 post-infection by histopathological examination, was remarkably alleviated after Saponin C treatment in a dose-dependent manner, and less inflammatory cell infiltration and pathological changes were observed (Fig. 6D). Moreover, Saponin C treatment significantly reduced viral titer in lung tissues by day 3 post-infection when administered at high-dose, which was comparable to the efficacy of oseltamivir, and reduced viral titers on day 5 post-infection in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 6E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that Saponin C exhibit anti-viral potential against IAV in vivo.

To further assess the therapeutic potency of Saponin C, mice were received 50 mg/kg of Saponin C from 24 h before (Pre-24h) or 2 h after (Post-2h) PR8 infection (Fig. 7A). The results showed that Saponin C could protect mice from weight loss when the treatment started at either per- or post-infection (Fig. 7B). The Post-2h treatment of Saponin C was found to increase the survival rate up to 83.33%, compared to 33.3% provided by oseltamivir administered at the same time point, while the Pre-24h treatment of Saponin C resulted in 100% survival (Fig. 7C). Similar to pre-treatment, the therapeutic administration of Saponin C also substantially altered lung pathology and viral load following IAV infection (Fig. 7D and E). It suggests that when Saponin C is administered intraperitoneally, it can elicit a therapeutic effect even after infection. To test the antiviral efficacy of Saponin C against SARS-CoV-2, Vero-E6 cells were treated with a serial concentration of Saponin C and infected with SARS-CoV-2 Omicron. The viral load was quantitated by qRT-PCR at 24 h post-infection. Subsequently, the inhibitory effect of Saponin C on viral entry of SARS-CoV-2 was evaluated. Both Vero E6-hACE2 and 293T-hACE2 cells were treated with Saponin C, at concentrations ranging from 1 μM to 20 μM, and infected with lentiviral-based pseudovirus harboring the SARS-CoV-2 spike. Notably, Saponin C inhibited pseudovirus infection in hACE2+ cells in a dose-dependent manner, with IC50 values of 7.18 μM in Vero E6-hACE2 cells and 1.5 μM in 293T-hACE2 cells, respectively (Fig. 8B and C). In contrast, Saponin C treatment had no impact on vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein (VSVG) pseudotyped viral entry (Supplementary Fig. S3). Meanwhile, we evaluated its inhibitory activity against the viral main protease (Mpro) using a FRET-based enzymatic assay (Ye et al., 2025, 2026). As shown in Supplementary Fig. S4, Saponin C at concentrations up to 200 μM exhibited no inhibitory effect on Mpro activity. Collectively, these results indicated that Saponin C primarily inhibits SARS-CoV-2 infection by targeting viral entry.

Saponin C blocks SARS-CoV-2 entry by targeting the spike protein

To further confirm that Saponin C mainly acts at the early events of SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus infection, we performed time-of-addition assay. As shown in Fig. 9A, Saponin C exhibited potent antiviral activity when added at the time of infection, whereas its efficacy was markedly reduced but remained significant when added 2 h post-infection (P < 0.001 versus DMSO control). Since the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 binds to the ACE2 receptor on target cells is crucial for viral entry (Shang et al., 2020), we first examined whether the compound inhibits the binding of spike–hACE2 using ELISA. Given that spike–ACE2 engagement and subsequent internalization occur predominantly within the first 2 h of infection, the remaining antiviral effect observed when Saponin C was added at 2 h post-infection likely results from interference with post-binding events. To further explore this possibility, the action of Saponin C on the spike-mediated membrane fusion was examined using a cell-cell fusion assay. 293T cells co-overexpressing SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and GFP were used as the effector cells, and 293T-hACE2 cells were used as target cells (Wang et al., 2021). When the effector and target cells were cocultured, cell-cell fusion (syncytia formation), was extensively observed in control group, characterized by larger cell size and weaker fluorescence intensity (Fig. 9D). In contrast, the syncytia formation was significantly reduced after the addition of saponin C in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 9E). This result indicates that the compound can interfere with spike-mediated membrane fusion, although this effect may be secondary to the inhibition of spike–ACE2 interaction.

In addition, Saponin C demonstrated moderate-to-strong binding affinity with SARS-CoV-2 spike protein in the S1-RBD region (−7.4 to −7.1 kcal/mol) and the S2 fusion peptide (−7.2 kcal/mol) (Supplementary Table S2, Supplementary Fig. S5). Interestingly, its binding to hACE2 was weaker at −5.9 kcal/mol, suggesting Saponin C selectivity towards viral proteins. These findings suggest that the saponin may exert its anti-SARS-CoV-2 potency by targeting SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, and action on spike-hACE2 interaction and spike-mediated membrane fusion.

Discussion

Respiratory viruses possess a remarkable capacity to cause epidemics and pandemics (Muthukutty et al., 2024). Antiviral therapy, serving as a valuable complement to prophylactic vaccines, is indispensable for the alleviation of respiratory symptoms and the prevention of secondary complications (Gao et al., 2025). Accordingly, the efforts to develop more potent and broad-spectrum antivirals could assist in better disease management and pandemic preparedness (Jochmans et al., 2023).

Various species from genus Liriope, including L. platyphylla, L. muscari, L. graminifolia and L. spicata, etc, have been utilized as traditional medicine across East and South Asia since ancient times (Wu et al., 2014; Shang et al., 2017). Especially, ingredients from the tubers of Liriope have displayed a variety of biological and pharmacological functions. Saponin C is a steroidal saponin isolated from the roots of L. muscari. Previous studies have predominantly focused on the effects of Saponin C in cancer therapy. Here, we report another therapeutic feature of Saponin C: the antiviral property against influenza A and SARS-CoV-2.

Indeed, there were few reports on the antiviral effects of phytochemicals from genus Liriope. In one study, the effect of the L. platyphylla ethanol extract (LPE) on Hepatitis E Virus (HEV) replication was reported, and spirostane-type saponin A, isolated from the ethyl acetate fraction of LPE, specifically inhibited the replication of genotype 3 HEV replicons (Park et al., 2019). Compound LPRP-Et-97543, another chemical isolated from the roots of L. platyphylla, exhibited antiviral effects against Hepatitis B Virus (HBV) by inhibiting viral DNA replication, and viral HBsAg and HBeAg secretion (Huang et al., 2014). In our study, Saponin C from L. muscari blocked both influenza virus and SARS-CoV-2 replication without cytotoxicity at therapeutic doses, which highlights the broad-spectrum antiviral potential of Saponin C. Additionally, Saponin C is less toxic as compared to other saponins of its class. It showed no genotoxicity (500 μg/plate in Ames and 5000 mg/kg in mouse micronucleus tests), and did not cause mortality or clinical abnormalities in SD rats after repeated oral administration (360 mg/kg body weight per day) (Ma et al., 2011). Collectively, Saponin C not only serves as an efficient anti-viral compound but also possesses a high safety profile for in vivo applications.

With the time-of-addition assay, we found that Saponin C inhibited the IAV replication at the post entry stages. Our results showed that Saponin C failed to target HA receptor and block viral replication at the very first steps (attachment and internalization). However, the compound did inhibit the synthesis of viral RNA, mRNA and cRNA, and delayed the nuclear export of vRNPs, thereby reducing progeny virus production. After entering host cells, influenza virions release vRNPs into the cytoplasm, which translocate to the nucleus to initiate vRNA transcription (vRNA→mRNA). Viral mRNA is then exported to the cytoplasm for protein synthesis. Newly synthesized NP and RdRp components (PA, PB1, and PB2) return to the nucleus, enabling vRNA replication (vRNA→cRNA→vRNA) and progeny vRNP assembly (Du et al., 2023). During this process, the RdRp is responsible for the transcription and replication of the IAV genome in the nucleus. Notably, Saponin C was found to inhibit the RdRp activity in the mini-replicon assay. This finding suggests that Saponin C regulates IAV replication through targeting RdRp, although the precise mechanism by which Saponin C inhibits RdRp remains unclear and requires further investigation. Additionally, since RdRp is highly conserved among all influenza strains, antivirals targeting RdRp, including Saponin C, are more likely to be broad-spectrum and less likely to develop drug resistance (Malik et al., 2024).

Saponin C is a steroidal saponin consisting of a non-polar aglycone (sapogenin) bonded to sugar moieties. Upon oral administration, saponin undergoes extensive gut microbiota-mediated deglycosylation, releasing sapogenin as the primary metabolite (Navarro Del Hierro et al., 2018; Li et al., 2020; Sharma et al., 2021). Consistently, the oral bioavailability of Saponin C in rats is very limited, reported to be only 5.51% (Chen et al., 2011). Importantly, our in vitro study showed that the sugar moieties are indispensable for the antiviral activity of Saponin C (Supplementary Fig. S6). Although ruscogenin exhibited lower cytotoxicity than its glycosylated precursor, it failed to inhibit viral replication even at high concentrations (up to 80 μM). Therefore, to avoid deglycosylation and preserve the essential sugar moieties, Saponin C was administrated via IP injection in the present study. Pharmacokinetic study also showed that following IP injection, saponins achieve significantly higher concentrations in the lungs and other tissues compared with oral administration (Zhu et al., 2025). Thus, following IP injection, Saponin C likely reaches target organs primarily in its glycosylated form.

Our results showed that administration of Saponin C starting at 24 h before infection or 2 h post-infection protected mice from a lethal H1N1 challenge. In fact, following intravenous administration the plasma levels of Saponin C could reach a peak at about 0.14 h with a half-life of 2.83 h (Chen et al., 2011). In this study, when Saponin C was administrated 2 h post-infection, viral replication had already initiated before the compound reached its peak concentration in the target organ. Thus, the therapeutic treatment yielded lower survival rate (83.33%) as compared with prophylactic treatment, which achieved 100% survival.

Previously studies also found that Saponin C exhibited anti-inflammatory properties via regulating the NF-κB pathway in vivo and in vitro (Fan et al., 2018; Khan et al., 2018; Raina et al., 2024). Here, we cannot rule out the possibility that apart from direct anti-pathogen effects, Saponin C may alleviate the influenza-related symptoms and enhance the survival rate by modulating the host inflammatory response. In fact, histological analysis confirmed that Saponin C significantly attenuated the lung pathology of IAV infected mice in both prophylactic and therapeutic contexts. However, it is worth to further study the anti-inflammatory properties of Saponin C in the context of viral infection.

In addition, our study showed that Saponin C blocked the replication of SARS-CoV-2 by acting as an entry inhibitor. It effectively inhibited SARS-CoV-2 spike mediated receptor binding and membrane fusion. Similarly, saponins isolated from other medicinal plants also showed inhibitory activity against SARS-CoV-2 by targeting viral entry. For example, astersaponin I and astersaponin J from Aster koraiensis act as SARS-CoV-2 viral fusion blockers and suppress the spike-mediated membrane fusion, thereby inhibit the infection of SARS-CoV-2 variants and syncytium formation (Kim et al., 2022, Kim et al., 2024). Icorice-saponin A3, the natural product from licorice, binds with Y453 of spike receptor-binding domain (RBD) and targets the RBDs of various SARS-CoV-2 variants, including Beta, Delta, and Omicron BA.1, XBB and BQ1.1 (Yi et al., 2023). Additionally, the in silico virtual screening found that ophiopogonin D, which shares the same sapogenin with Saponin C, interacts with the Q493, Q498, and N501 residues of the SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD (Stalin et al., 2021). Therefore, it is probable for Saponin C to exhibit its inhibitory property by directly binding to the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein. Meanwhile, although Saponin C does not target SARS-CoV-2 main protease, we cannot exclude other post-entry mechanisms (e.g., targeting other viral enzymes or host replication factors). Future studies are needed to fully characterize the antiviral mechanism of Saponin C against SARS-CoV-2.

Conclusions

This study provides support for the novel function and application of Saponin C from L. muscari in the context of respiratory infectious diseases, and highlights the potential of Saponin C serving as a therapeutic agent for treating influenza and COVID-19. The identification of this previously-unknown antiviral feature from Saponin C will undoubtedly contribute to the current and future fight against influenza and COVID-19.

Materials and methods

Reagents and plasmids

Saponin C from L. muscari and ruscogenin (purities ≥99%) were purchased from MedChem Express (NJ, USA). Oseltamivir phosphate (purity ≥99%) was purchased from TargetMol Chemicals Inc. (Shanghai, China). The compounds were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma, USA) at a concentration of 10 mM to prepare stock solutions for in vitro experiments.

Plasmids for mini-replicon assay (pCAGGS-PB2, pCAGGS-PB1, pCAGGS-PA, pCAGGS-NP, pRL-TK [Renilla luciferase], and reporter plasmid pPolI-Luci-NP) were constructed and reported previously (Wang, Q. et al., 2025). The pRK-HA-NP plasmid has been described elsewhere (Wei et al., 2020). The human CRM1-expressing plasmid pCMV-XPO1 (human)-3 × Myc-Neo was obtained from MiaoLingBio (Wuhan, China). Plasmid pVAX1-nCoVS expressing the SARS-CoV-2 Spike protein (Wuhan-Hu-1 strain with D614G mutation and C-terminal 19 amino acids deletion) was constructed and provided by the Shanghai Institute of Immunity and Infection, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Yu et al., 2021). The HIV-1 backbone plasmid pNL4.3-Luc.R-E-, pCMV-VSV-G expressing the VSV-G gene, GFP-expressing plasmid pGIPZ (Open Biosystems) were available in our laboratory.

Cells, viruses and animals

Vero E6-hACE2 and 293T-hACE2 cells were obtained from Shanghai Institute of Immunity and Infection, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK), A549, human embryonic kidney (HEK)-293T, Vero E6, Vero E6-hACE2 and 293T-hACE2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Biological Industries, Israel) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Invigentech, USA) and 1‰ (v/v) antibiotics (100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin and 250 ng/mL amphotericin B; Solarbio, Beijing, China). All cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. SPF BALB/c mice were purchased from Charles River (Beijing, China).

Influenza A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 virus (A/PR8, H1N1), A/swine/Shandong/TA05 (H3N2), A/duck/Hunan/HN4/2018(H6N6), A/chicken/Hunan/38/2018(H9N2) and reporter viruses H1N1-HiBiT were described previously and stored at the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Wang, Z. et al., 2025). The IAV were propagated in Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF) embryonated chicken eggs and the titration was conducted using the 50% egg infectious dose (EID50) assay. SARS-CoV-2 Omicron strain (XBB.1.16) was provided and conserved by the Centre for Microbial Virus Collection, Wuhan Institute of Virology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The virus titers were measured as the median tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50).

Cytotoxicity assays

The cytotoxicity was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay (APE-BIO, USA). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at the desired density and cultured overnight to form 80–90% confluent cell monolayers. Two-fold diluted compounds were added into the wells for 48 h incubation. Cell control treated with medium containing 0.1% (v/v) DMSO, and blank control (with the same culture medium but no cells) were included. The supernatant was then replaced with 100 μL of CCK-8 mixture (DMEM: CCK-8 = 9:1) for an additional 2 h incubation. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and the percentage of cell viability was calculated as: (ODcompound – ODblank)/(ODcontrol – ODblank) × 100%, in which cytotoxicity (%) was calculated as 1 - cell viability. The drug concentration that reduced the cells viability by 50% (CC50) was calculated using nonlinear regression analysis in GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 Software (San Diego, CA, USA).

Antiviral activity against IAV

MDCK and A549 cells in white-walled 96-well plates were infected with H1N1-HiBiT (MOI = 0.01). After 1 h incubation at 37 °C, the cells were washed and treated with two-fold serially diluted Saponin C or oseltamivir phosphate in Opti-MEM with 1 μg/mL TPCK-trypsin for 24 h. Control cells were infected with H1N1-HiBiT and treated with medium containing 0.1%(v/v) DMSO. Relative luminescence units (RLU) of NanoLuc luciferase activity were quantified using Nano-Glo® HiBiT Lytic Detection System (Promega, USA). The Inhibition rate was calculated as follows: Inhibition rate (%) = [1 – (RLUcompound/RLUcontrol)] × 100%. The IC50 defined as the concentration of compound required for 50% inhibition of viral replication, was calculated with the GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 software using the nonlinear regression analysis. Viral titer reduction assay

MDCK and A549 cells in 12-well plates were infected with H1N1, H3N2, H6N6, or H9N2 influenza virus (MOI = 0.01) for 1 h at 37 °C. After PBS washing, compounds (Saponin C gradients, Oseltamivir phosphate, or 0.1% (v/v) DMSO) in Opti-MEM with 1 μg/mL TPCK-trypsin were added. Supernatants containing progeny virus were harvested at 24 and/or 48 h post-infection (hpi), and quantified by TCID50 assay, in which the titer was calculated by the Reed-Muench method (Ramakrishnan, 2016).

Indirect immunofluorescence assay

MDCK cells seeded onto coverslips in 12-well plates were infected with PR8 at 37 °C for 1 h, and treated with diluted Saponin C for corresponding time period. The cells were then washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by blocking with QuickBlock™ Blocking Buffer (Beyotime, P0260) containing Triton X-100 for 30 min at room temperature (RT). Subsequently, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a human Fc-conjugated anti-IAV-NP nanobody (C7) (1:200; selected and produced based on the previous descriptions (Chen et al., 2025)), followed by incubation with an FITC-conjugated goat anti-human IgG secondary antibody (1:500; Beyotime) for 1 h at RT. After washing, the nuclei were stained with 2-(4-amidinophenyl)-6-indolecarbamidine dihydrochloride (DAPI, Beyotime, China). The images were acquired using an inverted fluoresce microscope (Olympus, Japan), or a Stellaris 5 laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica, Germany).

Time-of-addition assay

MDCK cells seeded in 96-well plates were infected with H1N1-HiBiT (MOI = 0.05). Saponin C (final concertation of 10 μM) or 0.1% (v/v) DMSO was added at the indicated time intervals (-2–0, 0–2, 2–4, 4–6, 6–8, and 8–10 h p.i.) or continuously (−2 to 24 h p.i.). Viral replication was assessed at 24 h p.i. using the Nano-Glo® HiBiT Lytic Detection System.

IAV attachment and entry assay

The viral binding and internalization assays were performed as described previously (Ni et al., 2024). Briefly, for attachment: MDCK cells were pre-cooled (4 °C, 30 min) and treated with H1N1-HiBiT (MOI = 10) and Saponin C (10 μM) mixtures at 4 °C for 1 h (adsorption). Then the cells were washed on ice with chilled PBS for three times, and the attached virus was quantified by Nano-Glo® HiBiT Lytic Detection System. For entry: MDCK cells were infected for 1 h, and Saponin C (10 μM) was added at 1 h p.i. after the unbound virus was removed by PBS washes, and the cells were shifted to 37 °C for 2 h (internalization). The cells were then washed 8 times with chilled acidic PBS (pH 1.3) to remove the virus retained on the surface of the cell membrane, and intracellular virions were quantified by Nano-Glo® HiBiT Lytic Detection System.

Hemagglutination inhibition assay (HAI)

PR8 virus (4 Hemagglutinating Unit/25 μL) was incubated with two-fold serially diluted Saponin C (25 μL) in 96-well V-shaped plates at RT for 30 min. Then, 50 μL of 1% chicken erythrocytes (diluted in PBS) was added, and hemagglutination was observed after 1 h of incubation at RT.

Mini-replicon assay

293T cells (1.5 × 104 cells/well) in 96-well plates were co-transfected with plasmids encoding influenza virus polymerase subunits (PB2, PB1, PA) and nucleoprotein (NP), pRL-TK and pPolI-Luci-NP using Lipofectamine® 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After 6 h, medium was replaced with fresh medium containing Saponin C or 0.1% (v/v) DMSO. Luciferase activity was measured at 24 h using a Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA).

Real-time reverse-transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Ambion, USA) from IAV-infected MDCK cells. cDNA was synthesized from total RNA (1000 ng) using a reverse transcription kit (TaKaRa, Kyoto, Japan). qRT-PCR was performed in technical duplicates using gene-specific primers and TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (TaKaRa, Kyoto, Japan). The primer sequences were described previously (Kawakami et al., 2011) and listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Co-immunoprecipitation

293T cells were co-transfected with plasmids encoding HA- NP (pRK-HA-NP) and Myc-CRM1 (pCMV-XPO1-3 × Myc-Neo). At 12 h p.t., cells were either left untreated or treated with different amounts of Saponin C. At 48 h p.t., cells were harvested and lysed on ice using NP-40 Lysis Buffer (Beyotime, P0013F) supplemented with complete protease inhibitor cocktail and PMSF. Approximately 10% of each lysate was reserved as input control. The remaining lysates were incubated with Pierce Anti-c-Myc Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 88842) for 2 h at room temperature under gentle rotation. Then, the beads were captured with a magnetic stand and washed four times with ice-cold lysis buffer. Immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted in Laemmli lysis buffer (Sigma, 38733) by heating at 95 °C for 10 min and then analyzed by Western blotting with mouse anti-HA mAb (BioLegend, 901513) and mouse anti-Myc mAb (Proteintech, 60003-2-Ig).

Cellular thermal shift assay

The assay was performed based on a previously described method with modifications (Martinez Molina et al., 2013). MDCK cells infected with PR8 or 293T cells transfected with Myc-CRM1 were harvested and lysed on ice for 30 min in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013) supplemented with protease inhibitors. The lysates were divided into two aliquots and treated with either 40 μM Saponin C or an equal concentration of DMSO for 1 h at room temperature. Each sample was then subdivided into nine 50-μL aliquots, incubated at various temperatures for 5 min, and immediately chilled on ice for 3 min. After centrifugation (12,000×g, 20 min, 4 °C), the supernatants were analyzed by Western blotting using mouse anti-NP mAb (Sino Biological, 11675-MM03T) or anti-Myc antibody. The relative chemiluminescence intensity at each temperature was used to generate a melting curve, from which the Tagg values were calculated by nonlinear regression using GraphPad Prism.

Animal experiments

Female BALB/c mice (6-week-old) were randomly assigned into groups, and intranasally infected with PR8 virus (1 × 105 EID50 in 50 μL PBS). In the dose dependent experiment, mice were intraperitoneally treated with Saponin C (12.5, 25 or 50 mg/kg/day), oseltamivir phosphate (15 mg/kg/day) or vehicle (40% PEG400, 5% Tween-80 and 45% normal saline) once per day from one day before infection for six consecutive days. In the therapeutic experiment, mice received Saponin C (50 mg/kg/day) treatment either 2 h after infection (Post-2h) or one day before infection (Pre-24h). The treatments were repeated once daily and ended at 6 days post-infection.

The body weight and survival were monitored for 14 days. The humane endpoint was defined as body weight loss exceeding 30% of the initial (day 0) weight (Brochut et al., 2024; Moon et al., 2024). Mice reaching this endpoint were humanely euthanized and counted as dead on the day of euthanasia for survival analysis. The lungs were harvested on days 3 and 5 post infection for analysis. Lung homogenates were titrated in 9-day embryonated eggs (three eggs/dilution). After 48 h incubation at 37 °C, allantoic fluid was tested by hemagglutination assay. Viral titers (log10 EID50/mL) were calculated using the Reed-Muench method. Histopathological analysis was performed according to standard procedures using haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Pathological changes, including inflammation, tissue damage, and infiltration, were examined microscopically.

Antiviral activity against SARS-CoV-2

The assay was performed in the BSL-3 laboratory at the Wuhan Institute of Virology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Briefly, Vero E6 cells (2 × 104/well) were pretreated with two-fold serially diluted Saponin C for 1 h and infected with SARS-CoV-2 Omicron (XBB.1.16, MOI = 0.01) in the presence of Saponin C. After 24 h, viral RNA in supernatant was quantified by qRT-PCR, and IC50 was calculated accordingly.

SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus entry assay

SARS-CoV-2 Spike pseudotyped HIV-1 particles containing a luciferase reporter were generated by co-transfection of 293T cells with plasmids pVAX1-nCoVS and pNL4.3-Luc.R-E- using polyethylenimine. The pseudovirus in the supernatant were harvested at 48–72 h post-transfection and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter. For infection, Vero E6-hACE2 and 293T-hACE2 cells were incubated with SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus in the presence of serially diluted Saponin C or 0.1% (v/v) DMSO (control) at 37 °C for 48 h. Luciferase activity, which reflects pseudovirus infectivity, was quantified with Bright-Glo® Luciferase Assay System (Promega, USA).

Cell-cell fusion assay

The effector 293T cells were co-transfected with pVAX1-nCoVS and pGIPZ-GFP, or pGIPZ-GFP alone (as negative control) for 48 h. Then the effector cells were detached and incubated with Saponin C or 0.1%(v/v) DMSO at 37 °C for 30 min, and overlaid on 70–80% confluent target 293T-hACE2 cells. The syncytia formation was imaged after co-culturing for 6 h, and the fusion rate from at least 4 randomly selected fields was counted and calculated using Image pro plus software.

Spike-ACE2 binding assay

The effect of Saponin C on Spike/ACE2 interaction was assessed using the CoviDrop SARS-CoV-2 Spike-ACE2 Binding Inhibitor Screening Fast Kit (EpigenTek, USA; D-1004) following manufacturer’s instructions.

ACE2 activity assay

The possible effect of Saponin C on the carboxylase activity of hACE2 was measured by ACE2 Inhibitor Screening Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai; P0320S) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Statistical analysis

All values were expressed as the mean ± SEM from at least three independent experiments. Statistical significance between two groups was assessed using the Student’s t-test, and among more than two groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001 and ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001 were considered statistically significant at different levels. Survival rates were analyzed with a log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).
