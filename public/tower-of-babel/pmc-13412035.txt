Effects of theory-based interventions on sedentary behavior, physical activity intensity, cardiometabolic outcomes, and self-efficacy among sedentary older adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Yulin Tang, Hong Yan, Xiaorong Qin, Weiya Meng, Jingyun Xu, Rumei Zhou, Linlin Liu, Yang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to examine the effects of theory-based interventions on sedentary time, physical activity intensity, cardiometabolic indicators, and self-efficacy among older adults with sedentary behavior.MethodsComputerized searches were conducted in databases including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, CINAHLL (EBSCOhost), Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), China Science Journal Database (VIP), Wanfang Data, and China Biomedical Literature Service System (CBM). The search aimed to identify randomized controlled trials of theory-based interventions for sedentary behavior in older adults, from the databases’ inception through July 31, 2025. Two researchers independently screened the literature according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, extracted data, assessed quality, and conducted a meta-analysis using Stata15 software.ResultsA total of 17 studies involving 2,344 older adults with sedentary behavior were included. Compared with the control group, theory-based interventions significantly reduced sedentary time in older adults (standardized mean difference [SMD] = -0.24, 95% CI: -0.33, -0.15, p < 0.05); increased moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) by 8.43 min per day (95% CI: 4.73, 12.12, p < 0.05), light-intensity physical activity (LPA) (SMD = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.06, 0.43, p < 0.05), and daily step count by 867.30 steps (95% CI: 391.73, 1342.87, p < 0.05); and significantly improved cardiometabolic indicators, including body mass index (BMI) by 0.21 kg/m2 (95% CI: -0.23, -0.19, p < 0.05), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) by 0.043% (95% CI: -0.071, -0.014, p < 0.05), and systolic blood pressure by 0.45 mmHg (95% CI: -0.81, -0.10, p < 0.05). Unexpectedly, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was significantly increased by 0.99 mmHg (95% CI: 0.78, 1.20, p < 0.05). However, there were no statistically significant differences in outcome indicators such as moderate-intensity physical activity (MPA) in physical activity intensity, blood lipids (triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, total cholesterol) among cardiometabolic indicators, and self-efficacy (p > 0.05).ConclusionsTheory-based interventions can effectively reduce sedentary time, increase MVPA, LPA, and daily step count in older adults with sedentary behavior, and significantly improve cardiometabolic indicators, including BMI, HbA1c, and SBP. Additionally, the increase in DBP remains a potential safety concern requiring clinical attention. However, they do not show advantages in moderate-intensity MPA, blood lipid indicators (triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, total cholesterol), or self-efficacy. In the future, more large-sample studies with rigorous designs are needed to verify this conclusion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07710-5.Keywords: Elderly people, Sedentary behavior, Theoretical frameworks, Physical activity intensity, Cardiometabolic outcomes, Self-efficacy, Meta-analysis

Introduction

As the global population ages, the number of older adults is projected to reach 2 billion by 2050 [1]. In recent years, sedentary behavior (SB)—a health risk factor for older adults—has drawn close attention because of its high prevalence [2]. Sedentary behavior refers to activities performed while sitting or reclining (e.g., lying at an angle) during wakefulness, with an energy expenditure of ≤ 1.5 metabolic equivalents (METs) [3]. A global survey reports that older adults spend an average of 9.4 h sitting per day, accounting for 65% to 80% of their waking hours [4]. Sedentary behavior may lead to adverse health outcomes in older adults. A 10-year prospective cohort study published in 2025 used isotemporal substitution modeling to quantify the mortality risk associated with displacing time spent in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) with sedentary behavior. The study found that replacing 10 min of sedentary time per day with MVPA was significantly associated with a 12% reduction in all-cause mortality risk [5]. Additionally, sedentary behavior is closely linked to an increased risk of accelerated aging [6], depression [7], sleep disorders [8], cardiometabolic diseases [9], and dementia [10, 11], which imposes a burden on healthy aging.

Currently, interventions targeting sedentary behavior primarily include behavior change techniques based on self-monitoring, regular physical activity, and the use of digital technologies as health delivery channels to modify lifestyles, among others [12–14]. However, these interventions often focus on a single method and lack complex, interactive theoretical frameworks to support existing intervention programs. Accordingly, the Medical Research Council (MRC) recommends that theory-guided interventions are more likely to achieve the expected outcomes when developing interventions involving behavior change techniques [15, 16]. At present, the main theories for changing sedentary behavior in older adults include the Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), the Transtheoretical Model of Change (TTM), and the Social-Ecological Model (SEM) [17–19]. The application of these theories or models can help us identify key targets and select appropriate intervention techniques [20]; secondly, intervention programs constructed based on theories, after final data analysis and feedback on results, facilitate understanding of the role of component factors in different theoretical structures in the overall intervention effect [21].

Theory-based interventions apply theories or frameworks to develop and evaluate measures to change a specific behavior. For example, Zhang and others used theory-based interventions to improve physical activity in pregnant women and to improve maternal and neonatal outcomes [22]. However, the effects of theory-based interventions on sedentary behavior in older adults remain inconsistent. Cheng and others found that, compared with the sham intervention group, the intervention group based on the “Capability, Opportunity, Motivation, Behavior” (COM-B) model and behavior change theories showed no significant reduction in sedentary behavior among older adults [23]. Cruz and others found that, compared with the conventional rehabilitation group, the intervention group based on the Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) had a significant increase in MVPA and daily step count, while there were no significant differences in exercise capacity, muscle strength, or quality of life [19]. However, there is currently no systematic review or meta-analysis evaluating the impact of theory-based interventions on sedentary behavior in older adults.

Therefore, to objectively and accurately evaluate the impact of interventions on physical activity intensity and to further develop personalized intervention plans, outcomes such as “LPA, MPA, and MVPA” were included. To assess whether the intervention could provide direct health benefits for cardiometabolic outcomes, outcomes including “body mass index (BMI), blood lipids, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure” were incorporated. Self-efficacy, as a crucial mediator of behavioral change and a key factor in sustaining long-term health behaviors, was also considered necessary as an outcome measure. To provide a scientific basis for establishing an integrated evidence framework from “theory driven” to “behavior change” and then to “physiological improvement”, this study included randomized controlled trials. Through a systematic review and meta-analysis, it evaluates the effects of theory-based interventions on sedentary behavior in older adults and identifies the effectiveness of such interventions in reducing sedentary time, increasing physical activity intensity, improving cardiometabolic indicators, and enhancing self-efficacy. By comprehensively evaluating these outcomes in conjunction with the theoretical background, this study provides an empirical basis for the future development of systematic behavioral intervention programs.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis has been registered on PROSPERO (Registration Number: CRD420251087045). The review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) statement (see Supplementary Table S1).

Search strategies

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in databases including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, CINAHLL (EBSCOhost), Cochrane Library, CNKI, VIP, Wanfang Data, and CBM for randomized controlled trials of theory-based interventions for sedentary behavior in older adults. The search period spanned from each database’s inception to July 31, 2025. In addition, literature tracking was performed based on previously published meta-analyses and reviews in this field. The following MeSH terms were used, including “Aged”, “Models, Theoretical”, and “Sedentary Behavior”. The detailed search strategy is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: Studies were included based on the PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study design) framework. (1) Population: Older adults with sedentary behavior. Regarding the definition of older adults: the United Nations’ definition of “older persons” as adults aged 60 years and above was adopted [24], and self-identification of older age among adults aged 60 and above was considered [25]. Therefore, adults aged 60 years and above were selected as the age criterion for this study. All participants had no history of mental or psychological disorders. (2) Intervention: Interventions were developed based on at least one theory (the name of the theory should be mentioned in the article, or it should be emphasized that the intervention program was formulated based on a specific theory), with a focus on improving sedentary behavior. The intervention could be delivered through physical exercise, e-health technology, or other methods. (3) Comparison: Non-theory-based groups, including usual care, waiting list, and routine health education. (4) Outcome: The primary outcome measure was sedentary time. Secondary outcome measures included physical activity intensity (light-intensity physical activity [LPA], moderate-intensity physical activity [MPA], moderate-to-vigorous physical activity [MVPA], daily step count), cardiometabolic indicators (body mass index [BMI], blood lipids, glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c], systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure), and self-efficacy. (5) Study design: Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), including pilot RCTs and parallel RCTs. Exclusion criteria: (1) Study types: Quasi-experimental studies, reviews, conference abstracts, and case reports. (2) Studies from which relevant outcome indicators or data could not be extracted, or those lacking such indicators or data. (3) Studies for which the full text could not be obtained. (4) Studies not published in Chinese or English. (5) Low-quality literature.

Literature screening

Two researchers imported the retrieved literature into Endnote 21 for literature management and duplicate removal. All titles and abstracts were independently screened in accordance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria, followed by full-text review to determine the final included studies. In case of discrepancies between the two researchers during the screening process, a third researcher would be consulted to reach a final decision.

Data extraction

In accordance with the PRISMA guidelines, two researchers systematically extracted and documented the following information from the included studies: study characteristics (authors, year of publication, country, and sample size); participant characteristics (age and definition of sedentary behavior); intervention measures (delivery format and underlying theory); comparison groups (usual care, waiting list, routine health education); and outcome measures (sedentary time, physical activity intensity, cardiometabolic indicators, and self-efficacy). Any disagreements arising during this process were resolved through discussion with a third researcher.

Study quality assessment

Two researchers independently assessed the risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for Randomized Controlled Trials (ROB2) [26]. This assessment tool covers five domains: randomization process, bias in intervention delivery, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result. The overall risk of bias for a study was determined as follows: It was rated as “low risk” if all five domains were rated “low risk”; it was rated as “moderate risk” if at least one domain was rated “moderate risk”; and it was rated as “high risk” if at least one domain was rated “high risk”. Any disagreements during the assessment were resolved through discussion with a third researcher.

Certainty of evidence assessment

We used the Grades of Recommendation, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach to assess the certainty of evidence for each outcome, using the GRADEpro GDT (McMaster University and Evidence Prime) software [27]. Evidence from randomized controlled trials initially started at a high certainty level. Two researchers independently rated the evidence for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The quality of evidence was categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low. Any disagreements that arose during the assessment process were resolved through discussion with a third researcher.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Stata 15. All extracted data were continuous variables. For the same outcome assessed with different tools or units, the standardized mean difference (SMD) was used to represent the pooled effect size, and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI) was calculated. For the same outcome assessed with the same tool or unit, the mean difference (MD) was used to represent the pooled effect size, and its 95% CI was also calculated. The Q statistic was used to assess heterogeneity of the SMD or MD. The I² statistic was used to indicate the percentage of total variation across studies attributable to true heterogeneity rather than other factors, to avoid errors in heterogeneity assessment when using the Q statistic alone. Significant heterogeneity was defined as I² ≥ 50% and P < 0.1, in which case a random-effects model was adopted. When I² < 50% and P ≥ 0.1 (indicating low heterogeneity), a fixed-effects model was used [28]. If the number of included studies exceeded 10, meta-regression was conducted to explore the sources of heterogeneity, and sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the stability of the results. If the sources of heterogeneity remained unclear, a descriptive assessment was carried out. When the number of studies was more than 10, Egger’s test was used to assess publication bias. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Study selection

A total of 2822 studies were retrieved from the databases. Of these, 1127 were excluded due to duplication, 26 were excluded via EndNote, and 623 were excluded for ineligible study design or study population. After screening the titles and abstracts of the remaining 1046 studies, 998 were excluded. Of the remaining 48 studies, 14 were excluded because their full texts could not be obtained, leaving 34 studies for full-text review, with 17 studies finally included [17–19, 23, 29–35] (see Fig. 1).

Study selection flow chart

Study characteristics

A total of 17 studies were included, with sample sizes ranging from 25 to 589 participants and a total of 2344 older adults. These studies were published between 2013 and 2024, including 16 English-language studies [17–19, 23, 29–37] and 1 Chinese study [38]. Among the English-language studies, 6 were from the United States [31–34, 36, 39], 2 from Australia [18, 23], and 1 each from New Zealand [40], Canada [17], Mexico [30], the United Kingdom [41], Italy [29], Germany [37], Belgium [42], and Portugal [19]. Three studies used a three-arm randomized controlled trial design [30, 37, 42], while the remaining 14 used a two-arm randomized controlled trial design. Four studies developed intervention programs based on two theories [17, 23, 29, 31], 1 study based on three theories [39], and the rest based on one theory. Among these theories, the Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) was the most widely used in the included studies [17, 19, 29–31, 36, 39]. Risk of bias assessment of included studies

Among the included studies, 23.53% (4/17) had a moderate risk of bias, and 76.47% (13/17) had a low risk of bias. For the 4 studies with moderate risk of bias: 1 study reported no significant differences in baseline racial data (P < 0.5) [33]; 2 studies did not describe allocation concealment [38, 41]; 2 studies failed to report on missing data [17, 33]; and 1 study reported that the researchers assessing the outcomes were aware of the interventions received by the participants [17]. All studies were consistent with their pre-specified study protocols. See Figs. 2 and 3.

Meta-analysis

Main outcome

Fourteen RCTs reported sedentary time (minutes or hours/day) [18, 19, 23, 29–32, 34–39, 42]. Significant heterogeneity was observed across studies (I²=49.6%, p = 0.01), so a random-effects model was used for analysis. The results showed that, compared with the control group, the theory-based intervention group had a significant reduction in sedentary time (SMD=-0.24, 95% CI: -0.33, -0.15, p < 0.05). See Fig. 4.

Forest plot of the effect of theory-based interventions on sedentary time

Meta-regression analysis showed that the study publication year (β = -0.386, 95% CI: -0.894, 0.120, p = 0.125), sample size (β = -0.088, 95% CI: -0.627, 0.451, p = 0.733), country (β = -0.367, 95% CI: -1.008, 0.277, p = 0.243), and type of research (β = -0.473, 95% CI: -1.022, 0.076, p = 0.086) had no statistically significant moderating effects on the overall pooled effect size, suggesting that these factors might not be the main sources of heterogeneity. See Supplementary Table S3. A descriptive analysis was conducted on the potential main causes of high heterogeneity, which might stem from differences in the theories or frameworks used, intervention methods in the intervention group, or methods in the control group.

Visual assessment of the funnel plot and the results of Egger’s test (p = 0.84) also showed no evidence of publication bias. See Supplementary Figures S1 and S2. Sensitivity analysis indicated that sequentially excluding each included study did not substantially change the magnitude, direction, or statistical significance of the pooled effect size, suggesting that the results were relatively robust. See Supplementary Figure S3.

Certainty of evidence (GRADE assessmevnt)

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE approach (see Supplementary Table S3). Due to unclear descriptions of allocation concealment and blinding in most included studies, the evidence was downgraded for risk of bias across all outcomes. For MVPA and blood lipids, the evidence was downgraded for inconsistency due to high heterogeneity. For MPA, blood lipids, and HbA1c, the evidence was downgraded for imprecision because of small sample sizes and an overall non-significant change from baseline. Overall, sedentary time, LPA, daily step count, BMI, SBP, and DBP were rated as having moderate certainty. MVPA, MPA, and HbA1c were rated as having low certainty. Blood lipids were rated as having very low certainty.

Discussion

Principal findings

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the effects of theory-based interventions on sedentary behavior in older adults. The results of this study support the effectiveness of theory-based interventions in reducing sedentary time, increasing moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), light physical activity (LPA), and daily step count, and in improving cardiometabolic indicators, including body mass index (BMI), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and measured values of systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP).

Main outcome

Sedentary time was the primary outcome measure of this study, and the results showed that, compared with the control group, theory-based interventions could effectively reduce sedentary time in older adults (P < 0.05). This finding is consistent with the findings of Ashe, Cruz, and their respective colleagues—Ashe and others developed an intervention program based on the Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) combined with the Social Ecological Model, while Cruz and others constructed their intervention program using the Social Cognitive Theory alone—thus, the Social Cognitive Theory plays a crucial role in influencing the sedentary time of older adults [17, 19]. The core framework of the Social Cognitive Theory is reciprocal determinism, which posits that learning behavior is the result of the interaction between cognitive, behavioral, and environmental factors [43]: cognitive factors mainly include knowledge and attitudes, and through health education lectures or brochures, older adults’ cognition and attitudes towards sedentary behavior can be changed, helping them learn about the serious harms of sedentary behavior and attach greater importance to it; behavioral factors encompass skills and self-efficacy, with self-efficacy and outcome expectations being the potential mechanisms driving behavior change in older adults [44, 45], and professional teams can provide personalized activity plans for older adults to support this; environmental factors include family or peer influence and social norms, which highlights the importance of incorporating social support into intervention programs, as external factors from the environment affect whether positive behaviors can be sustained over time [46, 47], and these may ultimately be the determinants of whether older adults choose to reduce their sedentary time.

Secondary outcome

Physical activity intensity

This study showed that theory-based interventions could improve MVPA (P < 0.05), consistent with findings from Ashe, Balducci, Cruz, Poppe, Wijsman [17, 19, 29, 40, 42]; could improve LPA, aligning with Balducci [29]; and could increase daily step count (P < 0.05), in agreement with Ashe, Cruz [17, 19]. Regardless of whether the improvement was in MVPA, LPA, or daily step count, the most widely applied theory was the Social Cognitive Theory (SCT). A recently published meta-analysis indicated that theory-based interventions can significantly increase daily step count in patients with cardiovascular disease, improve self-efficacy for physical activity and exercise, and enhance quality of life [48]. Most studies have also confirmed that intervention programs based on the Social Cognitive Theory are more effective in improving physical activity intensity [49, 50]. One study found that social support, self-efficacy, normative beliefs, observational learning, behavioral skills, and knowledge are predictors of physical activity intensity [50]. Another study implementing physical activity interventions based on the Social Cognitive Theory revealed that early physical exercise experiences can lead to higher self-efficacy (i.e., a behavioral factor), enabling patients to proactively reduce sedentary behavior [51]. The latest research has found that when sufficient social support is provided by external sources (such as family members and friends), it exerts a positive impact on older adults, including reducing sedentary behavior (i.e., an environmental factor). Even when facing external challenges (e.g., weather conditions), older adults can maintain a high level of physical activity by integrating activities such as housework [52]. This may explain the potential mechanism by which the Social Cognitive Theory improves physical activity intensity.

In addition, we found that theory-based interventions did not significantly improve MPA among sedentary older adults. This may have been because the intervention protocols in the studies encouraged shifting sedentary time to LPA or, based on theoretical foundations, enhanced older adults’ executive ability and confidence in exercise, thereby facilitating a transition to MVPA. For instance, strategies such as assisting older adults in self-monitoring and providing additional action plans (e.g., taking stairs instead of elevators) aimed to help them become more active [42]. This phenomenon may have diluted measurable changes in MPA. Second, the increase in daily step count may have been primarily derived from LPA (e.g., daily walking) and MVPA (e.g., brisk walking), which further blurred the delineation of MPA. It is also worth considering that accelerometers used to measure MPA could have introduced measurement errors.

Cardiometabolic indicators

This study found that, compared with the control group, theory-based interventions effectively improved measured values of cardiometabolic indicators, including body mass index (BMI), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and systolic blood pressure (SBP), and significantly increased diastolic blood pressure (DBP) (P < 0.05), consistent with the results of Wijsman and others [39]. Although the observed reductions in body mass index (BMI, -0.21 kg/m²) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c, -0.043%) reached statistical significance, their magnitudes were likely below thresholds considered clinically meaningful. Wijsman and others mainly adopted the I-Change health behavior change model, which integrates various models related to health behavior [53], such as the Health Belief Model (HBM) [54], Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) [43], and Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) [55]. The I-Change health behavior change model proposes three key stages in the behavior change process: awareness, motivation, and behavior [56]. Targeting these factors, Wijsman and others enhanced older adults’ understanding of their physical activity behavior (i.e., awareness), provided feedback on their recent physical activity through accelerometer-based activity monitors (i.e., motivation), and offered support for the sustained change in physical activity behavior (i.e., behavior) [40]. Regarding the role of the I-Change health behavior change model, it is worthwhile to adopt a longitudinal design to further explore the impact of sedentary behavior on cardiometabolic indicators in older adults.

However, we also observed a noteworthy phenomenon. The theory-based physical activity intervention resulted in a slight increase in DBP among sedentary older adults. This may have stemmed from the following mechanisms: a sudden surge in activity levels among long-term sedentary individuals, influenced by acute hemodynamic effects, triggered adaptive regulation; increased muscle mass or metabolic activity led to age-related changes in vascular reactivity, resulting in insufficient vasodilatory capacity, increased resting blood flow demand, and a consequent upward shift in DBP [57, 58]. Alternatively, it may have stemmed from measurement error or bias. This finding still needs to be viewed within the overall context of significant improvements in multiple aspects, such as blood glucose, body fat, and activity levels.

Our study did not find that theory-based interventions significantly improved lipid profiles in sedentary older adults. This may be related to the short duration of the interventions included in the studies (3 or 4 months), as longer intervention periods have been shown to have more pronounced effects on lipid levels. Rosenberg et al. also supported this view [39]. Second, lipid regulation typically involves more complex hepatic metabolic and synthetic pathways and is simultaneously influenced by aging, which diminishes catecholamine-stimulated lipolysis [59]. This may have resulted in sedentary older adults being less responsive to low-intensity interventions.

Self-efficacy

Our study found that theory-based interventions did not significantly improve self-efficacy among sedentary older adults. A review of the three included studies revealed that both Song et al. and Cruz et al. reported no statistically significant differences. Song et al. applied the Transtheoretical Model, and an analysis of potential reasons suggested that this might be because the model encompasses four core constructs (e.g., stages of change, processes of change, self-efficacy, and decisional balance), with the “stage of change” being central [60]. This model emphasizes the timing of behavioral change, positing that the critical period for such change is within the next six months. However, all three studies in our analysis had intervention durations of only three months, which may explain the lack of significant findings. Cruz et al. applied Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), which emphasizes the central role of individual cognition in the behavior change process. This suggests that theory-based interventions may initially act on more immediate factors such as knowledge and skills [61]. For sedentary older adults, a more sustained change in self-efficacy beliefs might require a longer intervention period to become apparent.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, there is currently no systematic review or meta-analysis evaluating the effects of theory-based interventions on sedentary behavior in older adults, making this an innovative research topic. Second, this study included only randomized controlled trials (RCTs), and most of these studies were of high quality. Finally, to ensure the comprehensiveness of the search results, nine databases were searched, and detailed information about the search process is reported in Supplementary Table S2.

This study still has certain limitations, and its results should be interpreted with caution. First, due to the diversity of theories and the complexity of intervention formats, we were unable to conduct subgroup analyses, which makes it difficult to identify potential sources of bias. Second, the sample size of some studies was small, which may lead to insufficient statistical power and hinder the detection of small but clinically meaningful results. Therefore, larger sample sizes and standardized study designs are needed in the future to validate these findings.

Conclusions

Theory-based interventions are promising for reducing sedentary time in older adults with sedentary behavior and for improving physical activity intensity (including MVPA, LPA, and daily step count) and cardiometabolic indicators (including measured values of BMI and HbA1c). However, a paradoxical increase in diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was observed alongside a reduction in systolic blood pressure (SBP), warranting further mechanistic investigation. The Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) and the I-Change health behavior change model are regarded as promising theoretical foundations. This study has not yet found that theory-based interventions produce significant improvements in MPA (a physical activity intensity), lipid profiles (a cardiometabolic indicator, including triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and total cholesterol), or self-efficacy. Future studies should explore the potential mechanisms underlying these theories and develop personalized intervention strategies.
