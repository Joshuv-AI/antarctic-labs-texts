Improving the Performance of a Syndromic Surveillance Definition for Sexual Violence
Authors: Amy Ising, Lucas M. Neuroth, Lubna S. Hossain, Julie M. Kafka, Anna E. Waller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContext:Timely and representative data for sexual violence surveillance in the United States are lacking.Objective:To address this gap, we aimed to improve the identification of sexual violence in syndromic surveillance emergency department (ED) visit data through evaluation of the' latest Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) sexual violence surveillance definition (CDC V3) using North Carolina (NC) syndromic surveillance data and development of an enhanced definition (V4).Design:We created a gold standard dataset of visits representing incident sexual violence, history of sexual violence, or no sexual violence through manual review, developed an enhanced V4 definition based on this review, and then compared the performance of the CDC V3 and V4 definitions against the gold standard.Setting:North Carolina.Participants:Emergency department visits in NC, 1/1/2016-6/3/2024.Main Outcome Measures:We compared the precision measures (Sensitivity, Specificity, Positive Predictive Value, Negative Predictive Value, and F1) of the CDC V3 and new V4 definitions for sexual violence. We applied both definitions to NC ED visit data for 2019-2023 to compare demographics and crude rates.Results:The CDC V3 sexual violence definition proved highly specific. The V4 definition identified a 6% increase in incident sexual violence ED visits compared to the CDC V3 definition, with additional chief complaint search terms and the selective incorporation of triage notes. Trends in patient sex, age group, race, and ethnicity were similar across both definitions. The F1 score showed improvement for the V4 definition compared to the CDC V3 definition, suggesting that it better optimizes the need to comprehensively identify ED visits for sexual violence while minimizing false positives.Conclusions:Syndromic surveillance ED visit data provide timely, population-based data on this complex topic. Building upon the CDC V3 definition and incorporating triage notes where available allowed us to develop a more sensitive and accurate surveillance definition for incident sexual violence.

Introduction

Timely and representative data for sexual violence incidence in the United States are lacking. The most recent published data on sexual violence from the Federal Bureau of Investigation National Incident-Based Reporting System (NIBRS) are from 2019 and survey data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey (NISVS) were last collected in 2017 and published as a report in 2022.1,2 The NISVS is a telephone survey and includes data for those 18 years and older only. While NIBRS reports sexual assaults recorded by law enforcement for all ages, the data are reported by only 20 states. To address the current gap in timely data on sexual violence, the CDC has developed definitions that can be used to identify suspected or confirmed sexual violence in syndromic surveillance emergency department (ED) visit data.3 In this paper, we describe our approach to applying the latest CDC-developed sexual violence definition (CDC V3) to syndromic surveillance ED visit data in North Carolina (NC), and identifying opportunities to update the definition to improve its sensitivity.

Background

Syndromic surveillance systems typically collect de-identified ED data at least once daily to detect and characterize disease outbreaks, monitor injuries such as opioid overdoses, and assess the health impacts of natural and man-made disasters.4 NC DETECT, NC’s syndromic surveillance system, receives ED data twice daily from all civilian hospitals across the state through a collaboration of the North Carolina Division of Public Health (NC DPH), the North Carolina Healthcare Association, and the Carolina Center for Health Informatics in the Department of Emergency Medicine at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (CCHI).5 These data are available for surveillance purposes to hospital-based users and public health practitioners in NC through a secure, role-based Web application and include basic patient demographics, ED chief complaint and triage notes, initial blood pressure and temperature, ED disposition, and ICD-10-CM codes. The Web-based application provides access to a variety of disease and injury indicators, as well as graphical, line listing, and tabular formats for review within the Web browser. Authorized users can also download these data for further analysis. In addition, publicly available dashboards and reports on a variety of topics are developed by NC DPH and CCHI using NC DETECT ED visit data.6 While previous projects in NC have evaluated syndromic surveillance definitions for topics including overdose, firearm injury, and mental health, this was the first project in NC to evaluate the use of syndromic surveillance ED visit data for sexual violence.7-10 A key component of this project was to evaluate the impact of including ED triage notes into a syndromic surveillance definition for sexual violence, in addition to the chief complaint and ICD-10-CM codes included in the existing CDC V3 definition.

Methods

Data source and identification of sexual violence ED visits

For our analysis of sexual violence ED visits, we used data from January 1, 2016, through June 3, 2024, from NC DETECT, received through a data use agreement with the NC Division of Public Health. We leveraged the CDC V3 definition developed by the CDC (CDC Sexual Violence V3) for use in the National Syndromic Surveillance Program (NSSP) ESSENCE platform that searches for key terms in the chief complaint, as well as ICD-10-CM and SNOMED-CT codes. The CDC V3 definition builds off earlier definitions, adding more chief complaint terms and including a broader list of ICD-10-CM codes compared to the V2 definition.3 We translated the definition from the NSSP ESSENCE syntax to structured query language compatible with NC DETECT, removing the SNOMED-CT codes, which are not received in NC. The CDC V3 definition in NC DETECT searches for the ICD-9-CM codes 995.83, 995.53, V71.5, or E960.1, or the following ICD-10-CM codes (all extensions are included) T74.21, T74.22, T74.51, T74.52, T76.21, T76.22, T76.51, T76.52, Z04.41, Z04.42, Z56.81, or the following chief complaint terms (accounting for abbreviations and misspellings): sexual assault, sexual abuse, rape, SANE, SART, SAFE exam, mention of punch or force with the word sex, or ICD-9/10-CM codes listed earlier (put in the chief complaint field).11 Using the CDC V3 definition we extracted 2 datasets from NC DETECT for manual review (Figure 1). For the first dataset, we pulled all ED visits identified by the CDC V3 definition (N = 30 641) and drew a stratified random sample based on patient county of residence (n = 1480) to ensure representation from all 100 counties in NC. For the second sample, we applied the chief complaint search terms from the CDC V3 definition to the chief complaint and triage note fields (N = 21 357) and then excluded visits already identified by the full CDC V3 definition. This dataset (n = 5121) represented suspected sexual violence ED visits identified through terms in the triage note field only. We generated a random sample of these visits (n = 1009), stratified by patient county of residence. As not all ED facilities reporting to NC DETECT send triage notes, 92 counties (92%) were represented in this triage note only dataset.FIGURE 1Dataset Generation for Manual Review.

Manual review and classification of sexual violence ED visits

Five reviewers each annotated a subset of records from each sample. The reviewers coded ED visits as “incident sexual violence” if the free text described a patient or concerned caregiver seeking evaluation for sexual violence. When free text was inconclusive, reviewers relied on the presence of ICD-10-CM codes for confirmed sexual violence or evaluation for sexual violence to identify incident sexual violence. Reviewers classified ED visits as “history of sexual violence” if the data indicated a primary complaint unrelated to evaluation for sexual violence, but the free text described a history of sexual violence as contributing to the ED visit. Reviewers coded ED visits as “not sexual violence” if the record did not involve an evaluation for sexual violence and noted the cause of the false positive (eg, a typographical error, negated sexual violence, etc.). A sixth reviewer annotated an overlap of 150 (10%) records from the CDC V3 sample and 100 (10%) records from the triage note only sample to calculate inter-rater reliability. Manual record review and inter-rater reliability calculations were conducted using Microsoft Excel.

Development of the V4 sexual violence definition

After compiling the annotated sample datasets, we calculated unigram and bigram term frequencies in the chief complaint and triage notes separately to assist in identifying definition improvements. Based on these findings and expert consensus among the project team, we developed an updated sexual violence definition for use in NC DETECT (V4).

Analytic approach

We calculated sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and F1 scores for the CDC V3 and V4 definitions for incident sexual violence, using the manually annotated samples as the gold standard. These measures are calculated using the proportions of true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN) identified by each rules-based definition relative to the gold standard.12 Sensitivity measures the proportion of TP identified by the rules-based definition among records that received a positive SV label during manual annotation (TP/(TP + FN). Specificity measures the proportion of TN identified by the rules-based definition among records that received a negative label during manual annotation TN/(TN + FP). PPV and NPV similarly describe the proportion of TP and TN that occurred, respectively, but the denominator is among all records that received a positive or negative label, respectively, according to the rules-based definitions (PPV: TP/(TP + FP); NPV: TN/(TN + FN)). There are trade-offs between sensitivity and PPV, so optimally balancing the 2 was the goal for our rules-based definition. Thus, we calculated the F1 score, the harmonic mean of sensitivity and PPV (2/(sensitivity-1 + PPV-1), providing a single performance measure that balances the value of comprehensively identifying ED visits for sexual violence while minimizing FP. Like our other measures, F1 scores range from 0 to 1, with 1 indicating perfect sensitivity and PPV. F1 score is one of the most used measures in the natural language processing, machine learning, and information retrieval disciplines to characterize model performance in classification problems.12 In addition, we compared ED visit demographics between definitions and calculated crude rates per 100 000 NC residents for the 5-year period of 2019 through 2023. Term frequency and statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1 (2024-06-14 ucrt). All components of this study were determined to be exempt by the UNC Chapel Hill Institutional Review Board (24-0759).

Results

Manual review of sexual violence ED visits

Of the 1480 records we reviewed identified by the CDC V3 definition, 1455 (98%) were classified as incident sexual violence, 20 (1.3%) history of sexual violence, and 5 (0.3%) were FP. Among the 1455 records identified as incident sexual violence, 236 (16%) had no SV ICD-10-CM codes, meaning they were identified by text terms in the chief complaint alone. In the triage note only sample (n = 1009), 488 (48%) were coded as incident sexual violence, 208 (21%) were history of sexual violence, and 313 (31%) were FP. When comparing how the 150 CDC V3 records were annotated by the project team to the auditor, there was 97.3% agreement in classifying sexual violence encounter type. When comparing how the 100 triage note only sample records were annotated by the project team to the auditor, there was only 64% agreement in the classification of sexual violence encounter type. Given the agreement of 64%, the lead author re-reviewed the triage note only records and brought suggested annotation changes to the project team for consensus review. As a result, we updated the classifications for 26 records, the majority of which were reclassifying visits coded as history of sexual violence to incident sexual violence.

Development of V4 definition

Based on our manual review, term frequency analysis, and expert consensus discussions, we made several changes to the CDC V3 definition to create a V4 definition focusing on incident sexual violence. First, we removed the subsequent and sequelae “T” ICD-10-CM codes to emphasize the focus on incident sexual violence. We added O9A ICD-10-CM codes to capture visits for sexual abuse during pregnancy. We added chief complaint search terms to capture additional misspellings, eg, asault with one s only, as well as coded chief complaints that indicate sexual violence (eg, “code 86,” “code 600,” “code x”). Finally, we added a component that searches the triage notes for sexual violence-specific terms if the chief complaint has a non-specific term that could indicate sexual violence (eg, “SA,” “SAV,” “assault”).11 Compared to the CDC V3 definition, our V4 definition had greater sensitivity (0.78 [CDC V3] vs 0.83 [V4]), with no appreciable decrease in specificity (Table 3). PPV was similar between definitions (0.99), while NPV improved in V4 (0.54 [V3] vs 0.57 [V4]). Characteristics of sexual violence ED visits in North Carolina

Over the 5-year period from 2019 through 2023, the CDC V3 definition identified 18 765 sexual violence ED visits in NC, compared to 19 823 (+1058) visits flagged using our V4 definition. Both definitions identified similar trends in the rates of sexual violence ED visits in NC. Women had substantially higher rates of sexual violence ED visits (60-65 visits per 100 000) compared to men (8-9). The highest rates of sexual violence ED visits were among children aged 2-4 (100 visits per 100 000), while the most visits were among those aged 25-44 years (Table 4). Black or African American North Carolinians, as well as those with other racial classifications, had higher rates of sexual violence ED visits compared to White and American Indian or Alaska Native residents. Discussion

Our manual review and analysis of ED visits identified by the CDC V3 sexual violence definition confirmed that the CDC V3 definition is highly specific. We demonstrated that the definition’s sensitivity could be improved with additional keyword search terms and the incorporation of triage notes. The V4 definition identified an additional 1058 sexual violence related ED visits from 2019 to 2023, a 6% increase compared to the CDC V3 definition. However, trends in patient sex, age group, race, and ethnicity were similar across both definitions. The F1 score also showed improvement for our V4 definition compared to the CDC V3 definition, suggesting that it better optimizes the need to comprehensively identify ED visits for sexual violence while still minimizing FP.

ED visits for sensitive topics can be challenging to identify in syndromic surveillance data as some hospitals, or specific practitioners in hospitals, use codewords in their chief complaints to mask the sensitive nature of these visits (eg, “Code X”). Codewords for sensitive topics were traditionally used when chief complaints were prominently displayed within an ED (eg, handwritten on a whiteboard). Some hospitals continue to use codewords in chief complaints even with the transition to electronic health records. Before incorporation into a surveillance definition, some codewords may require confirmation directly from hospitals that they are exclusive to the topic of interest, eg, sexual violence, and do not represent broader categories, such as any assault.

In addition to confirming the precise meaning and inclusion criteria for specific codewords, accurate processing of these chief complaints may conflict with text pre-processing steps often applied to syndromic surveillance systems. For example, it is common to remove single letters when cleaning text, which would change “Code X” to “Code.” Thus, free text preprocessing may result in a loss of meaning for codewords included in chief complaint text and a potential loss of sensitivity if the record is excluded. Additionally, ED chief complaints often contain abbreviations that may be standardized to expanded terms in a pre-processing step, (eg, OD will be expanded to overdose). On occasion, these abbreviations can have multiple meanings. For example, a common unigram in the chief complaints was “SA,” indicative of sexual assault. However, “SA” is also commonly used to indicate a suicide attempt. To include ED visits with a chief complaint of “SA” in our V4 definition, we combined this chief complaint term with a required positive mention of sexual violence in the triage note, contributing to a modest increase in sensitivity. Our approach to incorporating triage notes into the V4 definition was deliberately conservative to prevent the inclusion of ED visits mentioning a history of sexual violence. While the importance of understanding the lifelong impacts of sexual violence is clear, the development of a surveillance definition for history of sexual violence may not be possible, as these are primarily identified via triage notes, which are not as universally received and complete as chief complaints in syndromic surveillance systems. Future research is needed to determine how to conduct surveillance for ED visits where history of sexual violence is a contributing factor and to evaluate other methods of classifying sexual violence ED visits, eg, machine learning approaches.

Surveillance based on ED visits for sexual violence may not be comparable to data sources documenting additional and more comprehensive sexual violence related services. For example, while the fact sheet from the NC Council for Women and Youth Involvement shows women seeking services 6 times more frequently than men and is therefore comparable to the ED visit trends, their data show higher rates of service utilization among Hispanic and American Indian or Alaska Native populations compared to the ED visit data in our study.13 These differences may reflect data quality issues from one or both data sources as well as differences in populations served.

As of this writing, trends and demographic patterns of sexual violence ED visits in NC have been shared with community partners, and initial feedback on the utility of these data to inform prevention efforts has been positive. We are determining the most efficient processes to provide ongoing updates based on our V4 definition.

Limitations

This study focused on evaluations for sexual violence in ED visit data; ED visits for sexual violence may not represent the true burden of sexual violence in a community, and ED visit counts include evaluations for sexual violence that do not find evidence of confirmed sexual violence. Counties that have non-hospital organizations established to conduct sexual assault examinations may have lower ED visit utilization for sexual violence-related services. We relied on manually annotated sexual violence records as the gold standard for performance metrics across definitions. As these manually annotated data were pulled using the CDC V3 definition and its associated keywords, our validation data do not reflect the expected performance for these definitions within the entirety of the NC DETECT ED visit data. We took a conservative approach to building our V4 definition to limit the introduction of new FP from the greater NC DETECT ED visit data, but given our sampling strategy for manually reviewing ED visit records, additional evaluation against a broader dataset is needed to fully assess the impact of this approach. Care should be taken when attempting to generalize the PPV, NPV, and F1 score results to populations with different prevalences of sexual assault. While our inter-rater reliability was high for our manual review of the CDC V3 definition dataset sample, each record was reviewed by one person, and human error or inconsistency in coding may impact these results. Finally, the V4 definition may not be generalizable to other states if sexual violence codewords and/or text pre-processing steps differ from those used in NC.

Conclusion

ED-based surveillance of sexual violence through syndromic surveillance offers an opportunity for timely, population-based data on this complex topic. ED visits related to sexual violence include a mix of incident sexual violence visits and those where historical sexual violence may be impacting a patient’s current ED presentation. Building upon the CDC V3 definition and incorporating patient triage notes where available allowed us to develop a more sensitive and accurate surveillance definition for incident sexual violence. Continued improvement of surveillance definitions is important not only for the timely identification of public health issues, but also to provide essential data to community partners.Implications for Policy & Practice■ Syndromic surveillance ED visit data can complement other data sources traditionally used for understanding trends in sexual violence. They provide a timely source of information that can contribute to informing and evaluating prevention programs. Our evaluation demonstrated that incorporation of coded chief complaints and leveraging triage notes with nonspecific chief complaint terms can improve the accuracy of a sexual violence syndromic surveillance definition. Additional resources are needed to fully leverage syndromic surveillance systems to understand the full health-related impacts of sexual violence, especially for survivors of sexual violence who may have ongoing mental and physical health care needs.
