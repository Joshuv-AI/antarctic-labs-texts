Social learning theory application and potential in health education conducted by healthcare workers on TikTok - a content analysis observational study
Authors: Łucja Zielińska-Tomczak, Piotr Przymuszała, Agata Krasińska, Wiktoria Klimek, Ryszard Marciniak, Magdalena Cerbin-Koczorowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial media is increasing its role as a key source of health information and a platform for sharing health-related content. Among different platforms, TikTok has emerged as a popular medium used for promoting a healthy lifestyle, particularly among younger demographics. The potential basis for health education activities may involve the Social Learning Theory, which emphasises the role of behaviour modelling and social reinforcement in learning. This study aims to identify elements attributable to Social Learning Theory in health education content posted by healthcare practitioners on TikTok and to consider how the theory might explain how their activities on the platform could effectively influence lifestyle change through social media.MethodsThe study used content analysis, a method for systematically examining patterns, themes, and meanings embedded in user-generated communications, including textual, visual, and audiovisual materials. An analysis was performed on 30 purposively sampled profiles of healthcare professionals who conduct educational activities on TikTok. The goal was to identify elements related to the assumptions of Social Learning Theory. The selection of TikTok accounts was carried out using a purposive sampling method, searching for keywords that corresponded to medical professions. This approach aimed to ensure representativeness and diversity among the medical professions in the sample, as well as to select accounts that varied in terms of follower counts and engagement rates.ResultsThe authors of the analysed profiles employed various strategies to conduct educational activities, striving to create a unique style. The analysis revealed the presence of Social Learning Theory mechanisms, including attracting attention, supporting content memorization, enhancing motivation to adopt health‑related behaviours, and reinforcing reproduction and self‑efficacy. These mechanisms were identifiable across the examined profiles and reflected in diverse ways in creators’ communication strategies.ConclusionsWhile Social Learning Theory emphasises the importance of observation and internal processes—such as attention, retention, and reproduction—in the acquisition of behaviour, it is also essential to consider the external context in which creators operate. Many of the observed strategies align with those recommended in social media marketing, including capturing attention, storytelling, and building emotional engagement.Keywords: Attention, Education, Engagement, Health education, Health-related TikTok videos, Internet, Motivation, Social, Social learning theory, Social media

Introduction

Social media are playing a growing role as a source of health information, often becoming the primary source of information, including health-related content [1]. Users of social media platforms actively use them to seek information about diseases, treatment options, and prevention strategies, share their own experiences, and obtain emotional support from other people facing similar health challenges. Social media are gaining importance as a tool for health education activities due to their broad reach, low costs, and the ability to continuously and interactively share knowledge [2]. Their growing popularity, with a projected 5.42 billion users worldwide by 2025 [3], makes them an indispensable element of health promotion strategies and a tool for equalising opportunities in accessing health information.

Among these platforms, TikTok has emerged as a promising medium used for promoting healthy lifestyle choices [4], particularly among younger demographics [5, 6]. TikTok is a short video creation app with 1.590 million users worldwide, most of whom are between 16 and 24 years old [3]. Since its inception, the app has seen its popularity grow exponentially. TikTok was the most downloaded app, with 738 million downloads in 2019 and a total download count of over 1.5 billion. TikTok’s analytics firm, Sensor Tower, reported in May 2020 that the app had over 315 million downloads as of January 2020 [7–10]. Its appeal lies in the dynamic, accessible nature of short-form video content, which often draws upon the personal experiences of content creators. This narrative style, coupled with its engaging format, plays a key role in influencing behaviour through mechanisms such as observational learning and imitation [11]. However, despite TikTok’s growing popularity, significant concerns remain regarding its impact on users in the context of health education. For instance, the rapid content turnover on TikTok may hinder users’ ability to process information, resulting in only a superficial understanding of the presented topics [12, 13]. Additionally, studies indicate that a considerable portion of health-related content on the platform contains low-quality information or even misinformation, potentially misleading users and encouraging unsafe health behaviours [14–16]. Therefore, while TikTok holds promise as an educational platform, caution and critical evaluation of the presented content, as well as the expertise of its authors on the presented topic, especially in the area of medicine and healthcare, seem important.

Meanwhile, the incorporation of behavioural science theories into health education may significantly enhance the impact of such interventions [17]. Health education is not solely about disseminating information but also about creating meaningful learning opportunities that foster the motivation, skills, and self-efficacy necessary for individuals to take informed action toward improving their health [18, 19]. In this regard, Social Learning Theory (SLT) offers a particularly relevant conceptual framework, emphasising the importance of behaviour modelling and social reinforcement in the learning process [18, 20]. SLT assumes that many human behaviours are shaped by observing other people. This process can occur under the influence of observing another person who acts as an authority figure and a role model. These role models can be both close people, such as parents and teachers, as well as public figures who are somehow attractive to the individual or gain their sympathy (e.g., influencers). A key aspect of this process is that learning new behaviours occurs naturally, and the attractiveness of the model is a motivation to imitate their actions. In the context of education, key elements of this theory include capturing the recipient’s interest, using mechanisms that support remembering the content, and emphasising the outcomes of the actions taken—such as motivating the learner and strengthening their sense of self-efficacy, understood as the belief that they have the necessary skills and resources to perform a given task. This allows not only learning new skills but also integrating new behaviours into everyday life, which is crucial for maintaining long-term changes in health behaviours [20, 21].

Bandura [20] identifies four key processes as conditions for effective observational learning (attention, retention, reproduction, and motivation) (Fig. 1). Attention is a selective process, dependent on the characteristics of the model (e.g., attractiveness) and the social context; media such as television or social media can effectively attract attention and support modelling. Then, the observed behaviour must be encoded in memory - using an imagery system (e.g., images) or verbal system (e.g., instructions) - so that it can be later reproduced. This process can be reinforced through symbolic rehearsal or actual practice, with the relevance and form of the message having a significant impact on the durability of memory. The next stage is reproduction. If the behaviour has been remembered, a lack of appropriate sense of competence may prevent its execution. The final element is motivation: even if an individual is able to reproduce a given behaviour, they will not do so without appropriate reinforcing stimuli, such as social approval or a sense of efficacy. Reinforcements can be both external (e.g., rewards) and internal (e.g., satisfaction from success), and their strength depends on how they are perceived and interpreted. Bandura’s theory emphasises that learning is not simple copying, but a complex process in which observation, memorisation, reproduction, and motivation interact, and its effectiveness depends on the characteristics of both the model and the observer, as well as the conditions in which the educational process takes place [20].

Taking all of the above into account, this study aims to identify elements attributable to Social Learning Theory in the health and dietary education content posted by Polish healthcare practitioners on TikTok and to consider how the theory might help explain how their activities—within the specific context of this platform—could effectively influence lifestyle change through social media.

Materials and methods

Social media transcends being merely a platform for information sharing; it is an instrument that creates and fosters communities. Accordingly, this study employed qualitative content analysis as its primary methodological approach, with SLT serving as the guiding theoretical framework.

Qualitative content analysis is a well-established method for systematically examining patterns, themes, and meanings embedded in user-generated communications, including textual, visual, and audiovisual materials. This approach enables the structured coding, categorisation, and interpretation of media content, allowing researchers to extract both manifest and latent meanings without requiring direct engagement with the social media community under study [22, 23]. SLT provided an analytical lens for interpreting how health-related behaviours and norms are communicated through repeated visual and narrative cues, emphasising mechanisms of observational learning that are particularly salient in social media environments. This combined approach ensured that the analysis remained centred on content characteristics and message construction.

Given that the analysis was grounded in social learning theory, a deductive approach to qualitative content analysis was adopted. In deductive content analysis, theoretical concepts developed earlier inform the coding scheme and guide data interpretation. Therefore, coding categories were defined prior to the analysis based on key SLT constructs [24]. Although the analytical process was primarily deductive, the researchers remained open to refining category definitions in response to the research material to ensure conceptual coherence and sensitivity to platform-specific communication practices [25].

In order to meet the aims of the study, thirty TikTok accounts run by Polish healthcare professionals and containing health or dietary educational content were selected and analysed. The size of the sample was determined in accordance with principles of qualitative content analysis, which emphasises that the adequacy of a sample is defined not by its numerical size but by the depth, relevance, and informational richness of the data in relation to the research aim. Consistent with this approach, a limited number of cases may be sufficient when the material is focused, theoretically informed, and allows for systematic comparison across units of analysis [26]. The selection of TikTok accounts was conducted using a purposive sampling method using a search for keywords corresponding to the names of medical professions in such a way as to maintain the representativeness and diversification of the medical professions in the sample and to select accounts also varying in terms of their number of followers and engagement rates. The purpose of ensuring a diversified sample was to capture possible variations in communication styles and educational strategies used at different scales of operation of the influencers. Additionally, to ensure the content was up to date, only accounts that had been active in the last 3 months preceding data collection were analysed. Each creator of the selected account was also verified for authentically being a member of the declared medical profession. The content of the profile description, the subject matter of the published materials, and, if such data were available, the information confirming the creator’s current professional license (data from public registers [27–29]) were checked. Data collection and analysis were conducted between September 1 and November 30, 2024. For publication purposes, all data were anonymised, and individual accounts were assigned ascending codes (e.g., TT1, TT2). The analysis was carried out independently by two researchers, who reviewed the content published within the specified timeframe and coded the material according to thematic blocks derived from SLT. These included strategies for attracting attention, mechanisms supporting memorization, procedures facilitating replication, elements enhancing self-efficacy, and motivational aspects related to learning new behaviours.

An author-developed coding template in Microsoft Excel was used to ensure consistency and transparency in the analytical process. Following independent coding, the researchers compared their findings and discussed the results collaboratively. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion within the research team.

The trustworthiness of the qualitative analysis was addressed through strategies to enhance credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transparency [31]. Credibility was strengthened by the use of a clear theoretical framework (Social Learning Theory) that guided both data selection and interpretation, as well as by independent coding conducted by two researchers. Investigator triangulation enabled comparisons of interpretations, a broader research perspective, and confirmation of the findings [32]. Dependability was supported by an author-developed coding template, which ensured a consistent analytical procedure across all steps of the analysis. Also, all coding discrepancies were collaboratively discussed and resolved. Confirmability was ensured by systematically documenting observations and supporting evidence (e.g., quotes). Furthermore, engaging the entire research team in discussing the data helped reduce the potential for subjectivity in their interpretation. Finally, transparency was ensured through reporting of inclusion criteria, anonymisation of accounts, and then presentation of the analysed profiles’ characteristics, enabling readers to assess the scope of the findings.

The Bioethical Committee of the Poznan University of Medical Sciences determined that, in accordance with Polish law, the study did not demand its approval (Decision No. KB—406/24).

Results

The authors of the analysed profiles utilised different strategies to conduct educational activities on their profiles. It can be observed that they attempt to create their own unique style, which, once achieved, and if they have been on the platform for a long time, they tend to continue with the strategies or styles they have developed. For instance, among the profiles’ authors, some people spoke directly to the camera, while others sang along or acted out scenes that were both serious and humorous. As per the assumptions of Social Learning Theory, the analysed material demonstrates the presence of mechanisms related to attracting attention, enhancing content memorisation, fostering motivation to adopt new behaviours, and supporting reproduction and self-efficacy. These mechanisms appear across the examined profiles, with individual creators applying them in ways consistent with the core concepts of SLT.

Discussion

This study examined health and dietary educational content published by Polish healthcare professionals on TikTok using Social Learning Theory (SLT) as an analytical framework. The analysis showed that creators incorporated elements reflecting core SLT mechanisms [20], including attracting attention, supporting content retention, facilitating behavioural reproduction, and strengthening self‑efficacy. These mechanisms appeared across the analysed profiles, with individual creators applying them in diverse ways aligned with SLT principles.

Attention

According to SLT, attention is the first and essential stage of the observational learning process [20]. The analysed materials confirm that content creators use a variety of attention-grabbing techniques, ranging from expressive behaviour and catchy slogans to dynamic visual elements and references to cultural context and current trends. These tactics are particularly important in the social media environment, where intense competition for users’ attention is driven by information overload and algorithmic content filtering [13, 33]. These findings are consistent with previous research on digital health communication, which indicates that emotionally arousing and visually engaging content is more likely to capture attention and increase message reach in social media environments [34, 35]. Moreover, studies on short-form video platforms suggest that the first few seconds of a video are critical for audience retention, reinforcing the importance of strong initial stimuli [36]. Efforts aimed at attracting recipients’ attention seem also important given the occurrence of digital distractions that may reduce concentration during online learning, causing more frequent instances of loss of control and mind wandering [37]. It is noteworthy that certain strategies aimed at capturing attention, such as inducing anxiety or shame at the beginning of a video, can also serve as negative reinforcement, which will be further discussed later.

Retention

Research on learning strategies shows that achieving lasting educational outcomes requires active cognitive engagement rather than mere passive exposure to content [38]. Hence, supporting retention will be an important element of effective health education. Techniques supporting retention - i.e., the memorisation of observed content - were also observed in the analysed materials. Creators employed specific educational tools such as data visualisations, simplified schemes, repetition, rhymes, and metaphorical comparisons. It is also worth emphasizing that the use of mechanisms such as repetition and metaphors promotes the retention of new information. These approaches may facilitate encoding information into long-term memory and are consistent with principles of effective education [38, 39]. Importantly, some of these strategies, such as step-by-step instructions, simultaneously supported behavioural reproduction mechanisms. Research indicates that these types of instructions and demonstrations of health behaviours, especially those conducted within a behavioural framework, are more likely to lead to changes in actual behaviour than general information alone [17]. However, some of these techniques may not always be universal, for instance, metaphors can facilitate the educational process as long as the metaphor is perceived as common by the users [40].

Reproduction

Strategies facilitating behavioural reproduction and enhancing viewers’ self-efficacy were also identified, which corresponds with SLT assumptions. Examples such as “Anyone can do this - even you!” or “Start with just 10 minutes a day” are typical ways of strengthening the belief in one’s own capabilities. According to SLT, self-efficacy is a key determinant of whether an individual decides to adopt a new behaviour [20]. Such messages not only lower the entry barrier but also increase the likelihood of behaviour implementation and repetition in daily life, especially when linked to realistic goals and positive emotions [41, 42]. From the SLT perspective, to increase the effectiveness of TikTok-based education, more emphasis should be placed on reinforcing self-efficacy and encouraging behavioural reproduction.

Motivation

Motivational elements were present in the analysed materials in the form of both positive and negative reinforcement. Creators encouraged behaviour change by showcasing real-life success stories (“transformations”) or highlighting the positive outcomes of simple actions. Conversely, some employed deterrent strategies, such as shaming, fear induction, or controversial statements, to discourage unhealthy behaviours. This seems consistent with previous literature reports. As O’Donnell et al. [11] have shown, materials designed to shock or scare people away from engaging in some type of activity had good reach and engagement. On the other hand, they may not provide practical guidance on how to implement real changes in health behaviours [11]. Although such strategies may prove effective, they also pose risks of negatively affecting viewers’ psychological well-being or reinforcing guilt [43, 44]. It is important to note that fear-based motivation does not always lead to behaviour change; in some cases, it may result in information avoidance [45], thus producing effects contrary to the intended ones. Finally, even though the nature of sharing content on social media limits influencers’ opportunities for evaluating whether their observers actually try to adopt a new behaviour or change their lifestyle, or how knowledgeable, confident, motivated, or committed they feel in doing so, their comments or reactions left under the published content can also serve as a partial indication or a hint for the influencers on that matter allowing them to evaluate and, if required, adjust their educational content to better suit their observers’ needs [46].

SLT and marketing

While SLT emphasises the role of observation and internal mechanisms (e.g., attention, retention, reproduction) in behaviour acquisition [20], the external context in which creators operate should not be overlooked. Many of the observed strategies overlap with those recommended in social media marketing, including attention capture, storytelling, fostering interaction, and building emotional engagement [47, 48]. This indicates that some elements consistent with SLT mechanisms may be used not solely for educational purposes, but also to maximise reach, build a personal brand, or attract commercial partnerships. Consequently, health education on TikTok is often embedded in broader content strategies that balance educational value with algorithmic optimisation. However, this does not diminish the effectiveness of the message - on the contrary, it may enhance it. Studies in media education and health marketing have shown that engaging content tailored to the platform’s language and audience increases the likelihood of user receptiveness and real-world behavioural implementation [49, 50]. Algorithm-based content may also prove to be a threat. These algorithms often promote viral content, which is not always trustworthy. For instance, TikTok’s algorithm tends to favour posts that generate high engagement, even when those posts contain false or misleading information. Observations have shown that evidence-based content may receive less promotion than controversial content (attracting more engagement), which may favour emotionally charged, controversial, or sensationalised messages over evidence-based information [51, 52]. Misleading or oversimplified health information can spread rapidly on social media, potentially undermining public trust and leading to harmful health decisions [53].

From the SLT standpoint, the key issue is that, regardless of the creator’s motivation, the content may fulfil the conditions for effective modelling. Nonetheless, this raises important questions about the boundaries between education and marketing, and about creators’ responsibility for the accuracy and quality of the information they share. Moreover, exposure to inaccurate models may result in the adoption of ineffective or harmful behaviours, particularly when creators are perceived as credible healthcare professionals. This underscores the ethical responsibility of health influencers to ensure accuracy, transparency, and proportionality in their messaging, as well as the need for platform-level and institutional strategies to promote reliable health information [54].

Public health implications

The findings of this study have implications for understanding how health-related content on social media reflects principles of Social Learning Theory. Social media platforms, including TikTok, are increasingly serving as informal spaces where health information is presented, particularly for younger audiences who may have limited access to traditional healthcare systems [55]. The widespread presence of SLT-consistent content features suggests that such platforms may be conducive to observational learning processes. Integrating SLT-informed principles into digital health communication strategies may enhance the effectiveness of population-level interventions by supporting observational learning, reducing perceived barriers, and fostering sustainable behaviour change [56]. From a public health perspective, collaboration between healthcare professionals, educators, and policymakers could help establish best-practice guidelines for ethical, evidence-based, and most of all, effective health communication on social media platforms, thereby maximising benefits while minimising risks [53, 57].

Limitations

It is important to note that this study employed purposive sampling, which is an appropriate and commonly used strategy in qualitative research aimed at in-depth exploration of selected phenomena rather than statistical generalisation. Accordingly, the findings are not intended to represent the entirety of TikTok content, but to illustrate selected styles and strategies relevant to SLT communication. Additional limitations stem from the platform-specific nature of TikTok, including its recommendation algorithm, which shapes content visibility and user exposure in ways that are not fully controllable or transparent to researchers. Consequently, the analysed content reflects not only creators’ strategies but also algorithmic dynamics that may influence which videos gain prominence.

Future research directions

Future research could build on these findings by expanding the scope of analysis, incorporating a wider range of user demographics (e.g., adolescents vs. adults, healthcare professionals vs. laypersons), and examining the real-life behavioural impact of specific strategies used in health-related TikTok content.

Conclusions

To conclude, the conducted research shows that the authors of the analysed educational content posted on the TikTok platform utilised strategies aligning with the mechanisms and attributable to the assumptions of the Social Learning Theory. Among the observed ones, the strategies aimed at capturing users’ attention and enhancing content memorability should be mentioned, including dynamic visual formats, emotionally engaging openings, simplified explanations, and repetition of key information. Strategies supporting behavioural reproduction and strengthening viewers’ self-efficacy were also observed in the analysed material, but appeared rather as a specific style of selected creators. Similarly, motivational mechanisms—although present—were sometimes limited to showcasing positive outcomes or using deterrent, fear-based messaging, with less emphasis on practical guidance facilitating sustained behaviour change.

From the perspective of Social Learning Theory, these imbalances may reduce the long-term effectiveness of such content in translating knowledge into actual behavioural adoption. Therefore, conclusions regarding effectiveness should remain limited to the characteristics of the analysed material, highlighting the need for further research on how SLT-informed content features relate to real-world public health outcomes.
