Evaluation of sulphate contamination in surface and groundwater with health hazards in the largest opencast lignite mine region of Asia
Authors: B Preethi, T Subramani, D Karunanidhi, Berihu Abadi Berhe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWater contamination from sulphate is a growing concern in the Neyveli region of South India, where industrial activities have significantly impacted water quality. This study presents a comprehensive assessment of sulphate contamination and associated non-carcinogenic health risks in surface- and groundwater systems within the Neyveli lignite-mining region of Tamil Nadu, India. A stratified pre-monsoon survey of 81 samples in which 30 groundwater samples (bore wells, hand pumps, and open wells), and 51 surface water (ash ponds, drainage canals, ponds) were analysed for pH, EC, TDS, salinity, and sulphate using UV–VIS spectrophotometry, and spatial patterns were visualised via IDW interpolation in ArcGIS 10.8. Sulphate concentrations ranged from ~ 6–2,486.7 mg L⁻¹, exceeding the WHO permissible value of 400 mg L⁻¹; hotspots clustered near mine pits, ash-pond cells, and drainage corridors (SW3, SW36, SW38, SW50, GW10). Correlation analysis indicated negligible to very weak associations with EC/TDS/salinity (r ≈ 0.15–0.17), suggesting localised enrichment mechanisms rather than uniform ionic loading. Age-/gender-specific risk appraisal showed Hazard Quotient (HQ) > 1 at 19 sites for children (23.4%), 18 for teenagers (22.2%), 13 for adult males (16.0%), and 11 for adult females (13.6%). Critical contamination hotspots were identified at SW3, SW36, SW38, SW50, and GW10, where HQ values consistently exceeded acceptable risk thresholds across multiple demographic categories. Analytical QA/QC supported data reliability (recoveries 96.4 ± 2.1%, duplicate RPD 1.8–4.2%). In response to these findings, Neyveli Lignite Corporation India Limited (NLCIL) has undertaken several mitigation initiatives, including Water Treatment Plants and groundwater recharge structures. To enhance water quality management, remediation strategies like RO, gypsum ion exchange, fly ash adsorption, and sulphate-reducing bioreactors are recommended.Keywords: Groundwater, Surface water, Sulphate contamination, Human health hazard, Remediation strategies, Lignite mine regionSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Natural hazards

Introduction

Water quality encompasses the physical, chemical, and biological attributes of water that determine its suitability for drinking, recreation, and ecosystem support. Key indicators include pH, temperature, dissolved oxygen, turbidity, and specific pollutants such as pathogens, heavy metals, and organic compounds1. Anthropogenic pressures—industrial activity, agricultural runoff, and urbanisation—frequently degrade these attributes2, with substantial implications for public health: contaminated domestic water contributes to a large share of the global disease burden3. Groundwater interactions with heavy metals, fluoride, arsenic, nitrate, iodine, and other constituents pose immediate risks4,5; globally, exceedances such as As > 10 µg/L, F < 1 mg/L, and I > 100 µg/L affect billions of people6. With respect to sulphate, we provide only the essential context here (expanded details are discussed later): elevated sulphate in drinking water primarily causes gastrointestinal distress and acceptability concerns, may mobilise metals in site-proximal settings, and can accelerate corrosion and scaling in distribution systems5,7–9.

In lignite-mining terrains, sulphate enrichment typically arises from the exposure and oxidation of marcasite (FeS₂), gypsum (CaSO₄·2 H₂O), and pyrite (FeS₂), generating acid mine drainage (AMD) that affects both surface water and groundwater10. International observations from Spain and Brazil report AMD-linked sulphate elevation in rivers near lignite operations11,12, a pattern relevant to Neyveli where open-cast mining similarly exposes sulphide minerals. Related evidence from other mining/mineral districts—such as radionuclide risks in South Indian limestone belts and season-linked shifts in domestic water quality in the Guanzhong Plain—highlights the importance of post-extraction rehabilitation and seasonal risk appraisal; notably, dry-season conditions can elevate non-carcinogenic risk, and children often exhibit higher vulnerability13. Against this backdrop, focused investigation of sulphate pathways and demographic-specific health outcomes in Neyveli remains limited relative to the extensive attention paid to other contaminants, including fluoride, arsenic, and trace metals.

The presence of high levels of sulphate in drinking water can lead to several health concerns. While sulphate is generally non-toxic at low concentrations, excessive levels can cause gastrointestinal disturbances such as diarrhoea, dehydration, and discomfort. These effects are particularly pronounced in individuals who are not acclimated to high sulphate levels in their drinking water14. Chronic exposure to elevated sulphate levels can lead to more serious health issues, including an increased risk of developing chronic diseases. Vulnerable populations, such as infants, the elderly, and individuals with pre-existing health conditions, are particularly at risk. Long-term studies have indicated that persistent exposure to high sulphate concentrations in drinking water can impact overall health and well-being. For example15, highlighted the need for stringent monitoring and regulatory measures to ensure that sulphate levels in water sources remain within safe limits, particularly in areas affected by industrial activities such as lignite mining. Although the impacts of water contamination have been well-documented globally, specific investigations into sulphate contamination in lignite mining regions remain limited. In Neyveli, where large-scale open-cast lignite mining and thermal power generation have significantly altered the hydrogeological regime, there is a notable gap in the literature concerning demographic-specific health risk assessments related to sulphate exposure. Most existing studies in the region have either focused on general water quality trends or on contaminants such as fluoride, arsenic, and heavy metals, without isolating sulphate as a distinct pollutant of concern.

Accordingly, the present study was designed to establish a comprehensive hydrogeochemical and health-risk assessment framework for the Neyveli lignite-mining region of Tamil Nadu, South India. The research specifically aims to: (i) quantify the extent and spatial variability of sulphate contamination in both surface and groundwater systems through field-based measurements and GIS-driven spatial interpolation (Inverse Distance Weighting, IDW) to delineate contamination hotspots; (ii) interpret the hydrogeochemical relationships among sulphate, pH, electrical conductivity (EC), total dissolved solids (TDS), and salinity by applying correlation and statistical analyses to identify dominant geochemical processes such as pyrite oxidation, and ash-pond leachate migration; and (iii) evaluate the non-carcinogenic human-health risks arising from sulphate ingestion across multiple demographic categories—children, teenagers, adult males, and adult females—using the United States Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) hazard-quotient (HQ) model.

The scope of this investigation encompasses a quantitative appraisal of contamination magnitude, spatial distribution, and demographic vulnerability within the mining-affected hydrological system. By integrating hydrochemical characterisation, spatial analytics, and human-health risk modelling, this study provides a holistic understanding of sulphate behaviour in mining-impacted aquifers and identifies high-priority zones requiring remediation. Furthermore, the outcomes are aligned with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 3 – Good Health and Well-Being; SDG 6 – Clean Water and Sanitation), ensuring that the research contributes to evidence-based strategies for sustainable water-resource management and public-health protection in industrial regions such as Neyveli.

Materials and methods

Study area

Neyveli, located in the Cuddalore district of Tamil Nadu, India, lies between latitudes 11.35° N–11.70° N and longitudes 79.30° E–79.65° E, covering approximately 443 km² (Fig. 1). The town is situated about 62 km inland from the Bay of Bengal and plays a crucial role in India’s energy sector owing to its extensive lignite reserves. It hosts the Neyveli Lignite Corporation India Limited (NLCIL)—a Government of India enterprise established in 1956—which operates Asia’s largest open-cast lignite mining complex. The population of the surrounding area is about 150,00016, with most residents depending on groundwater for drinking, domestic, and agricultural purposes. The region encompasses a mixture of industrial, residential, and natural zones, offering a representative setting for assessing the environmental and health impacts of mining-related activities. The Neyveli Lignite Mine Complex extends over ≈ 470 km² with an annual production capacity exceeding 28 million tonnes of lignite. Mining depths vary between 40 m and 100 m, involving continuous excavation, dewatering, and large-scale overburden removal. The exposure of sulphur-bearing strata,chiefly pyrite (FeS₂) and marcasite, which releases sulphate and associated metals into groundwater and surface-water systems.

Location map of neyveli lignite mining region in south india.

Geologically, the area is underlain by the Cuddalore Formation, a Miocene–Pliocene sedimentary sequence composed of alternating clay, silt, sand, and lignite beds deposited in a fluvio-lacustrine environment (Fig. 217,18;. The Neyveli Lignite Formation, a member of this sequence, hosts several lignite seams at shallow depths (10–100 m), interbedded with clayey aquicludes and sandy aquifers. The clayey layers function as confining beds, forming confined and semi-confined aquifers, whereas the sandy horizons serve as primary groundwater reservoirs.

Geology of the study region.

Hydrogeologically, the region comprises a dual-aquifer system—an upper unconfined aquifer and a deeper confined aquifer within the Cuddalore Formation. Recharge occurs mainly through monsoonal rainfall, but its natural regime has been substantially modified by mine dewatering operations required to keep pits dry19. Prolonged pumping has caused localized water-table drawdown, enhanced groundwater–surface-water interactions, and created conditions conducive to sulphate movement from mining zones. The alternation of permeable and semi-permeable layers promotes ion exchange and sulphate migration through secondary porosity. Continuous mining has also produced several man-made water bodies, such as mine-sump lakes, ash ponds, and settling basins, which have altered the natural drainage pattern and increased connectivity between surface and subsurface hydrological systems. These anthropogenic modifications, coupled with oxidation of exposed sulphide minerals, contribute to elevated sulphate, iron, and total dissolved solids (TDS) concentrations in both groundwater and surface-water sources20,21.

Previous investigations have reported sulphate levels up to 1600 mg L⁻¹ in shallow aquifers near active mine pits. These studies attribute contamination to pyrite oxidation and ash-pond leachate seepage, confirming the region’s hydrogeochemical vulnerability. Consequently, an updated, high-resolution spatial and health-risk assessment is warranted to evaluate current contamination dynamics and their implications for public health.

Sample collection and analysis

A total of 81 water samples were systematically collected from the Neyveli lignite-mining region, comprising 51 surface water samples (mine discharge channels, rivers, ponds, and drainage canals) and 30 groundwater samples (hand pumps, open wells, and bore wells). To ensure comprehensive spatial representation, the study area was divided into three zones based on mining intensity and land-use characteristics: (i) the core mining zone, (ii) an intermediate buffer zone, and (iii) the peripheral rural/residential zone. Within each zone, sampling points were selected using a stratified random design, ensuring balanced inclusion of both surface and subsurface sources and avoiding bias toward easily accessible locations. Sampling was conducted once during the pre-monsoon season (May 2023), representing the period of lowest groundwater levels and maximum contamination potential. During field collection, samples were drawn from wells, bore wells, ash ponds, lakes, and rivers using acid-washed, sterile 250 mL high-density polyethylene bottles pre-rinsed with distilled water. Prior to collection, each water source was flushed adequately to obtain representative samples, and the bottles were rinsed with the same water to prevent memory effects. On-site field measurements for pH, Total Dissolved Solids (TDS), and salinity were performed immediately using portable meters to minimise in-situ variations. All samples were filtered through 0.45 μm cellulose nitrate membrane filters to remove suspended particulates and were preserved at < 4 °C in insulated boxes during transport to the laboratory. Analyses were completed within 48 h of collection to maintain chemical integrity.

UV-VIS spectrophotometer analysis

Sulphate concentration in the water samples was analysed using a UV-VIS spectrophotometer. This instrument measures a sample’s absorption or transmission of light across the ultraviolet (UV) and visible (VIS) spectrum. The UV-VIS spectrophotometer measures and identifies the compounds in the sample by using the premise that numerous molecules absorb light at various wavelengths22,23. For quality assurance, the instrument was calibrated using sulphate standard solutions prepared from analytical-grade potassium sulphate (K₂SO₄). Reagent blanks and duplicate samples were used to check precision, while recovery was assessed using standard reference materials, achieving > 95% recovery throughout the analyses.

Spatial analysis of parameters using GIS

The software ArcGIS (v.10.8) was utilised to create many thematic layers using the spatial technique. The geographical information system (GIS) was used to generate spatial distribution plots for pH, TDS, salinity, and sulphate concentrations for the collected 81 water samples from the field. For this collected water samples, the spatial distribution has been done for the water quality parameters using the inverse distance weighted (IDW)24–26.

Quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC)

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the dataset, quality assurance and control (QA/QC) protocols were rigorously implemented throughout the study. The UV-VIS spectrophotometer used for sulphate analysis was calibrated using standard sulphate solutions prepared from analytical-grade potassium sulphate (K₂SO₄), in accordance with23. The analytical performance of the dataset was quantitatively evaluated using replicate and standard reference analyses. The relative percent difference (RPD) for duplicate samples ranged between 1.8% and 4.2%, indicating good analytical precision. Method blanks showed no detectable sulphate, confirming the absence of cross-contamination. The recovery rate for sulphate standards averaged 96.4% ± 2.1% (n = 10), demonstrating consistent instrument accuracy. These metrics collectively affirm the reliability and reproducibility of the sulphate concentration data used in subsequent analyses. All samples were analysed within 48 h of collection, and laboratory glassware was acid-washed and rinsed with deionised water to prevent cross-contamination. Instrument performance was monitored daily to detect and correct any drift during measurement.

Spatial and statistical analysis

Spatial mapping of water quality parameters was performed using ArcGIS 10.8, applying the Inverse Distance Weighted (IDW) interpolation technique to delineate spatial variability and contamination hotspots for pH, TDS, salinity, and sulphate. In addition to correlation analysis, multivariate statistical analysis was conducted to identify key processes controlling sulphate behaviour. A Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was carried out using the correlation matrix of five hydrochemical parameters (pH, EC, TDS, salinity, and sulphate). Prior to analysis, all data were normalised (z-scores) to eliminate dimensional bias. Components with eigenvalues > 1 were retained, and Varimax rotation was applied to maximise interpretability. PCA results were used to distinguish between natural geogenic sources (e.g., gypsum dissolution, pyrite oxidation) and anthropogenic contributions (e.g., ash pond leachates, mining effluents).

Acidity and alkalinity of water: pH insights

The pH of water is a vital indicator of its chemical properties and suitability for various uses, such as drinking, agriculture, and industry. In surface water, pH levels predominantly range from neutral to slightly alkaline in the central and northern regions, suggesting stable water chemistry with minimal direct impact from industrial activities like mining. These stable pH conditions support local biodiversity and make the water suitable for agricultural purposes. However, in the southwestern region, particularly around SW 27 and SW 28, higher pH levels indicate more alkaline water (Fig. 4). This elevated alkalinity could be attributed to natural geological formations releasing carbonate ions or anthropogenic activities such as agricultural runoff and wastewater discharge. Alkaline water can disrupt nutrient availability and negatively affect aquatic ecosystems, especially in areas where neutral pH is more favorable for biodiversity. Moreover, elevated pH may also influence soil chemistry, which could impact agricultural productivity in the region.

Alkalinity and acidity of surface water and groundwater based on pH.

Groundwater, in contrast, exhibits greater variability in pH across the study area. While the central and eastern regions show slightly acidic to neutral conditions, which indicate relatively good groundwater quality, the northeastern and southwestern areas display more acidic conditions, particularly near groundwater sampling points GW 1, GW 2, GW 3, and GW 27. These acidic conditions are likely influenced by the proximity to the Neyveli Lignite Mines, where industrial activities and acid mine drainage can lower pH levels. Acidic groundwater can lead to the leaching of heavy metals like iron and aluminium, posing risks to human health, soil quality, and agricultural output. Additionally, the southwestern region, near GW 27, is impacted by both mining and commercial fish culture, which involves deep bore wells exceeding 550 feet, further exacerbating the acidification of groundwater. Overall, compared to surface water, groundwater is more susceptible to industrial impacts and shows more pronounced acidity, while surface water tends to remain more stable but faces localized alkaline challenges.

Total dissolved solids: indicators of water purity

The Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) distribution across the region surrounding the Neyveli Lignite Mines in Tamil Nadu shows significant variation in water quality for both surface water (SW) and groundwater (GW). TDS, which measures the concentration of dissolved salts, minerals, and organic matter, is a key indicator of water quality (Fig. 5). Elevated TDS levels reduce water’s suitability for drinking, agriculture, and industrial use, while lower levels indicate cleaner water. In the northeastern and northwestern regions, TDS levels in surface water range from 138 to 500 mg/L, indicating high-quality water, particularly around sampling points like SW 1, SW 2, and SW 3. These areas, distant from mining operations, experience minimal industrial impact. In contrast, central, western, and eastern regions show moderate TDS levels, ranging from 500 to 1,500 mg/L, as seen at sampling points like SW 10, SW 20, and SW 26, likely due to a combination of natural geological processes and indirect mining influences. The southern regions, particularly around SW 49, SW 34, and SW 39, exhibit TDS concentrations exceeding 1,500 mg/L, primarily due to the proximity to mining activities, industrial runoff, and mineral dissolution. Elevated TDS levels in these areas result in water that is unsuitable for agriculture and drinking without significant treatment, as they can cause scaling in irrigation infrastructure and reduce soil fertility, ultimately affecting crop productivity.

Fitness of surface water and groundwater for consumption based on TDS. IDW interpolation method in ArcGIS 10.8 used. Red zones indicate exceedance of WHO limit (> 1500 mg/L).

In groundwater, TDS levels also show considerable variation. Northern and northeastern regions, especially around GW 28, GW 2, and GW 29, exhibit low TDS levels (138 to 500 mg/L), indicating relatively pure water unaffected by mining operations. This low mineralisation is due to the distance from industrial activities, ensuring groundwater quality is preserved for drinking and agricultural use with minimal processing. However, central and southwestern regions show moderate TDS levels (500 to 1,500 mg/L), particularly at GW 21, GW 30, and GW 27, where the influence of mining runoff and rock-water interactions contributes to the elevated dissolved solids. Groundwater in southern and southeastern regions has the highest TDS levels, exceeding 1,500 mg/L at points like GW 9, GW 12, and GW 14, driven by both surface runoff and subsurface leaching from mining activities. This high TDS renders groundwater unsuitable for agricultural or drinking purposes without significant treatment, as prolonged use of such water can lead to soil degradation, reduced crop yields, and health risks. In comparison, surface water generally shows more spatial heterogeneity in TDS levels due to the immediate impact of surface runoff and industrial discharges, while groundwater demonstrates higher concentrations near mining areas due to long-term mineral leaching and deeper geological influences.

Salinity hotspots and their environmental implications

Salinity, representing the concentration of dissolved salts in water, is a key determinant of water quality, especially in regions affected by anthropogenic activities like mining. Elevated salinity can impact the suitability of water for drinking, agriculture, and industrial use (Fig. 6). In the northern and northeastern sectors of the study area, salinity levels are notably low, ranging from 74.64 to 600 mg/L. Sampling points such as SW 1, SW 2, and SW 3 show that surface water in these regions remains largely unaffected by industrial or saline intrusion, reflecting a pristine hydrological environment. This low salinity makes the water suitable for irrigation, domestic use, and ecosystem support without requiring significant treatment. In contrast, the central and western regions display moderately elevated salinity levels between 600 and 900 mg/L, influenced by both natural processes like soil and rock weathering and human activities such as agricultural runoff and minor industrial effluents. Although the water remains usable, these elevated salinity levels could pose long-term risks to agriculture, particularly by contributing to soil degradation. In the southern region, particularly around SW 17 and SW 21, salinity levels exceed 900 mg/L. Such elevated salinity renders the water unsuitable for irrigation and human consumption without proper treatment, as excessive salts can degrade soil and reduce crop yields.

Groundwater in the northern and northeastern regions shows similarly low salinity levels, ranging from 74.64 to 600 mg/L, particularly around GW 28, GW 3, and GW 29. This indicates high-quality groundwater with minimal salt intrusion, suitable for drinking, agricultural, and industrial purposes. These areas are relatively insulated from the saline influence of mining activities, maintaining lower salinity levels. However, in the central and southwestern regions, salinity levels range from 600 to 900 mg/L, particularly around GW 21, GW 30, and GW 17, reflecting moderate salinity from both natural and anthropogenic sources, including industrial runoff and agricultural practices. While this groundwater is still generally usable, continuous monitoring is required to prevent further increases in salinity, which could compromise agriculture and human health. The southern and southeastern regions show significantly elevated salinity levels in groundwater, exceeding 900 mg/L, with some areas as high as 2,798 mg/L around GW 9, GW 12, and GW 14. This severe salinity is likely caused by industrial activities from the Neyveli Lignite Mines, making the groundwater unsuitable for most agricultural and domestic uses without significant treatment. The comparison between surface water and groundwater reveals that both sources are affected by salinity, but groundwater in proximity to mining areas shows higher salinity, posing risks for agriculture and human consumption. Surface water generally exhibits lower salinity but is still at risk in regions closer to industrial activities.

Sulphate concentration: indicators of pollution and mining impacts

The sulphate distribution surrounding the Neyveli Lignite Corporation India Limited (NLCIL) mines in Tamil Nadu exhibits significant spatial variation in both surface water (SW) and groundwater (GW), influenced by natural geological conditions and extensive mining operations. Sulphate serves as a crucial indicator of water quality in mining-impacted zones, where elevated concentrations commonly arise due to the oxidation of sulphur-bearing minerals and industrial effluents. In the northern and western sectors—particularly around sampling sites SW 1, SW 2, SW 5–10, SW 12, SW 22, SW 30, SW 33, and SW 51—sulphate levels in surface water range from 14.1 to 250 mg/L, indicating relatively good quality and minimal industrial influence, except at SW 50, which was sampled from a fly ash pond and recorded significantly elevated sulphate, reflecting contamination (Fig. 7). Conversely, central and southern regions (e.g., SW 22, SW 25, SW 26, and SW 49) show moderate sulphate concentrations (250–400 mg/L), pointing to increasing effects of rock weathering and anthropogenic activity. Notably, in central areas such as SW 3, SW 36, SW 38, SW 40, and SW 50, concentrations exceed 400 mg/L, a strong signal of contamination from mining activity, particularly due to pyrite oxidation.

Groundwater trends mirror this pattern: the northern and northeastern regions (GW 1–3, GW 18, GW 19, GW 21, GW 27, GW 28) exhibit lower sulphate levels (6.06 to 250 mg/L), reflecting primarily natural geochemical processes. Central regions (GW 4–8, GW 14, GW 16) show intermediate values (250–400 mg/L), and the southern and southeastern areas (GW 9, GW 10, GW 12, GW 13, and GW 30) display concentrations above 400 mg/L, with peaks reaching 1,637 mg/L—clearly influenced by pyrite oxidation and rock water interaction. These values render the groundwater unsuitable for consumption or agriculture without advanced treatment.

Importantly, these findings are consistent with32 report, which also documents elevated sulphate concentrations in the aquifer systems surrounding the Neyveli mining region. According to CGWB’s aquifer mapping in the Lower Vellar watershed, persistent sulphate enrichment was attributed to mining activity, industrial discharge, and geogenic factors within the Cuddalore aquifer system.

Correlation analysis of hydrochemical parameters

A Pearson correlation matrix was computed to evaluate the interrelationships among the key hydrochemical parameters-sulphate, pH, electrical conductivity (EC), total dissolved solids (TDS), and salinity-based on the data obtained from 81 water samples. The resulting correlation coefficients are presented in Table 2. The correlation analysis revealed that sulphate exhibits negligible to very weak correlations with EC (r = 0.166), TDS (r = 0.160), and salinity (r = 0.148), indicating that sulphate behaves largely independently of the bulk ionic load in the study area. This suggests that its occurrence is primarily controlled by localised geochemical and anthropogenic processes, such as pyrite oxidation and ash pond leaching, rather than by overall mineral dissolution that governs general water salinity. This pattern implies that sulphate is not a major contributor to the overall ionic load and is likely derived from specific sources such as oxidation of pyritic lignite seams, ash pond percolation, and mine drainage pathways, which are characteristic of lignite-mining environments. In contrast, strong and statistically significant correlations were observed among EC, TDS, and salinity (r > 0.99), which is expected given that these parameters are directly influenced by the total concentration of dissolved ions. This strong association reinforces the reliability of the field measurements and reflects the influence of bulk mineral dissolution and evapotranspiration on water chemistry in the region. The correlation between pH and the other parameters was generally low, with r = 0.071 for sulphate and moderate values for EC (r = 0.287), TDS (r = 0.279), and salinity (r = 0.265). This reflects the complex buffering capacity of the aquifer system, where pH is regulated by carbonate equilibria and does not directly reflect the concentration of dissolved salts or sulphate. These findings are consistent with previous hydrogeochemical studies conducted in mining-impacted regions, where sulphate often behaves independently of general salinity indicators due to its origin from site-specific redox processes and mineral oxidation10,21. Therefore, sulphate may serve as a reliable tracer for mining-related contamination, particularly in areas proximal to lignite seams and overburden waste deposits.

Geochemical correlation of iron and sulphate

The Neyveli lignite mining area is geochemically predisposed to the co-occurrence of iron and sulphate in both surface and groundwater systems due to the oxidation of sulphide minerals. This relationship is especially evident in zones affected by extensive lignite extraction, dewatering operations, and the disposal of fly ash. Iron and sulphate are commonly mobilised together through the oxidative weathering of pyrite (FeS₂), a mineral frequently found within lignite seams and associated overburden. The oxidation process releases ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) and sulphate ions (SO₄²⁻) into the surrounding groundwater and surface water systems, while also contributing to increased acidity (lower pH). The resultant acidic conditions promote the further dissolution and mobility of iron and other trace metals. Spatially, groundwater samples such as GW9, GW10, GW12, GW13, and GW30, and surface water samples including SW 3, SW 36, SW 38, SW 40, SW 50, which exhibit high sulphate concentrations (> 400 mg/L)32. This co-contamination is most prominent in the central, northern and southeastern portions of the study area, where lignite exposure and ash pond proximity are greatest. Under anaerobic or reducing conditions, sulphate can also undergo microbial reduction by sulphate-reducing bacteria (SRB), producing hydrogen sulphide (H₂S). This H₂S may further react with dissolved iron to precipitate as iron sulphide (FeS). Such interactions lead to water discoloration, foul odor (rotten egg smell), and further alteration of aquifer chemistry. The redox-sensitive behaviour of both ions underscores the complex interaction between geological, hydrological, and biological processes in the Neyveli subsurface environment. The co-occurrence of high sulphate and iron concentrations has important implications for water treatment. Iron-rich waters can foul filters and promote bacterial growth, while high sulphate levels contribute to laxative effects, especially in children and sensitive populations.

Demographic-based non-carcinogenic risk assessment

The study presents a comprehensive analysis of the Hazard Quotient (HQ) values for various demographic groups exposed to different water sources, with the aim of assessing the potential non-carcinogenic health risks posed by contaminants in these water samples (Table 3). A detailed demographic analysis was conducted to assess the non-carcinogenic health risks from sulphate-contaminated surface and sub-surface water in the Neyveli region. Innovative approaches and sustainable solutions for reducing sulphate contamination and enhancing water quality

Innovative approaches to reducing sulphate levels in water systems

Sulphate is a prevalent anion in groundwater, playing a significant role in geochemical interactions between water and surrounding geological formations. The reduction of sulphate, converting it into less harmful substances, can naturally occur through both chemical and biological processes. Biological reduction, for example, uses organic carbon to convert sulphate into hydrogen sulphide and bicarbonate. Addressing elevated sulphate levels in groundwater is critical, and various methods—physical, chemical, and biological—are employed to mitigate contamination. Traditional Pump-and-Treat (PAT) systems, which extract and treat polluted groundwater, are commonly used, though their limitations have led to the development of alternative technologies such as reverse osmosis (RO) and electrodialysis (ED)33. Chemical methods like ion exchange and adsorption also play a crucial role, with techniques like the GYP-CIX method and CESR (Cost-Effective Sulphate Reduction) showing success in reducing sulphate concentrations to acceptable levels34,35. Additionally, biological methods, particularly those involving sulphate-reducing bacteria (SRB), are important for natural sulphate reduction. SRBs thrive in anaerobic environments, converting sulphate into sulphide, and are often used in controlled systems like in situ bioreactors for effective treatment36,37. These methods, when combined, offer a comprehensive approach to addressing sulphate contamination in groundwater.

A practical, location-specific plan is proposed for the Neyveli region based on the level of contamination. Pump-and-Treat (PAT) in Neyveli Township and Periyakurichi to rapidly depress groundwater sulphate (typically 800–1000 → <250 mg L⁻¹, ~ 70–75% removal over 6–12 months), and pair PAT with ion-exchange (GYP-CIX) or CESR (lime/Ca(OH)₂ gypsum precipitation) to polish effluent and limit reagent use. For surface waters in Kurinjipadi and Vadalur, implement Reverse Osmosis (RO) and Electrodialysis (ED) for sulphate/salinity control, with strict brine management via lined evaporation ponds, reuse/recovery units, or controlled return to treatment headworks to prevent secondary salinisation. In plume cores at Periyakurichi and Kurinjipadi, install in-situ SRB bioreactors (permeable reactive zones) to biologically reduce sulphate and co-precipitate metals, providing a lower-O&M, long-term barrier. To support decentralised and rural settings in Vadalur and Valayammadevi, add adsorptive units using locally available fly ash/limestone, either as kiosks or barriers, integrated with PAT/IX trains where feasible. Basin-wide non-structural measures—buffer zones around water bodies and sustainable farming (reduced sulphate fertilisers)—should be prioritised in Valayammadevi and Prasannarajapuram to curb runoff and lower treatment demand downstream. This combined strategy aligns technologies with site conditions while balancing near-term risk reduction and long-term sustainability.

Environmental initiatives for water quality improvement

NLC India Limited (NLCIL) has implemented various initiatives to enhance water quality and promote community development, with a focus on environmental sustainability in the Neyveli Lignite Mines region. One key aspect of NLCIL’s efforts is its mine water management system, which supplies treated mine water for drinking and irrigation. This initiative benefits over 150,000 residents (Census, 2011) in Neyveli Township, with water also extended to the Chennai Metro. Surplus water is used for irrigation across 40 villages, including Kurinjipadi, Periyakurichi, Vadalur, and Valayammadevi, boosting agricultural productivity. NLCIL also supports rainwater harvesting systems like check dams and percolation wells, which help recharge groundwater, ensuring sustainable water resources for both domestic and agricultural use. NLCIL’s focus on afforestation and greenbelt development plays a critical role in improving air quality and reducing pollution. Greenbelts, consisting of native tree species, act as buffers against industrial emissions, while afforestation in mined-out areas restores ecological balance, prevents soil erosion, and creates habitats for wildlife. Additionally, social forestry programs engage local communities in tree planting, promoting economic benefits through fruit-bearing and medicinal trees. By integrating treated water into the community, NLCIL significantly improves water quality by reducing contamination from mining activities. Treated water is safe for consumption and irrigation, while the recharging of groundwater ensures long-term water availability. These efforts, combined with afforestation and sustainable agricultural practices, create a resilient and environmentally sustainable region, benefiting villages like Neyveli Township, Kurinjipadi, and Vadalur.

Despite these efforts, elevated sulphate levels in groundwater remain a challenge, particularly in regions surrounding the Neyveli Lignite Mines. Integrating more advanced sulphate reduction technologies, such as ion exchange, reverse osmosis, bioreactors, and adsorption methods, will be crucial in further improving water quality. By continuing to innovate and expand its water treatment capabilities, NLCIL can ensure a more sustainable and healthier environment for the communities it serves.

Comparison with Indian and international studies

The Neyveli dataset—sulphate spanning 18.4–2,486.7 mg L⁻¹ (median ≈ 279 mg L⁻¹), negligible associations with bulk ionic indicators (EC/TDS/salinity; r ≈ 0.15–0.17), and hotspot clustering adjacent to mine pits, ash-pond cells, and drainage corridors with the greatest HQ exceedances in children and teenagers—accords with patterns reported from Indian coal/lignite provinces where acid mine drainage (AMD), ash-pond seepage, and evaporative concentration frequently elevate sulphate beyond the 250–400 mg L⁻¹ guideline envelope in site-proximal waters20,31,32, and38. Comparable magnitudes and spatial coupling are documented internationally in AMD-impacted systems of the Appalachian coalfields (USA), the Witbank/Highveld coalfields (South Africa), and the Iberian Pyrite Belt (Spain), where sulphate commonly occurs in the hundreds to > 1,000 mg L⁻¹ and exhibits strong proximity to mine voids, waste-rock dumps, and tailings10,39,40, and41. Mechanistically, the Neyveli correlations support a non-uniform, process-driven control dominated by pyrite oxidation with contributions from gypsum/anhydrite dissolution and cation exchange, consistent with the Indian and international literature10,38, and39. The frequent co-occurrence of Fe/Mn noted elsewhere aligns with our field context near pits and ash-handling areas, and age-stratified risk assessments similarly identify higher susceptibility among younger cohorts due to intake-to-body-mass ratios32,42, and43. Collectively, the magnitude, spatial expression, and risk hierarchy observed in Neyveli are congruent with those documented across India and abroad, underscoring the need for hotspot-focused mitigation and for seasonal, multi-contaminant extensions (Hazard Index) to refine cumulative risk.

Conclusions

This study presents a detailed hydrogeochemical and health-risk assessment of sulphate contamination in both surface and groundwater systems of the Neyveli lignite-mining region, Tamil Nadu. Analysis of 81 pre-monsoon water samples—comprising 51 surface-water (mine discharge channels, rivers, ponds, and drainage canals) and 30 groundwater (hand pumps, open wells, and bore wells)—revealed widespread exceedance of permissible sulphate limits. Concentrations ranged from 6 to 2486.7 mg L⁻¹, with maximum values observed near mine pits and fly-ash pond zones, clearly implicating lignite mining, ash disposal, and associated dewatering activities as the dominant contamination sources.

The weak correlations of sulphate with bulk ionic indicators (EC r = 0.166; TDS r = 0.160; salinity r = 0.148) indicate localised enrichment rather than uniform mineralisation, suggesting that sulphate originates primarily from oxidation of sulphide minerals (pyrite, marcasite) and industrial leachates rather than general lithological dissolution. The co-occurrence of elevated Fe and SO₄²⁻ reduce pH, contributing to secondary pollution and infrastructural corrosion.

Spatial interpolation using ArcGIS 10.8 (IDW) highlighted contamination hotspots concentrated within approximately 145 km² (≈ 33% of the study area) in central and southeastern Neyveli. These zones correspond closely with areas of intense mining, ash-pond seepage, and interconnected surface- and groundwater systems. Based on population data (Census 2011), nearly 45,000 residents (≈ 30%) are exposed to sulphate-contaminated water exceeding the WHO (400 mg L⁻¹) and BIS (200 mg L⁻¹) limits, underlining the scale of public-health concern.

The health-risk assessment revealed that children and teenagers are the most vulnerable demographic groups, with HQ > 1 in 19 and 18 locations, respectively, reflecting the influence of higher ingestion-to-body-weight ratios. Adults also exhibited risk exceedances at multiple sites, confirming widespread exposure across age groups.

From a hydrogeochemical standpoint, the results underscore that pyrite oxidation, gypsum/anhydrite dissolution, and ash-pond leachate migration are the key mechanisms controlling sulphate mobility. These processes are further intensified by the altered hydrostratigraphy resulting from large-scale mine dewatering, which enhances vertical mixing between surface ponds and shallow aquifers. The findings mirror observations in other lignite and coal-mining basins worldwide—such as Singrauli (India), Witbank (South Africa), and the Iberian Pyrite Belt (Spain)—demonstrating that Neyveli represents oxidation of sulphide minerals requiring targeted intervention.

Environmental implications of these findings are critical. Persistent sulphate contamination can accelerate aquifer acidification, metal mobilisation, and salinity buildup, threatening both water security and ecosystem stability. Long-term exposure may impair agricultural productivity, corrode pipelines, and compromise drinking-water infrastructure.

The recommended mitigation and management strategies focus on both immediate control and long-term sustainability. Immediate actions include improving ash-pond linings, installing leachate collection systems, and diverting mine drainage for proper treatment.

Rapid sulphate reduction can be achieved through Pump-and-Treat systems combined with gypsum precipitation or ion-exchange (GYP-CIX) units, while in-situ bioreactors using sulphate-reducing bacteria (SRB) provide long-term, low-maintenance remediation.

To enhance groundwater recharge and dilution, construction of check dams and percolation ponds is recommended, along with mine-water reuse schemes under NLCIL’s sustainability programs for safe domestic and agricultural use.

For future work, a continuous groundwater monitoring network should be established to track seasonal and spatial variations. Expanding to a multi-contaminant hazard index (HI) covering fluoride, nitrate, and heavy metals will improve overall risk assessment. The use of geostatistical and machine-learning tools is suggested for predictive mapping, while community-based programs promoting awareness, rainwater harvesting, and safe-water alternatives will ensure long-term water security and sustainable resource management.

Overall, this study provides an integrated framework linking mining-induced geochemical transformations, human health exposure, and spatial risk mapping. The findings directly support SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-Being) and SDG 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation) by informing evidence-based policies for sustainable groundwater governance in industrial mining regions.

Recommendations

Remediation strategies such as physical methods like Pump-and-Treat, chemical techniques like adsorption and gypsum precipitation, and biological treatment like sulphate-reducing bacteria in bioreactors may be adopted to provide a safe drinking water supply to the community.

Innovative solutions, like in-situ bioreactors and local material adsorption techniques, may offer cost-effective and sustainable solutions for sulphate reduction.

Environmental initiative by NLC India Limited, including treated mine-water supply for drinking and agriculture purposes, is one of the best exercises for sustainable mining practices. Further, advanced remediation technologies are still needed to avoid human health risks due to sulphate contamination in surface and groundwater of the Neyveli region, South India.

Rainwater harvesting techniques adopted by NLCIL, like the construction of check dams and percolation wells, significantly balance the rainwater recharge and groundwater abstraction for mining activities. However, a few more artificial recharge structures are to be created in the Neyveli hydrological basin to replenish the multi-layered aquifer system.

Continuous monitoring of groundwater quality and groundwater fluctuation is required for protecting water quality, public health, and ecosystems in the region.

This study demonstrates widespread sulphate contamination across the Neyveli lignite mining region, with exceedances concentrated near active mining and industrial zones, and the highest non-carcinogenic risk borne by children and teenagers. While findings are robust under reported uncertainty bounds, a multi-contaminant Hazard Index and seasonal monitoring are needed to fully capture cumulative and temporal risks. In the near term, targeted provision of treated water in hotspots, source control around pits/ash ponds, and sentinel-well monitoring can meaningfully reduce exposure. Policy measures aligned with WHO limits, transparent public reporting, and integration of remediation and managed aquifer recharge into mine-water governance are recommended to safeguard health and sustain groundwater resources.
