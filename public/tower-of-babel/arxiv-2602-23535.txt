Partition Function Estimation under Bounded f f -Divergence
Adam Block, Abhishek Shetty

Abstract
We study the statistical complexity of estimating partition functions given sample access to a proposal distribution and an unnormalized density ratio for a target distribution. While partition function estimation is a classical problem, existing guarantees typically rely on structural assumptions about the domain or model geometry. We instead provide a general, information-theoretic characterization that depends only on the relationship between the proposal and target distributions. Our analysis introduces the integrated coverage profile, a functional that quantifies how much target mass lies in regions where the density ratio is large. We show that integrated coverage tightly characterizes the sample complexity of multiplicative partition function estimation and provide matching lower bounds. We further express these bounds in terms of f f -divergences, yielding sharp phase transitions depending on the growth rate of f and recovering classical results as a special case while extending to heavy-tailed regimes. Matching lower bounds establish tightness in all regimes. As applications, we derive improved finite-sample guarantees for importance sampling and self-normalized importance sampling, and we show a strict separation between the complexity of approximate sampling and counting under the same divergence constraints. Our results unify and generalize prior analyses of importance sampling, rejection sampling, and heavy-tailed mean estimation, providing a minimal-assumption theory of partition function estimation. Along the way we introduce new technical tools including new connections between coverage and f f -divergences as well as a generalization of the classical Paley-Zygmund inequality.

2
Problem Setup and Preliminaries

We consider a measurable space
\mathcal{X}
and base probability measure
\mu
over
\mathcal{X}
.
We will also consider a target distribution
\nu
over
\mathcal{X}
from which we would either like to produce a sample or to estimate its normalizing constant given access to samples from
\mu
and an unnormalized density ratio
\lambda:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
defined using the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\nu
with respect to
\mu
as
\lambda(x)=Z\cdot\nicefrac{{d\nu}}{{d\mu}}(x)
for some unknown normalizing constant
Z
.
We are primarily interested in understanding the difficulty of obtaining an estimate
\widehat{Z}
of the normalizing constant
Z
; more precisely, we ask the following question:

Given access to i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\sim\mu
and the ability to evaluate an unnormalized density ratio
\lambda
on each sample, how large must
n
as a function of
\mu
,
\nu
,
\varepsilon
and
\delta
be to ensure that there exists an estimator
\widehat{Z}=\widehat{Z}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},\lambda(X_{1}),\ldots,\lambda(X_{n}))
satisfies
(1-\varepsilon)Z\leq\widehat{Z}\leq(1+\varepsilon)Z
with probability at least
1-\delta
?

The rate at which such an estimate can be obtained depends on the similarity between the two distributions
\mu
and
\nu
.
For example, if
\mu
and
\nu
had disjoint supports, then no finite number of samples from
\mu
would suffice to estimate
Z
.
The main focus of this work is provide a sharp characterization of the sample complexity in terms of quantitative measures of similarity between
\mu
and
\nu
.

The key notion measuring the divergence between
\mu
and
\nu
is that of
coverage
, considered by
[
8
,
10
]
.
Conceptually, coverage measures the mass that
\nu
places on regions where the density ratio
\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}
is large and thus good coverage implies that
\mu
places sufficient mass in high-density regions of
\nu
. Formally, coverage is defined as follows.

Definition 1

(Coverage and Integrated Coverage)
.

For two probability measures
\mu,\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
and
M>0
, the
coverage
function at
M
is defined to be

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)=\nu\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\nicefrac{{d\nu}}{{d\mu}}(x)\geq M\right\}\right),

(3)

where
\nicefrac{{d\nu}}{{d\mu}}
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\nu
with respect to
\mu
.
Further, we introduce a notion that we term the integrated coverage profile as

\displaystyle\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)=\int_{0}^{M}\mathrm{Cov}_{t}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)dt.

(4)

We first note several basic properties of coverage and integrated coverage. First,
it is immediate that
\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq 1
for all
M>0
, and thus
\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq M
; moreover,
\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)
is clearly non-increasing in
M
. Furthermore, an easy calculation implies that
M\mapsto\nicefrac{{\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)}}{{M}}
is also non-increasing in
M
and whenever
\nu\ll\mu
, this quantity tends to zero as
M\to\infty
. We will see that this latter map is fundamental to characterizing the sample complexity of partition function estimation.

The notion of coverage has previously been used to analyze the sample complexity of importance sampling
[
8
]
, the sample complexity of rejection sampling
[
3
]
and, interestingly, for understanding the efficacy of pretrained language models
[
10
,
19
,
20
]
.
The notion of integrated coverage, introduced here, is a more refined measure of the relationship between
\mu
and
\nu
that allows for sharper characterizations of sample complexity.

A more standard notion of discrepancy between two measures is the
f
-divergence
[
12
,
35
]
, which is defined as follows.

Definition 2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a convex function satisfying
f(1)=f^{\prime}(1)=0
. For two probability measures
\mu,\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
, the
f
-divergence between
\nu
and
\mu
is defined to be

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[f\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\right)\right]+\nu\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}=\infty\right)\cdot f^{\prime}(\infty).

(5)

The
f
-divergences generalize many well-known notions of distance between probability measures, including total variation distance (where
f(t)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\left|t-1\right|
), the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence (where
f(t)=t\log t
), and (a monotone transformation of) the
\alpha
-Renyi divergences (where
f(t)=t^{\alpha}-\alpha t
for
\alpha>0
,
\alpha\neq 1
). Of special note is the
\alpha
-Renyi divergence with
\alpha=2
, which corresponds to the
\chi^{2}
divergence, defined as
\chi^{2}(\nu\|\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[\left(\nicefrac{{d\nu}}{{d\mu}}(X)-1\right)^{2}\right]
.

Conceptually, a bounded
f
-divergence controls the tail behavior of the density ratio
\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}
with faster growing
f
leading to stronger control. As we show in
Appendix

C
, this intuition is made precise in several ways, most critically in the observation that
\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq\nicefrac{{M\cdot\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)}}{{M}}
for any
f
-divergence; thus, bounded
f
-divergence implies coverage decays quickly. As we shall see, our sample complexity results involving the
f
-divergences will depend on the growth rate of
f
through that which we term the
\gamma_{f}
function, defined to be the inverse of the map
t\mapsto\nicefrac{{f(t)}}{{t}}
on
[1,\infty)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\gamma_{f}(M)=\inf\left\{t\geq 1:\nicefrac{{f(t)}}{{t}}\geq M\right\}.

(6)

The growth rate of
f
determines the behavior of
\gamma_{f}
; for example,
when
f
is
superlinear
, i.e.,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\nicefrac{{f(t)}}{{t}}=\infty
, the function
\gamma_{f}
is well-defined on all of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
; examples of superlinear
f
include those corresponding to KL divergence (where
\gamma_{f}(M)\asymp\exp(M)
) and
\alpha
-Renyi divergences with
\alpha>1
(where
\gamma_{f}(M)\asymp M^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}
). We will also have occasion to distinguish between
superquadratic

f
, where
\lim_{t\to\infty}\nicefrac{{f(t)}}{{t^{2}}}=\infty
(e.g.,
\alpha
-Renyi divergences with
\alpha>2
) and those that are not (e.g., KL divergence and
\alpha
-Renyi divergences with
1<\alpha\leq 2
such as
\chi^{2}
). In the case that
f
is
linear
, we let
\gamma_{f}(M)=\infty
for
M>\sup_{t}\nicefrac{{f(t)}}{{t}}
; note that by the assumption of convexity,
f
cannot be sublinear.
These distinctions will control the precise rates in our sample complexity results.

3
Main Results

We now present our main results regarding the sample complexity of estimating the partition function given access to samples from the base distribution and an unnormalized density ratio.

3.1
Upper Bounds for Estimation

We begin by stating our main upper bound on the sample complexity of estimating the partition function in terms of the integrated coverage profile defined in
Definition

1
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be probability measures on
\mathcal{X}
. Suppose that we have access to i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\sim\mu
and an unnormalized density ratio
\lambda
.
Let

M^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq\varepsilon/4

Then there is an estimator
\widehat{Z}
such that as long as

\displaystyle n\gtrsim\nicefrac{{M_{\varepsilon}\cdot\log\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\delta}}\right)}}{{\varepsilon}},

(7)

then with probability at least
1-\delta
it holds that
(1-\varepsilon)\cdot Z\leq\widehat{Z}\leq(1+\varepsilon)Z
.

As we discussed above, as long as
\nu\ll\mu
, such an

always exists for any positive

; thus the above theorem always provides a finite sample complexity bound for estimating the partition function to
(1\pm\varepsilon)
accuracy in the absolutely continuous setting.

In order to appreciate (
7

\chi^{2}(\nu||\mu)
is bounded.
In this case, a simple tail bound implies
\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq\chi^{2}(\nu||\mu)
for all
M
.
Therefore, we can set
M_{\varepsilon}\asymp\chi^{2}(\nu||\mu)/\varepsilon
in Theorem
4
, and obtain that
n\gtrsim\chi^{2}(\nu||\mu)/\varepsilon^{2}
samples suffice to estimate the partition function to
(1\pm\varepsilon)
accuracy with high probability.
This recovers standard results bounding the sample complexity of importance sampling by computing the variance
[
34
]
.
It is natural to ask, then, how slowly can
M_{\varepsilon}
grow as a function of
\varepsilon
? Perhaps one might hope that with improved moment bounds, one could achieve a faster estimation rate via
M_{\varepsilon}
, but this is not the case. Indeed, this can be seen intuitively by observing that, because
\mathrm{Cov}_{0}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)=1
for any two distributions
\nu,\mu

M_{\varepsilon}\gtrsim\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
and
Theorem

4

\Omega(\varepsilon^{-2})
, as one would expect for estimating a mean to
(1\pm\varepsilon)
accuracy due to the central limit theorem.
Thus, we should instead view the above theorem as a generalization of the bound to more general heavy-tailed settings where the
\chi^{2}
divergence may be infinite.

We may thus ask whether we can provide more explicit bounds on the sample complexity in terms of the more familiar notion of
f
-divergences, which amount to control on the tails of the density ratio. This is the content of our next theorem.

.

Let
\mu,\nu
be probability measures on

3.2
Lower Bounds

In order to complement our upper bounds, we provide several lower bounds on the sample complexity of estimating the partition function, both in terms of integrated coverage and in terms of the
f
-divergences that demonstrate the tightness of our bounds. The lower bound constructions, deferred to
Appendix

, are inspired in part by

f
-divergences. We begin with the integrated coverage lower bound.

Theorem 7
.

For any non-atomic
\mu
and any
\varepsilon\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, there exists a family of distributions
\nu
such that any estimator
\widehat{Z}
that achieves
(1\pm\varepsilon)
multiplicative accuracy with probability at least
\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}
requires at least
\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot M_{\varepsilon}
samples, where

satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{ICov}_{M_{\varepsilon}}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq M_{\varepsilon}\cdot\varepsilon.

(10)

In particular,
Theorem

7
shows that the sample complexity bound of

is tight in its dependence on integrated coverage and

. Unfortunately,
Theorem

7
does not directly translate to lower bounds in terms of

-divergences; while it is indeed the case that integrated coverage can be tightly controlled via

-divergence, it is not clear that this tight control is realized by the lower bound construction of
Theorem

7
. We thus complement
Theorem

5

Proposition 1
.

Let
f
be a convex function as in

is bounded. For any

, there exist measures
\mu,\nu
such that
\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)\leq C
such that no estimator given access to finitely many samples from
\mu
is capable of producing a

approximation of

with probability at least
\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}
for any
\varepsilon\leq\nicefrac{{f^{\prime}(\infty)}}{{(f^{\prime}(\infty)-C)}}
. Moreover, if
C\geq f^{\prime}(\infty)
, then there exist measures
\mu,\nu
such that
\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)\leq C
and no such estimator can produce a

\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}

.

Proposition

1
shows that when
f
is linear, no finite number of samples suffices to estimate the partition function to within any nontrivial multiplicative accuracy. This fact is unsurprising given that
f
-divergences with linear
f
(such as total variation or Hellinger) do not control the tails of the density ratio
\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}
in any meaningful way and indeed allow for the possibility that
\nu
is singular with respect to
\mu
, where clearly estimation of
Z
is impossible. We now proceed to the second case, where
f
is superlinear but subquadratic.

Theorem 8
.

For any
C>2
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
, there exists a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
and class of probability measures
\mathcal{V}
on
\mathcal{X}
such that for all
\nu\in\mathcal{V}
, it holds that
\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)\leq C+f(1-\varepsilon)
and any estimator
\widehat{Z}
that satisfies
(1-\varepsilon)Z\leq\widehat{Z}\leq(1+\varepsilon)Z
with probability at least
\frac{2}{3}
for all
\nu\in\mathcal{V}
must use at least
n\gtrsim\nicefrac{{\gamma_{f}\left(\frac{C}{2\cdot\varepsilon}\right)}}{{\varepsilon}}

samples.

While this lower bound holds for arbitrary
f
-divergences, it is only tight in the superlinear/subquadratic regime, where
\gamma_{f}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})\cdot\varepsilon^{-1}\gg\varepsilon^{-2}
; this applies to KL-divergence and Renyi divergences for
1<\alpha\leq 2
. For the superquadratic case, we have the following lower bound.

Proposition 2
.

Fix
0<\varepsilon<\nicefrac{{1}}{{8}}
. Then there exist a family of probability measures
\mathcal{U}
on
\mathcal{X}
and probability measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
such that for all
\mu\in\mathcal{U}
, it holds that
\left\|\nicefrac{{d\nu}}{{d\mu}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 4
and any estimator
\widehat{Z}
that for all
\nu\in\mathcal{V}
with probability at least
\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}
is a
1\pm\varepsilon
approximation of
Z
must use at least
n\gtrsim\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}
samples.

Taken together, the lower bounds presented in this section demonstrate that each of our upper bounds are tight in their respective regimes both in terms of integrated coverage and
f
-divergences.

3.3
Comparison to Sampling

While our primary focus is on partition function estimation, it is instructive to compare our results to those for the related problem of
sampling
from the target distribution
\nu
given access to samples from the base distribution
\mu
and an unnormalized density ratio
\lambda
. In
3
, the authors examined the question of
approximate rejection sampling
, where the learner is given access to
n
i.i.d. samples from
\mu
and the
normalized
density ratio
\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(\cdot)
and must produce a sample from a distribution
\nu_{n}
that is close to
\nu
in total variation distance under the assumption of bounded
f
-divergence between
\nu
and
\mu
. We generalize their result, as well as that of
14
to the setting of
unnormalized
density ratios and arbitrary
f
-divergences.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be probability measures on
\mathcal{X}
and let
f
be a convex function as in
Definition

2
. Suppose that we have access to i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\sim\mu
and an unnormalized density ratio
\lambda
. Then there is an algorithm that produces a sample
X_{\widehat{j}}
from a distribution
\nu_{n}
such that
\mathrm{TV}\left(\nu_{n},\nu\right)\leq\varepsilon
as long as

\displaystyle n\gtrsim M\cdot\log\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right)\quad\text{where }\Cov_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\lesssim\varepsilon.

(11)

In particular, this holds for

\displaystyle n\gtrsim\log\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right)\cdot\gamma_{f}\left(\Theta(1)\cdot\nicefrac{{\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)}}{{\varepsilon}}\right).

(12)

We observe that the lower bounds in
3
for approximate rejection sampling already demonstrate that (
12
) is tight up to logarithmic factors as the regime in that work is strictly stronger than ours (i.e., access to the normalized density ratio rather than the unnormalized one). We defer a proof of
Proposition

3
to
Appendix

F
, where we generalize analysis of the
A^{*}
-sampling algorithm in
27
,
14
.

It is instructive to note that the sample complexity of sampling is strictly smaller than that of partition function estimation.
To see this note that
\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq M^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)
for all
M
, since
\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)
is a decreasing function of
M
. Thus it follows immediately that we have at least an
\varepsilon^{-1}
factor improvement in sample complexity when comparing (
11
) to (
8
) in
Theorem

4
. The improvement can be even more stark, however, depending on the behavior of the coverage profile. Indeed, we saw that
M^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)
can never shrink more quickly than
M^{-1}
as
M
increases; thus, in the superquadratic regime, where
\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)
is bounded for quickly-growing
f
, we can have almost a quadratic separation between the sample complexity of sampling and that of partition function estimation. Indeed, in the extreme case where the density ratio is uniformly bounded, i.e.,
\left\|\nicefrac{{d\nu}}{{d\mu}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq M
, sampling requires only
n\gtrsim\log\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right)
samples to produce an
\varepsilon
-approximate sample from
\nu
in total variation distance, while partition function estimation requires
n\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-2}
samples. One way to interpret this is that estimation depends on the entire coverage profile, while sampling only depends on the coverage at a single value of
M
.

In this way we see that, in contradistinction to the well-known class of ‘self-reducible’ problems, the problem of ‘counting’ (i.e., partition function estimation) is strictly harder than that of ‘sampling’ under general
f
-divergence or coverage constraints.

4
Application: Improved Finite Sample Bounds for Importance Sampling

Importance sampling is a fundamental technique in statistics and machine learning which serves as a primitive in many domains, including causal inference, reinforcement learning
[
36
,
39
]
, variational inference
[
6
]
, and probabilistic programming
[
44
]
.
It serves as a tool for estimating expectations of a function
g
(we focus on the bounded, positive case) under a target distribution
\nu
using samples from a proposal distribution
\mu
.
Given i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\sim\mu
, the importance sampling estimator for
\nu_{g}=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[g(X)]
is

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{\mathrm{IS}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})g(X_{i}).

(13)

This estimator is unbiased, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{g}_{\mathrm{IS}}\right]=\nu_{g}
and standard results on importance sampling estimators bound the variance of
\hat{g}_{\mathrm{IS}}
which can be bounded rely on the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between the target and proposal distributions, since the variance is bounded by
\chi^{2}(\nu\|\mu)
.

Towards obtaining sharper bounds, we consider the target distribution weighted by the function
g
, i.e., the measure
\nu\cdot g
defined with density given by

\nicefrac{{g(x)}}{{\nu_{g}}}\cdot\nicefrac{{d\nu}}{{d\mu}}(x)
with respect to
\mu
.
This allows us to define the coverage between the weighted target distribution
\nu\cdot g
and the proposal distribution
\mu
, which in this context maybe expressed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\cdot g\|\mu\right)=\frac{1}{\nu_{g}}\int g(x)\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(x)\mathbb{I}\left[g(x)\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(x)\geq M\cdot\nu_{g}\right]d\mu(x)

(14)

Further this allows us to define the integrated coverage and the
f
-divergence
\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\cdot g\middle\|\mu\right)
between the weighted target distribution
\nu\cdot g
and the proposal distribution
\mu
in the natural way.

Applying
Theorem

4
, we obtain finite-sample bounds for importance sampling estimators in terms of general
f
-divergences.

Theorem 9

(Importance Sampling)
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be measures and
g
be a bounded, positive function.
Then the importance sampling estimator
\hat{g}_{\mathrm{IS}}
satisfies
\left|\hat{g}_{\mathrm{IS}}-\nu_{g}\right|\leq\varepsilon\cdot\nu_{g}
with probability at least
1-\delta
, as long as
n\gtrsim\nicefrac{{M_{\varepsilon,\delta}}}{{\varepsilon}}
.
where
M_{\varepsilon,\delta}
satisfies
\mathrm{ICov}_{M_{\varepsilon,\delta}}\left(g\nu\|\mu\right)\leq\nicefrac{{\varepsilon\delta}}{{6}}
.

This generalizes the standard variance bounds for importance sampling, which correspond to the case of the
\chi^{2}
-divergence.
The dependence on probability parameter
\delta
can be improved to
\log(1/\delta)
using the median of means technique as in the proof of
Theorem

4
but state it in this form to maintain the form of the estimator that is commonly used in practice.

More importantly, our results bound the error as jointly as function of
g,\nu
and
\mu
, rather than separately e.g. in terms of
D_{f}(\nu\|\mu)
and
\|g\|

[
8
]
.
This is particularly useful in settings when we get to design the proposal distribution
\mu
based on the target functions
g
and distribution
\nu
, bound above can be used to optimize the proposal distribution
\mu
to minimize the required sample complexity for a given set of target functions
g
.
In particular, the optimal proposal distribution is a well studied problem in importance sampling (See
[
34
,
29
]
for a survey-level treatment) and is typically chosen to minimize the variance of the importance sampling estimator.
Our results suggest that a more refined objective is to minimize the integrated coverage between the weighted target distribution.

Our techniques also extend to self-normalized importance sampling (SNIS)
[
34
]
, which estimates expectations
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[g(X)]
when we can only sample from a proposal distribution
\mu
and evaluate the unnormalized density ratio
\lambda(x)=\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(x)\cdot Z
for some unknown normalizing constant
Z=\int\lambda(x)d\mu(x)
.
Given i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\sim\mu
, the SNIS estimator is
\hat{g}_{\mathrm{SNIS}}=\nicefrac{{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda(X_{i})g(X_{i})}}{{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda(X_{i})}}
.
Under the assumption that
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu
, the SNIS estimator is asymptotically unbiased
[
34
, Theorem 9.2]
.
The asymptotic variance is
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[\left(g(X)-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\nu}[g(X)]\right)^{2}\cdot\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\right)^{2}\right]

which for bounded functions
g
is at most
\|g\|_{\infty}^{2}\cdot\chi^{2}(\nu\|\mu)
.
These asymptotic bounds yield finite-sample sample complexity of
n\geq\frac{4\|g\|_{\infty}^{2}\chi^{2}(\nu\|\mu)}{\delta\varepsilon^{2}}

[
32
, Proposition 9]
.

Our results yield an improved finite-sample analysis of the SNIS estimator in terms of general
f
-divergences, applicable even when the
\chi^{2}
-divergence is infinite.

Theorem 10

(Self-Normalized Importance Sampling)
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be measures and
g
be a bounded, positive function.
Then the self-normalized importance sampling estimator
\hat{g}_{\mathrm{SNIS}}
satisfies
\left|\hat{g}_{\mathrm{SNIS}}-\nu_{g}\right|\leq\varepsilon\cdot\nu_{g}
with probability at least
1-\delta
, as long as
n\gtrsim\nicefrac{{M_{\varepsilon,\delta}}}{{\varepsilon}}
,
where
M_{\varepsilon,\delta}
satisfies
\mathrm{ICov}_{M_{\varepsilon,\delta}}\left(g\nu\|\mu\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{6}
and
\mathrm{ICov}_{M_{\varepsilon,\delta}}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{6}
.

Note that the requirement on the integrated coverage of both the weighted target distribution
g\nu
and the target distribution
\nu
is necessary since the SNIS estimator is a ratio of two importance sampling estimators where the denominator estimates the normalizing constant
Z
.

5
Proof Techniques for Upper Bounds

In this section, we outline some of the key proof techniques used to establish our main upper bounds, with a focus on technical results that may be of independent interest. We defer full proofs of all results to the appendix.

5.1
Upper Bounds on Sample Complexity

Our upper bound relies on the same general proof strategy and the same estimator, namely the
median-of-means

[
30
,
1
]

Formally, given
n
samples from
\mu
, we partition them into
k
groups of size
m=\lfloor\nicefrac{{n}}{{k}}\rfloor
and compute the sample mean within each group, i.e., for each group
j\in[k]
, we let

\displaystyle\widehat{Z}_{j}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda(X_{i}^{(j)})\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{Z}=\Med\left(\widehat{Z}_{1},\dots,\widehat{Z}_{k}\right),

(15)

where
\Med(\cdot)
denotes the median operator. Using standard analysis (cf.
Lemma

4
), it suffices to show that each group mean
\widehat{Z}_{j}
is within a constant factor of the true partition function
Z
with constant probability, say
\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}
. Thus, the main technical challenge is to analyze the concentration of the sample mean
\widehat{Z}_{j}
around
Z
. While we defer full proofs of
Theorems

4
and
5
to
Appendices

B
and
C.2
, we sketch the main ideas below.

Proof Sketch of
Theorem

4
.

The first natural approach to analyzing the concentration of
\widehat{Z}_{j}
would be to apply standard concentration inequalities such as Chernoff bounds.
Unformately as discussed earlier, this approach fails because the density ratio
\nicefrac{{d\nu}}{{d\mu}}
may be heavy-tailed, and thus the variance of the summands may be infinite.
In order to overcome this challenge, we look at the concentration of truncated versions of the density ratio; note that this truncation purely an analytical tool and the estimator
\widehat{Z}_{j}
does not involve any truncation. We thus analyze the ‘bulk’ of the density ratios and the ‘tail’ separately. To lower bound
\widehat{Z}_{j}
, we can ignore the tail entirely, while to upper bound the estimate, we apply Markov’s inequality to the definition of coverage.

Thus, much of the effort arises in controlling the average of the truncated density ratios, whose expectation can be appropriately controlled via the definition of coverage. One might at first like to apply a standard Chernoff bound, because truncated at
M
the summands are bounded, but this could at best achieve a sample complexity scaling as
M/\varepsilon^{2}
where
M
is such that
\Cov_{M}(\nu\|\mu)\leq\varepsilon
, which is loose. Instead, we apply Bernstein’s inequality and bound the
variance
of the truncated density ratio, which we show can be controlled by the integrated coverage itself (
Lemma

5
). This interesting
self-normalization
type property that relates the variance of the truncated density ratio to the bias introduced by truncation is the key technical tool that allows us to improve the dependence on
\varepsilon
in the sample complexity.
\blacksquare

We can then use this result to prove
Theorem

5
by relating the integrated coverage to the
f
-divergence, as we sketch below.

Proof Sketch of
Theorem

5
.

In order to get a sample complexity that depends on the
f
-divergence, we need to relate the integrated coverage to the
f
-divergence.
At first glance, this seems simple since an application of Markov’s inequality directly relates the two quantities (
Lemma

7
).
Unfortunately, this direct relationship is not sufficient to get us the desired bound on the integrated coverage.
Towards this end, we need to use the growth properties of the function
f
. In particular, since we are interested primarily in the subquadratic regime, we use the assumption that
t^{-2}\cdot f(t)
is decreasing for
t\geq c
to relate the integrated coverage to the
f
-divergence as shown in
Lemma

9
.
\blacksquare

5.2
Asymmetric Estimation via Coverage

In order to obtain improved dependence on
\varepsilon
, in
Theorem

6
we relaxed the symmetric estimation requirement to only tightly controlling the lower tail while allowing a looser constant factor upper tail. The technical approach is built on the following lemma which can be read as a generalization of the Paley-Zygmund inequality to
f
-divergences, whose proof can be found in
Section

E.1
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be probability measures on
\mathcal{X}
and let
f
be a convex function as in
Definition

2
. Then for any
0<\varepsilon,u<1
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\mu}\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\geq 1-\varepsilon\right)\geq\nicefrac{{(1-u)\varepsilon}}{{M}}\quad\text{where }\Cov_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq u\cdot\varepsilon.

(16)

In particular,
\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\mu}\left(\nicefrac{{d\nu}}{{d\mu}}(X)\geq 1-\varepsilon\right)\geq\sup_{0<u<1}\nicefrac{{(1-u)\cdot\varepsilon}}{{\gamma_{f}\left(\nicefrac{{\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)}}{{u\cdot\varepsilon}}\right)}}
.

While
Lemma

1
is phrased in terms of
f
-divergences and likelihood ratios, it could otherwise be stated for arbitrary nonnegative random variables, providing a lower bound on the probability that such a variable exceeds a
(1-\varepsilon)
fraction of its mean. Indeed, taking
f(t)=(t-1)^{2}
and letting
Y\geq 0
, we see that we recover Paley-Zygmund up to a factor of
4
. We similarly recover the standard generalization of Paley-Zygmund to higher moments by taking
f(t)=t^{p}-1
for
p>1
. In the case that we only assume a
\left|X\right|\log(\left|X\right|)
moment, we derive a bound that, to the best of our knowledge, is novel, showing that a nonnegative random variable exceeds
(1-\varepsilon)
times its mean with probability at least
\varepsilon\cdot\exp\left(-O(1)\cdot\nicefrac{{\mathbb{E}[X\log(X)]}}{{\varepsilon\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]}}\right)
. We now sketch the proof of
Theorem

6
using
Lemma

1
; full details can be found in
Appendix

E
.

Proof Sketch of
Theorem

6
.

Unlike the previous upper bounds, we do not use a median-of-means estimator here, instead simply taking the
\left(1-\alpha\right)
-quantile of the samples for appropriately chosen
\alpha
. The key idea is to use
Lemma

1
to lower bound the probability that a single sample exceeds
(1-\varepsilon)Z
, which then allows us to control the lower tail of the quantile estimator via a Chernoff bound. We then control the upper tail of the estimator via the definition of coverage to ensure that the quantile does not exceed
M\cdot Z
with high probability.
\blacksquare

6
Discussion and Related Work

In this work, we have provided a complete characterization of the sample complexity of partition function estimation in terms of integrated coverage and
f
-divergences between the target and proposal distributions.
We now discuss related work.

Partition Function Estimation.

Partition function estimation is a classical problem with roots dating back to Gibbs and Boltzman in statistical mechanics, with many classical techniques developed and analyzed in the intervening years
[
33
,
15
,
22
,
34
]
. One common line of work in theoretical computer science and statistical mechanics assumes some kind of discrete structure and constructs sophisticated annealing schedules and samplers in order to estimate partition functions of complex models such as the Ising model or counting problems such as the permanent of a matrix
[
22
,
2
,
21
,
25
,
38
]
. Another line of work, especially in scientific and Bayesian applications, focuses on continuous distributions in Euclidean space and makes use of strong regularity assumptions in order to ensure quality
[
9
,
17
,
28
]
. In contradistinction to those works, we focus on a minimal assumption setting where we characterize the difficulty of partition function estimation in terms of purely information theoretic quantities, without making any structural or regularity assumptions.

Sampling and Importance Sampling.

In parallel to partition function estimation, the closely related problem of approximate sampling from a target distribution
\nu
given sample access to a proposal distribution
\mu
and the ability to evaluate the density ratio
\nicefrac{{d\nu}}{{d\mu}}
in order to construct mean estimates under target distributions has also been extensively studied
[
24
,
7
,
34
]
. Most relevant to our work is those of
13
,
8
. The former, while not explicitly about partition function estimation, considers heavy-tailed mean estimation under polynomial moment constraints; our
f
-divergence recover their results when considering Renyi divergences. On the other hand,
8
consider importance sampling and examine the sample complexity under KL constraints, proving that exponentially many samples in the KL divergence are necessary and sufficient for importance sampling to succeed, but do not consider more general
f
-divergences or coverage. Our results generalize and sharpen theirs, recovering their bounds as a special case and unifying these paradigms. More recently,
3
studied the problem of approximate rejection sampling, which is closely related to our results, except they assume
normalized
density ratio access. On the other hand,
27
consider sampling with unnormalized density ratios but assume bounded density ratios, whereas
14
generalizes this to a bounded KL constraint. We apply the same algorithm, introduced in
31
, and generalize these prior results to arbitrary
f
-divergences and coverage constraints.

f

A.1
Concentration Inequalities

We first state the standard Chernoff bound (cf. for example
[
4
]
).

Lemma 2

(Chernoff Bound)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
be independent random variables such that for each
i\in[m]
, it holds that
X_{i}\in[0,M]
almost surely and
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]
. Then for any
1\geq t>0
if holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}\leq(1-t)\mu\right)\leq e^{-\frac{m\mu t^{2}}{2M}}.

(17)

Moreover, for any
t>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}\geq(1+t)\mu\right)\leq\exp\left(-\nicefrac{{m\mu}}{{M}}\cdot\left((1+t)\log(1+t)-t\right)\right).

(18)

Because we are generally considering
t\lesssim\varepsilon\ll 1
, we will often simplify the upper tail bound using the fact that
(1+t)\log(1+t)-t\geq\nicefrac{{t^{2}}}{{3}}
for
t\in(0,1)
so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}-\mu\right|\geq t\mu\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\nicefrac{{m\mu t^{2}}}{{3M}}\right)

(19)

for
t\in(0,1)
.

We also make use of the classical Bernstein’s inequality (cf. for example
[
4
]
), which we state here for completeness.

Lemma 3

(Bernstein’s Inequality)
.

Let
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{m}
be independent random variables such that for each
i\in[m]
, the following properties hold:

1.

The variables are centred, i.e.
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}]=0
.

2.

The variables are bounded almost surely, i.e.
\left|\xi_{i}\right|\leq M
.

3.

The variables have variance at most
\sigma^{2}
, i.e.
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{i}\right]^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}
.

Then it holds for any
0<\delta<1
that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^{2}\log\left(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}\right)}{m}}+\frac{2M\log\left(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}\right)}{3m}.

(20)

Appendix B
Proof of
Theorem

4

B.1
Choice of Estimator

In this section, we define the estimator we will use to prove our upper bounds. Recall that the median of
k
samples
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}
is defined to be
y=\Med\left(\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}\}\right)
such that
y\in\left\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}\right\}
satisfying

\displaystyle\left|\left\{j\in[k]|Y_{j}\leq y\right\}\right|\geq\frac{k}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\left\{j\in[k]|Y_{j}\geq y\right\}\right|\geq\frac{k}{2}.

(21)

We will use a median of means estimator to prove the upper bound. Let
k=\lceil 8\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\delta}})\rceil
and partition the
n
samples into
k
groups of size
m=\lfloor\nicefrac{{n}}{{k}}\rfloor
. Recall that we define our estimator such that for each group
j\in[k]
, we let

\displaystyle\widehat{Z}_{j}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda(X_{i}^{(j)})\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{Z}=\Med\left(\widehat{Z}_{1},\dots,\widehat{Z}_{k}\right).

(22)

The key fact about medians we will use is the following standard lemma, which allows us to convert constant probability bounds into high probability bounds using the median of means technique.
This lemma is typically used in the context of estimation with bounded variance, but we state it here in a more general form for completeness.

Lemma 4
.

Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}
be independent random variables such that for each
i\in[k]
, it holds that
\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{i}\in(a,b)\right)\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+c
for some
c>0
. Then it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\Med\left(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}\right)\in(a,b)\right)\geq 1-\exp\left(-2kc^{2}\right).

(23)

Proof.

Let
y=\Med\left(\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}\}\right)
and let
X_{i}=\mathbb{I}\left[Y_{i}\in(a,b)\right]
. Note that
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+c
for all
i\in[k]
and let
S=\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}
. Note that if
y\notin(a,b)
, then it must hold that
S\leq\nicefrac{{k}}{{2}}
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(y\not\in(a,b)\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(S\leq\nicefrac{{k}}{{2}}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{Bin}\left(\nicefrac{{k}}{{2}}+c,k\right)\leq\nicefrac{{k}}{{2}}\right)\leq e^{-2kc^{2}}

(24)

where the last inequality follows from a standard Chernoff bound (
Lemma

2
).
∎

B.2
Analysis of Sample Mean

Our upper bound proofs will proceed by showing that
\widehat{Z}_{j}
is
\varepsilon
-close to
Z
with constant probability for each
j\in[k]
, and then conclude by applying
Lemma

4
.
Further, since our estimator is scale invariant and the guarantees are multiplicative, we can analyze the case where
Z=1
without loss of generality.

The first key lemma we need involves controlling the variance of truncated versions of the density ratio
\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}
in terms of integrated coverage.

Lemma 5
.

For any two probability measures
\mu,\nu
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\right)^{2}\mathbb{I}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\leq M\right]\right]\leq\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)

(25)

Proof.

We use a standard tail integration argument. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\right)^{2}\mathbb{I}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\leq M\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\nu}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(Y)\mathbb{I}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(Y)\leq M\right]\right]

(26)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{Y\sim\nu}\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(Y)\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(Y)\leq M\right]\geq t\right)dt

(27)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{M}\mathbb{P}_{Y\sim\nu}\left(t\leq\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(Y)\leq M\right)dt

(28)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{M}\left(\mathrm{Cov}_{t}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)-\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\right)dt

(29)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{M}\mathrm{Cov}_{t}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)dt-M\cdot\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right).

(30)

The result follows.

∎

Next, we prove a concentration result for the empirical average of the density ratio in terms of integrated coverage.
The key idea is to note that even though the density ratio may have unbounded variance, we can analyze truncated versions of the density ratio to control the variance.

Lemma 6

(Sample Mean Concentration via Integrated Coverage)
.

For any two probability measures
\mu,\nu
, let
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
be i.i.d. samples from
\mu
.
Suppose
M_{\varepsilon,\delta}>0
is defined to satisfy

\displaystyle\mathrm{ICov}_{M_{\varepsilon,\delta}}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq\varepsilon\delta M_{\varepsilon,\delta},

(31)

with the convention that
M_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\infty
if no such finite value exists.
Then for any
0<\varepsilon,\delta<1
, it holds that as long as

\displaystyle m\geq\frac{M_{\varepsilon,\delta}\cdot\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon},

(32)

then with probability at least
1-\delta
it holds that

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})\leq 1+\varepsilon.

(33)

Proof.

Fix an
M
to be chosen later and let
\eta_{i}=\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})\leq M\right]
for
i\in[m]
.
It is immediate that
\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{i}\right]\leq 1
.
Further, from
Definition

1
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{i}\right]\geq 1-\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)

(34)

By
Lemma

5
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{i}^{2}\right]\leq\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)

(35)

and clearly
0\leq\eta_{i}\leq M
almost surely.
Thus by
Lemma

3
, it holds that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\eta_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{i}\right]\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\log\left(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}\right)}{m}}+\frac{2M\log\left(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}\right)}{3m}.

(36)

We now analyze the upper and lower tails separately.

Lower Tail.

Combining (
36
) and (
34
), we have that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\eta_{i}

(37)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)-\sqrt{\frac{2\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\log\left(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}\right)}{m}}-\frac{2M\log\left(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}\right)}{3m}.

(38)

Applying Young’s inequality, which says that for
a,b,\lambda\geq 0
it holds that
\sqrt{ab}\leq\nicefrac{{a}}{{\lambda}}+\lambda b
, we see that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\log\left(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}\right)}{m}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)}{M}+\frac{M\log\left(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}\right)}{2m}.

(39)

This gives that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)-\frac{\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)}{M}-\frac{2M\log\left(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}\right)}{3m}.

(40)

Setting,
M
such that
\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq\varepsilon/3
,
\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq\varepsilon M/3
and
m
such that
\frac{2M\log\left(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}\right)}{3m}\leq\varepsilon/3
, we have that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})

\displaystyle\geq 1-\varepsilon.

(41)

Upper Tail.

We separate the sample mean as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\eta_{i}+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})>M\right]

(42)

and analyze each term separately. By Markov’s inequality, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})>M\right]>t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})>M\right]\right]}{t}

(43)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Cov_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)}{t}.

(44)

Thus, as long as
\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{4}
it holds that with probability at least
1-\nicefrac{{\delta}}{{2}}
, that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})>M\right]\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(45)

We apply
Lemma

3
again to control the first term.
Indeed, it holds that with probability at least
1-\nicefrac{{\delta}}{{3}}
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)+\sqrt{\frac{2\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\log\left(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}\right)}{m}}+\frac{2M\log\left(\nicefrac{{2}}{{\delta}}\right)}{3m}

(46)

As before, applying Young’s inequality gives that with probability at least
1-\nicefrac{{\delta}}{{3}}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)+\frac{\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)}{M}+\frac{7M\log\left(\nicefrac{{3}}{{\delta}}\right)}{6m}.

(47)

Setting,
M
such that
\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
,
\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon M}{2}
and
m
such that
\frac{7M\log\left(\nicefrac{{3}}{{\delta}}\right)}{6m}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
, we have that with probability at least
1-\nicefrac{{\delta}}{{3}}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\varepsilon.

(48)

Combining the two parts of the upper tail and applying a union bound, we get the result.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

4
.

By the previous lemma, we have that for each
j\in[k]
, as long as

\displaystyle m\geq\frac{M_{\varepsilon,1/4}\cdot\log(4)}{\varepsilon/2},

(49)

it holds that with probability at least
3/4
,

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon\leq\widehat{Z}_{j}\leq 1+\varepsilon.

(50)

Applying
Lemma

4
with
c=1/4
and
k=\lceil 8\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\delta}})\rceil
gives that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon\leq\widehat{Z}\leq 1+\varepsilon

(51)

as required.
∎

Appendix C
Relationship between Coverage and
f
-Divergences and the Proof of Theorem
5

In this appendix we establish some relationships between coverage and
f
-divergences, which we then use to prove
Theorem

5
in
Section

C.2
. We begin with these relationships, culminating in a key relationship (
Lemma

9
) used in the proof of
Lemma

10
, which is in turn necessary to prove our upper bound.

C.1
Coverage and
f
-Divergences

We begin by stating a fundamental relationship between coverage and
f
-divergences, which will be useful in proving upper bounds on the sample complexity in terms of
f
-divergences.

Lemma 7
.

For any two probability measures
\mu,\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
and any convex function
f
as in
Definition

2
, it holds for all
M>1
that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq\frac{M\cdot\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)}{f(M)}.

(52)

Thus if
M\geq\gamma_{f}(\nicefrac{{\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)}}{{\varepsilon}})
for some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, then
\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq\varepsilon
.

Proof.

By definition of coverage, we have that

\displaystyle\Cov_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\nu}\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\geq M\right)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\geq M\right]\right]

(53)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1+\frac{M-1}{f(M)}\cdot f\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\right)\right)\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\geq M\right]\right]

(54)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\mu}\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\geq M\right)+\frac{M-1}{f(M)}\cdot\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right),

(55)

where the first inequality follows from
Lemma

8
with
x=\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)
for
X\sim\mu
and the second inequality follows from the definition of
f
-divergence in (
5
) and the fact that
f\geq 0
.
To conclude, we observe that for
M\geq 1
, it holds that
f
is monotone increasing and thus we may apply Markov’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\mu}\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\geq M\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\mu}\left(f\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\right)\geq f(M)\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[f\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\right)\right]}{f(M)}\leq\frac{\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)}{f(M)}.

(56)

Adding this to the previous bound yields the result.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a convex function such that
f(1)=f^{\prime}(1)=0
and let
M\geq 1
. Then it holds that for any
x\geq M
,

\displaystyle x\leq 1+\frac{M-1}{f(M)}\cdot f(x).

(57)

Proof.

Let
t=\frac{M-1}{x-1}
; by the assumption that
1<M\leq x
we have that
t\in[0,1)
. Observe that
M=t\cdot 1+(1-t)\cdot x
and so by convexity of
f
, it holds that

\displaystyle f(M)\leq t\cdot f(1)+(1-t)\cdot f(x)=(1-t)\cdot f(x)=\frac{M-1}{x-1}\cdot f(x).

(58)

The result follows by rearranging the above inequality.
∎

We now prove a key relationship between the second moment of the truncated density ratio and the
f
-divergence between the two measures.

Lemma 9
.

Let
f
be a convex function as in
Definition

2
such that there exists some
c>1
such that for all
M\geq t\geq c
it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{f(M)}{M^{2}}\leq\frac{f(t)}{t^{2}}.

(59)

Then for any probability measures
\mu,\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
and any
M\geq c
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)}{M}\leq\frac{c^{2}}{M}+\frac{M\cdot\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)}{f(M)}.

(60)

Proof.

Note first that if
\nicefrac{{f(M)}}{{M^{2}}}\leq\nicefrac{{f(t)}}{{t^{2}}}
then

\displaystyle t^{2}\leq\frac{M^{2}\cdot f(t)}{f(M)}.

(61)

We compute:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\leq M\right]\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\leq M\right]\right)^{2}\mathbb{I}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\leq c\right]\right]

(62)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\leq M\right]\right)^{2}\mathbb{I}\left[M\geq\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)>c\right]\right]

(63)

\displaystyle\leq c^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mu}\left[\frac{M^{2}\cdot f\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)\right)}{f(M)}\mathbb{I}\left[M\geq\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)>c\right]\right]

(64)

\displaystyle\leq c^{2}+\frac{2M^{2}\cdot\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)}{f(M)},

(65)

where the first inequality follows from (
61
) and the second inequality follows the nonnegativity of the integrand.
∎

Note that this lemma connnects the key quantity
\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)/M
used in our upper bounds to the
f
-divergence between the two measures. In particular, it shows that if we choose
M
sufficiently large so that
\nicefrac{{M\cdot\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)}}{{f(M)}}
is small, then
\nicefrac{{\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)}}{{M}}
is also small. We are now ready to prove our main upper bound in terms of
f
-divergences.

C.2
Proof of Theorem
5

As before, we will use the median-of-means estimator defined in (
15
) and thus it suffices to understand the behavior of the sample mean estimators in terms of
f
-divergences.

Lemma 10

(Sample Mean Concentration via
f
-divergence)
.

For any two probability measures
\mu,\nu
, let
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
be i.i.d. samples from
\mu
.
Let
f
be a convex function as in
Definition

2
such that there exists some
c>1
such that for all
M\geq t\geq c
it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{f(M)}{M^{2}}\leq\frac{f(t)}{t^{2}}.

(66)

Then for any
0<\varepsilon,\delta<1
, as long as

\displaystyle m\geq\frac{\gamma_{f}\left(\nicefrac{{\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)}}{{\varepsilon\delta}}\right)\cdot\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\vee\frac{c^{2}\cdot\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}\delta},

(67)

then with probability at least
1-\delta
it holds that

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X_{i})\leq 1+\varepsilon.

(68)

Proof.

The first step is the following lemma relating coverage to
f
-divergences, the proof of which we defer to
Appendix

C
.

To apply
Lemma

6
, we need to chose
M
such that the integrated coverage
\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)\leq\varepsilon\delta M
.
From
Lemma

9
, we have that we need
c^{2}/M+M\cdot\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)/f(M)\leq\varepsilon\delta
.
Recall from the definition of
\gamma_{f}
in (
6
), we have if we set
M=\gamma_{f}(\nicefrac{{\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)}}{{\varepsilon\delta}})
, then by definition of
\gamma_{f}
it holds that
\frac{M\cdot\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)}{f(M)}\leq\varepsilon\delta
.
Thus, we setting
M\geq\max\left\{c^{2}/\varepsilon\delta,\gamma_{f}\left(\frac{\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)}{\varepsilon\delta}\right)\right\}
and applying
Lemma

6
completes the proof.
∎

In this appendix, we provide complete proofs of our lower bound results. We first prove the most interesting case, the general lower bound in Theorem
8
(Section
D.1
) for superlinear but subquadratic
f
-divergences. We proceed with the lower bound in terms of integrated coverage (
Theorem

7
) in
Section

D.2
and then the linear lower bound in Proposition
1
(Section
D.3
); finally we prove the superquadratic lower bound in Proposition
2
(Section
D.4
).

D.1
Proof of Theorem
8

We first outline the construction and then prove the proposition. Let
\mu
be any measure and consider a family of distributions
\nu_{p,\varepsilon}
parameterized by
p\in[0,1]
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
defined such that

\displaystyle\frac{d\nu_{p,\varepsilon}}{d\mu}(X)=\begin{cases}1-\varepsilon&\text{w.p. }1-p,\\
1+\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{p}-1\right)&\text{w.p. }p.\end{cases}

(69)

We will let
\mathcal{V}\subset\left\{\nu_{p,\varepsilon}\right\}
such that for all
\nu\in\mathcal{V}
, it holds that
\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)\leq C+f(1-\varepsilon)
. To help with this objective, we first compute the
f
-divergence between
\nu_{p,\varepsilon}
and
\mu
.

Lemma 11
.

Suppose that

\displaystyle\gamma_{f}\left(\frac{C}{2\varepsilon}\right)\geq 2.

(70)

Then it holds that
\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu_{p,\varepsilon}\middle\|\mu\right)\leq C+f(1-\varepsilon)
whenever

\displaystyle p\geq\frac{2\cdot\varepsilon}{\gamma_{f}\left(\frac{C}{2\cdot\varepsilon}\right)}.

(71)

Proof.

By definition of the
f
-divergence and (
69
), we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)

\displaystyle=(1-p)\cdot f(1-\varepsilon)+p\cdot f\left(1+\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{p}-1\right)\right)

(72)

\displaystyle\leq f(1-\varepsilon)+p\cdot f\left(1-\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon}{p}\right).

(73)

Thus as long as

\displaystyle p\cdot f\left(1-\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon}{p}\right)\leq C,

(74)

the
f
-divergence condition will be satisfied.

We first claim that if (
74
) holds for
p
, then it holds for all
p<q\leq 1
. To see this, observe that for such
q
,

\displaystyle q\cdot f\left(1-\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon}{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(q(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\right)\cdot\frac{f\left(\frac{q(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon}{q}\right)}{\frac{q(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon}{q}}

(75)

\displaystyle\leq\left(p(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\right)\cdot\frac{f\left(\frac{p(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon}{p}\right)}{\frac{p(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon}{p}}

(76)

\displaystyle=p\cdot f\left(1-\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon}{p}\right)\leq C,

(77)

where the first inequality comes from the fact that
t\mapsto\nicefrac{{f(t)}}{{t}}
is non-decreasing for
t\geq 1
.

Now observe that the (
74
) holds if and only if

\displaystyle\frac{f\left(\frac{p(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon}{p}\right)}{\frac{p(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon}{p}}\leq\frac{C}{p(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon}.

(78)

By definition of the
\gamma_{f}
function in (
6
), this holds whenever

\displaystyle\frac{p(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon}{p}\leq\gamma_{f}\left(\frac{C}{p(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon}\right),

(79)

which, after rearranging, is satisfied whenever

\displaystyle p\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{\gamma_{f}\left(\frac{C}{p(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon}\right)-1}.

(80)

By the assumption that
\gamma_{f}\left(\frac{C}{2\varepsilon}\right)\geq 2
, this last is implied by
p\geq\frac{2\cdot\varepsilon}{\gamma_{f}\left(\frac{C}{p(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon}\right)}
. Note that for
p\leq\varepsilon
it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{2\cdot\varepsilon}{\gamma_{f}\left(\frac{C}{p(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon}\right)}\leq\frac{2\cdot\varepsilon}{\gamma_{f}\left(\frac{C}{2\cdot\varepsilon}\right)},

(81)

and thus the result follows.
∎

Thus for fixed
\varepsilon
, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}=\left\{\nu_{p,\varepsilon^{\prime}}\bigg|\frac{2\varepsilon^{\prime}}{\gamma_{f}\left(\frac{C}{2\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right)}\leq p\leq 1,\,0\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq\frac{1}{4}\right\}

(82)

We can now complete the proof of the proposition.

Proof of
Theorem

8
.

Let
\mu
be any probability measure and let
\mathcal{V}
be defined as in (
82
). By
Lemma

11
, it holds that for all
\nu\in\mathcal{V}
, we have that
\mathrm{D}_{f}\left(\nu\middle\|\mu\right)\leq C+f(1-\varepsilon)
.

Note that for any fixed
p
, in order to distinguish between distributions
\nu_{0}=\mu
and
\nu_{p,\varepsilon}
, with probability at least
\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}
, one must observe at least one sample from the high-density region of
\nu_{p,\varepsilon}
, which occurs with probability
p
under
\mu
. On the other hand, the probability of observing only low-density samples after
n
samples is

\displaystyle(1-p)^{n}\geq e^{-2np}.

(83)

Thus for
n\leq\nicefrac{{\log(\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}})}}{{2p}}
, no estimator can distinguish between
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{p,\varepsilon}
with probability at least
\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}
. On the other hand, the normalizing constants satisfy
Z=\lambda(X_{1})
in the former case and
Z=\nicefrac{{\lambda(X_{1})}}{{1-\varepsilon}}
in the latter case, and thus any estimator that achieves
(1-\varepsilon)Z\leq\widehat{Z}\leq(1+\varepsilon)Z
with probability at least
\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}
must be able to distinguish between these two distributions. The result follows by setting
p=\frac{2\cdot\varepsilon}{\gamma_{f}\left(\frac{C}{2\cdot\varepsilon}\right)}
.
∎

D.2
Proof of Theorem
7

We use the same construction as in the proof of
Theorem

8
. Indeed, let
\nu_{p,\varepsilon}
be defined as in (
69
). Observe that for any
\nu_{p,\varepsilon}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}_{M}\left(\nu_{p,\varepsilon}\|\mu\right)=\begin{cases}1&M<1-\varepsilon\\
p&1-\varepsilon\leq M<1+\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{p}-1\right)\\
0&M\geq 1+\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{p}-1\right)\end{cases}

(84)

Thus it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu_{p,\varepsilon}\|\mu\right)=\begin{cases}M&M<1-\varepsilon\\
(1-\varepsilon)+p\cdot(M-(1-\varepsilon))&1-\varepsilon\leq M<1+\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{p}-1\right)\\
1+\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{p}-1\right)&M\geq 1+\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{p}-1\right)\end{cases}.

(85)

By the identical argument as the proof of
Theorem

8
, in order to achieve
(1\pm\varepsilon)
multiplicative accuracy with probability at least
\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}
, one must be able to distinguish
\nu_{0}=\mu
from
\nu_{p,\varepsilon}
with probability at least
\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}
, which requires at least
\nicefrac{{\log(\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}})}}{{2p}}
samples. Letting
p=\nicefrac{{\varepsilon^{2}}}{{1+\varepsilon}}
, we claim that for
M=\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{p}}
, it holds that
\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu_{p,\varepsilon}\|\mu\right)\leq M\cdot\varepsilon
. Indeed, observe that for such
M
, we are clearly are in the intermediate regime above and thus

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{ICov}_{M}\left(\nu\|\mu\right)}{M}=\frac{(1-\varepsilon)(1-p)}{M}+p\leq\frac{p}{4\cdot\varepsilon}+p\leq\varepsilon.

(86)

On the other hand, we have established that at least
\nicefrac{{\log(\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}})}}{{2p}}=\Theta\left(\nicefrac{{M_{\varepsilon}}}{{\varepsilon}}\right)
samples are required to achieve the desired accuracy. The result follows.
\blacksquare

Let
\mathcal{X}=\left\{0,1\right\}

\displaystyle\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}(X)=\begin{cases}0&X=0\\

(90)

\nu
with respect to
\mu_{p}

p

\mu^{\prime}=\mu_{p^{\prime}}

(1\pm\varepsilon)

\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}

\mu^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\log\left(\frac{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-2\varepsilon}\right)+\log\left(\frac{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+2\varepsilon}\right)\right)

(95)

Lemma 12

Let

be probability measures on
\mathcal{X}
.
Then for any

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[Y\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[Y\geq 1-\varepsilon\right]\right]

\displaystyle=M\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(Y\geq 1-\varepsilon\right)+\Cov_{M}(\nu\|\mu).

We restate the result with constants included.

Theorem 11

\mu,\nu

\mathcal{X}

We use the classical

[

]

and
\nu

.

\mu,\nu
