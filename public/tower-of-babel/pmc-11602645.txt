Juno Observations Set New Constraints on the Electrodynamic Interaction Between Io and Jupiter
Authors: S. Kotsiaros, J. E. P. Connerney, J. Saur, A. Kokkalis, M. Herceg, Y. M. Martos, S. Schlegel, J. L. Jørgensen, S. J. Bolton
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractJuno's highly elliptical polar orbits provide unprecedented in‐situ observations of the electrodynamic interaction between Jupiter and its volcanic moon Io. These observations occur in regions never sampled before both near Io's orbit and near Jupiter's ionosphere and at distances between the two. Magnetic field data obtained during multiple traversals of magnetic field lines mapping to Io's orbit reveal remarkably rich and complex magnetic signatures near flux tubes connected to Io's orbital position. Here we present a methodology to model the distribution of currents along Io's flux tube (IFT) and Alfvén wings in such a way as to match the magnetic field signature observed during Juno's traversals of the IFT and Alfvén wings downstream of Io. We obtain the location, size and morphology of the current‐carrying region as well as the distribution of currents within the IFT and Alfvén wings. The observed field‐aligned currents exhibit strong filamentation, with upward and downward currents splitting into secondary cells rather than forming uniform structures. Additionally, there is a strong correlation between total field‐aligned current intensity, particle energy flux, and Poynting flux, indicating efficient energy transfer and coupling in the Jupiter‐Io system. Using all of Juno's traversals up to perijove (PJ) pass 42, we estimate the strength of the interaction with regards to distance along Io's extended tail, Io's position in the plasma torus and the magnetic field intensity at the footprint in Jupiter's ionosphere, illuminating the interaction of Jovian magnetospheric plasma with Io and setting important constraints in the Io‐Jupiter interaction.

Introduction

The discovery of the Io phase dependence of Jovian decametric radio emissions (Bigg, 1964) was the first evidence of an electrodynamic interaction between Io and Jupiter (Goldreich & Lynden‐Bell, 1969; Marshall & Libby, 1967; Piddington & Drake, 1968). The interaction causes the acceleration of particles from the dense Io plasma torus to Jupiter where they precipitate onto the planet's ionosphere producing bright and complex auroral features (Connerney et al., 1993; Connerney & Satoh, 2000; Mura et al., 2018). Electrical currents flow along magnetic field lines connecting Io with the Jovian ionosphere in both hemispherical directions (Goertz, 1980; Goldreich & Lynden‐Bell, 1969; Hess et al., 2010; Neubauer, 1980) Additionally, the transition of Alfvén waves into the inertial regime, particularly at high latitudes (Hess et al., 2010), can lead to particle acceleration, which further contributes to the dynamics of the system. The first direct observations of this field‐aligned current system were obtained by Voyager 1 on 5 March 1979. The Voyager measurements of both the magnetic field (Acuña et al., 1981) and the plasma (Belcher et al., 1981) perturbations together showed that disturbance was consistent with an Alfvén wave propagating away from Io. The spacecraft flew by Io's Alfvén wing within 20,500 km south of Io and measured a magnetic signature of ∼75 nT in amplitude attributed to a nearby current dipole with a total current intensity of 2.8 ±0.1 MA flowing along the Alfvén tube, providing an important constraint on the strength and Alfvén Mach number (0.15 ± 0.01) of the interaction.

In the historical context of Io‐Jupiter interaction models, two prominent frameworks have emerged: the unipolar induction model (Goldreich & Lynden‐Bell, 1969) and the Alfvén wing model (Neubauer, 1980). The unipolar induction model posits a single, direct, closed current loop between Jupiter's ionosphere and Io's surface. In contrast, the Alfven wing model, postulated post‐Voyager 1, suggests an open current configuration, wherein the current flowing into Io's ionosphere closes in the external plasma surrounding Jupiter rather than directly at Io. This distinction is pivotal in understanding the differing mechanisms underlying the interaction between Io and Jupiter's magnetosphere. Generally, the unipolar inductor is an extreme case of the non‐linear Alfvén wing theory (Saur et al., 2004). The most significant difference is that in the unipolar induction model, the currents close directly to the Jovian ionosphere, limited by the local Jovian ionospheric Pedersen conductivity, whereas in the Alfvén wing case, the currents close in the surrounding plasma subject to the Alfvén conductivity.

Almost 40 years after Voyager 1 observations, the Juno spacecraft provides the first recurring in‐situ observations of the Io‐Jupiter interaction during its multiple encounters of the IFT and Alfvén wings near Io's orbit as well as near Jupiter's ionosphere and at distances between the two. Juno's unique observations elucidate various aspects of the interaction and provide important information on the understanding of the physical processes that control the interaction. Electron and proton acceleration above the Io torus was linked to Io's Alfvénic interaction with Jupiter's magnetosphere (Damiano et al., 2019; Gershman et al., 2019; Hinton et al., 2019; Sulaiman et al., 2020; Szalay et al., 2019, 2020), whereas wave‐particle heating was also found to play an important role in the Io‐Jupiter interaction (Clark et al., 2020). Moreover, charged particle losses were observed when Juno traverses field lines that map to Io's orbit and were associated not only with absorption by Io but also with wave‐particle interactions (Paranicas et al., 2019).

The complex dynamics of the Io‐Jupiter interaction are manifested in the intricate structure of the Io‐induced aurorae on Jupiter. Juno's infrared investigation (Adriani et al., 2017) provided high resolution H3 + images of swirling patterns, multiple spots and bifurcations in Io's extended auroral tail suggesting electrodynamic processes occurring at different scales both close to Io and Jupiter (Moirano et al., 2021; Mura et al., 2018). The Hall effect in Io's ionosphere was found to be the most important mechanism to break the symmetry in Io's tail causing its alternating auroral emissions (Schlegel & Saur, 2022). Moreover, the strength of Io's extended tail emissions was found to decrease exponentially downstream of the interaction region along Io's orbit (Bonfond, et al., 2009, 2017; Clarke et al., 2002). Electron energy fluxes also diminish exponentially in the tail direction (Szalay et al., 2020). Sulaiman et al. (2023) found that the efficiency of the Io‐Jupiter interaction is consistent with acceleration from Alfvén wave filamentation (Chust et al., 2005) via a turbulent cascade process. Lysak et al. (2023) suggest that the current system of the interaction primarily consists of field‐aligned currents that are generated at the surface of Io and its ionosphere and secondary currents that are consistent with the reflection of Alfvén waves from the boundary of Io's plasma torus.

In this paper, we analyze all of Juno's in‐situ magnetic observations pertaining to the electrodynamic interaction between Io and Jupiter. We describe the main physical characteristics of the electrical coupling of Io with Jupiter's ionosphere. We develop comprehensive models to describe the strength and distribution of electrical currents within both the IFT near Io and the Alfvén wings downstream of Io. For simplicity, we will commonly refer to both the IFT and Alfvén wings collectively as Alfvén wings.

Lastly, we develop a generalized model that characterizes the total current in the Alfvén wings based on three physical parameters: the distance from Io along Io's extended tail, Io's position in the plasma torus, and the magnetic field intensity at the observation location's footprint in Jupiter's ionosphere. Our results provide important additional constraints in the Io‐Jupiter electrodynamic interaction filling in a missing piece in the understanding of the magnetospheric processes that influence the interaction.

Observations of the Io‐Jupiter Electrodynamic Interaction

The Juno spacecraft traverses magnetic field lines connected to Io multiple times during each PJ, depending on Juno's approach longitude and Io's phase, at least once in the northern and once in the southern hemisphere. When Io's position (Io phase) is within a relatively small longitudinal separation with respect to the Juno spacecraft trajectory, Juno measures in‐situ the magnetic signature of the Io‐Jupiter interaction. Figure 1 shows an example of one such traversal that occurred during PJ12 (1 April 2018). This passage produced the largest signature to date since Juno passed through the Main Alfvén Wing (MAW). Juno's trajectory is represented in magnetic coordinates (ρ,Z) using JRM33 (Connerney et al., 2021) model dipole coefficients. During part of the inbound leg of Juno's trajectory, the magnetometer onboard Juno measured a significant magnetic field perturbation of ∼1,350 nT attributed to passage through the MAW. The magnetic field line originating from Juno's trajectory at the time of observation was drawn using the JRM33 internal magnetic field model combined with the magnetodisc model (Connerney et al., 1981, 2020); it maps to Io's orbit in the magnetic dipole (ρ,Z) coordinate frame.

Magnetic field perturbations observed during Juno's crossing of Io's MAW in the inbound leg of its PJ12 trajectory (left). Juno's trajectory in magnetic dipole coordinates (using JRM33 model dipole coefficients) during PJ12 where Juno measured a significant magnetic field disturbance during its traversal of magnetic field lines mapping to Io's orbit (right).

Juno transited through Io's current‐carrying flux tube in the high‐latitude magnetic field region near Jupiter's ionosphere (at 1.34 R J radial distance, with R J = 71,492 km the Jupiter's radius), a region never sampled prior to Juno's orbit insertion. The magnetic field perturbations recorded during the Io MAW crossing were calculated by taking high‐resolution (64 Hz) magnetic field observations and subtracting estimates of Jupiter's internal field using JRM33 model as well as estimates of the magnetic field contribution from the magnetodisc (Connerney et al., 2020), simply referred to in what follows as the magnetodisc model. The residuals were detrended using robust spline smoothing which brings the magnetic perturbations to the zero baseline while preserving their relative amplitudes. Figure 1 shows Juno's high‐resolution observations of the magnetic field perturbations during the Io MAW crossing. The remarkable variability, turbulence and amplitude of the observed magnetic field signature is indicative of Juno passing through Io's MAW and the source region of intense current system during the encounter.

Model of the Field‐Aligned Current Distribution

Observations of magnetic field perturbations from PJ12, shown in Figure 1, were used to derive a model of field aligned currents emerging from the Io torus and flowing toward and away from the Jovian ionosphere along an Alfvén wing connecting Io's plasma torus with the Jovian ionosphere. The model search area for currents consists of a spherical grid with radius r=6 RIo, with Io's radius R Io =1821.6 km, at Io's instantaneous positions along its orbit around Jupiter. The choice of r=6 RIo has been found to provide convergent solutions that satisfy both current continuity and conservation constraints while accurately capturing the full current distribution within the Alfvén wing. Then, field lines are traced originating from the grid points to Jupiter's ionosphere using the JRM33 and magnetodisc models, as illustrated in Figure 2. To determine the Alfvén wing location relative to the spacecraft trajectory, and to derive the configuration and intensity of field‐aligned currents that best fit the observations, we solve Maxwell equations for various current configurations inside Io's flux tube and minimize the misfit between the observed and modeled vector magnetic field. This is a standard non‐linear optimization problem which we solve iteratively using the interior point algorithm (Byrd et al., 1999; Waltz et al., 2006). The objective function to be minimized is defined as: fmin=‖δBr−Bˆr2+δBθ−Bˆθ2+δBφ−Bˆφ2‖+λ·Itot, with δBr, δBθ, δBφ, the observed magnetic field perturbation and Bˆr, Bˆθ, Bˆφ, the estimated magnetic field due to the model configuration of field aligned currents. The term Itot represents the total current intensity flowing within the Alfvén wing and the total current penalizes the objective function with λ=10−2 A−1, the Lagrange multiplier constant. In other words, we are seeking for a current configuration with the smallest magnitude of the total current that best fits the observations. In addition, we implement a current conservation constraint that accepts only solutions that have a net field‐aligned current equal to zero. The net field‐aligned current is calculated as the sum of all individual current elements where the currents that flow parallel to the background magnetic field and toward Io are positive and the currents that flow antiparallel to the background field and away from Io are negative.

Schematic showing the model parametrization for the Io‐Jupiter interaction. Field lines threading the Io torus and map to Jupiter's ionosphere establishing the source region where field aligned currents flow.

Figure 3 shows the model field fitted to the PJ12 observations, subject to the constraints discussed previously. The resulting magnetic field (shown in green) due to the modeled current configuration is plotted atop Juno's observations of the vector magnetic field perturbations (shown in black). The model field fits the observations with a Root Mean Square (RMS) error of 22.07 nT in δBr, 49.73 nT in δBθ and 58.85 nT in δBφ. The estimated magnitude of the total field aligned current is 3.34 MA which is close to, but somewhat larger than, the 2.8 MA measured by Voyager 1 when the spacecraft passed near Io's flux tube in close proximity to Io (Acuña et al., 1981). It is not surprising that in passing through the MAW Juno revealed a slightly more intense and considerably more complicated current system, whereas the Voyager observations captured the less complicated signature of a distant current dipole. The turbulence in Juno's magnetic field perturbations is indicative of the spacecraft passing through the current source region as opposed to the Voyager observations that exhibited a smooth variation and simple appearance suggesting that the spacecraft passed near the main current carrying region and not through it (Acuña et al., 1981). Juno measured a perturbation signature of 1,350 nT in a background field of 2.93 G, which is a signal of ∼0.5% of the ambient field, whereas Voyager measured 75 nT in a background field of about 2,000 nT, a signal of 3.75% of the ambient field. Juno's in‐situ observations of the Alfvén wing are unique in that the Io‐Jupiter interaction was never sampled in the far‐field disturbance region, meaning the region that includes Jupiter and the inner Jovian magnetosphere that extends from Jupiter up to a few Io radii from Io (Saur et al., 2004).

Model results of the Io‐Jupiter interaction for PJ12. Magnetic field perturbations, shown in black, observed during Juno's crossing of Io's MAW in the inbound leg of its trajectory together with the magnetic field, shown in green, due to the field aligned current configuration determined by the model.

The model reveals currents largely distributed among two systems, one flowing toward Io on the Jupiter‐facing side and one flowing away from Io on the anti‐Jupiter‐facing side, as illustrated in Figure 4. This is as predicted by the unipolar inductor model of the interaction (Goldreich & Lynden‐Bell, 1969; Piddington & Drake, 1968), by the Alfvén wing models of (Goertz, 1980; Neubauer, 1980) and in accordance with our expectations of the large‐scale current system of the Io‐Jupiter interaction (Saur et al., 2004). The cross section of the Io flux tube shows the in situ current configuration as well as how Juno crossed the flux tube. It seems Juno penetrated the current‐carrying flux tube slightly off‐center. The flux tube is oblate at Juno's location due to Juno's proximity to Jupiter and its intense magnetic field at the time of observation, causing the magnetic field lines to compress. Specifically, 74% of the total field aligned current is contained within 2Io diameters and 88% within 2. 5 Io diameters which gives a scale size of the Io plasma torus where most of the ion acceleration occurs. Interestingly, the current distribution is not characterized by two uniformly symmetric systems, with exclusive regions of downward and upward currents. Instead, it exhibits a more dynamic distribution and apparent filamentation. Upward currents appear to be broader in spatial extent, while downward currents are narrower and more concentrated. Since these currents were measured at a considerable distance from Io and close to Jupiter, the variability of the individual current systems observed could partially result from reflections and interactions of Alfvén waves with the Jovian ionosphere. Moreover, the distribution of field‐aligned currents within the Alfvén wing is characterized by two distinctly separated regions of downward and upward currents. The separation of these current systems occurs primarily along the longitudinal direction, reminiscent of the observations of the split tail in Io's infrared footprint (Mura et al., 2018) and the dual structures of electron flux detected by JADE (Szalay et al., 2018).

Results

The Io‐Jupiter interaction is complicated due to different physical processes controlling the interaction. These processes are for example, plasma pickup (Cloutier & Daniell, 1973), current closure in Io's and Jupiter's conductive ionospheres as well as Alfvén wave propagation through a non‐uniform plasma. To separate the factors that describe the different processes, we analyzed Juno's traversals of magnetic field lines connected to Io and further downstream along Io's orbit and selected events for which Juno measured magnetic field perturbations associated with the Io‐Jupiter interaction (Table 1). For each event, the field‐aligned current intensity and distribution that best fit Juno's observations were modeled in a similar fashion to the PJ12 event presented in the previous section. In order to find which factors control the strength of the interaction, the estimated total current intensity within the Alfvén wing for each event is plotted on Figure 5 as a function of: distance down tail of Io, magnetic field strength at the footprint in Jupiter's ionosphere during the event and Io's location with respect to the centrifugal equator at that time. These factors are also summarized in Table 1.

Total field aligned current intensity as a function of (a) distance down tail, (b) magnetic field intensity at the spacecraft's footprint during the peak perturbation associated with the crossing of the Alfvén wing and (c) Io's centrifugal latitude at the time Io was closest to the magnetic field line Juno crossed while measuring the magnetic field perturbation due to the Alfvén wing. The red curves are fitting functions that outline the dependency of the interaction with the associated parameter in the x‐axis.

The distance down tail is defined as the length of the part of Io's orbit that starts from the main Alfvén wing at its origin, which corresponds to Io's position at the time of the measured magnetic field perturbation and ends at Juno's magnetically mapped footprint at Io's orbit. The magnetically mapped Juno's footprint downstream along Io's orbit does not exactly coincide with the down tail Alfvén wave since Alfvén waves do not travel entirely along magnetic field lines. The longitudinal separation, ΔλAlfvén, between Io and the Alfvén wave connected to Juno's magnetic footprint has been proven to be a key parameter in the organization of the energy fluxes in the plasma electrons and energetic ions (Clark et al., 2023; Sulaiman et al., 2023; Szalay et al., 2020). However, here we select the downtail distance parameter over the ΔλAlfvén parameter because the downtail distance provides a clearer indication of the geometrical extent of the field‐aligned currents along Io's tail. Calculating the downtail distance is more straightforward and less prone to model errors, as it relies solely on a magnetic field model to trace the magnetic field lines. In contrast, calculating ΔλAlfvén depends on both the magnetic field model and predictions of Alfvén wave trajectories, making it susceptible to uncertainties in the magnetic field model and varying plasma conditions such as density, temperature, and composition. Nevertheless, we find that using predictions of the down tail Alfvén wave trajectories (Hinton et al., 2019; Szalay et al., 2020) instead of Juno's footprint at Io's orbit does not provide a better correlation with the total current within the Alfvén wing. This is possibly due to the fact that the uncertainty in the predictions of the Alfvén wave trajectories due to varying plasma conditions such as density, temperature, composition etc. (Bonfond et al., 2017) is comparable to the uncertainty in the determination of Juno's footprint at Io's orbit with respect to the actual down tail Alfvén wave. Further analysis revealed that the total current associated with the Io‐Jupiter interaction exhibits a nearly identical relationship with both the downtail distance and ΔλAlfvén. Consequently, the downtail distance, as defined in this study, is equally valuable and interchangeable with ΔλAlfvén.

Moirano et al. (2023) found a considerable non‐System III variability of the plasma density and temperature possibly due to local time asymmetry of the Io torus or due to temporal variability related to the torus mass loading. Our results show that the total current associated with the Io‐Jupiter interaction along the Alfvén wing tail exhibits an exponential decay along the distance down tail, shown with the red fitted curve, with an e‐folding length of approximately 37,000 km which corresponds to 10° of longitude with upper and lower values of the 95% confidence interval 17° and 7° respectively. This range aligns with the e‐folding distance reported by Grodent et al. (2006), Bonfond et al. (2017), Szalay et al. (2020). Magnetic field perturbations extend to a distance of ∼9.5RJ downstream Io's location. In other words, field‐aligned current systems persist for almost 2 hr following the initial magnetic flux tube disturbance by Io. On a different scale, this is almost one quarter of the Jovian rotation behind Io's magnetic projection onto Jupiter. The distance down‐tail is the governing factor for the power in the Alfvén wing which is controlled by the strength of the Io‐Jupiter interaction (Saur et al., 2013). Specifically, the intensity of the current within the Alfvén wing Juno encounters in various distances along Io's tail, which henceforth we will call strength of the electrodynamic interaction or simply strength of the interaction, is exponentially decreasing from a value of 4.6 MA measured at ∼0.3RJ, to an average value of 0.16 MA after ∼1.5RJ and 0.08 MA after ∼2.5RJ. The damping of the total current intensity until ∼2.5RJ is consistent with Delamere et al. (2003) who showed that the Alfvénic disturbance due to the decreasing density gradient is reflected between ∼1.25RJ and ∼2.5RJ and that most of the Iogenic plasma momentum is contained within one to two torus scale heights, with scale height ho=1.25RJ (Delamere et al., 2003), after which a steady state decoupling of the mass loaded magnetic flux tube from Jupiter takes place resulting in significant weakening of the interaction. However, recent findings by Bonfond et al. (2017) provide evidence that challenges the steady‐state theory, suggesting that the footprint tail is influenced by dynamic Alfvénic processes rather than quasi‐static processes.

The electrical coupling between Io and Jupiter ought to be more intense when field lines originating from Io connect to regions of the Jovian ionosphere with locally higher conductivity in the Pedersen layer if we do not consider the reflection of Io's Alfvén wings at Jupiter's ionospheric plasma density gradient. Therefore, higher Pedersen conductivity is expected to lead to a larger flux of electrons into the ionosphere and therefore more intense field‐aligned currents. Gérard et al. (2021) produced maps of the spatial distribution of the Pedersen conductance in Jupiter's polar regions. However, these maps only partially extend to Io's footprint latitudes, and reliable data on conductance at these latitudes remains incomplete. Therefore, indirect information on the Pedersen conductivity is drawn from the magnetic field intensity at the footprint locations. A more intense magnetic field at the footprint location presents a higher gyro frequency and results in a lower collision‐to‐gyro‐frequency ratio and therefore lower Pedersen conductivity and vice versa. Given the lack of detailed knowledge about the electrical conductivity at Io's footprint latitudes, the magnetic field intensity at the footprint is used as a proxy. It is important to note that several other parameters, including the Alfvén conductance, may also vary inversely with the field strength. Consequently, the magnetic field intensity at the footprint could represent these factors as well.

We show in Figure 5b that the magnetic field intensity at the footprint of the current‐carrying Alfvén wing on the Jovian ionosphere moderates, although indirectly, the strength of the Io‐Jupiter interaction. The strength of the interaction decreases for increasing magnetic field strength at the footprint as one would anticipate by virtue of Pedersen conductivity. However, the deviation in the strength of the interaction from the fitting curve (marked in red) is larger than the deviation from the fit with respect to the distance down‐tail. The larger deviation is a manifestation of the indirect relationship of the strength of the interaction with the magnetic field strength at the footprint as well as the lower efficiency of the magnetic field strength at the footprint in controlling the Io‐Jupiter interaction compared to the distance down tail. The latter is demonstrated well by the PJ41 outlier where one would expect significantly weaker total current due to the strong magnetic field at the footprint. However, during PJ41, Juno crossed the current‐carrying flux tube near Io, resulting in small down tail distance. The small downtail distance is a significant factor in the efficiency of the interaction and therefore, strong field‐aligned currents are maintained.

The cold Io plasma torus, as well as the main (warm plasma) torus, deviates from Jupiter's equatorial plane and aligns with the centrifugal equator at about 2/3 of the way toward the magnetic equator (Phipps & Bagenal, 2021), which is offset by 10° from the rotation axis. As Io orbits around Jupiter, it exhibits variations in the surrounding plasma density as a function of the moon's vertical position with respect to the centrifugal equator. This variation affects the current system induced by Io's motion in the Jovian magnetosphere (Hinson et al., 1998). The strength of Io‐Jupiter interaction as function of Io's centrifugal latitude is shown on Figure 5c. The centrifugal equator is calculated based on the empirical model by Phipps and Bagenal (2021) with Io's System III longitude taken at the time of the initial disturbance of the Alfvén wing, meaning when Io was closest to the magnetic field line associated with Juno's measurement of the magnetic field perturbation.

The total current is generally highest when Io is positioned close to the center of the centrifugal equator, where the plasma density is highest (Bagenal, 1994), and decreases following a quadratic function, shown in red, for increasing distance from the centrifugal equator. Here, there are two clear outliers, PJ12 and PJ41. For those events, one would expect lower total current if the strength of the interaction was only a function of Io's centrifugal latitude. However, PJ12 and PJ41 Alfvén wing crossings occurred at very small distances down the tail and Juno seemingly crossed the main Alfvén wing, therefore a high total current is maintained regardless of Io's centrifugal latitude. On the contrary, PJ7 appears to have lower total current than expected given Io's centrifugal latitude. However, the PJ7 crossing happened at a very long distance down tail where the strength of the Io‐Jupiter interaction is diminishing. An interesting case is PJ29 where given Io's centrifugal latitude and its distance down tail, one would expect higher total current. However, the PJ29 crossing maps to a footprint with very high magnetic field strength at the Jovian ionosphere meaning the Pedersen conductivity and Alfvén conductance is expected to be low and therefore the field‐aligned currents are smaller.

Figure 5 demonstrates the dependency at various scales and with various significance of the strength of the Io‐Jupiter interaction on all three variables, the down tail distance d, the magnetic field intensity BFP at the footprint on the Jovian ionosphere, and Io's centrifugal latitude θc. Therefore, to describe the interaction with a generalized model, it is important to take into account all three variables. We assume a relationship of the total field‐aligned current intensity ITot=fd,BFP,θc with the distance down tail d in RJ, the magnetic field BFP at the footprint in Gauss, and Io's centrifugal latitude θc in radians in the form of fd,BFP,θc=Αeβ·d·BFPγ·|θc|δ and we determine the model fit parameters A,β,γ,δ, using nonlinear least‐squares regression where fd,BFP,θc is the regression function. The values of the estimated parameters are: A=31.23, β=−0.60, γ=−2.22, δ=−0.65.

In summary, the parameters A,β,γ, and δ collectively describe the physical behavior of the Io‐Jupiter interaction model, reflecting the dependencies and sensitivities of the field‐aligned current intensity to changes in the down tail distance, magnetic field intensity at the ionospheric footprint, and Io's centrifugal latitude.

Given that the model fd,BFP,θc is an empirical model, the estimated parameter A serves as a scale factor and lacks a direct physical interpretation. Its units are MAGaussγ·radδ to ensure that fd,BFP,θc yields units in MA. The field‐aligned current intensity decreases exponentially as the distance down tail increases. Specifically, the field‐aligned current intensity diminishes by approximately 60% per RJ increment in the downtail distance. Second, the total intensity of the field‐aligned current exhibits an inverse relationship, approximately square, with the magnetic field intensity observed at the flux tube's ionospheric footprint. Lastly, the field‐aligned current intensity decreases with increasing offset from the zero centrifugal latitude, reflecting the inverse relationship between the field‐aligned current and the decrease in plasma density as we move away from the centrifugal equator.

The generalized model fd,BFP,θc, shown in Figure 6, fits the total field‐aligned current estimated for each Alfvén wing crossing relatively well. Specifically, the mean square error of the model misfit is 0.04 MA, whereas as shown from the quantile‐quantile plot, the model residuals are, to first approximation, normally distributed. To quantitatively determine the relative efficiency of each physical variable d,BFP, and θc in controlling the interaction we modify the regression function such as fd,BFP,θc=Adeβ·d·ABBFPγ·Aθ|θc|δ, and we estimate the parameters, Ad, AB, Aθ, by performing a regression analysis where the physical variables d,BFP, and θc have been standardized so that their variances are equal to one. Therefore, the relative magnitudes of the estimated parameters Ad, AB, Aθ, represent absolute weights that are insensitive to the different scales of their corresponding physical parameters d,BFP, and θc, and show the importance of each physical parameter in the total model. We find: Ad=0.36, AB=0.34, and Aθ=0.29. Specifically, we find that the distance down tail is the most influential parameter in regulating the electrical coupling of Io with Jupiter's ionosphere, contributing 36% to the total variation of the field‐aligned current intensity. The magnetic field strength at the footprint comes next, contributing 34%, while Io's centrifugal latitude has the least impact at 29%. That order can be read, in large part, from inspection of Figures 5a and 5ure 5c directly.

Our results of the total field‐aligned current intensity can be directly compared with the particle energy flux and inferred Poynting flux presented by Szalay et al. (2020) and Sulaiman et al. (2023). Figure 7 demonstrates a strong correlation between total field‐aligned current intensity and both energy flux and Poynting flux, with correlation coefficients of r = 0.89 and r = 0.91, respectively. This strong correlation between total field‐aligned current intensity, particle energy flux, and Poynting flux in the Jupiter‐Io system underscores the interconnected nature of energy transfer mechanisms within the Jovian magnetosphere. Specifically, it indicates that the energy transferred through electromagnetic fields and carried by particles effectively drives current systems in the magnetosphere, suggesting efficient coupling between energy transfer mechanisms and current generation. Moreover, it highlights the feedback mechanisms where currents can modify electromagnetic fields, influencing further energy transfer and particle dynamics. These insights reveal a highly dynamic and interdependent system, enhancing our understanding of energy distribution and conversion within Jupiter's magnetosphere.

Mosaics displaying the current distribution across all cross sections of the modeled events are presented in Figure 8, arranged by increasing downtail distance from top left to bottom right. These mosaics reveal that the current distribution within the Alfvén wing is neither strictly symmetric nor uniform. The field‐aligned currents exhibit strong filamentation, akin to the twisting and mixing of currents due to reflections of the Alfvén wings between the Io torus and the Jovian ionosphere, as demonstrated by Lysak et al. (2023), and the twisting discussed by Schlegel and Saur (2022).

Mosaics of the current distribution across all cross sections of the modeled encounters of Juno with Io's Alfvén wings are arranged by increasing downtail distance from top left to bottom right. These mosaics have been computed by applying linear interpolation on the grid of current elements projected onto the cross‐sectional surface at the observation location.

Specifically, the observed strong filamentation suggests that neglecting electron inertia in the usual magnetohydrodynamic approximation may be invalid, as the spatial scales of the current filaments can approach those of electron inertia. Additionally, the downward and upward currents are not organized into uniform cells; instead, they split into secondary cells. By calculating the Euclidean distance between the splits in the upward currents, typically associated with auroral emissions, and propagating this to the Jovian ionospheric height, we find a mean split distance of approximately 257 km with a median of 224 km. These estimates align with the average Io‐tail split distance of 224 km based on JIRAM observations, as reported by Moirano et al. (2021).
