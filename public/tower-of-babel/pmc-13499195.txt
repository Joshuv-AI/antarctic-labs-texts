High-risk pulmonary embolism in the first trimester of pregnancy: Successful thrombolysis with tenecteplase
Authors: llyass Laaribi, Mohamed Zakaria Bouayed, Iliass Benaini, Ikram Zaid, Houssam Bkiyar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulmonary embolism is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication during pregnancy, and its diagnosis remains challenging because of nonspecific clinical manifestations that may overlap with other pregnancy-related conditions. We report the case of a 38-year-old pregnant woman who presented with bilateral pulmonary embolism associated with hemodynamic and respiratory instability. Computed tomography pulmonary angiography was performed to confirm the diagnosis despite concerns regarding fetal radiation exposure, and the patient subsequently received thrombolytic therapy with a favorable short-term outcome. This case highlights the critical role of imaging in the diagnosis of high-risk pulmonary embolism during pregnancy and emphasizes the importance of timely multidisciplinary management. Early recognition and appropriate treatment are essential to improve maternal and fetal outcomes in this potentially fatal condition.

Introduction

Pulmonary embolism is a serious medical condition that can occur during pregnancy, posing a significant risk to both the mother and the developing fetus. Thromboembolic complications rank third among the leading causes of maternal death in developed countries [1,2]. Although relatively rare, pulmonary embolism is potentially fatal, underscoring the need for timely diagnosis and appropriate management.

In this report, we present the case of a 38-year-old patient in the first trimester of her seventh pregnancy who was admitted to our department for the management of pulmonary embolism.

Case presentation

A 38-year-old woman, gravida 7 para 5, presented to the emergency department with sudden-onset acute respiratory distress. A viable intrauterine pregnancy at 11 weeks of gestation was confirmed by last menstrual period dating. The patient had no prior history of venous thromboembolism, thrombophilia, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, autoimmune disorder, or cardiac disease. Her surgical history was limited to a laparoscopic cholecystectomy performed 9 years earlier. She was on no chronic medications and had no history of tobacco use. Family history was unremarkable for thromboembolic disease, hereditary thrombophilia, or premature cardiovascular disease.

On initial evaluation, she was conscious with a body mass index of 37 kg/m². She was hemodynamically unstable, with a blood pressure of 81/46 mmHg and a heart rate of 130 beats per minute. Respiratory instability was also noted, with a respiratory rate of 30 breaths per minute and oxygen saturation of 78% on room air, improving to 93% with 12 L/min of supplemental oxygen. No clinical signs of deep vein thrombosis were observed.

Laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis of 16,090/mm3 (reference range: 4000-10,000/mm3) with neutrophil predominance, hemoglobin of 13.5 g/dL (reference range: 12-16 g/dL), platelet count of 144,000/mm3 (reference range: 150,000-400,000/mm3), elevated D-dimer levels of 2235 ng/mL (reference value <500 ng/mL), and markedly elevated high-sensitivity troponin of 2587.8 U/L (reference value <14 U/L).

Arterial blood gas analysis revealed a pH of 7.31, PaO2 of 66 mmHg under 12 L/min supplemental oxygen, PaCO2 of 21.1 mmHg, bicarbonate of 13.6 mmol/L, and lactate of 3.5 mmol/L.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated acute cor pulmonale with right ventricular dilation and dysfunction (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion 9 mm, S wave 0.08 m/s), a positive McConnell sign, and pulmonary hypertension estimated at 50 mmHg. No thrombus was visualized, and the cardiac index was estimated at 1.5 L/min/m2 (Fig. 1). Lower limb venous compression ultrasonography was normal. Initial management included oxygen therapy, central venous catheter placement, and invasive blood pressure monitoring via radial artery cannulation. Thoracic CT pulmonary angiography revealed bilateral proximal pulmonary embolism with intraluminal filling defects involving the proximal pulmonary arterial circulation (Fig. 2). The examination also showed features suggestive of right ventricular pressure overload, consistent with the transthoracic echocardiographic findings of acute right ventricular dysfunction. Following multidisciplinary discussion and with informed patient consent, systemic thrombolysis was initiated. The patient received Tenecteplase 50 mg intravenously over 5 minutes. Within an hour, her vital signs stabilized with a blood pressure of 113/50 mmHg and heart rate of 102 beats per minute, allowing rapid oxygen weaning. Repeat arterial blood gas analysis showed pH 7.46, PaO2 178 mmHg under 4 L/min oxygen, PaCO2 34.4 mmHg, bicarbonate 19.7 mmol/L, and lactate 2.15 mmol/L.

The patient was subsequently transferred to the cardiology department, where she was maintained on curative anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin 6000 IU every 12 hours. She remained hemodynamically stable and achieved complete oxygen weaning. Follow-up TTE demonstrated regression of right ventricular dilatation with preserved systolic function and no residual pulmonary hypertension. Obstetric ultrasound follow-up was normal.

The patient was discharged after 1 week. At follow-up, she showed no evidence of hemorrhagic or fetal complications.

The subsequent course of the pregnancy was clinically uneventful, with structured multidisciplinary follow-up involving cardiology and obstetrics. Therapeutic anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin was continued throughout pregnancy. The patient achieved term gestation and underwent spontaneous vaginal delivery at 39 weeks of amenorrhea. A healthy male neonate weighing 3600 g was born with Apgar scores of 10 and 10 at 1 and 5 minutes, respectively, and experienced no adverse perinatal outcomes. No maternal hemorrhagic complications, recurrent thromboembolic events, fetal growth restriction, or congenital abnormalities were observed during follow-up.

Discussion

Thromboembolic disease during pregnancy results from physiological changes unique to this period. Several factors contribute to the increased risk, including hormonal changes, a hypercoagulable state due to elevated coagulation factors and decreased endogenous anticoagulant activity, reduced fibrinolysis, and decreased mobility associated with the enlarging gravid uterus. Endothelial injury caused by venous dilatation further compounds the risk. Pre-existing conditions such as obesity, hypertension, and thrombophilia also increase the likelihood of pulmonary embolism [2,3].

These elements collectively form Virchow’s triad, which is present in most cases of PE. The thrombus generally originates from a peripheral vein, most commonly in the left leg, which is subject to greater compression by the gravid uterus. Obstruction of the pulmonary arteries leads to pulmonary arterial hypertension, increased right ventricular afterload, dilation, and dysfunction. This in turn compresses the left ventricle, reduces stroke volume and cardiac output, and manifests clinically as hemodynamic instability. Gas exchange is impaired by a shunt effect, leading to hypoxemia. Without prompt and adequate treatment, PE can be fatal [4].

The incidence of PE during pregnancy and the postpartum period is estimated at 0.1-1 per 1000 deliveries [5]. Although less common in the first trimester compared to later stages, it remains a potentially fatal event that requires rapid recognition and management.

Diagnosis in pregnancy is particularly challenging owing to nonspecific symptoms such as dyspnea, tachycardia, and lower limb edema, which may also be explained by physiological changes of gestation. Moreover, clinical probability scores validated in the general population are not applicable to pregnant women, making paraclinical investigations essential [6].

d-Dimer levels are physiologically elevated during pregnancy, limiting their positive predictive value; however, they may retain value as a negative predictive marker [7]. Additionally, troponin and BNP can serve as markers of disease severity [8,9].

Imaging techniques such as CT pulmonary angiography and ventilation–perfusion scans remain the diagnostic standards, but their use must weigh maternal benefit against fetal radiation exposure. In the present case, lower-limb compression ultrasonography was negative despite a strong clinical suspicion of pulmonary embolism and evidence of acute right ventricular dysfunction on echocardiography. Because systemic thrombolysis was being considered in a hemodynamically unstable patient, definitive imaging confirmation was required. Although fetal radiation exposure is a legitimate concern, particularly during the first trimester, the maternal benefits of rapid and accurate diagnosis outweighed the potential fetal risks. This approach is consistent with current recommendations emphasizing that potentially life-saving diagnostic investigations should not be delayed when high-risk pulmonary embolism is suspected. While less invasive investigations are preferred, delays in diagnosis increase the risk of maternal and fetal mortality. In suspected cases, traditional diagnostic algorithms often begin with Doppler ultrasound of the lower limbs when DVT signs are present. A positive result justifies initiating treatment without further imaging [6], whereas inconclusive results require CT or V/Q scanning [10].

Thrombolysis in pregnancy, particularly during the first trimester, remains controversial because of bleeding and fetal safety concerns [11]. The decision must be individualized, balancing maternal risk if untreated against potential harm to the fetus, and considering the availability of alternatives [12,13]. Published data remain limited to case reports and small case series. Current guidelines (e.g., ACCP) recommend considering thrombolysis in pregnant women with massive PE, intermediate-high-risk PE with right heart strain, or persistent hypotension despite adequate resuscitation, after careful risk–benefit evaluation [14]. Alteplase is the recommended agent, typically 100 mg over 2 hours, with the option to stop early in case of rapid improvement to reduce bleeding risk [12].

Other treatment options include catheter-directed thrombolysis or thrombectomy, which should only be performed in specialized centers, and surgical embolectomy, generally reserved for cases in which thrombolysis is contraindicated or unsuccessful. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) may be considered in severe cardiac or respiratory failure, either as a bridge to reperfusion or when thrombolysis is contraindicated, though evidence is limited [13,15].

Ongoing management relies on anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) to prevent clot propagation, though its use in the first trimester requires close maternal and fetal monitoring [13].

The use of tenecteplase in pregnancy remains off-label, and available evidence is limited to isolated case reports and small case series [12,16]. In contrast, alteplase is the thrombolytic agent most frequently reported in the literature and is generally recommended by current guidelines because of greater accumulated clinical experience [12,14]. Nevertheless, tenecteplase offers several practical advantages over alteplase, including single-bolus administration, greater fibrin specificity, and simplified administration in emergency settings [17]. In our patient, a weight-adjusted dose of tenecteplase (50 mg) was administered because alteplase was not available at our institution. The favorable maternal and fetal outcomes observed in this case contribute additional evidence supporting the potential use of tenecteplase in selected life-threatening situations, although larger studies are required to better define its safety and efficacy during pregnancy.

Our case illustrates the complexity of managing high-risk pulmonary embolism during early pregnancy. The diagnostic process required balancing maternal hemodynamic instability against concerns regarding fetal radiation exposure, while the therapeutic strategy involved weighing the life-saving benefits of thrombolysis against potential maternal and fetal risks. This case is particularly noteworthy because the pulmonary embolism occurred during the first trimester and was successfully treated with tenecteplase, whereas most published reports and current guidelines primarily recommend alteplase. Although evidence remains limited to case reports and small case series, available data support reperfusion therapy in life-threatening pulmonary embolism during pregnancy when maternal survival is threatened. In our patient, rapid hemodynamic recovery, complete resolution of right ventricular dysfunction, continuation of pregnancy to term, and the absence of maternal or fetal complications highlight the potential effectiveness of this approach. This experience underscores the importance of prompt diagnosis, multidisciplinary decision-making, and timely reperfusion therapy. The principal lesson from this case is that close collaboration among emergency physicians, intensivists, cardiologists, radiologists, and obstetricians is essential for optimizing outcomes. Early diagnosis, timely reperfusion therapy, and coordinated follow-up allowed both maternal recovery and successful continuation of pregnancy, demonstrating that favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes can be achieved even in highly complex clinical situations.

Conclusion

Pulmonary embolism during the first trimester of pregnancy is a rare but potentially life-threatening condition. Diagnosis remains challenging, and optimal management requires a multidisciplinary approach. Formal therapeutic guidelines specific to pregnancy remain limited. In selected cases of high-risk pulmonary embolism associated with hemodynamic instability, systemic thrombolysis may represent a life-saving therapeutic option when the maternal benefits outweigh the potential maternal and fetal risks. Early diagnosis and timely management are essential to optimize outcomes for both mother and fetus.
