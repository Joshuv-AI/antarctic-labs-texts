Zero Events, Limited Precision, and the Interpretation of Olanzapine Safety in Patients with Diabetes Receiving Cisplatin [Response to Letter]
Authors: Toshinobu Hayashi, Chihiro Shiraishi, Masakazu Abe, Susumu Kaneshige, Takashi Egawa
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We sincerely thank Dr. Shen and Dr. Wang for their thoughtful interest in our recent publication, “Safety of Short-Term Low-Dose Olanzapine in Patients with Cancer and Diabetes Receiving Cisplatin-Based Chemotherapy: A Retrospective TriNetX Cohort Study,” and for their constructive comments on the interpretation of real-world data and zero-event outcomes.

We agree with the reviewer that the absence of observed or zero events does not imply the absence of risk, nor does it establish non-inferiority or absolute safety. We acknowledge that some phrases in our Discussion—such as stating that olanzapine “does not substantially exacerbate preexisting DM” or that glycemic fluctuations are “modest and manageable”—may have been somewhat overstated given the inherent limitations of a retrospective cohort study. We agree with their suggested wording that our results are best interpreted as indicating that “no severe glycemic safety signal was detected” in this cohort.

As noted in our original article, the absence of events in a relatively small cohort does not exclude clinically meaningful differences in the true incidence rates between groups. In matched cohorts with zero events, the upper limits of the exact confidence intervals were approximately 4%, indicating that clinically meaningful event probabilities remained compatible with the data. Moreover, because no events occurred in either group, these data did not permit meaningful estimation of the relative risk or establish that any excess risk associated with olanzapine was below a clinically acceptable threshold. Therefore, larger prospective studies with pre-specified and clinically justified safety or non-inferiority margins are required to evaluate these rare outcomes with adequate precision. We appreciate the opportunity to further clarify the practical implications of this limitation.

We agree that the definition of exposure warrants careful consideration. The exposure window was chosen to reflect real-world prescription patterns, in which olanzapine may be initiated shortly before, on, or after the day of cisplatin administration. Nevertheless, defining exposure to include olanzapine initiation through Day +7 did not fully align the exposure classification with the start of follow-up. Classifying patients who initiated olanzapine treatment after the cisplatin index date as exposed from baseline may have introduced exposure-time misclassification and possible immortal time bias. Given the available data, we could not determine the direction or magnitude of the potential bias. The available analytical framework in TriNetX did not permit us to model olanzapine use as time-varying exposure; consequently, this concern could not be fully addressed in the present study.

The exposure window may have included both prophylactic and rescue administrations. Because the database did not allow these indications to be reliably distinguished, we were unable to determine whether olanzapine use was associated with differences in the severity of nausea and vomiting, oral intake, dehydration, electrolyte abnormalities, need for additional treatment, or healthcare utilization. Therefore, we cannot exclude the bias arising from the combination of these conditions. Our prespecified Day −1 to Day +1 sensitivity analysis narrowed the exposure window; however, as the correspondents correctly note, it still allowed post-index initiation and included only 20 patients per group. Accordingly, this analysis does not eliminate this concern. Future studies should verify the timing and indications for the first olanzapine dose, align exposure classification with the start of follow-up, distinguish prophylactic from rescue use, and, where feasible, model exposure as time-varying.

We agree that residual confounding and channeling biases remain important concerns. Although the covariates were selected a priori based on clinical judgment and available evidence, propensity score matching did not account for factors that were unavailable or incompletely captured in the electronic health records. Clinicians may also preferentially select patients who are perceived to be metabolically stable or suitable for close monitoring. Such a channeling bias may persist after matching and may have influenced our findings.

Dexamethasone exposure is another potential source of residual confounding. Although glucocorticoid use was included as a covariate, detailed information regarding dexamethasone dose, duration, timing, and dose modification was unavailable. As corticosteroid exposure is an important determinant of chemotherapy-associated hyperglycemia, adjusting for glucocorticoid use as a binary variable may not adequately account for the relevant differences in exposure. Therefore, we could not determine the extent or direction of the residual confounding attributable to dexamethasone.

We agree that analyses based on mean glucose concentrations may obscure clinically important peaks and within-patient variability. The available data did not allow us to comprehensively evaluate peak glucose concentrations, changes from baseline, prespecified hyperglycemic thresholds, or the initiation or intensification of glucose-lowering treatment. These outcomes, together with verified olanzapine and dexamethasone exposure, should be systematically assessed in future studies.

Finally, we appreciate the correspondents’ concern that the plain language recommendation may extend beyond what can be substantiated by a retrospective zero-event analysis. We agree that our findings alone do not establish comparative safety, non-inferiority, or absence of metabolic risk. However, our argument was not based solely on the absence of severe glycemic events in the present cohort. This also reflects the substantial clinical burden of CINV, the established antiemetic efficacy of olanzapine, and the broader international regulatory and clinical context in which diabetes mellitus is not generally regarded as an absolute contraindication to olanzapine, although careful metabolic assessment and monitoring are recommended. Against this background, our intention was neither to advocate for routine or indiscriminate use, nor to suggest that the present study resolves the question of the appropriateness of the current Japanese contraindication. Rather, we sought to emphasize that olanzapine should not necessarily be excluded as a treatment option for all patients with diabetes mellitus and that in carefully selected patients, its use may be considered based on an individualized benefit–risk assessment with appropriate glucose monitoring. We acknowledge that the plain language recommendation could have clearly distinguished this clinical proposition from the more limited inferences directly supported by our observational findings.

In conclusion, we greatly appreciate the insightful critique provided by Drs. Shen and Wang, which reinforces the caveats necessary to interpret real-world evidence. As stated in our original article, our findings should be regarded as hypothesis generating rather than definitive. We hope that our preliminary findings, together with the corresponding methodological insights, will stimulate adequately powered prospective studies aimed at optimizing the safety of CINV management in patients with diabetes.
