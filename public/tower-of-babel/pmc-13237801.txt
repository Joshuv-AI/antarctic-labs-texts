Loss of ribosomal protein RPL22 restricts African swine fever virus replication by inducing PERK-dependent ER stress
Authors: Pengfei Li, Zheyu Liao, Hua Cao, Guiqian Wang, Xinglin He, Xiaohang Li, Yumei Sun, Mengjia Zhang, Ahmed H Ghonaim, Peng Gao, Anan Jongkaewwattana, Yongtao Li, Guiqing Peng, Qigai He, Wentao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAfrican swine fever virus (ASFV) is a large DNA virus that causes highly lethal disease in domestic pigs and wild boars, posing a significant threat to the global swine industry. The development of effective antivirals and vaccines is hindered by the complex structure of the virus and its sophisticated manipulation of host cellular processes. The inner envelope protein p54 is essential for multiple stages of the viral life cycle, including attachment, entry, and replication. In this study, we employed TurboID-based proximity labeling to systematically map the host protein interactome of ASFV p54. Our screen identified 257 potential host interacting partners. Among these, the interaction between p54 and the 60S ribosomal protein L22 (RPL22) was validated by co-immunoprecipitation and confocal microscopy. Functional studies using an RPL22-knockout (RPL22-KO) cell line revealed that RPL22 serves as a regulatory host factor for ASFV infection. Mechanistically, the loss of RPL22 significantly inhibited ASFV replication through the activation of the PERK signaling pathway. This finding was further substantiated by pharmacological intervention; activation of the ERS/PERK pathway using thapsigargin (Tg) or CCT020312 suppressed viral replication, whereas its inhibition with 4-phenylbutyric acid (4-PBA) or AMG-PERK 44 promoted replication. In conclusion, this study presents the first systematic interactome of ASFV p54 and reveals a novel mechanism by which the host factor RPL22 modulates ASFV replication via the ERS-PERK pathway. These findings offer new insights into ASFV-host interactions and identify the ERS pathway as a promising therapeutic target for the development of anti-ASFV strategies.KEYWORDS: ASFV, P54, RPL22, TurboID, proximity labeling, ER stress, PERK signaling pathway, host–virus interaction

Introduction

African swine fever (ASF) is a highly pathogenic and lethal transboundary animal disease caused by African swine fever virus (ASFV). Transmitted through direct contact or via vectors such as soft ticks, ASFV poses a persistent and severe threat to the global swine industry [1–4]. As the sole member of the genus Asfivirus in the family Asfarviridae, ASFV is a large, double-stranded DNA virus characterized by a complex, multilayered structure [5–7]. The virion, approximately 200 nm in diameter, comprises a central nucleoid, an inner envelope, an icosahedral capsid, and an outer envelope [8,9]. ASFV has a large genome of over 170 kb that encodes 150–170 open reading frames (ORFs), many of which remain functionally uncharacterized [10–12]. The development of effective countermeasures is hindered by the biological complexity, potent immune evasion mechanisms, and high genetic variability of this virus. Although live attenuated vaccines (LAVs), such as ASFV-G-ΔI177L, have been approved for limited commercial use in regions including Vietnam [13], their widespread application remains restricted due to critical safety and efficacy concerns. Recent investigations indicate that these vaccine strains may revert to virulence and induce severe reproductive failure in pregnant sows [14]. Moreover, they fail to confer complete protection against the recombinant genotype I/II strains currently circulating in Asia [15]. Consequently, there remains a lack of widely accepted, safe vaccines, and control strategies continue to rely heavily on strict biosecurity measures [16]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms of ASFV infection and replication, particularly through the identification and characterization of key viral proteins and their interactions with the host, is of critical for advancing disease control strategies.

Among ASFV's structural proteins, the inner envelope protein p54, encoded by the E183L gene, is a type II transmembrane protein essential for several stages of the viral life cycle [17]. Previous studies have shown that p54 cooperates with other viral proteins, such as p30 and p12, to mediate the binding of virions to host cell receptors. Furthermore, preincubation of cells with p54 can block the early phase of infection, highlighting its role in viral entry [18]. Following entry, p54 binds to host dynein light chain 8 (DLC8), a critical interaction that facilitates intracellular transport of virions along microtubules to the viral factory, the main site for viral replication and assembly. Disruption of this p54-DLC8 interaction significantly impairs ASFV replication [19,20]. Additionally, p54 has also been implicated in triggering apoptosis via activate caspase-3 [21]. These findings underscore p54 as a multifunctional and essential component of the ASFV life cycle, making it an attractive target for antiviral drug development.

As obligate intracellular parasites, viruses are highly dependent on host cellular machinery, including the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). Consequently, a dynamic interplay exists between ERS and viral infection [22,23]. Many viruses, including ASFV, have evolved mechanisms to manipulate ERS signaling to create conditions favorable for replication [24]. For example, ASFV has been reported to suppress the phosphorylation of eIF2α during early infection, thereby sustaining host translation machinery for the synthesis of viral proteins [25]. Furthermore, several ASFV-encoded proteins, such as B117L, K205R, MGF110-7L, and the essential gene EP152R, modulate host translation, intracellular Ca²+ homeostasis, or stress granule formation by targeting various branches of the ERS response [26–29]. Collectively, these studies illustrate a continuous and complex interplay between ASFV and the host machinery. However, the mechanisms by which specific structural proteins such as p54 influence ERS pathways, and how this regulation contributes to viral replication, remain poorly understood. Deeper insight into this interface may uncover novel antiviral targets within the host ERS machinery.

Ribosomal proteins (RPs) have traditionally been regarded as structural components of the ribosome, primarily responsible for maintaining ribosomal stability and participating in protein translation. However, accumulating evidence suggests that eukaryotic RPs possess extraribosomal functions independent of their structural role. They play a pivotal regulatory role in viral replication, transcription, and translation by interacting with viral proteins or host factors [30]. The large ribosomal subunit protein RPL22 has been shown to regulate T-cell development by modulating endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, particularly via the PERK signaling pathway [31,32], and plays a significant role in infections caused by EBV [33,34].

Mapping virus–host protein interactions is a central strategy for uncovering infection mechanisms and identifying therapeutic targets. Traditional approaches such as affinity purification-mass spectrometry (AP-MS), are effective at detecting stable, high-affinity interactions but often miss transient, weak, or low-abundance interactions that can be critical for the viral replication [35]. Given the large coding capacity and structural complexity of ASFV, its infection cycle is likely supported by a vast and dynamic host interactome, much of which remains unexplored using conventional methodologies [36]. In recent years, proximity labeling (PL) has emerged as a powerful tool for characterizing complex protein interaction networks. Among its variants, TurboID offers a particularly advantageous solution due to its high labeling efficiency and speed [37]. Within a short labeling period (e.g. 10 min), TurboID can efficiently biotinylate proteins within approximately 10 nm of a bait protein, enabling comprehensive capture of its dynamic interactome, including transient, weak, and low-abundance interactors [38,39]. This capability is particularly valuable for dissecting the molecular machinery of large, complex viruses such as ASFV, which encode numerous proteins with uncharacterized functions. Indeed, PL-based approaches have already been successfully applied to numerous viruses, including EBV, HCMV, HIV, and SARS-CoV-2, leading to the discovery of critical host factors and potential antiviral targets [40–47]. These successes highlight the significant potential of TurboID in virology research.

Despite its advantages, the application to ASFV remains notably underexplored. This gap has consequently limited our understanding of the functional roles of many ASFV proteins and the precise mechanisms by which they manipulate the host cellular processes. Leveraging TurboID to map the interactome of essential ASFV proteins, such as p54, could help close this knowledge gap and identify novel therapeutic targets.

In this study, we employed TurboID-based proximity labeling using the ASFV inner envelope protein p54 as bait to systematically identify its host protein interactors. This approach yielded 257 high-confidence candidate interactors. The robustness of our dataset was supported by the identification of DLC8, a previously known p54-binding partner [20]. Among the novel interactors, we focused on the 60S ribosomal protein RPL22. We demonstrated that RPL22 interacts with p54 and modulates ASFV replication via the PERK signaling pathway of the ERS response. Furthermore, we show that pharmacological modulation of this pathway significantly alters viral replication, highlighting the ERS-PERK axis as a promising antiviral target.

Materials and methods

Cells and viruses

HEK-293 T (ATCC CRL-3216) and LLC-PK1 (ATCC CL-101) cell lines were maintained in our laboratory as previously described [48]. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ExCell Bio, Shanghai, China), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). Primary porcine alveolar macrophages (PAMs) were isolated by flushing the lungs of 20-day-old pigs with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) containing 10% FBS, 200 U/mL penicillin, and 200 μg/mL streptomycin. The ASFV genotype II strains SXH1 and 0428C used in this study were isolated and preserved in our laboratory. The ASFV-GFP(0428C) reporter virus, whose replication kinetics and infectivity were previously confirmed to be comparable to the wild-type strain [49].

Plasmid construction

To construct the plasmids pcDNA-V5-TurboID-p54 and pcDNA-V5-p54-TurboID, the TurboID coding sequence was amplified from the laboratory-maintained plasmid pcDNA-V5-TurboID via PrimeSTAR MAX DNA polymerase (Takara, Japan). The amplified TurboID fragment was then inserted into a linearized pcDNA backbone prepared by restriction enzyme digestion. The ASFV E183L gene was amplified from viral genomic DNA extracted from ASFV strains. The three fragments (TurboID, E183L, and vector backbone) were assembled using a homologous recombination kit (Abclone, RK21020, China). For the construction of pcDNA-hRPL22-FLAG and pcDNA-pRPL22-FLAG, the RPL22 gene was amplified using cDNA from HEK-293 T or LLC-PK1 cells respectively, as the template. The PCR products were inserted into the linearized pcDNA-FLAG vector by homologous recombination. All the constructs were verified via Sanger sequencing (Sangon, Shanghai, China).

Streptavidin-based protein enrichment

HEK-293 T cells were transfected with pcDNA-V5-TurboID, pcDNA-V5-TurboID-p54, or pcDNA-V5-p54-TurboID using JetPrime transfection reagent (Polyplus, Paris, France), following the manufacturer’s protocol. At 28 h post-transfection, the culture medium was replaced with complete DMEM supplemented with 500 μM biotin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After labeling, cells were washed three times with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed on ice for 20 min using Western/IP lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF (Beyotime, China). Cell lysates were collected with a cell scraper, transferred to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes and rotated at 15 rpm at 4 °C for 1 h. After centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was transferred to new tubes and incubated overnight at 4 °C with 100 μL streptavidin magnetic beads (NEB, USA), which were pre-washed three times with binding buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.5], 0.5 M NaCl, 1 mM EDTA). Following overnight incubation with gentle rotation (7.5 rpm), the beads were washed five times with Western/IP lysis buffer. Biotinylated proteins were eluted by adding 100 μL of SDT buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM DTT, pH 7.6), followed by boiling at 100 °C for 10 min. The eluted proteins were then analyzed by Western blotting or subjected to mass spectrometry.

Probe-based quantitative PCR for viral genome quantification

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed to determine ASFV genome copy numbers using the Probe Master Mix (Vazyme, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The ASFV-specific probe sequence used is listed in Table 1. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. For absolute quantification, a standard curve was generated using serial dilutions of a plasmid containing the ASFV B646L gene with known copy numbers. The genome copy number of each test sample was calculated by comparing its cycle threshold (Ct) value with the standard curve.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

To assess exogenous interaction between ASFV p54 and RPL22, HEK-293 T cells grown in 10 cm dishes were transfected with combinations of pcDNA-p54-ST and pcDNA-hRPL22-FLAG or pcDNA-pRPL22-FLAG. Cells were harvested at 28 h post-transfection. To evaluate endogenous interaction, PAM cells were infected with ASFV SXH1 strain at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1 and collected 36 h post-infection. Cell lysis was performed as described in Section 3. Clarified lysates were incubated overnight (12 h) at 4 °C with 2 μg of one of the following antibodies: anti-ST, anti-FLAG, anti-p54, anti-RPL22, or an isotype-matched IgG (negative control). The following day, 50 μL of Protein A/G agarose beads (Beyotime, China) was added to each sample and incubated at 4 °C for 3 h with rotation to capture immune complexes. Beads were then washed five times with 1 mL lysis buffer, resuspended in 40 μL PBS and 10 μL 5× SDS loading buffer, then boiled before being subjected to Western blot analysis to evaluate the protein–protein interaction.

Indirect immunofluorescence and confocal microscopy

HEK-293 T or LLC-PK1 cells were seeded onto confocal culture dishes. Following transfection or ASFV infection, cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature. Permeabilization was performed using 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min at room temperature. After permeabilization, cells were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Biosharp, China) diluted in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The next day, samples were washed five times with PBST (PBS + 0.1% Tween-20) and incubated with Alexa Fluor 488- or 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse or anti-human; Thermo Fisher, USA) at 37 °C for 45 min in the dark. After five additional washes with PBST, cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Lentiviral packaging and transduction

For lentivirus production, HEK-293 T cells cultured in 100 mm dishes were co-transfected with 5 μg of lentiviral transfer vector, 0.5 μg of pMD2.G, and 5 μg of psPAX2 using JetPrime transfection reagent (Polyplus, Paris, France), following the manufacturer’s protocol. At 48 h post-transfection, the supernatant containing lentiviral particles was harvested and filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter (Millipore, USA). The filtered supernatant was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 2.5 h at 4 °C to pellet the viral particles. The pellet was resuspended in PBS and stored at −80 °C until further use. For transduction, target cells at approximately 60% confluence were incubated with the lentiviral suspension diluted in complete medium supplemented with 8 μg/mL Polybrene (Beyotime, China). After 24 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh complete culture medium.

Generation of RPL22 knockout cell lines

To generate RPL22 knockout (RPL22KO) cell lines, single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were designed using the CRISPOR tool (https://crispor.gi.ucsc.edu/crispor.py). High-ranking sgRNA sequences were selected, and their corresponding primers were synthesized and annealed. The annealed oligonucleotides were then ligated into the sgLenti-lentiviral vector, which had been linearized by restriction enzyme digestion. Following confirmation of correct insertion by Sanger sequencing, lentivirus was packaged as described in Section 7 and used to transduce LLC-PK1-Cas9 cells. At 48 h post-transduction, cells were subjected to puromycin selection (final concentration: 4 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), which continued until all untransduced control cells were eliminated. Genomic DNA was then extracted from the surviving population, and the target region was PCR-amplified and sequenced to confirm gene editing.

Establishment of RPL22 overexpression and rescue cell lines

To establish RPL22-overexpressing cell lines, the coding sequence (CDS) of RPL22 was cloned into the pQXIP-FLAG-puro vector. For rescue experiments in RPL22KO cells, a modified RPL22 CDS with silent mutations at the sgRNA binding site and PAM sequence to prevent re-editing was cloned into the pQXIP-FLAG-G418 vector. Lentiviral particles were packaged as described in Section 7 and used to transduce either LLC-PK1 or LLC-PK1-RPL22KO cells. After 48 h, cells were selected using either puromycin (4 μg/mL) or G418 (300 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, USA), depending on the resistance marker encoded by the vector. Selection continued until all untransduced control cells were eliminated. The expression of RPL22 in the surviving cells was confirmed by Western blotting.

RNA extraction and quantitative RT–PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from various cell types using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan) to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was carried out using SYBR Green Master Mix (Bio-Rad, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences used for target gene amplification are listed in Table 1. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method and normalized to GAPDH expression.

Drug treatment and infection assay

To evaluate the impact of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress modulators on ASFV replication, PAM or LLC-PK1 cells were pretreated with the indicated compounds for 6 hours. Subsequently, cells were infected with ASFV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1, PAMs with the SXH1 strain and LLC-PK1 cells with the 0428C strain. After 2 hours of viral adsorption, the inoculum was replaced with maintenance medium containing the corresponding ERS- modulating compounds. At 36 h post-infection (hpi), cells were harvested for qPCR, Western blot, and immunofluorescence analyses to assess viral replication and cellular responses.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using a CCK-8 assay kit (Biosharp, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After drug treatment or experimental manipulation, CCK-8 reagent was added directly to the culture medium, and cells were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 3 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. All experiments were performed with three biological replicates, each measured in technical triplicates to ensure accuracy.

Virus titration (TCID50 assay)

To determine the TCID50 of ASFV under different treatment conditions, viral samples were subjected to 10-fold serial dilutions to generate eight dilution gradients. Each dilution was inoculated into eight replicate wells of LLC-PK1 cells seeded in 96-well plates. Fluorescence by ASFV-EGFP infection was observed under a Fluorescence microscope. The TCID50 value was calculated using the Reed–Muench method.

Western blot analysis

Protein samples were resolved using10% or 12.5% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk (BD, USA) in TBST for 2 hours at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight (12 h). After five washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse or anti-human secondary antibodies for 45 minutes at room temperature. Signals were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence detection kit and captured with the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). All antibodies used in this study are listed in Table 2.

Results

Proximity labeling using p54-TurboID identifies potential host interactors of ASFV p54

To characterize the interactome of the essential ASFV inner envelope protein p54, we generated expression constructs in which the promiscuous biotin ligase TurboID was fused to either the N- or C-terminus of p54 (Figure S1A). Upon transfection into 293 T cells, robust expression of the C-terminal fusion protein (p54-TurboID, ∼64 kDa) was confirmed by Western blotting. In contrast, the N-terminally tagged TurboID-p54 was not detectable by this method (Figure S1B). These findings were supported by immunofluorescence assays, which showed strong signal for p54-TurboID but negligible signal for TurboID-p54 (Figure 1(A)). Consequently, p54-TurboID was selected for all subsequent experiments. To confirm that the TurboID fusion did not alter the subcellular localization of p54, we performed confocal microscopy. p54-TurboID exhibited a localization pattern consistent with that of p54 expressed alone, suggesting that the TurboID tag did not interfere with its proper targeting (Figure 1(B)).

Cells were stained with anti-p54 antibodies. Scale bar, 500 µm. (B) Confocal microscopy of HEK-293 T cells co-expressing p54-ST (red) and V5-p54-TurboID (green). Enlarged view of boxed area is shown. We next optimized biotinylation conditions to enhance labeling efficiency while minimizing background. Time- and dose-dependent experiments revealed increased protein biotinylation in p54-TurboID-expressing cells upon biotin supplementation (Figure 1(C)). To rule out potential cytotoxicity caused by the labeling conditions, we assessed cell viability using a CCK-8 assay. The results confirmed that incubation with 500 μM biotin for up to 4 h did not significantly affect cell viability (Figure S2) Based on these results, a final concentration of 500 μM biotin with a 60-minute incubation was selected for subsequent proteomic analyses, as this condition yielded robust labeling with minimal non-specific signal. Proximity labeling was performed in HEK-293 T cells, a system chosen for its high transfection efficiency, well-annotated proteome, facilitating mass spectrometry interpretation, and demonstrated susceptibility to ASFV infection [51]. As shown in Figure 1(D), western blot analysis revealed a prominent and distinct pattern of biotinylated proteins in cells expressing p54-TurboID, whereas control cells expressing TurboID alone displayed minimal background labeling. These findings confirm that the p54-TurboID fusion effectively biotinylated nearby proteins in situ, enabling the capture of the p54 proximal proteome.

Identification and functional enrichment analysis of p54-proximal host proteins

Biotinylated proteins enriched by p54-TurboID labeling were subjected to mass spectrometry for identification. After subtracting proteins also enriched in the TurboID-alone control group, a total of 257 host proteins were identified as high-confidence proximal interactors of p54 (Figure 2(A)). The complete list of these interactors, ranked based on their identified peptide counts, is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Notably, the previously reported p54-binding partner, DLC8, was among the identified candidates and ranked with a high confidence score, thereby validating the reliability and specificity of our proximity labeling approach.

To explore the biological significance of these proteins, we conducted comprehensive bioinformatics analyses. First, we constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network using the STRING database, which revealed a dense network of interactions among the 257 candidates, suggesting they form functional modules(Figure 2(B)).

Next, we conducted KEGG pathway enrichment analysis to understand the broader biological pathways involved. The analysis showed significant enrichment in several key pathways, including “Spliceosome,” “Basal transcription factors,” and critically, pathways related to viral life cycles and host responses like “Coronavirus disease - COVID-19” (Figure 2(C)). To gain a more granular view of their functions, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, covering Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF) (Figure 2(D)). The GO analysis indicated that these proteins are involved in a wide range of fundamental cellular activities. For Biological Process (BP), they were significantly enriched in ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis, rRNA processing, and translation. The Cellular Component (CC) analysis showed a primary association with the ribosome, nucleolus, and spliceosomal complex. At the Molecular Function (MF) level, the most enriched categories were RNA binding and structural constituent of the ribosome. These results collectively indicate that the p54-proximal network is intimately linked to the host's RNA processing and protein translation machinery.

Ribosomal protein RPL22 interacts with ASFV p54

To validate the candidate interactors identified by proteomics, we selected high-ranking proteins for confirmation via co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays in transfected 293 T cells. These assays confirmed that the host ribosomal protein RPL22 interacts with ASFV p54 (Figure 3(A)). To evaluate this interaction under more physiological conditions, endogenous Co-IP was performed in ASFV-infected porcine alveolar macrophages (PAMs). The results demonstrated that endogenous p54 physically associates with RPL22 during infection (Figure 3(B)). To further support this finding, confocal microscopy revealed significant spatial co-localization of p54 and RPL22 in the perinuclear region of infected or transfected cells (Figure 3(C)).

To assess whether this interaction occurs directly in living cells, we conducted a bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay. Constructs encoding p54 or RPL22 fused to the N- or C-terminal fragments of RFP (p54-nRFP and RPL22-cRFP, respectively) were co-transfected into 293 T cells. A robust, specific fluorescent signal was observed in the perinuclear region only in cells co-expressing both fusion proteins, while no signal was detected in cells expressing either construct alone (Figure 3(D)). This finding further supports a direct interaction between p54 and RPL22 within the cellular environment. Collectively, results from Co-IP, confocal microscopy, and BiFC assays provide evidence that RPL22 is an interacting partner of ASFV p54.

Knockout of RPL22 impairs ASFV replication efficiency

First, to determine whether RPL22 is regulated during infection, we examined its expression dynamics in ASFV-infected PAMs and LLC-PK1 cells. Sanger sequencing confirmed a single-base insertion in the RPL22 coding sequence, predicted to cause a frameshift mutation (Figure 4(A)). Strikingly, the restoration of RPL22 completely recovered viral replication to levels comparable to those in the WT control, a finding confirmed by both fluorescence microscopy and TCID50 assays (Figure S5) Together, these findings demonstrate that RPL22 is an important host factor involved in ASFV replication.

RPL22 overexpression does not enhance ASFV replication

To complement the loss-of-function studies, we established a stable RPL22-overexpressing (RPL22-OE) LLC-PK1 cell line to evaluate whether increased RPL22 levels could enhance ASFV replication. Western blot analysis confirmed markedly elevated RPL22 protein expression in the RPL22-OE line compared with wild-type (WT) controls (Figure 5(A)). However, upon ASFV infection, no enhancement of viral replication was observed in the RPL22-OE cells. Consistent results from fluorescence microscopy, qPCR quantification of viral genome copy numbers, and viral growth curve analysis all indicated that viral replication levels in RPL22-OE cells were comparable to those in WT cells (Figure 5(B–D)). These findings contrast with the replication reduction observed in RPL22-KO cells and suggest that a basal level of RPL22 is necessary for efficient ASFV replication, but that its overexpression does not further potentiate the viral life cycle.

RPL22 deficiency induces ER stress activation via PERK pathway during ASFV infection

Mounting evidence suggests a close relationship between African swine fever virus (ASFV) infection and the induction of ERS. Notably, a previous study has reported that the loss of RPL22 can activate the PERK pathway of the unfolded protein response (UPR)[31,32]. To explore the functional interplay between RPL22, ERS, and ASFV infection, we first assessed the expression of canonical ER stress markers in RPL22-knockout (RPL22-KO) and WT LLC-PK1-Cas9 cells at 24 hpi. Quantitative RT–PCR revealed that ASFV infection alone did not significantly elevate the transcription of BIP, CHOP, or ATF4 in WT cells. However, RPL22-KO cells exhibited a significant upregulation of these markers upon ASFV infection, confirming that RPL22 loss induces ERS (Figure 6(A)). Immunoblotting further validated these findings: phosphorylation of PERK and eIF2α, along with increased protein levels of BIP, CHOP, and ATF4, were only observed in infected RPL22-KO cells, but not in infected WT cells, indicating that ASFV does not inherently activate the PERK pathway in this cellular context (Figure 6(B)). Taken together, these findings indicate that ASFV infection does not activate the PERK signaling pathway in LLC-PK1 cells, and that RPL22 depletion triggers PERK activation.

To further clarify the regulatory role of RPL22 in this context, we analyzed both transcript and protein levels of ERS markers across RPL22-KO, RPL22-Rescue, and WT LLC-PK1-Cas9 cell lines (Figure 6(C,D)). In RPL22-KO cells, phosphorylation of PERK (p-PERK) and eIF2α (p- eIF2α) was significantly elevated, accompanied by increased expression of canonical UPR markers including BIP, CHOP, and ATF4. Importantly, re-expression of RPL22 in the rescue cell line reversed these changes, restoring both transcriptional and protein profiles to WT levels. These results confirm that RPL22 deficiency is solely responsible for activation of the ERS-PERK pathway, establishing RPL22 as a key modulator of this signaling axis.

To determine whether the antiviral effect of RPL22 depletion was mediated through the activation of the PERK pathway, we performed a functional rescue experiment using AMG-PERK-44, a selective PERK inhibitor (working concentration determined by CCK-8 assay, Figure S6D). AMG-PERK-44 treatment effectively normalized BIP, CHOP, and ATF4 mRNA levels in RPL22-KO cells, reducing them to near-WT levels (p > 0.05) (Figure S7). Consistently, western blotting demonstrated that AMG-PERK-44 abolished the elevated phosphorylation of PERK and eIF2α as well as downstream marker expression (Figure 6(E)), indicating efficient inhibition of the ERS-PERK pathway. Next, we evaluated whether PERK inhibition could rescue ASFV replication in RPL22-KO cells. Cells were infected with ASFV-GFP at an MOI of 0.1, and treated with 5 μM AMG-PERK-44 at 2 hpi for 24 h. Fluorescence microscopy revealed a substantial recovery of viral GFP signal in RPL22-KO cells treated with the PERK inhibitor, suggesting restoration of viral replication (Figure 6(F)). This was further supported by qPCR quantification of viral genome copy numbers (Figure 6(G)). Collectively, these results demonstrate that RPL22 loss restricts ASFV infection by activating the ERS-PERK signaling pathway, and that this replication restriction can be reversed by pharmacological inhibition of PERK. This establishes a mechanistic link between RPL22 and ERS regulation, identifying the PERK pathway as a critical effector in RPL22-mediated restriction of ASFV replication.

Modulation of ER stress and PERK Signaling impacts ASFV replication

To systematically assess the regulatory role of ERS in African swine fever virus (ASFV) replication, we first investigated the effects of the pharmacological modulation using the ERS activator Thapsigargin (Tg) and inhibitor 4-phenylbutyric acid (4-PBA). Working concentrations were optimized via CCK-8 cytotoxicity assays (Figure S6A,S6B). LLC-PK1 and primary porcine alveolar macrophage (PAM) cells were pretreated with various concentrations of Tg or 4-PBA for 6 hours, followed by infection with either the ASFV-SXH1or the ASFV-GFP strain at an MOI of 0.1. At 36 hpi, fluorescence microscopy revealed a dose-dependent reduction in GFP-positive cells upon Tg treatment, indicating suppressed viral replication. In contrast, 4-PBA treatment increased the proportion of infected cells in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 7(A)). Western blot analysis supported these findings: Tg upregulated BiP, a key ERS marker, while suppressing viral P72 expression (Figure 7(B,C)). In contrast, 4-PBA treatment downregulated BiP expression and enhanced P72 levels. These changes were further validated by qPCR quantification of viral genome copies in the supernatant (Figure 7(D,E)). Similar patterns were observed in PAMs, reinforcing that global ERS activation restricts, whereas ERS suppression promotes ASFV replication.

To further dissect the specific contribution of the PERK arm of the UPR, we employed CCT020312, a PERK activator, and AMG-PERK 44, a selective PERK inhibitor. In LLC-PK1 cells, CCT020312 induced a dose-dependent decrease in ASFV-infected cells, while AMG-PERK 44 led to a dose-dependent increase, as observed by fluorescence microscopy at 36 hpi (Figure 7(F)). Immunoblotting showed that CCT020312 enhanced PERK phosphorylation (p-PERK) and reduced P72 expression in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 7(G,H)). Conversely, AMG-PERK 44 inhibited PERK phosphorylation and increased viral P72 levels. These observations were mirrored by qPCR analyses, which demonstrated that CCT020312 significantly reduced, while AMG-PERK 44 increased, ASFV genome copy numbers (Figure 7(I,J)). Parallel experiments in PAMs yielded comparable results, reinforcing that PERK activation exerts a potent antiviral effect, while its inhibition facilitates viral propagation. Collectively, these data demonstrate that pharmacological manipulation of ERS, particularly via the PERK signaling pathway, impacts ASFV replication. Specifically, PERK activation restricts, and its inhibition promotes, viral replication, suggesting this pathway as a potential therapeutic target against ASFV.

Discussion

The development of commercial vaccines against ASFV remains a formidable challenge, largely due to the virus's complex structure and inherent safety concerns linked to its capacity for homologous recombination [52,53]. As a result, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying ASFV infection and replication and identifying key host-virus interactions has emerged as a vital strategy for antiviral targets discovery [54,55]. In this study, we systematically mapped the host protein interactome of the essential ASFV inner envelope protein p54 using TurboID-based proximity-labeling technology. Our screen identified RPL22 as a novel p54-interacting partner and an important host factor that facilitates ASFV replication. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that RPL22 deficiency activates the PERK-eIF2α branch of the ERS response, which in turn suppresses ASFV replication. This suggests that ASFV depends on the host's basal RPL22 function to maintain ER homeostasis and avoid triggering an antiviral stress response. Importantly, we validated this antiviral mechanism pharmacologically, demonstrating that PERK pathway activation using CCT020312 strongly inhibits ASFV replication. Based on these findings, we propose a working model (Figure 8) that illustrates the role of RPL22 in maintaining cellular homeostasis and preventing host antiviral responses during ASFV infection. It’s also establish the ERS-PERK pathway as a promising therapeutic target for developing therapeutic target for host-directed antiviral strategies.

A proposed model for the role of RPL22 as a key host factor in modulating the antiviral PERK signaling pathway to impact ASFV replication.

Our proximity-labeling proteomics, enabled by the high specificity and temporal resolution of TurboID, successfully identified 257 high-confidence host proteins in proximity to p54. The identification of DLC8, a known p54 interactor, supported the validity of our approach. Among the novel candidates, RPL22 stood out due to its central cellular functions and previously suggested roles in virus-host interactions. While traditionally classified as a structural component of the 60S ribosomal subunit, ribosomal proteins (RPs) are increasingly recognized for their extra ribosomal functions, including roles in apoptosis, stress responses, and viral replication [56, 57]. Previous studies have shown that other ASFV proteins such as UBCv1 and pCP312R also target RPs to modulate host translation machinery [50, 58]. Our identification of RPL22 as a p54 interactor adds to the growing body of evidence that ASFV engages the host ribosome at multiple levels to fine-tune translation and stress signaling, thereby optimizing the intracellular environment for viral replication. Notably, A concurrent study [59], which independently reported that p54 can induce ER aggregation, a function similar to that of pE146L. This convergent finding solidified p54's role as a key player at the virus-ER interface.

In this study, we observed that ASFV infection failed to activate the PERK pathway in wild-type LLC-PK1 cells, whereas the depletion of RPL22 resulted in robust PERK activation and significant inhibition of viral replication. This finding stands in sharp contrast to recent reports indicating that specific ASFV proteins, such as EP152R [60], K205R [61], and MGF110-7L [62], are capable of inducing ER stress (ERS) and activating the PERK pathway. Consistent with the theory of “selective regulation” of ERS by viruses [63, 64], viruses often require the activation of specific UPR branches – such as increased expression of the chaperone BiP – to facilitate membrane remodeling and protein folding at viral factories [61,64], while simultaneously suppressing downstream translation shutdown signals to maintain viral replication [65]. Indeed, a recent study confirmed that although ASFV infection may trigger early stress, the virus subsequently recruits protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) to dephosphorylate eIF2α, thereby maintaining global protein synthesis [25]. Our data further extend this model, suggesting that the host ribosomal protein RPL22 may serve as a key host factor in maintaining this balance.

The critical role of the PERK signaling in restricting ASFV was further confirmed through pharmacological modulation. Broad ERS induction by Tg, as well as selective PERK activation by CCT020312, both significantly inhibited ASFV replication in LLC-PK1 cells and PAMs. Conversely, inhibiting the pathway with 4-PBA or the specific inhibitor AMG-PERK44 promoted viral replication and, importantly, rescued the replication defect in RPL22-KO cells. These data identify PERK as a relevant node in the host-virus interaction network and suggest that therapeutically targeting the PERK pathway may offer a viable antiviral approach against ASFV. Interestingly, overexpression of RPL22 failed further enhance viral replication, indicating that of RPL22 is permissive rather than rate-limiting for infection. This finding suggests that ASFV requires only baseline RPL22 activity to facilitate replication and prevent stress responses, rather than benefiting from its overabundance [60, 66–68].

While this study sheds light on a previously unrecognized host dependency factor for ASFV, several questions remain. Most notably, the molecular mechanism by which p54-RPL22 interaction suppresses PERK activation is still unclear. Future studies should explore whether this interaction alters RPL22 stability, localization, or its association with stress-sensing complexes. Structural and mutational analyses will be critical to define precise binding domains and to dissect whether RPL22’s extra ribosomal functions, such as its roles in transcriptional control or immune signaling, are exploited by ASFV.

In conclusion, we present a comprehensive proteomic analysis of ASFV p54 host interactors and identify RPL22 as a regulator of the PERK-mediated ERS response during infection. Our findings underscore the virus's reliance on host ER homeostasis and highlight the PERK signaling axis as an important interface in ASFV-host interactions. These insights deepen our understanding of ASFV biology and point to host-directed interventions targeting ERS responses as promising therapeutic strategies.
