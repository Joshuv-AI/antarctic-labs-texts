Combined value of AIP and NHR for identifying obstructive coronary heart disease in premature cases with zero calcium
Authors: Min Wang, Chao Xue, Rong Zhu, Alian Zhang, Qizhi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA coronary artery calcium (CAC) score of zero is generally considered a strong negative predictor for coronary heart disease (CHD). Background

Premature coronary heart disease (CHD) exhibits distinct biological characteristics compared with late-onset atherosclerosis [1]. In symptomatic adults, the absence of coronary artery calcium (CAC = 0) is widely interpreted as indicating low physiological risk. However, the absence of calcification does not exclude non-calcified, lipid-rich plaque. This discordance is particularly evident in younger patients, in whom calcification frequently lags behind atherosclerotic burden development [2–4]. Consequently, reliance on CAC alone creates a clinical challenge in balancing the risk of missed obstructive disease against the burden of unnecessary invasive testing. We sought to identify markers that could stratify risk in symptomatic patients where CAC = 0 may not be sufficient to rule out obstructive disease [5].

In contrast to calcified plaque, which often signifies a stabilized or quiescent stage of atherosclerosis, non-calcified plaque is characterized by biological instability. Contemporary evidence suggests that this phenotype is driven significantly by residual inflammatory risk and qualitative lipid abnormalities, often independent of absolute low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) concentrations [6, 7]. Specifically, mechanistic studies indicate that triglyceride-rich lipoprotein remnants and neutrophil-driven endothelial erosion are critical drivers of non-calcified plaque progression [8]. Therefore, the detection of this specific burden requires biomarkers that capture functional lipoprotein qualities and inflammatory activity rather than static lipid concentrations alone.

The Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP) serves as a validated surrogate for small dense low-density lipoprotein (sdLDL) particle size, thereby characterizing the metabolic substrate of atherogenicity [9–12]. Similarly, the Neutrophil-to-HDL Ratio (NHR) functions as a composite index of systemic inflammation, reflecting the dynamic imbalance between neutrophil-mediated endothelial injury and the protective, anti-inflammatory mechanisms of HDL [13–16].

Given these distinct mechanistic domains, we postulated that their combined assessment would provide a more comprehensive risk profile. Although AIP and NHR are established independent risk factors, their joint utility in refining risk stratification within the diagnostic context of a zero calcium score remains underexplored. We hypothesized that concurrent elevation of AIP and NHR identifies a subgroup of symptomatic CAC = 0 patients with higher likelihood of angiographic obstructive CHD.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a retrospective analysis of a prospective invasive coronary angiography (ICA) registry at Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital (2018–2024). Consecutive symptomatic adults with suspected premature CHD (men aged < 55 years, women aged < 65 years) who underwent ICA were linked to the imaging archive to identify nongated chest CT performed within 3 months before ICA. Patients with CAC = 0 were included.

The study cohort was established through a stepwise selection process (Fig. 1). The primary aim was likelihood refinement within this specific ICA-referred population, and results should not be extrapolated to asymptomatic individuals undergoing CAC screening.

Study Flowchart. Flowchart showing inclusion and exclusion criteria leading to the final cohort of 362 patients with a Coronary Artery Calcium (CAC) score of zero. Abbreviations: CAC, Coronary artery calcium; ICA, Invasive coronary angiography

Exclusion criteria included prior percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), severe renal insufficiency (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²), non-diagnostic computed tomography (CT) quality, active malignancy or systemic inflammatory diseases, and acute infection within two weeks. To ensure clear phenotypic differentiation, patients with non-obstructive coronary artery disease (1–49% stenosis) were excluded.

The primary endpoint was angiographic obstructive CHD, defined as ≥ 50% luminal stenosis in at least one major epicardial coronary artery on ICA. Consequently, the control group consisted of patients with angiographically normal coronary arteries.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (SH9H-2019-T160-2). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained.

Data collection

Demographics, medical history, and laboratory data were extracted from electronic medical records. Venous blood samples were collected after an overnight fast prior to ICA. Laboratory parameters included lipid profiles (including LDL-C, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides), fasting glucose, and complete blood counts including absolute neutrophil count, measured using standard automated assays in the hospital laboratory.

CAC was quantified from nongated chest CT using an open-source AI algorithm (https://github.com/Raffi-Hagopian/AI-CAC/) applying standard Agatston criteria (> 130 HU). The validity of AI-derived CAC quantification from non-gated CT has been demonstrated in multiple studies [17–19].

AIP was calculated as the base-10 logarithm of the molar ratio of triglycerides to HDL-C [log(TG/HDL-C)]. NHR was calculated as the ratio of absolute neutrophil count to HDL-C. All lipid measurements were in mmol/L.

Statistical methods

Continuous variables were compared using t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Variables are summarized as mean ± SD, median [IQR], or n (%), as appropriate.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate associations with angiographic obstructive CHD, adjusting for traditional risk factors (age, sex, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, LDL-C). Model discrimination was summarized by area under the curve (AUC), Akaike information criterion (AIC), net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).

Participants were classified into four groups using internally derived cutoffs. Bootstrap validation (1000 resamples) was performed. Sensitivity analyses included multiple imputation and inverse probability weighting.

All analyses were performed in Python using standard scientific/statistical libraries. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Compared with controls (n = 247), the CHD group (n = 115) was younger (49.4 ± 7.7 vs. 53.5 ± 8.5 years) and had a higher proportion of males (73.0% vs. 29.1%). Smoking and hypertension were more prevalent in the CHD group (55.7% vs. 14.6% and 54.8% vs. 42.9%, respectively). Diabetes was numerically more frequent in the CHD group (18.3% vs. 10.6%).

Traditional LDL-C levels did not differ significantly between groups (3.10 ± 0.99 vs. 2.96 ± 0.91 mmol/L, P = 0.215). HDL-C was lower (0.99 ± 0.24 vs. 1.21 ± 0.37 mmol/L, P < 0.001) and triglycerides higher (1.95 [1.38, 2.76] vs. 1.33 [1.02, 1.88] mmol/L, P < 0.001) in the CHD group. AIP (0.30 [0.13, 0.50] vs. 0.07 [-0.13, 0.29]) and NHR (4.96 [3.29, 6.76] vs. 3.20 [2.24, 4.52]) were significantly higher in the CHD group (both P < 0.001).

BMI was higher in CHD (25.71 ± 3.19 vs. 24.81 ± 3.63 kg/m²; P = 0.021), and apolipoprotein B (ApoB) was higher (0.92 ± 0.25 vs. 0.83 ± 0.22 g/L; P = 0.002). White blood cell (WBC) count (7.28 [5.91, 9.22] vs. 6.09 [5.14, 7.36] ×109/L; P < 0.001) and neutrophils (4.55 [3.52, 6.08] vs. 3.69 [2.81, 4.75] ×109/L; P < 0.001) were higher in CHD. C-reactive protein (CRP) was numerically higher but not statistically different (P = 0.113) (see Table 1).

Multivariable associations and clinical stratification

Multivariable logistic regression was performed in complete cases (N = 331) (Table 2). Missing data were present in 31 (8.6%) participants and primarily affected laboratory parameters (NHR 8.3%; AIP 4.7%), while demographic and clinical history variables were complete.

Adding AIP and NHR significantly improved reclassification. The continuous Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) was 0.359 (95% CI 0.131–0.667, P = 0.002), and the Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) was 0.023 (95% CI 0.005–0.065, P < 0.001), indicating that these biomarkers reclassified a substantial proportion of patients into more appropriate risk categories compared with the traditional model.

In Model 3, AIP remained independently associated with obstructive CHD (OR 2.96, 95% CI 1.10–7.94) after adjustment for traditional risk factors (see Table 2 for full model coefficients).

Combined effect (four-group stratification)

Participants were classified into four groups using internally derived cutoffs (AIP > 0.13, NHR > 3.08), which were determined by maximizing the Youden’s index (Sensitivity + Specificity − 1) from the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis for predicting obstructive CHD. Compared with the dual-low reference group (n = 93), the high AIP/low NHR group (n = 25; OR 1.51, 95% CI 0.40–5.66) and low AIP/high NHR group (n = 71; OR 1.71, 95% CI 0.64–4.59) showed no significant association with obstructive CHD. The dual-high group (n = 142) demonstrated significantly higher adjusted odds (OR 4.59, 95% CI 1.90–11.07, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this study of symptomatic adults with suspected premature CHD and zero coronary calcium, we identified a dual-high phenotype characterized by concurrent elevation of AIP and NHR. This phenotype was strongly associated with angiographic obstructive disease. These findings suggest that the reassurance typically conferred by a zero CAC score may be limited in younger symptomatic populations [20–23]. Our results indicate that even in the absence of calcification, the biological drivers of active atherosclerosis—lipid-driven atherogenicity and systemic inflammation—can promote obstructive disease [2, 3, 6, 14]. This immunometabolic profile may help identify patients at higher risk who harbor clinically significant but radiologically occult disease [2, 20, 21].

CAC = 0 and residual obstructive disease

The association between the dual-high phenotype and obstructive disease in our CAC-negative cohort illustrates the biological gap between active atherosclerosis and radiological calcification. Calcification represents a late-stage reparative process that reflects plaque healing or stabilization [24]. Consequently, a CAC score of zero is a strong negative prognostic marker in general populations; however, this prognostic value is attenuated in symptomatic, younger, or high-risk cohorts [20–23]. The CONFIRM registry of 10,037 symptomatic patients undergoing coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) found a 3.5% prevalence of obstructive CAD in those with CAC = 0 [20]; this prevalence is higher in higher-risk populations. Data from symptomatic patients referred for invasive angiography indicated that 19% of those with a zero calcium score harbored obstructive disease [21], which was consistent with the high prevalence observed in our ICA-derived cohort. Similarly, the PROMISE trial demonstrated that while CAC = 0 generally implied a low event rate (1.4%), it did not strictly rule out obstructive disease or events in all stable chest pain patients [22]. More recently, findings from the Western Denmark Heart Registry clarified this age-dependent divergence. Among young symptomatic patients (aged 18–45 years), the prognostic reassurance of CAC = 0 may be reduced in the presence of multiple risk factors, with event rates increasing 6-fold in high-risk young adults despite a zero calcium score [23]. Collectively, these findings suggest that in young adults, the absence of calcification may reflect disease that has not yet calcified rather than absence of disease, supporting the use of biomarkers such as AIP and NHR to assess risk related to non-calcified plaque.

Mechanistic considerations: integrated metabolic and inflammatory pathways

While CAC = 0 reflects the absence of a calcified plaque burden, elevated AIP and NHR may indicate residual metabolic and inflammatory risk that is relevant to non-calcified disease. Conceptually, the dual-high phenotype may be consistent with the coexistence of an atherogenic lipoprotein milieu and heightened innate immune activation [6, 14]. These considerations are intended to provide biological context for the observed associations rather than establish lesion-specific mechanisms.

AIP

The Metabolic Domain. AIP integrates triglycerides and HDL cholesterol and correlates inversely with LDL particle diameter, supporting its use as a surrogate for sdLDL predominance [25]. sdLDL and triglyceride-rich lipoprotein (TRL) pathways have been implicated in endothelial dysfunction and atherogenesis, including increased susceptibility to oxidative modification and impaired clearance [9, 26, 27]. In addition, TRL remnants can promote endothelial activation and leukocyte recruitment [28]. Taken together, elevated AIP may reflect an atherogenic dyslipidemia pattern that could be relevant to earlier accumulation of non-calcified plaque before calcific stabilization [24, 29]. However, AIP does not directly quantify plaque composition and should be interpreted as an accessible proxy of lipid-related risk.

NHR

The Inflammatory Domain. NHR combines circulating neutrophil burden with HDL-related anti-inflammatory capacity and may therefore index an imbalance between pro-inflammatory effector activity and lipoprotein-mediated protection [14]. Neutrophil activation has been implicated in plaque erosion and thrombosis through pathways involving myeloperoxidase and neutrophil extracellular traps, which may injure endothelium and promote thrombus formation on intact plaques [1, 30]. In younger adults, erosion-related mechanisms may be relatively more prominent in some acute coronary events [1, 6]. Accordingly, NHR is best interpreted as a systemic index of inflammatory milieu rather than a lesion-specific marker. In this framework, concurrent elevation of AIP and NHR is consistent with non-calcified plaque-related risk driven by overlapping metabolic and inflammatory pathways.

Finally, it is important to acknowledge that while the addition of AIP and NHR to traditional risk factor models yielded statistically significant improvements in discriminative ability (P < 0.001), the absolute magnitude of the AUC increment remains modest. At this juncture, these indices should be viewed as providing an incremental refinement to existing risk stratification tools rather than standalone diagnostic replacements. Furthermore, while these ratios provide accessible, pragmatic proxies, future translational research investigating the fundamental molecular markers of triglyceride and neutrophil metabolism is necessary to further refine these indices and elucidate their precise underlying mechanisms in premature atherogenesis.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, this was a single-center retrospective study involving an ICA-referred cohort, which may introduce selection bias; findings are specific to this high-risk population and cannot be extrapolated to asymptomatic screening. Second, while we adjusted for traditional risk factors, residual confounding (e.g., from unmeasured lifestyle factors) remains possible. Third, plaque morphology was inferred from biomarker associations and angiographic stenosis; we lacked intravascular imaging (intravascular ultrasound or optical coherence tomography [IVUS/OCT]) to definitively confirm plaque erosion or rupture characteristics. Finally, the dual-high cutoffs were derived internally; prospective external validation is necessary before clinical application.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that the combination of elevated AIP and NHR identifies a distinct high-risk phenotype associated with angiographic obstructive CHD among symptomatic young adults with CAC = 0. These findings suggest that contemporary rule-out strategies based on CAC = 0 alone, as currently endorsed by major clinical guidelines [31, 32], may be insufficient for patients with this dual-high profile. Integrating these accessible biomarkers into clinical assessment could refine risk stratification and guide more targeted management of residual metabolic and inflammatory risks in non-calcified atherosclerosis, consistent with emerging evidence for anti-inflammatory therapies [33, 34].
