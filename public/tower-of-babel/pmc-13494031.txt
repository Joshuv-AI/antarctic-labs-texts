Detecting vegetation anomalies in mediterranean ecosystems in southern Italy using sentinel-2 NDVI time series and principal component analysis
Authors: Giuseppe Cillis, Antonio Lanorte, Gabriele Nolè
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vegetation is central to the functioning of ecosystems, supporting biodiversity and human livelihoods. However, it is highly sensitive to climate variability, land-use changes, and natural disturbances. Vegetation anomalies, or areas where plant growth significantly deviates from expected patterns, can indicate ecological stress, altered productivity and potential land degradation. Therefore, monitoring these anomalies is critical for understanding ecosystem resilience and informing sustainable management. This study examines vegetation anomalies in two adjacent hydrographic basins in southern Italy (Basilicata region) over a seven-year period (2017–2023), using Sentinel-2 imagery and the Normalised Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI). Selective Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to enhance the detection of localised temporal changes, reduce data dimensionality and extract key ecological signals, including seasonal dynamics, interannual variability and abrupt disturbances. The results reveal the spatial and temporal patterns of vegetation anomalies across different ecosystems, emphasising the impacts of climate variability and land-use practices, such as land abandonment. This study demonstrates the effectiveness of multitemporal PCA in identifying significant deviations in vegetation dynamics. It offers a valuable framework for assessing the functionality, resilience, and management of ecosystems under changing environmental conditions.

Introduction

Vegetation plays a fundamental role in regulating ecological processes, supporting biodiversity, and sustaining human livelihoods. Its spatial and temporal dynamics are closely linked to climatic variability, land-use practices, and natural disturbances1–3; Quang et al., 2025). In recent decades, growing concern over environmental change has intensified the need to monitor vegetation conditions and identify deviations from expected patterns4. Reliable, long-term monitoring of vegetation patterns at multiple spatial scales is vital for evaluating the impacts of shifting local environmental conditions on ecosystem health.

In this context, vegetation anomalies have emerged as a key indicator of ecosystem alteration. These anomalies generally refer to areas where plant growth or vegetation conditions differ significantly from what is normally expected for a given place and time, due to either human or natural factors5–9. Detecting and interpreting vegetation anomalies is essential for understanding ecosystem responses to stressors such as drought, heatwaves, deforestation or land degradation. Advances in remote-sensing technologies and the availability of long-term vegetation indices now allow for detailed assessments of these deviations at multiple spatial and temporal scales10–12. By examining the occurrence, intensity, and drivers of vegetation anomalies, researchers can improve early warning systems, support environmental planning, and evaluate the impacts of global change13,14. Recent studies have significantly advanced this field. For instance, Ma et al.15, investigated how vegetation resistance and resilience to drought vary using long-term NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index) and SPEI (Standardised Precipitation-Evapotranspiration Index) time series in Jilin Province, China. Moreover, Xie et al.9, proposed a universal framework for near-real-time detection of vegetation anomalies from Landsat data from different missions, thereby improving the timeliness and generality of anomaly-detection methods. At the global scale, van Jaarsveld et al.16, used machine-learning (random forests) to downscale and gap-fill EVI (Enhanced Vegetation Index) data, producing spatially and temporally coherent products useful for drought impact monitoring. Furthermore, the availability of improved long-term datasets enhances anomaly analysis; for example, Li et al.17, released a spatiotemporally consistent global GIMMS NDVI (Global Inventory Modeling and Mapping Studies) product (PKU GIMMS NDVI) covering 1982–2022, overcoming issues related to sensor degradation and orbital drift. When deviations from normal vegetation patterns occur, they can signal underlying environmental stressors such as climate variability, land-use change, or anthropogenic disturbances (Yarahmadi et al., 2023;18; Dalponte et al., 2025). These anomalies not only disrupt plant physiological performance and ecosystem productivity but also serve as early indicators of land degradation19,20. By examining changes in vegetative efficiency, functionality, and spatial distribution, researchers can better understand the ecological consequences of such disturbances and assess their long-term impacts on ecosystem resilience21,22. Prolonged and widespread vegetation anomalies can compromise the stability of ecosystem services by impairing photosynthesis and transpiration. Hence, the precise and timely identification of such anomalies is crucial23.

One of the most significant land-use processes associated with vegetation anomalies is land abandonment, in which agricultural activity ceases on formerly cultivated or managed land. In many parts of Europe, Asia, and the Americas, land abandonment has increased in recent decades due to demographic decline, agricultural restructuring, and shifting economic conditions24–26. Following abandonment, vegetation typically undergoes ecological succession, showing distinct spectral and phenological signatures, initial irregularities in productivity or greenness, followed by a gradual transition to grasslands, shrublands, or secondary forests27. Monitoring vegetation anomalies associated with land abandonment is therefore essential for understanding landscape transformation, carbon sequestration potential, and biodiversity recovery in the context of global environmental change. Land abandonment is a double-edged sword: it can lead to positive outcomes such as carbon sequestration and vegetation recovery (Rey-Benayas and Bullock, 201228;, but also to risks such as erosion29, increased fuel loads and fire risk30, and the loss of cultural landscapes31, especially in the first years after abandonment and in fragile semi-arid environments32,33.

Recent advances in remote sensing and machine learning now allow us to monitor vegetation anomalies with unprecedented spatial and temporal resolution. For instance, Sur et al.18, employed a multi-sensor, multi-temporal approach to detect greening and browning hotspots across India, attributing changes to climatic versus anthropogenic drivers. Similarly, Sun et al.19, developed a global monthly dataset of vegetation and soil fractions that disentangles bare soil from non-photosynthetic vegetation, improving the fidelity of degradation proxies. In degraded landscapes such as mining quarries or drought-affected regions, combined vegetation metrics (e.g., NDVI, NDMI Normalized Difference Moisture Index, EVI, VCI Vegetation Condition Index) have been shown to effectively capture anomalies linked to ecological stress and declining functionality20,22,34. In this context, vegetation anomalies function as a critical proxy for identifying and monitoring land degradation, providing valuable insights for sustainable land management and conservation strategies.

This paper focused on vegetation anomalies, aiming to provide a comprehensive analysis of their patterns, causes, and implications within the study area. Through a combination of remote-sensing data and statistical methods, the research seeks to contribute to a better understanding of vegetation dynamics and to offer insights relevant for sustainable environmental management. For this purpose, we used the Principal Component Analysis (PCA) applied to Sentinel-2 digital image data series spanning a 7-year period (2017–2023) to detect vegetation anomalies in two contiguous hydrographic basins of the Mediterranean region (Southern Italy, Basilicata Region) characterized by various ecosystems (from mountainous to grassland vegetation). The NDVI was used as input to a selective PCA procedure. The PCA was used as a first step of data transformation to enhance regions of localized change in multi-temporal data sets35,36. PCA provides an efficient solution to these challenges by transforming correlated NDVI layers into an orthogonal set of components that capture the dominant temporal modes of variability. Through dimensionality reduction, PCA enhances the interpretability of NDVI time series, enabling the extraction of key ecological signals including seasonal vegetation dynamics, interannual variability and abrupt disturbances. This approach has proven valuable for various environmental applications, including land-cover change detection, drought assessment, and crop monitoring (D’Ercole et l., 2025;37. This study applies PCA to multitemporal NDVI imagery to characterize spatial and temporal patterns of vegetation dynamics. The analysis aims to (i) reduce dataset dimensionality while retaining major vegetation signals, (ii) identify primary temporal modes of variation, and (iii) assess the spatial distribution of PCA components to support environmental interpretation.

Study area and dataset

Study area

The study area (Fig. 1) covers approximately 78,000 hectares and encompasses two of the Agri River’s secondary hydrographic sub-basins: the Sauro and Camastra streams, located in the Basilicata region of southern Italy. While these watercourses are characterised by significant channel dimensions, they exhibit low, seasonally variable discharge rates.

These two areas were selected due to their considerable altitudinal range (from 150 to 1,780 m above sea level) and geomorphological variability. These features have facilitated the development of high ecosystem diversity within a relatively restricted area, including markedly contrasting habitat types. Furthermore, this region remains relatively understudied from an ecological perspective, as it lacks designated national protected areas, primarily because of its historical focus on agriculture. as it lacks designated national protected areas, primarily due to its historical focus on agriculture. The recent widespread abandonment of inland areas has triggered significant landscape and ecological reorganisations that require further investigation, particularly as similar dynamics are occurring in other Mediterranean regions. Additionally, given the area’s microclimatic characteristics, it is among the zones that require enhanced monitoring of land degradation38.

Satellite data source

The satellite data was extracted from the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform39. We chose to use images from the Sentinel-2 mission, despite the fact that their spatial resolution does not allow for a very long time series. This is because they allow for more detailed ecological investigations. Specifically, the COPERNICUS/S2_SR_HARMONIZED dataset was used. This dataset comprises a global collection of Sentinel-2 images in surface reflectance (SR) processed with Sen2Cor and radiometrically harmonized across Sentinel-2 A and Sentinel-2 B. Harmonization reduces systematic differences between the two sensors, ensuring greater temporal consistency in multitemporal analyses. The dataset provides multispectral data at resolutions of 10, 20, and 60 m, with a revisit frequency of five days, allowing detailed observation of environmental dynamics and terrestrial processes. In addition to optical bands, it includes auxiliary layers such as scene classification (SCL), aerosol optical thickness (AOT), and water vapor content (WVP), which are essential for assessing information quality and correcting residual atmospheric effects. Thanks to its near-global coverage and high level of pre-processing, S2_SR_HARMONIZED is a reference resource for scientific applications such as vegetation monitoring, land use change analysis, hydrological studies, and climate impact assessments. For this study, monthly NDVIs for the area were calculated for the period from 2017 to 2023. For each month of the May-October semester, a single cloud-free image was chosen, thus avoiding a bias toward certain periods of the year with more images (Table 1). In the case of excessively cloudy months, these were discarded and replaced with a cloudless mosaic with images from different days or from the previous month. In the presence of small clouds, which have been automatically replaced with the GEE code using QA60 band and maskS2clouds function. Habitat mapping

For habitat mapping, the Italian CORINE biotopes database was chosen because, although dated 2014, it is the only detailed mapping available for the Basilicata region. The CORINE biotopes database has been derived from “Nature Map of Italy”, an Italian project developed by the Higher Institute for Environmental Protection and Research (ISPRA40, with the aim of systematically mapping the natural and semi-natural habitats of the Italian territory and assessing their conservation status. The data produced is made available through the “Nature Map Information System”, which is accessible online free of charge, thus promoting transparency and use by public bodies, researchers, and professionals in the environmental sector. The map produced is on a scale of 1:50,000, and the habitats are provided in vector format and coded according to the European CORINE Biotopes classification system. The dataset was produced in 2014 and in Italy is the last product of this typology, but its quality and reliability make it an indispensable tool for monitoring natural habitats that undergo slower, and therefore negligible, changes over the years considered in the study. Considering authors knowledge of the study area, the dataset was modified through manual editing in areas where significant habitat changes have occurred.

Given the objectives of the study and the complexity of the descriptive information contained in the dataset, the spatial analysis only considered three macro-categories of habitat: forest, herbaceous and shrubland. In view of the need to identify each of the classes in greater detail, three tables (Tables 2, 3 and 4) were created for each of these macro-categories. tables present the simplified habitats considered in the study, including wording, EUNIS codes, and descriptions. This is necessary to allow subsequent assessments of the produced data and to evaluate relationships with it.

Methods

PCA is a statistical method for reducing the dimensionality of datasets while retaining most of the variation in the original variables. In the context of multitemporal NDVI data, PCA is commonly used to extract dominant patterns in vegetation dynamics across time43,44. Multitemporal NDVI datasets include multiple images captured at different dates, often showing similar or correlated vegetation patterns. PCA helps by reducing redundancy among highly correlated NDVI layers, highlighting major temporal vegetation trends, such as seasonal cycles, extracting features for classification, change detection, or phenological analysis45,46. Originally formulated by Pearson47 and later extended by Hotelling48, PCA transforms a set of possibly correlated variables into a new set of orthogonal, uncorrelated variables called principal components (PCs). These components are constructed so that the first component captures the maximum possible variance, the second captures the maximum remaining variance, and so on. PCA has long been recognized as a robust statistical method in remote sensing for feature extraction and dimensionality reduction49. Its application to multitemporal imagery was highlighted by Singh50, who demonstrated PCA’s utility for detecting land-cover change and isolating temporal anomalies. Subsequent studies strengthened the use of PCA for extracting phenological signals51, identifying vegetation stress52, and monitoring agricultural systems (Sibanda et al., 2015). Across the literature, PCA consistently enhances the interpretability of NDVI time series by condensing complex datasets into a small number of ecologically meaningful components. This methodological consistency and demonstrated effectiveness underscore PCA’s suitability for multitemporal vegetation analysis. The NDVI, derived from multispectral satellite data, remains one of the most widely used indicators for estimating vegetation vigor, chlorophyll content, and canopy development53. When NDVI is acquired across multiple time periods, it provides detailed insights into phenological cycles, seasonal transitions, and long-term environmental change. However, the high dimensionality and redundancy characteristic of multitemporal remote-sensing data present challenges for effective analysis, visualization, and interpretation.

The NDVI index was introduced by Rouse et al.54, and soon became a cornerstone of satellite-based vegetation monitoring. Its strong correlation with vegetation biomass and photosynthetic activity was established in early remote-sensing research12. The development of long-term NDVI datasets from satellite sensors has facilitated extensive investigations into vegetation phenology55; Reed et al., 1994), land-cover change56, and ecological responses to climatic variability11.

In this study, multitemporal NDVI datasets were generated using satellite imagery from Sentinel-2 MSI (.MultiSpectral Instrument) For each acquisition date, NDVI was computed using:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{I}=\frac{\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{I}\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{d}}{\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{I}\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{d}}$$\end{document}

The red band (RED) corresponds to the portion of the electromagnetic spectrum ranging from approximately 620 to 700 nm, a region in which chlorophyll present in leaves strongly absorbs solar radiation to drive the photosynthetic process. The near-infrared band (NIR), by contrast, extends from approximately 700 to 1100 nm and is highly reflected by the cellular structure of the leaf mesophyll54.

As reported, the NDVI calculation was performed in series using the GEE platform. The potential of the GEE platform was fully exploited to speed up and perform the processing required for the study in series. In order to have greater control over the operation, the images were selected using the Copernicus Browser (Copernicus Data Ecosystem Service, 2025) while the mosaicing of the tiles, the clipping of the study area, the calculation of the NDVI, and the download of the rasters reprojected in EPSG:32,633 for subsequent spatial analysis were all carried out using code created on the GEE platform. PCA was applied to the mean-centered NDVI data matrix. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors were then derived and ranked in descending order of eigenvalue magnitude. Principal component scores were calculated as (9):9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{P}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{\boldsymbol{i}}={\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{c}}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\boldsymbol{i}}$$\end{document}

PCA converts the original variables into a new set of principal components (PC1, PC2, PC3, …), which are mutually uncorrelated and arranged in descending order according to the amount of variance they explain in the original dataset. Spatial representations of PC1, PC2, and PC3 reveal the ecological significance of each component. PC1 typically represents average vegetation productivity across the time series. Previous work demonstrated that the first principal component of NDVI time series captures broad-scale differences in vegetation vigor50,51. PC2 generally reflects temporal transitions in vegetation growth, particularly seasonal fluctuations and phenological contrast. This component effectively discriminates between phenological stages—such as peak greenness, senescence, and dormancy—and is therefore highly informative for monitoring seasonal landscapes. Similar interpretations have been reported by Justice et al.55, and Reed et al., (1994). PC3 often isolates abrupt or localized changes in vegetation condition, such as wildfire scars, drought impacts, clearing, or cropping cycles. These patterns are consistent with previous research, which found that higher-order PCA components were linked to disturbance processes52,56. Remaining components typically represent atmospheric residuals, sensor variability, or minor ecological fluctuations. Their low explained variance and lack of coherent spatial structure limit their practical ecological significance.

In this study, the analysis was focused on the second principal component (PC2) of the PCA.

To identify significant positive and negative anomalies based on PC2 values, we applied the fixed-threshold filtering method described by Jönsson and Eklundh57. It was possible to apply the same filter to all the NDVI temporal configurations (six, three, and two months) analyzed, since the variance of the PC2 data was low in all of them, and the difference in variance among them was negligible (max 0.13).

Therefore, the analysis of anomalies in PC2 must necessarily consider the period used to generate them and should be interpreted in relation to that period. In this study, however, we demonstrate that combining different temporal configurations enables the extraction of information that cannot be obtained from the analysis of a single period while remaining mutually consistent. This is evident from the analysis comparing the PC2 results with the land-cover classes. Indeed, when considering the entire six-month period, the results show that more than 90% of significant positive anomalies are concentrated in vegetation categories associated with forest formations. In contrast, almost 70% of significant negative anomalies affect herbaceous vegetation types, with nearly 20% affecting shrub ecosystems and only a small proportion affecting forest formations and other categories. This overall pattern is consistent with the quarterly results with the results on a quarterly basis. In the first quarter (May–July), 90% of significant positive anomalies occur in forest ecosystems, while 80% of significant negative anomalies are concentrated in herbaceous categories, 10% in shrub ecosystems and 5% in forest ecosystems. Around 80% of the significant positive anomalies in the second quarter (August–October) are concentrated in forest formations, with shrub and herbaceous formations accounting for 10% and 5% respectively. Almost 70% of significant negative anomalies were found in herbaceous formations, while shrub and forest formations accounted for 15% and 10% respectively. In conclusion, the six-month and quarterly analyses show that significant changes occur in the multitemporal NDVI series over the seven years considered. These changes are generally positive in forest ecosystems, where nearly 60% of areas show significant (mostly positive) anomalies over the six-month period, with an overall variation between 20% and 30% in the two quarterly periods. By contrast, the trend in herbaceous ecosystems is predominantly negative. Here, the proportion of areas showing significant anomalies (mostly negative) is almost 70% over the six-month period, varying between 45% and 50% in the two quarterly periods.

Shrublands, on the other hand, are the most stable ecosystem within the multitemporal NDVI series over the considered period, with 40% of areas showing significant anomalies (mostly negative) over the six-month period and around 20% in both quarters. In essence, we observe different impacts on NDVI changes across vegetation types, driven by factors we will analyse. These impacts are generally very positive for forests, highly negative for grasslands and moderately negative for shrublands.

Examining the vegetation categories more closely, we find that, over the six-month period, forest types exhibiting NDVI changes above average (60%) include beech forests, oak forests with Quercus cerris, Abies alba forests and Castanea sativa woodlands (Fig. 9). All these types show an overwhelming predominance of significant positive NDVI anomalies. In other forest habitats, the NDVI trend is generally more stable, with a clear tendency towards positive variability. However, some riparian forest types, such as Riverine scrub with willow and Southern riparian galleries, as well as Thermophilous oak forests, show a modest or more consistent negative variability in a generally stable context.

Discussion

Methodological rationale and consistency across temporal configurations

This study aims to develop and evaluate a robust methodology for identifying significant changes in the temporal dynamics of the NDVI, which is closely associated with vegetation condition and functional status. However, the identified anomalies are highly dependent on the study period, meaning the results cannot be generalised. Nevertheless, the detected changes still reflect the impact of factors influencing vegetation within the relatively limited temporal window considered. Furthermore, this time period is long enough to clearly identify these anomalies. From a methodological standpoint, the six-month period from May to October was extracted for each of the seven study years, as this corresponds to the vegetative growth window of the different vegetation types considered. The six-month window captures the core of the Mediterranean growing season, a period characterised by intense solar radiation and progressive water deficit, which are key ecological drivers in the region60. Therefore, this is the period during which variations in vegetative development can be detected, and NDVI can be a useful indicator. The first objective of this study is to demonstrate the applicability of the method across different combinations of NDVI time-series configurations. Accordingly, PCA was applied across the seven study years, analysing results for the entire six-month period and selected portions of it.

As previously mentioned, the aim is not to compare maps of significant anomalies (based on predefined thresholds) across different configurations (i.e., the six-month period, quarterly, or bimonthly). This is because areas classified as significant anomalies (i.e., those with absolute values exceeding the defined thresholds) tend to increase as the temporal analysis window becomes longer. Consequently, a quantitative comparison of the results across configurations is not meaningful. Furthermore, when transitioning from the six-month period to the quarterly and bimonthly configurations, it was evident that the significant-anomaly areas that were ‘lost’ were generally the least significant. This is evident from the configuration comparison, which shows that the mean absolute values of the second PCA component are markedly higher in areas where significant anomalies occur, such as where the six-month map overlaps with the quarterly maps and the quarterly maps overlap with the bimonthly maps, than in areas without overlap. Therefore, reducing the number of months included in the analysis effectively selects for anomaly pixels with higher significance. However, we are interested in evaluating trends in the index’s behaviour, i.e., verifying whether the different configurations to which the methodology is applied exhibit internal consistency. This consistency becomes clearer when the results are associated with vegetation types. In this regard, the categories identified by the Corine Biotopes map provide reliable and stable spatial and temporal data for almost all the mapped homogeneous environmental units for the period considered. Therefore, the six-month analysis provides an overview of trends in significant index variations across different groups and categories. In contrast, the quarterly and bimonthly configurations offer a basis for more detailed analysis and better interpretation of the results. The results across the various configurations are generally consistent. Indeed, when analysed at the different configuration levels, the index trends and the anomalies detected using the described methodology do not contradict each other across the vegetation types studied.

Based on the ancillary data available, it is therefore possible to hypothesize the causes underlying the observed anomalies. In particular, the influence of meteorological factors is evident, becoming even more significant when analysed not only in relation to broad categories (e.g., herbaceous, shrub and forest habitats), but also with respect to individual types. Such a differential response reflects the well-known ecological heterogeneity of Mediterranean ecosystems, where plant functional types exhibit contrasting adaptive strategies to cope with summer drought and inter annual climate variability61. This reflects the differing impacts while identifying a common trend across the broad categories.

Herbaceous ecosystems: predominance of negative anomalies and phenological shifts

Herbaceous habitats exhibit a clear predominance of negative anomalies in all analysed configurations. It is therefore reasonable to assume that atmospheric factors during the period under study negatively impacted the vegetation index in these ecosystems. Consistent with the high sensitivity of Mediterranean herbaceous communities to water stress, this finding aligns with the key ecological process that limits primary production during the dry season62. However, even within this general framework, individual types exhibit different behaviours. As the results show, Mediterranean xeric grasslands exhibit negative anomalies over at least two-thirds of their area during the spring–early summer period. This relatively stable condition persists until the end of summer, after which negative anomalies increase again, affecting approximately one-third of the area during the summer–autumn transition. Our interpretation of the observed pattern focuses on the annual life cycle of the dominant species in this habitat. These species have a rapid vegetative cycle, flowering in spring and drying out between June and July. This is followed by a summer dormancy phase, after which new seeds germinate with the first autumn rains. A classic adaptive strategy of Mediterranean therophytes to escape the harshest drought conditions is early flowering and summer dormancy63. It is possible that the progressively higher-than-average winter and spring temperatures during the study period may have caused an earlier onset of the annual cycle for many species, resulting in a vegetation index deficit relative to the normal trend as early as May. During the summer, the situation stabilises as the plants are usually already dormant. However, anomalies reappear at the beginning of autumn when there is insufficient water for the germination of dormant seeds. The critical role of rainfall timing for the regeneration niche of Mediterranean annual plants, a process often disrupted by climate change, is highlighted by this autumn bottleneck (Jongen et al., 2019).

In conclusion, the factors driving the negative anomalies in Mediterranean xeric grasslands may be progressively increasing temperatures in late spring–early summer and decreasing precipitation at the beginning of autumn. Mediterranean tall-grass habitats exhibit a pattern similar to that of Mediterranean xeric grasslands, albeit with substantial differences. Firstly, a lower proportion of the area is affected by significant anomalies (between 40% and 50%). Secondly, unlike Mediterranean xeric grasslands, the percentage of negative anomalies is higher during the summer–autumn transition than during the late spring–early summer period. That difference can be explained by the greater prevalence of perennial grasses with deeper root systems, which are more resilient to early season water deficits but still suffer from cumulative summer drought64; Volaire, 2018). This type is likely influenced by the greater abundance of perennial species compared with xeric grasslands, leading to a lower incidence of meteorological–climatic factors. A different pattern is observed in the Mesic grassland type, where higher temperatures may advance the life cycle, which normally reaches its vegetative peak in June–July. This shift leads to an earlier drying phase, predominantly during this period rather than in late spring as seen in Mediterranean xeric grasslands and Mediterranean tall-grass habitat. This results in significant negative anomalies over a substantial portion of the area (30–40%) during the second bimonthly period. The July–August–September interval is relatively stable, but negative anomalies increase again during the summer–autumn transition due to insufficient precipitation to support autumn germination. Overall, the total area affected by negative anomalies is estimated to be between 60% and 70%.

Mediterranean-montane grasslands, significant negative anomalies affecting 30–40% of the area occur over a longer interval in the early phase (June–August), decrease in September and increase again in October. The shorter growing season and later snowmelt in mountain environments, where vegetation is particularly vulnerable to both advanced spring warming and autumn water deficits, are reflected in this pattern65,66. As in other cases, the interpretation of the results must consider the characteristic annual cycle, which typically shows a reduction in vegetative activity only at the end of summer within the multi-year period examined. Therefore, the anomalies emerge while vegetative activity is ongoing and again at the beginning of autumn due to insufficient water availability preventing new germination. Of all the herbaceous formations, Mediterranean-montane grasslands are the only ones to show significant positive anomalies, concentrated in the two seasonal transition periods and likely attributable to specific local conditions. The behaviour of the Xeric grassland type is completely different, with significant negative anomalies increasing steadily from June to October. This type exhibits a high proportion of significant anomalies during the relatively stable August–September period. These anomalies affect high-altitude grasslands on steep slopes. In such environments, shallow soils and high drainage amplify the effect of water deficits, a typical ecological constraint in alpine and subalpine Mediterranean habitats65. The marked increase in the second quarter is likely to be associated with water deficits that impact the vegetation before the summer–autumn transition. Overall, it is estimated that the area affected by negative anomalies ranges between 45% and 55%. The two synanthropic herbaceous types also exhibit different index behaviour and associated anomalies. The first type, ‘Permanent Pastures’, includes pasture–meadow systems, degraded fertilised meadows, abandoned pastures and post-cultivation vegetation. These semi natural systems are often in an early successional stage, characterised by ruderal species with high phenotypic plasticity but low resistance to prolonged drought67. In this case, significant negative anomalies affect 70–75% of the area, with two peaks occurring in June–July (due to an advanced phenological cycle) and September–October (due to a lack of vegetative regrowth). However, significant anomalies, albeit with lower percentages, are also present in other the two-monthly periods.

The second type (sub-nitrophilous Mediterranean grasslands) includes post-cultivation grasslands, abandoned pastures and ruderal formations. A later successional stage following abandonment is represented by this type, often dominated by nitrophilous and drought tolerant species (e.g., Bromus, Diplotaxis), which are better adapted to the typical Mediterranean summer stress68. Significant negative anomalies affect 60–65% of the area, with the most relevant periods being May–July (due to an advanced phenological cycle) and October (due to a lack of vegetative regrowth). The other temporal intervals are of limited importance. The first category includes more mesophilic ecosystems, which reach their vegetative peak in June–July. The second category is characterised by a higher proportion of xerophilic species and an earlier vegetative peak, with slightly lower NDVI variability overall. The second habitat encompasses more stabilised formations, while the first includes areas that were abandoned more recently, with relatively richer soils and therefore a greater presence of mesophilic species. A classic ecological process in Mediterranean abandoned farmlands, leading to a more stable but less productive system, is the successional pathway from ruderal annual grasslands to perennial, drought tolerant communities69. Nevertheless, the first category also includes areas that are still managed as meadow-pasture, a practice far less common in the second category.

Forest ecosystems: prevalence of positive anomalies and phenological extension

In forest ecosystems, the most notable finding is the prevalence of substantial positive anomalies across various types, including Beech forests, oak forests with Quercus cerris, Castanea sativa woodlands, mixed broadleaf forests, Abies alba forests, and Coniferous woodlands. In the absence of severe drought, many Mediterranean forest types benefit from warmer temperatures and a prolonged growing season: a pattern consistent with the concept of ‘greening’ under climate warming in water limited ecosystems, provided that water is not the primary limiting factor70. Beech forests, and Abies albaforests in particular, show a strong tendency to increase the index across all configurations. The difference lies in the seasonal pattern: in Beech forests, this tendency is more pronounced during the summer–autumn transition than during the spring–summer period. A known ecological response to warmer autumn temperatures is delayed senescence in beech, which can extend carbon uptake into the dry late summer period but also increase the risk of hydraulic failure under extreme drought (Gárate Escamilla et al., 202071;. In Abies alba forests, however, the opposite is observed. Analyses based on quarterly configurations also show very low overlap between the first and second quarters in Abies alba forests: 80% of areas exhibiting significant positive anomalies in the second quarter do not overlap with those in the first quarter. This temporal shift indicates that fir forests experience an advanced spring reactivation, a process that can decouple their phenology from historical climate norms, potentially increasing vulnerability to late frosts72.

By contrast, Beech forests are largely affected by significant positive variations in the index, with up to 90% of their area showing such increases. During the period under study, it is plausible that increased temperatures led to an earlier onset of vegetative regrowth in spring and summer, particularly in Beech forests and even more so in Abies alba forests. Furthermore, an extension of the vegetative period was observed, which was particularly pronounced in Beech forests (delayed leaf fall) and, to a lesser extent, in Abies alba forests.

When analysing the four oak-dominated forest types (Quercus cerris forests, Q. cerris-Q. frainetto forests, Quercus pubescens forests, Thermophylous oak woodlands), it is interesting to highlight the differences that emerge, bearing in mind that the degree of thermophily increases and the altitudinal distribution decreases in the order listed above. The contrasting water use strategies among Mediterranean oaks (Q. cerris, a more mesic deciduous oak, vs. Q. pubescens and thermophilous oaks, more drought tolerant), are reflected in this gradient73,74. Oak forests dominated by Quercus cerris exhibit significant positive anomalies over a substantial area (estimated at 25% −40%), primarily in the early phase (May–June). The same applies to Quercus cerris–Quercus frainetto forests, albeit over a smaller total area (15–30%). Significant negative anomalies are also observed in these forest types, which is notable because they affect a substantial area (estimated at approximately 300 hectares for oak forests with Q. cerris and 100 ha for Q. cerris–Q. frainetto forests). Even if the percentage of the total area affected is low (2–3%), it still occurs at the end of the summer period. Incipient water stress that may be exacerbated by climate change, leading to canopy desiccation and increased mortality in drought prone years, is pointed to by this late summer negative anomaly in mesic oak forests75. Contributing factors may include fires (affecting approximately 200 hectares of Q. cerris forests), logging, and canopy drying due to water stress.

For the other two oak-dominated types, significant positive anomalies are concentrated around the summer–autumn transition, although they are over small areas (less than 10% for Quercus pubescens forests and less than 5% for Thermophilus oak forests). Additionally, both types exhibit a slightly higher, albeit modest, incidence of negative anomalies (5% for the former and 10% for the latter). In Thermophilus oak forests, significant negative anomalies are more widespread than positive ones. Even drought adapted sclerophyllous oaks can experience stress when water deficits are prolonged, highlighting the limits of their physiological adaptations75. Thus, while the index generally remains stable across these two types, some areas show increases and others decreases, particularly during the September–October period. Therefore, it is plausible that in some areas, increasing temperatures delay the onset of autumn vegetative decline. In contrast, in other areas, the progressive reduction in rainfall contributes to vegetative stress or canopy drying. This manifests as negative anomalies in September–October in more degraded areas or those with lower tree density. There is also a modest contribution of fires to negative anomalies in both types, and some areas are presumably affected by logging. Nevertheless, after discounting the effects of fires and potential mapping errors or logging, it is possible to estimate a sensitive area of approximately 300 hectares for the four oak-dominated forest types. This corresponds to more than 1% of the total area of oak forests and shows significant negative anomalies attributable to drought. The most notable feature across other broadleaf forest types (dominated by Ostrya and Alnus species) and Castanea sativa stands is the extension of the vegetative season, as evidenced by positive anomalies during September–October. The most notable finding across all riparian-formation types is the presence of both positive and significant negative anomalies (affecting 10–15% of the area) during the September–October bimonthly period, which are modest in absolute terms but significant. Riparian vegetation, though adapted to higher water availability, is highly sensitive to reductions in summer baseflow and groundwater levels in consequence of prolonged drought in Mediterranean catchments76. As this vegetation is closely linked to water availability, the general trend towards a reduction in the index during the summer–autumn transition may be related to a water deficit. Conversely, positive anomalies, when present, may indicate an extension of the vegetative season under conditions of sufficient water availability (or a slowing of senescence). Overall, the total area affected by negative anomalies in riparian formations is estimated to range from 10% to 30%, depending on the type, while positive anomalies affect 5–10% of the area.

Shrubland ecosystems: stability, negative trends and fire interactions

Regarding shrub formations, the two dominant types — distinguishable by their altitudinal distribution (hilly to mountainous for Temperate thickets and scrubs, and more strictly hilly for Sub-mediterranean thickets and brushes) — exhibit a predominance of negative anomalies from June to October. Shrublands in Mediterranean ecosystems often act as a transitional or persistent community following disturbance, with a high resilience to fire but a mixed response to drought depending on species composition (e.g., Cistus spp. vs. Quercus coccifera)77. These anomalies are estimated to affect between 20% and 30% of the area occupied by Temperate thickets and scrubs, and between 25% and 35% of the area occupied by Sub-Mediterranean thickets and brushes. However, for the latter, the high fire incidence must be taken into account. Fire is a recurrent ecological process in Mediterranean shrublands, and the observed negative anomalies could reflect both direct combustion effects and post fire regeneration phases, which are naturally part of the ecosystem dynamics but may be altered by climate change60,78. Positive anomalies in the temperate thickets and scrubs (affecting 5–10% of the total area) and, to a lesser extent, in the sub-Mediterranean thickets and brushes are likely due to an increased presence of tree cover. Significant negative changes in the index have also affected the Mediterranean maquis, over an estimated 15–20% of its area. Such negative anomalies indicate that even this highly adapted vegetation type is not immune to the cumulative effects of rising temperatures and prolonged water deficits79. The contribution of fires should also be considered here, probably affecting one-third of the area with significant negative anomalies. However, these changes are primarily due to reduced water availability and increased evapotranspiration associated with higher temperatures. Overall, the study found that Mediterranean shrublands and maquis ecosystems were negatively impacted, with significant changes predominantly reflecting the observed meteorological conditions during the study period.

Methodological significance and broader ecological context and implications

In conclusion, vegetation responds differently to the same meteorological conditions depending on its type, life cycle, site conditions, and health status. This differential vulnerability is a hallmark of the ecological complexity of Mediterranean landscapes, where sharp gradients in aridity, soil depth, and disturbance history create a mosaic of responses63; Scarascia Mugnozza et al., 2000). These effects can be detected even over a relatively short period, such as the one considered here. Herbaceous and shrubland ecosystems consistently exhibit negative changes in the vegetation index, as evidenced by significant negative shifts in the NDVI series. The extent to which these reductions occur varies by vegetation type, ranging from 30 to 70% in herbaceous habitats and 10–30% in shrublands and Mediterranean maquis. On the other hand, forest ecosystems are mostly positively influenced by the meteorological conditions over the seven-year period considered. Consequently, the vegetation index shows significant positive changes, though these vary considerably across forest types. Significant positive NDVI anomalies range from 3 to 4% in Thermophilus oak forests to 90% in beech forests. However, riparian forests are an exception to this general trend, showing a more pronounced tendency towards a reduction in the vegetation index. Depending on the specific type, this affects an estimated 10–30% of the area. In the more Thermophilus oak forests, the positive effects observed in other forest types are largely absent or very limited. Instead, there are signs of a negative trend in the vegetation index in some areas, with significant anomalies affecting 5–10% of the surface area.

The results of this study demonstrate that it is possible to identify significant changes in the NDVI vegetation index over a comparatively brief temporal window, even with a relatively short NDVI time series, using the proposed methodology. When this is extended to a longer timescale and meteorological and climatic conditions are consistent with those observed during the study period, these results could indicate significant impacts on vegetation efficiency and functional performance with potential ecological consequences for the involved ecosystems. Substantial literature is available on these aspects, and it is appropriate to refer to it, at least in part, in order to contextualise the findings of this study and assess its theoretical implications. Rising temperatures, more frequent and intense droughts, and altered rainfall regimes — all of which are associated with climate change — lead to a progressive loss of soil humus and nitrogen in herbaceous ecosystems, resulting in reduced biodiversity and declining ecosystem stability and productivity. These changes further contribute to soil erosion and an increased risk of desertification, as well as reducing the capacity to sequester carbon80,81. These processes are particularly acute in the Mediterranean basin, which is a global hotspot of climate change and desertification risk82. Our study has highlighted a possible link between negative anomalies and shifts in grassland phenology. Several studies83,84have demonstrated that even minor phenological alterations can result in significant ecosystem-level impacts, notably altering species complementarity and potentially leading to severe consequences for the conservation of these ecosystems. Beard et al.85, describe phenological mismatches between species in herbaceous ecosystems and their impact on plant–pollinator interactions, productivity and carbon and energy balances.

The findings presented here appear to support the view that herbaceous ecosystems may experience increased variability driven by altered seasonality under intensified climate-change conditions. This is likely to result from more severe summer droughts and less frequent but more intense rainfall66. Such changes directly affect the typical Mediterranean rainfall regime, where the timing of autumn rains is crucial for seed germination and the spring peak determines maximum biomass accumulation (Jongen et al., 2019). Furthermore, changes in rainfall patterns are likely to increase stress in herbaceous species inhabiting water-sensitive ecosystems. However, our understanding of the specific effects of altered precipitation patterns on the various phenophases of herbaceous species remains limited86. Grünzweig87, highlight that the imbalance between precipitation and evapotranspiration is the main factor shaping the spatial and temporal patterns of biological processes in many herbaceous ecosystems. One of the most concerning aspects of climate change is the projected increase in aridity across grasslands worldwide, as this could lead to abrupt and widespread changes in the physical, biological and chemical properties of herbaceous ecosystems, particularly with regard to the carbon cycle88,89. Under prolonged water-deficit conditions, plants reduce their leaf area and photosynthetic activity, leading to significant reductions in primary productivity and vegetation cover90. Soil microbial communities are also impacted by limited water availability, experiencing changes in size, composition, and growth91. This, in turn, slows carbon cycling and soil decomposition processes. Under such conditions, declines in soil organic matter, microbial biomass and soil nutrient concentrations are commonly observed92.

This decline is often accompanied by changes in the composition of of plants, soil fauna and microbial communities. In herbaceous ecosystems, there is typically an increase in xerophilous species at the expense of mesophilous ones. Such shifts can have negative consequences, including the dominance of non-native species and biodiversity loss. This affects multiple ecosystem processes93,94. In the context of Mediterranean grasslands, this shift represents a functional simplification, often leading to the dominance of a few highly competitive ruderal or annual species, reducing the overall resilience of the ecosystem95. The impact of land abandonment on significant changes in NDVI time series is particularly complex to assess, as subsequent vegetation dynamics may be influenced by a large number of anthropogenic and natural factors29,96–100.

Our results suggest a correspondence between the vegetation dynamics reported in the literature, particularly in semi-arid ecosystems following land abandonment, and the observed NDVI trends. Indeed, a high percentage of negative anomalies is observed in the vegetation types occupying areas closest to the time of abandonment, namely ‘Permanent pastures’ (which include both abandoned areas and grasslands/pastures) and ‘Sub-nitrophilous Mediterranean grasslands’. However, significant anomalies are more pronounced in the former than in the latter. It can essentially be hypothesised that the ‘Permanent pastures’ type also includes grasslands that formed immediately after the abandonment of agricultural fields, with a predominance of annual, mostly mesophilous herbaceous species. This would explain the greater impact, as evidenced by significant negative NDVI anomalies during the study period. In contrast, Sub-nitrophilous Mediterranean grasslands may largely represent a subsequent stage of succession following agricultural abandonment. That successional pathway, from ruderal annual grasslands to perennial, drought tolerant communities, is a classic ecological process in Mediterranean abandoned farmlands, often leading to a more stable but less productive system69. This stage is dominated by drought-tolerant or opportunistic plants that are relatively better adapted to increased temperatures and water deficits.

Shrublands are a stage in the succession of grassland ecosystems (and, less frequently, of degraded forests). They can therefore be considered replacement shrublands, whose ecological role has positive and negative aspects. Positive effects include protecting the soil, increasing vegetation cover and promoting succession82,101, while negative effects include replacing mature or open habitats, leading to the loss of specialised species and increasing fire risk102,103. In Mediterranean ecosystems, shrub encroachment is often a double edged sword: it can enhance carbon sequestration and soil stability but also alter fire regimes and reduce herbaceous biodiversity101 b). Our study indicates that shrubland ecosystems are generally more stable than herbaceous ecosystems with respect to vegetation index variability related to meteorological and climatic factors. However, significant changes were predominantly negative, reflecting the impact of local environmental stressors during the study period. Of particular interest are the findings for Mediterranean maquis. Despite its relatively high adaptive capacity, it may face increasing stress due to rising temperatures, more frequent droughts and intensifying climate extremes in the Mediterranean Basin104. This could lead to reduced plant community diversity and shifts in species composition, favouring some drought-tolerant shrubs over others. It could also result in a reduced capacity for ecosystem functions such as soil stabilisation, carbon storage and habitat provision105.

In terms of the theoretical implications of the results of this study for forest ecosystems, it is important to note that a number of recent studies have shown that environmental changes linked to global warming are significantly affecting forest phenology106; Tang et al., 2016;107–112. Several studies have demonstrated that early spring warming can lead to earlier leafing and the onset of tree growth113,114. However, this early onset may also be associated with reduced or prematurely ceased tree growth due to water stress or radiation-induced leaf ageing115,116. In Mediterranean forests, that trade off between early greening and summer water stress is a critical determinant of tree health and survival, particularly for deciduous species like oaks and beech at the drier limits of their range (Diaz-Martinez et al., 2023). Increases in spring temperature cause earlier leaf unfolding, but may also result in higher water consumption, which could intensify drought stress during the summer months109; Salomón and Camarero, 2024). This latter aspect was not particularly evident in our study, although a limited tendency in this regard was observed in certain forest types, such as Quercus cerris forests, Q. frainetto–Q. cerris forests and Coniferous plantations. For information on the vegetative decline of various Quercus species in relation to climate change, see Sánchez-Salguero et al., (2020) and Conte et al.,) and117. Although the processes underlying variations in spring phenology are well understood, the same cannot be said for autumn phenological patterns, which are still in the early stages of study118. The factors and interactions controlling autumn phenology are certainly more complex than the mechanisms regulating spring phenology119,120. It is most likely that, in addition to temperature, photoperiod and light intensity also significantly affect leaf senescence17,121,122.

Contrary to what Delpierre et al.123, reported, that senescence begins only when both temperature and photoperiod simultaneously reach specific thresholds. Lang et al.124, found that autumn senescence can be induced independently by temperature and photoperiod125,126 found that climate change induces earlier leaf senescence due to increased springtime vegetation activity, but the progression is slower, resulting in a later end to senescence. Therefore, rising temperatures do not delay autumn leaf senescence; rather, they slow down its rate of progression.

In a study of European beech, Fu et al.71, found that increases in summer and autumn temperatures significantly delayed leaf senescence, even in the absence of water or nutrient limitations. The same authors also observed that temperature increases have a greater impact on autumn senescence than spring leafing in beech, suggesting that delays in autumn senescence contribute more to extending the growing season under future warming conditions than advances in spring leafing. Our study fully supports this observation. In general, the impact of climate change on on vegetation phenology affects the global carbon balance127. This influences the exchange of energy, carbon and water between the atmosphere and terrestrial surfaces, consequently affecting the global carbon budget. Higher temperatures over a prolonged period may result in faster tree growth in some montane forests. However, this is not guaranteed everywhere; some studies of European mountain ecosystems have observed that a lengthening of the growing season does not always result in proportional increases in productivity128.

As temperatures rise, many tree species may ‘migrate’ to higher elevations: thermophilic species (those that prefer warmer temperatures) can colonise higher slopes. This could alter the composition of Apennine forests, with some ‘classic’ high-altitude species being outcompeted or replaced by those better suited to the warmer climate129. An upward shift is already being documented in Mediterranean mountain ranges, where relict populations of mesic species (e.g., beech, silver fir) are increasingly threatened by competition from more thermophilic oaks and pines130,131.

Quantitative validation: correlation with meteorological anomalies

The quantitative correlation (r = 0.58, lag 1 month) between the De Martonne aridity anomaly and PC2 vegetation anomalies provides a robust, statistically significant validation of our methodological approach. This result directly addresses concerns about whether the detected anomalies reflect actual ecological responses rather than data-driven variability or methodological choices. In ecological time series analysis, correlations between meteorological drivers and vegetation indices are often modest132,133 due to multiple confounding factors (soil heterogeneity, land use history, species composition, fire, human intervention). Thus, r = 0.58 is at the upper end of what is typically observed in Mediterranean regions, where vegetation response is buffered by soil water storage, plant functional adaptations, and non-climatic disturbances. This value indicates a strong ecological signal that is not easily dismissed as random noise or methodological artefact.

The coefficient of determination r² = 0.34 means that approximately one-third of the variance in PC2 anomalies can be directly explained by the previous month’s aridity anomaly. The remaining 66% of variance is attributable to local factors (soil depth, aspect, slope, elevation), land use history (abandonment, grazing pressure, fire events), biological legacies (species composition, successional stage), non-linear responses (threshold effects, drought memory) and other meteorological variables (wind, radiation, humidity). Crucially, the fact that a single, simple meteorological index (De Martonne) explains 34% of the variance in PC2, despite these confounding factors, strongly supports the ecological validity of our PCA-based anomaly detection. The one-month lag is consistent with plant physiological responses: water deficit first affects stomatal conductance, then leaf water potential, then chlorophyll content and canopy greenness. This cascade typically requires 2–6 weeks to become detectable in NDVI134,135. Therefore, the absence of significant correlation at lag 0 and the emergence of a strong correlation at lag 1 month reinforce the causal interpretation: meteorological stress precedes vegetation response, not vice versa22,59,125,136–151.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that combining multitemporal NDVI analysis with PCA is an effective method for identifying significant vegetation anomalies. Negative anomalies predominantly affected herbaceous formations, reflecting earlier phenological cycles, higher temperatures and reduced autumn precipitation. Shrublands and Mediterranean maquis also showed mainly negative trends, though these ecosystems were more stable than herbaceous ones. In contrast, forest ecosystems generally exhibited positive anomalies, indicating extended growing seasons and enhanced photosynthetic activity, particularly in Beech and Abies alba forests. However, exceptions included riparian and Thermophilus oak-dominated forests, where negative anomalies were associated with localised water stress, canopy drying and human-caused disturbances. The study highlights that climate variability and land use changes impact vegetation differently depending on species composition, life cycle and site conditions. The proposed methodology is a valuable tool for monitoring vegetation dynamics, assessing ecosystem resilience, and supporting sustainable environmental management amid changing climatic conditions.

Our study could open up interesting avenues for future research, particularly in long-term monitoring, phenology research, ecosystem-specific responses, and climate change modelling. In terms of methodology, improving temporal resolution, integrating multi-source data, and adopting machine learning could enhance the detection and interpretation of anomalies. From an ecological perspective, identifying the mechanistic links between NDVI anomalies, vegetation phenology, and environmental stressors could provide actionable insights for conservation and management.
