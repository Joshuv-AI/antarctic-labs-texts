Sorting nexin 10 promotes triple-negative breast cancer progression through modulating cholesterol biosynthesis via stabilizing SREBP2
Authors: Yinping Guo, Jiahui Li, Wenjie Zhang, Min Xia, Gaohua Liu, Xiaolin Zhong, Yan Yi, Yajun Chen, Jing Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) lacks effective therapeutics in clinical practice. Sorting nexin 10 (SNX10) plays essential roles in various cancers, and the role of SNX10 in TNBC remains unknown. This study investigated the role and action mechanism of SNX10 in TNBC.MethodsUsing the Kaplan–Meier Plotter database and bc-GenExMiner database to analyze the SNX10 expression and clinical significance. Functional assays, including MTT, colony formation, transwell assays, and xenograft experiments, were used to evaluate the role of SNX10 in TNBC. The GeneChip PrimeView Human Gene Expression Array was used to determine the downstream targets and pathways. Cholesterol levels and the expression of enriched cholesterol synthesis genes were detected by Filipin assay, RT-PCR, and Western Blot. The relationship of SNX10 and cholesterol biosynthesis was confirmed using CO-IP and immunofluorescence staining assays.ResultsSNX10 is positively related to poor prognosis of breast cancer and markedly up-regulated in TNBC. Knockdown of SNX10 suppressed cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, and slowed the tumor growth in vivo. Mechanistically, SNX10 positively regulates the content and distribution of cellular cholesterol, as does the expression of enriched cholesterol synthesis genes, and SNX10 interacts with SREBP2 and prolongs its half-life. Blocking with lovastatin, cholesterol, or siSREBP2 reversed these effects caused by altering SNX10 in TNBC cells in vitro.ConclusionSNX10 promoted TNBC progression through the regulation of cholesterol biosynthesis, which was mediated by interaction with SREBP2 and maintenance of its stability. SNX10 may be an effective therapeutic target for TNBC, and our study provides new insights into therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most commonly diagnosed cancers and the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. 1-3 Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a subgroup of breast cancer that constitutes approximately 15%–20% of all breast cancers. 4 , 5 Current treatment options for TNBC are limited to adjuvant chemotherapy, surgery, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy, in which adjuvant chemotherapy is the main approach. 5-7 Because of the lack of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), patients with TNBC are not sensitive to established endocrine therapy or HER2-targeted drugs, and still do not respond well to programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) monotherapy, which leads to high invasiveness, high metastatic potential, proneness to relapse, and poor prognosis. 8-10 Therefore, it is necessary to explore the molecular mechanisms underlying TNBC to identify potential therapeutic targets.

Sorting nexin 10 (SNX10) belongs to the sorting nexin (SNX) family, which contains a phox homology (PX) domain that binds to the phosphoinositide on the membrane structure and localizes to endosomes, where it plays an important role in signaling transduction, endosome homeostasis, and intracellular protein trafficking and stabilization. 11-13 SNX10 is highly expressed in the brain, bone, and gut, in which it mediates bone mineral and calcium homeostasis, respectively. 12-14 In recent years, research on SNX10 has also mainly focused on cancers. 12 , 15 In colorectal cancer (CRC), the protein and mRNA levels of SNX10 are significantly decreased in tumor tissues and negatively associated with tumor differentiation, tumor node metastasis classification (TNM), and lymph node metastasis, and knockout of SNX10 significantly increases tumor size and number in the AOM-DSS mouse model, suggesting that SNX10 acts as a tumor suppressor in CRC. 15-17 In stomach adenocarcinoma, SNX10 is weakly expressed in tumor tissues and cell lines, and is negatively linked to poor prognosis. 18 , 19 In glioblastoma, high SNX10 expression is correlated with poor prognosis, and the knockdown of SNX10 inhibits cell proliferation and viability and reduces the self-renewal capacity of glioblastoma stem cells. 20 In squamous cell carcinoma of esophageal and cervical cancer, the expression of SNX10 increases in tumor tissues. 21-23 To date, only two studies have evaluated the role of SNX10 in breast cancer. One study showed that SNX10 deficiency inhibits HER2 recycling, promotes HER2 trafficking into lysosomes, and causes anti-HER2 antibody-drug conjugate resistance in HER2-positive breast cancer. 24 The other showed that SNX10 is transcriptionally regulated by CBX3, physically interacts with the phospholipid scramblase ANO6, maintains protein stability through suppressing proteasome-mediated degradation, and promotes metastasis. 25 However, the role of SNX10 in TNBC remains unknown. Above all, SNX10 may play different roles in various cancers, these findings inspired us that exploring the role and mechanism of SNX10 in TNBC may be crucial for developing and applying SNX10.

Dysregulation of lipid metabolism is the most prominent metabolic alteration in cancer. 26 , 27 Cholesterol is an essential lipid component of the cell membrane, and its homeostasis is linked to the regulation of cellular and body metabolism. 28-30 Cholesterol homeostasis depends on the precise coordination of four metabolic modules, which include de novo biosynthesis, intestinal absorption, conversion, and clearance. 31 Cholesterol homeostasis is mainly transcriptionally regulated by sterol regulatory element (SRE) binding protein 2 (SREBP2), liver X receptors (LXRs), and nuclear factor erythroid 2 related factor 1 (NRF1), in which SREBP2 participates in regulation of cholesterol biosynthesis and uptake, LXRs involves up-regulation the expression of gene related to cholesterol efflux, and NRF1 takes part in the inhibition of LXR pathway. 32-34 As for cholesterol biosynthesis, 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and squalene monooxygenase are two key factors, which are both transcriptionally regulated by SREBP2. 30 , 31 Reprogramming of cholesterol homeostasis may cause cholesterol accumulation, dysregulation of cholesterol balance, and alteration of immune function, thereby influencing cardiovascular diseases and cancer progression. 33-35 Therefore, the regulation of cholesterol homeostasis is essential for cancer progression.

Here, we studied the role and action mechanism of SNX10 in TNBC. We found that knockdown of SNX10 inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of TNBC cells, as well as tumor growth, in vivo. Regarding the molecular mechanism, SNX10 positively regulated the cholesterol biosynthesis pathways by interacting with SREBP2 and maintaining its stability. Additionally, lovastatin, cholesterol, or siREBP2 could reverse the effect mediated by altering SNX10. These findings revealed the role of SNX10 on cholesterol homeostasis in TNBC, and provided new potential targets for the therapeutic strategy for TNBC.

Results

Knockdown of SNX10 inhibits the progression of TNBC both in vivo and in vitro

To identify the potential role of SNX10 in breast cancer, we analyzed the expression levels of SNX10 in a public database. The Kaplan–Meier plotter results showed that the expression of SNX10 was positively correlated with worse overall survival (OS) and relapse-free survival (RFS) among all breast cancer cohorts (HR = 1.29 (1.03 – 1.62), log rank p = 0.026, HR = 1.17 (1.06 – 1.3), log rank p = 0.0025) (Figure 1A, B). Bc-genexminer v5.2 database showed that SNX10 displayed higher expression in breast cancer with higher levels of Ki67, lymph node metastases, and in TNBC (Figure 1C–E). These findings reveal that SNX10 expression is positively associated with poor outcomes in breast cancer. As the role of SNX10 in TNBC remains unknown, to determine the biological function, overexpression of SNX10 significantly increased cell proliferation and colony formation capability compared with the vector group, while SNX10 knockdown (SNX10 KD) showed an opposite effect in MDA-MB-231 and HCC1806 cells (Figure 1F–K, Figure S1 A–F). Transwell assays showed that the migration and invasion capabilities of MDA-MB-231 and HCC1806 cells were enhanced in the SNX10 overexpression group, while knockdown of SNX10 showed an inhibitory effect compared with the NC group (Figure 1L–M, Figure S1 G–H). Moreover, to validate these findings in vivo, we observed that tumors from the SNX10 knockdown group grew slower than tumors from the vector group, as did tumor size, weight, and total fluorescence (Figure 1N–Q). These data suggest that SNX10 plays a crucial role in regulating the progression of TNBC, in which the knockdown of SNX10 shows a significant anti-tumor effect.

Knockdown of SNX10 lowers the cholesterol levels in TNBC cells

To explore the mechanism by which SNX10 promotes the progression, we scanned a GeneChip PrimeView Human Gene expression array in MDA-MB-231 cells with or without SNX10 knockdown, accompanied by Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA). A total of 1137 genes increased in the SNX10 knockdown group, while 1362 genes showed a decreasing trend compared to the control group (Figure 2A). Focusing on pathway enrichment by IPA analysis (Figure 2B), 15 canonical pathways were enriched, of which 5 pathways were activated significantly (Z-score ≥ 2), and 10 pathways were inhibited significantly (Z-score ≤ -2). Among these pathways, four cholesterol biosynthesis pathways were enriched, indicating that SNX10 may play an important role in regulating cholesterol homeostasis. To verify this hypothesis, we found that overexpression of SNX10 could increase the cholesterol content compared with the vector group, while knockdown of SNX10 displayed an opposite effect on MDA-MB-231 and HCC1806 cells (Figure 2C–F). Filipin staining showed that overexpression of SNX10 increased the distribution of cellular cholesterol, while knockdown of SNX10 decreased the distribution of cholesterol in MDA-MB-231 and HCC1806 cells (Figure 2G–J). These results indicated that SNX10 is positively correlated with cholesterol levels and plays a potential role in regulating cholesterol homeostasis.

SNX10 regulates cholesterol biosynthesis in TNBC cells

To further determine the role of SNX10 in cholesterol homeostasis, we also extracted the expression of genes enriched in cholesterol biosynthesis from GeneChip PrimeView Human Gene expression array. Besides key regulatory factors SREBP2, EBP, ACAT2, DHCR24, HSD17B7, MSMO1, SC5D, and CYP51A1 were also shown a significant decreased level in SNX10 knockdown group (Figure 3A). In MDA-MB-231 and HCC1806 cells, compared with the vector group, overexpression of SNX10 increased the mRNA levels of these enriched genes, as well as HMGCR, SCAP, and SREBP1, while knockdown of SNX10 could decrease them significantly by RT-PCR (Figure 3B–E). As cholesterol biosynthesis is mainly regulated by HMGCR and squalene monooxygenase, and SREBP2 can transcriptionally regulate cholesterol synthesis genes, 30 , 36 , 37 we focused on SREBP2 and HMGCR in a subsequent study. At the protein level, overexpression of SNX10 increased the levels of SREBP2, SREBP1, HMGCR, and SCAP in MDA-MB-231 and HCC1806 cells, whereas knockdown of SNX10 decreased their levels compared to those in the control group (Figure 3F, G, Figure S2 A–D). These data imply that SNX10 could positively regulate the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway through affecting the mRNA and protein levels of its key regulatory factors.

SNX10 interacts with SREBP2 and promotes its stability

Considering the crucial regulatory role of SNX10 in the endosomal/lysosomal pathway, 38 , 39 we next investigated whether SNX10 interacts with these key regulatory factors, which could be essential for their stability. First, Co-IP assays showed that SNX10 interacted with SREBP2, especially the N-terminal of mature SREBP2 (nSREBP2), but not SREBP1 or HMGCR in MDA-MB-231 and HCC1806 cells transfected with SNX10 overexpression plasmids (Figure 4A, B). Next, we co-transfected SNX10-Flag and SREBP2-HA overexpression plasmids into cells, and both full-length SREBP2 (pSREBP2) and nSREBP2 were detected in SNX10-Flag immunoprecipitates from the lysates (Figure 4C). Immunofluorescence staining showed that SNX10 physically co-localized with SREBP2 in MDA-MB-231 cells (co-localization rate: 72.12% ± 2.48%) and in HCC1806 cells (co-localization rate: 95.65% ± 0.67%), as well as in both the nucleus (co-localization rate: 85.41% ± 3.65% in MDA-MB-2321 cells and 74.04% ± 5.16% in HCC1806 cells) and cytoplasm (co-localization rate: 59.76% ± 3.60% in MDA-MB-2321 cells and 97.61% ± 0.62% in HCC1806 cells) (Figure 4D, E). These data demonstrate that SNX10 physically co-localizes and interacts with SREBP2 in TNBC cells. Next, we determined whether SNX10 regulates the stability of SREBP2. Cycloheximide (CHX) was used to treat TNBC cells with or without SNX10 overexpression to block translation in a time-course manner. Overexpression of SNX10 prolonged SREBP2 half-life significantly compared to the vector group in MDA-MB-231 and HCC1806 cells (Figure 4F, G). These results demonstrated that SNX10 interacts with SREBP2 and maintains its stability.

SNX10 promotes progression of TNBC through modulation of cholesterol homeostasis

To determine the pro-tumor effect of SNX10 in TNBC through cholesterol biosynthesis, lovastatin (MCE, USA) was used to lower cholesterol. As shown in Figure 5A–D, overexpression of SNX10 promoted cell proliferation and colony formation capability, whereas the addition of lovastatin inhibited these effects induced by SNX10 overexpression in MDA-MB-231 and HCC1806 cells. Additionally, the migration and invasion capabilities were increased in the SNX10 overexpression group but decreased after the addition of lovastatin in MDA-MB-231 and HCC1806 cells (Figure 5E–H). Similarly, the addition of cholesterol reversed the effects of knockdown SNX10 on cell proliferation, colony-formation capability, migration, and invasion capability in MDA-MB-231 and HCC1806 cells (Figure 5I–P). These data demonstrated that SNX10 promotes TNBC progression by regulating cholesterol homeostasis.

SNX10-SREBP2 axis promotes progression of TNBC

We also used siRNA to knock down SREBP2 to verify the role of the SNX10-SREBP2 axis in TNBC cells. SREBP2 knockdown reversed the altered expression of HMGCR induced by SNX10 overexpression in TNBC cells (Figure 6A, Figure S3 A–L). Knockdown of SREBP2 could inhibit cell proliferation and colony formation capability compared to the control group in MDA-MB-231 and HCC1806 cells, and the pro-tumor effects of SNX10 overexpression were significantly decreased after knockdown of SREBP2 (Figure 6B–G). And the migration and invasion capabilities of MDA-MB-231 and HCC1806 cells were inhibited in the SREBP2 knockdown group compared to the control group, and the promoting effects of SNX10 overexpression were also significantly reduced after SREBP2 knockdown (Figure 6H–K). These results demonstrated that SREBP2 inhibited TNBC progression and reversed the pro-tumor effects of SNX10 in TNBC cells. Above all, SNX10 promotes the progression of TNBC by modulating cholesterol biosynthesis via stabilizing SREBP2 (Figure 6L).

Discussion

Previous studies have reported that SNX10 acts as a tumor suppressor in CRC, 15-17 while in stomach adenocarcinoma and glioblastoma, SNX10 acts as a risk factor, suggesting that SNX10 may play different roles in various cancers. 19 , 20 Bioinformatics analysis showed SNX10 was positively correlated with poor outcomes of breast cancer, and highly expressed in TNBC. To date, the role of SNX10 in TNBC remains unknown, and we first found that SNX10 promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities of TNBC cells and that the knockdown of SNX10 significantly inhibits tumor growth. Our study elucidates the role of SNX10 in TNBC for the first time. Still, SNX10 promotes the metastasis of breast cancer, 25 and SNX10 deficiency impairs sensitivity to anti-HER2 antibody‒drug conjugates in HER2-positive breast cancer. 24 These research demonstrate that SNX10 plays different roles in breast cancer, suggesting that research focused on SNX10 can provide basic theoretical support for the development and application of targeting SNX10.

To date, researches on the SNX10 mechanism have mainly focused on chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) and signal transduction. SNX10 regulates CMA-dependent amino acid metabolism in CRC, 17 and mediates the metabolic reprogramming of macrophages in atherosclerosis. 40 These studies suggest that SNX10 may play a role in body metabolism. In our study, we found that cholesterol biosynthesis pathways are enriched using the GeneChip PrimeView Human Gene Expression Array, and SNX10 positively regulates the content and distribution of cellular cholesterol, as well as the mRNA and protein levels of their key regulatory factors. These data demonstrate that SNX10 positively promotes cholesterol biosynthesis in TNBC.

In intestinal epithelial cells or intestinal stem cells, SNX10 enhances SREBP2-SCAP anchored to the endoplasmic reticulum and suppresses intracellular cholesterol synthesis, 11 implies that SNX10 participates in the regulation of cholesterol homeostasis. Cholesterol synthesis is mainly regulated by SREBP2, HMGCR, and squalene monooxygenase, in which SREBP2 functions as a master transcriptional regulator, whereas HMGCR and squalene monooxygenase are the rate-limiting enzymes. 30 When cholesterol in the endoplasmic reticulum membrane is depleted, SCAP binds to SREBP2 and moves the complex from the endoplasmic reticulum to Golgi, where nSREBP2 is generated via site 1 protease (S1P) and S2P. When cholesterol is above 5 mol% of total endoplasmic reticulum lipids, the SCAP-SREBP2 complex remains in the endoplasmic reticulum, and thus, the generation of nSREBP2 is suppressed. 30 , 41 , 42 The regulation of SREBP2 mainly focused on the transcriptional level, translocation process, and protein degradation, 30-32 and nSREBP2 could enter the nucleus and up-regulate the transcription of cholesterol synthesis genes by recognizing and binding to the SRE sequence in the promoters, including SREBP2 and HMGCR.

Moreover, we found that SNX10 co-localizes and interacts with SREBP2, both pSREBP2 and nSREBP2, not HMGCR and SREBP1, and maintains the stability of SREBP2 in TNBC cells. It has been reported that SNX10 has an impact on protein stability. SNX10 controls autophagic degradation of SRC, 15 regulates CMA degradation of p21Cip1/WAF1 in CRC. 16 SNX10 deficiency promotes lysosome degradation of HER2 in breast cancer. 24 In our study, SNX10 maintains the stability of SREBP2, which is essential for transcription regulation of cholesterol biosynthesis genes, as well as cholesterol biosynthesis.

In addition, the addition of lovastatin or cholesterol or knockdown of SREBP2 reverses the effect on cell proliferation, migration, and invasion mediated by altering SNX10 in TNBC cells. These data demonstrate that SNX10 promotes TNBC progression by regulating cholesterol homeostasis and SREBP2. However, there were still some limitations in our study. Firstly, due to limitations on animal experimental conditions, only a knockdown model was used to validate the in vivo role of SNX10, while the role of SNX10-SREBP2-cholesterol axis was verified in two TNBC cells, but lacks animal experiments. Additionally, our study was conducted in cell and animal aspects, but we did not validate it in tumor biopsies collected from patients with TNBC.

In summary, we first found the pro-tumor roles of SNX10 in TNBC, in which SNX10 positively regulates cholesterol biosynthesis through interacting and stabilizing SREBP2. Our study provides potential targets into the therapeutic strategies for TNBC and provides basic theoretical support for the development and application of targeting SNX10.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

HCC1806 (CL-0726, Procell) and MDA-MB-231 (CL-0150, Procell) were purchased from Procell Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China), and authenticated by STR. HCC1806 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA; see Supplementary Table 1 for details) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Excell, China), whereas MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium (Gibco, USA; see Supplementary Table 1 for details) with 10% FBS (Hakata, China). Cell lines were both maintained at 37 ℃ in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Plasmids and lentivirus

SNX10-EGFP-3Flag plasmids, SNX10-HA plasmids, and negative control vectors were purchased from Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for the overexpression of SNX10. All plasmids were transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions at a density of 70%–80% cells. The siRNA targeting the human SREBP2 gene was designed and generated by GenePharma and transfected with a riboFECT CP Transfection Kit (RIBOBIO, China) with a density of 40%–50% cells. Human recombinant SNX10 shRNA lentivirus (KD1 and KD2) and the negative control (NC) were purchased from Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for the knockdown of SNX10 in MDA-MB-231 and HCC1806 cells. The shRNA sequences targeting human SNX10 were TTTGTTACCCAGCCTAATT and GCCTTAATGAATAGACGTTTC, respectively. For infection, 30%–40% confluent cells were incubated with medium containing 10% FBS at a multiplicity of infection of 10 for 24 h using HitransG P promoting reagent (Genechem, China). After incubation for 24 h, the medium was replaced, and the cells were cultured further for 48 h.

Western blot analysis (WB)

The cell samples were digested using trypsin-EDTA solution (Hycyte Biotechnology, China), harvested, and extracted using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) containing 1 mM PMSF (Beyotime, China), and analyzed using a BCA assay kit (CWBIO, China). Cell proteins were separated on 8%–12% SDS–PAGE gels and transferred to 0.22 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA), which were blocked with 10% skim milk powder for 3 h and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The membranes were then washed with PBS (Service Biotechnology, China) containing 0.1% Tween-20 (Solarbio, China) and incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature (see Supplementary Table 2 for details). Images were captured using an ECL detection system and quantified using ImageJ software. β-Actin was used as an internal control. The antibodies used in this study were β-actin (1:5000, 66009-1-Ig, Proteintech), SNX10 (1:500, sc-293380, Santa Cruz, 1:1000, TA808896S, OriGene Technologies), SREBP1 (1:1000, 14088-1-AP, Proteintech), SREBP2 (1:1000, 28212-1-AP, Proteintech), HMGCR (1:1000, ET1702-41, Hanzhou Huaan Biotech), SCAP (1:500, Sc-13553, Santa Cruz), Flag-tag (1:1000, T0003, Affinity Biosciences), and HA-tag (1:1000, AE105, ABclonal).

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol and TRIzon Pal™ reagent (CWBIO, China) according to the protocol, and total RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA) as follows: incubation at 65 °C for 5 min, 42 °C for 60 min, and 7 °C for 5 min. The PCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq (Tli RNaseH pLUS) (TaKaRa, Japan) in the LightCycler480 PCR system (Roche, Switzerland) as follows: prevariant at 95 °C for 30 s, cyclic reaction at 95 °C for 5 s, and 60 °C for 30 s, and repeated 40 cycles. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method for the normalization of β-actin expression. The primer sequences used are listed in Table 1.

MTT assay and colony formation assay

Transfected cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2000–3000 cells/well. After culturing, 10 μL of 5 mg/mL MTT (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) was added, and incubated for 4 h. Then, 100 μL of DMSO (Sangon Biotech, China) was added after carefully removing the supernatant. Finally, the absorbance values were determined using a microplate reader (Biotek, USA). The time points were set as 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h.

For colony formation assay, equal numbers of cells were seeded in 6-well plates with the indicated treatments, cultured for 10–14 d, fixed with 1 mL methanol (Sinopharm Chemical, China), and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (APPLYGEN, China). Finally, the images were recorded and quantified using ImageJ software.

Transwell assay

A total of 1–2*105 cells with the indicated treatments were seeded into the upper insert of transwell chambers (Corning, USA) with or without Matrigel (BD Biosciences, USA), and 750 μL of medium with 10% FBS was added to the lower chambers. After incubation for 48 h, the cells on the lower surface were fixed with methanol and stained with crystal violet. The relative number of cells was recorded and quantified using ImageJ software.

Cholesterol content determination

The total cellular cholesterol content was determined using a total cholesterol enzymatic determination kit (APPLYGEN, China). The cells were harvested and lysed in RIPA buffer for 10 min, and part of the supernatant was heated at 70 °C for 10 min and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 5 min. Then, the supernatant was incubated with the working solution at 37 °C or 25 °C for 20 min, and the absorption value was detected at a wavelength of 550 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer. The cholesterol content was normalized to the corresponding protein content.

Filipin assay

Cells were seeded in a confocal dish, followed by the indicated treatments. After culturing, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Service Biotechnology, China) for 30 min at room temperature, and stained with Filipin (MCE, USA) at 37 °C for 2 h. Filipin can specifically bind to cholesterol and produce blue fluorescence. After washing with PBS, the staining was observed through laser scanning confocal microscopy (Leica, Germany) and quantified by ImageJ. All images were acquired with identical laser outputs, gains, and offsets.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Transfected cells were collected and lysed with IP cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF. The cell lysates were immunoprecipitated with the indicated antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the Protein A/G magnetic beads (MCE, USA) at 4°C for 6 h. The immunocomplexes were washed three times with lysis buffer and analyzed by WB.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were co-transfected with SNX10 and SREBP2 plasmids and seeded in a confocal dish at a density of 40%–50% cells. After 24 h, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, China) for 15 min, and then blocked with 5% BSA blocking buffer (Solarbio, China) for 1 h at room temperature. The primary antibodies against SNX10 (1:200, TA808896S, OriGene Technologies) or SREBP2 (1:200, 28212-1-AP, Proteintech) were added to dishes overnight at 4 °C. After washing with cold PBS three times, the cells were incubated with secondary antibodies labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 or 594 dye (1:500, SA00013-1, SA00013-4, Proteintech, China) for 1 h in the dark. The nuclear DNA was stained with DAPI (COOLABER Biotechnology, China) for 5 min, and then washed with cold PBS three times. Images were captured using a STELLARIS 5 confocal microscope (Leica).

Animal experiments

A total of 18 BALB/c nude mice (female, 4 weeks), supplied by Lingchang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), were acclimated to specific-pathogen-free conditions at 22 ± 2 °C and 60% ± 5% humidity with a 12/12 h light‒dark cycle for a week. The mice were randomly divided into two groups, namely, lv-shNC (NC, n = 9) and lv-shSNX10 (SNX10 KD, n = 9). MDA-MB-231 cells (1 × 107 cells), with or without SNX10 knockdown, were suspended in 200 μL of PBS and subcutaneously injected. The maximum (L) and minimum (W) length and weight were measured twice a week, and the tumor volume was calculated as follows: volume (mm3) = π/6 × L × W2. Humane endpoints are strictly enforced when the mice weight loss is up to 10%, or the maximum diameter of tumors exceeds 20 mm. After 7 weeks, the mice were intraperitoneally injected with D-Luciferin potassium salt (1.5 mg/kg, Beyotime, China) and imaged using an IVIS Spectrum System (PerkinElmer, USA). The mice were euthanized using intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg), and the tumors were collected. All experiments were performed in accordance with the protocol approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of University of South China (Approval No.: 2024LL0408001).

Bioinformatics analysis

Survival analysis of SNX10 in the breast cancer cohort was obtained from the Kaplan‒Meier Plotter database (https://kmplot.com), and the expression of SNX10 in various types of breast cancer was analyzed using bc-GenExMiner database (version 5.2, https://bcgenex.ico.unicancer.fr/BC-GEM/GEM-Accueil.php?js = 1). For the bioinformatics analysis of GeneChip PrimeView Human Gene expression array, differentially expression genes (DEGs) were identified based on the following criteria: fold change ≥ 1.5 or fold change ≤ −1.5 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Subsequently, the list of significantly DEGs was subjected to functional annotation and pathway enrichment analysis using IPA. The criteria of significantly enriched pathways were in the following: Z-score ≥ 2 or Z-score ≤ −2.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using a two-tailed unpaired t-test between two groups and one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for more than two groups using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0). All in vitro experiments were performed at least three times, and data are represented as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. Significant differences are expressed as not significant (ns), p > 0.05, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.
