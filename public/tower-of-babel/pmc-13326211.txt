Dual-Access Secure-and-Pull Technique of an Entrapped Left Main Stent With Late Mechanical Failure
Authors: Kaïs Ouerghi, Mihai Cocoi, Tahar Lazizi, Victor Mateus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStent entrapment within a previously implanted left main (LM) scaffold is a rare but high-risk complication. Although snare retrieval is an effective bailout strategy, traction may induce occult structural injury.Case SummaryA 63-year-old man underwent emergent LM-to–left anterior descending (LAD) bifurcation stenting after resuscitated cardiac arrest (day 0). At month 8, angiography for lateral ischemia showed mid-distal left circumflex (LCx) disease and moderate LAD ostial in-stent restenosis. An initial kissing-balloon inflation (LAD: 3.5 mm, LCx: 3.0 mm) was performed. During LCx stent delivery, the device became entrapped within proximal aorto-ostial LM struts. A dual-access ping-pong “secure-and-pull” snare technique enabled controlled retrieval while preserving distal guidewire access. Immediate kissing-balloon optimization followed. At month 21, limited inferior ischemia prompted reassessment. Despite no critical LM stenosis, StentBoost revealed complete scaffold disorganization, requiring LM restenting. Systematic intravascular ultrasound at month 31 confirmed durable expansion and apposition.ConclusionsDual-access controlled snare retrieval provides a structured bailout for aorto-ostial LM entrapment. However, angiographic normalization may underestimate traction-induced injury, and systematic intravascular imaging is mandatory after complex LM retrieval.Key words: intravascular imaging, left main coronary artery, longitudinal stent deformation, percutaneous coronary intervention, snare retrieval, stent entrapment

Stent entrapment during percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is uncommon, with reported incidences between 0.02% and 0.7%.1 Risk increases in complex anatomies, particularly bifurcations and aorto-ostial segments, where acute angulation and proximal metallic struts create zones of mechanical interaction.

Longitudinal stent deformation is a recognized complication when proximal scaffolds are subjected to compressive or traction forces.2,3 However, the delayed structural consequences of snare-assisted retrieval in a left main coronary artery (LM) scaffold are poorly documented.

Intravascular imaging is an integral component of contemporary left main PCI, both for procedural optimization and for the detection of scaffold-related complications that may be underestimated by angiography alone.

Case Presentation

Day 0: initial event

A 63-year-old man was admitted after resuscitated cardiac arrest.

Coronary angiography revealed subocclusive thrombotic mid-LM stenosis, proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD) stenosis, acute mid–left circumflex artery (LCx) occlusion, and significant mid-distal right coronary artery (RCA) disease. Hemodynamic support included intra-aortic balloon pump and inotropes.

A 3.5 × 18 mm Xience Skypoint drug-eluting stent (DES; Abbott) was implanted from the LM to the proximal LAD, followed by proximal optimization technique and LAD-LCx kissing balloon inflation. The LCx was treated with balloon angioplasty alone. Left ventricular ejection fraction was preserved (55%). Dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and ticagrelor was initiated.

Importantly, the acutely occluded vessel was a mid circumflex branch, whereas the circumflex ostium and proximal circumflex segment were not significantly diseased. Therefore, no immediate circumflex stenting or planned 2-stent bifurcation strategy was considered necessary. Intravascular imaging was not performed because of the emergency context of resuscitated cardiac arrest and cardiogenic shock, in which rapid reperfusion and hemodynamic stabilization were prioritized.

Day 7: staged PCI

The fractional flow reserve of the RCA measured 0.63. DES implantation was performed in the mid and distal RCA.

Month 8: LCx reintervention and entrapment

Because the mid-LCx had initially been treated with balloon angioplasty alone, myocardial scintigraphy was scheduled. At 8 months, lateral ischemia was detected, prompting repeat angiography.

Angiography showed a long mid-LCx stenosis (–70%) and moderate in-stent restenosis (50%) at the LAD ostium (Figure 1A, Video 1).

Wiring of both the LAD and LCx was straightforward. Initial bifurcation optimization was performed with kissing-balloon inflation using noncompliant Accuforce balloons (Terumo) (LAD: 3.5 × 12 mm, LCx: 3.0 × 12 mm), both inflated to 12 atm (Figure 1B, Video 2). A drug-coated 3.5 × 15 mm Agent balloon (Boston Scientific) was then used in the LAD to treat ostial restenosis. The LCx lesion was predilated with a 2.5 × 20 mm semicompliant balloon.

During delivery of a 2.75 × 38 mm Onyx Frontier DES (Medtronic) to the LCx, the device became entrapped within the proximal aorto-ostial LM struts, remaining partially within the coronary artery and partially protruding into the aorta (Video 3). Gentle traction allowed withdrawal of the delivery balloon, but the undeployed stent remained locked within the LM scaffold.

One guidewire was positioned in the LCx and another in the LAD. Because an additional guidewire could not be advanced alongside the LCx wire, the LAD wire was redirected toward a marginal branch. A 2.0 × 20 mm semicompliant balloon was advanced over this wire and inflated to 12 atm without dislodging the stent (Video 4, Figure 1C). A guide extension catheter was advanced over the existing wires but failed to fully engage the trapped stent. The decision was made to proceed with a dual-access retrieval strategy.

Earlier use of a guide extension catheter may have represented the most effective preventive strategy by creating a protected delivery tunnel across the left main scaffold before long-stent advancement.

The “secure-and-pull” strategy

A dual-access ping-pong configuration was established:

•A 12-20 mm EN Snare (Merit Medical) was advanced.

The protruding aorto-ostial segment of the undeployed stent was captured with the snare.

Distal guidewires were intentionally preserved (ie, “secured”), and controlled traction was applied (ie, “pulled”) under fluoroscopy (Video 5, Figure 1D).

Extraction did not require excessive force. The stent was retrieved without abrupt resistance. The removed device showed marked longitudinal elongation with severe strut disorganization, consistent with significant longitudinal deformation (Figures 1E and 1F).

Immediate postextraction management

A repeat kissing-balloon inflation was performed using noncompliant balloons (LAD: 3.5 × 15 mm, LCx: 3.0 × 15 mm) (Figure 2A). StentBoost imaging (Philips Medical Systems) was performed with balloons inflated and showed no apparent LM scaffold abnormality. However, balloon superimposition within the LM likely masked structural distortion.

Distal wiring was preserved to avoid substrut rewiring and potential LM compromise. A 5.5-F GuideLiner (Teleflex) was advanced using balloon-assisted tracking over a semicompliant 2.0 × 15 mm Trek balloon (Abbott) positioned distally in the LCx (Figure 2B, Video 6). This protected tunnel allowed delivery of a 2.75 × 38 mm Onyx Frontier DES to the LCx (Figure 2C).

Final LAD-LCx kissing-balloon inflation was performed using noncompliant Accuforce balloons (LAD: 3.5 × 15 mm, LCx: 3.0 × 15 mm), achieving a satisfactory angiographic result (Figure 2D, Video 7). No intravascular imaging was performed at that stage, which is an important limitation of this case. In retrospect, intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) or optical coherence tomography may have identified structural damage to the pre-existing LM scaffold immediately after retrieval and could have prompted immediate corrective treatment.

Antiplatelet therapy was adjusted to aspirin plus clopidogrel for 6 months, followed by clopidogrel monotherapy.

Month 21: delayed mechanical failure

The patient remained asymptomatic. Routine scintigraphy demonstrated limited inferior ischemia, prompting angiography. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol was 0.47 g/L.

Angiography showed no critical LM stenosis but an unusual heterogeneous LM appearance (Figure 2E, Video 8). StentBoost revealed complete structural disorganization of the LM scaffold (Figure 2F).

No intravascular imaging was performed during the month 21 left main reintervention, either before or after stent implantation. Management was therefore based on angiography and StentBoost findings alone.

LM-to-LAD restenting was performed with a 3.5 × 18 mm Xience Skypoint DES. Proximal optimization technique was performed using a 4.5 × 15 mm semicompliant balloon (Figure 2G). Final kissing-balloon inflation was performed using noncompliant Accuforce balloons (LAD: 3.5 × 15 mm, LCx: 3.0 × 15 mm), achieving a satisfactory angiographic result (Figure 2H, Video 9).

Month 31: follow-up

Systematic angiographic follow-up was performed at month 31 despite the absence of symptoms.

Coronary angiography showed a satisfactory LM-to-LAD result (Figure 3A, Video 10). StentBoost confirmed overall scaffold integrity, although protruding struts at the LM ostium extending into the aorta were noted (Figure 3B).

Discussion

Percutaneous intervention of the LCx, particularly in its mid and distal segments, is technically demanding in the setting of LM disease.3 The retroflexed LCx takeoff frequently results in suboptimal coaxial alignment and increased friction during device advancement.1,2 When a previously implanted LM-to-LAD scaffold is present, stent delivery toward the LCx requires recrossing proximal metallic struts under angulation, substantially increasing the risk of device-strut interaction. Systematic lesion preparation and predilatation are therefore essential.

Recrossing proximal LM struts—especially when minimal aorto-ostial protrusion is present—creates a mechanical conflict zone within a hemodynamically critical segment. Entrapment in this setting must never be managed by abrupt traction. Sudden pulling may transmit uncontrolled longitudinal stress to the proximal scaffold and guiding catheter, leading to strut disruption, aorto-ostial distortion, catheter damage, or acute LM compromise.4,5 When entrapment occurs, controlled extraction—not rapid removal—is the procedural priority.

The dual-access ping-pong configuration provides a structured bailout strategy. Maintaining distal guidewire access while introducing a secondary guide for snare capture allows stabilization of the entrapped device before gradual, coaxial traction. This “secure-and-pull” concept separates fixation from extraction and limits uncontrolled shear forces.6 Preservation of distal wire position is critical, as recrossing distorted LM struts may be hazardous or impossible in an emergency.

An important nuance in this case was the procedural sequence. Immediately after successful snare retrieval, kissing-balloon inflation was performed before new stent delivery. This maneuver likely resulted in temporary reapposition of the traumatized LM scaffold. Fluoroscopic assessment and StentBoost imaging—performed with balloons inflated—did not reveal structural abnormality. Balloon superimposition within the LM may therefore have masked occult mechanical injury.

This illustrates a diagnostic pitfall: Angiographic normalization after kissing-balloon inflation does not exclude structural compromise.7 In our patient, ischemia at month 21 was limited to the inferior territory and did not clearly implicate the LM. Although no critical LM stenosis was visible, the abnormal heterogeneous fluoroscopic appearance prompted StentBoost imaging, which revealed complete scaffold disorganization.

Two preventive principles emerge. First, systematic use of a guide extension catheter when delivering a stent across a previously implanted LM scaffold should be strongly considered. Guide extension systems improve coaxial alignment, reduce proximal friction, and create a protected delivery tunnel in complex bifurcation anatomy.8

Second, intravascular imaging is integral to contemporary left main PCI, particularly in complex bifurcation anatomy. Beyond lesion characterization and vessel sizing, IVUS or optical coherence tomography helps confirm adequate stent expansion and apposition, detect scaffold injury, and guide reintervention strategy. The dissociation between acceptable angiography at month 8 and structural collapse at month 21 highlights the limitations of angiography and StentBoost alone. Although intravascular imaging might not necessarily have prevented the initial entrapment event itself, it would very likely have enabled earlier recognition of scaffold damage after retrieval and facilitated prompt corrective treatment.9,10

This case demonstrates that acute procedural success does not guarantee long-term scaffold integrity. Mechanical trauma during stent retrieval may remain clinically silent and manifest later as structural failure. These findings further emphasize that intravascular imaging is an integral component of contemporary left main PCI, particularly after complex retrieval procedures, because angiography alone may fail to identify occult scaffold injury.

Conclusions

Circumflex PCI through a previously implanted left main scaffold is a high-risk mechanical scenario. Sudden traction during stent entrapment must be avoided. A dual-access ping-pong “secure-and-pull” strategy enables controlled extraction while preserving distal safety.

Even when acute retrieval appears successful, mechanical trauma may result in delayed scaffold failure. Preventive guide extension use, systematic lesion preparation, and intravascular imaging are essential to prevent late catastrophic events.

In left main PCI, intravascular imaging should be considered mandatory for procedural optimization and for the detection of complications that may be missed by angiography alone.
