Green synthesis of wurtzite ZnO nanoparticles via Indigofera tinctoria stem extract: linking crystallinity with photocatalytic and biological performance
Authors: Mirza Nusrat Sweety, Shahin Sultana, Md. Ashraful Alam, Fariha Chowdhury, Anika Tasnim, Shariful Islam, Asma Shafiq Shathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The green synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles has been reported through a simplified route using Indigofera tinctoria stem extract, utilised as a bio-reducing agent. ZnO with a hexagonal wurtzite structure and a 35.51 nm crystallite size was confirmed by diffraction peaks in the XRD pattern. The ZnO nanoparticles exhibit a hexagonal single phase (space group P63mc), as determined by a Rietveld refinement study of XRD data. Symmetry between the obtained and observed diffraction patterns is shown by profile parameters (Rp, Rwp, and χ2) with minimal values. The FTIR spectra showed a strong band at 458 cm−1 and a small band at 877 cm−1, attributed to Zn–O and Zn–OH distinctive stretching. SEM images revealed the formation of quasi-spherical ZnO nanoparticles exhibiting an average diameter of ∼36 nm. The TEM image also showed quasi-spherical to hexagonal particle shapes having an average particle size of 36.19 nm. The purity of ZnO nanoparticles was disclosed by the EDX spectrum, which showed the existence of Zn and O elements and the non-existence of peaks related to other elements. The ZnO nanoparticles showed absorption peak maxima at 374 nm with a calculated band gap of 3.21 eV, according to the UV-vis spectra. The zeta potential (ζ) distribution of ZnO nanoparticles was found to range from −54.8077 to +11.9954 mV at pH 7 in aqueous solutions with a hydrodynamic diameter of 129.480 nm. The Raman spectrum supported the presence of crystal defects in the synthesized ZnO. The remarkable MB dye degradation efficiency of ∼93% under sunlight irradiation at 120 minutes was revealed by the photocatalytic experiment's results. After the 5th cycle, the produced ZnO nanoparticles show ∼85% degradation efficiency. Potent antibacterial activities were demonstrated by the green-produced ZnO nanoparticles against both Gram-positive (+) and Gram-negative (−) microorganisms. Additionally, using the DPPH technique, the produced ZnO nanoparticles showed modest antioxidant properties.

Introduction

The scientific field of nanotechnology—which manipulates matter at the nanoscale—has garnered significant attention and, as a result, connects fundamental and applied scientific fields of study.1,2 Using a variety of synthesis techniques, including hydrothermal, co-precipitation, sol–gel, ultrasonication, microwave irradiation, etc., nanotechnology is essential for engineering materials and producing nanomaterials with special optical, electrical, thermal, chemical, and physical characteristics.3,4 In recent times, nanoparticles have gained noteworthy attention in various fields because of their distinctive physical and chemical characteristics, namely, elevated strength with significant thermal conductivity, improved damping characteristics, and structural integrity.5 Many metal oxides, including ZnO,6–8 TiO2,9,10 CuO,11 Fe2O3,12 NiO,13 CoO,14 MnO2,15 MgO,16 SnO2,17etc., have been synthesized over recent years for a variety of uses, including wastewater treatment and photocatalysis. Among different metal oxides, ZnO, a flexible semiconductor featuring a significant excitation binding energy (60 meV) and broad band gap (3.2–3.37 eV), is the most studied owing to its non-toxic characteristics, cost efficiency, chemical stability, non-reactivity, eco-friendliness, potential photocatalytic effectiveness, elevated stability, non-harmfulness, single oxidation state, natural abundance, appropriate band gap and improved biocompatibility.18–21 As a result, it has been utilized in various distinct fields, such as electronic transistors, catalysis, skincare products, biosensors, food safety, and food packaging.22 The significant photocatalytic efficiency of ZnO nanoparticles enables them to effectively break down organic pollutants, helping to reduce environmental contamination and enhance overall sustainability.23 Additionally, ZnO nanoparticles demonstrate remarkable stability when exposed to light, guaranteeing their sustained and reliable performance during prolonged illumination. Moreover, ZnO exhibits strong antibacterial effects and extensive use in industry, and it additionally has the capacity to function as a reliable and efficient alternative to antibiotics.24–27 Leading factors that control the photocatalytic performance of ZnO nanoparticles are band gap energy,28 particle size,29 surface area,30 and degree of crystallinity.31 Two main categories of methods under which ZnO nanoparticles are produced encompass both chemical and physical methods; however, these techniques possess constraints of specialized equipment needs, dangerous precursor utilization, intricate process, significant energy demand, inconsistent size and shape distribution of particles, high cost, low quality, waste and by-product production, minimal efficiency in production, and environmental pollution.32,33 On the other hand, the eco-friendly production of nanomaterials resolves issues linked to chemical and physical techniques through the process of preparation of nanomaterials in an environmentally friendly, safe, simple, and economical way.34 In eco-friendly synthesis, extracts obtained from plants have been shown to be a common biological source for the synthesis of metal oxide semiconductor nanoparticles due to their extensive distribution of flora biodiversity.35 Phytochemicals, such as phenols, flavonoids, ketones, and aldehydes in plants, exhibit a reducing nature and thus convert metal salts into metal oxide nanoparticles.36 Additionally, complex biomolecules function as capping and shape-guiding agents, leading to the efficient formation of nanomaterials with limited sizes and specific shapes.37 A number of green species, such as Salvia officinalis,38Aloe barbadensis,39Syzygium cumini,40Nauclea latifolia,41Salvadora persica,42Acacia caesia,43etc., were used for eco-friendly ZnO synthesis. Indigofera tinctoria is a perennial shrub that contains several bioactive compounds such as indirubin, flavonoids, alkaloids, saponins, terpenoids, phenolic compounds etc., which contribute to antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties. Very few studies have been found to synthesize ZnO nanoparticles from the leaf extract of this plant.44,45

Focusing on the green approach, in this research, the stem extract of Indigofera tinctoria was used as an eco-friendly reducing and stabilizing agent, whereas zinc nitrate hexahydrate [Zn(NO3)2·6H2O] acted as the metal precursor for the production of ZnO nanoparticles. The nobility of this work lies in the use of the stem extract of Indigofera tinctoria. The morphological, structural, crystallographic, optical, and functional group properties were elaborately studied to ensure the successful synthesis of the nanoparticles. Furthermore, their capability to degrade cationic dye methylene blue (MB) under solar light was explained based on band gap tuning and crystallinity (%). Moreover, the disc diffusion technique was employed to assess the antimicrobial effectiveness of synthesized ZnO nanoparticles to target a range of pathogenic bacteria. The antioxidant properties of the synthesized ZnO were further studied to detect the scavenging effect of the newly prepared ZnO nanoparticles. The results of this study will help to develop a sustainable and eco-friendly route to synthesize ZnO nanoparticles from a novel source to serve different environmental and biomedical remediation purposes.

Materials & methods

Materials

Indigofera tinctoria stems were collected from Rangpur, Bangladesh. Zn(NO3)2·6H2O (99%) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich; methanol, ethanol, ascorbic acid, and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) were purchased from Merck Research Lab, India; Fine Chem Industries, India; Panreac, EU; and Merck, Germany, respectively.

Preparation of Indigofera tinctoria stem extract

The collected stems were washed, dried under sunlight, and then ground into powder. 10 g of the powder was soaked in distilled water overnight. Then the soaked powder was heated at 100 °C for 2 hours. The obtained mixture was then filtered, and the filtrate was collected as an extract for further use.

Synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles from Indigofera tinctoria stem extract

45 mL of prepared stem extract was taken into a beaker with subsequent addition of 2 g of precursor [Zn(NO3)2·6H2O] with continuous stirring at room temperature for 60 minutes. Then the mixture was placed in a water bath at 60 °C for 60 minutes. Subsequently, the mixture was collected in a 50 mL crucible and then dried in an oven at 105 °C for 30 minutes to form a yellow-brownish foamy paste. Though studies have shown that higher temperature46,47 or longer time at low temperature40–43 is required for this step, in our study, we optimized the temperature to 105 °C for quick foamy paste formation, indicating rapid crystal formation. The resultant paste was then placed in a muffle furnace at 600 °C for 2 hours. The calcination was carried out to obtain the desired wurtzite phase of the ZnO nanoparticles. The obtained yellowish powder was then ground using a mortar and pestle and stored as ZnO nanoparticles. The extract preparation and ZnO synthesis are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Characterization of synthesized ZnO

An XRD machine from Rigaku Smart Lab was utilized to examine the ZnO's crystalline properties. Within the 2θ range of 5–90°, the data were collected at intervals of 0.01 and 30° min−1 scan speed. The data were gathered using a copper X-ray source functioning at 40 kV and 50 mA. The collected data were analyzed using the Smart Lab Studio II software and then compared with the ICDD PDF 4+ database. The morphology of the surface of the synthesized ZnO was analyzed using a FESEM-EDX spectrometer (JSM-7610F). The voltage used was 15.00 kV. EDX analysis was performed to determine the atomic% and mass% of the components found in the ZnO. Prior to analysis, the sample was coated using a JEOL (JEC-3000FC) auto fine coater. The interior morphology and crystallography of the synthesized ZnO nanocrystals were investigated using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). SAED revealed individual crystal planes diffracted in various orientations, as well as a single or polycrystalline nature. EDS was used to determine the elemental composition of the powder using a JEM-2100 Plus (JEOL, Japan). FTIR spectroscopy (Frontier, PerkinElmer, UK) was performed over a wavenumber range of 400 to 4000 cm−1 and a resolution of 4 cm−1 to confirm the functional groups present in the as-prepared ZnO. The synthesized powder's colloidal properties were examined using a zeta potential and nano particle analyzer [NanoPartica (SZ-100-Z2), Horiba Scientific, Japan] by the dynamic light scattering (DLS) method. The hydrodynamic diameter and the zeta potential value were assessed by dispersing the ZnO in an aqueous solution using an ultrasonic bath [POWERSONIC 510, United Kingdom] at room temperature (30 °C). The Stokes–Einstein equation was employed to calculate the hydrodynamic diameters. The maximum absorbance was determined from the absorbance vs. wavelength data collected using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (Lambda 35, PerkinElmer, Germany) within the wavelength range of 300–700 nm. The band gap was determined from the absorbance data using the Tauc plot method. The Raman spectrum of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticle was recorded using a Raman spectrometer (Horiba MacroRam) with a 785 nm diode laser as the excitation source (laser power of 5 mW) at room temperature. The electrochemical impedance spectrum was obtained using an impedance analyzer (Agilent 4294A).

Photocatalytic activity evaluation under sunlight

The decomposition of methylene blue (MB) in sunlight was examined. The time was selected between 11 am and 1 pm to get the maximum sun exposure of the day. The suspension was obtained by incorporating 50 mg of ZnO into 100 mL of 20 ppm MB solution in a beaker. The degradation of MB was assessed by monitoring the absorbance variation through UV-vis spectroscopy at 10-minute intervals over a duration of 120 minutes. After its use in the photocatalytic degradation of MB, ZnO was subjected to recyclability tests. The solution was centrifuged three times with distilled water after the initial photocatalytic degradation. Following the wash, the ZnO was dried at 70 °C and reused. Employing fresh MB dye, this procedure was executed consecutively four times for additional photocatalytic cycles. The radical trapping experiment was conducted using ascorbic acid (AA), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), isopropyl alcohol (IPA), and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) as scavengers to find out the radicals responsible for photocatalysis.

Antimicrobial activity analysis

The synthesized ZnO nanoparticles were tested against Gram-negative (E. coli) and Gram-positive (S. aureus and B. subtilis) bacteria through the agar well diffusion method to examine their antimicrobial properties. Nutrient agar medium was kept at 37 °C overnight to promote bacterial growth. The Petri dish was then drilled to create wells containing seeded organisms. In each dish, a sterile 6 mm cork borer was used to produce wells. 50 µL of each sample (1.0 mg mL−1, 0.5 mg mL−1, and 0.25 mg mL−1) was transferred into the wells. The dishes were kept for 24 hours at 37 °C. DMSO was used as a negative control. Mean values were utilized in determining the zone of inhibition. The MIC and MBC values were also determined using the broth dilution method.

DPPH scavenging activity analysis

A solution containing 100 µM DPPH was made in 99.9% methanol in order to assess the DPPH scavenging activity at different dosages. 3.0 mL of DPPH solution and 1 mL of sample solution at several concentrations (50 µg mL−1, 100 µg mL−1, 250 µg mL−1, 500 µg mL−1, and 1000 µg mL−1) were then combined to perform the analysis. After that, this mixture was incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature in the dark. Methanol was utilized as a blank and ascorbic acid as the standard. Results and discussion

Possible mechanism of ZnO formation

Though the precise mechanism of ZnO formation from the stem extract is not understood yet, the possible mechanism is outlined below based on previous studies. The plant extract contains phytochemicals with hydroxyl, carboxyl, carbonyl, and amino functional groups that coordinate with zinc ions to create zinc–phytochemical complexes. These interactions allow for the regulated precipitation of zinc precursor species, whereas phytochemicals adsorbed on the particle surface serve as capping and stabilising agents, avoiding agglomeration and regulating particle development. Calcination causes heat breakdown of the zinc precursor, removing the linked organic elements and generating crystalline ZnO nanoparticles. The mechanism involves phytochemical-assisted complexation and precipitation, rather than direct reduction of Zn2+ ions. Rietveld refinement in WPPF analysis

The findings from the Rietveld refinement's WPPF method were quantitatively analyzed to confirm phase purity and structural details.

The WPPF refinement shown in Fig. 4 demonstrates that the predicted and experimental ZnO diffraction profiles fit each other quite well. The pseudo-Voigt peak profile properly reproduces the reported peak positions, intensities, and widths, while the smooth background appropriately accommodates diffuse scattering. At the strongest reflections, the residual curve just slightly deviates from zero, suggesting little systematic error and a trustworthy refinement. The 100% phase purity and crystallinity of the synthesized ZnO sample are confirmed by the indexed diffraction peaks, which only correspond to the hexagonal wurtzite ZnO phase with no observable impurity reflections. The experimental diffraction pattern of synthesized ZnO nanoparticles is robustly and accurately described by the improved profile parameters, which include background coefficients, scaling factor, zero shift, lattice constants, peak-shape parameters, and FWHM broadening terms.48 The estimated parameters, adjusted with SmartLab Studio II, show a strong correspondence with the ICDD data [card no. 01-078-2585] according to fitting conditions. This shows that the wurtzite phase of ZnO was generated. Morphology analysis

FESEM and EDX analysis

The FESEM image of ZnO biologically reduced with Indigofera tinctoria stem extract was captured at various magnifications to clarify its morphology and size and is illustrated in Fig. 6. The surface structure of the low-magnification (1 µm, 25 000×) FESEM image in Fig. 6(a) illustrates the creation of ZnO nanoparticles in an aggregated manner; however, the precise dimensions of the nanoparticles could not be identified. Conversely, the highly magnified (100.00 nm, 50 000×) FESEM image in Fig. 6(b) distinctly illustrates the main presence of quasi-spherical ZnO nanoparticles with an average size of approximately 36.193 ± 6.055 nm. The average particle size was calculated by the ImageJ software and is shown in Fig. 6(c). Individual ZnO nanoparticles cluster together with nearby ZnO nanoparticles because of electrostatic attraction, forming multiple small spherical aggregates in a cluster-like structure.58 The emergence of clusters could similarly be expected due to the elevated surface energy of ZnO NPs and possibly as a result of proximity between nanoparticles.58 Bioreduced ZnO nanoparticles synthesized from Indigofera tinctoria stem extract are believed to possess a structure that is ideal for increased surface area, due to their tiny particle size with spherical form, and as a result, larger active sites are projected on their surface.59 Phytochemical analyses indicate that the aqueous stem extract of Indigofera tinctoria contains non-toxic bioactive compounds and secondary metabolites such as alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids, tannins, saponins, sterols, glycosides, quinones, amino acids, and proteins acting as natural reducing and capping agents, promoting the formation, stabilization, and clustering of ZnO nanoparticles during green synthesis.60,61 Elemental analysis of ZnO nanoparticles bio-reduced with Indigofera tinctoria stem extract reveals the peaks and the associated energy values observed in the EDX spectrum. The spectrum verified the existence of elements linked to Zn (∼1, ∼8.7, and ∼9.7 keV) and O (∼0.5 keV) in ZnO nanoparticles (Fig. 6(d)). The presence of intense Zn and O peaks indicates that the sample is primarily made up of ZnO. Furthermore, the absence of peaks associated with impurity elements demonstrates the purity of ZnO nanoparticles.

(a) 1 µm, 25 000×; (b) 100.00 nm, 50 000×; SEM image, (c) particle size distribution, and (d) EDX image of ZnO nanoparticles synthesized from Indigofera tinctoria stem extract.

The elementary assessment showed weight percentages of 78.72% for Zn and 21.28% for O, which coincide with the stoichiometric theoretical projected weight% of Zn (80.34%) and O (19.66%) elements, respectively, highlighting excellent purity and minimal impurities present in ZnO nanoparticles bio-reduced with Indigofera tinctoria stem extract.62 Alterations in weight% can be linked to a small number of organic remains produced from the phytochemicals of Indigofera tinctoria stem extract in the process of bio-reduction, a process that could generate oxygen vacancies in ZnO nanoparticles, which is beneficial for photocatalytic use.63

TEM, SAED, and EDS analysis

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis shown in Fig. 7(a) reveals that the synthesized nanoparticles have a primarily quasi-spherical to hexagonal morphology with particle sizes ranging from 25 to 45 nm, resulting in substantial agglomeration due to their high surface energy. Despite the aggregation, discrete particle boundaries were seen, confirming the production of individual nanocrystals. The associated selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern in Fig. 7(b) shows well-defined concentric diffraction rings with discrete bright spots, indicating that the sample was polycrystalline and extremely crystalline.

(a) TEM image at 50.00 nm, (b) SAED pattern, (c) particle size distribution, and (d) EDS spectrum of ZnO nanoparticles synthesized from Indigofera tinctoria stem extract.

The diffraction rings of ZnO nanoparticles correspond to the hexagonal wurtzite structure's characteristic planes (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), and (103). The average particle size was calculated to be 36.195 ± 9.54 nm, as shown in Fig. 7(c). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) validated the elemental composition of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles. The spectrum in Fig. 7(d) shows characteristic peaks of O Kα (∼0.52 keV), Zn Lα (∼1.0 keV), Zn Kα (∼8.64 keV), and Zn Kβ (∼9.57 keV), indicating the production of ZnO. No impurity peaks were found other than C and Cu arising from the grid, indicating a high level of chemical purity. Atomic% of Zn was 80.35% and O was 19.65%. These findings are consistent with the XRD, SEM, TEM, and SAED investigations, demonstrating the effective synthesis of highly crystalline and phase-pure ZnO nanoparticles. The nanoscale particle size, along with excellent crystallinity, results in a large active surface area and efficient charge transfer, making the synthesized material ideal for photocatalytic and antibacterial applications.

Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) analysis

Fig. 8(a) highlights that the zeta potential (ζ) distribution of ZnO nanoparticles was found to range from −54.8077 to +11.9954 mV at pH 7 in aqueous solutions. At neutral pH, the observed zeta potential indicates that the remaining phytochemical capping molecules (such as phenolic and carboxylate groups) from the plant extract employed in green synthesis are responsible for the ZnO nanoparticles' somewhat negatively charged surface. These surface functional groups encourage the adsorption of positively charged contaminants on the ZnO surface in addition to offering modest colloidal stability. Values that are around or above ±30 mV indicate adequate electrostatic repulsion to avert aggregation in aqueous dispersion. As a result, ZnO NPs show moderate stability under these circumstances.64 The image of the size distribution acquired through DLS for green-synthesized ZnO nanoparticles is presented in Fig. 8(b). The average size distribution of the produced ZnO particles is 129.480 nm, whereas the average particle size obtained from SEM and TEM is 36.193 ± 6.055 nm and 36.195 ± 9.54 nm, respectively. Typically, the particle diameter measured by DLS is greater than those obtained through other techniques due to the presence of biomolecular coatings in the aqueous medium and the attractive forces between particles.54

UV-vis absorption analysis

The absorption spectrum obtained for the ZnO nanoparticles is displayed in Fig. 9(a). The highest absorbance is seen at 374 nm, which may be attributed to the intrinsic bandgap of the semiconductor transition possessing the surface plasmon resonance alongside quantum-scale effect phenomena. The distinctive slim peak achieved at 374 nm is consistent with previous findings.65,66 The optical band gap (3.21 eV) shown in Fig. 9(b) is slightly lower than the bulk ZnO (∼3.3 eV). The slight reduction in the band gap could be attributed to defect-induced energy levels such as oxygen vacancies, zinc interstitials, and lattice disorder rather than size-dependent quantum effects.51,67,68 These defect states facilitate visible-light absorption and promote efficient charge carrier generation, thereby enhancing visible-light photocatalytic activity.16 Additionally, the emergence of an acute, sharp peak without any minor peaks in the absorption spectrum signifies the high purity of synthesized ZnO nanoparticles.69

Raman spectroscopy analysis

The Raman spectrum of synthesized ZnO nanoparticles is presented in Fig. 10. The sharp rise at 100.53 cm−1 is associated with the E2 low phonon mode, resulting from the oscillations of the Zn crystal structure, and the main peak at 438.38 cm−1 is part of the E2 high frequency mode, which occurs due to the vibrations of lattice oxygen. The narrow, wide summit at 333.47 cm−1 relates to the multi-phonon mode (E2M). This wide peak is also referred to as the second-order mode resulting from the multi-phonon mode (merged vibrations related to the ZnO lattice). The A1(TO) mode, which may result from lattice distortion or crystal orientation effects, is represented by the band at 387.06 cm−1. The A1(LO)/E1(LO) mode is responsible for the peak at 570.19 cm−1, which is linked to inherent structural defects in the ZnO lattice and oxygen vacancies. Overall, the development of crystalline wurtzite ZnO with a moderate concentration of inherent lattice defects is confirmed by the Raman spectrum. This study is based on the slightly reduced band gap of the produced ZnO. The presence of a well-ordered ZnO crystal structure along with a suitable level of intrinsic defects is anticipated to improve photocatalytic performance by facilitating efficient charge separation, prolonging the lifespan of photo-generated carriers, and speeding up the breakdown of organic pollutants.69

Photocatalytic activity assessment

Degradation profile

The degradation profile of MB dye under sunlight illumination using bioreduced ZnO NP catalysts: (1) photocatalytic reaction, (2) under dark conditions, and (3) the photolysis process, was investigated to assess the photocatalytic performance of the prepared ZnO nanoparticles.70Fig. 11(a) displays the UV-vis absorbance spectra of MB during the photocatalytic degradation, which were recorded at 10-minute intervals. Only 7.34% of the MB dye was degraded due to the photolysis reaction when the solution was exposed to sunlight without the ZnO NP catalyst. The adsorption in the dark by the bioreduced ZnO nanoparticles eliminated only 12.25% of the MB dye. The ZnO catalyst led to an exponential reduction in dye concentration and consequently, ∼93% of MB dye was eliminated within 120 minutes of sunlight illumination, which occurs through a photocatalytic reaction, as shown in Fig. 11(b). The peak of maximum absorption intensity observed at 664 nm for the MB dye can be attributed to n to π* shifts. The notable photocatalytic activity observed in the current investigation could be attributed to factors such as a small average particle size of ∼36 nm, reduced crystallinity (46.32%) compared to the standard, and oxygen vacancies in the ZnO nanoparticles (Zn and O constituents possess the wt% of 78.72% and 21.28%, respectively), along with suitable band gap energy (3.21 eV).

(a) MB dye degradation (photolysis + photocatalysis), (b) efficiency plot of MB dye removal by ZnO nanoparticles, (c) pseudo-first-order kinetics, and (d) pseudo-second-order kinetics.

The slightly lower band gap value of 3.21 eV allows for partial absorption of visible light, thus improving solar-driven photocatalytic performance relative to bulk ZnO.51 The reduced band gap probably results from inherent defect states created during green synthesis (shown in the Raman spectrum), facilitating effective charge separation and enhanced reactive oxygen species production under sunlight exposure.69 In this study, probably the larger surface area of quasi-spherical-shaped particles offered additional active sites for photocatalytic processes, facilitating an increased number of interactions involving ZnO nanoparticles produced through green synthesis with Indigofera tinctoria stem extract and MB dye compounds.60 Additionally, the ZnO nanoparticles frequently demonstrate excellent dispersibility in solution because of their balanced form.58,61 This consistent distribution guarantees that every particle is uniformly exposed to the reacting entities, encouraging uniform and effective photocatalytic breakdown of MB dye. Moreover, the existence of oxygen vacancies may result in enhanced charge carrier mobility inside the particles that enables the effective separation ratio and movement of photo-induced electron–hole pairs.61 Again, positively charged MB dye molecules might electrostatically adsorb onto the surface of negatively charged ZnO nanoparticles. When exposed to sunlight, the produced oxidative radicals attack indiscriminately the MB dye molecules that are adsorbed on ZnO nanoparticles.38,59

Radical trapping

The radical trapping experiments offer proof that reactive species are involved in reactions. Ascorbic acid (AA), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), isopropyl alcohol (IPA), and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) were used as scavengers for superoxide radicals (O2−*), electrons (e−), hydroxyl radicals (OH*), and photogenerated holes (h+), respectively, in reactive species trapping experiments. It is evident from Fig. 12(b) that the use of DMSO, AA, EDTA, and IPA reduced the photocatalytic dye removal efficiency to 89.6%, 67.1%, 42.9%, and 22.5%, respectively. Hydroxyl radicals (OH*) and photogenerated holes (h+) appear to be major players in the photocatalytic degradation of MB over the ZnO catalyst, as indicated by the marked decrease in photocatalytic activity in the presence of IPA and EDTA. The radical trapping experiment result matches the previous study.70,71

Plausible mechanism

The photocatalytic mechanism of the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles can be elucidated based on the above experimental results and previous studies.72,73 as shown in Fig. 13. When UV light with energy exceeding the band gap energy of ZnO (3.153 eV) hits its surface, electrons (e−) are energized from the valence band (VB) and transition to the conduction band (CB). This process leads to the creation of electrons (e−) and holes (h+) in the conduction band (CB) and valence band (VB), respectively (eqn (7)).

Possible mechanism of MB dye degradation by ZnO nanoparticles synthesized from Indigofera tinctoria stem extract.

The h+ interacts with nearby water molecules and subsequently creates hydroxyl (OH*) radicals (eqn (8)). In the meantime, e− engages with the nearby oxygen molecule, producing superoxide (O2−*) radicals (eqn (9)). O2−* subsequently bonds with a water molecule to create H2O2, which reacts with e− to transform into OH* (eqn (10)–(12)). Nyquist plot

The electron transfer during photocatalysis can be explained by the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis. The ZnO photocatalyst's Nyquist plot obtained from electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is displayed in Fig. 14.

Nyquist plot of ZnO nanoparticles synthesized from Indigofera tinctoria stem extract.

The plot shows two depressed semicircular arcs that represent the bulk and grain-boundary/interfacial charge-transfer processes. Non-ideal capacitive behavior resulting from surface imperfections, grain-size dispersion, oxygen vacancies, and reduced crystallinity is indicated by the depressed semicircular shape. The charge-transfer resistance (Rct) and the semicircle's diameter are directly correlated. Rapid migration of photogenerated electrons from the ZnO bulk to the catalyst surface is made possible by a comparatively lower Rct, which suppresses electron–hole recombination and increases the production of reactive oxygen species such as OH* and O2−*. These radicals are essential for the oxidative breakdown of organic contaminants.

On the other hand, a wider semicircle indicates slower charge transfer, more interfacial resistance, and a greater likelihood of electron–hole recombination, all of which result in lower photocatalytic efficiency. Consequently, EIS analysis shows that the efficiency of interfacial charge transfer greatly controls photocatalytic performance, with higher photocatalytic activity being correlated with lower impedance.72

Stability analysis of ZnO nanoparticles after photocatalytic activity

The combined XRD, UV-vis, and SEM investigations show that the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles retained their structural, optical, and morphological properties after photocatalytic degradation, as shown in Fig. 15(a–c).

(a) XRD pattern, (b) UV-vis spectrum, and (c) SEM image of ZnO nanoparticles after the 5th cycle of photocatalytic activity.

The slight changes in XRD peak intensity and UV-vis absorbance, as well as increased particle agglomeration observed by SEM, are due to surface adsorption of degradation intermediates and particle aggregation rather than ZnO crystal structure degradation. Apart from green syntheses, ZnO nanoparticles have been found in composite form showing different types of applications. Studies have been reported for Ag-decorated ZnO composites showing the production of sustainable H2 gas as well as pollutant degradation.70,86 The g-C3N4Fe–ZnO/RGO composite has been reported to show 100% and 82% photo degradation of MB dye and Cr(vi) under visible light at 50 and 120 minutes, respectively.87 The 5% Er/ZnO and 2% Nd/ZnO composites also showed 100% and 87.2% MB as well as 89.5% and 78.6% RR-241 degradation under visible light.72 A disposable screen-printed electrode-based non-enzymatic glucose sensor that is extremely selective and enhanced with Fe@ZnO (SPE/Fe@ZnO) has been also reported.88 Cu-doped ZnO nanoparticles showed 100% removal of crystal violet dye at pH 12.0 in another study.89 Adsorption of Pb2+via biosynthesized ZnO nanoparticles produced from Azadirachta indica (neem) leaf extract was also studied.90 The (Mn, Ce) co-doped ZnO nanoparticles, Zn(1−x−y)MnxCeyO nanoparticles, have been developed in a study for applicability to optoelectronic devices.91 Compared to other green-synthesized ZnO nanoparticles and ZnO composites, our synthesized ZnO from Indigofera tinctoria stem extract is a viable alternative due to its ease of synthesis, excellent structural, morphological, functional, and optical properties, and excellent photocatalytic activity with high stability.

Possible mechanism

Fig. 17 illustrates the proposed antibacterial mechanism of ZnO nanoparticles. The nanoparticles initially adsorb onto the bacterial cell surface through electrostatic interactions, leading to disruption of the cell wall and membrane integrity. Simultaneously, ZnO nanoparticles generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and superoxide radicals (O2˙−), which induce severe oxidative stress. The ROS oxidize membrane lipids, denature intracellular proteins, and damage chromosomal as well as plasmid DNA, thereby inhibiting essential cellular functions such as DNA replication, transcription, and enzyme activity.

Possible antimicrobial mechanism of ZnO against bacterial cells.

Increased membrane permeability causes leakage of cytoplasmic contents and loss of cellular homeostasis. The cumulative effects of membrane disruption, oxidative stress, protein denaturation, and genetic damage ultimately result in irreversible bacterial cell death. These multiple bactericidal pathways contribute to the broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity of ZnO nanoparticles and reduce the likelihood of bacterial resistance development.92–94

Consequently, this study aims to both assess the Indigofera tinctoria stem extract as a potential source of phytochemicals for ZnO nanoparticle synthesis and to elucidate the connection between crystallinity, morphology, photocatalytic efficiency, and biological activity. Limitations and future aspects

Although this study successfully demonstrates the green synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles using Indigofera tinctoria stem extract and highlights their excellent structural, optical, and crystalline properties and their connection with photocatalytic, antimicrobial, and antioxidant properties, several limitations remain. Photocatalytic activity was assessed only with methylene blue as a model pollutant, and large-scale synthesis was not evaluated. The antimicrobial and antioxidant activities were tested in vitro without mechanistic insights or cytocompatibility analyses. Moreover, this study lacks an analytical study of the Indigofera tinctoria stem extract. Our future aim is to focus on extract characterization, mechanistic studies, broader pollutant degradation, biocompatibility assessments, catalyst durability, and scalability to support practical environmental and biomedical applications.

Conclusion

Indigofera tinctoria stem extract was used in a simple, environmentally benign, and financially feasible bioreduction process to create ZnO nanoparticles. FESEM images showed that quasi-spherical ZnO NPs with a mean size of about ∼36 nm had formed. The TEM image showed a quasi-spherical to hexagonal particle with an average size of 36.19 nm. The SAED pattern showed rings corresponding to hexagonal wurtzite planes. The Indigofera tinctoria stem extract contains low-cost, non-toxic natural secondary metabolites that function as capping and bioreducing agents for the formation and stabilization of ZnO NPs. The presence of elements related to Zn and O was confirmed by the EDX spectrum. The hexagonal wurtzite phase of ZnO was represented by diffraction peaks in the XRD pattern. ZnO NPs were discovered to have a mean crystalline size of ∼35 nm and a band gap value of 3.21 eV. ZnO NPs with a hexagonal wurtzite structure and a space group of P63mc were identified by Rietveld refinement analysis. Good profile refinement was revealed by lower values of Rp, Rwp, and χ2. The crystallinity observed in XRD, defect-induced band gap narrowing (3.21 eV), and yellow coloration caused by oxygen vacancies collectively indicate a ZnO structure formed after calcination at 600 °C. Meanwhile, the negative zeta potential produced by phytochemical capping agents enhances nanoparticle dispersion and pollutant adsorption. Together, these structural and surface properties contribute to the improved photocatalytic performance of the green-synthesized ZnO nanoparticles under visible or sunlight irradiation. After 120 minutes of exposure to sunlight, photocatalytic studies demonstrated a significant breakdown of MB dye with an efficiency of ∼93%. After the 5th cycle, the produced ZnO nanoparticles show acceptable reusability characteristics. The eco-friendly synthesized ZnO nanoparticles showed remarkable antibacterial activity against both Gram positive and negative pathogens as well as demonstrated mild antioxidant properties. The green synthesized ZnO nanoparticles from Indigofera tinctoria stem extract can therefore be used in environmental as well as biomedical field.
