Comprehensive comparative analysis of eight predictive indices for survival after deceased donor liver transplantation: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jiyoung Baik, Hayeon Do, Suk Min Gwon, Youngju Ryu, Eunjin Lee, Namkee Oh, Jinsoo Rhu, Gyu-Seong Choi, Jongman Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDeceased donor liver transplantation (DDLT) remains constrained by organ shortages, emphasizing the need for accurate predictive models to optimize allocation and improve outcomes. Although multiple indices, such as Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD), MELD-Na, and donor-specific models, exist to predict recipient and graft survival, their comparative performance remains uncertain.MethodsThis study compared 8 predictive models (MELD, MELD-Na, Gender-Equity Model for Liver Allocation [GEMA], GEMA-Na, Donor Rejection Organ Procurement Evaluation, Donor Risk Index, Kidney Donor Profile Index [KDPI], and Korean KDPI) in predicting overall and graft survival after DDLT. Data from 328 donor–recipient pairs at Samsung Medical Center (2000–2020) were retrospectively analyzed. Calibration was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and R2 values, and discrimination using the Kaplan-Meier curves, concordance statistics (c-index), and receiver operating characteristic curves.ResultsMELD-Na demonstrated the highest calibration and discrimination for both overall and graft survival, particularly at short-term follow-up. Recipient-based models (MELD, GEMA) showed satisfactory performance, with MELD closely following MELD-Na in predicting overall survival. In contrast, donor-based indices (KDPI, K-KDPI) exhibited weak predictive power. Integrating recipient and donor parameters improved the overall comprehensiveness of prediction.ConclusionMELD-Na is the most reliable predictor of short-term survival after DDLT. Donor-specific indices provide valuable insights into organ quality but are limited in survival prediction. Integrating both donor and recipient factors may refine organ allocation and enhance posttransplant outcomes. Further validation across diverse populations and the incorporation of advanced technologies, including machine learning, are warranted to optimize predictive accuracy in liver transplantation.Keywords: Organ allocation, Prognosis, Survival analysis, Tissue donors

INTRODUCTION

A critical and persistent challenge in liver transplantation is the scarcity of available donor organs. This shortage has driven ongoing efforts to optimize donor organ allocation by predicting posttransplant prognosis. To address the complexity and variability of outcomes following deceased donor liver transplantation (DDLT), models must be developed that accurately predict both graft and patient survival.

The Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score is widely used to prioritize patients on the waiting list for a DDLT [1]. This score is calculated based on serum creatinine, bilirubin, and the international normalized ratio (INR) of prothrombin time. It is used to assess the severity of liver dysfunction and serves as an important tool in allocation decisions [2]. MELD score has been used as the Korean allocation criteria since 2016 [3]. Adding serum sodium (Na) concentration to MELD scores has been proposed to improve survival prediction accuracy and optimize resource allocation. This is because hyponatremia is recognized as an independent prognostic factor in patients with cirrhosis [4,5]. The MELD-Na score, incorporating serum Na, has been utilized for liver graft allocation across the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) regions since 2016 [6]. Despite these advances, the application of the MELD system revealed gender differences in outcomes, with women having a 30% higher probability of death or removal from the transplant waiting list than men. This difference was attributed to women’s lower serum creatinine levels, which resulted in lower MELD-Na scores [7,8]. To address this imbalance, the Gender-Equity Model for Liver Allocation (GEMA) was introduced. GEMA replaces serum creatinine with renal function-adjusted glomerular filtration rate (RFH-GFR) in the MELD and MELD-Na equations [9].

Although MELD, MELD-Na, GEMA, and GEMA-Na scores focus on recipient-related factors, they do not consider donor characteristics, which can significantly impact transplant outcomes. To address this issue, donor-specific predictive models have been developed. The Donor Rejection Organ Procurement Evaluation (DROP) score [10] and the Donor Risk Index (DRI) [11] were developed to quantify donor-related risks and evaluate the suitability of organs for transplantation. These indices provide valuable insights into how donor characteristics influence transplant success.

Another noteworthy model specific to donors is the Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI), which has been widely used in the field of kidney transplantation to predict organ performance after transplantation [12]. Recent studies suggest that KDPI can be used as an alternative to the Liver Donor Risk Index in liver transplantation-related decisions [13]. In Korea, a new Korean KDPI (K-KDPI) has been developed, taking into account the characteristics of national transplantation. This locally developed model is based on data from the Korean Organ Network Sharing (KONOS) and the National Health Insurance Service, providing a customized approach to predicting transplantation outcomes for Korean patients.

Despite the existence of various prediction models, little research has explored the relative effectiveness of each model in predicting the prognosis of DDLT. This study aims to address this issue by evaluating and comparing the ability of 8 established models (MELD, MELD-Na, GEMA, GEMA-Na, DROP, DRI, KDPI, and K-KDPI) to predict overall and graft survival after DDLT at our institution.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Declaration of Istanbul and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Samsung Medical Center (No. SMC-2024-10-028). As this was a retrospective observational study using data collected from patient medical records, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB.

Patient population

The dataset included recipients who received DDLT at Samsung Medical Center from January 1, 2000 to December 31, 2020, and their brain-dead donors. Recipient data were extracted from Samsung Medical Center’s electronic medical records, and donor data were collected from the KONOS. Patients who received multiorgan transplants, re-transplants, or pediatric transplants under the age of 18 years were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as the median and interquartile range or the mean and standard deviation. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage. We evaluated the calibration of each prognostic index using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit chi-square test, as well as calibration curves at 1, 3, and 5 years, and overall posttransplant. Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test [17] categorized cases into ten equal groups (deciles) based on risk and compared the observed and expected probabilities of an event within each decile. A smaller χ2 value and a P-value greater than 0.05 indicate optimal calibration, reflecting minimal discrepancies between observed and predicted outcomes, whereas a low P-value suggests poor calibration. Calibration curves were constructed using linear regression analysis. The R2 value represented the proportion of the observed dropout rates that could be predicted by the model. An R2 value of 1.0 indicates perfect prediction, with the observed and predicted values aligned on a 45° line (slope = 1, intercept = 0). An upward shift in the line indicates an underestimation of dropout rates, and a downward shift indicates an overestimation.

The capacity of each index to discriminate in predicting the risk of graft failure and death at 1, 3, and 5 years and overall posttransplant was evaluated using multiple methods. Kaplan-Meier survival estimates were generated by dividing the study cohort into quintiles for each index to assess graft survival and overall survival across score categories. Concordance statistics (c-statistics) were calculated using Uno’s c-statistic [18], with values ranging from 0.5 (indicating poor concordance) to 1.0 (indicating perfect concordance). Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC) [19,20] were estimated using logistic regression models, and pairwise comparisons of the AUCs were performed. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Optimal cutoff values for each prognostic index were determined from ROC analyses by maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). Sensitivity and specificity at the selected thresholds were reported for 1-, 3-, and 5-year outcomes.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software ver. 3.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

A total of 497 DDLT cases were performed at Samsung Medical Center between 1 January 2000 and 31 December 2020. Of these cases, 169 were excluded due to re-DDLT (n = 76), the absence of data required to calculate recipient or donor-based scores (n = 87), or the absence of recorded survival time (n = 6). The final study cohort consisted of 328 donor–recipient pairs that met the inclusion criteria. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the donors.

Discrimination analysis

The discriminatory power of each index was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival curves, c-statistics, and the AUC.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier survival curves for overall survival (Fig. 1) demonstrated significant stratification across quartiles for scores such as MELD (P < 0.001) and MELD-Na (P < 0.002). The GEMA (P = 0.021) and GEMA-Na (P = 0.014) also indicated significant, although less pronounced, stratification. However, donor-specific scores such as KDPI (P = 0.534) and K-KDPI (P = 0.816) did not significantly differ in survival rates between quartiles, indicating that they may have weaker discriminatory power with respect to overall survival (Supplementary Fig. 1).

DISCUSSION

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of 8 predictive indices (MELD, MELD-Na, GEMA, GEMA-Na, DROP, DRI, KDPI, and K-KDPI) for predicting overall and graft survival following DDLT. By analyzing both calibration and discrimination across short- and long-term survival periods, the findings offer valuable insights into the relative strengths and limitations of these scores in clinical practice.

MELD-Na was identified as the most effective and reliable score for predicting overall and graft survival. The model demonstrated consistently high discrimination ability, as evidenced by superior concordance statistics (c-index) and AUC values, and exhibited robust calibration performance across all time points. These characteristics highlight the critical role of the MELD-Na score in recipient prioritization and short-term survival prediction. Recipient-based scores, including MELD, GEMA, and GEMA-Na, showed satisfactory performance. MELD showed comparable discrimination to MELD-Na but slightly lower calibration. GEMA-based scores showed moderate utility, especially in addressing gender disparities in access to transplantation. However, their predictive capacity was inferior to that of MELD-Na. Donor-based scores, including KDPI, K-KDPI, and DRI, showed adequate calibration, as determined by Hosmer-Lemeshow tests. Nevertheless, their low R2 values suggest that they are more useful for evaluating donor organ quality than for directly predicting survival outcomes.

This study is distinctive in its integration of both calibration and discrimination analyses of recipient- and donor-based scores using a long-term, single-center dataset. In contrast to previous studies that have focused on individual indices or specific outcomes, this analysis evaluates 8 indices across multiple survival time points, thereby providing a comprehensive perspective on their relative strengths and applications. The findings underscore the complementary roles of these indices in informing clinical decision-making, particularly regarding the balance between recipient prioritization and donor evaluation.

This study has several limitations. First, it is based on a single institution, and it is a retrospective study. Therefore, the results may not be generalizable to other populations or healthcare systems. Additionally, this study did not consider dynamic factors, such as posttransplant complications or changes in recipient health, which are important in predicting long-term outcomes.

This study emphasizes the critical role of MELD-Na as the most reliable and discriminatory score for forecasting both overall and graft survival rates. Furthermore, recipient-based indices such as MELD and GEMA demonstrated utility, while donor-based scores like KDPI and K-KDPI provided robust calibration but limited predictive discrimination. By addressing the complementary strengths and limitations of these indices, this study provides a foundation for advancing predictive tools and refining organ allocation strategies in liver transplantation. The integration of recipient- and donor-specific factors represents a promising direction for improving survival outcomes and addressing the persistent challenges in organ allocation. Future research should focus on validating these findings across diverse populations and exploring advanced predictive technologies, such as machine learning, to further optimize transplant outcomes.
