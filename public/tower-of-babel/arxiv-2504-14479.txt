Stochastic Norton dynamics: An alternative approach for the computation of transport coefficients in dissipative particle dynamics
Xinyi Wu, Xiaocheng Shang

Abstract
We study a novel alternative approach for the computation of transport coefficients at mesoscales. While standard nonequilibrium molecular dynamics (NEMD) approaches fix the forcing and measure the average induced flux in the system driven out of equilibrium, the so-called “stochastic Norton dynamics” instead fixes the value of the flux and measures the average magnitude of the forcing needed to induce it. We extend recent results obtained in Langevin dynamics to consider the generalisation of the stochastic Norton dynamics in the popular dissipative particle dynamics (DPD) at mesoscales, important for a wide range of complex fluids and soft matter applications. We demonstrate that the responses profiles for both the NEMD and stochastic Norton dynamics approaches coincide in both linear and nonlinear regimes, indicating that the stochastic Norton dynamics can indeed act as an alternative approach for the computation of transport coefficients, including the mobility and the shear viscosity, as the NEMD dynamics. In addition, based on the linear response of the DPD system with small perturbations, we derive a closed-form expression for the shear viscosity, and numerically validate its effectiveness with various types of external forces. Moreover, our numerical experiments demonstrate that the stochastic Norton dynamics approach clearly outperforms the NEMD dynamics in controlling the asymptotic variance, a key metric to measure the associated computational costs, particularly in the high friction limit.



1
Introduction

Molecular dynamics is a computer simulation method for analysing the physical movements of atoms and molecules, and has been widely used in chemical physics, materials science, and biophysics
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. In classical molecular dynamics, the equations of motion of individual atoms and molecules are governed by Newton’s law in the microcanonical ensemble, where the energy (i.e., the Hamiltonian of the system) is conserved. However, the constant energy setting is not ideal for a real-world laboratory environment since energy, as an extensive variable, is dependent on the size of the system. Instead, the canonical ensemble, where the temperature is conserved using suitable “thermostat” techniques, is typically used.

One popular thermostat is the Langevin dynamics, commonly used for molecular dynamics simulations. In Langevin dynamics, in addition to the conservative forces, each particle is subject to dissipative and collisional interactions with the particles of an artificial “heat bath”. Langevin dynamics supplements the conservative Newtonian equations of motion with balanced damping and stochastic terms in such a way that the desired target system temperature is maintained. Langevin dynamics has been widely applied in a range of applications in molecular dynamics
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
; more recently, variants of Langevin dynamics (e.g., adaptive Langevin dynamics) have attracted increasing attention in data science
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
.

However, as previously discussed in physics literature
[
10
]
, Langevin dynamics is not suitable for simulations where hydrodynamic interactions are of interest. In order to be consistent with hydrodynamics, a particle model should respect Galilean invariance, and particularly should conserve momentum
[
11
]
; however, the momentum is not conserved in Langevin dynamics. Moreover, due to the violation of global momentum conservation, Langevin dynamics can lead to nonphysical screening of hydrodynamic interactions
[
12
]
. Therefore, when hydrodynamic interactions are important, standard thermostats should be replaced by momentum-conserving thermostats, in particular the so-called dissipative particle dynamics (DPD) method proposed by Hoogerbrugge and Koelman
[
11
]
.

In DPD, each particle is regarded as a coarse-grained particle consisting of several molecules. In its traditional formulation, these DPD particles interact in a soft (and short-ranged) potential, which allows larger integration timesteps than would be possible in molecular dynamics. Simultaneously, the coarse graining nature of DPD particles means that the number of degrees of freedom required is (significantly) decreased. It is worth mentioning that alternative potentials have also been widely used in DPD, including the widely used Lennard-Jones potential in polymer melts
[
13
]
. As in Langevin dynamics, a thermostat consisting of well-balanced damping and stochastic terms is applied to each particle in DPD. However, unlike Langevin dynamics, both damping and stochastic terms in DPD are pairwise. Given also that the damping term is based on relative velocities, the formulation of DPD leads to the conservation of both the angular momentum and the linear momentum, essential for the preservation of the hydrodynamics. Moreover, the dependence on the relative velocity makes DPD a profile-unbiased thermostat (PUT)
[
14
,
15
]
by construction and therefore it is an ideal thermostat for nonequilibrium molecular dynamics
[
16
]
. Due to the aforementioned properties, DPD has been widely used in a large number of complex fluids and soft matter applications, including colloids
[
17
]
, blood
[
18
]
, and polymers
[
19
]
(see more applications in an excellent review
[
20
]
and references therein).

One particular challenge in Langevin dynamics and DPD, and molecular dynamics in general, is the computation of transport coefficients (e.g., mobility, diffusion coefficient, and shear viscosity) in an accurate and efficient manner. Transport coefficients measure how rapidly a perturbed system returns to equilibrium. One standard approach in equilibrium is the celebrated Green–Kubo formulas
[
21
,
22
]
based on integrals of suitable time correlation functions
[
23
,
24
]
. For example, the shear viscosity can be calculated by integrating the stress-stress autocorrelation function (see examples in the DPD literature
[
25
,
26
]
). However, time correlation functions represent the average response to the naturally occurring (therefore very small) fluctuations in the system properties. Given that the equilibrium stress is subject to large fluctuations in measuring the shear viscosity
[
27
]
, the signal-to-noise ratio is particularly unfavorable at long times, where there may be a significant (but unrelated) contribution to the integral defining a transport coefficient
[
1
,
28
]
. Therefore, the simulation must be run for long times in order to have highly converged values for the Green–Kubo integrand over the range of times which contribute significantly to the integral
[
28
]
.

Another popular approach is the so-called nonequilibrium molecular dynamics (NEMD) method, where the steady state of the system is subject to external perturbations (either stationary fluxes or spatial gradients of some quantities), in order to artificially induce larger fluctuations, thereby improving the signal-to-noise ratio of the measured response
[
15
,
29
]
. By measuring the steady state response to such a perturbation, the decay to the equilibrated state is then related to the corresponding transport coefficient, which avoids problems with long-time behaviour of correlation functions
[
1
]
. However, estimators derived from these approaches also suffer from large statistical errors, as quantified in
[
30
, Proposition 2.4]
. As a consequence, convergence requires the simulation of very long trajectories, resulting in high computational cost. To this end, a key metric to measure this cost is, for any given method, the asymptotic variance of estimators of the transport coefficient. Although variance
reduction techniques have been proposed to reduce the variance in the computation of transport coefficients (see
[
31
]
for a recent review), efficiently estimating these quantities is a challenging but still important area of research.

While standard NEMD approaches fix the forcing and measure the average induced flux in the system driven out of equilibrium, the so-called “Norton dynamics”, studied by Evans and Morriss in the 1980s, instead fixes the value of the flux and measures the average magnitude of the forcing needed to induce it
[
32
]
. While showing promising results, the Norton dynamics was only considered in a deterministic setting. Recently, Blassel and Stoltz extended the Norton dynamics to a stochastic setting
[
33
]
. They first developed a general formal theory for a broad class of diffusion processes, before specialising it to Langevin dynamics. Moreover, they provided numerical evidence that the “stochastic Norton dynamics” provides an equivalent measure of the linear response, and demonstrated that this equivalence extends well beyond the linear response regime. In this article, our aim is to extend the results in
[
33
]
to consider the generalisation of the stochastic Norton dynamics in DPD, important for a wide range of complex fluids and soft matter applications. Moreover, we will compare the performance of the stochastic Norton dynamics with NEMD in the DPD context, focusing on the computation of the mobility and the shear viscosity.

The rest of the article is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we describe the mathematical formulations of DPD and its variants with NEMD (i.e., DPD-NEMD) and the stochastic Norton dynamics (i.e., DPD-Norton), respectively. Section
3
discusses the numerical methods used for the integration of the stochastic Norton dynamics in DPD. A variety of numerical experiments are performed in Section
4
to compare all the approaches described in the article for the computation of the mobility and the shear viscosity. Our findings are summarised in Section
5
.

2
Mathematical formulations

In this section, we introduce the mathematical formulations of DPD and its DPD-NEMD and DPD-Norton variants, respectively.

2.1
DPD

Originally updated in discrete timesteps, DPD was later reformulated by Español and Warren
[
34
]
as a proper statistical mechanics model written as a system of Itō SDEs. Consider an
N
-particle system evolving in dimension
d
with position
{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
and momentum
{\mathbf{p}}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
, the equations of motion of the DPD system (known as the “reference dynamics”), in a vector form, are given by
[
10
]

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t\,,

(1a)

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{p}}

\displaystyle=-\nabla U({\mathbf{q}}){\rm d}t-\gamma{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}({\mathbf{q}}){\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t+\sigma{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}({\mathbf{q}}){\rm d}{\mathrm{\mathbf{W}}}\,,

(1b)

where
{\mathbf{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN\times dN}
is the diagonal mass matrix,
-\nabla U
is the conservative force represented in terms of a potential energy function
U=U({\mathbf{q}})
(
\nabla
represents the gradient operator),
\gamma
and
\sigma
represent the dissipative and random strengths, respectively, and
{\mathrm{\mathbf{W}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times 1}
is a vector of
S=N(N-1)/2
independent Wiener processes with mean zero and variance
{\rm d}t
. Moreover,
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}({\mathbf{q}})\in\mathbb{R}^{dN\times dN}
and
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}({\mathbf{q}})\in\mathbb{R}^{dN\times S}
are two position-dependent matrices that satisfy the following relation:

{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}={\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{\mathrm{T}}\,,

(2)

where the matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
can be written explicitly as, when
N\geq 5
,

{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{q})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}\omega_{12}^{{\mathrm{R}}}{\mathbf{e}}_{12}&\omega_{13}^{{\mathrm{R}}}{\mathbf{e}}_{13}&\cdots&\omega_{1N}^{{\mathrm{R}}}{\mathbf{e}}_{1N}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\
\omega_{21}^{{\mathrm{R}}}{\mathbf{e}}_{21}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}&\omega_{23}^{{\mathrm{R}}}{\mathbf{e}}_{23}&\omega_{24}^{{\mathrm{R}}}{\mathbf{e}}_{24}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\omega_{31}^{{\mathrm{R}}}{\mathbf{e}}_{31}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}&\omega_{32}^{{\mathrm{R}}}{\mathbf{e}}_{32}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\iddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\omega_{N1}^{{\mathrm{R}}}{\mathbf{e}}_{N1}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\omega_{N,N-1}^{{\mathrm{R}}}{\mathbf{e}}_{N,N-1}\end{array}\right)\,,

(3)

where

\omega_{ij}^{{\mathrm{R}}}=\omega^{{\mathrm{R}}}\left(r_{ij}\right)
is a weight function defined in the DPD system
[
23
,
10
]

and
\mathbf{e}_{ij}=\left({\mathbf{q}}_{i}-{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\right)/r_{ij}
is the unit vector pointing from particle
j
to particle
i
, with
r_{ij}=\left|{\mathbf{q}}_{ij}\right|=\left|{\mathbf{q}}_{i}-{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\right|
being the distance between the two particles.

In addition to (
2
), the following relation needs to be satisfied

\sigma^{2}=2\gamma k_{\mathrm{B}}T\,,

(4)

where
k_{\mathrm{B}}
and
T
are the Boltzmann constant and the equilibrium temperature, respectively, in the DPD system (
1
) in order to preserve the momentum-constrained canonical ensemble with density

\rho_{\beta}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})={Z}^{-1}\exp(-\beta H({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}}))\times\delta\!\left[\sum_{i}p^{x}_{i}-\pi_{x}\right]\delta\!\left[\sum_{i}p^{y}_{i}-\pi_{y}\right]\delta\!\left[\sum_{i}p^{z}_{i}-\pi_{z}\right]\,,

(5)

where
Z
is a suitable normalising constant (i.e., the partition function),
\beta^{-1}=k_{\mathrm{B}}T
,
H({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})={\mathbf{p}}^{\mathrm{T}}{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}/2+U({\mathbf{q}})
represents the system Hamiltonian, and
\boldsymbol{\pi}=\left(\pi_{x},\pi_{y},\pi_{z}\right)
is the linear momentum vector. It is worth mentioning that the ergodicity of the DPD system has only been established in the case of high particle density in one dimension by Shardlow and Yan
[
35
]
; extending the result to higher dimensions with more general conditions is highly nontrivial.

2.2
DPD-NEMD

In standard NEMD approaches, we consider adding an external nongradient force, in the form of a smooth vector field
{\mathbf{F}}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
, in the drift of a “reference dynamics” in equilibrium, leading to a “perturbed dynamics”. As in
[
33
]
, we restrict our attention in this article to position-dependent external force, i.e.,
{\mathbf{F}}={\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})
, in the NEMD method applied to the DPD system (
1
). In this case, the equations of motion of the DPD-NEMD dynamics are given by

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t\,,

(6a)

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{p}}

\displaystyle=[-\nabla U({\mathbf{q}})+\eta{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})]{\rm d}t-\gamma{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}({\mathbf{q}}){\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t+\sigma{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}({\mathbf{q}}){\rm d}{\mathrm{\mathbf{W}}}\,,

(6b)

where
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
represents the strength of the force. In the case of
\eta=0
, the perturbed dynamics (
6
) reduces to the reference dynamics (
1
). As in
[
33
]
, we assume that, if
|\eta|
is small enough, the perturbed dynamics (
6
) is ergodic and admits a unique invariant probability measure. The corresponding expectation associated with the perturbed invariant probability measure is denoted by
\mathbb{E}_{\eta}
. Based on the linear response theory
[
36
,
29
]
, for a given response observable
R\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathbb{E}_{0}[R]=0

(7)

(i.e., zero expectation associated with the invariant probability measure of the reference dynamics (
1
)), the associated transport coefficient
\alpha
can be defined as

\alpha=\lim\limits_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\eta}[R]}{\eta}\,,

(8)

provided that it is well defined. The definition above suggests that one can first compute ergodic averages of
R
over trajectories of the perturbed dynamics (
6
), and then estimate the linear relation between
R
and
\eta
for one or several values of
\eta
in the linear response regime. We can further write down the finite difference estimator for the limit (
8
) as the following ergodic average:

\widehat{\alpha}_{T,\eta}=\frac{1}{\eta T}\int_{0}^{T}R\left({\mathbf{q}}_{t},{\mathbf{p}}_{t}\right)\,{\rm d}t\,.

(9)

2.3
DPD-Norton

While standard NEMD approaches, as shown in the previous subsection, fix the forcing and measure the average induced flux in the system driven out of equilibrium, an alternative approach, the stochastic Norton dynamics, instead fixes the value of the flux and measures the average magnitude of the forcing needed to induce it
[
33
]
. In the stochastic Norton dynamics approach, we only consider the response observable
R
in the following specific form, as in
[
33
]
:

R({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})={\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot{\mathbf{p}}\,,

(10)

where
{\mathbf{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
is a position-dependent vector function. When applied to the DPD system (
1
), the equations of motion of the DPD-Norton dynamics are given by

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{q}}_{t}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}_{t}{\rm d}t\,,

(11a)

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{p}}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla U\left({\mathbf{q}}_{t}\right){\rm d}t-\gamma{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\left({\mathbf{q}}_{t}\right){\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}_{t}{\rm d}t+\sigma{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\left({\mathbf{q}}_{t}\right){\rm d}{\mathrm{\mathbf{W}}}_{t}+{\mathbf{F}}\left({\mathbf{q}}_{t}\right){\rm d}\Lambda_{t}\,,

(11b)

\displaystyle R\left({\mathbf{q}}_{t},{\mathbf{p}}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=R\left({\mathbf{q}}_{0},{\mathbf{p}}_{0}\right)=r\,,

(11c)

where
r\in\mathbb{R}
represents the strength of the response, and
\Lambda_{t}
is a stochastic process required to constrain the response. Moreover,
\Lambda_{t}
can be defined as an Itô process and decomposed as follows:

\Lambda_{t}=\Lambda_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\lambda\left({\mathbf{q}}_{s},{\mathbf{p}}_{s}\right)\,{\rm d}s+\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t}\,,\quad\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left({\mathbf{q}}_{s},{\mathbf{p}}_{s}\right)\,{\rm d}{\mathrm{\mathbf{W}}}_{s}\,,

(12)

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times S}
are two functions. In what follows, unless necessary, the subscripts associated with
{\mathbf{q}}
,
{\mathbf{p}}
,
\Lambda
, and
{\mathrm{\mathbf{W}}}
will be dropped for notational simplicity. Note that, neglecting the zero-mean contribution of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, the average forcing in the stochastic Norton dynamics can then be defined as the expectation of
\lambda
, also known as the forcing observable. Based on the form of the response observable
R
in (
10
), we can write down the expression of the forcing observable
\lambda
associated with the DPD-Norton dynamics (
11
):

\lambda({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})=\frac{1}{{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})}\left({\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot\left[\nabla U({\mathbf{q}})+\gamma{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}({\mathbf{q}}){\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}\right]-\nabla{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}}){\mathbf{p}}\cdot{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}\right)\,.

(13)

Note that the controllability condition
{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})\neq 0
needs to be satisfied. As in
[
33
]
, we assume the existence and uniqueness of the invariant steady-state probability measure for the DPD-Norton dynamics (
11
), whose expectation is denoted by
\mathbb{E}^{*}_{r}
. We further assume that

\mathbb{E}^{*}_{0}[\lambda]=0\,,

(14)

which is the Norton counterpart of the centering condition (
7
) for the observable in DPD-NEMD simulations. When (
14
) holds, the transport coefficient for the DPD-Norton dynamics (
11
) can be defined by analogy with (
8
) as

\alpha^{*}=\lim\limits_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{r}{\mathbb{E}^{*}_{r}[\lambda]}\,,

(15)

provided that the limit exists. Assuming the DPD-Norton dynamics (
11
) is ergodic with respect to the steady-state, a natural estimator of the transport coefficient (
15
) can be constructed by replacing ensemble averages by trajectory averages:

\widehat{\alpha}^{*}_{T,r}=\frac{rT}{\int_{0}^{T}\lambda\left({\mathbf{q}}_{t},{\mathbf{p}}_{t}\right)\,{\rm d}t}\,,

(16)

which is similar to what is done in DPD-NEMD simulations (
9
).

3
Numerical methods

A great deal of effort has been devoted to developing accurate and efficient numerical methods to numerically discretise DPD and related systems. It has been demonstrated that splitting methods often outperform alternative schemes in various settings
[
37
,
23
,
10
,
13
,
38
,
39
]
. In this section, we will apply splitting methods in both the DPD-NEMD and DPD-Norton dynamics.

3.1
DPD-NEMD

We decompose the vector field of the DPD-NEMD system (
6
) into three pieces, labeled as
\mathrm{A}
,
\mathrm{B}
, and
\mathrm{O}
, respectively:

{\rm d}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\mathbf{q}}\\
{\mathbf{p}}\end{array}\right]=\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right]{\rm d}t}_{\mathrm{A}}+\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{0}\\
-\nabla U({\mathbf{q}})+\eta{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})\end{array}\right]{\rm d}t}_{\mathrm{B}}+\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{0}\\
-\gamma{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}({\mathbf{q}}){\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t+\sigma{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}({\mathbf{q}}){\rm d}{\mathrm{\mathbf{W}}}\end{array}\right]}_{\mathrm{O}}\,.

(17)

We can write down the generator of each piece as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{A}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\,,

(18a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}

\displaystyle=-\nabla U({\mathbf{q}})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}+\eta{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}\,,

(18b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{O}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}({\mathbf{q}}){\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}({\mathbf{q}})\left[{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}({\mathbf{q}})\right]^{\mathrm{T}}:\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}\,,

(18c)

where
\nabla^{2}
is the Hessian matrix and
:
represents the Frobenius inner product defined as
{\mathbf{A}}:{\mathbf{B}}=\mathrm{Tr}\left({\mathbf{A}}^{\mathrm{T}}{\mathbf{B}}\right)
. The generator of the DPD-NEMD system can be written as

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{O}}\,.

(19)

The elementary dynamics associated with
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{A}}
is given by

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t\,,

(20a)

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{p}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\,,

(20b)

while the elementary dynamics associated with
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}
is given by

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\,,

(21a)

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{p}}

\displaystyle=[-\nabla U({\mathbf{q}})+\eta{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})]{\rm d}t\,.

(21b)

Both elementary dynamics (
20
)–(
21
) can be solved exactly. The elementary dynamics associated with
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{O}}
is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process on the momentum

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\,,

(22a)

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{p}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}({\mathbf{q}}){\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t+\sigma{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}({\mathbf{q}}){\rm d}{\mathrm{\mathbf{W}}}\,,

(22b)

and can also be solved exactly based on each interacting pair (see more details in
[
23
,
40
,
39
]
). Moreover, the flow map (or phase space propagator) of the DPD-NEMD system is given by the shorthand notation

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\exp\left(t\mathcal{L}\right)\,,

(23)

where the exponential map is used to formally denote the solution operator. A number of approximations of
\mathcal{F}_{t}
can be obtained as products (taken in different arrangements) of exponentials of the splitting terms. However, it turns out that different splittings (other than “A”, “B”, and “O” in the current article, e.g., the stochastic position Verlet and stochastic velocity Verlet methods in
[
41
]
where the system is split into two elementary dynamics) and/or combinations give dramatically different performance in practice
[
37
,
23
,
10
,
13
,
39
]
. In particular, it has been demonstrated in various numerical experiments, on both equilibrium and
transport properties that the so-called “ABOBA” splitting method
[
39
]
outperforms popular alternative schemes in the literature. Therefore, we will apply the ABOBA method in both NEMD and stochastic Norton dynamics approaches in DPD. To this end, the phase space propagation of the ABOBA method can be written as

e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ABOBA}}}=e^{\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{A}}}e^{\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}}e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{O}}}e^{\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}}e^{\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{A}}}\,,

(24)

where
\exp\left(\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{f}\right)
denotes the phase space propagator associated with the corresponding vector field
f
. Note that the steplengths associated with various operations are uniform and span the interval
\Delta t
. Therefore, each of the A and B steps in (
24
) is taken with a steplength of
\Delta t/2
, while a steplength of
\Delta t
is used in the O step. The detailed integration steps of the ABOBA method for the DPD-NEMD system can be written as follows:

Step 1
: for all particles,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{q}}^{n}+(\Delta t/2){\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}^{n}\,,

(25)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}^{n+1/3}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{p}}^{n}-(\Delta t/2)\nabla U\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)+(\Delta t/2)\eta{\mathbf{F}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)\,.

(26)

Step 2
: for each interacting pair within cutoff radius (
r_{ij}<r_{\mathrm{c}}
), where
r_{\mathrm{c}}
is the cutoff radius, in a successive manner,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}_{i}^{n+2/3}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{p}}_{i}^{n+1/3}+m_{ij}\left[\Delta v_{ij}^{\mathrm{D}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2},{\mathbf{p}}^{n+1/3}\right)+\Delta v_{ij}^{\mathrm{R}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)\right]{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}^{n+1/2}\,,

(27)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}_{j}^{n+2/3}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{p}}_{j}^{n+1/3}-m_{ij}\left[\Delta v_{ij}^{\mathrm{D}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2},{\mathbf{p}}^{n+1/3}\right)+\Delta v_{ij}^{\mathrm{R}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)\right]{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}^{n+1/2}\,,

(28)

where
m_{ij}=m_{i}m_{j}/\left(m_{i}+m_{j}\right)
, with
m_{i}
being the mass of the particle
i
, is the “reduced mass” and

\Delta v_{ij}^{\mathrm{D}}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})=\left[\mathbf{e}_{ij}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{ij}\right]\left(e^{-\tau\left(r_{ij}\right)\Delta t}-1\right)\,,\quad\Delta v_{ij}^{\mathrm{R}}({\mathbf{q}})=\frac{\sigma\omega^{\mathrm{R}}\left(r_{ij}\right)}{m_{ij}}\sqrt{\frac{1-e^{-2\tau\left(r_{ij}\right)\Delta t}}{2\tau\left(r_{ij}\right)}}\mathrm{R}^{n}_{ij}\,,

(29)

where
\mathbf{v}_{ij}=\mathbf{p}_{i}/m_{i}-\mathbf{p}_{j}/m_{j}
is the relative velocity,
\tau\left(r_{ij}\right)=\gamma\omega^{\mathrm{D}}\left(r_{ij}\right)/m_{ij}
,
\mathrm{R}_{ij}
is a normally distributed variable with zero mean and unit variance.
\omega^{{\mathrm{D}}}\left(r_{ij}\right)
is another weight function
[
23
,
10
]
that satisfies the relation of
\omega^{\mathrm{D}}\left(r_{ij}\right)=\left[\omega^{\mathrm{R}}\left(r_{ij}\right)\right]^{2}
. Note that one of the two weight functions can be arbitrarily chosen, for instance, a popular choice of
\omega^{\mathrm{R}}\left(r_{ij}\right)
that is also used in this article is given by

\omega^{\mathrm{R}}\left(r_{ij}\right)=\begin{cases}1-r_{ij}/r_{\mathrm{c}}\,,&r_{ij}<r_{\mathrm{c}}\,,\\
\quad\quad 0\,,&r_{ij}\geq r_{\mathrm{c}}\,.\end{cases}

(30)

Step 3
: for all particles,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{p}}^{n+2/3}-(\Delta t/2)\nabla U\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)+(\Delta t/2)\eta{\mathbf{F}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)\,,

(31)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{q}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}+(\Delta t/2){\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}^{n+1}\,.

(32)

3.2
DPD-Norton

We can similarly decompose the vector field of the DPD-Norton system (
11
) into three pieces, again labeled as
\mathrm{A}
,
\mathrm{B}
, and
\mathrm{O}
, respectively:

\displaystyle{\rm d}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\mathbf{q}}\\
{\mathbf{p}}\end{array}\right]=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t\\
{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}}){\rm d}\Lambda^{\mathrm{A}}\end{array}\right]}_{\mathrm{A}}+\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{0}\\
-\nabla U({\mathbf{q}}){\rm d}t+{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}}){\rm d}\Lambda^{\mathrm{B}}\end{array}\right]}_{\mathrm{B}}

(33)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{0}\\
-\gamma{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}({\mathbf{q}}){\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t+\sigma{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}({\mathbf{q}}){\rm d}{\mathrm{\mathbf{W}}}+{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}}){\rm d}\Lambda^{\mathrm{O}}\end{array}\right]}_{\mathrm{O}}\,,

where the stochastic process
\Lambda
are divided into three parts corresponding to each piece,
\Lambda^{\mathrm{A}}
,
\Lambda^{\mathrm{B}}
, and
\Lambda^{\mathrm{O}}
, respectively. As demonstrated in
[
33
]
, the expressions of the function
\lambda
and the martingale
\widetilde{\Lambda}
can be made precise, thereby allowing us to write down the DPD-Norton dynamics without explicitly referencing to the forcing. We can write down the generator of each piece as follows:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{\mathrm{A}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}-\frac{\nabla{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}}){\mathbf{p}}\cdot{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}}{{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})}{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}\,,

(34a)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{\mathrm{B}}

\displaystyle=-\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{{\mathbf{F}},{\mathbf{G}}}({\mathbf{q}})\nabla U({\mathbf{q}})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}\,,

(34b)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}_{\mathrm{O}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{{\mathbf{F}},{\mathbf{G}}}({\mathbf{q}}){\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}({\mathbf{q}}){\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{{\mathbf{F}},{\mathbf{G}}}({\mathbf{q}}){\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}({\mathbf{q}})\left[\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{{\mathbf{F}},{\mathbf{G}}}({\mathbf{q}}){\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}({\mathbf{q}})\right]^{\mathrm{T}}:\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{p}}\,,

(34c)

where the nonorthogonal projector-valued map
\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{{\mathbf{F}},{\mathbf{G}}}({\mathbf{q}})
is given by

\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{{\mathbf{F}},{\mathbf{G}}}({\mathbf{q}})={\mathbf{I}}-\frac{{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})\otimes{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})}{{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})}\,,

(35)

where
\otimes
represents the Kronecker product and
{\mathbf{I}}
is an identity matrix. The generator of the DPD-Norton system can then be written as

\mathfrak{L}=\mathfrak{L}_{\mathrm{A}}+\mathfrak{L}_{\mathrm{B}}+\mathfrak{L}_{\mathrm{O}}\,.

(36)

It is worth mentioning that the decomposition (
33
) is motivated by the fact that each elementary dynamics individually preserves the response observable (
10
), i.e.,
\mathfrak{L}_{\mathrm{A}}R=\mathfrak{L}_{\mathrm{B}}R=\mathfrak{L}_{\mathrm{O}}R=0
. Therefore, the solutions and approximation to the elementary dynamics are designed in such a way that each of them preserves the response.

The elementary dynamics associated with
\mathfrak{L}_{\mathrm{B}}
is given by

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\,,

(37a)

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{p}}

\displaystyle=-\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{{\mathbf{F}},{\mathbf{G}}}({\mathbf{q}})\nabla U({\mathbf{q}}){\rm d}t\,,

(37b)

while the elementary dynamics associated with
\mathfrak{L}_{\mathrm{O}}
is given by

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\,,

(38a)

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{p}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{{\mathbf{F}},{\mathbf{G}}}({\mathbf{q}})\left[-\gamma{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}({\mathbf{q}}){\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t+\sigma{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}({\mathbf{q}}){\rm d}{\mathrm{\mathbf{W}}}\right]\,.

(38b)

Similar to the cases for the DPD-NEMD system in the previous subsection, both elementary dynamics (
37
)–(
38
) can be solved exactly. However, the elementary dynamics associated with
\mathfrak{L}_{\mathrm{A}}

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}{\rm d}t\,,

(39a)

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\mathbf{p}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\nabla{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}}){\mathbf{p}}\cdot{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}}{{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})}{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}}){\rm d}t\,,

(39b)

is not exactly solvable, and has to be numerically approximated.

In describing the splitting method associated with the DPD-Norton dynamics (
33
), we introduce a discrete flow,
\Phi_{\Delta t,r}
, associated with each elementary dynamics. More specifically, the discrete flow associated with the elementary A-dynamics (
39
) is given by

\Phi^{\mathrm{A}}_{\Delta t,r}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}},\ell)=\left({\mathbf{q}}+\Delta t{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}},{\mathbf{p}}+\xi_{\Delta t,r}^{\mathrm{A}}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}}){\mathbf{F}}\left({\mathbf{q}}+\Delta t{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}\right),\ell+\xi_{\Delta t,r}^{\mathrm{A}}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})\right)\,,

(40)

where
\xi^{\mathrm{A}}_{\Delta t,r}\in\mathbb{R}
is a Lagrange multiplier to ensure the response will be preserved at a constant
r
for each elementary dynamics, and
\ell\in\mathbb{R}
is an auxiliary variable to accumulate the bounded-variation component of Lagrange multipliers during the integration step (see more details in
[
33
]
). When the response observable
R
is of a specific form as in (
10
), we can explicitly solve

{\mathbf{G}}\left({\mathbf{q}}+\Delta t{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}\right)\cdot\left({\mathbf{p}}+\xi_{\Delta t,r}^{\mathrm{A}}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}}){\mathbf{F}}\left({\mathbf{q}}+\Delta t{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}\right)\right)=r

(41)

for the Lagrange multiplier
\xi^{\mathrm{A}}_{\Delta t,r}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})
:

\xi^{\mathrm{A}}_{\Delta t,r}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})=\frac{r-{\mathbf{G}}\left({\mathbf{q}}+\Delta t{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{p}}}{{\mathbf{F}}\left({\mathbf{q}}+\Delta t{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{G}}\left({\mathbf{q}}+\Delta t{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}\right)}\,.

(42)

Similarly, the discrete flow associated with the elementary B-dynamics (
37
) is given by

\Phi^{\mathrm{B}}_{\Delta t,r}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}},\ell)=\left({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}}-\Delta t\nabla U({\mathbf{q}})+\xi_{\Delta t,r}^{\mathrm{B}}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}}){\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}}),\ell+\xi_{\Delta t,r}^{\mathrm{B}}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})\right)\,.

(43)

Again, for a specific form of the response observable
R
as in (
10
), we can explicitly solve

{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot\left({\mathbf{p}}-\Delta t\nabla U({\mathbf{q}})+\xi_{\Delta t,r}^{\mathrm{B}}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}}){\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})\right)=r

(44)

for the Lagrange multiplier
\xi^{\mathrm{B}}_{\Delta t,r}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})
:

\xi^{\mathrm{B}}_{\Delta t,r}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})=\frac{r-{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot\left({\mathbf{p}}-\Delta t\nabla U({\mathbf{q}})\right)}{{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})}\,,

(45)

in which case, (
43
) coincides with the analytical integration of the elementary B-dynamics (
37
) over one time step
\Delta t
when the constant flux condition (
44
) is satisfied. Note that, unlike the other two elementary dynamics (i.e., (
37
) and (
39
)), the discrete flow associated with the elementary O-dynamics (
38
) is stochastic, and is given by

\widehat{\Phi}^{\mathrm{O}}_{\Delta t,r}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}},\Delta{\mathbf{p}},\ell)=\widehat{\Phi}^{\mathrm{O}_{\xi}}_{\Delta t,r}\circ\widehat{\Phi}^{\mathrm{O}_{N-1,N}}_{\Delta t,r}\circ\cdots\circ\widehat{\Phi}^{\mathrm{O}_{1,3}}_{\Delta t,r}\circ\widehat{\Phi}^{\mathrm{O}_{1,2}}_{\Delta t,r}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}},\mathbf{0},0)\,,

(46)

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi}^{\mathrm{O}_{i,j}}_{\Delta t,r}\left({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}},\Delta{\mathbf{p}},\ell|R_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\left({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}}+m_{ij}\left[\Delta v_{ij}^{{\mathrm{D}}}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})+\Delta v_{ij}^{{\mathrm{R}}}({\mathbf{q}})\right]\widehat{{\mathbf{e}}}_{ij},\Delta{\mathbf{p}}+m_{ij}\Delta v_{ij}^{{\mathrm{D}}}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})\widehat{{\mathbf{e}}}_{ij},\ell\right)\,,

(47a)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi}^{\mathrm{O}_{\xi}}_{\Delta t,r}\left({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}},\Delta{\mathbf{p}},\ell|R_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\left({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}}+\widehat{\xi}_{\Delta t,r}^{\mathrm{O}}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}}){\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}}),\Delta{\mathbf{p}},\ell-\frac{{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot\Delta{\mathbf{p}}}{{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})}\right)\,,

(47b)

with
\widehat{{\mathbf{e}}}_{ij}
being defined as

\widehat{{\mathbf{e}}}_{ij}=\left(\mathbf{0},\dots,\mathbf{0},\underbrace{{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}^{\mathrm{T}}}_{d(i-1)+1,\dots,di},\mathbf{0},\dots,\mathbf{0},\underbrace{-{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}^{\mathrm{T}}}_{d(j-1)+1,\dots,dj},\mathbf{0},\dots,0\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}\,.

(48)

For a specific form of the response observable
R
as in (
10
), we can again explicitly solve

{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot\left({\mathbf{p}}+\sum_{i}\sum_{j>i}m_{ij}\left[\Delta v_{ij}^{{\mathrm{D}}}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})+\Delta v_{ij}^{{\mathrm{R}}}({\mathbf{q}})\right]\widehat{{\mathbf{e}}}_{ij}+\widehat{\xi}_{\Delta t,r}^{\mathrm{O}}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}}){\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})\right)=r

(49)

for the Lagrange multiplier
\widehat{\xi}^{\mathrm{O}}_{\Delta t,r}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})
:

\widehat{\xi}^{\mathrm{O}}_{\Delta t,r}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})=\frac{r-{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot\left({\mathbf{p}}+\sum_{i}\sum_{j>i}m_{ij}\left[\Delta v_{ij}^{\mathrm{D}}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})+\Delta v_{ij}^{\mathrm{R}}({\mathbf{q}})\right]\widehat{{\mathbf{e}}}_{ij}\right)}{{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})}\,,

(50)

in which case, (
46
) is expected to be a second order approximation of the analytical integration of the elementary O-dynamics (
38
) over one time step
\Delta t
when the constant flux condition (
49
) is satisfied
[
39
]
. Since the contribution of the random noise
R_{ij}
to the Lagrange multiplier
\widehat{\xi}^{\mathrm{O}}_{\Delta t,r}({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})
is a centered Gaussian, we can remove it entirely to only accumulate the nonmartingale component in
\ell
, which is equivalent to a variance reduction technique
[
33
]
.
We will again apply the ABOBA method, whose update rule can be written as

\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1},{\mathbf{p}}^{n+1},\ell^{n+1}\right)=\Phi^{\mathrm{A}}_{\Delta t/2,r}\circ\Phi^{\mathrm{B}}_{\Delta t/2,r}\circ\widehat{\Phi}^{\mathrm{O}}_{\Delta t,r}\left(\cdot|R_{ij}\right)\circ\Phi^{\mathrm{B}}_{\Delta t/2,r}\circ\Phi^{\mathrm{A}}_{\Delta t/2,r}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n},{\mathbf{p}}^{n},\ell^{n}\right)\,.

(51)

The forcing observable
\lambda
can then be estimated by averaging the auxiliary variable
\ell
in each increment. The detailed integration steps of the ABOBA method for the DPD-Norton system can be written as follows:

Step 1
: starting from
\ell^{n}=0
, for all particles,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{q}}^{n}+(\Delta t/2){\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}^{n}\,,

(52)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}^{n+1/5}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{p}}^{n}+\xi^{\mathrm{A}}_{\Delta t/2,r}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n},{\mathbf{p}}^{n}\right){\mathbf{F}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)\,,

(53)

\displaystyle\ell^{n+1/5}

\displaystyle=\ell^{n}+\xi^{\mathrm{A}}_{\Delta t/2,r}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n},{\mathbf{p}}^{n}\right)\,,

(54)

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathbf{p}}}^{n+2/5}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{p}}^{n+1/5}-(\Delta t/2)\nabla U\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)\,,

(55)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}^{n+2/5}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{{\mathbf{p}}}^{n+2/5}+\xi^{\mathrm{B}}_{\Delta t/2,r}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2},{\mathbf{p}}^{n+1/5}\right){\mathbf{F}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)\,,

(56)

\displaystyle\ell^{n+2/5}

\displaystyle=\ell^{n+1/5}+\xi^{\mathrm{B}}_{\Delta t/2,r}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2},{\mathbf{p}}^{n+1/5}\right)\,.

(57)

Step 2
: starting from
\Delta{\mathbf{p}}^{n}=\mathbf{0}
, for each interacting pair within cutoff radius (
r_{ij}<r_{\mathrm{c}}
), in a successive manner,

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathbf{p}}}_{i}^{n+3/5}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{p}}_{i}^{n+2/5}+m_{ij}\left[\Delta v_{ij}^{{\mathrm{D}}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2},{\mathbf{p}}^{n+2/5}\right)+\Delta v_{ij}^{{\mathrm{R}}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)\right]{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}^{n+1/2}\,,

(58)

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathbf{p}}}_{j}^{n+3/5}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{p}}_{j}^{n+2/5}-m_{ij}\left[\Delta v_{ij}^{{\mathrm{D}}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2},{\mathbf{p}}^{n+2/5}\right)+\Delta v_{ij}^{{\mathrm{R}}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)\right]{\mathbf{e}}_{ij}^{n+1/2}\,,

(59)

\displaystyle\Delta{\mathbf{p}}^{n+1}_{i}

\displaystyle=\Delta{\mathbf{p}}^{n}_{i}+m_{ij}\Delta v_{ij}^{{\mathrm{D}}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2},{\mathbf{p}}^{n+2/5}\right){\mathbf{e}}_{ij}^{n+1/2}\,,

(60)

\displaystyle\Delta{\mathbf{p}}^{n+1}_{j}

\displaystyle=\Delta{\mathbf{p}}^{n}_{j}-m_{ij}\Delta v_{ij}^{{\mathrm{D}}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2},{\mathbf{p}}^{n+2/5}\right){\mathbf{e}}_{ij}^{n+1/2}\,,

(61)

for all particles,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}^{n+3/5}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{{\mathbf{p}}}^{n+3/5}+\widehat{\xi}^{\mathrm{O}}_{\Delta t,r}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2},{\mathbf{p}}^{n+2/5}\right){\mathbf{F}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)\,,

(62)

\displaystyle\ell^{n+3/5}

\displaystyle=\ell^{n+2/5}-\frac{{\mathbf{G}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)\cdot\Delta{\mathbf{p}}^{n+1}}{{\mathbf{F}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{G}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)}\,.

(63)

Step 3
: for all particles,

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathbf{p}}}^{n+4/5}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{p}}^{n+3/5}-(\Delta t/2)\nabla U\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)\,,

(64)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}^{n+4/5}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{{\mathbf{p}}}^{n+4/5}+\xi^{\mathrm{B}}_{\Delta t/2,r}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2},{\mathbf{p}}^{n+3/5}\right){\mathbf{F}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}\right)\,,

(65)

\displaystyle\ell^{n+4/5}

\displaystyle=\ell^{n+3/5}+\xi^{\mathrm{B}}_{\Delta t/2,r}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2},{\mathbf{p}}^{n+3/5}\right)\,,

(66)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{q}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2}+(\Delta t/2){\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}^{n+4/5}\,,

(67)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}^{n+1}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{p}}^{n+4/5}+\xi^{\mathrm{A}}_{\Delta t/2,r}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2},{\mathbf{p}}^{n+4/5}\right){\mathbf{F}}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1}\right)\,,

(68)

\displaystyle\ell^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\ell^{n+4/5}+\xi^{\mathrm{A}}_{\Delta t/2,r}\left({\mathbf{q}}^{n+1/2},{\mathbf{p}}^{n+4/5}\right)\,.

(69)

The average value of the forcing variable
\lambda
over the corresponding time step can then be estimated as follows:

\lambda^{n+1}=\frac{\ell^{n+1}}{\Delta t}\,,

(70)

which can be further averaged along the trajectory as

\mathbb{E}^{*}_{r}[\lambda]=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{iter}}}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N_{\mathrm{iter}}}\lambda^{n}\,,

(71)

where
N_{\mathrm{iter}}
is the number of iterations. Note that one clear advantage of the above strategy is that we do not have to compute
\lambda
(
13
) along the trajectory, instead the estimation appears as natural byproducts of the integration procedure.

4
Numerical experiments

In this section, we conduct various numerical experiments to compare the performance of the DPD-NEMD system with the DPD-Norton system in computing transport coefficients in DPD, focusing on the computation of the mobility and the shear viscosity.

4.1
Simulation details

Although a soft potential (e.g., in
[
39
]
) is used in the traditional formulation of DPD, alternative potentials have also been widely used in DPD. In this article, we consider the following Lennard-Jones total potential energy, which was also used in
[
33
]
:

U(\mathbf{q})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N-1}\sum\limits_{j=i+1}^{N}\varphi\left(r_{ij}\right)\,,

(72)

where
\varphi\left(r_{ij}\right)
represents the pair potential energy, obtained by truncating the range of
u
to ensure
\varphi
is
C^{1}
(i.e., continuously differentiable),

\varphi\left(r_{ij}\right)=\begin{cases}u\left(r_{ij}\right)-u\left(r_{\mathrm{c}}\right)-u^{\prime}\left(r_{\mathrm{c}}\right)\left(r_{ij}-r_{\mathrm{c}}\right)\,,&r_{ij}<r_{\mathrm{c}}\,,\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad 0\,,&r_{ij}\geq r_{\mathrm{c}}\,,\end{cases}

(73)

where
u
is given by

u(r)=4\varepsilon\left[\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r}\right)^{12}-\left(\frac{r_{0}}{r}\right)^{6}\right]\,,

(74)

where
\varepsilon
and
r_{0}
are two constants that set the energy and length scales
of the beads, respectively, in reduced Lennard-Jones units. It is worth mentioning that we have also tested the soft potential (e.g., in
[
39
]
) and the results are very similar to the Lennard-Jones potential, and only the results of the Lennard-Jones potential are presented.

In our numerical experiments, a system of
N=500
identical particles was simulated in a cubic box (i.e.,
d=3
) with periodic boundary conditions, unless otherwise stated. The following parameter set was used:
\varepsilon=r_{0}=m_{i}=1
(for defining reduced units), and
k_{\mathrm{B}}=T=1
. Particle density
\rho_{\rm d}=0.85
was used with cutoff radius
r_{\mathrm{c}}=2.5
. The particles are initiated on a cubic lattice with equal spacing, while the initial momenta were independent and identically distributed normal random variables with mean zero and variance
k_{\mathrm{B}}T
. We included the case of
\gamma=4.5
for a thorough investigation, and we also compared the performance with a friction coefficient of
\gamma=40.5
.
Cell lists
[
42
]
were used in each numerical simulation.

Unless otherwise stated, the system was simulated for 1000 reduced time units, with a stepsize of
\Delta t=0.01
, but only the last 80 % of the data were collected to calculate the physical quantities in order to make sure the system was well equilibrated—we have numerically verified that this was indeed the case. Ten different runs were averaged to reduce the sampling errors. All the numerical simulations were performed on the University of Birmingham supercomputer
BlueBEAR
by using the Julia package
Molly

[
43
]
.

4.2
Transport coefficients

In this subsection, we introduce two transport coefficients (i.e., the mobility and the shear viscosity) that will be computed and compared in our numerical results in Section (
4.4
).

4.2.1
Mobility

Proportional to the diffusion coefficient, mobility is an important transport coefficient in physics, and has been widely studied for the Langevin dynamics
[
15
,
44
,
45
,
46
,
30
]
. The response
R
associated with the mobility is given by

R({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})={\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot{\mathbf{p}}={\mathbf{F}}\cdot{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}\,,

(75)

where the external force
{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})={\mathbf{F}}=\left({\mathbf{F}}_{1},{\mathbf{F}}_{2},\cdots,{\mathbf{F}}_{N}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}
is a constant vector field and we further assume that it is normalised such that
|{\mathbf{F}}|=1
. We consider two types of drifts as follows
[
15
]
:

1.

Two drifts
: this approach corresponds to a perturbation where the force acts on one particle in one direction, and on another particle in the opposite direction, which can be assumed (by indistinguishability of the particles) to be the
x
component of the first two particles

{\mathbf{F}}_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1,0,0\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\,,\quad{\mathbf{F}}_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(-1,0,0\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\,,\quad{\mathbf{F}}_{i}=\mathbf{0}\,,\quad i=3,4,\ldots,N\,,

(76)

2.

Colour drifts
: this approach corresponds to a perturbation where the force acts on one half of the particles in one direction, and on another half of the particles in the opposite direction, which we again choose by convention to be the
x
direction

{\mathbf{F}}_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left((-1)^{i},0,0\right)^{\mathrm{T}},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,N\,,

(77)

where we assume
N
is an even number to ensure the momentum conservation.

It is worth mentioning that the controllability condition
{\mathbf{G}}({\mathbf{q}})\cdot{\mathbf{F}}({\mathbf{q}})={\mathbf{F}}\cdot{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{F}}\neq 0
is satisfied in both cases described above.

Based on the Einstein relation
[
47
]
, the mobility
\alpha
is related to the diffusion coefficient
D
as follows:

\alpha=\beta D\,.

(78)

In addition to the two popular approaches (i.e., Green–Kubo and NEMD) to measure the transport coefficients, another common method to approximate the diffusion coefficient is the so-called “mean squared displacement (MSD)”. The MSD at time
t
is defined as an ensemble average

\mathrm{MSD}(t)=\left\langle\left|{\mathbf{q}}(t)-{\mathbf{q}}(0)\right|^{2}\right\rangle=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left|{\mathbf{q}}_{i}(t)-{\mathbf{q}}_{i}(0)\right|^{2}\,.

(79)

The diffusion coefficient can then be approximated as follows:

D=\frac{1}{2d}\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathrm{MSD}(t)}{t}\,.

(80)

The MSD approach has been widely adopted in DPD
[
48
,
25
,
26
]
, and in what follows we will use the approximation obtained from the MSD approach via (
78
) as the reference value for the comparison of the mobility approximations.

4.2.2
Shear viscosity

Shear viscosity is another important transport coefficient in physics. A common approach to computing the shear viscosity in DPD is to apply Lees–Edwards boundary conditions in nonequilibrium simulations to generate a steady shear flow
[
49
,
39
]
, whereas an alternative method in equilibrium simulations is to use the Green–Kubo integration of the stress autocorrelation function
[
25
,
26
]
. Based on the linear response theory, a rigorous framework for computing the shear viscosity in two-dimensional Langevin dynamics was developed in
[
29
]
, and the corresponding numerical investigation in three dimensions, including an extension to Norton dynamics, was carried out in
[
33
]
. In this article, we present a detailed mathematical derivation demonstrating how the shear viscosity of a three-dimensional DPD system can be obtained from the average longitudinal
velocity.

Denote the lengths of the DPD system in each spatial direction as
L_{x}
,
L_{y}
, and
L_{z}
, respectively, we consider an external force acting in the longitudinal
x
-direction, with its magnitude determined by the transverse configuration in the
y
-direction. Specifically, three types of external forces are examined in this article, as defined in
[
29
,
33
]
:

1.

sinusoidal force:
F(Y)=\sin\left(\frac{2\pi Y}{L_{y}}\right)
,

2.

piecewise linear force:
F(Y)=\begin{cases}\frac{4}{L_{y}}\left(Y-\frac{L_{y}}{4}\right)\,,&0\leq Y\leq\frac{L_{y}}{2}\,,\\
\frac{4}{L_{y}}\left(\frac{3L_{y}}{4}-Y\right)\,,&\frac{L_{y}}{2}<Y\leq L_{y}\,,\\
\end{cases}

3.

piecewise constant force:
F(Y)=\begin{cases}1\,,&0\leq Y\leq\frac{L_{y}}{2}\,,\\
-1\,,&\frac{L_{y}}{2}<Y\leq L_{y}\,.\\
\end{cases}

The average longitudinal velocity
U_{x}^{\varepsilon}(Y,{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})
, the

are defined, respectively as follows:

\displaystyle U_{x}^{\varepsilon}(Y,{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})

\displaystyle=\frac{L_{y}}{Nm}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}p^{x}_{i}\chi_{\varepsilon}\left(q^{y}_{i}-Y\right)\,,

(81a)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{xy}^{\varepsilon}(Y,{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L_{x}L_{z}}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\frac{p^{x}_{i}p^{y}_{i}}{m}\chi_{\varepsilon}\left(q^{y}_{i}-Y\right)+\sum\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq N}F_{ij}^{x}\left(r_{ij}\right)\int_{q^{y}_{j}}^{q^{y}_{i}}\chi_{\varepsilon}(s-Y)\,{\rm d}s\right)\,,

(81b)

where

is a smooth approximation of the Dirac delta function on

with

(see more details in

), and the intermolecular force

F_{ij}^{x}\left(r_{ij}\right)=-U^{\prime}\left(r_{ij}\right)e_{ij}^{x}-\gamma\omega^{{\mathrm{D}}}\left(r_{ij}\right)\left(\mathbf{e}_{ij}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{ij}\right)e_{ij}^{x}\,,

(82)

In analogy with the case of Langevin dynamics proposed in

To derive a closed-form expression for the shear viscosity, we consider the average longitudinal velocity and the off-diagonal term of the stress tensor in the limits of vanishing forcing and spatial averaging in the context of DPD, and present the following proposition.

Proposition 1

Suppose that the limits

\displaystyle u_{x}(Y)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\lim\limits_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\left\langle U_{x}^{\varepsilon}(Y,{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})\right\rangle_{\eta}}{\eta}\,,

(83a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{xy}(Y)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\lim\limits_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\left\langle\Sigma_{xy}^{\varepsilon}(Y,{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})\right\rangle_{\eta}}{\eta}\,,

(83b)

exist and are smooth with respect to
Y\in\left[0,L_{y}\right]
, where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\eta}
denotes the average associated with the measure of the dynamics (
6
), then

\frac{\partial\sigma_{xy}(Y)}{\partial Y}=\rho F(Y)\,,

(84)

where
\rho=\frac{N}{L_{x}L_{y}L_{z}}
is the density and
F(Y)
is the external force.

The proof of Proposition
1
is provided in

. In this article, we consider a bulk homogeneous system and assume that the external force is sufficiently small to remain within the linear response regime, in which the fluid exhibits the Newtonian behaviour with constant shear viscosity. Hence, by the definition of shear viscosity, denoted by

, we obtain the following relation:

\sigma_{xy}(Y)=-\nu\frac{{\rm d}u_{x}(Y)}{{\rm d}Y}\,,

(85)

from which (

\nu u_{x}^{\prime\prime}(Y)=-\rho F(Y)\,.

(86)

Finally, the shear viscosity can be computed by applying a Fourier transform to (

\nu=\frac{\rho F_{1}}{U_{1}}\left(\frac{L_{y}}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\,,

(87)

where

U_{1}=\frac{1}{L_{y}}\int_{0}^{L_{y}}u_{x}(Y)e^{\frac{2i\pi Y}{L_{y}}}\,{\rm d}Y\,,\quad F_{1}=\frac{1}{L_{y}}\int_{0}^{L_{y}}F(Y)e^{\frac{2i\pi Y}{L_{y}}}\,{\rm d}Y\,.

(88)

In estimating the shear viscosity,

is analytically known, and

can be computed by the corresponding response

R({\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{p}})={\mathbf{F}}\left({\mathbf{q}}_{y}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}{\mathbf{p}}=\frac{1}{mN}\sum\limits^{N}_{j=1}p^{x}_{j}\exp\left(\frac{2i\pi q^{y}_{j}}{L_{y}}\right)\,.

(89)

4.3
Asymptotic variances

The challenge associated with NEMD methods is that the estimator (
9
) suffers from large statistical errors when
|\eta|
is small. More precisely, we can show that the asymptotic variance of the estimator (
9
) scales as
\eta^{-2}
as
\eta
approaches zero
[
51
,
33
]
. Although there exist techniques to extend the linear regime in NEMD methods
[
30
]
, in many cases the linear regimes are limited, resulting in large asymptotic variances. Furthermore, we have

\sigma^{2}_{\alpha,\eta}=\frac{2}{\eta^{2}}\mathrm{Var}_{\eta}(R)\Theta_{\eta}(R)\,,

(90)

where
\mathrm{Var}_{\eta}
denotes the variance with respect to
\mathbb{E}_{\eta}
and the correlation time
\Theta_{\eta}(R)
is given by

\Theta_{\eta}(R)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\left[\Pi_{\eta}R\left({\mathbf{q}}_{t},{\mathbf{p}}_{t}\right)\Pi_{\eta}R\left({\mathbf{q}}_{0},{\mathbf{p}}_{0}\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\left[(\Pi_{\eta}R)^{2}\right]}\,{\rm d}t\,,

(91)

where
\Pi_{\eta}
is the centering operator given by
\Pi_{\eta}\varphi=\varphi-\mathbb{E}_{\eta}[\varphi]
.

Similar to the analyses in NEMD methods, we can show that the asymptotic variance of the estimator (
16
) scales as
r^{-2}
as
r
approaches zero
[
33
]
. More precisely, we have

\sigma^{2}_{\alpha^{*},r}=\frac{2r^{2}}{(\mathbb{E}^{*}_{r}[\lambda])^{4}}\mathrm{Var}_{r}^{*}(\lambda)\Theta_{r}^{*}(\lambda)=\frac{2(\alpha^{*})^{4}}{r^{2}}\mathrm{Var}_{r}^{*}(\lambda)\Theta_{r}^{*}(\lambda)+O\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)\,,

(92)

where
\mathrm{Var}_{r}^{*}
denotes the variance with respect to
\mathbb{E}^{*}_{r}
and the correlation time
\Theta_{r}^{*}(\lambda)
is defined similarly to the NEMD case (
91
) by

\Theta^{*}_{r}(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{*}_{r}\left[\Pi^{*}_{r}\lambda\left({\mathbf{q}}_{t},{\mathbf{p}}_{t}\right)\Pi^{*}_{r}\lambda\left({\mathbf{q}}_{0},{\mathbf{p}}_{0}\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}^{*}_{r}\left[(\Pi^{*}_{r}\lambda)^{2}\right]}\,{\rm d}t\,,

(93)

where
\Pi^{*}_{r}
is the centering operator with respect to
\mathbb{E}^{*}_{r}
.

5
Conclusions

We have reviewed the mathematical formulations of the dissipative particle dynamics (DPD), and have introduced its variants with nonequilibrium molecular dynamics (NEMD) and stochastic Norton dynamics. We have discussed the numerical methods for both the DPD-NEMD and DPD-Norton dynamics. Particularly, we have proposed a numerical method for the DPD-Norton dynamics. The proposed ABOBA method is based on splitting the vector field of the system into ‘A’, ‘B’, and ‘O’ three elementary dynamics, each of which individually preserves a response observable. Therefore, overall the ABOBA method preserves the response observable by construction. We have also proposed an alternative approach for the computation of the shear viscosity in DPD systems. By introducing a spatially dependent external force and constructing the velocity and stress observables in DPD, we have derived a Poisson-type equation that connects the average longitudinal velocity to the applied force, where the shear viscosity can be obtained from the Fourier coefficient of the associated averaged quantities. Furthermore, we have demonstrated in our numerical experiments that the DPD-Norton dynamics can indeed act as an alternative approach for the computation of transport coefficients, including the mobility and the shear viscosity, as the DPD-NEMD dynamics, generalising the results in a recent study in Langevin dynamics
[
33
]
.

To be more precise, in the computation of the mobility, the responses profiles for both the DPD-NEMD and DPD-Norton dynamics coincide in both linear and nonlinear (even for extreme values of the forcing magnitude) regimes in the colour drifts case, while the estimated mobility obtained from the DPD-Norton dynamics is closer to the reference value (obtained from the MSD method) than that from the DPD-NEMD dynamics in the two drifts case. Moreover, we have observed that the colour drifts approach is able to maintain a linear response regime for a wider range of the values of the forcing magnitude than the two drifts approach for both the DPD-NEMD and DPD-Norton dynamics. We have also numerically verified that the corresponding asymptotic variance, which is a key metric to measure the associated computational costs, indeed scales as
\eta^{-2}
for the DPD-NEMD dynamics and
r^{-2}
for the DPD-Norton dynamics, at least in the small forcing regime when the friction coefficient is relatively small (larger forcing magnitudes may be needed in the higher friction coefficient limits). The asymptotic variances from the DPD-NEMD dynamics are almost indistinguishable between the two drifts and colour drifts approaches, while those from the DPD-Norton dynamics are generally smaller, especially for larger values of
\gamma
. Notably, with
\gamma=40.5
, the asymptotic variance from DPD-Norton is significantly reduced compared to that from DPD-NEMD, and the regime with the expected order of
-2
scaling extends to larger forcing magnitudes.

Meanwhile, we have carried out a thorough numerical investigation for the computation of the shear viscosity by using both the DPD-NEMD and DPD-Norton dynamics. The methodology is based on the relation between the spatially dependent external force and the Fourier response of the average longitudinal velocity as presented in (
87
). In our numerical experiments, the sinusoidal, piecewise linear, and piecewise constant forces have been considered, where both dynamics generate nearly identical results in a wide range of the values of the forcing magnitude, including both linear and nonlinear regimes. We have further verified that the estimator
F_{1}/U_{1}
converges as a function of the system size
N
with a rate asymptotically consistent with the theoretical expression in (
87
) of order
N^{-2/3}
. In addition, the histograms of the relevant observables (
R
and
\lambda
) in equilibrium become increasingly concentrated around zero as
N
increases, with variances scaling as
N^{-1}
for the DPD-NEMD dynamics and
N^{-5/3}
for the DPD-Norton dynamics, reflecting stronger concentration in the Norton formulation. Moreover, the computed shear viscosity increases monotonically with the friction coefficient
\gamma
, consistent with previous theoretical studies showing the dominance of the dynamic contribution in the high friction limit. Lastly, we have compared the asymptotic variances of
U_{1}
with different types of forces and different values of
\gamma
, and have observed that both the DPD-NEMD and DPD-Norton dynamics are relatively insensitive to the types of forces in the linear regime. Moreover, as
\gamma
increases, the asymptotic variances obtained from the DPD-Norton dynamics appear to be smaller than those from the DPD-NEMD dynamics.

Furthermore, it will be of interest to extend the present analysis to further investigate how the DPD-Norton dynamics performs with more complicated potential energies, for instance, in polymer melts
[
13
]
.
