Association between triglyceride-glucose index and early vascular aging in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus: a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Qi Fu, Yujiao Yang, Fenfen Liu, Long Wang, Ruoling Teng, Jiao Wang, Ping Liu, Yanan Chen, Yi Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly vascular aging (EVA) is a key contributor to the elevated risk of cardiovascular disease, which remains a leading cause of mortality and disability in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). This study aims to investigate the association between the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and EVA in T2DM patients.MethodsT2DM patients were enrolled, and their clinical data were collected. Bilateral brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV) was measured and used to determine the presence of EVA. Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the effect of the TyG index and identify factors associated with EVA. A predictive model was constructed using a nomogram, and its performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsA total of 380 patients were included in the study, among whom 144 (37.89%) were identified as having EVA. After adjusting for all relevant covariates, the TyG index remained independently and positively associated with EVA (odds ratio [OR] = 2.76, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.57–4.85), patients in the highest TyG tertile had a 4.30-fold increased risk of developing EVA compared to those in the lowest tertile (95% CI: 1.97–9.42). Elevated TyG index (OR = 2.53, 95% CI: 1.79–3.57) and systolic blood pressure (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.06) were positively associated with EVA in T2DM patients, while male sex (OR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.30–0.83) and absence of T2DM complications (OR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.34–0.92) were negatively associated with EVA. Based on these findings, a predictive model was developed, which demonstrated good discrimination with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.776 (95% CI: 0.728–0.824).ConclusionsA higher TyG index and elevated systolic blood pressure were independently associated with the presence of EVA, whereas male sex and the absence of T2DM complications were associated with a lower likelihood of its occurrence. These findings provide valuable insights for the early identification of high-risk individuals.Keywords: Early vascular aging, Triglyceride-glucose index, Type 2 diabetes mellitus

Introduction

Vascular aging (VA) is the result of endothelial and smooth muscle cell senescence. Morphologically, VA is characterized by intimal and medial thickening of the vascular wall, increased collagen fiber deposition, elastic fiber fragmentation, and disorganized smooth muscle cell arrangement. Functionally, VA is characterized by increased vascular stiffness, enhanced vasoconstriction, and impaired vasodilation and regenerative capacity [1]. Blood vessels are essential components of multiple organ systems. VA represents a critical pathophysiological basis for the aging of various organ systems and plays a significant role in the onset and progression of numerous chronic diseases. VA also contributes to the increased risk of acute and chronic cardiovascular diseases. This is due to characteristic morphological and molecular changes in aging blood vessels, such as vascular wall thickening, reduced nitric oxide production, and increased oxidative stress. These alterations lead to vascular imbalance and dysfunction over time [2].

VA generally refers to the physiological aging process associated with increasing age. However, when blood vessels are exposed to certain stressors, such as abnormally elevated blood glucose levels, VA occurs prematurely, resulting in early vascular aging (EVA) [3]. A meta-analysis of 102 prospective studies revealed a strong association between elevated fasting blood glucose levels and an increased risk of vascular aging-related diseases, such as ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke and coronary artery disease. In non-diabetic populations, fasting blood glucose levels were generally positively correlated with the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. In diabetic individuals, the risk of developing these diseases was approximately twice that of healthy individuals [4]. In China, the prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has been increasing annually. By 2020, the prevalence of diabetes among adults aged 18 and above reached 12.8%, with a total diabetic population of 130 million. Cardiovascular disease is one of the major complications of T2DM, affecting approximately one-third of T2DM patients, and is a leading cause of mortality and disability [5]. EVA exacerbates the occurrence and progression of cardiovascular diseases. Therefore, early detection and intervention of EVA in T2DM patients are crucial for preventing or delaying the onset of cardiovascular diseases.

Insulin resistance (IR) precedes the onset of abnormal plasma glucose levels and is present in both the prediabetic and diabetic stages [6]. IR is considered a central pathophysiological mechanism in the development of T2DM. It is defined as a pathological condition characterized by reduced insulin sensitivity in metabolically active tissues, primarily adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, and the liver. In addition to diabetes, IR is closely associated with obesity, dyslipidemia (elevated triglycerides and decreased high-density lipoprotein cholesterol), hypertension, and other symptoms of metabolic syndrome. These metabolic components have been proven to be independent risk factors for cardiovascular diseases [7]. The triglyceride glucose index (TyG index) is a simple, reliable, and validated surrogate marker for IR, and reflects an individual’s glucose and lipid metabolic status [8]. It has been shown to be superior to the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) and quantitative insulin sensitivity check index (QUICKI) in evaluating IR among T2DM patients. Unlike HOMA-IR and QUICKI, the TyG index does not rely on insulin measurements and is therefore not affected by pancreatic β-cell function [9, 10]. Furthermore, the TyG index has been confirmed to be associated with the occurrence and poor prognosis of various cardiovascular diseases, such as acute coronary syndrome and heart failure [11–13]. Therefore, the TyG index may be associated with the presence of EVA in T2DM patients and could potentially be used to assess the risk of EVA in these patients.

This study is the first to focus on T2DM patients to investigate the association between the TyG index and EVA. It also aims to develop a risk prediction model for EVA in T2DM patients to assess their risk of developing EVA. By identifying high-risk individuals, timely and effective interventions can be implemented to reduce the incidence of cardiovascular complications and improve long-term outcomes in T2DM patients.

Methods

Study population

A total of 424 patients who were hospitalized and diagnosed with T2DM at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (Changzhou, Jiangsu, China) between September 2022 and September 2024 were enrolled as the study population. Some patients were identified with abnormal blood glucose levels during routine health examinations, while others were hospitalized due to poor glycemic control, or evaluation of comorbid conditions. The diagnostic criteria for T2DM include the presence of typical diabetic symptoms, along with one of the following: random blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/l, fasting blood glucose (after at least 8 h of fasting) ≥ 7.0 mmol/l, 2-hour blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/l in an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5% [14].

Exclusion criteria: (1) Incomplete data on blood glucose, blood lipids, or brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV); (2) Ankle-brachial index (ABI) ≤ 0.9, indicating arterial stenosis or obstruction, or potential coexisting peripheral vascular disease that could affect baPWV measurement accuracy; (3) Acute or chronic liver diseases; (4) Severe renal insufficiency, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 ml/(min·1.73 m²); (5) Acute infection within the past week; (6) Surgical history within the past month; (7) Coexisting malignancy; (8) Coexisting autoimmune diseases and currently receiving corticosteroid therapy.

Grouping criteria: According to expert consensus, EVA is defined as a PWV value exceeding the mean of the general healthy population by more than two standard deviations after age adjustment [15]. In this study, the baPWV was used as the assessment parameter. The measurement of baPWV and the determination of EVA (based on a database of 7,881 healthy participants) were both conducted using a non-invasive arteriosclerosis detection device (BP-203 RPE III; Omron; Japan) [16]. A total of 380 patients were included for this study based on strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, and categorized into the EVA group (n = 144) and the non-EVA group (n = 236) (Fig. 1).

Participant enrollment flow diagram

A post hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power (version 3.1). Given the sample sizes of the EVA group (n = 144) and non-EVA group (n = 236), with a mean difference in TyG index of 0.57 (SDs: 0.79 and 0.70, respectively), the calculated effect size (Cohen’s d) was 0.76. At a significance level of 0.05, the achieved statistical power was 0.95, indicating that the sample size was more than adequate to detect a meaningful difference between groups.

Data collection

General information. Age, sex, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), pulse pressure (PP), smoking and excessive alcohol consumption history, presence of T2DM complications, whether the patient had newly diagnosed and untreated T2DM, presence of hypertension or hyperlipidemia, and history of antidiabetic medication use were recorded. T2DM complications were categorized as acute complications (such as diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic syndrome) and chronic complications (including diabetic cardiomyopathy, nephropathy, retinopathy, and peripheral neuropathy). The identification of T2DM complications was based on objective evidence derived from both instrumental examinations and laboratory tests. In this study, the term “presence of T2DM complications” referred to a history of acute complications or the current presence of chronic complications.

Vascular indicators. baPWV, ABI, presence of arterial stiffness, and aortic root diameter. Both baPWV and ABI were measured using a noninvasive arterial stiffness detector. baPWV and ABI were automatically calculated by the noninvasive arterial stiffness measuring device (BP-203 RPE III; Omron; Japan). baPWV=(La − Lb)/ΔTba [17]. La is the estimated distance from the suprasternal notch to the ankle (cm), Lb is the estimated distance from the suprasternal notch to the brachium (cm), ΔTba is the time delay between the pulse waveforms of the brachial and ankle arteries (seconds). ABI = ankle SBP/higher brachial SBP [18]. When ABI ≤ 0.90 indicates severe lower limb arterial stenosis. Arterial stiffness was defined as a baPWV ≥ 1400 cm/s.

Laboratory indicators. Blood routine: White blood cell (WBC), red blood cell (RBC), platelet (PLT), hemoglobin (HB). Liver function: Aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLB), direct bilirubin (DBIL), indirect bilirubin (IBIL). Kidney function: Serum creatinine (SCr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), uric acid (UA), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, calculated by CKD-EPI equation), 24-hour urine protein (24 h UP), urinary microalbumin (UMA), urinary creatinine (UCr), urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR). Heart function: B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), troponin I (TnI). Lipid profiles: Total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDLC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDLC). Blood glucose-related indicators: Fasting and postprandial (1-hour, 2-hour, and 3-hour) blood glucose and C-peptide levels, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Blood glucose levels were measured using the OGTT, and C-peptide levels were assessed using the C-peptide release test. Blood samples were collected on the morning of the day following hospitalization. Patients fasted for approximately 12 h prior to blood collection in a fasting state, and samples were uniformly sent to the clinical laboratory of our hospital for analysis.

The TyG index was calculated as: ln [fasting triglycerides (mg/dL)×fasting glucose (mg/dL)/2] = ln [fasting triglycerides (mmol/L)×fasting glucose (mmol/L)×88.545 × 18/2].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 25.0 and R software (version R 4.2.2). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to classify all continuous variables into normal distribution (P ≥ 0.05) and non-normal distribution (P < 0.05). For baseline table construction, continuous variables following a normal distribution were compared between two groups using the independent sample t-test and among three groups using one-way ANOVA, and variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (±sd). Non-normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed using non-parametric tests and presented as median (first quartile, third quartile) [M (P25, P75)]. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test and expressed as frequency (n, %). Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate the correlation between two variables, with the correlation coefficient (r) reported. The TyG index was transformed into a categorical variable by dividing it into tertiles, and the distribution of vascular indicators across different TyG index categories was compared. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the effect of the TyG index on the risk of EVA under different models, with results presented as odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Forward stepwise regression analysis was employed to identify independent risk and protective factors for EVA, leading to the development of a predictive model for EVA risk in T2DM patients. The predictive performance of the model was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, with the area under the curve (AUC) as the assessment metric. A nomogram was constructed to visualize the predictive model. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics and consent to participate

This study adhered to the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee, and all data were anonymized and handled with strict confidentiality.

Results

Comparison of baseline characteristics between the EVA and Non-EVA groups

In total, 380 patients were included (136 women and 244 men; mean age 54.69 ± 12.45 years). Among T2DM patient, females were more likely to exhibit EVA compared to males (P = 0.006). Compared to the non-EVA group, patients in the EVA group had significantly higher levels of blood pressure and pulse pressure, a greater prevalence of T2DM complications, and a higher proportion of individuals with hypertension and hyperlipidemia. Additionally, the EVA group showed elevated baPWV, and a higher incidence of arterial stiffness. Laboratory findings revealed increased levels of platelet count, globulin, uric acid, urinary microalbumin, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, total cholesterol, triglycerides, fasting blood glucose, 1-hour postprandial blood glucose, fasting C-peptide, and the TyG index in the EVA group, in contrast, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels were significantly lower (P < 0.05, Table 1).

Relationship between the TyG index and vascular indicators

To assess the correlation between the TyG index and vascular indicators, including baPWV, ABI, aortic root diameter, Spearman correlation analysis was performed. The results revealed that the TyG index was positively correlated with right and left baPWV (r = 0.219, P < 0.001; r = 0.207, P < 0.001. Table 2). In contrast, a negative correlation was observed with left ABI (r = −0.140, P = 0.006, Table 2). To further examine the distribution of vascular indicators across different levels of the TyG index, patients were stratified into tertiles based on the TyG index values: T1 (7.025–8.972, n = 127), T2 (8.973–9.603, n = 127), and T3 (9.604–12.160, n = 126). The right baPWV values increased progressively from 1451.0 cm/s in T1 to 1593.5 cm/s in T3, while the left baPWV values rose from 1464.0 cm/s to 1608.5 cm/s across the same groups. The prevalence of arterial stiffness increased from 66.14% in T1 to 80.16% in T3, and the proportion of EVA rose from 18.90% to 61.11% (P < 0.05, Table 3). Trend analysis indicated that with increasing TyG index, both bilateral baPWV values and the prevalence of arterial stiffness and EVA showed a gradual upward trend (P for trend < 0.05, Table 3).

Evaluation of the impact of the TyG index on the risk of EVA

Based on the baseline characteristics, variables with a P-value < 0.05 were selected for covariate adjustment. In addition, several established confounding factors, including age, body mass index, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, glycated hemoglobin, smoking history, excessive alcohol consumption, and the use of antidiabetic medications, were incorporated into the multivariate analysis [19]. The results demonstrated that when the TyG index was treated as a continuous variable, the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for EVA in the unadjusted model, Model I, and Model II were 2.79 (2.03–3.84), 2.49 (1.68–3.69), and 2.76 (1.57–4.85), respectively (all P < 0.001, Table 4). When treated as a categorical variable, using the lowest tertile (T1) as the reference, the risk of EVA in the highest tertile (T3) was 6.74-fold higher (95% CI: 3.81–11.93) in the unadjusted model, 5.04-fold higher (95% CI: 2.54–9.99) in Model I, and 4.30-fold higher (95% CI: 1.97–9.42) in Model II after adjustment for all relevant covariates (all P < 0.001, Table 4). Trend analysis indicated a significant positive association between increasing TyG index and EVA risk among T2DM patients (P for trend < 0.001, Table 4).

Construction of a risk model for EVA in T2DM patients

The TyG index and all relevant covariates were incorporated into the model using a forward stepwise logistic regression analysis. The results showed that in T2DM patients, a higher TyG index (OR = 2.53, 95% CI: 1.79–3.57) and higher systolic blood pressure (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.06) were positively associated with EVA. Conversely, male sex (OR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.30–0.83) and the absence of T2DM complications (OR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.34–0.92) were protective associations (all P < 0.05, Table 5). ROC curves were separately plotted for systolic blood pressure, the TyG index, gender, T2DM complications, and their combination. The results showed that the combined model had a higher predictive value, with greater sensitivity and specificity compared to individual indicators. The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.776 (95% CI: 0.728–0.824), with a sensitivity of 75.7% and a specificity of 70.8%, yielding a Youden index of 0.465 (Table 6; Fig. 2). Based on the results selected through forward stepwise regression analysis, the TyG index, systolic blood pressure, gender, and T2DM complications were used to construct a nomogram for visualizing the EVA risk prediction model in T2DM patients (Fig. 3). Clinicians can estimate an individual’s risk by locating each variable value on its axis, summing the corresponding points, and identifying the total risk on the bottom scale.

Discussion

This study focused on T2DM patients who presented with concomitant EVA. The findings revealed that a higher TyG index and elevated systolic blood pressure were independently associated with a higher prevalence of EVA. In contrast, male sex and the absence of T2DM complications were associated with a lower prevalence of EVA. These results suggest that metabolic and hemodynamic parameters, as well as disease burden, may play important roles in the vascular aging process among T2DM patients.

In this study, 37.89% of T2DM patients had concomitant EVA. Previous studies have found that the overall prevalence of EVA in the Portuguese population is 12.5% [20], while in Austria, it is 37.3% [21], indicating that the prevalence of EVA is influenced by geographic and racial factors. IR and impaired glucose homeostasis were associated with vascular impairment, thereby contributing to the development and progression of EVA and its higher prevalence in T2DM patients [19]. Among T2DM patients, the prevalence of EVA was higher in females (47.06%) compared to males (32.79%). Notably, the majority of female patients (77.21%) were aged 50 years or older, suggesting that the increased risk of EVA in females may be related to estrogen deficiency following menopause. This observation is consistent with existing evidence that estrogen is an important regulator of vascular homeostasis, including promoting vasodilation, inhibiting atherosclerotic plaque formation, enhancing arterial repair processes, and facilitating the development of collateral circulation [22], and its decline after menopause may accelerate vascular aging and increase the risk of cardiovascular diseases [23]. The development of complications in T2DM patients is closely associated with glycemic variability (GV), which refers to fluctuations in blood glucose levels over short (intra-day) or long (inter-day) periods. In the presence of IR and impaired glycemic regulation, GV tends to increase, leading to heightened oxidative stress and endothelial injury [24]. These processes contribute to endothelial dysfunction and arterial stiffness, thereby providing a plausible explanation for the higher prevalence of EVA among T2DM patients with complications. In this study, the proportion of newly diagnosed T2DM patients was higher in the EVA group (47.92%) compared to the non-EVA group (38.56%), however, this difference did not reach statistical significance. Likewise, no significant difference was observed between the two groups regarding use of antidiabetic medications. These findings suggest that the development of EVA may be influenced by multiple factors, such as individual heterogeneity, medication adherence, treatment strategies, and the degree of glycemic control. HbA1c reflects the average glycemic control over the preceding 2 to 3 months. In this study, mean HbA1c levels were elevated in both the EVA group (9.98%) and the non-EVA group (9.92%), significantly exceeding the recommended target of 6.5%, indicating poor glycemic control across both cohorts. This finding suggests the presence of selection bias, which may account for the lack of a statistically significant difference in HbA1c levels between the two groups. Previous studies have demonstrated that antidiabetic medications such as glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors not only reduce blood glucose levels but also exert protective effects on the vasculature, with potential benefits in reversing EVA [25]. In future research, we plan to prospectively follow newly diagnosed T2DM patients, with comprehensive documentation of their antidiabetic treatment regimens and baseline clinical characteristics. Serial measurements of baPWV will be performed to monitor longitudinal changes, aiming to elucidate the effects of various antidiabetic treatment strategies on the progression or potential reversal of EVA.

In this study, a higher TyG index was independently associated with the presence of EVA in T2DM patients, even after adjusting for all relevant covariates (OR = 2.76, 95% CI: 1.57–4.85). Moreover, forward stepwise logistic regression further confirmed this finding (OR = 2.53, 95% CI: 1.79–3.57), highlighting the potential utility of the TyG index as a clinical marker for EVA risk stratification. The TyG index integrates both lipid and glucose metabolic parameters, offering a more comprehensive reflection of an individual’s metabolic status compared to isolated indicators. An elevated TyG index signifies underlying lipid dysregulation and impaired glucose homeostasis. Accumulating clinical evidence has demonstrated that a higher TyG index is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events and poorer clinical outcomes [26]. Che et al. conducted a large-scale prospective cohort study using data from 403,335 individuals without baseline cardiovascular disease in the UK Biobank (2006–2010). Their findings indicated that individuals in the highest quartile of the TyG index had a 19% higher risk of developing cardiovascular disease and a 25% higher risk of coronary heart disease compared to those in the lowest quartile, whereas no significant association was observed with stroke. Mediation analysis further demonstrated that the increased cardiovascular risk associated with a higher TyG index was largely attributable to intermediary metabolic disorders, including diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia [27]. Zhang et al. analyzed data from 1,072 individuals with diabetes or prediabetes and cardiovascular disease from the NHANES database (2001–2018). They reported a U-shaped relationship between the TyG index and both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease-specific mortality. The optimal TyG index thresholds were identified as 9.05 for all-cause mortality and 8.84 for cardiovascular mortality. Below these thresholds, the TyG index was inversely associated with mortality risk, whereas values above the thresholds were positively associated with adverse outcomes. These findings suggest that both excessively low and excessively high TyG index levels may be detrimental, highlighting the importance of maintaining metabolic homeostasis [28].

This study demonstrated a positive correlation between the TyG index and baPWV, with both arterial stiffness prevalence and baPWV values progressively increasing alongside higher TyG index levels. Wu et al. conducted a prospective cohort study involving 6,028 participants from the Kailuan study and reported that each 1-unit increase in the TyG index corresponded to a 39 cm/s increase in baseline baPWV. Furthermore, compared to the lowest quartile, participants in the highest quartile of the TyG index exhibited a 58% higher risk of arterial stiffness, with a significant nonlinear dose-response relationship observed between the TyG index and arterial stiffness [29]. A systematic review and meta-analysis including 26 observational studies with a total of 87,307 participants also demonstrated a nonlinear positive association between the TyG index and the risk of arterial stiffness. Specifically, for each 1-unit increase in the TyG index, the risk of arterial stiffness increased by 51%. Furthermore, compared to the lowest group, individuals in the highest TyG index group had an 83% higher risk of arterial stiffness [30]. These findings are consistent with the results of this study.

TyG index has been confirmed as a reliable biomarker for IR status [8]. IR is typically accompanied by compensatory hyperinsulinemia in the early stages. C-peptide is secreted in equimolar amounts with insulin by pancreatic β-cells and serves as a reliable marker of endogenous insulin secretion. This compensatory mechanism may explain the higher fasting C-peptide levels observed in the EVA group compared to the non-EVA group in this study [31]. Under physiological conditions, insulin binding to its receptor activates endothelial nitric oxide synthase, thereby promoting the production and release of nitric oxide. This process facilitates vasodilation and inhibits the expression of vascular cell adhesion molecule-1, ultimately exerting anti-inflammatory and anti-atherosclerotic effects [32]. IR reciprocally promotes the development of VA. Exposure to hyperglycemia and hyperinsulinemia induces senescent phenotypes in vascular endothelial cells, characterized by upregulated expression of aging-related genes (p16, p53) and increased activity of senescence-associated β-galactosidase [33, 34]. Moreover, Senescent endothelial cells secrete a senescence-associated secretory phenotype, which induces aging-like alterations in adipose tissue and triggers metabolic dysregulation, ultimately impairing insulin signaling [35]. Studies have shown that decreased AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activity may represent a key mechanism underlying both IR and VA [36, 37]. Additionally, levels of adipokines such as C1q/TNF-related protein 1 (CTRP1) and CTRP5 are significantly elevated in T2DM patients, and have been associated with IR. These adipokines may contribute to the development of atherosclerosis and VA through mechanisms involving inflammation and endothelial injury [38].

Higher systolic blood pressure was also identified as an independent predictor for EVA in T2DM patients based on forward stepwise regression analysis (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.06). Studies have found that elevated blood pressure accelerates VA, potentially through excessive activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system [39]. Excessive activation of angiotensin II receptors and mineralocorticoid receptors induces oxidative stress and vascular inflammation, resulting in endothelial dysfunction, increased arterial stiffness, and accelerated VA [39]. Arterial stiffness, in turn, elevates systolic blood pressure and widens pulse pressure. This increased blood pressure further accelerates arterial stiffness and vascular remodeling, establishing a vicious cycle that exacerbates vascular damage [40]. A quantitative meta-analysis comprising 22 cohorts with 11,781 participants revealed that elevated baseline systolic blood pressure was independently and significantly associated with the progression of arterial stiffness after adjustment for age. In contrast, the relationships between diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, and arterial stiffness remained inconclusive. Elevated systolic blood pressure may promote arterial stiffness by increasing pulse pressure and shear stress, which in turn induce structural and functional damage to the arterial wall [41]. Compared to a single systolic blood pressure measurement, cumulative systolic blood pressure more accurately reflects the chronic burden of hypertension on the cardiovascular system and is associated with a higher risk of major cardiovascular events, cardiovascular mortality, and all-cause mortality in T2DM patients [42]. These findings underscore and explain the harmful effects of elevated systolic blood pressure, highlighting the critical importance of long-term and stricter blood pressure management in T2DM patients to slow the progression of arterial stiffness and reduce adverse cardiovascular outcomes.

This study also found that urinary microalbumin levels were elevated in the EVA group compared to the non-EVA group. Urinary microalbumin is commonly used for the early detection of kidney disease and evaluation of renal impairment. Additionally, it is recognized as a marker of cardiovascular risk, as it reflects systemic endothelial dysfunction and vascular injury. Urinary microalbumin has been shown to predict cardiovascular events, cardiovascular mortality, and all-cause mortality in both T2DM patients and the general population [43]. Microalbuminuria is closely associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD). Evidence suggests that abnormal myocardial and vascular remodeling in patients with CKD contributes to the development of cardiovascular complications, including cardiomyopathy, arteriosclerosis, ischemic heart disease, congestive heart failure, and increased cardiovascular mortality [44]. Sun et al. conducted a cross-sectional study in T2DM patients and found that IR was significantly associated with a higher prevalence of microalbuminuria. Moreover, improved pancreatic β-cell function was linked to a lower incidence of microalbuminuria [45]. Uric acid levels were also found to be higher in the EVA group. Studies have shown that elevated serum uric acid is associated with increased arterial stiffness and may negatively impact insulin signaling, thereby contributing to IR [46]. Beyond being a metabolic byproduct, uric acid acts as a pro-inflammatory mediator. It can activate NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 inflammasomes, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β and interleukin-18, thereby initiating systemic inflammation. Furthermore, uric acid inhibits nitric oxide synthesis and promotes the generation of reactive oxygen species, resulting in impaired vasodilation and endothelial dysfunction [47]. These findings may partially account for the increased urinary microalbumin and uric acid levels observed in patients with EVA.

In this study, although LDL-C levels were slightly higher in the EVA group than in the non-EVA group, the difference was not statistically significant. Despite being a traditional risk factor for atherosclerosis, LDL-C does not appear to be significantly associated with arterial stiffness or EVA. Mikael et al. conducted a cohort study involving 2,679 participants and identified hyperglycemia, elevated triglyceride levels, and reduced HDL-C as independent risk factors for arterial stiffness, whereas LDL-C showed no significant association [48]. Additionally, the EVA group exhibited significantly higher 1-hour postprandial glucose levels, while 2-hour postprandial glucose levels did not differ significantly between the two groups. Although 2-hour plasma glucose remains a diagnostic criterion for T2DM, emerging evidence suggests that 1-hour post-load glucose levels may more accurately reflect early-phase insulin secretion and are more sensitive markers of early organ damage, including vascular impairment. Wang et al. reported a strong correlation between 1-hour post-load glucose and baPWV, even after adjustment for potential confounders [49]. Nakagomi et al. demonstrated that elevated 1-hour post-load glucose was an independent predictor of arterial stiffness in normotensive individuals with normal glucose tolerance [50]. Cross-sectional data from the Relationship between Insulin Sensitivity and Cardiovascular Risk (RISC) study further support the notion that 1-hour post-load glucose more sensitively reflects β-cell dysfunction and IR compared to 2-hour post-load glucose [51]. These findings may help explain the lack of significant differences in LDL-C levels and 2-hour postprandial glucose between the EVA and non-EVA groups.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center, retrospective, cross-sectional study with a relatively small sample size, and the participants were predominantly middle-aged and elderly individuals, introducing potential selection bias. Second, although patients’ medication histories were included in the analysis, the study did not compare clinical parameters before and after treatment in the same individuals, therefore, the effects of specific antidiabetic regimens on EVA could not be clearly determined. Third, information on patients’ diet, physical activity, and other lifestyle factors was not collected, making it difficult to evaluate their potential influence on EVA. Furthermore, the constructed nomogram may be useful for early risk stratification, but its clinical application requires external validation due to the limitations of the retrospective design. Lastly, due to the cross-sectional design, causality cannot be established, and the findings require further validation in prospective cohort studies. In future research, we plan to include outpatient and community populations to expand the sample size and increase the proportion of younger individuals to reduce selection bias. We also aim to comprehensively document each participant’s medical history, medication use, treatment regimens, dietary habits, and physical activity, and conduct annual follow-ups to better clarify the relationship between the TyG index and EVA.

Conclusions

In summary, this cross-sectional study found that 37.89% of T2DM patients exhibited EVA. A higher TyG index and elevated systolic blood pressure were independently associated with the presence of EVA, whereas male sex and the absence of T2DM complications were associated with a lower likelihood of its occurrence. When combined, these four factors showed good predictive value for identifying patients at increased risk of EVA. The TyG index, as a simple, accessible, and cost-effective marker, may serve as a useful tool in EVA risk assessment. This predictive model may help clinicians identify high-risk individuals and facilitate early preventive strategies in clinical practice.
