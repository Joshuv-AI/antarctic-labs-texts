Women’s perceptions of a tailored diabetes education program in underserved Saudi communities: An exploratory sequential mixed-methods study
Authors: Ahmed Alshehri, Asaad Abdulrahman Abduljawad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to explore women’s perceptions of a gender-tailored diabetes education program and examine self-reported behavioral, psychosocial, and self-management changes following participation among women in underserved Saudi communities and identify barriers influencing sustained diabetes management. An exploratory sequential mixed-methods study was conducted from January to June 2024 in community health centers in Al-Leith and Adham, Makkah region. Semi-structured interviews were carried out with 20 women aged 18 to 65 years to explore experiences and perceptions. Findings informed a structured questionnaire administered to 136 program participants. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize behavioral changes and perceptions, and thematic analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s 6-phase framework. Participants reported positive changes in lifestyle and self-care behaviors. Approximately 70% reduced sugar intake, 65% increased physical activity, 72% improved hydration practices, and 68% performed regular glucose monitoring. Most respondents agreed that the advice was easily applicable (73.7%), addressed women-specific challenges (71%), and enhanced motivation (72.4%). Qualitative themes highlighted daily life changes, helpful program aspects, challenges, and recommendations. Barriers included time constraints (22%), family duties (20%), financial limitations (19%), and limited healthcare access (8%). Participants perceived the tailored diabetes education program as helpful in supporting healthier behaviors, self-management practices, and motivation in underserved Saudi settings. Addressing social expectations, time pressures, and access barriers through digital follow-up and community support could strengthen long-term outcomes and advance Vision 2030 preventive health goals. Because findings were based on self-reported cross-sectional data, causal relationships regarding program effectiveness cannot be established.

1. Introduction

Diabetes mellitus represents one of the most pressing global public health challenges of the 21st century. The International Diabetes Federation estimates that 537 million adults are currently living with diabetes, a number projected to rise to 783 million by 2045.[1] Low- and middle-income countries bear the majority of this burden due to rapid urbanization, lifestyle transitions, and health system constraints.[2] Within these populations, women experience disproportionate risk and poorer outcomes, influenced by biological factors such as gestational diabetes and sociocultural determinants that limit access to timely diagnosis and sustained care.[3,4]

In Saudi Arabia, the prevalence of diabetes has reached alarming levels, with national estimates suggesting that 1 in 5 adults is affected.[5] This rising trend poses a major threat to the goals of Vision 2030, which seeks to build a healthier, more vibrant society through preventive and community-based healthcare reform.[6] Initiatives such as the Quality of Life Program and the Health Sector Transformation Program emphasize early detection, lifestyle modification, and the promotion of physical activity as essential strategies to reduce chronic disease burden.[7] However, these system-level initiatives may not adequately address the specific barriers women face in managing diabetes, particularly in rural and underserved regions.

Saudi women encounter multiple challenges that compound diabetes management. Cultural expectations often restrict independent mobility and opportunities for exercise,[8] while limited autonomy in food choices and financial constraints can reduce adherence to recommended dietary practices.[9] Moreover, health education initiatives have historically adopted a gender-neutral approach that overlooks women’s caregiving roles, emotional stressors, and social constraints.[10,11] Studies indicate that culturally sensitive and gender-tailored health education interventions are more effective in promoting behavioral change, enhancing self-efficacy, and improving long-term glycemic control.[12,13]

Globally, diabetes self-management education (DSME)[14] has emerged as a cornerstone of chronic disease care.[15] When adapted to local context, DSME can bridge knowledge gaps, foster peer support, and empower patients to take an active role in their health.[10] In Saudi Arabia, few programs have targeted women specifically, despite their central role in family health decision-making and household nutrition.[16] Addressing this gap is crucial to advancing equity and achieving Vision 2030’s preventive health objectives.

This study explores women’s perceptions and self-reported behavioral, emotional, and social experiences associated with participation in a culturally tailored diabetes education program in underserved Saudi communities. It examines reported changes in daily practices, perceptions of program usefulness, and barriers influencing sustained lifestyle modification. By centering women’s voices and contextual realities, the study contributes to evidence-informed strategies for gender-responsive health promotion and community-based diabetes management.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This study adopted an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design to explore perceptions of a tailored diabetes education program among women in underserved Saudi communities. The qualitative phase explored women’s lived experiences and perceptions of the program, while the subsequent quantitative phase validated and extended the qualitative findings through survey-based analysis. Integrating both strands enabled triangulation of data and enhanced interpretive validity.[17]

2.2. Study setting and participants

The study was conducted in community health centers and outreach programs located in underserved semi-urban and rural communities in Al-Leith and Adham within the Makkah region. These sites were selected because they actively deliver diabetes health education initiatives targeting women with limited access to healthcare. The cultural and social norms of the region, particularly those affecting women’s mobility and health decision-making, were considered central to the program’s design and evaluation.[18]

Participants were Saudi women aged 18 to 65 years with a confirmed diagnosis of diabetes mellitus, residing in the selected underserved communities. Recruitment occurred through local clinics, community centers, and health education programs. Eligibility required the ability to provide informed consent and participate in at least 1 full cycle of the diabetes education program. Women without a confirmed diagnosis or not residing in the target communities were excluded.

The educational program consisted of structured community-based health education sessions delivered through local community health centers and outreach initiatives in Al-Leith and Adham. The core program focused on DSME, including dietary modification, blood glucose monitoring, hydration, physical activity, medication adherence, stress awareness, and prevention of diabetes-related complications.

Sessions were delivered in Arabic by healthcare educators and nurses trained in community health education. Educational content was adapted to the local sociocultural context using practical examples relevant to women’s daily responsibilities, family roles, and dietary practices. Interactive teaching strategies included group discussions, visual educational materials, demonstrations, and question-and-answer activities.

In addition to diabetes-focused education, some supplementary sessions addressed broader women’s health topics such as mental-health awareness, breast cancer awareness, first aid preparedness, and healthy lifestyle promotion. These supplementary topics were incorporated to support holistic well-being and community engagement but were not the primary focus of the evaluation. Participants attended at least 1 complete educational cycle, with sessions typically lasting 60 to 90 minutes.

2.3. Variables and outcome measures

The primary outcomes were behavioral changes in diet, physical activity, hydration, glucose self-monitoring, and motivation following participation in the education program. Secondary outcomes included participants’ perceptions of program usefulness, cultural relevance, and psychosocial changes such as stress management and emotional awareness.

Qualitative variables explored women’s lived experiences, routine modifications, perceived challenges, and recommendations for improvement. Quantitative variables were measured through structured questionnaire items assessing behavioral practices and motivational indicators.

2.4. Sampling and recruitment

A purposive sampling approach was used for the qualitative phase to capture diverse perspectives across age, education, and family responsibilities.[19] A total of 20 women participated in semi-structured interviews until thematic saturation was achieved. For the quantitative phase, a structured questionnaire was distributed to 136 women who completed the program. The larger sample ensured representation of varied demographic backgrounds and enhanced the generalizability of findings.[20,21]

2.5. Data collection

2.5.1. Qualitative phase

Semi-structured interviews were conducted in private settings to ensure confidentiality and comfort. Each interview lasted approximately 30 to 60 minutes and was audio-recorded with consent. The interview guide explored participants’ perceptions of the program, lifestyle modifications, encountered barriers, and overall satisfaction. Transcriptions were completed verbatim and verified for accuracy before analysis.[22]

2.5.2. Quantitative phase

Following the qualitative phase, a structured self-administered questionnaire was developed based on themes identified during the interviews and supported by relevant literature on DSME and women’s health behaviors. The questionnaire was distributed in person at participating community health centers. Items assessed self-reported changes in dietary behavior, physical activity, hydration practices, glucose self-monitoring, motivation, stress awareness, and perceived usefulness of the educational sessions.

Most questionnaire items used a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree, while selected items used categorical response options to assess behavioral practices and perceived barriers. The questionnaire was reviewed by academic and community health professionals to assess clarity and cultural appropriateness. Pilot testing was conducted with a small group of participants to improve wording, comprehension, and relevance before formal data collection. Data were collected anonymously and stored securely in password-protected files accessible only to the research team.

2.5.3. Addressing bias and validity

Several measures were implemented to minimize bias throughout the study. Recruitment from multiple community health centers increased diversity in participants’ socioeconomic and demographic backgrounds, reducing selection bias. The questionnaire underwent pilot testing to improve clarity and minimize measurement error. Confidential and voluntary participation helped reduce social desirability bias in self-reported behaviors. Qualitative credibility was strengthened through independent code checking and methodological triangulation, which enhanced dependability and interpretive validity across both data strands.[23]

2.5.4. Integration of qualitative and quantitative findings

The study followed an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design in which qualitative findings informed the development of the quantitative questionnaire. Themes identified during the interview phase were used to guide questionnaire content related to behavioral practices, motivation, perceived barriers, and psychosocial experiences.

Integration of qualitative and quantitative findings occurred during interpretation and reporting. Qualitative data provided contextual understanding of participants’ experiences, while quantitative findings helped describe the frequency and distribution of self-reported behaviors and perceptions across the larger participant group. Triangulation of both data sources was used to identify areas of convergence and complementarity.

2.6. Data analysis

2.6.1. Qualitative analysis

Interview transcripts were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s 6-step framework. Coding was performed inductively to allow themes to emerge naturally from the data. NVivo (version 14; Lumivero) software facilitated systematic data management. Codes were reviewed by an independent qualitative researcher to enhance dependability and confirmability.[24]

2.6.2. Quantitative analysis

Survey data were analyzed using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Statistics (version 26; IBM Corp.). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize demographic characteristics, behavioral changes, and participants’ perceptions of program usefulness. No inferential statistical tests were conducted because the quantitative component was cross-sectional and did not include pre- and post-intervention numeric scores. Results are presented using tables and figures for clarity.[25]

2.7. Ethical considerations

The study was reviewed and approved by the College of Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee at Umm Al-Qura University (2023/015). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. All procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without repercussion. Data confidentiality was preserved through anonymization and secure electronic storage.[26]

3. Results

3.1. Participant characteristics

A total of 156 women participated in the study, including 20 qualitative interviewees and 136 survey respondents. Participants were aged 18 to 65 years, with the majority (55%) between 30 and 39 years. All women resided in underserved semi-urban and rural communities within the Makkah region (Al-Leith and Adham) and had completed at least 1 structured health education program.

Participants represented a range of occupational backgrounds, including homemakers, students, and employed women, reflecting diverse personal and social contexts. The qualitative participants attended various educational sessions focused on diabetes management, nutrition, mental health, cancer awareness, physical activity, and emergency preparedness. Thematic analysis of interviews produced 4 major themes: daily life changes post-program, most helpful program aspects, challenges in applying advice, and recommendations for program improvement. The 4 major qualitative themes are illustrated in Figure 1.

3.2.1. Daily life changes

Many participants reported positive changes in dietary habits, such as reducing sugar intake, incorporating fruits and vegetables, and becoming more conscious of food labels. Women also described enhanced hydration practices and the inclusion of light physical activity in their routines. Preventive behaviors, such as blood sugar monitoring and self-examinations, were also adopted. Participants emphasized the importance of psychological growth, including increased self-awareness, effective stress management, and prioritization of self-care.

One participant stated, “I started checking food labels and reducing sugar in my daily meals after attending the sessions.” Another participant shared, “The program made me more aware of how stress and emotions affect my diabetes management.”

3.2.3. Challenges in applying program advice

Despite positive experiences, women reported several barriers to implementing the program’s recommendations. These included difficulties reducing sugar consumption, time constraints, competing family responsibilities, and unsupportive social environments. Emotional challenges, such as stress, trauma, and low motivation, further complicated behavior change. In addition, participants noted the influence of cultural norms, limited healthy food options, and media-driven pressures, all of which hindered sustained adherence.

3.4. Integration summary

Findings from both qualitative and quantitative components suggested positive self-reported behavioral changes in dietary habits, physical activity, hydration, and glucose self-monitoring among women participating in the program. The qualitative data provided context for these reported behavioral changes by highlighting increased motivation, emotional growth, and a clearer understanding of health practices. Conversely, the survey results quantified the extent of behavioral change and reinforced the perceived usefulness of the program.

Both strands identified similar barriers, including time constraints, family responsibilities, financial limitations, and limited access to supportive environments. Integration of the 2 datasets suggests that participants perceived the educational program as supportive of behavioral awareness and self-management practices while also highlighting ongoing structural and social barriers affecting long-term adherence.

4. Discussion

This study explored women’s perceptions and self-reported experiences following participation in a tailored diabetes education program in underserved Saudi communities. Participants reported positive changes in dietary practices, hydration, physical activity, and blood glucose monitoring behaviors following participation in the educational sessions. Emotional and psychological gains, such as greater self-awareness and stress control, were also observed. However, persistent barriers related to social obligations, limited time, and restricted access to healthy resources were identified.

The self-reported behavioral changes in dietary habits and preventive practices reflect participants’ increased awareness of diabetes management and self-care. Similar findings have been reported in community-based diabetes education programs, where patient knowledge and engagement led to improved adherence to nutritional and exercise recommendations.[27] In this study, participants’ emphasis on balanced meals, reduced sugar intake, and regular physical activity indicates the translation of educational content into daily behavior. The high level of motivation and sustained effort to monitor blood glucose also supports earlier evidence linking education-based interventions with increased self-efficacy and glycemic awareness.[28,29]

The psychosocial benefits highlighted by participants are equally important. Reduced stress, better emotional control, and increased self-confidence were consistent with outcomes from gender-specific diabetes management programs.[30,31] These programs provide safe, relatable environments for women to discuss emotional struggles associated with chronic illness, helping to normalize challenges and encourage continued engagement. The strong preference expressed by participants for culturally relevant examples and peer-led discussions reinforces the value of contextual adaptation in Saudi women’s health programs.[32]

Several factors could explain the persistence of behavioral and environmental challenges despite improved awareness. The limited availability and higher cost of healthy foods, together with women’s restricted mobility and household responsibilities, may hinder the long-term adoption of lifestyle changes.[33,34] Social expectations around shared meals and traditional diets can also discourage adherence to medical advice. These findings mirror other Saudi studies that emphasize the need for family-centered education to complement individual learning.[35,36]

The findings suggest that culturally tailored health education programs may be valuable for supporting women’s diabetes self-management and behavioral awareness in underserved communities. Previous studies have suggested that educational interventions that integrate local dietary patterns, gender norms, and emotional well-being have been shown to enhance retention and sustainability of behavioral change.[37] Practical follow-up mechanisms, such as peer groups, mobile applications, and community outreach, may strengthen program continuity. Incorporating digital access could also address the time constraints faced by women balancing multiple roles.[38]

This study contributes to the growing literature describing women’s experiences with culturally tailored community-based diabetes education programs in Saudi Arabia. When combined with ongoing psychosocial and community support, such interventions may support Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 health goals related to prevention and quality of life.[39,40]

5. Conclusion

The findings suggest that participants perceived the culturally tailored diabetes education program as helpful in supporting dietary awareness, hydration practices, glucose self-monitoring, motivation, and psychosocial well-being. Qualitative and quantitative findings together highlighted the perceived value of culturally relevant education and community-based support for women living with diabetes in underserved Saudi communities. However, because the study relied on self-reported cross-sectional data without baseline or comparison measures, causal conclusions regarding program effectiveness cannot be established. Future longitudinal studies incorporating objective clinical indicators and follow-up assessments are recommended.

5.1. Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, all behavioral outcomes were based on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall errors and social desirability bias. Second, the quantitative component used a cross-sectional design, limiting the ability to establish causality or assess physiological improvements over time. Third, the study did not collect biometric indicators such as HbA1c, weight, or blood pressure, which would have provided objective evidence of clinical impact. Fourth, the sample was drawn from underserved communities in Al-Leith and Adham, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other regions of Saudi Arabia. In addition, participants were women who voluntarily engaged in health education programs and may represent a more motivated and health-conscious group, introducing potential selection bias. Future research should incorporate longitudinal follow-up with objective clinical measures to validate and extend these findings. In addition, because the study relied on cross-sectional self-reported data without baseline or comparison measures, causal relationships regarding program effectiveness cannot be established.
