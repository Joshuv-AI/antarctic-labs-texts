Determinants of Low Birth Weight in Ghana: Analysis of the 2022 Demographic and Health Survey
Authors: Michael Arthur Ofori, David Kwamena Mensah, Theophilus Asamoah, Michael Fosu Ofori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLow birth weight (LBW) remains a significant public health challenge in developing countries, contributing substantially to infant mortality and morbidity.ObjectiveTo examine sociodemographic, maternal health, and healthcare utilization factors associated with low birth weight in Ghana.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study analyzed data from 8,581 women 15–49 years from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (DHS). LBW was defined as birth weight < 2,500 grams. Logistic regression models were used to identify predictors of LBW. Crude and adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated.ResultsThe prevalence of LBW was 16.8%. The independent predictors of LBW included household poverty (aOR = 1.66), middle‐income status (aOR = 1.49), lack of malaria prevention (aOR = 2.77), absence of skilled antenatal care (aOR = 1.71), and lack of iron supplementation (aOR = 1.31). Protective factors were maternal secondary education (aOR = 0.68), advanced maternal age (aOR = 0.74), and overweight BMI (aOR = 0.56). Significant interactions were identified between young maternal age and underweight status (aOR = 2.89). Also, there was an interaction between poverty and lack of malaria prevention (aOR = 0.42).ConclusionsLow birth weight in Ghana is influenced by socioeconomic inequalities, maternal nutritional status, and healthcare access, with synergistic effects between young maternal age and undernutrition. Targeted interventions addressing poverty, maternal nutrition, and ensuring access to skilled antenatal care, iron supplementation, and malaria prevention are essential for reducing LBW prevalence.Keywords: antenatal care, Ghana, health inequalities, low birth weight, malaria prevention, maternal nutrition

Introduction

Birth weight is an important indicator of neonatal health. It serves as a crucial predictor of infant survival, childhood development, and long‐term health outcomes. According to Minda et al., low birth weight (LBW) is one of the key factors which influence perinatal survival rates, infant morbidity rates, infant mortality rate, the risk of future developmental disabilities and morbidity [1]. World Health Organization indicates that LBW refers to weight at birth that is below 2,500 grams. It is estimated that around 30 million LBW babies are born every year, which is approximately 23.4% of all births [2]. Studies have shown that the levels of LBW in sub‐Saharan Africa are estimated at 15% [2, 3]. Tessema et al. used secondary data sources from 35 Sub‐Saharan countries' Demography and Health Survey (DHS) from 2010 to 2018 [2].

In Ghana specifically, LBW remains a persistent public health challenge. The Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS) report revealed a national LBW prevalence of 11% among infants born in the last 2 years who had their birth weight reported [4]. These figures represent a modest improvement from prior survey rounds; the 2014 GDHS reported that 10% of newborns in Ghana had LBW. Subnational estimates revealed considerably higher burdens in some regions, including 26% in Northern Ghana and 21% in the Ashanti region [5]. Importantly, these national averages mask substantial subnational variation. Spatial analyses of nationally representative Ghanaian survey data have identified LBW hotspot clusters concentrated in the northern and middle zones of the country. Among these, urban areas recording significantly lower LBW burdens compared to rural areas [6]. Furthermore, regional administrative estimates from DHS data confirm wide variation. Specifically, the Upper East region recorded LBW prevalence of 14.5%, compared to 9.9% in Greater Accra. This underscores that structural, geographic, and socioeconomic inequalities drive differential birth outcomes across the country [7]. Approximately 46% of all LBW babies in Ghana die in the first 28 days of life [8] making the burden of LBW not only a matter of prevalence but a direct driver of neonatal mortality.

Like most other low‐ and middle‐income nations, Ghana is still struggling to overcome the problem of poor birth outcomes. Although, the last 20 years have experienced enhancement of healthcare access and maternal health services. The multifactorial nature of the factors affecting birth weight must also be understood to come up with specific interventions and policies that can help to improve maternal and child health. The determinants of birth weight are a complex set of factors that include maternal biological factors, socioeconomic status and nutritional status, health care use, and environmental factors [9].

Factors such as advanced maternal age, multiple pregnancies, multiparity, obstetric complications, among others have been found to play a major role in birth weight [10]. Recent study from Ghana revealed that low birthweight is associated with maternal anemia during the first and third trimester of gestation [11]. This makes maternal nutritional status during pregnancy very important. Socioeconomic determinants establish the overall environment in which pregnancy will take place and have a considerable impact on the outcome of birth. Some factors linked to demographics risk include young maternal age, and low educational level, and poor maternal nutritional status (both before and during pregnancy). The factors are well‐known risk factors of birth outcomes [2]. Among uneducated women, the probability of having low birth weight is higher than in those who are either secondary school graduates or highly educated [12], These results are impressive enough to prove that the strength of educational attainment influences health outcomes.

The modifiable determinants which are directly related to healthcare include the timing and frequency of antenatal care visits. Also, the skilled birth attendance, and the accessibility of facility‐based delivery, are very critical determinants of birth weight. WHO recommendations before 2016 suggested a minimum of four antenatal care contacts in pregnancy. Evidence showing that limited frequency increases the risk of perinatal deaths made WHO updated their baseline recommendation to eight antenatal care contacts during pregnancy [13]. The number of prenatal deaths can be reduced to about 8 per 1000 births when there are eight or more contacts for antenatal care compared to 4 visits [14]. Ghana in particular, research found that mothers that had eight or more antenatal care visits were less likely to give birth to low‐weight infants [11, 15].

Although studies on the determinants of birth weight have increased worldwide and particularly in sub‐Saharan Africa, country specific research is still lacking and could be used to make health‐specific interventions. The 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey is the latest demographic and health survey to estimate the basic demographic and health indicators. The survey gathered data on fertility, contraceptive use, antenatal and delivery care, maternal and child health, childhood mortality, nutritional status, and other relevant indicators to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [4]. This is an all‐inclusive, nationally representative dataset that can be analyzed to determine the factors related to the outcomes of birth weight in a variety of people in Ghana.

The result of this analysis carries significant policy implications on the maternal and child health policy in Ghana. Target 3.2 of SDGs asks all nations to ensure that the neonatal mortality rate is 12 or below deaths per 1000 live births by 2030 [16]. Enhancing birth weight outcome is essential to this target. To execute the targets of child mortality reduction of SDGs 2030, there is the need to produce sufficient evidence on the levels of low birth weight and other related factors to help in the formulation of timely interventions. This study examines sociodemographic, maternal health, and healthcare utilization factors associated with low birth weight in Ghana.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

The research adopted a cross‐sectional design with a secondary data from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS). The GDHS is a stratified two‐stage household survey that is representative of the whole country. On the first stage, enumeration areas (clusters) were sampled out of the 2021 Ghana Population and Housing Census frame and on the second stage, a systematic sampling of households was monitored within sampled clusters. The survey included all the details about maternal and child health, the use of health care services, and sociodemographic factors.

Study Population

The population to be studied included women who were of reproductive age (15–49). These women gave birth in the 5 years prior to the survey. Multiple births per woman were eligible. The final analysis used 8,581 weighted observations. Cases containing missing data on key variables were imputed using PROC MI in SAS with the Fully Conditional Specification (FCS) method. Five imputed datasets were generated (nimpute=5) using a random seed of 12345 for reproducibility. The imputation model included the following variables: birth weight (continuous), maternal age (continuous), BMI (continuous), low birth weight, residence, smoking, working status, age group, wealth index, marital status, BMI category, parity category, partner's education, regional category, anemia status, skilled antenatal care, iron supplementation, and malaria prevention. Continuous variables were imputed via linear regression, binary variables via logistic regression (with augmented likelihood to handle sparse cells), and polytomous categorical variables via discriminant function analysis. The PROC MI pattern analysis steps (nimpute=0) were run to assess missingness.

Variables and Measurements

Outcome Variable

Low birth weight (LBW) was the main outcome which was defined as weight less than 2,500 grams. LBW was categorized as a binary variable (1 = Yes, 0 = No).

Independent Variables

The independent variables were considered under three major categories. Sociodemographic variables included; Mother age (under 20 years, 20–34 years, and 35 years and above), residential area (urban vs. rural), mother in paid employment (yes vs. no), household wealth index (poor – poorest+poor, middle, and rich – richest +rich), marital status (never married, currently married, and formerly married), the education level of the partner (no education, primary, secondary and higher), and geographical location (Northern, Middle, and Southern/Coastal). Maternal health factors included Body Mass Index (BMI) (underweight (less than 18.5), normal (18.5–24.9), and overweight (25 and above) and maternal anemia (anemic and non‐anemic according to hemoglobin level) and parity (0, 1–2, 3–4, and 5 and above children). Finally, factors of healthcare use were made up of Skilled antenatal care (yes or no), antenatal attendance (yes or no), iron supplementation during pregnancy (yes or no), malaria prevention (whether the respondent took SP/Fansidar (sulfadoxine‐pyrimethamine) during pregnancy as intermittent preventive treatment for malaria) measures during pregnancy (yes or no).

Statistical Analysis

All the analyses were done with the help of the survey weights to take into consideration the complex sampling design and make it national. Descriptive statistics were carried out to describe the population of the study. Frequencies and weighted percentage were provided to categorical variables. The weighted chi‐square tests were employed to test the relationship between each independent variable and LBW to perform bivariate analysis. Bivariate logistic regression was used to compute crude ORs with 95% CIs. The variables with p‐values less than 0.2 and conceptually important covariates were entered into the multivariate model. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals were computed. All sociodemographic and maternal health and healthcare utilization variables were captured in the model to effectively address the independent effects of these variables. All the analyses were performed with the help of SAS statistical software (version 9.4). All results were considered statistically significant at 0.05 and tests were 2‐sided.

Ethical Considerations

Data usage was requested from the DHS group, and a written approval was given. The 2022 GDHS received ethical approval from all the appropriate Institutional Review Boards. The study used secondary de‐identified data.

In terms of socioeconomic status, 44.2% of the women belonged to poor families whereas 36.3% were rich families. Majority of women (66.6%) were currently married with 22.6% of them having been formerly married. Regarding the maternal nutritional status, half the women (50.4%) had normal BMI, almost half (45.1%) were overweight, and 4.6% were underweight. Parity distribution revealed that 42.2% had 1–2 children, 33.0% had 3–4 children and 24.8 had five or more children.

Maternal education levels were quite different as 52.2% of mothers had secondary education, 23.2% had no formal education and 9.4% had higher education. Tested geographically, 32.7% of the respondents were in the Southern region, 42.3 in the Middle region and whereas 25.0% were in the Northern part. On the health status, 39.6% of women were anemic. The usage of healthcare was mostly high with 97.6% of the respondents receiving skilled antenatal care, 91.9% took iron supplements, and 89.8% used malaria prevention measures in pregnancy.

Bivariate Analysis

The bivariate analysis outcome of the crude relationships of maternal, socioeconomic, and healthcare factors with low birth weight among children in Ghana were examined. Several factors were statistically significant, and they had a relationship with birth outcomes in the unadjusted analysis.

Socioeconomic Determinants

There were observed significant socioeconomic inequalities around birth weight in various dimensions. The mothers who reside in rural areas had odds that were 43% greater in delivering low births children than mothers living in urban areas (cOR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.25–1.64). There was a strong relationship between household wealth and birth outcomes. Mothers from poor households had 93% greater odds of low birth weight (cOR = 1.93, 95% CI: 1.61–2.30). Likewise, mothers from middle‐income households had odds of low birth weight that were 55% greater (cOR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.22–1.97) than wealthy households. Maternal education was also strongly associated with birth weight outcomes. The odds of low birthweight was 43% higher among children of mothers who have had no formal education, than mothers who have had higher education (cOR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.05–1.94). The bivariate analysis failed to identify statistical significance association for the primary (although the odds were 18% higher compared to higher education) and secondary education levels. The regional differences were observed, with Southern (cOR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.03–1.25) as well as Northern (cOR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.09–1.31) having higher odds of LBW than the reference region.

Maternal Characteristics

Protective effect was found with advanced maternal age (35 years or older) which had lower odds of LBW (cOR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.65–0.91). Meanwhile, younger mothers who are less than 20 years did not have significant association (cOR = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.80–1.71). The considerable independent correlation between maternal BMI and birth outcomes persisted. Compared to mothers with normal BMI, overweight moms were 51% less likely to give birth to LBW babies (cOR = 0.0.49, 95% CI: 0.42–0.58), whereas underweight mothers did not exhibit a significant main effect (cOR = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.83–1.43). The bivariate analysis did not find some of the maternal characteristics to be significantly associated with LBW. These include maternal smoking status (cOR = 1.18, 95% CI: 0.55–2.53), employment status (cOR = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.99–1.39), marital status (never married: cOR = 1.02, 95% CI: 0.80–1.29; formerly married: cOR = 1.13, 95% CI: 0.94–1.36), parity, and maternal anemia (cOR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.82–1.06).

Access and Utilization of Healthcare

Bivariate analysis of issues related to healthcare proved to have strongest associations with low birth weight. Absence of qualified antenatal care was found to be 2.51 times more likely to have low birth weight (cOR = 2.51, 95% CI: 1.74–3.62), the strongest crude association present. Lack of iron supplementation at the time of pregnancy predisposed the odds by 77% (cOR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.43–2.20) and lack of malaria prevention procedures predisposed the odds nearly 2 times (cOR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.59–2.32).

Multivariate Logistic Regression Analysis

The results from Table 2 showed that there are two important interaction effects that alter the risk of LBW. These were maternal age and maternal BMI interactions. The young mothers (below 20 years) and underweight condition were shown to be significantly interacting (b = 1.0622, aOR = 2.89, 95% CI: 1.05–7.98, p = 0.041). The odds ratio of young mothers giving birth to low weight babies was almost triple that of the reference group. This means that there is synergy between young age and maternal undernutrition. Other age‐BMI interactions were not significant. A significant interaction was observed between poverty status and lack of malaria prevention (β = −0.8764, aOR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.27–0.65, p < 0.001). Poor households had 66% higher odds of LBW compared to rich households (aOR = 1.66), and the absence of malaria prevention was independently associated with nearly three times the odds of LBW (aOR = 2.77). The negative interaction term indicates that the combined effect of being poor and not using malaria prevention is smaller than would be expected if the two effects simply added together. This likely reflects a floor or ceiling effect, where poor women already face such a high baseline burden of LBW risk factors that the marginal contribution of any single factor becomes relatively smaller. It does not imply that poor women are protected by the absence of malaria prevention. Rather, the overall risk among poor women without malaria prevention remains substantially elevated when the main effects are considered jointly.

Discussion

This paper examined LBW determinants based on national representative data (2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey). The prevalence of LBW was 16.8%, which is quite high compared to the world average of about 14.6%. It is also higher than the 13% that was reported by other researchers [19, 20, 21]. This implies that LBW is still a major health issue in Ghana. The observation is in line with other researchers in sub‐Saharan Africa who have reported high rates of LBW [17, 20].

Socio‐Demographic Determinants of Birth Weight

The 2022 GDHS data demonstrates that there are vast socio‐demographic differences in birth weight outcomes. These are in line with the available literature on social determinants of health. The association between maternal education and birth weight shows that there is a distinct effect of gradient. This result is consistent with recent reports on sub‐Saharan Africa that maternal education is a protective factor in several ways, including the use of better health literacy, better prenatal care use, and improved nutritional behaviors during pregnancy [22, 23]. The protective role of maternal education is executed both indirectly and directly. Well‐educated mothers tend to be more responsive to the signs of danger during pregnancy. They tend to follow the dietary guidelines and make more qualified decisions regarding the use of healthcare [24]. Additionally, education provides women with the increased freedom in the household decision‐making process. This is reflected in more favorable access to the resources needed to achieve the most positive results of pregnancy. These findings are relevant in the Ghanaian context that still encounters educational inequality between rural and urban education.

Household wealth has significant association with LBW, whereby the children of richer households were found to have very low risk of been born with low birth weight. It is a wealth‐health gradient that indicates the complex benefits of economic resources such as access to healthy foods, quality healthcare services, and living environments that prevent maternal health issues [25]. Financial resources are directly associated with the possibilities to afford the best of prenatal care, nutritional supplements, and facility delivery in Ghana, where still out‐of‐pocket spending on healthcare is high even with the presence of the National Health Insurance Scheme [26].

The urban‐rural gap in the birth weight outcome found in this study was not significant in the Ghana health care system and resource allocation [6]. Although, there are chronic issues in rural settings, such as inaccessibility of specialized care in the field of obstetrics, poor transport infrastructure, and a shortage of qualified specialists [27]. Patterns of maternal age indicated that both adolescent mothers and older maternal age groups had U‐shaped associations with birth weight outcomes where older maternal age groups and adolescent mothers had high risks of low birth weight. The issue of adolescent pregnancy has been a great national health problem in Ghana, and biological immaturity, lack of prenatal care, and socioeconomic susceptibility have contributed to negative consequences [28]. On the other hand, older maternal age implies higher risks of pregnancy complication, such as gestational hypertension and placental insufficiency with effects of limiting fetal growth [29]. These results reinforce the necessity of age‐based interventions, such as full‐fledged adolescent sexual and reproductive health programs and increased surveillance of pregnant women of high age.

Maternal Health Factors

The nutritional status of the mother was one of the most significant predictors of birth weight in this study, and both underweight and overweight mothers exhibited different patterns of risk. Low birth weight as a result of maternal undernutrition as measured by low BMI showed an association with high odds of underfeeding of the fetus in terms of nutrient status [30]. This observation is of high significance in Ghana where maternal malnutrition was very high, particularly in the north [31]. Being underweight means that the mothers are energy‐deprived and this restricts the development of the placenta and the provision of nutrients to the fetus, and this directly affects birth weight.

Interestingly, maternal overweight and obesity were also found to be associated with adverse birth weight outcomes, but the relationship is more complex. Although obesity is largely linked to macrosomia, micronutrient malabsorption in overweight mothers living in resource‐restricted environments even with sufficient caloric intake can be observed, which is the so‐called hidden hunger paradox [32]. Moreover, complications that are related to obesity, like gestational diabetes and blood pressure, may also cause intrauterine growth restriction in some instances. These results highlight the dual nature of the malnutrition problem in Ghana and the importance of extensive nutritional programs that can tackle the issue of under and over nutrition.

Maternal anemia proved to be strongly independent of low birth weight, which is in line with the substantial volume of literature describing the negative consequences of iron deficiency on pregnancy outcomes [33]. Anemia lowers capacity to carry oxygen and therefore oxygen and nutrients do not reach the developing fetus leading to restricted growth. This is a very important modifiable risk factor in Ghana, where prevalence of anemia in pregnant women has been greater than 40% in some places [34]. The results justify the increased efforts to enhance the coverage of iron supplementation and dietary diversification interventions in expectant women.

The maternal height and birth weight are interconnected with genetic and non‐genetic factors contributing to fetus development. Childhood undernutrition leads to maternal short status, which is linked to a lack of uterine capacity and the impairment of uteroplacental blood flow [18]. Maternal height is an indicator of cumulative nutritional deprivation that predicts outcomes in offspring birth weights in environment where intergenerational malnutrition is present. To stop this intergenerational cycle, it is necessary to interfere at the age of childhood and adolescence with the help of nutrition programs and prevention of early pregnancy.

During pregnancy maternal morbidities, such as infections, and chronic conditions, had a severe effect on the birth weight outcomes. Malaria and other parasitic diseases are also endemic in most of Ghana and are established risk factors of intrauterine growth restriction [35]. Likewise, hypertensive pregnancy‐related and gestational diabetes disorders, which are coming into the limelight of the epidemiological transition in Ghana, have a role in placental malfunction and placental growth anomalies [36]. These results highlight the need to have holistic antenatal care that involves screening, preventing, and controlling maternal health conditions.

Healthcare Utilization

The interval and the frequency of antenatal care visits showed to be important determinants of birth weight outcomes in the study. ANC early initiation (i.e. attendance during the first trimester) had lower odds of low birth weight, which notes the significance of early access to healthcare services. The early ANC attendance will ease the detection and treatment of maternal health issues, initiating nutritional supplements, and delivering health education to the mothers during key stages of fetal growth [37]. Most Ghanaian women start care in the second or third trimester despite WHO recommendations of early ANC initiation, which is a missed opportunity of giving early interventions.

ANC visit indicated a positive dose‐response relationship where women who made fewer than the recommended ANC visits had high risks of low birth weight. The 2016 WHO guidelines suggest four to eight ANC contacts during pregnancy to enhance maternal and perinatal outcomes, which is more than the model of four contacts that was previously used [14]. Nevertheless, ANC coverage of eight or more visits in Ghana is below the optimal levels, especially in the rural regions [38]. Is a hindrance to proper ANC usage comprises transportation expenses, opportunity expenses of forsaking economic endeavors and perceived services.

ANC services besides attendance quality are also significant in birth weights. This involves thorough analysis, proper nutritional education, iron‐folic acid, prevention of malaria, and control of maternal illnesses [39]. Research carried out in Ghana has reported quality gaps in ANC, such as inadequate counseling, screening of complications, and shortages of necessary commodities [40]. The results indicate that any interventions aimed at enhancing birth weight performance should deal with the accessibility and quality of the antenatal services.

The location of delivery and attendance by the skilled birth attendants were related to birth weight records and outcomes. Facility based deliveries provide the precise measurement of birth weight and prompt intervention towards low‐birth‐weight babies. Facility delivery rates have improved significantly in Ghana, to about 79 nationwide, although there are still notable disparities in rates in terms of regions and socioeconomic status [41]. Home deliveries, especially where unskilled people are involved, do not necessarily have proper weight measurement and do not have chances of undertaking newborn care interventions.

The effectiveness of interventions that address the outcomes of birth weight depends on the factors in the healthcare system, such as the knowledge of the providers, the availability of commodities, and the infrastructure. The Community‐based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) project in Ghana has increased access to basic maternal health services in underserved regions and fields, but resource limitations and staffing issues restrict the quality of services [42]. Making health systems stronger to provide comprehensive and quality maternal health services is critical to enhancing outcome in terms of population‐wide birth weights.

These results are significant to the maternal and child health programs in Ghana. First, effective solutions based on social determinants (such as education and poverty reduction) play a vital role in achieving long‐term successes in birth weight outcomes. Second, the quality and coverage of antenatal care (especially in rural regions) should be improved. Third, scale‐up of specific nutritional interventions towards the double burden of malnutrition is needed. Fourth, teenage health initiatives that deter early pregnancy and aid the young mothers should be reinforced. Lastly, the health system investments which enhance infrastructure, workforce capacity, and commodity security are important in bringing effective maternal health services.

Conclusion

The study found LBW prevalent in Ghana to be 16.8%. In addition, the risk factors of LBW included poverty, lack of malaria prevention, no skilled ANC, and no iron supplementation. Protective factors such as advanced maternal age, secondary education, and overweight BMI were also identified. Also, we found key interaction involving young underweight mothers and the poverty–malaria prevention pattern. The implications of the findings on policy and practice are quite numerous. To begin with, maternal nutritional status, especially underweight in mothers, is one of the risk factors of LBW. Extensive preconception and prenatal nutrition education to risk women is urgently required. Second, the enduring birth outcomes due to poverty mean that interventions in the health sector are not adequate. We need multisectoral interventions on social determinants of health. Third, quality antenatal care and malaria prevention should be made accessible to everyone, specifically the vulnerable segment with no access to these services.

To ensure that low birth weight in Ghana is reduced, an all‐inclusive, equity‐based strategy that integrates effective healthcare delivery strategies, nutrition strategies, poverty alleviation strategies, and empowering the vulnerable women is needed. It is in such concerted efforts that Ghana is likely to attain the Sustainable Development Goal targets of maternal and child health.
