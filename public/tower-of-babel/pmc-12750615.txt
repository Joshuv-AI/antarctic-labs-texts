Fever of unknown origin, muscle weakness, and an adrenal nodule—the cat we chased like a hare: a case report

Background

Fever of unknown origin (FUO) is frequently a clinical challenge in patients hospitalized worldwide. A previous prevalence study, including 21 countries, found that most common diagnoses were infections (51.6%), neoplasms (11.4%), and collagen vascular diseases (9.3%). Interestingly, about 21% of causes of FUO went undefined [1]. Although unusual, some adrenal gland diseases may also be a cause of FUO, as in adrenal insufficiency or in IL-6-producing pheochromocytomas [2].

The objective of this report is to present a case of FUO in which the principal radiological finding was an adrenal nodule exhibiting a hyperintense signal on contrast-enhanced T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging. While such description is characteristic of pheochromocytoma or malignant adrenal lesions, this report aims to explore alternative diagnostic considerations in light of the clinical context. By examining this case, we seek to underscore the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis and to illustrate that radiologic features, although suggestive, may not be always pathognomonic.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old white male Brazilian was admitted to a university hospital in southern Brazil for investigation of persistent fever and lack of response to systemic antibiotics. He reported experiencing daily episodes of evening fever around 39 °C for approximately 6 months, accompanied by profuse sweating, significant unintentional weight loss of approximately 15 kg, and progressive asthenia.

The patient had no cardiopulmonary symptoms, arthralgia, lymphadenopathy, or other gastrointestinal complaints. He had recently performed an outpatient evaluation (upper endoscopy, screening colonoscopy, and prostate evaluation), all with normal results. He had a past medical history of treated and cured pulmonary tuberculosis in 1980. He was not taking any chronic medications and had no history of smoking, drinking, or using other drugs. He denied recently traveling outside of his region, and there were no endemic diseases associated with his symptomatology where he lives.

At the physical exam, he was in a fair general condition, was febrile, and had notable cachexia. No palpable lymphadenopathy was detected. A neurological examination revealed muscle strength grade 4 in lower limbs. No focal neurological deficits or sensory impairment were observed, and the deep reflexes were preserved. Cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were normal, and abdominal examination was within normal limits. No signs of joint inflammation or cutaneous or other inflammatory manifestations were found upon ectoscopy (Supplementary Fig. S1).

An aggressive workup for infectious disease was performed, and serial hemocultures, urine, and tracheal secretion samples cultures were consistently negative. Laboratory results revealed normocytic and normochromic anemia, consistent with chronic inflammation, and mild leukocytosis (Table 1). The inflammatory markers were raised (C-reactive protein 21.6 mg/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate 112 mm/hour). Inflammatory myopathies were considered to be due to progressive muscle weakness. Muscle enzymes (CK and aldolase) were within normal ranges, as were the myositis-associated autoantibodies. The full panel of investigations performed is available in Supplementary Table S1.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was requested, which showed an adrenal nodule of 0.8 cm, which was hyperintense in the T2 sequence, and which demonstrated arterial/venous wash-in and washout, concerning for pheochromocytoma or neoplastic disease (Supplementary Fig. S2). A diagnostic adrenalectomy was performed, and the histopathologic findings showed a necrotizing vasculitis with thrombosis in peri-adrenal fat vessels accompanied by lymphocytic and neutrophilic infiltrates and segmental fibrinoid necrosis of the vascular walls, without granulomas or giant cells, leading to the diagnosis of polyarteritis nodosa (PAN) (Fig. 1).

Pulse therapy with methylprednisolone was initiated (total dose of 3 g), followed by maintenance therapy with prednisone at a dosage of 1 mg/kg/day. This intervention led to a marked improvement in muscle strength and resolution of febrile episodes. On the basis of these findings, azathioprine was initiated with progressive titration up to 100 mg/day, while prednisone was gradually tapered.

Discussion and conclusion

We described a case of a male patient with FUO, muscle weakness, and an adrenal nodule on MRI with T2 hyperintensity, in which the investigation workup led to the diagnosis of PAN vasculitis with adrenal involvement. PAN is a medium-sized systemic vasculitis characterized by nonspecific symptoms such as fever, weight loss, myalgia, and arthralgia. Some frequently encountered specific manifestations including cutaneous ones (such as nodules, livedo reticularis, and purpura), and kidney involvement [3].

In our patient, the presence of adrenal involvement and muscle weakness was the cardinal manifestation of PAN. The association between adrenal involvement and PAN is well known but rare. If the adrenal arteries are involved in the inflammatory process, this may result in adrenal infarction, bleeding, or, less commonly, ischemic necrosis of the gland. In most cases, they are asymptomatic and discovered incidentally on imaging or at autopsy [4]. Myalgia, sometimes accompanied by muscle weakness, is relatively common in PAN. In a systematic review, the percentage with perivascular inflammation in muscle biopsy was greater than 90% in PAN, and the presence of fibrinoid necrosis of the vessel walls was approximately 67% on biopsy specimens. Nevertheless, true myositis seems less frequent, indicating that nonspecific myalgia or mild muscle involvement are more common than overt myositis [5].

In this case report, a definitive diagnosis of PAN was reached after an extensive diagnostic investigation, and full recovery was obtained by treatment with high-dose pulse corticosteroid therapy with immunosuppressive agents. The complexity of FUO is illustrated in this case, where a FUO causes progressive clinical decline, but ultimately results in dramatic improvement in patient care when the diagnosis is established. It emphasizes the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis and timely etiologic investigation to avoid treatment delays.
