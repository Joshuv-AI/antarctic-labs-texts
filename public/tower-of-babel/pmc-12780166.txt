Artifact-free preparation of biological samples for SEM by optimized water freeze-drying
Authors: Hideki Ishida, Naoya Fukuda, Maho Shimada, Kousei Yamamoto, Liudmyla Gaponova, Rina Higuchi, Andrii Kolosiuk, Ryo Hoshina, Mikihiko Arikawa, Yasuhiro Fukuda, MD Shafiq Islam, Terue Harumoto, Yumeng Wan, Toshiki Itoh, Yoko Inai, Asuka Takeishi, Hitoshi Aonuma, Takuto Kikuchi, Shijo Nishigori, Tadashi Maruyama, Kenichi Ikeda, Hideyuki Iriko, Toshinobu Suzaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWater freeze-drying has traditionally been considered an inappropriate drying method for biological samples for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) because ice crystals can potentially form and damage cell structures. Chemical fixation is also considered essential for the SEM observation of soft cells, such as protozoa and animal cells. We succeeded in preparing specimens of freshwater protists for SEM using simple water freeze-drying without chemical fixation. Specifically, a suspension of live cells in water was frozen by contact with a − 80 °C copper block using an outer cylinder that ensured precise planar contact. This significantly reduced artifact formation and increased the success rate from ~ 10% to 20–30%. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observations confirmed that intracellular ice crystal formation was significantly suppressed in approximately 5% of Paramecium cells, representing a notable improvement over conventional methods. This method also shortens preparation time to 2–3 h and enables high-resolution SEM imaging of various organisms. Overall, based on these findings, we standardized a simple and low-artifact method using water freeze-drying as a novel and improved technique for SEM sample preparation.Keywords: Electron microscopy, Freeze-drying, Protists, SEM, TEM, Unicellular algaeSubject terms: Biological techniques, Biophysics, Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is an essential tool for examining the external morphology of organisms with soft cell surfaces, such as unicellular protists1. However, traditional sample preparation methods involving fixation, dehydration, critical-point drying or t-butanol freeze-drying can significantly cause artifact generation and structural changes in delicate protist cells2–4. To address this limitation, we developed an artifact-minimizing method. This improved water freeze-drying approach successfully yields high-resolution, artifact-free cell morphology, previously difficult to achieve with conventional protocols, with an empirical success rate of 20–30%. Researchers have explored alternative methods that aim to preserve the native state of cells more faithfully. One promising approach is the use of cryogenic techniques, such as freezing samples in liquid nitrogen or in a cryochamber, followed by freeze-drying under vacuum1. Unlike chemical fixation, this method avoids the introduction of exogenous substances and the associated structural changes. Rapid cooling also helps minimize the formation of damaging ice crystals. However, the high vacuum and temperature conditions required for freeze-drying can still cause significant shrinkage and distortion of delicate cells5,6. To address these challenges, we developed an improved water freeze-drying method that preserves the native morphology of various protist cells3,4,7.

Our previous reports have indicated that water freeze-drying can greatly reduce the formation of artifacts that occur during fixation and dehydration in some freshwater ciliates. However, it is unknown whether this method can be applied to other organisms. Moreover, there is no theoretical explanation of the effectiveness of this method. Therefore, this paper presents the results obtained by applying this method to ciliates not used in previous reports, non-freshwater protozoa, and even biological samples other than protozoa such as Caenorhabditis elegans, and proposes a standard protocol for this method. Furthermore, based on the results of investigating the changes in temperature and pressure when freezing samples, we discuss the theoretical background of why significant ice crystal formation does not occur using this method.

Water freeze-drying of chemically fixed organisms

Organisms and tissues that live in environments other than fresh water cannot be freeze-dried in their original state. This is because the components of the external solution may precipitate out. In such cases, it is necessary to chemically fix the sample in advance. After chemical fixation, dehydration with an organic solvent is often performed, but shrinkage of the sample during this process can be problematic. To overcome this, the preferred method is to first freeze the sample to achieve cryo-immobilization. This is followed by stabilization using a chemical fixative solution and washing with pure water. This approach avoids the cell shrinkage typically associated with organic solvent dehydration, allowing subsequent water freeze-drying. Figure 4 shows examples of these: bovine rumen ciliates (A), marine protist, Labyrinthula sp. (B), human red blood cells (C), insect tissues (D and E), and cultured mouse cells (F and G). For all samples, the water freeze-drying method yielded satisfactory results.

Application of the “double-freezing method” to large organisms

For unicellular protists and small animal cells, the method described above gave good results. However, when applying the same method to large multicellular organisms, we observed a marked shrinkage of the organisms. For example, nematodes (C. elegans) formed prominent wrinkles on their body surfaces when freeze-dried in water without the use of a fixative, as shown in Fig. 5A. This shrinkage of the body surface occurred during drying and not freezing. To solve this problem, we developed a double-freeze method targeting large multicellular organisms and verified its effectiveness. As shown in Fig. 5C, it was possible to prepare samples without noticeable shrinkage. Specifically, the samples were frozen once, thawed in a 3% glutaraldehyde solution, rinsed with pure water, and frozen again. This method may increase the potential for artifacts during freezing; however, tissue shrinkage due to dehydration was minimized.

Effect of pressure on structure preservation during water freezing

In this method, samples were frozen by applying a cold copper block. Because the pressure applied to the sample could potentially affect the preservation of its morphology during freezing, the pressure applied to the sample was measured using a pressure-sensitive film placed beneath the sample. The morphology of the same sample after freeze-drying was examined with a field emission (FE)-SEM. Three different conditions were used to freeze the samples. The first was to gently place the cooling block on top of the sample (Fig. 6A), the second was to suspend the cooling block 3.5 cm above the sample (Fig. 6B), and the third was to suspend the cooling block 8 cm above the sample (Fig. 6C). For all freezing methods, the morphology and actively beating cilia of Paramecium were found to be well preserved. When the cooling block was gently placed, the pressure was below the limit of measurement (0.6 MPa), except for a small portion, as indicated by the arrow in Fig. 6A. The pressure increased with the height at which the cooling block was dropped; however, when the block was suspended 8 cm above the sample (Fig. 6C), the film coloration was sparse, indicating that the average pressure did not reach 2.5 MPa. These results indicate that the sample pressure does not increase significantly during freezing and that it is not necessary to tap the cooling block against the sample surface at a high speed to freeze the sample.

Cooling curve of pure water

To understand how the specimens froze under the conditions used in this study, the cooling profile of the pure water was examined. Temperature measurements were obtained at 25-ms intervals using a small thermocouple positioned at the center of the water sample through a 0.5-mm-thick silicone spacer (Fig. 7). In this experiment, a cooling block was gently placed on top of the sample. At the initial moment (time = 0), the sample stage, which had been set to room temperature (25.0 °C), made contact with a copper block that had been cooled to the temperature of dry ice (–78.5 °C). After an interval of approximately 300 ms, the temperature surpassed 0 °C and decreased at a rate that remained nearly constant at 70 °C/s until an evident inflection point in the graph was attained at 675 ms. At this point, the cooling rate increased abruptly to approximately 380 °C/s.

TEM after water freezing

Live P. multimicronucleatum cells frozen using the method described in this paper were examined using TEM after freeze substitution. The cytoplasm of most cells exhibited spongy artifacts; however, a small percentage (approximately 5%) of cells were observed to be in excellent condition, even after freezing. As shown in Fig. 8, such cells showed no significant ice crystal formation at least approximately 10 μm inward from the cell surface. Moreover, the tubular cristae inside the mitochondria and the finely furrowed surface structures of the trichocysts were also well-preserved (Fig. 8B and C).

Discussion

We have previously reported satisfactory results for the water freeze-drying method using various ciliates3,4,7. The present study also showed good results in other ciliates, microalgae, and protozoa, as well as in small animals and cultured cells. In particular, we have shown that samples of unfixed freshwater organisms can be prepared and that the water freeze-drying method can be used even when the external environment is not freshwater, especially if the samples were chemically fixed in advance.

The conditions for reducing ice crystal formation upon water freezing include either rapid freezing or extremely high pressures10. Because the environmental conditions used in this study were open-type, it is unlikely that high pressure (> 200 MPa) was reached to suppress ice crystal formation. Measurements using a pressure-sensitive film supported this hypothesis (Fig. 6). Slamming the cooling block into the sample suspension at high speed seems to produce artifacts, such as unnecessary physical shock to the cells before freezing, which causes the partial desorption of cilia in Paramecium. These results indicate that gently bringing the cooling block into contact with the sample was the best freezing method. The minimal variation in macroscopically detected pressure, even when comparing a ‘gently placed’ block to one ‘dropped from 8 cm’ (Fig. 6), confirms that the procedure is robust against slight operator variability in applying the cooling block.

When water is supercooled at atmospheric pressure, its solidification is initiated via partial freezing, resulting in a rapid rise in temperature to 0 °C. This phenomenon is attributed to the release of latent heat. A temperature of 0 °C represents the thermal equilibrium temperature at which ice and water coexist at atmospheric pressure. Subsequent solidification occurs while maintaining this temperature, and once all water turns into ice, the temperature begins to decrease. In contrast, the temperature change observed in this study indicates that the solidification process occurred without any temperature jump or subsequent temperature plateau.

Measurements of the cooling profile of water showed that, in the center of the sample chamber, water showed a temperature drop of − 70 °C/s in the first phase, passing 0 °C in approximately 300 ms. Although this cooling rate cannot be called “rapid cooling,” as it is commonly referred to, the cooling rate at the moment when the sample cells are frozen may actually be much faster due to the existence of supercooling phenomena, as suggested by the presence of an inflection point in the cooling curve at around − 20 °C (Fig. 7). If the sample water freezes without exhibiting supercooling, the inflection point would exist at the point where the temperature of the sample water reaches 0 °C. However, actual measurements show that temperatures are decreasing at the same rate as they pass through 0 °C. This indicates that the sample water may have supercooled and become rapidly frozen when it reached − 20 °C.

The most notable feature of our results is the absence of a temperature jump and subsequent temperature plateaus, which are generally observed when supercooled water freezes. The differences in behaviour may be due to the confinement of water in a small space during the experiment. As shown in Fig. 7, in such a confined space, rapid solidification may have resulted in an instantaneous high pressure owing to volume expansion. Under high pressure, expansion generates significant mechanical force, and a portion of the latent heat is utilized in this process. Furthermore, the freezing point on the water-ice phase boundary under high pressure is much lower than 0°C11, the freezing point at atmospheric pressure. This freezing point lowering is also considered one of the factors preventing temperature jumps. To decrease the freezing point of water to − 20 °C, approximately 200 MPa of pressure is needed11. The ice formed under these pressure and temperature conditions was considered to be in the Ice IV phase, where ice crystal formation was highly suppressed. We acknowledge that due to technical limitations (specifically the size and placement constraints of the current sensors), simultaneous measurement of temperature and pressure at the exact micro-location was not feasible in this experimental setup. This mechanism, which is similar to that of a high-pressure freezer12, may have led to the good drying results observed in this study.

The internal structure of the cells was examined using TEM, and a small percentage of cells with good freezing conditions were observed. In such cells, the preservation of the surface layer structure was good and almost equivalent to that prepared using the metal contact method of rapid cooling to the liquid nitrogen temperature, as shown by Song and Suzaki13. This indicates that the formation of ice crystals during freezing was effectively suppressed, at least locally. The percentage of cells with well-preserved internal structure was approximately 5%, which was very low compared to the percentage of cells with good observations using SEM, which was about 20–30%. This may be attributed to the fact that ice crystal formation is more likely to be promoted when the intracellular cytoplasm is frozen compared to when pure water freezes outside the cell. Because of its low impurity content, pure water can easily maintain a supercooled state and remain liquid at low temperatures14. However, since good results were obtained in the present study, this freezing method may be useful for observing intracellular structures using state-of-the-art rapid-freezing equipment.

The water freeze-drying method introduced here was first used by Ishida et al.7for the ciliate Spirostomum ambiguum and was introduced as the only effective method to instantaneously stop cell movement. Subsequently, Ishida et al.3,4 reported satisfactory results for several other ciliates. The current study further showed that this method can be applied to cells and tissues of protists and animals other than ciliates.

The greatest advantage of the water freeze-drying method is that it minimizes the production of artifacts caused by chemical fixation and dehydration. Unfixed SEM observations are possible for organisms living in freshwater environments. Dehydration with organic solvents, such as ethanol or acetone, is considered essential for sample preparation in conventional SEM and TEM; however, dehydration has been shown to cause significant cell shrinkage in both SEM3and TEM13. The water freeze-drying method virtually eliminates such artifacts from the sample, enabling the observation of an accurate three-dimensional shape. In the double-freezing method, applying the fixative (glutaraldehyde) to the cryo-immobilized structure after rapid freezing, rather than to a living organism, avoids the structural reorganization typically caused by conventional chemical fixation. This feature shares a mechanistic advantage with high-end cryogenic techniques such as High-Pressure Freezing followed by Freeze-Substitution, thus further minimizing potential fixation artifacts. In addition, this method does not require the use of toxic chemicals as fixatives (especially for protozoa living in freshwater environments), and the only equipment required is for vacuum evacuation. Another significant advantage of this method is that the time from sample freezing to the completion of drying was short (only 2–3 h).

However, this drying method has several limitations. The most important limitation of this method was that not all samples showed good drying results. We previously reported that the success rate of this method was approximately 10%4; however, with the modification presented in this paper, the success rate increased to 20–30%. Overcoming this problem is a major goal of future investigations.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated that water freeze-drying is an excellent sample preparation method for the observation of various biological samples using SEM. The application of the proposed standard experimental procedure revealed no substantial impairment in either the external morphology or the internal microstructure of the cells that can be attributable to ice crystal formation. Furthermore, an examination of the pressure and temperature changes imposed on the sample during the freezing process suggested that rapid freezing occurred due to supercooling, resulting in the suppression of ice crystal formation.

Evaluation of success rate and statistical details

The success rates of the specimens presented in this paper were calculated empirically based on the total number of cells observed (N value) and the number of independent replicates used. The criteria for judging a specimen as “successful” were defined as being artifact-free, meaning no cell deformation or damage (e.g., deciliation) was observed. The overall success rate for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observation was 20–30%, and the success rate for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observation was 5%. For example, in experiments using Paramecium (P. multimicronucleatum), the successful rate for obtaining favorable specimens was 16.8% (n = 286), and the criteria were defined as no cell deformation and no deciliation (loss of cilia).

Protists

The Sterkiella histriomuscorum F01 strain was originally collected from a pond in Fukui City, Japan (36°04’26.4"N 136°10’22.2"E). Extracted DNA was subjected to PCR using the universal primers SR1 (5’ 18 S rDNA)/SR12 (3’). The PCR products were directly sequenced (LC890396), and the BLAST results showed 100% identity to the S. histriomuscorum 17 S ribosomal RNA gene (FJ545743.1).

The EaKb1 strain of Euplotes aediculatus was collected in a pond inside Kobe University, Japan (34°43’32.8"N 135°14’07.6"E).

Blepharisma japonicum (strain R1072, mating type I) was cultured as described by Kobayashi et al.15. The unicellular green alga Micrasterias hardyi was collected in Lake Biwa, Japan (35°06’33.2"N 135°55’14.7"E).

Amoeba sp. was collected from the Kamo River (35°01’45.9"N 135°46’19.5"E) and cultured on brown rice grains as a nutrient source.

A centrohelid heliozoan F02 was collected from the same pond as S. histriomuscorum in Fukui, Japan. The 18 S rDNA sequence (LC890397) indicated that this species is related to the genera Raphidiophrys and Choanocystis.

Mayorella viridis strain NS-1 was collected from a freshwater lake in Tochigi, Japan (37°06’42.2” N 139°56’07.9” E) and cultured in mineral water.

The freshwater dinoflagellate Ceratium hirundinella (NIES-4632) was originally collected in Lake Ashino-ko, Kanagawa, Japan (35°11’51.4"N 139°01’30.3"E). It was grown in a culture medium containing 0.01% KW21 (Daiichi Seimo Co. Ltd., Kumamoto, Japan) in mineral water (Crystal Geyser, Ohtsuka Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd., Japan) containing 1% boiled natural seawater at room temperature, 18–23 °C, under 4.2 W of white LED light illumination (20–80 µmol·m− 2·s− 1) with a 14-h:10-h light-dark cycle.

Labyrinthula sp. was collected from the coast of Sumoto City, Hyogo Prefecture, Japan (34°16’26.3"N 134°57’09.6"E), and cultured in sterile seawater at 20 °C with diatoms as a food source.

Animal specimens

Wild-type N2 Caenorhabditis elegans were cultured on NGM agar plates (0.25% peptone, 50 mM NaCl, 25 mM KPO4, 5 µg/mL cholesterol, 1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 1.7% agar) with Escherichia coli OP50 as a food source. C. elegans was washed twice with M9 buffer (20 mM KH2PO4, 40 mM Na2HPO4, 8.5 mM NaCl, 20 mM NH4Cl) and resuspended in distilled water. Adult C. elegans were used for SEM imaging. C. elegans N2 strain and E. coli OP50 were obtained from the Caenorhabditis Genetics Center (CGC).

Workers of the trap-jaw ant Odontomachus kuroiwae (Matsumura 1912) were used in this study. Briefly, ant colonies were collected from Okinawa and reared as described by Aonuma et al.16. After anesthesia with CO₂ gas, the ant’s head was cut off, and the distal part was mounted on the tip of a plastic tube with a diameter of 2 mm using UV-curable resin (Bulufixx PW, BLUFIXX, Ellwangen, Germany). The antennae, mandibles, and mouthparts were removed from the head using fine forceps. The samples were then mounted on a micro slicer (THK, Kenis, Osaka, Japan) and sliced into 200-µm-thick sections. Cross-sections of the head at 600 μm and 800 μm from the rostral end were stored in 60% glycerol at 4 °C and then fixed with 3% glutaraldehyde. Human erythrocytes infected with the Plasmodium falciparum NF54 strain were fixed with a fixative containing 1% paraformaldehyde and 0.2% glutaraldehyde. RAW 264.7 cells, a murine macrophage cell line, were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; 08458-16, Nacalai Tesque, Tokyo, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (F7524, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) under a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C.

Mouse neuroblastoma (Neuro-2a) cells were obtained from Health Science Research Resources Bank and also cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin. The mouse cells were fixed with 3% glutaraldehyde for 30 min at room temperature and washed with pure water before use.

Rumen fluid collection and prefixation

Bovine rumen fluid was collected from Holstein cows, which are bred in the Kawatabi Field Science Center (Tohoku University, Miyagi, Japan), using a stomach tube. An equal volume of a 4% paraformaldehyde solution was then added to the collected ruminal fluid for prefixation. The prefixed ruminal fluid was stored at 4 °C until further processing. Ruminal fluid collection was carried out by the authors at the Kawatabi Field Science Center. This procedure for ruminal fluid collection was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tohoku University (Approval number: 2019AgA-033). This study was conducted and described in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines, and it was also conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Temperature measurement

The temperature of the deionized distilled water (pure water) was measured using homemade chromel–alumel thermocouples comprising wires of equal thickness (25 μm each) with a Keithley 2182 nanovoltmeter (Keithley, USA) at 25-ms intervals. Calibration of the measurement temperatures was performed at 0 °C (ice water), − 78.5 °C (dry ice), and − 195.8 °C (liquid nitrogen).

Pressure measurement

An LLW two-sheet-type Fuji Prescale film (Fuji Photo Film Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was placed immediately below the sample suspension to measure the pressure generated during the freezing process, which was estimated using the color samples provided by the manufacturer.

Water freeze-drying without chemical fixation

The freshwater organisms (protists and unicellular algae) were lightly rinsed with pure water and freeze-dried using the method shown in Fig. 1. The method used was essentially the same as that previously reported4, but this time, a PVC outer casing was used as a guiding structure when freezing. This made it possible to precisely adhere the top surface of the specimen to the bottom surface of the cooling block and drop the cooling block at a constant speed. The use of an outer cylinder also maintains a low sample temperature during cooling, thus dramatically improving the success rate of sample preparation.

Water freeze-drying after chemical fixation

Mouse Neuro2A neuroblastoma cells were cultured on glass coverslips. To ensure cryo-immobilization before chemical stabilization, the cells were first frozen using the cold copper block method. The frozen sample was then fixed by rapid immersion in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution (or similar chemical fixative) to stabilize the structure. The samples were subsequently washed with pure water and freeze-dried using the method described above.

Double-freezing procedure for the nematode C. elegans

Owing to their robust outer coats, centipedes and crustaceans may survive for some time after exposure to chemical fixatives. For example, the nematode C. elegans can move around in 3% glutaraldehyde for approximately 30 min. This suggests that rapid freezing is more effective than chemical fixation in rapidly stopping the movement of these organisms and preserving their morphology. Since the freeze-drying of unfixed C. elegans resulted in significant shrinkage of the organism, a “double-freezing procedure” combining freezing and chemical fixation was devised in this study.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of freeze-substituted P. multimicronucleatum

To investigate the extent of ice crystal formation in samples frozen using the method described in this paper, live P. multimicronucleatum cells were frozen in water and freeze-dried in acetone containing 1% osmium tetroxide. After resin embedding, ultrathin sections were prepared and observed under a Hitachi H-7100 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at 75 kV. Samples were prepared as described by Song et al.17.
