Application of machine learning in the discovery of antimicrobial peptides: exploring their potential for ulcerative colitis therapy
Authors: Hui Miao, Ziwei Wang, Shihu Chen, Jiaqi Wang, Hongyue Ma, Yifan Liu, Hui Yang, Ziyi Guo, Jiamei Wang, Pengfei Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUlcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory bowel disease with complex aetiology and limited treatment options. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), as endogenous immune effectors, have recently emerged as promising therapeutic agents in UC. However, systematic identification and functional evaluation of AMPs remain underexplored. We aimed to discover novel AMPs with potential therapeutic efficacy in UC by leveraging machine learning-based prediction and validating their impact in an experimental colitis model.MethodsWe established a machine learning-driven pipeline to predict candidate AMPs based on their structural and functional features. Top-ranked peptides were synthesised and subjected to in vitro antibacterial assays and proteolytic stability tests. Their therapeutic potential was evaluated using a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis mouse model, assessing clinical indicators, histopathology, inflammatory markers and gut microbiota alterations. Metagenomic and metabolomic analyses provided insights into microbial community dynamics and metabolic pathways. To probe the role of gut microbes in AMP-mediated gut homeostasis, we conducted Akkermansia (A.) muciniphila replenishment experiments.ResultsSeveral AMPs identified by machine learning exhibited potent antimicrobial activity and resistance to proteolytic degradation. In vivo, AMP administration ameliorated DSS-induced colitis symptoms, including body weight loss, Disease Activity Index and histological damage. Treatment also modulated the gut microbiome, increasing the abundance of A. muciniphila and restoring microbial balance. Functional metagenomic profiling revealed enrichment of genes involved in mucosal barrier protection and immunoregulation. These findings were supported by improved inflammatory cytokine profiles and enhanced epithelial integrity.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that machine learning-guided discovery of AMPs is a viable approach to identify promising therapeutic agents for UC. By integrating multi-omics analyses, we reveal potential microbiota-mediated mechanisms underlying AMP efficacy. These insights provide a strong foundation for advancing AMP-based strategies in UC management.Keywords: Antimicrobial Peptides; Colitis, Ulcerative; Gastrointestinal Microbiome; Gastrointestinal Tract; Intestinal Barrier Function

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic, recurrent inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) that predominantly affects the colon and rectum.1 2 Although its precise pathogenesis remains elusive, its rapidly increasing global prevalence has become a significant healthcare challenge.3,5 UC is characterised by symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhoea and haematochezia.6 Moreover, inflammation may lead to complications, including intestinal bleeding, toxic megacolon, perforation and a heightened risk of colorectal cancer. In addition, environmental factors, genetic susceptibility, dysregulated immune response and alterations in the gut microbiome are considered to be among the most important factors in the formation of UC.7 8 Despite advances in treatment, which now include multiple lines of therapy such as 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA), antibiotics, immunosuppressants and biologics, there remains no non-surgical cure for UC.9 Furthermore, many patients experience intolerance or unacceptable side effects associated with long-term use of these medications.10,13 Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify novel therapeutic strategies for UC treatment.

The gut microbiome constitutes a complex ecosystem that plays a pivotal role in the host’s digestion, nutrient absorption and immune regulation.14 Dysbiosis, or imbalances in the composition and function of the gut microbiota, can disrupt intestinal homeostasis, leading to various diseases.15 Therefore, gut microbiome imbalance may contribute to UC pathogenesis.

The immune system contributes to maintaining a balanced host-microbe relationship, primarily through the production of immunoglobulin A (IgA) and the induction of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs).16 AMPs, also known as host defence peptides,17 are small, naturally occurring amphipathic molecules, typically comprising 12–50 amino acid (AA) residues.18 These peptides exhibit net positive charges under physiological conditions, which enables them to interact with microbial membranes.18 AMPs possess broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, along with additional properties such as promoting wound healing and binding lipopolysaccharides (LPS).18

As key effectors of the innate immune system and the first line of defence against pathogenic infections, AMPs hold significant clinical potential.19,22 However, their application remains limited, particularly in the treatment of conditions like UC, where few studies have so far explored the use of AMPs as therapeutic approaches. This limited use is partly due to the challenges in reliably identifying AMPs and balancing their antimicrobial efficacy with cytotoxicity. Current methods for discovering AMPs primarily rely on extraction from living organisms, a process that is both time-consuming and unpredictable, often necessitating repeated experimental validation.23,25

In this study, we developed a machine-learning-based AMP discovery pipeline, identified LR (an AMP named based on its N- and C-terminal AA residues) as a lead candidate and comprehensively evaluated its antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, microbiota-modulating and therapeutic effects in dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis (figure 1).

Materials and methods

Dataset for training the machine learning model for screening AMPs

Construction of data sets

AMP dataset construction

To develop a comprehensive dataset of AMPs, we commenced by collecting data from publicly accessible AMP repositories, specifically the Antimicrobial Peptide Database (APD, accessed in April 2024)26 and the Database of Antimicrobial Activity and Structure of Peptides (DBAASP, accessed in April 2024).27 Following the amalgamation of these datasets, we eliminated redundant entries and excluded peptides that exhibited solely anticancer activity, as well as those exceeding 60 AAs in length. This process yielded a final set of 13 637 unique AMP sequences, with lengths ranging from 5 to 50 AAs.

Non-AMP dataset construction

Concurrently, we curated a dataset of non-AMPs from the UniProt database (accessed in April 2024).28 To ensure the integrity of the dataset, we applied the ‘Subcellular Location’ filter to select entries specific to the cytoplasm while excluding those associated with antimicrobial, antibiotic, antiviral, antifungal, effector or excretory substances.29 Following the elimination of duplicate sequences and those identical to entries in the AMPs dataset, we retained a total of 9385 non-AMP sequences ranging in length from 5 to 50 AAs.

Optimisation of model construction and AMPs screening

Descriptor description

Subsequently, we used the modlamp library’s modlamp.descriptors module to compute a variety of descriptors for the AA sequences.30 We employed all descriptor scales available within this module and computed autocorrelation descriptors for each sequence, configuring the calculate_autocorr parameter to a value of 5. These descriptors facilitated a quantitative assessment of the sequences’ physicochemical properties and structural attributes.31

Model construction

We used the RandomForestClassifier from the sklearn library to develop a random forest classifier for sequence classification tasks.32,34 During the model training phase, critical hyperparameters were configured to enhance performance. Specifically, the ‘max_depth’ parameter was set to 10 to limit the maximum depth of the trees, ‘max_features’ was configured to ‘auto’ to enable automatic feature selection and ‘n_estimators’ was fixed at 100 to specify the number of trees in the forest. All other hyperparameters were maintained at their default settings.

Model performance was assessed using metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall and the F1 score. To ensure the robustness of the evaluation, a fivefold cross-validation procedure was employed.

Hyperparameter optimisation

We systematically fine-tuned the hyperparameters of the random forest classifier to enhance model performance. A comprehensive hyperparameter search space was delineated, encompassing the maximum depth of the trees (max_depth) with values ranging from 5 to 20, feature selection strategies (max_features) including ‘auto’, ‘sqrt’ and ‘log2’ methods, as well as the number of trees in the forest (n_estimators) varying from 10 to 100. Furthermore, we evaluated the minimum number of samples necessary to split an internal node (min_samples_split) within the range of 2–10, as well as the allocation of class weights (class_weight) for imbalanced datasets, opting for balanced weights and balanced subsampling strategies.

Employing scikit-optimize (skopt), we systematically explored hyperparameter combinations within this predefined search space to identify the optimal configuration that maximises model accuracy, precision, recall and F1 score. This process was accompanied by a fivefold cross-validation to ensure robustness and generalisability of the results.35 36

AMPs screening

We used a genetic algorithm in conjunction with a trained prediction model to screen potential AMPs.37 The initial population comprised 6000 sequences, with an equal distribution of known AMPs and non-AMPs to ensure diversity and variability.

During each iteration of the genetic algorithm, the prediction model was employed to preliminarily evaluate the sequences, assessing their antimicrobial and toxicity properties. Subsequently, the charge and hydrophobicity of the sequences were computed using the modlamp.descriptors module. The screening criteria targeted sequences exhibiting a charge range of 5–7 and hydrophobicity between 40% and 60%, attributes commonly linked to the efficacy of AMPs.

The selected sequences were then subjected to crossover operations, emulating natural selection and genetic recombination processes. To preserve genetic diversity and circumvent local optima, 3000 novel sequences were introduced. Following 10 rounds of iteration, 22 target sequences that met the specified criteria were selected from a pool of 6373 candidate sequences, thereby enhancing the screening efficiency and increasing the likelihood of discovering novel AMPs. Next, we used the APD to search for natural AMPs targeting intestinal pathogens, and subsequently modified them. After modification, the algorithm was applied for screening.

In vitro characterisation of AMPs

Synthesis and structural parameter characterisation of AMPs

Synthesis of AMPs

The aforementioned AMPs were synthesised by GL Biochem (Shanghai, China) using the standard solid-phase fluorenylmethyloxycarbonyl method, with amidation at the C-terminus. All synthesised AMPs underwent purification via high-performance liquid chromatography, achieving a purity exceeding 95%. The molecular weights of the AMPs were verified through mass spectrometry (MS). Subsequently, all AMP samples were stored at −80°C.

Prediction of AMPs’ structural parameters

The molecular weights of all AMPs were determined using the Novopro tool (https://novopro.cn/tools/). The net charge and hydrophobic content of the peptides were calculated using the prediction tool available in the APD (https://aps.unmc.edu/AP/). Secondary structure predictions of the AMPs were conducted using AlphaFold3 (https://alphafoldserver.com/).

Bactericidal validation of AMPs screened by algorithm in vitro

In vitro antimicrobial activity assay

The minimal bactericidal concentration (MBC) was determined via the colony-count assay.38 39 The bacterial suspensions were co-incubated with serially diluted antimicrobial peptides for 2 hours. Subsequently, the mixtures were plated onto agar plates, and bacterial colonies were enumerated after incubation to assess the antimicrobial activity. Further technical details are provided in online supplemental material text S1.

In vivo biocompatibility determination of AMPs screened by an algorithm

Cell cultivation

Murine macrophages (RAW264.7) and mouse colon cancer cells (CT-26.WT) were cultured for subsequent experiments. See further details in online supplemental material text S2.

Anti-inflammatory assays

Cell viability

RAW264.7 and CT-26.WT cells were incubated with AMPs in cell culture medium for 12 hours. Subsequently, cell viability was determined employing the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. See detailed methods in online supplemental material text S3.

Haemolytic activity assays

Phosphate Buffer Saline (PBS)-washed erythrocytes were mixed with serially diluted AMPs, and the absorbance of the supernatant at 570 nm was subsequently measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Multiskan MK3, Shanghai, China). A detailed method was provided in online supplemental material text S4.

Cell life and death staining assay

The cytotoxicity of the AMPs after 12 hours of treatment was assessed in RAW264.7 and CT-26.WT cell lines using a Calcein-AM/PI double staining kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The staining results were observed using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti2, Tokyo, Japan). See detailed methods in online supplemental material text S5.

Anti-inflammatory assays

An inflammation model was established in RAW264.7 cells using LPS (0.1 µg/mL). Cells were treated with lead peptide (LR), and interleukin (IL)-6/tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α levels in supernatants were quantified by ELISA. See detailed methods in online supplemental material text S6.

Circular dichroism spectroscopy detection of AMPs

The secondary structures of LR were analysed by circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy in PBS (pH 7.4), 50% 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol (TFE) and 30 mM sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS). A detailed method was provided in online supplemental material text S7.

Stability of AMPs in vitro

The MBC of AMPs against Escherichia (E.) coli and Staphylococcus (S.) aureus was determined following exposure to various conditions, including 60°C heat, acidic (pH 2.0) and alkaline (pH 8.4) buffers, as well as artificial gastric and intestinal fluids. See technical details in online supplemental material text S8.

In vivo characterisation of AMPs

Animal experiments

Mice

All mice used in this study were procured from Jinan Pengyue Experimental Animal Breeding (Jinan, China) and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions.

Female C57BL/6J mice, aged 6–8 weeks and weighing 18–20 g, were given 3% DSS for 7 days to induce colitis, then randomised into five groups (n=6): Control, DSS, LR (5 mg/kg), 5-ASA (5 mg/kg) and ciprofloxacin (CIP) (5 mg/kg). Treatments were administered for 7 days. At 7 days post-treatment, the mice were weighed, anaesthetised and blood was collected from the retro-orbital sinus. Additionally, the colon and spleen were harvested for further analysis.

Mice treatment-related indicators

Disease Activity Index evaluation

The Disease Activity Index (DAI) was scored daily according to online supplemental table S1.40 Daily scores were computed for each mouse by averaging body-weight loss, stool consistency and the extent of intestinal bleeding.

Histopathological analysis

Colon tissues from DSS-induced colitis models were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin and sectioned. The sections were dewaxed and subjected to H&E staining. Briefly, after staining with haematoxylin, sections were differentiated and blued, followed by counterstaining with eosin. Subsequently, the sections were dehydrated through an alcohol gradient, cleared in xylene and mounted with neutral gum. Histological images were captured using a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 microscope.

Myeloperoxidase activity assay

Myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity in homogenised colon tissues was quantified using a commercial assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance was measured at 460 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Assay of inflammatory factors in serum and colonic tissue

The concentrations of IL-6 and TNF-α in serum and colon tissue homogenates were measured using ELISA kits (Jiang-lai Bio., Shanghai, China). Absorbance was read at 450 nm on a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Multiskan MK3). Cytokine concentrations were calculated from a standard curve.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from colonic tissues using TRIzol reagent (Genstone Biotech, Beijing, China) and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA using a commercial kit (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Tolobio) and gene-specific primers (online supplemental table S2). The relative messenger RNA expression levels were normalised to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and calculated using the Double Delta Ct (ΔΔCt) method.

Western blot analysis

Colon tissue proteins were extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA) buffer containing protease inhibitors (Solarbio Life Sciences, Beijing, China). Protein concentrations were determined by a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Solarbio), and equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS - polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualised using enhanced chemiluminescence, and band intensities were quantified with GAPDH as the loading control.

Transmission electron microscope analysis

E. coli and S. aureus suspensions treated with AMP, antibiotics and PBS were subsequently washed and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde. The fixed samples were then washed with PBS, dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol and acetone, and embedded in EPON 812 epoxy resin. Polymerisation was carried out at 37°C, 45°C and 60°C for 24 hours each. Ultrathin sections were prepared and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate before observation under a transmission electron microscope (JEOL JSM-840, Tokyo, Japan).

16S rRNA gene sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from mouse faecal samples using the Faecal Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (TianGen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quality was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis, and quantification was performed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR with barcoded primers 343F/798R and Ex Taq DNA polymerase (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan). Amplicons were purified with AMPure XP beads (Agencourt) and library quality was assessed. After quantification with the Qubit dsDNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), equimolar amounts of amplicons were pooled for sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at OE Biotech Company (Shanghai, China) to generate 250 bp paired-end reads.

Metabolomic analysis

Faecal metabolites were extracted and analysed by Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography-Tandem MS (UPLC-MS/MS). Differential metabolites and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) pathways were identified. See detailed methods in online supplemental material text S9.

Akkermansia muciniphila replenishment experiment

Using the method mentioned earlier to construct the DSS-induced colitis model, the mice were divided into four groups: the healthy group (CT (blank control)), the control group (CT+Akkermansia (A.) muciniphila), the disease group (DSS) and the A. muciniphila treatment group (DSS+A. muciniphila). The healthy group and the disease group were treated with PBS by gavage, while the control group and the A. muciniphila treatment group were treated with A. muciniphila by gavage. The treatment lasted for 7 days, after which various indicators of the mice were assessed.

Data processing

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the test results were expressed in mean±SD form. One-way analysis of variance, correlation analysis and graphs were performed using GraphPad Prism V.8 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The differences were considered to be statistically significant if p<0.05.

Results

Establishment of machine learning model trained by data set

Hyperparameter optimisation significantly enhanced the performance of the Antimicrobial Peptide Predictor (Peptide_Predictor) and the Toxicity Predictor (Toxic_Predictor) (online supplemental figure S1A and B). The optimised Peptide_Predictor achieved an F1 score of 0.85, indicating high reliability in AMP identification. The Toxic_Predictor attained an F1 score of 0.75; analysis of the confusion matrix revealed a slight bias in distinguishing true positives from false negatives, likely attributable to the correlation between AMP toxicity and bactericidal efficacy. The literature review41 supports this inherent relationship, which may explain the model’s relatively lower toxicity prediction performance.

Using a genetic algorithm, we evaluated 6373 AMP sequences over 10 iterative rounds, identifying 22 candidate sequences meeting the predefined selection criteria (online supplemental table S3), thereby improving screening efficiency and the potential for novel AMP discovery.

Modified synthesis and characterization of AMPs

Five modified AMPs (LR, KC, KI, KP, WR — five antimicrobial peptides generated by an algorithm) were synthesised (online supplemental table S4). Their molecular weights closely matched theoretical values, with purities exceeding 95% (online supplemental table S5 and figure S2). The AMPs exhibited net charges of +6 to +7 and hydrophobicity ranging from 40% to 61%. Structural predictions using AlphaFold3 indicated that LR, KC, KP and WR form α-helical structures, while KI adopts a random coil conformation (online supplemental figure S2).

Prediction results showed that all five AMPs exceeded 50% accuracy. Toxicity probabilities were 46.31% for LR, 62.66% for KC, 63.05% for KI, 63.53% for KP and 37.33% for WR (online supplemental table S5).

In vitro antibacterial activity and biocompatibility of AMPs

To verify the accuracy of this algorithm, the toxic effect on RAW264.7 cells at an antimicrobial peptide concentration of 200 µg/mL was examined. As the AMP concentration increased, RAW264.7 cell viability progressively declined. Therefore, we conducted in vitro bactericidal and biocompatibility assays of AMPs. The findings indicated that LR and KC exhibited the most effective bactericidal activity among the five AMPs tested, achieving a 100% bactericidal rate at a concentration of 25 µg/mL(figure 2A). Subsequently, we evaluated the biocompatibility of the five AMPs: LR, KC, KI, KP and WR. The CCK-8 assay indicated that KC displayed a marked dose-dependent cytotoxicity, with cell viability reduced to only 20.44% at 200 µg/mL. This suggests that KC exhibits significantly higher cytotoxicity compared with LR. Furthermore, the cytotoxic effects of KI, KP and WR were observed to increase with rising concentrations, displaying greater cytotoxicity than LR but lower than KC (figure 2B). Based on the combined bactericidal and cytotoxicity assessments, LR and KC were selected for subsequent experiments.

Consequently, we chose E. coli, S. aureus and carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii to assess the MBC of AMPs. The findings indicated that the bactericidal rates of LR against E. coli, S. aureus and resistant A. baumannii were 96.6%, 96.3% and 93.8% at a concentration of 20 µg/mL, respectively (figure 2C). In contrast, the bactericidal rates of KC against these bacterial strains were 100% at the same concentration (figure 2D).

Simultaneously, the cell staining results indicated that the cytotoxicity of KC was significantly higher than that of LR, with an increase in the number of dead cells correlating with higher KC concentrations (figure 2E). The assessment of the haemolytic activity of AMPs revealed that, within the MBC range, the haemolytic rates for LR and KC were below 3%. At a concentration of 100 µg/mL, LR exhibited a haemolytic rate of less than 8%, indicating slight haemolysis, whereas KC demonstrated a haemolytic rate of less than 34%, indicating substantial haemolysis (figure 2F).

In vitro evaluation of the anti-inflammatory effect of AMPs and results of the CD spectrum

Initially, LPS at concentrations of 0.1 µg/mL, 1 μg/ml, 10 µg/mL and 100 µg/mL were employed to stimulate RAW264.7 cells. Subsequently, the CCK-8 assay was used to determine cell viability. The findings indicated a cell survival rate of 91.4% at 0.1 µg/mL, with a significant decline in survival rate observed as LPS concentration increased (online supplemental figure S3A). An inflammation model was established using LPS at 0.1 µg/mL. Following this, varying concentration gradients of LR and KC were introduced, and the levels of inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α were quantified using an ELISA kit (Jiang-lai Bio.). The results indicated a decrease in the levels of inflammatory factors with increasing concentrations of LR, as demonstrated in online supplemental figure S3B. Specifically, LR at a concentration of 200 µg/mL significantly reduced the levels of inflammatory factors compared with the LPS group. In contrast, KC at the same concentration also exhibited a significant effect but a weaker inhibitory effect on the reduction of inflammatory factors (online supplemental figure S3C). Consequently, it can be inferred that LR possesses a certain degree of anti-inflammatory activity relative to KC. Considering the aforementioned bactericidal, cytotoxic, haemolytic and anti-inflammatory findings, LR was ultimately selected for subsequent experiments.

Furthermore, the CD spectroscopy data (online supplemental figure S3D) indicate that the LR and KC spectra in 10 mM PBS at pH 7.4 exhibited unordered conformations, lacking the formation of α-helix structures. In contrast, in a 50% TFE solution and a 30 mM anionic SDS solution, both LR and KC demonstrated α-helical structures, as evidenced by signals at 208 nm and 220 nm.

In vitro stability of AMPs

The results from the in vitro thermal stability experiments revealed that, at 60°C, the bactericidal rate remained at 100% when the concentration of AMPs was 30 µg/mL. The findings suggest that the bactericidal activity of AMPs was consistent before and after thermal treatment (figure 3A).

The in vitro acid-base stability test results demonstrated that following treatment with AMPs in simulated gastric fluid at pH 2 (figure 3B) and simulated intestinal fluid at pH 8.4 (figure 3C), the bactericidal efficacy of LR against S. aureus remained consistent before and after exposure to pH 2. However, for E. coli, the bactericidal rate decreased to 96.5% at a concentration of 30 µg/mL. Upon treatment at pH 8.4, the bactericidal rates for E. coli and S. aureus declined from 100% to 82.3% and 84.8%, respectively, at a concentration of 30 µg/mL.

The in vitro proteolytic stability assessment of AMPs revealed that following pepsin treatment, the bactericidal efficacy of LR remained at 100% at a concentration of 100 µg/mL. However, at a reduced concentration of 30 µg/mL, the bactericidal rate of LR decreased to approximately 95% for both E. coli and S. aureus, as illustrated in figure 3D. Furthermore, post-trypsin treatment (figure 3E) resulted in a decline in the bactericidal rate against S. aureus from 100% to 92% at 50 µg/mL, while for E. coli, it decreased to 75%. These findings suggest that the bactericidal activity of AMPs is partially compromised following trypsin treatment.

Effects of DSS and various therapeutic drugs on clinical symptoms

Mice were administered DSS water ad libitum for a duration of 7 days to induce a DSS-induced colitis model. The spleen index and colon length serve as critical indicators of the DSS-induced colitis phenotype. Treatment with DSS led to significant weight loss (figure 4A), an increase in DAI (figure 4B), a reduction in colon length and an elevated spleen index. Based on these observations, the DSS-induced colitis model was successfully established. Subsequently, experimental groups receiving AMPs, 5-ASA and CIP were established to evaluate the effects of various therapeutic agents on colitis. Among these, the AMPs group demonstrated the most pronounced therapeutic efficacy compared with the CIP and 5-ASA groups. In comparison to the DSS group, the body weight of mice progressively increased starting from the second day of gavage treatment, while both the DAI score and spleen index gradually decreased throughout the treatment period (figure 4C–E). The administration of AMPs via gavage demonstrated the most pronounced effects, resulting in the absence of occult blood in the stool and body weight closely resembling that of the healthy control group by the final day of treatment. In comparison to the control group, which exhibited a spleen index of 2.22±0.23 mg/g, the DSS group demonstrated a significantly elevated spleen index of 5.85±1.00 mg/g. Conversely, the spleen indices of the AMPs group (3.67±0.50 mg/g), the 5-ASA group (4.29±0.25 mg/g) and the CIP group (5.22±0.50 mg/g) were comparatively lower.

Similarly, the colons of the DSS group were notably shorter, measuring 5.75±0.55 cm, compared with the control group, which measured 7.78±0.50 cm. However, post-treatment recovery was observed in the colon lengths in the AMPs group (7.12±0.82 cm), the 5-ASA group (7.06±0.44 cm) and the CIP group (6.85±0.31 cm), as illustrated in figure 4F.

HE staining of distal colon tissue revealed an absence of lesions in the control group. In contrast, the DSS group exhibited pronounced mucosal structural damage, extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells and crypt destruction. Treatment with AMPs markedly reduced mucosal ulceration and inflammatory cell infiltration, restored crypt architecture and improved tissue structure. The therapeutic efficacy of the 5-ASA group was moderate, whereas the CIP group demonstrated a comparatively weak therapeutic effect (figure 4G).

To assess the therapeutic efficacy of each drug on the intestinal inflammatory response, we analysed the MPO content in colon tissue, as well as the levels of inflammatory factors and gene expression in both serum and colon tissue. The MPO levels in the DSS group were significantly elevated compared with the control group. In contrast, the MPO levels were markedly reduced in the AMPs treatment group, followed by the 5-ASA group, whereas the therapeutic effect observed in the CIP group was comparatively weak (figure 4H).

Analysis of inflammatory factors content and gene expression in serum and colonic tissues

The levels of IL-6 and TNF-α quantified in both serum and colonic tissue were significantly elevated in the DSS group compared with the control group. After treatment with AMPs, the inflammatory factor levels in the serum of mice nearly returned to those observed in the control group. Following treatment with 5-ASA and CIP, there was a reduction in the levels of inflammatory factors; however, these levels remained significantly higher than those observed in the control group (figure 5A). In summary, AMP treatment demonstrated a more pronounced inhibition of DSS-induced proinflammatory cytokine production compared with 5-ASA and CIP.

RT-qPCR analysis revealed that the expression levels of IL-6 and TNF-α genes in the colon tissues of the DSS group were significantly elevated relative to the control group. Post-treatment with the drugs, there was a notable decrease in the expression levels of these inflammatory factor genes. Compared with 5-ASA and CIP, the administration of AMPs resulted in a greater reduction in the expression levels of IL-6 and TNF-α. The efficacy of 5-ASA treatment was observed to be intermediate, while CIP treatment exhibited the least effect. Consequently, AMP treatment markedly diminished the gene expression of inflammatory markers in colon tissue, as illustrated in figure 5B.

Furthermore, the structural integrity of the colon was restored following pharmacological intervention. The expression levels of tight junction (TJ) proteins in the colon were evaluated using RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses. The findings indicated that the protein levels of ZO-1, Claudin-1 and Occludin were significantly reduced in the DSS group compared with other groups. Subsequent pharmacological intervention led to an upregulation of colonic TJ protein levels in DSS-induced colitis mice, with AMPs exhibiting the most pronounced effect, followed by 5-ASA and CIP treatment, as illustrated in figure 5C.

E. coli and S. aureus treated with PBS, AMPs and CIP were subjected to transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis. TEM revealed morphological alterations and cell membrane damage induced by AMPs and CIP. The results indicated that the E. coli and S. aureus specimens in the control group, as depicted in figure 6A, exhibited intact and smooth surfaces, characterised by a clear and complete double-layer membrane structure, with uniform cytoplasmic density.

Following treatment with AMPs, as illustrated in figure 6B, the E. coli surface transformed into a bubble-like layer, featuring numerous vesicle-like structures that detached from the surface. Additionally, the cytoplasm displayed significant heterogeneity, resembling bacterial vacuolation. Nevertheless, such vesicle-like structures were absent in S. aureus (figure 6B). Notably, when both bacterial types were treated with CIP, it was observed that E. coli did not exhibit a vesicle-like structural layer (figure 6C). On examining the results of ultrathin-section analysis, it was observed that the primary alterations included the presence of a disrupted membrane structure, indistinct boundaries, leakage of intracellular contents and extensive vacuolisation of the bacteria.

Changes in intestinal flora structure and diversity after treatment in DSS-induced colitis mice

To determine whether the therapeutic effects of the drug were associated with alterations in the gut microbiota of mice with DSS-induced colitis, 16S rRNA sequencing was conducted. The rarefaction curve, rank abundance curve and species accumulation box plot indicated that the diversity within the samples was sufficiently captured by the sequencing efforts. Principal Coordinates Analysis, using weighted UniFrac distance, revealed a significant separation of the DSS group from the control and AMPs groups. This finding suggests distinct intestinal flora compositions among the groups (figure 7A).

(C) Classification composition analysis of phylum level, class level and family level. Significant differences are denoted as *p<0.05. Microbial abundance and diversity were assessed through α diversity analyses, including Chao 1, Good’s coverage and the Shannon index (figure 7B). The findings indicated that the rich diversity of gut microbiota was altered following LR treatment. Furthermore, species distribution histograms were employed to examine the distribution of intestinal flora species. At the phylum level, the dominant taxa were identified as Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Verrucomicrobiota and Proteobacteria (figure 7C). Treatment with AMPs, 5-ASA and CIP resulted in an increased relative abundance of Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobia. At the class level, the main classes identified were Bacilli, Verrucomicrobiae, Clostridia and Bacteroidia. At the family level, the predominant families identified were Prevotellaceae_UCG-001, Alistipes, Akkermansia and Bacteroides. In the DSS group, there was a decrease in the relative abundance of Lactobacillus, whereas the relative abundances of Prevotellaceae_UCG-001 and Alistipes increased. Furthermore, in the AMPs and 5-ASA treatment groups, the relative abundances of Alistipes, Prevotellaceae and Bacteroidaceae, which are positively correlated with the progression of DSS-induced colitis, were reduced (figure 7C). Notably, within the treatment groups, a marked increase in Akkermansiaceae was observed, particularly in the AMPs group. Recent research indicates that the abundance of A. muciniphila, as determined through metagenomic analysis, is inversely correlated with IBD, obesity and diabetes.42

In summary, the AMP treatment group has the potential to alter the composition of the gut microbiota by decreasing the prevalence of pathogenic bacteria linked to the development of colitis and enhancing the abundance of beneficial bacteria. This intervention ultimately contributes to the partial restoration of the intestinal microbial ecological imbalance induced by DSS (online supplemental Material Text S10) (online supplemental figure S4).

Selective antimicrobial activity of LR towards pathogens over A. muciniphila

To specifically address the potential impact of the lead AMP, LR, on commensal microbiota, we evaluated its effect on the growth kinetics of the beneficial bacterium A. muciniphila in comparison to the pathogenic strains E. coli and S. aureus. As illustrated in online supplemental Figure S5, LR exhibited a potent and dose-dependent inhibitory effect on the growth of both E. coli and S. aureus. In stark contrast, the growth curve of A. muciniphila remained virtually unaffected across the same concentration range of LR (0–200 µg/mL) over the 24-hour incubation period. This clear disparity in susceptibility highlights a selective antimicrobial profile for peptide LR. The ability of LR to effectively target pathogens while sparing A. muciniphila—a keystone probiotic species associated with improved gut barrier function and amelioration of colitis—is highly advantageous. It suggests that the therapeutic application of LR is unlikely to disrupt this beneficial species, thereby supporting a favourable ecological outcome in the gut microbiome during treatment.

A. muciniphila replenishment can treat DSS-induced colitis

Based on the 16S rRNA sequencing results, we observed that A. muciniphila was significantly enriched in the AMPs treatment group. Therefore, we used A. muciniphila to replenish and treat DSS-induced colitis. The results showed that after A. muciniphila replenishment treatment, the body weight of the diseased mice increased, the DAI decreased, the spleen index decreased and the colon length increased. Mice in the control group and healthy group showed no significant changes in any of these indicators (figure 8). Together, these results suggest that supplementation with A. muciniphila alone is sufficient to ameliorate several key pathological features of DSS-induced colitis in mice.

Discussion

The development of our predictive models, Peptide_Predictor and Toxic_Predictor, represents a useful step in the field of AMP research. Our models have demonstrated considerable strengths, with Peptide_Predictor showing high accuracy in identifying AMPs, a crucial capability for the rapid screening of potential candidates in large datasets. The optimisation process has notably enhanced the F1 score for both models, indicating a balanced improvement in precision and recall, which are pivotal metrics in classification tasks. The comprehensive set of descriptors from the modlamp library has been instrumental in characterising peptide sequences, contributing significantly to the models’ predictive power. Furthermore, the implementation of fivefold cross-validation has ensured that our models’ performance is consistent and reliable across different subsets of the data.

However, our models also face certain limitations. The Toxic_Predictor, while integral for assessing the safety of potential AMPs, has encountered challenges in accurately predicting toxicity.43 This is a critical factor in the practical application of these peptides, as their toxicity profiles can significantly influence their therapeutic utility. Additionally, the observed correlation between toxicity and bactericidal activity has complicated the model’s ability to distinguish between toxic and non-toxic peptides, potentially due to overlapping features, which may be indicative of both properties.

To address these challenges and further enhance our models, we plan to explore more advanced models, such as machine learning models, which could potentially capture more complex patterns in the data and improve toxicity prediction.44 45 We also intend to refine our feature set, possibly by incorporating domain-specific knowledge or alternative descriptor sets that could better differentiate between toxic and non-toxic peptides. Implementing strategies to address class imbalance, such as oversampling or adjusting class weights, could also improve the model’s ability to predict less frequent outcomes, like toxicity.

Furthermore, we aim to integrate additional data sources, such as peptide structure information or experimental toxicity data, which could provide the models with a more comprehensive understanding of peptide properties. Establishing a framework for continuous model evaluation as new data becomes available will ensure that the models remain up to date and relevant. By addressing the current limitations and exploring new avenues for model development, we aim to enhance the predictive capabilities and applicability of our models in the field of antimicrobial research, ultimately contributing to the discovery and development of novel AMPs with optimised safety profiles.

In the current study, we evaluated the stability profile of our lead candidate, LR, under physiologically relevant conditions. The peptide exhibited remarkable thermal and acidic stability, retaining full bactericidal activity after incubation at 60°C and in simulated gastric fluid (pH 2.0). This robustness is a promising attribute for oral administration.

Building on these favourable stability characteristics, we demonstrate that our algorithmically screened and optimised AMP, LR effectively ameliorated intestinal inflammation in a DSS-induced murine colitis model. This therapeutic benefit was associated with a marked improvement in the inflammatory state and a restoration of the intestinal epithelial barrier integrity. Notably, the AMP treatment group showed superior efficacy in mitigating disease activity and promoting histological recovery compared with the 5-ASA and CIP groups. These findings highlight the potential of our machine learning-driven approach to identify and design novel AMPs with significant therapeutic promise for inflammatory bowel conditions.

The nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) pathway is a highly important pathway in cell signalling, and it plays a key role in regulating various physiological processes such as immune response and inflammatory response. This pathway regulates the expression of multiple genes in response to a variety of stimuli, including pro-inflammatory cytokines, free radicals, bacterial and viral infections. Studies have shown that activation of the NF-κB pathway produces pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, in UC mice, leading to inflammation of the colon.46 47 Consistent with this finding, we found elevated levels of inflammatory factors in serum and colon tissue in the DSS group, and decreased levels of TNF-α and IL-6 after treatment, while in our in vitro cellular anti-inflammatory experiments, we found that AMP reduced LPS-induced cellular inflammation and reduced levels of TNF-α and IL-6.

At present, studies have shown that intestinal mucosal barrier dysfunction also plays an important role in the pathogenesis of UC.48 It was found that the physical structure of intestinal epithelial TJ changed, and intestinal mucosal barrier dysfunction appeared in patients with UC,49 50 which was consistent with our experimental results, and the expression of TJ proteins ZO-1, Claudin-1 and Occludin was decreased in the DSS group. This study found that AMPs increased the expression of TJ proteins, suggesting that AMPs have a positive effect on restoring the integrity of the colonic epithelial barrier.

We also found that AMPs can regulate the composition of gut microbiota, with a significant increase in the proportion of A. muciniphila in the stool of mice in the AMP-treated group. In addition, our A. muciniphila replenishment experiment results also demonstrated that A. muciniphila supplementation could alleviate the symptoms of UC, although the mechanism is still unclear. A. muciniphila, which is mainly colonised in the intestine, is known to improve intestinal immune function and is important for maintaining normal intestinal function.51 52 Studies have found that A. muciniphila can improve a variety of intestinal diseases,53 54 and the abundance of A. muciniphila was significantly reduced in mice with intestinal inflammatory diseases such as IBS, UC,55 appendicitis56 and allergic diarrhoea.57 Moreover, studies have also shown that A. muciniphila is associated with reduced weight gain, decreased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and restoration of intestinal epithelial barrier function in DSS-induced colitis.58,60 Therefore, based on the results of our experiments, we hypothesised that AMPs can restore colonic barrier damage by regulating intestinal flora composition and increasing A. muciniphila.

Changes in small molecule metabolites affected by AMPs are also an important part of UC therapy. The results of stool metabolomics in mice in this study showed significant changes in the levels of deoxycholic acid, glycolithocholic acid, taurodeoxycholic acid and other metabolites.

Limitations of the study

While our findings highlight the therapeutic promise of machine learning-driven AMPs in UC, several limitations merit consideration. First, the inherent ‘black-box’ nature of machine learning models limits mechanistic interpretation of how specific physicochemical features govern AMP functionality, despite their predictive accuracy. Second, the translational relevance of our DSS-induced murine colitis model remains constrained by inherent differences between murine and human gut microbiome composition and immune responses. Third, while 16S rRNA sequencing provided insights into microbial shifts, metagenomic or metatranscriptomic analyses would better resolve functional alterations in low-abundance taxa and strain-level dynamics. Fourth, our study focused on short-term therapeutic efficacy; long-term safety assessments of AMPs, including potential off-target effects on commensal microbiota or epithelial integrity, remain to be addressed. Fifth, although our study demonstrates the therapeutic potential of the identified AMPs in a DSS-induced colitis model, it is important to note that their small peptide nature may pose challenges for oral administration due to potential degradation in the gastrointestinal tract and low systemic bioavailability. Finally, although A. muciniphila enrichment correlated with AMP efficacy, causality was partially validated through A. muciniphila replenishment treatment; the underlying mechanisms remain to be fully explored. These limitations underscore the need for expanded preclinical models, multi-omics integration and rigorous safety profiling to advance AMPs toward clinical translation.

Conclusion

In summary, we combined machine learning-based prediction with experimental validation to identify LR as a stable, selective and biocompatible antimicrobial peptide with therapeutic potential for UC. LR alleviated intestinal inflammation, restored epithelial barrier integrity and reshaped gut microbiota composition, including the enrichment of A. muciniphila. These findings highlight the value of computational screening for AMP discovery and suggest that LR may serve as a promising candidate for the development of microbiota-friendly therapeutics for IBD.
