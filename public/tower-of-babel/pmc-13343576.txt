Genome-wide characterization of LPMOs and CAZymes in camel rumen-derived Paenibacillus barengoltzii highlights strong lignocellulolytic potential
Authors: Shaswat Parmar, Shivangi Patel, Srinivas Duggirala, Ashish Patel, Ketankumar Panchal, Subhash Jakhesara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCamels exhibit remarkable adaptability to harsh environments, in part due to their unique ruminal microbiota. Paenibacillus barengoltzii, isolated from camel rumen, plays a crucial role in rumen fermentation and plant biomass degradation. The present study aimed to identify fermentative genes and assess their lignocellulolytic potential.ResultsWhole-genome sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform to enable comprehensive comparative genomic analysis of carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) across 11 Paenibacillus species. It was observed that species-specific CAZymes repertoires, with P. barengoltzii harboring genes for plant biomass degradation, including GH2, GH13, GH20, GH27, GH28, GH32, GH62, GH65, GH67, GH68, GH88, GH105, AA4, AA10, and PL8, indicating its polysaccharide catabolic potential. Genome-wide analysis identified one Lytic Polysaccharide Monooxygenase (LPMO) gene involved in oxidative polysaccharide cleavage. LPMOs co-localized with chitin-active glycoside hydrolase GH18, along with its association with cellulose enzymes. Protein-protein interaction and gene networking studies revealed LPMO co-occurrence and interaction with chitinase and cellulose degradation. Structural predictions provided LPMO substrate-binding sites, emphasizing their functional significance in biomass degradation. Functional annotation identified 121 key domains implicated in plant biomass degradation.ConclusionThe present study elucidates the genomic organization of LPMO genes in P. barengoltzii, highlighting its potential for biomass hydrolysis. The genomic insights underscore the need for further experimental validation of P. barengoltzii to enhance ruminal fermentation and plant biomass decomposition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05134-0.

Background

Camels are an outstanding example of adaptability, thriving in some of the toughest environments on the planet, such as deserts, where extreme temperatures, water scarcity, and limited food availability prevail. Other ruminants, such as cattle, sheep, and goats, are well characterized and exhibit highly efficient digestive and metabolic systems under typical environmental conditions. In contrast, camels have evolved distinct morphological, physiological, and behavioral adaptations that enable them to survive in extreme, arid environments. These adaptations include a high tolerance to dehydration, efficient fat storage in the humps for later energy use, and significant fluctuations in body temperature [1]. While traditional ruminants possess a four-chambered stomach, camels have a three-chambered system, comprising the C1, C2, and C3 compartments, with C1 functioning similarly to the rumen in true ruminants [2]. This unique gastrointestinal modification has led to the classification of camels as pseudo-ruminants, as they exhibit significant deviations from true ruminants in terms of foregut structure, compartmentalization, and associated digestive physiology.

Rumen microbiology is a research area that has recently attracted considerable attention, as it involves studying microbes inhabiting the digestive tract of ruminants [2]. The rumen is important for digestion, especially for fermenting and breaking down tough plant materials to release nutrients [3–5]. The bacteria, archaea, protozoa, and fungi are the main organisms present in the rumen, and they all contribute to the degradation of cellulose and hemicellulose, thus making these nutrients accessible to the host animal. Recent technological innovations in molecular methods and next-generation sequencing have broadened the scope of research in rumen microbiology, enabling detailed studies of microbial diversity and function. The technological innovations in this field have provided the scientific community with important information on the interrelations among microbial communities, the host’s nutrition and health, and even the host’s adaptability [3–5].

In camels, however, the study of rumen microbial ecosystem is still in its early stages, despite their importance as livestock in many arid and semi-arid regions. Currently, compared to other ruminants, no extensive research has been conducted on the camel digestive system, particularly on the specific microbial composition and its functions [5]. Understanding these microbial communities is necessary to improve camel nutrition and health, as these animals are becoming increasingly important for sustainable agriculture in climate-change-affected regions. The present research aims to address this gap by isolating and characterizing microorganisms from the camel rumen, focusing on their ability to degrade complex plant substrates. The sequencing technology, along with bioinformatics, will provide valuable insights into the functional characteristics of these organisms and thus facilitate the overall understanding of camel adaptation and digestive efficiency [6–8].

P. barengoltzii is a Gram-positive, spore-forming, rod-shaped facultative anaerobic bacterium motile with peritrichous flagella that grows at 37 ℃ and pH 7.0. P. barengoltzii is well known for its extracellular enzyme production, such as cellulase, hemicellulose, Xylanase, chitinase, and many more, which play a significant role in metabolic processes [9]. Advances in molecular techniques, especially next-generation sequencing (NGS), offer enhanced speed and accuracy by massively parallelizing DNA fragment sequencing, encompassing library preparation, sequencing, and data analysis [10]. NGS platforms such as Illumina, Ion Torrent, Oxford Nanopore, and Pacific Biosciences enable diverse applications, including whole-genome sequencing and metagenomic analysis [11]. This comprehensive approach enables the examination of entire genomes or cellular contents, facilitating the identification of gene products and offering broad utility across various disciplines [12, 13]. The present study aimed to isolate an anaerobic bacterium from the camel rumen and perform whole-genome sequencing to examine its role in degrading complex plant substrates.

Materials and methods

Culturing of camel rumen isolates

Samples used in the present study were obtained from animals sampled under ethically approved protocols described in a previously published study [14].

Rumen liquor was aseptically collected from fistulated dromedary camels fed a high-fiber diet and transferred in airtight tubes under anoxic conditions. Samples were diluted in a 0.5% NaCl and 0.5% cysteine-HCl solution to maintain anaerobic conditions. All handling steps—dilution, inoculation, and transfer—were conducted in an anaerobic glove box using Hungate roll tubes [14].Enrichment was performed in anaerobic basal medium (bicarbonate buffer, mineral salts, vitamins, cysteine-HCl, and resazurin) supplemented with 2% starch, cellulose, or birchwood xylan as carbon sources [15]. Tubes were flushed with N₂/CO₂ (80:20), sealed, and incubated at 39 °C for 7 days. Hydrolytic activity was screened on solid media using Lugol’s iodine and Congo red staining to visualize clear zones (Menke, n.d.). Active cultures were sub-cultured to obtain stable fibrolytic bacterial isolates, following standard anaerobic cultivation protocols [15, 16].

API (Analytical Profile index) 20E system

A single colony from a 24-hour pure culture was suspended in sterile saline to 0.5 McFarland turbidity and inoculated into API 20 strips. Tubes for CIT, VP, and GEL were filled, while ADH, LDC, ODC, H₂S, and URE were overlaid with mineral oil. Strips were incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. After incubation, reagents (Ferric chloride, Kovac’s, KOH + α-naphthol) were added to the TDA, IND, and VP tests, respectively. Color changes were recorded using the reference chart to generate a 7-digit biochemical profile.

Biolog MicroStation with GEN III microplate system

Phenotypic identification of bacterial isolates was performed using the Biolog GEN III MicroPlate system (Biolog Inc., Hayward, CA, USA), which assesses 94 biochemical tests, including 71 carbon-source utilization tests and 23 chemical-sensitivity assays. Bacterial cultures grown on BUG medium at 37 °C for 24 h were suspended in Inoculating Fluid A (IF-A) to 95% transmittance and inoculated (100 µL/well) into GEN III plates. Plates were incubated at 33 °C in an OmniLog™/Odin reader, and metabolic activity was monitored kinetically every 20 min. The obtained profiles were automatically matched with the Biolog GEN III database for species-level identification. The system evaluates diverse carbon sources, amino acids, organic acids, and inhibitory compounds, including sugars (e.g., glucose, fructose, sucrose), amino acids (e.g., alanine, arginine, serine), organic acids (e.g., citric and succinic acids), and stress agents (e.g., fusidic acid, rifamycin SV, NaCl, and pH sensitivity) [16–18].

DNA isolation and genome sequencing

The QIAamp DNA Mini kit was used to extract the genomic DNA of P. barengoltzii. Gel electrophoresis and the ND1000 Spectrophotometer (Nano Drop Technologies, Inc., USA) were used to assess the quality and quantity of isolated DNA, respectively. The library was prepared using the Nextera XT kit and analyzed on the Bioanalyzer 2100 with the High Sensitivity DNA chip. The Illumina MiSeq sequencing platform was used for cluster generation and sequencing using 2 × 250 bp chemistry.

Raw read generations and pre-processing of reads

After Illumina sequencing, a large number of raw reads are generated and saved as FASTQ files, which include sequence data, quality scores, and metadata (Fig. 1).

These raw reads must be processed to remove sequencing errors and low-quality regions before they can be used for downstream analysis. FastQC (v0.11.9) was used to assess raw data quality, and PRINSEQ (v0.20.4) to preprocess it [19].

De novo genome assembly, genome annotation, and taxonomic identification

De novo genome assembly was performed using SPAdes (v3.13.1) [20], and the quality assessment of the assembled genome was conducted with QUAST (v5.20) [21]. Genome annotation was achieved through a combination of various bioinformatics tools and pipelines, including Prokka (v1.14.6) [22], RAST (v2.0) [23], Prodigal (v2.6.3) [24], and Glimmer (utilised via OmicsBox v3.0) [25]. For taxonomic identification of whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data, multiple bioinformatics tools with distinct algorithms were employed, including NCBI BLAST, Proksee, FastANI (v1.1.0) [26], BWA (v0.7.17), SAMtools (v1.13) [27], CLC Genomics Workbench (v23.0.2) [28], PubMLST [29], GTDB-Tk (v1.7.0) [30], JSpeciesWS [31], and DIAMOND BLAST [32]. NCBI BLAST and DIAMOND BLAST utilize heuristic sequence alignment algorithms for similarity searches, while FastANI and JSpeciesWS apply k-mer–based and ANI algorithms for genome comparison and species delineation. GTDB-Tk performs phylogenomic classification using marker genes and evolutionary tree placement, and Proksee integrates genome visualization and comparative annotation, collectively enabling accurate taxonomic classification. PubMLST, which uses allele-based typing for strain identification, was also applied to support and validate the findings. BWA, which uses the Burrows–Wheeler Transform for efficient read alignment, along with SAMtools for sorting and variant calling, was used to perform a reference-based genome assembly to confirm further and validate the results. In parallel, CLC Genomics Workbench employs de Bruijn graph-based assembly and mapping approaches.

Identification of CRISPR

CRISPRCasTyper(v1.8.0) [33] was used to analyze CRISPR-Cas systems in the assembled genome.

In silico identification of Carbohydrate-Active Enzyme Repertoires (CAZymes)

The identification of fermentative genes was carried out using CUPP(v2.0.0) [34] and dbCAN(v3.0.0) [35]. The dbCAN database, which contains curated information on carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes), was used to annotate the functions of these enzymes. Genome sequence data were compared against dbCAN’s HMM profiles using tools such as HMMER and DIAMOND, facilitating the identification of CAZymes and other carbohydrate-active enzymes. CUPP was employed to predict fermentative genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism and the specific protein domains associated with these genes.

Comparative analysis of CAZymes

For comparative genome analysis, previously characterized, publicly available complete genomes of the genus Paenibacillus were used. All selected genomes were retrieved from the NCBI database under their respective Bioproject numbers, as listed: P. algicola HB172198 (PRJNA531793), P. polymyxa ND24 (PRJNA323402), P. polymyxa ND25 (PRJNA323402), P. amylolyticus 27C64 (PRJNA499118), P. polymyxa A18 (PRJNA225496), P. alvei JR949 (PRJNA631360), P. dendritiformis CRN18 (PRJNA623154), P. polymyxa HK4 (PRJNA603023), Paenibacillus sp. 79R4 (PRJNA474007), and P. lautus BHU3 (PRJNA411887). Comparative analyses of CAZyme-related genes in the selected genomes were performed using dbCAN3.

Functional analysis of genome

Genome functional analysis is essential for deciphering the roles that individual genes play in microbial functioning. Functional annotation was performed using InterProScan (v5.59-91) [36], while Gene Ontology (GO) annotation was carried out with Blast2GO [37], EggNOG-mapper [38], and KEGG pathway analysis [39]. Additionally, BlastKOALA(v3.0) [40] was used for the functional annotation and pathway mapping of genes.

Protein-protein interaction and network pathway analysis

STRING (Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins) is a widely recognized database that provides comprehensive information on known and predicted protein–protein interactions across a broad range of organisms. By integrating data from experimental studies, computational predictions, text mining, and curated pathway databases, STRING facilitates the exploration of functional protein associations and the analysis of complex biological pathways. In this study, the STRING server was used for protein–protein interaction mapping and network pathway analysis [41].

Results

API 20E-based biochemical identification

The isolate exhibited the following biochemical characteristics: catalase, gelatinase, urease, β-galactosidase, motility, and H₂S production was positive, while oxidase was negative. In the API 20 sugar fermentation test (BioMérieux), the strain showed positive utilization of glucose, maltose, sucrose, arabinose, xylose, rhamnose, cellobiose, lactose, and fructose, indicating broad carbohydrate metabolism.

Metabolic and phenotypic profiling by biolog GEN III MicroPlate assay

The Biolog GEN III MicroPlate assay showed positive metabolic activity in selected wells, indicated by bluish-purple coloration. The strain utilized a range of substrates, including carbohydrates and amino acids, reflecting moderate metabolic versatility. It also demonstrated tolerance to conditions such as 1% NaCl, pH 6, and sodium lactate, suggesting environmental adaptability. Limited or variable responses to compounds such as minocycline and sodium bromate were observed (Supplementary Table 1).

Genome sequencing and assembly

P. barengoltzii was isolated from the camel rumen as part of an effort to identify microbiota involved in the breakdown of plant biomass. The genome of P. barengoltzii was sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform, yielding 545,628 raw reads. De novo genome assembly using SPAdes produced 2,676 contigs totaling 6,129,285 bases (6.12 Mb), with an average GC content of 50.11%.

Genome annotation

The genome of Paenibacillus barengoltzii was annotated using multiple pipelines, including Prokka, Prodigal, RAST, and Glimmer, to ensure comprehensive gene prediction and annotation. Structural RNA features, including rRNAs, tRNAs, tmRNA, and CRISPR elements, were identified by Prokka and RAST, are shown in Table 1 and Fig. 2. Gene prediction by Prodigal identified 7,448 coding sequences, while Glimmer predicted 5,864 coding regions.

Taxonomic identification

Taxonomic identification of P. barengoltzii was performed using a stepwise bioinformatics approach. Initially, NCBI BLAST and DIAMOND BLAST were used for preliminary sequence similarity searches against reference databases, indicating high similarity (96–99.61%) to P. barengoltzii. This was followed by genome-wide similarity assessment using FastANI and JSpeciesWS, which confirmed species-level identity based on Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) thresholds. Subsequently, GTDB-Tk was employed for phylogenomic classification, placing the genome within the P. barengoltzii clade. For further validation, reference-based genome assembly was conducted using the P. barengoltzii G22 genome from the NCBI database, with read alignment performed with BWA and processed with SAMtools. CLC Genomics Workbench was used for assembly refinement and validation. The analysis showed 77.28% and 77.8% genome similarity with the P. barengoltzii G22 reference, respectively. Collectively, these results provide robust evidence supporting the taxonomic classification of the organism under study as P. barengoltzii.

Identification of CRISPR

The CRISPR-CasTYPER tool has identified three CRISPR sequences in the genome of P. barengoltzii, located on Node 8 and Node 69, as depicted in Fig. 3. Node 8, with a complete CRISPR-Cas system containing 5 cas genes (cas1, cas2, cas3, cas7, cas8c), is considered to have a well-developed immune system capable of inhibiting foreign genetic elements. However, Node 69 contains a less complex CRISPR region with a small number of cas genes, primarily Csm5gr7, suggesting a rudimentary immune mechanism primarily targeting interference. These findings are illustrated in Fig. 3, which highlights the distinct CRISPR systems present on each node.

Identification of fermentative genes

The fermentative genes recognized by dbCAN3 and CUPP were those belonging to several CAZyme classes, which included AA (Auxiliary Activities), CBM (Carbohydrate-Binding Modules), CE (Carbohydrate Esterases), GH (Glycoside Hydrolases), GT (Glycosyltransferases), PL (Polysaccharide Lyases), and SLH (S-layer Homology Domains), among others. The dbCAN3 tool builds upon the HMMER and DIAMOND algorithms to provide extensive analysis of CAZyme gene content in genomic data, making it well-suited for in-depth functional studies. The gene families involved in cellulose digestion include GH3, GH2, GH39, GH4, GH5, GH8, GH94, GH130, GH151, CBM32, CBM9, CE9, and CE32, according to an investigation of the authorship of fermentative genes. Several CAZyme families, including GH88, GH105, GH27, GH20, AA10, and PL8, were rarely detected but contribute significantly to functional potential. These family groups contribute significantly to the overall degradation of plant biomass, as they are the primary agents in breaking down the cellulosic and hemicellulosic fractions. Their particular enzyme activities demonstrate their contribution to more thorough and effective biomass decomposition.

A total of 27 and 18 different CAZymes for hemicellulose and cellulose, respectively, were identified in P. barengoltzii. Certain species showed greater variation, and three species shared the largest number of hemicellulose-degrading genes, i.e., P. barengoltzii (26) and Paenibacillus sp. 79R4 (20), and P. lautus BHU3 (17). Under cellulose degradation, P. barengoltzii (17), P. algicola (5), and P. polymyxa ND24 (9) were the ones with a relatively high number of genes.

The Paenibacillus species group exhibited a wide range of abilities to degrade starch through enzymes from 19 CAZy families. The one species, P. barengoltzii, had all 19 families detected. In contrast, the species P. algicola (9), P. polymyxa ND24 (7), and P. amylolyticus (10) were found to have fewer CAZyme families active on starch.

To carry out lignin degradation, a highly complex process, the cooperation of multiple CAZyme families is required. P. barengoltzii was identified as a member together with seven other Paenibacillus species that have CAZyme families associated with the degradation of plant biomass. Among them, P. alvei, with five CAZyme families, and P. polymyxa ND25, with four CAZyme families, were also recognized as possible candidates for lignin degradation.

When it comes to chitin degradation, P. barengoltzii showed a greater power to do so, as the genome of this species contains all seven known chitin-related CAZyme families. Other Paenibacillus species, for example, P. algicola and P. polymyxa ND24, being two and four families, respectively, showed less chitin-degrading potential.

The CAZyme analysis showed that P. barengoltzii has the genetic potential to encode 24 CAZyme family members involved in the breakdown of complex polysaccharides. P. algicola HB17219 (13 families), P. polymyxa ND24 (10 families), and P. alvei JR949 (12 families) are some of the other strains that also have considerable ability to degrade polysaccharides.

Pectin degradation in P. barengoltzii was attributed to the presence of a single CAZyme family, PL1, which was also identified in several other Paenibacillus species, including P. polymyxa ND25, A18, and HK4, P. amylolyticus 27C64, P. lautus BHU3, Paenibacillus sp. O199, and P. alvei JR949.

Functional analysis of genome

In the present analysis, 4,492 Gene Ontology (GO) terms were identified, associated with a wide range of metabolic processes. Notably, several GO terms related to carbohydrate degradation were observed, as illustrated in Fig. 6.

Functional classification of carbohydrate breakdown processes by gene ontology (GO) (legend displays the square root-transformed counts of the carbohydrate metabolic pathways)

As shown in Fig. 6, functional annotation identified several mechanisms associated with P. barengoltzii’s carbohydrate breakdown that help transform plant biomass into simpler forms. Carbohydrate catabolism, glycolysis, cellular carbohydrate breakdown, carbohydrate derivative catabolism, glucose catabolism, cell wall macromolecule metabolism, and cellular polysaccharide metabolism are among the important metabolic processes found. To convert complex carbohydrates into simpler forms, including polysaccharides, monosaccharides, oligosaccharides, glucose, fructose, mannose, xylose, and volatile fatty acids, several processes are crucial.

InterProScan analysis identified 3,254 protein domains in the genome, indicating that many proteins have multifunctional roles. These IPR domains include specific motifs involved in degradation pathways and other cellular activities. Notably, approximately 121 putative novel domains were predicted to play critical roles in the breakdown of carbohydrates and their derivatives, including polysaccharides, monosaccharides, and oligosaccharides, among which cellulose-degrading enzymes, domains such as IPR036966 (CBM3) associated with cellulose degradation, and IPR010383, IPR012341, and IPR037825 linked to cellobiose phosphorylase activity were identified. The frequent occurrence of IPR018120 suggests it is a conserved glycoside hydrolase-associated domain, commonly linked to β-glucosidase and β-galactosidase activities.

In the hemicellulose degradation, the highest diversity of IPR domains was observed, including IPR006102, IPR006101, and IPR023230 (GH2-related), as well as IPR003476 (GH42) and IPR019801 (GH35), indicating enzymatic machinery for degrading structurally complex polymers such as xylan and mannans. Domains such as IPR000602, IPR041147, and IPR041509 were associated with α-mannosidase activity, reflecting substrate-specific specialization.

The starch-degrading enzyme group was dominated by GH13-associated domains, including IPR006046, IPR006047, IPR006048, and IPR031319, corresponding to the catalytic and structural regions of α-amylase. Additional domains (IPR005194–IPR005196) linked to maltose phosphorylase further indicate diverse starch utilization pathways.

For pectin degradation, domains such as IPR000743 (polygalacturonase), IPR012234 (pectin lyase), and IPR012334 (pectate lyase) were detected, while chitin-degrading enzymes were characterized by domains including IPR001223 (chitinase) and IPR002037 (chitosanase).

The category of other polysaccharides and simple sugars included domains such as IPR002241 (α-galactosidase/dextranase), IPR032477 (β-1,3-glucanase), and IPR001362 (invertase-related enzymes), indicating broad substrate versatility. Importantly, several IPR domains (e.g., IPR018120, IPR001223, IPR032477) were repeatedly identified across multiple substrate categories, suggesting conserved multifunctional roles.

Overall, the InterProScan results demonstrate a highly diverse, and modular CAZyme system, with the identified 121 domains collectively contributing to efficient carbohydrate degradation and metabolic adaptability which is shown in Supplementary Table 3.

An IPR domain is a conserved protein region identified by InterProScan, associated with specific protein functions or structural features. Additionally, the analysis revealed 12 IPR domains involved in the degradation of cellulosic and hemicellulosic substrates, as shown in Table 2. Furthermore, 74 unknown IPR domains were detected, whose functions have not yet been characterized.

The InterProScan analysis was used to identify protein domains important for understanding enzyme activity in carbohydrate metabolism, glycan degradation, and microbial interactions. This investigation is critical because protein domains define the functional properties of enzymes and other proteins, providing insight into metabolic processes. InterProScan identified important domains in the genome, providing crucial insights into the protein machinery that underpins key biological processes such as glucose digestion and enzymatic activity. This method is critical for understanding complicated microbial metabolic networks. The studied proteins display various enzymatic functions related to carbohydrate metabolism, glycan degradation, and microbial interactions.

The 449-amino-acid-long protein belongs to the GlcNAc-Chitin Interaction and Degradation family (Fig. 7). It carries domains including LPMO_AA10, chi_5, cellulose/chitin-binding, immunoglobulin-like folds, and FN3. These structural elements suggest possible roles in chitin recognition, degradation, and substrate binding, thereby contributing to glycan processing and antimicrobial defense.

Arabinoxylan and hemicellulose are degraded by proteins such as C812_03920, C812_00141, and C812_00397, which are believed to belong to the GH43 family. C812_01731, on the other hand, belongs to the GH8 family of endoglucanases and aids in the breakdown of cellulose. Other enzymes that help break down cellobiose and chitin include the cellobiose phosphorylase C812_02014 and chitinases from the GH18 family, such as C812_01813, C812_01448, and C812_02845. Additionally, proteins like C812_03947 and C812_03473 have been discovered as endo-1,4-beta-xylanases, whilst C812_03498, C812_02699, and C812_00771 are involved in the breakdown of arabinoxylan. Finally, the identification of proteins such as C812_03928 and C812_02932, which are associated with the GH11 family’s xylose-releasing exo-oligoxylanase and endo-1,4-beta-xylanase, together with a highly scoring L-arabinose isomerase (araA), suggests a coherent route for lignocellulose breakdown.

The STRING interaction data indicated that these proteins exhibited strong, high-confidence associations (0.700–0.900), suggesting a highly coordinated metabolic network. Identification of prfC (527 aa, Peptide chain release factor 3, involved in ribosomal termination) and C812_02309 (1-phosphofructokinase family hexose kinase, carbohydrate kinase PfkB family) suggests additional functionalities in protein synthesis and glycolysis.

The EggNOG mapper has been used in this work to reveal the distribution of Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) categories. The categories of carbohydrate transport and metabolism (G-10.62%), transcription (K-6.0%), amino acid transport and metabolism (E-5.34%), and signal transduction pathways (T-1.67%) were found to be substantially prevalent. These results highlight the significance of cellular signaling pathways, glucose metabolism, transcriptional control, and amino acid usage in the biological context of P. barengoltzii.

A comprehensive understanding of the organism’s metabolic capacities was provided through pathway analysis, with a particular focus on carbohydrate metabolism. A total of 31 pathways related to carbohydrate utilization and degradation were identified through analyses using two platforms, OmicsBox and BLASTKOALA. Most notably, OmicsBox discovered 15 distinct pathways linked to carbs, whereas BLASTKOALA discovered 16. The body’s capacity to enzymatically convert complex carbohydrates into simpler forms more accessible to biological systems is demonstrated by these routes taken together. This demonstrates its metabolic adaptability in efficiently using various carbon sources.

Discussion

Camels (Camelus dromedarius) are an essential component of desert livestock. Camels are incredibly resilient animals capable of enduring harsh weather conditions and temperature fluctuations [42]. The camel’s rumen, specifically the C1 chamber, is inhabited by a dense population of microorganisms, including bacteria, protozoa, archaea, and fungi. The ruminal ecosystem comprises a wide diversity of microorganisms that form symbiotic relationships within a strictly anaerobic environment [2, 42]. Some of the new phyla found in the camel rumen include Bacteroidetes & firmicutes which represented a higher proportion of the bacterial community, along with Fibrobacteres, Spirochaetae, Elusimicrobia, Proteobacteria, Synergistes, Verrucomicrobia, Paenibacillus, Actinomyces, Clostridium, Cyanobacteria, Chloroflexi, Lentisphaerae, and Planctomycetes [3]. These microbes are crucial for the breakdown and digestion of the plant materials consumed by the camel [42]. The camel demonstrates superior efficiency in digesting fiber from a variety of sources, such as range plants, forage, and grasses, compared to other domestic ruminants. P. barengoltzii shows potential for contributing to lignocellulose degradation, indicating a possible role in plant material breakdown and nutrient release in diverse biological environments such as the ruminant gut [4, 43].

Paenibacillus barengoltzii is a Gram-positive, facultatively anaerobic, spore-forming, rod-shaped bacterium, motile by peritrichous flagella. Growth occurs within the temperature range of 10–50 °C and pH 4.5-9.0, with optimal conditions at 37 °C and pH 7 [44]. P. barengoltzii is notable for its capacity to generate several extracellular enzymes such as proteases, lipases, and cellulases, positioning it as a promising candidate for numerous biotechnological uses [45]. Additionally, P. barengoltzii produces bacteriocins, antimicrobial peptides that inhibit the growth of other bacteria. This characteristic could make it useful for the development of novel antibiotics [43]. The present study revealed that extracellular enzymes produced by P. barengoltzii, such as cellulase, hemicellulase, xylulose, and chitinase, play a vital role in the metabolism of various plant and fungal materials.

Fibrolytic bacterial isolates obtained from camel rumen liquor exhibited pronounced carbohydrate-degrading potential, highlighting their enzymatic capacity to hydrolyze complex macromolecules such as starch, cellulose, and xylan. The observed clearing zones on solid media confirmed active extracellular hydrolytic enzyme secretion, primarily cellulases and glycosidases, consistent with the functional adaptation of rumen microbiota to lignocellulosic feed digestion [46].

Biochemical tests revealed that it was catalase-positive, oxidase-negative, motile, and urease-positive, consistent with the characteristics of facultative anaerobic fiber-degrading bacteria. Besides a diverse sugar fermentation pattern, which included glucose, maltose, sucrose, arabinose, xylose, and rhamnose, the isolate exhibited β-galactosidase and gelatinase activities, further showing remarkable saccharolytic efficiency and metabolic diversity [46, 47].

These findings were supported by the Biolog GEN III metabolic test results, indicating favorable utilization of various carbohydrate types, including mono-, di-, and polysaccharides, as well as aminosugars such as N-acetyl-D-glucosamine and N-acetyl-D-mannosamine. The use of D-mannitol and L-serine indicates an increased capacity to metabolize polyols and amino acids. The organism is suitable for industrial enzyme applications due to its resistance to 1% NaCl, 1% sodium lactate, and a low pH of 6, indicating the organism to be resilient at a variety of physicochemical conditions [46].

The fact that Minocycline and Sodium Bromate did not elicit any reaction might suggest intrinsic resistance and/or detoxification mechanisms that increase the strain’s stability under bioprocess conditions. The metabolic and phenotypic studies were conducted to assess the high potential of the camel rumen isolate for breaking down lignocellulosic biomass and synthesizing enzymes. According to the fundamental ideas of sustainable bioprocessing, its enzymatic system can be used for the transformation of agricultural residues into value-added biomacromolecules, biofuels, or biopolymers [47, 48].

The taxonomic identification of P. barengoltzii was confirmed using both sequence similarity and genome-scale comparative analyses for high-resolution classification. BLAST-based similarity (96–99.61%) suggests close phylogenetic relatedness but lacks precision for species-level discrimination [42–44]. Therefore, genome-wide metrics such as ANI (via FastANI and JSpeciesWS) and GTDB-Tk were used for robust confirmation [45]. Variations observed in reference-based assembly likely indicate strain-specific genomic diversity, highlighting the importance of multi-method approaches for accurate taxonomy [47–49].

The various CAZyme families, such as GH3, GH2, GH39, GH4, GH5, GH8, GH94, GH130, GH151, CBM32, CBM9, CE9, and CE32, can degrade lignocellulose, making them suitable for industrial applications. In this study, AA10 family enzymes, identified as lytic xylan monooxygenases, were found to target and cleave glycosidic bonds within xylan, a key hemicellulosic polysaccharide, thereby facilitating cellulose degradation [49–51] Moreover, the CAZyme families like GH19, GH18, GH46, GH23, CBM50, and CBM5 cleave the chitin with the help of Chitosanases, which form soluble oligomers of β-1,4-linked N-acetyl-D-glucosamine [52]. The CAZyme families (GH5, GH10, GH152, CBM46) are linked to the production of enzymes like β-glucosidases, β-galactosidases, β-xylosidases, endo-β-1,4-xylanases, hemicellulose-binding domains, and acetyl xylan esterases by Paenibacillus barengoltzii. These enzymes are essential for the breakdown of hemicellulose [53]. A CAZyme research study identified that P. barengoltzii harbors a variety of key carbohydrate-degrading enzymes, which have tremendous applications in biofuel production, plant biomass degradation, and the bioremediation of agricultural and industrial waste [7, 52–54].

Comparative genomics has shown that P. barengoltzii contains more genes associated with the degradation of hemicellulose, cellulose, and starch than 11 other Paenibacillus species known for breaking down plant biomass. Notably, it features families such as GH13, GH129, GH128, GH127, and GH125, along with sub-families like GH13_9, GH13_36, GH13_31, and GH13_30 that are linked to α-amylase function. This indicates its significant genomic capacity to decompose plant biomass. Consequently, these insights position P. barengoltzii as a strong candidate for degrading lignocellulosic materials. Additionally, this research suggests that rumen microbiomes could be an important yet underutilized source of bioproducts with potential pharmaceutical benefits [54].

The IPR domains IPR000743 and IPR003766, which correspond to the enzymes Polygalacturonase and Uronate Isomerase, respectively, are associated with the CAZyme family GH28. These enzymes were used for pectin breakdown [6]. The presence of key aromatic residues suggested dual activity, supporting previous findings on AA10 LPMOs. The comparison with other species further confirmed its broader substrate range and its role in polysaccharide degradation within microbial ecosystems. An analysis using InterProScan identified significant carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) in the microbial genome, underscoring the genome’s capacity to degrade complex polysaccharides [7]. InterProScan analysis revealed key carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) in the microbial genome, highlighting its ability to degrade complex polysaccharides. Domains such as LPMO_AA10, GH43, GH94, and GH18 are implicated in biomass degradation, sugar interconversion, and glycan processing [7]. Protein-protein interaction analysis using STRING identified a coordinated metabolic network, with enzymes such as chitinases, xylanases, and arabinofuranosidases supporting lignocellulose breakdown and pentose metabolism. The strong functional linkages between these enzymes, along with proteins involved in glycolysis and protein synthesis, position this organism as a promising candidate for biofuel and industrial applications COG distribution indicates critical microbial processes in the rumen, such as carbohydrate metabolism (10.62%), which converts polysaccharides into energy-rich VFAs, and amino acid metabolism (5.34%), which produces microbial protein to fulfil the host’s nutritional requirements. Furthermore, transcriptional control (6.0%) and signal transduction (1.67%) enable bacteria to adapt to the changing ruminal environment, thereby promoting efficient nutrient use and ecological stability [6, 7].

Conclusion

Overall, whole genome sequencing was performed to characterize the functional attributes of P. barengoltzii. This study is the first to investigate P. barengoltzii, identifying a range of Carbohydrate-Active enzymes (CAZymes) with specialized roles in the breakdown of hemicellulose, cellulose, lignin, pectin, chitin, and other polysaccharides. A few important genes, mainly GH88, GH105, GH27, and GH20, were identified as playing key roles in the degradation of plant biomass, particularly cellulosic and hemicellulosic substances. Further functional analysis identified approximately 121 important domains, including 12 newly identified IPR domains, primarily related to plant biomass breakdown. Additionally, comparative genomic analysis using CAZyme profiling revealed substantial diversity across species of the genus Paenibacillus in their ability to digest cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, and other polysaccharides. Hence, the results from the study implied that P. barengoltzii exhibits several features that make it a promising candidate for lignocellulosic and cellulosic biomass degradation. This research provides the possible applications of P. barengoltzii in the camel’s digestion, feed, and health.
