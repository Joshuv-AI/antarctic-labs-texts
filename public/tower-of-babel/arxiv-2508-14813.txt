Pricing Options on Forwards in Function-Valued Affine Stochastic Volatility Models Thanks: Asma Khedher is grateful for the financial support by the Research Foundation Flanders (FWO) under the grant FWO WOG W001021N


Abstract
. We study the pricing of European-style options written on forward contracts within function-valued infinite-dimensional affine stochastic volatility models. The dynamics of the underlying forward price curves are modeled within the Heath-Jarrow-Morton-Musiela framework as solution to a stochastic partial differential equation modulated by a stochastic volatility process. We analyze two classes of affine stochastic volatility models: (i) a Gaussian model governed by a finite-rank Wishart process, and (ii) a pure-jump affine model extending the Barndorff–-Nielsen–Shephard framework with state-dependent jumps in the covariance component. For both models, we derive conditions for the existence of exponential moments and develop semi-closed Fourier-based pricing formulas for vanilla call and put options written on forward price curves. Our approach allows for tractable pricing in models with infinitely many risk factors, thereby capturing maturity-specific and term structure risk essential in forward markets. Keywords: option pricing, stochastic volatility models, affine processes, forward curve dynamics, commodity derivatives, Wishart processes, Fourier methods, HJM framework, exponential moments.

1.
Introduction

We consider a tractable framework for modeling forward price and forward rate curves in commodity and fixed-income markets, and develop semi-closed pricing formulas for the valuation of European vanilla options written on (potentially) the entire forward curve. Following the Heath–Jarrow–Morton–Musiela (HJMM) paradigm, originally proposed for interest-rate markets by
[
29
]
and later transferred to commodity markets by
[
11
,
3
]
, we describe forward prices directly in time-to-maturity coordinates. In this representation, the forward curve evolves according to a hyperbolic stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) driven by a cylindrical Brownian motion whose covariance is itself modeled by a stochastic process that we call the
instantaneous covariance process
. We investigate two
affine
specifications of the covariance
process: (i) An extension of the finite-rank Wishart process to Hilbert–Schmidt operators introduced in
[
14
]
and (ii) a state-dependent jump extension of the operator-valued Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process introduced in
[
16
]
. As both models are from the affine class, their associated Fourier-Laplace transforms admit semi-explicit expressions in terms of the solutions to a set of
generalized Riccati equations
, which allows for semi-closed pricing formulas for vanilla call and put options. The derivation of these pricing formulas, together with rigorous conditions for the existence of exponential moments and a numerical analysis, constitut the principal contribution of this article.

1.1.
The HJMM framework

For any time
t\geq 0
and maturity date
T\geq t
, the forward price
F(t,T)
represents the agreed-upon price at time
t
for a transaction occurring at the future date
T
. Utilizing the Musiela parametrization
x\equiv T-t
, forward prices become functions of time-to-maturity. We define the logarithmic forward prices as
f_{t}(x)\equiv\ln(F(t,t+x))
, where
x,t\geq 0
, and call the mapping
x\mapsto f_{t}(x)
the (logarithmic)
forward price curve
at time
t\geq 0
.

The forward curve dynamics are described by the stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE):

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}f_{t}(x)&=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{t}(x)+g_{t}(x)\right)\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{t}^{(i)}(x)\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}W_{t}^{(i)},\\
f_{0}(x)&=F(0,x),\end{cases}

where
(W^{(i)}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
,
{i=1},\ldots,{d}
, are independent Brownian motions. The drift term
g_{t}(x)
satisfies a
HJM no-arbitrage condition
(see
[
9
, Sec. 2.4]
and Section
3
below), ensuring that each forward price process
(F(t,T))_{0\leq t\leq T}
is a martingale under some risk-neutral measure
\mathbb{Q}
.

The functions
\{\sigma_{t}^{(i)}(x))_{0\leq t\leq T}
,
{i=1},\ldots,{d}
, are referred to as the
instantaneous volatilities
of the forward curve. They characterize the local covariance structure between forward prices of different maturities. The integer
d\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{+\infty\}
is called the
rank of the noise
and corresponds to the number of independent risk factors driving the forward curves. If
d<\infty
, we refer to (
1.1
) as a
finite-rank HJM
model; otherwise, it is an
infinite-rank HJM
model.

1.2.
Stochastic covariance models for forward curve dynamics

The diffusion term in (
1.1
) can be interpreted in a more abstract form as the stochastic integral of an operator-valued process
(\sigma_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
acting on the increments of a cylindrical Brownian motion
(\mathrm{d}W_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
. We understand
(\sigma_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
as the stochastic volatility operator, encoding the time-varying covariance structure of the forward curve dynamics. The functions
\sigma_{t}^{(i)}
can therefore be interpreted as the principal components of
\sigma_{t}
, capturing the volatility structure along different directions in the underlying space of forward curves. The joint evolution of the forward curve and its stochastic volatility, represented by the process
(f_{t},\sigma_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
, thus defines an
infinite-dimensional stochastic volatility model
for forward curve dynamics.

The motivation for adopting a stochastic volatility framework stems from empirical evidence in commodity markets: volatility is time-varying, exhibits clustering, and is prone to abrupt spikes; see, e.g.,
[
24
,
6
]
. Classical models with constant volatility cannot capture these phenomena, whereas stochastic volatility models allow for a more realistic and flexible description of market behavior. Furthermore, to accurately capture volatility smiles in commodity option markets, incorporating stochastic volatility is essential. In contrast to approaches that perform a priori dimension reduction of the covariance, we do not restrict the rank of the volatility operator based on principal component analysis (PCA) or other variance-based criteria. Instead, we adopt a flexible, full-rank modeling approach, retaining all potential sources of risk. This is motivated by the observation that discarding components solely on the basis of explained variance can result in suboptimal choices for higher-order, non-linear functionals, such as those relevant for pricing, hedging, or risk management
[
12
]
. Note that even when the true rank is only moderately high, our methodology first emphasizes maximal expressiveness and generality. Finite-rank approximations are introduced a posteriori, and we study convergence of option prices as the rank increases. Empirical studies support this design choice: for instance,
[
1
]
and
[
34
]
demonstrate that power forward markets exhibit substantial idiosyncratic risk, and that high-dimensional noise is needed to capture the observed variability. In particular, the latter shows that even in PCA, around 10 factors are typically required to explain the majority of the variance, underlining the importance of accommodating high-dimensional dynamics from the outset. Here our function-valued formulation helps, as it is essentially non-parametric, relying on the full initial forward curve and the full covariance operator as model inputs. The covariance structure is encoded via a positive kernel operator, which can capture empirical features with a minimal set of hyperparameters and enables direct calibration to market data, including option prices and historical term structure dynamics.

The stochastic volatility models considered in this paper belong to the class of
affine processes
, which play a central role in modern financial modeling due to their analytical tractability. Affine processes satisfy the so-called
affine transform formula
, which expresses the cumulant generating function as an affine function of the initial state and the solution of a system of generalized Riccati ordinary differential equations (ODEs). A key advantage of this framework is the quasi-explicit availability of the Fourier–Laplace transform, which enables efficient and accurate pricing of options and other derivatives via Fourier inversion methods; see
[
10
,
22
,
23
]
. The extension of affine modeling techniques to infinite-dimensional settings has been the subject of extensive research; see, e.g.,
[
37
,
19
,
5
,
8
,
30
,
31
,
28
,
17
,
2
]
. In parallel, robust statistical methods have been developed for the estimation and analysis of stochastic volatility and covariation in term structure models. These approaches address both the functional nature of the data and the dynamic consistency required for sound modeling; see
[
39
,
38
]
.

1.3.
Contribution

In this paper, we contribute to this growing literature by developing a general pricing framework for European options written on forward contracts under function-valued affine stochastic volatility. We formulate the forward curve dynamics within the HJMM framework and model the instantaneous covariance as an operator-valued affine process. Two concrete model classes are considered: a Gaussian specification driven by an infinite-dimensional Wishart process, and a pure-jump model based on an extension of an operator-valued Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process to accommodate state-dependent jumps. The inclusion of jumps in the volatility process enhances the model’s ability to reflect sudden market shocks, which commonly arise from supply disruptions, geopolitical tensions, or abrupt changes in demand; see, e.g.
[
35
]
. In both settings, we rigorously extend the affine transform formula to a complex strip in an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space and establish conditions under which exponential moments exist in real and complex domains. In particular, we characterize the maximal domain of the moment generating function using generalized Riccati equations in Banach spaces, building on the analytic machinery introduced in
[
36
]
, and extending prior finite-dimensional results such as those in
[
33
]
.

Based on these results, we derive semi-closed-form pricing formulas for vanilla call and put options, which enable efficient numerical evaluation via Fourier inversion. We validate the accuracy and robustness of this pricing methodology through a comparison with Monte Carlo simulation. The numerical results show that both the moment-generating function (MGF) and option prices derived via the affine transform are consistent with those obtained from Monte Carlo simulations, see Figure
1
,
2
and
3
below. This comparison not only validates the accuracy of our approach but also illustrates its computational advantages. For example, in the case of the infinite-dimensional Wishart-type volatility model, direct simulation is challenging due to the absence of a closed-form expression for the square root of the operator-valued volatility process. Approximating this square root requires advanced numerical techniques, making Monte Carlo simulation computationally intensive. Similarly, in the case of the infinite-dimensional pure-jump affine volatility model, Monte Carlo methods demand significant computational resources, both in memory and runtime, due to the complexity of the jump structure. By contrast, the affine structure allows for efficient computation of key quantities such as the MGF and derivative prices without resorting to discretization of the infinite-dimensional dynamics. This leads to considerable gains in computational speed and accuracy. These advantages make affine stochastic volatility models particularly suitable for practical applications. In particular, they facilitate the calibration to implied volatility surfaces and option price curves observed in fixed-income or commodity markets, which is a notoriously delicate task. Our results demonstrate that, despite the infinite-dimensional nature of the models, their tractability and analytical structure make them powerful tools for capturing the rich dynamics in forward and futures markets.

1.4.
Layout of the article

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the modeling framework for forward curve dynamics using the HJMM approach and define the class of infinite-dimensional stochastic volatility models under consideration. Sections
2.1
and
2.2
present two concrete affine specifications for the volatility process: a pure-jump model based on an extension of an operator-valued Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, and a Gaussian model driven by an infinite-dimensional Wishart process. In Section

, we provide semi-closed-form pricing formulas for European call and put options on forwards, obtained via Fourier inversion. Section

Infinite-Dimensional Affine Stochastic Volatility Models

2.2.4.
Approximation of the Laplace transform of the infinite-dimensional Wishart stochastic volatility model

Our primary focus in this paper is the numerical implementation of option pricing in a setting where the forward price or rate follows an infinite-dimensional stochastic differential equation. To enable computations, we approximate the model by finite-dimensional systems.

For the Wishart stochastic volatility model, we do not perform a detailed convergence analysis of these approximations. Such an analysis, while important for theoretical completeness, would introduce significant technical complexity and is therefore left for future work.
Our objective here is to demonstrate the practical feasibility and computational tractability of the method. For comparison, the case in which the covariance process is driven by jumps has been analyzed in
[
30
,
31
]
.

To keep the presentation concise and highlight the numerical methodology, we approximate the Laplace transform of the infinite-dimensional Wishart stochastic volatility model directly.
Let
(X_{t},\mathcal{Y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}

3.
Pricing options on forwards in infinite-dimensional stochastic volatility models

We begin with a brief introduction to the modeling of commodity forward
curves in the Heath-Jarrow-Morton framework, here we closely follow

[

(X_{t},\mathcal{Y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}

(X_{t},{\sf Y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}

Suppose

is a non-decreasing function such that
w(0)=1

\int_{0}^{\infty}w^{-1}(x)\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}x<\infty

H_{w}
be the space of
absolutely continuous functions

such that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{w}^{2}:=f(0)^{2}+\int_{0}^{\infty}w(x)|f^{\prime}(x)|^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}x<\infty\,,

where
f^{\prime}
is the weak derivative of
f

H_{w}
is a separable Hilbert space when endowed with the inner product

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{w}:=f(0)g(0)+\int_{0}^{\infty}w(x)f^{\prime}(x)g^{\prime}(x)\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}x.

This space is proposed in
[
25
]
as a class of Hilbert spaces that match the economic reasoning about the forward rate curve in fixed income markets (see also
[
4
]
for the case of commodity forward markets).

The left-shift semigroup is a strongly continuous semigroup on
H_{w}
with infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{A}
being the operator of differentiation (in time-to-maturity coordinate).
Moreover, the point evaluation
\delta_{x}(h)=u_{x}
is a continuous linear functional on
H_{w}
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
It can be expressed as (see
[
25
, Lemma 5.3.1]
)

(3.1)

\displaystyle\delta_{x}=\langle\cdot,u_{x}\rangle_{w}\,,

where for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
u_{x}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
y\mapsto 1+\int_{0}^{x\wedge y}w^{-1}(z)\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}z
.

Let
F(t,\tau)
be the forward price at time
t>0
for delivery at
\tau>t
.
The log–forward in the Musiela parametrisation (time to maturity
x=\tau-t
) is

f_{t}(x):=\ln F(t,t+x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Fix a weight function
w
and assume the state process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}X_{t}=\big(\mathcal{A}X_{t}+C_{t}\big)\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t+D^{1/2}Y_{t}^{1/2}\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}W_{t},\qquad X_{0}=x\in H_{w},

where
\mathcal{A}=\partial_{x}
is the derivative (shift) operator,
C
is an integrable adapted
H_{w}
–valued process,

D\in\mathcal{L}(H_{w})
is self-adjoint and positive,
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a cylindrical Brownian motion, and
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}

is either

from (

) or
({\sf Y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
from (
2.2
).
On a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T},\mathbb{P})
, we model the (arbitrage-free) forward curve via

(3.3)

F(t,t+x):=\exp\{\delta_{x}(X_{t})\}=\exp\{\langle X_{t},u_{x}\rangle_{w}\},\qquad 0\leq t\leq T,

where

is the Riesz representer of

(see (

is an
H_{w}
–valued adapted process such that

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\!\Big\{-\int_{0}^{T}\langle\xi_{s},\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}W_{s}\rangle_{w}-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\|\xi_{s}\|_{w}^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s\Big\}\right]=1.

By Girsanov’s theorem for cylindrical Brownian motion (e.g.,
[

),

In this section we study several specifications of the coupled process

4.1.
General settings

In the implementation, we choose as a weight function

4.2.
Validation of the MGF in the Infinite-Dimensional Wishart Stochastic Volatility Model

For the infinite-dimensional Wishart stochastic volatility model, we first validate the theoretical moment generating function (MGF) against its empirical counterpart obtained by Monte Carlo. This is the right diagnostic because, in light of (
2.2.4
), option prices are computed from a fixed finite-rank approximation; consistency of the MGF is therefore the primary check for pricing accuracy.

Throughout this experiment we work in a finite-rank setting: the Wishart process takes values in the cone of self-adjoint trace-class operators of rank at most
n
. We fix an arbitrary but finite
n\in\mathbb{N}
and an orthonormal basis
(f_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
H
as introduced earlier, and project all operators onto
H_{n}:=\mathrm{span}\{f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\}
. The theoretical MGF is evaluated via the affine transform/Riccati solution on
H_{n}
, while the empirical MGF is computed from Monte Carlo samples as the sample average of
\exp\{\langle X_{T},u_{1}\rangle-\mathrm{Tr}(Y_{T}u_{2})\}
for the same
(u_{1},u_{2})
.

We define the initial value
Y_{0}
of the Wishart process as a rank-
n
positive operator given by

Y_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}f_{k}\otimes f_{k},

where
\lambda_{k}>0
are constant eigenvalues chosen for numerical stability and interpretation. The volatility-driving Wishart process
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is governed by the SDE

\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}Y_{t}=nQ\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t+Y_{t}\mathbb{A}\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t+\mathbb{A}^{*}Y_{t}\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t+\sqrt{Y_{t}}\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}B_{t}\sqrt{Q}+\sqrt{Q}\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}B_{t}^{*}\sqrt{Y_{t}},

where
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a cylindrical Brownian motion on
L^{2}(H)
, and
Q\in\mathbb{S}_{1}^{+}(H)
and
\mathbb{A}\in\mathcal{L}(H)
are model parameters. We specify the operator
Q
as

Q=\sum_{k=1}^{n}q_{k}f_{k}\otimes f_{k},

with
q_{k}>0
to control the volatility of the Wishart process along each principal direction
f_{k}
. The drift operator
\mathbb{A}
is chosen to be diagonal in the basis
(f_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, such that

\mathbb{A}=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}f_{k}\otimes f_{k},

with
a_{k}>0
ensuring mean reversion of the covariance process. In this example, we set the
q_{k}=a_{k}=\frac{1}{k^{2}}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We use
D\in\mathbb{S}_{1}^{+}(H)
for the volatility loading, defined as

D=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{2}}f_{k}\otimes f_{k}.

The Wishart process is coupled withththth the forward dynamics via the volatility-modulated SPDE given in equation (
1.1
).

A solution to the Riccati equations (
2.15a
)-(
2.15c
), with

and
R

2.11
) and (
2.12
), is not available in closed form.
In Appendix
B
, we present an efficient numerical scheme for solving these equations under the given parameterization.
The method is flexible and can be adapted to alternative parameter choices.

Monte Carlo simulation of the Wishart model is considerably more demanding.
The main difficulty lies in computing the square root of the operator-valued volatility process
Y_{t}
at each time step, this is analytically intractable and must be approximated numerically.
To limit computational overhead, we simulate only a single time step corresponding to a short horizon of
T_{0}=1/365
. However, at such a small horizon
T_{0}
, the inverse Fourier transform used for option pricing becomes numerically unstable.
Therefore, instead of the option prices, we compare the moment generating function (MGF), which is the key intermediate quantity in affine models.

In this example, we find that the MGF computed from the affine representation aligns closely with the MGF estimated via Monte Carlo simulation, thereby confirming pricing consistency.
This agreement is illustrated in Figure

4.4.
Pricing in the operator-valued Barndorff–Nielsen–Shephard stochastic volatility model

Let
\mathcal{A}
,
D
,
(\widetilde{W}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, and
(J_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be as in Example
4.3
, with

J_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}D
a compound Poisson process of intensity
\beta>0
, independent of
\widetilde{W}
.
Let
\mathcal{H}_{w}
be the space of self-adjoint Hilbert–Schmidt operators on
H_{w}
and
\mathcal{H}_{w}^{+}
its positive cone. We consider the operator-valued BNS dynamics

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}X_{t}

\displaystyle=\big(\mathcal{A}X_{t}+D^{1/2}\mathsf{Y}_{t}D^{1/2}\Upsilon^{\vartheta}\big)\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t+D^{1/2}\mathsf{Y}_{t}^{1/2}\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}\widetilde{W}_{t},\qquad X_{0}=x\in H_{w},

(4.11)

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}\mathsf{Y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\big(Q\mathsf{Y}_{t}+\mathsf{Y}_{t}Q^{*}\big)\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}t+\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}J_{t},\qquad\mathsf{Y}_{0}=y\in\mathcal{H}_{w}^{+},

where
Q\in\mathcal{L}(H_{w})
is a (mean-reverting) drift operator (we will take it diagonal below) and

\Upsilon^{\vartheta}=-\tfrac{1}{2}S^{*}(\vartheta)h_{0}
as in (
3.7
). The associated complex Riccati system (
2.8a
)–(
2.8c
) becomes

\partial_{t}\phi(t,u)=F(\psi_{2}(t,u)),\qquad\psi_{1}(t,u)=u_{1}+\mathcal{A}^{*}\int_{0}^{t}\psi_{1}(s,u)\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s,\qquad\partial_{t}\psi_{2}(t,u)=R(\psi_{1}(t,u),\psi_{2}(t,u)),

with maps

R(h,u)=uQ^{*}+Qu-\tfrac{1}{2}\,(D^{1/2}h)^{\otimes 2}-D^{1/2}h\otimes D^{1/2}\Upsilon^{\vartheta},\qquad F(u)=\beta\big(\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{-\langle D,u\rangle}-1\big),

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the Hilbert–Schmidt pairing on
\mathcal{H}_{w}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
w(x)=e^{\alpha x}
with
\alpha>0
and
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the orthonormal basis from (
4.2
).
Assume the OU drift is diagonal,

Q\;=\;-\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}\,f_{n}\otimes f_{n},\qquad a_{n}\geq 0,

and set
D^{1/2}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{n\geq 1}\tfrac{1}{n}\,f_{n}\otimes f_{n}
,

c_{j}(\vartheta):=\langle f_{j},S^{*}(\vartheta)h_{0}\rangle_{w}
(so
D^{1/2}\Upsilon^{\vartheta}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{j\geq 1}\tfrac{1}{j}c_{j}(\vartheta)\,f_{j}
).
For
u_{1}=(\nu+i\lambda)u_{\vartheta}
with
\nu>1
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the complex Riccati solution to (
4.4
) satisfies

(4.12)

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(t,u)(x)

\displaystyle=(\nu+i\lambda)\!\left(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}\big(1-e^{-\alpha(x\wedge t)}\big)+\frac{1}{\alpha}e^{-\alpha t}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{x\geq t\}}\big(1-\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{-\alpha(\vartheta\wedge x-t)}\big)\right),

(4.13)

\displaystyle\psi_{2}(t,u)f_{n}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j\geq 1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{-(a_{n}+a_{j})(t-s)}\Big[\frac{(\nu+i\lambda)^{2}}{4\,nj}\,f_{n}(s+\vartheta)f_{j}(s+\vartheta)+\frac{(\nu+i\lambda)}{2\,nj}\,c_{j}(\vartheta)\,f_{n}(s+\vartheta)\Big]\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}sf_{j},

and

(4.14)

\displaystyle\phi(t,u)

\displaystyle=\beta t-\beta\int_{0}^{t}\exp\big(\frac{1}{8}(\nu+\mathrm{i}\lambda)^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{4}}e^{\frac{s}{n^{2}}}\int_{0}^{s}\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{-\frac{l}{n^{2}}}f_{n}(l+\vartheta)\left(f_{n}(l+\vartheta)+\frac{2c_{n}(\vartheta)}{\nu+\mathrm{i}\lambda}\right)\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}l\big)\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s.

In particular, we have

(4.15)

\langle D,\psi_{2}(t,u)\rangle=-\int_{0}^{t}\Big[\frac{(\nu+i\lambda)^{2}}{8}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n^{4}}f_{n}(s+\vartheta)^{2}+\frac{(\nu+i\lambda)}{4}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{c_{n}(\vartheta)}{n^{4}}f_{n}(s+\vartheta)\Big]\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{-2a_{n}(t-s)}\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s.

Proof.

Since
\psi_{1}
solves
\psi_{1}(t,u)=u_{1}+\mathcal{A}^{*}\int_{0}^{t}\psi_{1}(s,u)\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s
with
u_{1}=(\nu+i\lambda)u_{\vartheta}
,
we have the mild form
\psi_{1}(t,u)=S^{*}(t)u_{1}
, which yields (
4.12
) exactly as in Lemma
4.1
.

The
\psi_{2}
-equation reads

\partial_{t}\psi_{2}(t)=\underbrace{\psi_{2}(t)Q^{*}+Q\psi_{2}(t)}_{\mathcal{L}_{Q}(\psi_{2}(t))}\;-\;\frac{1}{2}\,(D^{1/2}\psi_{1}(t))^{\otimes 2}\;-\;D^{1/2}\psi_{1}(t)\otimes D^{1/2}\Upsilon^{\vartheta},\qquad\psi_{2}(0)=0.

Hence, by variation of constants,

(4.16)

\psi_{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{Q}}\left[\,-\frac{1}{2}(D^{1/2}\psi_{1}(s))^{\otimes 2}-D^{1/2}\psi_{1}(s)\otimes D^{1/2}\Upsilon^{\vartheta}\right]\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s.

Assume
Q=-\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}\,f_{n}\otimes f_{n}
(with
a_{n}\geq 0
). Then, for basis tensors,

\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{Q}}(f_{n}\otimes f_{j})=\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{-(a_{n}+a_{j})(t-s)}\,f_{n}\otimes f_{j}.

Moreover,

D^{1/2}\psi_{1}(s)=\frac{\nu+i\lambda}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}\,f_{n}(s+\vartheta)\,f_{n},\qquad D^{1/2}\Upsilon^{\vartheta}\;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{1}{j}\,c_{j}(\vartheta)\,f_{j},\quad c_{j}(\vartheta):=\langle f_{j},S^{*}(\vartheta)h_{0}\rangle_{w}.

Using
(a\otimes b)h=\langle a,h\rangle_{w}\,b
and (
4.16
), we obtain for each
n
:

\displaystyle\psi_{2}(t)f_{n}

\displaystyle=-\!\sum_{j\geq 1}\!\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(a_{n}+a_{j})(t-s)}\Big[\frac{(\nu+i\lambda)^{2}}{4\,nj}\,f_{n}(s+\vartheta)f_{j}(s+\vartheta)+\frac{(\nu+i\lambda)}{2\,nj}\,c_{j}(\vartheta)\,f_{n}(s+\vartheta)\Big]\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s\;f_{j},

which is precisely (
4.13
). Since
D=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\,f_{n}\otimes f_{n}
,

\langle D,\psi_{2}(t)\rangle=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\,\langle f_{n},\psi_{2}(t)f_{n}\rangle_{w}.

By orthonormality, only the
j=n
terms survive in
\langle f_{n},\psi_{2}(t)f_{n}\rangle_{w}
, giving

\langle f_{n},\psi_{2}(t)f_{n}\rangle_{w}=-\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2a_{n}(t-s)}\left[\frac{(\nu+i\lambda)^{2}}{4\,n^{2}}\,f_{n}(s+\vartheta)^{2}+\frac{(\nu+i\lambda)}{2\,n^{2}}\,c_{n}(\vartheta)\,f_{n}(s+\vartheta)\right]\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s,

from which we deduce formula (
4.15
).
Finally, since for the compound-Poisson input without finite-variation drift it holds that

\partial_{t}\phi(t,u)=F(\psi_{2}(t,u))=\beta\big(e^{-\langle D,\psi_{2}(t,u)\rangle}-1\big)
,
integrating over
[0,t]
yields (
4.14
).
∎

Therefore, the option price is

(4.17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[(F(t,t+\vartheta)-K)^{+}\mid\mathcal{F}_{0}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(\lambda)\,\exp\!\Big(-\phi(t,u)+\langle X_{0},\psi_{1}(t,u)\rangle_{w}-\langle Y_{0},\psi_{2}(t,u)\rangle\Big)\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(\lambda)\,\exp\!\Big(-\beta\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\big(\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{-\langle D,\psi_{2}(s,u)\rangle}-1\big)\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s\big)

\displaystyle\times\exp\!\Big((\nu+i\lambda)\,X_{0}(t+\vartheta)-\langle Y_{0},\psi_{2}(t,u)\rangle\Big)\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}\lambda,

where
u_{1}=(\nu+i\lambda)u_{\vartheta}
with
\nu>1
, and
\langle D,\psi_{2}(t,u)\rangle
is as in (
4.15
),
with
Q=-\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}\,f_{n}\otimes f_{n}
(
a_{n}\geq 0
) and
c_{n}(\vartheta):=\langle f_{n},S^{*}(\vartheta)h_{0}\rangle_{w}
.
The term
\langle X_{0},\psi_{1}(t,u)\rangle_{w}=(\nu+i\lambda)X_{0}(t+\vartheta)
comes from
\psi_{1}(t,u)=S^{*}(t)u_{1}
.

For the numerics, we truncate the series at
N
:

(4.18)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[(F(t,t+\vartheta)-K)^{+}\mid\mathcal{F}_{0}\big]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(\lambda)\,\exp\Big(-\beta\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\big(e^{-\langle D,\psi_{2}^{(N)}(s,u)\rangle}-1\big)\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s\Big)

\displaystyle\times\exp\Big((\nu+i\lambda)\,X_{0}(t+\vartheta)-\langle Y_{0},\psi_{2}^{(N)}(t,u)\rangle\Big)\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}\lambda,

with

\langle D,\psi_{2}^{(N)}(t,u)\rangle=-\int_{0}^{t}\Big[\frac{(\nu+i\lambda)^{2}}{8}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{n^{4}}f_{n}(s+\vartheta)^{2}+\frac{(\nu+i\lambda)}{4}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{c_{n}(\vartheta)}{n^{4}}f_{n}(s+\vartheta)\Big]\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{-2a_{n}(t-s)}\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s.

In our experiments, we specialize to
a_{n}=\frac{1}{2n^{2}}

.

5.
Computation of complex moments

In this section, we prove the existence of solutions to the complex-extensions of the generalized Riccati equations (
2.8a
)-(
2.8c
) and (
2.15a
)-(
2.15c
). These existence results are then used to compute exponential moments for the affine pure-jump stochastic volatility model and to derive an approximation for the Laplace transform of the Wishart stochastic volatility model. To this end, we introduce the concept of quasi-monotonicity.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
V
be a real Banach space,
K\subset V
a wedge. Given
D\subset V
and
f\colon D\to V
is said to be quasi monotone (with respect to
K
) if
\forall x,y\in D
satisfying

x\leq_{K}y
and
\forall x^{\prime}\in K^{*}
satisfying
\langle x^{\prime},x-y\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}=0
, it holds
\langle x^{\prime},f(x)-f(y)\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}\leq 0\,.

5.1.
Real and complex moments for the affine pure-jump stochastic volatility model

In the following proposition we show the existence of a unique solution to the
extended Riccati equations (
2.7a
)-(
2.7c
)
up to its maximal lifetime.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
(b,B,m,\mu)
be an admissible parameter set satisfying Assumptions
Assumption

A

and let
(\mathcal{A},\dom(\mathcal{A}))
be the
generator of a strongly continuous semigroup, let
D\in\mathcal{L}(H)
be a positive
self-adjoint operator, and let
\Upsilon\in H
. Then for every
u=(u_{1},u_{2})\in H\times\mathcal{O}
there
exist a positive real number
T_{\sf q_{2}}
and a unique solution
({\sf p}(\cdot,u),{\sf q}_{1}(\cdot,u),+{\sf q}_{2}(\cdot,u))
to (
2.7a
)-(
2.7c
)
on
[0,T_{\sf q_{2}})
.

Proof.

Standard semigroup theory ensures that for any
u_{1}\in H
and
T>0
the unique mild solution to
(
2.7b
) is given by
{\sf q}_{1}(t,u)=S^{*}(t)u_{1}
for
t\in[0,T]
. We define
\mathcal{R}_{u_{1}}(t,\cdot)\colon\mathcal{O}\to\mathcal{H}
, by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{u_{1}}(t,u)

\displaystyle=\tilde{B}^{*}(u)-\tfrac{1}{2}\big(D^{1/2}S^{*}(t)u_{1}\big)^{\otimes 2}-D^{1/2}S^{*}(t)u_{1}\otimes D^{1/2}\Upsilon

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\mathcal{H}^{+}\setminus\{0\}}\big(\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{-\langle\xi,u\rangle}-1\big)\frac{\mu(\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}\xi)}{\|\xi\|_{2}^{2}}.

Plugging
{\sf q}_{1}(t,u)
into (
2.7c
), we thus obtain the equation

(5.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial{\sf q}_{2}}{\partial t}(t,u)=\mathcal{R}_{u_{1}}(t,{\sf q}_{2}(t,u))\,,\\
{\sf q}_{2}(0,u)=u_{2}\,.\end{cases}

Observe that the function

\mathcal{R}_{u_{1}}(t,\cdot)
is locally Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{O}
for every

t\geq 0
and
u_{1}\in H
. Since
\mathcal{O}
is assumed to be open, it follows from
standard ODE results (see
e.g.
[
36
, Chapter 6, Proposition 1.2]
) that for every
u_{2}\in\mathcal{O}
there exist a
T_{\sf q_{2}}>0
and a unique solution

{\sf q}_{2}(\cdot,u)
of (
5.1
) on
[0,T_{{\sf q}_{2}})
with

\displaystyle T_{\sf q_{2}}=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left\{t\geq 0\colon\|{\sf q}_{2}(t,u)\|_{2}\geq n\text{ or }{\sf q}_{2}(t,u)\in\partial\mathcal{O}\right\}.

By inserting
\sf q_{2}(\cdot,u)
in (
2.7a
) and observing that

F
is continuous on
\mathcal{O}
, the statement follows.
∎

To compute exponential moments for the process
(X_{t},\mathsf{Y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in (
2.4
), we first consider
the joint process
(X^{(n)}_{t},\mathsf{Y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
obtained by replacing
\mathcal{A}
in (
2.4
) by its Yosida approximation
\mathcal{A}^{(n)}:=n\mathcal{A}(nI-\mathcal{A})^{-1}
. Namely,
we consider the process
X^{(n)}\colon[0,\infty)\times\Omega\rightarrow H
given by the solution to (see
[
21
, Proposition 6.4]
for the existence of the solution to
X^{(n)}
)

(5.2)

\displaystyle X^{(n)}_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}(\mathcal{A}^{(n)}X^{(n)}_{s}+D^{1/2}X^{(n)}_{s}D^{1/2}\Upsilon)\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}D^{1/2}X_{s}^{1/2}\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}W_{s}\,,\quad t\geq 0.

The use of the approximation allows us to exploit the semimartingale theory and to apply the Itô formula and standard techniques in order to compute exponential moments for

(X^{(n)}_{t},\mathsf{Y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. Then we take the limit when
n
goes to
\infty
. Observe that from
[
21
, Proposition 7.5]
it holds

(5.3)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|X^{(n)}_{t}-X_{t}\|^{2}_{H}\right]=0\,.

We are ready to derive the exponential moments of the affine pure-jump stochastic volatility model.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
(b,B,m,\mu)
be an admissible parameter set satisfying Assumptions
Assumption

A

and
Assumption

B

,
let
(X,Y)
be the stochastic volatility model (
2.4
),
let
u=(u_{1},u_{2})\in H\times\mathcal{O}
, and let
({\sf P}(\cdot,u),{\sf q}_{1}(\cdot),u),{\sf q}_{2}(\cdot,u))
be the mild solution to the Riccati equation (
2.7
) up to time

T
the existence of which is guaranteed by Proposition
5.2
.
Then we have:

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{\langle X_{T},u_{1}\rangle_{H}-\langle{\sf Y}_{T},u_{2}\rangle}\right]<\infty.

Moreover, for every
t\leq T
, it holds

(5.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{\langle X_{T},u_{1}\rangle_{H}-\langle{\sf Y}_{T},u_{2}\rangle}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{-{\sf P}(T-t,u)+\langle X_{t},{\sf q}_{1}(T-t,u)\rangle_{H}-\langle{\sf Y}_{t},{\sf q}_{2}(T-t,u)\rangle}.

Proof.

Let
({\sf P}^{(n)}(\cdot,u),{\sf q}_{1}^{(n)}(\cdot,u),{\sf q}_{2}^{(n)}(\cdot,u))
be the solution to (
2.7
) with
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}^{(n)}
(the
n
th Yosida approximation). We define the function
g_{u}^{(n)}(t,y,x)\colon[0,T]\times H\times\mathcal{H}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as follows

g_{u}^{(n)}(t,x,y)=\exp{\{-{\sf P}^{(n)}(T-t,u)+\langle x,{\sf q}^{(n)}_{1}(T-t,u)\rangle_{H}-\langle y,{\sf q}^{(n)}_{2}(T-t,u)\rangle\}}\,.

Similarly to the proof of
[
16
, Theorem 3.3]
, we apply the Itô’s formula to
g_{u}^{(n)}(t,X^{(n)}_{t},{\sf Y}_{t})
to deduce that it is a local martingale.

Then observing that
g_{u}^{(n)}(t,X_{t}^{(n)},{\sf Y}_{t})
is strictly positive for all
t\in[0,T]
, we infer it is a
supermartingale and we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{\langle X^{(n)}_{T},u\rangle_{H}-\langle{\sf Y}_{T},u\rangle}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[g_{u}^{(n)}(T,X_{T}^{(n)},{\sf Y}_{T})\right]

\displaystyle\leq g_{u}^{(n)}(0,x,y)<\infty\,,\quad(x,y)\in H\times\mathcal{H}\,,

from which it follows that
(g_{u}^{(n)}(t,X_{t}^{(n)},{\sf Y}_{t}))_{t\in[0,T]}
is a martingale and hence it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{\langle X_{T}^{(n)},u_{1}\rangle_{H}-\langle{\sf Y}_{T},u_{2}\rangle}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{}\!\textnormal{e}^{-{\sf P}^{(n)}(T-t,u)+\langle X_{t},{\sf q}_{1}^{(n)}(T-t,u)\rangle_{H}-\langle{\sf Y}_{t},{\sf q}_{2}^{(n)}(T-t,u)\rangle}.

Following similar arguments as in the proof of
[
16
, Proposition 3.5]
, we deduce that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|{\sf P}^{(n)}(t,u)-{\sf P}(t,u)|=0

and

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|{\sf q}_{1}^{(n)}(t,u)-{\sf q}_{1}(t,u)\|_{H}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|{\sf q}_{2}^{(n)}(t,u)-{\sf q}_{2}(t,u)\|_{2}\right)\,.

Hence taking limits for
n\rightarrow\infty
and invoking (
5.3
) yields the statement.
∎

Before proving the existence and uniqueness of a solution to (
2.8a
)-(
2.8c
), we give the following
lemma:

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
{\sf R}
be as in (
2.6
). Then there exists a locally Lipschitz continuous function
g
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
for all
h\in H^{\mathbb{C}}
and
u\in S(\mathcal{O})
we have

(5.6)

\displaystyle\Re(\langle\overline{u},{\sf R}(h,u)\rangle)\leq g(\Re(u))(1+\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{C}}}^{2})(1+\|h\|_{H^{\mathbb{C}}}),

Proof.

We first split
\Re(\langle\overline{u},{\sf R}(u,h)\rangle)
into three parts as follows:

(5.7)

\displaystyle\Re(\langle\overline{u},R(h,u)\rangle)

\displaystyle=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}\,,

where

Appendix A
Laguerre polynomials and evaluation of the basis function
f_{k}

When computing the option prices, one has to calculate the integrals w.r.t. the basis function
f_{k},k=1,\ldots,n
, see for instance, equations (
4.9
) and (
4.17
). This requires the evaluations of the basis function
f_{k}
,
k\geq 2
which themselves are integrals of Laguerre polynomials with weight function
e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)}
, see equation (
4.2
). We start by introducing the Laguerre polynomials (see, for example,
[
27
, Chapter V]
for a good introduction to Laguerre polynomials).

The
Laguerre polynomials

L_{n}(x)
are defined by

L_{n}(x)=\frac{e^{x}}{n!}\frac{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}^{n}}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}x^{n}}\left(x^{n}e^{-x}\right)\,,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

These polynomials are orthogonal with respect to the weight function
e^{-x}
on
[0,\infty)
, i.e.,

\int_{0}^{\infty}L_{n}(x)L_{m}(x)e^{-x}\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}x=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }n\neq m,\\
1&\text{if orthonormalized},\\
\frac{1}{n!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[L_{n}(x)\right]^{2}e^{-x}\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}x=\frac{1}{n!}&\text{(optional scaling)}.\end{cases}

They fulfill the so called
three-term recurrence relation

(A.1)

L_{n+1}(x)=\frac{(2n+1-x)L_{n}(x)-nL_{n-1}(x)}{n+1}\,,\qquad n\geq 2

with initial conditions

L_{0}(x)=1,\quad L_{1}(x)=1-x\,.

The derivative of the standard Laguerre polynomial
L_{n}(x)
satisfies the identity

(A.2)

x\frac{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}}{\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}x}L_{n}(x)=nL_{n}(x)-nL_{n-1}(x)\,.

We make use of these formulas for the Laguerre polynomials to present a fast evaluation approach for
f_{k}
,
k\geq 1
by iterations.
From (
A.1
), it follows that, for
k\geq 1

\displaystyle f_{k+3}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}L_{k+1}(s)e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)s}\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{2k+1}{k+1}\int_{0}^{x}L_{k}(s)e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)s}\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s-\frac{k}{k+1}\int_{0}^{x}L_{k-1}(s)e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)s}\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{k+1}\int_{0}^{x}sL_{k}(s)e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)s}\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{2k+1}{k+1}f_{k+2}(x)-\frac{k}{k+1}f_{k+1}-\frac{1}{k+1}\int_{0}^{x}sL_{k}(s)e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)s}\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s.

Using (
A.2
), we infer

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}sL_{k}(s)e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)s}\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\alpha+1}sL_{k}(s)e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)s}|_{0}^{x}+\frac{2}{\alpha+1}\int_{0}^{x}[L_{k}(s)+sL_{k}^{\prime}]e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)s}\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\alpha+1}xL_{k}(x)e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)x}+\frac{2}{\alpha+1}f_{k+2}+\frac{2}{\alpha+1}\int_{0}^{x}sL_{k}^{\prime}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)s}\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\alpha+1}xL_{k}(x)e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)x}+\frac{2}{\alpha+1}f_{k+2}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{\alpha+1}\int_{0}^{x}[kL_{k}(s)-kL_{k-1}(s)]e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)s}\mathop{}\!\textnormal{d}s

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\alpha+1}xL_{k}(x)e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)x}+\frac{2}{\alpha+1}(k+1)f_{k+2}-\frac{2}{\alpha+1}kf_{k+1}\,.

Finally, we have the iteration for the basis functions
f_{k},k\geq 1

\displaystyle f_{k+3}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{2k+1}{k+1}f_{k+2}(x)-\frac{k}{k+1}f_{k+1}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{k+1}\frac{2}{\alpha+1}\left[xL_{k}(x)e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+1)x}-(k+1)f_{k+2}+kf_{k+1}\right]\,.
