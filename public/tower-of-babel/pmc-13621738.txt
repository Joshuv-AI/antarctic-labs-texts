Multinational pharmacovigilance assessment of drug-associated esophageal ulceration and perforation: evidence from FAERS, JADER, and Canada Vigilance
Authors: Lihua Zhang, Xinchi Luan, Caiqi Liu, Xiaofei Xin, Xiaolei Zhang, Shufen Zhao, Wensheng Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrug-associated esophageal ulceration and perforation are uncommon but clinically serious adverse events. This study characterized post-marketing reporting patterns for these events across three spontaneous reporting systems.MethodsReports from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), Japanese Adverse Drug Event Report database (JADER), and Canada Vigilance from 2004 to 2025 were analyzed using predefined Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) preferred terms. Disproportionality was assessed using the reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN), and multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS). Reported time-to-onset was summarized for drug-report records with valid therapy start and event onset dates.ResultsFAERS contained 8,061 reports meeting the prespecified composite case definition. Reported time-to-onset was heterogeneous, with both short and long recorded intervals.ConclusionsThis multidatabase analysis identified prominent reporting signals for established pill-injury drugs and exploratory reporting patterns involving other drug categories. Introduction

Drug-associated esophageal injury is an uncommon but clinically meaningful medication-safety problem. Its clinical spectrum ranges from transient esophagitis to ulceration, hemorrhage, stricture, rupture, and, in severe cases, esophageal perforation. Symptoms are often nonspecific, including retrosternal pain, odynophagia, dysphagia, heartburn-like symptoms, upper gastrointestinal bleeding, or acute chest discomfort. Because these presentations overlap with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), infectious esophagitis, malignancy-related symptoms, and treatment-related mucosal injury, drug-associated esophageal injury may be under-recognized in routine practice. This is particularly relevant for older adults, patients with dysphagia or impaired mobility, patients receiving multiple medications, and patients with cancer or organ transplantation who often have complex therapeutic exposures. Although many pill-related injuries are preventable through administration counseling, serious ulceration, hemorrhage, rupture, and perforation continue to be reported, making post-marketing signal detection clinically relevant [1–3].

Classic drug-induced esophageal injury is usually attributed to prolonged contact between a tablet or capsule and the esophageal mucosa. Contributing factors include insufficient water intake, recumbency shortly after administration, large pill size, caustic drug formulation, pre-existing esophageal motility disorder, dysphagia, advanced age, and structural esophageal disease [1–3]. Several drug classes have long-standing biological plausibility for esophageal mucosal injury. Bisphosphonates, especially alendronate, have been associated with characteristic endoscopic and pathological esophageal injury, particularly when administration instructions are not followed [4]. Doxycycline remains one of the best-recognized causes of pill-induced esophagitis and ulceration, with reports spanning classic case series, histopathologic descriptions, and recent case literature [5–9]. Clindamycin-induced esophageal injury may also be underdiagnosed because its symptoms can resemble reflux disease or infectious esophagitis [10]. Dabigatran represents a distinct example because its tartaric acid-containing capsule may contribute to local acidic mucosal injury, while systemic anticoagulation may worsen bleeding or delay mucosal recovery [11, 12].

Contemporary prescribing patterns have broadened the clinical context of esophageal ulceration and perforation beyond classic pill retention [13]. Antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) may not always act as direct caustic agents in the esophagus, but they may amplify bleeding, impair mucosal defense, or delay recovery once mucosal injury has occurred [14–16]. In immunosuppressed patients, esophageal ulceration may reflect drug-related mucosal injury, impaired mucosal repair, or opportunistic infection rather than a single direct toxic mechanism [17, 18]. Mycophenolate-associated gastrointestinal mucosal injury has been described histologically [19]. Opportunistic infections such as cytomegalovirus can involve ulcerative esophageal disease in immunocompromised hosts [17, 18, 20]. In oncology practice, cytotoxic chemotherapy, targeted therapy, antiangiogenic therapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors may coincide with mucosal inflammation, impaired tissue repair, infection susceptibility, tumor-related factors, or intensive diagnostic surveillance. Evidence on immune checkpoint inhibitor-related esophagitis supports clinical awareness but cannot be extrapolated directly to disproportionality estimates without clinical validation [21].

Spontaneous reporting systems provide an important opportunity to detect rare or unexpected adverse drug event signals after marketing approval. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), Japanese Adverse Drug Event Report database (JADER), and Canada Vigilance collect reports from healthcare professionals, consumers, manufacturers, and regulatory sources. These systems are useful for identifying potential safety concerns involving rare but serious outcomes that are difficult to characterize in pre-approval trials. However, they are affected by underreporting, duplicate reporting, stimulated reporting, incomplete clinical information, selective reporting, regional differences in drug utilization, and the absence of exposure denominators [22, 23]. Disproportionality analyses therefore cannot estimate incidence, absolute risk, or causality. These limitations are particularly relevant for esophageal injury, where a drug may be reported because it treated pre-existing or prodromal symptoms. For example, proton pump inhibitor (PPI) signals may reflect GERD, erosive esophagitis, ulcer symptoms, or gastroprotection in patients receiving NSAIDs or antithrombotic agents, rather than direct PPI toxicity [14–16, 24, 25].

Previous evidence has clarified the clinical presentation, endoscopic features, mechanisms, and management of drug-induced esophageal injury, but the literature remains dominated by clinical reviews, case reports, and single-system pharmacovigilance designs [22]. Several gaps remain. First, evidence from different spontaneous reporting systems has rarely been considered together, making it difficult to understand whether signals are observed across different reporting environments. Second, most clinical discussions focus on classic local irritants, whereas signals involving antithrombotic agents, immunosuppressants, and antineoplastic therapies remain less systematically characterized. Third, time-to-onset information is often underused, although onset patterns may help distinguish early local irritation from more heterogeneous delayed reporting patterns. Fourth, patient-safety implications are not always clearly linked to pharmacovigilance findings.

The present study addressed these gaps using a multidatabase post-marketing pharmacovigilance approach [26]. Reports meeting a prespecified composite definition of esophageal ulceration- and perforation-related events were analyzed separately in FAERS, JADER, and Canada Vigilance from 2004 to 2025. Disproportionality analyses were used to identify reporting signals, while exploratory least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to prioritize selected FAERS drug-event pairs. Time-to-onset analyses described recorded temporal patterns among eligible drug-report records. The study was designed for descriptive comparison across reporting systems, not causal inference, incidence estimation, or formal external validation.

Materials and methods

Study design and data sources

This was a retrospective multidatabase pharmacovigilance study designed to identify post-marketing reporting signals for drug-associated esophageal ulceration and perforation. Three spontaneous reporting systems were analyzed: FAERS, JADER, and the Canada Vigilance Adverse Reaction Online Database. The study was conducted as a signal-detection and signal-prioritization analysis rather than a causal inference study. Therefore, all findings were interpreted as reporting associations rather than incidence rates, absolute risks, or confirmed causal relationships.

FAERS quarterly data files from the first quarter of 2004 through the first quarter of 2025 were downloaded and imported into PostgreSQL version 14.4. The following FAERS tables were used: DEMO (demographic and administrative information), DRUG (drug information), REAC (adverse-event terms), OUTC (patient outcomes), RPSR (report sources), THER (therapy dates), and INDI (reported indications). Reports were linked using the corresponding report identifiers. JADER and the Canada Vigilance Adverse Reaction Online Database were analyzed as additional spontaneous reporting systems for descriptive cross-database comparison. Adverse events were harmonized using Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) preferred terms where applicable.

Because FAERS, JADER, and Canada Vigilance are spontaneous reporting systems, the unit of analysis was the adverse event report rather than the exposed patient. The number of patients exposed to each drug was not available. Therefore, incidence rates, exposure-adjusted risks, and causal effects could not be calculated.

Deduplication and data cleaning

Duplicate FAERS reports were removed according to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-recommended procedure. When multiple records shared the same FAERS case identifier (CASEID), the report with the most recent FDA receipt date (FDA_DT) was retained. If both CASEID and FDA_DT were identical, the record with the highest primary report identifier (PRIMARYID) was retained. Remaining duplicate PRIMARYID records were then removed.

Drug names were standardized to generic active ingredients where possible, with spelling variants, salt forms, and brand names harmonized when they referred to the same ingredient. Reporting country names were standardized to reduce duplicate categories. Age values outside 0-120 years and weight values greater than 400 kg were treated as implausible in analyses involving these variables. Missing demographic information was reported explicitly and was not imputed.

Case definition

Terminology was defined to distinguish clinical entities from the analytical outcome. ‘Esophageal injury’ was used only as a broad clinical descriptor and was not a coded endpoint. The ulceration-related component comprised the MedDRA preferred terms ‘esophagitis ulcerative,’ ‘esophageal ulcer haemorrhage,’ ‘esophageal ulcer,’ ‘necrotising esophagitis,’ and ‘acute esophageal mucosal lesion.’ The perforation-related component comprised ‘esophageal ulcer perforation,’ ‘esophageal rupture,’ and ‘esophageal perforation.’

For the primary pharmacovigilance analyses, these eight preferred terms were combined into a composite outcome termed ‘esophageal ulceration- and perforation-related events.’ Pooling supported signal detection for rare related events but did not imply clinical, pathological, mechanistic, or severity equivalence. Drug-specific outcome- and severity-stratified disproportionality analyses were not performed. Reports were classified from coded preferred terms without independent clinical adjudication. Non-cases consisted of all other reports in the corresponding database during the same period; the FAERS primary analysis focused on drugs recorded as primary suspects.

Descriptive analysis of FAERS reports

Descriptive analyses were performed for FAERS reports meeting the composite case definition. Baseline characteristics included age, sex, body weight, reporter occupation, reporting country, reporting quarter, and retained serious-outcome code. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, and missing values were retained as separate categories. Disproportionality analysis in FAERS

Disproportionality analyses evaluated whether individual primary-suspect drugs were reported disproportionately with the composite outcome relative to other reported outcomes. For each drug-composite-outcome comparison, a two-by-two contingency table was constructed:

a = number of reports containing both the drug of interest and the composite outcome;

b = number of reports containing the drug of interest and other outcomes;

c = number of reports containing the composite outcome with other primary-suspect drugs;

d = number of reports containing other primary-suspect drugs and other outcomes.

Four signal-detection algorithms were used: reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN), and multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS) [27–32]. BCPNN was summarized by the information component (IC) and its lower credibility limit (IC025). For MGPS, openEBGM version 0.9.1 fitted a two-gamma-mixture prior across the 809 FAERS comparisons; the empirical Bayes geometric mean (EBGM) and its posterior 5th percentile (EBGM05) were reported. Formulas, fitted hyperparameters, and signal thresholds are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Two-sided Fisher’s exact tests were used when any expected cell count was < 5; otherwise, two-sided Pearson chi-square tests were used. The multiplicity family comprised 809 FAERS primary-suspect drug-composite-outcome comparisons. Bonferroni-adjusted P values were calculated as min(809 × raw P, 1); thus, adjusted P < 0.01 corresponded to raw P < 1.236 × 10-5. Complete results are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

The 50 primary-suspect drugs with the largest numbers of composite-outcome reports in FAERS were displayed in forest and volcano plots. These analyses were used for signal detection and prioritization only. ROR, PRR, BCPNN, and MGPS results were not interpreted as incidence rates, exposure-adjusted risks, or evidence of causality.

Exploratory signal prioritization using LASSO and multivariable logistic regression

Candidate screening for LASSO was performed exclusively within FAERS and did not require a drug to appear in JADER or Canada Vigilance. Entry required all three prespecified criteria: more than 100 reports meeting the composite case definition, an ROR lower 95% confidence limit > 1, and a Bonferroni-adjusted P value < 0.01. Eleven of the 809 evaluated drug-composite-outcome comparisons met these criteria and entered LASSO.

The binomial LASSO analysis used the restricted report-level preprocessing object containing 3,120,039 reports, including 2,137 reports meeting the composite case definition. The model used alpha = 1, standardized predictors, a fixed random seed of 20,260,826, and outcome-stratified 10-fold cross-validation based on binomial deviance. The minimum-deviance criterion was selected (lambda.min = 1.128109 × 10-5); lambda.1se was 6.607358 × 10-5. All 11 candidate drug indicators had nonzero coefficients at both penalty values and were carried forward to multivariable logistic regression. LASSO therefore did not reduce the prescreened set and was retained as an exploratory shrinkage step. In this object, candidate indicators were derived from one standardized primary-suspect DRUG value per report; a separate DrugList field retained all recorded drug entries and roles and was used for the PPI-exclusion analysis.

For multivariable logistic regression, 389 records with age values outside 0-120 years, including one report meeting the composite case definition, were excluded from the preprocessing object. The valid-age model therefore included 3,119,650 reports and 2,136 composite-outcome reports. The object was restricted to records with non-missing age and body weight, although body weight was not included in the model. Age and sex were included a priori; female and male indicators were compared with missing, unknown, or other sex. The resulting adjusted odds ratios are estimates from this FAERS case/non-case model and do not represent independent clinical risk factors or causal effects. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was calculated in the same sample as an apparent measure of discrimination; no internal or external model validation was performed.

Exploratory sensitivity and stratified analyses

To assess PPI-related reporting context, an exploratory report-level sensitivity analysis was performed within the restricted regression-preprocessing object containing 3,120,039 reports, including 2,137 composite-outcome reports. The DrugList field was used to exclude reports containing esomeprazole, omeprazole, pantoprazole, lansoprazole, rabeprazole, or dexlansoprazole in any recorded drug role. The four disproportionality measures were recalculated for the 10 non-PPI candidates; MGPS priors were fitted across all primary-suspect drug comparisons in the restricted data before and after exclusion. This object is a restricted subset of the 18,937,868-report primary FAERS population, so the analysis evaluates stability only within that subset.

Age-stratified analysis used reports in the same object with valid ages from 0 to 120 years. Reports were classified as < 65 or ≥ 65 years. Stratum-specific RORs were calculated for the 11 candidate drugs, and heterogeneity was assessed with the ratio of the older-group ROR to the younger-group ROR. Heterogeneity P values were adjusted across 11 drug comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. A 0.5 continuity correction was applied only when a stratum-specific two-by-two table contained a zero cell. These analyses describe reporting heterogeneity, not age-specific incidence or susceptibility.

Cross-database signal comparison using JADER and Canada Vigilance

Disproportionality analyses were conducted separately in JADER and Canada Vigilance using the same general case definition and signal-detection framework. JADER results were displayed for the 50 suspected-drug entries with the largest numbers of composite-outcome reports. The archived Canada Vigilance extract contained product-name-specific aggregate rows, so branded, biosimilar, and generic entries were retained separately. Because report-level identifiers were unavailable in this extract, aliases could not be deduplicated reliably and product-specific rows were not summed into ingredient-level totals.

Cross-database analysis was exploratory and descriptive. FAERS, JADER, and Canada Vigilance differ in database size, population coverage, reporting rules, regulatory context, drug utilization, reporter composition, and data completeness; their ROR magnitudes were therefore not compared directly. Recurrence was described as database-specific overlap that may increase priority for further study, not external validation. Absence of a signal in another database was not interpreted as evidence of no association.

Time-to-onset analysis

Time-to-onset was calculated in FAERS as the interval between the reported therapy start date for a drug and the adverse-event onset date. Each eligible drug-report pair contributed one onset record; multiple drug-specific records could therefore arise from the same adverse-event report. Records with missing, incomplete, implausible, or negative intervals were excluded. Eligible onset records were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), grouped interval counts, and cumulative percentages.

Weibull analysis was performed for 37 drugs that met the ROR signal criterion among the 50 most frequently reported FAERS primary suspects and had available onset data. The scale parameter describes the time scale of the fitted recorded-onset distribution, whereas the shape parameter describes its temporal pattern. An upper 95% confidence limit for the shape parameter < 1 was classified as an early-failure pattern, a 95% confidence interval including 1 as a random-failure pattern, and a lower 95% confidence limit > 1 as a wear-out pattern [33]. Because scale and shape parameters are positive, their confidence intervals were calculated on the log scale.

Time-to-onset and Weibull findings were interpreted only as descriptions of recorded onset patterns. They do not estimate incidence, an exposure-based hazard, or a biological risk window because the data lack risk sets, censoring information, verified treatment timelines, and independence across drug-report records.

Bias considerations for proton pump inhibitor signals

Proton pump inhibitor-related signals were interpreted cautiously. Drugs such as esomeprazole, omeprazole, and rabeprazole are frequently prescribed for gastroesophageal reflux disease, erosive esophagitis, ulcer symptoms, and gastroprotection in patients receiving non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, antiplatelet agents, or anticoagulants [14]. Therefore, reporting signals for proton pump inhibitors may reflect confounding by indication or protopathic bias rather than direct esophageal toxicity.

Statistical software and ethical considerations

Primary data management was performed using PostgreSQL version 14.4, and the submitted primary analyses used R version 4.4.2. Reproducibility, sensitivity, and stratified analyses used R version 4.5.0, glmnet version 4.1–10, and openEBGM version 0.9.1. All statistical tests were two-sided where applicable. Bonferroni-adjusted P values < 0.01 were used for candidate screening; age-stratum heterogeneity P values were adjusted across 11 drug comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method.

This study used publicly available, anonymized, and de-identified spontaneous adverse event reporting data. No individual patient identifiers were accessed. Therefore, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required.

Results

Characteristics of FAERS reports

A total of 8,061 FAERS reports met the prespecified composite case definition after deduplication and data cleaning (Fig. 1A). Among these reports, 3,799 (47.1%) involved female patients and 3,529 (43.8%) involved male patients, while sex was missing or unknown in 733 reports (9.1%). Age was available in 6,013 reports and missing in 2,048 reports (25.4%). The largest age group was 18-64.9 years, accounting for 3,076 reports (38.2%), followed by 65–85 years with 2,453 reports (30.4%). Reports involving patients younger than 18 years and older than 85 years accounted for 215 (2.7%) and 269 (3.3%) reports, respectively. Body weight was missing in 5,534 reports (68.7%) (Table 1).

Disproportionality signals in FAERS

Among the 50 most frequently reported FAERS primary-suspect drugs, the largest disproportionalityestimates were observed for doxycycline, alendronic acid, and clindamycin (Fig. 2). Doxycycline had an ROR of 41.15 (95% confidence interval [CI] 36.47–46.43), PRR of 40.48, EBGM of 38.59, and IC of 5.29. The corresponding estimates were ROR 21.45 (95% CI 19.14–24.04), PRR 21.27, EBGM 20.35, and IC 4.36 for alendronic acid; ROR 18.59 (95% CI 15.40-22.44), PRR 18.45, EBGM 17.89, and IC 4.19 for clindamycin; and ROR 10.82 (95% CI 9.66–12.13), PRR 10.78, EBGM 10.36, and IC 3.38 for esomeprazole. These values quantify reporting imbalance within FAERS and do not rank clinical risk.

Additional FAERS reporting signals were observed for antithrombotic and anti-inflammatory agents, including clopidogrel, acetylsalicylic acid, ibuprofen, dabigatran, rivaroxaban, warfarin, and naproxen. Signals were also observed for mycophenolic acid, tacrolimus, bevacizumab, and sunitinib. Across all 809 comparisons, 495 P values were obtained with Fisher’s exact test and 314 with Pearson’s chi-square test; 53 comparisons met the MGPS criterion of EBGM05 > 2. Complete drug-level results and multiplicity-adjusted P values are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Signals were also observed for several antineoplastic therapies, including bevacizumab (50 reports; ROR 3.71, 95% CI 2.78–4.94), fluorouracil (47 reports; ROR 5.54, 95% CI 4.12–7.44), cisplatin (47 reports; ROR 4.63, 95% CI 3.44–6.22), docetaxel hydrate (19 reports; ROR 3.87, 95% CI 2.45–6.11), and capecitabine (12 reports; ROR 2.56, 95% CI 1.44–4.52). JADER therefore showed descriptive overlap for several classic irritants, dabigatran, bisphosphonates, antiplatelet agents, and selected oncology therapies, without providing formal external validation.

Exploratory signal prioritization in FAERS

Eleven FAERS drug-composite-outcome variables met the prespecified screening criteria and entered LASSO. At lambda.min = 1.128109 × 10-5, all 11 had nonzero coefficients and were included in the multivariable logistic regression with age and sex (Fig. 5A and B). The same variables remained nonzero at lambda.1se. The absence of variable reduction limits the incremental selection value of LASSO in this prescreened set.

In the valid-age FAERS case/non-case model, all 11 drug variables had adjusted odds ratios above 1 (Fig. 5D). The largest adjusted odds ratios (ORs) were observed for doxycycline (25.98, 95% confidence interval [CI] 20.27–33.29), alendronic acid (25.38, 95% CI 20.60-31.27), clindamycin (11.03, 95% CI 7.16–16.99), and esomeprazole (10.16, 95% CI 8.38–12.32). These estimates describe adjusted reporting associations within the restricted regression sample and are not clinical risk estimates.

Age had a statistically detectable but small adjusted odds ratio (OR 1.011 per year, 95% CI 1.009–1.013). Neither female sex (OR 0.73, 95% CI 0.46–1.16) nor male sex (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.62–1.58) showed a clear association relative to missing, unknown, or other sex. The apparent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was 0.677 in the same sample (Fig. 5C), indicating limited discrimination. Because no internal or external validation was performed, the model should not be used for individual prediction or clinical decision-making.

Sensitivity analysis excluding PPI-containing reports

Within the restricted preprocessing object, 419,314 PPI-containing reports, including 662 composite-outcome reports, were excluded. The remaining sample comprised 2,700,725 reports, including 1,475 composite-outcome reports. Across the 10 non-PPI candidates, ROR changes ranged from − 9.2% to + 27.6%. Post-exclusion RORs were 25.01 (95% CI 19.08–32.80) for doxycycline, 23.15 (95% CI 18.30-29.28) for alendronic acid, and 10.76 (95% CI 6.84–16.94) for clindamycin. No algorithm-specific signal classification changed (Supplementary Table S3).

Age-stratified reporting patterns

The age-stratified analysis included 3,119,650 reports with valid ages, including 2,136 composite-outcome reports. The < 65-year group contained 1,965,030 reports, including 1,152 composite-outcome reports; the ≥65-year group contained 1,154,620 reports, including 984 composite-outcome reports. Doxycycline had an ROR of 30.37 (95% CI 23.32–39.55) in the younger group and 5.25 (95% CI 2.49–11.06) in the older group, giving an older-to-younger ROR ratio of 0.173 (95% CI 0.078–0.381; BH-adjusted P = 0.000149). Esomeprazole also showed lower disproportionality in the older group (ROR ratio 0.535, 95% CI 0.357–0.802; BH-adjusted P = 0.0135). No other candidate met the multiplicity-adjusted heterogeneity criterion. Mycophenolic acid had no composite-outcome reports in the older stratum, so its continuity-corrected estimate was imprecise (Supplementary Table S4).

Integrated signal pattern

Taken together, FAERS showed prominent reporting signals for established esophageal irritants, particularly doxycycline, alendronic acid, and clindamycin, alongside exploratory patterns for NSAIDs, antithrombotic agents, and immunosuppressants. JADER and Canada Vigilance showed overlapping but heterogeneous database-specific patterns. The regression analysis provided limited exploratory prioritization, and the onset analyses showed temporal heterogeneity rather than a single clinical risk window.

Discussion

Principal findings and study contribution

The principal finding was that the largest FAERS disproportionality estimates among common primary suspects involved established pill-injury drugs, particularly doxycycline, alendronic acid, and clindamycin. Descriptive overlap for selected drug-event pairs was observed in JADER and Canada Vigilance, but these database-specific estimates are not directly comparable and do not constitute external validation. The findings may help prioritize clinical awareness and further study, but they do not establish a hierarchy of drug-specific clinical risk.

The study also organized reports into clinically relevant contexts: classic local irritants, antithrombotic and NSAID therapies, immunosuppressive agents, and oncology-related therapies. These categories differ in biological plausibility, preventability, treatment context, and susceptibility to reporting bias. This framework aids interpretation but does not establish a common mechanism or causal contribution for every reported drug.

The findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating safety signals rather than causal risk estimates. Spontaneous reporting systems are valuable for detecting rare, serious, or unexpected adverse-event patterns after marketing, but they lack reliable exposure denominators and are affected by underreporting, duplicate reporting, stimulated reporting, incomplete clinical details, and confounding by indication [22, 27–29]. Therefore, disproportionality measures and adjusted odds ratios from the restricted FAERS case/non-case model cannot be interpreted as incidence rates, absolute or comparative risks, or causal effect sizes. Their appropriate role is to support clinical signal recognition and prioritize drug-event pairs for further denominator-based pharmacoepidemiologic evaluation.

Classic pill-induced injury remains the most clinically actionable signal

Doxycycline had one of the largest FAERS disproportionality estimates, consistent with established literature on pill-induced esophagitis [6, 7, 34]. Prolonged mucosal contact is plausible when a tablet or capsule is taken with insufficient water, before recumbency, or in the presence of dysphagia or impaired transit. Clinical reports continue to document doxycycline-associated esophageal ulceration [5, 8, 9]. This prior evidence supports biological plausibility, but the present ROR remains a measure of reporting imbalance rather than a causal effect size.

Alendronate-associated esophagitis and ulceration are well described, and administration precautions are integral to prescribing [4, 35, 36]. The FAERS pattern and descriptive overlap in JADER indicate continued post-marketing reporting despite long-standing awareness. They support reinforcing existing administration advice, not estimating additional incidence or comparative risk.

Clindamycin-induced esophageal injury may be difficult to recognize because its symptoms can resemble reflux disease, infection, or nonspecific retrosternal discomfort [10, 37, 38]. Its prominent FAERS signal supports continued clinical awareness [10, 39]. Doxycycline, alendronic acid, and clindamycin therefore form a plausible group for administration counseling, while the current data remain insufficient for causal ranking.

Antithrombotic and NSAID-related signals broaden the clinical spectrum

Reporting signals involving acetylsalicylic acid, clopidogrel, dabigatran, rivaroxaban, ibuprofen, and related agents broadened the observed context beyond classic local irritation [15, 16]. These patterns should not be interpreted as proof of direct esophageal toxicity. They may reflect bleeding from an existing lesion, impaired recovery, older age, cardiovascular morbidity, polypharmacy, treatment indication, or differential surveillance.

Dabigatran warrants clinical attention because prior endoscopic evidence describes a recognizable esophageal injury pattern [11, 12]. Its tartaric acid-containing capsule may contribute to local irritation [11, 12]. The FAERS and JADER findings are consistent with that prior concern, but recurrence across spontaneous reporting systems remains descriptive and does not quantify individual risk.

For NSAIDs and antiplatelet agents, interpretation should remain cautious. These drugs may contribute to mucosal vulnerability or bleeding once injury occurs, but the observed reporting signals may also reflect age, cardiovascular comorbidity, concomitant medications, gastroprotective prescribing, and greater clinical surveillance. Therefore, these signals should be interpreted as prompts for medication review and clinical evaluation rather than as evidence of direct esophageal toxicity [14].

Immunosuppressive and oncology-related signals require context-specific interpretation

The reporting spectrum also included immunosuppressive and oncology-related therapies, including mycophenolic acid, tacrolimus, bevacizumab, sunitinib, fluorouracil, cisplatin, crizotinib, and selected immune checkpoint inhibitors [17, 40–43]. These estimates are especially vulnerable to treatment-context bias because recipients may have malignancy, transplantation, opportunistic infection, prior radiotherapy, procedural exposure, or extensive polypharmacy [18, 20].

Mycophenolate-related gastrointestinal mucosal injury has been described in previous pathological studies [19]. In immunocompromised patients, ulcerative esophageal disease may also arise from opportunistic infections such as cytomegalovirus or herpes simplex virus [17, 18, 20]. Therefore, signals involving immunosuppressants may reflect direct mucosal injury, impaired epithelial repair, infection susceptibility, underlying disease severity, or concomitant therapy. The present data cannot distinguish among these possibilities. Accordingly, these signals should be interpreted as context-dependent reporting associations rather than evidence of a specific causal pathway.

Immune checkpoint inhibitor-related reports require particular caution. Although immune-mediated esophagitis has been described [21, 44, 45], positive signals in this study were confined mainly to small-count Canada Vigilance observations and were not reproduced as positive signals for nivolumab, pembrolizumab, or ipilimumab in JADER. These database-specific patterns warrant clinical review rather than causal attribution.

Potential confounding and reporting biases across drug categories

Interpretation depends on why a drug was prescribed and the clinical setting in which a report arose. Confounding by indication is plausible when the treated condition itself predisposes to esophageal injury; protopathic bias may occur when a drug is prescribed for early symptoms of an unrecognized event; and channeling bias may arise when patients with greater baseline susceptibility receive a particular therapy. PPI signals are especially susceptible because these agents are prescribed for reflux, erosive esophagitis, ulcer symptoms, and gastroprotection [24].

Established pill irritants have stronger prior biological and clinical support, but their signal magnitudes may still be influenced by dysphagia, impaired mobility, administration technique, older age, and stimulated reporting. Antithrombotic and NSAID patterns may reflect cardiovascular and gastrointestinal comorbidity, bleeding from an existing lesion, or delayed healing rather than initiation of mucosal injury. Age- and sex-adjustment cannot resolve these factors.

For immunosuppressive and antineoplastic therapies, confounding by indication and channeling bias are particularly plausible because treated populations may have malignancy, transplantation, infection susceptibility, prior radiotherapy, mucosal injury from other treatments, invasive procedures, polypharmacy, or intensive surveillance. The available data cannot separate these explanations from possible drug-related contributions.

The PPI-exclusion analysis produced no change in algorithm-specific classifications for the 10 non-PPI candidates within the restricted sample. This finding does not remove confounding by indication, protopathic bias, unrecorded concomitant therapy, or selection introduced by preprocessing.

Age-stratified reporting patterns

Lower relative reporting was observed for doxycycline and esomeprazole in reports involving adults aged 65 years or older; the remaining candidates did not show multiplicity-adjusted heterogeneity. Differences may reflect prescribing, formulation, medication burden, dysphagia, esophageal motility, comorbidity, missingness, or reporting behavior rather than biological effect modification. The restricted sample and absence of age-specific exposure denominators preclude inference about incidence or causal risk.

Temporal pattern and clinical implications

The time-to-onset analysis describes intervals recorded for drug-report pairs, not event incidence over time. The nearly equal proportions of records at 0–30 days and beyond 360 days demonstrate substantial heterogeneity. Inaccurate or incomplete therapy dates, delayed recognition or reporting, long-term treatment, interruption or re-exposure, disease progression, and concomitant treatment may all contribute [46, 47].

A Weibull shape estimate below 1 describes a decreasing pattern within the fitted recorded-onset distribution but does not define a clinical early-onset period or biological risk window. The absence of exposure-based risk sets, censoring, verified timelines, and independence among drug-report records precludes hazard interpretation. The scale and shape parameters are therefore descriptive summaries only [33].

The practical implication is focused clinical awareness rather than automatic treatment change. Administration counseling is appropriate for established pill-injury drugs. New odynophagia, dysphagia, retrosternal pain, hematemesis, or unexplained upper gastrointestinal symptoms after treatment initiation should prompt clinical assessment. For antithrombotic, NSAID, immunosuppressive, and oncology therapies, evaluation should include co-medications and alternative causes such as infection, malignancy-related injury, radiotherapy, or pre-existing esophageal disease.

Strengths and limitations

This study focused on clinically important esophageal ulceration- and perforation-related events and analyzed three reporting systems separately. The use of four disproportionality algorithms, reproducible exploratory regression, descriptive onset analyses, and explicit bias assessment provides several complementary views of the reporting data. The multidatabase design permits descriptive comparison of overlap and heterogeneity, not formal external validation.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. Spontaneous reporting systems are subject to underreporting, duplicate and stimulated reporting, missing information, variable report quality, and the absence of exposure denominators; incidence, comparative risk, and causal effects therefore cannot be estimated. Indication, comorbidities, dysphagia, pre-existing esophageal disease, formulation, administration technique, endoscopic findings, infection, malignancy, radiotherapy, and concomitant treatment were unavailable or incomplete. The regression adjusted only for age and sex and used a restricted preprocessing object selected by data completeness. Candidate indicators were based on one standardized primary-suspect DRUG value per report, all 11 prescreened drugs remained in the model, and the apparent AUC of 0.677 indicated limited discrimination and was not internally or externally validated. The PPI-exclusion and age-stratified analyses used the same restricted object and may therefore be affected by selection.

Time-to-onset records were available only for selected drug-report pairs with valid dates, were not necessarily independent, and may contain date error or reporting delay. Canada Vigilance rows were product-name-specific and could not be combined reliably without report-level identifiers. The eight preferred terms pooled ulceration, hemorrhage, rupture, and perforation despite differences in severity, mechanism, and drug attribution; the preferred-term sex analysis did not provide drug-specific outcome or severity stratification. Accordingly, the present analysis cannot determine whether established local irritants and antithrombotic agents differ in their component-specific reporting patterns. Clinically adjudicated, outcome- and severity-stratified denominator-based studies are therefore needed. E-values were not calculated because spontaneous-reporting RORs measure reporting imbalance rather than causal risk and do not provide the causal estimand, exposure denominators, sampling probabilities, or confounder measurement required for interpretable E-value analysis.

Overall, the data identify prominent reporting patterns for established pill-injury drugs and exploratory patterns in other treatment contexts. Their appropriate use is to support clinical awareness and prioritize denominator-based pharmacoepidemiologic validation, not to infer causal rank or comparative drug risk.

Conclusion

This multidatabase pharmacovigilance analysis identified disproportionate reporting patterns for selected drug-event pairs involving esophageal ulceration- and perforation-related events. The largest FAERS estimates among common primary suspects involved doxycycline, alendronic acid, and clindamycin; other drug categories showed exploratory, context-dependent patterns. These findings are hypothesis-generating and should not be interpreted as estimates of incidence, comparative clinical risk, or causal effects. Further evaluation requires data sources with exposure denominators, validated outcomes, and adequate control of confounding.
