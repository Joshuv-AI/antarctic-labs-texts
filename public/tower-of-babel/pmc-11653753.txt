Is expectant management feasible for select patients diagnosed with a heterotopic tubal pregnancy following in vitro fertilization/intracytoplasmic sperm injection?
Authors: Yuyao Mao, Yangqin Peng, Mingxiang Zheng, Pei Cai, Fei Gong, Hui Chen, Ge Lin, Yan Ouyang, Xihong Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeterotopic pregnancy (HP) is a rare type of pathological pregnancy, and the most common site of concomitant ectopic pregnancy (EP) is the fallopian tube. Some studies have shown that expectant treatment could be considered because some EPs tend to regress spontaneously. However, data on the expectant treatment of HP are lacking. This study aimed to examine the outcomes of eutopic pregnancies following expectant management of concomitant tubal EP after in vitro fertilization (IVF) or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI).MethodsThis was a retrospective, observational matched cohort study. All patients who conceived with tubal HP (HTP) via IVF/ICSI and were treated expectantly between January 2010 and December 2021 were enrolled. Each HTP patient managed expectantly (A) was randomly matched with one control patient who conceived with a solely eutopic pregnancy (B) and one HTP patient who underwent surgical therapy (C) from our clinical database according to predefined criteria. The expectant group and the control groups were matched for maternal age (MA; ±1 year), gravidity (0; ≥1) and gestational age (GA) at presentation (± 2 days). The pregnancy outcomes of the study group and the control groups were compared.ResultsMore than three-quarters of the EPs in patients treated expectantly were inhomogeneous solid masses (n = 95, 76.6%), and an empty gestational sac was the second most common (n = 18, 14.5%). The rates of live birth (76.6%, 79.0%, 73.4%; P = 0.647 (A versus B); P = 0.557 (A versus C)) and early miscarriage (21.8%, 19.4%, 25.8%; P = 0.637 (A versus B); P = 0.456 (A versus C)) in the expectant group were similar to those in the eutopic group and those in the surgical group. There were no significant differences in of the preterm birth rate (7.3%, 7.3%, 7.3%; P = 1.000 (A versus B); P = 1.000 (A versus C)), perinatal mortality (1.0%, 1.0%, 1.1%; P = 1.000 (A versus B); P = 1.000 (A versus C)), GA at delivery (38.7 ± 2.1, 38.4 ± 2.4, 38.5 ± 2.3 weeks; P = 0.286 (A versus B); P = 0.425 (A versus C)) or birth weight (3294.8 ± 507.2, 3238.2 ± 646.1, 3194.5 ± 452.9 g; P = 0.498 (A versus B); P = 0.157 (A versus C)).ConclusionExpectant management was an option for select women with HTP. The prognoses of the concomitant eutopic pregnancies were comparable to those of the solely eutopic singleton pregnancies and the HTPs managed surgically. Background

Heterotopic pregnancy (HP) is the coexistence of a normal-site (eutopic) pregnancy and an ectopic pregnancy (EP) [1]. This is a rare type of pathological pregnancy. Previously, the incidence rate of HP was estimated to be 1/30,000 for spontaneous pregnancies and 1/100 for assisted reproduction technology (ART)-related pregnancies [2, 3]. More recent reports have indicated a prevalence of 0.05% (1/2000) in women visiting early pregnancy units (EPUs) [4] and 0.04% (1/2500) in ART-related pregnancies [5].

A concomitant EP can occur anywhere outside the uterine cavity, such as the fallopian tube, cervix, caesarean section scar, intramural, or even the ovary and abdomen, and the most common site is the fallopian tube [6]. Interstitial pregnancies were classified as tubal pregnancies, by ESHRE 2020 [1].

The value of serum beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) levels is limited in HP due to the coexisting eutopic pregnancy [7]. Transvaginal sonography (TVS) plays a critical role in the diagnosis of HP, and experienced sonographers have a high detection rate (76-92.4%) [8, 9] and high positive predictive value (100%) [8]. However, in patients with nonspecific clinical manifestations, EP might be overlooked, leading to potentially delayed diagnosis with an increased risk of adverse maternal and pregnancy outcomes [6].

The primary aim of HP management is to ensure maternal safety with the least harm to the normal-site gestational sac (GS). Surgery has been the first-line treatment modality for HPs in most publications [6, 9, 10]. Some studies have shown that expectant treatment could be considered for some simple EPs that tend to regress spontaneously, without the need for surgical or medical intervention [11, 12]. However, there are limited data indicating whether expectant management is feasible for treating HPs. Due to the rarity of HPs, previous studies have focused mostly on case reports or series analyses, with both successful and unsuccessful expectant treatments [13–15], and only a few of them have been carried out as case‒controlled observations and comparisons with a surgical group [7, 16].

To date, no study has compared HPs managed expectantly with both solely eutopic pregnancies and HPs managed surgically, making it difficult to advise women on the risks associated with expectant treatment of EP. The purpose of this study was to examine the outcomes of eutopic pregnancies following expectant management of concomitant tubal EP after in vitro fertilization (IVF) or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI).

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective observational matched-cohort study was carried out at the Reproductive and Genetic Hospital of CITIC-Xiangya (Changsha, China). The study protocol was approved by the local ethical review committee (LL-SC-2023-016). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. All patients who conceived with tubal HP (HTP) via IVF/ICSI and were treated expectantly between January 2010 and December 2021 were enrolled. The maternal demographic characteristics and procedure details were derived from the hospital’s electronic medical records, and ultrasound findings were obtained from ultrasound workstation.

The conditions for patient inclusion were as follows: patients had ART indications and received IVF/ICSI at our centre. They were diagnosed with HTP (including heterotopic interstitial pregnancy) via ultrasound in our imaging department and received the expectant treatment throughout the entire pregnancy period. The exclusion criteria were as follows: EP was diagnosed after curettage of the miscarried eutopic pregnancy, treatment was converted to surgical treatment or ultrasound-guided aspiration afterwards, the patient was lost to follow-up before 12 weeks, the EP was selectively terminated for congenital foetal structural or chromosomal abnormalities, HTP was present with multiple eutopic pregnancies, or EP diagnosis was missed.

For each HTP patient receiving expectant treatment, one patient who conceived with a solely eutopic pregnancy and one HTP patient who underwent surgical therapy were matched with predefined criteria for the control groups and selected randomly from our clinical database. The study group and the control groups were matched for maternal age (MA; ±1 year), gravidity (0; ≥1) and gestational age (GA) at presentation (± 2 days). The pregnancy outcomes of the expectant group and the control groups were compared.

Ultrasound diagnosis

A routine blood test for serum beta-human chorionic gonadotrophic hormone (β-hCG) was performed on day 14 post embryo transfer (ET, blastocysts on day 12). A TVS scan using a GE Voluson E10/E8/730 (GE Tech Co., Ltd., New York, NY, USA) equipped with a 5–9 MHz endocavitary transducer was conducted by experienced sonographers 2 weeks later to examine the viability and location of the pregnancies. If low serum β-hCG levels (< 200 mIU/ml) were measured, an earlier TVS scan was performed on day 21–22 (blastocysts on day 19–20) after ET to confirm whether the GS was at the normal site. TVS scans were arranged whenever patients complained of abdominal pain and/or vaginal bleeding.

TVS detection of a yolk sac (YS) and/or fetal pole without considering cardiac activity in the GS within the uterine cavity indicated an eutopic pregnancy [1, 9]. A diagnosis of HTP (interstitial) was suspected when products of conception were located outside the endometrial cavity and surrounded by a continuous edge of the myometrium within the interstitial area [17] and another GS was observed separately in the uterine cavity (Fig. 1). A diagnosis of HTP (non-interstitial, i.e., isthmic or ampullary) was suspected when a eutopic pregnancy was accompanied by one of the following: (1) a heterogeneous adnexal mass or ‘blob sign’ adjacent to the ovary (Fig. 2); (2) an empty extrauterine GS with a hyperechoic ring (Fig. 3); or (3) a YS and/or fetal pole with or without cardiac activity in an extrauterine GS (Fig. 4) [18]. Once HTP was suspected, another senior sonographer would be consulted for further confirmation; thus, a diagnosis of HTP was from a consensus of at least 2 doctors.

The EP was further classified according to its morphological characteristics into five groups [6]: (1) a GS with a live embryo; (2) a GS with an embryo and without cardiac activity; (3) a GS with a YS only; (4) an empty GS; and (5) an inhomogeneous solid mass. All ultrasound images of the enrolled patients were reviewed by an experienced senior sonographer (X.L.) on an ultrasound workstation to confirm the diagnosis.

Clinical strategy

The treatment approach was selected by the same postpregnancy management specialist team at our centre, considering the clinical manifestations, EP location, EP morphological features and patient preference. The indications for expectant treatment were as follows [8, 11, 12]: haemodynamically stable; no intraperitoneal bleeding or other signs of EP rupture, asymptomatic or mild abdominal pain and/or vaginal bleeding; negative or extremely weak cardiac activity of EP; a strong desire for non-surgical management; and a willingness to undergo potentially prolonged follow-up. Patients receiving expectant treatment as first-line management were advised to be hospitalized or stay near the hospital to facilitate close ultrasound examination to monitor changes in the EP. Once EP rupture was suspected, emergency surgery was performed immediately. Surgical treatment was recommended for women with EP with visible cardiac activity on an ultrasound scan, a nonviable EP mass that was observed in serial follow-up examinations to develop further, evidence of rupture of the EP mass, or haemodynamic instability [8]. The benefits and risks of each treatment option were explained to women with no signs of EP rupture, and the final treatment was chosen based on patient preference.

Outcome measures

The GA was calculated as the number of days after transfer plus 17 days for cleavage embryos or 19 days for blastocysts. All patients were tracked until the end of pregnancy by a dedicated follow-up team at our centre, and the pregnancy and perinatal outcomes were recorded via telephone. The primary outcome of this study was the live birth rate. The secondary outcome was the pregnancy outcome at 12 weeks of gestation.

Preterm birth was defined as a delivery that occurred at a GA of at least 22 but less than 37 weeks. Term birth was defined as delivery at a GA of at least 37 but less than 42 weeks. Live birth was defined as the complete expulsion or extraction from a woman at a GA of at least 22 weeks of fertilized products that, after separation, breathed or showed any other signs of life, regardless of whether the umbilical cord had been cut or the placenta was attached. Perinatal death was foetal or neonatal death that occurred during late pregnancy (at a GA of at least 22 weeks), during delivery, or within 7 days after birth. Low birth weight was defined as a birth weight < 2500 g [19].

Statistical analysis

We used the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 24.0 software (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) to select the control cases, and statistical analyses were also performed via SPSS version 24.0 software. The distributions of the baseline variables were analysed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and nonnormally distributed continuous variables are presented as the median (ranges). The frequency and percentage are presented for categorical variables. Student’s t test was used to compare continuous variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to assess categorical variables between the study group and the control groups. A P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

HTP was diagnosed in 789 patients during the study period. Expectant therapy was used as the primary treatment in 138 patients (17.5%). We excluded women who were diagnosed during the second trimester (n = 2), who were transferred to emergency surgical treatment (n = 2), who selectively terminated their eutopic pregnancy (n = 2), or who had twin eutopic pregnancies (n = 8). Ultimately, 124 women with HTP, including interstitial (n = 14) and non-interstitial (n = 110) HTP, that were treated expectantly were included in the final analysis (Fig. 5). The patients presented no evidence of peritoneal bleeding or severe abdominal pain and were clinically stable. As the control groups, we matched 124 patients whose pregnancies were solely eutopic singletons and 124 patients whose pregnancies were HTPs receiving surgical management.

There were significant differences in EP morphology in HTP patients treated expectantly or surgically (P < 0.001) (Table 2). Among the 124 patients treated expectantly in this study, more than three-quarters of their EPs were inhomogeneous solid masses (n = 95, 76.6%), an empty GS was the second most common (n = 18, 14.5%), and only 3 (2.4%) had an embryo with cardiac activity (but the fetal heart rate was extremely weak and eventually disappeared during the monitoring period). An empty GS (n = 44, 35.5%) and an embryo with cardiac activity (n = 39, 31.5%) were the most common morphological types in the surgical group, followed by an inhomogeneous solid mass (n = 10, 8.1%). The proportion of pelvic fluid with a ground glass appearance and uterine cavity fluid was not significantly different between the two groups (p > 0.05).

Pregnancy and obstetric outcomes

Pregnancy and obstetric outcomes are described in Table 3. The rates of live birth (76.6% versus 79.0%, P = 0.647, and 76.6% versus 73.4%, P = 0.557, respectively) and early miscarriage (21.8% versus 19.4%, P = 0.637, and 21.8% versus 25.8%, P = 0.456) in the expectant group were similar to those in the eutopic group and surgical group. There were no significant differences in terms of preterm birth rate (7.3% versus 7.3%, P = 1.000, and 7.3% versus 7.3%, P = 1.000), perinatal mortality (1.0% versus 1.0%, P = 1.000, and 1.0% versus 1.1%, P = 1.000), GA at delivery (38.7 ± 2.1 versus 38.4 ± 2.4 weeks, P = 0.286, and 38.7 ± 2.1 versus 38.5 ± 2.3 weeks, P = 0.425) or birth weight (3294.8 ± 507.2 g versus 3238.2 ± 646.1 g, P = 0.498, and 3294.8 ± 507.2 g versus 3194.5 ± 452.9 g, P = 0.157). The caesarean section rate of the expectant group was significantly greater than that of the eutopic group (88.5% versus 70.7%, P = 0.002), but there was no significant difference compared with that of the surgical group (88.5% versus 78.3%, P = 0.058).

Discussion

In this study, the pregnancy outcomes of select HTP patients who were treated expectantly were analysed. We found that expectant management in select cases of HTP yielded rates of live birth and ongoing pregnancy that were comparable to those of the solely eutopic singleton pregnancies and HTPs managed surgically under close follow-up. An inhomogeneous solid mass was the most common morphological feature of EPs under expectant treatment.

Early diagnosis and appropriate treatment are crucial because of the serious maternal and infant complications that HTP may cause. In this study, most patients with HTP had no obvious symptoms or only mild abdominal pain or bleeding, which may have caused the examiner to relax their guard against EP. Moreover, there was no significant difference in β-hCG levels between HTP patients and those with only eutopic pregnancies, indicating that β-hCG has limited reference value in the diagnosis of HTP. In addition, pelvic fluid or enlarged ovaries can also result in atypical ultrasound presentations of HP after ART and increase the difficulty of exploring EP [8]. Therefore, for patients who have received ART, especially multi-ET, the accessory area should be scanned carefully, and routine follow-up reviews should be emphasized to minimize the risk of missed HP diagnosis. On this basis, single embryo transfer is safer and should be advocated [20].

The challenge faced by clinicians was not only to diagnose HTPs but also to decide on the most suitable management plan for each individual woman. Women receiving ART have a strong desire to preserve the eutopic pregnancy, and the treatment methods should be carefully selected to balance maternal safety and the preservation of concomitant eutopic pregnancies. Surgery is the most commonly used treatment method for HTP [21]. Previous studies have confirmed the safety of surgical management, and some studies have indicated that reasonable surgical management does not increase the risk of miscarriage in patients with concomitant eutopic pregnancy [6, 22]. The live birth rate of patients who received surgical treatment in this study (73.4%) was also comparable to that reported in previous studies [23, 24] and is considerable. However, previous studies have also reported cases of eutopic miscarriage after surgery for EP [13, 25, 26].

Many HTP patients after ART are very hesitant about surgery, fearing that it may have adverse effects on eutopic pregnancy. Expectant management does not involve any intervention and seems to be the optimal option for patients who have a strong desire to preserve the eutopic pregnancy. However, the prerequisite for this option is that there is no change in or regression of the EP and that the patients’ clinical symptoms are stable. In this study, 2 patients underwent surgical treatment because their EPs continued to progress, resulting in fetal cardiac activity during the process of expectant treatment. Therefore, close dynamic follow-up is essential for patients who have chosen to undergo expectant therapy.

In this study, an inhomogeneous solid mass was the most common morphological feature of EPs under expectant management, which is consistent with our previous research [8]. This finding indicates that an EP mass that has not developed a GS is more likely to stop developing; that is, the inhomogeneous solid mass type is relatively more suitable for expectant management. However, 23.4% of the patients in our study had an EP that developed with a GS (3 of them with cardiac activity), which indicates that in cases where clinical symptoms are stable and patients have strong willingness, expectant management can also be used as an alternative treatment for such patients under close monitoring. However, the patient must be informed that as the ectopic mass changes, it may be possible to switch to surgical management at any time.

Data regarding pregnancy outcomes following expectant treatment for HTP are limited. This is the first study to compare the outcomes of HTPs managed expectantly with those of eutopic pregnancies and HTPs treated surgically. The main strength of this study is the large sample size of HTP, and the control groups were carefully matched. However, there are also several limitations. First, one issue with expectant treatment is that it is not possible to verify this matter itself through surgery or pathology, and all discussions are based on images detected under ultrasound. This means that some asymptomatic patients may be missed and that the possibility of misdiagnosis also cannot be ruled out, which causes possible bias in the study samples. Second, our institution is merely a reproductive centre, and follow-up expectant observation of the ectopic mass and the birth of a eutopic pregnancy are performed at other hospitals. As a result, the patients’ complications and the perinatal clinical data obtained by telephone are also less detailed. In addition, the rates of live birth [6] and miscarriage [26] among patients with HTPs in this study were consistent with those reported in previous studies. However, due to the expectant management, it was impossible to further analyse the influence of the location and characteristics of the ectopic mass on the outcome of a eutopic pregnancy.

In conclusion, this study showed that expectant management is an option for select women with HTPs. The prognoses of patients with concomitant eutopic pregnancies were comparable to those of patients with only eutopic singleton pregnancies and HTPs treated surgically. However, the prerequisite for this protocol is that there is no change in or regression of the EP and that the patient’s clinical symptoms are stable. Notably, close follow-up is required, and patients need to be vigilant and have immediate access to medical resources in case of an emergency.
