Syzygium aromaticum Attenuates Cerebellar Oxidative Stress: An Integrated in silico, in vitro, and in vivo Evaluation of Multi-Target Antioxidant Mechanisms
Authors: Ekom Monday Etukudo, Ibe Michael Usman, Augustine Oviosun, Vivian Onyinye Ojiakor, Wusa Makena, Elna Owembabazi, Patrick Maduabuchi Aja, Emeka Anyanwu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe central role of oxidative stress in neuronal injury and the progression of neurological disorders underscores the need to identify multi-target agents capable of restoring redox homeostasis. This study evaluated the antioxidant and pharmacological potential of Syzygium aromaticum ethyl acetate fraction (SEAF) using integrated phytochemical, computational, and experimental approaches.MethodsGC-MS was used to characterize the phytochemical composition of the ethyl acetate fraction of Syzygium aromaticum (SEAF). Thirteen phytometabolites with relative peak areas ≥1.0% were selected for ADME-Tox profiling and molecular docking against monoamine oxidase B (MAO-B) and 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX). In vitro antioxidant assays, acute oral toxicity testing, and in vivo evaluation of cerebellar oxidative stress biomarkers were subsequently performed.ResultsGC-MS identified eugenyl acetate (43.08%), eugenol (18.86%), and β-caryophyllene (1.64%) as the predominant metabolites. The prioritized phytometabolites exhibited favorable predicted ADME-Tox profiles and notable binding energies for MAO-B (up to −8.4 kcal/mol) and 5-LOX (up to −6.8 kcal/mol), involving key interactions with HIS367 and HIS372 in MAO-B and GLN363, TYR435, and CYS172 in 5-LOX, comparable to the standard ligands. Although ascorbic acid exhibited greater radical-scavenging and ferric-reducing potencies than SEAF in the DPPH and FRAP assays, respectively, SEAF showed higher total antioxidant activity than ascorbic acid. In vivo, SEAF administration was relatively safe up to 2000 mg/kg and significantly increased cerebellar superoxide dismutase and catalase activities while reducing malondialdehyde levels in HgCl2-treated mice, with effects comparable to vitamin E. SEAF preserved cerebellar cortex histoarchitecture and Nissl substance in Purkinje cells against mercuric chloride-evoked oxidative damage.ConclusionSyzygium aromaticum exhibited potent antioxidant effects through combined radical scavenging, enzymatic enhancement, modulation of oxidative stress-related targets, and preservation of cerebellar histoarchitecture, supporting its potential as a candidate for further preclinical development.Keywords: antioxidants, cerebellum, oxidative stress, phytomedicine, Syzygium aromaticum

Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, are the leading causes of disability and mortality worldwide. Oxidative stress is a central unifying mechanism in its pathogenesis, affecting quality of life.1,2 The brain is particularly vulnerable to oxidative damage owing to its high oxygen consumption, abundance of redox-active metals, and enrichment in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), which are prone to lipid peroxidation.3,4 Within the brain, the cerebellum is especially susceptible to this vulnerability, as its Purkinje neurons carry an unusually high mitochondrial and oxidative metabolic burden that renders them intrinsically prone to redox imbalance and oxidative stress-driven degeneration.5,6 When reactive oxygen species (ROS) overwhelm endogenous antioxidant defenses, oxidative modifications of lipids, proteins, and DNA accumulate, driving neuronal dysfunction and death.3,7

Lipid peroxidation is a self-propagating chain reaction initiated by ROS attack on PUFAs, generating reactive aldehydes, such as malondialdehyde (MDA) and 4-hydroxynonenal. MDA serves as both a biomarker and an effector of oxidative injury, forming adducts with proteins and DNA that impair mitochondrial function and trigger neuroinflammation.8 Neurons rely on a coordinated enzymatic antioxidant system to counteract ROS. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) catalyzes the dismutation of the superoxide anion (O2∙−) to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), while catalase (CAT) and glutathione peroxidase decompose H2O2 to water. Imbalances in SOD or CAT activity permit H2O2 accumulation, which, in the presence of Fe2+, drives Fenton chemistry and hydroxyl radical (∙OH) formation, amplifying lipid peroxidation and MDA production.9

In addition to mitochondrial ROS leakage, specific enzymes contribute to the oxidative burden in the CNS. Monoamine oxidase-B (MAO-B), localized to the outer mitochondrial membranes of astrocytes and serotonergic neurons, deaminates dopamine and other monoamines, producing H2O2 as a stoichiometric byproduct.10,11 Age-related upregulation of MAO-B elevates basal H2O2, and MAO-B inhibitors such as safinamide are clinically used to reduce oxidative stress in Parkinson’s disease.12,13 Similarly, 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX) catalyzes the conversion of arachidonic acid to leukotrienes, generating lipid hydroperoxides and promoting neuroinflammation.14 5-LOX activation is elevated in Alzheimer’s disease models and contributes to both oxidative damage and amyloidogenesis,15 making it a dual target for neuroprotection. Zileuton, a selective 5-LOX inhibitor, reduced brain leukotriene levels and improved cognitive outcomes in preclinical models.16

Plants have gained attention as multi-target agents that simultaneously scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) and modulate pro-oxidant enzymes.17,18 Syzygium aromaticum (clove) is rich in eugenol and β-caryophyllene, phytochemicals with documented antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties.19 Eugenol donates hydrogen atoms to neutralize radicals and inhibits MAO-B in modern in silico and in vitro models, whereas β-caryophyllene binds cannabinoid receptor 2 and has been implicated in LOX modulation.20–22

Despite these promising potentials, most studies on S. aromaticum have assessed only in vitro radical scavenging or isolated enzyme inhibition, without integrating chemical profiling, target-specific docking (for MAO-B and 5-LOX), or in vitro and in vivo antioxidant evaluations within a single framework. Thus, a critical gap exists in linking the phytochemical composition of S. aromaticum to its neuroprotection against lipid peroxidation and enzyme-mediated oxidative stress, particularly in the cerebellum owing to its susceptibility.

Therefore, the present study aimed to provide an integrated neuropharmacological evaluation of S. aromaticum ethyl acetate fraction by combining Gas chromatography and mass spectrometry (GC-MS) metabolite profiling, in silico ADME-TOX screening, molecular docking against MAO-B and 5-LOX, in vitro 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP), and Total Antioxidant Capacity (TAC), cerebellum biochemical antioxidant (SOD, CAT, and MDA) assays, and histological Nissl staining in the mouse cerebellum. These findings will inform the design of neurobehavioral studies and immunohistochemical evaluations to validate the translation of antioxidant effects to neuropathological pathways.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Drugs

All the solvents and reagents used were of analytical grade. Ethyl acetate was obtained from Central Drug House Ltd. (New Delhi, India), dichloromethane from Loba Chemie Pvt. Ltd. (Mumbai, India), ethanol and methanol from Zayo Sigma (Paderborn, Germany), and n-hexane from ChemLab (Zedelgem, Belgium). Ammonium molybdate, sodium phosphate, Tween 80, sulfuric acid, ascorbic acid, 2,4,6-tripyridyl-s-triazine (TPTZ), 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), and Cresyl Fast Violet acetate stain were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Corp. (St. Louis, MO, USA). Vitamin E (E-Nat 400 IU capsules) was procured from Mega Lifesciences Ltd. (Samutprakam, Thailand), and mercuric chloride was obtained from Span Chemie (Mumbai, India).

Study Location

The study was conducted at the Kampala International University, Western Campus (KIU-WC), Ishaka Municipality, Bushenyi District, and Western Uganda (GIS coordinates: Lat. - 0.538393, Long. 30.144510). Experimental procedures were carried out in the Animal House (Department of Pharmacology), Institute of Biomedical Research (IBR) laboratory, and the Histology Laboratory (Department of Anatomy). In silico ADME-Tox and molecular docking studies were performed in the Department of Anatomy postgraduate study room, all in the Faculty of Biomedical Sciences.

Plant Material

Two kilograms (2 kg) of dried Syzygium aromaticum (L). Merr. and L.M. Perry (Family: Myrtaceae) (clove) buds were purchased from the Botanical Garden of the Department of Botany, Ahmadu Bello University (ABU), Zaria, Kaduna State, Nigeria. The buds were identified and authenticated by Dr. Namadi Sunusi, a qualified botanist at the Herbarium of the Department of Botany, ABU, Zaria, and assigned a voucher number (ABUH09184) for future reference.

The plant material was weighed using an electronic weighing balance (WANT Balance Instrument Co. Ltd, China), blended into powder form using an electric blender (ST-5504, Winning STAR, China), and stored before the extraction process in the Pharmacology Laboratory, Faculty of Biomedical Sciences, Kampala International University-Western campus, Ishaka-Bushenyi, Uganda.

Plant Extraction

In the extraction process, a total of 1000 grams (1 kg) of the powdered Syzygium aromaticum buds was sequentially extracted twice with each of the following solvents: distilled water, ethanol (Zayo SIGMA, Germany), ethyl acetate (Central Drug House, India), dichloromethane (Loba Chemie, India), and n-hexane (Chem Lab, Belgium). The solvents were measured using a 1500 mL capacity measuring cylinder (Glass, Borosil Limited, India). The mixture of the solvent and powdered plant material was subjected to sequential filtration (filter paper, 125 mm, Whatman, United Kingdom) of the bottom fluid compartment using a separating funnel (NS 29/32, 1500 mL, FORTUNA, Germany) at each stage of solvent use. This method was adopted as previously described, with some modifications by Rali et al23 In the previous study, methanol was used as the second polar solvent; however, in this study, ethanol was used because methanol is more toxic than ethanol. The crude ethyl acetate fraction of Syzygium aromaticum obtained after solvent recovery was purified by liquid-liquid partitioning and rotary evaporation (Evaporator RE-52CS, ZX, China) to remove residual solvents. The semi-dried extract was subjected to column chromatography using silica gel as the stationary phase and a gradient of increasing solvent polarity (n-hexane to ethyl acetate) as the mobile phase.

Each extraction cycle utilized 1500 mL of solvent, and the mixture was placed on a digital orbital shaker (SK-O180-S, DRAWELL, China) for 48 h before filtration and solvent recovery. A second wash was performed on the same plant material using 1200 mL of the same solvent, followed by shaking for 24 hours. The filtrates from both washes were combined, filtered, concentrated using a rotary evaporator (RE-52CS; ZX, China), and air-dried at room temperature.

GC-MS Characterization

The Syzygium aromaticum ethyl acetate fraction (SEAF) was analyzed using a Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS) system (GC-MS-QP2020 NX, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan) fitted with an HP-5MS column (30m x 0.25 mm x 0.25μm) and helium as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The oven temperature was programmed from 50 °C (held for 2 minutes) to 300 °C at a ramp rate of 10 °C/min.

The sample was introduced in split mode (split ratio 10:1), with the injector temperature set at 250 °C. A solvent delay of 3 min was employed. Mass spectrometric detection was performed using electron-impact ionization at 70 eV, with data acquired in the full-scan mode over the m/z 40–600 range. The retention time per minute, peak area, peak height, and spectral line patterns were used to identify the components present in the sample when compared with spectral lines from the database of authenticated compounds stored in the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) 2020 library.25

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis of the Syzygium aromaticum ethyl acetate fraction identified 64 distinct metabolites based on retention time, peak area, and spectral matching. Of the 64 metabolites identified, 13 with relative peak areas ≥1.0% were selected for ADME-Tox profiling and molecular docking analyses. Among these, the five most abundant compounds were eugenyl acetate (43.08%), eugenol (18.86%), 14-hydroxycaryophyllene (4.94%), 10,10-dimethyl-2,6-dimethylenebicyclo[7.2.0]undecane (3.94%), and β-caryophyllene (1.64%). These compounds primarily belong to key phytochemical classes: phenylpropanoids (eugenol and eugenyl acetate), sesquiterpenes (β-caryophyllene and 14-hydroxycaryophyllene), and terpenoid hydrocarbons (bicyclic sesquiterpene derivatives), reflecting the dominance of bioactive aromatic and terpene-based constituents.26

In Silico Pharmacokinetic, Drug-Likeness, and Toxicity Assessment

After GC-MS characterization, the SEAF phytometabolites with relative peak areas ≥1.0% were selected for ADME-Tox profiling and molecular docking. The pharmacokinetic, drug-likeness, and toxicity profiles of these metabolites from Syzygium aromaticum were evaluated using SwissADME, ADMETlab 3.0, pkCSM, and ADMET-AI. Canonical SMILES were obtained from PubChem and used as inputs.

SwissADME was used to predict absorption and distribution parameters (Gastrointestinal tract (GI) absorption, BBB permeability, P-glycoprotein interaction, and CYP450 inhibition), physicochemical properties, and Lipinski’s Rule of Five for drug-likeness. ADMETlab 3.0, further assessed drug-likeness and ADMET features, including NP score, QED, SA score, GASA, and rule-based filters (Pfizer, GSK, and Golden Triangle).

Toxicity profiling was conducted using ADMETlab 3.0 (organ toxicity and BBB probability), pkCSM (AMES, hERG inhibition, hepatotoxicity, LD50, LOAEL, and environmental toxicity), and ADMET-AI (half-life, clearance, and pathway-related toxicity such as Nrf2, mitochondrial function, and tumor protein 53). This integrated approach enabled concise evaluation of the pharmacokinetic behavior, drug-likeness, and safety profiles of the metabolites.

Identification and Preparation of Ligands and Target Proteins

The ligands used for molecular docking consisted of the predominant metabolites identified from SEAF, together with standard inhibitors (Safinamide, PubChem CID: 131682 for MAO-B and Zileuton, PubChem CID: 60490), and the respective co-crystallized ligands associated with each target protein. The three-dimensional (3D) chemical structures of all ligands were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), accessed on 14th April, 2026, in their Structure Data File (SDF) formats. Each compound was downloaded in its 3D conformational state to ensure compatibility with downstream molecular docking procedures.

The three-dimensional (3D) crystal structures of the target proteins involved in oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation pathways, monoamine oxidase-B (MAO-B; Protein Data Bank (PDB) ID: 2V5Z) and 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX; PDB ID: 6N2W), were obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) on 14th April, 2026, in the PDB legacy formats.

Protein preparation was performed using Biovia Discovery Studio 2021 (v21.1.0.20298, Dassault Systèmes, USA, https://www.3ds.com/products/biovia/discovery-studio). During preprocessing, all water molecules and heteroatoms were removed, and polar hydrogen atoms were added to stabilize the protein structures and improve interaction prediction. Where protein structures contained multiple chains, the chain in which the co-crystallized ligand was bound was selected for docking. Active binding sites were identified, and the resulting chains were chains A and B for MAO-B and 5-LOX, respectively. This was performed to ensure that the docking analysis focused on the relevant active sites. The prepared protein structures were subsequently saved in the PDB format for use in docking simulations.

Molecular Docking

Molecular docking simulations were performed using PyRx virtual screening software (v0.8; Scripps Research Institute, USA; https://pyrx.sourceforge.io/). This software is user-friendly and enables multiligand docking with target proteins. The prepared protein structures were first imported into the AutoDock plugin within PyRx and were defined as macromolecules for docking analysis.

Ligands, including the 13 S. aromaticum metabolites, together with the standard drugs and the respective co-crystallized ligands associated with each target protein, were imported into the Open Babel plugin in PyRx. Within this module, the individual energies of the ligands were minimized to obtain stable conformations. The ligands were subsequently converted to the AutoDock-compatible PDBQT format. After conversion, the ligands were loaded into an AutoDock docking environment.

Before docking, the protocols for both targets were validated using re-docking of unique co-crystallized ligands (Safinamide for MAO-B and NDGA 4-[(2r,3s)-3-[(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)methyl]-2-methylbutyl]benzene-1,2-diol for 5-LOX). These ligands were extracted from the targets and saved in the SDF format using Biovia Discovery Studio. These were minimized and converted into pdbqt formats using the Open Babel plugin in PyRx. They were loaded in the AutoDock console and subsequently docked with the targets (macromolecules). The docked complexes were saved in the PDB format. Validation was performed using Biovia Discovery Studio, where the extracted ligands were pasted in the hierarchy of the docked complex, superimposed using the molecular overlay option, and RMSD values were obtained. When the RMSD value was higher than 2.0 Å, this process was repeated until the RMSD value was less than 2.0 Å. The grid box parameters were noted and applied during the main docking with S. aromaticum metabolites, co-crystallized ligands, and standard drugs.

Docking simulations were conducted using the Vina Wizard plug-in. A docking grid box was defined around the predicted binding pocket of each protein target, ensuring that the grid center coordinates were positioned close to or slightly adjusted relative to the X, Y, and Z coordinates of the Site Directed Binding (SDB) sphere values.

During docking, the grid box center coordinates for the selected targets were defined as X: 53.4726, Y: 147.883, Z: 24.4664 for MAO-B (2V5Z), and X: 35.8124, Y: 67.7845, Z: 44.2518 for 5-LOX (6N2W) to appropriately encompass the active binding sites during molecular docking. The exhaustiveness parameter was set to eight to balance docking accuracy with computational efficiency.

After docking was completed, the Vina wizard automatically generated binding affinity values for all ligand-protein complexes. The docking output files were exported as CSV and converted to Microsoft Excel spreadsheets to ensure data integrity and prevent information loss during further analysis. For each ligand-protein interaction, the best docking pose (Mode 1), corresponding to the lowest binding energy, was selected. The resulting docked complexes were then saved in the PDB format.

Post-docking visualization and interaction analyses were conducted using Biovia Discovery Studio. For each target protein, the three test ligands with the highest docking scores and the top-scoring reference ligand (co-crystallized ligand or standard ligand, as applicable) were selected for two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) protein-ligand interaction analyses and visualization. These interaction diagrams were generated to illustrate the ligand-binding orientation within the active pocket. These visualizations enabled the detailed identification of interaction types (eg, hydrogen bonding, π-π stacking, π-alkyl interactions, and hydrophobic contacts), the specific amino acid residues involved, and the protein chains participating in the interactions. This analysis provided structural insights into molecular interactions.

Radical Scavenging in vitro Assays

DPPH Radical Scavenging Assay

The previously described method was adopted Adeyanju et al27 The free radical scavenging activity of the samples was evaluated using a DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) assay. A 0.3 mM DPPH solution was prepared in 100% methanol. An aliquot of 1.0 mL of DPPH solution was mixed with 1.0 mL of the sample solution (1 mg/mL). For standard comparison, ascorbic acid solutions were prepared at concentrations (0.03125 to 1.0 mg/mL) and treated similarly.

The reaction mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min to prevent photodegradation. Absorbance was measured at 517 nm against a reagent blank containing methanol instead of the sample. A methanol vehicle control containing DPPH reagent and methanol, but without SEAF or ascorbic acid, was included to account for background absorbance and potential solvent interference.

Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power Assay

The ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) of SEAF was determined according to a previously described method with a modification of the standard used by Donoso-Bustamante et al28 Ascorbic acid was used as previously described Schlesier et al29 The FRAP reagent was freshly prepared by mixing 25 mL of 30 mM acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 2.5 mL of 10 mM 2,4,6-tris(2-pyridyl)-s-triazine (TPTZ) dissolved in 40 mM hydrochloric acid, and 2.5 mL of 20 mM ferric chloride (FeCl3).

FRAP reagent (270 μL of the FRAP reagent) was combined with 30 μL of each compound solution prepared in 100% methanol in a microplate. A methanol vehicle control containing the FRAP reagent and methanol, but without SEAF or ascorbic acid, was included to account for background absorbance and potential solvent interference. The mixtures were incubated at 20 °C for 30 min, after which absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a microplate reader.

Total Antioxidant Capacity Assay

The total antioxidant capacity of the S. aromaticum ethyl acetate fraction (SEAF) was determined using a previously described phosphomolybdenum method Adeyanju et al27 This method is based on the reduction of molybdenum VI to molybdenum V (Mo V) and subsequent formation of a green phosphate/Mo V complex under acidic conditions.

A stock solution of each sample was prepared at a concentration of 1 mg/mL in distilled water. The sample solution (1.0 mL) was mixed with 1.0 mL of reagent solution containing 0.6 M sulfuric acid, 4 mM ammonium molybdate, and 28 mM sodium phosphate in a stoppered test tube. A distilled-water blank containing the assay reagent without SEAF was used to correct for background absorbance, and absorbance was measured at 695 nm against this blank. The reaction mixture was incubated in a water bath at 95 °C for 90 minutes, then allowed to cool to room temperature.

Ascorbic acid standard solutions were prepared at different concentrations (20–100 µg/mL) and were processed under identical conditions. A standard calibration curve was constructed by plotting the absorbance against ascorbic acid concentration. The antioxidant capacities of the samples were expressed as ascorbic acid equivalents (AAE).

Acute Toxicity Test and Dose Determination

The acute oral toxicity of Syzygium aromaticum ethyl acetate fraction (SEAF) was evaluated using the up-and-down procedure in accordance with OECD guideline 425.30 Six adult female albino mice (Mus musculus) were used in this study.31 A maximum dose of 2000 mg/kg body weight was administered orally, as the toxicity profile of the plant’s ethyl acetate fraction has not been previously established. Animals were observed individually for clinical signs of toxicity or mortality within the first 30 min post-administration, periodically during the first 24 h, and daily thereafter for 14 days. A limit dose of 2000 mg/kg was considered safe. These signs include changes in the skin, fur, eyes, mucous membranes, behavior, and neurological functions (reduced locomotion, tremors, convulsions, salivation, diarrhea, lethargy, sleep, and coma).

Dose selection for the main study was based on the outcomes of the acute toxicity test, applying a geometric progression of LD50 fractions (1/2, 1/4, and 1/8; 50%, 25%, and 12.5%), as previously adopted by Jama et al31 Based on the limit dose of 2000 mg/kg, the corresponding doses were defined as high (1000 mg/kg), medium (500 mg/kg), and low (250 mg/kg). The dose of mercuric chloride (HgCl2) (2 mg/kg body weight) was adopted with a slight modification in dosage from Gu et al32 Vitamin E was administered orally by gavage at a dose of 200 mg/kg body weight, based on a previously reported protocol with modification from Shahidi et al33 However, the route of administration was modified from intraperitoneal injection to oral gavage in our study.

Stock solutions were prepared before administration. Mercuric chloride was prepared by dissolving 0.025 g (25 mg) in 50 mL distilled water. A 2% Tween 80 solution was prepared by mixing 2 mL of Tween 80 with distilled water to a volume of 100 mL. The S. aromaticum ethyl acetate fraction (SEAF) was prepared by dissolving 1 g of the extract in 10 mL of 2% Tween 80, and vitamin E was prepared by dissolving 0.5 mL (one capsule, 268 mg) in 10 mL of 2% Tween 80.

The administered volumes of mercuric chloride, SEAF, and vitamin E were calculated individually for each animal based on body weight and the respective doses of mercuric chloride (2 mg/kg), SEAF (250, 500, and 1000 mg/kg), and vitamin E (200 mg/kg).

Animals

Thirty-six adult male albino mice were used in the present study. The animals were aged 6–8 weeks and weighed between 20 and 32 g. The sample size (n = 36) was determined using the resource equation method as previously described by Arifin and Zahiruddin.34 A projected attrition rate of 20% was adopted from Charan and Kantharia.35 This was in line with OECD recommendations for minimal animal use and reduced risk exposure.

All animals were housed in standard polypropylene/polycarbonate cages (290 × 220×140 mm; floor area, 388 cm2), with a maximum of three mice per cage, providing a minimum floor space of approximately 129 cm2 per mouse. Animals were maintained under controlled environmental conditions of ambient temperature (22 ± 2 °C) and relative humidity (50–60%), with a 12-hour light/dark cycle applied throughout the experimental period. During the 30-day study, a reverse light/dark cycle was implemented, adopted from a previous study by Said et al36 The animals had ad libitum access to a standard pellet diet and clean drinking water ad libitum.

Experimental Design

Following a 7-day acclimatization period, the animals were randomly assigned to six experimental groups as follows: Group 1 received 5 mL/kg of 2% Tween 80 adopted from a previous study by Welu et al37 Group 2 received HgCl2 at 2 mg/kg body weight adopted from a previous study by Gu et al32 Group 3 received 250 mg/kg of S. aromaticum ethyl acetate fraction (SEAF) + HgCl2 (2 mg/kg). Group 4 received 500 mg/kg SEAF + HgCl2 (2 mg/kg). Group 5 received 1000 mg/kg SEAF + HgCl2 (2 mg/kg). Group 6 received Vitamin E (200 mg/kg body weight) adopted from Shahidi et al33 + HgCl2 (2 mg/kg).

All treatments were administered by oral gavage for 30 consecutive days.36 In all the groups receiving SEAF or Vitamin E in combination with HgCl2, the extract or Vitamin E was administered first, followed by HgCl2 after 1 hour, as adopted from a previous study by Orheruata and Tobalu.38

All experimental procedures were conducted in compliance with the care and use of laboratory animal guidelines of the School of Veterinary Medicine and Animal Resources Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (SVAR-IACUC), Makerere University, Uganda.

At the end of the experimental period, the animals were euthanized by intraperitoneal administration of Ketamine and Xylazine (80 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg body weight, respectively) in line with the American Veterinary Medical Association guidelines.39 To complete the process, the administration was followed by cardiac puncture through a midline incision in the lower thoracic region as described by Adeniyi et al40 There were checks for lack of respiration, heartbeat, and no response to the hind paw pinch. Following confirmation of euthanasia, a midsagittal cranial incision was made to expose and carefully excise the cerebellum. The excised organs were immediately weighed and processed for subsequent analyses. Portions designated for oxidative stress assays were homogenized in phosphate buffer under appropriate conditions as previously described by Jama et al.31

All 36 animals were humanely euthanized in accordance with the IACUC guidelines. In line with the pre-planned study design, tissue samples from five animals per group were randomly selected for the planned analyses. In contrast, one sample per group was retained as an archival contingency specimen for potential repeat analyses or technical validation, if required. This pre-specified approach ensured unbiased sample selection and supported the 3Rs principle of Reduction by avoiding the need for additional experimental animals in the event of repeat analyses or future reference.

In vivo Biochemical Oxidative Stress Assessment

Superoxide Dismutase

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined based on the ability of the enzyme to inhibit the auto-oxidation of epinephrine.41 The reaction mixture consisted of 200 µL of sample supernatant and 2.5 mL of 0.05 M carbonate buffer (pH 10.2). The reaction was initiated by adding 300 µL of freshly prepared 0.3 mM epinephrine, followed by immediate mixing. The reference cuvette contained 300 µL of epinephrine, 2.5 mL of buffer, and 200 µL of distilled water.

The increase in absorbance due to adrenochrome formation was measured at 480 nm at 30-second intervals up to 150 s. SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to inhibit 50% epinephrine auto-oxidation. The rate of increase in absorbance was calculated as (A1 − A0)/2.5, where A0 is the absorbance at 30 seconds and A1 at 150 seconds. Percentage inhibition was determined relative to the blank, and the results were expressed as % inhibition of superoxide-mediated epinephrine auto-oxidation.

Catalase

Catalase (CAT) activity was determined as previously described by Afolabi et al42 Briefly, 200 µL of tissue supernatant was added to ice-cold tubes, and the reaction was initiated by adding 5 mL of 30 mM H2O2. The mixture was gently inverted and allowed to react for 3 min, after which the reaction was terminated using 1 mL of 6 M H2SO4. Subsequently, 7 mL of 0.01 M KMnO4 was added, and absorbance was measured at 480 nm within 30–60 seconds.

Malondialdehyde

Malondialdehyde (MDA), a marker of lipid peroxidation, was determined as previously described by Hacioglu et al43 The assay is based on the reaction of MDA with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) to form thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS), which are measurable spectrophotometrically.

Briefly, 0.5 mL of 10% tissue homogenate was mixed with 3 mL of 1% phosphoric acid (H3PO4) and 1 mL of 0.6% TBA solution. The mixture was heated in a boiling water bath for 45 min, cooled, and then 4 mL of n-butanol was added. After vigorous mixing and centrifugation, the organic (butanol) layer was separated, and the absorbance was measured at 535 and 520 nm.

MDA levels were calculated as the difference (A535 − A520) to correct for non-specific turbidity and expressed as nmol MDA/g tissue.

Histological Evaluation

The Cresyl Fast Violet (CFV) staining was performed to evaluate neuronal integrity and Nissl substance preservation within the cerebellar cortex. CFV selectively stains the rough endoplasmic reticulum and ribosomal RNA of neurons, thereby enabling the assessment of neuronal morphology, Nissl substance integrity, chromatolysis, and neuronal degeneration.44

Following a previous procedure described for CFV (Nissl) staining, sections (5 μm thick) were cut using a rotary microtome (Model HS-3345, Guangzhou MeCan Medical Limited, Gangzhou, China), deparaffinized and rehydrated, then immersed in 0.1% Cresyl Fast Violet solution for 8–10 minutes at 37 °C, differentiated in 95% ethanol with a few drops of glacial acetic acid, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted as described by Kang et al45 The slides were subsequently stained and examined under a light microscope (Olympus, CX23, Tokyo, China) equipped with a camera at a x400 magnification.

Data Analysis

GraphPad Prism (v. 6.01, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for data analysis. For the in vitro radical scavenging assays, the differences between SEAF and ascorbic acid were analyzed using an unpaired t-test. Statistical significance was set at P ≤ 0.05. For in vivo cerebellar oxidative stress evaluation, differences among group means were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Statistical significance was set at P ≤ 0.05. 2D and 3D protein-ligand interactions were analyzed using Biovia Discovery Studio Visualizer, 2021 (v21.1.0.20298, Dassault Systèmes, USA).

Results

In silico ADME-TOX Profiling

Numerical ADME profiling (Figure 1) showed that most SEAF metabolites possessed favorable physicochemical properties, including molecular weight, lipophilicity (iLogP), and topological polar surface area (TPSA), largely within acceptable drug-likeness thresholds. The majority of the compounds demonstrated moderate molar refractivity and hydrogen-bonding capacity, with good bioavailability scores and minimal violations of Lipinski’s rule. Interaction analysis revealed that in MAO-B, binding was predominantly driven by hydrophobic and aromatic interactions involving HIS367, HIS372, LEU368, ILE406, and ALA410. Hydrogen bonding was consistently observed with HIS367, which is a key catalytic residue. The top metabolites, particularly benzyl benzoate (2345) and β-caryophyllene (5281515), closely mimicked the interaction profile of the standard ligand safinamide, sharing critical residues, including HIS367, LEU368, and HIS372. This overlap indicates occupation of the canonical MAO-B binding pocket and suggests potential competitive inhibition.

In 5-LOX, the metabolites exhibited more diverse interaction profiles, combining hydrogen bonding and aromatic stabilization. Key hydrogen bond interactions involved GLN363, HIS367, TYR435, and PRO569, whereas aromatic and hydrophobic interactions were observed with TYR326, TYR398, PHE359, and LEU368. Notably, π–sulfur interactions with CYS172 and occasionally unfavorable donor–donor contacts were also detected. Metabolites such as eugenol (3314) showed interaction patterns comparable to those of the standard (zileuton) and co-crystallized ligand (NDGA), with consistent overlap at TYR398, TYR435, and GLN363.

MAO-B interactions are largely hydrophobic and structurally conserved, whereas 5-LOX binding involves a combination of polar and aromatic interactions. The consistent residue overlap (HIS367 and LEU368 in MAO-B) and (TYR398, TYR435, and GLN363 in 5-LOX) suggested conserved binding site engagement. These findings support the ability of S. aromaticum metabolites to interact effectively with key catalytic residues, suggesting a mechanistic plausibility for multi-target antioxidant and neuroprotective activities.

In vitro Oxidative Stress Results

The in vitro antioxidant activity of the Syzygium aromaticum ethyl acetate fraction (SEAF) was evaluated using DPPH radical scavenging, ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP), and total antioxidant capacity (TAC) assays (Mean ± SEM, N=3, in each of the assays) (Figures 8–10). In the DPPH assay, SEAF exhibited a significantly higher IC50 value (96.93 ± 0.94 µg/mL) than ascorbic acid (72.80 ± 0.35 µg/mL), indicating lower free radical scavenging potency (t = 24.08, df = 4, P < 0.0001). Similarly, SEAF showed a significantly higher FRAP IC50 value (12.70 ± 0.23 µg/mL) compared with ascorbic acid (5.13 ± 0.12 µg/mL), reflecting lower ferric reducing activity (t = 29.06, df = 4, P < 0.0001). In contrast, the phosphomolybdenum assay demonstrated a significantly greater total antioxidant capacity for SEAF (34.07 ± 1.23 µg AAE/mL) than for ascorbic acid (6.63 ± 0.24 µg AAE/mL) (t = 21.83, df = 4, P < 0.0001), indicating a higher overall antioxidant capacity under the assay conditions.

Mercuric chloride (2 mg/kg) exposure was associated with significantly lowered SOD activity compared with the Tween 80 control group (P < 0.0001). SEAF pretreatment at 250, 500, and 1000 mg/kg, as well as Vitamin E (200 mg/kg), significantly attenuated SOD activity relative to the HgCl2-treated group (all P < 0.0001). No significant differences were observed among the three SEAF-pretreated groups and the Vitamin E group (P > 0.05).

Similarly, HgCl2 administration was associated with significantly lowered CAT activity compared with the Tween 80 control group (P < 0.0001). All SEAF doses and Vitamin E significantly increased CAT activity relative to the HgCl2 group (all P < 0.0001). Moreover, the 500 and 1000 mg/kg SEAF-treated groups exhibited significantly higher CAT activity than the 250 mg/kg SEAF group (both P < 0.0001).

Mercuric chloride was associated with significantly higher cerebellar MDA levels compared with the Tween 80 control group (P < 0.0001). Treatment with 500 mg/kg and 1000 mg/kg SEAF, as well as Vitamin E were associated with significantly lowered MDA levels compared with the HgCl2-treated group (all P < 0.0001), whereas the 250 mg/kg SEAF group showed no significant decrease (P > 0.05). No significant differences were detected between the 500 and 1000 mg/kg SEAF groups and the Vitamin E group (P > 0.05).

Overall, SEAF dose-dependently attenuated HgCl2-induced cerebellar oxidative stress by restoring antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD and CAT) and reducing lipid peroxidation (MDA), with the 500 and 1000 mg/kg doses producing effects comparable to those of Vitamin E.

Discussion

In this study, phytochemical profiling, multi-level antioxidant assessment, ADME-TOX prediction, and molecular docking were used to evaluate Syzygium aromaticum metabolites as modulators of oxidative stress. The combination of in vitro radical-scavenging assays, in silico analysis of oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation targets, and in vivo assessment of cerebellar enzymatic antioxidant status provides a better understanding of plant antioxidant mechanisms.

In our study, the moderate yield of the extract suggests the efficient recovery of semi-polar bioactive constituents, which are often enriched in phenolic and terpenoid compounds with recognized antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties.46,47 This finding supports the suitability of ethyl acetate as a partitioning solvent for concentrating pharmacologically relevant plant metabolites. The observed yield was consistent with the selective enrichment of compounds likely to contribute to the bioactivity of the extract, particularly those involved in redox homeostasis and enzyme modulation.48 Although the yield supports extraction efficiency, it is important to note that solvent polarity, plant origin, harvesting conditions, and processing methods may influence extraction performance and phytochemical consistency. This underscores the need for standardized extraction protocols for future translational studies.

GC-MS profiling revealed a chemically rich extract comprising 64 metabolites, with marked dominance of eugenyl acetate, eugenol, and β-caryophyllene. These metabolites include phenylpropanoids and terpenes, which are well established for their redox-modulating and anti-inflammatory properties.49–53 The predominance of these metabolites supports the notion that the extract is chemically positioned to exert potent biological effects via multiple pathways linked to oxidative stress control and cellular protection. The dominance of key metabolites may also improve reproducibility and strengthen translational potential. However, as with many botanical preparations, environmental factors, geographical origin, and postharvest handling may alter the phytochemical composition. This can lead to variations in the phytochemical profiles.

In silico ADME-Tox profiling further demonstrated that the identified metabolites exhibited favorable drug-likeness and safety characteristics, supporting their potential for therapeutic development. These findings are consistent with previous evidence that many plant-derived polyphenols and terpenoids exhibit acceptable pharmacokinetic behavior and relatively low toxicity,19 thereby supporting their relevance as lead compounds in drug discovery. The ADME-Tox results strengthen the confidence that the detected metabolites are not only biologically active, but may also possess properties compatible with systemic application. Although computational predictions cannot replace experimental pharmacokinetic studies, they provide an important initial framework for assessing the absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity profiles before advancing to more resource-intensive validation studies.

Molecular docking analysis provided further mechanistic insight into target engagement, demonstrating that key metabolites, particularly benzyl benzoate and β-caryophyllene, exhibit strong binding affinities with monoamine oxidase B (MAO-B) and 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX). These enzymes play important roles in the propagation of oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation,11,14 and their modulation may contribute to the observed antioxidant and cytoprotective effects. Although the binding affinities of the phytocompounds were lower than that of the standard MAO-B inhibitor, several compounds displayed comparable interactions with the 5-LOX reference ligand, suggesting biologically meaningful selectivity. Interactions with residues such as HIS367 in MAO-B and TYR398/GLN363 in 5-LOX support the engagement of the conserved binding site and reinforce the plausibility of target-based inhibition. These findings align with those of previous studies showing that S. aromaticum and its metabolites may exert antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects by modulating the enzyme systems involved in reactive oxygen species generation and inflammatory mediator synthesis.54–57

Interestingly, the most abundant GC-MS compounds were not necessarily the top docking hits, indicating that compound abundance does not directly determine predicted binding affinity. GC-MS abundance reflects relative concentration and analytical characteristics, whereas docking scores depend mainly on structural compatibility and interactions within the target-binding site. Thus, less abundant compounds such as benzyl benzoate showed stronger predicted binding than more abundant constituents such as eugenol, although this does not confirm greater biological activity. However, despite the value of docking in predicting ligand-target interactions, these findings remain theoretical. Molecular dynamics simulations and experimental kinetic validation are required to confirm true inhibitory activity. In vitro enzyme inhibition assays using SEAF and its major pure constituents (eugenyl acetate, eugenol) to calculate actual IC50 values for inhibitory activities will advance the predictive docking findings.

In the in vitro antioxidant assays, the higher IC50 values of SEAF in DPPH and FRAP assays suggest that pure Ascorbic acid is a more potent direct free radical scavenger and reductant on a per-weight basis. However, the markedly higher TAC value of SEAF indicates that the extract contains a broader range of antioxidant phytochemicals that contribute collectively to total reducing capacity. This discrepancy may be attributed to synergistic effects of multiple compounds in SEAF, such as phenylpropanoids (a sub-class of phenolics) and terpenoids detected by GC-MS, which may be more effectively detected by the phosphomolybdenum method used in the TAC assay than by single-radical assays.

These findings are consistent with previous reports that phenolic-rich S. aromaticum has antioxidant potential, stabilizes free radicals, and interrupts oxidative chain reactions.46,58 The consistency across multiple antioxidant assays strengthens the reliability of this observation and suggests that the extract possesses broad-spectrum antioxidant properties rather than assay-specific activities. Despite these results, some limitations are notable Firstly, the in vitro antioxidant assays were performed using technical replicates rather than independent biological replicates. Although this approach ensured analytical reproducibility, future studies incorporating biological replicates are warranted to enhance robustness and generalizability. Secondly, these assays are performed under non-physiological conditions and do not consider bioavailability, metabolism, intracellular uptake, or tissue-level interactions, all of which are important determinants of biological efficacy in vivo.

In vivo evaluation of oxidative stress biomarkers provided additional evidence of biological efficacy and supported the functional relevance of in vitro antioxidant findings. There was a significant increase in cerebellar superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activity and a reduction in malondialdehyde (MDA) levels. This confirms that S. aromaticum extract enhances endogenous antioxidant defense systems while attenuating lipid peroxidation. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) are critical enzymatic antioxidants that detoxify superoxide radicals and hydrogen peroxide,59 thereby preventing oxidative injury to lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. The reduction in MDA, a marker of membrane lipid peroxidation,8 suggests that the extract limited oxidative damage at the cellular level. The observed effects were comparable to those of vitamin E, an FDA-approved antioxidant.60,61 This underscored the functional potency of SEAF. This result is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that S. aromaticum can restore endogenous enzymatic defenses and reduce oxidative damage in vivo.62,63 However, it should be acknowledged that the assessment was limited to a narrow coverage of oxidative stress biomarkers without detailed molecular or tissue-specific confirmation of protection.

The histological observations using Cresyl Fast Violet (CFV) provide morphological support for the antioxidant and neuroprotective effects of SEAF. Preservation of cerebellar cytoarchitecture and neuronal Nissl substance suggests that the extract limited oxidative injury to neuronal membranes, rough endoplasmic reticulum, and protein-synthetic machinery. This interpretation is consistent with the enhancement of SOD and CAT activities and the reduction in MDA, indicating that improved endogenous antioxidant defense and suppression of lipid peroxidation translated into meaningful protection of cerebellar neurons. Similar relationships between improved antioxidant status and preservation of neuronal morphology have been reported in mercury-exposed rats treated with thymoquinone and Syzygium aromaticum extracts.38,64 The histological evidence therefore complements the DPPH, FRAP, and TAC findings by indicating that the radical-scavenging and reducing capacity of SEAF was associated with tissue-level neuroprotection. Nevertheless, quantitative stereological assessment and molecular markers of neuronal injury and apoptosis are required to confirm the extent and mechanisms of protection.

Nevertheless, the predominantly qualitative histological assessment limits definitive conclusions regarding the magnitude of cerebellar protection. Future studies should incorporate stereological quantification of Purkinje and granule cells, measurements of molecular and granular layer thickness, and blinded histopathological scoring. Immunohistochemical assessment of calbindin and NeuN, together with GFAP, Iba-1, cleaved caspase-3, Bax, Bcl-2, synaptophysin, and PSD-95, would further clarify the effects of SEAF on neuronal survival, glial activation, apoptosis, and synaptic integrity. Behavioral evaluation of motor coordination, balance, gait, learning, memory, and anxiety would help determine whether the observed structural preservation translates into functional neurological benefit. Electrophysiological assessment of Purkinje-cell activity and cerebellar synaptic plasticity could also establish whether the morphologically preserved neurons remain functionally competent.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Syzygium aromaticum exerts antioxidant and neuroprotective effects through a multi-level mechanism involving direct radical scavenging, enhancement of endogenous antioxidant enzyme activity, modulation of ROS-generating enzymatic targets such as MAO-B and 5-LOX, and preservation of cerebellar cytoarchitecture, neuronal integrity, and Nissl substance. The combined evidence from in silico ADME-Tox and molecular docking, in vitro antioxidant assays, in vivo oxidative stress markers, and cerebellar cortex histology provides a coherent and mechanistically plausible basis for the observed bioactivity.

The integrated pattern of evidence strengthens the translational relevance of S. aromaticum and supports its potential as a natural source of antioxidant and therapeutic metabolites. This potential is particularly relevant because mercury exposure remains an important environmental and occupational health concern; however, Syzygium aromaticum (clove) is relatively affordable and widely available, providing a practical basis for developing accessible plant-derived interventions, subject to further validation of efficacy, dosage, bioavailability, and long-term safety. A limitation of this study is that the in vitro and in vivo effects were evaluated using the complex SEAF rather than isolated compounds; thus, the relative contributions and potential synergistic interactions of individual metabolites remain to be established. Also, in vivo evaluation was restricted to the cerebellum and a short-term biochemical timeframe. Therefore, further studies incorporating enzyme kinetics, addressing control gaps by adding SEAF only (1000 mg/kg), Safinamide + HgCl2 or Zileuton + HgCl2 in the in vivo workflow, evaluation of regional brain responses, long-term neuroprotective effects, and functional outcomes, including cerebellar-dependent motor coordination, the testing pure isolated abundant SEAF compounds, pharmacodynamic validation, tissue-level assessment, and long-term safety evaluations are essential to define its therapeutic value.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the ethyl acetate extract of Syzygium aromaticum possesses considerable antioxidant and pharmacological potential, as supported by the integrated in silico, in vitro, and in vivo findings. GC-MS profiling revealed that the metabolite-rich extract was dominated by eugenyl acetate, eugenol, benzyl benzoate, and β-caryophyllene, indicating a concentrated pool of biologically relevant compounds. The in silico ADME-Tox analysis indicated that these metabolites possessed favorable drug-likeness and safety characteristics. Molecular docking demonstrated meaningful interactions between MAO-B and 5-LOX, supporting a plausible multi-target mechanism of action.

Although less potent than ascorbic acid in the DPPH and FRAP assays, SEAF exhibited greater total antioxidant capacity. It significantly enhanced SOD and CAT activities while reducing MDA levels in vivo. These biochemical improvements were corroborated histologically by preservation of Purkinje cell morphology, retention of Nissl substance, and improved cerebellar cortical histoarchitecture in the SEAF-treated groups, with the highest dose exhibiting changes comparable to those of the vitamin E-treated group.

Together, these findings suggest that Syzygium aromaticum exerts coordinated antioxidant effects through direct radical scavenging, reinforcement of endogenous antioxidant defense systems, and modulation of oxidative stress-related enzymatic targets. This multi-level activity may underlie its observed biological efficacy and support its potential as a promising natural source of therapeutic agents against oxidative stress-associated neurological disorders. Nevertheless, the present findings are limited to early preclinical investigation and require further mechanistic, pharmacokinetic, safety, and clinical studies before any therapeutic application can be considered.

Other limitations of this study include reliance on in silico predictions, in vitro assays, and limited oxidative stress biomarkers in vivo. Therefore, future studies should incorporate enzyme inhibition assays, pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic and tissue-level analyses, immunohistochemistry, molecular dynamics simulations, long-term neurodegenerative models, and metabolomic standardization to strengthen the mechanistic claims and translation.
