Dispersive and Strichartz estimates for Dirac equation in a cosmic string spacetime
Piero D’Ancona, Zhiqing Yin, Junyong Zhang

Abstract
. In this work we study the Dirac equation on the cosmic string background, which models a one–dimensional topological defect in the spacetime. We first define the Dirac operator in this setting, classifying all of its selfadjoint extensions, and we give an explicit kernel for the propagator. Secondly, we prove dispersive estimates for the flow, with and without weights. Finally, we prove Strichartz estimates for the flow in a sharp restricted set of indices, which are different from the classical Euclidean ones.



Key Words: Decay estimates, Strichartz estimates, Dirac equation, Cosmic string

AMS Classification:

42B37, 35Q40, 35Q41.

1.
Introduction

A
cosmic string
is a one–dimensional defect line in spacetime that might have formed during the early phases of the universe (see
[
35
,
39
]
). The existence of such objects is predicted by some grand unified theories. In the simplest axisymmetric model, the string lies along the
z
–axis and the defect is described by the metric

ds^{2}=dt^{2}-dz^{2}-dr^{2}-r^{2}d\theta^{2},\qquad 0\leq\theta<2\pi(1-4G\mu)

where
G
is Newton’s gravitational constant and
\mu
is the ‘unit mass’ of the string.
We see that the metric is locally flat but the angular coordinate is restricted to a range smaller than
[0,2\pi]
, giving rise to a flat cone metric in the
(r,\theta)
plane

[
11
,
20
,
21
,
26
,
41
,
42
]
.

In this work we study the Dirac equation on a cosmic string background. We are mainly interested in the effect of the topological defect on the dispersive properties of the Dirac flow. Since the singularity is present only in the transverse dimensions, one can ignore the
z
variable and consider the reduced 2D metric (see
[
29
]
)

dl^{2}=dr^{2}+r^{2}d\theta^{2},\quad-\pi\sigma\leq\theta<\pi\sigma,

(1.1)

where we have set
0\leq 1-\sigma=4G\mu<1
and translated the angular variable.

Dispersive equations perturbed by electromagnetic fields or on a curved background have inspired a great deal of research in recent years. We mention in particular the Aharonov–Bohm (AB) field
[
1
]
, where a charged particle can interact with the field also in those regions with vanishing field. This two dimensional model has a strong similarity with the axisymmetric cosmic string.
Indeed, in both models the singularity is concentrated at the origin, and at least formally the field (for the AB model) or the curvature (for the cosmic string model) is zero away from the origin; however, a physical effect can be measured. In the first case, this is precisely the content of the AB experiment
[
1
]
, while
e.g. in
[
24
]
the effects of a cosmic string spacetime away from the singularity were studied. The dispersive properties of the Dirac equation perturbed by the Aharonov–Bohm field were studied by the authors of the present paper in
[
12
]
.

In physics, the Dirac oscillator has been studied in various contexts including the Aharonov–Bohm field
[
25
]
, chiral phase transitions under a constant magnetic field
[
8
]
, and, more recently, the cosmic string background.
For the Dirac oscillator in a cosmic string spacetime we refer to
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
19
]
, for spinning cosmic strings to
[
28
,
30
]
, and for magnetic cosmic strings in
[
3
,
4
]
. These works motivated us to investigate the decay of Dirac flows in a cosmic string spacetime.

1.1.
Dirac operator on the cosmic string

For a complete derivation of the Dirac equation in curved space-time, we refer to Section 5.6 in
[
35
]
.
The concrete form we adopt here is presented
also in
[
10
, Section 2.1]
.

We use the choice of matrices

\gamma^{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad\gamma^{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
-i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\gamma^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}

(1.2)

satisfying the usual anticommutation relations (see
[
37
]
)

\{\gamma^{a},\gamma^{b}\}=2\eta^{ab}
,

\eta=\mathop{\rm diag}[+1,-1,-1]
.
Recall that the metric of
X
in global radial coordinates

(x_{1},x_{2})=(r,\theta)
is given by

dl^{2}=dr^{2}+r^{2}d\theta
;
we denote derivatives w.r.t. these coordinates by

(\partial_{1},\partial_{2})=(\partial_{r},\partial_{\theta})
.
We introduce the
zweibein
formalism:

e_{1},e_{2}
are the orthogonal vector fields

e_{1}=\partial_{r},\qquad e_{2}=\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}

and the corresponding dual forms are

e^{1}=dr,\qquad e^{2}=rd\theta.

Writing
e^{a}=e^{a}_{j}dx^{j}
, the matrix
e^{a}_{j}

takes the form

\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&r\end{pmatrix}.

The Dirac operator on
X
is defined as

\mathcal{D}=e^{j}_{a}\gamma^{a}D_{j}
, where

D_{j}=\partial_{j}+B_{j}
are
the covariant derivatives for spinors
(see
[
35
, Section 5.6, page 226 (5.270)]
); more explicitly,

B_{j}=\frac{1}{8}\omega^{ab}_{j}[\gamma^{a},\gamma^{b}],\qquad\omega^{ab}_{j}=e^{a}_{i}\Gamma^{i}_{jk}e^{kb}+e^{a}_{i}\partial_{j}e^{ib}.

B_{j}
contains an algebraic part
[\gamma^{a},\gamma^{b}]
,
corresponding to the generators of the underlying Lie algebra
for Dirac bispinors, and the spin connection
\omega^{ab}_{j}
,
which can be computed via Cartan’s first structural equation

de^{a}+\omega^{ab}\wedge e^{b}=0,\qquad\omega^{ab}=\omega^{ab}_{j}dx^{j}.

Since
de^{1}=0
,
de^{2}=\frac{1}{r}e^{1}\wedge e^{2}=-\frac{1}{r}e^{2}\wedge e^{1}
,
we get
\omega^{12}=-\frac{1}{r}e^{2}
and
\omega^{21}=\frac{1}{r}e^{2}
,
and
\omega^{12}_{2}=-1
and
\omega^{21}_{2}=1
. It follows
that
B_{1}=0
and

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\omega^{ab}_{2}[\gamma^{a},\gamma^{b}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\omega^{12}_{2}[\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2}]+\frac{1}{8}\omega^{21}_{2}[\gamma^{2},\gamma^{1}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\omega^{12}_{2}\gamma^{1}\gamma^{2}\qquad\text{(since $[\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2}]=2\gamma^{1}\gamma^{2}$)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{1}\gamma^{2}.

We obtain

e^{j}_{a}\gamma^{a}D_{j}=\gamma^{1}D_{1}+\frac{1}{r}\gamma^{2}D_{2}=\gamma^{1}\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}(\gamma^{2}\partial_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{1})

and in conclusion the Dirac operator takes the form

\mathcal{D}=\gamma^{1}\left(\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{2r}\right)+\frac{1}{r}\gamma^{2}\partial_{\theta}.

(1.3)

The time dependent Dirac equation on
X
is then

[\gamma^{0}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{D}]u\equiv\left[\gamma^{0}\partial_{t}+\gamma^{1}\left(\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{2r}\right)+\frac{1}{r}\gamma^{2}\partial_{\theta}\right]u=0.

(1.4)

Multiplying the equation by
i\gamma^{0}
we obtain the
equivalent Hamiltonian form

i\partial_{t}u=\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}u,

where the operator
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}
now is given by

\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}=\gamma^{2}\left(\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{2r}\right)-\frac{1}{r}\gamma^{1}\partial_{\theta}.

(1.5)

We shall denote by
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{S}_{\sigma}^{1}}

the Dirac operator on
\mathbb{S}_{\sigma}^{1}:=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\sigma\mathbb{Z}
is a circle of radius
0<\sigma\leq 1
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{S}_{\sigma}^{1}}=-\gamma^{1}\partial_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}0&i\partial_{\theta}\\
i\partial_{\theta}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad D_{\mathbb{S}_{\sigma}^{1}}=i\partial_{\theta}.

(1.6)

Then the operator
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-(\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{2r})+\frac{1}{r}D_{\mathbb{S}_{\sigma}^{1}}\\
(\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{2r})+\frac{1}{r}D_{\mathbb{S}_{\sigma}^{1}}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(1.7)

The operator
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{S}_{\sigma}^{1}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{S}_{\sigma}^{1}}\varphi_{k}(\theta)=\lambda_{k}\varphi_{k}(\theta),\quad\varphi_{k}(\sigma\pi)=\varphi_{k}(-\sigma\pi),

(1.8)

where
\lambda_{k}=\frac{k}{\sigma}
and

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}(\theta)=\Big(\begin{smallmatrix}e^{-i\frac{k}{\sigma}\theta}\\
e^{-i\frac{k}{\sigma}\theta}\end{smallmatrix}\Big)

(1.9)

are eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the
(obviously diagonizable)
operator
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbb{S}_{\sigma}^{1}}
.
This leads to the decomposition of
[L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})]^{2}
as

[L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})]^{2}=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}L^{2}(rdr)^{2}\otimes h_{k}(\mathbb{S}_{\sigma}^{1}),

(1.10)

where
h_{k}(\mathbb{S}_{\sigma}^{1})
is the one dimensional space

h_{k}=h_{k}(\mathbb{S}_{\sigma}^{1})=\left[\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\frac{k}{\sigma}\theta}\\
e^{-i\frac{k}{\sigma}\theta}\end{pmatrix}\right]=\left\{c\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\frac{k}{\sigma}\theta}\\
e^{-i\frac{k}{\sigma}\theta}\end{pmatrix}:c\in\mathbb{C}\right\}.

Then any

f=(\begin{smallmatrix}\phi\\
\psi\end{smallmatrix})\in\big[L^{2}(X)\big]^{2}

can be expanded in the form

\phi=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\phi_{k}(r)e^{-i\frac{k}{\sigma}\theta},\qquad\psi=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi_{k}(r)e^{-i\frac{k}{\sigma}\theta}.

The corresponding decomposition of
\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}

is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\sigma}=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}d_{k}\otimes I_{2},\quad I_{2}:=\mathrm{Id}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

(1.11)

where

\displaystyle d_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-(\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{2r})+\frac{k}{\sigma r}\\
(\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{2r})+\frac{k}{\sigma r}&0\end{pmatrix},

(1.12)

and we are reduced to study
the dispersive properties of the equations

i\partial_{t}u_{k}-d_{k}u_{k}=0,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

1.3.
The relation to the previous results

The techniques of the present paper are closely related to
those introduced in
[
12
]
for the 2D Aharonov–Bohm–Dirac
equation, however the setting here is quite different;
indeed, the geometry of the cosmic string spacetime
is more similar to geometry of flat cones. More new ingredients are needed in the proof, for example, in estimating the heat and Schrödinger kernels and obtaining Strichartz estimates.
We recall that the wave diffraction and the dispersion of wave
and Schrödinger equations on flat cones were analyzed in

[
11
,
20
,
21
,
26
,
42
]
.
For the Dirac equation the situation is even harder, due to the rich algebraic structure of the equation. For the scalar Schrödinger operator on flat cones, a natural distinguished selfadjoint extension is available, namely, the Friedrichs extension; this choice is not available for Dirac, since the spectrum is unbounded from below.
Note that the choice of a good selfadjoint extenson is crucial in order to study dispersive properties of the equation, as it is evident from the results presented above.
An additional difficulty arises from the spin structure and the covariant derivative, which introduce variable coefficients on non flat manifolds and make the analysis harder.
To the best of our knowledge, the only available results in this direction are provided in
[
10
,
13
,
15
,
16
,
14
]
, where some local smoothing and Strichartz estimates with angular regularity loss are proved for Dirac equations on asymptotically flat manifolds, and for suitable spherically symmetric manifolds. Recently, in
[
33
]
, the authors studied Strichartz estimates for the Dirac equation on hyperbolic spaces. The Strichartz estimates proved in this paper present no loss of regularity, but the range of admissible pairs is influenced by the choice of the selfadjoint extension.

A brief outline of the paper. In Section
2
we
classify all selfadjoint extensions of the Dirac operator on a cosmic string. In Section
3
we construct the necessary tools to represent the Dirac flow in this setting, and the actual construction of the propagator is carried out in Section
3.3
, where an explicit representation of the kernel is obtained. The last two
Sections
4
and
5
are dedicated to the proofs of the main results i.e. dispersive and Strichartz estimates.

2.
Self-adjoint extensions

In this section we prove that the symmetric radial Dirac operators

defined in (

, and allow a one–parameter family of selfadjoint extensions for

Recalling the decomposition (

(4.25)

A_{N,\nu}=N^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\|\varphi(\frac{\rho}{N})\mathcal{H}_{\nu}g(\rho)\|_{L^{2}_{\rho d\rho}},

(5.44)

applying the Schur test argument with (
5.43
) we obtain

\Bigl\|\sum_{N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}\omega_{\nu}(R)Q(NR)A_{N,\nu}\Bigr\|_{\ell^{2}_{R}}\leq(\mathcal{Q}_{1}\mathcal{Q}_{2})^{1/2}\|A_{N,\nu}\|_{\ell^{2}_{N}}.

Thus we have proved

\|\omega_{\nu}(r)(T_{\nu}g)(t,r)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R};L^{\infty}_{dr})}^{2}\leq C_{\nu}\sum_{N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}|A_{N,\nu}|^{2}=C_{\nu}\|g\|_{\dot{H}^{1-\frac{1}{p}}}^{2}.

\bullet

If
\nu=-\frac{1}{2}
we make the following adjustments to the above argument.
On the one hand, if
NR\lesssim 1
one has

\omega_{\nu}(R)Q(NR)=\frac{(NR)^{\nu-\frac{\epsilon}{2}}}{1+R^{\nu-\epsilon}}=\frac{(NR)^{\nu-\epsilon}}{N^{\nu-\epsilon}+(NR)^{\nu-\epsilon}}(NR)^{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}N^{\nu-\epsilon}\leq(NR)^{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}N^{\nu-\epsilon}.

On the other hand, for
NR\geq 1
we have

\omega_{\nu}(R)Q(NR)=\frac{(NR)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}}{1+R^{\nu-\epsilon}}=\frac{N^{\nu-\epsilon}}{N^{\nu-\epsilon}+(NR)^{\nu-\epsilon}}(NR)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}\leq(NR)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}N^{\nu-\epsilon}.

Then, taking
\epsilon>0

is sufficiently small so that
1-\frac{1}{p}+(\nu-\epsilon)\geq 0
, we obtain the following bounds

N^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\omega_{\nu}(R)Q(NR)\leq N^{1-\frac{1}{p}}N^{\nu-\epsilon}\widetilde{Q}(NR)\leq\langle N\rangle^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\widetilde{Q}(NR)

where
\langle N\rangle=(1+N^{2})^{1/2}
and

\tilde{Q}(NR)=\begin{cases}(NR)^{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}&\ \text{if}\ NR\lesssim 1\\
(NR)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{2}}&\ \text{if}\ NR\gg 1,\end{cases}

(5.45)

which implies

\|\omega_{\nu}(r)T_{\nu}g(t,r)\|_{L^{p}_{t}L^{\infty}_{dr}}^{2}\leq\sum_{R\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}\biggl(\sum_{N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}\langle N\rangle^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\tilde{Q}(NR)\left\|\varphi(\frac{\rho}{N})\mathcal{H}_{\nu}g(\rho)\right\|_{L^{2}_{\rho d\rho}}\biggr)^{2}.

We use the previous argument again with replace

Q(NR)
by
\tilde{Q}(NR)
and
A_{N,\nu}
by
\tilde{A}_{N,\nu}
which defined by

\tilde{A}_{N,\nu}=\langle N\rangle^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\left\|\varphi(\rho/N)\mathcal{H}_{\nu}f(\rho)\right\|_{L^{2}_{\rho d\rho}}

then we get

\|\omega_{\nu}(r)T_{\nu}f(t,r)\|_{L^{p}_{t}L^{\infty}_{dr}}^{2}\leq C_{\nu}\sum_{N\in 2^{\mathbb{Z}}}|\tilde{A}_{N,\nu}|^{2}=C_{\nu}\|f\|_{H^{1-\frac{1}{p}}}^{2}.

Then we proved the estimate (
5.31
). Choosing appropriate
q
in (
5.31
) and (
1.23
) (which will be proved in the Subsection
5.2
) respectively we will conclude the proof by using the complex interpolation.
