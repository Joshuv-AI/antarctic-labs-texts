Autonomous Uncertainty Quantification for Computational Point-of-Care Sensors
Authors: Artem Goncharov, Rajesh Ghosh, Hyou-Arm Joung, Dino Di Carlo, Aydogan Ozcan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Computational point-of-care (POC) sensors enable rapid, low-cost, and accessible diagnostics in emergency, remote, and resource-limited areas that lack access to centralized medical facilities. These systems can use neural network-based algorithms to accurately infer diagnoses from signals generated by rapid diagnostic tests or sensors. However, neural network-based diagnostic models are subject to hallucinations and can produce erroneous predictions, posing a risk of misdiagnosis and inaccurate clinical decisions. To address this challenge, here we present an autonomous uncertainty quantification technique developed for POC diagnostics. As our test bed, we used a paper-based, computational vertical flow assay (xVFA) platform developed for rapid POC diagnosis of Lyme disease, the most prevalent tick-borne disease globally. The xVFA platform integrates a disposable paper-based assay, a hand-held optical reader, and a neural network-based inference algorithm, providing rapid and cost-effective Lyme disease diagnostics in under 20 min using only 20 μL of patient serum. By incorporating a Monte Carlo dropout (MCDO)-based uncertainty quantification approach into the diagnostics pipeline with minimal computational and memory overhead, we identified and excluded erroneous predictions with high uncertainty, significantly improving the sensitivity and reliability of the xVFA in an autonomous manner, without access to the ground truth diagnostic information on patients. Blinded testing using new patient samples demonstrated an increase in diagnostic sensitivity from 88.2% to 95.7%, indicating the effectiveness of MCDO-based uncertainty quantification in enhancing the robustness of neural network-driven computational POC sensing systems.

Introduction

Point-of-care (POC) sensors represent an emerging class of diagnostic devices designed to enable rapid and inexpensive patient testing in emergency, underserved, and remote settings that lack access to specialized equipment and highly trained medical personnel. − A common example of a POC sensor is a paper-based lateral flow assay (LFA) − a disposal rapid diagnostic test that typically provides diagnostic results in <20 min using a single droplet of patient sample, such as whole blood, serum, , saliva, or urine. Conventional LFAs are primarily designed for single-analyte testing and can be interpreted visually by minimally trained or untrained users through the inspection of test and control lines within the active sensing region. In contrast, more advanced paper-based POC sensing systems have also emerged to support multiplexed testing, , enabling the parallel detection of multiple biomarkers or characterization of complex immune responses. − Such assays generate high-dimensional nonlinear signals that are not readily interpretable by human users and therefore require integration with specialized readers and computational algorithms to process sensor outputs and infer diagnostic outcomes. These computational POC sensors are particularly beneficial in scenarios that require clinician oversight, since test results generated at the POC can be remotely shared with physicians, who can then provide diagnostic interpretation and follow up clinical consultation.

Computational POC sensing platforms frequently employ advanced machine learning algorithms, such as neural networks, to accurately learn complex functional relationships between multiplexed sensor signals and underlying diagnostic states. ,,− Despite their powerful function approximation capabilities, neural networks used in computational POC sensors are susceptible to hallucinations or unreliable predictions, which may result in erroneous diagnostic outcomes and compromise the reliability of computational biomedical sensing systems, potentially limiting their acceptance in clinical practice. , To address these challenges, uncertainty quantification , techniques have been developed to estimate prediction uncertainty and improve the reliability of neural network-based diagnostics decision-making. In the context of computational POC sensing, uncertainty quantification can be applied to autonomously evaluate the confidence of individual diagnostic predictions and perform reliability testing that determines whether a given test outcome should be trusted or notall without access to ground-truth patient information. As a result, each diagnostic outcome can be supplemented by a specific reliability action: “Trust” or “Do not use” (Figure ). A “Trust” action indicates that the sensor has successfully passed the autonomous reliability assessment and the corresponding neural network prediction is considered trustworthy, whereas a “Do not use” action signifies that the prediction failed the reliability check and should be excluded from decision-making. Samples that fail this reliability testing step can undergo a follow up testing.

Overview of the computational point-of-care sensing pipeline with uncertainty quantification. The pipeline outputs a reliability action for each sensor, classified as either “Trust” or “Do not use”. A “Trust” outcome indicates that the sensor measurement has passed reliability testing and that the corresponding diagnostic prediction can be used in the clinical decision-making process. In contrast, a “Do not use” outcome signifies that the test result is unreliable and that the associated diagnostic prediction should be excluded from the diagnostic workflow.

Monte Carlo dropout (MCDO) is a widely used Bayesian uncertainty quantification approach that applies stochastic dropout to the weights of a trained baseline neural network during the blind inference phase. , MCDO primarily captures model-related (epistemic) − uncertainty, while also indirectly reflecting data-related (aleatoric) uncertainty. , In MCDO, multiple forward passes with randomly sampled dropout masks are applied to each test sample during the blind testing stage, producing a distribution of predictions that can be compared against the baseline model output to quantify predictive uncertainty. Based on this uncertainty estimation, a reliability action“Trust” or “Do not use”can be assigned to the baseline prediction in an autonomous manner, without access to the ground truth information (Figure ). The MCDO method has been successfully applied to quantify uncertainty and improve predictive performance in various computational diagnostic systems, including quantitative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and optical coherence tomography (OCT). However, while MCDO demonstrated effectiveness in imaging-based systems, its applications to computational POC sensing platforms remain unexplored.

MCDO-based uncertainty quantification method for a classification neural network model. The baseline model score is compared with the distribution of scores generated by N MCDO models to perform uncertainty quantification in an autonomous manner, without access to ground truth patient information. Based on this comparison, each sample undergoes reliability assessment and is assigned one of the two reliability actions: “Trust”, indicating that the prediction has passed the uncertainty testing, or “Do not use”, indicating that the prediction has failed the test and should be excluded from the clinical workflow.

Here, we demonstrate MCDO-based uncertainty quantification for reliability assessment of computational POC sensing systems. As a test-bed, we utilize a paper-based multiplexed vertical flow assay (xVFA) for rapid and cost-effective diagnosis of Lyme disease (LD). , The xVFA is a rapid diagnostic platform that performs multiplexed serological analysis using a droplet of patient serum, providing diagnostic results in <20 min. It incorporates a 2D sensing membrane containing spatially separated immunoreaction spots that generate optical signals in response to antibody/antigen binding, which are subsequently imaged using a custom-designed portable optical reader and processed by deep learning-based algorithms to produce accurate diagnostic prediction (Figure a,b). The xVFA platform has been previously applied to detect various disease biomarkers, including cardiovascular disease biomarkers, ,,, COVID-19, and LD, ,, demonstrating its versatility as a POC diagnostic technology. In this work we use as our test-bed LDthe most common tick-borne disease globallycaused by Borrelia burgdorferi bacterium that is transmitted to humans through the bite of infected ticks (Ixodes scapularis). The current gold-standard laboratory diagnostics recommended by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) rely on a tedious and costly two-tier testing procedure consisting of a first-tier enzyme immunoassay (EIA) followed by a second-tier Western blot (WB) or a second EIA. This multistep testing strategy suffers from limited sensitivity during early stage LD, when antibiotic treatment is most effective at preventing bacteria dissemination across the human body. In addition, two-tier testing requires centralized laboratory infrastructure, leading to prolonged turnaround times and reduced accessibility to timely diagnostics, particularly in remote and rural areas where the LD prevalence is higher. To address these challenges of the two-tier testing process, a single-tier POC diagnostics platform for LD was developed based on the paper-based xVFA technology, enabling rapid and accessible LD diagnostics in POC settings. ,, This paper-based, cost-effective xVFA platform detects LD in under 20 min using a 20 μL droplet of patient serum. The assay incorporates a 2D sensing membrane functionalized with a curated panel of LD-specific peptides (Figure a), ,− which selectively bind to human antibodies developed in response to the infection, enabling sensitive and specific serological detection. Following assay operation, an image of the activated sensing membrane is captured using a hand-held smartphone-based optical reader and processed by a neural network-based inference algorithm, in which a pretrained network model analyzes multiplexed assay signals to infer LD diagnostic outcomes , (Figure b).

To provide an autonomous reliability assessment of the xVFA-based LD POC diagnostic platform, we developed an MCDO-based uncertainty quantification framework integrated into the diagnostic pipeline. In addition to processing each xVFA sample with the baseline diagnostic neural network, termed the Lyme model (L0), we performed inference using N = 100–1000 MCDO models (Ln, n = 1, ..., N), generating a distribution of prediction scores for each patient serum sample. By comparing the baseline prediction scores with the corresponding distribution of MCDO model scores, we introduced an uncertainty figure of merit (F) that autonomously quantifies diagnostic prediction uncertainty and excludes test samples with high uncertainty values from the decision-making process (see Figure c, Table S1). Applying the MCDO-based uncertainty quantification pipeline to blinded patient samples improved LD diagnostic sensitivity from 88.2% to 95.7%, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed uncertainty quantification approach in enhancing the reliability of computational POC sensing systems. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first application of an MCDO-based uncertainty quantification technique to a computational POC sensing platform. As AI-enabled sensors continue to advance and become integrated into clinical workflows, the development of robust, autonomous reliability assessment techniques will be essential to enable the smooth translation of these technologies into real-world settings and support their adoption among patients and medical professionals.

Results and Discussion

xVFA Platform for Lyme Disease Diagnostics

The xVFA platform integrates a paper-based sensor, a hand-held optical reader, and a deep learning-based LD diagnostics algorithm, providing single-tier testing of LD in less than 20 min using a single droplet of patient serum. This cost-effective assay utilizes a vertical flow architecture that supports a 2D array of immunoreaction spots, enabling the incorporation of tens of individual reaction spots (channels) across the sensing membrane. These spots are functionalized with synthetic peptides containing B. burgdorferi-specific epitopes that efficiently bind human IgM and IgG antibodies developed in response to Lyme infection. In total, the LD sensing membrane contains 25 spots precoated with 9 distinct peptides in duplicate (i.e., 18 in total), along with 3 positive control spots and 4 negative control spots (Figure a, also see the “Fabrication of the xVFA for Lyme disease” section in Methods for details). The assay operation takes ∼ 16 min and incorporates two sequential steps: a sample loading step where human antiborrelia IgM and IgG antibodies bind to the peptide-functionalized spots, and a signal generation step where gold nanoparticle (AuNP) conjugates react with human immunoglobulins, producing colorimetric optical signals (see the “Operation of xVFA for Lyme disease” section in Methods Section). The AuNPs used in the LD xVFA have ∼40 nm diameter and absorb light in visible spectrum with a peak absorption in the 520–560 nm range. Following assay completion, an image of the activated sensing membrane is captured using the optical reader and colorimetric intensities from the immunoreaction spots are extracted from the image by a custom segmentation code executed on a computer (Figure S1). Finally, these extracted signals are processed by a neural network model to infer the LD diagnostic outcome for each patient (Figure b); see the Methods Section for details. The use of multiplexing in the xVFA design and the selected peptide panel is crucial for LD diagnostics since combining peptides that target multiple B. burgdorferi-specific epitopes reduces cross-reactivity with other bacterial infections while maintaining high diagnostic sensitivity for LD.

Before developing the autonomous uncertainty quantification framework to improve the reliability of neural network-based LD diagnostic inference, we first focused on selecting an appropriate baseline POC network model. Among various performance metrics for POC sensing systems, diagnostic sensitivity is often considered the most critical from the patient’s health standpoint. False negative predictions, which lower the sensitivity of a test, leave infected individuals undetected and untreated, potentially leading to disease progression and associated complications, unless follow-up testing yields a true positive result. In the context of LD, the detection of earlier-stage infections is particularly beneficial due to the effectiveness of antibiotic-based treatments at these earlier time periods, whereas undiagnosed infections can progress to long-term health complications, including neurological, cardiac, or rheumatoid manifestations. The negative implications of an increased false positive rate (which lowers specificity) for a POC test are less of a concern since additional testing beyond the initial POC screening would often be performed, which can mitigate false positive decisions. Therefore, to explicitly demonstrate the impact of uncertainty quantification on diagnostic performance, we intentionally focused on improving the sensitivity of the LD POC test in an autonomous manner, without access to ground-truth patient information. Accordingly, we selected a baseline neural network model, termed Lyme model (L0), that utilized all the xVFA spots (including nine peptides) as input features to the network architecture; see Figure . Specifically, the inputs to L0 represent 25 absorption signals calculated by normalizing xVFA signals captured after assay activation by the corresponding background signals captured before activation (see “Handheld optical reader and image pre-processing” section in Methods Section for details). The L0 prediction scores range from 0 to 1, reflecting the output of the sigmoid activation function applied at the output layer of the LD diagnostic network; see the Methods Section. The final diagnostic classification is performed by comparing the L0 score to a score threshold of 0.5: samples with scores below 0.5 are classified as Lyme-negative, while samples with scores equal to or above 0.5 are classified as Lyme-positive.

The architecture of L0 was selected based on its performance on a validation test set consisting of 93 activated xVFA cartridges from 31 patient serum samples obtained from the Lyme Disease Biobank (LDB). This selected model (L0) demonstrated a specificity of 97.4% (95% CI: 86.8–99.5%, where CI refers to the confidence interval) and a relatively low sensitivity of 81.5% (95% CI: 69.2–89.6%) on the validation data set, as shown in Figure c and Table S2 (also see “Baseline Lyme model architecture and training” section in Methods for details on the architecture and training of L0). The same model (L0) was subsequently evaluated on an independent blind testing data set consisting of 87 xVFA samples from 29 different patients collected from the same biobank, demonstrating a specificity of 100% (95% CI: 90.4–100.0%) and a sensitivity of 88.2% (95% CI: 76.6–94.5%; see Figure c, Table S3).

MCDO-Based Autonomous Uncertainty Quantification for POC LD Testing

Both the xVFA and the L0 model are subject to uncertainty due to multiple sources of noise within this computational sensing system. For instance, variations in the chemical sensor assembly and operation, sample matrix effects, and nonuniform sample flow through stacked paper layers increase measurement noise, contributing to aleatoric uncertainty. In parallel, the relatively small training data set and stochastic variability between patient cohorts introduce epistemic uncertainty into the L0 model. While normalization of colorimetric signals by background signals partially mitigates aleatoric effects, such as illumination nonuniformities, variations between sensing membrane batches and minor sensor misalignments during assay imaging, other sources of uncertainty still persist, highlighting the need for an uncertainty quantification pipeline to enhance the robustness and reliability of xVFA-based diagnostic predictions. Therefore, we developed an MCDO-based uncertainty quantification framework to improve sensitivity and overall reliability of the baseline LD diagnostic model in an autonomous manner, without access to patient ground truth information. In this pipeline, each xVFA sample under test is processed by N = 1000 MCDO Lyme models (Ln, n = 1, ..., N), where each model has the same architecture and weights as the L0 model, however, applies a different random dropout mask with a 10% dropout rate to L0 during the blind inference phase (see “Development of the MCDO-based uncertainty quantification pipeline” section in Methods Section for details). Figures a and a show histograms of the prediction scores generated by these Ln models (blue) overlaid with the corresponding L0 outputs (green and pink) for the validation and blind testing data sets, respectively, with samples ordered from top to bottom by increasing L0 scores (see “Generation of Monte Carlo dropout model histograms” section in Methods for more details). Similar to L0 outputs, prediction scores generated by Ln models also span the [0:1] range.

The distributions of Ln scores are compared against the corresponding L0 predictions on the validation data set to define a quantitative uncertainty metric. By analyzing the absolute difference between the L0 and the average Ln prediction scores, we observed that the gap between these two sets of scores increases as the L0 output approaches the decision threshold of 0.5. At the same time, the majority of false negative predictions in the validation data set had L0 scores clustered around the same threshold. Therefore, to quantitatively capture prediction uncertainty and potentially improve the predictive performance of the L0 diagnostic model, we introduced a decision uncertainty figure of merit (F) as follows: F=1|B0−<MC>| where B0 is the L0 prediction score (i.e., B0 = L0(x)) and <MC> is the average score of Ln models defined as <MC>=1N∑n=1NLn(x) where Ln(x) are the prediction scores of individual MCDO Lyme models (n = 1, ..., N), and x is the array of xVFA input signals fed into the models.

Based on this definition, a low F value for a given patient sample indicates high predictive uncertainty, as the prediction score from L0 and the average score generated by Ln models significantly diverge. Accordingly, we introduced an uncertainty figure of merit threshold (Fth) as a cutoff to autonomously identify and exclude patient samples with low F values that are deemed unreliable due to elevated prediction uncertainty. Within this framework, uncertainty quantification for an xVFA sample is performed by computing its F value and comparing it to Fth. Each sample is then assigned one of the two reliability actions: “Trust” or “Do not use”. Samples with F values greater than or equal to Fth are classified as reliable (“Trust”), and the corresponding Lyme diagnostic predictions from L0 are acceptable for clinical interpretation. In contrast, samples with F values less than Fth are labeled as unreliable (“Do not use”), and the associated L0 predictions are excluded from decision-making (Figure c, Table S1).

The uncertainty threshold Fth was optimized on the validation data set by selecting a single cutoff value that removed a substantial fraction of false predictions at the expense of missing a minimal number of correctly classified samples. By using Fth = 8.5 and excluding samples with lower F values, we filtered out 6 of the 11 misclassified samples in the validation data set at the expense of only 3 correctly classified true negative predictions (see Figure b and Figure S2a). This reliability testing step filtered out 5 false negatives and 1 false positive predictions, improving the validation test sensitivity of L0 from 81.5% to 89.8% and increasing the overall validation accuracy from 88.2% (95% CI: 80.0–93.3%) to 94.0% (95% CI: 86.8–97.4%, Figure c, Table S2). The L0 prediction scores for the excluded samples span a broad range between 0.24 and 0.59, indicating substantial variability in the baseline model output scores, and this variability limits the ability to assess model reliability from the prediction scores alone. In contrast, MCDO-based uncertainty quantification framework provides a quantitative metric (F) that efficiently filters out a significant portion of false predictions, irrespective of their baseline scores.

After developing the MCDO-based uncertainty quantification framework and optimizing Fth on the validation data set, we applied this pipeline to an independent blind testing cohort to evaluate its performance on previously unseen patient samples. All patient samples from this blind testing set were processed using the same operation protocols and preprocessing steps as the samples from training/validation set; however, they were completely new to the L0 model and the uncertainty quantification framework, providing unbiased/blinded evaluation of our approach. Applying the Fth = 8.5 cutoff to the blind testing samples excluded 4 of the 6 false negative predictions, resulting in an increase in the blind testing sensitivity from 88.2% to 95.7% and an improvement in overall LD test accuracy from 93.1% (95% CI: 85.8–96.8%) to 97.4% (95% CI: 85.5–98.8%) (see Figure b,c, Figure S2b and Table S3). These performance gains were achieved at the expense of excluding a small fraction of the correctly classified samples, specifically 1 true positive and 4 true negative predictions. To evaluate the repeatability of the MCDO-based uncertainty quantification, the optimized threshold Fth was repeatedly applied to the blind testing data set over 100 iterations, each involving inference of N = 1000 Ln scores and sample-wise calculation of F values. Across these repeats, the computed F values showed minimal variability, with an average coefficient of variation (CV) of 1.4% across all blind testing samples and <1% for samples with F < 10 (Figure S3). Figures S4–S7 further show distributions of the Ln scores and the corresponding F values over 100 repeats for 4 representative blind testing samples, demonstrating strong consistency of F values. Importantly, the overall performance after applying uncertainty quantification remained unchanged across all repeats providing identical accuracy and sensitivity improvements as reported in Figure c. These results indicate that performance gains are primarily driven by the behavior of the L0 and Ln models, with minimal impact from the stochastic run-to-run variability of MCDO.

Although we used N = 1000 Ln models to generate a well-sampled distribution of prediction scores, we note that substantially smaller numbers of Ln models can also achieve comparable performance on the blind testing data set with good repeatability and a reduced computational cost. For example, using N = 100 Ln models with the same uncertainty threshold (Fth = 8.5) filtered out 5 false negative predictions in the validation set, and 4 false negative samples in the blind testing set, resulting in the same level of performance improvement in the blind testing sensitivity, achieving 95.7% with a strong repeatability across 100 testing repeats (Figures S8 and S9). Generating N = 1000 MCDO predictions for 87 blind testing samples required ∼14.5 s of computation time, whereas the same process for N = 100 only took ∼1.6 s, reducing the inference time by over an order of magnitude (Figure S10a). Nevertheless, both inference times are negligible compared to the total xVFA operation time (∼16 min). In addition, the xVFA platform is primarily targeting decentralized and personalized LD testing, where testing batch sizes are typically small (on the order of 1–10 samples per run), further reducing inference times (i.e., to <10 s for N = 1000 and <1.2 s for N = 100, Figure S10b,c). Therefore, a primary motivation for using a lower N in POC settings is not computational speed but rather practical considerations related to data storage and system constraints. In settings with limited data storage capacity, such as remote clinics lacking connectivity to centralized data centers or cloud computing services, data might be stored locally on benchtop computers or directly on the optical reader hardware, and lower N values may be beneficial to minimize data storage requirements and simplify data management.

In the classical formulation of MCDO, identical dropout rates are applied during both the training and blind inference stages to maintain consistency of the variational distribution over network weights and support a Bayesian interpretation. In practice, however, different dropouts can also be used, since stochastic forward passes still generate a distribution of predictions that captures model sensitivity to weight perturbations and enables meaningful uncertainty estimation. In our case, all N = 1000 MCDO Lyme models also utilized a different dropout rate for training (i.e., 50%) vs the MCDO blind testing phase (i.e., 10%). To investigate the stability of MCDO-based uncertainty quantification pipeline for different dropout percentages, we also evaluated MCDO dropout rates ranging from 1% to 90%. For consistency, all experiments used the same L0 model trained with a 50% dropout rate. For each MCDO dropout rate, the uncertainty threshold Fth was independently optimized using the validation data set. For dropout rates in the 1–75% range, we observed only minor variations in the optimal Fth values as well as in the overall performance of the uncertainty quantification pipeline on the blind testing data set (see Figures S11–S13a). In contrast, larger dropout rates (≥40%) resulted in more significant variability, characterized by a broader distribution of F values for false negative samples and increased exclusion of true negative predictions (Figure S13b). Furthermore, a dropout rate of 90% caused a substantial shift of F values toward lower ranges for both true negative and false negative predictions, with a significantly broader distribution of F values for false negatives. As a result, a cutoff value of Fth = 4 excluded only 1 false negative sample at the expense of excluding 4 true negatives, yielding a modest sensitivity improvement of 1.8%. To partially address this behavior for larger dropout rates, we introduced two uncertainty thresholds, FthLow and FthHigh , and excluded samples with F values falling between these boundaries (i.e., FthLow≤F ≤ FthHigh , see Figure S11c,f). This approach increased the sensitivity gain to 3.9% (Figure S14), however, due to a significant mismatch in the F value distributions between the validation and blind testing data sets and the scattered distribution of F values for misclassified samples, the uncertainty quantification pipeline with a 90% dropout rate exhibited an inferior performance (see Figure S13a,b). Overall, these results indicate that lower dropout rates (≤30%) are preferable for MCDO-based uncertainty quantification in our POC sensing system, as excessively large dropout percentages induce unstable discrepancies between the performances of L0 and Ln.

In fact, the preference for lower dropout rates in MCDO stochastic forward passes is not specific to the selected L0 model architecture but rather represents a general trend for shallow neural network models. To illustrate this, we applied the same MCDO-based uncertainty quantification pipeline across different baseline models with varying architectures and regularization settings, using dropout rates from 1% to 90%. Specifically, we evaluated three models with different regularization parameters (i.e., Models 1–3 with regularization parameters of 0.1, 0.001, and 0.0001, respectively) and two models with different architectures (i.e., Models 4 and 5 with [256, 128, 64] and [512, 256, 128, 64] units, respectively). Across all models, higher dropout rates consistently yielded lower F values (Figure S12) and an increased exclusion of true negative samples, indicating inferior generalization to the blind testing set and less stable behavior of the figure of merit F (see Figure S13). In addition, higher dropouts reduced the repeatability of the MCDO-based uncertainty quantification pipeline, as reflected by higher variability between performance metrics across 100 repeated runs on the blind testing set (see, e.g., Figure S13c,f,h,j). These results indicate that, for compact inference models, selection of lower dropout rates improves stability by avoiding large deviations between baseline (L0) and MCDO models (Ln) predictions, while higher dropouts lead to relatively unstable behavior for the uncertainty quantification pipeline.

The MCDO-based uncertainty quantification pipeline is flexible and can be adapted to alternative definitions of figures of merit based on the distribution of Ln scores. To showcase this, we compared the proposed figure of merit F with several distribution-aware metrics, including standard deviation (Fσ), predictive entropy (FH), and mutual information (FMI) based figures of merit (see Supplementary Note 1 for details). For each metric, the optimal threshold was independently determined using the training data set (Figure S15) and subsequently applied to the blind testing set. For a fair comparison, all metrics were optimized and blindly tested using the same Ln models with a 10% dropout rate and identical training and blind testing sets. Our analyses indicate that the distribution-based metrics (Fσ, FH, FMI) consistently yielded inferior level of improvements in the blind testing performance compared to F. Specifically, sensitivity improvements were 5.5%, 1.8%, and 5.3% for Fσ, FH, and FMI, respectively, compared to >7% achieved with F. Varying dropout rates in the Ln models between 1% and 90% did not significantly improve the performance of these alternative metrics, with sensitivity and accuracy gains remaining under 7% and 4%, respectively (Figure S16). The superior performance of F likely comes from its incorporation of both L0 and Ln prediction scores, whereas the alternative figures of merit that we analyzed rely solely on the distribution of Ln outputs and may therefore be less aligned with the behavior of the baseline L0 model.

Real-world implementation of MCDO-based uncertainty quantification will involve routine testing on samples collected across different time periods, biobanks, and geographical regions, making the evaluation of its generalization performance essential for clinical translation. To test its temporal generalization, we applied the uncertainty quantification pipeline to a longitudinal data set comprising repeated measurements of 2 control samples (1 LD-positive and 1 LD-negative) over 60 days. For this data set, the L0 model achieved 95.8% accuracy, with 2 false positive predictions that were successfully identified autonomously and excluded by the uncertainty quantification pipeline (Figure S17). Future work will incorporate temporally diverse samples into the L0 training set to improve further the robustness and temporal generalization of the uncertainty quantification pipeline.

Moreover, to evaluate generalization across different biobanks, we tested the uncertainty quantification pipeline using blinded patient samples obtained from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) biobank. For this analysis, the baseline model and the figure of merit threshold were trained and optimized on 141 samples from both LDB and CDC biobanks, yielding an updated baseline model L0CDC with a new threshold, FthCDC =3.8. On an independent blind testing set consisting of 135 samples from both biobanks, the L0CDC model achieved an accuracy of 88.1% and a sensitivity of 76.8%, which improved to 91.8% and 83.9%, respectively, after applying our uncertainty quantification pipeline. This improvement was achieved at the expense of removing 6 true negative samples (<10% of all true negatives), demonstrating reliable interbiobank generalization (Figure S18). In contrast, the original threshold Fth = 8.5, optimized solely on LDB data, showed a relatively low generalization to the CDC data set, removing over 50% of true negative predictions. These results highlight that, to achieve optimal generalization of the uncertainty quantification framework, the original training data set should ideally be expanded to include patient samples from multiple biobanks, across various collection time periods, and from diverse geographical regions. Such an expansion would enable the generation of a unified uncertainty threshold (or a set of thresholds) capable of supporting reliable and autonomous uncertainty quantification across diverse patient cohorts. As the xVFA platform and its associated neural network models are extended to broader applications and target populations, the uncertainty quantification pipeline should be calibrated accordingly to maintain consistent, reliable performance across all sample groups and diagnostic applications.

Even though sample numbers in the training/validation and blind testing sets are limited (i.e., 93 training/validation and 87 blind testing samples), the reported performance improvements are statistically significant, with a substantially higher error rate within the excluded samples relative to the retained ones (Fisher’s exact test, p < 0.001 for both data sets, see Supplementary Note 2 for details). The overlap of the 95% CI before and after applying the MCDO-based uncertainty quantification pipeline is expected for paired evaluations on the same samples and does not compromise the reported performance gains, as confirmed by the Fisher’s exact test (p < 0.001). Nevertheless, several sources of distributional shifts and generalization limitations should be considered in future studies to comply with regulatory guidelines for platforms with integrated AI/ML software (i.e., AI/ML-enabled [or -embedded] software as a Medical Device [SaMD]). Specifically, future prospective multisite validation will need to incorporate patients across geographically and demographically diverse sites, span different LD stages (e.g., early- and late-stage LD), and include relevant cross-reactive diseases (e.g., syphilis, mononucleosis, autoimmune conditions). To adhere with regulatory guidance, real-world deployment of both the L0 model and the MCDO-based uncertainty quantification will follow concrete expansion pathways: (i) curating a diversified multicenter training set to mitigate population-specific biases; (ii) submitting a Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP) to prospectively define the model and uncertainty threshold updates as new patient data are accumulated; (iii) implementing postmarket continuous performance monitoring to support early detection of data set shifts and trigger threshold recalibration under the PCCP; and (iv) integrating analytical validation studies to ensure reagent stability and inter-reader and interoperator consistency. These practices will allow the proposed MCDO-based uncertainty quantification technique to mature from a proof-of-concept demonstration to a regulatory-grade reliability assessment layer for next-generation computational POC sensing systems in clinical use.

In clinical practice, samples assigned a “Do not trust” reliability action can undergo retesting using the following suggested two-step procedure. First, two additional images of the same assay (i.e., without taking a new serum sample) should be captured and analyzed, yielding 3 successive diagnostic predictions and reliability actions for the same test. If at least two actions are “Trust” and their predictions agree, the result is considered reliable. Otherwise, the patient proceeds to the second step, where a repeat test is performed with a new xVFA cassette, generating 3 additional predictions. If all three predictions pass the reliability check and agree with each other, the diagnostics result is deemed trustworthy; if not, the patient is referred to a standard two-tier testing procedure in a central lab. In settings with limited access to this testing, a repeat xVFA testing after ∼2 weeks may be recommended to allow for a potential increase in antibody titers. In this two-step retesting procedure, the first step primarily accounts for imaging-related uncertainty due to assay misalignment or illumination nonuniformity, while the second step addresses uncertainty arising from the assay assembly and sample flow. Prediction outcomes from such repeated tests can also be utilized to enable multilevel reliability assessment by supplementing each LD diagnostic prediction with a continuous confidence score rather than a binary “Trust”/”Do not use” label. For example, when all diagnostic predictions agree, the confidence score can be defined as the ratio of predictions labeled “Trust” to the total number of measurements (i.e., 3–6). Such a framework could enable stratification of diagnostic confidence and can guide follow-up testing decisions to maintain a balance between testing throughput and timely clinical intervention. Future work will also focus on extracting additional diagnostic information from repeat patient testing and assessing its clinical relevance, including the use of majority voting across replicates to obtain patient-level inference. Another direction to explore is nonlinear aggregation schemes, such as combining replicate-level signals into a single inference model or pooling replicate predictions weighted by their F values, to capture inter-replicate dependencies that may not be accessible through simple linear aggregation methods.

Besides uncertainty quantification, another strategy to improve the diagnostic accuracy and sensitivity of xVFA, especially at the early stages of LD, is the incorporation of quantitative antibody measurements. Tracking the dynamics of IgM/IgG antibody concentrations can help reveal early stage infections when absolute antibody titers remain below the thresholds for binary diagnostics. In addition, longitudinal tracking of antibody concentrations post-treatment may provide insights into the risks of reinfection and bacteria dissemination. Although we have not directly performed LD antibody quantification, our prior work has applied xVFA for the quantification of biomarkers in serum samples at concentrations comparable to the early stage LD antibody titers, which stay in ∼1–10 ng/mL range, suggesting that the xVFA platform can be adapted to quantify LD antibody concentrations at different stages of LD. For detection of even lower antibody titers during the earliest stages of infection, xVFA can be further enhanced using high-sensitivity modalities such as fluorescence, Au-ion reduction-based amplification, and CL, as established in our previous studies.

MCDO-based uncertainty quantification can be readily extended to quantitative xVFA platforms, ,,, which similarly rely on shallow, fully connected neural networks for biomarker quantification. In this context, the concentration predicted by the baseline model can be compared with the distribution of concentrations generated by MCDO models, allowing for the definition of a figure of merit based on the absolute difference between deterministic and stochastic concentration predictions, analogous to the classification case that we reported in this work. This uncertainty quantification pipeline can then be used to identify and exclude samples with large prediction inconsistencies or poor repeatability. Performance gains can be evaluated using quantification metrics such as linearity (i.e., R2) and CV before and after applying uncertainty quantification. Importantly, this approach is compatible with quantitative xVFA systems employing different optical detection modalities (e.g., colorimetric, fluorescence, or signal amplification techniques). As the xVFA continues to mature and transition into clinical settings, dedicated modality-specific uncertainty quantification pipelines can be further developed to ensure robust and reliable performance across a diverse set of assay configurations and applications.

The MCDO-based uncertainty quantification framework demonstrated in this work requires access to the baseline Lyme model, L0, to generate uncertainty estimates by using the dropout strategy. Consequently, implementation of MCDO-based uncertainty quantification should be integrated into the diagnostic pipeline by the POC sensor developers/providers during the development stage, with a predetermined value of N. As the xVFA sensor and its associated neural network models evolve, the uncertainty quantification algorithm would need to be continuously updated and revalidated to ensure reliable performance across successive generations of POC sensors. As an alternative to this approach, model-free uncertainty quantification methods that enable reliability testing without direct access to the underlying diagnostic model are also important to consider, as they can facilitate the broader adoption of uncertainty quantification techniques by both sensor developers and end-users. Such model-free uncertainty quantification approaches would offer appealing alternatives to model-based methods, including the MCDO-based approach presented in this work, especially in scenarios where access to the baseline diagnostic models is limited or proprietary.

While current work focused on MCDO-based uncertainty quantification, alternative techniques, including deep ensembles, Bayesian neural networks, temperature scaling and input perturbation, could also be applied to improve the reliability of the xVFA platform. Our selection of MCDO is primarily motivated by its computational efficiency and minimal memory requirements relative to most other methods. In MCDO, uncertainty is estimated using stochastic forward passes through a single, shallow neural network, enabling rapid inference (e.g., < 10 s for N = 1000 forward passes) with minimal storage overhead, typically <100 kB for shallow models. In contrast, although deep ensembles can tackle both epistemic and aleatoric uncertainties, they require training and storing multiple independent models, leading to significantly higher computational and memory costs. Similarly, Bayesian neural networks introduce additional computational complexity due to weight distribution sampling, increasing both training and inference costs and typically requiring ≥ 2x memory overhead, depending on the parametrization of the weights. Temperature scaling and input perturbation methods are computationally lightweight and memory-efficient but primarily capture aleatoric uncertainty and do not account for epistemic (model) uncertainty, which is highly relevant in our settings due to limitations in training data diversity and potential distribution shifts across sample cohorts. From a practical standpoint, MCDO-based uncertainty quantification can enable a triage-style decision logic, where predictions assigned to a “Do not use” action are withheld from clinical reporting and routed for repeat testing, while confident predictions (labeled as “Trust”) are immediately used to guide diagnosis and follow-up treatment. This strategy is particularly valuable in POC settings, where access to confirmatory laboratory tests is limited and each misclassification can have a disproportionate impact on the diagnostic workflow. By efficiently capturing meaningful epistemic uncertainty with minimal additional memory and low computational cost, MCDO further allows uncertainty estimates to be computed on the same edge device that runs the baseline diagnostic model. The continuous sample-wise figure of merit F can also be stored alongside diagnostic predictions to support real-time monitoring of interbatch, interdevice, and interoperator variations and to inform PCCP-driven threshold recalibration as patient data accumulate. Together, these properties make the proposed MCDO-based uncertainty quantification technique particularly well-suited for POC settings.

Conclusions

In this work, we demonstrated an uncertainty quantification method based on MCDO to quantify diagnostic uncertainty and improve the performance of neural network-based computational POC sensing systems. As a proof-of-principle demonstration, we applied this approach to a multiplexed vertical flow assay (xVFA) platform for Lyme disease diagnostics, achieving an improvement in blind testing sensitivity from 88.2% to 95.7%. To the best of our knowledge, this work represents the first application of MCDO-based uncertainty quantification techniques to a computational POC sensing platform.

The proposed approach demonstrated robust generalization across independent data sets, including temporally diverse samples and multiple biobanks, supporting its relevance to real-world diagnostic scenarios. By enabling the identification and exclusion of unreliable predictions with minimal computational and memory overhead, this method enhances diagnostic robustness while remaining compatible with resource-limited, decentralized settings. Providing an explicit uncertainty measure alongside diagnostic predictions is critical to improve trust and facilitate the clinical adoption of computational POC sensors. Overall, these results highlight the potential of uncertainty-aware computational sensing to advance the reliability and clinical utility of next-generation POC diagnostic systems.

Methods

Ethical Statement

All human serum samples used in this study were from existing specimen stored before this work and were deidentified prior to our analysis. The samples were acquired by Lyme Disease Biobank with informed consent under Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval Advarra IRB protocol Pro00012408. All experiments are in compliance with the University of California, Los Angeles’s institutional review board.

Fabrication of the xVFA for Lyme Disease

Peptide Panel Design

The sensing membrane was functionalized with nine distinct synthetic peptides, each printed in duplicate. The panel comprised modVlsE-FlaB, Var2FlaB, BBA64–7, OspC1, OppA4, p35–21, ErpP59, Flil-B4, and BBA73 (196–99). These peptides were selected based on procedures established in previous studies. ,− Briefly, B-cell epitope mapping was performed to identify epitopes specific to B. burgdorferi proteins, including OspC, DbpA, DbpB, BBK32, ErpP, p35, OppA2, RecA, LA-7, FlilB, BBA64, BBA65, BBA66, and BBA73. Synthetic peptides containing the identified epitopes were then screened by ELISA using samples from LD patients, healthy controls, and individuals with clinically similar conditions. From this analysis, 18 peptides demonstrating optimal sensitivity and/or specificity for LD were selected and further evaluated using the xVFA platform with a panel of 12 well-characterized serum samples (6 LD-positive and 6 LD-negative). Correlation analysis across these samples was subsequently performed to refine the panel. Peptides exhibiting strong intercorrelation were excluded, while those showing higher variability among LD-positive samples and lower redundancy were retained. This process resulted in the final selection of a nine-peptide panel.

Preparation of the Multiplexed Sensing Membrane

The sensing membrane was fabricated by wax printing 25-spot patterns onto a nitrocellulose membrane sheet (0.22 μm pore size, Bio-Rad) using a ColorQube 8580DN printer (Xerox). Following printing, the membrane sheet was incubated in a forced-air convection oven at 120 °C for 30 s to melt the wax, forming hydrophobic barriers around the hydrophilic reaction spots. Each sensing membrane was then functionalized by conjugating nine peptides onto the reaction spots via physical adsorption. Peptides (0.8 μL, 1 mg/mL) were manually pipetted in duplicate onto designated spots. Of the remaining seven spots, three were loaded with goat antimouse IgG (0.8 μL, 0.1 mg/mL, Southern Biotech) as positive controls, while four were left unmodified to serve as negative controls. The membrane sheet was subsequently dried in an oven at 37 °C for 15 min and then immersed in a 1% (w/w) BSA solution for blocking (30 min, room temperature). After blocking, the sheet underwent a second drying step (15 min, 37 °C), followed by cutting into individual sensing membranes using a razor. The prepared membranes were stored in a humidity-controlled cabinet until use.

Assembly of the xVFA

All paper-based components of the xVFA for LD were fabricated following protocols described in our previous work. ,,− , Briefly, the xVFA comprised two modules, a top and a bottom module, each consisting of vertically stacked functional paper layers enclosed within a 3D-printed housing. The top module included seven layers arranged from top to bottom as follows: sampling membrane, vertical flow diffuser, first spreading layer, double-sided foam tape, interpad, second spreading layer, and supporting layer. The interpad, vertical flow diffuser, and supporting layer were pretreated with a 1% BSA solution (in PBS) to reduce nonspecific binding. The bottom module consisted of five layers, including the sensing membrane, multiple absorption pads, and double-sided foam tape. The number of absorption pads was optimized to accommodate up to 1 mL of liquid volume. All paper layers were precisely cut using a laser cutter (Trotec Speedy 100), and both the top and bottom housings were fabricated using a Formlabs 3D printer.

Conjugation of AuNPs to Anti-Human IgM and IgG Antibodies

Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs; Ted Pella, #15707) were conjugated to mouse antihuman IgM and IgG antibodies (Abcam) via passive adsorption. Briefly, 100 μL of 0.1 M borate buffer (pH 8.4) and 20 μL of antibody solution (0.5 mg/mL; 10 μL each of IgM and IgG) were added to 1 mL of AuNP suspension (40 nm, 1 OD) in a sterile Eppendorf tube. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 1 h, followed by the addition of 100 μL of 1% BSA (in PBS) as a blocking agent. After blocking for 30 min at 25 °C, the solution was further incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. To remove unbound antibodies, the conjugates were centrifuged (15 min, 4 °C) at 8000 rpm and washed three times with 1 mL of washing buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 7.2). The resulting pellet was resuspended in 100 μL of borate buffer (0.1 M, pH 8.4) containing 0.1% BSA and 1% sucrose. The final concentration of the antibody–AuNP conjugates was determined by measuring optical density at 525 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy 2 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader, BioTek Instruments, Inc.).

Operation of xVFA for Lyme Disease

Prior to assay operation, all paper layers were fabricated using a laser cutter (Trotec) and a wax printer (Xerox), and then vertically assembled within the top and bottom housings of the xVFA cartridge. Before starting the assay, an image of the unused sensing membrane was acquired using the optical reader to record baseline background signals. The top and bottom modules were then assembled using a twisting mechanism, and the assay proceeded in two sequential stages. The first stage (sample loading) involved three steps: (i) addition of 200 μL of running buffer to activate the device, (ii) introduction of a 20 μL serum sample, allowing B. burgdorferi-specific antibodies to bind to the peptide-functionalized reaction spots, and (iii) addition of 200 μL of washing buffer to remove unbound antibodies and proteins. After an 8 min washing period, the initial top module was removed and replaced with a second top module to begin signal generation. In the second stage, 200 μL of running buffer, 50 μL of 40 nm AuNP-conjugated detector antibodies (a 1:1 mixture of mouse antihuman IgM and IgG), and 200 μL of washing buffer were sequentially introduced. This enabled visualization of antiborrelia antibodies through specific binding between human immunoglobulins and AuNP-labeled detector antibodies. After an additional 8 min, the second top module was opened, and an image of the developed sensing membrane was captured using the hand-held optical reader.

Hand-Held Optical Reader and Image Pre-Processing

The optical reader used for xVFA readout consisted of a smartphone (LG G7 ThinQ) integrated with a custom 3D-printed optical attachment fabricated using Object 30 (Stratasys) and Ultimaker 3 (Ultimaker) 3D printers. This attachment was designed to securely hold the smartphone, an external optical module and xVFA cartridges to ensure stable and reproducible assay imaging. The optical module incorporated 4 green LEDs arranged in a circular pattern to provide uniform illumination of the sensing membrane, and an external lens to enable optimal focusing of the smartphone camera onto the sensing membrane plane. The LED peak emission wavelength (532 nm) was selected to match the peak absorption range of the AuNPs labels (520–560 nm) used for colorimetric signal generation. The LEDs were polished from the front to further improve illumination uniformity across the sensing membrane area. The 3D-printed attachment was also equipped with optical posts for pedestal installation, enabling both hand-held and benchtop operation. The xVFA sensing membrane imaging was performed using the standard LG smartphone camera application operated in manual mode, with imaging parameters set to ISO 50, an exposure time of 0.625 ms, and autofocus enabled.

For each assay, the optical reader captured images of the sensing membrane twice: before and after the assay was activated. Captured images were processed using a custom segmentation code on a computer to extract per-spot colorimetric signals by averaging pixel intensities within circular masks overlaid with the spots (Figure S1). Final absorption (colorimetric) signals from the 25 immunoreaction spots were calculated according to xij=1−sijbij where bij and sij are the segmented per-spot signals before and after the xVFA activation, respectively, the index i refers to the type of immunoreaction condition (9 peptide conditions, positive control, and negative control), and the index j refers to the spot repeat within the given condition. These absorption signals (x) were used as inputs to the Lyme diagnostic models.

Baseline Lyme Model Architecture and Training

The baseline Lyme diagnostic model (L0) represents a shallow fully connected binary classification neural network. The model used all 25 absorption signals (x) extracted from the xVFA sensing membrane as input features. The network architecture was selected through a hyperparameter grid search using the validation data set of clinical samples. The selected LD diagnostic model included two hidden layers (256 and 64 units), each with ReLU activation functions and L2 regularization (λ = 0.01). Each hidden layer was followed by a batch normalization layer and a dropout layer with a dropout rate of 50% (which was only used during model training). The output layer contained 1 unit with a sigmoid activation function to generate a prediction score in the [0:1] range. The model was trained using a binary cross-entropy loss function, compiled with the Adam optimizer, a learning rate of 1e-2, and a batch size of 8. The binary cross-entropy loss is defined as LBCE=−1Nb∑i=1Nb(yilog(yi′)+(1−yi)log(1−yi′)) where N b is the batch size, y i are the ground truth labels (“0” for LD negative and “1” for LD positive), and yi′ are the prediction scores (yi′∈[0:1]) generated by the sigmoid activation function: yi′=11+e−ŷi where ŷ i is the model’s inference before the sigmoid function. The final diagnostics prediction per sample was determined by comparing the yi′ score with the decision threshold of 0.5. Diagnostics predictions for samples with yi′ < 0.5 were assigned to Lyme-negative, while the predictions for the samples with yi′ ≥ 0.5 were assigned to Lyme-positive.

The L0 model was trained and optimized on the training/validation data set consisting of 93 xVFA samples activated with 31 unique patient serum samples. The optimized L0 model was then blindly tested on 87 xVFA samples from 29 different patients that were never seen by the model during the training and optimization stages. Due to the limited data set size, the same data set was used for both training and validation of the L0 model, since conventional cross-validation approaches can overfit to small validation subsets, resulting in unreliable performance on unseen data. Importantly, the blind testing set, consisting of samples never used during L0 training and optimization, provides an unbiased assessment of model performance. Prior to the application of MCDO-based uncertainty quantification, L0 achieved a sensitivity of 81.5% and a specificity of 97.4% on the validation data set (Figure c). The L0 model was further blindly tested on 87 xVFA samples from 29 different patients (never seen before), achieving a sensitivity of 88.2% and a specificity of 100%, as reported in the “Results and Discussion” section (Figure c). All the samples used for training, validation and blind testing were patient serum samples collected by the Lyme Disease Biobank (LDB) and were split between the data sets (i.e., training/validation and blind testing), maintaining balance between LD-positive and LD-negative samples (i.e., 54 positive/39 negative in training/validation set and 51 positive/36 negative samples in blind testing set).

To evaluate the generalization of the MCDO-based uncertainty quantification pipeline over time, we used a longitudinal data set consisting of two control samples from SeraCare (1 LD-positive and 1 LD-negative) measured repeatedly over 60 days. Specifically, each sample was measured in triplicate on 8 different days (days 1, 3, 7, 10, 15, 22, 35, 60), generating a total of 48 different samples. On this data set, the L0 achieved an accuracy of 95.8% prior to the application of MCDO-based uncertainty quantification, as reported in the “Results and Discussion” section (Figure S17). A different data set from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) biobank was utilized to evaluate the generalizability across different biobanks. For this testing, a new baseline model (L0CDC) was optimized using combined data from both LDB and CDC biobanks. The CDC data set included 96 xVFA samples activated with 32 different patient serum samples, split equally between training/validation and blind testing sets (i.e., 48 xVFA samples from 16 unique patients in each set), with no overlap between the two subsets to ensure unbiased evaluation. The L0CDC model was optimized on 141 samples from both LDB and CDC data sets (i.e., 93 from LDB [same as for L0 model training] and 48 from CDC) and blindly tested on an independent blind set of 135 samples from both sets (87 from LDB [same as for L0 model blind testing] and 48 from CDC). As reported in the “Results and Discussion” section, this model achieved a blind testing accuracy of 88.1% and a sensitivity of 76.8% prior to the application of the uncertainty quantification pipeline.

All neural network models were trained and evaluated on a desktop computer equipped with a GeForce GTX 1080 Ti graphics processing unit (NVIDIA). The models were created in Python using NumPy, TensorFlow, and Keras libraries. Training time for L0 model was ∼20 s, while the blind testing of the trained L0 model was substantially faster, taking less than 0.3 s to process all 87 blind testing samples.

Development of the MCDO-Based Uncertainty Quantification Pipeline

To implement MCDO-based uncertainty quantification pipeline, each xVFA sample was first processed by the L0 model without applying a dropout during the blind inference stage. At this step, the effective dropout rate was 0%, since the 50% dropout layers specified in the L0 architecture were only active during model training. This step generated the baseline score (B0). Subsequently, each sample was processed by N = 100–1000 MCDO Lyme models that shared the same architecture and trained weights as L0, but applied random dropout masks at a 10% rate during the blind inference stage, generating a distribution of MCDO scores (Ln(x), n = 1,...,N). Importantly, the L0 model was trained once using a 50% dropout rate, and the dropout rate was modified only during the inference stage to generate stochastic forward passes for uncertainty estimation. The average MCDO score and the corresponding uncertainty figure of merit (F) were then computed according to the equations defined in the “Results and Discussion” section. The uncertainty threshold (Fth = 8.5), optimized on the training/validation set, was used to identify and exclude samples with low F values deemed unreliable due to high prediction uncertainty. The optimal Fth value was applied to an independent blind testing set, consisting of samples never used during MCDO-based uncertainty quantification pipeline development and Fth selection. For consistency, the same training and blind testing data sets were used for the development of both the L0 model and the MCDO-based uncertainty quantification pipeline (see “Baseline Lyme model architecture and training” section for more details on the split between training and blind testing sets).

The Fth = 8.5 value excluded 4 false negative samples from the blind testing set, improving accuracy by 4.3% (from 93.1% to 97.4%) and sensitivity by 7.5% (from 88.2% to 95.7%), as reported in the “Results and Discussion” section. The same threshold was further applied to the longitudinal data set (see Figure S17 and the “Baseline Lyme model architecture and training” section for details on the data set) to assess temporal generalization, where it excluded 2 false positive predictions and increased overall accuracy to 100%.

To test the generalization of the MCDO-based uncertainty quantification pipeline across biobanks, the uncertainty threshold was optimized using a combined data set of 141 samples from the LDB and CDC biobanks (see the “Baseline Lyme model architecture and training” section for details on the data set split). The resulting threshold (FthCDC=3.8) excluded 11 false negative samples from the validation set at the expense of 2 true negatives and 1 true positive, improving validation accuracy from 83.0% to 90.5%. When applied to the independent blind testing set, the FthCDC removed 6 false negatives, along with 6 true negatives and 1 true positive, resulting in accuracy and sensitivity improvements from 88.1% to 91.8% and from 76.8% to 83.9%, respectively, as outlined in the “Results and Discussion” section (Figure S18).

The procedures described above outline the MCDO-based uncertainty quantification pipeline for Ln models with a 10% dropout rate and a figure of merit defined using the average Ln prediction score. However, for other dropout rates or alternative definitions of the figure of merit, the same pipeline can be applied without modification. The primary differences lie in the distribution of figure-of-merit values and the corresponding optimal threshold. Therefore, the proposed uncertainty quantification pipeline is flexible and can be adapted to different uncertainty metrics depending on application-specific requirements.

The MCDO-based uncertainty quantification pipeline was developed on the same desktop computer as the baseline neural network model using Python, NumPy, TensorFlow, and Keras libraries. Blind testing using N = 1000 MCDO Lyme models required ∼14.5 s, which was reduced to ∼1.6 s when using N = 100 MCDO Lyme models (Figure S10b,c).

Generation of MCDO Model Histograms

Figures a and a contain histograms of Ln scores for the validation and blind testing data sets, respectively. To generate these histograms, each xVFA sample was first processed by N = 1000 Ln models, each producing a prediction score in the [0:1] range based on the sigmoid activation function at the network output layer. Next, these scores were discretized into 50 bins, each with a width of 0.02, generating a 1D histogram for every sample. In this representation, the value of each bin represents Ln models density calculated as ρj=1N∑n=1Nrect(Ln(x)w−j−12)×100% where j = 0, ..., 49 is the bin number and w = 0.02 is the bin width. Based on this definition, ρ j takes values between 0% and 100%; ρ j = 0% if no Ln models for this sample have output scores within bin j and ρ j = 100% if all N = 1000 Ln models for a given sample have scores within bin j.

Each 1D histogram was further overlaid with the corresponding L0 score classified into one of the 50 bins. For each patient sample, the L0 score (B 0) was assigned to a bin number ⌊B 0 /w⌋, and the corresponding bin was marked with a green (for Lyme negative predictions) or a pink (for Lyme positive predictions) circle. The resulting sample-wise 1D histograms were then stacked row-wise to form a 2D histogram, in which each row represented an individual test sample and each column corresponded to a different classification bin, covering the Ln score distribution in the [0:1] range. Samples in this 2D histogram were sorted from top to bottom according to increasing L0 scores.

Statistical Analysis

Diagnostic performance metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, were computed from the distributions of true-positive, true-negative, false-positive, and false-negative samples in the validation and blind-testing data sets before and after applying the uncertainty quantification framework. To account for statistical uncertainty arising from finite sample size, 95% CI values were computed using the Wilson score method for binomial proportions (Tables S2 and S3). Blind testing of the MCDO-based uncertainty quantification pipeline was repeatedly performed over 100 iterations to generate a statistically significant distribution of the performance metrics. To evaluate the statistical significance of our improvements, Fisher’s exact test was applied to both the validation and blind testing data sets (see Supplementary Note 2 for details).
