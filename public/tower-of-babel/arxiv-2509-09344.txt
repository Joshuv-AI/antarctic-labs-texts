Lifetime of bimerons and antibimerons in two-dimensional magnets
Moritz A. Goerzen, Tim Drevelow, Soumyajyoti Haldar, Hendrik Schrautzer, Stefan Heinze, Dongzhe Li

Abstract
Soliton-based computing architectures have recently emerged as a promising avenue to overcome fundamental limitations of conventional information technologies, the von Neumann bottleneck. In this context, magnetic skyrmions have been widely considered for in-situ processing devices due to their mobility and enhanced lifetime in materials with broken inversion symmetry. However, modern applications in non-volatile reservoir or neuromorphic computing raise the additional demand for non-linear inter-soliton interactions. Here we report that solitons in easy-plane magnets, such as bimerons and antibimerons, show greater versatility and potential for non-linear interactions than skyrmions and antiskyrmions, making them superior candidates for this class of applications. Using first-principles and transition state theory, we predict the coexistence of degenerate bimerons and antibimerons at zero field in a van der Waals heterostructure Fe 3 GeTe 2 /Cr 2 Ge 2 Te 6 – an experimentally feasible system. We demonstrate that, owing to their distinct structural symmetry, bimerons exhibit fundamentally different behavior from skyrmions and cannot be regarded as their in-plane counterparts, as is often assumed. This distinction leads to unique properties of bimerons and antibimerons, which arise from the unbroken rotational symmetry in easy-plane magnets. These range from anisotropic soliton-soliton interactions to strong entropic effects on their lifetime, driven by the non-local nature of thermal excitations. Our findings reveal a broader richness of solitons in easy-plane magnets and underline their unique potential for spintronic devices. I
INTRODUCTION

Magnetic skyrmions – topologically protected quasi-particles with a whirling spin texture in real space – have raised great attention due to their rich physics and promising applications for future spintronic devices
Fert et al. 2017
;
Göbel et al. 2021
. Unique properties of skyrmions for applications include their nanoscale size
Romming et al. 2013
;
Meyer et al. 2019
, good thermal stability due to their integer topological charge
Göbel et al. 2021
, efficient manipulation by electric currents
Fert et al. 2013
;
Sampaio et al. 2013
, all-electrical detection
Hanneken et al. 2015
;
Maccariello et al. 2018
;
Perini et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2024a
;
Chen et al. 2024
, and all-optical topological switching
Buttner et al. 2021
;
Dabrowski et al. 2022
;
Khela et al. 2023
.
Magnetic bimerons, which emerge as vortex-antivortex pairs in easy-plane magnets, carry the same topological charge
as skyrmions that occur in easy-axis magnets.
Therefore, bimerons have been interpreted by theory as in-plane counterparts of skyrmions
Göbel et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Shen et al. 2020
, and have also been observed experimentally in ultrathin films
Jani et al. 2021
;
Nagase et al. 2021
and magnetic multilayers
Ohara et al. 2022
. More recently, bimerons have been predicted in two-dimensional (2D) van der Waals (vdW) magnets in numerous theoretical studies
Sun et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025
. However, despite the growing interest in bimerons and proposals for spintronic devices
using bimerons
Bo et al. 2025
, their thermal stability – crucial for all applications and quantified by lifetime – remains largely unexplored.

Calculating the lifetime of magnetic solitons, such as skyrmions and bimerons, is a challenging task that can be addressed using
transition state theory. In harmonic approximation, one finds the Arrhenius law for the mean lifetime
\tau
at temperature
T

Bessarab et al. 2012

\tau=\Gamma_{0}^{-1}\exp\left(\frac{\Delta E}{k_{\text{B}}T}\right)

(1)

where
\Delta E
is the energy barrier of the transition and
\Gamma_{0}
is the pre-exponential factor which can be explicitly calculated
Bessarab et al. 2018
.
However, many conceptual and technical complications arise due to different possible transition mechanisms, the complexity of identifying the minimum energy
path in a complex energy surface, and the need to determine not only
\Delta E
but also
\Gamma_{0}

which requires identifying and treating Goldstone modes that depend on the type of the considered soliton.
While the collapse and creation of skyrmions has been studied based on various theoretical approaches and by different groups
Bessarab et al. 2018
;
Hagemeister et al. 2015
;
Desplat et al. 2020
;
von Malottki et al. 2019
;
Varentcova et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2022
;
Goerzen et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024b
and even quantitative agreement of skyrmion lifetimes
with experimental data has been demonstrated
Muckel et al. 2021
;
Goerzen et al. 2022
,
the transition mechanisms and lifetime of bimerons have so far not been reported.

Here, we address this gap by presenting the first calculations of the lifetimes of bimerons and antibimerons based on transition-state theory using the prototypical
2D vdW magnet Cr
2
Ge
2
Te
6

with a honeycomb lattice
as a model system. We obtain the interaction
parameters for the atomistic spin model via
density functional theory (DFT) from
the vdW heterostructure
Fe
3
GeTe
2
/Cr
2
Ge
2
Te
6
(FGT/CGT), where FGT shows easy out-of-plane axis anisotropy and CGT exhibits an easy-plane anisotropy with exchange frustration. This
gives rise to the possibility of having multiple topological spin textures in one interface.
In the CGT layer,
bimerons and antibimerons emerge
which are degenerate at zero magnetic field
leading to identical lifetimes.

Surprisingly, the entropy – contained in the pre-exponential factor of the Arrhenius law – dominates the lifetime leading to a weak temperature dependence. In an external magnetic field, the degeneracy of bimerons and antibimerons is lifted, and we find a homotopical transition to skyrmions and antiskyrmions, respectively. We argue that the spin structures of bimerons and antibimerons differ in key features from those of skyrmions and antiskyrmions, which define the interplay with magnetic interactions and therefore increase their stability in terms of energy and entropy. The lifetime of solitons, which evolve from bimerons, increases upon applying a magnetic field in contrast to the decrease known from skyrmions.

Intriguingly, the pre-exponential factor shows almost no change with magnetic field and is identical for bimerons and antibimerons. This is explained by the absence of localized magnetic excitations in the presence of unbroken

\mathrm{U}(1)
-symmetry, which in our case applies to the rotation of spins within the easy-plane of CGT.
In accordance with the Hohenberg-Mermin-Wagner theorem
Mermin and Wagner 1966
;
Hohenberg 1967
, which for 2D materials predicts the existence of gapless magnons in such a situation, we find that the entropy of bimerons and antibimerons, up to a certain field, is dominated by long-range excitations of the magnetization. Thus, only bounded by system dimensions, the pre-exponential factor becomes independent of the exact shape of magnetic solitons in a sufficiently large environment. Based on this fundamental concept, our work contradicts the widespread notion
that bimerons are the
in-plane counterparts of skyrmions. Using an experimentally feasible vdW interface of FGT/CGT, we demonstrate that these symmetry-based long-range interactions make bimerons and antibimerons superior for applications that require a high degree of non-linearity.

B. Transition state theory

The reaction path for the annihilation of a magnetic soliton into the field-polarized state (e.g., the ferromagnetic (FM) state) is computed using the geodesic nudged elastic band method (GNEB)
Bessarab et al. 2015
. If this method converges, it returns a minimum energy path (MEP) in the space of magnetic configurations that connects a metastable initial state (IN)
with a final state, which in our case always is the FM state. From the MEP, a first-order saddle point (SP) can be obtained using a climbing image procedure
Bessarab et al. 2015

\Delta E=E_{0}^{\text{SP}}-E_{0}^{\text{IN}}~.

(4)

For a thermodynamic classification of the stability, not only the IN has to be considered, but also its thermal fluctuations. Therefore, we perform a harmonic approximation of the energy landscape in the vicinity of the IN and the SP

E=E_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{k}\lambda_{n}q_{n}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(q^{4}),

(5)

where
q_{n}
are normal coordinates. The number
k
of positive eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}

of the Hessian at the IN or the SP is bound by the number of degrees of freedom
k\leq 2N
for a simulation box with
N
lattice sites
This approximation allows for efficient computation of the partition function
Z
for both states, which are required for the estimation of the free energy
\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{F}=-k_{\text{B}}T\ln Z~,\quad Z=\int_{E_{0}}^{\infty}\varrho(E)\mathrm{e}^{-\beta E}~\mathrm{d}E~.

(6)

Here
\varrho
is the density of states and
\beta^{-1}=k_{\text{B}}T
.
Note that the partition of states that are degenerate to
E_{0}
is computed in zero-mode approximation to the respective degrees of freedom.
Within the framework of harmonic transition state theory (HTST)
Bessarab et al. 2012
, the rate
\Gamma=\Gamma^{\text{IN}\to\text{FM}}
for the annihilation of a soliton IN can be written in the form of an Eyring equation
Goerzen et al. 2022

\Gamma=\frac{\Lambda^{\ddagger}}{\sqrt{2\pi\beta}}\textrm{e}^{-\beta\Delta\mathcal{F}}~,\quad\Delta\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{\text{SP}}-\mathcal{F}^{\text{IN}}~.

(7)

The factor
\Lambda^{\ddagger}
contains the dynamic contributions from the Landau-Lifshitz equation to the rate and reads
Potkina et al. 2020

\Lambda^{\ddagger}=\frac{\gamma_{e}}{\mu}\sqrt{\left(\sigma_{2}\mathbf{v}_{\perp}\right)^{T}\mathrm{H}_{\text{SP}}\left(\sigma_{2}\mathbf{v}_{\perp}\right)},

with
2N{\times}2N
-Pauli matrix
\sigma_{2}
, electronic gyromagnetic ratio
\gamma_{e}
and Hessian of the SP
\mathrm{H}_{\text{SP}}
as well as its eigenvector
\mathbf{v}_{\perp}
associated with the unstable mode so that
\mathrm{H}_{\text{SP}}\mathbf{v}_{\perp}=\lambda_{\perp}\mathbf{v}_{\perp}
and
\lambda_{\perp}<0
. In this framework, the free energy itself can be expressed in terms of Hessian eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}
so that

\Delta\mathcal{F}=\Delta E-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\left[\left(\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\right)^{\Delta k/2}\frac{L_{\text{sp}}\prod_{n=1}^{k_{\text{in}}}\sqrt{\lambda_{n}^{\text{in}}}}{L_{\text{in}}\prod_{n=1}^{k_{\text{sp}}}\sqrt{\lambda_{n}^{\text{sp}}}}\right]

(8)

Linking the free energy
\Delta\mathcal{F}=\Delta\mathcal{U}-T\Delta\mathcal{S}
to the difference in average thermal energy
\Delta\mathcal{U}=\Delta E+k_{\text{B}}T\Delta k/2
and entropy
\Delta S
the rate is most often expressed as an Arrhenius law

\Gamma=\frac{\Lambda^{\ddagger}}{\sqrt{2\pi\beta}}\textrm{e}^{\Delta\mathcal{S}/k_{\text{B}}+\Delta k/2}\textrm{e}^{-\beta\Delta E}=\Gamma_{0}\textrm{e}^{-\beta\Delta E}~.

(9)

The form of the internal energy used above follows directly from the equipartition theorem for any system with energy as given in Eq. (
5
).

RESULTS

A. Bimerons and antibimerons in 2D magnets

In order to point out the advantage of easy-plane magnets over easy-axis magnets in terms of hosting degenerate magnetic particles, we study not only the stability of bimerons but also antibimerons. These states are magnetic configurations
\mathbf{m}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
which exhibit
opposite topological charges
Q=\pm 1
. In accordance with Refs.
Nagaosa and Tokura 2013
;
Kuchkin and Kiselev 2020
, we define particles and antiparticles in analogy to electrons and positrons, so that bimerons and skyrmions possess a charge of
Q=-1
and antibimerons and antiskyrmions possess
Q=1
as illustrated in Fig.
1
. Note that this does not coincide with the definition in other works
Jiang et al. 2017
. The difference between bimerons and antibimerons becomes apparent when imagining them as bound states of two merons, as depicted in Fig.
1
. Each meron is characterized by a vortex- or winding number
\nu_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and the polarity of its core
\xi_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\cos\Theta(\rho)\right]^{\infty}_{0}
against the polarized background at a radial distance
\rho=\infty
(see Appendix A). In this picture, we can write a meron-resolved topological charge

Q(\mathbf{m})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbf{m}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{m}}{\partial x}{\times}\frac{\partial\mathbf{m}}{\partial y}\right)~\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{r}=\sum_{i=1,2}\nu_{i}\xi_{i},

(10)

so that each primitive state with
Q=\pm 1
can be identified with its meron composition by a function
\mathrm{idm}

\mathrm{idm}(\mathbf{m})=(\nu_{1},\xi_{1})+(\nu_{2},\xi_{2}),

(11)

under the restriction to vortex-antivortex pairs with
\nu_{1}=-\nu_{2}
, which is inevitable for a constant magnetization at
\rho=\infty

Kuchkin and Kiselev 2020
and
\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}=\pm 1
. As indicated by the white arrows in the sketches of Fig.
1
, the shape of bimerons and antibimerons within the
xy
-plane is identical
and they only differ in signs of
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
, visible by red (
\xi_{i}>0
) or blue (
\xi_{i}<0
) meron cores.

It is important to note that all interactions in the energy function in Eq. (
2
),
apart from Zeeman interaction, are invariant under spin inversion
\mathbf{m}\to-\mathbf{m}
. Since Eq. (
10
) and (
11
) state that the inverted state
\mathrm{idm}(-\mathbf{m})=(\nu_{1},-\xi_{1})+(\nu_{2},-\xi_{2})
has to have inverted topological charge, it follows that bimerons and antibimerons are degenerate at zero field.

B. Distinction from (anti-)skyrmions

The degeneracy of bimerons and antibimerons is in strong contrast to skyrmions and antiskyrmions.
The major difference between (anti-)bimeron and (anti-)skyrmion is their response to DMI. This can be seen from the continuum formulation for interfacial DMI of a (anti-)skyrmion

E_{\text{DMI}}=2\pi\mathcal{D}\delta_{\nu,1}\cos\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\partial\Theta}{\partial\rho}+\frac{\nu}{\rho}\sin\Theta\cos\Theta\right)\rho\mathrm{d}\rho~,

(12)

which shows a dependency on the vortex number
\nu
via the Kronecker-Delta term
\delta_{\nu,1}
.
The DMI with a magnitude given by
\mathcal{D}
in the continuum model, therefore, only affects the energy of solitons with vortex number
\nu=1
, which accounts for skyrmions, but not antiskyrmions.
Bimerons and antibimerons, on the other hand, both possess a meron with a favored vortex number due to the vortex-antivortex restriction to Eq. (
11
). The evolution of the DMI under continuous transformation between (anti-)bimeron and (anti-)skyrmion is schematically illustrated in Fig.
1
. It is shown, that bimerons and antibimerons are stabilized equally by DMI, while a homogeneous rotation of all spins by
\Theta_{\text{FM}}
around the
y
-axis lifts this degeneracy until the maximum difference is reached for skyrmions with favored remaining composition
\mathrm{idm}(\mathbf{m})=(1,-1)
and antiskyrmions with a composition
\mathrm{idm}(\mathbf{m})=(-1,-1)
that is ignored by the DMI in Eq. (
12
).

C. Spin spiral dispersions

D. Field-induced transformation

Interestingly, as predicted in Fig.
1
, our atomistic spin simulations demonstrate the emergence of bimeron–skyrmion and antibimeron–antiskyrmion transformations under an external magnetic field in the CGT layer, as shown in Fig.
3
(a) for soliton configurations at selected magnetic fields. The external magnetic field induces a canting of the magnetic background, such that for high fields the (anti-)bimerons transform into (anti-)skyrmions. In such a background, skyrmion-like textures are energetically favored due to their vorticity
\nu=1
, as illustrated in Fig.
3
(b).

Such a rotation of the magnetic background arises from the competition between the Zeeman interaction and the MAE
Yu et al. 2024
, and the associated canting angles can be estimated. This competition is quantified in the potential energy given in Eq. (
3
).
A homogeneous magnetization with
z

m_{z}=\cos\Theta_{\text{FM}}
that minimizes the potential energy while being subject to the spherical constraint

Due to the constraint, this linear canting of the FM background with magnetic field strength

is only valid up to a saturation field of

in CGT with

/atom and

meV/atom. This behavior of the magnetic background is numerically validated and displayed in Fig.

Q=-1
(bimeron
\to

up to

(antibimeron

E. Localization in easy-plane magnets

Regarding the usually strong correlation between soliton size and lifetime, it is mandatory to point out qualitative differences between soliton shapes in different regimes of
B_{z}
. For that purpose, we define the
\Theta
-profile with respect to the orientation of the FM background

\Theta(\mathbf{r})=\arccos\left[\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{r})\cdot\mathbf{m}_{\text{FM}}\right]

(14)

which is thus applicable in arbitrarily canted backgrounds. Figs.
4
(a)-(c) show
\Theta
-profiles of solitons with
Q=-1
at three exemplary magnetic field strengths chosen, including

\epsilon_{i}\big|_{B_{z}=B_{z}^{\text{sat}}}=K-K\,(1-m_{i}^{z})^{2},

(15)

with the saturation field defined by

In this situation, it can analytically be shown that the
\Theta

-polarized background decays algebraically

instead of by an exponential double domain wall profile

\Theta(\mathbf{r})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2\arctan\left(\frac{\kappa}{\rho}\right)&\text{algebraic}\\
2\sum_{\pm}\arctan\left(\mathrm{e}^{\frac{-\rho\pm c}{w}}\right)&\text{exponential}\end{array}\right.,

(16)

with constants
\kappa,c,w
.
This is reflected by the spin textures in Fig.
4
(b) and (c), which
look similar at first glance. However, their
\Theta
-profiles reveal significant differences.

In order to quantify the differences, the profiles are evaluated
along the two perpendicular directions

\mathbf{v}_{1}
and
\mathbf{v}_{2}
, depicted by white arrows and the results are displayed in Fig.
4
(e),(f).
By displaying the profile on a double logarithmic axis, an algebraic decay
\propto 1/\rho
is visible as a straight line of slope
-1

B_{z}^{\text{sat}}
(grey)
until reaching the boundary of the simulation box, which in turn is indicated by a vertical red line. For the state at
B_{z}=1.8~
T (green), we find that it decays faster than
1/\rho

However, the most interesting case is the bimeron at
B_{z}=0~
T. It can be seen that the profile converges faster than

along
\mathbf{v}_{2}
(cf. Fig.

\mathbf{v}_{1}

Despite the lack of an analytical solution for the

\mathbf{v}_{1}
and
\mathbf{v}_{2}
can be modeled as double domain walls, illustrated in Fig.

Because of the in-plane MAE, the domain wall along
\mathbf{v}_{2}

We conclude that solitons at fields

F. Bimeron collapse mechanism

To obtain a meaningful measure for the thermal stability of solitons in CGT, we compute their average lifetime (cf. Eq. (
1
)) within the framework of HTST
Bessarab et al. 2012
. The general procedure is described in Section
II
and requires knowledge about the collapse mechanism for the annihilation of a soliton. An example of the energy along a minimum energy path (MEP) for the annihilation of a bimeron with
Q=-1
at
B_{z}=0
T is shown in Fig.
5
(a).
From the decomposition of the energy along the path into individual interactions, it becomes apparent that the energy barrier is dominated by exchange (red), arising from frustration between short- and long-range interactions (see Table
1
): short-range FM coupling (
J_{1},J_{2},J_{3}
) competes with long-range AFM coupling (
J_{4},J_{5},J_{6},J_{7}
).

Contributions from DMI (cyan) and MAE (violet)
play a minor role in the stability of bimerons.
In the collection of images along the MEP shown in Fig.
5
(b), the state containing the Bloch point (BP) is between the images labeled 1 and 2, but not sampled directly. This position along the MEP is characterized as the point where the total topological charge switches, in our case, accompanied by a local change of sign in the topological charge density (see Appendix A for computational details), which is visible in the topological density map below the corresponding configurations (Fig.
5
(b)).
The SP, in contrast, is the image of the highest total energy and seems to match the image, where the minimum distance between the two meron centers is evidently reached. Interestingly, we find that the SP is different from the BP. Such SP-BP separation is unusual in continuum models, where the zero-size skyrmion
Bernand-Mantel et al. 2022
;
Büttner et al. 2018
resembles both at the same time. However, no theory states that the BP has to be the SP in discrete models.
This highlights the complexity of the bimeron topological transition in the CGT layer. Moreover, the two-meron picture for the collapse is also supported by the elliptic shape of the topological density along the path, which distinguishes it from the radial collapse known for skyrmions
von Malottki et al. 2019
;
Muckel et al. 2021
.
Having understood the anisotropic nonlocality of bimerons and their collapse mechanism, in the following subsection, we explain how to define the size of bimerons, going beyond standard skyrmions widely studied in the literature. We will also carefully address how these large finite-size effects influence key properties of bimerons, such as their stability, the dynamical contribution, and the entropy difference.

G. Soliton size and stability estimation in canted magnets

An often used procedure for the estimation of the radius
R
of radially symmetric solitons, especially skyrmions
Bocdanov and Hubert 1994
, is based on the inflection point
\rho_{c}
of the
\Theta
-profile, where
\ddot{\Theta}(\rho_{c})=0
and

R=\rho_{c}-\frac{\Theta(\rho_{c})}{\dot{\Theta}(\rho_{c})}~.

(17)

Due to the broken radial symmetry of solitons at
B_{z}\leq B_{z}^{\text{sat}}
and the fact that algebraic profiles (cf. Eq. (
16
)) do not obey an inflection point, an invariant approach is needed. Because of this, we give an estimate of all soliton sizes not in terms of a radius but rather of the soliton area
A
. This is in contrast to previously used methods for the size estimation of bimerons
Kharkov et al. 2017
.

We interpolate the
\Theta
-profile (cf. Eq. (
16
)) of metastable solitons by cubic polynomials to obtain a continuous function
\Theta(\mathbf{r})
. This function is then sampled by a fixed number of
X_{\Box}=4\cdot 10^{6}

discrete points on a square superlattice that superimpose the simulation box with area
A_{\Box}
. The area of the soliton is then computed as the ratio of the number
X(A_{\Box})
of sampling points within the contour
\Theta(\mathbf{r})\geq\pi/2
and the total number of sampling points
X_{\Box}

Due to the slow spatial convergence of the solitons and accompanying finite-size effects, this estimation is performed for different system sizes and extrapolated to infinitely large simulation boxes so that the final procedure comes down to

A=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}H\left[\Theta(\mathbf{r})-\frac{\pi}{2}\right]~\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{r}\approx\lim_{A_{\Box}\to\infty}A_{\Box}\frac{X(A_{\Box})}{X_{\Box}},

(18)

with the Heavyside function
H
.
Thereby, because of
\Theta
being defined with respect to the polarized background (cf. Eq. (
14
))
A
is invariant under the global rotation of the magnetization. In addition, the boundary of this contour resembles the line where the magnetization is perpendicular to the background, which is also an experimentally used criterion for skyrmion size
Meyer et al. 2019
.

The evolution of the soliton area
A
with increasing simulation box size
A_{\Box}
is shown in Fig.
6
(a). In order to overcome the limitations of our results by finite-size effects we take advantage of the observation that not only the soliton area
A
, but also the energy barrier
\Delta E
against a collapse into the FM (Fig.
6
(b)) and the constituents of the pre-exponential factor, namely the dynamic contribution
\Lambda^{\ddagger}
and the entropy difference
\Delta\mathcal{S}
as shown in Fig.
6
(c-d), can be fitted by

f(A_{\Box};f_{\infty},\kappa,\xi)=f_{\infty}+\frac{\kappa}{A_{\Box}^{\xi}}~,

(19)

so that the quantity for the infinite simulation box is simply the fitting variable
f_{\infty}=\lim_{A_{\Box}\to\infty}f(A_{\Box})
if
\xi>0
.

As shown in Fig.
6
, proper convergence cannot be achieved for magnetic fields
B_{z}\leq 1.54~
T within the range of computationally reasonable system sizes. For skyrmion-like states above this field (green), where there is no
\mathrm{U}(1)
-symmetry, the situation is different, and reasonable convergence for all quantities is reached within the range of directly computed system sizes. Overall, we note that
\Lambda^{\ddagger}
and
\Delta\mathcal{S}
(Fig.
6
(c–d)) converge faster with increasing simulation box size than
A
and
\Delta E
(Fig.
6
(a–b)). This indicates that finite-size effects are less pronounced in the pre-exponential factor than in the energy barrier. However, the entropy difference exhibits a very slow convergence near the critical field. Since all quantities were calculated in the limit of infinitely large simulation boxes, their convergence is guaranteed.

Interestingly, this behavior originates from the unbroken rotational symmetry, which is present below the critical magnetic field and leads to a long-range Belavin-Polyakov shape of solitons in at least one direction. In the following, using the prcedure from Eq. (
19
), all GNEB and HTST calculations are
extrapolated to infinitely large honeycomb lattices in order to achieve fully converged physical quantities.
To the best of our knowledge, the stability and lifetime of bimerons and antibimerons have not been reported in the literature to date for any material. In the following two subsections, we address this gap.

H. Entropy under unbroken rotational symmetry

Similar to the long-range soliton profiles reported in Subsec. E, the unbroken rotational symmetry within the easy-plane allows for gapless long-range excitations of magnetic textures. Away from thermal vortex formations that reduce the spin-correlation length in a realistic 2D material, the extent of these excitations is usually bound by the size of the simulation box
Bramwell and Holdsworth 1994
. Regarding the magnon gap of our Hamiltonian

\lambda_{\text{mag}}=\mu B_{z}+2K,

(20)

it is clear that this is true for all fields
B_{z}\leq B_{z}^{\text{sat}}=-2K/\mu
and thus applies to backgrounds that feature unbroken
\mathrm{U}(1)
-symmetry, as indicated by circles in Fig.
1
. Due to the strong connection between symmetry and entropy, this highly affects the lifetime of solitons in this regime.

In the framework of HTST, the entropy (see Section
II
) directly depends on the local curvatures
\lambda_{n}
of the energy landscape around the IN and SP, as well as the configuration space volume of symmetry equivalent states
L
, also referred to as zero-mode partition functions

\frac{\Delta\mathcal{S}}{k_{\text{B}}}=\ln\left[\left(\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\right)^{\Delta k/2}\frac{L_{\text{sp}}\prod_{n=1}^{k_{\text{in}}}\sqrt{\lambda_{n}^{\text{in}}}}{L_{\text{in}}\prod_{n=1}^{k_{\text{sp}}}\sqrt{\lambda_{n}^{\text{sp}}}}\right]-\frac{\Delta k}{2}~,

(21)

with
\beta^{-1}=k_{\text{B}}T
. Here, the products run over all
k
harmonic degrees of freedom of a given state and
\Delta k=k_{\text{sp}}-k_{\text{in}}
marks their difference, so that the factor
(2\pi/\beta)^{\Delta k/2}
ensures that the quantity in Eq. (
21
) is dimensionless.

The curvatures
\lambda_{n}
, obtained as the eigenvalues of the spherical constraint Hessians
Bessarab et al. 2012
are shown in Fig.
7
. The absence of the magnon gap below
B_{z}^{\text{sat}}
(cf. Eq. (
20
)) means that all eigenvalues in this regime correspond to long-range modulations of the magnetic texture and therefore are heavily pronounced by the size of the simulation box.

We show low-energy excitations with
0\leq\lambda\leq 0.4
meV for a simulation box of
40{\times}40
unit cells, with 2 atoms per unit cell. Below
B_{z}^{\text{sat}}
, we identify three symmetries for bimerons (Fig.
7
(a)) and antibimeron (Fig.
7
(c)) in agreement with Ref.
Deng et al. 2025
, which manifest as zero-energy excitations: the rotation of the helicity
(cf. Eq. (
29
))
and the translation into two orthogonal directions. The respective zero-mode partition function is computed as described in Ref.
Haldar et al. 2018
;
Goerzen et al. 2023
. Since this mode involves a rotation of all spins in the
xy
-plane, the respective partition function rises with increasing system size
A_{\Box}
, becoming more and more independent on the localized soliton texture and thus approaches similar values for initial state and saddle point. Therefore, for
A_{\Box}\to\infty
the contribution of this mode to the entropy difference vanishes.

An important difference to the eigenvalue spectrum of skyrmions or antiskyrmions
von Malottki et al. 2019
;
Goerzen et al. 2023
;
Schrautzer et al. 2022
is the existence of a rotation, describing a rotation of the soliton by leaving the direction of the background unchanged, next to the usual rotation of helicity
Lin and Hayami 2016
, which rotates all spins but leaves the meron centers unchanged. Consider the helicity operator
\mathrm{R}_{\Phi}
, which rotates all spins within the
m_{xy}
-plane,
and the canting operator
\mathrm{R}_{\Theta}
, which mimics the magnetic field induced canting by
\Theta_{\text{FM}}
. It can be shown that both do not commute for
\Theta_{\text{FM}}>0

(see Appendix C). As a consequence, their image spaces are entangled, so that a single degree of freedom is not sufficient to describe all rotated states for
B_{z}\leq B_{z}^{\text{sat}}
. However, since both the (anti-)bimeron and the corresponding SP in Fig.

(b),(d) exhibit a similar rotation mode, it has nearly no influence on the entropy in Eq. (
21
).

IV
CONCLUSIONS AND OUTLOOK

In this combined study between DFT and HTST, we investigated the lifetimes of meron bound states with topological charges
Q=\pm 1
in the CGT layer of the FGT/CGT interface.
and observed the evolution of their stability under an applied magnetic field perpendicular to the easy-plane of CGT. Along this continuous transformation, we distinguish two regimes separated by the critical magnetic field
B_{z}^{\text{sat}}=1.54~
T. Below this field, the rotation within the easy-plane remains an unbroken symmetry, which affects the stability but mostly their shape and entropy. While the unbroken symmetry leads to Belavin-Polyakov-like long-range profiles of solitons, it also hinders the formation of a magnon gap, which in turn means that the entropy is dominated by contributions from non-local excitations.
Beyond the discussion of relevant particle-antiparticle effects observed in our simulations, we find that despite the broken inversion symmetry in CGT, this entropic stabilization does not distinguish between particles and antiparticles in the easy-plane magnet below a certain magnetic field of
B_{z}=1.54~
T, which is in strong contrast to skyrmions and antiskyrmions
Goerzen et al. 2023
. We further conclude that the entropic stabilization of magnetic solitons in CGT exceeds that in the heavily investigated ultrathin film Pd/Fe/Ir(111).
Since entropy is the leading state function for lifetimes beyond ultra-cold temperatures, we emphasize the viability of easy-plane solitons in 2D vdW magnets for future computing schemes that utilize more than one kind of soliton.

From a materials perspective, the proposed 2D vdW heterostructures can serve as a promising platform to realize a richer variety of magnetic solitons with distinctive symmetry and topology, whose stability can be easily tuned by external stimuli such as gating, strain, or twistronics
Burch et al. 2018
;
Li et al. 2023
.

For future device applications, our proposal is extremely beneficial for neuromorphic and stochastic spintronic devices
Pinna et al. 2018
;
Song et al. 2020
, which require a high degree of non-linear soliton-soliton interactions, or in magnetic soliton-based quantum computing
Psaroudaki and Panagopoulos 2021
,
Therefore, the stabilization mechanism we propose is general for easy-plane solitons with arbitrary
Q
, not limited to
Q=\pm 1
, but also applicable to higher-order (
|Q|>1
) solitons.
Thus, it can be expected that the stability of high-
Q
states in easy-plane magnets fundamentally exceeds that in easy-axis magnets because of their different response to broken inversion symmetry.
This is in strong contrast to DMI-stabilized easy-axis solitons, where skyrmions are stabilized by interfacial
Goerzen et al. 2023
;
Kuchkin and Kiselev 2020
and antiskyrmions by anisotropic DMI
Hoffmann et al. 2017
.
Finally, we emphasize that the proposed mechanism in this work is general and should apply to various systems such as ultrathin films, magnetic multilayers, and vdW interfaces.
By optimizing relevant magnetic interactions, particularly by combining the entropy-dominated lifetime with large energy barriers, we expect this novel mechanism to enable advances in soliton technology toward room-temperature operation.

(\sqrt{3}{\times}\sqrt{3})
FGT cell by fixing the FGT lattice constant. The CGT lattice is slightly stretched by about 0.15% by forming the CGT/FGT interface. To describe the vdW distance between CGT and FGT accurately, we also included in our calculations vdW interactions using semi-empirical dispersion corrections (DFT-D3) as formulated by Grimme
Grimme et al. 2010
. The interlayer vdW distance was found to be 3.78 Å, indicating a rather weak interlayer interaction. To treat the on-site correlation properly, we applied LDA+
U
with
U_{\text{eff}}=0.5
eV for CGT, close to the one used in Ref.
Gong et al. 2017
, reproducing the correct experimental magnetic ground state. In contrast, we have not considered the Hubbard
U
correction for FGT since LDA yields a better magnetic moment for the Fe atom closer to the experimental one, as previously pointed out in Refs.
Zhuang et al. 2016
;
Deng et al. 2018
.

To obtain the pair-wise Heisenberg exchange and DMI constants required for the atomistic spin model (Eq. (
2
)), we have performed spin spiral calculations via DFT
Kurz et al. 2004
;
Heide et al. 2009
;
Dupé et al. 2014
.
A spin spiral is characterized by a wave
vector
\mathbf{q}
, and the spin at lattice site
\textbf{r}_{i}
is given by
\mathbf{m}_{i}=(\cos(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_i),\sin(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_i),0)
.
The energy dispersion
neglecting SOC,
E_{\text{ss}}(\mathbf{q})
, is calculated via the
FLEUR
code
in the scalar-relativistic approximation
based on the generalized Bloch theorem
Kurz et al. 2004
.
The Heisenberg exchange constants are obtained by fitting the
self-consistently converged homogeneous flat spin spiral dispersions without SOC,
E_{\text{ss}}(\mathbf{q})

E_{\text{ss}}(\mathbf{q})=-\sum_{\sigma}J_{\sigma}\sum_{j}\cos\!\left(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}\right),

(23)

Here,
\sigma
labels the coordination shell, and the index
j
runs over the
symmetry–equivalent neighbors within shell
\sigma
. The vectors

\mathbf{r}_{j}
connect a reference magnetic site to its neighbors, such that
all
|\mathbf{r}_{j}|
are equal within a given shell. The parameters

J_{\sigma}
denote the corresponding shell–resolved exchange constants.

The DMI contribution to the dispersion,
E_{\rm DMI}(\mathbf{q})
,
is computed within first-order perturbation theory
Heide et al. 2009
as follows

\begin{split}E_{\mathrm{DMI}}(\mathbf{q})&=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\nu}n_{\mathbf{k}\nu}(\mathbf{q})\,\delta\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}\nu}(\mathbf{q}),\\
\text{where }\delta\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}\nu}(\mathbf{q})&=\left\langle\psi_{\mathbf{k}\nu}(\mathbf{q})\right|H_{\mathrm{SOC}}\left|\psi_{\mathbf{k}\nu}(\mathbf{q})\right\rangle.\end{split}

(24)

where
\psi_{\mathbf{k}\nu}
denotes scalar-relativistic spin-spiral eigenstates (without SOC), and
n_{\mathbf{k}\nu}
is the occupation number of the eigenstate
\psi_{\mathbf{k}\nu}
. The total energies in
Eq. (
23
) and
Eq. (
24
) are then mapped to the last term of
Eq. (
2
) in order to obtain exchange and DMI constants in arbitrary NN.

Note that we have used an extended BZ beyond the first BZ in order to describe accurately the magnetic interactions in the complex geometrical structure of the interface (see the following
subsection 2 for more details). A cutoff parameter for FLAPW basis functions of
k_{\text{max}}
= 4.1 a.u.
-1
was used in our calculations. We used a very dense mesh of
21{\times}21

\mathbf{k}
-points to obtain well-converged total energies of flat spin-spiral states.

Finally, the MAE (single-ion anisotropy constant
K
) was calculated using the band-energy difference (i.e., the so-called force theorem)

K=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathrm{occ.}}\left(\epsilon_{\alpha}^{\parallel}-\epsilon_{\alpha}^{\perp}\right),

(25)

where the summation runs over the occupied electronic states. Here,
\epsilon_{\alpha}
are the eigenvalues obtained from a single diagonalization of the Hamiltonian including SOC, starting from self-consistently converged charge densities with rotations to the appropriate spin-orientation axis. We used a denser
\mathbf{k}
-point mesh of
87{\times}87
in the full 2D BZ for these calculations.

2. Spin model parameters: Mapping from the hexagonal to the honeycomb lattice

For CGT, the magnetic Cr atoms, which are solely relevant for atomistic spin simulations, form a honeycomb lattice. To simplify DFT calculations, we treated the two Cr atoms in CGT collectively, meaning that all spin-spiral calculations were performed by rotating both Cr atoms simultaneously. By fitting the spin-spiral dispersion curves in Fig.
2
(c) and (d) of the main text, we directly extract the magnetic interaction parameters for the hexagonal lattice.

Figure 9:
Energy on reciprocal honeycomb and hexagonal lattices. (a) The hexagonal lattice can be decomposed into three hexagonal lattices (black, violet, hollow dots), each with lattice constant
a=1
. Removing one of them, here the hollowed dots, leaves a honeycomb lattice with a larger unit cell (red lines) of
\tilde{a}=\sqrt{3}
and rotated by
\pi/6
. (b) Exchange energy with respect to the
\Gamma
-point for the reciprocal hexagonal (left) and honeycomb (right) lattice models of CGT.
It is illustrated that the honeycomb BZ, compared to the hexagonal BZ, is rotated by
\pi/6
and scaled by
\sqrt{3}
. This is caused by the necessity of displaying the reciprocal dispersion in
\mathbf{q}
-spaces of the same scale, which requires shrinking and rotating the real-space honeycomb unit cell. Due to the inverse relation between length scales in real and reciprocal spaces, this causes a rotation and expansion of the BZ.

The honeycomb lattice can be formed from two hexagonal lattices, as shown in
Fig.
9
(a). This means that a set of magnetic interaction constants can be transformed so that the energy over the respective reciprocal lattices of the hexagonal and honeycomb lattices remains similar. A mapping between these two lattices is needed. The concept behind this mapping is illustrated in
Fig.
9
(b), where the energy dispersion within the first BZ of the honeycomb lattice (left panel, outer hexagon) coincides with the dispersion within the first BZ of the hexagonal lattice (right panel, inner hexagon).

To understand the transformation of the interaction constants that lead to this convergence between lattice geometries, consider that the rather complicated honeycomb lattice can be modeled from a hexagonal lattice by adding additional lattice sites in the center of each hexagon, as illustrated by the grey points in Fig.
9
(a). Further, each of these virtually added lattice sites at position
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
has a non-virtual counterpart at
\mathbf{r}=-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
. This has an important implication for spin spiral states, which are characterized by the vectors
\mathbf{R},\mathbf{I},\mathbf{q}

\in

\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In this construction,
\mathbf{q}
describes the direction in which the spin spiral propagates, while the length
|\mathbf{q}|
is inversely proportional to its wavelength. On the other hand, the vectors
\mathbf{R},\mathbf{I}
span the plane of rotation, so that the orientation of a spin
\mathbf{m}_{i}
at position
\mathbf{r}_{i}
reads

\mathbf{m}_{i}=\mathbf{R}\cos(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_i)-\mathbf{I}\sin(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}_i)~.

(26)

The energy of such a spin spiral
\mathbf{m}=\{\mathbf{m}_{1},...\mathbf{m}_{N}\}
reads

\displaystyle\frac{E(\mathbf{m})}{N}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\sigma}J_{\sigma}\sum_{\mathbf{r}\in Y(\sigma)}\cos(\mathbf{q} \cdot\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\quad-2\mathbf{I}{\times}\mathbf{R}\sum_{\sigma}D_{\sigma}\sum_{\mathbf{r}\in\sigma}(\mathbf{r}{\times}\hat{\mathbf{z}})\sin(\mathbf{q} \cdot\mathbf{r})-\frac{K}{2}.

(27)

from which it can be seen that their exchange interactions, DMI, and MAE are invariant under the inversion of spatial coordinates
\mathbf{r}\rightarrow-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
. This implies that within a spin spiral state, all virtually added lattice points have the same energy as their nonvirtual counterparts.

Since the virtual lattice sites only appear on certain shells, specifically the magenta
shells in Fig.
9
(a), these shells will contribute twice the energy on the hexagonal lattice
compared with their contribution to the honeycomb lattice. For a distinct notation, we can use the fact that the twice-interacting shells
\sigma
with lattice sites
\mathbf{r}\in\sigma
are not subsets of the sublattice
L_{0}
that contains the center at
\mathbf{r}=0
. In
Fig.
9
(a), this is reflected by the fact that hollow lattice sites never lie on black circles, at least within the range of investigated shells. With that, the double contribution can be treated effectively by scaling the interaction constants according to

C_{\sigma}^{\text{hon}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}2C_{\sigma}^{\text{hex}}&~\text{if }\sigma\in L_{1}\\
C_{\sigma}^{\text{hex}}&~\text{if }\sigma\in L_{0}\end{array}\right.~,

(28)

where
C_{\sigma}^{\text{hex}}\in{J_{\sigma},D_{\sigma}}
refers to the constants in the hexagonal lattice.

For example, the interaction between nearest neighbors on the honeycomb lattice has 3 atoms and an exchange constant of
J_{1}^{\text{hon}}
). A corresponding hexagonal lattice with 6 nearest neighbors and an exchange constant of
J_{1}^{\text{hex}}=\frac{1}{2}J_{1}^{\text{hon}}

with a weighting factor of
\frac{1}{2}

would produce the same energy contributions for all spin spirals.
The shell containing all next nearest neighbors in the honeycomb lattice is already centrosymmetric,
therefore the weighting-factor becomes 1 and we find
J_{2}^{\text{hex}}=J_{2}^{\text{hon}}
. Exchange constants and the absolute value of the DMI vector always share the same weighting factor,
which is always
\frac{1}{2}
or 1, based on whether the shell of neighbors is centrosymmetric or not. The interaction parameters for the honeycomb lattice in Table 1 in the main text could be calculated by fitting with the dense hexagonal lattice and determining the weighting factors. Note that, as we have checked, with these two sets of parameters for CGT-hexagonal and CGT-honeycomb, our atomistic spin simulations predict similarly shaped bimerons at
B_{z}=0
T that only differ by a factor
\sim\sqrt{3}
in size.

3. Soliton model

Each soliton is associated with a field of spins
\mathbf{m}:~\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
at discrete lattice sites
\mathbf{r}
, which are initialized as

\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{r})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\cos\Phi(\mathbf{r})\sin\Theta(\mathbf{r})\\
\sin\Phi(\mathbf{r})\sin\Theta(\mathbf{r})\\
\cos\Theta(\mathbf{r})\end{array}\right)~,\quad\Phi(\mathbf{r})=\nu\phi+\gamma~.

(29)

where the polar profile function
\Phi
depends on the vorticity
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
and helicity
\gamma\in[0,2\pi]
. The radial
\Theta
-profile is assumed to be either exponentially or algebraically decaying (cf. Eq. (
16
)) with radial distance
\rho
from the soliton center.
While the exponential profile is motivated phenomenologically
Bocdanov and Hubert 1994
and describes the skyrmion as a cyclic domain wall, the algebraic profile is the analytic solution to the continuum model
Barton-Singer et al. 2020
if the potential energy can be brought to a form, that is similar to the situation at
B_{z}^{\text{sat}}
mentioned in the main text. After initialization of the solitons, the spin textures were relaxed with respect to energy by the velocity projection optimization algorithm
Bessarab et al. 2015
.

4. Calculation of the topological charge density

To characterize magnetic solitons, we calculate the topological charge
Q
as the sum over the topological density
q
of selected spin structures in the honeycomb lattice of CGT.
For a discrete model, the topological charge from Eq. (
30
) has the form
Berg and Lüscher 1981
:

Q=\sum\limits_{\mathbf{r^{*}}}q(\mathbf{r}^{*}),

(30)

where the topological density
q(\mathbf{r}^{*})
is defined at the vertices
\mathbf{r}^{*}
of the dual lattice.

For the honeycomb lattice of the CGT layer, six spins are adjacent to the dual lattice point
\mathbf{r}^{*}
. Thus, a triangulation can be used to define the spherical triangles. We use a Delaunay triangulation, yielding one large main triangle and three smaller triangles. For each of these triangles, the topological density can be computed and assigned to the dual lattice points of the triangulated lattice. The topological density assigned to the center
\mathbf{r}^{*}
of the hexagons is then:

\displaystyle\begin{split}q(\mathbf{r}^{*})=\frac{1}{4\pi}(&\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{m}_{1},\mathbf{m}_{5},\mathbf{m}_{3})+\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{m}_{1},\mathbf{m}_{3},\mathbf{m}_{2})\\
&+\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{m}_{3},\mathbf{m}_{5},\mathbf{m}_{4})+\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{m}_{5},\mathbf{m}_{1},\mathbf{m}_{6})).\end{split}

(31)

For the definition of the triangulation as well as for the indexation of the magnetic moments in the formulas above, as shown in Fig.
10
.

It is worth noting that, to obtain a smooth visualization in Fig.
5
(b) in the main text, the density was plotted at the midpoints of all four triangles forming a hexagon but normalized with respect to the area of these triangles formed by sites on the honeycomb lattice.

APPENDIX B: INTERLAYER INTERACTIONS

The total energy of the magnetic bilayer system FGT/CGT with layers FGT and CGT is given by

\displaystyle E_{\text{FGT/CGT}}(\mathbf{q})=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\sigma}J^{\parallel,\sigma}_{\text{FGT}}\sum_{j}\cos(\mathbf{q} \cdot\mathbf{r}_{j})

(32)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\sigma}J^{\parallel,\sigma}_{\text{CGT}}\sum_{j}\cos(\mathbf{q} \cdot\mathbf{r}_{j})

\displaystyle-\sum_{\sigma}J^{\perp,\sigma}_{\text{inter}}\sum_{j}\cos(\mathbf{q} \cdot\mathbf{r}_{j}),

where the first sum
\sigma
is the shell index, and in the second sum
j
denotes the different sites within shell
\sigma
. Here, magnetic moments
\mathbf{m}_{j}(\mathbf{q})
are sorted in shells under the condition that the distance
|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{i}|
is the same for every
j
in the same shell. The first two terms (
J_{\text{FGT}}^{\parallel,\sigma}
,
J_{\text{CGT}}^{\parallel,\sigma}
) describe the shell-resolved intralayer magnetic interactions in the FGT and CGT layers, while the last term (
J_{\text{inter}}^{\perp,\sigma}
) corresponds to the interlayer interactions between FGT and CGT.

In Fig.
11
(a), we plot the total spin spiral dispersion curve for FGT/CGT, meaning that the spin spiral propagates in both the FGT and CGT layers. By mapping these total energies onto Eq. (
32
), one obtains exchange constants for FGT/CGT as summarized in Table
2
.
The corresponding interlayer contribution to the Heisenberg exchange is shown in Fig.
11
(b). By fitting this curve, we extract the interlayer contribution in the bilayer system.
We can conclude that the interlayer interactions are relatively weak due to the vdW nature of the interlayer interaction. Therefore, we have ignored them in our atomistic spin simulations for simplicity.

APPENDIX C: EMERGENCE OF BIMERONS ROTATION MODES

It is illustrated in Fig.
12
that, starting from a skyrmion, there exist two ways for constructing rotated bimeron states. These ways differ in the sequence in which canting
\mathrm{R}_{\Theta}
and in-plane rotation
\mathrm{R}_{\Phi}
of all spins on the lattice are performed. The resulting configurations are mirror images of each other with respect to a mirror plane placed between the two merons, described by the operation
\mathrm{D}
. Thus we have
\mathrm{R}_{\Theta}\mathrm{R}_{\Phi}\mathbf{m}_{\text{sk}}=\mathrm{D}\mathrm{R}_{\Phi}\mathrm{R}_{\Theta}\mathbf{m}_{\text{sk}}
and taking into account the magnetic field induced canting by
\Theta_{\text{FM}}
we end up with the magnetic field dependent commutator

\left[\mathrm{R}_{\Phi},~\mathrm{R}_{\Theta_{\text{FM}}}\right]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathrm{D}&\text{if}~~\Theta_{\text{FM}}>0\\
0&\text{else}\end{array}\right..

(33)

This means, that in regime
B_{z}<B_{z}^{\text{sat}}\approx 1.54~
T. where
\Theta_{\text{FM}}>0
is fulfilled, the image spaces of
\mathrm{R}_{\Phi}
and
\mathrm{R}_{\Theta_{\text{FM}}}
are entangled, thus they are not independent of each other. Consequently, there can not exist a single degree of freedom that describes all rotated bimeron states. This becomes apparent by the coexistence of a helicity mode, as depicted in Fig.
12
(a) and an additional rotation, depicted in Fig.
12
(b). The eigenvalues corresponding to these degrees of freedom are found in Fig.
7
in the main text.
