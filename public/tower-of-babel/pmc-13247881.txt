Deep learning-assisted creation of a large-scale cone beam computed tomography dataset for the segmentation of impacted mandibular third molar
Authors: Cheng Niu, Bin Wang, Tingyuan Zeng, Jiangling Sun, Xubo Duan, Zhengfen Li, Meiyan Rong, Liming Chen, Wei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImpacted third molar (ITM) extraction is a common procedure in oral and maxillofacial surgery. The segmentation of ITM is a crucial step in surgery planning. Manually segmenting ITM via cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) images requires significant time and effort from clinicians and may lead to treatment delays. With the advancement of deep learning, medical image segmentation is gradually becoming automated, but it still requires large amounts of well-annotated data for training. The aim of this study was to construct a large-scale CBCT dataset with the assistance of deep learning methods for the segmentation of ITM.MethodsA large-scale CBCT dataset for mandibular ITM segmentation, named “ITM-mandibular nerve (ITM-MN)”, was constructed and comprised 1,262 CBCT image volumes, 186 expert manual annotations, and 1,076 deep learning-assisted annotations. The AttentionUnet3D model, based on an attention mechanism, was employed with a merged annotation strategy to adaptively focus on important feature regions for segmentation tasks.ResultsOn the ITM-MN dataset, AttentionUnet3D achieved the best segmentation results compared to other models, with an average Dice coefficient of 86.46%, an average recall of 84.69%, and a Hausdorff distance of 48.49 mm. The speed of deep learning-assisted annotation was only 16.51±3.78 seconds per case, with quality assessment scores of 3.07±0.66 and 3.29±0.74 from two physicians, respectively.ConclusionsITM-MN is the first large-scale CBCT dataset specifically designed for segmenting ITM. The AttentionUnet3D model achieved effective automatic segmentation of ITM, second molars, and the MN.

Introduction

Third molars often fail to erupt due to insufficient anatomical space, resulting in impacted third molar (ITM). Due to ethnic and geographic variations, the incidence rate among individuals aged 23 years and older ranges from 22% to 95% (1,2). The mandible accounts for 22% to 52.3% of all ITM cases (3-5). ITM can lead to a series of complications, including pericoronitis, distal caries of second molars (SMs), relapse after orthodontic treatment, and temporomandibular joint injury. The shape, size, depth, and orientation of mandibular ITM are highly variable, and there are significant differences across individuals (6). Additionally, the surgical difficulty varies due to influences from SMs, the mandibular nerve (MN), oblique line, and mandibular ramus. However, the execution of ITM extraction relies heavily on the dentist’s experience and may be highly subjective (7). Therefore, development of computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) methods may be needed to provide more objective surgical plans and reduce trauma (8).

Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) is an imaging modality commonly used prior to ITM extraction and facilitates the accurate assessment of the position and morphology of ITM at relatively low cost and radiation dose (9). However, the classification and segmentation of ITM on CBCT images involves certain challenges. First, the presence of erupting third molars and SMs complicates clear differentiation of structural anatomical boundaries. Second, the presence of metal fillings and restorations can introduce artifacts in CBCT images, causing distortion that significantly complicates tooth identification. Third, alveolar bone exhibits density similar to that of tooth structures (10-13). Manually tracing ITM, SMs, and the MN layer by layer on CBCT images is extremely time-consuming and labor-intensive, rendering this approach unsuitable for use in clinic (14).

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) methods based on traditional machine learning and deep learning have demonstrated considerable value in the field of dental CAD; of particular note, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been successfully applied for tooth classification and segmentation. Chen et al. employed occlusal surface morphology analysis using CNN, decomposing the classification task of eight molar types from a single three-dimensional (3D) point cloud model into a two-stage cascaded classification subtask (15). Gao et al. proposed Grouped Bottleneck Transformer, which takes 3D CBCT image patches containing regions of interest for teeth as input and outputs the tooth type, and achieved a classification accuracy of 91.3% (16). Yoo et al. employed a residual neural network (ResNet) to predict the C1 (depth), C2 (lambertian relationship), and C3 (angle) of mandibular third molars on panoramic radiographs (17). Wu et al. proposed a two-stage grid deep learning approach for automatic tooth segmentation in 3D intraoral scans (18). Moreover, Shaheen et al. employed a 3D U-Net to automatically segment multiple tooth classes in cone-beam computed tomography images through a three-step approach (19). Sheng et al. introduced the Swin-Unet architecture, a Transformer-based U-shaped encoder-decoder model for panoramic radiographic tooth segmentation (20). Finally, Li et al. developed a spatial prior-guided Transformer method designed to extract tooth position features from CNN and remote context information from visual transformers for dental panoramic X-ray image segmentation (21).

These aforementioned methods have yielded promising results, further demonstrating the immense potential of deep learning approaches in ITM segmentation tasks. However, certain limitations persist in the application of these methods to ITM segmentation tasks. First, the majority of the previous work exclusively conducted training and evaluation on internal datasets and primarily focused on permanent teeth. Meanwhile, few studies have specifically addressed the analysis of the third molars, and virtually no research has examined the significant multiscale challenges posed by ITM, SMs, and MN segmentation. Second, most prior work has addressed classification and segmentation on two-dimensional (2D) images, but the 3D segmentation of ITMs via CBCT scans remains largely underexplored (22,23). Consequently, there is an urgent need for a publicly available ITM-related dataset with high-quality expert annotations and effective models capable of tackling the multiscale challenges inherent in the 3D segmentation of ITM (24).

In the study by Wang et al., a dental X-ray dataset containing 400 cephalometric radiographs and 120 bitewing radiographs was employed. Nineteen landmarks were manually annotated in each cephalometric radiograph, and seven locations including caries, enamel, crown, dentin, pulp, root canal treatment and restoration were manually annotated in each bitewing radiograph (25). Meanwhile, the dataset proposed by Silva et al. contains 1,500 annotated panoramic X-ray images. Images in this dataset were manually categorized into 10 categories, and all images were cropped to 1,991×1,127 pixels (26). This dataset is suitable for 2D tooth detection and semantic segmentation research. CTooth (27) and CTooth+ (28) were developed by Cui et al. in 2022 and were the first public 3D CBCT datasets. The datasets consist of 5,504 annotated CBCT slices of 22 patients and 25,876 unlabeled CBCT slices of 146 patients, but most lack any annotations. The National Kaohsiung University of Science and Technology (NKUT) dataset, developed by Zhou et al. in 2024, was the first of its kind specifically designed for pediatric mandibular wisdom teeth segmentation. It comprises 133 CBCT volumes with over 53,000 slices. Patient ages range from 7 to 22 years, with a mean age of 13.2 years (24). These datasets involved random selection and were not specifically designed for ITM, and none provide MN annotations. These factors impose significant limitations when these datasets are directly applied for ITM extraction procedures. Therefore, constructing a publicly available, large-scale, ITM-specific dataset is crucial for advancing specialized research on ITM.

The overarching goal of this study was to construct a large-scale CBCT imaging dataset dedicated to the automatic segmentation of ITM and to systematically benchmark several baseline deep learning segmentation models on this dataset. It is hoped this work can serve as provide a foundational resource for subsequent research in computer-aided surgical planning. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD+AI reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1-2791/rc).

Methods

Data acquisition

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Guiyang Hospital of Stomatology (approval No. GYSKLL-KY-20250117-01). Informed consent was obtained from all the participants.

A total of 1,262 CBCT volumes were collected from the Guiyang Hospital of Stomatology (a grade A tertiary hospital) with the i-CAT17-19 intraoral X-ray CT device (Imaging Sciences International, Inc., Hatfield, PA, USA) and the 3D Accuitomo F17 intraoral X-ray digital tomography device (J. Morita Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Data were only included from cases who met the following criteria: (I) presence of at least one impacted mandibular third molar and (II) adequate CBCT image quality for diagnostic evaluation. Meanwhile, cases with severe artifacts obscuring the MN or tooth boundaries were excluded. The age distribution of the include cases was as follows: 9% were younger than 20 years old, 62% were between 20 and 30 years old, 22% were between 30 and 40 years old, and 7% were over 40 years old. In terms of gender distribution, 33% were male and 67% were female. No visual enhancements were applied, and all files were anonymized before public access was facilitated.

The annotation tool used for the 186 manually annotated cases was Slicer 3D, with the process divided into two stages: annotation and review. There were five types of pixel-level annotations: mandibular left SM [37], mandibular left third molar [38], mandibular right SM [47], mandibular right third molar [48], and the MN. During annotation, physicians from Guiyang Hospital of Stomatology marked coronal images at 0.25-mm intervals, with approximately 150 slices per case. They then refined annotations in the transverse and sagittal views to achieve high-quality pixel-level labels. The single instance annotation process took approximately 4 hours. In the review phase, a senior dental specialist conducted two rounds of slice-by-slice verification, completing manual annotation as shown in Figure 1. Deep learning model architecture and training process

We randomly partitioned the 186 expert-annotated cases into a training set (149 cases) and an independent test set (37 cases) at an 8:2 ratio. The training set was used for model training, while the test set was used for the final evaluation and comparison of the models’ performance. During the training process, we did not set up an independent validation set for early stopping or hyperparameter tuning; the final model was selected based on the highest average Dice coefficient achieved on the test set. All model performance evaluations were conducted exclusively on the aforementioned manually annotated independent test set. We strictly ensured all splits were performed at the patient level, meaning all data from the same patient appeared in only one of the training or independent test subsets to prevent information leakage. As shown in Figure 2, automatic segmentation of ITM, SMs, and the MN in CBCT images was conducted via the proposed AttentionUnet3D architecture based on attention mechanisms. This network introduces attention gate mechanisms on top of the standard U-net, reduces redundant skip connections, and can adaptively focus on important feature regions, thereby enhancing segmentation accuracy for complex anatomical structures.

Overall structure of the proposed AttentionUnet3D. 3D, three-dimensional; ReLU, rectified linear unit.

To benchmark AttentionUnet3D’s performance, we compared it against two widely adopted and foundational 3D segmentation architectures: Unet3D (29) and Vnet (30). These models were selected as baseline comparators because they are well-established standards in the field, representing core architectural paradigms (U-shaped encoder-decoder and volumetric fully convolutional networks, respectively), which allowed for a clear assessment of the benefits introduced by our attention gate modifications.

Each stage of the encoder consists of two 3×3×3 convolutional layers with rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation functions, followed by a 2×2×2 max pooling layer for downsampling. The channel counts are 32, 64, 128, 256, and 512, with feature map dimensions progressively reduced from the input 160×160×160 to 20×20×20. This path is responsible for extracting multiscale feature representations, capturing information ranging from local details to global context. The attention weight ψ ∈ [0,1] is used to weight encoder features, highlighting regions relevant to the given segmentation task. The final output layer employs a 1×1×1 convolution to map the 32-channel feature map onto 4 output channels, corresponding to the background, SMs (the mandibular left AM [37] and mandibular right SM [47] combined), ITM (the mandibular left third molar [38] and mandibular right third molar [48] combined), and the MN.

The window level and window width for CBCT images were set to 600 and 3,200, respectively. During the 3D training phase, we employed a region-of-interest-based block extraction strategy. All CBCT volumetric data were resampled to an isotropic resolution of 0.5 mm × 0.5 mm × 0.5 mm via bilinear interpolation. During training, an overlapping sliding window strategy was employed for inference, with 160×160×160 voxel image blocks being randomly cropped for the network input.

For label handling, we employed a merged annotation strategy: the 37 and 47 are merged into category 1, the 38 and 48 are merged into category 2, the MN is labeled as category 3, and the background regions are labeled as category 0.

We applied data augmentation techniques such as horizontal flipping, vertical flipping, and random rotation to mitigate overfitting (31). All models were trained with the AdamW optimizer (32) at an initial learning rate of 0.0003. The minimum training cycle count was set to 50, and PyTorch Lightning’s built-in early stopping mechanism (33) was employed to terminate the training process promptly. We employed a stochastic weight averaging strategy (34) for learning rate scheduling, adjusting the learning rate from its initial value to 5×10−2 over 20 epochs using a cosine annealing strategy. This enhances the model’s generalization capability and training stability. During training, a gradient accumulation strategy is employed with an accumulation step size of 8 and an effective batch size of 8. If the validation loss fails to decrease for 10 consecutive epochs, the training process is terminated early to prevent overfitting. The above parameters were selected based on preliminary experiments to ensure stable convergence. All training was implemented via the PyTorch, MONAI, and PyTorch Lightning frameworks, with 3D experiments conducted on an RTX 3090 GPU (Nvidia, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

The following metrics were systematically adopted to evaluate model performance: Dice coefficient, recall, 95% Hausdorff distance (HD; in millimeters), and parameter count.

Deep learning-assisted annotation generation

For the 1,076 CBCT images, the same preprocessing method was applied as described above. The model checkpoint with the best performance on the independent validation set (in terms of average Dice coefficient) was selected. The probability map output by the model underwent softmax activation and argmax operations to obtain the predicted category for each voxel. The prediction results were resampled to the original image dimensions via k-nearest neighbor interpolation, with the original image’s spacing, origin, and orientation matrix being inherited to maintain spatial consistency. The final segmentation results were saved in nearly raw raster data (NRRD) format.

A random sample of 10% (107 cases) from the generated 1,076 annotated cases was assessed in double-blinded fashion by two maxillofacial surgeons with over 3 years of experience using the following scoring criteria: 5 points = excellent, directly usable for surgical planning without modification; 4 points = good, requiring only minor adjustments (<5 minutes); 3 points = fair, requiring moderate modifications (5–10 minutes) but not without judgment of primary anatomical structures; 2 points = poor, requiring significant revisions (>10 minutes) and with errors in critical structure boundaries; and 1 point = unusable, completely incorrect and requiring complete reannotation.

The ITM-MN dataset

We constructed a CBCT dataset named “ITM-MN” specifically for the 3D segmentation task of ITM. The dataset comprised 1,262 CBCT volumes, over 600,000 slices, 186 experts manually annotated cases, and 1,076 annotations generated with the assistance of a deep learning model.

The 1,076 autogenerated masks were not subsequently manually corrected by experts. In the evaluation of automatically generated annotation quality, the average scores provided by the two physicians (3.07±0.66 and 3.29±0.74) indicated that the quality of the automatically generated annotations was moderate, meaning they required a moderate level of manual modification (5–10 minutes) but did not affect the judgment of primary anatomical structures.

We also found that generating single-instance annotations using deep learning models took approximately 16.51±3.78 seconds, whereas manual annotation requires about 4 hours. Automated annotation was 872 times faster than was manual annotation, significantly boosting efficiency and reducing physicians’ workload.

Discussion

Due to the lack of a large-scale, publicly available CBCT dataset for segmenting ITM, SMs, and the MN (35,36), we successfully constructed ITM-MN, a large-scale CBCT dataset specifically tailored for segmenting ITM. Compared to existing dental datasets, ITM-MN is not only larger but also incorporates essential annotation information such as ITM type and the MN. In contrast to the similar previous research—which primarily focused on tooth classification or the segmentation of all permanent teeth in 2D or 3D (15,16,18,20,21)—our study addresses a distinct and surgically critical niche: the integrated 3D segmentation of the ITM, its adjacent SM, and the MN. We consider these three structures to be indispensable for determining the type of impaction, analyzing the surgical approach, and assessing the risk of nerve injury. This task-specific design presents unique challenges while also offering potential advantages over more general dental image analysis methods.

Experimental results demonstrated that the proposed AttentionUnet3D performs exceptionally well in complex anatomical segmentation tasks. Its attention mechanism effectively addresses the multiscale challenges inherent in ITM segmentation. The model achieved high segmentation accuracy (a Dice coefficient of 86.46%) while maintaining a relatively reasonable parameter count (23.6 million). The high average Dice coefficient indicates that the model can accurately capture the vast majority of the ITM, SM, and MN voxels. This is a fundamental prerequisite for generating reliable 3D models that can be used for preoperative simulation and planning (37). Specifically, accurate segmentation of ITM and SMs allows for the precise assessment of the impaction type, which is critical for deciding upon the approach for surgical access and the need for coronectomy (38,39). More critically, the segmentation of the MN is paramount for risk assessment. A lower HD value suggests that the worst-case localization error of the nerve canal is bounded, which provides surgeons with a quantifiable confidence margin when evaluating the risk of iatrogenic nerve injury (40). AttentionUnet3D reduces the single-instance annotation time from 4 hours to under 20 seconds. Our approach consisting of expert annotation and model assistance offers a viable solution for the construction of large-scale medical image datasets.

It is worth noting that despite the model’s overall excellent performance, certain limitations remain. In some cases, AttentionUnet3D may segment MN to be slightly thinner than manual annotations. This could stem from several interrelated factors: (I) the inherent physical resolution limit of CBCT scanners, which may not fully resolve the finest nerve branches, leading the model to learn a more conservative, central estimate; (II) the anisotropic downsampling applied during preprocessing to standardize voxel spacing, which might cause subtle, discontinuous nerve structures in the z-axis to be suppressed; and (III) the attention mechanism itself, which, while focusing on salient regions, might occasionally oversuppress faint or noisy features at the nerve boundary (41). In certain instances, a layer may be missing, potentially due to the attention mechanism oversuppressing features on specific blurred slices or z-axis information loss caused by 3D downsampling (42). Continuous refinement and expert review are required to meet clinical application standards. Furthermore, the model comparison in this study was conducted against fundamental baseline architectures. It served as a necessary baseline evaluation rather than an exhaustive comparison against all contemporary state-of-the-art methods. Future work should involve more comprehensive benchmarking against a broader range of advanced models for facilitating further optimization. Finally, the reported time-saving (i.e., 872 times faster) was determined based on the idealized endpoints of full manual annotation and fully automated inference. A more practical assessment should account for the necessary expert review and correction time for the autogenerated masks, which, based on our quality evaluation, is estimated to be an additional 5–10 minutes per case. Another limitation is that all data were sourced from a single center. Although this ensured internal consistency, it might have introduced selection bias and may limit the model’s generalizability to data from other institutions with different scanners and patient demographics. In addition, there were several methodological limitations: (I) in the data division, a training set and a test set were established, but not an independent validation set. This practice may not completely avoid potential overfitting to the test set during the model selection process, which could result in a slightly optimistic estimate of the reported performance metrics; (II) we used the built-in metric calculation functions of the MONAI framework to obtain the average values of the model metrics and did not save the intermediate scores of each individual sample in the default workflow. This means that we are currently unable to directly extract the raw scores of each test case from the completed experiment records to calculate the standard deviation. Thus, it is difficult to judge robustness across cases, especially for the MN. In future work, we will implement a case-wise evaluation log to facilitate such analysis.

Specifically, we will first expand the scale and diversity of our dataset by, for example, adding mandibular alveolar bone annotations and incorporating more complex cases. Second, we will optimize the model architecture to enhance segmentation accuracy for minute structures, aiming to meet clinical application standards. Third, we will develop an intelligent diagnostic and planning system for ITM surgery that can determine impaction types based on 3D models, decide whether to perform crown resection, and plan extraction paths based on biomechanical principles. It is anticipated that the release of the ITM-MN dataset will foster research collaboration and technological advancement in this field. Moreover, we hope our work can assist clinicians in evaluating surgeries more efficiently and objectively and in determining optimal surgical plans.

Conclusions

We collected and annotated a 3D CBCT dataset, ITM-MN, which is the first large-scale dataset specifically designed for 3D ITM segmentation. We believe ITM-MN holds importance for segmentation research in dental ITM extraction procedures. Experiments were conducted on the proposed ITM-MN dataset, which demonstrated that our AttentionUnet3D network can serve as a baseline for ITM 3D segmentation tasks and assist in dataset creation.
