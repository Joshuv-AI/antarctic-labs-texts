Rapid phospholipid flipping protects the rice plasma membrane against heat stress
Authors: Tao Chen, Yi Han, Yanglin Ding
Published: 2026

Main text

Rising global temperatures severely compromise crop yields, and destabilization of the plasma membrane (PM) is among the earliest forms of cellular damage caused by heat stress (Ding et al., 2020). As the primary physical barrier and signaling hub of plant cells, the PM relies on biophysical properties, such as membrane thickness and fluidity, to withstand heat. Changes in these properties may be detected by PM-resident sensor proteins that activate distinct protective pathways (Renne and Ernst, 2023). For example, the PM-localized E3 ligase TT3.1 (THERMOTOLERANCE 3.1) translocates to endosomes during heat stress and promotes the degradation of toxic chloroplast precursors, thereby establishing a PM–chloroplast axis that protects photosynthetic membranes (Zhang et al., 2022). The BAM1 (BARELY ANY MERISTEM 1)–PBS1 (AVRPPHB SUSCEPTIBLE 1) receptor complex initiates reactive oxygen species-mediated signaling to activate heat-responsive transcriptional reprogramming (Li et al., 2025). FER (FERONIA) serves as a thermal receptor that is activated by RALF34 (RAPID ALKALINIZATION FACTOR 34) under moderate heat (e.g., 37°C); it assembles sterol-rich PM nanoclusters to maintain ordered lipid microdomains and activates heat shock factor (HSF)-dependent stress signaling (Wang et al., 2026). Together, these PM sensory modules are crucial for plant heat tolerance, highlighting the need to maintain PM integrity and preserve the sensing and signal-transduction functions of membrane-localized receptors.

Bacteria and animals have evolved diverse mechanisms to counteract heat-induced structural defects in the PM (Inda et al., 2014; Ma et al., 2015). A recent study identified a P4-ATPase lipid-flipping complex responsible for rapid membrane structural stabilization (Fan et al., 2026). This study distinguishes this rapid response from previously characterized thermoregulatory pathways in microbes and animals and expands the framework for understanding plant membrane homeostasis.

In prokaryotes and nematodes, integral membrane proteins couple biophysical changes in the bilayer to transcriptional programs that adjust global lipid composition and maintain membrane integrity (Figure 1). In Bacillus subtilis, the membrane-localized histidine kinase DesK directly detects temperature-dependent changes in bilayer thickness. Heat-induced bilayer thinning alters the charged linker domain of DesK and switches its catalytic activity. Downstream transcriptional cascades then regulate fatty acid desaturase genes, gradually adjusting lipid saturation (Inda et al., 2014). In Caenorhabditis elegans, ACDH-11 (acyl-CoA dehydrogenase) sequesters cytosolic C11/C12 fatty acids and inhibits nuclear hormome recetor (NHR)-49-dependent fat-7 (stearoyl-CoA desaturase) expression. This response lowers unsaturated lipid levels and limits heat-induced PM hyperfluidity (Ma et al., 2015). Together, these conserved pathways depend on de novo lipid synthesis and transcriptional reprogramming and therefore operate over intermediate timescales to sustain cellular function during prolonged temperature changes.

Working model of plasma membrane-mediated thermal adaptation strategies in diverse organisms.

(A) In B. subtilis, temperature-dependent changes in bilayer thickness alter the interaction between the lipid bilayer and the linker region of DesK (green). This interaction switches DesK between kinase and phosphatase states, thereby modulating downstream transcription by controlling DesR phosphorylation and the expression of the fatty acid desaturase gene des. 25°C and 37°C represent low and high temperatures, respectively.

(B) In C. elegans, EGL-25 is proposed to increase cellular C11/C12 fatty acid pools and facilitates signaling through nuclear hormone receptors (NHRs), thereby supporting cold adaptation at 15°C. Under heat stress at 25°C, ACDH-11 is induced. ACDH-11 sequesters C11/C12 fatty acids and blocks NHR activation, thereby repressing transcription of the desaturase gene fat-7. This cascade reduces membrane lipid desaturation and stabilizes the plasma membrane (PM) to confer thermotolerance.

(C) In rice (Oryza sativa), under heat stress (45°C), the PM-localized OsALA5–OsALIS2 complex rapidly transfers saturated phosphatidylcholines (PCs) from the outer to the inner membrane leaflet, increasing the proportion of saturated PCs in the cytoplasmic PM leaflet and thereby enhancing membrane stability, reducing heat-induced damage, and promoting heat tolerance. 28°C represents the normal growth temperature.

Fan et al. (2026) identified a minute-scale, transcription-independent heat defense module centered on the rice PM P4-ATPase complex OsALA5–OsALIS2, revealing an additional layer of membrane thermoprotection (Figure 1). Biochemical, lipidomic, and genetic analyses show that heat induces conformational expansion in the substrate pocket of OsALA5, selectively increasing its ATPase activity and transport preference for saturated phosphatidylcholine (PC). Within minutes, the complex flips saturated PCs to the cytoplasmic leaflet to increase bilayer packing, limit heat-induced hyperfluidity and ion leakage, and prevent cell damage. Loss of OsALA5 causes severe heat sensitivity, whereas its overexpression substantially improves seedling survival and reproductive yield under heat stress. Orthologous PC-transporting P4-ATPases (Arabidopsis ALA10/11 and yeast Dnf1/Dnf2) provide similar protection, indicating that this lipid-flipping strategy is conserved across eukaryotes. Population genetic analyses further identified a rare high-expression OsALA5 haplotype (Hap7); multi-site, multi-year field trials showed that this allele helps maintain grain set and yield during natural heatwaves, highlighting its value for crop improvement (Fan et al., 2026).

Conceptually, the study extends the canonical framework of thermal membrane homeostasis. Previous models of heat adaptation in prokaryotes and nematodes have centered on transcriptional reprogramming of fatty acid desaturases. In contrast, OsALA5-driven lipid redistribution operates independently of de novo lipid production. Together, these responses form a two-tiered protective system that combines rapid physical stabilization of the bilayer with delayed, transcriptionally controlled lipid remodeling. By linking membrane biophysics, evolutionary physiology, and crop breeding, this work identifies a mechanism for rapidly mitigating membrane damage and offers a tractable genetic target for developing climate-resilient cereal varieties amid rising global temperatures.

Lipids have two major roles in plant heat acclimation: as structural components, they maintain bilayer fluidity; as second messengers, they initiate heat-signaling cascades. A recent study showed that heat activates DGK7 (DIACYLGLYCEROL KINASE 7), which converts diacylglycerol into phosphatidic acid. The resulting phosphatidic acid acts as a lipid messenger that transmits thermal signals from the PM to the nucleus and coordinates heat-responsive transcription (Kan et al., 2025). DGK7-mediated phosphatidic acid production constitutes a distinct lipid thermosignaling pathway. By contrast, OsALA5 stabilizes the membrane rapidly and independently of the slower, transcription-dependent remodeling of lipid synthesis and fatty acid saturation that occurs during sustained heat. It remains unknown whether OsALA5-dependent structural remodeling acts early to preserve bilayer integrity, sustain the activity of PM-localized signaling proteins such as DGK7, and enable downstream lipid-messenger signaling. Together, these studies distinguish the structural homeostatic and signal-transducing functions of lipids during heat stress.

Together, findings from prokaryotes, animals, and land plants support an integrated model of thermal homeostasis in which temporally layered PM effectors and signaling pathways coordinate to preserve bilayer properties during heat stress.

Despite these advances, several research priorities and questions remain: (1) A systematic functional characterization of P4-ATPase lipid-flipping modules across a broad range of dicot species is needed. (2) Does OsALA5-mediated lipid redistribution directly influence downstream thermal signaling after heat-induced membrane hyperfluidity, and does the resulting PM stabilization modulate PM-localized thermosensors? (3) Do vegetative and reproductive tissues differ in their reliance on OsALA5-mediated lipid homeostasis? (4) How did eukaryotes acquire the rapid, transcription-independent P4-ATPase defense module alongside the slower transcriptional remodeling pathways found in prokaryotes and nematodes? Determining whether intermediate eukaryotic lineages possess both systems and whether the pathways can compensate for one another will help clarify their evolutionary relationship.
