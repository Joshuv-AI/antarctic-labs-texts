Evaluating the reliability of large language models for clinical data extraction in bladder cancer prognosis
Authors: Di Sun, Lubomir Hadjiiski, Grace Bruno, John Gormley, Heang-Ping Chan, Elaine M. Caoili, Richard H. Cohan, Ajjai Alva, Rada Mihalcea, Chuan Zhou, Vikas Gulani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Advances in natural language processing (NLP) and machine learning could assist human users in clinical data extraction from unstructured electronic medical records (EMRs). This study investigates the accuracy and consistency of several Large Language Models (LLMs) - including Dolly, Vicuna, Llama, and GPT-4 - in extracting critical clinical information pertinent to bladder cancer survival prediction. Using EMRs from 163 bladder cancer patients, we assessed the impact on LLM performance by factors such as differences in the trained models, model evolution, input text length, and sequencing of case inputs. GPT-4 demonstrated superior performance with Fleiss’ Kappa values exceeding 0.97, accuracy consistently above 93%, and survival prediction metrics closely aligned with ground truth (AUC ± 0.02). Among offline models, Llama-2.0-13b and Llama-3.3-70b exhibited the highest reliability in both information extraction and survival prediction. This study underscores the potential of LLMs to automate clinical data extraction for predictive modeling while highlighting the challenges related to LLM variability and reliability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27593-7.

Introduction

The potential applications of large language models (LLMs) in healthcare are vast, from automating routine tasks1–3 to supporting complex decision-making processes4–6. For instance, in oncology, where patient records often contain extensive unstructured information, LLMs could streamline workflows by extracting and organizing relevant data for further use with imaging and/or clinical evaluation7. Furthermore, the extracted data could be integrated rapidly into predictive models for more robust assessments of patient prognosis.

Despite these potential advantages, deploying LLMs in healthcare poses several challenges. Model performance variability, particularly due to the inherent probabilistic nature8,9 of LLMs and differences in input prompts10,11 or model updates, can potentially lead to outcome inconsistencies. Consistent LLM performance is essential for reliable information retrieval. Even with identical prompts and contexts, LLMs can exhibit variability, which must be investigated to ensure accurate and reproducible outcomes in clinical settings. Minimizing this variability may be achieved by controlling model stochasticity (i.e. LLM temperature) or through prompt engineering10 with the addition of explicit constraints.

Bladder cancer remains a significant clinical challenge, ranking as the fourth most common malignancy in men in the United States12. It is also one of the most challenging and expensive cancers to diagnose and treat13,14. Accurate survival prediction for bladder cancer patients is critical for guiding treatment decisions and post-surgical care, particularly following radical cystectomy, a commonly performed procedure for the treatment of localized muscle-invasive bladder cancer.

Current survival prediction models15 rely primarily on nomograms associated with clinicopathological information from patient records, including age, gender, pathologic T stage (pT), pathologic node stage (pN), pathologic grade, and presence or absence of lymphovascular invasion (LVI). Manually extracting such data from unstructured records, such as pathology reports and surgical notes, has traditionally been labor-intensive and error-prone. However, advances in LLMs offer a promising solution by automating the process of data extraction.

In previous studies, we proposed a multi-modal predictive model16 that integrated clinical, radiomics, and deep-learning descriptors (termed the Clinical-Radiomics-Deep learning (CRD) model) to predict five-year survival of bladder cancer patients. Subsequently, we proposed an enhanced LLM-aided CRD model7 that utilized LLMs to facilitate the automatic retrieval of clinical information. Building on this foundation, here we evaluate the variability of several LLMs in extracting clinical information from unstructured medical records of bladder cancer patients and the impact of this variability on the predictive performance of the multi-modal LLM-aided CRD model7. LLMs could have a high clinical impact by automatically extracting clinical data as precursors or adjuncts to image review or other clinical tasks. Thus, we aim to deepen the understanding of LLM variability and its implications for clinical data extraction, as well as the impact of this variability on clinical decision-making models, using bladder cancer survival prediction as an exemplar.

Results

Using either snippet or full reports, different LLMs, and runs conducted on different dates, we devised several experimental settings to evaluate the reliability of the LLMs. The details of these experimental settings and the results for each setting are shown in Table 1, which include: (1) agreement (measured by Fleiss’ Kappa) among the extracted information across 10 runs for 163 medical records, (2) accuracy (mean ± standard deviation), (3) agreement (measured by Krippendorff’s Alpha) among the nomogram-predicted survival scores across the 10 runs, and (4) survival prediction performance in terms of the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). We also presented the inference time of each model in Table 2.

Information extraction consistency

Model effect

The reproducibility of clinical information extraction by six LLMs — Dolly-v2-12b, Vicuna-v1.3-13b, Llama-2.0-13b, Llama-3.3-70b, GPT-4 Turbo, and GPT-4o — showed considerable differences. GPT-4 Turbo and GPT-4o exhibited the highest consistency (κ > 0.98) averaging the degree of agreement across the three variables (pT, pN, LVI), followed by Llama-2.0-13b (κ = 0.96) and Llama-3.3-70b (κ = 0.94). Dolly-v2-12b and Vicuna-v1.3-13b demonstrated lower consistency (~ 0.85).

Analysis of each individual variable revealed that determination of the presence or absence of LVI had the lowest consistency by Dolly-v2-12b, Vicuna-v1.3-13b, and Llama-3.3-70b, with values 4%-9% below the average, whereas Llama-2.0-13b, GPT-4 Turbo, and GPT-4o maintained similar consistency across all three variables.

Accuracy comparisons mirrored consistency trends, with GPT-4o achieving the highest accuracy (> 96%), followed by GPT-4 Turbo, Llama-2.0-13b, and Llama-3.3-70b (~ 93%). Dolly-v2-12b and Vicuna-v1.3-13b recorded significantly lower accuracy at 84% and 73%, respectively.

Heatmaps in Fig. 1 illustrate 10-run agreement and accuracy. Ideal performance, indicated by yellow cells for 10-time agreement and alignment with the ground truth (red boxes), was most evident for GPT-4o. Among offline models, Llama-2.0-13b and Llama-3.3-70b displayed better consistency and accuracy, while Dolly-v2-12b and Vicuna-v1.3-13b lagged.

Model evolution over time

GPT-4o was tested at four time points (22 Nov, 02 Dec, 13 Dec 2024, and 11 Jan 2025) to assess the impact of model updates on performance. Across the first three time points separated by 10-day intervals, the model maintained exceptional agreement (AVG κ > 0.98) and high accuracy (95%-97%). Extending the interval to 30 days resulted in similar agreement, though pT accuracy decreased slightly to 93%.

Input length and order effects

The performance of GPT-4 Turbo and GPT-4o was evaluated using both snippet-length and full-length reports to determine the impact of input length on the accuracy of clinical information extraction. GPT-4 Turbo consistently achieved high Kappa values (≥ 0.95) across both input lengths. While there were slight differences in accuracy, snippet-length reports yielded a lower accuracy of 89% on pT, compared to 92% for full-length reports. These results indicate that GPT-4 Turbo effectively handles both brief and detailed inputs with comparable accuracy, provided the input remains within the token limit. Additionally, randomizing the order of input cases had no significant effect on either the 10-run agreement or the accuracy of the extracted information. This demonstrates that GPT-4 Turbo operates independently in each session, maintaining the integrity of extracted data and avoiding any carryover effects from prior interactions.

Similarly, GPT-4o exhibited minimal sensitivity to input length or sequence order. Kappa values and extraction accuracy remained consistently high across all experiments, with Kappa values exceeding 0.97 and accuracy consistently above 95%. Compared to GPT-4 Turbo, GPT-4o demonstrated slightly higher agreement and accuracy across all conditions, showing its stronger reliability and robustness in clinical information extraction tasks.

Survival prediction reliability

We evaluated survival prediction using two models: a nomogram based solely on clinical information17 and a multi-modal model (CRD)7 that incorporates clinical information, radiomics features, and deep-learning-derived features from CT images. Figure 2 illustrates the performance of the nomogram (evaluated on 163 and 64 cases) and CRD (evaluated on 64 cases). The ideal model, as indicated by the boxplot, would exhibit the highest AUC value with the narrowest distribution across the 10 runs. The results show that the CRD model achieves a higher AUC than the nomogram (N = 64), consistent with findings from our previous study7.

Model effect

In the nomogram analysis, survival predictions based on information extracted by Llama-3.3-70b, GPT-4 Turbo, and GPT-4o closely matched the ground truth prediction (AUC = 0.78), with each model achieving an average prediction of 0.77 among the 10 runs. Llama-2.0-13b and Dolly-v2-12b demonstrated similar average performance (AUC = 0.75); however, predictions from Dolly-v2-12b showed higher variability (± 0.015) compared to those from Llama-2.0-13b (± 0.004). Among the models, Llama-2.0-13b, Llama-3.3-70b, and GPT-4o exhibited the least variation (± 0.004), followed by GPT-4 Turbo (± 0.008), while Dolly-v2-12b and Vicuna-v1.3-13b showed the largest variations (± 0.015).

For the CRD model, survival predictions from Dolly-v2-12b, Llama-3.3-70b, GPT-4 Turbo, and GPT-4o closely aligned with the ground truth prediction (AUC = 0.89), with an average around 0.87. Llama-2.0-13b had a slightly lower AUC of 0.85, and Vicuna-v1.3-13b yielded the lowest at 0.80. GPT-4o showed the smallest variation (± 0.002), followed by Llama-2.0-13b (± 0.006). GPT-4 Turbo, Dolly-v2-12b, and Llama-3.3-70b exhibited moderate variations (between ± 0.01 and ± 0.02), while Vicuna-v1.3-13b had the largest variation (± 0.023).

Model evolution over time

With the extracted information by GPT-4o across four time points, survival prediction performance for both the nomogram and CRD models remained closely aligned with those using ground truth clinical information, with minimal variation ( < ± 0.005 for nomogram; <±0.006 for CRD). This stability highlights a critical consideration for clinical applications of LLMs: while updates to LLMs are intended to enhance overall capabilities, they may inadvertently introduce variability that impacts performance in precision-dependent tasks like medical data extraction. Ongoing monitoring and validation of LLM performance are essential to ensure updates do not negatively affect clinical decision making.

Input length and order effects

Variations in input length and sequential order had no notable impact on the performance of either the nomogram or CRD models in survival prediction. This robustness underscores the reliability of these LLMs for the task being studied.

Discussion

The findings of this study have significant implications for the application of LLMs in clinical practice, particularly in oncology, where timely, accurate, and reliable extraction of information from EMRs is critical. Our work is clearly distinct from other research exploring LLM-based or NLP-based information extraction for clinical contexts, which has largely emphasized improving accuracy through model design or prompt optimization. For example, Hu et al.18 explored prompt engineering to enhance clinical named entity recognition (NER) performance, achieving relaxed F1 scores of 0.794 and 0.861 on the MTSamples dataset with GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. Mutinda et al.19 used a BERT-based NER model to extract Participants, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcomes (PICO) information from research abstracts for meta-analysis, achieving extraction F1 scores above 0.80 but reporting limited downstream statistical performance. Chen et al.20 proposed FinePICO, a semi-supervised model for fine-grained PICO entity recognition and Zhang et al.21 developed a span-based model to extract overlapping PICO entities from randomized controlled trial publications— neither of these two studies involves the use of LLMs. Thatoi et al.22 reviewed applications of NLP techniques for electronic health records (EHRs) information extraction in cancer prognosis.

In contrast, our experiments with state-of-the-art LLMs, such as Llama and GPT models, achieved extraction accuracy exceeding 0.93 and downstream model performance comparable to predictions relying on ground truth clinical data. Nevertheless, rather than focusing solely on maximizing extraction accuracy, our study systematically evaluates the reliability of LLMs in clinical information extraction across multiple dimensions: model architecture, temporal evolution of LLMs, input length, and input order. We further investigate how this reliability affects downstream prognosis models in the context of bladder cancer.

This emphasis on reliability is critical, as it directly impacts the feasibility of deploying LLMs in a high-stakes clinical application. Our findings provide a deeper insight into the conditions under which LLMs can be trusted for complex medical decision-making tasks.

We next highlight several aspects of our findings that warrant in-depth discussion:

Type of information

Our results demonstrate that the type of information being extracted can impact the accuracy and consistency of LLMs. Specifically, we observed lower agreement among Dolly-v2-12b, Vicuna-v1.3-13b, and Llama-3.3-70b for lymphovascular invasion (LVI), compared to other data, such as pathological tumor (pT) and node (pN) staging. In contrast, GPT-4 Turbo and GPT-4o exhibited slightly higher agreement for LVI.

LLM output ambiguity

An important aspect observed in our evaluation is the presence of “invalid” responses—answers that are entirely irrelevant to the given questions. These outputs can be regarded as manifestations of hallucination, a known limitation of LLMs, where the model generates nonsensical or factually incorrect content. The frequency of such responses varied across models, reflecting differences in robustness and reliability. Summary statistics of invalid response rates are provided in Appendix I, and the top 3 most frequently occurring “invalid” responses for each of the six models used in this study are listed in Appendix II for transparency and further reference.

The occurrence of these responses underscores the need for more reliable mechanisms to detect and mitigate hallucinations in LLMs, especially in high-stakes domains like healthcare. These failures may also stem from limitations in retrieval grounding due to insufficient or missing contextual information. This could potentially be mitigated through the integration of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) techniques or improved prompt design to ensure better alignment between queries and generated content.

Implications for model selection

In comparing the performance between Llama-2.0-13b and Llama-3.3-70b, it is evident that the assumption that models with more parameters necessarily yield higher accuracy or reliability does not hold true for tasks like clinical information extraction. Larger models also require significantly more computational resources, which may not be feasible in many clinical settings. When selecting models for such tasks, the number of parameters should not be the sole determining factor. Instead, performance consistency and computational efficiency should be prioritized.

Notably, we found that survival prediction accuracy and overall information extraction accuracy were not linearly correlated. Furthermore, the propagation of inaccuracies varied among models depending on the type of information extracted, emphasizing the need for careful evaluation of downstream impacts.

Among the evaluated models, GPT-4o stood out for its high reproducibility and accuracy (Fleiss’ Kappa > 0.97 and accuracy > 93%) across various conditions. This consistency underscores its potential to reduce manual data entry time significantly, allowing clinicians to focus more on patient care. By accurately extracting key clinical variables such as pathologic stage, lymph node status, and LVI, LLMs like GPT-4o facilitate the deployment of survival prediction models, improve clinical decision-making, and streamline workflows. In large healthcare systems, where vast amounts of unstructured data must be processed quickly and accurately, automation provided by LLMs can be transformative.

Considerations for offline models

For healthcare environments with strict data privacy, security, or resource constraints, offline models are often necessary. Among the offline models, Llama-2.0-13b and Llama-3.3-70b demonstrated excellent agreement across repeat runs and consistently high accuracy in survival prediction, closely aligning with ground truth. However, for scenarios with limited computational resources, Llama-2.0-13b serves as a preferred choice with comparable performance. Dolly-v2-12b could be considered a secondary option, while Vicuna-v1.3-13b may require further investigation or technical fine-tuning to improve its suitability for clinical tasks like this study. The relatively lower performance of Dolly-v2-12b and Vicuna-v1.3-13b may stem from differences in training data quality, model architecture, or their ability to generalize across complex medical domains like bladder cancer prognosis. Fine-tuning these models with domain-specific datasets or employing transfer learning techniques could potentially improve their accuracy and consistency.

Multi-Modal predictive models

One critical implication of this study is the potential for integrating LLMs into multi-modal predictive models. Bladder cancer prognosis, similar to many other clinical assessment tasks—benefits from combining textual data (such like pathology reports) with imaging data (such like CT scans). The Clinical-Radiomics-Deep-Learning (CRD) model evaluated in this study integrates clinical variables extracted by LLMs with radiomic and deep-learning imaging features to predict patient outcomes with high accuracy.

While these survival prediction results demonstrate the downstream utility of LLM-extracted data, we emphasize that they should not be interpreted as direct evidence of extraction accuracy. High predictive performance in AUC may still occur even if some extraction errors are present, particularly in multi-modal settings where other modalities can compensate. Therefore, we consider the variable-level agreement analysis (accuracy and Fleiss’ Kappa) as a more appropriate and direct measure of extraction reliability.

Nevertheless, the consistent and automated clinical variable extraction enabled by LLMs strengthens the scalability and real-world applicability of such multi-modal models, ultimately enhancing their potential clinical impact.

LLM extraction error propagation

To evaluate how errors in LLM-extracted clinical variables affect downstream performance, we conducted ablation studies comparing models that used ground truth variables versus those using LLM-extracted inputs. As shown in Table 1, with GPT models achieving an average accuracy between 94% and 96% across the three variables (pT, pN, and LVI), we observed up to a 0.02 reduction in AUC when using LLM-extracted variables for survival prediction. In contrast, models with lower extraction accuracy—such as Vicuna-v1.3-13b, which achieved an average accuracy of 73%—led to AUC reductions of up to 0.1.

The impact of extraction errors was not uniform. Inaccuracies in high-risk clinical variables (for example, misclassifying pT3 as pT2) amplified the downstream effects of misclassification. These findings underscore that survival models relying on discrete clinical inputs are particularly sensitive to upstream NLP errors, even when such errors are relatively infrequent.

Variability in extraction quality introduces a non-negligible source of noise. To mitigate this issue, integrating confidence estimation into LLM outputs, applying ensemble strategies, or implementing conformal prediction methods may improve the reliability of variable extraction.

Future directions

While this study provides valuable insights into the variability of LLMs in clinical data extraction, several areas warrant further exploration.

First, for the bladder cancer survival prediction models presented in this work, incorporating more granular and diverse clinical variables, such as margin status, histologic subtype, or comorbidities, may enhance both the performance and clinical utility.

Second, additional research is needed to assess the generalizability of these findings across a broader range of medical domains. Although bladder cancer pathology reports offered a well-defined and clinically relevant test case, it remains uncertain whether similar performance could be expected in domains such as cardiology or neurology. Reports in these specialties often differ markedly in language style, terminology, and clinical focus. For instance, cardiology reports tend to emphasize structured numerical data (such like ejection fraction and blood pressure readings), procedural documentation (such like catheterizations and stent placements), and abbreviated terminology, which may require models to interpret quantitative trends and specialized shorthand. In contrast, neurology reports are typically more descriptive and narrative, containing detailed accounts of patient symptoms, neurological exams, and clinical reasoning, often expressed in less standardized language. These domain-specific linguistic and structural characteristics may pose additional challenges for LLMs, particularly in accurately identifying and extracting relevant clinical variables. As such, achieving similar performance in these contexts may necessitate domain-specific adaptation or customization. Future work should explore strategies like few-shot learning, RAG, or specialty-specific fine-tuning to enhance LLM robustness across varied clinical settings. RAG also offers a more transparent and controllable framework by grounding the model’s output in an explicit set of retrieved documents or knowledge sources. This could not only improve performance but also enhance trust and interpretability by linking outputs to specific and verifiable evidence.

Finally, healthcare institutions adopting LLMs must establish robust validation frameworks to ensure that model updates, whether online or offline, do not inadvertently compromise performance in critical applications. Regular quality assurance monitoring and calibration are essential to maintaining the reliability of these tools as they evolve.

Conclusion

This study highlights the degree of variability in LLM performance when applied to clinical information extraction. The ability of LLMs to automate the extraction of critical clinical variables from unstructured medical records has significant implications for improving predictive modeling and clinical decision-making in oncologic patients, as well as, potentially, in patients with other diseases. As can be seen in our study, LLMs can be used for expediting relevant data extraction from a patient’s medical records, which is then utilized for accurate prediction of a patient’s prognosis. However, the variability observed in certain models underscores the need for careful selection and continuous evaluation of LLMs in clinical applications.

A summary of the important findings and recommendations (“take home messages”) for the practice of utilizing LLMs in clinical information retrieval is presented in Table 3.

Future research should focus on strategies for improving the reliability of lower-performing models and validating the robustness of LLMs across the wide range of medical disciplines. As LLM technology advances, it holds immense potential for transforming healthcare by enabling more accurate, efficient, and scalable clinical data extraction and analysis.

Methods

Study design

We adhered to the TRIPOD-LLM (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis – Large Language Models) reporting guidelines where applicable, to promote transparency and reproducibility in clinical AI research.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Michigan. The experimental protocol received IRB approval, and the requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB due to the retrospective nature of this HIPAA-compliant cohort study.

This retrospective study analyzed the medical records and CT Urograms (CTU) of 163 bladder cancer patients (mean age 64 ± 9 years; Male: Female 131:32; data created time 2003–2020) from 781 bladder cancer patients who were treated at Michigan Medicine, ranging from 2001 to 2020. The 163 eligible patients met these criteria: (1) having undergone radical cystectomy and neoadjuvant chemotherapy; (2) having at least one set of pre- and post-chemotherapy CTU scans following neoadjuvant chemotherapy; and (3) having available five-year follow-up data post-cystectomy to determine survival status. The selected cohort included 79 patients who survived at least five years post-surgery and 84 who died within this period. Clinical records comprised detailed pathology reports, surgical notes, and follow-up summaries.

The study design is illustrated in Fig. 3. Each report was input into the LLMs 10 times independently to assess reproducibility. The extracted information was used as input for survival prediction models: a clinical nomogram based solely on clinical variables and a multi-modal model incorporating clinical and imaging features. Details of the study design are elaborated below.

Model selection

We selected six LLMs to evaluate their performance in clinical information extraction: Dolly-v2-12b23, Vicuna-v1.3-13b24, Llama-2.0-13b25, Llama-3.3-70b26, GPT-4 Turbo27, and GPT-4o28. These models were chosen for their diverse architectures, accessibility, parameter counts, and operational capabilities.

Dolly, developed by Databricks, is a fine-tuned version of EleutherAI’s GPT models and was specifically designed to democratize LLM capabilities. It focuses on aligning pre-trained models with human-like responses by leveraging open datasets. Dolly-v2-12b emphasizes simplicity and cost-effectiveness while retaining strong generative performance, making it a practical choice for organizations requiring locally deployable solutions.

Vicuna, a fine-tuned variant of LLaMA, is known for its conversational abilities and has been optimized using publicly available instruction-tuning datasets. We used Vicuna version 1.3 with 13 billion parameters (Vicuna-v1.3-13b). Vicuna-v1.3-13b’s architecture inherits the strengths of LLaMA while enhancing its alignment for dialogue-based tasks. Its lightweight nature and focus on efficient local deployment make it a strong choice for use cases demanding robust conversational abilities without relying on external application programming interfaces (APIs).

Llama (Large Language Model Meta AI) is a series of transformer-based models developed by Meta, which employ a standard transformer architecture but are optimized for fine-tuning and local deployment, making them suitable for research and practical applications requiring flexibility and privacy. We used two Llama models, Llama 2.0 with 13 billion parameters (Llama-2.0-13b) and Llama 3.3 with 70 billion parameters (Llama-3.3-70b).

GPT-4 Turbo and GPT-4o were accessed via OpenAI’s API. These advanced transformer-based models are known for their state-of-the-art capabilities in natural language understanding and generation.

Data preprocessing

All patient-identifying information was removed to ensure anonymity. Further, reports were truncated to ensure compatibility with the token limits of each LLM. Token limits were 2048 for Dolly-v2-12b and Vicuna-v1.3-13b, 4096 for Llama-2.0-13b, 130k for Llama-3.3-70b, 124k for GPT-4 Turbo, and 128k for GPT-4o. Reports were truncated to fewer than 2048 tokens to ensure fair comparison across all models. We referred to the truncated versions as “snippet reports” and the reports without truncation as “full reports.” The snippet reports were input to every model, and the full reports were input to GPT-4 Turbo and GPT-4o.

To truncate a report, a research assistant reviewed the full report and selected the portion of the report that contains the relevant information. For some cases, the three descriptors were found in different sections of a long report or in different reports. In these instances, we manually consolidated the parts containing relevant information from different sections or from multiple reports. This simulated a single report within the maximum length that can be analyzed by all six LLMs being compared. To minimize the effect of the manual manipulation, we strictly followed the rules: other than the necessary combination operation, no additional editing was done—such as changing sentence sequence, removing sentences, or modifying the format and layout—to preserve the original writing of the clinical reports.

Data extraction protocol

Each LLM extracted three key clinical variables: (1) pathologic stage (pT0, pTis, pTa, pT1, pT2, pT3, pT4), (2) lymph node status (N0, N1, N2, N3), and (3) LVI status (yes, no). These variables were selected because a nomogram17 demonstrated its ability to predict survival using them as inputs.

A standardized prompt (Fig. 4) was developed to reduce ambiguity and improve consistency. This prompt was iteratively refined using a sample of medical reports until the LLM outputs became relevant and consistently stable.

Experimental design

We designed a series of experiments to evaluate the reliability of LLMs in extracting clinical information from medical reports. Each “complete run” consisted of processing 163 medical reports, with each report being presented as an input to the model ten times. To ensure the independence of individual reports and their repeated runs, the LLM was restarted before processing each report and then again before each run. The baseline experiment processed snippet reports and full reports in their original sequence, while subsequent experiments processed the reports in differently randomized sequences. The details of these variations are outlined below.

Model effect

A “complete run” was conducted using each selected model to evaluate their performance in extracting clinical information.

Model evolution over time

To investigate the impact of updates to online models, specifically GPT-4o, assessments were performed at multiple time points. The first three experiments were conducted at 10-day intervals. After observing no significant changes, a longer interval of 30 days was implemented for the fourth experiment. The specific dates are provided in the results section (Table 1). For these experiments, we used GPT-4o, performing a “complete run” at each time point. By evaluating identical input cases across these time points, we quantified the influence of potential updates in GPT-4o on the performance in clinical information extraction.

Input length and order effects

Additional experiments examined the impact of input length and order on extraction performance. Both GPT-4 Turbo and GPT-4o were provided with snippet-length and full-length reports, with input cases presented in their original order for 10 runs, or in a randomized order, with each run featuring a different random order. This was done to assess whether the case order influenced the consistency of results. These experiments primarily used GPT-4 due to its extended token limit, which supports processing full-length reports.

LLM output processing

Although we instructed the LLMs to generate responses in a structured format, the outputs varied across models. The extracted variables are essential inputs for downstream models to perform survival prediction. To ensure consistency, we standardized the outputs into predefined categories: pT (N/A, invalid, pT0, pTis, pTa, pT1, pT2, pT3, pT4), pN (N/A, invalid, N0, N1, N2, N3), and LVI (N/A, invalid, Yes, No). Clearly identifiable responses were mapped directly to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM (Tumor, Node, Metastasis) staging system, such as pTis, pTa, pT0–pT4, N0–N3, and LVI (Yes/No). If the LLM indicated that relevant information was unavailable or absent in the input report, we classified the output as “N/A.” Conversely, if the response was contradictory or entirely irrelevant, it was categorized as “invalid.”

Survival prediction model

For all experiments, the extracted clinical information was input into two survival predictive models: a pre-trained nomogram17 and the CRD model7. The nomogram’s performance was evaluated on all 163 cases. For the CRD model, 99 cases were used for training and validation, while the remaining 64 cases served as the test set. For comparison, the nomogram was also run on the same set of 64 cases.

The nomogram requires five input variables: pN, pT, lymphovascular invasion (LVI) (yes or no), neoadjuvant chemotherapy (yes or no), and adjuvant radiotherapy (yes or no). The last two variables remained constant for all patients in our study— all 163 patients underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and none received adjuvant radiotherapy. The LLM-extracted information for pT, pN, and LVI, along with the known values for neoadjuvant chemotherapy and adjuvant radiotherapy, were input into the survival models for performance evaluation.

Statistical analysis

The variability of LLM outputs was assessed by measuring agreement across ten runs for each of the 163 cases within a “complete run” for each experimental scenario. Fleiss’ Kappa was used to quantify agreement, with values interpreted using a standard framework (Table 4).

In addition to measuring agreement, we compared LLM-extracted outputs to manually extracted clinical information (defined as ground truth in this study) to calculate extraction accuracy. Subsequently, the extracted information was used as input to the nomogram and the CRD model, and the survival prediction performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Agreement of the predicted survival probability across 10 runs for each of the 163 cases was also measured by Krippendorff’s Alpha29.
