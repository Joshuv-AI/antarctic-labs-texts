An audio encryption method based on quaternary logic operations
Authors: Hongjian Wang, Yuxin Dai, Kuiyan Li, Xin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Audio data has strong correlations between adjacent samples, which is challenging for encryption methods. Current audio encryption methods mostly rely on complex transformations and algorithms to ensure their security, but overly complex algorithms will inevitably affect efficiency. We propose a novel audio encryption method by combining quaternary logic operations with MixColumns operations; this method provides higher security than existing audio encryption methods while having faster encryption and decryption speeds. The fast speed is due to the fact that encryption and decryption processes are mainly symbol replacements, which can be implemented very fast by quaternary logic operations. The high security comes from three aspects: (1) Quaternary logic operations can provide a huge space for encryption rules, because there are at most (4!)4 types of quaternary logic operations available for encrypting every 2 bits of data; (2) MixColumns operations can reduce the correlation between adjacent samples in audio data, enhancing the diffusion effect while improving key sensitivity; (3) We also introduce dual encryption to resist statistical attacks. To prove our method’s security, we analyze key sensitivity and calculate security indicators including key space, number of sample change rate, unified average changing intensity, and peak signal-to-noise ratio, where we compare our method with other existing audio encryption methods. Results show that the security indicators of our method are superior to most of the compared methods. Our method is also faster than existing audio encryption algorithms based on chaos theory, AES encryption, and lightweight password Ascon. When implemented in C++, the encryption speed of our method can reach an average of about 0.000257 s/KB. When implemented on FPGA, the path delay is measured as 2.750 ns reaching a maximum frequency of 363.6 MHz, which is faster than AES, Ascon and other FPGA-based audio encryption methods. Meanwhile, our method consumes fewer FPGA resources than AES and Ascon.

Introduction

With the continuous development of the digital world, audio data can contain rich information and has been closely related to people’s privacy, so the security of audio data in transmission and storage becomes more and more important. Due to the large amount of data in audio files and the strong correlation between adjacent audio samples, the traditional encryption algorithms suffer from shortcomings such as ineffectiveness, insecurity, and low speed. For example, when the AES1 block cipher algorithm is used alone for audio encryption, it cannot resist differential attacks and does not have a good avalanche effect. Therefore, some methods combine AES-128 encryption algorithm with CBC encryption mode and PKCS7 padding to make them more suitable for audio encryption, such as Apple’s DRM2. However, due to the complexity of such methods, the encryption speed will be affected when processing a large amount of audio samples. Therefore, it is necessary to study secure and efficient new encryption methods specifically designed for audio data.

In addition to traditional encryption algorithms, researchers have proposed many algorithms specifically designed for audio encryption. Among them, some widely used encryption algorithms are based on the cosine transform and chaos theory to protect audio data. For example, Lima et al.3 proposed audio encryption based on the cosine function transform (CNT), which enhances encryption diffusion by partially overlapping adjacent sample blocks and achieves encryption through key-controlled recursion times. Some examples of chaos-based audio encryption have been well studied: Albahrani4 combined block encryption with chaotic mapping, where they applied chaotic mapping in the permutation stage to replace byte blocks; Wang et al.5 combined DNA encoding with chaotic systems, where they encrypted SHA-256-based keys using DNA encoding/decoding rules and DNA key matrix; Rahul et al.6 used biometric images, SHA-256, and chaotic maps to deal with the strong correlation between adjacent audio samples, demonstrating superior performance in reducing this correlation; Thuraka et al.7 designed a dual-layer hybrid encryption scheme (RSA + AES) to ensure both fast data processing and enhanced resistance against cryptographic attacks compared to single encryption methods, while maintaining audio fidelity by using deep learning techniques; Khan et al.8 proposed a novel and secure framework that collaborates BDLT with quantum resilient schemes to ensure long-term data integrity and privacy preservation. Although the above audio encryption algorithms can achieve good security through complex transformations and operations, overly complex encryption and decryption operations will inevitably affect the speed and efficiency of the algorithm when processing large amounts of audio data.

In 2021, Wang et al.9,10 proposed a fast and secure encryption method based on multiple-valued logic operations (MVLOs). They used a reconfigurable quaternary logic operator (RQLO) circuit11,12 to build encryptor/decryptor and key derivation operator, and introduced the concept of generalized key to achieve the one-time pad (OTP) encryption. In 2024, Zhou et al.13 applied Wang’s method to video streaming data encryption and implemented a real-time encryption/decryption device on FPGA. Their methods are very fast because the encryption and decryption process can be done by the RQLO circuit in one clock cycle of the hardware; their methods are very safe because the RQLO can provide massive encryption rules. Following Wang’s and Zhou’s work, in this paper, we study an audio encryption method based on quaternary logic operations (QLOs). The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

(1) We propose a new audio encryption method by combining QLOs with MixColumns operations, which can deal with the strong correlation between adjacent samples in audio data. Using permutations of (4!)4 types of QLOs for encryption/decryption makes our method secure and fast. Adding the MixColumns step enables our method to have stronger inter-block diffusion effect.

(2) We introduce dual encryption to our method for encrypting audio files, so that the impact of changing one bit of key spreads across all bits of ciphertext. This further improves key sensitivity and enhances anti-attack capability. As a result, an attacker cannot obtain any valid audio information with a partially cracked key.

(3) We analyze key sensitivity and calculate security indicators, including key space, number of sample change rate (NSCR), unified average changing intensity (UACI), and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR). We compare our method with other existing audio encryption methods in terms of security indicators and speed, showing that our method is safe and fast.

Method

Mathematical analysis of QLOs

This section presents the definitions of QLOs, along with the formal definitions and proofs concerning their bijectivity and reversibility13.

Definition 1

A two-input QLO is a bivariate function that satisfies the following condition: Let set Z = {0, 1, 2, 3}. If ∀a ∈ Z and ∀b ∈ Z, f(a, b) = c ⇒ ∀c ∈ Z, then the bivariate function f is called a two-input QLO.

Definition 2

For a two-input QLO f satisfying the conditions in Definition 1, if there exists a bivariate function f−1 such that for ∀a ∈ Z and ∀b ∈ Z, f(a, b) = c ⇔ f−1(c, b) = a, then the bivariate function f−1 is defined as the inverse operation of the QLO f.

Theorem 1

The inverse operation of a QLO is also a two-input QLO.

Theorem 2

Let f(a, b) = c be a QLO, with all inputs a form set A and all outputs c form set C. A necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of an inverse operation f−1 is that when b is fixed, the mapping from set A to set C defined by f is a bijection.

Proof

Given f(a, b) = c, to ensure that the result of the inverse operation f−1(c, b) is unique, the mapping from set A to set C via f must be a one-to-one correspondence between a and c, i.e., the function f must be injective. According to Definition 1, A = C = {0, 1, 2, 3}, so f is bijection.

Theorem 3

The number of QLOs that have an inverse operation is (4!)4.

Proof

According to Theorem 2, the truth table of a QLO with an inverse operation has the following characteristic: in each row, the values of c are non-repeating. To ensure non-repetition of c in a single row, there are P(4, 4) = 4! ways to fill the c-values in that row. Since the truth table of a QLO has 4 rows, the total number of such truth tables where c is non-repeating in every row is (4!)4.

Therefore, the encryption and decryption processes are done via symbol substitution. The four symbols (values) in QLOs can be represented by 2 bits of information, i.e., {00, 01, 10, 11}, so the basic unit of p, k, or c is 2-bit data.

QLO-based block encryptor and key derivation operator

Our method can be viewed as a block cipher, where the block size is set to 128-bit for audio data. Like the work of Wang et al.9,10, our method is designed to be used in a OTP fashion9,10. Before each use, we randomly select 64 different encryption rules of f to form a 64-quit block encryptor F. (Here, 1 quit means 1 quaternary unit, which operates on 2 bits.) We use F to encrypt 128-bit of data as a block each time, where each plaintext block P and ciphertext block C are all 128-bit. The key K for each block is also 128-bit, and we call it digital key in the rest of this paper. The block decryptor F− 1 is also 64-quit, where the operational rule of each quit is the inverse of the corresponding quit of F. The same K is needed for a pair of F and F− 1, so it is a symmetric encryption method.

For audio data containing multiple blocks, it is unsafe to use the same digital key K for all blocks. So, we randomly select another 64 different QLO rules to form a 64-quit key derivation operator (KDO), denoted as F’. The KDO generates a new 128-bit digital key for the next block from the current block. Any of the 416 QLO rules can be used for KDO.

MixColumns operation for digital keys

For the operations of either block encryptor or KDO, each quit is independent of each other, meaning there is no diffusion inside a block. Therefore, we add MixColumns operations to enhance the diffusion properties. The MixColumns operations can be performed on any of the following: P, C, or K. We choose to perform them on K for two reasons: (1) it enhances key sensitivity and (2) enables parallel processing in the mode of operation of our method.

Mode of operation with dual encryption

To use our method, audio data needs to be divided into 128-bit blocks. For the encryption and key derivation operation of the first block, a 128-bit initial digital key is needed, which is called seed key. The seed key goes through the MixColumns operations, before being inputted to the KDO. The other input of the KDO is the current ciphertext block. The KDO uses the current block of ciphertext, rather than plaintext, to derive digital keys for the next block. This is because compared with the plaintext audio data, the ciphertext data is more confused, with lower correlation; it should be safer to derive digital keys from ciphertext. To verify this idea, we have performed experimental analysis showing that the encryption is much more unlikely to be cracked when digital keys are derived from ciphertext than from plaintext.

Due to the sequential block encryption process, changes in the current plaintext block will only affect subsequent ciphertext blocks, weakening the ability to resist differential attacks. To solve this problem, we introduce dual encryption to our audio encryption method. The plaintext goes through a first round of encryption in the forward direction, that is from the first block to the last block; then the result of the first round, called temporary ciphertext, goes through a second round of encryption in the backward direction, that is from the last block to the first block, to generate the final ciphertext. As a result, changes in a block can affect both the previous and subsequent blocks. This dual encryption is specifically designed for audio files, where all the data to be encrypted are known in advance, from the first block to the last block. For streaming audio data where not all blocks are known in advance, we can still apply dual encryption by viewing a certain number of currently available blocks as an audio file.

The overall workflow of encryption mode is shown in Fig. 1, and the corresponding pseudocode is given in Algorithm 1. Suppose that the plaintext data includes n blocks, namely P1, P2, …, Pn. The first block P1, together with the seed key K1, is inputted to the encryptor, which outputs the first block of temporary ciphertext C’1 for the dual encryption operation. After that, C’1, together with the K1 after the MixColumns operation, is inputted to the KDO, which outputs the derived digital key K’2 for the next block. Similarly, P2 and K’2 generate C’2 via the encryptor; C’2 and the K’2 after the MixColumns operation generate K’3. This working process in a forward direction continues until the last block of temporary ciphertext C’n is obtained. Then the second round of encryption in a backward direction begins. The last block of temporary ciphertext C’n, together with the seed key K1, is inputted to the encryptor, which outputs the last block of final ciphertext Cn. After that, Cn, together with the K1 after the MixColumns operation, is inputted to the KDO, which outputs the derived digital key K2 for the next block. Similarly, C’n-1 and K2 generate Cn-1 via the encryptor; C’n-1 and the K2 after the MixColumns operation generate K3. The backward encryption is complete when the first block of final ciphertext C1 is obtained.

The operations of different blocks are sequential, firstly from the first block to the last block, and then in reverse. For the operations of each single block, the encryption operation and the MixColumns operation can be done in parallel, after which the key derivation operation can be performed. For the operations of each quit of the block, they are done in parallel thanks to the 64-quit encryptor or KDO.

The working process of decryption mode is opposite to the encryption mode. It begins with the last block of the ciphertext, going backward until all the temporary ciphertext blocks are obtained in a reverse order. Then, it goes from the first block of the temporary ciphertext to the last block, recovering all the plaintext blocks in normal order.

Software implementation and experimental results

We firstly implement our method as an algorithm using the C + + language, where we simulate the QLOs by lookup tables. Note that the original quaternary logic-based encryption technology10,13 was proposed based on the RQLO universal circuit11,12; only when implemented with this dedicated hardware can its full advantages be realized. However, we initially use a C + + software implementation of our method, mainly to facilitate the analysis of various security metrics during the encryption and decryption of audio files. This approach allows for more convenient testing and evaluation of resistance against attacks, while also providing a more cost-effective and accessible alternative to the hardware implementation using the RQLO universal circuit. We present the FPGA implementation of our method based on the RQLO universal circuit in next section.

Safety analysis

Key space analysis

A good audio encryption scheme must have a large key space to resist brute-force attacks well11. In our method, the key space depends on all the variables that can affect the resulting ciphertext; these variables are encryptor/decryptor pair, KDO, and seed key, which form the so-called generalized key11,12. Therefore, the key space is equal to the product of the number of encryptor/decryptor pairs, the number of KDOs, and the number of seed keys, that is, ((4!)4)64 × (416)64 × 2128>2 × 101008. This key space is much larger than the largest key space of 2256 that AES can provide, or other chaos-based encryption methods such as the one proposed by Norouzi et al.18 with a key space of 2100. Such a huge key space creates the conditions for implementing our method in an OTP fashion, where each encryption can use a unique key.

Histogram analysis

A histogram can reflect the statistical distribution characteristics of the original audio signal before and after encryption17. The analysis of the histogram can measure the anti-statistical attack performance of the encryption method; the more uniform the histogram distribution of the encrypted audio, the better the performance of the encryption method against statistical analysis attacks. The histogram results of the six test audio examples are shown in Fig. 3, where the histograms of the original audio examples are in the first column, and the encrypted audio examples in the second column. In the histograms of the original audio examples, the audio amplitude distribution is uneven; the largest number of samples are with an amplitude of 0. In the histograms of the encrypted audio examples, the number of samples corresponding to each audio amplitude is basically the same and evenly distributed, completely hiding the information of the original audio. It is difficult for attackers to obtain any information of the original audio by analyzing the histogram of the encrypted audio, which implies that our encryption method has good anti-statistical attack performance.

Key sensitivity test

A secure encryption algorithm should be highly sensitive to the key. In our method, the MixColumns operation for digital keys before the KDO enhances the diffusion effect and improves the key sensitivity. We test the key sensitivity by changing 1 bit of the seed key. The test result of Audio01 is shown in Figs. 4 and 5, where the waveform and histogram of the incorrectly decrypted audio (third column) with slightly changed seed key are very similar to those of the ciphertext audio (second column). Hence, even 1 bit error from the seed key leads to completely failed decryption, where no information about the original audio can be observed. The other 5 examples (Audio02- Audio06 in Table 1) have similar results, further proving the key sensitivity of our method.

To more strongly demonstrate the key sensitivity of our method, we conduct an additional experiment. First, the audio file Audio01 is encrypted. Then, after modifying a single bit in the seed key, Audio01 is encrypted again. A correlation analysis is performed on the two resulting encrypted audio files. Using the correlation coefficient formula provided in Sect. “Correlation coefficient analysis”, the calculated correlation coefficient between the two encrypted files is 0.00198. Furthermore, as shown in the scatter plot in Fig. 6, the data points are uniformly distributed. This result further confirms that our method exhibits very strong key sensitivity.

Resistance against differential attacks

Differential attack is an attack method for symmetric block encryption algorithms. It extracts the key by analyzing the influence of specific plaintext differences on corresponding ciphertext differences. We calculate three indicators of resistance against differential attacks, namely NSCR, number of bit change rate (NBCR), and UACI18. The ideal values of NSCR and UACI are 100% and 33.333% respectively19, indicating that a small change of the plaintext will have a great impact on the ciphertext. The NBCR is related to the avalanche effect of cryptographic algorithms, and its ideal value is 50%, because for two random and independent audio files C and C’, both C(i) and C’(i) have 50% probability of being 0 or 1. The closer the experimental values of NSCR, NBCR, and UACI are to the ideal values, the better the confusion and diffusion effect of the encryption algorithm is, and hence the stronger its ability to resist differential attacks is.

Hardware implementation and performance analysis

Encryptor/Decryptor implementation based on RQLO

We employ the RQLO hardware architecture invented by Jin et al.11 to implement the encryptor/decryptor using QLOs. The structure of the RQLO is shown in Fig. 10. A 1-quit reconfigurable quaternary logic operator comprises four reconfiguration registers: RG0, RG1, RG2, and RG3. Writing reconfiguration instructions into these registers determines the QLO rule for the 1-quit operator. By rewriting different reconfiguration instructions, different QLO rules can be reconfigured, and the operator’s universal circuit will be correspondingly configured into different specific quaternary logic operators. Consequently, with different reconfiguration instructions, the 1-quit RQLO can emulate any of the 416 possible quaternary logic operators. Specifically, it can realize (4!)4 = 331,776 types of quaternary logic operators suitable for encryptors/decryptors.

Fig. 101-quit RQLO universal circuit.

1-quit RQLO universal circuit.

By further integrating 64 1-quit RQLOs, a 64-quit RQLO is constructed to function as a block encryptor/decryptor. This 64-quit RQLO can be utilized through the following two steps. First, the required configuration instructions are loaded into each RQLO, thereby defining the encryption/decryption rule for the block encryptor/decryptor, with distinct operational rules assigned to each quit. Subsequently, two 128-bit data sets (plaintext/ciphertext and a seed key) are fed into the 64-quit quaternary logic operator, with every 2 bits treated as a single quaternary data. The 64 1-quit quaternary logic operators then process the data in parallel according to their respective configuration instructions to produce a 128-bit result (ciphertext/plaintext). Since each 1-quit RQLO operates independently and in parallel, the symbol replacement encryption or decryption of a data block can be completed within just one clock cycle, laying the foundation for high-speed data processing.

Generalized key synchronization mechanism

Since the operational rules of the encryptor/decryptor and KDO depend on the reconfiguration instructions of their respective 64-quit RQLO universal circuits, a generalized key is defined by a set of data comprising the reconfiguration instruction of the encryptor, the reconfiguration instruction of the paired decryptor, the reconfiguration instruction of the KDO, and the seed key. This set of data is referred to as the generalized key source data (GKSD). Since our method has a larger block size (128-bit) than existing quaternary logic-based encryption methods10,13,32, it can provide a greater number of generalized keys. Therefore, we adopt the existing process of pre-storing GKSD to ensure that both encryption and decryption parties pre-store a set of sufficient randomly selected, and matching generalized keys.

The generalized key synchronization between the encryption and decryption parties is accomplished through a reservation code technology9,10. This technology ensures that by selecting the matching pre-stored GKSD according to the reservation code, both sides can synchronously utilize the same seed key and the corresponding two sets of 64 QLO rules for the encryptor/decryptor and the KDO. Since the reservation code merely serves as an index for accessing the GKSD, identical reservation codes will retrieve different generalized keys from different GKSD sets, thus eliminating the need to encrypt the reservation code itself.

Development of FPGA-based hardware implementation

Our FPGA-based implementation is carried out on a ZYNQ MINI7010 development board using Vivado 2017.4 under the Windows operating system. The ZYNQ MINI7010 is a System-on-Chip (SoC) that integrates an FPGA and an ARM microprocessor. The board can be broadly divided into two parts: the Processing System (PS) and the Programmable Logic (PL). Leveraging this architecture, we implement the encryption and decryption modules on the FPGA at the PL part while using the PS part for inputting and outputting data.

The overall hardware implementation design of our method is shown in Fig. 11. Among them, EFrau_0 and DFrau_0 are the encryption module and decryption module, respectively, which are custom IP cores implemented by Verilog. Within the IP core, the corresponding functions of the encryptor/decryptor, the KDO, and MixColumns are implemented. Corresponding to the 128-bit audio block data, the encryptor, decryptor, and KDO are 64-quit quaternary logic operators. The detailed implementation principles of the encryptor/decryptor are described in Sect. “Encryptor/Decryptor implementation based on RQLO”. A partial screenshot of the encryptor’s RTL schematic is shown in Fig. 12. Based on the circuit structure of the 1-quit RQLO in Fig. 10, the encryptor is composed of 64 parallel 1-quit RQLO modules, with each quit comprising 4 column operator modules. The decryptor and the KDO share the same structure as the encryptor. The encryptor/decryptor, the KDO and the MixColumns module are connected via a key selector to form the encryption/decryption module. Taking the encryption module as an example, its RTL schematic is shown in Fig. 13. The MixColumns module receives the seed key or the current derived key, applies column mixing, and feeds the result into the KDO. Simultaneously, the key selector feeds the next derived key to the encryptor for encrypting the next block of plaintext data.

Since the width of each register in the IP core is fixed at 32-bit, EFrau_0 and DFrau_0 use 140 registers each for data storage. Specifically, 4 registers are used to store the 128-bit plaintext data, another 4 registers are used to store the 128-bit ciphertext data, and the remaining 132 registers are used to store the seed key and the reconfiguration instruction required by the encryptor/decryptor and the KDO.

During our experiment, the block audio data read from the PS side is transmitted to the encryption/decryption module on the PL side via the AXI bus for encryption/decryption operations. After the operations are performed, the encrypted/decrypted audio block data is returned to the PS side.

We use the FLASH on the development board to pre-store the GKSD. Since decryption and encryption operations require matching generalized keys, we employ the pre-stored GKSD and the reservation code for selecting and synchronizing generalized keys (see Sect. “Generalized key synchronization mechanism” for details). Before performing encryption and decryption, the two parties agree on the same reservation code through conventional communication methods to ensure that the encryption module and decryption module can read the matching pre-stored GKSD. The encryption module and decryption module use the same 128-bit seed key; the matching reconfiguration instructions are given to the encryptor and decryptor; the same reconfiguration instructions are given to the KDO. This ensures key synchronization and guarantees the correctness of encryption and decryption.

Speed and resources analysis

There exist various audio encryption methods that can be implemented using FPGAs. For example, Sayed et al.28 proposed a speech encryption system based on a generalized modified chaotic transition map and bit permutation, where their FPGA implementation can provide a throughput of 1.526 Gbit/s. Discussion

Parameter and QLO rules selection

The original quaternary logic-based encryption technology10,13 has a block size of 64-bit. Since our method integrates QLOs with the AES-128 MixColumns transformation, a block size of 128-bit is selected. This not only increases the generalized key space of the original method, thereby improving its resistance to brute-force attacks, but also increases the parallelism of QLOs, thereby improving encryption efficiency. Note that our method can be configured with other block sizes depending on users’ different needs regarding security, efficiency, and resource consumption. Different block sizes do not affect the design principle of the encryptor, decryptor, or KDO; only the quit width of the quaternary logic operator differs, and a corresponding column mixing method needs to be redesigned. Regarding the number of MixColumns rounds, we conducted relevant experiments which confirmed that two or more rounds provide sufficient security. To achieve higher encryption speed, the minimum effective number of MixColumns rounds (two rounds) is adopted.

The selection of QLO rules must satisfy the encryption and decryption rules described in Sect. “Method”, namely, each row of the truth table cannot contain duplicate elements. Furthermore, it is recommended that users avoid selecting the QLO rules that have excessive duplicate elements in each column of their truth tables. When choosing the QLO rules for KDO, it is also recommended to choose complex truth tables. In addition, the QLO rules for each quit of a 64-quit encryptor/decryptor or KDO should be different. Under the above premise, the specific choice of QLO rules has minimal impact on security and no effect on speed. Since the number of generalized keys provided by our method far exceeds the possible number of plaintexts/ciphertexts, there will inevitably be cases where two different generalized keys encrypt a block of plaintext into the same ciphertext; we call them equivalent generalized keys. We also recommend that users avoid selecting equivalent generalized keys when using our method in an OTP fashion.

Encryption effect of non-audio data

Although our encryption method is specifically designed to address the high correlation between adjacent samples in audio data, it is fundamentally a block cipher algorithm that should also be suitable for other types of data with similar correlation features, such as images. To verify this, we conducted preliminary experiments on image encryption. We tested the encryption effectiveness of three images (selected from the standard USC-SIPI image database33), as shown in Fig. 14, in terms of correlation coefficient and information entropy.

Conclusion

We proposed an audio encryption method based on QLOs. Since QLO-based encryption methods can be combined with various existing diffusion schemes, we combine the AES-128 MixColumns operation and dual encryption with the QLO-based encryption method, to enhance the diffusion effect and reduce the correlation between adjacent audio samples, making our method more secure for audio data encryption. Experimental results show that our method outperforms existing audio encryption methods and general encryption methods in terms of security and efficiency. In addition, we have implemented our method on an FPGA platform based on the RQLO universal circuit. It is compared with the FPGA implementations of the standard AES algorithm and the lightweight Ascon algorithm. This comparative analysis further demonstrates the advantages of our approach in terms of speed and resource consumption.
