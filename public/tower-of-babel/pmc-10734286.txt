Pilot Demonstration of a New Tip for Effective Gas Flaring in the Permian Basin: Part 1
Authors: Ahmed Kamel, Ahmed Alzahabi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
In the oil and gas industry, gas flaring through flare stacks plays a critical role in safely and efficiently releasing and burning gases and other materials from pressure-relief and vapor-depressurizing systems. Gas flaring is a contentious and formidable environmental challenge, both regionally and globally. Its adverse impacts encompass pollution, contribution to global warming, and substantial economic losses. Shockingly, gas flaring squanders approximately 5% of the global gas supply, resulting in the annual production of over 350 million tons of CO2 gas. While alternatives to gas flaring exist, they are either undergoing investigation or currently lacking economic viability. Consequently, the development of a more efficient gas flaring system is imperative. In addition, the reliable and efficient operation of flares is paramount, as they are expected to function seamlessly over extended periods under various service conditions. In this paper, a novel gas flaring tip to address the limitations of existing flaring systems is introduced. This innovative tip features a distinctive configuration, incorporating a bullet-shaped device positioned atop the flare. This bullet device boasts four strategically placed apertures equipped with internal deflectors, facilitating the convergence of gases into a single vortex outlet at its apex. For operational flexibility, the bullet is affixed to the flare tip using a system of three springs, enabling it to elevate at a pressure of 2.0 psig and achieve full extension at 8 psig. This dynamic design harnesses the Coanda effect, promoting efficient oxygen utilization. The comprehensive evaluation of this tip spanned a wide range of gas capacities, 4.0 and 10.0 MMSCFD. In this analysis, parameters such as the fraction of heat radiated, transmissivity, atmospheric humidity level, solar radiation adjustment, ambient temperature, and horizontal wind velocity were factored. The evaluation included both theoretical analysis and experimental investigation. The theoretical analysis employed a simplified open pipe flare tip approach to predict thermal radiation levels around the tip using the Brzustowski and Sommer model. The experimental setup included equipment such as thermocouple thermometers, heat flux sensors, digital sound level meters, mass flowmeters, and portable flue gas analyzers. Throughout testing, thermal radiation levels, isopleths, noise levels, and flue gas composition were measured. The collected data were subsequently compared to predictions generated using the Brzustowski and Sommer model for identical gas flow rates, flare heights, and flare diameters. Due to technical challenges and safety concerns, flue gas composition was not available. The authors are exploring different alternatives to overcome these obstacles and data should be available in the near future. The resulting data of heat radiation and noise levels unequivocally demonstrate the superiority of the new tip when compared to conventional gas flaring systems. With its cost-effectiveness, smokeless operation, heightened efficiency, and cleaner flame characteristics, the authors, due to the groundbreaking design of this tip, strongly advocate its adoption as a means to mitigate the environmental impact of gas flaring.

Introduction

In the realm of gas management, two prevalent practices, flaring and venting, hold pivotal significance for environmental preservation and energy optimization. Flaring involves the controlled combustion of gas, where it is meticulously mixed with oxygen to produce byproducts such as carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide, both known for their adverse effects. The combustion process within flares relies on a diffusion flame, which, if not supplied with sufficient oxygen or if carbon particles, cools below their ignition temperature and may produce smoke. Beyond these ecological concerns, gas flaring perpetuates an unjustifiable waste of precious energy resources.

The inefficiencies of gas flaring are 2-fold: environmental and energy-related. Routine flaring arises due to factors such as insufficient gas volumes, low-pressure scenarios, equipment scarcity, or economic rationales. Additionally, operational anomalies like equipment failures, temporary plant shutdowns, or during startup procedures contribute to further losses. Such inefficiencies underscore the urgent need for enhanced flaring practices that minimize the environmental impact while effectively utilizing available energy resources.

In contrast, venting involves the direct release of gas into the atmosphere without undergoing combustion. This practice, although expedient, bears a more pronounced environmental toll, exerting a significantly greater negative influence. Gas venting manifests across diverse oil production phases, encompassing maintenance, completion, and unloading stages. This pervasive phenomenon stems from triggers such as pressure releases or leakage occurrences.

However, at the heart of addressing flaring lies the imperative to enhance flaring efficiency. By meticulously refining combustion processes, optimizing oxygen/gas ratios, and deploying advanced technologies, there is a potential to curtail the detrimental effects of these practices. This, in turn, can lead to a 2-fold triumph: mitigated environmental impact and optimized energy extraction.

As the world accelerates its transition toward sustainable energy practices, focusing on flaring efficiency emerges as a non-negotiable priority. The prospect of minimizing waste and preserving both our environment and our energy resources is within reach. By embracing innovative methodologies and fostering cross-industry collaboration, we can chart a course toward a greener, more efficient future in gas management.

As climate change concerns intensify, our efforts to reduce carbon emissions must also increase as well. It is anticipated that the methane flares will contribute to the warming of the Earth in a manner similar to carbon dioxide. This is because the infrared radiation, which would otherwise escape into space, is trapped, and absorbed by the methane molecules, causing the planet to experience further warming.1 Oil and gas are considered one of the largest emitters in the world and more efforts are needed to offset greenhouse gas (GHG).2−5 Due to the high population density in a certain area, it is anticipated that public disapproval of smoking flares will escalate.6 Therefore, there is a critical need to decrease the number of smoking flares in use or improve the burning efficiency. The pathway to sustainable gas management begins with a resolute commitment to flaring efficiency. By transforming the paradigm of gas flaring from wastefulness to resourcefulness, we not only alleviate environmental burden but also harness the latent potential of energy resources.

Efforts by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) are advancing technologies in methane detection and quantifications, and over the course of the last five years, methane emissions have declined. For example, in the Permian Basin, methane emissions have been reduced by almost 50%, with a decline of 44.8%, according to an analysis done by Texans for Natural Gas.7 Methane and carbon dioxide (CO2) are both greenhouse gases that contribute significantly to climate change. While CO2 is the world’s primary human-caused greenhouse gas, methane is also a potent greenhouse gas and one of the most abundant anthropogenic gases after CO2. Emissions of these gases come from various sources, including electricity generation, transportation, manufacturing, and industrial sectors. The sheer volume of emissions underscores the need for effective carbon management technologies to address the problem of climate change. Between 1990 and 2021, global energy-related CO2 emissions rose by 60% according to International Energy Association (IEA).8

With the boom in shale oil and gas production in the US, excess capacity for associated gases and flaring was created. This issue is considered by environmentalists, media, regulators, and investors as a public concern. This is due to the increased intensity of GHG in the atmosphere and the expected results of climate change. However, oil producers have committed to minimizing flared gas. For example, in the Permian Basin, operators such as Occidental Petroleum would commit to zero routine flaring by 2030. In their earning calls for 2021, executives of Pioneer Natural Resources showed that the company’s data revealed that they flared only 2% of their associated gas, in contrast to the basin’s average of 5%.9 Routine flaring in the US has created an opportunity for capturing the associated gas, with operators such as Oxy committed to zero routine flaring by 2030.9

Environmental Impacts of Gas Flaring

The buildup of carbon dioxide (CO2) and other harmful air pollutants in the atmosphere is a leading cause of global warming (GW) and has adverse effects on climate change and human health. These pollutants, along with others, trap sunlight and solar radiation that are reflected off the earth’s surface, preventing them from escaping into space. This trapped sunlight and radiation cause the plant to get warmer. The pollutants are called GHGs. They include carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, water vapor, and artificial fluorinated gases. The persistence of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere for centuries has contributed significantly to the amplification of climate change and long-term air pollution.

Unfortunately, the process of flaring further worsens this issue further. Flaring also introduces toxic pollutants, including sulfur dioxide, into the atmosphere, leading to environmental problems such as acid rain, smog, and other harmful environmental conditions.

Additionally, flaring contributes to the generation of additional greenhouse gases that exacerbate global climate change, making it imperative to seek alternative solutions.

Another environmental impact of gas flaring is the release of toxic chemicals, including benzene, toluene, and formaldehyde. These toxic chemicals have health impacts on the people who live near gas flaring sites, including increased risks of cancer and respiratory problems.

Gas flaring can also have negative impacts on wildlife and ecosystems. It may harm plants and animals as well as the overall health of ecosystems of birds and wildlife due to the heat and brightness of the flames resulting from flaring.

Alternatives to Gas Flaring

As indicated previously, flaring can be categorized into three types: routine flaring, safety flaring, and nonroutine flaring.11 As a result, flaring emits volatile compounds (carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, nitrogen oxide, and methane). There are other alternatives to gas flaring, in which new technologies can be applied to use flared gas that would otherwise be burned off. These technologies can utilize natural gas at the source to help reduce flaring intensity in the atmosphere. For example, most of the hydrogen produced today is obtained from methane, which is sourced through a production process known as steam-methane reforming. Another example of the utilization of natural gas is the gas-to-liquid (GTL) process, which involves converting natural gas into longer-chain hydrocarbons such as diesel and gasoline. GTL technologies include steam reforming, autothermal reforming, Glid-arc plasma, and catalytic partial oxidation reforming.

Cryptocurrencies such as the Bitcoin network use a significant amount of electricity, which is often powered by natural gas, instead of flaring it. This method is being adopted by multiple oil and gas operators, who are using natural gas to generate electricity for Bitcoin mining. Additionally, natural gas can be used for power generation through different technologies. Examples of such products include liquefied natural gas (LNG) and compressed natural gas (CNG).

Optimizing petrochemical products through the manufacturing process in nearby facilities is another effective way to avoid flaring, especially for methanol, ethylene, and ammonia. Furthermore, associated gas can be used as a feed for nearby power plants. Also, there is a growing trend toward constructing processing plants that can capture gas during flow back operations. This captured gas can be utilized to reduce diesel consumption, generate electricity, or in any other way that can help to reduce emissions.12−15 By utilizing these methods, it is possible to minimize the amount of natural gas that is flared and maximize its potential for generating economic and environmental benefits. Gas Flaring Concepts

Natural gas consists mainly of methane, with other components such as ethane, propane, and butane. In addition to these hydrocarbon gases, natural gas may contain gases other than hydrocarbons, including nitrogen, carbon dioxide, and hydrogen sulfide. In gas flaring, gas flows to a remote and elevated flare, where it is burned in open air using a burner tip. A flare tip produces a fire during a controlled burring process, where natural gas is mixed with oxygen and burned. As a result, carbon dioxide and water are produced. However, if the combustion process is incomplete, then other harmful gases may be produced.

Gas flaring has a significant environmental impact. This includes noise, heat radiation, and the emission of pollutants. In the following sections, a detailed discussion about these impacts in addition to the Coanda effect and efficiency is presented.

Coanda Effect

Coanda effect, well-known as the wall attachment effect, has been applied in designing flare tips to increase combustion efficiency and to lower levels of heat radiation. This effect shows the tendency of moving gas to flow near the surface. Due to friction and drag, the gas flow is slowed and gas stays attached to the surface.23 Flares using the Coanda effect can add and mix more air, up to 20 times more air, with the flowing gas where flame initiation takes place by an external pilot. Flares using the Coanda effect generally produce short, stable, less heat, and smokeless flames that are not easily deflected. In addition, they can burn liquids, up to 25%, that can be carried by the gas stream.24

Heat Radiation

Since a portion of heat released by a flare is radiated,25 then levels of heat radiated from a flare has to be measured as it is crucial for both design and safety purposes. The fraction of heat radiated, f-factor, means how much power is radiated from the flare as an output relative to how much chemical power in gas flared as an input.26 The fraction of heat radiated can be affected by gas composition, flame type, aeration, flame temperature, and flare burner tip, etc. The fraction of heat radiated can be calculated by using both theoretical and empirical approaches. In addition to the fraction of heat radiated, ground levels of radiation in the vicinity of the flare have to be calculated for both health and safety reasons. Noise Level

When the gas is flared, noise is generated. This noise and sound level is a function of gas composition, gas rate, flare stack height, burner tip design, flare tip velocity, wind speed, and distance from the flare stack. Models are available to calculate the sound level released from a gas flare at both the flare tip and on the ground. Similar to heat radiation, levels of sound power must be defined for safety and health purposes. It is well-known that the dynamic range of human hearing goes up to 130 dB (The Pain Threshold), and at the lowest end, it is audiometric zero or 0 dB (The Hearing Threshold). Researchers suggest that the safe exposure limit is 85 dB for 8 h a day.28,29

Flue Gas Composition

Flue gases are gases released as combustion products when hydrocarbons are burned. Normally, these gases contain nitrogen oxides. In addition, harmful pollutants may be present, such as methane, VOC, carbon dioxide and monoxide, sulfur dioxide, mercury, particulates, etc. Gas composition and gas mass rate are essential to calculate flue gas composition and other parameters such as molar weight, mass, and volume flow rates, SO2 concentration and emission rate, and other pollutants emission rates, in addition to heat released. Flue gases have to be controlled to reduce their environmental impact and emitted pollutants.28,29

Combustion Efficiency

Combustion efficiency can be defined as the proportion of hydrocarbons in the flare gas that undergoes complete conversion to carbon dioxide and water vapor. Thus, a higher combustion efficiency is a must. The efficiency of the combustion process in a flare is governed by many factors. Among these factors is the gas composition. For example, lighter gas burns better, while gases with high carbon content produce smoke flames and thus require more air. Other factors that may be involved in the process are flame temperature, aeration, residence time in the combustion zone, burner tip design, flare tip velocity, wind speed, etc.28 Gas combustion is considered complete when the gas is fully converted into carbon dioxide and water; otherwise, other organic compounds, aldehydes for example, may occur.

New Tip

The tip itself is a unique design due to a bullet device seated on top of the flare. The bullet device has four holes at the bottom with deflectors inside to cause the flowing gas to a single outlet at the top in a vortex. The bullet is attached to the flare tip with three springs that allow it to lift at ∼2 psig and to extend fully at ∼8 psig. This design of the flare tip will allow gas to travel around the bullet using the Coanda effect, and the flare will be sonic.

The new tip includes a fluid flow fitting with a tubular structure comprising a void allowing the fluid flow, a plurality of fins at least partially transverse to the flow extending inward from the inner wall, and an outlet cap with an outlet-centered hole. An ignition source is placed proximal to the outlet cap to ignite the fluid. The fins cause a swirling motion of the fluid, thereby separating contaminants from combustible gas and leading to more efficient and clean combustion. The fitting can also have an inlet cap with at least one inlet hole where the holes are radially offset from the center and are positioned to align the fluid flow with the fins within the tubular.

The fitting is displaceable in the direction or opposite of fluid flow. For example, if the inlet holes are not able to allow all fluids in the fitting, then the fitting can be displaced to allow for flow around it.

The fins can be helically curved with respect to fluid flow, and a bonnet can be installed to cover the fitting to contain and/or direct the flame. The swirling or vertexing of the fluid causes the heavier elements to be separated radially outward to avoid inhibiting fluid combustion.30

These tips are one of a kind due to the unique patented design; positive reversal ratio (PRR) spring-loaded tips. Thus, this can be the only design capable of handling high-to-low or low-to-high pressure variations, as well as ounces to pounds and pounds to ounces without affecting the functionality.

In addition, the tips are designed with low maintenance and easy troubleshooting, unlike other flares that will add to the cost of required attachments such as blowers, vapor assist, motors, and other equipment. With this tip, maintenance does not require highly qualified technicians or any special training due to its simple design. The tip is manufactured with 100% stainless steel, which allows the flare to burn at the highest melting point and withstand it. The new tip helps bring down monthly costs due to its eco-friendly technology while helping the environment by reducing footprint as it is smokeless and can burn more VOC.30

In the course of our rigorous experimentation, we systematically gathered data encompassing ground radiation levels, ground noise levels, flue gas composition, and computed combustion efficiency. Notably, our investigation pertains to a novel flare tip design for which empirical data are either nonexistent or extremely limited. To address this limitation, we employed a simplified open pipe flare tip as a thermal radiation level model. Subsequently, we meticulously compared the measured data against predictions generated using the Brzustowski and Sommer model,25 while maintaining consistent parameters including gas rate, flare height, and flare diameter.

In the context of this analysis, we calculated the fraction of heat radiated, denoted as “ f″, yielding a value of 0.24. Additionally, the transmissivity, represented as “ τ,″ was determined using the Brzustowski and Sommer model for an atmospheric humidity level of 50%, resulting in an approximate value of 0.99, which was rounded to 1.0 for practical purposes.

Furthermore, our assessment took into consideration specific environmental factors. These factors included a solar radiation adjustment of 250 Btu/h ft2, an ambient temperature of 90 °F, atmospheric relative humidity at 50%, and a horizontal wind velocity of 30.0 miles/h.

Conclusions and Recommendations

In summary, the environmental consequences of gas flaring, which encompass greenhouse gas emissions, contributions to global warming, and pollution, are extensive and far-reaching. Addressing these challenges necessitates a dedicated focus on innovative technologies to mitigate these adverse effects. The introduction of this groundbreaking flaring tip design, characterized by reduced heat radiation, minimized noise emissions, and lower pollutant outputs, represents a major step in this endeavor.

This cutting-edge flaring tip distinguishes itself through exceptional performance, standing out among its counterparts with its ability to produce stable, smokeless flames and effectively manage pressure variations across a wide spectrum. Importantly, these enhancements do not compromise the tip’s durability. Furthermore, the new design aligns with economic considerations, proving to be cost-effective in both capital expenditure (CapEx) and operational expenditure (OpEx). It achieves this by requiring minimal maintenance and streamlining troubleshooting procedures.

Due to the limited empirical data available for this novel tip design, the authors utilized a simplified open pipe flare tip as a thermal radiation level model and compared the measured data to predictions from the Brzustowski and Sommer model. These findings carry significant implications for the practical application and effectiveness of our novel gas flaring tip design.

Considering these compelling attributes, the authors wholeheartedly recommend widespread adoption of this innovative flaring tip. Its implementation holds the promise of significantly reducing the environmental impact associated with gas flaring, marking a meaningful step toward a more sustainable and responsible energy landscape. This advancement underscores the importance of continued innovation in the pursuit of environmentally responsible energy solutions.

Moreover, the authors contend that the US administration should reorient its priorities away from providing incentives to bolster the carbon industry. Instead, the focus should be directed toward the development and adoption of technologies designed to directly mitigate emissions at their source. Notable examples encompass gas-to-liquid (GTL), liquefied natural gas (LNG), and compressed natural gas (CNG), among others.

Embracing a proactive stance in this context carries the potential to yield more substantial and enduring strategies for reducing carbon emissions. Such a shift in emphasis holds the promise of advancing our efforts toward environmental harmony, promoting heightened effectiveness, and enhancing sustainability in our environmental mitigation endeavors.
