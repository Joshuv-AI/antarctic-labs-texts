Evaluating the hydration efficiency of bottle gourd peel powder and its nutritional impact on food formulations
Authors: S. N. Raghavendra, Baishali Ghosh, Chetan A. Nayak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bottle gourd peel is rich in dietary fiber and bioactive compounds, offering potential for functional food development. The objective of this study was to investigate how particle size influences the hydration and functional properties of bottle gourd peel powder and to evaluate its suitability in selected food applications. The effects of the particle size of bottle gourd peel powder on hydration properties are investigated. Results depicted that water holding capacity increased as particle size decreased from 600 to 425 µm, but declined further till 150 µm. The swelling capacity was found to decrease from 16.4 to 11.2 mL/g as the particle size decreased from 600 to 150 µm. The oil absorption capacity was found to be higher for the particle size of 500 µm and lower for the particle size of 425 µm. The physicochemical characteristics of dietary fiber to identify the functional groups and the crystalline structure were evaluated and reported. Sensory evaluation identified PM1 (pancake mix) and SM3 (soup mix) as the most acceptable formulations. Proximate composition for pancake mix exhibited significantly higher antioxidant activity. Overall, the study demonstrates that optimizing particle size enhances the functional performance of bottle gourd peel powder and supports its effective utilization in value-added food products.

Introduction

Fruit and vegetable waste consists of the inedible parts of produce discarded during harvesting, processing, handling, and distribution1. The definition of dietary fiber varies with context: botanists describe it as a structural component of plant tissues, chemical analysts define it as a group of chemical compounds, while consumers recognize it for its health benefits. In the food and chemical industries, dietary fiber also serves as an important marketing attribute2. The amount of food waste produced annually in Europe is estimated to be 89 million tons, and it is expected to increase by 40 times in the future. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) report states that about 40% of the food produced in India is wasted3. The world’s need for food is growing yearly due to the ongoing expansion of the human population. Reduced resources and the agricultural sector’s enormous waste output have led to a growing problem with food security. Effectively utilizing food waste, such as vegetable and fruit by-products, can increase food sustainability. This practice aligns with the UN Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) of ensuring sustainable consumption and production patterns. In addition, it has been reported that a few agricultural byproducts have a high nutritional value and may find use in the food industry as food and functional ingredients4. Half of the fresh fruit and vegetable peels that are produced by the food processing industry and households are mechanically destroyed. These waste peels have a high nutritional value and antioxidant activity because of their high phenolic content, proteins, lipids, fiber, and energy content. Fruit and vegetable peels are gaining scientific interest as potential functional foods due to their rich phenolic content, bioactive compounds, and notable medicinal and biological properties. In the past decade, dietary antioxidants have gained significant attention for their crucial role in preventing chronic diseases such as cancer and cardiovascular disorders5. The concept of antioxidant dietary fiber refers to the indigestible fraction of food that contains more than 50% dietary fiber (on a dry matter basis) and exhibits significant antioxidant activity6. In contrast to fruits, not all vegetables have peels. Peels are found in the tuber, gourd and allium families; however, they are not typically consumed or used in cooking. Vegetable peels are thus frequently thrown away in the manufacturing line as production or kitchen waste4.

Bottle gourd peel is one of the vegetable wastes generated during food processing. Bottle gourd peel was chosen for this research due to its high concentration of dietary fiber and bioactive compounds. Furthermore, it is an abundantly available, underutilized by-product with functional properties that are highly relevant for food formulation, distinguishing it from general fruit and vegetable wastes. The Bottle Gourd (Lagenaria siceraria) is a robust, annual climbing vine with large leaves. It is a member of the Cucurbitaceae family and is known by different names in various parts of India, including Lauki, Dudhi, and Calabash. It is among the most affordable sources of dietary fiber, vitamins, and minerals, as well as a possible source of natural antioxidants. Along with moderate levels of vitamin C, it is a reliable source of choline and the vitamin B complex. The vegetable is fleshy, while it has a shiny skin that is typically removed before eating. As such, it is worthwhile considering the potential of bottle gourd peel. This aids the bottle gourd processing plant’s waste utilization5,7. Bottle gourd peels are an excellent source of antioxidants and minerals8. Bottle gourd peels range in calcium and iron content from 12.5 to 11.25 mg when raw, steamed, or boiled. Comparing it to the entire bottle gourd fruit, its phosphorus content is higher. The skin of bottle gourds has the highest scavenging activity (84.86%) compared to 20.73% for whole and pulp9.

Processed grains, pulses, condiments, spices, and other food ingredients are often combined in varying proportions to develop instant mixes or value-added products, which facilitate the rapid and convenient preparation of a wide range of foods10. Incorporating functional ingredients like bottle gourd peel powder into these formulations enhances nutritional quality. Simultaneously, this approach promotes the sustainable utilization of agro-food waste. Hydration properties such as water holding, swelling, and fat absorption capacities are critical determinants of the functional performance of ingredients in food formulations, influencing texture, viscosity, yield, and storage stability. These properties are strongly governed by the physical characteristics of dietary fiber, particularly particle size, surface area, and microstructure. Therefore, understanding particle size–dependent hydration behavior is essential for optimizing the incorporation of fiber-rich by-products into functional food systems11. This research introduces a novel method by correlating the particle size and hydration properties of bottle gourd peel powder with its performance in value-added foods. This establishes a scientifically validated framework for transforming underutilized agricultural waste into functional food ingredients.

Water holding capacity (WHC)

An increase in the water holding capacity was noticed upon the reduction of particle size from 600 to 425 µm. The ability to hold water decreased as the particle size was further reduced from 425 to 150 µm. A higher potential water holding capacity was shown by particles with a size of 425 µm. One of the most crucial factors affecting the WHC is the particle size. Food powders’ WHC consistently rise when particle size decreases because of their enhanced specific surface area and capacity to bind with water12. In contrast, Ma et al. reported that the water holding capacity of peel powder from Aloe barbadensis dropped from 6.21% to 3.52% when the particle size shrank13. The results show that some soluble substances were dissolved because of increasing cell rupture, and that high-speed abrasive impact shearing caused some long-chain structures to break into short-chain structures. This decreased the binding force of some active substances to water, which in turn caused the water holding capacity to decrease.

Swelling capacity (SWC)

The physical properties of the fiber matrix, such as its porosity and crystallinity, as well as the traits of the individual components, determine the swelling capacity14,15. As the particle size reduced from 600 to 150 µm, the bottle gourd peel powder’s swelling capacity was observed to drop from 16.4 to 11.2 mL/g. Effect of particle size on fat absorption capacity (FAC)

Fat Absorption Capacity (FAC) is a crucial factor in the development of new products and the stability of their storage. Higher fat absorption capacity was observed for 500 µm particle size, and reduced fat absorption capacity for 425 µm particle size. Particularly in cooked meat products that are prone to fat loss during cooking, FAC is essential for flavor retention and yield14–16. Figure 1c illustrates how the fat absorption capacity (FAC) of bottle gourd peel powder is affected by particle size. The fat absorption capacity value for 600 and 500 μm, the values increased from 1.036 g/g and 1.292 g/g. Further, the FAC values decreased with decreasing particle size.

FTIR analysis

FTIR analysis of dietary fiber extracted from bottle gourd peels was analyzed and depicted in Fig. 2. A broad and intense peak around 3400–3200 cm−1 corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations, indicating the presence of hydroxyl groups commonly associated with alcohols, phenols, or polysaccharides such as cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin17. The strong peak at 3296 cm−1 is characteristic of the O–H stretching vibration, which indicates the presence of hydroxyl groups, likely from alcohols, and signifies that hydrogen bonding is present. This identifies the functional groups within the dietary fiber, which are attributed to the presence of cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin. The moderate peak observed at 3000 to 2850 cm−1 indicates carbon-hydrogen stretching vibrations of the alkane group. A distinct absorption band around 1700–1600 cm−1 corresponds to C=O stretching, confirming the presence of carbonyl groups that may arise from carboxylic acids, esters, or aldehydes. The medium intensity bands near 1632 cm−1 indicates the C=C stretching typical of aromatic or unsaturated structures. A strong absorption in 1036 cm−1 region is due to carbon-oxygen molecules stretching a vibration, which is attributed to the presence of esters and alcohols. Overall, the spectral features confirm the presence of hydroxyl, carbonyl, and ether-based functional groups, characteristic of plant polysaccharides and lignin, highlighting the complex organic matrix of the dietary fiber. XRD analysis

The structural characteristics of the dietary fiber samples were analyzed using XRD. The XRD diffractogram of dietary fiber is shown in Fig. 3. The characteristic broad peak or “amorphous halo” around a 2θ value of 22° indicates that the material is predominantly amorphous. Unlike crystalline materials, which produce sharp, distinct peaks in an XRD pattern, amorphous materials lack a long-range, ordered structure, resulting in a broad, diffused peak. The broad peak observed in the pattern at approximately 22° is typical for amorphous or semi-crystalline substances like dietary fiber. Dietary fibers are complex carbohydrates, which are composed of both crystalline and amorphous regions. The amorphous portion is made up of substances like hemicellulose and pectin, while the crystalline portion is primarily cellulose. The dominance of the broad peak suggests a high degree of amorphous content in the sample. The XRD pattern is characteristic of a material with a low degree of crystallinity, which is consistent with the amorphous nature of many dietary fiber components like hemicellulose and pectin17,21.Fig. 3X-ray diffraction spectrum of the bottle gourd peel dietary fiber.The figure depicts XRD analysis of dietary fiber extracted from bottle gourd peels.

TGA analysis

The TGA analysis of the dietary fiber of bottle gourd peel was analyzed. The Fig. 5 shows the weight (%) v/s temperature (bold line) and the derivative of weight v/s temperature (dotted line). TGA is utilized for determining the thermal stability of the material on the change in mass when heated at constant rate. The degradation can be divided into 4 stages, Stage 1: Below 100 °C, Stage 2: 200 to 300 °C, Stage 3: 300 to 400 °C, and Stage 4: above 400 °C. In the first stage (<100 °C), a minor weight loss is observed, corresponding to the initial evaporation of free and bound water molecules present in the sample. This is indicated by a small curve in the derivative weight curve. The maximum weight loss was observed at the second stage (200–300 °C), characterized by rapid and significant weight loss. The large, sharp peak in the derivative weight curve in this region indicates the decomposition of hemicellulose and pectin, which are primary components of bottle gourd peel dietary fiber. The peak maximum around 295 °C suggests the temperature at which the rate of decomposition is highest. In the third stage, the second broader peak is visible in the derivative weight curve. This stage is primarily attributed to the thermal degradation of the more stable cellulose component of the fiber. The broader nature of this peak suggests a more gradual decomposition process compared to the hemicellulose. Further, the last stage, i.e., the fourth stage, wherein the char gets decomposed, which is a slow process, and the mass is reducing slowly. The weight loss significantly slows down and stabilizes, leaving a residual mass (around 25–30%). This residual mass represents the non-volatile inorganic matter and carbonaceous char formed during the decomposition process. These characteristic multi-stage trends in TGA curves are consistent with the reported thermal behavior of lignocellulosic fiber24 and anthocyanin pigments25.Fig. 5Thermogravimetric analysis of the bottle gourd peel dietary fiber.The figure shows the weight (%) v/s temperature (blue line) and the derivative of weight v/s temperature (dotted line red line).

Antioxidant activity analysis

The antioxidant activity in soup mix and pancake mix formulations extracted with methanol was measured by DPPH radical scavenging activity. The measured activity is as follows: the antioxidant activity was found to be 76.56% and 93.13% for the pancake mix control and the PM1 sample, respectively. Compared to the control sample, the pancake mix that was prepared had a remarkably high level of antioxidant scavenging activity. This may also be due to the addition of other ingredients like foxnut powder along with bottle gourd peel powder. One important natural source of antioxidants is foxnut. Whereas antioxidant activity was found to be 78.47% and 78.66% for the soup mix control and the SM3 sample, respectively. The prepared control sample and soup mix sample did not differ significantly from one another.

Dietary fiber extracts obtained from fruit and vegetable by-products, such as peels and pomace, have been widely reported to exhibit significant DPPH radical scavenging activity, reflecting their ability to neutralize free radicals in vitro. This antioxidant potential is often strongly correlated with the presence of fiber-bound phenolic compounds, which remain associated with the dietary fiber matrix after processing. For instance, banana flower powder, a fiber-rich by-product, demonstrated high DPPH radical scavenging activity along with substantial total phenolic content, underscoring the role of polyphenol-rich dietary fiber in contributing to antioxidant functionality28. These findings support the concept of antioxidant dietary fiber, in which the fiber fraction performs a dual role. It serves a physiological function while also acting as a carrier for bioactive compounds with free radical scavenging capacity, thereby enhancing the nutritional and functional value of fiber-enriched foods.

This study examined the effect of particle size on the hydration, functional, and physicochemical properties of bottle gourd peel powder and assessed its suitability for use in value-added food products. The results clearly demonstrate that optimizing particle size is a key factor in enhancing the functional performance of bottle gourd peel–derived dietary fiber. Among the tested fractions, the best hydration performance was observed at intermediate particle sizes, where water holding capacity increased from 600 to 425 µm and decreased upon further reduction in particle size. Swelling capacity showed a gradual reduction from 16.4 to 11.2 mL/g with decreasing particle size, while fat absorption capacity was highest at 500 µm, indicating suitability for food systems requiring improved texture, yield, and flavor retention. Physicochemical characterization using FTIR, XRD, SEM, and TGA confirmed the presence of polysaccharide-rich dietary fiber with predominantly amorphous structure, porous honeycomb-like morphology, and good thermal stability. These structural characteristics directly enhance water and oil binding capacities, thereby improving functionality in food formulations. Incorporation of bottle gourd peel powder into soup mix and pancake mix did not significantly alter proximate composition compared to control samples, while sensory evaluation identified SM3 and PM1 as the most acceptable formulations. Importantly, the pancake mix enriched with bottle gourd peel powder exhibited significantly higher antioxidant activity, highlighting added nutritional benefits. From a sustainability perspective, this study demonstrates a practical approach to valorizing bottle gourd peel into a functional food ingredient. Such utilization supports waste reduction, promotes circular economy practices, and offers cost-effective opportunities for the food industry. Overall, bottle gourd peel powder shows strong potential as a sustainable, fiber-rich ingredient for functional foods, with future scope for scale-up, shelf-life evaluation, and expanded product applications.

Methods

Materials

The bottle gourd was procured from a local supermarket, Mangaluru, Karnataka, India. The mature bottle gourd was cleaned with running tap water and peeled using a peeler. The peels were further processed for the preparation of the sample.

Chemicals and reagents

Potassium sulfate, Copper sulfate, Sodium hydroxide, Boric acid, Sulfuric acid, 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), Potassium bromide, Methanol (99.9% Purity), Hexane (>96% Purity). The chemicals were procured from Merck India Ltd., Mumbai, India. All the chemicals were of analytical reagent (AR) grade.

Preparation of the sample

The peels were collected and dried at 60 °C for 48 h using a hot air oven (IKA Oven, Model No.125 Basic, Manufacturer: IKA India Private Limited, India). The samples were stored at room temperature in an airtight container. The dried peels are subjected to grinding using a mixer (Preethi Zion Mixer Grinder, Model: MG-227, Preethi Kitchen Appliances Private Limited, India) for two minutes. The grounded sample was stored in airtight containers in refrigerated conditions at 8 ± 2°C until further usage.

Determination of particle size (sieve analysis)

Particle size distribution of Bottle Gourd Peel Powder (BPP) was determined using standard British Standard Sieves (BSS) with mesh numbers 25, 30, 36, 60, 85, and 100. The sieves, corresponding to aperture sizes ranging from 600 to 150 µm, were arranged in descending order, with the largest aperture at the top and the smallest at the bottom, using a tabletop vibratory Sieve shaker (Model:HSC—SS Sieve Shaker, Hindustan Scale Co, India). A 50 g sample of BPP was placed on the top sieve, and the sieve stack was mechanically shaken for 10 min. The material retained on each sieve was carefully collected and weighed to obtain the particle size fractions. Sieve analysis was performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Determination of hydration properties

The hydration properties were conducted as per the following protocol at ambient temperature and were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Water holding capacity (WHC)

The volume of water bonded to the fibers in the absence of any external forces aside from atmospheric pressure and gravity is known as the water holding capacity. Approximately 15 mL of water was added to a precisely weighed 0.5 g dry BPP sample that was placed in a graduated test tube. The hydration was carried out for 24 h. FTIR analysis

The bottle gourd peel ground was mixed well with potassium bromide (KBr) AR Grade and made into thin disks using a hydraulic press at 100 bar pressure. Preparation of pellets was completed without wasting time to avoid the moisture effect on pellets because KBr is very highly hygroscopic and will affect the results. The spectra were recorded using an FTIR spectrophotometer (Model Spectrum 2, PerkinElmer Spectrum Version 10.4.3) from 4000 to 500 cm−1 wave number at a data acquisition rate of 1 cm per point at ambient temperature. The analysis of IR spectroscopy was performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

XRD characterization of dietary fiber

X-ray diffraction studies were carried out using Panalytical X’pert-3 pro with λ = 1.544 Å and Bragg’s angle (2θ) ranging from 10° to 80°. Figure 4 reveals the XRD pattern of the bottle gourd peel dietary fiber. All experimentation was carried out in triplicate.

Morphological characterization

The morphology of the encapsulated fiber was observed by SEM. The dietary fiber grounded and sieved was attached to aluminum stubs using double-sided adhesive carbon tape and coated under vacuum with 200 Å gold before analysis using a scanning electron microscope (SEM Model: Tescan Vega 3 LM Octane Pro, Module Info:2.18/1116129, Czech Republic, Europe). The SEM was operated at 5 to 20 kV and magnified to 1000× and 2000×. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric analysis measures the mass loss of a sample as it is heated under a controlled atmosphere (usually nitrogen). In dietary fiber studies, TGA provides valuable information on the thermal stability, composition, and structural integrity of fiber fractions. TGA was performed on a PerkinElmer Thermal Analysis instrument. A 3 to 4 mg quantity of ground dietary fiber was heated and analyzed over the temperature range of 30 °C to 680 °C, at 10 °C/min in alumina crucibles, under the constant flow of Nitrogen gas (N2) at the rate of 20 mL min−1. The results of the TGA measurement are usually displayed as a thermogram, in which percent mass, the first derivative is plotted against temperature.

Product formulation

The bottle gourd peel powder was incorporated in products such as “Soup Mix” and “Pancake Mix”. The detailed preparation of the soup and pancake mix is discussed in the section below. All samples were prepared in triplicate to ensure consistency.

Preparation of soup mix

Procurement of raw materials: The fresh bottle gourd, papaya, cabbage, tomato, onion, garlic, green chili, coriander leaves, mint leaves, thickening agents like coconut milk powder, corn flour, and spices and herbs like Italian herbs, cumin seeds were procured from a local supermarket in Mangaluru.

Drying of the ingredients: Peels from papaya and bottle gourd were dried for 48 h at 60 °C. The ingredients onion, garlic, mint, coriander, and green chili were dried at 60 °C and tomato at 80 °C for varying periods of time. Each ingredient was then powdered individually and placed in an airtight container.

Formulations of soup mix: Three distinct formulations were created by adjusting the percentages of papaya and bottle gourd peel powder, while maintaining the same quantity of other ingredients in each formulation. Along with a control sample, the three formulations were coded as SM1 (10% + 10%), SM2 (5% + 15%), and SM3 (15% + 5%) to indicate the various percentages of bottle gourd and papaya peel powder, respectively. Table 3 shows the various proportions and weights of the ingredients that were combined to create the soup mix.

Preparation of soup: The soup was prepared by mixing the ingredients as per Table 3 with potable water under continuous stirring to obtain a uniform slurry. The mixture was heated to boiling and simmered for 6 to 7 min until a smooth and homogeneous consistency was achieved. The freshly prepared soup was served warm for sensory evaluation26.

Sensory analysis

Prepared soup mix and pancake mix were organoleptically evaluated for attributes such as color, appearance, taste, flavor, consistency, texture, and overall acceptability using a 9-point hedonic scale by a panel of 15 semi-trained members (age range 22–30 years; 8 males and 7 females), selected based on prior experience with similar food products. Samples were presented in a randomized order to minimize positional bias. All participants provided informed consent prior to this study.

Proximate analysis

The proximate analysis of the final products and control sample, namely moisture, ash, fat, protein, and crude fiber were determined according to standard methods given by ref. 30. The carbohydrate was determined by the difference method, and the energy value of the final product was determined theoretically by calculating energy values from the values of fat, protein, and carbohydrates. The sodium and potassium content were determined using flame photometry (Model 128, Systronics India Ltd., Ahmedabad, India)31.

Antioxidant activity analysis

The modified method to evaluate the scavenging activity of the free radicals and determine antioxidant activity using 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH)32. One gram of samples was extracted in a rotary shaker at 250 rpm (Remi Mini Rotary Shaker, RS-18plus, India) using 25 mL of methanol for a duration of 24 h. After shaking, the extract was centrifuged for 15 min at 5000 rpm. After gathering the supernatant, 0.5 mL was combined with 2.0 mL of freshly made 0.1 mM DPPH solution, and the volume was made to 3 mL using methanol. After that, it was incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. The absorbance was measured at 517 nm using methanol as the blank and standard DPPH as the control.
