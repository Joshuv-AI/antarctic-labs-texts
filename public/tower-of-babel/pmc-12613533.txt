Body mass index, waist circumference and the association with all-cause and cause-specific mortality: a 7.2-year follow-up cohort study in Shanghai, China
Authors: Minhua Tang, Yongmei Li, Xing Liu, Huiping Lu, Yiling Wu, Liping Yi, Yonggen Jiang, Genming Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveStudies on the effects of obesity on mortality have contradictory findings, and few studies have focused on the relationship between the combined effect of body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC) and the range of specific causes of death. We aimed to investigate the association between BMI, WC, or both and all-cause and cause-specific mortality among adults in China.MethodsThis prospective cohort study was performed in Songjiang District, Shanghai, and included 35,787 participants aged 20–74 years. Questionnaire interviews, anthropometric measurements, and laboratory assays were conducted to collect baseline data. BMI (kg/m2) was categorized as follows: underweight (< 18.5), normal weight (18.5–23.9), overweight (24.0–27.9), grade 1 obesity (28.0–31.9), grade 2 obesity (32.0–35.9), and grade 3 obesity (≥ 36.0). WC was categorized into central obesity and non-central obesity. Cox regression models were used to analyze the relationship between BMI and WC and the risk of mortality.ResultsDuring a median of 7.16 years of follow-up, 1250 deaths were recorded. Underweight was negatively associated with mortality. A BMI in the range of 24.0–31.9 kg/m2 was a protective factor for all-cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality, while central obesity was related to a high mortality risk. The joint effect of a high BMI and a normal WC presented lower all-cause (HR: 0.79, 95%CI: 0.68–0.91) and CVD mortality (HR: 0.67, 95%CI: 0.49–0.92). The restricted cubic spline showed a U-shaped association between BMI and overall (lowest mortality risk at 27.4 kg/m2) and CVD mortality (lowest mortality risk at 27.5 kg/m2), as well as a J-shaped association between WC and mortality.ConclusionMaintaining a relatively high BMI of 24.0–27.9 kg/m2 and a normal WC can reduce the risk of overall and CVD mortality. The U-shaped association between BMI and mortality also suggests that both underweight and extremely high BMI should be avoided.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25147-0.

Introduction

Overweight and obesity have become a great public health issue worldwide [1]. In China, the prevalence of overweight and obesity exceeds 50%, having increased rapidly in the past four decades [2]. Overweight and obesity are regarded as risk factors for a number of diseases such as hypertension, stroke, and coronary heart disease [3–5]. Thus, it is logical to expect obesity or overweight to be related to a high risk of mortality. Several studies have shown a J-shaped association between BMI and overall mortality, and the risk increases significantly in overweight and obese individuals compared to those with normal weight, indicating that low or high levels of BMI affect individuals’ health [1, 6–9]. However, numerous studies have shown a protective effect of overweight or obesity on the risk of mortality, especially death from cardiovascular disease (CVD), cancer, and respiratory disease, leading to what is known as the obesity paradox [10–13]. The protective role of obesity has become a hot research topic in recent years. It has also been found that obesity or overweight has no effect on mortality in a population [14]. Both BMI and waist circumference (WC) are common measurable indices to assess body fat distribution, reflecting total body fat mass and abdominal fat distribution, respectively. These two indicators are not independent of each other and can contribute to the development of different body shapes. However, only a few studies have explored the joint effect of BMI and WC on the risk of mortality [15–17] or have studied their impacts separately [18]. Individuals with a high BMI but a low WC, potentially due to a muscular physique, may be misclassified as being at high risk, whereas those with a low BMI but a high WC, indicative of accumulated visceral fat, may have their risk underestimated. Therefore, the study of BMI and WC in combination can allow us to better understand the impact of obesity on the risk of death in the population and can better provide the population with scientific weight and WC management recommendations to reduce the risk of death [15].

Considering the mixed results found in previous studies with different cutoffs or categories of BMI and WC and with populations from different regions, it is inappropriate to apply their conclusions to a specific population. In recent years, Songjiang District has experienced a rapid trend of urbanization. Urbanization has increased the population density and exposure to environmental risk factors, accelerated changes in people’s lifestyles, and aggravated population health disparities, such as the high prevalence of hypertension (52.19%), diabetes (16.14%), and hyperlipidemia (59.56%) in the district [19]. Therefore, to find an acceptable BMI and WC for people and to better reduce the risks of mortality, we aimed to explore the relationship between BMI and WC and all-cause and cause-specific mortality in a study carried out with a cohort from the southwest suburbs of Shanghai, China.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

The data were obtained from an ongoing prospective cohort study—the Shanghai Suburban Adult Cohort and Biobank (SSACB) study, Songjiang sub-cohort—which was conducted from June 2016 to December 2017. Songjiang District, situated in Shanghai’s southwestern suburban region, uniquely embodies a blend of urban and rural characteristics, allowing the research findings to meaningfully represent the health-related circumstances of Shanghai’s suburban population. Participants aged 20 to 74 years from 4 districts in Songjiang, Shanghai (Zhongshan, Xinqiao, Sheshan, and Maogang), who had resided in the area for more than 5 years, were recruited into the cohort using a stratified multistage random sampling method. Long-term residents have already adapted to the local environment and developed a relatively stable lifestyle. Additionally, they have abundant previous medical information, have low geographical mobility and are convenient for long-term follow-up. In Zhongshan and Xinqiao, we randomly selected 18 and 9 neighborhood committees, respectively, and in Sheshan and Maogang, we randomly selected 4 and 16 administrative villages, respectively, accounting for one-third of the committees or villages in each community. The details of the enrollment of the participants can be accessed through the profile of the cohort [20].

Participants in the cohort completed a face-to-face questionnaire interview that collected information on demographic characteristics (sex, age, educational level, etc.), self-reported medical history (malignant tumor, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, etc.), and lifestyle (physical activity, alcohol drinking status, smoking status, etc.). Information about the participants’ dietary consumption was collected using a food frequency questionnaire. Anthropometric measurements (weight, height, WC, etc.) were carried out by licensed physicians in the community. Prior to their measurement in the laboratory, each participant was required to abstain from eating for at least 8 h. Blood and urine samples were collected by licensed nurses and sent to Dian Medical Laboratory Center for testing. The follow-up information of the participants was collected from electronic medical records (EMRs) and cause-of-death surveillance data through multiple databases linkage using unique identification card numbers.

A total of 36,404 individuals were recruited into the cohort at baseline, and 35,787 individuals were eligible for final analysis after excluding 550 participants with abnormal or incomplete data on height, weight, and WC and 63 participants with incomplete data on smoking, alcohol drinking, physical activity and dietary intake (Fig. 1). Each participant provided written authorization confirming their informed consent prior to the collection of data. The Ethics Committee on Medical Research at the School of Public Health, Fudan University, approved the study protocol (IRB#2016-04-0586).

Assessment of BMI and WC

All measurements were performed according to standard protocol by licensed physicians in the communities [20]. When measuring height and weight, the participants were required to remove their shoes and hats, wear light clothing, stand on the scale with their eyes looking straight ahead, let their upper limbs hang naturally, keep their legs straight, and place their heels together. The measurements were recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm for height and 0.1 kg for weight, and the average of two measurements was taken. BMI (kg/m2) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m2). BMI was subdivided into six groups: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–23.9 kg/m2), overweight (24.0–27.9 kg/m2), grade 1 obesity (28.0–31.9 kg/m2), grade 2 obesity (32–35.9 kg/m2), and grade 3 obesity (≥ 36.0 kg/m2) [21]. WC was measured as the circumference of the midpoint line between the lowest rib and the upper iliac crest using a flexible tape. Central obesity was defined as a WC ≥ 90 cm in males and ≥ 85 cm in females according to the Chinese Diabetes Society [21].

Follow-up and outcomes

Every participant was linked to multiple databases that were updated every 6 months and checked by professionals to obtain detailed records on disease diagnoses, date of diagnosis, and medication information. The outcome was all-cause mortality and cause-specific mortality recorded from the cause-of-death surveillance data. We obtained details on the primary cause of death and date of death from the baseline to 30 May 2024.

Assessment of covariates

Information on sex, age, educational degree, alcohol drinking, and smoking was recorded using an in-person survey. The amount of smoking was calculated as the number of packs multiplied by the number of years of smoking, and participants were grouped into non-smokers or those who smoked < 20.0, 20.0–39.9, or ≥ 40.0 pack-years. Alcohol use was calculated as the average daily intake of alcohol (g/d). Physical activity was calculated as the total minutes in a week (mins/week) multiplied by the metabolic equivalent task (MET) [22] and divided into low (< 2000 MET-mins/week) moderate (2000–5999 MET-mins/week), and high (≥ 6000 MET-mins/week) levels [23]. Chronic diseases were determined according to self-reporting and/or the anthropometric measurements and/or the previous diagnosis in the EMRs. Hypertension was defined as self-reported diagnosed hypertension, measured systolic blood pressure of ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure of ≥ 90 mmHg, or administration of antihypertensive medication [24]. Diabetes was defined based on self-reported previous history of diabetes, fasting plasma glucose level of ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or glycated hemoglobin level of ≥ 6.5% [25]. Dyslipidemia was defined as any one of the following 5 conditions: (1) total cholesterol (TC) of ≥ 6.2 mmol/L; (2) high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) of < 1.0 mmol/L; (3) low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) of ≥ 4.1 mmol/L; (4) triglyceride (TG) of ≥ 2.3 mmol/L; (5) use of lipid-lowering drugs [26]. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined as a persistent decline in kidney function for more than 3 months (estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60mL/min/m2) or the presence of kidney damage (including proteinuria, hematuria, and leukocyturia) [27]. CVD, including stroke and coronary heart disease, was confirmed based on the self-reported questionnaire or a previous diagnosis history [28]. Cancer cases were collected from the Cancer Registry System in Shanghai. Total energy intake, including 29-item food intake, was collected via the food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) and was calculated as the average daily intake (g/d). The FFQ was validated using 3-day 24-h dietary recalls, and reliability was evaluated by administering a second FFQ to 152 randomly chosen participants from the Zhongshan community in the SSACB. The correlation coefficients of validity ranged from 0.12 to 0.42 for nutrients and from 0.20 to 0.41 for food groups [29].

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics are described by the BMI and WC groups. Normally distributed continuous variables are described as the mean ± standard deviation, and non-normally distributed data are expressed as median values with interquartile ranges, using one-way ANOVA analysis and the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney U-test for comparison between groups, respectively. Student’s t-test was used for the normally distributed continuous variables in the WC groups. Categorical variables are expressed as the frequency (%), and chi-square tests were used for inter-group comparisons.

Schoenfeld individual tests were performed first, and the data conformed to the proportional hazard assumption. Then, Cox regression models were used to analyze the relationship between BMI and WC (presented as continuous and categorical variables) and the risk of all-cause and cause-specific mortality. Model 1 was adjusted for sex (male and female) and age (continuous variable). Model 2 was additionally adjusted for education level (≤ 6 years, 7–12 years, and ≥ 13 years), smoking index (nonsmokers, < 20.0, 20.0–39.9, and ≥ 40.0 pack-years), alcohol use (continuous variable, g/d), and physical activity (low, moderate, and high). Model 3 was further adjusted for hypertension (yes and no), diabetes (yes and no), dyslipidemia (yes and no), CKD (yes and no), CVD (yes and no), cancer (yes and no), central obesity (yes and no), and total energy intake (continuous variable, kcal/d). Stratified analyses were conducted according to sex (male and female), age (< 60 and ≥ 60 years old), smoking status (yes and no), drinking status (yes and no), and a series of chronic diseases (yes and no). A possible dose–response association between BMI and WC and overall and cause-specific mortality was identified using restricted cubic spline analysis. In addition, to avoid reverse causality and ensure the robustness of our primary findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted by restricting the analyses to participants without a history of cancer and CVD before baseline and excluding the first year of death. Statistically significant differences were set at two-tailed p-values < 0.05. Data analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 and R version 4.1.3.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants and outcome data

The baseline characteristics of the 35,787 participants are shown in Table 1. The mean age was 56.4 ± 11.2 years. Male participants accounted for 40.7% of the total. The prevalence in the six BMI categories was 2.6% for underweight, 44.4% for normal weight, 39.5% for overweight, 11.6% for grade 1 obesity, 1.7% for grade 2 obesity, and 0.3% for grade 3 obesity. Compared to people with a low BMI, the overweight and obese participants were older and had higher levels of alcohol use per day, a higher total energy intake per day, and higher WC levels. Among the 35,787 participants, male participants, smokers, and people with low education levels, a high level of physical activity, and a series of chronic diseases had a higher prevalence of overweight and obesity. Female participants, non-smokers, people with high education levels and low physical activity, and people with cancer at baseline had a higher prevalence of underweight. The baseline characteristics of the study participants by WC category are shown in Table S1. During the 7.16 years of follow-up, 1250 deaths (4.8/1000 person-years) were recorded from the Cause-of-Death Surveillance System, and the top three causes of death were cancer (2.5/1000 person-years), CVD (1.1/1000 person-years), and injuries and external causes (0.3/1000 person-years).

Figure 2 presents the mortality density of all-cause and cause-specific mortality in the different BMI and WC groups. The mortality density was notably elevated in the < 18.5 kg/m2 and ≥ 32.0 kg/m2 groups, reaching 7.4 and 7.2 per 1000 person-years, respectively, while the normal-weight group exhibited the lowest mortality density (4.5/1000 person-years). A similar pattern was observed for CVD mortality. Both all-cause and CVD mortality demonstrated a U-shaped pattern (p < 0.001). However, cancer-related mortality and injury and external cause-related mortality showed no statistically significant differences among the BMI groups (p > 0.05). The all-cause, cancer, and CVD mortality was significantly higher in the central obese population than in the normal WC population (p < 0.05).

Association between BMI and WC and all-cause and cause-specific mortality

As shown in Table 2, after full adjustment for potential confounders, Cox regression analyses showed that people who were overweight (HR = 0.81, 95%CI = 0.71–0.93) and those with grade 1 obesity (HR = 0.76, 95%CI = 0.61–0.94) had a lower risk of total death compared to those with normal weight, whereas, underweight participants had a high mortality risk (HR = 2.14, 95%CI = 1.60–2.87). The same relationship was shown between BMI and CVD mortality. Overweight was related to a 33% (HR = 0.67, 95%CI = 0.50–0.89) lower risk of CVD mortality, and grade 1 obesity was associated with a 42% (HR = 0.58, 95%CI = 0.37–0.89) lower risk of CVD mortality. The risk of CVD death in the underweight group was 3.46 times (HR = 3.46, 95%CI = 1.97–6.08) higher than that in the normal-weight group. Central obesity was associated with a 24% (HR = 1.24, 95%CI = 1.07–1.44) higher risk of all-cause mortality and a 35% (HR = 1.35, 95%CI = 1.01–1.82) higher risk of CVD mortality. No statistically significant associations were found between the categorical BMI variable or central obesity and the risk of cancer-related death. Furthermore, no evidence was found between BMI or WC and the risk of injury and external death (data not shown).

We further studied the joint effect of BMI and WC on mortality in Table 2. Stratified analysis

Dose–response association between BMI and WC and all-cause and cause-specific mortality

Figure 3 presents the dose–response relationships between BMI and mortality using restricted cubic spline diagrams. In all participants, the curves displayed a U-shaped relationship for all-cause (mortality decreased at 24.3 < BMI < 31.7 kg/m2 and was lowest at 27.4 kg/m2) and CVD mortality (mortality decreased at 24.2 < BMI < 32.6 kg/m2 and was lowest at 27.5 kg/m2) (Fig. 3A, G; p for nonlinear < 0.001). However, when the BMI exceeded 28.0 kg/m2, the curve presented exactly the opposite direction in men and women (Fig. 3B, H). A negative association between BMI and mortality was observed in men, presenting a reversed J shape. In women, the curves showed that the overall and CVD mortality risk was relatively low at 24.2 < BMI < 29.3 kg/m2 (lowest at 26.6 kg/m2) and 24.2 < BMI < 30.5 kg/m2(lowest at 27.0 kg/m2), presenting a U shape in the relationship (p for nonlinear < 0.001). Discussion

In this study, we explored the effects of BMI and WC on overall and cause-specific mortality. We observed a U-shaped association between BMI and mortality (all-cause and CVD mortality) and a J-shaped association between WC and mortality (all-cause, cancer, and CVD mortality). Underweight was associated with high risks of death, while overweight and grade 1 obesity were related to lower all-cause and CVD mortality rates. When the BMI value increased and reached grade 3 obesity, a high overall mortality risk was observed in women. The joint effect of BMI and WC showed that people with a high BMI (≥ 24.0 kg/m2) and a normal WC presented lower all-cause and CVD mortality, while those who were underweight with a normal WC had higher all-cause and CVD mortality.

Our results are consistent with other studies, and we observed the low risks of mortality in overweight or obese subjects, especially in older populations. A large prospective cohort in China (SHARE study, Shenzhen Healthy Ageing Research) among 359,044 older people (≥ 65 years) concluded that a BMI between 24.0 and 29.9 kg/m2 demonstrated a protective effect [30]. A cohort study of 81,221 elderly people from Taiwan, China, showed that overweight and obese people had lower all-cause, circulation, and cancer mortality [31]. A meta-analysis, including more than 2.88 million participants, showed that overweight was associated with significantly lower all-cause mortality, and reported mortality reduced by 9% in people aged ≥ 65 years (HR = 0.91, 95%CI = 0.84–0.98) [32]. In a cohort study in rural South Africa (ACDIS, the Africa Centre Demographic Information System), those people (aged ≥ 15 years) with a BMI of 25.0 to 29.9 kg/m2 (HR = 0.8, 95%CI = 0.69–0.92) or 30.0 to 34.9 kg/m2 (HR = 0.75, 95%CI = 0.60–0.93) had a lower risk of death [33]. Our study supports the obesity paradox; however, when the BMI reached an extremely high level (≥ 36.0 kg/m2), the mortality risk increased significantly in certain people. The same results were shown in previous studies. In the meta-analysis mentioned earlier, the mortality increased across all ages (HR = 1.35, 95%CI = 1.16–1.57) when BMI was ≥ 35.0 kg/m2 [32]. Previous studies have also reported that the optimal ranges of BMI for the population raise gradually with age [13, 34]. This finding may suggest that the recommendations regarding healthy weight should account for age and the elderly population can maintain a certain degree of overweight or mild obesity.

When stratified by sex, the findings vary across different studies. In our study, we found trends in the opposite direction in men and women when the BMI exceeded 28.0 kg/m2. In addition, for those with an extremely high BMI of ≥ 36.0 kg/m2, higher mortality risks were only found in women. We observed that the optimal BMI range for reduced mortality is wider in men (from overweight to grade 1 obesity) than in women (overweight), indicating that the “obesity paradox” is more significant in male individuals. In the SHARE and ACDIS studies, both women and men presented lower mortality with an increasing level of BMI [30, 33]. However, several studies have suggested that both women and men show increased all-cause mortality, presenting a J shape [8, 13]. These discrepant findings may be related to the different definitions of obesity and the heterogeneity of the populations (including race, age structure, lifestyle, nutritional status, and geographic location).

Underweight is regarded as a risk factor for mortality and often represents clinically underlying organic diseases, including heart failure, fracture, issues relating to muscle and fat wasting, muscle weakness, malnutrition, and reduced bone density and bone metabolism [35–38]. The favorable prognosis for overweight or obesity can be explained by the following mechanisms. First, being overweight or obese can provide additional nutritional support or increase the metabolic reserve [10], which may prevent malnutrition and hinder the metabolic consequences of diseases [39]. The adipokines produced by adipose tissue appear to have a cardioprotective effect, and patients with heart failure often have a better prognosis and higher survival rate [39]. Second, the elderly population is more prone to severe calcium loss, which can lead to decreased bone density, osteoporosis problems, and an increased risk of fractures [37]. Obese adults have been found to have increased bone mineral density, which may effectively protect against bone loss [40]. Third, when weight gain is the result of muscle mass, the muscle-to-fat mass ratio can affect the survival rate. People with high muscle and low fat mass show a decreased mortality risk, and the relation is more significant in the male population [41, 42]. The myokines released by muscles can reduce inflammation and insulin resistance [43]. Fat mass and levels of subcutaneous fat are lower in men than in women at a given BMI, which could partly explain the statistically significant reduction in the risk of death from being overweight or obese in men compared to women [9]. However, excessive fat storage in the body can cause more serious issues. People with excess fat deposition are more likely to develop hyperlipidemia, and adipokines can induce insulin resistance, hypercoagulability, and systemic inflammation, which further increase the risk of atherosclerosis and mortality [44]. Based on the potential reasons above, adequate nutritional reserves are of great significance in reducing the mortality risk among the elderly. For women, avoiding excessive obesity is critical as it substantially increases mortality risk. Additionally, it may be beneficial to consider strategies for appropriately increasing muscle mass. However, we still need to conduct further studies to analyze the effect of weight gain and loss on these specific populations.

The association between WC and mortality showed a different relationship compared to BMI. A large WC has been proven to be a risk factor for all-cause and CVD mortality in a Mendelian randomization study [15]. A large WC means excessive fat around the abdomen and is associated with adverse metabolic characteristics, which lead to a higher cardiometabolic risk [45]. Thus, it is plausible to conclude that overweight or obese people with a normal WC have lower overall and CVD mortality. This is of great significance for clinical and public health practices. When providing advice on weight management, both BMI and WC should be taken into account concurrently. The combination of a relatively high BMI and a normal-range WC might constitute the best fit for reducing the risk of death.

In this cohort study with a large sample size, we collected detailed information on the history of diseases and the outcome of death through multi-database linkage, which may improve the data integrity and accuracy. We assessed the relationships between BMI and WC and all-cause and cause-specific mortality, and conducted sensitivity analyses to analyze the robustness of the results. People with severe diseases such as cancer often experience unintentional weight loss (the possibility of transforming from overweight or obese to normal weight or underweight), and these individuals have a high probability of short-term mortality. This scenario may lead to the misclassification of baseline BMI categories and reverse causation [46]. Therefore, in the sensitivity analysis, we excluded participants with a history of CVD or cancer at baseline, as well as those who died within the first year of follow-up. This exclusion strategy minimized the interference of disease-induced changes in weight and WC on the study results, thereby preserving the correct causal direction (from BMI or WC to mortality). The sensitivity analysis showed little difference in the estimated associations between BMI and WC and mortality. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first cohort study in the suburbs of Shanghai to examine BMI, WC, and their joint effect on the risk of mortality. Discussing BMI or WC separately has its own limitations, and joint analyses can help us understand what the optimal body figure is to reduce the risk of death. However, this study has several limitations. First, height and weight were measured only once at baseline, and weight indicators are easily affected by lifestyle and disease. The Framingham Heart Study using the maximum BMI during the weight history period may help mitigate reverse causality [6]. In the future, research could focus on the association between the highest or long-term changes in BMI and WC and mortality. Second, apart from BMI and WC, in previous studies, the waist-to-height ratio and waist-to-hip ratio were also shown to be good indicators for assessing obesity, and abnormal values in these ratios were associated with adverse health outcomes [47–49]. Further studies should consider the effect of these composite indices on mortality risk. Third, we could not analyze all causes of death (deaths from respiratory disease, digestive disease, etc.) comprehensively due to the short follow-up time. Future studies are needed to conduct long-term follow-up to study the association between BMI and WC and more cause-specific mortality, and to develop more rational strategies for controlling BMI and WC to reduce the risk of mortality. Fourth, participants were exclusively recruited from Songjiang District, Shanghai. Consequently, while the generalizability of these findings to the broader Chinese population may be constrained, they offer valuable insights for suburban populations within Shanghai.

Conclusions

A low or extremely high BMI and a high WC were associated with an increased risk of mortality, while overweight or grade 1 obesity was negatively related to all-cause and CVD mortality. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring weight and WC status. To improve health and reduce the risk of mortality, underweight and extremely high BMI should be avoided. Additionally, weight management requires comprehensive consideration of both the BMI index and the WC. The optimal BMI value can be appropriately increased, while the WC should be maintained within the normal range, especially in the elderly population.
