Comparison of the outcomes of cephalo-caudal to transverse uterine expansion in primary caesarean section: a study protocol for randomized controlled trial
Authors: Suying Miao, Yanmei Zheng, Zixing Zhong, Suxiao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCaesarean delivery (CD) is the most common surgical procedure, accounting for nearly forty million childbirths annually. The technique of uterine incision expansion-blunt vertical versus transverse-significantly impacts intra-operative blood loss. This randomized controlled trial (RCT) aims to compare the two techniques to provide evidence-based guidance.MethodsThis single-center, double-blind RCT includes healthy pregnant women (aged 18–40 years, non-anemic) undergoing primary CD at Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital. Participants are stratified by gestational age (≥ 37 + 0 vs. ≤36 + 6 weeks) and maternal age (≥ 35 vs. <35 years), then randomized 1:1 to cephalo-caudal (vertical) or transverse expansion. The primary outcome will be intra-operative blood loss, evaluated by two surgeons independently during the surgical procedure and further validated through difference between pre-operative and post-operative hemoglobin levels. Secondary outcomes will include lower uterine segment lacerations, additional sutures needed, and uterine vascular injury. The long-term outcome is the uterine niche formation via ultrasound one-year after childbirth. The minimal sample size will be 50 participants in each group to achieve 80% power (α = 0.05). Statistical analysis will be performed via SPSS (version 26.0) or GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). Ethical approval (No. QT2022315) was obtained from Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital Ethics Board (January 17th, 2025).DiscussionThis RCT will compare the novel vertical and conventional horizontal uterine expansion techniques during CD. It will be stratified by maternal age, gestational age and only one surgeon to operate to reduce heterogeneity. The outcomes will include subjective and objective assessment. Despite a relatively small sample size, the study will generate evidence from a nation ranking the top in CD rates to address gaps in CD technique standardization.ConclusionBy comparing vertical and transverse uterine expansion, findings will directly inform strategies to minimize surgical blood loss and associated complications.Trial registrationChiCTR2200065203. Background

Caesarean delivery (CD) is the most common surgical procedure performed worldwide to deliver a fetus when vaginal birth is considered too risky for either the mother or the baby. It accounts for about one fifths of all births globally in the recent decade [1]. This procedure is associated with both maternal and neonatal risks, including surgical infection, intra-operative or postpartum hemorrhage, and longer hospital stay and recovery times compared to vaginal delivery. In the long-term, it may predispose women to uterine niche or higher risk in subsequent pregnancies. Therefore, there has always been novel techniques to optimize the surgical procedure and minimize short-and long-term risks. The technique of uterine incision and the manually blunt uterine expansion is one of the key steps in the surgery, which can influence both maternal and fetal outcomes.

The blunt uterine expansion following scalp incision during caesarean delivery is usually conducted by bimanual horizontal traction, or in a relatively new approach, the cephalocaudal (head-to-tail) expansion [2]. The main difference is the direction of the extension of the lower segment incision [2, 3]. The risk of surgical complications also varies. The choice between these two methods has been debated in clinical practice. Current clinical studies have shown that cephalon-caudal extension may be beneficial for certain obstetric indications, such as in cases of high fetal station or in cases requiring a larger incision to accommodate the fetus. However, the transverse uterine extension is still predominantly preferred among most obstetricians [4].

A few existing randomized controlled trial (RCT) studies had compared the two approaches, the results were conflicting across different studies [2, 3, 5–10]. Cromi et al. suggested that cephalon-caudal extension may be associated with insignificantly more blood loss and longer operative times, while others’ studies indicate no significant difference in maternal or fetal outcomes [2, 8]. Sestito et al. and Inlo et al. reported that horizontal expansions were associated with fewer post-operative complications, though the evidence is inconsistent across studies [2, 3, 7]. Furthermore, previous studies were limited by variations in study design, sample sizes, ethnic diversity, and clinical settings, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions, especially in application to East Asian women [2, 3, 5–10].

The aim of this RCT is to compare the intra-and post-operative outcomes of cephalon-caudal versus transverse uterine extension in caesarean birth. The novel aspect of this study lies in its rigorous design with stratified randomization and integrated evaluation of blood loss, and long-term follow-up. This trial will provide practical insights for obstetricians and surgeons in selecting the most appropriate surgical approach in caesarean delivery.

Methods

Study design

This study is an investigator initiated, single-centre, double-blind, randomized controlled trial (RCT), designed to compare the outcomes of two different techniques for the blunt expansion of the lower uterine segment incision during caesarean delivery. This trial will be conducted at the Department of Obstetrics, Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital, Hangzhou, China.

Participants and eligibility criteria

The study population will consist of healthy pregnant women aged 18–40 years who are scheduled for primary elective or emergency caesarean delivery. The inclusion criteria are: (1) women carrying singleton pregnancy at (34+ 0 gestational weeks or more) undergoing primary caesarean section; (2) maternal haemoglobin level of 11 g/dL or above before surgery. The exclusion criteria include: (1) a history of uterine surgery, such as myomectomy; (2) severe maternal comorbidities such as heart disease (e.g., heart failure), thrombocytopenia (Platelet count less than 100*109/L), bleeding disorders (e.g., haemophilia A), thrombophilia (e.g., protein C deficiency), or obstetric complications like fetal demise. The enrollment is anticipated to start in February, 2025 and end in June, 2027.

Randomization

Participants will be randomly assigned to either the intervention group (cephalo-caudal, or vertical blunt expansion) or the control group (transverse blunt expansion) in a 1:1 ratio using a double-blind, stratified randomisation method. Stratification will be based on gestational age (36+ 6 weeks or less, or more than 37+ 0 weeks) and maternal age at expected date of delivery (younger than 35 years, or 35 years or older). An independent coordinator will apply the Excel software (version 2402) to generate the randomisation sequence to ensure participants were stratified and evenly allocated to either group in a 1:1 ratio. The coordinator will inform the operator of the specific intervention 30 min before surgery. The double-blind was defined as that the participants will be blinded for specific surgical technique, while the data analyser will also be blinded during data analysis. The operator will have to know who will undergo which specific surgical intervention.

Surgical intervention

All surgeries will be performed by one operator, who was also the correspondence author. He has been working in obstetrics for more than a decade, and is a fellow of European Board & College of Obstetrics and Gynaecology (EBCOG). The intervention group will undergo caesarean delivery with the cephalo-caudad blunt expansion of the lower uterine segment incision, while the control group will have the traditional transverse expansion (details can be seen in the Supplementary video). The remaining surgical procedures will be evidence-based: (1) skin incision will be Pfannenstiel for most cases and mid-line vertical incision for some cases depending on the maternal request and the surgeon’s preference; (2) blunt dissection of subcutaneous tissue; (3) a 2 cm sharp transverse incision on the lower uterine segment before bimanual extension; (4) two-layer suture for the lower segment; (5) continuous suture for peritoneum and rectal sheath; (6) intermittent suture for subcutaneous tissue if its thickness more than 2 cm; (7) continuous suture to close skin. Both groups will receive standard obstetric care before and after the surgery, with the only difference lying in the technique used to expand the uterine incision.

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes will include intra-operative blood loss, which was independently evaluated by the two surgeons (the chief surgeon and his assistant) involved in the operation to avoid subjective assessment. The blood loss will be further validated by comparing the haemoglobin levels before and after surgery (on Day 1 and Day 2). Secondary outcomes will comprise the lower uterine segment laceration, additional haemostatic sutures, and uterine vascular injury. A long-term outcome will involve the uterine niche formation by ultrasound one-year after caesarean delivery. The diagnosis of uterine niche is defined as a defect in the uterine wall with a depth of at least 2 mm after C-section on ultrasound, along with spotting, prolonged menstruation, dysmenorrhoea, etc.

Data collection and management

Data will be entered immediately after surgery, and managed using Excel software with patients’ personal information removed, retaining only case numbers for future verification. Data will include demographic information, surgical details, and outcomes. The details can be seen in Table 1.

Safety monitoring

An adverse event is defined as significant post-operative bleeding requesting an unexpected re-operation. Such serious event will uncover blinding. It will be monitored in real-time, with a rapid response for identification, recording, and reporting. An emergency plan, including risk management, and referral procedures, has been established. The study, being a modification of a step in caesarean delivery, is not expected to significantly increase adverse events according to previous RCTs and the operator’s clinical experience; however, any other events such as fetal injury during uterine incision will be reported as per standard obstetric adverse event protocols.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined based on Ozcan et al. study, with the primary outcome being intraoperative blood loss. Setting α at 0.05 and power at 0.8, at least 50 cases per group are required to detect a statistically significant difference [10].

Patient and public partnership

Patients were actively encouraged to participant in the development of the protocol. After completion of the clinical trial, a lay summary of the results will be disseminated to all pregnant women to inform them on the up-to-date evidence of caesarean delivery.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables will be presented as mean ± SD, and group comparisons will be made using Student’s T-test for normally distributed data and Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables will be analysed using Chi-square tests and Fisher’s exact tests where appropriate. A p-value of less than 0.05 will be considered statistically significant. Data analysis will be conducted by an independent investigator, who is blinded to the intervention allocation. All statistical analysis will be conducted on SPSS software (version 26.0) or GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0).

Discussion

As the global caesarean section (CS) rate has steadily increased for decades, and is anticipated to continue increase in the near future, coming up with the methods to minimize surgical complications and novel approaches to optimize the surgery will always be hot topics [1]. Caesarean delivery is significantly raised in some developing countries, which are more vulnerable to managing surgery-related infections, bleeding and potentially life-threatening complications [1]. Therefore, any efforts aiming at reduce surgical complications or improve neonatal, maternal well-being will be of essential importance. This will be particularly useful if the clinical trial is conducted in developing countries. As many well-designed RCT studies were conducted in developed countries, which could conclude with perfect results. However, the sufficient resources of the healthcare system from developed countries may preclude its conclusion to be extrapolated to developing world. Therefore, the low-income countries, which accounted almost four fifths caesarean births worldwide, urgently need practical evidence from countries with similar healthcare resources. As China remains a developing country, this is the most important clinical value of this RCT study.

Regarding surgical skill in CS, extreme caution should be paid to the process before delivery. The conventional transverse expansion at the lower uterine segment was usually wide enough to allow the smooth delivery of the fetus [2]. When the operator improperly applied his or her finger force, an excessively wide blunt dissection could be made. It can lead to major blood loss, or injuries to uterine vessels. This is particularly the case among less experienced obstetricians [2]. In contrast, when vertically pull the lower segment, the bilateral force was indirectly related to the operator’s exerting force. The expansion can be controlled, causing less extension or vessel injuries in theory.

In this randomized trial, we proposed a several novel aspects. Firstly, we designed the stratified randomisation to preclude the confounders of maternal age and gestational age. Generally, maternal age more than 35yrs can predispose women to more complications in pregnancy, including postpartum haemorrhage. To ensure participants less than 35yrs and more than 35yrs are divided in allocation best avoid the bias. Similarly, pregnant women underwent preterm caesarean delivery often had different surgical indications in comparison to full term caesarean. Those preterm indications, such as preterm premature rupture of membrane (PPROM) may cause more risk in developing intra-operative bleeding. Secondly, we had only operator to perform all surgeries. This may better avoid personal differences in surgery. We initially proposed four to five obstetricians to attend the RCT. However, before the recruitment, the independent coordinator observed the surgeries and concluded that the skills were very different across the surgical candidates, especially in assessing blood loss and extra sutures to the lower uterine segment. This may be owing to different educational and training backgrounds. Therefore, only one obstetrician to perform the surgery can decrease the heterogeneity of outcomes between cases. Thirdly, for the primary outcome, we integrated both subjective (blood loss evaluated by the operator intra-operatively) and objective (the decrease of haemoglobin post-operatively) measures to provide a more comprehensive assessment. In addition, we also designed a long-term follow-up outcome. This was the first in relative caesarean RCTs. Caesarean niche is gaining awareness and attention, the causes remain elusive. There were arguments that the surgical skills used in caesarean delivery may cause the niche. By far, the transverse expansion has been mostly used, and this may cause the distortion and anatomical widening of the lower segment. This may contribute to the pathological changes to the caesarean niche.

However, there were a few limitations to this clinical study. First, the sample size is relatively smaller than the current studies. Despite having met the calculated sample size, a larger study population may offer more clinical information. Second, we did not include patients with repeated caesarean section. We held that significant heterogeneity would occur in women with previous caesarean deliveries, such as different obstetric indications, different surgical skills or preferences (one-layer suture or two-layer suture for the lower segment), various inter-pregnancy intervals, and recovery. All factors mentioned may contribute to the outcomes of the RCT.
