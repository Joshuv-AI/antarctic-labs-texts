Spinal granulocytic sarcoma with both extradural and intradural involvement: a case report
Authors: Jian Zhang, Bao-Guo Han, Yan-Hao Ge, Ting-Chen Zhu, Ya-Feng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionGranulocytic sarcoma (GS) is a rare extramedullary accumulation of myeloid blasts. Cases of GS with isolated intradural presentation sparing the underlying bone are extremely rare.Case descriptionA 75-year-old male patient with a remote history of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in 30-year remission presented with progressive lumbar radiculopathy. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) revealed intradural lesions spanning L2–S1, mimicking nerve sheath tumors. The patient underwent emergency laminectomy and resection of a vascular, grayish-green L2 mass adherent to the cauda equina. Immunohistochemistry confirmed it was a GS (MPO+, CD43+, CD68+) with no evidence of bone marrow relapse. Following surgery and consolidation chemotherapy, the patient’s neurological status improved (ASIA C to D). However, he developed disseminated CNS relapse four months later and succumbed to disease progression 7 months postoperatively.ConclusionIsolated intradural GS constitutes a rare manifestation of extramedullary AML relapse and frequently masquerades radiologically as common benign nerve sheath tumors. While urgent surgical decompression facilitates significant neurological recovery, long-term survival is strictly dictated by the efficacy of systemic disease control. Therefore, adopting a multidisciplinary strategy integrating intraoperative frozen-section analysis, rigorous immunohistochemical profiling, and early adjuvant chemotherapy is paramount for avoiding diagnostic delays and optimizing patient outcomes.

Introduction

Granulocytic sarcoma (GS), also designated as myeloid sarcoma, is an uncommon extramedullary tumor composed of immature granulocytes or myeloid blasts. Although frequently concomitant with systemic hematologic malignancies or myeloproliferative neoplasms, isolated spinal cord involvement remains an exceedingly rare clinical entity. Such rarity presents substantial challenges regarding timely diagnosis and the implementation of optimal therapeutic strategies (1).

Case description

A 75-year-old male patient presented with a 1-month history of progressive lower back pain, accompanied by radiculopathy, paresthesia, and functional impairment of the left lower extremity. The patient reported that the pain radiated from the left gluteal region to the anterior thigh and calf, with associated numbness and subjective weakness. His medical history was notable for acute myeloblastic leukemia (AML) 30 years ago, which achieved remission after chemotherapy. He had no smoking, drinking, or radiation exposure history, and no family history of malignancy. Physical examination revealed tenderness in the left gluteal and inguinal regions. The patient’s vital signs were stable, and no significant abnormalities were detected on cardiopulmonary or abdominal examination. Orthopedic and neurological assessment revealed an antalgic gait; spinal inspection showed no obvious scoliosis or kyphosis with preserved lumbar lordosis. Tenderness was elicited at the L4-L5 and L5-S1 interspinous and paravertebral regions, more pronounced on the left, and localized deep tenderness over the left gluteal and groin areas provoked radiating pain to the anterior thigh and lower leg. On neurological examination, sensory testing demonstrated markedly diminished pinprick and light touch in the left L2, L3, and L4 dermatomes compared to the contralateral side, while L5 and S1 sensation was relatively preserved. Motor examination revealed left-sided weakness: iliopsoas 4/5, quadriceps femoris 4+/5, with other muscle groups 5/5 bilaterally. Reflexes showed significantly diminished left patellar and Achilles tendon reflexes; the left Babinski sign was negative and no ankle clonus was observed. The straight leg raise test was negative bilaterally, but the femoral nerve stretch test was positive on the left, reproducing anterior thigh pain. Distal blood perfusion and trophic status of the bilateral lower extremities were unremarkable. Sensation was decreased in the corresponding dermatomes. Laboratory tests revealed elevated hs-CRP (12.38 mg/L) and mildly reduced albumin (34.6 g/L). Renal function was mildly impaired (eGFR 61.7 mL/min/1.73m²) with elevated uric acid (464 μmol/L), urea (10.2 mmol/L), and cystatin C (1.46 mg/L). Peripheral blood morphology showed no blast cells, and tumor markers were within normal limits. Lumbar spine MRI (Figure 1) demonstrated multiple well-defined intraspinal extramedullary masses from L2 to S1 levels, along with L1-S1 intervertebral disc deformation and bulging. Based on clinical and imaging findings, the patient was diagnosed with symptomatic intraspinal occupying lesions at multiple levels causing nerve root compression. Histopathological confirmation was recommended to rule out hematologic recurrence.

Sagittal T2 magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) show severe spinal cord compression at L2–S1, axial T2 MRI show severe spinal cord compression at L2–S1.

Based on the patient’s clinical signs and imaging findings, the symptoms were attributed to compression caused by an intraspinal space-occupying lesion at the L1-L3 levels. On September 1, 2023, the patient underwent a posterior L1/L2 and L2/L3 laminectomy for decompression, L2 intraspinal tumor resection, and pedicle screw internal fixation under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation.

After induction of anesthesia, the patient was placed in the prone position. Following routine skin preparation and sterile draping, a posterior midline incision of approximately 12 cm was made. The subcutaneous tissue and deep fascia were incised sequentially. Bilateral paraspinal muscles were stripped along both sides of the spinous processes to fully expose the bilateral laminae and facet joints from L1 to L3. The spinous processes of L1 and L2 were resected. Subsequently, using a high-speed drill and laminectomy rongeurs, the laminae of L1 and L2 were resected along the medial base of the bilateral facet joints, and the superior margin of the L3 lamina was partially removed to expand the surgical field. The resected bone fragments were trimmed into fine granules, mixed with a bioactive glass bone graft substitute, and set aside for subsequent bone grafting.

After the ligamentum flavum was resected, the dural sac and bilateral L1/L2 nerve roots were fully exposed. The dural sac at the L2 level was visibly distended and tense. The thickened nerve root sheath dura was incised to enter the intradural space, and the dural edges were retracted bilaterally and secured with stay sutures.

Exploration revealed an oval-shaped intradural tumor measuring approximately 3 × 1 × 1 cm. The tumor was located slightly to the left within the spinal canal, with rich vascularity and tortuous vessels, and was densely adherent to a portion of the cauda equina. The tumor extended from the L1 nerve root into the extradural space.

After carefully dissecting the cauda equina nerves densely adherent to the tumor and achieving meticulous hemostasis, the solid intradural portion was mobilized en bloc as a single tumor. Re-exploration confirmed adequate relaxation of the cauda equina and a watertight dural closure. The medial portion of the left L2 superior articular process was resected. Further exploration revealed a gelatinous tumor attached to the origin of the left L1 nerve root extradurally, representing an extradural extension of the intradural tumor via the L1 nerve root. The gelatinous portion of the tumor was carefully dissected from the adherent nerve root. The completely resected tumor specimen and the gelatinous material were sent for pathological examination to determine the nature of the components in different regions of the lesion.

The surgical field was thoroughly irrigated with normal saline. Exploration confirmed no active bleeding, and the dural sac exhibited good pulsation. The bilateral intertransverse bone surfaces at L1/L2 and L2/L3 were decorticated using a high-speed drill. The previously prepared mixture of fine bone granules and bioactive glass bone graft substitute was implanted. L1 pedicle screws were placed bilaterally, a right L2 pedicle screw was placed unilaterally, and L3 pedicle screws were placed bilaterally, followed by rod installation and fixation. Due to tumor erosion and intraoperative resection of the facet joint resulting in suboptimal conditions for screw placement, the left L2 screw was omitted. Intraoperative C-arm fluoroscopy confirmed satisfactory positioning of the remaining 5 screws and connecting rods, with adequate overall stability of the internal fixation construct.

The incision was irrigated again with normal saline, and a negative pressure drainage tube was placed on the left side. The incision was closed in layers. The procedure was concluded, the wound was dressed, and the patient was fitted with a lumbar brace for external fixation.

The excised tumor, appearing gray-green (Figure 2), was sent for pathological evaluation. Sterile dressings were applied.

An oval, olive-gray, tumor measuring approximately 3 × 1 × 1 cm was removed from the spinal canal during surgery.

By postoperative day 6, the patient was ambulatory with lower extremity strength improved to MRC grade 4/5. Neurological examination revealed hyperactive patellar and Achilles reflexes, but diminished cremasteric and anal reflexes, with a negative Babinski sign. Spinal imaging demonstrated expected postoperative changes (Figure 3).

Postoperative X-rays showed postoperative changes and degenerative changes of the lumbar spine.

The final diagnosis, based on the pathological evaluation, was an intraspinal myeloid tumor consistent with sarcoma or leukemic infiltration (Figure 4). Histological examination showed tumor cells that were positive for CD68, Bcl-2, CD43, and myeloperoxidase (MPO), with partial positivity for Lysozyme and CD117. Scattered S-100 positivity was also observed. Conversely, the cells were negative for lymphoid markers (CD3, CD20, CD79a, PAX-5), plasma cell markers (CD138, CD38), epithelial markers (CK, EMA), and melanocytic markers (HMB45, SOX10). The Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 40%, and EBER in situ hybridization was negative.

Histological report of an intraspinal mass consistent with a myeloid sarcoma or leukemic infiltration. Further blood and bone marrow examination were recommended.

Regarding the postoperative bone marrow aspiration (Figure 5), the findings indicated a hypercellular marrow with a myeloid-to-erythroid ratio of 2.47:1. The analysis revealed a relative increase in the myeloid lineage (60.5%) characterized by a predominance of mature forms, alongside a markedly increased number of megakaryocytes (>100 per smear) with large platelet clusters.

Regrettably, cytogenetic and molecular analyses (including FLT3, NPM1, RUNX1, and KIT) could not be conducted due to institutional resource limitations and the patient’s economic situation. The patient also refused postoperative MRI reevaluation due to financial constraints and a prolonged hospital stay.

On the second postoperative day, the patient reported marked relief of left lower extremity pain and paresthesia. In this case, the patient recovered uneventfully after tumor resection and was immediately transferred to the hematology department of an external hospital for comprehensive treatment, including systemic chemotherapy. A six-month postoperative follow-up was conducted, during which no recurrence of spinal cord compression symptoms was observed. However, MRI surveillance after discharge revealed disease progression, indicating a poor prognosis. The external hospital MRI reporter showed the following findings: Abnormal signal intensities in the right cerebellar hemisphere and the left frontal lobe, suggestive of metastatic lesions; the left frontal lobe lesion was slightly enlarged compared with the October 2023 images, while the right cerebellar lesion remained essentially unchanged. Status post internal fixation at L1–L3 vertebrae, with local intraspinal abnormal signals—differential considerations include metastasis versus artifacts; clinical correlation is advised. An intraspinal space-occupying lesion extending from the inferior margin of L4 to the S2 vertebral level, with thickening and local enhancement of the left nerve root, and osseous destruction at S1, all consistent with metastatic disease and showing slight progression compared with prior studies. Heterogeneous decreased signal intensity on T1-weighted imaging involving the clivus, visualized cervical and lumbar vertebrae, several vertebral bodies, and the iliac bones—clinical correlation and follow-up are recommended. Soft tissue swelling in the lumbar subcutaneous region.

Unfortunately, subsequent follow-up contact with the family ascertained that the patient had passed away in April 2024. A schematic illustrating the patient’s clinical course has been prepared (Figure 5).

Discussion

Myeloid sarcoma is a distinct extramedullary tumor composed of myeloid blasts or immature myeloid cells. Although it was first described by Burns in 1811, the term ‘chloroma’ (from the Greek ‘chloros’ for green) was coined by King in 1853 based on his detailed postmortem investigations; he observed the tumor’s distinctive green color, noted that it faded upon exposure to air, and associated these lesions with underlying leukemia (2). In 1966, Rappaport proposed the term ‘granulocytic sarcoma.’ The current nomenclature, ‘myeloid sarcoma,’ was formally adopted by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2002 (3). In the most recent updates released in 2022 by both the WHO (4) and the International Consensus Classification (ICC) (5), myeloid sarcoma retains its definition as a distinct entity within the spectrum of myeloid neoplasms.

It is worth noting that the alternative name ‘chloroma’ (green tumor) originates from the high expression of myeloperoxidase (MPO) in myeloid sarcoma. This high MPO content causes the cut surface of the tumor to appear green and fade upon exposure to air, hence the name (6).

Clinically, GS can be stratified into four specific contexts (7): 1) isolated or de novo GS, occurring in the absence of bone marrow involvement; 2) concurrent GS, presenting simultaneously with a diagnosis of AML; 3) blast phase GS, acting as a harbinger of leukemic transformation in patients with antecedent myeloproliferative neoplasms, myelodysplastic syndromes, or chronic myeloid leukemia (8); and 4) relapsed GS, appearing as a recurrent extramedullary disease following chemotherapy or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

Previous studies (9, 10) indicate that GS accounts for 2.5%–9.1% of patients with AML. No significant age-specific predisposition has been established, with its distribution relatively balanced across age groups. The incidence of GS is significantly higher in male patients than in females, showing a distinct male predilection. This sex difference may be related to the interaction of multiple factors such as genetics, hormone levels, and immune microenvironment; however, its exact mechanism requires further in-depth research for precise explanation and analysis (11). This will deepen the understanding of the pathogenesis behind this disease’s sex disparity and provide a crucial theoretical basis and practical guidance for optimizing subsequent personalized diagnosis and treatment strategies.

The diagnosis of GS is a persistent dilemma in clinical practice, with reported misdiagnosis rates of 25%–47%. Due to its morphological heterogeneity, GS is prone to being mistaken for other malignancies, including Hodgkin’s and non-Hodgkin’s lymphomas, especially in cases with poorly differentiated blasts. In our review of the literature regarding spinal myeloid sarcoma from the past decade (12–27), we found that spinal cord compression is the predominant initial presentation, mirroring the current case. Clinical symptoms typically include dorsal and lower extremity pain, motor weakness, and autonomic dysfunction (Table 1).

Summary of reported cases of spinal myeloid sarcoma.

The imaging differential diagnosis for intradural extramedullary GS includes both neoplastic and inflammatory conditions. Spinal infections typically involve vertebrae and discs with endplate erosion and paravertebral abscess, unlike isolated intradural GS. Inflammatory demyelinating diseases usually present as intramedullary rather than intradural extramedullary lesions. Sarcoidosis may cause leptomeningeal nodules with systemic findings. Granulomatous lesions often show rim enhancement and central necrosis, while GS is usually well circumscribed (27). When imaging is inconclusive, histopathology with immunohistochemistry is essential for diagnosis. Common investigative modalities for GS

Blood and bone marrow routine examination

Primary GS generally shows no abnormalities in bone marrow or routine blood tests. If tumor invasion causes bleeding or jaundice in organs, related changes may be seen in routine blood tests.

Imaging examination

With the widespread use of Computed Tomography (CT) and MRI, these methods are also widely used in investigating cases of GS. However, imaging findings are non-specific, and no diagnostic imaging criteria have been established. Imaging findings are primarily used to determine the location and size of the tumor.

Histopathological examination

Pathological examination of the mass remains the only definitive diagnostic method. Common sites for extramedullary infiltration include the skull and orbit, as well as the breasts, liver, kidneys, and muscles, among others. Myeloid sarcoma can also occur inside or outside lymph nodes. In GS, due to the high concentration of myeloperoxidase (MPO) within granulocytes, the cut surface of the tumor tends to oxidize in air and appear green; it is also called chloroma or green tumor.

Treatment methods

If isolated myeloid sarcoma is left untreated, 88% of patients will progress to AML within 1 year. The treatment of GS depends primarily on the location of the myeloid sarcoma, whether it is newly diagnosed or recurrent, its clinical presentation, and the patient’s age. Systemic chemotherapy plays a crucial role in the treatment of myeloid sarcoma. Even if there is no bone marrow involvement at onset, myeloid sarcoma should be treated as a systemic disease with systemic chemotherapy.

GS involving the spinal cord typically occurs outside the spinal cord (extradural or intradural extramedullary), presenting as spinal cord compression due to the mass effect; however, a small number of intramedullary cases have also been reported.

GS involving the spinal cord is rare. Without a history of related leukemia, its diagnosis is quite difficult. MRI is an important method for diagnosing this condition, requiring differentiation from lymphoma, metastatic tumors, extramedullary hematopoiesis, and neurogenic tumors.

MRI findings of multiple or consecutive extramedullary masses combined with diffuse abnormal bone marrow signals can help in the preliminary diagnosis of leukemia complicated with spinal GS. GS appears isointense or slightly hyperintense on T2-weighted MRI images and isointense on T1-weighted images.

Due to the rarity of spinal cord-involving GS, there is no unified treatment model. Treatment mostly involves surgical decompression, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, stem cell transplantation, or combinations of these modalities. Most scholars believe that GS requires comprehensive treatment combining chemotherapy with surgical resection or radiotherapy. For isolated spinal GS, the optimal sequence of surgical resection and radiotherapy remains debated. Surgical decompression is prioritized for acute neurological deficits, as in our case, to preserve function. However, early initiation of systemic chemotherapy is paramount to eradicate micrometastatic disease and prevent systemic relapse or progression to AML. Surgical intervention is primarily indicated for patients with acute spinal cord compression and neurological symptoms. Microsurgical resection of the tumor generally leads to significant improvement in neurological symptoms and enhances the patient’s quality of life.

The prognosis of spinal GS is generally guarded and hinges on several factors. Favorable predictors include isolated de novo presentation, achievement of complete surgical resection when feasible, and prompt, effective systemic therapy leading to sustained remission. As illustrated by the present case, even with successful initial surgical and chemotherapeutic intervention, the risk of disseminated CNS relapse remains significant, highlighting the aggressive nature of this disease.

Patient perspective

The patient reported that the pain and numbness in his left leg improved after surgery. This symptomatic relief contributed to a perceived enhancement in his postoperative quality of life.

Limitation

In this case, we diagnosed the patient with spinal granulocytic sarcoma based on preoperative imaging, the patient’s medical history, intraoperative findings, and postoperative pathological examination. However, due to the limitations of our hospital’s resources and the patient’s economic situation., we were unable to perform cytogenetic examination, which precluded a precise diagnosis. This constitutes a shortcoming of the present case report.

Conclusion

This case offers critical insights into the management of spinal myeloid sarcoma, underscoring the inherent diagnostic complexity and therapeutic challenges of this rare entity. By detailing the trajectory from initial presentation and radiographic ambiguity to definitive histopathological confirmation, this report highlights the importance of a multidisciplinary approach. Such collaboration is essential to minimize diagnostic latency, prevent misidentification, and ensure the timely initiation of precise, systemic therapy.

The surgical intervention performed at our hospital effectively alleviated the symptoms of spinal cord compression, underscoring the critical role of surgery in acute neurological management. However, despite the initial benefit, the patient’s eventual poor prognosis, notwithstanding the unavailable details of his subsequent care at an outside institution, highlights the aggressive nature of spinal myeloid sarcoma and the significant limitations of current therapeutic modalities.

Given the rarity of myeloid sarcoma and the absence of a standardized therapeutic consensus, large-scale, multicenter collaborative studies are imperative. Such efforts are essential to evaluate the efficacy and safety of diverse treatment modalities, elucidate underlying pathological mechanisms, and establish precise, personalized therapeutic algorithms. Ultimately, bridging current therapeutic gaps through rigorous research is paramount to optimizing clinical management and improving survival outcomes for this challenging patient population.
