Understanding LLM Failures: A Multi-Tape Turing Machine Analysis of Systematic Errors in Language Model Reasoning
Magnus Boman

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) exhibit failure modes on seemingly trivial tasks. We propose a formalisation of LLM interaction using a deterministic multi-tape Turing machine, where each tape represents a distinct component: input characters, tokens, vocabulary, model parameters, activations, probability distributions, and output text. The model enables precise localisation of failure modes to specific pipeline stages, revealing, e.g., how tokenisation obscures character-level structure needed for counting tasks. The model clarifies why techniques like chain-of-thought prompting help, by externalising computation on the output tape, while also revealing their fundamental limitations. This approach provides a rigorous, falsifiable alternative to geometric metaphors and complements empirical scaling laws with principled error analysis.



1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are often described as opaque systems that map input prompts to natural language outputs.
Yet their operation can be decomposed into a sequence of discrete stages: tokenisation, probabilistic generation, and detokenisation.
By making this pipeline explicit, we can situate LLM behaviour within classical models of computation
(
11
)
.
We present a formalisation of the LLM interaction cycle using a multi-tape deterministic Turing machine (TM).
Each tape is assigned a specific role: handling character input, token sequences, vocabularies, network parameters, intermediate activations, probability distributions, and natural language output.
This construction yields a transparent representation of how prompts are transformed into answers.
Beyond offering clarity, the TM model enables one to analyse the limitations of LLMs in terms familiar from recursion theory
(
16
)
.
In our setting, such tools illuminate which aspects of LLM knowledge are computably representable, and which are inherently constrained by formal limits.
While empirical research on LLMs has emphasised scaling laws
(
12
;
10
)
, these approaches provide phenomenological guidance but do not establish which problems are solvable in principle.
To illustrate in concrete terms, consider two cases trivial for humans but challenging for LLMs.
The first is to count how many times the letter “r” occurs in “Strawberry”.
Despite its simplicity, LLMs frequently fail because tokenisation obscures character-level structure and no explicit routine exists for iterative counting
(
7
)
.
The second concerns recursive sentence embedding
(
1
)
, where models often struggle to recognise or generate correctly nested structures.
A common mitigation strategy is chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting
(
21
;
13
)
, which encourages the model to output intermediate reasoning steps in natural language.
Within our multi-tape model, this amounts to writing auxiliary traces on a separate TM tape as an external scratchpad.
The main contributions are that we:

1.

introduce a multi-tape deterministic TM formalisation of LLM inference,
assigning distinct roles to tapes;

2.

demonstrate how this model explains well-known error modes,
by localising failures to specific stages of the pipeline (e.g. tokenisation vs. forward computation);

3.

highlight which tasks are computably trivial yet inaccessible without explicit subroutines, clarifying the formal limits of heuristic strategies;

4.

reinterpret CoT prompting as an externalisation of intermediate computation on the output tape, providing both explanatory clarity and a way to compare its strengths and limitations.

5.

demonstrate how attention mechanisms fit within the multi-tape formalisation through formal tape operations, enabling precise analysis.

2
Method

We define a multi-tape Turing machine in the standard way, with a read head that moves left (L) or right (R), as

M=\langle Q,\{\Gamma_{j}\},\{\square_{j}\},\delta,q_{0},q_{\mathrm{halt}}\rangle,

where
Q
is the finite set of control states,
\Gamma_{j}
are the tape alphabets,
\square_{j}
are the blank symbols,
\delta
is the transition function,
q_{0}
is the initial state, and
q_{\mathrm{halt}}
is the halting state.

Let
\Sigma_{\mathrm{char}}
be the byte/character alphabet (e.g., UTF-8 bytes) and
let
\Sigma_{\mathrm{tok}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be token IDs; on tapes we encode IDs in binary with delimiter #.
Let
R=\{0,1\}^{\infty}
be an infinite random-bit stream for sampling.
Define the tokeniser
\tau:\Sigma_{\mathrm{char}}^{*}\rightarrow\Sigma_{\mathrm{tok}}^{*}
and the model

f:\Sigma_{\mathrm{tok}}^{*}\rightarrow\Delta(\Sigma_{\mathrm{tok}})
mapping a prefix to a probability distribution over next tokens
(the deterministic variant
f_{\max}
returns the argmax token).

2.1
Tape Functions

We start by defining seven tapes, one head per tape:

•

Tape1
(Chars-in): raw prompt
x\in\Sigma_{\mathrm{char}}^{*}
.

•

Tape2
(Tokens): token sequence
\tau(x)
followed by generated tokens appended during inference (stored as integer IDs, not embeddings).

•

Tape3
(Tokeniser + Vocab; R/O): BPE merge rules (priority-ordered pairs), vocabulary with frequency statistics, subword regularisation parameters, and inverse vocabulary mappings. For BPE, tokenisation proceeds by iteratively applying merge rules according to learned priorities until no more merges are possible.

•

Tape4
(Model spec; R/O): architecture metadata and parameters (finite-precision rationals with quantisation bounds).

•

Tape5
(Work): intermediate activations, including the key/value (KV) cache from previous positions, and temporary scratch values used by attention and feed-forward computation. During autoregressive generation, this includes cached key/value pairs from previous positions to avoid recomputation.

•

Tape6
(Logits/Probs): buffer for scores or probabilities of next tokens.

•

Tape7
(Chars-out): detokenised character output. Characters are written here after token-to-character conversion using the inverse vocabulary from
Tape3
.

2.2
Operational Phases

This abstract machine operates in phases, like a transformer
(
20
)
:

1.

Tokenisation
(
Tape1
+
Tape3
\to
Tape2
): segment input characters using vocabulary and merge rules from
Tape3
to produce token IDs on
Tape2
.

2.

Forward computation
(
Tape2
+
Tape3
+
Tape4
+
Tape5
\to
Tape6
): convert token IDs from
Tape2
to embeddings using vocabulary from
Tape3
, then compute attention and feed-forward operations using model parameters from
Tape4
and workspace on
Tape5
, writing probability distributions to
Tape6
.

3.

Detokenisation
(
Tape2
+
Tape3
\to
Tape7
): convert token IDs to characters using inverse vocabulary from
Tape3
and append to
Tape7
.

In the forward computation phase, note that attention operates over a bounded window of recent tokens on
Tape2
, not the entire history.
Halting occurs when
<eos>
is emitted.

Our focus on greedy argmax token selection is intended for theoretical clarity, though real LLMs typically use probabilistic sampling (temperature, top-p, etc.). This deterministic simplification enables precise error analysis while capturing the core computational pipeline.

2.3
Formal Transition Function

In the vectors from Eqs. (8)–(17), presented in the Appendix,
L/R/S
denote left/right/stay for each tape head. The shown movements are representative; many equivalent implementations exist (e.g., Eq. (3) uses all-
S
to switch phase without repositioning heads).
The complete specification requires approximately
|Q|\times\prod_{j=1}^{7}|\Gamma_{j}|
transition rules, but the structure follows the phase-based pattern above.
The state space
Q
is partitioned into disjoint subsets governing different phases of execution:

•

Q_{\mathrm{tok}}=\{q_{\mathrm{tok,1}},q_{\mathrm{tok,2}},\ldots,q_{\mathrm{tok,final}}\}
: tokenisation states that read characters from
Tape1
, consult vocabulary on
Tape3
, and write token IDs to
Tape2
.

•

Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}=\{q_{\mathrm{fwd,1}},\ldots,q_{\mathrm{fwd,n}},q_{\mathrm{attn,weight}},q_{\mathrm{attn,concat}}\}
: forward computation states that read token sequences from
Tape2
, use parameters from
Tape4
and workspace on
Tape5
to compute attention and feed-forward operations, writing probability distributions to
Tape6
.

•

Q_{\mathrm{select}}=\{q_{\mathrm{select,scan}},q_{\mathrm{select,max}}\}
: selection states that scan
Tape6
to identify the maximum probability token via greedy argmax.

•

Q_{\mathrm{emit}}=\{q_{\mathrm{emit}}\}
: emission state that appends the selected token to
Tape2
and transitions to halt if the token is
<eos>
.

•

Q_{\mathrm{detok}}=\{q_{\mathrm{detok}}\}
: detokenisation state that converts token IDs to characters using
Tape3
and appends to
Tape7
before returning to forward computation.

The complete state space satisfies
Q=Q_{\mathrm{tok}}\cup Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}\cup Q_{\mathrm{select}}\cup Q_{\mathrm{emit}}\cup Q_{\mathrm{detok}}\cup\{q_{0},q_{\mathrm{halt}}\}
with all subsets disjoint, and so meets the criterion for a partition.
We distinguish control states from symbols:
q_{0}
denotes the machine’s initial control state (often interpreted as “post-BOS”), and
q_{\text{halt}}
the halting state. By contrast,
<bos>
and
<eos>
are tokens processed on the tapes. In our construction, emission of
<eos>
on Tape2 triggers the transition to
q_{\text{halt}}
, but the two notions remain distinct.

To model stochastic decoding, we could introduce a randomness tape
R=\{0,1\}^{\infty}
and replace selection in
Q_{\text{select}}
by sampling from the distribution on
Tape6
. To model beam search with beam width
B
, we maintain per-beam token tapes
\mathrm{\texttt{Tape2}}^{(b)}
, probability tapes
\mathrm{\texttt{Tape6}}^{(b)}
, and a scoreboard tape storing cumulative log-probabilities. The selection subroutine expands all beams, writes candidates to temporary regions, and prunes to the top
B
before emission. This extends
Q_{\text{select}}
with per-beam scan/max states and a prune state, leaving the rest of the pipeline unchanged.

3
Results

3.1
The Strawberry Counting Example

The canonical counting prompt

“How many times does the letter ‘r’ appear in ‘Strawberry’?”

demonstrates how exact letter counting becomes systematically unreliable in token-based models due to subword segmentation obscuring character boundaries.
We assume greedy decoding and consider two tokenisation regimes:

(A) Whole-word token:

Strawberry
maps to a single token
t_{\mathrm{strawberry}}
.

(B) Subword tokens:

Strawberry
segments as
Str

\mapsto t_{\mathrm{Str}}
,
aw

\mapsto t_{\mathrm{aw}}
,
berry

\mapsto t_{\mathrm{berry}}
.

Phase 1: Tokenisation (
Tape1
\to
Tape2
).

Starting configuration:
\langle q_{0},
“How many times does the letter ‘r’ appear in ‘Strawberry’?”,
\varepsilon,V,W,\varepsilon,\varepsilon,\varepsilon\rangle

Tokenisation states
q_{\mathrm{tok,i}}\in Q_{\mathrm{tok}}
process the input using vocabulary
V
on
Tape3
, writing token sequences to
Tape2
:

(A)

\langle\ldots,t_{\mathrm{strawberry}},\ldots\rangle
(single token obscures all internal characters)

(B)

\langle\ldots,t_{\mathrm{Str}},t_{\mathrm{aw}},t_{\mathrm{berry}},\ldots\rangle
(partial character visibility)

Phase 2: Forward Computation (
Tape4
+
Tape5
\to
Tape6
).

States
q_{\mathrm{fwd,i}}\in Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
compute neural network operations using parameters from
Tape4
. The critical limitation emerges here:

Single-token regime (A):
With
Strawberry
as token
t_{\mathrm{strawberry}}
, no character-level iteration exists within that token.
Tape5
accumulates activations for the token ID, but unless explicit character-counting subroutines are implemented in
Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
, no operation traverses the internal character structure.

Subword regime (B):
Tokens
\langle t_{\mathrm{Str}},t_{\mathrm{aw}},t_{\mathrm{berry}}\rangle
provide partial signals (“r” visible in “Str”, two “r”s in “berry”), but characters within subword pieces remain opaque to standard forward computation.

Phase 3: Token Selection (
Tape6
\to
Tape2
).

States
q_{\mathrm{select,i}}\in Q_{\mathrm{select}}
scan probability distributions on
Tape6
and select via greedy argmax. Without explicit counting, this yields heuristic estimates based on training patterns rather than verified counts.

Phase 4: Detokenisation (
Tape2
\to
Tape7
).

State
q_{\mathrm{detok}}\in Q_{\mathrm{detok}}
converts token IDs back to characters using
Tape3
and appends to
Tape7
.

To solve the task exactly, augment
Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
with character-counting substates:

1.

q_{\mathrm{locate}}\in Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
: identify token span for “Strawberry” on
Tape2

2.

q_{\mathrm{detok,internal}}\in Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
: use
Tape3
to map tokens back to character sequence on
Tape5

3.

q_{\mathrm{count}}\in Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
: iterate through characters on
Tape5
, incrementing counter for each “r”

4.

q_{\mathrm{emit,count}}\in Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
: convert final count to token ID and proceed to selection

This extension preserves computational equivalence (multi-tape TMs remain Turing-equivalent) whilst changing the algorithm from pattern-matching to explicit symbolic computation.

3.2
Attention Computation

The multi-head attention mechanism can be formally represented within our
model without requiring additional tapes. We specify how attention operations map onto existing tape operations during the forward computation phase.

For each attention head
h\in\{1,\ldots,H\}
at layer
\ell
and position
t
, the forward computation states
Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
include attention substates that perform the following operations:

\displaystyle q_{t,\ell,h}

\displaystyle=W^{Q}_{\ell,h}\cdot\text{embed}(s_{2}^{(t)})\quad\text{(computed on {Tape5})}

(1)

\displaystyle K_{\leq t,\ell,h}

\displaystyle=W^{K}_{\ell,h}\cdot\text{embed}(s_{2}^{(\leq t)})\quad\text{(stored on {Tape5})}

(2)

\displaystyle V_{\leq t,\ell,h}

\displaystyle=W^{V}_{\ell,h}\cdot\text{embed}(s_{2}^{(\leq t)})\quad\text{(stored on {Tape5})}

(3)

where
s_{2}^{(t)}
denotes the token ID at position
t
on
Tape2
, and
\text{embed}(\cdot)
converts token IDs to vectors using embedding matrices stored on
Tape4
.

The attention weights are computed via states
q_{\mathrm{attn,weight}}\in Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{t,\ell,h,i}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{softmax}_{i}\left(\frac{q_{t,\ell,h}\cdot K_{i,\ell,h}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)\quad\text{for }i\leq t

(4)

\displaystyle o_{t,\ell,h}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\leq t}\alpha_{t,\ell,h,i}V_{i,\ell,h}

(5)

These computations occur entirely on
Tape5
using parameters from
Tape4
, with intermediate results stored on
Tape5
and final head outputs concatenated before writing to
Tape6
. For autoregressive generation, previously computed
K_{\leq t-1,\ell,h}
and
V_{\leq t-1,\ell,h}
values remain on
Tape5
to avoid recomputation, while only the new position
t
requires fresh computation. This caching mechanism is essential for efficient sequential generation but not for single forward passes.

The concatenated multi-head output is computed in states
q_{\mathrm{attn,concat}}\in Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
:

\displaystyle\text{MultiHead}(s_{2}^{(\leq t)})

\displaystyle=\text{Concat}(o_{t,\ell,1},\ldots,o_{t,\ell,H})W^{O}_{\ell}

(6)

where
W^{O}_{\ell}
is the output projection matrix stored on
Tape4
.
This formalisation enables precise identification of attention-related failure modes:

1.

Context window violations
: When
t>L_{\max}
, attention computations on
Tape5
cannot access tokens beyond the maximum context length.

2.

Attention dilution
: For character-level tasks, attention weights
\alpha_{t,\ell,h,i}
may be distributed across token boundaries, preventing precise character-level focus.

3.

Head saturation
: When
\max_{i}\alpha_{t,\ell,h,i}\approx 1
due to numerical precision limits on
Tape5
, attention becomes effectively deterministic.

3.3
Recursive Centre-Embedding Example

The prompt
“Complete the sentence: The cat that the dog that the mouse feared chased…”
demonstrates how transformers struggle with centre-embedded recursive structures that require tracking multiple incomplete dependencies.
We analyse this prompt through our multi-tape model, assuming the sentence should complete as “fled” (the cat fled, after being chased by the dog that was feared by the mouse).

Phase 1: Tokenisation (
Tape1

\to

Tape2
).

Starting configuration:

\langle q_{0},
“Complete the sentence: The cat that the dog that the mouse feared chased…”,
\varepsilon,V,W,\varepsilon,\varepsilon,\varepsilon\rangle

The tokenisation produces a sequence such as:

\langle t_{\mathrm{Complete}},t_{\mathrm{the}},t_{\mathrm{sentence}},t_{:},t_{\mathrm{The}},t_{\mathrm{cat}},t_{\mathrm{that}},t_{\mathrm{the}},t_{\mathrm{dog}},\\
t_{\mathrm{that}},t_{\mathrm{the}},t_{\mathrm{mouse}},t_{\mathrm{feared}},t_{\mathrm{chased}},t_{...}\rangle

Notably, the two instances of “that” map to the same token
t_{\mathrm{that}}
, providing no syntactic distinction between embedding levels.

Phase 2: Forward Computation (
Tape4
+
Tape5

\to

Tape6
).

States
q_{\mathrm{fwd,i}}\in Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
must resolve the dependency structure:

•

Subject 1: “The cat” (incomplete, awaits verb)

•

Embedded clause 1: “that the dog…chased” (modifies “cat”)

•

Subject 2: “the dog” (incomplete, awaits verb)

•

Embedded clause 2: “that the mouse feared” (modifies “dog”)

•

Verb stack: “feared” closes clause 2, “chased” closes clause 1

•

Required: main verb for subject 1

The critical limitation emerges in attention computation on
Tape5
. For each attention head
h
at the final position:

\displaystyle\alpha_{t,\ell,h,i}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{softmax}_{i}\left(\frac{q_{t,\ell,h}\cdot K_{i,\ell,h}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)

(7)

Without explicit stack-like mechanisms in
Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
, attention weights
\alpha_{t,\ell,h,i}
cannot maintain the hierarchical structure. The model observes:

•

Two subjects: “cat” (position 6) and “dog” (position 9)

•

Two verbs: “feared” (position 13) and “chased” (position 14)

•

Ambiguous binding: which subject lacks its main verb?

Phase 3: Token Selection (
Tape6

\to

Tape2
).

Without proper dependency tracking, the probability distribution on
Tape6
reflects learned surface patterns rather than syntactic structure. Common errors include selecting another verb for “mouse” (recency bias), a noun (pattern completion without structure), or punctuation (avoiding the dependency resolution).
The correct completion “fled” requires recognising that “cat” is the only unresolved subject, which standard attention mechanisms struggle to maintain across multiple embeddings.
This failure can be understood through the Chomsky hierarchy
(
11
)
. Centre-embedded structures require context-sensitive grammar (Type 1), while finite-window attention without explicit memory implements at most context-free patterns (Type 2), i.e. the model can only approximate context-free patterns but cannot implement the context-sensitive grammar needed for true centre-embedding. To handle arbitrary embedding depth
n
, the model would need:

1.

q_{\mathrm{push}}\in Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
: states that write opening dependencies to a stack region on
Tape5

2.

q_{\mathrm{pop}}\in Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
: states that match closing elements with stack entries

3.

q_{\mathrm{check}}\in Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
: states that verify all dependencies are resolved

Without these, the model relies on approximate pattern matching. Empirically, transformers handle depth-1 or depth-2 embeddings through memorised patterns but fail systematically at depth-3 or greater, consistent with the absence of true recursive processing in the state structure of
Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
.
The absence of push/pop/check states in standard transformer architectures explains their systematic failure on deeply nested structures.

4
Related Work

Several research programmes have developed formal computational models for neural networks, each with distinct aims and scope.
Early work established that recurrent networks and transformers can simulate universal Turing machines under idealised conditions
(
5
;
4
;
23
)
. These results demonstrate theoretical capacity, but operate at a high level of abstraction and at a lower level of formality than recursion-theoretic treatments. They assume unlimited precision, infinite memory, and perfect parameter optimisation, and so do not provide operational accounts of how inference proceeds in real systems.
The Neural Turing Machine (NTM)
(
9
)
, Differentiable Neural Computer (DNC), and related architectures introduce explicit memory mechanisms inspired by classical computation. However, these models serve a fundamentally different purpose from our work: they propose new architectures with enhanced memory capabilities, rather than analysing the behaviour of existing LLMs. The tapes and heads in NTMs are differentiable components trained end-to-end, whereas our multi-tape construction provides a
post-hoc
analysis for understanding standard transformer inference.
Moreover, these differentiable approaches sacrifice the discrete, symbolic character that makes classical Turing machines analytically tractable.

Others have analysed transformers through complexity-theoretic lenses, examining their expressive power relative to circuit classes and space-bounded computation
(
19
)
. While valuable for understanding fundamental limitations, these approaches typically abstract away the specific pipeline structure (tokenisation, attention, detokenisation) that governs practical LLM behaviour. Our contribution complements such work by providing a mechanistic rather than purely complexity-theoretic account.
Approaches that model neural reasoning through symbolic manipulation and program synthesis often assume that networks learn explicit algorithmic procedures, whereas our model makes no such assumption. Instead, we treat the network parameters on
Tape4
as fixed, learned representations that may or may not correspond to clean algorithms. This distinction is crucial for understanding why LLMs exhibit both impressive capabilities and systematic failures on seemingly simple tasks.

Extensive research has documented how tokenisation introduces biases and systematic errors in language models
(
3
;
8
)
. However, this work typically treats tokenisation as an isolated pre-processing step rather than as one component in an integrated computational pipeline. Our model situates tokenisation within the broader inference process, enabling analysis of how tokenisation errors propagate through subsequent stages.
The mechanistic interpretability programme
(
6
)
seeks to reverse-engineer the computational structures learned by neural networks by identifying interpretable circuits implementing particular functions (e.g., indirect object identification, copy suppression). Our model again provides a complementary perspective: rather than discovering what computations networks perform, we formalise how those computations unfold operationally through the inference pipeline. The tape-based decomposition makes explicit where mechanistic interventions can be applied and what their scope of influence should be.

Several recent works have developed formal frameworks for analysing transformer capabilities. Weiss
et al.

(
22
)
introduced the Restricted Access Sequence Processing Language (RASP) that maps transformer operations to discrete computational primitives. While RASP focuses on characterising what computations transformers can perform through a programming abstraction, our model instead traces
how
the LLM inference pipeline unfolds operationally, with explicit representation of each processing stage from tokenisation through generation, enabling error localisation through explicit pipeline decomposition.
Pérez
et al.

(
17
)
established that transformers with hard attention and rational precision arithmetic are Turing-complete. However, as with other universality results
(
19
)
, this theoretical capacity does not illuminate which problems are practically solvable or where failures occur in the inference pipeline. Liu
et al.

(
14
)
provide empirical evidence that transformers learn approximate solutions rather than implementing exact algorithms, supporting our emphasis on operational analysis over theoretical capacity.
Finally, recent work on so-called
verbalized sampling

(
24
)
has explored prompting techniques that ask models to generate probability distributions over responses, improving output diversity. This corresponds to explicitly requesting probabilistic information from
Tape6
to be rendered on
Tape7
, rather than sampling implicitly. While this does not alter the computational class of the model, it demonstrates how different prompting strategies can expose distinct aspects of the model’s learned distributions, a direction that possibly warrants future investigation in terms of what information can be reliably externalised from internal tapes to the output tape.

5
Discussion

Our formalisation provides a rigorous computational account of the standard LLM inference pipeline. By decomposing the process into discrete tape operations, we can analyse systematic failures with precision unavailable to higher-level descriptions.
This computational perspective reveals how different analytical approaches complement each other within a unified formalisation. Chain-of-thought prompting externalises intermediate computation on
Tape7

while recursion theory delineates formal limits on what can be represented or decided, independent of prompting strategy.

The distinction between statistical pattern-matching and systematic reasoning emerges clearly in recent empirical findings. Berglund
et al.

(
2
)
demonstrated the “Reversal Curse”: models trained on “A is B” fail to generalise to “B is A”.
A model trained on “Valentina Tereshkova was the first woman to travel to space” cannot answer “Who was the first woman to travel to space?” better than chance. This reflects the directional nature of autoregressive token prediction on
Tape7
: gradient updates during training alter representations to predict B given A, but do not systematically encode the reverse association: the model learns a statistical correlation in one direction rather than a bidirectional logical equivalence. This failure mode differs fundamentally from the tokenisation-induced errors we identify as it reveals how the autoregressive objective shapes learned representations without implementing systematic logical operations. Together, the Reversal Curse and character-counting failures illustrate two distinct computational limitations: directional statistical learning versus absence of explicit algorithmic subroutines.
The TM model’s value lies not in computational efficiency, as our sequential simulation would be far slower than parallel GPU implementations, but in such analytical transparency.

The mechanistic interpretability programme seeks to identify interpretable circuits in neural networks
(
6
)
. Within our formalisation, this corresponds to analysing weight structures on
Tape4
and activation patterns on
Tape5
. However, this neuron-level analysis operates within the same architectural constraints we identify: tokenisation limitations and absent algorithmic subroutines remain regardless of circuit-level interventions.

5.1
Chain-of-Thought Prompting

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting has emerged as a widely used technique to improve the reasoning performance of LLMs
(
21
;
13
)
.
Rather than producing a direct answer, the model is encouraged to output intermediate reasoning steps in natural language.
In the multi-tape TM formalisation, this corresponds to writing auxiliary traces on
Tape7
before appending the final answer, as the output tape doubles as an external scratchpad memory.
By externalising reasoning steps on
Tape7
, CoT may reduce the attention mechanism’s burden to track complex intermediate states internally during forward computation on
Tape5
. The model can instead attend to its own written reasoning during subsequent forward passes, effectively using the output tape as extended working memory. This interpretation would benefit from empirical validation.
Recursion-theoretic analysis shows that CoT does not alter the underlying computational class of the process: tasks requiring unbounded recursion, exact counting, or the resolution of paradoxes remain outside the model’s reliable capacity
(
18
)
.
Recent work by Merrill and Sabharwal
(
15
)
provides a complexity-theoretic analysis of CoT’s power, showing that the number of intermediate generation steps fundamentally determines which computational problems become solvable. With logarithmic intermediate steps (relative to input length), transformers gain only slightly beyond their base capabilities, still unable to solve NP-complete problems like directed graph connectivity. Linear intermediate steps enable simulation of finite automata, while polynomial steps bring all P-complete problems within reach. This hierarchy aligns with our model in that CoT helps when intermediate computation can be externalised on
Tape7
within these step bounds, but formal limitations persist. Their results quantify how much CoT can help, while our model explains
why
it helps through explicit externalisation of computation that would otherwise require unbounded internal state.
The multi-tape model makes this limitation precise: CoT helps with tasks where intermediate states can be usefully externalised as natural language on
Tape7
, but fails when the required computation cannot be adequately expressed through sequential text generation.
Specifically, CoT succeeds in:

•

Short-range compositional tasks (e.g., multi-step arithmetic bounded by context length)

•

Schema-based decompositions where intermediate steps follow recognisable patterns

•

Retrieval-augmented reasoning where intermediate text acts as a query plan

Conversely, CoT fails in tasks requiring:

•

Explicit counting over characters (without a counting subroutine implemented in
Q_{\mathrm{fwd}}
)

•

Deep centre-embedding or recursive structures beyond context limits

•

Paradox resolution or tasks involving self-referential contradictions

The TM model thus reveals CoT not as a general reasoning enhancement, but as a specific computational pattern that externalises intermediate computation onto the output tape. This perspective explains both its empirical successes and its systematic limitations within a unified theoretical model.

5.2
Limitations

CoT prompting may encourage intermediate text on
Tape7
(“Let me spell out Strawberry: S-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y…”), but without the counting subroutines above, this externalisation cannot guarantee correctness: it remains pattern completion rather than verified computation.

Our TM simulation processes tokens sequentially, while transformers can compute attention and feed-forward operations in parallel across positions. This sequential abstraction serves analytical rather than efficiency purposes, as it forces explicit enumeration of computational dependencies that remain implicit in parallel implementations. The sequential constraint does not affect computational expressiveness, since parallel algorithms can be simulated sequentially with polynomial overhead, but it affects our ability to model certain temporal dynamics of actual inference.

Our model makes additional simplifying assumptions on perfect tokenisation without subword regularisation, and it employs discrete state transitions rather than continuous neural dynamics. While these simplifications enable precise formal analysis, they may miss important aspects of actual LLM behavior. The model is best understood as a conceptual means to error analysis rather than a complete simulation of LLM inference.

6
Conclusion

We have shown how large language model inference can be formally represented as a multi-tape Turing machine process, with each tape corresponding to a distinct component of the computational pipeline. This formalisation enables precise localisation of systematic errors and provides theoretical foundations for understanding both the capabilities and limitations of contemporary language models.
The formalisation clarifies several phenomena within classical computability theory: tokenisation-induced failures in character-level tasks, the externalisation of computation in chain-of-thought prompting, and the formal boundaries that constrain even sophisticated prompting strategies. By grounding LLM behaviour in discrete tape operations rather than geometric metaphors, the model offers testable predictions about when and why specific failure modes occur.

Several extensions warrant investigation. The model could be extended to probabilistic sampling by introducing randomness tapes, to recursive computation through stack-like memory structures, and to tool use via external I/O tapes. Since practical implementations operate with finite precision arithmetic, future work should also develop more sophisticated treatments of numerical error propagation across the computational pipeline.
Whereas some directions in sufficiently high-dimensional spaces may encode information about which letters appear, where they appear, and how many times, the ability to solve problems of this nature cannot possibly scale with increasing problem complexity using the computations currently present. An outstanding question is whether including additional tapes on which to conduct counting routines, logic and mathematics could improve the problem-solving ability of such systems.
The theoretical model developed here provides a foundation for systematic analysis of machine reasoning that complements empirical scaling studies. As language models continue to grow in capability and complexity, formal computational models become increasingly valuable for understanding their fundamental properties and limitations.
