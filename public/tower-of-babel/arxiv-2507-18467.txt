Contraction, Criticality, and Capacity: A Dynamical-Systems Perspective on Echo-State Networks
Pradeep Singh , Lavanya Sankaranarayanan , Balasubramanian Raman , Department of Computer Science and Engineering Indian Institute of Technology Roorkee Roorkee-247667, India Department of Mathematical and Statistical Sciences University of Alberta Edmonton, AB T6G 2J5, Canada

Abstract
Echo-State Networks (ESNs) distil a key neurobiological insight: richly recurrent but fixed circuitry combined with adaptive linear read-outs can transform temporal streams with remarkable efficiency. Yet fundamental questions about stability, memory and expressive power remain fragmented across disciplines. We present a unified, dynamical-systems treatment that weaves together functional analysis, random attractor theory and recent neuroscientific findings. First, on compact multivariate input alphabets we prove that the Echo-State Property (wash-out of initial conditions) together with global Lipschitz dynamics necessarily yields the Fading-Memory Property (geometric forgetting of remote inputs). Tight algebraic tests translate activation-specific Lipschitz constants into certified spectral-norm bounds, covering both saturating and rectifying nonlinearities. Second, employing a Stone–Weierstrass strategy we give a streamlined proof that ESNs with polynomial reservoirs and linear read-outs are dense in the Banach space of causal, time-invariant fading-memory filters, extending universality to stochastic inputs. Third, we quantify computational resources via Jaeger’s memory-capacity spectrum, show how topology and leak rate redistribute delay-specific capacities, and link these trade-offs to Lyapunov spectra at the “edge of chaos.” Finally, casting ESNs as skew-product random dynamical systems, we establish existence of singleton pullback attractors and derive conditional Lyapunov bounds, providing a rigorous analogue to cortical criticality. The analysis yields concrete design rules—spectral radius, input gain, activation choice—grounded simultaneously in mathematics and neuroscience, and clarifies why modest-sized reservoirs often rival fully trained recurrent networks in practice.

Introduction

Brains as natural reservoirs.

Cortical micro-circuits are dense, heterogeneous and massively
recurrent; action potentials reverberate through loops whose synaptic
time-constants range from a few milliseconds in AMPA-dominated shafts to
hundreds of milliseconds in NMDA-rich dendritic spines

7

6
]
.
Such multi-scale reverberations endow neural tissue with a form of

short-term working memory and temporal context integration

[

,

]
.
At the same time, continuous synaptic bombardment drives cortical
networks towards a marginally stable, “edge-of-chaos” regime where
Lyapunov exponents hover near zero, maximising both sensitivity and
information throughput
[
3
,
2
]
.
These observations have catalysed the
reservoir-computing

paradigm, whose elegant realization is the
Echo-State Network

(ESN) of Jaeger
[
15
]
and the closely related

of Maass
et al.

[
20
]
.

Motivation.

Despite spectacular empirical success in chaotic weather

[
22
]
, brain-computer interfaces and analog
photonic processors, a first-principles explanation of
how
and

why
ESNs inherit the computational virtues of biological
networks remains fragmented. In particular,
a principled dynamical-systems description connecting ESN design
knobs (activation, topology, leak rate) to neuroscientific
measurements such as Lyapunov spectra and information rate is
largely absent.

Contribution.

Bridging neurobiology, functional analysis and contemporary dynamical
systems, we provide a self-contained mathematical narrative that
clarifies these gaps. Concretely, we:

(ii)

translate neuronal non-linearities into algebraic ESP tests

), covering both saturating (tanh,
sigmoid) and rectifying (ReLU) conductances;

(iii)

revisit universality: ESNs with fixed reservoirs and trainable
linear dendritic readouts are dense in the Banach space of
fading-memory filters (Sec.
5
), echoing
the brain’s ability to repurpose recurrent circuitry through
plastic synapses;

(iv)

quantify memory and computational trade-offs via capacity curves
and conditional Lyapunov spectra (Secs.
6

–

), linking the “edge-of-chaos”
hypothesis to concrete spectral-norm and topology choices.

Mathematical Preliminaries

Throughout,
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
denote metric spaces,

\|\cdot\|
the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and

\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
.

Compactness.

A subset
K\subset X
is
compact
if every open cover of
K

admits a finite sub-cover.
In Euclidean space this is equivalent to closedness and boundedness
(Heine–Borel theorem)
[
24
, Ch. 2]
.
Compactness guarantees the existence of extrema and convergent
subsequences—facts repeatedly used when analysing
reservoir trajectories on a bounded input alphabet
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Lipschitz continuity and contractions.

A map
f:X\to Y
is
Lipschitz
with constant
L\geq 0
if

d_{Y}\!\bigl(f(x),f(x^{\prime})\bigr)\leq L\,d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})

for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
.
If
L<1
the map is a
contraction
;
iterates shrink distances by at least
L

and converge to a unique fixed point
(Banach’s theorem, see
[
17
, §4.2]
).

Spectral (induced
2
-) norm.

For
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
the spectral norm

\|W\|_{2}=\sigma_{\max}(W)=\sup_{\|x\|=1}\|Wx\|

equals the largest singular value and bounds the worst-case
Euclidean amplification of vectors
[
14
, §5.6]
.
It is pivotal in contraction tests for ESNs.

Causal, time-invariant filters.

Let
U^{\mathbb{N}}
be the space of input sequences

u=(u_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
A map
H:U^{\mathbb{N}}\to Y
is a
filter
if it is

causal
—
H(u)
at time
t
depends only on

(u_{0},\dots,u_{t})
—and

time-invariant
(shift-equivariant):

H(\sigma u)=H(u)
for the left-shift

\sigma(u)_{t}=u_{t+1}

[
26
, Ch. 4]
.
The reservoir–readout cascade of an ESN is precisely such a filter.

Global attractor.

For a continuous self-map
F:X\to X
,
a set
\mathcal{A}\subset X
is a
global attractor
if
(i)
\mathcal{A}
is compact and invariant (
F(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{A}
),
(ii) every trajectory approaches it:

\operatorname{dist}(F^{t}(x),\mathcal{A})\to 0
as
t\to\infty

for all
x\in X

[
13
, Ch. 9]
.
When the ESN update is contractive, the attractor is a singleton,
yielding the Echo State Property.

Banach’s fixed-point theorem.

If
(X,d)
is complete and
F:X\to X
is a contraction with
constant
0<\beta<1
, then

F
has a unique fixed point
x^{\ast}
and the iterates satisfy

d(F^{t}(x_{0}),x^{\ast})\leq\beta^{t}d(x_{0},x^{\ast})

for all
x_{0}\in X

[
17
, §4.2]
.
This theorem underpins proofs that contractive ESN updates
possess unique, globally attracting state trajectories.

Alphabet.

In information and dynamical–systems theory an
alphabet
is a
set of symbols (or values)
from which the elements of a sequence are drawn.
Formally, if
A
is any non-empty set, the collection of all (one-sided) infinite sequences with entries in
A
is the product space
A^{\mathbb{N}}=\{(a_{0},a_{1},\dotsc)\mid a_{t}\in A\}
; in symbolic-dynamics texts the pair
(A^{\mathbb{N}},\sigma)
with left-shift
\sigma\bigl((a_{t})\bigr)=(a_{t+1})
is called a
full shift
over the alphabet
A

[
18
]
. When
A
is finite (e.g.
\{0,1\}
for binary strings) the terminology is literal—each element of
A
is a distinct “letter.” Nothing, however, restricts
A
to be finite: in machine-learning and control theory it is common to let

U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}

be a
compact
subset of Euclidean space and still speak of
U
as the
input alphabet
. In that case the “letters” are
m
-dimensional real vectors, so a signal
u=(u_{0},u_{1},\dotsc)\in U^{\mathbb{N}}
is an infinite sequence of bounded
m
-tuples. This is precisely the setting adopted when defining the Echo State Property or Fading-Memory Property for Echo State Networks: we declare in advance the set
U
of admissible input vectors and analyse the reservoir map on the product space
U^{\mathbb{N}}
. The word
alphabet
thus conveys only that the inputs are chosen from a fixed, predetermined set—whether that set is finite (symbols), countable (e.g. integers), or uncountable (a compact region in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
).

3

Echo-State and Fading-Memory Properties

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a
compact
input alphabet and

X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
the reservoir state–space.
An Echo State Network (ESN) is the causal, discrete-time system

x_{t}=F\!\bigl(u_{t},x_{t-1}\bigr)=\varphi\!\bigl(W_{\!in}\,u_{t}+W\,x_{t-1}+b\bigr),\qquad t\in\mathbb{N},

(1)

where
W_{\!in}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
,
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,

b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and

\varphi:X\to X
is a component-wise Lipschitz non-linearity with
constant
L_{\varphi}\leq 1

(e.g.
\tanh
or
\sigma
).

Definition 3.1

(Echo State Property,
[
15
,
20
]
)
.

System (
1
) has the

Echo State Property (ESP)
if, for every bounded
input sequence
\bigl(u_{t}\bigr)_{t\geq 0}\in U^{\mathbb{N}}

and every pair of initial states
x_{0},x_{0}^{\prime}\in X
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\;\bigl\|x_{t}(x_{0})-x_{t}(x_{0}^{\prime})\bigr\|=0.

(ESP)

Equivalently, ESP guarantees the existence of a

unique
state trajectory compatible with each input, so the
long-term dynamics are determined solely by the driving signal, not by
initial conditions. A useful sufficient condition is

\|W\|_{2}\,L_{\varphi}<1
, which makes

F
a contraction in its second argument.

Define the exponential (weighted) sup-norm on inputs

\|u\|_{\lambda}\;=\;\sup_{t\geq 0}\;\Bigl(\sup_{k\geq 0}\,\lambda^{k}\,\|u_{t-k}\|\Bigr),\qquad 0<\lambda<1.

Definition 3.2

(Fading-Memory Property,
[
4
]
)
.

A causal, time-invariant filter
H:U^{\mathbb{N}}\!\to X
has the

Fading-Memory Property (FMP)
if, for every
\varepsilon>0

there exists
\delta>0
such that

\|u-v\|_{\lambda}<\delta\;\Longrightarrow\;\|H(u)-H(v)\|<\varepsilon,

(FMP)

for all input sequences
u,v\in U^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Condition (FMP) states that perturbations in the
remote
past are
geometrically attenuated by the factor
\lambda^{k}
; the current
output depends predominantly on recent inputs.

Proposition 3.1

(ESP implies FMP under global Lipschitzness)
.

If system (
1
) satisfies the ESP
and the update map
F
is globally Lipschitz in
(u,x)
,
then the induced filter

H(u)=\lim_{t\to\infty}x_{t}

is continuous under
\|\cdot\|_{\lambda}
and therefore possesses
the FMP
[
5
,
19
]
.

Proof.

Fix a compact input alphabet
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and recall the ESN
update

x_{t}\;=\;F(u_{t},x_{t-1})

with
F\colon U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
globally
Lipschitz in
(u,x)
.
Write the Lipschitz constants as

L_{u}\;=\;\sup_{u\neq v}\,\frac{\|F(u,x)-F(v,x)\|}{\|u-v\|},\qquad L_{x}\;=\;\sup_{x\neq y}\,\frac{\|F(u,x)-F(u,y)\|}{\|x-y\|}.

Step 1:
L_{x}<1
.

Because the reservoir enjoys the Echo State Property,
for
every
admissible input sequence the map

x_{t-1}\mapsto F(u_{t},x_{t-1})

has a unique global attractor. A standard argument

[
5
, Th. 2]
shows this is possible only when the
update is
uniformly
contractive in
x
, hence

L_{x}<1
. Denote
\beta:=L_{x}
.

Step 2: A Lipschitz estimate for trajectories.

Let two input sequences

u=(u_{t})_{t\geq 0},\;v=(v_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in U^{\mathbb{N}}

be given and initialise the reservoir from an

arbitrary
common state
x_{0}=y_{0}
.
Inductively define

x_{t}=F(u_{t},x_{t-1}),\;y_{t}=F(v_{t},y_{t-1})

for
t\geq 1
.
By the triangle inequality and (A.1),

\|x_{t}-y_{t}\|\leq L_{u}\|u_{t}-v_{t}\|+\beta\|x_{t-1}-y_{t-1}\|.

Unfolding (A.2)
k
times yields

\|x_{t}-y_{t}\|\;\leq\;L_{u}\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\beta^{\,k}\;\|u_{t-k}-v_{t-k}\|.

Step 3: Choice of the weighted norm.

Select any
\lambda\in(\beta,1)
and endow

U^{\mathbb{N}}
with the exponential‐sup norm

\|u\|_{\lambda}=\sup_{t\geq 0}\sup_{k\geq 0}\lambda^{k}\|u_{t-k}\|.

Because

\|u_{t-k}-v_{t-k}\|\leq\lambda^{-k}\,\|u-v\|_{\lambda},

inequality (A.3) is bounded by the geometric series

\|x_{t}-y_{t}\|\;\leq\;L_{u}\|u-v\|_{\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{\beta}{\lambda}\Bigr)^{k}\;\leq\;\frac{L_{u}}{1-\beta/\lambda}\,\|u-v\|_{\lambda}.

Step 4: Passage to the limit.

Because the ESP guarantees

\lim_{t\to\infty}x_{t}=H(u)

and

\lim_{t\to\infty}y_{t}=H(v),

take
t\to\infty
in (A.4) to obtain

\|H(u)-H(v)\|\;\leq\;C\,\|u-v\|_{\lambda},\qquad C:=\frac{L_{u}}{1-\beta/\lambda}.

Thus
H
is
globally Lipschitz
under the weighted norm

\|\cdot\|_{\lambda}
, hence continuous.

Step 5: Continuity
\Rightarrow
Fading memory.

Given
\varepsilon>0
let

\delta:=\varepsilon/C
.
If
\|u-v\|_{\lambda}<\delta
then (A.5) yields

\|H(u)-H(v)\|<\varepsilon,

which is precisely the Fading‐Memory Property
(Def.
3.2
).

The chain

\text{ESP}\,\wedge\,L_{x}<1\;\Longrightarrow\;H\text{ Lipschitz under }\|\cdot\|_{\lambda}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{FMP}

completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

FMP alone
does not
imply ESP; one can construct filters with
geometric input forgetting yet persistent sensitivity to initial
states. In reservoir design, the empirical rule
\rho(W)<1
(spectral
radius) is popular precisely because it enforces both the contractive
behaviour required for ESP and the exponential decay that yields FMP.

5

Universal Approximation of Fading–Memory Filters

Having established the Echo–State and Fading–Memory Properties, we now turn
to the
expressive power
of Echo State Networks. The key result is
that ESNs with linear readouts are dense in the space of causal,
time–invariant fading–memory filters. Thus, under mild conditions, an ESN
can approximate any such filter to arbitrary precision.

Space of fading–memory filters.

Fix a compact input alphabet
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
choose a decay parameter
\lambda\in(0,1)
.
Let
\|\cdot\|_{\lambda}
be the weighted sup–norm on
U^{\mathbb{N}}

defined in (
3
).
Denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\!\lambda}\bigl(U^{\mathbb{N}},\mathbb{R}^{p}\bigr)

the Banach space of causal, time–invariant maps

H:U^{\mathbb{N}}\!\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
that are
continuous
w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{\lambda}
;
these are precisely the fading–memory filters of
Definition
3.2
. Endow
\mathcal{F}_{\!\lambda}
with the
uniform norm
\|H\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{u\in U^{\mathbb{N}}}\|H(u)\|
.

Reservoir–computer family.

For a fixed reservoir map
F\colon U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying
ESP & FMP, every linear readout

W_{\!out}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
,
b_{\!out}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

induces a filter

\mathcal{R}_{W_{\!out},b_{\!out}}(u)\;:=\;W_{\!out}\,H_{F}(u)+b_{\!out},

where
H_{F}(u)=\lim_{t\to\infty}x_{t}
is the state filter of
F
.
Let
\mathcal{R}_{F}
denote the set of all such affine readouts.

Theorem 5.1

(Universal approximation
[
12
]
)
.

Let
F
satisfy the ESP and be a polynomial map in its arguments
(e.g. affine activation followed by polynomial non–linearity), and
suppose
U
is a compact, convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Then the family
\mathcal{R}_{F}
is
dense

in
\mathcal{F}_{\!\lambda}(U^{\mathbb{N}},\mathbb{R}^{p})
with respect to

\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
; that is, for every

H\in\mathcal{F}_{\!\lambda}
and every
\varepsilon>0

there exists
W_{\!out},b_{\!out}
such that

\bigl\|\mathcal{R}_{W_{\!out},b_{\!out}}-H\bigr\|_{\infty}\;<\;\varepsilon.

Sketch.

The result follows a Stone–Weierstrass strategy in three steps:

The reservoir state space

\mathcal{S}:=H_{F}(U^{\mathbb{N}})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is compact
by continuity of
H_{F}
and compactness of
U^{\mathbb{N}}
.
Affine functions on
\mathcal{S}
,

\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}=\{x\mapsto W_{\!out}x+b_{\!out}\}
,
form a unital subalgebra of
C(\mathcal{S})
.

Because
F
is polynomial,
its state components span a polynomial algebra that separates points of

\mathcal{S}

[
12
, Lem. 2]
.
Therefore
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}
separates points and contains
constants.

By Stone–Weierstrass,

\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{S}}
is dense in
C(\mathcal{S})
.
Composition with the continuous map

H_{F}\colon U^{\mathbb{N}}\!\to\mathcal{S}

yields density of
\mathcal{R}_{F}
in

C\bigl(U^{\mathbb{N}}\bigr)
.
Restricting to the subspace

\mathcal{F}_{\!\lambda}\subset C(U^{\mathbb{N}})

preserves density because the uniform norm agrees on both spaces.
∎

Implications.

Theorem
5.1
establishes that
any

causal fading–memory system—be it analytic, stochastic,
or highly nonlinear—can be approximated arbitrarily well by a
single

finite–dimensional ESN with a suitably trained linear readout.
Hence the practical limitation of reservoir computing is not
representation but optimisation and generalisation.

Memory Capacity and Computational Capability of ESNs

While Sections
2
–
5

establish that Echo State Networks are
principally capable
of
approximating any fading–memory filter, it is equally important to
quantify
how much
past information a finite reservoir can retain
and exploit for computation. The classical notion that captures this
resource is
linear memory capacity
.

Linear memory capacity

Fix a scalar input stream

u=(u_{t})_{t\geq 0}\in[-1,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
with zero mean and variance

\sigma^{2}\!=\!\mathbb{E}[u_{t}^{2}]
.
For a delay
\tau\geq 1
define the target signal

y^{(\tau)}_{t}:=u_{t-\tau}
.
After driving the ESN (state
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
)
train a linear readout
w^{(\tau)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by ridge
regression,

\hat{y}^{(\tau)}_{t}=w^{(\tau)\!\top}x_{t}\!,

minimising the mean–squared error

E^{(\tau)}=\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[(y^{(\tau)}_{t}-\hat{y}^{(\tau)}_{t})^{2}\bigr]
.
The
capacity at delay
\tau
is

C(\tau):=1-\frac{E^{(\tau)}}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{\text{Cov}^{2}(y^{(\tau)},\hat{y}^{(\tau)})}{\text{Var}(y^{(\tau)})\,\text{Var}(\hat{y}^{(\tau)})},

which equals the squared correlation
between target and reconstruction
[
15
]
.

Definition 6.1

(Total linear memory capacity)
.

The
total linear memory capacity
of an ESN is

\text{MC}_{\text{tot}}:=\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}C(\tau).

Jaeger’s seminal result
[
15
]

bounds the capacity by the reservoir dimension:

\text{MC}_{\text{tot}}\;\leq\;n.

Equality is attained by a perfectly orthogonal reservoir driven by
independent Gaussian inputs; in practice, random sparse
reservoirs achieve
\text{MC}_{\text{tot}}\approx 0.6\,n
.

Nonlinear and task–specific capacities

Linear memory is only part of the picture; many tasks require nonlinear
functions of the past. Let
f_{\tau}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\tau}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
(possibly nonlinear) functional, e.g.
f_{\tau}(u_{t-1},u_{t-2})=u_{t-1}u_{t-2}
.
Define
y^{(f_{\tau})}_{t}:=f_{\tau}(u_{t-1},\dots,u_{t-\tau})
.
Training a linear readout for each such target yields
capacities
C(f_{\tau})
.
The reservoir’s
information–processing capacity
(IPC) is the sum
of all capacities across a complete orthonormal basis
of functionals
[
9
]
:

\text{IPC}\;=\;\sum_{f\in\mathcal{B}}C(f),\quad\text{IPC}\;\leq\;n.

Nonlinear activations (e.g. tanh) disperse input information into
higher–order Volterra coordinates, allowing the ESN to trade
linear–memory capacity for nonlinear computation.

Design implications

•

Spectral radius
\rho(W)
.

Setting
\rho(W)\approx 0.9
places the system near the edge of
contractivity, empirically maximising total capacity while
respecting ESP
[
3
]
.

•

Input scaling.

Larger
\|W_{\!in}\|
injects input energy deeper into the
state–space, increasing high–order capacities but potentially
reducing linear memory. A rule–of–thumb is

\|W_{\!in}\|\in[0.1,1]
.

•

Leak rate (leaky ESNs).

For slowly varying signals, a leak rate

\alpha<1
effectively enlarges memory depth by acting as a
low–pass filter on
x_{t}
.

•

Topology.

Structured reservoirs (cycle, small–world, hyperbolic)
redistribute eigenvalues to shape the delay–specific spectrum

C(\tau)
, enabling task–matched memory kernels

[
23
]
.

Beyond memory: separability and criticality

A high memory capacity alone does not guarantee
computational
separability
of inputs; too much linear memory can render states nearly
collinear.
Optimal performance often lies near a

critical
regime—borderline stable, rich in transient dynamics yet
satisfying ESP—where memory and separability coexist

[
2
]
.

7

Reservoir Dynamics from a Dynamical-Systems Perspective

Echo State Networks may be interpreted as
non-autonomous
discrete
dynamical systems driven by an external input. This section
formalises that viewpoint and connects reservoir design criteria to
classical notions such as random attractors, Lyapunov exponents and
bifurcations.

Skew-product formulation

Let
\Sigma:=U^{\mathbb{N}}
be the left-shift space with

\sigma(u)_{t}=u_{t+1}
.
Define the skew-product map

\Psi:\Sigma\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\Sigma\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad\Psi(u,x)\;=\;\bigl(\sigma u,\;F(u_{0},x)\bigr),

where
F
is the ESN update (
1
).
Iterating
\Psi
yields

\Psi^{t}(u,x_{0})=\bigl(\sigma^{t}u,\;x_{t}(u,x_{0})\bigr),

so analysing reservoir trajectories is equivalent to studying the

non-autonomous
system generated by
\Psi

[
1
, Ch. 2]
.

Random pullback attractor

Endow
\Sigma
with the product Borel

\sigma
-algebra and any ergodic shift-invariant probability

\mathbb{P}
(e.g. i.i.d. inputs).
A measurable family of non-empty compact sets

\{\mathcal{A}(u)\}_{u\in\Sigma}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a

random pullback attractor
of
\Psi
if

(a)

\Psi\bigl(u,\mathcal{A}(u)\bigr)=\bigl(\sigma u,\mathcal{A}(\sigma u)\bigr)
(invariance),

(b)

for every bounded
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

and
\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
u
,

\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\Psi^{t}(u,B),\;\mathcal{A}(\sigma^{t}u)\bigr)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.

The following theorem adapts
[
16
, §2.3]
.

Theorem 7.1

(Existence of a singleton random attractor)
.

Suppose
F
is globally Lipschitz with constants

L_{u},L_{x}
and
L_{x}<1
.
Then
\Psi
admits a unique random attractor

\mathcal{A}(u)=\{H(u)\}
consisting of a single point, and

\|x_{t}(u,x_{0})-H(\sigma^{t}u)\|\;\leq\;L_{u}\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}L_{x}^{\,k}\,\|u_{t-1-k}-u_{t-1-k}^{\prime}\|.

Consequently the Echo State Property holds and
H
is the fading-memory
filter of Section
3
.

Conditional Lyapunov exponents

For each
u\in\Sigma
fix the forward orbit

x_{t}=H(\sigma^{t}u)
.
Linearising
\Psi
along this orbit yields the cocycle

\partial_{x}F(u_{t},x_{t-1})=J_{t}(u)
.
Oseledets’ theorem gives Lyapunov exponents

\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}

of the product

J_{t-1}\dotsm J_{0}

[
1
, Ch. 3]
.
Because
F
is Lipschitz and
L_{x}<1
,

\lambda_{\max}\leq\log L_{x}<0,

so trajectories converge exponentially.
At the
edge of chaos
(
L_{x}\approx 1^{-}
)

\lambda_{\max}\to 0^{-}
, matching empirical observations that
memory and separability peak near criticality

[
2
]
.

Linear reservoir with identity activation.

If
\varphi(z)=z
and
b=0
then

F(u,x)=W_{\!in}u+Wx
,

J_{t}=W
, and

\lambda_{\max}=\log\rho(W)
.
Hence

\rho(W)<1
is both necessary and sufficient for ESP,
recovering Jaeger’s rule.

Bifurcations beyond contractivity

When
\rho(W)
moves above unity the fixed point
x=0
loses
stability; successive Neimark–Sacker or period-doubling bifurcations
can emerge, producing quasi-periodic or chaotic internal dynamics even

without
input
[
21
, Ch. 9]
.
Input acts as a forcing term, and the skew-product dynamics may exhibit

strange non-chaotic attractors
with fractal structure yet
non-positive Lyapunov spectrum—an effect exploited in
[
22
]

for climate-scale prediction.

State-space dimension and information rate

Let
d_{\text{KY}}
be the Kaplan–Yorke fractal dimension derived from
Lyapunov exponents; the information production rate is

h_{\text{KS}}=\sum_{\lambda_{i}>0}\lambda_{i}

(Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy)
[
21
, Ch. 5]
.
For contractive reservoirs
h_{\text{KS}}=0
but large
d_{\text{KY}}

is possible via input-driven embedding; thus

information storage
can coexist with stability.
Designing hyperbolic or small-world topologies distributes Lyapunov
spectra to tailor
d_{\text{KY}}
versus
h_{\text{KS}}
,
balancing memory and computational richness.

8

Conclusion

We explored a dynamical‑systems foundation for
Echo‑State Networks—one that is simultaneously faithful to mathematical
principles and cognisant of neurobiological evidence. By proving that
global Lipschitz contractivity turns ESP into a
strong Fading‑Memory guarantee, we clarified why cortical‐style
reverberations with balanced excitation–inhibition naturally support
robust stimulus encoding. Tight activation–dependent spectral‑norm
criteria then translated this theory into actionable design rules that
cover both classical saturating nonlinearities and modern rectifiers.

Building on these stability results, we revisited universality:
a fixed reservoir with merely a linear read‑out is dense in the Banach
space of causal fading‑memory filters—hence the representational power
of ESNs is limited not by architecture but by optimisation and data.
Quantitative memory‑capacity spectra, together with conditional Lyapunov
analysis, revealed how spectral radius, leak rate and topology jointly
govern the trade‑off between storage depth and nonlinear separability.
Finally, by casting ESNs as skew‑product random dynamical systems we
established existence of singleton pull‑back attractors and bounded
Lyapunov exponents, providing a rigorous analogue to “edge‑of‑chaos”
criticality observed in cortex.

Future directions.

Several open questions emerge.
(i) Stochastic reservoirs:

extending ESP
\!\Rightarrow\!
FMP to reservoirs with multiplicative
noise would bridge theory with neuromorphic hardware plagued by device
variability.
(ii) Optimal topology:
a systematic connection
between graph spectra, memory kernels and information‑processing
capacity could guide architecture search beyond random graphs.

(iii) Continuous time:
generalising our discrete‑time proofs to
contractive neural ODEs would unify delay‑based photonic reservoirs and
cortical microcircuits under a single mathematical umbrella.

(iv) Learning dynamics:
combining our stability criteria with
online synaptic plasticity rules remains largely unexplored and may shed
light on how biological networks balance adaptation with robustness.
