Optimal insurance coverage and pricing of outpatient drugs in Iran: a cost- and chronicity-based adaptation of the vertical equity model
Authors: Shekoofeh Sadat Momahhed, Atefehsadat Haghighathoseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo estimate welfare-optimal insurance coverage and drug prices in Iran’s NHI outpatient market, using drug cost and chronicity as proxies for patient financial vulnerability in the absence of income data.SettingA complete administrative census of 88,565 outpatient prescription claims across all NHI fund types and provinces in Iran, fiscal year 2023, covering 7,977 unique pharmaceutical products.DesignCross-sectional welfare analysis using the Westerhout–Folmer framework, adapted to replace income-based vertical equity with drug costliness and treatment chronicity as observable equity axes. Drugs were grouped into four categories (Low-cost Acute, High-cost Acute, Low-cost Chronic, High-cost Chronic). Claims-derived parameters were used to estimate consumer and supply-side surplus, insurer spending, and total welfare under observed versus second-best optimal scenarios, with sensitivity analyses over the social cost of public funds and fixed provision costs.ResultsThe welfare model recommended higher insurance coverage than currently observed for all four equity groups, with zero bounding violations in any sensitivity scenario. The largest coverage gap was identified for Low-cost Chronic drugs (Δr = + 0.279; observed mean 0.493, optimal 0.773) — the group with the lowest observed coverage ratio of all categories and the oldest mean patient age (64.1 years). Low-cost Acute drugs had the second largest gap (Δr = + 0.260). High-cost groups, which already benefit from cost-tier formulary reimbursement, showed smaller but positive recommended increases (+ 0.032 and + 0.081 respectively). Positive welfare gaps were observed for all groups (median per drug: Int$ +308 to + 436 PPP). All directional findings were fully stable across all 12 sensitivity scenarios.ConclusionIran’s NHI under-covers low-cost chronic medicines, leaving patients with the highest recurrent out-of-pocket burden despite the lowest reimbursement rates. Increasing coverage for this group is the most welfare-improving and administratively feasible reform within the current system. The framework also offers a replicable, income-independent approach to equity-oriented pharmaceutical policy in comparable middle-income settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-026-02889-w.

Introduction

Pharmaceutical expenditure represents one of the fastest-growing components of global health spending. Over the past two decades, outpatient drug costs have risen at a rate substantially exceeding that of most other health system inputs, driven by demographic ageing, the growing prevalence of chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs), and the introduction of high-cost specialty medicines [1]. Recent projections estimate that outpatient pharmaceutical spending in developed economies will grow by more than 20% in monetary terms between 2023 and 2027, while volume growth remains modest at 0–2% [2]. In lower- and middle-income countries (LMICs), the expansion is more pronounced: in India, for example, pharmaceutical expenditure has grown at rates of 10–30% annually, reflecting rapid expansion in access to new therapies [3]. In health systems with weaker financial protection mechanisms, outpatient drugs frequently constitute more than 40% of total health spending, predominantly financed by households through out-of-pocket (OOP) payments and often with limited insurance coverage [4].

The sustained rise in drug expenditures has intensified policy debates about the equity and efficiency of pharmaceutical coverage. Although insurance systems are designed to mitigate the risk of catastrophic health spending, coverage regulations frequently fail to align with clinical necessity or the actual costs of treatments, particularly for outpatient medicines essential to managing chronic conditions [5]. Vertical equity—the principle that individuals with greater needs or lower payment capacities should receive proportionately higher financial protection—is a foundational normative framework in health economics [6]. As Culyer and Wagstaff established, vertical equity demands the “unequal treatment of unequals,” implying that insurance design should differentiate financial protection according to patient vulnerability rather than applying uniform cost-sharing rules across all drug categories [7]. Most empirical applications of this principle in health financing have used income as the stratifying variable, assessing insurance burdens and coverage gaps across income quintiles [8]. However, this income-centred approach faces two structural limitations in many health systems: administrative insurance databases frequently do not record individual income data, and income may not fully capture the clinical and financial vulnerability that arises specifically from chronic illness or exposure to high-cost therapies [9].

The Islamic Republic of Iran exemplifies these challenges in an especially acute form. Pharmaceuticals account for approximately 32.6% of total health spending, with outpatient medicines constituting the single largest market segment [10]. Iran’s National Health Insurance (NHI) system—encompassing the Iran Health Insurance Organization, the Social Security Organization, and allied public funds—provides coverage to the vast majority of the population and annually processes tens of millions of outpatient prescription claims [11]. Despite this breadth, Iran’s health financing system remains heavily reliant on OOP payments, which account for approximately 39.5% of total health expenditure [12]. Existing reimbursement regulations exhibit limited differentiation: co-insurance rates are determined by broad risk categories, with the lowest-risk drugs subject to co-insurance rates of 76–84% and high-risk drugs covered at 38–45%, without systematic adjustment for disease chronicity or patient financial vulnerability [10].

Iran’s epidemiological profile compounds these structural challenges. The country has undergone a pronounced transition toward NCDs: in 2019, more than 326,000 Iranians died from NCDs—an increase of 88% compared to three decades earlier—with cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, cancer, and chronic respiratory diseases constituting the dominant disease burden [13]. The total economic cost of NCDs was estimated at IRR 838.49 trillion in 2018, equivalent to approximately 5% of Iran’s annual GDP [14]. These epidemiological trends translate directly into rising pharmaceutical demand for chronic medications, while Iran’s macroeconomic environment—marked by international sanctions, sustained high inflation, and currency depreciation—has severely constrained both household purchasing power and the NHI’s fiscal capacity [15], making the alignment of reimbursement policy with patient need an urgent national priority [16].

A growing body of Iranian empirical evidence confirms that patients with chronic and high-cost conditions bear a disproportionate share of catastrophic health expenditure. A systematic review and meta-analysis found a population-level rate of catastrophic health expenditure (CHE) of approximately 4.7% in Iran; however, among cancer patients this rate rises to 25.3%, and patients undergoing dialysis face the highest CHE burden of any disease group [17]. Studies of household pharmaceutical expenditure document that insulin and biologic therapies push a significant proportion of chronic patients into catastrophic spending, even among those with NHI coverage [18]. A further scoping review identified persistent upward trends in CHE across Iranian provinces, with chronic disease and pharmaceutical costs identified as primary structural drivers [19]. Crucially, these findings establish the empirical foundation for the methodological choice made in this study: in Iran’s administrative claims environment, drug cost and chronicity are not merely administrative classification attributes—they are the primary observable determinants of financial vulnerability for insured patients. Patients using high-cost chronic therapies constitute the group most exposed to catastrophic pharmaceutical expenditure, irrespective of income level. This empirical reality provides a principled basis for treating drug-level cost and chronicity as valid proxies for patient-level vulnerability in the absence of individual income data—a substitution that is appropriate in, and consistent with, the foundational definition of vertical equity as the need-based, rather than income-based, allocation of financial protection [7].

Despite this evidence, prior empirical applications of vertical equity in pharmaceutical coverage have overwhelmingly relied on income as the stratifying variable. This creates both a methodological and a conceptual gap in the literature. Methodologically, income-based vertical equity analyses are infeasible with routinely collected NHI administrative claims data in Iran and comparable LMICs, which do not record individual income. Conceptually, income alone does not determine pharmaceutical vulnerability: the financial risk arising from a high-cost chronic treatment regimen may far exceed that of income-defined disadvantage for many patients. The growing international consensus—reflected in the WHO’s financial protection framework and World Bank guidance on catastrophic expenditure monitoring—is that equity-oriented insurance design must account for need-based vulnerability defined by the nature, cost, and duration of health conditions [20, 21]. Operationalizing vertical equity through drug cost and chronicity is therefore not a departure from the foundational framework of Culyer and Wagstaff, but its contextually appropriate application where the conditions under which income-based stratification is feasible and sufficient are not met [7].

Differentiated pharmaceutical reimbursement according to drug cost and clinical indication is not, in itself, a novel institutional arrangement. Australia’s Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) and South Korea’s Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) have long implemented tiered reimbursement schedules that vary by therapeutic category, cost level, and acute versus chronic indication [22]. Among middle-income countries, Thailand’s Universal Coverage Scheme adopted a National List of Essential Medicines as the formal basis for pharmaceutical benefits, with explicit priority for medicines treating NCDs including cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and cancer—an approach that has measurably improved financial protection and medicine access [23, 24]. Comparable differentiated reimbursement reforms have been documented in Iran’s own policy context in relation to subsidy restructuring [25]. What is absent from the existing literature, however, is a formal welfare-theoretic framework that embeds cost and chronicity as equity axes to jointly optimize pharmaceutical coverage ratios and prices while making explicit the efficiency–equity trade-offs involved. The present study provides precisely this analysis.

Specifically, this study applies the welfare-theoretic framework of optimal pharmaceutical coverage and pricing developed by Westerhout and Folmer [26], which is grounded in the seminal health insurance economics literature of Arrow and Zeckhauser [27, 28]. Arrow established the fundamental role of uncertainty and moral hazard in the demand for health insurance, while Zeckhauser formalized the central trade-off between financial risk protection and the welfare cost of excess utilization induced by coverage. The Westerhout-Folmer framework extends these insights to a pharmaceutical market context, deriving optimal coverage ratios and prices as functions of demand parameters, marginal costs, consumer surplus, and the social cost of public funds. While this framework is presented as a working paper awaiting peer review, its theoretical structure is fully consistent with the established welfare economics of optimal health insurance, and the present study constitutes, to our knowledge, its first empirical application to a middle-income country context.

This study adapts the Westerhout-Folmer framework to Iran’s NHI outpatient drug market by introducing two empirically observable proxies for vulnerability: drug costliness (distinguishing high-cost drugs, defined as those at or above the 90th percentile of unit price, from low-cost drugs) and treatment chronicity (distinguishing chronic from acute therapies according to NHI administrative classification). Drawing on a cross-sectional dataset of outpatient prescription claims from Iran’s 2023 NHI database, we construct drug-level measures of observed coverage, pricing, demand, and welfare, and compare these with model-derived optimal benchmarks across four equity subgroups: low-cost acute, high-cost acute, low-cost chronic, and high-cost chronic. The study makes three contributions. First, it provides a formal welfare-theoretic analysis of vertical equity in pharmaceutical coverage using cost and chronicity as equity axes—dimensions empirically grounded in Iran’s catastrophic spending evidence—rather than income, which is structurally unavailable in administrative data. Second, it makes explicit the efficiency–equity trade-offs arising under alternative reimbursement designs, quantifying welfare impacts across consumer surplus, insurer expenditure, and total social welfare. Third, it provides evidence directly relevant to reforming Iran’s NHI pharmaceutical benefit design, offering actionable guidance for comparable middle-income social insurance systems facing analogous fiscal and epidemiological pressures.

Methods

Source of data

This study draws on a complete administrative extract of all outpatient prescription claims filed with Iran’s National Health Insurance (NHI) system year 2023. The raw dataset comprised 105,209 transaction-level records, each representing a single dispensed drug item, and included patient demographics (age, gender, insurance fund type), provider characteristics (prescribing province, physician specialty), and three financial components: the total prescription cost, the NHI insurer’s reimbursement share, and the Preferential Currency Subsidy (a supply-side government allocation that subsidizes the procurement cost of imported medicines at the manufacturer level). The dataset is a census of all NHI-covered outpatient drug claims for the study period across all fund types and provinces, not a probability sample. The phrase ‘nationally representative’ therefore refers to administrative completeness—encompassing all NHI beneficiaries filing outpatient drug claims in 2023—rather than to a statistical sampling design.

Exchange rate

All costs are expressed as 2023 international dollars (Int$) using the World Bank purchasing power parity (PPP) conversion factor for Iran (118,411.24 Iranian Rials per Int$). This PPP-adjusted metric is preferred over nominal exchange rate conversion because it reflects the domestic purchasing power of pharmaceutical expenditures relative to internationally comparable goods, and is standard in cross-country pharmacoeconomic analyses. The Preferential Currency Subsidy recorded in the NHI claims data is calculated and disbursed at the official subsidized rate; sensitivity analyses using the NIMA and free-market rates are presented in Supplementary Table S4.

Data preprocessing

Raw claims underwent a structured preprocessing pipeline comprising eleven sequential steps, each documented in Table 1 with exact record counts. All exclusion criteria were defined a priori on logical and statistical grounds; no records were excluded retrospectively to influence results.

Out-of-pocket expenditure definition

A pre-analysis diagnostic examination revealed that in 66,365 raw records (63.1% of the file), the sum of the insurer’s share and the Preferential Currency Subsidy exceeded the total prescription cost. Under a naive formula—OOP = total cost − insurer share − FX subsidy—this would produce economically impossible negative patient expenditures. Systematic investigation established that these records are not data errors: in every such case, the insurer share alone was less than or equal to total cost (coverage ratio ≤ 1.00 in all instances), and computing OOP as total cost minus insurer share alone yielded non-negative values without exception (minimum OOP = Int$ 0.00).

The Preferential Currency Subsidy is a supply-side transfer disbursed by the government directly to manufacturers or importers of designated medicines to reduce their procurement price, prior to NHI reimbursement calculation. It is not an additional payment made on behalf of the patient at the point of dispensing, and it does not further reduce the patient’s bill beyond what is captured in the NHI insurer share. The FX subsidy is retained as an independent analytical variable characterizing the degree of government supply-side support, which is itself a policy-relevant equity dimension: among the 88,565 clean records, 74,976 (84.7%) involved FX-subsidized drugs, which had substantially higher mean total costs (Int$ 10.92 vs. Int$ 4.36 PPP for non-subsidized drugs) but markedly lower mean patient OOP expenditure (Int$ 1.51 vs. Int$ 3.78 PPP), reflecting higher NHI coverage of imported high-cost medicines (mean coverage ratio 0.80 vs. 0.13; Supplementary Table S5). This definition also resolves an endogeneity problem affecting the marginal cost proxy: by deriving c as total cost per item—independent of the coverage ratio—any circularity between the cost parameter and the optimal coverage formula is eliminated (see Feature Engineering section).

Exclusion steps

Steps 4.1–4.3 screened for structural completeness. No records were missing a parseable prescription date, core financial field, drug name, or patient identifier, confirming the internal consistency of the NHI administrative records. Step 4.4 removed 1,069 records (1.0%) with total prescription cost ≤ 0, for which a unit price is undefined. One record was removed at step 4.7 where the insurer’s share alone exceeded total cost, a logical impossibility equivalent to a coverage ratio greater than 1.00. Step 4.9 removed 12 records with missing or implausible patient ages (outside 0–110 years), and step 4.10 removed 57 exact duplicates identified by simultaneous agreement across patient identifier, drug name, date, total cost, insurer share, and number of items.

Step 4.11 applied an IQR×1.5 rule simultaneously to total prescription cost and derived unit price (total cost / number of items), removing 15,505 records (14.8% of records entering this step) with extreme financial values. The upper fence corresponded to Int$ 66.49 PPP per prescription and per item. The final analysis dataset comprised 88,565 claims (84.2% of the raw file; Table 1). A sensitivity analysis using an IQR×3.0 threshold (upper fence: Int$ 105.42 PPP) retained 95,671 records and yielded qualitatively identical findings for all primary outcomes (Supplementary Table S4).

Comparison of excluded and retained records

To assess whether statistical outlier exclusion (step 4.11) introduced systematic bias, key financial and sociodemographic characteristics were compared between the 15,505 excluded records and the 88,565 retained records (Supplementary Tables S1–S2). Excluded records had substantially higher mean total prescription costs (Int$ 214.20 vs. Int$ 9.92 PPP; median Int$ 106.94 vs. Int$ 3.09 PPP), confirming that exclusion targeted extreme-value claims rather than specific patient groups. The gender distribution was closely comparable (female: 46.1% retained vs. 43.8% excluded). Insurance fund composition was similarly balanced across groups, with government employee fund claims comprising 40.5% of retained and 41.1% of excluded records, villager fund claims 27.7% and 27.6% respectively, and Iranians fund 18.3% and 21.5%. The proportion of records from ‘other’ fund categories was 13.0% retained vs. 9.4% excluded. No insurance type or province was systematically over-represented among excluded records in a manner that would bias the equity analysis. Mean patient age was nearly identical (63.0 years retained vs. 61.9 years excluded). These findings support the conclusion that step 4.11 exclusion was driven by extreme financial values and not by patient demographic or fund-type characteristics.

Variables

Initial (raw) features

These variables provide comprehensive information on prescription demographics, clinical context, and the three financial flows recorded by the NHI system for each claim: the total prescription cost, the insurer’s reimbursement share, and the Preferential Currency Subsidy. Feature engineering

Fourteen features were constructed from the raw variables across two levels: prescription-level transformations applied to each claim, and drug-level aggregations pooling all claims for a given pharmaceutical during 2023. Unique drugs were identified by the drug_name field, which corresponds to a specific product and dosage form as registered in the NHI formulary. All engineered features, their exact formulas, and their roles in the welfare model are presented in Table 3.

Diagnostic analyses preceding feature construction

Before constructing features, two diagnostics were conducted. First, pairwise Pearson correlations were computed among the raw financial variables at the claim level (n = 88,565) to assess the stability of inputs to the derived features (Supplementary Table S6). The three financial variables — total cost, insurer share, and FX subsidy — were highly intercorrelated (r = 0.869–0.959), reflecting Iran’s formulary-based NHI policy under which higher-cost drugs receive proportionally higher insurer shares and FX subsidies by design. The derived ratio features (ri = insurer share / total cost; FX ratio = FX subsidy / total cost) normalise this correlation; no unexpected collinearity was detected that would bias feature construction. Second, the within-drug coefficient of variation (CV = SD/mean) of total cost was computed across all claims for each drug to assess the stability of drug-level parameter estimates (Supplementary Table S8). 41% of drugs had CV = 0 (single-claim or uniform pricing); the median CV across all drugs was 0.25, indicating moderate price dispersion. Drugs with high CV (CV ≥ 0.50; 27.9% of drugs) have less precise parameter estimates, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

Following drug-level feature construction, the independence of the corrected marginal cost proxy c from the insurer coverage ratio r was verified empirically. The Pearson correlation between c and r across all 7,977 drugs was r = 0.164 (Spearman ρ = 0.177), confirming that the corrected c is effectively independent of r (|r| < 0.20) and that the endogeneity present in the original definition has been resolved.

Key model parameters

Marginal cost proxy (c)

The original definition, c = Median(OOP / n_items), was endogenous because OOP = total cost × (1 − r) when n_items = 1, making c a direct function of r. This introduced circularity into the optimal coverage formula, which also contains r. The corrected definition, c = P25(total cost) per drug, derives from the supply side (total prescription cost before any insurance deduction) and is statistically independent of r. The 25th percentile was chosen over the median because it serves as a conservative lower-bound cost proxy — interpretable as the price level at which the cheapest quarter of that drug’s prescriptions fall — and because it guarantees the ordering c ≤ p for all drugs by the mathematical property P25 ≤ P50 (verified: zero violations). Under the corrected definition, no drug has a negative c value (quality check confirmed).

Average unit price (p)

The original definition, p = Σtotal cost / Σn_items (weighted mean), was revised to p = Median(total cost) per drug. The weighted mean definition produced 1,068 drugs (13.4%) with p < c, a violation of the theoretical requirement that the market price exceeds the lower-bound cost proxy, arising because right-skewed price distributions produce mean < median. The median is robust to such skewness; for single-claim drugs the two definitions yield identical values. The ordering p ≥ c is now structurally guaranteed for all drugs (zero violations verified).

Willingness-to-pay scale (η)

The original definition, η = Median(unit price), produced η ≈ p (as both used the same formula), collapsing the demand function q = 1 − (1 − r · p) / η into a near-mechanical expression. The corrected definition, η = Max(total cost) per drug, represents the revealed-preference ceiling price — the highest cost at which the NHI system has observed a transaction for this drug — and is genuinely distinct from p (the median price). By the property max ≥ median, the ordering η ≥ p is structurally guaranteed for all drugs (zero violations verified). In 59.5% of drugs η > p (demand function strictly valid); in the remaining 40.5% (single-claim or uniform-price drugs), η = p and the demand function is evaluated at its boundary condition, acknowledged as a limitation.

Structural correlation among price parameters

Because n_items = 1 universally, all three price parameters (p, c, η) are order statistics of the same underlying variable (total cost) for each drug, producing high pairwise correlations (r = 0.865–0.983; Supplementary Table S7). This structural dependence is an irreducible consequence of the NHI database design and is acknowledged as a limitation. It does not affect model validity because p, c, and η enter different sub-formulas of the Westerhout-Folmer framework and are not treated as simultaneous regressors in any estimation equation.

Chronic flag and equity grouping

The chronic_flag variable is derived from the drug_type field of the official NHI drug formulary, which classifies each reimbursable pharmaceutical as Chronic (approved for continuous treatment of more than 90 days) or Acute based on the Ministry of Health approved indication list. This is a drug-formulary-level classification and does not vary within a drug across claims (zero inconsistencies detected across 7,977 drugs). Dual-indication drugs are assigned to the primary NHI-approved indication. Of 7,977 unique drugs, 3,464 (43.4%) were classified as Chronic, 3,257 (40.8%) as Acute, and 679 (8.5%) remained unclassifiable (drug_type values of ‘TAB’ or missing) and were excluded from equity subgroup analyses.

The four equity subgroups were formed by crossing the chronic_flag with the is_high_cost_drug indicator (p ≥ 90th percentile of the drug-level p distribution; threshold: Int$ 27.55 PPP), yielding: Low-cost Acute (n = 3,257 drugs, 46,883 claims), High-cost Acute (n = 211, 5,195 claims), Low-cost Chronic (n = 3,464, 30,624 claims), and High-cost Chronic (n = 366, 4,233 claims). These four groups operationalise chronicity and drug costliness as equity axes in the absence of individual income data in NHI administrative records. Descriptive statistics for all model parameters by equity group are presented in Supplementary Table.

Methodology

Conceptual foundation: welfare-theoretic framework

This study is grounded in the welfare-theoretic model of optimal pharmaceutical coverage and pricing developed by Westerhout and Folmer [26]. Their framework addresses the central policy dilemma of how a health insurer should set reimbursement rates and prices in the presence of efficiency constraints and equity concerns. While this model is presented as a working paper and has not yet appeared in a peer-reviewed journal, its theoretical structure is fully consistent with — and directly extends — the canonical health insurance economics literature originating with Arrow and Zeckhauser [27, 28]. Arrow established the fundamental role of uncertainty and moral hazard in the demand for health insurance; Zeckhauser formalised the welfare trade-off between financial risk protection and the distortionary cost of coverage. The Westerhout-Folmer framework extends these insights to a pharmaceutical market setting, incorporating equity considerations that canonical models abstract away.

The framework evaluates pharmaceutical markets through three interacting components: consumer surplus (CS), representing the welfare gain to insured patients from financial risk protection; supply-side surplus (SS), capturing the net return to manufacturers and dispensers that sustains the supply of medicines; and insurer expenditure (IE), reflecting the fiscal burden borne by the NHI. Social welfare is defined as the sum of these three components, with insurer expenditure weighted by the factor (1 + λ), where λ is the social cost of public funds. This weighting reflects the economic principle that each unit of government expenditure costs society more than one unit in social terms because public funds are raised through distortionary taxation. The parameter λ therefore introduces a fiscal efficiency constraint into the equity-oriented coverage optimisation.

A distinctive feature of the Westerhout-Folmer model is the explicit incorporation of vertical equity into pharmaceutical pricing. In the original formulation, equity was defined along an income dimension, such that patients with lower ability to pay were entitled to higher coverage ratios. This generates second-best solutions for drug prices and coverage ratios that balance efficiency (minimising distortions to supply and demand) with equity (providing greater protection to the most vulnerable).

Adapting the framework to Iran’s institutional context

Applying this framework to Iran’s outpatient drug sector requires a conceptual adaptation on two dimensions. First, Iran’s NHI claims database does not record individual patient income, precluding the use of income-based vertical equity analysis. Second, empirical evidence from Iranian household surveys and catastrophic expenditure studies consistently identifies drug cost and treatment chronicity — rather than income rank alone — as the primary drivers of pharmaceutical financial vulnerability [17–19]. Patients using high-cost imported biologics or long-term chronic medications face recurrent and potentially catastrophic out-of-pocket expenditures regardless of their nominal income level.

We therefore redefine the vertical equity dimension along two observable, policy-relevant axes: the costliness of the drug (distinguishing high-cost from low-cost therapies, operationalised as unit price at or above the 90th percentile of the drug-level price distribution) and the chronicity of treatment (distinguishing chronic from acute therapies based on the NHI official drug formulary classification). This operationalisation is consistent with the foundational definition of vertical equity by Culyer and Wagstaff [7]— ‘unequal treatment of unequals’ — applied to clinical and financial need in a data-limited setting where income is unavailable. It is also aligned with WHO’s financial protection framework and World Bank guidance on catastrophic expenditure monitoring, both of which emphasise need-based vulnerability rather than income rank as the criterion for prioritising insurance protection [20, 21].

Under this adaptation, vertical equity implies that drugs which impose greater clinical or financial burden — specifically, chronic or high-cost therapies — should receive relatively higher insurance coverage compared with acute or low-cost medicines. This re-interpretation extends the Westerhout-Folmer model beyond its original income focus while preserving its welfare-theoretic structure. The framework generates second-best optimal coverage ratios and prices for each drug, with the equity-adjusted optimal solutions reflecting the higher protection warranted for costlier and chronic therapies.

Welfare-theoretic formulas

For each drug d, we implement the following sequence of formulas from the Westerhout-Folmer framework. All cost-based quantities are expressed in 2023 international dollars, PPP-adjusted (World Bank conversion factor: 118,411.24 Iranian Rials per Int$). Parameters are defined as follows:

p = median unit price across all claims for drug d (observed market price proxy); r = drug-level insurer coverage ratio; c = 25th percentile of unit prices for drug d (lower-quartile cost proxy, supply-side, independent of r); η = maximum unit price for drug d (revealed-preference willingness-to-pay ceiling); q = empirical demand share (distinct patients / total patients); λ = social cost of public funds; F = fixed cost of pharmaceutical provision.

Observed demand

The observed demand implied by the current coverage and price structure is:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q\_d = 1 - \left({1 - r\_d*p\_d} \right)/\eta \_d$$\end{document}

This expression estimates the demand share consistent with the observed insurance generosity r and market price p. When η = p (which applies to 40.5% of drugs — those with a single claim or uniform pricing during 2023), the formula simplifies to q = r, meaning demand equals the coverage ratio at the boundary condition. This is mathematically well-defined and economically interpretable: when the highest observed price equals the median price, demand is entirely determined by insurance generosity.

Optimal price (second-best)

The welfare-optimal drug price is derived by maximizing social welfare subject to the supply-side surplus constraint (ensuring manufacturers remain willing to supply). The second-best optimal price is:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eqalign{ \tilde p\_d = & c\_d + \left({\eta \_d \cdot F \cdot \left({1 + 2\lambda } \right)} \right) \cr & /\left({\left({\eta \_d - c\_d} \right) \cdot \left({1 + \lambda } \right)} \right) \cr}$$\end{document}

The optimal price exceeds marginal cost c by a mark-up that reflects the need to cover fixed supply costs F and the social cost of funds λ. Higher λ implies a larger mark-up because recovering fixed costs through prices is socially less costly than recovering them through public subsidy. When η = c (extremely rare: drugs where the maximum and minimum observed price are identical), the formula is undefined and the observed price p is retained. Computed values of p̃ < 0 (which arise when fixed costs are very low relative to the price-cost margin) are bounded at zero.

Optimal coverage ratio (second-best)

The welfare-optimal coverage ratio is derived by maximizing social welfare subject to the demand function and the supply-side surplus constraint:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eqalign{ \tilde r\_d = & \left[ {\eta \_d \cdot F \cdot {{\left({1 + 2\lambda } \right)}^{\rm{2}}}} \right. \cr & \left. { - {{\left({\eta \_d - c\_d} \right)}^{\rm{2}}} \cdot \lambda \cdot \left({1 + \lambda } \right)} \right] \cr & /\left[ {\left({1 + 2\lambda } \right)} \right. \cdot {\rm{(}}\eta \_d \cdot F \cdot \left({1 + 2\lambda } \right) \cr & \left. { + c\_d \cdot \left({\eta \_d - c\_d} \right) \cdot \left({1 + \lambda } \right))} \right] \cr} $$\end{document}

Computed values of r̃ are bounded to [0, 1]: values below zero (implying negative coverage, economically impossible) are set to zero, and values above one (implying more than full coverage) are set to one. The proportion of drugs requiring each type of bounding is reported in the results.

Optimal demand

Optimal demand is derived by substituting the optimal price and coverage into the demand function:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eqalign{ \tilde q\_d & = 1 - \left({1 - \tilde r\_d \cdot \tilde p\_d} \right) \cr & /\eta \_d \cr} $$\end{document}

As with observed demand, this expression is algebraically determined by the model’s structural assumptions and is bounded to [0, 1]. The demand gap (q̃ − q) reflects the change in utilisation implied by moving from observed to optimal policy. It is a model-structural quantity — algebraically equal to (r̃·p̃ − r·p) / η — and does not constitute an independent empirical finding about unmet need.

Consumer surplus

Consumer surplus measures patient welfare as the area under the demand curve above the out-of-pocket price. Under observed and optimal coverage, respectively:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eqalign{ CS\_d & = {\left({\eta \_d - \left({1 - r\_d} \right) \cdot p\_d} \right)^{\rm{2}}} \cr & /\left({2\cdot\eta \_d} \right) \cr} $$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eqalign{ C\tilde S\_d & = {\left({\eta \_d - \left({1 - \tilde r\_d} \right) \cdot \tilde p\_d} \right)^{\rm{2}}} \cr & /\left({2\cdot\eta \_d} \right) \cr} $$\end{document}

Supply-side surplus

Supply-side surplus captures the net financial return to manufacturers and dispensers after covering variable and fixed costs. We use this term in preference to ‘firm profit’ throughout because Iran’s NHI operates as a social insurance system in which supply-side actors include public dispensaries and public hospitals for which profit maximisation is not the primary objective:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$SS\_d = \left({p\_d - c\_d} \right) \cdot q\_d - F$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S\tilde S\_d = \left({\tilde p\_d - c\_d} \right) \cdot \tilde q\_d - F$$\end{document}

Negative supply-side surplus values are retained in the analysis because they are economically meaningful: they indicate that, for some drugs, the observed or optimal price does not cover the estimated cost floor plus fixed supply costs. Such drugs could not be sustainably supplied in a profit-constrained environment, which is itself a policy-relevant equity finding.

Insurer expenditure

Insurer expenditure captures the fiscal cost to the NHI of reimbursing each drug under observed and optimal scenarios:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$IE\_d = r\_d \cdot p\_d \cdot q\_d$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde IE\_d = \tilde r\_d \cdot \tilde p\_d\, \cdot \,\tilde q\_d$$\end{document}

Because Iran’s NHI is a public social insurer rather than a commercial entity, the gap between insurer expenditure and premium revenue (IE − premium income) is not treated as a ‘loss’ but as an expenditure shortfall normally covered by government fiscal transfers. This distinction is important for interpreting the policy implications of the model results.

Total social welfare

Total social welfare consolidates all three components, with insurer expenditure penalised by the social cost of funds:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W\_d = CS\_d + SS\_d + \left({1 + \lambda } \right) \cdot IE\_d$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde W\_d = C\tilde S\_d + S\tilde S\_d + \left({1 + \lambda } \right) \cdot I\tilde E\_d$$\end{document}

The (1 + λ) multiplier on insurer expenditure encodes the key efficiency–equity trade-off in the model: expanding coverage raises consumer surplus but also raises public expenditure, whose true social cost exceeds its face value by λ. The welfare gap (W̃ − W) measures the net welfare change from moving from observed to optimal policy. Welfare figures are reported on a per-drug basis (Int$ PPP per drug) and on a per-claim basis (Int$ PPP per prescription). Aggregate welfare summed across all drug-observations is not reported as a policy-relevant quantity because it accumulates the model’s structural assumptions across thousands of drugs and produces figures that are benchmarks of the model’s internal accounting, not estimates of Iran’s total pharmaceutical welfare or fiscal gap.

Parameter calibration and sensitivity analysis

λ (Social cost of public funds)

The social cost of public funds measures the distortionary cost of raising one additional unit of government revenue through taxation. Empirical estimates for middle-income countries typically range from 0.10 to 0.30 [cite relevant source], reflecting moderate efficiency costs of tax collection. For Iran, which operates under international sanctions, sustained high inflation, and significant fiscal constraints, the social cost of public funds is likely toward the upper end of or above this range. We adopt a baseline value of λ = 0.20, consistent with estimates for comparable lower-middle-income contexts, and conduct sensitivity analyses at λ = 0.10, 0.30, and 0.50. The upper bound of 0.50 reflects Iran’s more constrained fiscal environment and tests whether qualitative conclusions are robust to substantially higher public fund costs.

F (Fixed cost of provision)

Fixed costs of pharmaceutical provision include regulatory, distribution, and inventory costs that do not vary with the volume of a specific drug dispensed. True fixed costs are not observable in claims data. We calibrate F scenarios as a percentage of the median observed drug revenue: a low scenario (F ≈ 5% of median revenue), a mid scenario (F ≈ 10%), and a high scenario (F ≈ 20%). These are computed dynamically from the observed distribution of drug-level total costs and are expressed in PPP-adjusted international dollars in all sensitivity tables. The F values correspond approximately to the Rial-level scenarios used in the original analysis (approximately Int$ 0.02, 0.05, and 0.10 PPP respectively), reframed relative to the observed drug revenue distribution rather than as arbitrary absolute values.

For each combination of λ and F, we compute the distribution of optimal prices, coverage ratios, consumer surplus, insurer expenditure, and total welfare across all drugs and within each equity group. Descriptive statistics (mean, median, standard deviation, 10th and 90th percentiles) are reported. This approach makes the sensitivity of results to calibration assumptions transparent and allows readers to assess the robustness of the equity findings.

Equity grouping and comparative analysis

To translate the welfare results into an equity narrative, drugs are classified into four subgroups by crossing the chronic_flag with the high-cost indicator (p ≥ 90th percentile of the drug-level price distribution, threshold: Int$ 27.55 PPP): Low-cost Acute (n = 3,257 drugs), High-cost Acute (n = 211), Low-cost Chronic (n = 3,464), and High-cost Chronic (n = 366). The 679 drugs with unclassifiable drug type are excluded from equity subgroup analyses. The 90th percentile threshold for the high-cost classification is consistent with approaches used in health expenditure analyses to identify the upper tail of skewed cost distributions [17–19], and anchors the cut-off in the empirical distribution of Iran’s NHI data rather than an arbitrary absolute value.

Vertical equity is assessed by comparing the observed and optimal coverage ratios across the four groups. The adapted equity principle implies that the optimal model should recommend higher coverage for chronic and high-cost drugs than for acute and low-cost drugs, holding all else equal. The magnitude of the coverage gap (r̃ − r) across groups provides the primary equity metric. Consumer surplus gains, insurer expenditure changes, and welfare gaps are presented for each group to characterise the efficiency–equity trade-offs associated with moving toward optimal coverage.

Methodological limitations

Several limitations of the welfare model warrant explicit acknowledgement. First, the price parameters p, c, and η all derive from the same underlying variable (total cost per claim) because the NHI database records exactly one item per prescription line (n_items = 1 universally). This structural feature means that the three parameters are statistically correlated (r = 0.865–0.983), which is an irreducible database-level limitation. It does not affect model validity because the parameters enter different sub-formulas and are not estimated simultaneously, but it limits the conceptual independence of p, c, and η as distinct theoretical constructs.

Second, c proxies marginal cost using the 25th percentile of observed prescription prices — a supply-side quantity independent of the coverage ratio by construction (Pearson r (c, r) = 0.164) — rather than true production costs, which are unavailable from administrative claims data in any health system. The assumption that the lower-quartile observed price is proportional to marginal cost across drugs is standard in pharmaceutical insurance modelling but remains an approximation.

Third, for 40.5% of drugs, η = p (single-claim or uniform-price drugs), placing the demand function at its boundary condition. For these drugs, demand equals the coverage ratio (q = r), which remains economically interpretable but limits the model’s ability to differentiate demand sensitivity across price levels for this subset.

Fourth, the calibration of λ and F involves assumptions that are not directly observable from claims data. The sensitivity analyses described above are designed to quantify the extent to which policy conclusions depend on these assumptions. All model results should be interpreted as welfare benchmarks under the specified assumptions rather than as point estimates of actual optimal policy.

Table 4 reveals several patterns that are central to the equity analysis. The mean patient age of 63.0 years (median 64) confirms that Iran’s NHI outpatient drug claimant population is predominantly older, reflecting the country’s epidemiological transition toward chronic non-communicable diseases as the dominant health burden. The near-identical mean and median for age (63.0 vs. 64) indicates a symmetrically distributed age variable with limited skewness.

Prescription costs were markedly right-skewed across all financial variables. For total cost, the mean (Int$ 9.92 PPP) was more than three times the median (Int$ 3.09), signalling that a small number of high-cost prescriptions drove aggregate expenditure while the typical claim was modest in absolute terms. This pattern repeated for insurer share (mean Int$ 8.06 vs. median Int$ 1.82) and FX subsidy (mean Int$ 4.24 vs. median Int$ 0.85), indicating that high-cost imported medicines accounted for a disproportionate share of both insurer reimbursement and government supply-side support.

The coverage ratio distribution is particularly informative for the equity analysis. The mean coverage ratio of 0.68 and median of 0.80 indicate that the distribution is left-skewed: the majority of prescriptions received generous reimbursement (median 80%), but a substantial minority — visible in the P10 of 0.00 — received no insurer support at all. This bimodal structure suggests that NHI coverage was not uniformly distributed across prescriptions, with some claims fully covered and others entirely out-of-pocket.

OOP expenditure (mean Int$ 1.86, median Int$ 0.66) was low in absolute terms but highly skewed, with a P10 of Int$ 0.12 and a maximum of Int$ 65.37 — nearly equal to the maximum total cost. This indicates that while most patients paid relatively little out of pocket, a small subset bore the full cost of their prescriptions without insurer support. The FX subsidy ratio (mean 0.32, median 0.32) was notably symmetric, suggesting a consistent policy application of the preferential currency mechanism across the mid-range of the cost distribution.

Discussion

This study applied the Westerhout-Folmer welfare-theoretic framework to Iran’s NHI outpatient pharmaceutical sector, adapting its equity dimension from income to two administratively observable proxies for patient vulnerability: drug costliness and treatment chronicity. Across 88,565 prescription claims covering 7,977 unique drugs from Iran’s 2023 NHI database, the analysis produced four principal findings. First, the observed coverage structure already disadvantages the group with the greatest clinical vulnerability: Low-cost Chronic drugs had the lowest mean coverage ratio of all four groups (0.493), lower even than Low-cost Acute drugs (0.544), despite the greater long-term financial exposure associated with sustained chronic medication use. Second, the welfare model recommended higher coverage for every equity group, with the largest recommended increases concentrated in the chronic categories: +0.279 for Low-cost Chronic drugs and + 0.081 for High-cost Chronic drugs. Third, these directional findings were entirely stable across all 12 combinations of the social cost of funds parameter (λ = 0.10–0.50) and fixed provision cost (F = Int$ 55.05–220.20 PPP), with zero drugs requiring bounding of optimal prices or coverage ratios in any scenario. Fourth, the welfare gaps were positive for all groups, confirming that the second-best optimal policy yields higher model-implied total social welfare than the status quo in each equity category.

The descriptive finding that Low-cost Chronic drugs are under-covered relative to acute drugs of comparable cost is particularly striking because it contradicts the assumption that NHI formulary design broadly protects the most vulnerable. Chronic disease patients in Iran face a double disadvantage: they require medications indefinitely, generating recurrent OOP payments even at modest per-prescription cost, yet they receive lower average insurer coverage (mean 0.493) than patients filling acute prescriptions at similar unit prices. This structural inequity is reinforced by the age profile of the Low-cost Chronic group, whose patients are on average 64.1 years old — the oldest of any group — suggesting that the population bearing the highest cumulative medication burden also faces the lowest rate of reimbursement. These findings align with the principle of vertical equity articulated by Culyer and Wagstaff [7], which holds that unequal treatment is justified when based on differences in need, and with Iran’s own catastrophic spending evidence: a meta-analysis found that while the population-level rate of catastrophic health expenditure is approximately 4.7%, it rises to 25.3% among cancer patients and even higher among dialysis patients, suggesting that chronic pharmaceutical users face financial exposure far exceeding the administrative coverage ratios would imply [29].

The welfare model’s recommendation of the largest coverage increases for Low-cost Chronic drugs is consistent with this pattern. From the perspective of the Westerhout-Folmer framework, the social welfare gain from expanding coverage is largest where the current coverage gap is widest and where the demand for the drug is sustained over time — precisely the conditions that define the low-cost chronic segment. This finding echoes Glied and Frank, who demonstrated that targeted coverage expansion for a clinically vulnerable subgroup — mental health drugs under parity legislation in the United States — significantly reduced financial disparities in access without generating the utilisation distortions predicted by standard moral hazard theory [30]. The applicability of this parallel to Iran’s chronic drug segment is direct: if low-cost chronic medicines are already associated with modest per-unit prices and high price inelasticity (given clinical necessity), expanding their coverage will primarily reduce OOP burden rather than induce excessive utilisation.

For High-cost Acute and High-cost Chronic drugs, the model recommended smaller but positive coverage increases (+ 0.032 and + 0.081 respectively). This reflects the formulary’s existing cost-tier policy, which already provides substantially higher reimbursement for expensive medicines (mean observed rd of 0.790 and 0.699 respectively). The welfare model endorses the directional logic of this policy but suggests it does not go far enough: even at the highest observed coverage levels, there remains a meaningful gap between observed and welfare-optimal coverage. The FX subsidy ratio was highest in the High-cost Chronic group (0.566), indicating that the government’s supply-side preferential currency mechanism disproportionately supports this segment — an instrument whose efficiency depends on whether manufacturer cost reductions are passed through to patients rather than captured as rents. This concern is broadly analogous to the welfare losses from mispriced insurance contracts documented by Bundorf, Levin, and Mahoney [31] and the distortions from coverage design studied by Einav, Finkelstein, and Cullen [32], though the structural mechanism in Iran’s NHI — a formulary-based social insurer — differs fundamentally from the competitive US markets analysed in those studies.

The welfare gap analysis requires careful interpretation. Mean welfare gaps per drug were large (Int$ +1,051 for High-cost Acute to Int$ +94,684 for Low-cost Chronic), driven primarily by a small subset of drugs with very high maximum unit prices (ηd) generating high optimal prices under the model. Median welfare gaps (Int$ +308 to + 436 per drug) are substantially lower and provide a more robust representation of the typical drug’s welfare benefit under optimal policy. These figures should be understood as model-internal welfare benchmarks under the specified calibration of λ and F, not as fiscal cost projections. The negative observed total welfare Wd for all groups reflects that the calibrated fixed provision cost F (Int$ 110.10 PPP) exceeds the observed price-cost margin for most drugs — a structural feature of second-best welfare accounting under constrained pricing, analogous to the sustainability challenge identified by Ghili et al. in dynamic insurance contracting contexts, where balancing patient financial protection against supply-side solvency requires explicit optimisation rather than uniform pricing rules [33].

From a comparative perspective, the optimal mean coverage ratios recommended by the model (0.773–0.804 for the two largest groups) are consistent with coverage generosity levels observed in comparable middle-income social insurance systems that have explicitly prioritised chronic disease protection. Thailand’s Universal Coverage Scheme, which adopted a National List of Essential Medicines with explicit prioritisation of NCD therapies, achieved comparable effective coverage rates for chronic medications while containing pharmaceutical budgets through price negotiation and volume-based purchasing [32, 33]. Isaiah Luc et al. (2026). similarly differentiated pharmaceutical reimbursement by indication type, directing higher coverage toward chronic and high-cost therapies under explicit equity mandates [34]. Van de Ven and colleagues demonstrated that sustaining equity in insurance markets under budget constraints requires both explicit risk adjustment and structural differentiation of coverage by need [35] — precisely the mechanism this study operationalises through the Westerhout-Folmer framework. These comparators suggest that the directional shift recommended by the welfare model — higher coverage for chronic drugs, particularly low-cost chronic medicines — is both theoretically grounded and empirically feasible within a social insurance architecture comparable to Iran’s NHI.

Policy implications

For Iran’s NHI, the results support three specific, results-grounded policy directions. First, the most immediate and tractable implication is a targeted reimbursement rate increase for Low-cost Chronic medicines. The observed mean coverage ratio of 0.493 for this group represents the largest absolute gap from the model’s optimal recommendation (+ 0.279) and involves the largest number of unique drugs and the highest patient age. Raising the reimbursement rate for this category from the current approximately 49% toward 75–80% of total cost would align reimbursement practice more closely with the welfare-theoretic benchmark and directly reduce recurrent OOP burdens for older patients managing chronic conditions. This adjustment does not require structural reform of the NHI benefit architecture — it is an administrative reimbursement rate reclassification within existing formulary categories.

Second, the results reveal that the current reliance on the Preferential Currency Subsidy as the primary tool for protecting High-cost Chronic patients is inadequate as a financial protection instrument for patients. The FX subsidy is a supply-side transfer to manufacturers; its patient-level impact depends on pass-through of cost reductions into prices, which the NHI cannot guarantee. A complementary patient-facing instrument — such as an OOP cap or an expanded coverage ratio — would provide more direct and verifiable financial protection for the 366 high-cost chronic drugs identified in this study. The welfare gap per claim for High-cost Chronic drugs was Int$ +1,286 PPP under baseline calibration, indicating meaningful scope for welfare improvement at the prescription level.

Third, the sensitivity analysis demonstrates that the directional equity recommendations are stable across a wide range of λ values (0.10–0.50). This robustness finding has direct policy relevance in Iran’s constrained fiscal environment: even under the assumption that the social cost of public funds is as high as 0.50 — reflecting a severely constrained public budget — the model continues to recommend higher coverage for all equity groups, and particularly for chronic medicines. Policymakers cannot use fiscal constraints as a justification for maintaining the status quo coverage structure, since the model’s recommendations hold even under conservative assumptions about the opportunity cost of NHI expenditure. For comparable LMICs operating NHI systems with limited fiscal space and high chronic disease burdens, the methodological approach demonstrated here — applying a welfare-theoretic equity framework to administrative claims data using drug cost and chronicity as equity axes — provides a replicable template for evidence-based pharmaceutical benefit design that does not require individual income data.

Limitations and future research

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study.

First, the key drug-level parameters (pd, cd, ηd) used in the analysis are derived from a common underlying variable—total cost per claim—reflecting the structure of the NHI database, in which each prescription line contains a single recorded item. While this does not compromise the internal validity of the model, it implies that these parameters represent different statistical transformations of the same distribution and are therefore inherently correlated. In the absence of direct production cost data, the proxy used for supply-side costs represents a pragmatic but imperfect approximation.

Second, for a subset of drugs with limited variation in observed prices, the demand function operates at its boundary condition. Although these cases are methodologically tractable within the model, the associated results should be interpreted with additional caution due to limited price dispersion.

Third, the welfare analysis is based on the Westerhout–Folmer framework, which, while theoretically consistent with established optimal insurance models, has not yet undergone extensive empirical validation across diverse healthcare settings. This limits the extent to which the findings can be generalized beyond the current context without further external validation.

Fourth, the exclusion of a portion of raw claims during data preprocessing, although systematically documented, introduces the possibility of selection bias if excluded observations are non-randomly distributed across cost or chronicity categories.

Finally, the equity framework is constructed at the drug level rather than the patient level. As a result, classification may not fully capture variation in clinical use, particularly for medicines with multiple indications or cases where chronic medications are prescribed for acute conditions.

Future research should build on these findings in three key directions. Linking administrative claims data with household-level socioeconomic information would enable direct validation of the extent to which drug cost and chronicity serve as effective proxies for patient vulnerability. Extending the model to a dynamic framework that incorporates pricing negotiations, treatment trajectories, and long-term budget constraints would enhance its relevance for policy planning. Finally, applying the same analytical approach to comparable health insurance systems in other low- and middle-income countries would allow cross-country comparisons and support the development of coordinated pharmaceutical policies at the regional level.

Conclusion

This study provides the first formal welfare-theoretic analysis of vertical equity in pharmaceutical coverage using drug cost and chronicity as equity axes within the Westerhout-Folmer optimal coverage framework, applied to Iran’s NHI outpatient drug market. The results demonstrate that Iran’s current reimbursement structure systematically under-protects the most clinically vulnerable segment — patients relying on low-cost chronic medications — while the welfare model’s second-best optimal solution robustly recommends higher coverage for all drug categories, with the largest improvements concentrated precisely where observed coverage is lowest. The median coverage gap for Low-cost Chronic drugs, stable across all 12 sensitivity scenarios and consistent with zero model bounding violations, represents a well-identified, actionable target for NHI benefit design reform. Conditional on the model’s assumptions regarding the social cost of public funds and fixed provision costs — and acknowledging the inherent limitations of administrative cost proxies for theoretical welfare parameters — these findings offer a principled, empirically grounded, and replicable framework for equity-oriented pharmaceutical reimbursement reform in Iran and comparable middle-income social insurance systems where income data are structurally unavailable but clinical vulnerability is measurable through the characteristics of the medicines patients require.
