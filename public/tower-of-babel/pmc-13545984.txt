Evaluation of mosquito transmission of equine hepacivirus in laboratory and field studies
Authors: Tereza Magalhaes, Danilo de Carvalho Leandro, Emrah Ozel, Xiao Liang, Brandon Lyons, Troels K H Scheel, Jordan Salomon, Daniela Luethy, Phillip E Kaufman, Joy E Tomlinson, William Reisen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEquine hepacivirus (EqHV) is a hepatotropic virus, closely related to human hepatitis C virus (HCV), that can cause acute or chronic infection in horses. As with HCV, transmission is thought to occur primarily through parenteral, particularly blood-borne, routes. Vertical transmission from mare to foal has been reported but appears to be uncommon. The high seroprevalence in horse populations and the occurrence of infection in isolated horses indicate efficient horizontal transmission routes exist. One such possibility is biological or mechanical transmission by arthropods, such as mosquitoes, stable flies, or ticks. Here, we conducted 2 complementary studies to evaluate the possible role of mosquitoes as EqHV biological vectors. In the laboratory, we orally exposed Aedes albopictus (Skuse) females to EqHV through artificial bloodfeeding with blood collected from 2 chronically infected horses. Mosquito samples (body, legs, and saliva) were collected at multiple time points post-exposure and tested for EqHV RNA by molecular assays. In the field, 2,138 mosquitoes were collected from New York and Florida, United States, and screened for EqHV RNA. No EqHV RNA was detected in any samples from the laboratory experiment or among field-collected mosquitoes. These findings indicate that the Ae. albopictus strain evaluated here is unlikely to serve as a biological vector of EqHV and corroborate previous negative findings in field-collected mosquitoes. Nevertheless, low infection rates may have gone undetected. Laboratory studies of additional horse-feeding mosquito species and expanded field collections in EqHV-endemic regions are needed to further clarify any potential role for mosquitoes in EqHV transmission.Keywords: nonprimate hepacivirus, flavivirus, Aedes albopictus, vector transmission, equine viral hepatitis, horse feeding mosquito

Introduction

Equine hepacivirus (EqHV) is a hepatotropic virus, closely related to the human hepatitis C virus (HCV), that can cause acute or chronic infection in horses (Burbelo et al. 2012, Pfaender et al. 2015, Scheel et al. 2015, Pacchiarotti et al. 2022). Seroconversion in infected animals is usually delayed, and viral clearance is accompanied by a transient increase in liver enzyme activities, consistent with immune-mediated liver damage (Pfaender et al. 2015, Tomlinson et al. 2021). Chronic infections occur in a subset of infected animals, particularly those infected as foals (Pfaender et al. 2015; Ramsay et al. 2015; Gather et al. 2016a, 2016b; Tomlinson et al. 2019). Persistent hepaciviral infection can lead to chronic hepatitis that presents as subclinical or mild hepatitis or can progress to fatal liver failure (Jager et al. 2026, Elia et al. 2017, Tegtmeyer et al. 2019, Tomlinson et al. 2019). Beyond its importance as an equine pathogen, EqHV is of interest as a surrogate model for studying HCV pathogenesis, immunity, and chronicity mechanisms (Tomlinson et al. 2021, Cavalleri et al. 2022, Kennedy et al. 2024).

EqHV is globally distributed and seroprevalence in horse populations ranges from 22.6% to 83.7% (Bezerra et al. 2022). Between 2.4% and 47.2% of animals in endemic regions have detectable viral RNA in their blood, indicating acute or persisting infection (Bezerra et al. 2022).

Parenteral transmission of EqHV has been demonstrated experimentally, and iatrogenic transmission (unintentional transmission during medical examination or treatment), which relates to the parenteral route, is thought to play a role in the spread of the virus among equine populations (Pacchiarotti et al. 2022). The fecal-oral route, another horizontal mode, is unlikely to be relevant in EqHV epidemiology (Pacchiarotti et al. 2022), and although vertical transmission from mare to foal has been documented, it appears to be uncommon (Gather et al. 2016b). The high exposure rates of horses to EqHV, represented by the high seroprevalence in equine populations, posit that other horizontal modes of EqHV transmission exist. One possibility is the involvement of hematophagous arthropods, such as mosquitoes, as biological or mechanical vectors of the virus. Indeed, EqHV transmission events in research horses isolated at least 70 yards from all other horses have been observed during seasons with active insect populations (J.E.T., unpublished data, Pfaender et al. 2015).

In this context, 2 studies evaluated the potential role of arthropods in EqHV transmission. In Badenhorst et al. (2019), approximately 5,000 mosquitoes were collected from regions of Austria and Italy, including areas endemic for EqHV, but none of the pools tested positive for EqHV RNA, indicating that mosquitoes were not involved in transmission in those areas. More recently, also in Austria, detection of EqHV RNA in stable flies [(Stomoxys calcitrans (L.)] collected from horse barns led the authors to propose a possible role for this species in the mechanical transmission of EqHV (Frisch et al. 2025).

To further understand transmission modes of EqHV, we conducted complementary laboratory and field studies on mosquitoes. In the laboratory, we exposed Aedes albopictus (Skuse), to EqHV and assessed its permissiveness to virus replication. This mosquito species was selected because it is globally distributed, susceptible to multiple flaviviruses (Garcia-Rejon et al. 2021, Bohers et al. 2024), and exhibits opportunistic feeding behavior that includes feeding on horses (Fikrig et al. 2021, Garcia-Rejon et al. 2021). In parallel, we collected mosquitoes across different regions of the United States in areas with high horse populations and evidence of EqHV circulation and screened them for the presence of EqHV RNA.

Materials and Methods

EqHV

The source of EqHV was venous blood collected in heparinized tubes in 2024 from 2 horses (KK and KW) chronically infected with the virus and shipped overnight on ice to the Texas A&M University (TAMU) laboratory. At the time of the study, KK was a 17-yr-old Thoroughbred gelding, and KW was a 16-yr-old Warmblood mare, both known to be EqHV serum RT-qPCR positive for at least 4 yr. No clinical signs of infection and no biochemical evidence of hepatitis were present in either horse. At the time of collection, KK viremia was 3.0 × 107 genome equivalents (GE)/ml serum and KW viremia was 4.6 × 105 GE/ml serum. Although KK and KW were seropositive for EqHV and anti-EqHV antibodies could potentially reduce mosquito infection, these horses were used because chronically infected horses appear to be important reservoirs for EqHV transmission among equids, and plasma from seropositive horses has been shown to infect naive horses following intravenous inoculation (J.E.T., unpublished longitudinal epidemiologic studies). Blood was also collected from an uninfected horse (JZ) and used as a negative control in the molecular assays. JZ was a 22-yr-old Quarter Horse mare seropositive for EqHV but serum RT-qPCR negative and clinically healthy.

Oral Exposure of Ae. albopictus to EqHV

Aedes albopictus eggs were collected in October 2023 in Brazos County, Texas, and brought to the Arthropod Containment Level 2 (ACL-2) facility at TAMU. Mosquitoes were maintained at 28 °C, 70% relative humidity, and a 12:12 light:dark cycle, and reared to adults using standard methodology. Adult females (F4), 4 to 6 d post-emergence, were used for the virus exposure experiment.

Fresh (never frozen), uncoagulated EqHV-infected blood from individual horses was offered to approximately 200 adult female Ae. albopictus that had been starved for 24 h. Exposed mosquitoes were maintained under the same conditions described above and provided with access to water and 10% sucrose solution. Groups of up to 20 females were collected at 7, 14, and 21 d post-exposure (dpe) and mosquito bodies, legs, and saliva were tested to determine virus infection, dissemination, and transmission rates, respectively. For that, mosquitoes were cold-anesthetized at 2 °C, and wings were removed and discarded. Legs were dissected and placed into tubes containing 250 μl of mosquito diluent (PBS with 20% FBS and antibiotic/antimycotic) and borosilicate beads. Saliva was collected at room temperature by inserting the proboscis into a glass capillary tube containing immersion oil (forced salivation). After 30 min, capillary tubes were transferred to microcentrifuge tubes containing 80 μl of mosquito diluent and centrifuged (11,000 × g, 3 min, 2 °C). Mosquito bodies were then placed into microcentrifuge tubes with 250 μl of mosquito diluent and borosilicate beads. Leg and body samples were homogenized using a TissueLyzer II (Qiagen, Germantown, Maryland, United States). Samples were processed individually, with each tube containing a single sample type (body, legs, or saliva) from one mosquito. All samples were stored at −80 °C until further use.

Field Mosquito Collection

Adult mosquitoes were collected either by our team (Gainesville, Florida), or by mosquito control district personnel in 3 US locations: Suffolk County, New York (Suffolk County Mosquito Control), Sarasota County, Florida (Sarasota County Mosquito Management Services), and St. Johns County, Florida (Anastasia Mosquito Control District). These locations were selected based on high population of horses and known circulation of EqHV (3.9% and 5.6% EqHV viremia and 42% and 52% seroprevalence in NY and FL horses, respectively; J.E.T., unpublished data).

Collections conducted by our team used gravid traps consisting of pans with water and hay infusion as attractant and fans for collection (Frommer Updraft Gravid Trap Model 1719, John W. Hock Co., Gainesville, Florida, United States), which were placed approximately monthly from May, 2023 to March, 2024 on farms with at least one known persistent EqHV-infected horse on each property. Mosquitoes were frozen at −80 °C until species identification and processing. When species-level identification was not possible, specimens were identified to genus. Mosquitoes were identified based on morphological keys (Darsie and Morris 2003, Darsie and Ward 2005) by trained TAMU staff.

Collections by the mosquito control personnel were conducted between January 2021 and August 2023 as part of routine surveillance programs, using mostly CO2-baited CDC light traps, and on a few occasions gravid traps. Specimens were held frozen at −20 °C until processed. Most samples were collected in 2023 from 8 sites in New York, each situated within ½ mile from horse farms. Mosquitoes were identified based on morphological keys (Darsie and Morris 2003, Darsie and Ward 2005) by trained individuals at the respective mosquito-control district offices or by trained TAMU staff.

RNA Extraction

For the laboratory EqHV-exposed Ae. albopictus, RNA was extracted from previously processed body, legs, and saliva samples using the Mag-Bind Viral DNA/RNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Inc., Norcross, Georgia, United States) on a KingFisher Flex System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States) with 96-deep well head, following the manufacturers’ instructions. The input sample volume was 50 µl and RNA was eluted in 50 µl of ultra-pure water.

Samples from Florida mosquito control counties were received as extracted RNA. For specimens collected by our team or received as whole specimens, female mosquitoes were pooled based on sampling location, species, and collection date which covered a 30-d sampling timeframe. These pools were then processed with Mag-Bind Total RNA 96 Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Inc., Norcross, Georgia, United States) and total RNA was eluted into 100 µl of ultra-pure water. For pools that had less than 5 mosquitoes, the original RNA extraction protocol was modified to reduce all volumes by half so the final elution volume for these samples was 50 µl.

Reverse Transcription Quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

For the molecular assay, the positive controls were RNA extracted from blood of the 2 EqHV-infected horses, whereas the negative control was RNA extracted from blood of the uninfected horse.

The following primers and probe targeting the EqHV 5′ UTR and amplifying a 160-bp fragment were used as previously described (Burbelo et al. 2012): forward primer (5′-3′)—GAGGGAGCTGRAATTCGTGAA; reverse primer (5′-3′)—GCAAGCATCCTATCAGACCGT; probe (5′-3′)—CCACGAAGG/ZEN/AAGGCGGGGGC (5′ 6-FAM/ZEN/3′ IBFQ). The iTaq Universal Probes One-Step Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, California, United States) was used for the reactions, which were run on a Bio-Rad CFX96 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, California, United States) under the following conditions: 50 °C for 10 min, 95 °C for 2 min; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Samples with cycle threshold (Ct) values ≤38 were considered positive. Using a quantified in vitro-transcribed EqHV RNA standard, the limit of detection (LOD) of the RT-qPCR was estimated to be 15 RNA copies per reaction, with Ct values ranging from 35.63 to 36.93 at the LOD. Positive and negative controls were included in each 96-well plate.

This RT-qPCR protocol was used for RNA extracted from orally EqHV-exposed Ae. albopictus and from field-collected mosquitoes.

Infection, Dissemination, and Transmission Rates

For laboratory-exposed mosquitoes, infection, dissemination, and transmission rates were calculated at each dpe as the proportion of body, leg, and saliva samples, respectively, that tested positive for EqHV by RT-qPCR. The use of body, legs, and saliva samples to assess infection, dissemination, and transmission rates is well established in vector competence studies (Wu et al. 2022).

EqHV Screening of Field-Collected Mosquitoes

To further assess whether wild-caught mosquitoes were infected with EqHV, field specimens were pooled by species, geographic region, and collection period, and subjected to molecular assays for EqHV RNA detection. A total of 2,138 mosquitoes were grouped into 384 pools containing 1 to 50 mosquitoes each. These pools represented 8 genera and at least 26 species (Table 2), most of which have been reported to feed on horses occasionally or frequently, including Aedes canadensis (Theobald), Ae. vexans (Meigen), Ae. sollicitans (Walker), Anopheles quadrimaculatus Say, Coquillettidia perturbans (Walker), and Culex nigripalpus Theobald (Edman 1971; Magnarelli 1977; Nasci and Edman 1981; Apperson et al. 2004; Molaei and Andreadis 2006; Molaei et al. 2006, 2008, 2009; Cohen et al. 2009; Navia-Gine et al. 2013).

Discussion

To perform the vector competence experiments for EqHV, Ae. albopictus was selected due to its susceptibility to several flaviviruses, opportunistic feeding habits, with horses among the vertebrate hosts it feeds on, and its global distribution (Fikrig et al. 2021, Garcia-Rejon et al. 2021, Bohers et al. 2024). Our results showed that the Ae. albopictus strain tested under the experimental conditions described here does not support EqHV replication and, therefore, is unlikely to serve as a biological vector for the virus.

In vector competence experiments, measuring the infectious virus titer in the bloodmeal offered to mosquitoes is standard practice because higher titers are associated with higher infection, dissemination, and transmission rates (Pesko et al. 2009, Tesla et al. 2018). EqHV, however, is not culturable, and thus infectious particles in horse blood could not be quantified. Instead, we estimated viral RNA copy numbers using RT-qPCR. The inoculum from horses KK and KW had 105 to 107 GE/ml, which in our assay corresponded to Ct values of 23 to 26. Because infected horses typically exhibit viremia levels within this same range (Pfaender et al. 2015, Scheel et al. 2015, Tomlinson et al. 2021), these samples represent the viremia levels that would be expected to be sufficient for infecting competent mosquitoes. We cannot exclude, however, the possibility that neutralizing antibodies concurrently circulating in chronically infected animals would impact the level of infectivity in the samples used here.

Another important consideration relates to the possibility that insect tissues may inhibit detection of viral nucleic acids in molecular assays (Frisch et al. 2025). However, the molecular methodology described here is a standard procedure in our laboratory and has proven successful and highly sensitive for the detection of various arboviruses, including other flaviviruses like Zika virus, in mosquito tissues (Magalhaes et al. 2018, Magalhaes et al. 2019, Carvalho-Leandro et al. 2026), with no evidence of inhibition in either RT-qPCR or plaque assays. Importantly, no amplification signal was detected in any mosquito sample (ie no Ct values were generated). Considering that the LOD of the molecular assay was approximately 15 RNA copies per reaction, and that arbovirus genome copies in individual mosquito samples from vector competence studies involving compatible vector-arbovirus pairs commonly exceed this threshold by several orders of magnitude, especially in body and leg samples (Brustolin et al. 2016, Magalhaes et al. 2018, Soto et al. 2023, Merkling et al. 2025), the complete lack of amplification signal strongly suggests assay sensitivity did not account for the negative results.

The relatively limited sample size assessed at each timepoint in the vector competence experiment does not completely exclude the possibility that very low infection, dissemination, or transmission rates may have gone undetected. However, it is important to note that 2 EqHV-infected horse sera were evaluated independently and that samples were collected at 3 time points, including 14 and 21 dpe, which would have allowed sufficient time for viral replication and completion of the extrinsic incubation period (the time it takes for a vector to become infectious after ingesting the pathogen) in a competent species. Therefore, assuming that the horse sera contained infectious virus and that the Ae. albopictus strain tested here was competent, it would be very unlikely that no mosquito sample would test positive at any time point in either experimental group.

Field-collected mosquitoes tested negative for EqHV RNA, corroborating previous findings where mosquitoes from regions of Austria and Italy also tested negative for EqHV RNA (Badenhorst et al. 2019). Our study adds to this previous work by representing, to our knowledge, the first investigation of EqHV in field-collected mosquitoes from the United States and by evaluating mosquito species not included in the European study. Nevertheless, even when the vectors of a pathogen are known, negative results from field collection should be interpreted with caution due to potential sampling biases and low mosquito infection rates (Gu et al. 2008, Bustamante and Lord 2010). Although approximately 2,000 mosquitoes were tested in our study, these specimens represented multiple species and the number of specimens tested was limited for several species. Also, sampling was not exclusively performed near horses confirmed to be positive for EqHV. Thus, while no EqHV-positive mosquitoes were detected, these negative findings should be interpreted in the context of the sampling design. The absence of positive mosquitoes may reflect limited sampling of some species and/or collections conducted in areas where active EqHV transmission was not occurring at the time of sampling. Future field studies should focus on collections near EqHV-infected horses and increase the number of specimens collected per species while ensuring good coverage of the local mosquito fauna.

In conclusion, our laboratory and field findings provide no evidence supporting a role for mosquitoes as biological vectors of EqHV. However, mosquito transmission cannot be definitively ruled out due to the limitations of the field collections described above and the evaluation of a single mosquito species under laboratory conditions. In addition, the blood used for experimental infections was obtained from chronically infected, seropositive horses. While chronically infected horses are believed to contribute to EqHV maintenance in horse populations, the potential impact of circulating antibodies on mosquito exposure to infectious virus remains unknown. Further field studies on the biological and mechanical transmission potential of insects should focus on horse-feeding species collected from areas where horses testing positive for EqHV RNA are present. Laboratory studies should evaluate additional insect species, include study designs that assess both biological and mechanical transmission, and explore potential differences in transmission outcomes when insects are exposed to blood from acutely infected seronegative horses versus chronically infected seropositive horses.
