Barriers and facilitators of implementing cardiovascular health assessment in children and adolescents in primary care settings in Malaysia: a qualitative study
Authors: Carmen Jia Wen Chuah, Hooi Min Lim, Muhammad Yazid Jalaludin, Chirk Jenn Ng, Wen Ting Tong, Wooi Hang Tan, Bee Kiau Ho, Yin Hui Leong, Neusa Jessen, Shailaja Patil, Darwin R Labarthe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death in Malaysia, with risk factors emerging in childhood. The American Heart Association’s Life’s Essential 8 defines cardiovascular health (CVH) through eight factors: diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep, body mass index, blood pressure, cholesterol, and glucose. While CVH assessment in primary care is critical for long-term CVD prevention, insights from low- and middle-income countries, including Malaysia, remain limited. This study aimed to explore the barriers and facilitators of implementing CVH assessment in children and adolescents in the Malaysian primary care settings.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study using semi-structured interviews with policymakers, healthcare professionals (HCPs), adolescents (12–18 years), and parents of children (5–11 years). Data were analysed using directed content analysis guided by the Tailored Implementation for Chronic Diseases (TICD) framework.ResultsInterviews with 51 participants identified multilevel barriers across system, provider, and patient levels. Policymakers and HCPs highlighted limited school health and primary care capacity, resource constraints, insufficient paediatric-specific training, and fragmented care coordination. HCPs perspectives reflected variation in perceived feasibility, with some expressing concerns regarding implementation complexity and competing priorities. Parents and adolescents reported a low perceived need for screening, fear of procedures, and stigma. Facilitators included alignment with existing adolescent health programmes, perceived feasibility when integrated into existing workflows, and opportunities for task-sharing and community engagement, although these were often dependent on local resources and coordination.ConclusionsImplementation of CVH assessment is shaped by interacting system-, provider-, and patient-level factors within a broader healthcare orientation. Supporting implementation requires enhancing paediatric-specific training, enabling task-shifting, and integrating CVH assessment into routine workflows. Improved referral pathways, care coordination, and integration into existing adolescent health programmes may further facilitate uptake in primary care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03414-2.Keywords: Cardiovascular health, Primordial prevention, Risk factors, Children, Adolescents, Implementation science, Barriers, Facilitators

Background

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide, with low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) bearing nearly 80% of the burden [1]. In Malaysia, ischemic heart disease has consistently been the primary cause of death despite ongoing national prevention efforts [2, 3]. While clinical risk assessment has traditionally focused on adults, there is growing recognition that cardiovascular risk factors often emerge much earlier in life, during childhood and adolescence [4, 5].

Cardiovascular health (CVH), as defined by the American Heart Association’s Life’s Essential 8 (LE8), encompasses eight modifiable behavioural and biological metrics: diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep, body mass index, blood pressure, cholesterol, and glucose [6]. Maintaining optimal CVH from early life is strongly associated with a reduced lifetime risk of CVD, improved quality of life, and lower healthcare costs [7]. Importantly, CVH trajectories are established early, with evidence demonstrating that disparities in CVH emerge in childhood and track into adulthood [8, 9], underscoring the importance of primordial prevention during these formative years [10, 11].

Primary care settings are uniquely positioned to support early CVH assessment and intervention. As the first point of contact within the healthcare system, primary care provides longitudinal, accessible, and preventive care for children and adolescents [12]. In Malaysia, primary care services, including school and adolescent health programmes, play a central role in health promotion and screening [13]. These platforms offer valuable opportunities to identify cardiovascular risk factors early and promote heart-healthy behaviours. However, translating comprehensive CVH frameworks such as LE8 into routine primary care practice remains challenging due to competing priorities, resource constraints, and workflow limitations [14, 15].

Despite increasing attention to CVH, several critical gaps remain. Evidence on comprehensive CVH assessment in children and adolescents is limited, particularly in LMICs. Most existing studies focus on individual risk factors rather than integrated risks, and very few have evaluated all eight LE8 metrics in paediatric populations [16]. Furthermore, the majority of studies have been conducted in high-income countries, limiting their applicability to LMIC contexts [14, 15]. In Malaysia, while studies such as the MyHeART cohort have highlighted a substantial burden of cardiovascular risk factors among adolescents [4, 5], little is known about how comprehensive CVH assessment can be implemented in routine primary care settings.

Importantly, existing research has largely been descriptive, with limited focus on the practical challenges of integrating CVH assessment into real-world clinical workflows. An implementation science approach is therefore needed to understand not only what should be implemented, but how it can be effectively integrated into routine practice. To address these gaps, this study applies an implementation science approach, using the Tailored Implementation for Chronic Diseases (TICD) framework [17], to examine the determinants of implementing comprehensive CVH assessment in Malaysian primary care. By incorporating perspectives from multiple stakeholders—including policymakers, healthcare professionals, adolescents, and parents—it provides context-specific insights into the barriers and facilitators of implementing an eight-metric CVH assessment in children and adolescents. These insights are essential to inform feasible, scalable, and sustainable strategies for early cardiovascular disease prevention.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a qualitative study using semi-structured interviews to explore barriers and facilitators to implementing cardiovascular health (CVH) assessment among children and adolescents in Malaysian primary care. The study adopted an interpretive description approach [18], which is well-suited for applied health research as it generates clinically meaningful insights relevant to healthcare practice through the integration of participant perspectives and the researchers’ contextual knowledge. This pragmatic approach enables the identification of shared patterns and themes that are transferable to clinical practice rather than theory development.

The study was conducted at two urban, government-funded primary care clinics—Setapak Health Clinic (Kuala Lumpur) and Bandar Botanic Health Clinic (Selangor)—under Malaysia’s Ministry of Health. These clinics were selected due to the availability of adolescent and maternal-child health services, feasibility based on prior research engagement, and representation of diverse socioeconomic backgrounds, including urban low-income populations. Both clinics are staffed by family physicians, family medicine trainees, medical officers, nurses, and medical assistants.

Implementation framework

We applied the Tailored Implementation for Chronic Diseases (TICD) framework to guide data collection and analysis. TICD was selected due to its strong relevance to primary care and chronic disease implementation, and its comprehensive coverage across seven domains (guideline factors, individual health professional factors, patient factors, professional interactions, incentives and resources, capacity for organizational change, and social, political, and legal factors) [17]. Not all 57 determinants within the framework were explicitly explored; instead, determinants were selected based on relevance to the research question, existing literature, and researchers’ clinical expertise.

Compared to broader frameworks such as CFIR (Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research), which provide a flexible and comprehensive conceptual structure, TICD offers a more operationalised and structured set of determinants applicable to identifying actionable barriers and facilitators in primary care. These determinants facilitated the development of the interview guide and supported systematic coding across multiple stakeholder groups.

Study population and eligibility

We recruited four stakeholder groups to capture multilevel perspectives: (1) policymakers, (2)healthcare professionals (HCPs) (3), adolescents aged 12–18 years, and (4) parents of children aged 5–11 years.

Policymakers were individuals involved in developing or implementing child and adolescent health programs at national and district levels, representing both policy and operational perspectives. HCPs included doctors (family physicians, family medicine trainees, and medical officers), nurses, and medical assistants involved in CVH assessment in children and adolescents.

Adolescents aged 12–18 years and parents of children aged 5–11 years were included to reflect distinct developmental stages and commonly recognised age groupings in Malaysia, distinguishing adolescents from younger children of preschool and primary school age. We excluded policymakers and HCPs who had not practiced within the past two years. Adolescents and parents of children with congenital heart disease, chronic conditions, or cognitive impairments were excluded as they are more likely to undergo disease-specific cardiovascular monitoring, which differs from population-level CVH assessment and primordial prevention strategies.

Sampling and recruitment

We used purposive sampling to select participants with relevant experience in CVH assessment and applied maximum variation sampling to ensure diversity in roles, age, gender, ethnicity, and educational backgrounds.

Policymakers were identified through professional networks, while HCPs were recruited via recommendations from clinic heads. Adolescents and parents were approached during clinic visits, and snowball sampling was used to identify additional participants.

Recruitment through clinic leadership and professional networks may have introduced selection bias by favouring more engaged participants; however, this approach facilitated the inclusion of information-rich participants with in-depth knowledge of service delivery.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. For adolescents under 18, parental consent and participant assent were obtained, while participants aged 18 provided independent consent. Ethical approval was obtained from the Malaysian Research and Ethics Committee (MREC), Ministry of Health Malaysia (NMRR ID 23-01593-IDO).

Data collection

We conducted semi-structured in-depth interviews (IDIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs) between May 2023 and February 2024. IDIs allowed in-depth exploration of individual perspectives, while FGDs facilitated discussion of shared experiences, group norms, and healthcare system dynamics. The use of both methods enabled methodological triangulation and enhanced data richness.

A total of 26 IDIs and 7 FGDs (3–5 participants per group) were conducted. IDIs lasted approximately 20–40 min, while FGDs lasted approximately 30–60 min. Policymakers, parents, and most adolescents participated in IDIs to accommodate scheduling and ensure privacy, while FGDs were conducted primarily with HCPs.

Interviews were conducted by trained researchers (CJWC and HML), fluent in English, Bahasa Malaysia, and Mandarin, in participants’ preferred language. Interviews were audio-recorded (or video-recorded for virtual sessions via Microsoft Teams). Field notes were taken throughout data collection to capture contextual observations and support interpretation of the data. The interview guide (Supplementary Appendix 1) was developed based on TICD domains and pilot-tested prior to data collection. A visual aid (Supplementary Appendix 2) illustrating the eight CVH metrics was used to guide discussion and ensure a consistent understanding of CVH.

Data collection and preliminary analysis were conducted concurrently. Data saturation was achieved when no new themes or codes emerged from consecutive interviews, based on field notes and iterative team discussions.

Data analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim and de-identified. Transcription and coding were performed in the original language to preserve meaning and minimize loss of nuance. Selected quotations were subsequently translated into English for reporting, with accuracy verified by the researchers, who are fluent in English, Bahasa Malaysia, and Mandarin.

We analysed the data using directed content analysis [19] guided by the TICD framework. While coding was primarily deductive using TICD domains, themes not captured within the framework were allowed to emerge inductively.

Two researchers independently coded transcripts, and inter-rater reliability was assessed on five transcripts using percent agreement (93.1%) to establish initial coding consistency.

After establishing coding consistency, both researchers continued coding the remaining transcripts independently, with frequent discussions to resolve discrepancies and ensure consistency in interpretation. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with a third researcher consulted where necessary. The research team met regularly to review codes, refine themes, and ensure consistency in interpretation.

Data from different stakeholder groups were triangulated to identify areas of convergence, divergence, and complementary perspectives. IDIs and FGDs were analysed together using a unified coding framework, with consideration that responses in FGDs may be influenced by group interactions during interpretation. Qualitative data management was supported using Atlas.ti to facilitate systematic coding and organization of data.

Reflexivity was maintained throughout the study. The primary researchers conducting data collection and analysis were CJWC and HML. CJWC is a Master’s student with a medical background, while HML is a family physician and academician with expertise in CVH and implementation science. Neither researcher practiced at the study sites. While their clinical backgrounds facilitated contextual understanding, we remained aware of potential biases. Reflexive field notes were maintained, and regular team discussions were conducted to minimize bias and enhance analytical rigor.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 51 participants were interviewed (Table 1), including two policymakers, 29 healthcare professionals (HCPs), 10 adolescents, and 10 parents. Adolescents were aged 12–17 years (mean 14.6 years). Most parents had at least secondary education and belonged to the B40 category income group (i.e., the bottom 40% of Malaysian households, defined as monthly household income < USD 1100).

Guideline factors

Limited compatibility with existing workflows was a key barrier. Policymakers and HCPs described overstretched school health teams and the burden of manual documentation as major constraints. One participant noted, “Our school health team is pretty overstretched… they can’t cover all schools.” Another HCP highlighted the documentation burden, stating, “Even for one patient, there are too many listings and books we need to fill out, and it’s manual as well.”

In contrast, alignment with existing guidelines facilitated implementation. The majority of HCPs highlighted similarities between CVH assessment and current adolescent health screening practices, particularly the Borang Saringan Status Kesihatan Remaja (BSSK/R; Adolescent Health Screening Tool), which already captures several CVH-related indicators. Overall, implementation within this domain was influenced by a tension between conceptual alignment with existing practices and operational constraints related to workload and documentation.

Individual health professional factors

HCP attitudes and perceptions acted as both barriers and facilitators depending on perceived feasibility and value. While the majority supported early CVH assessment, several expressed concerns regarding implementation complexity, cost, and long-term impact. For example, some questioned the necessity of certain investigations in younger children, whereas others viewed early screening as an opportunity for prevention.

This variation was reflected in contrasting views, with one participant noting, “Even screening the adults have proved to be challenging… I think implementation will be very difficult…” while another stated, “It’s doable because it’s similar to other screening program that we have been doing.”

Capacity to implement CVH assessment was also shaped by workload and time constraints, which limited opportunities for comprehensive assessment and patient education. At the same time, several stakeholders perceived the assessment as feasible and straightforward to administer, particularly when integrated into existing workflows.

Limited training and familiarity with paediatric CVH assessment were frequently cited barriers. However, several participants expressed confidence in their clinical skills, particularly among more experienced staff.

Overall, HCP-related factors reflected variability in readiness, where support for CVH assessment depended on perceived feasibility, resource availability, and familiarity with paediatric guidelines.

Parent/adolescent factors

Perceived lack of need for screening was a major barrier. Several parents and adolescents viewed CVH assessment as unnecessary in the absence of symptoms, reducing motivation to participate. As one parent described, “If the child is not seriously ill… people rarely take the initiative to address these things.”

Beliefs and knowledge functioned as both barriers and facilitators depending on participants’ level of awareness and perceived relevance of CVH assessment. Some parents lacked understanding of CVH concepts or expressed distrust, particularly regarding costs or unfamiliar programs. Adolescents also had difficulty articulating health behaviours. However, others recognised the benefits of early detection and supported screening. One parent noted, “It’s good for us to know if our child has any issues early on.”

Motivational barriers included preference for shorter consultations, reluctance during illness visits, and fear of procedures such as blood sampling. For example, one adolescent expressed a simple but important concern: “I’m scared (of needles).” Despite this, the majority of participants indicated willingness to participate when clear health benefits were explained.

Behavioural factors, including stigma and privacy concerns, influenced adolescent engagement. For example, concerns about weight-related stigma led to reluctance in participation. Conversely, school-based communication and early health promotion were identified as facilitators. Overall, engagement was influenced by perceived relevance, convenience, and trust, with willingness increasing when benefits were clearly understood.

Professional interactions

Effective teamwork and task delegation facilitated implementation. HCPs described structured roles, where nurses and medical assistants conducted assessments while doctors provided oversight.

Referral processes acted as both facilitators and barriers depending on system integration. Existing pathways to family physician, paediatricians, and dietitians supported continuity of care. However, poor integration between primary care and hospital services, along with difficulties in tracking patients after screenings, limited effectiveness.

Importantly, a policymaker raised ethical concerns regarding implementation without adequate follow-up: “You can’t just do screening for screening’s sake… is it morally acceptable to screen when there’s nothing to offer?”

Overall, while collaborative practices supported implementation, gaps in coordination and continuity of care remained significant challenges.

Incentives and resources

Limited availability of resources was a major barrier. Participants reported shortages of equipment, including paediatric blood pressure cuffs, as well as insufficient supplies for laboratory testing.

Staffing constraints further limited implementation, particularly for conducting education and follow-up activities. Financial limitations within the healthcare system also restricted the feasibility of systematic screening programs. As a result, CVH-related assessments were often conducted opportunistically, depending on available manpower and resources.

Overall, implementation within this domain was constrained by structural limitations in funding, staffing, and infrastructure.

Capacity of organizational change

Low prioritisation of paediatric CVH assessment was a key barrier. Policymakers and HCPs described competing health priorities, with greater emphasis placed on managing existing conditions rather than preventive care.

This reflects a broader system orientation toward treatment rather than prevention, where paediatric CVH-related assessment remains reactive and is often limited to body mass index-based evaluations.

Support for organisational change varied across settings. While some participants reported difficulties in securing collaboration with the Ministry of Education, others described successful partnerships at the district level. External collaborations with NGOs and community organisations were also seen as facilitators. Clear mandates and performance targets further facilitated implementation by promoting accountability.

Overall, implementation was influenced by competing priorities and variability in intersectoral collaboration.

Social, political, and legal factors

Fewer data were generated for this domain, and some constructs were not directly relevant to the study context and were therefore not explored in depth. However, influential community figures, such as local leaders and policymakers, were identified as facilitators, particularly in promoting participation in health programs. One participant noted that community leaders could significantly influence public engagement.

Overall, while less prominent, social and political influences played a supportive role in implementation through community mobilisation.

Discussion

This study identified multilevel barriers and facilitators to implementing CVH assessment among children and adolescents in Malaysian primary care. Across stakeholders, determinants were identified at the levels of guidelines, healthcare professionals, patients, professional interactions, resources, and organisational capacity.

Importantly, these determinants did not operate in isolation but interacted across levels. Resource constraints at the system level appeared to intensify provider workload, which in turn limited opportunities for patient engagement and follow-up. Similarly, parental perceptions of low necessity for screening interacted with organisational priorities that favour acute care over prevention, reinforcing low implementation of CVH-related assessment. These findings suggest that implementation challenges are shaped by interconnected system, provider, and patient-level factors.

A key finding from our study was that implementation is influenced by a broader system orientation toward treatment rather than prevention. Healthcare priorities are largely focused on managing existing conditions, with preventive efforts often deprioritised or conducted opportunistically. This reflected a reactive model of care, where CVH assessment is typically triggered by clinical concerns rather than integrated into routine preventive practice [20, 21].

Our findings also highlighted important ethical considerations in implementing CVH screening, raising concerns about conducting screening without adequate follow-up capacity. This raised questions about the value of identifying risk factors when appropriate interventions may not be available. This reflected a cross-cutting issue, where limitations in follow-up systems intersect with resource constraints, provider workload, and system prioritisation. These findings were consistent with established screening principles, which emphasised that screening programmes should only be implemented when effective diagnostic and treatment pathways are in place [22].

At the provider level, our study found positive attitudes toward early CVH assessment among healthcare providers but highlighted concerns regarding feasibility, complexity, and competing priorities. In our study, limited training and familiarity with paediatric-specific CVH assessment were also identified as barriers. These findings were consistent with previous studies reporting gaps in knowledge and confidence in paediatric risk assessment [23, 24]. Educational support and simplified decision-making tools may therefore help improve implementation.

At the patient level, CVH assessment was perceived as unnecessary in the absence of symptoms, reflecting an illness-oriented understanding of healthcare [25, 26]. However, participants were more receptive when the purpose and benefits of screening were clearly explained. Practical barriers, including time constraints, clinic flow, and fear of procedures, were also identified. These findings align with existing literature on barriers to preventive care uptake in paediatric populations [15, 27].

Professional interactions played an important role in shaping implementation. Team-based approaches and task-sharing were identified as facilitators, particularly where nurses and medical assistants supported screening activities [28, 29]. However, gaps in coordination and referral processes limited continuity of care. Although referral pathways exist in principle, their implementation appeared inconsistent, highlighting challenges in integrating primary and secondary care services [30, 31].

Resource constraints were consistently identified as a major barrier. Limitations in staffing, equipment, and funding restricted the feasibility of comprehensive CVH assessment. As a result, screening was often conducted opportunistically rather than systematically. These findings are consistent with previous research in resource-constrained settings, where preventive services compete with other clinical priorities [32–34].

Implications for practice and policy

Findings from this study suggested several practical strategies for implementation. To address individual health professional factors, targeted paediatric-specific training and simplified assessment tools may improve confidence and consistency. Task-shifting approaches, involving nurses and medical assistants, may enhance feasibility within routine care. Brief, opportunistic assessments during existing consultations may also support integration without substantially increasing workload.

At the system level, integrating CVH assessment into existing adolescent health programmes may reduce duplication and improve uptake. Strengthening referral pathways and coordination between primary care and specialist services is essential to ensure continuity of care. Digital health systems may offer potential strategies to improve documentation and follow-up; however, these approaches require further evaluation within this context.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It employed data triangulation by incorporating perspectives from policymakers, HCPs, adolescents and parents, enabling a comprehensive understanding of multilevel implementation factors. The use of both in-depth interviews and focus group discussions enhanced data richness, while the application of the TICD framework provided a structured and theory-informed approach to analysis.

However, several limitations should be considered. The study was conducted in urban public clinics, which may limit transferability to rural and private healthcare settings. Nevertheless, findings may be transferable to similar resource-constrained primary care settings, including rural clinics with comparable organisational structures, although contextual differences should be considered. Most parent participants belonged to the B40 income group, and findings may therefore be most applicable to low-income urban populations. Recruitment through clinic leadership may have introduced selection bias by favouring more engaged participants; however, this approach also facilitated the identification of key informants in clinical practice. The small number of policymakers limited the breadth of policy-level insights. Social desirability bias may have influenced participant responses, particularly among healthcare professionals and parents. In addition, inter-rater reliability was assessed using percent agreement, which may overestimate coding consistency.

Future research

Future research should prioritise feasibility studies to evaluate the integration of CVH assessment into routine primary care, cost-effectiveness analyses to inform policy decisions, and the evaluation of digital tools to support implementation and follow-up.

Conclusions

This study highlights the complex, multilevel factors influencing the implementation of CVH assessment in children and adolescents in Malaysian primary care. Implementation was shaped by interacting system, provider, and patient-level determinants. Addressing these through coordinated, context-specific strategies may support the integration of CVH assessment into routine care and contribute to early cardiovascular disease prevention.
