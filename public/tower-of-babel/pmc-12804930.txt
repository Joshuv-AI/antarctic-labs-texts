Rheological performance and strength on two component backfilling grout in shield TBM
Authors: Tae-Young Kim, Geun U. Ryu, Hee-Hwan Ryu, Jun-Hyuk Jang, Tae-Hyuk Kwon, Jun-Beom An, Song-Hun Chong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ground settlement and segmental displacement during mechanized tunneling require immediate and stable filling of annular gap. Two-component grouts are widely adopted to control gelation and compressive strength. Previous studies suggest that partial replacement of OPC with ground granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBFS) enhances performance, yet the combined effects of cement type and silicate hardener dosage have not been fully clarified. This study investigates the influence of cement type and hardener-to-water (H/W) ratio on rheological, mechanical, and microstructural properties of two-component backfill grouts. Component A is prepared using OPC or plasticized cement (PLC) with bentonite, stabilizer, and deionized water, while Component B is a sodium silicate-based hardener. Rheological properties (bleeding and flow time of Component A, gelation time, plasticity retention time, and flow rate after mixing with Component B) are evaluated along with uniaxial compressive strength (UCS). Microstructural analyses (XRD, SEM, and MIP) examine hydration and pore structure. Results show that PLC exhibits lower bleeding and shorter flow time in Component A, as well as longer gelation time, shorter plasticity retention time, and higher long-term UCS after mixing with Component B compared to OPC. Increasing H/W extends gelation time but shortens plasticity retention time, reduces flow rate, and enhances strength. XRD patterns reveal reduced crystallinity with increasing H/W, accompanied by an intensified amorphous hump at 28°–32°, attributed to the formation of C–S–H gel. Consistently, MIP and SEM confirm the evolution of pore structures as H/W increases, reflecting progressive development of C–S–H gel. These microstructural changes collectively contribute to enhanced compressive strength.

Introduction

Mechanized tunneling using tunnel boring machines (TBM) has been increasingly adopted for large-scale underground infrastructure projects, such as subways, highways, and electric power utilities, due to its ability to minimize noise and disturbances to the surrounding ground1,2. During the excavation process, precast concrete segments are installed as the primary lining to support the tunnel. However, the excavation by TBM cutter-head generally results in an overcut, creating an annular void between the outer surface of the lining and the surrounding ground3,4. This void, typically ranging between 13—20 cm in thickness, requires immediate filling to ensure structural stability5. The main functions of backfill grout are: (1) distributing the concentrated stresses applied to the segmental lining, (2) reducing the surface settlement, (3) limiting the segment displacement, (4) preventing the groundwater migration.

Single-component systems for backfilling in shield tunneling have traditionally been used due to their advantages of simple mixing and the use of a single pumping line. However, their long setting time (i.e., the time required for the grout to harden) allows infiltrating groundwater to disperse and dilute the fresh grout before hardening, which can cause grout loss under saturated ground conditions and significantly reduce the filling efficiency. In addition, its application is often constrained by delayed setting, excessive bleeding, and lower mechanical performance relative to two-component grouts6–8. Recently, two-component grout systems, consisting of Component A (a cement-based slurry) and Component B (a silicate-based hardener), have been widely used in shield tunneling. Silicate solution undergo hydrolysis and condensation under alkaline conditions to induce gelation, and their interactions with calcium ions govern the initial rate and structural development of the gel network9,10. In addition, sodium silicate also influences early-age reactions in cementitious systems, as it markedly delays the setting of Portland cement by inhibiting C3A hydration and reducing ettringite formation11. By adjusting the dosage of the silicate hardener, these systems enable precise control of gelation time and compressive strength, thus providing flexibility to accommodate varying ground conditions and tunneling requirements12,13.

Increasing attention has been directed toward modifying Component A of two-component grout systems by the partial replacement of ordinary Portland cement (OPC) with supplementary cementitious materials. In particular, the ground granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBFS) has emerged as an attractive material, as it not only contributes to reducing CO₂ emissions from clinker production and promotes the recycling of industrial by-products, but also enhances the long-term performance of hardened grout14,15. Several studies have investigated the incorporation of GGBFS into two-component grouts. Sha et al16. revealed that GGBFS, when combined with steel slag, substantially improved the fresh-state properties, mechanical strength, and impermeability of composites grout, making them suitable for underground engineering applications. Song et al6. showed notable improvements in flowability and durability in mixtures incorporating GGBFS, but gelation times longer than 60 s limited rapid stabilization under saturated conditions. André et al12. demonstrated that partial replacement of OPC with GGBFS reduced bleeding, enhanced long-term strength, and shortened gelation to several seconds or tens of seconds. Song et al17. showed that grouts combining multiple industrial solid wastes, including GGBFS and sand, exhibited resistance to water dispersion and impermeability. Liu et al18. statistically investigated that GGBFS delays the setting time of shield tunneling backfilling grout while significantly enhancing its long-term compressive strength. Liu et al19. systematically evaluated the rheological properties of backfilling grouts incorporating GGBFS, including yield stress, plastic viscosity, and thixotropy, and proposed on optimal GGBFS contest suitable for practical engineering applications. Previous studies have primarily examined macroscopic properties such as flowability, bleeding, and compressive strength, but have rarely quantified the time-dependent evolution of two-component backfill grouts after mixing, particularly the transition from a fluid state to gelation and subsequently to hardening, which is critical for pumping stability and early-age performance of tunnel backfilling material. Moreover, the combined effects of GGBFS incorporation and silicate hardener dosage on the microstructural development of these systems have not yet been systematically clarified.

To address these gaps, this study provides a quantitative and integrated evaluation of two-component backfill grouts by rheological, mechanical, and microstructural analyses. The combined effects of cement type (OPC vs. PLC incorporating GGBFS) and hardener-to-water (H/W) ratio are examined in a unified framework, enabling a clearer mechanistic understanding of how cement chemistry and silicate dosage jointly regulate early-age behavior and long-term performance. By linking rheological transitions with strength development and microstructural changes verified through XRD, SEM, and MIP, this study offers new insights into the governing mechanisms of two-component grouts and provides practical guidance for optimizing grout formulations in mechanized tunneling applications.

Materials and methods

Two-component backfill materials

Two-component backfill grout consists of two solutions: Component A (cement, bentonite, stabilizer, and deionized water) and Component B (a sodium silicate-based hardener). Two types of cement are used: ordinary Portland cement (OPC) and plasticized cement (PLC). The PLC is formulated by partially replacing OPC with 5.56% GGBFS by weight of OPC, resulting in a lower Ca/Si ratio cement compared with pure OPC (Ca/Si = 2.8). The GGBFS particles are generally characterized by a smooth glassy surface and lower specific gravity compared with cement particles, which not only influences the packing density and water demand but also reduces interparticle friction20. The GGBFS incorporated in the PLC in this study exhibits these typical characteristics, thereby influencing the rheological behavior of Component A. The bentonite, primarily composed of montmorillonite, is included to prevent material segregation caused from the sedimentation of the cement particles21. A stabilizer is added to improve the bubble stability of fresh Component A22. The bentonite and stabilizer play a crucial role in maintaining the freshness of Component A during its long-distance pumping, while simultaneously reducing the risk of pipeline blockage. The hardener used as Component B is a sodium silicate-based solution with a silica-to-sodium ratio (Si/Na) of 1.74, which is pre-manufactured at a fixed concentration optimized for field application. Deionized water with an electrical conductivity below 10 μS/m is used throughout this study. According to ASTM C59523, slag contents in the range of 5–30% fall within the classification of Type IS slag cement. Experimental design

The performance of two-component backfill grout systems is highly sensitive to the mixing design. Previous studies reported that the bentonite requires approximately 7—30 min to sufficiently disperse and swell in water to prevent the sedimentation of cement21,24. For cement-based mixtures, a mixing time of approximately 60 min was recommended to ensure adequate homogeneity and durability25. In this study, however, the addition of a stabilizer is intended to minimize the bleeding rate, and the mixing time is set to 30 min. Since the properties of Component A are strongly affected by the sequence and duration of additive incorporation, its proper preparation is critical to achieving the desired stability and performance of the grout. Mixing is carried out using a propeller-type stirrer equipped with a 3 cm blade. Testing methods

The testing program covers both the properties of the grout before setting (bleeding, flow, gelation, and plasticity) and after hardening (compressive strength and microstructure). Bleeding test (ASTM C23226)

The bleeding rate represents the proportion of water that separates from Component A during the initial stage of hydration, as heavier solid particles, such as cement and bentonite, settle under gravity. It reflects the stability and uniformity of the fresh grout, with higher bleeding rates indicating greater segregation and lower quality of the mixture. A 100 mL sample of Component A is poured into a glass cylinder with a diameter of 2.7 cm and a height of 17.5 cm. To prevent evaporation, the top of cylinder is sealed with parafilm. The volume of bleed water is extracted using a syringe at 3 and 20 h, respectively. The bleeding test is conducted at a 20 ± 2 °C. The bleeding ratio over time is calculated by using Eq. where Vo is the initial volume of Component A, BVt is the volume of the bleeding water at time t, and αt is the bleeding ratio at time t.

Flow cone test (ASTM C93927)

The efflux time is measured using the flow cone method. 1,725 mL of freshly prepared Component A is allowed to flow through an outlet with a 1.27 cm diameter under gravity. The cone is positioned at a height of 43 cm from the base. Component A is poured into the cone, and the efflux time is recorded as the duration from the moment the outlet is opened until the continuous flow of the liquid is first interrupted. This test provides an indication of the flowability and viscosity of the fresh grout.

Gel time test

Gelation time, which indicates the onset of structural build-up in the grout, is measured to evaluate the workability and ensure proper placement of the backfill material before it begins to set. Gelation refers to the transition from a liquid to a plastic state, occurring when the cement slurry (Component A) reacts with the sodium silicate solution (Component B) and loses its fluidity. The gelation time is evaluated using a beaker. For the test, 100 mL of Component A and the corresponding amount of Component B are prepared according to the specified mixing ratio. The two components are placed in the same beaker and mixed thoroughly, with timing initiated immediately. The mixture is observed until it ceases to flow, at which point the elapsed time is recorded as the gelation time.

Plasticity retention time test

Plasticity retention time is defined as the duration during which the grout remains workable, ensures that the backfill material can completely fill the target voids around the tunnel lining before hardening. After gelation, the backfill grout gradually transitions from a plastic to a semi-solid state, during which it loses its ability to deform. The gelation time is evaluated using a bag-mixing technique. For the test, 350 mL of Component A solution and the corresponding amount of Component B solution are prepared according to the designated mixing ratio. Component A is placed in the lower section of a transparent plastic bag and Component B in the upper section, keeping them separated. The two components are then mixed until the gelation time is reached. Immediately after mixing, the lower end of the plastic bag is cut, and the grout is discharged at fixed time intervals in small portions. The elapsed time is recorded until the discharged material either breaks apart or exhibits a loss of uniformity, which is defined as the end of the plasticity retention period.

Flow table test (ASTM C143728)

The flowability of grout is a critical parameter that governs its filling performance in tunnel backfilling applications. To evaluate this property, a flow table test is conducted using a steel mold with inner dimensions of 10 cm at the base, 7 cm at the top, and 5 cm in height, placed at the center of a circular plate. For the test, 300 mL of Component A solution and the corresponding amount of Component B solution are prepared according to the designated mixing ratio. The two components, mixed in a beaker for half of the gelation time, are poured into the mold, and the top surface is leveled using a straightedge. After removing the surrounding water, the mold is lifted vertically within 1 min of mixing without applying compaction, in order to avoid disrupting the intermolecular bonds of the mixtures. The flow table is then dropped 25 times over 15 s from a height of 1.28 cm. Subsequently, the spreading diameter of the grout is measured four times at 90° intervals, and the average value is calculated for data analysis. Uniaxial compression strength test (ASTM C10929)

The compressive strength of the backfill grout is evaluated using uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) tests, which provide a direct measure of the material’s ability to resist axial loading and ensure structural support for the tunnel lining. UCS is a key parameter for assessing the mechanical performance of backfill grout, as it reflects the early and long-term strength development necessary to maintain the stability of the surrounding ground and prevent deformation or failure under service loads. For the test, 200 mL of Component A solution and the corresponding amount of Component B solution are prepared according to the specified mixing ratio. Components A and B are mixed in a beaker for half of the gelation time and poured into cylindrical molds with dimensions of 50 mm in diameter and 100 mm in height. Within 1 min, the top surface of the specimen is leveled using a spatula. The specimens are then covered with a wet towel and initially cured at 20 °C for 24 h. After the initial curing, the specimens are demolded and submerged in tap water at the same temperature until testing. UCS tests are conducted at 1, 3, 7, and 28 days. Three specimens are tested for each curing time, and the average value is used to represent the compressive strength. A constant strain rate of 1%/min (i.e., 1 mm/min) is applied. Axial strength and deformation are automatically recorded using a data acquisition system.

Microstructural analyses

To investigate the microstructural characteristics of the specimens, X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) analyses are conducted. The 28-days specimens are used for this analysis. Prior to XRD and SEM analyses, the specimens are oven-dried and finely ground to pass through a 0.75 μm sieve. For SEM analysis, the samples are further coated with a thin layer of platinum to enhance electrical conductivity and then are conducted using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (S-4800, Hitachi High-Technologies, Japan). For MIP analysis, the hardened samples are manually crushed to obtain fragments with particle sizes ranging from 5 to 10 mm. The crushed samples are then immersed in isopropanol to terminate hydration and subsequently dried prior to testing. The dry sequence is conducted at 40 °C for 48 h to avoid hydrated product destruction30. This low-temperature drying protocol is widely adopted to minimize microstructural alteration, as higher-temperature oven drying may cause pore shrinkage or microcracking. The MIP analysis is conducted using an Autopore VI mercury porosimeter (Micromeritics Instruments, USA). The measurement ranges under 5000 nm to 6 nm of pore diameter. XRD measurements are carried out using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer at 25 °C, with a scanning rate of 2°/min over a 2θ range of 10° to 60°.

Results and analysis

Properties of Component A: bleeding and flow time

In a two-component grouting system, lower viscosity and higher resistance to material segregation in Component A are advantageous for long-distance pumping in shied tunnels. Figure 2a presents the visual bleeding test results, while Fig. 2b presents the quantitative bleeding rates according to cement type (PLC and OPC) at 3 and 20 h. The results demonstrate that OPC exhibits higher bleeding than PLC over time. In particular, the bleeding rates of PLC are 2.5% at 3 h and 12.5% at 20 h, while those of OPC are 2.9% and 17.3%, respectively. The lower bleeding tendency of PLC can be attributed to the inclusion of GGBFS, which promotes a more stable and uniformly dispersed particle structure than that of OPC. Furthermore, the finer particle size and higher packing efficiency of GGBFS enhance slurry homogeneity and water retention31,32. This reduction in water separation ultimately lowers the bleeding in PLC-based grouts.Fig. 2Bleeding test results of Component A for different cement types (OPC and PLC) over time. Rheological and mechanical properties for two-component grout

Gelation time

Figure 4 shows the variation in gelation time as a function of the hardener-to-water ratio (H/W) for different cement types. Regardless of the cement type, the gelation time consistently increases with higher H/W values. This trend was also reported in previous study12,33. The hardener used in this study possesses a relatively high SiO2/Na2O molar ratio compared with that of binder materials in Component A. Increasing its dosage raises the Na+ concentration in the mixtures. According to Özçelik and White9, when Na+ substitutes Ca2+ within C-S–H gel, the local charge neutrality is disturbed, inducing additional H+ migration and OH- formation for rebalancing. Such structural adjustments hinder the immediate formation of stable Ca–Si linkages at early-ages. In addition, Katoueizadeh et al10. reported that increased alkali concentrations enhance the negative charge of silanol (-Si–OH) groups and delay early cluster growth due to their lower reactivity. Guo et al11. demonstrated that sodium silicate retards early hydration reactions and prolongs the setting process. Consistent with these mechanistic insights, the present study also confirms that increasing the hardener-to-water ratio clearly delays the onset of gelation, indicating that elevated Na+ levels and the accumulation of silicate species collectively govern the early-age retardation behavior.Fig. 4Gelation time of backfill grout as a function of hardener-to-water ratio for different cement types (OPC and PLC).

Gelation time of backfill grout as a function of hardener-to-water ratio for different cement types (OPC and PLC).

Furthermore, PLC series exhibits slightly longer gelation than OPC series. This behavior can be attributed to the enhanced water retention capacity of GGBFS, which reduces the effective concentration of reactive ions and moderates the interaction between the cement hydrates and the silicate-based hardener. Although the inclusion of GGBFS in PLC reduces bleeding rate due to its finer particles and higher water retention capacity (Fig. 2), it simultaneously delays the gelation time (Fig. 4). This contrasting behavior arises because the enhanced water retention of GGBFS suppresses water separation physically, while its lower reactivity and dilution effect moderate the chemical interaction, thereby extending the gelation time.

Plasticity retention time

Figure 5a shows the backfill grout extracted at 5 min intervals after gelation, while Fig. 5b quantitatively presents the plasticity retention time for two cement types with respect to H/W ratio. The reduction of plasticity retention time indicates the progressive loss of workability and the transition from a plastic to a semi-solid state over time. In all cases, increasing the H/W ratio causes a pronounced decrease in plasticity retention time. In particular, OPC series consistently exhibits longer retention times than PLC series across all mix proportions. These results contrast with the earlier observation that PLC series showed longer gelation times than OPC series. Indeed, the presence of GGBFS in PLC delays the initial gelation, yet it simultaneously accelerates the subsequent loss of plasticity.Fig. 5Plasticity retention time of backfill grout after gelation: (a) appearance of extracted samples at successive time intervals; (b) quantitative retention time for different cement types and hardener-to-water ratios.

Plasticity retention time of backfill grout after gelation: (a) appearance of extracted samples at successive time intervals; (b) quantitative retention time for different cement types and hardener-to-water ratios.

In aggregate-free two-component backfill grouts, the lower specific gravity of GGBFS increases the solid volume fraction, which could potentially raise suspension viscosity and accelerate the loss of plasticity, thereby requiring a higher water content to maintain plastic stage. However, the smooth glassy surface of GGBFS particles and their favorable interaction with dispersing agents reduce interparticle friction and additional water demand. As a result, the observed accelerated loss of plasticity is likely governed by the combined influence of the increased solid volume fraction (due to the lower specific gravity of GGBFS) and the denser micro-scale packing of binder particles (cement, GGBFS, and bentonite). By contrast, in concrete or mortar systems containing aggregates, the increased paste volume derived from the lower density of GGBFS reduces interparticle friction among aggregates and thereby lowers the water demand, as reported in previous studies34,35. This contrasting behavior highlights that the role of GGBFS in governing workability and plasticity strongly depends on whether the system is binder-only or aggregate-containing. Further investigation is required to provide a more quantitative interpretation of the physicochemical interactions that govern plasticity retention. In particular, particle size distribution (PSD) and zeta potential measurements would enable a more rigorous evaluation of how particle packing and surface charge contribute to water mobility and structural build-up in GGBFS-modified binder systems.

Flow rate

Figure 6a shows the spread of backfill grout after the flow table test for each mix proportion. Figure 6b illustrates the flow rate of PLC and OPC series in terms of the H/W ratio. For both cement types, the flow rate decreases with increasing H/W. At the initial H/W of 0.063, OPC (= 0.166) exhibits a higher flow rate but a more uniform radial spread than PLC (= 0.151). As the H/W increases, the difference in flow behavior between the two cement types becomes less pronounced. At an H/W of 0.188, both PLC (= 0.103) and OPC (= 0.104) exhibit nearly identical spread diameters, indicating that the influence of cement type on flow characteristics diminish with higher hardener contents. As reported in previous study, increasing the hardener dosage raises the concentration of sodium silicate solution, thereby promoting greater silicate gel formation and consequently increasing the viscosity of the mixture36. This accelerates the transition of backfilling material from a plastic to a semi-solid state and affects its hardening behavior. As a results, the flow behavior is governed more by the chemistry of the silicate hardener than the cement types, explaining the diminishing difference in flow rate between OPC and PLC at higher H/W ratios.Fig. 6Flow table test results of backfill grout: (a) spread diameter; (b) flow rate for different cement types and hardener-to-water ratios.

Uniaxial compressive strength

Figure 7 presents shows the uniaxial compression stress–strain behavior of PLC and OPC series at different H/W ratios (0.063, 0.125, and 0.188) and curing ages. The UCS increases with curing age, and the extent of strength gain varies with the cement types and H/W ratio. Higher H/W ratio promotes greater strength development, as indicated by steeper slopes and higher peak stresses in the stress–strain curves, irrespective of cement type. A comparison among two cement types shows that OPC series develops strength more rapidly at early ages (1–7 days), whereas PLC series benefits from the incorporation of GGBFS, resulting in superior strength at later ages (28 day). These results indicate that the addition of GGBFS is advantages for enhancing the long-term durability of backfill grout. A more detailed explanation of the influence of chemical composition on the mechanical properties is provided in the discussion section.Fig. 7Uniaxial compression stress–strain curves backfill grout for different cement types at varying H/W ratios and curing ages: (a) H/W = 0.063; (b) H/W = 0.125; (c) H/W = 0.188.

Furthermore, Fig. 8a quantitatively compares the strength gain of cement type under different H/W ratios. At a low H/W ratio of 0.063, the 3-day strength relative to the 1-day strength increases by 3.06 times for OPC, and 2.50 times for PLC, indicating that OPC exhibits early-age strength development. Empirical relationships among rheological and mechanical parameters

Figure 9 illustrates the relationship between gelation time and plasticity retention time. Overall, the two variables exhibit an inverse trend: shorter gelation times correspond to longer plasticity retention, whereas longer gelation times are associated with earlier loss of plasticity. This indicates that rapid gelation allows the grout to remain in a workable plastic state for a longer duration, providing sufficient time for injection. Conversely, mixtures with delayed gelation exhibit initial flowablility but tend to lose plasticity more quickly, reducing the available window for proper placement. These findings suggest that gelation time can serve as a practical indicator for estimating plasticity retention in the field, thereby facilitating injection control and minimizing the risk of premature setting.Fig. 9Relationship between gelation time and plasticity retention time of backfill grout.

Relationship between gelation time and plasticity retention time of backfill grout.

Discussions: microstructural analyses

Figure 11 presents the XRD patterns of PLC and OPC specimens at H/W ratios of 0.063 and 0.125, respectively. The major crystalline phases identified include ettringite (Ca6Al2(SO4)3(OH)12·26H2O, ICSD 98–002-7039), calcite (CaCO3, ICSD 98–016-6364), and portlandite (Ca(OH)2, ICSD 98–006-4950), with minor components such as pyroaurite (Mg6Fe23+(OH)16(CO3)·4H2O, ICSD 98–000-6295) and larnite (CaSiO4, ISCD 98–002-9551). With increasing dosage of Component B, both binder systems show a reduction in overall crystallinity, as evidenced by the attenuation of sharp diffraction peaks and the relative enhancement of the amorphous background, particularly in the range of 28° to 32° 2θ. This region is typically associated with the diffuse hump of calcium–silicate–hydrate (C–S–H) gel, which appears as a broad feature in XRD patterns37.Fig. 11XRD patterns of backfill grout: (a) overall spectra; enlarged view of the 28° to 32° range for (b) PLC and (c) OPC. Major phases detected are ettringite (E), calcite (C), portlandite (P), pyroaurite (Y), and larnaite (L).

XRD patterns of backfill grout: (a) overall spectra; enlarged view of the 28° to 32° range for (b) PLC and (c) OPC. Major phases detected are ettringite (E), calcite (C), portlandite (P), pyroaurite (Y), and larnaite (L).

The increase in Component B, composed primarily of sodium silicate, introduces additional reactive silica into the system without contributing calcium. This compositional change is likely to reduce the effective Ca/Si ratio within the hydrating matrix. Kunther et al38. demonstrated that lower Ca/Si ratios favor the formation of a volume of C–S–H gel slightly, while simultaneously reducing the precipitation of crystalline phases such as portlandite and calcite. As observed in this study, a larger the dosage of Component B reduces crystallinity of calcite and portlandite. This behavior suggests that increased silicate availability promotes the formation of intensified amorphous C–S–H structures, and eventually improve the compressive strength observed in Fig. 8.

In addition, the intensity of the portlandite peak (main peak 18°) is significantly higher in the OPC compared to the PLC. The peak is more pronounced in higher H/W ratio at the same cement type. This observation suggests that the early-age hydration of OPC is more extensive, which can be attributed to the absence of latent hydraulic components such as GGBFS in the binder. In contrast, the PLC mixture exhibits stronger peaks associated with pyroaurite (main peak 11°), which reflect the activation of supplementary cementitious reactions involving GGBFS. This behavior can be attributed to the presence of magnesium-rich phases derived from the slag component.

While overall crystallinity decreases with increasing H/W ratio, the intensity of the ettringite main peak (16°) remains relatively stable. This behavior can be attributed to the higher dosage of Component B, a silicate-based hardener with strong alkalinity, which enhances the dissolution of sulfate ions from the cementitious phases. The increased sulfate availability under alkaline conditions promotes ettringite formation during early hydration, thereby compensating for the reduction in crystallinity induced by higher H/W ratios. However, the excessive formation of ettringite is known to exert detrimental effect on the fire resistance of cementitious systems.

Previous studies reported that capillary pores are detrimental to the mechanical performance of cementitious materials, particularly compressive strength, due to their relatively large size and tendency to act as stress concentrators and crack initiation sites41,42. Conclusions

This study investigates the combined effects of cement type and hardener-to-water (H/W) ratio on the properties of two-component backfill grouts. Ordinary Portland cement (OPC, Ca/Si ≈ 2.8) and plasticized cement (PLC, OPC partially replaced with 5.56% GGBFS, lower Ca/Si ratio) are used to evaluate rheological, mechanical, and microstructural behavior. The main findings are summarized as follows:Bleeding and flowability: PLC exhibit lower bleeding and slightly shorter flow time than OPC. This improvement is attributed to the GGBFS component, whose smoother surface and lower specific gravity enhanced particle packing and water retention. Consequently, segregation and water release are suppressed in PLC, whereas OPC showed greater bleeding and longer flow time.Gelation and plasticity: Gelation time increases with higher H/W ratios due to the concentration of Na⁺ and silicate ions, which slows early Ca–Si reactions. PLC shows longer gelation but shorter plasticity retention than OPC. The prolonged gelation of PLC results from the water-retention effect and lower Ca/Si ratio of GGBFS, which weaken early ionic interactions with silicate species. In contrast, the higher solid volume fraction and denser packing of GGBFS-containing binders accelerate structural buildup after gelation begins, shortening the plasticity retention period.Flow rate: Flow rate decreased as the H/W ratio increased, indicating higher viscosity and reduced flowability. Although OPC initially displayed slightly higher flow rates than PLC, this difference nearly disappeared at higher H/W values. This convergence occurs because a larger hardener dosage increases sodium silicate concentration, accelerates silicate gel formation, and raises mixture viscosity. As a result, the flow behavior at high H/W ratios is governed primarily by the silicate hardener rather than the cement type.Strength development: OPC exhibited faster early-age strength gain (1–7 days), whereas PLC achieved higher long-term strength at 28 days. This contrast reflects compositional differences: OPC, with its higher Ca/Si ratio, promotes rapid early hydration, while PLC, with its lower Ca/Si ratio, supports sustained C–S–H gel development. An inverse relationship is observed between flow rate and compressive strength, suggesting that reduced flowability corresponds to denser microstructures and higher strength.Microstructure: XRD, SEM, and MIP analyses confirmed that increasing the H/W ratio reduced crystalline phases and promoted the formation of amorphous C–S–H gels. This transformation is accompanied by the conversion of capillary pores (> 10 nm) into gel pores (< 10 nm), indicating progressive microstructural densification. PLC specimens, with their lower Ca/Si ratio, exhibited a more refined pore structure and greater development of C–S–H gels at later ages, consistent with their superior long-term strength.
