SmartGridDrive: an integrated adaptive Q-learning framework for precision self-parking and reverse navigation in dynamic grid environments—a proof-of-concept study
Authors: Revati Raman Dewangan, Deepali Thombre, Vivek Parganiha, Monika Verma, Bhupesh Kumar Dewangan, Amit Pimpalkar, Nilesh Shelke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research proposes an adaptive Q-learning framework for precision self-parking and reverse maneuvering in dynamic grid-based environments. The vehicle state includes position, orientation, and velocity and the environment model incorporates moving obstacles and dynamically changing parking slot availability. Key technical contributions are: (i) adaptive exploration where the epsilon-greedy parameter decays only when recent reward improves; (ii) look-ahead obstacle prediction using a Markov transition model with directional persistence; and (iii) priority-based Q-value updates that focus on state-action pairs with large temporal-difference errors. The hybrid control architecture combines Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT) for global path planning, Model Predictive Control (MPC) for smooth trajectory tracking and Q-learning for local corrective adjustments. In simulation with a 10 × 10 dynamic grid and moving obstacles, the proposed method achieves a success rate of 92.4% ± 1.2%, collision rate of 1.8% ± 0.5%, and parking alignment error of 6.7 ± 0.8 cm. Ablation studies confirm that each module contributes significantly (p < 0.01). The method proposed in this paper is shown to be more efficient than DDPG, PPO and DQN baselines all evaluated under the same simulation settings. These results confirm the proof-of-concept (POC) viability of light-weight tabular Q-learning with predictive extensions in a simulated dynamic grid-based parking domain.Keywords: Reinforcement learning, Q-learning, Autonomous driving, Self-parking systems, Dynamic environment navigation, Obstacle prediction, Real-time path planning, Automated parking and reversingSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The autonomous driving technology not only makes the road safer but also eases traffic congestion and makes smarter transport systems. Nevertheless, deploying self-driving cars is challenging in high-density cities. Such tasks as self- parking and reversing are especially challenging because of tight parking spaces, moving obstacles, and environmental obstacles1. Traditional path planners and controllers tend to lack the flexibility necessary for reverse manoeuvers, as well as anticipating dynamic obstacles in our application2. Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)3 frameworks such as DDPG and PPO4–6 achieve strong performance but require substantial computational resources, limiting real-time deployment and multi-vehicle scalability7–11 proposed a modified tabular Q-learning framework designed for grid-based environments with dynamic obstacles, specifically targeting self-parking and reverse maneuvers. Conventional path planning techniques often struggle to detect dynamic obstacles. Although autonomous driving has achieved significant progress, most of the existing technologies have serious shortcomings when deployed in such a complex environment. Older path planning techniques usually have problems with the ability to detect dynamic obstacles and to act in real time, so the approach cannot be used in unpredictable urban environments12,13. Similarly, classical control systems do not maintain steady states and are not capable of successful administration of unforeseen traffic variations or obstacle designs14. Also, some models are based on physics-driven methods for learning component degradation, but are not intended to be used in controlling the vehicle in real-time. Although traditional forms of Q-learning in RL operate well in the controlled environment they are usually not suitable in changing environments in the accuracy of such actions as self- parking and reversal.

The recent deep learning methods, e.g. Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) and transformers, are predominantly devised for the temporal prediction problem. These solutions provide an easy, straightforward and powerful solution to driving independently. The autonomous driving demands fine navigation in dynamic conditions, especially in manoeuvre like parking and reversing. These activities demand immediate response to the dynamic barriers and path arrangements. Reinforcement Learning (RL) and fuzzy based RL15, particularly Q-learning, provides strong potential for acquiring such behaviours through interaction with the environment. Borole6 suggested a Q-learning algorithm that involves look-ahead obstacle prediction, prioritized learning and adaptive exploration. Moreover, a well-developed reward system helps a driver to conduct safe, effective, and accurate decisions on their driving16. The ability of the improved version of Q-learning method to do self-parking and reversing behaviours is tested in simulations of other dynamic grid cases. A demonstration of the agent capabilities and to perform the specific maneuvers even under the complicated conditions was framed by Gao17. Such results support the promise of the use of RL to solve the problems of autonomous driving in dynamic conditions. Moveable grid display records all the live variations in obstacles and parking plan, thus enhancing situational knowledge9. Autonomous navigation in dense cities involves rapid re-planning based on obstacles that are in motion, and varying road conditions. The outcomes of simulations prove real-time flexibility to dynamic situations, avoidance of obstacles, and energy-saving maneuvering18. Conventional motion control techniques and traditional Q-learning find it challenging to deal with reverse maneuvers, quick adaptation, and prediction of object movement19. The DRL methods are also effective in such a case, but they are multifaceted to apply and require high performance computing and have large temporal-difference errors10,20. That is, they are not employed in either simple or time sensitive systems. Moreover, most models tend to neglect the need of quick policy change and predicting obstacles and results in unrealistic or risky behaviour. These weaknesses underscore the need to have a more efficient and adaptable learning paradigm that is proposed in this paper by the means of an adapted version of the Q-learning algorithm that would suit the task of autonomous navigation in dynamic settings.

The primary novelty lies in system-level integration and regime-based performance characterization rather than in proposing a new standalone learning algorithm. The proposed Q-learning framework is designed to operate in a dynamic grid-based simulated environment, enabling more precise maneuvers such as automated parking and reversing. The reward system has been well planned to be based on safety, efficiency and accuracy where the collision and superfluous movements are punished, to promote the best navigation. The proposed approach improves precision by introducing dynamic re-planning and backward navigation in confined spaces in comparison to the existing techniques used, including DDPG and MPC. Our system uses a dynamic grid which helps self-driving cars to accomplish a task like autonomous parking. The reliability of predictions achieved in our system is not simply based on estimations by the other approaches like the one used in the adoption of predictions like computer-based prediction systems that come before the car. This integration enables adaptive decision-making within the simulated navigation environment by combining RL-based control with grid-based planning. Simulation experiments show that the agent is able to respond to changes occurring in the real-time, to circumvent the obstacles, and to perform precise maneuvers in a high success rate. This paper addresses the paradigm shift represented by RL and the particular problems of automated parking and reversing, providing a simulation framework tested in large dynamic grid-structure military simulation environments in the last few years as related work.

This work is positioned as an integration and regime-characterization study that combines established methods (Q-learning, RRT, and MPC) into a unified framework. Rather than introducing a fundamentally new algorithm, the contribution lies in analyzing their combined behaviour and performance under dynamic grid-based scenarios. This work is an integration and characterization study, where well-known techniques (Q-learning, RRT, MPC) are used for precise self-parking and reverse maneuvers. Instead of proposing another learning paradigm, this work contributes by analyzing their collective behaviour in dynamic grid environments and by characterizing performance in different navigation conditions. Tabular Q-learning is particularly useful when state representations can be discretized, real-time simulated performance is crucial, and we are in a structured, low-dimensional environment such as grid-based navigation. On the other hand, deep RL methods are better suited for high-dimensional continuous spaces but come at a huge computational and training cost.

Path planning using rapidly exploring random trees and control using MPC

Initial path planning uses the RRT algorithm to build a tree of paths that are feasible by sampling random points and linking them with motions without collisions to avoid obstacles this procedure is known as initial path planning; upon finding a path to the destination the algorithm is used as a guide to the vehicle43. The RRT is continuously updated within a dynamic environment in order to address any changes in the positions of the obstacles or the goal point so that the vehicle is able to continuously adjust during execution. MPC is used in tracking of a trajectory by tracking the reference path calculated by RRT with optimal control inputs in form of steering angle and speed calculated on a prediction horizon, constraints such as acceleration limits and turning radius are considered, and control actions are adjusted in real time to adapt to environmental changes and dynamic obstacles to ensure minimal deviation of the planned path.

The pseudocode implements RRT to find a collision-free route between the vehicle’s start and target points. By picking random sites and extending pathways from the closest existing node, it repeatedly expands a search tree. RRT first gives a route plan that serves as a reference trajectory in the simulation. It handles path finding in situations with multiple obstacles and dynamic changes.

This algorithm enables the vehicle to follow a predetermined course smoothly and securely. It employs a kinematic model of the vehicle and generates optimum control inputs (steering angle, velocity) across a future prediction horizon, utilising optimisation. MPC ensures the vehicle follows the intended RRT course with realistic motion, taking into consideration vehicle characteristics like turning radius and speed constraints. This facilitates the reliable and exact execution of pathways in simulation.

Hybrid integration of RRT, MPC, and Q-learning

An effective system for self-driving in crowded environments must be capable of planning, executing, and adapting in real time; this is achieved through an integrated approach where RRT determines a feasible route, MPC ensures accurate trajectory tracking, and Q-learning enables corrective actions when deviations or uncertainties arise. The last control applied on the vehicle combines both trajectory tracking of the MPC and correction of the Q-learner thus, ensuring global feasibility (RRT), smooth control (MPC), and simulated dynamic responsiveness (Q-learning). A discrete Q-learning agent produces corrective actions, such as local steering or reversing adjustments, which are treated as control corrections and combined with the continuous MPC outputs via the final control law. MPC continuously delivers nominal inputs for trajectory tracking, while Q-learning prepares adaptive local adjustments to dynamic obstacles, forming a two-layer closed-loop control structure.

Statistical analysis and error estimation

All uncertainty values reported in the manuscript (e.g., ± values in Tables 5, 6, 7 and 8) indicate mean ± standard deviation of 100 independent runs with different random seeds. These values reflect variability in performance across repeated experiments rather than confidence intervals. A comparison with baseline was done where allowed. Paired two-tailed t-tests were used to assess the statistical significance of the means, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. The results obtained from 100 independent simulation runs were statistically analyzed, and the error estimates of measurement were obtained to enhance the evaluation of the proposed method. The enhanced Q-learning agent achieved a mean parking orientation error of 2.1° ± 1.4°, mean episode reward of 85.3 (± 5.7) and a success rate of 92.4% ± 1.2%. Positional alignment error (cm) and orientation error (degrees) are reported separately to avoid ambiguity. These measures demonstrate the sure and correct performance in varying circumstances. Also, both efficiency and collision avoidance showed significant improvements (p < 0.01) compared to the baseline Q-learning method using a paired t-test. Statistical significance between the proposed model and ablation variants was assessed using two-tailed paired t-tests. For comparisons involving multiple ablation variants, Bonferroni correction was applied to control family-wise error, with adjusted significance thresholds maintained where applicable. These findings indicate the effectiveness of the advanced RL model in precision and uncertainty tasks, such as self-parking and reversing. This study reinforces the soundness of its results by incorporating measurement-error analysis and statistical validation, in line with the previous literature’s recommendations for best practices in RL assessments. The comparative evaluation further demonstrates that the proposed method consistently outperforms strong baseline approaches. In addition, the sensitivity analysis confirms the robustness and scalability of the framework across varying conditions. The results suggest that the proposed approach is valid and consistent within the analyzed dynamic grid-based simulation setup.

To the best of our simulation system, there are also random motions of obstacles, and sensor-like input noise as well as kinematic vehicle constraints. These features were introduced to replicate the uncertainty and movement behaviour of the actual car-parking scenarios45. In order to deal with extreme-case performance, we used test conditions where obstacle density was high, where there was sudden change of direction and where there was very little clearance to park. When this occurred, the agent had reduced success rates, increased alignment error, and increased collisions. These failure instances were mostly associated with those cases when the dynamic obstacles prevented the passage of several critical points or the situation when the agent was affected by the unexpected changes in the target location.

Discussion of key findings

The results must be taken to be POC evidence in a highly simplified simulated environment. Thus, conclusions around the deployment of full-scale autonomous driving should be seen as provisional. It is still conceptual rather than full-scale real-world feasibility with autonomous vehicles, promising, nonetheless. Thus, the framework proposed here should be understood in that the ideas should be validated in a simulation but not yet used in the deployment of an autonomous driving system. Tabular RL is preferable when: (i) state and action spaces are small enough for a complete table (e.g., 800 states); (ii) interpretability and deterministic convergence are required; (iii) computational resources are limited (e.g., embedded systems). Deep RL becomes necessary for high-dimensional or continuous spaces. Our grid environment stays within tabular feasibility to serve as a reproducible baseline. Conclusion and future work

A modified version of Q-learning framework to suit the dynamic grid-based setting where precision tasks, self-parking and reversing, are part of autonomous driving is presented in the work. Our approach combines the advantages of previous approaches such as obstacle prediction, continuous control, or collaboration into a lightweight, scalable framework that functions well in dynamic real-time simulated situations without the need for intricate multi-agent systems or deep architectures. The look-ahead functions to predict obstacles, adaptive exploration strategies and prioritized updates of highly variable states are included in the proposed system that can deal well with the uncertainties and challenges inherent in dynamic environments. Through RL along with RRT and MPC is a strong base of real-time vehicle path planning and control. The technique uses simulation settings and mathematical models to enhance vehicle performance in a variety of circumstances. When compared to earlier systems, the ARP system was found to be much more accurate and efficient. In simulations of six different situations, Under the matched simulation setup used in this study, the suggested technique achieved improved performance compared with the MPC-only baseline, with an average accuracy of 94.4% and an error rate of 5.5%. The use of RRT, MPC, and RL in this study provides a good basis for ARP. The technique not only ensures accuracy in parking maneuvers but also identifies potential uses in larger navigation and control systems for autonomous cars. These results demonstrate the capability of the model to adjust to the changing environments and prevent collision as well as do the correct parking and reversing procedures. The outcome of these results makes RL an interesting aspect of for autonomous navigation that can be pursued in simulations of dynamic environments. Instead of much of the state of the art being an entirely new RL algorithm, we show that integrating and characterizing complementary existing methods can yield a successful and robust adaptive autonomous navigation framework. Future work will include implementing all baselines under a unified simulation environment to enable fully matched experimental comparisons. This study emphasizes integration and performance characterization of established methods rather than algorithmic novelty. The results demonstrate how such hybrid combinations behave under varying dynamic conditions. However, since the simple grid abstraction does not fully account for vehicle dynamics, perception uncertainty, or interactions with other traffic, we consider our conclusions regarding deployment on-road to be preliminary.

The findings in this study are to consider the potential of a dynamic system based on grids and to offer a POC assessment of how such a system could operate in the near future within an existing grid. Although the results are promising, the framework is yet to be tested in a simplified simulation environment (10 × 10 grid, single-agent), which is not realistic to the complexities of the real world such as sensor noise, perception uncertainty and continuous vehicles dynamics. The proposed framework shows better ability for self-parking and reverse driving but has not been evaluated on real autonomous vehicle platforms and a large-scale real-world driving database. Moreover, the table-based formulation of Q-Learning might not scale well to high-dimensional continuous spaces.

Next, work is planned on hardware-in-the-loop testing, extensions of the continuous-control RL, and finally, selected real urban traffic scenarios. This results in a sim-to-real gap, which may inhibit easy application to real-world autonomous driving systems. The future work will enhance robustness and generalization via domain randomization, hardware-in-the-loop validation with ROS and benchmarks on real-world data (such as nuScenes Zhang et al.,40, Fu et al.46 and Xu et al.47. Additionally, the method used currently has issues with scaling to larger and multi-agent environments, where state complexity may affect performance. The further development should be aimed at the increased scalability, more realistic simulation, and new features, namely vision-based sensors to apply it to the real world. This POC simulation framework can potentially act as a basis for future research initiatives towards realistic, closed-loop autonomous driving and offers a substantial piece of information regarding designing adaptive and intelligent navigation models to make transportation less risky and efficient. The grid based dynamic environment can be supplied with sensor information including LiDAR and another sensor information. This leads to the ability of the agent to act in a manner of a real vehicle and be tested on noisy and uncertain conditions. Expanding the system to multi-agent cases will make it possible to have cooperative and competitive interactions in the environment like shared parking lots and congested roads.
