Deep steganographic approach for reliable data hiding using convolutional neural networks and adaptive loss optimization
Authors: Malathi P., Gireesh Kumar T.
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper proposes a deep steganography framework using a three-layered Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architecture—preparation, hiding, and revealing networks—for robust data hiding. The preparation network employs 50, 10, and 5 filters (3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}3, 4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}4, 5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}5) for edge feature extraction, followed by adaptive embedding in the hiding network. Optimized with four loss functions, Loss Function 3 (LF 3), integrating mean and variance terms, achieves a payload of 3–5 bits per pixel and improved PSNR across Tiny-ImageNet, Linnaeus 5 dataset, Sky-text dataset and RGB-BMP datasets. LF 3 ensures robustness against Gaussian noise, cropping, and rotation, with low detection rates in histogram, statistical, and CNN-based steganalysis. Compared to state-of-the-art Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) based methods, LF 3 offers higher payload-robustness balance and computational efficiency, advancing secure data hiding for applications like medical imaging.

Introduction

The domain of information security aims to safeguard information from unauthorized access. Information security1 encompasses two primary subcategories: Information encryption and Information hiding. Regardless of an attacker’s presence, information encryption involves transforming data into an unreadable format using various techniques manually conducted by humans or computer algorithms. Two primary categories comprise information hiding: Watermarking and Steganography. Watermarking is essential for preventing copying or usage fraud, embedding identifiable markers into digital media like CDs, DVDs, and flash drives to ensure authenticity and ownership, particularly in media and publishing industries where intellectual property protection is critical2–6.

Steganography, in contrast, conceals data within a medium so that it goes undetected. Steganalysis is a method of detecting hidden transmissions while reducing any adverse effects on regular transmissions. In Steganography, the term “Cover” pertains to media that does not possess concealed information, while “Stego” refers to media with hidden data. Steganographic techniques exhibit different variations contingent upon the nature of the secret information and the cover media involved. These techniques conceal information within various media types, including text, images, videos, audio, and network communications. The primary emphasis of this paper is on image steganography, which involves concealing either text or image data within an image (cover) to create a stego or container image.

Embedding techniques are broadly classified into spatial and frequency domains. Spatial domain methods, such as Least Significant Bit (LSB) substitution7 and Pixel Value Differencing (PVD)8, directly modify pixel values, offering simplicity and high capacity (e.g., 1 bpp for LSB) but limited robustness to image processing like compression or noise. Frequency domain methods, including Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) and Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT), embed data in transformed coefficients, providing better resistance to compression but requiring higher computational resources and often yielding lower payloads.

Traditional schemes in these domains, such as LSB-based techniques7 and DCT-based approaches8, have been foundational but exhibit significant limitations. They achieve low payload capacities (e.g., 0.3 bpp for LSB variants) and are vulnerable to advanced steganalysis8, particularly machine learning-based detectors that exploit statistical patterns. The evolution of steganalysis, with deep learning tools9,10, has further exposed weaknesses in adaptability to transformations like cropping or rotation, necessitating more robust solutions.

Deep learning techniques8 have gained significant attention in Steganography, with various methods applied to Steganography and Steganalysis. One notable approach is the utilization of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), which employ adversarial training involving two competing networks: One network embeds confidential data into the original image. In contrast, the other network acts as a Steg analyzer, detecting the presence of hidden information. However, methodologies based on Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) demonstrate resource-intensive characteristics and are susceptible to instability issues. In contrast, Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) based approaches for embedding confidential data by minimizing discrepancies in loss functions, making them more robust against steganalysis. The integration of optimization techniques with neural networks enhances computational efficiency by enabling faster convergence during training and reducing the need for extensive manual parameter tuning, as evidenced by the streamlined training process and reduced computational overhead in our experiments.

Deep learning approaches, including CNN-based9,10 and GAN-based methods11,12, improve imperceptibility but face limitations. GANs suffer from training instability and high computational costs, while CNN-based methods often prioritize payload over robustness to transformations like cropping or rotation. Proposed methodology

The proposed architecture, depicted in (Fig. 1) comprises three networks. The preparation network accepts the cover image and pre-processed secret image as inputs for embedding. In scenarios where the secret image is smaller than the cover image, the preparation network resizes it to match the dimensions, ensuring an even distribution of secret image bits across all pixels. Furthermore, this network transforms color-based pixels into edge features for embedding. The second is the Hiding network,which receives input from the preparation network and generates the stego image as output. Together, the preparation network and the hiding network constitute the sender block. The receiver side functions as the third network, where the stego image is taken as input, and the cover image is separated from it to extract the embedded secret image. The primary aim of the entire model is to minimize loss to the extent that detecting the presence of a secret image in the stego image becomes challenging. Additionally, the receiver should achieve enhanced clarity when recovering the secret image. Therefore, selecting a suitable loss function plays important role in achieving these objectives.

Subsequently, feature maps generated by these convolutional layers are merged and processed through a secondary convolutional operation, employing 50, 10, and 5 filters with 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}3, 5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}5, and 4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}4 sizes, respectively. This process ensures the inclusion of all features of the hidden image within the resultant feature maps. The output is produced by integrating these concatenated feature maps with the cover image as input. The architectural layout of the Preparation network is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Hidden network

The preparation network’s output feeds the hidden network, consisting of 5 convolutional layers. It has 50, 10, and 5 filters with 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}3, 4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}4, and 5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}5 dimensions. The preparation network’s output is fed into the first convolutional layer block. Each block’s concatenated outputs are then sent as inputs to their corresponding subsequent blocks. The hidden network’s final output is obtained by passing the output of the last convolution block with three filters of size 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}3. This final output combines elements from the cover and hidden images to generate the container image, closely resembling the cover image and resulting from the intricately designed model. The architecture of the hidden network, including the concatenation of feature maps and their transmission to the respective convolution blocks, is depicted clearly in Fig. 3.

Reveal network

The architecture of reveal comprises five blocks of convolutional layers, which take both noise and output from the hidden network as input. It integrates 50, 10, and 5 filters with 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}3, 4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}4, and 5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}5 dimensions. The result of the hidden network is fed into the initial convolutional block, while subsequent blocks receive concatenated outputs. Ultimately, the final result of the reveal network is generated through a single convolutional layer with three filters of size 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}3, processing the output of the last convolution block. This resulting output represents the decoded secret image, aimed at closely resembling the original secret image. Additionally, the model is fine-tuned to facilitate the receiver’s retrieval of the secret image with minimal distortion. Metrics and dataset

This section outlines the evaluation metrics and datasets used to evaluate the proposed deep steganography framework, focusing on imperceptibility, robustness, and resistance to steganalysis. The metrics ensure a comprehensive analysis of the framework’s performance across diverse scenarios, while the datasets provide varied image characteristics for robust evaluation.

Evaluation metrics

The following metrics are used to evaluate the four loss functions (LF 1–4), with a focus on Loss Function 3 (LF 3), across imperceptibility, robustness, and steganalysis resistance:

Mean Squared Error (MSE): Quantifies the average squared pixel-wise difference between two images, measuring error in cover-stego and secret-recovered pairs. It is defined as

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {MSE} = \frac{1}{h \times w} \sum _{i=1}^{h \times w} \left\| C_i - S_i \right\|$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w$$\end{document} are the height and width of the image, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_i$$\end{document} are the original and processed images (e.g., cover vs. stego or secret vs. recovered).

Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR): Measures image quality by comparing the maximum possible signal with the noise introduced by embedding. It is calculated as follows:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {PSNR} = 10 \log _{10} \left( \frac{R^2}{\text {MSE}} \right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R$$\end{document} is the maximum pixel value (e.g., 255 for 8-bit images). Higher PSNR indicates better imperceptibility, computed for both cover-stego and secret-recovered pairs.

Structural Similarity Index (SSIM): Assesses perceptual similarity by comparing luminance, contrast, and structure. It is defined as:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {SSIM}(x, y) = \frac{(2\mu _x \mu _y + C_1)(2\sigma _{xy} + C_2)}{(\mu _x^2 + \mu _y^2 + C_1)(\sigma _x^2 + \sigma _y^2 + C_2)}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x, y$$\end{document} are the two images being compared (e.g., cover and stego, or secret and recovered), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _x$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _y$$\end{document} denotes the mean intensity (luminance) of images x and y, calculated as the average pixel value over a window or the entire image, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _x^2, \sigma _y^2$$\end{document} are the variance (contrast) of images x and y, measuring the spread of pixel intensities. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _{xy}$$\end{document} denotes the covariance of x and y, which captures the structural similarity between images. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_1\ and\ C_2$$\end{document} are the small constants that stabilize the division when the denominator is small.

Bit Error Rate (BER): Measures the error rate in recovering the secret image’s binary data, defined as:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {BER} = \frac{\text {Number of bit errors}}{\text {Total number of bits}}$$\end{document}

Lower BER indicates higher accuracy in secret recovery, critical for robustness against distortions like noise or cropping.

Steganalysis detection metrics: Resistance to steganalysis is evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, and f1-score of CNN-based steganalyzers (e.g., trained on J-UNIWARD, JMiPOD, UERD). Lower values (<0.50) indicate stronger security against detection by histogram, statistical, and neural network-based attacks.

These metrics collectively assess the framework’s ability to balance high payload, maintain image quality, and resist real-world distortions and steganalysis.

Robustness evaluation

The robustness of the proposed framework, optimized with Loss Function 3 (LF 3), is evaluated against transformations and steganalysis attacks. Table 7 presents MSE and PSNR results for cover-stego and secret-recovered pairs under Gaussian noise, 10–50% cropping, and rotation across datasets (DS-1 to DS-4). LF 3 achieves lower cover error per pixel (e.g., 13.81–19.99) and competitive PSNR (e.g., 22.11–25.98 dB for cover-stego, 20.58–21.66 dB for secret-recovered) compared to LF 1, 2, and 4, demonstrating robustness to transformations. For example, under 50% cropping, LF 3 maintains a cover error of 248.496 and PSNR of 48.571 dB, outperforming LF 4 (244.367, 48.713 dB).

To further quantify robustness, we introduce SSIM and BER metrics2–4,29–31. SSIM results (Table 8) show LF 3 achieving high perceptual similarity (0.92–0.97) across transformations, indicating preserved structural integrity. Table 9 presents BER results which demonstrate low bit error rates (0.03–0.08) for secret recovery, confirming accurate data extraction even under severe distortions like 50% cropping or rotation.

Steganalysis resistance is assessed using histogram, statistical, and CNN-based steganalysis (section “Comparison with state-of-the-art methods”, Figs. 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 and 18). LF 3 achieves low detection rates (< 45% accuracy, < 0.50 precision/recall/f1-score) against CNN-based steganalysis trained on J-UNIWARD, JMiPOD, and UERD, outperforming traditional methods (e.g., HUGO, 0.3 bpp, vulnerable to statistical attacks) and GAN-based approaches (e.g., Zhu et al., detectable by deep learning steganalysis). Histogram and statistical steganalysis (Figs. 9, 10) show indistinguishable cover-stego distributions, confirming LF 3’s ability to evade detection.

Feature-based steganalysis – tool 1

As detailed in35, introducing high-frequency steganographic noise to the cover image creates a vulnerable signal highly influenced by the image’s content. An image pre-processing step involving high-pass filtering is implemented to address this issue. This pre-processing step enhances the faint steganographic signal and mitigates the influence of image content. Subsequently, the pre-processed images are employed to train the convolutional neural network, where a Gaussian function serves as the activation function. The Gaussian activation function assists in effectively distinguishing between stego and cover signals based on prediction error values. Furthermore, a two-way softmax function determines whether the image is a cover or stego image.

Conclusion and future enhancements

This study presents a deep steganography framework leveraging a three-layered CNN architecture (preparation, hiding, reveal networks) optimized with Loss Function 3 (LF 3) to achieve a robust balance of high payload, imperceptibility, and resistance to steganalysis. LF 3, integrating mean and variance terms, achieves a payload capacity of 3–5 bits per pixel (bpp), surpassing traditional methods like HUGO (0.3 bpp) and rivaling GAN-based approaches (e.g., Zhu et al., 1–3 bpp; Kevin et al., 4.4 bpp). The framework demonstrates strong imperceptibility, with PSNR values of 22.11–25.98 dB for cover-stego and 20.58–21.66 dB for secret-recovered pairs, and SSIM values of 0.92–0.97 across Tiny-ImageNet, RGB-BMP, Linnaeus 5, and Sky-text datasets.

Robustness is a key strength of the proposed algorithm, as evidenced by numerical results under various transformations and steganalysis attacks. Against Gaussian noise, 10–50% cropping, and rotation, LF 3 maintains low cover error per pixel (13.81–19.99 without noise, 248.496 under 50% cropping) and high PSNR (47.675–73.481 dB for cover-stego, 46.883–71.213 dB for secret-recovered). SSIM remains high (0.90–0.97 under transformations), indicating preserved perceptual quality, while Bit Error Rate (BER) stays low (0.03–0.08), ensuring accurate secret recovery even under severe distortions like 50% cropping (BER: 0.07–0.08). The framework’s resistance to steganalysis is demonstrated by low detection rates against histogram, statistical, and CNN-based steganalysis (trained on J-UNIWARD, JMiPOD, UERD), with accuracy <45% and precision, recall, and f1-score <0.50, outperforming traditional methods (e.g., HUGO, vulnerable to statistical attacks) and GAN-based methods (e.g., Gao et al., detectable by deep learning steganalysis).

Compared to state-of-the-art methods, LF 3 achieves a superior balance of payload, quality, and robustness with lower computational cost (20% less memory,and 30% faster embedding than Zhu et al.). These results position the framework as a practical solution for secure data hiding in applications like medical imaging and covert communication.

Limitations

Despite the promising results, the proposed framework has certain limitations. The current implementation is primarily optimized for spatial domain embedding and may not fully exploit the potential benefits of frequency domain techniques. The computational efficiency, while improved compared to GAN-based approaches, still requires substantial GPU resources for training large-scale models. Additionally, the framework’s performance on very high-resolution images (beyond 512\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}512 pixels) has not been extensively tested due to memory constraints. The current loss function optimization, while effective, may benefit from further refinement to handle extreme compression scenarios more effectively37.

Future enhancements

Future work will explore extending the neural network architecture to the transform domain (e.g., DCT, wavelet) to further increase payload capacity without compromising visual quality. Integrating explainable AI techniques will enhance model interpretability, addressing trust issues in critical applications. Additionally, developing adaptive loss functions to counter emerging deep learning steganalysis methods will strengthen security against evolving threats. Research will also focus on optimizing the framework for real-time applications and exploring federated learning approaches to enhance privacy during training.
