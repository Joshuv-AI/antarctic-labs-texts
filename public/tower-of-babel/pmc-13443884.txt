CRA5 a high-fidelity compressed reanalysis atmospheric dataset for weather and climate research
Authors: Tao Han, Dazhao Du, Zhenghao Chen, Song Guo, Wanli Ouyang, Lei Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ERA5 reanalysis dataset, developed by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF), provides high-resolution, hourly global climate and weather data from 1950 to the present. However, its massive volume poses substantial storage and distribution challenges. To address this, we introduce CRA5, a highly compressed version of ERA5 generated by the neural network framework Aeolus. CRA5 reduces the 400 TB uncompressed float32 dataset to just 0.85 TB, achieving a 470× compression ratio. Notably, it offers over 100 times higher compression than the lossless GRIB files from the Copernicus Climate Data Store (CDS). Extensive experiments validate its numerical accuracy: CRA5 maintains consistent climatology and comparable power spectral density, yielding a mean absolute error of only 0.17 K for temperature across 37 vertical levels. Furthermore, it faithfully reconstructs extreme weather events and large-scale climatological patterns. By significantly lowering infrastructure barriers, CRA5 accelerates data access and facilitates broader collaboration in large-scale atmospheric research.

Background & Summary

The systematic observation and understanding of Earth’s climate system have undergone a significant transformation over the past century1,2. From early manual measurements taken at sparse surface stations, we have advanced to a global, integrated observational infrastructure encompassing satellite remote sensing, radar networks, and automated weather stations3. This observational revolution, coupled with major breakthroughs in numerical modeling4,5, has led to the generation of atmospheric datasets of unprecedented scale and complexity. Among these, ERA56, developed by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF), stands out as a widely used global reanalysis dataset. Spanning from 1950 to the present with hourly temporal resolution and approximately 31 km spatial resolution, ERA5 assimilates vast volumes of observational data using advanced data assimilation and numerical modeling techniques. It is widely used across a range of applications, including weather and climate research7,8, energy resource assessment9, agriculture10, hydrology11, and environmental monitoring12,13. Owing to its high accuracy, long temporal coverage, and broad variable set, ERA5 has become a foundational dataset for reconstructing historical weather patterns, validating predictive models, and informing decision-making in both scientific and industrial contexts6.

As shown in Fig. 1, as of 1 July 2025, the Copernicus Climate Data Store (CDS), which serves as the platform for accessing ERA5 data, has attracted over 140,000 registered users worldwide, with thousands of active users each day. Among the wide array of datasets available on the platform, ERA5 and its derived subsets are among the most frequently accessed data products. The CDS handles approximately 500,000 user requests and delivers over 100 TB of data daily. This popularity, however, comes at a cost. Due to its comprehensive coverage of global meteorological variables, the ERA5 dataset reaches approximately 400 TB in uncompressed float32 format, creating significant challenges in terms of data storage and distribution. For example, the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) currently generates approximately 287 TB of new data every day (https://www.ecmwf.int/en/computing/our-facilities/data-handling-system). This volume is projected to quadruple within the next decade, driven primarily by plans to increase model resolutions to the kilometer scale, expand ensemble prediction systems, and increase data output frequency14. Moreover, the transmission of such vast datasets imposes both technical and environmental burdens. ECMWF currently distributes over 100 TB of data per day to users worldwide, often saturating networks and driving up operational costs. Bandwidth limitations further exacerbate the situation, leaving many data requests queued or delayed. To alleviate the growing burden of data storage and distribution associated with ERA5, we introduceCRA5—a novel, compressed representation of the widely used ERA5 reanalysis dataset. Leveraging a fast and efficient neural network-based compression framework, CRA5 reduces the original 400 TB ERA5 dataset to just 0.85 TB, achieving a compression ratio of over 470× while preserving high data fidelity. Quantitatively, CRA5 achieves a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of approximately 0.17 K for temperature, approximately 0.33 m/s for meridional and zonal winds, less than 0.09 hPa for mean sea-level pressure, and about 7 m2/s2 for geopotential. Furthermore, CRA5 faithfully reproduces the spatial wind field structure and pressure distribution of extreme weather events such as hurricanes with high fidelity. CRA5’s efficient compression of multi-level atmospheric variables significantly reduces storage demands and data transmission costs, while simultaneously enhancing access speed and computational efficiency. This advancement is particularly beneficial for data-driven meteorological research7,15–18, where I/O bottlenecks frequently hinder performance. CRA5 facilitates real-time monitoring, accelerates data pipelines, and enables secure and scalable data sharing. By lowering infrastructure barriers and streamlining research workflows, CRA5 not only boosts scientific productivity but also fosters broader collaboration within the atmospheric and climate science communities.

Methods

Input Atmospheric Data and Preprocessing

The ERA5 dataset6, developed by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF), represented the fifth generation of atmospheric reanalysis data, which provided hourly estimates of global climate variables from 1950 to the present with a 31 km spatial resolution. ERA5 was accessed via the Copernicus Climate Data Store through the following links: https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/datasets/reanalysis-era5-pressure-levels and https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/datasets/reanalysis-era5-single-levels. As ECMWF’s flagship reanalysis product, ERA5 combined vast observational data with advanced modeling through 4D-Var data assimilation, delivering consistent atmospheric, ocean-wave, and land-surface parameters at 37 vertical levels. The CRA5 dataset was generated by compressing the ERA5 reanalysis dataset. For computational purposes and compatibility with our neural network framework, all variables were processed as single-precision floating-point numbers (float32). ERA5 data is distributed openly by the Copernicus Climate Change Service under the Copernicus Products License, which provides free, full, and open access to this data, and explicitly permits its use, modification, and redistribution. Due to these explicit reuse permissions, we are legally authorized to publish our derived CRA5 product under a CC BY 4.0 license. We focused on two representative subsets of ERA5: Pressure-level dataset. This dataset spans 37 isobaric surfaces from 1000 hPa to 1 hPa (lower stratosphere) and includes seven commonly used variables: geopotential (z), relative humidity (r), specific humidity (q), zonal wind component (u), meridional wind component (v), air temperature (t), and vertical velocity (w). Neural Network-based Atmospheric Data Compression

In atmospheric science, an ‘atmospheric state’ at a specific time was defined by the complete set of surface and pressure-level variables over a geographical grid. For computational purposes, this multi-dimensional structure was represented as a single high-dimensional tensor, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{V\times {L}_{a}\times {L}_{o}}$$\end{document}x∈RV×La×Lo. In this tensor representation, the first dimension, V = 268, stacked all atmospheric variables from their respective levels into a single channel axis. The other two dimensions, La = 721 and Lo = 1440, corresponded to the number of latitude and longitude points, respectively.

As shown in Fig. 2, the CRA5 dataset was generated using a data production workflow driven by a neural network-based compression framework, Aeolus (described in19). Rather than directly archiving the enormous raw ERA5 arrays, this workflow extracted and preserved the essential meteorological information through a two-stage data transformation process. First, we utilized a pre-trained variational autoencoder to reduce each hourly 3D atmospheric state tensor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{268\times 721\times 1,440}$$\end{document}x∈R268×721×1,440 from the ERA5 dataset to a compact feature representation (latent) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{256\times 72\times 144}$$\end{document}y∈R256×72×144, achieving approximately a 105× compression ratio while limiting information loss. Subsequently, we applied scalar quantization to convert the continuous latent representation y into discrete integer values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{y}$$\end{document}y^ by rounding each element to its nearest integer: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{y}=\lfloor \,y+0.5\rfloor $$\end{document}y^=⌊y+0.5⌋. This discretization step is required because the subsequent entropy encoder can only compress sequences of discrete symbols. To minimize information loss, the encoder network was trained jointly with this quantization step, such that the learned latent values cluster near integers. Second, we employed context-adaptive arithmetic coding to losslessly compress these integer symbols into the final binary stream. Arithmetic coding is a standard entropy encoding technique that maps a string of data symbols to a single floating-point value between 0.0 and 1.0. Unlike Huffman coding, which assigns an integer number of bits to each symbol, arithmetic coding can encode symbols using fractional bits, allowing it to approach the theoretical limit of compression (Shannon entropy). To achieve this, the arithmetic encoder requires an accurate probability distribution for each symbol. In our framework, this probability distribution was dynamically estimated by a probability model (often termed an entropy model or hyperprior network), which learned the spatial dependencies and frequency of patterns within the latent representation. By assigning shorter bit sequences to more probable symbols (common atmospheric patterns) and longer sequences to less probable ones, the arithmetic encoder significantly reduced the file size without any additional loss of information. For deep learning technical specifications (e.g., transformer architectures, hyperprior-based probability models, and the exact arithmetic coding implementation), we recommend readers refer to our methodology paper19. Through this two-stage process, we successfully transformed the massive volume of ERA5 records into the highly compressed CRA5 dataset, achieving an overall compression ratio of approximately 470×.Fig. 2A schematic illustration of neural network-based atmospheric data compression framework.

Data Records

The dataset generated during the current study is available in the Zenodo repository20. The full dataset (0.85 TB) is hosted on Hugging Face21 at https://huggingface.co/datasets/taohan10200/CRA5-Dataset to accommodate its large scale, while the metadata and core reproduction code are securely archived on Zenodo with the registered 10.5281/zenodo.15970494. This section describes the availability and content of the data. Due to the substantial storage capacity required, the CRA5 data records are distributed across these reliable platforms to optimize accessibility.

The dataset structure is carefully organized to reduce access barriers: Structured Binary Data (0.85 TB): The dataset contains meteorological information equivalent to the original 400+ TB ERA5 data, spanning continuous hourly records from 1979 to 2023. The data is intuitively nested within a CRA5/ main directory, which branches into 45 yearly subdirectories (1979–2023). Each file represents an hourly weather snapshot stored as a mathematically encoded binary stream (e.g., 1979/1979-01-01T01:00:00.bin for 01:00 UTC on 1979-01-01). These .bin files are the exact product of our context-adaptive entropy coding process.Neural Network-based Compression Algorithm Framework: Rather than merely supplying static model weights, we provide a complete, open-source computational ecosystem that powers the entire atmospheric data compression/decompression pipeline. Hosted alongside the pre-trained decoding weights (e.g., cra5_268v_300.pth within the models/ directory), this suite encompasses the Aeolus framework—a deeply integrated architecture featuring a transformer-based Variational Autoencoder (VAE) for multi-level spatial feature extraction, scalar quantization mechanisms, and a hyperprior-guided arithmetic encoding engine. Researchers can readily deploy these algorithms to seamlessly invert the context-adaptive compression process, efficiently translating the .bin bitstreams systematically back into high-fidelity, highly-resolved global atmospheric variables.

Technical Validation

To validate the quality of our compressed dataset CRA520, we compare the reconstructed (decompressed) CRA5 dataset with the original ERA5 dataset from the following perspectives. First, we introduce the evaluation metrics. Then, we assess the quality of CRA5 in terms of reconstructed atmospheric variables, mean and standard deviation distributions, power spectral density, extreme weather events, and global surface weather station observations.

A compression method might achieve low point-wise errors but at the cost of smoothing the data, thereby losing the energy associated with smaller-scale weather phenomena. To evaluate this crucial aspect, we employ Power Spectral Density (PSD) analysis22. Validation of Climatological Mean and Standard Deviation

While low instantaneous errors suggest that long-term statistics should be well-preserved, a direct examination of the climatology is crucial for diagnosing any systematic spatial biases introduced by the compression. Validation of Power Spectral Density

Figure 7 compares the power spectra23 of CRA5 and ERA5 across different scales for multiple variables. We note a key characteristic of the baseline ERA5 spectrum itself: a sharp drop-off in energy at high wavenumbers, corresponding to spatial scales smaller than approximately 100–200 km. This is an expected feature of any numerical weather model operating at a finite resolution (31 km for ERA5). The model’s effective resolution is coarser than its grid spacing, and numerical dissipation is employed to dampen energy at these poorly resolved small scales to maintain stability. Overall, CRA5 demonstrates spectral characteristics similar to ERA5. On large-scale (>2,000 km) stage, CRA5 closely matches ERA5 in all variables, confirming its reliability for analyzing large-scale systems such as the Siberian high, subtropical highs, atmospheric long waves, extratropical cyclones, and jet streams. At mesoscales (approximately 2,000 km), variables like geopotential and horizontal winds exhibit comparable energy levels, supporting the use of CRA5 in studying Meso-α phenomena, including East-Atlantic waves (approximately 1,500 km), tropical cyclones (300–1,500 km), troughs (200–1,000 km), low-level jets (300–1,000 km), and mesoscale convective systems (500–1,000 km). It also partially resolves Meso-β systems such as squall lines (100–1,000 km), gust fronts (10–300 km), and storm-scale features (20–100 km)24. Validation of Station Observations

To quantitatively assess the quality of the compressed CRA5 dataset, we conduct a comprehensive evaluation based on global surface weather station observations. Specifically, we used the WEATHER-5K dataset29, derived from the Integrated Surface Dataset (ISD) of the National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI, https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/land-based-station/integrated-surface-database), to evaluate 2-meter temperature, sea level pressure, and wind speed measurements against observations at 18,150 stations. Data from NCEI/NOAA is in the public domain and can be freely used, modified, and redistributed, permitting its use in our validation pipeline. For each day, we consider two timestamps: 00:00 and 12:00 UTC. Both the original ERA5 data and the decompressed CRA5 data are linearly interpolated to the geographic locations of these stations. Notably, the wind speed is computed by combining the u-wind (zonal) and v-wind (meridional) components. We then calculate the root mean squared error (WRMSE) between the interpolated values and the corresponding ground truth station measurements. As illustrated in Fig. 10, the RMSEs of CRA5 match closely those of ERA5 throughout all months in 2022 and 2023. The RMSEs of t2m are consistently around 2.5 °C, the WRMSEs of sea level pressure remain 1.5-3 hPa and the RMSEs of wind speed are below 2.25 m/s. These results demonstrate that CRA5 achieves reconstruction accuracy comparable to that of the original ERA5 when evaluated at real-world weather stations.Fig. 10Monthly RMSE comparison of ERA5 and CRA5 against global weather station observations in 2022 and 2023. Limitations

Despite its high compression ratio, the practical application of CRA5 has several limitations which also point to future research directions. The CRA5 dataset presented here is a static archive for the period 1979–2023. However, the AEOLUS workflow provided in our code repository allows users to independently download, compress, and append new ERA5 data from 2024 onwards. While the static archive provides a historical baseline, our open-source tools ensure the dataset can be kept up-to-date by the community.CRA5 is designed to decompress a full global atmospheric state at once. This makes it less efficient for common use cases that require only a small subset of the data, such as a single variable over a limited geographic domain. For such tasks, the overhead of decompressing the entire global field can outweigh the storage benefits. Developing a chunked or indexable format is a key area for future improvement.The full benefit of CRA5 for reducing data transfer times is contingent on its adoption by data providers like ECMWF. Without official hosting, the primary advantage of CRA5 remains its efficiency for local storage and archival, rather than as a primary distribution format.Because CRA5 relies on inherently lossy compression algorithms, it unavoidably introduces subtle floating-point precision differences compared to the source ERA5 dataset. While these absolute errors are low (as validated in prior sections) and entirely adequate for data-driven machine learning, climate statistics, pattern recognition, and general model validation, they may precipitate uncertainty in highly sensitive downstream computations. For instance, specific niche computational fluid dynamics applications that enforce strict physical conservation laws—such as solving higher-order derivatives for atmospheric divergence or calculating intricate mass/energy budgets—might be susceptible to localized error accumulation. We advise users undertaking such rigorous analytical tasks to carefully evaluate whether this fractional loss impacts their specific sensitivities.

While these absolute errors are low (as validated in prior sections) and entirely adequate for data-driven machine learning, climate statistics, pattern recognition, and general model validation, they may precipitate uncertainty in highly sensitive downstream computations. For instance, specific niche computational fluid dynamics applications that enforce strict physical conservation laws—such as solving higher-order derivatives for atmospheric divergence or calculating intricate mass/energy budgets—might be susceptible to localized error accumulation. We advise users undertaking such rigorous analytical tasks to carefully evaluate whether this fractional loss impacts their specific sensitivities.

Usage Notes

Data Efficiency and Practical Advantages

To contextualize the performance of CRA5 from a user’s perspective, we compared it with the standard data formats available from the CDS. Users typically download ERA5 data as either NetCDF or GRIB files. NetCDF files from the CDS are generally uncompressed. The GRIB format typically reduces the data size by a factor of approximately 2×-4×. Compared to this GRIB baseline, our 0.85 TB CRA5 dataset achieves a further size reduction of over 100×. Therefore, CRA5 offers a practical advantage of at least two orders of magnitude for data archival and transfer.

Computational Performance

The compression/decompression process is highly efficient, achieving compression and decompression speeds of 2,728.5 MB/s and 1,734.3 MB/s, respectively, on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, thus extensively alleviating the widespread I/O bottlenecks encountered in modern data-driven meteorological research. It is important to note that the decoding process jointly reconstructs all variables from the latent representation. After the full data is decompressed, users can then extract the specific variables relevant to their needs.
