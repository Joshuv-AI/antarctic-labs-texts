Study on the formation of micro-scale shaped charge jet based on diameter effect
Authors: Dexu Li, Zhengxiang Huang, Qiangqiang Xiao, Xin Jia, Yiming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractShaped charge jets (SCJ), characterized by high power, favorable directionality, and broad platform compatibility, are well suited for low-overload miniature weapon platforms. At the micro-scale, however, charge diameter introduces pronounced size effects that are not captured by classical shaped-charge theory, leading to a systematic overestimation of jet velocity. This study aims to address the limitations of conventional theory, particularly the inadequate prediction of jet velocity for micro-scale charges. Theoretical analyses and detonation experiments are first conducted to verify the existence of a size effect, with the experimental results confirming a marked reduction in detonation velocity at reduced charge diameters. Building on these findings and integrating classical jet-formation theory, the formulations for liner collapse velocity and jet velocity are refined. A pulsed X-ray test employing a 10 mm diameter shaped charge was performed to validate the proposed model. The measured jet tip velocity was [3077.0 m/s], while the conventional model predicted [3921.5 m/s] and the modified model predicted [3189.6 m/s]. The modified model exhibits strong agreement with the experimental data, reducing the prediction error relative to the conventional theory by approximately 20%. These results provide essential guidance for the design and optimization of micro-scale shaped charges.Keywords: Shaped charge, X-ray, Jet formation, Micro-scale chargeSubject terms: Engineering, Physics

Introduction

Recent years have witnessed substantial progress in microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) and artificial intelligence (AI), which has significantly advanced the development of micro aerial vehicles (MAVs). Contemporary MAVs achieve higher energy efficiency and lower operational costs, leveraging their compact size, agility, and stealth capabilities to undertake specialized missions in complex terrain and confined environments1. Regarding physical scale, MAV dimensions have undergone a marked reduction—from initial sizes of several meters, to tens of centimeters, and now to the centimeter scale—representing true miniaturization of aerial platforms. Concurrent weight reductions have yielded MAVs weighing only a few grams, as exemplified by NUS‑Robot2, the RF‑powered MAV3, KUBeetle4, and RoboBee5. Owing to their compact form, high maneuverability, and enhanced stealth, these vehicles are exceptionally well suited for specialized operations in complex terrains and confined spaces, underscoring their considerable potential for military applications.

Employing these MAVs as weapon‑system platforms could substantially enhance their utility for specialized missions. However, the platforms’ compact size and limited payload capacity impose stringent constraints on ammunition integration, underscoring the need for advances in micro‑munition technology. Compared with most conventional munition types, shaped charges offer superior penetration capability, precise directional control, and high compatibility with compact platforms. Moreover, their ability to function without imposing excessive overload on the delivery system renders them especially well suited for low‑overload platforms such as MAVs. Nonetheless, existing shaped‑charge warheads—both domestically and internationally—typically range in diameter from 20 mm to 100 mm, and investigations into smaller calibers, particularly at the millimeter scale, remain scarce6.

A shaped charge consists primarily of two main components: the liner and the explosive. Detonation of the explosive drives the liner to collapse, forming a focused jet characterized by high temperature, high velocity, and substantial kinetic energy, which is subsequently employed to damage or destroy the target7. The jet‑formation process involves a range of intricate, nonlinear dynamic mechanical phenomena, including the detonation behavior of high‑energy explosives and the high‑strain‑rate response of liner materials. These inherent complexities pose substantial challenges to the development of accurate theoretical models.

Research on the formation characteristics of shaped charges has advanced considerably since Birkhoff et al. first proposed the jet‑formation theory in 19488. Early models, however, tended to overestimate jet velocities. To account for velocity gradients within the jet, Pugh, Eichelberger, and Rostoker9refined Birkhoff’s framework and developed a one‑dimensional unsteady theory (PER theory), which applies to conical and wedge‑shaped liners under planar detonation initiation. Subsequently, Carleone et al10. extended the PER jet‑formation model to accommodate liners of more general geometries—such as spherical or trumpet‑shaped configurations—while incorporating both spherical and annular detonation waves. These successive refinements culminated in the establishment of a generalized jet‑formation theory.

Current research on shaped charges seldom accounts for diameter effects on detonation behavior. When a detonation wave propagates through a charge of uniformly small diameter, both the detonation velocity and the wave-front shape attain a quasi-steady state that is independent of the initial conditions11. In small-diameter charges, lateral expansion during detonation induces energy losses within the reaction zone. When the charge diameter is sufficiently large, these losses become negligible and the detonation wave propagates at near-ideal velocities. However, as the diameter decreases toward a threshold value (the limit diameter), the detonation velocity declines correspondingly. At the critical diameter, energy losses in the reaction zone become substantial enough to disrupt the balance between energy release and dissipation, thereby causing the detonation wave to fail to sustain itself—resulting in detonation failure12.

Research on the diameter effect in explosives has been extensive. Eyring et al13. were among the first to propose empirical formulas and theoretical models describing the relationship between detonation velocity and charge diameter. Subsequently, Wood and Kirkwood14 established the conservation laws for mass, momentum, and energy behind curved detonation wave fronts. The majority of later investigations have built upon the Wood–Kirkwood framework to further elucidate the diameter effect in explosives.

Lambourn, Bdzil, Stewart, and co-workers15–17successively proposed a theoretical model for detonation shock dynamics (DSD). This approach effectively characterizes the relationship between reaction rates and the stability of the detonation reaction zone, thereby establishing a quantitative link between the ideal detonation velocity and the curvature of the detonation wave front. Aslam et al18. pioneered a numerical method grounded in DSD theory, employing the Level Set technique to simulate detonation wave propagation under complex two-dimensional boundary conditions. Chiquete et al19. decomposed detonation performance calculations into separate timing and energy-release components. The timing analysis employed DSD theory to describe wave propagation, whereas energy release was modeled using the Pseudo-Reaction Zone (PRZ) approach. Analogous to a combustion reaction framework, the PRZ model simulates the finite-rate conversion of reactants to products, with the reaction rate governed by the normal surface velocity obtained from DSD computations.

Yoo20investigated detonation mechanisms in non‑uniform explosives, deriving a correlation between normal detonation velocity and wave‑front curvature that aligns with DSD theory. These findings underscore the capability of DSD theory to accurately represent curved detonation waves under non‑ideal conditions. Short et al21. applied DSD theory to examine the stable propagation of detonation waves in curved geometries, establishing relationships among angular detonation velocity, arc radius, thickness, and confinement parameters. Jiao et al22. developed a theoretical model for curved detonation wave propagation in strongly confined cylindrical small‑diameter charges, combining DSD theory with a linear streamline deflection‑angle assumption. Jiang et al23. utilized DSD theory to investigate the Level Set method for simulating detonation‑front propagation in two‑dimensional body‑fitted coordinate systems. Moreover, Jiang et al24. employed the DSD framework to analyze non‑ideal detonation wave propagation under inert‑media confinement, demonstrating that the shock polar method effectively captures interfacial interactions. Zhong et al25., also relying on DSD theory, studied the Level Set method for three‑dimensional non‑ideal detonation‑front propagation, introducing a finite‑difference scheme for the governing equations in non‑orthogonal body‑fitted coordinates and presenting the corresponding numerical solutions.

At present, DSD theory has emerged as a mainstream approach for describing non‑ideal detonation in explosives. Through a combination of numerical and analytical techniques, it enables the determination of key parameters—including detonation‑front propagation, detonation pressure, and detonation velocity—as functions of wave evolution. Consequently, DSD theory constitutes an exceptionally effective tool for investigating the non‑ideal detonation behavior of small‑scale shaped charges.

This study focuses on micro‑scale shaped charges, specifically those with charge diameters below the explosive’s limit diameter. Detonation velocity measurements were conducted on multi‑scale charges to establish the quantitative relationship between detonation velocity and charge diameter. Starting from classical DSD theory, an equation analogous to geometric optics was employed to describe the evolution of the detonation wave‑front shape. This approach yielded a detonation‑wave propagation model for JH‑2 explosive at small scales, which was subsequently validated against experimental data. Building upon the derived non‑ideal detonation model for JH‑2 explosive, we integrated shaped‑charge formation theory to analyze and characterize the formation of micro‑scale shaped‑charge jets (MSCJ). The theoretical model was further verified through pulsed X‑ray experiments, revealing the influence of diameter effects on MSCJ formation. The findings of this study offer valuable insights for the design and application of micro‑scale shaped charges.

Theoretical model

Over recent decades, research efforts have predominantly focused on enhancing jet velocity to improve penetration depth. Accordingly, studies of shaped charges have prioritized liner material optimization (e.g., copper versus tantalum alloys) and geometric innovations (e.g., conical, trumpet, and multi‑layered liners), while micro‑scale charge dynamics have received comparatively limited attention. The widely adopted Pugh–Eichelberger–Rostoker (PER) theory9accurately predicts jet‑formation parameters for planar‑initiated conical and wedge‑shaped liners. Carleone et al10. subsequently extended this framework by developing a generalized semi‑analytical model capable of simulating jet formation under diverse initiation conditions and liner configurations.

These effects fundamentally alter jet‑formation dynamics relative to those of conventional charges, imposing constraints on key parameters such as the jet velocity gradient and mass distribution. While current theoretical frameworks are effective for standard‑scale charges (diameter > 20 mm), they exhibit significant limitations in predicting micro‑scale jet behavior, owing to their inherent assumptions of steady‑state detonation fronts and steady‑state detonation. Consequently, it is imperative to refine the jet‑formation theory from new perspectives in order to enhance its engineering applicability.

Charge diameter effect

The DSD theory plays a critical role in studying the diameter effect of small-scale charges. Current research primarily focuses on charge types (e.g., PBX-950226, LX-1627, and JO-11C28 and confinement conditions, with limited application of diameter effect studies to shaped charges. Additionally, for shaped charges, JH-2 is a commonly used explosive, yet few studies have investigated its diameter effect. Therefore, this paper will study the diameter effect of JH-2, develop a detonation model tailored for JH-2, and provide insights into its detonation propagation characteristics.

Micro-scale shaped charge jet formation

The PER theory is one of the most fundamental and widely used frameworks in shaped charge jet formation theory. However, the PER theory is based on the Chapman-Jouguet (CJ) detonation model for jet formation research, which is suitable for large-scale shaped charge calculations. In contrast, for micro-scale shaped charges, the previous section’s findings indicate that the diameter effect of explosive detonation cannot be neglected, as it is a critical factor influencing jet formation. Moreover, the morphology of the detonation wave front varies with the charge diameter. Under these conditions, both the magnitude and direction of the detonation velocity on the liner surface change along the generatrix. Therefore, it is imperative to accord greater consideration to the explosive diameter effect in computations of the liner collapse process.

The formation process of SCJ can be divided into three main stages31: first, the collapse phase of the liner; second, the collision of the liner near the axis to form the jet; and finally, the elongation and eventual breakup of the shaped charge jet al.ong the axis. The most pronounced distinction between MSCJ and SCJ manifests during the liner collapse phase. Variations in detonation parameters induced by charge diameter effects result in non-uniform liner collapse velocities across the charge cross-section. Computational analyses based on conventional shaped charge jet theory exhibit deviations in predicted jet velocity.

Upon initiation of the shaped charge, the detonation wave and subsequent detonation products propagate radially outward, inducing inward collapse of the liner toward the central axis. The test uses an electrical method to determine the detonation velocity of multi-scale charges. Three probe wires are attached to the charge. The core principle of this measurement technique relies on the detonation wave’s instantaneous energy severing the probe wires, altering their resistance and producing three pulse signals. This enables accurate measurement of the time intervals between the detonation wave passing through the three probe wires. By carefully spacing the probe wires, the average detonation velocity can be computed. The test procedure involves initiating a electric detonator with a DC power source, which then detonates the charge. As the detonation progresses, it breaks each probe wire in sequence, generating signals that are converted by a multi-channel signal generator and recorded by a time interval measurement instrument.

The detonation velocity increases monotonically with charge radius and asymptotically approaches the ideal detonation velocity at larger radii. Notably, the rate of increase in detonation velocity accelerates markedly for smaller charge radii (e.g., < 10 mm).

By the detonation wave model discussed in this section, the wave front profiles at charge radiu of 2 mm, 3 mm, and 5 mm were obtained, as shown in Fig. 9. The results demonstrate an inverse relationship between charge radius and wave front curvature: smaller radii correlate with higher curvature magnitudes. Notably, the curvature exhibits a pronounced increase near the charge boundary.

Under the combined effects of the diameter dependency and steady-state non-ideal detonation processes, reductions in lateral expansion, energy density in the reaction zone, detonation wave intensity, and chemical reaction initiation rate lead to decreased detonation velocity and curvature of the shock front. The impact of this energy loss on shaped charge performance will be investigated in subsequent studies.

Conclusions

The purpose of this study is to overcome the limitations of conventional shaped charge theory, particularly its inadequacy in predicting the jet velocity of micro-scale shaped charges. These shortcomings primarily stem from the neglect of energy losses caused by diameter effects in miniaturized charges, which leads to reduced prediction accuracy. The main conclusions drawn are as follows:

(1) This study conducted both experimental and theoretical investigations into the diameter effects of JH-2 explosive, analyzing the influence of confinement materials and lateral rarefaction waves on the propagation of curved detonation waves. The resulting detonation wave propagation model effectively captures the actual behavior of detonation wave propagation, with calculated results showing good agreement with experimental data.

(2) For JH-2 explosive, the smaller the charge diameter, the more pronounced the diameter effects become in detonation wave propagation. Within the calculated range, when the charge diameter is 10 mm, the detonation velocity deficit is approximately 8.6%. As the charge diameter decreases, this velocity deficit gradually increases, reaching about 29.4% at a charge diameter of 4 mm.

(3) By incorporating detonation wave parameters into the conventional shaped charge calculation model, this approach explicitly accounts for the coupled effects of micro-scale charge diameter effects and the liner collapse process. Compared to the conventional model, this method reduces the deviation between calculated jet velocity and experimental values from approximately 27.4% to below 5%. This advancement significantly improves calculation accuracy and provides a solid theoretical foundation for optimizing the structural design of micro-scale shaped charges.

(4) Comparison between the conventional model and the modified model reveals that the conventional theory overestimates both the collapse velocity and the jet velocity. Additionally, deviations emerge in the velocity trends as they develop along the generatrix of the liner. This phenomenon suggests that the design of micro-scale shaped charges may deviate from conventional scaling laws, indicating that a direct scaling-down approach from large-diameter shaped charges is not feasible for micro-scale applications.

The results obtained in this study provide new perspectives and conclusive findings regarding the formation mechanisms of micro-scale shaped charges, offering valuable references for advancing the miniaturization and micro-scale development of shaped charge technology.
