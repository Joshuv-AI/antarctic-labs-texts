Deoxycholic acid derived from the gut microbiota involved in the regulation of adaptive thermogenesis in response to dietary protein restriction in plateau pika
Authors: Fuyu Shi, Zhiteng Yang, Liangzhi Zhang, Desheng Zou, Jiangkun Yu, Na Guo, Shien Ren, Xianjiang Tang, Chen Gu, Ruiping Xu, Yuning Ru, Yanming Zhang, Dehua Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMost winter-active mammals experience protein restriction. Gut microbiota is a key regulator of host energy homeostasis during nutrient deficiency, yet cross talk between microbiota and factors (e.g., hormones, signaling molecules) that regulate host energy metabolism in a low-protein (LP) context has not been studied sufficiently.ResultThe LP diet triggered the hepatic FGF21 adaptive metabolic pathway, which increased thermogenesis and reduced body weight, and this adaptive response was dependent on the composition and function of gut microbiota. Specifically, the LP diet induced a reshaping of the gut microbiota, altering its metabolic profile to increase deoxycholic acid levels and thereby increasing UCP1-induced thermogenesis of brown adipose tissue in an FGF21-dependent manner. Fecal transplantation with LP-associated microbiota increased thermogenesis through activation of GCN2-eIF2α-FGF21 signaling. Supplementation of the LP diet with yak fecal bacteria in plateau pika reduced UCP1-associated thermogenesis by altering the gut microbiome, decreasing deoxycholic acid production, suppressing activation of GCN2-eIF2α-FGF21 signaling, and alleviating LP-induced weight loss.ConclusionsOur study reveals an association between the gut microbiota and LP diet-associated regulation of FGF21 signaling and thermogenesis and further demonstrates that this relationship is influenced by interspecies microbial transfer, indicating a critical mechanism whereby horizontal microbial exchange between sympatric species enhances host energy homeostasis. These findings provide novel insights into our understanding of the adaptations of mammals to high-elevation environments. Introduction

During winter in the northern temperate zone, the progressive decrease in ambient temperature is followed by an increase in nutritional requirements for winter-active animals; the nutritive value of the available forage decreases, however, further reducing body mass and body fat mass [1–3]. Evidence suggests that when forced to weigh protein and energy intake in the face of an imbalance in nutrient supply, wild animals will prioritize protein over energy [4, 5]. This “protein-leverage effect” has been reported in both mice and humans [6–8]. Protein-deficient diets usually slow animal growth and decrease energy efficiency, and these effects are particularly notable for herbivores [9, 10]. For these herbivores, it is not merely a dietary inconvenience but a chronic physiological stressor that threatens survival. In turn, animals have evolved an array of behavioral and physiological adjustments to mitigate the adverse effects of long-term protein deficiency. These adjustments often involve novel foraging strategies and sophisticated metabolic reprogramming.

Metabolic strategies for maintaining health during nutrient deficiency exist across all species. In rodents, a low-protein (LP) diet can increase food intake, energy expenditure (EE), and non-shivering thermogenesis while reducing body weight to maintain energy homeostasis [11–14]. These adaptive metabolic stress responses are driven by hepatic fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), an endocrine hormone induced by a LP diet [15–18]. A LP diet causes the accumulation of uncharged tRNAs (i.e., lacking an attached amino acid) that bind to general control nonderepressible 2 (GCN2). This, in turn, triggers the phosphorylation of eukaryotic translation initiation factor-α (eIF2α), which activates FGF21 [19, 20]. FGF21 directly acts in adipose tissue to burn more energy and trigger more uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) expression, which is a thermogenic marker found in the inner membrane of mitochondria in brown adipose tissue (BAT) and beige fat [21, 22].

The gut microbiota of mammals is complex and ever-changing [23], susceptible to disruptions from nutrition, environmental factors, and enteric pathogens [24, 25]. The microbiota enhances the host’s capacity for nutrient uptake, utilization, and energy harvesting and generates numerous metabolites such as secondary bile acids (BAs) and short-chain fatty acids; hence, the microbiota contributes to host metabolism, immune function, and development [26, 27]. Microbiota-generated metabolites can act as signaling molecules via their cognate receptors to regulate appetite [28], energy storage [29], EE [30, 31], and thermogenesis [18]. One study indicated that the gut microbiota contributes to hepatic FGF21 adaptive metabolism under chronic protein deficiency [32]. This may be achieved via secondary BA-mediated activation of the gene encoding endogenous farnesoid X receptor (FXR), which further upregulates expression of FGF21 [33, 34]. These results suggest that BAs generated by the gut microbiota-induced BAs regulate host adaptive EE and thermogenesis via the gut-liver axis.

The Qinghai-Tibet Plateau presents a formidable challenge for mammals, characterized by a dual pressure of extreme energy demand due to severe cold and profound nutrient scarcity. With an average elevation exceeding 4000 m above sea level, the environment imposes relentless hypothermia and hypoxia. It encompasses various grassland types, ranging from meadows at ~ 3000 m to cold deserts at > 5000 m [35]. The average annual temperature is less than 0 °C, with winter temperatures as low as −40 °C. The average annual precipitation is only ~ 400 mm. The harsh cold temperatures and limited rainfall result in a short grass growth period of only 4 months [36]. Moreover, the biomass and nutrient composition of the forage varies substantially across seasons. The protein content of grassland forage drops sharply during winter, from ~ 18% in August to ~ 5% in April [37]. Critically, this maximum energy need in harsh winter coincides with a nadir in nutritional quality, thus creating a pronounced energy–protein imbalance for resident herbivores.

Plateau pika (Ochotona curzoniae) is a small herbivorous lagomorph that plays a crucial ecological role in alpine meadow ecosystems. It inhabits elevations ranging from 3100 to 5200 m and can survive the long, cold winters without hibernating or storing food [38, 39]. Surprisingly, unlike other plateau nonhibernating herbivores, plateau pika maintains relatively stable body weight and body fat content in winter, even in the face of poor forage nutrition—especially protein content [40, 41]. This remarkable physiological resilience makes it an attractive model for investigating winter-adaptive nutritional strategies. Plateau pikas have evolved typical physiological responses to the long cold winter, such as reduced daily EE, resting metabolic rate, body temperature, and triiodothyronine level as well as a relatively lengthy digestive tract and increased BAT mass and BAT thermogenesis [40–44]. However, these adaptations are not merely responses to cold but are intrinsically linked to the challenging nutritional landscape.

To cope with the multiple stresses (hypoxia, extreme cold, and low-quality forage), pikas have evolved unique gut microbiota profiles that enhance energy extraction and metabolic flexibility [45]. These specialized microbial communities are capable of enhancing energy harvest from fibrous plants and modulating host metabolism through short-chain fatty acid and bile acid transformation to mitigate nutritional deficiencies under harsh alpine conditions [46, 47]. Thus, the interaction among high-altitude environmental pressures, gut microbiota, and the scarcity of high-quality forage is critical for the survival and thermogenesis adaptation of pikas. Seasonal shifts in gut microbial enterotypes have been shown to contribute directly to host thermogenesis and thermoregulation in pikas [48, 49]. However, the means by which microbial symbionts affect dietary protein-induced thermogenesis in small mammals remains poorly understood. In winter, one intriguing behavior observed in pikas is the supplementation of their winter diet with yak feces, which causes a shift in their gut microbiota composition [50, 51]. This suggests a potential microbial strategy to assist host energy metabolism and maintain body weight during periods of protein scarcity [50, 51].

Therefore, to elucidate the interplay between dietary protein, gut microbiome, and adaptive thermogenesis, we tested the following three hypotheses: (1) a LP increases adaptive thermogenesis and reduces body weight in plateau pika via activation of the FGF21 signaling pathway; (2) specific microbial metabolites, particularly the secondary bile acid deoxycholic acid (DCA), are involved in modulating the LP-induced FGF21 stress response; and (3) LP diet supplemented with yak fecal bacteria in pikas (i.e., LPY diet) suppresses the FGF21 stress response and inhibits thermogenesis by altering the composition of the gut microbiota (and thus overall microbial function) with concomitant effects on the maintenance of body weight.

Results

LP diet regulates adaptive thermogenesis through FGF21 signaling

To investigate the effect of dietary protein content on host thermogenesis, 24 plateau pikas were fed one of three diets with varying protein content for 4 weeks (Fig. 1a). Body weight in the LP (6% protein) group was significantly lower than that of the high-protein (HP, 18% protein) and moderate-protein (MP, 12% protein) groups (analysis of variance ANOVA with post hoc Tukey’s test: p < 0.05; Fig. 1b), although pikas of the LP group consumed significantly more daily food and energy (p < 0.05; Figs. 1c and S1a). The mass of inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT) in the LP group was significantly lower than that of the HP and MP groups (p < 0.01, Fig. 1d and e). The ratio of serum triiodothyronine (T3) to thyroxine (T4) was significantly higher in the LP group than in the HP group (Fig. S1b; p < 0.001). The level of circulating FGF21 as well as FGF21 expression in the liver was elevated in the LP group relative to HP group (Fig. 1f and g), although the levels of free fatty acids did not differ significantly among the three groups (p > 0.05; Fig. S1c).

The eIF2α is phosphorylated by GCN2, resulting in increased FGF21 expression in the liver. We found that the Gcn2 expression and phosphorylation status of eIF2α were both significantly elevated in the LP group compared with the HP group (p < 0.05; Fig. 1h and i), coinciding with activation of FGF21 signaling. In addition, we sought to determine if the increased FGF21 signaling in the LP group led to an increase in thermogenic adaptation. Therefore, we measured expression of UCP1 and other thermogenic genes as well as the FGF21 receptor (βKlotho) in BAT and iWAT. As expected, the expressions of βKlotho and UCP1 were both significantly elevated in the LP group compared with the HP group (p < 0.05; Fig. 1j and 1k). The relative expression of cell death-inducing DNA fragmentation factor, alpha subunit-like effector A (Cidea) and β3-adrenergic receptor (Adrb3) in BAT, as well as the expression of Ucp1 and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1 (Pgc1) in iWAT was significantly upregulated in the LP group compared with the HP group (p < 0.05; Fig. 1l; Fig. S1d). The surface body temperature (Tsurface) and skin temperature of BAT (TBAT) were both significantly higher in the LP group than that of HP group (p < 0.01, Fig. 1m). The relative area of adipocytes in iWAT was significantly lower in the LP group compared with the HP group, indicating that the LP diet induced browning of iWAT (p < 0.01; Fig. S1e). The relative expression of adipose triacylglyceride lipase (Atgl) and hormone-sensitive lipase (Hsl) was significantly upregulated, while the acetyl coenzyme A carboxylase 1 (ACC1) in iWAT was deregulated in the LP group compared with the HP group (p < 0.05; Fig. S1f). These results suggested that a LP diet could regulate host thermogenesis by activating the GCN2-eIF2α-FGF21 pathway in plateau pika.

LP diet alters both the composition and function of the gut microbiota as well as its metabolites

To investigate how a LP diet affects gut microbiota composition and function and its metabolites, shotgun metagenomic sequencing of genomic DNA in cecal content was performed, and the concentration of BAs was measured. Following quality control and removal of reads assigned to the pika genome, > 640 Gb of paired-end sequencing data were generated from the metagenomes. The α-diversity, as determined with the Chao1, was higher, while the Shannon index was lower in the LP group than in the HP group (p < 0.01; Fig. 2a). Taxonomic profiling of the metagenomes indicated that the composition of the cecum microbiota was distinct among the LP, MP, and HP groups based on principal coordinate analysis (Adonis p = 1e-4; Fig. 2b). Bacillota (66.5%) and Bacteroidetes (22.2%) were the most abundant phyla in all three groups, and Oscillospiraceae (40.1%), Lachnospiraceae (15.9%), Prevotellaceae (8.8%), and Bacteroidaceae (4.0%) were the most abundant families (Fig. S2a and b; Supplementary data 1). Therein, the abundance of the family Oscillospiraceae and Lachnospiraceae in the LP group was greater compared to the HP and MP diet groups (p < 0.05 or p = 0.09; Fig. 2c; Supplementary data 1). In the top 10 most abundant genera, the abundance of Flavonifractor, Dysosmobacter, Lawsonibacter, and Oscillibacter, which belonged to Oscillospiraceae, was greater in the LP group compared to the HP and MP groups (p < 0.05; Figs. 2d and S2c; Supplementary data 1).

Targeted metabolomics was further conducted using ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry to profile and quantify the BAs in the HP and LP groups. A total of 21 BAs were analyzed, and both the concentrations and relative compositions of the BAs varied between the HP and LP groups (Fig. 2e; Fig. S3a and b; Supplementary data 2). The relative proportion of DCA (the most abundant of the secondary BAs) was significantly greater in the LP group (43.7%) than in the HP group (24.0%; p < 0.01; Fig. 2e). Based on the co-assembly and binning of all metagenomes for the cecal microbiota, 1382 high- or medium-quality prokaryotic metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) were recovered (Supplementary data 3). Specifically, a correlation analysis revealed that in the LP group, the microbiome belongs to the families Lachnospiraceae, Oscillospiraceae, and Ruminococcaceae and had the closest correlation with DCA abundance and FGF21 abundance (Fig. 2f and Supplementary data 4). In addition, we measured expression of the hepatic FXR protein and gene, which can upregulate production of FGF21, leading to increased adiponectin release from adipocytes and adipose-tissue browning [34]. The LP diet upregulated the expression of FXR protein and the Fxr gene and its downstream targets, small heterodimer partner (Shp) and bile salt export pump (Bsep) gene expression in the liver compared with the HP diet (Fig. 1n and o; Fig. S1g and h; p < 0.05), although the expression of Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5 (Tgr5) in BAT and iWAT did not differ significantly among all groups (p > 0.05; Fig. S1i and j). Tgr5 is activated by BAs to stimulate adaptive thermogenesis [52].

Metagenomic data were subjected to metabolic pathway analysis with annotation from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database [53]. The metabolic functions differed among the HP, MP, and LP diet, with the “carbon fixation pathways in prokaryotes” “citrate cycle (TCA cycle),” and “glycolysis/gluconeogenesis” contributing to energy metabolism, with significant enrichment in the HP and MP groups compared with the LP group (false discovery rate, FDR < 0.05; Fig. 2g; Supplementary data 5). In addition, for annotation analysis based on KEGG orthology genes, 278 MAGs (> 20%) were identified as being involved in BA transformations, including deconjugation, oxidation, and dehydroxylation. Of these, 275 MAGs encoded at least one bile salt hydrolase (BSH: EC3.5.1.24), which hydrolyzes conjugated bile salts into deconjugated BAs, such as deoxycholic acid (DCA; Supplementary data 6). These MAGs were assigned to five phyla, namely Bacillota (264 MAGs), Bacteroidota (7 MAGs), Patescibacteria (2 MAGs), Pseudomonadota (1 MAG), and Actinomycetota (1 MAG), indicating that multiple phylotypes are capable of bile-salt hydrolysis in the cecum of plateau pika. These BSH-encoding MAGs primarily belonged to families Lachnospiraceae (44.0%), CAG-74 (13.5%), Oscillospiraceae (8.7%), Anaerovoracaceae (7.6%), and Ruminococcaceae (7.3%) and were predominately assigned to genera UBA3282 (53 MAGs), Acutalibacter (31 MAGs), UBA11524 (31 MAGs), and RGIG8773 (11 MAGs; Supplementary data 6). We next investigated the relative abundance of the MAGs. One million random metagenomic reads from each sample were extracted and aligned to the genomes, resulting in an average mapping rate of 61% (Supplementary data 2). We found that the BSH counts and BSH activity were more enriched in the LP group than the HP group (p < 0.05; Fig. 2h and i). Furthermore, 36 and 26 BSH-encoding MAGs were more enriched in the LP group than the HP and MP groups, respectively (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 2j and Fig. S2d).

LPY counters the increase in BAT thermogenesis induced by LP diet

To investigate whether inclusion of yak fecal bacteria in the pika diet could alter the activation of the FGF21 pathway and further downregulate thermogenesis, we administered yak fecal bacteria to each LP pika via daily oral gavage (i.e., the LPY group; Fig. 3a). Body weight gain in the LPY group was significantly greater than in the LP group (Fig. 3b; Student’s t-test: p < 0.05), although dairy food and energy consumption did not differ between the two groups (Student’s t-test: p > 0.05; Figs. 3c and S4a). Both iWAT weight and the ratio of iWAT to body weight in the LPY group were significantly greater than in the LP group (p ≤ 0.05; Fig. 3d and e). The ratio of serum triiodothyronine (T3) to thyroxine (T4) was significantly lower in the LPY group than that of the LP group (Fig. S4b; p < 0.05). Moreover, the level of circulating FGF21 as well as FGF21 expression in the liver was lower in the LPY group compared with the LP group (Fig. 3f and g), although the concentration of free fatty acids did not differ significantly between the two groups (p > 0.05; Fig. S4c).

The expression of Gcn2 and phosphorylation status of eIF2α, which can activate FGF21, were both significantly decreased in the LPY group compared with the LP group (p < 0.05; Fig. 3i). In addition, we examined whether the LPY diet could reduce thermogenesis. In this regard, we measured the expression of UCP1 and other thermogenic genes as well as the FGF21 receptor (βKlotho) in BAT and iWAT. Each of UCP1, Cidea, and βKlotho in BAT and Ucp1 and Pgc1α in WAT was significantly downregulated in the LPY group compared with the LP group (p < 0.05; Fig. 3k and l; Fig. S4d). The surface body temperature (Tsurface) and interscapular BAT (TBAT) were higher in the LP group than that of LPY group (p < 0.05, Fig. 3m). The relative area of adipocytes in WAT tended to be higher in the LPY group than in the LP group, which indicated that the LPY diet reduced adipose-tissue browning (p < 0.05; Fig. S4e). The relative expression of Atgl and Hsl was significantly deregulated, while the Acc1 in iWAT was upregulated in the LPY group compared with the LP group (p < 0.05; Fig. S4f).

LPY decreases thermogenesis by changing the cecal microbial composition and function

The α-diversity as determined with the Chao1 index was lower (p < 0.05), while Shannon index was not significantly different (p > 0.05) in the LPY group than in the LP group (Fig. 4a). Principal coordinate analysis revealed a significant separation between the LP and LPY groups with regard to the composition of the gut microbiota (Adonis p = 0.015; Fig. 4b). Bacillota (70.3%) and Bacteroidetes (18.7%) were the most abundant phyla, and Oscillospiraceae (43.1%) and Lachnospiraceae (16.8%) were the most abundant families (Fig. S4g and h; Supplementary data 1). Therein, the abundance of the family Lachnospiraceae in the LP group was greater compared to the LPY diet groups (p = 0.09; Fig. 4c). Gene ratio data for fatty-acid biosynthesis genes indicated a higher ratio for the LPY group than the LP group (p < 0.05; Fig. 4d; Supplementary data 5), indicating that the LPY diet contributed to adipogenesis. We found that the BSH counts (p = 0.055) and BSH activity (p < 0.05) were tend to be lower in the LPY group than the LP group (Fig. 4e and f). Twenty-two MAGs (10 MAGs belong to family Lachnospiraceae) encoded BSH genes that were more enriched in the LP group than the LPY group (Fig. 4g). Targeted metabolomics analysis revealed that the proportion of DCA among BAs was significantly lower in the LPY group (36%) than in the LP group (43.7%; p < 0.05; Fig. 4h; Fig. S3b and c; Supplementary data 2). In addition, the LPY diet downregulated the expression of FXR protein and Fxr, Shp, and Bsep genes in the liver compared with the LP group (Fig. 3n and o; Fig. S4i and j; p < 0.05), although Tgr5 expression in BAT and iWAT did not differ significantly between the two groups (p > 0.05; Fig. S4k and l).

LP diet-induced changes in the gut microbiota alter the gut-liver axis by activating the FGF21 pathway

To investigate the role of the gut microbiota in mediating host metabolic phenotypes, we transplanted cecal microbiota from each of the HP, MP, LP, and LPY groups of pikas to pikas that had been treated with antibiotics (Fig. 5a). This fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) did not affect body mass, food intake, or iWAT weight among the FMT-HP, FMT-MP, and FMT-LP groups (Fig. S5a, b, c, d; p > 0.05), although the FMT-LP group had higher circulating levels and hepatic protein expression of FGF21 compared with the FMT-HP group (p < 0.05; Fig. 5b and c). Gcn2 expression and the phosphorylation status of eIF2α were both significantly elevated in the FMT-LP group compared with the FMT-HP group (p < 0.05; Fig. 5d and e). The expression of UCP1, Cidea, Pgc1α, Pparγ, and βKlotho in BAT and of Ucp1 and Pgc1α in iWAT was higher in the FMT-LP group than in the FMT-HP group (p < 0.05; Figs. 5f, g, - 5 h and S5e). The relative area of adipocytes in WAT was significantly higher in the FMT-HP group than in the FMT-LP group (p < 0.05; Fig. S5f and g). The expression of Fxr gene was also higher in the FMT-LP group than in the FMT-HP group (p < 0.05; Fig. 5i). Metagenomics analysis revealed that the shifts in the recipient cecal microbiota were confirmed by the results of the transplantation experiment. Treatment of the pikas with an antibiotic mixture reduced α-diversity, as indicated by the Chao1 and Shannon index. The Chao1 and Shannon index were lower in the FMT-LP group than in the FMT-HP group (p < 0.001, Fig. 5j). The composition of the cecum microbiota was distinct among the FMT-HP, FMT-MP, and FMT-LP groups based on principal coordinate analysis (p < 0.05, Fig. 5k). Microbial composition analysis revealed that the relative abundance of family Lachnospiraceae, as well as the genus Claveliimonas, Mediterraneibacter, and Sporofaciens belonging to Lachnospiraceae, were significantly higher in the LP group than in the HP group (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 5l and m; Fig. S6b and c; Supplementary data 7).

Results of the metagenomic analysis showed that antibiotic treatment reduced α-diversity, as indicated by the Chao1 and Shannon index. The Chao1 and Shannon index tended to be higher in the FMT-LPY group than in the FMT-LP group (p = 0.05 or p = 0.08, Fig. 5j). The composition of the cecum microbiota was distinct between the FMT-LPY and FMT-LP groups based on principal coordinate analysis (p < 0.05; Fig. 5k). Microbial composition analysis revealed that the relative abundance of family Lachnospiraceae, as well as the genera Claveliimonas, Mediterraneibacter, and Sporofaciens belonging to Lachnospiraceae, was significantly higher in the LP group than in the LPY group (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 5l and m; Figure S6b and c; Supplementary data 7).

Discussion

The survival and fitness of a mammalian host and its symbiotic gut microbiota rely on bidirectional communication to promote metabolic homeostasis, and any long-term nutritional imbalance can alter host-microbe interactions, with possible negative consequences [54, 55]. The seasonal protein content of natural grasses varies widely across the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, so it is challenging for winter-active mammals to maintain energy homeostasis. In the present study, we investigated the effects of a LP diet on the makeup and function of the gut microbiota of a small herbivore at high elevation and how relative protein deficiency affected the regulation of thermogenesis. We found that a LP diet directly altered the composition and function of gut microbiota and increased the abundance of both the microbial metabolite DCA and FGF21 in liver and promoted host adaptive thermogenesis. An FMT experiment confirmed that LP-induced changes in the microbiota increased the abundance of a BA receptor (FXR), activated GCN2-eIF2α-FGF21 signaling, and upregulated the abundance of the FGF21 receptor, thus leading to increased thermogenesis. Furthermore, FMT with LPY-induced microbiota attenuated the GCN2-eIF2α-FGF21 pathway that was induced by host thermogenesis, demonstrating a critical mechanism whereby interspecies microbial transfer between sympatric species enhances host energy homeostasis. These data suggest that BAs such as DCA that are synthesized by the gut microbiota regulate adaptive thermogenesis through the GCN2-eIF2α-FGF21 pathway during dietary protein restriction in a small mammal at high elevation (Fig. 6).

Model for the cross talk between gut microbiota and FGF21 via the FGF21 signaling pathway in mediating thermogenesis in pikas fed a LP diet. Upward/downward arrows denote an increase/decrease in hormone levels or gene expression in the LP or LP-FMT group as compared with the HP or HP-FMT and LPY or LPY-FMT groups

Small mammals in the wild have evolved multiple behavioral and physiological strategies for surviving in nutrient-poor environments; these strategies include increasing food intake and energy, non-shivering thermogenesis, and upregulating UCP1 expression [41, 56, 57]. Strong evidence has suggested that dietary protein restriction—without energy restriction—is sufficient to increase both food intake and EE while decreasing body weight in mice [7, 12, 16, 56, 58–60]. The LP-induced increase in food intake and energy represents an adaptive response to the increase in EE, a classic manifestation of the “protein leverage hypothesis” [8, 61]. Consistent with previous studies in mice, the LP diet resulted in significantly higher food intake and energy intake while exhibiting a significant decrease in body weight, although we did not directly measure EE in pikas. This apparent energy imbalance strongly points to a substantial upregulation of their metabolic rate. In this study, the significant increase in the T3/T4 ratio in the LP group—a key indicator of heightened thyroid hormone activity—directly reflects an elevated basal metabolic rate (BMR) [62]. Moreover, our infrared thermography revealed a marked increase in the Tsurface and TBAT in the LP group, a direct indicator of enhanced adaptive thermogenesis. UCP1-dependent thermogenesis is a key driver of EE in rodents [59]. Protein restriction, even at different environmental temperatures, increases UCP1 expression in BAT and promotes the browning of WAT, thus verifying that UCP1-dependent thermogenesis contributes to the increase in EE during protein restriction [63]. This LP-induced thermogenic signal activation requires hepatic FGF21, which is activated by the GCN2-eIF2α-FGF21 pathway [17, 22, 64]. βKlotho is a FGF21 receptor that is widely distributed in BAT, WAT, and brain, and βKlotho activates signaling that promotes UCP1-dependent thermogenesis [65]. Our results indicate that the LP diet increased FGF21 abundance in both plasma and liver and upregulated the expression of βKlotho and UCP1 in both BAT and WAT. These results establish that the GCN2-eIF2α-FGF21 pathway is involved in regulating food intake and adaptive thermogenesis during protein restriction in plateau pika.

In mammals, the composition of the gut microbiota can be affected by diet, and gut microbes constitute a key link between nutrient utilization and the physiological well-being of the host [66]. In our study, the LP diet affected both the diversity and composition of the gut microbiota, such as increasing the ratio of Bacillota to Bacteroides, which increases the sensing and utilization of dietary protein [67, 68]. We also found that the LP diet caused an overall average increase in Bacillota, and that this could be attributed to the families Lachnospiraceae (mainly the genus UBA3282) and Ruminococcaceae. Increased abundance of Lachnospiraceae species in the gut has been positively correlated with host lipid catabolism and thermogenesis [49, 69, 70] and negatively linked with obesity [71]. In addition, many studies have shown that for mammals inhabiting extreme environments, the gut microbiota mainly helps promote fat deposition and body weight gain in summer and promotes lipolysis in winter, at least in brown bears (Ursus arctos) [72] and yaks (Bos grunniens) [37]. This seasonal metabolic regulation is underpinned by the unique evolutionary adaptations of the plateau pika’s gut microbiota. It is specifically enriched for strong fermenters like Lachnospiraceae that can degrade high-fiber forage and protein, a trait that provides a crucial survival advantage during the resource-scarce winter months [45, 73]. Moreover, as suggested by previous studies, it possesses a sophisticated capacity for BA modification, allowing it to fine-tune host energy conservation and select for functional bacteria to maintain a resilient community [74]. Our pathway enrichment analysis of gut microbiota revealed that the microbial genes that were enriched in response to the various experimental diets were mainly related to energy and lipid metabolism (carbon fixation pathways in prokaryotes and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis), and that fewer of these genes were enriched in response to the LP diet than the HP diet, indicating that the LP diet may place limitations on energy utilization.

The gut microbiota contributes to the host FGF21 pathway’s adaptive response to dietary protein restriction [32]. Yang et al. [75] reported that FMT from mouse donors that had been fed a methionine-restricted diet led to increased BAT thermogenesis and decreased fat deposition [75]. Our network analysis revealed that the abundance of Acetatifactor sp. bin195, Acetatifactor sp. bin179, UBA3282 sp. bin315, UBA3282 sp. bin347, UBA3282 sp. bin348, UBA3282 sp. bin349, and CAG-590 sp. bin 228 positively correlated with FGF21 abundance in the liver, DCA abundance in the cecum, and host thermogenesis. These findings point to a robust connection between the gut microbiota, DCA, and FGF21 signaling in this context, although further research is required to mechanistically confirm this link. Our FMT experiment confirmed that the LP-induced microbiota increased hepatic FGF21 expression, significantly upregulated expression of UCP1 protein in BAT and Ucp1 mRNA in WAT, and promoted iWAT browning. Microbial functional analysis revealed that the LP-induced microbiota was significantly enriched in genes encoding BSH compared to the HP-induced microbiota. Consistent with this genetic enrichment, we also observed a significant increase in the overall BSH activity in the LP group. Expressed exclusively by gut bacteria, BSH catalyzes the deconjugation of primary bile acids—a prerequisite for secondary BA synthesis—making it a direct biomarker of microbial functional capacity [76, 77]. Ample evidence suggests that microbial modification of BAs is an important mechanism by which the microbiota can interact with the host to affect appetite, lipid metabolism, energy metabolism, and thermogenesis [78–80]. A diet rich in protein, but not one rich in fiber, was found to strongly correlate with changes in the BA profile in obese people, and protein restriction could significantly increase levels of unconjugated BAs [81]. Consistent with these results, our results showed that the LP diet increased the cecal concentration of DCA, which is an unconjugated BA. Both FXR and TGR5 are major receptors for BAs. The FXR is co-expressed with members of the FGF19 subfamily (contains FGF15, FGF19, and FGF21) in the liver and enteroendocrine cells [34], and TGR5 is highly expressed in BAT, resulting in activation of UCP1 expression in BAT [82]. Secondary BAs, including DCA, cholic acid, and chenodeoxycholic acid, play roles in the FXR-mediated reduction of liver triglyceride levels via the gut microbiota-FXR-FGF21 pathway [83], and an overabundance of FGF21 reduces cholesterol and triglyceride synthesis, induces conversion of adipocytes from WAT to beige fat cells, and increases the expression of thermogenic genes in BAT [22, 84]. In the present study, the LP diet upregulated FXR protein levels in the liver, which occurred concurrently with the activation of the GCN2-eIF2α-FGF21 signaling pathway, and increased the expression of UCP1 and the FGF21 receptor βKlotho in BAT. The FMT experiment confirmed that in comparison with the HP-induced microbiota, BAT thermogenesis induced by the LP-induced microbiota was mainly caused by the increase in FXR abundance and activation of GCN2-eIF2α-FGF21 signaling. While we will further investigate this link, our finding is bolstered by existing literature showing that FXR activates FGF21 [34]. Our results also showed that none of our three diets affected Tgr5 expression, ruling out the possibility that BA-mediated activation of Tgr5 increased thermogenesis. All these data indicate that LP-induced thermogenesis is regulated by the gut microbiota as well as the gut-liver axis via activation of FGF21 signaling.

Pezeshki et al. [85] reported that for mammals fed a strictly plant-based diet, inadequate consumption of plant protein may lead to amino-acid deficiency and decrease the efficiency of energy utilization [85]. Thus, herbivores have evolved strategies to mitigate the adverse physiological effects of chronic protein deficiency. While plateau pikas and yaks have traditionally been considered complete competitors [86], their mutualism represents a far more significant evolutionary dynamic. Plateau pikas supplement their food intake by eating yak feces, as demonstrated by the identification of yak DNA in pika stomach contents and convergence in the yak and pika microbiotas in winter [50, 51]; thus, neither pika body weight nor body fat mass changes significantly across seasons [41]. This interaction challenges the simple competition hypothesis, which would predict that allopatric pikas (those not living with yaks) would develop a lax gut microbiota and less efficient gut microbiomes due to a lack of competitive pressure [51]. Conversely, sympatric species may evolve strategies that confer a competitive ecological advantage [87, 88]. Indeed, coprophagy has been shown to maintain host energy metabolism and cognitive performance by stabilizing the gut microbiota [70]. This interaction provides direct metabolic benefits and suggests a mechanism of inter-species microbial support [50, 89]. This finding further explains the horizontal transmission of microbes between these sympatric species. Such horizontal transmission can allow a host to acquire microbes from other species and maintain high species richness [46, 90]. Gut microbial diversity is a critical biomarker for health and metabolic capacity [91]. A higher diversity expands the dietary niche of wild animals, improving host fitness by providing a comprehensive capacity to process complex compounds in plant-based diets [92, 93]. Therefore, this strategy of acquiring gut microbiota from sympatric yaks is not merely a dietary supplement but a profound evolutionary adaptation that maintains a functional and resilient gut community, directly underpinning the pika's ability to maintain energy homeostasis and survive the unforgiving plateau winter. Our present results showed that compared with the LP diet, the LPY diet clearly improved the ability of pikas to synthesize fatty acids, which was manifested as enrichment of genes in the fatty-acid biosynthesis pathway. This finding suggests that the gut microbiota contributes to fat storage in the host, supporting our observation that LPY could increase WAT weight. Moreover, LPY decreased the microbiota (UBA3282 sp. bin314, UBA3282 sp. bin346 and Lawsonibacter sp. bin572) that catalyzed DCA production and reduced DCA concentration, and LPY-induced microbiota inhibited UCP1 expression via downregulation of the GCN2-eIF2α-FGF21 signaling pathway, thus maintaining growth.

Mammals living at high elevations often experience both cold temperatures and nutrient deficiency. When animals are exposed to acute cold, they initially shiver to maintain core body temperature until BAT thermogenesis is activated. Because plateau pikas fed the LP diet synthesized more UCP1 in BAT and had increased WAT browning, we speculate that a LP diet may promote BAT thermogenesis more readily and rapidly in response to cold temperatures. This adaptive thermogenesis may benefit animal survival in extreme environments. Some investigators have hypothesized that prior experience with dietary protein restriction may allow animals to more readily maintain temperature during acute cold stress, and cold-induced increases in EE were found to be lower in mice fed a low-protein diet because BAT thermogenesis may be more energy efficient than shivering thermogenesis [63]. Moreover, by consuming yak feces, plateau pikas partition resources, reduce direct dietary overlap, and transform a competitive interaction into an asymmetric mutualism. This strategy allows them to benefit from a byproduct of yak foraging, fostering a unique form of commensalism at the microhabitat level.

Furthermore, while strong correlational evidence supports the proposed DCA-FGF21 signaling pathway [94, 95], it requires direct experimental validation. Establishing DCA’s role as the key effector molecule will necessitate future studies, for instance, through in vivo DCA supplementation or inhibition experiments in the pika model. This future experiment is critical for moving from correlation to causation and fully elucidating this novel metabolic pathway. Additionally, we will functionally validate the inferred BSH activity. A key future direction is to isolate the identified BSH-producing strains and perform in vitro metabolism assays. This will causally link these microbes to the observed bile acid profiles and solidify our proposed mechanism.

Materials and methods

Animals

All pikas used in this study were licensed, approved, and compliant with the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shandong University for animal experimentation and research (SYDWLL-2023–067). Plateau pikas were captured using nooses placed at burrow entrances in the alpine meadow of Laji Mountain (3700 m above sea level) in Guide, Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau, in December 2023. They were then transported to the animal laboratory at the Northwest Institute of Plateau Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Xining city, Qinghai Province) where they were housed individually in cages (50 cm × 35 cm × 20 cm). The room temperature was maintained at a constant 14 ± 1 °C with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. Their gender was assessed and cages marked accordingly. The pikas were fed standard rabbit pellets (Xiaoshu Youtai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and given pure water ad libitum. Pikas were acclimated in the laboratory for 3 weeks prior to experimentation.

Experimental design

Experiment 1

To investigate the impact of dietary protein on pika physiology and cecal microbiota, 24 pikas were divided into 3 groups (8 per group, 4 males and 4 females). Each individual pika was randomly assigned to be fed the HP, MP, or LP diet (Table S1). Body weight and food intake were assessed every 2 days throughout the 4-week experiment. After an additional 4 weeks of acclimation, FGF21 abundance in liver, UCP1 abundance in BAT, cecal microbiota, and microbial metabolites (specifically BAs) were measured.

Experiment 2

To investigate the effects of supplementing the pika diets with yak fecal bacteria on the spectrum of microbial metabolites and species of the cecal microbiota, 16 pikas were divided into 2 groups (8 pikas per group, 4 males and 4 females). One group was fed the LP diet as per the LP group in experiment 1. The other group was fed the LP diet supplemented with yak fecal bacteria (i.e., LPY) in plateau pika. Preparation of yak fecal bacteria: fresh yak fecal samples were collected from areas with high pika activity and transported to the laboratory on dry ice. Fecal samples (about 200 mg) were immediately diluted in 2 mL of 0.9% saline and centrifuged (500 × g for 1 min), and the supernatant was used for FMT (the experiment was conducted in a laminar flow hood). Each LP pika was gavaged with 400 µL of yak fecal microbiota every morning. After an additional 4 weeks of acclimation, many factors were measured, including body mass, food consumption, FGF21 abundance in liver, UCP1 abundance in BAT, cecal microbiota, and microbial metabolites such as BAs.

Experiment 3

To examine the possible influence of the gut microbiota on regulation of body thermogenesis, we transplanted the gut microbiota from donor pikas belonging to the HP, MP, LP, and LPY groups into separately housed recipient pikas. Thirty adult pikas were randomly assigned to five groups (six pikas per group, three males and three females). The fresh cecal content (200 mg) from the donors (HP, MP, LP and LPY pikas) was suspended in sterile 0.9% saline (2 mL) and centrifuged (500 × g for 1 min) and the supernatant was used for FMT (The experiment was conducted in a laminar flow hood). All pikas were initially given intragastric administration of 400 μL of an antibiotic mixture (50 μg/mL streptomycin, 100 μg/mL neomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin; Sigma, Germany) daily for 5 days. Pikas in each of the five groups were then transplanted with one of the following daily for 2 weeks: 400-μL sterile saline (Abx), 400-μL MP microbiota (FMT-MP), 400-μL LP microbiota (FMT-LP), or 400-μL LPY microbiota (FMT-LPY). One animal in the Abx group developed health issues unrelated to the experimental treatment during the FMT experimental period. Therefore, its data were excluded from all analyses prior to any outcome assessment for this specific experiment. Therefore, the final valid sample size for the Abx group in the FMT experiment is n = 5. All pikas were housed under conditions described in the subsection “Animals”. Experimental procedures and data collection methods were identical to those used in Experiment 1, including measuring body mass, food consumption, and effects of treatments on tissues and blood.

Measurement of body mass and dairy food and energy intake

The body mass and food intake of pikas were measured at 0800 daily using an electronic balance (Sartorius Model BL). Food and water were provided ad libitum, and the amount consumed (in grams) was determined by subtracting the weight of uneaten food from the initial weight.

Surface body temperature and BAT temperature

In the last week of acclimation, Tsurface and TBAT were measured non-invasively using a calibrated infrared camera (FLIR E60) from a distance of 40 cm. Data were analyzed using FLIR Tools software [96].

Thyroid hormone measurements

The serum total T3 and T4 levels were quantified by radioimmunoassay using commercial RIA kits with 125I-labelled tracer (the Beijing North Institute of Biological Technology, Beijing, China). The lower limit of the assay when using a 50-μL sample was 0.25 ng/mL for T3 and 3.0 ng/mL for T4, respectively.

Measurement of serum FGF21 content

An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (Shanghai Gewei Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was used to determine FGF21 levels in serum samples from plateau pikas. The detection range for the FGF21 kit was 0.125–10 ng/mL.

Western blot analysis of phospho-eIF2α, FGF21, and UCP1

Approximately, 0.1 g of liver or BAT tissue was homogenized in RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and the homogenate was then subjected to centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Equal amounts of protein in the supernatants were subjected to SDS-PAGE on a 10% gel, and the separated proteins in the gel were transferred electrophoretically to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. The membrane was incubated three times, each for 10 min, in Tris-buffered saline containing 10% bovine serum albumin and 0.05% (w/v) Tween-20 at room temperature. The membrane was then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody against one of the following proteins: FGF21 (abcam, ab171941, diluted 1:1000), eIF2α (Proteintech, 11,233–1-AP, 1:1000), phospho-eIF2α (Proteintech, 68,023–1-Ig, 1:1000), UCP1 (abcam, ab209483, 1:5000), or β-actin (Proteintech, 20,536–1-AP, 1:1000). The secondary antibody employed was peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Proteintech, SA00001-2, 1:5000). Band intensities were analyzed using Image Lab software from Bio-Rad and scaled to the intensity measured for β-actin and expressed as relative units.

Quantification of hepatic FXR protein

Total protein concentration was measured using the Pierce® BCA Protein Assay Kit (G2026, Servicebio; Wuhan). Hepatic FXR protein was determined using Mouse NR1H4 (bile acid receptor) ELISA kit (EM2178, FineTest, Wuhan, China). FXR protein was normalized against total nuclear protein content.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from frozen tissues (liver, BAT, and WAT) using TRIzol reagent and the standard phenol–chloroform extraction method. Total RNA yield was evaluated by performing 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the concentration of RNA in solution after extraction of bands was measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US). Total RNA (200 ng) was reverse transcribed to yield cDNA (in 20 μL) using the PrimeScript RT kit (G3337-100, Servicebio, China). Using the cDNA sequence of the plateau pika genome available in GenBank (genome number: GCF_017591425.1), customized primers were designed using Primer 5.0 based on conserved sequences in various genes (see Table S2). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using a LightCycler® 96 System (Roche Applied Science) with SYBR®Green real-time PCR Master Mix (RK21203, AB clonal, American). The amplification efficiency of each target gene and the internal reference gene (Gapdh) ranged from 0.97 to 1.07. Relative gene expression was estimated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Measurement of cecal BSH activity

The BSH activity in cecal contents was determined using the microplate assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the assay kit (Grace Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). Briefly, BSH catalyzes the hydrolysis of bile acids, releasing free amino groups. TNBS (2,4,6-trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid) reacts with these free amino groups to form a yellow complex, thus enabling the quantification of BSH activity by measuring absorbance at 420 nm.

Hematoxylin–eosin staining of iWAT

WAT samples were fixed with 4% buffered paraformaldehyde. Samples were then embedded in paraffin and stabilized using hematoxylin–eosin staining. The initial procedures involved cutting the tissue samples into sections measuring 4 μm and then transferring them to a solution of 70% ethanol. Next, the sections were treated with xylene to remove the wax and then dehydrated using a series of ethanol solutions of varying concentration (100%, 95%, 80%, 75%). Finally, the sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Ultimately, the tissue underwent dehydration using ethanol, clarification using xylene, and subsequent sealing. Images of the sections were quantified using ImageJ software (vrsion 1.51, National Institutes of Health, USA).

Metagenomic sequencing and preprocessing

The cecum content of each pika was obtained immediately after euthanization and then preserved in a 2-mL cryostorage tube at –80 °C. The cecum contents were subjected to bead beating and gel filtration chromatography to obtain the total genomic DNA. The QIAamp DNA Stool Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used for DNA extraction, following the methodology reported by Yu and Morrison [97]. After assessing DNA purity and concentration via 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, DNA libraries were created according to the DNBSEQ protocol [98]. Subsequently, the paired-end raw sequencing reads were filtered and utilized to construct de novo assemblies [99].

The sequence of pika genomic DNA was obtained (genome number: GCF_017591425.1) using Bowtie2 v2.5.3 [100]. MEGAHIT [101] was used to predict the contigs from each sample, and Prodigal [102] was used to predict the contigs. Subsequently, the ORFs derived from assembled contigs were maintained and clustered into a nonredundant dataset by CD-HIT. The abundance profile of genes was calculated and transformed to gene per million (GPM), with corrections for variations in gene length and mapped reads per sample.

Metagenomic binning was performed for each sample to obtain MAGs. The initial binning process involved the utilization of MetaBAT2, Concoct, and Maxbin2 modules, which were integrated into MetaWRAP. The assembled contigs were utilized to build three sets of genomes using MetaBAT2, Concoct, and Maxbin2. These sets were then concatenated and improved using the bin_refinement module of the MetaWRAP version 1.3.2 pipeline. The process of de-replication involved selecting bins and using dRep v3.0.0 [103] with a threshold of 0.95 for Genome Average Nucleotide Identity (commonly known as gANI) (-pa 0.90, -sa 0.95). The completeness of the bins had to be > 50, and the contamination had to be < 5 (-comp 50, -con 10), which was determined using CheckM (v.1.0.18) for clustering MAGs [104]. The prediction tool Prodigal (v2.6.3) was used to predict open-reading frames [102], which were annotated for function using the Genome Taxonomy Database Toolkit (GTDB-Tk v2.3.2, Database release214) [105]. The abundance of species per MAG sample was calculated by MetaWRAP and reported in terms of transcripts per kilobase million [106].

Measurement of the BA profile

A sample of cecal content (25 mg) was combined with magnetic beads and 400 µL of 50% aqueous methanol. The mixture was macerated in a grinder at a frequency of 50 Hz for 120 s and then precipitated at a temperature of −20 °C for 2 h. The precipitant was centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant (20 µL) was mixed with 180 µL of cold (4 °C) 50% aqueous methanol. The mixture was centrifuged at 500 × g for 1 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected and used for ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography.

Chromatographic conditions: The chromatographic column used was BEH C18 (2.1 mm × 5 cm, 1.7 µm, Waters, USA). The mobile phase consisted of a mixture of water containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid and 10 mM ammonium acetate (solvent A) and 70% (v/v) methanol and 30% (v/v) 10 mM ammonium acetate containing 0.1% formic acid (solvent B). The following step gradient was used for elution: [0–0.80 min, 30% solvent B; 0.80–0.90 min, 33% solvent B; 0.90–1.00 min, 40% solvent B; 1.00-3.20 min, 45% solvent B; 3.20-3.30 min, 55% solvent B; 3.30-3.40 min, 65% solvent B; 3.40-4.00 min, 100% solvent B; 4.00-4.10 min, 100% solvent B; 4.10-5.00 min, 30% solvent B. The flow rate was 0.5 mL/min, and the column temperature was 50°C.

Mass spectrometry conditions: The ion spray voltage was −4500 V (negative ionization mode), the atomizing gas and auxiliary gas were at 40 psi, the collision gas was at 6 psi, and the curtain gas was at 30 psi. The ion source temperature was 50°C. Multiple reaction monitoring was used to conduct the scans.

Statistical analysis

The statistical software R version 4.3.3 was used to perform the data analysis. Prior to statistical analyses, all the data were examined for assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance by Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Levene tests, respectively. The data with abnormal distribution were transformed by natural logarithms to normalize them [107]. Body mass and food intake during the 3-week acclimation period for pikas were examined using the repeated measures of ANOVA. Tsurface, TBAT, serum hormone levels, BA concentrations, and gene and protein expression measurements were all compared using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test. Results are presented as the mean ± SEM, and p < 0.05 was used as the threshold for assessing the statistical significance of differences between values.

All microbial diversity (Chao1 and Shannon index) analyses were conducted in R using the “vegan” packages. The Kruskal–Wallis test was employed for three separate comparisons: among the HP, MP, and LP groups; among the FMT-HP, FMT-MP, FMT-LP, and ABX groups; and among the FMT-LP, FMT-LPY, and ABX groups. If the overall test was significant, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was utilized to compare alpha diversity between the LP and LPY groups. We used Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix and Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) to assess compositional differences among (HP, MP and LP groups) or between (LP and LPY groups) microbial communities. Differences among (HP, MP and LP groups) or between (LP and LPY groups) treatments and microbiota variation were permutated with 9999 permutations and false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Standard R commands were performed to generate variations in the relative abundance of MAGs in different treatments, and the Kruskal–Wallis test (HP, MP and LP groups; FMT-HP, FMT-MP, FMT-LP and ABX; FMT-LP, FMT- LPY and ABX) or the Wilcoxon test (LP and LPY) was used to measure significance in non-parametric relative abundance profiles. The p-values acquired for MAGs were subsequently utilized to adjust the FDR using the Benjamini–Hochberg algorithm in R, employing the"p.adjust"parameter. Correlation analysis among serum hormone levels, BA concentrations, and gene and protein expression were performed using Spearman’s rank correlation, with |R| (Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient) > 0.6 and FDR value < 0.05 considered significant. The correlation network was visualized by Cytoscape (Version 3.10.3, http://www.cytoscape.org).
