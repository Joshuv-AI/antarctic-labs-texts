Bidirectional crosstalk between cancer-associated fibroblasts and tumor immunity: shaping the microenvironment and response to immune checkpoint therapy
Authors: Shiqing Wang, Di Wang, Yipeng Zhou, Wen Li, Guofu Sun, Lijuan Cao, Chao Feng, Yongjing Chen, Changshun Shao, Qi Gui, Peishan Li, Yufang Shi, Jiankai Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immune checkpoint therapy (ICT) has revolutionized cancer treatment by restoring cytotoxic T-cell activity, yet primary and acquired resistance remain pervasive. Increasing evidence reveals that such resistance is not dictated solely by tumor-intrinsic factors but arises from the dynamic interplay between malignant and stromal compartments within the tumor microenvironment. Among these, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs)—the dominant stromal population—have emerged as key orchestrators of immune accessibility and therapeutic outcome. Beyond forming structural barriers through extracellular matrix remodeling, CAFs actively shape immune cell recruitment, polarization, and effector functions via diverse cytokines, chemokines, and metabolic pathways. In turn, immune-derived mediators reprogram CAF states, establishing bidirectional feedback circuits that continuously recalibrate stromal and immune homeostasis. These reciprocal networks transform the immune microenvironment into a self-adaptive ecosystem that governs tumor progression and responsiveness to ICT. This review synthesizes recent advances in understanding CAF–immune crosstalk, emphasizing how such interactions drive both primary and acquired resistance to checkpoint blockade. We further highlight emerging therapeutic strategies aimed at reprogramming the CAF–immune axis—rather than eradicating it—to restore durable and systemic antitumor immunity.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint therapy (ICT) has revolutionized cancer treatment by unleashing durable antitumor immunity across a broad spectrum of malignancies [1, 2]. Landmark trials of CTLA-4 and PD-1/PD-L1 blockade have established immune checkpoint inhibition as a standard of care in multiple cancer types, producing long-term survival in subsets of patients [3–9]. However, therapeutic responses remain highly variable, and durable benefit is achieved only in a minority. Many patients exhibit primary resistance, whereas others develop secondary resistance during treatment, underscoring the urgent need to elucidate mechanisms that limit ICT efficacy [10, 11].

The heterogeneous nature of the tumor immune microenvironment is a key determinant of resistance to ICT [12–16]. Building on conceptual frameworks such as Paget’s “seed-and-soil” hypothesis, the tumor microenvironment is now recognized as a dynamic ecosystem in which malignant, immune, and stromal components co-evolve [17–19]. Within this complex network, the tumor stroma—particularly fibroblastic and mesenchymal compartments—functions as a critical regulatory interface that integrates mechanical, metabolic, and immunologic cues to support malignant progression. Importantly, emerging evidence indicates that stromal reprogramming is not confined to primary tumors but can be systemically induced prior to metastatic dissemination [20]. For example, tumor-derived inflammatory signals such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) can metabolically rewire lung-resident mesenchymal cells at the pre-metastatic stage by inducing hypoxia-inducible lipid droplet-associated (HILPDA)-dependent lipid droplet accumulation through repression of ATGL activity, generating lipid-laden stromal cells that promote metastatic outgrowth [21]. These metabolically reprogrammed stromal cells can further transfer lipids to tumor cells and natural killer (NK) cells via extracellular vesicle–like structures, simultaneously enhancing tumor cell fitness while impairing NK cell cytotoxic function. In parallel, distinct subsets of lung fibroblasts characterized by cyclooxygenase-2 expression have been shown to orchestrate an immunosuppressive pre-metastatic niche by reprogramming infiltrating myeloid cells toward dysfunctional or suppressive states, and targeting such stromal programs can reduce metastatic burden and enhance the efficacy of immunotherapeutic interventions [22].

Echoing Dvorak’s concept of cancer as “a wound that does not heal,” tumors exhibit chronic, non-resolving inflammation that continuously activates stromal and immune elements [23, 24]. This parallel has led to the reasonable assumption that fibroblasts, which are central orchestrators of tissue repair and extracellular matrix remodeling during wound healing, must also play indispensable roles in tumor biology. Advances in single-cell and spatial profiling have further revealed that cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) actively remodel the tumor immune microenvironment and influence tumor behavior, underscoring their emerging importance in shaping disease progression and therapeutic responses [25, 26]. CAFs are activated fibroblasts within the tumor microenvironment that exhibit remarkable functional diversity, constituting the predominant stromal population in most solid tumors and executing critical regulatory roles in tumor progression and immune modulation. They originate not only from resident fibroblasts but also from various non-fibroblast precursors—including mesenchymal stem cells, pericytes, and epithelial cells undergoing epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition [27–31]. The activation of CAFs is driven by key signaling cues—including transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), platelet-derived growth factor, and inflammatory cytokines—that induce a myofibroblastic and immunomodulatory phenotype within the tumor immune microenvironment [32–35]. Once activated, CAFs profoundly shape the tumor immune microenvironment through multiple mechanisms. The majority of current research emphasized their structural roles, showing that CAFs remodel the extracellular matrix through collagen and fibronectin deposition and crosslinking, thereby increasing tissue stiffness and creating physical barriers that hinder immune cell infiltration into the tumor core [36]. However, beyond these architectural functions, CAFs are now recognized to possess potent immunomodulatory capacities—actively influencing immune cell recruitment, activation, and differentiation within the tumor immune microenvironment [37, 38].

In early immune activation, fibroblasts act as sentinel and effector cells, responding to IL-1β and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) by releasing mediators such as IL-6, CXC-chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12), and CC-chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) to recruit and sustain leukocytes at inflamed sites [39]. As inflammation resolves, fibroblasts transition toward a suppressive phenotype characterized by prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), and TGF-β production, which restrains effector T-cell responses and promotes regulatory T-cell differentiation [40]. This capacity to dynamically oscillate between proinflammatory and immunoregulatory states establishes fibroblasts as key arbiters of immune balance and tissue homeostasis. In cancer, this equilibrium is pathologically disrupted. Persistent cytokine signaling and immune infiltration maintain fibroblasts in a chronically activated, immunomodulatory state [41, 42]. CAFs integrate inflammatory and immune-derived cues to maintain tumor-promoting inflammation while regulating leukocyte recruitment, polarization, and effector functions. Through extensive bidirectional crosstalk—mediated by cytokines, chemokines, and direct contact—CAFs and immune cells establish dynamic feedback circuits that remodel both stromal and immune architecture. These reciprocal networks allow the tumor microenvironment to adapt to immune pressure, reinforcing immune suppression or, under certain conditions, sustaining immune activation [43, 44]. Building upon this dynamic framework, ICT introduces a distinct perturbation by reinvigorating T cell–derived inflammatory signals, which serve as a secondary wave of stimulation for CAFs. Rather than functioning as static suppressors, CAFs undergo adaptive reprogramming and operate as context-dependent regulators embedded within evolving CAF–immune feedback networks [45, 46]. Such stromal plasticity contributes directly to immune heterogeneity by reinforcing spatially and temporally distinct immune niches, thereby enabling tumors to adapt to immune pressure and promoting resistance to checkpoint blockade. This framework highlights CAF–immune feedback circuits as key determinants of heterogeneous immunotherapy responses and as rational targets for therapeutic intervention.

This review synthesizes recent advances in understanding the bidirectional signaling between CAFs and immune cells within the tumor microenvironment, emphasizing its impact on responses to ICT. We first outline the heterogeneity and immunoregulatory functions of distinct CAF subsets, then explore how immune-derived cues dynamically reprogram CAF states. Particular attention is given to the feedback circuits formed between CAFs and immune populations that sustain immune suppression and drive therapeutic resistance. Finally, we discuss emerging translational strategies that target CAFs in concert with checkpoint blockade, offering a conceptual framework to inform future mechanistic studies and the development of next-generation immunotherapies.

CAF subtype diversity and immune landscape remodeling

Fibroblasts exhibit remarkable phenotypic and functional diversity, which has been increasingly recognized across both physiological and pathological contexts [33]. This diversity reflects differences in developmental origin—such as neural crest– versus mesoderm-derived lineages—and tissue specialization, exemplified by dermal fibroblasts supporting hair follicle morphogenesis or synovial fibroblasts maintaining joint homeostasis. Under pathological stress, fibroblasts within the same tissue display pronounced phenotypic plasticity that is governed by spatially patterned, cell-extrinsic cues. Multi-omic and spatial analyses in inflamed synovium indicate that differential exposure to myeloid- and T cell–derived cytokines (e.g., TNF, IL-1β, and IFN-γ, or their absence) is sufficient to drive discrete fibroblast states within the same anatomical site [47, 48]. In tumors, fibroblasts become chronically activated and dominate the stromal compartment as CAFs. High-resolution omics technologies, including single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics, have revealed that CAFs are far from uniform; instead, they comprise diverse subtypes distinguished by lineage origin, spatial localization, activation states, and immunoregulatory functions [49–53]. This heterogeneity underlies their dual capacities to either restrain or support antitumor immunity, thereby influencing the immune landscape and determining the efficacy of ICT. Understanding how distinct CAF subsets interact with immune cells to remodel the tumor immune microenvironment will be crucial for explaining variable therapeutic responses and for designing stroma-targeted immunomodulatory strategies.

The phenotypic and functional diversity of CAFs forms the basis of their multifaceted roles in orchestrating tumor immunity and microenvironmental remodeling. During tumor progression, stromal activation recapitulates key features of wound healing—such as inflammation, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix remodeling—but these reparative programs become chronically sustained, driving persistent immune activation and structural dysregulation (Fig. 1). Within this pathological continuum, CAFs differentiate into distinct subtypes that collectively shape the tumor immune landscape [54, 55]. Inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs) are characterized by elevated expression of pro-inflammatory mediators—such as IL-6, IL-1, and various chemokines—and by activation of JAK/STAT and NF-κB signaling pathways. Through these signals, iCAFs orchestrate the recruitment, polarization, and effector functions of immune cells within the tumor immune microenvironment [36, 56, 57]. Antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs) are distinguished by their expression of major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) molecules and associated antigen processing machinery. By presenting antigens and engaging CD4⁺ T cells, apCAFs actively remodel the immune landscape, yet their impact can be dualistic—promoting antitumor immunity under certain cytokine milieus while fostering immunosuppression and tumor growth in others, depending on local cell–cell interactions and co-stimulatory versus co-inhibitory signal balances [45]. Vascular CAFs (vCAFs), defined by elevated expression of angiogenesis-associated genes, influence tumor neovascularization; however, their exact mechanistic roles in vessel formation and function are still being elucidated [58, 59]. Myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs) represent the most extensively characterized subset; through actomyosin-mediated contractility and secretion of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), collagen isoforms, and crosslinking enzymes such as lysyl oxidases, they orchestrate ECM remodeling and stromal stiffening, critically modulating tumor biomechanics and progression (Table 1) [25, 60–66].

Representative markers of major CAF states across cancer types

These major CAF subsets do not exist in isolation but rather display extensive spatial and temporal overlap, enabled by a dynamic “signal network–cell state” coupling. Within the tumor microenvironment, CAFs acquire plastic phenotypic features in response to the dominant cytokine milieu. For example, microenvironmental cues such as TGF-β and IL-1 can reversibly drive transitions between iCAF and myCAF states. IL-1 signaling induces leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) expression and downstream JAK/STAT activation to promote an inflammatory CAF program, whereas TGF-β antagonizes this process by suppressing IL-1 receptor expression and reinforcing myofibroblastic differentiation. This reciprocal regulation establishes distinct but interconvertible fibroblast niches within tumors, illustrating how local cytokine networks dynamically couple extracellular signals to CAF cell states and generate spatially heterogeneous stromal landscapes [67]. This plasticity implies that effective stromal intervention will require simultaneous modulation of multiple signaling axes rather than single‑target approaches.

Despite extensive efforts to characterize CAF subtypes across tumor types, inter-cohort variability, methodological discrepancies, and analytical heterogeneity have hindered the establishment of a unified CAF taxonomy. To address these challenges, recent pan-cancer analyses have systematically integrated large-scale single-cell and spatial transcriptomic datasets, revealing conserved stromal programs that transcend tissue boundaries [68]. For instance, a comprehensive single-cell atlas of 517 human samples spanning 11 tissues and diverse pathological states delineated both pan-cancer and tissue-specific fibroblast subpopulations. This study revealed the dynamic evolution of immune regulatory and myCAF phenotypes across inflammatory and tumor niches, demonstrating their independent prognostic significance [51]. Another seminal investigation profiled over 16,000 stromal cells from 14 breast cancer patients and identified nine distinct CAF phenotypes alongside pericytes, validating these subtypes in four additional tumor types via high-plex imaging mass cytometry. By integrating spatial omics datasets from ten cancer types across seven technological platforms—encompassing approximately 14 million cells—the study uncovered four conserved spatial CAF niches, mapped their ligand-receptor interaction networks and transcriptional programs, and demonstrated how each niche influences immune cell infiltration patterns and correlates with patient survival outcomes [69]. Collectively, these efforts converge on a unifying principle that CAF heterogeneity is organized by conserved stromal–immune circuits extending beyond tissue boundaries. Integrating these insights into molecular and spatial taxonomies bridges CAF biology with immune ecosystem modeling and establishes a conceptual and computational foundation for precision strategies designed to reprogram stromal–immune dynamics across diverse malignancies.

Immune-to-CAF

Chronic inflammation sustains a tumor milieu in which immune and stromal compartments are persistently intertwined through reciprocal activation. Diverse immune cell populations within the tumor immune microenvironment continuously release cytokines, chemokines, and cytotoxic mediators that not only orchestrate antitumor responses but also shape stromal behaviors. During ICT, the reinvigoration of T-cell activity amplifies immune-derived inflammatory signals within the tumor stroma, providing a renewed wave of cytokine stimulation [70]. In response to persistent exposure to interferons, TNF, IL-1, and related mediators, CAFs undergo adaptive activation and transcriptional reprogramming, driven by key signaling pathways such as STAT1/IRF1, NF-κB, and AP-1 [71]. This immune-driven plasticity endows CAFs with context-dependent phenotypes that modulate leukocyte recruitment, cytokine gradients, and antigen-presenting circuits. Depending on the prevailing cytokine milieu—dominated by either IFN-driven inflammatory cues or TNF/IL-1–associated wound-healing signals—CAFs may adopt either immunostimulatory or immunosuppressive states. Understanding these immune-to-CAF transitions is critical for unraveling how inflammation and therapy-induced immune activation reshape stromal architecture and drive resistance to immunotherapy. In the following sections, we summarize the molecular pathways and immune cell–derived cues that trigger CAF activation and reprogramming within the tumor immune microenvironment.

Tumor-associated neutrophil-derived signals

Neutrophils are among the earliest immune responders recruited to sites of tumor initiation, where their transient role in acute inflammation evolves into sustained regulatory functions that define the tumor-associated neutrophil (TAN) phenotype [72]. Under persistent exposure to tumor-derived and inflammatory cytokines, neutrophils undergo extensive transcriptional and metabolic reprogramming, extending their lifespan and enabling continuous interaction with the stromal compartment [73–75]. While most studies on TANs have centered on their roles in angiogenesis, metastasis, and immune regulation, emerging evidence now highlights their ability to directly modulate stromal dynamics. Through the secretion of cytokines, chemokines, and proteolytic enzymes, TANs remodel the extracellular matrix, modulate fibroblast activation, and influence the recruitment and polarization of immune populations, thereby linking innate immune activity to stromal evolution within the tumor microenvironment [76].

Mechanistically, TAN-driven stromal remodeling can occur via at least two complementary routes (Fig. 2). First, TAN-derived cytokines can transcriptionally reprogram fibroblast identities and functions. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, neutrophil-derived IL-1β promotes inflammatory CAF polarization and sustains an IL-6/STAT3 feedback loop, thereby coupling myeloid inflammation to stromal remodeling and chemoresistance [77]. Similarly, in gastric cancer, tumor-educated neutrophils secrete IL-17, IL-23, and TNF-α, activating AKT and p38 signaling in mesenchymal precursors to facilitate their conversion into CAFs [78]. Second, beyond soluble signaling, neutrophils can reshape stromal architecture through the release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)—web-like chromatin scaffolds decorated with elastase and proteases. NET-associated DNA and enzymes engage RAGE-dependent signaling in fibroblasts and stellate precursors, enhancing their activation and migratory capacity [79]. Reciprocally, CAF-derived amyloid-β can further potentiate NET formation, establishing a self-reinforcing inflammatory circuit that stabilizes neutrophil–stromal interactions within tumors [80]. Together, these dual mechanisms—cytokine-driven transcriptional reprogramming and NET-mediated structural activation—illustrate how neutrophils integrate innate immune inflammation with stromal remodeling.

Immune checkpoint blockade introduces an altered inflammatory context that can further remodel neutrophil states, including IFN- and innate-sensing–associated programs. Direct evidence that “ICT-induced” TAN signals causally reprogram CAFs remains limited; nevertheless, therapy-shaped TAN states are well positioned to deliver a secondary wave of inflammatory and ECM-remodeling cues that may reinforce CAF plasticity and contribute to adaptive stromal remodeling during acquired resistance. In this setting, TANs are frequently implicated in resistance to checkpoint blockade through the release of immunoregulatory mediators such as arginase 1 (ARG1), lipid mediators including PGE2, and ECM-associated factors such as thrombospondin 1, which collectively foster an immunosuppressive milieu and constrain effective T cell–mediated antitumor immunity [81–83]. Importantly, TAN-derived signals are not uniformly suppressive. In murine lung adenocarcinoma, successful immunotherapy is associated with the emergence of treatment-induced neutrophils enriched for IFN-responsive gene signatures [83]. Consistently, an IFN-licensed Ly6Ehigh neutrophil subset engaging STING signaling has been shown to promote antitumor immunity via neutrophil-derived IL-12–dependent activation of T cell cytotoxicity and to associate with favorable responses to anti–PD-1 therapy across tumor models [84].

Tumor-associated macrophage-derived signals

Among the diverse immune components within the tumor microenvironment, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) have emerged as pivotal orchestrators that bridge inflammation, tissue remodeling, and immune regulation [85]. Moving beyond the simplified M1/M2 paradigm, TAMs exist along a continuous spectrum of functional states within the tumor microenvironment, dynamically shaped by cytokine networks, metabolic conditions, and stromal-derived cues. As the second wave of immune cells infiltrating inflamed or neoplastic tissues, macrophages coordinate complex regulatory programs that modulate both the intensity and resolution of immune responses. By coupling immune-derived cues with tissue remodeling programs, TAMs act as central orchestrators of fibroblast activation and phenotypic programming within tumors. Through this immune–stromal interface, macrophages convert inflammatory signals into fibroblast reprogramming events that sustain fibrosis, reshape immune cell accessibility, and drive the progression of tumor-associated pathology [86–88].

A representative example can be found in diffuse-type gastric cancer, where macrophages adopt a mixed M1/M2 polarization state upon interacting with tumor and stromal cells. These hybrid macrophages secrete IL-1β, which converts normal fibroblasts into CAF-like cells characterized by elevated IL-6, IL-24, and LIF expression. In turn, IL-6 and GM-CSF reinforce macrophage plasticity, forming a self-sustaining inflammatory–fibrotic circuit that accelerates tumor invasion [89]. In metastatic contexts, macrophage-derived signals also construct fibrotic niches that facilitate colonization and therapy resistance. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, metastasis-associated macrophages secrete granulin to activate hepatic stellate cells into periostin-producing myofibroblasts. This process generates a rigid, metastasis-permissive microenvironment that supports tumor cell survival, while blockade of this macrophage subset recruitment or granulin signaling suppresses fibroblast activation and metastatic outgrowth [90]. A parallel paradigm occurs in breast cancer, where TAM-secreted CCL18 activates the PITPNM3–NF-κB axis in fibroblasts, inducing a CD10⁺GPR77⁺ CAF subset that promotes cancer stemness and chemoresistance through IL-6 and IL-8 signaling. Interfering with this axis restores chemosensitivity, emphasizing the translational relevance of targeting TAM–CAF communication [91].

Beyond cytokine-mediated activation, macrophages can establish spatially organized stromal programs through secreted matrix regulators. Recent single-cell and spatial transcriptomic studies across multiple cancer types have consistently delineated a subset of tumor-associated macrophages characterized by high expression of SPP1 (osteopontin), often accompanied by activation of APOE, metabolic, and extracellular matrix–related pathways [92, 93]. These SPP1⁺ macrophages spatially co-localize with specific CAF subpopulations and are closely associated with fibrotic and immune-excluded tumor states. In colorectal cancer, SPP1⁺ TAMs establish spatially coupled niches with FAP⁺ CAFs, sustaining desmoplastic and immune-excluded architectures through paracrine signaling mediated by chemerin, TGF-β, and IL-1. Elevated SPP1 or FAP expression levels are frequently observed in patients who respond poorly to PD-L1 blockade, indicating a close association between this macrophage–fibroblast axis and the immunosuppressive tumor milieu [93]. To further elucidate the potential mechanisms by which macrophage-derived SPP1 regulates fibroblast function, several studies may offer valuable insights, although these investigations did not explicitly identify the cellular source of SPP1. SPP1-mediated activation of CAFs also contributes to the progression of colorectal liver metastases, where enhanced SPP1 secretion induces CXCL12 production in CAFs through β-catenin/HIF-1α–dependent transcription, promoting epithelial–mesenchymal transition and restricting CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration. Blockade of SPP1 or CXCL12 signaling restores intratumoral T-cell entry and enhances responsiveness to PD-1 blockade [94]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, SPP1 has been shown to engage CD44 and integrin receptors on fibroblasts, activating the PI3K–AKT pathway to drive fibroblast differentiation and extracellular matrix deposition, thereby reinforcing the fibrotic tumor stroma [95]. Collectively, these findings define SPP1⁺ TAMs as stromal coordinators that link immune signaling with structural remodeling, establishing biochemical barriers that blunt antitumor immunity and immune checkpoint therapy efficacy.

In addition to cytokines and matrix-associated cues, metabolic signaling has recently emerged as an additional layer of macrophage–CAF communication. In gastric cancer, a metabolic crosstalk mediated by nicotinamide metabolism has been described between macrophages and CAFs. TAMs secrete extracellular vesicles carrying nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase, which interfere with nicotinamide metabolism in CAFs by suppressing nicotinamide N-methyltransferase expression, thereby reshaping an immunostimulatory tumor microenvironment. This intercellular metabolic interplay contributes to enhanced CD8⁺ T cell–mediated antitumor immunity and correlates with responses to immune checkpoint blockade [96].

Finally, another intriguing finding reveals that TAMs themselves can serve as a cellular source of CAFs through a process termed macrophage–myofibroblast transition [97]. Single-cell analyses in non–small cell lung carcinoma and Lewis lung carcinoma models identified macrophage-lineage cells that acquire fibroblastic signatures, confirming a direct TAM–to-CAF transition both in vivo and in vitro. This process is governed by a Smad3-centered transcriptional program, as macrophage-specific deletion or pharmacologic inhibition of Smad3 effectively blocks macrophage–myofibroblast transition, reducing CAF accumulation and tumor growth. These findings highlight macrophage–myofibroblast transition as a novel mechanism of stromal expansion and a potential target for disrupting macrophage-driven fibroblast genesis in cancer.

Together, these insights reveal that TAMs are not merely modulators of stromal activity but active architects of the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 2). By integrating inflammatory, fibrogenic, and lineage-reprogramming cues, TAMs orchestrate the formation of a fibrotic and immunosuppressive stroma that dictates tumor evolution and therapeutic resistance. This conceptual shift positions the macrophage–fibroblast axis as a central organizer of tumor ecology and a strategic target for reprogramming the microenvironment toward immune responsiveness.

T cell-derived signals

T cells represent a central adaptive immune component of the tumor microenvironment, capable of both executing cytotoxic responses and shaping the behavior of surrounding stromal elements. Traditionally regarded as the primary executors of antitumor immunity, T cells also act as potent sources of cytokine-mediated signals that extend beyond immune regulation to influence the transcriptional and functional states of CAFs, thereby linking adaptive immunity with stromal remodeling. ICT restores T-cell cytotoxicity and amplifies type I inflammatory signaling within tumors, unleashing IFN-γ, TNF, and other effector mediators that act beyond immune targets to reshape the stromal compartment. CAFs endowed with high transcriptional plasticity, respond to these immune cues through adaptive reprogramming that alters their secretory profile, matrix remodeling, and spatial organization—establishing feedback interactions that modulate the immune landscape and potentially limit therapeutic efficacy [98, 99].

In immune-inflamed tumors, cytokines derived from activated T cells dynamically remodel the transcriptional and functional states of CAFs, endowing them with context-dependent immunoregulatory properties. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, single-cell transcriptomic profiling has revealed a subset of CAFs with reduced CXCL12 and elevated CXCL9 expression that correlates with increased T-cell infiltration. Mechanistically, cytokines released by activated CD8⁺ T cells—most prominently TNF-α and IFN-γ—act in concert to induce this chemokine switch, converting CXCL12⁺/CXCL9⁻ immune-suppressive fibroblasts into CXCL12⁻/CXCL9⁺ immune-activating ones. TNF-α selectively represses CXCL12, while its synergy with IFN-γ amplifies CXCL9 induction, promoting T-cell chemotaxis and immune accessibility [100]. Similar cytokine-driven remodeling has been observed in non-small cell lung cancer, where IFN-γ and TNF-α signaling in CAFs triggers a broad inflammatory secretome—including IL-6, LIF, and CXCL9-11—while suppressing CXCL12, highlighting the plasticity of CAFs in adapting to immune cues [101].

Beyond modulating chemokine profiles, persistent IFN-γ signaling can reprogram CAFs toward more specialized immunomodulatory phenotypes. Upon binding to its receptors on fibroblasts, IFN-γ activates the JAK1/2–STAT1 signaling axis, inducing the expression of immune-response genes and components of the MHC-II pathway [102]. Recent studies have demonstrated that IFN-γ exposure drives fibroblasts toward an apCAF phenotype, characterized by elevated MHC-II and PD-L2 expression [46]. Functionally, apCAFs engage CD4⁺ T cells as “non-canonical” antigen-presenting cells; however, lacking classical co-stimulatory molecules, they often promote T cell dysfunction or Treg expansion, thereby reinforcing immune suppression [46, 103]. Thus, IFN-γ exemplifies the duality of T cell–derived cytokine signaling: transient exposure promotes immune infiltration and activation, whereas chronic signaling reinforces immune regulation and tolerance. This context-dependent reprogramming underscores how adaptive immune activity is tightly coupled to stromal evolution within the tumor microenvironment. Th17- and Th2-associated cytokines further diversify CAF phenotypes across distinct immune milieus. IL-17, secreted by Th17, Tc17, and γδ T cells, activates fibroblast IL-17R–HIF-1α signaling, stimulating type I collagen synthesis and matrix molecule production, which collectively form dense stromal barriers that restrict T-cell infiltration. Genetic deletion of IL-17 receptors in CAFs markedly reduces collagen deposition, facilitates effector T-cell entry, and improves anti-PD-L1 therapy response [104, 105]. Moreover, IL-17 often synergizes with TNF-α, a cytokine co-released by Th17 and Tc17 subsets, to induce inflammatory gene and chemokine expression in CAFs, thereby promoting iCAF polarization and tumor progression [106]. Conversely, Th2 cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-13 drive fibroblast activation along a fibrotic axis. While direct tumor-based evidence remains limited, findings from fibrotic disease models reveal that IL-4/IL-13 activate the STAT6 pathway in fibroblasts, upregulating α-SMA and collagen production to establish myCAF phenotypes [107]. In tumors exhibiting Th2-dominant immune polarization—such as subsets of colorectal and pancreatic cancers—these signals likely sustain ECM-rich, immunosuppressive stromal niches reminiscent of myCAF-driven desmoplasia.

Collectively, these studies delineate a cytokine hierarchy through which distinct T-cell lineages—Th1, Th17, and Th2—impart differential cues that reprogram CAFs toward immunoactive, inflammatory, or fibrotic states, thereby coupling adaptive immune polarization to the structural and functional evolution of the tumor stroma (Fig. 2).

B cell-derived signals

In recent years, the concept of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes has expanded beyond a T cell–centric framework to encompass the diverse and context-dependent roles of B cells and plasma cells within the tumor microenvironment. In many malignancies, tumor-infiltrating B cells hold strong prognostic and predictive value, correlating with enhanced responses to immune checkpoint blockade [108]. Functionally, tumor-infiltrating B cells act as both antigen-presenting and antibody-producing cells, shaping adaptive immunity by amplifying T cell activation and coordinating innate effector mechanisms involving complement, macrophages, and natural killer cells [109].

Within the stromal niches of immunologically “hot” tumors, tumor-infiltrating B cells often aggregate with T cells and dendritic cells (DCs) to form tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS)—ectopic immune hubs that support antigen-specific immune activation and predict favorable outcomes to immune checkpoint therapy. The presence of TLS is closely linked to increased lymphocyte infiltration and improved survival across multiple cancer types, including melanoma, lung cancer, and renal carcinoma [110, 111]. Emerging evidence indicates that stromal–B cell crosstalk is a key determinant of TLS neogenesis and organization. Notably, a distinct fibroblast lineage secreting CCL19 has been shown to facilitate B cell recruitment and promote TLS formation; exogenous CCL19 can further drive TLS neogenesis and restrain tumor growth in vivo, consistent with a causal role of CCL19⁺ fibroblasts in TLS establishment and humoral antitumor immunity [112]. In addition, CAFs can shape TLS development indirectly by modulating CXCL13 production, thereby influencing both T-cell effector function and the capacity of the immune milieu to recruit B cells and support TLS maturation [113]. Mechanistically, studies in murine melanoma further demonstrate that tumor-associated TLS development is orchestrated by CAFs with lymphoid tissue organizer–like properties induced by TNFR signaling; CAF organization into reticular networks involves CD8⁺ T cells, whereas TLS expansion depends on CXCL13-mediated recruitment of LTα1β2-expressing B cells [114].

Despite these emerging insights, the broader mechanisms by which B cells regulate CAF behavior remain largely undefined. Nevertheless, given the widespread presence of B cells within the tumor immune microenvironment and their capacity to produce a diverse repertoire of cytokines, growth factors, and immunoglobulins, it is plausible that B cells engage in previously underappreciated modes of communication with stromal cells [109]. Elucidating how B cells influence CAF differentiation, matrix remodeling, and immune modulation represents a promising direction for future research—one that could uncover new stromal–immune checkpoints amenable to therapeutic targeting.

Evidence from diverse tumor models reveals that immune-derived signals are central architects of fibroblast fate and function within the tumor microenvironment. Rather than being passive responders to tissue stress, CAFs emerge as dynamic interpreters of immune cues—translating cytokines, costimulatory ligands, and metabolic mediators from neutrophils, macrophages, T cells, and B cells into persistent stromal remodeling programs. Through pathways such as TGF-β/SMAD, IL-1/NF-κB, OSM/STAT3, and LTβR/NF-κB, immune cells instruct fibroblasts to adopt context-dependent states ranging from contractile myofibroblasts to inflammatory or lymphoid-like CAFs. This immune-to-stroma communication shapes the mechanical, metabolic, and immunologic architecture of tumors—dictating whether the stroma acts as a barrier to, or a scaffold for, effective antitumor immunity (Fig. 2). From a translational perspective, these insights redefine CAFs as immunologically imprinted stromal populations whose behavior reflects the dominant immune milieu. Immunotherapies that modulate the immune cytokine landscape—such as checkpoint blockade, CD40 agonists, or STING activation—inherently reshape CAF states, either dismantling fibrotic barriers or fostering immunoreactive niches that favor T-cell infiltration. Understanding and therapeutically leveraging this bidirectional immune–stromal circuitry offers a new avenue to overcome resistance to ICT and to engineer a tumor microenvironment conducive to durable immune control.

Signaling hubs in immune–CAF reprogramming

Immune cell–derived cues that drive CAF activation and reprogramming in the tumor microenvironment converge on a limited number of recurrent intracellular signaling modules. Rather than acting as isolated cytokine effects, these pathways function as integrative response hubs that translate immune inflammation into durable fibroblast state changes governing inflammatory transcription, differentiation, and matrix/chemokine remodeling. Focusing on these shared signaling hubs provides a framework for understanding how immune inputs are consolidated into stable stromal programs and highlights points at which CAF plasticity may be reshaped to influence responses to immune checkpoint therapy [115, 116].

One prominent example is the IL-1–NF-κB axis, which links myeloid inflammation to inflammatory CAF programs. IL-1α/β, frequently produced by TANs and TAMs, activates IL-1R on stromal fibroblasts, triggering MyD88–interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 4 (IRAK4) signaling and downstream NF-κB activity. This cascade induces proinflammatory mediators such as IL-6, CXCL1/2, and CCL2, thereby establishing an amplification loop that couples stromal inflammation, myeloid recruitment, and immunosuppression. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, tumor- and immune-derived IL-1β engages this pathway to sustain fibroblast-driven cytokine and extracellular matrix remodeling programs that support tumor progression and therapy resistance [117]. Importantly, genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of IRAK4 disrupts this stromal inflammatory circuit and has been shown to enhance antitumor immunity and sensitize tumors to therapy, identifying IRAK4 as a tractable combinatorial target in stroma-rich malignancies. Consistent with this concept, the IRAK4 inhibitor emavusertib (CA-4948) has advanced into multiple clinical studies, underscoring the translational feasibility of targeting this axis [118, 119].

iCAF reprogramming downstream of IL-1 signaling is further reinforced by JAK/STAT activation, for example through LIF-mediated engagement of STAT3, whereas TGF-β signaling antagonizes this program by suppressing IL-1 receptor expression and promoting myofibroblastic differentiation. This reciprocal IL-1–JAK/STAT versus TGF-β signaling architecture provides a mechanistic framework for reversible CAF state transitions along the iCAF–myCAF axis and highlights multiple intervention points capable of reshaping stromal phenotypes [67]. In parallel, OSM–GP130/OSMR–JAK1/2–STAT3 signaling represents another immune-to-CAF pathway with clear druggability. In PDAC and other tumors, OSM produced by myeloid cells activates STAT3 in fibroblasts, promoting CAF activation and paracrine programs that foster tumor growth and immune suppression. Targeting this axis—through IL-6/IL-6R blockade, JAK inhibition, or STAT3-directed strategies—has been extensively explored in preclinical models and clinical studies, providing a rational basis for combination approaches with immune checkpoint inhibitors [120, 121].

Finally, TGF-β–SMAD signaling is tightly linked to myCAF activation, extracellular matrix deposition, and the establishment of immune-excluded tumor phenotypes. Across multiple tumor models, stromal TGF-β activity correlates with poor responses to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, whereas inhibition of TGF-β signaling restores T-cell infiltration and enhances immunotherapy efficacy [122]. Druggable strategies targeting this pathway include neutralizing TGF-β ligands, inhibiting TGFBR1 kinase activity, and bifunctional agents such as PD-L1–TGF-β trap molecules, which have shown superior antitumor immune activation in preclinical studies [123, 124].

Collectively, these examples illustrate that immune-to-CAF communication is not a diffuse cytokine overlay but a structured, context-dependent signaling system that funnels immune-derived inputs into a restricted set of actionable stromal hubs. Mapping immune cues onto these downstream signaling nodes provides a mechanistic rationale for rational combination therapies aimed at modulating CAF plasticity to overcome resistance and improve the durability of immunotherapy responses.

CAF-to-immune

If immune signals shape fibroblast identity, CAFs, in turn, act as powerful architects of the immune landscape. Once activated, CAFs transcend their structural role to become central regulatory nodes that modulate immune cell recruitment, activation, and effector functions. Through their ability to remodel the extracellular matrix, secrete chemokines and cytokines, and present metabolites or antigens, CAFs construct a spatial and biochemical framework that dynamically tunes the balance between immune activation and suppression. In this sense, the stroma is not a passive scaffold but an active immunological organ, capable of dictating the quality and duration of antitumor immunity [125]. The reciprocal interplay between CAFs and immune cells establishes multilayered feedback circuits that dynamically orchestrate immune activity, metabolic adaptation, and tissue remodeling, collectively determining the trajectory of tumor immunity and therapeutic response (Table 2). In the following sections, we will delineate the principal axes of CAF–immune communication and dissect the molecular mechanisms through which CAFs orchestrate and remodel antitumor immunity (Fig. 3).

(5) Functional suppression/activation: CAFs directly tune immune effector functions by impairing dendritic-cell (DC) activity (e.g., CAF-derived WNT2), reducing cytotoxic efficacy (e.g., PLA2G2A, galectin-9), dampening NK-cell cytotoxicity via tryptophan catabolism (e.g., IDO1, TDO2), and modulating T-cell responses through checkpoint/adhesion and antigen-presentation pathways (e.g., PD-L1/2, ICAM1, MHC-II), thereby influencing CD4⁺ T-cell activation and Treg recruitment/differentiation. (6) Additional mechanisms: CAF-associated factors such as SDF-1α–linked PD-L1 upregulation, amyloid-β, and ECM/enzymatic mediators (e.g., LAMA4, HPGD) are illustrated to contribute to NETosis, immune-cell aging, and CD8⁺ T-cell trafficking defects. Collectively, these CAF-driven programs integrate to sculpt immune composition, localization, and function within the tumor immune microenvironment, biasing the balance toward immune exclusion or suppression depending on context

CAF-mediated modulation of TANs

Following their activation by TANs, CAFs reciprocally modulate neutrophil behaviors through a variety of paracrine mechanisms. One key mode of regulation involves CAF-derived chemokines that orchestrate neutrophil recruitment and retention within the tumor microenvironment. In hepatocellular carcinoma, CAFs have been shown to attract neutrophils via the SDF-1α/CXCR4 signaling axis, establishing a chemotactic gradient that reinforces neutrophil infiltration and fosters a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment characterized by elevated PD-L1 expression [126]. Similarly, in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, CAFs recruit neutrophils through the IL-1α/IL1RAP pathway, which amplifies the expression of neutrophil-attracting chemokines such as CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL5, CXCL6, and CXCL8. Pharmacological blockade of IL1RAP with the antagonist nadunolimab markedly suppresses this chemokine cascade, attenuating CAF-driven neutrophil infiltration and tumor-promoting inflammation [127].

Once recruited into the tumor microenvironment, TANs are further conditioned by CAFs through multiple regulatory pathways that shape their phenotype and effector functions. Within the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, neutrophils are continuously exposed to TGF-β and IL-6 signals that drive their polarization toward an N2-like, immunosuppressive CAFs represent a major source of these cues, maintaining a TGF-β-dominated milieu through paracrine interactions with tumor cells [126]. Notably, CAF-derived cardiotrophin-like cytokine factor 1 (CLCF1) induces CXCL6 and TGF-β expression in tumor cells, which in turn recruit neutrophils and bias them toward the N2 state. These same signals reactivate ERK1/2 signaling in CAFs, enhancing CLCF1 secretion and establishing a self-reinforcing stromal–immune circuit that accelerates tumor progression [128]. Pharmacologic blockade of this pathway—such as with the oral TGF-β receptor kinase inhibitor SM16—restores N1-type TAN accumulation and potentiates antitumor immunity in murine models [129].

Beyond controlling TAN localization and polarization, CAFs also regulate neutrophil cell death programs to perpetuate malignancy. Through soluble mediators such as amyloid β, CAFs trigger reactive oxygen species-dependent NETosis, leading to the release of NETs that paradoxically reactivate CAFs and reinforce a fibrotic, tumor-supportive niche [80]. This reciprocal activation between CAFs and TANs thus represents a potent stromal–immune feedback loop that sustains inflammation, fibrosis, and immune suppression within the tumor microenvironment.

These findings establish the CAF–TAN axis as a self-reinforcing inflammatory circuit that sustains fibrosis, immune suppression, and tumor progression. By guiding neutrophil recruitment, polarization, and NET formation, CAFs convert TANs into chronic effectors of a tumor-supportive niche, while TAN-derived mediators further activate fibroblasts, perpetuating stromal remodeling. This bidirectional loop exemplifies how stromal–immune reciprocity transforms transient inflammation into persistent immunosuppressive architecture, ultimately restricting cytotoxic lymphocyte infiltration and contributing to resistance against ICT. Targeting this feedback network may thus offer a promising strategy to reprogram the inflammatory stroma and enhance immunotherapeutic efficacy.

CAF-mediated modulation of TAMs

Building upon the notion that TAMs actively drive CAF activation, it is increasingly evident that CAFs reciprocally reprogram TAMs, forming a dynamic signaling network that underlies the functional diversity and context-dependent plasticity of both cell types within the tumor microenvironment [130–132]. Through cytokine exchange, metabolic coupling, and matrix remodeling, this TAM–CAF circuitry continuously reshapes the stromal and immune landscape—generating macrophage states that range from pro-inflammatory to tissue-remodeling or tolerogenic, and fibroblast phenotypes that oscillate between contractile and immunomodulatory. Such bidirectional communication not only sustains tumor-promoting inflammation but also dictates spatial organization and immune accessibility. Consequently, the heterogeneity emerging from the TAM–CAF axis has profound implications for ICT, where stromal–immune coordination often determines whether immune activation translates into durable tumor regression or therapeutic failure [133–137].

Through metabolic reprogramming, CAFs secrete a variety of metabolites that promote tumor growth and metastasis; at the same time, these metabolites act on neighboring immune cells to modulate their function and shape the tumor immune microenvironment [115]. Among the diverse metabolic alterations mediated by CAFs, enhanced glycolysis and lactate production have emerged as prominent immunomodulatory features of the tumor microenvironment. Consistent with this notion, CAF-derived lactate promotes macrophage polarization toward an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype in hepatocellular carcinoma [138]. Beyond glycolytic metabolites, glutamine has emerged as an important CAF-derived metabolite implicated in tumor metabolic support and immune suppression. CAF-derived glutamine can be utilized by TAMs to promote protumorigenic polarization, and disruption of glutamine synthetase in CAFs has been shown to impair tumor growth in a TAM-dependent manner [139].

Recent findings demonstrate a complex interplay between CAFs and TAMs [140, 141]. Across multiple cancer types, CAFs and TAMs exhibit striking spatial proximity within the tumor microenvironment. In pancreatic cancer, TSG-6-expressing CAFs were found to be co-localized with immunosuppressive CD44⁺ TAMs, and inhibiting TSG-6 significantly improved the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors [142]. Recent spatial‑omics and multiplex imaging studies have extended these findings across multiple cancers: in gastric carcinoma, CCL2⁺ CAFs spatially associate with STAT3‑activated TAMs, jointly predicting poor survival [143]; colorectal tumors exhibit tightly intermingled SPP1⁺ macrophages and FAP⁺ CAFs within the same microanatomic niches [93]; and in hepatocellular carcinoma, DAB2⁺ TAMs concentrate at the invasive front alongside FAP⁺ CAFs, whereas intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma features adjacent SPP1⁺ TAM–FAP⁺ CAF regions [144]. These recurring patterns of spatial co‑localization underscore the functional synergy between CAFs and TAMs in sculpting an immune‑excluded microenvironment that fosters tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. One of the principal mechanisms underlying this spatial cooperation is the establishment of immunosuppressive barriers. In basal cell carcinoma, a subset of CAFs and TAMs co-induced by Activin A was found to highly express extracellular matrix remodeling genes, such as FAP-α and MMPs, resulting in matrix stiffening and exclusion of CD8⁺ T cells from the tumor parenchyma [145]. Similar CAF–TAM interactions have also been observed in squamous cell carcinoma and melanoma [146]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, SPP1⁺ TAMs and CAFs colocalize at the tumor margin to form a ligand–receptor–mediated immune barrier. Genetic or pharmacologic disruption of SPP1 signaling dismantles this barrier and restores T-cell infiltration [147]. Similarly, in a peritoneal metastasis model of gastric cancer, THBS2⁺ myCAFs promote the conversion of peritoneum-resident macrophages into SPP1⁺ TAMs via the C3–C3R axis, thereby establishing an immunosuppressive stromal–myeloid niche [148]. Collectively, these findings highlight that CAFs and TAMs form a spatially organized, immunosuppressive ecosystem, wherein reciprocal signaling reinforces immune exclusion and drives resistance to immune checkpoint therapy.

Within these shared stromal niches, CAFs function as niche organizers that profoundly regulate TAM recruitment and polarization, thereby sustaining an immunosuppressive microenvironment. CAFs secrete IL‑6, IL‑8, IL‑33, IL‑34, CCL2, CXCL12, and GM‑CSF to attract circulating monocytes and bias their differentiation toward an immunosuppressive M2‑like phenotype. For example, in oral squamous cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, and mycosis fungoides, the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis drives monocyte infiltration [149–151]; in gastric cancer, CPT1C⁺ CAFs exploit IL-6 signaling to promote M2 skewing [152]; and in triple negative breast cancer, AKAP12⁺ CAFs co-localize with CSF1R⁺ TAMs to induce M2 phenotypes via IL-34/CSF1R 135. In colorectal cancer, MCAM⁺ CAFs similarly deploy IL-34, and MCAM knockout models confirm reduced IL-34 expression, diminished TAM infiltration, slowed tumor growth, and prolonged survival [153]. CAFs can also regulate TAM polarization through mechanisms beyond classical cytokine signaling. SAA3, a member of the serum amyloid A gene family, has been implicated in CAF-mediated TAM polarization. In mice, NNMT⁺ CAFs upregulate SAA3 expression by reducing histone H3K27 methylation at its promoter region. This, in turn, activates NF-κB signaling in macrophages and promotes their polarization toward an M2-like phenotype [154]. In breast cancer, FAP⁺ CAFs secrete high levels of fibronectin 1, which binds to integrin α5β1 on the surface of TAMs, activating the FAK–AKT–STAT3 signaling cascade and further promoting M2 polarization [155]. Additionally, hypoxia-inducible factor 2α has been implicated in the CAF-mediated regulation of TAMs. In pancreatic cancer models, genetic ablation of HIF-2α in CAFs significantly reduces the infiltration of immunosuppressive M2-like TAMs [156]. Collectively, these pathways demonstrate how CAFs sculpt an immunosuppressive myeloid niche and identify multiple nodes—chemokine axes, cytokine receptors, matrix ligands, and transcriptional regulators—as promising targets to reprogram TAMs toward antitumor phenotypes.

Although the immunosuppressive roles of CAFs have been extensively studied, emerging evidence suggests that certain CAF subpopulations possess immunostimulatory properties and can suppress tumor progression through interactions with macrophages. In patients with clear cell renal cell carcinoma and urothelial carcinoma, the expression of Meflin on CAFs was positively associated with better responses to ICT. Further investigations revealed that treatment with Am80, a synthetic retinoid, significantly increased the number of Meflin⁺ CAFs. These cells enhanced the production of chemerin, promoting the infiltration and polarization of M1-like TAMs, ultimately boosting the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy. Although the study identified that the optimal timing of Am80 administration was prior to anti-PD-L1 treatment, this approach of reprogramming CAFs to improve ICT responsiveness offers a novel direction for enhancing cancer immunotherapy [157].

By governing macrophage recruitment, polarization, and spatial organization, CAFs orchestrate the establishment of immunosuppressive stromal–myeloid niches that restrict cytotoxic T-cell infiltration and blunt antitumor immunity. These interactions not only reinforce local immune tolerance but also contribute to primary and acquired resistance to immune checkpoint therapy. Conversely, reprogramming CAFs toward immune-supportive states—such as Meflin⁺ or chemerin-producing phenotypes—can redirect macrophages toward pro-inflammatory, M1-like profiles and restore immune accessibility. Thus, the CAF–TAM axis functions as a central rheostat of tumor immune equilibrium, and its therapeutic modulation holds promise for transforming immune-excluded tumors into immunologically responsive ones.

CAF-mediated modulation of NK cells

As sentinels of innate immunity, NK cells represent a crucial frontline defense against malignant transformation [158]. They execute rapid, antigen-independent cytotoxicity through the release of perforin and granzymes, secrete IFN-γ to amplify adaptive immune responses, and bridge innate and adaptive immunity by shaping DCs and T-cell function. However, within the tumor microenvironment, NK cell activity is profoundly constrained by stromal and soluble factors that reprogram their activation thresholds, migration patterns, and cytolytic potential. Among these regulators, CAFs have emerged as central modulators of NK-cell fate [159]. Multiple CAF subpopulations—including PDPN⁺ CAFs, DKK1⁺ CAFs, NCAM1⁺ senescent CAFs, and myCAFs—have been shown, predominantly in breast carcinoma models, to suppress NK cell cytotoxicity via diverse immunoinhibitory pathways. In trastuzumab-refractory HER2-positive breast cancer, PDPN⁺ CAFs proliferate and elevate IDO1/TDO2 expression to block NK cell–driven antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity [160]. Furthermore, in the 4T1 and E0771 mouse models, NK cells form direct contacts with CAFs via NKG2D and DNAM1 engagement, leading to CAF lysis and a compensatory downregulation of NK activating receptors, which ultimately permits tumor immune escape [161]. Conversely, under certain microenvironmental conditions, CAFs may also sustain NK-cell effector function. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, CAF-derived IL-15 has been shown to enhance NK-cell cytotoxicity; however, this stimulatory pathway is counteracted by sensory neuron–mediated repression of IL-15, ultimately diminishing NK-cell–driven tumor control and promoting tumor growth [162].

CAF-driven metabolic programs have also been implicated in the regulation of NK cell–mediated immunosurveillance. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, NetG1⁺ CAFs suppress NK cell activity by rewiring glutamate/glutamine metabolism, characterized by increased expression of glutamine synthetase and vesicular glutamate transporter 1. This immunosuppressive metabolic program is orchestrated by a NetG1-dependent signaling axis involving AKT/4EBP1 and p38/FRA1 [163]. Beyond these mechanistic insights, a bioinformatic analysis in colorectal cancer revealed reduced NK cell abundance in tumor regions enriched for iCAFs. Functional enrichment analyses further indicated that iCAFs were associated with lipid metabolism–related pathways, including cholesterol homeostasis, bile acid metabolism, and fatty acid metabolism [164]. Together, these observations suggest a potential link between CAF metabolic reprogramming and NK cell infiltration or functional states, although the precise mechanisms by which CAFs regulate NK cell responses remain to be fully elucidated.

Although direct clinical correlations remain limited, current evidence indicates that CAF-mediated modulation of NK-cell activity constitutes an underappreciated layer of immune regulation within the tumor microenvironment. By altering NK-cell activation, CAFs may indirectly influence the initiation and amplitude of adaptive immune responses, thereby shaping the overall sensitivity of tumors to immunotherapy. Understanding how stromal cues reprogram NK-cell states—balancing activation versus exhaustion—will be critical for delineating the full immunologic impact of the CAF–NK cell axis. Therapeutically, restoring NK-supportive cytokine signaling or alleviating stromal-derived inhibitory pressure holds considerable promise for reinvigorating innate immune surveillance and augmenting the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade. Ultimately, integrating NK-cell–directed and stroma-modulating strategies may unlock new opportunities to convert immune-excluded tumors into immunologically responsive ecosystems.

CAF-mediated modulation of T cells

As discussed above, signals derived from activated T cells profoundly influence the state and function of CAFs, driving their phenotypic diversification within the tumor microenvironment. In response to cytokines such as IFN-γ, TNF, and IL-1, CAFs undergo distinct transcriptional and functional reprogramming, giving rise to heterogeneous subpopulations with immunomodulatory capacities. These CAF subsets, in turn, exert multilayered control over T-cell function—modulating their recruitment, activation, and effector persistence through cytokine secretion, metabolic regulation, and extracellular matrix remodeling. Through this reciprocal signaling network, T cells and CAFs co-evolve to shape the immune landscape of tumors, ultimately determining whether the microenvironment supports sustained antitumor immunity or fosters immune dysfunction and therapeutic resistance [165].

CAFs play multifaceted roles in regulating T-cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment. On one hand, they establish physical barriers that spatially segregate tumor and immune compartments. In several solid tumors—including lung, pancreatic, and breast cancers—MYH11⁺α-SMA⁺ CAFs form a continuous monolayer encasing the tumor margins, which correlates with markedly reduced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration within the tumor nests [52]. On the other hand, CAFs shape chemotactic landscapes that misdirect T cells into stromal rather than intratumoral regions. For example, in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, CAF-derived CXCL9 and CXCL10 attract CD8⁺ T cells but confine them to the peritumoral stroma, thereby impeding their entry into tumor nests [166]. In soft tissue sarcoma, a glycolytic subset of CD73⁺ CAFs highly expresses CXCL16, which binds CXCR6 on T cells and restricts their accumulation to the tumor periphery [167]. Conversely, in hepatocellular carcinoma patients who respond favorably to PD-1 blockade, FMO2⁺ CAFs overexpress CCL19 to engage CCR7 on T cells, promoting intratumoral CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and enhancing immunotherapeutic efficacy [168]. Multiple regulatory pathways govern CAF expression of these chemokines. For instance, glucose transporter GLUT1 promotes CAF-derived CXCL16 production [167], whereas in hepatocellular carcinoma, endosialin (CD248) suppresses STAT1 activation and consequently downregulates CXCL9 and CXCL10, thereby limiting T-cell recruitment [169]. Beyond chemokine-mediated guidance, CAFs further restrict T-cell access through extracellular matrix remodeling and vascular modulation. In colorectal cancer, a CXCL14⁺ CAF subset reprograms ECM composition at the tumor–stroma interface, creating an exclusionary barrier that prevents T-cell penetration [170]. Similarly, in murine Lewis lung carcinoma, SFRP2^hi CAFs downregulate HPGD in endothelial cells, impairing CD8⁺ T-cell trafficking into the tumor bed [171]. Targeting the T-cell–exclusionary functions of CAFs represents a promising therapeutic strategy. A preclinical study employing FAP-directed CAR-T cells demonstrated that selective depletion of FAP⁺ stromal populations effectively overcame desmoplastic barriers in pancreatic tumors, reshaped the immune landscape, and enhanced the infiltration of endogenous CD8⁺ T cells and NK cells [172]. This work provides a theoretical basis for developing stromal-targeted strategies to enhance immunotherapy in the clinic.

Further, CAFs actively modulate T cell activation and effector functions—such as inducing CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion and senescence, stimulating CD4⁺ T cell activation, and facilitating Treg differentiation—through a complex interplay determined by tumor context, CAF subpopulation identity, spatial distribution, and intrinsic phenotype. Evidence indicates that apCAFs with antigen-presenting capacity enhance CD4⁺ T cell responses. In the lung carcinoma model, ablation of the MHC II gene I-Ab in apCAFs abrogated CD4⁺ T cell–driven antitumor immunity, demonstrating the pivotal role of apCAF-expressed MHC-II in CD4⁺ T cell recruitment and activity [173]. As critical biomarkers of checkpoint inhibitor response and clinical outcome, tertiary lymphoid structures are spatially juxtaposed with apCAFs in gastric carcinoma [174, 175]. These apCAFs upregulate MHC-II to engage and activate CD4⁺ T cells, thereby potentiating antitumor immunity [176]. Additionally, in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma patients and autochthonous tumor models, p16⁺ senescent CAFs have been described. Experimentally driving CAF senescence diminishes activated CD8⁺ T cell counts, while genetic clearance of these cells restores CD8⁺ T cell activation, potentially through enhanced expression of ECM-modifying, stress-response, and immunomodulatory genes [177]. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, a PLA2G2A-positive CAF subpopulation inhibits the cytotoxic efficacy of infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells; soluble PLA2G2A engages CD8⁺ T cells and, through MAPK/ERK and NF-κB signaling, downregulates key effector molecules IFN-γ and GZMB [178]. Furthermore, in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, the prevalence of galectin-9-expressing CAFs is negatively associated with TCF1⁺GZMK⁺CD8⁺ T cell levels. Galectin-9 drives T cell exhaustion and diminishes intratumoral TCF1⁺CD8⁺ T cell proportions [166, 167]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, CD90⁺ CAFs drive CD8⁺ T cell aging through LAMA4 engagement of T cell ITGA6; LAMA4 knockdown by siRNA potentiates anti-PD-1 antibody responses in hepatocarcinogenesis models [179].

Beyond cytokines and chemokines, CAF-derived metabolites have emerged as potent regulators of T-cell fate and function within the tumor microenvironment. A representative example is lactate, a key metabolic byproduct of glycolytic CAFs. In prostate cancer models, CAF-derived lactate directly acts on CD4⁺ T cells to reshape T-cell polarization, thereby subverting antitumor immunity. Mechanistically, lactate suppresses Th1 differentiation by inducing SIRT1-dependent deacetylation and degradation of the lineage-defining transcription factor T-bet, while simultaneously promoting Treg differentiation through lactate-driven NF-κB activation and FOXP3 induction. This metabolically conditioned immune milieu not only dampens effector T-cell responses but also reinforces immunosuppression, creating conditions permissive for tumor progression. Notably, the lactate-enriched, CAF-shaped immune environment further feeds back on tumor cells by activating a miR-21/TLR8 signaling axis that enhances invasive behavior, highlighting how CAF-derived metabolic cues can couple immune dysfunction to malignant progression [180]. Together, these findings position CAF metabolism as an active determinant of T-cell plasticity and underscore metabolic crosstalk as a critical, non-canonical mechanism by which CAFs orchestrate immune evasion.

Tolerogenic Tregs execute immune suppression through the expression of inhibitory receptors (e.g., CTLA-4, PD-1), ectoenzymes (CD39, CD73), and suppressive cytokines (IL-10, IL-35, TGF-β) [181]. CAFs interact with Tregs in the tumor microenvironment to modulate Treg differentiation and potentiate immunosuppressive activity. Evidence indicates that in non-small cell lung cancer, oral squamous cell carcinoma, and the B16F10 melanoma model, CAFs are spatially juxtaposed with Tregs, implying direct crosstalk. Notably, in vivo depletion of α-SMA⁺ CAFs via transgenic approaches markedly decreases Treg numbers [182]. Furthermore, analysis of publicly available myofibroblast subset data from lung, liver, and breast tumors revealed an MMP1⁺ myofibroblast subset that engages Tregs and potentially recruits them through the ICAM-1–IL2RA/IL2RG chemokine receptor pathway [51]. This raises the question: by what mechanisms do CAFs interact with Tregs? Research has shown that α-SMA⁺ CAFs establish immunological synapses with FOXP3⁺ Tregs via an autophagy-driven process; conditional knockout of the autophagy gene Atg5 in CAFs reduces Treg infiltration and potentiates checkpoint inhibitor responses [182]. Within pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, IL-1/TGF-β signaling reprograms mesothelial cells into MHC-II⁺ apCAFs that selectively promote the differentiation of naïve CD4⁺ T cells into Tregs [183]. In addition, CAF-expressed immune checkpoints, including PD-L1 and PD-L2, directly modulate the induction of Tregs. For instance, anti-PD-1 therapy expands apCAF populations in non-small-cell lung cancer and elevates PD-L2, which engages Treg surface RGMB to drive FOXP1⁺ Treg differentiation; interruption of the PD-L2–RGMB signaling axis augments checkpoint inhibitor efficacy [46]. In ovarian cancer, the Activin/SMAD2 signaling pathway within CAFs upregulates PD-L1 expression, thereby inducing Treg differentiation [184]. Tumor-infiltrating CD73⁺ γδ Tregs constitute the predominant Treg subset in breast cancer and correlate with poor prognosis. Further studies revealed that CAF-derived IL-6 is the key cytokine driving the differentiation of CD73⁺ γδ Tregs [185].

CAF-mediated modulation of DCs

DC differentiation and subsequent maturation—hallmarked by the upregulation of co-stimulatory molecules and the acquisition of robust cytokine-secreting capacity—are indispensable for effective T cell–mediated antitumor immunity. Accumulating evidence indicates that CAFs across multiple tumor types, including lung, pancreatic, hepatic, and prostate cancers, actively interfere with these processes. In particular, CAFs have been shown to impair the differentiation of monocyte-derived DCs and restrain their maturation toward an immunostimulatory phenotype, thereby limiting efficient T-cell priming and fostering an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [186–189].

Mechanistically, CAFs shape DC fate and function through a range of soluble mediators, including WNT2, thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), and IL-6, which collectively dampen DC immunostimulatory capacity. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, CAF-derived WNT2 suppresses DC-driven antitumor T-cell responses via activation of the SOCS3–JAK2–STAT3 signaling axis, and therapeutic targeting of WNT2 has been proposed as a strategy to enhance ICT efficacy [190]. In pancreatic cancer, activated CAFs induce upregulation of the TSLP receptor (TSLPR) on myeloid DCs, promote the production of Th2-attracting chemokines, and confer a TSLP-dependent Th2-polarizing program in vitro, suggesting that blockade of CAF-derived TSLP may improve clinical outcomes [188]. Similarly, in prostate cancer, stromal cells inhibit the differentiation of CD14⁻DC-SIGN⁺ monocyte-derived DCs and induce PD-L1 expression on DCs through IL-6/STAT3 signaling, collectively reinforcing an immunosuppressive DC phenotype [186]. Together, these studies support a model in which CAFs exert broad inhibitory effects on DC differentiation and function. Notably, however, the existing literature has largely focused on monocyte-derived DCs. How CAFs regulate DC subsets of distinct ontogeny—particularly conventional type I DCs, which are specialized for cross-presentation to cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells—remains poorly defined. Addressing this knowledge gap will be critical for the rational design of CAF-targeted combination strategies aimed at improving responses to ICT.

Collectively, the intricate web of interactions between CAFs and immune cells—spanning adaptive and innate lineages—constitutes a fundamental regulatory layer of the tumor microenvironment. By integrating cues from neutrophils, macrophages, NK cells and T cells, CAFs act as dynamic sensors and amplifiers of immune activities, translating transient immune signals into lasting structural and biochemical changes. Through cytokine exchange, metabolic coupling, and matrix remodeling, CAFs sculpt immune cell localization, activation thresholds, and effector persistence, thereby defining the balance between inflammation, immune tolerance, and tissue repair. In turn, immune-derived mediators continuously reprogram CAF phenotypes, reinforcing the plasticity of stromal–immune circuits that can either sustain antitumor immunity or restore immune suppression. From a therapeutic perspective, this bidirectional immune–stromal communication has profound implications for ICT. While immune checkpoint inhibitors reactivate T cells and revitalize innate immune surveillance, it simultaneously induces stromal remodeling that can reestablish immune exclusion and dampen treatment efficacy—a hallmark of secondary resistance. Thus, deciphering and targeting CAF-centered immune feedback loops—encompassing TANs, TAMs, NK cells, T cells and DCs—offers a compelling strategy to reprogram the tumor microenvironment from a fibrotic, immunosuppressive niche into one that sustains durable immune engagement and long-term tumor control.

CAF–immune crosstalk in ICT response

The therapeutic efficacy of ICT hinges not only on the reinvigoration of exhausted T cells but also on the capacity of the tumor microenvironment to sustain productive immune activation. Building upon the growing recognition that CAFs are not merely structural elements but dynamic regulators of tumor immunity, increasing evidence suggests that their activity substantially influences the outcome of ICT [191, 192]. The remarkable heterogeneity of CAFs confers context-dependent immunoregulatory properties—ranging from fostering immune exclusion and T-cell dysfunction to supporting tertiary lymphoid structure formation—that collectively dictate therapeutic response or failure (Fig. 4). Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of CAF diversity and its immunomodulatory circuitry is essential for decoding ICT resistance, identifying stromal biomarkers predictive of response, and designing rational combination strategies [193–195]. In this chapter, we synthesize current evidence detailing the direct involvement of CAFs in modulating immune dynamics during checkpoint blockade, with a focus on three prototypical stroma-rich malignancies—non–small cell lung cancer, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, and triple-negative breast cancer—selected for their abundant and functionally diverse CAF populations and their historically poor or heterogeneous responses to immunotherapy (Table 3).

Non-small cell lung cancer

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies worldwide. The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has revolutionized the treatment paradigm for advanced NSCLC, establishing them as a mainstay in both first-line and subsequent-line regimens [196, 197]. Despite these advances, therapeutic resistance continues to limit their efficacy. Approximately 5–20% of patients exhibit primary resistance, failing to respond to initial therapy, while 60–85% eventually develop secondary resistance after six months or longer of treatment [193, 198, 199]. CAFs critically shape the therapeutic landscape of NSCLC by orchestrating stromal–immune interactions that determine response or resistance to immune checkpoint blockade. High densities of FAP⁺α-SMA⁺ and MYH11⁺α-SMA⁺ CAFs give rise to a fibrotic and immunosuppressive niche characterized by CD8⁺ T-cell exhaustion and regulatory T-cell accumulation, thereby fostering an immune-excluded microenvironment that drives primary resistance to checkpoint blockade [200, 201].

With the increasing clinical application of ICT in NSCLC and the rapid advancement of single-cell and spatial omics technologies, it has become possible to analyze large, well-characterized patient cohorts with distinct therapeutic responses. In one study analyzing paired pre- and post-treatment samples from NSCLC patients undergoing neoadjuvant anti–PD-1 plus chemotherapy, COL11A1⁺ CAFs were found to be consistently enriched in non-responders both before and after therapy. Further investigation revealed that COL11A1⁺ CAFs cooperated with SPP1⁺ macrophages to deposit and interlink collagen fibers at the tumor margins, forming a physical barrier that restricted T-cell infiltration and correlated with poor prognosis [92]. Another single-cell analysis of NSCLC patients treated with the same regimen identified five functional CAF subtypes—matrix CAFs (mCAFs), antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs), inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), metabolic CAFs (meCAFs), and proliferative CAFs (pCAFs). Notably, mCAFs, characterized by high COL11A1 expression, were preferentially enriched in non-responders, suggesting that ECM-remodeling CAFs may contribute to immune exclusion and treatment failure [53]. While these findings delineate transcriptional features associated with ICI resistance, the functional mechanisms underlying CAF-mediated immune modulation remain largely unresolved. Future efforts integrating independent single-cell datasets through cross-cohort meta-analysis and batch correction could yield a comprehensive multi-patient atlas of stromal–immune dynamics in NSCLC, providing a valuable foundation for mechanistic studies and the development of CAF-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Secondary resistance to ICT affects the majority of NSCLC patients, reflecting adaptive reprogramming within both tumor cells and the tumor microenvironment. Among the key mediators of this adaptive process is interferon-γ, a central effector cytokine of reinvigorated T-cell responses during ICT. While IFN-γ plays a critical role in antitumor immunity, sustained or dysregulated IFN-γ signaling can paradoxically remodel the stromal compartment. Recent findings demonstrate that heightened IFN-γ activity reprograms CAFs, establishing feedback loops that modulate immune activation and contribute to therapeutic resistance. Specifically, IFN-γ–driven expansion of apCAFs upregulates PD-L2 expression, which interacts with repulsive guidance molecule B to recruit FOXP1⁺ regulatory T cells and suppress antitumor immunity. Pharmacologic blockade of IFN-γ signaling or disruption of the PD-L2–RGMB axis restores immune equilibrium and enhances responsiveness to PD-1 blockade [46]. Collectively, these findings underscore the multifaceted roles of CAF subtypes in creating both permissive and prohibitive microenvironments for ICT in non-small cell lung cancer and highlight the therapeutic potential of subtype-specific interventions to overcome stromal-mediated resistance.

Pancreatic cancer

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma typifies a “cold” tumor, with minimal sensitivity to ICT [202, 203]. In the KEYNOTE 158 trial, 22 MSI-H/dMMR pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma patients achieved an objective response rate of 18.2%, including three partial and one complete response, with a median duration of response of 13.4 months, but the median overall survival was only 13.4 months [204]. By contrast, microsatellite stable pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma remains largely unresponsive: PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy or combined PD-L1/CTLA-4 blockade produces objective response rates of merely 0–3.1%, and combinations with vaccines, oncolytic viruses, or targeted agents have failed to yield significant survival improvements [205]. Thus, while ICT offers benefit for the MSI-H/dMMR subset, microsatellite stable pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma continues to resist ICT, underscoring an urgent need for novel combination strategies to surmount this therapeutic barrier.

Recent investigations in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma have uncovered how discrete CAF subsets undermine ICT efficacy and critically identified avenues to counteract this stromal-mediated resistance. FAP⁺ CAFs are the principal source of CXCL12, which forms a peritumoral “cloak” that repels effector T cells and sequesters them away from malignant cells. Pharmacologic blockade of CXCR4 with AMD3100 rapidly reverses this chemokine barrier, enabling T cell infiltration around cancer cells and, when combined with anti-PD-L1 therapy, effecting near-complete eradication of Trp53 heterozygous tumor foci—leaving only premalignant epithelium and reactive inflammatory infiltrates [206]. In parallel, selective depletion of LRRC15⁺ CAFs reprograms the stroma toward a progenitor-like fibroblast state, relieves CD8⁺ T cell suppression, and synergizes with anti-PD-L1 to induce robust tumor regression [64]. In a separate preclinical investigation, combined MEK and STAT3 inhibition in murine models depletes both iCAF (IL-6⁺/CXCL1⁺) and myCAF (LRRC15⁺), enriches an MSC-like (Ly6G⁺/CD34⁺) CAF subset, repolarizes tumor-associated macrophages to an M1 phenotype, and dramatically enhances CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and anti-PD-1 efficacy [207]. Conversely, TSG-6⁺ CAFs promote ICT resistance by recruiting immunosuppressive CD44⁺ macrophages [142]. Spatial mapping in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma further demonstrates that LRRC15⁺ CAFs assemble into a dense peritumoral “shell” around malignant islets—absent in normal tissue—and that elevated LRRC15⁺ CAF signatures predict poor response to anti-PD-L1 therapy [208]. These findings underscore the dualistic nature of CAFs as both barriers to effective antitumor immunity and as promising therapeutic targets, providing a theoretical foundation for the development of subtype-specific strategies to enhance ICT responses in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma.

Triple-negative breast cancer

In PD-L1–positive triple-negative breast cancer, the addition of ICT to chemotherapy significantly prolongs survival and increases objective response rates. However, approximately 50% of patients still exhibit primary resistance. Resistance rates are even higher—exceeding 80%—when immune checkpoint inhibitors are used as monotherapy in later-line settings or in non-triple negative breast cancer subtypes [194, 209–211].

Recent studies have uncovered multiple, non–mutually exclusive CAF-driven circuits that foster immune exclusion and primary resistance to checkpoint blockade. One axis involves CAF-derived IGF2, which amplifies CXCL12 and PD-L1 expression to exclude CD8⁺ T cells. Genetic ablation of IGF2 or pharmacologic inhibition of IGF1R restores T cell infiltration and synergizes with anti-PD-1 therapy, while elevated tumor or circulating IGF2 levels predicte ICT poor response [212]. In parallel, AKAP12⁺ CAFs establish an immunosuppressive niche by secreting IL-34, which engages CSF1R to polarize macrophages toward an M2 phenotype. Blockade of the IL-34/CSF1R pathway similarly reinstates anti-PD-1 efficacy [213]. Complementing these findings, a distinct ECM-secreted myCAF subset (cluster 0) has been shown to upregulate PD-1 and CTLA-4 on regulatory T cells. The feedback signals from these cells then expand TGF-β–driven myCAFs (cluster 3), creating a self-reinforcing loop that underlies primary resistance to combined PD-1/CTLA-4 blockade [214]. Together, these insights highlight the multifaceted roles of CAF subtypes in mediating both cellular and molecular barriers to effective ICT and underscore the potential of targeting these stromal circuits to overcome therapeutic resistance in triple-negative breast cancer.

Despite the growing body of research delineating CAF heterogeneity across individual tumor types, the unifying mechanisms through which CAFs orchestrate ICT resistance remain incompletely defined. Moving beyond tumor-specific observations toward a systems-level understanding of stromal immunoregulation requires comprehensive pan-cancer analyses that integrate single-cell and spatial multi-omics data. Such cross-contextual comparisons can uncover conserved CAF-derived programs—whether chemokine–cytokine networks, metabolic circuits, or extracellular matrix remodeling modules—that recurrently underpin immune exclusion and therapeutic failure. Defining these shared molecular architectures will illuminate the fundamental principles by which the stroma constrains adaptive immunity, enabling the design of next-generation interventions that recondition, rather than eradicate, the tumor stroma. By targeting these evolutionarily conserved resistance circuits, future therapies may achieve durable immune reinvigoration across diverse malignancies, transforming CAFs from barriers to facilitators of antitumor immunity and extending the reach of ICT to currently unresponsive cancers.

Targeting the CAF–immune axis in ICT

The growing realization that stromal reprogramming can dictate the outcome of ICT has positioned CAFs as both barriers and potential allies in immunotherapy. Rather than being a uniform population, CAFs comprise molecularly and functionally distinct subsets whose interactions with immune cells fundamentally shape therapeutic responsiveness. Mounting clinical and preclinical evidence demonstrates that specific CAF populations can either restrain or potentiate immune activation through diverse mechanisms—ranging from chemokine-mediated T-cell exclusion and extracellular matrix remodeling to modulation of cytokine and metabolic circuits [30, 215]. This recognition has shifted the therapeutic paradigm from indiscriminate stromal ablation toward selective targeting and functional reprogramming of CAF subsets that drive immune suppression. Emerging strategies now focus on disrupting the CAF–immune crosstalk that underlies resistance to ICT. These include targeting canonical stromal markers such as FAP and α-SMA, inhibiting signaling mediators like TGF-β, WNT2, or HIF2α, and reversing CAF-driven immune exclusion through precision interventions such as bispecific antibodies, small-molecule inhibitors, or stromal reconditioning agents [216–222]. The expanding landscape of CAF-directed clinical and translational studies underscores a central principle: effectively integrating stromal modulation with checkpoint blockade requires a precise understanding of CAF heterogeneity and its immunoregulatory consequences. FAP is one of the most feasible CAF markers for potential clinical application and is associated with poor prognosis in various tumor types [223, 224]. As mentioned, spatial and transcriptomic analyses have revealed a positive correlation between FAP⁺ CAFs and SPP1⁺ macrophages, which cooperatively generate immune-excluded stromal bridges that hinder T-cell infiltration and attenuate the therapeutic efficacy of anti-PD-L1 treatment in FAPhi tumors [93]. Targeted depletion of FAP⁺ CAFs can overcome stromal constraints on perivascular invasion, reverse immune exclusion, enhance tumor immune responses, and inhibit tumor growth. FAP-targeted therapies include small molecule inhibitors [225], antibodies [226], DNA vaccines [227], and nanoparticles [228, 229]. In clinical trials, FAP-specific antibodies have demonstrated safety and efficacy [230]. Current clinical trials for FAP focus on improving diagnostic efficiency for malignancies when combined with radio tracers and exploring its potential for predicting the efficacy of tumor immunotherapy.

α-SMA is another common marker of CAFs. α-SMA⁺ CAFs exhibit dual roles in tumor progression. In models of breast cancer and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, depletion of α-SMA⁺ CAFs has been shown to inhibit cancer cell metastasis and tumor angiogenesis [231, 232]. α-SMA⁺ CAFs can also enhance Treg cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment, which promotes tumor invasion and progression. FAP⁺ α-SMA⁺ CAFs are associated with inflammatory responses and CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion, while MYH11⁺α-SMA⁺ CAFs favor an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment characterized by Treg infiltration. These specific CAF subpopulations emerge at distinct stages of tumor progression and play a key role in primary resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors in non-small cell lung cancer patients [201].

Beyond traditional biomarkers, novel markers for CAFs in various cancer types are progressively being identified. For example, a negative correlation exists between WNT2⁺ CAFs and activated CD8⁺ T cells, and targeting WNT2⁺ CAFs has been shown to enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy [190]. In breast cancer, the CAF receptor Endo180 (MRC2) is predominantly expressed on myCAFs. Loss of MRC2 increases sensitivity to immunotherapy. Moreover, in clinical trials with melanoma patients, high tumor MRC2 mRNA levels were correlated with resistance to anti-PD-1 treatment [165]. Single-cell sequencing analysis of prostate cancer revealed that extracellular matrix-related CAFs (ECM-CAFs) promote collagen deposition and cancer cell progression. Yes-associated protein (YAP) facilitates the phenotypic transition of CAFs towards the ECM-CAFs phenotype. Selective in vivo deletion of YAP1 in ECM-CAFs promotes CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and activation, enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of anti-PD-1 in prostate cancer [233]. POSTN⁺ CAFs are enriched in advanced non-small cell lung cancer and exhibit gene expression signatures linked to extracellular matrix remodeling, tumor invasion, and immune suppression, correlating with poor prognosis in non-small cell lung cancer [234]. Across multiple cancer types, LRRC15⁺ CAFs have been shown to correlate with resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. MAPKhi CAFs exhibit TGF-β signaling and hypoxia responses, are enriched within myCAFs from various cancers, and are associated with reduced responses to immune checkpoint inhibition in melanoma [235]. HIF2α⁺ CAFs are a significant component in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. Treatment with the HIF2α inhibitor PT2399 enhanced the response to immunotherapy in two murine models of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. Consequently, HIF2α represents a viable therapeutic target for mitigating the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment and augmenting immune responses [156]. In hepatocellular carcinoma patients, the FMO2⁺ (Flavin-Containing Monooxygenase 2) CAF subpopulation is associated with response to anti-PD-1 therapy and better prognosis. FMO2⁺ CAFs enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-1 treatment by promoting immune cell infiltration and tertiary lymphoid structure formation via the CCL19-CCR7 axis. Favorable immunotherapy responses were observed in hepatocellular carcinoma patients with high serum CCL19 levels. Thus, the FMO2⁺ CAF subpopulation serves as a key regulator of the immunologic characteristics of the hepatocellular carcinoma microenvironment and as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response. Concurrently, combining CCL19 with anti-PD-1 therapy may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for hepatocellular carcinoma [168]. Subclassification of CAF populations in gastric cancer identified an MFAP5⁺CFD⁺PI16⁺ CAF subtype negatively correlated with overall survival and response to immunotherapy. This association is linked to an immunosuppressive microenvironment [236]. A recent study identifies nicotinamide N-methyltransferase (NNMT) as a critical regulator in CAFs that promotes immune suppression in the tumor microenvironment. NNMT-mediated histone hypomethylation enhances complement secretion, which recruits MDSCs and impairs CD8⁺ T cell activation. Inhibition of NNMT has been shown to reduce tumor growth and metastasis, while also restoring the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade. These findings highlight NNMT as a promising therapeutic target for modulating CAF-driven immunosuppression and enhancing the effectiveness of ICT [237].

However, depletion of CAFs and the reversal of their functions remain significant challenges [71, 219, 238]. Consequently, research has shifted towards targeting signaling molecules or regulatory factors essential to CAF biology, which appears more feasible. TGF-β plays a critical role in the activation of CAFs, their phenotypic conversion, and interactions between CAFs and immune cells. Targeting TGF-β could potentially restore the impaired immune response within the tumor microenvironment [67, 239]. In patients with metastatic urothelial carcinoma, features associated with TGF-β signaling correlate with resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [240]. In mouse models, combined administration of a TGF-β-blocking antibody and anti-PD-L1 therapy reduced TGF-β signaling in stromal cells, promoted T-cell infiltration into the tumor core, and elicited robust antitumor immunity, leading to tumor regression [122, 241]. Clinical trials have also demonstrated that the TGF-βR1 inhibitor galunisertib (LY2157299) increased the survival duration of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma [242]. Furthermore, patients exhibiting T-cell exhaustion may benefit from combination therapy with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and TGF-β RI blockade. Clinical studies combining galunisertib with nivolumab reported that the regimen was well-tolerated and showed preliminary efficacy [243]. Additionally, bispecific molecules, such as an anti-PD-L1 + TGF-β RII bifunctional fusion protein, have also been shown to enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors [241].

Furthermore, similar to all other cancer treatment modalities, the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade is limited by primary and acquired resistance. Primary resistance is defined as a lack of response upon initial administration of immune checkpoint blockade. Conversely, acquired resistance occurs in patients who initially show promising signs of tumor regression, and it also compromises the duration of clinical benefit. In fact, there is no strict binary distinction between primary and acquired resistance phenomena, as the underlying mechanisms are not significantly different [193]. As outlined in our prior discussion, CAFs promote resistance to immune checkpoint blockade by forming physical barriers, fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment, and shaping pro-tumorigenic phenotypes. Within the tumor microenvironment, immune cells remodel CAF phenotypes. While this CAF-immune interplay explains part of the primary resistance, acquired resistance often stems from the dynamic remodeling of the immune-stromal microenvironment following treatment. A high baseline enrichment level of apCAFs is a marker of innate resistance to ICT. When immune checkpoint blockade is administered, the tumor microenvironment is altered, promoting the production of the cytokine IFN-γ. This further impacts apCAFs, ultimately leading to acquired resistance. This process establishes a self-perpetuating cycle of immune stimulation-CAF adaptive reprogramming and immunosuppressive stromal remodeling, ultimately impairing T-cell function [46]. Targeting such dynamic circuits may be key to overcoming acquired resistance. Therefore, during immunotherapy, as the tumor immune microenvironment changes, whether it affects CAF phenotype alteration, further contributing to immune checkpoint blockade resistance, and whether CAFs are drivers rather than merely passive bystanders in acquired resistance, needs to be investigated by analyzing CAF evolutionary trajectories before and after ICT using longitudinal single-cell sequencing. This therapy-triggered CAF reprogramming is not only a core mechanism of acquired resistance but also offers new rationale for developing combinatorial strategies targeting the CAF-immune axis. Future research should focus on the dynamic evolution of CAFs during acquired resistance, specifically elucidating how secondary immune stimulation induced by immune checkpoint blockade drives their functional transformation via relevant signaling pathways. Developing temporally sequenced combination therapies based on such mechanisms may extend the treatment response period.

To overcome the immunosuppressive mechanisms mediated by CAFs within the tumor microenvironment and enhance the efficacy of immunotherapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, multiple clinical trial strategies based on targeting CAFs have been initiated. These strategies primarily focus on disrupting key biomarkers of CAFs and their core effector molecules that drive tumor progression, immunosuppression, and therapy resistance. Although these pioneering efforts have opened promising avenues for combination therapies, the complex adaptive resistance mechanisms of solid tumors remain an unresolved core challenge. Particularly noteworthy is the significant heterogeneity in CAF subpopulations and their functionalities. Different subpopulations may exert diametrically opposed effects and dynamically undergo phenotypic transformation in response to tumor evolution and therapeutic pressures. Therefore, to significantly improve the efficacy, safety, and precision of combination therapies targeting CAFs, it is imperative to explore and validate novel, reliable, and specific molecular biomarkers capable of distinctly identifying key pathogenic CAF subpopulations that drive malignant tumor progression and immunosuppression. These biomarkers must not only possess high tissue specificity and functional relevance but also facilitate the development of targeted drugs against the core pathways of these pathogenic subpopulations. This approach aims to achieve the precise dismantling of immune barriers within the tumor microenvironment, overcome drug resistance, and unlock the full therapeutic potential of immunotherapy in solid tumors.

Conclusions and future perspectives

Over the past decade, advances in single-cell and spatial multi-omics have redefined our understanding of CAFs as active participants in tumor immunity rather than passive structural supporters. CAFs integrate inflammatory, metabolic, and mechanical cues to orchestrate stromal remodeling, immune cell localization, and therapeutic responsiveness. Together, these findings establish CAF–immune crosstalk as a fundamental regulatory axis that governs whether the tumor microenvironment evolves toward immune activation or immune evasion, profoundly shaping outcomes of ICT.

Despite these advances, several fundamental questions remain unresolved. The remarkable heterogeneity and plasticity of CAFs continue to challenge our ability to define consistent and functionally meaningful subsets across cancer types. Their capacity to dynamically shift phenotypes in response to tumor evolution or therapeutic pressure further complicates efforts to selectively target pathogenic CAFs without perturbing stromal and immune homeostasis. Addressing these challenges requires the development of precise biomarkers and refined strategies capable of distinguishing and modulating distinct CAF subpopulations in a context-specific manner. Future research should leverage multi-dimensional technologies—such as single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, multiplexed imaging, and lineage-tracing approaches—to resolve the spatiotemporal organization and functional dynamics of CAF–immune interactions across cancers. Integration of these datasets will be crucial for identifying conserved CAF programs that mediate immune suppression and ICT resistance, thereby informing the rational design of next-generation stromal-targeted immunotherapies.

Moreover, CAF-targeting strategies face inherent challenges: global depletion risks disrupting stromal integrity and immune homeostasis, whereas subset-specific interventions are constrained by the lack of robust, lineage-resolved markers. Importantly, CAF-mediated resistance to ICT rarely acts in isolation—CAF–immune interactions are embedded within broader stromal and metabolic networks that cooperatively sustain immunosuppression and therapeutic escape. Addressing these challenges will require a biologically integrated framework that captures the spatiotemporal dynamics and intercellular dependencies of CAFs within the tumor ecosystem. Identifying CAF subpopulations that either promote or restrain antitumor immunity will be essential for rational therapeutic design. Future strategies should move beyond CAF ablation toward functional reprogramming—redirecting fibroblast activities from immune exclusion to immune activation. For instance, selective inhibition of TGF-β signaling in CAFs has been shown to relieve immune exclusion and restore T-cell infiltration, while emerging approaches aim to activate “beneficial” CAF phenotypes capable of sustaining anti-tumor immunity. Ultimately, the next frontier in CAF research lies in mapping the regulatory circuits that drive fibroblast plasticity and immune modulation, enabling the development of combinatorial therapies that dismantle stromal immunosuppression while preserving the integrity of the therapeutic immune response.

From a clinical perspective, the immune–fibroblast axis represents an actionable layer of the tumor ecosystem that can be therapeutically leveraged to convert immune-excluded tumors into immunologically accessible lesions and to extend the durability of ICT. Rather than pursuing indiscriminate CAF depletion—which risks compromising tissue integrity and immune homeostasis—emerging strategies increasingly emphasize selective rewiring of stromal–immune communication. Conceptually, these approaches fall into several complementary categories: (i) attenuating fibroblast programs that enforce immune exclusion and dysfunctional antigen presentation by disrupting key upstream cues and downstream signaling hubs (e.g., TGF-β–driven matrix programs or inflammatory IL-1/STAT-related circuits); (ii) dismantling stromal–myeloid barriers by interrupting CAF-mediated recruitment and polarization of suppressive myeloid populations; and (iii) promoting immune-supportive fibroblast states that facilitate lymphocyte infiltration, intratumoral organization, and TLS maturation through chemokine and niche-factor reprogramming. Importantly, these interventions are likely to be most effective when integrated with ICT in a temporally informed manner, guided by biomarkers that report stromal state, immune accessibility, and therapy-induced remodeling. As longitudinal single-cell and spatial profiling becomes increasingly feasible in clinical settings, it should enable patient stratification based on stromal–immune circuitry and support rational, mechanism-driven combination regimens targeting the immune–fibroblast axis to improve response rates and prevent acquired resistance.

Ultimately, deciphering the regulatory principles that govern CAF plasticity and immune communication will redefine how we conceptualize—and therapeutically manipulate—the tumor microenvironment. Transforming CAFs from suppressive architects into cooperative partners of antitumor immunity may unlock the next generation of durable and broadly effective immunotherapies.
