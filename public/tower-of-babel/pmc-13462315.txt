Differential Neutrophil Responses to Smooth Titanium: A Double‐Edged Sword for the Abutment‐Gingival Interface (An In Vitro Study)
Authors: Tian Xu, Tulio Fernandez-Medina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe role of immunity in the failure of implantable devices has been historically overlooked in implant dentistry. This study investigates how neutrophils and blood plasma proteins interacting with titanium (Ti) surfaces influence the early inflammatory response.MethodsThree distinct Ti surfaces (Ti‐320, Ti‐1200, and Ti‐4000) with varying roughness were prepared by serial grinding. For subsequent functional assays, human neutrophils and plasma were isolated from healthy donor blood and characterized for phenotype confirmation. We then analyzed surface roughness, protein corona deposition, and the Ti‐protein interface. Neutrophil responses were assessed by quantifying the release of cytokines and enzymatic proteins, in addition to the extent of NETosis area and nuclear circularity.ResultsThe average roughness was 11.94 ± 1.99 nm (Ti‐320), 7.52 ± 1.21 nm (Ti‐1200), and 2.63 ± 0.49 nm (Ti‐4000). The isolated neutrophils exhibited high recovery (7.05 × 106 ± 3.45 × 106 cells/mL), viability (91.1 ± 2.3%), and purity (97.27 ± 2.06%). Protein deposition on all surfaces comprised medium molecular weight, low molecular weight (LMW), and high molecular weight (HMW) proteins. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy revealed a difference in protein structure between the surfaces, although this finding was not statistically significant. Functionally, Ti‐4000 surfaces exhibited significantly higher levels of proinflammatory cytokines (IL‐1β, IL‐6, TNF‐α, and CXCL‐10), elastase, and myeloperoxidase (MPO). Conversely, Ti‐320 surfaces promoted higher levels of the anti‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐4 and IL‐10.ConclusionsThis in vitro study demonstrates that Ti surface properties are associated with distinct protein corona compositions that promote a proinflammatory response on Ti‐4000 surfaces and a more favorable, anti‐inflammatory profile on Ti‐320 surfaces (reduced elastase and NETosis). These findings suggest surface modifications can guide neutrophil responses toward a less proinflammatory state, though further in vivo research is needed to confirm these results.

1. Introduction

Dental implants are a common treatment in modern dentistry [1]. While osseointegration is crucial for long‐term stability, achieving stable soft tissue integration around the abutment is challenging [2–4]. Titanium (Ti) is recognized as an active biological substrate that influences host immune responses, rather than being inert [5]. Despite high osseointegration success rates [6], peri‐implant infections affect up to 38% of patients within 5 years, leading to a 17% failure rate [7], primarily due to poor soft tissue integration [8]. While historical implant design has prioritized surface modifications to prevent bacterial colonization [9], a critical knowledge gap exists regarding the direct effects of these surfaces on the host’s immune response. Upon implantation, the Ti surface immediately adsorbs blood plasma proteins, forming a protein corona that acts as the initial signaling layer for host cells [10]. Ti is recognized not as an inert material but as a dynamic biological substrate that actively influences host immune responses [11]. Understanding how a biomaterial’s surface properties alter the composition of this protein corona is crucial, as this in turn modulates the behavior of key immune cells like neutrophils.

Upon implantation, the biomaterial surface is immediately exposed to blood plasma, which leads to the formation of a protein corona via the Vroman effect [12]. This corona, which includes both a “hard” and a transient “soft” layer [13], can be altered by customizing surface properties to modulate immune responses [14]. Plasma contains growth factors [15] and anti‐inflammatory cytokines [16, 17] like TGF‐β, which can induce a proangiogenic and anti‐inflammatory neutrophil behavior [18]. Neutrophils are the first cells recruited to the implant site, initiating matrix formation and inflammatory responses that are critical for tissue integration [19, 20]. Despite their short lifespan [21], recent evidence indicates that neutrophils exhibit significant functional plasticity, allowing them to modulate their phenotype and adopt context‐dependent functional states. This dynamic behavior enables them to transition from a proinflammatory role to a proresolving one, which is central to orchestrating a balanced immune response that facilitates healing rather than causing tissue damage [22, 23], promotes angiogenesis [24–26], and contributes to inflammation resolution through mechanisms like NETosis [27–29].

The transmucosal abutment interface is a critical biological zone where implant surface properties remain in direct, sustained contact with the peri‐implant soft tissue. Unlike the osseointegrated fixture, the abutment is continuously exposed to the oral environment and the cellular constituents of the gingival sulcus, among which neutrophils are the primary innate immune effectors [30]. Disruption of the soft tissue seal at this interface triggers the progression from healthy peri‐implant tissue to peri‐implant mucositis and, ultimately, peri‐implantitis. These conditions carry a reported global prevalence of ~43% and 22%, respectively [31]. Because neutrophil activation at the implant surface can drive a proinflammatory microenvironment—characterized by elastase release, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and NETosis—understanding how surface topography modulates these responses carries direct translational significance. Designing surfaces that bias neutrophil behavior toward an attenuated proinflammatory state may reduce the chronic, low‐grade inflammation driving tissue breakdown, offering a rational strategy to prevent peri‐implant disease.

Neutrophils are active producers of a broad range of soluble mediators—including proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines (IL‐1β, TNF‐α, IL‐6, and CXCL‐10) and anti‐inflammatory markers (IL‐4 and IL‐10)—that collectively shape the early immune environment at the implant surface [20, 32]. Although comprehensive cytokine profiling captures a wider secretome landscape, targeting two mechanistically direct and clinically relevant functional endpoints—elastase release and NETosis—provides a precise characterization of surface‐driven neutrophil behavior at the abutment interface [33, 34].

Building upon these findings, this study addresses the knowledge gap by examining the direct link between biomaterial surface properties, protein corona formation, and neutrophil behavior. We hypothesize that distinct protein‐biomaterial interactions will influence neutrophil phenotype, polarizing these cells toward a less proinflammatory state that promotes a prohealing microenvironment. Therefore, the primary aim of this research is to elucidate how the adsorption of blood plasma proteins on varying Ti surfaces influences neutrophil inflammatory responses, thereby providing crucial insights for optimizing implant abutment design and enhancing long‐term clinical success.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Ti Surface Fabrication

Ti disks (#TI‐453605 99.5% 15 mm ø) were provided by Nilaco Corporation and generated as previously described [35, 36]. Surfaces were generated by grinding with wet silicon‐carbide (SiC) paper (Struers) at a grinding speed of 200 rev/min on a rotating grind wheel (Mataserv). Samples were subsequently categorized into three distinct roughness groups. The Ti‐320 surface group was sequentially ground with 320 (#40400008), 800 (#40400010), 1200 (#40400012), 2400 (#40400024), 4000 (#40400025), and finally 320‐grit SiC paper. The Ti‐1200 surface group underwent sequential grinding with 320, 800, 1200, 2400, 4000, and ultimately 1200‐grit SiC paper. The Ti‐4000 surface group was prepared by sequential grinding with 320, 800, 1200, 2400, and 4000‐grit SiC paper. Following the grinding procedure, all samples were subjected to sonication in water for two 5‐min cycles, followed by a 15‐min sonication in 70% alcohol, and then air‐dried at room temperature (RT).

2.2. Ti Surface Characterization

An atomic force microscopy (AFM‐Bruker Dimension ICON XR) was employed to investigate the morphology of the three distinct Ti surfaces, with the scanning position confirmed under an optical microscope. A ScanAsyst‐in‐air probe, featuring a spring constant of 0.4 N/m, was utilized and landed on the sample surfaces. Raw AFM images were subsequently analyzed using Gwyddion free and open‐source software (Gwyddion) for both 2D and 3D structural analysis. For characterizing surface roughness, parameters including roughness average (Ra), root mean square roughness (Rq), and average maximum height of the profile (Rz) were obtained by making horizontal strokes on the images within Gwyddion, combined with data processing in Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). Surface texture graphs were measured using the same method. Two technical replicates were used for characterization per roughness group (n = 6).

2.3. Human Whole Blood

Experimental procedures complied with the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research (2007) and were approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee (Ethics ID: #2021/HE000391). Fresh whole blood samples from healthy donors were supplied by Australian Red Cross Lifeblood under Agreement #21‐05QLD‐20 to isolate total blood plasma and neutrophils. For downstream applications, flow cytometry was performed using samples from independent donors (n = 3), while functional assays—specifically elastase release and NETosis assessments—utilized a separate cohort of independent donors (n = 10). Prior to investigator receipt, Lifeblood deidentified all samples, supplying only age and blood type metadata. Given that these samples comprised surplus clinical material, specific individual informed consent was waived under institutional protocols.

2.3.1. Total Blood Plasma Isolation

Whole blood, collected and gently stirred to prevent coagulation and ensure homogeneity, was transferred into 50 mL Falcon tubes (#0045021, Sarstedt). These tubes were then centrifuged (Eppendorf 5810R/S‐4‐104 rotor—radius: 18.7 cm) for 15 min at 2000 × g to yield the three characteristic layers: plasma, buffy coat, and red blood cells (Supporting Information 1: Figure S1A). The resulting supernatant (~30 mL) was carefully collected with a micropipette, avoiding turbulence and buffy coat aspiration. Samples were subsequently double‐filtered using 0.22 µm‐pore‐size membranes (Millex‐GP) to exclude cells typically found in blood plasma, transferred into fresh 10 mL tubes, and then frozen at −80°C until further use.

2.3.2. Total Protein Quantification

Protein quantification was performed using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay (Thermo Scientific). Briefly, 25 µL of plasma protein was mixed with 200 µL of BCA working reagent and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Absorbance was then measured at 562 nm using a spectrophotometer (Infinite M200 Pro).

2.4. Neutrophil Isolation

Neutrophils were isolated from human whole blood using a MACSxpress Whole Blood Human Neutrophil Isolation Kit (Cat#130‐104‐434, Miltenyi Biotec). Eight milliliter of whole blood was pipetted onto 4 mL of isolation mix and incubated at RT for 5 min on a tube rotator (MACSmixTM REF#130‐090‐753, Miltenyi Biotec) at 12 rpm. Samples were then incubated under a magnetic field separator (MACSxpress REF#130‐098‐308, Miltenyi) for 15 min at RT. The isolated neutrophil fraction was subsequently transferred into a fresh tube for cell counting (Supporting Information 1: Figure S1B).

2.4.1. Characterization of Isolated Neutrophils by Flow Cytometry

Isolated cells were stained following manufacturer protocols (Bio‐legend Australia), with prior antibody titration performed for each stain to determine the appropriate staining index. Single‐stained controls for compensation, including unstained and heat‐killed cells for Zombie Aqua (ZA), CD14, CD15, CD16, CD45, and CD193, were included for acquisition (Supporting Information 2: Figure S2). An antibody cocktail for multiple staining was prepared by adding the following human antibodies to a stain buffer (FBS‐Biosciences): ZA Fixable Viability Kit; CD16, APC, REAfinity, Clone REA423; CD193 (CCR3), FITC, REAfinity, Clone REA57; CD14, PerCP‐Vio 700, REAfinity, Clone REA599; CD45, VioBlue, REAfinity, Clone REA747; and CD15, PE, Clone VIMC. Gating controls, detailed dilutions, brand, and catalog numbers are provided in Supporting Information 3: Figure S3A,B. Prior to staining, red blood cells were lysed, and samples were washed with 1× phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min. Cell counting was performed using trypan blue (1:2 dilution) with a hemocytometer. For the ZA single‐color control, 0.4 million cells per tube (53 µL) were heat‐killed at 55°C in a water bath for 5 min and then stained with 100 µL of 1:1000 diluted ZA in 1× PBS for 15 min at RT, followed by a 3‐min spin down at 10,000 rpm. Subsequently, 100 µL of staining solution (25 µL antibody cocktail and 75 µL staining buffer) was pipetted onto cells and incubated for 30 min in the dark at 4°C, along with single staining controls. Samples were then washed twice with 2 mL of 1× PBS and spun down for 5 min at 500 × g. Acquisition was performed on a BD LSR Fortessa Cell Analyzer (BD Biosciences, New Jersey, USA), and data (FCS files) were analyzed using FlowJo 10.6.1 (Becton, Dickinson and Company). For the gating strategy, a forward scatter (FSC) versus side scatter (SSC) plot was used to exclude debris. A FSC height (FSC‐H) versus FSC area (FSC‐A) density plot was applied to exclude doublets, followed by assessment of ZA staining for viability. From there, CD45+ cells were gated to detect leukocytes; CD193− and low CD14+ expressions were used to exclude eosinophil and basophil populations. Final gating for CD15+ and CD16+ identified granulocyte neutrophils. Results were represented by positive staining on mean and standard deviation from three different biological replicates.

2.5. Ti and Blood Plasma Interface Characterization

2.5.1. Protein Dynamic Binding by Electrophoresis

Protein dynamic binding from plasma was analyzed on three distinct Ti surfaces using three technical replicates from three biological replicates (n = 9) per concentration. Protein adsorption on the Ti surfaces was determined by immersing the disks in 200 µL of double‐filtered plasma within 48‐well cell culture plates (Ref#3548, Life Sciences) and incubating at 37°C for 1 h. Nonspecifically adsorbed “soft corona” proteins were then removed by three rinses with PBS (Lot#2189349, Life Technologies Limited). Subsequently, 200 µL of lysis buffer containing 2% SDS (sodium dodecyl sulfate, Sigma–Aldrich) and supplemented with a 2% protease inhibitor cocktail (P8340, Merck) was added to each well. Plates were maintained on ice under shaking incubation (ZWYC‐290A, Labwit Scientific) at 800 rpm for 30, 60, 120, and 180 min to collect the adhered “hard corona” proteins. Protein concentrations of each supernatant were evaluated using a BCA protein assay reagent (Thermo Scientific/Pierce), with data read at 562 nm using a spectrophotometer (Infinite M200 Pro). The supernatants collected at the four time points were further analyzed by SDS‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE). Samples were reduced by adding β‐mercaptoethanol (Lot#BCBK8223V, Bio‐Rad Laboratories) to Laemmli sample buffer (Cat#161073, Bio‐Rad Laboratories), denatured at 100°C for 10 min, and cooled to RT. All samples were loaded onto 4%–20% Mini‐PROTEAN TGX gels (Cat#4561086, Bio‐Rad Laboratories) and run in Tris/Glycine/SDS buffer (Cat#1610772, Bio‐Rad Laboratories) at 100 V using a PowerPac HV High‐Voltage Power Supply (Cat#1645056, Bio‐Rad Laboratories) for 60 min. Precision Plus Protein Marker (Cat#1610376, Bio‐Rad Laboratories) served as a standard. Protein components were visualized by staining with an Instant Coomassie Brilliant Blue solution (Lot#GR3420847‐1, Abcam). Gels were immediately scanned using a ChemiDoc MP system (Cat#12003154, Bio‐Rad Laboratories) with optimized exposure for intensity band setting. The obtained images were analyzed for optical density using the “Analysing Electrophoretic Gel” plugin in ImageJ. Data from plot profiles were expressed as a percentage by calculating the areas of peaks corresponding to different bands. The percentages of three molecular weight categories of proteins (high molecular weight (HMW: >100 kDa), middle molecular weight (MMW: <100 kDa and >37 kDa), and low molecular weight (LMW: <37 kDa) were plotted against time to visualize the adsorption–desorption events on the different Ti surfaces (Supporting Information 4: Figure S4A–C).

2.5.2. Protein Adsorption on Ti Surface Spectroscopy (Fourier Transform Infrared [FTIR])

Protein surface adsorption was analyzed by FTIR spectroscopy (Nicolet 5700). Ti sheets were incubated in 1 mL of total plasma for 60 min. Three biological replicates per roughness group were assayed using two technical replicates (n = 3 for each roughness). Following incubation, the samples were transferred to a plate and dried in a fume hood. FTIR spectroscopy was then used to verify the presence of different blood fractions on the Ti sheets. Spectra were acquired by collecting 32 scans over a range of 400–4000 cm−1 at a resolution of 8 cm−1.

2.6. Neutrophil Activity on Plasma/Ti Surface Interface

2.6.1. Cell Seeding

Isolated neutrophils were counted using trypan blue (Lot#1832964, Sigma–Aldrich) and seeded into a 24‐well plate (Ref#3524, Life Sciences) at a density of 200,000 cells/cm2 per well (n = 6 per variable). Complete media was prepared using RPMI buffer (Cat#11875093, Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 5% double‐filtered plasma. For control groups, 1000 ng/mL lipopolysaccharides (LPS, Lot#2254154, Thermo Fisher) was added to complete media per well as a positive control, while 2.5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Lot#BCCC1351, Sigma–Aldrich) in complete media served as a negative control. The plate was incubated for 8 h at 37°C, after which the supernatant media were harvested, and attached cells were lysed with 0.05% Triton X‐10. Additionally, neutrophils were seeded directly onto the three different Ti surfaces at a density of 200,000 cells/cm2 per sample (n = 6 per variable) in 24‐well plates and incubated at 37°C for 15 min, followed by the addition of complete media. After an 8‐h incubation, the supernatant media were harvested, and attached cells were lysed with 0.05% Triton X‐10 (Lot#SLBV4122, Sigma–Aldrich).

2.6.2. Elastase and Myeloperoxidase (MPO) Neutrophil Activity

The secretion of MPO (ab119605) and neutrophil elastase (ab270204, Abcam) proteins in conditioned media (CM) was quantified using respective ELISA kits according to manufacturer protocols. For MPO, 50 µL of standards or samples was added in duplicate and incubated at RT for 90 min. After discarding the well contents, 50 µL of diluted biotin antibody was added in duplicate and incubated at RT for 1 h. Wells were then aspirated and washed three times with 100 µL of 0.01 M PBS per well, with each wash buffer remaining for 1 min. Subsequently, 50 µL of diluted avidin‐biotin‐peroxidase complex was added in duplicate and incubated at RT for 30 min. Following this, the wells were washed as previously described. Finally, plates were incubated for color development for 30 min at RT before adding 45 µL of the TMB liquid substrate, followed by 50 µL of the TMB stop solution per well. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer (Infinite M200 Pro, Tecan Group). For elastase, 50 µL of standards or samples was added in duplicate, followed by 50 µL of diluted antibody cocktail, and incubated at RT for 60 min. After incubation, well contents were aspirated and washed three times with 350 µL of Wash Buffer PT per well, with each wash buffer remaining for 10 s. Plates were then incubated for color development for 15 min at RT before adding 50 µL of TMB liquid substrate, followed by 50 µL of stop solution per well. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using the same spectrophotometer. Results for both assays were normalized by total protein content per sample (pg/mL per µg of protein).

2.6.3. Neutrophil Inflammatory Cytokine and Chemokine Production (ELISA)

To evaluate neutrophil inflammatory cytokine and chemokine production, the secretion of proinflammatory (IL‐1β and IL‐6), chemokine (CXCL‐10), and anti‐inflammatory (TNF‐α, IL‐4, and IL‐10) proteins was measured by ELISA. Prior to ELISA, protein quantification was performed using a BCA protein assay (Thermo Scientific/Pierce, Australia): 25 µL of extracted protein was mixed with 200 µL of BCA working reagent and incubated at 37°C for 30 min, with absorbance read at 562 nm using a spectrophotometer. The total protein content from the sample was assessed to subsequently normalize the cytokine/chemokine content measured by ELISA. ELISA was conducted to quantify IL‐10 (#900‐M21), IL‐4 (#900‐M14), IL‐1β (#900‐TM95), CXCL‐10 (#900‐M39), TNF‐α (#900‐TM25), and IL‐6 (#900‐TM16) (Peprotech‐Lonza) according to manufacturer protocols, utilizing TMB (#900‐T00) and ABTS (#900‐K00) ELISA buffer kits. Plates were read using a spectrophotometer at different wavelengths, as specified by the manufacturer’s protocol. Final values were normalized to the protein concentration and expressed in pg/mL per µg of protein per sample.

2.6.4. Neutrophil Morphology on Surfaces

Neutrophils were seeded onto Ti sheets at a density of 200,000 cells/cm2 per well for each sample group. After 2 and 8 h of incubation, cells were fixed sequentially with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Cat#08168, Sigma–Aldrich) for 1 h, followed by 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Cat#1607224274, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at RT. A subset of experimental samples was qualitatively investigated using a scanning electron microscope (FESEM; JEOL‐JSM 7001F) with a 10 kV acceleration voltage, after being coated with gold for 150 s prior to visualization. For fluorescent analysis, DNA was stained with 0.2% 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI, Cat#D1306, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and visualized using a Nikon C2 laser scanning confocal microscope (NIS‐Elements confocal microscope) with an excitation peak at 359 nm and an emission peak at 457 nm. NETosis was characterized by measuring the area and circularity of the stained DNA from the acquired images. Images were processed with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health) to calculate the area of DAPI staining and circularity (defined as 4π(area/perimeter2), where a value of 1.0 indicates a perfect circle and values approaching 0.0 indicate increasing elongation). Neutrophils on Ti surfaces (Ti‐320, Ti‐1200, and Ti‐4000) were utilized exclusively for SEM and DAPI staining. As most morphological changes in neutrophils, indicative of NETosis, occurred after 2 h postincubation, data extraction for NETosis area and circularity was performed at the final 8‐h time point.

2.7. Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Software v.7 (GraphPad Software). To compare the three distinct Ti surfaces (Ti‐320, Ti‐1200, and Ti‐4000), a one‐way ANOVA was performed for each of the six outcome variables, following the confirmation of assumptions of normality via the Shapiro–Wilk test and equal variances via the Brown–Forsythe test. The variables analyzed included surface roughness parameters (Ra, Rq, and Rz), protein plasma adsorption (electrophoresis and FTIR), neutrophil cytokine secretion (IL‐1β, TNF‐α, IL‐6, CXCL10, IL‐4, and IL‐10), enzymatic protein secretion (elastase and MPO), NETosis area, and nuclear circularity.

To ensure reproducibility and capture biological variability, statistical analyses clearly distinguished between biological and technical replicates. Flow cytometry experiments were performed using independent biological replicates derived from n = 3 healthy donors. For the downstream functional endpoints—specifically elastase release and NETosis assessments—the sample size was increased to capture wider donor variance, utilizing independent biological replicates from n = 10 donors. For all assays, technical replicates were performed in triplicate per independent donor to minimize intraexperimental error, and the resulting data points were averaged to generate a single value representing each true biological replicate. To account for multiple comparisons across these six outcome variables, a Bonferroni correction was applied, adjusting the nominal significance level to p < 0.0083 (0.05/6). A Tukey’s post hoc test was subsequently used to identify specific group differences if the overall ANOVA was significant. Pearson correlation was performed to analyze potential relationships between donor characteristics (age vs. total protein and age vs. cell/mL). For gender‐based differences in cell recovery and total protein content, a Mann–Whitney test was used due to the nonparametric nature of the data. All normally distributed data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while nonparametric data are expressed as median ± interquartile range (IQR).

3. Results

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was employed to compare the roughness of three distinct Ti surfaces (Ti‐320, Ti‐1200, and Ti‐4000) at both 1 µm and 10 µm scan lengths. The primary AFM results are presented as representative two‐ and three‐dimensional surface topographies (Figure 1A), along with the corresponding surface profiles (Figure 1B). Quantitative evaluation of surface topography was conducted using the roughness average (Ra), root mean square roughness (Rq), and average maximum height of the profile (Rz) values (Figure 1C), which revealed that surface roughness was consistently higher at the 10 µm scan length across all samples. Qualitatively, the Ti‐320 and Ti‐1200 surfaces exhibited rough and regular topographies, contrasting with the more uniform distribution of peaks and valleys observed on the Ti‐4000 surface. Specifically, the average Ra values at 1 µm were 1.11 ± 0.25 nm (Ti‐320), 0.87 ± 0.11 nm (Ti‐1200), and 0.35 ± 0.06 nm (Ti‐4000), increasing to 11.94 ± 1.99 nm, 7.52 ± 1.21 nm, and 2.63 ± 0.49 nm, respectively, at 10 µm. These differences in Ra values were statistically significant ( ∗∗∗ p < 0.008) between the Ti‐320 and Ti‐4000 groups at both scan lengths. Similarly, the average Rq values for Ti‐320, Ti‐1200, and Ti‐4000 were 14.54 ± 3.74 nm, 9.62 ± 1.51 nm, and 3.61 ± 0.74 nm, respectively, at the 10 µm scan length, with a statistically significant difference observed between the Ti‐320 and Ti‐4000 groups ( ∗∗∗ p < 0.008). For Rz, values at 1 µm were 5.63 ± 1.08 nm (Ti‐320), 5.66 ± 1.13 nm (Ti‐1200), and 2.32 ± 0.37 nm (Ti‐4000), while at 10 µm, they reached 56.19 ± 13.28 nm, 45.56 ± 6.43 nm, and 14.87 ± 2.57 nm. Statistically significant differences were observed between Ti‐320 and Ti‐4000 at the 10 µm scan length ( ∗∗∗ p < 0.008). These data confirm that grinding treatments successfully modified the Ti surfaces, producing distinct surface morphologies and, generally, higher average surface roughness, with a clear hierarchy of roughness: Ti‐320 > Ti‐1200 > Ti‐4000.

Throughout this manuscript, each surface group is identified by a color code as follows: blue (Ti‐4000), green (Ti‐1200), and red (Ti‐320).

Ten biological replicates (donors) were included in this study. Due to confidentiality guidelines, only gender, age, and blood type were provided by the Australian Red Cross. A Mann–Whitney U test was conducted to assess for gender‐based differences in neutrophil count and protein content, as these data were determined to be nonparametric. The median neutrophil count for females was 7.4 × 106 cells/mL (IQR: 2.9 × 106), which was not statistically different from the median count for males at 6.7 × 106 cells/mL (IQR: 3.9 × 106) (p = 0.76). Similarly, the median protein content was 36.4 µg/µL (IQR: 7.3) for females and 32.3 µg/µL (IQR: 11.9) for males, with no significant difference observed (p = 0.52). Age, which was normally distributed, is reported as mean ± standard deviation, with females having a mean age of 43.8 ± 23.2 years and males having a mean age of 59.6 ± 11.6 years. Blood type was presented as categorical data, showing frequencies of A+ (2) and O+ (3) for females and O+ (3), A+ (1), and B+ (1) for males. Pearson correlation analyses revealed no statistically significant relationship between participant age and either total protein concentration (r = 0.16, p = 0.65) or cell density (r = 0.22, p = 0.52). These data indicate that participant age did not significantly influence either biological parameter within the study (Supporting Information 5: Table S1).

These donor characteristics are important considerations for future point‐of‐care tissue engineering strategies utilizing neutrophils isolated from peripheral blood (Supporting Information 5: Table S1). Neutrophil phenotype was characterized by flow cytometry (Supporting Information 2: Figure S2A), with samples demonstrating successful viability (91.1% ± 2.3%) (Supporting Information 2: Figure S2B) postisolation from peripheral blood. Cell phenotyping revealed a predominant CD45+ staining (97.27 ± 2.06) and negligible CD193− expression (1.11 ± 0.3), coupled with low CD14+ expression (33.37 ± 4.42). Finally, gating for CD15+ and CD16+ markers showed prevalent immunostaining (98.53 ± 0.91) for all samples included in this study (Supporting Information 2: Figure S2C). Unstained and single‐color controls for CD14, CD15, CD16, CD45, CD193, and ZA for heat‐killed cells confirmed negative staining and proper compensation during acquisition (Supporting Information 3: Figure S3). Overall, the magnetic field separation method successfully preserved the viability of untouched neutrophils isolated from peripheral blood. Furthermore, the proposed gating strategy (CD45+/CD14Low/CD193−/CD15+/CD16+) proved reliable for identifying neutrophils from the bloodstream, effectively separating leukocytes (CD45+) with subsequent selection of myeloid cells (CD15+/CD16+) that were negative for eosinophil/basophil markers (CD193−) and displayed low monocyte/macrophage staining (CD14Low) [37–41].

Protein plasma adsorption was investigated on three distinct Ti surfaces (Ti‐320, Ti‐1200, and Ti‐4000) using electrophoresis and FTIR spectroscopy to quantify protein deposition via BCA and electrophoresis assays at 30, 60, 120, and 180 min (Figure 2A). The study elucidated the dynamic adsorption–desorption interchange of blood plasma proteins, which form a “protein corona” that provides the biological identity of the surface and influences cellular response in situ. Overall protein deposition (Figure 2B) on all Ti surfaces was primarily composed of MMW proteins (≈70%), followed by LMW (≈20%) and HMW (≈10%) groups. The superiority of MMW proteins was statistically significant compared to both HMW and LMW groups (p < 0.0001), regardless of the surface. A similar pattern was observed on Ti‐320 and Ti‐1200 when comparing LMW and MMW groups, where LMW percentages were higher than HMW (p < 0.008). Over time (Figure 2C), the MMW protein concentration generally increased, while LMW and HMW proteins decreased across all surfaces. At 30 min postincubation, a significant increase in the MMW group was observed on Ti‐4000 compared to Ti‐320 (p < 0.0001) and on Ti‐1200 compared to Ti‐320 (p < 0.008). This trend of Ti‐4000 showing higher MMW values persisted at 180 min, although this difference was not statistically significant at later time points. Within the LMW and HMW categories, Ti‐320 exhibited a higher percentage at 30 min compared to Ti‐1200 and Ti‐4000. This nonsignificant difference in LMW was also observed at 180 min, with both Ti‐320 and Ti‐1200 having higher percentages than Ti‐4000. These results confirm that the characteristics of the Ti surface determine the dynamic adsorption–desorption interchange of blood plasma proteins over time. FTIR spectroscopy was utilized to investigate potential conformational changes of adsorbed plasma proteins on the different Ti surfaces. Amide A (~3400 cm−1), amide I (~1645 cm−1), amide II (~1550 cm−1), and amide III (~1300 cm−1) spectral signatures were evaluated for protein secondary structure analysis. The FTIR spectra revealed characteristic patterns of protein deposition, represented by peaks at these approximate wavenumbers on samples exposed to blood plasma (Figure 3A). Although Ti‐1200 showed higher values in the amide A region (3280.89 ± 2.76 cm−1), this difference was not statistically significant compared to Ti‐320 (3279.1 ± 1.24 cm−1) and Ti‐4000 (3278.14 ± 2.35 cm−1) (Figure 3B). A similar pattern was observed for the amide I and amide II regions, where Ti‐320 demonstrated superiority (amide I: 1637.15 ± 0.12 cm−1; amide II: 1541.19 ± 0.19 cm−1), followed by Ti‐1200 (amide I: 1636.38 ± 1.33 cm−1; amide II: 1540.79 ± 0.24 cm−1) and Ti‐4000 (amide I: 1635.93 ± 1.57 cm−1; amide II: 1540.34 ± 1.85 cm−1). Furthermore, the amide III region showed a clear, albeit not statistically significant, trend between a higher Ti‐320 value (1395.62 ± 0.26 cm−1) and a lower Ti‐4000 value (1392.48 ± 1.78 cm−1). These results suggest that while overall FTIR spectra were similar across the experimental surfaces, the specific Ti surface characteristics play a crucial role in determining the nature and structural conformations of the adsorbed proteins similar to a previous report [42].

These findings demonstrate that Ti surface characteristics significantly modulate the inflammatory effects of neutrophils, with smoother surfaces promoting a proinflammatory response and Ti‐320 surfaces promoting an anti‐inflammatory one.

A smaller number of cells retained distinguishable morphology, extending cytoplasmic prolongations between adjacent cells and exhibiting areas of NETosis ranging from individually distributed structures on Ti‐320 to wider areas of cell aggregates on Ti‐4000. 4. Discussion

This study characterized protein adsorption and associated neutrophil behavior on three distinct Ti surfaces varying in roughness, utilizing plasma proteins and neutrophils isolated from human peripheral blood. Employing standard characterization methods, we demonstrated the creation of unique protein corona/Ti interfaces capable of directly influencing neutrophil responses and, consequently, altering the release of proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, enzymatic proteins, and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). These findings underscore the clinical significance of the host’s early neutrophil response as a determinant of long‐term dental implant success [43]; a dysregulated reaction at the transmucosal interface compromises the biological seal [44], initiating a cascade that leads to peri‐implant mucositis, irreversible bone resorption, and eventual implant failure [45].

It must be acknowledged that all Ti surfaces tested in this study were nanosmooth (Ra: 2.6–12 nm); clinical implant surfaces utilized in osseointegration contexts typically feature a much higher roughness (Ra > 1 µm). Therefore, the neutrophil behavior and protein corona dynamics observed on these ultrasmooth experimental substrates cannot be directly extrapolated to clinically rough implant topographies. Future studies incorporating rougher, clinically representative surfaces are required to validate whether these surface‐driven trends persist in a more complex geometric microenvironment.

Using differential serial grinding, we fabricated three Ti surfaces that exhibited distinct roughness values and a uniform distribution of their topographical features. Notably, the Ti‐320 surface displayed deeper valleys and a higher surface area compared to the other textures. AFM data unequivocally confirmed that SiC treatments yielded diverse surface morphologies, with treatments increasing the average surface roughness, as quantitatively supported by the Ra, Rq, and Rz values; Ti‐320 consistently showed the most significant differences across these parameters, followed by Ti‐1200 and Ti‐4000. These findings align with Albrektsson’s classification, where Ti‐320 was identified as the roughest surface, followed by Ti‐1200 and Ti‐4000 [46]. Analysis of neutrophil and plasma protein characteristics from various donors indicated that age and gender did not significantly determine cell yield recovery or protein content, consistent with studies showing no association between age and neutrophil number [47, 48] and no significant differences in plasma protein content [49]. While existing evidence does not definitively exclude interactions involving donor age or gender, our data suggest their limited influence in the context of this study. Consistent with prior evidence of low CD14 expression on neutrophils [41, 50], our finding validates the specific cellular phenotype identified as mediating the interactions with the protein corona and subsequent inflammatory responses. This validation opens opportunities for the point‐of‐care transition of the technique, where “untouched neutrophils” were successfully isolated from peripheral blood by magnetic bead exclusion. This method demonstrated high cell recovery (female 6.7 × 106 ± 3.9 × 106−male 7.4 × 106 ± 2.9 × 106 cells/mL), excellent viability (91.1% ± 2.3 %), and high purity (97.27% ± 2.06 %).

Dynamic protein adsorption studies on Ti surfaces showed time‐dependent shifts in protein corona composition [51]. Notably, Ti‐320 surfaces exhibited enhanced, though not statistically significant, adsorption of LMW proteins. This phenomenon was evident at the initial time points and persisted, albeit to a lesser degree, at the experiment’s conclusion. This aligns with different findings [52] that rough Ti surfaces with higher radiality and steeper slopes enhance protein adsorption, a process also influenced by surface hydrophilicity. Complementary FTIR analysis indicated a higher adsorption intensity in the amide I, II, and III stretching vibrations on the Ti‐320 protein corona. In FTIR spectroscopy, the amide regions are a crucial tool for analyzing the secondary structure and conformational changes of proteins, as they correspond to specific vibrational modes of the peptide backbone (C( = O)—NH—) that are highly sensitive to hydrogen bonding [53]. The amide I band (1700–1600 cm−1) is the most intense and widely utilized, as it is dominated by the C = O stretching vibration whose frequency directly correlates with structures such as α‐helices and β‐sheets [54]. Complementary information is provided by the amide II (1600–1500 cm−1) and amide III (1350–1200 cm−1) bands, which arise from coupled N–H bending and C–N stretching vibrations. While amide II is susceptible to water interference, the amide III region is valuable for confirming secondary structure estimations [55]. While our findings were not statistically significant, they are consistent with prior reports [56]. No significant differences were observed, especially in the amide I and II regions, which are crucial for peptide and protein adsorption [57]. However, the lack of detailed hydrophilic and hydrocarbon analyses during the experimental pretreatment of Ti surfaces may have influenced the observed protein adsorption patterns [52]. However, while FTIR spectroscopy effectively identifies changes in protein secondary structure, it cannot establish definitive causation regarding the presence of specific adsorbed proteins without complementary LC‐MS/MS proteomics data [58]. Furthermore, surface wettability (contact angle) and X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) compositional data were not collected. Because these physicochemical parameters critically influence early protein adsorption dynamics, their omission represents a clear limitation in the comprehensive characterization of biomaterial surfaces.

Assessment of cytokine, chemokine, and enzymatic protein secretion showed that smooth surfaces (Ti‐4000) enhanced chemokine secretion by neutrophils, known to activate and attract inflammatory cells [59, 60]. Similar to studies suggesting a lower proinflammatory response of neutrophils on hydrophilic Ti surfaces [61], our findings demonstrated that neutrophils interacting with the preadsorbed protein corona on the Ti‐320 surface produced the lowest levels of proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and enzymes. These reductions were statistically significant for IL‐1β, CXCL10, and elastase when compared among the experimental groups. Although definitive conclusions cannot be drawn solely from the current in vitro experiment, the clinical implications of these preliminary results challenge the perspective of smooth Ti surfaces as the gold standard for implant transmucosal elements [62]. Furthermore, the elevated, though not statistically significant, release of IL‐10 on Ti‐320 suggests that surface roughness is critical in polarizing neutrophil activation toward a less proinflammatory functional state, which is distinct from its proinflammatory counterpart and characterized by an anti‐inflammatory, prohealing role through the secretion of mediators such as IL‐10 and IL‐4 [32]. Our data consistently underscore that neutrophil polarization is associated with by surface roughness and the unique protein corona formed by peripheral blood plasma proteins. A limitation, however, is our inability to precisely identify the specific proteins mediating this mechanism.

Interestingly, a reduction in NETosis‐associated nuclear morphological changes was observed on Ti‐320 surfaces. In contrast, smoother surfaces promoted wider cellular aggregating areas of NETosis‐associated nuclear morphological changes (despite no change in circularity). This phenomenon could be attributed to the enhanced stimulation of initial cell adhesion by Ti‐320 surfaces, resulting in larger adhesion areas and more distinct cell morphology, as previously described [63]. Platelets and IgG, adsorbed within the plasma protein layer on smooth material surfaces, are proposed to be key inflammatory triggers, leading to enhanced ROS production and subsequent NETosis‐associated nuclear morphological changes [64]. The reduction of NETosis‐associated nuclear morphological changes is widely recognized to contribute to healing and promote positive osseointegration outcomes by decreasing the proinflammatory polarization of macrophages interacting with NETs [61, 65, 66].

These findings reveal a critical surface‐engineering trade‐off for transmucosal components. While smooth topographies like Ti‐320 suppressed neutrophil elastase releases, supporting clinical strategies to minimize roughness for biofilm and inflammatory control, they simultaneously expanded the spatial footprint of NETosis‐associated nuclear changes. Because larger NETs can amplify localized cytotoxicity, this architectural divergence indicates that surface topography dictates the qualitative character of the innate immune response rather than its magnitude alone. Consequently, no single roughness parameter simultaneously mitigates all neutrophil‐driven pathways. This underscores a central clinical paradox: The smooth surfaces required to reduce proinflammatory neutrophil activation at the soft‐tissue barrier directly conflict with the rougher topographies mandatory for endosseous osseointegration. Resolving this design challenge requires zonal, dual‐surface optimization strategies that structurally decouple the implant component, minimizing coronal proteolytic and NET‐mediated tissue injury while maximizing apical bone anchorage.

5. Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, from a material standpoint, surface characterization was restricted to nanosmooth Ti substrates (roughness average [R a ]: 2.6–12 nm); consequently, these findings may not generalize to clinically rough implant surfaces (R a > 1 µm). Additionally, because surface wettability (contact angle) and XPS data were not collected, the collective influence of alcohol pretreatment and surface roughness on Ti surface energy—which critically dictates the initial formation and ultimate biological effect of the protein corona [67]—remains incompletely understood. Second, the elucidation of the protein corona itself faced analytical constraints. The use of anticoagulated blood for plasma protein isolation may have altered native protein‐protein and cell‐protein interactions [68]. Furthermore, while FTIR spectroscopy effectively identified changes in protein secondary structure, it was utilized without complementary LC‐MS/MS proteomics data, preventing the definitive identification of specific adsorbed proteins or the resolution of participant‐specific variations in conformational states within the complex corona landscape. Third, regarding the experimental design and biological model, evaluations were restricted to a single 8‐h time point, precluding the assessment of dynamic temporal kinetics. The in vitro model was also limited to isolated neutrophils; this omits the complex, multicellular interactions involving macrophages, platelets, complement factors, fibroblasts, and the epithelium that characterize the in vivo peri‐implant microenvironment. Additionally, NETosis was quantified solely via nuclear morphology changes without verification of definitive molecular markers such as citrullinated histone H3 (Cit‐H3), MPO‐DNA complexes, or peptidylarginine deiminase 4 (PAD4). Finally, a priori power analysis was omitted, as the final sample size was ultimately constrained by the availability of healthy donors.

6. Conclusions

Based on the findings and limitations of this in vitro study, we conclude the following:1.Ti surfaces are associated with distinct compositions of the adsorbed protein corona, which, in turn, is associated with the subsequent biological response in human neutrophils.2.Ti‐4000 surfaces enhance the release of proinflammatory mediators, including IL‐1β, CXCL10, and elastase.3.Ti‐320 surfaces reduced both elastase release and NETosis‐associated nuclear changes from neutrophils when compared to the Ti‐1200 and Ti‐4000 surfaces.4.Further in vivo research is needed to confirm these preliminary findings and to more comprehensively understand the biological events that lead to tissue integration.
