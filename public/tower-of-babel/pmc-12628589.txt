The impact of sleep quality on body weight among young adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Fatema Rahaman, Sabkat Kamal, Rajib Ul Islam, Labina Taher, Fariha Noshin, Tabeen Taher, Mst Sabrina Moonajilin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveObesity and poor sleep quality are rising concerns in health discussions today, especially among young adults. Young adults may be more vulnerable to weight gain due to academic pressures and lifestyle choices, making this research particularly relevant. The goal of this study is to examine how body mass index (BMI) relates to various aspects of sleep quality in young adults in Bangladesh. An online cross-sectional survey was conducted involving 445 participants aged 18 to 25, who were mostly students. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was used to assess sleep quality.ResultsMore than half of our participants (56.4%) reported having poor sleep quality. Those with poor sleep had a higher average BMI of 24.11 kg/m2 compared to 22.36 kg/m2 for participants with good sleep. There was a notable inverse relationship between BMI and sleep duration, while correlations were positive with sleep latency, sleep disturbances, and the overall PSQI score. Individuals who were overweight and obese faced significantly greater odds of poor sleep quality. Additionally, being female and sleeping less than 6 h a night were also significant predictors. This study reveals a troubling prevalence of poor sleep quality among young adults in Bangladesh and is associated with higher BMI, which has important implications for public health. Integrating sleep health promotion within obesity prevention initiatives could foster healthier futures for young adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07544-1.Keywords: sleep quality, overweight/obesity, BMI, young adults, health promotion

Introduction

In recent years, obesity and sleep disturbances have emerged as pressing public health challenges, particularly among young adults. As individuals navigate busy lifestyles filled with academic pressures and social obligations, it has become clear that these two issues are closely intertwined [39, 41, 45]. Alarmingly, more than a billion people worldwide are classified as overweight/obese, with this problem becoming increasingly prevalent in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) like Bangladesh [16, 47]. Rapid socio-economic changes in these regions can disrupt traditional lifestyles and contribute to unhealthy habits [23, 24]. At the same time, poor sleep quality affects the amount and effectiveness of health and has been recognised as a significant risk factor for numerous health issues, including heart disease, diabetes, and even early death. Research from high-income countries (HICs) suggests a complicated, two-way relationship between excess weight and sleep disturbances [6, 18]. Factors like hormones, disrupted body clocks, and lifestyle choices all play a role [1, 40]. Overweight/obesity accounted for 3.69% of all healthcare expenditures and around 0.13% of Bangladesh’s GDP [22, 48]. The growing number of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) linked to overweight/obesity in Bangladesh puts a heavy load on the healthcare system. This includes not only the cost of medical care but also indirect costs like lost work and lower productivity [3, 13]. However, less is known about how these dynamics play out in South Asian countries, where different cultural and economic factors could influence both how well people sleep and how much they weigh.

Young adults, especially, are at a heightened risk for issues with sleep. They often juggle demanding academic schedules, extensive screen time, and the stress of transitioning into adulthood, all of which can lead to insufficient sleep [7, 10, 25]. These difficulties are made worse by infrastructure and socioeconomic limitations. Many young adults in LMICs live in crowded apartments with little privacy, noisy surroundings, erratic power supplies, and no climate control, all of which can make it difficult to get a good night's sleep [44, 49]. Competitive tests for admission and a lack of job opportunities frequently increase academic pressure, resulting in long-term stress and unstable study habits [14, 15, 33]. Expectations from family and culture, especially for women, can also restrict leisure and personal time, which worsens the quality of sleep [35, 42, 43]. Additionally, in LMIC contexts, late-night screen use has increased due to rapid urbanisation and the widespread use of low-cost mobile devices, frequently without corresponding public awareness of healthy sleep practices [21, 32]. These interrelated factors increase the risk of downstream health issues in addition to affecting sleep. Studies consistently show that poor sleep quality is linked to a higher BMI, a common measure of obesity [5]. These connections raise concerns, not just for physical health, but also for mental well-being and academic success.

While research from HICs has confirmed these links, there is still a significant gap in data from regions like Bangladesh. Understanding how sleep and weight relate in this context is critical for developing effective health interventions. As urbanisation increases and traditional lifestyles evolve in Bangladesh, young adults are struggling with many health issues, particularly obesity [36, 37]. In Bangladesh, urbanisation, changes in eating habits, and less physical activity have led to a quick epidemiological transition, and obesity is now affecting more young adults. There is a rapid change in the country's diet, and more people in cities and with higher education are becoming overweight/obese [26, 34]. At the same time, young adults in school are dealing with more stressors that could affect their sleep quality. Young adults are at risk for unique health problems [8, 27]. For example, high academic demands, irregular schedules, long periods of screen time, and eating more energy-dense foods can all make it harder to sleep and cause weight gain [7, 33]. Cultural norms and gender roles may also affect how people behave when it comes to their health. For example, women often have more trouble getting enough exercise and sleep [2, 35, 42]. Even though these new worries are coming up, sleep health is still mostly left out of national NCD prevention plans, and there is not much research on how it relates to obesity in Bangladeshi populations.

It is essential to identify vulnerable groups and modifiable behaviours by jointly examining both sleep duration and sleep quality in relation to BMI. To guide this inquiry, we use the socio-ecological model (SEM), which recognises that health behaviours, including sleep and weight regulation, are shaped by multiple interacting levels: individual, interpersonal, institutional, community, and societal [12, 20]. This framework offers a comprehensive lens to assess not only individual sleep habits but also the systemic and cultural factors that may contribute to obesity risks among young Bangladeshi adults. This focus addresses a significant research gap, as most current evidence derives from high-income countries and underrepresents LMIC contexts. This study might help pave the way for healthier futures for young people in this rapidly changing landscape by better understanding the factors that influence sleep and its relationship with body weight.

Methods

Study design and participants

An online cross-sectional study was conducted to examine the relationship between sleep quality and body weight among young adults in Bangladesh. This study opted for an online recruitment method, which allowed us to reach a diverse group of young adults, a critical population that often faces unique challenges related to both sleep and weight. Young adults (18 to 25 years old) and a willingness to complete an online survey in English were prerequisites for inclusion. Participants with known sleep disorders or chronic illnesses were excluded. A target sample size of at least 384 was calculated using Cochran’s formula for proportions (with 5% margin of error and a 95% confidence level), assuming a prevalence of poor sleep quality of approximately 50% based on previous literature. To account for potential non-responses or incomplete submissions, the sample was increased by 20%, resulting in a final sample of 445 participants.

Data Collection Tools.

A semi-structured online questionnaire was distributed through digital platforms (such as Facebook Messenger and WhatsApp) to collect data between April and June 2022 (supplementary file). To assess sleep quality, this study used the PSQI. This tool is well-regarded for its ability to cover multiple facets of sleep, making it perfect for this study [9]. The PSQI helped to evaluate not just how long participants slept, but also the quality of that sleep and how rested they felt upon waking. The BMI of participants was calculated based on their self-reported height and weight. Participants were asked to report their current height and weight so a BMI could be calculated for each participant. Studies found that women self-report their height and weight with a high level of accuracy [28, 29]. The World Health Organisation (WHO) classifications were followed to categorise them as underweight, normal weight, overweight, or obese, providing a picture of how their BMI aligned with their sleep quality [47].

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 25. To dive deeper into the relationships between BMI, various sleep parameters, and demographic factors, this study used several statistical analyses. Independent t-tests were used to compare the average BMI between those who reported good sleep quality and those who did not. Pearson's correlation was used to explore the relationships between sleep duration, sleep disturbances, and BMI scores. Finally, binary logistic regression was applied to identify which predictors, like gender, sleep duration, and BMI, were most significantly linked to poor sleep quality. This study aimed to identify the complex interplay between sleep and body weight in this specific group of young adults, ultimately shedding light on the challenges they face and the factors that influence their health. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

This study revealed some findings about the sleep quality and body weight of young adults in Bangladesh. The analysis included 445 participants, of whom 45.8% were female and 54.2% were male. Most participants (63.2%) were undergraduates, while a smaller percentage had graduate (27.6%) or postgraduate (2.7%) degrees. Most reported a family income of between 11,000 and 40,000 BDT per month (47.4%), followed by > 40,000 BDT (30.1%) and less than 10,000 BDT (13.5%). 14.6% were underweight, 54.2% were normal weight, 22.5% were overweight, and 8.8% were obese. Poor sleep quality was experienced by more than half of the sample (56.4%) (PSQI > 5). This suggests that sleep issues are quite prevalent in this group, raising important questions about their overall health and well-being (Table 1).

The results demonstrate that poor sleep quality is associated with a higher average BMI. Participants reporting poor sleep exhibited a mean BMI of 24.11 kg/m2, whereas those with better sleep had a mean BMI of 22.36 kg/m2. This difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001), indicating a substantial relationship at the group level (Table 2). Analysis of correlations between specific sleep parameters and BMI revealed that these associations were weak. The inverse correlation between BMI and sleep duration (r = –0.28) indicates that higher BMI is associated with shorter sleep duration, although the effect size is small. Positive correlations between BMI and sleep latency (r = 0.22) and between BMI and sleep disturbances (r = 0.31) were also weak, despite statistical significance. The strongest observed correlation was between BMI and the total PSQI score (r = 0.38), which remains within the weak-to-moderate range.

These results underscore that, although the associations are statistically significant and reflect consistent patterns within the sample, the effect sizes are small. Therefore, while sleep quality and BMI are related, the relationships are insufficient to imply direct or deterministic effects. These associations likely represent components of a broader set of factors, such as stress, dietary habits, physical activity, and socio-environmental conditions, that collectively influence both sleep and body weight. The logistic regression analysis strengthens the case for these associations, showing that overweight and obese participants had significantly higher odds of reporting poor sleep quality (OR = 1.80 and OR = 2.29, respectively), and that being female and reporting shorter sleep durations further increased the likelihood of poor sleep. These findings indicate that, although the correlations were weak, the overall model suggests meaningful patterns that warrant attention in health promotion and intervention design (Table 3).

Discussion

The results of this study offer crucial insights into the relationship between sleep quality and body weight among young adults in Bangladesh. This connection is particularly concerning because, as previous research indicates, poor sleep can lead to hormonal changes that affect appetite and metabolism, potentially creating a cycle of weight gain and worsening sleep issues [11, 18, 38]. A significant number of participants are grappling with poor sleep, which aligns with growing global concerns around sleep health. This high prevalence is particularly alarming, given that quality sleep is essential for overall physical and mental well-being. One of the standout findings is the association between poor sleep quality and elevated BMI. Inadequate sleep is associated with higher BMI, which suggests that sleep disturbances might be related to weight gain in this population.

Studies revealed that gender plays an important role in sleep quality, with female participants more likely to report poor sleep. This may reflect the societal pressures and stressors that young women face, such as balancing academic demands and family responsibilities. Enhancing awareness about these gender differences can help tailor interventions to meet the specific needs of young women, helping them improve both their sleep and overall health [4, 17, 31]. At the individual level, shorter sleep duration was inversely associated with BMI, whereas increased sleep latency and greater frequency of sleep disturbances were positively associated with BMI. At the interpersonal level, gender was identified as a significant predictor; women exhibited higher odds of poor sleep quality, which may be attributable to increased caregiving responsibilities and societal expectations regarding gender roles. This finding reiterates the importance of promoting healthy sleep habits among young adults, who often prioritize academics, work, and social commitments at the expense of sufficient rest. Encouraging students to make sleep a priority could yield significant benefits for their health and academic performance.

Studies revealed the potential interaction of institutional factors (e.g., academic workload, dorm environments), interpersonal influences (e.g., peer stress), and individual behaviours (e.g., sleep hygiene) to affect both sleep and weight outcomes [19, 30, 46]. The findings of the study are also consistent with previous findings. These findings highlight the necessity of multifaceted, integrated approaches to health promotion that address both more general structural and cultural factors as well as modifiable behaviours.

Under the direction of the SEM, this study shows a relationship between young adults' BMI and sleep quality, especially in the South Asian context. Understanding the intricate relationship between sleep and obesity requires an understanding of the SEM's premise that individual behaviours are influenced by interacting factors at several levels, including personal, interpersonal, institutional, and societal. Higher BMI is associated with poorer sleep quality, shorter sleep duration, and more disturbances. This relationship may be influenced by biological processes like altered circadian rhythms, impaired glucose metabolism, and dysregulation of leptin and ghrelin [11, 18, 38]. The findings at the individual level support the global evidence. Additionally, participants who do not get enough sleep might feel more exhausted, which could make them less active and more likely to eat foods high in energy. Sleep and eating patterns may be impacted at the interpersonal level by peer pressure and family expectations. Participants, particularly women, may experience additional psychological stress in cultures like Bangladesh, where family obligations and social expectations regarding academic achievement are high. This can lead to sleep disturbances and unhealthy coping mechanisms like emotional eating. The literature on gender differences in stress reactivity and sleep disorders is consistent with our finding that female participants were more likely to report poor sleep quality.

This study demonstrates that structural factors such as academic workload, gendered expectations, and urban environments, as well as cultural practices including late-night socialising and Ramadan routines, interact with individual sleep behaviours to influence obesity risk. This knowledge is instrumental in shaping targeted interventions that address both sleep and obesity, promoting healthier lifestyles. These context-specific insights contribute to the global literature by establishing that sleep functions as a socially embedded practice rather than solely a physiological determinant. Recognising this distinction is essential for the development of effective, culturally sensitive interventions in South Asia and similar contexts. Empowering young adults with education about the importance of sleep could lead to healthier lifestyles and improved health outcomes, laying a stronger foundation for their futures.

Conclusion

In summary, this study sheds light on a significant public health concern facing young adults in Bangladesh: the worrying relationship between poor sleep quality and higher body weight. Integrating sleep health into obesity prevention strategies has the potential to significantly improve public health practice in LMICs. National NCD prevention frameworks should formally include sleep health promotion. Universities are encouraged to establish institutional policies, such as wellness programs and awareness campaigns, that promote adequate sleep in conjunction with balanced diets and regular physical activity. Gender-sensitive and culturally tailored interventions are particularly necessary, given the multiple vulnerabilities faced by young Bangladeshi women. Reframing sleep health as a public health priority, rather than an individual lifestyle choice, may provide substantial benefits for reducing obesity and enhancing youth wellbeing.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, participants were primarily recruited through online platforms, and most respondents were urban students. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to rural populations or individuals outside higher education, where sleep patterns, dietary practices, and body weight often differ. Second, BMI was calculated using self-reported height and weight. Previous studies indicate that self-reported data are subject to recall bias and systematic misreporting, such as underestimating weight and overestimating height. This bias may result in an underestimation of BMI. Future research should incorporate objective measures, including anthropometric assessments, to enhance accuracy. Third, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences regarding the relationship between sleep and body weight. Although associations between poor sleep and higher BMI were observed, longitudinal approaches such as trajectory analysis or cohort follow-up studies are required to clarify the temporal and potentially bidirectional relationships between these variables. Growth curve modelling and trajectory analysis can clarify developmental patterns in sleep and BMI, helping determine whether poor sleep leads to weight gain or the reverse. Finally, the application of the SEM situated the findings within multiple levels of influence. However, only individual-level factors were directly measured, and interpretations at broader levels relied on contextual synthesis of participant narratives. Future research should incorporate mixed-methods designs to capture higher-level influences systematically. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the relationship between social and behavioural factors, sleep, and body weight among Bangladeshi youth. The findings underscore the need for more comprehensive and context-sensitive health promotion.
