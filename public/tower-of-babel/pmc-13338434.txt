Analyzing the perceived sensitivity of Murrah buffalo-based livestock production system toward changing climate using the Analytical Hierarchy Process
Authors: Ruchi Singh, Sanjit Maiti, Sanchita Garai, Mukesh Bhakat, Sujeet Kumar Jha, Anil Kumar Dixit, Anjali Aggarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change poses escalating threats to livestock production systems globally, with extreme weather events including intensified heat waves and cold spells increasingly compromising animal productivity, reproductive performance, and resource availability. The Murrah buffalo, a high-yielding dairy breed central to India’s national milk output, remains particularly vulnerable in Haryana’s Trans-Gangetic Plain, where climatic extremes are pronounced yet breed-specific sensitivity assessments are largely absent. This study employed the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP), a multi-criteria decision-making framework, to systematically quantify farmers’ perceived climate change sensitivity across the Murrah buffalo-based livestock production system. A total of 320 farmers were surveyed across eight agro-ecologically representative blocks in Haryana. Eight primary indicators and 35 sub-indicators were identified and prioritized through pairwise comparisons. Results revealed that type of farming system was the most critical sensitivity indicator (priority weight = 0.23), with the specialized crop-based farming system sub-indicator carrying the highest global priority (0.10). Reproductive performance (0.18) and feed availability (0.16) emerged as the next most sensitive indicators, followed by impacts on milk yield, conception rates, vector-borne disease prevalence, water resource availability, and agro biodiversity. These findings highlight the deeply interconnected nature of climate vulnerabilities within Murrah buffalo husbandry and underscore the urgency of transitioning toward diversified, climate-resilient farming models. Introduction

Climate change refers to long-term alterations in the mean values of climate variables such as rainfall and temperature, typically measured over periods exceeding 50 years. Globally, rising temperatures and increasing frequency of extreme events such as heat waves, droughts, and floods have emerged as the most significant consequences of climate change. These changes pose serious challenges to agriculture, forestry, and livestock systems, particularly in developing countries1 where livelihoods are highly dependent on climate-sensitive sectors. India reflects these global trends2. According to the India Meteorological Department (2018), the rate of temperature increase in the country is nearly identical to the global average. The Centre for Science and Environment, New Delhi, has reported that the annual mean temperature in India has risen by 1.2 °C since the beginning of the 21st century. Regional studies further highlight localized impacts; for instance, Haryana has experienced increases of about 1 °C in maximum temperature and 1.2 °C in minimum temperature3. Such climatic shifts intensify the vulnerability of rural communities and production systems.

At the same time, demographic pressures are mounting. The global population is projected to rise from 7.8 billion today to 9.2 billion by 20504, 5, accompanied by a continuous increase in demand for livestock products6. For resource-poor households in developing countries, livestock remains a critical source of food security and income. India, in particular, holds a unique position: it ranks first worldwide in buffalo numbers and milk production, with approximately 109.85 million buffalo contributing 210 million tons of milk annually7. Among these, the Murrah breed is especially significant, accounting for 44.39 percent8 of India’s buffalo population and being widely distributed across Asia, South America, and Europe9. Meeting the rising demand for milk requires an annual growth rate of 9.2% in production10. However, buffalo dairy systems face considerable challenges under climate change11, 12, 13. The breeding tracts of Murrah buffaloes are characterized by hot, dry summers with temperatures reaching 45 °C, and cold winters with occasional frost. Physiologically, buffaloes are more sensitive to heat stress due to their thick black skin, sparsely distributed sweat glands, and limited body hair14. These traits make them particularly vulnerable to climatic extremes, which can negatively affect their productive and reproductive performance.

Given this context, the present research focuses on analyzing the sensitivity of Murrah buffalo production systems to climate change. Sensitivity is conceptualized as the degree to which a system is negatively affected by climate-related stimuli15. It can be assessed both from farmers’ perceptions and from impacts on animal physiology and productivity. Since direct data on climate-induced performance losses are difficult to obtain, sensitivity is operationalized here as the perceived consequences of climate extremes on key productive and reproductive parameters16. To quantify these perceptions, the analytic hierarchy process (AHP) was applied, using identified components and indicators. However, despite India’s global leadership in buffalo production, limited studies have examined the perceived sensitivity of Murrah buffalo systems to climate change. Addressing this gap is essential to guide policymakers in designing robust adaptation strategies for the dairy sector.

Need and limitations of study

Climate change is amplifying the frequency and intensity of extreme events that pose significant risks to agriculture and livestock systems. India, home to the world’s largest buffalo population and the highest milk output, is particularly vulnerable to these impacts. Within this context, the Murrah buffalo a high-yielding and widely distributed dairy breed plays a pivotal role in sustaining the national milk economy. Yet, research examining its perceived sensitivity to climate change from the perspective of farmers remains limited. Such insights are essential for informing adaptation strategies and safeguarding food security.

Nevertheless, the study is constrained by several methodological challenges. The scarcity of long-term performance data necessitates reliance on farmer perceptions, which may introduce subjectivity. The geographic focus on specific breeding tracts, notably Haryana, restricts the generalizability of findings. Furthermore, the study’s timeframe may not adequately capture longer-term processes such as genetic improvement or adaptive management. Finally, while the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) offers a structured approach to assessing climate sensitivity, it may not fully encompass the multidimensional nature of climate impacts on livestock systems.

Materials and methods

Research area

The present study was conducted in purposively selected Haryana state in North India to analyze the sensitivity of the Murrah buffalo-based livestock production system in their breeding tract. Haryana was purposively chosen because it is the native and breeding tract of the Murrah buffalo, hosts the densest purebred population, and serves as a genetic resource base for India’s dairy sector. Its economic importance and authentic farmer practices make it the most scientifically relevant site for assessing climate-sensitivity perceptions for this breed. The state of Haryana comprises 22 districts, of which four districts namely; Hisar, Bhiwani, Jind, and Rohtak were selected because these districts have the maximum Murrah buffalo population. A farmer who had been rearing Murrah buffalo for at least 10 years and maintained a minimum herd size of four Murrah buffalo was selected as a respondent. Two blocks from each district and four villages from each block were selected randomly, yielding a total of 32 villages. Finally, 10 buffalo farmers from each village (n = 320) were drawn based on the aforementioned criteria.

Data sources and study design

The present study adopted a cross sectional diagnostic design based on farmers’ perceptions to prioritize sensitivity indicators of the Murrah buffalo based production system using the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP). Diagnostic research design was used to understand the multi-criterion decision-making to quantify the perceived sensitivity of climate change on the Murrah buffalo-based production system. The study was based on primary data. The personal interview method, observation method, and participatory rural appraisal (PRA) tools such as focused group discussion were used with the help of a pre-tested structured interview schedule to collect the primary data. The interview schedule consisted of questions on general information about respondents as well as selected independent and dependent variables. Respondents were contacted at their doorsteps and/or livestock farms or fields of crop production. The primary data collected from the farming community were tabulated and statistically analyzed to achieve the objectives of the present study. The study does not involve experimental manipulation and focuses on perception-based prioritization rather than causal inference.

Ethical statement

This study was conducted in accordance with ethical principles governing research with human participants. Prior to data collection, farmers were clearly briefed about the objectives, voluntary nature, and use of the study in the regional language (Haryanvi/Hindi). Informed consent to participate was obtained verbally from all respondents before the interview commenced. Participation was entirely voluntary; only farmers who willingly agreed to participate were included in the study. No personal identifiers were recorded in the data, ensuring participant anonymity and confidentiality. As this study involved a voluntary perception-based survey among adult participants with no experimental intervention, no clinical procedures, and no risk to participants, it qualifies for exemption from formal ethical committee review under institutional guidelines.

Furthermore, this research work was formally approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of ICAR–National Dairy Research Institute (NDRI), Karnal, vide approval letter reference number 4–11/Ph.D/IDS/2020/UO/NDRI/488 dated 18-06-2020.

Perceived sensitivity of Murrah buffalo-based livestock production system using analytic hierarchy process

The Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) is a structured, Multi criteria decision-making technique developed by Thomas L. Saaty (2008) for organizing and analyzing complex decisions, based on mathematics and psychology. It aids the decision-maker to set priorities and make the best decision. By reducing complex decisions to a series of pair-wise comparisons, and then synthesizing the results, the AHP helps to capture both subjective and objective aspects of a decision. Saaty (2008) proposed a nine-point scale (see Table 1) for pairwise comparison (Eq. 1) of different adaptation strategies and components.

While the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) has been widely applied in agriculture and climate adaptation research, its application to breed-specific livestock sensitivity assessment remains limited. Existing AHP-based studies in climate-livestock contexts have largely examined generic or mixed livestock systems without differentiating breed-level responses, thereby masking the distinct production, reproduction, and management vulnerabilities that characterize high-value breeds. The present study uniquely addresses this gap by applying AHP specifically to the Murrah buffalo, India’s most economically significant dairy breed and the cornerstone of North India’s milk economy within its native breeding tract in Haryana.

This breed-specific focus represents a substantive methodological departure from prior AHP-based studies that treat livestock systems as homogeneous entities. By integrating farmers’ experiential and observational knowledge through structured pairwise comparisons, the study operationalizes farmer perception as a scientifically valid source of evidence, complementing but not substituting empirical physiological research. The AHP framework, applied at the component and sub-component level across eight dimensions of climate sensitivity, generates a hierarchical weighting of indicators that is directly actionable for policy, extension services, and breed conservation programs. Furthermore, the study advances scientific understanding in two specific ways: (i) it demonstrates the utility of AHP for capturing multidimensional climate sensitivity at the breed level, filling a gap in the AHP literature that has not previously targeted purebred buffalo systems; and (ii) it generates prioritized, farmer-validated evidence that can inform targeted adaptation strategies and genetic resource management beyond generic livestock assessments. The Murrah buffalo-specific model developed here provides a replicable template for other high-value indigenous breeds across South Asia, thereby extending the scientific and applied scope of AHP in climate-livestock research. Identification and selection of indicators

The indicators and sub-indicators of sensitivity were identified through a systematic three stage process. First, an extensive review of published literature on climate change impacts on livestock production systems17, 18, 19, 20, 21 was undertaken. Second, expert consultations were conducted with subject matter specialists in dairy extension, animal physiology, livestock management and agricultural economics to ensure content relevance. The third, a pilot survey was carried out with Murrah buffalo farmers to assess field level relevance and comprehensibility. Many components were identified to see the sensitivity of the Murrah buffalo-based livestock production system. Components such as quantity and quality of feeds and forage crops22, 23, animal growth and milk production24, 25, water availability26, 27, livestock diseases4, reproduction and biodiversity27 loss were the major areas of livestock production system that were affected by climate change. Based on convergence across these three sources, eight major indicators and thirty five sub-indicators (Fig. 2) were finalized, grouped and classified in a manner, so all the aspects of the concerned system were covered.

Development and validation of questionnaire

The present study focused on Murrah Buffalo based production system specific in their breeding tract. Since existing tools did not capture region-specific information across the multiple interrelated components required for farmers’ perceived sensitivity toward climate change impacts on the Murrah buffalo system, a context-specific instrument was developed. Questionnaire items were generated for each sub-indicator, and content validity was examined through an expert panel. A pre-testing or pilot study was subsequently performed. The questionnaire was originally developed in English, translated into Hindi (the local language), and validated through back-translation and pre-testing to ensure semantic equivalence. Interviews were conducted in Haryanvi (regional dialect) to ensure better comprehension. The complete questionnaire has been provided as supplementary material (Appendix S1).

Informed consent to participate in the study was obtained verbally from all respondents prior to data collection, after explaining the purpose of study in the regional language. This is a perception based study, hence Helsinki guidelines was not applicable for the present study.

Results and discussion

The present study employed the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) to derive priority weights for sensitivity index calculation. This approach was chosen because the study’s diagnostic design relies on farmer perceptions, not measured physiological data. AHP is specifically designed to incorporate stakeholder judgments through pairwise comparisons, capturing both subjective knowledge and systematic decision-making priorities. This makes AHP particularly suitable for perception-based research contexts where long-term quantitative performance data are unavailable or scarce. The consistency ratio (CR < 0.10) across all components confirms the internal consistency and reliability of farmers’ pairwise judgments. The priority weights generated represent the relative perceived importance of sensitivity indicators and should be interpreted as reflecting farmers’ knowledge-based judgments rather than statistically significant inter-variable differences.

The type of farming system (priority weight = 0.23) emerged as the most sensitive component, followed by reproductive performance, feed and fodder availability and utilization, productive performance, water, disease incidence and susceptibility, soil, and biodiversity. This finding is best understood in the context of the socioeconomic and agro-ecological realities of Haryana. The study area lies in the Trans-Gangetic Plains Region, where the majority of farming households depend on integrated crop-livestock farming as their primary livelihood. Farmers in this region are acutely aware that extreme climatic events particularly heat waves and erratic monsoon patterns simultaneously affect both crop production and livestock performance, making the farming system as a whole the most climate-exposed unit of their livelihoods. This integrated dependency explains why farmers assigned the highest sensitivity weight to the farming system type: disruption of one component (crops) cascades directly into the other (livestock feed, income, and herd maintenance), amplifying perceived overall vulnerability. This finding differs from an earlier study28, 29, 30 in which feed and fodder availability and utilization were perceived as the most sensitive component. This divergence is attributable to differences in the farming system context: the earlier study31 appears to have been conducted in predominantly pastoral or agro-pastoral settings, whereas Haryana farmers operate within a mixed crop-livestock system where farming system type integrates multiple pathways of climate risk. These contextual differences highlight that farmers’ perceptions of climate sensitivity are not universal but are shaped by local livelihood structures, resource access, and historical experience with climatic variability.

Prioritization of sensitivity sub-indicators across eight components of Murrah buffalo-based livestock production system

Across all eight sensitivity components, 35 sub-indicators were identified. The following sub-sections present the AHP-derived priority weights and interpret the perceptual priorities of Murrah buffalo farmers in light of their socioeconomic context, farm management practices, and agro-ecological conditions.

Productive performance

Among five sub-indicators, average daily milk yield, lactation length, peak yield, dry period, and stage of lactation, average daily milk yield (weight = 0.35) was perceived as the most climate-sensitive, followed by lactation length (0.23), peak yield (0.21), stage of lactation (0.17), and dry period (0.04). The high priority assigned to average daily milk yield reflects the direct economic calculus of Murrah buffalo farmers: daily milk sale is the primary source of cash income for most households, making any observable reduction in daily yield immediately and tangibly felt. Farmers who have managed Murrah buffalo over 10 or more years have directly observed that high-temperature events during summer cause rapid and perceptible declines in daily output, reinforcing their perception of this indicator’s sensitivity. In contrast, dry period and stage of lactation received lower weights, likely because farmers perceive these as more biologically fixed and less susceptible to direct climatic intervention within a production cycle. The alignment of these farmer perceptions with the well-documented physiological effects of heat stress32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37 on high-producing dairy breeds validates the perceptual framework: farmers are effectively integrating observable production outcomes into their climate sensitivity assessments.

Reproductive performance

Conception rate (highest weight) and estrous cycle were identified as the most climate-sensitive reproductive indicators, followed by age at first calving, service period, and calving interval (CR = 0.065). Farmers’ prioritization of conception rate reflects their practical experience with the summer breeding season: reduced conception rates during hot months directly extend calving intervals, delay the onset of the next lactation, and reduce annual milk yield, all of which have immediate economic consequences. The perception that estrous cycle disturbances are highly sensitive further reflects farmers’ observations of weak or silent estrus during peak summer, making timely detection and insemination difficult. These perceptions align with documented hormonal and reproductive disruptions in buffaloes38, 39, 40 during high-temperature-humidity conditions, but importantly, they are grounded in farmers’ own experiential observations rather than clinical measurement. The discrepancy with a previous study31, 41, 42 that ranked calving interval as most sensitive may reflect differences in the economic orientation of farmers: calving interval is a strategic, longer-term indicator, whereas conception rate and estrous detection are operational, season-specific management challenges that Haryana farmers directly confront during summer breeding cycles.

Disease incidence and susceptibility

Vector-borne diseases (weight = 0.37) and common health ailments (0.36) were perceived as the most climate-sensitive disease sub-indicators, while metabolic diseases and epidemiological effects received lower weights. Farmers’ prioritization of vector-borne diseases is consistent with their direct, observable experience: seasonal increases in fly, tick, and mosquito populations during warmer and more humid periods are visibly apparent and temporally correlated with increased animal morbidity in their herds. This pattern is distinct from findings in coastal Odisha (Chilika region)28, where heat stress-related illness and mortality were perceived as most sensitive, a difference explicable by the contrasting climatic and geographic contexts: Haryana faces semi-arid heat stress without the cyclone and flood risks of coastal Odisha. This comparison illustrates how local environmental exposure shapes farmers’ perceptual hierarchies of disease risk under changing climate.

Farming system type

Specialized crop-based farming (weight = 0.47) was perceived as the most climate-sensitive farming system type, followed by specialized livestock-based farming (0.35). This result reflects the structural economic reality of Haryana agriculture: crop production is the dominant land use and income source, and farmers recognize that climate-driven crop failures simultaneously reduce fodder availability, household income for purchasing concentrate feeds, and the overall resource base for maintaining the buffalo herd. The high sensitivity assigned to specialized crop-based farming underscores farmers’ perception33 that crop failure is a primary pathway through which climate change indirectly affects their livestock system a linkage that purely livestock-centric studies may underestimate.

Soil

Water holding capacity (weight = 0.40) and organic matter (0.35) were identified as the most climate-sensitive soil sub-indicators. Farmers’ emphasis on water holding capacity reflects their direct experience with erratic rainfall events: soils with poor water retention43 fail to support fodder crop cultivation during dry spells, directly reducing on-farm feed availability for their buffaloes. The prioritization of organic matter is consistent with farmers’ observed experience of declining soil fertility over successive seasons of extreme weather, which reduces the productivity of fodder crops. These perceptions indicate that farmers understand climate sensitivity not just as a direct animal-level phenomenon, but as a cascading land-soil-crop-livestock system effect (Table 3).

Water

Availability of water resources (weight = 0.43) was perceived as the most climate-sensitive water sub-indicator, followed by quality of water, availability for irrigation, and availability for drinking. The strong emphasis on water resource availability44 reflects the agro-ecological vulnerability of Haryana to drought and groundwater depletion, both of which are exacerbated by erratic monsoon patterns. Farmers recognize that reduced water availability affects not only crop irrigation and buffalo drinking needs, but also the practice of wallowing45 a critical thermal comfort behavior for buffaloes in summer with consequences for milk production and reproductive performance. The relatively lower weight assigned to drinking water availability (compared to resource availability and quality) suggests that farmers currently consider direct animal drinking water supply as somewhat more manageable, while systemic water scarcity is perceived as a more fundamental and less controllable sensitivity.

Biodiversity

Fodder crop diversity (weight = 0.37) and large ruminant population (0.30) were perceived as the most climate-sensitive biodiversity sub-indicators. The prioritization of fodder crop diversity is directly linked to feed security: farmers with long experience in mixed farming recognize that reduction in fodder crop variety, whether due to extreme heat, drought, or flood narrows dietary options for their buffaloes and increases vulnerability to feed shortages. Asia is reported to experience higher biodiversity loss than Africa46. The high sensitivity assigned to large ruminant populations reflects farmers’ awareness of herd attrition during severe climatic events and the consequent loss of both productive and genetic assets.

Feed and fodder availability and utilization

Feed and fodder intake was perceived as the most sensitive sub-indicator, followed by feed and fodder availability, grazing duration during summer, and feeding pattern. The primacy of feed intake over mere availability reflects a sophisticated perceptual understanding among farmers: they have observed that even when fodder is physically available, buffaloes voluntarily reduce intake29, 47, 48 during high-temperature periods, a behavioral response that directly diminishes production. This perceptual finding aligns with documented research49 on voluntary feed reduction in heat-stressed ruminants, but the importance here is that Haryana farmers have independently arrived at this understanding through years of practical observation, without access to formal research literature. Their prioritization therefore validates the use of farmer perception as a knowledge system that complements scientific evidence in climate sensitivity assessment.

Conclusion

As the greenhouse gas emissions increase year by year and cause global warming, they are imposing severe pressure on maintaining current levels of agricultural (crops, livestock, fisheries, etc.) production. It is a fact that the productivity of an organism (plant, animal, fish, etc.) is different as its production system will change. Every production system has a differential level of sensitivity against climate change, due to which the productivity is uneven. In making a resilient livestock production system, the identification of various indicators was found necessary to ensure the food security of the ever-increasing global population. The present study identifies and analyzes the indicators of sensitivity of the Murrah buffalo-based livestock production system as perceived by the farming community in the state of Haryana, using AHP, a multi-criteria decision-making tool. It was found that the farmers perceived different indicators of sensitivity in the production system namely, the types of farming system, productive performance, reproductive performance, feed and fodder availability and utilization, soil, water, disease incidence and susceptibility, and biodiversity toward the changing climate. While making decisions for sensitivity indicators, farmers were mainly concerned about the farming system, followed by reproductive performance, and feed and fodder availability and utilization. Specialized crop-based farming sub-indicators were perceived as highly sensitive indicators by the majority of the farmers. Therefore, it would be beneficial for the rural community to engage in livestock-based farming with more diversified crops and animal breeds. Overall, the current study has made a significant contribution to advancing knowledge about the perceived sensitivity of the Murrah buffalo-based livestock production system toward changing climate using the analytical hierarchy process. More comprehensive studies are suggested in the future to leverage the findings gained in the current study.
