Room-temperature spin control in WS2 enabled by chiral seeded lead-free chiral perovskites
Authors: Zuoming Jin, Peilin Wang, Bing Chen, Tianpeng Li, Zhiguo Zhang, Qiyi Fang, Jia Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Because of the temperature-induced intervalley scattering, transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) typically exhibit diminished valley polarization at room temperature, limiting their application in valleytronic devices. To address this issue, combining chiral perovskites with monolayer TMDCs provides a promising route for room-temperature valley polarization control. Here, the enhanced chiral-induced spin selectivity (CISS) effect in R-(+)- and S-(−)-α-methylbenzylamine (MBA)–based tin perovskites is substantially strengthened by introducing chiral seed layers. Leveraging spin-valley coupling, spin extraction at the chiral perovskite interface improves the valley polarization of monolayer tungsten disulfide (WS2) to reach as high as 14.9% at room temperature. The enhanced CISS effect enables more efficient spin-selective charge transfer, thereby amplifying the carrier population imbalance between the K and K′ valleys in monolayer WS2. This asymmetry is further improved by the high-quality chiral perovskite substrates, which help suppress valley carrier recombination. Our study offers a promising approach by combining chiral seed–induced enhancement of the CISS effect in chiral perovskite with monolayer TMDCs to achieve excellent performance of valley manipulation.

INTRODUCTION

In momentum space, a valley refers to an energy extremum that provides carriers with a distinct degree of freedom beyond charge and spin, defined as the valley degree of freedom (1–4). In the post-Moore era, following the rise of spintronics, the valley degree of freedom has attracted rising interest in the field of electronics research, due to its potential for lower energy dissipation and enhanced information security (5–7). Two-dimensional (2D) materials, such as monolayer transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs), have emerged as ideal platforms for investigating valleytronics (8–11). Their electronic band structure features two inequivalent valleys at the K/K′ points of the hexagonal Brillouin zone, which are coupled by strong spin-valley locking (12). This spin-valley coupling dictates optical selection rules, where σ+/σ− circularly polarized light (CPL) selectively excites carriers in distinct valleys (13). The resulting valley polarization can be read out by left- and right-handed circularly polarized photoluminescence (PL) (14). These optical excitation and detection processes resemble write and read operations, highlighting the promise of valleytronics for next-generation information processing. Nevertheless, because of intervalley scattering at room temperature, the valley polarization is suppressed, and most reported valley polarization effects have been achieved under cryogenic conditions (15, 16), which restricts the practical application of valleytronic devices. Thus, exploring effective strategies to realize room-temperature valley polarization has become an urgent and crucial task (17, 18).

Chiral perovskites have emerged as promising candidates for room-temperature valley polarization modulation, owing to their intrinsic chiral-induced spin selectivity (CISS) effect (19, 20). Most studies to date have focused on lead-based chiral perovskites, in which the helical configuration or chiral organic cations induce a spin-filtering effect through asymmetric electronic interactions. Specifically, spin-up/spin-down electrons encounter different potential barriers during transport, resulting in a pronounced imbalance of carrier population between the two spin states (12, 19, 21, 22). This spin-filtering effect constitutes the fundamental mechanism for room-temperature valley polarization. Compared to lead-based chiral perovskites, tin-based chiral perovskites have inherent advantages in terms of higher chiral transfer efficiency and stronger chiroptical responses (23). In addition, tin-based chiral perovskites also exhibit higher conductivity, which could facilitate the spin-charge transport (24, 25). Lu et al. experimentally demonstrated a high spin polarization of 94% in tin-based perovskite (23), validating the potential of tin-based perovskite in constructing spin-filter devices. Beyond material performance, tin-based chiral perovskites offer an environmentally friendly alternative to their lead-based counterparts by eliminating the inherent toxicity of lead while retaining comparable chiroptical and spin-selective properties. Although the stability of tin-based perovskites has long been a concern, recent advances in materials engineering have enabled operational lifetimes approaching those of lead-based systems (26–28). In this work, the use of chiral seeding, together with the partial protection provided by the overlying TMDC layer and standard encapsulation strategies, further improves the environmental robustness of the system. To harness these chiral spin functionalities in practical valleytronic applications, constructing tin-based perovskite/TMDC heterostructures represents a promising strategy, which provides effective control over spin and valley degrees of freedom through interfacial synergy effects. Leveraging their strong CISS effect, chiral 2D perovskites act as efficient spin filters to enable selective spin injection into monolayer TMDCs, achieving robust valley polarization over 10% under linearly polarized light excitation without external magnetic fields, and a spin injection efficiency of up to 78% at 200 K (29). When integrated with chiral perovskites, robust room-temperature valley polarization in MoS2 has been achieved via the CISS effect in a hybrid heterostructure, enabling spin-filtered charge transfer without external fields (12). In this heterostructure, the chiral perovskite layer acts as an efficient spin filter, leveraging its pronounced CISS effect to generate highly spin polarized carriers without requiring an external magnetic field. Upon injection into the TMDC layer, these carriers selectively occupy specific valleys (K or K′ valleys) due to the inherent spin-valley locking properties of TMDC materials, resulting in pronounced valley polarization at room temperature. This effective spin-to-valley conversion can be achieved without the need for cryogenic temperatures or external magnetic fields, establishing an effective platform for developing advanced valleytronic devices.

In this work, we constructed a heterostructure of pure 2D tin-based chiral perovskite film as a chiral substrate for monolayer WS2, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This heterostructure achieved a valley degree of polarization (DOP) as high as 14.9% under CPL excitation at room temperature. Although high valley polarization (30 to 80%) has been reported at room temperature, it typically relies on external stimuli (30–33). In contrast, our system achieves a DOP of as high as 14.9% without magnetic fields or resonant excitation, indicating that efficient valley polarization can be realized through intrinsic material and interfacial design. The introduction of the chiral seeds enhances interfacial carrier transport and amplifies the CISS effect, thus promoting a more pronounced population imbalance between the K and K′ valleys in WS2. Our work provides valuable insights into the role of CISS effect in modulating room-temperature valley polarization and reinforces the potential of chiral perovskite–based heterostructures for practical valleytronic applications.

RESULTS

Chiral activity enhancement mechanism of chiral perovskites

The lattice architecture in chiral perovskites dominates the chirality transfer. A highly ordered lattice facilitates the efficient propagation of chirality, whereas a disordered lattice might hamper this process due to defect or impurity scattering (34, 35). In this work, we applied chiral R-(+)-/S-(−)-α-methylbenzylamine (R/S-MBA) as the chiral spacers to fabricate the Ruddlesden-Popper (RP) phase 2D chiral perovskite (fig. S1). In addition, the R/S-MBA was selected as chiral seeds precoated before depositing chiral perovskite films. In this approach, chiral molecules R/S-MBA served as seeds for inducing chiral perovskite crystallization, providing guidelines for chiral orientation and minimizing high-energy nucleation sites in the initial configuration (36). On the other hand, this seeding layer plays an important role in releasing residual stresses from crystallization, effectively enhancing the long-term stability of chiral perovskite films (37, 38). The annealing time also plays a critical role in obtaining high-quality films (39–41). We found that a shorter annealing time (∼30 s) produced better crystalline perovskite films (fig. S2). To explore the effect of the chiral seeds on the quality of chiral perovskites, we first spin coated a layer of R/S-MBA on the substrate and immediately spin coated the chiral perovskite precursor (fig. S3). For brevity, we designate the original chiral perovskite film as R/SPVK and the chiral perovskite film with chiral seeds as seeded-R/SPVK. The x-ray diffraction (XRD) results (fig. S4A) confirm the high crystallinity and structural integrity of 2D layered chiral perovskite films with the introduction of chiral seeds, indicating that the introduction of chiral seeds does not cause any noticeable degradation in film quality. To verify the effect of chiral seeds on suppressing film defects, we characterized the PL spectra of the films, as shown in fig. S4B. After introducing chiral seeds, the PL intensity of the seeded-SPVK decreased markedly with the defect suppression. This phenomenon can be attributed to the passivation of the defects at the bottom of the perovskite film by chiral seeds, thereby improving the overall film quality (26, 42). The chiral seeds located at the bottom of the film play a critical role in regulating residual stress during the crystallization growth of the film, owing to the coordinated capability of R/S-MBA seeds. To investigate the residual tensile strain of the SPVK and seeded-SPVK, we used the depth-dependent grazing incidence x-ray diffraction (GIXRD) technique (43), as demonstrated in fig. S5 (A and B). The diffraction peaks of both chiral perovskite films gradually shift toward larger angles as the incident angles increases, indicating the presence of compressive stress. Moreover, the extent of the shift was gradually attenuated in the seeded-SPVK films compared to the film without chiral seeds. We obtained the residual stresses of the two films by the 2θ-sin2ψ method (44), by linearly fitting the 2θ value as a function of sin2ψ. The compressive stress of the film without seeds is 34.77 MPa, whereas the introduction of seeds reduces the stress of the film to 26.72 MPa (fig. S5C). The decreased compressive stress is favorable for obtaining high-quality chiral perovskite films with fewer defects for efficient chirality transfer (45). The surface morphology of the SPVK films was characterized by atomic force microscopy (AFM). The introduction of the chiral seed layer results in only minor changes in surface morphology, with surface roughness values of 5.11 and 4.21 nm, respectively (fig. S6, A and B), suggesting that it acts as a relatively gentle modification.

Chiral optical properties of films

The ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectra of R/SPVK and seeded-R/SPVK show similar absorption edges at around 460 nm, with a slightly decreased absorption intensity observed in the seeded films (fig. S7). We hypothesize that the introduction of the chiral seeds disrupts the long-range conjugated structure of the original chiral perovskite structure. To further understand the effect of chiral seeds on the chiral activity of the chiral perovskite lattice, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations on SPVK and seeded-SPVK. The optimized geometric structures of SPVK and seeded-SPVK are shown in Fig. 2 (A and B). The introduction of the chiral seeds will change the lattice of the host chiral perovskite, leading to more pronounced distortion of the inorganic octahedra. Figure 2 (C and D) demonstrates the top view of the (2×2) unit cell of the bottom inorganic layer from the SPVK and seeded-SPVK. The corner-sharing Sn─I─Sn angles between two octahedra are 153.2° for SPVK and 146.3° for seeded-SPVK, representing substantial deviations from the ideal 180° of an undistorted lattice. The calculated distortion factor D and bond angle variance δ2 for seeded-SPVK (46) are both larger than those of SPVK, with the analysis method described in note S1 and summarized in table S1. The lattice distortion directly affects the transfer of chirality and spin-dependent properties. To further investigate the effect of chiral seeds on the chiroptical activity of the films, we conducted circular dichroism (CD) measurements on both PVK films, as shown in Fig. 2E. The CD signal of both films coincides with their absorption band edge, consistent with the Neumann-Curie principle (20). At an identical incident light wavelength, the CD spectra peaks of the opposite handedness chiral perovskites exhibit mirror-opposite characteristics, indicating that the perovskite films with opposite handedness have selective absorption toward specific circularly polarized photons. Notably, the CD intensity of the films increased after the introduction of the chiral seeds, indicating a stronger interaction between the chiral organic cations and the inorganic octahedra, which leads to more efficient chirality transfer. The |gCD| values of seeded-RPVK and seeded-SPVK were 5.5 × 10−4 and 6.0 × 10−4, respectively (fig. S8), higher than their counterparts without seeding layers, which were 4.3 × 10−4 and 4.1 × 10−4, respectively, representing the highest |gCD| value reported on tin-based chiral perovskites (47–49). In addition, the introduction of the chiral seeding layers induced an anomalous Cotton effect, typically associated with the splitting of the excitation band (50–52). We plotted |gCD| values based on seven data points for each sample (fig. S9), which better illustrate the enhancement of the original sample’s chiral activity by the chiral seeds. The splitting of the excitation band results from the lifting of degeneracy within the excitation band, caused by the stronger interaction between adjacent chromophores (detailed analysis is provided in note S2 and fig. S10, A and B). We therefore hypothesize that the introduction of the seed layers enhances the hydrogen bonding interactions between the organic chiral cations and the inorganic octahedra within the PVK lattice, thereby strengthening the exciton coupling and giving rise to the Cotton effect observed in the CD spectra. On the basis of convolutional analysis of the CD spectra of chiral perovskite films (53), we found that the introduction of chiral seeds induces distortion of the inorganic octahedra and promotes the formation of chiral-oriented structures. This structural modulation enhances the excitonic coupling effect and leads to pronounced excited-state splitting (Davydov splitting) (50). Detailed analysis is provided in note S3 and fig. S11 (A and B). Geometric phase analysis (GPA) analysis on high-resolution aberration-corrected transmission electron microscopy (AC-TEM) images reveals that seeded-SPVK films exhibit substantially enhanced lattice heterogeneity compared to pristine SPVK film, with broader strain distributions and steeper gradients near grain boundaries. This distortion arises from asymmetric steric constraints of chiral seeds during crystallization, which break local mirror symmetry and induce lattice distortion. Detailed analysis is provided in note S4 and fig. S12. These findings provide direct structural evidence for chiral seed–induced lattice distortion. We also conducted circularly polarized luminescence experiments to verify the chiral excited-state optical response of the films. As shown in fig. S13, seeded-SPVK and seeded-RPVK exhibit clear circularly polarized luminescence signals with opposite signs, consistent with their opposite chirality.

To further elucidate the effect of chiral seeds on the electronic structure of the chiral perovskite lattice, we conducted DFT calculations on the energy band structure of the two chiral configurations. Taking SPVK as an example (fig. S14), the valence band (VB) maximum and conduction band (CB) minimum exhibit no apparent splitting due to the relatively weak spin-orbit coupling (SOC) effect (54). With the introduction of chiral seeds, the VB and CB become denser and exhibit pronounced energy band splitting (Fig. 2, F and G), indicating the emergence of spin splitting in the electronic structure. This enhanced splitting is associated with the seed-induced lattice distortion, which leads to increased inversion symmetry breaking and asymmetry of the crystal potential in the inorganic framework (table S1). Such symmetry breaking is known to contribute to spin splitting in chiral semiconductors (46, 55, 56). The energy band splitting gives rise to the emergence of the Cotton effect. As shown in Fig. 2H, analysis of the spin-resolved band structures reveals that the spin-down bandgap in seeded-SPVK is smaller than that of the spin-up counterpart, making spin-down states more favorable for excitation and population. Thus, the spin-down channels are more pronounced at both the CB minimum and VB maximum, consistent with the reported results that S-handedness prefers spin-down carriers (57). The recombined bands showing Rashba-type splitting near the band edge are provided in fig. S15. Carrier recombination dynamics demonstrated that the introduction of chiral seeds enhances the radiative recombination lifetime of chiral perovskite films, with the lifetime increasing from 7.38/6.75 ns for R/SPVKs to 10.81/7.01 ns for seeded-R/SPVKs, respectively (detailed analysis is provided in note S5 and fig. S16, A and B). We also performed conductive AFM (c-AFM) to investigate the surface current of the chiral perovskite films, as demonstrated in fig. S17 (A and B) (detailed analysis is provided in note S6). It is evident that, upon the addition of seeds, the average value of the surface current exhibits an ∼5-fold increase, attributed to the reinforced chiral lattice orientation. These results indicate that the introduction of chiral seed layers not only enhances the spin-selective band structures but also promotes the recombination lifetime and carrier transport in chiral perovskites, therefore facilitating the intrinsic spin-state filtration of charge carriers.

Room-temperature valley polarization

The enhanced CISS performance of the seeded-R/SPVK films provides a more effective platform for manipulating room-temperature valley polarization. To this end, WS2/chiral PVK heterostructures were fabricated by mechanical exfoliation of a monolayer WS2 on chiral perovskite films (Fig. 3A). The monolayer nature of WS2 was confirmed by Raman spectroscopy (fig. S18). To confirm the physical contact between the monolayer WS2 and chiral PVKs, we further performed AFM measurements on the heterojunction. As shown in fig. S19, the overall RMS roughness of the heterojunction region reached 5.22 nm, and the 3D AFM image reveals that WS2 forms a good and uniformly covered contact with the chiral PVK surface. The energy band structures of the two chiral perovskites were determined from ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) and UV-vis spectra (fig. S20, A to C). Combined with established data for monolayer WS2 bands (58), a type II band alignment at the WS2/chiral perovskite interface was identified (Fig. 3B), which facilitates the transfer of photogenerated holes from the VB of WS2 to that of the chiral PVKs. This process is further evidenced by the quenching of the PL intensity in the WS2/chiral PVK heterostructure compared with that of the pristine WS2 monolayer (Fig. 3C). The pronounced PL quenching indicates efficient interfacial charge transfer, which is essential for spin-selective carrier extraction. Furthermore, PL mapping under linear light excitation (Fig. 3D) reveals the high quality of the WS2 monolayer and the uniform, well-contacted interface of the heterostructure over a large area. The PL measurements of the heterojunction at low temperatures also confirm the effective alignment of the type II band structure. As shown in fig. S21, at 4 K, in addition to the main excitonic peak at ∼623 nm, a weak and broad low-energy emission around ∼715 nm is observed, which is consistent with possible interlayer exciton recombination arising from spatially separated charge carriers.

We further characterized the CPL emission of the monolayer WS2 and the WS2/chiral PVK heterostructure samples using a polarization-resolved microscopy system (Fig. 4A; detailed information is provided in Materials and Methods). As illustrated in Fig. 4B, σ− (σ+) light preferentially excites the K (K′) valley, which subsequently emits σ− (σ+) photons. However, at room temperature, thermally induced intervalley scattering diminishes the difference in emission between the two valleys, thereby suppressing the degree of valley polarization. As a result, the σ− and σ+ components of the CPL emission from pristine WS2 excited by σ+ or σ−exhibit no noticeable difference (fig. S22, A and B). To quantitatively evaluate the polarization state, the DOP was calculated using the following equationDOP=Iσ+−Iσ−Iσ++Iσ−×100%(2)where Iσ+ and Iσ− correspond to the peak intensity of the left- and right-handed components of the PL emission, respectively. The calculated DOP for the pristine monolayer WS2 was ∼3.8% under σ+ excitation and −2.8% under σ− excitation. This negligible polarization can be attributed to the strong intervalley scattering at room temperature, which rapidly dissipates the population imbalance generated by the optical selection rules in monolayer WS2, thereby preventing the establishment of robust valley polarization. In contrast, the incorporation of the chiral perovskite effectively disrupts the equilibrium of carriers within the K and K′ valleys by selectively accepting either spin-up or spin-down holes from the photoexcited monolayer WS2. Statistical analysis across multiple independent heterostructures (n ≥ 3 per type) yielded a narrow DOP distribution. As shown in fig. S23, the error bars denote the SD, confirming the high reproducibility of the room-temperature valley performance of the heterostructures. The measurement error of DOP is estimated to be 0.5% (see Materials and Methods). In the WS2/SP-VK heterostructure, the CISS effect of the SPVK selectively extracts spin-down holes from the VB of the K′ valley in WS2, resulting in dominant σ+ emission from K valley. This spin-selective charge transfer directly creates a population imbalance between the K and K′ valleys, as evidenced by the positive DOP under σ+ excitation, whereas a negative DOP is observed under σ− excitation. The σ+ components mapping clearly illustrates the spatial distribution of SPVK and seeded-SPVK (Fig. 4, C and D), confirming uniform emission under σ+ excitation across the heterostructures. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 4E, the σ+ emission intensity becomes markedly higher than that of σ−, yielding a DOP of 5.1 ± 0.5% in the WS2/SPVK structure. To further amplify the valley population imbalance and enhance the DOP, chiral seeds were introduced to strengthen the CISS effect. The WS2/seeded-SPVK heterostructure exhibited a markedly increased DOP of 10.3 ± 1.5%, and the highest DOP reaches 11.3% at room temperature (Fig. 4F). Notably, the WS2/seeded-RPVK heterostructures exhibit an enhanced DOP of −11.2 ± 1.6%, representing a 64.7% enhancement compared to the −6.8 ± 0.8% value observed in WS2/RPVK. Furthermore, the highest DOP value achieved among the WS2/seeded-RPVK reached −14.9%, demonstrating the potential of chiral seeding for efficient valley-polarized emission (fig. S24, A and B). To benchmark the performance of our system, we summarized reported room-temperature valley polarization values under comparable conditions (i.e., without external electric or magnetic fields), as shown in fig. S25. Most reported values remain below ∼10% (12, 14, 29, 59–62), whereas our system achieves as high as 14.9%, placing it among the highest values under such conditions.

To evaluate the temporal stability of the valley polarization, the heterojunction devices were subjected to a 12-day test under nitrogen protection (fig. S26). The DOP gradually decreases from ∼15 to ∼11% over time, indicating moderate degradation under extended conditions. Nevertheless, a pronounced valley polarization is maintained throughout the testing period. All measurements reported in this work were performed within shorter timescales, where the influence of degradation is minimized. In addition, we prepared control heterostructures using achiral seed layers (racemic or achiral seeds) to verify the specific role of chiral seeds in enhancing the chiral activity of the chiral perovskite films. The results show that, in the presence of achiral seeds, the chirality of the perovskite films is only marginally enhanced, and the room-temperature valley polarization remains in the range of 5 to 6% (fig. S27), which is clearly lower than that achieved with chiral seeds. To investigate the thickness dependence of spin transport, perovskite films with four different thicknesses (52, 97, 155, and 203 nm) were fabricated, and room-temperature valley polarization measurements were performed on the corresponding heterojunctions. The results show that the 155-nm film exhibits the highest DOP of 13.7%, indicating a clear thickness dependence with an optimum at an intermediate thickness. This behavior suggests that spin-selective charge transfer is influenced by the evolution of chiral ordering across the film (figs. S28 and S29).

Thermal molecular vibrations at room temperature may influence the chiral properties and spin transport in chiral perovskites, thereby affecting the valley polarization of the heterojunction. To clarify this effect, we performed temperature-dependent valley polarization measurements. As shown in fig. S30, the DOP increases from 12.5% at 300 K to 22.1% at 60 K, which can be attributed to reduced phonon scattering and suppressed spin relaxation at lower temperatures. Despite thermal fluctuations, the seeded-SPVK–based heterojunction still maintains a pronounced valley polarization of 12.5% at room temperature, which is substantially higher than that of monolayer WS2 (∼3.3%).

To verify the generality of this strategy, we extended this strategy to other chiral perovskites and TMDCs, as shown in fig. S31. Similar enhancement in valley polarization is observed, although the magnitude varies across different material combinations. Notably, the reduced DOP in the WSe2-based heterostructure is likely associated with less favorable type I band alignment, which limits the efficiency of spin-selective charge transfer. The UPS, steady-state PL, and ultrafast transient absorption (TA) spectra on the WSe2/seeded-SPVK heterostructure indicated the necessity of the type II alignment to achieve a high DOP (detailed analysis is provided in note S7 and fig. S32). These results suggest that, although the chiral seed strategy is generally applicable, its effectiveness is governed by band alignment and interfacial coupling.

An intriguing observation is the emergence of a trion (charged exciton) emission peak at longer wavelengths in the WS2/chiral PVK heterostructures. Trions are typically formed by an exciton binding an extra electron or hole (63). In this system, they are primarily generated through the capture of extra holes by excitons, a process that is highly facilitated during carrier transport across the heterostructure. The enhanced CISS effect further imparts valley polarization characteristics to these trions, which are formed with holes of a specific spin direction (spin-down for SPVK, spin-up for RPVK). This effect is substantially weaker in heterostructures without the chiral seed layer (Fig. 4, E and F), providing direct evidence for the enhanced spin extraction efficiency enabled by the chiral seed substrates.

To gain deeper insight into how the chiral seed layer influences spin-selective carrier transport, pump-probe TA spectroscopy was conducted on two types of heterostructures. The samples were excited by an ultrafast pulse (200 fs) at 2.25 eV (fluence: 40 μJ cm−2), and the resulting absorption changes were probed by a white light continuum at a controlled time delay. The TA contours of the monolayer WS2/seeded-SPVK and WS2/SPVK are shown in Fig. 4G and fig. S33A, respectively. Analysis of the TA spectra near 2.0 eV (Fig. 4H and fig. S33B) reveals two characteristic features: an excited-state absorption (ESA) band at ∼2.24 eV and a ground-state bleach (GSB) signal at around 2.15 eV. The GSB at 2.15 eV corresponds to the neutral exciton in WS2, matches well with the steady-state absorption spectrum. The ESA feature at 2.24 eV exhibits a complex decay feature, consisting of a prominent long-lived component attributed to the trion formation and a short-lived component attributed to the biexcitons formation. To further elucidate the carrier dynamics within the heterojunction, we extracted the transient bleaching signals at short delay times (up to 0.6 ps), as shown in fig. S34. A clear increase in the bleaching signal at ∼2.75 eV is observed in the chiral seeded heterostructure. This feature is attributed to state filling in the perovskite layer, indicating carrier transfer from WS2 to the chiral perovskite. These results provide further evidence of efficient interfacial charge transfer in the heterostructure. The carrier dynamics probed at 2.0 eV are compared in Fig. 4I for heterostructures fabricated on different chiral substrates. By fitting the decay kinetics of the GSB signal with a single-exponential function, the time constants for hole transfer can be extracted. The WS2/seeded-SPVK heterostructure exhibits a markedly shorter carrier lifetime of 109.4 ps compared to the 899.8-ps lifetime observed in the WS2/SPVK heterostructure. The decreased lifetime provides direct evidence that the chiral seed layer substantially enhances the efficiency of hole extraction from WS2 to the chiral perovskite. The accelerated hole transfer enables more efficient and selective carrier depletion in one valley, thereby amplifying the population imbalance between the K and K′ valleys, resulting in a markedly enhanced valley polarization at room temperature.

Theoretical calculation of spin transport difference

To elucidate the microscopic mechanism of spin-selective transport induced by chiral seeds at the heterostructure interface, we performed DFT calculations to obtain the electronic band structures and spin-polarized charge density distributions of the heterostructures with and without chiral seeds. Analysis of the band structures (Fig. 5, A and C) reveals that the introduction of chiral seeds drastically reduces the bandgap of the WS2/seeded-SPVK heterostructure to 0.03 eV, thereby facilitating interlayer carrier transport. Furthermore, the spin-polarized charge density distributions (Fig. 5, B and D) demonstrate pronounced spin modulation, indicating a strong interfacial coupling between the seeded-SPVK layer and the WS2 monolayer. Quantitative evaluation of the integrated area under the curve (AUC) of the spin density in the monolayer WS2 region shows a reversal from +0.12 in the seed-free system to −0.49 in the seeded system. The sign inversion (positive to negative) indicates a switch of the dominant spin polarization from spin-up to spin-down, whereas the increased AUC magnitude reflects enhanced net spin polarization. Spin difference density diagrams (Fig. 5, E and F) provide visual evidence: Negligible net spin accumulation is observed at the interface of the heterostructure without chiral seeds (Fig. 5E), whereas a pronounced net interfacial spin density emerges in the present of chiral seeds (Fig. 5F). These results collectively demonstrate that the introduction of chiral seeds amplifies the CISS effect at the chiral perovskite interface, promoting more efficient extraction of spin-polarized holes. This enhancement strengthens the population imbalance between the K and K′ valleys in the WS2 layer, thereby improves valley polarization at room temperature.

DISCUSSION

In summary, we developed an effective strategy to enhance the CISS effect in tin-based chiral perovskites by introducing a chiral seeding layer. The seeded chiral PVK/WS2 heterostructures demonstrate robust valley polarization at room temperature via spin-selective charge transfer. Specifically, the heterostructures achieved a remarkably high average DOP of 10.3 ± 1.5% (with a highest value of 11.3%) for seeded-SPVK/WS2 and −10.9 ± 1.1% (with a highest value of −14.9%) for seeded-RPVK/WS2. We attribute this enhanced DOP to the chiral seeded PVKs, which function as an effective spin filter that controls the valley population in monolayer WS2. Furthermore, ultrafast TA spectroscopy provided direct evidence for efficient charge transfer from WS2 to the chiral PVKs. Our results establish a simple and robust route to achieve high valley polarization in monolayer TMDCs without requiring low temperatures, external magnetic fields, or complex device structures, thus paving the way for practical valleytronics devices.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

R-(+)-/S-(−)-α-methylbenzylamine bromide (R/S-MBA:Br), tin iodide (SnI2; Anhydrobeads, 99.99%), N,N′-dimethylformamide (DMF; anhydrous, 99.8%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received with no further purification.

Heterostructure fabrication

Chiral seeds and chiral perovskite precursor preparation

Liquid R/S-MBA was mixed well with ultradry DMF solution at a volume ratio of 1:3 and left to stand for 2 hours for further use. R/S-MBA:Br (2 mmol) and SnI2 (1 mmol) were mixed and dissolved in DMF for further chiral perovskite fabrication.

Fabrication of chiral seeds layers

The patterned ITO (indium tin oxide) glass or quarter substrates were sequentially cleaned in an ultrasonic bath with detergent, deionized water, and anhydrous ethanol. Each step takes 15 min. Then, the substrates were dried and cleaned with UV-ozone for 15 min. Then, the substrate is transferred into a glove box with nitrogen atmosphere and the next film preparation is completed. First, the preformulated chiral seeds precursor solution was spin coated on the substrates at 5000 rpm for 60 s. After the first process, the chiral precursor was spin coated on the seed-coated substrates immediately with the same spin-coating procedure. The films were annealed at 100°C for 30 s and transferred to a room-temperature plate. The crystallization process will finish without continuous heating. Control chiral perovskite is fabricated without a seed-coated step.

Fabrication of heterostructures

The monolayer WS2 was mechanically exfoliated onto the polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrate and then dry transferred using a point-to-point transfer method onto the top surface of chiral perovskite film. Before transferring, monolayers on the PDMS substrate were identified with an optical microscope and confirmed with Raman spectra. All transfer steps are performed in the glove box to ensure the quality of chiral substrates and fabricated heterostructures.

Characterizations

The morphologies of the films (morphologies of ETLs) and devices were characterized by a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SU8010, Hitachi). XRD pattern data for 2θ values were collected using a Bruker AXD8 Advance diffractometer with nickel-filtered Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). The ex(in) situ GIWAXS experiments were carried out at beamline BL14B1 of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra were achieved by a streak camera (Hamamatsu, C6860). The laser source is an amplified titanium/sapphire laser providing 800-nm 35-fs pulses at 2 kHz, which is then frequency doubled for a 400-nm excitation. Steady-state PL spectra were measured by an FLS920 transient optical spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments).

CD spectroscopy of chiral perovskite thin films on quartz substrates was measured with Applied Photophysics Chirascan in the range of 300 to 550 nm with a 0.5-nm resolution at room temperature.

TA spectroscopy uses a custom-built femtosecond pump-probe setup, as described in detail in a previous publication (64). The experimental setup consisted of an optical parametric amplifier (OPA) driven by 1030-nm fundamental light from an amplified Yb:KGW laser system with a 750-kHz repetition rate. The OPA generated a pump at 550 nm, whereas a portion of the fundamental light was directed onto a YAG (yttrium-aluminum-garnet) crystal to generate a broadband probe light spanning from 430 to 750 nm. An optical chopper modulated the pump at a frequency of 195 Hz, and a motorized linear stage controlled the time delay between the pump and the probe, providing a maximum delay of ∼1 ns. The pump and probe beams were overlapped and focused on the sample by using a 40x objective with a numerical aperture (NA) of 0.6. The reflected light was collected by the same objective and directed toward a spectrometer for analysis. To prevent exposure to air and moisture during measurements, the samples were housed in a vacuum chamber (ST-500, Janis).

PL was measured with a home-built confocal microscope consisting of an Olympus IX81 inverted microscope equipped with a ×20 dry objective lens (NA: 0.7; Olympus America) and delivering a spatial resolution of ∼1 μm. Samples were excited with a continuous-wave monochromatic laser light of 633 nm (HeNe, Melles Griot), 594 nm (HeNe, Melles Griot), or 532 nm (diode laser, Sapphire, Coherent). A polarizing beam splitter cube was used to linearly polarize the excitation beam. Appropriate laser-line optical filters were used to clean the excitation laser. The PL emitted from the sample was collected by the same objective lens. A dichroic beam splitter mirror and a long-pass filter were used to filter out the excitation laser from the emission. For PL spectra, the emission was directed to a monochromator (Acton SpectraPro 2750) via a multimode optical fiber. A quarter-wave plate and a linear polarizer were added in front of the optical fiber to separately detect the left and the right circularly polarized PL emission for helicity-resolved PL measurements. Similarly, another quarter-wave plate was added after the linear polarizer to create circularly polarized laser excitation. Before conducting the circularly polarized PL test, we calibrated the test system (details in note S8 and fig. S35) and analyzed the effects of measurement errors and sample anisotropy on the test results (details in note S9 and fig. S36). For PL mapping, the emission was spatially filtered with a 100-μm pinhole. To simultaneously measure PL and reflection images, the emission was split by a 50:50 cube and imaged onto two single-photon-counting avalanche photodiodes (MPD, Picoquant).

Computational details

All the first-principles calculations were performed using the DFT as implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) (65). The projector-augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotential method and plane-wave basis set were used to describe the ionic cores and to take valence electrons into account (66), respectively. The configurations 4d105s25p2 of Sn, 5s25p5 of I, 2s22p2 of C, 2s22p3 of N, 1s1 of H, 5p65d46s2 of W, and 3s23p4 of S atoms were treated as valence electrons for the PAW pseudopotentials. An energy cutoff of 520 eV was used for expansion of the plane-wave basis set. To eliminate the artificial interactions between periodic images, a distance of around 15 Å was typically added to the periodic slab geometries to create a vacuum space. The 4×1×4 gamma-centered grid was used to sample the Brillouin zone (BZ). The generalized-gradient approximation (GGA) (67) of Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) was used for the electronic exchange-correlation functional (68). All structures investigated here are fully relaxed until the Hellmann-Feynman forces acting on all atoms are less than 0.02 eV/Å, and the total energy was smaller than 1 × 10−5 eV. Effective mass was obtained by combining the VASPKIT software.
