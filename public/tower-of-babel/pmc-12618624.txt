Cortical hierarchy underlying homeostatic sleep pressure alleviation
Authors: Qihong Zou, Guangyuan Zou, Shilei Wang, Yan Wang, Jing Xu, Yujie Long, Shuqin Zhou, Xiuwen Wu, Guoyuan Yang, Lang Qin, Zi Hui Su, Zaixu Cui, Xi-Nian Zuo, Xiangdong Tang, Hengyi Rao, Jia-Hong Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sleep dissipates accumulated sleep pressure and restores brain function, yet how this recovery unfolds across the cortical hierarchy remains unclear. Here, we record simultaneous electroencephalogram (EEG) and blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) functional magnetic resonance imaging data from 130 healthy adults to map spatial patterns underlying sleep pressure alleviation. Compared to wakefulness, sleep elicits spatially heterogeneous changes in BOLD fluctuation along a sensory-association cortical gradient. The magnitude of these sleep-wake differences correlates with individual slow-wave activity and is most pronounced during the first hour of sleep. As slow waves dissipates, these hierarchical differences are progressively downscaled, implicating homeostatic regulation in sculpting cortical plasticity. In addition, the homeostatic regulation of BOLD fluctuation amplitude is spatially associated with the regional distribution of glycolysis. Finally, recovery sleep reinstates hierarchical BOLD dynamics after sleep loss in an independent sleep deprivation study. These findings consistently suggest a cortical hierarchy underlying the dynamic changes in sleep homeostasis.

Introduction

Humans spend approximately one-third of their lives sleeping, a fundamental physiological necessity for rejuvenating optimal brain function and maintaining metabolic health1–5. The drive to sleep, known as homeostatic sleep pressure, increases with prolonged wakefulness, signaling the brain’s need for restorative processes in both humans and animals6. Increasing evidence bolsters the role of sleep in supporting the following mechanistic functions in a homeostatic manner: essential to maintain synaptic and cellular equilibrium, to revitalize brain plasticity and cognitive acuity, to recover network criticality, and to rebalance brain energy metabolism7–11. Slow-wave activity (SWA) measured by low-density scalp electroencephalogram (EEG) power between 0.5 and 4.0 Hz, is a reliable indicator of sleep pressure and synaptic homeostasis. SWA typically peaks during the initial sleep cycle and gradually decreases with continued sleep across the night6,12. This decrease is accompanied by decreased incidence of high-amplitude slow waves and decreased wave slopes13.

Quite a lot of effort has been devoted to documenting changes in brain electrical activity, hemodynamic activity, and metabolism across different stages of sleep and wakefulness14–23. It is demonstrated that the impact of sleep homeostasis on SWA is heterogeneously distributed over the scalp23. However, beyond this coarse scalp topography, a measure of how sleep homeostasis affects the amplitude of brain activity with high spatial resolution remains elusive in humans. Elucidating how brain activities are spatially reorganized during the dynamic process of alleviation of sleep pressure will offer valuable insights into the function of sleep homeostasis. In particular, capturing which cortical regions are maximally regulated by sleep homeostasis can provide insights into developing more effective interventions for restoring impaired sleep. Blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) functional MRI (fMRI) provides a large-scale whole-brain measurement that are coupled to neuronal activity24–26, and has demonstrated its great potential to characterize gradually and locally modulated cortical responses to sustained attention through increases in homeostatic sleep pressure27. The amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF)24,28–34, derived from fMRI signals, is a widely recognized, reliable metric for assessing the amplitude of BOLD fluctuations. Previous studies have consistently observed a spatially hierarchical pattern in sleep-related changes in intrinsic brain activity, with more significant alterations in primary sensory regions compared to higher-order association cortices21,35,36. Moreover, increased ALFF in sensory regions37 has also been observed to be associated with higher SWA during sleep compared to wakefulness. Indeed, experimentally enhancing sleep pressure by sleep deprivation has been shown to elicit changes in local BOLD fluctuations in the association regions38.

Here, we delineate the spatiotemporal dynamics of BOLD fluctuation amplitude in response to alleviated sleep pressure by integrating dense sampling of simultaneous EEG and fMRI during nocturnal sleep, and we perform a sleep deprivation and recovery validation procedure. We hypothesized that hierarchical changes in BOLD fluctuation amplitude during sleep would be downscaled with alleviated sleep pressure. Our findings elucidate the hierarchical spatiotemporal interplay between BOLD fluctuation amplitude and dynamic changes in sleep homeostasis, providing novel insights into the restorative effect of nocturnal sleep.

Results

Cortical fluctuation differences between sleep and wakefulness are distributed along the sensory-association hierarchy

We collected simultaneous EEG-fMRI data during resting-state wakefulness and nocturnal sleep from 130 non-sleep-deprived healthy adults recruited from colleges and communities in Beijing, China. Complementary data included sleep diary, whole-night sleep recordings, and cognitive tests. The participants showed a high variability in sleep habits (Table 1), sleep structure, and cognitive performance, aligning with the literature on healthy adults. The fMRI data demonstrated a high temporal signal-to-noise ratio (Fig. 1b) and stable head motion (Fig. 1c) during the sleep sessions. A total of 2361 5-min epochs, each from a continuously same stage, were extracted from the wakefulness and sleep sessions for analysis, comprising 568 wakefulness epochs from 130 participants, 193 N1 epochs from 80 participants, 968 N2 epochs from 114 participants, 561 N3 epochs from 85 participants, and 71 REM epochs from 23 participants (Fig. 1c). There was a significant difference in motion measured by meanFD between sleep and wakefulness (χ2 = 33.58, P = 3.29 × 10−27). Post-hoc comparisons showed that sleep states were associated with significantly lower head motion than wakefulness (all P < 0.0001), consistent with a previous sleep EEG-fMRI study40. In addition, N3 sleep exhibited higher motion compared to N2 (P = 0.0387) and REM sleep (P = 0.0099).

The amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) derived from BOLD fMRI signals was used to non-invasively measure the voxel-wise amplitude of intrinsic hemodynamic activity within the frequency range of 0.01–0.08 Hz24,28–34. ALFF calculations were performed for each 5-min epoch during wakefulness and various stages of sleep. Although the spatial distributions of the ALFF across the brain were visually similar across different stages (Supplementary Fig. 1a), a significant main effect of stage on ALFF (corrected P < 0.05) was observed in 98.36% of the 144,065 voxels over the cerebral cortex (Fig. 1d) using a linear mixed-effect (LME) model41, exhibiting large effect sizes (average coefficient of determination = 0.98 over the cortex). Post hoc pairwise comparisons of ALFF differences between each sleep stage and wakefulness revealed spatially bilateral, yet substantially heterogeneous changes in BOLD fluctuation amplitude across the brain (Fig. 1e for corrected results in the left hemisphere and Supplementary Fig. 1b for uncorrected whole cortex results). These ALFF difference maps were spatially similar, exhibiting a spatial correlation greater than 0.83 between any pair of the four maps. Consequently, all NREM and REM sleep stages were treated as a combined sleep stage in further analyses, unless otherwise specified. This approach produced a group-level difference map in BOLD fluctuation amplitude (Fig. 1f), i.e., the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\triangle$$\end{document}△ALFF map, derived from the comparison of ALFF maps between all stages of sleep and wakefulness across the participants. We tested whether the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\triangle$$\end{document}△ALFF map was related to ALFF during wakefulness. Thus, sleep-related changes in ALFF were not significantly correlated with the spatial pattern of ALFF map in wakefulness.

We next evaluated whether the cortical \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\triangle$$\end{document}△ALFF pattern aligned with a predefined cortical functional hierarchy. Using the brain network atlas based on the Chinese Human Connectome Project dataset42, we divided the cortex into seven canonical functional networks. Notable decreases in ALFF, indicated by negative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\triangle$$\end{document}△ALFF, were observed in the association cortices during sleep compared to wakefulness (Fig. 1g), including the frontoparietal (FP) and default mode (DMN) networks. In contrast, significant increases in ALFF, indicated by positive \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\triangle$$\end{document}△ALFF, were observed in sensory cortices, such as the visual (Vis) and somatomotor (SM) networks. Previous studies have demonstrated that the human functional hierarchy can be probed by gradients of functional connectivity in the cerebral cortex using task-free fMRI43. Specifically, extracting the principal functional gradient from the functional connectivity matrix produces a sensory-association hierarchy. Here, the principal functional gradient was adopted from prior literature43. Using Spearman’s rank correlation, we found a significantly negative alignment between the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\triangle$$\end{document}△ALFF map (Fig. 1f) and the principal functional gradient (r = −0.78, Pspin < 0.0001, Fig. 1h) across the cortex, confirming hierarchical changes in ALFF during sleep, progressing from sensorimotor increases to association decreases across the cortex.

BOLD fluctuation amplitude differences between sleep and wakefulness capture individual variability in sleep pressure

Having delineated the differences in BOLD fluctuation amplitude during sleep compared to wakefulness across the brain, we next evaluated how these differences are associated with the inter-individual differences in sleep pressure. Animal and human studies have shown that homeostatic sleep pressure accumulates during wakefulness and alleviates during sleep12,44. SWA peaks during early sleep when sleep pressure is high and decreases with accumulated sleep as sleep pressure alleviates6. Therefore, SWA during sleep is considered a reliable electrophysiological marker of homeostatic sleep pressure. BOLD fluctuation amplitude differences between sleep and wakefulness are downscaled with alleviated sleep pressure

Synaptic strength progressively restores with accumulated sleep44, and loss of synapses has been shown to be greatest during high-pressure sleep after prolonged wakefulness, while lowest during low-pressure sleep in the latter half of an undisturbed night10. Whether and how differences in BOLD fluctuation amplitude during sleep, compared to wakefulness, are reorganized in the cortex with alleviation of sleep pressure remains unknown. To address this question, we first validated that sleep pressure is alleviated with time spent asleep6,12 in a noisy and space-constrained sleep environment during EEG-fMRI scan. Specifically, we categorized the 5-min epochs into ‘within’ and ‘after’ the first-hour sleep epochs based on the duration of accumulated sleep prior to each epoch. As the first sleep cycle is generally comprised of 1 h of sleep6, and the median accumulated sleep prior to all the 5-min sleep epochs was 1.04 h, which was rounded to 1 h in the current study, we adopted 1-h accumulated sleep as a threshold to separate the two sleep pressure categories. We coded a wakefulness/sleep epoch situated within the first-hour sleep period when there was less than 1 h of accumulated sleep prior to this epoch; and coded a wakefulness/sleep epoch situated after the first-hour sleep period when there was at least 1 h of accumulated sleep prior to this epoch (Supplementary Fig. 2a). There were 1262 epochs within the first-hour sleep period and 1099 epochs after the first-hour sleep period, both of which were predominantly characterized by NREM sleep stages N2 and N3. We then evaluated the effect of sleep pressure on BOLD fluctuation amplitude. Using the same two-stage and two-category LME model, we tested the interaction effect for each voxel on the 2361 ALFF maps. A significant interaction effect was observed, primarily in the association regions (e.g., the superior and middle frontal gyrus, the superior medial gyrus, the angular gyrus, and the inferior parietal lobule) and the sensory regions (mainly the visual cortices, the inferior temporal gyrus, and the posterior central gyrus). To investigate the spatial alignment between the effect of accumulated sleep on BOLD fluctuation amplitude and the cortical hierarchy, we compared the average ∆ALFF between the two categories of sleep pressure in each of the seven large-scale functional networks. We found that, in the FP network, the decreased activity observed within the first hour of sleep returned closer to wakefulness after the first hour of sleep in the night (P < 0.001, Fig. 3d), i.e., the BOLD fluctuation amplitude in the FP were significantly downscaled with dissipated sleep pressure. Furthermore, the increased BOLD fluctuation amplitude seen within the first hour of sleep in the Aud (P < 0.001, Fig. 3d) and Vis (P < 0.01, Fig. 3d) networks also approached wakefulness as sleep progressed. The SM, ventral attention network and DMN showed non-significant changes in cortical activity. Having identified the regulation of ∆ALFF modulated by dynamic changes in sleep homeostasis and their spatial alignment with the sensory-association hierarchy, we further characterized the global proportional relationship between ∆ALFF of within and after the first hour of sleep across all voxels in the brain using linear regression analysis. The regression fit (∆ALFFAFS = 0.87 ∆ALFFWFS) significantly deviated from the identity line, i.e., ∆ALFFAFS = ∆ALFFWFS (P < 0.0001, Fig. 3f), suggesting that the pronounced \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\triangle$$\end{document}△ALFF within first-hour sleep period became less pronounced, i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\triangle$$\end{document}△ALFF is globally downscaled with accumulated sleep. Together, these findings showed that sleep had pronounced effects on BOLD fluctuation amplitude during the first hour of sleep, and the magnitude decreased with alleviated sleep pressure. Thus, sleep-related BOLD fluctuation amplitude differences were modulated by changes in sleep homeostasis.

Regulation of BOLD fluctuation amplitude by changes in sleep homeostasis is spatially associated with glycolysis

Sleep is characterized by a decrease in inefficient glycolysis along with an increase in efficient oxidative metabolism45 compared to wakefulness. We explored whether ∆ALFF between sleep and wakefulness would spatially correlate with glycolysis and oxidative metabolism. As glycolysis increased significantly during late sleep46, we further explored whether the spatial patterns of downscaling in BOLD fluctuation amplitude (∆ALFFAFS – ∆ALFFWFS) by dissipated sleep pressure would be correlated and glycolysis. We extracted the cerebral metabolic rate for glucose data from a positron emission tomography (PET) dataset47 and further decomposed glucose metabolism into glycolysis and oxidative metabolism (Fig. 4) to quantify these two pathways of glucose metabolism. We observed that the spatial pattern of ∆ALFF during nocturnal sleep, compared to wakefulness, exhibited a negative correlation with glycolysis (Spearman’s r = −0.44, Pspin = 0.0015; Fig. 4) and a positive correlation with oxidative metabolism (Spearman’s r = 0.39, Pspin = 0.0037; Fig. 4). This finding suggests that a strong difference in BOLD fluctuation amplitude during sleep, compared to wakefulness, lies in a brain region with low glycolysis and high oxidative metabolism. Furthermore, a positive spatial correlation was observed between the spatial pattern of downscaled ∆ALFF with dissipated sleep pressure and glycolysis (Spearman’s r = 0.52, Pspin < 0.0001; Fig. 4). Second, we repeated all the analyses based on an independent sleep EEG-fMRI dataset (Dataset 2)49 from 33 healthy adults. Although the participants showed a lower probability of sleep and the total scanning duration was shorter (1.77 h on average), the tSNR was reasonable, and the motion during fMRI was moderate (Fig. 5f). All 28 EEG channels showed higher SWA during sleep compared to wakefulness, and sleep-related changes in ALFF were distributed along the functional hierarchy (Fig. 5g). Inter-participant changes in ∆SWA at the frontal and parietal channels significantly correlated with ∆ALFF, with regression coefficients hierarchically distributed over the cortex (Fig. 5h). Although there was only a trend toward sleep pressure dissipation, ∆ALFF was significantly downscaled (Fig. 5i). In addition, glycolysis was spatially correlated with both ∆ALFF (Spearman’s r = −0.41, Pspin = 0.0035) and ∆ALFF downscaling (Spearman’s r = 0.17, Pspin = 0.0060) (Fig. 5j).

Third, regulation of BOLD fluctuation amplitude by sleep homeostasis was validated using sleep deprivation and recovery designs. Sleep pressure increases with time spent awake and continues to increase when nocturnal sleep is deprived in humans, while it decreases with recovery sleep6. We evaluated how recovery sleep restores BOLD fluctuation amplitude from sleep deprivation with extremely heightened sleep pressure using Dataset 3. After a whole night of total sleep deprivation (24 h of continuous wakefulness), the BOLD fluctuation amplitude compared to that after baseline sleep was negatively associated with the principal functional gradient (Spearman’s r = −0.58, Pspin < 0.0001; Fig. 6a), shifting toward a sleeping brain even though the participants were in a state of wakefulness (see Fig. 1h for comparison). This finding is consistent with the degradation in information processing after sleep deprivation50. In addition, the spatial pattern of BOLD fluctuation amplitude differences during extremely high pressure compared to baseline is negatively correlated with glycolysis (Spearman’s r = −0.48, Pspin < 0.0001), mirroring the finding of sleep-related changes (See Fig. 4 for comparison). In contrast, after two nights of recovery sleep with dissipated sleep pressure, the BOLD fluctuation amplitude was restored compared to that after sleep deprivation, showing regained BOLD fluctuation amplitude in both the association and sensory networks, and significant spatial correlations with both the sensory-association hierarchy (Spearman’s r = 0.58, Pspin < 0.0001) and glycolysis (Spearman’s r = 0.38, Pspin < 0.0001; Fig. 6b). Discussion

By continuously tracking nocturnal human sleep during the sleep pressure alleviation process with dense EEG-fMRI sampling, the study demonstrates widespread differences in BOLD fluctuation amplitude along the sensory-association hierarchy during sleep compared to wakefulness. These differences in BOLD fluctuation amplitude are associated with individual variability in sleep pressure. Importantly, the pronounced BOLD fluctuation amplitude differences within the first hour of sleep period became hierarchically downscaled after the first hour of sleep. Moreover, modulation of BOLD fluctuation amplitude in response to increased sleep pressure by sleep deprivation was restored by recovery sleep. These findings indicate that dynamics in sleep pressure regulate cortical activity and sleep promotes the restoration of cortical activity. In addition, these modulations of BOLD fluctuation amplitude by sleep homeostasis correlate with the spatial distribution of glycolysis. Together, these findings present a hierarchical spatial map underlying dynamic changes in sleep homeostasis.

This study reveals a hierarchical pattern of changes in BOLD fluctuation amplitude across nearly the entire human cortex during nocturnal sleep compared to wakefulness, aligning with previous findings on cerebral blood flow changes from PET sleep studies19. We found that such heterogeneous changes in BOLD fluctuation amplitude during sleep exhibit spatial anti-alignment with the predefined sensory-association cortical hierarchy43,60. Recent work has shown that intrinsic BOLD fluctuation amplitude shifts in the opposite direction to the principal gradient during NREM sleep35. In addition, an increased occurrence of global propagations along the principal gradient while a decreased occurrence of global propagations against the principal gradient has been demonstrated during sleep57,59. Altogether, these findings suggest disrupted information processing during sleep. The spatial heterogeneity observed in BOLD fluctuation amplitude changes between sleep and wakefulness is consistent with the findings of animal studies that neuronal firing rates showed differential patterns of changes across various regions of the brain44,61,62 and varying modulation effects on sleep homeostasis among brain regions63. Additionally, the close correspondence between hierarchical differences in BOLD fluctuation amplitude and inter-individual differences in sleep pressure indicates that BOLD fluctuation amplitude may serve as a macroscale mapping of sleep homeostasis across the human brain.

It is worth noting that the unimodal-transmodal hierarchical patterns have been widely observed and well documented across various states of consciousness64. The current findings in our study serve as complementary evidence to existing work, as sleep is a very important model of loss of consciousness. Specifically, sleep and wakefulness are natural, non-invasive, reversible and circadian processes, which makes sleep distinct from other models of consciousness loss, such as disorders of consciousness. Although anesthesia shares numerous neurophysiologic traits with the process of natural sleep, e.g., both cause reversible loss of consciousness, there are interactions between sleep and anesthesia. For instance, sleep loss increases the sensitivity to anesthesia65, but sleep debt accumulation by lesions in sleep-promoting neurons produces resistance to anesthesia66. In addition, EEG characteristics and neural substrates of anesthesia are more similar to those of NREM sleep than those of REM sleep67. Together, our findings of sleep-related changes in BOLD fluctuation amplitude spatially correlated with the unimodal–transmodal functional axis represent unique contributions to the studies on loss of consciousness.

The key findings of this study are hierarchical spatial patterns of BOLD fluctuation amplitude regulated by dynamics in sleep homeostasis in humans. Hierarchical patterns of BOLD fluctuation amplitude were consistently revealed by the regulation of sleep homeostasis using three experimental designs: accumulated nocturnal sleep, total sleep deprivation followed by sleep recovery, and partial sleep restriction. We observed a drop-off in sleep-wakefulness differences in the amplitude of BOLD fluctuations after the first hour of accumulated nocturnal sleep, hierarchical reorganization of BOLD fluctuation amplitude due to promoted sleep pressure from sleep deprivation, and restoration of brain activity changes following sleep deprivation after two nights of recovery sleep. The findings of reorganized brain activity by heightened/dissipated sleep pressure spatially correlated with the unimodal–transmodal functional axis, shed light on the homeostatic regulation of cortical activity along the functional hierarchy. These findings elucidate that the brain regions at the two extremes of the cortical hierarchy are maximally influenced by dynamic changes in sleep homeostasis. This spatial hierarchy in BOLD fluctuation amplitude underlying sleep homeostasis aligns with previous research showing that sleep pressure buildup during sleep deprivation modulates cortical responses to sustained attention27. The downscaling of changes in BOLD fluctuation amplitude along the sensory-association cortical hierarchy with alleviation of sleep pressure is supported by prior studies on the loss and recovery of consciousness in patients with brain injury. These studies have shown that patients with loss of consciousness exhibit elevated SWA68 and decreased BOLD fluctuation amplitude in the association regions69, both of which are restored after the recovery of consciousness. The sleep-wake differences in BOLD fluctuation amplitude along the sensory-association hierarchy are more pronounced within the first hour of sleep, raising the question of whether sleep has additional functions associated with divergent cognitive processing during the later sleep period70,71. Moreover, as sleep-related ALFF changes start to drop off after the first hour of sleep, the spatial patterns of sleep-related ALFF changes after longer sleep (e.g., 4/5/6 h of sleep) warrant further investigation. Potential findings will give rise to a broader view of sleep function, from the aspects of dissipated sleep pressure, evolving sleep cycles, and alternately appearing NREM and REM sleep.

The current study provides a cortical mapping of sleep-promoted functional restoration of the brain, which is distributed along the sensory-association hierarchy. This brain restoration mapping promoted by sleep in humans has potential implications for the restorative role of sleep in animal models, as the sensory-association functional hierarchy is a fundamental principle of brain architecture across mammalian species, which has been consistently identified in non-human primates and humans43,72. In addition, sleep patterns exhibit developmental73 or evolutionary5 trajectories, and the sensory-to-association hierarchy gradually emerges as the brain matures74,75 or evolves76. The interconnected relationship between the development of sleep restoration function and the cortical hierarchy warrants further investigation.

We find that sleep-related changes in BOLD fluctuation amplitude, their downscaling with dissipated sleep pressure during nocturnal sleep, and restoration after recovery sleep compared to sleep deprivation, all exhibit moderate associations with the spatial map of glycolysis. These results could be related to previous metabolic findings in both animals45 and humans77, which show a metabolic shift from elevated glycolysis during wakefulness to more oxidative metabolism during sleep, and possibly indicating a less efficient metabolic basis for cortical activity during dissipated-pressure sleep. It is worth noting that the effect sizes of the glycolysis association findings in the current study (around 0.4) are smaller than several previous reports of molecular associations. For instance, Burt and colleagues60 found T1w/T2w maps spatially correlated with gene expression of specific NMDA and GABA receptors (r > 0.60). The observed difference in effect sizes may be due to several factors, including the different methods of measurement (gene expression data vs. metabolic imaging) and the different biological processes functioning (brain’s functional architecture vs. sleep homeostasis). Indeed, brain regions with the highest glycolysis exhibit increased gene expression related to synapse formation and growth78, which is tightly correlated with the function of sleep7,8. In addition, we validated that the spatial correlation between ALFF changes regulated by dynamics in sleep homeostasis and glycolysis are specific and not significantly influenced by the principal patterns of gene expression79 or neurotransmitter receptor patterns80. Together, we state that our metabolic association findings, though with moderate effect sizes, still offer valuable insights into the metabolic underpinnings of sleep homeostasis.

In our study, there are several important limitations that need to be addressed for further improvement in the future. Firstly, the current study is limited by its reliance on spatial correlations with PET-derived metabolic measures from a separate dataset. While comparing experimentally generated maps to reference maps helps facilitate cross-disciplinary scientific discovery81, future work could benefit from integrated multimodal imaging techniques, including within-subject EEG-PET and EEG-fMRI data, to strengthen the understanding of sleep pressure alleviation and brain energy metabolism. Secondly, we revealed hierarchical patterns of BOLD fluctuation amplitude regulated by sleep homeostasis using nocturnal sleep EEG-fMRI and sleep deprivation-recovery designs, and validated these findings using independent datasets. Nonetheless, a cross-species approach could provide a deeper understanding of the sleep homeostasis effects on hierarchical functional activity, offering valuable comparative insights. Thirdly, although participants completed an adaptation session around 1 h to promote sleep in the scanner and we started the sleep scan at the participants’ usual sleep onset time, they woke up around 3 AM, and much earlier than their sleep offset time. For future work, performing a full adaptation night for sleeping in the MRI scanner, facilitating a better sleeping environment with more comfortable bedding and quieter pulse sequence40, should help ensure an all-night sleep in the scanner. Fourthly, although we observed inter-individual correlations between SWA and ALFF changes, these associations may partly arise from measurement-related co-variation within individuals, rather than reflecting stable inter-individual traits. Because we did not conduct a test–retest reliability assessment, we cannot determine the extent to which the observed correlations capture genuine trait-like differences as opposed to variability inherent to the measurements. Future studies incorporating repeated assessments will be necessary to establish the reliability and stability of these associations. Lastly, while the spatial correlations between the impacts of sleep homeostasis on BOLD fluctuation amplitude and metabolic patterns are moderate, our results are comparable to the effect sizes reported in prior literature evaluating the molecular associations80,81.

In summary, this study uncovers spatially hierarchical changes in BOLD fluctuation amplitude during nocturnal sleep, which are associated with individual variations in sleep pressure and are downscaled during the dynamic process of sleep pressure alleviation. In addition, the hierarchically reorganized BOLD fluctuation amplitude induced by extremely heightened sleep pressure due to total sleep deprivation was restored by recovery sleep. These findings provide a detailed spatial mapping of changes in brain activity during sleep, highlighting the hierarchical restoration that occurs with alleviated sleep pressure. Our findings offer valuable insights into sleep homeostasis and have implications for developing more effective interventions for restoring impaired sleep.

Sleep diary and whole-night sleep monitoring

Participants were instructed to keep a 2-week sleep diary, tracking sleep onset and offset times, as well as time spent in bed.

Whole-night sleep recordings were obtained as a baseline measure of objective sleep quality, using either standard polysomnography (Grael, Compumedics, Melbourne, Australia; n = 32) conducted in the sleep lab, or a Single electroencephalography-based wearable forehead sleep recorder82,83 (UMindSleep, EEGSmart Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China; n = 97) used at participant’s home, dormitory, or in the sleep lab. Sleep monitoring was conducted for a minimum of two nights, with the first night serving as an adaptation period. If the researchers found the second night’s data unsatisfactory or the participant felt that night’s sleep quality was unsatisfactory, then a third or more nights of recordings were conducted until both the quality of data and participants’ sleep were satisfactory, with a maximum of eight nights.

As the portable sleep device classifies sleep into four stages, including wakefulness, light sleep (combining N1 and N2), deep sleep (N3), and REM sleep, we thus report sleep parameters as the duration of the four stages. Participants averaged 7.10 h of sleep per night, with 8.48% wakefulness, 58.03% light sleep, 15.73% deep sleep, and 17.77% REM sleep. N3 sleep mostly occurred in the first half of the night, while REM sleep predominated in the second half.

Questionnaires and cognitive tests

All participants (N = 130) completed subjective sleep questionnaires as part of the recruitment process to evaluate their subjective sleep quality. The average scores (mean ± SD) were 4.44 ± 2.07 for Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI)84, 8.88 ± 4.17 for Epworth sleepiness scale (ESS)85, 50.78 ± 8.90 forMorningness-Eveningness Questionnaire (MEQ)86,87, and 36.20 ± 10.34 Fatigue severity scale (FSS)88.

Two cognitive assessments were administered to evaluate the participants’ cognitive performances: the N-back working memory (WM) task and the psychomotor vigilance test (PVT). The cognitive tests were conducted on a laptop at the participants’ convenience prior to the sleep EEG-fMRI scan. A total of 95 participants completed these assessments.

The N-back WM task89 followed a block design and included three conditions: two active WM blocks (two-back and three-back) and a low-level vigilance task (zero-back). Each 32-second block comprised a 2-second instruction indicating the task difficulty level, followed by 30 consecutive trials of single-letter stimuli (500 ms in duration, with a 1,500-ms inter-stimulus interval). During the vigilance task, participants were instructed to press a button each time the letter ‘D’ (uppercase or lowercase) appeared on the screen, following the instruction “press for D”. In the two active WM conditions, participants pressed a button when the current letter matched the one shown “N” (either two or three) items back, according to the instruction “N-back”. The task comprised four runs, totaling to about 7 min. Each run consisted of three experimental blocks, presented in either the order “zero-back, two-back, and three-back” or “zero-back, three-back, and two-back”. Behavioral performance was primarily assessed using the hit rate across the varying task difficulty levels90. The hit rates for both the two-back and three-back conditions showed substantial variability among participants, with mean ± SD values of 0.91 ± 0.08 and 0.76 ± 0.14, respectively, consistent with previous findings91.

The PVT is a simple reaction time (RT) task that measures responses to randomly presented visual stimuli. It is widely used to evaluate sustained attention and alertness, particularly in studies of sleep homeostasis and circadian rhythms12,27. The PVT task was administered via the Psychtoolbox program on the MATLAB platform and comprised a total of 72 trials. In each trial, participants were instructed to press a button as quickly as possible to stop a millisecond counter displayed on the black computer screen. Upon button press, the counter would stop and display the reaction time. The PVT task lasted approximately 10 min, with random inter-stimulus intervals ranging from 2 to 10 seconds. Effective responses were defined as those with reaction times (RTs) between 200 and 500 ms; RTs exceeding 500 ms were recorded as omission times. For effective responses, the fastest 10% of RTs were averaged to calculate the fastest reaction time (RT10), and the average of the fastest 10% to 90% of RTs was defined as the intermediate reaction time (RT80). In this study, RT10 and RT80 were calculated as measures of sustained attention (RT10 (mean ± SD): 259.05 ± 19.88 ms; RT80 (mean ± SD): 316.83 ± 25.48 ms), aligning with the literature12,27,82.

Sleep EEG-fMRI data acquisition

Within a week after the 2-week sleep monitoring, an adaptation session familiarized participants with the sleep environment in the MRI scanner (3 T Prisma with a 20-channel head coil, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). During this adaptation session, participants underwent a 6-min T1-weighted scan and a 30-min BOLD fMRI scan while wearing an EEG cap. The simultaneous sleep EEG-fMRI scan was conducted in the same scanner within a week of the adaptation session, at the individual’s usual bedtime. The sleep EEG-fMRI scan continuously monitored the participants’ nocturnal sleep, with scanning duration ranging from 0.67 to 6.83 h. The scan commenced around midnight, closely aligning with participants’ reported regular sleep onset times (two-sided paired t-test, t = -0.69, P = 0.4903), and ended around 3 AM when the participants were fully awake and could not fall asleep again, or until 6:30 AM was reached. Sleep in the fMRI scanner ended earlier than average sleep offset times over the preceding two weeks (two-sided paired t-test, t = −32.16, P < 0.0001), due to the inevitable influence of scanner noise and limited space. After the sleep EEG-fMRI scan, participants were allowed to return to sleep in their usual environment.

EEG data were recorded using a 64-channel MR-compatible EEG system (Brain Products, Munich, Germany; sampling rate = 5000 Hz) simultaneously with fMRI acquisition. The EEG caps contained EEG electrodes positioned according to the international 10/20 system (56 or 57 channels), two reference electrodes placed at bilateral mastoids, two electrooculography electrodes, one electrocardiogram (ECG) electrode, and electromyography electrodes (2 or 3 electrodes). The impedance was checked and verified before commencing the wakefulness session prior to the sleep sessions and the first sleep EEG-fMRI session, so that the reference and ground electrodes was below 10 kOhm, while the impedance of all other electrodes was below 20 kOhm. To avoid interrupting the participants’ sleep, we did not attempt to adjust any impedances higher than the threshold before the following sleep sessions.

High-resolution T1-weighted structural images were acquired using a magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient-echo sequence (repetition time (TR) = 2530 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.98 ms, inversion time = 1100 ms, flip angle (FA) = 7°, number of slices = 192, matrix = 512 × 448, and voxel size = 0.5 × 0.5 × 1 mm3) for subsequent image registration. Participants were then instructed to sleep, and T2-weighted BOLD fMRI images were acquired using an echo planar imaging sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, FA = 90°, number of slices = 33, slice thickness = 3.5 mm, slice gap = 0.7 mm, matrix = 64 × 64, in-plane resolution = 3.5 × 3.5 mm2, and maximum number of TRs = 4096 in each session, imposed by the Siemens product acquisition). Multiple sleep EEG-fMRI sessions (ranging from 1 to 4 sessions across participants) were performed.

EEG data preprocessing and sleep structure of the EEG-fMRI data

We processed the EEG data using BrainVision Analyzer 2.1 (Brain Products, Munich, Germany). First, MR gradient artifacts in the EEG data were removed using the average artifact subtraction method92. Subsequently, the data were downsampled to 500 Hz after MR gradient artifact correction. Cardiac cycles were detected on the ECG channel with the QRS detection technique93. The positions of the marked R peaks were manually checked and corrected. Ballistocardiogram artifacts were removed using the average artifact subtraction method94. The data were re-referenced to the mean of the channels at the bilateral mastoids and temporally filtered (10–100 Hz for EMG channels and 0.3–35 Hz for the other channels). The physiological stage for the preprocessed EEG data was manually scored for every 30-second epoch according to standard criteria39. Each epoch was labeled as W, NREM sleep stages N1, N2 and N3, or REM sleep. Two research technologists with substantial experience in manual sleep scoring, particularly for stage scoring on polysomnography data simultaneously acquired with fMRI, performed the manual sleep scoring independently. Sleep scorings from Rater 1 were adopted for the main analyses and those from Rater 2 were used for validation analyses.

Functional MRI quality control

The fMRI data underwent manual visual inspection to verify the quality of the raw data. Furthermore, the temporal signal-to-noise ratio (tSNR) was calculated to assess the data quality for each non-overlapping 5-min fMRI data epoch following the initiation of the sleep EEG-fMRI scan regardless of the physiological stages. For each epoch, tSNR was calculated based on the raw fMRI data without any preprocessing, such as realignment, and by dividing the mean signal intensity by the standard deviation of linearly detrended signal intensity values. The mean tSNR for each 5-min epoch was calculated as the average tSNR across all voxels within a brain mask95 generated by 3dmaskave implemented in AFNI. Then the average tSNR value was computed across participants with the initiation of the sleep EEG-fMRI scan aligned. Head motion for each epoch was quantified by maximum head displacement/rotation, mean frame-wise displacement (FD), and the number of volumes with large FD (FD > 0.5 mm). FD of head movement was evaluated as the sum of the absolute values of the 6 motion parameter derivatives96. The meanFD was calculated across volumes within an epoch, and then the average meanFD was computed across participants.

The average tSNR (Fig. 1b) and head motion (Fig. 1c) remained relatively stable throughout the scan. An inverse relationship was observed between average tSNR and average meanFD over the sleep EEG-fMRI scan (r = −0.75, P < 0.0001). Data epochs that met at least one of the following motion criteria were classified as having excessive head motion: (1) maximum head motion >3 mm or 3 degrees, (2) mean FD > 0.4 mm, and (3) more than 45 volumes with FD larger than 0.5 mm. The tSNR of the data epochs that survived the motion criteria was significantly higher than that of those epochs with excessive in-scanner head motion (two-sided paired t-test, t = 48.78, P < 0.0001).

Calculation of normalized power of slow-wave activity (SWA) based on EEG data

To investigate the sleep-related changes in brain activity during sleep, we extracted all available 5-min continuous epochs for each sleep stage. For sleep stages longer than 5 min, the first 5 min formed one epoch, the subsequent 5 min formed another epoch, and this process continued until the remaining duration was less than 5 min. In total, 2591 epochs were extracted, and 2361 epochs that met the motion criteria were included in the analysis. A spectral pipeline (https://eeglab.org/tutorials/11_Scripting/automated_pipeline.html) was adopted to detect bad channels (3.28%, interpolated with neighboring channels), followed by independent component analysis to remove stereotyped muscle and eye components. SWA power (0.75–4 Hz) for each of the 52 available EEG channels across the 130 participants was estimated using Welch’s method with a 4-s Hanning window and 50% overlap for each 5-min epoch. The SWA power of each channel was then log-transformed and used for further analyses23.

fMRI data preprocessing and ALFF calculation

The fMRI data were preprocessed using AFNI and FMRIB Software Library (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/docs/#/). For each of the 2180 5-min fMRI epochs, preprocessing steps included slice timing, realignment (motion correction), and removal of nuisance covariates including Friston-24 head motion parameters97, mean signals in white matter and cerebral spinal fluid, and their first derivatives. Spatial normalization to the International Consortium for Brain Mapping standard template (ICBM152) was performed with a voxel size of 2 × 2 × 2 mm3, using a nonlinear transformation estimated from spatial normalization of the high-resolution T1 image to the same template. The preprocessed fMRI data for each 5-min epoch were then spatially smoothed with a full-width at half-maximum of 6 mm. ALFF is an index that calculates the voxel-wise amplitude of low-frequency BOLD fMRI signals, which provides an indirect measurement of cortical activity28,69. It is used as a biomarker for local changes in low-frequency hemodynamic activity over the cortex across the sleep-wake cycle. ALFF was computed to quantify the average spectral power within the 0.01–0.08 Hz frequency range, following methodologies from previous studies34. As the BOLD signal has arbitrary units; ALFF is sensitive to the scale of the raw BOLD signal. Z-score normalization of each ALFF map is necessary before statistical analyses. An ALFF map was calculated for each 5-min epoch, so that the Z-score of ALFF was calculated at the epoch level. The Z-score map of ALFF was normalized by the epoch mean and standard deviation of ALFF values across the cortex.

Linear mixed-effect modeling on ALFF and SWA

The main effect of sleep stage on ALFF, and the interaction between the accumulated sleep duration and sleep stage on ALFF were evaluated using linear mixed-effect (LME) modeling. LME modeling was chosen due to its ability to accommodate imbalanced data across participants and different levels of variables, such as varying numbers of epochs for different participants, physiological stages, and bins of accumulated sleep duration, which are common in EEG-fMRI data acquired during sleep.

The main effect of sleep stage and the interaction effect between the accumulated sleep duration and sleep stage on voxel-wise ALFF were analyzed using 3dLMEr implemented in AFNI. Statistical values for the main effect and interaction effect were stored in the output as chi-square values with two degrees of freedom. The main effect of stage and the interaction effect on SWA of each channel was assessed with the same LME model using the lme4 package (version 1.1-32; Bates et al. 2015) in R (version 4.2.3). Statistical results on SWA were corrected for the number of channels using Bonferroni correction.

Principal functional gradient generation

The principal functional gradient used in this study was generated in a prior study43. The volumetric principal functional gradient, matching the resolution of the current data (2 × 2 × 2 mm3) in the MNI space, was obtained from a public repository (https://www.neuroconnlab.org/data/) for further analyses.

Extraction of brain metabolism values

Cerebral metabolic rate for glucose (CMRGlu) and cerebral metabolic rate for oxygen (CMRO2) values, representing brain metabolism, were kindly shared by Vaishnavi et al.47 and provided by Markello et al.81 (https://osf.io/4mw3a/). PET imaging was performed on 33 healthy, right-handed adults in a resting, awake state with eyes closed to obtain CMRGlu and CMRO2 maps. Group-average CMRGlu and CMRO2 maps were calculated across all 33 healthy participants. CMRGlu was decomposed into two components: (1) oxidative metabolism, measured by CMRO2, and (2) glycolytic metabolism, calculated as the residuals from linear regression analysis between CMRGlu and CMRO247. These two components were then normalized to Z-scores over the cortex (Fig. 4).

Spatial permutations test for spatial autocorrelation correction

Spatial autocorrelation, where neighboring data points are not statistically independent, violates the assumptions of many common inferential frameworks98, such as computing spatial correlation between brain maps in this study. To ensure statistical inferences accurately reflect the underlying data, it is necessary to generate null distributions preserving the spatial autocorrelation of the brain maps. The spatial permutation test, called the spin test99 (https://brainsmash.readthedocs.io/en/latest/index.html), generates a null distribution of randomly rotated brain maps that preserve spatial covariance structure of the original map.

An independent sleep EEG-fMRI dataset (Dataset 2) and processing

An independent dataset with simultaneous EEG and fMRI data during sleep from 33 healthy participants was used for validation analyses49. EEG and fMRI data were preprocessed in the same way as in the main analyses based on 130 participants. The dataset consisted of two 10-min resting-state sessions and several 15-min sleep sessions for each participant. Since the scanning sessions were shorter and to make the best use of this dataset, we extracted all available 5-min data epochs, and staged each epoch using the winner-take-all strategy. That is, an epoch with at least half of the time spent in sleep was labeled as sleep, and the rest was labeled as wakefulness. ALFF maps from fMRI and SWA topography from EEG were calculated for each epoch. The same statistical analyses as in the main analyses on 130 participants were performed.

Sleep deprivation and recovery dataset (Dataset 3) and processing

Based on strictly controlled in-laboratory sleep deprivation and fMRI protocols, this dataset100 included resting-state fMRI scans from 54 healthy adults at baseline after normal sleep (BS), following one night of total sleep deprivation (TSD), and after recovery sleep (RS). We calculated ALFF maps and characteristics of cross-hierarchy propagations for each participant under BS, TSD, and RS scans, and performed two-sided paired t-tests between TSD and BS, and between RS and TSD.

Partial sleep deprivation dataset (Dataset 4) and processing

The partial sleep deprivation dataset was collected from 86 healthy volunteers using a cross-over comparison between partial sleep deprivation (PSD, 3-h sleep) and baseline sleep (BS)51. Participants were randomized to undergo both conditions in a counterbalanced order, with an interval of approximately one month. MRI imaging was performed in the evening following partial sleep deprivation or baseline sleep. Two resting-state fMRI scans were acquired for each participant during both PSD and BS conditions. We calculated ALFF maps and characteristics of cross-hierarchy propagations for each participant under PSD and BS scans, and performed two-sided paired t-test between these two scans.

Validation of methodological variation and potential confounds

As there is no consensus on whether or not to perform global signal regression (GSR) during resting-state fMRI analysis, we calculated ALFF based on the data with additional GSR preprocessing. Fractional ALFF (fALFF), which is an alternative measure for voxel-wise amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations and shows improved sensitivity and specificity for detecting cortical activity, was adopted52. Regional homogeneity (ReHo) is a characterization of local connectivity and functional homogeneity within a region, calculated by Kendall’s coefficient of concordance of a voxel with its neighboring voxels53. Global brain connectivity (GBC)54, which is a measure of degree centrality calculated as the average functional connectivity of each voxel to other voxels in the cortex, was also adopted. The four maps were normalized to Z-scores for each 5-min epoch, and the same statistical analyses were conducted on these Z-score maps as those on the ALFF maps.

Temporal dynamic patterns demonstrated by cross-hierarchy global waves

We quantified temporal dynamic patterns of low-frequency cortical fluctuations using a strategy similar to the one proposed by Gu and colleagues57. Cortical regions were grouped into 70 position bins of equal size along the functional principal gradient direction. A time-position graph with the average time course of each bin over the 70 bins was generated to represent temporal-spatial dynamic pattern for each 5-min epoch. This time-position was then divided into time segments based on the troughs of the global mean signal over the cortex, and a local peak was identified as the largest peak for each bin in each time segment. The time segment with local peaks located in at least 56 positions (80%) was included in further analyses. For each included segment, Pearson’s correlation was calculated between the time delay relative to the global peak and position order along the principal functional gradient across the 70 bins. Segments with significant (P < 10−5) positive and negative time delay-position correlations were categorized as bottom-up (SM to DMN) and top-down (DMN to SM) propagations. We adopted three characteristics to evaluate the cross-hierarchy global propagations: the width of a global propagation as the duration of that time segment, fractional occurrence as the ratio of the total durations of the global propagations to 5 min, and the amplitude of a propagation as the peak amplitude relative to the average of two neighboring troughs. We obtained the average amplitude and width of cross-hierarchy propagations for each epoch. The same statistical analyses were conducted on the three characteristics of the cross-hierarchy propagations as on the ALFF maps. All the results were corrected using Bonferroni correction, i.e., original P < 0.05/6 were considered significant.

Summary of sample size of the datasets and in the analyses

Taken together, there are four datasets used in the current study, collected from four independent cohorts, separately.

The large sleep EEG-fMRI dataset (Dataset 1; all the analyses and results refer to this dataset if not specified) had 173 participants came for screening, 43 of them were excluded due to contraindications to MRI, data collection failure with simultaneous recording, poor quality of EEG data, or difficulty to sleep in the fMRI environment. Data from 130 participants were used for investigating brain activity during sleep and wakefulness and sleep-related changes with dissipated sleep pressure, as well as their spatial correlations with metabolic measures. The inter-participant correlation analysis between sleep-related changes in ALFF and sleep pressure were based on data from 125 participants with at least one epoch of both sleep and wakefulness.

The independent sleep EEG-fMRI dataset (Dataset 2) were collected from a cohort of 40 participants, 7 of them were with incomplete data or had technical problems during data collection. All the analyses were conducted on the remaining 33 participants. To note, the inter-participant correction analysis between sleep-related changes in ALFF and sleep pressure were based on data from 27 participants with at least one epoch of both sleep and wakefulness.

The sleep deprivation and recovery dataset (Dataset 3) were collected from a cohort of 54 participants, 22 of them showed drowsiness or excessive head motion during at least one of three scans. All the analyses were conducted on the data from the remaining 32 participants.

The partial sleep deprivation dataset (Dataset 4) were collected from 86 participants, 77 of them with two resting-state fMRI scans during both baseline and sleep deprivation conditions. Twelve additional participants were excluded due to excessive motion during at least one of the conditions. All the analyses were conducted on the data from the remaining 65 participants.
