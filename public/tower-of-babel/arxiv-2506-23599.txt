Truncated symbols of differential symmetry breaking operators
Toshihisa Kubo, Víctor Pérez-Valdés

Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce the truncated symbol symb 0 ​ ( 𝔻 ) \mathrm{symb}_{0}(\mathbb{D}) of a differential symmetry breaking operator 𝔻 \mathbb{D} between parabolically induced representations, which generalizes the symbol symb ⁡ ( 𝔻 ) \mathrm{symb}(\mathbb{D}) for abelian nilpotent radicals to the non-abelian case. The inverse symb 0 − 1 \mathrm{symb}_{0}^{-1} of the truncated symbol map symb 0 \mathrm{symb}_{0} enables one to perform a recipe of the F-method for any nilpotent radical. As an application, we classify and construct differential intertwining operators 𝒟 \mathcal{D} on the full flag variety S ​ L ​ ( 3 , ℝ ) / B SL(3,\mathbb{R})/B and homomorphisms φ \varphi between Verma modules. It turned out that, surprisingly, Cayley continuants Cay m ​ ( x , y ) \mathrm{Cay}_{m}(x;y) appeared in the coefficients of one of the five families of operators that we constructed. At the end, the factorization identities of the differential operators 𝒟 \mathcal{D} and homomorphisms φ \varphi are also classified. Binary Krawtchouk polynomials K m ​ ( x , y ) K_{m}(x;y) play a key role in the proof.

1.
Introduction

Differential symmetry breaking operators (DSBOs)
\mathbb{D}
are differential

G^{\prime}
-intertwining operators from a representation of
G
to that of
G^{\prime}

for a pair of Lie groups
G\supseteq G^{\prime}
.
The classification and construction of DSBOs
\mathbb{D}
are in general
very difficult problems. In case that

G\supset G^{\prime}
are a pair of reductive Lie groups and
that the representations of
G
and
G^{\prime}
are
both parabolically induced representations,
one can apply a stunning machinery called
F-method
to solve the problems.
Loosely speaking,
via a certain Fourier transform
F_{c}
,
the F-method converts
the problem of the classification and construction of DSBOs
\mathbb{D}

into that of solving a system of partial differential equations over
polynomials
\psi(\zeta)\in\mathrm{Pol}({\mathfrak{n}}_{+})

on the nilpotent radical
{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}
of a parabolic subalgebra
{\mathfrak{p}}={\mathfrak{l}}\oplus{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}
.
Since its invention by T. Kobayashi around 2010,
the F-method has been intensively used to study DSBOs
\mathbb{D}

(cf.
[
6
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
17
,
25
,
26
,
28
]
).

One of the key ideas
of the F-method is the symbol map
\mathrm{symb}
,
which effectively transforms the polynomial solutions
\psi(\zeta)
to
the system of differential equations into DSBOs
\mathbb{D}
.
Nevertheless, it is defined only in the case that the nilpotent radical

{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}
is abelian (
[
12
, Sect. 4.2]
).
In this paper, we introduce
a variant of the symbol map, which we call
the
truncated symbol map

\mathrm{symb}_{0}
, to overcome this issue.
As an application to the truncated symbol map
\mathrm{symb}_{0}
,
we take
G=G^{\prime}=SL(3,\mathbb{R})
and consider
DSBOs
\mathbb{D}
between parabolically induced
representations for
(SL(3,\mathbb{R}),B)
,
where
B
is a Borel subgroup of
SL(3,\mathbb{R})
.

In the following, we shall discuss the F-method and truncated
symbol map
\mathrm{symb}_{0}
,
the DSBOs
\mathbb{D}
for
(SL(3,\mathbb{R}),B)
, and
a relationship between our results on DSBOs
\mathbb{D}
and
the ones on
the homomorphisms
\varphi
between Verma modules in the literature in order.

We remark that, to distinguish the symmetry breaking setting

G\supseteq G^{\prime}
from the classical one
G=G^{\prime}
,
a DSBO
\mathbb{D}
in the latter case will be referred to as
a differential intertwining operator (DIO)
\mathcal{D}
in the rest of the paper.

1.1.
F-method and truncated symbol map
\mathrm{symb}_{0}

In principle, the F-method can be applied no matter what
the nilpotent radical
{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}
is abelian or not;
nevertheless, there is a certain difficulty for the non-abelian case.
To compute DSBOs
\mathbb{D}
, one needs to evaluate
the inverse image of
F_{c}^{-1}(\psi(\zeta))
of the Fourier transform
F_{c}
.
The problem is that the inverse image
F_{c}^{-1}(\psi(\zeta))
is not apparent
unless the nilpotent radical
{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}
is abelian.
Then, in this paper,
we first grappled with this issue and successfully resolved it.
This is done in Section
3.3
.
A key technique is to consider the symmetrization of
the symmetric algebra
S({\mathfrak{n}}_{-})
to the universal enveloping algebra

\mathcal{U}({\mathfrak{n}}_{-})
of the opposite nilpotent radical
{\mathfrak{n}}_{-}
.
This in particular allows one to carry out a recipe of the F-method
for any nilpotent radical
as in the abelian setting
[
12
, Sect. 4.4]
.

The elucidation of the inverse image
F_{c}^{-1}(\psi(\zeta))

leads us to the notion of the aforementioned symbol

\mathrm{symb}_{0}(\mathbb{D})
of
\mathbb{D}
.
This is the symbol
of the constant coefficient part
\mathbb{D}_{0}
of
\mathbb{D}
in the usual sense.
For instance, we shall show in Example
4.30
that
the differential operator

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}=\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}\partial x_{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{3}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}x_{2}\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial x_{3}^{3}}+x_{2}\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{2}\partial x_{3}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}x_{2}^{2}\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial x_{2}\partial x_{3}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}x_{1}\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}\partial x_{3}}-\frac{1}{2}x_{1}x_{2}\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{3}^{2}}-\frac{1}{8}x_{1}x_{2}^{2}\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial x_{3}^{3}}

is a DSBO (or DIO) between parabolically induced representations
for
(SL(3,\mathbb{R}),B)
.
The truncated symbol
\mathrm{symb}_{0}(\mathbb{D})
is then given by

\mathrm{symb}_{0}({\mathbb{D}})=\zeta_{1}^{2}\zeta_{2}+\zeta_{1}\zeta_{3}.

By the intertwining property,
the DSBO
\mathbb{D}
is determined at the identity
e
,
which in principle allows one to recover
\mathbb{D}
from
\mathrm{symb}_{0}(\mathbb{D})
.
By definition, if the nilpotent radical
{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}
is abelian,
then
\mathrm{symb}_{0}(\mathbb{D})=\mathrm{symb}(\mathbb{D})

as
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{D}_{0}
in the case (cf.
[
21
, Sect. 2.8]
).
We shall discuss the details in Section
3.4
.

Remark that Křižka–Somberg
also gave
F_{c}^{-1}(\psi(\zeta))
in their formulation
in
[
17
]
. Their proof is, however, different from ours.
For instance, their proof requires some brute-force computations,
whereas ours doesn’t. We would also like to note that the truncated symbol

\mathrm{symb}_{0}{(\mathbb{D}})
is not discussed in their paper.

1.2.
Differential intertwining operators
\mathcal{D}
for
(SL(3,\mathbb{R}),B)

As an application to the truncated symbol map
\mathrm{symb}_{0}
,
we provide the classification of DIOs
\mathcal{D}

for the pair
(SL(3,\mathbb{R}),B)
with their explicit formulas.
This is accomplished
in Theorem
4.11
for
the classification of the parameters

(\varepsilon,\delta;\lambda,\nu)\in(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{4}\times\mathbb{C}^{4}
,
for which the space
\mathrm{Diff}_{G}(I(\lambda)^{(\varepsilon)},I(\nu)^{(\delta)})

of DIOs
\mathcal{D}
is non-zero.
The classification of DIOs
\mathcal{D}
with explicit formulas
is obtained in Theorem
4.17
.

Our results show that the DIOs
\mathcal{D}
other than the identity map
\mathrm{id}

are classified as the following
five families of operators:

\mathcal{D}_{1}^{k},\quad\mathcal{D}_{2}^{k},\quad\mathcal{D}_{+}^{(k,\ell)},\quad\mathcal{D}_{-}^{(k,\ell)},\quad\mathcal{D}_{c}^{(s;k)},

where
k,\ell\in 1+\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
s\in\mathbb{C}
.
By a celebrated theorem of BGG–Verma, it is well-known that
every DIOs
\mathcal{D}
is associated with a Weyl group element.
If
\alpha,\beta,\gamma=\alpha+\beta
denote the positive roots of
\mathfrak{sl}(3,\mathbb{C})
,
then the corresponding Weyl group elements are given as follows:

\mathcal{D}_{j}^{k}\;(j=1,2)\,\leftrightarrow s_{\alpha},s_{\beta},\qquad\mathcal{D}_{\pm}^{(k,\ell)}\leftrightarrow s_{\alpha}s_{\beta},s_{\beta}s_{\alpha},\qquad\mathcal{D}_{c}^{(s;k)}\leftrightarrow s_{\gamma},

(1.1)

where
s_{\alpha},s_{\beta},s_{\gamma}
are
the root reflections with respect to
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
, respectively.

In relation to their formulas,
the first two operators

\mathcal{D}_{j}^{k}
for
j=1,2

are given by powers of root vectors for negative simple roots.
The next two
\mathcal{D}_{\pm}^{(k,\ell)}
are characterized as the ones
having products of two binomial coefficients in their coefficients.
The last operator
\mathcal{D}_{c}^{(s;k)}
, which can be thought of as
the most “exotic” operator, has
so-called Cayley continuants
\{\mathrm{Cay}_{m}(x;y)\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
,
a series of tridiagonal determinants studied by Cayley in 1858
(cf.
[
2
]
and
[
24
, Intro.]
), in its coefficients.
Remark that the “Cayley operator”
\mathcal{D}_{c}^{(s;k)}
is defined
for a continuous parameter
s\in\mathbb{C}
.
Although there are a number of studies for DIOs
\mathcal{D}

in the form of homomorphisms between Verma modules,
none of them seems to identify
the classical tridiagonal determinants (see Section
1.3
).
We shall discuss in detail
the explicit formulas of
\mathcal{D}
in Section
4.3

and
the correspondence (
1.1
) in Section
4.4
.
Further, it is also shown in Section
7

that the coefficients of
\mathcal{D}_{\pm}^{(k,\ell)}
and
\mathcal{D}_{c}^{(s;k)}
are
given in terms of the values of Jacobi polynomials
P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{m}(z)

at
z=0
. The uniform expressions play a role to study factorization identities
of
\mathcal{D}
in the degenerated cases (see
Remark
7.15
and Cases (5) and (6) of
Theorems
8.8
and
8.14
).

Remark that
our families of operators include some differential operators
constructed by Barchini–Kable–Zierau
[
1
]
, Kable
[
7
]
,
and the first author
[
19
]
. For instance, Kable
constructed a second order differential operator
\square_{s}^{(n)}

with complex parameter
s\in\mathbb{C}
for
(\mathfrak{sl}(n,\mathbb{C}),{\mathfrak{p}}_{\text{Heis}})

in
[
7
]
, where
{\mathfrak{p}}_{\text{Heis}}={\mathfrak{l}}\oplus{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}

is the parabolic subalgebra of
\mathfrak{sl}(n,\mathbb{C})
with Heisenberg nilpotent radical
{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}
.
The operator
\square_{s}^{(3)}
with
n=3

is exactly the Cayley operator
\mathcal{D}_{c}^{(s;1)}
with
k=1
.

The DIOs
\mathcal{D}
associated with
(\mathfrak{sl}(n,\mathbb{C}),{\mathfrak{p}}_{\text{Heis}})
are also studied by
Křižka–Somberg in connection with a branching law
of generalized Verma modules.
In
[
17
]
, they made some attempts to construct
DIOs
\mathcal{D}
for
(\mathfrak{sl}(n,\mathbb{C}),{\mathfrak{p}}_{\text{Heis}})

with
n>4

and DSBOs
\mathbb{D}
for

(\mathfrak{sl}(n,\mathbb{C}),\mathfrak{sl}(n-r,\mathbb{C});{\mathfrak{p}}_{\text{Heis}},{\mathfrak{p}}^{\prime}_{\text{Heis}})

with
n-r>4
,
where
{\mathfrak{p}}^{\prime}_{\text{Heis}}
is the parabolic subalgebra with Heisenberg
nilpotent radical of
\mathfrak{sl}(n-1,\mathbb{C})
.
Their expressions are, nevertheless, not fully explicit.
Concrete expressions are provided for some degree-two cases
(
[
17
, Examples 3.6]
).

Recently, Ditlevsen–Labriet
[
5
]
studies DSBOs
\mathbb{D}

for
(G,G^{\prime})=(GL(3,\mathbb{R}),GL(2,\mathbb{R}))
with
(B,B^{\prime})
their Borel subgroups,
respectively, from a distribution point of view.
They considered several
embeddings
\iota\colon G^{\prime}/B^{\prime}\hookrightarrow G/B

and constructed DSBOs
\mathbb{D}
with respect to each
embedding
\iota
.
A study of DSBOs
\mathbb{D}
for the pair in light
of the F-method will be reported on elsewhere.

1.3.
Homomorphisms
\varphi
between Verma modules and
factorization identities

It is well-known that the category of DIOs
\mathcal{D}
between parabolically induced
representations for a pair
(G,P)

is equivalent to that of
({\mathfrak{g}},P)
-homomorphisms
\varphi

between the corresponding generalized Verma modules for

({\mathfrak{g}},{\mathfrak{p}})

(see, for instance,
[
3
,
12
,
15
,
16
]
).
Here
{\mathfrak{g}}
and
{\mathfrak{p}}
denote the complexified Lie algebras of
G
and
P
,
respectively.
Thus, we classify and construct homomorphisms
\varphi

between Verma modules corresponding to DIOs
\mathcal{D}
as well.
These are achieved in Theorems
4.12
and
4.18
.

Some homomorphisms
\varphi\colon M(\mu_{3})\to M(\mu_{1})

may admit an identity

\varphi=\varphi_{1}\circ\varphi_{2}

with
\varphi_{1}\colon M(\mu_{2})\to M(\mu_{1})
and

\varphi_{2}\colon M(\mu_{3})\to M(\mu_{2})
.
Such an identity is called a
factorization identity
.
We shall show the factorization identities of our homomorphisms
\varphi

in Section
8
.
It is noteworthy that
a binomial Krawtchouk polynomial
K_{m}(x;y)
,
which is an incarnation of a Cayley continuant
\mathrm{Cay}_{m}(x;y)
,
appears in the proof.

As Verma modules are fundamental objects in the representation theory of
finite-dimensional complex simple Lie algebras,
there are already a number of works for the construction
of the homomorphisms
\varphi
between Verma modules,
or more precisely,
the explicit formulas of singular vectors.
Those include the works of
Malikov–Feǐgin–Fuks
[
23
]
,
de Graaf
[
4
]
,
Xu
[
33
,
34
]
,
and
Xiao
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
, among others.
For instance,
Malikov–Feǐgin–Fuks
[
23
]
studied
explicit formulas of singular vectors of Verma modules over Kac–Moody algebras.
They, in particular, gave singular vectors for
\mathfrak{sl}(n,\mathbb{C})
.
Remark that the Cayley continuants
\mathrm{Cay}_{m}(s;k)
do not appear
in any of the aforementioned works. A key difference between ours and theirs
is symmetrization; they fix a certain order of root vectors to obtain
the singular vectors, while we take symmetrization of them.
The little trick of symmetrization reveals the tridiagonal determinants
hidden in the coefficients.

1.4.
Organization of the paper

In the first two sections, Sections

,

.

\mathrm{symb}_{0}^{-1}\colon\mathrm{Sol}(\mathfrak{n}_{+};V,W)\mathrel{{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}}\operatorname{Diff}_{G^{\prime}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})

in (
3.28
) and Theorem
3.29
.
The truncated symbol map
\mathrm{symb}_{0}
is then discussed in Section
3.4
.
At the end of the section, we give a recipe of the F-method with
the inverse map
\mathrm{symb}_{0}^{-1}
,

The aim of Section
4
is to state our main results on
the classification and construction of DIOs
\mathcal{D}

and
({\mathfrak{g}},B)
-homomorphisms
\varphi
between Verma modules
for
(SL(3,\mathbb{R}),B)
.
The main results are stated in Theorems
4.11
and
4.12

for classification of the parameters and Theorems
4.17
and
4.18

for explicit formulas.
We also summarize
the homomorphisms

in terms of infinitesimal characters
in Theorem
4.21
.

Sections
5
and
6
are devoted to the proof
of the theorems in Section
4

We proceed with the first half of the steps in Section
5
and

6
.

, we discuss about some uniform expressions

Preliminaries: the F-method

\mathrm{symb}_{0}(\mathbb{D})

of a DSBO
\mathbb{D}
.
In this section we mainly take the expositions from
[
18
,
21
]
.

2.1.
Notation

Let
G
be a real reductive Lie group and
P=MAN_{+}
a Langlands decomposition of a parabolic subgroup
P
of
G
. We denote by
{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathbb{R})
and

{\mathfrak{p}}(\mathbb{R})={\mathfrak{m}}(\mathbb{R})\oplus{\mathfrak{a}}(\mathbb{R})\oplus{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
the Lie algebras of
G
and

P=MAN_{+}
, respectively.

For a real Lie algebra
\mathfrak{y}(\mathbb{R})
, we write
\mathfrak{y}

and
\mathcal{U}({\mathfrak{y}})
for its complexification and the universal enveloping algebra of

{\mathfrak{y}}
, respectively.
For instance,
{\mathfrak{g}},{\mathfrak{p}},{\mathfrak{m}},{\mathfrak{a}}
, and
{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}
are the complexifications of
{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathbb{R}),{\mathfrak{p}}(\mathbb{R}),{\mathfrak{m}}(\mathbb{R}),{\mathfrak{a}}(\mathbb{R})
, and
{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
,
respectively.

For
\lambda\in{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}\simeq\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathfrak{a}}(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{C})
,
we denote by
\mathbb{C}_{\lambda}

the one-dimensional representation of
A
defined by

a\mapsto a^{\lambda}:=e^{\lambda(\log a)}
.
For a finite-dimensional irreducible
representation
(\xi,V)
of
M
and
\lambda\in{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}
,
we denote by
\xi_{\lambda}
the outer tensor product
representation
\xi\boxtimes\mathbb{C}_{\lambda}

on
V
,
namely,

\xi_{\lambda}\colon ma\mapsto a^{\lambda}\xi(m)
. By letting
N_{+}
act trivially, we regard

\xi_{\lambda}
as a representation of
P
. Let
\mathcal{V}:=G\times_{P}V\to G/P

be the
G
-equivariant vector bundle over the real flag variety
G/P

associated with the
representation
(\xi_{\lambda},V)
of
P
. We identify the Fréchet space

C^{\infty}(G/P,\mathcal{V})
of smooth sections with

C^{\infty}(G,V)^{P}:=\{f\in C^{\infty}(G,V):f(gp)=\xi_{\lambda}^{-1}(p)f(g)\;\;\text{for any $p\in P$}\},

the space of
P
-invariant smooth functions on
G
.
Then, via the left regular representation
L
of
G
on
C^{\infty}(G)
,
we realize the parabolically induced representation

\pi_{(\xi,\lambda)}=\mathrm{Ind}_{P}^{G}(\xi_{\lambda})
on
C^{\infty}(G/P,\mathcal{V})
.
We denote by
R
the right regular representation of
G
on
C^{\infty}(G)
.

Let
G^{\prime}
be a reductive subgroup of
G
and
P^{\prime}=M^{\prime}A^{\prime}N_{+}^{\prime}

a parabolic subgroup of
G^{\prime}

with
P^{\prime}\subset P
,
M^{\prime}A^{\prime}\subset MA
, and
N_{+}^{\prime}\subset N_{+}
,
so that there is a natural morphism
p\colon G^{\prime}/P^{\prime}\to G/P

from the real flag variety
G^{\prime}/P^{\prime}
into
G/P
.

As for
G/P
, for a finite-dimensional irreducible
representation
(\varpi_{\nu},W)
of
M^{\prime}A^{\prime}
, we define
an induced representation
\pi^{\prime}_{(\varpi,\nu)}=\mathrm{Ind}_{P^{\prime}}^{G^{\prime}}(\varpi_{\nu})
on the
space
C^{\infty}(G^{\prime}/P^{\prime},\mathcal{W})
of smooth sections for the

G^{\prime}
-equivariant vector bundle
\mathcal{W}:=G^{\prime}\times_{P^{\prime}}W\to G^{\prime}/P^{\prime}
.

Via the morphism
p\colon G^{\prime}/P^{\prime}\to G/P
, one can define
differential operators
\mathbb{D}\colon C^{\infty}(G/P,\mathcal{V})\to C^{\infty}(G^{\prime}/P^{\prime},\mathcal{W})

with respect to
p

although
G/P
and
G^{\prime}/P^{\prime}
are different manifolds
(see, for instance,
[
12
, Def. 2.1]
).
As
C^{\infty}(G/P,\mathcal{V})
is a
G
-representation and
G^{\prime}\subset G
,
the space
C^{\infty}(G/P,\mathcal{V})
is also a
G^{\prime}
-representation.
We then write
\mathrm{Diff}_{G^{\prime}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})
for the space of

differential symmetry breaking operators

(differential
G^{\prime}
-intertwining operators)

\mathbb{D}\colon C^{\infty}(G/P,\mathcal{V})\to C^{\infty}(G^{\prime}/P^{\prime},\mathcal{W})
.
When
G=G^{\prime}
and
P=P^{\prime}
, we also simply refer to
them as
differential intertwining operators

(or intertwining differential operators).

Let

\mathfrak{g}(\mathbb{R})=\mathfrak{n}_{-}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathfrak{m}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathfrak{a}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{+}(\mathbb{R})

be the
Gelfand–Naimark decomposition of
\mathfrak{g}(\mathbb{R})

with
{\mathfrak{p}}(\mathbb{R})=\mathfrak{m}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathfrak{a}(\mathbb{R})\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
,
and write
N_{-}=\exp({\mathfrak{n}}_{-}(\mathbb{R}))
. We identify
N_{-}
with the
open Bruhat cell
N_{-}P
of
G/P
via the embedding

\iota\colon N_{-}\hookrightarrow G/P
,
\bar{n}\mapsto\bar{n}P
.
Via the restriction of the vector bundle
\mathcal{V}\to G/P
to the open Bruhat cell

N_{-}\mathrel{{\mathop{\hookrightarrow}\limits}}G/P
,
we regard
C^{\infty}(G/P,\mathcal{V})
as a subspace of

C^{\infty}(N_{-})\otimes V
.

Theorem 3.38

\diamond

m=0:

,

\diamond

\mathrm{Cay}_{1}(x;y)=x

\diamond

\mathrm{Cay}_{m}(x;y)=\small\begin{vmatrix}x&1&&&&\\
y&x&2&&&\\
&y-1&x&3&&\\
&&\dots&\dots&\dots&\\
&&&y-m+3&x&m-1\\
&&&&y-m+2&x\end{vmatrix}

The polynomial

is also given as

\mathrm{Cay}_{m}(x;y)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{m}{j}\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)^{\underline{j}}\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right)^{\overline{j}},

where
r^{\underline{j}}
and

denote the
falling factorial and rising factorial, respectively, namely,

\displaystyle r^{\underline{j}}

\displaystyle:=r(r-1)(r-2)\cdots(r-j+1)=\frac{\Gamma(r+1)}{\Gamma(r-j+1)},

\displaystyle r^{\overline{j}}

\displaystyle:=r(r+1)(r+2)\cdots(r+j-1)=\frac{\Gamma(r+j)}{\Gamma(r)}.

(See, for instance,
[
24

Let

and

\varpi_{i}(H_{j})=\delta_{i,j}
for

in (

).
Then the differential

d\chi_{A}^{(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})}
of the character

\chi_{A}^{(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})}

d\chi_{A}^{(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})}=\mu_{1}\varpi_{1}+\mu_{2}\varpi_{2}.

(4.19)

Therefore the Verma module

defined in
(
4.5
)
can be be understood as

Now, for
\mu\in{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}
, we denote by

N(\mu):=\mathcal{U}({\mathfrak{g}})\otimes_{\mathcal{U}({\mathfrak{b}})}\mathbb{C}_{\mu-\rho},

the Verma module with infinitesimal character
\chi_{\mu}
.
As
\rho=\varpi_{1}+\varpi_{2}
,
for
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
, we have

M(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=N(\mu_{1}+1,\mu_{2}+1).

We wish to state Theorem
4.18
in terms of
N(\mu)
.
To do so,
it is convenient to identify
{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}
with

{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}=\{(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})\in\mathbb{C}^{3}:v_{1}+v_{2}+v_{3}=0\}
.
In the realization we have

\varpi_{1}=\tfrac{1}{3}(2,-1,-1)\quad\text{and}\quad\varpi_{2}=\tfrac{1}{3}(1,1,-2);

in particular,
\rho=\varpi_{1}+\varpi_{2}=(1,0,-1)
.

In this notation, the weight
\mu_{1}\varpi_{1}+\mu_{2}\varpi_{2}+\rho
is given by

\mu_{1}\varpi_{1}+\mu_{2}\varpi_{2}+\rho=\tfrac{1}{3}(2\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+3,-\mu_{1}+\mu_{2},-\mu_{1}-2\mu_{2}-3).

Then we put

\displaystyle\mu_{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=-(\lambda_{1}\varpi_{1}+\lambda_{2}\varpi_{2})+\rho

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}(-(2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}-3),\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2},\lambda_{1}+2\lambda_{2}-3),

which gives

\displaystyle M(-\lambda_{1},-\lambda_{2})^{(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})}

\displaystyle=N(-\lambda_{1}+1,-\lambda_{2}+1)^{(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})}

\displaystyle\equiv N(\mu_{\lambda})^{(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})},

where the identification
{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}\subset\mathbb{C}^{3}
is applied from the first
line to the second.

For Theorem
4.21
below, we put

\alpha:=\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}=(1,-1,0),\quad\beta:=\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{3}=(0,1,-1),\quad\gamma:=\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{3}=(1,0,-1).

.

.

\displaystyle\colon N(s_{\beta}s_{\alpha}\mu_{\lambda})^{((-)^{2-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}},\,(-1)^{1-\lambda_{1}})}

that,

7.
Uniform expressions of
p_{\pm}^{(k,\ell)}(t)
and
p_{c}^{(s;k)}(t)

by Jacobi polynomials
P_{m}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(z)
at
z=0

In this short section we aim to show that
the key polynomials
p_{\pm}^{(k,\ell)}(t)
and
p_{c}^{(s;k)}(t)
obtained in
Section
6
can be uniformly expressed by Jacobi polynomials

P_{m}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(z)
for certain parameters
\alpha,\beta
at
z=0
.
This is accomplished in Theorem
7.5
.
We also discuss that
p_{c}(s;k)
can be
given also by binary Krawtchouk polynomials
K_{m}(x;y)
,
which play a role in the factorization identities in Section
8
.

7.1.
Jacobi polynomials
P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{m}(z)

First, we briefly recall some basic properties of
Jacobi polynomials
P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{m}(z)
.
For
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we write

P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{m}(z):=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{m+\alpha}{m-j}\binom{m+\beta}{j}\left(\frac{z-1}{2}\right)^{j}\left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right)^{m-j}.

The polynomials
P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{m}(z)
are called
Jacobi polynomials

(cf.
[
29
, p. 68]
).

The Jacobi polynomials
P_{m}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(z)
satisfy the following
generating function:

(1+\frac{z+1}{2}w)^{\alpha}(1+\frac{z-1}{2}w)^{\beta}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}P_{m}^{(\alpha-m,\beta-m)}(z)w^{m}.

(7.1)

In particular, if
z=0
and
w=2t
, then

(1+t)^{\alpha}(1-t)^{\beta}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}2^{m}P_{m}^{(\alpha-m,\beta-m)}(0)t^{m}.

(7.2)

It follows from the generating function (
7.1
) that

P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{m}(-z)=(-1)^{m}P_{m}^{(\beta,\alpha)}(z).

(7.3)

In particular,

P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{m}(0)=(-1)^{m}P_{m}^{(\beta,\alpha)}(0).

(7.4)

7.2.
Uniform expressions by
P_{m}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(z)

Now we show that the polynomials

p_{\pm}^{(k,\ell)}(t)
and
p_{c}^{(s;k)}(t)
are given in terms of
Jacobi polynomials
P_{m}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(z)
.

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
s\in\mathbb{C}
.
Then
p_{\pm}^{(k,\ell)}(t)
and
p_{c}^{(s;k)}(t)
can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle p_{+}^{(k,\ell)}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}k^{\underline{m}}\,P_{m}^{(\ell-m,\,-m)}(0)t^{m}&\text{if $k\geq\ell$},\\[5.0pt]
\sum_{m=0}^{k}\ell^{\underline{m}}\,P_{m}^{(k-m,\,-m)}(0)t^{m}&\text{if $k<\ell$},\end{cases}

(7.6)

\displaystyle p_{-}^{(k,\ell)}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}k^{\underline{m}}\,P_{m}^{(-m,\,\ell-m)}(0)t^{m}&\text{if $k\geq\ell$},\\[5.0pt]
\sum_{m=0}^{k}\ell^{\underline{m}}\,P_{m}^{(-m,\,k-m)}(0)t^{m}&\text{if $k<\ell$},\end{cases}

(7.7)

\displaystyle p_{c}^{(s;k)}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{k}k^{\underline{m}}\,P_{m}^{(\frac{k+s-2m}{2},\,\frac{k-s-2m}{2})}(0)t^{m},

(7.8)

where
r^{\underline{m}}:=r(r-1)(r-2)\cdots(r-m+1)=\frac{\Gamma(r+1)}{\Gamma(r-m+1)}
. In particular, we have

p_{c}^{(\pm k;k)}(t)=p_{\pm}^{(k,k)}(t).

(7.9)

Proof.

The identity (
7.9
) readily follows from
(
7.6
), (
7.7
), and (
7.8
).
Thus, we only focus on showing the latter three identities.

We start with the proof for (
7.6
).
Without loss of generality, we assume
k\geq\ell
. Thus,

p_{+}^{(k,\ell)}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\frac{1}{2^{m}}m!\binom{k}{m}\binom{\ell}{m}t^{m}=\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\frac{k^{\underline{m}}}{2^{m}}\binom{\ell}{m}t^{m}.

(7.10)

Observe that the polynomial
(1+t)^{\ell}
is expanded as

(1+t)^{\ell}=\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}t^{m},

(7.11)

which is the case for
(\alpha,\beta)=(\ell,0)
in (
7.2
). Therefore,

\binom{\ell}{m}=2^{m}\cdot P_{m}^{(\ell-m,-m)}(0).

(7.12)

Now (
7.12
) and (
7.10
) conclude the proposed identity (
7.6
).
The identity (
7.7
) for
p_{-}^{(k,\ell)}(t)
is obtained by applying
(
7.4
) to (
7.6
).

To show (
7.8
), recall from (
6.3
) that

p_{c}^{(s;k)}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\frac{1}{2^{m}}\binom{k}{m}\mathrm{Cay}_{m}(s;k)t^{m}=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\frac{1}{2^{m}}\frac{k^{\underline{m}}}{m!}\mathrm{Cay}_{m}(s;k)t^{m}.

(7.13)

It is known that
the generating function of Cayley continuants
\{\mathrm{Cay}_{m}(x;y)\}

is given by

(1+t)^{(\frac{y+x}{2},\frac{y-x}{2})}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\mathrm{Cay}_{m}(x;y)t^{m},

which is the case for
(\alpha,\beta)=(\frac{y+x}{2},\frac{y-x}{2})
in (
7.2
)
(cf.
[
24
, (4)]
). Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cay}_{m}(s;k)

\displaystyle=m!\cdot 2^{m}\cdot P_{m}^{(\frac{k+s-2m}{2},\frac{k-s-2m}{2})}(0).

(7.14)

The formula (
7.8
) is then given by substituting (
7.14
) into (
7.13
).
∎

Remark 7.15
.

The identity (
7.9
)
plays a role in the degenerate cases of
the factorization identities of IDO
\mathcal{D}

and
({\mathfrak{g}},B)
-homomorphisms
\varphi
.
(See Cases (5) and (6) of
Theorems
8.8
and
8.14
.)

For
x,y\in\mathbb{C}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we write

K_{m}(x;y):=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{j}\binom{x}{j}\binom{y-x}{m-j}.

(7.16)

The polynomials
K_{m}(x;y)
are called (binary) Krawtchouk polynomials
(cf.
[
22
, p. 151]
and
[
27
]
).

Corollary 7.17
.

For
k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, the following hold.

\displaystyle p_{c}^{(k-2\ell;k)}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\frac{1}{2^{m}}k^{\underline{m}}K_{m}(\ell;k)t^{m},

(7.18)

\displaystyle p_{c}^{(2k-\ell;\ell)}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{2^{m}}\ell^{\underline{m}}K_{m}(k;\ell)t^{m}.

(7.19)

Proof.

To show (
7.18
),
observe that if
s=k-2\ell
, then

\tfrac{1}{2}(k+s-2m)=k-\ell-m\quad\text{and}\quad\tfrac{1}{2}(k-s-2m)=\ell-m.

Then, by Theorem
7.5
, we have

p_{c}^{(k-2\ell;k)}(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{k}k^{\underline{m}}P_{m}^{(k-\ell-m,\ell-m)}(0)t^{m}.

(7.20)

On the other hand, it follows from (
7.16
) that

K_{m}(x;y)
satisfies the following generating function:

(1+t)^{y-x}(1-t)^{x}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}K_{m}(x;y)t^{m},

(7.21)

which is the case for
(\alpha,\beta)=(y-x,x)
in (
7.2
). Thus,

K_{m}(\ell;k)=2^{m}\cdot P_{m}^{(k-\ell-m,\ell-m)}(0).

(7.22)

Now (
7.20
) and (
7.22
) yield the desired formula.

Likewise, if
s=2k-\ell
, then the same argument shows that

\displaystyle p_{c}^{(2k-\ell;\ell)}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\ell^{\underline{m}}P_{m}^{(k-m,\ell-k-m)}(0)t^{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\ell^{\underline{m}}(-1)^{m}P_{m}^{(\ell-k-m,k-m)}(0)t^{m},

where (
7.4
) is applied from line one to line two.
The identity
K_{m}(k;\ell)=2^{m}\cdot P_{m}^{(\ell-k-m,k-m)}(0)

then concludes the other formula (
7.19
).
∎

We end this section by showing an identity on the binary
Krawtchouk polynomials
K_{m}(x;y)
, which will
be used in the proof of the factorization identities in the next section.

Lemma 7.23
.

For
x,y\in\mathbb{C}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we have

\binom{x}{m}=(-1)^{m}\sum^{m}_{r=0}\binom{x-y}{m-r}K_{r}(x;y).

(7.24)

Proof.

Observe that

(1-t)^{x}=(1+t)^{x-y}(1+t)^{y-x}(1-t)^{x},

which implies that

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{m}\binom{x}{m}t^{m}=\big(\sum_{u=0}^{\infty}\binom{x-y}{u}t^{u}\big)\big(\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}K_{r}(x;y)t^{r}\big)

by (
7.11
) and (
7.21
).
Now take the coefficients of
t^{m}
on the both sides to conclude (
7.24
).
∎

The aim of this section is to show the factorization identities
of the differential intertwining operators
\mathcal{D}
and

({\mathfrak{g}},B)
-homomorphisms
\varphi
.
Such identities are accomplished
in Theorems
8.8
and
8.14

for
\varphi
and

8.1.
General theory of homomorphisms between Verma modules

We begin with a quick overview of a general theory of homomorphisms
between Verma modules.
Let
{\mathfrak{g}}
be a complex simple Lie algebra and fix a Cartan subalgebra
{\mathfrak{h}}
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
We also fix an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
.
Let
\Delta
denote the set of roots of
{\mathfrak{g}}
with respect to
{\mathfrak{h}}
.
Choose a positive system
\Delta^{+}
for
\Delta
and write
\Pi
for the
set of simple roots for
\Delta^{+}
. We write
{\mathfrak{b}}={\mathfrak{h}}\oplus{\mathfrak{n}}_{+}
for
the Borel subalgebra corresponding to
\Delta^{+}
.
For
\alpha\in\Delta
and
\mu\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
,
we write
s_{\alpha}\mu=\mu-\langle\mu,\alpha^{\scriptscriptstyle\vee}\rangle\alpha

with
\alpha^{\scriptscriptstyle\vee}=\frac{2}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\alpha
.

As in Section
4.4
, for
\mu\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
, we write

N(\mu):=\mathcal{U}({\mathfrak{g}})\otimes_{\mathcal{U}({\mathfrak{b}})}\mathbb{C}_{\mu-\rho},

where
\rho
is half the sum of the positive roots.

It is well known that we have

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathfrak{g}}}(N(\nu),N(\lambda))\leq 1

for any
\nu,\lambda\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
.
To describe the condition when
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathfrak{g}}}(N(\nu),N(\lambda))=1
,
we next recall the notion of a
link
between weights.

Definition 8.1

(Bernstein–Gelfand–Gelfand)
.

Let
\nu,\lambda\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
and
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{t}\in\Delta^{+}
. Set
\lambda_{0}=\lambda

and
\lambda_{i}=s_{\beta_{i}}\cdots s_{\beta_{1}}(\lambda)
for
1\leq i\leq t
.
We say that the sequence
(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{t})

links

\lambda
to
\nu
if
the following two conditions are satisfied:

(1)

\lambda_{t}=\nu
;

(2)

\langle\lambda_{i-1},\beta_{i}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\vee}}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
for
1\leq i\leq t
.

Let
L(\nu)
denote the unique irreducible quotient of
the Verma module
N(\nu)
.
The following celebrated result of BGG–Verma shows when

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathfrak{g}}}(N(\nu),N(\lambda))=1
.

Theorem 8.2

(BGG–Verma)
.

The following three conditions on
\nu,\lambda\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
are equivalent.

(i)

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathfrak{g}}}(N(\nu),N(\lambda))=1
.

(ii)

L(\nu)
is a composition factor of
N(\lambda)
.

(iii)

There exists a sequence
(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{t})
with
\beta_{i}\in\Delta^{+}

that links
\lambda
to
\nu
.

To show factorization identities,
singular vectors of the composition of
two homomorphisms between Verma modules have to be computed.
The next two lemmas will be useful for this matter.

Lemma 8.3
.

For
\lambda,\nu,\mu\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
,
let

\varphi_{1}\colon N(\nu)\to N(\mu)
and
\varphi_{2}\colon N(\mu)\to N(\lambda)

be
homomorphisms
between Verma modules such that

\varphi_{1}(1\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\nu-\rho})=u_{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\mu-\rho}

and
\varphi_{2}(1\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\mu-\rho})=u_{2}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\lambda-\rho}
.
Then
(\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{1})(1\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\nu-\rho})

is given by

(\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{1})(1\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\nu-\rho})=u_{1}u_{2}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\lambda-\rho}.

One ensures the order of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
in Lemma
8.3
,
namely,
(\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{1})(1\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\nu-\rho})\neq u_{2}u_{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{\lambda-\rho}
.
For the proof, see, for instance,
[
20
, Lem. 3.7]
.

Lemma 8.4
.

Retain the hypothesis of Lemma
8.3
.
Let
\widehat{d\pi}\equiv\widehat{d\pi_{(\xi,\lambda)^{*}}}
denote the
transformed representation of
{\mathfrak{g}}
defined as in (
2.10
).
Then the algebraic Fourier transform

F_{c}(\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{1})
of
\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{1}\in\operatorname{Hom}_{{\mathfrak{g}}}(N(\nu),N(\lambda))

is given by

F_{c}(\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{1})=(\widehat{d\pi}(u_{1})\widehat{d\pi}(u_{2}))\,{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}\,1=\widehat{d\pi}(u_{1})\,{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}\,(\widehat{d\pi}(u_{2})\,{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}\,1).

(8.5)

In particular, if
u_{j}\in\mathcal{U}({\mathfrak{n}}_{-})
for
j=1,2
, then

F_{c}(\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{1})=(\widehat{dL}(u_{1})\widehat{dL}(u_{2}))\,{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}\,1=\widehat{dL}(u_{1})\,{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}\,(\widehat{dL}(u_{2})\,{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}\,1).

(8.6)

Proof.

The identities (
8.5
) and (
8.6
)
are immediate consequences of Lemma
8.3
and (
2.22
).
∎

8.2.

Now we consider the factorization identities of

({\mathfrak{g}},B)
-homomorphisms
\varphi
and IDOs
\mathcal{D}
in order.
Recall from Section
4.2
that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{a}

\displaystyle=(-\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})\times\mathbb{C},

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\Lambda}_{a}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}\times(-\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}),

\displaystyle\Lambda_{b}

\displaystyle=\{(1-k,1-\ell+k):k,\ell\in 1+\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\},

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\Lambda}_{b}

\displaystyle=\{(1-k+\ell,1-\ell):k,\ell\in 1+\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{c}

\displaystyle=\{(\tfrac{1}{2}(2-k-s),\tfrac{1}{2}(2-k+s)):k\in 1+\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\;\text{and}\;s\in\mathbb{C}\}.

Further, for
\square\in\{>,<,=\}
,
we put

\displaystyle\Lambda_{b}^{(k\,\square\,\ell)}

\displaystyle:=\{(1-k,1-\ell+k)\in\Lambda_{b}:k\,\square\,\ell\},

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\Lambda}{}_{b}^{(k\,\square\,\ell)}

\displaystyle:=\{(1-k+\ell,1-\ell)\in\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\Lambda}_{b}:k\,\square\,\ell\}.

One can easily check that

\Lambda_{b}\subset\Lambda_{a}\cap\Lambda_{c},\quad\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\Lambda}_{b}\subset\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\Lambda}_{a}\cap\Lambda_{c},\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda_{b}^{(k<\ell)}=\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\Lambda}{}_{b}^{(k>\ell)}.

Also, recall from Section
4.4
that
in the identification

{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}=\{(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})\in\mathbb{C}^{3}:v_{1}+v_{2}+v_{3}=0\}
,
we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=-(\lambda_{1}\varpi_{1}+\lambda_{2}\varpi_{2})+\rho

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{3}(-(2\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}-3),\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2},\lambda_{1}+2\lambda_{2}-3).

We put

\theta_{(k,\ell)}:=\frac{1}{3}(k+\ell,\ell-2k,k-2\ell)\quad\text{and}\quad\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\theta}_{(k,\ell)}:=\frac{1}{3}(2k-\ell,2\ell-k,-(k+\ell)).

Then,

\mu_{\lambda}=\begin{cases}\theta_{(k,\ell)}&\text{if $(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=(1-k,1-\ell+k)\in\Lambda_{b}$},\\[3.0pt]
\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\theta}_{(k,\ell)}&\text{if $(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=(1-k+\ell,1-\ell)\in\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\Lambda}_{b}$}\end{cases}

and

\theta_{(k,\ell)}=\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\theta}_{(\ell,\ell-k)}\quad\text{for $k\leq\ell$}\quad\text{and}\quad\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\theta}_{(k,\ell)}=\theta_{(k-\ell,k)}\quad\text{for $k\geq\ell$}.

Moreover, we have

s_{\beta}s_{\alpha}\theta_{(k,k)}=s_{\gamma}\theta_{(k,k)}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{\alpha}s_{\beta}\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\theta}_{(k,k)}=s_{\gamma}\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\theta}_{(k,k)}.

First we show that, to study factorization identities,
it suffices to consider
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\Lambda_{b}\cup\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\Lambda}_{b}
.
As in Theorem
4.21
,
we write

\alpha=(1,-1,0),\quad\beta=(0,1,-1),\quad\gamma=(1,0,-1).

Proposition 8.7
.

Suppose that

\varphi\colon N(\mu^{\prime}_{\lambda})^{(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})}\to N(\mu_{\lambda})^{(+,+)}

is a non-zero
({\mathfrak{g}},B)
-homomorphism in Theorem
4.21
.
If
\varphi
admits a factorization identity, then

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\Lambda_{b}\cup\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\Lambda}_{b}
.

Proof.

Since
\varphi
admits a factorization identity,
it follows from Theorem
8.2
that

\mu_{\lambda}^{\prime}
must be

\mu^{\prime}_{\lambda}\in\{s_{\alpha}s_{\beta}\mu_{\lambda},\,s_{\beta}s_{\alpha}\mu_{\lambda},\,s_{\gamma}\mu_{\lambda}\}
.
Theorem
4.21
shows that
if
\mu_{\lambda}^{\prime}\in\{s_{\alpha}s_{\beta}\mu_{\lambda},\,s_{\beta}s_{\alpha}\mu_{\lambda}\}
, then

\varphi=\varphi^{(k.\ell)}_{\pm}
for some
k,\ell\in 1+\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Thus, in this case,
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\Lambda_{b}\cup\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\Lambda}_{b}
.

We now show that even if
\mu_{\lambda}^{\prime}=s_{\gamma}\mu_{\lambda}
,
the parameters
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
satisfy the proposed condition.
Indeed, in this case, we have
\varphi=\varphi^{(s;k)}_{c}
and

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=(\tfrac{1}{2}(2-k-s),\tfrac{1}{2}(2-k+s))

for some
k\in 1+\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
s\in\mathbb{C}
.
Let
\varphi^{(s;k)}=\varphi^{\prime\prime}\circ\varphi^{\prime}
denote a factorization
of
\varphi^{(s;k)}
. Then

\varphi^{\prime}\in\{\varphi^{(\ell)}_{1},\varphi^{(\ell)}_{2},\varphi^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}_{\pm}\}
for some
\ell,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in 1+\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
If
\varphi^{\prime}=\varphi^{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}_{\pm}
, then

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\Lambda_{b}\cup\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\Lambda}_{b}
.
Next, suppose that
\varphi^{\prime}\in\{\varphi^{(\ell)}_{1},\varphi^{(\ell)}_{2}\}
.
If
\varphi^{\prime}=\varphi^{(\ell)}_{1}
, then
Theorem
4.21
