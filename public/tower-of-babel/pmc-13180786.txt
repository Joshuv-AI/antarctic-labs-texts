Nodular Fasciitis in Breast in an Elderly Woman With a Giant Mass: A Case Report
Authors: Mingsheng Yu, Hongyun Liu, Wenchao Han, Lu Liu, Yao Bi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNodular fasciitis (NF) is a rare, self‐limiting benign soft tissue disorder characterized predominantly by fibroblastic proliferation. NF in breast is rare. We report a 61‐year‐old female patient who was pathologically diagnosed with NF. She had a rapidly increasing mass in the central region of the right breast. After imaging examinations, complete mass resection was performed on the patient. By pathological examination and immunohistochemical tests, NF was the final diagnosis.

Introduction

Nodular fasciitis (NF) is a rare and typically benign reactive proliferative lesion of fibroblasts with self‐limited nature. NF occurs in the connective tissue of muscles, fascia, or other soft tissues [1], arise throughout the body with common locations include the extremities, head and neck, and is rare in the breast [2]. The etiology remains unclear, and some researchers suggest that trauma may serve as a triggering factor in the development of NF.

Case Presentation

Presenting Complaints and Disease History

A 61‐year‐old female patient with a history of hypertension for the past 5 years was admitted to the hospital on January 4, 2025. The patient found a lump in her right breast 4 months ago, which grew slowly. The lump grew rapidly after trauma 3 days ago. And at presentation, the patient had a large, clear‐borders, painful mass (15 × 12 cm) in the central region of the right breast without axillary lymph node enlargement. The patient did not receive any treatment before admission.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment)

Ultrasonography (US) of the breast showed (Figure 1) an irregular 16 × 8 cm mixed echogenicity mass in the right breast (BI‐RADS 4B), suggesting a high risk of malignancy. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated (Figure 2) a large, well‐circumscribed oval‐shaped abnormal signal intensity lesion in the right breast, measuring approximately 13 × 12 cm, with associated skin edema (BI‐RADS 4C). Through imaging studies, the tumor was considered suspicious for a borderline or malignant neoplasm, requiring pathological examination for definitive diagnosis.

MRI showed a large, well‐circumscribed oval‐shaped abnormal signal intensity lesion in the right breast.

The patient underwent ultrasound‐guided core needle biopsy of the breast mass. Pathological examination of the mass revealed diffusely distributed elongated and short spindle cells, and no malignant cells were identified. These pathological findings raise differential considerations of nodular fasciitis, phyllodes tumor, fibromatosis, or metaplastic carcinoma. Core needle biopsy is often insufficient in these diseases, and excision is recommended for definitive diagnosis to exclude malignancy.

We decided to perform complete mass resection to the patient (Figure 3). During the surgery, a periareolar incision was made in the right upper breast quadrant. After dissecting through the skin and subcutaneous tissues, the tumor was completely excised. The tumor was well‐encapsulated with clear boundaries, exhibiting a lobulated morphology, and measured approximately 15 × 10 cm. On histologic examination, the lesion was unencapsulated but relatively well‐demarcated from the surrounding soft tissue. Hematoxylin and eosin–stained sections revealed a highly heterogeneous architecture, characterized by alternating hypercellular and hypocellular areas. The cellular component consisted predominantly of plump spindle‐shaped fibroblasts and myofibroblasts with moderate amounts of eosinophilic cytoplasm and oval, vesicular nuclei containing fine chromatin and small but distinct nucleoli. No significant nuclear pleomorphism or atypical forms were identified. No atypical or bizarre mitoses were present (Figure 4).

Mass was completely resected from the right breast, and the mass was well circumscribed.

Pathological findings indicated a spindle cell tumor with diffusely distributed cells and no mitotic figures identified, suggesting NF as the most likely diagnosis (× 200).

A distinctive growth pattern was noted at low magnification, with the spindle cells arranged in short, irregular, interwoven fascicles showing a characteristic curvilinear. The stroma was variably myxoid, particularly in the hypocellular areas, where a pale basophilic ground substance with scattered microcystic spaces or clefts was seen. Throughout the lesion, numerous thin‐walled, branching small blood vessels were present. Scattered lymphocytes, occasional plasma cells, and rare osteoclast‐like multinucleated giant cells were also identified. No tumor necrosis was observed. Immunohistochemical tests showed tumor cell positive for SMA (Figure 5), Vimentin, Calponin, cytoplasmic β‐catenin, CD10, and negative for S100, EMA, CK (Figure 6), p63, CD34, ER or PR, suggesting NF as the final diagnosis.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient's postoperative incision healed uneventfully, with no erythema or swelling. Sutures were removed on postoperative day 10, and no complications were observed. In light of the pathological diagnosis, regular breast examinations are recommended. The most recent breast ultrasound follow‐up demonstrated no signs of tumor recurrence, revealing only localized scar tissue at the incision site.

Discussion

We present a case of an elderly female with NF of the breast with a giant mass over 10 cm. The mean age of patients in a previously reported study was 39 years [3]. Paliogiannis et al. described that the mean size of the lesion was approximately 20 mm (ranging 6–60 mm) [4]. In this case, the lesion was 16 cm in ultrasound examination and about 15 cm in surgery, which may be the largest lesion in the reports.

NF was first described as “subcutaneous pseudosarcomatous fibromatosis” by Konwaler et al. in 1955 [5]. NF is a rare reactive, self‐limiting benign soft tissue disorder characterized predominantly by fibroblastic proliferation. Its ultrasonographic and MRI features are significant diagnostic challenges in distinguishing it from soft tissue sarcomas and mammary carcinomas [6]. In this case, the US and MRI findings raised significant concern for a malignant or borderline tumor.

Even through core needle biopsy (CNB), it was difficult to diagnose. CNB often fails to provide a definitive diagnosis of nodular fasciitis due to its pseudosarcomatous features—including hypercellularity, mitotic activity, and pleomorphism—which overlap with those of malignancies, combined with sampling limitations that preclude assessment of the lesion's full histological architecture. In the present case, the initial biopsy raised concern for a malignant spindle cell neoplasm; however, complete excision, comprehensive immunohistochemical evaluation (including negative epithelial markers, CD10 positivity, CD34 negativity, and cytoplasmic β‐catenin staining) ultimately confirmed the benign diagnosis of nodular fasciitis. This case underscores the critical importance of complete excision and a systematic diagnostic approach when faced with a rapidly growing spindle cell lesion on core needle biopsy, thereby preventing unnecessary overtreatment.

The differential diagnosis of nodular fasciitis encompasses a variety of spindle cell lesions, such as fibromatosis, myxoid sarcoma, myofibroma, and phyllodes tumor [7]. And the distinction is primarily based on immunohistochemical profiling and morphologic features. In contrast to metaplastic carcinoma, which typically expresses epithelial markers, the present case showed negative staining for CK, P63, and CK5/6. This immunophenotype effectively rules out a carcinoma diagnosis [8]. Phyllodes tumor is classified as a biphasic neoplasm containing both epithelial and mesenchymal elements. In the current case, extensive sampling of the tumor revealed no epithelial component, thereby excluding the diagnosis of phyllodes tumor [9]. Distinction from myofibroblastoma is particularly challenging due to overlapping morphological features. However, immunohistochemistry provides reliable discriminatory power. Nodular fasciitis is characterized by diffuse and strong positivity for CD10, as observed in the present case. In contrast, myofibroblastoma typically expresses CD34. The absence of CD34 expression in this case further supports the diagnosis of nodular fasciitis [10]. Although both nodular fasciitis and fibromatosis are composed of bland spindle cells with abundant collagen deposition, they exhibit distinct clinical and histopathological features. Nodular fasciitis typically presents as a rapidly growing, self‐limited mass with myxoid stroma and extravasated red blood cells, whereas fibromatosis shows infiltrative growth, long fascicles of uniform fibroblasts, and dense keloidal‐like collagen. Immunohistochemically, nuclear β‐catenin expression is characteristically positive in most cases of fibromatosis. In contrast, nodular fasciitis shows negative or only cytoplasmic β‐catenin staining, without nuclear accumulation. Additionally, smooth muscle actin (SMA) is often positive in both lesions, but desmin is consistently negative in fibromatosis. In the present case, the positive expression of cytoplasmic β‐catenin expression, along with the characteristic loose myxoid stroma and rapid clinical course, supports the diagnosis of nodular fasciitis over fibromatosis [11].

Local surgical excision is the primary treatment, with a favorable prognosis due to its self‐limiting nature. Considered the small number of cases at present, the specific treatment plan is still under investigation. In summary, this case emphasizes that nodular fasciitis, while benign, is a great mimicker of both malignant and locally aggressive spindle cell neoplasms. A systematic diagnostic approach incorporating clinical history, morphological assessment, and targeted immunohistochemistry is essential to establish the correct diagnosis, thereby guiding appropriate and individualized patient management.

Conclusion

Breast masses are a frequently encountered clinical presentation among women in the reproductive age group. Nodular fasciitis is rarely occurring in breast; however, the features of NF in US and MRI are difficult to distinguish from breast cancer. Patients with a painful and rapidly growing mass in breast should be considered for the diagnosis of NF.
