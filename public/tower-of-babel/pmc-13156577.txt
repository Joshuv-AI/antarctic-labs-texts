Antenatally diagnosed severe congenital pulmonary airway malformation requiring life-saving lobectomy in the early neonatal period: A case report
Authors: Maroua El Ouaer, Hayet Ben Hamida, Tasnim Frigui, Seifeddine Ben Hammouda, Samia Bel Hssan, Amina Ben Salem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCongenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM) ranges from asymptomatic lesions to life-threatening neonatal presentations. Large lesions can lead to severe respiratory failure requiring urgent intervention. We report an early neonatal lobectomy that was indicated for a symptomatic newborn and we present the perioperative challenges. We describe a case of a male neonate conceived via in vitro fertilization (IVF) and born at term. He had an antenatal diagnosis of large right-sided CPAM. Immediately after birth, he developed severe respiratory distress requiring intubation and mechanical ventilation. Refractory hypercapnia prompted urgent surgical intervention. On day 3 of life, a right lower lobectomy was performed. Postoperative complications included persistent pulmonary hypertension and transient neonatal seizures. Extubation was achieved on postoperative day 11, followed by gradual weaning to room air. This case illustrated the successful management of a large antenatally diagnosed CPAM with immediate postnatal decompensation requiring early lobectomy, emphasizing that success relies on accurate prenatal assessment and timely surgical decision-making.Keywords: Cystic adenomatoid malformation of lung, Congenital-prenatal diagnosis- infant, Newborn-pneumonectomy—case report

Introduction

Congenital cystic adenomatoid malformation (CCAM), now more commonly referred to as congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM), is a rare developmental lung anomaly often detected antenatally. It accounts for about 25% of all congenital lung malformations and up to 95% of congenital cystic lung diseases [1]. However, its reported incidence is rising due to advances in prenatal imaging and increased clinical awareness. Most studies report CPAM incidence as 1 in 25,000 to 1 in 35,000 live births [2,3]. It results from abnormal branching morphogenesis and proliferation of terminal bronchioles, leading to cystic or adenomatoid replacement of healthy tissue [4]. CPAMs were classified by the Stocker classification: type I is macrocystic, type II is mixed, and type III are solid/microcystic [5].

Prenatal detection has become increasingly common due to advances in high-resolution ultrasonography and fetal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), enabling risk stratification and coordinated perinatal management in specialized centers [6]. Although many CPAMs remain asymptomatic, large or expanding lesions can cause severe respiratory distress, mediastinal shift, or pulmonary hypoplasia, sometimes requiring urgent neonatal intervention [7]. Neonates with CPAM require thorough postnatal evaluation, including chest radiography and often chest computed tomography (CT) to confirm the lesion. Management in neonates depends on symptom severity, lesion characteristics, and evolving evidence, with ongoing debate regarding the optimal approach. Most cases remain stable, but a small subset of large lesions can cause significant mass effect, leading to severe respiratory failure, hydrops, or refractory hypercapnia immediately after birth. In such cases, urgent surgical intervention within the first days of life becomes necessary. Reports of very early neonatal lobectomy remain rare due to the associated perioperative and physiological challenges [8,9].

Early surgery is associated with faster symptom resolution and good long-term outcomes, but morbidity remains higher in neonates with low birthweight or cardiac anomalies [9].

This report describes a term neonate with an antenatal diagnosis of massive right-sided CPAM, causing severe respiratory failure. This case stands out for the exceptional timing of lobectomy on day 3 of life in the setting of refractory hypercapnia, as well as the uncommon perioperative challenges posed by associated pulmonary hypertension and neonatal seizures.

Case report

Prenatal findings

The newborn was conceived via IVF after 2 years of infertility from a non consanguineous couple. The mother was 32 years old with a history of asthma controlled with as-needed short-acting beta-agonists and autoimmune thyroiditis also controlled with levothyroxine. Routine ultrasound at 19 gestational weeks revealed a right lung lesion consistent with congenital CPAM type II. Serial imaging demonstrated progressive enlargement and mediastinal shift. Fetal MRI at 36 gestational weeks confirmed large right-sided cystic hyperlucent lung lesions (CPAM type I/II) with significant compression of the contralateral lung (Volume: 23.86 mL vs expected 93.8 mL) (Fig. 1). Fetal echocardiography showed normal cardiac anatomy.

Sagittal (A) and coronal (B) fetal MRI sections demonstrating large right-sided cystic hyperlucent lung lesions (red asterisks), appearing hyperintense on T2-weighted sequences, consistent with fluid-filled spaces. These lesions are associated with marked mass effect, including significant compression of the contralateral lung and mediastinal displacement (blue arrow).

Delivery and initial stabilization

Elective cesarean section was performed at 39 gestational weeks in a tertiary perinatal center. The male infant had Apgar scores of 7 and 7 at 1 and 5 minutes, respectively. Severe respiratory distress with cyanosis (SpO₂ = 40%) and ineffective breathing required endotracheal intubation in the delivery room. At birth, the infant was eutrophic, weighing 3500 g, and presented no dysmorphic features.

Mechanical ventilation was initiated and umbilical venous catheterization was established. Respiratory management was tailored to the severity of respiratory failure. Conventional mechanical ventilation was initiated as first-line support; however, it proved insufficient, with persistent hypercapnia reaching 72 mmHg. High-frequency oscillatory ventilation (HFOV) was subsequently instituted in the presence of severe respiratory acidosis, refractory hypercapnia, and inadequate oxygenation despite optimized conventional settings. Key HFOV parameters, including mean airway pressure, amplitude, frequency, and FiO₂, are summarized in Table 1. At 10 hours of life, the patient started to have hypotension, which necessitated vasoactive drugs. We started dobutamine infusion (10 gamma/kg/min) and norepinephrine for vasopressive support (0.9 gamma/kg/min). Inhaled Nitric Oxide (iNO) was administered at a dose of 20 ppm when we confirmed persistent pulmonary hypertension. The Fraction of inspired Oxygen (FiO2) was up to 100% to reach 88 to 90% of peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) during the second day of life. Despite optimized ventilation, the neonate developed refractory hypercapnia (PaCO₂ = 94 mmHg). Escalation to High-Frequency Oscillatory Ventilation did not result in sustained improvement. Exogenous surfactant was not administered, as the underlying condition was not suggestive of surfactant deficiency. Although extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) was considered, it was not available in our setting, further supporting the indication for early surgical management.

Imaging

Thoracic X-ray showed multiple cystic hyperlucent lesions of heterogeneous size occupying the whole right lung field and resulting in mediastinal and cardiac shift to the left (Fig. 2).

(A) Preoperative frontal chest X-ray demonstrating a right-sided congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM) type 1, characterized by multiple cystic hyperlucent lesions in the right lung (blue asterisk), causing ipsilateral lung expansion and contralateral mediastinal shift (red arrows). (B) Postoperative chest X-ray demonstrating regression of the cystic lesions with re-expansion of the right lung and resolution of the mediastinal shift (blue arrow).

Chest computed tomography (CT) confirmed a large CPAM type I in the right lower lobe, with hyperinflation of the remaining right lung, marked mediastinal shift, and near-complete collapse of the left lung.

Surgical intervention

Urgent right lower lobectomy was performed on day 3 via posterolateral thoracotomy. A tunneled cuffed central venous catheter and chest drain were placed.

Then, a right lower lobectomy was performed for the Type I CPAM. Under general anesthesia and right lateral decubitus positioning, a left posterolateral thoracotomy was carried out through the fifth intercostal space. Cysto-pleural adhesions were identified and carefully released. The triangular ligament was divided, and fissure dissection was achieved with coagulation of the fissural leaflets. The lower pulmonary vein was ligated and divided. The right middle and upper lobes were normal. The procedure was completed without complications. Specimens were submitted for histopathological examination.

Histology

The specimen, received in formalin, measures 8 × 6 × 4.5 cm. The cut surface revealed a spongy parenchyma containing numerous cysts ranging in size from 0.5 cm to 3 cm in diameter.

Microscopic examination demonstrated lung tissue extensively replaced by numerous cysts of varying size, including some very large cysts. These cysts were lined by a ciliated, pseudostratified columnar epithelium without mucin-secreting cells. The epithelial lining showed no cytonuclear atypia and exhibited focal pseudopapillary projections. In areas, these cysts communicated with adjacent alveolar spaces, with frequent transitions observed between the thick cyst walls and the thin alveolar walls (Fig. 3).

(A) (Hematoxylin & Eosin (HE) x 100): samples involved pulmonary tissue containing numerous cysts of varying sizes, some of which were large (black asterisk). (B) (HE x 200): some cysts showed focal pseudopapillary projections (black arrow). (C) (HE x 400): these cysts were lined by ciliated columnar epithelium lacking mucin-secreting cells, and the epithelium exhibited no cytonuclear atypia (blue arrow). (D) (HE x 400): cysts (black arrow) intermittently communicated with adjacent alveoli (blue asterisk), with frequent transitions between the thickened cyst wall and the alveolar wall. Based on the above features, the diagnosis of congenital malformation of the pulmonary airways (or congenital cystic adenomatoid malformation) of the right lower lobe, stocker type 1 was made.

The adjacent pulmonary parenchyma is compressed by these cystic structures. The interstitial tissue shows hemorrhagic changes associated with a mild inflammatory infiltrate.

In conclusion, it was a CPAM of the right lower lobe, classified as stocker type I.

Postoperative course

Concerning respiratory management, high-frequency oscillatory ventilation was required during 3 days postoperatively, then conventional ventilation was sufficient. Persistent neonatal pulmonary hypertension was treated successfully with inhaled nitric oxide (10 ppm for 4 days) and transitioned to oral sildenafil. Vasoactive support was discontinued 12 hours following surgical intervention. Chest X-ray control showed a re-expansion of the right lung with resolution of the mediastinal shift (Fig. 2).

On postoperative day 5, tonic seizures occurred, controlled with midazolam and phenobarbital. A metabolic panel, including serum electrolytes, serum calcium, and blood glucose, was performed and was normal. A transfontanellar ultrasound showed normal findings. Also, infection was ruled out. Neurological examination normalized afterward.

About nutrition and supportive care, enteral feeding was initiated on day 7. The neonate received continuous gastric feeding of expressed maternal breast milk, and it was well tolerated. The chest drain was removed on day 6. Extubation occurred on day 11, and gradual ventilation weaning was initiated by high-flow nasal cannula during 8 days before full unassisted breathing (Table 1).

Outcome

The infant was discharged on day 21 of life in stable condition, without a supplemental oxygen requirement. Nutritional support was established as mixed feeding, combining maternal breast milk and commercial infant formula. At discharge, weight gain was appropriate for gestational and chronological age.

During ambulatory follow-up, the infant has demonstrated normal anthropometric parameters and age-appropriate neurodevelopmental progress. No seizure recurrence has been documented. Respiratory status remains stable, with no ongoing oxygen dependence.

Discussion

Antenatal diagnosis and risk stratification of CPAM: Key predictors and approaches

CPAM is most often detected during a routine second-trimester ultrasound. Accurate antenatal diagnosis and risk stratification are crucial for guiding surveillance, counseling, and intervention planning, as outcomes range from spontaneous regression to life-threatening complications.

Ultrasound is the primary tool for prenatal detection, identifying echogenic lung lesions and differentiating CPAM from other anomalies. The best‑validated prenatal predictor of postnatal respiratory problems and surgery is an elevated CPAM volume ratio (CVR). A CVR ≥ 0.39 at 25 to 29 weeks was associated with a higher risk of support within 24 hours [7].

Lesions are classified as macrocystic, microcystic, or mixed, and further characterized by size, location, and vascular supply. When macrocystic CPAM lesions are described in infants, the need for respiratory support at birth and surgical resection within 28 days is higher [7,10].

Another sonographic sign that could be a predictor of higher mortality is hydrops. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis found that fetal/perinatal loss occurred in 42 % of fetuses with CPAM and hydrops [11].

MRI can complement ultrasound, especially for complex or late-gestation cases, but does not significantly outperform ultrasound for prognosis [3,10,12,13].

Large cohort meta‑analyses and systematic reviews show that children conceived with assisted reproductive technologies have a slightly higher overall risk of congenital anomalies than spontaneously conceived children (relative risk roughly 1.3–1.4). A direct causal link to CPAM remains unproven [[14], [15], [16]].

Indications for early neonatal surgery

Early versus delayed surgical management of CPAM remains a matter of debate, with recent meta-analyses confirming that most prenatally diagnosed lesions remain asymptomatic at birth, though reliable predictors of postnatal respiratory distress are still evolving [11,12]. While elective lobectomy is often recommended in stable patients to reduce perioperative risk, accumulating evidence indicates that early surgical intervention in symptomatic neonates with severe respiratory compromise can be life-saving, despite a potentially higher immediate postoperative risk [9,17]. In our case, the indication for urgent surgery was supported by refractory respiratory failure and progressive hypercapnia, aligning with previous observations in critically ill neonates where delayed resection was associated with increased morbidity [18,19]. Furthermore, postoperative neurological complications such as seizures are rarely reported in CPAM cases, making this presentation unusual and clinically noteworthy. Another distinctive feature of our case is the association of a large CPAM with significant pulmonary hypertension, a combination infrequently described in the literature, although prior reports have noted that congenital lung malformations may occasionally coexist with cardiac anomalies and elevated pulmonary pressures, further contributing to clinical severity [20,21]. This unique constellation of findings justified the need for early surgical intervention.

Almost 90% of neonates with CPAM are asymptomatic at birth; they may be followed up, and surgery is considered at a later stage. But those with severe respiratory distress require rapid multidisciplinary intervention [20,21]. Prompt stabilization and urgent surgical management are the mainstays of care for symptomatic neonates.

It is important to stabilize the patient before surgery by initiating respiratory support with supplemental oxygen, non-invasive ventilation, or intubation as needed. High-Frequency Oscillatory Ventilation may be beneficial in severe cases or when conventional ventilation fails [22,23].

After birth, an urgent chest X-ray and CT are fundamental to confirm the diagnosis, assess lesion size, and rule out complications such as pneumothorax or mediastinal shift. As in our case, large lesions can cause mediastinal compression and compromise cardiac output. For that, it is necessary to monitor and support cardiovascular status [23].

Finally, while elective resection for asymptomatic lesions remains debated, early surgery is standard for symptomatic neonates presenting with respiratory compromise and failure of ventilation [18,24]. Refractory hypercapnia requiring high ventilatory pressures is a widely accepted indication for urgent lobectomy, particularly when mass effect threatens cardiopulmonary stability [19].

Lobectomy, open or thoracoscopic, is the definitive treatment for neonates with persistent or worsening distress. Early surgery leads to rapid symptom resolution and favorable outcomes, even in preterm infants [19,25,26]. The timing of surgery on day 3 in our case aligns with current evidence supporting prompt intervention in life-threatening CPAM.

Postoperative complications

Large, space-occupying CPAM lesions can cause mediastinal shift, lung hypoplasia, and vena cava compression, leading to fetal and postnatal cardiopulmonary compromise. A study by Xu et al. noted that one of their 36 symptomatic neonates presented with mediastinal displacement due to a large lesion [17]. This mass effect can elevate pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), a primary driver of pulmonary hypertension. While not explicitly stated, severely symptomatic neonates requiring semi-emergent surgery for respiratory failure, as described by Ito et al., may have had elevated PVR preoperatively [27].

The physiological stress of major thoracic surgery can trigger factors that worsen or unmask pulmonary hypertension. These include hypoxia, hypercapnia, acidosis, pain, and atelectasis—all common perioperative concerns. The review by Alberti et al. [23] on respiratory support in surgical neonates extensively discusses the management of pulmonary hypertension in the context of congenital diaphragmatic hernia, emphasizing that echocardiographic assessment is essential. This principle is transferable; neonates undergoing major lung resection for CPAM may similarly benefit from preoperative and postoperative echocardiography to assess right ventricular function and tricuspid regurgitant jet velocity to estimate pulmonary artery pressure. The goal of avoiding Ventilator-Induced Lung Injury (VILI), as outlined by Alberti et al. [23] becomes even more crucial in a patient at risk for pulmonary hypertension. Alveolar overdistension (volutrauma) can increase PVR, while atelectasis can cause hypoxia, a potent pulmonary vasoconstrictor. Therefore, a lung-protective ventilation strategy with careful titration of positive end-expiratory pressure and inspiratory pressures is paramount in the postoperative management of these neonates to prevent iatrogenic exacerbation of pulmonary hypertension.

Seizures in the postoperative period may relate to hypoxia, hypercapnia, or perioperative hemodynamic instability. Continuous neurological monitoring improves detection and outcomes [28].

Future prospective studies focusing on neonatal CPAM surgery should include systematic echocardiographic and hemodynamic monitoring to better define the incidence and impact of postoperative pulmonary hypertension.

This study addresses a critical neonatal condition where rapid intervention can be life-saving, providing valuable insights into the management of severe, symptomatic CPAM in the immediate postnatal period. While the acute postoperative course is described, the study lacks information on long-term outcomes, such as pulmonary function, growth and development, or potential recurrence of symptoms, which are essential for comprehensive evaluation.

Conclusion

Severe respiratory distress due to CPAM in neonates requires immediate stabilization, targeted interventions, and urgent surgical resection. Early multidisciplinary management is critical for survival and optimal outcomes. Severe CPAM complicated by acute respiratory failure can be treated with early neonatal lobectomy. Success depends on precise prenatal evaluation, timely multidisciplinary decision-making, and vigilant postoperative management of complications.
