Output-Sparse Matrix Multiplication Using Compressed Sensing
Huck Bennett, Karthik Gajulapalli, Alexander Golovnev, Evelyn Warton

Abstract
We give two algorithms for output-sparse matrix multiplication ( OSMM \mathrm{OSMM} ), the problem of multiplying two n × n n\times n matrices A , B A,B when their product A ​ B AB is promised to have at most O ⁡ ( n δ ) O(n^{\delta}) many non-zero entries for a given value δ ∈ [ 0 , 2 ] \delta\in[0,2] . We then show how to speed up these algorithms in the fully sparse setting, where the input matrices A , B A,B are themselves sparse. All of our algorithms work over arbitrary rings. Our first, deterministic algorithm for OSMM \mathrm{OSMM} works via a two-pass reduction to compressed sensing. It runs in roughly n ω ⁡ ( δ / 2 , 1 , 1 ) n^{\omega(\delta/2,1,1)} time, where ω ⁡ ( ⋅ , ⋅ , ⋅ ) \omega(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot) is the rectangular matrix multiplication exponent. This substantially improves on prior deterministic algorithms for output-sparse matrix multiplication. Our second, randomized algorithm for OSMM \mathrm{OSMM} works via a reduction to compressed sensing and a variant of matrix multiplication verification, and runs in roughly n ω ⁡ ( δ − 1 , 1 , 1 ) n^{\omega(\delta-1,1,1)} time. This algorithm and its extension to the fully sparse setting have running times that match those of the (randomized) algorithms for OSMM \mathrm{OSMM} and FSMM \mathrm{FSMM} , respectively, in recent work of Abboud, Bringmann, Fischer, and Künnemann (SODA, 2024). Our algorithm uses different techniques and is arguably simpler. Finally, we observe that the running time of our randomized algorithm and the algorithm of Abboud et al. are optimal via a simple reduction from rectangular matrix multiplication.

The Matrix Multiplication Problem (
\mathrm{MM}

AB

The running time

While one line of work has sought to improve

In this work, we give new algorithms for the

Specifically, we consider
\mathrm{MM}

We denote this problem for matrices over a ring

1.3
Technical Overview

We next give an overview of our algorithms, which use simple primitives: fast rectangular matrix multiplication, a compressed sensing scheme with good parameters, and an algorithm for a variant of matrix multiplication verification.
Define
\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{0}
(respectively,
\lVert M\rVert_{0}
) to be the number of non-zero entries in (i.e., Hamming weight of) a vector
\boldsymbol{v}
(respectively, matrix
M

\boldsymbol{v}
(respectively, matrix
M
)
t
-sparse
if
\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{0}\leq t
(respectively, if
\lVert M\rVert_{0}\leq t
).

Output-column-sparse
\mathrm{MM}
using compressed sensing.

In the
compressed sensing
problem with parameters
n,m,t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with
1\leq t\leq n
, the goal is to compute an
m\times n
matrix
H=H(m,n,t)
called a
measurement matrix
, and to design an efficient
sparse recovery
algorithm
{\mathcal{R}}
(depending on
H
) such that for every
t
-sparse vector
\boldsymbol{x}
of length
n
,
{\mathcal{R}}(H\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{x}
. That is, the goal is for
{\mathcal{R}}
to be able to recover any
t
-sparse vector
\boldsymbol{x}
from
H\boldsymbol{x}
.

Several previous works including
[
IS09
]
use compressed sensing to solve

\mathrm{MM}
in the case where every column
\boldsymbol{c}_{i}
of the output matrix
C=(\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{c}_{n}):=AB
is promised to be
t
-sparse.
The technique works by computing
HAB
parenthesized as
(HA)B
using fast rectangular matrix multiplication, and then recovering the columns
\boldsymbol{c}_{i}
of
C=AB
from
HC
using
{\mathcal{R}}
as
\boldsymbol{c}_{i}={\mathcal{R}}(H\boldsymbol{c}_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
If computing
H
takes
T_{{\mathcal{M}}}(n)
time,
{\mathcal{R}}
takes

time, and

for some
\beta\in[0,1]
, then for any constant
\varepsilon>0
the overall running time of this algorithm is

O(T_{{\mathcal{M}}}(n)+n^{\omega(\beta,1,1)+\varepsilon}+n\cdot T_{{\mathcal{R}}}(n))\ \text{.}

Reducing

This reduction appears formally as
Algorithm

1

More specifically, assume that

Reducing
\mathrm{OSMM}
to compressed sensing and a variant of

Our second reduction is from

2

The main idea of our second reduction is to sequentially “guess and check” all columns of
C
of sparsity at most

Namely, in iteration

In particular, our measurement matrices

[

from Parvaresh-Vardy codes. They are sparse and binary.
The main theorem summarizing this scheme is

.

For completeness, we present and analyze the expander construction in

We emphasize the subtlety of identifying an appropriate compressed sensing scheme for our purposes. We need algorithms that compute the measurement matrix and perform sparse recovery not just in (arbitrary) polynomial time, but in nearly quadratic and nearly linear time, respectively. Moreover, we need these algorithms to be deterministic—many works on compressed sensing sample a random measurement matrix
H
(see, e.g.,
[
CRT06
,
GI10
]
).
Finally, for
2
we need multiplication by

to be very efficient.

The algorithm for
\mathrm{Column\textrm{-}Wise\textrm{-}MMV}
that we use (stated formally in

) is a modified version of Freivalds’s algorithm for “plain”
\mathrm{MMV}

[

]

\delta

(Similarly, this algorithm is the only use of randomness in our randomized

An alternative randomized algorithm.

We note that it is possible to implement a variant of our randomized algorithm that achieves a similar running time to
2
if it is possible to approximate the number of non-zero entries in each column of
C=(\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{c}_{n})=AB
to within a constant factor efficiently.

In fact, we can compute estimates
\tilde{c}_{i}
of the sparsity of each column
\boldsymbol{c}_{i}
satisfying

1/2\cdot\lVert\boldsymbol{c}_{i}\rVert_{0}<\tilde{c}_{i}<2\cdot\lVert\boldsymbol{c}_{i}\rVert_{0}

with high probability using a randomized algorithm from
[
PS14
]
.
We can then use these estimates to multiply
A
and
B
efficiently. We first partition the columns of
B
(and therefore
AB
) into blocks
B_{j}
, where
B_{j}
consists of all columns
\boldsymbol{b}_{i}
such that
2^{j-1}\leq\tilde{c}_{i}<2^{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,r
, where
r\approx\log_{2}n
. We then compute
AB_{j}
for each

using the algorithm for output-column-sparse matrix multiplication sketched earlier using the assumption (which holds with high probability) that each column of
AB_{j}
is
2^{j+1}
-sparse.

We note that the column-sparsity-estimation-based algorithm sketched here uses randomness in a different way from
2
; it uses randomness to estimate column sparsities and does not need to solve
\mathrm{Column\textrm{-}Wise\textrm{-}MMV}

\mathrm{OSMM}
to
\mathrm{Column\textrm{-}Wise\textrm{-}MMV}
that we use to prove

2

Proposition 2.7

(Naïve Input-Sparse Matrix Multiplication)
.

Let

for a ring

and

. Let

be
t
-column-sparse for

. Then
\lVert AB\rVert_{0}\leq t\cdot\lVert B\rVert_{0}
, and there is a deterministic algorithm that computes

using

R
.

Proof.

For
i\in[n]
, let

be the number of non-zero elements in the
i
th row of

. If we consider the multiplication of
A
and

as a sum of outer products, then for every

, the outer product of the

th column of

and the
k
th row of

can be computed using at most

∎

3.3.1
Column-Wise Matrix Multiplication Verification

In this section, we introduce and give an algorithm for the variant of
\mathrm{MMV}
in which the goal is to check whether each column of
AB
is equal to its corresponding column in
C^{\prime}
.

Definition 3.4
.

For a ring
R
, the Column-Wise MMV problem over
R
(
\mathrm{Column\textrm{-}Wise\textrm{-}MMV}_{R}
) is defined as follows.
The input consists of matrices
A\in R^{m\times n}
,
B\in R^{n\times p}
, and
C^{\prime}=(\boldsymbol{c}_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\boldsymbol{c}_{p}^{\prime})\in R^{m\times p}
.
Let
C=(\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{c}_{p}):=AB
.
The goal is to output the set
J\subseteq[p]
of indices of columns on which
C
and
C^{\prime}
differ, i.e.,

J:=\{{j\in[p]:\boldsymbol{c}_{j}\neq\boldsymbol{c}_{j}^{\prime}}\}\ \text{.}

We note that “normal”
\mathrm{MMV}
corresponds to deciding whether
J=\emptyset
in
Definition

3.4
, and so
\mathrm{MMV}
easily reduces to
\mathrm{Column\textrm{-}Wise\textrm{-}MMV}
.
We next argue that a slight variant of Freivalds’s algorithm
[
Fre79
]
also solves
\mathrm{Column\textrm{-}Wise\textrm{-}MMV}
in nearly quadratic time.

Lemma 3.5

(Freivalds’s algorithm for
\mathrm{Column\textrm{-}Wise\textrm{-}MMV}
)
.

Let
R
be a ring and let
c\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
be a constant. For any
m,n,p\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, there is a randomized algorithm that solves
\mathrm{Column\textrm{-}Wise\textrm{-}MMV}_{R}
on matrices
A\in R^{m\times n},B\in R^{n\times p},C^{\prime}\in R^{m\times p}
performing
O(\max\{{mn,np}\}\cdot\log(mnp))
arithmetic operations over
R
and has a success probability of at least
1-1/(mnp)^{c}
.
In particular, when
m,p=O(n)
, the algorithm performs
O(n^{2}\log n)
arithmetic operations over
R
and has a success probability of at least
1-1/n^{c}
.

Proof.

The algorithm works as follows. First, it samples a uniformly random matrix
X\sim\{{0,1}\}^{N\times m}
for
N:=\lceil{(c+1)\log(mnp)}\rceil
. It then computes
D=(\boldsymbol{d}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{d}_{p}):=X(AB-C^{\prime})
, and outputs
J^{\prime}:=\{{i\in[p]:\boldsymbol{d}_{i}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}\}
.

We start by showing correctness of the algorithm, i.e., that
J^{\prime}=J
for
J
as defined in
Definition

3.4
.
For a fixed non-zero vector
\boldsymbol{y}\in R^{m}
and a uniformly random
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\{{0,1}\}^{m}
,
\Pr[\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle\neq 0]\geq 1/2
. Indeed, this follows by noting that each term
x_{i}y_{i}
in
\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}y_{i}
for non-zero
y_{i}
is equal to
0
with probability
1/2
and an induction argument.
So, for any non-zero column
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
of
AB-C^{\prime}
, we get that
\Pr[X\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=\boldsymbol{0}]\leq 1/2^{N}
. (If
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=0
, then
X\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=\boldsymbol{0}
with probability
1
.)
Taking a union bound over the at most
p
non-zero columns of
AB-C^{\prime}
, we then get that the probability that there exists a non-zero column
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
of
AB-C^{\prime}
such that
\boldsymbol{d}_{i}=\boldsymbol{0}
is at most
p/2^{N}\leq p/(mnp)^{c+1}\leq 1/(mnp)^{c}
, as needed.

Furthermore, the algorithm runs in
O(N\cdot\max\{{mn,np}\})=O(\max\{{mn,np}\}\cdot\log(mnp))
time, which finishes the analysis.
∎
