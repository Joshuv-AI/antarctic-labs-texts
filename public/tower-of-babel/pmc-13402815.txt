Concurrent chemical reaction and cluster restructuring in a frustrated fluxional catalytic system identified via simple kMC model
Authors: Alexander Korobov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The possible restructuring of cluster catalysts concurrent with the catalyzed reaction is one of the most challenging issue in the field of cluster catalysis. Such out-of-equilibrium materials tempt with the prospect of essentially new functionality but are beyond established theoretical approaches, especially if catalytic system is frustrated. This increases the role of intuition and experience formalized in simple tractable models to expand the fundamental understanding. This paper suggests an as-simple-as-possible model of a fluxional frustrated catalytic system with inseparable restructuring dynamics and reaction dynamics. Its study via graph-theoretic kinetic Monte Carlo method reveals some useful characteristic features, in particular connected with the role of conformations. Hopefully, this may be useful in elaboration of next-generation dynamic catalytic systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53125-y.

Introduction

The increase of reaction rates is not the only and, perhaps, not the superior objective of the use of catalysts. Thus, efforts of the catalyst community are directed towards elaboration of essentially new catalytic processes (in particular, meeting requirements of sustainable development and green chemistry), creation of life-like catalytic systems, enhancement of selectivity and/or robustness of known processes, atom saving. In the realm of heterogeneous catalysis, much hope in this regard is associated with cluster catalysis1–11. Cluster catalysts strongly ranges in the nature and degree of fluxionality. In some cases fluxionality plays no important role which makes it possible to create stable and well–dispersed catalytic systems with high activity and selectivity12,13. The other pole of the spectrum is represented by highly fluxional catalytic systems (both thermal and reagent-induced). They possess more degrees of freedom and thus are more intriguing and promising. An essential task here is to embrace their dynamic behaviour and to learn to work with it. A central question is what is active catalytic center and how it works.

Current literature demonstrates multiple understanding of the term “dynamic behaviour”14–16. It may refer to an isolated particle (e.g. cluster) or to a much bigger catalytic system. In the later case it ranges from the set of interconverting particles that jointly works towards required chemical transformation to natural oscillations of the catalytic system to forced oscillations. The common uniting idea is that the most effective catalysts are not always the most stable or the most well-defined but most adaptive and capable to cooperative behaviour under operando conditions.

The most challenging area of cluster catalysis is fluxionality assisted catalysis17–25. This relatively fresh term overlaps to a large extent with the term dynamic catalysis and covers a vide range of related phenomena. The main hope within this paradigm is to get a deeper insight into the functioning of catalytic centres under operando conditions by going beyond the static description of their geometric and electronic structure. A considerable enhancement of the overall performance of catalytic systems is expected under proper out-of-equilibrium conditions dominated by kinetics rather than thermodynamics1. The sub-nanometre metal clusters are representative examples of high fluxionality and reshaping in response to different reaction conditions.

Thermal-driven fluxionality of clusters (due to thermal fluctuations) is in the core of this paradigm. Sometimes it is divided into plastic and elastic fluxionality. The former is the restructure of initial geometry while the latter is just ample deformations of a cluster around its equilibrium geometry. In terms of the free energy surface (FES) the former is the transition from one basing to the other while the latter occur within one basing. Note that the elastic fluxionality alone may create deformations sufficient for considerable increase of the catalytic activity relative to equilibrium geometry of a cluster. This means, in particular, that the global rearrangement of a cluster is not obligatory required; changes may affect only a small number of atoms. On the other hand, these two types of fluxionality can play together, in which case elastic fluxionality may play an important role on the background of plastic fluxionality. Finally, the two types of fluxionality can convert one to the other with the change of ambient temperature.

If we consider a set of initially identical clusters, the thermal-driven fluxionality may result in the ensemble of isomers with different stability and catalytic activity. The main catalytic activity of this ensemble may be due to metastable isomers the percentage of which is relatively small. The ensemble representation of the catalytic activity of clusters is presented in4,7–9.

In the case of adsorption of small molecules from the gas phase or substrate the picture is even more involved. Adsorbate lowers the free energy of nanoclusters. Strictly speaking, cluster and adsorbent form new entity and we get a new FES with new minima. And then above thermal fluctuations come into play on this new FES.

Finally, when it comes to a chemical reaction, there is the possibility that one cluster shape might be preferred in the reactant state, another in the intermediate state, and yet another in the product state. This reshaping is accompanied by the change in electronic structure. Here we deal with the interplay between chemical reaction and cluster reshaping (isomerization). In this context, a crucial question is the relative timescales of the reaction and of the reshaping.

The available literature suggest that the answer to this question is system-specific and generally all variants are possible. Seemingly, the simplest case is when the restructuring is sufficiently slower than chemical reaction. In this case the catalytic centre may be considered as practically static. An opposite case is when the restructuring is sufficiently faster than chemical reaction. The catalytic cluster remains invariable during the catalytic cycle. The above ensemble representation approach assumes this case.

The most problematic and most intriguing case is when the two timescales are comparable. Generally this may result in the significant coupling between the restructuring coordinate and the reaction coordinate17,21,24. It is worth noting that this coupling is not obligatory. In some cases restructuring and reaction can be separated. Then the process is stepwise, i.e. computationally more tractable. But this approach runs the risk of missing the point if coupling does take place. In this case the catalytic activity may be determined by transient species that only formed under reaction conditions.

Multiple restructuring pathways are typical for fluxional systems, and there are good grounds to expect that the free energy landscape of some of them possess energetically close local minima separated with surmountable barriers. Under certain operando conditions this set the stage for frustrated behaviour. Two main expectations associated with this behavior are higher functionality and peculiar properties of catalytic systems.

Here the cluster catalysis borders on a rapidly developing and promising fields of out-of-equilibrium materials, self-assembly materials and active matter26–28. We need to learn from nature not only how to increase speed but also how to create highly organized systems. Cluster catalysis has similarities with protein folding, enzymes and molecular machines. In this context, the concept of local frustration is of particular interest. It successfully relates protein dynamics (essential for enzymatic activity) with catalytic power. Partly this is due to asperities of the energy landscapes that provides control of thermal motion. Frustration-driven mechanism prove to be efficient in the field of enzyme catalysis: local frustration tunes enzymes internal dynamics. Most likely it will find its place in the field of cluster catalysis.

One more informative border is the border with single atom catalysis29–32. Some important nuances become clearly evident when both types of catalysis are discussed together in one context. In particular, the role of substrate, which was not discussed till now. Actually, substrate may be treated as an important part of operando conditions. Two important aspects here are immediate in situ formation of true active sites with peculiar geometric and electronic structures and peculiarities of integrating catalyst structural dynamics with reaction dynamics with the account of metal–support interactions. This considerably complicates the real fluxional frustrated dynamics of catalytic systems.

What experimental and theoretical tools are available to explore this area of cluster catalysis?

The impressive recent progress in operando experimental techniques33–38 provides the catalysis community with ever increasing insight into the dynamic nature of catalytic clusters under operando conditions. In quite a number of cases the correlation is confirmed between turnover frequency and atomic level fluxional behaviour. In some others, higher spatial and temporal resolutions are required to uncover the dynamic structure of clusters. But as we move into the realm of the most labile frustrated states we face a peculiar problem: these systems are highly sensitive to experimental impact. In other words, the operando experimental trial itself may distort the real picture. As of today, this challenge has no obvious resolution.

The recent progress in theoretical and computational methods is not less impressive39–48. The main achievement is going beyond the paradigm of global thermodynamic equilibrium. A new paradigm is elaborating. Cluster catalysts are treated as out-of-equilibrium materials to understand the reaction mechanism and catalyst active site evolution during catalysis (in particular with the account of catalyst–support interactions45–48. In addition to the complexity and high cost of the first-principle results, a stumbling block here is the propagation of errors in first principles rate constants onto the higher level of the micro-macro hierarchy, which may have strong impact on the predicted behaviour. The reagent-induced fluxional dynamics when reaction coordinate and restructuring coordinate cannot be separated is the most involved case. Strictly speaking, cluster with adsorbate need to be considered as a single entity and all degrees of freedom need to be taken into account. This is currently unattainable, which determines the importance and role of the reductionist approach within which a few key parameters are selected that ensures correct description and efficient computational screening38.

Therefore, three different sub-fields are distinguished in the field of cluster catalysis. (i) Timescales of the cluster restructuring and catalyzed reaction differ enough to make them independent. (ii) Timescales are comparable but separate treatment is an acceptable approximation. (iii) Timescales are comparable and restructuring dynamically couple to the reaction coordinate. A fundamental difference is worth emphasizing between cases (i) and (ii), on the one hand, and case (iii) on the other hand. The former, whatever involved, are within the well established (“equilibrium”) computational paradigm. For the latter, which belongs to the out-of-equilibrium systems, the proper paradigm is far from being elaborated. In this case, especially if it is complicated by frustration26, simple heuristic models are a tools that may be useful in advancing the field, e.g. as building blocks for constructing more realistic reaction networks. Note in this connection the discussion of the subtle interplay between behaviour and mechanism in the case of different, much simpler systems49. Thus, accurate predictions can be made in some cases without full knowledge of the mechanisms, jut based on identifying the behavioural signatures that constrain possible mechanisms. One more efficient possibility in this logic is to use machine learning to explore the connections between local frustrations, dynamics and function15.

Generally, one can assume three scenarios according to which a single cluster exhibits its catalytic activity.

i.After adsorption of reactant molecule(s) cluster plays its role as a catalyst, while the cluster itself does not undergo any significant changes during the elementary act of the reaction. Cluster with adsorbed reactant(s) form a new entity (ensemble) within which the components retain to a considerable extent their autonomy. Reaction coordinate in this case does not involve degrees of freedom associated with the cluster.ii.Cluster has suitable adsorption centre, but the formed adsorption complex is not catalytically active. At the same time, adsorption significantly facilitate restructuring. And the newly formed geometry is catalytically active. Upon desorption of products the cluster return to its original geometry. Strictly speaking, cluster and adsorbate form a single entity. But on the time scale processes of restructuring and chemical reaction are well separated: first restructuring and then chemical reaction. As a result, restructuring coordinate and reaction coordinate are well separatable.iii.The third option resembles the previous one in that the initial adsorption result in catalytically inactive entity and significantly facilitate the subsequent restructuring. But this restructuring leads to another catalytically inactive geometry. The transient transition state that leads, in particular, to product(s) is formed on the way between these two geometries. In this, most involved, case the reaction coordinate and restructuring coordinate cannot be separated. They have the same timescale.

The present paper deals with the third, most involved, scenario. A simplest possible model is represented by the graph shown in Fig. 1. The first step is the adsorption of the reactant by the cluster. Thus formed adsorption complex A, formally a single entity, is catalytically inactive and very fluxional. It tends to restructure into another geometry C, also catalytically inactive. A peculiarity of this restructuring is the highly catalytically active transient transition state B on the way from A to C and back. Thus, in B the system has a chance to react or, alternatively, convert into A or C. In the former case the cluster is released from the product (vertex D) and is ready for a new cycle. Otherwise it continues to fluctuate between A and C.

The model is characterized by six energy barriers: E1f and E1b for the reversible restructuring edge A−B, E2f and E2b for the reversible restructuring edge B−C, E3 for the irreversible reaction edge B−D, and E4 for the irreversible adsorption edge D−A. Note that modern experimental and theoretical methods generally make it possible to establish the structure of small clusters, free or with an adsorbate. But this applies to fairly stable clusters. In our context, we are interested in very labile clusters, sensitive to small experimental impacts and theoretically intractable. This applies equally to all four states. Accordingly, the above barriers are ad hoc parameters of the model. They should be close enough to set the stage for frustrated behaviour of the system.

The graph-theoretic kinetic Monte Carlo approach is used to test the behaviour of the model. The probability of passing along edges of the graph is determined by corresponding rate constants. They are calculated from the above energy barriers for each temperature T according to Arrhenius equation; the pre-exponential factor is A = 1013 s− 1. These rate constants determine the navigation through the graph. The cumulative method is used. Each edge pass is accompanied by the advance of the clock by Δt = − lnu/R, where u is the uniform random number (u∈(0,1]) and R is the total rate.

It is convenient to subdivide each trajectory into cycles. Each cycle start and finish at D. The B→D transition means the appearance of one entity of product. Two main characteristics are registered during the cycle: time spend to obtain the product entity tt (total time of the cycle) and total number of edge passes nt. Two more characteristics are the attendance of each vertex and the number of passes along each particular edge.

The statistical level is the same as in previous papers, 106 runs. All characteristics are averaged and normalized per one product entity. The averaged time required to obtain one product entity is inversely proportional to the average product formation rate. In this way we get the kinetic characteristic of the model extracted from trajectory statistics. To test the model for Arrhenius behaviour, this quantity was computed for five values of temperature in the range 300–1100 K. In all studied cases dependence of ln(tt) on (1/T) demonstrate perfect linearity. This means the possibility to calculate two quantities, the apparent activation energy Eapp = αkB and the apparent pre-exponential factor Aapp = exp(-β), where α is the slope, β is the free term, kB is the Boltzmann constant. These characteristics of the model are obtained from the trajectory statistics and are not linked to any particular energy barrier.

Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4 S of the Supplement give an idea of ​​the behaviour of the catalytic centre with an alternate increase or decrease of one of the barriers.

In Tables 1S and 2S, in the second column, time t0 of formation of unit product is given. The rest columns give the ratio of the formation time t with an increased (Table 1S) or decreased (Table 2S) barrier to t0. This ratio shows how much the product formation time increases or decreases as a result of changing the barrier height. The efficient rate constant is inversely proportional to this time. Activation energies and pre-exponential factors are given at the bottom of the table. There are two type of the response on the barrier change: (i) strong and temperature dependent increase of t and (ii) moderate decrease of t with weak temperature dependence. The former case gives a prefect straight line in Arrhenius coordinates. Figure 2 illustrate this for the barrier E1f. When the barrier height is increased, strong response is observed in four cases. Two remaining barriers demonstrate weak response. And just these barriers demonstrate strong response when decreased.

Table 3 S and 4 S show the total number of runs of the representative point along the graph edges per unit product. In the case of all equal barriers, this value is equal to 7 and is independent of both temperature and barrier height. There are three barriers, the change in the height of which does not affect the total number of runs: Е1f, Е2b and Е4. They are the same for the increase and decrease of the barrier height. When the other three change, the total number of runs also changes.

These include the reaction barrier E3. When it increases, the representative point becomes largely locked in the conformational subsystem А↔В↔С; the number of runs along just these edges increases significantly. It is strongly temperature dependent: the higher the temperature, the greater the chance that the representative point will overcome the reaction barrier. Accordingly, the shorter the time of product formation. The temperature dependence is exponential (Fig. 3). The efficient rate constant decreases accordingly; for example, k(700 K) by approximately 15 times. As can be seen from the Table 3 S, this decrease occurs both due to an increase in the efficient activation energy and due to a decrease in the pre-exponential factor. But if the reaction barrier is lowered, the picture is completely different (Table 4 S). The sum of the runs along the edges is reduced. The temperature dependence of the sum of runs is very weak. The reduction in product formation time also depends weakly on temperature. The efficient rate constant increases, e.g. in the case of k(700 K) by 2.3 times. Note that in this case the effective activation energy remains equal to 1 eV, and the increase in the rate constant is due exclusively to the increase in the pre-exponential factor (Fig. 4).

The invariance of nt with respect of the barrier height means that the main route of the representative point is preserved and the fluxionality is not increased. When these barriers are increased, the time for product formation increases significantly due to the fact that the representative point is locked, respectively, in A or C or D. For barriers E2b and E4, the values ​​of the ratio t/t0 are practically the same, for E1f they are approximately twice as large since the representative point falls in A more often. As any of the three barriers increases, the efficient rate constant decreases. It is noteworthy that this occurs solely due to an increase in the efficient activation energy. Moreover, the pre-exponential factor increases significantly. On the contrary, when these barriers are reduced, the rate constant increases solely due to the pre-exponential factor, while the efficient activation energy remains practically unchanged. The values ​​of the ratio t/t0 are practically independent of temperature.

The pre-exponential factor is sensitive to all changes in barriers. For the efficient activation energy this is not the case: in half of the cases it remains practically unchanged. In particular, when the barrier of reaction E3 is lowered, the effective rate constant increases only due to the pre-exponential factor. This relationship between the efficient activation energy and pre-exponential factor indicates that the “energy cost” of product formation has not changed, but the conditions for reaching the limiting barrier have improved50–54. The steric factor can be improved, for example, due to a more favourable mutual orientation of the parts of the reaction centre, in particular during restructuring. It follows that in such cases, to control the reaction rate, one should work with features that influence the pre-exponential factor. Within the model under study, an interesting possibility is the gradual decrease of the reaction barrier due to conformational dynamics of the reaction centre.

This conjuncture may be formalized as follows. Suppose that each time when the representative point, in the course of restructuring, passes the edge А→В or С→В the height of the reaction barrier E3 decreases by the amount δb.

Overall, the picture remains without significant changes. But there are nuances that can be clarified in terms of reaction barrier histograms.

The reaction barrier is lowered from the initial value E3.0=1.0 eV in step δb. The histogram shows parts of the product formed with the overcome of the barriers Е3,0−δb, Е3,0−2δb, etc. In the case of the M1 model (left part of Fig. 3), when the representative point first passes along the A→B edge, the reaction barrier decreases by 0.01 eV and becomes smaller than the others. Accordingly, the B→D reaction channel has an advantage over the B→A and B→C channels, which determine the conformational dynamics. Accordingly, the conformational dynamics is fairly small, nt=5. As a result, the first barrier (Е3,0−δb) dominates the set of five barriers that form sequentially at 300 K. As the temperature increases to 700 K, states A and C become more accessible and conformational dynamics somewhat more active, nt = = 6. As a result, a greater percentage of the product is formed via lower barriers. In the case of the M2 model the picture is completely different (right part of Fig. 3). Here, at first, the conformational dynamics, namely the movement of the representative point along the B↔C edge, clearly predominates (nt=34). This leads to a gradual decrease of the reaction barrier. Overcoming it happens rarely at first. As the barrier decreases, the frequency of overcoming it increases and reaches a maximum when the reaction barrier is comparable with barriers E2f and E2b. An increase in temperature leads simultaneously to a decrease in conformational dynamics (nt=20) and overcoming a higher barrier. As a result, the higher barrier predominates, but the lower barriers also account for a significant percentage of the product formation. As δb increases, the number of steps to lower the reaction barrier decreases. Ultimately, in one step, the reaction barrier becomes significantly smaller than the barriers E2f and E2b, the conformational component of the model disappears and it is reduced to the model of a static active centre.

In the case of conventional static catalytic centre the only way to increase its productivity is the increase of temperature. In the case of a fluxional frustrated catalytic centre the conformational dynamics promises one more option.

The role of conformational dynamics has been studied with special attention in the field of enzymatic catalysis. The accumulated body of knowledge indicates that it can also take place in cluster catalysis. Understanding similarities and differences in these cases may be important for both a deeper understanding of the phenomenon and for the creation of new cluster catalysts. The issue of the interplay between reaction coordinate and restructuring coordinate is one of main stumbling blocks. The most involved and intriguing case is when they are inseparable, i.e. have the close time scales. There are significant difficulties in all approaches to this case. Within the bottom-up approach this is a huge number of the degrees of freedom and also the problem of propagation of errors in first principles rate constants onto the higher level. Within the top-down approach this is, first of all, a high sensitivity to experimental interventions. With this in mind, any additional tools that provide an insight into the nature and functionality of catalytic systems under study seem to be in demand. Thus, heuristics may be helpful to capture main regularities and trends, which can guide the design and optimization of catalytic systems. Previously, simple models prove to be useful in getting insight into several issues of dynamic frustrated catalytic systems: stability55,56, efficiency57, supply- production dependence58.

The suggested model illustrates the principle possibility to increase the efficiency of a frustrated fluxional catalytic cluster due to its conformational dynamics. An important feature of the model is equal starting barriers for reaction and (adsorption-induced) restructuring, which makes it impossible to separate the reaction coordinate and restructuring coordinate. Another distinctive feature is the possible decrease of the reaction barrier as a result of the interplay of restructuring dynamics and reaction dynamics. This may be interpreted as an adjustment of the reactant with respect to cluster.

The model provides an idea about distinctive features of behaviour of the considered catalytic system. An unexpected feature concerns the Arrhenius behaviour. In spite of initial expectations, Arrhenius plots were perfectly linear in all studied cases (R2 = 1). The calculated efficient activation energy for the system as a whole far not always respond to changes in the elementary barriers. Thus, in the most interesting case of the decrease of the reaction barrier it was practically invariable, remaining in the range of 0.98–1.0 eV. It is the pre-exponential factor that appeared to be sensitive to absolutely all transformations of the catalytic system. In the case of E3 decrease it quantifies the increase of the efficiency of the catalytic system. This is illustrated for the efficient rate constant computed, as an example, for T = 700 K.

Note that when all barriers are equal, the total number of runs along the edges of the graph does not depend on temperature. It is also independent of the height of barriers. But if one barrier is slightly changed, this quantity is also changed and becomes temperature dependent. Thus, it is a sensitive indicator of the difference between restructuring and reaction barriers. When the former strongly dominates, the model is reduced to simple static active centre. When the later strongly dominates, no reaction is possible. This shows that the model under discussion assumes a certain proximity of barriers. Beyond this, it converts into another model. Seemingly, certain restrictions on the ratio of barrier heights is a general condition of the existence of fluxional frustrated systems.

To what extent the obtained results are model-specific? To answer this question, similar computations were made with different values of the Arrhenius prefactor, with the reversible adsorption stage, and with breathing reaction barrier E3. Table 5 S shows the results of computations in which two orders of magnitude smaller Arrhenius prefactor was used in computing reaction constants. These results practically reproduce regularities given in corresponding Table 2S. To simulate the reversible adsorption, additional A→D edge is added to the graph; the corresponding barrier is denoted E4b. Results given in Table 6 S shows that reversibility of adsorption does not significantly change the general picture. The rule of the behaviour of reaction barrier was modified as follows. When representative point passes the edge A→B the height of the reaction barrier E3 decreases, as before, by the amount δb. But when it passes the edge С→В the height of E3 increases by the same amount. Thus, the barrier is breathing around its average value. As can be seen from Table 7 S, this modification of the model also does not change the noted interplay between the efficient activation energy and efficient prefactor.

To sum up, fluxionality during reaction is rightly considered one of the most significant challenges. The issue is well recognized, but it is outside the established theoretical approaches. Significant problems within both bottom-up and top-down approaches justify the use of heuristics in order to advance in fundamental understanding and, hopefully, find ways towards designing next-generation dynamic catalytic systems. This requires, in particular, integration of structural dynamics with reaction dynamics. Works in the field are relatively few. This paper suggests and examines via GT-kMC simulation as simple as possible model of a fluxional frustrated catalytic system with inseparable restructuring dynamics and reaction dynamics. In particular, the principle possibility is shown to increase its efficiency due to conformations. The reviled combination of the practically invariable efficient activation energy with very sensitive pre-exponential factor can serve as a guide in screening real catalysts for fluxionality and frustration.

There are two main options for further development, theory-driven and data-driven. The leitmotif of the former is to stay within the microkinetic (bottom-up) approach and to make a step towards a more realistic model, supported nanoclusters. The results obtained in58 give reason to expect that some valuable information may be inferred from the interplay between the intensity of reagent supply and regularities of product formation. In principle, experimental and simulated supply-performance curves may be compared. But this general possibility is restricted by such factors as substrate defects, insufficient control of the microenvironment of nanoclusters, etc. Another option, that has been rapidly developing lately, is data-driven (top-down) approach40,42,59,60. In this case the leitmotif is to predict and explore complex catalytic systems beyond the reach of fully mechanistic modelling. This is especially promising in the case of high-dimensional and nonlinear systems. The methodology has impressive results exemplified e.g. by60. They are based on the availability of the sufficient amount of quantitative and well-structured data. In the case under study the available data is much more limited and the chemical nature of nanocluster was not specified. Under these conditions it is logical to start with the inverse problem: proceeding from a particular pattern (e.g. practically invariable efficient activation energy + very sensitive efficient pre-exponential factor) restore particular values of selected descriptors (e.g. cluster size, d-band centre, adsorption energy of key intermediate, energy spread of isomers). These values may be then related with corresponding values from available data bases. Also, thus trained algorithm may be useful for ML-assisted literature data mining. Anywhere, this will contribute to the growth of relevant data.

The above two options are not incompatible. A modern trend is hybrid approaches42 within which microkinetic modelling provides physics constraints and machine learning captures complexity. Within ML part descriptors are embedded into activation barriers and pre-factors, after which MKM model is solved. In the long run this tend to develop into an efficient approach, and simple MKM models may play the role of useful seeds.
