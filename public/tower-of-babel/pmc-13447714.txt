Postnatal Care Practice and the Facilitating Factors Among Japanese Primary Care Physicians: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Miho Endo, Keiichiro Narumoto, Makoto Kaneko, Tomoko Iwata, Motoi Sugimura, Machiko Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPostnatal care encompasses multiple health and social domains requiring a comprehensive approach. Primary care physicians (PCPs) are well‐positioned to provide postnatal care, but research on their practice remains limited. This study aimed to investigate PCPs' practice frequency and attitudes toward postnatal care, and to examine PCP attributes associated with postnatal care provision.MethodsWe conducted a cross‐sectional study with an online questionnaire targeting certified PCPs via the Japan Primary Care Association. The questionnaire assessed frequency, perceived importance, and educational experience across 13 postnatal care domains. Practice frequency and perceived importance were examined descriptively on a five‐point Likert scale, and the association between PCP attributes and postnatal care provision was analyzed by logistic regression.ResultsWe analyzed 259 responses (response rate: 17.4%). Practice frequency was highest for follow‐up of non‐pregnancy‐related chronic conditions (79%) and maternal health care maintenance (71%), and lowest for intimate partner violence (25%) and postpartum gynecological examination (20%). Mean perceived importance scores exceeded mean practice frequency scores across all postnatal care domains. Intimate partner violence showed the largest practice‐attitude discrepancy. Having received education in one domain was positively associated with practice in that domain. Other factors related to practice included female gender, immunization of infants, physicians' experience with raising children or postnatal health problems, and practicing in a rural area.ConclusionThere were variations in practice frequency and discrepancies between practice frequency and attitudes across postnatal care domains among Japanese PCPs. Addressing medical education as a modifiable factor may help reduce these gaps.

Introduction

Postnatal care encompasses a broad spectrum of healthcare and social and developmental support services to maintain and promote the health and well‐being of women, parents, caregivers, and families [1, 2]. It is an ongoing, personalized care process that addresses individual needs throughout the postnatal period [3], which can extend beyond the typical six‐week postpartum timeframe. Postnatal care includes multiple domains, such as maternal physical recovery and mental health, family planning, and support for returning to work, with the priority of each domain evolving throughout the postpartum period. Therefore, primary care physicians (PCPs) are well positioned to provide comprehensive and coordinated postnatal care while better understanding the life context surrounding postpartum women and their families in a longitudinal patient‐provider relationship [4].

In Japan, postnatal care is primarily provided by perinatal care teams, including midwives, public health nurses, and obstetricians, particularly at the four‐week routine postpartum checkup. However, ensuring seamless, comprehensive support across all postnatal domains beyond this time remains challenging. PCPs trained in providing continuity of care across various health issues and family‐oriented primary care are well‐suited to be involved in comprehensive postnatal care. Although Japanese PCPs encounter postpartum women in their daily practice [5], no data on their engagement in postnatal care exist.

Globally, research on the extent to which PCPs provide each domain of postnatal care is very scarce. Gunn et al. revealed that 92% of general practitioners in Australia were involved in the postpartum check‐up at six weeks [6], but their study focused on postnatal care as a whole, without considering individual domains (e.g., physical recovery, mental health, infant care, family planning, and support for return to work). On the other hand, Krishnamurti et al. [7] investigated PCPs' practices across individual domains in the United States. They focused on discrepancies between the PCPs' perceived priority and their practice; however, no data on the actual frequency of practice in each domain were provided. Both studies limited their scope to the six‐week postpartum visit.

Regarding PCPs' attributes related to postnatal care provision, previous studies identified the PCP's female gender [6, 8, 9, 10], age [6], certification in obstetrics and gynecology [10], childcare experiences of their own children, lack of knowledge and training experiences, and unfamiliarity with local postnatal care services [9]. However, these attributes were examined in relation to postnatal care as a whole, without considering individual domains.

Given the lack of evidence on Japanese PCPs' engagement in postnatal care and the limitations of previous studies, this study aimed to investigate Japanese PCPs' practice frequency and attitudes across postnatal care domains and to examine PCP factors associated with the provision of each postnatal care domain. It sought to establish a benchmark for the potential role of Japanese PCPs in postnatal care and contribute to addressing existing knowledge gaps in this field.

Method

Design

This was a cross‐sectional study of Japanese PCPs using an online questionnaire. The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Hamamatsu University School of Medicine on February 2, 2022 (21–294).

Setting

In Japan, primary care services are provided by various medical specialties [11], not all of whom necessarily have had formal training in family/general medicine. The Japan Primary Care Association (JPCA), the largest professional organization representing family medicine/general medicine, initiated the certification of primary care specialists in 2010, which was subsequently replaced by the Japanese Medical Specialty Board (JMSB) in 2018 [11]. However, the JMSB curriculum guidelines for primary care residency do not require mandatory obstetrics/gynecology (OB/GYN) rotations [12]. As a result, primary care residents have limited opportunities to acquire comprehensive knowledge and skills in women's health [13], and women's health care, including contraception and gynecologic cancer screening, is the least frequently provided by Japanese PCPs [14].

Additionally, Japanese PCPs are not generally involved in routine postnatal care. It is typically provided at four weeks postpartum by perinatal care teams at the facility where women have childbirth. Obstetricians tend to focus on physical recovery and follow‐up of obstetric complications. No formal or informal handoff is established between perinatal care teams and PCPs. Public health nurses provide home visits to all postpartum women around two weeks after delivery and thereafter as needed. Although they are likely to address a range of postpartum health issues, such as support for breastfeeding, physical recovery, and emotional disturbances [15], their postpartum care is not covered by health insurance and therefore does not include prescribing medications or ordering diagnostic tests.

Participants

In the study, PCPs were defined as those certified by either the JPCA or JMSB. Participants were recruited through the JPCA mailing list (5811 registered physicians), including 1417 JPCA‐certified PCPs. According to demographic data on the JPCA‐certified PCPs at the beginning of the study, 81.2% were male, with a mean age of 47.6 years and 20.7 years of clinical experience. There were 74 JMSB‐certified PCPs, according to the JMSB records. However, their number on the JPCA mailing list was uncertain. To ensure comprehensive coverage of the target population, we assumed 1491 PCPs (1417 JPCA‐certified and 74 JMSB‐certified) on the mailing list and distributed an online questionnaire to all 5811 registered physicians on March 11, 2022; responses were collected until May 8, 2022. Five email reminders were sent at two‐week intervals. Informed consent was considered obtained when participants proceeded with the survey, as it indicated their agreement to participate. The first 200 respondents were awarded a gift card worth 1000 Japanese yen (approximately 6 US dollars) as an honorarium.

Measurements

The postnatal period is typically defined as six to twelve weeks following childbirth [2, 3]. However, physical and mental recovery from pregnancy and childbirth can extend beyond this timeframe. Hence, for the purpose of this study, the postnatal period was defined as 12 months after childbirth [16].

To ensure terminological clarity, the term “postnatal” was used in a broad context encompassing both mothers and infants [16], whereas “postpartum” was used when focusing specifically on mothers.

Additionally, PCPs' practices of postnatal care were operationalized as the frequency of care provision and their attitudes toward postnatal care as the perceived importance of it, respectively.

Online Questionnaire

The questionnaire was developed based on the previous studies examining potential contributing factors to the provision of postnatal care [6, 9, 10, 17, 18] (File S1). It consisted of a total of 28 questions, encompassing participant demographics and practice characteristics, practice frequency, perceived importance, and educational experience across 13 domains of postnatal care: postpartum gynecological examination, postpartum physical recovery, follow‐up of pregnancy‐related complications, follow‐up of non‐pregnancy‐related chronic conditions, breastfeeding and breast‐related issues, infant care, sexual health issues, family planning, maternal mental health, family member's/partner's mental health, maternal health care maintenance, intimate partner violence (IPV), and support for return to work [1, 3, 19].

Demographics and practice characteristics included: age, gender, years in practice, certified specialty, average number of outpatient visits, number of examinations of postpartum women, examination and vaccination of infants, personal experience with childcare, childbirth, and postnatal health problems, type of medical facility where they mainly work, rurality of the location of the medical facility, population of the practice location, and training background in OB/GYN and psychiatry [6, 9, 10, 17, 18]. In this study, training experiences in OB/GYN and psychiatry were defined as block rotations lasting at least four days per week, which inherently included the mandatory one‐month training for each specialty required during the initial two‐year postgraduate clinical training in Japan. The questionnaire also assessed general and postnatal mental health care; the results for postpartum mental health care were reported separately [5].

The frequency of PCPs' practice and their perceived importance for each domain of postnatal care were assessed using a five‐point Likert scale, following Krishnamurti et al. (1 = not at all, 2 = rarely, 3 = occasionally, 4 = often, 5 = always for frequency of practice; 1 = not at all important, 2 = a little important, 3 = moderately important, 4 = very important, 5 = extremely important for perceived importance) [7]. The educational experiences were assessed using binary response options (yes/no).

The questionnaire was revised based on feedback from 15 PCPs and family/general medicine trainees to improve clarity before distribution. For example, less action‐oriented wording such as ’touch upon the topic during the consultation’ was changed to ’address the topic during the consultation’ to better capture active clinical engagement.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of the study included the frequency of PCPs' practice and their perceived importance across 13 domains of postnatal care. Each postnatal care domain was plotted based on the average scores, with the frequency of practice on the x‐axis and the perceived importance on the y‐axis, following the approach employed by Krishnamurti et al. [7]. Then, the characteristics and trends of the relationships between practices and their attitudes were examined.

The secondary outcomes included PCP demographics and practice characteristics associated with their practice in each domain of postnatal care.

Analysis

All available data were included in the analysis, as there were no missing values. Due to the unknown number of JMSB‐certified PCPs on the JPCA mailing list, we assumed that all 74 JMSB‐certified PCPs, who were mutually exclusive from JPCA‐certified PCPs, were on the mailing list to evaluate the response rate.

Descriptive analysis was conducted for PCPs' demographics and practice characteristics data. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and proportions, while continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on data normality. The patterns and trends in the relationships between their practice frequency and perceived importance of postnatal care were described.

Logistic regression analysis with the forced entry method was employed to examine the PCP factors associated with their postnatal care provision. The frequency of PCPs' postnatal care practice was categorized into two groups as a prespecified categorical dependent variable (yes/no): ‘always/often/occasionally’ and ‘not at all/rarely,’ respectively. PCPs' age and years in practice exhibited the variance inflation factors (7.8 and 8.8, respectively) and high variance proportions (0.99 and 0.77, respectively) on a given dimension, indicating potential collinearity between these variables [20, 21, 22]. Therefore, we included years in practice, considered more clinically relevant, in the logistic regression model. Additionally, we incorporated either OB/GYN or psychiatry training into the model based on their relevance to the specific domains of postnatal care. Given that all potential factors associated with PCPs' practice of postnatal care were based on previous studies and that our analysis was exploratory, all relevant variables were included in the logistic regression analysis. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated.

Since logistic regression analysis typically requires a minimum of 10 events per variable [23], and there were up to 12 independent variables in this study, a minimum sample size of 120 per event was deemed necessary. Considering a previous study [10] and the limited data availability in Japan, we assumed that approximately 50% of PCPs would practice a given domain of postnatal care. This calculation resulted in a minimum required sample size of 240. IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 was used for data analysis.

Results

We received a total of 339 responses. After excluding 80 responses from non‐PCPs, we analyzed a final sample of 259 valid responses (response rate: 17.4%).

Table 1 shows the PCP demographics and practice characteristics. The mean age of the participants was 44 years, with males comprising 71% of the sample. Fifty percent of the participants reported working in clinics, and 19% reported working in rural areas. The median duration of their medical practice was 17 years. Approximately 80% of the participants had received training in OB/GYN, and another 80% in psychiatry. Among the participants, 39% reported seeing 20 or more outpatients per half‐day, and 79% had opportunities to examine at least one postpartum woman within the past six months. Nearly half of the participants provided care or administered immunizations to infants. Regarding personal experience, 80% of the participants had raised children, and 37% had encountered postnatal health problems.

Figure 1 shows the proportions of Japanese PCP participants by practice frequency in postnatal care domains. The domains with the highest frequency of practice (the proportion responding to always, often, or occasionally) were the follow‐up of non‐pregnancy‐related chronic conditions (79%), maternal health care maintenance (71%), and maternal mental health (61%), while those with the lowest frequency of practice were sexual health issues (27%), intimate partner violence (25%) and postpartum gynecological examination (20%).

Frequency of practicing postnatal care domains. Figure 1 shows the proportions of Japanese PCP participants according to their frequency of practicing the postnatal care domains.

Regarding educational experiences, the domains with the highest rates of educational attainment were the follow‐up of non‐pregnancy‐related chronic conditions (53%), follow‐up of pregnancy‐related complications (48%), and maternal health care maintenance (46%), while those with the lowest rates were support for return to work (13%), intimate partner violence (22%), and a family member's or partner's mental health (22%) (File S2).

Figure 2 illustrates the distributions of practice frequency and the perceived importance of the postnatal care domains. Overall, there was a general trend of high perceived importance across the domains, while practice frequency was lower. Several domains of postnatal care, including the follow‐up of chronic diseases, maternal mental health, and maternal health care maintenance, were perceived as highly important and practiced frequently. IPV demonstrated the largest gap between practice frequency and perceived importance.

The perceived importance and the frequency of PCPs' practice of postnatal care. Figure 2 illustrates the distribution of the frequency of practice and the perceived importance across postnatal care domains. The x‐ and y‐axes indicate the frequency of practice and the perceived importance of each domain, respectively. The distance of each point from the diagonal reference line, where perceived importance equals practice frequency, visually represents the practice‐attitude discrepancy. For example, points located higher and further to the left indicate domains with a larger discrepancy, such as intimate partner violence.

Table 2 shows the results of logistic regression analysis, indicating that across all domains of postnatal care, receiving education in one domain was significantly associated with actual practice in that domain. The female gender of PCPs was positively associated with PCPs addressing postpartum physical recovery (OR 2.43, 95% CI 1.32 to 4.50), breastfeeding and breast‐related issues (OR 2.54, 95% CI 1.32 to 4.90), support of infant care (OR 2.20, 95% CI 1.11 to 4.34), IPV (OR 1.94, 95% CI 1.01 to 3.72) and support for return to work (OR 2.19, 95% CI 1.22 to 3.94). Administering vaccines to infants showed a positive association with the follow‐up of non‐pregnancy‐related chronic conditions (OR 3.50, 95% CI 1.29 to 9.52). The rurality of practice location was associated with increased provision of support of infant care (OR 2.73, 95% CI 1.23 to 6.07). Personal experience with postnatal health problems was positively associated (OR 2.45, 95% CI 1.23 to 4.88), but experience with raising children was negatively associated (OR 0.34, 95% CI 0.15 to 0.78) with family planning. Neither clinical psychiatry nor OB/GYN training showed any significant associations with the relevant domains.

Discussion

This study revealed a consistent pattern in which Japanese PCPs' perceived importance exceeded practice frequency across postnatal care, with IPV demonstrating the largest practice‐attitude discrepancy. Additionally, we found a positive association between receiving education in a specific domain and the corresponding practice across all the postnatal care domains.

Strengths and Limitations

This study is the first to describe Japanese PCPs' postnatal care practices and their relationship with attitudes, highlighting the practice‐attitude gap, and to comprehensively examine PCP factors associated with their practices across individual postnatal care domains rather than postnatal care as a whole.

Nevertheless, the interpretation and generalizability of the results require caution due to several limitations. First, the response rate was low. The survey may not have reached all PCPs through our approach due to several potential reasons: some JMSB‐certified PCPs might not have registered for the JPCA mailing list, we did not employ mixed‐mode surveys to contact nonrespondents by mail, nonrespondents' email functions might have blocked messages, or the reminders were insufficient [24]. The low response rate may indicate the potential for selection bias [25], particularly self‐selection bias, in which PCPs with a greater interest in or experience with postnatal care may have been more inclined to respond to the questionnaire. Additionally, PCP respondents in the study had a higher proportion of females than that of the JPCA‐certified PCP population (28.6% versus 18.8%), which may overestimate the results, given that female physicians tend to be more involved in postnatal care [10]. In this context, the practice‐attitude gap could be even greater in the broader PCP population, as PCPs who were less engaged in postnatal care may have been underrepresented. However, a low response rate does not necessarily compromise the accuracy of study estimates, and the response rate itself does not determine the value of the study findings [26, 27]. Second, the findings may have limited external validity for countries with primary care systems that significantly differ from those of Japan. Third, the logistic regression model included a relatively large number of variables, raising concerns about multicollinearity, reduced robustness of associations, spurious findings, and overfitting [28]. While we examined multicollinearity, other limitations may remain. Finally, due to the cross‐sectional design of this study, causality cannot be inferred. For instance, the positive association between educational experience and practice frequency may be subject to reverse causation, wherein physicians with a preexisting interest in postnatal care are more likely to seek out relevant training opportunities. Potential confounding factors influencing this association should also be acknowledged.

Comparison With Existing Literature

In our study, we plotted the PCPs' practice frequency and perceived importance for each domain of postnatal care, following the methods of Krishnamurti et al. [7]. Japanese PCP participants provided postpartum gynecological exams, sexual health issues, and family planning less frequently than U.S. primary care providers in their study. Unlike in the United States and Australia, where family physicians or general practitioners typically receive OB/GYN training during residency and are expected to provide postnatal care as part of their professional role, Japanese PCPs are not required to complete OB/GYN clinical rotations during residency and are generally not involved in routine postnatal care within the current healthcare structure in Japan [13]. Additionally, our study defined the postnatal period as extending to 12 months after delivery, while previous studies [6, 7] focused on the traditional 6‐week postpartum period. These differences in healthcare systems, training structures, expected professional roles, and the definition of the postnatal period may contribute to the observed variation in practice patterns across countries.

In contrast, we observed two consistent trends: IPV was identified as having the largest practice‐attitude gap, and maternal mental health was highly perceived as important and frequently addressed by PCPs. These findings align with observations made by Perone et al. [29], where IPV screening occurred infrequently (8.5%) compared to screenings for anxiety (37.3%) and depression (71.3%).

Despite recommendations for routine IPV screening, systemic and personal barriers remain. From the physicians' perspective, key challenges include personal discomfort, lack of knowledge, and time constraints [30]. However, physicians with greater IPV education are less likely to cite time constraints, suggesting this perception stems primarily from a lack of skills and knowledge [30]. Furthermore, patient‐related barriers including cultural stigma significantly hinder screening [29, 30, 31]. In Japan, only 4.8% of women experiencing IPV seek professional help [32], often minimizing their situation as “not serious enough” [33]. This reluctance to disclose violence due to shame is a major barrier. To overcome these barriers, PCPs can leverage continuous relationships to discuss IPV during routine encounters, such as infant vaccinations [34]. Because traditional didactic lectures are often insufficient for this sensitive role, incorporating simulation‐based learning and role‐playing into residency programs is essential to improve physicians' communication skills, attitudes, and confidence in addressing IPV [35].

The study confirmed previous research findings [6] that PCPs being female is a facilitating factor for the provision of postnatal care. Our findings provided new evidence that female PCPs were positively associated with practice in the specific domains of postnatal care, including postpartum physical recovery, breastfeeding and breast‐related issues, support of infant care, and IPV. This positive relationship may be explained by gender concordance between a physician and patient, as it has been shown to be associated with addressing gender‐specific health problems [36], screening, and counseling [37]. Female concordant dyads have shown a higher level of patient‐centeredness [38], which may encourage postpartum women to disclose gender‐sensitive health issues and enhance their help‐seeking behaviors. However, the observed relationship might be due to a system factor at the practice level, in which female physicians are more likely than male physicians to have clinical encounters with female patients [39, 40, 41].

Several findings in our study diverged from those of prior research, likely due to differences in measurement approaches. Brodribb et al. [18] identified limited consultation time as a barrier to the provision of postnatal care. In contrast, our study assessed the number of outpatients as an independent variable rather than consultation time and found no significant relationship between patient volume and PCPs' practice of postnatal care. Similarly, Poon et al. [9] suggested that PCPs' personal experience with child‐caring facilitated postnatal care; however, our study found that personal experience with raising children was an inhibiting factor. Poon et al. focused on the influence of PCPs' child‐caring experiences on their attitudes, such as confidence and empathy, toward postnatal care, whereas our study examined their actual practices of postnatal care. Although the reason for the inverse association between PCPs' own childcare experiences and their practice of family planning is not entirely clear, one possible explanation is that PCPs who are actively involved in childcare may work part‐time, thereby reducing their opportunities to provide postnatal care. In Japan, resources available to support full‐time work alongside childcare remain limited, which may further hinder PCPs' involvement in postnatal care. Further research is needed to explore this relationship and its implications.

Implications for Practice Improvement and Future Research

Our study highlights a consistent association between receiving education in each domain and practice in the corresponding domain within postnatal care, whereas no such association was found between clinical training in OB/GYN or psychiatry and practice in domains relevant to those fields. Although approximately 80% of PCP participants reported receiving such training, this is likely to reflect mandatory block rotations during their initial postgraduate years. Such training typically focuses on urgent/emergent care and inpatient management addressing only limited aspects of postnatal care (e.g., postpartum hemorrhage, postpartum depression). Since our study only investigated whether PCPs had received any type of education in each postnatal care domain, without inquiring about specific educational formats, it limits understanding of specific educational approaches to effectively improve PCPs' postnatal care practice. Nevertheless, focusing on educational interventions in a particular domain, such as IPV, which our study showed was less frequently practiced (Figure 1) or taught (File S2), may be effective, as existing evidence indicates [35]. Furthermore, providing comprehensive postnatal care requires advanced clinical skills, as the multiple domains of postnatal care are intricately intertwined within family and social contexts. Therefore, there is a need to develop effective teaching methods that enhance understanding of the interconnectedness and interactions among the postnatal care domains.

Beyond educational factors alone, structural barriers within the Japanese healthcare context may also contribute to the gap between attitudes and practice. In Japan, early postnatal care is primarily provided by perinatal care teams (e.g., obstetricians and midwives) and public health nurses. Because formal handoff systems between perinatal facilities and primary care clinics are not yet widely established, PCPs have limited opportunities to participate in this early postnatal phase. This structural fragmentation, together with existing role delineation, may lead PCPs to view postnatal care as falling outside their scope of practice. In addition, limited consultation time in high‐volume outpatient settings and the lack of reimbursement mechanisms that adequately compensate PCPs for providing comprehensive and complex care may further discourage their involvement in postnatal care. Addressing these systemic barriers will require interprofessional education and policy‐level healthcare reforms to support a collaborative, team‐based care model that more effectively integrates PCPs into the postnatal care continuum.

In addition to improving clinical education and addressing systemic issues, it is imperative to establish a system to assess the effectiveness of interventions in postnatal care. This includes establishing quality‐of‐care indicators specifically for postnatal care and implementing data monitoring systems [42] to evaluate the impact of these interventions. While patient‐reported outcome measures are valuable indicators of care quality from the patient's perspective, currently available assessment tools do not encompass all domains of postnatal care [43]. Additionally, given the complex nature of postnatal care, there is a need to develop evaluation tools that capture its comprehensiveness, complexity, and degree of care integration. These advancements in research and evaluation can provide valuable insights into PCPs' practices in postnatal care, offer feedback for improvement, inform the development of educational frameworks, and promote practicing and policy‐making with appropriate reimbursement systems to enhance postnatal care.

Conclusion

Japanese PCPs' practices varied across postnatal care domains. They perceived postnatal care to be of high importance, but practiced it less frequently, particularly in the domain of intimate partner violence. Enhancing medical education is expected to close the practice variations and practice‐attitude discrepancies in postnatal care.
