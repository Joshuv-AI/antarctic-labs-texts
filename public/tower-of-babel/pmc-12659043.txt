The effect of mental health-based self-care model counseling on anxiety and depression in women with gestational diabetes: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Mahboubeh Daraie, Hajar Adib-Rad, Fatemeh Nasiri‑Amiri, Mahbobeh Faramarzi, Hemmat Gholinia, Satinik Darzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnxiety and depression caused by gestational diabetes can lead to adverse outcomes for both the mother and the baby. Mental health self-care counseling focusing on awareness, recognition, and coping strategies can play a protective role. This study aimed to determine the effect of a mental health-based self-care counseling model on anxiety and depression in women with Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM).MethodsThis randomized clinical trial was conducted from June 2023 to May 2024 on 84 pregnant women with gestational diabetes at Amir al-Momenin Hospital in Semnan. After block randomization, the women were divided into a self-care mental health counseling group (42 participants) and a control group (42 participants). The intervention group received six individual weekly sessions (30–45 min each) of computer-linked self-care mental health counseling. Data were collected before, immediately after, one month after the intervention, and six weeks after delivery using the questionnaires including demographic and reproductive, Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), health behavior during Pregnancy, mental health self-care, and gestational diabetes self-efficacy.ResultsThe study results showed that the mean (standard deviation) anxiety scores in the intervention and control groups were 6.85 (2.11) and 7.31 (2.26) before the intervention, 4.02 (1.78) and 7.49 (1.73) at the end of the intervention, 3.88 (1.56) and 7.13 (1.36) one month after the intervention, and 2.07 (1.14) and 4.14 (1.16) six weeks after delivery (p < 0.001, η² = 0.56), respectively. For depression scores, the two groups had means of 6.03 (2.15) and 6.10 (1.71) before the intervention, 4.07 (1.20) and 5.82 (1.88) at the end of the intervention, 3.62 (1.12) and 5.82 (1.35) one month after the intervention, and 5.15 (1.25) and 8.62 (1.62) six weeks after delivery (p < 0.001, η² = 0.60). Compared to the control group, intervention group showed a significant reduction in mean anxiety and depression scores at all three follow-ups after the intervention. Additionally, the mean scores for health-promoting behaviors, mental health self-care, and self-efficacy increased significantly more in the intervention group. Based on linear regression analysis, mental health self-care was the strongest predictor of reduced anxiety and depression (β = -0.393, p < 0.001).ConclusionMental health-based self-care counseling is effective in reducing anxiety and depression, as well as promoting health behaviors, self-efficacy, and mental health self-care in women with gestational diabetes. Therefore, it is recommended as a valuable strategy for managing the complications of gestational diabetes.Trial registrationIRCT20230130057274N1. Date of registration: 2024-08-06.

Introduction

Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) is the most common metabolic disorder during pregnancy, leading to numerous adverse physical and psychological effects on the pregnant woman [1, 2]. GDM is any degree of glucose intolerance that is first recognized or diagnosed during pregnancy, regardless of the severity of hyperglycemia. This definition provides a uniform strategy for the diagnosis and classification of gestational diabetes [3]. Pregnancy is associated with a significant increase in estrogen, progesterone, prolactin, cortisol, and placental lactogen levels, which exacerbates maternal insulin resistance to facilitate placental nutrient transfer to the fetus. Pancreatic cells in pregnant women often can produce more insulin to counteract pregnancy-related hormones. However, if the pancreas fails to secrete sufficient insulin, blood glucose levels rise, ultimately leading to gestational diabetes [4].

The prevalence of gestational diabetes in Iran has been reported as 9.7% [5]. The global standardized prevalence of gestational diabetes is approximately 14% [1, 6]. According to the latest estimates by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), gestational diabetes affects about 1 to 14% of pregnancies worldwide [7]. In women with a history of gestational diabetes, the risk of recurrence in a subsequent pregnancy increases by 30 to 69%, and they are nearly 10 times more likely to develop type 2 diabetes compared to women with normal pregnancies [8]. In addition to adverse physical effects, gestational diabetes also contributes to increased mood disorders [9]. It raises the incidence of anxiety and depression in pregnant women. Conversely, anxiety or depression during pregnancy can also increase the risk of developing diabetes [1]. Women with gestational diabetes have a lower quality of life compared to healthy women due to higher stress levels. Concerns about themselves and their baby, along with the preventive measures they must take to manage diabetes such as following a strict diet, weight control, and physical activity contribute to stress among mothers with gestational diabetes [10]. The poorer the blood sugar control in women with gestational diabetes, the more severe their depression becomes [11]. Mental health screening is recommended among women undergoing prenatal care [12]. Assessing at-risk women during pregnancy greatly aids in predicting the likelihood of postpartum depression [13]. To improve quality of life and prevent acute and long-term complications of diabetes, blood glucose monitoring and control should be integrated with underlying factors such as mental health and self-care behaviors [14].

Mental health self-care refers to purposeful actions taken by individuals to improve well-being and reduce stress [15]. Self-care behaviors in mental health enhance a person’s ability to manage stress, anxiety, or mental health-related issues [16]. Self-efficacy is defined as an individual’s belief in their ability to perform a task and can empower them to adopt health-promoting behaviors and abandon harmful ones [17]. The greater a person’s perception of self-efficacy, the more successful their performance becomes, influencing how they feel, think, stay motivated, behave, and ultimately adhere to treatment regimens [18]. Few studies have been conducted on mental health-based self-care counseling for women with GDM. Given the increased adverse pregnancy outcomes associated with rising anxiety and depression following GDM, this study aimed to determine the effect of mental health-based self-care counseling on anxiety and depression in women with gestational diabetes mellitus.

Methods

Design

The present study is a randomized clinical trial. The research was carried out after obtaining approval from the Ethics Committee of Babol University of Medical Sciences (Ethics Code: IR.MUBABOL.HRI.REC.1401.221) and was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20230130057274N1).

Participant recruitment

This study was conducted from June 2023 to May 2024 on 84pregnant women with gestational diabetes, aged over 20 years, and at a gestational age of 24–30 weeks, who visited the Gynecology and Prenatal Clinic of Amir al-Momenin (AS) Hospital in Semnan. The participants were divided into two groups—an intervention group and a control group—both of whom expressed their willingness to participate in the study. In this study, initially, 210 pregnant women who were found to have glucose metabolism disorders during gestational diabetes screening under the National Safe Motherhood Program (Integrated Maternal Health Care) [19] were invited to participate. After evaluating the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 90 women with gestational diabetes did not meet the entry criteria. 120 women completed the anxiety and depression questionnaire at the beginning of the study, 36 of whom were excluded due to scoring less than 8 or more than 14 on anxiety or depression scales. Pregnant women (84 people) with gestational diabetes were enrolled in the study and were randomly assigned into two groups of 42 each (42 in the intervention group and 42 in the control group) (Fig. 1). Randomization was performed using random number generation and random list creation via the Random Allocation Software. Half of the numbers were assigned to the intervention group, and the other half to the control group. The allocation of samples to the groups was done through block randomization with equal-sized intervention and control groups, using blocks of four. As a result, we had 21 distinct blocks (21 quadruple blocks), totaling 84 participants divided equally into two groups (42 in the intervention group and 42 in the control group). Participants were randomly allocated to each study group using block randomization with a block size of four and a 1:1 allocation ratio. The researcher could not predict the next participant’s assignment to either group. The statistician responsible for data analysis generated the randomization list and was unaware of the coding of the two groups. The inclusion criteria for the study consisted of pregnant women diagnosed with gestational diabetes by a physician during diabetes screening, Iranian nationals residing in Semnan, gestational age of 24 to 30 weeks, singleton pregnancy, minimum elementary-level education, completion of a written informed consent form to participate in the study, possession of a smartphone or access to a personal computer and the internet scoring between 8 and 14 on the HADS. Exclusion criteria included any other underlying physical illness, known psychiatric disorders, speech and hearing impairments that hinder communication, drug addiction, studying in medical sciences fields, experiencing a traumatic event in the past six months, and pregnancy-related complications such as preterm birth and preeclampsia. The primary outcome was the change in anxiety and depression scores, while the secondary outcomes included changes in health-promoting behavior scores, mental health self-care scores, and self-efficacy scores.

Measures

The demographic-fertility characteristics checklist

This contains questions on age, occupation, education, spouse’s age, spouse’s occupation, spouse’s education, number of pregnancies, gestational age at delivery, type of delivery, pre-pregnancy Body Mass Index (BMI), residence, income satisfaction, and family support.

The hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS)

This scale is a 14-item self-report questionnaire initially developed by Zigmond and Snaith to screen for the presence and severity of depression and anxiety symptoms over the past week in patients. Each component of this scale is scored from zero to three. Therefore, the scores of the depression and anxiety subscales in the HADS questionnaire range from zero to 21, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety and depression. For both subscales, scores between zero and seven are considered normal, eight to ten mild, 11 to 14 moderate, and 15 to 21 severe [21]. Its reliability, measured using Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.83 for the depression subscale and 0.85 for the anxiety subscale [22]. Montazeri et al. translated the Iranian version into Persian, and its reliability was confirmed as 0.78 for the anxiety subscale and 0.86 for the depression subscale [23].

The Psychological Self-Care Scale (PSCS) is a 46- item and seven dimensions. This questionnaire consists of 46 items and seven attributes, including self-awareness, communication effectiveness, time effectiveness, coping with problems, development and maintenance of a social support system, religious activities, and physical self-care. The questionnaire is assessed using a four-point Likert scale (1 = rarely, 2 = sometimes, 3 = often, 4 = always). The total score ranges from 46 to 184. The reliability of this tool was calculated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and demonstrated high reliability. The reliability coefficients for dimensions 2 to 5 were 0.82, 0.86, 0.90, and 0.86, respectively (16). The validity of the Persian version was confirmed in 2020 by Mirtabar et al., reporting a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.71 [24].

The Prenatal Health Behavior Scale (PHBS) is a 24-item self-report questionnaire designed to assess health behaviors during pregnancy. It was first developed by Lobel et al. in 1995. This scale covers a range of health and unhealthy behaviors, including nutrition, exercise, smoking, and substance abuse. Each question on this questionnaire is rated on a scale of 0 (never) to 4 (very often). The range of total scores is 0–96 [25]. The validity and reliability of the Persian version were evaluated by Abdollahi et al. in 2020. The questionnaire’s validity was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87 for the entire scale, and the test-retest reliability correlation coefficient for all items was 0.96 [26].

The Self-Efficacy Scale Gestational Diabetes (SESGD) was developed by the Stanford University Research Center. This questionnaire comprises 8 items, each scored on a 10-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all sure) to 10 (completely sure). The total score ranges from 8 to 80, where higher scores indicate greater self-efficacy. Scores are divided into three categories, including poor (8–32), average (33–56), and good (57–80). The reliability of the questionnaire, assessed by Lorigg using the test-retest method, was reported as 0.80 [27]. The reliability of the Persian version, calculated by Bastani et al. based on Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, was 0.87 [28]. Additionally, the internal consistency of the questionnaire was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.72 [29].

Interventions

At the beginning of the study, pregnant women with gestational diabetes were invited to participate. During the initial interview, those who met the inclusion criteria were identified. A preliminary session, lasting 30–45 min, was designed and conducted to introduce the study to the participants, establishing proper communication, build trust, explain the study objectives, ensure voluntary participation, guarantee confidentiality of information, provide instructions on the study procedure and questionnaire completion, emphasize the freedom to withdraw from the study at any time. Following this, participants completed the 14-item HADS. Individuals who scored between 8 and 14 in either subscale (anxiety or depression) were included in the study, indicating mild to moderate anxiety/depression symptoms. It should be noted that pregnant women with gestational diabetes who scored above 14 in either subscale (anxiety or depression)—suggesting a likelihood of severe anxiety/depression disorder—were referred to the center’s psychologist for a clinical interview. If necessary, they were further referred to a psychiatrist for anxiety/depression treatment.

The women who entered the study were randomly assigned into two blocks: the intervention group and the control group. The remaining questionnaires were completed in person by participants in both groups at four time points: once before the intervention, immediately after the intervention, one month after the intervention, and six weeks after delivery. For women with lower literacy levels who had difficulty understanding certain questionnaire items, the researcher provided necessary explanations. Data on pregnancy outcomes, including shoulder dystocia and the infant’s Apgar scores at the first and fifth minutes, were extracted from the participants’ medical records. In addition to routine prenatal care, the intervention group received six sessions of mental health self-care counseling, while the control group received no intervention and was placed on a waiting list. After the study concluded, and in adherence to research ethics, the control group was given the opportunity to receive the six sessions of mental health self-care counseling free of charge if they wished.

The mental health self-care technique was conducted by an experienced licensed psychotherapist. Before beginning the work, the midwifery counseling student received three months of necessary training in mental health self-care counseling under the supervision of a psychology advisor. After gaining the required expertise in the techniques of this model, the counseling session content was developed based on the mental health self-care questionnaire and regarding women’s health, psychology, and scientific journals, under the supervision and approval of psychology and midwifery professors [1, 3, 16, 30–32]. The protocol for this study has been published, and full information is available [33]. The counseling content, guided by the supervisors and advisors, was allocated to six consecutive sessions lasting 30–45 min each. The prepared content was then converted into computer links and sent individually to each member of the intervention group via text message. In this study, we implemented a self-care model counseling approach grounded in mental health principles to support patients with GDM. During counseling sessions, the researcher provided comprehensive explanations of the causes of gestational diabetes, risk factors, diagnostic methods, complications, and management of gestational diabetes, health-promoting behaviors, unhealthy behaviors, posture correction, exercises during pregnancy, self-care in gestational diabetes, self-monitoring of blood glucose, dietary guidelines, self-efficacy, and coping strategies. To manage mental health and blood sugar control, patients were assigned tasks, including blood glucose monitoring, nutritional instructions, exercise routines, and CBT (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy) techniques. The researcher consistently monitored progress by reviewing completed tasks and provided timely feedback to ensure adherence and address any challenges. The counseling sessions were presented in a multimedia format, combining text, audio, and visuals. To facilitate interaction between the intervention group members and the counselor, questions were posed to each participant during every session. The responses to these questions were made visible and reviewable by the counselor at the end of the session. After sending the links, the student maintained contact with the participants for 20–25 min per week via phone or social media platforms (WhatsApp, Telegram), providing counseling based on the session content and the participant’s responses to the questions. Statistical analyses

After data collection, the data were analyzed using SPSS-26 software with descriptive statistics indicators (mean, standard deviation, frequency distribution table, and relative frequency) and inferential statistics, including Chi-square test (to examine the linear relationship between qualitative variables), independent t-test (to compare means between two independent groups), Repeated Measures ANOVA (RMANOVA) (to calculate intervention effects and time effects), partial eta squared (η2) to examine the effect sizes, and linear regression. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To address the ‘Lost to Follow-up’ approach in this study, an Intention-To-Treat (ITT) analysis was performed.

Results

In this study, 84 individuals (42 in the intervention group and 42 in the control group) were evaluated. The mean age of participants in the intervention group was 31.21 ± 6.28 years, and in the control group, it was 31.14 ± 5.55 years. The mean pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) of the participants was 28.23 ± 5.10 kg/m² in the intervention group and 27.64 ± 5.41 kg/m² in the control group, placing them in the overweight range. Most participants in both groups were homemakers, had university education, lived in urban areas, and were relatively satisfied with their income. There were no significant differences between the intervention and control groups in any demographic or fertility characteristics, as well as in pregnancy outcomes (Table 2). According to the results presented in Table 3, before the intervention, there was no significant difference in the mean scores of anxiety, depression, health behaviors, mental health self-care, and self-efficacy between the intervention and control groups. However, immediately after the intervention, one month after the intervention, and six weeks after delivery, the mean scores of anxiety and depression in the intervention group showed a significantly greater decrease compared to the control group. Additionally, the scores of health-promoting behaviors, mental health self-care, and self-efficacy in the intervention group increased significantly more than in the control group. In the intervention group, the mean and standard deviation of the anxiety score before the intervention were 6.85 (2.11), at the end of the intervention 4.02 (1.78), one month after the intervention 3.88 (1.56), and six weeks after delivery 2.07 (1.14), indicating a significant decrease at all three follow-up stages compared to before the intervention (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The study results showed that in the intervention group, the mean scores of anxiety and depression decreased significantly immediately after, one month after the intervention, and six weeks after delivery compared to before the intervention. In line with this study, research examining various dimensions of mental health self-care will be discussed. A study by Khoozan et al. showed that psychological self-care plays a significant role in enhancing professional quality of life and reducing anxiety and depression [34]. In the quasi-experimental study by Kheirkhah et al., which aimed to investigate the effect of self-care on anxiety and self-efficacy in women with GDM, a 4-week group-based self-care education intervention led to a reduction in anxiety among women with GDM, both immediately after the intervention and at the 4-week follow-up [35]. The results of the cross-sectional study by Mirtabar et al., which examined the role of psychological self-care, revealed that psychological self-care scores in pregnant women had a protective effect against the occurrence of depressive symptoms and psychological distress [24]. The results of another study showed that psychological self-care training improves mental health (depression, anxiety, and stress) [36]. The findings of the current study were consistent with previous research, demonstrating that counseling significantly reduced the mean scores of anxiety and depression in women with gestational diabetes. A review of existing literature reveals a gap in studies examining the effects of mental health-based self-care counseling models on anxiety and depression in women with gestational diabetes. This underscores the novelty and significance of the present study. Therefore, mental health-based self-care counseling can be an effective solution for reducing anxiety and depression caused by gestational diabetes. The intervention was more effective in reducing depression among women immediately after the intervention and one month later compared to six weeks postpartum. Although the mean depression scores increased in both groups at six weeks postpartum compared to the two earlier follow-ups, the intervention group showed a significant reduction in depression scores compared to both the baseline and the control group. Over time, the effectiveness of the intervention diminished somewhat, and various factors contributing to postpartum depression—such as hormonal, environmental, emotional, Social support, limited counseling time, and genetic influences—may have played a role. Therefore, researchers should develop strategies to ensure the sustained effectiveness of these methods, ensuring they remain effective in reducing the severity of depressive symptoms across different periods.

According to the findings of our study, the mean scores of health-promoting behaviors increased significantly immediately after the intervention, one month after the intervention, and six weeks after delivery compared to before the intervention. Meanwhile, the mean scores of health-damaging behaviors decreased significantly at all three follow-up stages after the intervention compared to before the intervention. The study by Esfandiari et al., which aimed to examine the effect of group supportive counseling on pregnancy-specific stress, general stress, and healthy behaviors in pregnant women, found that in the intervention group, anxiety and unhealthy behavior scores decreased significantly, while healthy behaviors improved significantly [37]. In the study by Asli Ural, health-promoting lifestyle education for pregnant women with gestational diabetes led to a significant increase in health responsibility, physical activity, and the subscales of nutrition and stress management at six weeks postpartum compared to the control group [38]. The consistency of these results with the findings of the present study demonstrates the applicability and effectiveness of mental health self-care counseling interventions in promoting healthy behaviors among pregnant women with gestational diabetes. Therefore, midwives can improve the lifestyle of women with gestational diabetes and reduce related complications by providing appropriate care and health behavior counseling in healthcare centers [39].

The results of this study showed that the mean score of mental health self-care immediately after the intervention, one month later, and six weeks after delivery was significantly higher in the intervention group compared to the control group. This finding demonstrates the effectiveness of mental health self-care counseling in improving mental health self-care behaviors among pregnant women with gestational diabetes. According to the findings of Karbalai Harafteh et al.‘s study, self-care education led to a significant increase in health literacy and self-care behaviors among women with gestational diabetes [40]. The results of these studies were consistent with the findings of the present study. Mental health self-care counseling, which includes self-awareness, coping with problems, maintaining a social support system, religious activities, and physical self-care, can be effective in improving the psychological well-being of women with gestational diabetes. However, the results of Jeon et al.‘s study indicated that a web-based postpartum care program for women with gestational diabetes did not have a significant effect on their self-management [41]. The difference between the findings of these studies and those of the present study may be attributed to variations in educational content and methods, the number of training sessions, the duration of each session, and the cultural context of the societies in the different studies.

According to the findings of the present study, the mean self-efficacy score in the intervention group showed a significantly greater increase compared to the control group immediately after, one month after the intervention, and six weeks after delivery. In line with our study, the results of the research by Kheirkhah et al. indicated that a group-based self-care education intervention led to an increase in self-efficacy among women with gestational diabetes, both immediately and four weeks after the intervention [35]. Similarly, in the study by Bakhteh et al., the cognitive-behavioral counseling intervention group showed increased self-efficacy in women with gestational diabetes [42]. Higher levels of self-efficacy and self-care lead to more successful lifestyle modifications and adherence to treatment regimens, empowering individuals and resulting in better management of gestational diabetes. In the intervention group of the present study, the highest mean scores for health-promoting behaviors, mental health self-care, and self-efficacy, along with the lowest mean scores for health-damaging behaviors, were observed one month after the intervention. This indicates the effectiveness of the intervention in promoting healthy behaviors following increased self-efficacy and self-care in pregnant women with gestational diabetes. Although six weeks postpartum, the mean scores for health-promoting behaviors, mental health self-care, and self-efficacy decreased compared to the post-intervention and one-month follow-up, while health-damaging behavior scores increased, they still showed a significant improvement compared to pre-intervention levels. Health-promoting behaviors, mental health self-care, and self-efficacy remained significantly higher, while health-damaging behaviors were significantly lower.

In the present study, no significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding pregnancy outcomes, such as gestational age at delivery, mode of delivery, fifth-minute Apgar score, shoulder dystocia, neonatal weight, or neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission. The regression results of our study showed that age and number of pregnancies were not predictors of anxiety, depression, and self-efficacy in GDM. However, mental health self-care, health-promoting behaviors, and health-damaging behaviors were effective factors. The study by Kolivand et al. on 152 women with gestational diabetes showed that using a self-care package had a positive effect on maternal self-efficacy, two-hour postprandial plasma glucose, Apgar score, and neonatal hospitalization. However, no significant difference was observed between the two groups regarding gestational age at delivery, mode of delivery, or neonatal weight [43]. Although the results of the present study align with those of Kolivand’s study concerning the mode of delivery, neonatal weight, and gestational age at birth, they are inconsistent regarding Apgar score and neonatal hospitalization. Therefore, further research in this area is needed.

GDM is one of the most common health problems during pregnancy. Counseling is an active process aimed at increasing awareness, adaptation, and behavior change to achieve health goals. It enhances self-confidence, improves mental state, reduces anxiety, and leads to changes in perception, beliefs, attitudes, behaviors, and lifestyle. Mental health self-care counseling is effective in promoting healthy behaviors, improving psychological well-being, and enhancing self-efficacy in women with GDM. Self-Care Mental Health Counseling for women with gestational diabetes, through teaching coping strategies, has been effective in enhancing their ability to handle difficult situations. It helps them maintain a positive and constructive outlook when facing challenges. As a result, they can safely navigate their pregnancy and achieve better maternal and fetal outcomes.

Strengths and limitations of the study

One of the strengths of the present study was its virtual and internet-based approach, which—considering the current digital era and the need for time and cost efficiency—was an optimal choice. Another strength was the presence of the principal researcher at the medical training center, facilitating close communication with participants, addressing their ambiguities, and ensuring their full cooperation and responsiveness in actively engaging with the study. The use of standardized questionnaires was another strong point. One limitation of the study was the self-reported nature of the questionnaires, which may have led some participants to withhold truthful answers and provide inaccurate responses. Although the researcher assured participants that their information would remain completely confidential and made efforts to mitigate this issue, the possibility of bias remains. Another limitation was the exclusion of individuals with anxiety and depression scores above 14 on the HADS questionnaire during the initial sampling phase, meaning the results may not be generalizable to the entire population. Due to the study’s design, blinding was not possible; however, the statistician responsible for data analysis was unaware of the coding of the intervention and control groups. For future studies, it is recommended that, alongside self-report questionnaires, clinical interviews be used to more accurately diagnose anxiety and depression. Additionally, women with anxiety and depression scores above 14—who were excluded from this study and require further counseling and care—should be examined in future research. Further research and interventions in diverse populations using other non-pharmacological therapeutic methods are recommended to reduce anxiety and depression in women with gestational diabetes. Comparing these results with those of the self-care counseling model based on mental health could provide further confirmation of this method’s efficacy.

Conclusion

Overall, the results of the present study indicate that self-care counseling based on the mental health model was able to improve adverse psychological outcomes in pregnant women with gestational diabetes, while also enhancing health behaviors, mental health self-care, and self-efficacy. This intervention had no adverse effects on the mother or the newborn and did not interfere with routine healthcare programs. Therefore, it can be considered alongside other mental health promotion programs for pregnant women.
