Choreographies with Dihedral Symmetry in the Planar n n -Body Problem
Juan Manuel Sánchez-Cerritos

Abstract
We prove the existence of planar D n D_{n} –equivariant choreographies in the n n –body problem with homogeneous potential of degree − α -\alpha , 0 < α < 2 0<\alpha<2 . Each body follows the same closed path, rotated and time–shifted, forming a choreography whenever the winding number W W is coprime with n n . Using Mawhin’s coincidence degree, we establish collision–free periodic solutions under a simple nonresonance condition. The proof relies on the spectral structure of the linearized operator, symmetry–induced separation of the bodies, and uniform energy bounds ensuring compactness of the nonlinear term. This provides a topological route to choreographies beyond variational and numerical frameworks.

choreographies, coincidence degree, Mawhin,
n
–body problem.

Introduction

The planar
n
–body problem in Celestial Mechanics with a homogeneous potential of degree
-\alpha
,
0<\alpha<2
, is a classical field where dynamical analysis, bifurcation theory, and variational methods meet. Among its remarkable configurations are the
choreographies
, introduced by Chenciner and Montgomery
[
4
]
, in which all bodies move along the same closed curve in the plane, uniformly shifted in time. The discovery of the celebrated “figure–eight” choreography for three equal masses opened an active research line combining symmetry principles, action–minimization techniques, and topological arguments.

Subsequent studies have revealed numerous families of choreographies: Simó
[
19
]
explored a wide range of them through numerical computations; Ferrario and Terracini
[
6
]
applied variational principles with symmetry constraints; Kapela and Zgliczyński
[
9
]
provided computer–assisted rigorous proofs of existence; Chen and Ouyang
[
3
]
investigated bifurcations from central configurations, among others. Many of these solutions exhibit polygonal or dihedral symmetries, suggesting that choreographies with
D_{n}
symmetry may have implicitly emerged in earlier studies.

In this paper we adopt a complementary perspective: we establish the existence of
D_{n}
–equivariant choreographies by means of the
Mawhin coincidence degree theorem

[
14
]
. This fundamental result in nonlinear analysis applies to operator equations of the form

Lu=N(u),

where
L
is a linear Fredholm operator of index zero and
N
is an
L
–compact nonlinear perturbation. The theorem guarantees the existence of solutions under suitable a priori bounds, providing a topological alternative to variational and perturbative methods. Although this framework has been applied to various nonlinear differential equations, its use in Celestial Mechanics and in the context of choreographies remains relatively unexplored. Our goal is to fill this methodological gap and to show that Mawhin’s degree provides a unified scheme to handle symmetry, compactness, and collision exclusion simultaneously.

The analysis relies on two geometric ingredients:

1.

Polar description of
D_{n}
symmetry.

Each trajectory can be represented in polar form
u(t)=r(t)(\cos t,\sin t)
,
where the radial function
r(t)
encodes the dihedral symmetry pattern.
In particular,
D_{n}
–equivariance corresponds to a periodic and reflection–symmetric
behavior of
r(t)
, a property that later ensures a uniform separation between the bodies.

2.

Winding number around the origin.

Beyond the basic case where the curve winds once around the origin (
W=1
),
we also consider higher winding numbers
W>1
, which generate more intricate families of choreographies.

Mawhin’s coincidence degree has proved to be a robust tool for establishing periodic solutions of nonlinear differential equations
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
2
,
16
,
18
,
11
]
, including problems with gyroscopic or resonance effects analogous to those appearing in the
n
–body equations.

The main contribution of this paper is to prove, under a natural nonresonance condition, the existence of
D_{n}
–equivariant choreographies for any winding number
W\geq 1
such that
W
and
n
are coprime. This arithmetic restriction ensures that the configuration corresponds to a single shared orbit rather than multiple identical sub–orbits. Formally, we establish the following result.

Theorem

(Existence of
D_{n}
–equivariant choreographies)
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
W\geq 1
be coprime integers (
\gcd(W,n)=1
), and consider the planar
n
–body problem with homogeneous potential
r^{-\alpha}
,
0<\alpha<2
.
Assume the nonresonance condition
\Omega\neq\pm k\,\tfrac{2\pi}{T}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then there exists at least one
T
–periodic function
u\in H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that

Lu=N(u),

where
L:H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}\to L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}
is the linear Fredholm operator associated with the rotating frame.
The corresponding configuration

q_{k}(t)=R_{2\pi k/n}\,u\!\left(t+\tfrac{kT}{n}\right),\qquad k=0,\dots,n-1,

defines a collision–free
D_{n}
–equivariant choreography with winding number
W
.

Section
2
introduces the
D_{n}
symmetry, the notion of winding number, and the reduction of the
n
–body system to a single functional equation for the generating curve.
Section
3
formulates the problem in a rigorous functional setting, defining the periodic Sobolev (Hilbert) spaces and the linear and nonlinear operators that lead to the abstract equation
Lu=N(u)
.
Section
4
presents Mawhin’s coincidence framework, providing the topological degree setting in which the existence argument is carried out.
Section
5

Section
7

(W,n)
.

2
Dihedral symmetry
D_{n}
and problem formulation

2.1
D_{n}
–equivariant choreographies and the winding number
W

We consider the planar
n
–body problem with equal masses
m>0
, whose positions

q_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfy Newton’s equations

m\ddot{q}_{i}(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\frac{m^{2}\,(q_{j}(t)-q_{i}(t))}{\|q_{j}(t)-q_{i}(t)\|^{\alpha+2}},\qquad i=0,\ldots,n-1,

(1)

where
0<\alpha<2
is the exponent of the homogeneous potential
V(r)=r^{-\alpha}
.
We seek periodic solutions of (
1
) exhibiting the
choreographic symmetry
, namely that all bodies follow the same closed trajectory
u(t)
with a fixed time shift.

Let
R_{2\pi/n}
denote the rotation in the plane by angle
2\pi/n
,
and fix the reflection

S=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\[2.0pt]
0&-1\end{pmatrix},

so that
SJS=-J
, where
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
.
Let
T>0
denote the period.

We define a
choreographic configuration
by

q_{i}(t)=R_{2\pi i/n}\,u\!\left(t+\frac{iT}{n}\right),\qquad i=0,\ldots,n-1,

(2)

where
u:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a sufficiently regular
T
–periodic curve.
This condition imposes a discrete symmetry isomorphic to the dihedral group
D_{n}
,
generated by the rotation
R_{2\pi/n}
and the space–time involution

\mathcal{S}:u(t)\mapsto S\,u(-t)
.
The set of such curves forms the closed subspace

X_{D_{n}}=\Bigl\{u\in H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2}):u\!\bigl(t+\tfrac{T}{n}\bigr)=R_{2\pi/n}u(t),\;u(-t)=S\,u(t)\Bigr\}.

The embedding
H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}\hookrightarrow C^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}
guarantees that
the pointwise evaluations in (
2
) are well defined.
In this space, a single function
u
completely determines the motion of the
n
bodies.

The
winding number

W\in\mathbb{N}
records how many full turns the curve
u(t)

performs around the origin during one period
T
.
Writing
u(t)=r(t)e^{i\theta(t)}
, we set

W=\frac{\theta(T)-\theta(0)}{2\pi}\in\mathbb{Z}.

The parameter
W
controls the topology of the generating curve and will be fixed throughout the analysis.
When
W\geq 1
, the trajectories may have self–intersections and a
“wrapped polygon” structure, analogous to limacón–type choreographies.

The arithmetic relation between
W
and
n
determines the nature of the choreography.
Indeed, the
D_{n}
symmetry imposes

u\!\left(t+\tfrac{T}{n}\right)=R_{2\pi/n}\,u(t),

so that after
n
iterations one winds
W
times around the origin.
If
W
and
n
are coprime, the action generated by

(t,u)\mapsto\bigl(t+\tfrac{T}{n},\,R_{2\pi/n}u\bigr)

is transitive on the
n
bodies, hence they all traverse the same planar curve.
Conversely, when
W
and
n
have a common divisor
d>1
, the motion splits into
d
disjoint subsets of bodies,
each subset following a distinct curve congruent under rotations.

Proposition 2.1

(Coprimality condition for choreographies)
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
W\geq 1
be integers, and consider the
D_{n}
–equivariant configuration given by (
2
). Here
\gcd(W,n)
denotes the greatest common divisor of
W

and
n
. Then:

(i)

If
\gcd(W,n)=1
, the configuration is a
choreography
: all bodies trace the same curve
u(t)
, time–shifted by
T/n
.

(ii)

If
\gcd(W,n)=d>1
, the configuration decomposes into
d

disjoint sub–choreographies
, each formed by
n/d
bodies sharing a common trajectory.

Proof.

Iterating the symmetry
u(t+\tfrac{T}{n})=R_{2\pi/n}u(t)
yields

u\!\left(t+\tfrac{jT}{n}\right)=R_{2\pi j/n}u(t),\qquad j\in\mathbb{Z}.

The time shift
t\mapsto t+\tfrac{T}{n}
combined with
R_{2\pi/n}
generates an action of the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
on the set of bodies
\{q_{0},\dots,q_{n-1}\}
.
The number of orbits of this action equals
\gcd(W,n)
.
If
\gcd(W,n)=1
, the generator acts transitively, hence all bodies belong to a single orbit, i.e., a single generating curve.
If
\gcd(W,n)=d>1
, the action splits into
d
disjoint orbits of size
n/d
, each corresponding to a distinct curve traced by
n/d
bodies.
∎

The condition
\gcd(W,n)=1
ensures that there are no exact phase repetitions between different bodies,
avoiding collisions due to temporal overlaps.
When
\gcd(W,n)>1
, spatial coincidences may occur between bodies in different sub–choreographies,
although each subconfiguration remains collision–free internally.

In what follows we restrict attention to the
coprime
case
\gcd(W,n)=1
,
so that the configuration (
2
)
indeed represents a choreography in which all bodies share a single generating curve
u(t)
,
uniformly shifted in time.
Under this hypothesis, all subsequent expressions and results are to be understood
in this transitively symmetric regime.

Remark 2.2

(Spectral structure of the
D_{n}
–symmetry)
.

The condition

u\!\left(t+\tfrac{T}{n}\right)=R_{2\pi/n}u(t)

imposes a specific constraint on the Fourier spectrum of
u
.
Writing
u(t)
in complex form as

u(t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{k}\,e^{ik\omega t},\qquad\omega=\tfrac{2\pi}{T},

we obtain

e^{ik\omega T/n}\,a_{k}=e^{i2\pi/n}\,a_{k},

so that only the modes satisfying
k\equiv 1\pmod{n}
may have nonzero coefficients.
Consequently, admissible functions in
X_{D_{n}}
contain only
Fourier modes of the form
k=1+\ell n
with
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The dominant mode
k=1+\ell n
determines the winding number

W=1+\ell n
, which is automatically coprime with
n
.
This characterization links the dihedral symmetry of the configuration space
to its harmonic structure and explains the multi–winding
patterns observed in the generating curves illustrated later.

Representative examples of generating curves
u(t)
satisfying
the dihedral symmetry constraint

u(t+\tfrac{T}{n})=R_{2\pi/n}u(t)

and having winding number
W>1
are shown in
Figure
1
.
Each curve corresponds to a dominant Fourier mode

k=1+\ell n
, consistent with the spectral characterization
in Remark
2.2
.
They illustrate the multi–winding,
D_{n}
–equivariant
patterns that define the configuration space
X_{D_{n}}
.

u(t)

.

k=1+\ell n
, ensuring that
\gcd(W,n)=1
.

2.2
Equations of motion in the rotating frame

To study periodic solutions with
D_{n}
symmetry, it is convenient to switch to a
rotating frame
with constant angular velocity
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}
.
Writing
q_{i}(t)=R_{\Omega t}\,x_{i}(t)
, Newton’s equations (
1
) become

\ddot{x}_{i}+2\Omega J\dot{x}_{i}-\Omega^{2}x_{i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=0\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\frac{m\,(x_{j}-x_{i})}{\|x_{j}-x_{i}\|^{\alpha+2}},\qquad i=0,\ldots,n-1.

(3)

Here, the gyroscopic term
2\Omega J\dot{x}_{i}
and the centrifugal term
-\Omega^{2}x_{i}

represent the fictitious forces in the rotating frame.

Under the choreographic condition (
2
), the system (
3
)
reduces to a single equation for the generating curve
u(t)
:

\ddot{u}+2\Omega J\dot{u}-\Omega^{2}u=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{m\,(R_{2\pi k/n}u(t+\tfrac{kT}{n})-u(t))}{\|R_{2\pi k/n}u(t+\tfrac{kT}{n})-u(t)\|^{\alpha+2}}.

(4)

To express the problem in the Fredholm form required by Mawhin’s theorem,
we multiply both sides of (
4
), by
-1
, thus obtaining

-\,\ddot{u}-2\Omega J\dot{u}+\Omega^{2}u=-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{m\,(R_{2\pi k/n}u(t+\tfrac{kT}{n})-u(t))}{\|R_{2\pi k/n}u(t+\tfrac{kT}{n})-u(t)\|^{\alpha+2}}.

(5)

We define the linear operator

Lu=-\ddot{u}-2\Omega J\dot{u}+\Omega^{2}u,\qquad L:H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})\longrightarrow L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2}),

(6)

and the nonlinear operator
N:X_{D_{n}}\to L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}
given by the right–hand side of (
5
), which couples the
n-1
rotated and time–shifted copies of
u
.

Thus, the
D_{n}
–choreography problem in the rotating frame takes the compact operator form

Lu=N(u),\qquad u\in X_{D_{n}}\subset H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2}),

(7)

which provides the functional framework for the application of Mawhin’s coincidence degree.

2.3
Uniform separation and collision exclusion

A key point for applying Mawhin’s theorem is to ensure that the choreographic trajectories under consideration remain collision–free.
Geometrically, the
D_{n}
symmetry imposes a minimal separation between bodies, independent of the winding number
W\geq 1
.
This is formalized as follows.

Proposition 2.3

(Uniform separation under
D_{n}
symmetry for all
W\geq 1
)
.

Let
n\geq 3
and consider
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
of the form

\gamma(s)=\rho(s)\,(\cos s,\sin s),\qquad\rho\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}),\quad\rho(s+2\pi/n)=\rho(s),\quad\rho(-s)=\rho(s),\quad\rho(s)\geq r_{*}>0.

For a winding number
W\geq 1
, define the choreographic trajectories

q_{k}(t)=R_{2\pi k/n}\,\gamma\!\Big(Wt+\tfrac{2\pi k}{n}\Big),\qquad k=0,\dots,n-1.

Then, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and all
k=1,\dots,n-1
,

\|q_{k}(t)-q_{0}(t)\|\ \geq\ 2\,r_{*}\,\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{\pi}{n}\Big)\ =:\ \rho_{0}>0.

(8)

In particular, the configuration is collision–free and uniformly separated,
with a bound that does not depend on
W
.

Proof.

By definition,

q_{k}(t)-q_{0}(t)=R_{2\pi k/n}\,\gamma\!\Big(Wt+\tfrac{2\pi k}{n}\Big)-\gamma(Wt).

Using the
2\pi/n
–periodicity of
\rho
and the identity

R_{2\pi k/n}(\cos s,\sin s)=(\cos(s+\tfrac{2\pi k}{n}),\sin(s+\tfrac{2\pi k}{n}))
,
we obtain

q_{k}(t)-q_{0}(t)=(R_{2\pi k/n}-I)\,\gamma(Wt).

For any rotation
R_{\phi}
and vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
one has

\|(I-R_{\phi})x\|=2\|x\|\sin(\phi/2)
, hence

\|q_{k}(t)-q_{0}(t)\|=2\,\|\gamma(Wt)\|\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{\pi k}{n}\Big).

Since
\|\gamma(Wt)\|=\rho(Wt)\geq r_{*}
and

\min_{1\leq k\leq n-1}\sin(\pi k/n)=\sin(\pi/n)
, it follows that

\|q_{k}(t)-q_{0}(t)\|\ \geq\ 2\,r_{*}\,\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{\pi}{n}\Big)=:\rho_{0}>0.

∎

The estimate (
8
) depends only on
n
and the minimal radius
r_{*}
,
but not on the winding number
W
.
Thus, even for choreographies performing multiple turns around the origin,
the minimal pairwise separation remains uniform.

Corollary 2.4

(Collision exclusion)
.

Under the hypotheses of Proposition
2.3
,
all trajectories
q_{k}
are collision–free for all times.
Consequently, the nonlinear operator
N(u)
is well defined on
X_{D_{n}}
,
since the denominators

\|u(t)-R_{2\pi k/n}\,u(t+\tfrac{kT}{n})\|^{\alpha+2}

are uniformly bounded away from zero.

3
Functional framework and operators

The goal of this section is to formulate the
D_{n}
–equivariant choreography problem
in a rigorous functional setting, so that the equation

Lu=N(u)

is well posed as an identity between Hilbert (hence Banach) spaces.
We specify the functional spaces
X
and
Z
,
define the linear and nonlinear operators
L
and
N
,
and express
L
in a compact form using the
rotating covariant derivative

D_{t}=\partial_{t}+\Omega J
, which reveals its geometric and coercive structure.

3.1
Spaces
X
and
Z

Let
T>0
be the period of the generating curve
u(t)
.
We work with Sobolev spaces of
T
–periodic functions taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

We define

X=H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})=\{u\in H^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2}):u(0)=u(T),\,\dot{u}(0)=\dot{u}(T)\},

endowed with the norm

\|u\|_{X}^{2}=\|u\|_{H^{2}}^{2}=\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\ddot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

We additionally impose the
zero–mean
condition

\int_{0}^{T}u(t)\,dt=0,

which removes the translational invariance and guarantees the injectivity of
L
under nonresonance.

The target space is

Z=L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2}),\qquad\|v\|_{Z}=\|v\|_{L^{2}}.

The pair
(X,Z)
forms a Hilbert couple, and the embedding

X\hookrightarrow Z
is compact by the Rellich–Kondrachov theorem
[
1
]
.
Thus, the embedding
X\hookrightarrow Z
being compact implies that

L^{-1}:Z\to Z
is compact.

The
D_{n}
–equivariant symmetry is enforced by restricting
X
to the closed subspace

X_{D_{n}}=\Bigl\{u\in H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2}):u\!\bigl(t+\tfrac{T}{n}\bigr)=R_{2\pi/n}u(t),\;u(-t)=S\,u(t)\Bigr\}.

This subspace is invariant under both
L
and
N
because

SJS=-J
and the interaction terms are rotation–equivariant.
The existence problem will be analyzed entirely within
X_{D_{n}}
.

3.2
Definition of the operators
L
and
N

On
X_{D_{n}}
consider the
linear operator

Lu=-\ddot{u}-2\Omega J\dot{u}+\Omega^{2}u,\qquad L:X\to Z.

(9)

The minus sign is introduced for analytical convenience:
it renders
L

coercive
and interpretable as the negative of the
dynamical operator
\partial_{t}^{2}+2\Omega J\partial_{t}-\Omega^{2}
.
The principal part of
L
is the elliptic operator
-\partial_{t}^{2}
,
so
L
is a Fredholm operator of index zero from
H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}
to
L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}
.

The
nonlinear operator

N:X\to Z
is given by

(Nu)(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{m\,\bigl(u(t)-R_{2\pi k/n}\,u(t+\tfrac{kT}{n})\bigr)}{\|u(t)-R_{2\pi k/n}\,u(t+\tfrac{kT}{n})\|^{\alpha+2}},\qquad 0<\alpha<2.

(10)

Each term represents the gravitational interaction between the reference particle
and its rotated copy
R_{2\pi k/n}u(t+\tfrac{kT}{n})
.
By Proposition
2.3
,
the denominators remain uniformly bounded away from zero,
so
N
is well defined and continuous on
X_{D_{n}}
.
Moreover, since rotations and time shifts commute with differentiation,

N
preserves the
D_{n}
symmetry:
if
u\in X_{D_{n}}
then
N(u)\in X_{D_{n}}
.

Thus, the
D_{n}
choreography problem takes the abstract form

Lu=N(u),\qquad u\in X_{D_{n}}\subset H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2}),

(11)

which is the functional formulation required for the application of
Mawhin’s coincidence theorem.

3.3
Covariant derivative and compact form of
L

The operator
L
can be written compactly
in terms of the
rotating covariant derivative

D_{t}=\partial_{t}+\Omega J.

(12)

A direct computation gives

D_{t}^{2}=\partial_{t}^{2}+2\Omega J\partial_{t}+\Omega^{2}J^{2}=\partial_{t}^{2}+2\Omega J\partial_{t}-\Omega^{2}I,

so that

L=-D_{t}^{2}.

(13)

Hence
L
is the
negative covariant Laplacian
associated with
the flat connection determined by the constant matrix
\Omega J
.
In terms of
D_{t}
, the abstract equation (
11
) reads

-D_{t}^{2}u=N(u),

so that
D_{t}^{2}u
represents the covariant acceleration
as measured in the rotating frame.

3.4
Geometric and energetic interpretation

The operator
D_{t}
has a natural geometric meaning as a covariant derivative
associated with the rotating connection on the trivial bundle

[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[0,T]
,
whose fibers rotate with angular velocity
\Omega
.

In the inertial frame,
\dot{u}
measures the absolute variation of
u(t)
,
while in the rotating frame an additional change arises from the rotation of the basis.
This correction is expressed by the term
\Omega Ju
,
so that the effective derivative in the rotating frame is

D_{t}u=\dot{u}+\Omega Ju.

If
D_{t}u=0
, the vector
u(t)
co–rotates with the frame,
maintaining its orientation relative to it.

The associated energy identity is obtained by integration by parts:

\langle Lu,u\rangle_{L^{2}}=-\|D_{t}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

which shows that
L
is negative–semidefinite and that

\|D_{t}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
represents the kinetic energy
in the rotating frame, already incorporating the Coriolis and centrifugal effects.

In Section
5
we perform the spectral analysis of
L

using the Fourier basis, proving that under the nonresonance condition

k\omega\neq\Omega
with
\omega=2\pi/T
,
the operator
L:H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}\to L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}
is an isomorphism.
Its inverse
K=L^{-1}
is continuous and compact on bounded subsets of
L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}
,
thus satisfying the Fredholm and compactness hypotheses required by
Mawhin’s coincidence degree.

4
Mawhin’s framework and coincidence degree

Mawhin’s method provides a unified topological framework for proving
the existence of solutions to operator equations of the form

Lu=N(u),

(14)

where
L
is a linear Fredholm operator of index
0
between Banach (in particular, Hilbert) spaces,
and
N
is a continuous nonlinear perturbation that is
L
–compact
on bounded sets.
This generalizes the Leray–Schauder fixed point theorem
and is particularly suited to nonlinear differential equations of second order,
such as those arising in Celestial Mechanics.

4.1
Fredholm operators and canonical decomposition

Let
X
and
Z
be Banach spaces.
A bounded linear operator
L:\mathrm{dom}(L)\subset X\to Z

is
Fredholm of index
0
if

\dim\ker L<\infty,\qquad\mathrm{im}\,L\text{ is closed in }Z,\qquad\dim(Z/\mathrm{im}\,L)=\dim\ker L.

It follows from classical results (see, e.g.,
[
10
, Chap. IV]
) that there exist bounded projections

P:X\to X,\qquad Q:Z\to Z,

such that

X=\ker L\oplus\ker P,\qquad Z=\im L\oplus\im Q.

(15)

Hence:

•

P
projects onto
\ker L
along a closed complement
\ker P
;

•

Q
projects onto a closed complement
\im Q
of
\im L
in
Z
.

The equation
Lu=Nu
can only be solvable if
N(u)\in\im L
, that is,

Q\,N(u)=0.

Projecting (
14
) in the decomposition (
15
), one obtains

Lu=(I-Q)N(u)+QN(u),\qquad Lu\in\im L,\quad(I-Q)N(u)\in\im L,\quad QN(u)\in\im Q.

Since
\im L\cap\im Q=\{0\}
, the compatibility condition
QN(u)=0
is necessary and sufficient for solvability.

The restriction

L:\ \dom L\cap\ker P\longrightarrow\im L

is a bijection.
By the Closed Graph Theorem, its inverse is bounded, and we denote it by

K_{P}:\ \im L\longrightarrow\dom L\cap\ker P,\qquad L\circ K_{P}=I_{\im L}.

Therefore, for any
u\in X
the map

K_{P}(I-Q)N(u)

is well defined and compact when
N
is
L
–compact.
This decomposition underlies the functional structure of Mawhin’s degree.

Operational decomposition of
Lu=Nu
.

With the projections above, (
14
) is equivalent to the coupled system

\begin{cases}QN(u)=0,&\text{(compatibility condition)},\\[3.0pt]
u-K_{P}(I-Q)N(u)\in\ker L.&\text{(reconstruction equation)}.\end{cases}

When
\mathrm{ind}(L)=0
, we have
\dim\ker L=\mathrm{codim}\,\im L=\dim\im Q
,
and the condition
QN(u)=0
can be regarded as a finite–dimensional equation on
\ker L
,
while the second equation defines the correction on the complement.

Assuming temporarily that
L
is an isomorphism
(from
X
onto
Z
, a fact established later under the nonresonance condition

\Omega\neq\pm k\,\tfrac{2\pi}{T}
),
we may take
P=0
,
Q=0
, and
K=L^{-1}
.
The equation
Lu=Nu
then reduces to the fixed-point form

u=KN(u),

and the compactness of
K\circ N
follows from
the compact embedding
H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}\hookrightarrow L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}
.

4.2
L
–compactness and admissible homotopies

Let
L
be Fredholm of index
0
and
N:\overline{\mathcal{O}}\to Z
a continuous map
on the closure of a bounded open set
\mathcal{O}\subset X
.
The map
N
is said to be
L
–compact on
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
if:

(i)

QN(\overline{\mathcal{O}})
is bounded in
Z
;

(ii)

(K_{P}\circ(I-Q)N)(\overline{\mathcal{O}})
is relatively compact in
X
,
where
K_{P}=(L|_{\ker P})^{-1}
.

Intuitively,
N
is compact in the direction of
\im L

and uniformly bounded in the direction of
\ker L
.

A homotopy
H:[0,1]\times\overline{\mathcal{O}}\to Z
is called
L
–compact

if each map
H(\lambda,\cdot)
is
L
–compact
and the solutions of
Lu=H(\lambda,u)
remain in a bounded subset of
X

for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
.
These are the admissible homotopies for the coincidence degree.

4.3
Definition of the coincidence degree

The
Leray–Schauder degree
extends the Brouwer degree
to compact operators on Banach spaces.
Given a bounded open set
\mathcal{O}\subset X
and a compact operator

K:\overline{\mathcal{O}}\to X
,
the degree of
I-K
with respect to
0
is denoted by

\deg_{LS}(I-K,\mathcal{O},0)

and satisfies the usual properties of existence, additivity, and homotopy invariance
(cf.
[
5
,
20
,
14
]
).

Under Mawhin’s assumptions
[
12
]
,
the
coincidence degree

\deg_{L}(N,\mathcal{O},0)

associated with the problem
Lu=N(u)
is defined and enjoys the following properties:

•

\deg_{L}(N,\mathcal{O},0)
is well defined whenever

Lu\neq N(u)
for all
u\in\partial\mathcal{O}
.

•

If
\deg_{L}(N,\mathcal{O},0)\neq 0
,
then the equation
Lu=N(u)
has at least one solution in
\mathcal{O}
.

•

The degree is invariant under
L
–compact homotopies
that do not introduce solutions on
\partial\mathcal{O}
.

When
L
is an isomorphism between
X
and
Z
,
the coincidence degree reduces to the Leray–Schauder degree:

\deg_{L}(N,\mathcal{O},0)=\deg_{LS}(I-L^{-1}N,\mathcal{O},0).

In this case, the existence of a solution follows directly
from the Leray–Schauder fixed point theorem
(
[
5
,
20
,
14
]
).

4.4
Mawhin’s coincidence theorem

Theorem 4.1

(Mawhin
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
)
.

Let
X
and
Z
be Banach spaces,

L:\mathrm{dom}(L)\subset X\to Z
a linear Fredholm operator of index
0
,
and
N:\overline{\mathcal{O}}\to Z
a continuous map
which is
L
–compact on
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
,
where
\mathcal{O}\subset X
is a bounded open set.
Assume that

Lu\neq\lambda N(u),\qquad\forall u\in\partial\mathcal{O},\ \lambda\in[0,1],

and that
\deg_{L}(N,\mathcal{O},0)\neq 0
.
Then there exists at least one
u\in\mathcal{O}
such that
Lu=N(u)
.

This theorem extends the classical Leray–Schauder principle
and is particularly effective for periodic boundary value problems,
since the Fredholm and
L
–compactness hypotheses
are automatically satisfied by differential operators of second order
with periodic or zero–mean constraints.

4.5
Nonresonant case for the choreography problem

In our setting,
the linear operator
L:H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})

defined in (
9
) is Fredholm of index

,
and under the nonresonance condition
k\omega\neq\Omega

(with

)
it is an isomorphism.
Hence, the coincidence degree coincides with the Leray–Schauder degree,
and the
D_{n}
–choreography equation (

) takes the form

u=KN(u),\qquad K=L^{-1}.

The operator

is bounded,
and the embedding
H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}\hookrightarrow L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}
is compact,
so

is completely continuous.
Therefore, by the Leray–Schauder fixed point theorem,
any bounded open set
\mathcal{O}\subset X_{D_{n}}
satisfying

contains at least one fixed point of

,
equivalently one solution

of
Lu=N(u)
.

Geometrically, the coincidence equation (
14
)
represents an intersection between the graph of
N

and the subspace
\im L
.
Mawhin’s degree counts these intersections algebraically,
and its homotopy invariance guarantees persistence of solutions
along the family

Lu=\lambda N(u),\qquad\lambda\in[0,1],

as long as no solutions appear on the boundary of the chosen domain.
This principle underlies the application in the next section,
where we verify the
L
–compactness of

and establish a priori bounds
for the homotopy family above.

5
Properties of the linear operator
L

In this section we describe the analytical and spectral properties of the operator
Lu
:
its action on the Fourier basis, diagonalization via the spectral
projectors of
J
, characterization of its spectrum, and a
Gårding-type coercive estimate ensuring the invertibility of
L

under nonresonance conditions.

5.1
Fourier decomposition and action of
L

Since
L
has constant coefficients in
t
, its action is diagonal in the Fourier basis. Let
\omega=2\pi/T
.
Every
T
–periodic function
u:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
with zero mean admits the Fourier expansion

u(t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{i\omega kt}\,\hat{u}_{k},\qquad\hat{u}_{k}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}u(t)\,e^{-i\omega kt}\,dt\in\mathbb{C}^{2},\qquad\hat{u}_{-k}=\overline{\hat{u}_{k}}.

(16)

Recalling that

\frac{d}{dt}e^{i\omega kt}=i\omega k\,e^{i\omega kt},\qquad\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}e^{i\omega kt}=-(\omega k)^{2}e^{i\omega kt},

we compute the action of the operator
L=-\ddot{u}-2\Omega J\dot{u}+\Omega^{2}u

on each Fourier mode:

\displaystyle Lu(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{i\omega kt}\left[(\omega k)^{2}I-2i\omega k\Omega J+\Omega^{2}I\right]\hat{u}_{k}

(17)

We define the complex
2\times 2
matrix

A_{k}=(\omega k)^{2}I-2i\omega k\Omega J+\Omega^{2}I.

(18)

Then

Lu(t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{i\omega kt}A_{k}\hat{u}_{k}.

(19)

Thus
L
acts diagonally on the Fourier basis:
each frequency mode
k
is multiplied by
A_{k}
.
The spectral analysis of
L
therefore reduces to the eigenvalues of the matrices
A_{k}
.

The dihedral symmetry
u(-t)=S\,u(t)
translates into

\hat{u}_{-k}=S\,\overline{\hat{u}_{k}},\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z},

which couples opposite modes but preserves the diagonal structure of
L
.

5.2
Spectral projectors of
J

We extend to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
to diagonalize the rotation matrix

J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad J^{2}=-I,\qquad J^{*}=-J,

which has eigenvalues
\pm i
and orthonormal eigenvectors

v_{+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\binom{1}{i},\qquad v_{-}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\binom{1}{-i},\qquad Jv_{\pm}=\pm i\,v_{\pm}.

The corresponding spectral projectors are

P_{+}=\frac{1}{2}(I-iJ),\qquad P_{-}=\frac{1}{2}(I+iJ),

satisfying

P_{\pm}^{2}=P_{\pm},\quad P_{+}P_{-}=0,\quad P_{+}+P_{-}=I,\qquad\mathrm{im}\,P_{\pm}=\ker(J\mp iI).

(20)

Remark 5.1
.

The reflection matrix
S=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)
, used in the dihedral symmetry
u(-t)=S\,u(t)
,
satisfies
SJS=-J
. Hence
S
interchanges the two spectral subspaces of
J
, that is,

SP_{\pm}S=P_{\mp}
. This symmetry exchanges the “positive” and “negative”
rotational modes in the complex basis
\{v_{+},v_{-}\}
without affecting the overall spectrum.

5.3
Diagonalization of
A_{k}

Using
Jv_{\pm}=\pm iv_{\pm}
in (
18
), we obtain

A_{k}v_{\pm}=\big[(\omega k)^{2}+\Omega^{2}\mp 2\omega k\Omega\big]v_{\pm}=(\omega k\mp\Omega)^{2}v_{\pm}.

Hence
A_{k}
is diagonal in
\{v_{+},v_{-}\}
, with eigenvalues

\lambda_{k}^{\pm}=(\omega k\mp\Omega)^{2}.

(21)

In terms of the projectors (
20
),

A_{k}=\lambda_{k}^{+}P_{+}+\lambda_{k}^{-}P_{-},\qquad A_{k}^{-1}=(\lambda_{k}^{+})^{-1}P_{+}+(\lambda_{k}^{-})^{-1}P_{-},

(22)

whenever
\lambda_{k}^{\pm}\neq 0
.

Resonance occurs when
\lambda_{k}^{\pm}=0
, i.e.

\omega k=\pm\Omega.

To avoid singular modes we impose the
nonresonance condition

\omega k\neq\pm\Omega,\qquad\forall\,k\in\mathbb{Z}.

(23)

Under (
23
), all
A_{k}
are invertible, and consequently

L:H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})\longrightarrow L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})

is a continuous linear isomorphism.

Since
SJS=-J
, we have
SA_{k}S=A_{-k}
.
Thus the dihedral symmetry couples the modes
k
and
-k

but leaves the spectrum invariant.

The operator norm satisfies

\|A_{k}^{-1}\|=\frac{1}{\min\{|\omega k+\Omega|^{2},\,|\omega k-\Omega|^{2}\}},

(24)

so that

\|K\|=\|L^{-1}\|\leq\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\min\{|\omega k+\Omega|^{2},\,|\omega k-\Omega|^{2}\}}.

5.4
Energy identity and coercivity

Lemma 5.2

(Periodic Poincaré inequality)
.

Let
u:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a
T
–periodic function with zero mean,

\int_{0}^{T}u(t)\,dt=0.

Then the following inequality holds:

\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}\leq\frac{T}{2\pi}\,\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}.

(25)

Equivalently, in terms of the fundamental frequency
\omega=2\pi/T
,

\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\omega}\,\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}.

Proof.

Since
u
is
T
–periodic and has zero mean, it admits a Fourier series expansion

u(t)=\sum_{k\neq 0}\hat{u}_{k}\,e^{i\omega kt},\qquad\omega=\tfrac{2\pi}{T}.

Differentiating termwise gives

\dot{u}(t)=\sum_{k\neq 0}i\omega k\,\hat{u}_{k}\,e^{i\omega kt}.

By Parseval’s identity,

\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=T\sum_{k\neq 0}|\hat{u}_{k}|^{2},\qquad\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=T\sum_{k\neq 0}(\omega k)^{2}|\hat{u}_{k}|^{2}.

Since
|k|\geq 1
for all nonzero modes,

\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq\omega^{2}T\sum_{k\neq 0}|\hat{u}_{k}|^{2}=\omega^{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

and taking square roots yields (
25
).
∎

Lemma 5.3

(Coercivity of the operator
L
)
.

Let

Lu=-\ddot{u}-2\Omega J\dot{u}+\Omega^{2}u,

acting on the space of real
T
–periodic functions
u:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
with zero mean.
Then, if
|\Omega|<\omega
with
\omega=2\pi/T
, there exists a constant

c>0
such that

\langle Lu,u\rangle_{L^{2}}\geq c\,\|u\|_{H^{1}(0,T)}^{2},\qquad\forall\,u\in H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})\text{ with }\int_{0}^{T}u(t)\,dt=0.

(26)

Proof.

Multiplying
Lu
by
u
and integrating by parts over
[0,T]
, using the periodic boundary conditions, we obtain

\langle Lu,u\rangle_{L^{2}}=\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-2\Omega\int_{0}^{T}\langle J\dot{u}(t),u(t)\rangle\,dt+\Omega^{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Since
\|Jv\|=\|v\|
for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the mixed term is bounded by

\Bigl|2\Omega\int_{0}^{T}\langle J\dot{u},u\rangle\,dt\Bigr|\leq 2|\Omega|\,\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}\|u\|_{L^{2}}.

Therefore,

\langle Lu,u\rangle_{L^{2}}\geq\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-2|\Omega|\,\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}\|u\|_{L^{2}}+\Omega^{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\big(\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}-|\Omega|\|u\|_{L^{2}}\big)^{2}.

Applying the periodic Poincaré inequality (Lemma
5.2
),

\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq\omega^{-1}\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}
, gives

\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}-|\Omega|\|u\|_{L^{2}}\geq\Bigl(1-\frac{|\Omega|}{\omega}\Bigr)\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}},

so that

\langle Lu,u\rangle_{L^{2}}\geq\Bigl(1-\frac{|\Omega|}{\omega}\Bigr)^{2}\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Finally, since
\|u\|_{H^{1}}^{2}=\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq(1+\omega^{-2})\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
by Poincaré’s inequality again,
inequality (
26
) holds with

c=\frac{\bigl(1-\tfrac{|\Omega|}{\omega}\bigr)^{2}}{1+\omega^{-2}}>0.

∎

5.5
Fredholm property and right inverse

Proposition 5.4

(Fredholm property of
L
)
.

Under the nonresonance condition (
23
),
the operator

L:H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})\longrightarrow L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})

is Fredholm of index
0
, with

\ker L=\{0\},\qquad\im L=L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}},\qquad\mathrm{ind}(L)=0.

Moreover,
L
admits a bounded inverse
K=L^{-1}:L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}\to H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}

given by

(Kf)(t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{i\omega kt}A_{k}^{-1}\hat{f}_{k},\qquad f(t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{i\omega kt}\hat{f}_{k},

(27)

which satisfies

\|Kf\|_{H^{2}}\leq C\,\|f\|_{L^{2}},

and is compact on bounded subsets of
L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}
by Rellich’s theorem.

Proof.

If
Lu=0
, then each Fourier coefficient satisfies
A_{k}\hat{u}_{k}=0
.
By (
23
), all
A_{k}
are invertible, so
\hat{u}_{k}=0
for all
k
, hence
u=0
.
Conversely, for any
f\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}
, define
\hat{u}_{k}=A_{k}^{-1}\hat{f}_{k}
.
Then
u
given by (
27
)
\in H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}
and satisfies
Lu=f
.
Boundedness and compactness of
K
follow from the decay
\|A_{k}^{-1}\|=O(k^{-2})
.
∎

Corollary 5.5

(Isomorphism and symmetry invariance)
.

Under (
23
),
the operator
L
is a bounded isomorphism

L:H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})\longrightarrow L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Moreover, if
u(-t)=S\,u(t)
, then
L(u)(-t)=S\,L(u)(t)
;
hence
L
maps the symmetric subspace
X_{D_{n}}
into itself.

The results above guarantee that
L
satisfies all requirements
of Mawhin’s framework: it is a continuous Fredholm operator of index
0

with compact inverse and coercivity estimate (
26
).
In the next section we derive
a priori
bounds for the homotopy

Lu=\lambda N(u),\qquad\lambda\in[0,1],

which ensure boundedness and collision exclusion,
thereby completing the hypotheses of Mawhin’s coincidence theorem.

6
A priori bounds for the homotopy
Lu=\lambda N(u)

A crucial step in applying Mawhin’s degree is to establish
a priori
bounds for the solutions of the homotopic family

Lu=\lambda N(u),\qquad\lambda\in[0,1],

(28)

We show that any solution
u
of (
28
)
remains bounded in
H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}
by a constant independent of
\lambda
,
and that there are no nontrivial solutions on the boundary of the working domain.

Taking the
L^{2}
inner product of (
28
) with
u
and integrating over
[0,T]
yields

\langle Lu,u\rangle_{L^{2}}=\lambda\,\langle N(u),u\rangle_{L^{2}}.

(29)

using Lemma
5.3
,
we have

\langle Lu,u\rangle_{L^{2}}\geq c\,\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\qquad c=\Bigl(1-\frac{|\Omega|}{\omega}\Bigr)^{2}>0.

Substituting this estimate into (
29
) yields

c\,\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\lambda\,|\langle N(u),u\rangle_{L^{2}}|.

(30)

6.1
Homogeneity of the nonlinear term

For equal masses,
N(u)
derives from the homogeneous potential

U(u)=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{m^{2}}{|u_{i}-u_{j}|^{\alpha}},\qquad N(u)=\nabla U(u),

with homogeneity degree
-\alpha
(
0<\alpha<2
).
By Euler’s identity,

\langle N(u),u\rangle=\langle\nabla U(u),u\rangle=-\alpha\,U(u)\leq 0.

Hence
|\langle N(u),u\rangle|=\alpha\,|U(u)|
, and from (
30
)

c\,\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\lambda\,\alpha\,|U(u)|.

(31)

This inequality shows that the mean kinetic energy is controlled by the interaction energy.

6.2
Uniform separation and collision exclusion

The
D_{n}
symmetry ensures a uniform geometric separation between the choreographic particles.
Let

d(t)=\min_{i\neq j}|u_{i}(t)-u_{j}(t)|

be the instantaneous minimal interparticle distance.
By symmetry and regularity of
X_{D_{n}}
, there exists
\rho>0
such that

d(t)\geq\rho,\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T].

(32)

This excludes collisions and guarantees smoothness of
N
on the closed set

\overline{B_{R}}=\{u\in X_{D_{n}}:\|u\|_{H^{1}}\leq R,\ d(t)\geq\rho\}.

For such configurations,

|U(u)|\leq\frac{n(n-1)}{2\rho^{\alpha}}=:C_{U}(R,\rho).

Substituting into (
31
) gives

\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{\alpha\,C_{U}(R,\rho)}{c},\qquad c=1-\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\omega^{2}}>0.

(33)

6.3
Bound in
H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}

For zero–mean
T
–periodic functions the Poincaré inequality

\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\omega}\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}},\qquad\omega=\frac{2\pi}{T},

yields

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\Bigr)\|\dot{u}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(34)

Combining (
34
) and (
33
),

\|u\|_{H^{1}(0,T)}^{2}\leq\frac{\alpha\,C_{U}(R,\rho)}{c}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\Bigr).

(35)

Setting

R_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha\,C_{U}(R,\rho)}{c}}\,\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{\omega}\Bigr)\sqrt{\alpha\,C_{U}(R,\rho)},

we obtain a uniform bound

\|u\|_{H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}}\leq R_{0},\qquad\forall\,\lambda\in[0,1].

(36)

This bound is independent of
\lambda
and depends only on the physical parameters

\alpha
,
\Omega
,
\omega
,
n
, and the separation
\rho
.

6.4
Definition of the working domain

We define the open set

\Omega_{R,\rho}=\{u\in X_{D_{n}}:\ \rho<\|u\|_{H^{1}}<R\},

where
R>R_{0}
and
\rho>0
is small enough so that
no nontrivial solution satisfies
\|u\|_{H^{1}}=\rho
.
Indeed, for
\|u\|_{H^{1}}
sufficiently small,

N(u)=O(\|u\|_{H^{1}})
by smoothness of
N
,
and the coercivity of
L
implies
u=0
.

Therefore:

•

there are no solutions on the inner boundary
\|u\|_{H^{1}}=\rho
;

•

all homotopic solutions satisfy
\|u\|_{H^{1}}\leq R
.

These conditions ensure that the
\deg(L-N,\Omega_{R,\rho},0)

is well defined and constant in
\lambda
.

7
Properties of the nonlinear operator
N

We now analyze the analytical properties of the nonlinear operator

N:X\to Z
that represents the gravitational interaction in the rotating frame.
Its explicit form was given in (
10
);
here we recall that
N(u)=-\nabla U(u)
,
where
U(u)
is the homogeneous potential of degree
-\alpha
(
0<\alpha<2
).
The operator

is smooth on the collision–free region

and satisfies

as used in the previous section.

These properties ensure that

is continuous, compact,
and of controlled polynomial growth on bounded subsets of

,
as required by Mawhin’s framework.

7.1
D_{n}
–equivariant reduction

The configuration space
X_{D_{n}}
consists of
D_{n}
–symmetric

T
–periodic trajectories satisfying

u_{j}(t)=R\!\left(\frac{2\pi(j-1)}{n}\right)u_{1}\!\left(t+\frac{(j-1)T}{n}\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,n,

so that the entire motion is generated by a single function
u_{1}(t)
.
Under this symmetry, both
L
and
N
leave
X_{D_{n}}
invariant,
and
N
induces a smooth map

N:X_{D_{n}}\longrightarrow Z_{D_{n}},

which coincides with the restriction of the full gravitational interaction
to the collision–free subspace
\{u\in X_{D_{n}}:\,|u_{i}-u_{j}|\geq\rho>0\}
.
Hence
N
is
D_{n}
–equivariant, continuous, and compact on bounded subsets of
X_{D_{n}}
.

7.2
Regularity and compactness

The potential
U(u)
is
C^{\infty}

on the collision–free set

\mathcal{D}=\{u\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{n}:\ u_{i}\neq u_{j}\ \forall\,i\neq j\}.

Therefore
N=-\nabla U
is also
C^{\infty}
on
\mathcal{D}
.

Let

B_{R}=\{\,u\in H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ \|u\|_{H^{2}}\leq R\,\}

be the closed ball of radius
R
in
X=H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

If
B_{R}\subset X
is a bounded set such that a minimal inter–particle distance

\rho>0
exists on
B_{R}
, then
each denominator
|u_{i}-u_{j}|^{\alpha+2}
is uniformly bounded away from zero,
and there exists
C_{R}>0
with

\|N(u)\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}\leq C_{R},\qquad\forall\,u\in B_{R}.

(37)

Let
(u_{k})
be a bounded sequence in
X=H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
By the compact embedding

H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}\hookrightarrow C^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}

(and thus into
C^{0}_{\mathrm{per}}
),
we have
u_{k}\to u
uniformly (up to a subsequence).
Each term in (
10
) depends continuously on
(u_{i},u_{j})
,
so
N(u_{k})\to N(u)
in
L^{2}
.
Hence

N:X\to Z\quad\text{is completely continuous (compact).}

7.3
Local Lipschitz continuity

Lemma 7.1

(Lipschitz estimate for
N
)
.

Let
N:X_{D_{n}}\to Z_{D_{n}}
be the nonlinear operator
associated with the homogeneous potential of degree
-\alpha
,
with
0<\alpha<2
.
For every bounded collision–free set
B_{R}\subset X_{D_{n}}
,
there exists a constant
C=C(R,\alpha,n,m,T)>0

such that for all
u,v\in B_{R}
,

\|N(u)-N(v)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\,\|u-v\|_{C^{0}}\,\big(\|u\|_{C^{0}}^{-\alpha-1}+\|v\|_{C^{0}}^{-\alpha-1}\big).

(38)

Proof.

Each interaction term

f(x,y)=-(y-x)/|y-x|^{\alpha+2}
is
C^{\infty}
for
x\neq y

and homogeneous of degree
-(\alpha+1)
.
Its derivative satisfies

\|Df(x,y)\|\leq C_{0}|y-x|^{-(\alpha+2)}
.
If
|x_{i}-y_{i}|\geq\rho>0
, the mean–value theorem gives

\|f(x_{1},y_{1})-f(x_{2},y_{2})\|\leq C_{1}\rho^{-(\alpha+2)}\|(x_{1}-x_{2},y_{1}-y_{2})\|.

The uniform separation on
B_{R}
(Proposition
2.3
)
ensures such a
\rho>0
; summing over the
n-1
interactions
and integrating over
[0,T]
yields

\|N(u)-N(v)\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{2}\sqrt{T}\,\rho^{-(\alpha+2)}\|u-v\|_{C^{0}},

and the homogeneity of
f
produces the factor

(\|u\|_{C^{0}}^{-\alpha-1}+\|v\|_{C^{0}}^{-\alpha-1})

in (
38
).
∎

Thus
N
is locally Lipschitz on bounded collision–free subsets of
X_{D_{n}}
.

In summary, for all
u\in X_{D_{n}}
in the collision–free region:

(i)

N:X_{D_{n}}\to Z_{D_{n}}
is
C^{\infty}
;

(ii)

N
is completely continuous on bounded sets;

(iii)

\langle N(u),u\rangle=-\alpha\,U(u)\leq 0
;

(iv)

N
is locally Lipschitz with growth (
38
).

7.4
L
–compactness of
N

Recall that

L:H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2}),\qquad Lu=-\ddot{u}-2\Omega J\dot{u}+\Omega^{2}u,

is a Fredholm operator of index
0

and is invertible under the nonresonance condition

\omega k\neq\pm\Omega
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

When
L
is invertible, the notion of
L
–compactness simplifies:
a continuous map
N:\overline{\Omega}\subset X\to Z

is said to be
L
–compact on
\overline{\Omega}
if

K\circ N
is compact, where
K=L^{-1}
.
Since
N
is completely continuous on bounded sets and

K:L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}\to H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}
is linear and bounded,
their composition
K\circ N
is compact on
H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}
.

Proposition 7.2

(
L
–compactness of
N
)
.

The nonlinear operator
N:X\to Z
defined by (
10
)
is
L
–compact on any bounded subset
\overline{B_{R}}\subset X
.
Equivalently,

K\circ N:H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})\longrightarrow H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})

is completely continuous.

Proof.

As shown in the previous subsection, the operator

N:X\to Z=L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}

is continuous and compact on bounded subsets of
X
.
The inverse operator
K=L^{-1}:L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}\to H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}

is linear and bounded because
L
is an isomorphism.
Hence, for any bounded sequence
(u_{k})\subset X
,
the sequence
(N(u_{k}))
has a convergent subsequence in
L^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}
;
applying
K
preserves convergence in
H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}

since
K
is continuous.
Therefore
K\circ N
maps bounded sets into relatively compact sets in
H^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}
,
and is thus completely continuous.
∎

Since
L
is a Fredholm isomorphism of index
0

and
N
is
L
–compact on bounded subsets of
X
,
the coincidence equation
Lu=N(u)

fits precisely within Mawhin’s framework.

8
Existence of solutions via Mawhin’s degree

In this section we apply Mawhin’s coincidence degree to establish
the existence of a solution
u\in X
to

Lu=N(u),

(39)

describing a
D_{n}
–equivariant choreography of the
n
–body problem
in the rotating frame.
All analytical hypotheses required for Mawhin’s theorem
have been verified in the previous sections.

The setting now fits precisely into the abstract framework described in Mawhin’s theory, with
L
linear Fredholm of index
0
and
N
a compact perturbation.

8.1
Fredholm setting and projections

From Section
5
we know that:

\bullet

L
is a Fredholm operator of index
0
;

\bullet

under the nonresonance condition
\omega k\neq\pm\Omega
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,

L
is an isomorphism from
X
onto
Z
;

\bullet

its inverse
K=L^{-1}:Z\to X
is linear and bounded,
with
\|Kz\|_{H^{2}}\leq C_{L}\|z\|_{L^{2}}
.

In Mawhin’s general framework one introduces projections

P:X\to\ker L,\qquad Q:Z\to Z/\mathrm{im}\,L,

and a generalized inverse
K_{P}:\mathrm{im}\,L\to(\ker L)^{\perp}

with
LK_{P}=I-Q
and
K_{P}L=I-P
.
Since
L
is invertible (thus
\ker L=\{0\}
and
\mathrm{im}\,L=Z
),
we have simply

P=Q=0,\qquad K_{P}=K=L^{-1}.

Hence Mawhin’s construction simplifies considerably.

8.2
Verification of Mawhin’s hypotheses

Theorem
4.1

states that if
L:X\to Z
is Fredholm of index
0
,

N:X\to Z
is
L
–compact on a bounded open set
\Omega\subset X
, and

\displaystyle Lu\neq\lambda N(u),

\displaystyle\forall u\in\partial\Omega,\ \lambda\in(0,1],

(40)

\displaystyle QN(u)\neq 0,

\displaystyle\forall u\in\partial\Omega\cap\ker L,

then
\deg(L-N,\Omega,0)
is well defined and,
if nonzero, there exists
u\in\Omega
such that
Lu=N(u)
.

In our case the verification proceeds as follows:

(i)

L
is Fredholm of index
0
.

Established in Section
5

by the Fourier decomposition and the diagonalization of
A_{k}
.
Under nonresonance,
L
is an isomorphism with bounded inverse.

(ii)

N
is
L
–compact on bounded sets.

Shown in Section
7.4
.
In particular,
K\circ N
is compact from
X
into
X
.

(iii)

A priori bounds.

Section
6
provided constants
\rho,R>0
such that
every solution of
Lu=\lambda N(u)
,
\lambda\in[0,1]
, satisfies the uniform bound

\|u\|_{H^{1}}\leq R
and no solution exists with
\|u\|_{H^{1}}=\rho
.
Thus there are no zeros on the boundary of
\Omega_{R,\rho}
.

(iv)

Choice of domain.

We take

\Omega_{R,\rho}=\{\,u\in X:\ \rho<\|u\|_{H^{1}}<R\,\},

with
R>\rho
fixed by the previous bounds.

Since
L
is invertible,
\ker L=\{0\}
and the image is the whole

40

8.3
Value of the degree and existence of solutions

Define the homotopy

H_{\lambda}(u)=Lu-\lambda N(u),\qquad\lambda\in[0,1].

(41)

By the a priori bounds,
H_{\lambda}(u)=0

has no solutions on the boundary
\partial\Omega_{R,\rho}
.
Hence
\deg(L-\lambda N,\Omega_{R,\rho},0)

is well defined for every
\lambda\in[0,1]
and remains constant in
\lambda
.

For
\lambda=0
one has
H_{0}(u)=Lu
.
Since
L
is an isomorphism and
0\notin L(\partial\Omega_{R,\rho})
,

\deg(L,\Omega_{R,\rho},0)=\deg(I,\Omega_{R,\rho},0)=1.

By homotopy invariance of the coincidence degree,

\deg(L-N,\Omega_{R,\rho},0)=\deg(L-\lambda N,\Omega_{R,\rho},0)=\deg(L,\Omega_{R,\rho},0)=1.

Therefore there exists
u\in\Omega_{R,\rho}\subset X

such that
Lu=N(u)
.

8.4
Main existence result

Summarizing the above arguments, we obtain the following existence result for

–equivariant choreographies.

–equivariant,

Proof.

Under nonresonance,
