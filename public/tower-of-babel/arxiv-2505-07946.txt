Interplay between Electroweak Symmetry Breaking and Dark Matter Relic Density Preprint: IPPP/25/26
Sreemanti Chakraborti, André Milagre, Rui Santos, João P. Silva

Abstract
Models of Dark Matter must contend with the fact that the presence of electroweak symmetry breaking along the thermal evolution of the Universe modifies the masses, interactions and, thus, the thermally averaged cross sections. We study in detail the impact of taking (not taking) the presence of the electroweak symmetry breaking into account in the calculations of the Dark Matter relic density, providing a model-independent measure of such differences. By focusing on a particular model, we show that ignoring this effect can lead to the inclusion (exclusion) of wrong (viable) regions of parameter space.

Introduction

The spectrum of temperature fluctuations in the Cosmic Microwave Background has offered physicists an extraordinary opportunity to measure the relative abundances of the various energy components of the Universe.
The structures that make up these fluctuations are consistent with the existence of a non-baryonic matter component that exceeds the amount of ordinary baryonic matter by a factor around five, offering, together with a number of experimental results from many other experimental sources

Namely, photons and neutrinos.
This mechanism assumes that in the early stages of our Universe,
i.e.
at very high temperatures, the interaction rate between DM and particles in the primordial thermal bath was strong enough to keep them in chemical and thermal equilibrium. However, as the Universe expanded and cooled, there arose a moment (the freeze-out instant) when the annihilation rate of DM into lighter particles became subdominant to the Hubble expansion rate, resulting in the decoupling of DM from the thermal plasma and the subsequent
freeze-out
of its comoving number density.

In the freeze-out scenario, the present relic density is computed by solving the Boltzmann Equation, which describes the change in the number density of the DM species over time. As an input, one needs to provide a cosmological history for our universe (contained in the Hubble parameter) and a set of thermally averaged DM depletion processes (coming from the particle physics model chosen, provided it has a DM candidate). The solution to the Boltzmann Equation can then be written implicitly as an integral over
all time
. With this information, one may predict the corresponding DM relic density and test it against the observed value in Eq. (
1
).

In practice, analytical and numerical solutions are hard to find, so one usually resorts to approximations to perform the aforementioned integral. One such approximation relies on the assumption that DM particles remain in thermal equilibrium with the primordial bath until the freeze-out instant, implying that the Boltzmann equation becomes dominated by DM depletion processes occurring at temperatures below freeze-out, and hence below the DM mass scale. In effect, this reduces the problem to integrating the Boltzmann Equation only from freeze-out to the present time
Cline:2013gha
;
Arcadi:2024ukq
. Since the relevant integration range lies at lower temperatures, the process becomes effectively low-energy, and finite temperature corrections are typically neglected in this scenario. This contrasts with other DM production mechanisms where these higher-order corrections play an important role in accurately predicting the present DM abundance
Baker:2017zwx
;
Chakrabarty:2022bcn
.

The
standard
approach, therefore, is to calculate all thermally averaged DM depletion processes in the low-energy regime and input them into the Boltzmann Equation. However, this reasoning misses the unavoidable fact that the Higgs boson acquires a non-zero vacuum expectation value (vev), triggering electroweak symmetry breaking (EWSB) at temperatures around
T_{\rm EWSB}\approx 160\,
GeV
Laine:2015kra
.
It is therefore expected that the particle masses and interaction rates change across the EWSB transition. In this sense, we propose an
improved
approach for calculating the DM relic density in which both before and after EWSB interactions are considered. As we will demonstrate, neglecting the effects of EWSB in the calculation of the DM relic density is especially concerning for DM candidates that freeze-out before EWSB and can lead to large discrepancies between the
standard
and
improved
approaches. We prove that the deviations from the
standard
approach are driven by a change in the freeze-out instant and/or by a change in the thermally averaged DM depletion processes. In addition, we provide an analytical way to quantify this deviation.

II.1
Relic density: the standard approach

Consider a SM extension with a single DM candidate of mass
m_{\rm DM}
which, at high enough temperatures, is kept in thermal and chemical equilibrium with the primordial bath through
2\to 2

To calculate the present abundance of DM in our Universe, we make use of the Boltzmann Equation to track its number density

For practical purposes, it is useful to define the particle number density per comoving volume (or yield) as
Y_{\rm DM}\equiv n_{\rm DM}/s

The Boltzmann equation describing the time evolution of the yield in this scenario can be written as

;

\frac{dY_{\rm DM}}{dx}=Z(x)\left(\overline{Y}(x)^{2}-Y_{\rm DM}^{2}\right),

(2)

\overline{Y}(x)=\frac{45}{4\pi^{4}}\frac{x^{2}}{h_{\mathrm{eff}}(x)}K_{2}(x),

(3)

Z(x)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{45}}\frac{m_{\rm DM}M_{\rm Pl}}{x^{2}}\sqrt{g_{*}}\langle\sigma v\rangle(x).

(4)

In Eqs. (

h_{\rm eff}
and
g_{\rm eff}

Further,

is the total thermal averaged DM annihilation cross-section.

Equation (
2

), and after which they decouple, leading to their comoving number density freezing out (meaning
Y\gg\overline{Y}

In this sense, it is convenient to reparameterize the yield as
Y_{\rm DM}\approx(1+\delta)\overline{Y}
, where
\delta
is a monotonically increasing function of
x

In this approximation, the decoupling instant

can be numerically obtained by iteration through the following expression
Cline:2013gha

x_{f}=\log\left(e^{x}\,\frac{\delta_{f}(2+\delta_{f})}{1+\delta_{f}}\frac{Z\,\overline{Y}}{\left(-\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{Y}}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)}\right)_{x=x_{f}},

(5)

where
\delta_{f}
is a
\mathcal{O}(1)
number
2
2

2

For example,

micrOMEGAs

;

\overline{Y}(x_{f})

After the freeze-out instant, the

In turn, the present-day DM yield is given by

Y_{\rm DM}^{\rm today}\approx\frac{Y_{f}}{1+Y_{f}A_{x_{f}\to\infty}},

(6)

where

A_{x_{1}\to x_{2}}=\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}Z(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{45}}m_{\mathrm{DM}}M_{\mathrm{Pl}}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\,\frac{\sqrt{g_{*}}\left<\sigma\mathrm{v}\right>}{x^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(7)

Then, the present DM relic density is given by

\Omega h^{2}\approx 2.744\times 10^{8}\,m_{\mathrm{DM}}\,Y_{\rm DM}^{\rm today}.

(8)

II.2
Relic density: the improved approach

As explained in the previous section, the
standard
way to calculate the present DM relic density is to integrate the function
Z(x)
from the
x
value at decoupling (
x=x_{f}
) until the present epoch (
x\to\infty

.

However, when EWSB occurs
after
the DM freeze-out,
i.e.
, when
T_{f}>T_{\rm EWSB}

7

7

and

Note that EWSB is expected to occur at a temperature

T_{\rm EWSB}\sim 160\,\mathrm{GeV}

\displaystyle x_{f}<x_{\rm EWSB}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle m_{\rm DM}>160\times 25\ {\rm GeV}\sim 4\,{\rm TeV}\,.

standard
approach to break down for DM masses above 4 TeV, motivating the need for an
improved
approach to more accurately calculate the present relic density for heavy DM candidates.

V
Conclusions

We propose a new approach to calculate the DM relic density in which the interplay between the occurrence of EWSB and the DM freeze-out instant is taken into account. In thermal freeze-out, one assumes that the DM particles
remain in thermal equilibrium with the primordial bath until freeze-out occurs. In turn, this implies that the Boltzmann equation is solved by integrating from the freeze-out instant to the present time. In the standard approach, the DM particles and processes
are always taken as those of the model’s broken phase.
We have shown that in scenarios where DM freezes-out before EWSB, the use of the standard approach can lead to substantial discrepancies in the calculated value of the relic density.
This has an important consequence for model building. By sticking to the standard approach, one can either exclude points in parameter space which are good, or permit points which in fact do not yield the correct relic abundance. In the limit, one may exclude a good model or permit a wrong one.
The improved approach, described in our
study, makes this distinction:
we use the model in the unbroken phase, before EWSB;
and the model in the broken phase, after EWSB.
The differences between the two approaches have their origin in the thermally
averaged cross section, which is modified by EWSB with a redefinition
of masses and interactions.
We were able to quantify the difference between the calculations in the two scenarios
in a model independent way, in Eqs. (
20
)-(
23
).

We then made use of a simple model with two singlets and two independent symmetries; after EWSB, both the Higgs doublet and one of the scalars acquire vevs, breaking the
SU(2)\times U(1)
and
Z_{2}^{\prime}
symmetries, respectively.
We chose to work with this model and vacuum configuration
not only to simplify our analysis, but also because the differences between the

standard
and
improved approaches
were not that significant
in the (simpler) real scalar singlet extension of the SM.
Taking the DM mass above about 4 TeV after EWSB, we showed that indeed the two approaches lead to different relic densities.
Moreover, this difference can be either negative or positive,
implying the
improved
relic density can lie below or above
the
standard
relic density, respectively.

It is important to note that these heavy DM particles are not excluded by experiment. The ongoing and planned direct and indirect detection experiments (see Ref.
Cirelli:2024ssz
for a comprehensive review) will most certainly
give rise to an increase (or even exclusion) in the upper bound of the DM mass in this simple scenario with two singlets and two independent symmetries. However, it is easy to extend the model with, for example, an extra singlet, such that the couplings responsible for direct and indirect detection are not the same as the ones that determine the value of the relic density. We defer a detailed investigation of such extensions to future work.

The study also builds a strong case for testing TeV-scale phenomena in upcoming high-energy particle colliders such as the FCC-hh
Benedikt:2022kan
.
In fact, although models for which our approach is important have very heavy DM candidates, they could in principle be probed at future colliders. In this case the approach could prove instrumental in the reconstruction of a given potential from an extended scalar sector
