Comparative Evaluation of Microleakage of Glass Ionomer and Glass Ionomer with Ethanolic Extract of Propolis – An In Vitro Study
Authors: Deepti V. Balehosur, Jasmine Mary Antony, Deeksha Sharma, Vijayalaxmi Divater, Alan Winston David, Praveen Kumar Bali
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:Aim of the study is to evaluate microleakage of conventional GIC and addition of 1% ethanolic extract of propolis to conventional GIC.Methodology:A study was conducted between two groups. Group A was the conventional GIC group (control) and Group B was treated with GIC incorporated with 1% ethanolic extract of propolis (experimental). To evaluate microleakage, 15 samples were used and class V cavity preparation and restoration were done. Later, they were placed in 0.6% Rhodamine-B dye and evaluated for dye penetration under stereomicroscope.Results:Results showed that microleakage of the experimental group was decreased compared to that of the control group, but it was not statistically significant (P = 0.38).

INTRODUCTION

In the current age of adhesive dentistry, conservation of tooth structure is of paramount importance, which aims to arrest dentinal demineralization and degradation.[12] Atraumatic restorative treatment (ART) is a conservative and minimal intervention approach that involves the removal of demineralized tooth tissues using manual instruments and restored with adhesive restorative material like glass ionomer cement.[3] Despite this, there are certain limitations in ART technique as bacteria remain within the tubule of the affected dentin even after excavation, and these lead to secondary caries and restoration failure.[4] It is reported in many studies that the antibacterial property of glass ionomer cement (GIC) is due to fluoride release, but newer viscous GIC releases less fluoride ions.[56] Therapeutic benefit may be gained by combining antibacterial agents with glass ionomer cement.[7] In literature, the addition of antimicrobial agents to GIC was reported to increase the antibacterial effect, but this addition to GIC led to antibiotic resistance and failed to maintain the international organization for standardization (ISO) standard for physical and mechanical properties.[8] Hence, there is a need for an alternative material which does not cause changes in physical properties.

In the past few decades, there has been an increased use of natural products for medicinal purposes.[9] One such natural mode of treatment is apitherapy. Apitherapy is the art and science of treatment and healing through honeybee and its products. Propolis is a resin collected by the honeybee Apis mellifera and is used for thermal isolation, closure, and protection of the hive against microorganisms. Propolis has a complex chemical composition, and it possesses antibacterial, antifungal, and anti-inflammatory properties and immune-modulating effects. It has been shown to exert antibacterial action against a number of oral microorganisms including Streptococcus mutans.[10] Effect of propolis on the physical properties of GIC as a natural antibacterial agent has not been clearly understood. Hence, the present study evaluated microleakage of addition of 1% ethanolic extract of propolis to conventional GIC.

METHODOLOGY

Subjects and methods

In this study, Group I-conventional GIC (control group) Group II-conventional GIC with 1% ethanolic extract of propolis (experimental group).

Preparation of 1% ethanolic extract of propolis-incorporated GIC

We used 20% propolis in 60% ethanol. Filtration was carried out, and the obtained product was dried, which was sticky in consistency. Dilutions of propolis were prepared to get a final concentration of 1% ethanolic extract of propolis (EEP). To achieve a final concentration of 1% EEP, 0.08 ml of EEP was added to 7.92 ml of glass ionomer liquid.[11]

Microleakage

Class V cavity preparation was done on the buccal and lingual surfaces of 15 extracted human premolar teeth. Buccal side was used in the experimental group and lingual side in the control group; they were subjected to a thermocycling regimen of 500 cycles between 50°C and 550°C, with a dwell time of 1 min and 3 sec transfer between the baths. Teeth were dried and coated with two layers of nail varnish with two different colors for the two groups, leaving a 1-mm window around the cavity margins. After drying, the apical foramen was sealed with modeling wax and samples were placed in a solution of 0.6% Rhodamine-B dye for 24 h at 37°C under vacuum. After 24 h, the specimens were removed from the dye, the dye that was adhered to the surface was rinsed in tap water and dried, and the specimens were sectioned longitudinally. The section with the greater leakage was evaluated with a stereomicroscope at 25 × magnification to determine the extent of dye penetration. The scores were evaluated using Michal staininec and mark holtz (1988) scores system. The scoring was as follows: score 1- no dye penetration; score 2- dye penetration along the occlusal wall, but less than halfway to the axial wall; and score 3- dye penetration along the occlusal wall, but more than halfway to the axial wall.[12]

RESULTS

The obtained data for microleakage was collected, tabulated, and subjected to statistical analysis. Mean and standard deviations were calculated by Chi-square test and Mann–Whitney test [Table 1]. The control group showed a mean dye penetration of 2.5 ± 0.6 and the experimental group showed 2.8 ± 0.4, which was not statistically significant (P-value 0.22).

DISCUSSION

ART has been developed for treatment of caries in those parts of the world with limited access to dental treatment facilities. However, hand instruments alone do not remove carious dentin as effectively as rotary burs, and cariogenic bacteria can survive incarceration under GIC restorations for up to 2 years. Research has shown that few ART restorations fail because of secondary caries over a period of 6 years.[13] However, annual clinical surveys found that secondary caries still remain as the main reason for GIC failure, indicating that the fluoride released from GIC is not sufficiently potent to inhibit bacterial growth or combat the effects of bacterial destruction. Although numerous efforts have been made to improve the antibacterial activity of dental restoratives, most of them have been focused on the release or the slow release of various incorporated low-molecular-weight antibacterial agents such as antibiotics, zinc ions, silver ions, iodine, and chlorhexidine.[13] However, incorporation of antibacterial agents can lead to a reduction in the mechanical properties of the restoration over time and possible toxicity to the surrounding tissues if the dose or release is not properly controlled.[13] Propolis is a raw material with a complex structure, and therefore cannot be used directly. Most common solvents used for commercial extraction are water, methanol, ethanol, chloroform, dichloromethane, ether, and acetone. Solvents remove the inert material and preserve the desired compounds based on the method of extraction. The different forms of propolis are ethanolic and lyophilized type. Since Ethanolic extract of propolis (EEP) is the most commonly used in literature hence ethanol extract was used.[14] The study of Troca et al.[8] showed that when lyophilized propolis was added to GIC, the manipulation was difficult; however, manipulating EEP was not difficult, and hence, EEP was used in this study. Troca et al.[8] proved by incorporating 2% of EEP in GIC had increase in the water sorption, thus affecting the physical properties of the cement. Studie done by Steinberg, Kaine and Gedalia have shown that 0.4% being the lowest concentration of propolis having antibacterial activity hence 1% EEP was used in study.[11]

There are several methods to detect microleakage, which include the use of dyes, chemical tracers, and radioactive tracers, scanning electron microscopy, neutron activation analysis, and fluid filtration. Dye leakage method is a simple, inexpensive, fast technique, the most frequently used method, and does not require the use of complex laboratory equipment. Hence, in this study, this method was used.[12] Rhodamine-B is an organic dye compounded by a red-violet powder, classified as a xanthene dye, and presents greater diffusion on human dentin than methylene blue; hence, in this study, this dye was used. Thermocycling was carried out in this study because it is a widely used method in dental research to simulate temperature changes that take place in the oral environment.[12]

Spanberg et al. and Goldman et al. have reported that entrapped air can inhibit the penetration of dye into the gap between the filling materials and dentinal walls when utilizing passive dye penetration. In the present study, dye penetration under vacuum was used because vacuum pressure decreases the volume of entrapped air and allows complete dye penetration.[13]

In the present study, samples were scored 0, 1, 2, and 3 based on the dye penetration. In the experimental group, three samples were scored 2 and 12 samples were scored 3. In the control group, one sample was scored 1, five samples were scored 2, nine samples were scored 3 [Table 1]. On comparison of microleakage scores, it was found that they were not statistically significant (P-value 0.22). In the present study, the control group showed a mean dye penetration of 2.5 ± 0.6, whereas the experimental group showed a mean dye penetration of 2.8 ± 0.4. The increase in microleakage could be because of polymerization shrinkage causing an area of tension in between teeth and restoration. In addition, there could be formation of microcracks at the margins, which led to increase in microleakage; however, it was not statistically significant.
