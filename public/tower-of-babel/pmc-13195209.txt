The Impact of Social Determinants of Health on Complications and Health Care Utilization After Tibial Tubercle Osteotomy
Authors: Joshua G Sanchez, Kevin Lehane, Emily Berzolla, Seongho Jeong, Laith M Jazrawi, Jonathan N Grauer, Alexander Golant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Social determinants of health disparities (SDHD), encompassing environmental, health care, social, educational, and economic factors, are known to increase the risk of complications after various orthopaedic procedures. However, their impact on tibial tubercle osteotomy (TTO) outcomes remains undefined.Hypothesis/Purpose:This study aimed to compare complication risks and health care utilization within 90 days and 1 year of primary TTO in patients with versus without SDHD. It was hypothesized that SDHD patients would show higher odds of health care utilization and complications.Study Design:Cohort study; Level of evidence, 3.Methods:Adult (>17 years) patients who underwent primary TTO from January 2010 to April 2023 were identified using the PearlDiver M170 database. The exclusion criteria included inactivity within 90 days after TTO or a history of neoplasm or infection within 90 days before the procedure. The overall cohort was divided into SDHD and non-SDHD groups. Patient characteristics—including age, sex, Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, tobacco or nicotine use, and substance use disorder—were abstracted. Patients without versus with SDHD were matched (2:1) based on all demographic variables. Moreover, 90-day and 1-year complications were identified and compared using the Pearson chi-square test and multivariable logistic regression, adjusted for demographic variables.Results:Of 11,419 overall patients, 1333 patients (11.7%) had SDHD. After matching, 736 SDHD and 1375 non-SDHD patients were analyzed. On multivariable analysis, SDHD was associated with increased odds of 90-day readmission (odds ratio [OR], 3.71 [95% CI, 2.22-6.37]), urinary tract infection (UTI) (OR, 3.11 [95% CI, 1.96-5.01]), emergency department (ED) visit (OR, 2.22 [95% CI, 1.78-2.76]), and venous thromboembolism (VTE) (OR, 1.85 [95% CI, 1-3.44]). At 1 year, SDHD patients demonstrated higher odds of revision TTO (OR, 8.10 [95% CI, 2.95-33.44]) and nonunion (OR, 2.05 [95% CI, 1.03-4.13]) (all P < 0.05).Conclusion:The present study found that SDHD patients were associated with increased odds of ED visits, readmissions, VTE, and UTI after primary TTO. Additionally, SDHD patients demonstrated higher odds of revision TTO and nonunion. Further research is needed to identify the root causes of these differences, enabling targeted strategies to reduce future outcome inequities associated with SDHD.Keywords: complications, disparity, social determinants of health, tibial tubercle osteotomy

Tibial tubercle osteotomy (TTO) is a surgical procedure designed to address various patellofemoral disorders, such as patellar instability, malalignment, and maltracking. This procedure seeks to realign the knee extensor mechanism by distalizing, anteriorizing, or medializing the tibial tubercle to reduce pain, decrease patellar dislocation risk, improve function, and prevent further cartilage damage.11,26

Although numerous studies have highlighted the safety and success of TTO, it is not without complications.16,22,36 The overall complication rate has been reported as high as 46%, ranging from minor adverse events, such as superficial infections and painful hardware, to major complications, such as tibial fracture, nonunion, and neurovascular complications. 26 Notably, the readmission rate for TTO is high,10,36 ranging from 4% to 20%, which illustrates the elevated postoperative health care utilization rate for these patients. It is therefore imperative to understand the factors that contribute to postoperative outcomes and health care utilization after TTO.

One emerging field of research implicated in patient outcomes is the impact of social determinants of health (SDoH), which encompass environmental, social, educational, and economic factors. These nonmedical factors can be as important, if not more important, than health care quality or lifestyle choices, with several studies suggesting that SDoH accounts for up to 55% of health outcomes. 35 Recent evidence has demonstrated the impact of social determinants of health on orthopaedic outcomes, ranging from rotator cuff repair to total knee arthroplasty.5,27,31 However, there is no literature reporting the impact of SDoH on TTO outcomes.

SDoH can impact postoperative outcomes after TTO in several ways. Social determinants of health disparities (SDHD), or inequalities in SDoH, are associated with several medical comorbidities, including obesity and diabetes, which negatively impact postoperative healing and result in increased complication rates.2,13 Additionally, the recovery and rehabilitation process after TTO can be heavily influenced by SDHD. Patients with better access to postoperative care, physical therapy, and social support are more likely to experience successful outcomes and quicker recoveries.7,23 Conversely, those lacking these resources may experience prolonged recovery periods, higher complication rates, and increased health care utilization. As such, the purpose of the present study was to compare complication risks and health care utilization within 90 days and 1 year of primary TTO in patients with versus without SDHD. We hypothesized that SDHD patients would show higher rates of health care utilization and overall postoperative complications.

Methods

Study Population

The present study is a retrospective matched-cohort study that used the M170 PearlDiver administrative database. The M170 dataset is a large United States insurance database that encompasses 170 million patients from January 1, 2010, to April 30, 2023. Because the output data are deidentified and aggregated, our institutional review board deemed studies using this dataset exempt from review.

Adult patients (at least 18 years old) undergoing primary TTO were identified using the first occurrence of the Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code 27418. The exclusion criteria included a coded history of infection or neoplasm within 90 days before the procedure or inactivity in the database within 90 days after surgery.

Patients meeting the inclusion criteria who also demonstrated a coded history of SDHD, encompassing environmental, health care, social, educational, and economic factors, were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 9th revision (ICD-9) and International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) diagnostic codes. All diagnostic codes utilized to identify SDHD were provided in Supplementary Table 1. Patients were then stratified into 2 cohorts: patients with or without SDHD before the index procedure.

Demographic Characteristics and Matching

Demographic factors were then abstracted, including age, sex, Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (ECI), a quantitative measure of comorbidity burden,20,37 preoperative obesity (defined as body mass index [BMI] ≥ 30 kg/m2), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), tobacco or nicotine use, substance use disorder (SUD), and SDHD category. Obesity, tobacco or nicotine use, and SUD were defined as a coded history of each comorbidity within 120 days before surgery, whereas T2DM was defined based on diagnoses within 2 years before surgery. Substance use disorder included alcohol, opioid, cannabis, sedative, hypnotic, anxiolytic, cocaine, stimulant, hallucinogen, inhalant, or other psychoactive substance use.

To account for baseline demographic differences, non-SDHD and SDHD cohorts were matched. As such, a 2-to-1 match was then completed between non-SDHD and SDHD cohorts based on all collected demographic variables.

Postoperative Complications

Ninety-day adverse events were identified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 coding, as previously described.12,14,15,28-30 Adverse events were analyzed individually and in aggregated groups, including any adverse events (AAEs), serious adverse events (SAEs), or minor adverse events (MAEs).

SAEs were defined as at least 1 occurrence of sepsis, cardiac events (defined as myocardial infarction or cardiac arrest), postoperative infection, or venous thromboembolism (VTE), defined as deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism. MAEs were defined as at least 1 occurrence of bleeding events (defined as transfusion or hematoma), acute kidney injury (AKI), wound dehiscence, urinary tract infection (UTI), or pneumonia. AAEs were defined as at least 1 occurrence of either a severe or minor adverse event.

For health care utilization events within 90 days postoperative, emergency department (ED) visits were identified by CPT coding, but were not included in the aggregated adverse events. Readmissions were identified based on the “ADMISSIONS” code within the PearlDiver interface, using methods previously described,6,30 and were also not included in the aggregated adverse events.

CPT, ICD-9, and ICD-10 codes were used to identify and calculate the incidence of 1-year postoperative complications. These events included nonunion, manipulation under anesthesia (MUA) of the knee, hardware removal, extensor mechanism injury (defined as patella fracture, patellar tendon rupture, or tibial tubercle fracture), and revision TTO. Revision TTO was identified using ipsilateral ICD diagnostic codes corresponding to both the index TTO procedure and a subsequent revision TTO. Notably, laterality-specific ICD diagnostic codes were available only with ICD-10 coding, which was implemented 19 in the United States in October 2015.

Statistical Analysis

Univariable analyses were performed to evaluate demographic differences between the SDHD and non-SDHD cohorts. Student's t tests were used to compare continuous variables—including age and ECI. Categorical variables—including demographic characteristics and the incidence of all analyzed adverse events—were compared using the Pearson chi-square tests.

Multivariable logistic regression was subsequently performed to determine independent odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs for adverse events in the SDHD cohort relative to the non-SDHD cohort, adjusted for patient age, sex, ECI, preoperative obesity, T2DM, tobacco or nicotine use, and SUD.

Statistical analyses were conducted using PearlDiver Bellwether software (PearlDiver) and GraphPad Prism Version 10.3.1 (GraphPad Software). Statistical significance was defined as P < .05 for all analyses. To protect patient privacy, PearlDiver does not report exact cell counts when fewer than 11 events are identified. Therefore, values were reported as “<11” where applicable.

Results

Study Cohort

Overall, 11,419 patients who underwent primary TTO and met the study criteria were identified. Of these, 1333 patients comprised the SDHD cohort, and 10,086 comprised the non-SDHD cohort. The non-SDHD cohort was slightly older (31.9 ± 11.8 vs 30.6 ± 12.1 years) and had a lower comorbidity burden (ECI, 1.9 ± 2.2 vs 4.2 ± 3.2). Both cohorts were predominantly women, with women comprising >75% of each group.

Compared with non-SDHD patients, the SDHD cohort was more frequently identified with preoperative obesity (12.5% vs 1.9%), T2DM (12.5% vs 4.9%), tobacco or nicotine use (15.6% vs 4.9%), and SUD (11.6% vs 3.6%). Among SDHD patients, economic (71.9%) and social (32.3%) disparities were the most commonly identified categories. All unadjusted significant comparisons demonstrated a P < .001.

After successful matching, 736 SDHD patients and 1375 non-SDHD patients remained, with no statistically significant differences in any demographic variable. All data are presented in Table 1.

Discussion

The major findings of our study demonstrate that SDHD patients were associated with increased odds of health care utilization, reflected by increased odds of 90-day readmissions (OR, 3.71 [95% CI, 2.22-6.37]) and ED visits (OR, 2.22 [95% CI, 1.78-2.76]). Additionally, SDHD patients experienced higher odds of 90-day medical complications, including VTE and UTI, as well as increased odds of revision TTO (OR, 8.10 [95% CI, 2.95-33.44]) and nonunion (OR, 2.05 [95% CI, 1.03-4.13]) within 1 year postoperatively. Notably, these associations persisted after rigorous matching between SDHD and non-SDHD cohorts and adjustment for age, sex, ECI, obesity, T2DM, tobacco or nicotine use, and substance use disorder, suggesting that SDHD independently contributes to elevated postoperative risk beyond traditional medical comorbidities.

In line with these data, Kiwinda et al 18 demonstrated that SDHD, as measured by the area deprivation index (ADI), a neighborhood deprivation index, was associated with an increased risk of 90-day hospital readmission after total joint arthroplasty. Furthermore, these authors also utilized a large national database of nearly 630,000 Medicare patients and adjusted for confounding patient factors (age, sex, ECI, and hospital characteristics). In another related study, Rahman et al 25 demonstrated that increased SDHD was associated with increased length of stay, ED visits, and readmissions, based on both the ADI and the social vulnerability index, another neighborhood deprivation index, in a large cohort of 19,321 patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty. 25 While these aforementioned studies did not analyze TTO, these data support the findings of the present study that demonstrated SDHD is an important risk factor for increased postoperative health care utilization after TTO, even after the consideration of preoperative medical patient factors that may be associated with SDHD. However, the mechanism for this increased risk remains difficult to define, given the limitations of an administrative database.

Importantly, the present study also demonstrated that SDHD was associated with increased odds of ipsilateral revision TTO. Previous studies have found a similar low incidence of revision TTO—including Miltenberg et al, 21 who found a revision fixation rate of 2.8% in a systematic review of 7836 patients, and Payne et al, 22 who demonstrated a 0.6% revision rate in a systematic review of 787 patients. Several factors may contribute to the increased incidence of revision TTO observed in the SDHD cohort. Notably, indications for surgery and concomitant procedures performed at the time of the index or subsequent TTO were not characterized in the present study. Additionally, patients with SDHD may face economic, time, or insurance-related barriers to consistent postoperative rehabilitation, which could contribute to suboptimal recovery and an increased risk of revision surgery. Overall, the association between SDHD and revision TTO represents an important consideration for surgeons when evaluating and counseling this patient population.

Finally, the SDHD cohort demonstrated higher odds of 90-day individual complications, including VTE and UTI, and aggregated adverse events, including AAEs and MAEs. While there is no literature on the risks of SDHD in TTO, the findings of the present study align with those of a previous study by Kamalapathy et al, 17 which evaluated the impact of SDHD on surgical outcomes after hip or ankle fractures. These authors demonstrated that education disparity was associated with increased risk of major medical complications, defined as pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular accident, and sepsis, within 90 days of both hip and ankle fracture surgery. Furthermore, Atrey et al 3 demonstrated that lower socioeconomic status was associated with increased revision surgery and surgical complications, defined as myocardial infarction, VTE, and pneumonia, within 90-days after total hip arthroplasty in a study of over 100,000 patients. As such, the findings of the present study are in line with previous literature and support elevated postoperative consideration of complication risks for this patient population.

Several potential factors could contribute to the increased complication risk and health care utilization observed in the SDHD cohort. In this study, the matched SDHD group primarily exhibited economic, social, and environmental disparities. Consequently, poorer outcomes may partly stem from nutritional discrepancies, as inadequate nutrition has been linked to delayed wound healing and increased infection risk.4,13,34 Another factor could be limited transportation access or inflexible schedules, creating challenges for attending follow-up visits or physical therapy, which may lead to greater reliance on the ED rather than clinic evaluations. 9 Finally, language or cultural differences might contribute to reduced comprehension of postoperative care and precautions, particularly among those less comfortable with English.1,8,24,32 Overall, these findings suggest that patients with SDHD may benefit from increased postoperative surveillance and enhanced education focused on recognizing symptoms that warrant immediate evaluation rather than routine clinic follow-up.

There are limitations inherent to the present study that should be noted. Firstly, data were obtained from a large national administrative database that is based on insurance claims. As such, more detailed variables, patient-specific factors, radiographical details, and intraoperative surgical details that are not coded within the database cannot be identified. Further, the data obtained in the present study relies on the validity and accuracy of the provider's administrative coding, which may not always be accurate. Second, the present study did not account for concomitant procedures associated with the index TTO, which may have contributed to the increased risk of certain complications for the SDHD cohort. Third, the indication for the subsequent readmission, ED visit, or revision TTO was not characterized. Next, identification of SDHD relied on ICD diagnostic codes, which have been shown to be underutilized and may underestimate the true prevalence of social disparities, potentially biasing results toward the null. 33 Finally, surgeon experience and previous case volume were not analyzed, which also could have differentially affected outcomes between the non-SDHD and SDHD cohorts.

Conclusion

The present study found that SDHD patients were associated with increased odds of ED visits, readmissions, VTE, and UTI after primary TTO. Additionally, SDHD patients demonstrated higher odds of revision TTO and nonunion. Further research is needed to identify the root causes of these differences, enabling targeted strategies to reduce future outcome inequities associated with SDHD.
