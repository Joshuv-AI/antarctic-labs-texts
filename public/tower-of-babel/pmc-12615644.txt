Association between modified Rankin Scale scores and fall risk in post-stroke rehabilitation inpatients: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Songhui Wu, Qingfang Zhang, Jie Yan, Jianjun Long, Dianrui Hou, Yulong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFalls are among the most common complications after stroke, potentially delaying functional recovery. Although the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) is widely used in stroke assessment, its relationship with fall risk remains poorly understood. The study aims to identify the association between mRS scores and fall risk in stroke patients. In this multicenter cross-sectional study, data on sociodemographics, clinical factors, fall characteristics, and mRS scores were collected via face-to-face interviews. Univariate analysis, binary logistic regression, and threshold effect models were employed to examine the association between fall risk and mRS scores. Among 6,192 enrolled patients, 524 (8.46%) experienced falls. The mRS showed a non-linear association with fall risk, peaking at mRS = 3 (P < 0.05). For scores < 3, each 1-point increase in mRS raised fall risk by 34% (OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.09– 1.60, P = 0.0046), whereas for scores > 3, each 1-point increase reduced risk by 26% (OR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.61–0.90, P = 0.0027). An inverted U-shaped relationship exists between mRS scores and fall risk, peaking at an mRS score of 3, identifying a potential priority group for fall prevention.Keywords: Stroke, Fall risk, Modified rankin scale, Functional impairment, Cross-sectional studySubject terms: Stroke, Risk factors

Introduction

Falls are a common and serious complication in stroke survivors, occurring in up to 73% of patients within the first year post-stroke1, and are associated with fractures, rehospitalization, functional decline, and loss of independence2,3. A recent retrospective cohort study of 2,518 stroke inpatients undergoing rehabilitation reported a fall incidence of 10.8 per 1,000 patient-days, with 27.9% of patients experiencing at least one fall during hospitalization4. Furthermore, a multicenter cross-sectional study in China found that among 741 older stroke patients who had fallen, 74.76% sustained injuries5. These findings underscore the high burden of post-stroke falls and highlight the need for early risk identification and targeted preventive strategies.

Several fall risk assessment tools are used in clinical practice, including the Morse Fall Scale (MFS)6, Berg Balance Scale (BBS)7, and Hendrich II Fall Risk Model8. However, they often require considerable time and staffing resources9, and general-purpose scales like the MFS do not adequately capture stroke-specific neurological impairments (e.g., unilateral neglect), which may lead to inaccurate fall risk identification10. Additionally, recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported significant heterogeneity in BBS studies predicting fall risk in stroke, further limiting its generalizability11,12.

Functional status has been explored as a predictor of falls in stroke patients, but findings are inconsistent due to differences in study design, sample characteristics, and assessment tools. For instance, a prospective cohort study found a linear inverse association between Functional Independence Measure (FIM) scores and fall risk in ambulatory stroke patients13. In contrast, a cross-sectional study involving non-bedridden stroke rehabilitation inpatients reported an inverted U-shaped association between Barthel Index scores and fall risk, which may be explained by mild-to-moderate deficits permitting mobility—and thus greater exposure to fall-prone activities—whereas severe deficits restrict mobility and exposure, reducing the observed fall incidence14. The modified Rankin Scale (mRS), a simple and widely used measure of functional disability in stroke15, may offer a pragmatic alternative for fall risk screening, particularly in settings where comprehensive assessment tools are not feasible. However, its predictive value for falls has not been fully explored.

The study aims to identify the association between mRS scores and fall risk in stroke patients. We hypothesize that higher mRS scores, indicating greater functional impairment, are associated with an increased risk of falls. This study seeks to provide a simple and practical screening tool for fall risk in clinical practice, thereby enhancing the efficiency of post-stroke care and improving patients’ quality of life.

Methods

Study design

This multicenter cross-sectional study was based on data from a national clinical research project titled “The Promotion of Application Research of Longshi Scale”, led by our research team16. The study protocol was approved by the local Ethics Committee (Approval No. 20180926006) and was registered with the China Clinical Trial Registration Center (ChiCTR-2000034067). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013)17.

Participants

Patients were consecutively recruited from the Departments of Rehabilitation Medicine at 103 hospitals across 23 provinces in China between December 2018 and May 2020. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age 18–85 years; (2) diagnosis of stroke according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10)18. The exclusion criteria were: (1) missing data on mRS scores or fall history; (2) cognitive or communication impairments that precluded effective assessment (Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score ≤ 24). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized representatives. The patient selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Flow diagram of the identified, in- and excluded patients at each stage.

Data collection

All data were obtained via face-to-face interviews conducted within 24 h of admission to the rehabilitation ward. Data were uploaded and securely stored using the Quicker Recovery Line (QRL) platform, an online rehabilitation evaluation and management system. The data collection was carried out by registered physicians or rehabilitation therapists who had received standardized training and passed an assessment. After verification, the data were securely stored in the cloud and managed by a dedicated data administrator.

Variables

Variables were selected based on previous studies and their clinical relevance to fall risk19,20. Sociodemographic variables included sex (male/female), age (in years), marital status (married/unmarried), living status (living alone vs. not), religious belief (yes/no), education level, retirement status (yes/no), and annual household income. Clinical variables included stroke type (ischemic or hemorrhagic), disease duration (in years since stroke onset), Longshi Scale classification (bedridden, domestic, or community group), history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, heart disease, and kidney disease (all recorded as yes/no). Additionally, data were collected on psychotropic medication use (yes/no), smoking status (yes/no), alcohol consumption (yes/no), and engagement in social activities (yes/no). Environmental accessibility was assessed by asking whether the participant’s residence had elevator access (yes/no).

Functional status assessment

During the questionnaire survey, the patients’ functional status was evaluated using the mRS, a validated measure of global disability ranging from 0 (no symptoms) to 6 (death)21. Assessments were conducted by trained clinicians following a standardized protocol to ensure interrater reliability.

Fall assessment

Fall history was obtained during the same admission interview and verified via medical records. A fall was defined as an unexpected event in which the participant comes to rest on the ground, floor, or lower level22. Only falls occurring within the three months prior to the interview, with all falls occurring post-stroke, were included in the analysis; falls that occurred before the stroke event were excluded23.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using EmpowerStats version 2.0 (http://www.empowerstats.com, X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, MA, USA)24 and R software (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation). Continuous variables with non-normal distributions (e.g., age and disease duration) were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR, Q1– Q3), and group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, and differences between groups were tested using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Given the minimal proportion of missing data (0%–6%), no imputation procedures were undertaken25. Standardized differences were calculated to evaluate covariate balance, with values < 10% indicating negligible imbalance.

To minimize overfitting, Model II included only variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analyses (Table 2), ensuring an events-per-variable ratio within recommended limits. Model assumptions were assessed prior to interpretation: the linearity of the logit for continuous predictors was evaluated using the Box–Tidwell test; influential observations were examined via Cook’s distance and leverage statistics; and variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated to assess multicollinearity, with all VIFs < 5.

Univariate results by binary logistic regression.

Univariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to explore associations between covariates and fall occurrence. Multivariate logistic regression models were subsequently applied to determine independent associations between mRS scores and the risk of post-stroke falls. To explore potential non-linear relationships, a two-piecewise linear regression model was fitted, with the knot location identified through a grid search across integer mRS values from 1 to 4. The optimal knot was defined as the value yielding the highest model likelihood, and the fit of the two-piecewise model was compared with a simple linear model using the log-likelihood ratio test. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 6,192 patients were included in the final statistical analysis. The mean age of participants was 66.00 years (IQR, 55.00–76.00), and 36.92% were female (n = 2,285). Among the participants, 8.46% (n = 524) had experienced at least one fall within the three months prior to data collection. The distribution of mRS scores was as follows: 96 participants (1.55%) scored 0, 565 (9.12%) scored 1, 509 (8.22%) scored 2, 1,088 (17.57%) scored 3, 2,764 (44.64%) scored 4, and 1,170 (18.90%) scored 5 (Table 1). Table 1 also presents the characteristics of the participants, including sociodemographic, clinical, and environmental variables, as well as the fall status. Several variables showed statistically significant differences between participants who experienced falls and those who did not (P < 0.05). Specifically, age (P = 0.340), stroke type (ischemic vs. hemorrhagic, P = 0.221), and history of hypertension (P = 0.268) were not significantly associated with fall occurrence. In contrast, disease duration (P < 0.001), marital status (P = 0.005), living status (P < 0.001), religious belief (P < 0.001), educational level (P < 0.001), household income (P < 0.001), mRS score (P < 0.001), Longshi Scale classification (P < 0.001), smoking (P < 0.001), alcohol consumption (P < 0.001), use of psychotropic medications (P < 0.001), and participation in social activities (P = 0.022) were significantly associated with fall occurrence. Furthermore, the presence of an elevator in the home (P = 0.033) also showed a statistically significant association with fall risk.

Univariate logistic regression results

Our univariate logistic regression analyses showed that, in unadjusted models, post-stroke falls were significantly associated with married (OR 1.47; 95% CI 1.12–1.93; P = 0.0055), living alone (OR 1.92; 95% CI 1.34–2.75; P = 0.0004), having religious beliefs (OR 1.74; 95% CI 1.31–2.32; P = 0.0002), lower educational attainment (all P < 0.001 for middle school, high school and college/university vs. primary or below), higher household income categories (all P < 0.05 vs. < 50,000 CNY), longer disease duration (OR 1.03; 95% CI 1.01–1.05; P = 0.0029), Longshi Scale classification (domestic vs. bedridden: OR 1.83; 95% CI 1.50–2.24; P < 0.0001), presence of hyperlipidemia (OR 1.39; 95% CI 1.06–1.81; P = 0.0162), smoking (OR 1.55; 95% CI 1.28–1.88; P < 0.0001), alcohol consumption (OR 1.68; 95% CI 1.31–2.16; P < 0.0001), psychotropic medication use (OR 2.44; 95% CI 1.79–3.32; P < 0.0001), participation in social activities (OR 0.81; 95% CI 0.67–0.97; P = 0.0227), and home elevator access (OR 0.81; 95% CI 0.67–0.98; P = 0.0334) (Table 2).

Multivariate analyses by binary logistic regression

Multivariable logistic regression analyses revealed that the association between mRS scores and post-stroke falls was not statistically significant after adjusting for potential confounders. Specifically, when adjusting only for age and sex (Model I, Table 3), none of the mRS categories (scores 1–5) showed a significant association with fall risk compared to mRS 0 (all P > 0.05). This non-significant association persisted after further adjustment for all covariates with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis presented in Table 2, with all adjusted odds ratios remaining non-significant (P > 0.05 for all comparisons) (Model II, Table 3).

Results of threshold effect analysis

After further adjustment for all covariates with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis presented in Table 2, a threshold effect was observed in the association between mRS scores and post-stroke fall risk. Using a two-piecewise linear regression model, we identified an inflection point at an mRS score of 3. Below this threshold (mRS < 3), higher mRS scores were significantly associated with an increased risk of falls (OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.09–1.60, P = 0.0046), whereas above the threshold (mRS > 3), higher mRS scores were associated with a decreased risk of falls (OR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.61–0.90, P = 0.0027). The log-likelihood ratio test comparing the two-piecewise and standard linear regression models was statistically significant (P < 0.001), indicating that the threshold model provided a better fit to the data (Table 4).

Discussion

In this multicenter cross-sectional study involving 6,192 hospitalized stroke patients undergoing rehabilitation, we found that 8.46% experienced a fall. This incidence was lower than the 16% reported by Eikenberry et al. in an inpatient rehabilitation cohort of acute stroke patients26, yet was nearly identical to the 8.5% (428/5,065) reported by Persson et al. in a large Swedish register-based study27. Furthermore, we observed that the risk of falling peaked in those with moderate disability (mRS score = 3), compared to both less and more severely disabled patients. This finding provides new evidence for a non-linear, inverted U-shaped relationship between functional status and fall risk among stroke patients in the inpatient rehabilitation setting.

Falls are a common complication that can significantly impede the progress of functional recovery in stroke survivors28. The observed inverted U-shaped pattern may reflect the interaction between patients’ physical capabilities and their exposure to fall-related activities. Patients with low mRS scores (indicating better functional status) generally retain good balance, coordination, and motor control, which protect them from falls29. Those with high mRS scores (indicating poorer functional status) often suffer severe mobility restrictions, limiting their exposure to fall-prone situations despite their impaired balance. Patients with moderate disability, however, maintain a certain level of mobility but still experience significant balance impairments and postural control deficits, increasing their susceptibility to fall30. These findings highlight the need to tailor fall prevention strategies specifically for stroke patients with moderate disability.

Few studies have explored the association between mRS scores and post-stroke falls. Callaly et al.31 reported that stroke survivors with mild-to-moderate disability (mRS 2–3) had a higher risk of falling than those with no disability (mRS 0–1) or severe disability (mRS 4–5). Similarly, Wei et al.20 identified an inverted U-shaped association in a national cohort of ischemic stroke patients, where fall risk peaked at an mRS score of 2. Interestingly, our findings indicate a peak at mRS = 3, which may reflect differences in study populations, stroke types, and fall definitions. Unlike Wei et al., we included both ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes and adjusted for a broader range of confounders. Additionally, our fall assessment captured all falls—including non-injury-related events—whereas their study focused only on injury-related falls requiring emergency or hospital care.

Other functional assessments have revealed similar non-linear associations. Kato et al.32 demonstrated that patients with intermediate transfer ability (FIM score 4–6) had higher fall rates than those with either very low or high transfer ability. Likewise, Yan et al.14 observed a peak in fall risk at a Barthel Index score of approximately 60 in non-bedridden stroke patients. These studies support our findings that patients with moderate functional impairment may face the greatest fall risk. Our study extends this evidence by identifying a similar non-linear trend using the mRS in a diverse hospitalized stroke patients undergoing rehabilitation, highlighting the need for targeted interventions in this high-risk group.

By contrast, some studies have reported a linear association between functional independence and fall risk. For instance, Wei et al.13 found that each one-point increase in FIM score was associated with a 10% reduction in fall risk among non-bedridden stroke survivors. Similarly, Cho et al.33 observed a moderate linear correlation between modified Barthel Index scores and fall risk. These discrepancies may arise from differences in study populations, assessment tools, or the assumption of linearity in statistical models. The use of linear models may obscure underlying non-linear relationships between global disability and fall risk.

Our findings have important clinical implications. Fall prevention strategies should be individualized across the spectrum of functional ability. Stroke survivors with moderate disability represent a particularly vulnerable group and may benefit most from tailored interventions aimed at reducing fall risk and optimizing recovery outcomes. Future research should investigate the effectiveness of fall prevention programs specifically designed for this subgroup.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, fall events were self-reported, which may introduce recall bias and fail to capture fatal falls occurring prior to enrollment, potentially leading to survivor bias. While medical records provide a useful supplement to interview-based fall history, the possibility of underreporting fall events remains. Third, although mRS scores were assessed during inpatient rehabilitation, they may not fully reflect patients’ functional status in community settings, and the mRS itself may be subject to floor and ceiling effects, potentially limiting its sensitivity in detecting small changes at the extremes of disability. Finally, despite adjusting for multiple covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured factors may still have influenced our findings. Nonetheless, the large multicenter sample, rigorous assessment procedures, and comprehensive covariate adjustment strengthen the validity and generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

Our study identified an inverted U-shaped relationship between mRS scores and fall risk in stroke patients undergoing inpatient rehabilitation, with the highest risk observed at moderate disability (mRS = 3). While these findings highlight a potential priority group for fall prevention, further prospective research is needed to confirm the association before clinical implementation.
