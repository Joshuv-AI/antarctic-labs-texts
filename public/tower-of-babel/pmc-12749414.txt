Selected nutritional properties of Ganoderma lucidum and substrate degradation during multi-cycle reuse with hazelnut branch waste
Authors: Caglar Akcay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, the effects of reusing spent Reishi (Ganoderma lucidum) (Curtis) P. Karst mushroom substrate (SMS) from cultivation—by mixing it with hazelnut branch-based substrate (HB) at different ratios (HB100%, HB75%:SMS25%, HB50%:SMS50%, HB25%:SMS75%, SMS100%)—on the yield, quality, and nutritional composition of mushrooms were investigated. Yield performance, ash contents, and mineral composition of the harvested mushrooms were evaluated. In addition, Total Organic Carbon (TOC), Total Organic Matter (TOM), Nitrogen (N), C/N and Ash contents of the substrates used were determined. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) and Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) were also conducted to assess structural and thermal changes in the substrates during successive cultivation cycles. Results indicated that the highest biological efficiency (8.32%) and total yield (37.5 g/kg substrate) were observed in the SMS100% and SMS25%:HB75% substrates, respectively. A 29.9% increase in yield was observed when comparing 100% SMS with 100% HB. Mineral contents in mushrooms produced with SMS 100% generally increased compared to HB 100%, except for Cu. A 23.03% decrease was observed in the Cu content when the substrate was changed from 100% HB to 100% SMS. The total phenolic contents of the mushrooms cultivated were found to differ significantly among the substrates. FTIR analysis confirmed structural changes in the substrates, particularly indicating partial hemicellulose degradation and relative lignin enrichment after mushroom cultivation. TGA results showed that spent Ganoderma lucidum substrates exhibited higher thermal stability than raw hazelnut branch substrate (HB), supporting their reuse potential in circular bio-based applications. This study clearly showed that spent Ganoderma lucidum substrate-based HB can be successfully reutilized in new cultivation cycles, offering an economically and environmentally sustainable approach for mushroom production systems.Keywords: Waste recycling, Spent Mushroom Substrate (SMS), Ganoderma lucidum, FT-IR, TGA, Mushroom cultivation

Introduction

In the event of a large-scale nuclear conflict, the associated risks would extend far beyond the immediate effects of blast and radiation. The massive explosions are expected to inject vast quantities of smoke, soot, and particulate matter into the stratosphere, significantly reducing the amount of solar radiation reaching the Earth’s surface. This phenomenon, known as nuclear winter, would likely result in extended periods of diminished sunlight, severely impairing conventional agricultural systems and potentially leading to global food shortages1,2. Under such extreme conditions, food sources that require minimal or no sunlight to grow would assume critical importance3. Among these, cultivated mushrooms, which can be produced in controlled environments without reliance on sunlight, represent a highly viable and resilient nutritional resource. Moreover, in a nuclear winter scenario, access to raw materials may be severely limited. Consequently, it may become necessary to reuse existing substrates in mushroom cultivation to ensure continued food production under resource-constrained conditions4.

Global production of mushrooms and truffles rose significantly from 42.9 million tons in 2020 to 48.3 million tons in 20225, largely fueled by increasing consumer interest in health-promoting foods. As a rich source of protein, dietary fiber, essential vitamins, and minerals, edible mushrooms have gained popularity as a nutritious dietary option. Moreover, they are known to contain diverse bioactive compounds with notable antioxidant, anti-cancer, and anti-diabetic effects6. Mushroom cultivation is an environmentally sustainable practice that efficiently utilizes organic waste materials as a growing substrate. However, the disposal of spent mushroom substrate (SMS) remains a challenge in large-scale production systems. It was reported that 5 kg of SMS is generated for each kg of fresh mushroom after mushroom harvesting7,8. Due to the global expansion of mushroom cultivation, approximately 242 million tons of SMS are generated annually worldwide in 20225. SMS composed of partially degraded organic materials, still contains significant nutrients and can be reutilized in several ways. It has potential applications in areas such as animal feed, soil amendment, bioenergy generation, wastewater treatment and mushroom re-composting. The reuse of SMS has emerged as a potential approach to improve sustainability in mushroom farming. This approach not only minimizes waste but also improves substrate quality and overall productivity, making it an essential component of modern circular agricultural systems9.

Reuse involves the biological degradation and transformation of used substrates into nutrient-rich material that can be reintegrated into the mushroom production cycle. This process enhances substrate porosity, improves water retention capacity, and boosts microbial activity, thereby creating optimal conditions for fungal growth10,11. By applying microbial inoculation, controlled aeration, and moisture management, the nutrient profile of the substrate can be revitalized, making it suitable for subsequent cultivation cycles. Research has shown that reuse can enhance substrate efficiency, reduce production costs, and contribute to circular economy principles by closing the loop on organic waste utilization12,13. Additionally, studies indicate that properly re-used substrates can improve the yield and biological efficiency of cultivated mushrooms, reducing the dependency on fresh raw materials14.

Annually, the Black Sea region of Turkey generates approximately 1.7 million tons of hazelnut branch waste, a significant byproduct of the region’s extensive hazelnut production, which can be utilized for various sustainable applications such as biomass energy and organic farming15,16. Among various lignocellulosic wastes, hazelnut branch represents an underutilized biomass source, especially abundant in hazelnut-producing regions. Despite their rich cellulose and hemicellulose content, these residues are often discarded or incinerated, contributing to environmental burden. Exploring their potential as a substrate component for fungal cultivation offers a sustainable valorization pathway17. Previous studies have shown that G. lucidum can be cultivated successfully on a wide range of agricultural residues, including wheat straw, rice bran, corn cobs, and sawdust, with substrate composition significantly affecting yield and quality18,19. Research has also examined the reuse of SMS, particularly in species such as Pleurotus, Lentinus edodes and Agaricus, indicating that SMS can be partially recycled to support new cultivation cycles10. However, very few studies have investigated the recycling of spent G. lucidum substrates, especially in combination with woody residues like HB. Furthermore, analytical techniques such as Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) and Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) have been widely used to characterize the structural and compositional changes in substrate composting and SMS20,21. Yet, there is limited research linking these physicochemical changes with the nutritional composition of the fruiting bodies. This study focuses on the reuse of spent G. lucidum substrates containing hazelnut branch biomass, aiming to evaluate their physicochemical properties and suitability for circular bioresource applications.

In this study, hazelnut branch waste was used for the first time as substrate components in the reuse process of G. lucidum cultivation. These residues, rich in lignocellulosic compounds, represent a promising alternative to conventional substrates. Their incorporation into the re-composting process not only contributes to the valorization of agricultural by-products but also improves substrate structure and nutrient availability for mushroom growth22,23. Moreover, these materials were analyzed for the first time using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) and Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) to evaluate their thermal stability and decomposition behavior during their reuse in mushroom cultivation.

Materials and methods

Fungal strain and inoculum preparation

Ganoderma lucidum (Curtis) P. Karst spawn was obtained from the Republic of Türkiye Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry, Atatürk Horticultural Central Research Institute (Yalova, Türkiye). Pure mycelial cultures were maintained on potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates at 25 °C. For spawn preparation, sterilized wheat grains (moisture 60–65%) were inoculated with actively growing mycelium and incubated at 25 °C for 20–25 days until complete colonization. The fully colonized grain spawn was then used as the inoculum source for substrate bags.

Preparation of compost materials and mushroom cultivation

In this study, different substrate mixtures were prepared to assess the impact of reusing spent compost materials on mushroom cultivation. The substrates included hazelnut branch (HB) and spent G. lucidum mushroom substrate (SMS). The HB compost was prepared using fresh, non-degraded hazelnut branches as the base material. Hazelnut branches were collected from local farmers in Düzce, Türkiye, with prior permission obtained for the use of HB waste in this study. The HB materials were ground into small pieces of approximately 1–3 cm in length and were mixed with water to adjust the moisture content to approximately 65%, the optimal moisture level for fungal growth. 1% gypsum was added to adjust pH (final pH of HB: 7.01, pH of SMS was 6.5). The material was then sterilized at 121 °C under 1.1 atm pressure for 90 min by steaming to eliminate any microbial contamination in an autoclave. After cooling, the materials were transferred to 1 kg compost bags, and Ganoderma lucidum (Curtis) P. Karst mycelium was inoculated into each compost at a rate of 3% relative to the wet compost.

Similarly, the spent Ganoderma lucidum mushroom substrate (SMS) was obtained after completing the first cultivation cycle. G. lucidum mushrooms were initially cultivated on the primary compost, and following harvest, the remaining substrate was collected, dried, and sterilized for reuse. The SMS was then utilized either as a sole substrate (100%) or blended with hazelnut branch (HB) material at different mixing ratios, as presented in Table 1.

Cultivation conditions

The prepared compost blocks were transferred into an incubation room with a controlled dark environment. During the initial incubation period, the room was maintained at a temperature of 24 ± 2 °C and a relative humidity of approximately 70%. After full colonization of the substrate by G. lucidum mycelium was achieved, the temperature was increased to 28–30 °C and the relative humidity was raised to 80–90% to promote fruiting body development. Upon the appearance of pins, low-intensity light (approximately 50–60 lx) was introduced into the incubation chamber to support the development of mature fruiting bodies.

Mature fruiting bodies were carefully harvested and weighed using a precision digital scale. Both fresh and dried samples were preserved under appropriate storage conditions for subsequent chemical and nutritional analyses. Substrate composition

The hazelnut branch (HB) substrate primarily consists of lignocellulosic components. According to our previously published study, HB typically contains 64.76% holo-cellulose, 47.83% α –cellulose, and 35.24% theroic lignin (acid-insoluble)20. These structural polymers play a key role in fungal colonization and degradation. In this study, no additional chemical pretreatment was applied to alter their natural composition before use in G. lucidum cultivation.

Determination of mineral elements in G. lucidum fruiting bodies

The elemental profiles of the mushroom fruiting body samples were determined via Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES), following the ISO 22,036 protocol designed for solid environmental matrices. Instrument calibration was conducted using traceable certified reference materials (Certipur, Merck, Germany) to ensure analytical accuracy. Prior to analysis, samples were subjected to closed-vessel microwave digestion using a mixture of concentrated 10 mL nitric acid (HNO₃) and 2 mL hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), ensuring complete mineralization of the organic matrix.

Physicochemical properties of substrates

Ash, total organic matter (TOM) and total organic carbon (TOC) content determination

Ash content was determined by dry ashing the samples in a muffle furnace. Approximately 5 g of oven-dried sample was placed in pre-weighed porcelain crucibles and combusted at 550 °C for 4 h. After cooling in a desiccator, crucibles were reweighed to calculate ash content. The percentage of ash was calculated as the residue remaining after combustion relative to the initial dry sample weight.

Total organic matter was determined by subtracting ash content from 100% (TOM = 100 – ash %). TOC was calculated by dividing the TOM value by a conversion factor of 1.72.

Total phenolic contents of the cultivated mushrooms

The total phenolic content of G. lucidum fruit bodies cultivated from HB and SMS was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method. First, 0.5 g of dried mushroom powder was mixed with 10 mL methanol and filtered. Then, 800 µL Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and 800 µL Na2CO3 solutions were added. After that, the solution was kept in the dark for 30 min, and absorbance values were measured at 720 nm using a spectrophotometer. The results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalent per gram of sample (mg GAE/g).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) Analysis

FTIR spectroscopy was performed to characterize the structural changes in fresh hazelnut branch (HB) and spent mushroom substrates (SMS1, SMS2) using a Shimadzu IRTracer-100 Spectrum equipped with an ATR accessory (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Substrate samples were dried at 60 °C for 48 h and ground to < 1 mm particle size prior to analysis. Spectra were recorded in the range of 400–1800 cm⁻1 (fingerprint region) with 32 scans at 4 cm⁻1 resolution.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed to evaluate the thermal degradation behavior of the raw hazelnut branch substrate (HB) and the first (SMS1) and second-cycle (SMS2) spent G. lucidum mushroom substrates. Approximately 10 ± 0.5 mg of finely ground and oven-dried substrate samples (< 1 mm particle size) were analyzed using a thermogravimetric analyzer. The measurements were carried out under a constant nitrogen atmosphere (flow rate: 20 mL/min) to prevent oxidative degradation. The samples were heated from room temperature to 800 °C at a constant heating rate of 10 °C/min. The weight loss (%) was recorded as a function of temperature to determine decomposition stages related to moisture release, polysaccharide degradation (hemicellulose and cellulose), and lignin decomposition.

Statistical analysis

At least three independent substrate batches were prepared for each substrate type; both the substrates and the mushrooms grown on these substrates were analyzed separately. All experimental data were subjected to statistical analysis using SPSS software (Version 17, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to assess the effects of different substrate formulations on mushroom yield, biological efficiency, spawn run time, first harvest time, dry matter, and mineral contents. When significant differences (p < 0.05) were detected, means were compared using Tukey’s) post hoc test.

Results and discussion

Spawn run time, first primordium, earliness, yield, biological efficiency (BE) and dry matter

Spawn run time, time to first primordium, time to first harvest, yield, biological efficiency (BE), and dry matter content are presented in Table 2. The results showed significant differences in spawn run times among the substrates (p < 0.005). The 100% HB substrate showed the shortest spawn run time, which was significantly different from all other substrates. Compared to the 100% HB control, spawn run time increased by approximately 34.6% in the 100% SMS substrate (9.80 days) and by up to 40.1% in the 50% SMS + 50% HB and 75% SMS + 25% HB mixtures (10.2 days). In contrast, no significant differences were observed either in the time to first primordium formation or the time to first harvest (p > 0.005). However, substrate composition had a significant effect on yield (p < 0.005). The lowest yield (20.09 g/kg) was recorded for the 75% SMS + 25% HB mixture, whereas the highest yield was obtained from the 25% SMS + 75% HB mixture. However, the yield from the 25% SMS + 75% HB substrate was not statistically different from that of the 100% HB and 100% SMS substrates. A comparison of the pure substrates showed that the yield from 100% SMS was 29.9% higher than that from 100% HB. Conversely, the 75% SMS + 25% HB mixture yielded 28.9% less than the 100% HB substrate. Regarding Biological Efficiency (BE), the 100% SMS substrate resulted in the highest BE value (8.32%), representing a 47.3% increase over the 100% HB substrate (5.65%). The 25% SMS + 75% HB mixture also showed a high BE of 7.50%, which is a 32.7% increase over the HB control. In contrast, the 50% SMS + 50% HB and 75% SMS + 25% HB mixtures exhibited lower BE, with decreases of 14.0% and 29.0%, respectively, compared to 100% HB. Finally, for dry matter content, the highest value was achieved in mushrooms from the 100% SMS substrate, which was significantly different from that of the 100% HB substrate and all other mixtures. This represents a 10.2% increase in dry matter when using spent substrate alone compared to fresh hazelnut branches.

An evaluation of yield values revealed that the 75% SMS + 25% HB substrate had the lowest yield, while the 25% SMS + 75% HB substrate had the highest. These findings align well with previous studies. The literature suggests that a higher proportion of lignocellulosic-rich fresh substrate enhances mycelial growth and, consequently, increases biological yield24. However, the fact that 100% HB and 100% SMS substrates also provided high yields indicates that the effect of substrate ratios on mushroom yield is complex and directly influenced by the specific composition and nutrient profile of the materials used. In conclusion, spent G. lucidum substrate can be effectively reused in mushroom cultivation, as it retains essential nutrients and organic matter that support fungal development. This approach not only supports a circular economy by reducing waste but also enhances resource efficiency in agricultural systems.

In terms of biological efficiency (BE), the highest value was observed in the 100% SMS substrate. This finding is consistent with Zhang et al.14, who highlighted that substrate composition and structural properties significantly impact mycelial colonization and nutrient absorption. A key factor contributing to this high BE is the pre-degradation of lignin in the spent substrate, which facilitates easier access to cellulose and thereby enhances the conversion efficiency into fungal biomass. The authors emphasized that such pre-degraded lignocellulosic components improve nutrient availability, leading to increased biological efficiency in mushroom cultivation.

The highest dry matter content in mushrooms was obtained from the 100% SMS substrate. This finding is in good agreement with the study conducted by Koutrotsios et al.25, who reported that the dry matter content of mushrooms varies depending on the cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin composition of the substrate. They noted that harder woody materials generally produce mushrooms with higher dry matter content. Overall, the results of the present study confirm the influence of substrate composition on mycelial growth, yield, biological efficiency, and the nutritional quality of mushrooms, which is consistent with the existing literature.

Mineral contents of G. lucidum mushrooms

Table 3 shows the mineral contents of G. lucidum mushrooms cultivated from different substrates and SMS. According to Table 3, significant differences were observed particularly in the levels of copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), magnesium (Mg), and potassium (K). The highest Cu content was found in the 100% HB group (33.99 ± 0.1 mg/kg), which was significantly higher than all other treatments. This indicates that freshly prepared HB compost is a richer source of copper, whereas the SMS material likely had most of its Cu depleted during the first cultivation cycle. When the substrate was changed from 100% HB to 100% SMS, a 23.03% decrease in Cu content was observed. Interestingly, Zn and Mg levels were found to be highest in the 25% SMS + 75% HB mixture. The Zn content in this mixture (10.00 mg/kg) was 33.7% higher than in the 100% HB substrate (7.48 mg/kg). Similarly, the Mg content in the 25% SMS + 75% HB mixture (677.3 mg/kg) was 15.1% higher than in the 100% HB substrate (588.5 mg/kg). This suggests that although SMS has been previously used, it still retains certain mineral elements and may exhibit a synergistic effect when mixed with HB in balanced proportions. As for K and P, which are vital macronutrients, the highest values were also detected in the 25% SMS + 75% HB group. A clear decline in these nutrients was observed with increasing SMS proportions beyond this point, For instance, the 75% SMS + 25% HB mixture showed a 23.9% decrease in K and a 5.1% decrease in P compared to the 100% HB substrate, indicating that excessive reliance on spent compost can reduce the nutritional quality of the final mushroom product.

The mineral composition of mushrooms is highly influenced by the substrate formulation, particularly by the availability and bioaccessibility of essential elements within the growing medium26. In the present study, significant variations in mineral content were observed among mushrooms cultivated on different combinations of hazelnut branch compost (HB) and spent G. lucidum mushroom substrate compost (SMS), suggesting that both the freshness and the prior usage history of compost play a critical role in nutrient uptake.

The markedly higher copper (Cu) content in mushrooms grown on 100% HB substrate is consistent with findings by Jasinska et al.27, who reported that fresh lignocellulosic materials tend to have higher trace metal availability compared to previously utilized compost. The depletion of Cu in the SMS likely results from prior fungal metabolism during the initial G. lucidum cultivation cycle, which significantly alters the nutrient composition of the compost28.

Interestingly, the highest concentrations of zinc (Zn) and magnesium (Mg) were observed in the 25% SMS + 75% HB mixture. This may be attributed to a synergistic effect arising from the residual bioavailable minerals in SMS combined with the nutrient-rich HB base. Similar observations were reported by Lou et al.29, who demonstrated that partial substitution of primary compost with spent mushroom substrate can enhance the mineral content of Pleurotus ostreatus and Pleurotus eryngii through improved microbial activity and substrate aeration.

A noteworthy trend was also observed for potassium (K) and phosphorus (P), with the 25% SMS + 75% HB compost providing the highest levels of both elements. Similar results were also found by Hoa et al.30, who emphasized that moderate inclusion of spent mushroom substrate can maintain or even boost macronutrient availability when paired with a fresh, lignin-rich substrate. However, as the SMS proportion increased beyond 50%, a decrease in K and P content was observed, highlighting the limitations of using heavily exhausted compost without nutrient supplementation.

Peksen and Yakupoglu31 reported that the copper content of G. lucidum cultivated on different substrates ranged from 8.87 to 39.27 mg/kg on a dry weight basis. In the present study, the copper concentrations measured in G. lucidum samples grown on various SMS + HB mixtures were found to be between 24.01 and 33.99 mg/kg. These values are in good agreement with those reported by Peksen and Yakupoglu31. When compared to the study conducted by Peksen and Yakupoglu31, the mineral contents of G. lucidum obtained in the present study were at the lower end of the reported range for zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), and potassium (K), whereas copper (Cu) and calcium (Ca) values were found to be at similar levels and consistent. In contrast, the magnesium (Mg) content (ranging from 497.1 to 577.3 mg/kg) was considerably higher than that reported in the aforementioned study (63.62–119.84 mg/kg), indicating that the hazelnut branch (HB) substrate is a rich source of this mineral.

Total phenolic content of G. lucidum mushrooms

The results of this study show that the total phenolic content of G. lucidum mushrooms varies depending on the compost type used (Fig. 1). The 75% SMS + 25% HB mixture exhibited the highest phenolic content (1.23 mg GAE/g), which was 50.0% higher than the phenolic content found in the 100% HB substrate (0.82 mg GAE/g,) indicating a potential for increased health benefits. However, the 25% SMS + 75% HB mixture had a lower phenolic content (0.82 mg GAE/g), suggesting that phenolic production can be influenced by the compost composition. In the literature, G. lucidum mushrooms are generally reported to have higher phenolic content levels compared to this study. However, the results obtained in this study are consistent with those found in the study conducted by Akcay et al.17.

Total organic carbon (TOC), Total organic matter (TOM), Nitrogen, C/N and Ash content of the substrates

Table 4 shows total organic carbon (TOC), total organic matter, nitrogen (N) and ash content of the recycled substrates. The analysis revealed significant differences in the total organic carbon (TOC), nitrogen (N), C/N and ash contents among the various recycled substrates. The highest TOC content was observed in the hazelnut branch (HB) sample at 56.6%, followed by 100% SMS-1, 25% SMS-1 + 75% HB, 50% SMS-1 + 50% HB, and 75% SMS-1 + 25% HB mixtures. The lowest TOC value was recorded in the 100% SMS-2 sample at 52% representing an 8.1% decrease compared to the raw HB substrate. In terms of nitrogen content, the highest value was found in the 75% SMS-1 + 25% HB mixture (1.41%) which was 24.8% higher than that of the 100% HB substrate (1.13%). The lowest was in the 100% SMS-1 substrate (0.98%), a 13.3% decrease compared to HB. Overall, nitrogen content tended to increase with the addition of spent mushroom substrate (SMS). Regarding ash content, the lowest percentage was recorded in the HB sample (2.62%), whereas the highest was observed in the 100% SMS-2 substrate (10.5%). This indicates a dramatic 301% increase in ash content after two consecutive cultivation cycles on the same substrate, highlighting the accumulation of inorganic minerals.

The total organic matter (TOM) content of the substrates showed a decreasing trend with increasing spent mushroom substrate (SMS) ratio. The highest TOM value was observed in the raw hazelnut branch (HB) substrate (97.3%), while the lowest was recorded in 100% SMS-2 (89.47%) marking an 8.0% reduction. This gradual reduction in TOM indicates the progressive degradation of organic components during mushroom cultivation cycles. The reduction in TOM can be attributed primarily to the fungal breakdown of hemicellulose and cellulose, which serve as key carbon sources for G. lucidum32. As the cultivation cycles proceed, enzymatic activity leads to a depletion of these components, reflected in the lower TOM values. Notably, the TOM content of 100% SMS-1 (96.44%) was only slightly lower than that of HB, suggesting that the first cultivation cycle causes limited degradation. However, the second cycle (SMS-2) caused a more substantial decrease in organic matter, confirming a cumulative effect. The TOM values of the mixed substrates (SMS + HB) were found to be intermediate between the pure substrates, supporting the hypothesis that blending spent substrate with fresh lignocellulosic material can balance the nutrient availability and maintain adequate organic matter levels.

FTIR and thermal assessments of the substrates

FTIR spectroscopy was employed to investigate the structural changes in hazelnut branch-based lignocellulosic biomass during G. lucidum cultivation and subsequent compost reuse (Fig. 2). The characteristic peaks that exhibited significant changes after the initial biomass (HB), the spent mushroom substrate after the first cultivation (SMS-1), and after the second reuse cycle (SMS-2) were examined.

FTIR spectra of HB, SMS-1 and SMS-2.

The broad band observed at 3330 cm−1 is attributed to O–H stretching vibrations associated with both hydrogen bonding in the lignocellulosic matrix and hydroxyl groups of water molecules, while the peaks at 2920 and 2850 cm−1 are assigned to the C–H stretching of aliphatic acids33. The weak band at 1729 cm−1 is assigned to C = O stretching vibrations of hemicellulose, whereas the strong band at 1622 cm−1 corresponds to C = C and C = O stretching vibrations of lignin. The same band is also attributed to C = O stretching vibrations in peptide bonds of proteins originating from fungal hyphae present in SMS-1 and SMS-234. The weak band levels at 1460 and 1420 cm−1 are respectively assigned to C = C stretching vibrations of aromatic rings in lignin and cellulose and to C–O stretching vibrations of aryl–alkyl (O–CH3) ethers in lignin35. The C–H deformations of cellulose and hemicellulose structures and CH2 in-plane bending of crystalline cellulose are attributed to the 1360 cm−1 and 1320 cm−1 bands, respectively, while the C–O vibrations of hemicellulose and lignin are attributed to the 1230 cm−1 band36. The strong band at 1030 cm−1 is one of the characteristic peaks of the lignocellulosic structure and is attributed to C–O stretching and C–OH bending vibrations of cellulose37. The broad bands in the range of 700–400 cm−1 are attributed to C–H bending, aliphatic C–H stretching, and inorganic groups (e.g., Si–O vibrations) [38).

Our findings provide strong evidence that spent mushroom substrate (SMS-1) can be effectively reused for mushroom cultivation under controlled conditions. The first cultivation cycle resulted in complete hemicellulose degradation (disappearance of the C = O band at 1729 cm−1), oxidative modification of lignin (increased band intensity at 1622 cm−1 corresponding to C = C and C = O stretching vibrations, alongside the reduction of guaiacyl units), partial preservation of the cellulose structure (C–O stretching and C–OH bending vibrations at 1030 cm−1), and an increase in the presence of inorganic groups (greater band intensity in the 700–400 cm−1 range). During the second cultivation cycle, the cellulose structure was again preserved, while the other effects were partially diminished. In both cultivation cycles, the variations in band intensities were associated with the oxidative action of white-rot fungi, which decompose the cell wall components of the lignocellulosic matrix, leading to the emergence of new functional groups39. Especially, the increased band intensity in the 700–400 cm−1 range indicates that fungal degradation of the biomass results in an increase in fixed carbon content. This observation is consistent with the TGA results (Fig. 3), which demonstrate an increase in ash content with successive cultivation cycles.

TGA curves of HB, SMS-1 and SMS-2.

Notably, SMS-2 analysis revealed two key findings: (1) significant preservation of the cellulose structure even after the second cultivation cycle (persistence of the 1030 cm−1 band) and (2) moderate lignin modification (1622 cm−1) that did not inhibit fungal growth. These results indicate that G. lucidum preferentially metabolizes hemicellulose while the substrate remains suitable for up to two cultivation cycles. Furthermore, lignin modification appears to contribute positively to compost stability.

Despite complete hemicellulose degradation during the first cultivation cycle Ge et al.40, the partially retained cellulose structure and the conversion of lignin into by-products preserved a sufficient carbon source for reuse. Moreover, the persistence of O–H stretching bands (3330 cm−1) throughout both cycles indicates that the water retention capacity of the substrate was maintained. During the second cultivation cycle, the decreased intensity of this band can be attributed to the desorption of water molecules41.

Importantly, the phenolic compounds that may result from lignin degradation during the second cycle did not exhibit antifungal properties, thereby allowing continued mycelial development. This finding strongly supports the idea that mushroom compost can be effectively reused for at least two production cycles of G. lucidum. This observation is consistent with the conclusion of Pandey and Pitman36, who suggested that certain lignin-derived compounds may, in fact, stimulate fungal growth.

Nevertheless, uncertainties remain regarding substrate performance beyond two cycles. The increased cellulose amorphization observed during the second cycle suggests a potential decline in productivity with further reuse. This highlights the need for future studies investigating long-term reuse scenarios to define the practical limits of substrate recycling.

In this study, the thermal degradation behavior of hazelnut branch (HB)-based lignocellulosic biomass used in G. lucidum cultivation was evaluated by Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) (Fig. 3). The thermal behavior of lignocellulosic structures generally occurs in three main stages: dehydration, primary thermal decomposition, and carbonization42. The results indicate that mushroom cultivation induces notable chemical and structural alterations in the biomass composition. A comparative analysis of mass losses for HB, the spent mushroom substrate after the first cultivation cycle (SMS-1), and the substrate after a second cultivation cycle (SMS-2) across three temperature ranges revealed the following key findings:

Firstly, the mass loss observed in the 30–200 °C range is associated with the removal of physically bound moisture and volatile low-molecular-weight compounds43,44. In this region, mass losses ranged between 8.5 and 10.5%. HB exhibited a lower mass loss compared to the other samples, which may be attributed to the higher porosity and water retention capacity of SMS-1 and SMS-2.

Secondly, during the main decomposition stage occurring within the 200–400 °C range, the greatest weight loss is observed. At this stage, the thermal degradation of the main components of the lignocellulosic structure—hemicellulose, cellulose, and lignin—takes place Dorez et al.,45, and the unstable structures (such as side chains and heterocyclic units) begin to decompose easily at lower temperatures46. These components exhibit completely different decomposition temperatures. Hemicellulose, a branched and amorphous polymer, degrades within the temperature range of 225–325 °C and possesses the lowest thermal stability. Cellulose, a homopolymer consisting of relatively stable linear chains of β-(1,4)-D-glucopyranose units, decomposes between 300 and 375 °C. Lignin, characterized by its high molecular weight and cross-linked structure, exhibits a wide degradation range from 250 to 500 °C47. The high degree of decarboxylation and the breakdown of C–C bonds suggest the decomposition of aliphatic compounds in this range48. The mass losses recorded for HB, SMS-1, and SMS-2 were 54.9%, 51.7%, and 48.9%, respectively. The lower mass loss observed in SMS-1 and SMS-2 compared to HB indicates that the fungus selectively degrades hemicellulose and partially cellulose, while lignin undergoes oxidative modification. Approximately 68–69% of the total mass loss in SMS-1 and SMS-2 occurred during this stage. Similarly, a study by Huang et al.49 reported that 63.3% of the total weight loss in a SMS sample occurred in the second stage.

Thirdly, in the 400–750 °C range, the mass losses for HB, SMS-1, and SMS-2 were 10.5%, 11.7%, and 13.7%, respectively. At this stage, the mass loss occurs more slowly, which is associated with the decomposition of stable aromatic structures, lignin molecules, and inorganic components34,50. SMS-2 exhibited the highest residual content at elevated temperatures, highlighting its greater thermal stability. This can be attributed to the accumulation of inorganic components and the decrease (an increase in fixed carbon residue) in degradable organic matter as a result of repeated substrate use38. With the increase in the number of utilization cycles, the increase in band intensity within the 700–400 cm−1 range of the FTIR spectrum (Fig. 2) and the increase in ash content presented in Table 4 support this result.

In conclusion, TGA analyses revealed that with each successive cultivation cycle, the thermal stability of the substrate increased, accompanied by a reduction in organic content and an increase in inorganic residues. Similar studies have also reported the degradation of polysaccharides and cellulose fibers, along with a decrease in the concentration of degradable compounds in the SMS content. Accordingly, as the number of utilization cycles increases, the weight losses observed in the thermal degradation range gradually decrease, while the amount of residues increases44.

The higher residual content and lower weight loss observed in SMS-2 suggest that repeated reuse of the substrate may eventually compromise its quality. Therefore, while a limited number of reuse cycles may be feasible, the quality of the substrate must be carefully monitored in subsequent applications.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of recycling spent Ganoderma lucidum substrates derived from hazelnut branch waste, suggesting their potential for sustainable mushroom production. The key findings reveal that spent substrates maintain sufficient structural and nutritional properties for reuse. The yield value of mushrooms produced from 100% Spent Mushroom Susbtrate (36. 72 g/kg) was found to be higher than that of mushrooms produced from 100% Hazelnut Branch (28. 27 g/kg), however, this difference was not statistically significant. The reuse of 100% Spent Mushroom Substrate generally enhanced the mineral composition of the mushrooms, with significant increases observed in most analyzed elements including Fe, Mn, Zn, Mg, Ca, K, and P compared to those cultivated on 100% Hazelnut Branch. The sole exception was copper (Cu), which remained higher in mushrooms grown on the Hazelnut Branch substrate. The lower phenolic content obtained in this study compared to literature values may be related to the chemical composition of Hazelnut Branch, which could provide a more limited source of phenolic precursors for Ganoderma lucidum. Thermal analysis supported the enhanced stability of spent substrates, attributed to lignin enrichment and mineral accumulation during cultivation. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy revealed progressive hemicellulose degradation and lignin modification. Notably, the substrates retained functionality for at least two cultivation cycles without antifungal effects, highlighting their potential in circular bioeconomy systems. However, further studies are needed to evaluate long-term reuse (> 2 cycles) and scalability under diverse conditions. These results contribute to sustainable mushroom cultivation practices and may offer a resilient food production strategy in resource-limited scenarios, such as agricultural crises or nuclear winter.
