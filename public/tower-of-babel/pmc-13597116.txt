Mostly getting along –Borrelia burgdorferi and its manipulation of the host immune system
Authors: Shilpa Sachan, Nicole Baumgarth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLyme disease is caused when mammalian hosts not part of the sylvatic life cycle, including humans, are infected with Borrelia burgdorferi (Bb) following the bite of an Ixodes tick. Ticks and natural mammalian host reservoirs, including rodents, however, are largely disease tolerant, despite Bb establishing persistent infection. We explore the complex and ongoing interplay between Bb and the immune system. We discuss how Bb infection-induced innate responses seem maladapted to this extracellular bacterial infection, perhaps explaining why CD4+ T cells are ineffective contributors to Bb infection control. While B cell responses critically control Bb tissue burden and disease development they cannot clear the infection, showing a bias towards ongoing plasmablast responses and a lack of functional germinal centers. Identifying the mechanisms that hinder effective immune clearance of Bb may lead to new approaches for the treatment of Lyme Disease.Keywords: bacterial infection, Lyme disease, immune regulation, immune suppression, Tickborne diseases

Introduction

Lyme disease, caused by spirochetal bacteria Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato (Bb), is the most common tickborne illness in the Northern Hemisphere, with approximately 476,000 human cases reported annually in the United States alone.1 Lyme disease is largely an inflammatory condition caused when Bb infects species that are not part of its sylvatic life cycle, including humans, dogs and horses (“dead end” or “accidental” hosts). In these species, the infection often triggers robust inflammatory responses without achieving Bb clearance. But clinical outcomes are highly variable, ranging from self-limiting disease to disseminated manifestations of inflammation, affecting the joints, nervous system, and heart. In 10% to 15% of human infections disease symptoms persist for 6 mo or longer despite antibiotic treatment, a condition identified as Post-Treatment Lyme Disease Syndrome, or PTLDS.2,3

Bb is maintained through a highly efficient enzootic transmission cycle involving Ixodes ticks and mammalian reservoir hosts. In the latter, Bb establishes persistence, thereby ensuring ongoing transmission to newly developing larval and nymph ticks with a blood meal.4 In the Eastern United States, the white-footed mouse (Peromyscus leucopus) serves as a principal reservoir, supporting long-term Bb colonization without overt pathology.5,6 In these rodents, infection is characterized by effective control of bacterial burden rather than sterilizing immunity, allowing sufficient numbers of spirochetes to persist in the skin, where they remain transmissible to feeding ticks.6 Similar patterns of disease tolerance7 and non-resolving infections by Bb are observed in several other mammalian hosts, including inbred laboratory mice (Mus musculus). The latter show overall few and mostly transient signs of disease.8,9 An exception are C3H mice, which lack the lysosomal enzyme β-glucoronidase, causing accumulation of glycosaminoglycan, a DAMP, in the joints of these mice, triggering arthritis.10 Local inflammation in this species is further enhanced via inappropriate Type I IFN-β activation and the induction of IFN-γ producing pathogenic CD4 T cells.11,12

Thus, Bb persistence in reservoir hosts reflects an evolved state of disease tolerance, in which inflammatory and effector responses are constrained, limiting tissue damage while permitting bacterial survival. By contrast, immune responses in humans are often skewed toward resistance, generating inflammation that contribute to pathology but it do not reliably result in sterilizing immunity9,11 (Fig. 1). The laboratory mouse model provides a biologically relevant and experimentally tractable system for dissecting Bb infection-induced immune response outcomes that result in control of the bacterial tissue burden and inflammation-mediated diseases.

B. burgdorferi infection outcome depends on the type of host infected. Infection of Ixodes ticks with Bb occurs at the larval, nymph or adult stage, while vertical transmission from adult ticks to eggs has not been consistently observed. Infected ticks transmit Bb at their next blood meal (one per developmental stage). In its tick vector, as well as in mammalian reservoir species Bb establishes persistent infection but these animals are disease tolerant. In contrast, in “accidental” hosts such as humans and domesticated animals, the infection appears variable but often requires antibiotics for clearance and causes a variety of inflammatory diseases, including skin rash (erythema migrans), arthritis, carditis and neurological inflammatory conditions, which are correlated with distinct adaptive immune response outcomes. Several studies firmly established the importance of B cell-mediated immunity and the secretion of anti-Bb IgG for disease prevention and resolution, as well as control of Bb tissue load.13–18 Surprisingly, the antibody-mediated protective effects appear to be induced largely independent of CD4 T cell help.13,19 Here we review innate and adaptive immune responses to Bb in laboratory mice with emphasis on the regulation and dysregulation of adaptive CD4 T cell and B cell responses. Understanding how the immune system is directed during Bb infection of rodents, and how a failure to induce a similarly balanced response may contribute to the plethora of disease outcomes observed in Lyme disease, may provide guidance for therapeutic approaches that help to reduce the short and long-term consequences of human infections.

Innate recognition and intracellular signaling in Bb infection

Upon a tick bite and encounter with Bb, the local, innate immune system of the skin rapidly initiates responses to limit bacterial dissemination. These responses are affected by the injection of tick salivary proteins at the bite size, which have a plethora of immunomodulatory functions. For an extensive review of those topics we refer to recent publications.20–23 Innate immune cells detect Bb through a variety of pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs), which trigger inflammatory signaling cascades and shape downstream adaptive immunity.

Toll-like receptor (TLR) sensing of Bb

TLR2 and its downstream adaptor molecule MyD88 play a central role in Bb recognition by macrophages and their sensing bacterial lipoproteins, including outer surface proteins (Osp)A and OspB.24–26 TLR2-deficient mice harbor significantly higher spirochete burdens in multiple tissues compared to wild-type controls, despite mounting comparable antibody responses.27 TLR2 functions through heterodimerization with either TLR1 or TLR6, enabling recognition of triacylated and diacylated surface expressed Bb lipopeptides. These heterodimers exhibit distinct ligand specificities, allowing the innate immune system to sense a broad range of Bb-derived lipoproteins.28,29 Although TLR5 recognizes bacterial flagellins, it does not bind to flagellin of Bb. This has been attributed to their unique periplasmic localization, rendering flagellin inaccessible to surface PRRs.30

Beyond surface recognition, intracellular Toll-like receptors can also sense Bb. Endosomal TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 recognize bacterial nucleic acids and are engaged following phagocytosis or cellular damage that exposes Bb-derived RNA and DNA within endosomal compartments,31 highlighting the importance of intracellular signaling pathways in the innate immune response to Bb.

Type I interferon responses to Bb

Type I interferon (IFN-I) signaling is strongly associated with disease severity, persistent inflammation, and tissue-specific pathology in both human Lyme disease and murine Bb infections.32–35 Although Bb is considered an extracellular bacterium and IFN-I is considered a response to intracellular pathogens, host cells seem to engage intracellular nucleic acid–sensing pathways following phagocytosis or uptake of borrelial components, resulting in potent IFN-I responses. Cytosolic nucleic acid–sensing pathways play a dominant role in IFN-I induction. Following degradation of internalized spirochetes, bacterial DNA enters the cytosol, where it is detected by cGAS, leading to STING activation.36 Bb derived cyclic di-adenosine monophosphate can directly activate STING, further amplifying IFN-I signals independent of cytosolic DNA sensing.37 Furthermore, endosomal TLR7 and TLR9 can detect borrelial RNA and DNA within dendritic cells and macrophages, triggering MyD88-dependent induction of IFN-α and IFN-β.38,39 Genetic or pharmacologic disruption of endosomal TLR signaling significantly reduced IFN-I and IFN-stimulated gene expression during infection.40

Early after infection of mice, Bb rapidly reaches the infection site draining LN, inducing LN enlargement, disruption of demarcated T and B cell zones, and an unusually strong accumulation of naive B cells.41 The B cell accumulation was shown to depend on Type I IFN receptor (IFNAR)-induced B cell repositioning rather than proliferation. It occurred independent of MyD88 and TRIF expression, that is, TLR activation, or major changes in homing chemokine (CXCL12, CXCL13, CCL19/21) expression.34 IFN-I responses correlate more strongly with inflammatory pathology than with effective bacterial clearance, as IFNAR-deficient mice exhibit reduced arthritis severity without a substantial increase in spirochete burden.34,36,40 Furthermore, studies with recombinant inbred mouse strains showed that arthritis development in C3H but not C57BL/6 mice was associated with the IFN-I locus.12 Similarly, neuroborreliosis33 and arthritis42 development in humans has been associated with strong IFN-I induction. Thus, strong evidence indicates that intracellular sensing driven IFN-I responses contribute to immune dysregulation and tissue damage in both mice and humans.43 While IFN-I signaling can induce IFN-γ producing NK, NKT, and CD4 T cells, which in turn can activate macrophages to increase their anti-bactericidal activity, it can also lead to the induction of IL10.43 The suppressive effects of IL10 on both, Bb-induced inflammation and Bb clearance in mice are well understood.11,44 Thus, the combined effects of IFN-I on Bb infection are likely highly dependent on the genetics of the infected host. In contrast to human viral infections, including with SARS-COV-2 or influenza A,45–47 to our knowledge no human studies have linked genetic IFN-I deficiencies or the presence of IFN-I-neutralizing antibodies to altered anti-Bb infection outcomes. Instead, the higher pathogenicity of Bb RST 1 isolates, manifested in vigorous systemic dissemination in both humans and mice, has been linked to its enhanced induction of Type I and Type III IFN, but not IFN-γ.39

The innate immune response to Bb infection

The earliest immune responses to tick-transmitted Bb are initiated in the skin of mice and humans to both the pathogen and the tick bite itself.48,49 One of the earliest observed clinical signs of Lyme disease in a majority of Lyme disease patients is the development of a skin rash, at the site of the tick bite, the so-called bullseye rash, or erythema migrans (EM).50 These skin lesions consist of perivascular and interstitial T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells, but few neutrophils or B cells,51,52 although local IgM production has been noted.53 In mice, which do not develop EM, neutrophils are rapidly recruited to the skin where they deploy antimicrobial mechanisms, including neutrophil extracellular traps that can restrict spirochete motility.54 However, analysis of murine skin revealed a rapid loss of neutrophils, resulting in a macrophage-dominant infiltrate within ∼16 h.9 Sustained local neutrophil engagement is important for control of Bb; however, as genetically modified Bb strains that promote prolonged neutrophil recruitment via secretion of the neutrophil chemoattractant KC were controlled better in mice than non-KC expressing Bb controls.9,55 Thus, indicating that evasion of neutrophil-mediated immunity is central to Bb persistence, and suggesting that the rapid loss of skin neutrophils immediately early after Bb infection might represent an active immune evasion strategy for Bb.

The invasion of Bb into the human and murine skin will result in the activation of the complement cascade designed to kill invading pathogens through opsonization for uptake through complement-receptor mediated phagocytosis, chemoattraction of various innate immune cells that can aid in this process, and/or through direct lysis via formation of the membrane attack complex on the Bb cell surface.56 Counteracting this ancient immune defense mechanism are several known proteins of Bb, including at least 5 complement regulator acquiring surface proteins (CRASPs) that inhibit complement activation via the alternative or lectin pathways, as summarized elsewhere.57

Studies in mice and with human EM biopsies have identified epidermal Langerhans cells and dermal dendritic cells as primary antigen-sensing cells following Bb infection58–60 (Fig. 2). Langerhans cells recognize Bb via TLR2 and migrate to draining lymph nodes to initiate adaptive immunity.61 Importantly, tick-feeding mouse models and human skin explant studies showed that tick-derived salivary factors can induce a tolerogenic APC phenotype, delaying effective T cell priming and facilitating Bb persistence.59,62 Subsequently, the inflammatory milieu at the infection site recruits circulating monocytes into the tissue where they differentiate into macrophages. These macrophages become primary drivers of phagocytic Bb clearance; however, they also serve as a major source of the inflammatory cytokines that define the clinical manifestations of human disease.63–65

The quality of early produced cytokines is a critical determinant of the resulting adaptive response. Early secretion of IL-12 promotes the differentiation of CD4 T cells toward IFN-γ producing Th1 cells, which are essential for improving macrophage-mediated bacterial killing.66,67 Conversely, the production of IL-6 and TGF-β promotes development of Th17 cells, which promote neutrophil activation and extracellular pathogen clearance but also neutrophilic infiltration and joint damage in human Lyme arthritis.68 Ultimately, the collective action of these innate cells provides the three essential signals required for the initiation of adaptive immunity: Signal 1: The presentation of Bb antigens on MHC-II molecules, Signal 2: The expression of co-stimulatory molecules like CD80 and CD86. Signal 3: The secretion of polarizing cytokines.67 By integrating these signals, the innate immune system determines the specificity, magnitude, and pathological consequences of the ensuing T and B cell responses. These cellular interactions should ensure that the host eventually develops high-affinity antibodies and cellular defenses, even as the spirochete employs various strategies to evade these very mechanisms.

The adaptive immune response to Bb infection is complex, however, and ultimately unsuccessful in clearing infection,9 suggesting that the earliest immune response signals induced by the tick bite and/or Bb infection in their natural reservoirs drive anti-Bb immunity toward responses that promote disease tolerance and persistent infection. Existing literature supports this by demonstrating misdirected or ineffective stimulation in the skin of Langerhans cells and perhaps other APC, which after traveling to draining lymph nodes may provide tolerogenic rather than activating signals to CD4 T cells (Fig. 2 and below). Understanding the signals induced at these sites will be critical in identifying molecular mechanisms driving an ultimately unsuccessful T cell response to Bb infection.

Adaptive immunity to Bb infection

The adaptive immune system of mice is indispensable for effective control, albeit not clearance, of Bb as shown in numerous studies with SCID mice, which lack both T and B lymphocytes. These mice are unable to control spirochete levels in the blood and poorly control Bb tissue loads. This causes heightened inflammation, including carditis and arthritis, requiring eventual euthanasia of infected animals.8,9,69,70 Early studies established the importance of B cells in control of Bb tissue burden, while surprisingly minor effects were noted in mice lacking all T cells, or selected T cell subsets.9,13,16 T cell deficiencies do result in moderate increases in spirochete tissue loads and heightened arthritis severity in mice.16,71,72

B cells are essential also in preventing disease manifestations such as arthritis, carditis, and myocarditis in mice. Antibody-mediated resolution of arthritis, albeit not carditis, was noted after passive transfer of serum from previously infected but not non-infected mice or humans, or the transfer of both B and T cells (but not T cells alone), into SCID or similarly T and B cell-deficient rag1−/− mice.13–15,18,69,73,74 Furthermore, Bb tissue burden in mice that harbor B cells unable to secrete any antibodies (AID × sIgM−/− double knock out mice) was increased compared to wild type mice but comparable to Bb burden seen in SCID mice,17 suggesting that control of Bb burden is mainly through the secretion of class-switched Ig. In contrast, the early T-independent and continued production of IgM provided rapid clearance of Bb from the blood but did not affect Bb tissue burden.17 The above-mentioned modest increases of Bb burden in CD4 T cell-deficient mice are correlated with a lack of Ig class switch recombination and the resulting decreased production of Bb specific IgG.72,73,75

T cell responses to Bb infection

Surprisingly scant evidence exists for impactful effector or helper CD4 T cell responses to Bb, with early studies finding little immune-protective effects of T cells during Bb infection, making it important to consider what we know about how T cells contribute to disease development and/or Bb persistence. We will focus our remarks on CD4 T cells.

Effector CD4 T cells in Bb infection

CD4 T cell activation and clonal expansion is usually followed by polarization of their responses towards one of several effector responses through the secretion of cytokines and other immunomodulators by antigen-presenting cells. As discussed above, skin epidermal Langerhans cells seem to be affected by tick salivary proteins that inhibit effective CD4 T cell activation and altered effector T cell polarization as measured by cytokine production59,62 (Fig. 2). However, ineffective CD4 T cell activation was seen also when Bb infection of mice was done via needle inoculation with cultured or via tissue transfer with host-adapted spirochetes, suggesting that T cell activation alterations are due at least in part to the presence of the pathogen itself.72 In this context it is interesting to note that while Bb is not known to harbor any classic secretion systems, recent studies suggest that they generate extracellular vesicles with immunomodulatory capacity.76 In both, mice and humans, the CD4 Th1 immune response to Bb seems to drive pathogen defense at the cost of inflammatory tissue damage. In murine models, the polarization toward a Th1 phenotype is driven by cytokines like IL-12 and characterized by high levels of IFN-γ and TNF-α which directly correlates with the severity of Lyme arthritis.66,77 Langerhans cell depletion was shown to increase IFN-γ production by CD4 T cells after tick-transmitted Bb infection of mice.62 While a TH1 response was shown supportive for limiting spirochete burden, it is fundamentally incapable of achieving sterile clearance; instead, it causes joint swelling and tissue pathology.78 Ex vivo and in vitro studies of human cells from Bb infected patients suggested that Bb exposure may suppress effective antigen presentation and induce more tolerogenic than TH1-driving signals.79,80 Consistent with these findings, scRNA sequencing analysis of murine LN following Bb infection showed upregulation of suppressor of cytokine signaling genes Socs1 and Socs3, as well as downregulation of genes associated with MHC II antigen presentation in LN B cells.81 In contrast, a distinct Th1 predominance is found in the synovial fluid of patients with Lyme arthritis.82,83 Moreover, the blockade of Th1-inducing cytokines in experimentally infected mice was shown to reduce inflammation that led to a modest increase in bacterial numbers, while adding IFN-γ exacerbated the disease.84 Ultimately, it appears that Th1 responses in both mice and humans function as a modulator of disease severity that provide partial Bb control but lack the capacity to fully eradicate tissue-resident spirochetes.

Th2 polarization does not appear to be strongly induced in response to Bb infection. Although, a hallmark of Th2 effector responses, eosinophilia, has been described in Bb infected Peromyscus leucopus mice.85 Early studies suggested that Th2 polarization could prevent inflammatory disease in Bb infected BALB/c mice.86 However, C57BL/6 mice deficient in either IL-4 or IL-5 signaling showed enhanced rather than reduced resistance from Bb infection.87 Studies conducted in human Lyme Borreliosis show that initial IFN-γ responses, followed by up-regulation of IL-4, is associated with nonchronic manifestations, whereas a persistent IFN-γ response may lead to chronic disease.83 Specifically, longitudinal studies of Lyme patients indicate that an initial Th1-driven IFN-γ response must be followed by a timely up-regulation of Th2-associated cytokines, such as IL-4, to ensure a self-limiting, non-chronic clinical course.88 In contrast, a failure to transition from this pro-inflammatory Th1 environment to a regulatory Th2-mediated state, or a lack of proper immune downregulation, is a primary factor associated with the development of persistent symptoms and antibiotic-refractory arthritis.83

Effector Th17 cells are critical effectors controlling extracellular bacterial and fungal infections by promoting neutrophil recruitment and anti-bacterial immunity but are also implicated in autoimmune diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis and IBD.89 Thus, it is not surprising that Th17 responses have been implicated in the pathogenesis of Lyme arthritis. In mice, it was suggested that Bb infection triggers the production of IL-6 and IL-23, which then drives the differentiation of Th17 cells and the subsequent secretion of IL-17A.90 This response might be responsible for the recruitment of neutrophils to the joints. Notably, experimental neutralization of IL-17 in mice was shown to reduce joint swelling without significantly impacting bacterial loads, indicating that Th17 induction during Bb infection signaling occurs in the context of inflammatory disease rather than as part of a response to control Bb infection.91 In humans, the Th17 response is a hallmark of late-stage Lyme disease complications, particularly antibiotic-refractory Lyme arthritis. Patients with persistent joint inflammation exhibit significantly elevated levels of IL-17 and IL-22 in their synovial fluid, which promotes synovial hyperplasia and further recruitment of inflammatory cells.92 While the Th17 response in mice is closely tied to the presence of Bb, the human Th17 response in Lyme arthritis can become self-perpetuating even after an apparent eradication of the bacteria. This is often attributed to a breakdown in immune tolerance and potential molecular mimicry between Bb antigens and host proteins, leading to a chronic, autoimmune-like state that distinguishes the human clinical experience from most murine models.93 However, while IL-17 and IFN-γ have both been implicated in the development of Bb induced arthritis, blocking IFN-γ in the presence or absence of IL-17 did not lead to reductions in inflammation,94 suggesting that Bb induced arthritis is a multifactorial inflammatory condition only partially induced or regulated by T cell activation.

T follicular helper cell (Tfh) responses to Bb

Tfh cells are distinct from effector CD4 T cells by phenotype, transcriptional profile and function. Their differentiation is controlled by the transcription factor Bcl6. Hallmark surface markers include PD-1 and CXCR5, the latter facilitating migration of activated CD4 T cells to the T-B border and eventually into the B cell follicle, where they participate in germinal center (GC) responses in part by secreting IL-21 and providing CD40L mediated signaling supporting immunoglobulin class-switching and somatic hyper-affinity maturation of B cells.95

During Bb infection, flow cytometric analysis revealed that ICOS+ PD-1+ CXCR5+ pre-Tfh cells are induced quite rapidly, even prior to the appearance of GC B cells.96 In vitro, CD4 Tfh isolated from the LN of Bb infected mice drove B cells toward plasmablast differentiation rather than B cell proliferation, consistent with the strong skewing of B cell responses to Bb toward extrafollicular plasmablast responses over GCs96,97 (see below). However, the extent to which Bb-induced Tfh support extrafollicular plasmablast responses in vivo remains to be more fully established. Our recent studies also identified the presence of FoxP3+ ICOS+ PD1+ CXCR5+ CD4 T follicular regulatory T cells (Tfr; Sachan and Baumgarth, unpubl.). Although it is tempting to speculate that their appearance might downmodulate Bb-induced B cell responses, their functional assessment in the context of Bb infection has, to our knowledge, not yet been done.

We previously demonstrated that arthritis-related protein (Arp) of Bb induces strong T-dependent IgG responses in C57BL/6 mice, while IgG responses to decarin binding protein A are induced independent of T cell help.72 Indeed, peptide screening of Arp identified a MHCII I-Ab-restricted CD4 T cell epitope that is conserved between various sensu stricto Bb strains.98 Using MHCII tetramers of the Arp peptide formally demonstrated the presence of Arp-specific CD4 Tfh in LN of Bb infected mice as early as day 8 of infection. However, despite the strong induction of IgG to Arp, the abundance of Arp-Tfh was low and their frequencies decreased even further by day 12 of infection,98 a time when GCs were not yet fully developed. Given the rapid collapse of GC by about 4 wk of infection,96,97 the findings may suggest a deficiency not in the initial generation but rather maintenance of GC-Tfh and/or GC B cells.

Regulatory T cell responses during Bb infection

The overall modest induction of CD4 T cells in the LN of Bb infected mice and the lack of effective T-dependent B cell responses, including the initiation but then rapid collapse of GCs may suggest an imbalance between the induction of effector and follicular helper CD4 T cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs), respectively. However, while our earlier studies identified small increases in Foxp3+ Tregs in the LN of Bb infected mice, their frequencies and the ratios of effector and regulatory T cells did not suggest that classical Tregs suppress effective immune responses but rather a modest induction of T cell activation overall.72 Instead, Tregs may control of inflammatory arthritis induced by Bb in mice by counteracting TH17 cells.75,99 TGF-β and IL-10–producing Tregs have been reported to occur also in the cerebrospinal fluid of human patients with ongoing Lyme Borreliosis,100 although their effects on the disease is largely unexplored. Blockade of IL10, often associated with Treg activity was shown to cause significant reductions in Bb load and corresponding increases in Bb-specific IgG responses in C57BL/6 mice, although the anti-inflammatory effects of IL-10 were considered due to suppression of macrophage activation.101 Overall, the blockade of IL-10 caused increased inflammatory disease after Bb infection, including infection-induced arthritis. Thus, the balance of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine responses are key to controlling Bb-induced disease11,44 and the extent of inflammation is less correlated with Bb tissue load, but rather the quality of the host response.

Collectively these data provide strong indications that effective CD4 T cell responses are suppressed during Bb infection, including effective “help” for T-dependent B cell responses, and that this T cell response suppression contributes to disease tolerance but also persistence of Bb in its native reservoir hosts.

Antibody-mediated control of Bb

Antibody-mediated control of Bb arthritis after passive transfer was correlated with reduced Bb loads, suggesting that Bb-induced IgG enters the synovial space and supports the killing of Bb. This is likely aided by activation of the antibody-complex mediated classical pathway of complement activation,56 although most strains of Bb express surface proteins such as BBK32 and p43 that inhibit this immune defense pathway.57,102 In contrast, while mice show only intermittent carditis, an inflammatory condition enhanced in the absence of B cells,13 resolution of carditis in SCID mice was observed only after co-transfer of both B and T cells. Transfer of immune serum alone was not sufficient to reduce carditis, while transfer of T cells alone enhanced immunopathology.16 The data indicate immunoregulatory functions for B cells entering or residing in the heart that are independent of, or in addition to, their production of antibodies by mechanisms that remain to be identified.

Another antibody-mediated Bb control mechanism still unresolved, is how passive protection via transfer of immune IgM and IgG from persistently infected mice is achieved. Protection is observed after infection with culture-grown Bb, as well as infection via a tick bite, as long as antibodies are given prior to or shortly after infection, but not after infection is established.14,18,19,103 Similarly, serum from human Lyme disease patients was shown to contain antibodies that are passively protective when transferred to mice, even though disease resolution in these patients requires antibiotic treatment.104,105 Given the known extensive changes in the gene and protein expression profile of Bb as it moves from the tick (or culture) into the mammalian host,106,107 and the fact that passive transfer was unable to protect from infection with host-adapted spirochetes,18 it is tempting to speculate that the antigen-specificity of the immune serum is distinct from the antigen-expression profile of Bb in host tissue, or that Bb acquires resistant mechanisms that inhibit antibody-mediated destruction, such as upregulation of inhibitors of the classical pathway of complement (BBK32, p43), or interference with opsonization via Bb receptor binding. Recent technical advances in defining the transcriptional profile of Bb in infected hosts107,108 might help to resolve this long-standing puzzle.

Antibody escape mechanisms of Bb

Changes in antigen expression are common immune evasion strategies among various arthropod-transmitted pathogens,109,110 including Bb.111–114 The best-known example of protein expression changes facilitating immune escape by Bb is the downregulation of the strongly immunogenic Outer surface protein A (OspA) prior to transmission of Bb from the tick to the mammalian host.115,116 While OspA is the target of vaccination strategies, anti-OspA antibodies act as transmission blockers of Bb after their take-up by ticks with the blood meal, rather than eliminating Bb in the mammalian host.117 Another example is OspC, a protein required for the establishment of Bb infection in mammalian hosts,118 a process supported by the interaction of OspC with tick salivary proteins.119 Although relatively low titers of antibodies against OspC are generated in mice72,96 and their presence prior to an infection can be protective,120 the rapid downregulation of OspC prevents induction of strong OspC responses.121

Antigenic variation due to gene recombination is another mechanism that has received much attention as an immune evasion strategy of Bb. The Vls (Vmp-like sequence) locus of Bb contains an expression site (VlsE) that encodes the lipoprotein VlsE and a contiguous array of 15 silent cassettes located on the 28 kb linear plasmid, lp28-1, which facilitate recombination.111–113,122,123 The cassette segments may act as a source of non-reciprocal recombination events to produce large numbers of distinct antigenic variants during infection.112 Such recombination of VlsE occurs even in antibody-deficient SCID mice, with nearly 80% of isolated clones undergoing recombination within 14 d after infection with Bb B31.122 Thus, antibody-mediated immune pressure does not appear to be a driving force of VlsE recombination. When present, however, VlsE must recombine for Bb to persist.114,124 Studies showed that the N40 strain of Bb also establishes persistence, including in C57BL/6 mice,41,97 even though N40 harbors a truncated VlsE locus and infected mice do not generate antibodies against the constant region of VlsE from either N40 or B31. So, it remains puzzling how Bb establishes persistence in mice, despite the presence of several strongly immunogenic surface lipoproteins other than VlsE that induce bactericidal antibodies,114,125 and that antibodies against VlsE are not required for serum passive protection.114 Bankhead and colleagues suggested that VlsE may act as a large “umbrella,” shielding the detection of other otherwise immunogenic surface proteins of Bb from antibody binding.124

B cell immunity to Bb infection

T-independent B cell responses in Bb infection

A potential explanation for the overall much stronger effects of B cell rather than CD4 T cell depletion on Bb control is that Bb infection induces mostly T-independent B cell responses. Support for this has come from early studies in mice lacking CD40L, a critical co-stimulatory molecule expressed by T cells that induces B cell activation and Ig class switch recombination. Passive transfer of immune serum from CD40L-deficient and WT control mice protected WT mice from Bb infection to the same extent following challenge infection.19 Similarly, serum from Bb infected mice deficient in total T cells, CD4 T cells, or MHCII maintained passive protective capacity to challenge via tick bite or syringe inoculation of culture-grown Bb.13,19,126 Also, T-independent infection-induced IgM alone can provide full passive protection.17 Furthermore, Bb infected mice are unable to mount maximal IgG responses to irrelevant T-dependent antigens after immunization and instead showed enhanced production of IgM compared to their sham-infected controls.17,72 Not only does Bb infections induce unusually strong T-independent IgM responses in mice,41 reports from both humans and mice have demonstrated the presence of Bb-specific serum IgM for months after initial infection.127 Given the very short half-life of IgM, considered to be less than 24 h, this means either the continued presence of IgM-secreting plasma cells or their ongoing de novo generation.

Recently we showed that the failure to generate effective class-switched antibody responses following Bb infection of mice extends to gut microbiome-induced anti-microbial IgA and IgG production.128 The reductions in local IgA production resulted in Bb infection-induced gut dysbiosis and correlated with a “leaky gut” syndrome in these animals.128 We earlier demonstrated that Bb induces B cell responses that are characterized by robust extrafollicular plasmablast responses in lymph nodes,41 which can be induced in both a T-dependent or T-independent manner.129 In contrast, the T-dependent germinal center (GC) responses, although induced, rapidly after Bb in mice contract and appear non-functional.41,96,97 Collectively, these data demonstrate that T-independent B cell responses alone can provide immune control of Bb tissue loads, prevent Bb-induced disease, and provide passive immune protection, possibly preventing super-infection of rodents with a related Bb strain.

T-dependent B cell responses to Bb infection

Given the expression of hundreds of protein antigens by Bb, it is surprising that T-independent humoral responses should dominate the Bb-specific response. Indeed, recent studies identified CD4 T cell epitopes to 2 Bb proteins, arthritis-related protein98 and methyl-accepting chemotaxis protein peptide 4130 in C57BL/6 mice, which supported the induction of T-dependent antigen-specific IgG responses; however, their numbers as assessed by tetramer staining were low. Furthermore, strong reductions in class switched Bb-binding antibodies (except for IgG2b) were apparent in mice lacking CD40L, MHCII, CD4, or all T cells, compared to controls,13,19,126 suggesting that T-B interactions do occur during infection. Since immune sera from T-deficient mice have similar passive protective capacity than those from wild type mice, it appears that other functional outcomes of T-B interaction, such as hyper-affinity maturation are ineffective.

B cell activation results in the induction of often short-lived, extrafollicular responses and/or development of longer-lived germinal centers (GC)131,132 (Fig. 3). While an extrafollicular, rapid differentiation of activated B cells into antibody-producing plasmablasts and memory B cells can be induced via both T-independent and T-dependent processes, GC are considered outcomes of T-dependent B cell activation. GCs support the development of highly affinity-matured, class-switched and long-lived antibody responses, through generation of bone marrow-residing, long-lived plasma cells, as well as circulating memory B cells that can be rapidly reactivated.133

Bb infection induces both, extrafollicular and GC responses, however, these responses appear atypical. The strong induction of extrafollicular plasmablasts dominate the infection-induced responses in all lymph nodes of infected mice. In addition, lymph nodes are filled with unusually large numbers of naive B cells, beginning with responses at the site of infection and then spreading across the mouse.41 In contrast to textbook responses, as observed after acute viral infections, where extrafollicular foci disappear within 1 to 2 wk after infection,132,133 Bb-induced extrafollicular responses contract but continue to generate antibodies in infected mice throughout the infection.41 They appear to provide much of the circulating serum antibodies, and cause ongoing hypergammaglobulinemia.134

GC responses to Bb infection in mice are initiated also but strongly contract within about two weeks following their induction, without evidence of sustained somatic hyperaffinity maturation, or the generation of long-lived plasma cells and memory B cells.96–98 While no information on GC or extrafollicular responses exist from infected humans, it is noteworthy that patients with early Lyme disease have increased levels of both Bb specific and non-specific IgM in serum and high frequencies of activated B cells.135,136 This is followed by rising levels of total serum IgG, including antibody to an expanding array of Bb proteins.137–140 Both specific and non-specific IgM and IgG continue to increase for about 90 days in experimentally infected mice and B cells strongly accumulate in all peripheral lymph nodes.41,97,141 As infection proceeds in humans, IgM titers decline, but there can be a persistence of IgM reactivity for months to years against a small number of proteins in the range of 37–41 and 20–25 kDa on immunoblots made from Bb lysates. During early infection, IgG reactivity initially arises to proteins in this range as well.137–140,142–144 A similar pattern is seen in naturally infected dogs and experimentally infected mice.18,145–147 Despite this robust antibody response, recurrent episodes of disease can occur in both, humans148,149 and susceptible mouse strains.146 Antibiotic-treated humans150–152 and mice,96,153 which have recovered from infection, are often susceptible to reinfection, suggesting a failure to induce effective immunological memory, as documented in mice96 and in humans.154

Aiming to understand the mechanisms underlying the strong contraction of GCs in lymph nodes of Bb infected mice, our recent studies showed that GC responses are maintained in Bb-infected mice that lack B cell-intrinsic expression of the FcγRIIb, and in mice with reduced Bb-induced IgG production either through deletion of prdm1, the gene encoding Blimp-1, or aicda, the gene encoding AID, regulating plasma cell differentiation and class-switch recombination, respectively. The data suggest that the strong and ongoing IgG production from extrafollicular plasmablasts may cause IgG-mediated negative direct feedback and GC B cell apoptosis.134 Furthermore, and supporting previous studies demonstrating the lack of functional GC responses, rescue of GC responses in the Bb-infected FcγRIIb−/− mice had little effect on Bb-specific IgG levels, explaining the unaltered Bb tissue loads in these mice.134

Thus, additional factors are likely contributing to the failure of Bb-infected mice to produce affinity-matured memory B cells and long-lived plasma cells. In addition to the infection-induced disruption of the LN architecture,34,41 insufficient activation or functionality of B cells, follicular dendritic cells, or CD4 Tfh should be considered. Indeed, GC defects seem to occur in the context of a broader disruption of B cell positioning and signaling networks. Elevated levels of the B cell–attracting chemokine CXCL13 are consistently observed during Bb infection in humans, including in Lyme neuroborreliosis and inflammatory joint disease, where CXCL13 serves as a robust biomarker of disease activity.155,156 While CXCL13 supports GC organization and B cell trafficking, sustained dysregulated expression during infection of mice might act as another driver of follicular collapse and B cell desensitization, contributing to aberrant B cell recruitment and ineffective humoral immunity.34,156 The precise mechanisms by which chronic CXCL13 signaling intersect with GC maintenance, B cell fate decisions, and the generation of durable GC B cell responses in either mice or humans warrant further investigation.

Systemic humoral immune dysregulation during Bb infection

Consistent with broadly suppressive effects of Bb infection on the organization of adaptive humoral responses, infected mice are unable to mount strong IgG responses to immunization with unrelated pathogens, including influenza and SARS-CoV-2.96,157 Furthermore, ineffective humoral responses to microbes have been observed in the gastrointestinal tract of mice, where Bb infection resulted in reduced intestinal IgA production and a diminished coating of luminal microbes with IgA.128 These changes appeared responsible, at least in part for the observed enhanced gut permeability, systemic low-grade endotoxemia and a gastrointestinal microbiota that showed changes later in infection.128

While our ongoing studies aim to identify the mechanisms underlying this systemic immune suppression, the data clearly demonstrate that Bb infection induces a broad and durable impairment of T-dependent B cell–mediated immunity in mice, consistent with the establishment of persistent infection in this species. We posit that these findings may be relevant for understanding the disease chronicity and post-infectious sequelae observed in a subset of human patients treated for Lyme disease, who develop ongoing symptoms, including fatigue, musculoskeletal pain, and cognitive dysfunction, despite having signs of detectable active infection.158 Although the etiology of this PTLDS remains incompletely understood and might be multifactorial, a role for immune dysregulation, and chronic inflammation, and effects on the microbiota with symptoms similar to other post-infection syndromes provide a compelling mechanism.159,160

In summary, Bb infection induces exuberant, partly T-independent, extrafollicular IgG responses in mice that control Bb tissue loads and prevent pathogen-induced immunopathology. This may be one of the most obvious differences to immune responses in disease-susceptible humans, where limited and slow seroconversion is a hurdle to serodiagnosis of Lyme disease (Fig. 1). Interesting more robust B cell responses in humans have been associated with better outcomes after treatment of Lyme disease,161 further suggesting that the humoral response is critical for establishment of disease tolerance not just in mice but also in humans. That said, humoral responses fail to mature over time in infected mice and remain short-lived in nature, with memory B cell responses and long-lived plasma cells failing to develop for many months, consistent with the lack of functional T-dependent GC response induction and maintenance.

Conclusion

Strong evidence supports the notion of an evolutionary adaptation between Bb and its natural mammalian hosts, in which the manipulation of innate and adaptive immune pathways affects the balance of pro- and anti-inflammatory responses that result in disease tolerance and Bb persistence (Fig. 1). The Bb infection-induced immune modulation appears highly dynamic and mediated by multiple overlapping pathways rather than a single dominant suppressive axis. Attempting to alter this balance therapeutically by inducing immune activation risks exacerbating inflammation and tissue pathology, whereas suppressing inflammation may further compromise bacterial clearance and immune memory induction.

These challenges underscore the need for deeper mechanistic understanding of the specific pathways that are being activated and/or suppressed during Bb infection. Whole-cell based or broadly antigenic vaccine approaches may reinforce immunodominant but non-protective responses, which could limit durable immunity. In contrast, rationally designed subunit vaccines targeting defined immunodominant antigens may selectively amplify T-dependent humoral responses to protective antigens expressed in the mammalian host such that natural infection can provide a boost to vaccine-induced immunity, helping to overcome an infection, even if sterile immunity cannot be induced. Existing efforts to provide long-lasting immunity to a Bb protein, OspA, that is only expressed in the tick will always be a compromise until we have learned how to induce strong and durable antibody responses that avoid the need for repeat boosters.

Thus, future work should focus on elucidating what defines and suppressive effective long-lasting humoral immunity against Bb, including epitope specificity, antibody affinity and durability, and the required quality of CD4 T cell help. Integrating these mechanistic insights with tools to modulate immunity in time or in a tissue-specific manner may enable the development of vaccines or immunotherapies capable of restoring protective immunity while preserving immune homeostasis. Such approaches hold promises also preventing or curing PTLDS.
