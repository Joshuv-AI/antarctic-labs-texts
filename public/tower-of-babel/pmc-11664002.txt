Food waste end-of-life management for the United States: Parameterized life cycle inventory datasets
Authors: Kyle McGaughy, Ben Morelli, Abby Martell, James Santa Ana, Wesley W. Ingwersen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A life cycle inventory (LCI) dataset for food waste management was developed using secondary data from scientific literature and government reports. EPA reports on food waste management were used as the basis for collecting literature to review. Unit process parameters from the reviewed literature are compiled and combined with engineering calculations to generate LCI for food management pathways. Both the literature values and engineering calculations are reviewed and documented with notes indicating any potential limitations in the dataset.The Excel-based inventory model includes pathways for anaerobic digestion, composting – windrow and aerated static pile, landfilling, and incineration. The model includes default parameter value selections, but the inventory may also be generated using user-specified parameter values. The model includes site-specific unit process models that do not have default parameter values pre-selected. Users can specify parameter values within these unit processes to generate LCI data for the food waste management pathways in line with their project scope. Site-specific unit processes include options such as biogas and landfill gas utilization and treatment of compost and digestate. This dataset and model can be used to generate LCI for evaluating food waste management in a comprehensive life cycle assessment study.

Value of the Data

•The developed life cycle inventory (LCI) data harmonizes the scope of food waste management modeling assumptions for common U.S. management pathways. The scope of the developed inventory data is broader than that produced by other available tools, such as WARM, and can support analyses looking beyond assessment of greenhouse gas emission potential impact.•The supporting parameterized inventory model provides a transparent connection between input parameters and final LCI values that supports clear understanding of the developed inventory data and the ability to update developed inventory data for assessments with different goal and scopes, changing technology, and the availability of new or better data.•The delivered inventory model also includes several tables of input parameters (extracted from literature) that supports model validation, sensitivity/uncertainty assessment, and future analysis.•The developed LCI data can be used as an input to a full life cycle assessment model, where the modeled product system includes food waste management at end-of-life. The parameterized inventory model can be used to customize inventory data for a specific project scope. It is exportable into a machine-readable csv format.

Background

Food waste disposal leads to environmental impacts such as climate change associated with methane emissions or eutrophication impact from landfill leachate. A life cycle approach is the most appropriate perspective for considering the best means of managing food so that decision making can be informed by potential impacts and trade-offs that occur both directly and indirectly. LCI data is essential for quantitatively evaluating management and policy choices for food waste using life cycle assessment (LCA). Furthermore, LCI that includes quantification of all known pollutant releases to air, water, and soil can be used to estimate potential environmental and human health impacts, rather than limit analysis to a single impact category (e.g., climate change). In the context of food waste management in the U.S., a dynamic and freely available model for generating LCI quantifying all known pollutant releases that can be used in common LCA tools is still lacking. This inventory data model harmonizes the scope of generated food waste management LCI data and allows for streamlined sensitivity/uncertainty assessment and easy updating to support future LCA studies.

Data Description

The described dataset is LCI data for common food waste end-of-life management pathways in the United States [1]. These pathways include anaerobic digestion (AD), composting by windrow or aerated static pile, landfilling, and incineration with energy recovery. The described data product(s) are housed in a single Excel file that includes documented input parameters, a dynamic inventory model and the resulting LCI tables for each food waste management pathway. The LCI sheet can be exported into a machine-readable csv format. Table 1 describes the contents of the data file detailed in this article. Inventory scope

Remaining subsections within the Data Description section describe the scope of the developed inventory model. Tables are included in each subsection describing the value and reported range of default input parameters used in the model.

Food waste

Generated inventory data represents processing of typical mixed food waste (plant and animal waste) that would be generated at a household or commercial kitchen. The default inventory represents impacts associated with pure food waste and excludes impacts associated with any co-treated contaminants (e.g., packaging waste) and co-wastes. A co-waste is a waste material that is processed simultaneously with food waste within a waste management pathway (e.g., yard debris composted with food waste).

The presence, type, and subsequent treatment of food waste contaminants are site-specific and may be necessary to include in studies using this data depending on the analysis scope. Users can specify the presence of contaminants within collected food waste. Pre-treatment

Food waste destined for AD and compost pathways is expected to be source separated by hand at the household or commercial entity generating the waste. Additional pre-treatment steps can occur onsite at the waste processing facility or at a separate facility where food waste is aggregated. Food waste pre-treatment steps include processes necessary for contaminant removal, homogenization, or other steps necessary for subsequent management. Simple pre-treatment processes are assumed as inventory defaults and are detailed in Table 4. The compost model assumes screening and conveyance of incoming material. The AD model assumes use of a screw press for material homogenization and contaminant removal. No pre-treatment is modeled for the landfill and incineration pathways. Anaerobic digestion model

The anaerobic digestion (AD) pathway was modeled as a stand-alone digester that treats solely food waste under wet, mesophilic conditions. Fig. 5 shows the AD pathway system boundary diagram and Table 5 presents the default parameter selections. Food waste is pre-treated to remove contaminants such as plastics and inorganic materials. The separated food waste is then fed into the digester where it is broken down into digestate and biogas. Biogas is managed via either a combined heat and power (CHP) unit, electric engine, renewable natural gas (RNG) upgrading system, or flare. Digestate is either directly land applied (includes all water and solid contents) or is dewatered and sent to composting or a landfill. Food waste is often co-digested with manure on farms or with biosolids at wastewater treatment plants (WWTP). The effects of these co-wastes are not modeled in the dataset. Co-digestion can increase biogas yield of less degradable wastes such as biosolids and change digestate properties. This complicates assigning impacts to food waste inputs and other digestion feedstocks as the synergistic contributions of different digestion feedstocks is not always based on BOD, volatile solids, or solids content. A non-default inventory module is available which considers an alternative volatile solids reduction value for a co-digestion process. A pre-selected volatile solids reduction value of 90 % was included in the model for co-digestion, compared to the default value of 86 %, but the value will be site-specific and is up to the user to specify. Other synergistic effects of co-digestion are not considered.

Compost model

Windrow and Aerated Static Pile composting methods are both modeled as pathways. A windrow composting system uses a mechanical pile turner to aerate and mix the compost feedstocks at appropriate times during the composting period to mix decomposing materials and maintain pile temperature and aerobic conditions. An aerated static pile (ASP) composting system uses a forced aeration system to maintain pile temperature and aerobic conditions without the need for turning. Windrow and ASP systems are separate composting methods and occur at different sites in this model.

Compost process system boundaries are shown in Fig. 6. Table 4 presents default model parameters for each compost method. Resulting inventory data represents implementation of typical best management practices related to starting carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, pile moisture, and aeration management practice. As with other pathways, inventory values have been derived to exclude impacts or benefits associated with co-waste processing. However, it is recognized that co-waste processing is necessary to maintain best management practices. In effect, this means that process emissions and carbon sequestration benefits attributable to co-wastes are excluded from the model. In the context of food waste, a typical co-waste would be yard debris.Fig. 6System diagram of the food waste compost inventory model(s).Fig. 6

System diagram of the food waste compost inventory model(s).

Two common methods of composting have been modeled and are differentiated based on process energy demand (electricity and diesel), carbon degradation and methane and nitrous oxide emissions released during composting process. Carbon and nutrient content of the resulting compost depends on selected food waste characteristics and degradation and emission parameter selections.

Leachate generation and treatment is included within the inventory model. The user has the option to specify whether generated leachate is recirculated to the compost pile, reducing the volume of leachate generated, and whether remaining leachate is treated onsite or trucked to a local WWTP. Incineration model

The incineration pathway was modeled to represent a mass burn grate furnace with energy recovery; generally called waste-to-energy (WTE). Fig. 7 shows the system boundary diagram and Table 7 presents default parameters for the incineration pathway. In most WTE incinerators, food waste is combusted with various other wastes. However, in this model only the process inputs and outputs that can be allocated to food waste are considered. The heat generated from waste incineration is based on an average lower heating value (LHV) of food waste. Emissions to air and water are calculated based on the specified food waste composition. The inventory includes both electricity required to operate the incinerator and the gross electricity produced, rather than a single line for net electricity consumption/production. Landfill inventory parameters.

Captured landfill gas can be utilized for energy recovery or flared, but default gas utilization parameter values are not specified since these values are site-specific. The same processes for biogas utilization are included in the landfill model, as described for the AD model. Users can specify the fraction of captured landfill gas that goes to flaring, electric engine, CHP, or RNG upgrading. The RNG upgrading module has material inputs for activated carbon and zeolite that are used to remove siloxanes and CO2, respectively. User should consider additional site-specific material inputs that fit their modeling scope. The processes considered in these non-default modules are not exhaustive and do not include common processes such as desulfurization.

Leachate generation and treatment is included within the inventory model. Captured leachate is typically sent to either a WWTP or an evaporation pond. While wastewater treatment and evaporation ponds are available as pathway options that the user can select, there are no default parameters selected. There are significant differences in leachate treatment LCI depending on site-specific factors (e.g., proximity to a wastewater facility, average temperature, annual precipitation) [20].

Transport required for material inputs and trace compound emissions for the anaerobic digestion and compost pathways were excluded from the model. These were excluded due to either a lack of data, lack of an average value that was generally applicable to most processes, or an expected lack of impact. In addition to these flows, processes for source separation and contaminant management (i.e. inorganic material sorted from food waste during pre-treatment) are excluded. Pathway selection

Food waste management pathways were selected based on pathways identified in EPA's recent report on the environmental impacts of food waste management in the U.S. [27]. Anaerobic digestion, composting, incineration, and landfilling were the most commonly studied pathways with 85 % of reviewed LCAs including at least one of the pathways. While these pathways are modeled in the EPA Waste Reduction Model (WARM), currently only GHG emission impacts are inventoried. This dataset was developed to inventory flows for food waste management that affect all impact categories, not just GHGs.

Modeling methodology

Documented input parameters, which are the raw secondary data used to develop the LCIs, were identified in a review of the scientific literature. Input parameter values are processed into final LCI values using standard engineering calculations that are documented in the published data file.

The Background and Data Description sections of this article describe the Goals & Scope of this project. A narrative description of the inventory modeling methodology is presented for each pathway. Inventory calculations are available in the accompanying data file.

Anaerobic digestion

Process input factors for anaerobic digestion are standardized on a wet mass basis of food waste processed. Biogas generation is derived from volatile solids content of food waste and the overall destruction of volatile solids. The inventory model estimates the share of generated methane that is lost as fugitive emissions based on the methane content of produced biogas and a fugitive methane loss fraction.

Digestate production is calculated by mass balance, assuming 13 % solids content in the digester. Additional water is required for typical food waste to meet this 87 % moisture requirement. Water inputs are excluded from the inventory as they are assumed to be provided burden free by other processed co-wastes.

Modules are included for both biogas utilization and digestate processing, but no default treatment pathways are selected. Biogas utilization options include flaring, upgrading to renewable natural gas, and utilization in either an electric engine or combined heat and power system. Combustion emission profiles are provided for each biogas utilization option and are scaled to the quantity of biogas combusted. Digestate handling options include direct land application, dewatering, landfilling, and composting. When digestate is dewatered, partitioning factors are used to determine the share of total nutrient and carbon content that is retained in the solid phase. Dewatered digestate can be composted or landfilled. The compost and landfill pathway models can be used to generate inventory data for digestate management. The land application module estimates the quantity of avoided synthetic fertilizer and carbon sequestration over a 100-year time frame based on digestate composition and fertilizer substitution factors from literature.

Compost

Emission factors for compost process emissions are standardized to be on the basis of the quantity of carbon, nitrogen or phosphorus that enters the composting system. In cases where the original data source presents emission factors as a fraction of total carbon, nitrogen or phosphorus emitted, the original emission factor is converted to the desired basis based on study specific estimates of the share of total carbon, nitrogen or phosphorus released during composting. The emission factor for non-methane volatile organic compounds (to air) was identified in the form of unit mass of emissions released per unit mass of food waste processed. This emission factor was utilized as is. The quantity of carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus in the resulting compost is the quantity present in the initial food waste less any emission losses. The fraction of carbon degraded during composting is specified as an input parameter. The quantity of released carbon not released as methane or carbon monoxide is assumed to be released as carbon dioxide.

A leachate treatment module is included, but default values are not selected as leachate treatment will be site-specific. The module includes options for onsite treatment by climatic evaporation and offsite treatment at a WWTP. Nitrogen, phosphorus and chemical oxygen demand (COD) are modeled within the compost leachate module based on typical constituent concentrations and leachate generation from literature. Trace compound emissions are not included in the compost leachate model. If leachate recirculation is specified, the volume of leachate is reduced according to cited literature. The concentration of COD in leachate is reduced during the recirculation process. Nitrogen and phosphorus are assumed to be conserved during leachate recirculation due to a lack of data regarding removal. Inventory data for the offsite WWTP is scaled based on the strength of compost leachate relative to typical wastewater assuming influent WWTP nitrogen and COD concentrations of 40 and 500 mg/L, respectively. WWTP nitrous oxide emissions are scaled based on influent nitrogen concentration. All other inventory parameters are scaled based on influent COD concentration. A removal fraction is applied to chemical constituents within compost leachate that is sent for offsite treatment to estimate effluent emissions to water.

The onsite evaporation option for leachate treatment assumes that 100 % of leachate is evaporated, resulting in no liquid discharge, in a shallow anaerobic lagoon. Methane and nitrous oxide emissions to air are estimated based on the quantity of biological oxygen demand and nitrogen influent to the treatment process, respectively, based on International Panel for Climate Change (IPCC) emission factors [24]. Final uses of compost include in agriculture or landscaping. A module is included in the inventory model that estimates the quantity of avoided fertilizer and long-term (100-year) carbon sequestration that results when compost is land applied as an agricultural input.

Incineration

Food waste incineration emission factors are standardized to the burnable fraction of food waste, where possible. The burnable fraction is how much of the feedstock is actually combusted after considering process inefficiencies and includes both volatile and non-volatile solids. Process inputs for incineration are standardized to the wet weight of food waste managed and process inputs for air pollution control are calculated based on the composition of the combustion gases. For example, calcium oxide and sodium hydroxide are used to remove acidic components from the combustion gases, and their LCI values are calculated based on the amount of sulfur, nitrogen, phosphorus, and chlorine in the combustion gas. Trace compounds in food waste (e.g., sulfur, chloride, aluminum, arsenic, cadmium, chromium, copper, mercury, nickel, lead, and zinc) are used to calculate combustion gas composition and process inputs for air pollution control, but default concentrations for these compounds were not selected.

Transfer coefficients for combusted food waste are primarily from an LCI study by Doka Life Cycle Assessments, which used data from Swiss, German, and Austrian municipal waste incineration facilities to calculate elemental transfer coefficients [17]. Emissions to water are from releases and disposal of scrubber sludge generated in the air pollution control system. These emissions are standardized the same way as emissions to air; based on the burnable fraction of food waste and the elemental composition of the food waste. The ash composition is calculated by mass balance after considering emissions to air and water, no specific emissions factors were used to determine ash composition.

The default LHV parameter has been verified using the default food waste composition in the Dulong Formula for calculating higher heating value (HHV) [28]. How heat is utilized is site-specific, so default values for thermal and electric efficiency are not specified in the model. If these efficiencies are specified, then a simple efficiency calculation is included that calculates gross usable heat and electricity generation.

Landfill

Landfill process inputs are standardized to the wet mass of food waste. Landfill gas (LFG) generation is calculated based on the amount of degradable organic carbon (DOC) present in food waste using a standard first order decay model. The degradable organic carbon fraction (DOCf) determines the percentage of DOC that degrades within landfill conditions LFG generation and capture is estimated on an annual basis over a 100-year time frame. A progressive gas collection schedule is used to estimate gas capture assuming 0 % capture – years 0 to 5, 50 % capture – years 5 to 10, 75 % capture – years 10 to 15, 82.5 % capture – years 15 to 21, 90 % capture – years 21 to 35, and 0 % capture in subsequent years [18,19]. The methane oxidation factor specifies the share of uncaptured landfill gas that is oxidized to carbon dioxide as it exits the landfill. Carbon not degraded to LFG within 100 years is assumed to be sequestered.

LFG utilization emissions factors do not have default values as they are site-specific. The same combustion modules used by the anaerobic digestion process are included for landfill gas utilization. This module includes utilization via flaring, upgrading to renewable natural gas, and utilization in either an electric engine or combined heat and power system. Parameters that are on a methane or total gas volume basis are assumed to be interchangeable between biogas and landfill gas. For example, electricity input for upgrading is scaled to the total amount of gas processed, or fugitive methane emissions are scaled to the methane concentration.

Leachate generation is estimated based on national average precipitation data and landfill capacity and disposal area data from a sample of California landfills. Capacity and disposal area data is used to estimate the mass of material landfilled per unit area of landfill surface. The fraction of precipitation becoming leachate over a 100-year period was drawn from the literature. Total leachate generated is standardized to the wet mass of food waste.

The composition of leachate is determined based on the dry mass composition of food waste. The leachate partitioning model was adapted from Doka Life Cycle Assessments’ 2009 inventory for landfilling; in their model, constituents within landfilled waste are either released in landfill gas, leachate, or are retained in the landfill over a 100 year time period [29]. Release factors for nitrogen, phosphorus and trace compounds were drawn from this resource. Only chemical constituents within the mass of landfilled waste that degrades over a 100-year period partitioned to leachate and landfill gas. The DOCf of food waste was used to determine the share of landfilled food waste that would degrade within 100 years. A leachate treatment module is included, but default values are not selected as leachate treatment will be site-specific. The module includes options for onsite treatment by climatic evaporation and offsite treatment at a wastewater treatment plant. An additional removal fraction is applied to chemical constituents within leachate that is sent for offsite treatment. Reported emissions to water are the sum of uncontrolled emissions and the fraction of each compound remaining in WWTP effluent following treatment.

Inventory data for the offsite WWTP is scaled based on the strength of compost leachate relative to typical wastewater assuming influent WWTP nitrogen and COD concentrations of 40 and 500 mg/L, respectively. WWTP nitrous oxide emissions are scaled based on influent nitrogen concentration. All other inventory parameters are scaled based on influent COD concentration. A removal fraction is applied to chemical constituents within landfill leachate that is sent for offsite treatment to estimate effluent emissions to water. Leachate generation is modeled as releases of nitrogen, phosphorus, and COD to water. Calculations for trace compound emissions are included in the model, but do not have default parameters selected.

The onsite evaporation option for leachate treatment assumes that 100 % of leachate is evaporated, resulting in no liquid discharge, in a shallow anaerobic lagoon. Methane and nitrous oxide emissions to air are estimated based on the quantity of biological oxygen demand and nitrogen influent to the treatment process, respectively, based on IPCC emission factors.

Limitations
