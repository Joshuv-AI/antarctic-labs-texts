Automated DFU detection through GA-selected CNN ensemble with Grad-CAM interpretability
Authors: Shreya Girotra, Achin Jain, Sarita Yadav, Arvind Panwar, Harsh Taneja, Lukas Herout, Ondrej Krejcar, Hamidreza Namazi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diabetic foot ulcers (DFU) are a highly dangerous and even fatal sequela of diabetes mellitus, very often becoming a lower limb amputation and imposing significant morbidity and mortality all over the world. Deep learning and machine learning have recently made breakthroughs, creating potential for being applied in medical image analysis towards the detection of DFU, but the field still faces the challenge of optimizing the performance of the models in both terms of accuracy and interpretability. The present paper solves this issue by introducing a more optimized deep learning model to detect DFUs that has been proposed by integrating a proprietary Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) structure and a Genetic Algorithm (GA) based on ensembles. Seven different optimizers were distinctly trained by the custom CNN. The genetic algorithm (GA) was then applied to pool a final ensemble model out of individual models with the most remarkable performance. This GA based ensemble had an accuracy of 97%, a precision of 95%, a recall of 99%, and an F1 score of 97%, which showed better and stronger performance than the individual single models that made up it. In addition, the study implemented Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) to increase interpretation and transparency. The results obtained demonstrated that Grad-CAM was able to accurately describe the features associated with ulcers and, therefore, offers invaluable information on how the model makes its decisions to healthcare professionals.

Introduction

Diabetes Mellitus (DM), commonly called diabetes, is a chronic metabolic condition characterized by sustained hyperglycaemia, or elevated levels of glucose in the blood1. This disorder arises from the body’s failure to produce enough insulin or to use it effectively, resulting in complications such as cardiovascular diseases, renal failure, vision loss, and lower limb amputations due to diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs)2. DFUs are a significant complication of diabetes, impacting up to 34% of diabetic patients during their lifetime and playing a major role in morbidity and mortality on a global scale. Diabetes has emerged as a critical health issue that increasingly affects a large number of people in modern times. In 1980, it was estimated that 108 million people globally were living with diabetes; by 2014, this number had surged to 422 million, with projections suggesting it could reach 600 million by 20353. This escalation is particularly concerning in developing nations, where limited healthcare resources and a lack of awareness exacerbate the challenges posed by diabetes-related complications4. Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) alone account for more than a million lower limb amputations each year due to inadequate management and diagnosis. Consequently, individuals and healthcare systems, especially in low- and middle-income countries, face significant financial burdens as a result of these challenges5. Artificial intelligence has made significant progress in tackling the issues related to the identification and management of diabetic foot ulcers. Health scans have been utilized to detect conditions such as foot sores resulting from diabetes using methods such as deep learning (DL) and machine learning (ML).6 Medical images can be analyzed using AI-driven segmentation techniques, including brightness adjustment and area expansion, which have proven to be effective in identifying abnormal regions. However, these systems require large datasets for training and validation to ensure precision and reliability5,7. Advances in deep learning have resulted in the development of sophisticated Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) that excel in various classification tasks. However, optimizing CNN architectures remains a critical challenge, particularly in medical imaging and high-dimensional data classification. Optimizers are algorithms designed to modify the parameters of a model to minimize the loss function. The selection of an appropriate optimizer plays a crucial role in determining the convergence speed and overall performance of the model.8 Learning in CNNs involves fine-tuning network parameters to effectively lower error rates. There are numerous strategies to improve the efficiency and stability of models, such as Adam, SGD, RMSprop, Adagrad, Adamax, Nadam, and AdamW.9 Adaptive learning rate controllers (such as Adam, RMSprop, and Nadam) adjust learning rates for different components, accelerating the process and preventing excessive deviation. In contrast, SGD-based optimizers perform well when fine-tuned with an appropriate learning rate and momentum, often leading to better generalization in unseen data.10 While CNNs are highly effective, the choice of learning algorithm significantly impacts their performance and adaptability in diverse datasets. Research indicates that various optimizers perform optimally depending on the characteristics of the data and the complexity of the model. For example, Adam and its variants (AdamW, Nadam) have shown exceptional performance in handling large datasets with numerous features, whereas SGD and RMSprop are particularly effective in situations that require strict constraints on the model. Our paper aims to investigate how different optimization techniques affect the performance of CNNs in image classification, ensuring a rapid and adaptable learning process.11 Moreover, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) exhibit a significant degree of accuracy; however, their efficacy is heavily influenced by the choice of hyperparameters, complicating the process of generalization and optimization for novel datasets. Hyperparameter tuning, batch normalization, and regularization techniques are needed to address overfitting and improve robustness.12 By systematically evaluating multiple optimizers in CNN-based architectures, our paper seeks to improve the performance of deep learning models through a detailed analysis of various optimizers within CNN-based frameworks.13 In our paper, explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) plays a crucial role in being used for the detection of diabetic foot ulcers by providing transparency and interpretability. The Grad-CAM technique, known as Gradient weighted Class Activation Mapping,14 was employed to investigate its impact on model predictions. Using this approach, we were able to identify the specific areas of skin images that the model relies on to determine whether the skin is healthy or affected by an ulcer, thereby facilitating a clearer understanding of the model’s decisions for medical professionals. By incorporating this XAI methodology, our paper improves the trustworthiness, accountability, and fairness of the ensemble CNN model, ultimately aiding healthcare practitioners in validating the model’s output, revealing potential biases. This paper introduces a classification method based on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) for the detection of Diabetic Foot Ulcers (DFU). It employs a single CNN architecture that is trained using seven distinct optimizers: Adam, SGD, RMSprop, Adagrad, Adamax, Nadam and AdamW to evaluate their effects on the performance and generalization of the model.

In our paper, deep learning (DL) techniques are utilized to train and refine the CNN model, ensuring optimal detection of ulcers. The DFU dataset15, which comprises 1055 images, serves as the basis for training and evaluation. The objective is to identify the most effective optimizer that can achieve improved classification accuracy, stable convergence, and improved generalization in the context of medical image analysis. Related work

Numerous studies have investigated detecting and classifying diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) data, primarily aiming to distinguish between ulcerated and healthy skin. Several researchers have emphasised image processing and machine learning techniques, focusing on several visual attributes such as texture, colour, and/or structural patterns. The effectiveness of these approaches largely depends on the selected algorithms and training methodologies. In recent years, deep learning (DL) has become a dominant and extensively used technique for tasks involving medical image segmentation,16 classification, and detection. DL models have consistently outperformed traditional methods when applied to DFU images from patients with diabetes. Goyal et al.17 proposed DFUNet, a model based on convolutional neural networks (CNN) specifically aimed at differentiating diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) skin from normal skin. This model emphasized deep feature extraction and included cross-validation to guarantee strong performance. By utilizing the DFU dataset, DFUNet achieved an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 96.2%, surpassing conventional deep learning (DL) and machine learning (ML) methods. Wang et al.18 proposed a two-phase Support Vector Machine (SVM) strategy for detecting ulcers in DFU images. Their approach consisted of an initial segmentation phase followed by feature extraction, focusing on features derived from deep learning to effectively pinpoint ulcer locations. The model, when applied to the DFU dataset, reached a sensitivity of 73.3% and a specificity of 94.6%, indicating its capability to accurately identify healthy cases while maintaining moderate sensitivity to the presence of ulcers. Juan et al.19 presented DFU_VIRNet, a deep learning neural network specifically designed for the classification of DFU skin. This model highlighted the importance of feature and activation maps in precisely identifying areas susceptible to ulcers. Utilizing the DFU dataset, DFU_VIRNet attained outstanding results with an accuracy of 97.75%, an AUC of 0.993, a recall of 0.982, and an F1 score of 0.978. Alzubaidi et al.20 developed created DFU_QUTNet, a CNN-based architecture specifically aimed at classifying diabetic foot ulcers. The network prioritized increasing width over depth to improve gradient propagation and ensure error distribution across the channels of the network. With a dataset of 754 images featuring both normal and ulcer-affected feet, the model achieved a precision of 0.954, an F1 score of 0.945, and a recall of 0.936, demonstrating significant effectiveness in identifying DFU characteristics. Doulamis et al.21 presented an innovative, non-invasive photonic-based platform for monitoring DFU that utilized hyperspectral and thermal imaging. Their methodology employed deep learning techniques to improve pixel quality and implement super-resolution imaging, facilitating accurate biomarker monitoring in diabetic patients. The methodology proposed in the work differs with the traditional convolutional-neural-network (CNN) architectures in that it adds to its mix other forms of imaging that are not conventional. The model instead uses hyperspectral and thermal imaging instead of the RGB data. Hyperspectral imaging increases the frequency band below the visible light thereby providing intricate listing of the constituent of tissues as well as physiological changes. The system successfully predicted essential biomarkers such as deoxyhemoglobin and oxyhemoglobin, showcasing the potential of imaging-based monitoring in the clinical evaluation of DFU. Integration of eXplainable AI (XAI) techniques with CNN models for DFU prediction is a major advancement that can address the inherent “black box” problem of deep learning. Biswas et al.22 put forth XAI-FusionNet, a multi-scale feature fusion model that integrates explainability into DFU detection by employing SHAP, LIME, and Grad-CAM algorithms. This model merges pre-trained CNNs: DenseNet201, VGG19, and NASNetMobile with a meta-tuner module to optimize feature extraction and classification. Trained on an extensive DFU dataset consisting of 6963 images, XAI-FusionNet achieved an impressive accuracy of 98.97%, a precision of 99%, an F1 score of 98%, and a recall of 98%, while also offering transparency and interpretability through XAI methodologies. Further, Thotad et al.23 ] investigated the application of EfficientNet for the detection of diabetic foot ulcers (DFU), optimizing convolutional neural network (CNN) parameters such as depth, width, and resolution to attain high accuracy in the diagnosis of early-stage DFUs. Their methodology concentrated on maximizing the quality of feature extraction while ensuring computational efficiency. With a dataset comprising 844 foot images, the model achieved an accuracy of 97.02%, an F1 score of 96.18%, and an AUC of 98.60%, which is comparable to leading performance in the field. Proposing a unique approach experimenting with parallel CNN layers, Das et al.24 gave AESPNet, a CNN-based framework that integrates parallel convolutional layers with varying kernel sizes and bottleneck attention modules. This design was intended to improve feature extraction for the classification of DFUs. Utilizing the DFU dataset, AESPNet exhibited outstanding performance, reaching an accuracy of 97.02%, an F1 score of 96.18%, and an AUC of 98.60%, demonstrating its robustness and efficiency in ulcer detection. Although the success of modern deep learning architectures has been empirically proven widely, this paper suggests an innovative framework which takes advantage of the Genetic Algorithm (GA) ensembling. The ensemble constructs many convolutional neural network (CNN) models systematically combining them and, therefore, generating a model significantly better than any independent element. Interpretability would also be preferred, and further XAI measures, based mostly on Grad-CAM, are employed to justify prediction outcomes. Methods and materials

Dataset description

The dataset utilized in this study was obtained from Kaggle and originally collected at the Diabetic Foot Centre of Nasiriyah Hospital in southern Iraq15,20. The dataset comprises labeled images categorized into two classes: diabetic foot ulcers (abnormal skin) and healthy skin (normal). After augmentation, a total of 6,164 images were obtained, ensuring a balanced distribution between the two classes. All images maintain a consistent resolution of 224\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}224 pixels, ensuring uniformity across the dataset. The dataset was then split into training, validation, and test subsets using a stratified split to preserve class proportions. The resulting counts were as follows: 5,110 training images, 842 validation images, and 212 test images. Before model training, the pixel intensities were normalized by dividing each pixel value by 255.0 to reduce computational complexity and ensure consistent data processing in all samples. To enhance dataset diversity and mitigate overfitting, data augmentation was implemented using ImageDataGenerator with transformations including 40 degree rotation, width and height shifts of up to 20%, shearing, zooming, and horizontal flipping. These augmentations expose the model to various input variations, thereby improving generalization capability. Following augmentation, the dataset was partitioned into three subsets using a stratified split to preserve class distribution across all splits: 70% for training, 10% for validation, and 20% for testing. Since patient identifiers were not available in the dataset, the division was performed at the image level, which is acknowledged as a limitation of this study. The test set was left unaugmented to provide a clean benchmark for model evaluation. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs)

CNNs have emerged as a reliable approach for early detection, classification, and segmentation of diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), contributing to the prevention of severe complications such as infections and amputations. Building upon their success in image-based tasks, CNNs designed for DFU analysis comprise several specialized layers, each serving distinct computational purposes with specific mathematical operations.

Ensemble of CNN models

Ensemble learning is a collective prediction approach that combines multiple models trained on the same dataset to enhance predictive performance and generalizability31. The fundamental principle leverages the diversity of base learners, where the strengths of one model compensate for the weaknesses of another, resulting in reduced error rates and improved robustness. For DFU classification, we used a Genetic Algorithm (GA) based ensemble selection technique to identify the optimal subset of CNN models, each trained with different optimization algorithms. Unlike traditional weighted ensemble methods that assign varying importance to individual models, our approach selects a model subset and employs majority voting for final classification. To enhance the interpretability of the model and provide clinical insights into the decision making process, eXplainable AI (XAI) techniques were implemented using Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM). Proposed methodology

The proposed methodology employs a custom-designed Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architecture specifically developed for binary classification of Diabetic Foot Ulcer (DFU) images. The framework begins with input preprocessing, where all images are resized to a standardized dimension of 224\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}224\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}3 pixels and normalized by scaling the pixel values to the range [0,1] by division by 255 to ensure numerical stability and accelerate convergence during training. The CNN architecture consists of multiple Conv2D layers with progressively increasing filter sizes (32, 64, and 128 filters), each utilizing 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}3 kernels and ReLU activation functions to capture hierarchical features from input data, followed by MaxPooling2D layers with 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}2 pooling windows that reduce spatial dimensions while preserving essential information. After convolutional and pooling operations, the resulting feature maps are flattened into a one-dimensional vector and passed through fully connected dense layers containing 128 neurons with ReLU activation to learn abstract representations of high level. To avoid overfitting, a dropout layer with a dropout rate of 0.5 is incorporated during training, followed by a final output layer containing a single neuron with sigmoid activation that produces probability values for binary classification, effectively distinguishing between healthy skin and ulcerated tissue.

Multi-optimizer CNN training

To enhance model performance and explore diverse convergence behaviors, seven independent CNN models were trained using different optimization algorithms: Adam, SGD, RMSprop, Adagrad, Adamax, Nadam, and AdamW. Each optimizer employs distinct gradient update rules and adaptive learning mechanisms, contributing to varied convergence patterns and classification accuracies. The performance of the model was evaluated using accuracy, precision, loss curves, confusion matrices, ROC curves, and F1 scores to identify the most effective optimization strategies for detection of DFU.

Genetic algorithm-based ensemble selection

A genetic algorithm (GA)-based optimization strategy was employed to identify the optimal subset of CNN models for the ensemble classification of images of diabetic foot ulcers (DFU). The objective of the GA is to select a subset of pretrained CNN models whose majority-voted ensemble maximizes validation accuracy (fitness). The GA operates on binary selection vectors encoding inclusion/exclusion for each candidate model. Model selection during GA optimization was performed using a dedicated validation split (held-out from training) to avoid training-test leakage: all base CNNs were trained on the training partition, GA fitness evaluation used the validation partition, and final reported performance was measured on an independent test partition. To ensure robust assessment and avoid optimistic model selection, we used a nested evaluation scheme: the outer loop provides the final test folds, while the inner loop (or a fixed validation split within each outer fold) is used for GA-based model selection and hyperparameter tuning. Thus, the GA never directly optimizes on the held-out test data. Each model in the candidate pool was encoded as a binary decision variable, where a value of one indicated inclusion in the ensemble and zero indicated exclusion. The GA searched for the subset of models that maximized validation accuracy, formally expressed as:41\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} M^{*}_{GA} = \arg \max _{M_{GA} \subseteq M} \; \mathcal {A}\left( \{\hat{y}_i \; | \; i \in M_{GA}\}, Y_{\text {val}}\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {A}$$\end{document} denotes the validation accuracy, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}_i$$\end{document} represents the prediction of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i^{th}$$\end{document} CNN model, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_{\text {val}}$$\end{document} is the corresponding ground truth.

The GA was implemented using the DEAP framework, initialized with a population of randomly generated candidate subsets. The ensemble predictions were obtained by aggregating the results of the selected models via majority voting, where the individual model outputs were binarized (threshold = 0.5), and the ensemble decision was determined as the rounded mean. Evolutionary operators included two-point crossover for recombination, bit-flip mutation for diversity, and tournament selection to propagate the fittest individuals. The fitness function was defined as a weighted combination of F1-score and ROC-AUC to balance precision-recall trade-offs. The algorithm was executed for 100 generations with a population size of 20, a crossover probability of 0.8, and a mutation probability of 0.1, which provided convergence without overfitting.

Upon convergence, the optimal model subset (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M^{*}_{GA}$$\end{document}) was used to form the final ensemble. The GA-based ensemble demonstrated improved performance compared to individual CNNs and naive top-k combinations, effectively balancing precision, recall, and robustness while minimizing redundancy among the selected models. algorithm was executed with a population size of 10 over 20 generations, after which the best performing ensemble was retained.

XAI results

Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) provides crucial interpretability for our DFU detection model by visualizing which regions of input images most significantly influence CNN’s classification decisions, computing gradients flowing into the final convolutional layer to generate heatmaps that highlight discriminative areas and offer clinicians insight into the model’s decision-making process. Our Grad-CAM implementation reveals distinct activation patterns in which abnormal (ulcer) cases demonstrate strong activation over pathological features, including ulcer boundaries, tissue discoloration, erythema, necrotic tissue regions, and inflammatory markers, while normal (healthy) cases show uniform, low intensity activation in intact skin regions without the lack of concentrated attention to pathological features. Figure 13 presents representative Grad-CAM heatmaps generated by our GA ensemble model, with red-to-yellow color mapping indicating regions of high importance and blue regions representing areas with minimal influence on classification decisions, validating that our model focuses on clinically significant anatomical structures and pathological indicators rather than background artifacts. The integration of Grad-CAM with our GA ensemble model provides improved diagnostic confidence through visual validation, educational value for healthcare professionals, identification of potential model biases, and support for clinical decision making by highlighting areas requiring closer examination, effectively bridging the gap between complex AI models and practical clinical application to make our DFU detection system more trustworthy and actionable for healthcare professionals.Fig. 13Grad-CAM heatmaps showing model attention for DFU classification with normal and ulcer cases.

Limitations and future work

Despite its promising results, this work has several limitations. Although augmentation expanded the dataset, the number of original samples remains relatively small and may not fully capture the variability observed in real-world clinical cases. The study was conducted at the image level without incorporating patient metadata (e.g., age, comorbidities, or ulcer history), which could improve diagnostic reliability. Additionally, no external validation was performed using independent datasets or evaluation in real clinical settings, which limits the assessment of generalizability.

Future work will focus on expanding validation using cross-hospital and multi-center DFU datasets to enhance generalizability and ensure data diversity. Incorporating patient-level identifiers will enable subject-held-out or cross-site splitting to strengthen split hygiene and reduce the risk of information overlap. Further, model interpretability will be enhanced by performing Grad-CAM sanity checks with regional masking to verify that attention maps correspond to clinically relevant ulcer areas25. The evaluation framework will also include worst-group performance metrics to ensure model fairness and robustness across subpopulations.

In addition, future research will explore the integration of patient specific clinical parameters such as age, gender, diabetes duration, and comorbidities with imaging features to provide a multimodal diagnostic perspective. Extending the approach to object detection architectures (e.g., YOLO, Faster R-CNN) with annotated lesion regions will allow simultaneous localization and classification. Comprehensive sensitivity analyses of the GA parameters (e.g., population size, crossover rate, mutation probability, and number of generations) will be conducted to evaluate ensemble robustness. Finally, embedding advanced explainable AI (XAI) methods beyond Grad-CAM and integrating the model into clinical decision support systems remain key directions for facilitating real-world clinical deployment.

Conclusion

This study presented a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architecture trained with seven different optimizers: Adam, SGD, RMSprop, Adagrad, Adamax, Nadam, and AdamW for the accurate classification of diabetic foot ulcers (DFU). The systematic evaluation of these optimizers demonstrated that the optimizer choice plays a crucial role in improving accuracy, convergence, and generalization in medical image analysis. Data augmentation strategies further improved the robustness of the model by mitigating the challenges of limited data set size. To improve predictive performance, a genetic algorithm-based ensemble strategy was employed, integrating the best performing models into a strong ensemble that achieved 97% accuracy, 99% recall, and 97% F1 score. Furthermore, Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) methods, such as Grad-CAM, were applied to highlight discriminative regions in input images, thus improving transparency and clinical trust. Beyond technical performance, ethical and clinical deployment considerations are essential for the responsible use of AI in healthcare. The deployment of DFU detection models must ensure patient privacy, data security, and clinical accountability. Interpretability through Grad-CAM can assist clinicians in understanding and verifying the model’s decisions, supporting its integration into clinical workflows. Future deployment should involve rigorous validation across diverse patient populations, integration with electronic health record (EHR) systems, and adherence to medical device regulatory standards. By combining optimizer analysis, ensemble learning, and interpretability techniques, this research provides a reliable and high-performing solution for early DFU detection. With appropriate clinical validation and ethical safeguards, the proposed framework has strong potential for real-world integration as a decision-support tool to assist healthcare professionals in reducing the risk of severe complications such as infection and amputation.
