Comparative validation of intraoperative ultrasound versus intraoperative cholangiography for the detection of choledocholithiasis
Authors: Luis Tresierra, Helena Carmen Gómez Facundo, Camilo Andrés López Arevalo, Andrea Sanz Llorente, Juan Carlos Castaño Ortega, Sergio González Martínez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntraoperative cholangiography (IOC) is widely used for biliary tract evaluation during laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Intraoperative ultrasound (IOUS), available for over two decades as a radiation-free alternative, remains underutilized. This study aimed to validate the diagnostic performance and clinical applicability of IOUS compared to IOC in identifying choledocholithiasis and delineating biliary anatomy.MethodsA prospective, single-center study included patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy with intermediate or high risk of choledocholithiasis according to the 2019 ASGE criteria. All patients underwent both IOUS and IOC. Diagnostic accuracy, procedure duration, and intraoperative findings were analyzed using IOC as the reference standard.ResultsThirty patients were included. IOUS successfully visualized the biliary anatomy in 96.7% of cases. Ten patients (33.3%) had choledocholithiasis or biliary sludge. IOUS identified 9 of these cases, with a sensitivity 90.0% (95% CI 55.5–99.7) specificity 100.0% (95% CI 83.2–100.0) while IOC detected 7, sensitivity 70.0% (95% CI 34.8–93.3) specificity 100.0% (95% CI 83.2–100.0). The mean procedure time was significantly shorter for IOUS (5.6 min) than for IOC (12.5 min; p < 0.0001). No postoperative complications or readmissions occurred.ConclusionsIOUS demonstrated favorable diagnostic performance and shorter procedure times compared to IOC, suggesting that it may represent a reliable intraoperative imaging option in appropriately trained surgical teams.Keywords: Choledocolithiasis, Intraoperative ultrasound, Intraoperative cholangiography, Cholangitis

Introduction

Choledocholithiasis, defined as the presence of gallstones within the common bile duct (CBD), occurs in approximately 10–20% of patients undergoing cholecystectomy and may lead to serious complications including obstructive jaundice, acute cholangitis, biliary pancreatitis, and sepsis [1–4]. Accurate intraoperative identification and management of CBD stones is therefore essential to prevent postoperative morbidity, additional interventions, and healthcare burden.

The widespread adoption of laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) as the gold standard for symptomatic cholelithiasis has transformed biliary surgery. However, the transition to minimally invasive techniques has reduced tactile feedback, increasing reliance on imaging modalities for intraoperative assessment of biliary anatomy [5]. Preoperative imaging methods such as magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) and endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) play a central role in risk stratification, yet intraoperative evaluation remains crucial for confirming anatomy and detecting occult choledocholithiasis [6–8].

Intraoperative cholangiography (IOC) has traditionally been regarded as the reference standard for biliary tree evaluation. It provides real-time fluoroscopic visualization following cystic duct cannulation and contrast injection, allowing identification of ductal anatomy, filling defects suggestive of stones, and anatomical variations [9]. Reported sensitivities range from 75% to 87%, with specificities approaching 98% [10, 11]. IOC is particularly useful in patients with inconclusive preoperative imaging or those at elevated risk of CBD stones. Nevertheless, it requires dissection and cannulation of the cystic duct, prolongs operative time, involves ionizing radiation exposure, and may be technically challenging in the presence of inflammation or fibrosis [12–14]. Consequently, its routine versus selective use remains a subject of ongoing debate.

Intraoperative ultrasound (IOUS) has emerged as a radiation-free alternative that enables real-time visualization of the biliary tree without the need for duct cannulation [15]. Previous studies have reported sensitivity ranging from 76% to 100% and specificity from 96% to 100% for the detection of choledocholithiasis [16, 17]. IOUS can be performed before dissection of Calot’s triangle and repeated as needed during the procedure, offering flexibility and potential advantages in complex or high-risk cases. Meta-analyses have also demonstrated shorter imaging times for IOUS compared to IOC [4]. Additionally, IOUS allows identification of adjacent vascular structures, which may enhance intraoperative safety [1, 18].

Despite these advantages, IOUS is operator-dependent and requires dedicated training, which has limited its widespread implementation. Interpretation of ultrasonographic findings may be challenging for less experienced surgeons, particularly in cases with severe inflammation or poor acoustic windows [19, 20]. As a result, although IOUS has been available for decades, its adoption remains inconsistent across institutions.

Although numerous studies have compared IOC and IOUS, no global consensus has been reached regarding the optimal intraoperative modality for biliary evaluation during LC. While IOC continues to be widely used as the conventional reference technique, increasing evidence suggests that IOUS may offer comparable diagnostic performance with potential practical advantages, including reduced operative time and absence of radiation exposure.

Therefore, the aim of this prospective study was to compare intraoperative ultrasound (IOUS) and intraoperative cholangiography (IOC) for visualization of the biliary tree and detection of common bile duct stones during laparoscopic cholecystectomy, and to evaluate whether IOUS may represent a reliable intraoperative alternative in appropriately selected patients.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a prospective, single-center, comparative study at Sant Joan Despí Moisès Broggi Hospital, including patients scheduled for laparoscopic cholecystectomy with a preoperative suspicion of choledocholithiasis. All procedures were performed by six primary surgeons from the hepatobiliary team, each with established experience in laparoscopic cholecystectomy and intraoperative biliary imaging, between June 2023 and November 2024.

Preoperative assessment

Preoperative imaging was performed according to clinical presentation and biochemical findings in accordance with the 2019 ASGE risk stratification guidelines. MRCP was carried out in patients classified as high probability for choledocholithiasis according to ASGE criteria.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were informed about the nature and purpose of the study and voluntarily provided written informed consent prior to inclusion. Patient confidentiality and data anonymity were strictly maintained throughout the study.

Surgical procedure

A french approach for laparoscopic cholecystectomy was used [22, 23]. After standard laparoscopic access was established, IOUS was performed using a laparoscopic linear ultrasound probe (L44La Hitachi). The following structures were assessed: the common hepatic duct up to the cystic duct insertion and the CBD along its entire length including the ampullary region. We recorded the presence of biliary tract dilation and the presence of stones and/or biliary sludge.

In intraoperative ultrasound (IOUS), biliary stones typically appear as hyperechoic structures within the common bile duct, often accompanied by posterior acoustic shadowing. In contrast, biliary sludge is usually seen as mobile, low-level echogenic material without acoustic shadowing [24].

Following IOUS, IOC was systematically performed using a transcystic approach. Intraoperative cholangiography (IOC) was performed using iodinated contrast diluted to 50%, which allows adequate opacification of the biliary tree while minimizing image saturation. On IOC, filling defects within the contrast-filled ducts suggest the presence of stones or sludge. These defects may vary in shape and density, and their interpretation depends on image quality and contrast flow [25].

The IOC served as the reference standard for evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of IOUS. Visualization quality, presence of filling defects, and CBD dilation were documented. In cases where choledocholithiasis was confirmed, the surgical team proceeded with bile duct exploration as appropriate, either via flushing, transcystic extraction, or choledochotomy. Any adjunctive therapeutic measures, including selective administration of intravenous glucagon to facilitate bile duct clearance, were performed only after completion of both IOUS and IOC. Careful evaluation of the biliary anatomy and dynamic imaging was essential in both techniques to differentiate true positive findings from artifacts.

Data collection and outcomes

The following variables were collected: patient demographics, evidence of biliary duct dilation on preoperative imaging, results of IOUS and IOC, procedure times for both techniques, postoperative complications, and 30-day readmissions due to biliary-related events.

The primary outcome was to evaluate the diagnostic performance of IOUS in detecting choledocholithiasis, specifically its sensitivity and specificity, using IOC as the reference standard. Secondary outcomes included a comparison of operative times between both techniques and an assessment of postoperative morbidity.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as means ± standard deviation or as medians with interquartile range (IQR), depending on their distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The diagnostic performance of IOUS was evaluated in terms of sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), using IOC as the reference standard. The McNemar test was applied to compare paired proportions. Additionally, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess differences in operative times between IOUS and IOC. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 30 patients were included in the study, of which 18 (60.0%) were women and 12 (40.0%) were men. The overall mean age was 54.1 years (range: 26–82), with women averaging 50.8 years and men 58.9 years. All patients underwent laparoscopic elective cholecystectomy with intraoperative evaluation of the biliary tree using both IOUS and intraoperative IOC.

The most common preoperative diagnosis was acute biliary pancreatitis, observed in 25 patients (83.3%). Moreover, mild cholangitis, resolved prior to surgery, was identified in 3 patients (10.0%), while obstructive jaundice was observed in 2 patients (6.7%).

Preoperative imaging findings

Preoperative abdominal ultrasound was performed in 29 of the 30 patients (96.7%). Among these, dilatation of the common bile duct (CBD) was identified in 4 patients (13.8%), while no cases of choledocholithiasis were detected. In one patient, MRCP was performed as the initial imaging modality due to diagnostic considerations, and no preoperative ultrasound was conducted. Additionally, MRCP was performed in 12 patients (40.0%). MRCP revealed CBD dilatation in 2 cases (16.7%) and suspected choledocholithiasis in 5 cases (41.7%).

Intraoperative findings

Operative time

The total operative time ranged from 45 to 281 min, with a mean of 112.5 min. The duration of IOUS ranged from 3.0 to 15.0 min (mean: 5.6), while IOC required between 5.2 and 26.4 min (mean: 12.5). A Wilcoxon signed-rank test confirmed this difference as statistically significant (p < 0.0001).

Biliary tree visualization

IOUS successfully visualized the biliary anatomy in 29 out of 30 patients (96.7%), while IOC achieved full visualization in 100% of cases. The only instance in which IOUS failed to provide complete visualization of the biliary tree occurred in a patient with acute pancreatitis and pancreatic edema, which caused slight compression of the common bile duct wall, interfering with proper visualization of the intrapancreatic segment (Fig. 1).

Detection of choledocholithiasis

A total of 10 patients (33.3%) were found to have common bile duct occupation, 7 with choledocholithiasis and 3 with biliary sludge. IOUS identified 9 of these 10 cases, including 7 with ultrasonographic features consistent with bile duct stones and 2 with mobile, hypoechoic material compatible with biliary sludge. The only case not detected by IOUS involved a small sludge deposit located adjacent to the muscular complex of the ampulla of Vater, which likely limited sonographic visualization [25]. In comparison, IOC detected 7 of the 10 cases, correctly identifying 6 patients with choledocholithiasis and 1 with impacted sludge at the papilla. IOC failed to visualize two cases of sludge in the mid-CBD and one small stone within the bile duct (Fig. 2). Based on these findings, IOUS demonstrated a sensitivity of 90.0% (95% CI 55.5–99.7) and specificity of 100.0% (95% CI 83.2–100.0) while IOC showed a sensitivity of 70.0% (95% CI 34.8–93.3) and a specificity of 100.0% (95% CI 83.2–100.0), with no false positives observed (Table 1).

In cases of discordance between IOUS and IOC, intraoperative findings were correlated with therapeutic intervention and documented duct clearance. These cases were supported by successful intraoperative management and absence of residual stones in the postoperative period. Although IOC was used as the statistical reference standard, its known limitations in detecting small or mobile intraductal contents were evident in this study (IOUS: Intraoperative ultrasound, IOC: Intraoperative cholangiography).

Treatment of Choledocholithiasis

Among the patients in whom choledocholithiasis was identified intraoperatively, a tailored therapeutic approach was employed depending on the nature of the biliary findings, the size and number of stones, and the surgeon’s intraoperative judgment. All interventions were performed during the same surgical procedure, and no patient required conversion to open surgery.

Flushing of the common bile duct was sufficient to clear the biliary tract in 6 patients, representing the most frequently used technique. This approach was typically applied in cases with small or mobile stones and in the presence of biliary sludge. In selected cases, intravenous glucagon was administered after completion of IOUS and IOC to promote relaxation of the sphincter of Oddi and facilitate bile duct clearance. Postoperative outcomes

No postoperative complications were observed in this cohort. Specifically, no cases of bile leakage, infection, or bleeding were reported. Furthermore, there were no readmissions within the 30-day postoperative period due to biliary causes.

Discussion

The optimal approach for intraoperative evaluation of the biliary tree during laparoscopic cholecystectomy continues to be debated. Intraoperative ultrasound (IOUS) and intraoperative cholangiography (IOC) are the two most commonly employed techniques, each with distinct advantages and limitations. In this prospective, single-center study, we directly compared IOUS and IOC in patients classified as intermediate or high risk for choledocholithiasis according to the 2019 ASGE guidelines. Our findings offer practical insight into the diagnostic performance and intraoperative utility of IOUS in real-world surgical settings [21].

In our study, IOUS demonstrated high diagnostic performance, with a sensitivity of 90% (95% CI 55.5–99.7) and a specificity of 100% (95% CI 83.2–100.0). The relatively wide confidence intervals further reflect the limited precision associated with the small sample size. A total of nine positive cases were identified by IOUS (seven with choledocholithiasis and two with biliary sludge), all of which were confirmed intraoperatively through clinical evaluation and surgical management. In contrast, IOC failed to detect two of these cases—one with biliary sludge and one with microlithiasis. This discrepancy is likely related to inherent technical limitations of IOC. Visualization of stones depends on the presence of a contrast “filling defect” within the duct. In cases involving very small stones or biliary sludge, the contrast may completely surround the material, making detection difficult or impossible. Additionally, small stones or sludge may have been displaced into the duodenum during contrast injection, rendering them invisible on fluoroscopy. These findings suggest that IOUS may provide improved detection of subtle intraductal findings in this cohort, although this observation should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size.

Beyond sensitivity and specificity, the predictive values observed in our study further support the potential clinical utility of IOUS. The positive predictive value (PPV) was 100% (95% CI 66.4–100), indicating that all patients identified with pathology by IOUS underwent appropriate intraoperative management. The negative predictive value (NPV) was 95.2% (95% CI 76.2–99.9), suggesting that IOUS may be a reliable tool for ruling out choledocholithiasis in this clinical setting, although these estimates should be interpreted with caution given the limited sample size.

IOUS achieved successful visualization of the extrahepatic biliary tree in 96.7% of patients, a rate consistent with previous literature. The only failure occurred in a patient with acute pancreatitis and extensive peripancreatic edema, highlighting one of the known limitations of ultrasound in the presence of severe inflammation or poor acoustic windows. In contrast, IOC achieved 100% visualization in our cohort. However, this technical completeness does not necessarily translate to diagnostic superiority, particularly when considering the limitations of IOC in detecting small or non-obstructive intraductal findings.

Nonetheless, IOUS provided valuable real-time visualization without requiring cystic duct cannulation or contrast administration, which not only avoids radiation exposure but also preserves the sterile field and reduces the risk of mechanical injury or catheter misplacement. The anatomical clarity offered by IOUS has been demonstrated to be sufficient in a variety of scenarios, including acute and chronic biliary disease [1, 18, 19].

Time efficiency is a critical consideration in modern surgical practice. Our results showed a significant difference in procedure duration between IOUS and IOC. The average time for IOUS was 5.6 min (median: 5.3), while IOC required 12.5 min on average (median: 9.7). These findings are in line with those of multiple studies showing that IOC adds 8–15 min to operative time, depending on technique and experience. Faster imaging with IOUS may translate into reduced anesthesia time, less intraoperative stress, and potentially lower costs. These benefits are particularly relevant in resource-limited settings or in high-volume surgical centers, where operative time is a critical metric for efficiency.

All cases of choledocholithiasis identified intraoperatively were managed during the same surgical procedure using a stepwise approach-beginning with flushing, progressing to transcystic extraction, and resorting to choledochotomy only when necessary. IOUS played a central role not only in diagnosis but also in guiding these treatment decisions in real time. Notably, the identification of biliary sludge and small stones was resolved in most cases through simple flushing, without the need for more invasive techniques. This intraoperative adaptability represents one of the key advantages of IOUS, offering a level of dynamic surgical guidance that is not achievable with IOC alone.

No complications related to IOUS or IOC were recorded in our cohort. This is in agreement with published literature indicating that both techniques, when performed by experienced surgeons, are safe. However, it is important to remember that IOC carries additional risks associated with ductal cannulation, such as bile leakage or ductal injury, and involves radiation exposure.

One of the limitations often cited for IOUS is its operator dependency. In our case, the surgical team has several years of experience using IOUS for intraoperative visualization of the common bile duct, which likely contributed to the high rates of successful identification of biliary pathology observed in this study.

Despite the limitations inherent to the present study, particularly the relatively modest sample size and single-center design, our findings are consistent with previously published literature. However, these results should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger, multicenter cohorts.

Our data suggest that IOUS may represent a valuable intraoperative imaging option during laparoscopic cholecystectomy in patients at risk for choledocholithiasis, particularly within experienced surgical teams. While IOC remains an established modality in selected or complex cases, IOUS demonstrated favorable diagnostic performance and shorter operative times in this cohort.

Conclusion

In this prospective single-center study, IOUS appears to be a safe, rapid, and accurate technique for evaluating the biliary tree during laparoscopic cholecystectomy. In this single-center prospective cohort, IOUS demonstrated high sensitivity and favorable predictive values, along with significantly shorter procedure times compared to IOC. These findings suggest that IOUS may represent a reliable intraoperative imaging option in appropriately trained surgical teams. However, larger multicenter studies are required to confirm these results and further define its role in clinical practice.
