Leveraging Omics to Decode Oral and Systemic Health Linkages
Authors: Azeez Alade, Praveen Kumar Gajendrareddy, Veerajalandhar Allareddy, Veerasathpurush Allareddy
Published: 2026

Context

The oral cavity serves as a dynamic interface that is closely linked to overall systemic health. Over the past two decades, substantial evidence has shown strong connections between chronic oral inflammatory conditions, such as periodontal disease, and a wide range of systemic illnesses.1 These associations include cardiovascular disease, diabetes, Alzheimer’s disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and rheumatoid arthritis.1,2 Studies demonstrate that periodontitis significantly elevates cardiovascular risk, with an adjusted odds ratio of 1.34 for ischemic heart disease for at-risk individuals. These individuals also have an increased association with hypertension and atherosclerosis.3,4 A bidirectional relationship between metabolic disease and systemic conditions, such as type 2 diabetes, is also well documented. These conditions are known to alter the composition of the host microbiota. Additionally, periodontal dysbiosis is shown to exacerbate systemic metabolic dysfunction.1,5 Despite numerous studies demonstrating these associations, the exact biological mechanisms underlying them remain unclear.

A multitude of methodological and biological challenges hinder our ability to clearly resolve these associations. Diseases share many overlapping risk factors with systemic conditions. These include age, tobacco use and metabolic diseases, which serve as comorbidities in these conditions. This makes it difficult to isolate the independent biological influence of oral inflammation from the broader systemic dysfunction.3,6 Additionally, the clinical definition and classification of oral diseases, such as periodontitis, limit meaningful cross-study data analysis. In bidirectional conditions, such as type 2 diabetes, systemic, immune and metabolic disturbances simultaneously contribute to periodontal tissue breakdown and shifts in the microbial community.1 The field also lacks large scale human studies which are mechanistic and have tracked molecular and inflammatory pathways. Together, these limit epidemiological studies from being able to provide more meaningful data. Resolving gaps in our understanding of oral and systemic diseases will require a paradigm shift towards experimental approaches that can isolate specific pathways in disease pathogenesis. This will also necessitate a focused investigation of the microbiome, which interfaces with the host mucosal tissues.

During the past few years, there has been an increasing interest in precision/personalised healthcare. In this perspective, we argue that the current health ecosystem provides an ideal platform for healthcare providers and researchers to leverage the power of multi-omics, integrated health records and the analytical capabilities of artificial intelligence-based approaches to study the biological mechanisms underpinning oral and systemic health linkages. In the following narrative, we provide a high-level overview of the oral microbiome and how it influences systemic health, the evolving field of omics and their applications, costs and scalability of using omics, ethical challenges stemming from use of omics, applications and challenges of genomic variables and mendelian randomisation, and potential applications and challenges of using integrated health records to study oral and systemic health linkages.

Oral microbiome and systemic health

The oral microbiome presents as an active mechanistic interface between oral health status and systemic disease pathogenesis. The oral cavity houses a diverse microbial community. These comprise of bacteria, viruses, archae, fungi and protozoa.1,7

The predominant bacterial flora includes actinobacteria, fusobacteria, firmicutes and proteobacteria. These bacteria organise into structured site-specific communities whose composition is modulated by dietary pH and local environmental conditions.1 Under healthy conditions, this ecosystem regulates mucosal immunity and helps preserve barrier integrity. However, when the mucosal barrier is disrupted, they can promote chronic inflammation and systemic immune regulation.3,8 Dietary changes, environmental shifts and systemic immune suppression can rapidly induce dysbiosis. This dysbiosis is often characterised by a functional and compositional imbalance of microbiota. This facilitates the systemic dissemination of oral pathogens and their associated virulence factors.1

Once disseminated these microorganisms contribute to the pathogenesis of remote conditions. Specific oral microbiota such as P. Gingivalis have been identified in colorectal tumours, brain tissue of Alzheimer’s disease patients and atherosclerotic plaques. These findings indicate bacterial involvement in the progression of these conditions.1,9 Additionally, transcriptome analyses have demonstrated substantial changes in microbial metabolic activity in disease states. This fundamentally alters host-pathogen interactions and potentially downstream inflammatory signaling.5 Changes in the composition of the microbiome also promote systemic immune dysfunction. This contributes to chronic inflammatory changes that underlie conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity.3 Despite the plethora of studies that provide mechanistic insights, the inherently correlative nature of sequence-based profiling data makes establishing definitive causal relationships difficult. Longitudinal human trials designed to manipulate selective oral microbial communities also face considerable logistical and ethical constraints. Despite these challenges, the oral microbiome provides a biologically and clinically relevant avenue to study the oral-systemic axis. This possibility also provides an opportunity for the development of targeted investigations, such as novel biological models and pharmacological therapies. These can be used to selectively modulate the microbiome to overcome current translation barriers and study the biological basis of systemic inflammatory disease.

To address these mechanistic gaps, we propose a five-stage investigative pipeline. These include oral inflammatory phenotyping, multi-omics characterisation, integration of electronic health records, analyses and modelling and translational application. Omics

The suffix ‘-omics’ denotes the comprehensive, high-throughput analysis of a molecular class or biological layer, such as genes, transcripts, proteins, or metabolites.10,11 Genomics, the study of an organism’s complete genetic content, was transformed by the Human Genome Project, which provided a reference human genome sequence for genome-wide investigation of human genes and genetic variation.12 Although DNA sequence variants can influence gene structure and function, sequence information alone cannot explain how largely the same genome gives rise to distinct cell types, or why gene activity differs across tissues, developmental stages and biological conditions. In addition, host genomic data alone do not capture the microbial ecosystems that interact with host tissues. Microbiomics and metagenomics address this dimension by profiling microbial community composition and microbial genetic or functional potential, respectively. Large-scale human microbiome studies have shown that microbial communities vary across body habitats, including oral sites and possess distinct functional capacities.13

Beyond genetic sequence and microbial composition, gene activity is shaped by regulatory mechanisms. Epigenomics examines features such as DNA methylation, chromatin accessibility and histone modifications, which influence gene expression without altering the underlying DNA sequence.14 Because these features reflect regulatory state rather than direct gene output, transcriptomics provides a complementary layer by profiling RNA molecules expressed in a cell or tissue at a given time.15 RNA abundance, however, does not always predict protein abundance or function, as post-transcriptional regulation, translation efficiency and protein turnover also determine cellular protein levels.16 Proteomics captures this additional layer by measuring protein abundance, localisation, interactions and post-translational modifications.17

Although proteomics can provide insight into cellular function, protein abundance and modification status do not fully capture biochemical activity, which also depends on enzyme activity, substrate availability, nutrient status and metabolic context. Cost, scalability and infrastructure demands of multi-omics

While multi-omics holds great promise for improving our understanding of the oral-systemic link, its implementation on a large-scale remains limited by financial and computational demands.10,27 Integrating genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics and other omics approaches is resource-intensive, and generates large, multidimensional datasets that require substantial storage infrastructure and advanced computational resources for processing.27 Beyond cost and data complexity, analysis and interpretation of multi-omics data requires multidisciplinary expertise, including bioinformaticians, molecular biologists and clinicians.27 Such expertise and infrastructure are rarely available within a single research group. Consequently, large-scale multi-omics studies are typically conducted through collaborative consortia, where different research groups generate complementary omics datasets that are subsequently integrated for analysis.10 Large initiatives such as the UK Biobank and the All of Us Research Program have demonstrated the feasibility of integrating multi-layer molecular and clinical data within large population cohorts.28,29 However, because dental records are typically maintained separately from medical records, these resources have largely focused on medical phenotypes, with limited representation of dental and oral health phenotypes.

Data harmonisation and reproducibility challenges

A major challenge to translating multi-omics discoveries into clinical applications is the lack of harmonisation across studies.10 Multi-omics datasets are highly heterogeneous due to differences in laboratory protocols, sequencing platforms, preprocessing pipelines, normalisation methods and bioinformatic workflows.30 Within oral health research, additional variability arises from differences in biological specimens, including saliva, supragingival plaque, subgingival plaque, gingival crevicular fluid and oral tissue biopsies, each of which captures overlapping yet distinct biological processes.31 Furthermore, differences in disease definitions and participant characteristics, such as smoking status, medication use and oral hygiene practices, make comparisons across studies difficult.32

These methodological differences contribute to challenges with reproducibility in multi-omics studies.33 As a result, molecular signatures identified in one cohort may not replicate in independent populations.33 Therefore, efforts toward standardised sample collection protocols, harmonised metadata reporting, external validation cohorts and transparent bioinformatic workflows are important to enhance reproducibility. Emerging machine learning and artificial intelligence approaches provide opportunities for integrating data across platforms, although they require careful validation to avoid overfitting and ensure that the results are generalisable and reproducible.34

Integrated health records and data analytics

The advent of electronic health records systems and increasing interest in establishing large-scale biobanks in the pursuit of precision/personalised medicine have expedited discoveries and altered the healthcare research landscape.35 Integrated medical and dental records systems are being increasingly implemented at several large-scale healthcare organisations in the United States, and these endeavours facilitate oral and systemic health linkage research at scale.36,37

While there are numerous advantages of integrating health records to study oral and systemic health linkages, we have to keep in perspective the practical limitations and challenges of current integrated health records systems. Some of the major limitations of integrated health records include: the infrastructural requirements and substantial costs associated with implementation and maintenance; data quality issues arising from missing information, incorrect documentation/coding, and inconsistent data capture between different providers and electronic health records systems; increasing cybersecurity concerns; and lack of comprehensive data standardisation.38,39

During the past few years, there has been a tremendous infusion of artificial intelligence based analytical methods such as machine learning and deep learning into healthcare.40, 41, 42 These analytical approaches transcend the boundaries set by traditional statistical methods in terms of data analysis and assumption underlying the data. For example, multi-modal data (such as images, clinical narratives, genomics data, etc) can be analysed by artificial intelligence based methods. Large language models can analyse clinical narratives at scale. Several critical drawbacks of prior studies examining oral and systemic health linkages include omitted variable bias, inadequate explanation of reverse causality and endogeneity of explanatory variables. What has been established so far are only associations as opposed to a cause and effect relationship.

Omics methods and instrumental-variable analytical approaches can serve as a powerful framework for establishing the causal mechanisms underlying oral and systemic health linkages. Multi-omics methods help us to better understand the biological pathways connecting oral and systemic health. Instrumental variables estimate the causal effect of an exposure on an outcome when confounding or reverse causality is present.43 An ideal instrumental variable should be strongly associated with the exposure of interest, independent of factors that confound the exposure-outcome relationship and affect the outcome only through its effect on the exposure.44 Genetic variants that are identified through genome-wide studies can serve as powerful instrumental variables, as they precede disease onset and are not affected by disease status.45

Ethical considerations in biobanking and the use of genomic instrumental variables

The increasing use of biobanks and large-scale genomic repositories has introduced ethical considerations that extend beyond those typically encountered in conventional clinical research. Multi-omics studies often involve long-term storage of biological specimens and broad sharing of genomic and phenotypic data, requiring informed consent models that account for future research uses that may not have been anticipated at the time of enrollment.46 Because genomic data are inherently identifiable, concerns related to privacy, data security, and potential for re-identification remain important despite advances in data de-identification and anonymisation methods.47

Equally important is the issue of equity in genomic research. Most large-scale genomic resources have historically been dominated by populations of European ancestry, limiting the transferability of findings and potentially exacerbating health disparities.48 This limitation is particularly relevant in oral-systemic research because oral disease prevalence, microbiome composition and environmental exposures vary substantially across populations.49

Limitations of mendelian randomisation

Mendelian randomisation (MR) has become an important framework for investigating causal relationships and has more recently been applied to the oral-systemic link, particularly where randomised controlled trials are impractical or unethical.50,51 However, MR relies on several assumptions that are fundamental to its validity.52 One of the most important assumptions is that genetic instruments used as proxies for the exposure explain a sufficient proportion of the variability in the exposure of interest.52 In addition, these genetic variants should not be associated with potential confounders, and their effects on the outcome should occur only through the exposure of interest. Violations of these assumptions can introduce bias and affect the interpretation of MR findings.52

A common source of bias in MR studies is horizontal pleiotropy, which occurs when genetic instruments influence the outcome through pathways that are independent of the exposure of interest, thereby violating the exclusion restriction assumption.53 Methods such as MR-Egger regression, weighted median estimation, and MR-PRESSO can help detect potential pleiotropic effects and factor them into the interpretation of MR estimates.54 Other potential sources of bias in MR studies include weak instrumental variables, population stratification, and sample overlap between exposure and outcome datasets.55 Weak instruments reduce statistical power and can bias causal effect estimates toward the null. Instrument strength should therefore be assessed by calculating the F-statistic, with values >10 generally considered indicative of adequate instrument strength.56 Differences in ancestry across populations can introduce spurious associations if not adequately controlled for, as genetic variants and outcomes may vary for reasons unrelated to the exposure of interest.55 Therefore, exposure and outcome GWAS should ideally be derived from the same underlying population or from populations with similar ancestry.55 When exposure and outcome GWAS include overlapping individuals, the correlation between genetic associations may introduce bias into MR estimates, particularly when instruments are weak.57 To minimise this bias, researchers should exclude overlapping individuals whenever possible or apply methods that are robust to sample overlap.57 Additionally, MR findings should be interpreted alongside complementary approaches, such as colocalization and mediation analyses, which rely on different assumptions and can provide additional context for interpreting MR findings.58

Conclusions

Over the past few decades, substantial scholarly work has documented associations between oral health and a wide array of systemic conditions, including cardiovascular disease and diabetes. However, the causal mechanisms underlying these associations remain poorly understood. Recent advances in omics technologies, infusion of artificial intelligence based analytical approaches, establishment of large-scale biorepositories, advent of integrated health record systems, and use of instrumental variables present unprecedented opportunities for the scientific communities to elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying oral and systemic health linkages.

There is an urgent need for collaborative, data-driven research transcending disciplines to move beyond identifying associations to establishing a precise understanding of the connections between oral and systemic health. The first steps would be to establish uniform data collection standards, ensure representation of diverse population groups in healthcare datasets, promote interoperable data and harmonise analytical pipelines. Dentists have an important role in advancing research on the linkages between oral and systemic health. They can easily collect biological samples (such as saliva, oral tissues and oral microbiome) and champion the integration of dental and medical records. These activities can enable large-scale studies to examine shared molecular pathways across oral and systemic health.
