Genome-Wide Meta-QTL Analysis and InDel Markers for Salt Tolerance in Rice: Bridging the Gap from Genomics to Breeding
Authors: Raheleh Mirdar Mansuri, Amir‑Hossein Azizi, Hadi Darzi Ramandi, Parisa Daryani, Zahra-Sadat Shobbar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Salt stress is a primary abiotic constraint on global rice productivity. To establish a robust genomic framework for breeding, we performed a genome-wide meta-QTL (MQTL) analysis integrating QTL data from both the seedling and reproductive stages. A high-density consensus map was constructed, and initial QTLs from independent studies were curated and filtered for redundancy. Confidence intervals were refined via meta-analysis to define stable MQTL regions. From 926 original QTLs at the seedling stage, 87 seedling MQTLs (SeMQTLs) were identified. Notably, SeMQTL1-5, which encompasses the major locus Saltol, and three additional high-confidence SeMQTLs (CI < 1 cM, physical interval ≤ 1 Mb, average PVE > 17%) were associated with ion homeostasis under salt stress. At the reproductive stage, 43 reproductive MQTLs (ReMQTLs) were consolidated from 241 QTLs, with PVE values ranging from 4.84% to 46.36%. Subsequent analysis nominated MQTLSIH1-2 (the most stable region) and MQTLSIH1-5 (containing SKC1) as key candidates for seedling tolerance, while MQTLPF11-1 (PVE > 28%) was prioritized for reproductive-stage breeding. A total of 39 MQTLs co-localized with SNP-based selection hotspots. Within these regions, we developed 21 InDel markers, of which 18 were validated as effective allele-specific markers for discriminating salt tolerance. This work delivers the first consolidated set of high-confidence, MQTL-derived InDel markers for salt tolerance in rice, providing a valuable resource for marker-assisted selection and the pyramiding of resilience loci into elite genetic backgrounds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-026-00900-0.

Introduction

Agriculture currently encounters multiple challenges, including climate change, rapid population growth, and the constant reduction of cultivable land, all of which require urgent attention (Paul et al. 2020). Among these challenges, soil salinity is a major factor limiting soil productivity; it has been reported that nearly 50% of arable land is affected by salt stress. It is predicted that salt-affected soil will increase by up to four times by the year 2050, causing food insecurity worldwide (Tisarum et al. 2020). Consequently, enhancing the yield and quality of crops under salt stress has become a critical issue in modern agriculture (Zhao et al. 2021). Therefore, improving salt stress tolerance in crops is critical for ensuring stable food, feed, and industrial feedstock production on salt-affected lands (Kang et al. 2019).

Rice (Oryza sativa) is the major staple food for approximately half the world’s population. Its genomic features make rice a model plant for genetic research (Yu et al. 2021). Rice is considered a salt-sensitive major crop (Tin et al. 2021). Numerous studies have demonstrated that rice is extremely sensitive to salt during the seedling stage, while salt stress at the reproductive stage leads to substantial yield reductions (Razzaq et al. 2020; Tin et al. 2021). Therefore, the most cost-effective and feasible strategy is breeding for salt tolerance in rice genotypes to enhance productivity and ensure global food security (Qin et al. 2020).

However, salt tolerance is a complex trait controlled by multiple stress-responsive genes and their interactions (Lekklar et al. 2019). Moreover, the rice phenotypic response to salt stress is significantly affected by environmental conditions (Jahan et al. 2020). Consequently, progress in rice breeding for enhanced salt tolerance has been relatively slow (Jahan et al. 2020). Furthermore, the molecular and physiological mechanisms underlying salt tolerance in rice genotypes have not yet been fully elucidated. Understanding such mechanisms would be effective in enhancing sustainable agricultural productivity.

Various approaches have been employed to improve our knowledge of the genetic basis underlying rice responses to salt stress. Among these, quantitative trait loci (QTL) mapping has been widely used to dissect the genomic regions controlling salt-responsive traits, leading to the identification of numerous QTLs associated with salt tolerance (De Leon et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2017; Puram et al. 2018; Liang et al. 2015). Although QTL mapping has been broadly employed to dissect the genetic basis of rice responses to salt stress, its direct application in QTL cloning and marker-assisted selection (MAS) breeding is limited. This limitation occurs because the results of QTL mapping are strongly influenced by environmental conditions, the type and size of mapping population, the density of genetic markers and the statistical methods employed (Swamy et al. 2011).

In contrast, QTL meta-analysis is a promising approach to integrate QTL results from independent studies and identify their precise genomic positions. Meta-QTLs (MQTLs) with a narrow confidence interval (CI), high phenotypic variance explained (PVE), and large effect on a trait can serve as a significant reference for MAS (Swamy et al. 2011). Such MQTL regions provide a more precise shortlist of candidate genes. Although a few studies have identified MQTLs associated with salt tolerance, they have focused only on the seedling-stage (Islam et al. 2019) or did not dissect the reproductive stage (Mirdar Mansuri et al. 2020), and the number of initial QTLs included in those studies was relatively limited.

Recent research has focused on integrating physiological, molecular, and breeding approaches in rice to improve salt tolerance. Advanced molecular methods such as QTL mapping, GWAS, and genome editing (e.g., CRISPR‑Cas9) have been employed to recognize and manipulate key genes associated with ionic homeostasis, osmotic adjustment, and stress signaling pathways, leading to improved salt tolerance in rice cultivars (Li et al. 2025; Wei et al. 2025). For example, CRISPR/Cas9 as an advanced molecular technique, was used to understand the functions of stress-responsive genes, such as OsHKT1;5, OsSOS1and OsARF12 (Cheng et al. 2025).

In this study, the salt-tolerant line FL478 and the salt-sensitive cultivar Hashemi were selected as controls. FL478 is a standard reference genotype in salinity research, known for its well-characterized tolerance conferred by the Saltol QTL and its ability to maintain a high K⁺/Na⁺ ratio under stress. In contrast, Hashemi is a premium, market-preferred Iranian cultivar prized for its grain quality and aroma but documented to be relatively sensitive to abiotic stresses like salinity. This pairing provides a clear phenotypic and genetic contrastFL478 representing a known tolerance mechanism and Hashemi representing a high-value but stress-sensitive genetic background against which the responses of our experimental lines can be effectively evaluated. We previously integrated 265 QTLs from 15 published studies to identify hotspot regions associated with salt tolerance (Mirdar Mansuri et al. 2020). Herein, we conducted a comprehensive QTL meta-analysis using 58 and 17 mapping reports for the seedling and reproductive stages, respectively. We sought to find the precise genomic locations controlling key traits in response to salt stress. To achieve this, we performed a distinct MQTL analysis for each salt stress-responsive trait, enabling the identification of large-effect MQTLs with strong influence on these traits. Notably, we have and experimentally validated markers for the promising MQTL regions, demonstrating their ability to distinguish between tolerant (FL478) and sensitive (Hashemi) genotypes. This work addresses a critical gap in integrating MQTL mapping with genome-wide marker development for salinity tolerance in rice, providing a robust pipeline from QTL integration to marker validation. These markers offer applicable tools for marker-assisted selection and molecular breeding, accelerating the development of salt-tolerant rice varieties.

Results

Initial QTLs Distribution on Rice Chromosomes

A total of 1243 and 342 initial QTLs related to salt tolerance were collected from rice seedling and reproductive stages, respectively (Table S1). Next, the collected initial QTLs were filtered to eliminate those with a low R2 value (< 5%) and large CI (> 50 cM) after calculating new CI values. After filtering, the initial QTLs were grouped into 10 salt tolerance-related traits at each growth stage. Finally, 970 and 245 initial QTLs were selected for further analysis in the seedling and reproductive stages under salt conditions (Table S2). The QTLs from 58 independent studies for the seedling stage were derived from different populations backgrounds including Recombinant Inbred Lines (RIL), Backcrossed lines (BC), F2, F3, F4, Doubled Haploid (DH) and Backcross Inbred Line (BIL) (Table S2). Further, QTLs derived from diverse types of mapping populations including RIL, F2, DH, BC and BIL populations collected from 17 independent studies were utilized for meta-analysis in the reproductive stage (Table S2).

The distribution of the initial QTLs indicated that SIH and ST traits had the highest frequency of the initial QTLs, while SRL and PH had the lowest frequency of the initial QTLs (Fig. 1a). Development of a Rice Integrated Map and MQTL Analysis

A high-density genetic map was developed, and six previously referenced maps enabled the construction of a consensus map for the 12 rice chromosomes (Table S3). This consensus map had a total length of 1894.2 cM, ranging from 126.8 cM (Chr 7) to 220.5 cM (Chr 3), having a mean of 157.85 cM. The highest number of markers were observed on Chr 1 (975 markers) followed by Chr 3 (770 markers) and Chr 2 (685 markers). However, Chr 10 had the lowest number of markers (327 markers) on the consensus map. The marker density of the genetic map varied from 2.57 (Chr 9) to 5.03 (Chr 1) markers each cM, with an average of 3.51 per cM.

At the seedling stage, 926 initial QTLs out of 970 collected QTLs were significantly projected onto the high-density consensus map, and only the associated markers of 44 initial QTLs were absent from the consensus map (Table S4). The meta-analysis of 926 QTLs resulted in the identification of 87 SeMQTLs associated with different investigated traits at the seedling stage (Fig. 5a). The number of SeMQTLs per chromosome varied from 10 on chr1 and chr3 to 4 on chr12. The maximum projected initial QTLs were found on chr1: SeMQTL1-5 followed by SeMQTL1-10 and SeMQTL1-1, whereas SeMQTL2-5, SeMQTL7-3, SeMQTL9-3, and SeMQTL10-3 had the lowest number of the initial QTLs (Table S4). Remarkably, 73 SeMQTLs (83.9%) contained initial QTLs originating from three or more distinct studies with different genetic backgrounds. The PVE of the detected SeMQTLs varied from 5.4% (SeMQTL7-1) to 33.53% (SeMQTL10-2) with an average of 13.2%. The results showed that SeMQTL10-1 covered a narrow CI (0.01 cM), while the widest CI was found in SeMQTL5-5 with 10.89 cM. The average CI of the detected SeMQTLs was 2.73 cM, which is a reduction of 4.14 times compared to the average CI of the initial QTLs. Moreover, the physical distance of individual SeMQTLs ranged from 0.018 Mb (SeMQTL1-10) to 16.78 Mb (SeMQTL4-5). Among the 17 SeMQTLs with a CI of less than 1 cM, four SeMQTLs (including SeMQTL2-1, SeMQTL1-5, SeMQTL1-10 and SeMQTL1-1) had a physical interval ≤ 1 Mb and an average PVE greater than 17%. At the reproductive stage, 241 QTLs out of 245 initial QTLs were projected onto a consensus map, which led to the prediction of 43 ReMQTLs (Fig. 5b; Table S4). The highest number of ReMQTLs were identified on chr1 (n = 6), while only one ReMQTL was detected on chr9. The number of initial QTLs per chromosome ranged between two for ReMQTL1-4, ReMQTL3-2, ReMQTL3-4, Re MQTL4-3, ReMQTL6-4, ReMQTL11-1 and ReMQTL12-1, and 20 initial QTLs for ReMQTL7-3. Notably, 20 ReMQTLs (47%) with a stable effect from three or more separate studies were identified. The PVE for each ReMQTL ranged from 4.84% to 46.36% with an average of 16.12%. Exploring for Putative Genes within the MQTL Regions

A total of 9677 putative genes were identified in the 87 SeMQTL regions at the seedling stage (Table S4; Table S5). On average, 111 putative genes were found in the SeMQTL zones, with the lowest number in SeMQTL4-8 and the highest in SeMQTL6-5. The highest number of genes was observed on Chr6, while Chr10 had the lowest number of genes (Table S4 and S5).

At the reproductive stage, 8486 putative genes were detected in 43 ReMQTL regions with an average of 197 genes per ReMQTL (Table S4 and S6). The number of putative genes varied from four in ReMQTL3-2 to 614 in ReMQTL6-2. Moreover, chr8 had the most genes, while chr7 had the least number of putative genes with 380 (Table S4 and S6).

Identification of MQTL Regions Controlling Each Trait at the Seedling Stage

To better understand the genetic architecture of salt tolerance, a distinct MQTL analysis was performed based on 10 trait categories at the seedling stage. Among the investigated trait categories, ST and SIH possessed the highest number of MQTLs with 43 and 42, respectively. Meanwhile, PH with four MQTLs had the lowest number of MQTLs. (Table S7). The details of the ten separate QTL meta-analysis are presented below:

A total of 43 MQTL regions were identified for the ST trait, which were distributed on 12 chromosomes of rice. The number of MQTLs per chromosome ranged from two (chr4, chr6 andchr10) to six (chr1 and chr7). The PVE varied from 5% (MQTLST2-3) to 37.06% (MQTLST10-1), whereas the 95% CI ranged from 0.64 cM (MQTLST2-4) to 12.18 cM (MQTLST1-5) representing an average reduction of 4.17 times compared to the mean of the corresponding initial QTLs. A total of 5871 putative genes were observed within the MQTL regions, with the lowest and the highest number of genes in the MQTLST9-1 and MQTLST11-2 regions, respectively (Table S7).

Of the 248 projected QTLs, 42 MQTLs were detected for SIH trait. Chromosome 2 had the highest number of MQTLs, while chr9 and chr10 had only one MQTL. MQTLSIH1-2 was identified as the most stable genomic region, containing the highest number of projected initial QTLs (n = 59) from 13 independent studies. The designation of stability here primarily reflects the high repeatability of QTL detection across diverse genetic backgrounds and environments. The PVE of MQTLs varied from 6.9% (MQTLSIH12-3) to 44.77% (MQTLSIH8-4), while the 95% CI ranged from 0.31 cM (MQTLSIH1-5) to 23.96 cM (MQTLSIH8-1) representing an average reduction of 4.77 times. Remarkably, MQTLSIH1-5 contains SKC1 (located in Saltol region) having a PVE > 13% with 40.1 times lower than the 95% CI mean of the initial QTLs, making it a suitable region for salt tolerance breeding. A total of 7697 putative genes were identified in the MQTL intervals. On average, 183 putative genes were found in the MQTL regions, with the lowest number in MQTLSIH3-3 and the highest in MQTLSIH4-2 (Table S7).

Furthermore, 36 MQTL regions were identified for SI trait with the lowest (1) and highest (6) number of MQTL on chr9 and chr6, respectively. MQTLSI1-3 had the highest PVE, while MQTLSI6-1 had the lowest of PVE. The 95% CI of identified MQTLs varied between 0.91 cM (MQTLSI1-1) and 13.94 cM (MQTLSI5-4), which is sharply narrower (average reduction by 3 times) than the 95% CI of their original QTLs. A total of 36 MQTL regions encompassed 6006 putative genes, with the lowest and the highest number of genes in the MQTLSI12-2 and MQTLSI8-1 positions, respectively (Table S7).

Using meta-analysis, 33 MQTL regions were identified for the RT trait. The number of MQTL per chromosomes ranged from one (chr4, chr6, chr7, chr10 and chr12) to six (chr3). Of the identified MQTLs, 19 had PVE values greater than 10%, among these, MQTLRT10-1, MQTLRT4-1, MQTLRT12-1, MQTLRT3-5, MQTLRT2-1 and MQTLRT7-1 (20.4%) with PVE ≥ 20% were considered major-effect MQTLs. The 95% CI ranged from 1.07 cM (MQTLRT10-1) to 17.83 cM (MQTLRT3-6) with an average of 4.14 cM. The total number of putative genes within the physical intervals varied from 8 (MQTLRT3-1) to 860 (MQTLRT1-2), with a mean of 158 putative genes per MQTL (Table S7).

A total of 14 MQTL regions were identified for SRL trait, which were distributed on chromosomes 1, 2, 3, 4 and 8. Moreover, five MQTLs out of 14 identified MQTLs had the PVE ≥ 10%, of which MQTLSRL1-4 was considered a major-effect MQTL. The 95% CI of identified MQTLs was 1.4 (MQTLSRL8-1) to 13.17 (MQTLSRL2-4) times lower than the average 95% CI of the respective original QTLs. A total of 2924 putative genes were found within 14 MQTL regions. The highest number of putative genes was observed in MQTLSRL3-3, and the lowest in MQTLSRL2-4 (Table S7).

The MQTL analysis led to the identification of 13 MQTL regions for B trait, which were dispersed on seven chromosomes out of 12 chromosomes of rice. The PVE of the MQTLs ranged from 3.13% (MQTLB1-4) to 31.53% (MQTLB2-1). Further, the 95% CI ranged from 0.91 cM (MQTLB2-1) to 15.67 cM (MQTLB5-3) with an average reduction of 2 times compared to the mean of corresponding initial QTLs. Totally, 13 MQTL regions encompassed 2987 putative genes, with an average of 200 putative genes per MQTL (Table S7).

Meta-analysis resulted in the identification of 13 MQTL regions for CT trait on six chromosomes out of 12 chromosomes of rice. The PVE of identified MQTLs varied from 4.69% (MQTLCT2-3) to 16.71% (MQTLCT1-1), while the 95% CI ranged from 2.81 cM (MQTLCT1-1) to 16.7 cM (MQTLCT12-1) with an average reduction 1.89 times as compared to the mean of corresponding initial QTLs. The total number of putative genes within MQTLs was 3257 that varied from 12 in MQTLCT11-1 to 609 in MQTLCT6-1(Table S7).

Additionally, 12 MQTL regions for RIH trait were identified, which were distributed on seven chromosomes out of 12 chromosomes of rice. The greatest number of MQTLs were identified on chr1 (four), while only one MQTL was found on chr6, chr7, chr9 and chr11. The PVE of MQTLs ranged from 8.48 (MQTLRIH8-2) to 19.28% (MQTLRIH7-1) and the 95% CI ranged 3.33 cM (MQTLRIH1-1) to 12.72 cM (MQTLRIH11-1). The PVE of nine MQTLs (75%) was greater than 10%. Notably, MQTLRIH1-1 exhibiting the greatest number of initial QTLs (19) and MQTLRIH7-1, with a PVE of more than 19% were identified as stable and major-effect MQTLs. Totally, 12 MQTL regions encompassed 2449 putative genes, with the lowest (52) and the highest (570) number of genes in the MQTLRIH1-1 and MQTLRIH1-3 positions, respectively (Table S7).

A total of 12 MQTL regions were detected for SR trait on eight chromosomes out of 12 chromosomes of rice. The PVE of MQTLs varied from 7.6% (MQTLSR11-1) to 22.36% (MQTLSR4-1). The 95% CI ranged from 1.29 cM (MQTLSR4-1) to 7.28 cM (MQTLSR1-2) with an average reduction of 4.82 times as compared to the mean of corresponding initial QTLs. The results indicated that MQTLSR4-1 with the PVE ≥ 20% and MQTLSR5-1, which had the greatest number of initial QTLs from four independent studies, were recognized as major-effect and the most stable MQTLs for this trait. The total number of putative genes within MQTLs was 1372 that varied from 20 genes in MQTLSR4-1 to 363 genes in MQTLSR1-2 (Table S7).

In addition, four MQTLs were only found for PH trait on chr1, chr7 and chr12. The PVE of identified MQTLs varied from 10.5% (MQTLPH7-1) to 38.8% (MQTLPH1-2), while the 95% CI ranged from 2.89 cM (MQTLPH1-1) to 5.19 cM (MQTLPH12-1). The highest and lowest number of putative genes were found within MQTLPH1-1 (182) and MQTLPH1-2 (110), respectively (Table S7).

Identification of MQTL Regions Controlling Each Trait at the Reproductive Stage

A distinct meta-analysis of QTLs was performed for 9 out of 10 trait groups during the reproductive stage. The GY trait had the highest number of the identified MQTLs (16), while no MQTL exclusively grouped the initial QTLs related to SI trait. The highest number of GY- related MQTLs were observed on chr 6, while only one MQTL was detected on chromosomes 1, 2, 4, 8 and 9. The PVE of the MQTLs varied from 3.9% (MQTLGY4-1) to 35.73% (MQTLGY6-1). The 95% CI ranged from 6.45 cM (MQTLGY11-1) to 85.39 cM (MQTLGY6-3) with the average reduction 1.70 times as compared to the mean of corresponding initial QTLs. MQTLGY6-2 and MQTLGY6-1 had the highest and the lowest number of putative genes, respectively. Notably, MQTLGY6-1, MQTLGY10-1, MQTLGY11-1, MQTLGY11-2 and MQTLGY10-2 having the PVE ≥ 20% were considered as major-effect MQTLs on GY trait (Table S8).

Meta-analysis of the initial QTLs led to the identification of 10 MQTLs regions for the PT trait. There were two PT-associated MQTLs on chromosomes 1, 3 and 7, while only one MQTL was identified on chromosomes 2, 4, 9 and 10. MQTLPT7-1 with PVE ≥ 20% was a major-effect MQTL, while MQTLPT3-2 had the lowest PVE. The 95% CI ranged from 5.17 cM (MQTLPT7-1) to 26.65 cM (MQTLPT1-2) with an average reduction 9.09 times. The highest and the lowest number of putative genes were found within MQTLPT1-2 and MQTLPT7-1, respectively (Table S8).

A total of 10 MQTL regions were detected for HI trait on six chromosomes out of 12 chromosomes of rice; chr1 had the greatest number of MQTLs. MQTLHI3-2, MQTLHI8-1and MQTLHI2-1 were identified as major-effect MQTLs. The 95% CI ranged from 3.19 cM (MQTLHI8-1) to 14.61 cM (MQTLHI3-1) with the average reduction 2.19 times. The total number of putative genes within MQTLs was 3721 that varied from two in MQTLHI3-2 to 945 in MQTLHI4-1 (Table S8).

Among the eight PF-associated MQTLs, MQTLPF11-1 with the highest PVE (28.9%) and the lowest 95% CI (7.45 cM) was the most effective MQTL on PF trait. Remarkably, the average of 95% CI was reduced by 12.97-fold compared to that of corresponding initial QTLs in average. Totally, eight MQTL regions encompassed 3966 putative genes, with the lowest (98) and the highest (1136) number of genes in MQTLPF11-1 and MQTLPF6-1, respectively (Table S8).

Furthermore, four MQTL regions were detected on four chromosomes for PH trait; chr7 had the most MQTLs (Three), while only one MQTL was found on chr4. MQTLPH1-2, MQTLPH7-1, MQTLPH1-1 having PVE ≥ 20% were considered as the most effective MQTLs on PH trait. The 95% CI was different from 3.42 cM (MQTLPH1-2) to 23.23 cM (MQTLPH4-1). The highest and lowest number of putative genes were found within MQTLPH4-1 (795) and MQTLPH7-2 (171), respectively (Table S8).

Four MQTL regions were identified on chromosomes 4, 7, 10 and 11 for the HD trait using meta-analysis. Among the identified MQTLs, MQTLHD11-1 and MQTLHD10-1 had the highest PVE (27%) and the lowest CI (7 cM). The total number of putative genes within MQTLs was 1073 that varied from 116 in MQTLHD10-1 to 699 in MQTLHD7-1 (Table S8).

Meta-analysis generated three MQTL regions on chromosomes 2, 8 and 11 for the TN trait. The PVE of MQTLs varied from 11.15% (MQTLTN8-1) to 14.68% (MQTLTN2-1). The 95% CI ranged from 8.77 cM (MQTLTN2-1) to 21.13 cM (MQTLTN11-1). A total of 1278 putative genes were identified within these MQTL regions (Table S8).

Additionally, two MQTL regions were identified for B trait on chr6 and chr7, of which MQTLB7-1 had the highest PVE (12.44%) and the lowest CI (2.76 cM), encompassing 192 putative genes (Table S8).

Only one MQTL zone was found for LT trait on chr7, derived from three independent studies. MQTLLT7-1 had the PVE of 10.53% and the 95% CI of 4.19 cM, while it’s CI was by 9.52 times lower than the mean CI of the initial QTLs and it contained 35 putative genes (Table S8).

Identification of Real QTLs using QTL-Overview Index

To further explore genomic regions most involved in the salt stress response in rice, the QTL-overview index was computed for 970 and 241 QTLs projected onto the consensus map at the seedling and reproductive stages. A total of 97 peaks were identified, which were higher than 0.0186 as a threshold for the average overview index across the 12 chromosomes at the seedling stage. Therefore, these regions were considered as real QTLs, of which 15 peaks exceeded 0.0931 (the high-value threshold); these can be considered as hotspot regions. Moreover, the number of significant peaks ranged from one peak on chromosomes 3, 5, 6, 10, 11 and 12 to three peaks on chr1 (Fig. S1a).

A total of 26 peaks were significantly detected to be higher than 0.0038 (the genome-wide mean threshold) at the reproductive stage, which were recognized as real QTLs; among these, 24 peaks exceeded 0.0190 as the high-value threshold. These peaks can be considered as hotspot regions affecting salt stress tolerance in rice. The number of significant peaks varied from five peaks on chr1 to one peak on chromosomes 2, 4, 5, 9 and 12 (Fig. S1b).

Reliability Assessment of the MQTL-Derived Inferences through GWAS Studies

A total of 5701 Single-Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) from 16 previous studies were identified that were linked to RIH, SIH, RT, ST, PH, B, SR, SI and CT traits at the seedling stage. The results indicated that 76 out of 88 identified MQTLs were co-located with 1543 SNP peak locations discovered for the salt tolerance related traits; among these, 13 MQTLs were in hotspots regions overlapping with high-value threshold of overview index. Remarkably, 215 genes were located in SNP peak positions, among which 23 genes were in significant SNP peaks and QTL hotspots. These genes can be considered as candidates for further research (Table S9).

Moreover, several significant overlaps were found between the MQTLs and SNP peaks at the reproductive stage. From five previous GWAS studies, 1068 SNPs associated with the traits including B, IH, LT, PT, PH, SI and TN were identified. A total of 23 out of 43 identified MQTLs colocalized with 306 SNP peak positions; among them, 15 MQTLs were in hotspots regions overlapping with the high-value threshold of the overview index. Interestingly, 21 genes were located within SNP peak positions and could be suggested as promising candidate genes (Table S9).

Mining the Promising MQTLs for Breeding Purposes under Salt Stress

The identified MQTLs were further explored to pinpoint promising ones fulfilling the following conditions: overlap with SNP peak positions, low CI, high PVE, and control of important traits at both the seedling and reproductive stages. Additional criteria were also utilized for prioritizing, e.g., the inclusion of the Saltol locus at the seedling stage. Stringent filtering of the identified MQTLs left 15 and 24 hotspot regions as promising MQTLs for breeding at the seedling and reproductive stages, respectively (Table S10).

As mentioned, based on MQTL analysis, 87 and 43 MQTL regions were identified at the seedling and reproductive stages, respectively, for 10 trait categories. Comparative Assessments of Genomic Variants between FL478 and Hashemi Cultivars

A comparison between FL478 (salt-tolerant) and Hashemi (salt-sensitive) WGS revealed thousands of variants. Using the snpEff software, the types of all discovered variants were identified, determining the impact of each mutation on gene products. At the seedling and reproductive stages, 14,959 and 25,131 moderate-impact variants were found, respectively (Tables S11, S12, S13 and S14).

Comparison of high-impact variants between FL478 and Hashemi, showed 969 and 1,492 variants between these two genotypes, at the seedling and reproductive stages, respectively (Fig. S2). Among FL478 variants at the seedling stage, 976 were InDels and 865 were SNPs; most InDels (744) had loss-of-function (LOF) effects, impacting 573 genes, with 7 variants falling within promising MQTL regions (Table 2). For Hashemi, 511 InDels and 428 SNPs were identified; 380 InDels had LOF effects, impacting 924 genes, with 9 variants located in MQTL regions (Table 3). At the reproductive stage, in FL478, 1,888 InDels (mostly affecting protein-coding genes) included 1377 LOF and 25 NMD variants, impacting 913 genes, with 41 variants located in promising MQTL regions (Table 4). To develop cost-effective markers, large InDels within MQTL regions were prioritized for their suitability in agarose gel-based PCR detection. Sequences were retrieved and specific primers were designed (Table S15).

Experimental Validation of the Developed InDel Markers

All the primer pairs (Table S15) successfully amplified PCR products, and amplicons showed the expected band size on the agarose gel. Remarkably, 18 out of 21 InDel markers, including ReMQ11-2, ReMQ7-1, ReMQ10-1, ReMQ8-3, ReMQ3-2, ReMQ10-3, ReMQ6-1, SeMQ2-1, SeMQ10-2, SeMQ1-5-1, SeMO1-5-2, SeMQ4-1 (1), SeMQ4-1 (2), SeMQ3-1, SeMQ5-3, SeMQ11-2, SeMQ2-2, and SeMQ6-3, were polymorphic (Fig. 7). However, the markers with size differences between contrasting genotypes less than 15 bp (i.e., SeMQ1-5-1 with 13 bp, SeMQ4-1(1) with 13 bp, SeMQ6-3 with 11 bp) are less practical, as detection of the differences is difficult. Similarly, markers that produced bands in only one genotype (i.e., ReMQ6-1 and SeMQ4-1 (2)) are not recommended because they cannot detect heterozygotes. Therefore, we confirmed that 18 polymorphic InDel markers were able to distinguish between FL478 (salt-tolerant) and Hashemi (salt-sensitive), which have different genetic backgrounds. Discussion

Rice is a main component of global food security; however, increasing soil salinity represents a significant constraint on its sustainable production (Mirdar Mansuri et al., 2020). Progress in breeding for salt tolerance has been hindered by the limited availability of efficient molecular markers and the complex polygenic nature of this trait. In this study, we addressed these challenges through a comprehensive meta-QTL analysis across seedling and reproductive stages, identifying key genomic regions controlling salt-responsive traits and developing experimentally validated InDel markers. Unlike previous meta-analyses that focused solely on QTL integration, our work bridges the gap between genomic discovery and practical breeding by translating MQTL regions into functional markers validated in contrasting genotypes. Our work integrates a larger QTL collection than previous studies, performs both inclusive and trait-specific MQTL analyses, and delivers practical breeding tools collectively advancing both the mechanistic understanding and applied breeding of salt tolerance in rice.

Seedling-Stage Salt Tolerance Loci: Integration of Ion Homeostasis and Signaling Pathways

The inclusive MQTL analysis showed that chromosome 1 plays the most significant role in regulating salt tolerance at the seedling stage, and has the highest density of initial QTLs (Mirdar Mansuri et al. 2020; Kim et al., 2023). Notably, SeMQTL1-5 harbors the SKC1 locus which plays a key role in maintaining shoot Na+/K⁺ homeostasis (Ren et al., 2005). This MQTL encompassed 70 initial QTLs from 17 independent studies and was defined by a remarkably narrow confidence interval (0.58 cM). Furthermore, SeMQTL1-5 displayed the highest phenotypic variance explained (PVE) on chromosome 1 and contained 89 putative genes (Tables S8, S9). Beyond ion transport, SeMQTL1-5 integrates broader stress signaling networks. Under stress, ROS productions increases; these function as signaling molecules that activate stress response pathways, leading to the upregulation of ROS-scavenging enzymes like peroxidase (Kibria et al. 2017; Mirdar Mansuri et al. 2022). OsPERK1, which is found in this MQTL, belongs to the receptor-like protein kinase (RLK) family and shows similarity to the wall-associated kinase (WAK) subfamily. PERK-mediated signaling cascades play crucial roles in extracellular signal perception and transduction to the cell wall in both rice and Arabidopsis (Feng et al. 2019; Bai et al. 2009).

In our previous study, we identified pectinesterase as an extracellular component associated with the WAK family in the Saltol region. This protein may initiate downstream signaling cascades, potentially through a cytoplasmic serine/threonine kinase (Mirdar Mansuri et al. 2020). Furthermore, several genes encoding receptor protein kinases and WAKs were previously predicted to be targets of lncRNA.1, lncRNA.2, and lncRNA.4 in FL478 through lncRNA–miRNA interaction networks (Mirdar Mansuri et al. 2022). These results collectively demonstrate that SeMQTL1-5, encompassing the Saltol region, not only regulates Na⁺/K⁺ homeostasis but also plays a pivotal role in signal transduction (Fig. 8). The distinct QTL meta-analysis highlighted MQTLSIH1-2 as the most stable genomic region, while MQTLSIH1-5 harbored the well-characterized SKC1 locus on chromosome 1. Notably, Os01g0304100 (OsCCC2), encoding a cation-chloride cotransporter 2/ potassium-chloride cotransporter 2, located in MQTLSIH1-2, was identified as an ion transporter. CCC proteins mediate electroneutral Cl⁻ transport coupled with K⁺ and/or Na⁺ translocation (Henderson et al. 2018). This finding is mechanistically significant because it addresses Cl⁻ toxicity an often-overlooked component of salt stress complementing the Na⁺-centric tolerance conferred by SKC1. Precise regulation of Cl⁻ and K⁺ homeostasis, along with efficient Na⁺ efflux, is critical for plant salt acclimation, as excessive Cl⁻ accumulation is toxic and disrupts ion balance (Fig. 8) (Colmenero-Flores et al. 2007). Furthermore, we identified two transcription factors within MQTLSIH1-5: Os01g0865600 (encoding a BHLH protein) and Os01g0867300 (OsBZIP12), both known to mediate salt stress responses (Fig. 8) (Teng et al. 2022; Joo et al. 2014). Given these findings, we propose using these chromosome 1 loci in marker-assisted breeding programs to develop rice varieties with enhanced seedling-stage salt tolerance.

Reproductive-Stage Salt Tolerance and Yield-Related Loci: Protecting Grain Yield under Stress

Salt stress causes significant reductions in rice yield and yield components (Li et al. 2023a, b). Elucidating the molecular basis of salt stress responses is therefore essential for developing rice varieties with improved saline tolerance. Our analysis identified ReMQTL7-3 as containing the highest concentration of yield-related QTLs, with 40 putative genes (Table S8). Os07g0233300 encodes a PHD finger protein that regulates growth and development through transcriptional control and histone modification, known to confer salt tolerance (Wei et al. 2015). PHD finger proteins often act as epigenetic regulators, suggesting that ReMQTL7-3 may mediate chromatin-level adaptations to stress. Pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) proteins (Os07g0239600, Os07g0249100) mediate post-transcriptional regulation in organelles, showing roles in stress adaptation (Wang et al. 2021; Tan et al. 2014). The B3 domain transcription factor Os07g0232100 regulates grain formation and belongs to a superfamily that coordinates responses to multiple abiotic stresses through ABA signaling pathways (Kandeel et al. 2023). OsMTS1 (Os07g0247100), encoding a key melatonin biosynthesis enzyme, maintains cellular redox balance. Its disruption leads to premature senescence and yield reduction (Hong et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2019). OsAGPL4 (Os07g0243200), involved in starch biosynthesis, influences grain weight and quality. Salt stress alters starch properties, negatively affecting cooking characteristics (Bao et al. 2012; Li et al. 2023a, b). The Dof family protein Os07g0236700 integrates growth regulation with stress responses (Zou et al., 2023). These findings establish ReMQTL7-3 as a critical genomic region governing both yield potential and grain quality under salt stress conditions during reproductive growth. The identified genes represent valuable targets for molecular breeding programs aimed at enhancing salt stress tolerance in rice (Fig. 8).

Polygenic Complexity and Major-Effect Loci: Dissecting Quantitative Tolerance

The polygenic nature of salt tolerance, compounded by significant genotype-by-environment interactions, continues to pose major challenges for salt stress tolerance research (Atta et al. 2023). While identifying major-effect loci remains difficult, analysis of phenotypic variance explained (PVE) can reveal significant genomic regions. We identified six SeMQTLs (SeMQTL10-2, SeMQTL10-3, SeMQTL2-3, SeMQTL2-4, SeMQTL4-1 and SeMQTL2-1) with PVE > 20% (Table S8), including SeMQTL2-4, which harbored five major-effect QTLs (i.e., QSIH2_109, QSIH2_110, QSIH2_111, QSIH2_112 and QSIH2_113) that collectively explained substantial phenotypic variance in shoot ion homeostasis (SIH), a crucial salt-responsive trait. Notably, SeMQTL2-4 contains several functionally important genes, including Os02g0236200 (encoding Ser/Thr phosphatases), Os02g0241600 (CTR1, a negative regulator of ethylene signaling), and Os02g0241100 (a brassinosteroid LRR receptor kinase-like protein). These components of the ethylene signaling pathway were previously identified in FL478 through RNA-seq analysis (Mirdar Mansuri et al., 2020). Recent studies confirm that CTR1 phosphorylation activates ethylene signaling to enhance salt tolerance (Li et al. 2023a, b), while LRR-containing receptor kinases play well-established roles in stress signal transduction (Hong et al. 1997). Together, these findings suggest that SeMQTL2-4 might be involved in regulating ethylene-mediated salt stress responses during the seedling stage (Fig. 8). The identification of such multi-component signaling hubs within major-effect MQTLs provides mechanistic insight into how plants coordinate complex stress responses and offers breeders targets for pathway-level modification rather than single-gene approaches.

Several major-effect QTLs with PVE > 30% associated with yield components (e.g., QPF11_27, QGY11_49, QGY11_48, QGY6_24 and QGY6_23) were identified within ReMQTL11-2 and ReMQTL6-1. Detailed meta-analysis of QTLs associated with panicle fertility (PF) revealed that MQTLPF11-1 exhibited the highest PVE (28.9%) and encompassed 98 putative genes. Several transcription factors belonging to the MYB (Os11g0207600) and bZIP (Os11g0218000) families were identified within MQTLPF11-1 (Fig. 8). Among them, bZIP transcription factors play a significant role in regulating plant stress responses. These transcription factors regulate many biological processes, including seed maturation, flower development, dormancy, and senescence, particularly under abiotic stresses such as salt, drought, and osmotic stress (Liu et al. 2023). Furthermore, a gene (Os11g0216000) encoding Pyruvate kinase (OsPK5, OsPK10) was identified within this region, which plays crucial roles in seed germination, glycolytic metabolism, and GA/ABA balance. This enzyme significantly influences grain quality by modulating protein content, starch composition, and amylose levels. Disruption of OsPK5/OsPK10 reduces 1000-grain weight and seed setting rate (Dong et al. 2022), while salt stress directly impairs pyruvate kinase activity, leading to compromised grain filling (Hu et al. 2020). Thus, MQTLPF11-1 represents a direct link between stress-induced metabolic disruption and yield loss, making it a high-priority target for breeding programs.

Further analysis highlighted MQTLGY6-1 (PVE > 35%) as a key genomic region controlling grain yield under salt stress. This locus contains OsMADS5 (Os06g0162800), a MADS-box transcription factor essential for regulating panicle architecture and reproductive development (Zhu et al. 2022). Thus, MQTLPF11-1 and MQTLGY6-1 represent promising target regions for rice breeding programs aimed at improving grain quality and quantity under salt stress during the reproductive stage (Fig. 8).

Candidate Genes and Breeding Applications: from Discovery to Deployment

Conclusively, targeting SeMQTL1-5 (seedling tolerance), ReMQTL7-3 (yield stability), and MQTLPF11-1/MQTLGY6-1 (reproductive traits) may accelerate the development of salt-tolerant, high-yielding rice varieties (Fig. 8). Pyramiding these regions could achieve additive or synergistic effects.

Additionally, 22 genes were identified within significant SNP peaks and QTL hotspots (Table S9). Among these, Os01g0332200 in ReMQTL1-2 encodes GA2OX2, which in Arabidopsis thaliana is expressed in inflorescences and developing siliques where it reduces GA levels and promotes seed dormancy (Lo et al. 2008). Furthermore, Os10g0144700 encoding Cytochrome P450, was identified within SNP peaks. Cytochrome P450 enzymes play important roles in plant organ growth and grain yield enhancement, making them promising target for yield improvement in rice breeding programs (Usman et al. 2020).

Development of Gene-Based InDel Markers: Translating Genomics to Breeding

The accurate identification of efficient molecular markers is critical for accelerating rice breeding programs targeting improved salt tolerance. The development of co-dominant and cost-effective InDel markers can significantly enhance the efficiency of marker-assisted selection for identifying genotypes carrying salt tolerance genes. InDel polymorphisms can influence gene function, and their genomic density is higher than simple sequence repeats (SSRs), making them particularly valuable markers in genetic studies (Sathapondecha et al. 2023). Despite the aforementioned advantages, we identified InDel loci within the promising MQTL regions that are located in coding sequences directly associated with salt stress-responsive traits, increasing the likelihood that they tag causal variants rather than merely linked polymorphisms.

Our results highlight a set of hypothetical genes of unknown function that accumulate variants. These represent candidates for functional characterization. Future work should involve direct experimental validation, such as gene knockout studies using CRISPR/Cas9, transgenic overexpression in sensitive backgrounds, and field evaluation under controlled saline conditions, to definitively test their function for salt tolerance. The remaining InDels were found in genes encoding protein kinase and F-box proteins in FL478, and UDP-glucosyltransferase in Hashemi at the seedling stage. There are more than 1,000 protein kinases in the rice genome contains, which play significant roles in stress sensing, signal transduction, and cellular regulation (Dardick et al. 2007; Chen et al. 2021). These kinases are key regulators in responses to abiotic stress (Chen et al. 2021). Rice contains ~ 779 F-box genes, some of which play important roles in plant growth and development, particularly by modulating phytohormone signaling pathways (auxin, ethylene, GA, JA) under abiotic stress (Li et al. 2022). Moreover, we identified a 4 bp deletion in a UDP-glucosyltransferase-encoding gene (Os10g0331600) within SeMQTL10-2, in Hashemi. UDP-glucosyltransferases have been reported to play a role in plant responses to abiotic stresses. These enzymes interact with OsHOX22, a transcription factor that enhances abiotic stress tolerance. Homeobox TFs (such as OsHOX22 and OsHOX24) regulate plant development under abiotic stress (Bhattacharjee et al. 2016), and were among the most prevalent TF families in FL478 (Mirdar Mansuri et al. 2020).

Furthermore, the annotation of InDels/SNPs within coding sequence regions revealed distinctive variation patterns between FL478 and Hashemi within ReMQTLs. In terms of FL478-specific variations, a deletion was found in OsbHLH108 (Os06g0164400) within ReMQTL1-6, a gene associated with the OsMADS1 regulatory cascade which regulates carpel development and floral organ specification (Fig. 8) (Khanday et al. 2016). Moreover, a 4 bp insertion and single-base deletion in OsEPFL1 (RAE2; Os08g0485500) within ReMQTL8-3 were identified, encoding an epidermal patterning factor that regulates grain morphology and awn development (Jin et al. 2016; Bessho-Uehara et al. 2016). Molecular and genetic studies have demonstrated that EPFL positively regulates awn elongation (Bessho-Uehara et al. 2016).

A 4 bp deletion was identified in two proline-rich protein (PRP) genes (Os10g0149400, Os10g0149200) coding for proline-rich proteins (PRPs) within ReMQTL10-1. PRPs mediate stress responses, and their altered expression correlates with differential proline accumulation in sensitive versus tolerant genotype (Khedr et al. 2003; Bhusan et al. 2016). Insertions were identified in OSWAK103/104 (Os10g0151100/1500 in ReMQTL10-1). While WAK proteins contribute to stress responses, their specific role in salt tolerance remains undefined, though some family members affect fertility (Lin et al. 2021).

In the current study, we prioritized MQTL regions for marker development using a multi-criteria approach that included overlap with SNP peaks, the QTL-overview index, and high phenotypic variance (Table 1, Fig. 6). Within these regions, we designed InDel markers based on sequence variants between FL478 (salt-tolerant) and Hashemi (salt-sensitive). Accurate polymorphism detection proved critical for effective marker selection, and we successfully developed markers capable of resolving nucleotide differences using 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. The set of polymorphic InDel markers developed in this study effectively distinguished the Iranian local rice cultivar Hashemi, characterized as salt-sensitive, from FL478, which is salt-tolerant. The proposed InDel markers are user-friendly and hold significant potential to facilitate salt tolerance breeding programs in rice.

While the InDel markers developed in this study effectively distinguished the salt-tolerant FL478 from the salt-sensitive Hashemi, their broader applicability across diverse rice germplasm remains to be validated. Future studies should include larger panels of accessions with varying salt tolerance levels and diverse genetic backgrounds to assess marker robustness and to confirm the contribution of these MQTLs to salt tolerance. Such validation would strengthen the utility of these markers for marker-assisted selection in breeding programs.

Conclusion

This study successfully identified stable meta-QTLs (MQTLs) associated with salt tolerance at both the seedling and reproductive stages in rice, narrowing down critical genomic regions for marker-assisted selection (MAS). A high-density consensus genetic map enabled the detection of 87 SeMQTLs (seedling stage) and 43 ReMQTLs (reproductive stage), with several exhibiting high phenotypic variances explained (PVE) and narrow confidence intervals. Notably, key loci such as Saltol (SeMQTL1-5) and SKC1 (MQTLSIH1-5) were reaffirmed, while novel candidates like MQTLPF11-1 (PVE > 28%) were highlighted for breeding applications. Additionally, a set of polymorphic InDel markers was successfully developed, capable of distinguishing Hashemi (a high-quality cultivated variety, salt-sensitive), from FL478 (a salt-tolerant genotype). The results demonstrate that InDel markers located within salt-responsive genes provide an efficient, cost-effective, and reliable tool for MAS. By enabling rapid identification of salt-tolerant genotypes through simple PCR amplification and agarose gel electrophoresis, these markers significantly enhance the precision and speed of breeding programs targeting quantitative traits like salt tolerance. This work provides a practical framework for molecular breeding, supporting global food security efforts in saline-prone agricultural systems.

Methods

Meta-QTL Analysis

To achieve the research objectives, we employed the following steps to identify rice MQTL regions under salt stress in seedling and reproductive stages, separately. Seedling stage refers to the early vegetative phase beginning with the emergence of the radicle (germination) and lasting until the onset of tillering, typically a period of 15–30 days depending on cultivar and growing conditions (Counce et al. 2000; IRRI 2013). Morphologically, this stage encompasses from the emergence of the first leaf through the establishment of the seedling's root system and initial leaf development, prior to the initiation of tillers (Counce et al. 2000; Yoshida 1981). Reproductive stage begins at panicle initiation, when the panicle primordium becomes visible at the base of the developing tiller, and continues through booting, heading, and flowering (anthesis) (Counce et al. 2000; IRRI 2013). This stage is characterized by internode elongation, flag leaf emergence, panicle exertion from the sheath, and ultimately anthesis.

Two types of meta-analysis of QTLs were performed; (1) Inclusive MQTL analysis: a comprehensive evaluation incorporating all the original QTLs linked to salt tolerance and yield-related traits under salt stress. (2) Distinct MQTL analysis: individual trait-based assessment conducted for each of the 10 traits separately. The salt-related traits considered for the seedling stage included: Root Ion Homeostasis (RIH), Shoot Ion Homeostasis (SIH), Root Traits (RT), Shoot Traits (ST), Plant Height (PH), Shoot/Root Length (SRL), Biomass (B), Survival Rate (SR), Salt Injury Score (SI) and Chlorophyll Traits (CT). The traits associated with salt tolerance that were regarded at the reproductive stage include: Grain Yield (GY), Tiller Number (TN), Panicle Fertility (PF), Panicle Traits (PT), Heading Date (HD), Ion Homeostasis (IH), Biomass (B), Plant Height (PH), Leaf Traits (LT) and Salt Injury Score (SI).

Collection of QTL Data from Independent Studies

The QTL studies were collected from previously published reports on rice under salt stress, spanning the years 1999 to 2021 (Table.S16). We performed a comprehensive literature using multiple scientific databases, such as PubMed, Google Scholar, and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) with the following keywords: “rice”, “Oryza sativa”, “salt tolerance”, “salinity”, “QTL”, “quantitative trait locus”, “seedling stage”, “abiotic stress”, and “reproductive stage”. There were 58 (including 1243 initial QTLs) and 17 (including 342 initial QTLs) reports of QTL mapping at the seedling and reproductive stages, respectively (Table S1). The details of traits, parents of the mapping population, size and types of the mapping population (F2, BC, DH, RILs, BILs and NILs), Logarithm of the Odds (LOD) score, Phenotypic Variance Explained (PVE or R2), confidence interval (CI) and QTL positions were retrieved and compiled from independent studies at both the developmental stages. QTL reports with missing data, as well as redundant or low-confidence QTLs, were excluded. Confidence intervals (CIs) were refined using standard meta-analysis methods to reduce heterogeneity across studies.

Developing the Consensus Genetic Map

To maximize the number of initial QTLs in QTL projection, a new consensus map was constructed from six high-density genetic maps using the LPmerge utility in R package. The selected maps were (1) International Rice Micro Satellite Initiative (IRMI) composed of 1684 SSR markers developed by (McCouch et al. 2002), (2) the Cornel_SSR_2006 map comprising 706 markers, including 455 SSRs, 133 RFLPs and 116 specific gene markers available at https://archive.gramene.org, (3) the high density rice genetic map, JRGP RFLP 2000-R1, comprising 36,666 RFLP markers available at https://togodb.biosciencedbc.jp, (4) IGCN 1998-R3 map comprising 462 markers including 276 RFLPs, 34 RAPDs, 89 SSRs, 10 AFLPs, 10 TASs, 9 Isozymes available at (Cho et al. 1998), (5) CIAT SSR 2006-R4 map composed of 408 SSRs available at https://archive.gramene.org, (6) KRGRP 1998-R5 map comprising 529 markers, containing 231 AFLPs, 212 RFLPs and 86 SSRs (Cho et al. 1998). These six maps were specifically chosen to maximize genome coverage and marker density across all 12 rice chromosomes, and to include high-quality markers from multiple types (SSR, RFLP, AFLP, and gene-specific), ensuring accurate QTL projection. All the markers from the six maps were employed to develop the consensus genetic map (Table.S17).

Identification of Genes in MQTL Regions

The flanking markers of the QTLs in the consensus regions were used to search the genes within MQTL positions. The flanking markers of the MQTLs were queried against the genome browser for the rice reference genome IRGSP 1.0 (ftp://ftp.ensemblgenomes.org/pub/plants), and genes were identified within the MQTL intervals.

Verification of QTL Meta-Analysis by Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS)

A total of 21 GWAS papers from the past 10 years were gathered, including 16 studies at the seedling stage and five studies at the reproductive stage (Table S18). The identified SNP peak positions were then clustered based on traits associated with the salt tolerance in both growth stages. Next, overlaps between the SNP peak positions and the identified MQTL regions were identified. Finally, the overlapping loci (± 20 kb) were screened to find genes located at SNP peak positions by mapping their physical positions to the rice reference genome IRGSP 1.0 (ftp://ftp.ensemblgenomes.org/pub/plants).

Detection of the Insertions/Deletions (InDels)

The whole genome sequence (WGS) of FL478 (a salt-tolerant indica rice line, ENA Accession no.: ERR619432) and Hashemi (a high-quality but salt-sensitive Iranian rice variety, NCBI Accession no.: PRJNA1376254), were mapped to the rice reference genome IRGSP_1.0 (GCA_001433935.1). Once all the reads were mapped to the reference genome, the samtools utility was employed to add read groups (RGs) to the resulting (.bam file). The tagged.bam files for each run of every genotype were then sorted and merged together using samtools utility. Variant calling was performed on these merged BAM files using bcftools with default parameters. Next, the snpEff tool was used to annotate all sequence variants and their potential mutational effects on associated genes. Finally, the snpSift utility was employed to identify candidate phenotype-relevant variants (Cingolani et al. 2012). Specifically, we distinguished high and moderate-impact variants that were present or absent in FL478 and Hashemi rice genotypes. The following were considered high-impact variants: chromosome number variation, exon loss variant, frameshift variant, rare amino acid variant, splice acceptor variant, splice donor variant, start lost, stop gained, stop lost and transcript ablation. Moderate-impact variants included: 3′ UTR truncation & exon loss, 5′ UTR truncation & exon loss variant, coding sequence variant, conservative inframe deletion, conservative inframe insertion, disruptive inframe deletion, disruptive inframe insertion, missense variant, regulatory region ablation, splice region variant and TFBS. The results were filtered for the moderate- and high-impact variants in both FL478 and Hashemi. Subsequently, the variants were filtered to include only those located within MQTL regions.

Prioritizing MQTL Regions for Marker Design

To determine the most important MQTLs, selection criteria included the QTL-overview index, phenotypic variance, and overlapping with SNP peak regions (based on relevant GWAS reports).

Development of Insertion-Deletion (InDel) Markers

To develop genome-wide InDel markers, variants between the salt tolerant (FL478) and the salt sensitive (Hashemi) sequence were found within the selected MQTL regions at the seedling and reproductive stages, separately. Based on the InDel loci, suitable primers were designed using the Primer 3.0 software. Primer BLAST was employed to select primers producing products of 100–150 bp containing an InDel > 10 bp with the optimal melting temperature of 60 °C and ~ 50% of GC content.

Plant Materials and DNA Extraction

Two rice genotypes, including Hashemi and FL478, were grown in a hydroponic system (Yoshida Solution) in the growth chamber (14 h light/10 h dark at a temperature of 28 ± 2 °C) for 21 days. (Yoshida et al. 1981). Young leaves of each seedling were then collected, immediately placed in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction. Genomic DNA was extracted using a modified CTAB method (Aboul-Maaty et al. 2019).

InDel Genotyping

In total, 21 primer pairs were selected for polymorphism analysis (Table S15). Polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) were conducted in 20 μL reaction mixtures. The PCR mix consisted of the following reagents: 7 μL of the PCR mix solution (Sinacolon, Iran), 0.6 μL of the forward primer (10 nmol/L), 0.6 μL of the reverse primer (10 nmol/L), and 1 μL of the DNA template (10 ng/L). The PCR program was as followings: an initial one cycle of 94 °C for 9 min, followed by 35 cycles of 1 min at 94 °C, 1 min at 58 °C, 1 min at 72 °C, and a final extension of 10 min at 72 °C.
