Large Core Stroke Thrombectomy Is Safe and Effective Regardless of Prior Antithrombotic or Thrombolytic Treatment: A Secondary Analysis of the Randomized TENSION Trial

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relevance of prior antithrombotic and thrombolytic treatment for decision‐making regarding endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) for acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion with established large infarcts is uncertain. This study investigates associations of prior antithrombotic medication and thrombolysis with the efficacy and safety of EVT for acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion with established large infarct.MethodsTENSION (Efficacy and Safety of Thrombectomy in Stroke With Extended Lesion and Extended Time Window) was a prospective randomized open‐label blinded–end point clinical trial. Patients with acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion and established large infarct were randomized to EVT with medical therapy or medical therapy alone. Exposures were preadmission antithrombotic treatment with antiplatelet agents or anticoagulants and intravenous thrombolysis. The primary efficacy end point was functional outcome at 90 days. Safety outcomes included death and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage.ResultsThe study included 246 patients (median age, 74 years; interquartile range, 65 to 80 years; 49% women); 124 (50%) were assigned to EVT. Of 176 patients (72%) with prior antithrombotic therapy, 75 (31%) received antiplatelets, 56 (23%) anticoagulants, and 89 (36%) intravenous thrombolysis. EVT was associated with better functional outcome in patients with (common odds ratio [cOR], 2.40 [95% CI, 1.22–4.99]) and without (cOR, 2.29 [95% CI, 1.53–3.46]) antiplatelet therapy; with (cOR, 2.45 [95% CI, 1.17–5.28]) and without (cOR, 2.12 [95% CI, 1.44–3.15]) anticoagulation; as well as receiving (cOR, 1.46 [95% CI, 0.83–2.61]) and not receiving (cOR, 2.89 [95% CI, 1.87–4.51]) thrombolysis. Interaction analyses were consistent with similar treatment effects across subgroups. Mortality and rates of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage were similar between groups.ConclusionsBenefit and safety of EVT were not modified by prior antithrombotic/thrombolytic therapy. Preadmission exposure to antiplatelets or anticoagulants or use of intravenous thrombolysis should not exclude eligible patients with stroke from EVT.RegistrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique Identifier: NCT03094715.

Many patients with acute ischemic stroke are taking prescribed antithrombotic medications at the time of their stroke. 1 Although effective anticoagulation is currently a contraindication to intravenous thrombolysis (IVT), 2 prior antithrombotic therapy, including thrombolysis, does not exclude patients from endovascular therapy (EVT). 3 Indeed, despite bleeding risks associated with periprocedural aspirin and heparin use, 4 EVT for acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion without a large established infarct is safe and effective regardless of preadmission antiplatelet use or anticoagulant use, 5 , 6 or IVT. 7

Recent trials have demonstrated clinical benefits of EVT also in ischemic stroke patients with large established infarcts corresponding to Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Scores (ASPECTS) of <6. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Although a secondary analysis of the SELECT2 (A Randomized Controlled Trial to Optimize Patient's Selection for Endovascular Treatment in Acute Ischemic Stroke) trial did not find evidence for the efficacy of EVT in patients receiving oral anticoagulants, 15 a precise understanding of the clinical relevance of antithrombotic therapy before EVT in patients with large established infarcts is missing.

The aim of the present study was to investigate the treatment effect and safety profile of EVT for acute ischemic stroke with established large infarct in patients receiving different forms of antithrombotic treatment before hospital admission, or thrombolysis before EVT.

METHODS

Data that support the results reported in this article are available upon request to investigators whose proposed use of the data has been approved by the TENSION steering committee.

Study Design

The study presents a secondary analysis of the multicenter prospective open‐label blinded–end point randomized TENSION trial of EVT in adult patients with acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion of the anterior circulation and large established infarct corresponding to an Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score of 3 to 5. 8 , 16 Eligible patients were previously independent with prestroke modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores of 0 to 2, had National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores not exceeding 25, and were randomized to either EVT plus best medical management (BMT) or BMT alone within 11 hours of stroke symptom onset. Randomization was stratified by time from symptom onset (</>6 hours) and severity of stroke symptoms (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score ≤/>18). Complete inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as details on trial procedures, are available with the TENSION protocol. 8 Patients with missing primary end point data were not included in the analysis.

The TENSION protocol was approved by the institutional review board of the University of Heidelberg (Heidelberg, Germany; ref S‐248/2016) and all participating hospitals before the start of patient enrolment. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal representatives. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03094715.

Exposures

Antithrombotic medications taken regularly before enrolment were classified as antiplatelet agents (including aspirin, clopidogrel, and glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors) or anticoagulants (including vitamin K antagonists, direct oral anticoagulants, and low‐molecular weight and unfractionated heparins unless given for prevention of deep vein thrombosis). Exposure to IVT was defined as administration of an initial bolus of alteplase. Tenecteplase was not used in this trial. Intraprocedural administrations of antiplatelet agents or heparins were not considered prior antithrombotic therapy.

End Points

Following the TENSION protocol, the primary efficacy end point was functional outcome 90 days after randomization, assessed on the mRS (with lowest scores 0 and 1 merged into a single category) by investigators blinded to treatment allocation. Secondary efficacy end points were rates of independent (mRS ≤2) and ambulatory (mRS ≤3) functional outcomes. 17 Safety end points included mortality and the rate of death or dependency (mRS 4–6) at 90 days, as well as rates of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) according to the SITS‐MOST (Safe Implementation of Thrombolysis in Stroke‐Monitoring Study) definition 18 and any ICH.

Statistical Analysis

For efficacy end points, treatment effects of EVT were quantified using common odds ratios (cOR) and Wald 95% CIs from ordinal and binary regression models. Safety end points were compared between treatment arms using unadjusted risk differences with 95% CIs obtained from logistic regression models.

End points and treatment effects of EVT were analyzed in subgroups defined by exposure to preadmission antiplatelet therapy, preadmission anticoagulation, or administration of thrombolytics before EVT. Differences between subgroups were quantified by ratios of ORs and differences of risk differences obtained from interaction analyses. Antithrombotic/thrombolytic‐associated heterogeneity of treatment effects was formally assessed by partitioning the sample into participants with no prior exposure to antithrombotic drugs; exclusive exposure to either antiplatelets, anticoagulation, or thrombolytic agents; and exposure to >1 class of antithrombotic/thrombolytic drugs; and assessing the interaction between the corresponding subgroup and EVT.

In the primary analysis, models for efficacy end points were adjusted for the randomization stratification variables, baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, and time from symptom onset/last seen well. 8 Sensitivity analyses included unadjusted models, as well as models additionally adjusted for age and sex as possible confounders of an observed association between prior antithrombotic/thrombolytic medication and efficacy of EVT.

In this secondary analyses, no formal hypothesis tests were carried out. All analyses were performed in the intention‐to‐treat population.

RESULTS

Baseline Data

Between July 2018 and February 2023, 253 patients were recruited into the TENSION trial at 40 sites from across Europe and Canada. Randomization assigned 125 patients (49%) to receive EVT plus BMT and 128 (51%) to BMT alone. The primary end point was missing in 7 patients (Figure 1A). Of the remaining 246 patients included in the analysis, 176 patients (71.5%) received antithrombotic medication before randomization (Figure 1B). Three patients assigned to BMT received EVT outside the trial protocol.

Of 75 patients (30.5%) who received antiplatelet agents, 70 were on single (60 aspirin, 10 clopidogrel) and 5 on dual therapy. Anticoagulants were prescribed in 56 patients (22.8%) and included vitamin K antagonists (coumarin derivatives, 14), direct oral anticoagulants (38), and heparins (unfractionated, 2; low‐molecular‐weight, 2). IVT was administered to 89 patients (36.2%), of whom 66 patients (74%) were transferred to the treating study center from a different hospital. More than a single class of antithrombotic/thrombolytic medication was given to 43 patients (17.5%), with the most common combination being prior antiplatelet therapy and thrombolysis in 35 patients (14.2%). One patient was treated with IVT after receiving idarucizumab for reversal of anticoagulation with dabigatran and was not considered anticoagulated at the time of randomization.

Further demographic and baseline clinical characteristics stratified by antithrombotic/thrombolytic therapy are shown in Table 1. Preadmission antithrombotic treatment was associated with higher age (76 versus 70 years, difference 5 years [95% CI, 2.5–7.6 years]), male sex (58% versus 44%, difference 14% [95% CI, 0.62%–27%]), and greater prevalence of cardiovascular comorbidities. Patients receiving thrombolysis were more likely to have received preadmission antiplatelets (40% versus 25%, difference 13% [95% CI, 3.4%–28%]), were less likely to be anticoagulated (2.2% versus 34%, difference 32% [95% CI, 24%–40%]) or have atrial fibrillation (20% versus 41%, difference 21% [95% CI, 9.1%–33%]), and had shorter times from onset to randomization (3.8 versus 5.9 hours, difference 2.1 hours [95% CI, 1.7–3.0 hours]) compared with patients not treated with IVT.

Outcomes

Efficacy and safety end point data stratified by antithrombotic/thrombolytic medication and treatment assignment are tabulated in Table 2. Functional outcome after 3 months was similar in patients without preadmission antithrombotic therapy (median mRS score 5; interquartile range, 3–6) and those with prior antiplatelet therapy (5 [4, 5, 6]) as well as in patients with and without thrombolysis (both 5 [4, 5, 6]). Disability at 90 days was greater in patients on prior anticoagulation (median mRS score 6, [5, 6]). The association of prior anticoagulation with poorer functional outcomes (cOR, 0.55 [95% CI, 0.31–0.96]) was partly attenuated after adjustment for age, sex, and treatment arm (Figure 2A).

Rates of independent ambulation (mRS score ≤3) after 3 months were similar in patients without preadmission antithrombotic treatment (25%), in those with prior antiplatelet therapy (24%), and in patients without thrombolysis (25%). Ambulatory functional outcome was numerically less frequent in patients on prior anticoagulation (14%) and in patients receiving IVT (18%).

Mortality was similar across groups, numerically ranging from 42% in patients without preadmission antithrombotic treatment to 52% in patients with prior anticoagulation. Exposure to neither class of antithrombotic/thrombolytic medication was associated with death at 90 days (Figure 2B). Symptomatic ICH occurred at similar rates in patients with and without prior antithrombotic therapy, numerically ranging from 3.6% (prior anticoagulation) to 6.7% (prior antiplatelets). Symptomatic ICH occurred more frequently in patients receiving IVT (10% versus 2.5%, risk difference, 7.6% [95% CI, 0.83%–14%]).

Treatment Effects of EVT

Primary efficacy end points in patients with and without preadmission antithrombotic therapy and with and without thrombolysis stratified by treatment assignment are shown in Figure 3. Irrespective of preadmission antithrombotic treatment, EVT was associated with better functional outcomes after 90 days compared with BMT, indicated by adjusted cORs for a shift toward lower mRS scores of 2.25 (95% CI, 1.40–3.66) without preadmission antithrombotic treatment, 2.40 (95% CI, 1.22–4.99) in patients taking antiplatelet agents, and 2.45 (95% CI, 1.17–5.28) in anticoagulated patients, respectively. The treatment effects of EVT on ambulatory (mRS score ≤3) functional outcomes were also similar with treatment effects ranging from 1.94 (95% CI, 1.04–3.73) to 4.09 (95% CI, 1.48–15.5), in patients without preadmission antithrombotic therapy and having received antiplatelets, respectively.

Patients who did not receive IVT had better functional outcomes after 90 days with EVT compared with BMT (median mRS score 4 versus 6; cOR, 2.89 [95% CI, 1.87–4.51], Figure 2C). In those patients, EVT was also associated with a higher rate of ambulatory functional outcomes (mRS score ≤3, 37% versus 13%; OR, 2.86; [95% CI, 1.63–5.27]). After thrombolysis, EVT was associated with numerically better functional outcomes (median mRS 5 versus 6; OR, 1.46 [95% CI, 0.83–2.61]) and numerically higher rates of independent ambulation (22% versus 13%; OR, 1.96 [95% CI, 0.80–5.48]). Data were consistent with equal efficacy of EVT in patients with and without IVT (ratio of cORs, 0.51 [95% CI, 0.25–1.03]). Further exploratory analyses yielded a treatment effect of 1.82 (95% CI, 0.93–3.69) in 66 patients receiving IVT being transferred from a different hospital and 0.78 (95% CI, 0.24–2.42) in 23 patients receiving IVT after presenting directly to the thrombectomy center.

EVT was associated with similar reductions in 90‐day mortality in patients with and without preadmission antithrombotic treatment, with absolute risk differences ranging from 16% to 21%. Rates of death or dependency (mRS score 4–6) were similarly reduced across groups with risk differences ranging from 17% to 23%. ICH occurred at higher rates in the EVT group compared with BMT (28% versus 16%, risk difference, 11.8% [95% CI, 1.5%–22%]). The risk difference in patients without preadmission antithrombotic treatment (20%) was numerically higher than in patients with prior antiplatelet therapy (3%) or anticoagulation (7%). In patients both with and without preadmission antithrombotic treatment, symptomatic ICH occurred rarely and in similar frequencies in the EVT and BMT groups, with no evidence of heterogeneity between groups.

Mortality after 90 days was reduced by EVT compared with BMT in patients not receiving IVT (31% versus 55%, risk difference, 24% [95% CI, 9.2%–39%]), but similar in patients receiving IVT (47% versus 53%, risk difference, 5.6% [95% CI, −15% to 26%]). Data were consistent with equal EVT‐associated mortality benefit in patients with and without IVT (difference of risk differences, 19% [95% CI, −7.0% to 45%]). Whereas any ICH was more common in the EVT groups, rates of symptomatic ICH were not associated with EVT in patients having or not having received thrombolysis.

EVT treatment effect estimates stratified by exposure to different classes of antithrombotic/ thrombolytic medication taking before admission are shown in Figure 2C and 2D. Treatment effects of EVT on 90‐day functional outcome and 90‐day mortality were similar in patients who took or did not take antiplatelet agents (ratio of cORs, 1.10 [95% CI, 0.51–2.40]; difference of risk differences, 4.2% [95% CI, −24.7% to 37.7%]), and in patients who were or were not receiving anticoagulants (1.00 [95% CI, 0.45–2.23]; −1.5% [95% CI, −27.0% to 27.1%]).

In formal interaction analyses there was no evidence of heterogeneity of treatment effects between patients with no prior antithrombotic therapy; exclusive exposure to either antiplatelets, anticoagulation, or thrombolysis; and patients having received >1 class of prior antithrombotic/thrombolytic medications.

DISCUSSION

We found that EVT for acute ischemic stroke with large vessel occlusion and established large infarct was safe and effective regardless of preadmission antithrombotic treatment with antiplatelet agents or oral anticoagulation. EVT was also safe in patients receiving IVT, but the efficacy of EVT at reducing disability and preventing death after stroke in patients who have received IVT may be smaller than in patients who have not received thrombolysis.

EVT for acute large core ischemic stroke has recently been investigated in 6 randomized clinical trials. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Both qualitative and quantitative summaries of these trials found strong evidence for a benefit of EVT in patients with limited prestroke disability and large infarcts. 14 , 19 , 20 , 21

Associations of prior antiplatelet therapy with risks and benefits of endovascular therapy in populations with established large infarcts have not previously been reported. For patients with large vessel occlusion stroke treated with EVT in clinical practice, however, observational registry data agree with our findings and indicate that prior antiplatelet use is not associated with safety concerns or reduced efficacy of endovascular therapy.

In an analysis of the MR CLEAN registry, for instance, 937 patients (30%) received prior antiplatelet therapy and were compared with 477 matched patients without prior antiplatelet therapy. 6 Rates of symptomatic ICH (7.9% versus 5.6%) and functional outcome (median mRS score at 90 days, 4 versus 4) were similar in both groups. An analysis of the French ETIS (Endovascular Treatment in Ischemic Stroke) registry included 2939 patients 877 of whom (30%) received prior antiplatelet therapy. 22 In adjusted comparisons, their rates of symptomatic ICH (11%) and favorable functional outcome (mRS score 0–2, 39%) were not different from those in patients without prior antiplatelet therapy (7.8% and 48%, respectively).

In the RESCUE (Recovery by Endovascular Salvage for Cerebral Ultra‐acute Embolism)‐Japan Registry 2, 1281 patients with large vessel occlusion stroke treated with EVT were included. 23 In 254 patients (20%) who had received prior antiplatelet therapy, the rates of symptomatic ICH (2.8%) and favorable functional outcome (mRS score 0–2, 36%) were, after adjustment, similar to end points in patients without prior antiplatelet therapy (2.6% a 43%, respectively). In an analysis of the ANGEL‐ACT (Endovascular Treatment Key Technique and Emergency Work Flow Improvement of Acute Ischemic Stroke) registry, 1349 patients (16%) were reported to have received antiplatelet therapy before EVT. 24 In this group, rates of symptomatic ICH (6.9%) and functional outcome (median mRS score 3) did not differ from end points in the group of patients without prior antiplatelet therapy (7.2%, median mRS score 3).

In 2 additional registries, prior antiplatelet use was associated with improved outcomes after EVT. 25 , 26 Given the extensive adjustment for baseline covariates, including comorbidities, in these studies, their findings might reflect a general protective effect of appropriate cardiovascular therapy, rather than a mechanism specific to patients undergoing EVT.

Prior Anticoagulation

Participants receiving prior anticoagulation had numerically higher mRS scores after 90 days than patients not receiving prior anticoagulation. This observation is in line with a recent meta‐analysis of the safety and efficacy of EVT in anticoagulated patients, where, without adjustment for differences in age and cardiovascular comorbidities, prior anticoagulation was associated with worse functional outcomes and higher mortality. 27 After adjustment for clinical covariates, including EVT, however, prior anticoagulation was not associated with efficacy or safety end points, including death and symptomatic hemorrhage. Thus, the effect observed in the unadjusted analysis likely reflects a higher comorbidity burden and greater frailty in patients taking oral anticoagulants.

Treatment effect estimates of EVT were similar in patients with and without prior anticoagulation. These findings are in contrast to a secondary analysis of the SELECT2 trial, where EVT was not associated with improved functional outcomes in patients receiving oral anticoagulants. 15 The absence of a treatment benefit of EVT in anticoagulated patients in SELECT2 could not be attributed to greater frailty and burden of comorbidities. Despite the reported absence of symptomatic ICHs, it might, however, be related to the high proportion of anticoagulated patients taking vitamin K antagonists (>50% in the EVT group), which are known to be associated with a less favorable safety profile of EVT compared with nonvitamin K antagonists. 28 , 29 , 30

Our results are consistent with observational data in patients without established large infarct: for instance, similar efficacy and safety profiles of EVT were observed in 502 patients both with and without prior anticoagulation included in the Madrid Stroke Network registry. 31 A large retrospective analysis of 1282 patients undergoing EVT for distal or medium vessel occlusion stroke also did not find an independent association of prior anticoagulation with functional outcome or ICH. 28

Prior Thrombolysis

In the present analysis, despite higher rates of symptomatic ICH, patients receiving IVT had similar functional outcomes to patients not receiving IVT. Consistent with recent data from the Get With The Guidelines registry, 32 the increased rate of sICH persisted after adjustment for baseline covariates. However, even after adjustment, there was no association between IVT and functional outcomes or mortality and thus no evidence of harm associated with IVT in this large core stroke population.

Similar to reported subgroup analyses from the RESCUE‐Japan LIMIT (Recovery by Endovascular Salvage for Cerebral Ultra‐Acute Embolism–Japan Large Ischemic Core Trial), ANGEL‐ASPECT, and LASTE (Large Stroke Therapy Evaluation) trials, in the present study, the main efficacy estimate of EVT in patients receiving IVT was in favor of EVT but numerically smaller than in patients not receiving IVT. 9 , 10 , 12 Of the 6 recent large core EVT trials, only TESLA (Thrombectomy for Emergent Salvage of Large Anterior Circulation Ischemic Stroke) reported a numerically higher efficacy estimate in patients pretreated with IVT. 13 A smaller treatment effect in patients receiving IVT would distinguish ischemic stroke with and without established large infarct, because for the latter, the treatment effect of EVT is not modified by thrombolysis with alteplase. 7 Observational data support a net benefit of bridging thrombolysis in patients with large core stroke. 33 , 34 Based on these observations and evidence of randomized clinical trials, 35 current guidelines recommend not withholding IVT in eligible patients before EVT. 2 , 36 The precise determination of the net benefit of bridging thrombolysis in large core ischemic stroke remains a challenge for future research. 37

In addition to improving functional outcome, TENSION also demonstrated a mortality benefit for patients receiving EVT. 8 This effect was reproduced in the subgroup of patients who did not receive IVT. In patients who did receive IVT, EVT was, in the presented analysis, not associated with reduced mortality. Absence of evidence for a mortality benefit is consistent with reported results of RESCUE‐Japan LIMIT, ANGEL‐ASPECT, SELECT‐2, and TESLA trials, where, irrespective of IVT, rates of death at 90 days were similar between treatment groups. Apart from TENSION, only LASTE found an effect of EVT on mortality. 12 A significant reduction in the rate of death after 3 months has also not been demonstrated for EVT for ischemic stroke without established large infarct in the early and late time windows, 7 , 38 suggesting that EVT after IVT in patients with large core is no less safe than in unselected populations.

Limitations

As TENSION was not powered for subgroups analyses, some associations between antithrombotic therapy and endovascular therapy may have been overlooked. Exposure to antithrombotic/thrombolytic medications was not randomized and associations with outcomes and treatment effects of EVT may therefore be affected by residual confounding. In particular, IVT use is likely confounded with time from symptom onset/last known well, which itself is a potential treatment effect modifier for large core stroke thrombectomy. Poorer functional outcomes and higher rates of complications, such as symptomatic ICH, in patients receiving late‐window thrombectomy may have skewed the safety and efficacy profiles of IVT observed in this population with large core stroke, which therefore warrant further research.

Conclusions

Endovascular therapy was safe and effective in patients with acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion and large established infarct regardless of prior antithrombotic therapy. Preadmission exposure to antiplatelets or anticoagulants, or use of thrombolytics should not exclude otherwise eligible patients with stroke from endovascular thrombectomy.
