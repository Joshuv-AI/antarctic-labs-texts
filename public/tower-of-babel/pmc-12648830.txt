Macular hole caused by intense pulsed light: a case report
Authors: Xiang Wang, Weihua Xu, Fengqin Hao, Yan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntense pulsed light (IPL)–induced macular holes are rare but potentially severe. We report a case with anatomically successful surgical closure but poor functional recovery.Summary of findings and treatmentA 30-year-old male sustained accidental ocular exposure to 808 ± 10 nm IPL without eye protection. One week later, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/200; optical coherence tomography (OCT) revealed a large full-thickness macular hole (basal diameter: 1639 μm) with cystoid edema and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) disruption. Despite oral corticosteroids, the hole remained open at 1 month. Pars plana vitrectomy with internal limiting membrane peeling and sterile air tamponade achieved anatomical closure by postoperative day 7. However, BCVA remained 20/200 at 1 month due to persistent IS/OS discontinuity and RPE defects.Clinical implicationsThis case highlights that IPL can cause large, traction-associated macular holes. Even early surgical closure may not restore vision if photoreceptor and RPE integrity are compromised. Eye protection is essential during IPL handling.

Introduction

In recent years, the growing sales of household and medical intense pulsed light (IPL) devices have correspondingly elevated the potential risk of accidental ocular exposure [1]. However, cases resulting in full-thickness macular holes are exceedingly rare, with only sporadic case reports documented in the English literature to date. Unlike monochromatic coherent lasers, IPL emits a broadband (515–1200 nm), non-collimated beam, which can produce a larger photothermal footprint in the retina-choroid complex [2], suggesting that its injury mechanism and prognosis may fundamentally differ from those of laser-induced macular holes. This article reports a case of retinal injury caused by an 808 ± 10 nm IPL device and systematically describes its clinical features, surgical management, and visual outcomes, aiming to provide evidence-based insights for early clinical recognition and patient communication.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old male process engineer sustained accidental ocular exposure to IPL while testing a cosmetic device without protective eyewear. He immediately noted a central scotoma and loss of vision in his right eye. The patient presented to our hospital 1 week post-injury. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in the right eye, limited to the peripheral residual field, was 20/200 with a refractive error of − 4.00/−0.25 × 20 = 0.1, whereas BCVA in the left eye was 20/20. Fundus photography (Oberbauer) revealed a round, full-thickness macular hole measuring approximately one-third disc diameter (Fig. 1A). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) demonstrated a full-thickness macular defect with a mean minimum diameter of 856 μm and a basal diameter of 1639 μm (Fig. 1B, C). A diagnosis of IPL-induced full-thickness macular hole was made. To alleviate secondary retinal inflammation and reduce macular edema, the patient was administered prednisone tablets at 0.5 mg/kg/day (patient weight 70 kg → 35 mg/day), taken once daily in the morning for 5–7 days [3]. Starting from day 6, the dosage was reduced by 5 mg every 2 days, with discontinuation of the drug within 10–14 days.

One month after injury, fundus examination and OCT revealed persistent macular fissure with no significant change in diameter. Hyperreflective material was observed at the base of the hole, accompanied by cystic macular edema (Fig. 2A-C). Ocular B-scan ultrasound showed rough retinal elevation in the macular region (Fig. 2D). According to the Asia-Pacific Vitreo-Retinal Society (APVRS) consensus recommendations, early surgery within 3 months of symptom onset is advised for large macular holes or cases with deteriorating vision [4]. Therefore, upon observing progressive visual decline, ruling out the possibility of spontaneous closure, and adhering to the principle of surgical intervention in the absence of improvement, the patient underwent vitrectomy with internal limiting membrane peeling and sterile air tamponade, the detailed surgical procedure was as follows: The patient was placed in a supine position, and after routine disinfection and draping, a combined retrobulbar and anterior ethmoidal nerve block anesthesia was administered using a mixture of 3.5 ml of 2% lidocaine and 1.5 ml of 1% ropivacaine, followed by compression for 3–5 min. An eyelid speculum was then inserted, and the conjunctival sac was irrigated with povidone-iodine and normal saline. A scleral incision was made 3.6 mm posterior to the corneal limbus at the inferotemporal quadrant, with additional ports created at the 10 o’clock position (for vitreous cutter access) and the 2 o’clock position (for fiberoptic illumination). The infusion cannula was secured in place. During the procedure, both anterior and posterior vitreous were excised. Triamcinolone acetonide was injected to stain the vitreous, facilitating posterior vitreous detachment and complete vitreous removal. Under fiberoptic illumination, a round macular hole measuring approximately 1/3 disc diameter was identified. The macular epiretinal membrane and internal limiting membrane were carefully peeled up to the vascular arcade. The macular hole was then closed, followed by fluid-air exchange and gas tamponade, with intraocular pressure adjusted to normal levels. Finally, the scleral and conjunctival incisions were sutured with 8 − 0 absorbable sutures, ensuring no leakage. Tobramycin-dexamethasone ointment was applied to the conjunctival sac, and the operated eye was patched. The surgery proceeded smoothly, with stable vital signs throughout. The patient was safely returned to the ward postoperatively. No intravenous fluids were administered during the procedure, with only lidocaine and ropivacaine used for anesthesia. followed by one week of postoperative prone positioning. Given the macular hole had a large basal diameter, extra mechanical tamponade was required to promote apposition of the retinal pigment epithelium and photoreceptors. Studies have shown that extending the prone positioning to 5–7 days improves the single-surgery closure rate in traumatic large holes [5]. Therefore, the patient was instructed to maintain a prone position for one week.

OCT at 1 week post-operation showed closure of the macular fissure and regression of macular oedema. However, disorganization of the retinal structural hierarchy in the optic macular region and discontinuity of the inner/outer segment (IS/OS) junction were still observed. Although the continuity of the retinal pigmented epithelium (RPE) layer showed some improvement compared with the preoperative state, full-thickness ruptures and foci of hyper-reflectivity within the subfoveal recess remained evident (Fig. 3A, B). No improvement in BCVA was observed at 1 month post-operation. OCT did not show further retinal improvement (Fig. 3C, D). Table 1 summarizes the patient’s clinical course and management timeline.

Discussion and conclusion

Intense pulsed light (IPL) devices typically incorporate a xenon-filled flash lamp that generates high-intensity light pulses, which are delivered through a small crystal window embedded in the plastic treatment head [6]. Unlike the monochromatic light emitted by lasers, IPL produces a broad spectrum of wavelengths ranging from approximately 515 nm to 1200 nm [7]. Internally, water-cooled mirrors are utilized to enhance light reflection, while optical filters surrounding the flash lamp narrow the spectral bandwidth of each pulse. Additionally, specific filters are mounted on the crystal window to optimize light transmission for various clinical applications. Numerous human tissues contain high concentrations of endogenous pigments, making the IPL transmission spectrum subject to wavelength-specific absorption. The retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), which is rich in pigment granules, represents the initial retinal structure exposed to incident light. Damage initiates upon IPL penetration into the ocular interior via this site. The pathophysiological mechanism underlying IPL-induced macular holes involves the rapid absorption of light by RPE melanin, resulting in localized temperature elevations of 50–150 °C. This thermal insult induces coagulative necrosis of the RPE and photoreceptors, disruption of Müller cell end-feet, generation of tangential traction, and eventual formation of a macular hole [8, 9]. Lee et al. [10] described two cases of IPL exposure in female patients undergoing periorbital IPL treatment, leading to iris thinning, pupillary abnormalities, and permanent structural damage attributable to photoablation of the iris sphincter. The clinical manifestations of iris atrophy and defects in these patients were comparable to those observed in other IPL-exposed individuals [11, 12]. Various lasers, including the titanium sapphire laser [5], blue laser [13, 14], argon laser [15] and the most common Q-switched Nd: YAG laser [16–19], may also accidentally cause whole-layer macular holes.

Compared to previous reports on laser-induced macular holes, the present case of IPL-induced damage exhibits distinct differences in morphology, treatment, and prognosis. First, laser-induced macular holes are typically less than 250 μm in diameter, with well-defined edges and mild surrounding retinal edema [13, 16]. In contrast, the macular hole induced by 808 nm IPL in this case was accompanied by extensive cystoid macular edema and diffuse RPE disruption, suggesting that the non-collimated, multi-wavelength nature of IPL may inflict a broader range of photothermal damage to the retina and choroid. Second, small laser-induced macular holes may close spontaneously or require only vitrectomy with gas tamponade [17], whereas the larger hole observed here, compounded by tangential traction, necessitated a standard three-port vitrectomy with inner limiting membrane (ILM) peeling and sterile air tamponade. This approach aligns with Ciulla’s findings on vitrectomy and ILM peeling for laser burn-induced macular holes [5]; however, the extensive scope of IPL-induced damage warrants more aggressive surgical intervention. Third, regarding prognosis, Stein et al. [19]reported that six cases of Nd: YAG laser-induced macular holes achieved BCVA recovery to 20/25–20/40 within 3–6 months postoperatively. In the current case, however, BCVA remained at 20/200 at 1 month after surgery, with persistent disruptions in the IS/OS junction and RPE discontinuity evident on OCT. Prior studies indicate that failure to restore IS/OS continuity following macular hole surgery is associated with minimal visual acuity improvement [19]. Moreover, when RPE defects exceed 1 disc diameter (DD), successful anatomical closure does not yield statistically significant BCVA gains by 6 months postoperatively [20]. Additionally, early reductions in choriocapillaris density following laser or intense light injury correlate positively with poorer final BCVA [21]. We hypothesize that the broad-spectrum energy of IPL penetrates deeper, potentially compromising choroidal perfusion. Notably, even with anatomical closure via early vitrectomy, visual recovery is markedly limited in the presence of extensive photoreceptor-ellipsoid zone disruption and RPE defects. Furthermore, pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) may accelerate cataract progression, necessitating careful assessment of surgical timing and individualized management. Thus, this case provides novel insights into the early identification and proactive surgical management of IPL-associated macular holes.

Some limitations should also be acknowledged. This single-case report is limited in generalizability. Assessments were restricted to conventional OCT and BCVA, lacking microperimetry or choroidal perfusion data. The absence of a control group precludes definitive comparisons. Future large-scale, multi-center studies incorporating advanced imaging are warranted.

With the increasing clinical and industrial application of intense pulsed light (IPL) devices, the potential risk of ocular injury warrants significant attention. Strict implementation of appropriate preventive measures is essential, including the provision and use of certified protective goggles for all personnel involved in the manufacturing, operation, and clinical application of IPL systems. Ophthalmologists should remain vigilant in promptly recognizing the clinical signs and symptoms of IPL-induced ocular damage and take swift action to prevent recurrence or progression to irreversible vision impairment.
