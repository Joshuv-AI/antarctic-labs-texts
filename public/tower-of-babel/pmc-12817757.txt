Potassium carbonate as an alternative solution for detecting Anoplocephalid eggs in horse faecal samples
Authors: Oya Girisgin, Ender Gülegen, Ahmet Onur Girisgin, Veli Yilgor Cirak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnoplocephalid species are responsible for causing tapeworm infection in horses. The disease may cause serious health issues, including discomfort, colic, and even intestinal perforation and death. A study was carried out to compare the detection rates of tapeworm eggs in naturally infected horses using two different examination solutions. For this purpose, faecal samples of 241 horses were examined with a combined sedimentation + centrifugation + flotation technique using saturated sugar solution (specific gravity (SG) = 1.30) as the basis and potassium carbonate (K2CO3) solution (SG = 1.45).ResultsTapeworm eggs were detected in 43 samples in saturated sugar solution and 59 samples in potassium carbonate solution (P < 0.05), whereas 42 samples were detected egg-positive in either. The potassium carbonate solution detected significantly more positive samples than the saturated sugar solution (P < 0.05).ConclusionsAccording to the results, potassium carbonate, as a non-toxic and high-density solution, can be a feasible alternative for detecting tapeworm eggs in horses.Keywords: Horse, Anoplocephalid eggs, Detection, Sugar solution, Potassium carbonate solution

Introduction

Tapeworm infections in horses are caused by cestodes such as Anoplocephala perfoliata, A. magna and Paranoplocephala mamillana. Among them, A. perfoliata is the most common species worldwide [1]. A. perfoliata resides mainly in the caecal wall and the ileocaecal junction, and may cause various signs of disease, such as diarrhea, unthriftiness, spasmodic colic or even intestinal perforation [2–4].

Diagnosing tapeworms in horses is typically performed using coprological egg detection methods, which involve sedimentation and flotation steps with various density solutions [5–7]. Over the past three decades, various serological methods, such as ELISA, which utilize serum, saliva, or feces, and molecular-based techniques have been developed with differing levels of success [8–11]. However, most of these methods require well-equipped laboratories and skilled personnel, making them time-consuming and often not cost-effective. On the other hand, coprological egg detection methods also have limitations, such as low sensitivity values [1, 12]. To overcome this issue, various flotation solutions, such as saturated NaCl, concentrated sugar, ZnSO4, ZnCl2 with different densities, have been tested alone or in combinations. Consequently, many studies have shown that a concentrated sugar solution is the most effective and preferred method for detecting tapeworm eggs [5, 6, 13–15].

Potassium carbonate (K2CO3), also known as potash or pearl ash, is a white salt that dissolves in water and forms a strongly alkaline solution. It is commonly used in the food and agricultural sector as an acidity regulator, stabiliser and fertiliser [16]. Parasitologically, Fok et al. [17] and Rehbein et al. [18] reported its use in a solution form to detect helminth ova in dog and sheep faeces, respectively. Moreover, it has recently been suggested as a non-toxic, high-density floating solution for microplastic isolation from beach sediments [19]. Here, we evaluated potassium carbonate solution (PCS) for detecting tapeworm eggs in horse faeces and compared it with saturated sugar solution (SSS) by using them in a combined sedimentation + flotation technique.

Methods

To evaluate the diagnostic potential of PCS, two different examination solutions were employed to compare the detection rates of tapeworm eggs in naturally infected horses.

Faecal sampling

Faecal samples were collected from 241 horses on 9 farms with a known history of tapeworms, of both sexes and different ages, that had not been treated with anthelmintics for 3–5 months prior to the study. The animals were primarily Thoroughbreds and appeared healthy on the sampling day; however, no specific clinical examination was conducted. Faecal samples were taken from the rectum or picked up from the uncontacted parts of the ground which had been freshly defecated. All samples were placed in plastic bags, tagged, and transported to the laboratory at 4 °C.

Preparation of floating solutions

SSS was prepared by dissolving 450 g of granulated sugar in 350 ml of hot water (specific gravity (SG = 1.30). PCS was prepared after dissolving 500 g K2CO3 in 500 ml hot water and leaving it at room temperature for 24 h. Finally, it was filtered on filter paper and the SG (1.45) was measured.

Faecal examination

All samples were examined for tapeworm eggs using a combined sedimentation + flotation (sedimentation + centrifuge-sedimentation + flotation) technique modified from Proudman and Edwards [5]. Briefly, after thorough mixing of each faecal material, 20 g of each sample was homogenized with 250 ml of tap water in a ceramic dish. The faecal suspension was sieved through a 240 μm mesh into 500 ml glass bottles and left at room temperature overnight. Thereafter, the supernatant was decanted; the remaining sediment was transferred into two centrifuge tubes and re-suspended in tap water. Both tubes were centrifuged at 200 g for 10 min and supernatants were removed with a water-jet aspirator pump. The sediment was stirred with a glass stick; SSS and PCS were added to the first and second tubes, respectively. When a slight convex meniscus formed, a 22 mm × 22 mm coverslip was placed on the top of both tubes and left for 30 min. Then, the coverslips with the attached suspension were removed, placed on a microscope slide and examined for tapeworm eggs using a compound light microscope at a magnification of ×100. Samples with at least one egg were considered positive. Additionally, the visual estimation of the slide preparations’ conditions was also performed based on criteria such as clarity and adverse effects on egg appearance.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using the statistical software package Minitab [20]. The results of the SSS and PCS were compared for significant differences (P < 0.05) by the McNemar’s test. The kappa statistic was calculated to assess the agreement between the results of the two methods using the SSS and PCS, respectively.

Results

All recovered eggs were identified as Anoplocephalids and no species differentiation was made. In total, faecal samples from 60 (24.9%) of all 241 horses tested positive for Anoplocephalid eggs, of which 43 (17.8%) and 59 (24.4%) were detected by SSS and PCS, respectively (Table 1). The PCS detected significantly more positive samples than the SSS (P < 0.05). The results of the PCS substantially agreed with those of the SSS (Kappa value: 0.78; 95% confidence interval: 0.65–0.90).

Discussion

Because Anoplocephalid species detection was not performed, it is challenging to determine the species proportions among the positive samples. There is limited data on the species composition of Anoplocephalids in the study area, with only two records indicating the presence of both A. perfoliata and A. magna [21, 22].

Diagnosing cestode infections in horses is generally a significant challenge for parasite management. While there has been significant research on detecting tapeworms, particularly A. perfoliata, through serological assays (identification of coproantigens or detection of antibodies against parasite antigens) [9, 11] and PCR (identification of copro-DNA) [23], practical diagnosis often relies on examining faeces for tapeworm eggs [24]. However, an essential issue in the coprological detection of tapeworm infections is the sporadic release of proglottids by adult cestodes, resulting in irregular egg excretion [1]. There are several coprological methods to detect Anoplocephala eggs, which exhibit generally high specificities, ranging from 98% to 100% [5, 25], but lower sensitivities, varying from 0% to 62% [14, 26]. Modified techniques and various solutions have been examined to improve the sensitivity of tapeworm egg detection. For instance, a double centrifugation technique has demonstrated improved detection rates for Anoplocephala eggs and has proven to be more efficient than traditional and even advanced methods, such as Mini-FLOTAC [6, 27]. Similarly, in the present study, sedimentation followed by centrifugation was employed, corresponding to double centrifugation, mainly based on the technique by Proudman and Edwards [5].

Furthermore, the selection of an appropriate flotation solution is a critical factor that can significantly influence the accuracy of egg detection. A concentrated sugar solution proved to be the most effective for detecting tapeworm eggs [5, 6, 13–15]. Accordingly, we also used a saturated sugar solution (SSS) as a baseline to compare the results obtained by PCS. Thus, the results of the PCS substantially agreed with those of the SSS, and the PCS detected clearly more positive samples than the SSS (59/241 vs. 43/241, p-value = 0.0004). This increased egg detection rate of PCS may be attributed to its greater specific gravity (SG = 1.45) compared to that of SSS (SG = 1.30). Indeed, the SG of a flotation solution can significantly influence the recovery of eggs in coprological examinations [23]. It has long been documented that the optimal flotation of parasite eggs is achieved with solutions having an SG between 1.22 and 1.35 [28]. In fact, numerous flotation media, including SSS, fall within this SG range. However, the inability to consistently detect parasite ova using these solutions suggests that additional factors may influence the effectiveness of the flotation process. This is well demonstrated by Norris et al. [29], who determined for the first time the SG of A. perfoliata eggs. The SG of A. perfoliata eggs was measured at 1.0636, placing it between the specific gravities of strongyle and Parascaris eggs, which indicates a tendency for these eggs to float readily. Nevertheless, since this has not always been observed in practice, the authors assert that the difficulties encountered in the coprological diagnosis of A. perfoliata infections are likely not attributable to the specific gravity of the eggs. In a subsequent study conducted to ascertain the passive flotation speeds of various endoparasite eggs within a flotation solution, findings indicated that the eggs of A. perfoliata exhibited slower flotation rates and a higher degree of variability in comparison to the eggs of Parascaris and strongylids [30]. Regarding the results of the latter two studies, the coprological detection of A. perfoliata eggs may require modifications to standard egg flotation techniques to establish protocols specific to tapeworms. Although Norris et al. [29] noted that the SG of flotation solutions was not considered crucial, the current study employed a flotation solution (PCS) with a high SG of 1.45 as a significant modification. This yielded better results than the concentrated sugar solution, preferred medium in many studies for detecting tapeworm eggs in equids. Therefore, further studies are required to identify such variables in the coprological methods influencing the detection of tapeworm eggs.

Fok et al. [17] compared PCS (density = 1.45 g/ml) with Breza’s combined concentrating fluid (three parts of concentrated MgSO4 + three parts of concentrated Na2S2O3 + one part of water; density = 1.3 g/ml) to detect helminth ova in dogs’ faeces. PCS detected eggs of Trichuris vulpis, Ancylostomatidae, Toxocara canis, Toxascaris leonina, Capillaria spp., Taenia-type, and Dipylidium caninum at similar levels to Breza’s fluid, or mostly lower. While the SG of T. canis, T. leonina and A. caninum eggs ranges from 1.0559 to 1.0900, it is measured as 1.1453 for T. vulpis and 1.2251 for Taenia spp. eggs [31], all clearly below the SG of PCS. Factors related to coprological methodology, as well as species-specific aspects concerning animals or parasites, may have influenced the observed lower detection rates. These findings again emphasize the importance of implementing species-specific endoparasite egg flotation protocols to ensure the reliable detection of clinically important parasite eggs.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that potassium carbonate solution is more effective than concentrated sugar solution for detecting Anoplocephala eggs in horse faecal samples and offers an alternative medium for diagnosing tapeworms. Controlling these parasites is now more critical than ever, as recent studies [32, 33] have reported treatment failures of praziquantel and pyrantel pamoate, the commonly used anthelmintics against tapeworm infections. Consequently, detecting infected animals both before and after treatments remains a significant challenge in the effort to combat these parasites. Hence, in practical field conditions, it is important to encourage the utilization of more effective fecal diagnostic methods, such as the double centrifugation technique paired with suitable flotation fluids like potassium carbonate. These approaches represent valuable advancements that can significantly enhance our diagnostic capabilities.
