Anvarbek Meirmanov. Joint filtration of compressible immiscible liquids.


Abstract
We consider initial boundary value problems arising in mathematical models for joint motion of two immiscible viscous liquids in a pore space of the solid skeleton. First, we consider this physical process at the microscopic level (the pore size ε \varepsilon is approximately 5-20 microns) governed by the model A ε \textbf{A}^{\varepsilon} , consisting of Lame equations for the solid skeleton and the Stokes equations for the liquid components. Next, assuming the existence of a weak solution to the corresponding initial boundary value problem at the microscopic level for the model the B ε \textbf{B}^{\varepsilon} , that approximate the model A ε \textbf{A}^{\varepsilon} , and using the homogenization procedure we derive the Biot’s model describing the physical process in consideration at the macroscopic level for two weakly viscous immiscible fluids in the elastic solid skeleton at the macroscopic level.



Key words:
Joint motion of two viscous fluids, free boundary problems, two-scale convergence, homogenization of periodic structures.

2000 Mathematics Subject Classification: 35Q35.

1
Introduction.

Mathematical models of joint filtration of two different fluids are the main part of mathematical models of oil slurry displacement or mathematical models of filtration of radioactive slurry from tailings ponds. Since mining is a very important and urgent economic task, accurate modeling of joint filtration of two different fluids is also an important and urgent economic task. It is well known that solid ground is a complex heterogeneous medium. Heterogeneity means that soil properties vary in space. Moreover, analyses of boreholes and cores show that physical properties of soil change in volumes of several tens of microns when both liquid and solid components of soil are present in these volumes. Such media are called heterogeneous porous media. The area occupied by the fluid is called the pore space, and the area complementing it is called the solid skeleton. In an ideal situation, to describe the displacement of oil, the characteristics of the soil and fluids, a block with mathematical models and a block for visualizing the corresponding initial-boundary value problems of this mathematical model. Today, most hydrodynamic simulators are based on the Buckley-Leverett macroscopic model (see [1]-[5]). By design, existing simulators must adequately represent the physical process being simulated. Does the Buckley-Leverett model adequately represent the physical process being modeled? In the case of phenomenological models, only experiment can serve as the criterion of adequacy. But is experiment a criterion for the adequacy of a mathematical model to the physical process being described?
The answer is no, and here’s why. Any phenomenological model contains sufficient free parameters and even functions that are in no way related to the reservoir geometry (porosity and pore structure) or the physical characteristics of the displacement process (viscosity and density of the filtered fluids and the density and elastic properties of the solid skeleton).

Therefore, by varying the specified parameters, it is always possible to achieve agreement with any experiment! All phenomenological models are constructed according to the same scenario: fluid dynamics are described by Darcy’s filtration law or its variants, and impurity migration is described by variants of the diffusion-convection equation. This description is quite understandable, since the main mechanism of the physical process is generated by processes occurring at an unknown (free) boundary between different liquids, and it is not described in any way in the proposed macroscopic models.

It is at the free boundary
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}(t)
that the interaction of two different fluids occurs, and it is this interaction that changes the dynamics of the entire flow. All these fundamental changes occur at the microscopic level, while any existing phenomenological model operates with completely different (orders of magnitude larger) ones. Thus, all phenomenological models are simply a set of axioms belonging to the authors themselves. However, it is impossible to accurately assess the adequacy of each phenomenological model to the physical process due to the lack of criteria for physical adequacy.
Such criteria are absent from the existing literature. Therefore, it is necessary to find reliable criteria for adequacy. Exact modeling involves describing a given process using equations for liquids and solids, which follow from the postulates of classical Newtonian continuum mechanics or their consequences. The validity of these postulates has been confirmed for centuries. The result of such modeling is exact mathematical model for filtration of two different fluids at the microscopic level. This description, which we call the
Microscopic description
, is the best possible. The liquid motion is governed by Stokes equations for compressible fluids and the motion of the solid skeleton is described by the Lame equations.

This description is the best possible. The only obstacle to using models at the microscopic level is that, in domains hundreds of meters in size, any numerical implementation of microscopic models at a spatial resolution of a few microns takes years, which is completely unacceptable.

A solution to this problem was proposed in the works of J. B. Keller, R. Burrige [6], and independently by E. Sanchez-Palencia [7]. This is a
Homogenization method
, that allows for a mathematically rigorous transition from a microscopic description to a
Macroscopic description
(average size of hundreds of decimeters). The key role is here played by the presence of small model parameter
{\varepsilon}
(the dimensionless size of periodicity the cell of the solid soil skeleton).
Homogenization
means the limiting procedure from a
Microscopic model
to
Macroscopic models
as small parameter
{\varepsilon}
tend to zero.

In the present manuscript we consider a joint filtration of liquids
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
in pore space of the solid skeleton
\Omega^{\varepsilon}_{f}
. This basic model we denote as microscopic model
\textbf{A}^{\varepsilon}
.

There are several options:

1) incompressible slightly viscous fluids in the absolutely rigid solid skeleton
(model
\textbf{A}_{1}^{\varepsilon}
),

2) compressible slightly viscous fluids in the elastic solid skeleton
(model
\textbf{A}_{2}^{\varepsilon}
),

3) incompressible slightly viscous fluids in the incompressible elastic solid skeleton
(model
\textbf{A}_{3}^{\varepsilon}
) and

4) compressible slightly viscous fluids in the compressible elastic solid skeleton
(model
\textbf{A}_{4}^{\varepsilon}
).

As a next step we derive al possible homogenizations of the microscopic model
\textbf{A}^{\varepsilon}
, describing the joint filtration of two liquids at the macroscopic level:

1) incompressible slightly viscous fluids in the absolutely rigid solid skeleton
(model
\textbf{H}_{1}
),

2) compressible slightly viscous fluids in the elastic solid skeleton (model
\textbf{H}_{2}
),

3) incompressible slightly viscous fluids in the incompressible elastic solid skeleton
(model
\textbf{H}_{3}
) and

4) compressible slightly viscous fluids in the compressible elastic solid skeleton
(model
\textbf{H}_{4}
).

The main problem in this procedure is correctness of the mathematical models at the microscopic level. The only one difference between fluids is viscosities. It can be seen that the viscosity of each particle remains the same throughout the entire process under consideration and coincides with its initial value. That is, the viscosities satisfy the transport equation. Keeping in mind following procedures and smoothness of the solutions to the transport equation we need to approximate the model
\textbf{A}_{k}^{\varepsilon}
by models
\textbf{B}_{k,h}^{\varepsilon},\,k=1,2,3,4
, for which the corresponding initial boundary-value problems have an unique solution (weak or classical) stable with respect to small perturbations and exist homogenization models
\textbf{H}_{k,h},\,k=1,2,3,4
.

Approximation means that all homogenizations
\textbf{H}_{k}
of the models
\textbf{A}_{k}^{\varepsilon}
coincide with corresponding homogenizations of the models
\displaystyle\,\textbf{B}_{k,h}^{\varepsilon}=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow{0}}\textbf{B}^{\varepsilon}_{k,h},\,k=1,2,3,4
.

In the present manuscript we use notations of functional spaces and norm adopted in [8], [9].
