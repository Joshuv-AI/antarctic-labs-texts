An extension of smooth numbers: multiple dense divisibility
Garo Sarajian and Andreas Weingartner

Abstract
. The i i -tuply y y -densely divisible numbers were introduced by a Polymath project, as a weaker condition on the moduli than y y -smoothness, in distribution estimates for primes in arithmetic progressions. We obtain the order of magnitude of the count of these integers up to x x , uniformly in x x and y y , for every fixed natural number i i .



1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{S}_{y}
denote the set of of natural numbers whose prime factors are all less than or equal to
y
.
Such integers are called
y
-smooth (or
y
-friable) and they appear in many different areas of number theory
[
3
,
4
,
7
]
.
Zhang
[
18
]
established the existence of infinitely many pairs of primes with bounded gaps
via distribution estimates for primes in arithmetic progressions to smooth moduli.
In the Polymath paper
[
6
]
, the condition of
y
-smoothness for the moduli
is relaxed to the condition of being
i
-tuply
y
-densely divisible, allowing for a larger set of moduli in their estimates.
The intent is to have a set of integers, as large as possible, where we have control over the location of enough “nice” divisors,
i.e. divisors who themselves have enough (nice) divisors whose location we can control, etc.
In Theorem
1
, we determine the order of magnitude of the count of
i
-tuply
y
-densely divisible numbers up to
x
, uniformly in
x
and
y
, for every fixed
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

1.1.
The
i
-tuply
y
-densely divisible numbers

We say that a finite, increasing sequence of natural numbers is
y
-dense
if the ratios of consecutive terms of the sequence are all
\leq y
.

Define
\mathcal{D}_{0,y}=\mathbb{N}
. For
i\geq 1
, let
n\in\mathcal{D}_{i,y}
if and only if the increasing sequence
\{d:d|n,d\in\mathcal{D}_{i-1,y}\}
forms a
y
-dense sequence containing
1
and
n
. We say that the members of
\mathcal{D}_{i,y}
are
i
-tuply
y
-densely divisible, or
(i,y)
-densely divisible, which we sometimes abbreviate as
(i,y)
-d.d.

For example, when
y=2
, the members up to
32
are given by

\mathcal{D}_{1,2}=\{1,2,4,6,8,12,16,18,20,24,28,30,32,...\},

\mathcal{D}_{2,2}=\{1,2,4,8,12,16,24,32,...\},

\mathcal{D}_{3,2}=\{1,2,4,8,16,24,32,...\},

and, for
i\geq 4
,

\mathcal{D}_{i,2}=\{1,2,4,8,16,32,...\}.

In general, the definition of
\mathcal{D}_{i,y}
implies

\mathcal{D}_{i+1,y}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{i,y}\qquad(i\geq 0).

By Corollary
1
,

\mathcal{S}_{y}=\bigcap_{i\geq 0}\mathcal{D}_{i,y}.

Thus, the integers that are
i
-tuply
y
-densely divisible for all
i\geq 0
are exactly the
y
-smooth numbers.

The following somewhat stronger definition is used in Polymath
[
6
]
:

Define
\mathcal{D}^{*}_{0,y}=\mathbb{N}
. For
i\geq 1
, let
n\in\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,y}
if and only if for each
j
,
0\leq j\leq i-1
,
the increasing sequence
\{d:d|n,d\in\mathcal{D}^{*}_{j,y}\text{ and }n/d\in\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i-1-j,y}\}
forms a
y
-dense sequence containing
1
and
n
.
We call the members of
\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,y}
strongly
i
-tuply
y
-densely divisible, or strongly
(i,y)
-densely divisible.

As above, the definition of
\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,y}
implies

\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i+1,y}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,y}\qquad(i\geq 0),

and, by Corollary
1
,

\mathcal{S}_{y}=\bigcap_{i\geq 0}\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,y}.

Considering
j=i-1
in the definition of
\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,y}
, we see that
\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,y}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{i,y}
for all
i\geq 0
.
Note that
\mathcal{D}_{i,y}=\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,y}
for
0\leq i\leq 2
.
The members up to
32
displayed above are unchanged if
\mathcal{D}_{i,2}
is replaced by
\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,2}
.
However,
8424\in\mathcal{D}_{3,2}\setminus\mathcal{D}^{*}_{3,2}
and
65520\in\mathcal{D}_{4,2}\setminus\mathcal{D}^{*}_{4,2}
.

We define the corresponding counting functions as

D_{i}(x,y):=|[1,x]\cap\mathcal{D}_{i,y}|,\quad D^{*}_{i}(x,y):=|[1,x]\cap\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,y}|.

1.2.
Describing
\mathcal{D}_{i,y}
(resp.
\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,y}
) by conditions on the prime factors

Let
\theta
be an arithmetic function and let
\mathcal{B}_{\theta}
be the set of positive integers containing
n=1
and all
n=p_{1}\cdots p_{k}
,
whose prime factors
p_{1}\leq\cdots\leq p_{k}
satisfy
p_{j+1}\leq\theta(p_{1}\cdots p_{j})
for
0\leq j<k
.
Tenenbaum
[
11
]
showed that
\mathcal{D}_{1,y}=\mathcal{B}_{\theta}
with
\theta(n)=yn
.

Lemma 1

(Tenenbaum
[
11
]
)
.

Let
y\geq 2
and
n=p_{1}\cdots p_{k}
, where
p_{1}\leq\cdots\leq p_{k}
are prime. Then
n\in\mathcal{D}_{1,y}
if and only if

p_{j+1}\leq yp_{1}\cdots p_{j}\qquad(0\leq j<k).

That is,
\mathcal{D}_{1,y}=\mathcal{D}_{1,y}^{*}=\mathcal{B}_{\theta}
with
\theta(n)=yn
.

Proposition
1
reveals
\theta(n)
when
i=2
and shows that for all
i\geq 1
, we have
\mathcal{D}_{i,y}=\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{i,y}}
for some function
\theta_{i,y}
.
The case
i=2
suggests that there is no simple explicit formula for
\theta_{i,y}
when
i\geq 2
.
We state the following result without proof, since we will not be using it here.

Proposition 1
.

We have

\mathcal{D}_{2,y}=\mathcal{D}^{*}_{2,y}=\mathcal{B}_{\theta}
with
\theta(n)=y\max\limits_{d\in\mathcal{D}_{1,y}\atop d|n}\min(n/d,d)
.
For
i\geq 1
,
\mathcal{D}_{i,y}=\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{i,y}}
with
\theta_{i,y}(n)=\max(\{1\}\cup\{p:p\text{ prime, }np\in\mathcal{D}_{i,y}\})
.

Question 1
.

Let
i\geq 3
. Is
\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,y}=\mathcal{B}_{\theta^{*}_{i,y}}
for some function
\theta^{*}_{i,y}
?

Even without an answer to this question, we can determine the order of magnitude of the counting functions of the sets

\mathcal{D}_{i,y}
and
\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,y}
, with the help of the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

For all
i\in\mathbb{N}
and real
y\geq 2
,

\mathcal{S}_{y}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{l}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,y}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{i,y}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{u}},

where

\theta_{l}(n)=\max(y,(yn)^{1/i}),\quad\theta_{u}(n)=yn^{1/i}.

The proof of Theorem
2
is given in Section
2
.

The sets
\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{l}}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{u}}
are easier to understand than the sets
\mathcal{D}_{i,y}
and
\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,y}
,
since the former two are defined by explicit conditions on the prime factors, whereas the latter two are defined recursively with conditions on the divisors.
Theorem
4
provides estimates for the counting functions of
\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{l}}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{u}}
.
Theorem
2
then implies the desired estimates in Theorem
1
.

Corollary 1
.

For
y\geq 2
we have

\mathcal{S}_{y}=\bigcap_{i\geq 0}\mathcal{D}_{i,y}=\bigcap_{i\geq 0}\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,y}.

Proof of Corollary
1
.

The first three inclusions of

\mathcal{S}_{y}\subseteq\bigcap_{i\geq 0}\mathcal{D}^{*}_{i,y}\subseteq\bigcap_{i\geq 0}\mathcal{D}_{i,y}\subseteq\bigcap_{i\geq 0}\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{u}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{y}

follow at once from Theorem
2
.
To show the last inclusion, first note that
1
is in each set. Assume
m\geq 2
satisfies
m\in\bigcap_{i\geq 0}\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{u}}
.
Let
p
be the largest prime factor of
m
and write
m=np
.
Then
m\in\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{u}}
implies
p\leq\theta_{u}(n)=yn^{1/i}
. Since this is true for all
i\geq 0
, it follows that
p\leq y
and
m\in\mathcal{S}_{y}
.
∎

An extension of Dickman’s function

The counting function of the set
\mathcal{S}_{y}

For the other four sets appearing in Theorem

Estimates for the counting functions

Let
\mathcal{B}_{a,y}
be the set corresponding to
\theta(n)=yn^{a}
and let
B_{a}(x,y)

That is,
B_{a}(x,y)

1+\sum_{k\geq 1}|\{n=p_{1}\cdots p_{k}\leq x,\,p_{1}\leq\cdots\leq p_{k},\,p_{j+1}\leq y(p_{1}\cdots p_{j})^{a},\,0\leq j<k\}|.

\mathcal{B}^{*}_{a,y}

Let
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{a,y}
(resp.
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}^{*}_{a,y}

(resp.
\mathcal{B}^{*}_{a,y}

1.6.
Asymptotic estimates for the exponent
\lambda_{a}

When

, the explicit inequalities
[

, Eq. (8), (9)]
imply

\lambda_{a}=\frac{e^{-\gamma}}{a}\left(1+\frac{e^{-\gamma}\log(a+1)}{a}+\frac{r_{a}}{a}\right)\qquad(a\geq 1),

where
|r_{a}|\leq\frac{2}{3}
.
Theorem
6
reveals the behavior of
\lambda_{a}
as
a\to 0^{+}
.

Theorem 6
.

The exponent
\lambda_{a}
, defined in Theorem

, satisfies

\lambda_{a}=\frac{1}{a}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{a}\right)-1+o(1)\right)\qquad(a\to 0^{+}).

Thus,
\lambda_{1/i}=i\bigl(\log(i)-1+o(1)\bigr)
as

,

.

, which takes a considerable amount of effort, is given in Section

2.
Proof of Theorem
2

In this section, we write
\theta_{i,y}(n):=\theta_{l}(n)=\max(y,(yn)^{1/i})
and
\mathcal{L}_{i,y}:=\mathcal{B}_{\theta_{l}}
for
i\geq 1
. We define
\mathcal{L}_{0,y}:=\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
i\geq 1
,
y\geq 2
and
n\in\mathcal{L}_{i,y}
. Then for all
1\leq R\leq yn
and all integers
v,w\geq 0
with
v+w=i-1
, there is a factorization

n=d_{v}d_{w}
with
R/y\leq d_{w}\leq R
and
d_{w}\in\mathcal{L}_{w,y}
,
d_{v}\in\mathcal{L}_{v,y}
.

Proof.

Let
i\geq 1
and
y\geq 2
. We prove the lemma by induction on
k=\Omega(n)
. The lemma clearly holds for
n=1
, i.e.
k=0
, since
1=1\cdot 1
.
When
k=1
,
n=p
, a prime. Then
n\in\mathcal{L}_{i,y}
means
n=p\leq\theta_{i,y}(1)=y
.
If
n<R\leq yn
, we take
d_{w}=n
and
d_{v}=1
. If
1\leq R\leq n
, take
d_{w}=1
and
d_{v}=n
.
Since
n=p\leq y
, both
1
and
n
are members of
\mathcal{L}_{v,y}
and of
\mathcal{L}_{w,y}
for all
v,w\geq 0
.

Let
k\geq 1
and assume the lemma holds for all
n
with
\Omega(n)\leq k
.
Now let
\Omega(n)=k+1
,
n=mp
,
p\geq P^{+}(m)
,
\Omega(m)=k
and
n\in\mathcal{L}_{i,y}
.
Then
m\in\mathcal{L}_{i,y}
and
p\leq\theta_{i,y}(m)=\max(y,(ym)^{1/i})
.
Let
1\leq R\leq yn
and
v,w\geq 0
be integers with
v+w=i-1
. We consider two cases:

Case (1): Assume
1\leq R\leq\frac{my}{p^{v}}
. By the inductive hypothesis, there is a factorization of
m=d_{v}d_{w}
with
R/y\leq d_{w}\leq R
,

d_{w}\in\mathcal{L}_{w,y}
,
d_{v}\in\mathcal{L}_{v,y}
. Define
d_{v}^{\prime}=d_{v}p
. Then
d_{v}=m/d_{w}
and
d_{w}\leq R\leq\frac{my}{p^{v}}
implies

d_{v}\geq\frac{p^{v}}{y}
. Thus
p^{v}\leq yd_{v}
, which means that
d_{v}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{v,y}
. The desired factorization is
n=d_{w}d_{v}^{\prime}
.

Case (2): Assume
\frac{my}{p^{v}}<R\leq ny
. Define
R^{\prime}:=R/p\leq my
. If
p\leq y
, then
n\in\mathcal{S}_{y}
and the conclusion holds. Thus we may assume
p>y
.
We claim that
R^{\prime}\geq 1
.
Indeed, if
R^{\prime}<1
, then
R<p
, so
p>R>\frac{my}{p^{v}}
, which implies
p^{i}\geq p^{v+1}>my
, contradicting

\begin{split}&\frac{L}{\sqrt{2\alpha}}\int_{-T_{1}}^{T_{1}}\cos(c_{1}t+c_{3}t^{3})dt\\
&=\int_{-U}^{U}\left[\cos\left(-\frac{3\nu}{2}u+\frac{\nu}{2}u^{3}\right)+O(U\sqrt{\alpha}/L+U^{3}\alpha)\right]du\\
&=O(U^{2}\sqrt{\alpha}/L+U^{4}\alpha)+\int_{-U}^{U}\cos\left(-\frac{3\nu}{2}u+\frac{\nu}{2}u^{3}\right)du\\
&=O(U^{2}\sqrt{\alpha}/L+U^{4}\alpha+U^{-2})+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\cos\left(-\frac{3\nu}{2}u+\frac{\nu}{2}u^{3}\right)du\\
&=o(1)+K(\nu).\end{split}

Combining everything, we obtain

\int_{-T}^{T}\exp\{M_{a,r,n}(t)\}dt=\frac{\sqrt{2\alpha}}{L}(K(\nu)+o(1)),

as
a\to 0
.

It remains to show that

\int_{T}^{\pi}\exp\{M_{a,r,n}(t)\}dt=o\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\alpha}}{L}\right).

Let
t_{0}=7/L
and write

\int_{T}^{\pi}\exp\{M_{a,r,n}(t)\}dt=\int_{T}^{t_{0}}+\int_{t_{0}}^{\pi}=:I_{2}+I_{3}.

For
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
, we estimate the absolute value of the integrand. As in the case of a real saddle point, we have

\re(M_{a,r,n}(t))=-r(1-\cos t)+Q_{1}+Q_{2},

where

Q_{1}+Q_{2}=\int_{0}^{1}e^{arv}(1-\cos(arv\sin t))\frac{dv}{v}+\int_{0}^{1}((e^{arv}-e^{arv\cos t})\cos(arv\sin t))\frac{dv}{v}.

We note that

\frac{t^{2}}{5}\leq 1-\cos t\leq\frac{t^{2}}{2}\qquad(|t|\leq\pi).

To estimate
I_{3}
, we write

Q_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1/(art)}e^{arv}(art)^{2}vdv+\int_{1/(art)}^{1/2}e^{arv}2\frac{dv}{v}+\int_{1/2}^{1}e^{arv}2\frac{dv}{v}\\
\leq\frac{t}{2}e^{1/t}+2(art)\frac{e^{ar/2}}{ar}+4\frac{e^{ar}}{ar}\leq\frac{5r}{L^{2}},

for
a
sufficiently small and
|t|\geq t_{0}
.
Since
1-e^{-x}\leq x
for all
x
,

Q_{2}\leq\int_{0}^{1}e^{arv}(1-e^{-arvt^{2}/2})\frac{dv}{v}\leq\int_{0}^{1/2}e^{arv}ar(t^{2}/2)dv+\int_{1/2}^{1}e^{arv}2dv\\
\leq e^{ar/2}\pi^{2}/2+\frac{2e^{ar}}{ar}\leq\frac{3r}{L^{2}}.

We have
r(1-\cos t)\geq rt^{2}/5\geq rt_{0}^{2}/5
. Combining these three estimates we obtain

|I_{3}|\leq 2\pi\exp(-rt_{0}^{2}/5+8r/L^{2})\leq 2\pi\exp(-r/L^{2})=o\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\alpha}}{L}\right).

Next, we estimate
I_{2}
. For all real
t
we have

r(1-\cos t)\geq r\left(\frac{t^{2}}{2}-\frac{t^{4}}{24}\right).

When
|t|\leq t_{0}=7/L
, then
|\sin t|\leq 7/L
and

1-\cos(arv\sin t)\leq\frac{(arvt)^{2}}{2}-\frac{(arvt)^{4}}{\kappa},

for some suitable constant
\kappa
. Thus

Q_{1}\leq\int_{0}^{1}e^{arv}\left(\frac{(arvt)^{2}}{2}-\frac{(arvt)^{4}}{\kappa}\right)\frac{dv}{v}\leq\frac{t^{2}}{2}\left(are^{ar}-e^{ar}+1\right)-\frac{t^{4}}{2\kappa}(ar)^{3}e^{ar}

and

Q_{2}\leq\int_{0}^{1}e^{arv}(1-e^{-arvt^{2}/2})\frac{dv}{v}\leq\int_{0}^{1}e^{arv}\frac{art^{2}}{2}dv=\frac{t^{2}}{2}(e^{ar}-1).

For sufficiently small values of
a
and
t_{0}\geq|t|\geq T=\frac{\alpha^{1/3}}{L^{2}}
, we obtain

\begin{split}-r(1-\cos t)+Q_{1}+Q_{2}\leq&-r\left(\frac{t^{2}}{2}-\frac{t^{4}}{24}\right)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}are^{ar}-\frac{t^{4}}{2\kappa}(ar)^{3}e^{ar}\\
\leq&\frac{t^{2}}{2}r\left(-1+ae^{ar}\right)-\frac{t^{4}}{3\kappa}(ar)^{3}e^{ar}\\
\leq&\frac{t^{2}}{2}r\alpha-\frac{t^{4}}{4\kappa}rL^{2}\leq-\frac{t^{4}}{5\kappa}rL^{2}\leq-\frac{T^{4}}{5\kappa}rL^{2}\\
=&-\frac{\alpha^{4/3}}{5\kappa L^{6}}r\leq-a^{-1/10}.\end{split}

It follows that

|I_{2}|\leq 2\pi\exp(-a^{-1/10})\ll a=o\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\alpha}}{L}\right).

Combining everything, we have

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\exp\{M_{a,r,n}(t)\}dt=\frac{\sqrt{2\alpha}}{L}(K(\nu)+o(1)),

as
a\to 0
.
The result now follows from (
24
).
∎
