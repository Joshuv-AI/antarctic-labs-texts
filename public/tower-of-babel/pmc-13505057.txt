Investigating dentists’ perspectives of dental tourism trends in the Eastern Province, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Faisal Alharamlah, Ahmed Alghamdi, Rana AlBraik, Danah Albuhmdouh, Abdulrahman Alsulaimi, Faris Alshuraim, Amal Asiri, Muhammad Ashraf Nazir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDental tourism is a growing trend involving patients traveling across borders for dental treatment. While dental tourism may benefit some patients, it also poses considerable risks. However, the dental tourism trends have not been explored in Saudi Arabia. Aim The aim of the study was to investigate the perspectives of dental professionals about dental tourism in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia.MethodsA cross-sectional questionnaire-based study was conducted among dental professionals in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia. The study included male and female dental professionals (general dentists, specialists, and consultants) working in the private and public sectors in Dammam, Al Khobar, Al Jubail, and Al Ahsa. A convenience sampling technique was used to recruit the participants. The researchers visited clinics and hospitals and provided a self-administered questionnaire to participating dentists. The questionnaire included demographic information and questions related to encountering dental tourists, reasons for getting treatment abroad, problems related to treatment, and views about dental tourism. SPSS (Version 22.0) was used for statistical analyses and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe study included data from 326 dentists, with 62.9% of males and 37.1% of females. The majority of the participants (97.5%) reported that they encountered dental tourists in their dental practice. Multiple logistic regression showed that general practitioners were significantly less likely to encounter dental tourists compared to specialists/consultants (OR=0.08, P= 0.022). Affordable treatment (95.1%) was the most common reason for getting dental treatment abroad. Lack of treatment planning (73.6%), lack of availability of practitioner post treatment (66.6%), and lack of follow up maintenance (59.5%) were the most common problems related to dental treatment received abroad. The majority of patients received dental treatment in Egypt (71.8%), Turkey (69.6%), and GCC countries (50%). The veneers (81.9%), crowns (78.5%), and bridges (76.1%) were the most common dental treatments received abroad. The dentists reported that dental tourism could endanger patients’ dental health (58.9%), but it enhanced their professional experience (43.6%) and understanding of dental treatment provided in other countries (41.4%).ConclusionThe study concluded that dental tourism was very common in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia. Specialists and consultants were more likely to encounter dental tourists compared with general practitioners. Affordability of dental treatment was the most common reason for dental tourism. The veneers, crowns, and bridges were the most frequently received dental treatments abroad. Most dentists reported that dental tourism could compromise oral health of patients.

Introduction

Dental tourism, or the practice of patients seeking dental care across national borders, is a rapidly expanding global phenomenon [1, 2]. This phenomenon is driven by several factors, including high expenses of dental treatment in the local area, difficulties in securing timely access to local dental practitioners, effective care offered by many overseas clinics, and affordable airfare [3–5]. While dental tourism may benefit some patients, it also poses considerable risks. Some extensive treatments require a proper follow-up visit, which dental tourists cannot obtain from overseas dentists [5]. Other risk factors include inadequate training for the dentist and possibly the unacceptable implementation of infection control, which may lead to significant health consequences [6].

Kamath et al. noted that India provides dental care at substantial cost savings compared to developed countries, making India an attractive destination for dental tourists [1]. Jaapar et al. found that dental tourists in Malaysia were extremely satisfied with the dental care services [3]. A survey of 273 dental practitioners identified potential reasons for Hungary’s continued dominance in dental tourism, such as a beneficial cost/benefit ratio and superior dental care standards [7]. In New Zealand, dentists voiced concerns about inadequate care, insufficiently informed consent, and the discontinuation of care for dental tourists [8]. Croatia holds a competitive edge in dental tourism due to high-quality support services, tourist attractions, and a favourable geographic location [9].

There is a lack of evidence about dental tourism in Saudi Arabia. Understanding the views of dental practitioners is essential for assessing how dental tourism affects patient care and local practice. It is important to understand the prevalence of dentists encountering dental tourists and associated factors such as gender, nationality, years of dental practice, place of work, and professional qualifications. These demographic and professional characteristics of dentists influence the likelihood of encountering dental tourists in their dental practices. For example, specialist dentists may see more dental tourists in their clinics than general dentists. Similarly, dentists with many years of clinical experience may encounter more dental tourists than dentists with fewer years of clinical experience. Studying dental tourism may improve the quality of dental care and reduce dental treatment costs within the country. In light of the growing global relevance of dental tourism, understanding its implications through the perspectives of Saudi dental professionals is both timely and necessary. Therefore, the aim of the study was to investigate the perspectives of dental professionals about dental tourism in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia.

Methods

Study design, participants, and settings

A cross-sectional questionnaire-based study was conducted among dentists in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia. The dentists working in Dammam, Al Khobar, Al Jubail, and Al Ahsa were included in the study. A convenience sampling technique was used to recruit dentists (general dentists, specialists, and consultants) of both genders from the public and private sectors. The researchers visited dental clinics and hospitals and invited the readily available participants who were willing to participate in the study. This sampling technique helped fast and easy recruitment of participants with fewer resources. The data collection started from February 25, 2024 to June 30, 2024. The sample size (N = 338) was calculated by using a 5% margin of error, 50% population proportion, 95% confidence interval, and the size of the estimated study population (N ≈ 2800) according to the Ministry of Health [10]. The sample size calculation used 50% for the population proportion because there was no previous study about dental tourism in the country. The use of a 50% population proportion yields the maximum sample size and ensures adequate power of the study.

Instrument and procedure

The questionnaire was adopted from previous studies [8, 9] and consisted of three sections. The first section included demographic data such as gender, age, nationality, years in practice, place of training, professional qualification, and place of work. The second section was about dental professionals’ encounter with dental tourists, the location of dental treatment received abroad, the type of treatment, the reasons for getting treatment, problems or issues with treatment, and the provision of remedial treatments and their impacts. The third section asked dental professionals about their views related to dental tourism and its impact on dentists and dental practice in Saudi Arabia.

The research team critically reviewed the questionnaire to ensure its content validity and relevance to dental care in Saudi Arabia. Additionally, the team conducted a pilot test of the questionnaire, which involved 20 dentists, and no further corrections were necessary based on the results. Dental professionals were invited to participate in the study by visiting various dental clinics and hospitals. Those who agreed to participate completed consent forms and the questionnaires. The questionnaire in the English language was administered among study participants.

Ethics

Ethical approval for the study (IRB-02-2024-243) was provided by the Deanship of Scientific Research at Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University, Dammam. Participants’ confidentiality and privacy were ensured during the conduct of the study. Approvals from the administration of hospitals and clinics in Dammam, Al Khobar, Al Jubail, and Al Ahsa were obtained, and participating dentists were requested to fill up a soft copy of the questionnaire. The study procedure and its advantages were explained to the participants who provided informed consent before questionnaire administration. The participants were informed that their participation in the study was voluntary and anonymous. The consent form contained information about the study, its objectives, and benefits. The ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki were followed during the execution of the study.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). For the study variables, frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were computed. Chi-square test and multiple logistic regression analysis were performed to investigate the association between demographic factors and dentists encountering dental tourists. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The total number of respondents in this survey was 326 with a mean age of 39.61 ± 8.88 years. Most respondents were males (62.9%), Saudis (75.8%), specialists/consultants (64.7%), received training in Saudi Arabia (52.8%), and worked in the private sector (67.5%). About half of the sample (50.3%) had more than 10 years of clinical experience (Table 1).

There were 97.5% of dental practitioners who encountered dental tourists in their practice. About 42.3% of the sample of dental professionals encountered dental tourists within a month.

The dental practitioners responded to questions about the problems or issues with treatment received abroad. These problems included lack of treatment planning, lack of availability of practitioner post-treatment, use of componentry which may need replacing but is not available in Saudi Arabia, cost of traveling again if the work is problematic, lack of follow-up maintenance, absence of informed consent, and lack of records. The lack of treatment planning (73.6%, n = 240) and the lack of practitioner availability post-operatively (66.6%, n = 217) were the most common problems or issues with dental treatment received abroad.

Affordable dental treatment was cited as the main reason for seeking treatment abroad by 95.1% of practitioners. Other important reasons included vacation travel (53.1%), studying abroad (52.8%), and better quality of treatment (23%). Bivariate analysis showed that years in practice, professional qualification, and place of work were significantly associated with encountering dental tourists. The dentists who practiced equal or less than 10 years were significantly less likely (OR = 0.14, P = 0.030) to encounter dental tourists than those who practiced for more than 10 years. The general practitioners were significantly less likely (OR = 0.07, P = 0.002) to encounter dental tourists than specialists or consultants. Dental professionals who worked in government sectors had significantly lower odds (OR = 0.67, P = 0.047) of encountering dental tourists than those who worked in the private sector (Table 3).

Discussion

The present study was conducted to investigate the perspectives of dental practitioners regarding patients seeking dental treatment outside of Saudi Arabia. The findings suggested that a high number of dental practitioners met with dental patients who previously sought dental treatment abroad. These findings are in accordance with the results reported by Lovelock et al., who observed that 96% of dental professionals in New Zealand encountered patients who received dental treatment abroad [8]. Similar trends were observed in the UK, where 95% of respondents met with patients who received dental treatment in foreign countries [11]. Low cost of dental treatment, quality of care, vacation travel, and easy access to dental services may account for many dental tourists travelling abroad [3–5]. In addition, a high prevalence of dental tourism points to a substantial market potential for the global market. For example, 67.5% of dental clinics were interested in investing in the dental tourism national program in Romania [12].

Professional qualification of participants was significantly and independently associated with encountering dental tourists in the present study. General practitioners were significantly less likely to encounter dental tourists compared to specialists or consultants. This important finding suggests that dental tourists may have experienced post-treatment complications after receiving low-quality dental treatments abroad, requiring more specialized procedures from specialists and consultants [11]. Therefore, they were more likely to consult specialists or consultants for the relief of their more complex dental problems. In addition, lack of continuity of dental treatment for dental tourists may aggravate their existing dental conditions, which requires treatment from a specialist or consultant [18].

Globally, popular destinations for dental tourists include Hungary, Romania, Poland, Bulgaria, Croatia, Turkey, Argentina, Mexico, Costa Rica, Peru, Thailand, Singapore, Malaysia, Korea, the Philippines, and India [13, 14]. The dentists in the present study reported that Egypt attracted 71.8% and Turkey 69.6%, while other GCC countries took 50% of dental tourists. This is quite different from what is observed in studies done in other geographical locations. For instance, Ashiti and Moshkun report that UK patients mostly travel to Hungary and Thailand for dental care [4]. Similarly, Jaapar et al. found that dental tourists who received dental treatments in Malaysia were mainly from Southeast Asia, Australia, and New Zealand [3]. These differences may be explained by cultural affinities, geographical proximity, affordability of dental treatment, and established infrastructure for dental tourism in destination countries [15].

Implants, prosthodontics, and oral surgery procedures are considered the most profitable dental treatments, and significant price differences in the home country and abroad are one of the reasons for dental tourism [13]. In the present study, dental treatments most sought abroad included cosmetic and restorative procedures such as veneers (81.9%), crowns (78.5%), and bridges (76.1%). In contrast, Ramachandra found that implants accounted for 59.5% of the treatments received abroad, followed by endodontic therapies (52.5%) and oral surgeries (44.2%) [16]. Another similar study by Lwin et al. stated that treatments received by dental tourists in Bangkok included teeth cleaning (40.6%), followed by restorations (32.1%), and oral surgery (26.4%) [17]. Variations in the types of treatments sought may reflect differences in patients’ needs, dentists’ recommendations, and the range of procedures available across countries.

The literature indicates that the most common motivators for dental tourism include lack of availability of dental treatment, dissatisfaction with treatment at home, lower cost of dental treatment, high-quality dental care abroad, and leisure travel [9, 14]. The present study showed a multi-factorial motivation pattern; however, cost was the major driving factor. Affordability of treatment was selected by 95.1% of our practitioners as the main reason for dental tourism. This is consistent with research conducted by Lovelock et al., where dentists in New Zealand expressed cost as the major motivator of dental tourism [18]. Kovacs and Szocska (2013) similarly reported that the cost in Hungary was 40%-60% lower compared to Western Europe [19]. On the other hand, Lwin et al.‘s study showed that the quality of treatment was the top driver for 78% of the dental tourists, followed by the accessibility of the location and appeal of the destination in Bangkok [17]. Similar findings were revealed by Jaapar et al. (2017) who found that 75% of patients identified high-quality dental care as their main motivation for dental tourism [3].

Regarding the problems associated with dental tourism, dentists in the present study identified the lack of treatment planning (73.6%), limited availability of post-treatment (66.6%), and inadequate follow-up maintenance (59.5%) as the most common issues. Similarly, a lack of follow-up maintenance (80.4%), insufficient availability of post-treatment (73.7%) and lack of treatment records (52.7%) were the most common problems related to dental tourism reported by the dentists in New Zealand [8]. It is understandable that dentists identify these significant concerns with dental tourism because of their impact on the success of dental treatment, continuity of care, and patient safety.

Additionally, concerns may be due to the differences in the training and licensing of dental professionals, regulations and accreditation of dental clinics, and quality of dental services globally [20]. While 58.9% of respondents in the present study believed dental tourism could impair patients’ dental health, 43.6% acknowledged that it enriched their professional experience because of novel challenges. It thus appears that there is a dual nature of the responses; this tallies with the results of investigation conducted in Croatia by Peručić’s wherein practitioners welcomed both opportunities and challenges associated with dental tourism [9].

The findings in the present study carry important implications for dental practice and policy in Saudi Arabia, contributed new knowledge to the growing body of evidence related to dental tourism, and call for coordination and standardization of dental care among dental professionals globally. The study results may inform policy makers to develop national guidelines for informed consent for patients considering treatment abroad, creating a referral network for follow-up care, and accreditation standards for clinics.

The present study has several limitations that warrant discussion. The data were based on self-reported responses from dental professionals and may introduce recall or subjective bias. A random sampling technique is ideal for a representative sample for a study. However, a convenience sampling technique was used in the study due to the challenges of accessing the centralized registry of dental professionals. The convenience sample may not represent the population of dentists, leading to selection bias. The study population was from the Eastern province, which limits the generalizability of its findings to dental practitioners in other geographic locations. Male participants were overrepresented in the study because there are more male than female dentists in the country. The cross-sectional study design precludes the ability to identify causality between dental tourism and treatment complications. Additionally, a lack of patient perspectives limits a full understanding of patient motivations for dental tourism. Future studies should explore the perspectives of both providers and patients related to dental tourism, strategies for risk mitigation, and mechanisms for improving coordination between dental practitioners across borders. In addition, a longitudinal study design should be considered to explore the association between independent factors (for example, age, gender, years in practice, etc.) and dependent factors (for example, encountering dental tourists).

Conclusions

The present study showed that dental tourism was highly prevalent in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia. Specialists and consultants were more likely to encounter dental tourists compared with general practitioners. The affordability of dental treatment was the most common reason for receiving dental treatment abroad. Egypt and Turkey were the major destinations for dental tourists seeking dental treatments. Aesthetic and restorative treatments, particularly veneers, crowns, and bridges, were the most common treatments sought abroad. Most respondents were concerned about treatment planning and follow-up care for dental tourists. These findings coincide with the broader growth of medical and dental tourism in Saudi Arabia, underscoring the need for coordinated policies and professional standards to ensure patient safety and continuity of care. The policymakers should create official accreditation standards, form a referral system for post-treatment care for dental tourists, and develop national guidelines for dental tourism in the country.
