Isometric immersions and weak solutions to the Darboux equation
Wentao Cao, Jonas Hirsch, and Dominik Inauen

Abstract
. We study the Darboux equation, a fundamental PDE arising in the theory of isometric immersions of two-dimensional Riemannian manifolds into ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} , in the low-regularity regime. We introduce a notion of weak solution for u ∈ C 1 , θ u\in C^{1,\theta} with θ > 1 / 2 \theta>1/2 , and show that the classical correspondence between solutions of the Darboux equation and isometric immersions remains valid in this regime. The key ingredient is an extension of the classical flatness criterion to Hölder continuous metrics, achieved via an analysis of a weak notion of Gaussian curvature.



1.
Introduction

It is well-known that sufficiently regular (e.g.,
C^{2}
or
C^{3}
) isometric immersions (i.e., length-preserving immersions from Riemannian manifolds
(M,g)
into Euclidean space) automatically satisfy higher-order equations that relate intrinsic to extrinsic geometry, such as the Gauss equation or the Codazzi-Mainardi system. One such equation, which serves as the central object of study in this paper, is the Darboux equation

\det\nabla_{g}^{2}u=K_{g}(1-|du|_{g}^{2}).

(1.1)

Here,
g
is a
C^{2}
Riemannian metric on a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
K_{g}
denotes its Gaussian curvature,
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a
C^{2}
function, and
\nabla_{g}^{2}u
is the “Riemannian Hessian” of
u
, i.e., the
(1,1)
-tensor obtained from the covariant Hessian
\nabla du
by raising an index, see Section
2.1
.

Equation (
1.1
) was first derived by Darboux in 1894
[
12
]
(see also
[
21
,
25
]
for later developments and related curvature equations). The connection between (
1.1
) and isometric immersions is given by the following correspondence (see
[
18
]
): if
\mathbf{r}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an isometric immersion of class
C^{2}
, then the function
u=\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{e}
solves equation (
1.1
) for each
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, a fact which follows a direct computation using the Gauss equation for
\mathbf{r}
. Conversely, given a smooth solution
u
of (
1.1
) with
|du|_{g}<1
, one can construct a smooth mapping
\phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
r=(\phi,u)
defines a smooth isometric immersion of
(\Omega,g)
into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This correspondence establishes the Darboux equation as a fundamental tool for studying the existence and properties of smooth isometric immersions.

In general, the validity of higher-order equations relating intrinsic and extrinsic geometry typically leads to uniqueness (also called rigidity) properties of isometric immersions—as exemplified by classical results for the Weyl problem
[
10
,
19
]
—or to non-existence results such as those of Hilbert
[
20
]
and Efimov
[
17
]
for isometric immersions of class
C^{2}
and higher.

By contrast, the celebrated Nash–Kuiper theorem
[
31
,
26
]
established the counterintuitive abundance and flexibility of isometric immersions of class
C^{1}
: any short immersion can be uniformly approximated by isometric
C^{1}
immersions, leading to the existence of highly oscillatory and non-unique isometries. This contrast naturally leads to the question of whether there exists a critical Hölder threshold
\theta_{0}
that distinguishes the flexible regime (
C^{1,\theta}
with
\theta<\theta_{0}
) from the rigid regime (
\theta>\theta_{0}
).

In recent years, significant work has been devoted to understanding this regime. The Nash-Kuiper theorem has been extended to
C^{1,\theta}
isometric immersions for various ranges of
\theta
in the works
[
11
,
14
,
13
,
9
,
6
,
4
]
. On the other hand, rigidity has been shown to persist at higher regularity: the solution of the Weyl problem remains rigid in the class
C^{1,\theta}
for
\theta>2/3

[
11
,
3
,
32
]
; see also
[
16
]
for a related rigidity result in the flat case. Analogous flexibility and rigidity phenomena have been shown to hold for very weak solutions to the Monge–Ampère equation, see
[
29
,
8
,
5
,
28
,
27
,
23
,
22
]
. Note that these very weak solutions are defined through the very weak Hessian (cf.
[
24
]
) and are therefore different from our Definition
1.1
below, even though the Darboux equation is of Monge–Ampère type.

In light of this flexibility-rigidity dichotomy, a natural and important question arises: does a suitable weak notion of higher-order constraint equations relating intrinsic and extrinsic geometry continue to hold in the low-regularity regime
C^{1,\theta}
for sufficiently large
\theta
? Indeed, such weak constraint equations can serve as the key technical tools for establishing rigidity results. For instance, the central ingredient in the proof of the rigidity theorem in the class
C^{1,\theta}
for
\theta>2/3
given in
[
11
]
is the validity of an integrated version of the Theorema Egregium,
K_{g}dV_{g}=N^{*}(dV_{\mathbb{S}^{2}})
, in this regularity regime. Similarly, in
[
13
,
7
]
, a weak variant of the Gauss equation was shown to hold for isometries of class
C^{1,\theta}
with
\theta>1/2
, yielding certain rigidity properties for isometries with “fixed boundary values”.

Given this context and the fundamental connection between (
1.1
) and smooth isometric immersions, the goal of this paper is to extend the correspondence between isometric immersions and solutions to the Darboux equation to the low-regularity regime
C^{1,\theta}
. In this setting, the Riemannian Hessian
\nabla^{2}_{g}u
is not classically defined, so we must introduce a suitable notion of weak solutions to (
1.1
). It turns out, see Proposition
3.1
below, that this is possible when
\theta>1/2
.

Definition 1.1

(Weak solutions to the Darboux equation)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a simply connected, bounded domain, and let
g\in C^{2}(\bar{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{\mathrm{sym}})
be a Riemannian metric with Gaussian curvature
K_{g}
. A function
u\in C^{1,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})
with
\theta>1/2
is a weak solution of the Darboux equation (
1.1
) if
|du|_{g}<1
and

\frac{\det(\nabla^{2}_{g}u)}{(1-|du|_{g}^{2})^{3/2}}dV_{g}=\frac{K_{g}}{(1-|du|_{g}^{2})^{1/2}}dV_{g}

(1.2)

as distributions on
\Omega
.

As we show in Proposition
3.1
, this definition is well-posed. The main result of our paper is the following equivalence between
C^{1,\theta}
isometric immersions and
C^{1,\theta}
weak solutions to the Darboux equation.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\theta>1/2
be a Hölder exponent and let
g\in C^{2}(\bar{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{\mathrm{sym}})
be a metric on a simply connected and bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then the following two statements hold.

(1)

If
\mathbf{r}\in C^{1,\theta}(\bar{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{3})
is an isometric immersion of
({\Omega},g)
into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
u=\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{e}
satisfies
|du|_{g}<1
for some unit vector
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, then
u\in C^{1,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})
is a weak solution to the Darboux equation (
1.1
).

(2)

If
u\in C^{1,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})
is a weak solution to the Darboux equation (
1.1
), then there exists
\phi\in C^{1,\theta}(\bar{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that the mapping
\mathbf{r}=(\phi,u):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an isometric immersion of
(\Omega,g)
into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

The central ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.1
is a generalization of the classical flatness criterion for Riemannian surfaces to the low-regularity regime. Classically, a two-dimensional Riemannian manifold
(\Omega,h)
with smooth metric
h
is flat (i.e., locally isometric to
(\mathbb{R}^{2},e)
where
e
is the Euclidean metric) if and only if its Gaussian curvature
K_{h}
vanishes identically. In Section
4
, we extend this criterion by introducing a weak notion of Gaussian curvature for Hölder continuous metrics
h\in C^{0,\theta}
with
\theta>1/2
, using conformal coordinates. We then prove that the flatness characterization remains valid in this setting.

With this extension in place, the rest of the proof follows naturally by considering the auxiliary metric
h=g-du^{2}
, built from the original metric
g
and the function
u
. In case (1), when
u=\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{e}
arises from a
C^{1,\theta}
isometric immersion
\mathbf{r}
, the flatness criterion yields
K_{h}=0
. An approximation argument together with the classical curvature formula for smooth perturbations
g-dv^{2}
then implies that
u
satisfies (
1.1
).
In case (2), where
u
is a weak solution to the Darboux equation, an approximation again yields
K_{h}=0
. The flatness criterion then provides the construction of the the desired
C^{1,\theta}
isometric immersion.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the notation used throughout the work. In Section
3
, we justify the definition of weak solutions by showing that the distribution in (
1.2
) is well-defined; this relies on a known result concerning distributional products, whose proof is provided in the appendix for completeness. Section
4.1
recalls the construction and key properties of conformal coordinates, which play a central role in our analysis. Building on this, Section
4
develops a weak notion of Gaussian curvature and establishes the extension of the flatness criterion to the low-regularity setting. Finally, Section
5
contains the proof of the main theorem.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Riemannian Hessian

Let
g
be a Riemannian metric on a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and let
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{2}
function. The squared length of the differential
du
with respect to
g
is given in local coordinates by

|du|_{g}^{2}=g^{ij}\,\partial_{i}u\,\partial_{j}u\,,

where
(g^{ij})=g^{-1}
denotes the inverse matrix of
(g_{ij})
, and we use the Einstein summation convention.

The Riemannian Hessian of
u
is defined as the
(1,1)
-tensor

\nabla_{g}^{2}u:T\Omega\;\to\;T\Omega,\qquad X\;\mapsto\;\nabla_{X}\nabla_{g}u,

where
\nabla_{g}u
is the Riemannian gradient of
u
. In coordinates, one has

\det\nabla_{g}^{2}u=\frac{\det\left(\partial^{2}_{ij}u-\Gamma_{ij}^{k}\partial_{k}u\right)}{\det(g)}\,.

(2.1)

with
\Gamma^{k}_{ij}
denoting the Christoffel symbols of
g
.

Note that the coefficients

\partial^{2}_{ij}u-\Gamma_{ij}^{k}\partial_{k}u=\nabla^{2}u(\partial_{i},\partial_{j})

are precisely those of the covariant Hessian
\nabla^{2}u=\nabla du
, a symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor. While some authors (see e.g.
[
18
]
) formulate the Darboux equation (
1.1
) in terms of
\nabla du
rather than
\nabla_{g}^{2}u
, we choose the Riemannian Hessian due to the fact that the determinant of a
(1,1)
-tensor is invariantly defined.

2.2.
A formula for Gaussian curvature

We also introduce a lemma in
[
18
]
for calculating the Gaussian curvature of perturbed metrics.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
18
]
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},g)
be a smooth 2-dimensional Riemannian manifold and
u
a smooth function on
\mathcal{M}
with
|du|_{g}<1
. Then
h=g-du^{2}
is a smooth Riemannian metric on
\mathcal{M}
and the Gaussian curvature of
h
is given by

K_{h}=\frac{1}{1-|du|_{g}^{2}}\left\{K_{g}-\frac{\det(\nabla du)}{(1-|du|_{g}^{2})\det g}\right\}\,.

The proof can be found in
[
18
, Lemma 2.1.2]
.

2.3.
Hölder spaces

For an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
we denote by
C^{l}(\bar{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{N})
the set of maps
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
whose derivatives
D^{\gamma}f
are uniformly continuous on bounded subsets of
\Omega
for all
|\gamma|\leq l
. The respective supremum norms are defined by

\|f\|_{0}=\sup_{x\in\Omega}|f(x)|\,,[f]_{j}=\max_{|\gamma|=j}\|D^{\gamma}f(x)\|_{0},~~\|f\|_{l}=\sum_{j=0}^{l}[f]_{j},

For any
0<\theta\leq 1
, the Hölder space
C^{l,\theta}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{N})
is the Banach space of maps
f\in C^{l}(\bar{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{N})
with finite the Hölder norm
\|f\|_{C^{l,\theta}}
. Here,

\|f\|_{C^{l,\theta}}=\|f\|_{l}+[f]_{l,\theta}\,,\text{ where }[f]_{l,\theta}=\sup_{x\neq y,x,y\in\Omega}\frac{|D^{l}f(x)-D^{l}f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\theta}}\,.

We denote by
C^{l,\theta}_{loc}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
the space of maps
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
f\in C^{l,\theta}(\bar{V},\mathbb{R}^{N})
for all open
V\subset\subset\Omega
.
Moreover,
C_{c}^{l,\theta}(\Omega)
is the space of functions in
C^{l,\theta}_{loc}(\Omega)
with compact support in
\Omega
and
\big(C_{c}^{l,\theta}(\Omega)\big)^{*}
denotes its dual space.

We say that a sequence
\{f_{\epsilon}\}
converges in
C^{l,\theta}_{loc}
to
f\in C^{l,\theta}_{loc}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
, if
f_{\epsilon}\to f
in
C^{l,\theta}(\bar{V},\mathbb{R}^{N})
for all open
V\subset\subset\Omega
.

2.4.
Mollification

We will use the regularization of maps by mollification with a standard, radially symmetric smoothing kernel
\Phi_{\epsilon}
. For
f\in C^{l,\theta}_{loc}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
we will abbreviate
f_{\epsilon}=f\ast\Phi_{\epsilon}
. The maps
f_{\epsilon}
are only defined on the set
\Omega_{\epsilon}=\{x\in\Omega:\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)>\epsilon\}
. However, for any open
V\subset\subset\Omega
it holds
V\subset\Omega_{\epsilon}
for small enough
\epsilon
, and
f_{\epsilon}\to f
in
C^{l,\theta^{\prime}}(\bar{V},\mathbb{R}^{N})
for any
\theta^{\prime}<\theta
(see for example Lemma 1 in
[
11
]
for a proof of this fact). Therefore,
f_{\epsilon}\to f
in
C^{l,\theta^{\prime}}_{loc}
.

3.
Weak solutions to the Darboux equation

In this section we show that the notion of a weak solution according to Definition
1.1
is well-posed. More precisely, we have

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded domain and let
g\in C^{2}(\bar{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{\mathrm{sym}})
be a metric on
\Omega
. If
\theta>1/2
and
u\in C^{1,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})
is a map with
|du|_{g}<1
on
\bar{\Omega}
, then

\frac{\det(\nabla^{2}_{g}u)}{(1-|du|_{g}^{2})^{3/2}}dV_{g}

is well-defined as an element in
\left(C^{1,\theta}_{c}(\Omega)\right)^{*}
with the following estimate:

\left\lVert\frac{\det(\nabla_{g}^{2}u)}{(1-|du|^{2}_{g})^{3/2}}\right\rVert_{(C_{c}^{1,\theta}(\Omega))^{*}}\leq C(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}})\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}}\,.

(3.1)

Moreover, if
\{u_{\epsilon}\}\subset C^{2}_{loc}(\Omega)
are such that
u_{\epsilon}\to u
in
C^{1,\theta}_{loc}(\Omega)
, then

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}\varphi\frac{\det(\nabla^{2}_{g}u_{\epsilon})}{(1-|du_{\epsilon}|_{g}^{2})^{3/2}}dV_{g}=\frac{\det(\nabla^{2}_{g}u)}{(1-|du|_{g}^{2})^{3/2}}dV_{g}[\varphi]

(3.2)

for any
\varphi\in C^{1,\theta}_{c}(\Omega)
.

Here and hereafter,
C
denotes a geometric constant which may depend on the metric
g
and the domain
\Omega
.
To prove the above proposition we need the following result about distributional products of Hölder continuous functions. Several versions of this result are known in the literature (see for example Proposition 3.6 in
[
15
]
). For completeness we provide a short proof in the appendix.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set and
\theta>1/2
. Then the multilinear operator

C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})\times C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})\times C^{1}_{c}(\Omega)\ni(f,w,\varphi)\mapsto\int_{\Omega}\partial_{i}fw\varphi\,dx

has a continuous extension to
C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})\times C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})\times C_{c}^{0,\theta}(\Omega)
, which we denote by
\int\partial_{i}fw\varphi
. Consequently, for
f,w\in C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})
the distribution
\partial_{i}fw\in\left(C^{0,\theta}_{c}(\Omega)\right)^{*}
can be defined by setting

\partial_{i}fw[\varphi]=\int\partial_{i}fw\varphi\,.

It satisfies the estimate

\|\partial_{i}fw\|_{(C^{0,\theta}_{c})^{*}}\leq C[f]_{0,\theta}[w]_{0,\theta}\,.

Moreover, if
\{f_{n}\},\{w_{n}\}
converge in
C^{0,\theta}_{loc}(\Omega)
to
f
and
w
respectively, then

\partial_{i}f_{n}w_{n}[\varphi]\to\partial_{i}fw[\varphi]

for any
\varphi\in C^{0,\theta}_{c}(\Omega)
.

To prove Proposition
3.1
, we need the following lemma for defining weak hessian determinant via differential forms.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded domain and
\theta>\frac{1}{2}
.
If
b(x,y)dy^{1}\wedge dy^{2}
is a
2
-form on
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

\left\lVert b(\cdot,y)\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})}+\left\lVert D_{x}D_{y}b(\cdot,y)\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})}\leq M

(3.3)

for some constant
M\geq 1
, then

b(x,\nabla u)d\partial_{1}u\wedge d\partial_{2}u=(x,\nabla u)^{\sharp}\left(b(x,y)dy^{1}\wedge dy^{2}\right)\in\left(C^{1,\theta}_{c}(\Omega)\right)^{*}

extends continuously from
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
to
u\in C^{1,\theta}(\overline{\Omega})
with estimate

\left\lVert b(x,\nabla u)d\partial_{1}u\wedge d\partial_{2}u\right\rVert_{(C_{c}^{1,\theta}(\Omega))^{*}}\leq CM(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}})\,.

Proof.

Fix a vectorfield
B
on
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

\textrm{div}_{y}(B(x,y))=b(x,y).

In particular it can be chosen such that it satisfies similar estimates to
(
3.3
), i.e.,

\sum_{i,j\in\{0,1\}}\left\lVert D_{x}^{i}D_{y}^{j}B(\cdot,y)\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})}\leq CM.

(3.4)

Consider the 1-form

\beta=B\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=0.3pt,width=5.0pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.3pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits(dy^{1}\wedge dy^{2})=B^{i}\star_{y}dy^{i}

and note that

\displaystyle d\beta

\displaystyle=\textrm{div}_{y}(B)\,(dy^{1}\wedge dy^{2})+\partial_{x_{j}}B^{i}(dx^{j}\wedge\star_{y}dy^{i})

\displaystyle=:b(x,y)(dy^{1}\wedge dy^{2})+b_{ij}(x,y)(dx^{i}\wedge dy^{j})

where we defined
b_{ij}\in C^{0,\theta}
by the previous equality where we sum over repeated indices. Hence,

b(x,y)(dy^{1}\wedge dy^{2})=d\beta-b_{ij}(x,y)(dx^{i}\wedge dy^{j})\,.

Now we observe that given
\nabla u\in C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})
by (
3.4
) we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert B^{i}(x,\nabla u)\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\lVert B^{i}\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}}+\left\lVert D_{y}B^{i}\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}}\right)(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}})

\displaystyle\leq CM(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}})\,.

Similarly we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert b_{ij}(x,\nabla u)\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left\lVert\partial_{x_{j}}B\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}}+\left\lVert D_{y}\partial_{x_{j}}B\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}}\right)(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}})

\displaystyle\leq CM(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}})\,.

Hence in both cases we can appeal to Proposition
3.2
to observe that

\displaystyle\left\lVert B^{i}(x,\nabla u)d\partial_{j}u\right\rVert_{(C^{0,\theta}_{c}(\Omega))^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq CM(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}})\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}}\,,

\displaystyle\left\lVert b_{ij}(x,\nabla u)d\partial_{j}u\right\rVert_{(C^{0,\theta}_{c}(\Omega))^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq CM(1+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}})\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{C^{0,\theta}}\,.

The continuous dependence on
u
follows from the continuous dependence observed in Proposition
3.2
and the observation that the maps

\nabla u\mapsto b_{ij}(\cdot,\nabla u),\quad\nabla u\mapsto B^{i}(\cdot,\nabla u)

are continuous maps from
C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})
into
C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

As recalled in Section
2.1

\det(\nabla^{2}_{g}u)=\frac{\det\left(\partial_{ij}^{2}u-\Gamma^{k}_{ij}\partial_{k}u\right)}{\det(g)}

and thus

\displaystyle\frac{\det(\nabla^{2}_{g}u)}{(1-|du|_{g}^{2})^{3/2}}=\frac{\det(D^{2}u)}{\det(g)(1-|du|_{g}^{2})^{3/2}}-\frac{\operatorname{cof}(\Gamma^{k}\partial_{k}u)\colon D^{2}u}{\det(g)(1-|du|_{g}^{2})^{3/2}}+\frac{\det(\Gamma^{k}\partial_{k}u)}{\det(g)(1-|du|_{g}^{2})^{3/2}}\,.

Note that by assumption there is
\epsilon>0
such that
g^{ij}\partial_{i}u\partial_{j}u<(1-2\epsilon)^{2}
on
\overline{\Omega}
. Now fix any
\tilde{b}(t)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
such that

\tilde{b}(t)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{3/2}}\text{ on }|t|<1-\epsilon

and define
b,A,E:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle b(x,y)

\displaystyle=\det(g)^{-1}\tilde{b}(g^{ij}(x)y_{i}y_{j})\,,

\displaystyle A(x,y)

\displaystyle=b(x,y)\operatorname{cof}(\Gamma^{k}(x)y_{k})\,,

\displaystyle E(x,y)

\displaystyle=b(x,y)\det(\Gamma^{k}(x)y_{k})\,.

With these definitions it follows that for every
u\in C^{2}(\bar{\Omega})
satisfying
|du|_{g}<1-\epsilon
on
\Omega
it holds

\frac{\det(\nabla^{2}_{g}u)}{(1-|du|_{g}^{2})^{3/2}}=b(x,\nabla u)d\partial_{1}u\wedge d\partial_{2}u+A(x,\nabla u)\colon D^{2}u+E(x,\nabla u)\,.

The first term extends locally continuously to
u\in C^{1,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})
by Lemma
3.3
. For the second and third term we observe that
\nabla u\mapsto A(\cdot,\nabla u),\,\,\nabla u\mapsto E(\cdot,\nabla u)
are continuously mapping
C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})
into
C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})
. Hence we can apply Proposition
3.2
to the second term, while the third is classically defined. Finally the conclusion follows.
∎

4.
Distributional Gaussian curvature and flatness

In this section we define a distributional Gaussian curvature for Hölder continuous metrics
h\in C^{0,\theta}
(see also
[
32
]
for a distributional Gaussian curvature for
h\in C^{1}
). We then show that the classical characterization of flatness extends to this low-regularity regime: a two-dimensional Riemannian manifold
(\Omega,h)
with smooth metric is flat (i.e., locally isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
) if and only if its Gaussian curvature
K_{h}
vanishes identically.

We will exploit the two-dimensional setting by using isothermal (or conformal) coordinates. We review their definition and key properties in the next subsection.

4.1.
Conformal charts

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be an open set and
h\in C^{0,\theta}_{loc}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{\mathrm{sym}})
a metric. We call
(\psi,V)
a
conformal chart
for
h
if
V\subset\Omega
is open,
\psi:V\to\psi(V)\subset\mathbb{C}
is an orientation preserving diffeomorphism, and

h=\psi^{\sharp}(e^{2\sigma}|dz|^{2})\text{ on }V.

Here,
\sigma
is determined by
\psi
and
h
through
\sigma=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{tr}h}{|D\psi|^{2}}\circ\psi^{-1}\right)
. Nevertheless, for notational reasons we will denote the chart by
(\psi,\sigma,V)
.

The map
\psi
yields coordinates in which the metric
h
is diagonal. They are also known as isothermal coordinates.

Remark 4.1
.

(1)

The existence of conformal charts for a given metric is classical (see
[
1
]
and references therein), and can be reduced to solving the Beltrami equation
\psi_{\bar{z}}=\mu\psi_{z}
on
V
. Conversely, every conformal chart arises as a solution to the Beltrami equation. The coefficient
\mu
, which satisfies
|\mu|<1
on
\Omega
, is determined by the metric
h
. In particular, if
h\in C^{l,\theta}_{loc}
, then
\mu\in C^{l,\theta}_{loc}
as well.

(2)

If
h\in C^{l,\theta}_{loc}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{\mathrm{sym}})
is a metric with
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\theta\in(0,1]
, then any chart
(\psi,\sigma,V)
satisfies
\psi\in C^{l+1,\theta}_{loc}(V),\sigma\in C^{l,\theta}_{loc}(\psi(V))
(cf. Theorem 15.6.2 in
[
2
]
).

We will need the following facts.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
is an open, bounded set and
h\in C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{\mathrm{sym}})
. Then there exists a “global” conformal chart
(\psi,\sigma,\Omega)
, satisfying in addition
\psi\in C^{1,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})
. Moreover, if
\{h_{\epsilon}\}\subset C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{\mathrm{sym}})
is a sequence of metrics such that
h_{\epsilon}\to h
in
C^{0,\theta}
there are “global” conformal charts
(\psi_{\epsilon},\sigma_{\epsilon},\Omega)
for
h_{\epsilon}
satisfying

\|\psi_{\epsilon}-\psi\|_{C^{1,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})}+\|\sigma_{\epsilon}-\sigma\|_{C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega})}\to 0\,.

(4.1)

For the reader’s convenience we provide a proof of the preceding lemma in the appendix.

4.2.
Distributional Gaussian curvature

We now define a notion of Gaussian curvature for Hölder continuous metrics. Recall that for a smooth metric
h
, the Gaussian curvature
K_{h}
in conformal coordinates
(\psi,\sigma,V)
is given by

K_{h}\circ\psi^{-1}=-2e^{-2\sigma}\Delta\sigma.

Since moreover
\det h\circ\psi^{-1}=e^{4\sigma}
it follows that

\int_{V}\varphi K_{h}dV_{h}=\int_{\psi(V)}(\varphi\circ\psi^{-1})(K_{h}\circ\psi^{-1})(\sqrt{\det h}\circ\psi^{-1})dz=-2\int_{\psi(V)}(\varphi\circ\psi^{-1})\Delta\sigma dz\,.

This motivates the following

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be open,
\theta>1/2
, and let
h\in C^{0,\theta}_{loc}({\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{\mathrm{sym}})
be a metric. We define the distributional Gaussian curvature by

K_{h}dV_{h}[\varphi]=2\int\nabla(\varphi\circ\psi^{-1})\cdot\nabla\sigma

(4.2)

for
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
, where
(\psi,\sigma,V)
is any conformal chart such that
\mathrm{spt}(\varphi)\subset V
.

Proposition 4.2
.

The distributional Gaussian curvature is well-defined by (
4.2
) and extends to
K_{h}dV_{h}\in\left(C^{1,\theta}_{c}(\Omega)\right)^{*}
. Moreover, if
h_{\epsilon}\to h
in
C^{0,\theta}_{loc}(\Omega)
then
K_{h_{\epsilon}}dV_{h_{\epsilon}}\to K_{h}dV_{h}
as distributions.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
and
(\psi,\sigma,V)
a conformal chart such that
\mathrm{spt}(\varphi)\subset V
. It follows from Remark
4.1
(2) that
\psi\in C^{1,\theta}_{loc}(V)
and
\sigma\in C^{0,\theta}_{loc}(\psi(V))
. As
\nabla(\varphi\circ\psi^{-1})\in C^{0,\theta}_{c}(V)
, the right-hand side of (
4.2
) is well-defined as a distributional product thanks to Proposition
3.2
.

Now assume that
(\psi_{1},\sigma_{1},V_{1})
,
(\psi_{2},\sigma_{2},V_{2})
are two conformal charts with
\mathrm{spt}(\varphi)\subset V_{i}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{spt}(\varphi)\subset V_{1}\cap V_{2}=:\Omega_{0}
. We claim that

\int\nabla(\varphi\circ\psi_{1}^{-1})\cdot\nabla\sigma_{1}=\int\nabla(\varphi\circ\psi_{2}^{-1})\cdot\nabla\sigma_{2}\,.

For this we consider mapping

f:=\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{2}^{-1}:~~{\psi_{2}(\Omega_{0})}\to{\psi_{1}(\Omega_{0})}.

Observe that by definition

\psi_{1}^{\sharp}(e^{2\sigma_{1}}|dz|^{2})=h=\psi_{2}^{\sharp}(e^{2\sigma_{2}}|dz|^{2})

on
\Omega_{0}
, so that

\displaystyle e^{2\sigma_{2}}|dz|^{2}=(\psi_{2}^{-1})^{\sharp}h=e^{2(\sigma_{1}\circ f)}f^{\sharp}|dz|^{2}\,.

Consequently,
f
is conformal and hence, since it is also orientation preserving, holomorphic. In particular,
f^{\sharp}|dz|^{2}=|f^{\prime}|^{2}|dz|^{2}
,
which implies

\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{1}\circ f+\ln|f^{\prime}|

(4.3)

on
\psi_{2}(\Omega_{0})
. Note that
\ln|f^{\prime}|
is harmonic, so that

\int_{\psi_{2}(\Omega_{0})}\nabla\ln|f^{\prime}|\cdot\nabla\Psi dz=0,\text{ for any }\Psi\in C_{c}^{1}(\psi_{2}(\Omega_{0})).

(4.4)

Now consider the mollification
(\sigma_{2})_{\epsilon}
of
\sigma_{2}
with a standard, radially symmetric mollifier. By (
4.3
) it holds

(\sigma_{2})_{\epsilon}=(\sigma_{1}\circ f)_{\epsilon}+(\ln|f^{\prime}|)_{\epsilon}=(\sigma_{1}\circ f)_{\epsilon}+\ln|f^{\prime}|\,,

since
\ln|f^{\prime}|
is harmonic.
Since
(\sigma_{2})_{\epsilon}\to\sigma_{2}
in
C^{0,\theta^{\prime}}_{loc}(\psi_{2}(V_{2}))
for any
1/2<\theta^{\prime}<\theta
and
\psi_{2}^{-1}=\psi_{1}^{-1}\circ f
we have by continuity of the distributional product and (
4.4
)

\displaystyle\int\nabla(\varphi\circ\psi_{2}^{-1})\cdot\nabla\sigma_{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\psi_{2}(\Omega_{0})}\nabla(\varphi\circ\psi_{2}^{-1})\cdot\nabla(\sigma_{2})_{\epsilon}dz

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\psi_{2}(\Omega_{0})}\nabla(\varphi\circ\psi_{2}^{-1})\cdot\nabla\left((\sigma_{1}\circ f)_{\epsilon}+\ln|f^{\prime}|\right)dz

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\psi_{2}(\Omega_{0})}\nabla(\varphi\circ\psi_{1}^{-1}\circ f)\cdot\nabla\left((\sigma_{1})_{\epsilon}\circ f\right)dz

\displaystyle\quad+\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\psi_{2}(\Omega_{0})}\nabla(\varphi\circ\psi_{1}^{-1}\circ f)\cdot\nabla\left((\sigma_{1}\circ f)_{\epsilon}-(\sigma_{1})_{\epsilon}\circ f\right)dz

\displaystyle=:I+I\!\!I\,.

By the conformal invariance of the Dirichlet integral we get

I=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\psi_{1}(\Omega_{0})}\nabla(\varphi\circ\psi_{1}^{-1})\cdot\nabla(\sigma_{1})_{\epsilon}dz=\int\nabla(\varphi\circ\psi_{1}^{-1})\cdot\nabla\sigma_{1}\,.

On the other hand, since

(\sigma_{1}\circ f)_{\epsilon}-(\sigma_{1})_{\epsilon}\circ f=((\sigma_{1}\circ f)_{\epsilon}-\sigma_{1}\circ f)+(\sigma_{1}\circ f-(\sigma_{1})_{\epsilon}\circ f)\to 0

in
C^{0,\theta^{\prime}}
for any
1/2<\theta^{\prime}<\theta
we get by the continuity of the distributional product that
I\!\!I=0
, proving the claim.

For the second assertion, let
h_{\epsilon}
be a sequence of metrics such that
h_{\epsilon}\to h
in
C^{0,\theta}_{loc}(\Omega)
and let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
. Given an open
V\subset\subset\Omega
such that
\mathrm{spt}\,\varphi\subset V
we can find global conformal charts
(\psi,\sigma,V)
,
(\psi_{\epsilon},\sigma_{\epsilon},V)
for
h
and
h_{\epsilon}
such that (
4.1
) holds with
\Omega
replaced by
V
. Consequently, from the continuity of the distributional product in Proposition
3.2
and the convergence (
4.1
) we find

K_{h}dV_{h}[\varphi]=2\int\nabla(\varphi\circ\psi^{-1})\cdot\nabla\sigma dz=2\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int\nabla(\varphi\circ\psi_{\epsilon}^{-1})\cdot\nabla\sigma_{\epsilon}dz=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}K_{h_{\epsilon}}dV_{h_{\epsilon}}[\varphi]\,.\qed

4.3.
Flatness

We now show the generalization to metrics with low regularity of a classical theorem concerning the flatness of Riemannian manifolds.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\theta>1/2
and
h\in C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{\mathrm{sym}})
be a metric on a simply connected and bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then there exists an isometric immersion
\phi\in C^{1,\theta}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2})
of
(\Omega,h)
into
\mathbb{R}^{2}
if and only if
K_{h}dV_{h}[\varphi]=0
for any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega).

Proof.

Let
\phi\in C^{1,\theta}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2})
be an isometric immersion from
(\Omega,h)
into
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. As an immersion
\phi
is a local diffeomorphism, so there exists an open cover
\{V_{i}\}
of
\Omega
such that
\phi|_{V_{i}}
is a diffeomorphism onto its image for every
i
. Since

h=\phi^{\sharp}|dz|^{2}
,
(\phi|_{V_{i}},V_{i})
is a conformal chart (upto composing it with a reflection) with coefficient
\sigma_{i}=0
for every
i
. For a given
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
we can find a finite subcover
V_{1},\ldots,V_{N}
of
\mathrm{spt}\,\varphi
, and choose a partition of unity
\{\chi_{i}\}
subordinate to the cover. Since the right-hand side in (
4.2
) is independent of the choice of conformal chart it follows

K_{h}dV_{h}[\varphi]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{h}dV_{h}[\chi_{i}\varphi]=2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int\nabla(\chi_{i}\varphi\circ\phi|_{V_{i}}^{-1})\cdot\nabla\sigma_{i}=0\,.

Now assume conversely that
K_{h}dV_{h}=0
as a distribution and let
(\psi,\sigma,\Omega)
be a global conformal chart for
h
. We claim that
\sigma
is harmonic on
\psi(\Omega)
. Indeed, fix
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\psi(\Omega))
, and let
\sigma_{\epsilon}
be smooth approximations by mollification in
C^{0,\theta}_{loc}(\psi(\Omega))
of
\sigma
. Since
\tilde{\varphi}=\varphi\circ\psi\in C^{1,\theta}_{c}(\Omega)
is a valid testfunction in
K_{h}dV_{h}
it follows by the continuity of the distributional product

\int_{\psi(\Omega)}\sigma\Delta\varphi dz=-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\psi(\Omega)}\nabla\sigma_{\epsilon}\cdot\nabla\varphi dz=-\int_{\psi(\Omega)}\nabla\sigma\cdot\nabla(\tilde{\varphi}\circ\psi^{-1})=-\frac{1}{2}K_{h}dV_{h}[\tilde{\varphi}]=0\,.

Hence, by Weyl’s lemma for the Laplace equation it follows that
\sigma
is smooth and harmonic.
Since
\Omega
and so
\psi(\Omega)
are simply connected,
\sigma
is the real part of some holomorphic function
f
, i.e.,
\sigma=\Re f
. Appealing once more to the simply connectedness of
\psi(\Omega)
there is a holomorphic function
F\colon\psi(\Omega)\to\mathbb{C}
which is a primitive of
e^{f}
, i.e.,
F^{\prime}=e^{f}
. It satisfies

|DF|^{2}=e^{2\Re(f)}=e^{2\sigma}.

Consequently, the mapping

\phi:=F\circ\psi:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}

is an isometric immersion from
(\Omega,h)
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
∎

Appendix A
Distributional products

The proof of Proposition
3.2
follows as a corollary from the following two lemmas.

Lemma A.1
.

The trilinear operator

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{3}\ni(f,g,h)\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}Df(x)g(x)h(x)\,dx

extends continuously to a trilinear operator on
B^{s}_{p_{1},q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times B^{1-s}_{p_{2},q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times B^{1-s}_{p_{3},q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denoted by
\int Dfgh
for any set of parameters satisfying
\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}+\frac{1}{p_{3}}=1
,
\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}=1
and
0<s<1
.

Here,
B^{s}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denotes the (inhomogenous) Besov-space. Even though the result is probably well known and perhaps not optimal, we will present a short proof for the sake of completeness.

Proof.

Let us denote with
F(x,t)=\Phi_{t}*f(x),G(x,t)=\Phi_{t}*g(x),H(x,t)=\Phi_{t}*h(x)
be the smooth extensions of
f(x),g(x),h(x)
to
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
having the “good trace” property, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left\lVert t^{|\alpha|-s}\left\lVert\partial^{\alpha}F(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p}}\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\frac{dt}{t})}

\displaystyle\leq C[f]_{B^{s}_{p,q}}\text{ for }\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n+1}\text{ with }|\alpha|>0\,,

\displaystyle\sup_{t}\left\lVert F(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C[f]_{B^{s}_{p,q}}\,.

The first estimate can for instance be found in
[
30
, Theorem 1.4]
and the second is a simple consequence of
F
being a mollification of
f
.
Having these extensions at hand the proof is straight forward. Recall that
\partial_{j}fgh\,dx=ghd(f\star_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}dx^{j})
and properties of mollifier so that

\displaystyle\lvert\int\partial_{j}fgh\,dx\rvert=\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{0\}}GHd(F\star_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}dx^{j})\rvert=\lvert\int_{0}^{\infty}\int d(F\star_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}dx^{j})\wedge d(GH)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}t\left\lVert\partial_{j}F(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p_{1}}}\left(\left\lVert\partial_{t}G(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p_{2}}}\left\lVert H(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p_{3}}}+\left\lVert G(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p_{2}}}\left\lVert\partial_{t}H(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p_{3}}}\right)\,\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}t\left\lVert\partial_{t}F(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p_{1}}}\left(\left\lVert\partial_{j}G(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p_{2}}}\left\lVert H(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p_{3}}}+\left\lVert G(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p_{2}}}\left\lVert\partial_{j}H(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p_{3}}}\right)\,\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\left(\sup_{t}\left\lVert H(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p_{3}}}\right)\left\lVert t^{1-s}\left\lVert DF(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p_{1}}}\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\frac{dt}{t})}\left\lVert t^{1-(1-s)}\left\lVert DG(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p_{2}}}\right\rVert_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\frac{dt}{t})}

\displaystyle\quad+2\left(\sup_{t}\left\lVert G(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p_{2}}}\right)\left\lVert t^{1-s}\left\lVert DF(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p_{1}}}\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\frac{dt}{t})}\left\lVert t^{1-(1-s)}\left\lVert DH(\cdot,t)\right\rVert_{L^{p_{3}}}\right\rVert_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\frac{dt}{t})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C[f]_{B^{s}_{p_{1},q}}[g]_{B^{1-s}_{p_{2},q^{\prime}}}[h]_{B^{1-s}_{p_{3},q^{\prime}}}\,.

Together with the tri-linearity of the product on the left the claim follows.
∎

D{\tilde{\zeta}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{C})
and

\tilde{\zeta}_{\bar{z}}=\mu\tilde{\zeta}_{z}+\mu.

Hence by the Schauder estimates in Theorem 15.0.6 in
[
2
]
we get

[D\zeta]_{C^{0,\theta}(\mathbb{C})}=[D{\tilde{\zeta}}]_{C^{0,\theta}(\mathbb{C})}\leq C\left([\mu]_{\theta}+([\mu]_{\theta})^{1+1/\theta}\|\mu\|_{\infty}\right)\,.

Combining with the
L^{\infty}
bound and interpolation, in our case we see that
\psi_{\epsilon},\psi
and so also
f^{\epsilon}
are uniformly (in
\epsilon
) bounded in
C^{1,\theta}(K_{0})
for any compact
K_{0}\subset\mathbb{C}
.

Moreover, from the fact that
\psi_{\epsilon}
and
\psi
are principal solutions it follows that
Df^{\epsilon}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{C})
. Consequently, the Schauder estimates in Theorem 15.0.6 in
[
2
]
imply

\displaystyle[Df^{\epsilon}]_{C^{0,\theta}(\mathbb{C})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left([(\mu-\mu_{\epsilon})f^{\epsilon}_{z}]_{C^{0,\theta}(\mathbb{C})}+([\mu]_{C^{0,\theta}(\mathbb{C})})^{1+1/\theta}\|(\mu-\mu_{\epsilon})f^{\epsilon}_{z}\|_{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\|\mu-\mu_{\epsilon}\|_{C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega}^{\prime})}\|f^{\epsilon}\|_{C^{1,\theta}(\bar{\Omega}^{\prime})}\leq C\|\mu-\mu_{\epsilon}\|_{C^{0,\theta}(\bar{\Omega}^{\prime})}\to 0.

Since moreover
f^{\epsilon}\to 0
locally uniformly (cf. Lemma 5.3.5 in
[
2
]
) it follows by interpolation that
f^{\epsilon}\to 0
in
C^{1,\theta}_{loc}(\mathbb{C})
. Appealing to the relation between
\psi
and
\sigma
in Definition
4.1
, we also have
\sigma_{\epsilon}\to\sigma
in
C^{0,\theta}_{loc}(\mathbb{C})
as
\epsilon\to 0
, proving the claim.
