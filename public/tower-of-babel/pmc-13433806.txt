A molecular glue induces aberrant STING oligomerization and inhibits autoinflammatory diseases
Authors: Zi-Wen Feng, Ting Zeng, Qing Shu, Xiao-Zhong Li, Hai-Guo Yu, Wei Dai, Xue-Chun Liu, Xin-Jian Tian, Feng Xu, Hui Li, Ye-Ling Zhou, Shi-Duo Zhang, Lin-Lin Li, Nan-Nan Chen, Xiao-Yan Liu, Zi-Wen Liu, Jie Yin, Hong-Tao Shen, Chun-Chen Che, Mei-Rong Chen, Dan-Dan Wang, Ling-Yun Sun, Yi-Bei Xiao, Qi-Dong You, Xiao-Li Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dysregulated activation of the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway underlies various inflammatory and autoimmune pathologies. Since STING oligomerization is fundamental to its biological function, targeted modulation of this polymerization process presents a promising therapeutic approach. However, achieving precise control over STING polymerization has remained a significant challenge. In this study, we report that benzofuran derivatives serve as molecular glues to potently inhibit STING activity by inducing its aberrant oligomerization. Structural analyses demonstrated that these compounds function as glues, promoting an unconventional "head-to-head" STING dimerization configuration that differs markedly from the linear polymers formed during canonical pathway activation. These compounds exhibit potent inhibitory activity against different STING isoforms activated by 2',3'-cGAMP, dsDNA, gain-of-function SAVI-associated STING mutant, and herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) infection. DDO-6513 downregulates the expression of type I interferons (IFN-I), IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) and pro-inflammatory cytokines in clinical samples isolated from patients with STING-associated autoimmune disorders, including Aicardi–Goutières syndrome (AGS), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and dermatomyositis (DM). In vivo, DDO-7432 displays desirable drug-like properties, and exerts remarkable anti-inflammatory efficacy in murine myositis disease models. Our findings demonstrate that modulating protein oligomerization via molecular glues can effectively disrupt signal transduction, providing novel insights into therapeutic strategies for oligomerization-dependent targets.

Introduction

Innate immunity constitutes the first line of host immune defense, which rapidly recognizes exogenous pathogenic microorganisms and endogenous dangerous signals without relying on pre-sensitization and antigen-specific memory responses. Persistent aberrant activation of innate immune sensing cascades drives uncontrolled excessive inflammation, which is tightly implicated in the onset and progression of inflammatory disorders, autoimmune diseases and multiple metabolic illnesses. As a core signaling cascade in innate immunity, the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-STING pathway serves as a pivotal nucleic acid sensing axis that initiates host defense against microbial invasion and maintains immune homeostasis, and contributes substantially to the pathogenesis of multiple inflammatory and autoimmune disorders.1 The cytosolic DNA sensor cGAS efficiently recognizes aberrantly accumulated double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) derived from damaged host cells, viral genomes and microbial pathogens in the cytoplasm, and synthesizes the secondary messenger 2′,3′-cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP).2–5 This endogenous cyclic dinucleotide (CDN) specifically binds to the V-shaped ligand-binding domain (LBD) of STING dimers, triggering a series of sequential conformational rearrangements that promote STING oligomerization in a side-by-side arrangement,6–8 leading to phosphorylation of TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) and interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3).9,10 These events activate multiple downstream signaling cascades including IFN-I-dependent immune responses,11 NF-κB-mediated inflammation signals,12–14 and STING-dependent autophagy.15–17 Persistent and abnormally sustained STING hyperactivation causes pathological inflammation and autoimmunity,1,18 as evidenced by STING-associated vasculopathy with onset in infancy (SAVI) resulting from gain-of-function STING mutations,19 as well as autoimmune disorders such as AGS associated with hyperactive cGAS-STING signaling,20–22 DM23 and SLE.24–26

Most current STING inhibitory strategies rely on covalent modification of key amino acid residues or competitive binding to the classic CDN pocket. A seminal study revealed a covalent small-molecule STING inhibitor H151 targeting the transmembrane residue Cys91, which inspired extensive efforts to develop next-generation covalent STING inhibitors.27–30 The STING dimer features a distinct V-shaped LBD, which is a region well-suited for the development of small-molecule inhibitors. These inhibitors bind to CDN pockets to block CDNs binding, thereby inhibiting the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway.31–35 Beyond these canonical inhibitory modes, carnosic acid directly targets the STING C-terminal tail and ginkgetin selectively binds to the STING carboxy-terminal domain to block TBK1 recruitment and downstream inflammatory signaling.36,37 Y-230 allosterically targets the STING transmembrane domain and inhibits its activity.38 In addition, the identification of the STING molecular glue AK59 through screening represents a novel approach.39 Although current research has predominantly explored covalent inhibitors and CDN-competitive compounds, developing mechanistically novel inhibitors with distinct binding modes remains essential for advancing STING-targeted therapeutics.

Protein oligomerization is a highly conserved and essential post-translational assembly mechanism of eukaryotic proteins, which widely participates in the initiation, amplification and termination of various cellular signal transduction processes. Reasonable artificial modulation of protein oligomerization status and conformational assembly mode can expand the druggable target and enable precise regulation of protein function.40,41 Molecular glues, as emerging powerful tool molecules, can effectively mediate and remodel protein-protein interaction to influence protein function. Beyond their well-established classical biological function in inducing targeted protein degradation, molecular glues can also promote the formation of heteromeric or homomeric protein complexes to achieve specific biological functional regulation.42 The STING protein predominantly exists as oligomers in both the resting and activated states, and its functional activity is critically dependent on a specific linear oligomeric conformation, which is universally recognized as the structural basis for triggering downstream signaling. In line with this mechanism, currently reported STING-targeted molecular glue-like compounds mainly function to facilitate STING linear oligomerization and pathway activation, serving as immune agonists for antitumor immunotherapy.43,44 In contrast, precisely intervening and reversing the pathological linear polymerization of STING protein, and guiding STING to form an inactive non-canonical oligomeric conformation, may represent an alternative strategy for STING inhibition and autoimmune disease intervention.

In this study, we characterized how DDO-6513 induces “head to head” self-association of STING through a molecular glue-like mechanism, thereby disrupting its normal linear assembly and inhibiting its activity. The cocrystal structures reveal that both DDO-6501 and DDO-6513 bind to the wing region of butterfly-shaped LBDs of STING, which are significantly distinct from the endogenous CDN binding pocket. Functional assays further demonstrated that DDO-6513 induces high-order STING oligomerization on the endoplasmic reticulum, without initiating downstream signal transduction or activating the cGAS-STING pathway. Consistent with this functional feature, DDO-6513 possesses potent target affinity and human STING selective antagonistic activity, which can effectively suppress STING-dependent signaling activation induced by multiple stimuli in diversified cellular phenotypic assays, and exert reliable inhibitory effects in a humanized STING mouse model. Moreover, the elevated type I IFN signature in the peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of patients with AGS (1 sample), DM (10 samples) and SLE (30 samples) was significantly decreased after incubation with DDO-6513. Among the optimized derivatives, DDO-7432 exhibited improved drug-like properties and superior oral bioavailability. In the C57BL/6JSTING-Hu myositis mouse model, DDO-7432 exerts significantly superior inhibitory efficacy via oral administration compared to both covalent inhibitors28 and CDN pocket inhibitors,32 highlighting the therapeutic potential for STING-driven autoinflammatory diseases.

Results

Identification of a hit compound that induces self-association of STING

To rapidly identify potential STING-binding molecules, we initially performed a chemical screen against our in-house chemical library using a fluorescence-polarization (FP) competition assay. The fluorescent probe was designed by incorporating 5-carboxyfluorescein into the STING agonist diABZI, which was used to monitor the interactions between the C-terminal domain (CTD, aa 139–379) of human STING and compounds. Our screening identified DDO-6501 (Fig. 1a, b) as a candidate. This compound exhibited a prominent, dose-dependent decrease in the FP assay (IC50 = 31.71 ± 0.60 μM) without inducing autofluorescence (Fig. 1c). Cellular studies further revealed that DDO-6501 inhibited STING-mediated IRF reporter activity in a dose-dependent manner, with an IC50 value of 22.52 ± 1.12 μM in THP-1-Dual cells (Fig. 1d). No significant cytotoxicity was detected at a concentration of 100 μM (Fig. 1e). DDO-6501 demonstrated moderate affinity for the CTD of human STING, with Kd values of 5.5 μM in an isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) assay (Fig. 1f) and 18.13 μM in biolayer interferometry (BLI) assay (Fig. 1g). Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) profiling (Fig. 1h) revealed a specific interaction between DDO-6501 and STING. To further elucidate the atomic-level binding mode between DDO-6501 and STING, we cocrystallized the STING CTD with DDO-6501 and solved its binary complex at a resolution of 2.3 Å (X-ray statistics in Supplementary Table 1). The binding model revealed that DDO-6501 is comfortably nested within a hydrophobic pocket formed by the α1 helix, β2 sheet and β2-β3 loop regions from two separate STINGs, distinct from the canonical hydrophobic interface seen with cGAMP7,11,45,46 (Fig. 1i). The benzofuran cores of DDO-6501 are sandwiched between the two H185 residues, mediating π–π interactions from α1 of two STING protomers (Fig. 1j). Additional residues involved in the interaction with the benzofuran moiety of DDO-6501 include Y182 and L189 from both STING protomers. The side chain of the benzofuran core is embraced by R178, T181, P226 and Q227 from one of the STING protomers.

Structure-based optimization of potent inhibitors that induce aberrant STING oligomerization

To identify the pharmacophore and enhance the potency of DDO-6501, a preliminary structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis was performed on the basis of the cocrystal complex of DDO-6501 (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 2). The results indicated that the presence of carboxyl and chlorine groups was essential for the bioactivity of the compounds. Modifications to the core scaffold remained ineffective. This failure confirms the critical role of the carboxyl- and chlorine-bearing benzofuran core in the formation of a sandwich-like structure with the dual H185 residues of STING. Notably, structural optimization targeting the solvent-exposed region proved more successful. On the basis of the hydrophobic characteristics of the interaction sites, we found that introducing hydrophobic substitutions on the phenyl ring significantly enhanced activity. This approach led to compound DDO-6513 (Fig. 2b), which contained a tertiary butyl group conferring improved potency. Tertiary butyl substitution strongly inhibited STING activity, with an IC50 value of 1.93 ± 0.23 μM, representing an 11-fold increase in activity compared with that of DDO-6501 (Fig. 2c). DDO-6513 also significantly improved the binding affinity of STING, with a Kd of 151 nM in the ITC assay, and exhibited significant variations in the effects of the STD compared with those of DDO-6501 (Fig. 2d, e). We also cocrystallized the STING CTD bound to DDO-6513 and solved its binary complex at 1.7 Å resolution (X-ray statistics in Supplementary Table 1). The benzofuran core is necessary for the formation of π–π interactions with two H185 residues (Fig. 2f). To validate the proposed binding mode of DDO-6513, site-directed mutagenesis studies were also performed (Fig. 2g). These assays focused on both the interacting amino acids (H185A, T181A, P226A, H185L and T181M) and noninteracting residues (Q228S and R95C). DDO-6513 showed markedly reduced activity against STING variants with binding-site mutations. However, it demonstrated resistance to the Q228S and R95C mutations (Fig. 2g). DDO-6513, as a molecular glue, induces abnormal STING oligomerization

On the basis of the cocrystal results and the STING dimer structure, we performed crystal lattice symmetry expansion. This revealed unique STING oligomerization in which DDO-6501 acts as a molecular glue, bridging two STING protomers and forming an unconventional binding mode (Fig. 3a). To verify whether DDO-6513 induces STING oligomerization, native gel electrophoresis was employed to assess the effects of DDO-6513 on purified human STING-CTD. DDO-6513 promoted the formation of high-order STING oligomers, in contrast to the inability of cGAMP to induce similar effects under identical experimental conditions (Fig. 3b). In this assay, cGAMP induced STING to form a “closed” dimer in vitro, which migrated to a position lower than that of the apo-STING dimer in the gel, with no detectable oligomer formation. In fact, full-length STING sometimes failed to form high-order oligomers when stimulated by cGAMP alone in vitro.47 Consistently, size-exclusion chromatography analysis revealed a concentration-dependent shift toward higher-molecular-weight aggregates upon DDO-6513 treatment (Fig. 3c), demonstrating oligomeric states distinct from those of cGAMP-induced STING assemblies. Size distribution analysis by intensity consistently demonstrated dose-dependent increases in aggregate particle size upon DDO-6513 treatment, which were significantly different from those of cGAMP-induced assemblies (Fig. 3d). To investigate STING oligomerization induced by DDO-6513 in cells, immunofluorescence microscopy was used to characterize the DDO-6513-induced STING oligomerization patterns and their subcellular localization. DDO-6513 induced the formation of STING puncta, independent of the Golgi apparatus, and inhibited the translocation of STING from the ER to the Golgi upon cGAMP stimulation in GFP-STING-reconstituted HeLa cells and WI-38 cells (Fig. 3f, g). To circumvent potential disruptions in protein complex interactions,48 clear-native PAGE (CN-PAGE) was performed, and endogenous apo-STING polymers were detected, with alterations in their oligomeric state upon treatment with either DDO-6513 or cGAMP (Fig. 3h). However, cGAMP-induced oligomerization was more readily observable in cellular contexts. While both DDO-6513 and cGAMP induce STING oligomerization, they generate structurally distinct polymer forms. Moreover, owing to the mild electrophoretic conditions of CN-PAGE and the transmembrane domain of intracellular full-length STING, the high-order oligomers induced by cGAMP and DDO-6513 appeared blurred, a discrepancy from the Native PAGE results of the truncated STING protein. Even so, a discernible upward shift in the STING protein band was still observable at high concentrations of DDO-6513. In addition, STING oligomers are resistant to dithiothreitol (DTT) treatment, indicating that their assembly does not rely on disulfide bonds (Fig. 3i). On the basis of these results, we postulate that the compounds inhibit STING by inducing aberrant oligomerization, which is distinct from the linear polymers induced by cGAMP (Fig. 3j). Furthermore, the aberrant STING oligomeric conformation induced by DDO-6513 closely resembles the autoinhibited state of STING, providing additional evidence that DDO-6513 acts as a molecular glue to promote this abnormal oligomerization pattern on membranes. We propose that the compound induces spatial rearrangement of STING protomers in the autoinhibited state, leading to the formation of more stable aberrant aggregates that effectively suppress normal STING activation.48

DDO-6513 specifically inhibits STING-dependent signal transduction

To determine whether DDO-6513-induced STING oligomerization disrupted STING-dependent signaling, we investigated its effects across various human cellular models expressing multiple STING isoforms. DDO-6513 significantly suppressed the expression of inflammatory markers (including IFNB1, CXCL10 and IL6 mRNA) in THP-1 cells expressing the STING HAQ variant, which were activated by diverse cGAS-STING activators such as cGAMP, double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), cyclic di-GMP (c-di-GMP) and herring testis DNA (HT-DNA) (Fig. 4a, b). This inhibitory activity was consistent across human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (hPBMCs) (Fig. 4c), WI-38 human fibroblasts with wild-type (WT) STING and human foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs) with WT STING (Fig. 4d). These experimental results demonstrate that DDO-6513 has inhibitory effects on multiple STING subtypes and is a broad-spectrum STING inhibitor. Notably, it had no effect on STING mRNA levels (Fig. 4e). Moreover, DDO-6513 showed no cytotoxicity at concentrations ranging from 3.13 to 50 μM in THP-1 and WI-38 cells for 12 or 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 1a). We next investigated the impact of DDO-6513 on STING-initiated downstream signaling. The cGAMP-induced oligomerization of STING was inhibited by DDO-6513 (Fig. 4g). DDO-6513 also disrupted the recruitment of TBK1 to STING in cGAMP-activated THP-1 cells and in HEK293T cells expressing tagged STING and TBK1 (Fig. 4h, i) and effectively attenuated the phosphorylation of key proteins (TBK1 and IRF3) in a dose-dependent manner, upon activation by cGAMP in THP-1 (Fig. 4j) and HFF cells (Fig. 4k). Moreover, DDO-6513 inhibited IRF3 dimerization (Fig. 4l) and nuclear translocation (Fig. 4m). Importantly, these cellular experiments confirmed that DDO-6513 induces abnormal STING polymerization and remains active even under cGAMP stimulation. Additionally, we explored the effect of DDO-6513 on the STING-PERK-eIF2α pathway49 and reported that the phosphorylation of PERK and eIF2α remained unchanged during cGAMP-induced STING activation in the presence of DDO-6513. These findings suggest that STING-mediated PERK-eIF2α and TBK1-IRF3 signaling are relatively independent processes and that DDO-6513 specifically targets the STING-TBK1-IRF3 axis (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Furthermore, DDO-6513 did not affect the MAVS- or TLR4-dependent innate immune pathway, as shown in THP-1 or HFF cells stimulated with LPS, 3p-RNA or poly(I:C) (Fig. 4a, n and Supplementary Fig. 1d–f). Moreover, thermal proteome profiling (TPP) revealed the binding of DDO-6513 to STING, thereby confirming the compound’s selective interaction with this target in cells (Fig. 4o). These comprehensive analyses demonstrate that DDO-6513 is a specific inhibitor of the STING-dependent TBK1-IRF3 signaling pathway.

DDO-6513 ameliorates pathological STING overactivation

Given the significant role of STING overactivation in autoinflammatory diseases,50–54 we collaborated with the Department of Rheumatology and Immunology at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital and Children’s Hospital of Soochow University to further explore the biological effects in patients with autoinflammatory diseases. Screening of PBMCs from diverse autoimmune disease patients identified DDO-6513 as a potential therapeutic candidate. Notably, DDO-6513 attenuated the expression of ISGs (MX1, IFNB1 and ISG56) and TNFA in PBMCs from an AGS patient carrying TREX1 mutations that led to excessive activation of the STING pathway (Fig. 5a). Additionally, analysis of PBMCs from 10 DM patients, 30 SLE patients and 6 healthy donors revealed DM-associated increases in inflammatory markers.55 DDO-6513 treatment reduced ISGs and TNFA expression in these DM samples (Fig. 5b). DDO-6513 inhibited the inflammatory phenotype specifically in PBMCs from SLE patients with an elevated inflammatory phenotype (Fig. 5c). Intriguingly, despite the incomplete inhibitory effects on lupus PBMCs, DDO-6513 also inhibited ITGAM, which is strongly associated with SLE pathology.56 Furthermore, RNA-sequencing profiles of these cells indicated that DDO-6513 substantially suppressed the pathological innate immune gene signatures typically activated in SLE (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 2). The therapeutic potential of DDO-6513 was evaluated in cellular models mimicking SAVI induced by gain-of-function mutations in STING. DDO-6513 inhibited the expression of IFNB1, CXCL10 and ISG56 in HEK293T cells transfected with SAVI-associated STING mutants (N154S, V155M, G166E, C206Y, R281Q and R284G) (Fig. 5e). Additionally, the phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3 was reduced by DDO-6513 treatment (Fig. 5f). To further elucidate the impact of DDO-6513 on the subcellular localization of SAVI-associated STING mutants, immunofluorescence assays were performed. These experiments revealed that SAVI-associated STING mutants strongly colocalized with the Golgi apparatus under basal conditions. DDO-6513 administration diminished this colocalization of Golgi-STING and instead induced the formation of distinct STING aggregates at alternative subcellular sites (Supplementary Fig. 3). We next assessed the impact of pharmacological inhibition of STING in vivo. Pharmacodynamic studies in C57BL/6JSTING-Hu mice revealed that pretreatment with DDO-6513 attenuated the secretion of IFN-β (Fig. 6h) induced by SR-717, a recognized STING agonist,57 and mitigated various indicators of systemic inflammation (Fig. 6i). Moreover, toxicological assessments confirmed the favorable drug-like properties of DDO-6513 (Fig. 6j, k). To identify a more advanced antagonist of human STING, a further SAR study identified DDO-7432 with a trifluoromethoxy substitution (THP-1-Dual IC50 = 1.37 ± 0.06 μM) (Fig. 7a). DDO-7432 exhibited better inhibitory activity than did DDO-6513 (Fig. 7b–e). Moreover, it demonstrated favorable potency and selectivity in both hPBMCs and HFF cells (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). DDO-7432 also induced the self-association of STING to inhibit STING-dependent signal transduction (Fig. 7f and Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). Specifically, DDO-7432 suppressed the inflammatory response induced by HSV-1 infection in THP-1 cells, indicating its potential to inhibit inflammation associated with STING-dependent viral infection (Fig. 7g). Moreover, DDO-7432 exhibited favorable pharmacokinetic properties (Supplementary Fig. 4e). To evaluate the oral inhibitory effect of DDO-7432, 60 mg/kg DDO-7432 was administered intragastrically into C57BL/6JSTING-Hu mice, in contrast to the effects of the STING inhibitors H151 and SN-011. Notably, H151 showed almost no inhibitory activity because of its extremely low oral bioavailability.58 The experimental results demonstrate that compared with both a covalent inhibitor (H151) and a representative CDN pocket inhibitor (SN-011), DDO-7432, an inducer of aberrant STING polymerization, has drug-like properties. Discussion

Accumulating studies have highlighted the essential function of the cGAS-STING pathway in autoimmune and autoinflammatory disorders, validating STING as an attractive therapeutic target. In this study, we identified a pioneering strategy for STING inhibition in addition to covalent binding and CDN pocket occupancy. This strategy, which induces protein self-association to disrupt physiological oligomerization, allows us to investigate STING inhibitors from a fresh perspective and possesses significant therapeutic potential. We identified DDO-6501 as a lead compound via screening. This molecule specifically binds to the CTD of STING and effectively inhibits IRF luciferase reporter activity. Subsequent SAR analysis led to the discovery of a more potent analogue, DDO-6513, which markedly reduced the transcription of STING-activated immune and proinflammatory genes. The cocrystal structure revealed that DDO-6501 and DDO-6513 act as molecular glues and induce the formation of a binding pocket formed by the α1 helix, β2 sheet and β2-β3 loop of the two STING protomers. This pocket demonstrates species specificity because of amino acid variations in the adjacent region, which also partially explains the evolutionary divergences in STING oligomeric patterns across vertebrates. Mechanistically, a combination of crystallographic studies, native PAGE, CN-PAGE and immunofluorescence analyses collectively demonstrated that DDO-6513 induces self-association and impedes the generation of STING linear polymers required for cGAS-STING pathway activation. Our proposed inhibition model, informed by crystallographic data and cellular mechanism studies, suggests that DDO-6513 facilitates the self-association of STING, thereby blocking the recruitment and phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3 and preventing downstream signal transduction. This model is consistent with the reported autoinhibitory state of STING characterized by head-to-head and side-by-side packing in the bilayer.48 Notably, recent structural evidence has revealed that duck STING forms a distinctive Z-shaped tetramer stabilized by intermolecular disulfide bonds and polar interaction networks, which mediates its intrinsic autoinhibition.59 A similar self-assembly-driven autoinhibitory mechanism may also exist in human STING, providing a reasonable structural basis for the inhibitory effect elicited by our small-molecule compound. In conclusion, we reveal a novel inhibitory mechanism, which will facilitate the development of STING inhibitors with diversified structural scaffolds in future research.

Protein oligomerization is a critical biological process in cellular life activities and governs the function of numerous immune adaptor proteins. Disrupting or remodeling constitutive homomers represents an emerging strategy in drug design. Compared with traditional active site inhibition, targeting protein oligomerization is advantageous because of its enhanced druggability, reduced risk of drug resistance, and an expanded repertoire of therapeutic targets. However, physiological protein oligomers exhibit high stability, and homomeric interaction interfaces are often large yet shallow, making targeting protein oligomerization challenging. Molecular glues have wide applications as tool molecules for modulating protein conformational states and oligomeric assembly.60 Several molecular glues that induce protein oligomerization, such as those targeting BCL6 and PD-L1,42 have been reported. The STING protein adopts diverse multimeric assemblies in both its resting and activated states, and distinct assembly modes precisely underlie the state transitions from autoinhibition to full activation. In the resting state, STING adopts intrinsic head-to-head and side-by-side packing to maintain an autoinhibited conformation. Unlike STING within homogeneous protein solution systems, this native aggregated state in cells is more likely to be converted into aberrant oligomers by DDO-6513 or DDO-7432, which ultimately locks STING in an inactive form and prevents the assembly of signaling-competent linear polymers. In conclusion, our work demonstrates that targeted regulation of STING oligomerization may represent a novel and pharmacologically tractable approach to modulating protein activity, thereby broadening the druggable landscape for oligomerization-dependent immune signaling pathways and providing a valuable template for the development of STING modulators.

The essential role of the cGAS-STING pathway in treatment-resistant autoinflammatory disorders highlights the urgent demand for safe and effective STING inhibitors with favorable druggability.61 Current STING targeted compounds mainly focus on competitive occupancy of the ligand-binding pocket or covalent modification of key residues, while few compounds modulate STING function by disrupting its oligomeric rearrangement. DDO-6513 inhibited the expression of IFNB1 and other inflammatory genes induced by STING SAVI-associated mutants. Notably, these SAVI-related mutants were found to activate STING signaling independent of cGAMP binding.62 Clinically, Novartis has advanced a selective STING small-molecule inhibitor into clinical trials for the treatment of SAVI, further confirming that STING blockade is a viable therapeutic strategy for this rare monogenic autoinflammatory disease. In this context, our strategy of promoting STING oligomerization to inhibit its activation provides an alternative mechanistic route to suppress hyperactive STING signaling, offering a novel intervention direction for constitutively activated STING-related genetic autoinflammatory diseases. An inhibitor targeting cGAS has progressed to clinical trials for the treatment of SLE in 2023.63 SLE is a complex and heterogeneous disease driven by aberrant innate immune activation and persistent type I interferon signature. cGAS-STING pathway activation is considered one of the core pathological factors involved in disease progression. With respect to PBMCs from 30 SLE patients, our results reveal that in some patients whose expression of cGAS-STING-related mRNAs was relatively high, the inhibition of these genes was relatively strong. However, in other patients whose expression was not significantly elevated compared with that in normal individuals, the inhibitory effect was weaker. RNA sequencing analysis of samples with high expression of specific inflammatory factors clearly demonstrated that immune-regulatory factors were significantly enriched upon compound treatment. These results suggest that STING inhibition represents a promising therapeutic strategy for SLE, particularly for interferon pathway-driven disease subtypes with hyperactivated cGAS-STING signaling. Indeed, when multiple inflammatory autoimmune diseases, including rare AGS, were evaluated, the data revealed that samples from patients with DM exhibited the highest levels of inflammatory cytokine production, consequently showing the most significant therapeutic response to the STING inhibitor DDO-6513. In DM models with C57BL/6JSTING-Hu, DDO-7432 also demonstrated significant therapeutic potential, and validation of its efficacy laid a crucial foundation for further exploration into its application in DM and other idiopathic inflammatory myopathies. Moreover, DDO-7432 can alleviate inflammatory responses induced by viral infection, indicating its broad application prospects in both autoimmune diseases and infectious inflammation. Therefore, future studies should define the optimal therapeutic window and clarify whether preventive administration outperforms rescue therapy at the peak of inflammation, to maximize efficacy and reduce immune-related side effects.

Ethics approval statements

The study and protocols were carried out in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Affiliated Drum Tower Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (Protocol number 2021-246-02) and the Children’s Hospital of Soochow University (Protocol number 2024CS041).

Mice and in vivo studies

C57BL/6J mice were purchased from SiPeiFu. C57BL/6JSTING-Hu transgenic mice were generated by GemPharmatech. Mice were maintained under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. To assess the in vivo inhibitory effect of DDO-6513, C57BL/6JSTING-Hu (8–12 weeks of age) received intraperitoneal injection of vehicle or DDO-6513 (50 mg/kg) dissolved in 200 μL solvent consisting of 5% DMSO, 10% Tween-80 and 30% PEG400 in normal saline. After 3 h, SR-717 was intraperitoneally administered at 15 mg/kg. After consecutive daily dosing for 3 days, serum samples were collected 3 h after SR-717 dosing to detect SR-717-induced cytokine levels. For toxicology studies, 8-week-old mice were intraperitoneally injected with DDO-6513 at 50 mg/kg daily for 14 days. At day 14, tissues were fixed in 4% (wt/vol) paraformaldehyde, followed by paraffin embedding, sectioning into 5 μm slices, and hematoxylin-eosin staining. To assess the in vivo inhibitory effect of DDO-7432, C57BL/6JSTING-Hu (8–12 weeks of age) were intragastrically administered with DDO-7432 (60 mg/kg), H151 (60 mg/kg) or SN-011 (60 mg/kg) in 200 μL normal saline containing 10% DMSO, 20% Tween-80 and 30% PEG400. For the efficacy evaluation in the C57BL/6JSTING-Hu myositis model, 6-week-old mice were immunized once a week for four weeks with skeletal muscle homogenate emulsified in complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA, Beyotime) containing mycobacteria. Meanwhile, 250 ng pertussis toxin (List Labs) was injected intraperitoneally. Starting on day 18 after the initial immunization, mice were intragastrically given DMSO, DDO-7432 (30 or 60 mg/kg), H151 (60 mg/kg) or SN-011 (60 mg/kg) once daily for 17 consecutive days. All compounds were dissolved in normal saline with 5% DMSO, 10% Tween-80 and 30% PEG400.

Crystallization, data collection, and structure determination

Both co-crystals of STING complex with DDO-6501 and DDO-6513 were obtained by hanging drop vapor diffusion method. STING-CTD was incubated with 5 molar equivalents of compound overnight at 4 °C before crystallization. For DDO-6501, 10 mg/mL STING protein was mixed with an equal volume of reservoir solution consisting of 0.2 M calcium acetate, 20% (w/v) polyethylene glycol 3350 and 0.1 M sodium acetate (pH 6.0) at 16 °C. For DDO-6513, the protein sample was mixed equivalently with reservoir solution containing 2.5 M sodium formate and 0.1 M sodium acetate (pH 5.0). Diffraction data were collected at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility and processed with HKL2000 and XDS. Structures were solved by molecular replacement using PDB ID 4F5W as a search model. The model was further refined using automatic model refinement with Phenix64 and manual model building with Coot.65

Native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE)

Purified STING-CTD at a concentration of 20 µM was incubated with DMSO (control), 40 µM cGAMP, and 50, 100 or 200 µM DDO-6513 in the presence of buffer (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl) overnight at 4 °C. The samples were mixed with 5% glycerol and resolved using a 4–20% Tris-glycine native PAGE gel for 1 h at 150 V on ice. The gel was then stained with Coomassie brilliant blue.

Clear native PAGE

THP-1 cells were stimulated with cGAMP (2.5 μM) for 1 h or incubated with DDO-6513 or DDO-7432 for 4 h. THP-1 cells were lysed in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% DDM). The lysates were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, after which the supernatant was collected. Afterward, the supernatant was separated by 8% CN-PAGE on the basis of a previous protocol.48

Size-exclusion chromatography to analyze the complex

The purified STING-CTD protein was incubated with DDO-6513 at molar ratios of 2:1, 10:1 and 50:1 in the presence of buffer A (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl) overnight at 4 °C. The complex was loaded onto a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column equilibrated with buffer A.

Zetasizer nano ZS to measure the size distributions

STING (1 mg/mL) was mixed with different concentrations of DDO-6513 or cGAMP, and DMSO was used as a negative control overnight at 4 °C. A 1 mL sample was loaded into a Zetasizer Nano ZS, and the particle size distributions were measured in a protein model at 25 °C. The data were exported and analyzed using GraphPad Prism software.
