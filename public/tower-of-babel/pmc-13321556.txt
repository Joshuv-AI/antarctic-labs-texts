Understanding Substance Use in Alaska Native Youth- A Social Network Perspective
Authors: Madeleine Mason, Katie Schultz, Shuwen Zhang, Jerreed D Ivanich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlaska Native (AN) youth and their communities experience many health inequities, including increased rates of substance use. AN youth have earlier initiation and heavier use of substances compared to other youth, with over half of AN youth having engaged in use by 13. While prior research has examined risk factors, few studies have evaluated how social network structures may protect against substance use in youth. This study used social network analysis to examine associations between peer ties, connections with school staff, cultural connectedness, victimization, and substance use among AN youth.MethodsThis cross-sectional study collected social network data from 7th -12th grade students (n = 109) at an urban school in Alaska using Network Canvas, a tablet-based platform. Students nominated peers, school staff, family, and non-family, non-school individuals to whom they are close. They then completed survey items assessing cultural connectedness, substance use, depression, victimization, and suicidal ideation. Multivariate logistic regression models examined associations between demographic, psychosocial, and network characteristics and alcohol, tobacco, and marijuana use in AN youth.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 14.7 years, with youth nominating an average of 15.4 network members. Victimization was consistently associated with greater odds of alcohol, tobacco, and marijuana use. For alcohol use, contact frequency with staff was protective at higher levels, demonstrated by a negative quadratic effect, while greater emotional closeness to those in one’s network had a positive linear effect. Similar patterns were observed for marijuana use, including a significant negative quadratic effect of contact frequency and a positive linear effect of closeness. Neither contact frequency nor closeness was significantly associated with tobacco use. Greater cultural connectedness was associated with greater odds of tobacco use but was not significantly associated with alcohol or marijuana use.ConclusionSubstance use among urban AN youth is influenced by complex social processes involving victimization, peer relationships, and connections with school staff. Frequent contact with school staff appears to play a protective role against alcohol and marijuana use. This study demonstrates how network data can identify both risk and protective factors embedded in youths’ social environments and insights can be directly applied to the development of prevention strategies.Keywords: Indigenous, Alcohol, Marijuana, Tobacco, Adolescence, School connectedness, Victimization

Background

Historical and environmental factors are key contributors to the health inequities that Alaska Native (AN) communities and their youth face today, including rates of substance use. Previous literature has consistently demonstrated that AN youth have earlier initiation and heavier use of substances compared to other youth [1–3]. Studies indicate that over half of AN youth have engaged in substance use by the age of 13 [1]. The initiation of substances at a younger age and with higher frequency among AN youth has detrimental consequences during adolescence and persists into adulthood [3, 4].

Early initiation of substance use and problematic use are not isolated behaviors but are closely intertwined with other serious outcomes like suicide and violence. For example, Wexler [5] found rates of death by suicide 18 times higher among AN youth than non-AN youth, with the majority of suicidal behavior coinciding with alcohol use. Furthermore, though literature focused exclusively on AN youth remains limited, substance use has been associated with violence in AI/AN youth [6–8]. Substance use has been linked to an increased likelihood of perpetrating violence [6], becoming a victim of violence [8, 9], and witnessing violence [7] among American Indian/Alaska Native (AI/AN) youth. These consequences persist into adulthood. Among AI/AN adults who reported past-month alcohol use, 72% had engaged in binge drinking [10], suggesting the continuation of substance use behaviors across the lifespan. Thus, substance use is related to, and intertwined with, serious consequences, illustrating the need for further characterization of substance use among AN youth and related prevention approaches.

While much of the literature has focused on identifying risk factors, understanding protective factors is of equal or greater importance. AN communities possess longstanding relational and cultural strengths that may serve as protective assets for youth. Strong kinship systems are a central feature of AN communities, where geographic isolation and limited resources often increase the reliance on social connections among family and the broader community. Historically, interconnected families share responsibilities to support the collective well-being, and this emphasis on close relationships remains defining today. Intergenerational networks are also prominent in AN communities, where extended relatives often play central roles in caregiving [11]. Many AN youth grow up in communities with strong relational ties and interconnected family networks [11], which reinforces identity and communal responsibility. Furthermore, although AN communities have faced historical marginalization and limited access to healthcare and education, they have demonstrated remarkable resilience. Research in other populations suggests that social networks may play a crucial role in fostering adaptive capacity and resilience in response to social, economic, and political stressors [12, 13]. Taken together, the cultural and relational structures that exist in AN communities likely play a significant role in youth development and may act to protect against adverse behaviors, including substance use.

As youth transition into adolescence, the influence that their social networks have on their decision making begins to change. While family and community ties likely remain important, adolescents begin to rely more on their peer networks and less on their families for decision making [14–17]. As a result, understanding substance use during adolescence requires investigating the structure and influence of peer relationships. One promising and emerging approach to such work is the use of social network analysis (SNA) [16, 18–21]. SNA has historically provided important insights into substance use behaviors [22] and contributed to prevention and intervention efforts [19, 23, 24]. One area within the field of SNA that shows promise is school networks, as school peers are a major contributor to most adolescent social connections [25–27].

It is well established that youth who associate with risk-taking peers, including those who use substances, face a higher likelihood of engaging in substance use themselves [28]. However, network characteristics can also confer protection against substance use. Emerging evidence highlights the importance of positive peer relationships and connectedness to supportive adult figures, particularly school staff. A study in college students found that positive peer relationships were associated with lower rates of drunk driving, smoking, and marijuana use [29]. Similarly, adolescents (children ages 11–16), who reported stronger relationships to school staff were significantly less likely to use substances, with reductions of up to 45% [30]. Collectively, this growing body of research suggests that supportive peer and adult relationships may represent modifiable protective factors within adolescent networks. This may support shifting prevention efforts away from solely addressing negative peer influences and toward fostering positive friendships and social connections for improved youth outcomes.

Despite the promise of social network analysis, this research has largely overlooked AN youth. Social network data from AN youth will be essential for understanding how network structures shape both exposure to risk behaviors and access to protective relationships. A deeper understanding of these dynamics will enable the development of network-based intervention strategies tailored to AN youth.

The current study investigated the risk and protective factors, particularly connections with school staff and cultural connectedness, associated with alcohol, tobacco, and marijuana use in urban AN youth using social network analysis. We hypothesized that connectedness with school staff and culture will be protective against substance use. To our knowledge, this is the first SNA exploring connectedness with school staff on substance use among AN youth.

Methods

Sampling and recruitment

The research team collaborated with a 7th -12th grade charter school in Alaska. All students enrolled during data collection were eligible but not required to participate in the study. This school is located in one of the urban cities of Alaska. Youth and families opt to attend this school as an alternative to traditionally tracked public school placement based on geographic boundaries. We had an overall response rate of 80% during the two-day data collection period. The total sample size is 109.

Data collection

We used an interactive data collection platform specifically designed for social network data [31]. This platform, Network Canvas, affords the user a more intuitive, approachable, and fast means of data collection using tablet touch-based gestures. Each student was administered the data collection survey. These respondents, referred to as egos within social network literature, were first provided with a series of questions that resembled a typical survey. These questions focused on topics such as demographic information and information about their cultural connectedness, substance use, suicidal ideation, depression, and exposure to violence. The questions for suicidal ideation were adapted from the Center for Disease Control’s (CDC) Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System Survey [32, 33]. Survey questions pertaining to depression were adapted from Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale [34]. Exposure to violence, including both direct experiences and witnessing violence, is referred to here as victimization. Victimization was assessed via questions adapted from the Juvenile Victimization Questionnaire (JVQ) [35]. This questionnaire captures multiple forms of victimization, including direct experiences (e.g., physical assault, bullying, and sexual victimization) as well as indirect exposure such as witnessing acts of violence. Items were scored as yes/no, and a cumulative victimization score was created by summing endorsed items. Connection to culture survey items were adapted from the Cultural Connectedness Scale [36]. These items assess participation in cultural activities, relationships with cultural figures, and engagement in traditional practices.

After these questions, egos were then asked to complete a social network assessment. This assessment consisted of three phases. In phase 1, egos were given prompts that solicited people in their network; this is referred to as name-generator questions. For this study, egos were given four distinct name generators to capture a wide net of social connections. First, they were provided a school-based name generator, which prompted them to, “select up to 10 friends you are closest with in grades 7th-12th at your school. Search for specific friends using search box”. The school provided the student roster to help with this name generator. As egos began typing a name, names containing the letters typed would begin to appear and filter for students to select. This is critical for data quality as it provides a standardized set of names that could be directly linked afterward. For example, if an ego intended to nominate their friend, John Smith, it is entirely possible, and likely, that this name could be entered in several variations of spelling (Jon Smith vs. John Smith) or as nicknames/abbreviated names (Johnny Smith, Johno Smith, J Smith). Second, egos were asked to provide the names of up to eight family members outside of their school they are close to. Third, egos were asked to select up to eight school staff they are close with. Lastly, they were asked to provide up to eight names of non-school and non-family individuals they felt close to.

In the second phase of the social network assessment, egos were asked a series of questions about each of the people they nominated, called alters. The questions are referred to as name interpreter questions, intended to explore the attributes of alters. These include questions about the alters’ demographics (age, gender identity, race/ethnicity, and socioeconomic status). Among alters named as a school-based friend, we asked, “Are you related to this person?” and they could indicate by dragging and dropping the name of the alter into a yes or no category. For alters identified as family members they were asked how they were related (e.g., parent/caregiver, grandparent, sibling, cousin, aunt/uncle). Additionally, name interpreter questions were given for all alters regarding the type of relationship between the ego and alter, frequency of contact, and how close the ego felt to each alter.

The final phase of the social network assessment is an assessment of network density. To capture how dense an ego’s network is, the ego is shown a visual layout of all their alters randomly on the screen. They are then asked to indicate which alters are close to one another by selecting two alters to connect in Network Canvas by creating a line or edge between these two alters. Specifically, the question asked of the ego was, “Select people who are close, even when you are not around”. This allows for the ego to identify how socially embedded their network is.

Data were collected over two days during school hours. School administrators sent students to the research team in groups of about 30 students at a time. These students were selected for specific times of the day based on their class schedules. The research team surveyed students late in the fall semester to allow networks to stabilize after the start of the school year. Students entered responses directly on tablets, with research team members available to answer or clarify questions. Youth received a $25 gift card for participating in the survey. Parental permission was provided for all participating students, and student assent was completed before data collection. All protocols were reviewed and approved by the university ethics review board.

Data analysis

The survey allows us to produce data at various levels. First, this data allows us to explore ego-level descriptive factors (individual attributes/demographics). Second, this survey data allows us to produce social network data at three levels. The first type of social network data afforded is ego-network data. These data focus on the ego, so we can explore each egos’ connections across school, family, non-school/non-family, and school staff. The second type of social network data explored is the school-based network of all youth at the school to each other. The final is a depiction of the school network with the staff added into this network.

Data analysis for this paper took place in three stages. First, demographic distributions were calculated for the egos. This provides an overview of the sample for various characteristics valuable for understanding the contextual background of the respondents. Variables for this stage include age, gender, race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, current school, and length of attendance at current school.

Second, we analyzed the ego network data. We calculated several structural variables, including: (i) the average size of the ego network, (ii) the average proportion of school-based and family-based alters, (iii) the average proportion of non-school, non-family-based alters, (iv) the average proportion of overall network that are family (combined peer-related and family alters), (v) the number of Native alters, (vi) the average proportion of same-gender alters, (vii) the average proportion of alters who share the same socioeconomic status, and (viii) the average number of alters in different age categories.

Lastly, we examined the school-based networks. These include the whole school network of students and a separate network of students and school staff. We provide information on the density, average in-degree (nominations of an individual from others in the network), centralization, the proportion of same-gender ties, and the proportion of family ties. We provide visualizations of the school (Fig. 1). This initial description of these networks is limited to high-level descriptions of the overarching structures in the data; rigorous empirical data analyses of these structures and comparisons to networks in other populations are beyond the scope of this paper.

In this sample, 50.5% were female, 44.0% were male, and 5.5% another gender. The overwhelming majority of participants identified as AN alone (60.6%) with roughly 22.9% identifying as AN in combination with another race, and the remaining ~ 16% identifying as another race. Most students (60.6%) identified their sexual orientation as being straight.

Ego network descriptives

Finally, we describe the school-based peer network, defined as the sociocentric network of student-to-student nominations. The network comprised 109 students connected by 459 directed ties (Fig. 1). Network density was low (0.044), a level of sparsity that is typical for adolescent peer networks, particularly in a school spanning multiple grade levels (7th–12th). On average, students received 4.5 nominations from peers, and peer ties exhibited moderate gender homophily, with approximately 63.0% of nominations occurring between students of the same gender. Figure 1 illustrates the overall structure of this network and highlights a densely connected peer environment with relatively few isolates or peripheral students. These analyses are intended to provide a high-level description of the overall structure of the school peer network; more detailed modeling of these structures is beyond the scope of the present study.

We explored the role of gender, sexual orientation, depression, and victimization as demographic and psychosocial covariates in the base models. Compared with females, males had significantly lower odds of alcohol use across all three models (Model 1: OR = 0.27, 95% CI [0.09, 0.081], Model 2: OR = 0.3, 95% CI [0.09, 0.98]; Model 3: OR = 0.04, 95% CI [0.003, 0.52]; Table 3). Gender was not significantly associated with tobacco and marijuana use in any model (Tables 4 and 5). Sexual orientation was not significantly associated with any substance use across all models. Depressive symptoms were positively associated with alcohol use in the fully adjusted model only (OR = 1.22, 95% CI [1.00, 1.49]; Table 3) but were not significantly associated with tobacco or marijuana use. In contrast, victimization was consistently and positively associated with all three substance use outcomes, including alcohol (Model 3: OR = 1.67, 95% CI [1.13, 2.47]; Table 3), tobacco (Model 3: OR = 1.46, 95% CI [1.10, 1.94]; Table 4), and marijuana (Model 3: OR = 1.68, 95% CI [1.06, 2.66]; Table 5).

We included several network composition variables in our second set of models for all three outcomes, presented in Tables 3, 4 and 5. Specifically, we included the number of school alters nominated and the number of staff nominated. We did not find any significant relationship when introduced to the models. Furthermore, these null associations remained non-significant after adjusting for network interaction characteristics in model 3 of Tables 3, 4 and 5.

In our final adjusted models, we include network interaction characteristics and cultural connectedness. Network interaction characteristics exhibited more nuanced, substance-specific associations. For alcohol use, both contact frequency and emotional closeness demonstrated significant associations, with negative quadratic terms for contact frequency (OR = 0.001, 95% CI [< 0.001, 0.09]) and a positive linear effect of closeness (OR = 9.97, 95% CI [1.143, 69.7]) in the fully adjusted model (Table 3). Similar patterns were observed for marijuana use, including a significant negative quadratic effect of contact frequency (OR = < 0.001, 95% CI [< 0.001, < 0.001]) and a positive linear effect of closeness (OR = 66.7, 95% CI [1.92, 2316]) (Table 5). In contrast, neither contact frequency nor closeness was significantly associated with tobacco use in any model (Table 4). Cultural connectedness was positively associated with tobacco use in the fully adjusted model (OR = 2.25, 95% CI [1.02, 4.97]; Table 4) but was not significantly associated with alcohol or marijuana use.

Discussion & implications

The purpose of this study was to examine the risk and protective factors, particularly peer relationships and connections with school staff, associated with alcohol, tobacco, and marijuana use in urban AN youth using social network analysis. Overall, the findings highlight a clear association between social influence and substance use behaviors and demonstrate that both risk and protective factors exist within urban AN youth social environments. Victimization was a consistent risk factor across substances, while relationships with school staff, gender, depression, and cultural connectedness showed more nuanced, substance-specific patterns. These patterns highlight the value of a network-based approach for identifying social processes that can inform targeted prevention strategies.

Victimization was consistently associated with increased alcohol, tobacco, and marijuana use (Tables 3, 4 and 5). These findings align with previous literature demonstrating a correlation between victimization and substance use [2, 37]. This pattern may suggest that substance use functions as a coping response to victimization and reinforces that substance use and victimization are closely interconnected among urban AN youth. Importantly, the consistency of this association across all three substances suggests that victimization may represent a critical intervention point.

Several network interaction characteristics, particularly contact frequency with school staff and emotional closeness to alters, exhibited substance-specific associations. For alcohol use, contact frequency with staff demonstrated a significant, nonlinear association illustrated by a negative quadratic odds ratio (Table 3). Similar patterns were observed for marijuana use, including a significant negative quadratic effect of contact frequency (Table 5). In contrast, contact frequency was not significantly associated with tobacco use in any model (Table 4). The strong, negative, and non-linear effect of contact frequency for alcohol and marijuana use suggests that a threshold exists, such that minimal contact with staff may be insufficient, but that after some frequency threshold, this contact between students and staff confers protection. In practical terms, this may suggest that simply having access to supportive adults may not be adequate, but that consistent and repeated engagement may be necessary to meaningfully influence marijuana and alcohol use behaviors. Taken together, these results suggest that relationships with school staff are critical in protecting against substance use, similar to the limited data in the literature [30]. To our knowledge, this study provides some of the first evidence that frequent engagement with staff may protect against substance use in urban AN youth. This insight is particularly valuable because it identifies a modifiable, school-based support that could be strengthened through interventions like mentoring programs, staff trainings, and intentional relationship building initiatives.

In contrast, greater emotional closeness to alters was associated with an increased risk of alcohol (Table 3) and marijuana use (Table 5). There are a few possible explanations. Greater perceived closeness may strengthen social influence processes, increasing the likelihood that youth would adopt behaviors that are modeled or normalized within their close social ties [14, 15]. Furthermore, it is possible that this association reflects a selection process, in which youth who use substances may form closer ties with others who engage in similar behaviors. Regardless, this finding highlights that social connectedness can serve as both a risk and protective factor, depending on the norms, behaviors, and alters within one’s network.

Ego engagement with culture, gender, and depressive symptoms were also associated with nuanced, substance-specific patterns. Cultural connectedness was positively associated with tobacco use (Table 4) but not associated with alcohol or marijuana use (Tables 3 and 5). It is possible that cultural connectedness may serve as a proxy for AN identity. It is well established that these populations experience disproportionately high rates of tobacco use due to historical trauma, targeted marketing, and structural inequities [10, 38]. Moreover, it is important to highlight that tobacco is not traditionally used for ceremonial purposes in AN communities. Thus, the observed association likely reflects broader population-level disparities rather than a causal relationship between cultural engagement and tobacco use. Furthermore, males demonstrated significantly lower odds of alcohol use (Table 3). There is mixed literature on the role of gender in adolescent alcohol use, but recent trends may suggest that younger females are at the highest risk, consistent with our data [39]. Finally, depressive symptoms were associated with alcohol use (Table 3), suggesting a potentially unique relationship between alcohol and depression. These substance-specific data emphasize the importance of tailoring prevention strategies towards risk factors for specific substances.

Study strengths include a high participation rate (80%) and the use of a youth-friendly social network data collection system. However, there are a couple limitations. The cross-sectional nature of this study does not allow for causal understanding of these relationships. This study was also conducted at a single, urban school, which may limit generalizability to AN youth in rural or reservation-based communities. Finally, all measures were self-reported and may be subject to reporting bias, especially given that adolescents were asked to disclose stigmatized and illegal substance use behaviors. This may have resulted in underreporting due to confidentiality concerns.

Future research should continue to evaluate the role of school staff within youth social networks, as these relationships appear to represent a promising and actionable avenue for intervention. Expanding data collection to larger schools, multiple schools, or an entire school district would allow for a more comprehensive understanding of how social network structures influence substance use among this population. Longitudinal network studies would also help researchers understand how these behaviors and social structures adapt and change over time.

Conclusion

Taken together, this study demonstrates that substance use among urban AN youth is influenced by complex social processes involving victimization, peer relationships, and connections with school staff. Frequent contact with school staff appears to play protective roles against alcohol and marijuana use. This study demonstrates how network data can identify both risk and protective factors embedded in youths’ social environments. These insights can be directly applied to the development of targeted, culturally informed, and evidence-based prevention strategies that leverage systems already in place.
