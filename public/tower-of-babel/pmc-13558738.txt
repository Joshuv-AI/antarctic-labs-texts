Uniform periodic nano-patterning of metallic glass substrates via high-frequency nanosecond laser irradiation
Authors: Yongfeng Qian, Hong An, Hu Huang, Minqiang Jiang, Jiwang Yan, Luquan Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAchieving high spatial uniformity in laser-induced periodic surface structures (LIPSS) has long remained elusive, as even femtosecond lasers, despite their nominally “cold” processing capability, cannot eliminate the stochastic defects that compromise long-range order. Here, we present a strategy that utilizes high-repetition-frequency nanosecond pulses, a regime traditionally deemed unsuitable for precision nano-patterning due to pronounced thermal dissipation, to fabricate one-dimensional (1D) LIPSS with uniform morphology on structurally homogeneous metallic glass (MG) substrates. This is achieved by exploiting strong inter-pulse correlation, which simultaneously moderates thermal fluctuations and sustains a coherent optical near-field to template deterministic pattern formation from stochastic seeds. Performing repeated scanning along the same single-line path transform these 1D patterns into regular two‑dimensional (2D) nanodot arrays. Finite-difference time-domain simulations reveal that a unified optical near-field interaction governs the entire structural evolution, from initial 1D ordering to subsequent 2D reconfiguration. The process preserves the MG substrate’s amorphous state and chemical composition while being inherently scalable.Subject terms: Laser material processing, Sub-wavelength optics, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

Light sculpts matter, with the laser as its finest chisel. This principle finds a clear manifestation in laser‑induced periodic surface structures (LIPSS), which were initially reported on semiconductor surfaces in 19651. Over subsequent decades, research has been driven by the ability of LIPSS to manipulate light–matter interactions, charge transport properties, and interfacial chemistries2–4, enabling breakthroughs in nonlinear nanophotonics, biosensing, and interfacial engineering5–7. Nevertheless, their technological translation has been hindered by a persistent challenge: the inability to achieve high spatial uniformity over macroscopic areas8,9. This limitation stems from a fundamental conflict between intended optical interference and unavoidable energy dissipation, primarily thermal diffusion, which disrupts the coherent feedback required for long‑range order10,11. Achieving uniformity, therefore, demands precise control over energy flow, wherein effective thermal management becomes pivotal to preserving the optical coherence of laser‑matter coupling.

Conventional approaches have sought to circumvent this challenge by employing femtosecond lasers, wherein pulses deposit energy faster than thermal diffusion can dissipate it, thereby sharpening interference contrast12,13. Yet, even under these nominally athermal conditions, long-range disorder frequently persists, arising from stochastic, spatially uncorrelated nucleation events known as independent structural origins14. A more effective route to uniformity lies in suppressing such independent seeding through enforced local feedback, wherein nascent structures are templated directly by their predecessors15,16. For instance, confining the laser spot to a few wavelengths enables collective interaction via shared near-fields, preventing decoupled nucleation17. In dielectric systems, tailored multilayer architectures can suppress surface plasmon polaritons (SPPs) while promoting localized quasi‑cylindrical waves, establishing a self‑sustaining feedback loop that guides the sequential, domino‑like growth of uniform oxide‑based LIPSS18. However, these strategies are fundamentally constrained by a spatial trade-off: optical localization enhances coherence at the expense of throughput, whereas resonant field enhancement demands material- and geometry-specific photonic design. To transcend this constraint, we introduce a temporally coherent feedback framework based on high-repetition-frequency operation: strong pulse‑to‑pulse coupling not only locks each subsequent pulse to the evolving surface morphology left by its predecessor, but also mitigates inter‑pulse thermal fluctuations, thereby maintaining consistent optical near‑field conditions across successive irradiations. This synergistic mechanism contributes to the formation of large‑area, uniform LIPSS by suppressing stochastic nucleation.

A fundamental yet frequently overlooked determinant of LIPSS morphology lies in the substrate itself, specifically its microstructural uniformity and initial topographic state19,20. Prevailing studies predominantly employ crystalline materials, in which intrinsic grain boundaries, crystallographic anisotropy, and defect sites act as heterogeneous nucleation sources that inevitably disrupt spatial coherence21,22. This intrinsic microstructural noise masks the fundamental contribution of optical coherence to laser–matter interactions. Metallic glasses (MGs), in stark contrast, are free from such structural imperfections. Their structural homogeneity, devoid of long-range order, grain boundaries, and chemical segregation, provides an isotropic platform that cleanly decouples optical near-field effects from confounding microstructural variables23–25. This “clean” foundation not only helps elucidate the fundamental mechanisms governing LIPSS formation but also enables uniform nanoscale patterning.

Here, we integrate the sustained optical near-field and suppressed thermal fluctuations enabled by high-repetition-frequency pulses with a macroscopically homogeneous and isotropic Zr-based MG substrate to establish a deterministic platform for guided nanoscale patterning. Despite employing nanosecond-duration pulses, a regime typically eschewed in precision nano-patterning due to pronounced thermal dissipation, we achieve not only uniform one-dimensional (1D) LIPSS on structurally and topographically homogeneous MG substrates, but also their coherent transformation into well-ordered two‑dimensional (2D) LIPSS through a straightforward rescanning procedure. The 1D LIPSS exhibit long-range structural coherence across the patterned region. By adjusting the spatial overlap between successive scans, large-area uniform 1D or 2D LIPSS can be selectively produced while preserving the substrate’s amorphous integrity and chemical composition. These results demonstrate the potential of nanosecond lasers for scalable, uniform nano-fabrication via a robust, chemically inert route.

Results

Frequency-governed non-equilibrium thermodynamics

The scalable fabrication of large-area, uniform LIPSS has long been impeded by a fundamental trade-off between constructive optical interference and disruptive thermal dissipation during laser–matter interactions26,27. Conventional low-repetition-frequency regimes suppress heat accumulation but sacrifice inter-pulse coherence, weakening the coherent near-field coupling essential for long-range order28,29. Prior studies on femtosecond laser nano-structuring have demonstrated that deterministic patterns can emerge when optical phase coherence is maintained across successive pulses17,30. Extending this principle to nanosecond pulses, however, poses a distinct thermal challenge. This challenge becomes especially evident in conventional crystalline or heterostructured substrates, such as Au/Si bilayers, where LIPSS formation relies on laser-induced melting, thermocapillary-driven dewetting, and substrate-imposed crystallographic anisotropy rather than purely coherent optical feedback31. Consequently, pattern evolution in such systems is dominated by hydrodynamic instabilities and stochastic material redistribution, which inherently limit long-range order and preclude precise structural programmability. Here, we demonstrate that high-repetition-frequency nanosecond laser irradiation resolves this conflict through synchronized thermal-optical regulation: shortened inter-pulse intervals exploit thermal inertia to stabilize the transient temperature field32, while increased pulse density strengthens cumulative near-field feedback. This dual-timescale mechanism simultaneously stabilizes both the thermal environment and the optical near-field, enabling deterministic, pulse-correlated pattern formation.

To elucidate the thermodynamics underlying this dual-timescale mechanism, we simulated the temperature evolution on a MG surface irradiated by a 1064 nm nanosecond laser with a pulse duration of 7 ns (full width at 1/e2 of the peak intensity) under different repetition frequencies. The simulations reveal that repetition frequency critically governs thermal stability (Fig. 1a). Each laser pulse deposits energy into the material, and the peak surface temperature is reached ≈10 ns after the pulse onset (Fig. 1b). This delay reflects the characteristic time for thermal diffusion, during which absorbed energy redistributes within the material governed by its thermal diffusivity and volumetric heat capacity. Subsequently, an inter-pulse cooling interval follows, the duration of which depends on the repetition frequency. At 100 kHz, extended cooling leads to significant temperature decay and pronounced thermal fluctuations, which likely degrade near-field coherence and suppress cumulative optical feedback. At 400 kHz, thermal oscillations are moderated but may still be sufficient to locally disrupt near-field coupling, representing an intermediate regime. In contrast, at 800 kHz, the shortened cooling period favors a quasi-steady thermal state, helping maintain thermal inertia and support near-field coherence across successive pulses. These thermal predictions align with the experimentally observed morphological evolution (Fig. 1c), where surfaces were processed using the nanosecond laser fabrication system schematically depicted in Fig. 1d. Under identical power conditions, irradiation at 100 kHz produces only ablation features without LIPSS, consistent with pronounced thermal instability. At 400 kHz, LIPSS appear predominantly near the periphery of the irradiated region, while the center remains ablation-dominated. This spatial dichotomy is governed by the Gaussian beam profile and the resulting local thermal dynamics. The higher intensity at the beam center drives ablation, whereas the lower peripheral intensity enables the thermal stability and coherent near-field coupling conditions required for LIPSS formation. In stark contrast, 800 kHz irradiation yields fully developed, uniform LIPSS across the entire irradiated region without any ablation features. This transition arises from three interdependent factors: suppressed thermal fluctuations, reduced single-pulse energy, and elevated pulse density. The inter-pulse interval at 800 kHz (1.25 μs) is sufficiently short to maintain a quasi-steady thermal state, minimizing inter-pulse temperature variations and preserving near-field coherence over successive pulses. Simultaneously, the single-pulse energy, reduced by a factor of eight relative to that at 100 kHz under a fixed average power, confines energy deposition to a regime in which the material remains optically responsive, enabling coherent coupling between the incident laser field and SPPs without catastrophic surface disruption. Critically, the high pulse density at 800 kHz ensures that each subsequent pulse interacts with a near-field pattern partially imprinted by the preceding pulses, thereby reinforcing phase coherence and driving a domino-like growth process17,30. At 100 kHz, by contrast, the sparse pulse spacing prevents such inter-pulse correlation, rendering pulses effectively independent and incapable of sustaining coherent nano-structuring.

Near-field mediated patterning of 1D and 2D LIPSS

Leveraging the thermally stabilized regime afforded by high‑repetition‑frequency operation, we demonstrate that 800 kHz nanosecond pulses drive the deterministic emergence of uniform 1D LIPSS across MG over a broad fluence (F) range (Fig. 2a–i). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) topographies and corresponding two-dimensional fast Fourier transform (2D-FFT) patterns reveal a degree of spatial regularity and homogeneity surpassing that typically achieved with conventional nanosecond33–35 or femtosecond36–38 laser processing. This structural order of nanosecond laser-based LIPSS is further corroborated by quantitative comparisons with femtosecond laser-based counterparts produced under comparable processing conditions (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Systematic fluence tuning modulates LIPSS dimensions while preserving long-range order. As the fluence increases from 97.6 to 120.5 mJ cm−2, the period (Λ) remains nearly constant at 1042 ± 7 nm (Fig. 2j), slightly below the incident laser wavelength (λ) of 1064 nm. This behavior is characteristic of low-spatial-frequency LIPSS (LSFL), which arise from interference between the incident laser field and SPPs in the optical near-field39. As shown in Fig. 2k, the square of the LIPSS width (W2) scales linearly with the natural logarithm of the incident fluence (ln(F)), which is consistent with the theoretical prediction for a Gaussian beam40. The LIPSS height measured along the center of the irradiated region initially increases with fluence but subsequently declines at higher fluences, correlating with non-monotonic evolution of surface roughness (Ra) in the central region (Fig. 2l). This behavior arises from a competition between optical near-field mediated deterministic nano-patterning and thermally induced disruption. At fluences below a critical threshold, energy deposition remains insufficient to induce substantial melting, enabling progressive amplification of the periodic surface modulation over successive pulses via interference-mediated energy localization, thereby steadily increasing LIPSS height. Once the fluence exceeds this threshold, pronounced surface melting triggers hydrodynamic instabilities that perturb the optical feedback essential for LIPSS ordering, resulting in localized bending and partial degradation of nano-structures at the beam center, as evident in Fig. 2i. In contrast, structures near the periphery remain well-defined. The spatial distribution of mean LIPSS height across the irradiated region (Fig. 2m) exhibits a nearly symmetric V-shaped profile, with the maximum height occurring at ≈10 μm from the beam center on each side, where the fluence supports efficient near-field coupling without inducing significant melting. Closer to the center, excessive fluence suppresses LIPSS height through melting, whereas farther outward the fluence gradually decreases, leading to a progressive decline in LIPSS height until no structures are formed.

To evaluate process robustness, the scanning speed (v) was systematically varied from 2 to 6 mm s−1 at a constant laser fluence of 105.6 mJ cm−2. Across this range, LIPSS maintain uniform morphology, while their width decreases monotonically with increasing scanning speed (Supplementary Fig. 3), corresponding to the reduced energy deposition per unit length. Importantly, the spatial period remains invariant with scanning speed, as confirmed by 2D‑FFT analysis. This insensitivity indicates that LIPSS formation is governed by optical near‑field coupling rather than melt‑flow dynamics, such as thermocapillary convection, which would exhibit strong speed dependence41. We further investigated the influence of microstructural homogeneity on LIPSS formation and uniformity by comparing substrates with varying degrees of crystallinity. Although LIPSS can form on partially crystallized Zr-based alloy specimens (Supplementary Fig. 4), the threshold fluence required for LIPSS formation increases with crystallinity, primarily due to crystallization-induced modifications in optical reflectivity (Supplementary Fig. 5) and thermal conductivity42. Moreover, LIPSS formed on partially crystallized Zr-based alloy substrates under moderate laser fluence exhibit slightly reduced uniformity compared with those generated on the MG surface (Fig. 2d), displaying pattern meandering, increased local curvature, and intermittent blurring along the ridges. These irregularities arise from the disruption of coherent interference conditions by grain boundaries and heterophase interfaces43. Consistent with this trend, LIPSS fabricated on fully crystalline Ti6Al4V alloy exhibit markedly degraded spatial regularity (Supplementary Fig. 6). Similarly, LIPSS uniformity deteriorates progressively with increasing surface roughness, and periodic structures are completely suppressed when the roughness reaches ≈210 nm (Supplementary Fig. 7). Collectively, these results establish that both microstructural homogeneity and surface smoothness are critical for achieving long-range coherent nanoscale ordering, as crystalline defects and topographic irregularities act as sources of decoherence that disrupt deterministic nano-patterning44.

Through comprehensive mapping of the processing parameter space, we derive a deterministic phase diagram that delineates the surface morphology evolution under nanosecond laser irradiation at a repetition frequency of 800 kHz (Fig. 3a). The diagram defines four distinct morphological regimes as functions of laser fluence and scanning speed: (I) a non-ablative, feature-free region at low laser fluence and high scanning speed; (II) a stable processing window that yields uniform LIPSS; (III) a transitional regime characterized by incomplete LIPSS with central blurring due to moderate melting; and (IV) an ablation‑dominated regime at high fluence, where periodic structures are fully replaced by continuous ablation tracks. Representative optical micrographs for regimes II–IV are shown in Fig. 3b–d, respectively. The transition from regime I to II is driven by increased energy density, which elevates the optical near-field to a level sustained coherently over successive pulses45,46. This sustained field mediates deterministic pattern growth by selectively amplifying favorable nucleation sites. Beyond a critical fluence threshold, however, thermal dissipation progressively outweighs coherent optical feedback and consequently disrupts the pattern evolution pathway.

To quantitatively correlate the morphological transitions with the underlying thermal dynamics, finite element simulations of surface temperature evolution were performed. In regime II (laser fluence = 105.6 mJ cm−2, scanning speed = 2 mm s−1), the peak surface temperature remains below the liquidus temperature (Fig. 3e), establishing a thermal environment in which stochastically nucleated seeds are progressively aligned through optical near‑field guidance into macroscopically coherent LIPSS. In regime III (laser fluence = 120.5 mJ cm−2, scanning speed = 2 mm s−1), the temperature slightly exceeds the liquidus temperature. Due to the Gaussian beam profile, localized melting occurs at the center of the irradiated region, disrupting the coherent near-field feedback required for long-range order and resulting in incomplete LIPSS. In regime IV (laser fluence = 164.1 mJ cm−2, scanning speed = 2 mm s−1), the surface temperature rises far above the melting threshold, triggering violent ablation that completely erases periodic structures and produces a well-defined ablation track (Fig. 3d). These simulations demonstrate that the morphological progression across regimes is governed by a dynamic competition between ordering mediated by optical interference and disordering driven by thermal diffusion.

Since thermal simulations confirm that the peak surface temperature of the MG remains below its liquidus temperature throughout LIPSS formation (Fig. 3e), pattern evolution is primarily governed by optical interference rather than melt hydrodynamics. To explore whether optical near‑field interactions might contribute to the observed pattern regularity, we performed finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations of the electric field distribution. Details of the implementation, including boundary conditions and simulation parameters, are provided in Supplementary Fig. 8. The simulations reveal that a single surface ridge generates a periodically modulated optical near-field distribution along the x-axis, establishing a self-consistent intensity pattern (Fig. 3f, g). This near-field modulation amplifies the underlying periodic intensity template originally generated by interference between the incident laser field and SPPs, thereby driving the development of well-defined LIPSS47. The angle between the stripe orientation and the laser scanning direction is defined as θ. Near-field enhancement selectively amplifies spontaneously formed nanoscale perturbations oriented perpendicular to the scanning direction (θ = 90°), which constructively interact with subsequent laser pulses to enable progressive energy accumulation and continuous extension along the scanning path (Supplementary Fig. 9a). In contrast, perturbations with non-perpendicular orientations (θ ≠ 90°) couple inefficiently to the incident field, fail to sustain inter-pulse energy buildup, and are rapidly suppressed during growth (Supplementary Fig. 9b). This spatially selective feedback mechanism also governs the morphological features at the trailing edge of the scanning line. In the central region of the trailing segment, sufficient laser fluence sustains coherent interference, enabling the development of well-ordered LIPSS. Toward the lateral edges, however, the fluence gradually decreases, resulting in weaker periodic modulation. Furthermore, the trailing edge receives fewer laser pulses than the central region, impeding the maturation of initial surface perturbations into fully developed ridges. Unlike an isolated ridge that induces periodic electric-field intensity modulation along the scanning direction (Fig. 3f, g), two parallel ridges of unequal length produce a near-field enhancement that decays radially (Fig. 3h), generating an arcuate boundary rather than a sharp linear termination. This predicted morphology closely aligns with experimental observations. Quantitative support is provided in Fig. 3i, which shows simulated electric-field intensity profiles along the y‑axis at different x‑positions. At x = 2.5 μm, pronounced intensity modulation sustains robust LIPSS formation, whereas at x = 3.5 μm, the near-field enhancement is significantly attenuated, directly accounting for the tapered LIPSS termination. Together, these results demonstrate that the arcuate morphology arises from the concurrent radial decay of incident fluence and near-field intensity toward the trailing edge of the scanning line.

Under randomly polarized, high-repetition-frequency nanosecond laser irradiation, the orientation of the resulting LIPSS is consistently perpendicular to the scanning direction (Figs. 2a–h and 3b). This behavior stands in stark contrast to conventional LIPSS, whether generated through oxidation or ablation48, whose orientation is predominantly dictated by the laser polarization axis. Uniform LIPSS emerge across diverse scanning trajectories: straight lines inclined at 0°, 30°, 70°, and 90° relative to the horizontal axis, as well as circular paths, demonstrating that local scanning geometry exerts dominant control over orientation (Supplementary Fig. 10). Even when controlled vibrations impose sinusoidal modulations on the LIPSS morphology (Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12), their orientation remains tightly locked perpendicular to the instantaneous scanning direction, underscoring the robustness of this geometry-guided orientation control. When the laser electric field oscillates perpendicular to the scanning direction, laser-SPP interference competes with near-field enhancement, yielding LIPSS oriented perpendicular to the polarization axis but exhibiting degraded uniformity (Supplementary Fig. 13a). In contrast, when the field oscillates parallel to the scanning direction, laser-SPP interference and near-field effects act synergistically: laser-SPP coupling establishes a periodic seed pattern, while near-field enhancement selectively reinforces perturbations oriented at θ = 90°, enabling coherent energy accumulation across successive pulses (Supplementary Fig. 13b). Together, these findings demonstrate that under randomly polarized, high-repetition-frequency nanosecond laser irradiation, LIPSS orientation is not governed by polarization alone. Instead, it arises from a dynamic interplay in which the scanning trajectory sets the symmetry axis for laser-SPP interference and near-field amplification, thereby steering deterministic, multi-pulse evolution toward long-range ordered nano-structures.

Leveraging the deterministic near-field feedback afforded by high-repetition-frequency nanosecond laser irradiation, we demonstrate that uniform 1D LIPSS can be programmatically reconfigured into ordered 2D LIPSS through simple repeated scanning, without any modification to polarization, fluence, or beam geometry. SEM images and corresponding three-dimensional (3D) topographies (Fig. 4a–l) reveal a well-defined morphological evolution as a function of scanning pass (SP) at a fixed fluence of 97.6 mJ cm−2. A single pass (SP = 1) produces uniform 1D LIPSS across the entire irradiated region (Fig. 4a, g). With increasing passes, LIPSS ridges broaden, and inter-ridge spacing narrows (Fig. 4b, h). Between 20 and 30 passes, the structure undergoes a complete reconfiguration into hexagonally close-packed nanodot arrays with pronounced long-range order (Fig. 4c, d). Beyond 40 passes, cumulative energy deposition progressively degrades this order. Structural degradation initiates at the beam center, where laser intensity is highest, and propagates radially outward, leaving a disordered central region while hexagonal packing remains intact at the periphery (Fig. 4e, k). Continued scanning further diminishes structural regularity, leading to increasingly fragmented and irregular surface features across the entire irradiated region (Fig. 4f, l). This transformation, illustrated schematically in Fig. 4m, arises from a dynamic interplay between laser-induced near-field modulation and cumulative thermal effects. The initial scanning establishes a periodic near-field distribution that templates uniform 1D LIPSS. In subsequent passes, the pre-existing topography reshapes the local electromagnetic landscape, redirecting energy deposition to drive the transformation of 1D LIPSS into 2D LIPSS. However, once the cumulative thermal load exceeds a local stability threshold, first at the beam center where the Gaussian intensity profile concentrates energy, the long-range order collapses49. This degradation reproduces the fluence-dependent instability. At elevated fluence (105.6 mJ cm−2), the same number of scanning passes induces earlier and more severe structural breakdown, highlighting an intensified competition between near-field mediated ordering and thermally induced disruption (Supplementary Fig. 14).

To uncover how nanoscale surface topography governs light–matter interactions to enable programmable pattern formation, we performed systematic FDTD simulations. The simulations show that an array of three parallel ridges produces a periodic near-field enhancement along the x-axis (Fig. 4n), consistent with the grating-like interference pattern expected under idealized conditions. In real specimens, however, LIPSS terminate in non-parallel trailing ends, a feature explicitly incorporated into the simulation geometry shown in Supplementary Fig. 15, which breaks translational symmetry and reshapes the near-field into a hexagonally symmetric distribution (Fig. 4o). Under repeated scanning with cumulative multi-pulse irradiation, this emergent hexagonal near-field landscape progressively channels energy deposition, driving the deterministic formation of long-range ordered nanodot arrays. This mechanism is broadly applicable: as shown in Supplementary Fig. 16, other geometric singularities, such as nanoindentation corners or microparticle edges, similarly reshape the local optical near-field under identical irradiation conditions, templating site-selective nanodot arrays precisely at their tips or vertices. Further confirmation comes from simulations with two mutually perpendicular auxiliary stripes, which directly generate a square-symmetric near-field pattern (Fig. 4p). In all cases, the simulated modulation period matches experimental observations and remains slightly shorter than the wavelength of the incident laser, fully consistent with a near-field mediated growth paradigm. These results demonstrate that pre-formed surface topography actively engineers the optical near-field: non-parallel LIPSS terminations function as intrinsic scattering centers that interfere with the incident beam, creating localized field enhancements that guide the inter-pulse reconfiguration of 1D LIPSS into coherent 2D nanodot arrays under high-repetition-frequency nanosecond laser irradiation. While these FDTD simulations provide a robust mechanistic framework for near‑field‑mediated patterning, they are limited to static electromagnetic responses and do not capture dynamic thermal evolution or cumulative pulse‑to‑pulse feedback. To address this limitation and directly probe the transient near‑field dynamics, future investigations should combine time‑resolved optical diagnostics with in situ imaging of the evolving field distribution during multi‑pulse irradiation. Such experiments would provide critical validation of the proposed mechanism and help establish a more complete physical picture of LIPSS formation under high‑repetition‑frequency nanosecond laser irradiation.

Composition-preserving nano-patterning

To determine whether LIPSS formation entails chemical or structural transformation, we performed a multiscale compositional and structural comparison between laser-patterned regions and the untreated MG. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping reveals a homogeneous distribution of all constituent elements (Zr, Ti, Cu, and Ni) across the periodic pattern, with no detectable enrichment or segregation at either ridges or grooves (Fig. 5a, b). Raman spectra acquired separately from the groove and ridge of the LIPSS confirm that both features retain a fully amorphous state indistinguishable from that of the untreated MG (Fig. 5c). Cross-sectional lamellae containing intact LIPSS were prepared by focused ion beam (FIB) milling, as detailed in Supplementary Fig. 17. Transmission electron microscopy-energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (TEM-EDS) analysis of the cross-section demonstrates that the LIPSS exhibit elemental distributions identical to those of the underlying substrate, with no measurable compositional gradients between surface nano-structures and bulk material (Fig. 5d–f). Selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns collected from beneath a ridge and a groove (spots 3 and 4 in Fig. 5d) both display diffuse halo rings, confirming the absence of crystallization and the preservation of the amorphous structure throughout the patterned region. Quantitative compositional analysis further verifies that both surface and cross-sectional compositions match the nominal MG composition (Supplementary Fig. 18a). TEM imaging reveals no subsurface damage or abrupt interfacial transitions (Fig. 5g, h). Atomic-scale images of regions i and j, corresponding to ridge and groove regions, and their FFT profiles (Fig. 5i, j) consistently exhibit a continuous, disordered amorphous network across the entire patterned region. Collectively, these findings establish that LIPSS formation is a purely physical process of topographic modulation, devoid of chemical segregation, phase transformation, or crystallization.

Scalable large-area nano-patterning

Building on the mechanistic insights above, we implement a programmable scanning strategy to fabricate large-area uniform nano-structures (Fig. 6a), either 1D gratings or 2D nanodot arrays, by tuning the spatial overlap rate (r) between adjacent scanning lines. This parameter governs both cumulative energy deposition and near-field coupling between incident pulses and pre-existing topography, which functions as built-in optical scatters. At low overlap (r = 10%), the Gaussian intensity profiles of successive pulses generate a smooth energy gradient with minima at scanning boundaries. Within the narrow inter-pulse overlapping regions, moderate fluence enables robust amplification of nascent LIPSS, yielding uniform 1D gratings with a consistent height of ≈40 nm (Fig. 6b). Increasing the overlap rate to 50% significantly expands the region where pre-formed LIPSS ridges are repeatedly interrogated by subsequent pulses. The non-parallel terminations of these ridges act as engineered scattering centers that reshape the optical near-field into a hexagonally symmetric interference landscape. This deterministic feedback drives the formation of long-range ordered 2D nanodot arrays (Fig. 6c), as confirmed by 2D-FFT analysis. The resulting nanodots exhibit excellent uniformity in size and shape, with a height of ≈70 nm, a diameter of 0.972 ± 0.001 μm, and a circularity of 0.91 ± 0.02 (Supplementary Fig. 19). This uniformity is further demonstrated by the large-area 2D LIPSS presented in Fig. 6e. Importantly, both the 1D gratings and 2D nanodot arrays are free of stitching artifacts across the patterned regions (Supplementary Fig. 20). In contrast, excessive overlap (r = 90%) leads to overaccumulation of thermal energy, overwhelming the ordering feedback and degrading the uniformity of the 2D nanodot arrays (Fig. 6d), consistent with the trend shown in Fig. 4c–f. Critically, all laser-patterned surfaces retain the pristine amorphous structure and elemental composition of the MG substrate, as verified by X-ray diffractometer (XRD) patterns (Supplementary Fig. 18b) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra (Supplementary Fig. 21). These results demonstrate that the nano-patterning process induces only topographical changes, preserving the intrinsic chemical and structural integrity of the MG substrate. Functionally, both 1D and 2D nano-structures enhance optical absorption across the UV‑to‑NIR range (200–2500 nm) compared to the untreated surface (Supplementary Fig. 22), owing to gradient‑index‑mediated light trapping50.

In summary, we present a strategy that exploits high-repetition-frequency nanosecond laser irradiation to generate uniform nano-structures on structurally and topographically homogeneous MG substrates, highlighting the potential of such pulses for precision nano-fabrication. The method leverages strong inter-pulse correlation to suppress thermal fluctuations while sustaining a coherent optical near-field, which deterministically amplifies stochastic surface perturbations into long-range ordered patterns. This yields 1D LIPSS with a period of ≈1042 nm, fully preserving the substrate’s amorphous structure and intrinsic chemical composition. Performing repeated scanning along the same single-line path transforms these 1D ripples into hexagonally packed 2D nanodot arrays. By rationally tailoring the spatial overlap between adjacent laser scanning lines to modulate cumulative energy deposition and optical near-field coupling with pre-existing topography, we achieve deterministic large-area patterning of either 1D LIPSS or hexagonally ordered 2D nanodot arrays with uniform morphology. Our work demonstrates the capability of nanosecond lasers for advanced nano-manufacturing and provides a chemically inert, single-step method for creating functional surface structures.

Methods

Specimen preparation

The substrate used in this study was a Zr‑based MG with nominal composition Zr41.2Ti13.8Cu12.5Ni10Be22.5 (at.%), selected for its structural homogeneity and lack of grain boundaries, which provides an ideal platform for probing uniform LIPSS formation. The as-cast amorphous plate was sectioned into rectangular specimens measuring 20 × 20 × 2 mm3 via diamond wire cutting. High-resolution TEM image confirmed the fully amorphous nature of the material, revealing no discernible lattice fringes (Supplementary Fig. 23a). Prior to laser nano-patterning, the MG specimens were sequentially ground with 400, 800, and 1500 grit metallographic sandpapers, followed by mechanical polishing using a water-soluble diamond polishing paste with a particle size of ≈0.5 µm. The specimens were then ultrasonically cleaned in anhydrous ethanol (purity ≥99.7%) at 50 °C for 30 min and air-dried. This preparation procedure yielded a smooth surface with an arithmetic average roughness (Ra) of 0.639 nm (Supplementary Fig. 23b), effectively minimizing stochastic topographic perturbations during subsequent nano-patterning. The complex refractive index of the MG substrate was determined by spectroscopic ellipsometry (ME-L, Wuhan Eoptics Technology Co., Ltd.), revealing a value of ñ = 3.347318 + 4.243869i at 1064 nm (Supplementary Fig. 24), corresponding to a complex dielectric permittivity of ε = − 6.7551 + 28.4080i.

Laser nano-patterning

All nano‑patterning experiments were performed using a custom laser fabrication system, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 1d. A fiber laser (SP‑050P‑A‑EP‑Z‑F‑Y, SPI Lasers) provided the irradiation source, operating at a wavelength of 1064 nm with a pulse duration of 7 ns (full width at 1/e2 of the peak intensity) and a repetition frequency of 800 kHz. Both the laser fluence and scanning speed were independently adjustable, enabling precise control of the energy delivered to the specimen. The beam was focused through a F‑theta lens with a focal length of 160 mm, yielding a spot with a measured 1/e2 radius of ≈43 μm. Experiments were carried out under a continuous argon flow (99.999% purity) to prevent atmospheric oxidation, efficiently remove processing debris, and inhibit the re-deposition of contaminants onto the specimen surface during laser processing. The laser output exhibited random polarization, ensuring that no polarization-dependent effects influenced the formation of surface structures. Beam positioning was controlled by a galvanometer scanner synchronized with computer-aided design software, enabling programmable and reproducible patterning across the specimen surface. A systematic parameter study was conducted to establish parameter-morphology relationships by varying the laser fluence, scanning speed, number of passes, scanning direction, and polarization orientation. The spatial overlap rate between successive laser scanning lines was introduced as a key control parameter to explore its fundamental role in achieving large-area morphological uniformity. All detailed processing parameters are compiled in Supplementary Table 1.

Measurement and characterization methods

The surface morphology, chemical composition, and optical response of laser-patterned specimens were comprehensively characterized. Large-area uniformity and topographic features were evaluated using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, JEM-7600F, JEOL) and laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM, OLS4100, Olympus). Quantitative nanoscale topographic profiling was performed using AFM (Dimension ICON, Bruker) equipped with an OLTESPA-R4 probe. This probe features a tetrahedral sharpened silicon tip with a nominal radius of 7 nm (maximum 10 nm), a spring constant of 2 N m−1, and a resonant frequency of 70 kHz, ensuring high-resolution tapping-mode imaging with minimal specimen deformation. High-resolution AFM micrographs were subsequently analyzed by 2D-FFT to quantitatively determine the spatial periodicity and orientation of the laser-induced nano-structures. LIPSS height was determined from cross-sectional line profiles extracted perpendicular to the LIPSS orientation. For each measurement, a cross-sectional profile spanning ten consecutive ridge-groove pairs was selected from the center of the irradiated region. The height of an individual LIPSS period was defined as the vertical distance between a ridge peak and its adjacent groove trough. The reported LIPSS height was taken as the arithmetic mean of these ten measurements, with the standard deviation indicating measurement uncertainty. Surface roughness was evaluated as the arithmetic average roughness (Ra) along linear traces oriented perpendicular to the LIPSS orientation. Three independent 10‑µm-long traces were randomly positioned across the center of the irradiated region. For each trace, the Ra value was calculated using the AFM system’s integrated NanoScope Analysis 1.5 software, in accordance with the ISO 4287 standard. The average roughness value was obtained from these three independent measurements, with the standard deviation reported as the error bar. Chemical composition and structural state were analyzed by EDS (X-Max80, Oxford Instruments), XRD (D8 Advance, Bruker), Raman spectroscopy (DXR3, Thermo Fisher), and XPS (ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher). For cross‑sectional analysis, FIB milling (S9000G, TESCAN) was used to prepare lamellae encompassing the LIPSS, which were subsequently imaged by TEM (JEM-2100F, JEOL). Optical performance was quantified by measuring normal-incidence reflectance from 200 to 2500 nm using a spectrophotometer (LAMBDA 1050, PerkinElmer) to determine the modifications induced by the nano-structures.

FDTD simulation

The electromagnetic interactions were simulated using the FDTD method embedded in Lumerical FDTD Solutions (ANSYS Lumerical 2020 R2) to elucidate the optical near-field feedback underlying LIPSS formation. The model comprised an ambient layer and a MG substrate, the latter containing an embedded stripe of identical chemical composition (Supplementary Fig. 8). This stripe was modeled as a rectangular prism with cross-sectional dimensions of 200 nm along the x-axis and 200 nm along the z-axis, extending 125 nm above the substrate surface. The simulation domain was defined as a 3D rectangular cuboid with a lateral extent of 10 × 10 μm2 in the x-y plane, where periodic boundary conditions were imposed to model an infinite periodic array. Perfectly matched layers (PMLs) were applied at the top and bottom boundaries to absorb scattered waves. Illumination was provided by a 1064 nm plane wave positioned 2 μm above the MG substrate. To replicate the experimental condition of random polarization, the total electromagnetic response was constructed via incoherent superposition of results from two independent simulations using orthogonally polarized sources (0° and 90°). The normalized electric field distribution (E/E0) at the MG‑air interface was recorded using a frequency‑domain monitor. A uniform mesh with a maximum step size of 20 nm in all directions was employed, and convergence was reached when the residual energy in the domain fell below 1.0 × 10−5 of the injected energy. To systematically examine how near-field feedback governs structural characteristics, a series of simulations were performed with systematic variations in stripe arrangement, including spacing, orientation, and number.
