PUS7 mRNA pseudouridylation is driven by RNA sequence, structure, and cell-type-specific features
Authors: Rebecca Rodell, Ronit Jain, Hossein Shenasa, Matias Montes, Nicolas Robalin, Stefan Prodic, Eduardo Eyras, Nicole M. Martinez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPUS7 is a major mRNA pseudouridine synthase that influences gene expression and is dysregulated in neurodevelopmental disorders and cancer. PUS7 recognizes a prevalent and degenerate UNUAR sequence, but the mechanisms underlying PUS7’s specificity remain unknown. We developed Nano-Mod-Amp, a targeted Nanopore high-throughput pseudouridine detection method, to interrogate PUS7 regulatory features. We established that USUAG, accessibility of the target uridine, and RNA structure are drivers of mRNA modification by PUS7. Perturbing structure through mutations or antisense oligos modulates pseudouridine levels. In cells, pseudouridines are responsive to PUS7 levels, demonstrating the regulatory potential of varying PUS7 levels across cell states. Conversely, PUS7 activity varies across cell types independently of expression levels, suggesting a potential regulatory role for RNA-binding proteins, RNA structure, or other cellular factors. We uncovered principles guiding PUS7 activity, enabling site-specific modulation of pseudouridines. These epitranscriptomic mechanisms provide molecular insight into the regulation and dysregulation of PUS7.

Introduction

RNA modifications are chemical alterations to the canonical nucleosides that regulate the RNA life cycle to impact fate and function.1 Transcriptome-wide RNA modification sequencing methods have mapped out about 10 distinct modifications in mRNAs.2,3,4,5,6,7,8 Pseudouridine (Ψ), an isomer of uridine, is among the most abundant mRNA modifications.5,6,7 Thirteen pseudouridine synthases (PUSs) catalyze the isomerization of uridine to pseudouridine in humans. Over half of the pseudouridine synthases have been implicated in human disease, underscoring their importance to human health.1

Pseudouridines in mRNA can tune gene expression through impacts on splicing, translation, and mRNA decay.6,9,10,11,12,13,14,15,16 Specifically, pseudouridines added to pre-mRNAs directly impact splicing in nuclear extracts, and PUS7 depletion leads to alternative splicing.9,10 TRUB1-deposited pseudouridines increase mRNA stability.12 Pseudouridylation of disease-causing premature termination codons (PTCs) and stop codons promotes readthrough.13,14,15,16,17 Stress-induced pseudouridylation by PUS7 also impacts protein synthesis in the absence of changes in tRNA pseudouridylation.18

Pseudouridine-modified nucleotides revolutionized mRNA therapeutics by preventing inflammatory responses to foreign RNAs while boosting mRNA stability and translation.16,19,20,21 Engineered small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs) have been used to introduce pseudouridines site specifically to correct disease-causing PTCs through promoting pseudouridine-mediated stop codon readthrough.13,14,15,16 These findings underscore the promise of pseudouridylation for therapeutic applications and highlight the need to determine how pseudouridine synthases achieve target specificity for uncovering new modalities for programmable pseudouridylation.

PUS7 is among the PUSs with the most tRNA and mRNA targets.5,6,10,12,22,23,24 PUS7 modifies pre-mRNAs and impacts pre-mRNA processing, mRNA levels, and translation.6,10,18 Predicted loss-of-function and missense mutations in the catalytic domain of PUS7 lead to neurodevelopmental disorders and have been linked to age-related macular degeneration,25,26,27,28,29,30,31 while aberrant overexpression (OE) of PUS7 promotes glioblastoma tumorigenesis in a manner dependent on PUS7’s catalytic activity.23 The underlying molecular mechanisms leading to PUS7 dysregulation and their link to disease outcomes remain to be determined. PUS7-mediated mRNA pseudouridylation is also altered by changing cellular conditions such as heat shock, metal ion stress, and lead exposure.6,18,32 These observations indicate that PUS7 activity is critical for gene expression during development, is dysregulated in disease, and may be regulated in diverse cellular contexts, yet the features, conditions, and mechanisms governing human PUS7 activity remain poorly understood.

PUS7 mRNA targets are enriched for a UNUAR sequence motif, but only <5% of such motifs are pseudouridylated transcriptome-wide,10,33 suggesting additional determinants of specificity. The relative importance of each nucleotide to PUS7 catalytic activity has not been systematically interrogated, and additional features such as RNA structure and RNA-binding proteins likely influence PUS7 activity but remain uninvestigated. Studies of individual PUS7 targets reached mixed conclusions, with RNA structure reported as important for some tRNAs and non-coding RNAs but less critical for an mRNA modified by yeast Pus7.34,35,36 Because PUS7 modifies RNAs adopting distinct 2D and 3D structures,35 a unifying RNA structural motif and its impact on PUS7-dependent pseudouridine deposition remained to be elucidated.

Recently, sequencing methods using orthogonal detection modalities advanced transcriptome-wide study of pseudouridine and its stoichiometry (the fraction of molecules modified at a given position).5,6,7,12,22,37,38,39 However, these tools require high sequencing depth for single-nucleotide quantification and robust statistics, and their cost limit the number of biological conditions examined simultaneously. Existing targeted approaches are low throughput40 and lack the single-molecule resolution of long-read technologies such as Nanopore sequencing.

Here, we developed and applied orthogonal Nanopore sequencing tools for pseudouridine quantification, including Nanopore modification detection by amplicon sequencing (Nano-Mod-Amp), to uncover PUS7 regulatory features. We comprehensively characterize the mRNA targets of human PUS7 endogenously and with massively parallel reporter assays (MPRAs) in vitro and in cellulo. We identified mRNA pseudouridines responsive to changes in PUS7 abundance, indicating sites that are poised for regulation by enzyme concentration. We established UGUAG and UCUAG as the optimal PUS7 consensus motifs. However, additional layers of regulatory features influence PUS7 activity beyond sequence and enzyme levels. Despite marked structural heterogeneity among the probed 2D structures of human PUS7 mRNA targets, we discovered a unifying RNA structural signature and target-uridine accessibility as key determinants of modification. These determinants allowed us to design antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) that abrogate pseudouridylation through disruption of RNA structure, which demonstrates a modality for site-specific modulation of RNA modifications. Finally, we identified a subset of pseudouridines that are cell-type specific and that may be modulated by RNA-binding proteins and/or RNA structural remodeling, underscoring the influence of the cellular environment on pseudouridylation. Our framework and approaches are broadly applicable to study the regulation and function of other RNA modifications and RNA-modifying enzymes.

To benchmark pseudouridine detection and derive absolute stoichiometry, we generated synthetic concatemer standards containing all eight UNUAR motif variants modified by PUS7 (Figure S1A). Pseudouridines presented as U-to-C mismatches, demonstrating the suitability of this approach for identifying PUS7 targets. U-to-C mismatches are sequence context dependent, requiring motif-specific calibration curves to accurately estimate stoichiometry (Figures S1B–S1D)).

To identify PUS7-dependent mRNA pseudouridines, we sequenced total RNA from wild-type (WT) and PUS7 knockdown (KD) HepG2 cells. PUS7 protein is robustly depleted by ∼90% in PUS7 knockdown cells (Figures 1B and S1E). The standard Nanopore direct RNA sequencing protocol selectively captures mRNA.10,39 To assign PUS7-dependent pseudouridines, we required a U-to-C mismatch difference ≥15% between PUS7 WT and KD samples and a per-site read number ≥10, with reproducibility in two out of three biological replicates (Figure 1A). Because replicate coverage varied (∼2–6 million total reads per sample, as expected for direct RNA flow cells), low coverage in some replicates limited detection of PUS7-dependent pseudouridines. Thus, we also included sites observed in one replicate and also reported in other transcriptome-wide datasets.10,12,22

Our pipeline identified 101 candidate PUS7 pseudouridine mRNA sites with de novo motif enrichment analysis identifying UGUAG as the top enriched motif, consistent with PUS7 modifying in the context of UNUAR motifs as shown in previous studies (Figure 1E).42 We found that UGUAG, followed by UCUAG to a lesser extent, are enriched over other UNUAR motifs transcriptome-wide (Figures 1E and 1F; Table S1A). We focused on the 50 mRNA pseudouridines within the UNUAR that are dependent on PUS7 include RHBDD2, HDAC6, IGF2BP1, and STIM1 (Figures 1C and 1D; Table S1A). PUS7-sensitive positions outside the UNUAR motif could represent pseudouridines in an imperfect consensus context or RNA modifications indirectly impacted by PUS7 knockdown. We compared the U-to-C mismatch approach to Nanopore’s Dorado basecaller pseudouridine model. We tuned the parameters to optimize detection of pseudouridines in UNUAR motifs (Figures S1F and S1G); this approach identified 42 candidate pseudouridine sites (Table S1B), 17 of which overlapped with sites identified by U-to-C mismatch in UNUAR motifs.

A PUS7 knockout (KO) cell line validated sites identified by the knockdown approach, with comparable pseudouridine reduction (Figures 1D–1G; Figures S1H, S1I, and S1J; Table S1). Because residual levels of PUS7 in KD cells may still support pseudouridylation, we searched the direct RNA data for UNUAR sites with a U-to-C mismatch >15% in WT that did not decline by our threshold in PUS7 KD (−15%), which could be PUS7 dependent but missed by the KD. Analysis of 67 candidate sites detected in WT but unchanged in the KD revealed only nine additional KO-sensitive sites, five which were just marginally below the threshold in the KD (Figure S1K). The former sites that are also unchanged in the KO could represent pseudouridines installed by other PUSs or represent instances of redundancy with another PUS. 68% of our direct RNA PUS7-dependent pseudouridines were also reported in studies that genetically assigned PUS7 targets in poly(A)+ mRNA by bisulfite-induced deletion sequencing (Figure S1J), while 32% are newly identified.

We next asked whether identified PUS7-dependent pseudouridines in mRNAs were enriched in specific regions. Their distribution is skewed toward 3′ UTRs, similar to the background set of all detected uridines and consistent with the 3′ coverage of Nanopore sequencing in the 3′ to 5′ direction, indicating that PUS7 modifies across transcript regions as shown in previous studies (Figure S1L).9

Using our UNUAR calibration curves, we derived absolute stoichiometry for PUS7 mRNA targets, which ranged from 8% to 100% (median 31%) demonstrating that many are highly modified (Figures 1D–1G; Figure S1J; Tables S1A and S2). In summary, adding a genetic control and synthetic standards to the Nanopore direct RNA sequencing workflow enables quantification of pseudouridine stoichiometry, assigned to a specific PUS, a generalizable approach for comparing modification stoichiometry across sequence contexts and relating it to downstream functions.

Nano-BID-Amp orthogonally validates endogenous modification and PUS7 dependency

Although Nanopore direct RNA sequencing enables de novo discovery of RNA modifications transcriptome-wide, coverage limitations and method-specific biases make orthogonal validation essential in epitranscriptomic research. We developed Nano-Mod-Amp as a versatile, high-throughput approach to validate candidate sites and study their regulation across conditions.

Nano-Mod-Amp is a modular targeted Nanopore approach that detects RNA modifications as mutations introduced during reverse transcription of RNA following chemical labeling. This method achieves high coverage, thousands of reads per targets versus tens to hundreds for transcriptome-wide approaches, in medium to high throughput enabling robust statistical analysis. Library preparation uses unique molecular identifier (UMI)-containing template-specific primers compatible with Nanopore cDNA-PCR sequencing for quick ∼2-day turnaround (Figure 2A). We applied Nano-Mod-Amp to orthogonally assess candidate PUS7-dependent pseudouridine sites that we identified through Nanopore direct RNA sequencing. Bisulfite treatment of RNA followed by desulfonation leads to bisulfite-induced deletions (BIDs) at pseudouridines during reverse transcription12,22 that we read out with Nano-BID-Amp. In contrast to Nanopore direct RNA sequencing, BID treatment leads to more uniform deletion fractions across sequence contexts and does not require a synthetic standard calibration curve to compare across sequence contexts.12 Our targeted approach results in pseudouridine-dependent deletion fractions comparable to those obtained by Illumina sequencing (Figure S2A) while maintaining the long-read advantage of Nanopore sequencing despite BID-induced RNA fragmentation (Figures S2B and S2C).

We validated PUS7 dependence for five randomly selected endogenous amplicons (300–880 nt), HDAC6, IGF2BP1, RHBDD2, UBFD1, and STIM1 (Figure 2B; Table S3) using Nano-BID-Amp on poly(A)-selected mRNA from WT or PUS7 KD HepG2 (Figure 1B). Deletion fractions decreased significantly with PUS7 KD (Figure 2B) and correlated highly with those obtained in published transcriptome-wide studies (Figure S2D). Altogether, Nano-BID-Amp is a simple, robust method for targeted quantification of pseudouridines across conditions, validating sites identified by Nanopore direct RNA sequencing or other transcriptome-wide approaches.

To measure modification in vitro, we performed in vitro transcription and pseudouridylation with recombinant PUS710,44,45 (Figure S3A), followed by Nano-BID-Amp. Using thresholds (>20 reads, >0.05 Δ deletion fraction, and p < 0.05) that optimize for conformance to the UNUAR motif and number of called sites (Figure S3B), we detected 253 pseudouridines in vitro (Figure 3B; Table S4A), recapitulating 86% of PUS7-dependent pseudouridines in UNUAR motifs that we identified by direct RNA sequencing with U-to-C mismatches and 85% of those called by the Dorado pseudouridine basecaller (Figures 1B–1D; Figure S3C). HDAC6, RHBDD2, and STIM1 have representative pseudouridines recapitulated in vitro with recombinant PUS7 (Figure 3C).

To measure pseudouridylation in cellulo, we transiently expressed the cloned MPRA into HepG2 and HEK293T cells (Figure 3D). Pseudouridine levels in the MPRA context were comparable to endogenous loci (Figures 2B and 3E), demonstrating that 130 nucleotides of flanking sequence can suffice to recapitulate modification by PUS7 in vitro and in cellulo. To determine how PUS7 concentration impacts pseudouridylation, we transfected the MPRA into WT, PUS7 KD, and PUS7-V5 OE cells and performed Nano-BID-Amp (Figures 3D–3F). To establish PUS7 dependence, we looked for a statistically significant difference in pseudouridine levels between WT and KD or OE when HEK293T and HepG2 data were analyzed together or separately. We detected 184 PUS7-dependent pseudouridines in at least one condition (Figure 3D; Figure S3D; Tables S4B and S4C). The in cellulo MPRA recapitulated 74% of PUS7-dependent pseudouridine that we identified by Nanopore direct RNA sequencing (Figures 1B–1D). The majority of pseudouridines are modulated by PUS7 KD and PUS7 OE (Figures 3D and 3E). In contrast, some sites reached apparent modification saturation at WT PUS7 levels (Figure S3E), while others were undetectable in WT but modified upon OE (Figure S3F). Collectively, the in vitro and in cellulo MPRAs recapitulate PUS7 mRNA targets identified by Nanopore direct RNA sequencing and demonstrate that tuning PUS7 concentration can regulate pseudouridylation across biological conditions.

MPRAs reveal differences between in vitro and in cellulo PUS7 sensitivity

While in vitro pseudouridylation is dictated by sequence and enzyme levels alone, the cellular environment introduces trans-acting factors and spatiotemporal regulation. Comparing in vitro and in cellulo modification revealed 141 sites modified in both contexts, 112 modified to detectable levels only in vitro, and 43 modified only in cellulo (Figure 3G). These results suggest that cellular factors such as RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), dynamic RNA structures, co-transcriptional recruitment of PUS7, and/or compartmentalization shape the PUS7-dependent pseudouridine landscape in cells. The lack of these variables in vitro and excess enzyme likely enables broader modification of suboptimal sites (Figures 3H and 3I). In contrast, the cellular environment is important for a subset of PUS7 sites that cannot be efficiently pseudouridylated even with excess enzyme in vitro (Figures 3G and 3H).

PUS7 mRNA targets share sequence and structural features despite heterogeneous 2D structures

Sequence motifs drive the binding46,47,48 and catalysis of many RBPs and RNA-modifying enzymes.49 PUS7-dependent pseudouridines are found in the context of a UNUAR sequence motif,10,33 which we recapitulate in our transcriptomic direct RNA data, in vitro MPRA, and in cellulo MPRA (Figure 1; Figure 4A). However, less than 1% of UNUAR motifs in our direct RNA data are pseudouridylated (Figure 1). In the MPRAs, almost no sites are modified outside this context, yet not every UNUAR motif is modified (Tables S4A and S4B). Even in vitro, where only RNA and recombinant PUS7 are present, UNUAR is insufficient for robust modification in all cases, leaving RNA structure as the only feature that could influence modification. Distinct RNA structural motifs are necessary for pseudouridylation of mRNAs by yeast Pus133 and human TRUB1,43 which led us to hypothesize that both RNA sequence and structure influence PUS7-mediated pseudouridylation. We evaluated the enrichment of UGUAG and UCUAG in our in cellulo MPRAs over the background distribution. UGUAG is significantly enriched over other UNUAR motifs (Figure 4B), whereas UCUAG is not significant (Figure 4B), consistent with the smaller transcriptome-wide enrichment of UCUAG. In vitro, both UGUAG and UCUAG are preferentially modified, but UGUAG is less preferentially modified over UCUAG than in cellulo (Figure 1F), suggesting that the cellular context contributes to selection of UGUAG.

To identify shared RNA structural features among PUS7 mRNA targets, we performed in vitro RNA structure probing with the selective 2′-hydroxyl acylation analyzed by primer extension (SHAPE)50 reagent 2-methylnicotinic acid imidazolide (NAI)51 using our Nano-Mod-Amp workflow. SHAPE reagents react with the 2′ OH of flexible or unpaired RNA nucleotides forming adducts at which reverse transcriptase misincorporates during cDNA synthesis (SHAPE-MaP).52 The resulting mutations are read out by sequencing and subsequently converted to reactivities for experimentally informed structure prediction.53 Leveraging the modular Nano-Mod-Amp workflow (Figure 4C), we replaced BID with SHAPE treatment, (Nano-SHAPE-Amp) keeping the same library prep. Nano-SHAPE-Amp reactivities were highly concordant with Illumina sequencing of the same libraries (Pearson’s R = 0.969; Figure S4A) and produced equivalent structure predictions (Figure 4D; Figures S4A–S4C), demonstrating suitability for long-read, high-throughput, targeted RNA structure probing.

Nano-SHAPE-Amp reactivity data from our MPRA enables experimentally informed predictions of the 2D RNA structures present among PUS7 mRNA targets50 (Figure 4C; Table S5). SHAPE-informed folding of PUS7 mRNA targets using RNAfold54 mirrored purely in silico predictions (Figures 4E–4G; Figures S4D–S4F; Tables S6A and S6B). PUS7 targets have significantly lower free energy of folding than non-targets, indicating they are more structured (Figure 4E). Visual analysis of the SHAPE-informed 2D maximum expected accuracy (MEA) structures adopted by PUS7 mRNA targets revealed structural heterogeneity (Figure 4F).

To identify unifying features among PUS7 mRNA targets, we analyzed average pairing probabilities relative to a randomly sampled background uridines (Figure 4G). A paired-unpaired-paired-unpaired-paired “Pupup” signature emerged across direct RNA and MPRA PUS7-dependent sites, with the pseudouridine in the first unpaired region (Figures S4G and S4H). This signature is unique to PUS7 targets as it is absent among unmodified UNUAR sequences (Figure S4E) and strengthens with increased stringency of PUS7-dependence thresholds (Figure S3B). In silico folding predicted only downstream Pupup features (Figure S4E; Table S6), while experimental SHAPE probing (in vitro and in cellulo) captured the complete signature (Figure 4G; Figure S4F; Tables S5 and S7), demonstrating the importance of experimental structure determination.

Individual PUS7 target MEA structures showed full and partial matches: RHBDD2, FIS1, and IGF2BP1 each at least partially conform to the Pupup region (−10 to −3 and +3 to 15; Figure 4F). Our analysis confirmed the UNUAR sequence motif enrichment and identified a unifying RNA structure signature among otherwise heterogeneous 2D structures. The Pupup signature potentially explains why PUS7 does not modify all UNUAR sites, as RNA structure is associated with pseudouridylation.

The UGUAG motif and Pupup signature are associated with higher PUS7-dependent pseudouridine stoichiometry

To further dissect the features associated with modification by PUS7, we asked how sequence and structure varied with pseudouridine levels. To do this, we binned in vitro and in cellulo MPRA PUS7-dependent sites into four quartiles (Q1 least to Q4 most modified; Figure 4H). Lowly modified Q1 sites showed a UNUAG motif, indicating flexibility is most tolerated at the −1 position, albeit at the expense of lower modification levels. We observed a strong preference for the UGUAG sequence motif over UNUAR in highly modified Q4 compared to Q1 sites in cellulo, while, in vitro, both UGUAG and UCUAG are overrepresented among highly modified sites in Q4, supporting a direct association between these sequence motifs and higher relative stoichiometry (Figure 4I).

We observed differences in the pairing probabilities across quartiles, with Q4 sites more strongly conforming to the Pupup signature (Figure S4I). Strikingly, we also noticed a statistically significant decrease in the pairing probability of the target uridine residue between Q1 and Q4 (Figure 4J), suggesting higher possibility of modification when the target U is more accessible to PUS7. In summary, higher PUS7-dependent pseudouridylation is associated with an unpaired pseudouridine in a UGUAG sequence context and an RNA structural context that conforms to the Pupup signature.

RNA sequence and structure determine extent of modification by PUS7

To test the importance of sequence and structural features associated with pseudouridylation by PUS7, we performed mutagenesis with a second MPRA using in vitro pseudouridylation and Nano-BID-Amp. We tested the influence of varying the UNUAR motif, accessibility surrounding the target uridine, and the RNA structure associated with the Pupup signature.

To test the impact of sequence, we replaced the 5-mer centered on pseudouridine with every version of the UNUAR motif in 27 PUS7 target sites. We observe the highest modification levels in UGUAG and UCUAG motifs (Figure 5A). This is consistent with the enrichment of these motifs in cells and with the diminished preference for UGUAG over UCUAG in vitro in our MPRA of natural PUS7 mRNA target sequences. We also find that the effect of the UNUAR sequence is influenced by flanking mRNA sequence context (Figure S5A). Our mutagenesis revealed instances where UCUAG was more modified than UGUAG. These cases can be explained by a higher pairing probability for UGUAG than UCUAG across the motif (Figures S5A and S5B) in that sequence context, indicating UCUAG is more modified when it is more accessible than UGUAG in the same sequence context. To test the importance of accessibility, we systematically modulated the pairing status of the target uridine, the target uridine and the +1 position, and the UNUAR 5mer in a subset of PUS7 targets (Figure 5B; Figures S5C and S5D). We increased accessibility through mutating the pairing partners of these nucleotides to disrupt the pairing (break pair) and, conversely, decreased the accessibility by mutating the opposing strand to pair the target positions (make pair). We confirmed predicted pairing status by in silico folding with RNAfold, advancing only targets with predicted base pairing changes and minimal disruption elsewhere (Table S8). These mutations demonstrate that increased accessibility throughout the UNUAR sequence motif increases modification by PUS7 (Figure 5B; Figures S5C and S5D; Table S9A).

To evaluate the necessity of the Pupup (Figure 4G), we designed mutations to disrupt all base pairs within the Pupup region (−10 to +15), excluding the UNUAR 5mer motif and pairing partners. Since structure-disrupting mutations alter sequence, we designed compensatory mutations that restore the parental structure to directly test structural requirements. Using RNAfold, 10 out of 10 attempted mutations with sufficient data disrupted predicted structure and nine decreased pseudouridine levels. Five of the compensatory mutations largely restored the parental structure (Table S9B). For three PUS7 targets (RHBDD2, IGF2BP1, and PPP1R12B) with compensatory mutations highly similar to the parental structures, structure disruption decreased modification while compensation restored modification (Figure 5C; Table S9B). Chemical probing is needed to assess compensation for the two targets that did not restore pseudouridylation. We also tested the importance of downstream unpaired (+6, +7, and +8 positions), which constitute a statistically significant region of the Pupup, which we termed “unpaired2.” Mutations that pair this region in targets reduce modification, while mutations that break pairing in this region increase modification (Figure 5D; Table S9A).

To test the impact of RNA structure on modification without altering sequence, we designed ASOs to disrupt the upstream and downstream pairing regions of the PUS7 target FIS1. Modification by PUS7 is significantly reduced when this mRNA target is treated with structure-disrupting ASOs or an ASO that directly blocks the target uridine in vitro (Figure 5E). Conversely, control ASOs that target distal regions outside of the Pupup structural motif have no impact on pseudouridylation (Figure 5E), indicating that the surrounding local structure and accessibility of the target uridine are important for modification. Importantly, these ASOs do not impact the modification levels of non-targets, showing specificity for the intended target (Figure S5E). Together, these experiments demonstrate the importance of USUAG, accessibility of the target uridine, and RNA structure surrounding the modified position (Pupup signature) for robust modification by PUS7.

PUS7-dependent pseudouridines are differentially regulated by cell type

Pseudouridine profiles are altered in response to stress6,18,39 and vary across mouse tissues,12 suggesting condition-specific regulation. The in cellulo MPRA enabled us to interrogate PUS7-dependent pseudouridine for differential modification between human cell lines of different tissue origins: HepG2 (liver) and HEK293T (kidney). Many PUS7-dependent pseudouridines showed no cell-type specificity (Figure 6A). Our analysis revealed 68 PUS7 target sites with higher modification in HEK293T than HepG2 (Figures 6A–6C; Figures S6A and S6B; Table S10). Normalized read counts for these RNAs were highly correlated between the cell lines (Figure S6C) and uncorrelated with deletion fractions (Figure S6D), indicating that cell-type-specific differences in pseudouridylation are not driven by differences in RNA expression or coverage. We validated the differential PUS7-dependent pseudouridylation by cell type using endogenous Nano-BID-Amp. While both RHBDD2 and HDAC6 are PUS7 dependent (Figure 2B), HDAC6 is more highly modified in HEK293T compared to HepG2 cells while RHBDD2 is not (Figure 6D). We next investigated potential mechanisms behind the cell-type specificity of this subset of PUS7-dependent pseudouridines. RBP levels, RBP localization, RNA decay and export rates, and RNA structure, among other factors, vary with cell type55,56,57,58,59 and could tune pseudouridine levels. First, we demonstrated that PUS7 proteins levels are equivalent between cell types (Figure 6E). Recent work found cytoplasmic localization of PUS7 results in increased pseudouridylation.18 By immunofluorescence, PUS7 was primarily nuclear in both cell types (Figure S6E), although quantification of the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio indicated it is slightly more nuclear in HEK293T (Figure S6F). This difference is unlikely to explain the cell-type specificity, as the localization model would predict lower—not the observed higher—pseudouridylation in HEK293T, although localization effects cannot be entirely ruled out. Analysis of the identified cell-type-specific sequences revealed a similar strength of the UNUAR sequence motif and similar intrinsic folding propensity between cell-type-specific and constitutive PUS7 mRNA targets (Figure 6F). We then asked whether trans-acting RBPs might recruit PUS7 or compete with PUS7 to access mRNA target sites. Using STREME,42 we uncovered a CUG motif, which is a recognition motif for multiple RBPs, enriched in 38 PUS7-dependent cell-line-specific sequences over PUS7-dependent sequences lacking cell-line specificity (Figure 6G). These results suggest that RBP-mediated recruitment of or competition with PUS7 could represent a potential mechanism for cell-type specificity.

Discussion

Prior work identified PUS7 as a major pseudouridine synthase acting on mRNA, yet the full repertoire of PUS7 mRNA targets remains to be determined. Furthermore, why certain mRNAs sites are modified by PUS7 while others are not remained an open question in the field. Obtaining answers to these questions has been limited by the lack of quantitative tools to measure pseudouridine fraction in a site-specific manner across many experimental conditions. We used two complementary pseudouridine quantification methods, direct RNA sequencing and Nano-Mod-Amp, to comprehensively identify features associated with basal and condition-specific mRNA pseudouridylation by PUS7.

Direct RNA sequencing identifies PUS7 mRNA targets and pseudouridine stoichiometry

We implemented a Nanopore direct RNA sequencing pipeline that identifies PUS7-dependent mRNA pseudouridines transcriptome-wide38,39,60 from differential U-to-C mismatches between enzyme-depleted and control samples, with high validation rates by Nano-Mod-Amp as an orthogonal approach. A synthetic standard containing all eight PUS7 UNUAR motifs benchmarked U-to-C mismatch rates, enabling estimation of pseudouridine stoichiometries from the sequencing data. U-to-C mismatch and Nanopore’s Dorado basecaller are complementary, each identifying unique and shared sites. Our framework is a powerful approach to discover and determine the stoichiometry of PUS7 mRNA targets de novo with quantitative resolution. These data and standards will be a valuable resource for training PUS7 centric pseudouridine basecallers that estimate stoichiometry from raw signal.

Nano-Mod-Amp as a tool for studies of the epistructurome

Nano-Mod-Amp complements direct RNA sequencing by serving as an orthogonal validation approach for pseudouridines at known or predicted locations with high coverage across many experimental conditions. Nanopore sequencing libraries can be performed in house or sent out to a commercial vendor. We illustrate the modularity of the Nano-Mod-Amp approach by using two chemical probes that report on pseudouridine (bisulfite) and RNA structure (SHAPE reagents) with comparable accuracies to Illumina sequencing.

In principle, the Nano-Mod-Amp approach can be applied to any modification, natural or artificial, that produces mutational signatures during cDNA synthesis. For example, Nano-Mod-Amp could be combined with solvent accessibility probes that report on RBP binding61 to quantify protein-agnostic changes in RBP binding across conditions. Our complementary computational pipeline quantifies diverse mutational profiles in Nanopore sequencing data to report on statistically significant RNA features of interest. These approaches will enable elucidating the mechanisms of condition-specific regulation for various modified nucleotides and their relationship to RNA structures.

Sequence and structural features drive modification by human PUS7

The UNUAR motif enriched among PUS7 targets is insufficient to confer specificity in cells or in vitro (Tables S4 and S5), and prior studies of individual RNAs reached mixed conclusions about the role of RNA secondary structure in PUS7-mediated pseudouridylation.34,35,36

Our systems-level approach identified key features for human PUS7 mRNA pseudouridylation: (1) a UGUAG or UCUAG sequence; (2) an unpaired, accessible target uridine; and (3) surrounding RNA structure (Pupup). In cells, we find a strong selection for UGUAG, then UCUAG, over other UNUAR motifs, a preference not observed in vitro. Systematic mutations in identical mRNA backbones revealed cases where UCUAG outperformed UGUAG, which can be explained by the UGUAG being more paired or inaccessible in that backbone, indicating the motif can also indirectly affect pseudouridylation through RNA structure. Our data are consistent with the possibility that surrounding structure (Pupup) maintains accessibility of the target uridine. This is supported by mutational analyses in which pairing the target uridine of highly modified sequences consistently decreases modification (Figure 5B). However, unpairing lowly modified target uridines is insufficient to universally confer high modification levels. Furthermore, we find that mutations to the Pupup that are not predicted to influence target-uridine accessibility abrogate modifications (Figure 5C; Table S6D). Structure-disrupting and compensatory mutations thus support a broader role for RNA structural elements beyond accessibility alone. Future work will be needed to delineate the precise structural features that drive binding and/or catalysis.

The Pupup structural signature constitutes a unifying structural feature among the otherwise heterogeneous 2D structures adopted by PUS7 mRNA targets. In contrast, yeast Pus1 and human TRUB1 target mRNAs share well defined and homogeneous consensus structures, where pseudouridine is at the base of a stem or in a loop, respectively. While, for human PUS7, accessibility of the surrounding the target uridine significantly influences extent of modification, pairing at the target uridine does not significantly influence modification efficiency by yeast Pus1.33 The distinct RNA structural preferences among studied PUSs underscore the importance of characterizing the features that guide modification by each of the 13 human PUS enzymes.

Antisense oligos modulate pseudouridylation

Studying the functions of individual pseudouridines requires innovative tools to modulate pseudouridine levels without genetic perturbation. ASOs are emerging as powerful modulators of mRNA processing by targeting splice sites,62,63 RBP binding sites,64,65 and RNA structure66,67 but have not been used to tune RNA modification levels. Our discovery of structural features determining modification by PUS7 enabled design of ASOs that perturb local structure or accessibility of the target uridine to alter pseudouridylation. ASOs may thus represent a powerful modality to target individual pseudouridines for functional studies or therapeutic applications. Our characterization of sequence and structural features will improve PUS7 target prediction and enable dissecting the regulation and function of individual targets in developmental and disease.9

Condition-specific regulation of PUS7 activity in cells

Whether changes in PUS7 concentration are sufficient to drive changes in pseudouridylation had not been addressed. By varying PUS7 levels, we found many pseudouridines are titratable with changes in PUS7 protein level and thus amenable to regulation by differences in PUS7 concentration observed across cell types (Figure S6G) and in disease contexts such as cancer23 (Figure S6H). However, some sites saturate at basal levels while others required OE for detectable modification, suggesting that new basally unmodified sites could be gained by disease-related OE.

Regulation of PUS7 activity is also possible without differences in PUS7 protein levels or substantial differences in localization. Only a subset of targets show higher pseudouridylation in HEK293T than HepG2 cells, suggesting that features such as RBPs and/or context-specific RNA structure underlie cell-type-specific regulation. We hypothesize that differential pseudouridylation results from cell-type-specific RBPs that compete with or recruit PUS7. Supporting this, we found enrichment of CUGs upstream of PUS7-dependent pseudouridines, which is a motif bound by members of the CELF and MBNL family of RBPs that function in post-transcriptional gene regulation.46,48,63,68,69 RBP-mediated regulation of pseudouridine could be reminiscent of splicing regulation through RBP competition or recruitment of an RBP by another RBP bound to an adjacent motif,70,71,72 with different RBPs influencing PUS7-dependent sites based on surrounding sequence and structure. RNA structural remodeling across cell states73 and cell types55 could also contribute to cell-type-specific pseudouridines. Our in vitro versus in cellulo analysis further suggests the cellular environment enforces a stronger UGUAG-over-UCUAG preference, perhaps by sterically blocking other UNUAR motifs or altering structure. Alternative models for cell-type-specific regulation of PUS7 are certainly possible. Future work is needed to investigate whether RBPs and/or RNA structural remodeling between cell types impact PUS7 activity.

Using complementary Nanopore methods, we comprehensively characterized human PUS7-dependent pseudouridines and regulatory features. PUS7 targets fold into heterogeneous secondary structures but share key determinants of modification: an accessible target uridine and a Pupup structural signature. We also uncovered cell-type-specific PUS7-dependent pseudouridines that may be due to trans-acting factors and/or RNA structural remodeling or other aspects of the cellular environment. Nano-Mod-Amp is broadly applicable to study epitranscriptomic regulation and its relationship with RNA secondary structure. These PUS7 mRNA targets could represent new therapeutic targets, while our identified regulatory features pave the way for new modalities to control PUS7-dependent pseudouridylation and uncovering disease mechanisms.

Limitations of the study

We rigorously identified PUS7-dependent pseudouridines through direct RNA sequencing with synthetic standards, genetic assignment, and validation with Nano-Mod-Amp and reporter assays. This list is not exhaustive, as cell type differences, coverage limitations, method-specific biases, and selected bioinformatic thresholds are all confounding variables in modification detection. Both BID and direct RNA sequencing under-capture pseudouridines; thus, sensitivity or detection limit may miss lowly modified sites (Figure S1B; Figure S2A). A strength of Nano-Mod-Amp is the ability to enrich long or full-length transcripts. However, bisulfite labeling leads to RNA fragmentation, which could challenge detection of long low-abundance transcripts. Despite these limitations, our approaches discovered mechanisms behind PUS7-mediated pseudouridylation, setting the stage for future studies that explore how additional factors influence PUS7’s activity.

Method details

Cloning

To generate overexpression vectors, PUS7 cDNA was cloned into a pLentiCMV backbone with an N-terminal V5 tag via Gibson assembly. For an empty control, the V5 tag alone was left in the pLentiCMV backbone. To generate constitutive PUS7 KD, shRNA against PUS7 was cloned into a pLKO backbone under a U6 promoter. pLKO.1 - TRC Cloning Vector (Addgene) was digested with EcoRI and AgeI to remove the stuffer sequence. Two oligos containing the complementary shRNA targeting sequence with the corresponding overhangs were annealed and ligated into the vector by standard cloning: shPUS7_4F 5′- CCGGTCTTAGTTCAGACTCATATATCTCGAGATATATGAGTCTGAACTAAGATTTTTG -3′, shPUS7_4R 5′- AATTCAAAAATCTTAGTTCAGACTCATATATCTCGAGATATATGAGTCTGAACTAAGA-3’. An empty vector control was generated by filling in the gaps with Klenow and blunt ended ligation.

Cell culture standards

Human hepatocellular carcinoma cells HepG2 from ATCC HB8065 (lot 59635738) and human embryonic kidney cells HEK293T (kind gift from Dr. Lingyin Li) were grown in high glucose DMEM (ThermoFisher #11965118) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS HyClone SH30071.03) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (ThermoFisher #15140163). Cells were grown at 37C with 5% CO2 and maintained at sub-confluency.

Cell line creation

In this work, we generated cells lines for PUS7 overexpression, PUS7 dox-inducible knockdown, and PUS7 constitutive knockdown in HepG2 and HEK293T cells with a lentiviral approach. First, lentivirus was generated with Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher #11668030) wherein 2 μg of desired vector was mixed with 1.8 μg pCMV-psPAX2 and 0.2 μg pCMV-VSV-G according to manufacturer’s instructions. Solution was plated dropwise onto adherent HEK293T cells in a P6 dish at 70% confluency. Three days later, media was filtered through a 0.45 μm cellulose acetate filter to collect viral particles. HEK293T and HepG2 cells had been plated in a 6 well plate at 100,000 cells per well the day prior. Media was removed from these cells and replaced with 1 mL of fresh media, 1 mL of virus, and 1.6 μl of 10 mg/mL polybrene solution. Media was changed 24 h later and cells left to grow undisturbed for a total of 72 h. At that point, cells were placed under 3 μg/ml puromycin or blasticidin selection (as appropriate) for 48 h, until an uninfected control perished. Cells were allowed to recover for over 48 h before a sample was collected for PUS7 expression assessment by Western blot.

To generate the PUS7 KO, a dual guide approach was used with two gRNAs simultaneously. HepG2 cells were transiently transformed with two plasmids (Addgene #48138) containing Cas9, GFP, and one of two gRNAs (5′tcggaatgcagtctaaccaa3′ or 5′cttaacctccctcaaaacgc3′). Forty-eight hours following transfection, FACS sorting was performed to select GFP-positive cells. Cells were seeded at a very low density in a 10 cm tissue culture dish and grown into colonies for over two weeks. Single colonies were then transferred using cloning disks to a 48 well plate and, when enough material was present, assessed for PUS7 KO with Western blot and genotyping through sequencing.

Dox-inducible PUS7 shRNA knockdown

HepG2 or HEK293T dox-inducible cells were plated at subconfluency with a working concentration of 2 μg/μl doxycycline (Sigma Aldrich #D5207-10G). 48 h later, cells were expanded and fresh doxycycline at 2 μg/μl were added. Cells were collected 96 h after initial administration of doxycycline. To collect, media was aspirated from plate. Trizol (ThermoFisher #15596018) was added directly to the plate, per manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were homogenized in Trizol and processed immediately or stored at −80C until needed. Western blot of PUS7 depleted lysates confirmed depletion to ∼90%.

Nanopore direct RNA sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from stable HepG2 cell lines expressing empty vector pLKO-Tet-On or vector with a Dox-inducible shRNA targeting PUS7: shPUS7_4F 5′-CCGGTCTTAGTTCAGACTCATATATCTCGAGATATATGAGTCTGAACTAAGATTTTTG-3'

shPUS7_4R 5′- AATTCAAAAATCTTAGTTCAGACTCATATATCTCGAGATATATGAGTCTGAACTAAGA-3' from 3 biological replicates as described previously.10 shRNA expression was induced in the stable HepG2 cell lines with Doxycycline to a final concentration of 500 ng/mL. Cells were maintained in Doxycycline containing media for 96h. Total RNA was isolated from a single clone of a HepG2 PUS7 KO in a single biological replicate as described previously.10 Nanopore direct RNA sequencing was carried out with kit SQK-RNA004 and each sample was sequenced on an individual FLO-MIN004RA or FLO-PRO004RA flow cell to sequencing depths of 2-6M reads. Sequencing of the synthetic UNΨAR RNA Concatemer standard was carried out by barcoding each mix of defined ratios on modified to unmodified from 0 to 100% and demultiplexed using SeqTagger.74

Nanopore direct RNA sequencing data analysis

Multi-pod5 files from three replicates of PUS7 WT/KD Nanopore direct RNA sequencing data were basecalled with Dorado (7.3) using the high accuracy model. Reads were subsequently aligned to human genome assembly GRCh38 from GENCODE using minimap2 with the option “-ax splice -k14”. Resultant SAM files were converted to BAM files using Samtools (1.16.1) and subsequently visualized in IGV.

PUS7-dependent pseudouridine site assignment

A custom script, ModDetect, was adapted from PsiDetect39 to call putative RNA modification sites from mutational signatures in sequencing data from a test sample compared to a control. Extending PsiDetect’s original function of detecting pseudouridines from direct RNA U-to-C mismatches, ModDetect also enables readouts of pseudouridines based on deletion signatures in bisulfite-treated samples as well as detection of any other modified bases that manifest as mutations in sequencing reads. ModDetect was used to called PUS7-dependent pseudouridine sites from direct RNA U-to-C mismatches. We filtered out homopolymers as they are known to be error prone in Nanopore direct RNA sequencing.82 The following criteria were used to call PUS7-dependent pseudouridine sites: (1) number of T + C reads ≥ 10 in both WT and KD libraries; (2) U-to-C mismatch ≥ 15% between WT and KD where U-to-C mismatches rates were calculated as C/(T + C); (3) site is called in at least 2 out of 3 replicates. To account for sites that might have been missed due to insufficient coverage in our libraries, an additional set of sites was called which met criteria (1) and (2) in at least 1 of the replicates and was also annotated as a PUS7-dependent pseudouridine site in an external dataset.10,12,22

UNΨAR RNA concatemer design

To identify an appropriate spacing between PUS7 motifs for a synthetic construct, we studied IVT data from synthetic RNA molecules containing all possible 5-mers83 using IVTs without modifications or with 100% pseudouridine modification. We then selected pairs of pseudouridine sites (in the modified IVTs) or U sites (in the unmodified IVTs) separated by 9–20 nucleotide stretches of A/C/G. These pairs of sites were grouped according to non-U/non-pseudouridine gap length, and the effects on basecalling quality were analyzed.

For each group of pairs at a given distance, we compared the distribution of q-scores in the modified and non-modified reads. We assumed that for an optimal separation of consecutive pseudouridine sites, the basecalling accuracy should be similar between the modified and non-modified reads. For pairs of U-sites separated by 9 non-Us, the q-scores in non-U positions in the modified reads did not reach the non-modified IVT baseline, suggesting that the two pseudouridines at that distance likely have additive effects on basecalling. On the other hand, when the gap size is 14nt, the q-scores in the non-U stretches of the modified reads reach the non-modified q-score levels nearby the modified site. Moreover, basecalling is consistently more affected upstream of a modified site and less affected downstream. Analysis of these and other distances indicated that 14+ separation of non-modified bases between any two pseudouridines in the constructs would minimize the combined effects on basecalling. Based on these analyses, we opted to use a 14nt gap for the design of concatemers with adjacent pseudouridines.

A synthetic UNΨAR RNA concatemer was ordered from TriLink. It was designed to contain the 8 possible UNΨAR sequences separated by 14-nt spacer sequences, with a 10-nt poly-A tract placed at the 3′ end for compatibility with the standard Nanopore direct RNA sequencing kit SQK-RNA004. The spacer sequences were identical to those in a previous synthetic RNA oligo library.12

UNΨAR direct RNA stoichiometry derivation

To make calibration curves, data from each motif was independently plotted, with the background U-to-C mismatch rate at 0% UNΨAR subtracted from higher mixes. A linear line of best fit was applied to each curve and the equation was extracted (Table S2). To correct PUS7 site mismatch rate to real stoichiometry, the averaged mismatch rate for a given site across the replicates was used in the equation appropriate for the motif of the site.

Detection of PUS7-dependent sites using dorado modification calling

Raw pod5 data was basecalled using dorado 0.8.2 with modification model rna004_130bps_sup@v5.1.0_pseU@v1. Unaligned reads were converted to fastq using samtools fastq -T ∗ and aligned to hg38 primary assembly using minimap2 -ax splice -y to keep modification tags. Alignments were filtered to discard secondary and supplementary alignments and unmapped reads using samtools view -F 2308. Modification rate was computed using combinations of modkit parameters --filter-threshold and --mod-threshold each set to 0.5, 0.9, 0.99, and 0.995. Only reference T bases in the strand of mapped reads were considered, minimum coverage of 20 reads was used. Generalized linear model (GLM) was applied to the pooled replicates of the same condition, with null model assuming that modification readouts across conditions are drawn from the same distribution. Significant sites were selected with p p-value< 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction) and absolute difference in stoichiometry >0.05. Optimal modkit parameters (f0.9,m0.99) were chosen based on the ability to detect most PUS7-dependent sites with UNUAR motif while yielding a high proportion of significant sites (>90%) with both UNUAR motif and expected direction of change (control > KD).

MPRA generation

To build the MPRA (Figure 3), we included PUS7-dependent pseudouridines from our Nanopore direct RNA sequencing and from precious studies.10,12,22 Genomic sequences were extracted from the GRCh38 genome to center the pseudouridine in a 130-nucleotide sequence. To the 5′ end, a T7 promoter (GCTAATACGACTCACTATAG) and universal adapter (GACGCTCTTCCGATCT) were appended. To the 3′ end, a universal adapter (CACTCGGGCACCAAGGAC) was appended. The pool was ordered from Twist Bioscience.

To generate a vector for in cellulo transient transfection, PCR was performed on the pool using the universal handles and Phusion polymerase (New England Biolabs #M0530L) for 10 cycles with primers for overlap with the backbone. An SFFV-mCherry backbone, a kind gift from Xiaojing Gao, was digested with MluI and ApaI to create an opening in the 3′ UTR of mCherry. Gibson assembly with HiFi Assembly mix (New England Biolabs #E2621L) was performed. The resultant product was purified with Zymo DNA Clean and Concentrator-5 (Zymo Research #D4014) and eluted into 10 μl water. Endura electrocompetent cells (Biosearch Technologies #60242-1) were thawed on ice, 25 μl of cells were combined with 2 μl of purified vector. Enough bacteria for 1000x coverage of all sequences were transferred into 0.2 cm cuvettes and electroporated on a Biorad electroporator with settings of 10 μF, 600 Ohms, and 1800 Volts. Recovery media was immediately added to cells per manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were grown at 200 rpm and 37C for 1 h before being pooled and plated equally across 4 large LB + ampicillin bioassay plates. Cells were grown overnight. Then next day, all colonies were collected and plasmid DNA was extracted with two columns from PureLink HiPure Plasmid Filter Midiprep Kit (Thermo Fisher #K210014). To confirm pool presence and distribution, the same MPRA primers used in Nano-BID-Amp were used to amplify off the plasmid. The plasmid was sequenced in accordance with Nanopore cDNA Ligation kit (SQK-LSK114) and PCR Barcoding kit (EXP-PBC096) protocols.

Transient transfection for in cellulo MPRA

Prior to primary experiment, pilots were performed to determine transfection efficiency. The determined transfection efficiency was used to calculate how many cells to plate to ensure 1000x coverage of all sequences in the pool (cells needed = (# sequences in pool x 1000)/transfection efficiency). Cells were plated 24 h prior, targeting 70% confluency at the time of transfection. Manufacturer’s instructions for Lipofectamine 3000 (ThermoFisher #L3000008), scaled to the appropriate size, were followed for transfection. Cells were incubated for 48 h without media change. Cells were visualized under fluorescent microscope to establish transfection efficiency. To collect, media was aspirated from plate. Trizol (ThermoFisher #15596018) was added directly to the plate, per manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were homogenized in Trizol and processed immediately or stored at −80C until needed.

RNA extraction and polyA selection

RNA was extracted following manufacturer’s instructions for Trizol (ThermoFisher #15596018). In brief, chloroform was added to homogenized cell-Trizol mix, mixed thoroughly, and incubated at room temperature for 3 min. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000g for 15 min at 4C. The upper aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube for RNA extraction. The lower organic phase was transferred to a new tube for protein extraction. An equal volume of isopropanol was added. Samples were incubated on ice for 10 min. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000g for 10 min at 4C. Supernatant was discarded. RNA pellet was washed once with 75% ethanol and centrifuged at 7,500g for 5 min at 4C. Supernatant was discarded. Pellet was air dried and resuspended in RNAse free water. Concentration was quantified by Qubit RNA Broad Range Assay (ThermoFisher #Q10211).

Two rounds of polyA selection were performed with Oligo d(T) Magnetic Beads (New England Biolabs #S1419S) on ∼35 μg of total RNA. In brief, beads were equilibrated in Binding Buffer. RNA was denatured at 65C for 2 min and immediately placed on ice. RNA was added to beads and rotated at room temperature for 5 min. Samples were placed on magnets and washed twice with Washing Buffer B. Following final wash, all supernatant was removed. Cold 10 mM Tris HCl was added to samples. Samples were incubated at 75C for 2 min, placed on magnet, and quickly transferred to a new RNAse-free tube. Concentration was quantified by Qubit RNA High Sensitivity Assay (ThermoFisher #Q32855).

Protein extraction

From the Trizol extraction, 150 μl of the lower organic phase was combined with 1350 μl of ice-cold methanol. Samples were vortexed to mix. Samples were centrifuged at 14,000g for 10 min at 4C. Supernatant was discarded. Samples were washed with 1 mL of ice-cold methanol. Samples were centrifuged at 14,000g for 10 min at 4C. Supernatant was discarded. Samples were washed with 80% ethanol. Samples were spin at 14,000g for 10 min at 4C. Supernatant was discarded. Protein pellet was air-dried at room temperature. Samples were resuspended in 1% SDS 100 mM TAEB, freshly made. Nano-BID-Amp bioinformatic analysis and site calling

Nanopore sequencing produced several fastq files for each barcode to be prepared for further processing (trim_map_dedup.sh/trim_map_dedup_mpra.sh). The fastq files were concatenated together for each barcode and renamed based on the sample name. Nanopore adapters were trimmed using cutadapt in the sense and antisense direction with flags -m 125 -O 15 -g [linked … adapters] –discard-untrimmed. Antisense reads were reverse complemented with seqtk seq -r. UMIs were extracted from the 3′ end with UMI tools. For MPRA experiments, universal handles were trimmed with cutadapt with flags -m 120 -O 10 -g [linked … adapters] –discard-untrimmed. Samples were mapped with Minimap2 using parameters -ax sr to a fasta file containing only the target sequence (no adapters) for MPRA and parameters -ax splice for endogenous amplicons. For the mutagenesis experiment, the fasta for alignment also included barcodes to assist in mapping highly similar sequences. Mapped files were converted to bam, sorted, and indexed. Reads were filtered for primary alignments only and a MAPQ score greater than 30. Reads were deduplicated with UMICollapse79 and indexed. A custom Rscript (developed from ModDetect and PsiDetect39) used the package pileup to count the nucleotide calls at each position. Samples were filtered to only contain positions with at least 20 reads of coverage. Deletion fractions were calculated as (deletion counts/total reads).

Sites are then classified as Modified, Unmodified, or Inconclusive based on the difference between bisulfite-treated and input samples in a simple “treatment analysis” as follows:

First, all sites where the deletion fractions in all BS and input samples is less than 0.05 are classified as Unmodified. Data for a given site is then filtered to ensure at least two treatment conditions (ideally, BS and input), two replicates, and four data points in total.

Next, a Bayesian linear mixed-effects model (bglmer) was built to measure the impact of random effects (replicate, etc) and treatment (fixed effect) on the deletion fraction (response). The model is weighted so samples with higher total read count carry more weight, as a higher read depth (sample size) reduces the impacts of statistical noise. Fixed effects are regularized with a prior that assumes a normal distribution for all fixed effects to stabilize results that may result from noisy data or situations where all deletion fractions are zero for a given group. To determine statistically significant differences, an ANOVA test is then run on this model. Multiple-hypothesis test correction is performed with a Benjamini-Hochberg calculation. Sites with a corrected pp-value <0.05 and a difference in the deletion fraction between bisulfite-treated and input samples >0.05 are called as Modified.

Sites are then tested for statistical equivalence between bisulfite-treated and input sample. A baseline rate is established based on the average deletion fraction in input samples for a given site and converted to a log-odds scale. This baseline rate plus a smallest effect size of interest (0.05) is combined and converted to a log odds scale. The difference between these two logs-odds value is used as equivalency bounds. A confidence interval on the log-odds scale for the effect of the treatment parameter is then extracted from the model built in the step prior. The confidence interval is compared to the site-specific equivalence bounds. If the entire confidence interval falls within the equivalence bounds, the site shows equivalence in deletion fractions between bisulfite-treated and input samples and is called as Unmodified. If the upper confidence interval bound is less than the upper equivalence bound and the lower confidence interval is outside of the lower equivalence bound, or if the upper confidence interval bound is lower than the lower equivalence bound, the bisulfite-treated sample has a lower deletion fraction than the input sample and is called as Unmodified. If the confidence interval extends beyond the equivalence bound in both directions, the site is called as Inconclusive, likely due to noisy data or lack of power in the measurements.

To determine the impact of tertiary factors (PUS7 expression, cell type) on deletion fraction, only a test for statistical difference was applied in a more complex “factor analysis”. Two Bayesian linear mixed-effects model (bglmer) are fit to the data, one accounting for the factor (PUS7 level, cell type) having an effect and the other not. In both models, the deletion fraction is the response, the factor is a fixed effect, the replicate is a random effect, and each sample is weighted by its total reads, and fixed effects are constrained with a prior that assumes a normal distribution. The factor model has two indicators, L1andBS.ind and L2andBS.ind. L1 and L2 represent the two levels of the factor (WT and KD, WT and OE, HepG2 and HEK293T). These indicators carry a value of 1 if the sample is positive for both the level and BS treatment (so WT + BS for L1andBS.ind) and 0 in all other cases. The no factor model has one indicator, BS.ind, that is 1 is the sample is positive for BS and 0 if not. All three indicators are treated as fixed effects in their respective models. An ANOVA test then asks which model better fits the data for the site: distinguishing the factor levels or not. This is based on the logic that if there is no meaningful difference between the deletion fractions in the different conditions, either model would fit equally well. However, if there was a meaningful difference between the levels, the factor-aware model would fit better, producing a significant pp-value. All pp-values then undergo multiple hypothesis test correction via Benjamini-Hochberg. The corrected pp-value is then filtered for p < 0.05; all sites that fall below this threshold are called as significant and have difference deletion fractions between the two factors. Sites are then filtered to have a greater than 0.05 difference in deletion fraction between the average deletion fraction in the two factors under consideration (WT and KD, WT and OE, HepG2 and HEK293T).

The treatment analysis and PUS7 expression level analysis was performed on HepG2 and HEK293T data considered together, HepG2 data considered singularly, and HEK293T data considered singularly. The treatment analysis for in cellulo Nano-BID-Amp produced a list of sites that could be modified by any PUS. To determine PUS7-dependency in the in cellulo MPRA, the union of sites sensitive to PUS7 KD or PUS7 OE across any cellular background was taken. To determine PUS7-dependency in the in vitro MPRA, the site list from the treatment analysis was used as no other PUS was present.

Sites were determined to be modified in vitro only if they were Modified in the in vitro MPRA and Unmodified in the WT in cellulo MPRA. Sites were determined to be modified in cellulo only if they were PUS7-dependent in the PUS7 MPRA and Unmodified in the in vitro MPRA.

For comparison to BID-seq12 and PRAISE,22 the deletion fractions for wild-type HEK293T transcriptome-wide Illumina-based studies were downloaded from the supplemental tables of the respective papers. Two bisulfite-treated replicates were averaged to produce the deletion fractions. These were then compared to the HEK293T deletion fraction in our endogenous Nano-BID-Amp experiments.

Mutagenesis of PUS7 targets—Sequence motif

PUS7 target sequences were selected for UNUAR mutagenesis if they were modified in in vitro and in cellulo MPRAs, with a difference greater than 0.1 between the average deletion fraction in bisulfite-treated samples between WT and KD and WT and OE in the in cellulo MPRA, leading to a list of 27 sites in total. The 5-mer centered on the target uridine was mutated to every version of the UNUAR motif along with retaining the parent sequence.

Mutagenesis of PUS7 targets—Accessibility

Pairing status of targets was determined based on in vitro SHAPE-informed RNAfold predictions. For break pair mutations, the pairing partner of the target residue was identified and mutated to be the same residue as its pairing partner. For example, if the residue of interest is a uridine, its pairing partner would be mutated to a uridine to ensure disruption of the pair. For make pair mutations, two strategies were taken: extend stem or make hairpin. For extend stem, three nucleotides upstream and downstream of the target residue was scanned for the presence of a stem. If one was found, positions on the opposite side of the target residue are mutated to pair with the residues on the same strand as the target residue, protecting the UNUAR motif from mutation. If no adjacent stem exists within three nucleotides and all residues are unpaired, downstream residues are mutated to pair with the target residue, creating a two base-pair stem with a two base-pair loop for target uridine and target uridine and plus one targets and at minimum a two base-pair stem with a four base-pair loop for unpaired2 and 5mer targets.

To assess if mutations performed as expected, mutated sequences were folded with RNAfold. Three metrics were calculated from the resultant MEA structures in comparison to parental MEA structures: On Target Score, Off Target Score, and Tree Edit Distance. On Target Score checks if the target residues now have the desired pairing status (paired or unpaired) across the entire region of interest. Off Target Score calculates the symmetric distance between the base pairing partners of the parental structure and the base pairing partners of the mutant structure, minus the intended base pairing partner changes, where a value close to zero indicates retention of parental base pairing partners outside of the target region. Tree Edit Distance assesses the global topological similarity of the coarse-grained structure with RNAdistance, calculating the number of differences in stems, bulges, hairpins, and multiloops between the two structures, where a score closer to zero indicates high similarity. Mutants were filtered by these metrics to select those that best implement the intended changes with minimal perturbations to the rest of the structure, selecting those that met all of the following criteria: Off Target Score in the top 50%, Tree Edit Distance in the top 50%, and On Target Score equivalent to the desired mutation. For On Target Score, break pair mutations were checked for unpairedness over the defined region (1 position for target uridine, 5 positions for 5mer, etc) and make pair for pairedness over the defined region.

Mutagenesis of PUS7 targets—Structure

Structure and pairing partners of targets was determined based on in vitro SHAPE-informed RNAfold predictions. For disruption mutations, all paired residues within −10 to −3 and +3 to +15 (inclusive) were identified as target residues. The −2 to +2 positions were excluded to protect the sequence motif from mutation. The pairing partner of these target residues were mutated to be the same residue as its pairing partner, resulting in a mutation in the opposite strand. For example, if the residue of interest is a uridine, its pairing partner would be mutated a uridine to ensure disruption of the pair. For compensatory mutations, the target residues were mutated to pair with their original partners. To continue the example, if the residue of interest was a uridine, it would be mutated to an adenine to putatively pair with its mutant uridine partner.

To assess if mutations performed as expected, mutated sequences were folded with RNAfold. Three metrics were calculated from the resultant MEA structures in comparison to parental MEA structures: On Target Score, Off Target Score, and Tree Edit Distance. For disruption mutations, On Target Score calculates the length of the target region minus the number of unpaired residues within that region. For compensatory mutations, On Target Score calculates the length of the region minus the number of residues in the target reigon that have the same pairing partner as the parental structure. For both calculations of On Target Score, a value close of equal to zero indicates success. Off Target Score calculates the symmetric distance between all of the base pairing partners of the parental structure and the base pairing partners of the mutant structure, where a value close to zero indicates retention of parental base pairing partners. Tree Edit Distance assesses the global topological similarity of the coarse-grained structure with RNAdistance, calculating the number of differences in stems, bulges, hairpins, and multiloops between the two structures, where a value closer to zero indicates high similarity.

Targets were included in the pool if their disruption mutations were in the top 50% of On Target Score, top 30% of Off Target Score, and top 50% of Tree Edit Distance. Compensatory mutations were included if the disruption mutation for that sequence met the threshold, and the compensatory structure was in the top 50% of On Target Score, top 50% of Off Target Score, and top 50% of Tree Edit Distance. In addition, sites of interest (RHBDD2_chr7_75888787, FIS1_chr7_101245032, EIF5_chr14_103342863, IGF2BP1_chr17_49043468, HDAC6_chrX_48823353) were included regardless of score. When assessing the results of our experiment, we observed that many of the compensatory structures that passed these filters did not fully restore parental levels of modification of structure. Tighter filtering criteria that looked at compensatory structures with an On Target Score of 0, Off Target Score less than 5, and Tree Edit Distance less than 50 performed as expected (IGF2BP1_chr17_49043468, PPP1R12B_chr1_202587327_total_compensation), whereas compensatory structures above these values (RHBDD2_chr7_75888787) performed more variably depending on broader structural similarities to the parental structure.

Coverslips were placed into wells of a 24-well plate and sterilized with UV for 10 min, followed by a quick wash with DMEM. HepG2 cells were plated at a density of 100,000 cells per well; HEK293T cells were plated at a density of 50,000 cells per well. Cells were incubated overnight for attachment. Media was removed, cells were quickly washed with PBS. Cells were incubated with rocking in freshly diluted 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min, followed by two washes with PBS. Cells were permeabilized with freshly diluted 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min with rocking and washed three times with PBS. Cells were blocked with IF blocking buffer (5% FBS in PBS) for 1 h in a humidity chamber and 37C. Blocking buffer was removed and replaced with primary antibody (PUS7, abcam #ab289857) at a 1:500 concentration in IF blocking buffer for 1.5 h. Samples were washed with 1x Wash Buffer A (Millipore Sigma #DUO82049) two times for 5 min each. Samples with incubated with green anti-rabbit secondary (Invitrogen #A11008CATNO) at 1:1000 concentration in IF blocking buffer for 30 min, covered to protect from light. Samples were washed with 1x Wash Buffer B (Millipore Sigma #DUO82049) two times for 10 min each, followed by a final wash with 0.01x Wash Buffer B. Coverslips were mounted onto slides with In Situ Mounting Media with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich #DUO82040), cured overnight, and imaged on ECHO Revolve microscope.

Western blot

RIPA lysis was performed on cell pellets, wherein cells were resuspended in RIPA 150 mM NaCl, 0.8% NP-40, 0.5% deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 5 mM Tris pH 7.4), incubated on ice for 10 min with periodic vortexing, and spun at max speed for 15 min at 4C. Supernatant was transferred to a new tube and quantified with Qubit Protein Broad Range Assay (ThermoFisher #A50669). Appropriate amounts of sample were loaded into a Precast Mini-PROTEAN TGX Gel (Biorad #4561096) and run at 130V for 50 min. Samples then underwent a wet transfer to PVDF membrane and were blocked for at least 10 min in 5% milk in TBST. Samples were incubated overnight in primary antibody at various concentrations in 5% milk in TBST. Samples were washed three times with TBST and incubated in secondary antibody at 1:4000 dilution for 1 h. Following three washes, samples were imaged on a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System. Results were quantified in ImageJ and normalized to GAPDH loading control.
