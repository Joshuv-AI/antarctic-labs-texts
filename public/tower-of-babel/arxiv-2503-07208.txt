A Quadratic Vertex Kernel and a Subexponential Algorithm for Subset-FAST
Satyabrata Jana, Lawqueen Kanesh, Madhumita Kundu, Daniel Lokshtanov, Saket Saurabh

Abstract
In the Subset Feedback Arc Set in Tournaments ( Subset-FAST ) problem we are given as input a tournament T T with a vertex set V ⁡ ( T ) V(T) and an arc set A ⁡ ( T ) A(T) , along with a terminal set S ⊆ V ⁡ ( T ) S\subseteq V(T) , and an integer k k . The objective is to determine whether there exists a set F ⊆ A ⁡ ( T ) F\subseteq A(T) with | F | ≤ k |F|\leq k such that the resulting graph T − F T-F contains no cycle that includes any vertex of S S . When S = V ⁡ ( T ) S=V(T) this is the classic Feedback Arc Set in Tournaments (FAST) problem. We obtain the first polynomial kernel for this problem parameterized by the solution size. More precisely, we obtain an algorithm that, given an input instance ( T , S , k ) (T,S,k) , produces an equivalent instance ( T ′ , S ′ , k ′ ) (T^{\prime},S^{\prime},k^{\prime}) with k ′ ≤ k k^{\prime}\leq k and V ⁡ ( T ′ ) = 𝒪 ⁡ ( k 2 ) V(T^{\prime})={\mathcal{O}}(k^{2}) . It was known that FAST admits a simple quadratic vertex kernel and a non-trivial linear vertex kernel. However, no such kernel was previously known for Subset-FAST . Our kernel employs variants of the most well-known reduction rules for FAST and introduces two new reduction rules to identify irrelevant vertices. As a result of our kernelization, we also obtain the first sub-exponential time FPT algorithm for Subset-FAST .

Introduction

In this paper we focus on a subset variant of the well-studied
Directed Feedback Arc Set (DFAS)
problem in tournaments. In
DFAS
, the input consists of a directed graph
D
and a positive integer
k
. The goal is to determine whether there exists a set
F\subseteq A(D)
of size at most
k
such that
D-F
forms a directed acyclic graph (DAG). In the subset variant of this problem, referred to as
Subset-DFAS
, we are additionally given a subset
S\subseteq V(D)
(known as a
terminal set
) and aim to determine whether there exists a set
F\subseteq A(D)
of size at most
k
such that
D-F
contains no cycles that include any vertex from
S
. Notably, when
S=V(D)
,
Subset-DFAS
coincides with
DFAS
.

If we substitute arcs with vertices in the definitions above, we obtain the problems of
Directed Feedback Vertex Set (DFVS)
and
Subset-DFVS
. All four problems mentioned are known to be NP-complete
[
22
]
and admit a polynomial-time approximation algorithm with a factor of
{\mathcal{O}}(\log n\log\log n)

[
18
]
. Additionally, they have fixed-parameter tractable (FPT) algorithms with running times of
2^{{\mathcal{O}}(k^{3})}n^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}

[
10
,
9
]
. However, it remains unknown whether any of
DFAS
,
Subset-DFAS
,
DFVS
, or
Subset-DFVS
admit a constant factor approximation, a
c^{k}n^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
time FPT algorithm, or a polynomial kernel.
The lack of progress on these problems has motivated research into their special instances.

The algorithmic complexity of
DFAS
and
DFVS
changes significantly when the input is limited to tournaments—directed graphs where there is precisely one directed arc between each pair of vertices, or equivalently, orientations of complete undirected graphs. In fact, the problems
Feedback Arc Set in Tournament (FAST)
and
Feedback Vertex Set in Tournament (FVST)
are well-established problems in their own right. Both problems are known to be NP-complete
[
1
,
7
,
30
]
. While
FAST
has a polynomial-time approximation scheme
[
24
]
,
FVST
has a polynomial-time 2-approximation algorithm, which is optimal
[
27
]
. In the context of parameterized complexity,
FAST
offers a subexponential time algorithm with a runtime of
2^{\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{k})}n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}

[
2
,
19
,
21
]
, whereas
FVST
has an algorithm that runs in
1.618^{k}n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
time
[
26
]
. Concerning kernelization,
FAST
admits a linear vertex kernel
[
5
]
, whereas
FVST
possess an almost linear vertex kernel
[
4
,
29
]
. Furthermore, it’s crucial to note that solving
FAST
has significant implications across various applications, including ranking systems, sports competitions, voting systems, and preference aggregation
[
8
,
11
,
16
,
17
]
. In these scenarios, addressing cyclic inconsistencies is vital for generating a meaningful linear ordering.

A natural question that has been explored recently is what happens when we consider the generalization of
FAST
and
FVST
to
Subset-FAST
and
Subset-FVST
, respectively. Can we extend the results of the non-subset variants to their subset variants? It is not difficult to show that both of these problems admit algorithms with a running time of
3^{k}n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
by leveraging their connection to the
3-Hitting Set
problem. Bai and Xiao
[
3
]
made significant improvements by designing a
2^{k+o(k)}n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
time algorithm for
Subset-FVST
. More recently, Jana et al.
[
23
]
developed an algorithm for
Subset-FVST
with the same running time as that of
FVST
. Specifically, the algorithm runs in
1.618^{k}n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
time. Furthermore, it is possible to construct a kernel with
\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
vertices for
Subset-FVST
by utilizing its relationship with the
3-Hitting Set
problem.

In this paper, we consider
Subset-FAST
and derive results that go beyond what can be attained through its connection to the
3
-Hitting Set
problem. Formally, we will consider the following problem.

Subset Feedback Arc Set in Tournaments
(
Subset-FAST
)

Parameter:

k

Input:
A tournament
T=(V,A)
, a terminal set
S\subseteq V(T)
, and an integer
k
.

Task:
Find
F\subseteq A
with
|F|\leq k
such that
T-F
has no cycle containing a vertex of
S
.

1.1
Our Results and Methods

In this paper we obtain the first polynomial kernel for
Subset-FAST
parameterized by the solution size. More precisely, we obtain a polynomial time algorithm that, given an input instance
(T,S,k)
, produces an equivalent instance
(T^{\prime},S^{\prime},k^{\prime})
with
k^{\prime}\leq k
and
V(T^{\prime})={\mathcal{O}}(k^{2})
. In particular, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 1
.

Subset-FAST
admits a kernel with
{\mathcal{O}}(k^{2})
vertices.

Figure 1:
A summary of the steps of our kernelization algorithm.

A schematic diagram showing the main steps and reduction rules of our algorithm is shown in Figure
1
. We first show that the problem of deleting
k
arcs to eliminate all directed
S
-cycles (cycle containing some vertices from
S
) is equivalent to the problem of reversing
k
arcs to achieve the same outcome (
Lemma

5
). In particular our problem is equivalent to the following reversal problem. Given a directed graph
G
and a subset
F\subseteq A(G)
of arcs, we denote
G\circledast F
to be the directed graph obtained from
G
by reversing all the arcs of
F
.

Subset Feedback Arc Set Reversal
(
Subset-FAST-Rev
)

Parameter:

k

Input:
A tournament
T=(V(T),A(T))
, a vertex set
S\subseteq V(T)
, and an integer
k
.

Task:
Find
F\subseteq A(T)
with
|F|\leq k
such that
T\circledast F
has no
S
-cycle.

Lemma

5
allows us to focus on the problem
Subset-FAST-Rev
. Next, we establish a bound on the number of terminal vertices
S
(
Section

4
). A straightforward quadratic bound can be derived by applying reduction rules similar to those used in obtaining a quadratic kernel for
FAST

[
12
]
. Specifically, we implement two simple reduction rules: (i) remove a vertex
v
from
S
that does not participate in any triangles, and (ii) if an arc appears in more than
k
distinct
S
-triangles, we reverse the arc and decrease the budget
k
by
1
(classic
sunflower reduction rule
). To achieve a linear bound on the size of
S
, we closely follow the kernelization algorithm for
FAST
as outlined by Paul et al.
[
29
]
, and also by Bessy et al.
[
5
]
. In particular, we utilize the
conflict packing
technique and adapt the notion of a
safe partition
to secure a linear bound on the terminals.

After bounding the vertices in
S
by
{\mathcal{O}}(k)
, the algorithm shifts its focus to bounding the non-
S
vertices in
Section

5.1
. To accomplish this, we first partition the non-
S
vertices into equivalence classes based on their neighborhoods in
S
. Specifically, all non-
S
vertices sharing the same set of in-neighbors and out-neighbors in
S
belong to the same equivalence class. A loose upper bound on the number of such equivalence classes is
2^{|S|}
. However, if the input instance is a
Yes
instance, the number of equivalence classes is bounded by
{\mathcal{O}}(k)
.

To support this observation, we introduce the concept of a
{\mathsf{S}\text{-}\textsf{topological ordering}}
(
Definition

6
). Intuitively, a
{\mathsf{S}\text{-}\textsf{topological ordering}}

\Pi
with respect to
S
consists of an ordered partition
V_{1}\uplus V_{2}\uplus\ldots\uplus V_{\ell}
of the vertices of
T
, where parts containing
S
-vertices are singletons, and the parts that do not contain
S
-vertices form a strongly connected component in
T
. Furthermore, for every pair
i<j\in[\ell]
, all vertices of
V_{i}
precede all vertices of
V_{j}
in
\Pi
, and every arc
(u,v)
exists in
T
, where
u\in V_{i}
and
v\in V_{j}
.

We demonstrate that a tournament
T
is
S
-acyclic if and only if it admits such a
{\mathsf{S}\text{-}\textsf{topological ordering}}
. It follows that if
T
is an
S
-acyclic tournament, then each part
V_{i}
in the
{\mathsf{S}\text{-}\textsf{topological ordering}}
that contains only non-
S
vertices represents a neighborhood equivalence class defined based on neighbors in
S
. This further implies that the number of equivalence classes in an
S
-acyclic tournament is bounded by
|S|+1
. Consequently, in the case of a
Yes
instance, we can have at most
k
additional equivalence classes in
T
. Therefore, we obtain a bound of
|S|+k+1={\mathcal{O}}(k)
on the number of equivalence classes in any tournament.

Finally, we concentrate on each equivalence class, denoted by
Z
. In Sections
5.2
and
5.3
, we demonstrate that if the size of the equivalence class is large, we can identify a significant subset of vertices, referred to as
Z_{{\texttt{Irrelevant}}}
, such that “most” of the arcs incident to it can never be part of the solution (we call such arcs
sure arcs
). Furthermore, the only arcs that could potentially belong to
F
are those adjacent to at most
{\mathcal{O}}(k^{2})
vertices, which we designate as
Z_{{\texttt{Relevant}}}
.
In a sense, the vertices in
Z_{{\texttt{Relevant}}}
form a rigid skeleton (as discussed in
Section

5.2
). We can establish that if we refrain from removing the vertices in
Z_{{\texttt{Relevant}}}
, then a vertex in
Z_{{\texttt{Irrelevant}}}
can be safely removed.

One reason why the size of
Z_{{\texttt{Relevant}}}=\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
is that it contains in-neighbors and out-neighbors of all vertices of
Z
that have either an in-degree at most
k
or an out-degree at most
k
in
T[Z]
. It is known that the number of such vertices are upper bounded by
{\mathcal{O}}(k)
and hence the size of
Z_{{\texttt{Relevant}}}=\mathcal{O}(k^{2})
. We keep all these in-neighbors and out-neighbors in
Z_{{\texttt{Relevant}}}
because they validate the redundancy of the vertices in
Z_{{\texttt{Irrelevant}}}
. However, the redundancy of a vertex in
Z_{{\texttt{Irrelevant}}}
depends on the
number of in-neighbors
and
number of out-neighbors
in
Z_{{\texttt{Relevant}}}
, not on the actual neighbors themselves. We leverage this observation and apply an “arc swapping argument” (see
Figure

10
for an illustration) to obtain an equivalent instance in which, for any vertex in
Z_{{\texttt{Irrelevant}}}
, the number of in-neighbors and number of out-neighbors in
Z_{{\texttt{Relevant}}}
remains the same, but the size of
Z_{{\texttt{Relevant}}}
is reduced to
\mathcal{O}(k)
. This, together with our irrelevant vertex rule (
Reduction Rule

8
), gives the desired kernel.

The following is the second result of our paper.

Theorem 2
.

Subset-FAST
can be solved in time
2^{{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{k}\log k)}+n^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
.

We follow the approach of Alon, Lokshtanov, and Saurabh
[
2
]
, who developed a sub-exponential time
FPT
algorithm for
FAST
. However, due to the generality of our problem, we must deviate from their method while doing the dynamic programming step. Our algorithm builds upon the chromatic coding technique introduced in
[
2
]
, integrating it with a dynamic programming approach applied to the reduced instance obtained through
Theorem

1
.

Preliminaries

Let
[n]
represent the set of integers
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For a directed graph
G
, we denote the vertex set by
V(G)
and the arc set by
A(G)
. For a subset of vertices
X\subseteq V(G)
, we use the notation
G-X
to denote the graph
G^{\prime}=G[V\setminus X]
. For a subset
F
of arcs
F\subseteq A(G)
, we use the notation
G-F
to denote the graph
G^{\prime}=(V(G),A(G)\setminus F)
. Further for an ease of notation, we use
G-v
(resp.
G-e
) when
X=\{v\}
(resp.
F=\{e\}
) is singleton. Similarly for a subset
F\subseteq V(G)\times V(G)
, we use the notion
G+F
to denote the graph
G^{\prime}=(V(G),A(G)\cup F)
. For a directed graph
G
, a vertex set
X\subseteq V(G)
forms a strongly connected component (SCC) in
G
if
X
is a maximal set of vertices such that for any pair of vertices
u,v\in X
, there is a directed path from
u
to
v
and from
v
to
u
in the induced subgraph
G[X]
. For a cycle
C
in a directed graph, we use
A(C)
to denote set of the arcs in the cycle
C
.

A directed graph is called a tournament if there is an arc between every pair of vertices. For a tournament
T
, an arc set
F\subseteq A(T)
is called a feedback arc set of
T
if there is no directed cycle in the graph
T-F
. For a directed graph
G
and
S\subseteq V(G)
, we call a vertex
v\in V(G)
, a
S
-vertex if
v\in S
. A cycle (resp. path) in
G
is called
S
-cycle (resp.
S
-path) if the cycle (resp. path) contains some
S
-vertex. A
S
-triangle is a
S
-cycle with exactly three vertices. For a directed graph
D
, and vertex subset
S\subseteq V(D)
, the graph
D
is said to be
S
-acyclic if
D
has no
S
-cycle. For a tournament
T
and
S\subseteq V(T)
, an arc set
F\subseteq A(T)
is called a
S
-feedback arc set (in short,
S
-fas) of
T
if there is no directed
S
-cycle in
T-F
, in other words,
T-F
is
S
-acyclic.

Given a directed graph
G
and a subset
F\subseteq A(G)
of arcs, we denote
G\circledast F
to be the directed graph obtained from
G
by reversing all the edges of
F
. That is,
if
{\mathsf{rev}}(F)=\{(u,v)|(v,u)\in F\}
, then for
G\circledast F
the vertex set is
V(G)
and the arc set is
A(G\circledast F)=(A(G)\cup{\mathsf{rev}}(F))\setminus F
. For ease of notation, when the set
F=\{e\}
is singleton, we use
{\mathsf{rev}}(e)
and
G\circledast e
.

For a vertex
v\in V(G)
, we denote
{\mathsf{arc}}(v)
to be the set of arcs incident to the vertex
v
in
G
. For a pair of disjoint vertex sets
P,Q\subseteq V(G)
, let
{\mathsf{arc}}(P,Q)
denote the set of all the arcs between
P
and
Q
in
A(G)
, more formally
{\mathsf{arc}}(P,Q)=A(G)\cap\{(u,v)~|~u\in P,v\in Q~\text{or}~u\in Q,v\in P\}
. Whenever we say that "every arc in
{\mathsf{arc}}(P,Q)
is from
P
to
Q
", we mean that there in no arc
(u,v)\in{\mathsf{arc}}(P,Q)
with
u\in Q,v\in P
.

Kernelization.

A parameterized problem
\Pi
is a subset of
\Gamma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
for some finite alphabet
\Gamma
. An instance of a parameterized problem consists of
(X,k)
, where
k
is called the parameter. The notion of kernelization is formally defined as follows. A kernelization algorithm, or in short, a kernelization, for a parameterized problem
\Pi\subseteq\Gamma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
is an algorithm that, given
(X,k)\in\Gamma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
, outputs in time polynomial in
|X|+k
a pair
(X^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in\Gamma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
such that (a)
(X,k)\in\Pi
if and only if
(X^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in\Pi
and (b)
|x^{\prime}|,|k|\leq g(k)
, where
g
is some computable function depending only on
k
. The output of kernelization
(X^{\prime},k^{\prime})
is referred to as the kernel and the function
g
is referred to as the size of the kernel. If
g(k)\in k^{{\mathcal{O}}(1)}
, then we say that
\Pi
admits a polynomial kernel. We refer to the monographs
[
15
,
20
,
28
]
for a detailed study of the area of kernelization.

Our algorithms make use of reduction rules which transform one instance of a
problem to another instance of the same problem. We use
(T,S,k)
to represent
the instance given as input to each reduction rule, and
(T^{\prime},S^{\prime},k^{\prime})
to represent
the (modified) instance output by the rule. We say that a reduction rule is

sound
if for every input instance
(T,S,k)
the rule outputs an equivalent instance
(T,S^{\prime},k^{\prime})
. We update
T\rightarrow T^{\prime},S\rightarrow S^{\prime},k\rightarrow k^{\prime}

to get the input instance
(T^{\prime},S^{\prime},k^{\prime})

for further processing. We say that an instance
(T,S,k)
is reduced with respect to a reduction rule if none of the conditions of the rule is applicable to
(T,S,k)
.

3
Characterization of
Subset-FAST
as Arcs Reversal Problem

In this section, we demonstrate that the problem of deleting
k
arcs to eliminate all directed
S
-cycles is equivalent to the problem of reversing
k
arcs to achieve the same outcome. In particular our problem is equivalent to the following reversal problem.

Subset Feedback Arc Set Reversal
(
Subset-FAST-Rev
)

Parameter:

k

Input:
A tournament
T=(V(T),A(T))
, a vertex set
S\subseteq V(T)
, and an integer
k
.

Task:
Find
F\subseteq A(T)
with
|F|\leq k
such that
T\circledast F
has no
S
-cycle.

For our proof, we also need the following characterization relating
S
-cycle and
S
-triangles. It is well known that a tournament is acyclic if and only if it does not contain any triangle
[
14
]
. The following lemma, proved by Bai in
[
3
]
states a similar statement for the subset variant.

Lemma 3
.

[
3
, Lemma 2]

A tournament is
S
-acyclic if and only if it does not contain an
S
-triangle.

We present an observation regarding the presence of an
S
-triangle for every
S
-vertex that lies within some
S
-cycles.

Observation 4
.

Let
s\in S
. Then, there is an
S
-cycle containing
s
if and only if there is an
S
-triangle containing
s
.

Proof.

We only prove the forward direction as the reverse direction holds vacuously. Let
C
be a shortest
S
-cycle in a tournament
T
that includes
s
.
Let
c_{1}c_{2}\ldots c_{\ell}c_{1}
be the cycle where
c_{1}=s
. If
\ell=3
, then we are done. Otherwise, assume that
\ell\geq 4
. Consider the vertex
c_{3}
. If
(c_{3},s)\in A(T)
, then
sc_{2}c_{3}s
is a triangle. If
(s,c_{3})\in A(T)
, then we get a shorter cycle
sc_{3}c_{4}\ldots s
which is a contradiction to our assumption that
C
is a shortest
S
-cycle in a tournament
T
that includes
s
.
∎

Now we prove that
Subset-FAST
and
Subset-FAST-Rev
are equivalent. This is a known result for
FAST
.

Figure 2:
Illustration of
Lemma

5
: equivalence between
Subset-FAST
and
Subset-FAST-Rev
. Red vertices indicates
S
-vertices.

Lemma 5
.

Let
T
be a tournament,
S\subseteq V(T)
and
F\subseteq A(T)
. Then
F
is an inclusion-wise minimal solution for
Subset-FAST
if and only if
F
is an inclusion-wise minimal solution for
Subset-FAST-Rev
.

Proof.

In the forward direction, let
(T,S,k)
be a
Yes
instance of
Subset-FAST
and
F
be a inclusion-wise minimal solution. We first claim that
F
is also a solution for
Subset-FAST-Rev
. Assume towards contradiction that
T\circledast F
has an
S
-cycle, say
C
. Let
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{\ell}
be the arcs of
A(C)\cap{\mathsf{rev}}(F)
in the order of their appearance on the cycle
C
and
e_{i}={\mathsf{rev}}(f_{i})\in F
. Since
F
is a inclusion-wise minimal solution of
Subset-FAST
for
(T,S,k)
, this implies that for every
e_{i}\in F
, there exists an
S
-cycle
C_{i}
in
T
such that
A(C_{i})\cap F=\{e_{i}\}
. Now consider a following closed walk
W
in
T
. We start at some vertex of
C
and follow the cycle
C
except when an arc
f_{i}\in{\mathsf{rev}}(F)
comes. When
f_{i}
comes in the walk, then instead of following the arc
f_{i}
, we traverse the path
C_{i}-e_{i}
. By definition,
W
is a closed walk in
T
that does not contain any arc of
{\mathsf{rev}}(F)
from which we can retrieve an
S
-cycle in
T-F
, which contradicts that
F
is a solution for
Subset-FAST
.
F
is also an inclusion-wise minimal solution for
Subset-FAST-Rev
because if there is a proper subset
F^{\prime}\subset F
of arcs such that
F^{\prime}
is a solution for
Subset-FAST-Rev
, this will also be a solution for
Subset-FAST
of smaller size which contradicts minimality of
F
.

In the reverse direction, let
\widetilde{F}
be an inclusion-wise minimal solution for
Subset-FAST-Rev
. It is easy to see that
\widetilde{F}
is also a solution for
Subset-FAST
. Moreover,
\widetilde{F}
is also an inclusion-wise minimal solution for
Subset-FAST
, Suppose not, consider a proper subset
\widetilde{F}^{\prime}\subset\widetilde{F}
which is a inclusion-wise minimal solution for
Subset-FAST
, then by the argument of forward direction, this will become a inclusion-wise minimal solution for
Subset-FAST-Rev
, that contradicts the minimality of
\widetilde{F}
.
∎

By Lemma
5
we know that a minimal solution to
Subset-FAST
is the same as a minimal solution to
Subset-FAST-Rev
. For an illustration of
Lemma

5
, see
Figure

2
. Thus, from
now onward we focus on the problem
Subset-FAST-Rev
.

3.1
\boldsymbol{S}
-Topological Ordering

Next we define
{\mathsf{S}\text{-}\textsf{topological ordering}}
which is useful for our kernel. This enriches the notion of canonical sequence defined by Bai and Xiao
[
3
, Definition
1
]
.

Definition 6

(
{\mathsf{S}\text{-}\textsf{topological ordering}}
)
.

For a tournament
T,
and
S\subseteq V(T)
,
T
is said to have a
{\mathsf{S}\text{-}\textsf{topological ordering}}

\Pi
with respect to
S
if
T
has an ordered partition
V_{1}\uplus V_{2}\uplus\ldots\uplus V_{\ell}
of
V(T)
that satisfies the following:

(i)

For every
s\in S
, there exists
i\in[\ell]
such that the part
V_{i}=\{s\}
is singleton.

(ii)

For every
i\in[\ell]
, the part
V_{i}
forms a strongly connected component in
T
.

(iii)

For every pair
i<j\in[\ell]
, and every
u\in V_{i}
and
v\in V_{j}
,
(u,v)
is an arc in
T
.

We refer to a part consisting of a single
S
vertex as a
S
-singleton part
. Now we have the following lemma.

Lemma 7
.

Let
T
be a tournament and
S\subseteq V(T)
.
T
admits a
{\mathsf{S}\text{-}\textsf{topological ordering}}

\Pi
with respect to
S
if and only if
T
does not contain any
S
-triangle. Furthermore, if
T
does not have an
S
-triangle, we can compute
{\mathsf{S}\text{-}\textsf{topological ordering}}
in polynomial time.

Proof.

In the forward direction, let
\Pi
be a
{\mathsf{S}\text{-}\textsf{topological ordering}}
of
T
with respect to
S
. Let
V_{1}\uplus V_{2}\uplus\ldots\uplus V_{\ell}
be the corresponding ordered partition of
V(T)
. Assume towards contradiction that
T
has an
S
-triangle with arcs
(a,s),(s,b)
and
(b,a)\in A(T)
where
s\in S
. Let
V_{q}
be the
S
-singleton part that contains
s
. Since
(a,s)\in A(T)
, this implies that the vertices of the part
V_{p}
that contain
a
, precede the vertices of
V_{q}
in
\Pi
. Similarly, since
(s,b)\in A(T)
, this implies that the vertices of the part
V_{q}
precede the vertices of the part
V_{r}
that contains
b
in
\Pi
. Hence, it follows that all the vertices of the part
V_{p}
precede all the vertices of
V_{r}
in
\Pi
which is a contradiction that
(b,a)\in A(T)
with
b\in V_{r}
and
a\in V_{p}
.

In the reverse direction, suppose that
T
does not contain an
S
-triangle. We will now construct a
{\mathsf{S}\text{-}\textsf{topological ordering}}

\Pi
of
T
as follows. First, compute the maximal strongly connected components
V_{1},\ldots,V_{\ell}
of
T
. Since these components are maximal, they are pairwise disjoint. These components will form the parts of
{\mathsf{S}\text{-}\textsf{topological ordering}}

\Pi
. Observe that, since there are no
S
-triangles (or
S
-cycles), if
V_{i}
contains a vertex
s\in S
, then
V_{i}=\{s\}
. This implies that for every
s\in S
, there exists
i\in[\ell]
such that the component
V_{i}=\{s\}
is a singleton. We construct an auxiliary graph
T^{\prime}
where
V(T^{\prime})=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell}\}
. Here, the vertex
v_{i}\in V(T^{\prime})
corresponds to the strongly connected component
V_{i}
. There is an arc from vertex
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
in
A(T^{\prime})
if and only if there is an arc from a vertex in
V_{i}
to a vertex in
V_{j}
. Note that if an arc exists from a vertex in
V_{i}
to a vertex in
V_{j}
, then every vertex in
V_{i}
has an arc to every vertex in
V_{j}
; otherwise,
V_{i}\cup V_{j}
would form a larger strongly connected component. Therefore, the arc set
A(T^{\prime})
is well-defined. Furthermore, we can observe that
T^{\prime}
is also a tournament.

Finally, note that
T^{\prime}
is acyclic; otherwise, the union of the maximal strongly connected components corresponding to a cycle in
T^{\prime}
would create a larger strongly connected component in
T
. Since
T^{\prime}
is an acyclic tournament, there is a unique topological ordering of it, which can be found in
{\mathcal{O}}(m+n)={\mathcal{O}}(n^{2})
time
[
25
]
. The corresponding order of the maximal strongly connected components is the
{\mathsf{S}\text{-}\textsf{topological ordering}}

\Pi
of
T
. This concludes the proof.
∎

4
Bounding the number of Terminals

In this section, we design reduction rules to obtain an equivalent instance of
Subset-FAST-Rev
with at most
{\mathcal{O}}(k)
terminal vertices. That is,
|S|\leq{\mathcal{O}}(k)
. For a gentle introduction, we first provide a slightly worse quadratic bound on the number of terminals, and then refine it to a linear bound.

4.1
Quadratic Upper Bound on the Number of Terminals

The first reduction rule handles sanity checks; its correctness is self-evident.

Reduction Rule 1

(Sanity Check)
.

Let
(T,S,k)
be an instance of
Subset-FAST-Rev
where
k\leq 0
. If
k=0
and
T
has no
S
-triangle, return a trivial
Yes
instance; otherwise, return a trivial
No
instance.

To reduce the number of terminal vertices, we further remove irrelevant
S
-vertices from the graph. The following reduction rule is designed to accomplish this task.

Reduction Rule 2

(Irrelevant Vertex Rule 1)
.

Let
(T,S,k)
be an instance of
Subset-FAST-Rev
. If there is a vertex
s\in S
such that
s
does not participate in any triangle in
T
, then delete
s
from
T
. The resulting instance is
(T^{\prime}=T-s,S\setminus\{s\},k).

It is easy to verify that
Reduction Rule

2
can be applied in polynomial time. In the following, we prove the correctness of it.

Lemma 8
.

Reduction Rule

2
is sound.

Proof.

The key aspect of the proof is that the sets of
S
-cycles in
T
and
T^{\prime}
are identical.

In the forward direction, let
(T,S,k)
be a
Yes
instance of
Subset-FAST-Rev
and
F\subseteq A(T)
be a solution of size at most
k
. Clearly,
F^{\prime}=F\setminus{\mathsf{arc}}(s)
is a solution for
(T^{\prime}=T-s,S\setminus\{s\},k)
of size at most
k
, where
{\mathsf{arc}}(s)\subseteq A(T)
is the set of arcs incident to the vertex
s
in
T
.

In the reverse direction, let
(T-s,S\setminus\{s\},k)
be a
Yes
instance of
Subset-FAST-Rev
and
\widetilde{F}\subseteq A(T)
be a minimal solution of size at most
k
. We claim that,
\widetilde{F}
is also a solution of size at most
k
for
(T,S,k)
. Assume towards contradiction that there exists a
S
-triangle in
T\circledast\widetilde{F}
. Since
\widetilde{F}
was a solution for
(T^{\prime},S\setminus\{s\},k)
, the triangle must contain the vertex
s
. Let
\triangle
be such a triangle with the vertices
\{s,p,q\}
and arcs
\{(s,p),(p,q),(q,s)\}
. Now if
(p,q)\notin\widetilde{F}
we obtain a
S
-triangle in
T
with vertices
\{s,p,q\}
, a contradiction to the fact that
s
does not participate in any triangle in
T
. Else
(q,p)\in\widetilde{F}
. As
\widetilde{F}
is minimal solution there is a
S
-cycle
C
in
T-s
, such that
A(C)\cap\widetilde{F}=\{(q,p)\}
. That means there is a path from
p
to
q
in
T
containing the arcs
A(C)\setminus(q,p)
. As
(s,p),(q,s)\in A(T)
, we obtain a
S
-cycle
C_{1}
in
T-\widetilde{F}
where
s\in V(C_{1})
. By
Observation

4
,
T
has a
S
-triangle containing the vertex
s
, a contradiction.
∎

The next reduction rule is inspired by a similar rule for
FAST
, which states that if an arc is contained in at least
k+1
triangles, we must reverse this arc and reduce the budget by one
[
12
, Theorem
2.8
]
.

Reduction Rule 3

(Many
S
-triangles Rule)
.

Let
(T,S,k)
be an instance of
Subset-FAST-Rev
. Let
e\in A(T)
be an arc in
T
such that
e
participates in
k+1

S
-triangles, then reverse the arc
e
. The resulting instance is
(T\circledast e,S,k-1)

See Figure
3
for an illustration of Reduction Rule
3
.

Figure 3:
Illustration of
Reduction Rule

3
(Many
S
-triangle). Vertices with color red are from
S
.

It is easy to verify that
Reduction Rule

3
can be applied in polynomial time. In the following, we prove the correctness of it.

Lemma 9
.

Reduction Rule

3
is sound.

Proof.

In the forward direction, let
(T,S,k)
be a
Yes
instance of
Subset-FAST-Rev
and
F\subseteq A(T)
be a solution of size at most
k
. So
T\circledast F
does not contain any
S
-triangle. Observe that
F
must include the arc
e
. If it does not, then to hit all the
(k+1)

S
-triangles that contain the arc
e
, we need to reverse at least
k+1
arcs, which is not feasible since we are allowed to reverse at most
k
arcs. We claim that
F^{\prime}=F\setminus\{e\}
is a solution of size at most
k-1
for
(T^{\prime}=T\circledast e,S,k-1)
. Notice that
T\circledast F
and
T^{\prime}\circledast F^{\prime}
are the same tournaments. So, if
T^{\prime}\circledast F^{\prime}
has a
S
-triangle, then that will imply that
T\circledast F
also has a
S
-triangle which contradicts that
F
is a solution for
(T,S,k)
.

In the reverse direction, let
(T^{\prime}=T\circledast e,S,k-1)
be a
Yes
instance of
Subset-FAST-Rev
and
\widetilde{F}
be a solution of size at most
k-1
. Consider
\widetilde{F}^{\prime}=\widetilde{F}\cup\{e\}
. We claim that
\widetilde{F}^{\prime}
is a solution of size at most
k
for
(T,S,k)
.
Now note that
T^{\prime}\circledast\widetilde{F}
and
T\circledast\widetilde{F}^{\prime}
are the same tournaments. So, if
T\circledast\widetilde{F}^{\prime}
has a
S
-triangle, then that will imply that
T^{\prime}\circledast\widetilde{F}
also has a
S
-triangle which contradicts that
\widetilde{F}
is a solution for
(T^{\prime},S,k-1)
.
∎

Reduction Rule 4

(Bounded Terminal Rule)
.

Let
(T,S,k)
be an instance where none of the Reduction Rules
1
-
3
are applicable. If
|S|\geq(k+1)^{2}
, then return a trivial
No
instance.

It is easy to verify that
Reduction Rule

4
can be applied in polynomial time. In the following, we prove the correctness of it.

Lemma 10
.

Reduction Rule

4
is sound.

Proof.

Let
(T,S,k)
be a
Yes
instance for
Subset-FAST-Rev
where none of the Reduction Rules
1
-
3
are applicable. Let
F\subseteq A(T)
be a solution of size at most
k
. Let
V(F)
be the set of vertices that are incident to at least one of the arcs of
F
. Clearly
|V(F)|\leq 2k
. Now, consider
V(T)\setminus V(F)
. Since
Reduction Rule

2
is not applicable to
(T,S,k)
, every
S
-vertex in
V(T)\setminus V(F)
participates in some
S
-triangle. Moreover, since
F
is a solution, one of the arcs of that
S
-triangle must be in
F
. Since
Reduction Rule

3
is not applicable to
(T,S,k)
, it follows that for every arc
e\in F
, there are at most
k

S
-vertices that are involved in

-triangles where

serves as a common arc and the other arcs are disjoint. So, the number of such
S
-vertices in
V(T)\setminus V(F)
is upper bounded by
k^{2}
. So, the overall number of
S
-vertices is bounded by
k^{2}+2k=(k+1)^{2}-1
.
∎

Let
(T,S,k)
be an instance where none of the Reduction Rules
1
-
4
are applicable. This immediately bounds the number of
S
-vertices in
(T,S,k)
. We formalize it in the following Lemma.

Lemma 11
.

Let
(T,S,k)
be an instance where none of the Reduction Rules
1
-
4
are applicable. Then the number of terminals, that is,
|S|
is upper bounded by
(k+1)^{2}
.

4.2
Linear Upper Bound on the Number of Terminals

In this section, we aim to improve the bound of
|S|
from quadratic to linear, i.e.,
{\mathcal{O}}(k^{2})
to
{\mathcal{O}}(k)
. Our procedure for establishing a linear upper bound on the number of terminals is inspired by the
4k+1
vertex kernel for
FAST
obtained by Paul, Perez and Thomassé
[
29
]
, as well as the earlier linear kernel of size
(2+\epsilon)k
developed by Bessy et. al.
[
5
]
. For consistency with the literature, we adopt the notation used in
[
29
]
.

Consider an instance
(T,S,k)
of
Subset-FAST-Rev
such that none of the Reduction Rules
1
-
4
are applicable to the instance. By
Lemma

11
, we know that
|S|
is upper bounded by
(k+1)^{2}
. If
|S|\leq 4k
, then we are already done. So from now onwards we assume that
|S|\geq 4k+1
.

We use
T_{\sigma}=(V,A,\sigma)
to denote a tournament whose vertices are arranged according to a fixed ordering
\sigma=v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
on
V(T)
. We say that an arc
(v_{i},v_{j})
of
T_{\sigma}
is a
backward
arc if
i>j
, otherwise
forward
. We say that an arc
(v_{j},v_{i})
of
T_{\sigma}
is a
S
-backward arc
if
j>i
and either of (i), (ii) holds: (i) one of
v_{i},v_{j}
is a
S
-vertex, (ii) there exists a
q
such that
i<q<j
such that
v_{q}\in S
.

A partition
P=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{\ell}\}
of
V
is an ordered partition of an ordered tournament
T_{\sigma}
if for every
i\in[\ell]
,
V_{i}
is a set of consecutive vertices (interval) in
\sigma
. Hereafter, an ordered partition of
T_{\sigma}
will be denoted by
P_{\sigma}=(V_{1},\ldots,V_{\ell})
, with the assumption that for every
x\in V_{i}
and
y\in V_{j}
where
1\leq i<j\leq\ell
, it holds that
x<_{\sigma}y
. We call all arcs between the intervals (having their
endpoints in different intervals) as external arcs of
P_{\sigma}
and denote them by
A_{B}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})
, i.e.,
A_{B}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})=\{(u,v)~|~u\in V_{i},v\in V_{j},i\neq j\}
. Let
A_{I}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})=A(T)\setminus A_{B}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})
. See
Figure

4
for an illustration of an ordered partition
P_{\sigma}
.

Figure 4:
Illustration of an ordered partition
P_{\sigma}=(V_{1},\ldots,V_{5})
. Red and blue arcs signifies the arc sets
A_{B}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})
and
A_{I}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})
, respectively.

Definition 12

(certificate of an arc)
.

Consider an ordered partition
P_{\sigma}=(V_{1},\ldots,V_{\ell})
of an ordered tournament
T_{\sigma}
and let
f=(v,u)
be a backward arc of
T_{\sigma}
. We refer to a certificate of
f
, denoted by
c(f)
, as
any directed
S
-path from
u
to
v
that utilizes only forward arcs from
A_{B}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})
.

Definition 13

(certification of an arc set)
.

Consider an ordered partition
P_{\sigma}=(V_{1},\ldots,V_{\ell})
of an ordered tournament
T_{\sigma}
and let
F
be a set of some backward arcs of
T_{\sigma}
.
We say that we can certify
F
whenever it is possible to find a set
{\mathcal{F}}=\{c(f):f\in F\}
of arc-disjoint certificates for the arcs in
F
.

Definition 14

(safe partition)
.

Consider an ordered partition
P_{\sigma}=(V_{1},\ldots,V_{\ell})
of an ordered tournament
T_{\sigma}
. Let
B_{E}
denote the set of all backward arcs in
A_{B}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})
. For a vertex subset
S\subseteq V(T)
, we say
P_{\sigma}
is a

S
-safe partition if we can certify
B_{E}
.

Let
D_{\sigma}=(V_{\sigma},A)
be an ordered directed graph. We use
\textsf{Sfas}(D_{\sigma},S)
to denote the size of a minimum subset feedback arc set in the directed graph
D_{\sigma}
. Specifically, given a directed graph
D_{\sigma}
, and a vertex subset
S\subseteq V_{\sigma}
, it refers to the cardinality of the smallest
set
F\subseteq A
of arcs whose removal (or reversal) renders
D_{\sigma}

S
-acyclic. Given a tournament, for an arc subset
A^{\prime}\subseteq A[T]
, the directed graph
T[A^{\prime}]
is defined by the subgraph
T
containing the arc set
A^{\prime}
. More specifically,
T[A^{\prime}]=(V(T),A^{\prime})
. For a ease of notation, when the subset is clear to the context, we use
\textsf{Sfas}(D_{\sigma})
instead of
\textsf{Sfas}(D_{\sigma})
.

Lemma 15
.

Consider an ordered partition
P_{\sigma}=(V_{1},\ldots,V_{\ell})
of an ordered tournament
T_{\sigma}
and a terminal set
S\subseteq V(T)
. If
P_{\sigma}
is a
S
-safe partition then,

\textsf{Sfas}(T_{\sigma})=\textsf{Sfas}(T_{\sigma}[A_{I}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})])+\textsf{Sfas}(T_{\sigma}[A_{B}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})])

Moreover, there
exists a minimum sized
S
-feedback arc set of
T_{\sigma}
containing
B_{E}
, where
B_{E}
denote the set of all backward arcs in
A_{B}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})
.

Proof.

Given any bipartition of the arc set
A
into
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, we have

\textsf{Sfas}(T_{\sigma})\geq\textsf{Sfas}(T_{\sigma}[A_{1}])+\textsf{Sfas}(T_{\sigma}[A_{2}]).

Specifically, for the partition of
A
into
A_{I}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})
and
A_{B}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})
, it follows that

\textsf{Sfas}(T_{\sigma})\geq\textsf{Sfas}\big(T_{\sigma}[A_{I}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})]\big)+\textsf{Sfas}\big(T_{\sigma}[A_{B}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})]\big).

Next, we need to show that

\textsf{Sfas}(T_{\sigma})\leq\textsf{Sfas}\big(T_{\sigma}[A_{I}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})]\big)+\textsf{Sfas}\big(T_{\sigma}[A_{B}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})]\big).

This assertion holds because, after reversing all arcs in
B_{E}
, each remaining directed
S
-cycle is contained within
T_{\sigma}[V_{i}]
for some
i\in[\ell]
. In other words, once all arcs in
B_{E}
are reversed, every
S
-cycle is entirely contained within
T_{\sigma}[A_{I}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})]
. Observe that as
P_{\sigma}
is
S
-safe, the set of all backward arcs of
A_{B}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})
, i.e.,
B_{E}
can be certified using only arcs from
A_{B}(T_{\sigma},P_{\sigma})
. This concludes the proof of the first part of the lemma. Essentially, we have shown the existence of a minimum-sized
S
-feedback arc set for
T_{\sigma}
that includes
B_{E}
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Construction of a
\boldsymbol{S}
-safe partition.

Recall that an
S
-triangle is a directed cycle of length three that includes at least one vertex from
S
. It is clear that the number of arc-disjoint
S
-triangles provides a lower bound for the size of the smallest
S
-feedback arc set in a tournament. We illustrate how this set can be utilized to identify a safe partition in polynomial time.

Definition 16

(Conflict
S
-packing)
.

A conflict
S
-packing of a tournament
T
is a maximal collection of arc-disjoint
S
-triangles.

Observe that a conflict
S
-packing can be computed in polynomial time, for instance, by using the following
greedy
algorithm: find an
S
-triangle, delete its arcs, and continue. Given an
S
-conflict packing
\mathcal{C}
of an instance
(T,S,k)
of
Subset-FAST-Rev
, let
V(\mathcal{C})
denote the set of vertices covered by
\mathcal{C}
, and
A(\mathcal{C})
denote the set of all arcs contained in
\mathcal{C}
. See
Figure

5
for an illustration of conflict
S
-packing. The following observation is immediate.

Observation 17
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a conflict
S
-packing of a tournament
T
. If
(T,S,k)
is an
Yes
instance of
Subset-FAST-Rev
, then
|{\mathcal{C}}|\leq k
and
|V({\mathcal{C}})|\leq 3k
.

Illustration of Conflict
S
-packing. Vertices with color red are
S
-vertices.

Lemma 18

(Conflict
S
-packing Lemma)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a conflict
S
-packing of a tournament
T
and let
S_{1}\coloneq S\setminus V(\mathcal{C})
. There exists a vertex ordering
\sigma
of
T
such that
every
S_{1}
-backward arc
(u,v)
of
T_{\sigma}
satisfies
(u,v)\in A({\mathcal{C}})
.

Proof.

Consider the vertex set
\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\}
in
T
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be a conflict
S
-packing of a tournament
T
. We aim to construct a unique vertex ordering
\sigma
of
T
. Define the vertex set
S_{1}\coloneq S\setminus V(\mathcal{C})
. Since
|S|\geq 4k+1
, it follows that
S_{1}\neq\emptyset
. Let
V^{\prime}=V\setminus V(\mathcal{C})
.

By the maximality of
\mathcal{C}
, the induced subgraph
T[V^{\prime}]
is
S
-acyclic. Therefore, there exists a unique topological ordering
\sigma_{S_{1}}
for the vertices of
S_{1}
. For all other vertices in
V^{\prime}
, specifically for each non-
S
-vertex
u
in
V^{\prime}
, we know that
T[S_{1}\cup\{u\}]
is acyclic. Thus, for each non-
S
-vertex
u\in V^{\prime}
, there is a unique pair
p,q
of consecutive
S_{1}
vertices in
\sigma_{S_{1}}
such that inserting
u
between
p
and
q
yields the topological ordering of
T[S_{1}\cup\{u\}]
. In this way we obtain an ordering
\sigma_{V^{\prime}}
of
V^{\prime}
.

Our next aim is to insert the vertices of
V(\mathcal{C})
into the ordering
\sigma_{V^{\prime}}
. Observe that for each
v\in V(\mathcal{C})
,
T[S_{1}\cup\{v\}]
is acyclic; in fact,

5
Irrelevant Vertex for
Subset-FAST

In this section, we introduce reduction rules to eliminate non-terminal vertices. The idea is as follows: Let

be a

, and let

be a solution of size at most

of

in
\Pi

, all but at most
k
of its in-neighbors must appear before

in
\Pi
, and all but at most
k
of its out-neighbors must appear after
s
in
\Pi
. This observation is crucial for identifying the majority of vertices in the interval between two consecutive vertices of

in
\Pi
. We formalize this idea using an equivalence relation.

5.1
Partitioning Non Terminal Vertices into Equivalence Classes

Let
(T,S,k)
be an instance of

where none of the Reduction Rules
1
-
5
are applicable. By

24

.

Observe that each non-

We can then categorize all non-

denote the set of all non-

\texttt{EqvCls}_{T}^{S}[X]\coloneqq\{v\in V(T)\setminus S\mid{\mathtt{Type}}_{T}^{S}(v)=X\}.

We refer to

as

.

Reduction Rule 6
.

Let
(T,S,k)
be an instance where none of the Reduction Rules
1
-
5
are applicable. If the number of non-trivial equivalence classes in

is more than

, then return a trivial

It is easy to verify that

can be applied in polynomial time. In the following, we prove the correctness of it.

Lemma 25
.

Reduction Rule

is sound.

Proof.

Let
(T,S,k)
be a

, and let

. It follows that

Proof.

We only prove the soundness of the

Let

5.3
Linear Bound on the number of Relevant Vertices

In this section, we provide an improved bound for the size of

. Recall that

Z_{{\texttt{Relevant}}}={\sf IN}_{Z^{\prime}}(R_{<})\cup{\sf OUT}_{Z^{\prime}}(R_{>})\cup{\sf IN}_{Z^{\prime}}(\widetilde{Z}_{-})\cup{\sf OUT}_{Z^{\prime}}(\widetilde{Z}_{+})

is a subset of vertices in

. Let

.

One reason why the size of

is that each vertex in
\widetilde{Z}_{-}
could have

. Since

contains

|{\sf IN}_{Z^{\prime}}(\widetilde{Z}_{-})|\leq 2k^{2}+k
. We keep all these in-neighbors in

because they validate the redundancy of the vertices in

. However, the redundancy of a vertex in

depends on the

and
number of out-neighbors
in

, not on the actual neighbors themselves. We leverage this observation and apply an “arc swapping argument” (see
Figure

10
for an illustration) to obtain an equivalent instance in which, for any vertex in
Z_{{\texttt{Irrelevant}}}
, the number of in-neighbors and number of out-neighbors in

remains the same, but the size of

is reduced to

.

Lemma 35

(Arc Swapping Lemma)
.

Let
z\notin Z_{{\texttt{Relevant}}}
be any non-
S
vertex such that there exist two vertices
p,q\in Z_{{\texttt{Relevant}}}
such that
P=\{(z,p),(q,z)\}\subseteq A(T)
then
(T,S,k)
is a
Yes
instance if and only if
(T^{\prime}=T\circledast P,S,k)
is a
Yes
instance.

Proof.

In the forward direction, let
(T,S,k)
be a
Yes
instance and let
F
be a minimal solution of size at most
k
. Now we make a case distinction as follows.

Case 1:
(z,p),~(q,z)\in F
:

In this case, we will show that
F^{\prime}=F\setminus P
is a solution of size at most
k-2
for
(T^{\prime},S,k)
. Notice that
T\circledast F
and
T^{\prime}\circledast F^{\prime}
are the same tournaments. So, if
T^{\prime}\circledast F^{\prime}
has a
S
-triangle, then that will imply that
T\circledast F
also has a
S
-triangle which contradicts that
F
is a solution for
(T,S,k)
.

Case 2:
(z,p)\in F,~(q,z)\notin F
:

We only consider this case since the case
(z,p)\notin F,~(q,z)\in F
is symmetric. Consider
F^{\prime}=F\setminus(z,p)\cup{\mathsf{rev}}(q,z)
. We claim that
F^{\prime}
is a solution of size at most
k
for
(T^{\prime},S,k)
. Notice that the tournaments
T\circledast F
and
T^{\prime}\circledast F^{\prime}
are the same tournaments. Hence it directly follows that any
S
-triangle in
T^{\prime}\circledast F^{\prime}
will directly imply having the same
S
-triangle in
T\circledast F
which contradicts that
F
is a solution for
(T,S,k)
.

Case 3:
(z,p)\notin F,~(q,z)\notin F
:

In this case, we claim that
F
is also a solution of size at most
k
for
(T^{\prime},S,k)
. Suppose not, this implies that
T^{\prime}\circledast F
has a
S
triangle,
\triangle
. Since
T\circledast F
did not have any
S
-triangle, this implies that
\triangle
must contain at least one of the arcs
(p,z)
and
(z,q)
(reversed arcs corresponding to
(z,p)
and
(q,z)
, respectively). Without loss of generality assume that
(p,z)\in\triangle
(proof for the case when
(z,q)\in\triangle
is identical).

When
(p,z)\in\triangle
:

Since
z,p\notin S
, let the third vertex of
\triangle
be
s\in S
and the arcs of
\triangle
be
(p,z),(z,s)
and
(s,p)
. Since
|Z|>7k+4
and
p\in Z_{{\texttt{Relevant}}}\subseteq Z^{\prime}
, the arc
(s,p)
is a
sure arc
in
(T,S,k)
(by
Observation

31
). Moreover since
p\in Z^{\prime}
and for every
s^{\prime}\in S_{2}
, the arc
(p,s^{\prime})
is a
sure arc
in
(T,S,k)
(by
Observation

31
), this implies that
s\notin S_{2}
. So we assume that
s\in S_{1}
. Now consider
T\circledast F
. Since
q\in Z^{\prime}
, the arc
(s,q)
is also a
sure arc
in
(T,S,k)
(by
Observation

31
) and hence
F
avoids this arc in the instance
(T,S,k)
.
T\circledast F
has the arc
(q,z)
since
(q,z)\notin F
. Consider the arc
(z,s)
. If
(z,s)\notin F
, then we found a triangle
(s,q),(q,z)
and
(z,s)
in
T\circledast F
which contradicts that
F
is a solution for
(T,S,k)
. Let’s assume that
(z,s)\in F
. Since
F
is a minimal solution, there must exist a cycle
C
such that
{\mathsf{arc}}(C)\cap F=\{(z,s)\}
. Now, consider a closed walk

that traces the triangle formed by the arcs
(s,q),(q,z),
and
(z,s)
. The arcs
(s,q)
and
(q,z)
remain the same in

(z,s)
, we traverse the path

from the corresponding cycle of
(z,s)
. By definition,

is a closed walk, and notably, it contains the vertex

while avoiding all arcs from

Lemma

3
, we find a triangle containing

In the reverse direction, note that we apply the same operation on the instance
(T^{\prime},S,k)
to transform it back to

. Thus, the proof for the reverse direction mirrors the proof for the forward direction.
∎

The next lemma utilizes the Arc Swapping Lemma (

Lemma 36
.

Given an instance

We assume that the size of

Conclusion

In this paper, we developed the first quadratic vertex kernel for the
Subset-FAST
problem. Our kernel used variants of the most well-known reduction rules for
FAST
and introduced two new reduction rules
to identify irrelevant vertices. We believe that the methodology adopted for our kernelization algorithm will also be useful for other problems.

A non-trivial linear vertex kernel has been established for
FAST
. It would be interesting to design a linear vertex kernel for
Subset-FAST
that aligns with the bound of
FAST
or to provide a lower bound based on some established conjectures. The same question is also open for
Subset-FVST
.
