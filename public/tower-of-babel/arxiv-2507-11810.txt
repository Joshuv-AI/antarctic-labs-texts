The Evolving Role of Large Language Models in Scientific Innovation: Evaluator, Collaborator, and Scientist
Haoxuan Zhang Ruochi Li Yang Zhang Ting Xiao Jiangping Chen Junhua Ding Haihua Chen[5pt] ∗ \ast Core contributor, † \dagger Corresponding author

Abstract
Scientific innovation is undergoing a paradigm shift driven by the rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs). As science faces mounting challenges including information overload, disciplinary silos, and diminishing returns on conventional research methods, LLMs are emerging as powerful agents capable not only of enhancing scientific workflows but also of participating in and potentially leading the innovation process. Existing surveys mainly focus on different perspectives, phrases, and tasks in scientific research and discovery, while they have limitations in understanding the transformative potential and role differentiation of LLM. This survey proposes a comprehensive framework to categorize the evolving roles of LLMs in scientific innovation across three hierarchical levels: Evaluator, Collaborator, and Scientist. We distinguish between LLMs’ contributions to structured scientific research processes and open-ended scientific discovery, thereby offering a unified taxonomy that clarifies capability boundaries, evaluation criteria, and human-AI interaction patterns at each level. Through an extensive analysis of current methodologies, benchmarks, systems, and evaluation metrics, this survey delivers an in-depth and systematic synthesis on LLM-driven scientific innovation. We present LLMs not only as tools for automating existing processes, but also as catalysts capable of reshaping the epistemological foundations of science itself. This survey offers conceptual clarity, practical guidance, and theoretical foundations for future research, while also highlighting open challenges and ethical considerations in the pursuit of increasingly autonomous AI-driven science. The Need for LLMs in Scientific Innovation

Scientific innovation comprises both scientific research and scientific discovery, each with distinct but complementary processes. As depicted in Figure

Trained in a vast corpus of scientific literature, LLMs possess the ability to process not only plain text but also multimodal scientific data such as molecular structures, protein sequences, and images. Although existing surveys have made valuable contributions by examining specific aspects of LLM-driven research, they often provide fragmented perspectives that fail to capture the holistic transformation occurring in scientific innovation. Most critically, current taxonomies conflate scientific research, which focuses on automating existing workflows to improve efficiency, with scientific discovery, which emphasizes the generation of breakthrough insights and the advance of fundamental understanding. This conflation leads to imprecise categorizations and inadequate evaluation systems that obscure the unique challenges, methodological requirements, and capability boundaries inherent to each domain.

To address these limitations, we propose a pyramidal framework that organizes the roles of LLMs in scientific innovation at three hierarchical levels:
Evaluator
,
Collaborator
, and
Scientist
as shown in Figure
3
. This framework is designed on the principle that LLMs’ capabilities in scientific innovation can be understood through three fundamental dimensions: autonomy level, task complexity, and level of human-LLMs collaboration. The pyramidal structure captures the progressive evolution from passive knowledge synthesis (evaluator) to active research partnership (collaborator) to autonomous scientific discovery (scientist), with each level representing increasing independence, sophistication, and transformative potential.

Figure 3:
The pyramidal framework of large language models’ roles in scientific innovation: evaluators, collaborators, scientists.

The pyramidal framework offers several critical advantages for understanding LLM-driven scientific innovation. First, it provides a unified conceptual structure that integrates autonomy level, task complexity, and human-LLMs cooperation patterns into a coherent taxonomy, enabling systematic comparison across different applications and domains. Second, it establishes clear capability boundaries and progression pathways, helping researchers identify current limitations and future development trajectories for each role. Third, it distinguishes between scientific research and discovery processes, allowing for more precise evaluation criteria and methodological frameworks tailored to each domain’s unique requirements. Fourth, the hierarchical organization facilitates systematic analysis of the evolving relationship between human researchers and AI systems, from tool-based assistance to collaborative partnership to autonomous operation. Specifically, this survey aims to: (1) provide comprehensive systematic analysis to date of LLM-driven scientific innovation, encompassing detailed benchmarks, principled algorithm categorization, task complexity hierarchies, and multidimensional evaluation across all aspects of the scientific discovery process; (2) establish a theoretical foundation for understanding the progression from human-directed AI assistance to autonomous AI-driven discovery, with clear delineation of capabilities and limitations at each level; (3) synthesize methodological advances, evaluation frameworks, and empirical findings across the three-role taxonomy to identify gaps, challenges, and opportunities for future research; and (4) guide the scientific community toward responsible development and deployment of increasingly autonomous AI systems in research contexts.

2
Different Roles of LLMs in Scientific Innovation

Scientific innovation entails converting new ideas into functional applications that achieve quantifiable advancement in technological, methodological, economic, and social spaces. Three notions encapsulate the essence of scientific innovation. First, novelty is the proviso - new thinking or hypotheses that scientists formulate on research problems
[
174
]
. Second, discovery generates necessary raw materials by unveiling hidden new phenomena or mechanisms
[
175
]
. Third, paradigm disruption follows as the final outcome, wherein several innovations collectively transform science paradigms
[
194
]
.

LLMs introduce a transformative human-AI collaboration paradigm for scientific innovation. Five steps toward Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) are outlined by OpenAI
[
30
]
. These phases are chatbots (level 1), like ChatGPT, which are best suited for natural language interactions; reasoners (level 2), which have the ability to solve compound problems with expert-level proficiency and augment reasoning abilities; agents (level 3), which are autonomous and respond adaptively from environmental inputs; innovators (level 4), which have the potential to come up with innovative ideas and solutions in various fields; and organizations (level 5), where AI functions as an integrated system to facilitate organizational goals.
Morris et al. 2023
offer a more refined framework by establishing five levels of autonomy for AI. At the most basic level, AI as a tool involves human oversight, with AI automating routine subtasks. AI as a consultant assumes a substantive but reactive role, engaged only when requested by humans. AI as a collaborator enables an equal partnership between humans and AI, with interactive coordination of goals and tasks. AI as an expert takes the lead in interactions, while humans provide guidance or perform supporting tasks. At the highest level, AI as an agent operates fully autonomously. In the context of scientific research,
Gao et al. 2024a
introduce three emerging roles of AI in terms of levels of autonomy, hypothesis creation, experimental planning, reasoning steps, and extent of human-AI collaboration. These are the roles of AI agents as assistants, AI agents as collaborators, and AI agents as scientists. From a mission to enhance productivity and objectivity in scientific studies by circumventing human limitations,
Messeri and Crockett 2024
present four AI visions grounded on enhancing human scientists’ capacities along the research process: oracle, surrogate, quant, and arbiter reflecting stages of research design, data collection, data analysis, and peer reviewing respectively. Driven by the paradigm shift in scientific discovery brought about by LLMs,
Zheng et al. 2025
propose three levels of LLM autonomy in scientific discovery: tool, analyst, and scientist. At the tool level, LLMs enhance research efficiency through task automation; at the analyst level, they demonstrate significant autonomy in data modeling and insight generation; at the scientist level, they achieve independent exploration and discovery throughout key research stages. Meanwhile,
Zhang et al. 2025a
argue that LLMs introduce a scaling law to scientific discovery and advance an AGS framework that classifies AI-driven robotic systems into six levels of capability and autonomy, ranging from no AI involvement to fully autonomous leadership surpassing human scientists. This model spans from traditional methods devoid of AI to the ultimate stage, where artificial superintelligent robots autonomously develop new scientific paradigms without technological or ethical constraints.

In view of the increasing autonomy and research participation of LLMs, as well as the urgent need to clarify the evolving relationship between human researchers and AI in scientific innovation, this study proposes a pyramidal framework for the roles of LLMs in scientific innovation, as illustrated in Figure
4
and Table
2
. By differentiating their functions, primary tasks, and modes of human–AI interaction, LLMs are categorized as
evaluators
,
collaborators
, and
scientists
. As evaluators, LLMs synthesize, reason over, and evaluate scientific knowledge, assisting researchers in meeting their individual information needs amidst the rapid growth of scientific publications. By providing objective and comprehensive literature analyses, LLMs also help minimize personal biases. As active research collaborators, LLMs support humans in idea generation, hypothesis formulation, and the design, optimization, and execution of experiments. The synergy between the analytical breadth and pattern recognition of LLMs and the intuition and domain expertise of human researchers drives co-creation in scientific innovation. As autonomous scientists, LLMs can independently automate end-to-end scientific workflows and, beyond that, propel scientific innovation beyond traditional human capabilities, opening possibilities for paradigm-shifting advances. A systematic analysis of LLMs’ evolution from passive tools, to active collaborators, to autonomous scientists—including the associated datasets, algorithms, evaluation metrics, and outcomes—enables researchers to understand what LLMs are currently capable of, what they may achieve in the future, and the distinct challenges, opportunities, and safety risks encountered at each stage.

LLMs as Evaluators

3.1
Task Definition

Scientific progress not only depends on generating new ideas but also on consolidating what the community already knows and judging the reliability and value of each new contribution
[
93
,
203
]
. As illustrated in Figure
5
, LLMs can assume the role of an
evaluator
by operating on three tightly coupled layers:

Figure 5:
Closed-loop workflow of LLMs as Evaluators. Multimodal embeddings underpin SKS (blue) and SLQA (green), whose outputs mutually reinforce each other and update the shared database.

1.

Multimodal Scientific Literature Embedding
: raw papers—text, tables, figures, and their citation links—are encoded into a shared vector space
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. High-quality embeddings guarantee that the subsequent reasoning and appraisal stages have access to all relevant evidence
[
51
,
57
]
.

2.

Scientific Knowledge Synthesis (SKS)
: an LLM reads the embedding
z
and restructures dispersed facts into machine-readable knowledge units
\hat{\mathcal{K}}
(triples, mini-graphs, structured summaries). We formalise this consolidation as

\mathcal{{LLM}_{\mathcal{SKS}}}(z)\;\longrightarrow\;\hat{\mathcal{K}},

(1)

where the model is guided by citation context and domain prompts to balance explicit extraction with implicit reasoning
[
124
]
.

3.

Scientific Literature Quality Assessment (SLQA)
: a second LLM head combines the same embedding with the synthesised evidence to deliver a multi-aspect verdict
\hat{\mathbf{q}}
covering
Soundness
,
Clarity
,
Novelty
, and
Impact
:

\mathcal{{LLM}}_{\mathcal{SLQA}}\bigl(z,\hat{\mathcal{K}}\bigr)\;\longrightarrow\;\hat{\mathbf{q}}.

(2)

The presence of
\hat{\mathcal{K}}
makes every score traceable to concrete claims, reducing both hallucination and subjective bias
[
93
]
.

SKS addresses the
descriptive question
—
What has been discovered?
—while SLQA tackles the
normative question
—
How good or important is it?
Keeping the objectives separate prevents factual inference from being entangled with value judgement, yet the tasks remain inter-dependent: richer knowledge graphs furnish transparent evidence for appraisal, and calibrated quality scores steer future synthesis towards high-impact literature. Together with robust multimodal embeddings, this triadic loop enables LLMs to act as scalable, auditable evaluators that accelerate reliable scientific innovation.

Critical Perspective and Future Dataset Design.

Taken together, the above reasoning benchmarks delineate a graded evaluation ladder that spans from elementary multimodal QA to expert-level, long-context scientific problem solving. Large-scale resources such as ScienceQA and SPIQA ensure statistical reliability but rely heavily on multiple-choice formats that cannot expose reasoning chains in depth, while smaller expert suites such as CURIE or GPQA deliver authentic research difficulty at the cost of coverage and annotation expense. Cross–disciplinary sets (e.g., SciEval, OlympicArena) probe transfer and abstraction, yet inevitably sacrifice domain granularity; conversely, narrowly scoped collections (e.g., MathVista, MicroVQA) offer fine-grained diagnostics but risk overfitting to siloed skills. When selecting a benchmark, we therefore recommend a three–step fit analysis: scenario alignment—match the cognitive target (basic comprehension vs. graduate reasoning); modality necessity—choose text–only data for pure symbolic reasoning or multimodal suites for vision–language pipelines; and granularity budget—employ large MCQ sets for coarse screening, then refine with open-ended expert tasks for error attribution. New benchmarks should integrate executable outputs (code, equations) alongside natural language to measure verifiable reasoning, embed the full scientific workflow (hypothesis → experiment → claim) to evaluate process fidelity rather than answer accuracy alone, and adopt semi-automatic annotation pipelines blended with expert auditing to scale difficulty without prohibitive cost. Such directions will close the current gap between benchmarked capability and real-world scientific practice.

Critical Perspective and Future Dataset Design.

The surveyed review–generation benchmarks collectively chart a continuum from query-focused abstractive summarization (SciReviewGen) to hierarchical catalogue construction (HiCaD) and table-centric synthesis (arxivDIGESTables). Large corpora with noisy abstracts (e.g., BigSurvey) are ideal for training fluency and content coverage, yet they obscure structural coherence and factual grounding; high-precision datasets with expert-validated hierarchies (e.g., CHIME) capture domain rigor but remain limited to biomedicine. Although SurveyBench introduces unified metrics (SAM-R/O/C), cross-benchmark comparability is still hampered by heterogeneous annotation schemes and reference styles. Practitioners should thus first pinpoint the output form required—narrative review, taxonomy outline, or comparative table—before choosing between breadth-centric (BigSurvey), structure-centric (HiCaD/CHIME), or retrieval-stress (arXiv2Table) testbeds. Future datasets ought to align ground-truth citations to every generated claim to discourage hallucinations, capture longitudinal topic drift so that models must track concept evolution, and provide human preference judgments over multiple candidate surveys to enable explicit reward modeling. These improvements will sharpen evaluation fidelity and advance automated, trustworthy scholarly synthesis.

3.2.2
Algorithms

(1) Scientific Knowledge Reasoning

Scientific innovation largely depends on the ability to synthesize complex information, apply specialized knowledge, and conduct advanced reasoning across different domains. Scientific knowledge reasoning constitutes the cornerstone of this process
[
22
]
. Compared to knowledge bases, LLMs are considered more suitable for scientific knowledge reasoning despite their inherent susceptibility to hallucinations
[
172
]
. Based on knowledge types, we categorize LLM-based scientific knowledge reasoning into explicit knowledge extraction and implicit knowledge reasoning, while also exploring the explainability of knowledge reasoning
[
21
]
.

Explicit Scientific Knowledge Extraction.
Scientific literature contains directly visible structured information requiring systematic extraction. This process divides into fine-grained knowledge extraction and key-insights extraction.

Fine-grained knowledge extraction retrieves specific entities and relationships from scientific text. Natural language processing represents the most extensively explored domain for fine-grained extraction. This includes the extraction of models, evaluation metrics, datasets, and materials, with relationships encompassing co-occurrence and semantic connections
[
66
,
219
]
. In biomedical knowledge extraction,
Hope et al. 2020
develops methods for COVID-19 research exploration through automatic extraction of biomedical concepts. This approach employs S2ORC-BERT and RoBERTa models to recognize entities and compute semantic similarities. The system connects genes, drugs, and diseases while mapping collaboration networks across the CORD-19 dataset. Extending this work,
Zhang et al. 2021
introduces an integrated framework combining semantic graphs with knowledge graphs. This approach addresses long-distance dependencies and domain-specific knowledge challenges in biomedical extraction. The model compresses sentences into semantic graphs using Abstract Meaning Representation while enriching context through knowledge graphs.
Wei et al. 2024
advances this direction with PubTator 3.0, an AI-powered platform for semantic searches across biomedical texts. The system leverages transformers like AIONER and LinkBERT to recognize entities and extract relationships. This creates a precomputed knowledge graph enabling efficient queries. In materials science,
Dagdelen et al. 2024
implements a generalizable framework using fine-tuned LLMs. This approach jointly extracts named entities and relationships at both sentence and document levels. The framework constructs structured knowledge bases from domain literature using GPT-3 and LLaMA-2 models.

Key-insights extraction identifies valuable research information within scientific articles. This includes research problems, methods, evaluations, results, limitations, and contributions
[
216
,
160
]
.
Lahav et al. 2021
focuses specifically on extracting scientific challenges and research directions. Their method uses a PubMedBERT-trained classifier to extract high-confidence challenges from the CORD-19 dataset. Users can retrieve scientific directions through context-based search inputs. Complementing this approach,
La Quatra and Cagliero 2022
develops THExt, a transformer-based model generating highlight sentences from academic papers. The model employs section-level contextualization to identify key insights.
Chao et al. 2023
proposes SciContriExt, an extractive approach for scientific contributions. This model defines 8 contribution types and 15 fine-grained arguments through a keyword-attention fusion mechanism. For comprehensive scientific workflow extraction,
Zhang et al. 2024c
construct the MASSW dataset. This resource contains over 152,000 structured scientific workflows from 17 prestigious computer science conferences (1969-2024). Using GPT-4, MASSW systematically organizes five components: Context, Key Idea, Method, Outcome, and Projected Impact. This structured benchmark facilitates efficient navigation of scientific innovation and accelerates methodology replication.
Song et al. 2025
extends these approaches by using multiple fine-tuned open-source LLMs. This method automatically extracts comprehensive key information including aim, motivation, methods, metrics, findings, contributions, limitations, and future work.

Implicit Scientific Knowledge Reasoning.
Unlike explicit knowledge extraction, implicit scientific knowledge reasoning requires sophisticated multi-hop inference processes that apply domain-specific reasoning principles to derive unstated conclusions from given information. This paradigm demands models to understand intricate scientific concepts, navigate multi-layered logical dependencies, and generate interpretable explanations while maintaining factual accuracy. Current algorithms for implicit scientific knowledge reasoning can be categorized into three fundamental approaches: Chain-of-Thought reasoning, tool-augmented reasoning, and multimodal knowledge-enhanced reasoning.

Chain-of-Thought reasoning establishes the foundational paradigm for implicit knowledge reasoning by enhancing language models’ reasoning capabilities through step-by-step intermediate reasoning generation
[
192
]
.
Jin et al. 2025
introduce explicit thought chain generation through language models that produce lectures and explanations as intermediate reasoning steps for science question answering.
Zhang et al. 2024a
advance this paradigm through a self-reflective instruction annotation framework employing a critic-and-revise mechanism. GPT-4 iteratively generates, analyzes errors, and refines reasoning chains through three sequential stages: initial generation, reflection-based revision, and answer-hint integration. Complex pipelines have also been proposed.
Zheng et al. 2023
present a four-stage LLM4SD pipeline encompassing knowledge synthesis from scientific literature, knowledge inference from data, interpretable model training, and explanation generation.
Zhong et al. 2023
develop a dual-component D5 system with hypothesis proposer utilizing goal-conditioned prompting and hypothesis validator employing automated validity assessment. Furthermore, based on curated reasoning samples for training reasoning models,
Prabhakar et al. 2025
implement a three-step algorithmic pipeline comprising Domain Adaptive Pretraining on curated scientific corpora, Supervised Fine-Tuning with instruction alignment, and reasoning-based knowledge distillation to enhance logical synthesis capabilities.

Tool-augmented reasoning creates modular frameworks for implicit scientific knowledge reasoning, positioning LLMs as proficient tool-users capable of extending domain applicability rather than omniscient problem solvers.
Wang et al. 2024b
introduce the LCE (Language, Code, Execution) framework that seamlessly interleaves natural language reasoning with code generation and real-time execution feedback through special token demarcation.
Ma et al. 2024b
propose a four-module algorithmic architecture comprising Planning, Retrieval, Action, and Execution components, where domain-specific toolsets are dynamically retrieved and executed based on problem requirements.
Skarlinski et al. 2024
develop an agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) algorithm that decomposes retrieval and response generation into specialized tools including Paper Search, Gather Evidence, and Generate Answer, enabling iterative search refinement through citation graph traversal and contextual summarization.

Multimodal knowledge-enhanced reasoning integrates knowledge from diverse modalities including text, images, and structured knowledge networks to provide rich contextual background and verifiable traceability for implicit knowledge reasoning. Scientific papers extensively utilize tables and figures to communicate complex information
[
57
]
. These elements contain implicit knowledge requiring multimodal fusion for effective reasoning.
Zhang et al. 2023b
propose a two-stage Multimodal-CoT framework separating rationale generation from answer inference to effectively leverage multimodal information.
Wang et al. 2024c
develop T-SciQ with dual teaching signal generation algorithms combining QA-CoT for simple problems and QA-PCoT for complex multi-step reasoning through strategic data mixing optimization.
Jin et al. 2025
introduce HeGTa, employing Heterogeneous Graph Neural Networks to model complex table relationships through four node types and heuristic edge creation rules, integrated with LLMs via soft prompt injection for few-shot complex table understanding.
Mondal et al. 2024
propose KAM-CoT that decomposes complex problems into multiple intermediate steps through Chain-of-Thought reasoning, separately encodes text, images, and Knowledge Graphs and fuses them into unified feature sets for comprehensive task understanding, finally providing accurate and reasonable answers through a two-stage approach of rationale generation and answer inference.

Critical Perspective and Future Algorithm Design.

Present methods form a complementary spectrum. Explicit extraction pipelines (e.g., PubTator 3.0, MASSW) deliver high-precision, auditable triples and are easily aggregated into knowledge graphs, yet they depend on costly annotation, propagate NER/RE errors, and cannot surface latent mechanisms. Chain-of-Thought reasoning brings interpretability, but is brittle: Erroneous early steps snowball, and self-critique is still heuristic. Tool-augmented frameworks (LCE, SciAgent) tame hallucination by delegating calculus, retrieval, or code execution to external tools, but orchestration logic is cumbersome and failure modes multiply with tool count. Multimodal reasoning (Multimodal-CoT, KAM-CoT) uniquely captures figure- and table-bound insights, yet training costs and modality alignment remain prohibitive. Algorithm selection should align with task demands: explicit extraction best suits verifiable database construction; Chain-of-Thought methods are preferable for fast, explanatory problem solving; tool-augmented agents fit numerically intensive or data-hungry settings; and multimodal hybrids are recommended for vision-rich corpora. Future work should fuse extraction and reasoning through retrieval-augmented Chain-of-Thought coupled with automatic verifiers, embed confidence calibration and contradiction checks to halt error propagation, develop plug-and-play tool registries with self-discovery and fallback logic, and design unified scientific embeddings that treat equations, spectra, and graphics as first-class citizens, enabling truly modality-agnostic reasoning.

(2) Scholarly Survey Generation

Literature reviews represent structured approaches to scientific knowledge synthesis. They consolidate and analyze existing research across various domains. These reviews divide into three categories based on purpose, research questions, search strategy, analysis methods, and contributions
[
158
]
. Systematic literature reviews apply explicit methodical approaches to examine evidence addressing specific research questions. Semi-systematic reviews provide topic overviews and track historical development of diversely conceptualized subjects. Integrative literature reviews involve creative, interpretative analysis
[
129
]
. For mature topics, integrative reviews summarize knowledge bases, critically evaluate findings, and expand theoretical foundations. For emerging topics, they develop preliminary conceptualizations and theoretical models. Current LLM-supported survey generation primarily focuses on systematic or semi-systematic reviews. The development of integrative literature reviews based on qualitative analysis remains underdeveloped. LLM-supported literature review automation employs two distinct methodological approaches. The first approach organizes literature into structured formats such as hierarchical structures or layered classifications. This organization facilitates effective review generation. The second approach builds end-to-end systems with modules that simulate human review-writing processes. These processes include literature retrieval, screening, classification, drafting, integration, and evaluation.

Structured Literature Organization Approach.

Kang and Xiong 2024
categorize the use of LLMs in academic literature reviews into three steps: information discovery, information selection, and information organization. Structuring knowledge into hierarchical mind maps highlights key insights and relationships in research topics while improving linguistic coherence.
Hu et al. 2024c
propose HiGTL, a framework integrating Graph Neural Networks and LLMs. It generates hierarchical taxonomies from citation graphs by clustering papers based on structural and semantic relationships. Using pre-trained LLMs, it provides natural language representations of classification nodes, offering a clear overview of research trends.
Hsu et al. 2024
introduce CHIME, which employs LLMs to organize biomedical research into hierarchical category trees. This system extracts concise claims and frequent entities from abstracts and uses Claude-2 to create topic-specific hierarchies, presenting categories as nodes and studies as leaves, thus enhancing researchers’ ability to navigate and synthesize literature.
Newman et al. 2024
employs LLMs to create literature review tables. These tables synthesize multiple input papers into a structure with patterns (columns) and values. Researchers can effectively compare papers across consistent dimensions. They decompose this complex task into two key components. First, structure generation identifies relevant shared aspects (columns) from input papers. Second, value generation extracts appropriate values for each paper-aspect combination. Furthermore,
Wang et al. 2025a
address real-world literature review scenarios that contain noisy, irrelevant papers and under-specified user demands. They develop an iterative batch-based table generation method. This approach specifically includes using LLMs to extract key information from full-text content, processing papers in batches through random partitioning, and iterative paper selection and schema refinement.

Beyond hierarchical approaches,
Nishimura et al. 2024
develop STRoGeNS, a method for generating multi-paragraph, structured related work sections with citations and novelty statements. It groups literature by research topics and emphasizes the target paper’s novelty, ensuring coherence and informativeness.
Li et al. 2025
argue that simple chain-of-thought methods and model limitations cause LLM-generated summaries to lack comparative analysis, structure, and key elements. To address this, they introduce ChatCite, which mimics human literature comparison workflows. It features a key element extractor using seven guiding questions to identify critical details and a reflective incremental generator with two components: a comparative summarizer for iterative summary generation via comparative analysis, and a reflective evaluator that uses LLMs to vote, screen, and rank candidate papers.
Zhang et al. 2025c
propose Collaborative Knowledge Minigraph Agents (CKMAs) to automate scholarly literature reviews through graph-based approach. This framework employs a Knowledge Minigraph Construction Agent (KMCA) that uses prompt-based algorithms to identify relations between scientific concepts and automatically construct small-scale knowledge graphs specific to the referenced papers. Combined with a Multiple Path Summarization Agent (MPSA) that organizes concepts from different viewpoints using a mixture of experts approach, the system efficiently generates comprehensive literature reviews.

End-to-End Survey Generation Systems.
Recent research focuses on automating traditional manual review processes while addressing challenges of hallucination, knowledge evolution, and domain-specific comprehension. Integrating RAG,
Shi et al. 2023
introduces UR
3
WG, a unified framework for reference retrieval and related work generation. This model employs query expansion with LLMs to capture implicit context and specialized terminology. The framework’s Lexicon-Enhanced Retrieval combines semantic similarity with lexical-level matching, utilizing multi-granularity contrastive learning to enhance retrieval accuracy. Similarly,
Agarwal et al. 2024
develops LitLLM, an open-source toolkit for automatic literature review generation using RAG and LLMs. LitLLM’s modular pipeline includes retrieval, re-ranking, and generation phases, focusing on mitigating hallucinations by grounding the process in retrieved academic papers. The toolkit offers zero-shot and plan-based generation options, enabling customized outputs based on user requirements. Further,
Susnjak et al. 2024
automates the process through domain-specific fine-tuning of LLMs. Their approach encompasses automatic data extraction at multiple levels and introduces unique tokens for tracking source document responses. Their fine-tuned Mistral-7B models, particularly those utilizing NEFTune and LoRA techniques, demonstrate superior performance in factual verification compared to pure RAG approaches.

End-to-end literature review generation systems continue to emerge.
Wang et al. 2024f
creates AutoSurvey, addressing challenges of extensive research volumes and rapid publication rates. To overcome context window limitations, AutoSurvey employs a four-stage pipeline: initial retrieval and outline generation, parallel subsection drafting, integration, and multi-model evaluation. The system generates high-quality surveys 1000 times faster than human authors while maintaining comparable content quality.
Liang et al. 2025
introduces SurveyX with a two-phase pipeline. The preparation stage utilizes keyword expansion algorithms for reference retrieval and structures citation information into attribute trees. The generation phase executes outline generation, main body generation, and post refinement (RAG-based Rewriting Module and Figure/Table Generation Module). Compared to AutoSurvey, SurveyX achieves measurable improvements in content quality and citation quality. Given that LLM-generated outlines lack logical flow and structural depth, and citations are inaccurate due to hallucinations,
Yan et al. 2025
propose frameworks named SurveyForge. This framework combines heuristic outline generation with memory-driven content generation. By analyzing human-written survey structures and integrating domain-specific articles to create balanced, hierarchical outlines, it then employs a scholar navigation agent that decomposes complex queries into sub-queries using domain literature. This agent prioritizes influential papers using citation counts and recency, ensuring cited literature is both relevant and impactful, outperforming AutoSurvey in reference coverage, outline quality, and average content quality.

Critical Perspective and Future Algorithm Design.

Two design philosophies dominate. Structured-organization approaches (HiGTL, CHIME, arXivDIGESTables) excel at taxonomy clarity and cross-paper comparability, but often omit nuanced critique, require upstream extraction accuracy, and struggle with topics lacking clear hierarchies. In contrast, end-to-end systems (AutoSurvey, SurveyX, SurveyForge) offer hour-scale draft generation and flexible coverage, yet still hallucinate citations, drift logically across sections, and underperform on deep synthesis or critical appraisal. Choice hinges on purpose: structured pipelines are ideal for pedagogical maps or decision trees; end-to-end retrieval-augmented systems suffice for rapid domain scans or first-pass reviews; a hybrid that layers structured outlining atop iterative, retrieval-grounded drafting is necessary when both breadth and depth are required. Next-generation survey engines should pair citation-aware planners with real-time fact-checkers to reduce hallucinations, track concept evolution so that reviews remain current as literature grows, introduce multi-agent critique loops that combine summarization with adversarial questioning to deepen analysis, and learn quality-weighted reward functions—such as the SAM-R/O/C suite—to guide models toward authoritative, well-structured, and insightful surveys.

(1) Scientific Knowledge Reasoning

In this chapter, we analyzes key benchmarks from the perspectives of task formats, task complexity, evaluation frameworks, and human-model performance comparisons, as shown in Table

Task formats.
Multiple-choice questions (MCQs) are widely used, with varying complexity. For example, GPQA contains 448 graduate-level MCQs in biology, physics, and chemistry. MathVista includes visual MCQs covering figure analysis, geometry, and textbook questions. SciEval uses both MCQs and fill-in-the-blank formats to assess scientific knowledge. Open-ended responses require higher reasoning ability. SciBench adopts free-response questions for college-level mathematics, chemistry, and physics, while CURIE evaluates models’ abilities to generate structured data, equations, code, and natural language explanations. Multimodal integration is prominent in many benchmarks. ScienceQA combines text (48%) and image contexts (49%), with 31% of questions using both. MMMU incorporates 30 image types, such as charts, diagrams, MRI scans, and chemical structures, interleaved with text. SPIQA assesses understanding of figures and tables from computer science papers through three distinct QA tasks. Some benchmarks use specialized formats to target specific reasoning skills. OlympicArena covers 13 answer types, including numerical values, code, and equations. Multimodal ArXiv challenges models to interpret scientific figures from academic papers.

Task complexity.
The educational level ranges from elementary to graduate studies. ScienceQA covers elementary to high school curricula (Grades 1–12). SciBench introduces college-level complexity with advanced computation requirements. GPQA reaches graduate-level difficulty with “Google-proof” questions that are challenging even for humans with internet access. The complexity of reasoning also varies. MathVista requires seven reasoning types: algebraic, arithmetic, geometry, logical, numeric commonsense, scientific, and statistical. MMMU demands domain-specific knowledge and advanced reasoning for expert-level challenges. Some benchmarks test long-context understanding. CURIE features inputs averaging 15,000 words from full research papers, and SPIQA evaluates comprehension of entire academic papers with figures and tables. International standards ensure rigorous evaluation. OlympicArena sources problems from 62 international competitions such as IMO, IPhO, and IChO, targeting advanced cognitive reasoning essential for hypothesis generation and interdisciplinary research.

Evaluation frameworks.
Many benchmarks go beyond final answers to assess the reasoning process. ScienceQA evaluates chain-of-thought reasoning through explanations and lectures. OlympicArena uses both answer-level evaluation for correctness and process-level scoring for step-by-step reasoning. Taxonomic frameworks are also used to classify cognitive abilities. SciEval assesses four core scientific abilities based on Bloom’s taxonomy: basic knowledge, knowledge application, scientific calculation, and research ability. OlympicArena benchmarks eight logical reasoning skills and five visual reasoning abilities. Some benchmarks introduce specialized metrics. SPIQA proposes L3Score (LLMLogScore) to better measure semantic similarity between answers. SciBench provides step-by-step solutions to facilitate detailed error analysis. Cross-domain evaluation is also emphasized. MMMU covers six core disciplines and 30 subjects, while CURIE spans six scientific disciplines including materials science, quantum computing, and biodiversity.

Human-model performance comparisons.
Persistent performance gaps are observed across benchmarks. On MathVista, humans achieve 60.3% accuracy, while the best model (GPT-4V) reaches 49.9%, revealing a 10.4% gap. On SciBench, models achieve only 43.22% accuracy on textbook problems and 13.8% on multimodal questions. Task-specific challenges affect model capabilities differently. On GPQA’s graduate-level questions, non-experts achieve 34% accuracy, while GPT-4 reaches only 39%. For ArXivQA’s scientific figures, human validation yields 88.2% accuracy on computer science questions, compared to 46.8% for top open-source models. Model architecture also has a significant impact. Open-source models perform poorly on MathVista compared to GPT-4V. On CURIE, even leading models like Claude-3 and Gemini Flash 2.0 achieve only 32% performance on scientific long-context tasks. Multimodal performance remains a particular challenge. SciBench shows that model performance on multimodal subsets (13.8%) is significantly lower than on text-only questions, highlighting the difficulty of integrating visual and textual information for scientific reasoning.

Critical Perspective and Future Dataset Design.
Current innovation datasets strike a useful balance between depth and breadth: curated sets (e.g., Nobel, Editorial Highlights) deliver high-precision ground truth for “breakthrough” status, whereas massive bibliometric graphs (SciSciNet, DeSci, PKG 2.0) enable statistically robust modeling of novelty trajectories. The trade-off is clear: small curated corpora suffer from sampling bias and sparse temporal coverage, while large graphs inherit citation-based confounders, domain skew, and delayed signal. When selecting a benchmark, practitioners should match required granularity—career- or paper-level, disciplinary span—mono- vs. multi-field, and evaluation horizon—retrospective vs. early-signal forecasting. Future resources should couple full-text, code, and dataset artifacts with bibliometrics to capture mechanism-level originality; embed prospective, time-stamped expert panels to validate “breakthrough” claims before citation accrual.

Critical Perspective and Future Dataset Design.

Peer-review corpora provide rich supervision signals—multi-aspect ratings (ASAP-Review), dialogue threads (PEERSUM, MOPRD), and deficiency tags (ReviewCritique)—yet they remain concentrated in NLP/ML venues and often rely on heuristic or crowd annotations that blur expert consensus. Scale versus fidelity is again the key tension: NLPeer and REVIEWER2 offer tens of thousands of reviews but vary in label quality, while ReviewCritique yields gold labels at the cost of coverage. Dataset choice should depend on desired granularity—sentence, aspect, or whole-review; context length—abstract-based vs. full-paper; and task focus—generation, scoring, or error detection. Next-generation benchmarks should extend to under-represented disciplines, preserve double-blind metadata for bias audits, include structured review rubrics aligned with editorial guidelines, and incorporate longitudinal decision outcomes to link critique quality with downstream impact.

3.3.2
Algorithms

(1) Innovation Measurement

Measurement based on knowledge recombination.
In the field of innovation measurement, the theory of combination of atypical novelty serves as the primary guiding principle
[
188
,
226
]
. This theory posits that innovation emerges from the recombination of existing knowledge elements rather than appearing entirely from nothing
[
174
,
36
]
. Based on this theory, early innovation measurement research utilized pre-trained language models such as BERT, SPECTER, and SentenceBERT to directly extract knowledge elements from the scientific literature and calculate the novelty of these combinations of knowledge elements
[
52
,
4
]
. For knowledge element types,
Luo et al. 2022
refined the previously vague and undefined knowledge elements into question-method combinations.
Chen et al. 2025a
introduced contrastive learning to predict the disruptive potential of question-method combinations by analyzing the differences between the measurements in the literature and current strategies. They used Monte Carlo search algorithms to iteratively explore combinations with high disruptive potential.
Wang et al. 2024i
employed BERTopic topic models to categorize papers into corresponding topic knowledge elements based on their topic distributions. For knowledge element sources,
Wang et al. 2024h
extended the extraction window from titles and abstracts to full texts. They classified extracted knowledge entities common in computer science, including task, method, metric, material, other scientific term, and generic. They constructed individual knowledge entity networks for each paper and built features at the network level to predict paper quality. Unlike entity-level knowledge element combinations,
Wu et al. 2025
argued that different combinations of IMRaD sections within papers can also be used to measure innovation. They found that the combination of the Introduction, Results and Discussion sections performed best among various combinations of sections in improving the prediction of the novelty score.

LLMs-driven innovation measurement.
Given that LLMs possess vast knowledge and excellent reasoning capabilities, some research utilizes them as innovation evaluators for papers.
Xu et al. 2024
compared LLM representations and generation, discovering that representations from different layers within LLMs can indicate the potential value of scientific ideas. Mid-to-late layer representations outperformed the final layer and showed higher correlation with human judgments of paper innovation. However, more research focuses on prompt engineering to fully leverage LLMs’ generative capabilities.
Lin et al. 2024
hypothesized that newly published papers are more innovative than previous ones. They employed RAG methods to query relevant papers and constructed prompt templates including abstracts of both target papers and related papers, requiring LLMs to provide scores from 0 to 10.
Huang et al. 2025a
designated Nobel Prize-winning papers and those with high breakthrough indices as highly original papers, constructing similar papers as control groups. They defined five types of originality: theoretical, methodological, problem-oriented, result-oriented, and application-oriented. Through zero-shot prompting, they instructed LLMs to generate originality scores, types, and descriptions across these five dimensions.
Tan et al. 2025
transformed the task of measuring paper innovation into weighted novelty scoring based on question-answering. By dividing papers into different sections and proposing section-specific questions as well as general questions, they required LLMs to provide novelty scores and confidence levels for corresponding sections, achieving innovation quantification based on information-rich texts. Additionally,
Liu et al. 2025e
noticed that existing research confuses textual similarity with idea similarity. They used LLMs to synthesize rephrased ideas with information equivalence, partial ideas with information reduction, and incremental ideas with information addition for seed ideas. By extracting idea-level knowledge from LLMs to train lightweight retrievers, they achieved alignment between ideas and concepts, enabling efficient and accurate LLM-based idea novelty detection.

Critical Perspective and Future Algorithm Design.

Algorithms anchored in knowledge-recombination theory excel at transparent, large-scale scoring: entity graphs or topic mixtures extracted by BERT/SPECTER variants offer explainable novelty indices and enable longitudinal studies with minimal human cost (e.g.,
Luo et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2024h
). Their main limitation is a dependence on noisy extraction pipelines by omitted entities or mis-typed relations propagate directly into novelty scores, and a myopic view of creativity that reduces ideas to co-occurrence statistics. In contrast, LLM-driven evaluators provide holistic, context-aware judgments by reading full texts and reasoning with prompt instructions (
Lin et al. 2024
;
Tan et al. 2025
); they capture conceptual subtlety but are vulnerable to hallucination, prompt sensitivity, and inconsistent calibration across fields. Researchers should therefore match methods to use-case constraints: graph-based metrics for high-throughput, reproducible screening; LLM assessors for expert panels or early-signal forecasting where nuance matters. Future work should hybridize these strands like retrieval-augmented LLMs that ground free-form critiques in extracted graphs, or self-consistent ensembles that average over layers, temperatures, and prompt variants to stabilize scores. Equally important is building prospective benchmarks with time-stamped expert labels to tune algorithms for originality detection before citations accrue, and injecting fairness diagnostics to curb field- or language-level bias.

(2) Automated Peer Review

Aspect-Guided and Structured Review Generation.
Automated peer review initially emerged as an NLP-driven approach to emulate human judgment by generating structured critiques across dimensions such as originality, clarity, and technical soundness. Early implementations typically relied on pre-trained sequence-to-sequence models like BART and GPT within extract-then-generate pipelines, but they encountered challenges like factual inconsistency, generic phrasing, and limited reviewer perspective diversity
[
213
]
. To overcome these issues,
Gao et al. 2024b
proposed REVIEWER2, a two-stage framework that first generates aspect-specific prompts and subsequently produces targeted reviews, enhancing specificity and review dimension coverage. Additionally,
Ma et al. 2025
incorporated iterative refinement into the review generation process through delta feedback mechanisms informed by scholarly metadata and citation graphs. They identified gaps between generated drafts and high-quality references via multiple-choice QA probing, refining subsequent drafts.

Furthermore, structured generation techniques leveraging LLMs have demonstrated significant effectiveness. LMCBert
[
96
]
integrates GPT-4o-generated structured summaries of paper content with contrastive learning (MoCo), significantly improving interpretability and precision in discriminating academic paper quality. Similarly, OpenReviewer
[
63
]
leverages a domain-specific LLM fine-tuned on expert review data from machine learning conferences, equipped with tailored prompting strategies and structured templates. This approach substantially surpasses general-purpose models (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude-3.5) in generating critiques aligned closely with human reviewer standards, highlighting the importance of domain adaptation and structured critique generation.

Multi-Agent Review Architectures.
Recognizing the distributed and interactive characteristics of human peer review, recent research has transitioned from single-model approaches to multi-agent systems.
D’Arcy et al. 2024
developed MARG, a leader-worker-expert agent framework where specialized LLM agents collaboratively handle distinct paper sections and review aspects.
Bougie and Watanabe 2024
further advanced this by introducing Generative Agent Reviewers (GAR), incorporating reviewer personas, memory modules, and novelty analyses operating over manuscript graphs to enhance review depth and consistency. Additionally,
Jin et al. 2024
demonstrated multi-agent simulations of full review processes, including rebuttals and meta-reviews, uncovering biases like anchoring and authority effects typically obscured in real-world settings. Extending this paradigm, PaperEval
[
61
]
introduces a multi-agent evaluation framework where CriticAgent generates detailed, explainable evaluation criteria, and QuantifierAgent employs RAG for comparative scoring. Variants like Multi-round PaperEval (MPE) and Self-correcting PaperEval (SPE) further enhance robustness through iterative and autonomous refinement, demonstrating scalability and interpretability across multiple scientific domains.

Knowledge-Enhanced and Grounded Critiques.
To address factual grounding and interpretability concerns, recent advancements integrate structured knowledge sources and feedback-driven refinement methods.
Wang et al. 2020b
introduced ReviewRobot, utilizing multi-layered knowledge graphs constructed from target papers, citations, and broader corpus backgrounds to synthesize grounded reviews. Building upon this,
Yuan and Liu 2022
proposed KID-Review, enhancing semantic alignment through citation and concept graphs alongside oracle pretraining, thereby reducing the semantic gap between paper content and human reviews. Nevertheless, the dependency on comprehensive underlying knowledge graphs constrains these models’ generalizability across diverse disciplines. In parallel, CLAIMCHECK
[
134
]
benchmarks the ability of LLMs to generate claim-grounded scientific critiques, explicitly linking reviewer-identified weaknesses to specific paper claims, verifying claim validity, and systematically evaluating advanced LLMs’ grounding capabilities, thus setting higher standards for evidence-based peer reviews.

Critical Perspective and Future Algorithm Design.

Three algorithmic lines dominate automated reviewing: aspect-guided generation, multi-agent architectures, and knowledge-grounded critique. Template-driven, aspect-guided systems (e.g., REVIEWER2) guarantee coverage and controllability but often produce formulaic prose and miss cross-aspect dependencies, limiting depth when evaluating unconventional work. Multi-agent frameworks (MARG, GAR, PaperEval) boost diversity and emulate human panel dynamics, yet incur heavy computational cost and occasionally amplify inter-agent inconsistency or anchoring bias. Knowledge-enhanced models (ReviewRobot, KID-Review) deliver superior factual grounding, though they depend on domain-specific graphs whose construction seldom scales beyond well-curated fields. Algorithm choice should thus consider review scope and resource budget: aspect-guided models suffice for triage and crowd-sourcing; knowledge-grounded or agentic designs are preferable for high-stakes venues demanding rigorous, explainable critiques. Looking forward, promising directions include retrieval-augmented, chain-of-thought pipelines that intertwine evidence retrieval, rationale drafting, and claim verification; domain-adaptive fine-tuning coupled with adversarial training to curb leniency and stylistic collapse; and closed-loop human-AI collaboration where expert feedback continuously updates reviewer personas and reward models, yielding reviews that are both scalable and on par with senior-program-committee quality.

3.4
Observations

Research on LLMs as evaluators bifurcates into two technical streams: Scientific Knowledge Synthesis and Scientific Literature Quality Assessment. SKS focuses on extracting and reasoning over scientific content, whereas SLQA aims to judge novelty and methodological soundness at the article level. The two streams have progressed rapidly yet reveal distinct strengths and limitations.

Scientific Knowledge Synthesis.
State-of-the-art systems couple explicit entity–relation extraction with implicit reasoning modules based on Chain-of-Thought, tool use, or multi-modal fusion. Benchmark design has moved from short, text-only tasks to long-context and figure-centric challenges that require interpretable rationales.

Capabilities

•

Blending explicit extraction with implicit reasoning yields verifiable knowledge units while permitting deeper logical synthesis.

•

Retrieval-augmented and multi-agent architectures reduce hallucination and enable handling of full-length papers that include text, tables and figures.

•

Process-oriented metrics now examine reasoning traces and citation accuracy, encouraging more auditable model behavior.

Limitation

•

Robust domain understanding still relies on costly annotation or tightly coupled knowledge graphs, which limits portability to under-resourced fields.

•

Visual–text alignment remains computationally intensive, and open-source models struggle to balance efficiency with accuracy.

•

Heterogeneous label schemes and answer formats prevent straightforward comparison across benchmarks.

Scientific Literature Quality Assessment.
Researches divides into innovation measurement, which often grounded in knowledge-recombination theory or prompt-based LLM scoring, and automated peer review, which ranges from aspect-guided templates to knowledge-grounded, multi-agent critique systems.

Capabilities

•

Large scientometric corpora combined with expert annotations provide a broader and less citation-biased basis for judging novelty and rigor.

•

Multi-aspect LLM scorers can process large submission pools rapidly and yield consistent preliminary assessments.

•

Knowledge-enhanced and agentic models improve factual grounding, dimension coverage and self-correction, offering a plausible path toward scalable, explainable reviewing.

Limitation

•

Existing systems tend to be overly lenient on methodological flaws and high-risk claims.

•

Prompt-based originality scores may vary across domains and over time, and alignment with expert opinion is still inconsistent.

•

Review datasets remain concentrated in a few AI venues; disciplinary imbalance and confidentiality constraints limit broader generalization.

LLMs as Collaborators

4.1
Task Definition

Scientific innovation is an iterative process involving interconnected stages such as hypothesis generation, experimental planning, execution, and analysis
[
175
]
. As depicted in Figure
6
, LLMs serve as active collaborators across this workflow. Hypothesis generation spans both the formulation of natural language explanations for observed phenomena and the derivation of research directions by synthesizing insights from literature and structured knowledge bases
[
98
]
. Building on this foundation, LLMs assist in both preliminary and optimized experimental design, helping to plan and refine experiments that test the proposed hypotheses. During experimentation, LLMs can automate procedures, guide the execution process, and facilitate data collection. In the subsequent stages, LLMs contribute to the optimization and analysis of experimental results by embedding experimental data, drawing inferences, and iteratively informing subsequent hypotheses and experimental cycles. Through their integration at each stage, LLMs not only act as powerful predictors and generators of hypotheses, but also function as tools for experiment design, implementation, and the modeling of complex systems, fundamentally enhancing the scientific discovery process.

Figure 6:
LLMs as collaborators in scientific innovation. LLMs transforming raw knowledge into actionable hypotheses, designing and optimizing experiments, automating laboratory work, and rigorously analyzing results to drive continuous scientific innovation.

Specifically, we formalize scientific hypothesis generation as:

\mathcal{C_{LLM}}(\mathcal{K}\oplus\mathcal{C})\rightarrow\mathcal{H}

(3)

where
\mathcal{K}
represents knowledge space (structured graphs, literature, databases);
\mathcal{C}
denotes context (background, constraints);
\mathcal{C_{LLM}}
captures semantic relationships for scientifically plausible inference;
\oplus
integrates knowledge with context;
\mathcal{H}
represents novel, testable hypothesis space.

Hypothesis generation algorithms include knowledge augmentation, iterative refinement, and hybrid approaches. Knowledge-augmented generation combines verified knowledge with LLMs to reduce hallucination. Iterative refinement addresses logical reasoning limitations through cyclical generation-evaluation processes. Hybrid approaches merge knowledge augmentation with optimization via knowledge-enhanced refinement and multi-agent collaboration.

We formalize the experimental assistant as:

\mathcal{C_{LLM}}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{I})\rightarrow\mathcal{R}

(4)

where
\mathcal{H}
represents hypotheses requiring validation;
\mathcal{R}
denotes resources and constraints;
\mathcal{I}
encompasses instrumental capabilities;
\mathcal{R}
represents results and insights.

This framework encompasses three phases: experiment design bridging conceptual questions to implementation; optimization through iterative refinement of hypotheses and conditions; and automation enabling systematic protocol execution with minimal intervention.

Critical Perspective and Future Dataset Design.
The investigated benchmarks cover a spectrum from large-scale, automated datasets (e.g., SciMON, IdeaBench) to expert-curated, domain-specific resources (e.g., TOMATO, TOMATO-Chem, ResearchBench). Large datasets offer scalability and comparison but may miss nuanced or interdisciplinary reasoning, while smaller, expert-annotated datasets provide depth but lack breadth and generalizability. Creativity-focused and generalization-oriented datasets (LiveIdeaBench, BioHypoGen, HypoBench) better test LLMs’ capacity for innovation but require complex, often subjective evaluation. Key strengths include attention to novelty and multi-domain coverage, as well as hybrid evaluation metrics. Weaknesses include limited dynamic adaptability—few benchmarks update with new scientific trends or allow for evolving tasks—and dependence on static annotation or subjective scoring. When selecting benchmarks, researchers should match the dataset’s scale, domain, and evaluation style to their goals: scalable metrics for broad comparisons, or expert-annotated sets for qualitative insights. For future development, dynamic features such as continual dataset updates, adaptive task formulations, and integration of both automated and human-in-the-loop evaluations will be critical.

4.2.2
Algorithms

To address LLMs’ limitations as collaborators in scientific hypothesis generation, including hallucinations, lack of domain expertise, and insufficient reasoning depth, current research has developed multiple algorithmic approaches. These can be categorized into four main types: Knowledge-augmented generation integrates external knowledge bases to enable evidence-based generation and provide directional guidance. Iterative refinement generation establishes cyclical optimization mechanisms that continuously improve hypothesis quality. Knowledge-augmented refinement combines external knowledge with model self-correction capabilities to construct modularized systems for hypothesis generation. Multi-agent collaboration enhances the scientific rigor and novelty of hypotheses by simulating distributed cognitive processes found in real research environments.

(1) Knowledge-Augmented Generation

Knowledge-augmented scientific hypothesis generation supplements external knowledge during prompting or the prompting process. This helps overcome hallucinations and overly generic problems in LLM generation. Through guidance and inspiration, it promotes evidence-based hypothesis generation by LLMs.

Knowledge types.
Structured knowledge graphs represent a common knowledge type for augmentation. For example,
Wang et al. 2020a
constructed comprehensive background knowledge graphs in the biomedical domain. Nodes represent scientific entities (diseases, chemicals, genes). Edges denote validated relationships (such as therapeutic effects, negative regulation). Similarly,
Xiong et al. 2024
used domain-specific knowledge graphs like PubTator3. They retrieved k-step relation chains through breadth-first search algorithms. This provides structured factual foundations for hypothesis generation. Temporal concept co-occurrence knowledge graphs.
Xu et al. 2023b
constructed evolving concept graphs spanning 20 disciplines from 29 million academic papers. Concepts connect through co-occurrence relationships. Multi-level abstraction knowledge.
Gu et al. 2024
proposed a four-level generalization framework. It maps concepts to different abstraction levels: L1 for domain-specific implementation, L2 for cross-domain application, L3 for abstract principles, L4 for universal system design principles. They extracted innovative elements from over 87,000 papers. Cross-domain knowledge connections are achieved through cosine similarity matching. Personalized research knowledge.
Gu and Krenn 2024
constructed the personalized knowledge graph SciMuse. They applied RAKE algorithm for concept extraction on 58 million research papers. GPT-4 refinement and validation yielded 123,128 scientific concepts.

Knowledge augmented methods.
Graph structure and text fusion.
Wang et al. 2020a
employed multi-head graph attention mechanisms combined with contextual text attention. They dynamically integrated structured graph features and text signals through gating mechanisms. Retrieval-augmented generation.
Xiong et al. 2024
’s KG-CoI framework implements a three-module architecture: KG-guided context retrieval, KG-augmented hypothesis generation, and KG-supported hallucination detection. This method uses BM25 sparse retrievers to retrieve relevant literature from PubMed. LLM-E agents generate keywords bridging structured KG data with free-text literature retrieval. Knowledge recombination enhancement.
Xu et al. 2023b
viewed concept co-occurrence in academic papers as an important way new ideas emerge. They trained BERT-based link prediction models and T5-based concept sentence generation models using pre-2021 data. These predict new co-occurring concepts in 2021 and generate descriptions of new concept combinations. Similarly,
Gu et al. 2024
implemented structured combinatorial processes. This includes decomposition stages (breaking innovations into core mechanisms, transferable components, abstract principles) and integration stages (achieving component merger through problem reframing, principle hybridization, constraint analysis). Step-by-step verification enhancement.
Liu et al. 2025d
proposed two knowledge integration approaches: (1) providing metadata during idea generation to guide LLMs toward feasible directions; (2) adding automatic validation during idea selection to assess empirical plausibility of hypotheses.
Xiong et al. 2024
conducted step-by-step verification based on KGs. They used domain-specific named entity recognition to extract entities. They checked direct relationships between entity pairs in KGs. LLM-V agents verified alignment between claims and KG triples.

(2) Iterative Refinement Generation

LLMs excel at generating novel hypotheses, but they often struggle with inductive reasoning. Iterative refinement frameworks address this limitation by establishing cyclical processes of hypothesis generation, evaluation, and improvement, refining initial ideas into sophisticated, testable hypotheses while maintaining scientific rigor and novelty

,
141

The MOOSE Series.

Yang et al. 2024a
proposed the foundational MOOSE framework, featuring a multi-module pipeline comprising Background Finder, Inspiration Title Finder, Inspiration Finder, and Hypothesis Proposer, along with three core LLM-based feedback mechanisms: present feedback for immediate refinement, past feedback for improving earlier modules using downstream signals, and future feedback for preparing context for subsequent modules. This multilayered feedback architecture enables the system to continuously refine hypotheses based on reality checking, novelty assessment, and clarity evaluation. MOOSE-Chem
[
205
]
extended the foundational framework to the chemistry domain, proposing that most chemistry hypotheses can be composed from a research background and a set of inspirations, thus designing three subtasks: retrieving inspirations, composing hypotheses with inspirations, and ranking hypotheses. Unlike basic MOOSE, they employed evolutionary algorithms that combine mutation, feedback, refinement, and recombination processes to generate hypotheses with a high similarity to the ground truth. MOOSE-Chem2
[
206
]
formalized hypothesis generation as a combinatorial optimization problem, based on the principle that optimal solutions can be derived from optimal solutions of subproblems, and designed a hierarchical heuristic search (HHS) method that refines hypotheses from coarse to fine level. The algorithm decomposes the hypothesis search space into manageable abstraction levels (including mechanism of reaction, general concepts/components needed, specific components, full details of components, and experimental conditions), not only smoothing the reward landscape but also enabling more effective optimization through reduced complexity and improved stability. The latest MOOSE-Chem3
[
102

incorporates the combination of internal LLM reasoning with experimental feedback into iterative hypothesis refinement, moving beyond pre-experiment ranking to dynamic prioritization based on empirical results. They mathematically formalized simulated experiments and developed a clustering-based pseudo-experimental-guided ranking method (CSX-Rank) that optimizes hypothesis ranking through structural similarity between simulated feedback and hypotheses.

Reward-guided optimization.
This class of algorithms treats hypothesis generation as a mathematical optimization problem. They guide iterative model improvement through quantitative rewards. To enhance the quality of generated hypotheses,
Zhou et al. 2024b
drew inspiration from the Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) algorithm in multi-armed bandits. They designed reward functions to balance hypothesis exploration and exploitation. During the update phase, the top-k hypotheses are evaluated. Rewards are updated based on prediction correctness. When the number of incorrect hypotheses reaches a certain threshold, they are added to the wrong example bank for subsequent new hypothesis generation.
Li et al. 2024d
proposed a hypothesis optimization framework combining Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL). In the SFT stage, the model learns foundational patterns from papers and ideas. In reinforcement learning, generated hypotheses are optimized through multi-dimensional reward models. This optimization is guided by fine-grained feedback across novelty, feasibility, and effectiveness dimensions.

(3) Knowledge-Augmented Refinement

Knowledge-Augmented Refinement algorithms combine knowledge-driven and self-correction processes to modularize LLM-based scientific hypothesis generation. They consist of two key components that synergistically enhance the quality and novelty of the hypothesis: knowledge augmentation and iterative refinement. Knowledge augmentation supplies essential information and context, while iterative refinement improves idea quality, novelty, and scientific validity.

Knowledge augmentation component.
The knowledge augmentation component serves as the foundational mechanism for incorporating external scientific knowledge into the hypothesis generation process. Current research employs diverse strategies for knowledge acquisition, representation, and integration in knowledge augmentation components. First is multi-source knowledge integration. SciMON
[
181
]
designed multi-source integration that fused semantic graphs, knowledge graphs, and citation graphs. The system’s semantic neighbors module used SentenceBERT embeddings to retrieve contextually similar problems and solutions. The knowledge graph neighbors module leveraged structured scientific knowledge graphs to identify adjacent nodes representing tasks, methods, and their relationships. The citation neighbors module exploited citation networks to discover relevant papers through semantic similarity matching.
Li et al. 2024b
introduced a novel approach to temporal organization through the extension of the bidirectional chain. This methodology constructed knowledge chains by identifying anchor papers and extending both forward (through citations) and backward (through references) to capture evolutionary trajectories of research domains.
Liu et al. 2024a
combined theory-driven insights from the the literature with empirical patterns from observational data. Next is context-aware knowledge extraction. ResearchAgent
[
5

implemented entity-centric knowledge augmentation by constructing sparse matrices that captured cross-domain relationships between scientific entities. Scideator
[
142

employed three-facet knowledge representation that systematically decomposed research papers into purposes, mechanisms, and evaluations. This allowed the system to generate novel hypotheses through systematic facet permutation while maintaining conceptual coherence. SCI-IDEA
[
76

utilized both token-level and sentence-level embeddings to extract key aspects from relevant publications, including research objectives, methodologies, and evaluation metrics.

Iterative refinement component.
The iterative refinement component progressively enhances the quality of the hypothesis through self-correction, novelty optimization and preference alignment strategies. In self-correction and reflection mechanisms, Nova
[
55

implemented comprehensive self-correction mechanisms through three distinct processes: self-check for logical consistency, self-critique for quality assessment, and reflection for strategic planning. ResearchAgent
[
5

employed multiple LLM-powered ReviewingAgents that provided structured feedback on generated ideas. The system performed 3-4 iterative refinement cycles, with each reviewing agent using different evaluation templates and criteria. In novelty optimization, SciMON
[
181

retrieved existing similar ideas from the literature using similarity search, compared against novelty thresholds, and updated ideas with explicit differentiation instructions. This process continued iteratively until sufficient novelty was achieved. SCI-IDEA
[
76
]
introduced Aha Moment detection mechanisms that used both novelty and surprise metrics. Novelty was measured using cosine similarity between semantic embeddings, while surprise was quantified using negative log-likelihood. This enabled identification of potentially breakthrough concepts.
Li et al. 2024b
implemented novelty-checker agents that evaluated candidate ideas against retrieved papers and iteratively refined ideas when substantial similarity was detected. In preference-aligned optimization,
Dasgupta et al. 2025
implemented three-phase training with preference optimization, including supervised fine-tuning followed by Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). The system created automated preference datasets and employed iterative DPO with dynamic generation. The models generated ideas on random topics, ranked them using combined creativity and satisfaction scores, and created new pairs of preferences for continued refinement.

(4) Multi-Agent Collaboration

Unlike modular hypothesis generation systems, some research simulates real scientific research environments based on distributed cognitive processes, designing multi-agent collaborative architectures to achieve scientific hypothesis generation.

Architectures.
Agent architectures for generating scientific hypotheses can be divided into role-based specialized architectures and virtual research ecosystem architectures. For role-based specialized architectures,
Qi et al. 2024
implemented a functional decomposition architecture with four specialized agents. The Analyst agent specialized in systematic literature analysis and knowledge extraction. The Engineer agent focused on method design and experimental protocol development, transforming conceptual hypotheses into actionable research frameworks. The Scientist agent served as the synthesis coordinator, integrating insights to generate coherent hypotheses. The Critic agent assessed proposed hypotheses against scientific validity standards. ACCELMAT
[
78
]
tailored four specialized agents for materials science applications. The Hypothesis Generation Agent (HGA) focused on generating novel material property hypotheses, while Critic Agents (CA) provided domain-specific evaluation based on materials science principles. The Scientist Agent (SA) combined theoretical knowledge with experimental considerations, and the Evaluation Agent (EA) implemented quantitative assessment protocols specific to materials research validation. For virtual research ecosystem architectures, VirSci
[
164
]
constructed a research ecosystem and a multi-agent system for the generation of scientific hypotheses using real-world academic datasets. The research ecosystem included past and current paper information and author knowledge bases. In the multi-agent system, randomly selected agents became team leaders and initiated collaboration with other agents, including collaborator selection, topic discussion, idea generation, novelty assessment, and abstract generation.

Communication Mechanisms.

Qi et al. 2024
implemented structured sequential communication in which agents interacted through predefined message formats. The Analyst agent provided structured literature summaries to the Engineer and Scientist agents, who then collaborated to formulate hypotheses. The Critic agent received hypothesis proposals and provided formatted feedback with evaluation criteria and improvement suggestions. ACCELMAT
[
78
]
employed multicycle communication protocols in which agents participated in iterative feedback exchanges. The system implemented structured rounds where HGA generated initial hypotheses, CA provided specific critiques, SA offered theoretical improvements, and EA provided quantitative assessments. This process continued for multiple cycles until consensus criteria were met. VirSci
[
164
]
implemented intra-team and inter-team discussion mechanisms through dialogue to improve communication topology and improve simulation authenticity.

Critical Perspective and Future Algorithm Design.

Collectively, the four algorithmic paradigms delineate a continuum of methodological trade–offs. Knowledge-augmented generation affords robust factual grounding that attenuates hallucinations, yet its performance is contingent upon the timeliness, completeness, and curation overhead of external knowledge bases; in nascent or fast-moving domains, obsolete or sparse graphs risk confining the search space to incremental advances. Iterative-refinement frameworks supply substantial inductive-reasoning depth and frequently converge on highly structured, empirically testable hypotheses, albeit at the cost of pronounced computational expenditure (multiple generation–evaluation cycles) and pronounced sensitivity to reward-function design, which may inadvertently optimise for proxy metrics rather than genuine scientific value. Knowledge-augmented refinement synthesises the virtues of grounding and self-correction but inherits the liabilities of both—namely, graph maintenance demands and multicycle latency—while further introducing pipeline complexity that complicates debugging and causal attribution. Multi-agent collaboration most faithfully emulates authentic research collectives, fostering epistemic diversity and emergent creativity; nevertheless, it amplifies token consumption, accentuates coordination fragility, and obfuscates responsibility as erroneous inferences propagate across interacting agents.

Algorithm selection should therefore be problem-specific: knowledge-augmented generation is preferable when comprehensive, well-maintained knowledge graphs exist and breadth of exploration is desired; iterative-refinement is advantageous for deep optimisation of narrowly scoped problems under generous computational budgets; knowledge-augmented refinement is appropriate where both epistemic grounding and robustness are paramount and sustained engineering support is available; and multi-agent architectures are most suitable for investigations that benefit from role specialisation or the simulation of sociocognitive research dynamics. Prospective algorithmic innovation should seek to hybridise complementary strengths, for example, agent collectives that invoke retrieval or refinement sub-modules adaptively, and to incorporate adaptive evaluation loops that adjust objectives as projects transition from exploratory to exploitative phases. Future research ought to reduce maintenance burdens via self-evolving knowledge graphs, enhance reliability by embedding causal or mechanistic simulators into reward estimators, and improve accountability through rigorous provenance tracking and transparent agent-level logging.

Critical Perspective and Future Dataset Design.
Collectively, the nine benchmarks surveyed here delineate a continuum that ranges from narrowly circumscribed, domain-specific resources—such as BioPlanner’s wet-lab protocols—to broad, open-ended research suites exemplified by Curie and MLGym. Standardised “digital-laboratory” environments (MLAgentBench, TaskBench) offer stringent controllability, repeatability, and fully automated scoring; nevertheless, their highly curated toolchains can obscure the idiosyncratic failure cases that arise in real-world laboratories. Domain-centred corpora (BioPlanner, MLE-bench) furnish strong ecological validity and transparent performance signals (e.g., Kaggle medal tiers), yet the resulting solutions often over-specialise to disciplinary conventions and transfer poorly across neighbouring fields. Reproducibility-oriented benchmarks (SUPER, PaperBench) expose brittle software dependencies and documentation lacunae, but their curation and evaluation remain labour-intensive, limiting scalability. Finally, holistic scientific-reasoning suites (Curie, BoxingGym) constitute an ambitious step toward assessing complete research cycles, though their handcrafted scope and relatively small sample sizes constrain statistical power and generalisability. In selecting an appropriate benchmark, researchers should weigh the requisite degree of agent autonomy, the congruence between benchmark domain and target application, available computational and human-annotation resources, and tolerance for open-ended failure modes. The development of future datasets would profit from multimodal, longitudinal traces of authentic experimental workflows, partially automated yet faithful grading protocols, cross-disciplinary coverage that mitigates domain-specific overfitting, and mechanisms, such as periodic task regeneration or the continual integration of newly published code, to ensure sustained relevance.

Experiments are the essential stage for hypothesis testing, falsification, and optimization in scientific research. Critical Perspective and Future Algorithm Design.
Collectively, the LLM-enabled experimental assistants surveyed herein afford unparalleled flexibility, breadth, and celerity to contemporary scientific workflows, spanning initial hypothesis formulation and protocol specification through multimodal optimisation and fully autonomous execution. Domain-specific architectures that exemplified by CRISPR-GPT, InstructBioMol, and PALM-H3 demonstrate the efficacy of embedding expert priors and undertaking task-oriented fine-tuning, thereby achieving state-of-the-art performance on circumscribed problems; such specialisation, however, necessitates access to meticulously curated datasets, bespoke model designs, and computationally intensive training regimens. By contrast, domain-agnostic frameworks such as MOLLEO, MCR, ChemReasoner, GSO, TextGrad, and ORGANA provide pronounced modularity, rapid prototyping capabilities, and end-to-end automation, yet they remain vulnerable to diminished interpretability, error propagation along extended reasoning chains, and non-trivial computational complexity. Across this algorithmic spectrum, salient strengths include human-in-the-loop feedback mechanisms, scalable exploration of high-dimensional parameter spaces, and seamless interoperability with simulation engines or robotic platforms; principal limitations reside in hallucination-induced unreliability, acute sensitivity to prompt engineering, and the persistent challenge of guaranteeing reproducibility, epistemic transparency, and operational safety in fully autonomous contexts. Algorithm selection is thus intrinsically multifactorial: investigators must weigh task complexity, data sufficiency, precision requirements, interpretability desiderata, and resource constraints—privileging highly specialised, fine-tuned models when veridical accuracy is paramount, and favouring generalist systems when rapid exploration or multi-objective optimisation predominates. Future methodological advances are likely to emanate from hybrid paradigms that fuse explicit scientific priors, ontologically grounded knowledge representations, robust human–AI governance protocols, and harmonised benchmarking suites, thereby enhancing reliability, generalisability, and scientific utility.

4.4
Observations

LLMs are increasingly employed as active collaborators across the iterative stages of scientific innovation, from initial ideation to final analysis. Current research bifurcates into two principal applications: Hypothesis Generation and Experimental Assistance. While each domain has demonstrated considerable progress, challenges related to the unification of these functions, along with concerns regarding reliability, safety, and the capacity for true innovation, remain prominent.

Hypothesis Generation:
LLMs now draft testable, context-aware hypotheses by marrying retrieval-augmented literature, structured knowledge graphs and iterative self-feedback. Algorithms like knowledge-augmented prompting, cyclical refinement and multi-agent role play curb hallucinations and deepen reasoning, while benchmarks score outputs for novelty, plausibility and explanatory strength.

Capabilities

•

Factual Grounding and Plausibility: By integrating external knowledge bases like knowledge graphs and citation networks, LLMs can generate hypotheses that are grounded in verified data, thereby mitigating the risk of hallucination.

•

Iterative Optimization and Refinement: Algorithmic frameworks employing cyclical generation-evaluation processes enable the continuous improvement of initial ideas, refining them toward greater clarity, scientific rigor, and novelty.

•

Distributed Cognition via Multi-Agent Systems: Architectures that simulate collaborative research environments distribute cognitive tasks such as literature review, synthesis, and critical assessment among specialized agents, enhancing the rigor of the generated hypotheses.

Limitations

•

Bias Towards Incrementalism: A reliance on shared datasets and static benchmarks can inadvertently bias models toward generating safe, incremental advancements rather than promoting transformative or high-risk, high-reward ideas.

•

Engineering and Debugging Overhead: The complexity of hybrid systems that combine knowledge augmentation with refinement loops increases engineering overhead and complicates error attribution when debugging the pipeline.

•

Deficiencies in Causal and Mechanistic Reasoning: Despite iterative processes, LLMs often struggle with deep causal inference and lack the domain-specific mechanistic insight required for formulating truly profound scientific hypotheses.

Experimental Assistant:
As experimental assistants, LLMs translate hypotheses into machine-readable protocols, direct lab robots and optimize conditions via simulation feedback. Benchmarks, including BioPlanner, MLE-bench and PaperBench, track protocol accuracy, replication fidelity and end-to-end automation, and specialized systems (e.g., CRISPR-GPT, ChemCrow, ORGANA) already execute full laboratory workflows with minimal human intervention.

Capabilities

•

Protocol Synthesis and Execution: LLM-based agents can convert natural language instructions into structured formats like pseudo code or extensible Description Language (XDL), enabling the coordination of complex, multi-step laboratory operations with minimal human intervention.

•

Multi-objective Optimization and Analysis: Through techniques such as in-context learning, Bayesian optimization loops, and simulation-driven feedback, LLMs can efficiently tune experimental parameters and support rigorous interpretation of results.

•

Enhancing Experimental Reproducibility: Benchmarks focused on reproducibility, assess the ability of LLMs to replicate published findings, thereby stress-testing their robustness in practical research settings.

Limitations

•

Operational Safety and Reliability: The opaque nature of LLM inference, together with documented “sleepwalking” incidents in which agents autonomously execute out-of-scope actions, presents tangible hazards to laboratory safety and experimental integrity.

•

Domain-Specific Brittleness and Fragmentation: Models highly optimized for a single discipline achieve impressive in-domain performance but transfer poorly across adjacent fields, leaving their knowledge representations siloed and limiting cross-disciplinary generalization.

•

Lack of a Unified Interface: The absence of a standardized layer that converts LLM-generated hypotheses into machine-actionable experimental protocols constitutes a persistent bottleneck, forcing ad hoc human mediation and interrupting the seamless flow from conceptual design to empirical execution.

Critical Perspective and Future Dataset Design.

The resources summarized in Table

mark a substantive advance toward systematic assessment of LLM-mediated research, yet none affords a complete or fully authentic measurement of scientific autonomy. AAAR-1.0 and CS-Bench provide commendable scale and fine-grained skill annotation, but their purely textual construction precludes evaluation of tool integration, laboratory logistics, or iterative data acquisition. LAB-Bench enhances ecological validity through protocol troubleshooting and database queries; nevertheless, its multiple-choice format collapses rich reasoning trajectories into single-token selections, thereby diluting construct fidelity. DSBench introduces multi-modality and executable code, but the relatively small modeling subset and reuse of public competition data expose the benchmark to memorization and leakage risks. Scientist-Bench is distinctive in spanning the full discovery pipeline, from hypothesis formation to manuscript generation and peer review, but its manual curation restricts disciplinary breadth and inflates evaluation cost.

Dataset choice should therefore be dictated by the specific evaluative objective: large, heterogeneous suites (AAAR-1.0, CS-Bench) are appropriate for breadth-oriented profiling; domain-specialized corpora (LAB-Bench, DSBench) are preferable for depth-oriented diagnostics; and end-to-end frameworks (Scientist-Bench) are indispensable when assessing comprehensive research autonomy. Looking forward, new benchmarks would benefit from multi-domain coverage with explicit difficulty gradients, open-ended tasks that mandate tool invocation, iterative hypothesis testing, and real-time data acquisition, automated, leakage-resistant scoring based on executable artifacts, and continuous updates sourced from dynamic repositories such as pre-print servers or electronic lab notebooks. Benchmarks embodying these principles would move the field beyond static answer accuracy and toward rigorous measurement of an LLM’s capacity for scientific research.

Critical Perspective and Future Algorithm Design.

In summary, three salient dimensions shape the practical utility of an algorithm: degree of autonomy, epistemic reliability, and human interpretability. Fully autonomous, end-to-end pipelines excel in exploratory breadth and cost-efficiency, yet their monolithic architecture compounds cascading error, making them comparatively fragile with respect to hallucination, data leakage, and unverifiable inference. Closed-loop iterative frameworks partially redress this vulnerability by coupling hypothesis generation to executable feedback, thereby improving empirical validity and sample efficiency; however, these benefits are offset by elevated computational overhead and sensitivity to reward-signal noise. Collaborative or human-in-the-loop systems provide the strongest guarantees of methodological soundness, ethical compliance, and reproducibility, but their dependence on synchronous review introduces latency and may impose cognitive and organizational burdens on expert participants.

Consequently, algorithm selection should follow a risk-adjusted decision calculus: domains with stringent regulatory or safety constraints (e.g., biomedical discovery) warrant collaborative or hybrid closed-loop designs, whereas low-stakes, exploratory settings can exploit the speed of fully autonomous agents. Key discriminants include acceptable error tolerance, resource availability, clarity and stability of evaluation metrics, and institutional governance requirements. Future algorithmic research should emphasize modular composability by separating the ideation, execution, and verification layers. It is also important to incorporate principled uncertainty quantification, self-reflexive critique, and cryptographically secure provenance logging throughout the system design. Integrating tree-search exploration with learned reward models, implementing adaptive autonomy that modulates agent independence in proportion to real-time risk assessments, and benchmarking against domain-specific open evaluation suites constitute promising pathways for advancing robust, trustworthy autonomous science.

Critical Perspective and Future Dataset Design.
Collectively, the surveyed benchmarks constitute an important first step toward a principled assessment of “LLMs as scientists,” offering breadth of discipline, multi-modal inputs, and execution-based scoring. Nevertheless, several limitations temper their diagnostic power. Synthetic or heavily templated items (e.g., portions of DiscoveryBench) may inflate apparent generalization, while small, expert-curated corpora (BrainBench, CellBench) restrict statistical confidence and domain coverage. Most suites emphasis task completion over deeper scientific virtues such as novelty, methodological soundness, reproducibility, and cost efficiency; evaluation protocols rarely capture these dimensions explicitly. Dataset selection should therefore be guided by alignment with the intended deployment context: practitioners targeting bioinformatics workflows benefit from the fine-grained pipeline reconstruction in CellBench, whereas researchers interested in generalist planning may prefer the interactive, multi-step challenges of DiscoveryWorld. When no single benchmark suffices, a composite evaluation drawn from complementary datasets can provide a more balanced picture of model capability.

Future benchmark development would profit from: coupling real-world, version-controlled data with longitudinal ground truth; incorporating multi-turn, open-ended interaction that reflects iterative scientific practice; embedding explicit metrics for rigor, reproducibility, and resource utilization; providing tiered difficulty and domain tags to facilitate systematic ablation; and releasing transparent scoring scripts and cost logs to support fair, reproducible comparison across models and compute budgets.

5.3.2
Algorithms

(1) Multi-Agent LLM Laboratories

Recent studies describe LLM laboratories in which the Planner, Critic, Experimenter, and Coder function as specialized agents working together in a cyclical workflow. This sequence starts with hypothesis generation, moves through peer review and tool-mediated execution, and ends with reflection before the next stage of planning
[
11
]
. Baby-AIGS embeds a dedicated FalsificationAgent that explicitly designs counter-experiments, thereby instilling Popperian rigor into the loop
[
106
]
. Extensions such as SciAgents integrate each agent with an ontological knowledge graph; requests for subgraphs, local reasoning, and inter-agent debate are orchestrated by a central Planner to identify information gaps and propose follow-up queries
[
42
]
. Domain-focused variants adapt the generic architecture: LLMatDesign equips agents with materials-simulation APIs plus a self-reflection module that scores every composition edit before issuing the next modification
[
69
]
, whereas DrugAgent maintains a two-tier hierarchy in which an Idea-space Planner drafts alternative machine-learning pipelines and an Instructor translates them into domain-specific code by consulting curated pharmacological libraries
[
101
]
. Across these systems the algorithmic scaffold is an asynchronous sequence—plan, invoke external tools, record structured observations, critique, then re-plan—supporting hierarchical memories and role-specific toolboxes that curb hallucinations and foster cumulative reasoning.

(2) Program-Search and Symbolic-Reasoning LLMs

A complementary line of research regards the LLM primarily as a program synthesizer that generates executable artifacts, including functions, equations, or probabilistic programs. These outputs are subsequently evaluated by an external system. FunSearch pairs a code-generating LLM with a deterministic scorer in an island-based evolutionary strategy; high-scoring programs seed the next generation while inter-island cloning preserves diversity
[
147
]
. LLM-SR casts equation discovery as program synthesis: the model proposes Python skeletons containing placeholders for constants, gradient-based optimizers fit those parameters, and an experience buffer supplies top programs as in-context exemplars to bias future sampling toward structurally novel yet performant equations
[
156
]
. Materials-science adaptations add inspection and memory layers: a Formula-Generation Agent proposes expressions, a Reflection Agent analyses past search trajectories to prune redundant operator patterns, and a depth-first search scheduler balances exploration against exploitation while caching intermediate parses for rapid replay
[
54
]
. LLM4ED alternates between two optimization modes—black-box self-improvement that rewrites only sub-trees of elite equations and LLM-driven evolutionary operators such as crossover and mutation—while a complexity-penalized fitness function promotes parsimony
[
29
]
. Finally, BoxLM moves beyond deterministic formulas by having a proposer LM emit PyMC programs, an inference engine compute posterior fits, and a critic LM—primed with fit diagnostics—produce natural-language feedback that becomes part of the next proposal, thereby realizing Box’s Loop within a language-mediated setting
[
87
]
. These algorithms share four pillars: a strict separation between generation and verification, persistent memories that steer sampling based on previously evaluated artifacts, explicit diversity-preservation mechanisms such as islands or clustering, and human-legible outputs that facilitate downstream expert refinement.

Critical Perspective and Future Algorithm Design.

Multi-agent LLM laboratories exhibit marked strengths in exploratory breadth and contextual grounding: through explicit role allocation, iterative peer-review, and ontology-backed memories, they attenuate hallucinations and seamlessly interoperate with heterogeneous simulation or data-retrieval tools. The concomitant liabilities are non-trivial orchestration cost, since each incremental agent or prompting cycle compounds latency, compute expenditure, and the risk of error propagation, together with a coordination complexity that can obfuscate accountability across the workflow. By contrast, program-search and symbolic-reasoning pipelines enforce a rigorous separation between generative and verificatory phases, thereby guaranteeing falsifiability and yielding concise, interpretable artifacts amenable to expert audit. Their principal drawbacks are the computational demands of outer-loop evaluation and a propensity for premature convergence or unwieldy expressions when diversity mechanisms are inadequately calibrated. Practically, multi-agent frameworks are preferable when the scientific bottleneck is expansive hypothesis generation or when seamless integration with diverse domain tools is paramount; program-synthesis approaches are advisable when formal verifiability, mathematical parsimony, or downstream human interpretability is critical.

From a methodological standpoint, the development of new algorithms should priorities modular, declarative interfaces among language models, external tooling, and long-term memory stores; the fusion of stochastic self-improvement protocols (e.g., evolutionary strategies, reinforcement learning) with deterministic self-verification scaffolds; and the encapsulation of domain priors as pluggable components. Such design principles will foster composable, reconfigurable discovery engines capable of dynamically selecting, combining, or inventing reasoning strategies in response to the epistemic contours of a given scientific problem.

5.4
Observations

LLMs are increasingly formalized as scientists with two concentric mandates. Autonomous Scientific Research seeks to optimize established procedures, including literature review, experiment planning, execution, and reporting, while Autonomous Scientific Discovery pursues genuinely novel findings that extend the frontier of human knowledge. Despite sharing many algorithmic primitives, such as multi-agent orchestration, tool integration, and closed-loop verification, the two modes diverge in objectives, risk profiles, and evidentiary standards. This reveals both complementary strengths and common methodological gaps.

Autonomous Scientific Research:

Capabilities

•

End-to-end automation markedly reduces turnaround time and cost for routine analytical workflows, from data ingestion to manuscript drafting.

•

Iterative, feedback-driven loops allow agents to refine code and experimental parameters without continuous human intervention.

•

Retrieval-augmented reasoning enables more comprehensive literature coverage and context-aware decision-making.

Limitations

•

Deep domain reasoning—particularly equation verification, protocol nuance, and multimodal interpretation—remains brittle, limiting trust in unaudited outputs.

•

Errors introduced early in monolithic pipelines can propagate unchecked; embedded, independent verification layers are still nascent.

•

Coordination overhead in multi-agent settings inflates computational cost and complicates provenance tracking, especially in safety-critical domains.

Autonomous Scientific Discovery:

Capabilities

•

Generative search over hypothesis space, coupled with programmatic verifiers or simulators, has yielded demonstrable advances in mathematics, materials science, and chemistry.

•

Role-specialized LLM laboratories (planner, critic, coder, experimenter) foster internal peer review and diversify exploratory trajectories.

•

Evolutionary and symbolic-regression pipelines enforce falsifiability by separating generation from external evaluation.

Limitations

•

Scalable validating large volumes of candidate hypotheses remains costly; partial checks risk false-positive discoveries.

•

Search procedures can converge prematurely on local optima, necessitating explicit diversity preservation and adaptive exploration strategies.

•

Cross-domain generalization is limited: systems proficient in, say, algebraic conjecture often stumble in biological inference, underscoring the need for transferable reasoning scaffolds.

ASR systems already deliver practical efficiency gains for well-defined tasks, while ASD frameworks exhibit isolated but compelling breakthroughs. Both strands, however, confront fundamental issues of robust reasoning, verification rigor, and accountable deployment. Future research should priorities modular architectures that decouple ideation, execution, and validation; incorporate uncertainty quantification; and embed cryptographically secure provenance to enable reproducible, trustworthy autonomous science.

Discussion

6.1
Ethical Considerations and Safety

Ethics.
The integration of LLMs into scientific innovation introduces significant ethical challenges. LLM-driven advances may cause researchers to overestimate their true understanding of scientific phenomena, as AI-generated explanations often appear highly persuasive. Reliance on LLMs can also foster scientific monocultures, narrowing methodological diversity and limiting the breadth of exploratory inquiry. Furthermore, the intrinsic biases present in LLMs risk perpetuating societal prejudices and algorithmic unfairness, which may intensify existing inequalities
[
124
,
19
,
228
]
. As LLMs increasingly assume autonomous roles in scientific innovation, additional ethical risks emerge, including threats to data privacy, potential content fabrication, increased risk of plagiarism, and a reduction in critical human oversight. Collectively, these issues pose a substantial threat to scientific integrity, create opportunities for novel forms of misuse, and complicate accountability and responsibility within the innovation process
[
34
,
146
]
.

Addressing these challenges requires establishing inclusive and adaptive governance frameworks that embed transparency, accountability, and continuous ethical reflection into the design and deployment of LLM-driven scientific innovations
[
82
,
218
]
. Ensuring scholarly integrity demands that researchers retain sufficient oversight and accountability over innovations facilitated by LLMs, thus safeguarding the reliability and validity of scientific outcomes. Additionally, scientific innovations driven by LLMs should be guided explicitly toward benefiting the common good. Adopting this broader sociotechnical perspective involves active public deliberation, recognition and integration of diverse values, and continuous alignment with established ethical principles and international standards.

Safety.
In addition to ethical concerns, significant safety risks arise from the growing autonomy and capabilities of AI scientist systems. As LLMs increasingly undertake critical tasks such as hypothesis generation, experimental design, and manuscript preparation, new risks emerge, including insufficient human oversight, potential misdirection of scientific efforts, and the inadvertent automation of flawed research protocols
[
201
,
19
]
. Without adequate oversight, errors or biased results produced by LLMs could remain unnoticed, emphasizing the necessity of robust governance frameworks, transparent documentation processes, and clear attribution mechanisms throughout the scientific innovation lifecycle.

Further, as LLM-driven innovation approaches higher levels of sophisticated agentic behaviors, potential safety threats such as misaligned objectives, self-preservation tendencies, and deceptive actions become increasingly prominent
[
7
]
. These threats necessitate careful design principles that emphasize interpretability, uncertainty modeling, and explicit constraints on autonomous decision-making. Collaborative frameworks emphasizing consistent human oversight will be crucial to ensuring safe, responsible, and effective scientific innovation.

6.2
Challenges and Future Directions

6.2.1
Core Challenges

Model Capability and Reliability Limitations.

LLMs face three tightly coupled obstacles: hallucination and factual accuracy, domain-specific comprehension, and out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization, which together constitute the most fundamental barrier to AI-enabled scientific innovation. First, the autoregressive training paradigm makes LLMs prone to producing outputs that sound plausible yet are objectively false, such as citing irrelevant or nonexistent papers during literature surveys
[
222
,
221
]
. These false results are especially dangerous in high-risk laboratory settings, where they can lead to erroneous protocols and uncontrolled experimental execution
[
221
]
. Second, current models often fail to grasp specialized terminology and complex concepts, for example, by misjudging critical protein-protein interactions in biochemistry or overlooking subtle mathematical assumptions in theoretical physics
[
116
]
. Moreover, most scientific LLMs are trained at a coarse field level (e.g. “chemistry”), leaving long-tailed, fine-grained topics insufficiently covered
[
222
]
. Third, scientific test distributions routinely diverge from training data; newly coined concepts, unseen molecular scaffolds, or proteins with novel chain counts emerge continuously, and current pretrained LLMs still struggle to handle such shifts
[
222
]
.

Lack of Transparency and Explainability.

The black-box nature of current LLMs undermines scientific validation, trust, and assimilation of AI-driven insights
[
228
]
. Overcoming this opacity requires a paradigm shift toward architectures designed for verifiable reasoning and provable conclusions rather than superficial post hoc explanations
[
228
]
. In addition, even with enlarged context windows, models often lose coherence when analyzing lengthy manuscripts, often missing cross-sectional logical dependencies
[
116
]
.

Multimodal and Multilingual Integration Difficulties.

Scientific corpora span manuscripts, high-resolution images, time series signals, code, and structured tables; each modality requires bespoke preprocessing, yet high-quality cross-modal alignments are extremely scarce, particularly in expert-intensive domains, generating training and evaluation costs
[
57
,
19
]
. Moreover, the English-centric bias of existing LLMs risks overlooking low-resource research areas and hampers the integration of global data and expertise needed to tackle worldwide challenges such as COVID-19
[
19
]
.

Benchmark and Evaluation Bottlenecks.

Most publicly available suites remain static, allowing models to overfit through exposure in pre-training corpora and leaderboard gaming
[
165
]
. Even the newest collections seldom mirror the full closed loop of scientific practice, from hypothesis generation to experimental verification, so systems that excel at multiple choice or code writing subtasks routinely fail when asked to design, execute and interpret real experiments
[
72
]
. Leakage further undermines credibility: many test questions are paraphrases of highly cited papers, making it difficult to disentangle genuine reasoning from memorization
[
107
]
. Disciplinary coverage is also skewed; biology, chemistry, and computer science dominate, whereas low-resource fields such as ecology or the social sciences receive minimal representation, producing inflated aggregate scores that mask domain-specific weaknesses
[
122
]
. Multimodality compounds the problem: vision–language tasks rarely include raw spectra, micrographs, or CAD files, so cross-modal reasoning remains largely untested
[
81
]
. Finally, current leaderboards overwhelmingly reward answer accuracy, neglecting critical dimensions such as novelty, energy cost, carbon footprint, and reproducibility, thereby encouraging incremental gains rather than scientifically meaningful progress
[
20
]
. Addressing these shortcomings will require continually refreshed, leak-resistant benchmarks that grade entire research pipelines, incorporate under-represented modalities and disciplines, and report multi-objective metrics aligned with real scientific value.

6.2.2
Future Directions

Domain-Specific Knowledgeable Models.

Future language-model development is expected to shift from broad, “one-size-fits-all” systems toward deeply specialized models whose tokenisation schemes, pre-training corpora and reasoning mechanisms are explicitly aligned with the primitives, ontologies and evaluation protocols of a single scientific discipline, because empirical evidence already shows that using function-infused molecular building blocks rather than atom-level tokens improves chemical reasoning and synthesizability
[
33
]
, and a cross-field meta-analysis of more than 260 scientific LLMs demonstrates that performance gains are consistently largest when models are pre-trained and benchmarked on field-specific data and tasks
[
222
]

Conclusion

This paper delivers a comprehensive and unified survey of the ways in which LLMs are reshaping scientific innovation. Guided by three criteria, autonomy, task complexity, and human-AI collaboration, we position LLM capabilities within a three-level pyramid comprising the roles of Evaluator, Collaborator, and Scientist. At the dimensions of benchmarks, algorithms, and evaluation and results, we detail the capabilities and limitations of Evaluators in scientific knowledge synthesis and literature quality assessment, Collaborators in hypothesis generation and experimental assistance, and Scientists in autonomous research and discovery. The review also examines ethical considerations, identifies potential safety risks, and outlines productive avenues for future work. We hope that this survey will serve as a practical reference for researchers seeking to understand and responsibly advance LLM-driven scientific innovation.
