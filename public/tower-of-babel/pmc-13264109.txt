Investigating Cancer literacy and help-seeking barriers among individuals with intellectual disabilities in Hungary
Authors: Mohammed Elmadani, Simon Klara, Godfrey Mbaabu Limungi, Maha Besbes, Amer Mesmar, Osama Hamad, Lívia Tóth, Orsolya Máté
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study examined cancer literacy and help-seeking barriers among adults with intellectual disabilities in Hungary.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted between October 2024 and January 2026 with 380 adults with mild-to-moderate intellectual disabilities, recruited using simple random sampling within institutional and community-based settings. Cancer knowledge and perceived barriers were assessed using an adapted, easy-to-read Cancer Awareness Measure (CAM). Data were analyzed using nonparametric tests and multivariable linear regression with bootstrapped confidence intervals.ResultsParticipants showed moderate knowledge of warning signs (61.3% of maximum) and risk factors (66.3%), but poor knowledge of prevention (53.7%) and a moderate level of perceived barriers (50.7%). Living with family was independently associated with higher barriers, whereas care home residence was associated with higher risk factors and prevention knowledge in bivariate analyses. Employment and marital status were associated with multiple outcomes. Greater risk-factor knowledge was associated with higher barriers, whereas greater prevention knowledge was associated with fewer barriers.ConclusionsPrimary prevention knowledge gaps persist, and structural factors such as living arrangements and employment are associated with help-seeking. Accessible, prevention-focused education and system-level adaptations may support earlier detection and improved outcomes.Keywords: Intellectual disability, Cancer literacy, Help-seeking barriers, Health inequalities, Cancer prevention

Introduction

Cancer mortality among individuals with intellectual disabilities remains disproportionately higher than in the general population, a persistent public health inequity (McMahon et al., 2025; Cuypers et al., 2022). Although prevention and screening programs are recommended for all adults, substantial barriers limit equitable access, timely diagnosis, and effective care for this population (McMahon et al., 2025). Health literacy the ability to access, understand, and use health information is central to cancer prevention and early help-seeking (Housten et al., 2020), yet people with intellectual disabilities often experience reduced health literacy due to communication challenges and systemic barriers within healthcare services (Downs et al., 2024).

Increased life expectancy has led to a growing burden of age-related conditions, including cancer (O'Regan and Drummond, 2008). Despite comparable incidence for some malignancies, individuals with intellectual disabilities more often present at later stages and have poorer outcomes, largely due to delayed symptom recognition, limited screening, and barriers to help-seeking (Walsh et al., 2021). These disparities are exacerbated by socioeconomic disadvantage, lower educational attainment, and limited access to tailored health information (Hoffman-Goetz et al., 2009). Impairments in cognitive and adaptive functioning can further hinder processing of complex health information and navigation of healthcare systems (Keeley et al., 2025; Boot et al., 2017), so effective communication requires accessible formats, simplified language, and supportive environments (Boot et al., 2017; Chinn, 2016). Although involving people with intellectual disabilities in healthcare decisions improves outcomes, evidence on how best to support their participation in cancer prevention and early diagnosis remains limited (Downs et al., 2024).

Help-seeking links health literacy to cancer outcomes, influencing the timeliness of diagnosis and effectiveness of treatment (Yang et al., 2023). For this population, it is often constrained by communication difficulties, limited symptom awareness, reliance on caregivers, and professionals' insufficient understanding of disability-specific needs (McIlfatrick et al., 2010), and interacts with contextual factors including living arrangements, employment, and access to specialized services (McIlfatrick et al., 2010; Jackson et al., 2023; Lyra-González et al., 2024). Despite growing recognition of these issues, comprehensive, population-recruited evidence integrating cancer knowledge domains with sociodemographic and contextual determinants is lacking (Burus et al., 2024), particularly in Central and Eastern Europe, including Hungary, where additional structural and cultural barriers may exist (McMahon et al., 2025; Walsh et al., 2021; St. John et al., 2022). To date, no study has systematically examined cancer literacy and help-seeking barriers in this population at the population level.

This study therefore aimed to assess cancer literacy knowledge of warning signs, risk factors, and prevention measures and to identify barriers to help-seeking among adults with intellectual disabilities in Hungary.

Methods

Study design and population

A cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted across institutions and community-based organizations serving individuals with intellectual disabilities in multiple regions of Hungary, in collaboration with the Hungarian Organization for People with Intellectual Disabilities. Settings included residential care facilities and family-based living arrangements to capture variation in living conditions and support environments.

Eligible participants were adults aged ≥18 years with a formal diagnosis of mild or moderate intellectual disability, identified through disability service providers. Inclusion required the ability to provide informed consent independently or via a legal guardian and to communicate verbally or nonverbally with support. Severe or profound intellectual disability precluding meaningful engagement was excluded. The level of intellectual disability (mild versus moderate) was not recorded in a form available for analysis; this is addressed in the Discussion. Given the communication needs of this population, caregivers assisted with clarification only and were instructed not to influence responses.

The sample size was calculated using Cochran's formula (Ahmed, 2024) with a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error. Based on census data estimating that approximately 0.45% of the Hungarian population has an intellectual disability (Hungarian Central Statistical Office C, 2011), the minimum required sample was 380 participants. Simple random sampling was applied within participating organizations: eligible individuals were listed by service providers and randomly selected, with recruitment facilitated by institutional coordinators and caregivers. The sampling frame comprised two living-arrangement groups: adults living in a family-based household (with parents or relatives) and adults in residential care.

Measures

A structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire was used. The first section captured sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, marital status, educational level, employment status, living arrangement, cancer screening experience, family history of cancer). The category “8 grades” denotes completion of általános iskola (eight years of compulsory primary education, typically completed by age 14–15).

The second section assessed cancer literacy using an adapted, easy-to-read version of the Cancer Awareness Measure (CAM) (Munir et al., 2022; Cancer Research UK, 2025), developed and validated by our team in 232 adults with intellectual disabilities; full psychometric details are reported elsewhere (Elmadani et al., 2026), and the instrument is provided in Supplementary File S1. It comprised four subscales: warning signs (9 items; maximum = 9), risk factors (11 items; maximum = 55), prevention measures (7 items; maximum = 7), and barriers to seeking help (11 items; maximum = 33). The prevention subscale assessed knowledge of primary prevention only actions that reduce the likelihood of cancer occurring (not smoking, avoiding second-hand smoke, maintaining a healthy weight, eating fruit and vegetables, eating less red and processed meat, drinking less alcohol, and being physically active while protecting the skin from the sun) as distinct from secondary prevention (screening), which this subscale did not assess. The risk-factor and prevention subscales were designed to be parallel: risk-factor items assess theoretical awareness that a factor increases cancer risk, whereas the corresponding prevention items assess applied knowledge that modifying that factor reduces risk. The risk-factor subscale comprised modifiable risk factors, two non-modifiable risk factors (age over 70, family history of cancer), and one infectious risk factor (HPV).

Data were collected between October 2024 and January 2026 by trained nursing staff and research assistants affiliated with the partner organization. Face-to-face interviews used simplified language and visual aids; caregivers could assist with clarification but were instructed not to influence responses. Each interview lasted approximately 25–30 min. Data were recorded using Google Forms and stored in secure, password-protected systems.

Statistical analysis

The analytical approach was independently reviewed by a statistician. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages; continuous variables as mean (SD), median (IQR), and range. To provide an objective interpretation of knowledge levels, each knowledge subscale score was expressed as a percentage of the maximum possible and classified using Bloom's cut-off points (Seid and Hussen, 2018) as good (≥80%), moderate (60–79%), or poor (<60%).

Non-normal distributions (Shapiro–Wilk, Kolmogorov–Smirnov) necessitated non-parametric tests. Two-group differences used the Mann–Whitney U test (effect size r = |Z|/√N); comparisons across three or more groups used the Kruskal–Wallis H test (η2 = (H − k + 1)/(n − k)). Multivariable linear regression identified factors associated with help-seeking barriers and each knowledge domain; given the bounded, non-normal outcomes, bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals were estimated using 2000 resamples. For the barriers model, variables were entered hierarchically (sociodemographic/contextual first, then knowledge domains) to assess incremental explained variance (ΔR2). Ordered variables (age group, education) were modelled as ordinal terms; non-ordered categorical variables (employment, marital status) were entered as single linear terms for parsimony and comparability, acknowledged as a limitation. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factors. Significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Ethical approval was obtained from the Health Science Council Scientific and Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Health Sciences, University of Pécs (OM ID: FI 58544; Ikt. Sz.: PTE/116168–1/2025). Accessible information sheets and consent forms were provided in easy-to-read formats; informed consent was obtained from participants or their legal guardians, with ongoing confirmation of willingness throughout. Participants could withdraw at any time without consequence. Data were anonymized using unique participant codes and stored securely in accordance with applicable data protection regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki. The study met the institution's guidelines for the protection of human subjects with respect to safety and privacy. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

We included 380 adults with intellectual disabilities (Table 1); the mean age was 43.83 years (SD 12.52). Participants demonstrated moderate knowledge of warning signs (mean 5.52 ± 2.90; median 6, IQR 5; 61.30% of maximum) and risk factors (36.49 ± 7.29; median 37, IQR 8; 66.30%), both moderate under Bloom's cut-off, but poor knowledge of prevention measures (3.76 ± 2.02; median 3, IQR 4; 53.70%). Perceived barriers reached 50.7% of maximum (16.72 ± 6.00; median 15, IQR 9), a moderate level (Table 2).

Discussion

This study provides population-based evidence on cancer literacy and help-seeking barriers among adults with intellectual disabilities. Participants showed moderate knowledge of warning signs (61.3%) and risk factors (66.3%), poor prevention knowledge (53.7%), and a moderate level of barriers (50.7%), aligning with prior research on reduced cancer awareness, screening uptake, and access to care in this population (Cuypers et al., 2020). These findings highlight persistent gaps in accessible information and supportive care pathways, pointing to structural rather than solely individual-level barriers.

Interpreted against Hungarian general-population data, the deficit is most pronounced in prevention rather than across cancer literacy as a whole. No prior Hungarian study has administered the full CAM in the general population, precluding direct comparison; however, cancer-specific surveys are informative. Among 1150 Hungarian adults aged 40–70, fewer than one-third identified the recommended colorectal screening start age and few were well informed about risk factors or symptoms (Gede et al., 2018); among Hungarian women aged 25–65, only 7.0% had sufficient breast cancer risk-factor knowledge and 16.7% sufficient symptom knowledge (Reményi Kissné et al., 2021). Our participants' warning-signs and risk-factor performance is thus broadly comparable to, rather than markedly below, available Hungarian benchmarks, whereas prevention is where the gap is clearest mirroring international evidence that even general-population samples struggle to identify modifiable risk factors (Munir et al., 2022; Connor et al., 2020).

This pattern is conceptually informative when primary prevention is distinguished from secondary prevention. Primary prevention, not smoking, healthy diet, weight management, physical activity, limiting alcohol, and sun protection can prevent a substantial proportion of cancers, whereas organized screening (secondary prevention) targets few cancers and detect existing diseases rather than preventing it. Our prevention subscale assessed primary prevention specifically. Participants held moderate theoretical awareness of cancer risk factors but lower applied knowledge of the corresponding primary-prevention behaviors that is, knowing a factor that causes cancer did not consistently coincide with knowing the action that reduces that risk. This dissociation reflects prior evidence that cancer education for this population has emphasized symptom recognition and screening over primary prevention and health promotion (Gil et al., 2024). It identifies a specific target: translating risk-factor awareness into actionable, person-centered primary-prevention knowledge through accessible, supported formats (Swaine et al., 2014; Petitpierre et al., 2025).

Living arrangement showed mixed associations with knowledge family-living participants had higher warning-signs knowledge and care-home residents higher risk-factor and prevention knowledge in bivariate analyses, though none persisted after adjustment. By contrast, living arrangements were independently associated with barriers: family-living participants reported substantially higher barriers than care home residents. This contrasts with prior work emphasizing family's facilitative role in healthcare navigation (Wilkinson et al., 2011; Xu et al., 2017; Stokes et al., 2023) and merits cautious interpretation. Residential care may provide structured health monitoring and staff-coordinated access that lowers some practical barriers, whereas family-living adults may depend on relatives who themselves face obstacles navigating cancer services; differences in awareness, autonomy, or reporting may also contribute. Regardless of mechanism, these findings underscore the need for reasonable adjustments across both settings, including staff and family training, accessible communication, and proactive support for screening and diagnosis.

Sex differences were modest and partly attenuated after adjustment: females' higher bivariate warning-signs knowledge did not persist, while male sex was independently associated with lower risk-factor knowledge. Women's higher reported barriers may reflect anxiety, fear, or negative healthcare experiences (Cuypers et al., 2020). Participants with a family history showed higher warning-signs knowledge and, after adjustment, lower barriers, suggesting experiential exposure may improve familiarity with healthcare pathways and reduce uncertainty (Willis, 2016).

Employment was associated with all literacy domains and barriers, but the associations were non-monotonic full-time workers who showed relatively high warning-signs but lower risk-factor knowledge, while disability-benefit recipients showed the reverse, so they reflect heterogeneous patterns of social and healthcare engagement rather than a simple gradient of social inclusion. This is consistent with evidence linking community participation to preventive healthcare use (Stokes et al., 2023) while cautioning against unidimensional interpretation. Age and marital status were also associated with several outcomes, reflecting life-course and social-support influences, though marital status comprised non-ordered categories and is best interpreted descriptively. Education was not independently associated with knowledge after adjustment, suggesting formal education alone does not explain disparities; accessible communication and healthcare support may be more influential (Gil et al., 2024; Wilkinson et al., 2011). Cancer screening experience was associated with both higher warning-signs knowledge and higher barriers, suggesting healthcare contact increases awareness while exposing individuals to procedural and communication challenges (Xu et al., 2017; Willis, 2016; Wagner et al., 2025), access alone is insufficient without supportive care experiences.

Multivariable analyses showed higher risk-factor knowledge associated with increased barriers and higher prevention knowledge with reduced barriers, suggesting risk awareness without actionable guidance may increase concern while prevention knowledge enhances perceived control. This highlights the need for education combining risk awareness with actionable strategies and supported navigation (Gil et al., 2024; Wilkinson et al., 2011; Carneiro et al., 2025). Overall, cancer inequalities in this population are shaped by both individual knowledge and structural factors, particularly living arrangements, employment, and healthcare experiences, so public health strategies should move beyond knowledge-based approaches towards comprehensive, disability-inclusive prevention that explicitly distinguishes primary prevention from screening.

This study has several strengths and limitations. Strengths include a relatively large sample, comprehensive assessment of multiple literacy domains, and robust non-parametric and bootstrapped analyses suited to non-normal outcomes. Several limitations apply. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The sampling frame was limited to individuals engaged with institutional and community-based services, affecting generalizability to all Hungarian adults with intellectual disabilities. Self-reported, interviewer-administered responses may be subject to recall or social-desirability bias despite efforts to minimize caregiver influence. Non-ordered categorical predictors (employment, marital status) were modelled as linear terms for parsimony, which may obscure category-specific effects; future studies with larger subgroups should use dummy-coded contrasts. The study excluded individuals with severe or profound intellectual disability and did not measure the level of intellectual disability, so we could not verify whether the employment and living-arrangement associations were independent of cognitive level. Finally, unmeasured factors, cognitive functioning, health literacy support, quality of caregiver or staff interactions, and organizational practices may have influenced outcomes.

These findings have implications for policy and practice. Improving cancer outcomes for individuals with intellectual disabilities require system-level change rather than knowledge-based interventions alone. Structural determinants, living arrangements, employment, and healthcare experiences play a decisive role in cancer literacy and help-seeking. Strategies should prioritize disability-inclusive prevention: accessible, prevention-focused education that clearly distinguishes primary prevention from screening, reasonable adjustments within both residential and family-based settings, supported decision-making, and proactive navigation through screening and diagnostic services.

Conclusion

This study provides new quantitative evidence on cancer literacy and help-seeking barriers among adults with intellectual disabilities, integrating multiple knowledge domains with contextual and structural factors. Cancer literacy was uneven, with particularly poor prevention knowledge and a moderate level of perceived barriers. Both individual and environmental factors are associated with cancer literacy and help-seeking. Addressing these inequalities will require coordinated efforts to implement reasonable adjustments, accessible communication, and supported navigation across cancer prevention, screening, and early diagnosis throughout the life course.
