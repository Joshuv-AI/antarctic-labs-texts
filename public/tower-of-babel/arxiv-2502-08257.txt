The external version of a subclassical logic
Massimiliano Carrara, Michele Pra Baldi

Abstract
. A three-valued logic L is subclassical when it is defined by a single matrix having the classical two-element matrix as a subreduct. In this case, the language of L can be expanded with special unary connectives, called external operators . The resulting logic L e \textsc{L}^{e} is the external version of L , a notion originally introduced by D. Bochvar in 1938 with respect to his weak Kleene logic. In this paper we study the semantic properties of the external version of a three-valued subclassical logic L . We determine sufficient and necessary conditions to turn a model of L into a model of L e \textsc{L}^{e} . Moreover, we establish some distinctive semantic properties of L e \textsc{L}^{e} .



1.
Introduction

In the philosophy of logic debate departures from classical reasoning has been motivated by specific phenomena such as paradoxes, partial information and
vagueness.
But “paradoxical sentences - as G. Priest argues - seem to be a fairly small proportion of the sentences we reason with. It would seem plausible to claim that in our day-to-day reasoning we (quite correctly) presuppose that we are not dealing with paradoxical claims” (
[
34
, p. 235]
). And when we are not dealing with these phenomena, we want to be able to reason classically; indeed classical logic seems to be perfectly in order as it is. Now, the problem is: how can we secure we reason “classically” when we are not dealing with these non-normal phenomena? Or, put differently: how can we recover specific sets of classical inferences within a non-classical propositional logic, such as in many-valued logics? A widely recognized approach is to enrich the language with new unary connectives, commonly referred to as normality, classicality, consistency, or determinedness operators.

This is precisely the direction originally pursued by N. da Costa, the founder of the well-established tradition of the Logics of Formal Inconsistency within the field of paraconsistent logics (we refer the reader to
[
12
]
for a comprehensive survey of this tradition). Subsequently, similar intuitions led J. Marcos to the development of the Logics of Formal Undeterminedness
[
32
]
. More recently, D. Szmuc advanced this line of research by investigating consistency and determinedness operators for a specific family of logics of variable inclusion (also known as infectious logics)
[
37
]
.
However, the theoretical origins of this approach can be traced back to 1938, when the Russian logician D. Bochvar expanded his three-valued logic with the so-called

external
connectives
[
7
]
. Bochvar’s three-valued logic
B
is formulated within the classical language
\langle\land,\lor,\neg\rangle
(see Example
3.2
), and it was historically studied in connection with paradoxes.
Despite its modest success in dealing with paradoxes, this logic has been experiencing a resurgence in recent years. On the formal side, it has been explored within the framework of logics of variable inclusion
[
8
]
; in philosophy it has been applied to the debate on subject-matter
[
4
,
25
]
; and in epistemology it has been employed in belief revision theory
[
14
,
13
]
.
In this logic, assigning the non-classical truth value
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
to a sentence
\varphi
traditionally means that
\varphi
is considered meaningless (or off-topic), which is equivalent to the evaluation of an atom appearing in
\varphi
as
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.
Consequently,
B
is a theoremless logic, in the sense that every classical theorem (think of
\varphi\lor\neg\varphi
) admits a
counterexample by simply evaluating one of the variables appearing in
\varphi
to
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
. In less formal terms, when some of the information contained in
\varphi\lor\neg\varphi
is meaningless, the entire sentence also becomes meaningless. This distinctive feature is known as the
contamination principle
.
Nonetheless, we may want our logical language to express the idea that “
\varphi\lor\neg\varphi
is true whenever it is meaningful”.
This is precisely what Bochvar achieved by enriching the language with new unary connectives
\Delta_{0}
,
\Delta_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
,
\Delta_{1}
, called
external operators
. Their intended interpretation is “being meaningful and false”, “being meaningless”, “being meaningful and true” respectively. The same operators were independently introduced, under different names, by D’Ottaviano and da Costa in 1970
[
23
]
to formalize the paraconsistent logic
\textsc{J}_{3}
(See Example
3.2
), further refined in collaboration with Epstein few years later
[
24
]
.

These external operators allow us to express semantic features that the
internal
language
\langle\land,\lor,\neg\rangle
of
B
cannot capture. Semantically, they provide a yes/no answer, taking values among
\{0,1\}
, to whether their arguments are classical/non-classical or true/false.
Expanding
B
with these external operators yields Bochvar external logic (
\textsc{B}^{e}
). In this system, the statement “
\varphi\lor\neg\varphi
is true whenever it is meaningful” corresponds to the fact that
\Delta_{1}\varphi\lor\neg\Delta_{1}\varphi
is a theorem of
\textsc{B}^{e}
.

A closer look into investigations of Bochvar through the lens of modern algebraic logic shows that, when minimal conditions are met, the concept of
external version
of a logic does not rely on specific properties of
B
, but it can be applied to a wide family of three-valued logics, which we call
subclassical
(see Definition
3.1
).

Despite extensive logico-philosophical work on consistency, determinedness, and modal-like operators, a unified approach to external operators is still lacking. Likewise, the external version of subclassical logic has not been systematically studied using modern algebraic logic. This paper lays the groundwork for such a study.

A further motivation for this investigation is that some well-known logics, besides
\textsc{B}^{e}
, can be understood as the external version of a subclassical logic. One example is three-valued Łukasiewicz logic
\textsc{\L}_{3}
, that can be equivalently
formulated by replacing the implication
\to_{\textsc{\L}}
with the operator
\Delta_{1}
in the three-element Łukasiewicz chain (see Figure
1
).
In this algebra,
\Delta_{1}
is known as the Monteiro-Baaz operator, traditionally understood as a modal operator of necessity (see
[
15
,
16
]
and
[
18
]
).
Thus,
\textsc{\L}_{3}
can be viewed as the external version of Strong Kleene logic
\textsc{K}_{3}
, in symbols
\textsc{K}_{3}^{e}=\textsc{\L}_{3}
.
Likewise, the paraconsistent logic
\textsc{J}_{3}
corresponds to the external version of the Logic of Paradox
LP
. Despite this, the connection between these logics has not yet been explored within the conceptual framework considered here.

The key takeaway is that several well-known logics, which might initially seem unrelated, actually converge as external versions of certain three-valued logics.
This suggests that the notion of external version of a logic might provide a unifying framework for studying a broad class of logics.

In this paper, we tackle two central questions related to the external version
\textsc{L}^{e}
of a logic
L
. First, in Section
4
, we determine necessary and sufficient semantic conditions to define an external operator over a model of
L
(Theorem
4.7
). For the special case of Bochvar logic and Strong Kleene logic, this question has been addressed in
[
10
]
and
[
15
]
.
We prove that not every model of a subclassical logic supports the definability of an external operator, and we establish a useful criterion to detect when this is the case (Corollary
4.8
).
Furthermore, in Section
5
we provide an overview of the logical properties that are recaptured in the step from a logic to its external version. This is the case of well-known and desirable properties of classical logic, such as algebraizability and the deduction theorem.

2.
Preliminaries

For unexplained terminology the reader can refer to
[
26
]
or
[
22
]
. In this paper we will mainly deal with (expansions of) two propositional languages: the classical language
\langle\land,\lor,\neg\rangle
of type
\langle 2,2,1\rangle
and the language
\langle\land,\lor,\neg,\Delta_{1}\rangle
of type
\langle 2,2,1,1\rangle
. We do not consider constant symbols in the language. Once a propositional language is fixed, by
Fm
we denote the set of formulas built over it, and by
\mathbf{Fm}
the absolutely free algebra with universe
Fm
. When necessary, we will write
\mathbf{Fm}_{\mathcal{L}}
to empathize that we are referring to the language
\mathcal{L}
. We will often omit the notational distinction between an algebra
\mathbf{A}
and its underlying universe
A
, thus writing
a\in\mathbf{A}
in place of
a\in A
. Whenever
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
are algebras of the same type (or
similar
) and
h:\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{B}
is a homomorphism, we set
h[X]=\{b\in\mathbf{B}:b=h(a)\text{ for some }a\in X\}
, for
X\subseteq\mathbf{A}
. The dual notion applies to
h^{-1}[Y]
, where
Y\subseteq\mathbf{B}
. For a class of algebras
\mathsf{K}
we denote by
\mathbb{I},\mathbb{S},\mathbb{H},\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{SD}},\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{U}}(\mathsf{K})
the closure of
\mathsf{K}
under isomorphic copies, subalgebras, homomorphic images, products and subdirect products and ultraproducts.

Given a propositional language, a logic
L
is a reflexive, monotone and transitive
relation
\textsc{L}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Fm})\times\mathbf{Fm}
, which is also invariant under substitutions, meaning that if
\langle\Gamma,\varphi\rangle\in\textsc{L}
then
\langle h[\Gamma],h(\varphi)\rangle\in\textsc{L}
for every homomorphism
h:\mathbf{Fm}\to\mathbf{Fm}
. As usual, instead of using the infix notation, we denote a rule of the logic
\langle\Gamma,\varphi\rangle\in\textsc{L}
as
\Gamma\vdash_{\textsc{L}}\varphi
. We use
\psi\vdash\dashv_{\textsc{L}}\varphi
as a
shorthand for
\varphi\vdash_{\textsc{L}}\psi
and
\psi\vdash_{\textsc{L}}\varphi
.
For
\varPhi\subseteq Fm
, by
\Gamma\vdash_{\textsc{L}}\varPhi
we understand
\Gamma\vdash_{\textsc{L}}\varphi
, for each
\varphi\in\varPhi
. In this paper, we always assume that the logics we deal with are non trivial. Similar conventions apply when considering equations instead of formulas and the equational consequence
\vDash_{\mathsf{K}}
of a class of algebras
\mathsf{K}
instead of the consequence relation of a logic. A logic
L
is
finitary
if
\Gamma\vdash_{\textsc{L}}\varphi
entails
\Delta\vdash_{\textsc{L}}\varphi
, for some finite
\Delta\subseteq\Gamma
. In this paper, we will only deal with finitary logics, unless stated otherwise.
On the semantic side, our main tools will be provided by the theory of logical matrices. A matrix consists in a pair
\langle\mathbf{A},F\rangle
where
\mathbf{A}
is an algebra and
F
is a subset of its underlying set. Each matrix
\langle\mathbf{A},F\rangle
uniquely defines a logic
\vdash_{{}_{\langle\mathbf{A},F\rangle}}
(in the same language as
\mathbf{A}
) as follows

\Gamma\vdash_{{}_{\langle\mathbf{A},F\rangle}}\varphi\iff\forall h:\mathbf{Fm}\to\mathbf{A},\text{ if }h[\Gamma]\in F\text{ then }h(\varphi)\in F,

for
\Gamma\cup\{\varphi\}\subseteq\mathbf{Fm}
.
A matrix
\langle\mathbf{A},F\rangle
is
complete
or
characteristic
for a logic
L
when

\Gamma\vdash_{\textsc{L}}\varphi\iff\Gamma\vdash_{{}_{\langle\mathbf{A},F\rangle}}\varphi.

When the left-to-right implication of (
2
) in the above display holds, we say that the matrix is a
model
of
L
. Of course, this entails that
\mathbf{A}
is an algebra of the same type of
L
. In this case,
F
is called a
filter
of the logic
L
. Intuitively,
F\subseteq\mathbf{A}
is a
L
-filter when it is closed under all the possibile interpretations of
L
-rules over
\mathbf{A}
, and its elements are the so-called
designated
values. A formula
\varphi
is a
theorem
of
L
if
\vdash_{\textsc{L}}\varphi
. A set of formulas
\Gamma
is an
antitheorem
of
L
if for each model
\langle\mathbf{A},F\rangle
and
h:\mathbf{Fm}\to\mathbf{A}
it holds
h(\gamma)\not\in F
for some
\gamma\in\Gamma
. This is equivalent to saying that the formulas in
\Gamma
cannot be jointly designated within a model of the logic.
Each logic
L
is canonically associated with a class of algebras, its algebraic counterpart
\Alg\textsc{L}
. For instance, if we consider Classical logic (
CL
), intuitionistic logic (
IL
) and strong Kleene logic (
\textsc{K}_{3}
), we obtain that
\Alg\textsc{CL}
is the class of Boolean algebras,
\Alg\textsc{IL}
is the class of Heyting algebras, and
\Alg\textsc{K}_{3}
is the class of Kleene lattices. For any logic
L
, the class
\Alg\textsc{L}
is closed under
\mathbb{I}\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{SD}}
, but need not to be closed under
\mathbb{S}
.
\Alg\textsc{L}
is, intuitively, the class of algebras that support the “interesting” models of
L
, the so-called reduced models. These are the models
\langle\mathbf{A},F\rangle
such that
\widetilde{\Omega}^{\mathbf{A}}F
is the identity relation on
\mathbf{A}
, where the congruence
\widetilde{\Omega}^{\mathbf{A}}F
on
\mathbf{A}
is defined, for every
a,b\in\mathbf{A}
as

\langle a,b\rangle\in\widetilde{\Omega}^{\mathbf{A}}F\text{ if and only if }\varphi(a,\vec{c})\in G\iff\varphi(b,\vec{c})\in G,

for each formula
\varphi(x,\vec{z})
, each
\vec{c}\in\mathbf{A}
and each
L
-filter
G\supseteq F
on
\mathbf{A}
. Crucially,
\mathbf{A}\in\Alg\textsc{L}
if and only if, for some
L
-filter
F
on
\mathbf{A}
,
\widetilde{\Omega}^{\mathbf{A}}F
is the identity relation.

We say that the matrix
\langle\mathbf{A},F\rangle
is a submatrix of
\langle\mathbf{B},G\rangle
if
\mathbf{A}
is a subalgebra of
\mathbf{B}
(in symbols
\mathbf{A}\leq\mathbf{B}
) and
F=G\cap\mathbf{A}
. When
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
is a sublanguage of
\mathcal{L}
, given an
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
and an
\mathcal{L}
-algebra
\mathbf{B}
, we say that
\mathbf{A}
is a
subreduct
of
\mathbf{B}
when
\mathbf{A}
is a subalgebra of the algebra we obtain by restricting
\mathbf{B}
to the operations of
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
.
When this condition holds and
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
have the same universe, we say that
\mathbf{A}
is the reduct of
\mathbf{B}
. The same terminology applies to matrices if we replace “subalgebra” with “submatrix”. A class
\mathsf{K}
is a variety if it is closed under
\mathbb{H},\mathbb{S},\mathbb{P}
, and it is a quasivariety if it is closed under
\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{U}}
. When
\mathsf{K}
is a finite set of finite algebras, the smallest quasivariety containing
\mathsf{K}
is
\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathsf{K})
. A quasivariety is axiomatized by a set of quasi-equations, namely universal first-order sentences in prenex normal form, where the non quantified part is an implication whose antecedent is a finite conjunction of equations and the consequent is a single equation.

3.
The external version of a three-valued logic

We say that a logic
L
is
n
-valued if
n
is the smallest cardinality that a complete reduced model for
L
can have.
For instance, Classical logic
CL
is two-valued, Belnap-Dunn logic
FDE
is four-valued, while Intuitionistic logic is not
n
-valued, for any
n\in\omega
.
Three-valued logics typically arise as an attempt to weakening classical logic and rejecting some of its distinctive inferences. This is done by expanding the classical (Boolean) domain
\{0,1\}
with a third non-classical truth value (
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
), whose interpretation may vary depending on the specific context under consideration. For instance, in the Logic of Paradox (
LP
) the rules of disjunctive syllogism (
\varphi,\neg\varphi\lor\psi\vdash\psi
) and of ex falso quodlibet (
\varphi\land\neg\varphi\vdash\psi
) admit a countermodel by evaluating
\varphi
to
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
and
\psi
to
0
.

From now on, it is convenient to denote the universe of a three-valued matrix by
\{0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},1\}
, where
\{0,1\}
is the subuniverse of the two elements Boolean algebra. This kind of logics retain the classical language
\langle\land,\lor,\neg\rangle
as a (not necessarily proper) fragment and, semantically, their operations behave classically when restricted to the Boolean universe.
Examples can be found in the realm of Kleene logics (e.g.
\textsc{LP},\textsc{K}_{3},\textsc{B},\textsc{PWK}
), relevant logics (e.g.
\textsc{RM}_{3}
,
\textsc{S}_{3}
) and fuzzy logics (
\textsc{\L}_{3},\textsc{J}_{3}
), semantically defined in Example
3.2
.

subclassical logic

Definition 3.1
.

Let

\langle\mathbf{A},F\rangle
(which will be called

L
is a subclassical logic if

(i)

\mathbf{B}_{2}
is a subreduct of
\mathbf{A}
and
F\cap\mathbf{B}_{2}=\{1\}
;

(ii)

\mathbf{A}
satisfies
\varphi\approx\neg\neg\varphi
.

In general, no philosophical or arithmetical reading of the non-classical element

Before proceeding, we clearly state an assumption that will be effective for the rest of the paper.

Assumption 1
.

We assume that, whenever
L
is a subclassical three-valued logic whose defining matrix is
\langle\mathbf{A},F\rangle
, then
\Alg\textsc{L}=\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{A})
.

All logics of Example
3.2
satisfy Assumption

1
, which always holds when
L
is algebraizable (see Theorem
5.4
).
One can notice that the just mentioned three-valued subclassical logics can be partitioned into two
subclasses, depending of the set of designated values they preserve in the consequence relation. More precisely, given a three-valued algebra
\mathbf{A}
having
\mathbf{B}_{2}
as subreduct, the only two non-degenerate filters over
\mathbf{A}
are
\{1\}
and
\{1,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\}
. This fact is well-reflected by several logics discussed above, which are defined by matrices sharing the same algebraic reduct. The pairs
(\textsc{K}_{3},\textsc{LP})
,
(\textsc{B},\textsc{PWK})
,
(\textsc{S}_{t},\textsc{S})
,
(\textsc{\L}_{3},\textsc{J}_{3})
are instances of this phenomenon with respect to the algebras
\mathbf{WK},\mathbf{SK},\mathbf{S}_{3},\mathbf{L}_{3}
.
We are now ready to introduce the notion of external version of a subclassical logic.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
L
be a three-valued subclassical logic in the language
\mathcal{L}
, whose characteristic matrix is
\langle\mathbf{A},F\rangle
. The external version of
L
,
\textsc{L}^{e}
, is the logic defined by the matrix
\langle\mathbf{A}^{e},F\rangle
, where
\mathbf{A}^{e}
is the algebra in the language
\mathcal{L}\cup\{\Delta_{1}\}
with universe
A
and whose operations are defined as:

\circ^{\mathbf{A}^{e}}=\circ^{\mathbf{A}}
, for
\circ\in\mathcal{L}
and

\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{A}^{e}}(a)=\begin{cases}1\text{ if }a=1\\
0\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

With these notions at play it is possible to see that
\textsc{\L}_{3}
is the external version of
\textsc{K}_{3}
,
\textsc{J}_{3}
is the external version of
LP
and, of course,
\textsc{B}^{e}
(see
[
9
]
) is the external version of
B
.

It now seems natural to investigate the relationship between a subclassical logic
L
and its external version
\textsc{L}^{e}
. With this goal in mind, we proceed to we tackle two central questions.

•

How are the matrix models of
L
and of
\textsc{L}^{e}
related?

•

Which semantic properties are preserved or gained in the step from
L
to
\textsc{L}^{e}
?

4.
The semantic meaning of external operators

The goal of this section is to study the semantic effect that external operators have on the class of models naturally associated with a subclassical logic. One might expect that, given a model of a subclassical logic, it is always possible to define an appropriate external operation
\Delta_{1}
, in order to obtain a model if its external version. However, this is not the case. The definability of such an operator forces different, non-trivial semantic conditions. More precisely, the central question to answer is: when can the algebraic reduct of a reduced model of a subclassical logic
L
be expanded to a reduced model of its external version
\textsc{L}^{e}
?
In the special case of the logic
\textsc{K}_{3}
, a solution was given in the pioneering work of
[
15
]
. In the case of Bochvar logic, this result has been recently established, with different methods, in
[
10
,
9
]
.
In both cases, the conceptual framework employed rely on very peculiar properties of the respective algebras and of the associated logics. Here, as we shall se, we will provide a general solution to the problem which is based on the theory of
nuclei
. As a result, it turns out that the algebraic reduct of a reduced model
\mathbf{B}
of
L
can be turned into a reduced model of
\textsc{L}^{e}
is possible if only if a specific conucleus is definable on
\mathbf{B}
.

As in the rest of the paper, let us denote by
\mathbf{A}
the algebra that supports the reduced matrix
\langle\mathbf{A},F\rangle
of a three-valued subclassical logic
L
. Also recall that, as stated in Assumption
1
, we require
\Alg\textsc{L}=\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{A})
(notice that all the subclassical logics discussed so far fulfill this assumption). Now, if we consider
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{A})
, namely
\mathbf{B}\leq\mathbf{A}^{I}
, for some set of indexes
I
, each
b\in\mathbf{B}
is a tuple
(\pi_{i}b)_{i\in I}
such that
\pi_{i}b\in\{0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},1\}
for every
i\in I
, where
\pi_{i}b
denotes the
i
-th entry of
b
. In plain words, each element of
\mathbf{B}
is a tuple whose entries are values among the underlying set
\{0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},1\}
of
\mathbf{A}
. We now introduce the notion of
Boolean skeleton
for an algebra
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{A})
, as it will play a central role in this section.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathbf{C}\leq\mathbf{A}^{I}
. The Boolean Skeleton of
\mathbf{C}
,
\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{C}}
for short, is defined as

\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{C}}\mathrel{:=}\{c\in\mathbf{C}:\pi_{i}c\in\{0,1\},\text{ for all }i\in I\}.

One can see that, if
\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{C}}\neq\emptyset
(the case
\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{C}}=\emptyset
may arise, as we do not assume constant operations in our language), then it forms a subalgebra of
\mathbf{C}
. Moreover, for each
c\in\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{C}}
,
\pi_{i}(c\land\neg c)=0
,
\pi_{i}(c\lor\neg c)=1
for each
i\in I
. In other words, the elements of the Boolean skeleton of
\mathbf{C}\leq\mathbf{A}^{I}
are tuples whose values are among
\{0,1\}
and they form a Boolean subalgebra of
\mathbf{C}
.
Since
\Alg\textsc{L}
is a quasivariety, this notion can be naturally extended to an arbitrary
\mathbf{B}\in\Alg\textsc{L}
by taking isomorphic pre-images, as specified by the next definition.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{B}\in\Alg\textsc{L}
, namely
\mathbf{B}\cong\mathbf{C}\leq\mathbf{A}^{I}
, where
h
is an isomorphism
\mathbf{B}\to\mathbf{C}
. The Boolean skeleton of
\mathbf{B}
determined by
h
is
\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{B}}\mathrel{:=}h^{-1}[\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{C}}]
.

Notice that in Definition
4.2
the Boolean skeleton of
\mathbf{B}
is relative to a fixed isomorphism
h:\mathbf{B}\to\mathbf{C}
. Moreover, this definition has a purely technical role, and it does not introduce any real conceptual gain with respect to Definition
4.1
. Indeed, up to isomorphism, all the algebras belonging to
\Alg\textsc{L}
can be thought as subalgebras of
\mathbf{A}^{I}
.

Now, let us define a partial order
\preceq
on
\{0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},1\}
as
0\preceq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\preceq 1
. For
a,b\in\mathbf{C}\leq\mathbf{A}^{I}
, set
a\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}b
if and only if
\pi_{i}a\preceq\pi_{i}b
, for each
i\in I
. When
h:\mathbf{B}\to\mathbf{C}
is an isomorphism and
c,d\in\mathbf{B}
, we set
c\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}d
if and only if
h(c)\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}h(d)
. It is trivial to check that
\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}},\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}
are partial orders. It is clear that this order can be equally defined on algebras belonging to
\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{A}^{e})
.
The key tool which will allow to transparently describe the semantic meaning of external operators is the concept of
\emph{conucleus}
. The notion of conucleus found useful applications in logic, and historically plays a central role in the study of varieties of residuated lattices, namely algebraic varieties arising as the equivalent algebraic semantics of substructural logics
[
28
]
,
[
29
]
.
For our purposes, a convenient formulation of the definition of conucleus is the following.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\mathbf{B}\cong\mathbf{C}\leq\mathbf{A}^{I}
, and
h
be an isomorphism
\mathbf{B}\to\mathbf{C}
.
A conucleus on
\mathbf{B}
is a map
\sigma:\mathbf{B}\to{\mathbf{B}}
which is monotone with respect to
\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}
and such that:

(i)

\sigma(a)\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}a
;

(ii)

\sigma(a)\land\sigma(b)\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}\sigma(a\land b)
;

(iii)

\sigma(\sigma(a))=\sigma(a)
,

for every
a,b\in\mathbf{B}
.

We will refer to
\sigma[\mathbf{B}]=\{a\in\mathbf{B}:\sigma(b)=a\text{ for some }a\in\mathbf{B}\}
as the conuclear image of
\sigma
. As we shall see, we will be interested in special kinds of conuclei, whose features are defined below.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\mathbf{B}\cong\mathbf{C}\leq\mathbf{A}^{I}
, and
h
be an isomorphism
\mathbf{B}\to\mathbf{C}
. A conucleus
\sigma
on
\mathbf{B}
is called:

•

Boolean
if its conuclear image is
\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{B}}
;

•

complete
if, for each
a\in\mathbf{B}
,
\bigvee^{\mathbf{A}^{I}}\{b\in\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{A}^{I}}:b\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{A}^{I}}}h(a)\}\in h(\sigma[\mathbf{B}])
.

We will say that
\mathbf{B}\in\Alg\textsc{L}

admits
a conucleus when there exists
\mathbf{C}\leq\mathbf{A}^{I}
and an isomorphism
h:\mathbf{B}\to\mathbf{C}
such that
the conditions of Definition
4.3
apply, and similarly for the specifications introduced in Definition
4.4
.
Notice that if
\sigma
is a Boolean conucleus on
\mathbf{B}
, then
\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{B}}\neq\emptyset
,
\sigma[\mathbf{B}]=\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{B}}
and
a\in\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{B}}
entails
\sigma(a)=a
. Moreover, the condition that
\sigma
is complete entails that the join
\bigvee\{b\in\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{A}^{I}}:b\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{A}^{I}}}h(a)\}
exists in
\mathbf{A}^{I}
, for each
a\in\mathbf{B}
.

When needed, we will write
\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}
to emphasize the fact that this is conucleus on
\mathbf{B}
and we will sometimes omit parenthesis in order to simplify the notation. If we replace
\mathbf{A}^{I}
with
(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}
and
L
with
\textsc{L}^{e}
in Definitions
4.3
,
4.4
, they obviously extend to algebras belonging to
\Alg\textsc{L}^{e}
.The following fact is an immediate consequence of the definitions and the fact that the desired property of being a Boolean conucleus is preserved by isomorphic pre images.

Fact 4.5
.

Let
\mathbf{B}
be the
\Delta_{1}
-free reduct of an
\Alg\textsc{L}^{e}
-algebra
\mathbf{B}^{\Delta}
and let
h:\mathbf{B}^{\Delta}\to\mathbf{C}\leq(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}
be an isomorphism. Suppose
\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}
is a Boolean conucleus on
\mathbf{C}
. The map
\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}
defined for every
a\in\mathbf{B}
as
\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}(a)=h^{-1}\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}(h(a))
is a Boolean conucleus on
\mathbf{B}
.

The following lemma characterizes how the operation
\Delta_{1}
is computed in a power of
\mathbf{A}^{e}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
a\in(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}
. Then
\Delta_{1}a=\bigvee^{(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}}\{b\in\mathfrak{B}^{(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}}:b\preceq_{{}_{(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}}}a\}.

Proof.

Consider
a\in(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}
. In order to simplify the notation, let us write
\preceq
as a shorthand for
\preceq_{{}_{(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}}}
and set

X=\{b\in\mathfrak{B}^{(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}}:b\preceq a\}.

Notice that
X
is clearly non-empty, as
\Delta_{1}a\in\mathfrak{B}^{(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}}
and
\Delta_{1}a\preceq a
. This is true because the operation
\Delta_{1}
is computed component-wise over a power of
\mathbf{A}^{e}
. Suppose, towards a contradiction, that
b\in X
and
b\npreceq\Delta_{1}a
. Thus
\pi_{i}b=1
and
\pi_{i}\Delta_{1}a=0
for some
i\in I
, which entails
\pi_{i}a=0
. However,
b\preceq a
implies
\pi_{i}a=1
, a contradiction. This proves that
\Delta_{1}a
is an upper bound of
X
. The fact that
\Delta_{1}a=\bigvee X
follows from the fact that if
b\in X
and
b\preceq\Delta_{1}a
then
b=\Delta_{1}a
.
∎

In the view of the above lemma, an equivalent description of how the value of the
\Delta_{1}
operator is computed is the following:

\Delta_{1}a=\text{max}\{b\in\mathfrak{B}^{(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}}:b\preceq_{{}_{(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}}}a\}.

We are now ready to state the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\mathbf{B}\in\Alg\textsc{L}
. The following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathbf{B}
can be equipped with the structure of an
\Alg\textsc{L}^{e}
-algebra;

(ii)

\mathbf{B}
admits a complete Boolean conucleus.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii).
Let
\mathbf{B}\in\Alg\textsc{L}
and suppose it is possible to equip
\mathbf{B}
with a unary operation
\Delta_{1}
, i.e. we can expand
\mathbf{B}
with a unary operation
\Delta_{1}
, so that the expanded algebra
\mathbf{B}^{\Delta}\in\Alg\textsc{L}^{e}
. This means that
\mathbf{B}^{\Delta}
is isomorphic via a map
h
to some
\mathbf{C}\leq(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}
. Thus, the elements of
\mathbf{C}
are tuples whose entries are among
\{0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},1\}
, i.e., for
a\in\mathbf{C}
,
\pi_{i}c\in\{0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},1\}
for every
i\in I
. Since
\mathbf{C}\leq(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}
,
\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}
is map
\mathbf{C}\to\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{C}}
. This is true because, for any element
a\in\mathbf{C}
,
\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}a=(\Delta^{(\mathbf{A}^{e})}\pi_{i}a)_{i\in I}
and clearly
\Delta_{1}^{(\mathbf{A}^{e})}\pi_{i}a\in\{0,1\}
for every
i\in I
. Thus,
\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}
is a map
\mathbf{C}\to\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{B}}
. We now show that
\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}
is a Boolean conucleus on
\mathbf{C}
. To this end, recall the partial order
0\preceq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\preceq 1
and its realization
\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}
over the algebra
\mathbf{C}
defined, for every
a,b\in\mathbf{C}
as
a\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}b
if and only if
\pi_{i}a\preceq\pi_{i}b
for every
i\in I
. If we consider
a\in\mathbf{C}
, it holds that

\pi_{i}\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}a=\begin{cases}1\text{ if }\pi_{i}a=1\\
0\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

thus
\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}a\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}a
. Moreover, if
a\in\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{C}}
, clearly
\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}a=a
.

Let now
a\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}b
. By the above display (
4
) it is immediate to check that that if
\pi_{i}a\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}\pi_{i}b
then
\pi_{i}\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}a\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}\pi_{i}\Delta_{1}^{c}b
, so
\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}
is monotone with respect to
\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}
. The fact that
\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}(\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}a)=\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}a
can be checked similarly.
We now prove (ii) in Definition
4.4
, namely that
\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}a\land\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}b\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}(a\land b)
. Observe that
\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}a\land\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}b,\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}(a\land b)\in\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{C}}
and that, for every
c,d\in\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{C}}
it holds that
c\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}d
if and only if
c\land d=c
. Moreover, as
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{A}^{e})
, it satisfies all the quasi-equations that hold in
\mathbf{A}^{e}
. Since it is easy to check that

\mathbf{A}^{e}\vDash(\Delta_{1}\varphi\land\Delta_{1}\psi)\land(\Delta_{1}(\varphi\land\psi))\approx(\Delta_{1}\varphi\land\Delta_{1}\psi),

then

(\Delta_{1}a\land\Delta_{1}b)\land(\Delta_{1}(a\land b))=(\Delta_{1}a\land\Delta_{1}b)

holds in
\mathbf{C}
, thus
\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}a\land\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}b\preceq_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}(a\land b)
. This shows
\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}
is a Boolean conucleus. By Fact
4.5
the map
\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}
defined for every
a\in\mathbf{B}
as
\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}(a)=h^{-1}\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}(h(a))
is a Boolean conucleus on
\mathbf{B}
. It remains to show this conucleus is also complete, but this follows from Lemma
4.6
, upon noticing that, for
a\in\mathbf{B}
,
\Delta^{(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}}h(a)=\Delta_{1}^{\mathbf{C}}h(a)\in\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{C}}=h(\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}[\mathbf{B}])
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i). Consider
\mathbf{B}\in\Alg\textsc{L}=\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{A})
. Suppose
\mathbf{B}
admits a complete Boolean conucleus
\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}:\mathbf{B}\to\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{B}}
, where
\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{B}}
is the Boolean skeleton
determined by some algebra
\mathbf{C}\leq\mathbf{A}^{I}
isomorphic to
\mathbf{B}
via
h:\mathbf{B}\to\mathbf{C}
. It is immediate to check that the map defined for every
a\in\mathbf{C}
as
\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}a=h(\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}h^{-1}a)
is a Boolean conucleus on
\mathbf{C}
. Consider the algebra
\mathbf{B}^{\sigma}
obtained by expanding
\mathbf{B}
with the operation
\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}
, and similarly for
\mathbf{C}
with respect to
\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}
. Clearly the
isomorphism
h
extends to an isomorphism between the expanded algebras
h:\mathbf{B}^{\sigma}\to\mathbf{C}^{\sigma}
because, for
a\in\mathbf{B}
,
\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}h(a)=h(\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}h^{-1}ha)=h(\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}a)
. We only need to show that, for
h(a)\in\mathbf{C}^{\sigma}
,
{\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}}h(a)=\Delta_{1}h(a)
where
\Delta_{1}
is the operation computed on
(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}
. From the fact that
\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}
is complete, together with Lemma
4.6
, we obtain that

\Delta_{1}h(a)=\bigvee\{b\in\mathfrak{B}^{(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}}:b\preceq h(a)\}\in h(\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{B}}}[\mathbf{B}])=\mathfrak{B}^{\mathbf{C}},

where the join takes place in
(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}
and
\preceq
is a shorthand for
\preceq_{{}_{(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}}}
.
Clearly,
{\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}}h(a)\preceq h(a)
because
{\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}}
is a
Boolean conucleus, so by the above display
\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}h(a)\preceq\Delta_{1}h(a)
. Moreover, since
\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}
is monotone,
\Delta_{1}h(a)\preceq h(a)
entails
\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}\Delta_{1}h(a)=\Delta_{1}h(a)\preceq\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}h(a)
, thus
\sigma_{{}_{\mathbf{C}}}h(a)=\Delta_{1}h(a)
, as desired.
∎

The intuitive meaning of being a complete Boolean conucleus can be described as follows. If we see the elements of a model as truth-values, it expresses the fact that, whenever
a
is a non-classical truth value, then it is possible to select the greatest Boolean truth value below
a
. In other words, completeness ensures that a notion of “closest” Boolean value is always defined in a model.

The following corollary provide a valuable criterion to identify the algebras of
\Alg\textsc{L}
that cannot be equipped with the structure of an
\textsc{L}^{e}
-algebra.

Corollary 4.8
.

Suppose
\mathbf{B}\in\Alg\textsc{L}
admits a Boolean complete conucleus
\sigma
. Then, for different elements
a,b\in\mathbf{B}
it holds that

\text{if }\sigma(a)=\sigma(b)\text{ then }\sigma(\neg a)\neq\sigma(\neg b).

Proof.

Theorem
4.7
ensures that the expansion
\mathbf{B}^{\sigma}
of
\mathbf{B}
is isomorphic via
h
to some
\mathbf{C}\leq(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}
, thus, for
a\in\mathbf{B}
,
h(\sigma a)=\Delta_{1}h(a)
, where
\Delta_{1}
is the operation on
(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}
. It easy to check that

\mathbf{A}^{e}\vDash\Delta_{1}\varphi\thickapprox\Delta_{1}\psi~\&~\Delta_{1}\neg\varphi\thickapprox\Delta_{1}\neg\psi\implies\varphi\thickapprox\psi.

Since the validity of quasi-equations persists in isomorphic preimages of subalgebras of products, (
4.8
) is true in
\mathbf{B}^{\sigma}
. Thus, for different
a,b\in\mathbf{B}
,
\sigma(a)=\sigma(b)
entails
\sigma(\neg a)\neq\sigma(\neg b)
.
∎

Lemma 4.10

(
[
11
]
, Thm. 7.15)
.

Let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
be similar algebras. Then
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{B})
if and only if there exists a family of homomorphisms

such that
\bigcap_{i\in I}\ker h_{i}
is the identity relation.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
L
be a subclassical logic such that
0\lor\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\neq 0
is true in
\mathbf{A}^{e}
. Then, for
\mathbf{B}\in\Alg\textsc{L}
the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathbf{B}
can be turned into an
\Alg\textsc{L}^{e}
-algebra;

(ii)

there exists a Boolean conucleus
\sigma
on
\mathbf{B}
satisfying
x\preceq\sigma x\lor\neg x
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii).
If
\mathbf{B}
can be turned into an
\Alg\textsc{L}^{e}
-algebra, then the expansion
\mathbf{B}^{\Delta}
of
\mathbf{B}
is isomorphic to
\mathbf{C}\leq(\mathbf{A}^{e})^{I}
via
h
. Theorem
4.7
ensures that
\Delta_{1}
on
\mathbf{B}
is a Boolean conucleus. The fact that for
a\in\mathbf{B}
, it holds

is equivalent to

5.
External operators and classical recapture

As already hinted, the operator
\Delta_{1}
can be seen a way to provide a yes/no answer, understood as a
0/1
-answer, to the question of whether its argument is both classical and true. In other words,
\Delta_{1}
enriches the expressive power of the underlying logic
L
in order to re-capture the “external” notion of classical truth, which cannot be represented by the linguistic resources of
L
alone. This section explores some of the classical features that are recaptured in the step from a sublassical logic
L
to its external version
\textsc{L}^{e}
.

Informally speaking, it is easy to see that if
\Gamma\vdash\varphi
is a classical rule failing in
L
, the external version of
L
validates a rule encoding the idea that “whenever the arguments of the formulas occurring in the rule can be safely asserted, the rule can be safely applied”. The next proposition shows how to produce
\textsc{L}^{e}
-valid inferences of this kind.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}\vdash\varphi
be a classically valid rule and
\mathbf{Fm}
be the formula algebra in the language of
\textsc{L}^{e}
. Then

h(\gamma_{1}),\dots,h(\gamma_{n})\vdash h(\varphi)
is a rule of
\textsc{L}^{e}
where

h:\mathbf{Fm}\to\mathbf{Fm}
is the substitution mapping
x\mapsto\Delta_{1}x
for each variable
x
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}\vdash\varphi
be a classically valid rule and let
h
be as in the statement. The restriction of
h
to the language
\langle\land,\lor,\neg,\Delta_{1}\rangle
is a substitution on the formula algebra of
\textsc{CL}^{e}
, thus,
h(\gamma_{1}),\dots,h(\gamma_{n})\vdash h(\varphi)
is a rule of
\textsc{CL}^{e}
. Let
g:\mathbf{Fm}\to\mathbf{A}^{e}
, where
\langle\mathbf{A},F\rangle
is the defining matrix of
L
, a homomorphism such that
g(h(\gamma_{1}))\in F,\dots,g(h(\gamma_{n}))\in F
. By definition of
h
this entails
g(h(\gamma_{i}))=1
for each
1\leq i\leq n
. Since
h(\gamma_{1}),\dots,h(\gamma_{n})\vdash h(\varphi)
is a rule of
\textsc{CL}^{e}
we conclude that
g(h(\varphi))=1
.
∎

Let us briefly provide an example.

Example 5.2
.

Consider the logic
\textsc{PWK}^{e}
. Clearly the rule of modus ponens
\varphi,\neg\varphi\lor\psi\vdash\psi
fails in
PWK
. From Proposition
5.1
it follows that

\Delta_{1}\varphi,\neg(\Delta_{1}\varphi)\lor\Delta_{1}\psi\vdash\Delta_{1}\psi

is a valid rule of
\textsc{PWK}^{e}
.
A similar argument shows that
\Delta_{1}\varphi\lor\neg\Delta_{1}\varphi
is a theorem of
\textsc{B}^{e}
. It is important to notice that
\Delta_{1}(\varphi\lor\neg\varphi)
is not a theorem of
\textsc{B}^{e}
: this emphasizes the role of the substitution
h
in Proposition
5.1
.

We now recall the notion of algebraizability. This concept lies at the core of algebraic logic, and it requires the availability of a mutual interpretation between a logic
L
and the equational consequence of a specific class of algebras
\mathsf{K}
. This interpretation is witnessed by two maps
\boldsymbol{\tau}(x)
,
\boldsymbol{\rho}(x,y)
that assign a set of equations
\boldsymbol{\tau}(\varphi)
to each formula
\varphi
and a set of formulas
\boldsymbol{\rho}(\varphi,\psi)
to each equation
\varphi\approx\psi
.

Definition 5.3
.

A logic
L
is algebraizable with respect to the class of algebras
\mathsf{K}
(in the same language as
L
) if there are maps
\boldsymbol{\tau}:Fm\to\mathcal{P}(Eq)
from formulas to sets of equations and
\boldsymbol{\rho}:Eq\to\mathcal{P}(Fm)
from equations to sets of formulas such that the following hold:

(Alg1)

\displaystyle\Gamma\vdash_{\textsc{L}}\varphi\iff\boldsymbol{\tau}(\Gamma)\vDash_{\mathsf{K}}\boldsymbol{\tau}(\varphi)

(Alg2)

\displaystyle\varphi\approx\psi\mathrel{\mathchoice{\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vDash$}}{\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vDash$}}{\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vDash$}}{\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vDash$}}}\vDash_{\mathsf{K}}\boldsymbol{\tau}\boldsymbol{\rho}(\varphi\approx\psi).

In the case of classical logic the two maps witnessing the algebraizability with respect to the variety of Boolean algebras
\mathsf{BA}
are defined as
\boldsymbol{\tau}(\varphi)=\{\varphi\approx\varphi\lor\neg\varphi\}
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}(\varphi\approx\psi)=\{\varphi\to\psi,\psi\to\varphi\}
. Conditions
Alg1
,
Alg2
are instantiated accordingly by letting
\mathsf{K}=\mathsf{BA}
.
Remarkably, if a logic is algebraizable with respect to
\mathsf{K}
, then it is algebraizable with respect to
\Alg\textsc{L}
. In this case
\Alg\textsc{L}
is called the equivalent algebraic semantics of
L
.
The following important theorem will be used in the forthcoming part.

Theorem 5.4
.

[
22
, Thm.3.2.2]
. Let
L
be an algebraizable with respect to a finite set of formulas
\boldsymbol{\rho}(x,y)
. If
L
has a reduced characteristic matrix
\langle\mathbf{A},F\rangle
and
\mathbf{A}
is finite, then
\Alg\textsc{L}=\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{A})
.

In general, the notion of algebraizability states an equivalence, witnessed by the maps
\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{\rho}
, between the consequence relation of a logic and the equational consequence of its algebraic counterpart.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let

\Alg\textsc{L}^{e}=\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{A}^{e})

Recall that a logic

(DDT)

\displaystyle\Gamma,\varphi\vdash_{\textsc{L}}\psi\iff\Gamma\vdash_{\textsc{L}}I(\varphi,\psi).

Notice also that being algebraizable and having the DDT are independent properties.

Unsurprisingly, the Deduction Theorem is a further property recaptured in the step from

Fact 5.6
.

The external version of a three-valued subclassical logic has the DDT.

The set

witnesses the DDT for

\varphi\to_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\psi=\neg(\Delta_{1}\varphi\lor\Delta_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\varphi)\lor(\Delta_{1}\psi\lor\Delta_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\psi)
does it so for for
\textsc{L}^{e}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
. ∎

Notice that the traditional DDT-set for
\textsc{\L}_{3}
is a singleton containing the formula

never appears in the output when interpreted in

As we already sketched, subclassical logics come into pairs of logics of the form
(\textsc{L}_{1},\textsc{L}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})
sharing the same defining three-valued algebra. In many cases,
\textsc{L}_{1}
lack any theorem, while
\textsc{L}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
lacks antitheorems, and this suffices to show that a faithful mutual interpretation satisfying

(\textsc{K}_{3},\textsc{LP})
and

(\textsc{L}_{1},\textsc{L}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})
are deductively equivalent.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
\textsc{L}_{1},\textsc{L}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
be three-valued subclassical logics. Then
\textsc{L}_{1}^{e},\textsc{L}^{e}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
are pairwise deductively equivalent.

Proof.

Recalling Definition
5.7
we need to prove that

(De1)

\displaystyle\Gamma\vdash_{\textsc{L}^{e}_{1}}\varphi\iff\Delta_{1}\Gamma\vdash_{\textsc{L}^{e}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\Delta_{1}\varphi

(De2)

\displaystyle\varphi\vdash_{\textsc{L}^{e}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\Delta_{1}(\Delta_{1}\varphi\lor\Delta_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\varphi)\text{ and }\Delta_{1}(\Delta_{1}\varphi\lor\Delta_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\varphi)\vdash_{\textsc{L}^{e}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\varphi.

We begin by proving (
De1

\Gamma\vdash_{\textsc{L}^{e}_{1}}\varphi
and let

be such that

for each

\Delta_{1}
, this entails

. So, by assumption,
h(\varphi)=1
. Thus, we conclude

, which proves
\Delta_{1}\Gamma\vdash_{\textsc{L}^{e}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\Delta_{1}\varphi
. For the converse we reason by contraposition, so suppose there exists

such that

for each
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
h(\varphi)\neq 1
. . Thus,
h(\Delta_{1}\gamma)=1
for each

, i.e.
\Delta_{1}\Gamma\nvdash_{\textsc{L}^{e}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\Delta_{1}\varphi

For (

Thus, adding external operators to a subclassical logic ensures the recovery of several desirable properties of classical logic, such as the Deduction-Detachment Theorem–and consequently, modus ponens and theoremhood–as well as algebraizability. Moreover, this method of constructing expansions naturally yields examples of pairwise deductively equivalent logics.
