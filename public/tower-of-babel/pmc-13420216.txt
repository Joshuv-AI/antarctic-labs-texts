The 2026 World Delirium Awareness Day Survey: Findings From Turkish Intensive Care Units
Authors: Öznur Erbay Dalli, Ebru Kiraner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDelirium is a common and serious complication in intensive care units (ICUs) and is associated with adverse patient outcomes. World Delirium Awareness Day (WDAD), observed annually on the second Wednesday of March, provides a platform for international point prevalence studies to evaluate delirium burden and care practices across healthcare systems. Although WDAD studies have been conducted in numerous countries worldwide, no data from Turkiye have been reported to date.AimTo determine the point prevalence of delirium and examine current delirium assessment practices, management strategies and barriers to evidence‐based care in Turkish intensive care units on WDAD 2026.Study DesignA cross‐sectional point prevalence study was conducted on 11 March 2026, using the standardised WDAD survey. Delirium prevalence was assessed at 8:00 a.m. and 8:00 p.m.ResultsRepresentatives from 66 ICUs (52 adult and 14 paediatric) across seven geographical regions of Turkiye participated in the study. Among patients assessed for delirium, 30.8% tested positive at 8:00 a.m. (adult ICU: 32.2%; PICU: 25.0%) and 30.2% at 8:00 p.m. (adult ICU: 31.9%; PICU: 23.0%). The majority of units (87.9%) relied on clinical judgement for delirium assessment rather than validated screening tools. Written delirium protocols were present in only 30.3% of units. Dexmedetomidine was the most commonly used pharmacological agent (54.5%). Mobilisation was implemented in less than half of the units (43.9%). The most frequently reported barriers were missing knowledge (69.7%), attitude that delirium is not considered important (57.6%) and lack of awareness (50.0%).ConclusionRoutine use of validated delirium assessment tools, enhanced staff education and standardised protocol implementation are needed to improve delirium recognition and management in Turkish critical care settings.Relevance to Clinical PracticeThese findings provide the first insights into delirium care practices in Turkish ICUs, highlighting the urgent need for structured education programmes, validated screening tools and national delirium care guidelines.

Introduction

Delirium is an acute disturbance in attention and awareness, accompanied by a change in baseline cognition that tends to fluctuate during the day and cannot be explained by a pre‐existing neurocognitive disorder [1]. Risk factors for delirium can be divided into predisposing factors, such as older age, pre‐existing cognitive impairment and comorbidities, and precipitating factors, including severity of illness, infections, mechanical ventilation, use of sedatives and environmental factors such as sleep disruption and restricted family visiting [2, 3]. Delirium is a common complication in intensive care unit (ICU) patients, with reported prevalence rates ranging from 20% to over 80% depending on the patient population and assessment methods used [4, 5].

Delirium is independently associated with numerous adverse outcomes including prolonged mechanical ventilation, increased ICU and hospital length of stay, higher risk of falls and aspiration events, long‐term cognitive impairment, institutionalisation and increased mortality [3, 6]. The economic burden is substantial, with estimated annual costs exceeding 164 billion USD in the United States alone [7]. Despite these significant consequences, delirium remains frequently underrecognised in clinical practice, with studies suggesting that the majority of cases may be missed when validated screening tools are not routinely used [8, 9, 10]. Hypoactive delirium, characterised by decreased psychomotor activity and often mistaken for fatigue or depression, is particularly prone to underdetection and is associated with worse outcomes compared to other motor subtypes [11, 12].

The Pain, Agitation/Sedation, Delirium, Immobility and Sleep Disruption (PADIS) guidelines recommend routine delirium screening using validated tools such as the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM‐ICU) or the Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist (ICDSC), along with multicomponent prevention and management strategies [13]. However, the implementation of these evidence‐based practices varies widely across healthcare settings globally. Previous surveys have reported routine delirium screening rates ranging from 10% to 90%, and the presence of delirium management protocols ranging from 35% to 89% across different countries [14, 15]. In Turkiye, a recent study by Erbay Dalli et al. (2024) found that only 54.7% of ICU nurses performed routine delirium monitoring, and 79.2% reported that their units lacked a protocol for delirium assessment, suggesting substantial gaps in delirium care practices [16].

World Delirium Awareness Day (WDAD), held annually on the second Wednesday of March, was established to increase global awareness of delirium as a serious and often preventable condition [17]. This international initiative, supported by delirium associations worldwide, including the American Delirium Society, European Delirium Association and Australian Delirium Association, provides a platform for clinicians and researchers to collaborate on point prevalence studies assessing delirium burden and care practices across different healthcare systems. Previous WDAD studies have been conducted in several countries, including the Netherlands, the United States, Ireland, Italy and Canada, providing valuable benchmarking data on delirium prevalence and management practices [7, 14, 15, 18, 19, 20, 21]. However, no such nationwide study has been conducted in Turkiye to date. Given the limited data on delirium care practices in Turkish ICUs and the potential for significant variation from international standards, a comprehensive assessment is essential to identify current gaps and inform targeted quality improvement strategies. Establishing baseline data through participation in this global initiative may enable comparison with international benchmarks and facilitate the development of evidence‐based national guidelines for delirium care.

Aim

This study aimed to determine the point prevalence of delirium and to describe current delirium‐related structures, processes, management practices and barriers to evidence‐based care in Turkish adult and paediatric ICUs on WDAD 2026.

Methods

Study Design

This cross‐sectional point prevalence study was conducted on 11 March 2026 (World Delirium Awareness Day) to assess the prevalence of delirium among screened patients and evaluate delirium management practices in Turkish ICUs. This study is reported according to the Consensus‐Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS).

Data Collection Methods

The original 35‐question questionnaire developed by the WDAD Study Team was used in this study (20). Permission to use the survey was obtained via e‐mail from Dr Peter Nydahl. The survey included the following sections: (1) sociodemographic data; (2) hospital data; (3) unit/ward data; (4) delirium‐related structures and processes; (5) delirium prevalence in the morning; (6) delirium prevalence in the evening; (7) non‐pharmacological prevention and treatment; (8) pharmacological treatment; (9) barriers; and (10) free text boxes [19, 21].

The survey was translated into Turkish in accordance with the cross‐cultural adaptation guidelines recommended [22]. Two independent bilingual translators performed forward translations, which were compared and reconciled. The reconciled version was then back‐translated into English by a third independent translator who was blinded to the original survey. The back‐translated version was reviewed for conceptual equivalence with the original and was found to be consistent. The translated survey was subsequently reviewed by an expert panel consisting of five specialists (two intensivists, two intensive care nurses and one psychiatrist) for content and face validity. Content validity was evaluated using the Content Validity Index (CVI) according to the Davis technique [23]. Experts rated each item on a 4‐point scale (1 = not appropriate, 2 = item needs revision, 3 = appropriate but minor revision needed, 4 = highly appropriate). Items rated as 3 or 4 were considered appropriate. The CVI was calculated as 0.92, exceeding the acceptable threshold of 0.80 [23]. All translated items met the predefined content validity criterion, as each item was rated as 3 or 4 by the expert panel. Therefore, no item required substantive revision or removal after expert review; only minor wording adjustments were made to improve clarity and linguistic appropriateness.

Participants recorded delirium prevalence data in their unit on 11 March 2026, at two time points (8:00 a.m. and 8:00 p.m.). Data were collected using a web‐based survey platform (Google Forms). No individual patient data were collected; only aggregated, anonymised unit‐level data were obtained. The survey was conducted in accordance with the Turkish Personal Data Protection Law (KVKK), which aligns with the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). To ensure data quality and avoid incomplete or duplicate responses, a confirmation statement was added at the end of the survey: ‘I confirm that I have completed this survey by providing accurate information’.

Primary and Secondary Outcomes

The primary outcome was the prevalence of delirium in patients screened for delirium. Delirium was defined as a positive delirium score using a valid and reliable delirium detection tool at one of two time points during the study day (8:00 a.m. and 8:00 p.m.).

The secondary outcomes included unit‐level delirium practices such as delirium‐related structures and processes, non‐pharmacological and pharmacological treatments used and barriers to delirium care.

Sample Characteristics

Participants were recruited through the Turkish Intensive Care Nurses Association. A convenience sampling method was used. Because participation was based on voluntary responses from eligible ICU representatives recruited through the Turkish Intensive Care Nurses Association, and because the total number of ICUs in Turkiye is not systematically reported, no formal sample size calculation was performed. One week prior to WDAD, an invitation poster was distributed to association members via e‐mail and the official WhatsApp communication group.

Participants were eligible to participate if they were (a) a charge nurse, assistant charge nurse or senior nurse; and (b) had permission to access and provide data from the adult or paediatric ICU where they work. Participants who expressed interest were added to a dedicated WhatsApp group created specifically for the study. Within this group, participants were informed that each respondent must represent a different ICU, and duplicate responses from the same unit would not be accepted. As the total number of ICUs in Turkiye is not systematically reported, the representativeness of our sample could not be determined.

On 9 March 2026, an online training session was conducted with all participants. During this session, detailed instructions were provided regarding the data collection process, with particular emphasis on how to accurately collect delirium prevalence data and key considerations to ensure data quality. For ICUs that relied solely on clinical judgement and did not routinely use validated delirium screening tools, additional training was provided on the use of the CAM‐ICU and the ICDSC for adult patients, and the Paediatric Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (pCAM‐ICU) for paediatric patients. Participants were encouraged to contact the research team for any questions or clarifications throughout the study period.

The study procedures followed the original international WDAD 2023 data collection protocol, including the point‐prevalence design, two assessment time points and unit‐level aggregated reporting. The WhatsApp group was used only for recruitment coordination, procedural reminders and clarification of data collection procedures. No patient‐level, unit‐level or identifiable study data were shared or discussed within this group.

Survey Administration

Participants completed the survey for delirium prevalence on their unit on 11 March 2026, at 8:00 a.m. and 8:00 p.m. Data were entered using a web‐based survey platform (Google Forms). Participants had 2 days to complete the data entry, with the survey closing on 13 March 2026.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Bursa Uludag University Health Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2026/136/5‐16, Date: 01 March 2026). The study was conducted on a voluntary basis among nurses who were members of the Turkish Intensive Care Nurses Association, with data reported by participating nurses. No individual patient data were collected; only aggregated unit‐level data regarding the total number of patients, those with delirium and those who were unassessable were obtained. Therefore, patient informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. For participating nurses, an informed consent statement was provided at the beginning of the online survey, indicating that completion of the survey implied consent to participate in the study. To protect participant anonymity and ensure data confidentiality, all survey responses were de‐identified before analysis and securely stored in compliance with institutional data protection policies. The anonymised dataset is stored on a password‐protected institutional computer and in password‐protected institutional cloud storage accessible only to the research team. Data storage and handling procedures comply with institutional data protection policies and the Turkish Personal Data Protection Law. Participants were informed that the results of this study would be published as a scientific article, and those who contributed could be acknowledged in the ‘Acknowledgement’ section of the manuscript if they wished. Participants were given the option to voluntarily provide their name and e‐mail address for this purpose. It was clearly stated that providing this information was entirely optional, not required for participation and would be kept confidential.

Analyses

Survey responses were analysed using descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) in SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 29; IBM, USA). To ensure data completeness, all survey questions were set as mandatory in Google Forms and only responses with the final confirmation statement were included in the analysis. As a result, all 66 ICU responses were complete with no missing data. Responses to the open‐ended questions were analysed using content analysis. Two researchers independently reviewed the responses and grouped them according to similar content. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. The frequency of each response category was then calculated.

Results

Representatives from a total of 66 Turkish adult (n = 52, 78.8%) and paediatric (n = 14, 21.2%) ICUs completed the survey. Participating units were located across seven geographical regions of Turkiye, with the majority from Marmara (n = 34, 51.5%), followed by Central Anatolia (n = 11, 16.7%) and Mediterranean (n = 10, 15.2%) regions. Most survey participants were charge nurses (n = 36, 54.6%), followed by senior ICU nurses (n = 22, 33.3%) and assistant charge nurses (n = 8, 12.1%). Nearly half of the participating sites were from university or research hospitals (n = 32, 48.6%), with equal representation from public hospitals (n = 16, 24.2%) and city hospitals (n = 16, 24.2%). The majority of units were mixed/general ICUs (n = 40, 60.7%). Characteristics of participating units are displayed in Table 1.

Delirium‐Related Structures and Processes

Units could report multiple delirium assessment methods. The majority of ICUs reported using clinical judgement for delirium assessment (n = 58, 87.9%), followed by psychiatric consultation (n = 32, 48.5%). Use of validated delirium assessment tools was limited: CAM‐ICU was reported by 8 adult ICUs (15.4%), ICDSC by 3 adult ICUs (5.8%), while pCAM‐ICU was used by 3 PICUs (21.4%) and CAPD by 2 PICUs (14.3%). Four units (6.1%) reported no delirium assessment method.

Over half of the units (n = 37, 56.1%) reported assessing patients for delirium only in the case of sudden changes in consciousness, while routine assessment twice daily was reported by only 11 units (16.7%). Delirium was primarily assessed by nurses (n = 41, 62.1%), followed by physicians (n = 14, 21.2%).

The most commonly reported written protocols included pain management (n = 53, 80.3%), physical restraint (n = 51, 77.3%), nutrition management (n = 44, 66.7%) and sedation management (n = 38, 57.6%). Written protocols for delirium management were present in only 20 units (30.3%). Only 9 units (13.6%; 6 adult ICUs, 3 PICUs) demonstrated ‘delirium literacy,’ defined as the concurrent use of validated assessment tools and presence of written delirium protocols. Protocols for spontaneous awakening trials (SAT) and spontaneous breathing trials (SBT) were reported by 6 (9.1%) and 12 (18.2%) units, respectively. Only 17 units (25.8%) reported written protocols for family engagement and empowerment.

Delirium communication during staff handovers was the most common delirium awareness intervention (n = 50, 75.8%), followed by at least one educational training in the last year (n = 29, 43.9%). Complete data on delirium‐related structures and processes are displayed in Table 2.

Delirium Prevalence

A total of 798 patients (ICU: n = 649; PICU: n = 149) were present in the 66 surveyed units at 8:00 a.m. on 11 March 2026. Of these, 58.1% (n = 464/798; ICU: n = 376; PICU: n = 88) were assessed for delirium at 8:00 a.m. Among the patients assessed for delirium, 30.8% (n = 143/464) tested positive for delirium (ICU: n = 121/376, 32.2%; PICU: n = 22/88, 25.0%).

At 8:00 p.m., there were 757 patients (ICU: n = 611; PICU: n = 146) reported in the participating units. Of these, 60.4% (n = 457/757; ICU: n = 370; PICU: n = 87) were assessed for delirium. Among the patients assessed, 30.2% (n = 138/457) tested positive for delirium (ICU: n = 118/370, 31.9%; PICU: n = 20/87, 23.0%). Details on delirium assessment are presented in Figure 1.

Non‐Pharmacological Prevention and Treatment

The most common non‐pharmacological delirium management strategies reported in participating units included use of bed rails (n = 58, 87.9%), pain management (n = 49, 74.2%), use of physical restraints (n = 44, 66.7%), ensuring adequate fluid intake (n = 43, 65.2%), non‐disturbed sleep with reduction of noise and light (n = 41, 62.1%) and family information (n = 41, 62.1%).

Mobilisation was reported by less than half of the units (n = 29, 43.9%). Supporting day/night rhythm was more commonly reported in PICUs (n = 11, 78.6%) compared to adult ICUs (n = 28, 53.8%). Family engagement was reported by all PICUs (n = 14, 100%) but only 25.0% of adult ICUs (n = 13). Multi‐professional team rounds were conducted in approximately half of the units (n = 34, 51.5%), with higher rates in PICUs (n = 10, 71.4%) compared to adult ICUs (n = 24, 46.2%).

Sleep aids such as earplugs and eye masks were rarely used (n = 3, 4.5%), and animal‐assisted therapy was reported by only one PICU. Complete data on non‐pharmacological strategies are presented in Table 3.

Pharmacological Treatment and Management Strategies

The most commonly reported pharmacological treatments for delirium were dexmedetomidine (n = 36, 54.5%), midazolam (n = 29, 43.9%), haloperidol (n = 24, 36.4%) and quetiapine (n = 21, 31.8%). Reduction of deliriogenic drugs was reported by 26 units (39.4%), and evaluation of drugs by a specialist was reported by 32 units (48.5%). Notable differences were observed between adult ICUs and PICUs. Phenobarbital was used exclusively in PICUs (n = 7, 50.0%), while quetiapine was more commonly used in adult ICUs (n = 19, 36.5%) compared to PICUs (n = 2, 14.3%). Melatonin use was higher in PICUs (n = 4, 28.6%) compared to adult ICUs (n = 8, 15.4%). Complete data on pharmacological treatment of delirium is reported in Table 4.

The pharmacological management of patients with delirium most often depended on specific symptoms of each patient's delirium (n = 40, 60.6%), followed by reporting in handovers (n = 35, 53.0%), and recommendations for withdrawal of delirium‐related drugs (n = 28, 42.4%). A more general approach including a few pharmacological agents was reported by 27 units (40.9%), while an individualised approach depending on patients and side effects was reported by 18 units (27.3%). Only 9 units (13.6%) reported that pharmacological management was based on a standard operating procedure or protocol. The most commonly reported barrier to delirium care was missing knowledge about delirium (n = 46, 69.7%), followed by inappropriate attitude that delirium is not considered important (n = 38, 57.6%), and lack of awareness (n = 33, 50.0%). Discussion

This cross‐sectional survey describes the prevalence of delirium across seven geographical regions of Turkiye, encompassing nearly 1600 adult and paediatric ICU patient assessments. Delirium remains prevalent in both settings, with 32.2% of adult ICU patients and 25.0% of PICU patients testing positive for delirium at 8:00 a.m., and 31.9% of adult ICU patients and 23.0% of PICU patients testing positive at 8:00 p.m. The majority of units relied on clinical judgement (87.9%) rather than validated delirium assessment tools for delirium detection. All ICUs and PICUs reported using at least one non‐pharmacological delirium management strategy, with the use of bed rails, pain management and ensuring adequate fluid intake being most common. Similarly, pharmacological treatments were widely used, with dexmedetomidine, midazolam and haloperidol being most frequently reported. The reported variability in delirium assessment and management strategies across units highlights the ongoing challenge to managing delirium in critical care settings in Turkiye.

The proportion of patients assessed for delirium who tested positive (approximately 31% in adult ICUs and 24% in PICUs) is notably higher than findings from other WDAD studies. In the WDAD study from German‐speaking countries, Exl et al. (2025) reported mean delirium prevalence rates of 18.2% at 8:00 a.m. and 20.3% at 8:00 p.m. in adult ICUs, and 19.5% at 8:00 a.m. and 20.6% at 8:00 p.m. in paediatric and mixed ICUs [24]. Similarly, the Canadian substudy found that among adult ICU patients assessed for delirium, 18% had delirium at both 8:00 a.m. and 8:00 p.m., while paediatric ICUs showed higher rates, with 50% at 8:00 a.m. and 44% at 8:00 p.m. [19]. The United States substudy reported delirium prevalence of ICUs 23.0% at 8:00 a.m. and 23.4% at 8:00 p.m. [7]. The higher prevalence observed in Turkish ICUs compared to the global average may be attributed to several factors, including differences in patient populations and case‐mix severity. Additionally, the educational training session conducted before WDAD may have increased awareness and prompted more systematic assessment than routine practice. More research is needed to explore the impact of brief educational interventions on delirium screening practices and whether such effects are sustained over time.

A striking finding of this study was the low utilisation of validated delirium assessment tools. Only 12.1% of units reported using CAM‐ICU and 4.5% reported using ICDSC in adult ICUs, while pCAM‐ICU was used by only 21.4% of PICUs. The majority of units (87.9%) relied on clinical judgement for delirium assessment. This finding is consistent with a recent national survey of 342 Turkish ICU nurses, where only 54.7% reported routinely monitoring patients for delirium, and among those who monitored, CAM‐ICU (47.6%) and ICDSC (24.0%) were the most commonly used tools, while 36.4% reported using no validated tool [16]. However, this is in contrast to ICU‐specific WDAD findings from other countries. In the US WDAD substudy, CAM‐ICU was the most frequently used delirium screening tool in ICU settings (52.8%) [7]. In the Canadian WDAD substudy, 64% of adult ICUs used ICDSC and 23% used CAM‐ICU, while 40% of PICUs used CAPD and 40% relied on clinical judgement; delirium assessment was primarily performed by nurses in all adult ICUs [19]. Similarly, in the WDAD study from German‐speaking countries, the most frequently used delirium assessment instruments across adult and paediatric/mixed ICUs were ICDSC (43.9%) and CAM‐ICU (17.9%), with most units reporting assessment three times daily [24]. In Turkiye, this reactive approach was even more prevalent, with 56.1% of units reporting assessment only upon sudden changes in consciousness. This reactive rather than proactive approach to delirium screening may result in underdetection of hypoactive delirium, which is often subtle and easily missed without systematic assessment. This is particularly concerning because hypoactive delirium is associated with a worse prognosis, with increased 6‐month mortality compared to other subtypes [25, 26]. Furthermore, longer duration of hypoactive delirium is independently associated with worse long‐term cognition and executive function at 3 and 12 months after ICU discharge [27]. Therefore, the implementation of routine validated delirium screening in Turkish ICUs is essential not only for improving detection rates but also for enabling early intervention to mitigate these serious long‐term consequences. Notably, participants in our study also recognised this gap, identifying routine use of validated assessment tools and early detection strategies as key priorities for improving delirium care. Ornago et al. (2024) introduced the concept of ‘delirium literacy,’ defined as the concurrent use of validated delirium assessment tools and the presence of written delirium management protocols [20]. Applying this framework to our data, only 9 Turkish ICUs (13.6%) demonstrated delirium literacy, compared to 43.1% of Italian hospital wards in the Ornago et al. (2024) study. This low rate underscores the urgent need for structured delirium education and protocol implementation in Turkish critical care settings. Furthermore, delirium literacy could serve as a practical benchmark for monitoring quality improvement efforts, with annual assessments tracking progress toward evidence‐based delirium care.

The most commonly reported barrier to delirium care in Turkish ICUs was a lack of knowledge about delirium (69.7%), followed by an inappropriate attitude that delirium is not considered important (57.6%) and a lack of awareness (50.0%). These findings align with both the global WDAD study, which identified lack of resources and knowledge deficits as top barriers [14], and the Turkish ABCDEF bundle survey, where 60.3% of ICU nurses were unaware of the ABCDEF bundle and only 37.1% reported implementing PADIS guidelines in their units [16]. The prominence of attitudinal barriers in Turkiye (57.6% reporting delirium is not considered important) suggests a particular need for culture change alongside education. In contrast, the Canadian study reported a shortage of personnel/staff (73%) and a lack of time to educate staff (55%) as the most common barriers (19). While staff shortages were also reported in Turkish units (37.9%), with higher rates in PICUs (57.1%), knowledge and attitude barriers were more prominent, suggesting that educational interventions may be particularly impactful in the Turkish context. Therefore, targeted educational interventions may be particularly impactful in improving delirium care practices. Evidence from a scoping review demonstrates that educational interventions enhance nurses' perception of delirium, boost their confidence and knowledge in managing delirium, and lead to increased compliance with delirium assessment protocols, ultimately resulting in improved documentation accuracy and patient outcomes [28]. Quality improvement studies have shown that formal didactic training programmes for ICU nurses result in significant improvements in knowledge scores and increased awareness of delirium, adequately preparing them for prevention and management [29, 30].

Written protocols for delirium management were present in only 30.3% of Turkish units, which aligns with Erbay Dalli et al.'s (2024) finding that 79.2% of nurses reported no protocol for delirium management in their ICUs [16]. Both figures are considerably lower than the Canadian study where 91% of adult ICUs reported having delirium management protocols [19]. Similarly, protocols for SAT and SBT, key components of the ABCDEF bundle, were reported by only 9.1% and 18.2% of Turkish units in our study. Although Erbay Dalli et al. (2024) reported higher rates of SAT (88.8%) and SBT (92.4%) implementation, they noted that these were mostly carried out in collaboration with physicians rather than through standardised protocols, and that respiratory therapists, who play a key role in SAT‐SBT implementation internationally, are not available as a distinct profession in Turkiye [16]. Our lower rates likely reflect protocol availability rather than ad hoc practice, suggesting that while SAT/SBT may be performed, protocol‐based standardisation remains limited in Turkish ICUs. The lack of protocol‐based standardisation is clinically significant, as the ICU Liberation Collaborative demonstrated that implementation of the ABCDEF bundle, including both daily SATs and daily SBTs, was associated with a lower probability of hospital death, next day mechanical ventilation, coma, delirium, use of physical restraints, ICU readmission and discharge to a facility [31]. Studies have shown that using SATs and SBTs synergistically helps decrease mechanical ventilation days and delirium [32, 33]. The gap between ad hoc practice and protocol‐based standardisation in Turkish ICUs therefore represents a missed opportunity for improving patient outcomes.

Non‐pharmacological delirium management strategies were widely reported but with notable gaps compared to international benchmarks. Mobilisation was reported by only 43.9% of Turkish units, compared to 94.3% in the German WDAD study [24] and 85% in the Canadian WDAD study [19]. Multi‐professional team rounds were conducted in 51.5% of our units, compared to 69.9% in German‐speaking units [24] and 100% in Canadian units [19]. Pain management was reported in 74.2% of Turkish units, lower than the 95.9% reported in the German‐speaking countries and 96.0% in Canadian units [19, 24]. These gaps are clinically significant, as early mobility is the only intervention demonstrated to reduce delirium duration, while also decreasing mechanical ventilation days and ICU length of stay [34, 35]. In addition, family engagement showed a striking disparity between unit types in our study. While all PICUs (100%) reported engaging families in delirium care, only 25% of adult ICUs reported this practice, and written protocols for family engagement were present in only 25.8% of units overall. Similar gaps have been identified across other WDAD substudies [7, 18]. This gap is clinically significant, as a meta‐analysis of family interventions in ICU demonstrated that the risk of delirium was 24% lower in the family intervention group compared to controls, and family intervention was associated with fewer delirium days [36]. Furthermore, the physical presence of family and telephone calls were associated with decreased duration of delirium in all ICU patients [37]. Given this evidence supporting the protective role of family engagement in delirium prevention and management, strengthening family involvement practices (particularly in adult ICUs) represents a priority area for quality improvement in Turkish and international intensive care settings. Physical restraints were used in 66.7% of Turkish units, substantially higher than the 28.5% reported in the WDAD study of German‐speaking countries [24]. The global WDAD study demonstrated that wards with delirium protocols were significantly less likely to use physical restraints [14]. Given that physical restraint use is itself a risk factor for delirium and is associated with adverse outcomes [38], reducing restraint use through the implementation of standardised delirium protocols and alternative non‐pharmacological interventions should be a priority for Turkish ICUs.

Dexmedetomidine was the most commonly used agent in the present study (54.5%), followed by midazolam (43.9%) and haloperidol (36.4%). The high use of midazolam is concerning given the strong evidence that benzodiazepines are associated with increased delirium risk [39]. ICU‐specific WDAD findings from the United States showed that dexmedetomidine and reduction of deliriogenic drugs were each reported by 48.3% of ICUs, followed by quetiapine (39.7%), haloperidol (37.9%), lorazepam (29.3%), midazolam (12.1%) and diazepam (10.3%) [7]. Similarly, the Canadian WDAD substudy reported dexmedetomidine as the most commonly used pharmacological agent in ICUs/PICUs (74%), followed by haloperidol (63%) and quetiapine (59%) [19]. Compared with these ICU‐specific WDAD findings, midazolam use was notably higher in Turkish ICUs/PICUs. These findings indicate that Turkish ICUs need evidence‐based sedation protocols that minimise benzodiazepine use and prioritise routine sedation assessment.

The top barriers identified in our study were missing knowledge (69.7%), inappropriate attitude (57.6%) and lack of awareness (50.0%). These findings contrast with ICU‐specific WDAD data from Canada and German‐speaking countries. In the Canadian WDAD substudy, staff shortage (73%) and lack of time (55%) were the primary barriers, whereas Exl et al. (2025) reported shortage of personnel/staff (53.7%) and patients being difficult to assess (44.7%) as the most frequently identified barriers in adult and paediatric/mixed ICUs [19, 24]. The predominance of knowledge and attitude barriers in Turkish ICUs, rather than resource constraints, suggests that targeted educational interventions may be particularly impactful in this context. Evidence from scoping reviews indicates that educational interventions enhance knowledge, confidence and compliance with delirium protocols [28]. Given that the Irish WDAD study demonstrated a strong association between staff training and validated tool use [18], structured educational programmes represent a high‐priority intervention for Turkish ICUs.

Study participants identified education and training, family engagement, routine use of validated assessment tools and non‐pharmacological interventions as priorities for both delirium care and research. These priorities align with the identified gaps in current practice and mirror the conclusions from other WDAD substudies. The global WDAD study emphasised that the presence of delirium protocols was associated with improved non‐pharmacological interventions, better pharmacological management and reduced barriers [14]. The Irish WDAD study similarly concluded that staff training and education in delirium care are a crucial need [18]. Implementation of structured educational programmes, standardised ABCDEF bundle protocols and quality improvement initiatives focused on these areas may help address the current gaps in delirium care in Turkish critical care settings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. While our study included ICUs and PICUs from seven geographical regions across Turkiye, the sample did not encompass all regions, and the representation was uneven, with the majority of units from the Marmara region (51.5%). This may limit the generalisability of findings to the broader Turkish ICU and PICU populations. The recruitment through the Turkish Intensive Care Nurses Association may have introduced selection bias, as participating nurses may be more engaged and aware of delirium compared to the general nursing population. Recording delirium prevalence on World Delirium Awareness Day, following an educational training session, may have prompted units to assess delirium more systematically than routine practice, potentially affecting both the prevalence estimates and the reported practices. The self‐reported nature of delirium care practices may reflect what respondents believe should be done rather than what actually occurs at the bedside. Although participants reported the presence of written protocols in their units, these findings reflect participants' subjective reports. This study did not assess how consistently, how frequently or to what extent these protocols were implemented in accordance with evidence‐based standards in routine clinical practice. Given the descriptive nature of our study, inferences about the relationship between delirium‐related structures and delirium prevalence cannot be made. Finally, the cross‐sectional design limits our ability to assess temporal trends or causal relationships.

Implications for Practice and Future Research

Based on our findings, several practical implications can be identified. At the national level, development of Turkish‐specific delirium care guidelines adapted from PADIS recommendations would provide a standardised framework for ICU delirium management, including implementation of the ABCDEF bundle in which delirium assessment and management are a core component. Integration of validated delirium screening tools into electronic health record systems with mandatory documentation fields may facilitate routine assessment and improve compliance. Establishment of multidisciplinary delirium teams within ICUs could serve as a sustainable model for ongoing education, protocol adherence monitoring and quality improvement.

Future research should focus on evaluating the effectiveness of culturally adapted delirium education programmes in Turkish ICUs, examining the impact of structured family engagement interventions on delirium outcomes in the Turkish healthcare context and identifying specific barriers and facilitators to delirium care implementation in resource‐variable settings. Longitudinal studies tracking delirium outcomes following quality improvement initiatives would provide valuable evidence for optimising delirium care in Turkish critical care settings. Additionally, multicentre intervention studies comparing different implementation strategies for delirium protocols would help identify the most effective approaches for the Turkish healthcare system.

Conclusion

This nationwide study provides the first comprehensive snapshot of delirium care practices across Turkish ICUs on World Delirium Awareness Day. Our findings reveal a significant burden of delirium affecting nearly one‐third of adult ICU patients and one‐quarter of PICU patients. The predominant use of clinical judgement over validated assessment tools, limited availability of written delirium protocols and low implementation rates of evidence‐based non‐pharmacological interventions represent key areas requiring attention. The identification of knowledge and awareness deficits as primary barriers suggests that targeted educational initiatives, development of national delirium care guidelines and standardised protocol implementation are needed to improve delirium outcomes and patients' safety in Turkish critical care settings.

Ethics Statement

This study was approved by the Bursa Uludag University Health Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2026/04‐12, Date: 25 February 2026). The study was conducted on a voluntary basis among nurses who were members of the Turkish Intensive Care Nurses Association, with data reported by participating nurses. No individual patient data were collected; only aggregated unit‐level data regarding the total number of patients, those with delirium and those who were unassessable were obtained. Therefore, patient informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. For participating nurses, an informed consent statement was provided at the beginning of the online survey, indicating that completion of the survey implied consent to participate in the study. To protect participant anonymity and ensure data confidentiality, all survey responses were de‐identified before analysis and securely stored in compliance with institutional data protection policies. Participants were informed that the results of this study would be published as a scientific article, and those who contributed could be acknowledged in the ‘Acknowledgements’ section of the manuscript if they wished. Participants were given the option to voluntarily provide their name and e‐mail address for this purpose. It was clearly stated that providing this information was entirely optional, not required for participation and would be kept confidential.
