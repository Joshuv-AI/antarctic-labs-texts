Exploring the potential of compound–protein complex structure-free models in virtual screening using BlendNet
Authors: Sangmin Seo, Hwanhee Kim, Jieun Lee, Seungyeon Choi, Sanghyun Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdentifying new compounds that interact with a target is a crucial time-limiting step in the initial phases of drug discovery. Compound–protein complex structure-based affinity prediction models can expedite this process; however, their dependence on high-quality three-dimensional (3D) complex structures limits their practical application. Prediction models that do not require 3D complex structures for binding-affinity estimation offer a theoretically attractive alternative; however, accurately predicting affinity without interaction information presents significant challenges. We introduce BlendNet, a framework that employs a knowledge transfer strategy to improve affinity prediction accuracy by learning the interdependent relationships between compounds and proteins without relying on 3D complex structures. Compared with state-of-the-art models for affinity prediction, BlendNet demonstrated superior performance across various cold-start cases. The ability of BlendNet to interpret compound–protein interactions without utilizing complex structure data highlights its potential to accelerate and streamline drug development.Keywords: binding affinity, interaction interpretability, virtual screening, multiobjective learning, attention mechanism, knowledge distillation

Introduction

Historically, small molecules have dominated the pharmaceutical landscape, constituting the majority of Federal Drug Agency–approved drugs [1–3]. As the rising global incidence of chronic diseases such as cancer, diabetes, and respiratory conditions continues, the demand for innovative pharmaceutical solutions, including small molecule drugs, is anticipated to persist and intensify [4–6]. Early drug discovery aims to explore the chemical space to identify drug candidates with high binding affinities [7]. Typically, drugs’ binding affinity to a specific target is assessed via high-throughput screening [8]. Despite the availability of this technique, the expansive chemical space renders characterizing all possible compound–protein interactions unfeasible [9]. Consequently, advanced computational methodologies are required to accelerate drug discovery.

Protein structure–based virtual screening approaches are crucial for prioritizing drug candidates from extensive compound collections, effectively complementing high-throughput screening and providing a valuable starting point for drug discovery [10, 11]. Molecular docking, a prevalent tool in virtual screening, significantly enhances the drug discovery process and contributes substantially to numerous successful drug discovery projects [12–15]. Nevertheless, this approach has limitations (Supplementary Information 1), including the considerable computational resources required to achieve accurate results. Virtual screening–based drug discovery requires evaluating at least millions of compounds. Consequently, molecular docking tools are designed to balance speed and accuracy. This limited accuracy contributes to an elevated false-positive rate, as accurately differentiating between binders and non-binders of the target protein remains unfeasible.

A promising approach to address the limitations of molecular docking involves adopting models that do not rely on compound–protein complex structures. Various complex-free methods have been proposed and have demonstrated improvements in predicting binding affinity (Supplementary Information 2). Nonetheless, challenges persist. The foremost challenge is estimating drug activity measured in vitro with sufficient accuracy, which is the primary goal. This includes the challenge of accurately measuring the binding affinities of previously unobserved compound–protein pairs, known as generalization performance. Shen et al. [16] and Tran-Nguyen et al. [17] have reported that high accuracy in predicting known binding affinities does not necessarily ensure a good performance in virtual screening. This discrepancy largely stems from evaluation protocols that often depend on limited and potentially biased benchmarks, which obscure an accurate assessment of a model’s predictive capability. To ensure that high binding-affinity prediction accuracy translates into superior virtual screening performance, models must undergo more systematic and rigorous validation, encompassing diverse sample sets and evaluations of generalization performance (Supplementary Information 3).

The second challenge concerns the interpretability of complex-free models. Understanding the local sites mediated by noncovalent interactions between molecules is essential, as this knowledge can provide valuable insights into the design and development of new pharmacologically active molecules. While attention mechanisms can reportedly enhance interpretability [18–22], a systematic benchmark test has indicated that automatically identifying these local regions remains challenging without additional supervision [23]. The third challenge involves the limited availability of high-quality 3D compound–protein complex structures. Recent approaches aim to improve prediction accuracy by leveraging information extracted from 3D complex structures [23, 24]. However, the scarcity of experimentally determined complex structures substantially limits the number of samples available for training datasets. Thus, developing methodologies to effectively overcome the limitation and enhance prediction accuracy is an urgent necessity.

To address these challenges, we propose a BlendNet framework, which helps predict binding affinity using data devoid of complex structural information (only protein pocket sequences and compound molecular graphs) as input. BlendNet framework consists of two networks: BlendNet (T), trained with protein pocket sequences, compound molecular graphs, and noncovalent interaction labels extracted from RCSB PDB [25]; and BlendNet (S), trained with complex-free data collected from BindingDB [26]. Although the training of BlendNet (T) requires noncovalent interaction labels derived from the complex structural data, these labels are not required during the prediction phase. The primary function of BlendNet (T) is to transfer knowledge of noncovalent interaction and binding affinity to the student network, BlendNet (S). To ensure its reliability as an information provider, BlendNet (T) has undergone rigorous validation across various 3D complex structure benchmark datasets and has demonstrated prediction accuracy equal to or surpassing that of most state-of-the-art complex-based and complex-free models. Additionally, BlendNet (T) exhibited superior predictive performance in identifying interaction sites across large-scale complex structure benchmark datasets, indicating that the BlendNet architecture is well suited for modeling intricate interactions between compounds and proteins. This high level of interpretability is valuable for drug designers, aiding the drug development process.

During training, BlendNet (S) acquires interdependent relationship knowledge from BlendNet (T), enabling superior performance over state-of-the-art complex-free models in various cold-start cases. The most significant contribution of BlendNet is the first framework to incorporate interdependent information between compounds and proteins into a predictive model, thereby enhancing prediction accuracy (generalization performance) in scenarios with scarce complex structures. The low computational cost and outstanding accuracy of BlendNet (S) in identifying active compounds in virtual screening applications highlight its potential to accelerate the drug development process significantly.

Materials and methods

Overview

BlendNet is a deep learning framework designed to predict binding affinities by incorporating compound molecular graphs and protein pocket sequences. An overview of the BlendNet framework is depicted in Fig. 1. BlendNet consists of two networks, BlendNet (T) and BlendNet (S), each sharing the same architecture but differing in training processes. The architecture comprises five modules: (1) the compound encoder module, a pre-trained graph neural network that extracts features from individual atoms within compound molecular graphs; (2) the protein encoder module, a self-attention-based network that encodes amino acid residues within protein pocket sequences; (3) the cross-attention module, a network for modeling interactions between the compound atoms and residues in the binding pocket; (4) the pairwise interaction prediction module, a network that helps predict the probability of noncovalent interactions between the compound atoms and binding pocket residues; and (5) the affinity prediction module, which estimates the binding affinity of a compound–protein pair using a vector of interaction features embedded with interdependent relationship information. Specifically, the cross-attention module effectively models interactions between relevant (predicted) binding pocket residues and compound atoms, focusing on capturing the mechanism of compound–protein interactions accurately. The pairwise prediction module guides the cross-attention module in modeling intermolecular interactions, and the affinity prediction module leverages information on predicted noncovalent interactions to enhance the prediction accuracy.

The BlendNet framework comprises two networks, BlendNet (T) and BlendNet (S), which leverage compound molecular graphs and protein pocket sequences to predict binding affinities. Both networks share same architectures, comprising five modules. The protein pocket sequences are extracted from state-of-the-art binding pocket prediction model. BlendNet (T) employs noncovalent interaction information derived from 3D compound–protein complex structures to learn the intricate interdependencies between compounds and proteins. Serving as the teacher network, the trained BlendNet (T) transfers knowledge of binding affinities and noncovalent interactions to BlendNet (S), the student network, thus improving the accuracy of binding-affinity predictions.

Materials and methods

Data preparation

The primary training dataset was constructed using the PDBbind database (version 2020) [27], which contains three-dimensional structural data of protein–ligand complexes and their experimentally determined binding affinities (e.g. , , and ). From the 19 443 complexes available in PDBbind, we applied rigorous preprocessing rules (Supplementary Information 4). To find the binding sites of proteins, we used the algorithm proposed by Seo et al. [28], which is one of the most recent methods to extract the binding sites from protein sequences. Protein-Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP) [29] helps detect seven types of interactions—hydrogen bond, hydrophobic interaction, -stacking, -cation, salt bridge, water bridge, and halogen bond—offering a comprehensive interaction profile for any given protein–ligand complex. The interaction site labels were constructed based on these seven types of interactions (Supplementary Information 5). The statistics of the benchmark datasets used for comparing binding-affinity prediction and interaction site detection performance are provided in Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Table S2. We downloaded compound–protein interaction data from BindingDB database (version 2023) to construct datasets to further evaluate the performance of the complex-free models. To select high-quality interaction data, we extracted 837 155 - and 328 122 -labeled samples from the 2 707 575 interactions based on their unique (UniProt ID–PubChem CID) pairs. To rigorously assess the performance of the baselines across various virtual screening scenarios, we strategically partitioned the - and -labeled datasets into training and test sets (Supplementary Information 6).

Input representations

Compound

Molecular graphs, which represent atoms, bond types, and connections of a compound, are useful for analyzing the structural similarities between compounds. To leverage these advantages, we represented a given compound by a graph . We defined as a graph with node attributes for node and edge attributes for . The hydrogen atoms are not explicitly represented as nodes in the graph. More specifically, atom attributes included type, chirality, degree, formal charge, number of associated hydrogens, number of radical electrons, hybridization, aromaticity, and cyclicity. Edge attributes included bond type, direction, stereochemistry, and conjugated. RDKit libraries [30] were used to calculate atom and edge features, and compound graphs were generated using PyTorch Geometric libraries [31].

Protein

Protein sequences are commonly used in many complex-free affinity prediction models because they are simple forms of data compared with that of protein structures. However, binding sites constitute only ~5% of the whole protein sequence; therefore, binding-affinity prediction models may learn patterns unrelated to the interaction. This prompted the use of binding pocket sequence information, as employed in previous studies [32–35]. We utilized a ligand-binding pocket prediction tool [28] to extract subsequences related to the binding pocket of a given protein. Amino acid embeddings for the protein sequences were extracted using a pre-trained model based on a large-scale protein sequence database [36]. Results and discussion

Experimental design

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the efficacy of complex-free models as alternatives to complex-based models in virtual screening. We assessed whether complex-free models provide valuable insights into compound–protein interactions and examined their capability to handle unobserved target/compound cases effectively. Additionally, we explored the utility of complex-free models as tools for virtual screening and analyzed the contribution of each component of the BlendNet framework to the enhancement of binding-affinity prediction performance. In subsequent sections, we provide the experimental results that supported these objectives.

Comprehensive evaluation of binding-affinity prediction models across complex structure datasets

We evaluated the performance of 32 state-of-the-art binding-affinity prediction models, including BlendNet (T), on eight test datasets (Supplementary Table S1). We categorized complex-free models into four groups and compared their experimental results based on these classifications (Supplementary Information 2): (1) Separate Vector Integration-Based Prediction (SVIBP), (2) Data-Driven Interaction Modeling-Based Prediction (DDIMP), (3) Binding Pocket Information-Based Prediction (BPIBP), and (4) Explicit Interaction Information-Based Prediction (EIIBP). A detailed explanation of the baselines is provided in Supplementary Information 9.

We observed that PSH-ML [40], a complex-based model, consistently outperformed other models. However, complex-based models such as PLEC-NN [41], ECIF [42], and GGL-Score [43] showed high prediction accuracy on Comparative Assessment of Scoring Functions (CASF) benchmark datasets, which were derived from the same source as their training data but showed significantly lower accuracy on other benchmark datasets. These findings underscore the importance of evaluating models using datasets from diverse sources to ensure superior performance in virtual screening. Certain complex-free predictive models, particularly those categorized under the DDIMP (AttentionDTA [44] and PSG-BAR [45]) and EIIBP (MONN [23] and BlendNet (T)), performed comparably to the majority of complex-based models (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2). Contrary to our initial expectations, models under the BPIBP category exhibited poor prediction performance. This is attributed to either the lower concordance between the predicted and actual binding pockets [28] or the inadequacy of the model architecture that incorporates pocket information (Supplementary Information 9). For instance, the P2Rank [46] employed by CAPLA [32] could detect binding residues comprising the binding pocket for the Merged Interaction Sites Benchmark Dataset (MISBD) with a recall of ~74.96%. To accurately predict binding affinity, models within the BPIBP category must accurately reflect the interaction mechanism while minimizing missing information. By leveraging noncovalent interaction data to model the interdependent relationships, BlendNet (T) showed a significantly improving binding-affinity prediction (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S3). Notably, BlendNet (T) outperformed MONN, which also utilizes noncovalent interaction labels, indicating that the architectural design of BlendNet (T) is more adept at capturing and modeling these complex interactions.

Complex-based models significantly outperformed complex-free models in evaluating interactions involving proteins with low structural similarity (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. S4). However, models such as PLEC-NN [41], ECIF [42], SMPLIP-Score [47], and GGL-Score [43] achieved lower predictive accuracy than the average of complex-based models for samples with low structural similarity. As these models performed well in previous experiments, particularly on CASF benchmark datasets, these finding suggest that rigorous validation, including assessments of generalization performance, is essential for developing models suited to successful virtual screening. All complex-free models, except BlendNet (T), showed lower prediction accuracy below the average of complex-based models for proteins with low structural similarity. These results indicate that complex-free models lack generalizability to proteins. Nevertheless, some complex-free models achieved performance comparable to that of complex-based models in evaluating samples with low compound structural similarity. This observation is rational, given that quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) models, which predict activity based solely on the structure of compounds, are employed in drug development [48–50].

However, given that all complex-free models except BlendNet (T) exhibited poor prediction performance on samples with low protein structural similarity, this observation suggests that the complex-free models primarily learn patterns from the training data or independent compound-specific patterns, rather than capturing the interdependent, generalized relationships between compounds and proteins. In contrast, BlendNet (T) exhibited robust predictive performance across complexes with low structural similarity, effectively modeling both compounds and proteins. Collectively, these results suggest that the architectural design of BlendNet (T) substantially enhances performance over existing methods and also enables generalization to complexes with low structural similarity.

PSH-ML achieved the highest performance for experimentally determined compound–protein complex structures. However, in virtual screening, where such experimentally determined structures are often unavailable, docking simulations become essential to identify the docked pose of a compound relative to a protein structure. The accuracy of binding-affinity predictions is influenced by cumulative errors in the docking simulation process (Supplementary Information 10). Even with docked poses derived from proteins with known binding pocket locations, complex-based models generally underperform compared to experimentally determined structures. PSH-ML, which previously ranked first, dropped to 14th place with a decline of 13 positions (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. S5).

For further analysis, we compared the performance with that of complex-based models utilizing docked poses derived from docking simulation on samples with low similarity. Contrary to our expectations, all complex-free models, except BlendNet (T), exhibited lower accuracy than complex-based models when evaluating proteins with low structural similarity (Supplementary Fig. S6). Although some complex-free models performed as well as or better than complex-based models for low compound similarity samples, the majority still underperformed relative to the average of complex-based models. The limited generalization of complex-free models compared to complex-based models raises concerns about their reliability in virtual screening. This highlights the needs for caution when adopting complex-free models for virtual screening.

In terms of computational cost, complex-based models exhibit higher time complexity than that of complex-free models (Fig. 2e). In virtual screening scenarios, where millions of compounds may need testing, the lower time complexity of complex-free models presents a potential advantage. However, this computational efficiency does not compensate for their limitations in generalization performance, when reliability and accuracy are crucial.

Comparative analysis for interaction-related interpretability of complex-free models

To be an alternative to complex-based models in virtual screening, complex-free models must provide accurate binding-affinity predictions and insights into drug design. This includes the ability to interpret regions associated with local sites mediated by noncovalent interactions. To quantitatively evaluate this capability, we compared the performance of six complex-free models—FusionDTA [22], PSG-BAR [45], CAPLA [32], MONN [23], KIDA (S) [24], and BlendNet (T)—in identifying noncovalent interactions. A detailed description of the experimental design is provided in Supplementary Information 5. KIDA (T), a complex-based model, was included as a baseline for comparison with complex-free models.

FusionDTA and PSG-BAR, which leverage whole protein sequences, struggled to identify noncovalent interaction regions at the atom and residue levels, even with the latest attention mechanisms (Fig. 3a). This aligns with previous findings that models utilizing whole protein sequences face challenges in identifying these regions without additional supervision, despite the use of attention mechanisms [23]. In contrast, CAPLA, which targets protein pocket regions, exhibited a certain degree of success in identifying noncovalent interaction sites at the residue level, supporting the validity of BPIBP and EIIBP models that rely on protein pocket sequences for interaction modeling. However, at the atom and pairwise levels, most models encountered difficulties in accurate identification, except for MONN and BlendNet (T), which benefited from additional supervision. Even KIDA (T), which uses distance data between protein pocket atoms and compound atoms extracted from the complex structures, encountered difficulties in automatically identifying these regions. This highlights the greater challenge faced by complex-free models, which treat proteins and compounds independently, in accurately identifying interaction regions without additional supervision.

Although additional supervision using noncovalent interaction labels enhances interpretability, they have limitations for low-similarity complexes (Fig. 3b and c). For samples with low similarity, even MONN and BlendNet (T) exhibited reduced detection performance compared to those with higher similarity, with performance degradation exacerbated at the residue level for low protein similarity samples. Notably, MONN showed significantly lower detection performance at the residue level for low protein similarity samples compared to CAPLA despite being trained with interaction labels. These findings indicate that complex-free models, even when aided by noncovalent interaction labels, may not effectively provide valuable insights for interactions involving low-similarity complexes.

However, without additional supervised learning with interaction labels, complex-free models encounter challenges in identifying these regions. KIDA (S), which receives information from KIDA (T) that directly utilizes structural data, outperformed MONN and BlendNet (T) in residue-level detection for low protein similarity samples. However, KIDA (S) struggled with atom-level detection, leading to poor detection performance at the pairwise-level. Although BlendNet (T) exhibited lower residue-level detection performance than that of KIDA (S) for low protein similarity samples, it achieved the highest detection performance at the pairwise level, largely owing to its relatively high detection performance at the atom-level.

By integrating pre-trained embeddings from large datasets, (predicted) binding pockets, and supervised learning of interaction sites, we demonstrated that BlendNet (T) improves the ability to infer interaction patterns compared to MONN and KIDA (S). However, protein sequences alone prove insufficient in addressing the challenges posed by proteins with low structural similarity. To enhance the utility of complex-free models in virtual screening, more effective strategies must be developed to address the challenge of limited interpretability, particularly for samples with low similarity.

Performance comparison of binding-affinity prediction on a large-scale dataset

We previously demonstrated that complex-free models offer a computational cost advantage over complex-based predictive models. Another advantage of complex-free models is their independence from complex structures, enabling the use of large-scale compound–protein interaction datasets for training. BindingDB, with its extensive collection of compound–protein interactions reported in the literature, significantly surpasses the PDBbind dataset in both interaction counts and corresponding affinity labels. We evaluated the performance of complex-free models using the large-scale BindingDB dataset (Supplementary Table S4). Due to the limited complex structures in this dataset, MONN, initially in the EIIBP category, is reclassified into the DDIMP category, as it trained without noncovalent interaction labels. Additionally, KIDA (S) is excluded from this evaluation as training under this condition is not feasible.

For a more systematic evaluation, the -labeled dataset was divided into four cases: (1) random split, (2) new-protein, (3) new-compound, and (4) blind split. The results of all complex-free models for the-labeled dataset are shown in Supplementary Fig. S7. In the random split cases, PSG-BAR (DDIMP) achieved the highest prediction accuracy, measured using the Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC) and root mean squared error (RMSE), followed by FusionDTA (DDIMP) and GraphDTA (SVIBP), which performed high prediction accuracy for compounds or proteins observed during training (Fig. 4). Models in BPIBP category exhibited lower accuracy compared with those in the SVIBP and DDIMP categories across all cases (Fig. 4). These results align with those observed for the PDBbind dataset, emphasizing the importance of accurate binding pocket prediction and the need for additional interaction guidance to enhance the predictive accuracy of the BPIBP category (a more detailed analysis is provided in the ablation test section).

In the previous section, we verified that BlendNet (T) can adequately perform the role of the teacher network, enabling BlendNet (S) to achieve robust prediction performance.

Evaluation of screening performance in real-world applications

We utilize the Database of Useful Decoys: Enhanced (DUD-E) to evaluate performance in real-world applications [51]. Details on the experimental data are in Supplementary Information 11 and Supplementary Table S5. We compared active compound detection performance across five models: RF-Score [52], SMPLIP-Score [47], DeepGLSTM [53], PSG-BAR [45], and BlendNet (Fig. 5). The comparison began with models trained on complex structure dataset, PDBbind: RF-Score, SMPLIP-Score, DeepGLSTM (P), PSG-BAR (P), and BlendNet (T). In detecting selected 14 targets, BlendNet (T) exhibited competitive performance relative to complex-based models. This trend remained consistent when the evaluation was extended to include all available targets ( = 102). Other complex-free models, including DeepGLSTM (P) and PSG-BAR (P), did not surpass complex-based models in detecting active compounds. The virtual screening dataset comprised a diverse set of compounds and proteins, significantly different from the training dataset. Specifically, 14.85% of the targets and 90.93% of the active compounds in the DUD-E dataset were dissimilar to those in the PDBbind dataset. The generalization performance of the complex-free models, except BlendNet (T), was significantly lower than that of complex-based models (Supplementary Fig. S4). This limited generalization capability results in poorer detection performance for active compounds. Although complex-based models rely on slightly unstable complex structures obtained from docking simulation, their higher detection performance remains a critical factor, leading to the hesitancy in adopting complex-free models for virtual screening. These results underscore the need to prioritize improving generalization performance in the development of complex-free models.

The complex-free models—DeepGLSTM (B), PSG-BAR (B), and BlendNet (S)—trained on the BindingDB dataset exhibited significantly higher detection performance compared with those trained on the PDBbind dataset. However, even with the BindingDB dataset, certain samples in the virtual screening data remained unobserved during training. We identified that 3.96% of the targets and 38.42% of active compounds in the DUD-E dataset were dissimilar to those in the BindingDB dataset. Many active compounds in the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor delta, hexokinase type IV, and protein farnesyltransferase/geranylgeranyltransferase type-1 subunit alpha targets were dissimilar to those in the BindingDB dataset (Supplementary Table S5). For these targets, PSG-BAR (B) and DeepGLSTM (B) underperformed in comparison to BlendNet (S). BlendNet (S) achieved the best generalization performance among existing complex-free models across various cold-start cases (Fig. 4). Based on these capabilities, BlendNet (S) exhibited superior detection performance over other models in virtual screening tests. Moreover, BlendNet (S) maintained competitive performance when screened against similar compounds or proteins. These results suggest that BlendNet (S) is well suited for efficiently screening millions of compounds, a critical objective in virtual screening, and surpasses the capabilities of existing methods. Although DeepGLSTM (B) and FusionDTA (B) showed higher detection capabilities than complex-based models, this improvement can be attributed to the expansion of training data rather than enhanced generalization performance.

BlendNet (S) eliminates the tedious and time-consuming docking process and leverages the high parallel computing power of graphics processing units, offering speed advantages for large-scale virtual screening tasks. However, the interpretability of BlendNet (S) requires improvement (a more detailed analysis is provided in the ablation test section). To address this limitation while capitalizing on the superior detection performance of BlendNet (S), we propose the following virtual screening process. Interaction profilers, such as PLIP and ProteinsPlus [54], are effective tools for analyzing compound–protein interactions, but they rely on complex structures, making them computationally expensive when applied to large datasets. To mitigate these costs, we recommend first narrowing down the pool to the top 5%–10% of molecules identified by BlendNet (S). This strategy substantially reduces the number of docking operations required, thus decreasing computational costs while enabling the efficient application of interaction profilers. By lowering computational overhead, this strategy also enables the application of more advanced docking algorithms, which can deliver more precise results. We believe that this approach will substantially accelerate the drug discovery process.

Ablation test for BlendNet (T) and BlendNet (S)

Having established that the BlendNet framework demonstrated outstanding predictive performance across various experimental settings, we systematically analyzed the contributions of the individual components of BlendNet (T) and BlendNet (S) to improving binding-affinity prediction accuracy. The ablation test for BlendNet (T) investigates the impact of three components: “Pocket,” “Cross,” and “Interaction.” For instance, “Pocket” represents the BlendNet (T) architecture without the cross-attention module and the pairwise interaction prediction modules. “Pocket + Cross” includes the cross-attention module but omits the pairwise interaction prediction module. The results of this ablation test are illustrated in Fig. 6a. “Pocket” and “Cross” exhibited lower prediction performance than that of “Default,” with reductions of ~1.53% and 11.67%, respectively. These results highlight the necessity of appropriate model architecture to enhance affinity prediction accuracy. The “Pocket + Cross,” which falls under the BPIBP category, slightly outperforms “Default,” although the difference is not significant. These findings align with previous observations that BPIBP category models generally show inferior performance compared to SVIBP and DDIMP category models. While BPIBP models leverage binding pocket sequence information, they rely on predictive values that introduce uncertainty. For instance, Pseq2Sites [28] can accurately identify the binding pocket regions but may incorrectly classify nonbinding residues as binding residues. Accurate affinity prediction remains challenging without additional information, such as noncovalent interaction labels, to clarify molecular interaction mechanisms. Our experimental results indicate that supervised learning with interaction labels significantly enhances performance: “Interaction” alone contributed ~3.89%; “Pocket + Interaction” yielded ~5.56% improvement; and “Cross + Interaction” resulted in an ~4.44% improvement over “Default.”

Affinity prediction performance was assessed using the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) on the MBABD dataset. (b) Analysis of the impact of three distinct loss functions on the training of BlendNet (S). Performance improvements were quantified using PCC on the Ki-labeled dataset. (c) Comparison of interaction sites detection performance across different configurations, using samples from the MISBD dataset. The evaluation was conducted at the atom, residue, and pairwise levels, with performance measured by the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC).

The significance of incorporating interaction site labels into supervised learning remains evident even in case of low protein similarity. The “Interaction” component demonstrates a marked improvement over “Default,” highlighting the substantial contribution of interaction labels to performance enhancement (Fig. 6a). Specifically, “Interaction” yields a 28.24% increase in accuracy compared with that of “Default,” and “Pocket + Interaction” and “Cross + Interaction” improve by 12.94% and 31.76%, respectively. Thes results emphasize the relative impact of interaction labels compared with that of other components. We observed a trend of decreased interpretability for proteins with low structural similarity (Fig. 3), suggesting that addressing this issue could lead to more accurate affinity predictions for complexes with low protein similarity. Although a similar trend is observed with low compound similarity, the use of interaction labels does not consistently enhance performance. For instance, “Interaction” and “Cross + Interaction” performed worse than “Default,” a trend also observed with MONN in low compound similarity samples. Nevertheless, “Pocket + Interaction” achieved the second-highest prediction accuracy, indicating that, with the proper model architecture, supervised learning incorporating interaction labels can still improve performance, even for samples with low compound similarity (Fig. 6a).

BlendNet (S) demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to existing complex-free models across large-scale datasets and virtual screening. Given that BlendNet (S) has an identical architecture to BlendNet (T), we conducted an ablation study to evaluate the influence of three distinct training losses on its predictive accuracy. These losses were (1) Hint (IS): hint loss based on pairwise interaction sites predicted by BlendNet (T), (2) Hint (RV): hint loss based on compound and protein representations of BlendNet (T), and (3) Imitation (TP): imitation loss based on affinity prediction of BlendNet (T). In the experimental design, we evaluated models trained with two of these losses at a time, excluding one at each step.

The “Default,” which was trained without constraints from the teacher network, exhibited superior performance in the random split case but achieved poor prediction accuracy in unobserved samples case (Fig. 6b), similar to other complex-free models (Fig. 4). This observation suggests that independent compounds and proteins are unable to identify generalized interaction patterns without guidance based on interdependent relationships between compounds and proteins. The “Hint (IS),” “Hint (RV),” and “Imitation (TP)” components all contributed to improved generalization performance. However, excluding any of these three components resulted in diminished performance in cold-start cases relative to other complex-free models, indicating that no single component excels in all respects. This occurs because the predictions of teacher network did not sufficiently inform the training process. To address this limitation, BlendNet (S) leverages the synergistic effects of blending all three components, ensuring they complement each other effectively. Moreover, BlendNet (S) offers interpretability through training based on the pairwise interaction predictions of BlendNet (T), as illustrated in Fig. 6c. No other complex-free model, particularly in the context of limited 3D complex structures, can provide such interpretability (Supplementary Table S6). However, achieving high levels of interpretability across diverse compounds or proteins remains a challenge.

Conclusion

Compound–protein interactions are intricate and interdependent, posing significant challenges for complex-free models to infer relationships from independent data. To address this, we proposed the BlendNet to predict binding affinity by leveraging compound molecular graphs and protein pocket sequences. To effectively model the interdependent relationships between compounds and proteins, BlendNet (T) employs three components: predicted protein pocket sequences, cross-attention-based interaction modeling, and interaction site labels to guide interaction modeling. These components work synergistically to enhance performance when appropriately integrated. Our experimental findings demonstrate that inference of interdependent relationships is unattainable without explicit guidance during training. Notably, the ablation tests revealed that interaction labels have the most substantial impact on improving accuracy. However, this approach is not universally applicable. For interaction with low similarity, interpretability diminishes, and performance gains are elusive when the model architecture is inadequate.

Given that complex-free models do not rely on high-quality 3D complex structures, they offer a theoretically appealing alternative to complex-based models. However, our systematic analysis highlights significant limitations in the performance of current complex-free models, particularly in handling unseen samples during training. This deficiency limits their ability to accurately identify active compounds for a given target protein in virtual screening. These findings highlight the critical need to focus on enhancing generalization performance in the development of predictive models for successful virtual screening. By comparison, the BlendNet framework excels in characterizing generalized interaction patterns by transferring knowledge from BlendNet (T) to BlendNet (S). BlendNet (s) was rigorously evaluated using the datasets for cold-start cases, demonstrating notable improvements in generalization performance over traditional complex-free models. Moreover, in virtual screening tasks, BlendNet (S) exhibited robust performance, highlighting its potential to streamline drug discovery process.

Additionally, the findings suggest that BlendNet (S) can achieve interpretability through information transfer, even without high-quality 3D complex structures. A critical factor in the superior affinity prediction accuracy and interpretability of BlendNet (S) is the accuracy of predictions provided by the teacher network. While BlendNet (T) has effectively fulfilled this role, areas for improvement remain. Specifically, challenges with interpreting low-similarity interactions underscore the need for more robust models to better capture compound–protein interdependencies, which we intend to address in the future work. In conclusion, the insights and considerations presented in this study support the continued development and application of complex-free models, significantly advancing the field of predictive modeling in molecular interactions.
