From bioinformatics to clinical translation: BIRC5 as a pivotal diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target for NAFLD-driven HCC
Authors: Yushu Huang, He He, Xueqing Xia, Lifang Jiang, Qifeng Peng, Qingfeng Du, Yanting You
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to explore biomarkers linked to the progression from non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and their therapeutic potential. Using bioinformatics, we identified key differentially expressed genes from various databases, focusing on genes related to NAFLD, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, liver cirrhosis, and HCC. Among the upregulated genes, baculoviral IAP repeat containing 5 (BIRC5), cyclin B1 (CCNB1), cyclin-dependent kinase 1 (CDK1), and DNA topoisomerase II alpha (TOP2A) were found to be significant. In vivo and in vitro models of NAFLD and clinical HCC samples validated BIRC5 as a critical regulator in the disease progression. Functional assays revealed that knocking down BIRC5 alleviated fatty acid-induced liver damage and mitochondrial dysfunction in NAFLD models, while also inhibiting HCC cells proliferation and migration, further leading to mitochondrial dysfunction. YM155 (a specific BIRC5 inhibitor) also confirmed the previous experimental results. Then we performed experiments using BIRC5 overexpression plasmid. BIRC5 overexpression exacerbated hepatic steatosis and mitochondrial function in free fatty acid (FFA) -induced AML12 hepatocytes, and enhanced HCC cells proliferation, migration, and invasion. These findings highlight BIRC5 as a pivotal driver in the NAFLD-HCC transition, mediating metabolic dysfunction and malignant transformation. This study proposes BIRC5 as a therapeutic target and diagnostic biomarker, offering perspectives for HCC diagnosis and treatment of HCC. Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), a type of malignant liver tumor, is classified as either primary or secondary. Data taken from Ferlay et al. (2024) showed that HCC ranked third globally in cancer-related fatalities and sixth in cancer incidence (Singh et al. 2025), and it is projected that by 2040, 1.3 million people will die from HCC (Rumgay et al. 2022). HCC can be treated with locoregional therapies, including liver transplantation, surgical resection, radiofrequency ablation, and transarterial chemoembolization, as well as systemic therapies such as immunotherapy, targeted therapy, chemotherapy, and antivirals (Mu et al. 2024). Surgical resection remains the main approach for long-term survival; however, only 24% of patients recover after hepatectomy, and 50–70% develop recurrence or metastasis within 5 years (Xiong et al. 2023). Liver transplantation also provides limited benefit, with a median survival of only roughly 11 months (Bodzin et al. 2017). Because of subtle early symptoms, less than 30% of patients are eligible for curative treatment at diagnosis, and systemic therapy shows modest efficacy. For example, tyrosine kinase inhibitors benefit only 30% of patients, prolong overall survival by 3 months, and resistance usually develops within 6 months (Chidambaranathan-Reghupaty et al. 2021; Wang And Deng 2023). Conventional chemotherapy is also unsatisfactory due to poor efficacy and severe side effects.

HCC arises from multiple etiologies, including hepatitis B virus infection, alcohol, aflatoxin exposure, smoking, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). NAFLD, a progressive disease that may advance to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), fibrosis, cirrhosis, and eventually HCC. NAFLD is anticipated to emerge as the most prevalent possible etiologic causes for HCC as a result of the rising prevalence of type 2 diabetes and obesity (Fang et al. 2023). From NAFLD to HCC, modern scholars believe that it is a multi-hit mode, and the increasing collagen deposition promotes the gradual occurrence of liver cirrhosis and tumor formation. Moreover, NAFLD-related HCC exhibits poor immunotherapy efficacy, which may be attributed to impaired immune surveillance and chronic liver injury caused by abnormal T cell activation (Pfister et al. 2021; Yahoo et al. 2023), Therefore, finding the possible diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers linked to HCC progression is essential in order to develop therapeutic targets and treatment concepts.

In this study, multifaceted analyses and immune infiltration profiling were used to identified key genes implicated in the transition from NAFLD to HCC, and RT-qPCR was used to verifed the key genes. BIRC5, as the most differential key genes, was screened for further analysis. Functional validation was performed in normal liver cells and HCC cells via BIRC5 downregulation (siBIRC5 and YM155), and overexpression BIRC5 in vitro. The role of BIRC5 in lipid accumulation, liver function, and mitochondrial function was negatively associated with the NAFLD-HCC transition. Additionally, results from BIRC5 knockdown and overexpression experiments in HCC cells confirmed that BIRC5 was negatively correlated with HCC cells proliferation and migration. These findings shed light on the pathophysiology of HCC and highlight promising biomarkers and therapeutic targets for clinical translation, ultimately offering avenues for early intervention and improved outcomes in NAFLD-driven HCC.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition, processing and genetic screening

Data from TCGA, DisGeNET, Genecards, CTD, OMIM, and GEO databases (GSE14520, |logfc|≥ 1, p < 0.05, padj < 0.05) were integrated to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across NAFLD-HCC progression. Venn diagrams for gene overlaps, volcano plots and UMAP for expression patterns; TCGA-LIHC RNA-seq, clinical and mutation data were processed. Functional annotation (BP, CC, MF) and KEGG enrichment were visualized top 20 terms. Hub genes were identified through co-expression heatmaps, network diagrams, and Spearman’ s correlation.

Hub genes immune infiltration analysis

Using the TCGA database, correlations between hub genes and immune cell subsets (B cells, macrophages, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, etc.) were visualized. The "ESTIMATE" R package was utilized to calculate TME scores. Additionally, the TIMER database was used to analyze the correlation between the risk score and immune cells, and the infiltration of various immune cells was estimated via the TIMER algorithm in R (4.2.1).

Clinical samples collected

HCC tumor, adjacent tumor tissues and normal tissues were obtained from patients diagnosed with HCC via pathological examination who underwent curative resection at Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University. The data are not presented here as a manuscript containing relevant information is currently under submission. Preoperative therapy for HCC was not given to any of the patients. All tissue specimens were immediately snap-frozen and kept at −80 °C prior to analysis. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University (IRB Approval number: NFEC-2019–064), and all participants provided written informed consent. Researchers explained the study purpose, sample usage, confidentiality protections, and the right to withdraw at any time; patients signed the consent form only after confirming their understanding. Tumor dimensions and Edmonson-Steiner grades were determined via histopathological assessment.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

RNA isolation from human samples and cell lines was carried out using TRIZOL reagent (AG, Hunan, China) and reversed using the Prime Script RT Master Mix following the manufacturer's protocol. Gene-specific primers were produced in Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). RT-qPCR analysis was conducted with SYBR Premix ExTaq (AG, Hunan, China). The endogenous regulator of mRNA normalization was Gapdh. Using the 2–ΔΔCT technique, relative gene expression levels were ascertained. Supplementary Material contains detailed primer sequences (Tab S2).

Cell viability and colony formation assays

Cellular proliferation was assessed via CCK8 assay as following: 3 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates were exposed to serially diluted compounds for 24 h, 10 μL CCK8 reagent was added and measured at 450 nm. For colony formation, 500 transfected cells/well in 6-well plates were cultivated for 14 days, treated with 4% PFA for 30 min, stained with crystal violet for 20 min, rinsed, air-dried, then imaged and counted.

Oil Red O (ORO) staining and biochemical measurements in AML12 cell

AML12 cell were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well and exposed to siRNA at 20 μM with or without FFA at 0.3 mM for 24 h. For ORO staining, cells were fixed in 4% PFA, preincubated with 1,2-propanediol, stained with 0.5% ORO solution. For biochemical measurements, commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) were utilized to measure the levels of total cholesterol (TC), total triglycerides (TG), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) followed themanufacturer.

Mitochondrial activity and biogenesis

Mitochondrial functional profiles were characterized by quantifying ATP production, mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, following established methodologies. The amount of cellular ATP was determined using an ATP bioluminescence assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). In tracellular ROS levels were detected via DCFDA fluorescent labeling, and MMP was evaluated using the mitochondrial-specific probe JC-1 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), with fluorescence intensity analyzed by flow cytometry.

Migration-related assays

Transwell migration tests were assessed using transwell chambers (Corning Incorporated, Corning, NY, USA), which involved adding 2 × 104 HCC cells in serum-free medium to the top chamber with 10% serum-containing DMEM in the lower well. After 24 h, migrated cells were fixed with 95% ethanol, stained with crystal violet and imaged by microscope.

Wound healing assay proceeded by culturing HCC cells at a density of 8 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates to confluence then making a scratch with a 200 μL pipette tip. Cells were incubated in serum-free medium with images captured at 0, 24 and 48 h. Wound closure percentage was quantified via ImageJ.

Immunofluorescence (IF) assay

For IF analysis, cells were initially incubated with anti-BIRC5 (Immunoway YM8044), anti-Ki67 (Immunoway YM8189) and anti-Vimentin (Immunoway YM8324) antibodies, then stained with DAPI. IF images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing protease/phosphatase inhibitors (Servicebio, Hubei, China), and protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). Denatured proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Massachusetts, USA), and blocked with skim milk. Membranes were probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies at a specific dilution ratio (anti-BIRC5, Immunoway YM8044, 1:2000; anti-OPA1, Affinity DF8587, 1:1000; anti-MFF, Affinity DF12006, 1:1000; anti-FIS1, Affinity DF12005, 1:10,000; anti-β-actin, Affinity DF7018, 1:3000), followed by secondary antibodies for 1 h at 4 °C after TBST washes. Signals were detected via ECL (Millipore, Massachusetts, USA) and band densities analyzed using Image-J software.

Statistical analysis

For comparisons among multiple groups that met the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances, a one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was performed. Upon finding a significant overall effect from ANOVA, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey’ s test to control the family-wise error rate. Pearson correlation coefficient was used when both variables followed a bivariate normal distribution; otherwise, the non-parametric Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was applied. Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were compared with the log-rank test. Every statistical analysis was carried out utilizing GraphPad Prism 10.2.1 (Dotmatics, Boston, USA) and SPSS 29.0 (IBM, Armonk, USA). Data were derived from a minimum of three independent and randomized experiments, presented as means ± standard deviation. Multiple comparisons of means were performed using the ANOVA test. P value less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs

From 434 GEO-derived samples, we identified 1092 DEGs. By intersecting these DEGs with known disease-related genes from DisGeNET, GeneCards, CTD, and OMIM (1245 NAFLD, 1065 cirrhosis, 882 HCC), we obtained 84 common genes implicated in the NAFLD-HCC sequence (Fig. 1A).

GO enrichment analysis revealed that BP were enriched in xenobiotic, metal ion, and nutrient responses, and peptide/steroid metabolism (Fig. 1B, F, J, N). CC terms included cytoplasmic vesicle lumen, ER lumen, and plasma membrane exterior (Fig. 1C, G, K, O). MF centered on transcription factor binding, nuclear receptor binding, and monooxygenase activity (Fig. 1D, H, L, P). KEGG pathway analysis indicated associations with chemical carcinogenesis, cellular senescence, hepatitis B, alcoholic liver disease, and endocrine resistance (Fig. 1E, I, M, Q), suggesting involvement in inflammatory, metabolic, and tumorigenic processes.

Screening of hub genes in HCC

We utilized TCGA-LIHC data (371 tumors, 50 paracancer, 110 normal tissues) to explore candidate hub genes. Volcano and expression ranking plots highlighted upregulated DEGs (Fig. 2A-B). A PPI network via STRING (Fig. 2C) combined with MCC, NCC, and Closeness algorithms identified 8 hub genes, namely CDK1, cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4), CCNB1, BIRC5, cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2 A (CDKN2A), poly (adenosine diphosphate-ribose) polymerase-1 (PARP1), TOP2A, and cyclin A2 (CCNA2) (Fig. 2D). GeneMANIA revealed extensive co-expression and protein interactions among these genes (Fig. 2E). All eight were significantly upregulated in tumor vs. normal tissues (paired and unpaired; Fig. 2F-G, p < 0.001). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) from human protein atlas (HPA) confirmed higher protein expression in HCC tissues (Fig. 2I). Correlation analyses showed especially strong interactions among BIRC5, CCNB1, CDK1, CCNA2, and TOP2A (Fig. S1A-K, p < 0.01).Pathway activity profiling via GSCA indicated that all genes (except PARP1 and CDKN2A) activated apoptosis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and cell cycle pathways while inhibiting hormone, RTK, and RAS/MAPK pathways (Fig. S1L), highlighting their oncogenic roles in HCC.

Diagnostic value of hub genes in HCC

We evaluated correlations between hub gene expression and clinical features. Most genes, especially BIRC5, CDK1, TOP2A, CCNA2, and CCNB1, showed significant associations with alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) (Fig. 3A), disease-specific survival (DSS) (Fig. 3B), overall survival (OS) (Fig. 3C), pathologic stage (Fig. 3D), progression-free interval (PFI) (Fig. 3E), prothrombin time (Fig. 3F), T stage (Fig. 3G), tumor status (Fig. 3H), and vascular invasion (Fig. 3I, p < 0.01).

Relationship between hub genes expression and clinicopathological parameters. (A-I) HUB genes expression was correlated with AFP, DSS, OS, Pathologic stage, PFI, Prothrombin time, T stage, Tumor status and Vascular invasion

ROC curve analyses (Fig. S2) confirmed their diagnostic performance, with BIRC5 showing high AUCs for AFP and stage, while CDK1 and CCNA2 maintained predictive value across multiple clinical traits. Logistic regression and baseline data (Tab. S3–18) supported that high expression of TOP2A, BIRC5, CDK1, CCNA2, and CCNB1 was linked to advanced tumor stage, high grade, AFP elevation, and poor prognosis. Survival analyses were performed to refine prognostic relevance. In DSS (Fig. S3A-H), all genes except PARP1 showed significant expression-based differences (p < 0.05), with CDK1, CDK4, BIRC5, TOP2A, and CCNB1 associated with poorer outcomes. CCNA2 presented a contrasting pattern—low expression correlated with worse survival. LASSO regression (Fig. S3I) and nomograms (Fig. S3J-K, Tab. S19−21) confirmed these trends. OS and PFI analyses (Fig. S4-S5) yielded similar results: PARP1 was non-significant, while TOP2A, CDK1, BIRC5, CDK4, and CDKN2A consistently indicated higher mortality and disease progression risks (p < 0.05). CCNA2 again uniquely associated with favorable prognosis at higher expression levels. Ultimately, BIRC5, CDK1, CDK4, and TOP2A emerged as the most consistently significant across survival analyses, displaying both prognostic strength and clinical relevance.

Analysis of immune infiltration of hub genes in HCC

To investigate immunological relevance, we assessed immune infiltration using ssGSEA, CIBERSORT, ESTIMATE, and TIMER. ssGSEA (Fig. S6A-H) revealed strong correlations between hub genes and immune cell subsets, especially Th2 cells, macrophages, CD8+ T cells, and NK CD56 bright cells (p < 0.05). Single-gene enrichment scoring (Fig. 4A-B) confirmed consistent associations with Th2 and helper T cells, excluding CDKN2A. CIBERSORT identified significant differences in CD8T cells, regulatory T cells, and CD4+ memory activated T cells between high/low expression groups (Fig. S6I-P, Fig. 4C-D, p < 0.05). Correlation heatmaps revealed tight links between most hub genes (excluding PARP1/CDKN2A) and follicular helper T cells and myeloid-derived cells (p < 0.05). ESTIMATE analysis (Fig. 4E-F) showed StromalScore differences for BIRC5, CDK1, CDKN2A, and CCNB1 (p < 0.001), with moderate differences for TOP2A (p < 0.01) and CDK4 (p < 0.05). Notably, TOP2A, CDK1, and BIRC5 correlated significantly with ESTIMATEScore, reinforcing their immune microenvironment involvement.TIMER database further validated associations with regulatory T cells, activated CD4+ T cells, myeloid progenitors, and fibroblasts (Fig. S7–S9, p < 0.05). Collectively, these results highlight a close relationship between BIRC5, CDK1, CDK4, TOP2A and immune regulation in HCC.

Mutation analysis, Drug sensitivity analysis,and Transcription factor (TF) prediction of hub genes in HCC

For mutation analysis, we profiled CNVs and mutations by cBioPortal and GSCA. PARP1 and BIRC5 were amplified in 6% and 4% of cases, while CDKN2A exhibited frequent deep deletions (8%) (Fig. S10A). GSCA revealed heterozygous amplifications for PARP1, and homozygous deletions for CDKN2A and CCNA2 (Fig. S10B-D). Mutation-survival analysis showed that gene alterations, especially in TOP2A, CDK1, BIRC5, and CDK4, were linked to shorter DSS, OS, and PFI (Fig. S10E). Expression analysis under various CNV statuses demonstrated that amplification increased gene expression, whereas deletions reduced it (Fig. S10F-M). Mutation types included missense, splicing, and truncation, particularly in CDKN2A and PARP1, suggesting altered protein function.

For drug sensitivity analysis, we assessed drug sensitivity correlations using GDSC data (Fig. S10N-T). TOP2A, BIRC5, and CCNA2 expression positively correlated with sensitivity to multiple anti-HCC agents, suggesting that higher expression may improve drug efficacy. In contrast, CDK1 showed variable correlations, while CDK4 and CCNB1 lacked consistent associations. Overall, TOP2A, BIRC5, and CDK1 demonstrated the strongest drug-associated potential. These genes may serve as predictive biomarkers for therapy response, guiding combination strategies in clinical practice.

For TF prediction, we explored upstream transcriptional regulation by X2K and ChEA3 platforms (Fig. S10U). CDK1 and CDK4, as core kinases, formed dense networks with kinases (e.g., GSK3B, JNK1) and intermediates involved in cell cycle and mitosis. CCNA2 exhibited strong interactions with MYC, E2F4, and other TFs. ChEA3-based ranking of transcription factors (integrating GTEx, ReMap, ENCODE, ARCHS4) highlighted FOXM1, MYBL2, and E2F1 as key regulators. Notably, CDK1 and CCNB1 shared high overlap with these TFs (Fig. S10V, Tab. S22), suggesting coordinated transcriptional control of cell proliferation in HCC.

Verification of hub genes expression

To experimentally validate the dysregulation of the identified hub genes (BIRC5, TOP2A, CDK1, and CCNB1), we performed RT-qPCR analysis on clinical HCC samples, including normal liver tissues, adjacent non-tumor tissues, and HCC tissues. Consistent with our bioinformatic predictions, all four genes exhibited significant upregulation in HCC tissues compared to both normal and adjacent non-tumor controls (Fig. S11A, p < 0.01). Notably, BIRC5 is clearly linked to shortened OS, DSS, and PFI, acting as a key factor promoting tumor invasion and progression, and based on RT-qPCR results of clinical samples, validation set and literature search, it was selected for further research. To further investigate the relevance of BIRC5 in NAFLD, we assessed its expression both in vivo and in vitro NAFLD model. Strikingly, BIRC5 mRNA and protein expression levels were significantly elevated both in vivo and in vitro NAFLD model (Fig. S11B-H, p < 0.01). Additionally, a correlation heatmap analysis of the in vivo NAFLD model revealed significant positive associations between BIRC5 and key genes involved in lipid metabolism, and negative between BIRC5, liver function (TC, TG, ALT, and AST), and lipid β-oxidation genes. (Fig. S11I, p < 0.05), further suggesting a crucial role in promoting progression in NAFLD to HCC.

BIRC5 exhibits a negative correlation with the HCC phenotype

In order to verift whether BIRC5 attenuated the phenotypes of HCC cells, we used the BIRC5 siRNA and BIRC5 inhibitor YM155. The efficacy of the siRNA was validated using SK-Hep1 cells through RT-qPCR experiments. S2 siBIRC5 had the highest knockdown efficiency with an expression level of 50%, and thus was selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. 5A, p < 0.001). To dissect the functional relevance of BIRC5 in HCC, we first conducted downregulation of BIRC5 in HCC cells lines. BIRC5 was robustly downregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels, as validated by RT-qPCR validation and Western blot (Fig. 5B-H, Fig. S12A-C, p < 0.01). Meanwhile, IF staining results for BIRC5 in HCC cells consistently demonstrated a marked reduction in BIRC5 expression following BIRC5 knockdown (Fig. 5I-L, p < 0.05). CCK8 results revealed that BIRC5 silencing significantly impaired viability in HCC cells at 24, 48 and 72 h (Fig. 5M-O, Fig. S12D, p < 0.05). Colony formation assays further solidified that BIRC5 knockdown led to a marked reduction in both colony number and size across HCC cells (Fig. 5P-S, p < 0.05). Ki67 staining results further revealed that BIRC5 knockdown significantly reduced the expression of Ki67 (Fig. 5T-W, p < 0.05).

To evaluate the impact on metastatic traits, scratch wound-healing and transwell migration assays were performed. Scratch assays demonstrated that BIRC5-silenced HCC cells exhibited significantly delayed wound closure, especially after 48 h (Fig. 6A, D-F, Fig. S12E, G, p < 0.05), indicative of impaired migratory capacity. Similarly, transwell experiments revealed a substantial decrease in migration cell counts in BIRC5-downregulated HCC cells (Fig. 6B, G-I, Fig. S12F, H, p < 0.05), confirming that BIRC5 depletion weakens the invasive phenotype of HCC cells. Furthermore, BIRC5 knockdown significantly decreased the fluorescence intensity of Vimentin, which as a marker associated with cell motility and migration (Fig. 6C, J-L, p < 0.05). To systematically assess the correlation between BIRC5 expression and HCC phenotypic traits, we performed a correlation network analysis. The heatmap revealed significant negative correlations between BIRC5 and key malignant phenotypes, including transwell migration, colony formation, wound healing, and the expression of proliferation marker Ki67 and EMT marker Vimentin (Fig. 6C, J-L, p < 0.05). These findings collectively establish BIRC5 as a key regulator of multiple aggressive phenotypes in HCC, further supporting its potential as a therapeutic target.

Given the centrality of mitochondrial function in supporting cancer cell bioenergetics, we assessed mitochondrial homeostasis in BIRC5-downregulated HCC cells. ATP assays showed a significant reduction in ATP production, reflecting compromised mitochondrial energy metabolismin BIRC5-downregulated HCC cells (Fig. 7A-C, Fig. S12I, p < 0.05). JC-1 staining further revealed a marked decrease in MMP, a hallmark of mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 7D-F, Fig. S12J, p < 0.05), concurrent with elevated ROS levels in BIRC5-knockdown HCC cells, consistent with oxidative stress induced by mitochondrial impairment (Fig. 7G-I, Fig. S12K, p < 0.05). Western blot analysis revealed that siBIRC5 reduced the levels of mitochondrial dynamics-related proteins, including OPA1, FIS1, and MFF (Fig. 7K-M, p < 0.05). To systematically assess the correlation between BIRC5 expression and HCC mitochondrial function, we performed a correlation network analysis. The heatmap revealed significant negative correlations between BIRC5 and mitochondrial dynamic function (ATP, MMP, and mitochondrial dynamic related proteins), and positive correlations between BIRC5 and ROS (Fig. 7J, p < 0.05).

To further confirm the causal role of BIRC5 in NAFLD-HCC progression, complementary gain-function manipulations was performed by using overexpression BIRC5 in SK-hep1 HCC cells. CCK8 assays showed that overexpressed BIRC5 enhanced cell proliferation (Fig. S12L, p < 0.05). RT-qPCR and Western blot verified that overexpressed BIRC5 transfection upregulated BIRC5 at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. S12M-N, p < 0.01). Scratch wound-healing assays demonstrated that BIRC5 overexpression cells exhibited promoted wound closure at 24 and 48 h (Fig. S12P, R, p < 0.05). Transwell invasion assays further confirmed that overexpressed BIRC5 increased invasive cell counts (Fig. S12Q, S, p < 0.05). Mitochondrial function assays revealed that overexpressed BIRC5 promoted ATP production, increased MMP, and decreased ROS levels in SK-hep1 cells (Fig. S12T-V, p < 0.05).

Collectively, these data establish that BIRC5 depletion exerted a multifaceted suppressive effect on HCC progression, encompassing attenuated proliferation, impaired migration, and disrupted mitochondrial homeostasis, while BIRC5 overexpression promoted HCC progression, thereby positioning BIRC5 as a promising therapeutic target in HCC.

BIRC5 exhibits a negative correlation with the NAFLD phenotype

To elucidate the role of BIRC5 in NAFLD model, we first performed the BIRC5 siRNA and BIRC5 inhibitor YM155 in AML12 cells. Western blot and RT-qPCR results verified the successful knockdown of the BIRC5 gene (Fig. 8A-C, Fig. S13 A-C, p < 0.01). Cell viability assessment confirmed that BIRC5 downregulation did not compromise AML12 cell survival (Fig. 8D, Fig. S13D), establishing the suitability of this approach for functional studies. As expected, FFA exposure significantly upregulated BIRC5 gene expression, which effectively attenuated by si-BIRC5 pretreatment, and comfired in BIRC5 protein level (Fig. 8E, X, p < 0.05). ORO staining results showed that downregulated BIRC5 significantly reversed the effect of FFA in promoting lipid droplet accumulation (Fig. 8F, Fig. S13E). BIRC5 downregulation also significantly reduced TC, TG, ALT, and AST levels which upregulated by FFA (Fig. 8G-J, Fig S13F, p < 0.05). To investigate the mechanistic impact of BIRC5 on lipid metabolism, we analyzed the expression of key genes involved in fatty acid oxidation, lipolysis, and synthesis using RT-qPCR. FFA treatment significantly downregulated critical lipid metabolism genes (Agl, Cpt1, Hsl, Pdk4) and fatty acid β-oxidation genes (Pparα, Acadl, and Acox), consistent with impaired lipid catabolism in NAFLD. Strikingly, BIRC5 knockdown reversed this suppression, restoring their expression compared to FFA group (Fig. 8K-Q, p < 0.05). Conversely, lipogenic genes (Scd1, Fasn, and Srebp1α) were markedly upregulated by FFA exposure, while BIRC5 silencing attenuated this induction (Fig. 8R-T, p < 0.05), suggesting its role in curbing aberrant lipid synthesis. To conclude, these data demonstrate that BIRC5 depletion rebalances lipid homeostasis by coordinately increasing fatty acid oxidation while inhibiting de novo lipogenesis in hepatocytes.

Since mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of NAFLD progression, characterized by impaired β-oxidation and exacerbated oxidative stress, we comprehensively assessed mitochondrial function. Results showed that FFA treatment reduced ATP levels, while BIRC5 downregulation restored ATP production, indicating that BIRC5 silencing reversed FFA-impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism (Fig. 8U, Fig. S13G, p < 0.05). For ROS levels, FFA stimulation alone increased ROS accumulation, consistent with the heightened oxidative stress in NAFLD, whereas concurrent BIRC5 downregulation and FFA stimulation resulted in lower ROS levels compared to the FFA-only group, suggesting BIRC5 silencing alleviated FFA-induced oxidative stress (Fig. 8V, Fig. S13H, p < 0.05). JC-1 assay revealed that FFA stimulation decreased MMP, reflecting mitochondrial damage in NAFLD; in contrast, downregulated BIRC5 restored FFA-induced MMP decreased, indicating BIRC5 silencing mitigated FFA-mediated impairment and preserved mitochondrial membrane integrity (Fig. 8W, Fig. S13I, p < 0.05). Western blot analysis demonstrated that FFA treatment led to a reduction in mitochondrial dynamics-related proteins (OPA1, FIS1, and MFF), while siBIRC5 restored their expression levels deregulated by FFA (Fig. 8X, p < 0.05). Additionally, correlation heatmap analysis of the in vitro NAFLD model revealed significant positive associations between BIRC5 and key lipid metabolism genes, as well as ROS levels. In contrast, BIRC5 exhibited negative correlations with liver function markers (TC, TG, ALT, and AST), lipid β-oxidation genes, and mitochondrial function-related parameters (ATP production, MMP, and mitochondrial dynamics-associated proteins) (Fig. 8Y, p < 0.05), These results further support the involvement of BIRC5 in the maintenance of mitochondrial homeostasis.

We uesd the overexpressed BIRC5 to further confirm the pivotal role of BIRC5 in NAFLD progression in FFA-induced AML12 hepatocytes. CCK8 assays showed no significant cytotoxicity of overexpressed BIRC5 on AML12 cells (Fig. S13J). RT-qPCR and Western blot confirmed effective BIRC5 upregulation by overexpressed BIRC5 (Fig. S13K-M, p < 0.01). ORO staining revealed that BIRC5 overexpression exacerbated FFA-induced lipid droplet accumulation (Fig. S13N, p < 0.05). Biochemical analyses demonstrated that overexpressed BIRC5 promoted FFA-induced elevation of TC, TG, ALT and AST levels (Fig. S13O, p < 0.05). Mitochondrial function assessments showed that BIRC5 overexpression discreased ATP production and MMP, and increased ROS levels in FFA-challenged AML12 cells (Fig. S13P-R, p < 0.05).

Overall, these findings demonstrate that BIRC5 downregulation ameliorates FFA-induced liver function damage, lipid accumulation, dysregulated lipid metabolism, and mitochondrial dysfunction, while BIRC5 overexpression promotes steatosis and mitochondrial impairment.

Discussion

NAFLD represents a growing driver of HCC, with its progression from benign steatosis to NASH, cirrhosis, and ultimately HCC characterized by irreversible hepatic inflammation, collagen deposition, and metabolic dysfunction (Huang et al. 2024). Epidemiological studies highlight the rising incidence of metabolic-associated HCC (Garuti et al. 2021), yet the molecular mechanisms linking NAFLD to HCC remain incompletely understood, hindering targeted interventions. Given the poor prognosis of HCC due to high recurrence and metastasis rates (Zhang et al. 2020; Aytac et al. 2022), identifying key genes mediating NAFLD-HCC progression is critical for improving diagnostic and treatment approaches.

To elucidate the molecular basis driving NAFLD-HCC progression, we performed an integrated multi-omics analysis combining transcriptomic and disease-gene databases. This study integrated multi-omics data from 531 TCGA samples and 1092 GEO-derived DEGs with disease-specific genes from DisGeNET, Genecards, CTD, and OMIM, identifying 84 DEGs enriched in NAFLD-HCC progression pathways including oxidative stress, lipid metabolism reprogramming, cell cycle dysregulation, and inflammation (Kudo et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2021) They localize to key cellular compartments including cytoplasmic vesicle lumens, secretory granule lumens, and collagen-rich extracellular matrix (ECM), implicating their roles in metabolic transport and ECM remodeling during hepatic fibrosis, with network analysis identifying 8 hub genes (BIRC5, CCNA2, CCNB1, CDK1, CDK4, CDKN2A, PARP1, and TOP2A) that were subsequently validated through clinical correlation, survival analysis, immune infiltration profiling, and drug sensitivity assays, ultimately narrowing to four critical genes (BIRC5, CCNB1, CDK1, and TOP2A). These genes exhibited significant transcriptional (TCGA-GTEx cohort) and translational (HPA and IHC validation) overexpression in HCC specimens compared to normal liver tissues, confirming their oncogenic properties in hepatocarcinogenesis. While these genes represent viable therapeutic targets, BIRC5 emerged as the most promising candidate, exhibiting consistent associations with clinical outcomes, immune microenvironment remodeling, and metabolic dysfunction across analyses, warranting focused functional validation.

BIRC5, also called Baculoviral Inhibitor of Apoptosis Repeat-Containing 5 or survivin, is an important anti-apoptotic IAP family member that inhibits caspase activation to impair apoptosis (Kudo et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2021). In high fed diet (HFD)-fed obese mice, its upregulation in white adipose tissue suppresses isoproterenol-induced lipolysis via reduced Fsp27 degradation, linking it to lipid metabolism and NAFLD (Ju et al. 2017). BIRC5 is clinically targetable and drives tumorigenesis by promoting proliferation, invasion, immunosuppression and metabolic reprogramming. This function is conserved across multiple cancers: in HCC, 94.41% of circulating tumor cells are BIRC5-positive, with oncogenesis mediated by peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) or circulating RNA-induced BIRC5 upregulation (Yu et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2021; Mai et al. 2024); in head and neck cancer, it correlates with serum glucose levels and regulates glucose metabolism genes (Yu et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2021; Mai et al. 2024); in neuroblastoma, it inhibits mitochondrial activity and oxidative phosphorylation while enhancing glycolysis to support rapid proliferation (Hagenbuchner et al. 2016). Male-biased elevation of BIRC5 in obese individuals may underlie gender disparities in NAFLD-related HCC (Izquierdo et al. 2022). Consistent with these findings, our study further extends BIRC5’ s role to NAFLD-HCC, linking its metabolic regulatory function to oncogenic transformation.

In our study, BIRC5 showed strong associations with AFP, tumor stage, and status and demonstrated high diagnostic value (AUC > 0.7), consistent with its link to tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis (Lu et al. 2024; Seven et al. 2025). High BIRC5 expression predicted reduced DSS, OS, and PFI, while copy-number gains (~ 4%) correlated with elevated expression and unfavorable outcomes. Together with its effects on stromal remodeling and drug sensitivity, these findings highlight BIRC5 as a central regulator connecting metabolic dysfunction, immune suppression, and malignant transformation in NAFLD-HCC. Consistently, BIRC5 expression correlated with Th2 cells, tumor-associated macrophages, and CD4⁺ memory T cells, suggesting its role in reinforcing the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (Mo et al. 2022; An et al. 2025). Pan-cancer analyses confirmed its association with multiple immune subtypes and immunosuppressive infiltration, including regulatory T-cell enrichment and poor immune checkpoints inhibitors (ICIs) response in lung adenocarcinoma (Li et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2025), which may explain the limited efficacy of ICIs in NAFLD-HCC (Pfister et al. 2021). Clinical samples further validated BIRC5 upregulation in HCC tissues and NAFLD models. supporting its clinical and translational relevance across disease progression. Thus, BIRC5 may contribute not only to tumor initiation but also to immune evasion and therapeutic resistance in NAFLD-related HCC.

Comprehensive bioinformatics and in vitro analyses identified BIRC5 as a central mediator of NAFLD-HCC progression, orchestrating both metabolic homeostasis and oncogenic signaling. BIRC5 silencing markedly alleviated FFA-induced steatosis, reducing ALT, AST, TC, and TG levels by ~ 50% and decreasing lipid droplet accumulation. The functional impairment caused by elevated FFAs affects hepatocellular energy metabolism, prompting us to examine mitochondrial function. Since total cellular ATP content serves as the primary indicator of overall mitochondrial function, MMP reflects cellular energy metabolism status, and excessive ROS can cause cellular damage (Zhang et al. 2024), Mechanistically, BIRC5 depletion restored lipid balance by downregulating lipogenic and upregulating β-oxidation genes and preserved mitochondrial integrity, as evidenced by normalized ATP production, reduced ROS, maintained MMP, and stabilized mitochondrial dynamics-related proteins. Notably, YM155 treatment reproduced these protective effects by restoring mitochondrial and hepatic function, while oeBIRC5 overexpression conversely aggravated steatosis and mitochondrial dysfunction. Collectively, these findings establish BIRC5 as a pro-steatotic regulator linking lipid metabolic stress to mitochondrial dysfunction and hepatocarcinogenesis.

A malignant tumor represents a cellular mass exhibiting abnormally accelerated proliferation compared to healthy tissue (Vaghari-Tabari et al. 2021), Parallel experiments demonstrated that BIRC5 knockdown markedly suppressed HCC malignant phenotypes, significantly reducing cell proliferation (CCK8 assay) and colony formation efficiency by ~ 50%. Consistently, Ki67 expression, a canonical proliferation marker, was markedly decreased following BIRC5 silencing, confirming impaired proliferative activity. Pharmacologic inhibition of BIRC5 by YM155 reproduced these effects, while oeBIRC5 overexpression enhanced SK-hep1 cell proliferation. Together, these findings confirm BIRC5 as a critical oncogenic driver promoting hepatocellular carcinoma cell growth, further underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target in NAFLD-related HCC. Emerging evidence has also demonstrated that in pancreatic cancer, BIRC5 activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway to sustain the stemness of pancreatic cancer stem cells (Ram Makena et al. 2019), whereas in salivary adenoid cystic carcinoma, it interacts with the stem cell transcription factor SOX2 to promote tumor stemness and enhance chemoresistance (Luo et al. 2019). Thus, it can be inferred that BIRC5 may play a pivotal role in maintaining hepatocellular carcinoma stem cells, which warrants further validation in future studies.

BIRC5’ s pro-metastatic function is conserved across multiple cancers, including nasopharyngeal and breast carcinoma, where it promotes EMT and metastatic dissemination via miR-145-CD40 signaling and EMT transcriptional activation (Li et al. 2020; Xu et al. 2024). As a malignant disease, HCC is characterized by its high metastatic potential (Yang et al. 2024). In NAFLD-HCC, BIRC5 exerts similar stromal-modulating and EMT-promoting effects, enabling tumor invasion through fibrotic liver tissue. BIRC5-mediated mitochondrial fission and ROS accumulation further activate EMT pathways (Lee et al. 2021), functionally linking metabolic stress to metastatic competence. In our study, we further identified that BIRC5 exerts similar stromal-modulating and EMT-promoting effects, with mitochondrial dysfunction as a key downstream mediator. Specifically, siBIRC5 significantly impaired HCC cell migration and invasion and induced mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by ATP depletion, MMP collapse, and ROS accumulation. Consistently, pharmacologic inhibition by YM155 reproduced these effects, whereas oeBIRC5 overexpression enhanced migration, invasion, and mitochondrial activity. Collectively, these results establish BIRC5 as a key driver coupling mitochondrial dynamics, metabolic stress, and EMT activation, thereby promoting metastasis in NAFLD-related HCC.

Collectively, these findings identify BIRC5 as a pleiotropic regulator linking hepatic metabolic dysfunction to carcinogenic transformation via mitochondrial pathways. BIRC5 drives lipid accumulation through fatty acid metabolic reprogramming in NAFLD, while promoting HCC progression through anti-apoptotic, proliferative, and immunosuppressive effects, thus serving as a key bridge between metabolism and malignancy. Moreover, its interconnected roles in metabolism, stemness, metastasis, and immunity create a permissive tumor microenvironment and enhance therapy resistance. Together with its conserved oncogenic function across cancers, BIRC5 emerges as a promising biomarker and therapeutic target in NAFLD-HCC.

While our study provides multi-omics and experimental evidence identifying BIRC5 as a key nexus linking metabolic dysfunction, immune suppression, and malignant transformation in NAFLD-HCC, several limitations remain. First, although BIRC5’s expression and function were validated in a 12-week HFD-induced NAFLD model, later disease stages involving NASH, fibrosis, and tumorigenesis were not assessed. Long-term models are needed to determine whether BIRC5 deletion or inhibition (e.g., YM155) can block progression along the steatosi-fibrosis-tumorigenesis sequence. Secondly, in vitro experiments and correlation analyses demonstrated that BIRC5 regulates lipid accumulation, mitochondrial function, and tumor malignancy. However, its potential interaction with lipid metabolism enzymes (Cpt1a, Fasn) or mitochondrial fission regulator (DRP1-S616) remains unclear and requires further experimental validation.

Conclusion

In summary, integrated multi-omics analysis and functional validation identified BIRC5 as a critical driver of NAFLD-HCC progression, orchestrating metabolic reprogramming, mitochondrial homeostasis, immune modulation, and tumor cell survival. Among eight candidate genes linked to NAFLD-HCC transition, BIRC5 emerged as the core molecular hub with both diagnostic and prognostic significance. Targeting BIRC5 may simultaneously alleviate NAFLD-related hepatic injury and suppress HCC development, highlighting its dual metabolic-oncogenic therapeutic potential. Future studies should elucidate BIRC5’s upstream regulators (e.g., metabolic sensors and inflammatory pathways) and downstream effectors in lipid metabolism, and evaluate combination strategies integrating BIRC5 inhibition with metabolic regulators (such as PPAR agonists) or immune checkpoint inhibitors. Such efforts may refine BIRC5’s clinical utility as both a therapeutic target and biomarker, ultimately advancing precision interventions for metabolic-associated HCC.
