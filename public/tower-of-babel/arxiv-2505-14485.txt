Normal Quaternionic Matrices and Finitely Generated Witt Rings
Nico Lorenz, Alexander Schönert

Abstract




1991 Mathematics Subject Classification

0.
Introduction

In attempts to characterize (finitely) generated Witt rings over fields of characteristic different from two, there have been abstract approaches for more than 50 years now, starting with the work of Knebusch, Rosenberg, and Ware in the early 1970s (
[
KRW72
]
).
In our context,
abstract
means that we try to grasp the ring structure of Witt rings by mimicking the structure of the square class group along with the represented values of binary quadratic forms, leading to
quadratic form schemes
as introduced by Cordes in
[
Cor76
]
, or the behavior of 2-fold Pfister forms and their associated quaternion algebras, leading to
quaternionic structures
as in Marshall’s book in 1980
[
Mar80
]
.
The idea of looking at Witt rings from this perspective has its origin in the following result of Cordes from 1973.
Note that we assume all fields to be of characteristic different from two whenever we do not explicitly consider the field
\mathbb{F}_{2}
with cardinality
2
.

Proposition 0.1
.

(Cordes
[
Cor73
, 2.2/2.3]
)

Let
E,F
be fields with
G_{E}:=E^{\ast}/E^{\ast 2}
and
G_{F}:=F^{\ast}/F^{\ast 2}
.
The Witt rings of
E
and
F
are isomorphic as rings if and only if there is a group isomorphism
\Phi:G_{E}\to G_{F}
with
\Phi(-E^{\ast 2})=-F^{\ast 2}
preserving the isomorphy type of quaternion algebras, that is,

\forall a,b,c,d\in G_{E}:\quad(a,b)_{E}\cong(c,d)_{E}\iff(\Phi(a),\Phi(b))_{F}\cong(\Phi(c),\Phi(d))_{F}.

We focus on the approach to describe Witt rings using the properties of quaternion algebras (resp. their norm forms which are
2
-fold Pfister forms) and mainly follow Marshall’s book
[
Mar80
]
for our basic notations and definitions.

All so far known examples of finitely generated Witt rings are of the so called
elementary type
, meaning that they can be derived from certain Witt rings only using two simple construction types.
This gives rise to the
elementary type conjecture
(cf.
Remark

2.5
) stating that in fact all finitely generated Witt rings are of such type.
It is known that the elementary type conjecture holds for Witt rings and thus quaternionic structures over fields
F
of small
order

n:=\Log_{2}(|F^{\ast}/F^{\ast 2}|)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
The case
n\leq 2
can be taken care of trivially (cf.
Example

1.2
), while the case
n=3
was first done by Kula, Szczepanik and Szymiczek
[
KSS79
]
and the case
n\in\{4,5\}
by Carson and Marshall
[
CM82
]
.
The case
n=4
was also dealt with by Szczepanik in 1985 independently (
[
Szc85
]
).
In this paper we introduce a new perspective involving an abstract version of the
2
-torsion part of the Brauer group and developing
normal quaternionic matrices
which uniquely display quaternionic structures.
We apply this to prove some hitherto open cases for the elementary type conjecture, in particular showing that the elementary type conjecture holds for order up to
6
. We also verify the elementary type conjecture for order up to
7
in all but a few cases in which our computations get too time consuming.
While the theoretical parts from
Section

1
to
Section

5
were developed by the second author in terms of his PhD thesis, the computational approach described in
Section

6
was obtained by a joint effort of the authors.

We now give a brief outline of the structure of the article at hand.
In
Section

1
, we introduce our notation and give the definition of
quaternionic structures
.

Section

2
will be used to collect all necessary ingredients to state the
elementary type conjecture
along with some counting arguments which will be needed later on.
This will be followed up by
Section

3
where an abstract analogue to the
2
-torsion part of the Brauer group is explored.
In
Section

4
, we define so called
quaternionic matrices
as a means to efficiently compute and describe quaternionic structures of small order.
We also introduce the already mentioned
normal quaternionic matrices
which are then further investigated in
Section

5
.
Finally, in the last
Section

6
, we present the algorithm and the computational means by which we prove our main result.

1.
Quaternionic Structures

We summarize the main definitions and results needed for this article from Marshall’s book
[
Mar80
]
.
The results of classical quadratic form theory mentioned here can, for example, be found in Lam’s standard textbook
[
Lam05
]
.

Throughout this paper, we often consider groups
G
of exponent
2
, which means that every element in
G
is its own inverse.
These can be viewed as vector spaces over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, so by the
dimension
of
G
we always mean its dimension as an
\mathbb{F}_{2}
vector space.
This dimension is finite if and only if
G
is finite and in this case, we have
|G|=2^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Quaternionic structures as defined below imitate the properties of isomorphy types of quaternion algebras over a given field of characteristic different from
2
.

Definition
.

Let
G
be a multiplicative group of exponent
2
with neutral element
1\in G
and a distinguished element
-1\in G
(the case
-1=1
being possible).
Write
-a:=(-1)a
for
a\in G
.
Let
Q
be a set with distinguished element
0\in Q
and
q:G\times G\to Q
a surjective map.
We call the elements of
Q

quaternions
.
We say that
S:=(G,-1,q)
is a
quaternionic structure
if the following hold for all
a,b,c,d\in G
:

(Q1)

q(a,-a)=0

(Q2)

q(a,b)=q(b,a)

(Q3)

q(a,b)=q(a,c)\iff q(a,bc)=0

(Q4)

q(a,b)=q(c,d)\iff\exists x\in G:\ q(a,b)=q(a,x)=q(c,x)=q(c,d)

Furthermore, we define the
radical
of
S
as

\rad(S):=\{a\in G\mid q(a,x)=0\ \forall x\in G\}.

We say that
S
is
degenerate
if
\rad(S)\not=\{1\}
, and else
nondegenerate
.
If
G=\rad(S)
, we say that
S
is
totally degenerate
.

If
S^{\prime}=(G^{\prime},-1^{\prime},q^{\prime})
is another quaternionic structure, we say that
S
and
S^{\prime}
are
isomorphic
, in symbols
S\cong S^{\prime}
, if there exists a group isomorphism
\phi:G\to G^{\prime}
satisfying
\phi(-1)=-1^{\prime}
and for all
a,b\in G
:

q(a,b)=0\iff q^{\prime}(\phi(a),\phi(b))=0.

We refer to
\dim(G)
as the
order
of
S
.
Note that we sometimes refer to
G
as the
square class group (of
S
)
.

Remark 1.1
.

(a)

Every field
F
defines a quaternionic structure, using
G_{F}:=F^{\ast}/F^{\ast 2}
and
q_{F}(a,b):=(a,b)_{F}
, the quaternion algebra with slots
a
and
b
over
F
(for
(Q4)
, see
[
Lam05
, Common Slot Theorem III.4.13]
).
Here, the zero-element in
Q
can be viewed as the split quaternion algebra or the zero in the Witt ring.
We write
S_{F}:=(G_{F},-F^{\ast 2},q_{F})
.

(b)

It is an easy exercise to show that quaternionic structures are isomorphic if and only if the abstract analogue of the condition from
Proposition

0.1
is satisfied, justifying the above definition.

(c)

It is an immediate consequence of the axioms
(Q1)
,
(Q2)
and
(Q3)
that any quaternionic structure
(G,-1,q)
satisfies
q(a,1)=0
and for all
a,b\in G
, we have:

q(a,b)=q(a,-ab)=q(b,-ab)

We include some basic examples along with their notation:

Example 1.2
.

•

There is only one quaternionic structure of order zero, denoted as
\mathbb{L}_{0}
, and defined by the unique map
\{1\}\times\{1\}\to\{0\}
.
It is realized as
S_{F}
for any quadratically closed field
F
of characteristic different from
2
, for example
\mathbb{L}_{0}\cong S_{\mathbb{C}}
.
This structure is both totally degenerate and nondegenerate by definition.

•

There are exactly two non isomorphic totally degenerate quaternionic structures of order
n\in\mathbb{N}
, depending on the choice whether or not
-1=1
in the square class group
G
and defined by the unique map
G\times G\to\{0\}
.
For
n=1
, we denote this structure by
\mathbb{L}_{1,0}
if
-1=1
and
\mathbb{L}_{1,1}
if
-1\not=1
.

•

There are three quaternionic structures
S=(\{1,a\},-1,q)
of order
1
:
Firstly, if
-1=1
, then

q(1,1)=q(1,a)=q(a,1)=q(a,a)=0
,
so
S\cong\mathbb{L}_{1,0}
.
Secondly, if
-1=a
and
S\not\cong\mathbb{L}_{1,1}
, then it can be easily verified that
S\cong S_{\mathbb{R}}=:\mathbb{L}_{1}
.
Note that also
\mathbb{L}_{1,0}
and
\mathbb{L}_{1,1}
are realized by finite fields of cardinality
\equiv_{4}1
resp.
\equiv_{4}3
.

•

Let
S=(G,-1,q)
be a finite nondegenerate quaternionic structure of order
n\geq 3
with set of quaternions
Q
with
|Q|=2
, called a
local (quaternionic) structure
.
Then
q
can be viewed as a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear map
G\times G\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
(recall that
G
is an
\mathbb{F}_{2}
vector space).
Conversely, any such nondegenerate symmetric bilinear map defines a local structure.
There are exactly two local structures for even
n
, written
\mathbb{L}_{n,0}
(the case
-1=1
) and
\mathbb{L}_{n,1}
(the case
-1\not=1
).
Also, there is only one local structure for any odd
n
, written
\mathbb{L}_{n}
.
All the local quaternionic structures can be realised by certain field extensions of
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
, the
2
-adic completion of
\mathbb{Q}
, cf.
[
Mar80
, Chapter 5]
.

Remark 1.3
.

We do not give the definition of
abstract Witt rings
here as we do not need it explicitly.
We refer to
[
Mar80
, 4.5]
where it is shown that the categories of quaternionic structures and abstract Witt rings as defined there are equivalent.
We only outline the notation for quadratic forms and important properties that will be employed here.
From a classical standpoint, the quadratic forms considered here correspond to
nondegenerate
and
diagonalized
quadratic forms in the classical sense:

•

A
(quadratic) form over
S
of
dimension

n\in\mathbb{N}
is an
n
-tuple, denoted
\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle\in G^{n}
.

•

Isometry
of quadratic forms (denoted by
\cong
) is defined inductively as in (
[
Lam05
, §I, Exercise 19]
) and
isotropy
derived from it canonically.
Isometry defines an equivalence relation (technical proof by Marshall in
[
Mar80
, 2.6]
) and isometry is invariant under permutation of tuple entries which means that the following is well defined.

•

For quadratic forms
\varphi=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle
and
\psi:=\langle b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\rangle
, the
sum
and
product
are

\varphi\perp\psi:=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi\otimes\psi:=\perp_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}\varphi:=\perp_{i=1}^{m}\langle b_{i}a_{1},\ldots,b_{i}a_{n}\rangle.

•

The
hyperbolic plane

\mathbb{H}:=\langle 1,-1\rangle
is the unique isotropic form of dimension
2
.
A quadratic form is called
hyperbolic
if it is isometric to the
k
-fold sum of hyperbolic planes
k\times\mathbb{H}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

•

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in G
, we say that
\langle\!\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle\!\rangle:=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\langle 1,-a_{i}\rangle
is an
n
-fold Pfister form
.

•

The set of
represented elements
of a form
\varphi
over
S
is
D_{S}(\varphi):=\{a\in G\mid\varphi\cong\langle a,\ldots\rangle\}
.

•

The
value group of
a\in G
is defined as the set

V(a):=D_{S}(\langle\!\langle a\rangle\!\rangle)

and can also be written as

\{b\in G\mid q(a,b)=0\}
.
Note that in literature, similar to the ambiguity of the sign convention for Pfister forms, this set is sometimes defined as
V(-a)
.

•

Defining the (abstract) Witt ring
W(S)
of a quaternionic structure
S=(G,-1,q)
now works in most parts analogue to the classical case, by establishing
Witt cancellation
and
Witt equivalence
in the abstract setting.

•

Viewed as elements of the Witt ring of
S
, the
1
-fold Pfister forms additively generate the
fundamental ideal

I(S)
of (Witt equivalence classes of) even dimensional forms.
As in the classical theory, the
k
-th power
I^{k}(S)
of
I(S)
is additively generated by the
k
-fold Pfister forms.
Note that our notation usually does not distinguish between quadratic forms and their Witt equivalence class as an element of the Witt ring whenever the point of view is clear from the context.

Lastly, consider the following property for the
k
-th power of the fundamental ideal, with
k\in\mathbb{N}
:

(
AP_{k}
)

\displaystyle\text{If $\varphi\in I^{k}(S)\setminus\{0\}$ is anisotropic, then }\dim(\varphi)\geq 2^{k}

In the case that
S
comes from a field, the above holds for arbitrary
k\in\mathbb{N}
, which is known as the Arason-Pfister Hauptsatz
[
AP71
]
.
Generally,
AP_{1}
and
AP_{2}
hold in abstract Witt rings (cf.
[
Mar80
, 3.23]
) while it is an open question whether or not
AP_{k}
holds for any
k\geq 3
.
Especially
AP_{3}
plays an important role considering the abstract Brauer group as can be seen later.
For a quaternionic structure satisfying
AP_{3}
we henceforth simply say
AP_{3}
structure
.

2.
The Elementary Type Conjecture

In this section, we recollect all ingredients for the elementary type conjecture as stated by Marshall
[
Mar80
, Chapter 5, Problem 4]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
S=(G,-1,q)
,
S_{1}=(G_{1},e_{1},q_{1})
and
S_{2}=(G_{2},e_{2},q_{2})
be quaternionic structures with sets of quaternions
Q,Q_{1},Q_{2}
.

The
direct product

S_{1}\times S_{2}:=(G_{1}\times G_{2},(e_{1},e_{2}),q_{1}\times q_{2})
with quaternions
Q_{1}\times Q_{2}

q_{1}\times q_{2}((a_{1},a_{2}),(b_{1},b_{2})):=(q_{1}(a_{1},b_{1}),q_{2}(a_{2},b_{2}))

and zero-quaternion
(0,0)
is a quaternionic structure with abstract Witt ring

W(S_{1}\times S_{2})=(W(S_{1})\times W(S_{2}))_{\text{par}}:=\{(\varphi,\psi)\mid\dim(\varphi)\equiv_{2}\dim(\psi)\}.

Let
G_{x}
be the direct product of
G
with a cyclic group
\{1,x\}
of order
2
, written as an inner direct product.
The
group extension

S[x]:=(G_{x},-1,q_{x})
with quaternions in
Q\times G
,

q_{x}(ax^{\alpha},bx^{\beta})=(q(a,b),(-1)^{\alpha\beta}a^{\beta}b^{\alpha})

and zero quaternion
(0,1)
is a quaternionic structure with Witt ring
W(S[x])=W(S)[\{1,x\}]
, the group ring of the Witt ring of
S
and the cyclic group of order 2.
We often write
S\Delta:=S[x]
.
Constructing the group extension can be iterated and we write
S\Delta_{n}
for the
n
-fold group extension of
S
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Remark 2.2
.

If in the above proposition,
S\cong S_{F}
for a field
F
, then
S_{F}\Delta\cong S_{E}
for the field
E:=F(\!(x)\!)
of formal Laurent series over
F
.
In 1985, applying quite some valuation theory, Kula
[
Kul85
]
showed that if
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are realized by fields, then so is
S_{1}\times S_{2}
.

Example 2.3
.

•

The totally degenerate quaternionic structure of order
n\in\mathbb{N}
(cf.
Example

1.2
) are exactly
\mathbb{L}_{1,0}^{n}
(the
n
-fold direct product) and
\mathbb{L}_{1,1}^{n}
.

•

We have
\mathbb{L}_{0}\Delta\cong\mathbb{L}_{1,0}
which also follows from
Example

1.2
as there is only one quaternionic structure of order
2
and level
1
.

•

We have
\mathbb{L}_{1}\times\mathbb{L}_{1}\cong\mathbb{L}_{1}\Delta
.
This is in fact the only
decomposable
(meaning that it can be written as the direct product of two structures of order at least 1) group extension (cf.
[
Mar04
, p. 8]
).

Marshall introduced the following
normalized decomposition
(
[
Mar80
, 5.12]
).

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
S
be a finite quaternionic structure with
|\rad(S)|=2^{m}
.
Choose
\epsilon=0
if
-1=1
in
S
and
\epsilon=1
else.
There exist indecomposable nondegenerate quaternionic structures
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
such that

S\cong\mathbb{L}_{1,\epsilon}^{m}\times S_{1}\times\ldots\times S_{k}

and the
S_{i}
are unique up to isomorphy and permutation.

Definition
.

Let
S
be a finite quaternionic structure.
We call
S
of
simple elementary type
if
S
is isomorphic to one of
\mathbb{L}_{0},\mathbb{L}_{1,0},\mathbb{L}_{1,1},\mathbb{L}_{1}
or a local structure.
We call
S
of
elementary type
if
S
can be obtained from simple elementary types applying only direct products and group extensions.

Remark 2.5
.

The
elementary type conjecture (ETC)
states that all finite quaternionic structures are in fact of elementary type.
Along with Kula’s result mentioned in
Remark

2.2
, it follows that all elementary type structures are realized by fields.
Since it is not yet known whether all quaternionic structures are in fact realized by fields, a possibly weaker version of this conjecture is that all finite quaternionic structures which are realised by fields are of elementary type, this version sometimes being referred to as the
weak elementary type conjecture (wETC)
.
Both versions have so far only been proven for special cases.
Some of them will be stated at the end of this section.
However, we first give an overview of elementary type structures of small order, also considering some important invariants.

The following invariants of isomorphy types of quaternionic structures can be defined as in the classical setting:

Definition
.

Let
S=(G,-1,q)
be a quaternionic structure.
We define the
level
of
S
as

s(S):=\Inf\{k\in\mathbb{N}\mid-1\in D_{S}(k)\}

and the
Pythagoras number
of
S
as

p(S):=\Inf\{k\in\mathbb{N}\mid D_{S}(k)=D_{S}(k+1)\}

where
\Inf(\emptyset)=\infty
and we say that
S
is
pythagorean
if
p(S)=1
.
Furthermore, we define the
u-invariant
of
S
as

u(S):=\Sup\{\dim(\varphi)\mid\varphi\text{ is an anisotropic torsion form over }S\}

where ’torsion form’ means that there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n\times\varphi
is hyperbolic.
In the classical sense, this is often referred to as the
general u-invariant
(cf.
[
Lam05
, Chapter XI]
).

If
F
is a field, note that the values
s(S_{F})
and
p(S_{F})
are the level and Pythagoras number of
F
.
In particular, we point out that the level of
S
is generally not the ring theoretic level of the associated Witt ring.

Remark 2.7
.

There have been many attempts to find a field with finitely many square classes and finite level
\geq 8
.
This gave rise to the
level conjecture (LC)
which states that all finite quaternionic structures (coming from fields) have level in
\{1,2,4,\infty\}
.
It holds for elementary type structures and thus the LC would follow from the ETC.
In fact, the previous example shows that every elementary type structure has level in
\{1,2,4,\infty\}
, Pythagoras number in
\{1,2,3,4,5\}
and
u
-invariant in
\{0\}\cup\{2^{m}\mid m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
and all these values can be obtained (though not independently, since for example the Pythagoras number cannot exceed the level by more than
1
).
The ’smallest’ example for a quaternionic structure with pythagoras number
5
is
\mathbb{L}_{3}\Delta
.
Note that the LC is known to hold for Witt rings (of fields) of order up to 8 by a result of Becher in 2001
[
Bec01
]
.

Definition
.

Let
S=(G,-1,q)
be a quaternionic structure and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
We say that
a\in G
is
k
-rigid
if
\dim(D_{S}(\langle 1,a\rangle))=k
.
If
G
contains a
k
-rigid element, we say that
S
is
k
-rigid
.
If
k\geq 1
and
G
additionally does not contain a
(k-1)
-rigid element, we say that
S
is
strictly
k
-rigid
.
We usually only say
rigid
and
strictly rigid
instead of
1
-rigid and strictly
1
-rigid.

Note that
S
is
0
-rigid if and only if
1\in G
is
0
-rigid if and only if
S
is pythagorean.

We state a few results on the ETC, where especially those regarding
k
-rigid elements for small
k
will be important for reducing the length of the computations in
Section

6
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
S

.

(a)

Marshall in

: If

.

(c)

is of elementary type.

(d)

Assuming that every quaternionic structure of order up to

is of elementary type,

is also of elementary type in the following cases:

•

S

)

•

•

s(S)=1
and

s(S)=2

•

To close this section, we include a technical result to count elementary types.
This is a slightly refined version of the one given in (
[
CM82
, 4.5]
).

Notation
.

We write
\mathcal{E}_{k}(n)
for the set of isomorphy tyes of strictly
k
-rigid nondegenerate elementary type structures of order
n
and

\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{k}(n):=\{S\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(n)\mid S\text{ is indecomposable}\}.

Furthermore, we set
e_{k}(n):=|\mathcal{E}_{k}(n)|
and
\overline{e}_{k}(n):=|\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{k}(n)|
.
Lastly, we write
\mathcal{E}(n)\ /\ \mathcal{E}^{\prime}(n)\ /\ \mathcal{E}_{\text{d}}(n)\ /\ \mathcal{E}_{\Delta}(n)
for the sets of elementary types of order
n\ /\
elementary types of order
n
with level
1\ /\
degenerate elementary types of order
n\ /\
indecomposable group extensions of order
n
and respectively
e(n),\ e^{\prime}(n),e_{\text{d}}(n),e_{\Delta}(n)
for their cardinalities.

Definition
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We define the set
P_{k,n}
as

P_{k,n}:=\{(m_{i,j})_{0\leq i\leq k,\ 1\leq j\leq n}\in\Mat_{(k+1)\times n}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\mid\sum_{i,j}im_{i,j}=k,\ \ \sum_{i,j}jm_{i,j}=n\}.

Note that
P_{0,n}
is the set of partitions of
n
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
k,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
The following hold:

(a)

e_{\text{d}}(n)=e(n-1)+e^{\prime}(n-1)-e^{\prime}(n-2)
for
n\geq 2

(b)

e_{\Delta}(n)=e(n-1)
for
n>2
.

(c)

We have
e_{0}(0)=e_{0}(1)=e_{0}(2)=1=\overline{e}_{0}(1)
,
\overline{e}_{0}(0)=\overline{e}_{0}(2)=0
and
e_{1}(2)=\overline{e}_{1}(2)=2
.
For
k\geq n
, we always have
e_{k}(n)=0
.

(d)

If
n>2
and
k\geq 2
, we have

(i)

\overline{e}_{0}(n)=e_{0}(n-1)
and
e_{0}(n)=\sum_{m\in P_{0,n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\binom{\overline{e}_{0}(j)+m_{0,j}-1}{m_{0,j}}

(ii)

\overline{e}_{1}(n)=e_{\Delta}(n)-\overline{e}_{0}(n)
and
e_{1}(n)=\sum_{j=2}^{n}\overline{e}_{1}(j)e_{0}(n-j)

(iii)

\overline{e}_{k}(n)=\begin{cases}2,&\text{ if }k+1=n\text{ is even}\\
1,&\text{ if }k+1=n\text{ is odd}\\
0,&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

(iv)

e_{k}(n)=\sum_{m\in P_{k,n}}\prod_{0\leq i\leq k,\ 1\leq j\leq n}\binom{\overline{e}_{i}(j)+m_{i,j}-1}{m_{i,j}}
.

Proof.

Recall that by Marshall’s normalized decomposition in
2.4
, every quaternionic structure
S
decomposes as
\mathbb{L}_{1,\varepsilon}^{k}\times S_{1}\times\ldots\times S_{\ell}
for unique nondegenerate indecomposable quaternionic structures
S_{1},\ldots,S_{\ell}
(up to permutation and isomorphism) and unique
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Further, we have
\varepsilon=0
if and only if
s(S)=1
and
\varepsilon=1
else.

(a)
:
The degenerate structures are clearly the ones having
k>0
in the above notation.
If
S
is a degenerate structure of level
1
, it can uniquely be written as
S\cong\mathbb{L}_{1,0}\times S^{\prime}
for
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{n-1}
.
This shows that there are
e^{\prime}(n-1)
degenerate structures of level
1
and order
n
.
If
S
is a degenerate structure of level different from
1
, we uniquely write
S\cong\mathbb{L}_{1,1}\times S^{\prime\prime}
for a structure
S^{\prime\prime}
of order
n-1
that is either of level different from
1
or both nondegenerate and of level
1
.
Since the number of degenerate structures of level
1
and order
n-1
is
e^{\prime}(n-2)
by the above argument, there are

(e(n-1)-e^{\prime}(n-1))+(e^{\prime}(n-1)-e^{\prime}(n-2))=e(n-1)-e^{\prime}(n-2)

choices for
S^{\prime\prime}
.

(b)
:
The only decomposable group extension is
\mathbb{L}_{1}\Delta\cong\mathbb{L}_{1}^{2}
, so the result follows from the fact that
S\Delta\cong S^{\prime}\Delta\iff S\cong S^{\prime}
for any quaternionic structures
S,S^{\prime}
of order greater than
2
.

(c)
:
This is clear from the definitions and the list of small quaternionic structures (cf.
Example

2.6
).

(d)

(d)(i)
:
The set
\mathcal{E}_{0}(n)
contains exactly the pythagorean quaternionic structures of order
n
.
Since
n>2
and local structures are not pythagorean, such a structure is indecomposable if and only if it is the group extension of a pythagorean structure of order
n-1
.
Hence,
\overline{e}_{0}(n)=e_{0}(n-1)
.
For the second part, we have to count the number of products of indecomposable pythagorean structures of smaller orders that are adding up to
n
.
In other words, we just count the number of possible products for each partition
(m_{0,1},\ldots,m_{0,n})\in P_{0,n}
of the order
n
, we can choose
m_{0,i}
factors from
\overline{\mathcal{E}_{0}}(i)
for all
i
.
This gives the desired equation.

(d)(ii)
:
The first part follows immediately as the strictly rigid quaternionic structures are exactly the group extensions that are not pythagorean (cf. results stated in
Proposition

2.8
).
For the second part, note that the nondegenerate strictly rigid quaternionic structures are exactly those with one strictly rigid factor in the normalized decomposition while all other factors have to be pythagorean.
Hence, such a structure can be uniquely written as the product of an indecomposable strictly rigid structure of order
j\in\{2,\ldots,n\}
(
j\not=1
as the factors are nondegenerate) and a pythagorean structure of order
n-j
.

(d)(iii)
:
The indecomposable elementary type structures that have no rigid or
0
-rigid elements are exactly the local structures.
They have only two quaternions and are nondegenerate which immediately shows that local structures of order
n
are strictly
(n-1)
-rigid.

(d)(iv)
:
Clearly, for
S_{\ell}\in\mathcal{E}_{\ell}
and
S_{\ell^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{E}_{\ell^{\prime}}
, we get
S_{\ell}\times S_{\ell^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{E}_{\ell+\ell^{\prime}}
.
Hence, we have to count all products of nondegenerate indecomposable structures
S_{i,j}
from
\mathcal{E}_{i}(j)
such that
\sum_{i}i=k
and
\sum_{j}j=n
.
By definition of
P_{k,n}
, the desired equation follows in the same way as in
(d)(i)
.
∎

The Abstract 2-Brauer Group

To properly deal with quaternionic structures, we need some structure on the set of quaternions.
For this, we imitate the
2
-torsion part of the Brauer group.

Definition
.

Let
S=(G,-1,q)
be a quaternionic structure.
Let
(B,\ast)
be a group into which the set of quaternions
Q
embeds such that

(LR)

\displaystyle q(a,b)\ast q(a,c)=q(a,bc)\quad\text{for any }a,b,c\in G

and
B
is generated by
Q
.
We then call
B
a
2B-group
of
S
.
We call (
LR
) the
linkage relation
and say that two quaternions
q(a,b)
and
q(c,d)
are
linked
if there are
x,y,z\in G
such that
q(a,b)=q(x,y)
and
q(c,d)=q(x,z)
.
The free group
\overline{Q}
generated by the set
Q
with the linkage relations as its sole relations trivially is a 2B-group and will be called the
full 2B-group
of
S
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
S=(G,-1,q)
be a quaternionic structure with 2B-group
B
and set of quaternions
Q
.
The following hold:

(a)

B
is a group of exponent
2
with neutral element
0
.

(b)

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
a,b\in G
, we have
q(a^{\alpha},b)=q(a,b)^{\alpha}=q(a,b^{\alpha})
.

(c)

If
S
is of order
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then

\dim(B)\leq\begin{cases}\binom{n}{2}&\text{ if }1=-1\\
\binom{n}{2}+1&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

Proof.

(a)
:
By definition, we have
q(a,b)\ast q(a,b)=q(a,b^{2})=q(a,1)=0
and
q(a,b)\ast 0=q(a,b)\ast q(a,1)=q(a,b)
, so
B
is a group of exponent
2
with neutral element
0
.

(b)
:
Since both
G
and
B
are groups of exponent
2
, we can reduce to the case
\alpha\in\{0,1\}
.
If
\alpha=0
, then all quaternions are trivial by
Remark

1.1
.
If
\alpha=1
, all exponents are trivial, so there is nothing to show.

(c)
:
Choose a basis
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1})
.
Then
B
is generated by all
q(\prod_{i\in I}a_{i},\prod_{j\in J}a_{j})
for subsets
I,J\subset\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
.
Iteratively applying the linkage relation, we get

q(\prod_{i\in I}a_{i},\prod_{j\in J}a_{j})=\Ast_{i\in I}q(a_{i},\prod_{j\in J}a_{j})=\Ast_{i\in I,j\in J}q(a_{i},a_{j}),

so
B
is generated by the
q(a_{i},a_{j})
.
By symmetry
(Q2)
, we can always assume
i\leq j
here.
Furthermore, choosing
a_{0}=-1
if
-1\not=1\in G
, for any
i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
, we get (cf.
Remark

1.1
)

q(a_{i},a_{i})=q(-1,a_{i})=\begin{cases}q(a_{0},a_{i})&\text{ if }-1\not=1\\
0&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

Hence,
B
is generated by

(q(a_{i},a_{j})\mid 0\leq i<j\leq n-1)
and, if
-1\not=1
additionally
q(a_{0},a_{0})
.
The assertion follows.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
S=(G,-1,q)
and
S^{\prime}=(G^{\prime},-1^{\prime},q^{\prime})
be quaternionic structures with full 2B-groups
B
and
B^{\prime}
.
The following hold:

(a)

The full 2B-group of
S\times S^{\prime}
is isomorphic to
B\times B^{\prime}
.

(b)

The full 2B-group of
S\Delta
is isomorphic to
B\times G
.

Proof.

(a)
:
Let
Q
respectively
Q^{\prime}
be the sets of quaternions of
S
and
S^{\prime}
.
By
Proposition

2.1
, the set of quaternions of
S\times S^{\prime}
can be chosen as
Q\times Q^{\prime}
.
The linkage relations for
S\times S^{\prime}
are thus exactly the linkage relations for
S
and
S^{\prime}
and it follows that the full 2B-group of
S\times S^{\prime}
is isomorphic to
B\times B^{\prime}
.

(b)
:
Write
S\Delta=S[x]=(G\cup Gx,-1,q_{x})
,
Q_{x}
for its set of quaternions and
B_{x}
for the full 2B-group.
Take
a,b\in G
and
\alpha,\beta\in\{0,1\}
.
Again by
Proposition

2.1
, the elements of
Q_{x}
can be viewed as a subset of
Q\times G
with zero-element
(0,1)
, identifying
q_{x}(ax^{\alpha},bx^{\beta})
with
(q(a,b),(-1)^{\alpha\beta}a^{\beta}b^{\alpha})
.
Making use of
Remark

1.1
and
Lemma

3.1

(c)
along with the linkage relations for
q_{x}
, we also get

\displaystyle q_{x}(ax^{\alpha},bx^{\beta})

\displaystyle=q_{x}(a,b)\ast q_{x}(a,x^{\beta})\ast q_{x}(b,x^{\alpha})\ast q_{x}(x^{\alpha},x^{\beta})

\displaystyle=q_{x}(a,b)\ast q_{x}(a^{\beta}b^{\alpha},x)\ast q_{x}(x,x)^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=q_{x}(a,b)\ast q_{x}(a^{\beta}b^{\alpha},x)\ast q_{x}(-1,x)^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=q_{x}(a,b)\ast q_{x}((-1)^{\alpha\beta}a^{\beta}b^{\alpha},x).

Hence,
B_{x}
is generated by
\{q_{x}(a,b),q_{x}(a,x)\mid a,b\in G\}
.
We define

B^{\prime}:=\spn(q_{x}(a,b)\mid a,b\in G)\text{ and }G^{\prime}:=\spn(q_{x}(a,x)\mid a\in G).

Since
q_{x}(a,x)\ast q_{x}(b,x)=q_{x}(ab,x)
for all
a,b\in G
, we have
G^{\prime}\cong G
.
It remains to show that
B^{\prime}\cong B
and
B^{\prime}\cap G^{\prime}
is trivial.
For the former, take
a,b,c,d,e,f\in G
and
\alpha,\beta\in\{0,1\}
.
Since we only have the linkage relations, it follows that

(1)

\displaystyle\quad\quad q_{x}(a,b)\ast q_{x}(c,d)=q_{x}(ex^{\alpha},fx^{\beta})

if and only if there exist
r,s,t\in G
and
\rho,\sigma,\tau\in\{0,1\}
such that

q_{x}(a,b)=q_{x}(rx^{\rho},tx^{\tau}),\quad q_{x}(c,d)=q_{x}(sx^{\sigma},tx^{\tau}),\quad q_{x}(ex^{\alpha},fx^{\beta})=q_{x}(rsx^{\rho+\sigma},tx^{\tau}).

Using the identification from
Proposition

2.1
, this is equivalent to the existence of such
r,s,t,\rho,\sigma,\gamma
satisfying

q(a,b)=q(r,t),\ \ q(c,d)=q(s,t),\ \ q(e,f)=q(rs,t)\text{ and}

(-1)^{\rho\tau}r^{\tau}t^{\rho}=1,\ \ (-1)^{\sigma\tau}s^{\tau}t^{\sigma}=1,\ \ (-1)^{(\rho+\sigma)\tau}(rs)^{\tau}t^{\rho+\sigma}=(-1)^{\alpha\beta}e^{\beta}f^{\alpha}.

The term on the right hand side is the product of the two previous ones, so
(-1)^{\alpha\beta}e^{\beta}f^{\alpha}=1
.
If
\alpha=1
, this means that
f=(-e)^{\beta}
, so either
\beta=0
and
f=1
, meaning
q_{x}(ex^{\alpha},fx^{\beta})=q_{x}(ex,1)
, or
\beta=1
and
q_{x}(ex^{\alpha},fx^{\beta})=q_{x}(ex,-ex)
.
In both cases
q_{x}(ex^{\alpha},fx^{\beta})
is the trivial quaternion
q_{x}(1,1)
.
All in all, this shows that (
1
) holds if and only if

q(a,b)\ast q(c,d)=q(e,f)\text{ and }(\alpha=\beta=0\text{ or }q_{x}(a,b)=q_{x}(c,d)).

Since by
Proposition

2.1
, also
q_{x}(a,b)=q_{x}(c,d)\iff q(a,b)=q(c,d)
, it follows that
B^{\prime}
is generated by the set
\{q_{x}(a,b)\mid a,b\in G\}
with the exactly same (linkage) relations as
B
with generating set
\{q(a,b)\mid a,b\in G\}
and thus
B\cong B^{\prime}
.
Additionally, the above calculations show that the only element of
G^{\prime}
that is generated by
\{q_{x}(a,b)\mid a,b\in G\}
is the trivial quaternion, so
B^{\prime}\cap G^{\prime}
is trivial which remained to prove.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

Both upper bounds in
Lemma

3.1
(c)
can be obtained, taking the iterated group extensions
\mathbb{L}_{0}\Delta_{n}
or
\mathbb{L}_{1}\Delta_{n-1}
.

Proof.

Clearly, the full 2B-group of
\mathbb{L}_{0}
has dimension 0, so it follows from
Proposition

3.2
that the full 2B-group of
\mathbb{L}_{0}\Delta_{n}
has dimension

0+0+1+\ldots+(n-1)=\binom{n}{2}.

The full 2B-group of
\mathbb{L}_{1}
has dimension 1 as the unique nonzero quaternion necessarily must form a basis.
Hence, the full 2B-group of
\mathbb{L}_{1}\Delta_{n-1}
has dimension

1+1+\ldots+(n-1)=\binom{n}{2}+1.\qed

If
F
is a field and
I
the fundamental ideal of its Witt ring, the factor group
I^{2}/I^{3}
is isomorphic to the
2
-torsion part of the Brauer group by Merkurjev’s Theorem
[
Mer81
]
.
In our abstract setting, the full
2B
-group will function as a substitute for
I^{2}/I^{3}
.
We do so to avoid assuming
AP_{3}
as an additional property of quaternionic structures.
In the remainder of this section, we show that both
I^{2}/I^{3}
and the full 2B-group are in fact the same for elementary types.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
S
be an
AP_{3}
structure with fundamental ideal
I
.
Then
I^{2}/I^{3}
is a 2B-group.

Proof.

As pointed out in
Remark

1.3
, the
k
-th power of the fundamental ideal is additively generated by the
k
-fold Pfister forms.
In particular, the set of quaternions generates
I^{2}
, viewing
q(a,b)
as
\langle\!\langle a,b\rangle\!\rangle=\langle 1,-a\rangle\langle 1,-b\rangle
in the Witt ring
W
of
S
for
a,b\in G
.
Assuming
AP_{3}
, we have to show that
Q
also embeds into
I^{2}/I^{3}
.
To do so, we show that

q(a,b)=q(c,d)\in Q\iff\langle 1,-a\rangle\langle 1,-b\rangle-\langle 1,-c\rangle\langle 1,-d\rangle\in I^{3}\quad\text{for any }a,b,c,d\in G.

The implication from left to right is trivial.
If
\langle 1,-a\rangle\langle 1,-b\rangle-\langle 1,-c\rangle\langle 1,-d\rangle\in I^{3}
, then the Albert form

\varphi:=\langle-a,-b,ab,c,d,-cd\rangle

lies in
I^{3}
.
By
AP_{3}
, it follows that
\varphi
must be hyperbolic, proving the claim.

It remains to prove the linkage relation: For any
a,b,c\in G
, we have

\displaystyle\langle 1,-a\rangle\langle 1,-b\rangle+\langle 1,-a\rangle\langle 1,-c\rangle-\langle 1,-a\rangle\langle 1,-bc\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle 1,-a\rangle\langle 1,-b,-c,bc\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle 1,-a\rangle\langle 1,-b\rangle\langle 1,-c\rangle\in I^{3},

completing the proof.
∎

Definition
.

Let
S
be an
AP_{3}
structure with fundamental ideal
I
.
We then call
I^{2}/I^{3}
the
(abstract) 2-Brauer group
of
S
.

For the reader’s convenience, we recall Marshall’s results from
[

, proof of 5.7, 5.17]
on how the Arason-Pfister property

⁢
A

P

behaves for direct products and group extensions.
Note that the statement for the direct product is only proven there for

but the proof works in the same way for larger

.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
S
and
S^{\prime}
be quaternionic structures and

(a)

If
S
and

both satisfy

, then

.

(b)

If
S

.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
S=(G,-1_{G},q)
and
S^{\prime}=(G^{\prime},-1_{G^{\prime}},q^{\prime})
be
AP_{3}
structures with abstract Brauer groups

and

.
Then

and

are

B_{S\times S^{\prime}}\cong B\times B^{\prime}\quad\text{ and }\quad B_{S\Delta}\cong B\times G.

Proof.

The fact that both
S\times S^{\prime}
and
S\Delta
are
AP_{3}
structures follows directly from
Proposition

3.5
along with the fact that
AP_{1}
and
AP_{2}
hold for any quaternionic structure (cf.

1.3
).
Thus,
B_{S\times S^{\prime}}
and

are well-defined.
We first consider the direct product.
Write
W
and
W^{\prime}
for the Witt rings of
S
and
S^{\prime}
.
The Witt ring of
S\times S^{\prime}
is the subring of
W\times W^{\prime}
consisting of all
(\varphi,\psi)
such that
\dim(\varphi)\equiv_{2}\dim(\psi)
.
Since there are only even dimensional forms in the 2-Brauer groups, it immediately follows that the 2-Brauer group of
S\times S^{\prime}
must be isomorphic to
B\times B^{\prime}
.

Now we move on to the group extension which we henceforth denote as
S[x]=(G\cup Gx,-1,q_{x})
.
We write
I_{x}
for its fundamental ideal.
Note that we can write any
2
-fold Pfister form in
S[x]
as
\langle\!\langle a,b\rangle\!\rangle
or
\langle\!\langle a,bx\rangle\!\rangle
for
a,b\in G
as
\langle\!\langle ax,bx\rangle\!\rangle\cong\langle\!\langle-ab,bx\rangle\!\rangle
.
With the same notation, we have
\langle\!\langle a,b\rangle\!\rangle+\langle\!\langle a,x\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle a,bx\rangle\!\rangle
as elements of
I_{x}^{2}/I_{x}^{3}
, applying the linkage relation.
Comparing with
Proposition

3.2
(b)
,
I_{x}^{2}/I_{x}^{3}
is generated by
H_{1}:=\{\langle\!\langle a,b\rangle\!\rangle\mid a,b\in G\}
and
H_{2}:=\{\langle\!\langle a,x\rangle\!\rangle\mid a\in G\}
.
Again by the linkage relation,
\spn(H_{2})\cong G
.
Since
H_{1}
embeds into the abstract 2-Brauer group of
S
, we clearly get
\spn(H_{1})\cong B
and
\spn(H_{1})\cap\spn(H_{2})=\{0\}
.
As in
Proposition

3.2
(b)
, the assertion follows.
∎

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
S
be an elementary type structure with set of quaternions
Q
and 2-Brauer group
B
.
Then
B\cong\overline{Q}
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

3.2
and
Proposition

3.6
, it suffices to show this if
|Q|\leq 2
.
If
|Q|=1
, then any group of exponent
2
generated by
Q
is trivial, so the assertion follows.
If
|Q|=2
, there is also only one group of exponent
2
generated by
Q
because
Q
is multiplicatively closed (
0\ast 0=0=1\ast 1
,
1\ast 0=1=0\ast 1
), so
B=Q=\overline{Q}
.
∎

4.
Quaternionic Matrices

In the following, we only consider structures
S
of finite order
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
In this section, we establish a unique matrix
M_{S}\in\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
by which we can identify
S
.
This will be the groundwork for our computations later on.
We usually write
S=(G,-1,q)
with set of quaternions
Q
and full
2B
-group
B
.
We call its dimension
\dim(S):=\dim(B)
, which is finite by
Lemma

3.1
(c)
, the
dimension of
S
.

Notation
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we often consider matrices
M=(M_{i,j})_{0\leq i,j\leq n-1}\in\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
where we start counting the indices for rows and columns at
0
.
We bestow the set
\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
with the lexicographical order
<_{\text{lex}}
, applied row by row, and extending the usual order on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
We simply say that a matrix is ’smaller/greater/…’ than another if it is lexicographically smaller/greater/…

For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we take
I_{k}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
k=\sum_{i\in I_{k}}2^{i}
is the binary representation of
k
.
Then the binary operation
\ast_{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
where

k\ast_{\mathbb{N}_{0}}l:=\sum_{i\in I_{k}\triangle I_{l}}2^{i}

defines a group of exponent
2

(\mathbb{N}_{0},\ast_{\mathbb{N}_{0}})
where any set
\{0,1,\ldots,2^{n}-1\}
defines a subgroup with basis
(2^{k}\mid 0\leq k\leq n-1)
.
This is easily verified using linear algebra.
We often simply write
\ast
instead of
\ast_{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and define the
completion of
M
as the matrix

\overline{M}:=(\overline{M}_{k,\ell})_{0\leq k,\ell\leq 2^{n}-1}\in\Mat_{2^{n}}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\quad\text{with}\quad\overline{M}_{k,\ell}=\Ast_{(i,j)\in I_{k}\times I_{\ell}}M_{i,j}.

Note that we usually write
\overline{M}_{I_{k},I_{\ell}}:=\overline{M}_{k,\ell}
, using subsets of
\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
as indices.
For
k=0
or
\ell=0
, the above definition gives an empty product, meaning that the leading row and column of
\overline{M}
consist only of zeros.

We also define
(B(M),\overline{\ast})
as the free group of exponent
2

\langle\overline{M}_{I,J}\mid I,J\subset\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\rangle
with the (linkage) relations

\overline{M}_{I,J}\overline{\ast}\overline{M}_{I,K}=\overline{M}_{I,J\triangle K}

for all
I,J,K\subset\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
.

Definition
.

We say that a basis
\mathcal{A}=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1})
of
G
is a

if either
-1=1
or
a_{0}=-1
.
Given a basis
\mathcal{B}=(q_{0},\ldots,q_{m-1})
of

\phi_{\mathcal{B}}:(B,\ast)\to(\{0,\ldots,2^{m}-1\},\ast)\quad\text{with}\quad q_{k}\mapsto 2^{k}.

Then, we define the

S
via
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
as

M_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}(S):=(\phi_{\mathcal{B}}(q(a_{i},a_{j})))_{0\leq i,j\leq n-1}\in\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{N}_{0}).

If
\mathcal{B}
is fixed, we often identify
(B,\ast)
with
(\{0,\ldots,2^{m}-1\},\ast)
, writing

or – in abuse of notation – simply

instead of
\phi_{\mathcal{B}}(q(a,b))
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1})
be a basis of

\mathcal{B}

Set

.
The following hold:

(a)

(b)

(M1)

(M2)

(M3)

(M4)

(c)

(M0)

For any

Proof.

Write

(a)

(b)

For

Since we have chosen

For

As

(c)

from these quaternions.

.

In the following, we need some technicalities to assure getting a unique representation of

First, we establish how prequaternionic matrices already uniquely define quaternionic structures up to isomorphism.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
M\in\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
be a prequaternionic matrix of type
\epsilon\in\{0,1\}
with completion
\overline{M}
.
Then the following hold:

(a)

\overline{M}
is symmetric.

(b)

If
\epsilon=0
, then
\overline{M}_{I,I}=0
for all
I\subset\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
.

If
\epsilon=1
, then
\overline{M}_{I,I}=\overline{M}_{\{0\},I}
for all
I\subset\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
.

(c)

\overline{M}_{I,J}\ast\overline{M}_{I,K}=\overline{M}_{I,J\triangle K}
for all
I,J,K\subset\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
.

(d)

\overline{M}_{\{i\},\{j\}}=M_{i,j}
for all
i,j\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
.

Proof.

(a)
is an immediate consequence of
(b)(M2)
and the definition of
\overline{M}
.
Now take
I,J,K\subset\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
.
We get
(b)
by

\displaystyle\overline{M}_{I,I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Ast_{(i,j)\in I\times I}M_{i,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Ast_{i\in I}M_{i,i}\ast\Ast_{(i,j)\in I\times I,i<j}\underbrace{(M_{k,l}\ast M_{l,k})}_{\underset{\text{\tiny\ref{M2}}}{=}0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Ast_{i\in I}M_{i,i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Ast_{i\in I}0=0&\text{if }\epsilon=0\\
\Ast_{i\in I}M_{0,i}=\overline{M}_{\{0\},I}&\text{if }\epsilon=1.\end{cases}

In a similar fashion, we get
(c)
by computing

\displaystyle\overline{M}_{I,J}\ast\overline{M}_{I,K}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Ast_{(i,j)\in I\times J}M_{i,j}\ast\Ast_{(i,k)\in I\times K}M_{i,k}

\displaystyle\underset{\text{\tiny group of exponent $2$}}{=}

\displaystyle\Ast_{(i,j)\in I\times(J\triangle K)}M_{i,j}=\overline{M}_{I,J\triangle K}.

Lastly,
(d)
follows directly from the definition.
∎

The following shows that any prequaternionic matrix defines a quaternionic structure from which we can then recover the zeros in the completion of the matrix.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
M\in\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
be a prequaternionic matrix of type
\epsilon\in\{0,1\}
.
Let
G
be a group of exponent
2
with neutral element
1
and basis
\mathcal{A}:=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1})
.
If
\epsilon=0
, choose
-1:=1\in G
, else
-1:=a_{0}
.
Define

as the set of entries of

, naturally containing 0.
Then

defines a quaternionic structure
S(M)=(G,-1,q)
with
s(S)=1
if
\epsilon=0
and
s(S)>1
else.
For any basis
\mathcal{B}
of the full
2B
-group of

and
I,J\subset\{0,\ldots,n-1\}

\overline{M}_{I,J}=0\iff\overline{M_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}(S(M))}_{I,J}=0

Proof.

The property
(Q2)
follows from
Lemma

4.3
(a)
.

(Q1)
follows from
Lemma

4.3
(b)
, in the case

also making use of part

:

q\left(\prod_{i\in I}a_{i},-\prod_{i\in I}a_{i}\right)=\overline{M}_{I,I\triangle\{0\}}=\overline{M}_{I,I}\ast\overline{M}_{I,\{0\}}=0.

(Q3)
is another consequence of

4.3
(c)
, applying the fact that

is a group of exponent

.
Lastly,

follows from

.

The zeros in the completion of a quaternionic matrix, for fixed basis

5.
Normal Quaternionic Matrices

The main result of this section will be determining what the first two rows of normal quaternionic matrices look like.
The structure of the first row is determined quite easily:

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
M=(M_{i,j})_{0\leq i,j\leq n-1}
be a normal quaternionic matrix of type
\epsilon\in\{0,1\}
with quaternionic structure
S:=S(M):=(G,-1,q)
.
Set
x:=\dim(V(-1))
if
-1\not=1\in G
and otherwise
x:=\Max\{\dim(V(a))\mid a\in G\setminus\{1\}\}
.
We write
s:=s(S)
.
Then

(M_{0,0},M_{0,1},\ldots,M_{0,n-1})=\begin{cases}(0,\ldots,0,1,2,4,\ldots,2^{n-x-1}),&\text{ if }s\leq 2\\
(1,0,\ldots,0,2,4,\ldots,2^{n-x-1}),&\text{ else}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1})
be a basis of
S
such that
M_{\mathcal{A}}(S)=M
.
First note that
m_{0,0}=0
if
s\leq 2
(which means that
q(a_{0},a_{0})=0
) and
m_{0,0}=1
else.
By definition of the normal quaternionic matrix,
\mathcal{A}
is chosen such that we get the maximum number of ’leading zeros’, i.e.
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
maximal such that
q(a_{0},a_{i})=0
for all

i\in\begin{cases}\{0,\ldots,x^{\prime}-1\}&\text{if }s\leq 2\\
\{1,\ldots,x^{\prime}\}&\text{if }s>2.\end{cases}

This means that
a_{0},\ldots,a_{x^{\prime}-1}
resp.
a_{1},\ldots,a_{x^{\prime}}
form a basis of
V(a_{0})=\{b\in G\mid q(a_{0},b)=0\}
.
By definition, we get
x^{\prime}=x
.
Define

J=\begin{cases}\{x,x+1,\ldots,n-1\}&\text{if }s\leq 2\\
\{0,x+1,x+2,\ldots,n-1\}&\text{if }s>2.\end{cases}

The values
(q(a_{0},a_{j})\mid j\in J)
must be independent for else there would be a subset
\emptyset\not=I\subset J
such that

0=\Ast_{i\in I}q(a_{0},a_{i})=q(a_{0},\prod_{i\in I}a_{i})

and thus
\prod_{i\in I}a_{i}\in V(a_{0})
, a contradiction.
Hence, the first row of
M
is of the described form.
∎

As an immediate consequence of the above proof, we can specify exactly which bases of
S
produce a minimal first row:

.

Let
M
be a normal quaternionic matrix of order

and type
\epsilon\in\{0,1\}
with quaternionic structure

be any basis of

is identical to that of

if and only if

•

s(S)\leq 2
and

or

•

s(S)>2
and
V(a_{0})=\spn(a_{1},\ldots,a_{x})

We start with the following reduction technique.

To make use of this, we include the following results that show how how to read these properties off normal quaternionic matrices.

Considering

Lemma 6.2

Let

Proof.

The statements about level

and the fact that

If

as seen before and

.
Note that by the correspondence of

By a classical property which also holds in the abstract setting (cf.

Hence, we find

By bimultiplicativity and symmetry of

Conversely, if there are

∎

In the program, partial quaternionic matrices (or what is aimed to be completed to such) are created row by row and via depth search in order to minimize the data that has to be stored simultaneously.
To keep track of the current object, we save our current data in an
n\times n
matrix

whose entries are tuples.
The first entry of the tuple in

A

(i,j)
and the second entry of

We will make use of functions that increase resp. decrease
(i,j)

Quaternionic matrices are saved as a class object, among their attributes are a partial completion, the unique values up to the last computed element and their parameters.
Whenever a transformation is found that produces a smaller reduced matrix, or a violation of the common slot property

or another assumption (in particular: rigidity) is detected, the current class object is discarded.
This will be called

Whenever an object is discarded, the program continues with the last object that has not yet been discarded.
Note that we only consider selected transformations as the exponentially growing number of possible transformations takes up too much computation time to be practical.
As explained before in the proof of

Remark 6.5
.

In this remark, we highlight some computational aspects.

•

As mentioned before, it was not possible to check all applicable transformations during the validity check to assure the minimality of the constructed quaternion matrix a priori.
But since we have a lower bound on the number of abstract Witt rings of a given order by
Remark

2.10
, we could conclude the minimality of the quaternion matrices a posteriori by showing the effectiveness of the lower bound, implying that the computed matrices are in fact normal quaternion matrices.

•

While developing the code, we constantly extended the set of considered transformations by inspecting provisional results of earlier versions and identifying matrices in the output that are not normal quaternionic matrices.
We further considered partial quaternionic matrices appearing as intermediate results to find additional transformations.
By doing so, we established a list of transformations mainly consisting of products of few simple transformations.

•

Instead of applying transformations explicitly, we aimed to use transformations implicitly in order to exclude possible extensions of a given partial matrix as soon as possible, leading to a better performance.
The theoretical background is given in
Lemma

5.4
, mainly in parts
(a)
and
(b)
.
As another example of implicit transformations, we implemented
first row checks
and
second row checks
.
Since the parameters introduced in
Proposition

5.5
are unique, we can check whether permuting the basis element corresponding to the current row to the beginning of the basis would result in another set of parameters yielding a lexicographically smaller matrix.

•

The common slot property
(b)(M4)
can in general be checked only for completed rows.
Nevertheless, we implemented some rules to exclude possible next entries or to discard matrices early.
These work along the lines of
Lemma

5.4
(d)
.

•

The (partial) completion was not saved as a matrix but as a list of dictionaries, in order to check the common slot property
(b)(M4)
efficiently.
We save a dictionary for each row, and the entry for the key
k
in dictionary
i
contains a list of those
j
, for which
\overline{M}_{i,j}=k
.
The common slot check for rows
i,i^{\prime}
then boils down to showing that for each common key
k
in the dictionaries belonging to
i,i^{\prime}
, the corresponding lists have nonempty intersection.

In the following remark we discuss the runtime and our approaches to shorten the runtime, which are based on parallelization techniques.

(e)

While executing the computations for quaternionic structures of order
n=7
, we reached the limits of what is computable in reasonable time.
We identified the following problems:

•

In most of the cases, high powers of 2 led to big additional effort.
This comes from the obvious fact that given a partial quaternionic matrix in which
2^{0},\ldots 2^{k}
occur, the next entry has to be chosen in
\{0,\ldots,2^{k+1}\}
.
Hence introducing the next power of 2 results in doubling the possibilities for all upcoming entries of the matrix.
It would be thus desirable to have stronger conditions on possible next entries, especially with respect to excluding 2-powers in the sense of
Lemma

5.4
.

•

So far, we do not have criteria to identify nonreduced matrices while computing them.
Instead, we excluded them when already having complete matrices, so after the computational effort is already done.
E.g. for

, we computed the following matrices, both of them determining the same quaternionic structure
\mathbb{L}_{1,0}\Delta\times(\mathbb{L}_{1}\times\mathbb{L}_{1,1}\Delta)\Delta_{2}

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0&2&4&8\\

2&2&0&0&2&33&128\\
4&16&0&0&33&4&256\\

Even though the computation time for the nonreduced matrix is less than the time for the reduced matrix (mostly because it consists of smaller entries), it costs a lot of effort which would be desirable to exclude beforehand using a theoretical argument.
These ’copies’ of isomorphic structures also seem to appear in intermediate results in a great number, even if they cannot be extended to a normal quaternionic matrix.
This appears to be a major reason for the extreme computation times in certain cases.

•

From a purely computational point of view, it does not seem to be obvious a priori which parameters (or subcases thereof) need the most runtime.
It appears that the most time consuming parameters usually have large

Criteria to identify these critical parameters and their most time consuming subcases would enable us to apply automated strategies for parallelization of calculations, instead of relying on manual inspections for each case.

Remark 6.7
.

As mentioned before, Carson and Marshall first computationally verified the ETC for order
5
in their 1982 article
[
CM82
]
.
To compute all abstract Witt rings for a given group
G
of exponent
2
, they consider the value groups
V(a)
for
a\in G
.
There are some similarities to the approach presented here, as these value groups can be recovered by considering the zeroes in the rows of the completion
\overline{M}
of a given quaternionic matrix in the following way:
if
a\in G
is associated with row and column
k_{a}
in the completion, the set
V(a)
consists of all
b\in G
such that
\overline{M}_{k_{a},k_{b}}=0
for
b
associated with row and column
k_{b}
.

While we were not able to recreate the computations done by Carson and Marshall due to sparse documentation, it appears that our possibilities for early
validity checks
as described before enhance efficiency.
However, we cannot present any quantitative comparisons with the computation times achieved by Carson and Marshall.
