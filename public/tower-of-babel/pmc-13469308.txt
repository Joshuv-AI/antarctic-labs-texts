Induction of mitochondrial fission and mtDNA leakage via the Smad3/Drp1 axis by PEDV infection activates the TLR9/NF-κB/IL-1β inflammatory pathway
Authors: Hangao Xie, Jinlian Guan, Fengjie Tian, Ting Xiong, Haixia Feng, Fei Xu, Ziqing Zhou, Dongsheng He, Dingxiang Liu, Ruiai Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitochondrial homeostasis is intricately linked to the pathogenesis of many viral infections. The maintenance of mitochondrial homeostasis and normal cellular functions relies heavily on the delicate balance of mitochondrial dynamics. However, the precise impact of porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV) infection on mitochondrial dynamics and subsequent pathological processes is largely unexplored. In this study, through both in vivo and in vitro infections, we have demonstrated that PEDV infection induces mitochondrial fission and leakage of mtDNA by upregulating Drp1 expression, and identified Smad3 as a crucial transcription factor responsible for regulating Drp1 expression. By establishing an inflammatory model using PEDV-infected cells, we have identified Drp1-mediated mtDNA release as an upstream event triggering TLR9/NF-κB pathway activation during PEDV infection. These findings provide novel insights into the relationship between PEDV infection and host inflammatory responses from the perspective of mitochondrial dynamics.

Introduction

Porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV), an alphacoronavirus with a non-segmented, single-stranded positive-sense RNA genome, is the causative agent of porcine epidemic diarrhea (Pensaert and de Bouck, 1978; Cavanagh, 1997). This virus exhibits particular virulence in neonatal piglets, manifesting clinically as acute diarrhea accompanied by extensive intestinal inflammation, severe dehydration, and mortality in severe cases (Jung et al., 2020). The mortality rate in susceptible pig populations can be as high as 90% (Fan et al., 2012). The widespread outbreaks of PEDV pose significant challenges to animal health and impose substantial economic burdens on the pork industry, necessitating urgent and effective control measures (Zhang et al., 2023). Current strategies primarily rely on vaccination and supportive care (Li et al., 2020). However, in the face of continuously evolving viral strains and unprecedented outbreaks, these methods often fall short, calling for a deeper understanding of the viral pathogenesis and host-virus interactions.

Mitochondria, dynamic organelles with a double-membrane structure, perform numerous cellular functions, including energy production, lipid and amino acid metabolism, cell death, and innate immunity (Ni et al., 2015; Adebayo et al., 2021). The balance between mitochondrial fusion and fission is crucial for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and normal cellular activities (Meyer et al., 2017). Various cellular stressors such as viral infection, oxidative stress, nutrient deprivation, and hypoxia can induce structural and morphological alterations in mitochondria (Cervantes-Silva et al., 2021; Elesela and Lukacs, 2021). Substantial evidence implicates dysregulated mitochondrial dynamics in the pathogenesis of numerous diseases including Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, diabetes, cardiovascular disorders, and cancer (Bose and Beal, 2016; Vasquez-Trincado et al., 2016; Rovira-Llopis et al., 2017; Trotta and Chipuk, 2017; Blagov et al., 2022). In recent years, the role of mitochondrial dynamics in coronavirus infection has garnered increasing attention. For example, the ORF-9b protein encoded by SARS-CoV, a betacoronavirus, localizes to the outer mitochondrial membrane and suppresses mitochondrial fusion as well as antiviral immune responses by degrading Drp1 and the MAVS signaling complex (Shi et al., 2014). Infection of cells with MERS-CoV, also a betacoronavirus, has also been shown to induce alterations in the mitochondrial biology (Kohli et al., 2022). Our previous report has highlighted the impact of alphacoronavirus PEDV infection on the mitochondrial function (Xie et al., 2025). In this study, we report that infection with PEDV causes abnormalities in mitochondrial dynamics, and plays essential roles in regulating the pathogenesis of PEDV.

Mitochondria may also serve as central hubs for inflammatory immune responses (Poor and Chandel, 2023). As mitochondria may originate from ancient α-proteobacteria based on the endosymbiotic theory, many mitochondrial components, including mtDNA, mtRNA, and mitochondrial proteins, would be recognized by the host immune system as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) (Wang and Wu, 2015; Roger et al., 2017; Marchi et al., 2023). Mitochondrial dynamics changes are closely associated with the distribution of mtDNA in cells (Chen et al., 2010; Ishihara et al., 2015; Bao et al., 2019), and leakage of mtDNA to the cytoplasm may activate the pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as TLR9 and cGAS, thereby inducing pro-inflammatory cascades (Riley and Tait, 2020). Recent studies support that abnormal distribution of mtDNA is an important link between mitochondrial dynamics and inflammation (Irazoki et al., 2023), but similar mechanisms in viral infections to trigger inflammatory responses are yet to be revealed. In this study, we report the impact of PEDV infection on mitochondrial dynamics. Upon PEDV infection, the transcription factor Smad3 upregulates Drp1 expression and activates its function, leading to mitochondrial fission and mtDNA leakage. The leaked mtDNA activates the TLR9/NF-κB/IL-1β inflammatory pathway. These findings reveal a new regulatory mechanism through which PEDV infection triggers host cell inflammatory responses.

Results

PEDV infection induces mitochondrial fission and mtDNA leakage

Our previous studies have demonstrated that PEDV infection contributes to mitochondrial damage in porcine jejunal epithelial cells. To further investigate the effects of PEDV infection on mitochondrial dynamics, 3-day-old specific-pathogen-free piglets were orally inoculated with 2 mL of 106.5 TCID50 PEDV, and jejunal epithelial cells were isolated 3 days post-infection. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed normal, elongated tubular mitochondria in the jejunal epithelial cells of control piglets, while cells from PEDV-infected piglets exhibited smaller, fragmented mitochondria (Fig. 1A). Further analysis of mitochondrial morphology and average length was conducted via MitoTracker staining and confocal laser scanning microscopy. Compared to controls, PEDV infection significantly increased mitochondrial fragmentation in the jejunal epithelial cells, reducing the average mitochondrial length from 1.27 μm to 0.56 μm (Fig. 1B and C). In IPEC-J2 cells infected with PEDV at an MOI of 1 for 24 or 48 h, mitochondrial fragmentation was significantly increased. The average length of mitochondria decreased from 1.39 μm to 0.55 μm at 24 h and further decreased to 0.38 μm at 48 h post-infection (Fig. 1D–F). The abnormal fission of mitochondria can compromise the integrity of the mitochondrial membrane, resulting in the release of various mitochondrial components such as proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, enzymes, and cofactors into the cytosol. To investigate whether PEDV infection triggers the leakage of mtDNA, we examined total mtDNA levels in jejunal epithelial cells collected from both in vivo and in vitro PEDV infections. Cytosolic fractions were isolated and confirmed for efficient separation (Supplementary Fig. S1). Subsequently, cytosolic mtDNA levels and the ratio of cytosolic mtDNA to total mtDNA were analyzed, showing no significant alteration at total mtDNA levels in jejunal epithelial cells (Fig. 1G), but a 6.01-fold increase in the cytosolic mtDNA levels along with a corresponding 5.74-fold increase in the cytosolic mtDNA/total mtDNA ratio (Fig. 1H and I). Similarly, no notable change in total mtDNA levels was detected in IPEC-J2 cells at 24 and 48 h post-infection (Fig. 1J), a respective 3.74-fold and 3.38-fold increase was observed in the cytosolic mtDNA levels leading to proportional increases in the cytosolic mtDNA/total DNA ratio (Fig. 1K and L). These findings strongly suggest that PEDV infection induces the leakage of mtDNA into the cytosol.

PEDV infection regulates the expression of genes related to mitochondrial dynamics

Mitochondrial dynamics involve the processes of fission and fusion, primarily regulated by three key mitochondrial fusion-related genes (Mitofusin 1 (Mfn1), Mitofusin 2 (Mfn2), and optic atrophy 1 (Opa1)) and one mitochondrial fission-related gene (Drp1). We further investigated the mRNA and protein expression levels of these four genes following PEDV infection. In jejunal epithelial cells isolated from in vivo PEDV-infected piglets, both the mRNA and protein levels of Mfn1 and Mfn2 were decreased, while the mRNA and protein levels of Opa1 remained largely unchanged (Fig. 2A–C). In contrast, both the mRNA and protein levels of Drp1 were significantly increased, and p-Drp1 (S616) protein levels were also elevated (Fig. 2A–C). Similarly, in PEDV-infected IPEC-J2 cells at 24 and 48 h post-infection, a reduction in both the mRNA and protein expression of Mfn1 and Mfn2 was detected (Fig. 2D–F). The mRNA levels of Opa1 decreased only at 24 h post PEDV infection, whereas its protein levels remained unchanged at both 24 and 48 h post-infection (Fig. 2D–F). Conversely, both the mRNA and protein expression of Drp1 were significantly upregulated, and p-Drp1 (S616) protein levels were also increased (Fig. 2D–F). Drp1 mediates mitochondrial fission and mtDNA leakage dependent on mPTP during PEDV infection

Drp1 plays a crucial role in regulating the process of mitochondrial fission. In order to investigate whether the increased expression of Drp1 during PEDV infection is the primary factor driving mitochondrial fission, we initially transfected IPEC-J2 cells with either pCAGGS-Drp1 or si-Drp1 to validate the overexpression and knockdown of Drp1 (Fig. 3A and B). Subsequently, these cells were transfected with pCAGGS-Drp1 or si-Drp1 and infected with PEDV (MOI = 1). At 24 h post-infection, confocal microscopy was used to assess the mitochondrial morphology and average length. The results demonstrated that PEDV infection alone significantly induced mitochondrial fragmentation, leading to a decrease in average mitochondrial length from 1.54 μm to 0.62 μm (Fig. 3C and D). Furthermore, PEDV infection of IPEC-J2 cells overexpressing Drp1 led to an additional enhancement in the mitochondrial fragmentation, resulting in a further reduction of the average mitochondrial length from 0.62 μm to 0.41 μm (Fig. 3C and D). Conversely, in Drp1-knockdown IPEC-J2 cells infected with PEDV, an attenuation of the mitochondrial fragmentation was observed as indicated by an increase in the average mitochondrial length from 0.62 μm to 1.12 μm (Fig. 3C and D). Given the potential association between PEDV-induced mtDNA leakage and mitochondrial fission, we conducted further investigations to determine the involvement of Drp1 in mtDNA release during PEDV infection of Drp1-overexpressed and knockdown IPEC-J2 cells, respectively, by measuring the levels of total and cytosolic mtDNA at 24 h post-infection. Total cellular mtDNA levels were not significantly affected in cells infected with PEDV alone or co-transfection with either pCAGGS-Drp1 or si-Drp1 (Fig. 3E). However, PEDV infection alone led to a substantial increase in the cytosolic mtDNA levels as well as the ratio of cytosolic mtDNA to total mtDNA (Fig. 3F and G). Overexpression of Drp1 further elevated the cytosolic mtDNA levels and the cytosolic mtDNA/total mtDNA ratio, while knockdown of Drp1 significantly reduced the PEDV-induced increase in both parameters (Fig. 3F and G). These findings confirm that Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission plays a role in promoting mtDNA leakage during PEDV infection.

Furthermore, we sought to investigate whether the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) or voltage-dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1) channel is involved in Drp1-mediated mtDNA leakage during PEDV infection. In PEDV-infected IPEC-J2 cells with or without Drp1 overexpression, we assessed the effects of mPTP inhibitor cyclosporine A (CsA) and VDAC1 oligomerization inhibitor VBIT-4 on mtDNA leakage by qPCR analysis. The efficient overexpression of Drp1 was confirmed by Western blotting (Supplementary Fig. S2A and B). Both CsA and VBIT-4 were shown to significantly inhibit mitochondrial DNA leakage induced solely by PEDV infection, with CsA exhibiting a more pronounced inhibitory effect (Supplementary Fig. S2C–E). Drp1 overexpression markedly enhanced PEDV-induced mtDNA leakage, and this enhancement was significantly blocked by CsA treatment, whereas VBIT-4 had no significant effect (Supplementary Fig. S2C–E). These findings suggest that Drp1-mediated mtDNA leakage during PEDV infection is dependent on mPTP.

PEDV infection regulates Drp1 expression through Smad3, leading to mitochondrial fission and mtDNA leakage

To investigate the upstream regulators of Drp1 upregulation during PEDV infection, 13 potential transcription factors that regulate Drp1 expression were identified through bioinformatic analysis (Fig. 4A) (Supplementary Table S2). As Drp1 and p-Drp1 (S616) upregulation was also shown to potentially mediate the mitochondrial fission and mtDNA leakage in PEDV-infected LLC-PK1 cells (Supplementary Fig. S3A–H). PEDV-infected IPEC-J2 and LLC-PK1 cells were used to validate the expression of these regulators by qPCR analysis, showing that both Hic1 and Smad3 were upregulated (Fig. 4B). After confirming the knockdown efficiency of si-Hic1 and si-Smad3 in these cells (Fig. 4C), we then assessed the effect of their knockdown on the Drp1 mRNA levels in IPEC-J2 and LLC-PK1 cells infected with PEDV. Results showed that Smad3 knockdown significantly inhibited the PEDV infection-induced Drp1 mRNA upregulation while Hic1 knockdown rendered no significant effect (Fig. 4D). Smad3 knockdown in IPEC-J2 and LLC-PK1 cells significantly suppressed the PEDV-induced upregulation of Drp1 mRNA and protein levels, as well as p-Drp1 (S616) level (Supplementary Fig. S4A–C), suggesting that PEDV infection may regulate the total Drp1 protein level through Smad3, thereby affecting the phosphorylation level of Drp1 (S616). Moreover, Smad3 knockdown weakened the PEDV-induced mtDNA leakage, while Drp1 overexpression reversed this phenomenon (Supplementary Fig. S4D–I). Further investigation was conducted to identify potential Smad3 binding sites within the Drp1 promoter region. Motif analysis revealed the presence of three potential Smad3 binding sites located at −1510 to −1499 bp, −782 to −771 and −127 to −116 bp in the Drp1 promoter (Fig. 4E and F). In the CHIP assay, primers targeting three distinct binding sites were designed for subsequent qPCR analysis. The results indicated that Smad3 binds specifically to the Drp1 promoter fragment containing Smad3 binding site 1, while showing no binding toward the Drp1 promoter fragments containing binding sites 2 or 3 (Fig. 4G and H). Site-directed mutagenesis was performed on these sites individually, and the mutated promoters were cloned into the pGL3 reporter vector. Luciferase assays demonstrated that Smad3 significantly increased the luciferase activity carrying the wild-type Drp1 promoter (Fig. 4I and J). Mutation of the first Smad3 binding site significantly reduced the luciferase activity, while mutation of the second and third sites had no significant effect (Fig. 4I and J). Additionally, ChIP-qPCR was performed to compare the binding of Smad3 to the Drp1 promoter between uninfected IPEC-J2 cells and those infected with PEDV. The results revealed that PEDV infection significantly enhanced Smad3 enrichment at the previously identified binding site 1 (Supplementary Fig. S5A and B). These findings suggest that during PEDV infection, Smad3 regulates the expression of Drp1 by binding to the −1510 to −1499 bp region of the Drp1 promoter, thereby inducing mitochondrial fission and mtDNA leakage.

PEDV regulates inflammation through Drp1

Changes in the mitochondrial dynamics are closely associated with inflammatory responses, and PEDV infection often triggers excessive inflammation. To investigate the role of Drp1 in the inflammatory response during PEDV infection, we established two inflammation models by infecting IPEC-J2 and LLC-PK1 cells with PEDV (MOI = 1) in vitro. At 24 and 48 h of PEDV infection, both pro-IL-1β mRNA and protein levels were significantly upregulated in IPEC-J2 and LLC-PK1 cells, accompanied by a corresponding increase in IL-1β levels in the culture supernatant (Fig. 5A–C). Subsequently, we transfected pCAGGS-Drp1 and si-Drp1 into PEDV-infected IPEC-J2 and LLC-PK1 cells. After 24 h, we collected cells and supernatants to measure the intracellular pro-IL-1β mRNA levels, protein levels, as well as IL-1β protein levels in the supernatant. Our results indicated that PEDV infection alone resulted in elevated intracellular pro-IL-1β mRNA and protein levels along with increased supernatant IL-1β levels in both cell types (Fig. 5D–K). Overexpression of Drp1 during PEDV infection further enhanced the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines including pro-IL-1β mRNA and protein as well as secreted IL-1β level while knockdown of Drp1 led to a reduction thereof (Fig. 5D–K). Furthermore, in these experiments, neither Drp1 overexpression nor knockdown significantly affected the expression level of PEDV N protein (Fig. 5E and I). Discussion

Mitochondrial dynamics, governed by fusion mediators (Mfn1, Mfn2, Opa1) and the fission regulator Drp1, plays a critical role in cellular homeostasis and innate immunity (Scott and Youle, 2010; Tilokani et al., 2018). Disruption of this balance is increasingly recognized as a key event in viral pathogenesis. Dengue virus (DENV) targets Mfn1 and Mfn2 via the NS2B3 protease, shifting the mitochondrial dynamics toward fission, thus hindering the antiviral signaling of the RLR-MAVS pathway to facilitate DENV infection (Yu et al., 2015). The ORF-9b protein encoded by SARS-CoV localizes to the mitochondrial outer membrane, degrading Drp1 and MAVS/TRAF3/TRAF6 signaling proteins, leading to the inhibition of mitochondrial fusion and antiviral immune responses (Shi et al., 2014). However, whether such manipulation of mitochondrial dynamics represents a common viral strategy remains poorly understood. Our observations that PEDV infection induces and regulates mitochondrial fission by upregulating Drp1 expression and phosphorylation add one more example to the list and reveal a novel mechanistic insight into this process.

Abnormal changes in the balance between mitochondrial fusion and fission are closely linked to the leakage of mitochondrial contents. Drp1-induced mitochondrial fission leads to the oligomerization of Bax, disrupting the integrity of the mitochondrial outer membrane (OM) and resulting in cytochrome c release (Montessuit et al., 2010). Some studies have reported that during apoptosis, Drp1-induced mitochondrial fission is essential for the release of Smac/Diablo and cytochrome c (Ishihara et al., 2009). However, changes in the permeability of the mitochondrial outer membrane appear to be independent of Drp1 activation (Ishihara et al., 2009). Recent research has shown that alterations in mitochondrial dynamics can also affect mtDNA leakage. In HCC cells overexpressing Drp1, enhanced mitochondrial fission disrupts mitochondrial nucleoid structures and induces mtDNA release into the cytosol, resulting in cytosolic mtDNA stress (Bao et al., 2019). Subsequent studies reported that Drp1-induced mitochondrial fission regulates mtDNA release through the opening of mPTP and VDAC1 channels (Li et al., 2022). Interestingly, both Drp1 or Fis1 inhibition-induced mitochondrial fusion also leads to the release of mtDNA into the cytosol (Irazoki et al., 2023). A reasonable hypothesis is that the disruption of mitochondrial dynamics homeostasis, whether through mitochondrial fission or fusion, causes dramatic changes in the mitochondrial membrane, leading to mitochondrial membrane permeability damage and ultimately inducing mtDNA leakage. Our in vitro and in vivo results indicate that PEDV infection also induces mtDNA leakage. Further research revealed that PEDV infection regulates mtDNA leakage via Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission.

By combining modulation of Drp1 expression with pharmacological inhibition of candidate channels, we identified mPTP, rather than VDAC1, as the key effector of Drp1-mediated mtDNA leakage during PEDV infection. Notably, although the VDAC1 oligomerization inhibitor VBIT-4 partially suppressed mtDNA leakage induced by PEDV infection alone, it failed to exert any significant effect in the context of Drp1 overexpression. These findings suggest the existence of two potential mechanisms underlying mtDNA leakage: a basal, VDAC1-dependent pathway that may operate during the early stages of infection or under low-intensity stress, and a Drp1-driven, mPTP-dependent pathway that is activated under conditions of exacerbated mitochondrial fission. This model is consistent with recent studies indicating that VDAC1 and mPTP can individually or cooperatively mediate the release of mitochondrial contents under distinct pathological conditions (Tomasello et al., 2009; Gao et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2023).

Smad3, a member of the SMAD family, consists of two highly conserved domains, MH1 and MH2 (Attisano and Lee-Hoeflich, 2001). The MH1 domain is responsible for DNA binding, while the MH2 domain participates in nuclear localization and transcriptional regulation (Hata et al., 1997; Kuang and Chen, 2004). Our study demonstrates that PEDV infection upregulates Drp1 expression via Smad3. Given the facts that Smad3 is a key transcription factor in regulation of the pathogenesis of fibrosis, tumorigenesis and inflammation (Wu et al., 2022), and that abnormal Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission is common in these diseases (Trotta and Chipuk, 2017; Blagov et al., 2022), the Smad3/Drp1 axis may represent a conserved stress-response mechanism. Whether this axis operates in other pathological contexts, including TGF-β-induced fibrosis, cancer metabolic reprogramming and other viral infections, warrants future investigation.

Smad3 is typically activated by TGF-β ligands through C-terminal phosphorylation (Wang et al., 2009); however, non-canonical pathways, such as the MAPK and JNK signaling cascades, can also modulate its activity (Engel et al., 1999; Javelaud and Mauviel, 2005). Notably, recent studies have shown that the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N) protein interacts with the TGF-β type I receptor (TβRI), thereby promoting Smad3 phosphorylation, suggesting that viral components may be indirectly involved in Smad3 activation (Zhang et al., 2023). Whether PEDV infection activates Smad3 through TGF-β-dependent or -independent mechanisms, and whether viral proteins participate directly or indirectly in this process, remain important questions to be addressed. We anticipate that our identification of Smad3 as a Drp1 regulator would stimulate further investigation into these upstream events.

Released mtDNA serves as a potent inflammatory trigger by engaging pattern recognition receptors such as TLR9 and cGAS (Zhang et al., 2010; Riley and Tait, 2020). While both pathways can activate NF-κB, their relative contributions are context-dependent (Newman and Shadel, 2023). Depending on the physiological or pathological context, mtDNA can activate one or more innate immune sensors (Newman and Shadel, 2023). Recent studies have shown that PEDV infection-induced mtDNA leakage can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to the maturation and secretion of IL-1β (Bao et al., 2024). Our discovery that PEDV infection regulates inflammatory responses through Drp1-mediated mtDNA leakage and the released mtDNA interacts with TLR9 rather than cGAS, leading to the activation of NF-κB and induction of inflammatory responses adds a new mechanistic insight into the PEDV infection-induced inflammation.

Furthermore, a noteworthy finding is that modulation of Drp1, TLR9, or cGAS did not significantly affect PEDV replication, as reflected by comparable viral N protein expression levels (Fig. 5, Fig. 7D). Although pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β typically suppress viral replication, this apparent paradox can be explained by three considerations. First, our experiments were conducted within a single viral replication cycle, during which the magnitude and kinetics of IL-1β production may be insufficient to establish an effective antiviral state. Secondly, as PEDV N is an abundant viral structural protein produced during viral replication cycle, subtle variations in its absolute quantity would be difficult to be detected by Western blotting, as used in this study. Moreover, PEDV encodes immune-evasion proteins, such as 3CLpro and PLP2 that antagonize host innate immune responses, potentially uncoupling IL-1β signaling from direct antiviral effects. Thus, viral entry and replication proceed through pathways largely independent of Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission or TLR9/cGAS signaling.

NF-κB is a central transcription factor in inflammatory signaling. Some reports also suggest that there is a complex regulatory relationship between NF-κB and mitochondrial dynamics. Chen et al. found that after weakening Drp1-induced mitochondrial fission, NF-κB activity was significantly suppressed, slowing colorectal cancer progression (Chen et al., 2019). Similarly, in HCC cells with DNM1L knockdown or MFN1 overexpression, mitochondrial fission activates NF-κB and TP53 pathways through AKT, affecting apoptosis (Huang et al., 2016). On the other hand, NF-κB activity also regulates the mitochondrial dynamics. It has been reported that NF-κB activation is a necessary step for mitochondrial fragmentation in muscle cells induced by nutrient overload (Nisr et al., 2019). Additionally, CRH-dependent NF-κB mediates Drp1 activity to alter mitochondrial morphology and function (Battaglia et al., 2020). In cardiomyocytes, insulin stimulation controls Opa1 expression through the Akt-mTOR-NFκB signaling pathway, influencing mitochondrial fusion (Parra et al., 2014). The signaling mechanisms governing the regulatory relationship between NF-κB activity and mitochondrial dynamics may depend on specific cellular stress conditions. In this study, our data show that during PEDV infection, the activation of NF-κB is a downstream event of Drp1-mediated mtDNA leakage. Moreover, PEDV infection activates the TLR9/NF-κB/IL-1β inflammatory pathway via Drp1-mediated mtDNA leakage.

In summary, we found that PEDV infection induces mitochondrial fission and mtDNA leakage, a process regulated by the Smad3/Drp1 axis. Furthermore, during PEDV infection, Drp1-mediated mtDNA leakage activates the TLR9/NF-κB pathway, triggering inflammatory responses (Fig. 9). These findings suggest that Smad3/Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission is a new target for modulating inflammation in PEDV infection. Future in vivo studies using Drp1 inhibitors in animal models will be necessary to validate the therapeutic potential of this pathway.Fig. 9Schematic diagram of PEDV infection participating in inflammatory response by regulating mitochondrial dynamics. CONCLUTIONS

In conclusion, PEDV infection upregulates Drp1 via Smad3, inducing mitochondrial fission and mPTP-dependent mtDNA leakage. The leaked mtDNA activates the TLR9/NF-κB/IL-1β pathway, with Drp1-mediated mtDNA release acting upstream of the NF-κB activation. These findings define a novel Smad3/Drp1/mtDNA/TLR9/NF-κB/IL-1β axis in PEDV pathogenesis, underscoring mitochondrial dynamics as a potential therapeutic target for alleviation of PEDV-induced intestinal inflammation.

Materials and methods

Animal experiments

The ten 3-day-old Large White piglets, which were confirmed to be negative for porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus, pseudorabies virus, and porcine epidemic diarrhea virus, were randomly divided into two groups. The piglets in the challenge group were orally administered 2 mL of PEDV with a titer of 106.5 TCID50, while the piglets in the control group received an equal volume of 2 mL of cell culture medium. Three days after infection, the piglets were euthanized and jejunal tissues were collected for analysis. Ethical oversight for all experimental procedures was provided by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of South China Agricultural University, with all work conducted in accordance with the national Animal Ethics Procedures and Guidelines of the People's Republic of China.

Cell culture and virus preparation

The IPEC-J2 (AC338215, ATCC) and LLC-PK1 cells (AC100549, ATCC), as well as jejunal epithelial cells isolated from piglet jejunal tissue, were cultured in Dulbecco modified Eagle medium (DMEM, C11995500BT, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10099141C, Gibco). The cultures were maintained in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. The PEDV strains used in this study were field isolates previously obtained and preserved in our laboratory.

Isolation of porcine jejunum epithelial cells

The porcine jejunum epithelial cells were isolated using a modified protocol based on a previously published method (Bekebrede et al., 2021). Firstly, the intestines were flushed with modified Krebs-Henseleit buffer (KHB) and inverted to enhance exchange between the buffer and mucosa. Subsequently, the inverted sacs were incubated in a Ca2+-free KHB buffer containing 20 mM EDTA and 10 mM DTT for 20 min at 37 °C. After this initial wash, the sacs were reverted and filled with an isolation buffer consisting of Ca2+-free KHB, 2.5 g/L BSA, and 400 U/mL hyaluronidase type IV. Following a 15-min incubation period, gentle massaging of the intestines was performed to release the cells which were then collected, washed, and counted for further analysis. To enhance mucus removal efficiency, an additional wash step lasting for 20 min was included prior to enzymatic digestion.

Subcellular fractionation

The cells were divided into two equal parts following appropriate preparation procedures. One portion of the cells was used as a control for the total cellular component. The other portion underwent cytoplasmic and mitochondrial fractionation using the Mitochondria Isolation Kit (C3601, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the cells were placed on ice in a mitochondrial isolation buffer containing PMSF for 15 min, followed by thorough homogenization with a Dounce homogenizer. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 600 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected and further centrifuged at 11,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Finally, the cytoplasmic and mitochondrial fractions were represented by the final supernatant and pellet respectively.

Transmission electron microscopy

The cells were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS for 30 min, followed by collection and overnight preservation in fixative. Subsequently, the cells underwent dehydration using a graded ethanol series and were embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were prepared and examined utilizing a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1400Flash, JEOL).

Immunofluorescence and mitochondrial morphological analysis

The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.4% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. Subsequently, the cells were incubated overnight at a temperature of 4 °C with rabbit anti-PEDV N protein antibody (PAB01607, KA&M) and anti-mitochondrial TOM20 antibody (Z28704, Beijing BioLab Technology Co., Ltd). Following this step, the cells were incubated with FITC- or Cy3-conjugated secondary antibodies against rabbit immunoglobulins for a duration of 1 h. After DAPI staining was performed, images were captured using a laser confocal microscope (Leica, TCS-SP8). Mitochondrial morphology analysis was conducted utilizing the MiNA-Analyze Morphology plugin in Fiji ImageJ software (NIH, USA) to determine the average length of mitochondria (n = 30 cells).

Cell transfection

The porcine Drp1 gene sequence was acquired from the NCBI database and synthesized by Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. (China). Subsequently, it was cloned into the pCAGGS eukaryotic expression vector. Additionally, siRNAs targeting Drp1, Smad1, TLR9, and cGAS were also synthesized by the same company along with a negative control siRNA (si-NC). Transfections of either plasmids or siRNAs into cells were conducted using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, 11668-019) following the manufacturer's instructions.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (CHIP)

The IPEC-J2 or LLC-PK1 cells were transfected with pCAGGS-Smad3 for a duration of 24 h. Subsequently, the ChIP Assay Kit (P2078, Beyotime) was employed to perform chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP), following the manufacturer's protocol. In brief, the cells were cross-linked using 1% formaldehyde and then quenched with Glycine Solution (10X). The sonicated cell lysates were subjected to immunoprecipitation utilizing an anti-Smad3 antibody (ab216964, Abcam). Finally, the extracted precipitated DNA was analyzed through qPCR.

Luciferase gene reporter assay

The 293T cells were co-transfected with vectors containing either the wild-type Smad3 binding site in the Drp1 promoter (pGL3-Drp1-WT) or mutated binding sites (pGL3-Drp1-Mut1, pGL3-Drp1-Mut2, and pGL3-Drp1-Mut1,2), along with either the control vector (pCAGGS-vector) or the Smad3 expression vector (pCAGGS-Smad3). After a 24-h incubation period, the cells were harvested and luciferase activity was quantified following the manufacturer's instructions.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The supernatants were collected from the treated cells, and the concentration of IL-1β was determined using the Porcine IL-1β ELISA Kit (PI307, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

mtDNA depletion

The IPEC-J2 cells were exposed to 500 ng/mL of ethidium bromide (EtBr) for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. Subsequently, the cells were harvested at each time point and subjected to total DNA and cytosolic DNA extraction. The levels of mtDNA were then quantified using qPCR.

Immunoblot and immunoprecipitation analysis

The IPEC-J2 cells were pretreated with 10 μM BrdU for 48 h. Immunoprecipitation was performed using the Immunoprecipitation Kit with Protein G Magnetic Beads (P2177 M, Beyotime), following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with Protein G magnetic beads pre-bound with either TLR9 or cGAS antibodies. After washing the beads, SDS-PAGE Sample Loading Buffer was added, and samples were heated for 5 min to elute bound components. The eluates were blotted onto an NC membrane using UV cross-linking, followed by immunoblotting with BrdU antibodies. The corresponding eluates were also subjected to immunoblotting with TLR9 or cGAS antibodies. Protein quantification of TLR9 or cGAS in the eluates was conducted using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad, 5000201), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Based on the protein concentrations of TLR9 or cGAS, 10 ng of EGFP-N1 plasmid DNA per mg of protein was added to the eluates. DNA was then extracted from the eluates, and mtDNA levels were quantified by qPCR, normalized to EGFP.

Visualization of mtDNA and TLR9 or cGAS

To investigate the colocalization of mtDNA with TLR9 or cGAS following PEDV infection, IPEC cells were preincubated with 10 μM EdU for 24 h and subsequently infected with PEDV (MOI = 1) for an additional 24 h. Successful PEDV infection was confirmed by quantifying the viral genome levels in the cell supernatant using qPCR. Subsequently, cells were subjected to staining using the Click-iT™ EdU Alexa Fluor™ 647 Assay Kit (C10419, Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Immunofluorescence staining was performed utilizing TLR9 or cGAS antibodies, and colocalization analysis was conducted employing a laser confocal microscope (Leica, TCS-SP8).

p65 nuclear translocation assay

The nuclear translocation of p65 in cells was evaluated through immunofluorescence analysis. Briefly, cells were incubated with a primary antibody against p65, followed by staining with a Cy3-conjugated secondary antibody and DAPI. Subsequently, the subcellular localization of p65 was visualized using a laser confocal microscope (Leica, TCS-SP8).

qPCR analysis

The total RNA was extracted using the Trizol reagent (Solarbio, 15596026) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Subsequently, cDNA synthesis was performed utilizing the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara, RR036A), followed by qPCR analysis employing SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, RR820A). The relative mRNA levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized against the housekeeping gene GAPDH. A comprehensive list of all primers used can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

The experiments were conducted in triplicate (biological replicates). The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA with GraphPad Prism Software (version 9.5, GraphPad Software Inc.).
