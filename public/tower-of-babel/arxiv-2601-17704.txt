A variant of Tingley’s problem for positive unit spheres of continuous functions that vanish at infinity
Kazuki Ezumi, Min-Ruei Lin, Takeshi Miura

Abstract
. Let S ​ ( C 0 ​ ( X ) ) + S(C_{0}(X))^{+} and S ​ ( C 0 ​ ( Y ) ) + S(C_{0}(Y))^{+} denote the positive parts of the unit spheres of C 0 ​ ( X ) C_{0}(X) and C 0 ​ ( Y ) C_{0}(Y) , where X X and Y Y are locally compact Hausdorff spaces. We prove that every surjective isometry from S ​ ( C 0 ​ ( X ) ) + S(C_{0}(X))^{+} onto S ​ ( C 0 ​ ( Y ) ) + S(C_{0}(Y))^{+} is a composition operator induced by a homeomorphism between X X and Y Y . As a consequence, such a map extends to a surjective real-linear isometry from C 0 ​ ( X ) C_{0}(X) onto C 0 ​ ( Y ) C_{0}(Y) . We also characterize surjective phase-isometries on the positive unit sphere.



1.
Introduction

Let
E
and
F
be Banach spaces
with unit spheres
S(E)
and
S(F)
, respectively.
In 1987, Tingley
[
14
]
asked whether
every surjective isometry between
S(E)
and
S(F)

extends to a surjective isometry between
the entire spaces
E
and
F
.
This problem, now known as Tingley’s problem,
has attracted considerable attention
in the context of function spaces
(see, for example,

[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
8
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
).

When
E
is an ordered Banach space, we denote

S(E)^{+}=\{a\in S(E):a\geq 0\},

and call
S(E)^{+}
the
positive unit sphere

of
E
.
Peralta
[
12
]
proposed a variant of
Tingley’s problem for positive unit spheres
of certain Banach spaces.
Later, Leung, Ng and Wong
[
10
]

generalized Peralta’s question as follows.

Problem
.

Let
\Phi\colon S(E)^{+}\to S(F)^{+}
be a
surjective isometry between the positive
unit spheres of ordered Banach spaces

E
and
F
, that is,

\|\Phi(a)-\Phi(b)\|=\|a-b\|\qquad(a,b\in S(E)^{+}).

Does
\Phi
extend to a surjective
real-linear isometry between
the entire spaces
E
and
F
?

Leung, Ng and Wong
[
10
]
solved
this problem affirmatively for
L^{p}
spaces
with
1\leq p\leq\infty
, as well as for

C(K)
spaces of all continuous functions
on a compact Hausdorff space
K
, in which the authors assume the algebras are unital.
In the argument for
C(K)
, they use the constant function
\mathds{1}
to construct strictly positive functions.
However, in
C_{0}(X)
with
X
locally compact Hausdorff but not compact, the absence of
\mathds{1}
prevents a direct adaptation of their argument.

In this paper, we prove that the problem also has a positive answer for the non-unital case, that is, when
E=C_{0}(X)
and
F=C_{0}(Y)
, the spaces of continuous real-valued functions on locally compact Hausdorff spaces
X
and
Y
that vanish at infinity, respectively.
More precisely, we show that any such isometry
\Phi
is represented as a composition operator induced by a homeomorphism
\sigma
between the underlying spaces
X,Y
.

Our proof consists of several steps.
The main task is to define a suitable map

\sigma
between the underlying spaces.
We first establish that inclusions of
maximum sets are preserved by
\Phi
,
and then show that a family of peak sets
has a non-empty intersection.
This property enables us to construct

\sigma
and to verify that
\Phi
is indeed
a composition operator induced by
\sigma
.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we collect
basic notation and definitions
that will be used throughout the paper.
Section
3
states the main theorem
and provides its proof, together with
the necessary lemmas.
Section
4
contains an application
to surjective phase-isometries
on the positive unit sphere.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
C_{0}(K)
be the real Banach space
of all continuous real-valued functions
on a locally compact Hausdorff
space
K
that vanish at infinity,
equipped with the supremum norm

\|{\cdot}\|_{\infty}
.
We denote

S(C_{0}(K))^{+}=\left\{f\in C_{0}(K):\|{f}\|_{\infty}=1,f\geq 0\right\},

the positive part of the unit sphere of
C_{0}(K)
, and call it
the positive unit sphere of
C_{0}(K)
.
For each
f\in S(C_{0}(K))^{+}
, we set

M_{K}({f}):=\left\{t\in K:f(t)=1\right\},\qquad Z_{K}({f}):=\left\{t\in K:f(t)=0\right\},

which we call the maximum set and zero set
of
f
, respectively.
Following Leung, Ng and Wong
[
10
]
, we define for any
f\in S(C_{0}(K))^{+}
the set

D_{K}(f):=\left\{h\in S(C_{0}(K))^{+}:\|{h-f}\|_{\infty}<1\right\}.

This set will play a key role in our analysis,
and will be studied further
in Lemma
3.1
.
Note that
D_{K}(f)\neq\emptyset
,
as
f\in D_{K}(f)
.

3.
Main results

This section states and proves
the main result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be
locally compact Hausdorff spaces.
Let
\Phi\colon S(C_{0}(X))^{+}\to S(C_{0}(Y))^{+}
be a
surjective isometry between the positive unit spheres of
C_{0}(X)
and
C_{0}(Y)
.
Then
there exists a homeomorphism

\sigma\colon Y\to X
such that

\Phi(f)=f\circ\sigma
for all
f\in S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\Phi\colon S(C_{0}(X))^{+}\to S(C_{0}(Y))^{+}
be
a surjective isometry.
Then
\Phi
extends uniquely
to a surjective real-linear isometry
from
C_{0}(X)
onto
C_{0}(Y)
.

As a first step, we state a basic criterion
for membership in
D_{K}(f)
,
which will be used repeatedly in what follows.
Although the proof is straightforward,
we give it for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f,h\in S(C_{0}(K))^{+}
.
Then
h\in D_{K}(f)
if and only if
the following equalities hold:

(3.1)

M_{K}({h})\cap Z_{K}({f})=\emptyset=Z_{K}({h})\cap M_{K}({f}).

Proof.

Suppose that
h\in D_{K}(f)
.
Then
\|{h-f}\|_{\infty}<1
by definition.
For each
t_{0}\in M_{K}({h})
, we have
the following inequalities:

1-f(t_{0})=h(t_{0})-f(t_{0})\leq|h(t_{0})-f(t_{0})|\leq\|{h-f}\|_{\infty}<1,

which shows that
f(t_{0})>0
.
This yields

M_{K}({h})\cap Z_{K}({f})=\emptyset
.
Similarly, for each
t_{1}\in M_{K}({f})
,
we obtain the following result:

1-h(t_{1})=f(t_{1})-h(t_{1})\leq|f(t_{1})-h(t_{1})|\leq\|{f-h}\|_{\infty}<1,

which proves that
h(t_{1})>0
.
This shows that

Z_{K}({h})\cap M_{K}({f})=\emptyset
.
Thus (
3.1
) holds.

Conversely, we assume that
equalities (
3.1
) hold.
We prove that
\|{h-f}\|_{\infty}<1
.
Fix an arbitrary
t\in K
.
Because
f(t),h(t)\in[0,1]
, we obtain the following inequalities:

\displaystyle-1\leq-f(t)\leq h(t)-f(t)\leq h(t)\leq 1.

In particular,

\|{h-f}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{s\in K}|h(s)-f(s)|\leq 1
.
Suppose, for contradiction,
\|{h-f}\|_{\infty}=1
.
Then there exists an
s_{0}\in K

with
|h(s_{0})-f(s_{0})|=1
.
Without loss of generality,
we may and do assume that

h(s_{0})-f(s_{0})=1
.
We derive
from the preceding inequalities that

1=h(s_{0})-f(s_{0})\leq h(s_{0})\leq 1.

This shows that
h(s_{0})=1

and
f(s_{0})=0
.
This implies that

s_{0}\in M_{K}({h})\cap Z_{K}({f})
,
which is in contradiction with

M_{K}({h})\cap Z_{K}({f})=\emptyset
.
This shows that

\|{h-f}\|_{\infty}\neq 1
, thus

\|{h-f}\|_{\infty}<1
.
As a consequence, we obtain

h\in D_{K}(f)
.
This establishes the lemma.
∎

For each
t_{0}\in K
, we define a subset

P_{K}({t_{0}})
of
S(C_{0}(K))^{+}
as follows:

P_{K}({t_{0}}):=\left\{h\in S(C_{0}(K))^{+}:h(t_{0})=1\right\}.

We refer to
P_{K}({t_{0}})
as the family
of peak functions each of which peaks at
t_{0}
.
Although elementary, the following lemma will be used repeatedly in what follows.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
t_{0},t_{1}\in K
.
If
P_{K}({t_{0}})\subseteq P_{K}({t_{1}})
,
then
t_{0}=t_{1}
holds.

Proof.

Suppose that
t_{0}\neq t_{1}
,
then there exists an
f_{0}\in S(C_{0}(K))^{+}
such that

f_{0}(t_{0})=1
and
f_{0}(t_{1})=0
,
and thus

f_{0}\in P_{K}({t_{0}})\setminus P_{K}({t_{1}})
.
Therefore the assumption
t_{0}\neq t_{1}

implies
P_{K}({t_{0}})\not\subseteq P_{K}({t_{1}})
, completing the proof.
∎

The following lemma establishes a connection between the zero sets and the maximum sets of
functions and the associated sets
D_{K}(f)
and
D_{K}(g)
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f,g\in S(C_{0}(K))^{+}
.
If
M_{K}({g})\subseteq M_{K}({f})
and
Z_{K}({g})\subseteq Z_{K}({f})
, then
D_{K}(f)\subseteq D_{K}(g)
.
Conversely, if
D_{K}(f)\subseteq D_{K}(g)
, then
M_{K}({g})\subseteq M_{K}({f})
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary
h\in D_{K}(f)
.
We deduce from Lemma
3.1

that

M_{K}({h})\cap Z_{K}({f})=\emptyset=Z_{K}({h})\cap M_{K}({f}).

By the assumptions, we have
the following inclusions:

\displaystyle M_{K}({h})\cap Z_{K}({g})

\displaystyle\subseteq M_{K}({h})\cap Z_{K}({f})=\emptyset,

\displaystyle Z_{K}({h})\cap M_{K}({g})

\displaystyle\subseteq Z_{K}({h})\cap M_{K}({f})=\emptyset.

This shows that

M_{K}({h})\cap Z_{K}({g})=\emptyset=Z_{K}({h})\cap M_{K}({g})
,
which implies that
h\in D_{K}(g)

by Lemma
3.1
.
Hence
D_{K}(f)\subseteq D_{K}(g)
.

Conversely, suppose that
M_{K}({g})\not\subseteq M_{K}({f})
, we prove that
D_{K}(f)\not\subseteq D_{K}(g)
.
There exists a point

y_{0}\in M_{K}({g})\setminus M_{K}({f})
.
Let
v_{0}\in S(C_{0}(K))^{+}
be such that

v_{0}(y_{0})=0
and
v_{0}=1

on
M_{K}({f})
.
We set
w=v_{0}f
.
Then
w\in S(C_{0}(K))^{+}

with
y_{0}\in Z_{K}({w})
and
M_{K}({w})=M_{K}({f})
.
By the choice of
y_{0}
, we conclude that

y_{0}\in Z_{K}({w})\cap M_{K}({g})
.
This yields

Z_{K}({w})\cap M_{K}({g})\neq\emptyset
.
It follows from Lemma
3.1

that
w\not\in D_{K}(g)
.
On the other hand,
we have the following identities
by definition:

M_{K}({f})\cap Z_{K}({f})=\emptyset\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad Z_{K}({w})\cap M_{K}({w})=\emptyset.

By applying
M_{K}({w})=M_{K}({f})
,
we conclude that

M_{K}({w})\cap Z_{K}({f})=\emptyset\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad Z_{K}({w})\cap M_{K}({f})=\emptyset.

Now, we apply
Lemma
3.1
to conclude that

w\in D_{K}(f)
.
From this we deduce
w\in D_{K}(f)\setminus D_{K}(g)
,
which shows
D_{K}(f)\not\subseteq D_{K}(g)
.
We conclude that
D_{K}(f)\subseteq D_{K}(g)

implies
M_{K}({g})\subseteq M_{K}({f})
.
Therefore, the claim follows.
This completes the proof.
∎

In the remainder of this paper,
we assume that
\Phi
is a surjective
isometry from
S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
onto
S(C_{0}(Y))^{+}
,
where
X
and
Y
are locally compact
Hausdorff spaces.
Then the inverse map
\Phi^{-1}\colon S(C_{0}(Y))^{+}\to S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
is also a well-defined surjective isometry.

At this stage, we clarify the structure
of the image under
\Phi
by relating

\Phi(D_{X}(f))
and
D_{Y}(\Phi(f))
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For each
f\in S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
, we have

\Phi(D_{X}(f))=D_{Y}(\Phi(f))
.

Proof.

We first show that

\Phi(D_{X}(f))\subseteq D_{Y}(\Phi(f))
.
Fix an arbitrary
u\in\Phi(D_{X}(f))
.
By the surjectivity of
\Phi
,
there exists
h\in D_{X}(f)

such that
u=\Phi(h)
.
Because
\Phi
is an isometry and
h\in D_{X}(f)
, we obtain the following inequalities:

\|{u-\Phi(f)}\|_{\infty}=\|{\Phi(h)-\Phi(f)}\|_{\infty}=\|{h-f}\|_{\infty}<1.

This shows that

u\in D_{Y}(\Phi(f))
.
Therefore we conclude that

\Phi(D_{X}(f))\subseteq D_{Y}(\Phi(f))
.
To prove the reverse inclusion,
we apply the same argument
to the pair of
(\Phi^{-1},\Phi(f))
,
instead of
(\Phi,f)
.
It follows that

\Phi^{-1}(D_{Y}(\Phi(f)))\subseteq D_{X}(\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(f)))=D_{X}(f)
.
This proves that
D_{Y}(\Phi(f))\subseteq\Phi(D_{X}(f))
.
This completes the proof
of the identity.
∎

We next prove that finite families of
peak functions yield non-empty
intersections of their corresponding maximum sets.
This is the first step
toward the general case.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
x_{0}\in X
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

f_{j}\in P_{X}({x_{0}})
for
j\in\mathbb{N}

with
1\leq j\leq n
.
Then the intersection

\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}M_{Y}({\Phi(f_{j})})
is a
non-empty set.

Proof.

Let
g=\sum_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}/n

and fix an arbitrary
k\in\mathbb{N}

with
1\leq k\leq n
.
Since
0\leq f_{k}\leq 1

and
f_{k}(x_{0})=1
,
we have
0\leq g\leq 1
and

g(x_{0})=1
.
This yields
g\in P_{X}({x_{0}})
.
It is straightforward to verify that

M_{X}({g})=\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}M_{X}({f_{j}})\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad Z_{X}({g})=\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}Z_{X}({f_{j}}).

In particular, we have
M_{X}({g})\subseteq M_{X}({f_{k}})
and
Z_{X}({g})\subseteq Z_{X}({f_{k}})
.
We conclude that
D_{X}(f_{k})\subseteq D_{X}(g)
by Lemma
3.3
, and hence
\Phi(D_{X}(f_{k}))\subseteq\Phi(D_{X}(g))
.
Combining with Lemma
3.4
, this gives
D_{Y}(\Phi(f_{k}))\subseteq D_{Y}(\Phi(g))
.
It follows from Lemma
3.3
that

M_{Y}({\Phi(g)})\subseteq M_{Y}({\Phi(f_{k})})
.
Therefore
M_{Y}({\Phi(g)})\subseteq\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}M_{Y}({\Phi(f_{j})})

as
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
1\leq k\leq n

was arbitrary.
Moreover, since
\Phi(g)\in S(C_{0}(Y))^{+}
, we have
M_{Y}({\Phi(g)})\neq\emptyset
.
As a consequence, we obtain
\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}M_{Y}({\Phi(f_{j})})\neq\emptyset
.
This completes the proof.
∎

By extending the finite case established in
Lemma
3.5
,
we obtain the following result
for arbitrary families of peak functions.

Lemma 3.6
.

The intersection
\bigcap_{f\in P_{X}({x_{0}})}M_{Y}({\Phi(f)})
is a non-empty set for each
x_{0}\in X
.

Proof.

Fix
x_{0}\in X
arbitrarily.
Because
\Phi(f)\in S(C_{0}(Y))^{+}
vanishes at infinity, the set
M_{Y}({\Phi(f)})
is a compact subset of
Y
for all
f\in P_{X}({x_{0}})
.
By Lemma
3.5
,
the collection
\{M_{Y}({\Phi(f)}):f\in P_{X}({x_{0}})\}
of compact subsets of
Y

has the finite intersection property.
We thus conclude that

\bigcap_{f\in P_{X}({x_{0}})}M_{Y}({\Phi(f)})

is a non-empty set for each
x_{0}\in X
.
∎

The following lemma identifies a unique point

y\in Y
associated with each
x\in X
,
which allows us to define a map
between
X
and
Y
.

Lemma 3.7
.

For each
x_{0}\in X
there exists a unique

y_{0}\in Y
such that

\Phi(P_{X}({x_{0}}))=P_{Y}({y_{0}})
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary
x_{0}\in X
.
By Lemma
3.6
, we have

\bigcap_{f\in P_{X}({x_{0}})}M_{Y}({\Phi(f)})\neq\emptyset
.
Choose

y_{0}\in\bigcap_{f\in P_{X}({x_{0}})}M_{Y}({\Phi(f)})
.
We claim that

\Phi(P_{X}({x_{0}}))\subseteq P_{Y}({y_{0}})
.
Let
u\in\Phi(P_{X}({x_{0}}))
be fixed.
Then there exists an
h\in P_{X}({x_{0}})

such that
u=\Phi(h)
.
By virtue of the property that

y_{0}\in\bigcap_{f\in P_{X}({x_{0}})}M_{Y}({\Phi(f)})
,
we have
y_{0}\in M_{Y}({\Phi(h)})=M_{Y}({u})
.
This implies that
u(y_{0})=1
,
hence
u\in P_{Y}({y_{0}})
.
This shows that

\Phi(P_{X}({x_{0}}))\subseteq P_{Y}({y_{0}}).

Applying the same argument to

(\Phi^{-1},y_{0})
yields

\Phi^{-1}(P_{Y}({y_{0}}))\subseteq P_{X}({x_{1}})

for some
x_{1}\in X
, that is,

P_{Y}({y_{0}})\subseteq\Phi(P_{X}({x_{1}})).

Combining the previous inclusions gives

\Phi(P_{X}({x_{0}}))\subseteq P_{Y}({y_{0}})\subseteq\Phi(P_{X}({x_{1}}))
.
Since
\Phi
is injective,
it follows that

P_{X}({x_{0}})\subseteq P_{X}({x_{1}})
.
By Lemma
3.2
,
we have
x_{0}=x_{1}
.
It follows from the previous inclusions that

\Phi(P_{X}({x_{0}}))=P_{Y}({y_{0}})
.

Finally, we prove the uniqueness of such
point
y_{0}\in Y
.
Suppose that another point
y_{1}\in Y
satisfies

\Phi(P_{X}({x_{0}}))=P_{Y}({y_{1}})
as well.
Then we have

P_{Y}({y_{0}})=\Phi(P_{X}({x_{0}}))=P_{Y}({y_{1}})
.
By applying Lemma
3.2
,
we obtain
y_{0}=y_{1}
,
which proves the uniqueness of the point
y_{0}\in Y

that satisfies
\Phi(P_{X}({x_{0}}))=P_{Y}({y_{0}})
.
∎

Finally, we record the following auxiliary
lemma, which provides a technical
construction used in the proof of
Theorem
3.1
.
The idea of proof originated in

[
11
, Lemma 3.14]
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
v\in S(C_{0}(Y))^{+}
and
y_{0}\in Y
be such that
v(y_{0})<1
.
Then there exists a
u_{0}\in P_{Y}({y_{0}})

such that
v+(1-v(y_{0}))u_{0}\in P_{Y}({y_{0}})
.

Proof.

We set
r=1-v(y_{0})
.
Then
r>0
by assumption.
We define

\displaystyle Y_{0}

\displaystyle=\left\{y\in Y:|v(y)-v(y_{0})|\geq\frac{r}{4}\right\},

\displaystyle Y_{n}

\displaystyle=\left\{y\in Y:\frac{r}{2^{n+2}}\leq|v(y)-v(y_{0})|\leq\frac{r}{2^{n+1}}\right\},\quad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

We observe that
Y_{k}
is a closed subset of
Y
with
y_{0}\not\in Y_{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\left\{0\right\}
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a
u_{n}\in S(C_{0}(Y))^{+}
such that

(3.2)

u_{n}(y_{0})=1\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad u_{n}=0\quad\mbox{on $Y_{0}\cup Y_{n}$.}\quad

We define a function
u_{0}\colon Y\to\mathbb{R}

as follows:

u_{0}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{u_{n}}{2^{n}}.

Then
u_{0}
converges
in
C_{0}(Y)
with
u_{0}\geq 0
, as
u_{n}\in S(C_{0}(Y))^{+}

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
u_{n}(y_{0})=1=\|{u_{n}}\|_{\infty}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain the following inequalities:

1=u_{0}(y_{0})\leq\|{u_{0}}\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\|{u_{n}}\|_{\infty}}{2^{n}}=1.

Hence,
u_{0}(y_{0})=1=\|{u_{0}}\|_{\infty}
.
This yields
u_{0}\in P_{Y}({y_{0}})
.

We define a function

w\colon Y\to\mathbb{R}
as follows:

w=v+ru_{0}.

We obtain
w(y_{0})=1

as
r=1-v(y_{0})
and
u_{0}(y_{0})=1
.
We prove that
w\in S(C_{0}(Y))^{+}
, that is,
0\leq w(y)\leq 1
for all
y\in Y
.
Fix an arbitrary
y\in Y
.
We have
w(y)\geq 0
as
v,u_{0}\geq 0

with
r>0
.
We will consider three cases
to show that
w(y)\leq 1
.

•

If
y\in Y_{0}
, then equality (
3.2
)
shows that
u_{n}(y)=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
hence
u_{0}(y)=0
.
Then we obtain

w(y)=v(y)\leq 1
.

•

If
y\in Y_{m}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
then we have

v(y)-v(y_{0})\leq r/2^{m+1}

by definition.
By combining the last inequality
with
r=1-v(y_{0})
,
we obtain the following result:

v(y)\leq v(y_{0})+\frac{r}{2^{m+1}}=1+r\left(\frac{1}{2^{m+1}}-1\right).

We derive from equality (
3.2
)
with
y\in Y_{m}

that
u_{m}(y)=0
.
We obtain

u_{0}(y)=\sum_{n\neq m}^{\infty}\frac{u_{n}(y)}{2^{n}}\leq 1-\frac{1}{2^{m}}.

By combining the preceding inequalities,
we obtain the following results:

\displaystyle w(y)

\displaystyle=v(y)+ru_{0}(y)

\displaystyle\leq 1+r\left(\frac{1}{2^{m+1}}-1\right)+r\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{m}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{r}{2^{m+1}}<1.

Therefore, we have
w(y)\leq 1
.

•

If
y\not\in\cup_{k=0}^{\infty}Y_{k}
,
then we obtain
v(y)=v(y_{0})=1-r
.
We have the following inequality:

w(y)=1-r+ru_{0}(y)\leq 1.

We conclude that
w(y)\leq 1
.

As a consequence, we conclude that

w(y)\leq 1
for all
y\in Y
.
This shows that
w\in P_{Y}({y_{0}})
.
This completes the proof.
∎

The preceding lemmas provide
all the necessary ingredients,
and we can now assemble them
to prove the main result.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We first introduce two maps,

\tau,\sigma
, between
X
and
Y
.
By Lemma
3.7
, there exists a map

\tau\colon X\to Y
that satisfies

\Phi(P_{X}({x}))=P_{Y}({\tau(x)})

for all
x\in X
.
Similarly, applying Lemma
3.7
to
\Phi^{-1}

instead of
\Phi
, we obtain
a map
\sigma\colon Y\to X
satisfying that

\Phi^{-1}(P_{Y}({y}))=P_{X}({\sigma(y)})

for all
y\in Y
.
Consequently, this yields the following identities:

\displaystyle\Phi(P_{X}({x}))=P_{Y}({\tau(x)})\hskip 27.03003pt(x\in X),

\displaystyle\Phi^{-1}(P_{Y}({y}))=P_{X}({\sigma(y)})\hskip 17.07182pt(y\in Y).

By combining the
last two identities,
we obtain

(3.3)

\displaystyle P_{X}({x})

\displaystyle=\Phi^{-1}(P_{Y}({\tau(x)}))=P_{X}({\sigma(\tau(x))})\hskip 17.07182pt(x\in X),

\displaystyle P_{Y}({y})

\displaystyle=\Phi(P_{X}({\sigma(y))})=P_{Y}({\tau(\sigma(y))})\hskip 27.03003pt(y\in Y).

It follows from Lemma
3.2
that

x=\sigma(\tau(x))
and
y=\tau(\sigma(y))

for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
.
Hence,
\tau
and
\sigma
are bijections,
and moreover
\tau=\sigma^{-1}
.

Fix
f\in S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
and
y_{0}\in Y

arbitrarily. We prove that

\Phi(f)(y_{0})\leq f(\sigma(y_{0}))
.
To see this, we consider two cases.
First, suppose that
\Phi(f)(y_{0})=1
.
By equality (
3.3
), we have

\Phi(f)\in P_{Y}({y_{0}})=\Phi(P_{X}({\sigma(y_{0})}))
,
which implies
f\in P_{X}({\sigma(y_{0})})
.
This shows that

f(\sigma(y_{0}))=1=\Phi(f)(y_{0})
, and therefore the desired inequality holds in this case.
We next consider the case where

\Phi(f)(y_{0})<1
.
Let
u_{0}\in P_{Y}({y_{0}})
be as in Lemma
3.8
, corresponding to
v=\Phi(f)
.
By setting
r=1-\Phi(f)(y_{0})
,
Lemma
3.8
shows
that
u_{0}
satisfies

\Phi(f)+ru_{0}\in P_{Y}({y_{0}})
.
For brevity in notation,
we will write
w=\Phi(f)+ru_{0}
.
As
w\in P_{Y}({y_{0}})
,
it follows from equality (
3.3
) that

w\in\Phi(P_{X}({\sigma(y_{0})}))
,
hence
\Phi^{-1}(w)\in P_{X}({\sigma(y_{0})})
.
In particular,
\Phi^{-1}(w)(\sigma(y_{0}))=1
.
Because
\Phi
is an isometry,
we have:

\displaystyle 1-f(\sigma(y_{0}))

\displaystyle\leq|\Phi^{-1}(w)(\sigma(y_{0}))-f(\sigma(y_{0}))|

\displaystyle\leq\|{\Phi^{-1}(w)-f}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|{w-\Phi(f)}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|{ru_{0}}\|_{\infty}=1-\Phi(f)(y_{0}),

where we used
\|{u_{0}}\|_{\infty}=1
.
From this we conclude that
\Phi(f)(y_{0})\leq f(\sigma(y_{0}))
also holds if
\Phi(f)(y_{0})<1
.
Therefore,
\Phi(f)(y)\leq f(\sigma(y))
for all
f\in S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
and
y\in Y
.

Applying the above arguments
to the pair of
(\Phi^{-1},\sigma^{-1})
,
instead of
(\Phi,\sigma)
, we obtain

\Phi^{-1}(u)(x)\leq u(\sigma^{-1}(x))

for all
u\in S(C_{0}(Y))^{+}
and
x\in X
.
By letting
u=\Phi(f)
and
x=\sigma(y)

for
f\in S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
and
y\in Y

in the last inequality,
we conclude that
f(\sigma(y))\leq\Phi(f)(y)
.
As a consequence, the following equality holds
for all
f\in S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
and
y\in Y
:

(3.4)

\Phi(f)(y)=f(\sigma(y)).

Now, we prove that
\sigma\colon Y\to X

is continuous.
Fix an arbitrary open subset

U\subset X
.
We need to show that
\sigma^{-1}(U)
is an
open subset of
Y
.
Let
y_{1}\in\sigma^{-1}(U)
be an arbitrary point.
There exists an
f_{1}\in S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
such that

f_{1}(\sigma(y_{1}))=1
and
f_{1}=0

on
X\setminus U
.
Define a subset
V

of
Y
as follows:

V=\left\{y\in Y:\Phi(f_{1})(y)>1/2\right\}.

It is immediate that
V
is an open subset
of
Y
.
It follows from
equality
(
3.4
)
that

\Phi(f_{1})(y_{1})=f_{1}(\sigma(y_{1}))=1
,
which yields that
y_{1}\in V
.
For each
y\in V
, we obtain

f_{1}(\sigma(y))=\Phi(f_{1})(y)>1/2

by equality
(
3.4
).
This implies that
\sigma(V)\subseteq U

because
f_{1}=0
on
X\setminus U
.
We conclude that
y_{1}\in V\subseteq\sigma^{-1}(U)
with
V
open,
which proves that
\sigma^{-1}(U)
is an open subset
of
Y
.
As a consequence,
\sigma
is continuous on
Y
.

The above argument, applied to
the pair of
(\Phi^{-1},\sigma^{-1})
, instead of

(\Phi,\sigma)
, shows that

\sigma^{-1}\colon X\to Y
is continuous.
We conclude that

\sigma\colon Y\to X
is a homeomorphism.
This completes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
3.2
.

Let
\Phi\colon S(C_{0}(X))^{+}\to S(C_{0}(Y))^{+}
be
a surjective isometry.
Then there exists a homeomorphism

\sigma\colon Y\to X
such that

\Phi(f)=f\circ\sigma
for all
f\in S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
by
Theorem
3.1
.
Now we define a map

\tilde{\Phi}\colon C_{0}(X)\to C_{0}(Y)
as follows:

\tilde{\Phi}(f)=f\circ\sigma\qquad(f\in C_{0}(X)).

It is immediate that
\tilde{\Phi}
is a real-linear isometry
that satisfies
\tilde{\Phi}=\Phi
on
S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
.
It is plain that the map
g\mapsto g\circ\sigma^{-1}
from
C_{0}(Y)
to
C_{0}(X)
is the inverse of
\tilde{\Phi}
.
In particular,
\tilde{\Phi}
is a surjective isometry from
C_{0}(X)
onto
C_{0}(Y)
.

Finally we show that the extension
of
\Phi
is unique.
Let
\tilde{\Phi}^{\prime}\colon C_{0}(X)\to C_{0}(Y)
be
another surjective real-linear isometry
with
\tilde{\Phi}^{\prime}=\Phi
on
S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
.
By the Banach–Stone theorem,
there exists a continuous function

\alpha\colon Y\to\left\{\pm 1\right\}

and a homeomorphism

\varphi\colon Y\to X
such that

\tilde{\Phi}^{\prime}(f)=\alpha(f\circ\varphi)

for all
f\in C_{0}(X)
.
For each
y\in Y
, there exists
an
f_{y}\in S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
that satisfies

f_{y}(\varphi(y))=1
.
We obtain

\alpha(y)=\alpha(y)f_{y}(\varphi(y))=\tilde{\Phi}^{\prime}(f_{y})(y)=\Phi(f_{y})(y)\geq 0.

Because
\alpha(y)\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}
,
we obtain
\alpha(y)=1

for all
y\in Y
.
As a consequence, we obtain

f\circ\varphi=\tilde{\Phi}^{\prime}(f)=\tilde{\Phi}(f)=f\circ\sigma

for all
f\in S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
.
Since
S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
separates the points
of
X
,
we observe that
\varphi=\sigma
,
which shows that
\tilde{\Phi}^{\prime}=\tilde{\Phi}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Applications to phase-isometries

In this section we apply our main theorem
to the study of phase-isometries
on the positive unit sphere.
Let
A,B
be subsets of Banach spaces.
A map
T\colon A\to B
is called
a
phase-isometry
if it satisfies
the following equality for all
a,b\in A
:

\left\{\|T(a)+T(b)\|,\|T(a)-T(b)\|\right\}=\left\{\|a+b\|,\|a-b\|\right\}.

Phase-isometries have been extensively
investigated in the literature
(see, for example,
[
9
,
13
]
).
Our main result immediately yields
the following theorem,
which provides a concise characterization
of phase-isometries,
and generalizes
[
7
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
T\colon S(C_{0}(X))^{+}\to S(C_{0}(Y))^{+}
be
a surjective phase-isometry.
Then there exists a homeomorphism

\sigma\colon Y\to X
such that

T(f)=f\circ\sigma
holds
for all
f\in S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
.
Consequently,
T
extends uniquely
to a surjective real-linear isometry
from
C_{0}(X)
onto
C_{0}(Y)
.

Proof.

Let
T\colon S(C_{0}(X))^{+}\to S(C_{0}(Y))^{+}
be a surjective
phase-isometry.
We prove that
T
is an isometry.
Although this is essentially proved in

[
7
, Proposition 2.1]
,
we give its proof
for the sake of completeness.
Let
f,g\in S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
and
x\in X
be fixed.
Because
f(x),g(x)\geq 0
,
we obtain the following inequalities:

|f(x)-g(x)|\leq|f(x)+g(x)|\leq\|{f+g}\|_{\infty},

which yields
\|{f-g}\|_{\infty}\leq\|{f+g}\|_{\infty}
.
By applying the same argument
to
T(f),T(g)
, we conclude that

\|{T(f)-T(g)}\|_{\infty}\leq\|{T(f)+T(g)}\|_{\infty}
.
As
T
is a phase-isometry,
the preceding inequalities show
the following result:

\displaystyle\|{T(f)-T(g)}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\|{T(f)+T(g)}\|_{\infty},\|{T(f)-T(g)}\|_{\infty}\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\|{f+g}\|_{\infty},\|{f-g}\|_{\infty}\right\}

\displaystyle=\|{f-g}\|_{\infty}.

Therefore we conclude that
T
is
a surjective isometry.
Now we apply Theorem
3.1
to
T
.
Then we conclude that
T
is a
composition operator induced by
a homeomorphism.
By Corollary
3.2
,

T
extends uniquely to a surjective
real-linear isometry between

C_{0}(X)
and
C_{0}(Y)
.
∎

Remark
.

In
[
10
]
, Leung, Ng and Wong present an elegant argument
for the case
S(C(K))^{+}
, the positive unit sphere
of
C(K)
with
K
compact Hausdorff space.
The authors analyze strictly positive functions
in
S(C(K))^{+}
and their maximum sets.
Because
K
is compact, there exists the constant function

\mathds{1}\in S(C(K))^{+}
.
Then the maximum set,

M_{K}({f})
, of
f\in S(C(K))^{+}
is
that of a strictly positive function
(\mathds{1}+f)/2
.
Moreover, the zero set,

Z_{K}({f})
, of
f\in S(C(K))^{+}
with
Z_{K}({f})\neq\emptyset
is related
to a maximum set of a strictly positive function
\mathds{1}-f/2
,
that is
Z_{K}({f})=M_{K}({\mathds{1}-f/2})
.
In this sense, strictly positive functions
and the constant function
\mathds{1}

play essential role in their argument.
In contrast,
in the case of
S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
with
X
locally compact Hausdorff space,
it is known that the existence of strictly positive functions
in
C_{0}(X)
is equivalent to

X
being
\sigma
-compact
[
1
, pp. 187, Exercise 4]
.
In particular, the lack of assumption that

X
is
\sigma
-compact obstructs
an adaptation of the compact-case argument in
[
10
]
.

The key idea of this paper is to consider all functions
in
S(C_{0}(X))^{+}
along with their zero sets and
maximum sets, rather than restricting attention
to strictly positive functions with maximum sets.
In Lemma
3.3
, we obtain some properties
of the sets
D_{X}(f)
for
f\in S(C_{0}(X))^{+}

with respect to the maximum sets and zero sets.
By Lemma
3.4
, these properties are preserved
by surjective isometry
\Phi
.
This allows us to define a map
\sigma
from
Y
to
X
.
Then we can prove that
\Phi
is a composition operator
induced by
\sigma
.
