Genomic Analysis Using Liquid Biopsy: Considerations for Pretest Counseling in UK Practice
Authors: David John McMahon, Elizabeth Bancroft, George J Burghel, Ellen Copson, Michael Hubank, Jennifer Wiggins, Katie Snape, Natalie Cook, Ian Frayling, Matthew J Murray, Alastair Greystoke, Matthew G Krebs, Sanjay Popat, Terri Patricia McVeigh, On behalf of UK Circulating Biomarkers Network of Excellence and the National ctDNA Steering Committee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractModern cancer diagnostics is heavily reliant on molecular data. Next-generation sequencing using liquid biopsy (LB) is primarily focused on identifying predictive and prognostic biomarkers to guide systemic therapy decision making. However, additional findings including genomic variants of possible or probable germline origin are detected in a significant minority of patients (3%-18%), with significant implications for patients and their families. Pretest information provision regarding both the purpose and potential benefits of the test and additional findings on LB varies in clinical practice, with little guidance for clinicians regarding the degree of detail required regarding possible additional findings. LB requires explicit disclosure of these aspects. Implementing a standardized, context-dependent approach to this discussion, with nationally standardized information provision tools, should be prioritized to enable patient-centered decision making regarding LB, without prioritizing the incidentalome over the primary purpose of the test—refining and optimizing treatment options.

Background

Genomic Evolution of Cancer: Understanding Origin of Variants

Modern cancer diagnostics is heavily reliant on molecular data. Genetic testing using tumor-derived DNA (somatic testing) has long been applied as standard practice at the point of pathology review, in addition to standard immunohistochemical profiling. The aim of genetic testing of tumor-derived DNA is to identify somatic events to help refine the clinical diagnosis, inform treatment decisions in first or subsequent lines, and provide prognostic information.

Advantages and Applications of Liquid Biopsy

Tumors demonstrate temporal and spatial heterogeneity.1 This, in addition to inherent feasibility issues associated with repeated biopsies of patients with suspected/confirmed cancer, has fueled interest in the use of liquid biopsy (LB), which involves testing more readily accessible bodily fluids, to evaluate cancer biomarkers, such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA). Currently, the most common sample used for the analysis of ctDNA is whole blood, but other fluids may be preferable, depending on tumor site of origin; cerebrospinal fluid is favored over blood in patients with brain tumors, for example, given that the blood-brain barrier limits release of tumor-derived material into the bloodstream.2 Compared with solid biopsies, LBs are minimally invasive, enable multiple longitudinal sampling, may be more representative of tumor heterogeneity, can improve turnaround times for genomic testing, and allow patients to commence appropriate treatment more rapidly.3 Tissue biopsy continues to be a gold standard diagnostic test, providing histological confirmation of a cancer and informing assessment of the tumor immune microenvironment.

Current indications for National Health Service (NHS)-funded LB include assisting and refining diagnosis in symptomatic patients with suspected advanced non–small cell lung cancer and evaluation of mechanisms of resistance to systemic anticancer therapy at times of progression for patients with estrogen receptor–positive breast cancer.4 In an NHS context, turnaround time for LB next-generation sequencing (NGS) has been demonstrated to be 9 versus 25 days (P < .0001; Table 1).5 It has been demonstrated that patients with NGS available at the time of systemic treatment initiation have significantly longer overall survival (OS) relative to those without NGS results available, with a median OS of 24.6 months (95% CI, 18.6 to not reached) versus 6.2 months (95% CI, 2.8 to 10.3; log-rank P < .0001); therefore, LB in the advanced lung cancer indication is likely to have significant benefits in an NHS context, although published reports of OS outcomes after the implementation of LB in the United Kingdom are awaited.6

Beyond these indications, potential applications are multiple, including options to escalate or de-escalate treatment in the adjuvant or metastatic setting, detection of postresection residual disease, monitoring for disease progression or relapse, and use in the diagnostic setting for difficult-to- diagnose malignancies, such as hepatobiliary cancers and cancers of unknown primary.3 Furthermore, the use of LB as a screening test in asymptomatic individuals is being explored, with some controversy.7

Where LB is used to monitor disease progression, or to monitor patients after definitive cancer treatment (minimal residual disease monitoring), analysis may be targeted to a few or a single marker of disease, informed by results of analysis on solid biopsy.

Increasingly, however, analysis of tumor-derived DNA (from solid or LB) is broad and tumor agnostic, attempting to increase diagnostic yield. Broad testing strategies increase the likelihood of identifying incidental/additional findings and/or uncertain results. Analysis typically includes genes in which acquired variants are known drivers in the tumor of interest but may also include genes implicated in other cancer types or in clonal hematopoiesis. Somatic variants in certain genes may be known drivers of cancer, and heritable germline variants in the same genes may (or may not) be contributory to the cancer of interest and may also confer a risk of other cancers. Compared with solid biopsy, the proportion of DNA in LBs that is derived from neoplastic compared with non-neoplastic cells is much lower. This means that the prior probability of germline origin of a variant detected at high variant allele frequency (VAF) is higher. This is particularly the case if other variants detected (if any) are present at much lower VAF (Appendix Table A1, Glossary of Terms).8 Many such findings will be relevant to the patient's presenting cancer, such that the information is not wholly unexpected (so-called additional findings), whereas other findings may be truly incidental, unanticipated, and apparently unrelated to the presenting cancer.8 Depending on the technology used, some laboratories may undertake paired analysis of DNA from buffy coat and plasma samples, enabling subtraction and facilitating confidence in reporting variants as being tumor-derived, although complexity still exists in distinguishing variants of germline origin from those representing clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHiP).9

Variants Ascertained in a Liquid Sample

Oncogenic driver variants are genetic aberrations that confer a growth and/or survival advantage to the cell in which they occur. Approximately 1-10 driver variants may be involved in carcinogenesis, against a background of several thousands of passenger events—variants acquired as a consequence of inherent genomic instability of cancer.10

Acquisition of true driver aberrations predates neoplastic transformation, such that oncogenic variants may be identified in normal cells—a particularly common phenomenon in older individuals. This so-called mutational burden of aging reflects long-term exposure to endogenous and exogenous mutagens, combined with increasing inefficiencies in DNA repair.11 CHiP refers to the acquisition of oncogenic variants in hematopoietic stem cells (blood) and is common after age 60.12 CHiP is a recognized neoplastic precursor and is associated with a risk of leukemic transformation, in the order of 0.5%-1% per annum, although in the majority of individuals undergoing LB, particularly those with advanced cancer, CHiP has little meaningful clinical significance.13 When analysis is undertaken on whole blood, variants may be identified that represent genetic changes related to that cancer, germline variants, clonal hematopoiesis, or drivers from other primary cancers (Table 1).

Germline Versus Somatic Variants—Considerations

Classification

Targeting oncogenic drivers represents an attractive therapeutic strategy compared with systemic agents deleterious to all dividing cells, such that classification as (likely) oncogenic may be relatively permissive if a matched targeted therapy exists, and there is at least some evidence that therapeutic response is likely. Oncogenic drivers can be ranked in terms of actionability, depending on their clinical significance in a particular tumor type, and availability of a matched targeted treatment, with some frameworks focusing on variant classification and oncogenicity (eg, ASCO/AMP/CAP/SVIG) and others on context-specific treatment implications (eg, ESCAT).14-16

The burden of proof required to classify germline variants as (likely) pathogenic is substantial compared with the evidence required to classify somatic variants as (likely) oncogenic. Misattribution of pathogenicity to a germline genetic variant can have significant consequences for patients, such as irreversible surgical prophylaxis, increased psychological distress, and/or inappropriate use of preimplantation/prenatal testing with the potential for subsequent termination of affected embryos. Noncarriers of variants to which pathogenicity has erroneously been ascribed may be falsely reassured and discharged.

Pathogenicity and oncogenicity are not synonymous, and discordance has been reported, with variable rates depending on gene, variant type, and variant classification guidance applied.17,18 Certain variants are oncogenic only in the right context (tissue type or favorable environment) and would not necessarily confer a disease risk as a germline event. Variants of likely germline origin require interpretation based on germline variant interpretation guidance, with follow-up recommended only for those classified as (likely) pathogenic, and where possible this should be facilitated before reporting, although not at the expense of timely reporting.15

Actionability in Different Contexts

Pathogenicity and penetrance are related but distinct concepts; not all pathogenic variants confer a high disease risk, and any associated risk may be influenced by age, biological sex, and family history. Incidentally ascertained variants (off-tumor findings) may not necessarily be associated with the same level of risk as variants identified in a clinically relevant context (on-tumor findings; Table 2).19 The absolute risks associated with an incidentally ascertained pathogenic variant in SDHA, for example, are so low that neither surveillance nor cascade testing of relatives is recommended, such that the clinical utility of confirming a germline origin of such a variant in an off-tumor context is extremely limited.20,21

Where a variant of likely germline origin has been identified, it is important to give consideration to confirmatory germline testing and follow-up, if the result will have a meaningful impact on the management of the patient and/or their relatives.22 It may not be necessary to undertake targeted germline testing to confirm the origin of a variant in all cases—considerations for onward testing include estimated germline conversion rate (ie, likelihood that the variant is truly of germline origin), clinical utility, and actionability (Tables 1 and 2). When analyzing tumor-derived DNA, identification of an on- or off-target variant of likely germline origin is not infrequent, reported in 3%-18%, with higher yields where larger panels are used.22 The prior probability of identifying a variant of likely germline origin depends on the clinical context and is higher in individuals with cancers with strong heritable risk compared with cancers more frequently attributed to environmental exposures, in patients affected with cancer at younger ages, and in patients from ethnic backgrounds where germline cancer–predisposing variants are enriched. In individuals where a high suspicion of an underlying hereditary predisposition exists, an LB should not be considered a reliable substitute for formal germline genetic testing.22 Furthermore, in exceptional cases, postzygotic constitutional mosaicism (acquisition of variant after conception, during embryogenesis) may be present, complicating interpretation of VAF to predict origin of a variant. Distinguishing between somatic/germline origin, CHiP or postzygotic mosaicism can be challenging, particularly when considering genes such as TP53, commonly implicated in all such contexts.23

When considering current indications for ctDNA testing, the likelihood of identifying a high-risk variant of likely germline origin is higher in patients with metastatic breast cancer than in those with suspected lung cancer.24,25 Judgements related to the actionability of cancer-predisposing variants are context-dependent. In a patient with established cancer, considerations include use of a variant as a predictive biomarker, or as a therapeutic target, in addition to future cancer risk and intervention in the patient and their relatives. The factors immediately related to the current cancer are typically prioritized by individuals with advanced disease and/or limited treatment options.22

Unexpected identification of heritable variants associated with cancer risk is an issue that is not limited to somatic testing. As use of whole-genome sequencing increases, so too does detection of additional findings, including germline pathogenic variants (GPVs) predisposing to cancer in people undergoing testing to establish the genetic basis of a nonmalignant phenotype. When cancer-predisposing GPVs are detected in patients without cancer, their associated cancer risk will depend on opportunities for early detection or prevention of cancer. The British Society of Genetic Medicine has developed guidance for reporting of incidental findings of confirmed germline origin in this context.26 The UK Cancer Genetics Group has developed aligned guidance for follow up of these findings of likely germline origin detected through tumor testing, which is currently being refined to reflect updated national guidance and evidence (McVeigh T et al, Personal communication, March 18, 2026).22 It is accepted practice that patient outcomes are improved when cases are discussed at multidisciplinary team meetings, with input from relevant specialists involved in cancer care.27,28 In line with this, it is best practice for the results of genomic tests to be discussed at a genomic tumor advisory board (GTAB), including relevant scientific and clinical experts from the specialist genomic workforce.

Providing Pretest Information

Facilitating informed decision making becomes increasingly challenging when the spectrum of possible findings associated with a test is broad. Patients may struggle to assimilate hypothetical or low-probability results, particularly when such discussions occur alongside complex or distressing information, including the communication of a terminal diagnosis (Fig 1 and Table 1). These issues are comparatively more manageable, although no less important, in the context of on-treatment (compared to diagnostic) LB, where established patient-clinician relationships allow the concept to be introduced gradually over time.

Many authors comment on issues influencing genetic counseling in advance of germline tests, including issues regarding patients’ recall of the information provided.29-35 Suggestions that discussions regarding tumor-based analyses should mirror that of the pretest counseling for germline investigations have generated concerns regarding clinical capacity, logistical issues, delays in diagnostic pathway, and compromised patient care, as well as cognitive or emotional overload in the patient.36,37 A fundamental tension exists between conducting a detailed discussion of all possible outcomes of LB and constraints of time, information overload, and clinical urgency in the diagnostic or treatment pathway. The primary purpose of LB analysis is to detect variants that will benefit the patient in refining diagnosis or in optimizing their treatment. We recommend that the focus of a discussion regarding use of this testing should not prioritize the incidentalome at the expense of a robust description of the purpose and potential benefits of the test, although material risks should be disclosed. The European Society of Human Genetics argues that withholding cascade testing from at-risk relatives of carriers of known germline genetic variants, because of potential societal repercussions for individuals identified as carriers, is ethically problematic, given the clear medical and reproductive benefits such testing may provide.38 A similar dilemma arises in relation to ctDNA analysis for diagnosis and treatment planning. ctDNA testing offers substantial clinical value, yet concerns about incidental germline findings may lead (and, in some cases, have led) to barriers to the implementation of such testing. As with cascade testing, limiting or delaying access on the basis of secondary or hypothetical harms risks undermining patient care. The nature of the concern differs; cascade testing raises questions about societal consequences for carriers, whereas ctDNA testing raises challenges related to patient autonomy, beneficence and nonmaleficence, the breadth of possible findings, and the psychological burden on patients already facing serious illness. Nonetheless, the underlying principle is comparable: clinically beneficial testing should not be withheld or unduly delayed, and the appropriate response is to strengthen counseling and management pathways rather than restrict access. Molecular diagnostics is not optional in modern cancer care as it is essential for diagnosis and treatment planning. Somatic NGS on tumor-derived DNA is typically undertaken as part of routine diagnostic work-up, requested reflexively by the pathologist, not the patient-facing clinician. Consent for this analysis is implied, with no discussion between the clinical team and the patient. However, the material risks and benefits of tumor profiling have changed as the extent of testing, diagnostic yield, and potential for additional findings have all increased.

Modern techniques, including LB, require explicit disclosure, proportionate to the clinical context. In adults, it is presumed that they have the capacity, voluntariness, and are sufficiently informed to be able to provide consent for a medical intervention or test.39 The Montgomery standard, established following the Montgomery versus Lanarkshire case of 2015, requires clinicians to inform patients of risks, benefits, and alternatives that a reasonable patient would consider important.40 The discussion needs to be patient-centered, tailored to the individual context. It is accepted that patients must be given adequate time and space for medical decision making, but this must be contextualized relative to the clinical urgency of the result.41

It is possible to reduce the turnaround time for LB NGS to as low as 9 days in an NHS context—although anecdotally this may be longer in day-to-day NHS practice, it is typically faster than the typical 25-day turnaround for tissue NGS, enabling patients to start the most appropriate treatment faster.5 Clinical urgency does not obviate the need for delivery of information regarding benefits and risks of the test, but may influence the manner in which information is delivered. To enable timely, standardized provision of information related to genetic testing, it may be helpful to use other methods—videos, podcasts, or patient-facing apps to supplement discussion, building on the points raised in Figure 1. Such technologies have been used with great effect for delivery of information related to germline as well as somatic testing, and may be used to supplement or augment clinical discussions.42,43 It is imperative that nationally endorsed and standardized information be provided to every patient, specific to their clinical context (eg, diagnostic v on-treatment), to reduce regional variation in practice and outcomes. Patient input into the depth and format of these resources is paramount.

In some clinical contexts, tests associated with low risk and high likely societal or public health benefit (such as newborn screening or HIV testing) are undertaken on a routine basis unless a patient actively declines (opts out).44 When considering LB, a proportionate routine-testing model with a clear opportunity to decline may minimize delays in testing that can facilitate timely diagnosis and treatment planning. Under this approach, LB is undertaken as part of standard diagnostic care following provision of essential, context-appropriate information, supported by written or digital materials in an accessible language. Patients must be given a straightforward and timely mechanism to opt out before analysis is complete, ensuring that their decision is respected, without creating barriers to clinically important testing.21 Some authors have proposed that patients could be offered the option to decline receipt of variants of likely germline origin, although this could pose challenges in practice where such variants are relevant to the patient phenotype and their treatment options.45 However, if patients do not wish to confirm the germline origin of a clinically relevant variant, they may decline onward germline confirmatory follow-up testing, ensuring they understand the risks, benefits, and consequences of such a decision.

Discussions regarding this should be contemporaneously documented in the patient notes.

NHS England's Genomic Unit has implemented a policy on reporting additional genomic targets in solid tumors beyond that recommended as part of standard of care, to include selected variants relevant to emerging targeted therapies and clinical trials. Implementation of this policy has been phased, and the number of reportable targets is likely to expand pending the outcome of the Experimental Cancer Medicine Center Delphi process review of clinical trial targets. As part of this policy, variants of putative germline origin (VAF in excess of 40%) in a number of genes (BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, RAD51C, and RAD51D) will be reported as requiring further assessment, irrespective of tumor type.46,47 National stakeholder engagement on these pathways has indicated that most agree information about the potential for germline findings should be communicated to patients undergoing testing but that processes should not delay treatment decisions.

Patient and Clinician-Facing Resources

Implementing a standardized approach to the discussion of LB, particularly at FDC, may help in the delivery of complex information.29,30 Use of adjuncts to aid in discussions should be considered, and efforts to upskill the relevant medical workforce (including doctors, nurses, and allied health professionals) regarding clinical information derived from LB technologies and genomic testing will aid in appropriate discussion with patients at the time of LB.33,34 This will be particularly important as application of LB technologies expands in a variety of settings in the coming years.48 This will be enabled in the United Kingdom through collaboration between the Circulating Biomarker Network of Excellence Group, the UK Cancer Genetics Group, and other relevant constituent groups of the British Society of Genetic Medicine, and NHS England's Genomic Education Program, in consultation with relevant key stakeholders and patient groups. We believe that harmonized policies between institutions and multidisciplinary teams involving diagnostic services, oncology, pathology, and clinical genetics services can be feasibly aligned at this time point, as we are in the infancy of LB as a standard-of-care test in the NHS. We have an opportunity to get-it-right-first-time by aligning policies at institutional and national levels, with patient input vital to developing patient-facing resources and shaping future practice according to patient preferences. The principles we have outlined here above hold true across different health systems, but cross-jurisdictional differences in legislation and regulatory frameworks governing tumor tissue handling, genomic testing, consent, data sharing, and the management of incidental hereditary findings require careful consideration when implementing tumor-based genomic testing pathways in different regions.

Summary

LB is not equivalent to a germline genetic test. The primary purpose of LB is to identify variants relevant for diagnosis or treatment of a suspected/confirmed cancer. Variants of putative germline origin should be confirmed by testing through a relevant accredited diagnostic laboratory, and a negative LB analysis should not be considered a substitute for a germline test for patients in whom hereditary predisposition is suspected.

In conclusion, expansion of somatic testing enables provision of personalized medicine to patients with cancer. LB has multiple advantages compared with solid tissue assessment and is increasingly used for this purpose. As scope and purpose of testing expand, associated ethical, legal, health service, and clinical issues must be considered. Nationally, we must endeavor to standardize information provided to patients and proactively seek the patient voice. The patient voice will be particularly important regarding the format and content of information provided about LB, including patients’ perceptions of the importance (or potentially distant secondary importance depending on the clinical context) of additional findings in LB, and how these should be discussed and managed.
